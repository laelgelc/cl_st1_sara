### The Three Merchants of Wind

In the good town of Tours, where the Loire slides past the quays with the grave face of a counsellor who has heard every lie and means to repeat none, there lived, in the reign of I know not which Louis—for kings are changed more often than rogues—a certain innkeeper named Mathurin Caboche, master of the Golden Peacock, near the faubourg of Saint-Symphorien.

This Mathurin was prosperous, married, broad in the belt, narrow in the heart, and so exact in accounts that he could tell, by hearing a guest cough, whether the fellow had drunk one cup of his wine or two. He knew how to skin a flint, milk a weathercock, roast the same bone three Sundays, and sell the smell of the kitchen to those who could not afford its meat. Had the Almighty permitted it, he would have put a toll upon daylight, weighed the shadow beneath every traveller, and charged six deniers for the air swallowed while sleeping under his roof.

Nevertheless his inn was full, for it stood well, its sign was freshly painted, its beds had fewer vermin than those of its neighbours, and Mistress Perrette, his wife, kept a kitchen whose fumes converted Jews, reconciled Lombards, and brought fasting friars to the edge of apostasy. There were capons browned with bacon, eels in green sauce, pike stuffed with herbs, pies whose crusts rose like cathedral towers, sausages black and white, mustard fierce enough to bite through a halberd, cheeses of Brie, Neufchâtel, and Touraine, and wines red, white, pale, golden, sweet, sharp, and treacherous. Mathurin loved every dish chiefly at the moment when another man paid for it.

Now there came to Tours, for the great fair, three students who had studied law at Poitiers, theology at Orléans, medicine at Montpellier, and payment nowhere. Their names were Colin Brise-Pot, Gautier Trousse-Chemise, and Noël Mange-Miette; though, because scholars esteem Latin as thieves esteem darkness, they called themselves in public Colinius, Galterius, and Natalis. They had among them one velvet cap without velvet, two shirts with three sleeves, a broken sword, seven counterfeit letters, a pair of dice loaded like a bishop’s table, and eleven sols, of which eight were owed to a laundress and the rest to Saint Julian, patron of travellers, who has long been accustomed to bad debts.

Colin was a Picard, ardent as mustard and quick to strike before deciding whom; Gautier an Angevin, supple of tongue, who could borrow your purse and make you apologise for its emptiness; Noël a Norman, pale, solemn, and patient, who lied only when speaking and stole only when awake. These three limbs of Satan had been expelled from one college for singing indecent responses at matins, from another for selling indulgences against examination, and from a third because the rector discovered that his mule had been elected dean.

On entering Tours, they saw the fair spread along the streets: merchants of silk and leather, horse-dealers, tooth-drawers, jugglers, relic-sellers, Germans with knives, Spaniards with ribbons, Gascons with promises, peasants with poultry, ladies with masks, priests with appetites, and officers of justice looking everywhere except where justice might be found.

“Companions,” said Colin, “here is a harvest, but we lack a sickle.”

“Pardieu,” answered Gautier, “a bold face is the sharpest sickle.”

Noël pointed to the Golden Peacock, from whose door came the odour of roasting goose.

“There,” he said, “is our granary.”

They retired behind the church of Saint-Julien, where they composed their nobility. Colin became the Sieur de Montperdu, nephew to a Picardian count; Gautier became the Viscount de Beau-Soufflet, commissioner for certain rich merchants of Angers; and Noël, because gravity sits naturally upon an empty stomach, became Messire Noël de la Roche-aux-Croûtes, counsellor to a duke whose name they prudently forgot to mention. They brushed one another with a bunch of nettles, changed the velvet cap at each street corner, wrapped the broken sword in cloth as if it were a treasure from Damascus, and marched into the Golden Peacock with such lofty steps that a blind man might have taken them for princes and a wise man for actors.

Mathurin came bowing, smiling, rubbing his hands, advancing when he heard “crowns,” retreating when he heard “later,” and hovering between them like a cat between three covered dishes.

“Host,” said Gautier, “have you chambers fit for gentlemen?”

“The king himself—God preserve his purse!—could ask no better.”

“Have you wine of Vouvray?”

“Such wine as made Saint Martin charitable.”

“Capons?”

“Fat as abbots.”

“Trout?”

“Fresh enough to leap from the platter.”

“Beds?”

“Soft as a usurer’s conscience after confession.”

“Then,” said Colin, tossing him a silver crown which they had reserved as seed-corn for greater villainy, “prepare all. My servants and mules follow.”

Mathurin bit the crown, rang it upon the table, examined the edge, kissed it inwardly, and called them “my lords” so many times that the title might have become true through repetition.

For five days the three students lived as emperors delivered from physicians. At breakfast they had bread soaked in hippocras, eggs, ham, and little fried fish; at dinner, soups, roasts, pies, salads, tarts, cheeses, and fruits; at supper, whatever had escaped dinner, together with fresh reinforcements. They sent one capon back because it had died without nobility, another because its left thigh was less tender than its right, and a third because Colin declared it resembled a professor who had once refused him credit. They drank Vouvray to awaken thirst, Chinon to honour the country, Anjou wine from patriotism, and Orleans wine because refusing it would have been discourteous.

At table they disputed magnificently over who should pay.

“Not a denier, Viscount,” Colin would cry. “You paid for the twelve falcons at Blois.”

“And you, my dear Montperdu,” said Gautier, “lost eighty crowns at tennis for my amusement.”

“Gentlemen,” Noël interrupted, “the affair belongs to me. His Grace entrusted me with funds for hospitality.”

Then they threatened one another with friendship, drew imaginary bills of exchange, named bankers in Lyons, Genoa, Bruges, Venice, and Jerusalem, and spoke carelessly of sums large enough to rebuild Babel. Mathurin, listening behind the door, grew rounder at every thousand crowns and served an extra dish lest such treasure take offence.

At the fair they behaved like locusts educated in jurisprudence. They sold a blind horse to a spectacle-maker, persuaded a peasant that his own wife was a disguised princess, exchanged a monk’s cloak for three sausages, and announced that a dancing bear would preach against luxury, thereby filling the square with clergy. Colin fought two soldiers over the theological question whether a red nose proved courage; Gautier collected alms for the repair of a bridge that had never existed; Noël, dressed in a borrowed physician’s gown, prescribed hot wine to sixteen patients and drank the medicine himself.

They rang the bell of Saint-Gatien at midnight, set a constable’s breeches upon the statue of Charlemagne, released twenty geese during a performance of the martyrdom of Saint Denis, and made such uproar that an old woman declared Antichrist had come, while her husband replied that Antichrist would surely have brought money.

All this Mathurin forgave, for greed is a plaster broad enough to cover many bruises. But on the morning of the sixth day he reviewed his account: seventeen capons, four geese, two hares, a quarter of beef, thirty-six sausages, seven pies, eleven cheeses, five baskets of bread, ninety-two flagons of wine, three broken stools, one cracked window, a chamber pot used as a helmet, and compensation to a notary whose beard Colin had set alight while demonstrating Greek fire.

The total made Mathurin tremble with delight and fear. Delight, because it was enormous; fear, because the servants and mules had not arrived.

He therefore entered the private chamber where the three rogues were finishing a breakfast that might have sustained a monastery through Lent. He bowed first to Colin, next to Gautier, last to Noël, and finally to the table, which he loved most.

“My noble lords,” said he, “I ask pardon a thousand times. A miserable tradesman must preserve order in his miserable books. There is a little account—not worth mentioning, a fly, a grain, a crumb—but today is market day, and my suppliers are wolves.”

“An account?” cried Colin. “Bring it, that I may pay.”

“No,” said Gautier, striking the table. “You insult me. The honour is mine.”

Noël rose with ducal gravity.

“Gentlemen, draw swords if you wish, but neither shall rob me of this expense.”

Mathurin beamed. The students cursed one another with exquisite courtesy. Colin reached for an empty purse; Gautier slapped his pockets as if astonished not to hear cathedral bells; Noël searched his sleeves, boots, hat, and the saltcellar.

Then all three stopped.

“A singular misfortune,” said Noël.

“A scandal,” said Gautier.

“A conspiracy of bankers,” said Colin.

They explained that their money chests had been delayed; their servants had taken the wrong road; their letters of credit lay in a portmanteau on a mule, the mule upon a ferry, the ferry upon the Loire, and the Loire, very inconsiderately, had not yet brought it to the door.

Mathurin’s face fell as a pudding falls when the oven is opened. He demanded rings, jewels, arms, horses, servants, seals, pledges, cousins, sureties, or at least the address of one honest man. The students offered him the broken sword, but he unwrapped it and found that it possessed neither point, edge, nor hilt worth pawning.

“Thieves!” cried Mathurin.

“Softly,” said Gautier. “Among gentlemen, such a word is paid with blood.”

“Among innkeepers,” Mathurin replied, “such dinners are paid with crowns.”

Colin barred the door, not to prevent Mathurin’s escape but to make the scene more solemn.

“My host,” said he, “the axiom of Justinian teaches that where gold fails, wit may discharge obligation, provided the creditor consents and the wine is tolerable.”

“Justinian never kept an inn.”

“His loss. Hear our proposal. Each of us shall tell a true tale, shameful, foolish, bawdy, or abominable. You shall also tell one. He whose tale is judged the worst shall pay the entire account.”

Mathurin scratched his ear. He had no desire for tales; tales neither bought barley nor repaired windows. Yet he feared that if he called the watch, the students would escape during the argument, or beat him, or persuade the sergeant that he owed them money. Moreover, like all men who have one excellent anecdote, he believed himself the master of conversation.

“Who shall judge?” he asked.

“All four,” said Noël, “according to conscience.”

“That leaves three consciences against one.”

“Then according to laughter.”

Mathurin considered. He had seen them eat, and believed no purse could be as empty as their stomachs had been. Perhaps the game was merely a noble whim, after which the promised mule would arrive. Besides, he wished exceedingly to tell his story.

“Agreed,” he said. “But no lying.”

At this the students crossed themselves.

Colin began.

“In a village of Picardy,” said he, “whose name I conceal because half its inhabitants are my cousins and the other half my enemies, there lived a peasant called Hennequin, a fellow broad as a barn door and twice as difficult to move. He loved wine with a love that poets reserve for ladies and saints for paradise. He drank when hot to cool himself, when cold to warm himself, when sad for comfort, when merry for company, when alone to avoid melancholy, and when with others to avoid discourtesy.

“One Sunday Hennequin went to mass after spending the night beneath a tavern bench. The priest preached upon penance, fasting, mortification, abstinence, tears, ashes, and the narrow road to salvation. Hennequin slept through most of it, waking only when the priest thundered, ‘Three days upon bread and water!’

“Now, being lately troubled by dreams in which devils roasted him like a chestnut, Hennequin presented himself after mass for confession. He confessed that he drank, swore, gambled, beat his donkey, coveted his neighbour’s sow, and once, while drunk, attempted to kiss the stone saint at the crossroads because he took it for a widow.

“The priest, who had heard worse before breakfast, prescribed three days of bread and water.

“Hennequin staggered home repeating, ‘Three days, bread and water; three days, bread and water,’ lest the devil steal one word. But at the tavern he met Jeannot the miller, who asked why he looked like a condemned sausage.

“‘Penance,’ said Hennequin. ‘The priest orders three days of bread and water.’

“‘That is severe,’ said Jeannot. ‘You had better strengthen yourself first.’

“The reasoning appeared theological. Hennequin drank six cups. On leaving, the words of his penance had become ‘three breads and water.’ At the baker’s door he bought three loaves, but the baker’s wife asked what he wanted with so much bread.

“‘It is my penance: three breads and water.’

“‘Impossible,’ she said. ‘No priest prescribes water in winter. He must have said three breads and butter.’

“Hennequin bought butter. Farther along he met a butcher, who assured him that bread and butter without ham produced dangerous vapours. The butcher added ham. A fishwife declared ham too heating and sold him an eel. A cheese-maker proved by Saint Paul that eel required cheese; and the wine-seller, hearing the whole matter, said water was undoubtedly a corruption of the Latin vinum.

“Thus Hennequin came home bearing three loaves, a crock of butter, a ham, an eel, two cheeses, and a jug so large that a child might have been baptised in it.

“His wife, Alizon, a woman as mild as onion peel and twice as soothing to the eyes, demanded an explanation.

“‘My penance,’ said Hennequin. ‘Three days of bread, butter, ham, eel, cheese, and wine.’

“‘Then sin has improved since my girlhood,’ said she.

“They ate until midnight. On the second day Hennequin added a goose, because fasting had weakened him; on the third he invited Jeannot, the butcher, the baker’s wife, and two fiddlers, because penance without witnesses brings no edification.

“The priest, passing by, heard singing and entered. There sat Hennequin crowned with sausages, holding a cup and exhorting everyone to repent.

“‘Villain!’ cried the priest. ‘Did I not order bread and water?’

“Hennequin looked at the feast, then at the priest, and burst into tears.

“‘Alas, father, what a memory I have! If this is what I eat when I forget my penance, imagine the banquet I should have prepared had I remembered it exactly.’”

The company laughed, Mathurin included, though he observed that the tale was indecently favourable to unpaid eating.

“A fair beginning,” said Gautier. “But Picards are children in wickedness. Listen to an Angevin.”

He cleared his throat and continued.

“There was at Saumur a wealthy widow named Madame Ysabeau, neither young enough to be careless nor old enough to be resigned, who maintained a steward, two lackeys, a cook, a gardener, a chaplain, three lapdogs, and a servant girl called Marion. This Marion was fresh from the country, red-cheeked, strong-armed, simple in speech, and ignorant of city customs, having been taught that noble persons were made of finer clay and that Latin was a disease of priests.

“Madame Ysabeau’s nephew, Raoul, a young soldier with feathers above, spurs below, and little between but vanity, came to lodge in the house. From the first hour he cast eyes upon Marion, who carried water without spilling, kneaded dough without sighing, and could lift a sack that would have required two lawyers and a writ.

“‘Pretty child,’ said Raoul, ‘will you serve me?’

“‘I serve everyone in the house, monsieur.’

“‘But will you serve me privately?’

“‘If my mistress commands.’

“‘There are services,’ said he, twisting his moustache, ‘which a gallant gentleman commands for himself.’

“Marion did not understand. Raoul winked, sighed, placed a hand upon his heart, compared her eyes to stars, her teeth to pearls, her bosom to two hills of Paradise, and her innocence to a locked garden whose key he happened to possess. You can imagine: a soldier’s poetry is a ladder made to climb into another’s orchard.

“Marion listened gravely.

“‘And what must I do?’

“‘Come to my chamber tonight, after the household sleeps, and perform the little service love requires.’

“‘What tools shall I bring?’

“Raoul laughed. ‘Nature has supplied all tools.’

“Now Marion, fearing to appear stupid, went to the old cook and asked what private service a gentleman might require at night.

“The cook, who hated Raoul because he had called her soup horse-water, replied, ‘He means polishing.’

“‘Polishing what?’

“‘His equipment. Soldiers are very particular. Take stiff brushes, sand, grease, a file, and the strong vinegar.’

“Marion thanked her. At midnight she entered Raoul’s chamber carrying a bucket, two brushes, a whetstone, a rasp, goose fat, vinegar, and a cloth coarse enough to scour rust from a cannon.

“Raoul lay in bed perfumed, ribboned, smiling, and nearly naked beneath the cover.

“‘Ah, my little doe,’ said he, ‘you have come to serve me.’

“‘That is why I brought the tools.’

“‘Tools?’

“She displayed them. Raoul, supposing this some rustic game, extinguished the candle and told her to begin gently.

“Marion seized the first piece of military equipment she found, which was his fine Spanish boot, and scrubbed so vigorously that she removed the polish, the dye, and half the leather. Raoul, hearing the noise, cried, ‘Not that! The principal weapon!’

“‘Why did you not say so?’

“She found his sword.

“‘No, no—the weapon nature gave me!’

“Marion at last understood enough to pull back the cover. Yet, seeing what he meant and recalling that he had asked for polishing, she poured on vinegar, took the stiff brush in one hand and the file in the other, and prepared for honest labour.

“Raoul sprang from the bed with a howl fit to awaken the dead at Fontevraud. He ran naked into the corridor, struck his head upon a hanging lamp, upset the chaplain, frightened the dogs, and fell into a chest of flour.

“Madame Ysabeau appeared with a candle.

“‘What is this?’ she demanded.

“Raoul, white from head to foot, pointed at Marion. ‘The savage tried to murder me!’

“‘Murder you?’ said Marion. ‘He asked me to service his weapon. I had only got off the first rust.’

“The widow looked at the small weapon, then at the large file.

“‘Continue,’ she said. ‘My nephew has often boasted that he is made of steel. It is time someone tested him.’”

Mathurin laughed until his keys danced at his belt.

“Good,” said Colin. “The cook deserves canonisation.”

“She already has it among soldiers’ wives,” Gautier replied.

Noël, who had eaten during both tales, wiped his mouth and assumed the expression of a magistrate about to condemn a man for stealing less cleverly than himself.

“My story,” said he, “concerns marriage, that sacrament by which two persons take one house in order to quarrel without travelling.

“At Rouen lived a draper named Guillaume Grommelard, rich in cloth, poor in patience, who married a handsome woman called Tiphaine. She had black eyes, white hands, a sharp tongue, and that noble domestic virtue by which a wife remembers every word her husband wishes forgotten.

“They were fond of each other during the first month, civil during the second, suspicious during the third, and married ever afterward.

“Guillaume complained of everything. If the soup was hot, she wished to burn him; if cold, she desired his death by ague. If the room was swept, she had raised dust; if unswept, she kept a pigsty. She spent too much, saved too little, talked too loudly, answered too slowly, went to church too often, prayed too briefly, dressed for neighbours, undressed for no one, salted the meat, spoiled the fish, encouraged servants, insulted customers, and breathed at night with deliberate malice.

“Tiphaine returned accusation for accusation, with interest. He was stingy, sour, jealous, vain, cowardly before creditors, brave before maids, short in measure, long in sermons, quick at table, slow in bed, and so full of bad counsel that if he were drowned, the river would flow backward.

“One evening Guillaume returned from the tavern, where he had drunk enough to make every chair appear occupied by an enemy. He found a thread upon the floor.

“‘Filth!’ he cried.

“Tiphaine continued sewing.

“‘Do you hear? This house is full of filth.’

“She picked up the thread.

“He found an onion skin.

“‘Filth!’

“She threw it away.

“He discovered a spot of ashes, a crumb beneath the bench, and one feather escaped from a cushion, pronouncing each a separate proof of her unfitness for matrimony.

“‘If you desire a convent,’ said Tiphaine, ‘shave your head and enter one.’

“‘I desire cleanliness.’

“‘Then sleep in your shop among the folded cloth.’

“Guillaume overturned a basket while gesturing. Linen, yarn, stockings, and mending rolled across the floor.

“‘Look!’ he shouted. ‘Filth everywhere! Produce the filth, woman! Show me all the filth you have hidden, that I may judge it at once.’

“‘You command me?’

“‘I command you as husband, master, citizen, taxpayer, and man of good standing.’

“Tiphaine rose. ‘Wait here.’

“She went into the courtyard. Guillaume, pleased by obedience, sat down and poured himself another cup. Presently she returned carrying the night-soil bucket, which was full to the rim, for the servant had neglected her morning duty.

“‘What is that?’ asked Guillaume.

“‘The filth you demanded.’

“‘Take it away.’

“‘You required to judge it.’

“‘I revoke the order.’

“‘Too late. A husband’s command is sacred.’

“She set the bucket before him. Guillaume leaped up, caught his heel in the overturned basket, and fell backward. The bucket fell forward. What followed I shall describe in legal Latin: stercus super totum.

“Guillaume rose crowned, anointed, baptised, confirmed, and buried in the substance he had called for. He opened his mouth to curse, which was unfortunate. Tiphaine rang a handbell and cried through the window, ‘Neighbours, come quickly! My husband has found the filth, and he is wearing it to prevent its escape.’

“People arrived from three streets. Guillaume fled to the well, but the rope broke; he ran to the river, but two dogs followed him; he hid in his shop, where customers entered asking whether he had imported a new dye.

“From that day, whenever he complained of a crumb, Tiphaine merely asked, ‘Shall I bring the rest?’

“And Guillaume, who had learned cleanliness from its deepest source, replied, ‘No, wife. Let sleeping filth lie.’”

Even Mathurin pounded the table at this, though he protested that Noël had carried the privilege of narration too near the privy.

“Now, host,” said Colin, “we have delivered our merchandise. Show yours.”

Mathurin filled his cup. His eyes shone, for he believed the crown of infamy already on his head, and a man will confess much for a crown, particularly when he supposes it to be honourable.

“My tale,” he said, “is true in every syllable.”

“So much the worse for someone,” murmured Gautier.

“It concerns a bride,” Mathurin continued, “young, modest, beautiful, and so ashamed of bodily imperfection that she would blush if a hen laid an egg in her presence. She had been reared by an aunt who taught her embroidery, prayer, curtsies, preserves, and the concealment of every natural sound.

“This bride married a substantial man—handsome then, though prosperity has increased him—who loved order, good cooking, honest money, and quiet nights.”

“An admirable portrait,” said Noël.

Mathurin bowed.

“On their wedding night the bride sat upon the bed as trembling as a fish on straw. The husband spoke tenderly, praised her hair, kissed her hand, removed one pin, then another, and advanced through the fortifications of modesty with all lawful banners displayed.

“But during love’s conflict, nature, who respects neither rank nor sacrament, caused the bride to let slip a little wind.

“It was nothing—a sigh from below, a mouse’s trumpet, the death-rattle of a pea. Yet the bride believed heaven had cracked. She stiffened and whispered, ‘Did you hear?’

“The husband, wishing to spare her, said, ‘Hear what?’

“‘That noise.’

“‘It was the bed.’

“They resumed. Presently came a second report, louder and more resolute.

“‘That was not the bed,’ said the bride.

“‘The dog is beneath it.’

“‘We have no dog.’

“‘Then the neighbour has one.’

“They resumed again, but shame disturbs the humours, and the more the poor bride attempted to imprison the traitor, the more violently it beat against the gate. There followed a third blast, so long and various that it began in Burgundy and ended in Brittany.

“The candle flickered.

“‘Merciful Virgin!’ cried the bride. ‘What shall I do?’

“The husband, though courteous, began to laugh.

“She covered her face. ‘I shall die.’

“‘No, no,’ said he. ‘The first was the bed, the second the neighbour’s dog, and the third—’

“Here he lacked an invention.

“At that moment the town watch passed beneath the window crying the hour: ‘Midnight, and all is well!’

“‘There,’ said the husband. ‘The watchman blew his horn.’

“The bride thanked God and her husband. But scarcely had she done so when a fourth explosion burst forth, prodigious, rolling, martial, and triumphant, a bombardment fit to breach the walls of Jerusalem. The curtains swelled, the candle went out, a shoe fell from the stool, and in the courtyard a rooster, deceived by the magnitude of the signal, began to crow.

“The watchman below halted.

“‘Who blows there?’ he shouted.

“The bride buried herself beneath the blankets.

“The husband leaned from the window and replied, ‘A friend.’

“‘A friend?’ called the watchman. ‘Then tell your friend she has missed the gate, for the enemy is surely behind her!’

“The bride remained in bed three days from shame. But afterward, whenever the husband boasted before his friends that his wife had brought him a rich dowry, he would add, ‘And four trumpets, though one alone is enough to wake the watch.’”

Mathurin finished with a great slap of the table and laughed first, as prudent tellers do when uncertain of their audience.

The students stared at him. Colin’s mouth opened. Gautier’s cup remained halfway to his lips. Noël crossed himself.

“Host,” Colin whispered at last, “whose bride was this?”

Mathurin, swollen with triumph, tapped his chest.

“My own.”

“And the lady?”

“My wife Perrette, as sure as this is Tours.”

Then the three students rose together.

“Miracle!” cried Gautier.

“Prodigy!” cried Noël.

“Plenary indulgence!” shouted Colin. “We are defeated, buried, annihilated. Our tales are nursery prayers beside yours.”

Mathurin smiled, though not entirely certain whether to be pleased.

“What art!” said Gautier. “What truth! What delicacy! The first little wind, the dog, the watchman, the candle, the rooster! Aristotle himself could not have arranged the progression.”

“Nor Saint Augustine confessed it more humbly,” added Noël.

Colin seized Mathurin’s hand.

“My dear host, I have heard tales at Paris, Amiens, Arras, Chartres, Poitiers, Bourges, and in every tavern between, but never one equal to this. Your wife must be a lady of incomparable spirit.”

“She is,” said Mathurin, now as round with vanity as a barrel with new wine.

“A historical person!”

“A second Helen!”

“A Semiramis of the lower airs!”

“Gentlemen,” said Mathurin, “moderate yourselves.”

“Impossible,” replied Gautier. “We must see her.”

Mathurin hesitated. Perrette was upstairs counting linen and would not be delighted to discover that her wedding-night misfortune had become a competition among strangers. Yet praise, which opens doors that gold cannot, had already turned the key in his head.

“She is occupied.”

“One glimpse,” begged Colin. “We shall salute her as the tenth Muse.”

“There are only nine,” said Mathurin.

“Until today,” Noël replied.

They crowded around him with titles and compliments. They called him prince of narrators, emperor of innkeepers, phoenix of husbands, Demosthenes of the bedchamber, Cæsar of the counterpane, and lawful proprietor of the most famous trumpet in Christendom. Mathurin resisted as a miser resists payment: feebly when vanity promises interest.

“At least,” he said, “remain respectful.”

“We shall kneel.”

“No mention of the watchman.”

“We shall speak only of her beauty.”

“Nor of the rooster.”

“We have forgotten every bird.”

Mathurin led them from the private chamber, across the common room, and toward the stair. Now Fortune, who sometimes favours thieves because honest men bore her, had arranged that a carter entering with two barrels should leave the street door wide open. Beyond it lay the sunlit road, the shouting fair, the bridge, the lanes, the alleys, the whole broad kingdom of escape.

The students saw it at once.

Gautier nudged Colin. Colin trod upon Noël’s foot. Noël, without changing countenance, whispered, “The tenth Muse awaits above; the nine others wait outside.”

At the stair Mathurin turned.

“Follow closely, gentlemen. My wife dislikes noise.”

“Then we must enter softly,” said Colin.

Mathurin mounted first, for greedy men go before when collecting honour and behind when blows are distributed. He climbed six steps.

The students followed two.

He climbed six more.

They descended one.

At the landing he called, “Perrette! Perrette, my dove! Some noble gentlemen desire to pay you homage.”

From above came his wife’s voice.

“What noble gentlemen?”

“The Picardian lord, the viscount, and the ducal counsellor.”

“The three gluttons?”

Mathurin turned to explain that ladies speak familiarly in private. But the stair behind him was empty.

He heard a rush across the common room, a bench overturned, the carter swearing, and three pairs of heels striking the street. By the time he reached the door, Colin was among the ribbon stalls, Gautier had vanished behind a procession of penitents, and Noël was walking gravely beside two Dominican friars as though he had spent his life discussing predestination.

“Stop thieves!” cried Mathurin. “My account! Seize the merchants!”

“Which merchants?” shouted the crowd.

“The Picard, the Angevin, and the Norman!”

At this half the fair ran, the other half gave chase, and nobody knew why. A sausage-seller seized a judge; a constable arrested a mule; two Germans caught each other; and the dancing bear, taking alarm, dragged its keeper into a stall of devotional images.

Mathurin pursued Colin, then feared Gautier; pursued Gautier, then glimpsed Noël; pursued Noël, then collided with the widow Ysabeau’s actual nephew, who struck him for insulting his feathers. He received a kick from a horse, a cabbage in the ear, a basket over the head, and three pieces of advice, all contradictory.

When at last he returned to the Golden Peacock, bruised, breathless, and unpaid, Perrette stood in the doorway with her arms crossed.

“Where are the noble gentlemen?”

“Gone.”

“Did they pay?”

Mathurin said nothing.

“How much?”

He named the sum.

Perrette became very calm, which in wives is often the final trumpet before battle.

“And why,” she asked, “did you lead three unpaid strangers upstairs?”

Mathurin looked at the floor.

“To see you.”

“To see me?”

“They had heard of you.”

“Heard what?”

At this question Mathurin’s courage, which had survived soldiers, horses, constables, and the loss of ninety-two flagons, abandoned him entirely. He attempted to speak of beauty, fame, the Muses, Aristotle, and the lawful diversions of merchants, but each word tightened Perrette’s mouth.

At last she learned the truth, for husbands conceal nothing so badly as a joke they have told successfully.

There was silence.

Then Perrette seized the nearest object, which happened to be the full chamber pot carried down by a maid who, seeing the storm, abandoned both vessel and duty. Mathurin retreated toward the door.

“My dove—”

“Call the watchman.”

“My jewel—”

“Fetch the rooster.”

“My tenth Muse—”

She flung the contents.

Mathurin received them from chin to shoes, while the carter, the servants, two guests, a pilgrim, and half the returning crowd applauded as warmly as the students had applauded his tale.

Thus the master of the Golden Peacock lost his capons, his wine, his account, his dignity, and the secret of his wedding night; and instead of crowns he received another of his wife’s offerings, paid promptly, in full, and without appeal.

As for Colin, Gautier, and Noël, they met that evening beyond the bridge, where they divided three purses acquired during the confusion and drank to Mathurin’s health with money stolen from the constable. Afterward they went toward Angers, dressed as pilgrims and singing a psalm whose refrain concerned free lodging.

Whether they repented, I cannot say. Men of their profession generally postpone repentance until age, prison, or the gallows gives them leisure. But I know this: in Tours, for many years thereafter, whenever an innkeeper boasted of his cleverness, someone asked whether he too kept a tenth Muse upstairs; and whenever Mathurin demanded payment in advance, Perrette would smile and say:

“Take care, husband. The watchman may be passing.”

### THE MEN ON THE FLOE

There are places where civilization rests upon law, custom, and the policeman’s brass buttons; and there are other places where it rests upon the sobriety of one large man.

At Port Desolation, north of the Arctic Circle and considerably beyond the good intentions of Providence, that man was Big George Gannon. When George was sober, the camp thawed out its picks, weighed its dust, mended its harness, and attended to business. When George was drunk, business crawled beneath the nearest bunk and waited.

Port Desolation consisted of a trading shed, a bunkhouse, two cabins, a roofless blacksmith shop, and a saloon called the Aurora, although no light save that of seal oil and bad whisky had ever been seen in it. Behind the camp the white hills rose like the ribs of a buried monster. Before it stretched a waste of frozen marsh, and beyond that the Arctic Ocean went growling under its armour.

Big George had harpooned whales, broken horses, captained a schooner, and spent two seasons in jail at Nome without permitting the experience to improve him. He stood six feet four in his stockings, weighed two hundred and sixty pounds after a hard winter, and possessed a red beard which appeared to have been set on fire at some early date and never properly extinguished. His left ear was notched, his nose leaned toward the west, and a white scar divided his forehead like a crack in a cliff.

He also had a wife.

Her name was Nukka. She was small, quiet, and quicker with dogs, boats, weather, and human foolishness than any person within three hundred miles. George adored her with a devotion he concealed under roaring commands; she governed him with three words, two glances, and occasional silence.

On the evening when Captain came to Port Desolation, Nukka was away at the coastal settlement visiting her mother. This was unfortunate. George had been drinking since noon.

The newcomer was not in truth a captain. His name was Alan Mercer, but he had once led a surveying party through the Mackenzie country, and the title had stuck to him as burs cling to wool. He was tall and lean, with frost-grey eyes, a brown moustache, and the disagreeable habit of standing straight when other men attempted to bend him. His clothes were good, though travel-worn; his speech was measured; and he carried himself with the quietness which ignorant men sometimes mistake for weakness and wise men usually leave alone.

He entered the Aurora with ice on his shoulders and a rawhide packet under one arm. Every man in the room looked first at Captain and then at Big George.

George sat upon a barrel beside the stove. His fur cap was cocked over one eye; his boots occupied the only unbroken chair; and in one hand he held a tin cup of whisky which could have stripped paint from a schooner at ten paces.

“Stranger,” said George, in a voice resembling the cough of a bull-walrus in an iron tank, “you’re standin’ in my climate.”

Captain glanced at the ceiling, where smoke rolled in heavy layers.

“I had supposed it belonged to the government.”

The camp drew one long breath and held it.

George removed his boots from the chair and planted them on the floor. This operation was performed deliberately, and the boards complained under it.

“What might be your name?”

“Mercer.”

“That all?”

“I am sometimes called Captain.”

George’s beard spread in a grin.

“Captain! Hear that, fellers? We got the navy with us. Where’s your ship, Captain?”

“Behind the hills.”

“Got wheels on her?”

“Dogs.”

Someone near the stove strangled a laugh. George looked toward the sound, and silence fell so heavily that the flame of the oil lamp seemed to shrink.

Captain placed his packet on the bar.

“I’m looking for Jim Barton. He came through here three weeks ago with a man named Sullivan.”

“Kid Sullivan,” supplied the trader, a bent little man called Peabody. “They’re over at the coast. Went to fetch driftwood and seal meat.”

“They were due back yesterday,” another man said.

George drained his cup and struck it on the barrel.

“You come grub-staked?”

“I have my own outfit.”

“College feller, ain’t you?”

Captain took off his gloves.

“I attended one.”

“Thought so. I can smell education. Smells like soap, mostly.”

A few men laughed because George expected it. Captain looked at the giant’s cup, then at the giant.

“I have noticed that whisky sometimes destroys the sense of smell.”

The room froze.

Big George rose. It was as though a log wall had decided to stand up. His shoulders obscured the lamp; his arms hung beside him like rock pillars; and when he rolled his head, the bones of his neck cracked one after another.

“Stranger,” he said, “I been feelin’ poorly all day. Kind of low and peaceable. I believe I’ll make you over.”

“That seems ambitious.”

“I’ll start at the top and work down.”

George swung.

Captain moved half a step, and the fist passed his cheek with the rush of a thrown maul. Before George could recover, Captain drove his right hand into the red beard. The blow snapped the big head backward. Captain struck again beneath the heart, then again over the eye.

George sat down upon the barrel, went through it, and emerged amid a shower of staves, wearing one hoop across his shoulders.

No man laughed. Several remembered pressing business outdoors.

George tore off the hoop and came forward with a roar. He caught Captain around the body. His arms closed like the jaws of a bear trap. Captain’s feet left the floor, his ribs bent inward, and his face went white; but he brought the heel of his palm under George’s chin, twisted as they fell, and the two men crashed across a table in a storm of cups, cards, splinters, and curses.

They rolled against the stove. It rocked dangerously. Peabody seized the stovepipe with both hands and prayed in a practical manner.

George rose first, blood running from his eyebrow. Captain came up from one knee and met him. There was no science now. They struck at the same instant, collided, grappled, and staggered from wall to wall, tearing bunks loose and crushing stools into kindling. George laughed as he fought—a huge, bubbling laugh which made him more terrible—while Captain saved his breath and drove his fists like pistons.

At length George caught him by the throat and pinned him against the door.

“Now,” he grunted, “I reckon we’ll—”

The door burst inward.

A man wrapped in caribou skins fell against them, dragging a cloud of snow behind him. His face was grey with frost; his lashes were white; and one mitten hung by its thong.

“Barton!” he gasped. “Kid Sullivan! Ice broke loose—carryin’ ’em seaward!”

George released Captain.

The wind howled through the open doorway, seized the smoke, and flung it around the room. Beyond the threshold the night was a white madness. Snow streamed level with the ground; the sky had vanished; and the cold entered like a knife seeking ribs.

Captain wiped blood from his mouth.

“Where?”

“Black Head. We was haulin’ wood off shore ice. Crack opened between. I jumped it. They couldn’t. Whole field broke away.” The messenger sagged against the bar. “Blizzard shut down. Tide’s runnin’ out.”

“How long ago?”

“Three hours. Maybe four.”

Captain looked at the men.

“Get the dogs.”

No one moved.

Peabody released the stovepipe. “It ain’t possible, Captain.”

“I did not ask if it was possible.”

“The shore lead’ll be open,” said a miner. “No boat could live among that ice in this wind.”

“Then how do you propose to reach them?” Captain demanded.

The miner stared at the floor.

Another man said, “They’ll be five mile out by now. Maybe ten. You couldn’t find a church in this blow if you was inside it.”

Captain put on his gloves.

“Barton is my friend.”

“That don’t close the lead,” Peabody answered gently.

“No. It merely decides who goes.”

He turned toward the door.

Big George stepped in front of him.

For a moment they stood as they had stood before, bloodied and breathing hard, while the storm clawed at the building behind George’s shoulders.

“You don’t know the coast,” George said.

“I shall follow the trail.”

“There won’t be no trail in five minutes.”

“Then I shall manage without one.”

“You’ll manage to freeze.”

Captain’s grey eyes hardened. “Are you preventing me from leaving?”

George looked at him soberly. Whisky still burned in his blood, but the foolishness had gone out of it as flame goes out beneath a bucket of water.

“No,” he said. “I’m goin’ with you.”

The camp stirred.

Captain studied the battered face. “Why?”

“Because I know the ice. Because I got dogs. Because you’re too blamed ignorant to go alone.” George reached for his parka. “An’ because any man that can hit like you deserves a chance to die natural.”

They took George’s team—nine rangy dogs with the devil in their ancestry and ice between their toes. Peabody packed food, blankets, rope, a small oil stove, and every scrap of dry tinder in camp. Men who had declared the rescue impossible suddenly discovered a fever for buckling traces and lashing gear. Cowardice is often energetic once another man has assumed the risk.

The messenger gave them the bearing. George listened, asked three questions, and struck the dogs into motion.

They went out under the lash of the gale.

At once the camp disappeared. Snow swallowed the buildings, the lamplight, and the anxious faces at the door. The world became a rushing whiteness without sky or earth. Wind struck from the northeast, ravening across the flats, flinging ice dust against their cheeks until the skin burned as beneath hot sand. The dogs ran half blind, their bodies low, traces humming. The sled leaped and slewed behind them.

George stood on one runner and drove by instinct, memory, and the faint slope of the land beneath the snow. Captain ran beside the sled until his lungs rasped, then rode, then ran again to keep the blood alive in his feet.

For two hours they travelled through a universe that had been erased.

Once the sled struck a drift and turned completely over. Packs, stove, men, and blankets rolled together. George came up roaring at the dogs, which had continued into the storm dragging the overturned sled like a plough. Captain flung himself upon the trailing rope and was pulled forty yards before he could check them.

“Nice tame pets,” he said when George reached him.

“Tame?” George bellowed. “Them ain’t dogs. They’re creditors. Stop feedin’ ’em and they eat your future.”

Later the lead dog halted so suddenly that the team piled behind her. George crawled forward and found black water boiling beneath a thin lid of snow. They had reached a river channel, not yet wholly frozen. He turned west, feeling a path among pressure ridges while the ice cracked under them with sounds like distant rifle shots.

Near midnight a low shape materialized in the blizzard. A dog barked. Another answered. Then came the smell of seal oil, and a skin-covered shelter rose from the snow.

Nukka stood in the entrance.

She wore a fox-fur hood and sealskin trousers, and her dark eyes passed from George’s cut forehead to Captain’s swollen mouth.

“You drink,” she said to George.

“Some,” he admitted.

“You fight.”

“Some.”

“You lose?”

George drew himself up. “Woman, this here was interrupted.”

She looked at Captain.

“He was winning,” Captain said.

Nukka’s mouth moved very slightly. With her this was laughter.

Inside the shelter, George told her of the drifting floe. She listened without exclamation, then began gathering gear.

“There’s a three-hole skin canoe at Kalluk’s cache,” she said. “Half mile south. New cover.”

“Sea’ll be murder,” George answered.

“Yes.”

“Lead may be two mile wide.”

“Yes.”

“You stay here.”

“No.”

George’s beard bristled. “Nukka—”

“You find cache in this storm?”

George paused.

“You know where old ice meets new?”

He scowled.

“You know current after north wind?”

“I know plenty.”

She tied a line around a bundle of paddles. “Then you know I come.”

There are moments when a wise husband recognizes the limit of his authority. Big George was not consistently wise, but marriage had educated him in flashes.

They left the dogs with Nukka’s mother and hauled the canoe to the shore. It was a long, narrow craft of hide stretched over a light wooden frame, decked except for three round openings. On calm water it rode like a feather. In that sea it appeared as substantial as a dead leaf.

The ocean had torn off its white disguise. Between the shore ice and the moving pack ran a lane of black water, tossing and smoking under the wind. Cakes of ice heaved upon it, struck one another, split, and turned over with savage deliberation. Farther out, pressure ridges rose and fell like the backs of pale beasts. The blizzard drove over all, hiding the horizon and making distance a lie.

Nukka crouched at the edge, watching the ice.

“Current carries south now,” she said. “Later turns east.”

“How long?” Captain asked.

She measured the unseen tide by the motion of a half-submerged cake.

“Two hours. Maybe less.”

George pushed the canoe forward. “You stay ashore and keep fire. If we come back, we’ll need mark.”

Nukka fastened a spare line to the bow. “I wait.”

There was no farewell. In such weather words froze sooner than water.

George took the rear opening, Captain the front, and the middle place remained empty. They launched between two grinding slabs. A wave struck the bow and showered Captain with water which froze at once upon his parka. He dug his paddle deep. George shouted. The canoe leaped forward as the ice closed behind them with a crunch that would have broken timber.

They passed from the land’s last protection into the starving water.

For a while there was only labour. Paddle, brace, thrust; lift the blade before the next cake struck; bend beneath the wind; drive through slush that clutched the canoe like wet cement. Their shoulders burned. Ice formed on the paddles, increasing their weight. Every few minutes George ordered a halt while they beat the blades against the deck.

The canoe climbed waves it had no right to climb, poised upon crests with half its length in air, then plunged into black hollows. Floating ice rammed its sides. Once a cake as large as the Aurora rose under Captain’s paddle. He thrust away, missed, and was nearly torn from his seat. George flung his weight outward, balancing the craft while water poured over the deck.

“Sit down!” he roared.

“I am sitting.”

“Then sit harder!”

They drove onward.

No compass could help them among the wheeling floes. George judged the wind upon his left cheek, the set of the waves beneath him, and the drift of loose ice. At intervals they hauled onto a floe, dragged the canoe across, and launched from its farther edge. The ice shook under the blows of the sea. Cracks raced between their feet, blue and sudden as lightning.

On the third floe Captain slipped. One leg plunged through, and the current seized it. George dropped the canoe, caught him by the hood, and hauled him out as a slab sheared away beneath his body.

“Careful,” George said.

Captain’s teeth showed in a frozen grin. “I had considered walking to the bottom.”

“Too far.”

They launched again.

Dawn came, but it was merely a dilution of darkness. The air turned grey; the snow became visible as millions of separate knives; and the pack stretched around them, broken, tossing, without end.

George’s face had stiffened beneath a mask of ice. Captain could no longer feel two fingers of his right hand. They had been paddling for nearly five hours when George suddenly lifted his blade.

“Listen!”

At first there was only wind, the slap of waves, and the groaning of ice.

Then a cry came thinly across the waste.

“Hallo!”

Captain shouted until his lungs tore. The answer came again, off to the east.

They drove toward it and nearly passed the floe. It was a low, dirty fragment no more than thirty yards across, hidden between higher ridges. Two figures crouched beside a hummock. One rose and waved a strip of canvas.

“Alan!” he cried.

Captain knew the voice.

“Barton!”

The floe was splitting as they approached. A dark crack opened across its middle, widened, closed with a report, and opened again. Barton dragged the second man away from it.

George brought the canoe alongside. Captain scrambled out.

Jim Barton had once been broad and ruddy; now his face was hollow, white, and old. His mittens were gone. One hand was wrapped in torn cloth. Kid Sullivan lay curled in the lee of the hummock, his eyes closed and his breathing shallow.

“We thought you were gone,” Barton said.

“So did the camp.”

Captain gripped his shoulder, then knelt by Sullivan.

“How long has he been like this?”

“Couple hours. He got wet when the first floe split. I kept him moving till he couldn’t stand.”

George bent over the boy, rubbed snow against his cheeks, and forced him to swallow a little warm broth from the flask inside his parka. Sullivan moaned but did not wake.

“We got to get him ashore,” George said.

The canoe bumped beside the floe. Captain looked at its three openings.

“There are four of us.”

“Three’s all she’ll carry in this chop,” George said.

“We can lash one man on deck.”

“First wave rolls us. Then there’s none.”

“We’ll make two trips.”

The floe shuddered. A piece the size of a cabin broke from its western edge and vanished under another slab.

Barton looked from the canoe to the widening cracks.

“No time.”

George lifted Sullivan as if the young man were a child and lowered him into the middle opening. “Barton goes front. You take stern, Captain.”

“And you?”

“I stay.”

Captain stared at him.

George tightened the lashings around Sullivan’s body. “Quit gawpin’. Tide’ll turn.”

“You cannot remain here.”

“Looks like I can. Been practisin’ since we landed.”

“The floe is breaking.”

“That’s why I ain’t invitin’ you to tea.”

Barton seized George’s sleeve. “Listen, George—”

“Get in the boat.”

“We’ll draw lots.”

“No.” George’s voice cracked like a whip. “Kid can’t paddle. Captain knows where the shore mark is—or near enough. You got one hand froze and half the sense God issued you. I’m the only spare man.”

Captain stepped close. “I will stay.”

“You got to take your friend back.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It’s mine.” George thrust him toward the canoe. “Also, this here’s my country, an’ I know how to stand on it.”

For one instant the old bully showed through, immense and immovable. Yet there was no whisky in him now, no vanity and no hunger for applause. He had weighed four lives against three places and placed his own upon the lighter side.

Captain understood.

“I’ll return.”

“Sure,” George said. “Bring lunch.”

They launched.

Barton paddled clumsily with his bandaged hand. Captain drove from the rear. Sullivan lay unconscious between them, his head supported by spare clothing. As the canoe swung away, George stood upon the dwindling floe with his legs apart and the storm streaming from his shoulders.

He raised one arm.

Then the snow swallowed him.

The return became a punishment administered minute by minute. The tide had turned. Current and wind quarrelled over the canoe, hurling it sideways among the floes. Barton weakened. Twice his paddle slipped from his hands. Captain shouted at him, cursed him, begged him, and at last lashed the paddle to his wrist.

Sullivan ceased moaning. That silence was worse than any cry.

“Keep him awake,” Captain ordered.

Barton struck the boy’s cheeks.

“Kid! You owe me forty dollars.”

No answer.

“Fifty, you thief!”

Sullivan’s lips moved.

“What’d he say?” Captain called.

“Says it was thirty.”

“Then he’ll live.”

They paddled on.

A pressure ridge blocked their course. Cakes of ice had been crushed together into a wall twelve feet high, rising and falling with the sea. Captain found a narrow tongue of slush at its base. They drove into it. The canoe stopped dead.

A wave lifted the stern. Barton shouted. Captain leaped out waist-deep into icy water, heaved upon the frame, and felt the cold close around his body like iron jaws. Together they dragged the canoe over the slush while floes ground on either side. When he climbed back into his place, his clothing had become armour.

His legs soon lost sensation.

Time broke into fragments: a stroke, a breath, a wave, Barton’s bent back, the white face of the boy between them. The Arctic was no longer scenery, distance, or weather. It was an enormous enemy with a thousand hands. It struck their faces, pulled at the paddle, gnawed their fingers, shifted the road, and opened black mouths under the bow.

Then, through the snow, Captain saw fire.

It glowed and vanished, glowed again—a red eye low upon the shore.

“Nukka!” he shouted.

They drove toward it. The water shoaled. The keel struck ice, slid, struck again. Nukka came running through the surf with a line around her waist, two men from the settlement holding its end. She caught the bow. A wave broke over them all, but the men hauled, and the canoe scraped onto solid ice.

Captain tried to stand and fell.

Nukka knelt beside Sullivan, listened at his chest, and gave rapid orders. The settlement men wrapped the boy in skins and carried him toward the shelter. Barton followed, staggering.

Captain seized Nukka’s arm.

“George stayed.”

She became motionless.

“The boat would hold only three,” he continued. “He put us off. The floe was breaking. I promised to return.”

Nukka looked seaward. Darkness was already closing over the grey morning as another storm band swept down.

“You cannot go,” Barton said behind them.

Captain crawled toward the canoe.

Nukka took the front place.

“You are wet,” she said. “I steer.”

“No. You stay.”

Her eyes met his. “You find him alone?”

Captain stopped arguing.

They changed his outer parka, forced warm seal broth into him, rubbed his hands, and bound strips of fur around his feet. Twenty minutes later they launched again, Nukka in front and Captain behind.

The storm had thickened. The fire vanished before they had gone a hundred yards. Night descended without sunset, dropping like a black curtain over the ice. Nukka paddled by the feel of the current. She listened to the grinding pack and chose passages from their sound. Where Captain saw only darkness, she saw the pale loom of old ice, the dull shine of young ice, the trembling skin of water above a hidden current.

“Left,” she said.

They swerved as two floes crashed together where the bow had pointed.

“Wait.”

A ridge collapsed ahead, blocks tumbling with the thunder of artillery.

“Now.”

They shot through the opening.

Captain’s body had passed beyond pain. His arms rose and fell by command alone. At times he believed himself back in the Aurora, still fighting George, and each wave became the giant’s shoulder, each shock of ice his fist. Then Nukka’s voice cut through the dream.

“Call.”

“George!”

The wind took the name and tore it apart.

Again and again they called. No answer came.

They found the place by wreckage—a strip of canvas, a mitten, and fragments of dirty ice whirling in an eddy. The floe was gone.

Nukka made no sound.

Captain bowed his head. For one heartbeat despair entered him, cold and complete.

Then something knocked beneath the canoe.

A hand rose from the black water and closed upon the side.

Captain lunged. Big George’s face surfaced beside the boat, beard sheathed in ice, eyes nearly shut. He had lashed himself to a narrow slab with the canvas rope. The slab had overturned, but the rope had held him against it.

“Lunch,” George whispered.

Captain laughed—a cracked, savage sound—and caught his wrist. Nukka cut the rope. Together they hauled, balanced, dragged, and at last rolled the huge man across the deck. The canoe sank until water washed over its skin.

“Too heavy,” Captain gasped.

George opened one eye. “Always said so.”

He could not fit into the spare opening without upsetting them. Nukka shifted backward, George slid partly into the front place, and Captain crouched behind with his arms around the big man’s shoulders. The canoe rode low, burdened beyond safety, but it rode.

Nukka turned toward shore.

There followed a journey of which Captain afterward remembered only pieces: Nukka’s paddle rising like a dark wing; George’s frozen beard against his cheek; waves pouring over the deck; his own hands bailing with a sealskin cap; a roar as ice crushed ice behind them; and, very far away, a row of yellow lights trembling in the storm.

The settlement had built fires along the shore. Men waited with ropes. Women heated stones and water. When the canoe grounded, a dozen hands seized it.

Nukka stepped out, swayed once, and remained standing.

Captain was carried ashore with his arms still locked around George.

For three days the rescued men lay in the largest shelter of the village while the blizzard spent its rage over the coast. Sullivan lost two toes, Barton the tip of one finger, and George a portion of his left ear, which did not noticeably diminish its beauty. Captain’s feet recovered, though for weeks he walked as if each boot contained a private enemy.

George awakened on the second day.

Nukka sat beside him sewing a tear in his mitten. Captain lay across the shelter, propped against folded skins.

George considered the roof.

“This heaven?” he asked.

“No,” Captain said. “They do not serve whisky here.”

George turned his head. “Then I ain’t dead.”

“Narrowly.”

“Kid?”

“Alive.”

“Barton?”

“Complaining.”

“Good.”

Nukka bit off a thread.

George looked at her. His battered face altered. The change was small, for gratitude sat awkwardly upon such large features, but he reached out and enclosed her hand in his.

“You come after me,” he said.

She raised an eyebrow. “Someone must.”

“Reckon so.”

“You drink again, I leave you next time.”

George sighed. “Woman, you turn heroism into domestic oppression.”

A week later the weather cleared. The sky opened blue and hard above the white land. Smoke rose straight from the village shelters. Out on the sea, the pack lay quiet beneath the sun, glittering as innocently as a field of diamonds; but now and then a deep report rolled under it, reminding all men that the monster only slept.

The party returned to Port Desolation by sled.

Every inhabitant came out to meet them. Peabody shook Barton’s good hand, embraced Sullivan, slapped Captain’s shoulder, and finally stood before George with tears freezing in his whiskers.

“George,” he said, “that was the bravest—”

“Quit slobberin’,” George growled. “Fetch coffee.”

Inside the Aurora, the stove glowed red. The broken tables had been repaired, the ruined barrel removed, and a new chair placed against the wall. Captain stood beside the bar while the camp crowded around.

He raised a tin cup.

“I went onto the sea because Jim Barton was my friend,” he said. “Big George went because men were in danger. He remained upon a breaking floe so that three others might live. I have known brave men. I have known fools who resembled brave men until tested. George is not one of the latter.”

George shifted uneasily.

Captain continued. “And we came back for him because Nukka could read that sea in darkness as another person reads a road at noon. Without her, none of us would be here.”

The men looked toward Nukka. She accepted their attention without embarrassment and continued mending a dog harness.

Captain faced George.

“When I arrived, I judged you by your worst hour.”

“You hit me in it,” George observed.

“I did.”

“Three times before I got started.”

“Possibly four.”

George frowned. “Three.”

Captain smiled. “I was wrong about you. I ask your pardon.”

George examined him from boots to hair. Then his arm came forward. The great hand closed around Captain’s, not as an armoured claw this time, but firmly.

“Captain,” he said, “I judged you for a soft-handed, soap-smellin’, book-fed dude.”

“And now?”

“Now I know you’re a hard-handed, soap-smellin’, book-fed dude.”

The camp laughed.

George’s grip tightened. “Also, you came back.”

“I said I would.”

“Most men say.”

They stood silent for a moment, and in that silence passed the things which men of their kind did not enjoy naming: gratitude, shame, loyalty, and the knowledge of a debt too large for words.

Then George glanced around the repaired saloon.

“Seems to me,” he said, “we got some unfinished business.”

Captain removed his coat.

“I believe we had reached the stove.”

Nukka looked up from the harness.

“George.”

The single word crossed the room softly.

Big George sat down.

Captain put on his coat again.

Thus peace returned to Port Desolation—not by law, custom, or brass buttons, but through the authority of a small woman with a needle. And from that day forward, whenever George drank, Captain drank coffee beside him; whenever Captain travelled, George inspected his dogs; and whenever either man spoke of the other, he used the word “partner.”

Beyond the camp the Arctic wind continued to sharpen its knives, the starving sea worried at its chains, and the white hills watched without kindness. The North had not been conquered. It never is. It had merely tested four men and one woman, taken a little blood, a little flesh, and much pride, and permitted them—for reasons of its own—to come home.

Skeleton Lake lies beyond the last lumber road, in a region of black spruce, granite ridges, and innumerable waters, where, in those days, the maps were less trustworthy than the memory of an Indian. How the lake acquired its lugubrious name I never learned. Hank Barlow maintained that a surveyor had once found human bones upon the north shore; Silver Fizz, our cook and general factotum, declared that it was so called because its bays ran out from the main body like ribs from a spine. Whatever the origin, the name had an unpleasant fitness which none of us remarked until afterwards.

We had been in camp for eleven days when Rushton came.

Our party consisted of Professor Willoughby and his wife; Morris, an American sportsman; myself; and the two guides, Hank and Silver Fizz. The latter’s proper name was Silas Fitzgibbon, but in the camps no man is long permitted the dignity of the name his mother gave him. We occupied an old hunting lodge built on a low rise above the eastern shore. Behind it the forest stood impenetrably dark; before it the lake stretched six miles westward, its islands lying along the pale autumn water like sleeping animals.

The weather had been fine, with frosts at night and still, bright days. On the afternoon of the twenty-third of September the wind changed. Clouds gathered above the western ridge, and the lake, hitherto blue and mild, became iron-grey, with short waves that slapped resentfully among the stones. We had returned early from hunting and were seated about the cedar fire when Hank, who had gone down for water, called to us.

A man was coming out of the woods.

He did not walk so much as fall forward from tree to tree. At first I took him for a wounded hunter. His clothes hung in ribbons; one trouser-leg was torn away below the knee, and his face, beneath a crust of dirt and dried blood, had the colour of ashes. He saw Hank, stopped, and opened his mouth without speaking. Then, with a strange little motion as though he wished to ward off something behind him, he collapsed upon the path.

We carried him into the lodge. Morris got off his boots, Mrs. Willoughby heated broth, and Silver Fizz, whose gruffness concealed a woman’s capacity for tenderness, washed the man’s blistered feet with warm water. He was not unconscious, though he seemed scarcely aware of our presence. His eyes moved continually from face to face, never resting, and twice he raised himself to look towards the door.

“Shut it,” he whispered. “For God’s sake, shut it!”

The door was already shut.

Hank recognised him as Edward Rushton, a mining agent who had entered the country with another man a fortnight before. They had passed Crow’s Landing in a green canoe, bound for the upper lakes. Rushton had been cheerful then, Hank said afterwards, and his companion—Jake Marden—was a broad, quiet fellow, reputed to be the better woodsman of the two.

At the mention of Jake’s name Rushton began to tremble.

“He’s gone,” he said. “Jake’s drowned. There were only the two of us, and he’s drowned.”

No one questioned him. That should be understood. A man who has crawled for two days through cedar swamp and windfall is not examined like a prisoner at the bar. We fed him a few spoonfuls of broth, wrapped him in blankets, and waited for the exhaustion to have its way. Yet he would not rest. Before he had swallowed half the broth he pushed the bowl aside and insisted upon telling us what had occurred.

“It was in the west reach,” he began—“beyond the two islands, where the water is deep. You know the place? You must know it. A black cliff on the north and one small island, all birch, lying half a mile off. We were crossing there when the squall took us. It came from nowhere. One instant calm—the next, God Almighty!—the canoe was over.”

His voice rose upon the final words. Morris laid a hand on his shoulder.

“Easy, man. Tell it to us later.”

“No, now! I want you to understand. We did everything that men could do. We were not careless. Jake said we could cross. It was his decision as much as mine—more his, if the truth were known.”

He looked round our circle as though one of us had accused him.

The canoe, he told us, had not sunk, but floated bottom upwards. Both men caught hold of it. Their packs and rifles went down at once. The water was bitterly cold, and the wind drove them farther from the mainland. For hours—they had no means of measuring time—they clung to the smooth, rolling hull. Jake possessed a knife, and with this they cut two holes through the thin planking, hoping to obtain a safer hold.

“We put our arms through,” Rushton said, demonstrating with both hands. “Up to the elbows. Otherwise the waves would have torn us off. Wouldn’t you have done the same? Any man would. There was no wickedness in that.”

The phrase was so singular that Mrs. Willoughby glanced quickly at her husband. The professor, who was a man of exact thought and very few words, continued filling his pipe.

Towards evening, according to Rushton, the wind diminished. The island lay perhaps three hundred yards away. Jake proposed that they abandon the canoe and swim for it, but Rushton refused. He believed—reasonably enough—that their cramped limbs would fail before they had gone fifty yards. A quarrel followed. Jake abused him, calling him a coward, and at last tore himself free.

“I warned him,” Rushton cried. “I begged him not to go. I said, ‘Jake, stay here! Stay by the canoe!’ But he wouldn’t heed me. He struck out for the island, and after a little while he sank. That was all. One moment I saw his head, and the next there was only water. He never made a sound.”

Rushton covered his face. For half a minute we heard nothing but the crackling cedar and the wind fingering the eaves.

The remainder of his account was less distinct. He had drifted till after dark and had somehow contrived to work the overturned canoe towards a strip of reeds. There he found bottom, dragged himself ashore, and lay insensible. In the morning he righted the canoe, though it was half full of water and badly damaged, and paddled with a piece of split cedar to the southern shore. He had searched for Jake, calling from every point and examining the island from a distance; but he had seen nothing. Then, believing that our camp was nearer than any settlement, he abandoned the canoe and struck eastward on foot.

For two days he had eaten only berries. He had slept beneath a fallen tree, while wolves—or what he supposed to be wolves—moved about him in the darkness. Once he had lost himself in a tamarack bog and remained waist-deep for an hour, expecting every moment to sink beyond recovery. His recital was confused, but not impossible. Indeed, almost every separate circumstance bore the appearance of truth. It was the whole that failed to convince.

I have often considered why this was so. We had then no evidence against the man; and a more charitable company might have attributed his manner to privation, grief, and the nervous shock of his companion’s death. Yet I know that, before he finished, each of us felt the same cold repugnance. His story was true-false: woven of actual suffering, accurate places, and probable dangers, but with a dark thread running beneath which the eye perceived without being able to follow.

Most dreadful of all was his eagerness for our approval.

“What would you have done?” he demanded of Hank. “Suppose a man will not listen—suppose he leaves the only thing that can save him—is his death your fault? Answer me!”

Hank shifted a stick with his boot.

“Hard tellin’ what a man’ll do in cold water.”

“But I warned him!”

“So ye said.”

“I did warn him. God heard me. He can’t say I didn’t, wherever he is.”

“Better drink your broth,” said Silver Fizz. “Ye ain’t got strength enough fer all this talk.”

Rushton seized the bowl, swallowed ravenously, and immediately vomited upon the floor. He began to apologise with extravagant humility, but Mrs. Willoughby stopped him and cleaned the boards herself. The simple kindness appeared to distress him more than reproach would have done. Tears came into his eyes. He caught her wrist.

“You believe me, madam?”

“I believe you are ill,” she replied, disengaging herself.

Night had now closed about the lodge. The windows were black mirrors in which our lamp and moving figures hung unsupported, and beyond them the forest seemed to press close, listening. Rushton would not be left alone. We made up a bed for him near the fire, but when Morris and I rose to carry our blankets into the adjoining room, he struggled upright.

“Don’t go! There are eyes in the trees. I saw them last night—eyes looking between the branches. And something followed me along the shore. It kept pace when I ran and stopped when I stopped.”

“A fox, p’raps,” Silver Fizz said.

“No fox walks on two feet.”

He stared at the door. His face had become wet with perspiration.

“It came out of the water,” he whispered. “I heard it crawling over the stones.”

No one answered that.

Hank replenished the fire, and we remained together. To divert Rushton, Morris told a prodigious and wholly irrelevant tale concerning a moose which had chased him into a railway cutting in Maine. Silver Fizz followed with reminiscences of a winter at Lac des Loups, while Hank disputed every important detail. Their intention was merciful. The less Rushton spoke, the less likely he was to say what could never be withdrawn. But silence tortured him. Again and again he broke into their talk.

“There was no cry,” he would say. “That proves it was quick.”

Or: “A man can’t be blamed for holding to safety.”

Or, more horribly: “If he had reached the island, I should have seen him in the morning.”

At length Professor Willoughby removed his pipe.

“You said you heard him call your name,” he observed.

Rushton turned slowly.

The professor’s tone had been quiet, almost absent-minded; but the effect was as if a rifle had cracked in the room.

“I said no such thing.”

“While Mrs. Willoughby was getting the broth, you said, ‘I can hear him yet, calling me from the water.’”

“No. You misunderstood. I meant—I dreamed it afterwards. I imagined him calling. A man hears many things when he is exhausted.”

“Certainly,” said the professor.

He put the pipe back between his teeth and asked no more. Yet from that instant the man’s pretence—for pretence we knew it to be—had a visible fracture in it. His glance went furtively from the professor to the rest of us. He repeated twice that he had heard no cry, and then, with a feverish laugh, remarked that perhaps Jake had cried after all; the waves made such a noise, and memory was treacherous. Was it not natural that memory should fail after such an experience? Did we expect a drowning man to keep notes?

We broke up the circle soon afterwards. Mrs. Willoughby went into the smaller room, and the professor followed. Morris took the bunk by the stove. Hank and Silver Fizz stretched themselves near the door, while I lay in the loft overhead. The wind had fallen entirely. A little rain tapped upon the roof, and through a crack between the logs I could see one star suspended above the lake.

For perhaps two hours there was no sound except the breathing of the sleepers. Then Rushton began to talk.

At first the words were indistinguishable—a muttering burden carried beneath the crackle of the fire. Presently his voice became louder.

“Let go,” he said. “Let go, damn you! You’ll roll us both in.”

There followed a silence, then a cry so violent that I struck my head against the rafters in rising.

“Keep away from me! I didn’t mean to hit you again!”

Below, someone moved. I heard Hank whisper, “Lie still.”

Rushton groaned. His feet beat upon the floor, and he began pleading with someone in a voice that scarcely resembled his waking speech.

“You forced me. You said you’d tell. Jake—Jake, take your hands off! Don’t look up like that. The water’ll hide it. Deep water—deep—”

Silver Fizz stirred the embers noisily, and Rushton awoke.

“Who spoke?” he demanded.

“Nobody,” Hank replied.

“I heard somebody.”

“Jest me puttin’ wood on.”

Rushton sat up and peered around. The renewed fire painted one side of his face crimson and left the other in shadow.

“I had a dream,” he said. “Did I talk?”

“Most folks do, now an’ then.”

“What did I say?”

“Somethin’ ’bout yer ma,” Silver Fizz answered promptly.

Rushton stared at him. Then he laughed—a dry, obedient little laugh—and lay down again.

None of us slept thereafter, though in the morning we maintained the fiction that we had.

At first light Hank and I went in search of the canoe. Rushton had described the place where he abandoned it: a gravel point three miles west of camp, marked by a blasted pine. We found it there, drawn well above the water and partially concealed under alders. It was a stout, green-painted craft, scratched and bruised but by no means wrecked. One thwart had been split, and there were two jagged apertures in the bottom.

Hank turned the canoe over. He examined the holes without touching them; then he knelt and passed two fingers through the larger.

“Queer,” he said.

They were perhaps three inches across.

Even had the planking yielded—which it would not—a man could not have thrust his hand through either opening, much less his arm to the elbow. Moreover, the splinters showed outward beneath the hull and inward above. The cuts had been made from inside the canoe.

Hank looked at me, and I at him.

Those holes had been made afterwards.

We returned by another path, following the edge of the lake. The morning was extraordinarily beautiful. Mist lay in the bays like milk, and every birch upon the islands burned yellow in the early sun. A kingfisher flew before us, uttering its harsh cry; once a trout broke the polished surface, and the widening rings remained long after the fish had vanished. That peace, beside what we now believed, seemed not merely indifferent but accusatory. The whole wilderness was clean. Only man had brought a stain into it.

“What’ll we do?” I asked.

“Find Jake,” Hank replied.

Rushton was eating when we reached camp. He looked at our empty hands and then towards the shore.

“You found it?”

“Ayuh.”

“You saw the holes?”

“Ayuh.”

“I told you. We cut them to hold on.”

Hank helped himself to coffee.

“Too bad ye lost the knife.”

Rushton’s spoon struck the plate. “The knife?”

“Jake’s knife. Must ha’ gone down with him.”

“Yes. Of course. It was tied to his belt.”

“You said he had it in his hand when he swam away.”

Rushton blinked. “Did I? Well, he may have put it back. I don’t remember every movement. Why should I?”

“No reason,” Hank said.

Again the guides attempted to turn the conversation, but Rushton pursued it with that fatal persistence which had become more exhausting than his silence could have been.

“You examined the canoe carefully, I suppose? You found nothing else? No blood?”

Nobody had mentioned blood.

Morris got abruptly to his feet and walked outside. Mrs. Willoughby set down her cup. Her face was composed, but very pale.

“I think,” she said to her husband, “that I should like to leave this morning.”

It was impossible to depart immediately; our boats were at the northern end of the lake with the bulk of our supplies. The professor therefore proposed that he and Morris fetch them while Hank and Silver Fizz went to search the west reach. I was to remain at camp with Mrs. Willoughby and Rushton. At this arrangement Rushton displayed both relief and alarm.

“There’s no use searching,” he said. “The current runs north. The body may be miles away.”

“There ain’t much current in a lake,” Silver Fizz observed.

“The wind, then. You waste your time.”

“P’raps,” said Hank. “But a man don’t leave his pard out thar if he can fetch him in.”

Rushton’s lips tightened. “He was my partner, not yours.”

For the first time Hank looked directly at him. There was no menace in the guide’s weathered face; only a grave and almost sorrowful attention.

“Then ye’ll want him found,” he said.

The search party left before nine. From the shore I watched Hank and Silver Fizz take the green canoe—temporarily repaired with canvas and pitch—while the professor and Morris paddled our remaining craft northward. The lake had become so calm that the dipping blades sounded long after the men themselves were diminished to dark points.

Rushton would not enter the lodge. He sat beside the fire, though the day was warm, and fed it branch after branch until the flames leapt high and wastefully. Mrs. Willoughby occupied herself with packing. I cleaned a rifle that did not require cleaning.

For an hour Rushton said nothing. Then he asked whether Hank knew the islands well.

“Better than most men.”

“Reeds grow thick on the south side. A body might lie there unseen for weeks.”

“Possibly.”

“Or sink in deep water and never rise.”

“Possibly.”

He rubbed his hands across his knees. “If they find Jake, you must remember what I told you. There may be marks upon him. The canoe struck us when it rolled. He was struck by it.”

This was new.

“Where?”

“How should I know? The head, perhaps. I saw blood in the water—or I thought I did. The sunset was red.”

“It was afternoon when the squall came.”

“Afternoon, sunset—what difference does an hour make? You sit there judging words while a man has died!”

He sprang up. Mrs. Willoughby appeared in the doorway, and at sight of her his anger collapsed.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I am not myself. You understand, madam? I loved Jake like a brother. We quarrelled sometimes, but brothers quarrel. He had a temper. He threatened me that very day. He said—”

He stopped.

“What did he say?” I asked.

“Nothing. It had no bearing.”

He began to pace between the fire and the trees. That movement remains clear in my recollection: three steps in sunlight, three in shadow, his body passing again and again across the border between them. Presently he halted and listened.

“Do you hear splashing?”

There was no sound upon the lake.

“It’s only the leaves,” Mrs. Willoughby said.

“No. Something is wading in the reeds.”

He walked to the shore and stared westward. The haze had lifted. The island he had described was visible far off—a low yellow strip set in blue water.

“He didn’t reach it,” Rushton said. “He couldn’t have reached it.”

Neither of us replied.

Towards noon a flock of crows rose above the western trees. Rushton watched them circle, and I saw his mouth go slack. He came back to the fire, then returned to the shore; he entered the lodge, came out again, drank water, and asked the time four times within twenty minutes.

At half-past one we saw the canoe.

It approached from the west, moving slowly. Hank sat in the stern and Silver Fizz in the bow. Between them lay something long, wrapped in a blanket.

Rushton saw it before I did. He uttered no cry, but stepped backwards until he came against the woodpile. His hands moved behind him, clawing at the logs.

“They found something,” Mrs. Willoughby said.

“It may not be him.”

The canoe grated upon the beach. Hank climbed out first, holding the bow steady. Silver Fizz did not look towards us.

“Whar was it?” I asked.

“South side o’ the island,” Hank said. “Shallow water. Caught under a birch root.”

Rushton’s eyes were fixed upon the blanket.

“He nearly reached it, then,” he whispered. “You see? He swam, just as I told you.”

Hank and Silver Fizz lifted the burden into a canvas hammock. Water dripped steadily from it, darkening the stones. They carried it a little distance from the lodge and set it down beneath a cedar.

“Don’t uncover him,” Rushton said. “There is no need. We all know who it is.”

Hank bent and drew back the blanket from the face.

Jake Marden was a large man, larger in death than I had imagined him from Rushton’s story. His skin had the dreadful pallor of one who has lain beneath cold water, and weeds clung about his hair. The features were swollen but recognisable. His eyes were closed.

Rushton took one step nearer.

“He drowned,” he said. “You can all see that he drowned.”

No one answered.

Silver Fizz moved behind the hammock. With immense gentleness he turned the body slightly upon its side. Then we saw the back of the head.

The skull had been crushed by a prodigious blow from some heavy, narrow instrument—an axe-head, as Hank afterwards judged. There were two wounds, the second driven into the first.

For a while the drops falling from the hammock were the only sound.

Rushton looked from the wound to Hank, from Hank to me, and last of all towards the silent forest. What he saw there I cannot say. His face altered as though someone had approached him out of the trees.

We had thought the body heavy when the men brought it ashore.

What lay among us then was heavier.

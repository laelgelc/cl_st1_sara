# QUEBEC AND THE GREAT WAR

Before you condemn a people, you should know them.

You should have eaten their bread, heard their songs, travelled their roads, and slept beneath their roofs. Better still, you should have gone with them into the wilderness, where speech is stripped of ceremony and a man’s heart comes plainly into view beside the camp-fire.

It is easy to judge Quebec from a newspaper paragraph. A riot breaks out in a city street; a politician makes a reckless speech; a recruiting meeting fails; and immediately someone cries, “Cowards! Disloyal! Let them separate from Canada if they will not defend her!”

No! I cannot accept that verdict.

For more than twenty years I have loved French Canada—not with the enthusiasm of the tourist who sees a cathedral, buys a wooden bowl, and departs upon the evening train, but with the affection born of friendship. I have followed her rushing rivers, crossed her sparkling lakes, climbed her friendly mountains, and watched the northern twilight lingering like a benediction over her dark forests. Her canoemen have carried me through rapids; her guides have shared with me the last crust in a wet camp; her farmers have welcomed me to whitewashed kitchens where children rose politely when the stranger entered.

Memories are not arguments, perhaps. Yet memories would ask to be heard.

I remember men who could paddle from dawn to darkness, laugh at cold rain, face a wounded moose in the alder thickets, and kneel without shame when the little chapel bell sounded across the fields. Are these men cowards? I have known them to be mistaken, uninformed, suspicious of distant causes and foreign phrases. But disloyal by nature? No.

Take these recollections for what they are worth. I offer them as witnesses—not learned witnesses, not statesmen or professors, but men with axes, paddles, rifles, rosaries, and honest hands.

## I. TWO FLAGS  
### Lake Saint John Country, October 1894

Jean Morel was broad-shouldered, red-bearded, and deliberate. He wasted neither words nor matches. He could read the movement of a trout beneath brown water, smell rain before the clouds gathered, and mend a broken canoe-rib with a strip of cedar and a piece of copper wire.

For ten days he had guided me through the lake country north of Quebec. Autumn had taken possession of the forest. The birches shone pale gold; the maples burned crimson along the slopes; tamaracks stood like yellow candles among the black spruce. At evening the lakes held every colour twice, once in the sky and once in the still water, until Jean’s paddle broke the vision into widening circles.

Our last camp was pitched on a low point covered with balsam. A cold wind moved down from the north, and the fire snapped cheerfully between two stones. Jean had baked bannock in the frying-pan, and afterward we smoked in contented silence.

For some reason—perhaps because a newspaper, three weeks old, had wrapped the bacon—I began speaking of Europe. There had been one of those quarrels which statesmen manufacture from pride, suspicion, and ink. France and England, though then at peace, still looked at each other across old memories.

“Jean,” I said, “suppose France and Britain should go to war. What would you do?”

He took his pipe from his mouth and regarded me steadily.

“Why you ask me that, M’sieu’?”

“Because you are French.”

“French-Canadian,” he corrected.

“Very well. Your fathers came from France, but you live under the British flag. If the two flags were enemies, which would you follow?”

He did not answer at once. The firelight moved over his strong face. Beyond him the lake whispered against the stones, a peaceful sound in a peaceful land.

“At the church,” he said slowly, “we pray for the Queen.”

“Yes.”

“And my grandfather, he say the English law is good law. A man keep his farm. He keep his religion. Nobody make him speak English to the bon Dieu.”

“That is true.”

“But France—” He paused, searching for words. “France is like the old mother. We do not see her. We do not know her now. But we have the songs. My mother sing them. We have the names, the saints, the stories. When the people speak of France, something move here.”

He laid one rough hand upon his breast.

“Then you would fight for France?”

He shook his head impatiently.

“I am Canadian. If war come, my country call me under the British flag. My body, he march there.”

“And your heart?”

Again the hand touched his breast.

“My heart—he march with France.”

There it was: the whole problem in a woodsman’s sentence. Political allegiance upon one side, inherited affection upon the other; duty pointing westward, memory eastward. I thought then it was a tragic division.

Years passed. Kings died, ministries changed, and the world called itself civilized. Then the storm burst. Germany struck through Belgium, France stood in peril, and Britain drew the sword beside her.

I remembered Jean Morel.

Somewhere, if he still lived, that red-bearded guide must have heard the chapel bell ring news of war across the Laurentian fields. At last his two processions had joined. His body could march under Britain’s flag while his heart marched openly with France. Duty and affection went down the same road.

Surely such a man could understand the call. Surely thousands like him could understand it—if only the truth reached them plainly, not smothered beneath party cries and the dust raised by clever tongues.

## II. THE MAN IN THE BLUE COAT  
### Oxford, March 1917

In the spring of 1917 I visited a hospital at Oxford. It occupied a group of buildings never designed for suffering. Outside, ancient towers rose tranquilly above lawns touched with the first green of the year. Bells measured the hours as they had measured them for centuries. Within were rows of narrow beds, the odour of antiseptics, the soft steps of nurses, and the terrible patience of broken youth.

There were Englishmen from the Somme, Australians from Gallipoli, Canadians from Ypres, and men whose bodies carried fragments of places that would remain forever obscure to history. Each bed seemed a little frontier between hope and darkness.

A nurse stopped me beside a young fellow in a faded blue dressing-gown.

“He is from Quebec,” she said. “Perhaps you can talk to him.”

His face was pale beneath a shock of dark hair. One arm lay outside the blanket, strong and brown; the other was hidden beneath a rigid framework of bandages. His name was Étienne Gagnon, and his home was near the upper Saint-Maurice.

“You are a lumberman?” I asked.

He smiled.

“Yes, M’sieu’. In winter I cut. In spring I drive the logs. Sometimes in summer I work for the club—carry canoe, make camp, show gentlemen where the trout do not bite.”

“That last must require great skill.”

“Ah, yes! The gentleman, he must believe always the big trout is in the next pool.”

The nurse laughed and moved away.

“How did you come here, Étienne?”

“By train, then by ship.”

“I mean, why did you enlist?”

His expression suggested that the question was unnecessarily complicated.

“Britain was fighting for France.”

“That was enough?”

“Certainly. The Germans were in France. They burn houses, break churches, push the people away. My father say, ‘If I am young, I go.’ He is not young. So I go.”

“Did your priest approve?”

“Our old curé say nothing against. He bless me. My mother cry much; afterward she is proud. She send me a scapular, but the shell does not respect it very well.”

He glanced toward the imprisoned arm.

“What happened?”

“At Courcelette. We go over in the morning. There is smoke, noise—everything mixed. My captain fall. Then a shell come close, too close. I wake in a place under the ground. After that, many trains, many doctors, much soup.”

“Will the arm recover?”

The humour faded, though not the courage.

“The doctor say I keep it. That is good. But the elbow will not bend much, perhaps. The fingers—two of them do not obey.”

“And you were right-handed?”

“Yes.”

I looked at the visible arm. The wrist was thin, but the muscles still told of axe and paddle.

“What will you do when you go home?”

He considered.

“To cut timber, maybe no. A man cannot swing axe with one arm asleep. To drive logs, also difficult. The river does not wait when you say, ‘Please, my bad hand!’”

He smiled again, but his eyes had turned toward the window.

“Do you regret going?”

His head came round quickly.

“No, M’sieu’!”

The answer rang out so sharply that a soldier in the next bed stirred.

Étienne lowered his voice. “I regret only that I cannot go back. France is not free yet. The war is not finish.”

“But you have paid heavily.”

“All pay something. Some pay everything.”

“What will become of you?”

“My brother write that perhaps I make cook in lumber camp. The cook use two hands, yes, but he have helpers. I can order them with one.” He lifted his sound hand like a commander directing an army. “Also I know beans. Nobody can defeat a man who knows beans.”

We both laughed. Then he said quietly:

“A man does not need two good arms to be glad he has done his duty.”

That was all. No speech, no theory of empire, no hatred. He had seen France attacked; Britain had defended her; Canada had called; and he had gone. The reasoning was as direct as a wood road.

When I left the ward, the evening bells were sounding over Oxford. Their music seemed to mingle with another sound in my mind—the stroke of axes in the snowy forests of Quebec.

Tell me that province has given too few men, if you must. Tell me recruiting was mishandled, that suspicion was allowed to grow, that the appeal came in words unfamiliar to the habitant. But do not tell me there was no courage in Étienne Gagnon.

I have seen his face.

## III. ISIDE LEARNS THE SIZE OF HIS HOUSE  
### Sainte Marguerite River, September 1917

That autumn I went to the Sainte Marguerite for salmon. The river ran clear and cold between wooded hills, now grave with the first touch of frost. Here and there a maple flung out its scarlet banner. The spruces stood dark and faithful along the banks, and above them the sky widened into that luminous northern blue which seems to promise both peace and courage.

My bowman was Iside Lavoie, a wiry little man with quick black eyes. He could slip a canoe through broken water as neatly as a needle through cloth. He knew every stone in the river and had a name for each pool—some names belonging to saints, others to old accidents, and a few not suitable for Sunday.

One afternoon we rested below a rapid. The canoe lay on the shingle, and Iside crouched by a small fire, boiling tea black enough to stain a paddle. The war had been much in my thoughts.

“Iside,” I asked, “how old are you?”

“Twenty-seven, M’sieu’.”

“Married?”

“Yes. Three children. The smallest is very loud.”

“And you are strong?”

He looked at me suspiciously. “Strong enough for the canoe.”

“Why are you not in the army?”

He stirred the tea, though it needed no stirring.

“My cousin Alcide is gone.”

“I asked about you.”

“My wife need me.”

“So did the wives of other men.”

He frowned. “The curé say a man fight when his own country is attack. If somebody come to burn our church, take our farm, hurt our women—then I fight. But this war is far away. It belongs to kings.”

“Does it?”

“That is what we hear.”

“Who tells you?”

“People. The newspaper. Men in the village.”

“What men?”

He waved toward the forest, as if politicians were hidden among the spruces. “Men who know.”

“That is a dangerous tribe, Iside.”

He grinned, but said nothing.

“Suppose,” I continued, “a man begins to set fire to a house at the other end of your village. Do you wait until the flames reach your own roof?”

“If there are people inside, I help.”

“Even if it is not your house?”

“Certainly.”

“Why?”

“Because it is Christian.”

“Good. Suppose a thief attacks your neighbour on the road. Do you wait until he has finished and comes for you?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because then he is stronger. Also my neighbour may be my friend.”

“Exactly. Belgium was a little neighbour attacked upon the road. France was a house set on fire. Britain went to help. Canada belongs to the same family and enjoys the liberty protected by that family. Yet you tell me the affair is none of ours.”

Iside poured tea into two tin cups. His face was thoughtful.

“But Canada is not attack.”

“Not yet, perhaps. Do you imagine the ocean is a wall which no army, no fleet, no ambition can cross? Do you think liberty lives without defenders? If Germany conquered France and broke Britain, how long would Canada remain free to govern herself, keep her language, protect her church, and raise her children in peace?”

He glanced instinctively upstream. Beyond a bend lay the valley where his home stood.

“They would come here?”

“Perhaps not tomorrow in grey uniforms. Tyranny does not always arrive at the front door with a drum. First it destroys the friends who might help you. Then it controls the seas, the trade, the laws, the future. Your house, Iside, is larger than the cabin where your children sleep.”

He looked puzzled.

“Larger?”

“Your house is Canada. Its road reaches to the ocean. Its safety is joined to the safety of free nations. When France is struck, something in your house is struck. When Britain stands between you and danger, you owe her more than a prayer.”

The river spoke beside us, running with that composed strength which comes from many hidden springs. Iside watched it for a while.

“Our curé is a good man,” he said.

“I do not doubt it.”

“He care for the poor. When my little girl is sick, he come in snow.”

“Then speak to him respectfully.”

“You think he does not understand?”

“I think he may not have been told the whole truth. Many good men see only the field before their door. Show them the road beyond it.”

He sipped the tea and made a face—not because it was bitter, for he preferred it so, but because thought had become uncomfortable.

“The politicians say the English want to make us fight for England.”

“Some politicians would say the moon was made of cheese if they owned the cheese shop. Forget them. Ask yourself what England is fighting for now. Ask what happened to Belgium. Ask why Étienne Gagnon, a lumberman from the Saint-Maurice, lies with a shattered arm in an Oxford hospital.”

I told him about Étienne. Iside listened without interruption.

“At Courcelette?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“My wife’s cousin die there.”

“And was he defending nothing?”

The little bowman leaned forward, elbows upon knees.

“No. Not nothing.”

“Was he less a Canadian because he died in France?”

“No.”

“Was he a fool?”

Iside’s eyes flashed. “No, M’sieu’! He was brave.”

“Then perhaps bravery sees a danger before it reaches the church door.”

For a long minute there was only the rush of water over stones.

At last he said, “I will talk to the curé.”

“Do that.”

“And to Baptiste, who has seven sons.”

“Talk first to the sons. Baptiste may hide them in the hay.”

Iside laughed. “He hide nothing. His wife find it.”

Then his face grew serious again.

“I cannot promise I go. There is my wife, the children, the old mother. But I promise this: I tell what you say. The house is larger. The fire at the other end may come to us.”

“That is enough for one afternoon.”

We launched the canoe. Iside took his place in the bow, and the paddle entered the water without a splash. Above us the river narrowed between high banks, free and shining, obedient to its ancient channel yet always moving toward a wider world.

You have got to give men time, and light. A truth shouted in a strange language often sounds like an insult. The same truth spoken beside their own river may sound like duty.

## IV. THE FROTH AND THE CURRENT  
### Quebec City, Easter 1918

Then came the disturbance in Quebec City.

The reports travelled quickly: crowds in the streets, windows broken, offices attacked, soldiers summoned, shots fired, men killed. To many Canadians it appeared that the mask had fallen at last.

“There!” they said. “What more proof do you need? Quebec will not obey the law. Quebec is false to Canada.”

The accusation was understandable. It was also unjust.

A riot is noisy; loyalty commonly works in silence. One broken window makes more news than a thousand farms where men rise before daylight, pay their taxes, pray for sons overseas, and obey laws they do not altogether like. A crowd beneath electric lamps is easily photographed. Character in a distant parish is not.

Let us be honest. Conscription was bitterly disliked in much of Quebec. Recruiting had failed there in painful measure. Certain newspapers had fed distrust; certain speakers had whispered that the war was merely an English quarrel; certain political adventurers had discovered that fear, when stirred vigorously, brings votes to the surface.

But who made the riot?

Was it Jean Morel, weighing old France and present duty beside a northern fire? Was it Étienne Gagnon, offering his good arm and receiving a ruined one in return? Was it Iside Lavoie, slowly learning that the safety of his children might depend upon battles beyond the sea?

No.

The disturbance was the work of a mixed and inflammable crowd—boys hunting excitement, idlers hunting plunder, agitators hunting influence, and hired roughs hunting anything that could be smashed. Honest anxiety was present, no doubt; so was anger. But opportunists supplied the matches.

Such outbreaks froth up and go down like ginger beer. For an hour the bottle seems all foam. Presently the foam sinks, and one discovers how little substance it contained.

The riot collapsed. The law remained. Registration continued. The draft proceeded. Men obeyed who had declared they never would. Others volunteered rather than wait. Quebec did not secede, burn her churches, renounce Canada, or invite a foreign flag above her citadel.

Indeed, when a few zealots proposed a separate state, their grand scheme rose like a soap bubble, glittered with every colour of rhetoric, and burst at the touch of practical sense. The farmer wanted better roads, a fair price for his crop, schools for his children, and peace for his parish. He did not want a paper republic manufactured in a back room by gentlemen who had never guided a canoe through a rapid or brought hay in before a storm.

Separation sounds magnificent in a speech. It looks less attractive when one asks who will pay the railway debt.

More important still, the moral meaning of the war was becoming clearer. The Church, slow in some quarters and cautious in others, began to speak with a stronger voice. Priests who once had treated the conflict as a distant quarrel now saw the burned sanctuaries of Belgium, the ravaged homes of France, and the claim of militarism to master the conscience of mankind. Bishops called upon their people to recognize that justice also has a flag, and that Christianity cannot remain neutral when power tramples the weak.

It is the conduct that shows the Christian.

A man may cross himself at every roadside shrine, but if he will not lift a hand while his neighbour is robbed, his devotion has become an ornament. Another man may stumble over the words of doctrine, yet if he gives his strength to defend the oppressed, something of the true faith burns within him.

This lesson reached Quebec—not everywhere at once, not in the same form, and not without resistance. Truth travels slowly along country roads. But it travels.

The habitant began to understand that Britain had not entered the struggle merely to enlarge an empire; that France was not fighting for glory; that Canada’s sacrifice was not demanded for the amusement of kings. Behind the smoke and wire stood a plain question: Should nations live by law or by force? Should a treaty bind the strong as well as the weak? Should a small people be allowed its home, its language, and its worship—or should these things endure only until some armed master desired otherwise?

Put the matter that way to French Canada and you touch her own history. She knows what language means. She knows what religion means. She knows the value of laws which protect a minority. Ask her to die for an abstraction called imperial policy, and she may turn away. Ask her to defend the right of a people to remain itself, and she begins to listen.

Some will say, “You excuse too much. Other provinces understood sooner.”

Perhaps. Other provinces heard the appeal in their own political vocabulary. Their newspapers explained the cause; their schools had taught them the history behind it; their public men spoke in familiar terms. Quebec was too often addressed by scolding. When she hesitated, accusation replaced persuasion. Men told her she was disloyal before they had taken the trouble to tell her clearly what loyalty required.

There is no virtue in ignorance. But neither is there wisdom in insulting the ignorant.

The rural French Canadian is conservative in the deepest meaning of that word. He clings to family, field, parish, language, and inherited custom. He mistrusts sudden commands from distant offices. He asks, “Will this injure my children? Will it take away what my fathers preserved?” These instincts may narrow his horizon, but they do not make him a traitor. They are the roots from which loyalty grows, once he understands that the larger country shelters the smaller home.

He is loyal by nature—to what he knows.

The task is to enlarge his knowledge without mocking his affections. Tell him that Canada asks him to cease being French, and he will resist. Tell him that Canada needs the courage of its French sons precisely because their heritage is precious, and he will stand straighter. Show him France in danger, Britain defending the law, Belgium crucified for keeping faith, and Canada summoned to protect the liberty under which all her peoples live—and the road opens before him.

I do not pretend every Jean became a soldier, every Étienne a hero, every Iside a convert. Human beings are not symbols marching neatly in a parade. There were cowards in Quebec, as there were cowards in Ontario, Scotland, Australia, and every land that sent brave men to battle. There were selfish farmers, dishonest contractors, noisy demagogues, timid priests, and young men who preferred safety.

But cowardice is personal, not provincial. Disloyalty has no mother tongue.

When I think of Quebec, I do not first see the Easter crowd swirling through the narrow streets beneath the old walls. I see a red-bearded guide beside an autumn lake, his body pledged to Britain and his heart faithful to France. I see a pale lumberjack in a blue hospital coat, planning to command a kitchen with one hand because the other had been broken in the defence of freedom. I see a wiry bowman crouched beside the Sainte Marguerite, troubled by the sudden discovery that his house was larger than he had dreamed.

And behind them I see the long procession of the quiet people: farmers turning the dark soil, women reading letters from overseas, priests speaking beneath wooden crucifixes, children learning two histories that may yet become one. Above them are the Laurentian hills; before them, the great river; around them, the forests breathing their ancient lesson of endurance.

The river does not cease to be itself because tributaries enter it. It gathers them, carries them, and grows strong.

So Canada must gather her peoples—not by crushing memory, not by ridiculing difference, not by calling one race loyal and another suspect, but by showing all of them the common channel in which their freedom runs.

Give Quebec truth instead of taunts. Give her time, and light. Speak to her in the language of justice, liberty, humanity, and faith. Then, when danger comes near enough to be clearly seen, the men of her farms and forests will not hide behind their mountains.

They will take the paddle, the axe, the rifle—whatever duty places in their hands—and go with the others.

I know them.

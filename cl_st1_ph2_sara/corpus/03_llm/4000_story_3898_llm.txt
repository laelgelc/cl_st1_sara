# THE SOUND AT THE GATE

## I

At first there was pain; not pain localised and explicable, such as a physician might imprison within a Latin name and her husband within the amber walls of a medicine-bottle, but a pale, enormous pulsation, a sheet-lightning of anguish flashing soundlessly from the roots of her hair to the last cold extremity of her feet. Then the flashes grew fainter, farther apart; darkness flowed in between them, blue-black and tepid, a tide in which she was sinking, sinking, while from some immeasurable height voices dropped after her like pebbles down a well.

“Clara!”

That was Henry’s voice.

She knew it by the note of injured authority which had survived even terror, the accent with which he summoned servants who had misplaced his umbrella, porters who appeared negligent of his luggage, and Providence itself when the weather interfered with his Sunday walk. She wished to answer him, not because she had anything to say, but because for twenty-seven years she had answered whenever Henry called.

Her lips made no movement.

“Clara, can you hear me?”

Yes, she could hear him. She could hear also the physician’s whisper, the rustle of the nurse’s apron, the discreet stirring of the fire, and, beneath these nearer sounds, the rain fingering the window as though some timid creature desired admittance. Yet all were receding. The room had acquired vanishing edges; the bed floated like a rosy island in a vaporous sea; the brass knobs at its foot burned for an instant with a strange hieratic splendour, then went out like stars at dawn.

She remembered, incongruously, a sunset seen from San Miniato, when Florence had lain below her in violet shadow and the windows of the Arno palaces had flamed one after another, fiery tablets upon which some invisible angel appeared to write the names of the blessed. She had turned then to Henry, shaken by beauty, almost frightened by the magnitude of what she felt.

“Do you see?” she had whispered.

Henry had looked obediently.

“Yes. Very pretty. We must go down before the gates shut.”

All her married life seemed gathered into that answer.

Again the pain trembled through her, but now at a distance, like summer lightning beyond remote hills. She felt herself released from weight, from temperature, from the laborious necessity of breathing. Higher and higher she rose—or deeper and deeper she sank; direction had become meaningless—through opaline folds of obscurity where fragments of her existence drifted past: a child’s coral necklace; a schoolroom smelling of slate-pencil and geranium leaves; Shelley’s poems hidden beneath a French grammar; Henry’s first visiting card, very white and correct; a baby’s empty cradle; the dark-green canals of Venice; an unpaid dressmaker’s bill; the profile of a Botticelli angel; Henry asleep after dinner with a railway novel fallen open upon his waistcoat.

“Help!” she cried, though no sound issued from her.

The darkness received the cry and became luminous.

For one exquisite instant she hovered upon the frontier between memory and extinction. She saw her own nature as a house whose rooms she had inhabited unequally: some cheerful and serviceable, with chintz curtains, flowered carpets, and bells that answered promptly; others shuttered, dustless, waiting through the years for a guest who never came. In the central chamber, where she had imagined the soul must live, candles had always been burning before pictures Henry did not care for, books he did not read, music that disturbed his calculations, and flowers whose colours he confessed himself unable to distinguish. She had sometimes invited him in. He had entered, glanced about with good-humoured perplexity, and asked whether there was a draught.

Now the house fell away.

She opened her eyes—or believed that she opened them—and saw Henry standing at the bedside.

His broad, reliable face had collapsed. Tears, astonishing and almost indecorous, ran into his moustache. One hand gripped the bedrail; the other lay heavily upon the coverlet, so near her hand that in life she would have felt its warmth. The physician touched his shoulder.

“It is over,” he said.

Over! The word, with its blunt terrestrial finality, loosened the last thread. Clara felt a rush of compassion for the bent figure beside the bed, and with it, curiously interwoven, relief. Poor Henry! Dear Henry! Now at least there would be no more explanations; no more heroic attempts to persuade him that a phrase in Dante might alter the aspect of suffering, or that the curve of a hill against evening sky could make one happy and unhappy at once; no more dining at seven precisely when twilight was performing miracles over the elms; no more doors slammed in unconscious emphasis; no more—

Henry moved away.

His boots creaked.

They were excellent boots, heavily soled and conscientiously polished, but the left one had creaked for three winters. Clara had implored him to change it; Henry had replied that a sound boot should not be discarded merely because it expressed itself. Now the familiar complaint crossed the room—creak, pause, creak—and passed into the corridor.

The door shut with his usual violence.

Then she was dead.

## II

Death, she discovered, was not an event but a climate.

She lay for a time—if time persisted—in a soft suspension, free alike from expectation and regret. About her extended a pearly uncertainty, neither air nor water, in which colours slept before awakening into their earthly names. There was no sun, yet a tender radiance increased; no wind, yet something cool and fragrant moved against her consciousness. Her thoughts, relieved of the skull’s narrow architecture, spread with vanishing edges, touching distances which in life had seemed inaccessible.

Gradually the opaline void composed itself.

Mountains rose, not abruptly, but as though remembered by the light: first long amethyst inclinations, then pinnacles of rosy marble, then snowfields dissolving upward into sapphirine space. At their feet flowed a river so clear that its movement was visible only in the tremor of reflected stars. Trees stood beside it, their leaves dark as bronze on one surface and silver on the other; among the branches birds uttered three liquid notes, paused, and answered themselves from farther woods. Flowers, unfamiliar yet intimate, made jewel-like incrustations upon the grass. Beyond all stretched hyaline distances in which temples, towers, and forests appeared and vanished like thoughts too beautiful to retain.

Clara rose without effort. She wore no garment that she could see, yet felt neither naked nor clothed. Her body, if body remained, possessed the translucence and obedience of flame.

A figure awaited her beside the river.

It was impossible afterward to decide whether the figure had been man or woman, young or ancient. The face altered with each movement of Clara’s mind: now grave as an archaic Apollo, now compassionate as a Bellini Madonna, now impersonal as moonlight upon stone. Only the eyes remained constant, and in them she saw neither welcome nor judgment, but an attention so complete that concealment became absurd.

“You are the Spirit of Life,” Clara said.

“I am called so.”

The voice was quiet, almost plain. She had expected celestial harmonies, but its simplicity touched her more deeply than music.

“Then this is—?”

“What you are able to see of it.”

“Heaven?”

“If you prefer.”

Clara looked across the river. “It is very beautiful.”

“You desired beauty.”

“I desired it all my life.”

“Yes.”

The monosyllable, though not ironic, made her defensive.

“You cannot know how I desired it. Other people were content with objects—with houses, appointments, dinners, the proper number of calls. I wanted the significance behind objects, the secret flame. A street at dusk, a line of poetry, a face in an old picture—such things opened doors in me. But there was never anyone to pass through them.”

“Never?”

Clara hesitated.

“Not wholly.”

“You were married.”

“Yes,” she said, with a little sigh which habit had perfected. “To a very good man.”

“And goodness prevented understanding?”

“No, no. Henry’s goodness was beyond question. He was faithful, honourable, punctual; he remembered subscriptions and anniversaries, though not always the right anniversaries; he never permitted a tradesman’s account to remain unpaid. If I had required a carriage at midnight, he would have procured one, even in the rain. But if I required him to understand why the moon above a cypress made me think of death, he said I ought not to think of death and drew the curtains.”

The Spirit waited.

“He loved me,” Clara continued. “In his way.”

“What was his way?”

“Providing things. Preventing things. Asking whether I had warm stockings. He believed affection consisted in keeping discomfort at a distance.”

“And your way?”

“To share everything that cannot be provided.”

“What things?”

Clara turned eagerly toward the mountains.

“Oh, the first perception of beauty before the mind has named it; the shock of recognition in art; the mysterious correspondence between landscape and emotion; the sense, in reading history, that humanity is a mighty river and oneself a drop which has nevertheless known the whole sea! I wanted someone to whom I could say ‘Leonardo’ and not be asked whether I meant the scientist or the painter; someone who understood that in the grave sweetness of Mantegna there is more consolation than in cheerful pictures, that the Parthenon is not merely a ruin, that the Middle Ages were not simply insanitary.”

“Did you tell your husband this?”

“For twenty-seven years.”

“And he?”

“He listened. That was part of his goodness. But his listening was like a locked room into which one speaks through the keyhole.”

“Perhaps he understood more than he said.”

Clara smiled sadly. “Henry never understood anything he did not say. He distrusted the unexpressed.”

“Yet he did not express all his love.”

“That was different.”

The Spirit’s face became for an instant very young.

Clara perceived this and flushed with the delicate indignation of one who suspects that the celestial intelligence has scored a point by means beneath the dignity of argument.

“You think me unjust,” she said.

“I think you have not finished.”

“No. I have scarcely begun.”

She walked beside the river, and the Spirit accompanied her. Under their feet the grass gave out a faint aromatic sweetness, like thyme crushed on Mediterranean hills. Clara’s memories, released from chronology, arose around her in coloured presentments.

She spoke of childhood, when she had believed that every grown person possessed an interior kingdom and that marriage meant the joining of two kingdoms beneath one crown. She spoke of her engagement, during which Henry’s silences had seemed profound because she filled them with her own conjectures. She had seen in his composure the tranquillity of a deep nature; in his reluctance to discuss poetry, a masculine reticence before sacred things; in his admission that Italy was “interesting,” an emotion too powerful for common speech.

“Then we went to Florence,” she said, “and the truth became unavoidable.”

“What happened in Florence?”

“Everything happened in Florence.”

Her voice altered; the celestial landscape brightened beneath the pressure of recollection.

She described the first morning when, passing from the hard, sun-struck street into the cool cryptal dusk of Santa Maria Novella, she had seen the frescoes arise from shadow—saints, cities, towers, processions, all the severe and tender dream of medieval Christianity painted upon the walls. The world of the nineteenth century, with its railway timetables, Darwinian controversies, patent stoves, and correct morning calls, had fallen from her; history had opened like a flower, ring within ring, and she had stood at its golden heart.

“Henry sneezed,” she said.

“From emotion?”

“From dust.”

She spoke of Botticelli’s spring, where flowers seemed the alphabet of desire; of Orcagna’s terrible dead; of the grave young men of Bellini; of a Leonardo smile, hovering between knowledge and pity; of Dante, whose exile had suddenly become her own; of the brown Tuscan rivers, the pale olives, the cypresses writing their black exclamation points against the evening.

“And Henry?”

“He bought stamps.”

“Stamps?”

“He had begun collecting them at school. Florence afforded several specimens he lacked.”

“Did this diminish the pictures?”

“No,” Clara admitted. “But it diminished my happiness in them. Beauty unanswered becomes almost pain. I would turn to him, overflowing, and find him comparing perforations.”

“You desired an answering voice.”

“Yes! A voice springing to mine as one fountain answers another across an Italian garden; a mind in which my thought should not terminate but continue, enlarged and clarified. Once, while reading the *Vita Nuova*, I came upon a passage that seemed to divide my life into before and after. I went downstairs with the book in my hand. Henry was calculating whether we could reduce the coal bill by changing merchants. He marked his place with his finger and said, ‘Well, my dear?’ I read the passage.”

“What did he say?”

“He said, ‘Very nice.’ Then he asked whether Dante had earned his living by writing.”

The birds repeated their three lucid notes. Clara regarded the river with noble melancholy.

“I do not blame him,” she said, in the tone with which she had blamed him most effectively during life. “One does not blame a wall for not being a window. But imagine inhabiting for years a house full of rooms, and hearing another person forever pacing the passage outside, never guessing there are doors.”

“Did you enter his rooms?”

Clara looked surprised.

“His?”

“Did he possess none?”

“I suppose every nature does.”

“What hung upon his walls?”

“Maps, perhaps. Railway notices. Framed receipts.”

“What did he care for?”

“His business. The garden. Parliamentary reports. The children of his clerks. He knew all their birthdays. He liked detective stories of the crudest sort, in yellow covers, and books about polar expeditions. He could become quite animated over the proper treatment of leather. Once he spoke for forty minutes about boots.”

“And did you listen?”

“I was embroidering.”

“Did you understand?”

“There was nothing to understand.”

Again the Spirit’s face became young.

Clara stopped. “You are severe.”

“I ask questions.”

“That is the most severe thing one can do.”

The Spirit inclined its head.

After a moment Clara said: “If I failed him, it was because I starved. A starving person has little leisure to admire another’s appetite.”

“What would have satisfied you?”

“One soul—only one—formed to answer mine.”

“A kindred soul?”

The phrase, so often breathed over pages and twilight walks, sounded unexpectedly foolish when pronounced beside the eternal river. Nevertheless Clara lifted her head.

“Yes.”

“You sought such a soul?”

“Everywhere. In books chiefly, because the dead are less disappointing. Shelley sometimes seemed near; Shakespeare knew too much to be intimate; Dante was too stern; the women of the *Decameron* too easily diverted. I sought it in music, in pictures, in faces glimpsed across drawing-rooms. Once, at a concert, a stranger turned toward me during the slow movement, and I thought from his expression that he had felt precisely what I felt. I never saw him again.”

“Would you know the soul if you met it?”

“At once.”

“Even without boots?”

Clara stared. The Spirit’s expression was immutable.

“Must heaven be humorous?” she asked.

“Where else could humour be perfectly safe?”

Before Clara could answer, a movement occurred among the trees on the farther bank. It was no more than a deepening of shadow, a slight concentration of light; yet her whole being leaned toward it as a flower leans toward dawn.

Someone crossed the river.

He did not walk upon the water, nor through it; rather, the shining interval accepted him and made passage. As he approached, Clara saw a tall figure with a face neither handsome nor otherwise, because recognition obliterated criticism. His eyes were grey, but within their greyness slept colours she had seen in rain, in distant mountains, in old silver, in the wings of birds at evening.

He paused before her.

“San Miniato,” he said.

Clara’s heart—or the immortal equivalent of that exhausted organ—gave a great leap.

“At sunset,” she answered.

“When the city becomes a chalice—”

“And every window holds fire!”

They smiled with the astonishment of two musicians discovering that each has been carrying half of the same melody.

The Spirit said: “This is the soul you sought.”

“He is here,” Clara whispered.

Then all restraint vanished.

They spoke first of Florence, because Florence had been for both the visible capital of the invisible world. He understood the Botticelli sadness beneath the dancing line; she understood his preference for the austere angels of Mantegna. They had each lingered before the same obscure predella in the Uffizi while crowds streamed toward more celebrated works. They had each thought the Arno most beautiful under rain, when its ochre palaces wavered downward into another drowned city.

“Leonardo—” he began.

“—terrifies because he forgives nothing?”

“Exactly!”

“And Bellini—”

“—forgives before one has confessed.”

“Yes!”

Their voices rose and answered like fountains. They passed from painting to poetry, from poetry to architecture, from architecture to those fugitive moments when art seems merely a veil lifted by the wind. Shelley’s skylark flashed upward between them; Hamlet’s hesitation became a chamber they entered together; Dante’s mighty journey descended and climbed within their speech. They compared the pagan purity of the Parthenon with the aspiring fever of the Gothic spire. They agreed that certain sunsets were essentially Shakespearean, others Giorgionesque, and one rare green after storm unmistakably pre-Raphaelite, though neither wholly approved of the Pre-Raphaelites.

Clara had never been so happy.

No explanation was required. Each phrase opened in the other mind with all its petals intact. When she paused, he completed not merely her sentence but the emotion from which it sprang. When he said “the inexpressible,” she knew which inexpressible thing he meant. They discovered parallel memories: moonlight upon Venetian water; a line read in youth and forgotten for thirty years; the smell of box hedges after rain; the intolerable beauty of a dead bird’s folded wing.

“Have you ever,” he asked, “felt in a crowded room—”

“—that one was alone not because no one spoke, but because everyone spoke from the wrong depth?”

“And when music ceases—”

“—the silence continues it?”

“And ruins—”

“—are not dead buildings but buildings released from use!”

They laughed aloud.

The Spirit stood by, patient as eternity.

At length the stranger turned to Clara with an expression of serene certainty.

“We need never part,” he said.

The words opened before her a vista more immense than the hyaline distances: ages of conversation, journeys among stars, galleries where the archetypes of all lost masterpieces shone undecayed; perpetual understanding, perpetual response. No slammed door would break their reverie. No dinner-bell would summon them from sunset. No yellow-covered novel would intervene between a question and its answer.

“Together,” he continued, “we may follow beauty to its source. There are worlds where colour has sound, and worlds where thought flowers visibly. There are poets whose unwritten poems live here, cities dreamed but never built, music no mortal instrument could endure. Come.”

He held out his hand.

Clara looked at it.

It was a fine, spiritual hand, unburdened by ink-stains, account books, garden twine, or the small scar Henry had acquired while mending the nursery latch. She knew that if she took it, it would never grip too tightly, tap impatiently upon a table, or fumble at night for matches.

A faint uneasiness touched her happiness.

“Do you read novels?” she asked.

“All the great ones.”

“Only the great ones?”

He smiled. “Are there others here?”

“Henry read dreadful novels. Murders in country houses, missing jewels, mysterious Chinamen—most unsuitable things. He always guessed the criminal too early and then went back to see whether the author had cheated.”

The kindred soul looked charmingly perplexed.

“And he left them everywhere,” Clara went on. “On chairs, on the stairs, face downward upon the dining-room mantelpiece. I was forever putting them away.”

“You will not need to put anything away here.”

“No. Of course not.”

She gazed beyond him at the immaculate trees. No dead leaf disfigured the grass. No branch grew awkwardly across another. The river moved without mud, the mountains towered without cold, and even the birds seemed incapable of dropping feathers.

“It is perfect,” she said.

“Yes.”

“How fatiguing perfection may be.”

The stranger’s hand lowered slightly.

Clara turned to the Spirit. “Does no one slam doors here?”

“No door is shut against another.”

“And meals?”

“Hunger is unknown.”

“No one comes late to dinner?”

“There is no lateness.”

“Umbrellas?”

“It does not rain unless rain is desired.”

“Visiting cards? Parcels? Bills? Trains to be caught?”

“No.”

She felt suddenly that the celestial air lacked something essential—not oxygen, for she did not breathe, but resistance, friction, the dear inexorableness of objects. She remembered Henry at the front door in a downpour, unable to find the umbrella which stood directly before him; Henry searching six pockets for a railway ticket held between his lips; Henry insisting that dinner at half-past seven undermined the structure of society; Henry coming upstairs at midnight and dropping one boot, then the other, with two reverberations that shook the water-jug.

She had hated those sounds.

Had she?

The kindred soul spoke gently. “These are shadows of habit. They will fade.”

Clara recoiled. “Must they?”

“All earthly things fade.”

“But Henry is not merely an earthly thing.”

“No soul is.”

She addressed the Spirit. “Where is he now?”

“Alive.”

“Of course. How stupid of me. How long will he remain so?”

“Time is not measured here.”

“But it is measured there, and Henry has always measured it very carefully. Will he come?”

“All come.”

“To this place?”

“To what they are able to see of it.”

Clara considered. “What will Henry see?”

“Perhaps a well-ordered garden. Perhaps a library of information. Perhaps nothing you would call beautiful.”

“He likes dahlias,” she said absently. “Large, rather violent dahlias. He will expect me.”

“Yes.”

The answer was quiet.

“Why?”

“Because he believed you would be here.”

“He believed in immortality?”

“He believed in you.”

Clara’s face altered.

The Spirit continued: “He did not imagine a heaven in which you were absent.”

“But did he—did he think we were kindred souls?”

“He possessed no such phrase. He thought you were his wife.”

“That is not the same.”

“To him it was more.”

The mountains seemed to withdraw. The river darkened from silver to the colour of old glass.

Clara saw Henry in the room beside her dead body, gripping the bedrail while the physician touched his shoulder. She saw him descend the stairs, one hand sliding along the banister because his eyes were blinded; saw him pause in the hall where her shawl still hung; saw him return mechanically to the drawing-room and move her book from the chair before sitting, though no one would ever need the chair again. He would order dinner because dinner must be ordered. He would fail to eat it. At ten precisely he would lock the doors. Before bed he would wind her little clock as well as his own.

For days he would speak of practical matters. For months he would slam doors. For years perhaps he would read his dreadful novels, pausing now and then to make some observation and discovering, with fresh astonishment, that no one answered.

“He will be lonely,” she said.

“Yes.”

“But he was always sufficient to himself.”

“Was he?”

She remembered his nightly entrance into whatever room she occupied. He had not come to discuss her books. Often he said nothing at all. He merely sat near the fire, rustling newspapers, warming his solid boots, present with the unconscious fidelity of furniture, weather, and the earth. If she moved to another room, he presently followed, complaining that the first had grown chilly.

The kindred soul waited with sorrowful courtesy.

Clara looked at him and saw all she had desired: the answering mind, the unbroken sympathy, the guest for whom the inmost chambers had kept their candles burning. Yet because every door in him stood open, there was no threshold to cross; because he anticipated each thought, there was no discovery; because he understood everything, nothing need be forgiven.

“Come,” he said once more, and his voice held the cadence of remote seas.

She nearly went.

Above them opened the road to beauty: through starry archives, unfallen cities, landscapes finer than Claude had dreamed, paradisal gardens where every flower carried a history and every fountain spoke in verse. She imagined herself moving beside him, higher and higher, while ages unfurled like banners and knowledge became delight.

Then, from some cryptal depth of memory, there came a creak.

Pause.

Creak.

Clara closed her eyes.

“No,” she said.

The stranger bowed his head. He understood, naturally; he could do nothing else. His perfect understanding made departure painless and therefore, in some obscure way, more painful. He moved back across the river, growing first pale, then transparent, then indistinguishable from the light upon the farther trees.

The Spirit remained.

“May I wait here?” Clara asked.

“You may.”

“Alone?”

“Until you cease to be alone.”

“Will it be long?”

“There is no long here.”

“There will be for me.”

“Perhaps.”

Clara seated herself beneath the bronze-and-silver tree. The heavenly grass did not crush beneath her; no damp penetrated her incorporeal skirts. Before her the river carried its freight of reflected stars toward an undiscoverable sea.

“What if Henry, when he comes, prefers his garden?” she asked.

“Then you may visit it.”

“And if he does not recognise me?”

“He will.”

“What if he has found a kindred soul?”

The Spirit’s eyes seemed almost amused.

“He believed he had found one.”

Clara bent her head.

Far away, among those regions where the dead entered according to their several visions, bells sounded—not solemnly, but with the homely irregularity of bells rung by fallible hands. Clara listened. Sometimes figures crossed the luminous plain; sometimes voices called and were answered; sometimes the landscape dissolved again into pearl-coloured mist, through which she saw, as in dreams, the turning seasons of the world she had left.

She saw rain fall upon the house. She saw Henry’s dahlias flare beside the gravel path, monstrous and magnificent. She saw a yellow novel abandoned face downward upon the arm of his chair. She saw her books remain in their glass case, dusted punctually, unread. Once she thought she heard a door slam and rose with a cry, but it was only thunder over the mortal hills.

She waited.

Beauty surrounded her, inexhaustible and unshared. Dante’s stars burned above the mountains; Shelley’s wind moved in the trees; all the blue distances of art and longing opened their immaculate arms. Clara scarcely noticed them.

She sat at the threshold of eternity, listening for Henry’s boots.

### The Private Boreen

It was in the wet end of an October afternoon that I went up the hill to ask Michael and Katty Hennessey how two people might remain married for fifty years and still appear pleased with the arrangement.

The Hennesseys lived beyond a narrow boreen—a little road, if road it could be called—which wandered between banks of fern and blackthorn before arriving, almost apologetically, at their whitewashed farmhouse. Rain shone on the slates. A yellow light stood in the kitchen window, and smoke from the chimney lay along the hill like a grey scarf.

Michael himself opened the door. He was a tall, spare man, though bent a little by the years, with white hair under a brown caubeen and eyes that seemed to have retained all the blue of his youth.

“Come in out iv that,” he said. “Sure, a man’d be dhrowned shtandin’ upright.”

The kitchen into which he brought me had the substantial comfort of a place where nothing was displayed merely for ornament. The dresser held clean crockery, blue-edged and shining; sides of bacon hung from the rafters; a black kettle breathed above the turf fire; and in a cool recess beyond the half-door I saw the pale gleam of milk pans. There was an odour of turf, bread and cows—an aroma which, after the wet hillside, seemed to me the very perfume of hospitality.

Katty Hennessey sat at the hearth in a low chair, knitting a grey stocking. She was small and round, with a smooth pink face, alert dark eyes and a quantity of silver hair brushed back from her forehead.

“So this is the young gintleman,” she said.

“This is himself,” Michael answered.

“The one that’s goin’ to be married?”

I had not intended to make my private affairs the price of admission, and protested that my engagement had not yet been formally announced.

“Listen to him!” cried Katty. “Not announced, he says, an’ him carryin’ the news on his face like the bills outside the chapel gate.”

“I told ye,” said Michael, taking his pipe from the mantelpiece. “The first time I seen him I says, ‘There’s a man has lost his liberty, or is thinkin’ hard about losin’ it.’”

“I have not lost it yet,” I replied.

“Thin there’s time for ye,” said Michael.

Katty struck his knee lightly with the stocking.

“Pay no heed to him. Michael was always talkin’ about liberty, though sorra much use he ever made iv it. Sit down, sir, an’ I’ll wet the tay.”

I explained, while she measured tea into a brown pot, that their golden wedding had been spoken of throughout the parish; that everyone described them as the happiest couple in three counties; and that, being myself upon the threshold of matrimony, I hoped they might tell me the secret of so remarkable an achievement.

“The saycret!” Katty repeated, looking at Michael. “Do ye hear that now?”

“I do,” he said. “An’ I’m wondherin’ which of us has it.”

“Ye had it hid long enough, if ye have.”

“Maybe it’s behind the dresser wid the other things ye put safe an’ can never find again.”

She gave him a look of great severity, then placed before me tea, soda bread, butter and a dish of blackberry jam.

“There’s no saycret,” she said. “Is there, Michael?”

“None that I know.”

“But surely,” I persisted, “fifty years of peace cannot have come about by accident.”

“Peace!” Katty lifted both hands. “He calls it peace.”

“What else would ye call it?” asked Michael.

“I’d call it work. Milkin’ before daylight, calves bawlin’, hens in the kitchen, childer wid the measles, rain in the hay, an’ himself comin’ in at midnight sayin’ the mare had a stone in her shoe.”

“She had.”

“She had not.”

“She had, woman.”

“Well, if she had, ye were four hours findin’ it.”

Michael smiled into his pipe. Katty poured out the tea.

“No,” she said, “it wasn’t peace. But it was happiness, thanks be to God.”

“An’ thanks be to Katty,” Michael added quietly. “For there never was a better woman put two feet under a table.”

At this Katty’s face softened.

“Nor a better man sat at the head iv one,” she replied. “Though he’d thry the patience of Saint Job whin the notion took him.”

Their compliments, it seemed, required a protective covering of abuse. I returned to my inquiry.

“But how was it done? Did you never disagree?”

“Disagree?” said Katty. “Every day iv our lives.”

“Did you never quarrel?”

“Only the wanst,” said Michael.

Katty stopped with the teapot in the air.

“Now, Michael!”

“Only the wanst,” he repeated.

“Ye’ll give the gintleman a wrong notion.”

“Thin give him the right wan.”

She set down the pot and looked at me.

“Ye must undhershtand,” she said, “that happiness is like a road.”

“A road?”

“Aye. Some couples take the high-road, where every wan can see them, an’ they’re forever bowin’ an’ smilin’ an’ tellin’ the world how grand they are together. But maybe there’s stones under their feet, an’ dust in their mouths, an’ a ditch waitin’ for them beyond the turn.”

“That’s true,” Michael said.

“Some take a road that’s all mud, an’ they spend their lives blamin’ wan another for the weather. Some never agree which road they’re on at all. He’s marchin’ east, she’s pullin’ west, an’ the two iv them cryin’ out that the house is no nearer.”

“And which did you take?” I asked.

“Our own little boreen,” said Katty. “Narrow enough, an’ crooked in places, but there was room for the two iv us if we didn’t be elbowin’.”

“There’s the saycret for ye,” Michael observed.

But I had already perceived that the Hennesseys’ secret, if entrusted to metaphor, would disappear among hedges. I therefore asked how they had found this private road.

“Found it?” Katty said. “We made it.”

“The first mornin’,” said Michael.

“Will ye let me tell it?”

“I’m waitin’ this half-hour.”

Katty drew her chair nearer the fire. Rain ticked at the window, the kettle murmured, and Michael settled opposite us with his elbows on his knees and his pipe between his fingers.

“We were married,” Katty began, “in the little chapel at Ballycarrick, Father O’Daly sayin’ the Mass, an’ every soul from here to the river crowded in to see was my veil as long as Bridie Malone said it was.”

“It wasn’t,” Michael remarked.

“It was longer.”

“It stopped at your heels.”

“Where else would it stop? Did ye expect it to sweep the road after me?”

“I’m only sayin’ what I seen.”

“Ye never seen it at all. Ye were starin’ straight before ye like a man goin’ to the gallows.”

“I was thinkin’.”

“That was an early start for ye.”

Michael removed the pipe from his mouth and winked at me.

“There was a supper here that night,” Katty continued, “wid fiddlin’, dancin’, songs, two geese, a ham, an’ more whiskey than was good for the men or pleasant for the women. But next mornin’, when the people were gone, an’ the plates stacked, an’ the last neighbour put out through the door—small blame to him, for he could not find it alone—I woke up an’ remembered I was Mrs. Michael Hennessey.

“It was the strangest feelin’. The room was the same, the rain on the window was the same, but nothin’ else in the world was where it had been the day before. I looked at Michael asleep there, an’ I says to meself, ‘There’s my husband. That man belongs to me now.’”

“Ah,” said Michael. “There was the mistake.”

“I know that now. I did not know it thin. I was nineteen years of age, an’ I had notions enough for a parliament. I thought marriage meant himself was mine, the house was mine, the farm was mine, an’ the sun had no right to rise unless I gave it lave.”

“It rose,” said Michael.

“It did. An’ himself along wid it.

“He got up, shaved at the little glass, washed in the basin, an’ put on his good shirt. I lay watchin’ him, proud as a queen, for he was a fine-lookin’ man in them days.”

“In them days?”

“Ye’re a respectable ruin now, acushla.”

Michael bowed.

“Thin,” Katty said, “he reached for his Sunday coat.

“‘Where are ye goin’, Michael?’ says I.

“‘To the fair,’ says he.

“‘What fair?’

“‘The fair at Kilross.’

“‘Ye’ll not go to any fair this day,’ says I.

“He looks at me, slow an’ quiet. ‘An’ why not?’

“‘Because we’re only married since yesterday.’

“‘Sure I know that,’ says he.

“‘Thin ye’ll shtay at home wid your wife.’

“‘I’ve cattle to sell.’

“‘The cattle can wait.’

“‘The fair will not.’

“‘Let the fair go on without ye.’

“‘It might,’ says he, ‘but the cattle’ll not sell themselves.’”

Katty paused to push down a fragment of glowing turf with the tongs.

“Now, mind ye, I’d heard talk from the married women. ‘Begin as ye mean to go on,’ says wan. ‘Never let him think he’s his own master,’ says another. ‘If he gets the road the first week, ye’ll see no more of him than the smoke up the chimney.’ Bad advice is plentiful, sir, an’ generally given for nothin’.

“So while Michael was below in the yard, I took the rack-comb—the only comb we had between us—and hid it under the mattress.”

“The rack-comb?” I asked.

“A long comb with wide teeth,” I was told, “for ordering thick hair.”

“An’ Michael had hair thin,” Katty said, glancing at his shining crown. “A black forest iv it, wild as furze after a gale. He could not put on his caubeen decent without the comb, an’ I knew he’d not go to Kilross lookin’ like a frightened haystack.

“He came in, took up the little glass, an’ reached to the shelf.

“‘Katty,’ says he, ‘where’s the comb?’

“‘I don’t know.’

“‘It was here last night.’

“‘Maybe it walked away.’

“‘Combs have no legs.’

“‘Thin it should be where ye left it.’

“He searched the shelf, the dresser an’ the drawer.

“‘Katty, where’s the comb?’

“‘I told ye I don’t know.’

“‘Ye do know.’

“‘Maybe I do.’

“‘Give it to me.’

“‘I will not.’

“‘Give me the comb, Katty.’

“‘Shtay home from the fair, Michael.’

“‘I’m goin’ to the fair.’

“‘Thin go widout the comb.’

“‘I’ll not go widout it.’

“‘Thin ye’ll not go at all.’

“‘Katty,’ says he, very quiet, ‘give me the comb.’

“‘Michael,’ says I, just as quiet, ‘shtay home.’”

Her repetition of the exchange, although comic in retrospect, began to fill the warm kitchen with an apprehension that belonged to another morning half a century gone. Even Michael no longer smiled.

“He stood there,” Katty continued, “in his white shirt an’ stockings, wid the razor on the table an’ his coat across the chair. I was sittin’ up in the bed, houldin’ the quilt under my chin, though my heart was hammerin’.

“‘Are ye tellin’ me,’ says he, ‘that I cannot go out my own door?’

“‘I’m tellin’ ye your place is here.’

“‘An’ who will choose my place for me?’

“‘Your wife.’

“‘Not while I’m able to choose it meself.’

“‘Ye’re my husband now.’

“‘I am.’

“‘Thin ye must do as I bid ye.’

“He looked at me for a long whisht. Thin he says, ‘Katty, there can be wan captain in a boat, wan ploughman behind a plough, an’ wan master in a house.’

“‘An’ who made you the master?’ says I.

“‘The duty that’s on me,’ says he. ‘If the roof falls, I answer for it. If the crop fails, I answer. If hunger comes through the door, it’s me must meet it. I’ll listen to ye while breath is in me, an’ share all I have, but I’ll not be ordered like a gossoon hired by the day. Now give me the comb.’

“‘No,’ says I.”

The rain had strengthened. It hissed upon the hearthstone where a drop came down the chimney.

Katty looked, not at me, but into the red heart of the turf.

“He asked me again,” she said. “An’ I said no again. Thin he came to the bed, took me by the arm, an’ struck me once across the cheek wid the flat iv his hand.”

She lifted her fingers, lightly touching the place as if the young bride’s astonishment still slept beneath the old woman’s skin.

I confess that I moved uneasily. The neat kitchen, the clean cups and the mild old man opposite me seemed suddenly altered by the image. Michael’s eyes remained lowered.

“I gave a great cry,” Katty went on, “more from surprise than hurt, though it hurt too. There I was, married less than a day, my cheek burnin’, an’ all the grand advice of the women scattered like hens before a dog.

“Michael says, ‘Where is the comb?’

“I says, ‘Under the mattress.’

“‘Get it.’

“I got it an’ put it in his hand.

“He combed his hair. He put on his coat an’ his caubeen. Thin he took the blackthorn from behind the door.

“‘I’m goin’ to the fair,’ says he.

“I did not answer.

“‘Do ye undhershtand me, Katty?’

“‘I do.’

“‘Will ye ever hide the comb from me again?’

“‘I will not.’

“‘Will ye ever tell me I cannot cross my own threshold?’

“‘No.’

“‘Good,’ says he. ‘For I’ll never tell ye that ye cannot cross it either.’”

Katty fell silent.

“And he went?” I asked.

“He went down the boreen,” she said. “I heard the gate lift an’ fall. Thin I sat on the edge of the bed an’ cried till I’d no tears left to be wastin’.

“I was angry. Oh, I had rage enough to burn Kilross, fair, cattle an’ all. I said I’d go back to my mother. I said I’d never speak to him. I said I’d die, which is a foolish threat, for nobody was there to be frightened by it.

“Thin I dressed, made the bed, washed the cups, swept the floor an’ fed the hens. There’s great medicine in work when a body’s heart is boilin’. By the time the kettle sang I had begun to think.

“I thought of what he’d said. I thought of his face when he said it. An’ though I did not like the lesson, I knew there was truth hid in it. I had not asked him to stay; I had commanded him. I had not said I was lonely; I had stolen the comb. I wanted love, but I went lookin’ for it wid a rope.”

Michael nodded slowly.

“A rope makes a poor weddin’ ring,” he said.

“So I says to meself, ‘Katty, if ye tie a man to the hearth, he’ll spend his days lookin’ at the road. If ye leave the door open, maybe he’ll remember where his home is.’

“An’ while I was thinkin’ that, I heard the gate again.”

“The gate?” I said.

“Aye. I looked out, an’ there was Michael comin’ up the boreen, the blackthorn under his arm, his caubeen crooked, an’ not half an hour gone.”

Michael relit his pipe with a coal from the fire.

“He came in,” Katty said, “an’ put the stick behind the door.

“‘Did ye forget somethin’?’ says I.

“‘I did.’

“‘What?’

“‘Me breakfast.’

“‘Ye can get breakfast at Kilross.’

“‘I’m not goin’ to Kilross.’

“‘What about the fair?’

“‘Let the fair go on without me.’

“I looked at him, but I would not smile. ‘The cattle’ll not sell themselves.’

“‘They can wait.’

“‘An’ why can they wait now?’

“He took off the caubeen. ‘Because,’ says he, ‘I’m only married since yesterday.’

“Thin I began cryin’ again, an’ he came over an’ put his arms around me.

“‘Katty,’ says he, ‘I’ll not have ye rule me, but neither will I use my freedom to hurt ye. I had to know I could go. Now I know it, I’d sooner shtay.’

“‘An’ I had to know ye’d shtay,’ says I. ‘Now I know it, I’ll never stop ye goin’.’

“An’ that,” she concluded, “was our quarrel.”

For a little while the room held only the ticking clock and the soft collapse of turf in the grate.

“You never quarrelled again?” I asked.

“We differed,” said Michael. “There’s no harm in two minds under wan roof, so long as they remember the roof.”

“He went to fairs enough after that,” said Katty, “an’ I never hid the comb.”

“She went visitin’ her mother, an’ I never hid her shawl.”

“If I said, ‘Michael, I’d be glad if ye stayed,’ he listened.”

“If I said, ‘Katty, this thing must be done,’ she trusted me.”

“Not always,” she corrected.

“Nearly always.”

“Enough to keep ye respectable.”

I looked from one to the other. Their doctrine belonged, I felt, to a sterner age, and its first lesson was not one I could comfortably carry to my own prospective bride. Yet the conclusion was less simple than the blow with which it had begun. Michael had asserted a right to leave and immediately chosen to remain; Katty had yielded authority and thereby gained consideration. Neither spoke as though victory had been the object. They had discovered, painfully and imperfectly, the boundary between possession and devotion.

Michael seemed to read something of my uncertainty.

“A house needs a head,” he said, “but it needs a heart worse. A head without a heart makes hard laws. A heart without a head has everythin’ in confusion.”

“An’ a hand?” Katty asked sharply.

He looked at her.

“A hand,” he said, after a pause, “is best kept for workin’.”

“That’s well remembered,” she replied.

Then she smiled, and he reached across to pat her fingers.

When I rose to go, the rain had ceased. Katty wrapped bread in a cloth for me, rejecting my protests with the argument that unmarried men were never properly fed, while Michael accompanied me to the gate.

Below us the boreen shone between its dark hedges. Beyond it, the high-road ran pale in the last of the evening, broad enough for carts, cattle, disputes and all the traffic of the world.

“So,” Michael said, “did ye get the saycret?”

“I am not certain.”

“Good. A man that’s certain before marriage knows less after it.”

He pointed with his blackthorn toward the winding lane.

“Don’t be huntin’ our road,” he said. “It would not take ye where you’re goin’. Herself an’ me made it for our own two feet. You an’ your lady must make another.”

From the doorway Katty called, “An’ tell the girl to keep a comb for herself!”

Michael laughed.

I went down the hill with the bread under my arm and their voices behind me, mingling in affectionate contradiction. The boreen was crooked, puddled and hemmed in by thorns; yet every turn of it looked back toward the warm square of their window, and every stone, I thought, had been worn smooth by two travellers who had long ago ceased asking which of them owned the road.

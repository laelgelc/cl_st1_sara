I write this while I am still able to write it as myself.

That qualification is important. To-morrow—or after Wednesday’s matinée—I may dot every *i* with a dagger, describe the moon as “yon pallid confidante of guilty lovers,” and sign my name in a hand three inches high. Yesterday I caught myself bowing to the washstand. This morning, when my razor slipped, I did not say what any Christian gentleman would naturally say. I held the blade aloft, regarded the small wound in the mirror, and murmured:

“Blood! Then all is known!”

There was nobody in the room.

My name, for the present, is Arthur Lenton. I am twenty-seven years old, sober by upbringing, moderate by preference, and until last September was so little remarkable that even my landlady sometimes forgot whether I occupied the second floor or the third. I wrote municipal paragraphs for the *Morning Comet*: drains, paving contracts, deputations of ratepayers, and the annual dispute about the bandstand. I had a small salary, two grey suits, no enemies, and an engagement to Delia Marsh.

Delia liked quiet men. She said so.

“You don’t make a fuss, Arthur,” she once told me.

I remember the words with agony. No—let me be exact. I remember them while leaning one elbow upon the mantelpiece and hiding my eyes with the other hand. The agony came later.

My disposition has always been yielding. At school I sat beside a boy who stuttered, and within a fortnight I was sent to the headmaster for mocking him, though I had not intended mockery. At twenty I lodged with a retired naval officer and acquired, without ever going to sea, a rolling walk and the habit of calling the butter “ship’s stores.” These imitations passed when their causes were removed. Virgin soil, you understand. Anything planted in me grew.

Barnaby knew this. I cannot prove that he knew it, but editors know everything harmful to their staff.

Barnaby was editor of the *Comet*. He was broad, bald, cigar-bearing, and endowed with the mysterious journalistic power of ending a conversation before the other person had begun it. He sent for me one Monday.

“Lenton,” he said, “you’re taking the theatres.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Dramatic criticism. Vale’s ill.”

“But I have never been to a theatre.”

“Excellent.”

“I mean never. Not even pantomime.”

“Fresh eye.”

“I know nothing of acting.”

“Unprejudiced.”

“I have not read a play since *The Merchant of Venice* at school, and I thought the merchant was Shylock.”

“Public thinks so too. You’ll suit it.”

He pushed two tickets across the desk.

I pushed them back.

He pushed them across again.

“I should be ridiculous,” I said.

“My dear boy, that is no disqualification in criticism.”

“I must refuse.”

“Curtain at eight.”

And then he asked whether the new paving estimate had come from Clerkenwell, which was Barnaby’s method of burying the living. Five minutes later I found myself in the street with the tickets in my pocket and the conviction that I had resigned. I had done nothing of the kind.

That evening I attended the Imperial Theatre.

Perhaps you have always known the theatre and cannot conceive its effect upon an adult mind preserved from it for twenty-seven years. You have been vaccinated by Christmas entertainments, amateur tableaux, school recitations and elderly aunts impersonating Lady Macbeth. I had no such protection.

The curtain rose upon a drawing-room in which no human creature could have drawn a breath. The walls were crimson, the chairs gilded, the flowers as large as cabbages. Into this apartment came a young gentleman whose simplest movement would have dislocated an ordinary shoulder. He did not enter; he *arrived*. He paused at the door, flung one glove upon a table, crossed six paces, wheeled, passed his fingers through his hair, and announced to an empty chair that fate had hunted him to the abyss.

A lady entered. She wore white and carried six feet of sorrow behind her.

“Gerald!” she cried.

“Lucille!”

They stood at opposite ends of the room, breathing audibly. Then he advanced three steps; she retreated two; he extended his right hand; she turned her left cheek toward the gallery. I supposed they had toothache.

Within ten minutes everybody had communicated an emotion by striking some article of furniture. The father smote the desk in anger. The villain tapped a portrait with his forefinger in cynical triumph. The heroine clutched the curtains. An old servant embraced a fire-screen. Gerald, on discovering that he had accidentally married his own aunt—or something of that nature—fell upon an ottoman, rose from it, laughed three times without amusement, and appealed to Heaven from beneath the chandelier.

The audience was delighted.

I was appalled.

I went home feverish and wrote that the performance possessed “a certain flamboyant sincerity, marred only by occasional excess.” Barnaby cut out “marred only by occasional excess” and printed the rest as a poster.

The next morning, while requesting my landlady to boil my egg longer, I found myself resting two fingers upon the breakfast table.

“This,” I said, “is not an egg. It is a mockery—a pale, ovoid mockery!”

Mrs. Pottle stared at me.

I ate it in silence.

I might have recovered had Vale’s indisposition ended. But Vale developed complications. Every night there was another theatre, and every theatre implanted some fresh abomination.

At the Royalty I saw a comedian who answered all questions by thrusting out his chin and closing one eye. For three days I purchased newspapers in that fashion. At the Prince’s, a military actor twisted his moustache whenever he lied. I had no moustache, but my fingers went up notwithstanding, and I was twice suspected of falsehood while giving perfectly accurate information about tramway receipts.

The worst was a tragedy at the Parthenon. The leading actor, Cassian Vane, possessed a voice which seemed to begin in the cellar and emerge by way of the roof. He divided ordinary words into separate calamities.

“Do not pity me,” in his delivery, became:

“Do—not—Pit—y—Me!”

The final “me” was scarcely human. It was a tomb opening.

For a week I could not ask for tea without sounding like a fallen emperor.

Delia noticed the change before I admitted it. We met each Thursday at her mother’s house in Canonbury. Formerly I had kissed her, inquired after Mrs. Marsh’s rheumatism, and sat where I was put. On the third Thursday of my criticism I entered, paused beneath the lintel, removed my hat very slowly, and gazed round the room as though searching for the corpse of an ancestor.

“Why are you standing there?” Delia asked.

I came forward with Cassian Vane’s stride.

“Can a man not look,” I replied, “upon the chamber where his happiness lies sleeping?”

“My happiness isn’t sleeping. Mother is.”

I lowered my voice.

“Then let no rude syllable awaken her.”

“You’ve already dropped your hat.”

It is difficult to maintain tragic dignity while retrieving a bowler from beneath a chair.

Delia was patient at first. She supposed I was tired, then ill, then making some inexplicable joke. I promised to stop. Indeed, I meant to stop. I practised natural gestures in my room. I would stand before the glass and say, “Good morning,” with my arms at my sides. Yet the reflection looked lifeless. Before I knew it, my chest expanded, my nostrils dilated, and the greeting came out:

“Good mor-ning!”

There is no adequate notation for vocal italics. They entered my speech like a disease.

I went to Barnaby.

“I must give up the theatre work.”

“Why?”

“It is affecting me.”

“Good. Means you’re interested.”

“I imitate the actors.”

“Observation.”

“Yesterday I denounced a bus conductor.”

“What had he done?”

“He asked for my fare.”

“Ruffian. Review the Criterion to-night.”

“I am serious, Barnaby.”

“So is the piece. Problem play. Nine o’clock.”

He rang for a messenger. While I waited to continue, three proof sheets arrived, a subeditor asked whether Turkey had declared war, and Barnaby dictated an article beginning, “At this grave national crisis.” By the time the crisis was composed I was holding another ticket.

At the Criterion I learned the modern manner: less arm, more eyebrow. This did not improve me. Exaggeration in a small compass is still exaggeration, as arsenic remains arsenic in a pill. I returned to Delia with a new and sinister quietness. I looked at her for long periods before replying. I smiled at remarks which did not amuse me. Once, when she asked whether I had posted a letter, I turned toward the fire and said:

“There are letters which a man posts—and letters which post the man.”

“What does that mean?”

I had no idea.

My clothing altered next. One cannot nightly admire persons in beautifully cut coats without becoming dissatisfied with a suit whose principal merit is that both sleeves terminate at the wrists. I purchased a velvet waistcoat. Then a soft collar. Then a hat which made me resemble a minor poet acquitted for lack of evidence. My tailor, encouraged by ruin, suggested a cape.

“No,” I said firmly.

He draped it over my shoulders.

I looked in the glass.

“Perhaps,” I whispered, “for evening.”

Delia disliked the cape. She particularly disliked my method of throwing one end over the shoulder before crossing a road. She said it frightened horses.

But the corruption was no longer external. I ceased to experience feelings simply. If annoyed, I could not be merely annoyed; I must become ironical, freeze, break into hollow laughter, or stride to a window and address the weather. Affection demanded lowered eyelids and a vibration in the throat. Surprise required a backward step. Suspicion drew my brows together; despair passed a hand across them; resolution clenched the hand; hopeless resolution clenched both.

Once, in the office, I discovered an error in my copy and whispered, “Too late!”

It was not too late. The edition had not gone to press. Nevertheless I allowed the error to remain because the situation seemed to require it.

My reviews, meanwhile, grew famous.

At first I had written cautiously because I knew nothing. Later I wrote boldly for the same reason. I spoke of “the architecture of emotion,” “the chromatic value of silence,” and “the actor’s conquest of the invisible.” Barnaby liked “conquest of the invisible” and used it in an advertisement for binoculars. Managers sent invitations. Actors asked me to supper. One tragedian gripped my wrist and told me I alone had comprehended his pauses.

The alarming thing was that I began to comprehend them.

Actors surrounded me. Their habits adhered. I borrowed Vane’s voice, Mortimer Blythe’s walk, Felix Dray’s weary smile, and Julian North’s method of lighting a cigarette as if granting fire an audience. Even my laugh was not mine. I possessed a collection of laughs: the careless social ripple; the hard laugh of a man betrayed; the tender laugh behind which tears assemble; the sardonic “Ha!” I had no ordinary laugh left.

People moved away from me in restaurants.

Mrs. Pottle requested that I not rehearse after midnight. When I told her I never rehearsed, she crossed herself.

The catastrophe with Delia occurred in March. I call it a catastrophe because that is what it was, though I see—I see perfectly—that the word has acquired an unpleasant professional resonance.

We were to be married in June. We had chosen a small house near Ealing, with a strip of garden and a room in which I was to write. Delia had spoken of curtains. I had spoken of shelves. These plain objects belonged to Arthur Lenton’s life, and Arthur Lenton was already becoming difficult to locate.

That evening I went to Canonbury after seeing the first act of *The Bride of Vesuvius*, a drama of Roman passion in which every person wore sandals and wished to stab every other person. I had left early on purpose. I wanted to be myself with Delia.

She was alone. She stood by the table, not dramatically, but because she had been arranging flowers.

“Sit down, Arthur,” she said.

I sat. Or rather I sank into the chair and presented my profile.

“Not like that.”

I straightened myself.

She looked at me for several seconds. There was no theatrical pause in it. Delia’s silences were only silences, which made them terrible.

“I can’t bear this any longer,” she said.

“Bear what?”

“That voice. Those looks. The way you move your hands. The way you call me ‘Delia’ as if I had poisoned somebody.”

“De-li-a—”

“There! That.”

I put both hands beneath me.

“You’re ashamed of me,” she continued. “At Aunt Clara’s dinner you stared at the soup and said, ‘So—this is the end.’ You kissed Mrs. Bevan’s hand. You called the footman ‘fellow.’”

“He had the soul of a fellow.”

“He was fourteen.”

I rose, forgetting my hands were to be controlled, and extended one.

“Delia, listen! A malign influence has fastened upon me. Night after night I sit before painted passions. They enter through the eye; they coil about the nerves; they obliterate the man. I struggle—I tear at the bonds—but in vain!”

She closed her eyes.

I heard myself. I heard the rolling cadence, the fatal pauses, the hiss upon *obliterate*. I despised each gesture even as I made it. Yet some accursed inner stage-manager prompted the next.

I fell upon one knee.

“Pit—y me!”

Delia stepped back.

“Get up, Arthur.”

“If you abandon me, what remains?”

“Get up.”

“Only the darkness! The jeering world! The empty—”

“Arthur!”

Her tone was so ordinary that I stopped.

She removed the little ring I had given her and laid it on the table. No orchestra trembled. No thunder rolled. The lamp did not even flicker. A cab passed outside.

“I don’t know who you are,” she said. “But you are not the man I promised to marry.”

Then she went upstairs.

I remained kneeling until Mrs. Marsh entered and asked whether I had lost a collar stud.

Since that night the progress of my indisposition has accelerated. Grief, instead of curing me, provided fresh material. I do not suffer privately; I suffer from room to room. My meals go cold while I contemplate them. I walk at dusk along the Embankment, cape flung back, hat lowered, a noticeable figure beneath the lamps. Policemen observe me. Children follow me. Last Sunday a temperance worker offered assistance.

I have tried remedies.

I spent two days at the British Museum among Assyrian antiquities, supposing their repose might calm me. By noon I was leaning against a winged bull and murmuring about vanished empires. I read railway timetables, but even “London depart 6.42” acquired in my mouth the pathos of exile. I attended a lecture on sewage. The lecturer’s gestures were deplorable; by the conclusion I had added two of them to my repertory.

I attempted solitude. Solitude is worst. Without witnesses I invent them.

Three times I have written my resignation. The first began, “Dear Sir,” and ended, “Thus, Barnaby, I return to you the poisoned chalice.” The second was four pages long and contained a quotation from *Hamlet* which, on examination, was not in *Hamlet*. The third simply read:

“Choose! My freedom—or your victim’s doom!”

Barnaby pencilled beneath it: “Doom. Review Thursday. B.”

This afternoon I confronted him for the last time.

He was cutting an article on unemployment.

“Barnaby,” I said, “look at me.”

“Can’t. Busy.”

“You have destroyed a human personality.”

“Make it fifty words.”

“My engagement is broken. My friends avoid me. I cannot order a chop without apostrophising it.”

“Don’t order chops.”

“I am under a curse!”

“Try fish.”

I seized his desk.

“I resign.”

“No.”

“You cannot prevent me.”

“Contract.”

“I signed no contract.”

“Then nothing to worry about. Adelphi to-night.”

“Barnaby, I warn you—”

“Seven-forty curtain. Take a cab.”

He offered the ticket. I refused it. He placed it on the desk. I turned away. There it lay, small, pink, inevitable—the passport to my extinction.

I took it.

Do not judge me. You have not met Barnaby.

And now the hour approaches. In twenty minutes I must dress. On the chair lies my evening coat, narrowed at the waist by a tailor who says I have “a natural line.” Beside it are gloves of pearl grey. I did not buy them. I discovered them in a drawer, which is more alarming. My hair, once obedient to water, has begun to fall across my forehead in a manner suggestive of spiritual crime.

What am I to do?

Ordinary society will soon close against me. A man may glare once at a waiter and remain respectable; if he does it nightly, people infer drink or insanity. I cannot return to Delia. I passed her yesterday in Regent Street. She bowed as one bows to an acquaintance. I raised my hat with such ineffable sadness that a woman behind her applauded.

There is only one community in which my conduct would escape notice.

The stage.

Yes, reader—the stage! Behold the final irony, if irony it be and not the deliberate malice of Providence. I, who abhor false emotion, may be compelled to earn my bread by exhibiting it. I, whose identity has been eaten away by actors, must become an actor in order to seem a man.

The notion is hideous.

It is also, Barnaby says, practical.

He has already spoken to a manager.

This manager believes “Arthur Lenton” unsuitable. Too quiet. Too clerical. He proposes “Adrian Vale.” I confess the name has a certain—No! I will not admire it. I reject it. I trample it beneath my heel.

Still, it would look well upon a bill.

ADRIAN VALE  
in  
THE MASK OF A SOUL

There! You see? The disease writes for me. Even this confession, begun in plain English, has swollen, strutted, declaimed and thrown back its cape. My very punctuation poses. My dashes are gestures—my exclamations, cries from the gallery!

But I am not conquered.

I shall go to Barnaby to-morrow. I shall stand quietly before him. I shall speak in my own voice, with my hands at my sides. I shall say, “I resign.” Two words. Simple. Final.

Unless he interrupts.

Unless he offers me another ticket.

Unless—

Hark! The cab waits below. The curtain rises in half an hour, and destiny—black-browed, implacable destiny—summons me.

I come.

I come!

### THE ERROR OF MR. SAUMAREZ

*When the hot earth waits for thunder,*  
*And the heart for leave to speak,*  
*Who shall say what god or blunder*  
*Turns the strong aside to seek?*

There are three accounts of the Saumarez affair, and they are all untrue. The Station held one, Saumarez supplied another in fragments and under pressure, and I have constructed the third from what I saw, heard, guessed, and was afterwards requested to forget. The women’s account would settle the matter; but women do not render departmental reports upon their hearts, and, if they did, no man would understand the handwriting.

Therefore you must take this as an outside narrative.

Saumarez was the best match within three hundred miles—excluding a Lieutenant-Governor who had a wife in England and a liver in Calcutta. He was young for his appointment, drew a respectable number of rupees monthly, played polo without apology, and possessed that clean, finished manner which convinces elderly ladies that a man has principles. He had also a straight nose, a light hand with a horse, and rather too complete a knowledge of both advantages.

We forgave him because the Station was small.

In the rains, when the roads went out in sections and the dak arrived smelling of mildew, we discussed one another. In the cold weather, when strangers came through, we discussed them. In the hot weather, when no decent brain should be required to operate after ten in the morning, we discussed everybody from the Chief Commissioner to the last joined subaltern. A Station is a Select Committee perpetually in session, without minutes, chairman, or power of adjournment.

The Copleigh girls arrived in November to keep house for their father, old Copleigh of the Canal Department, whose ideas on irrigation had been rejected by three Governments and adopted, without acknowledgment, by a fourth. Maud was twenty-four, Edith twenty-one, and they were devoted to each other in that alarming fashion which makes an outsider feel he has interrupted a private service.

Maud was the prettier by all accepted measurements. She had warm brown hair, a colour that survived luncheon, a laugh audible across a tennis-ground, and the invaluable gift of appearing pleased with the person before her. Edith was slighter and quieter. At first one remembered only her grey eyes and the trick she had of looking down before answering; afterwards one remembered exactly what she had said. Their voices were alike enough to deceive anyone who could not see their faces, and, when they rode in broad hats and pale habits, a man meeting them against the sun had to wait for the laugh. If it rang out promptly, the rider was Maud. If there came a pause and then some little edged remark, it was Edith.

That distinction, plain to us in daylight, cost four people a night’s peace and very nearly one life.

Saumarez began to call at the Copleighs’. There was nothing remarkable in that. Every unmarried man called there, and several married ones went with messages from their wives which required an hour to deliver. But Saumarez called systematically. He lent Maud books, reserved dances for Maud, chose Maud’s side at tennis, and once rode nine miles to bring Maud a spray of hill jasmine which had ceased to be jasmine before it arrived.

The Station drafted its Resolution.

Saumarez would marry Maud.

You must not laugh at us. Society in exile lives by conclusions, as a man in the desert lives by water, and is not particular about purity. Mrs. Deane said the matter had been arranged at Simla. The Padre’s wife said she had observed a Spiritual Understanding. Young Prendergast of the Police offered two to one on an engagement before the rains. Even old Copleigh began enlarging a bungalow in his mind and complaining that married officers expected too many shelves.

Only Edith appeared untouched. She was always with Maud, of course, and often with Saumarez; but she moved at the edge of their talk, smiling when appealed to, silent when forgotten. Now and again I caught Saumarez watching her. Since men are idiots where two sisters are concerned, I supposed he was wondering whether she approved of him.

In justice to men, women sometimes take pains to keep us idiots.

The heat came early that year. By April the sky had hardened to brass, the plains trembled from noon till evening, and the crows sat with their beaks open in the tamarinds, too exhausted to abuse the world. We ate, slept, and quarrelled beneath punkahs. The horses went out before sunrise and after moonrise, and anyone who suggested exertion between those hours was regarded as a criminal lunatic.

It was Mrs. Deane who arranged the riding picnic. She had a genius for entertainment and catastrophe, and rarely produced one without the other.

We were to start at nine in the evening, ride to the old tombs beyond the dry river, dine beneath the peepul trees, and return by moonlight. There were fourteen of us, with servants, hampers, lanterns, rugs, and enough soda-water to float a district officer. The moon was nearly full. A wind had been restless all day, but no one minded that. In the hot weather one welcomes any movement in the air, even if it comes to remove the roof.

Saumarez rode beside Maud as we left the Station, and Edith kept with me for the first two miles. She talked too quickly and laughed twice where there was no occasion.

“You are over-tired,” I said, with the heavy tact of an old friend.

“I am perfectly well.”

“That is what people say before fainting.”

“Then I shall try to be original.”

After that she rode ahead and joined her sister. The three moved together in the white dust, their shadows running black and enormous across the road. I remember thinking that Saumarez had the air of a man escorted to his own wedding by two possible brides.

At the tombs we spread dinner in the ruins of a garden built by some forgotten prince who had understood shade better than the Public Works Department. The dome of the chief tomb shone like bone in the moonlight. Bats clicked within it; jackals called from the river-bed; and the servants moved among the trees with lanterns that made their white clothes flare and vanish.

We were all more cheerful than was reasonable. Heat and moonlight loosen English tongues abroad. Mrs. Deane sang half a song; the Padre told a story unsuitable to his coat; and young Prendergast attempted to open champagne with a cavalry sabre. Through it all Saumarez was grave and absent, and I saw him once stand alone by the ruined wall, looking towards the sisters.

He had, as I learned later, drafted his Resolution. He had considered the matter for six weeks, balanced objections, reviewed precedents, and formed a Select Committee of One. This is how an efficient man goes mad.

At eleven the wind dropped.

The leaves turned their pale undersides and hung still. Far away, beyond the river, a brown bar lay across the stars.

One of the servants touched my elbow. “Andhi, sahib.”

I saw the cooks tying cloths over the baskets and the syces catching at the bridles.

Mrs. Deane said, “A dust-storm? How delicious!”

That is the kind of thing people say before Nature answers them personally.

The brown bar rose. It climbed without sound at first, blotting out the low stars and then the high ones, until half the world was gone. The moon burned red above it. The horses lifted their heads, snuffing, and one began to back among the plates.

Then the wind struck.

It came across the river-bed like the advance of artillery—roaring, lashing, and carrying half the plain before it. The trees bent with a single crack of leaves. Lanterns went out. Rugs, napkins, hats, and paper plates sprang into the air and were whirled towards the tombs. Someone shouted to hold the horses; someone else screamed that the hampers were rolling downhill; and Prendergast, who had found no better use for his sabre, cut a guy-rope and brought the dinner-cloth over the Padre’s head.

In ten seconds there was no moon, no road, no party, and, so far as the senses could testify, no British Empire.

Dust filled the mouth and eyes. It drove like hot shot against the face and crept beneath collar, cuff, and pagri. The peepul branches thrashed above us; the horses plunged and snorted; women called names that the wind tore apart; and the blackness moved—solid, choking, alive. Lightning flickered somewhere behind the dust, not in strokes but in vast red pulses, as though the Pit had opened and shut its furnace doors.

I caught a bridle, lost it, collided with a tree, and embraced, successively, a frightened bearer and the Padre. Neither thanked me. Then a woman’s voice cried close at hand—

“I WANT to go home!”

It was Edith, as I believed, though in that uproar one might have mistaken Gabriel for a railway guard.

“Stay where you are!” I shouted.

“I WANT to go home!”

A man answered her. I heard Saumarez’s voice, sharp and commanding, and saw for an instant, by the red flare of lightning, two figures pressed into the lee of the tomb wall. Then the dust shut them away.

I turned towards the horses, where confusion had become mutiny. A syce was hanging to Maud’s mare; Prendergast’s Waler had broken loose; Mrs. Deane sat calmly on a hamper with her skirt over her head; and old Copleigh, who had not attended the picnic, was being loudly blamed for all of it.

There came a lull—not quiet, but a drawing of breath before the next assault. Through it I heard Saumarez say, very distinctly—

“I have tried not to tell you. I cannot. I love you. Will you marry me?”

A woman sobbed once and answered, “Yes.”

There are moments when one knows that the Station has been justified and feels no pleasure in it. I looked away. The wind returned, the trees bowed, and the whole night went mad again.

Five minutes later—perhaps fifteen; clocks have no authority in a dust-storm—someone clutched my arm. Saumarez’s face showed pale in a flicker of lightning. His eyes were full of dust and horror.

“Come here,” he said.

“I am here.”

“Not here—away from them.”

He dragged me behind a broken screen of masonry and gripped both my shoulders. He was shaking. Saumarez, who had played the last chukker of a cup-tie with a cracked collar-bone, shook like a novice before inspection.

“I’ve proposed to the wrong one!”

“What?”

“The wrong one! I thought it was Edith. It was Maud.”

For once in my life I had no maxim ready. I opened my mouth, and the dust filled it.

“She said yes,” he went on. “She put her arms round me. Then the lightning came, and I saw—HER.”

“You proposed to Maud?”

“I have told you!”

“But everyone expected—”

“Everyone is a fool. I love Edith. I have always loved Edith.”

This was unfair, because he had taken great pains to instruct everyone otherwise; but a man in extremity blames the nearest public.

“Where is Maud now?”

“With Mrs. Deane. Happy—Good God, you should have seen her face! I must tell her at once.”

“You will do nothing of the kind.”

“I will tell her it was a mistake.”

“You will tell a woman, five minutes after she has accepted you, that you mistook her for her sister?”

“Yes.”

“Then I shall hit you with a stone.”

Do not suppose I reasoned nobly. I had no more plan than a goat in a flooded nullah. I only knew that certain things are not done, and that if they must be done they should not be done in public, in a storm, beside the remains of a trifle and fourteen witnesses.

Saumarez clawed at my sleeve. “Edith heard.”

“How do you know?”

“She was near us. I saw her when the lightning came.”

At that instant a horse broke from the group with a squeal and a hammering of hoofs. A rider bent low over its neck. The next red pulse showed Edith’s pale habit flying against the darkness.

“Miss Edith!” a syce cried.

She was gone towards the river road.

Saumarez made for his horse. I held him.

“Let me go!”

“And leave Maud here while you gallop after her sister? The Station would have to invent a new Commandment.”

“She’ll kill herself.”

“Stay with Maud.”

I do not know why he obeyed. Perhaps my hand was on his bridle; perhaps, beneath all his selfishness, he saw what his going would proclaim. I mounted the first horse that could be persuaded to face the wind. It happened to be Prendergast’s Waler, a long, hard-mouthed brute that disliked me and civilisation equally.

“I’ll bring her back,” I said.

This was pure boasting. I could not see the road.

We went down into the storm.

The Waler fought for his head, and I let him have enough to choose the ground. Dust whirled in columns, broke, and whirled again. The dry branches came flying out of nowhere. Once a sheet of thorn-scrub rolled across our path like some many-legged beast, and the horse rose beneath me. Lightning pulsed behind the murk, turning every grain of dust blood-red, then dead black. There was no horizon. Earth and sky had been ground together, and we rode through the mill.

I listened for hoofs. The roaring swallowed everything; then, in a lull, I caught the faint hard beat ahead.

“Edith!”

No answer.

We crossed the dry channel at a slant, sliding among loose stones. On the farther bank the Waler stumbled to his knees, recovered, and bolted. I had branches across my face, reins burning through my fingers, and the firm conviction that the Day of Judgment had come by circular order and omitted to state the place of assembly.

Again I heard hoofs—nearer now, irregular.

“EDITH!”

A cry came back, thin as a lost soul’s.

“I WANT to go home!”

She did not mean the Copleigh bungalow. I knew that much, though I knew nothing else.

Her mare showed suddenly in front of us, swerved, and almost fell. I dragged the Waler across her path. Edith struck at him with her whip.

“Let me pass!”

“Your mare is lame.”

“I don’t care.”

“She does.”

“I WANT to go home!”

“Yes, yes. All Englishwomen want to go home in April. Get down.”

She tried to wheel away. The mare took three steps and stood trembling, her near foreleg held clear of the ground. Edith slipped from the saddle—or fell; I cannot say—and faced me with both hands clenched. Dust had plastered her hair across her cheeks. She looked very young and very terrible.

“You heard?” I shouted.

“I heard enough.”

“He thought you were Maud.”

She laughed. There was no amusement in it.

“Of course.”

“He meant to ask you.”

“Of course.”

“He loves you.”

“OF COURSE!”

Each answer cut like a whip. Then she turned away and leaned her forehead against the mare’s saddle.

I continued, because Providence, when compelled to use a middle-aged bachelor, must accept clumsy instruments.

“He has loved you all along. He behaved like a fool to hide it.”

“He made Maud love him.”

“I don’t know.”

“I do.”

This is the point at which the woman’s story begins, and I cannot give it. What passed between the sisters in looks, silences, denials, and those midnight councils from which men are excluded may have altered every fact I carried. Perhaps Maud knew. Perhaps Edith had sacrificed herself. Perhaps each believed she was shielding the other. A man sees two women kiss and thinks the matter simple.

“He has asked her,” Edith said. “She accepted him. That ends it.”

“Nothing ended tonight. Nothing could. The world itself has gone out of order.”

“He must marry her.”

“Would you marry a man who loved Maud?”

She lifted her head at that.

It was a brutal question, but brutality sometimes enters where tact sits knocking. The wind slackened. The dust still streamed past us, but overhead a ragged space opened, and one star looked down with the indecency of a spectator.

“He cannot humiliate her,” Edith said.

“He has done that already, though she does not know it. If he marries her, he will repeat the humiliation every day.”

She covered her face. I heard no sobbing; only the small, exhausted breath of someone who has held herself rigid too long.

“He called me Edith,” she whispered.

“When?”

“After she said yes. The wind took it. Maud did not hear.”

There it was—the entire case, appeal, and judgment in one sentence.

We stood among the flying dust while the mare trembled and the Waler tried to bite her. Then Edith said, “Take me back.”

We led the lame mare and rode by turns. The storm travelled eastward, grumbling like a defeated army. Behind it the moon returned, blurred and yellow; then the stars came out by companies. The plain had changed colour. Broken branches lay across the track, and the dust covered everything in one ghostly livery—horse, rider, thorn, and stone.

Near dawn the air grew almost cold. Edith rode before me on the Waler, straight-backed and silent. I walked beside the mare and wondered what I had done. There are men born to administer districts, men born to command regiments, and men born to interfere in love-affairs. The last should be drowned early.

At the tombs the party had reassembled beneath the trees. They looked like survivors of a siege—faces grey, clothes torn, hair full of leaves, dignity abandoned. Mrs. Deane had recovered her hat and lost one shoe. The Padre held a lantern though daylight was gathering. Maud sat on the low wall wrapped in Saumarez’s coat.

She was beautiful.

I say it because it is true, and because no one has ever properly remembered her in this affair. Happiness had cleared and softened her face. She watched the road with shining eyes, believing, as she had every right to believe, that her future had been settled.

Then she saw Edith.

Saumarez moved before anyone spoke. His face was drawn and dirty, and he walked like a man going to execution. Maud rose. Edith slipped from the saddle.

For one second the three stood together.

What Maud saw in her sister’s face, or in his, I cannot tell. Her own expression altered—only a little, only once. She put out a hand as though to steady herself against the wall.

Saumarez went past her.

He took Edith by both shoulders and kissed her before us all.

It was an outrageous act—un-English, unregulated, and wholly conclusive.

Someone began to clap. I believe it was Mrs. Deane. Prendergast followed, then the Padre, and in a moment the ghostly company beneath the peepuls were applauding as though the curtain had fallen upon a comedy. Perhaps relief made us cruel. Perhaps we wished to force a happy ending upon what we did not understand. At dawn, after a storm, mankind will applaud almost anything that proves the world continues.

Edith hid her face against Saumarez’s coat.

Maud stood very still.

Then Mrs. Deane crossed to her, put an arm about her waist, and spoke too low for me to hear. Maud smiled. It was a perfect Station smile—brave, easy, and fit to be discussed over breakfast. If you ask me what it cost, I cannot tell you.

We rode home in newly arranged couples. Saumarez went with Edith. Mrs. Deane kept Maud beside her. The Padre took charge of the lame mare. I returned Prendergast’s Waler, who had bitten me twice and saved one life, and was thanked as though I had borrowed a newspaper.

By eight o’clock the Station knew there had been an engagement. By luncheon it knew there had been a misunderstanding. By evening it had decided that Saumarez and Edith had always been intended for each other and that Maud had never seriously cared for him. This last conclusion was necessary to our comfort, and therefore became official.

Saumarez married Edith in the cold weather. They were happy, I think; but happiness within marriage is another department, and I have never held that appointment.

As for Maud, she went to the Hills, and later there were reports of a Major in the Guides, a man with a scar and private means. Perhaps she married him. Perhaps she did not. The Station preferred that she should, because society dislikes an unpaid account.

I have told you what Saumarez confessed, what Edith cried in the storm, and what we applauded beneath the trees. But I do not know what Maud said to Edith afterwards, nor why she smiled at dawn, nor whether the wrong proposal was altogether a mistake in her eyes.

That is the missing account—the woman’s version.

Without it, this story may be true.

It may also be all wrong.

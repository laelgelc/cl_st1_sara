## THE ROYAL CARPET

At nine o’clock on Thursday morning there were forty-two carpet-tackers at work in the State Capitol.

At nine-fifteen there were none.

The transformation occurred without noise, violence, or even undue haste. One man ceased tapping in the Senate Chamber; two more straightened themselves painfully in the House; six abandoned the east corridor; and the remainder came in from stairways, committee rooms, reception rooms and remote official apartments, carrying their hammers with the solemnity of soldiers stacking arms.

They assembled under the rotunda.

Mr. Silas Crump, who had been a carpet-tacker for eighteen years and a labour leader since breakfast, mounted a roll of crimson Wilton.

“Boys,” he said, “we are now Local Number One of the United Carpet-Fitters, Stretchers, Layers and Tackers of this State.”

The boys applauded.

“Furthermore,” said Mr. Crump, “we got rights.”

They applauded the rights.

“Furthermore, we want double pay from this minute until the royal feet has passed over the last yard.”

That was applauded most of all.

Five minutes later a delegation waited upon Amos Peacock, Superintendent of Public Buildings, who had not yet heard of the union and had never suspected that carpet-tackers possessed a collective soul.

“Double pay!” cried Peacock. “Why?”

“Emergency work,” said Crump.

“You knew it was emergency work when you took it.”

“We didn’t have no organization then.”

“You didn’t have one twenty minutes ago.”

“We got one now.”

Mr. Crump said it quietly. He had taken courage from the naked floor. It was an astonishingly bad floor—splintered, stained, scarred by generations of desks, boots, cuspidors and public questions. To leave that floor uncovered for Prince Ludwig of Hohenwald was to expose the Commonwealth in its stockings.

The Prince was due at four.

Superintendent Peacock looked at the floor, looked at the clocks, looked at Mr. Crump, and went to the Governor.

Governor Temple was dictating a proclamation on Forestry Week. He was a large, square gentleman with a fighting chin and a collar which made surrender physiologically difficult.

“They have done what?” he demanded.

“Organized, sir.”

“Overnight?”

“Since nine o’clock.”

“And they want double wages?”

“Until the Prince has walked over the carpets.”

The Governor removed his spectacles.

“Peacock, no man may hold up this State by sitting on its Brussels.”

“They aren’t sitting on it, Governor. They are standing around it.”

“Same principle. Tell them no.”

Peacock went out, returned, and said, “They say very well.”

“What do they mean by that?”

“They mean they will not work.”

The Governor’s chin advanced.

“Hire others.”

“We have telephoned every firm in the city. Crump’s men got to them first.”

“Bring men from outside.”

“The train would not get them here before three-ten.”

“Then use floor cloth.”

“There isn’t enough.”

“Paint the boards.”

“It would not dry.”

For the first time the Governor looked troubled. He walked to a window and saw the reviewing stand below, the flags along the avenue, the cavalry horses being rubbed to an official brilliance, and the bandmaster conducting an imaginary march with both arms. Everything was ready for royalty except the floors.

“Double wages!” said the Governor. “It is blackmail with a tack hammer.”

Secretary of State Wilbur Ames, who had entered during the crisis, coughed discreetly.

“Governor, could we not—merely as a practical matter—”

“No.”

“But the Prince—”

“If Prince Ludwig cannot admire a free people on bare boards, he is no democrat.”

“I believe he is a prince,” said Ames.

“All the more reason to educate him.”

Yet the Governor knew that the State could not educate a prince by inviting him to trip over carpet rolls. He called a conference for ten o’clock.

It was a distinguished conference. The Attorney-General came, and the Treasurer, and the Speaker of the House, and the President of the Senate. Chief Justice Bell arrived in robes because he had been interrupted in court. Senator Arnold came because he went everywhere. Harry Weston came because everybody sent for Harry when something impossible had to be made popular.

Weston was young for a political manager and cheerful for any kind of manager. He had an engaging grin, an inexhaustible supply of fresh collars, and the dangerous habit of making other men believe that his ideas had originally been theirs.

The situation was explained.

“Pretty tight,” he observed.

“Outrageous,” said the Attorney-General.

“Unconstitutional,” said the Treasurer.

“Against public policy,” said the Chief Justice.

“Can we arrest them?” asked the Speaker.

“For what?” said the Attorney-General.

“For being carpet-tackers at a time like this.”

“No statute covers it.”

“Then what good,” demanded the Speaker, “is the legislature?”

This question had often been asked, but never by the Speaker.

Weston strolled to the Senate Chamber. The others followed. Rolls of green carpet lay about like fallen columns. The floor stretched broad, bare and humiliating before them.

“How many men does it take?” Weston asked.

“Forty,” said Peacock.

“How long?”

“Professionals might finish by half-past three.”

“Could eighty amateurs do it by the same time?”

“They might ruin the carpet.”

“Could they cover the floor?”

“In a manner of speaking.”

“Then,” said Weston, “let us speak in that manner.”

The Governor stared at him.

“You propose that we lay it ourselves?”

“I propose that the State government go to the floor for the honour of the State.”

Senator Arnold bent down, picked up a tack hammer, tested its balance and struck a tack into a convenient crack. The tack went in. So did a portion of the Senator’s thumb.

He rose with dignity.

“There is nothing,” he said, concealing the thumb behind his back, “inconsistent between statesmanship and skilled labour.”

That decided it.

Messengers ran through the Capitol. Telephones rang. Committee hearings stopped in the middle of testimony. The House adjourned while Representative Dooley was speaking, an event which six previous Speakers had attempted without success. Judges postponed decisions, commissioners abandoned statistics, clerks came out of their offices, and prominent citizens were summoned from banks, clubs, law firms and luncheon counters.

The call was simple:

THE CAPITOL MUST BE CARPETED BY FOUR. BRING A HAMMER.

There was magic in it.

At ten-thirty the State-house resembled a workshop which had unexpectedly elected itself. Republicans and Democrats, reformers and regulars, wet men and dry men, corporation lawyers and enemies of corporations swarmed through the chambers. Some brought hammers; some brought enthusiasm; a few brought advice. The advice was plentiful and, as usual, hardest to use.

Harry Weston stood on the clerk’s desk in the House.

“Gentlemen,” he cried, “this is no time for party lines!”

Representative Mulrooney looked at the carpet.

“It’s full of lines,” he objected.

“Those are to be matched,” said Weston. “Party lines are to be forgotten.”

He divided them into squads. The Senate took its own chamber, on the theory that its members understood its irregularities. The House tackled the larger room, maintaining that anything the Senate could do slowly the House could do loudly. Judges were assigned to the Supreme Court rooms; department heads to the corridors; bankers to the grand staircase, because they were accustomed to high places; and newspaper men nowhere at all, because they refused to work while there was anything to report.

The first fifteen minutes were chiefly constitutional debate.

“Do we stretch north and south?”

“East and west.”

“Why?”

“Precedent.”

“What precedent?”

“Every carpet I ever saw.”

“That is custom, not precedent.”

The Chief Justice settled the point by ordering both sides to pull. The carpet stretched diagonally.

After that they learned.

Coats came off. Collars wilted. Suspenders appeared in public life. Men who had not knelt since childhood dropped to their knees and discovered that the human body contains joints which political office does not exercise. The air filled with taps, thumps, grunts and exhortations.

“Hold her!”

“I am holding her!”

“Not you—the carpet!”

“Who took my hammer?”

“You were using mine.”

“I deny it.”

“You’ve got my initials on the handle.”

“Then your initials have been using my hand.”

Stretch—pull—flatten—and tack, tack, tack!

Governor Temple moved through the building like a field commander. He had tied a white handkerchief around his collar, removed his coat, and acquired a long scratch across his left cheek.

“No wrinkles!” he ordered. “This administration will not be judged by wrinkles.”

“What about the west corridor?” Peacock asked.

“Put the opposition there.”

“Why?”

“They are experienced in smoothing things over.”

At eleven o’clock the union delegation returned to see how surrender was progressing.

It was not progressing.

Mr. Crump stood in the doorway of the House and watched the Lieutenant-Governor crawl backward under a desk while three legislators bounced on a stubborn rise in the carpet. The dignity of the scene impressed him less than its noise.

“You fellows are spoiling good goods,” he said.

The Lieutenant-Governor emerged, dusty but triumphant.

“They belong to the State.”

“And you ain’t laying them right.”

“They belong to the State when laid wrong, too.”

Crump examined the edge professionally.

“You’ll never make four o’clock.”

“Then,” said the Lieutenant-Governor, “we shall abolish four o’clock.”

Crump withdrew to consult his men.

The strikers had expected a darkened Capitol, terrified officials and a Governor begging for terms. Instead, they found a festival. More volunteers arrived every minute. Old political enemies shook the same carpet. Men who had not spoken for ten years shouted intimate insults at one another and enjoyed it. The President of the Teamsters’ Union, who came to inspect the labour controversy, was handed a hammer by Harry Weston and put in charge of six bank directors before he could escape.

“Now, see here,” he protested, “I’m union.”

“So are they,” said Weston. “Union of bank directors.”

“A bank director ain’t labour.”

“He will be in five minutes.”

The teamster president looked at the directors. They looked helpless. His face softened.

“You boys ever drive a tack?”

One of them admitted that he had once driven a nail into a packing case.

“Lord!” said the teamster president. “All right. Watch me.”

By noon he was the most energetic man in the north corridor.

At twelve-fifteen Bridget Haggerty took command of refreshments. Bridget supervised the Capitol cleaning women, six nieces, two aldermen and her husband, though Mr. Haggerty did not know it. She established coffee urns beneath the rotunda and sent baskets of sandwiches into every room.

“Eat!” she cried. “You can’t save a State on an empty stomach, though many of you have tried governing one with an empty head.”

No one resented Bridget. A Chief Justice may rebuke an attorney, a Governor may dismiss a commissioner, but only a woman with hot coffee can tell an entire government the truth and receive thanks for it.

While all this was occurring, Senator Felix Bruner was engaged in an enterprise of his own.

Bruner was a United States senator, a patriot, a practical joker and a man who considered secrecy wasted unless it led to a revelation. He had been placed in charge of receiving Prince Ludwig at the railway station at four o’clock. At eleven he telephoned Colonel Voss, the Prince’s military secretary, who had arrived with the royal party at a country estate twenty miles away.

“Colonel,” he said, “does His Highness truly wish to understand America?”

“That is his profound desire.”

“Then bring him in at two.”

“But the ceremony is at four.”

“Exactly.”

“I do not comprehend.”

“The ceremony is what we want him to see. Two o’clock is what we are.”

There was a pause.

“Is this proper?” asked Colonel Voss.

“No,” said Bruner. “That is why it will be valuable.”

The arrangement was made. The Prince’s motorcar would enter through the service gate. Bruner would meet him at the old tunnel beneath the east wing and conduct him unseen to the gallery above the rotunda.

Senator Bruner then joined the carpet-layers and said nothing.

At one o’clock the Capitol had passed from confusion to efficiency. Hands had blistered, tempers had warmed, and skill had appeared. The Secretary of Agriculture could judge a straight seam at twenty feet. A former Governor developed genius with a stretcher. The minority leader, who had opposed every measure introduced by the majority leader for twelve years, discovered that the two of them could fit a corner only by trusting each other.

“Pull when I say,” said the majority leader.

“You always say that.”

“This isn’t a bill. Pull!”

They pulled. The corner fitted.

The two men sat back and regarded it.

“We’ve been fools,” said the minority leader.

“About what?”

“This corner.”

“Oh,” said the majority leader.

Reconciliation has its limits.

In the Senate Chamber the elderly Senator Arnold worked beside young Representative Pike, who hoped to defeat him at the next election. Arnold’s knees ached; Pike’s hammering was violent and inaccurate.

“Young man,” said Arnold, “you strike as you speak.”

“How is that?”

“Often, hard and not quite on the point.”

Pike laughed.

“And you hold the tack as you hold office, Senator.”

“How is that?”

“Longer than anybody expects.”

The Senator considered this. Then he laughed too. Ten minutes later he showed Pike how to angle a tack beneath the baseboard, and Pike, who had never taken advice from him in public, took it gladly on the floor.

There should have been a historian present. There were reporters, of course, but reporters were occupied with the scratches, the torn trousers and the Attorney-General’s collision with a paste bucket. A historian might have noted the greater marvel: men changed positions without changing principles, accepted orders without demanding patronage, and performed useful labour without appointing an investigating commission.

At one-forty-five Senator Bruner slipped away.

He met the royal motorcar at the service gate. Prince Ludwig stepped out wearing a plain grey coat and an expression of intense relief. He was a tall, fair young man with a trained moustache and tired eyes.

“No band?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“No schoolchildren?”

“They believe you are twenty miles away.”

“No address of welcome?”

“I have it in my pocket.”

The Prince glanced at Colonel Voss.

“Senator, I like your country already.”

They entered through the tunnel, climbed a narrow iron stair and reached the circular gallery above the rotunda. A dusty curtain concealed them. Through a slit they could look down upon the great central floor.

The Prince looked.

Below him, the government of the State was eating sandwiches on carpet rolls.

Governors, senators, judges, mayors, clerks, labour leaders, millionaires and janitors sat in friendly disorder. Some were black with dust; some had bandaged fingers; one eminent jurist had a streak of paste in his hair. Bridget Haggerty moved among them with coffee. The Governor stood on an upturned bucket, speaking.

“Fellow workers,” he said, “we have two hours left.”

“Less eating, more tacking!” shouted someone.

“That is the substance of my remarks,” said the Governor.

They rose with a cheer.

The Prince leaned forward.

“Is this prepared for me?”

“The carpets are,” whispered Bruner. “The rest is an accident.”

“Who are those two men?”

“Political enemies.”

“They are carrying the same roll.”

“Tomorrow they may deny it.”

“And that gentleman without his collar?”

“The Governor.”

“The Governor?”

“He mislaid his dignity in the House Chamber. It may be under a desk.”

Prince Ludwig smiled. Then he laughed. It was the first unroyal laugh he had enjoyed in three weeks.

From their hidden gallery they watched the work recommence. The building rang again.

Pull—stretch—steady—and tap, tap, tap!

A judge crawled beneath a radiator. A corporation president held tacks in his mouth while an anti-corporation senator hammered. The teamsters’ leader scolded six bankers for loafing. Mr. Crump and three of the striking carpet-tackers stood at the edge of the rotunda, wearing the uncertain expressions of men whose grievance had become a community picnic without them.

Crump watched the Attorney-General struggle with a seam.

“That’s upside down,” he said at last.

The Attorney-General looked up.

“Which?”

“The strip. The nap runs the other way.”

“We meant it so.”

“You did not.”

“No,” admitted the Attorney-General. “We did not.”

Crump removed his coat.

“Gimme that stretcher.”

The other strikers stared.

“What about the union?” one asked.

“The union can keep its principles,” said Crump. “I’m saving the carpet.”

He dropped to his knees. In another moment all four were at work. No agreement was signed, no wage scale conceded, no victory announced. They simply could not endure bad craftsmanship.

The Prince nudged Bruner.

“The strikers have joined?”

“Temporarily.”

“Who won?”

Bruner considered.

“The floor, I reckon.”

At two-thirty the Senate Chamber was done. The Senate marched into the House to offer assistance and criticism. The House rejected the criticism but accepted the assistance. At three the corridors were complete. Only the rotunda remained, and there the great circular carpet had to be fitted around pillars, registers and the marble base of the statue of General Arbuthnot.

General Arbuthnot had stood in bronze for sixty years with one arm extended toward the west. No one knew why. Now his gesture seemed to say, “Pull it that way.”

They did.

The Prince had watched for more than an hour. His cheeks were flushed, and every time he saw a hammer idle he shifted impatiently.

“Senator,” he whispered, “there are wrinkles near that pillar.”

“His Highness has an eye.”

“They are pulling unevenly.”

“Amateurs.”

“I have laid carpet.”

Bruner turned.

“You?”

“During military service. Our colonel believed officers should know everything soldiers did.”

Below, Harry Weston called for six more men.

The Prince took off his coat.

“Your Highness!” breathed Colonel Voss.

“I cannot watch incompetence in silence.”

“You will be seen.”

“That,” said the Prince, “has been the object of my journey.”

Before either man could stop him, he came through the curtain, descended the narrow stair and stepped from a small door behind the statue.

For several seconds no one noticed him. Royalty, without decorations, is at a disadvantage in a crowd of shirt sleeves.

He went directly to the wrinkled carpet.

“Not so hard on the left,” he said. “You must ease it around the pillar.”

Senator Arnold handed him a stretcher.

“Then ease it.”

The Prince knelt and did so. He worked expertly.

“Where’d you learn that?” asked Representative Pike.

“In the army.”

“Regular?”

“For a time.”

“You with the visiting party?”

“Yes.”

“Foreign, ain’t you?”

“Yes.”

“Well, you got sense. Hold that edge.”

The Prince held it.

Colonel Voss appeared behind the statue looking as though monarchy had fallen down the stairs. Senator Bruner followed, enjoying himself indecently.

The Prince hammered three tacks. Then Governor Temple came across the rotunda, wiping his face.

“Weston, who’s the new man?”

Weston looked. His mouth opened.

The Governor looked again.

His hand went instinctively to his collar and found none.

“Your—Your Highness!”

Every hammer stopped.

Silence moved outward in circles. The Attorney-General removed two tacks from his mouth. The teamster president stood at attention. Bridget Haggerty, who had never met a prince, curtsied to the paste bucket and recovered quickly.

Prince Ludwig remained on one knee, holding the stretcher.

“I beg you,” he said, “do not stop. This wrinkle is almost conquered.”

Nobody moved.

The Governor attempted to remember his welcoming address. What he remembered was the paragraph comparing the State’s mountains to the everlasting friendship of nations. There were no mountains at hand, only a prince on the carpet and a hundred citizens looking as if they had been shaken out of a laundry bag.

“Your Highness,” he began, “the people of this Commonwealth—”

“Are doing excellent work,” said the Prince.

The Governor looked around.

They were. The bare, miserable boards had vanished beneath deep colours. The chambers and corridors had been transformed. His people, however, had not been improved ceremonially. The Speaker’s sleeve was torn. The Chief Justice’s knees were grey. Harry Weston had lost his fresh collar at last.

“We had expected,” said the Governor, “to receive you under somewhat different conditions.”

“So had I,” said the Prince. “I am glad we were both disappointed.”

That rescued everybody.

Someone laughed. Someone else applauded. Then the rotunda burst into cheers—not the measured cheers of a reception committee, but a loud, ragged, hammer-waving roar.

Prince Ludwig rose and bowed. Bridget Haggerty pushed through the crowd and handed him coffee.

“It’s been standing,” she said, “but princes can’t expect everything.”

He drank it.

“Madam, it is magnificent.”

“It is not. But you’re civil.”

The Governor regained command.

“Gentlemen—and Bridget—we still have forty minutes.”

“Thirty-eight,” called Superintendent Peacock.

“Then to work!”

They went back to it, now with royalty in the centre squad. Prince Ludwig stretched; the Governor held; Crump directed; Senator Arnold hammered; Pike supplied tacks; and Bruner sat on the base of General Arbuthnot’s statue offering advice until Bridget ordered him down to work.

At three-forty-seven the last tack went in.

At three-fifty the workers stood in the completed rotunda and surveyed their achievement. It was not perfect. There was a doubtful seam near the east arch, a slight bulge outside the Treasurer’s office, and one strip in the House which Crump said would haunt him to his grave. But it was down, it was handsome, and it belonged unmistakably to them.

At four o’clock the band outside began the royal march.

The official car, empty except for Colonel Voss’s hat, approached the main entrance according to schedule. The reception committee lined up hastily. Coats had been brushed, faces washed and collars borrowed, though several prominent men still carried bandaged thumbs.

Prince Ludwig insisted upon entering by the front door with the rest of them.

The speeches were shortened. The parade was shortened. The formal banquet that evening was not shortened, because formal banquets cannot be, but the Prince endured it cheerfully. Whenever an orator praised international fellowship, he looked down the table at the Governor’s bruised finger, Crump’s clean collar, Bruner’s secret smile and Bridget Haggerty, who had somehow been invited and was telling the ambassador to eat his potatoes.

Near midnight Senator Bruner asked the Prince what had most impressed him in America—the Capitol, the cavalry, the factories, the schools, the speeches or the banquet.

The Prince considered.

“I think,” he said, “a nation is most interesting before it has time to pose.”

Then he glanced toward the long corridor, where two old political enemies were on their knees pressing down a seam that had risen during dinner.

“And perhaps,” he added, “the best view of a free country is obtained when it does not know anyone is looking.”

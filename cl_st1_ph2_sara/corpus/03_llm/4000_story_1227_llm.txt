Once there was a milkmaid named Drusilla, who lived with her father in a cottage at the edge of the prettiest meadow you ever saw.

The cottage had a green door, two little windows curtained with white dimity, and a roof so thickly thatched that moss and yellow stonecrop grew upon it. A pink rose climbed over the chimney and dropped its petals down the chimney-pot whenever the wind was in the west, which sometimes made Drusilla’s father sneeze at breakfast. Behind the cottage stood six beehives, a crooked apple tree, and a shed containing a wheelbarrow, three rakes, and a very remarkable cow.

She was white—whiter than cream, snow, or a court lady’s shoulder—and her horns were of solid gold. Her eyes were soft and brown, her hoofs polished like black satin, and the tassel of her tail was tied each morning with a blue ribbon. She gave twelve silver pails of milk every day, and the milk tasted as lilies smell, only more nourishing. Physicians sent for it when dukes became pale from eating too many truffles, and rich ladies drank it from porcelain cups to improve their complexions.

Drusilla and her father were therefore comfortably provided for, though not rich; for even wonderful milk must be delivered, strained, measured, and paid for, and there are always persons who promise to settle their accounts on Tuesday and forget to say which Tuesday.

Drusilla rose every morning when the dew was still shining on the grass. She put on a blue petticoat, a white gown, and a broad straw hat, and went to the shed with a bunch of rose leaves for the cow.

“Good morning, Snowflower,” she would say.

Snowflower would turn her golden horns carefully, so as not to scratch the door, and answer, “Moo,” which meant anything from good morning to the weather is changing, according to the occasion.

Drusilla milked her, brushed her, polished her horns with chamois leather, and led her into the meadow. Afterward she cooked porridge for her dear old father, mended his stockings, swept the floor, and set a bowl of wild flowers upon the table. She was as industrious as she was pretty, and she was very pretty indeed. Her hair was brown with golden glints in it, her eyes were blue, and her cheeks had the delicate color of apple-blossoms just before rain.

Her father often said that she ought to marry a prince.

“No, Father,” Drusilla always replied. “Who would polish Snowflower’s horns?”

“Princes generally employ somebody for that,” said her father.

“But they might employ the wrong person.”

This argument could not be answered.

One afternoon in June, Drusilla led Snowflower into the meadow, where the buttercups were so thick that the whole field appeared to be wearing a cloth of gold. A brook ran through the grass, whispering over green stones, and wild roses leaned above it to look at themselves in the water.

Drusilla had been awake since four o’clock, for Snowflower had required an especially elaborate brushing. She sat beneath a willow, meaning to rest for one minute. The bees hummed, the brook whispered, and the cow cropped buttercups with the absent-minded expression common to cows and philosophers. Drusilla shut her eyes.

When she opened them, the sun was low.

“Snowflower!” she cried.

There was no answer.

The meadow was empty. Only a crushed blue ribbon lay beside the brook.

Drusilla searched behind the willow, though even an enchanted cow can scarcely hide behind a tree no wider than a milk pail. She searched among the roses, along the brook, across the hill, and beneath a blackberry hedge. She called until the thrushes began to repeat “Snowflower!” in a manner that was not at all helpful.

At last she ran toward the road. Just as she reached it, there came a splendid jingling and clattering. First rode twelve trumpeters in pink satin. Next came six noblemen upon gray palfreys with silver housings. Behind them rode the King beneath a green umbrella embroidered with his arms, his initials, his birthday, and a small but flattering portrait of himself.

The King wore a purple coat covered with medals, stars, ribbons, diamond bees, emerald oak leaves, and three jeweled orders which had been presented to him by countries he had never visited. His breast looked rather like a fence on circus day when every handbill in the county has been pasted upon it.

When he saw Drusilla, he reined in his horse. Every nobleman reined in; every palfrey reared; and every trumpeter stopped so suddenly that one of them swallowed a note and hiccuped in B-flat.

“Why do you weep, pretty maiden?” asked the King.

“I have lost our cow, your Majesty.”

“Pardy!” said the King. “Cows are not generally difficult to find.”

“Snowflower is not a general cow. She is white, with golden horns, and gives milk that tastes as lilies smell.”

The King raised his eyebrows. The noblemen raised theirs. Even the horses looked interested, except the King’s horse, who had heard many royal conversations and knew better.

“A gold-horned cow!” said the King. “Such an animal would be an ornament to the kingdom. We shall find her.”

“Oh, thank you, your Majesty!”

“On one condition,” continued the King, who seldom gave anything without attaching a ribbon to it. “You shall become our adopted princess, attend the Royal Seminary for Accomplished Young Ladies, and prepare to marry the Prince of Egypt.”

Drusilla clasped her hands.

“But my father—”

“He may remain here.”

“And Snowflower?”

“Our armies, detectives, huntsmen, astronomers, and Minister of Agriculture shall search for her.”

Drusilla did not wish to be a princess, and she knew nothing of the Prince of Egypt except that his portrait showed him wearing a very tall crown and looking uncomfortable. Yet she longed to find Snowflower, and it seemed disrespectful to refuse a king under an embroidered umbrella.

“I will go, your Majesty,” she said timidly, “if my father consents.”

The King considered that sufficient and ordered three trumpeters to announce it.

Drusilla’s father was astonished when the royal procession reached the cottage. He was also sorrowful, but he believed kings always knew what was best, this being before he had met many of them.

“You must go, my dear,” he said. “Perhaps you will learn where princesses put patches in their stockings.”

“Princesses do not wear patched stockings,” said the King.

“That must be very expensive,” replied the old man.

The next morning Drusilla packed her white gown, blue petticoat, two aprons, a pincushion, and Snowflower’s brush. She kissed her father many times and rode away upon a cream-colored palfrey. The King sent five hundred riders to search the countryside. Notices were nailed to trees, bridges, and one absent-minded bishop. They read:

LOST: ONE WHITE COW  
WITH GOLDEN HORNS.  
ANSWERS TO SNOWFLOWER.  
REWARD: SUITABLE.

Meanwhile Drusilla arrived at the Royal Seminary.

It was a magnificent building of blue marble with sixty windows, forty chimneys, and a gilded bell that rang whenever anybody made a grammatical error. At night the whole building went to sleep: the shutters closed like eyelids, the staircases curled up, and the chimneys snored. At seven each morning it awoke, shook the dew from its roof, and rang all its bells at once, which was thought excellent discipline.

The headmistress was Lady Euphemia Prink, a tall woman in lavender satin. Her principal accomplishments were entering rooms and leaving them. She could enter with majesty, hesitation, sorrow, suspicion, gracious surprise, or an umbrella; and she could leave so impressively that people sometimes applauded before remembering she had forgotten to dismiss them.

There were thirty-seven students. They were daughters of dukes, marquises, ambassadors, extremely successful soap-makers, and one pirate who had retired and become respectable. They wore pink or green gowns, silver slippers, and little lace caps that served no purpose whatever.

Drusilla was taught dancing, deportment, water-color painting, royal genealogy, six kinds of tatting, Kensington stitch, piano variations, and the proper manner of receiving an ostrich feather from a foreign ambassador. She learned to cross a ballroom without appearing to move her feet and to sit upon a gilt chair without leaning against it, even when the chair was exceedingly uncomfortable.

At breakfast the students received a spoonful of oatmeal and half a violet, because Lady Euphemia considered ordinary food injurious to the complexion. Drusilla, who had been accustomed to porridge, brown bread, honey, apples, and six cups of Snowflower’s milk, was often hungry enough to eat the ostrich feather.

Nevertheless, she worked faithfully. Her embroidery was so exquisite that the roses upon it attracted bees. She played seventeen variations of “The Moonlight Minuet” without once losing her place, although several listeners lost theirs. She painted a dish of peaches so naturally that the drawing mistress tried to peel one. She learned the names of three hundred and nineteen royal cousins and could remember which must be addressed as Serene, Illustrious, Translucent, or merely High.

Yet every night she cried a little behind her portière.

She missed her dear old father, the green cottage door, the meadow, and Snowflower’s gentle brown eyes. When the other young ladies whispered about balls, jewels, and eligible princes, Drusilla wondered whether her father had remembered to warm his slippers and whether rain was coming through the thatch.

The King’s search continued. Soldiers explored forests. Sailors dragged ponds. Detectives questioned every butcher and dairyman in the kingdom. The Royal Astronomer looked through his telescope and reported a white object with two golden points; but it proved to be the Queen’s best tiara upon a windowsill.

The Minister of Agriculture issued a book entitled *Reliable Methods for Finding Cows*, in nine volumes, none of which contained a reliable method for finding cows.

Months passed.

Among Drusilla’s schoolmates was Lady Celestina Crimp, who had black curls, green eyes, and a disposition like a pin left in an armchair. She had always been first in ornamental sighing, but Drusilla soon sighed more musically. Celestina’s satin-stitch had been considered unrivaled, but Drusilla stitched lilies that looked almost fragrant. Worst of all, Drusilla was expected to marry the Prince of Egypt, while Celestina was expected to marry nobody more distinguished than a viscount with red whiskers and a passion for turnips.

“She never had a gold-horned cow,” Celestina whispered one evening. “She invented it so that the King would make her a princess.”

The other students were shocked, delighted, and immediately convinced. By supper the cow had silver wings. By bedtime Drusilla had been accused of painting its horns herself. On the following morning, someone told Lady Euphemia that Drusilla kept twelve imaginary cows beneath her bed.

Lady Euphemia reported the matter to the Queen, who mentioned it to the King while he was polishing a medal.

“Nonsense,” said the King.

But a year had passed, and Snowflower had not been found. The neighboring monarchs had begun asking whether his celebrated cow hunt was progressing. One had sent him a toy cow with brass horns, and kings dislike being laughed at even more than ordinary people, because they employ poets to prevent it.

He summoned Drusilla.

She entered in a gown of white satin embroidered with silver cowslips and made the seven prescribed curtsies.

“My dear,” said the King, “did this gold-horned animal truly exist?”

“Yes, your Majesty.”

“Can you prove it?”

“My father saw her. Many people drank her milk.”

“People will drink anything if it is expensive,” said the King. “Pardy, we have searched every stable, forest, cavern, hill, valley, orchard, and respectable pond. No cow.”

“She must be somewhere.”

“That is precisely where we cannot find her.”

Drusilla lifted her blue eyes. “I did not deceive you, your Majesty.”

The King walked up and down, making his decorations clink. He did not really think Drusilla dishonest, but admitting that his entire kingdom had failed to locate one cow seemed a poor royal policy.

“At any rate,” he said, “the Prince of Egypt cannot marry a princess whose cow may be imaginary. You must return home.”

Drusilla’s cheeks flushed, and tears filled her eyes; but beneath her sorrow came a sudden leap of joy. She was going back to her father!

“I am sorry you do not believe me, your Majesty,” she said. “But I am grateful to go home.”

This was not the answer the King expected. He had supposed she would cling to the throne, the jewels, and the Egyptian marriage. Her contentment annoyed him almost as much as her imaginary cow.

She left the seminary the next morning, wearing her old blue petticoat and white gown. No trumpeters accompanied her. The palace supplied a small brown donkey, a packet of sandwiches, and a certificate stating that she had passed Advanced Tatting with Honors.

Her father saw her coming from far down the road. He ran out in one slipper, and Drusilla jumped from the donkey into his arms.

“My dear child!”

“My dear old father!”

They laughed, wept, and talked together until the candles burned low. Drusilla swept the cottage, mended the curtain, made porridge, and put flowers on the table. She had never found any palace half so beautiful.

But the next morning they counted their money.

Without Snowflower’s milk there was very little left. The old man had sold two beehives, the wheelbarrow, and the best rake. The thatch required mending, winter was approaching, and certificates in Advanced Tatting are not readily exchanged for potatoes.

“I can work,” said Drusilla.

“So can I,” said her father, though his hands trembled.

“We shall manage.”

They spoke bravely, but both were troubled. Drusilla went into the meadow to think. The buttercups had faded; the brook ran dark beneath bare willow branches. She found the very place where the blue ribbon had lain and sat down.

Then came a soft, familiar sound.

“Moo.”

Drusilla sprang up.

Across the meadow walked Snowflower!

She was as white as ever, and her golden horns shone in the pale sun. Her tail was tied with a new blue ribbon, wider and more fashionable than the old one. Over her back hung two great wicker baskets. One overflowed with gold coins, pearls, rubies, emeralds, and diamonds; the other contained silver cups, jeweled combs, ropes of coral, and a small crown that appeared to have been packed by mistake.

“Snowflower!” cried Drusilla.

The cow came to her and rested her white head upon Drusilla’s shoulder.

Where had she been? No one ever discovered. There were foreign coins in the baskets, seaweed tangled in one handle, and a feather belonging to a bird found only beyond the Mountains of the Moon. Snowflower herself offered no explanation. Cows are often discreet about their travels.

Drusilla led her home, and her father nearly fell into the rain barrel from astonishment. They embraced Snowflower, polished her horns, fed her rose leaves, and gave her warm bran in the blue china bowl.

News of the return spread rapidly. Before noon, seven villagers had seen the cow. Before sunset, seventy people had seen her arrive in a golden chariot drawn by swans. By the following morning, the King received a message claiming that Snowflower had returned with the crown jewels of twelve empires and a talking parrot.

The King came at once, accompanied by the Queen, thirty nobles, forty soldiers, and the Minister of Agriculture carrying all nine volumes of his book.

At the cottage gate the King reined in. Every nobleman reined in; every horse reared; and the Minister of Agriculture dropped Volume Four into a puddle.

There stood Snowflower, peacefully eating rose leaves. Sunlight glittered upon her golden horns. The treasure baskets rested beside the shed.

“Pardy!” exclaimed the King. “The cow!”

“Yes, your Majesty,” said Drusilla.

“I always believed in her.”

The Queen looked at him.

“That is,” he amended, “I believed in the possibility of her.”

Drusilla curtsied.

“My dear,” said the King, “you have been wronged. You shall return to court this afternoon. We will make you a princess again, and you shall marry the Prince of Egypt next Thursday, unless Wednesday is more convenient.”

“No, thank you, your Majesty.”

The trumpeters lowered their instruments. The nobles stared. Even Snowflower stopped chewing.

“No?” repeated the King.

“I do not wish to be a princess. I do not know the Prince of Egypt, and I should rather stay with my father and care for Snowflower.”

“But there will be diamonds!”

Drusilla glanced at the baskets.

“I have diamonds.”

“Servants!”

“I can sweep my own floor.”

“Banquets!”

“I prefer porridge.”

The King had never heard such dangerous opinions. He turned to the Queen, who was smiling.

“She is quite right,” said the Queen. “Besides, the Prince of Egypt has become engaged to a princess in Arabia who owns a blue camel.”

That settled the matter, for even kings cannot marry one prince to two princesses without creating considerable trouble at the wedding breakfast.

Drusilla kept her treasure, though she gave generously to the poor, repaired the village roofs, and paid every person who had ever purchased Snowflower’s milk and forgotten which Tuesday he intended to settle the account. She bought the meadow, the orchard, and the hill beyond the brook.

Then she built a castle.

It was not the sort of castle architects usually build when given baskets of jewels. It had no black towers, marble staircases, or galleries in which ancestors frowned from tarnished frames. It looked exactly like the old cottage, only much larger. The walls were creamy white; the doors were green; and the roof was thatched, though the thatch concealed electric lights, hot-water pipes, and a lightning conductor shaped like a daisy. Moss and stonecrop grew upon it, and pink roses climbed over every chimney.

Inside were broad hearths, cushioned settles, white dimity curtains, polished tables, blue china, and bowls of wild flowers. Drusilla’s father had a warm room overlooking the meadow, with six pairs of slippers and a special cupboard for honey.

Snowflower’s stable was magnificent. Its floor was tiled in green and white, its manger was silver, and lace curtains hung at the windows. She slept upon fresh moss and dined on rose leaves from porcelain dishes. Her tail ribbon was changed every morning—blue on weekdays, white on Sundays, and gold on royal birthdays, though Snowflower privately preferred blue.

The King and Queen came often to visit. The King liked the lily-tasting milk and usually drank three cups, though he said he came only to inspect the agricultural improvements. The Queen sat with Drusilla beneath the willow and talked about ordinary things, which queens find very refreshing.

For several years the King tried to persuade Drusilla to accept some title.

“Duchess?” he suggested.

“No, thank you, your Majesty.”

“Marchioness?”

“It sounds difficult to spell.”

“Baroness?”

“Snowflower might eat the certificate.”

At last the Queen proposed that she become a countess, explaining that a countess need not leave home, marry anybody, or wear a coronet while milking unless she particularly wished to.

Drusilla consented, chiefly to please them.

Thus she became Countess Drusilla of the Golden Horn, though in the village everyone still called her Drusilla, and Snowflower called her “Moo.”

She lived happily with her dear old father in the rose-covered castle, rising early, wearing her blue petticoat and white gown, and walking each morning through the dew to the splendid stable. She might have had fifty servants to milk Snowflower, but she would never permit it.

“Somebody else might do it wrongly,” she said.

And, as you must know by this time, Drusilla was perfectly right.

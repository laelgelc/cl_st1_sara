On the first warm Sunday of April, Paris awoke with the imprudent gaiety of a convalescent who throws his medicine out of the window.

Spring had entered the city during the night. It shone upon the roofs, trembled in the fresh leaves of the boulevards, floated over the river in a pale golden vapor, and crept beneath one’s coat with a soft, perfumed breath. The chest expanded; the eyes sought smiling faces; the feet refused all reasonable destinations. Even the old houses seemed less severe, as though their gray walls remembered youthful escapades. Shopgirls walked two by two beneath the budding trees, soldiers stared after them, clerks escaped from their offices with the haste of prisoners, and everywhere one heard laughter, fragments of songs, invitations shouted across the street.

That morning I felt capable of everything—of travel, of poetry, of devotion, perhaps even of happiness.

I had no mistress, which is a misfortune in winter and a catastrophe in spring. The sunshine made my solitude conspicuous. It seemed to point at me and cry: “Look at this fellow! The earth is putting on green, the birds have chosen their wives, the river carries couples beneath its bridges, and he is going to lunch alone!”

So I fled toward the Seine.

A steamer was leaving for Saint-Cloud. I went aboard without asking myself why. The deck was already crowded with families bearing baskets, young men in bright neckties, elderly citizens wrapped in flannel despite the warmth, nurses, children, two soldiers, three dogs and a stout lady who had brought an entire cold chicken in a newspaper.

The bell rang. The paddles struck the water; the quay glided away; Paris began to unfold along both banks, softened by sunlight and distance. The river smelled of wet wood, smoke, mud and new leaves. It was not a noble perfume, perhaps, but spring transformed it. Everything became fragrant that day—even the engine.

I stood near the rail, surrendering myself to the intoxication of movement, when I saw her.

She was seated opposite me beside an older woman, evidently a work companion or neighbor. She was one of those charming little Parisian working girls whose hands are never quite at rest. Even while talking she folded and unfolded the ribbon of her parasol. Her dress was modest, dark blue, with a narrow white collar; a black hat sat slightly crooked upon her chestnut hair; and beneath one ear a rebellious curl escaped, delicate and bright as a shaving of copper.

I observed first the curl, then her cheek, then the faint pale down near her temple which the sunlight revealed and which seemed to me, in my condition, more moving than all the masterpieces in the Louvre.

She noticed me looking.

I turned toward the river with the hypocritical gravity of a diplomat. A minute later I looked again. She was looking at me. Her eyes dropped, and a smile passed over her lips—not a laugh, not even a complete smile, but the promise of one, the tender dawn of one.

At once I knew her.

That is to say, I knew nothing whatever about her, but I attributed to her gentleness, courage, delicacy, a little melancholy, a taste for music, filial devotion, and the capacity to understand my soul. Such revelations require no evidence in April.

She whispered something to her companion. They both glanced at me. The older one became grave, while the young woman bit her lip to restrain her amusement. I interpreted this as favorable. Youth interprets everything as favorable until a door is shut in its face.

I began to prepare my approach. Should I ask whether the wind inconvenienced her? Offer my place, though she already possessed one? Pick up something she had not dropped? Boldness, tact, chance—love depends upon these three, and chance is usually the least reliable.

I had just advanced one foot when a voice beside me said:

“Do not do it, monsieur.”

I turned. A man of about forty-five stood at my elbow. He was thin, yellowish, carefully dressed and profoundly melancholy. His whiskers descended in two mournful lines toward his collar. Under one arm he carried a green office portfolio, and in his hand he held an umbrella, although there was not a cloud in the sky.

“I beg your pardon?” I said.

“Do not speak to her.”

“Why not?”

“Because it is spring.”

I stared at him.

He leaned nearer.

“Spring, monsieur, is more dangerous than bronchitis. Bronchitis attacks the lungs; spring attacks the judgment. With bronchitis one takes flannel, syrup and rest. Against spring there is nothing. Nothing! The government ought to paste notices on the walls: ‘Return of spring. French citizens, beware of love!’”

The young woman glanced at us, and I feared she might think this gloomy person my uncle.

“Monsieur,” I replied coldly, “I do not understand by what right—”

“By the right of a survivor.”

“A survivor of what?”

“Of precisely that curl of hair, that modest collar, that little smile. Ah! They are all different, naturally, yet they are always the same. Listen to me. You will thank me later.”

“I doubt it.”

“I also doubted. That is how calamities begin.”

He took my arm with the confidential firmness of a physician about to announce a fatal diagnosis.

“It happened here,” he said. “On this boat. Last year. The first Sunday of May—not April, but the danger is identical. The sun was shining, the water sparkled, the trees waved their young leaves, and every foolish creature believed itself immortal. I had been ill all winter.”

“Indeed?”

“A cold. A simple cold. I wore flannel. I drank infusions. I behaved sensibly. Then came spring, and in one afternoon it destroyed what six months of caution had preserved.”

He sighed so deeply that his portfolio slipped. He caught it against his knee and continued:

“She sat almost where your young person sits now. A pretty girl, pink and white, with black eyes and a little nose which appeared to ask questions. She worked in artificial flowers. Imagine the poetry of that profession! Roses in January, violets in November! I saw in it a symbol. That was the first symptom of my illness.

“She looked at me. I looked at her. She smiled. I smiled. The woods passed along the bank; a bird sang three notes, always the same three. I later discovered it was an intolerable bird, but then it seemed to be singing for us.

“She got off at Saint-Cloud. I got off at Saint-Cloud.

“Did I know where she was going? No. Had she invited me? Not in words. But there are glances, monsieur, which seem to open the gate of paradise. Sometimes they open only the gate of Saint-Cloud.”

Despite myself, I laughed.

“You laugh! I laughed also. I followed at a respectful distance. She turned twice. The second time she waited.

“‘Are you walking this way, monsieur?’

“‘Entirely this way, mademoiselle.’

“I would have walked to Versailles, to Chartres, to the Pyrenees.

“Her name was Augustine. Her companion, an aunt with a red shawl, soon disappeared to visit a charcoal dealer who was her cousin. Augustine and I entered the wood. Ah, that wood! Sunlight fell through the branches; children rolled upon the grass; couples vanished behind bushes; from a distant restaurant came the song of Musette, played upon a cracked cornet.

“Do you know the song of Musette?”

“No.”

“May you never know it.”

He removed his hat, wiped his forehead, and resumed:

“We walked for four hours. Four hours, monsieur! In four hours a woman can tell you enough to make you believe you know her, while omitting everything that would permit you actually to do so. She spoke of her mother, who was dead; of her employer, who was unjust; of a little canary, also dead; and of her desire for a peaceful home. She said she hated quarrels. Remember that detail. It has the beauty of a legal document later discovered to be forged.

“She listened when I spoke. That enchanted me. I did not realize that she listened because I was new. Novelty gives eloquence to the dullest man.

“We dined under an arbor. There were fried gudgeons, an omelet, sour wine, and Musette from the cornet. The wine tasted exquisite, the omelet seemed light, the song celestial. Such is love: it corrupts the palate before attacking the reason.

“After that we met every Sunday.

“Every Sunday, the river, the woods, the arbor, the dead canary, Musette! She leaned upon my arm. She called me her dear friend. Once she cried because I had to leave early. I mistook those tears for the overflowing of a profound heart. Perhaps her shoe hurt her. I never investigated.

“At the end of three months I married her.”

The stranger fell silent and looked at me with the solemnity of a man who has pronounced the word “earthquake.”

“Well?” I asked.

“Well? Monsieur, courtship occurs on Sundays; marriage lasts all week.”

He raised one finger.

“On Monday I discovered she disliked getting up. On Tuesday, that she quarreled with tradesmen. On Wednesday, that she considered my friends idiots. On Thursday, that she told the concierge how much I earned. On Friday, that she sang while dressing. On Saturday, that the song she sang was Musette. And on Sunday she reproached me because I no longer took her to Saint-Cloud.

“That was the first week.”

The boat moved past Suresnes. On the bank, laundresses spread white linen upon the grass, and the sheets swelled in the wind like sails. My young woman was pretending not to watch me. Each time our eyes met, she smiled more openly.

The stranger followed my gaze.

“Yes,” he said. “The poison is working. I must increase the dose of truth.

“Augustine had a gift for insult—not the coarse insult which ends a dispute, but the small, polished, domestic insult which can be inserted everywhere: into breakfast, into the placing of an umbrella, into the manner of shutting a drawer. If I coughed, she said I coughed to annoy her. If I remained silent, I was sulking. If I spoke, I talked nonsense. If I came home early, I disturbed her; if late, I was betraying her. Once I arrived exactly on time. She declared that such precision concealed something.

“She gossiped with everyone—the concierge, the fruit seller, the wife of a clerk on the third floor, a retired midwife across the courtyard. My private life circulated through the neighborhood like a newspaper. The charcoal dealer knew the condition of my digestion. The grocer knew what my chief had said about my handwriting. A woman whom I had never seen stopped me in the street and advised me not to wear gray stockings because Augustine considered them unbecoming.

“And the quarrels! She had said she hated quarrels. Perfectly true: she hated any quarrel in which she did not possess the final word. She fought with the butcher over a bone, with the landlord over smoke, with a cabman over ten centimes, with me over the butcher, the landlord, the cabman, the bone, the smoke and the ten centimes.

“Then, when exhausted, she sang Musette.

“Morning: Musette. Evening: Musette. While sewing: Musette. While angry: louder Musette. I now believe that song will be performed at my funeral, and I shall rise from the coffin to protest.”

“You exaggerate,” I said.

“Certainly! A miserable husband who does not exaggerate has thrown away the last pleasure remaining to him. But the facts, monsieur, need little assistance.”

“And yet you must have loved her.”

“I loved a Sunday in May. I married it. One cannot live all year with a Sunday in May.”

This phrase, delivered with sudden simplicity, silenced me.

For a moment we watched the banks. Gardens descended toward the water; lilacs foamed above walls; bells rang somewhere behind the trees. The air was full of that tender brightness which makes every life seem unfinished and every stranger a possible destiny.

The whistle sounded.

“Saint-Cloud!” called an attendant.

The young woman rose. Her companion gathered their parcels. As she passed me, the girl looked straight into my face, then toward the gangway. There could be no mistake. Her smile said plainly: Follow.

My heart gave a leap. The stranger’s story, Augustine, the charcoal dealer, gray stockings, all vanished like smoke. What did his disaster prove? That he had chosen badly. That he had been foolish. That his Augustine was not this girl. Had she not a finer mouth, a more delicate neck, a more intelligent way of holding her parasol?

I seized my hat and stepped forward.

A hand closed upon the back of my coat.

“Let go,” I whispered.

“No.”

“She is getting off.”

“That is why I am holding you.”

“Monsieur, you are insane!”

“Possibly. But I am experienced.”

The passengers began crowding toward the gangway. I pulled; he clung. My coat tightened beneath my arms.

“Release me at once!”

“Never!”

The girl had reached the landing stage. She turned. At first she looked surprised, then encouraging. I made another effort.

The stranger wrapped both arms around my waist.

“Help!” he cried loudly. “This gentleman is unwell!”

Every face turned toward us.

“I am not unwell!”

“He is feverish,” announced my captor. “A sudden attack. Spring fever. Stand back!”

The stout lady with the chicken regarded me anxiously. One soldier laughed. A child asked whether I would bite. The older companion tugged at the young woman’s sleeve, but she remained on the quay, staring.

“Let me pass!” I said through my teeth.

“You shall not ruin your life before my eyes!”

“I shall strike you.”

“Strike me tomorrow. Today I save you.”

The rope was cast off. The paddle churned. A strip of water opened between boat and quay.

The girl took one step toward the edge. Her inviting smile faded into disappointment, then into offended pride. She turned sharply and walked away beside her companion, the rebellious curl trembling at her ear.

I watched her diminish upon the landing stage while Saint-Cloud slipped behind us. Never had a human figure seemed to contain so much lost happiness. I imagined the walk beneath the trees, the intimate conversation, the dinner under the arbor—without sour wine, naturally—and perhaps a kiss at dusk. The entire unwritten romance floated away in a dark blue dress.

The stranger released me, straightened my coat, and brushed its collar with benevolent care.

“There,” he said, breathing hard. “It is done.”

I could not speak.

He picked up his green portfolio and opened his umbrella against the spotless sky.

“You are angry now, monsieur. That is natural. Patients often insult the surgeon. But later—when you are seated peacefully by your own fire, free, respected, with nobody singing Musette, nobody informing a charcoal dealer of your salary—you will remember me.”

He shook my hand with emotion.

“I have saved you.”

And while I stood rigid with rage, watching the vanished quay, he walked toward the bow with the modest satisfaction of a citizen who has pulled a drowning man from the river.

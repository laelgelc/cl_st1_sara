# The Freedom of Hilary

The first announcement appeared in the morning paper between the marriage of a duke’s second daughter and the death of an elderly baronet whom nobody remembered until he was dead.

> The marriage arranged between Viscount Merrivale and Miss Hilary St. Orme will not take place.

It was brief, dignified, and, to those who knew Miss St. Orme, unmistakably explosive.

At eleven o’clock three men at the Palladian Club had read it aloud; at twelve, six ladies had telephoned Hilary’s aunt; and by luncheon the affair had become public property.

Viscount Merrivale himself received the news over coffee.

He read the paragraph twice, folded the paper, and helped himself to another egg.

Reynolds, his servant, hovering near the sideboard, watched this performance with concern.

“Beg pardon, my lord,” he ventured, “but there’s them as would call that a shock.”

“Very likely.”

“And when a gentleman’s shocked,” pursued Reynolds, “he don’t commonly eat three eggs.”

“I have eaten two.”

“The third’s in your hand, my lord.”

Merrivale looked at it.

“So it is.”

He was a lean, broad-shouldered man of thirty-four, with a composed face, grey eyes, and the air of having made up his mind before less fortunate people had begun to consider theirs. Women called him distinguished. Men who disliked him called him infernally sure of himself. Hilary St. Orme had lately called him a tyrant.

“She put it in herself?” Reynolds asked gloomily.

“So I gather.”

“Without consulting your lordship?”

“Entirely without.”

Reynolds sighed.

“It’s a free country,” said Merrivale.

“Yes, my lord. But not so free as all that, surely.”

A shadow of amusement crossed Merrivale’s face. He rose and went to the writing-table.

The quarrel had occurred the previous afternoon in Lady Dene’s drawing-room. Hilary, who did not conduct quarrels privately when publicity would give them greater brilliance, had been magnificent.

Cosmo Fletcher had been the subject.

Fletcher was handsome in a sleek, narrow fashion; he had excellent clothes, bad debts, and a reputation which charitable people called unfortunate. Hilary had known him since childhood. Merrivale had asked her to see less of him.

Asked, Hilary maintained. Commanded, Merrivale corrected.

“You have no right whatever to dictate my friendships,” she had said, her chin very high.

“I shall have the right in three weeks.”

“You will not.”

“I think you will find that I shall.”

“I shall not marry a man who proposes to keep me in a cupboard!”

“I had not contemplated a cupboard.”

“A cage, then!”

“Nor a cage.”

“You are intolerable.”

“So you have mentioned.”

“And if I choose to dine with Cosmo to-morrow—”

“You will not.”

There had been a pause. Lady Dene, in the conservatory beyond, had ceased cutting roses. A young cousin behind the screen had forgotten to breathe.

Hilary’s eyes flashed.

“Then there will be no wedding.”

Merrivale had looked at her for several seconds. He had not pleaded. That was his crowning offence. He had merely bowed and said:

“You are overwrought, Hilary. We will discuss it when you are reasonable.”

She had sent the announcement to the papers within the hour.

Now Merrivale wrote six words on a sheet of club paper and rang the bell.

“Take this to the Gazette office, Reynolds.”

The servant read it and blinked.

> The marriage of Viscount Merrivale and Miss Hilary St. Orme will take place, as arranged, on the twenty-seventh instant.

“Will that be all, my lord?”

“That will be all.”

Reynolds reached the door, hesitated, and turned.

“Supposing the young lady puts in another?”

“Then,” said Merrivale, “we shall know that she has read mine.”

* * *

She read it at breakfast.

Her brother Bertie, who had the instincts of a born spectator and the morals of a Greek chorus, thoughtfully handed her the paper.

Hilary opened it. A flush rose slowly from her throat to her forehead.

“Of all the outrageous—of all the monstrous—Bertie, did you know of this?”

“My dear girl, I never know anything before breakfast.”

“He is laughing at me!”

“John doesn’t often laugh.”

“He is treating me as though I were a child.”

“You did throw the rattle out of the perambulator rather suddenly.”

Hilary crushed the paper.

“I hate him.”

“Rot.”

“I do.”

“Then why did you promise to marry him?”

“Because I was a fool.”

Bertie selected toast with care. He was a fair, agreeable young man whose occupation in life appeared to consist of knowing everybody and being welcomed everywhere. The only responsibility he willingly accepted was a house-boat on the upper river, and even that was managed by a married couple named Piggott.

“You were in love with him,” he said.

“I was fascinated by his arrogance. There is a difference.”

“Subtle.”

“He never consults anybody. He orders. He arranges. He assumes. If I say I wish to go north, he has already sent my trunks south.”

“Useful sort of chap.”

“He objected to Cosmo.”

“So do I.”

Hilary swept round upon him.

“That has nothing to do with it!”

“Of course not.”

“I have known Cosmo all my life.”

“And what a long, tiring life it must seem.”

“He is amusing.”

“So is influenza, afterwards.”

Hilary stared at her brother. Her eyes were remarkably blue, and at present full of dangerous light. She had dark hair, a proud little head, and a mouth which looked made for laughter but was admirably equipped for war.

“I am going away.”

“Excellent. Where?”

“To your house-boat.”

Bertie nearly dropped the marmalade.

“To the Kingfisher?”

“This afternoon.”

“My dear Hilary, Culver’s there.”

“I like Cousin Culver.”

“He has a man with him.”

“Then the man can go.”

“I’m afraid the man won’t.”

She raised her eyebrows.

“Who is he?”

“Calls himself Jacques. Jack, if one is feeling intimate.”

“What does he do?”

“Things with horses, chiefly. And mines. And forests, perhaps. He comes from somewhere west of civilisation.”

“How disgusting.”

“Culver picked him up abroad. He has a beard.”

“More disgusting still.”

“And clothes like a tobacco plantation.”

“Bertie, if you are trying to prevent me—”

“Prevent you? Great Scotland, no! I should not miss this for worlds.”

She narrowed her eyes.

“What do you mean?”

“Nothing. I shall wire Piggott.”

The wire which Bertie sent contained twelve words:

> HILARY ARRIVING TODAY. SHE HAS DECLARED WAR ON MERRIVALE. WARN THE WILD MAN.

Culver’s reply arrived before they left London:

> WILD MAN DELIGHTED. SHALL HIDE KNIVES. COME QUICKLY.

Hilary did not see either message.

* * *

The Kingfisher lay in a green reach beyond Marlow, moored beneath willows that stooped to gossip with their own reflections. It was a long white craft with striped awnings, scarlet geraniums in boxes, and a drawing-room in which comfort had triumphed over navigation.

The river was at its summer ease. Sunlight danced upon it; reeds nodded; somewhere behind the trees a church clock struck six with a dreamy disregard for accuracy.

Hilary stepped aboard and immediately felt London recede. Newspapers, announcements, telephones, aunts, and Viscount Merrivale seemed to belong to another and less agreeable planet.

Culver came to meet her. He was brown, thin, and elderly enough to be trusted by relations while remaining young enough to encourage their indiscretions.

“My dear child! Welcome to Arcadia. No engagements allowed.”

“Don’t mention engagements.”

“No marriages, then.”

“Nor marriages.”

“No titled tyrants?”

“Especially no titled tyrants.”

Culver kissed her cheek.

Behind him stood a man carrying a coil of rope.

He was tall—taller than Merrivale—and heavily built about the shoulders. His clothes were brown and rough, his boots frankly disreputable, and his beard, also brown, covered most of his face. The visible portion was burned by weather. His hair was longer than fashion approved. He might have stepped out of a Canadian forest, bringing portions of it with him.

Culver waved a hand.

“Hilary, this is my friend Jacques Delorme. Jacques, my cousin Miss St. Orme.”

The stranger put down the rope and bowed.

His bow was unexpectedly courtly.

“Mademoiselle.”

His voice held a faint foreign drawl—or perhaps merely the slow deliberation of a man accustomed to spaces larger than drawing-rooms.

Hilary inclined her head.

“How do you do?”

“I do very well,” said Jacques gravely. “But the rope, she does badly.”

Bertie made a choking sound.

Hilary turned to him.

“Have you swallowed something?”

“Only emotion.”

Jacques picked up the rope. His hands were large and brown; one wrist was circled by a strip of leather. Without apparent effort he drew the punt closer to the landing-stage and made it fast.

“Mr. Delorme is staying long?” Hilary asked Culver.

“As long as he likes.”

“How independent.”

The bearded man looked at her.

“It is not independence when one stays because one is asked.”

“No?”

“It is friendship.”

This was said simply. Hilary, who had expected rustic embarrassment, glanced at him again.

“Do you always define words, Mr. Delorme?”

“Only when they are important.”

“And is independence important?”

“To those who have not got it.”

“And to those who mean to keep it,” she returned.

Something moved in his eyes.

“Ah,” he said. “That is different.”

At dinner the wild man proved less wild than advertised. He used the proper forks, spoke when addressed, and listened without the restless desire to shine which afflicted most of Hilary’s acquaintances. He told one story of a flood in the Argentine and another of three winter days spent in a hut with a lame dog and a dislocated shoulder. He did not make himself the hero of either.

“But what did you do?” Hilary demanded.

“Waited until the snow stopped.”

“With your shoulder out?”

“Yes.”

“Couldn’t you put it back?”

“I could not. The dog objected.”

Bertie laughed.

Hilary did not. She was looking at Jacques’s hands.

“You must be very strong.”

“I have had to be.”

The answer irritated her faintly. It was not boastful enough to mock.

Later, on deck, Bertie found her watching the bearded figure in the stern.

“Well?” he asked.

“Well what?”

“How do you like the anthropoid?”

“He is not an anthropoid.”

“Not entirely.”

“He has rather good manners.”

“Devastating.”

“And he does not talk too much.”

“An insult to civilisation.”

Hilary settled herself among the cushions.

“Why is he called Jacques?”

“His mother, perhaps.”

“It is too commonplace. Half the men in the world are called Jack.”

From the stern came a sudden deep laugh.

Hilary looked round. Jacques was bending over a lantern, but his shoulders shook.

“Is he laughing at me?”

“Would anyone dare?” said Bertie.

* * *

The days that followed were golden, idle, and deceitful.

London continued to debate Hilary’s marriage. Lady Dene wrote three urgent letters. Hilary dropped them unopened into a drawer. Cosmo Fletcher sent roses, orchids, and a note which began, “My dear brave girl.” The roses went into the saloon, the orchids to Mrs. Piggott, and the note into the river.

Merrivale sent nothing.

This should have pleased Hilary. Instead, it offended her.

“He is so sure I shall return,” she told Culver. “He imagines silence is impressive.”

“Isn’t it?” Culver asked.

“No.”

“Then you needn’t notice it.”

“I don’t.”

She noticed it every morning.

Meanwhile Jacques became part of the river.

He rose early, vanished into mist in a skiff, returned with wet hair and a quiet face, and ate enormously. He mended a broken tiller, rescued Mrs. Piggott’s best saucepan from the stream, and once lifted a fallen branch which three men had failed to move. Children hailed him from the banks. Dogs followed him. Old Piggott, a severe critic of mankind, said that foreigners weren’t always as useless as Providence appeared to intend.

Hilary patronised him for four days.

On the fifth she discovered that he knew more of Italian painting than she did. On the sixth he corrected her knot while pretending not to. On the seventh she challenged him to a race across the river, lost, and accused him of using his arms unfairly.

He accepted her displeasure without apology.

“You are accustomed to winning, mademoiselle.”

“I am accustomed to fair play.”

“So am I.”

“Then why did you beat me?”

“Because I could.”

It was precisely what Merrivale might have said. Yet from Jacques it did not enrage her. Perhaps the beard altered the philosophy.

“You are insufferable,” she said.

“So I have been told.”

The echo tugged at her memory. Before she could catch it, Bertie shouted from the house-boat, and the moment went.

At times Jacques’s behaviour puzzled her. He spoke rarely of himself. He came, he said, from the West; but the West expanded or contracted according to inquiry. He had lived in Canada, Mexico, Colorado, and places with unpronounceable names. His French was excellent when he forgot to make it peculiar, and his knowledge of English politics would have done credit to a permanent under-secretary.

“Were you ever in London?” she asked one evening.

“For a little.”

“Did you like it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Too many walls.”

“That is exactly what I feel! Everybody watches everybody else. They tell you whom to know, what to think, whom to marry—”

“And you do not like to be told.”

“I will not be told.”

He rested his elbows on the rail. Sunset lay red across the water; his face was half in shadow.

“Suppose the teller is right?”

“That makes it worse.”

“Why?”

“Because then he is conceited as well as tyrannical.”

Jacques considered this.

“Suppose he tells you because he cares what happens to you.”

“He might ask.”

“And if you refuse?”

“He must accept my refusal.”

“Even if he believes you in danger?”

She turned sharply.

“We are speaking of Cosmo Fletcher.”

“Are we?”

“You have heard the story from Bertie.”

“I have heard some things.”

“And you agree with Lord Merrivale?”

His hands became still.

“I think Fletcher is not fit to be your friend.”

“How dare you!”

“Quite easily.”

She rose. Her cheeks were hot.

“You are no better than the rest of them.”

“That may be.”

“You have known me ten days.”

“I have known Fletcher longer.”

She stared.

“Where?”

“Outside civilisation.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means he has travelled, too.”

There was something beneath the words—something cold and formidable. Hilary saw suddenly that the quiet stranger could be dangerous. His gentleness was not weakness; it was strength held upon a rein.

She ought to have left him. Instead she asked:

“What happened?”

“He behaved badly.”

“And you?”

“I expressed an opinion.”

“With your hands?”

His eyes met hers.

“Yes.”

Hilary’s anger wavered. A vision of the sleek Cosmo receiving Jacques’s opinion was not wholly distressing.

“You thrashed him?”

“He needed it.”

“You are a savage.”

“Probably.”

“And proud of it?”

“No.”

That disarmed her. She moved to go, but he said quietly:

“Mademoiselle.”

She paused.

“I had no wish to make you angry.”

“You succeeded.”

“I am sorry for that. Not for what I said.”

There it was again—that intolerable steadiness. But because it belonged to Jacques, because the willows whispered and the warm air smelled of roses, it seemed not tyranny but homage: the rough man standing against her anger because he would not purchase her smile with a lie.

She held out her hand.

“I forgive you.”

His gaze dropped to it. For an instant he did not move. Then he took it very gently in his great brown hand and bowed over it.

The touch lasted no more than a second.

Hilary went below with her heart behaving absurdly.

* * *

A week later they travelled home by moonlight in a punt.

Bertie and Culver had gone to dine with neighbours. Hilary, pleading a headache, remained behind. At nine the headache vanished so completely that she proposed a walk. At ten they were two miles from the Kingfisher, and rain had transformed the path into a silver swamp.

Jacques found the punt tied beneath an alder.

“It belongs to Sir Timothy Bream,” Hilary said.

“Then Sir Timothy shall have it back.”

“He might call it stealing.”

“He will call it borrowing if I return it.”

“You possess a splendid morality.”

“It is suited to rivers.”

He placed her among the cushions and took the pole. The rain had ceased. Clouds parted, letting the moon spill down upon the wet world. Every leaf shone; the river stretched before them like a road into enchantment.

Hilary leaned back, trailing one hand just above the water.

“How silent it is.”

“No,” said Jacques. “Listen.”

There was the dip and whisper of water, the small secret stir of creatures in the bank, a night-bird’s cry, the faint laughter of the stream against the punt.

“The world is awake,” he said, “and singing.”

She looked at him.

With the moon behind him, the brown clothes and broad shoulders seemed almost picturesque. Even the beard had ceased to offend her. Indeed, a clean-shaven Jacques would have appeared as improper as a rose without leaves.

“You say beautiful things for a wild man.”

“Do I?”

“Sometimes.”

“And other times?”

“Other times you are impossible.”

He smiled.

The pole dipped. The punt glided beneath a willow whose branches brushed Hilary’s hair.

“Tell me,” he said, “what you will do with your freedom.”

She stiffened a little.

“Everything.”

“That is a large programme.”

“I shall travel. I shall have my own house. I shall see whom I please.”

“Fletcher?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly. She frowned.

“No,” she repeated. “Not Cosmo.”

Jacques said nothing.

“I may have been mistaken about him,” she conceded.

“Ah.”

“You needn’t sound triumphant.”

“I did not speak.”

“You can be triumphant in silence. It is one of your worst qualities.”

He drove the punt onward.

“And Lord Merrivale?” he asked presently.

“I shall forget him.”

The pole slipped an inch.

Hilary saw it.

“You dislike him,” she said.

“I have not said so.”

“You never say anything plainly when he is mentioned.”

“Perhaps I know him too well.”

“Do you?”

“A little.”

“Bertie said nothing of that!”

“Bertie forgets many things.”

“How do you know Lord Merrivale?”

Jacques recovered the pole and stood motionless. Moonlight touched the hard line of his cheek above the beard.

“We have met.”

“I suppose you fought him too.”

“No.”

“Would you win?”

A strange amusement lit his eyes.

“I think neither of us would survive.”

Hilary laughed, and her laughter floated across the water. Jacques’s expression changed as he watched her. The amusement went out of it, leaving something so grave, so hungry, that her breath caught.

The punt touched the bank softly.

He held out his hand to help her ashore.

Neither spoke.

At the Kingfisher, lamps burned beneath the awning. Bertie’s voice carried through the stillness.

“There they are! What ho, the wanderers!”

Hilary snatched her hand from Jacques’s.

“Must Bertie always shout?”

“He is a civilised man,” said Jacques.

She glanced up, uncertain whether he mocked her. Then she saw his smile and laughed again.

But that night she did not sleep.

She told herself the moon had bewitched her. She told herself Jacques was uncultivated, mysterious, and possibly disreputable. She told herself his beard was absurd.

At dawn, hearing his step on deck, she rose and looked through the curtain.

He stood alone in the pale light, his face turned toward the river.

Hilary knew then that she was in danger.

Not the danger of which Merrivale had warned her. Something far worse.

She was falling in love with the wild man.

* * *

Cosmo Fletcher arrived on a hot afternoon in a cream-coloured car and a temper.

He found Hilary beneath the awning, wearing white, with a bowl of cherries beside her. Jacques sat on the landing-stage, repairing a paddle. His sleeves were rolled above the wrist, and the movement of the muscles in his forearms was distinctly visible.

Fletcher noticed him at once.

“My dear Hilary,” he said, bending over her hand. “Arcadia appears well populated.”

“Cosmo! Who told you where I was?”

“Half London. May I sit down?”

“If you like.”

He sat and regarded Jacques through his eyeglass.

“Your boatman?”

“No.”

“Odd-looking fellow.”

Jacques continued working.

“He can hear you,” Hilary said.

“Can he? Does he understand English?”

“Yes,” said Jacques, without looking up.

Fletcher’s eyelids narrowed. There was something in the voice that disturbed him, though he could not place it.

Hilary offered him cherries. He refused them.

“I have come on serious business.”

“How alarming.”

“You have had my letters?”

“I had one.”

“And?”

“It was wet.”

“Wet?”

“It fell into the river.”

He flushed.

“You make a joke of everything. Do you realise the position you have created?”

“I did not know I had created one.”

“You have publicly broken with Merrivale.”

“He seems unaware of it.”

“Then make him aware. Put in another notice. Write to him. End this farce.”

Hilary’s brows rose.

“Are you giving me orders too?”

“I am trying to save you from humiliation.”

“How kind.”

“And perhaps,” Fletcher added, lowering his voice, “to save you from a worse mistake.”

His glance travelled toward Jacques.

Hilary went very still.

“What do you mean?”

“London is talking.”

“London has nothing better to do.”

“It is said that you are amusing yourself here with some foreign adventurer.”

The paddle ceased to scrape.

Hilary rose.

“You had better go, Cosmo.”

“I came because I care for you. I always have. You knew it before Merrivale appeared with his title and his cursed certainty.”

“Do not be melodramatic.”

“Why not? You are playing a dangerous game. Do you imagine Merrivale will marry you after this?”

Her face flamed.

“There is no ‘this.’”

“Then what is he?” Fletcher pointed with his cane.

Jacques stood up.

He did so without haste, but the effect was remarkable. Fletcher stepped back.

Hilary moved between them.

“Mr. Delorme is a guest of my brother. You will apologise.”

“To a groom?”

Jacques’s face did not alter.

“No,” he said. “Do not ask him. His apologies are not good.”

Fletcher stared at him.

“Have we met?”

“I think not.”

“That voice—”

“You hear many voices.”

Fletcher turned upon Hilary again.

“If you will not listen, I shall go to Merrivale.”

“And tell him what?”

“That his promised wife has spent three weeks making love to a bearded vagabond.”

There was a silence.

Hilary became white.

Jacques came one step forward, but she checked him with a look.

“You contemptible creature,” she said.

Fletcher smiled thinly.

“Truth is seldom polite.”

He reached for her wrist. Perhaps he meant only to detain her; perhaps his anger had outrun intention. Hilary pulled away sharply.

On the table beside her stood an alcohol lamp beneath a silver kettle. Her wide muslin sleeve swept across the flame.

There was a soft sound—a hungry little rush.

Fire leapt to her shoulder.

For one dreadful instant no one moved.

Then Jacques was upon her.

He caught the burning stuff in both hands, crushed it against his body, tore it from her arm. Flame ran over his fingers. Hilary heard Bertie shout from within the boat. Someone flung a rug. The table crashed. Cherries rolled like drops of blood across the deck.

The whole affair lasted seconds.

When Hilary understood anything, she was kneeling beside Jacques. The fire was out. Her arm smarted, but the sleeve had burned away before the skin was badly touched.

His hands had taken it.

“Jacques!”

“It is nothing.”

It was not nothing. The palms were scorched, the fingers blistering. The leather strip about his wrist had burned through and fallen.

Bertie ran for water. Culver shouted for Piggott. Hilary held Jacques’s arm, trembling so violently that she could hardly see.

Fletcher stood by the rail. His face had changed.

On the inside of Jacques’s exposed wrist, beneath the soot, showed a small blue tattoo: a crown above the letter M.

Fletcher saw it.

Recognition came in a flash.

“You!” he breathed.

Jacques looked up.

The foreign softness had vanished from his voice.

“Yes,” he said.

Fletcher recoiled as if struck.

Hilary scarcely noticed. She was bending over the ruined hands, tears running unchecked down her face.

“You are hurt. Oh, you are hurt!”

“Only my hands.”

“You saved me.”

“That was the intention.”

Even now, the dry answer! She made a broken sound between laughter and a sob.

Fletcher moved toward the gangway.

Bertie barred his path.

“Leaving?”

“I’ll fetch a doctor.”

“Piggott’s gone.”

“Then I can do no good here.”

“No,” said Jacques. “You never could.”

Their eyes met. Fletcher’s mouth twisted.

“I know you now.”

“Then remember.”

Fletcher left without another word.

Hilary looked from the empty gangway to Jacques.

“What did he mean?”

“Nothing that matters.”

“It mattered to him.”

Jacques tried to draw his hand away. She held it.

“Who are you?”

For the first time since she had known him, he appeared at a loss.

Culver arrived with bandages and an authority that admitted no questions.

“Later, my child. His hands first.”

Hilary obeyed. She sat beside Jacques while Culver dressed the burns, and each time he flinched she became whiter.

That evening a doctor came from Marlow. The burns were painful, he said, but not dangerous. Jacques was to keep both hands bandaged and do nothing heroic for a fortnight.

Hilary heard the verdict in silence.

When she went to his cabin afterwards, it was empty.

Jacques had gone.

* * *

The Kingfisher became hateful without him.

His pipe remained on the shelf. His brown coat hung behind the door. The paddle he had mended lay finished on the deck. Everywhere there were signs of his hands, and his hands—strong, gentle, wounded for her—haunted Hilary’s thoughts.

She demanded explanations.

Bertie knew nothing.

Culver knew less.

Their ignorance had a suspiciously polished quality.

“He cannot simply disappear!” she cried.

“Apparently he can,” said Bertie.

“He is hurt.”

“Not mortally.”

“He cannot dress himself.”

“Piggott suggested that problem would not prove insuperable.”

“Where has he gone?”

“West, perhaps.”

“West of what?”

“Civilisation.”

She stamped her foot.

“I hate you both.”

“We’re accustomed to it,” Culver assured her.

The next morning another letter arrived from Lady Dene, together with a folded newspaper. This time Hilary opened it.

The society column stated that Viscount Merrivale had returned to his town house after an absence abroad. His marriage to Miss St. Orme, the report added with magnificent caution, was still expected to take place on the twenty-seventh.

The date was ten days away.

Hilary stared at the print.

Merrivale returned. Jacques gone.

Fletcher’s recognition. The tattoo. The odd slips of voice, the knowledge of London, the deep laugh when she had said that half the world was called Jack.

A thought came to her.

It was preposterous.

She pushed it away.

It returned.

“No,” she whispered.

She saw Jacques standing in the moonlight. She saw Merrivale in Lady Dene’s drawing-room, cool beneath her anger. The height was the same. The eyes—

But Merrivale was clean-shaven, fastidious, civilised. Jacques was brown, bearded, rough-clad.

Faces could be altered. Voices could be altered.

Eyes could not.

Hilary rose so quickly that the chair overturned.

Bertie, entering, stopped.

“My dear girl?”

“Where is Lord Merrivale?”

“In London, according to the paper.”

“Where in London?”

“I suppose his house.”

“I am going there.”

Bertie’s expression became innocent.

“To terminate the engagement again?”

She looked at him.

“You knew.”

“Knew what?”

“You knew!”

“Hillary—”

She seized a cushion and threw it. He caught it neatly.

“Traitor! Idiot! Detestable, plotting wretch!”

“Steady! That cushion belongs to me.”

“You let me—you watched me—oh!”

She fled to her cabin.

Bertie put down the cushion and whistled.

Culver appeared from the saloon.

“Has the penny dropped?”

“With the force of a safe from a third-storey window.”

“Poor John.”

“Poor John? Poor old me, when she discovers the full extent of my treachery.”

“Was she angry?”

“She threw furniture.”

“Then she’s in love.”

Bertie brightened.

“That’s what I thought.”

* * *

Hilary did not go to London.

Pride prevented her for one hour, anger for two, and terror for the rest of the day. If Jacques were Merrivale, then she had been deceived outrageously. If he were not, then she had fallen in love with a man who had vanished without a word.

Neither possibility was pleasant.

At dusk she walked alone along the bank.

The radiant weather had broken. Low clouds pressed upon the river; the willows bent under a cool wind. The world which had been awake and singing now seemed to wait.

She reached the place where Sir Timothy Bream’s punt had been borrowed. There, beneath the alder, she found a man.

He wore a dark town coat. His face was pale, lean, and clean-shaven. Both hands were bandaged.

Merrivale.

Hilary stopped.

For several seconds neither spoke.

Then he bowed.

“Good evening.”

His voice was Jacques’s voice without the drawl.

She felt the last of her uncertainty vanish, and with it every prepared speech.

“You,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You were Jacques.”

“Yes.”

“You wore that hideous beard.”

“I did.”

“You lied to me!”

“Often by implication. Seldom in words.”

Her breath came quickly.

“How dare you quibble!”

“I am not quibbling. I am trying to be accurate.”

“Accurate! You made a fool of me.”

His face tightened.

“That was never my intention.”

“What was your intention?”

“To make you know me.”

“I knew you already.”

“No.” He spoke quietly. “You knew Viscount Merrivale. A title, a set of habits, a man who crossed rooms at the wrong moment and told you truths you did not wish to hear. You had decided what I was. Nothing I said could alter it.”

“So you dressed like a brigand!”

“A Canadian trapper, according to Bertie.”

“And grew a beard?”

“Purchased one.”

In spite of everything, her lips trembled.

“It was a false beard?”

“A particularly expensive one.”

“It was abominable.”

“I found it trying.”

She turned away because laughter was treacherously near. The river moved darkly beyond the reeds.

“You had no right.”

“No.”

The simple admission brought her round.

He stood with his bandaged hands hanging helplessly at his sides. Without the beard, without the brown roughness, he seemed both wholly familiar and strangely exposed.

“You admit it?”

“I had no right. Only a reason.”

“What reason?”

“I loved you.”

The wind stirred her skirt. Somewhere across the water a door closed.

Merrivale went on:

“When you ended our engagement, I might have accepted it. Perhaps I ought to have done so. But I knew you were angry, and I knew Fletcher had helped to make you angry. I also knew that if I pursued you as myself, you would run farther merely to prove that you could.”

“So you pursued me as someone else.”

“I went to Bertie. Culver helped. I meant only to remain near you for a few days—to see that Fletcher did not follow, perhaps to let you discover that a man may disagree with you without wishing to imprison you.”

Her colour rose.

“You laughed at me.”

“Sometimes.”

“You let Bertie laugh.”

“I could not prevent Bertie. No power on earth can.”

“You made me care for Jacques.”

His eyes were steadfast, but a pulse moved in his cheek.

“That,” he said, “I did not dare to hope.”

Hilary’s hands clenched.

“And now? Why did you go away?”

“Because Fletcher knew me. Because the deception was over. Because you had to be free to decide what remained when Jacques was gone.”

“You might have told me.”

“I am telling you now.”

“After letting me suffer for two days!”

His expression changed.

“Did you suffer?”

“No.”

The answer was too swift. A faint light entered his eyes.

She stamped her foot.

“Do not look pleased!”

“I beg your pardon.”

“You are not sorry at all.”

“I am sorry that you were distressed. I cannot be sorry that you cared.”

The words shook her. She looked down at his bandaged hands.

“Do they hurt?”

“Yes.”

Her lashes lifted.

“You never admitted that before.”

“I am trying a new policy of truth.”

“How admirable.”

“I thought so.”

They were silent again.

At last Merrivale said:

“The notice will be withdrawn.”

She looked up sharply.

“What notice?”

“Our marriage. I have instructed my solicitor to send an announcement to-morrow.”

The river seemed suddenly cold.

“I see.”

“You asked for freedom. You shall have it.”

He said the word without bitterness, but it lay between them like a drawn sword.

Hilary’s face went white.

“You mean—you no longer wish to marry me?”

His control broke then, only for an instant. He took one step toward her and stopped, his helpless hands making the movement pathetic.

“Do not ask me that.”

“Why not?”

“Because you know the answer.”

“I know nothing. I have known nothing for weeks!”

“I love you,” he said. “I loved you when you publicly dismissed me. I loved you when you called me a tyrant, when you patronised my beard, when you accused me of cheating at a swimming race. I love you now. But I will not hold you to a promise you regret.”

Her eyes filled.

“And Jacques?”

“Jacques has no claim upon you either.”

“He has.”

Merrivale became very still.

“He saved my life,” she said unsteadily. “He listened to me. He quarrelled with me. He took me home under the moon. He made me—”

She stopped.

“Made you what?”

“Understand.”

His voice was scarcely audible.

“What?”

She came closer. Tears brightened her eyes, but her chin remained gallantly raised.

“That freedom is a very splendid thing when one is fighting for it—and rather empty when one has got it.”

“Hilary—”

“I do not want your announcement.”

“You need not fear scandal. I shall make it plain that the fault was mine.”

“You are not listening.”

“I am trying not to presume again.”

“Oh, John, presume a little!”

He stared at her.

She gave a small, desperate laugh.

“I fell in love with Jacques. I tried not to. He was uncivilised and secretive, and his clothes were dreadful. But he was kind, and strong, and never afraid of me. Then he disappeared, and I thought my heart had gone with him.”

Merrivale’s face had changed utterly. Hope, incredulous and quivering, broke through its reserve.

“And when you knew?”

“That Jacques was you?”

“Yes.”

“I wanted to kill you.”

“Reasonable.”

“And Bertie.”

“More reasonable.”

“And then I remembered your hands.”

She reached for them, touching the bandages with infinite care.

“You foolish man.”

“I have been called worse.”

“You deceived me.”

“Yes.”

“You ordered me.”

“Frequently.”

“You announced our wedding after I had cancelled it.”

“I did.”

“You are arrogant, obstinate, and quite impossible.”

“Hilary, are you trying to propose to me?”

“I should not dream of it.”

“No?”

“No. I am merely informing you that if you withdraw that notice, I shall put in another.”

His grey eyes warmed.

“What will it say?”

She drew a breath.

“That the marriage arranged between Viscount Merrivale and Miss Hilary St. Orme will take place—as arranged.”

He made a sudden movement to take her in his arms, remembered his hands, and checked himself with an exclamation.

Hilary solved the difficulty. She slipped her arms about his neck and held him.

For a moment he did not move. Then his bandaged wrists rested lightly against her back, and his face bent to hers.

The river laughed softly at the bank. Above them the clouds parted, and a pale shaft of evening light touched the wet leaves.

Presently Hilary drew back and studied him.

“What is it?” he asked.

“You look very odd.”

“Without the beard?”

“Naked.”

“Shall I replace it?”

“Never!”

“I thought you admired it.”

“I endured it.”

“You kissed it once.”

“I did not!”

“In the punt—”

“That was your cheek.”

“A technical distinction.”

She flushed delightfully.

“I believe you are laughing at me again.”

“I expect to do so for the rest of my life.”

“Tyrant.”

“Savage.”

She touched his clean chin, shaking her head with mock severity.

“You hairless monster.”

* * *

The third announcement appeared two days later.

It was identical with the second, except that the date had been postponed a fortnight owing to an injury sustained by the bridegroom.

At the Palladian Club it was received with satisfaction and some disappointment.

“Always said it would come off,” declared Sir Timothy Bream.

“You said she’d elope with a boatman,” somebody reminded him.

“Same thing, apparently.”

Cosmo Fletcher left for the Continent before luncheon.

At the Kingfisher, Bertie read the notice aloud beneath the awning while Culver smoked and Hilary pretended not to listen. Merrivale sat beside her with both hands still wrapped in white bandages.

Mrs. Piggott brought coffee and six boiled eggs.

“Six?” said Bertie. “Good Lord, John, matrimony has given you an appetite.”

“They are not all for me.”

Hilary took one.

Reynolds, who had come down from London with his master’s clothes and a conviction that house-boats were unsanitary, watched them from the doorway.

“All’s well as ends well,” he informed Mrs. Piggott. “Though between you and me, it ain’t natural, the way his lordship eats when crossed in love.”

“He don’t look crossed now,” said Mrs. Piggott.

Merrivale did not. Hilary was buttering toast for him because his hands were useless, and doing it with an air of conferring a royal favour.

“You will not always have me to do this,” she warned.

“No?”

“When we are married, you must not imagine I shall wait on you.”

“I should not dare.”

“Nor dictate to me.”

“Never.”

“Nor interfere with my friends.”

He glanced at her.

“Within reason.”

Her eyes flashed. His answered them steadily.

Bertie put down the paper.

“What ho,” he murmured. “Here we go again.”

Hilary held Merrivale’s toast just beyond his reach.

“What do you mean by ‘within reason’?”

“Precisely what you suppose.”

“John!”

He smiled.

She attempted to look indignant, failed, and gave him the toast.

Beneath the willows, the Kingfisher stirred gently against her moorings. Roses nodded along the rail; sunlight ran in broken gold over the water; and the summer world, having witnessed freedom won and surrendered, was awake once more and singing.

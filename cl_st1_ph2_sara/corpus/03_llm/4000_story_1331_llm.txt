## THE BASEBALL HONEYMOON

When I took the Worcester club that spring, I had three ambitions, arranged in what I considered their proper order. The first was to win the pennant; the second was to get enough people through the gate to pay salaries; and the third was to keep Rube Hurtle from tearing the league to pieces before I could sell him to a major-league club.

Rube stood six feet three in his stockings, weighed two hundred and ten pounds without his breakfast, and had the manners of a boy who had been raised forty miles from a railroad. He could throw a baseball through a pine board, or so Spears claimed, and when he got his curve to breaking right it came up to the plate like a white streak, hesitated as if considering the batter, and then dodged under his club.

But Rube had one fault, and it was more dangerous to Worcester than wildness, sore arms, or bad company.

He was lonesome.

You might suppose a manager would have enough to do without doctoring a pitcher's heart. Not on your life. A manager must know whether his short-stop has been up all night playing poker, whether his catcher is nursing a split thumb, whether his left-fielder has received a letter from home, and whether his star pitcher is looking at the moon instead of the signs.

During May, Rube looked at the moon.

He lost weight. He stopped arguing with the umpire. He sat in hotel lobbies with his enormous hands spread on his knees and stared at the carpet. Worst of all, he pitched with a sort of sad politeness. He would put the first ball over, then the second, and if a batter happened to hit one, Rube appeared personally disappointed but not angry. A man with his arm ought to have hated every fellow carrying a bat. Instead, he behaved as though the batter had come to borrow flour.

One afternoon in Springfield he walked six men and let in the winning run.

Spears, my captain, followed me under the grand stand. He was a thin, knowing fellow with sharp eyes and a mustache that moved whenever he was planning deviltry.

``Connelly,'' he said, ``that big infant is in love.''

``With whom?''

``That,'' replied Spears, ``is what a scientific manager ought to find out.''

``I am a baseball manager, not a matrimonial bureau.''

``Same business. You match pitchers with catchers, hitters with hit-and-run signs, and umpires with abuse. Why stop there?''

I told him to shut up. But I knew he was right.

The cause of Rube's trouble appeared two days later at our grounds. In the sixth inning, with Worcester one run behind, a young woman in the box back of first base sprang up and shouted:

``Strike them out, Rube! Don't let those horrid men hit you!''

She was a bright, brown-haired girl in white, with a red ribbon fluttering at her throat. She had lively eyes, a small determined chin, and the kind of manner that made a whole grand stand understand she had opinions. Beside her sat an older woman trying unsuccessfully to pull her down.

Rube heard that voice. His head came up. He looked toward the box, and the girl waved her score card.

The next three batters might as well have tried to hit rifle bullets. Rube struck out nine men in the remaining three innings, and we won in the tenth when McCall knocked a double into the fence.

As we came off the field, Rube walked beside me with his ears scarlet.

``Who is she, boss?''

``Who?'' I asked, being naturally cautious.

``Girl in white.''

``Never saw her.''

He looked so downcast that I feared for the next series. So I found the ticket man, the ticket man found an usher, and the usher knew the older woman. Before supper I had learned that the girl was Miss Nan Brown, that she lived with her aunt on Chestnut Street, and that she had attended every Worcester game except two.

``Spears,'' I said that night, ``you will give a little dinner tomorrow.''

``I will?''

``You will invite Miss Brown, her aunt, Rube, and me.''

``And what do I get?''

``A chance at the pennant.''

That was enough for Spears. He would have invited the governor, a brass band, and the entire Buffalo club if he thought it would gain us half a game.

Nan Brown came to dinner wearing blue, and from the instant Rube saw her he was helpless. He knocked over a glass, forgot to unfold his napkin, and answered every question with either ``Yes, ma'am'' or ``I reckon.'' Nan watched him with wicked enjoyment.

``Mr. Hurtle,'' she said, ``is it true you killed a bear with a baseball?''

Rube stared.

``No, ma'am. It was a rock.''

``The baseball was a rock?''

``No, ma'am. I killed the bear with one.''

Spears buried his face in the menu.

``How romantic!'' Nan exclaimed. ``Did the bear charge you?''

``He was comin'.''

``And you threw the rock?''

``Yes, ma'am.''

``Did you use an overhand delivery?''

Rube considered this with grave attention.

``No. I had to throw quick, so I used a short swing.''

Nan laughed until the red ribbon at her throat trembled. Rube looked wounded, and then she leaned toward him.

``I am sorry. I wasn't laughing at you. I was only thinking what chance the poor bear had against Worcester's greatest pitcher.''

That restored him. In ten minutes he was telling her about the farm, his old mother, a river hole where he had learned to swim, and a hound dog named General Grant. I saw his shoulders loosen and his eyes brighten. By dessert he had ceased to notice the rest of us.

Spears kicked me under the table.

``Pennant,'' he whispered.

I kicked him back, but inwardly I was counting games.

From then on Nan came often to the grounds. Rube pitched for her as no man ever pitched for salary. When she sat in the grand stand, he wound the ball around batters' necks. When she applauded, he grew faster. If she arrived late, he was uncertain until he caught sight of her; then his arm became a machine, his curve broke like a falling hawk, and the opposition went back to the bench muttering.

We won twelve out of fifteen. Rube took six of them, allowing eight runs in all. Worcester, which had been buried in fifth place, climbed to third, then second. Buffalo still led us by four games, but I could see their manager beginning to study the standings with a worried expression.

Naturally, happiness brought complications.

One evening Rube came to my room and stood in the doorway twisting his cap.

``Boss, I got somethin' to tell you.''

His face frightened me. A manager expects the worst. I thought his arm had gone lame.

``What is it?''

``Nan says she'll marry me.''

I was so relieved I shook his hand.

``Fine! Splendid! Best thing that could happen! When?''

``Fourth of July.''

I released his hand.

On July Fourth we were scheduled to play Buffalo twice. After that came the hardest road trip of the season—Providence, Hartford, Newark, Toronto, Montreal, and Buffalo. Eighteen games in twenty-two days. A wedding meant excitement. A honeymoon meant absence. Absence meant a lost pennant. My happiness dried up like water on a stove.

``Why the Fourth?'' I demanded.

``Nan likes fireworks.''

``But we play a doubleheader.''

``I know.''

``Against Buffalo!''

``I know that, too.''

``And then we leave on the midnight train.''

Rube smiled with an expression so simple and blissful that I wanted to strike him.

``That's the honeymoon.''

I sent for Spears.

He entered, heard the news, and sat down slowly.

``Rube,'' he said, ``marriage is an honorable estate, favored by poets, ministers, and furniture dealers. But couldn't you postpone the ceremony until October?''

``No.''

``September?''

``No.''

``The next rain?''

``Nan wants the Fourth.''

``Suppose it don't rain.''

``Then we'll marry anyway.''

Spears walked to the window.

``Connelly, behold the modern athlete. Give him fame, fortune, a strong right arm, and a chance at the pennant, and he throws them all into the lap of a brown-eyed girl who likes fireworks.''

Rube rose.

``You leave Nan out of it.''

The room suddenly seemed too small for him.

``Easy,'' I said. ``Nobody's saying anything against Miss Brown. But you cannot desert the club.''

``I ain't desertin' it. Nan's comin' along.''

Spears turned from the window.

``On the trip?''

``Sure.''

``With this club?''

``Why not?''

Spears looked at me, and I looked at him. I knew the men. He knew the men. Rube did not know the men, despite having played beside them for three months.

``Why not?'' he repeated.

There are questions a manager cannot answer without ruining his own team. I swallowed hard.

``Very well,'' I said. ``You may marry on the Fourth, provided you pitch whenever I order you and travel with the club.''

``That's what I figured.''

``And there must be no nonsense.''

``What kind?''

``Any kind!'' I roared.

Rube departed cheerfully.

Spears waited until the door shut.

``No nonsense,'' he murmured. ``Connelly, that was a noble speech. You might as well have ordered a cyclone not to muss the infield.''

The wedding took place at nine o'clock on the morning of the Fourth. At seven, Rube was at the grounds warming up. At eight, I drove him to the church in a hired automobile with his uniform under his black suit. At five minutes before nine, Spears discovered Rube had left the ring in his locker and sent an outfielder racing back for it.

The church was packed with friends, reporters, cranks, small boys, and citizens who had never met either bride or groom but regarded the ceremony as part of the day's baseball programme. The Worcester band waited across the street. Somebody had hung a sign from a building: HURTLE HER TO THE ALTAR, RUBE!

Nan came down the aisle like a white bird flying into sunlight. Rube stood beside me, immense, stiff, and pale. His collar pinched him. Sweat showed on his forehead. I whispered:

``Steady. Buffalo couldn't frighten you this way.''

``Buffalo don't wear veils,'' he answered.

When the minister asked for the ring, Rube tried the wrong pocket, then another, and finally looked at me in terror. Spears stepped forward and produced it with the air of a magician. Nan laughed softly. That saved Rube. He put the ring on her finger, said the necessary words in a voice that shook the windows, and became a married man.

Then came the explosion.

The band played. Factory whistles shrieked. Bells rang. Boys fired crackers under the automobile. Rice filled the air. Old shoes struck the running board. Nan waved from beside Rube while I sat opposite them holding his uniform bag and wondering whether all that noise would ruin his control.

We reached the park twenty minutes before game time. The crowd overflowed the stands, lined the outfield ropes, and climbed neighboring roofs. Flags snapped above the grand stand. Vendors yelled, children squealed, and every time Rube showed himself the multitude roared.

Buffalo scored in the first inning. Their lead-off man scratched a hit, stole second, went to third on a sacrifice, and came home on a long fly. Rube was wild. The wedding had got into his arm.

I went to the coaching line.

``Settle down!'' I shouted.

He glanced toward our box, where Nan sat in her bridal dress under a long coat. She lifted one finger as if scolding him.

Rube settled down.

For eight innings Buffalo did not get another hit. He struck out eleven. We tied the score in the fifth, won it in the eighth, and the crowd carried Rube from the field while Nan stood on a chair and clapped both hands above her head.

Between games I ordered him to rest.

``I can pitch the second,'' he said.

``You've just been married and thrown a full game.''

``I can pitch.''

Spears came up.

``Let him. He's got that bear-killing look.''

I hesitated. We needed the game. Buffalo had been laughing over the prospect of facing our second pitcher. A sweep would put us only two games behind.

``All right,'' I said. ``But tell me the instant your arm feels heavy.''

Rube walked out for the second game while fifteen thousand people rose and shouted. He threw the first warm-up pitch so hard that it drove our catcher backward.

I stopped worrying about his arm.

What followed has grown into baseball legend, and men who were not born then will tell you they saw it. Buffalo sent twenty-seven batters to the plate, and twenty-seven went back. Not one hit. Not one run. Not one base on balls. Our third baseman made a leaping catch in the fourth; Spears stabbed a liner in the seventh; but for most of the game the fielders had nothing to do except watch Rube.

His speed cracked against the catcher's mitt like pistol fire. His curve started at a right-hand batter's ear and crossed the inside corner. He struck out fourteen. In the ninth, with two gone, Buffalo's best hitter fouled off two balls, then stepped away and asked the umpire for time.

The park became so still I heard a horse stamping beyond the center-field fence.

Rube looked at Nan.

She leaned over the rail.

``Finish it!'' she called.

He threw.

The batter swung at a ball that seemed waist-high and found nothing. It had dropped a foot. The umpire's arm went up.

For a moment there was silence—the deep breath before a storm. Then Worcester broke loose. Men threw hats, women waved parasols, boys crawled under ropes, the band lost its tune, bells began all over the city, and thousands poured upon the diamond.

I fought through them searching for Rube. He was nowhere. At last I saw him near the box. He had lifted Nan over the rail and held her high above the crowd, bridal white fluttering against his dirty uniform.

That was sentiment, I suppose. To me it looked like first place coming nearer.

That evening, before we boarded the train, a representative of the Chicago Nationals met me at the hotel.

``Connelly,'' he said, ``we want Hurtle after your season closes.''

``So do six other clubs.''

``Name your price.''

I named one that made him blink.

We argued an hour. At last he agreed to a figure larger than our club had cleared in three seasons, provided Rube's arm remained sound and he reported after the pennant race. I put the paper in my pocket and felt that marriage was the finest institution in America.

At the station that opinion began to weaken.

The club had decorated the bridal trunks. Rube's bore a sign reading: HER LITTLE LAMB. Nan's read: PROPERTY OF WORCESTER BASEBALL ASSOCIATION—HANDLE WITH CARE. Somebody tied cowbells under both. Somebody else filled Rube's suitcase with old score cards and substituted a bat bag for Nan's dressing case.

Nan discovered the signs, read them, and smiled.

``Gentlemen,'' she said to the players, who were lined up pretending innocence, ``I thank you for your delicate attention.''

``Speech!'' called McCall.

``I can only say that my husband and I are touched.''

``Rube looks touched,'' said Spears. ``Right in the head.''

Rube started toward him, but Nan caught his sleeve.

``Don't, Sweetheart. The poor man is jealous.''

That turned the laughter on Spears. He bowed.

``Madam, I confess it. Never have I seen a fairer flower plucked from the garden of girlhood and planted in the catcher's mitt of matrimony.''

``Nor a thinner weed left behind,'' Nan replied.

The men howled. From that instant she had half the club on her side, which was fortunate, because the other half devoted itself to tormenting her.

As our train pulled out, McCall began singing in a high, mournful voice:

``Oh, the lamb has found his shepherdess,
Beneath the moonlit sky—''

The team joined in.

``And never more shall Rube be free,
Until the day he die!''

Rube sat rigid, red to the roots of his hair. Nan kept time with a rolled newspaper.

At Providence the baggage went astray. The bride's trunk arrived at the hotel in my room. My trunk went to Rube's. Spears's bag appeared in the bridal suite, together with seven baseball bats, a catcher's mask, and a crate of lemons. Nan's hatbox was delivered to the umpire.

I held a court of inquiry.

``Who did this?''

Every man studied the ceiling.

``Spears?''

``Manager,'' he said solemnly, ``I have devoted my life to order.''

``McCall?''

``Never touched a thing.''

``Where is Mrs. Hurtle's luggage?''

At that instant Nan entered wearing Rube's long traveling coat and carrying my shaving mug.

``Mr. Connelly, I believe this belongs to you.''

Behind her came Rube with two porters, three trunks, the lemon crate, and an expression fit to curdle milk.

``I'll lick the whole club,'' he announced.

``Not before tomorrow's game,'' I said. ``Afterward, if your arm feels good, we'll discuss it.''

Nan perched on the edge of my desk.

``Rube, dear, you must not be angry. These gentlemen are merely expressing affection. They have no wives, no manners, and very little imagination. We should pity them.''

``Pity!'' exclaimed McCall from the hall.

``Especially that one,'' Nan called.

We won four straight in Providence. Rube pitched the second game, allowing three hits. Nan sat near our bench and encouraged him while the Providence players tried to rattle him.

``Ask your wife if you can throw a curve!'' yelled one.

Rube struck him out.

``Does she let you stay out after six?'' called another.

Rube struck him out, too.

Spears came past me, grinning.

``Marriage improves discipline.''

At Hartford the boys nearly caused bloodshed. Our train was to leave at ten-fifteen. Rube and Nan dined away from the club and reached the station with only five minutes to spare. As they hurried down the platform, McCall rushed up crying:

``Wrong station! Train's over on the lower level!''

Rube seized the bags and ran for the stairs. Two other players surrounded Nan with false directions. The conductor shouted, ``All aboard!'' The train began to move. Rube reappeared fifty yards away, saw the cars sliding out, dropped both bags, and charged.

Nan ran beside the train. Spears leaned from the rear platform calling:

``Farewell, bride! Your husband has chosen Hartford!''

I dragged Nan aboard. Rube caught the rail of the last car and swung himself up with one hand.

He came in like a thundercloud.

``Who told me lower level?''

McCall opened a newspaper.

``International affairs are growing serious,'' he observed.

Rube tore the paper in two.

``Rube!'' said Nan sharply.

He stopped.

She looked at him, though laughter danced in her eyes.

``If you kill our center-fielder, Mr. Connelly will fine you.''

``I'll pay it.''

``It might come out of my housekeeping money.''

That reached him. He glared at McCall, picked up the torn newspaper, and attempted to fit the halves together.

We won three out of four at Hartford.

The club's success increased the mischief. Ballplayers are like schoolboys: defeat makes them quarrelsome, victory makes them criminal. By Newark the honeymoon had become public property. Newspapers printed daily accounts. Crowds gathered at stations to see ``the pitching bridegroom and his baseball bride.'' Photographers pursued Nan. Hotel clerks winked at Rube. At every park the band played the wedding march when he stepped onto the field.

Nan enjoyed it. She chatted with reporters, waved to bleachers, and learned the batting averages better than some players did. She could tease Rube into good humor, coax him from anger, and, when he grew gloomy, make him believe the whole league existed for his entertainment.

But she also flirted.

I do not say she meant harm. She scattered smiles as naturally as a tree scatters blossoms in spring. Yet Rube counted every one. If she spoke twice to a man, he wanted to know who the fellow was. If an opposing player bowed to her, Rube pitched at his ribs. If a hotel clerk admired her hat, Rube stared until the poor man forgot the room number.

At Newark, Nan met an old acquaintance named Charlie Wilmot, a smooth young traveling salesman who wore striped waistcoats and knew too much about his own looks.

``Nan!'' he cried in the hotel lobby. ``Can it be you?''

``Charlie!''

They shook hands. That should have ended it, but Charlie held hers a little too long.

Rube came across the lobby.

``Who's this?'' he asked.

Nan withdrew her hand.

``Mr. Wilmot, this is my husband.''

Charlie looked up—and continued looking up.

``Delighted,'' he said faintly.

Rube did not offer his hand.

``You know Nan?''

``We are old friends.''

``How old?''

Nan stamped her foot.

``Rube!''

Spears slipped between them.

``Mr. Wilmot, welcome. We are always glad to meet any gentleman who has survived friendship with Mrs. Hurtle.''

Charlie escaped. Nan scolded Rube for ten minutes. Rube apologized, but he watched the hotel door as if expecting Wilmot to return with reinforcements.

That night came the sleeping-car prank.

We occupied nearly a whole Pullman. Rube and Nan had the drawing-room. At least, they believed they had it. While they were eating in the dining car, the players changed the berth cards, bribed the porter, and transferred their hand luggage. When Rube returned, he marched confidently into the drawing-room and found our veteran catcher asleep there in his undershirt.

The catcher opened one eye.

``Why, Rube! This is sudden.''

Rube backed out.

Nan stood in the aisle laughing. Spears appeared from an upper berth wearing a nightcap made from a towel.

``Lost, little lamb?'' he asked.

``Where's our room?''

``Beyond the purple hills, where the clouds kiss the valley and faithful lovers gather daisies.''

``Where is it?'' Rube repeated.

McCall poked his head through curtains.

``Seek Eden, brother. Follow the call of the shepherdess.''

From another berth came a falsetto voice:

``Sweetheart! Sweetheart! I wait beneath the stars!''

Curtains shook all along the car. Passengers who had nothing to do with us began laughing.

Rube pulled open the nearest berth and found the short-stop hugging a pillow.

``Beloved!'' cried the short-stop.

Rube dragged him into the aisle.

Then every curtain opened. Heads appeared high and low. One man bleated like a sheep. Another played the wedding march on a mouth organ. The porter fled to the washroom.

Rube made a rush for Spears, tripped over a pair of shoes, and struck the floor hard enough to shake the car.

Nan stepped over him and raised her hand.

``Gentlemen!''

The noise died.

``You have done us a great service. Mr. Hurtle and I feared our honeymoon might become dull.''

``Never!'' cried McCall.

``But we are tired. Therefore, the first person who tells me where our room has gone may breakfast with us tomorrow at Rube's expense.''

Six voices answered at once.

She got the room in twenty seconds.

Rube rose slowly.

``I ain't payin' for six breakfasts.''

``Only one, dear.''

``Which one?''

``The porter. He told me before the rest did.''

From the washroom came a despairing groan.

Nan defeated them that night, but the team took revenge at Toronto. There they arranged for a hotel maid to tell Rube that Nan had gone driving with a handsome stranger. The stranger was in fact the club's elderly secretary, who had taken her to buy ribbons. Rube searched three floors, burst into a meeting of traveling hat manufacturers, and descended to the lobby ready for murder.

Nan came in carrying parcels.

``Where have you been?'' he demanded.

``Shopping.''

``With who?''

``Mr. Griggs.''

``They said a handsome stranger.''

Nan looked at our secretary, who was bald, round, and sixty-eight.

``That must be Mr. Griggs.''

The lobby exploded. Even Rube laughed, though unwillingly.

Through all this foolishness we played the finest baseball in the league. We took three of five in Newark, swept Toronto, and divided four games in Montreal. Buffalo lost twice to Providence. By the time we started for Buffalo, Worcester led by half a game.

My life should have been happy. It was not. Happiness is impossible for a manager in first place by half a game. I woke at night thinking of sore arms, bad hops, rain, umpires, railway accidents, and matrimony.

Rube had won five games on the trip and saved another in relief. He ate enormously, slept well when permitted, and looked stronger than ever. Nan had been a blessing to him—except when she was a curse—and I began to believe we might survive until October.

Then they quarreled.

The cause was small, as causes usually are when the explosion is large. We were sitting in the Buffalo hotel after supper. Charlie Wilmot, the striped-waistcoat admirer, appeared again. He happened to be doing business in Buffalo. I have never decided whether that was true or whether he followed us.

Nan greeted him warmly. Rube's face darkened.

Charlie invited her and Rube to the theater. Rube refused for both.

``I can answer for myself,'' Nan said.

``You ain't goin','' said Rube.

``Indeed?''

``We got a game tomorrow.''

``You have a game. I have no game.''

``You're my wife.''

The words fell like a bat dropped on concrete.

Nan's chin lifted.

``Does that mean I have stopped being myself?''

``It means you ain't goin' around with that dude.''

Charlie murmured that he did not wish to cause difficulty and vanished with remarkable speed.

Nan stood very straight.

``You have insulted my friend.''

``He ain't your friend.''

``You have insulted me.''

``I didn't.''

``And you have ordered me about before everybody.''

The lobby had become silent. Ballplayers hid behind newspapers and potted palms, listening with all their might.

``Nan,'' Rube said, ``don't make a fuss.''

That was his great mistake.

Her eyes flashed.

``I shall make any kind of fuss I please.''

She turned and went upstairs.

Rube followed. Ten minutes later a door slammed hard enough to rattle the elevator. Rube came down alone, walked out of the hotel, and did not return until midnight.

I cornered him in the hall.

``What happened?''

``Nothin'.''

``Don't lie. You pitch tomorrow.''

``I know.''

``Then fix it.''

``She can fix it.''

``Rube, Buffalo is one game behind us.''

``What's that got to do with Nan?''

``Everything has got something to do with the pennant.''

He pushed past me.

In the morning Nan did not come to breakfast. Rube ate little and spoke to no one. At the grounds he warmed up savagely, throwing high, low, inside, and sometimes over the catcher's head.

``His control's gone,'' Spears said.

``It will come.''

``Maybe. But if Buffalo discovers he's quarreled with his bride, they'll ride him till sundown.''

Buffalo discovered it before the first inning. Ball clubs possess methods of obtaining information that would shame a government spy service. Their bench began the instant Rube stepped into the box.

``Where's the boss, Rube?''

``Get permission to pitch today?''

``Better not throw too many curves—she'll make you wash dishes!''

Rube struck out the first man on three balls. The second popped up. The third hit a weak roller.

``Good,'' I said. ``He's mad enough to pitch.''

Then Nan entered the grand stand with Charlie Wilmot.

She wore red. I can see her yet: red hat, red ribbon, red cheeks, and eyes bright with defiance. Charlie strutted beside her in a pale suit. They took seats directly back of our bench.

Rube saw them.

The first pitch of the second inning struck the batter between the shoulders.

The Buffalo bench shouted with delight.

``Jealous, Rube?''

``She traded you for a city fellow!''

The next batter ducked a fast ball aimed at his head. The umpire walked toward Rube.

``Keep it over the plate.''

``It slipped,'' Rube said.

I knew it had not slipped. Rube could have hit a button at sixty feet.

He walked the man. A sacrifice advanced both runners. Then Buffalo's short-stop came up, grinning.

``Your wife says I can take two bases,'' he called.

Rube hit him in the ribs.

The crowd roared. The bases were loaded. I started from the bench, but Spears caught my arm.

``Wait. If you pull him now, he'll tear down the grand stand.''

The next man struck out. Two gone. Rube needed one pitch to escape. Instead, he turned and looked at Nan.

Charlie was leaning close, speaking into her ear.

The batter called:

``She don't seem lonesome, Hayseed!''

Rube threw.

The ball missed the batter, missed the catcher, and crashed against the wire screen. One run scored. Rube did not move to cover the plate. He was staring into the grand stand.

I ran onto the field.

``Rube! Look at me.''

He looked, but his eyes were strange.

``Pitch baseball,'' I ordered. ``Whatever she is doing, win this game first.''

For an instant I reached him. He nodded. He struck out the batter with a curve so wicked the man spun around.

In our half we scored twice. Spears doubled, McCall singled, and a bad throw brought them home. Worcester led, two to one.

Rube was steadier in the third. He retired the side. Nan applauded, but he would not glance at her.

Buffalo kept after him.

``Did she pick your pitches on the honeymoon?''

``Hey, Rube, Charlie says he'll warm her up!''

Spears leaped from our bench.

``Shut your filthy mouths!''

The umpire warned both clubs.

In the fifth, a Buffalo runner slid into second with his spikes high and cut Spears's shin. Spears came up swinging, but I dragged him away. The crowd smelled trouble and began to howl.

Rube's anger rose like steam in a boiler. He struck out two men, then deliberately drove a fast ball into the third batter's thigh.

The batter charged the mound.

Rube met him halfway.

There are fights in which men strike, wrestle, fall, and are separated. This was not one of them. Rube picked up the batter and threw him. The fellow landed near third base. Buffalo's bench emptied. Our club rushed out. The crowd stood. Policemen ran along the foul lines.

I grabbed Rube from behind.

``Stop! You'll be suspended!''

He shook me off as if I had been a coat.

A Buffalo substitute shouted something about Nan. Rube wheeled, snatched the ball from the grass, and hurled it at the opposing bench. Men scattered. The ball smashed a water bucket.

Then war began.

Players poured across the diamond. Fists flew. Bats were seized and dropped. Spears fought two men at once, laughing like a lunatic. McCall crawled under the bench and pulled opponents down by their ankles. A policeman clubbed our catcher; our catcher, not recognizing the uniform, tackled the policeman. Another officer grabbed Rube's arm. Rube swung him in a half-circle and knocked down three Buffalo players.

The grand stand roared, hissed, screamed. Cushions and bottles came onto the field. Women fled up the aisles. Boys swarmed along the fence. The band, perhaps thinking noise would restore order, began playing.

I climbed onto the Buffalo bench.

``Get back! All of you! The game's forfeited!''

Nobody listened.

Rube broke through a knot of men and started toward the grand stand. His face was white except for a streak of blood along his cheek. I knew he was going after Charlie Wilmot.

Nan knew it, too.

She sprang to the rail.

``Rube!''

He stopped.

That pause allowed four policemen to seize him. It took six more to hold him. They dragged him toward the players' exit while the Buffalo crowd showered him with abuse.

``Jail the big brute!''

``Throw him out of the league!''

``Wife-beater! Farmer! Coward!''

At that last word Rube lunged so violently the whole pack of officers moved five feet.

I followed into the passage beneath the stand. Buffalo officials were shouting. A police captain demanded names. The league president appeared from nowhere and threatened suspensions. Outside, an angry crowd gathered around the exit.

``Take him by the back way,'' someone said.

``There ain't any back way.''

``Put him in the patrol wagon.''

``That'll start a riot.''

A trolley car had stopped beyond the gate. The police captain decided to put Rube aboard and take him downtown under guard. They formed around him and pushed into the street.

I went with them. So did Spears, limping and bleeding. The crowd closed behind us.

Rube no longer fought. His fury had burned down, leaving him dazed. He looked neither right nor left. His cap was gone, his shirt torn, and one hand had swollen to twice its proper size.

Stones struck the trolley. Men shouted through the windows. Police forced open a path, got Rube onto the rear platform, and held the crowd back.

Then a small red figure came flying through the gate.

Nan.

Charlie Wilmot ran after her, calling her name, but she never turned. She drove into the crowd, slipped between two policemen, lost her hat, and climbed onto the moving trolley.

An officer tried to stop her.

``That's my husband!'' she cried.

Something in her voice stopped him.

She reached Rube. For one second they faced each other—he enormous, battered, ashamed; she bareheaded, breathless, and blazing. Then the crowd surged, a window broke, and Nan threw both arms around him.

``You big fool!'' she sobbed.

Rube's swollen hand came up carefully and rested against her hair.

``Nan—I reckon I spoiled everything.''

``I spoiled it first.''

``What about Charlie?''

``Charlie can go to thunder.''

A cheer came from our players in the street. Even some Buffalo men joined it. The trolley gathered speed, bells clanging, stones dropping behind. Nan clung to Rube's side while two officers stood guard and pretended not to see her crying.

Spears wiped blood from his mustache.

``Connelly,'' he said, ``there goes the end of the honeymoon.''

``There goes my pitcher to jail.''

``He'll be out by supper.''

``He'll be suspended.''

``Probably.''

``We may lose first place.''

Spears regarded me sadly.

``You have the soul of an adding machine.''

Perhaps I had. But I was right about one thing: Rube was out by supper. Buffalo refused to prosecute when half its own club proved equally guilty. The league suspended him for ten days, fined six players, and warned both teams. We protested, appealed, threatened, and finally accepted the sentence.

During those ten days Nan never left Rube except when Worcester played. Then she sat beside our bench, kept score, and shouted harder than any crank in the bleachers. Rube sat in street clothes behind the dugout, his hand bandaged, looking peaceful.

The club, ashamed of having carried its pranks too far and terrified by Nan's opinion, behaved like choirboys. We won six of nine without Rube. When he returned, he pitched a shut-out against Providence and another against Buffalo three days later.

We took the pennant by two games.

Chicago completed the purchase at the agreed figure. Rube went to the majors, Nan went with him, and I used the money to strengthen Worcester for the next season. So, judged by the only practical measures I trusted in those days, my matchmaking was a perfect success.

Years afterward, men still asked me about Rube's no-hit game, the wedding trip, the sleeping-car riot, and the battle at Buffalo. Some said marriage made him great. Others said Nan nearly ruined him. Spears maintained that love added ten feet to a fast ball and took twenty feet from a man's judgment.

For my part, I reckon both forces were at work.

Nan could wind Rube up tighter than any manager, catcher, crowd, or pennant race. When he was happy, he threw as if the ball belonged to heaven and the batter had no right to touch it. When he was jealous, he threw as though every man at the plate had personally insulted his home, his wife, and the honor of Worcester County.

But she always knew how to reach him. That was what mattered. On the worst afternoon of his life, with the Buffalo crowd roaring for his blood and policemen hanging from his arms, she went straight through the mob and took her place beside him.

And I—though Spears never let me forget it—looked at those two clinging together on the trolley, heard the bell banging as they disappeared down the street, and felt a great tenderness rise in me.

For Rube and Nan?

Certainly.

But also because I knew, right then, that my pitcher would be ready when the suspension ended, and that Worcester still had an all-fired good chance to win the pennant.

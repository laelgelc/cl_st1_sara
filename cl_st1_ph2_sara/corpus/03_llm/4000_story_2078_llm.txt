Mrs. Kearney had been educated at a convent where the accomplishments were taught with greater seriousness than the commandments. She could play the piano, speak tolerable French, arrange flowers in shallow bowls and write invitations in a hand which suggested that the recipient was already late. In her girlhood she had been admired without being proposed to by anyone whom she considered admirable. After several seasons of this distinction she married Mr. Kearney, a prosperous boot manufacturer, partly because he was established and partly because two of her friends had married before her.

She respected her husband as she respected the Bank of Ireland: not for conversation, but because it was solid, public and unlikely to disappear.

Mr. Kearney was a heavy, fair man whose opinions arrived slowly and remained after their occasion had passed. He went every morning to his premises in Capel Street, returned at half-past six, ate with appetite and read the evening paper from the advertisements inward. His wife managed their social life, their daughters’ education and the exact degree of Irishness proper to a respectable household.

When the revival of national feeling became fashionable, Mrs. Kearney took care that Kathleen should not be behind it. A harp brooch was bought, though Kathleen disliked brooches; Irish lessons were arranged on Tuesday afternoons; and a small volume of songs, printed in green and gold, was placed on the drawing-room piano. Mrs. Kearney herself learned enough phrases to greet a visitor ceremoniously and enough history to correct one. She attended lectures where thin young men spoke of the soul of the race, and she returned home satisfied that the soul, though ancient and unconquered, required better management.

Kathleen had inherited her mother’s dark eyes and her father’s patience. She had been educated at a good convent, had studied music in the Academy and could accompany a singer without either following too humbly or asserting herself. Her mother said she had temperament. Her teachers said she was reliable. Kathleen accepted both descriptions.

It was at a lecture on the ancient laws of Ireland that Mrs. Kearney first heard of the concert scheme. The Eire Abu Cultural Society proposed to hold four grand evenings in the Antient Rooms during the first week of October. There were to be vocal selections, recitations, national airs, addresses by distinguished persons and, if arrangements permitted, a demonstration of the old dances by children. The object was at once patriotic and educational. The secretary, Mr. Fitzpatrick, spoke about it after the lecture with the embarrassed confidence of a man who had already printed the tickets.

He was a small, sandy person, with an uneven moustache and a habit of holding documents at his side as though they were private evidence.

“We mean to do the thing properly, Mrs. Kearney,” he said. “No expense spared, within reason.”

“Then you must begin on time,” she said.

“Certainly. That goes without saying.”

“And engage competent people.”

“Oh, the very best. We’ve a splendid committee.”

He pronounced committee as if it were itself an entertainment.

Mrs. Kearney learned that an accompanist had not yet been secured. She mentioned Kathleen without eagerness, praised her without warmth and permitted Mr. Fitzpatrick to feel that the discovery had been his. Two evenings later he called at the Kearneys’ house with a written agreement. Kathleen was to accompany at four concerts for the sum of eight guineas.

Mrs. Kearney read the paper twice. She asked whether travelling expenses were included, though there would be no travelling, and whether rehearsal was understood. Mr. Fitzpatrick said that everything would be made satisfactory.

“That is not a phrase I care for in a contract,” she said. “But the amount is plain.”

Mr. Kearney was called in to witness the signatures. He put on his spectacles and read only the line shown to him.

“Eight guineas,” he said. “Very good.”

When Mr. Fitzpatrick had gone, Mrs. Kearney placed the agreement in the locked drawer of her escritoire. She then began to work for the concerts.

The committee had intended to advertise by notices in public houses and by a paragraph in a weekly paper which one of its members knew. Mrs. Kearney explained that a public which respected art must first be given an opportunity to hear of it. She drafted a circular, corrected the title of a visiting lecturer and removed three exclamation marks from the programme. She persuaded a printer, who had supplied Mr. Kearney’s business cards for twelve years, to produce handbills at a reduced rate. When the green ink proved pale she sent the sheets back.

She advised Mr. Fitzpatrick about the order of performances, dissuaded him from placing two recitations together and obtained, through a cousin, the use of palms for the platform. She bought ribbons for the programmes and paid a boy to deliver envelopes in Kingstown and Blackrock. The sums were small, but she entered them in a notebook. She did not mention repayment because she expected gratitude to discover its own obligations.

The committee received her assistance as committees receive assistance: at first as a favour, afterwards as an arrangement and finally as an interference. Mr. Holohan, who had conceived the concerts, limped from meeting to meeting with a stick and a grievance. He was known as Hoppy Holohan among men who accepted his cigarettes. He praised Mrs. Kearney’s energy and privately asked Fitzpatrick whether she meant to run the whole society.

“She has experience,” Fitzpatrick said.

“Experience of what?”

“Of organising.”

Mr. Holohan admitted that organisation was desirable, but he preferred it in persons who could be ignored.

The first concert was held on Wednesday evening. Rain had begun at four o’clock and continued with a steady civic impartiality. At seven the pavement outside the Antient Rooms shone black, and the lamps trembled in the pools. Inside, the hall smelled of damp coats, gas and newly cut greenery. Rows of chairs faced a platform whose patriotic drapery sagged at the centre.

Mrs. Kearney arrived with Kathleen and her younger daughter shortly after half-past six. Kathleen wore a cream blouse and a dark skirt; her music was carried in a leather case. Mrs. Kearney wore a new mantle trimmed with braid, severe enough for business and handsome enough for victory.

There were six people in the audience.

“Of course,” she said, looking over the empty chairs, “people in Dublin never come before the hour.”

At a quarter past eight there were thirty-seven, including two children and a priest. The committee had gathered near the side door and were discovering that no one possessed the key to the room where the extra candles were kept.

The artistes arrived separately and looked at one another with the professional disappointment of travellers who have been placed in the same third-class compartment. Mr. Bell, the tenor, was a narrow young man with a high collar and a pulse which could be seen in his temple. He asked three times whether the piano had been tuned. Mr. Duggan, the baritone, was broad and careful. He carried his own glass in a velvet bag and drank water only after examining it against the light.

Madam Glynn came last. She had once sung in London, a fact which had survived her voice. Her purple dress was decorated with black beads, and the gas above the platform gave every bead a sharp, unfriendly sparkle. She spoke of her engagements and called the hall intimate.

Miss Healy, the contralto, was young, pink and cheerful. She flirted with the tenor because he was frightened and with the baritone because he was not. She asked Kathleen whether the secretary was married.

“I don’t know,” Kathleen said.

“Your mother would know.”

Mrs. Kearney did know, but considered the subject unprofitable.

The concert began forty minutes late. Mr. Holohan made an apology which became an address, and Mr. Fitzpatrick stood behind him with the programmes, smiling whenever the words Ireland or art were used. The audience listened in damp patience.

Kathleen played well. She followed Mr. Bell through a song whose upper notes he approached as if they were creditors. She supplied Madam Glynn with firm chords while the soprano searched among them for the tune, and she supported Mr. Duggan’s patriotic ballad without allowing it to become military. Mrs. Kearney sat in the second row, upright and vigilant. At each applause she glanced around to estimate whether it was sufficient.

The programme was too long. Before the final recitation, several people left for the last tram. The reciter, a young solicitor with curling hair, spoke to the backs of their coats about the graves of the patriots. When it ended, the remaining audience applauded with relief and went out under umbrellas.

Backstage, Mr. Fitzpatrick thanked the artistes.

“A very promising opening,” he said.

“Promising what?” Mr. Duggan asked.

Mr. Fitzpatrick laughed carefully.

Mrs. Kearney waited until the others were gathering their music.

“I presume the arrangements for payment are in hand?” she said.

“Oh, certainly, Mrs. Kearney. At the conclusion of the series.”

“The contract says four concerts for eight guineas.”

“That’s right.”

“And all four are to take place?”

“Why wouldn’t they?”

She looked at the empty hall, where a caretaker was setting the chairs upright.

“No reason,” she said.

On Thursday the weather was dry and the attendance was worse.

The committee blamed a political meeting, an operatic company at the Theatre Royal and the public’s ignorance of its own culture. Mr. Holohan blamed the newspapers. Mr. Fitzpatrick blamed Thursday. Neither blamed the programme, though the programme had been assembled as a parcel is filled at Christmas, each contributor putting in something unsuitable and expecting thanks.

Mrs. Kearney learned that the Saturday concert was expected to be the important one. Several journalists had promised to attend, and Mr. O’Madden Burke, who wrote social and artistic paragraphs, had consented to look in. His name carried a family distinction from which his occupation borrowed regularly. He was a long, solemn man with a yellowish face, a silk hat and an umbrella stout enough to support both his body and his reputation.

“If Saturday is the important concert,” Mrs. Kearney said to Mr. Holohan, “why were we engaged for Wednesday and Thursday?”

“You must build up to a thing,” he replied.

“You appear to be building down.”

Mr. Holohan moved his lame foot under his chair.

“It’s not my department, you know.”

“What is your department?”

He considered this unfair.

The second concert began late and proceeded heavily. Madam Glynn’s first song was received in silence. During her second, a boy in the gallery laughed and was removed. The reciter forgot four lines and supplied their place with a stare. Miss Healy sang pleasantly, and Mr. Bell was applauded because a party of his cousins had come.

Afterwards Mrs. Kearney again approached Mr. Fitzpatrick.

“I want an assurance that my daughter’s fee is secure.”

“It’s all in the contract.”

“Then the money is available?”

“The treasurer deals with the funds.”

“Who is the treasurer?”

“Mr. Crowley.”

“I haven’t seen Mr. Crowley.”

“He’s not here tonight.”

“Was he here last night?”

“No, but he’ll be here Saturday.”

“And tomorrow?”

Mr. Fitzpatrick looked towards Mr. Holohan, who had begun a conversation with the caretaker.

“Well,” he said, “there may be a little alteration tomorrow.”

“What alteration?”

“Nothing decided. You’d better speak to Mr. Holohan.”

Mrs. Kearney spoke to Mr. Holohan. Mr. Holohan said the subcommittee would meet. She asked when. He said presently. She asked where. He said in the usual place. She observed that no one had informed her of a usual place.

On Friday morning a messenger brought a note to the Kearney house. Owing to circumstances beyond the control of the committee, that evening’s concert had been postponed. Tickets would remain valid for Saturday’s grand concluding performance.

Mrs. Kearney read the note at breakfast.

“Postponed until when?” Mr. Kearney asked.

“It means cancelled.”

“Ah.”

“There were four concerts in the contract.”

“That’s plain enough.”

“And eight guineas.”

“Eight guineas, yes.”

Kathleen spread marmalade on her toast and said nothing.

Her mother turned to her.

“You are not to worry. This is a matter of principle.”

“I’m not worried, Mamma.”

“You have done everything asked of you. More than was asked.”

“Yes, Mamma.”

“They will not take advantage of you.”

Mr. Kearney folded his paper.

“They might pay six guineas,” he said. “For three evenings.”

His wife looked at him as one might look at a man proposing to cut a yard from the national flag.

“The agreement is for eight.”

“Certainly,” he said, reopening the paper.

That afternoon Mrs. Kearney called at the society’s office. It occupied one upper room over a tobacconist’s shop. A girl at a desk said that Mr. Fitzpatrick was out. Mrs. Kearney asked for Mr. Holohan and was told that he had just left. She asked for Mr. Crowley. The girl had never heard of him.

On the wall hung a map of Ireland, a calendar three months behind and a framed declaration concerning the preservation of national ideals. On the desk were unpaid bills held under a stone paperweight shaped like a round tower.

Mrs. Kearney left a note requesting immediate confirmation that the full contracted amount would be paid before Saturday’s performance. She underlined full once and before twice.

No reply came.

By Saturday evening her indignation had acquired a plan.

The concluding concert had been advertised with unusual energy. A favourable paragraph appeared in the afternoon paper, describing the series as brilliant and the forthcoming finale as eagerly anticipated. Complimentary tickets had been distributed to journalists, councillors, teachers, cousins of the committee and persons who might afterwards say they had been present. The weather was fine. At half-past seven a considerable crowd stood in the entrance hall.

Inside, the platform had been improved with flags, flowers and a table for distinguished visitors. A bust of a poet had been borrowed from a school and placed among ferns. From certain seats it appeared to be hiding.

The artistes assembled in a room behind the stage. It was narrow and overheated, with a cracked mirror, a hat stand and one washbasin. Music lay on chairs; hatboxes lay under them. People entered, apologised, remained and made the room smaller.

Mrs. Kearney arrived with Kathleen at seven. She removed her gloves finger by finger and asked at once for Mr. Fitzpatrick.

“He’s about somewhere,” said Mr. Holohan.

“I wish to see him.”

“He’s very busy.”

“So am I.”

Mr. Holohan leaned on his stick.

“There’s a great house tonight,” he said, hoping the audience might count as an answer.

“I am glad the public has recovered its taste. Where is Mr. Fitzpatrick?”

Kathleen sat beside the piano and opened her case. Her mother shut it.

“You need not take out your music yet.”

Miss Healy, fastening a bracelet, looked into the mirror. Mr. Bell walked up and down, humming a scale under his breath. Mr. Duggan poured water into his private glass. Madam Glynn sat in a corner where the light fell directly upon her dress. The purple material had faded unevenly, and a shadow from the hat stand exposed the difference with unnecessary accuracy.

Mr. Fitzpatrick came in carrying a bundle of programmes. He saw Mrs. Kearney and became interested in arranging the bundle.

“I left a note yesterday,” she said.

“Yes, yes. I received it.”

“You did not answer.”

“We were all rushed off our feet.”

“I want my daughter’s fee.”

“The treasurer hasn’t arrived yet.”

“Then send for him.”

“He’s expected every minute.”

“What time is the concert to begin?”

“Eight sharp.”

“It is ten minutes to eight.”

“He’ll be here.”

“And if he is not?”

Mr. Fitzpatrick put one corner of a programme between his teeth. He seemed to reflect through it.

“There’s no difficulty, Mrs. Kearney.”

“There is a very simple difficulty. My daughter has a contract for four concerts. One concert has been cancelled. I have asked repeatedly for an assurance that she will receive eight guineas. No one will give it.”

“You have the contract.”

“I have. Have you the money?”

The corner of the programme softened in his mouth.

“You’d better speak to Mr. Holohan.”

“I have spoken to Mr. Holohan. He tells me nothing is his department.”

Mr. Holohan, who was within hearing, struck the floor lightly with his stick.

“I never said nothing, Mrs. Kearney.”

“You said payment was not your department.”

“Well, strictly speaking—”

“And Mr. Fitzpatrick says it is not his. Is there a gentleman here whose department includes keeping an agreement?”

The room became attentive. Miss Healy ceased fastening her bracelet. Mr. Duggan held his glass near his lips but did not drink.

From the hall came a swell of voices and the scraping of chairs. Someone tried three notes on the stage piano. Kathleen looked at her hands.

Mr. Fitzpatrick drew Mr. Holohan aside. They whispered near the door, both glancing at Mrs. Kearney. Then Mr. Holohan went out, moving quickly despite his foot.

“You see,” Mr. Fitzpatrick said, “it’s being attended to.”

“I shall see when the money is paid.”

At five past eight there was no money. The chairman of the society appeared, a tall greying man named Mr. Egan, who had the composed beard of a person accustomed to being introduced. He shook hands with the artistes, apologised for the pressure of public duties and asked when they were beginning.

“When my daughter is paid,” Mrs. Kearney said.

Mr. Egan turned his head slowly.

“I beg your pardon?”

“My daughter will not perform until the sum due under her contract is paid.”

“Surely this is not the moment—”

“It is precisely the moment. There have been many others, but the committee allowed them to pass.”

Mr. Egan stroked his beard. It had served him during controversies before.

“I understand there is some minor question.”

“Eight guineas is not a question. It is an amount.”

“I’m sure the committee will treat everyone honourably.”

“I am not asking to be treated honourably. I am asking to be paid.”

A burst of clapping came from the hall. It began in the gallery and spread downwards. Feet stamped. The platform door opened and a steward looked in with a white face.

“They’re getting impatient.”

“Start with the address,” Mr. Fitzpatrick said.

“The chairman’s to be introduced after the opening song.”

“Then introduce him before it.”

Mr. Egan objected that the printed order should be observed. Mrs. Kearney agreed with him.

“Nothing should be altered,” she said, “except a contract, apparently.”

Mr. Holohan returned with an elderly man in a dinner jacket too large at the shoulders. This was Mr. Crowley, the treasurer. He smelled faintly of cloves and appeared surprised to find himself necessary.

“What’s all this?” he said.

Mr. Fitzpatrick explained in whispers. Mr. Crowley listened, blinked and searched his inner pocket.

“The receipts aren’t counted,” he said.

“That does not concern me,” Mrs. Kearney replied.

“We can’t pay out before the receipts are counted.”

“The contract was made before the receipts existed.”

Mr. Crowley looked towards Mr. Egan. Mr. Egan looked towards Mr. Holohan. Mr. Holohan examined the rubber tip of his stick. The committee, having distributed responsibility among itself, possessed none collectively.

The audience clapped again. This time there were whistles.

Mr. Bell’s temple pulsed visibly. “Really,” he said, “something must be done. It’s very bad for the voice, waiting like this.”

Mr. Duggan set down his glass.

“I don’t interfere in business matters,” he announced. “An artiste must keep himself outside them.”

Madam Glynn nodded. She had spent much of her career outside money that was due to her.

Mr. O’Madden Burke entered with his hat and umbrella. He surveyed the room, recognised a dispute and placed himself at once where he could observe it without belonging to it. His long body rested on the umbrella; his expression rested on his name.

“What’s delaying us?” he asked pleasantly.

“A private matter,” Mr. Egan said.

Mrs. Kearney faced him.

“It is not private if my daughter is expected to perform before the public. She has not been paid.”

O’Madden Burke raised his brows with journalistic sympathy.

“A regrettable misunderstanding.”

“There is no misunderstanding.”

“Of course not,” he said, having meant that there was.

Mr. Crowley counted four sovereigns and four shillings into his palm.

“We can give half now,” he said. “The balance after the concert.”

“The full amount now.”

“That’s impossible.”

“Then Kathleen will not go on.”

Kathleen flushed. All eyes turned towards her, as if she had personally invented the sentence.

“Mamma,” she said softly.

Her mother did not look at her.

“She won’t go on.”

The phrase passed through the room. Mr. Bell repeated it to Miss Healy; the steward carried it to the wings; someone in the corridor asked who would not go on. It acquired importance as it travelled.

Mr. Egan drew Mrs. Kearney towards the cracked mirror and spoke in a low voice.

“You are placing your daughter in a very awkward position.”

“The committee placed her there.”

“Her professional reputation may suffer.”

“Her professional reputation is not maintained by working without payment.”

“No one suggests without payment.”

“I have had suggestions for three days. I prefer money.”

The audience began to stamp in rhythm.

At last Mr. Crowley put four guineas into an envelope. He wrote something across the flap and handed it to Mrs. Kearney.

“That is half,” she said.

“And the balance will be paid at the interval.”

“Before the second half?”

“Yes, yes,” said Mr. Fitzpatrick.

“Is that the undertaking of the committee?”

Mr. Egan glanced around at the other men. They gave small nods, each hoping his own would not be regarded as official.

“You have my assurance,” he said.

Mrs. Kearney counted the money, placed the envelope in her bag and opened Kathleen’s music case.

“You may go on for the first half,” she said.

Relief moved through the room with almost physical warmth. The steward rushed out. Mr. Bell rearranged his cuffs. Miss Healy smiled at Kathleen, though not too openly. Mr. Holohan wiped his forehead.

The concert began twenty-seven minutes late.

Once the platform was occupied and the first applause had died, the disorder behind it vanished from public knowledge. Kathleen played the opening bars steadily. Mr. Bell sang better than he had on either previous evening. His fear, having found an external cause, became energy. Miss Healy’s song was encored, and Mr. Duggan delivered his ballad with a noble restraint which he had reserved for the large attendance.

The hall was bright and full. Flags concealed the stains on the wall; the borrowed palms stood green under the gas; the committee sat in the front row wearing expressions of cultural guardianship. Mr. Egan’s address referred to sacrifice, continuity and the duty owed by each generation to the next. He said that national art could flourish only when personal interests were forgotten in a common devotion. The audience applauded warmly. Behind the curtain Mrs. Kearney heard every word.

Kathleen accompanied without fault. Her face was pale and calm. Once, during a long introduction, she looked towards the wings where her mother stood. Mrs. Kearney inclined her head, encouragingly and commandingly.

At the interval there was much movement. Friends came backstage; journalists sought names; members of the committee congratulated one another on the success which had occurred after they ceased arranging it. The narrow room filled with coats, perfume, damp wool and praise.

O’Madden Burke spoke to Miss Healy near the mirror. He told her that her voice possessed an unforced national quality. She asked whether that was good. He said it was rare. Madam Glynn stood beside them, smiling with the patient bitterness of an elder artiste who has heard rarity before.

Mrs. Kearney approached Mr. Crowley.

“The balance, please.”

He was speaking to Mr. Egan and pretended not to hear. She repeated the words.

Mr. Egan turned.

“The receipts are still being dealt with.”

“You gave your assurance.”

“I assured you the matter would be settled.”

“You assured me the balance would be paid at the interval.”

“I said after the first half.”

“It is after the first half.”

Mr. Fitzpatrick came up, chewing another programme. Its corner had already become a grey twist.

“We can’t have this discussion now,” he said.

“We had it before the concert. You postponed it until now.”

Mr. Crowley buttoned his coat over the pocket containing the money.

“The committee has reconsidered the position,” he said.

“What position?”

“The third concert did not take place.”

“That was your decision.”

“There were only three performances.”

“There was a contract for four.”

“You cannot expect payment for work not done.”

“My daughter was ready to do it. Your committee cancelled.”

Mr. Egan raised a calming hand.

“We are prepared to pay the remaining amount appropriate to three concerts.”

“What amount?”

“Two guineas.”

Mrs. Kearney stared at him.

“You owe four.”

“We owe, at most, two.”

“You owe four.”

The room quietened by degrees. A violinist who had come for the second half tightened his bow noiselessly. Miss Healy lowered her eyes. Mr. Duggan moved his shoe along the floor and examined the mark it made.

Mr. Egan’s beard became judicial.

“If Miss Kearney refuses to continue, the committee will regard the contract as broken by her.”

“She has already performed more than half the programme tonight.”

“That is irrelevant.”

“It was relevant when the audience was stamping.”

“You accepted four guineas.”

“On account.”

“You accepted the committee’s arrangement.”

“I accepted your promise.”

Mr. Fitzpatrick took the paper from his mouth.

“Now, Mrs. Kearney, be reasonable.”

“I have been reasonable since Wednesday.”

“There’s a hall full of people.”

“You knew that when you promised payment.”

“You’re injuring the society.”

“The society has injured itself.”

Mr. Holohan intervened with an air of painful friendship.

“For Kathleen’s sake, let the girl finish. We’ll talk afterwards.”

“There has been too much talk afterwards.”

“You’ll ruin her chances.”

“What chances? To be cheated again?”

Kathleen stood near the piano, holding a sheaf of music against her blouse. Her eyes were bright, but she did not speak. Mrs. Kearney saw in her silence obedience, distress and gratitude in the proportions required by motherhood.

“She won’t go on,” she said.

Mr. Egan’s face hardened.

“Then we must replace her.”

“You may do as you please.”

A young woman named Miss Beirne, who had come to turn pages for the violinist, said that she could accompany some of the songs. She had studied them once. The committee surrounded her with sudden confidence.

“Of course you can,” said Mr. Fitzpatrick.

“It’s only the second half,” said Mr. Holohan.

“An artiste rises to an occasion,” said Mr. Duggan.

Miss Beirne looked alarmed and pleased. She sat at the piano and tried several bars. The violinist bent over her shoulder. Miss Healy found the music for her song and pointed out a cut.

Public opinion in the room rearranged itself quickly. While Mrs. Kearney had been demanding money, the artistes had admitted privately that she was right. Now that another accompanist had been found, her rightness became inconvenient, and inconvenience was a form of bad manners. Mr. Bell said it was a pity to make a scene. Madam Glynn said artists often had to overlook little irregularities. Mr. Duggan repeated that he never mixed himself in financial affairs, especially when his own fee was safe.

O’Madden Burke watched Mrs. Kearney with regretful interest. He had expected the evening to yield a paragraph about culture; it now offered character.

Mrs. Kearney helped Kathleen into her coat.

“We are leaving.”

“Mamma, my music—”

“Collect it.”

Kathleen gathered the loose sheets. One slid under a chair. Miss Healy picked it up and handed it to her.

“I’m sorry,” she whispered.

Mrs. Kearney heard.

“There is nothing for my daughter to be sorry about.”

Miss Healy coloured and turned away.

Mr. Holohan stood by the door.

“You’re taking a very serious course, Mrs. Kearney.”

“I shall take a more serious one on Monday.”

“What do you mean by that?”

“You will hear from my solicitor.”

Mr. Fitzpatrick gave a short laugh, but no one joined him.

“Oh, will we?” he said.

“You will. I am not done with this society.”

Mr. Egan stepped forward. The applause in the hall had begun again, summoning the second half. Behind him Miss Beirne struck a wrong chord and stopped.

“We have done all we can,” he said. “If you choose to behave in this fashion, the responsibility is yours.”

“The responsibility is yours. You made an agreement and broke it.”

“You have disrupted a public concert.”

“You delayed it because you would not pay.”

“You have embarrassed your own daughter.”

Mrs. Kearney’s face changed. Until then her anger had been kept within the forms of business; now it escaped them.

“No gentleman would speak to a mother like that.”

“No lady would conduct herself as you have done.”

The sentence produced a stillness more complete than shouting. Mrs. Kearney drew herself up. Her mantle, chosen for dignity, seemed suddenly too heavy for the room.

“I’m not done with you yet,” she said.

Mr. Egan held the door open.

“But I am done with you.”

She passed him without looking aside. Kathleen followed, carrying the music case, and Mr. Kearney’s younger daughter, who had waited in the corridor through the entire dispute, hurried after them.

They descended the broad staircase while the audience above clapped and stamped. At the entrance, a boy offered Mrs. Kearney a programme. She brushed it away. Outside, the street was cold and dry. Cars stood in a line beside the pavement, and drivers called to one another under the lamps.

Mrs. Kearney walked rapidly towards the tram stop. Kathleen kept pace beside her.

“You played extremely well,” her mother said.

“Thank you, Mamma.”

“They would have taken every advantage if I had allowed it.”

“Yes, Mamma.”

“Your father will agree.”

Kathleen looked down at the case in her hand.

“I’m sure he will.”

Behind them, in the Antient Rooms, Miss Beirne began the second half. She stumbled in the introduction, found the key and continued. The audience, which knew nothing of the amount in dispute and cared less, settled back to listen.

In the artists’ room Mr. Fitzpatrick straightened the remaining programmes and said that firmness had been necessary. Mr. Holohan agreed that there was no dealing with some people. Mr. Crowley counted the receipts at last. Mr. Duggan drank from his private glass, and Madam Glynn observed that professional life required tact.

O’Madden Burke opened his umbrella before reaching the street, though it had not begun to rain. He paused beneath it to tell Mr. Egan that the committee had acted with admirable spirit.

Mr. Egan accepted the compliment gravely.

The concert, they both agreed, had been a great success.

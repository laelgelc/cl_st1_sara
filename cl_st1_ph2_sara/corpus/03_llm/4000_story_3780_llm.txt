## THE THIRD SIGNAL

Uncle Peter had followed the controversy among the nature writers with an attention which he described, according to the company in which he found himself, as philosophical, philological, patriotic, or merely humane.

Philosophically, he wished to determine where observation ended and imagination began. Philologically, he was interested in the precise meaning of such expressions as “the fox reflected,” “the oak remembered,” and “the humble beetle smiled.” Patriotically, he considered it intolerable that foreign naturalists should possess rabbits with deeper emotions than our own. Humanely, he desired peace between two distinguished authors, one of whom had accused the other of attributing Presbyterian scruples to a woodchuck, while the other had replied by charging his opponent with spiritual blindness toward field-mice.

But the deepest reason was personal. One of the combatants had once reviewed Uncle Peter’s book on migratory fish in terms which suggested that he had never met either a fish or a verb.

“I shall intervene,” said Uncle Peter.

“Under your own name?” I asked.

“Certainly not. A peace-maker should be invisible. If either party suspects that peace is being made, he will resist it.”

He therefore retired to the summer-house with foolscap, ink, and a volume of improving tales which he said was necessary to put his mind into a pure condition. For three afternoons we heard nothing except the scratching of his pen and, once, a prolonged chuckle ending in a cough. On the fourth day he brought me a manuscript.

“I have attempted,” he said, “to combine sympathy with exact science. Every emotion in the story may be verified by anyone having a garden and sufficient imagination.”

This was the manuscript.

Little Red Tom was born upon a warm June morning, though the unimaginative observer might have said merely that he had set.

There are persons who can pass a tomato-vine without perceiving anything but leaves, blossoms, and perhaps the promise of a future sauce. Such persons are not wicked; they are only incomplete. To the sympathetic eye the garden is no mute assemblage of roots and stalks, but a commonwealth of hope, rivalry, domestic affection, private ambition, and green anxiety. The beans climb because they aspire; the onions preserve their reserve in layers; the corn, conscious of unusual height, looks down upon its neighbours; the sage reflects; the mint remembers; while the peppers, though outwardly composed, nourish feelings which only the rash would provoke.

In this various society Tom came into life.

He was the youngest of seven, and at first so small, so green, so inconspicuous beneath the sheltering leaves, that even the dew scarcely knew where to find him. His brothers, already round and firm, spoke among themselves of sunlight, size, and the great world beyond the paling. Tom listened, wondering. Who can trace the delicate channels by which knowledge enters a young tomato? Who shall say that because he possesses neither ears nor a schoolmaster he is therefore uneducated?

His mother bent over him with that tender curve which no botanist has adequately explained.

“My little Tom,” she whispered, when the afternoon breeze moved leaf against leaf, “you are a late child, but you must not be discouraged. Ripeness is not achieved in a day.”

“I want to be red now,” said Tom.

There is in extreme youth a touching impatience to resemble the mature.

“No, Tommykin. First you must grow.”

“Why?”

“Because growth comes before colour.”

“Why?”

“Because it does.”

At this answer, which has sustained parental authority since the beginning of the world, Tom was silent.

His mother taught him to receive the rain without complaint, to turn himself modestly toward the sun, and never to despise the soil merely because it was beneath him. She told him that a tomato must be true to his own nature.

“The bean may climb,” she said, “and the cucumber may trail. The radish may bury his gifts and the sunflower advertise hers. We must not judge them. But you, my son, must become round.”

Tom endeavoured to become round.

Simple process! Yet what struggles, what secret adjustments, what silent sacrifice are concealed in that easy phrase! Day after day he drew nourishment through the mysterious household economy of the vine. He expanded beneath the blue heaven. The small inequalities of childhood disappeared; his sides became smooth; he acquired weight, dignity, and a certain innocent self-importance.

His mother watched him with joy.

“Grow, dear one,” she murmured. “Grow large, but not proud.”

Thus passed the golden weeks of his infancy.

Yet even in those days of security, shadows crossed the mother-heart. She had lived through seasons. She had seen blossoms fall untimely, green fruit perish beneath cold rain, and whole branches stripped by enormous creatures carrying baskets. She knew that beauty, in a kitchen garden, is often only another name for danger.

One evening, when the sun lay low behind the currant bushes and the peas were composing themselves for night, she drew Tom close beneath a leaf.

“My child,” she said, “you are growing beyond my hopes. Soon you must learn the signals.”

“Are they difficult?”

“No signal is difficult to one who obeys promptly.”

She then told him of the birds.

“They come through the air, singing.”

“Then they are good?” asked Tom.

Alas, how naturally the young associate music with virtue!

“Not always. Beware especially of the robin. He has a bright eye, a confident manner, and a breast which poets praise. But he is capable of hunger.”

“Could anyone with a red breast injure one who hopes to have a red skin?”

“Resemblance does not always produce mercy,” replied his mother sadly. “When I see a bird, I shall give the first signal—‘Droop!’ At that word you must hang your head beneath the leaves and remain perfectly still.”

Tom practised until his mother was satisfied. At “Droop!” his stem yielded, his small body swung inward, and the great green leaf hid him.

“Excellent, Tommykin. Obedience is the armour of the tender.”

The trial came sooner than either expected.

At noon next day the garden slept in heat. The cabbages sat in heavy meditation; the lettuce, being of a more frivolous disposition, fluttered at every insect; and a row of beets maintained a dark reserve concerning subterranean matters. Suddenly a shadow passed over Tom.

On the fence stood a robin.

Who could think that such a creature could be cruel? His head was alert, his feathers neat, his eye polished like a bead on a child’s purse. He cocked his head toward the vine with an air almost conversational.

“Good morning,” he said.

Tom, flattered by the notice of so accomplished a traveller, moved forward.

“Droop!” cried his mother.

He obeyed.

The robin flew down, searched among the leaves, found nothing, and departed with a worm whose domestic history does not belong to this narrative. Tom remained hidden, trembling with the faint oscillation which the careless might attribute to wind.

“My mother saved me,” he thought.

Can we prove that he thought it? No. Can we prove that he did not? Still less. And shall the absence of documentary evidence close the courts of sympathy?

From that hour Tom’s love for his mother deepened. He grew obediently, rounded conscientiously, and bore himself with the humility appropriate to increasing circumference. But a new desire awakened within him.

“When may I become red?” he asked.

“When you are ready.”

“I feel ready.”

“That is often a sign that one is not.”

Nevertheless, a pale change had begun near his stem. Green softened into a thoughtful yellow; yellow warmed into orange; orange, after lingering delicately upon the borderland of indecision, surrendered to crimson. It was as if his childhood had been washed away by invisible sunsets.

His brothers regarded him with mixed emotions. The eldest, already flushed, advised patience. The second, who remained obstinately green, called colour a superficial distinction. The third claimed to have been red for several days on the side which nobody could see.

Tom said nothing. He ripened.

His mother now taught him the second signal.

“There are beings more dangerous than birds,” she said.

“Do they fly?”

“No. They walk heavily upon the earth, though they consider themselves its masters. They carry knives, baskets, strings, shears, and theories about cultivation. They call themselves gardeners.”

“What do they want?”

“They want whatever has grown well.”

The injustice of this idea overwhelmed Tom. “Then why do they help us grow?”

“My child,” said his mother, “there are questions at which even the oldest vine can only tremble.”

The second signal was “Snuggle!”

“When you hear it,” she explained, “press yourself close against my stem. Let the leaves surround you. Make yourself small.”

“But I am large now.”

“One may be large in prosperity and small in danger.”

Again Tom practised. Again his obedience was perfect.

Several mornings later footsteps sounded upon the path: deliberate footsteps, crushing gravel with the calm assurance of power. The gardener approached.

He was an elderly man with silver hair, mild blue eyes, and a face which had often been called benevolent. In one hand he bore a basket. In the other he carried a pair of shears.

Could those mild eyes behold suffering? Could those silver hairs crown a destroyer? Could the lips which whistled an old hymn pronounce sentence upon a family of tomatoes?

They could.

“What beauties!” he said.

Tom’s mother shuddered through every fibre.

“Snuggle!”

Tom pressed against her. A leaf fell over him just as the gardener lifted the first brother from the vine.

There was a slight snap.

“Mother!” cried Tom inwardly.

Another brother followed; then another. The gardener placed them in his basket without inquiry into their wishes, their education, or their views concerning salad. The eldest, the second, the boastful third—all were taken. One had not yet attained his full redness. Another had hoped to see the evening star. Their hopes were equal beneath the shears.

The gardener moved away, humming.

For a long time no one spoke.

At last Tom whispered, “Where have they gone?”

His mother did not answer directly. Nature’s parents, like our own, sometimes substitute an embrace for information.

“You are safe,” she said.

“But will they come back?”

A drop of moisture trembled upon a leaf. The scientific observer would have identified it as the residue of watering. The heart knows better.

After this escape Tom’s colour deepened with almost tragic rapidity. He became crimson from stem to base, glowing among the green like a small captured sunset. Every passer-by might now perceive him. Beauty had completed its fatal advertisement.

His mother watched in mingled pride and fear.

“You have learned two signals,” she said. “There is a third danger, more subtle than bird or gardener.”

“What is it?”

“I will tell you this evening, when the shade is cool.”

“Why not now?”

“Because,” she replied, with the mysterious confidence of motherhood, “there is time.”

Ah, fatal phrase! How many tragedies have entered through that little door! Time, which appears so broad before catastrophe, becomes afterward a thread too narrow to hold one regret.

The afternoon was of surpassing loveliness—the last!

Warm light slept upon the paths. Bees conducted their business with an air of public importance. The beans climbed higher, though there was nothing above them worth reaching; the corn tossed its tassels in high spirits; the eggplants brooded over dark schemes; the raspberries rasped their neighbours; and the pumpkins, possessing the serenity of bulk, spread themselves over territory which morality could scarcely call their own.

Tom was happy.

“I am large,” he thought. “I am red. I have escaped the bird and the gardener. This is life.”

Then the gate clicked.

A young woman entered the garden.

She wore a white dress and carried no basket. Her hands were empty; her face was fresh; a blue ribbon rested among her brown hair. She came down the path lightly, lightly, lightly, pausing to smell a rose.

Who could fear her?

She stooped to admire the lavender. She touched a sunflower without harming it. A butterfly settled upon her sleeve, remained there in confidence, and flew away uninjured.

Who could think her cruel?

She sang a little song.

Who, hearing that innocent voice, could imagine violence?

Tom’s mother saw her—and all her leaves contracted.

“The third signal!” she gasped.

But she had not taught it.

The young woman came nearer. She passed the sage, which offered philosophy too late; she passed the peppers, which burned with helpless indignation; she passed the onion bed, where many would presently weep, though for reasons imperfectly understood. Then she saw Tom.

“Oh!” she exclaimed. “What a lovely tomato!”

Praise, the oldest snare, entered his innocent heart.

“She understands my redness,” he thought.

Her fingers closed around him.

“Mother!” cried Tom.

The vine strained. His mother held him with every fibre that the laws of botany permitted. For one instant love opposed appetite.

Then came the wrench.

He was torn from home, nourishment, childhood, and all familiar green things. He saw his mother whirl away beneath him; he saw the sky widen; he saw the garden from an appalling height.

The young woman carried him into the house.

No signal could save him now.

In the kitchen she laid him upon a white board beside a cucumber whose cool exterior concealed despair. Nearby waited a head of lettuce, pale with apprehension, and several radishes, rosy, round, and doomed. A knife flashed.

“Perfect for supper,” said the young woman.

Perfect! Thus does the world compliment what it intends to destroy.

Tom was washed in icy water. He was turned over, examined, and praised. Then the knife descended.

Let us not dwell upon particulars. There are limits beyond which even sympathetic literature should not pass, especially before dinner. It is enough to say that the unity he had spent his whole life achieving was ruthlessly divided. His seeds, each perhaps containing an unborn dream of vine and sunlight, were scattered. Salt fell upon him. Pepper followed—pepper, his neighbour and former fellow-citizen, now ground past recognition. Oil covered all. Vinegar, sour product of a disappointed world, completed the ceremony.

Tom had become salad.

At the table sat the young woman, her father, her mother, and a small boy whose innocence was loudly admired. They spoke of the weather, music, politics, and the moral training of children. Not one spoke of Tom.

His portions were distributed among them.

The father ate wheat which had once waved freely in a golden field until cut down by machinery. The mother ate a fish whose silver youth had passed among cool streams and wavering weeds. The young woman ate grouse, torn from the purple moor by a gun she had never seen. The little boy, after rejecting the lettuce as if its sacrifice were insufficiently attractive, accepted Tom with sugar.

Around them lay the fallen commonwealth of earth and water: grain crushed, fruit peeled, roots boiled, leaves shredded, milk taken, eggs broken, fish silenced, bird dismembered. Yet the family smiled. They even gave thanks.

Was gratitude enough? Could a blessing restore the wheat to the wind, the fish to the river, the grouse to the heather, the lettuce to its cool bed, or Little Red Tom to the mother-vine that waited in the darkening garden?

Not a bit of it.

When evening came, Tom’s mother still held the tiny green cup from which he had been torn. The robin sang upon the fence. The gardener shut the gate. Stars appeared over the corn, and the night breeze moved softly among the leaves.

“Tommykin,” whispered the vine.

But no answer came.

Here Uncle Peter’s manuscript ended. Beneath the last line, in smaller handwriting, appeared the following note:

“Moral to be supplied when the author has discovered what, upon these principles, a sensitive person may safely eat.”

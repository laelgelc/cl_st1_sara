## The Paradise Behind the Mirror

In the year when the Council of Constance had gathered beneath one roof—or beneath five hundred roofs, for Christendom was too corpulent to lodge in one house—all the wisdom, holiness, ambition, avarice, learning, ignorance, continence, lechery, and good digestion of the Church appeared to have emptied itself into that excellent city.

There were cardinals in scarlet, bishops in violet, abbots round as wine-casks, doctors thin as quills, ambassadors stiff with embroidery, princes who heard Mass before cheating at dice, theologians who disputed concerning grace until supper and afterward concerning the quality of the cook, together with secretaries, beadles, notaries, cooks, muleteers, singers, soldiers, barbers, astrologers, physicians, Jews, Lombard bankers, German innkeepers, French parasites, Italian musicians, Bohemian heretics, and women of such beauty and various price that many a grave father of the Council mistook Constance for the antechamber of heaven.

It was calculated by a clerk of the Rota, who had a particular genius for figures whenever they related to money or women, that in those days the city contained three thousand priests, five thousand lackeys, seven hundred public cooks, four hundred private mistresses, and no fewer than six hundred and twelve recognized courtesans, without counting amateurs, widows, pilgrims, laundresses, and certain noble ladies whose sins, being protected by coats of arms, were not subject to arithmetic.

Among this multitude was one Philippe de Mala, a young priest attached to the household of the Archbishop of Bordeaux. Philippe possessed neither family, benefice, influence, nor belly. His cassock shone at the elbows, his shoes admitted both water and theology, his purse contained seven poor silver pieces that clung together for company, and his knowledge of the world consisted chiefly in knowing that those who spoke most loudly of renouncing it generally had the largest share.

He was twenty-two years of age, with smooth cheeks, grave grey eyes, a mouth made rather for kissing than chanting, and a figure so slender that the archbishop’s steward frequently forgot to include him when counting the mouths at table. He had good morals because he had lacked opportunities for bad ones; he was humble because no one had yet offered him anything worth being proud of; and he was chaste in the same manner that a fasting dog is temperate in a locked kitchen.

At first Philippe was overwhelmed by the magnificence of the Council. He watched cardinals pass beneath canopies while servants carried their trains out of the mud. He saw bishops lodged in palaces, abbots served upon silver, legates borne in chairs, and doctors of theology followed by pages with velvet caps. Each morning these holy men assembled to restore peace to the Church; each evening they quarreled over precedence, pensions, sauces, horses, beds, and women. The scandal astonished Philippe for three days. On the fourth he began to envy it.

“Surely,” he reflected, “God does not fatten His servants without purpose. If Monseigneur the Bishop of Toul eats six dishes at noon and four at night, it must be to sustain the faith. If the Cardinal of Ragusa keeps three cooks, two confessors, and one Venetian lady, then cookery, absolution, and beauty must all be roads to salvation. Why, therefore, should I reach paradise barefoot when these great men ride there upon mules?”

This argument appeared to him invincible, and indeed many benefices have been founded upon worse logic.

Now the windows of Philippe’s lodging overlooked a street through which the celebrated women of Constance often passed on their way to Mass, to supper, or from the one to the other; for they were devout in the mornings, merry at night, and charitable whenever charity wore gold spurs. Philippe began by lowering his eyes. Then he lowered them more slowly. At last he looked straight ahead and trusted to divine mercy.

Of all the ladies who dazzled that ecclesiastical marketplace, none equaled Imperia.

Her fame filled Constance as the smell of roasted peacocks filled an episcopal kitchen. Princes sent her jewels, ambassadors sent poems composed by their secretaries, captains sent Turkish silks, and prelates sent both poems and jewels, afterward charging them to the expenses of their dioceses. She was said to have refused a duke because his hands were dirty, accepted a banker because his money was clean, and dismissed a theologian because he tried to explain her beauty according to Aristotle.

Her house stood near the lake, with green shutters, a carved doorway, and a brass knocker in the shape of Cupid, though certain churchmen, approaching after dark, declared it represented Saint Michael. Within were tapestries of Troy, mirrors from Venice, carpets from the East, silver lamps, perfumed braziers, painted chests, cushions, lutes, goblets, and every other instrument by which the rich defend themselves against repentance.

Philippe first saw her clearly on the steps of the cathedral.

She came out after Mass with two maids and a little black page. Her gown was of dark blue velvet, sewn with pearls small enough to escape the commandment against excessive ornament, though numerous enough to break it in spirit. Her hair, black and heavy, was braided beneath a veil fine as a bishop’s excuse. Her skin had that warm ivory colour which painters seek by mixing milk with sunlight; her eyes were darker than midnight in a confessional; her feet were so small that they seemed to mock the long shoes worn by fashionable men; and when she smiled upon an old German count, the poor warrior bent double as if struck by a cannon-ball.

Philippe stood motionless, holding his breviary upside down.

Imperia saw him.

She had learned to read men as readily as a banker reads figures. One glance told her that Philippe was young, poor, innocent, frightened, and already lost. This combination amused her. She paused, turned her black eyes full upon him, and smiled—not the smile she gave the count, which had been valued at a ruby, but a lighter one, worth perhaps a rose.

Philippe’s heart leapt like a trout in a frying-pan.

He followed her.

Let us not accuse him too hastily. A dog follows meat, a courtier follows money, a bishop follows promotion, and a young priest follows beauty; each creature obeys the law written for it. Philippe went after Imperia through three streets, across a market, under an arch, and nearly into the lake, seeing nothing except the motion of her gown. When she entered her house, he remained before the door, gazing at Cupid’s brass nose.

“Knock,” said a voice above him.

Philippe looked up. Imperia stood at an open window.

“Madame?”

“You have followed me as faithfully as a creditor. Either knock or go away. Thou art making my door appear more holy than the cathedral.”

She vanished, laughing.

Philippe’s knees began to debate the matter. His right hand reached for the knocker, while his left tried to restrain it. Modesty said, “Depart.” Desire said, “Knock.” Poverty said, “She will mock thee.” Hope replied, “Perhaps she mocks without charge.” Finally youth, which is always chairman in such councils, decided the vote.

He knocked.

The little black page admitted him and led him through a hall where two greyhounds slept upon cushions finer than Philippe’s bed. In a chamber overlooking the lake sat Imperia, now without her veil, combing her hair before a Venetian mirror. A maid named Toinette stood behind her, arranging silver pins.

“Well, little clerk,” said Imperia, not turning, “what sin brings thee here?”

Philippe opened his mouth.

Nothing emerged.

“Perhaps he has taken a vow of silence,” said Toinette.

“No,” replied Imperia. “He was eloquent enough with his eyes.”

Philippe tried again. “Madame, I—I came because—”

“Because the door opened?”

“Because you are beautiful.”

This answer, being neither clever nor new, should have failed; but Philippe uttered it with such solemn distress that Imperia glanced at him in the mirror.

“How beautiful?”

“As—as the New Jerusalem.”

Toinette burst out laughing.

“Mind thy theology,” said Imperia. “The New Jerusalem has twelve gates of pearl, and I have but one door, which thou nearly lacked courage to enter.”

“I would have entered through the wall.”

“With what? Thy head?”

“If necessary.”

Imperia turned on her stool. “Come nearer.”

He obeyed, blushing from his collar to his hair.

She examined him as one examines a small horse offered unexpectedly in payment of a debt. His cassock was brushed but threadbare, his linen clean but coarse, his hands fine, his eyes sincere, and his shoes deplorable. She touched one sleeve.

“Thy velvet is very humble.”

“It is not velvet.”

“Indeed? Then poverty has invented a new cloth.”

Philippe dropped his gaze. “I have nothing, Madame.”

“No silver?”

“Seven pieces.”

“No horse?”

“I sometimes hold Monseigneur’s mule.”

“No benefice?”

“No.”

“No mistress?”

At this he looked up. “Not yet.”

Imperia laughed so suddenly that a silver pin fell from her hair.

“By the Pope’s slipper! Thou hast courage hidden somewhere beneath those rags. What is thy name?”

“Philippe de Mala.”

“Whom dost thou serve?”

“The Archbishop of Bordeaux.”

“Ah! A worthy prelate, famous for moderation. He eats only when awake and drinks only when thirsty, which is continually. Does he know thou art here?”

“No, Madame.”

“Then tell him. A priest should conceal nothing from his spiritual superior, unless it concerns money.”

She rose, and Philippe discovered that she was a little taller than he had imagined and infinitely more formidable. Coming close, she lifted his chin with one finger.

“Why didst thou follow me?”

“Because I love you.”

“Since when?”

“Since Mass.”

“Thy passion is three hours old.”

“It was born full-grown.”

“And what wilt thou do with it?”

“Whatever you command.”

This pleased her more than polished speeches would have done. Men generally entered Imperia’s house declaring what they possessed, what they desired, and what they would pay; Philippe came with seven coins, ruined shoes, and a heart beating like a hammer. Such novelty is precious to a woman bored by magnificence.

“Return tomorrow evening,” she said.

Philippe stared.

“Didst thou hear?”

“Yes.”

“At the hour of Vespers.”

“Yes.”

“Was that all?”

“No. I mean—yes. I mean—Madame—”

“Toinette, show this learned doctor to the door before grammar kills him.”

Philippe backed away, struck a stool, apologized to it, bowed to Imperia, bowed to Toinette, bowed by mistake to the greyhound in the hall, and reached the street in such beatitude that he walked directly into a fishmonger’s basket.

That evening the Archbishop of Bordeaux found his young priest kneeling before the hearth, stirring the coals with a spoon and smiling at nothing.

“Philippe,” said the archbishop, “thou lookest like a saint who has seen a vision, or a fool who has borrowed money. Which is it?”

Philippe had been taught that confession cleansed the soul. He therefore confessed, though not until he had made sure the door was shut.

“Monseigneur, I have seen Imperia.”

“So have half the Council.”

“I entered her house.”

“So has the other half.”

“She has commanded me to return tomorrow.”

The archbishop lowered the cup he was carrying. He was a man of fifty-eight, broad-shouldered, red-cheeked, and richly educated in canon law, diplomacy, and Burgundy wine. His morals were excellent whenever gout confined him to bed.

“Commanded thee?”

“Yes, Monseigneur.”

“Without payment?”

“She did not speak of payment.”

The archbishop looked upon Philippe with new respect.

“My son, there are three ways to rise in the Church: family, money, and miracle. Thou hast neither family nor money. Guard this miracle carefully.”

“Then you do not forbid me?”

“Forbid thee? I am thy archbishop, not thy executioner. Nevertheless, thou must not go looking like the resurrection of a scarecrow.”

The good prelate opened a chest and gave Philippe a lace collar, saying it had belonged to a nephew who died of fever; this was true, although the fever had been assisted by a jealous husband. He added a small flask of perfume, two pieces of advice, and no money.

Philippe spent the next day preparing as if for consecration. He paid a barber, hired a dark-green doublet from an actor, purchased a pair of silk stockings with a repaired knee, and bribed a shoemaker to lend him boots that pinched like inquisitors. He bought gloves, ribbons, and a little bunch of violets. When all was done, one silver piece remained, which he gave to a beggar in a transport of generosity, afterward regretting it when he remembered supper.

At Vespers he presented himself at Imperia’s door.

Toinette opened it, inspected him from boots to collar, and cried, “The scarecrow has flowered!”

Imperia received him in a small chamber hung with crimson damask. She wore a gown the colour of pale gold, low at the throat, with loose sleeves lined in white silk. A chain of emeralds rested upon her bosom like green leaves upon snow. Candles multiplied themselves in mirrors. The table was laid for two with Venetian glass, silver plates, white bread, trout from the lake, little pies, figs, oranges, sugared almonds, a capon perfumed with rosemary, and three wines, because two would have suggested poverty and four intemperance.

“You are transformed,” she said.

“I borrowed most of it.”

“Even thy face?”

“The barber scraped it.”

“He did well. Yesterday thou resembled a novice; tonight thou resembleth a novice dressed for temptation.”

She gave him her hand. Philippe kissed it too fervently and nearly drew off one of her rings with his lips.

“Gently,” she said. “I am not a saint’s relic to be carried away by pilgrims.”

They sat.

At first Philippe scarcely ate. He watched Imperia lift her cup, break bread, touch a fig with her white fingers, and every ordinary action appeared to him a mystery worthy of a council. She, observing his worship, amused herself by asking questions.

“Do priests think of women during Mass?”

“Some do.”

“And thou?”

“Not before yesterday.”

“Do they speak of us in confession?”

“Often.”

“Kindly?”

“Rarely.”

“Then they fear us.”

“Certainly.”

“Why?”

Philippe considered. “Because women can make men forget what men claim is most important.”

Imperia’s smile softened. “Thou art less foolish than thy shoes suggest.”

“They hurt too much to be foolish.”

She laughed and put one small foot upon his boot beneath the table.

“Does that improve matters?”

“It improves the whole world.”

Now the reader must not imagine that paradise came immediately, for paradise has always had guardians, serpents, theologians, and other inconveniences. Philippe had just raised Imperia’s fingers to his lips when a furious knocking shook the outer door.

Toinette entered with her eyebrows raised.

“Madame, the Bishop of Coire.”

Imperia uttered an expression not found in the Psalms.

“Tell him I am at confession.”

“He says he knows otherwise, having seen the candles.”

“Tell him I am ill.”

“He says he is skilled in physic.”

Another knock resounded, followed by a voice roaring, “Open, little she-devils! I know she is there!”

The Bishop of Coire was a vast Swiss prelate whose stomach entered rooms before the remainder of his theology. He had small eyes, three chins, and a beard like a wet broom. He was reputed to be a mighty hunter, although the beasts he pursued were chiefly roasted; and he had preserved his chastity until fifteen by a heroic effort much admired by his relatives.

Imperia turned to Philippe.

“Go into that cabinet.”

“Must I?”

“Unless thou wishest to debate precedence with a bishop.”

Philippe slipped behind a painted door just as the bishop burst into the chamber, wrapped in fur and breathing like a cow climbing stairs.

“Imperia!” he cried. “My dove! My rose! My little—”

He stopped, seeing two cups.

“Who is here?”

“No one.”

“Then why two cups?”

“One for wine, one for water.”

The bishop looked at the red contents of both.

“And two plates?”

“I have two appetites.”

“That I believe. And these violets?”

“I bought them.”

“With what money?”

“Yours, perhaps.”

This answer reassured him, for a man is generally willing to accept any lie which proves his importance.

He flung himself into Philippe’s chair, seized a capon leg, and began to eat.

“I have escaped three ambassadors, a Dominican, and the Elector’s secretary to come to thee,” he said through his mouthful. “The Council sits late. They are discussing reform.”

“What reform?”

“Whether abbots may wear red boots on feast days. A grave matter. Schism itself hangs upon it.”

“And how did you vote?”

“With the majority, naturally. I am no heretic.”

In the cabinet Philippe clenched his fists. Through a crack he saw the bishop devour the capon, drink from Philippe’s cup, paw Imperia’s sleeve, and scatter crumbs over the table like a miller feeding chickens. Jealousy bit the young priest in twelve places. Yet he dared not move.

The bishop had just begun to tell Imperia how dearly he loved her—an affection demonstrated by the gift of a silver reliquary which he had obtained from a ruined monastery—when the knocker sounded again.

This time there came three slow, commanding blows.

The bishop froze with a fig halfway to his mouth.

Toinette appeared pale.

“His Eminence the Cardinal of Ragusa.”

The fig fell.

The Cardinal of Ragusa was no fat provincial bullock to be deceived by cups and violets. He was a tall Dalmatian, lean, elegant, and dangerous, with a hooked nose, a black beard cut to a point, and eyes that could value a man, a jewel, or a kingdom before breakfast. He spoke six languages, lied beautifully in all of them, and possessed enough benefices to require a secretary merely to remember their saints’ days. He had made popes tremble, princes borrow, and ladies forgive him. Compared with him, the Bishop of Coire was a sausage wearing a mitre.

“Hide me!” gasped the bishop.

Imperia surveyed his breadth. “Where?”

“Anywhere!”

“You are too large for secrecy.”

The knocking came again.

“Behind the tapestry,” said Toinette.

They pushed the bishop against the wall and dragged a heavy tapestry before him. It represented the Last Judgment, and by good fortune his round face appeared beneath the painted demons in a manner agreeable to doctrine.

Philippe remained in the cabinet. The bishop remained behind the tapestry. Imperia resumed her seat. Toinette opened the door.

The cardinal entered.

He wore scarlet silk beneath a mantle lined with sable. A great ruby shone upon his hand, and behind him stood two gentlemen, a secretary, a chaplain, and four servants bearing lanterns. He dismissed them all with one movement.

“Wait below.”

Then he came into the chamber, kissed Imperia upon both cheeks, and looked around.

“You expected company.”

“I always expect misfortune, Eminence. It prevents surprise.”

“Two cups.”

“One for thirst, one for hope.”

“Two plates.”

“One for hunger, one for memory.”

The cardinal smiled. “And a capon with one leg gone?”

“Hope was hungry.”

He sat, but not before glancing behind the curtains and beneath the table. The Bishop of Coire, sweating within the tapestry, made the painted wall tremble.

“What is that?” asked the cardinal.

“A draft.”

“A fat draft.”

“All winds are fat in Germany.”

The cardinal’s eyes rested upon the cabinet door.

Philippe ceased breathing.

His Eminence crossed the room, opened the cabinet, and discovered the young priest wedged between a chest and three perfumed gowns.

For one instant no one spoke.

Then the cardinal said, very softly, “Come out.”

Philippe emerged.

Imperia raised her chin. “He is my confessor.”

“In green silk?”

“The Church adapts herself to circumstances.”

“At night?”

“Sin does not keep canonical hours.”

The cardinal looked Philippe over and recognized the archbishop’s lace collar.

“Whose man art thou?”

“Monseigneur of Bordeaux’s, Eminence.”

“Name?”

“Philippe de Mala.”

“Rank?”

“Priest.”

“Income?”

“None.”

“Then thou art either a saint or a fool.”

“I have not been examined for sainthood.”

“Nor is examination necessary for folly.”

The cardinal turned to Imperia. “Leave us.”

“This is my house.”

“And this is my command.”

“Commands are not legal tender here.”

His face hardened. “Five minutes.”

Imperia saw that he meant some private villainy and, being accustomed to the varieties of male authority, decided it was better overheard from the adjoining chamber. She rose.

“Five minutes,” she said. “At the sixth I return with the servants and tell them His Eminence is murdering a priest. They will consider it ordinary, but the neighbours may be entertained.”

She went out with Toinette.

The cardinal closed the door and faced Philippe.

“Listen, little tonsured mouse. Thou hast climbed into a cupboard belonging to a lion. There are two ways out.”

Philippe swallowed. “Through the door, Eminence?”

“Through death or obedience.”

“That makes three, if the door—”

“Silence.”

The cardinal leaned close. He smelled of amber, wine, and power.

“If thou art found here tomorrow morning, men will say thou didst insult a prince of the Church. Witnesses will swear thou drewest a knife. Thy body will be discovered in the lake, and a Mass may be said for thee if someone pays. Dost understand?”

“Perfectly.”

“Good. The second way is pleasanter. There is an abbey near Liège, rich in rents, vineyards, mills, woods, fishponds, and obedient peasants. Its abbot died yesterday.”

“Peacefully?”

“Eventually.”

“And you offer it to me?”

“If thou leavest this house now and never returnest.”

Philippe’s heart dropped into his borrowed boots.

An abbey! To a poor priest, an abbey is not merely a house but a roast ox with bells on it. He saw in one flash the cloister, the orchards, the cellars, the servants, the revenues, the warm rooms, the horses, the fat carp, the respectful villagers, the annual gifts, and himself enthroned in a carved chair while monks addressed him as Father. Against this vision stood Imperia, with her black eyes, her little feet, her capon half eaten, and the promise of a paradise for which he had spent every coin.

The cardinal watched him.

“Well?”

Philippe bowed his head. “I accept the abbey.”

Behind the tapestry the Bishop of Coire nearly sneezed, whether from dust or admiration one cannot say.

The cardinal smiled. “Wisely chosen. Thy archbishop shall have the letters tomorrow.”

“May I say farewell to Madame?”

“No.”

“May I take my violets?”

“No.”

“My cloak?”

“Take it and go.”

Philippe moved toward the door, then stopped.

“Eminence.”

“What?”

“There is one difficulty.”

The cardinal’s hand went to his ruby. “Another?”

“My master, the Archbishop of Bordeaux, has fallen sick.”

“Of what?”

Philippe lowered his voice. “The plague.”

The cardinal took one step backward.

“The plague?”

“This afternoon he developed a swelling beneath the arm. The physician said nothing publicly, for fear the Council would flee. I attended him. I held his hand. I wore this collar from his chamber.”

The cardinal stared at the lace as if every thread had become a serpent.

“And thou comest here?”

“I did not wish to die without seeing Imperia.”

“Brainless priest!”

“Quite possibly, Eminence.”

From behind the tapestry came a muffled whimper.

The cardinal spun around. “What was that?”

Philippe’s courage, once awakened, began to grow like dough near a fire.

“Perhaps,” he said, “someone else is afraid of infection.”

The cardinal understood at once. He strode to the tapestry and tore it aside.

There stood the Bishop of Coire, purple, dripping with sweat, and vainly trying to flatten his stomach against the wall.

“Coire!”

“Ragusa!”

“You fat spy!”

“You scarlet serpent!”

“What art thou doing behind the Last Judgment?”

“Praying!”

“With capon grease on thy chin?”

The bishop wiped his mouth. Then Philippe’s words returned to him.

“Plague?” he whispered.

The cardinal glanced at Philippe. A scheme passed between his eyes like a knife being sharpened.

“Yes,” he said solemnly. “Plague. The boy has attended his infected master. He has coughed twice.”

“I have not,” said Philippe.

“Thrice,” corrected the cardinal. “And now, my lord of Coire, thou hast remained in a closed chamber with him for—how long?”

The bishop crossed himself so violently that his pectoral cross struck his nose.

“Holy Virgin!”

“Moreover,” continued the cardinal, “thou hast eaten from his plate.”

The bishop looked at the capon leg in his hand and dropped it as though it had bitten him.

“And drunk from his cup.”

With a howl, the bishop sprang toward the door. Fear accomplished what diet, physicians, Lent, and six mules had failed to do: it made him agile. He ran through the hall, knocked down the little page, collided with one of the cardinal’s servants, lost a fur shoe on the stairs, and fled into the street shouting for his doctor.

“Excellent,” said the cardinal.

Philippe bowed. “I am happy to have served Your Eminence.”

“Now go.”

“Certainly.”

He took his cloak. As the cardinal opened the door, Imperia returned.

“What has happened? I heard Coire bellowing like an ox at slaughter.”

“He remembered an appointment,” said the cardinal.

“With death,” added Philippe.

Imperia looked from one to the other.

The cardinal gave Philippe a warning glance. “This young man has chosen prudence. He departs.”

Something flashed in Imperia’s eyes, but she smiled.

“So quickly? His confession was unfinished.”

“His penance is lifelong.”

Philippe bowed to her. He wanted to explain, but the cardinal stood close, and behind His Eminence waited an abbey, a lake, and perhaps a knife.

“Farewell, Madame.”

“Farewell, Monsieur l’Abbé,” said the cardinal.

Imperia heard the title. Her face changed by scarcely more than the movement of a candle flame, yet Philippe saw contempt close over her warmth like ice over water.

“Ah,” she said. “Monsieur l’Abbé. Then I congratulate you.”

Philippe went into the hall.

Now, in Imperia’s hall stood a great Venetian mirror taller than a man, set in a carved frame of gilded wood. Behind it was a narrow space used by servants when cleaning the wall hangings, and Philippe had noticed this upon entering, because lovers and thieves both study houses rapidly.

He wrapped his cloak around one hand, opened the outer door loudly, and shut it with equal force. Instead of descending the stairs, he slipped behind the mirror and squeezed himself into darkness.

Thus was his abbey founded upon a lie, his life protected by a plague that did not exist, and his love entrusted to Venetian glass. Theology itself could not have arranged matters more neatly.

Within the chamber the cardinal poured wine.

“Come, Imperia. The intruders are gone.”

“Not all.”

“Do not sulk over the boy.”

“I do not sulk.”

“Then smile.”

“I smile when amused.”

“Am I not amusing?”

“Frequently, though seldom by intention.”

The cardinal laughed and approached her. “Thou shouldst thank me. I have made thy little priest an abbot.”

“With what price?”

“Only his absence.”

“And he paid?”

“Immediately.”

Imperia turned away.

His Eminence took her waist. She removed his hand.

“What is this?”

“I have a headache.”

“I have cured queens of headaches.”

“By leaving them?”

“By less ecclesiastical remedies.”

He reached again. She crossed to the table.

At that moment Philippe, listening behind the mirror, heard the cardinal cough. It was a small cough, caused by wine; but inspiration enters through narrow cracks.

Imperia heard it also.

She stood motionless.

The cardinal coughed a second time.

Very slowly, Imperia turned.

“Eminence.”

“Yes?”

“That priest—why did the Bishop of Coire flee?”

The cardinal hesitated. “Cowardice.”

“Of what was he afraid?”

“Nothing.”

“You said plague.”

“A jest.”

“Plague is a strange jest in a closed chamber.”

The cardinal waved his hand. “The archbishop is unwell, that is all.”

“And Philippe attended him?”

“So he claimed.”

“Wearing the collar from his chamber?”

The cardinal looked at the lace collar’s absence, then at his own hands.

Imperia drew back.

“Do not come near me.”

“Do not be absurd.”

“You touched him.”

“Only his shoulder.”

“You stood face to face.”

“For a moment.”

“You have drunk from the cup beside which he breathed.”

“Imperia—”

She rang a handbell furiously.

Toinette hurried in. “Madame?”

“Open every window. Burn rosemary. Fetch vinegar. Tell the servants His Eminence has been exposed to plague.”

The cardinal went pale beneath his beard.

“Silence! There is no plague.”

“Then why did Coire run?”

“Because I told him—”

“Told him what?”

“That the archbishop—”

“Had plague.”

“To frighten him!”

“And Philippe?”

“Lied!”

“Perhaps. Perhaps not. You coughed.”

“I swallowed wine badly.”

“You are sweating.”

“It is hot.”

“You are pale.”

“I am angry!”

“Anger is often the herald of fever.”

Toinette, entering fully into the performance, crossed herself and retreated toward the door.

“Madame, my aunt’s husband began with anger. By midnight he had swellings. By dawn he was black.”

The cardinal touched his neck.

“Nonsense.”

“You touched your neck!” cried Imperia. “There is already discomfort!”

“There is no discomfort!”

“Then why touch it?”

“Because thou saidst—woman, cease this madness.”

He coughed again, this time from alarm.

Imperia screamed and seized a cushion as a shield.

“Out!”

“Listen to me.”

“Out, I say!”

“I am Cardinal of Ragusa!”

“Then die in a palace suitable to your rank.”

“I command thee—”

“You command in consistories. Here I command. Toinette, summon his servants, but keep them at a distance. Bring a brazier. Burn his chair after he leaves.”

The cardinal’s composure began to crack. Political courage is admirable before armies, councils, and creditors, but plague is a republican disease, showing no respect for scarlet hats.

“What did the boy say exactly?” he demanded.

“That question should be addressed to your confessor.”

“Did he look ill?”

“He looked flushed.”

“He was embarrassed.”

“Or feverish.”

“His eyes shone.”

“Fever.”

“His hands trembled.”

“Fever.”

“He was sweating.”

“Fever.”

Each proof had a natural explanation; together they constituted medical certainty, particularly to a man who feared death.

The cardinal rushed to the door, then stopped.

“My cloak.”

“Leave it.”

“It is sable.”

“It may be infected.”

“My hat.”

“Burn it.”

“My attendants will talk.”

“They always do.”

He pointed a trembling finger at her. “If this is a trick, thou shalt regret it. I can drive thee from Constance. I can have thy house searched, thy goods seized, thy servants whipped—”

“You may do all this tomorrow, if you survive tonight. For the present, go cough elsewhere.”

“Send for my physician.”

“Send yourself.”

“Imperia, my dove—”

“Out.”

“My rose—”

“Out.”

“My soul—”

“Out!”

The cardinal fled.

He passed through the hall holding a handkerchief over his mouth, ordered his servants not to approach, changed his mind and commanded two of them to carry him, changed it again upon realizing they might already be infected, and descended alone. His retinue followed at twenty paces. Before midnight he had swallowed vinegar, crushed pearls, powdered emerald, three cloves of garlic, a spoonful of treacle, and enough Greek antidote to poison a healthy mule. He also sent a written promise of the abbey to Philippe, for fear that breaking his word might provoke either God or the plague. Thus Providence, which sometimes employs curious instruments, made terror serve as notary.

When the outer door had slammed, Imperia stood listening.

“Is he gone?” she asked.

Toinette peered from the window. “He runs like a thief who has stolen his own skin.”

“Shut the door.”

“And burn the chair?”

“No.”

“The rosemary?”

“No.”

“The vinegar?”

“Drink it if thou art thirsty.”

Toinette looked closely at her mistress. “Madame?”

“Leave me.”

“But—”

“Leave me!”

Toinette withdrew.

The chamber, so lately crowded with bishops, cardinals, lies, threats, capon bones, and ecclesiastical authority, became suddenly still. Candles burned in their silver branches. Cold air entered through the open windows. Upon the table stood the two cups, one overturned by the Bishop of Coire, and the violets Philippe had brought, crushed a little but still fragrant.

Imperia picked them up.

She had dismissed princes without regret and watched bankers depart with lighter purses and heavier hearts. She knew vows lasted until breakfast, jealousy until another woman appeared, gratitude until the bill arrived, and fidelity until the road turned. She had armed herself with laughter because laughter was cheaper than guards and more effective than locks. Yet Philippe’s awkward worship had slipped beneath those defenses precisely because it had asked nothing cleverly.

Now he had sold her for an abbey.

“Fool,” she murmured.

Whether she meant Philippe or herself was uncertain.

She sat in the chair before the fire, holding the violets. Her face altered when no man watched it. The imperious beauty whom cardinals courted and bishops feared became for one moment only a tired young woman in a large house, surrounded by objects purchased with desire and protected by none of them.

“An abbey,” she said. “Vineyards, rents, fishponds, bells. Naturally. Why should he choose me?”

She laughed, but the sound broke.

“Men come in velvet and swear they would die. Then they count their money. This one came in borrowed sleeves and said he would enter through a wall. He had seven coins. Seven! I might have bought his whole world for eight, and instead I believed him rich in heart.”

A tear slid down her cheek. She wiped it away angrily.

“What am I to him? A supper. A sin. A story for old age, told between Matins and a fat dinner.”

Behind the mirror Philippe felt something twist within him more sharply than jealousy. Until that instant he had desired Imperia as a pilgrim desires a miracle: brightly, selfishly, and without considering whether the miracle itself suffered from being forever stared at. Her tears taught him in one breath what sermons had failed to explain—that the beautiful may be lonely, the powerful afraid, and those who sell delight sometimes starve for tenderness.

Imperia bowed her head.

“This life,” she whispered, “is a bridge made of smiles over a pit. A prince tires, a bishop threatens, a captain grows jealous, a servant steals, a physician lies, beauty fades, and every door that opens may admit fortune or murder. They call me queen because they wish to forget I have no kingdom.”

Philippe could bear no more.

He pushed the mirror.

It moved slightly in its frame with a wooden groan.

Imperia sprang up, seized a silver candlestick, and turned.

“Who is there?”

Philippe stepped out, dusty, cramped, and smiling uncertainly.

For one heartbeat she stared at him as if the mirror had produced a ghost.

Then she hurled the candlestick.

Philippe ducked. It struck the wall.

“You villain!”

“Madame—”

“Spy!”

“I did not mean—”

“Liar! Rat! Tonsured lizard!”

She seized a cushion and struck him with it. Feathers burst from a torn seam and filled the air like snow.

Philippe caught the cushion. “Listen!”

“You accepted his abbey!”

“Yes.”

“You abandoned me!”

“No.”

“You said farewell!”

“So that he would believe me.”

“You hid behind my mirror!”

“So that I could remain.”

“You overheard me!”

At this Philippe lost his cleverness, which had served him well against prelates but was useless before tears.

“Yes,” he said.

Imperia’s hand rose to strike him. He did not move.

She lowered it.

“You heard everything?”

“Everything.”

Her face flushed with shame and anger. “Then thou hast stolen what no man here has ever received.”

“What?”

“The truth.”

“I will guard it better than cardinals guard doctrine.”

She almost smiled, but resisted.

“And the abbey?”

“I chose it.”

Her eyes hardened.

“And I chose you.”

“That is impossible.”

“Why? Great churchmen choose six benefices at once.”

“Do not jest.”

“I am not. The cardinal offered me death or the abbey. I took the abbey. But he did not say I must be foolish. I gave him the plague to clear out Coire, pretended to depart, and hid there. Thus I kept my life, my abbey, and—if she will forgive me—the lady I came to see.”

Imperia stared, then began to laugh.

At first it was a small laugh, unwilling and breathless. Then she saw him properly: hair powdered with dust, rented doublet torn at the shoulder, lace collar crooked, one silk stocking sliding down, white feathers clinging to his cassock, and the solemn pride of a young fox who has accidentally outwitted two wolves. Her laughter grew until she sank into the chair.

“Thou chose all three!”

“If permitted.”

“Death, abbey, and woman?”

“No. I declined death. It appeared the least profitable.”

She laughed again.

“Philippe de Mala, yesterday thou couldst not knock at a door.”

“I have improved under instruction.”

“Today thou hast frightened a bishop, cheated a cardinal, won an abbey, hidden in a wall, and made a courtesan weep.”

“The last was not intended.”

“None of the best crimes are.”

She rose and approached him.

“Didst thou truly stay for me?”

“I stayed because the abbey seemed poor without you.”

“Poor? It has vineyards.”

“So has Burgundy.”

“Fishponds.”

“So has every monastery.”

“Rents.”

“Money can be counted.”

“And I cannot?”

“I began at the cathedral and lost the number.”

This answer, though extravagant, was delivered with such honest simplicity that Imperia forgave its theology. She touched a feather caught in his hair.

“Thou lookest like a plucked angel.”

“I feel like one.”

“Thy angelic life has been brief.”

“It was hungry.”

She drew closer. “And is it still?”

“Terribly.”

“For capon?”

“For everything.”

“Greedy priest.”

“I have studied my superiors.”

At this she put her arms around his neck and kissed him, not as she kissed princes in payment for jewels, nor bishops in exchange for favors, nor poets to silence their verses, but warmly, merrily, and with that rare generosity by which a woman gives what no treasury can purchase.

Philippe, beatified beyond the authority of Rome, nearly fell over the chair.

“Careful,” said Imperia. “An abbot should stand more firmly.”

“Am I truly an abbot?”

“If Ragusa lives through the night, he will make thee one to preserve the lie. If he dies, take his ring and forge the letters.”

“Is that lawful?”

“In Constance?”

“Ah. Certainly.”

She shut the windows, drew the curtains, and rang for Toinette.

The maid entered, saw Philippe, saw the displaced mirror, saw her mistress smiling, and understood enough to require no explanation.

“Bring another capon,” said Imperia. “This one has been profaned by a bishop.”

“And wine?”

“The best.”

“How many cups?”

Imperia looked at Philippe.

“One,” she said. “We shall share it.”

Toinette collected the fallen candlestick and went out, muttering that priests were like fleas: one might shake the bedclothes, burn the straw, and swear the room was empty, yet still another would leap out at supper.

Philippe and Imperia sat again at the table. He ate now with the appetite of one who has escaped death, gained a fortune, and discovered love before the soup cooled. Imperia fed him sugared almonds, laughed at his pinching boots, and made him recount every thought that had passed through his mind in the cabinet, behind the mirror, and beneath the cardinal’s hooked nose.

When he told her how the Bishop of Coire had stood behind the Last Judgment, she laughed until tears returned, but these were of another kind.

Outside, Constance continued its holy labors. Bells rang for Compline. Doctors disputed. Ambassadors intrigued. Monks snored. Courtesans counted rings. The Bishop of Coire sat naked in a tub of vinegar while three physicians argued whether to bleed him from the arm or the purse. The Cardinal of Ragusa dictated his will, revoked it, dictated another, and examined his armpits every quarter-hour. The Archbishop of Bordeaux, being informed that he had the plague, ordered a second supper to prove the report false.

Within Imperia’s chamber, however, there was peace.

Not the peace proclaimed by councils, signed by kings, interpreted by lawyers, taxed by princes, and broken by breakfast; but that smaller and more durable truce made between two young creatures who have defeated the old, the rich, and the powerful for one evening.

Imperia lifted the shared cup.

“To Monsieur l’Abbé.”

Philippe raised it with her.

“To Madame my paradise.”

“Take care. Paradises have serpents.”

“So do councils.”

“And forbidden fruit.”

“I have fasted long enough.”

“And angels with swords.”

“I shall hide behind the mirror.”

She kissed him again.

Thus Philippe de Mala received in one day the promise of an abbey, the favor of Imperia, and a practical education in ecclesiastical government. Of these three blessings, the abbey lasted longest, the education proved most useful, and Imperia remained sweetest in memory; though certain malicious persons later maintained that she visited Liège disguised as a widowed cousin and caused such disorder among the monks that three took vows of silence, two broke them, and the cellarer had to revise his accounts.

As for the plague, it vanished by morning, as many official truths do after serving their purpose. The Bishop of Coire declared that his escape had been miraculous and ordered a silver image of himself to be placed in a chapel. The Cardinal of Ragusa announced that he had never felt fear, but merely retired to pray for the city. The Archbishop of Bordeaux recovered from the disease he had never possessed and accepted congratulations with admirable dignity.

No one contradicted them.

For at Constance every man guarded his own lie as tenderly as a relic, and the greatest miracle of the Council was not that so many lies were told, but that, by common agreement, they were entered into the record as truth.

### The Quiet House

There were eleven of us inside the coach, not counting a child that belonged to somebody but was disowned by all whenever it cried, and three more on the box with the driver. We had been packed at Yarrabong in fair weather, with our knees fitted into one another like the works of a cheap watch; but before sundown the wind came up from the ranges, the rain came down from everywhere else, and the temperature fell until each man’s feet seemed as big as camel’s and about as useful.

The coach leaked according to no known natural law. Water entered at the roof, the windows, the doors, and, apparently, through the floor upwards. A thin stream ran down the neck of the Pilgrim, who had been to every goldfield from here to the Gulf and had profited by none of them. Another fell steadily upon the hat of a commercial bagman. He shifted twice, thereby acquiring two streams, and decided bitterly to remain where Providence had first placed him.

The Joker said that if the South Pole was not somewhere along that road, then the surveyors had put the road wrong.

Nobody laughed. We had reached that stage of misery when a joke is regarded as a personal insult, chiefly because the joker has still enough warmth in him to make it.

The driver sat hunched upon the box, with his shoulders level with his ears and the reins held in hands that had ceased to belong to him. Whenever we asked how far it was to the Four Posts, he answered, “‘Tain’t fur,” in a tone suggesting that distance was an abstract question and no affair of his. He had said “‘Tain’t fur” at three in the afternoon. He said it again at six, and at eight he admitted that we might get there if the creek had not risen.

“What if it has?” asked the Growler.

“Then we mightn’t,” said the driver.

The Growler demanded why he had not mentioned the creek before.

“You never arst,” said the driver.

This ended the discussion, for there is no reasoning with a man who is legally in charge of horses.

Darkness shut down over the bush, and the trees stood on either side like black walls that had been planted expressly to keep us on the road. The lamps showed two muddy ruts, a patch of steaming horse, and now and then a stump waiting with malicious patience. We crossed a flat where the wheels went in to the hubs. The driver swore at the leaders, the leaders communicated the message to the polers by kicking, and the coach leaned until all on the left were lying on all on the right.

The bagman’s sample case burst open, and for ten minutes we travelled upon packets of needles, patent braces, hair oil, and a set of ladies’ combs. The Pilgrim found a buttonhook inside his shirt, but was too numb to care.

At last a yellow point appeared through the rain.

“The Four Posts?” we cried.

“Might be,” said the driver.

It was. The building crouched at the edge of the timber, slab-walled and iron-roofed, with a verandah sagging in the middle and one greasy window shining like the eye of a sick bullock. We climbed down like so many cripples who had been folded for transport. Those on the roof were lowered by stages, having lost all confidence in their legs. The child was discovered asleep under a mailbag and was restored to its mother, who received it without enthusiasm.

We stamped into the bar, bringing half the road with us.

The landlord met us with a candle held high and one finger at his lips.

“Hush!”

He was a long, hollow man in shirt-sleeves, with a face arranged for a funeral. His boots were unlaced, his hair disordered, and he spoke in a whisper that could have been heard in the stable.

“Not a sound, gentlemen. For the love of Heaven—not a sound.”

The Growler, whose teeth were chattering, asked whether the fire was in the parlour.

“Hush!” said the landlord fiercely. “My wife’s dyin’.”

We became quiet at once. Even the child, impressed perhaps by the landlord’s expression, postponed its next complaint.

“Dyin’?” said the Pilgrim, whispering the word in three syllables.

“Doctor gives no hope. She’s upstairs there”—the landlord pointed to a ceiling blackened by smoke—“between this world an’ the next. A sudden turn. Only an hour ago she spoke my name.”

We looked at the ceiling. There was no sound above, but this seemed proper under the circumstances.

The landlord closed his eyes for a moment, then mastered himself as a man does who has both a breaking heart and customers.

“What can I get you?” he murmured.

“Hot tea,” said the bagman.

“None.”

“Coffee?”

“None.”

“Beefsteak?”

The landlord’s grief acquired an edge.

“Do you think I’ve been killin’ bullocks with my poor wife at death’s door?”

The bagman apologised.

There was rum, the landlord explained, and there was milk. The milk was cold and the rum was colonial, but these were facts over which death itself had no authority. We took rum. Those who did not drink took milk until they tasted it, after which they took rum also.

The Joker asked gently about the change of horses.

The landlord bent towards him.

“Missing.”

“What’s missing?”

“The horses.”

“All of them?”

“Every blasted one—hush!”

He glanced upwards with an injured look, as though the Joker had personally shortened his wife’s life.

The driver came in carrying a lantern.

“Where’s them greys?” he asked.

The landlord stared at him.

“Didn’t you hear? They’re missing.”

The driver scratched his head. This was his method of examining a difficult question.

“Where’d they go?”

“If I knew that, they wouldn’t be missing, would they?”

This appeared sound. The driver scratched again, looked into his lantern, and went out to see whether the greys had concealed themselves in it.

We stood steaming in the bar. The fire was a handful of red coals in a deep chimney, and every attempt to approach it was defeated by somebody who had approached it earlier. The landlord poured rum with slow, tragic movements. He sighed over each glass and took payment immediately, perhaps from fear that sorrow might affect his memory.

“What about beds?” asked the squatter—a small squatter, as squatters went, with one station mortgaged and another expected.

“No beds,” whispered the landlord.

“Then where are we to sleep?”

“Anywhere you can, so long as you don’t make a row. There’s bags in the shed, and two stretchers in the dining-room. Three shillings a head.”

“Three shillings for bags?”

“For shelter,” said the landlord. “Bags is extra if you tear ’em.”

The Growler showed signs of disputing this, but we restrained him. There are occasions when even a traveller must remember that he is a gentleman; and the presence of a dying woman, though invisible, weighs heavily upon good breeding.

The landlord brought another bottle.

“Last words?” asked the Pilgrim sympathetically.

“Not yet,” said the landlord. “She’s lingerin’.”

We drank to her recovery in undertones. The landlord shook his head and drank with us, standing apart in spirit but not neglecting his glass.

About ten o’clock two men entered from the rain. They were shearers by the look of them, thin in the face and muddy to the waist, carrying swags that had absorbed enough water to irrigate a selection. They had walked twelve miles to catch the night coach on to the railway, where one had a job promised and the other had a wife waiting.

“When d’yer start?” the taller one asked the driver.

“Don’t,” said the driver.

“What d’yer mean, don’t?”

“Horses missing.”

The tall man stared. “They can’t be. We seen four in the horse paddock comin’ up.”

“Hush!” cried the landlord.

“What for?”

“My wife is dyin’ upstairs.”

The shearer removed his hat. His mate did the same. For half a minute they stood respectfully; then the taller man said, in a lower but still urgent voice:

“Look, boss, I gotter make that train. If them horses—”

“Hush!”

“But we seen ’em.”

The Growler, who had now accepted the tragedy as a personal responsibility, turned on him.

“Can’t you understand plain English? There’s a woman dying.”

“So I hear, but—”

“No buts,” said the squatter. “A little common humanity costs nothing.”

This was true in theory. In practice our common humanity had already cost two rounds of rum, three shillings for floor space, and sixpence each for candles.

The shearer appealed to us. “Me job goes if I miss that train.”

“And my missus’ll think I’ve gone on the spree,” said his mate.

The Joker whispered that she would probably be right by the time he reached her, but we frowned him down.

The men tried the driver once more. He said the horses were not his horses, the pub was not his pub, the train was not his train, and, as far as could be established, nothing whatever was his affair. The landlord placed both hands on the bar.

“If you men can’t respect a house of sorrow,” he said, trembling, “you can take your swags outside.”

We approved this speech by silence. The shearers looked at us with a queer expression, as men look at a jury that has mistaken the prisoner for the judge. Then they went to the shed.

We settled down for the night. The dining-room contained two stretchers, one with three legs and one with a middle like a waterhole. The squatter and the bagman secured these by social precedence and speed respectively. The rest of us lay on flour bags, chaff sacks, mailbags, or the bare boards. A smoky candle burned upon the mantelpiece, throwing monstrous shadows and revealing nails wherever a man wished to put his head.

The landlord came in several times to ask us not to snore.

“Is she no better?” the Pilgrim whispered.

“Sinkin’,” he replied.

An hour later he came back to sell us more rum, necessary, he said, against the cold.

“Still sinking?” asked the Joker.

“Gradual.”

“She’s got a long way to go,” murmured the Joker.

We told him not to be heartless.

There was no sleep. The rain hammered the iron roof; wind pushed under the door and examined us individually; the child cried with the steady persistence of a sawmill. Whenever anyone shifted upon the bags, the landlord appeared and said, “Hush!” Once the Growler sneezed and was made to feel little better than a murderer.

Towards morning we heard horses moving outside.

The tall shearer sat up.

“There! What’d I tell yer?”

The driver, lying near the door, said they were probably our own team.

“They’re in the stable.”

“Then they ain’t missing,” said the shearer.

“I never said they was.”

“You heard him say it.”

“What a man says about another man’s horses,” replied the driver, “is his own affair.”

The shearer made a movement towards him, but the rest of us interfered. We were tired, cold, and conscious of our duty to the room above. Besides, a stoush in a dining-room is apt to involve innocent persons who are trying to sleep under the table.

At daylight the landlord announced that the greys had been found.

“Where?” asked several of us.

“Down the creek.”

The tall shearer looked at his mate.

“Funny creek,” he said. “Got rails round it and a feed-bin.”

The landlord ignored him. The coach would leave, he said, as soon as the team was harnessed and the bill settled.

The bill contained rum, milk, candles, shelter, bags, fire, attendance, and an item called “special inconvenience.” The Growler objected to the fire.

“There wasn’t enough to warm a lizard.”

“Fire was there,” said the landlord. “If you didn’t use it, that’s your lookout.”

The bagman disputed the milk, which he described as water that had once heard of a cow.

The landlord drew himself up.

“Gentlemen, at such a time as this—”

We paid. No man wished to haggle beneath the room where a woman was making such a prolonged and considerate death.

Before mounting the coach, the Pilgrim asked whether we might send a message to the doctor.

The landlord’s face softened.

“No need. She’s restin’ peaceful.”

“Passed away?” asked the Pilgrim.

“Not exactly. Turned the corner, like. Sleepin’.”

We congratulated him. He accepted our congratulations with modest sorrow and went inside with the takings.

The two shearers remained behind. Their train had gone beyond all catching, and they had not enough money for the coach fare after paying for shelter. The tall one said nothing as we left. He merely watched us from under the verandah, and it was not a friendly look.

For the first few miles we discussed the uncertainty of life, the nobility of the landlord, and the providential recovery upstairs. The rain had stopped, though drops still fell from the leaves, and the bush steamed greyly under a mean dawn. Our clothes dried into shapes not intended by their makers. Everyone was stiff, cross, and anxious to believe that the night had improved him morally.

Then the Joker climbed through to the box and sat beside the driver.

“Remarkable recovery,” he said.

“What?”

“The woman.”

“What woman?”

The Joker looked at him. “The landlord’s wife.”

The driver spat over the wheel.

“He ain’t got one.”

Inside the coach we became attentive.

“Widower?” asked the Joker.

“Dunno.”

“Dead long?”

“Ain’t dead.”

“Then where is she?”

“Cleared out.”

“When?”

The driver considered. Dates, like distances, were abstract questions.

“Six year. Maybe seven. Went off with a spieler from Wagga. Tall bloke with yaller whiskers.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

“Is there any woman living at the Four Posts?” asked the squatter carefully.

“No.”

The Pilgrim leaned forward. “Then who was upstairs?”

“No one, far as I know.”

The coach rolled on. Every jolt now had a sarcastic character.

“What about the greys?” asked the Growler.

“What about ’em?”

“Were they missing?”

The driver scratched his head.

“Might’ve been.”

“Were they in the paddock?”

“Might’ve been there too.”

“Did you know?”

“Not pertickler.”

“Did the landlord give you anything?”

The driver turned slowly. “That’s my affair.”

It was the worst answer he could have made, because it was also the most complete.

The Joker took out a pencil and began figuring on the back of an envelope. Eleven inside, three outside, two shearers, rum at bush prices, milk at funeral prices, three shillings for the privilege of lying on produce, and the ordinary commission on delay. He arrived at a sum that caused the bagman to whistle.

“Not bad for a dead wife,” said the Joker. “Better than sheep, and she doesn’t need shearing.”

The Pilgrim said he had suspected something from the first.

We silenced him immediately.

The Growler accused the driver of being in the swindle. The driver denied it in a manner which left the accusation stronger. The squatter blamed the shearers for not insisting. The bagman reminded him that he had personally lectured them on common humanity. The squatter replied that he had acted upon information supplied by others. The child began crying again, perhaps because it alone had nothing to reproach itself with.

By the time the roofs of Dead Camel showed beyond the timber, nobody trusted anybody. We suspected the driver, the landlord, the absent wife, the shearers, and particularly those among ourselves who claimed afterwards to have seen through the business. The Joker was disliked for his arithmetic; the Pilgrim for his foresight; the Growler for having paid without a fight; and the rest of us for remembering one another’s whispers of sympathy.

We entered Dead Camel in bitter silence and climbed down without offering a hand to the man beside us.

The driver drove off to change horses.

Whether he found them missing again was, of course, his affair.

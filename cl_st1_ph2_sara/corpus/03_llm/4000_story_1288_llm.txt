Evening descended dreamily upon the Ukrainian village, laying one cool hand upon the thatched roofs and with the other drawing a violet veil across the heavens. The last labourers had come from the fields; the oxen, released from their yokes, stood breathing heavily beside the wattled fences; and here and there a mother’s voice called some barefooted wanderer home to supper. From the broad street arose a song, now merry, now mournful, flowing stream-like beneath the darkening sky. The lads and maidens sang of a Cossack who had ridden far, far away, and of the girl who waited for him without surcease, counting the little stars until morning.

The song faded. Laughter followed it, and then the village fell by degrees into that soft unrest which is almost silence. A dog barked behind a barn; a gate creaked; the scent of crushed wormwood and cherry blossom floated from the gardens. Above the pine wood the first star trembled, timid as a maiden who has heard her name spoken by the man she loves.

At the far end of the village, hard by a deep pool begirt with willows, stood a low white cottage. Its shutters were closed, but through a chink shone one slender thread of light.

Along the shadow of the fence comes Leoko, the headman’s eldest son. His red sash is tied bravely, his dark hair curls from beneath his cap, and his eyes shine as brightly as though two of those little stars had fallen into them. He glances toward the street, hears no footsteps, and steals to the window.

“Hanna! My little dove! Are you asleep?”

Nothing answers but the leaves, which whisper among themselves.

“Hanna!” he says again. “If you do not come, I shall think you have forgotten me.”

The shutter opens by the breadth of two rosy fingers, and a hazel eye appears.

“Go away,” whispers Hanna. “Somebody will hear.”

“Let them hear! I shall tell them I came to ask whether your mother’s hens have begun laying silver eggs.”

“You foolish boy!”

The shutter widens, and Hanna’s face, fresh and pale beneath her dark brows, leans into the moonless dusk. A red ribbon is twined through her braid. Leoko reaches for her hand, but she draws it back.

“No, no. You have been with the lads all evening. I heard you singing.”

“And was I not singing to you?”

“You sang about Oksana.”

“What of that? The song was made long ago. Oksana must now be an old grandmother with scarcely a tooth in her head.”

“And perhaps you love old grandmothers.”

“I love one cruel maiden who denies me even her tiny hand.”

Hanna tried to frown, but the laugh would come, and Leoko caught her fingers and kissed them through the opening.

For a little while neither spoke. The village breathed around them, warm and still, while the heavens deepened and the stars multiplied, gathering like bright little homes in an immeasurable country.

“Leoko,” said Hanna at last, “is it true that the souls of good people live up there?”

“So they say.”

“Then why are some stars large and some very, very little?”

“The large ones belong to rich folk, no doubt, and the little ones to poor lovers who have only one another.”

“Do not jest about such things.”

“I am not jesting. When I die, I shall ask the Lord for a place beside your star. If He sets me far away, I shall cross the whole sky to you.”

“And if an angel stops you?”

“I shall give him my best pipe.”

“And if he will not take it?”

“Then I shall fight him.”

“Hush!” cried Hanna, crossing herself. “What wickedness! One does not fight angels.”

“For you, my little heart, I would fight the headman himself.”

“That would not be so difficult,” she said, and her smile vanished. “Your father already hates me.”

“He does not hate you.”

“He calls us beggars.”

“He calls everyone beggars who does not own three barns and a pair of grey horses. The old curmudgeon thinks silver coins sprout beneath his cabbages.”

“He will never consent.”

“He must.”

“But if he does not?”

Leoko drew himself upright. “Then we shall marry without his blessing.”

The leaves ceased whispering, as though they too waited to hear what Hanna would say.

She looked at him with troubled eyes. “Do not speak so boldly. A father’s curse is heavy. It can follow a man beyond seven rivers.”

“My father is headman, not the prophet Elijah. He can shout, stamp, threaten the village clerk, and frighten old women out of their geese; but he cannot command my heart. On Ascension Day I shall speak to him. I shall say, ‘Father, I will marry Hanna, and no other.’”

“And if he shuts you in the storehouse?”

“I shall climb through the roof.”

“If he sends you to the Cossacks?”

“I shall return with a sabre.”

“If he finds you another bride?”

“I shall tell her she squints.”

Hanna laughed, yet tears stood in her eyes. “Ah, Leoko, you turn everything into play.”

“Not everything.” He pressed her hand against his breast. “This is no play. Hear how my heart beats! It says, ‘Hanna, Hanna,’ and nothing else.”

Her cheeks flushed. “Mine says the same.”

“Then all is settled.”

But love, though it speaks bravely, knows a thousand fears. Hanna sighed and looked beyond him, past the sloping meadow to the pool. Darkness had gathered there before it had gathered elsewhere. The water lay smooth as black glass, and upon its farther bank stood an abandoned house, large and once handsome, with a broken roof and shutters fast closed. Moss covered its steps. Nettles crowded the threshold, and the willows bent over it like mourners.

“Leoko,” she whispered, “whose house is that?”

“That ruin? No one’s.”

“But who lived there?”

“Long, long ago, a Cossack captain. Why?”

“I do not like it. Even in daylight it seems to watch the pool.”

“Old houses must look somewhere.”

“Do not laugh. Paraska says evil spirits dance upon the roof.”

“Paraska sees evil spirits whenever she has drunk too much cherry brandy.”

“And she says a drowned maiden walks beneath the willows. Tell me the truth.”

Leoko turned toward the house. For all his mockery, he crossed himself before beginning.

“My grandmother told me the tale when I was very little. The captain who owned that house had one daughter, fair as the white blossom on an apple tree. Her mother was dead, and for many years the two lived happily together. Then, one autumn, the captain went to market beyond the pine wood and brought home a new wife.”

“A wicked one?”

“At first she was gentle. Her speech was sweeter than honey; she kissed the girl, combed her hair, and called her ‘little daughter.’ But her eyes were cold. The dogs snarled when she passed, and the crucifix in the holy corner trembled whenever she prayed.”

Hanna drew nearer the window.

“On the first night,” Leoko continued, “the girl awoke and saw a black cat crouching upon her breast. Its claws were at her throat, and its green eyes burned like coals. She seized it and flung it down; but before it escaped she struck one paw with her father’s sabre.

“On the second morning the stepmother came to breakfast with her hand bound in linen.

“The girl understood.

“She ran to her father. ‘Father,’ she cried, ‘your wife is a witch! Last night she came to me in the likeness of a cat.’

“But the stepmother wept so bitterly that the captain believed her.

“On the first day he ordered his daughter to carry water like a menial.

“On the second day he drove her from the best chamber.

“On the third day he gave her no bread.

“And on the fourth the witch wound her arms about him, whispered into his ear, and turned his heart to stone. Whereupon he called his own child a liar and cast her from the house.”

“Her own father?” Hanna breathed.

“Her own father. She went out at night, barefooted and weeping. No neighbour opened a door, for the witch had bewitched them all. The girl came to this pool. The moon shone upon it; the water moved softly, like a babe in a cradle, and seemed to say, ‘Come, poor maiden; here is rest.’ She prayed once, covered her face with her hair, and threw herself in.”

Hanna’s fingers became cold in Leoko’s hand.

“Afterward,” he said, lowering his voice, “the drowned maidens of every pool gathered to her. They wore wreaths of sedge and water lilies, and their faces were as white as snow. She became their queen. Yet she could not distinguish the witch among the living, because the witch could take any shape she pleased. So on moonlit nights the drowned queen rises, calls travellers to the bank, and begs them to find her stepmother. If they fail—”

“What then?”

“My grandmother never said.”

“She did!”

“Well, she said the drowned maidens tickle them until they die.”

Hanna shuddered and pulled her hand away. “That is dreadful.”

“It is an old woman’s tale.”

“Then why is the house empty?”

“Because the roof leaks.”

“And why does no one fish in the pool after sunset?”

“Because fools prefer dry clothes.”

“And why did you cross yourself?”

Leoko paused, caught like a boy beside a stolen honey-pot. “One may despise nonsense and still show proper respect to the Lord.”

At that moment the moon rose from behind the pine wood, broad and fiery, as though it had climbed out of some subterranean furnace. The meadows changed beneath it. Each blade of grass received a pale edge; the willows stretched long crooked arms across the ground; and the ruined house appeared not nearer, but more distinct, which was worse. Its walls gleamed deathly white between patches of moss, while its shuttered windows remained black.

A ripple passed over the pool.

Hanna stared. “Leoko—there!”

“What?”

“Beside the steps.”

He looked. A thin shape seemed to stand where the reeds met the water; then the willow leaves moved, and it was nothing but moonlight upon a pale stone.

“My frightened little dove,” he said gently, “you have listened too well.”

Voices sounded from the village street, with the thump of a tambourine and the careless laughter of lads returning from their merrymaking.

“They are coming!” Hanna whispered.

“Let them come.”

“If they see you, your father will know before morning.”

“He knows everything before morning. That is why he is headman.”

“Go, Leoko, please.”

He caught her hand once more. “Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow.”

“At the old cherry tree?”

“If Mother sleeps.”

“And if she does not?”

“The day after.”

“And if the world ends?”

“Then beside my little star.”

He kissed her fingers, leaped over the low fence, and vanished among the shadows just as three young Cossacks came singing around the bend. His voice presently joined theirs, boldest of all, and Hanna heard the song travel away through the village—fainter, fainter, until it seemed no louder than the humming of a bee imprisoned beneath a flower.

She should have closed the shutter.

Instead she remained at the window.

Moonlight poured over the pool, and the dark water rocked without wind. The ruined house stood silent beneath the willows, every door barred, every shutter closed. Yet Hanna fancied that somewhere within it a woman was walking, slowly, slowly, from chamber to chamber, seeking a child she had betrayed—or a child seeking the father who had cast her out.

Then something white stirred near the reeds.

Hanna’s breath failed. She crossed herself once, twice, and a third time. The white thing sank; a circle widened across the pool and broke the moon’s reflection into trembling pieces.

Far away Leoko’s companions shouted with laughter. Nearer at hand a night bird cried from the pine wood, and the abandoned house seemed to listen.

Hanna drew back at last. She shut the shutter, set the wooden bolt in its place, and stood in the little room with her heart beating violently. On the table the holy lamp burned before the painted saints, small, steadfast, and golden.

Outside, the village slept.

But the pool did not.

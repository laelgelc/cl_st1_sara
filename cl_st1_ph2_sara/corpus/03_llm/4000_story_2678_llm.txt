“Your tea, madam. I’ve put the little jug on this side because the handle gets hot—yes, it does, though it looks ever so innocent. China’s like people that way, isn’t it? There, I shouldn’t say such things. Her ladyship says comparisons encourage unkind thoughts.

“No, madam, she hasn’t come down again. Once she’s settled there’s no moving her, not with the lumbago. She’s doing her prayers now. She’ll be another twenty minutes, I should think, unless she gets thinking between the names. She prays for everybody separate. The butcher before the bishop, always, because she says the bishop has others praying for him and poor Mr. Vane has only his wife, who is Church of Rome and therefore doesn’t do it properly. Not that I’d know, madam.

“The kettle used to boil away while she prayed. I’d be in the dressing-room, listening to it jump on the spirit lamp, and I’d think, ‘One day it’ll boil itself dry and there’ll be something dreadful.’ But it never did. I watched it. That’s what service is mostly, I sometimes think—watching things so they don’t come to something dreadful.

“Only the last year, madam. Before that she knelt by the bed as straight as you please, though she’d be groaning when I helped her up. Our Saviour had many sorrows, she says, and never complained. But then, He didn’t have lumbago, did He? Oh! That was wicked. Please don’t repeat it. I only mean it must be difficult to remember heavenly things when your back catches.

“She’s very religious. Her mother was the same. I never knew the old lady, of course, but there’s a photograph downstairs beside the clock. Lace cap and a little nose, sharp as a paper-knife. Her ladyship’s getting like her. Sometimes when she’s asleep, with her hands outside the counterpane, I could almost fancy it was the dead lady lying there.

“No, madam, it doesn’t frighten me. Dead people never did, not really. My mother was the first I seen—saw, I mean. I was six, and they’d put purple pansies all round her neck because the collar didn’t sit right. Nobody told me why she was so quiet. I thought she was listening. I crawled under the tablecloth and watched her shoes below the coffin stand, and my aunt found me and slapped my legs for being disrespectful. But I wasn’t. I only wanted to see if she’d move.

“Father went before that. A cart, it was. They said he never knew, which seemed a kindness, though I couldn’t understand how not knowing could be kind. After Mother there was Grandfather and Aunt Lou, where we was living over the saddler’s yard. Grandfather had wonderful white hair, curled all round, and was proud of it. He curled mine too.

“Yes, madam, with the tongs. Heated at the kitchen fire. I hated Saturdays because of those tongs. ‘Beauty must be borne, Nell,’ he’d say, and once I turned my head just as he shut them, and he caught two fingers instead of the curl. There’s the mark, look. That pale place.

“Oh, he was sorry. Terribly proud men don’t say sorry, but he gave me the sugar off his bread-and-butter, so I knew. Aunt Lou said I’d done it to make a fuss. She rubbed lamp oil on and tied it with a rag, and I carried the tea tray one-handed till Monday.

“I went into service at thirteen. That sounds young now, doesn’t it? I was big for thirteen, except in the chest, and Mrs. Pelling said a flat girl showed off an apron nicely. Seven of us below stairs, and one bathroom, though nobody used it except Cook when her feet swelled. We had a pump. In winter the handle burned your skin with cold.

“I liked the silver best. You could make something out of it. Black and smeared when it came to you, then the cloth going grey, and presently there was your own face, long and wobbly, in the teapot. I used to pull expressions at myself. Once Mr. Pelling came in behind me and asked if I was ill. I said, ‘No, sir, I’m polishing,’ and he said he hoped polishing wasn’t painful.

“That was a happy house. Not happy like books, where everyone marries the proper person and there’s enough money, but busy-happy, as you might say. I had Sundays once a month, from after dinner until nine. Aunt Lou always expected me home, so it wasn’t much of a holiday. She’d save mending for me.

“There was a fair one August. I could hear the organ from her window—tinny, you know, and cheerful. They had silver-grey donkeys with red saddles, and all the girls from the laundry rode them. I’d never been on anything alive except Grandfather’s knee, and he wasn’t very lively. I wanted a ride something shameful.

“No, madam, I didn’t ask. Aunt Lou would have said I was flighty. So after we’d gone to bed I pretended I was talking in my sleep. I said, ‘Please, Auntie, only one ride on the little grey donkey.’ Quite loud. She sat up and said, ‘You’re awake, Ellen.’ And I said, still with my eyes shut, ‘The red saddle, please.’ Would you believe it, she laughed? I’d never heard her laugh before. It was like a cupboard door sticking.

“But we didn’t go. There was mending.

“I saw the donkeys going home next morning, tied nose to tail. Their ears drooped. I thought perhaps they hadn’t enjoyed it either, and that made me feel better. Funny, the things one remembers. I couldn’t tell you what Aunt Lou looked like on ordinary days, but I can see those red saddles as plain as the rug there.

“Her ladyship took me when I was twenty-one. I came as second housemaid and was made personal maid after Miss Burrows married the chemist. Her ladyship was delicate even then. Not ill, precisely, but every happiness gave her palpitations and every sorrow gave her migraine. She required quiet brushing. A hundred strokes, but quiet.

“She had beautiful hair—chestnut, with gold bits when the light caught. Mine never was anything. Mouse, Grandfather called it. Harry said it was the colour of willow bark after rain.

“Harry? Oh... Mr. Mercer, madam. He was florist’s assistant in those days, at Bell and Mercer, though there wasn’t any Mercer on the sign till later. He brought arrangements for the dining table. Great white lilies mostly, which smell like a church where somebody’s fainted, if you’ll excuse me.

“He had clever hands. Not gentleman’s hands; there were green marks in the cracks and little cuts from the wire. But he could turn three ferns and six roses into something you’d think had grown that way deliberately. He said flowers had manners and mustn’t be crowded. I thought that lovely.

“We walked on my evenings. Nothing improper. Along the square twice and sometimes as far as the river if her ladyship had no headache. He’d point out windows and say which shop he’d have. Not a grand one. Green paint, gold letters, and buckets standing outside with wet pavement round them. We planned it all.

“There was to be a room behind, and a yellow blind. I’d keep the accounts because Harry’s figures wandered, and I’d make tea for the customers if they were waiting for wreaths. He said I could arrange the window. Me! I’d have put violets low down for children to see, and tall branches at the back, and one surprising flower in the middle. Something scarlet.

“Yes, madam. We were engaged, in a way. He gave me this.

“It’s only silver. A little bird carrying a heart on a chain, and the heart’s pierced through with a dagger. Ducky, isn’t it? Though I don’t know why the bird doesn’t drop it if it hurts him. Harry said it meant faithful forever.

“Her ladyship knew. She noticed everything in the mirror while pretending not to look. One night she said, ‘That is a new ornament, Ellen.’ I said, ‘Yes, my lady.’ Then she said nothing for so long I could hear the brush moving through her hair and the clock downstairs striking wrong—it always gained seven minutes.

“She was ever so kind. That was the trouble. If she’d scolded I might have gone.

“She said I must naturally think of my own future. Naturally. Then her face went grey and she asked for the salts. She said marriage was holy and no loyal mistress would stand between a good servant and her happiness. Then she had to lie down.

“Harry wanted the wedding in October. He’d taken the shop—not the green one, another, near the cemetery, which was better for wreaths. He had two rooms ready. He’d bought a kettle and six cups, all different patterns because they were cheap. We went to see them.

“I stood in the little back room, and there was sun on the bare boards. I could smell damp plaster and chrysanthemums. Harry put his arms round me and said, ‘This is ours, Nell.’ And I looked at the cups. One had blue cornflowers. I thought: That will be mine every morning.

“Then I thought of her ladyship ringing.

“She couldn’t manage with a stranger. Miss Burrows had written to say she couldn’t return, with the baby coming. Her ladyship’s hands shook when she fastened hooks, and no one knew the order of her medicines. The red bottle after food, the clear one before, and never the sleeping drops if she’d had the heart mixture. A new girl might have killed her through eagerness.

“I told Harry the following Thursday.

“No, madam, not everything. Men don’t understand everything. I said I’d changed my mind. He asked if there was somebody else, and I said yes.

“I meant her ladyship.

“White! He turned as white as a woman. Then red. He took the brooch from my collar—I thought he meant to keep it—but he closed my fingers round it so hard the pin went into me. ‘You’ll want that,’ he said, ‘when you find out what you’ve married.’

“That was cruel, wasn’t it? Though pain makes people poetical, I believe.

“He married a widow who kept a fruit shop. I’ve passed it. Flowers one side, apples the other, and three children in the doorway, though only one was his. He grew stout. I don’t suppose he remembers the yellow blind.

“Only once did he write. Her ladyship was very poorly that winter, and the letter lay in my apron all day. I never opened it. I put it in the kitchen fire after prayers. The gum on the envelope bubbled, and for a second the paper opened by itself, like a little white mouth. But the writing had gone black.

“No, madam, I wasn’t unhappy. I’d chosen, hadn’t I? There’s comfort in choosing, even if you choose the thing that hurts. Her ladyship improved by spring. She gave me a week’s wages and said, ‘I don’t know what I should do without you, Ellen.’

“That pays for a great deal.

“She still says it. Tonight, when I lifted her feet into bed, she said it twice. They’re light as sticks now, her feet. I warm the sheet first and tuck it round each one, because they mustn’t touch when the joints are bad. Then I set the water, prayer book, bell and handkerchief in a line. She likes a line. Disorder makes her dream.

“What shall I do when...?

“Oh, I don’t think of that, madam. There’s no sense. She may outlive us all. Religious people often do, because they’ve something to look forward to and so they take their time going.

“But the house would be strange. No bell. No medicine glasses. No hair in the brush—scarcely any now, poor lamb. I suppose they’d send me away while the rooms was covered up. I’ve a little saved. Enough for a room somewhere. Perhaps I could help in a flower shop, though I’m no good with modern flowers. They twist them any fashion now and call it art.

“No. No, madam, I’m not afraid.

“Only sometimes I wake before her bell and don’t know what hour it is, and there’s a moment when nobody wants me. Just a moment. It feels like standing in a house after all the furniture’s been carried out, with your own footsteps coming back at you.

“Listen to me! And it’s nearly one. I’ve talked your poor head off.

“Shall I take the cup? There. Let me straighten that pillow. You’ll be more comfortable if I pull it down at the corners. Her ladyship likes hers so. And your feet—may I tuck them? The night turns cold toward morning.

“That’s better. Bell on the right, water on the left. I’ll leave the door a finger’s width, madam, in case you call.

“What was I saying? Nothing sensible, I’m sure.

“At it again—you silly girl!

“Good night, madam.”

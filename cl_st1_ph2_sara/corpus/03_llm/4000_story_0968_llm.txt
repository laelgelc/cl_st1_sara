# The Message of the White Birds

I

There were two explanations of Ainsley’s retirement to Willowmere, and both were accepted because one was respectable and the other was true.

The respectable explanation was that he had discovered, at thirty-six, a passion for scientific agriculture. He intended to restore exhausted fields, improve the native cattle, import a species of pig which possessed every virtue except beauty, and demonstrate to the neighbouring farmers that intelligence, capital, and a gentlemanly disregard of expense could make the soil profitable.

The true explanation was Polly Kirkland.

Willowmere lay in the northern part of New York State, where the wooded hills had been folded away from the railways as if by a housewife who did not expect to use them. The house, large, white, and tranquilly Georgian, stood above a lake called Lone Lake. This sheet of water formed the farm’s contemplative eye; the ancient oaks were its brows, the sedges its dark lashes, and upon clear evenings the western sky looked down into it and appeared agreeably surprised at its own magnificence.

Ainsley had bought the place because the terrace was conducive to hope, the lake to despair, and the railway station only eleven miles distant. A motor could do the distance in twenty-three minutes if the road was dry, in thirty if the road was wet, and in no time at all, according to Ainsley, if a cablegram arrived.

His agricultural experiments were less successful. The imported pigs caught cold. The new cattle regarded the old cattle with social reserve. An expensive machine for planting potatoes planted one of Ainsley’s silver cigarette cases and then ceased work permanently. But as these failures occupied only his money, while Polly occupied his mind, he bore them with fortitude.

He had proposed to Polly four times.

The first proposal had taken place in a conservatory and had been interrupted by an orchestra. The second had occurred in a taxicab, where Polly, after hearing him with composure, directed the driver to her club. The third was made at a country house under an elm during a thunderstorm. The fourth, which Ainsley considered final in all senses, was delivered in the drawing-room of Mrs. Kirkland’s house on East Sixty-Seventh Street.

“You don’t dislike me,” he had said.

“No,” Polly admitted.

“You enjoy being with me.”

“Usually.”

“You trust me.”

“With everything except small parcels, appointments before noon, and explanations.”

“Then why,” demanded Ainsley, “will you not marry me?”

Polly looked at him with that serious sweetness which made his intelligence, never robust in her presence, leave him altogether.

“Because I want to love you.”

“You can begin after we are married.”

“That would be convenient for you.”

“For both of us! Love is notoriously unbusinesslike. It should be given a sound establishment in which to develop.”

Polly shook her head.

“There are men one can live with,” she said, “and men one discovers one cannot live without.”

“And I am merely habitable?”

“At present.”

Ainsley stood up so violently that a little French table trembled under a vase of violets.

“Polly, if you go to Egypt without giving me an answer, I shall plain DIE.”

“You have said that before.”

“This time I have had medical advice.”

“You saw Dr. Bellingham about your shoulder.”

“He understands my constitution.”

“Your constitution is excellent.”

“That makes it worse. A delicate man might fade gracefully. I shall take months.”

She smiled, but there was trouble behind the smile, and because Ainsley loved her he saw the trouble; because he was Ainsley, he misunderstood it.

Polly was leaving within the week. Her mother, who had developed a conscientious interest in monuments after developing a less conscientious dislike of the New York winter, had taken a dahabiyeh for a journey up the Nile. They would be gone indefinitely, a word invented to torture lovers and accommodate mothers.

“You could cable from Port Said,” Ainsley urged. “Or from Cairo. Or from anywhere possessing an operator and an alphabet. One word would do.”

“What word?”

“‘Come.’”

“And if I sent ‘Don’t’?”

“You would never be so cruel.”

“I might send ‘Consider.’”

“I should come.”

“Or ‘Crocodiles.’”

“I should suppose it a code and come.”

“That is precisely the difficulty. You hear only what you wish to hear.”

“I hear that you love me but are afraid to say so.”

“I am not afraid. I simply do not yet know.”

Then, after a silence, she added, “Perhaps I shall send you a sign.”

Ainsley regarded her suspiciously.

“What sort of sign?”

“If I told you, it would not be a sign. It would be an instruction.”

“Instructions have much to recommend them.”

“You must read it in your heart.”

“My heart,” said Ainsley, “is an organ of considerable enthusiasm but no education. Be more definite.”

“No. If my feeling changes—if I know that I cannot live without you—you will know.”

“How?”

“You will know.”

“Polly!”

But she had risen, and he could get no more from her except her hand, which she allowed him to retain for a moment longer than was socially necessary. This did not satisfy him; it sustained him, which is frequently more dangerous.

II

The steamer sailed on a raw gray morning. New York, having furnished fog for the occasion, continued its ordinary life with offensive punctuality. Elevated trains roared, brokers telephoned, women bought hats, and a small boy on the pier ate an orange throughout Ainsley’s agony.

Polly stood above him at the rail in a coat the colour of camel’s hair. Mrs. Kirkland had already gone below because the wind was injurious to her throat, though not, apparently, to her daughter’s romance.

“Remember!” Ainsley called.

“I shall remember.”

“You will send it?”

“If there is anything to send.”

“The sign?”

“Yes.”

“Something I can understand?”

“You must not understand it here.” She touched her forehead. “You must understand it here.” Her gloved hand moved to her heart.

A whistle split the air. Porters shouted. Ainsley found himself urged down the gangplank by an officer who did not know that a man’s entire future stood at the rail above them and would probably not have delayed the sailing had he known.

“Polly!” Ainsley shouted. “If you send it, I’ll come!”

She leaned over.

“If you love me, you’ll come!”

The words were carried away by wind, whistles, and the indignation of the orange-eating boy, whom Ainsley had accidentally struck with his hat.

“What did she say?” Ainsley demanded of the nearest bystander.

The bystander, an elderly gentleman with maritime calm, replied, “Something about the sun.”

Ainsley stared after the vessel until Polly became a pale point, then a moving nothing, and finally an idea.

That afternoon he purchased two steamer trunks, a dressing case, tropical helmets in three degrees of rigidity, twelve linen suits, a medicine chest suitable for an expedition across equatorial Africa, and a revolver which his valet, Griggs, secretly unloaded. He reserved passage on three steamers sailing on successive weeks. Then he went to Willowmere to wait for the sign.

This was in November.

For the first ten days his faith was splendid. He rose early, rode about the farm, and returned before luncheon to inspect the telephone. In the afternoon he drove to the post office. At dinner he instructed Griggs to keep the motor ready. Before retiring he consulted sailing lists and composed imaginary replies to imaginary cablegrams.

COME AT ONCE, Polly would say.

LEAVING THURSDAY, he would answer, although if the cable arrived on Friday he would naturally leave Friday.

By the third week his confidence had acquired nerves. Every ring of the telephone brought him out of chairs at dangerous angles. He bribed the station agent to report all foreign telegrams. He installed wireless apparatus, with an operator, in a room over the stables, and received messages concerning weather, shipping, and once an inquiry addressed to a hardware merchant in Buffalo.

The last message read:

SHIP SIX DOZEN WHITE AT LOWEST PRICE.

Ainsley appeared at the breakfast table carrying the paper in a shaking hand.

“It is from her.”

His guest, Roger Merritt, read it twice.

“Polly wants you to ship six dozen white what?”

“That is the sign.”

“Then the sign is incomplete.”

“Of course it is incomplete. Otherwise any fool could read it.”

“What do you propose to do?”

“Go to Egypt.”

“Taking six dozen white objects?”

Ainsley frowned.

Roger was a lawyer and therefore trained to discover objections to happiness. He was thin, pleasantly ugly, and had known Ainsley since both had been expelled from the same preparatory school for an experiment involving a cannon and the headmaster’s greenhouse.

“White roses,” Ainsley said suddenly. “She once wore white roses.”

“Six dozen?”

“It is a number of significance.”

“What significance?”

“She has six letters in her Christian name.”

“Polly has five.”

“In Kirkland, then.”

“Eight.”

Ainsley rang for Griggs.

“Have the trunks put in the motor.”

“The large trunks, sir?”

“All of them.”

While Griggs, who had packed and unpacked those trunks five times, retired with the expression of a martyr recalled to the arena, Roger telephoned the wireless operator. The message proved to concern six dozen white enamel basins intended for a hotel in Syracuse.

Ainsley did not leave. The trunks remained in the hall for three days, where visitors mistook them for evidence that he had returned.

In December a package arrived from Cairo. Ainsley tore off the wrappings before Griggs could bring scissors. Within lay a small leather box, and within the box an ancient intaglio ring of green stone. A card bore the words:

For Ainsley, who likes old things only when they are expensive.  
—Polly

He turned the ring under the lamp. Cut into it was a slender bird with long legs and a curved beak.

“The sign!” he cried.

Griggs, who was brushing a coat, did not permit himself to jump.

“Very likely, sir.”

“What does the bird mean?”

“I could not say, sir.”

“It is flying east.”

Griggs looked at it.

“With respect, sir, it may be facing west.”

“Get the trunks.”

“Yes, sir.”

The ring was taken to the Metropolitan Museum, where a learned gentleman identified the figure as possibly a heron, possibly an ibis, and certainly not flying. The stone, he added, was Ptolemaic or modern, depending upon whether Miss Kirkland had paid twenty dollars or two hundred.

Ainsley wrote Polly a letter of sixteen pages, saying nothing of importance in eleven different ways. Her reply, delayed by travel, contained affectionate accounts of temples, bazaars, dust, tourists, and her mother’s struggle with dragomans. It did not mention the ring except to hope that it fitted.

He tried the ring on every finger. It fitted none.

January covered Lone Lake with ice and Willowmere with silence. The naked oaks rose black against a sky the colour of old silver. Ainsley skated alone, cutting great circles which always returned to the same place. At night the wind complained in the chimneys, and he sat before the library fire reading books on Egyptian symbols, telepathy, migratory birds, and the operation of the human heart.

The books disagreed.

One author held that thought could pass between sympathetic minds across oceans. Another proved that it could not pass across a dining table. An Egyptologist declared the ibis sacred to wisdom. A naturalist described it as an indiscriminate feeder of unattractive habits. A poet said love required no messenger. Ainsley threw the poet into the fire.

February brought another cablegram.

ARRIVING TUESDAY LOVE POLLY

Ainsley read it, shouted once, and put the entire household into motion. Fires were lit in the unused bedrooms. Flowers came up from New York. The chef, fetched from his winter holiday, began a dinner of eight courses. Ainsley drove to the station through a snowstorm, carrying furs, orchids, and a marriage licence.

The train arrived with two commercial travellers, a woman carrying a canary, three college boys, and a stout person named Polly Faber, who was visiting the housekeeper’s sister.

Ainsley went home without speaking.

Thereafter his hope became less noisy. It did not die, because hope, when fed upon ambiguity, is difficult to kill; but it grew thin and took to hiding. He no longer ordered the trunks down at every telegram. He ceased asking the postmaster whether foreign mail had been mislaid. Once he permitted two full days to pass without consulting a sailing list. Griggs observed this decay of enthusiasm with concern, for although it had inconvenienced the household, it had also warmed it.

In March Polly wrote from Luxor:

We are staying longer than we intended. The Nile is more beautiful than anyone has the right to expect, and the evenings have a softness which persuades even Mother to forgive Egypt for being foreign. Sometimes I think of Willowmere under snow. Do you still look for signs, dear Ainsley? Do not look too hard. The important things are seldom hidden; we hide from them.

Ainsley read this beneath the library lamp while rain struck the windows. He read it again on the terrace next morning, again by Lone Lake in the afternoon, and once more in bed. It seemed to mean everything except what he needed it to mean.

He wrote back:

I have stopped looking.

This was untrue. But it was the first lie he had ever told Polly for the purpose of preserving his dignity, and it gave him no pleasure.

III

Spring entered Willowmere cautiously. The ice loosened from the shore, broke into dim panes, and vanished. Reeds thrust green points through the shallows. The hills, which had seemed permanently gray, put on a faint haze of colour; then, almost overnight, the orchards flowered.

The farm demanded attention. Calves were born, fences fell, the imported pigs recovered their health and destroyed a kitchen garden of inestimable value. Ainsley attended meetings, signed cheques, and spoke intelligently about drainage. His neighbours concluded that adversity had made a farmer of him.

It had not. It had merely tired him.

By May Polly had been gone six months. Her letters came regularly and said less and less. She described a moonlit temple, a journey by donkey, an English archaeologist who had lost his spectacles in a tomb, and a scarab purchased by Mrs. Kirkland from a boy who swore it had belonged to Cleopatra. She asked about the lake, the dogs, Roger, and the unsuccessful pigs. She did not ask Ainsley to come.

One evening, when the air smelled of rain and wild apple, he walked down to Lone Lake with Roger. They climbed a granite ledge above the eastern shore and sat looking across the water.

Clouds had massed in the west, sombre at their bases and luminous along their upper edges. Beneath them the lake had turned from blue to pewter. A single lane of sunset lay across it like a path no boat could follow.

“A storm by midnight,” Roger said.

Ainsley nodded.

“You might go to Egypt anyway.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because she said she would send for me when she knew.”

“Did she?”

“She promised a sign.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is substantially the same.”

“No romantic statement is substantially the same as anything, including itself.”

Ainsley picked up a pebble and sent it skipping down the slope.

“She wishes to be certain.”

“Of you?”

“Of herself.”

“And while she becomes certain, you remain here studying pigs.”

“I do not study them. I finance them.”

Roger filled his pipe. “Suppose there is no sign.”

“Then there is no love.”

“Suppose she believes your coming would be the sign.”

Ainsley turned. “Whose side are you on?”

“Yours, which is why I object to your interpretation.”

“You lawyers think interpretation can alter facts.”

“It can alter inheritances, marriages, wars, and dinner engagements. Why not facts?”

Ainsley did not answer. Out beyond the reeds something white appeared against the darkening sky.

At first it looked like a torn scrap of cloud. Then another whiteness detached itself from the west, and another. Six shapes came slowly over the oaks.

They were birds, but their flight was unlike the sure, rhythmic passage of wild geese. These creatures travelled close together and low, every beat of their broad wings seeming the last they could endure. Their necks drooped; their legs trailed; they rose a little, sank, recovered, and sank again. Against the storm clouds their bodies shone with an almost unearthly pallor.

Roger took the pipe from his mouth.

“What in heaven are those?”

The birds crossed the sunset lane. For one instant their feathers caught the light and became rose, gold, and dazzling white. Then the colour passed, leaving them frail and ghostly.

“They are exhausted,” Ainsley said.

One bird fell behind. Its wings moved twice, ceased, and opened again just above the water. The others turned with it, circling the lake as though seeking a place remembered in a dream.

They descended together.

Their feet struck the surface; silver lines ran out beneath them. For several yards they stumbled through water, half flying and half falling, until they reached the shallows near the reeds. There they stood without motion, six pale figures bowed by utter weariness.

Ainsley was already sliding down the rock.

“Where are you going?”

“To them.”

“They may be dangerous.”

“They can hardly stand.”

“That does not prevent danger. It sometimes causes it.”

Ainsley cleared the last shelf of granite, landed in a bed of early iris, and ran along the shore. Roger followed by the path, protesting in a more dignified manner.

At the water’s edge Ainsley stopped.

The nearest bird lifted its head. It was nearly as tall as his waist, white except for black tips showing beneath the folded wings. Its beak was long and curved downward, its eye dark, remote, and curiously patient. The other five stood behind it, so still that they might have been carved from old ivory.

“Herons?” Ainsley whispered.

“Not our herons.”

“Egrets?”

“Too large.”

The gardener, attracted by Ainsley’s shout, came down with two stable boys. Griggs appeared on the terrace carrying a pair of field glasses and the expression of one who had long expected foreign complications.

“Bring grain,” Ainsley ordered. “Fish. Bread. Anything birds eat.”

“Those birds, sir?”

“Obviously those birds. Do you see others?”

Buckets, baskets, and saucers arrived. The birds ignored corn, sampled minnows, and drank greedily. One settled on the mud with its head beneath its wing. Another tottered and was supported, or seemed to be supported, by its neighbour.

Ainsley watched them with a tenderness he had recently denied to mankind.

Roger examined the nearest through the glasses.

“I have seen something like them,” he said. “In a book, perhaps. Long curved bill—white body—black wing feathers. They resemble the sacred ibis of the Nile.”

The words entered the evening quietly.

Ainsley did not move.

“What did you say?”

“The sacred ibis. Though I imagine these are some related species escaped from a collection. They certainly have not flown from Egypt.”

Ainsley turned upon him with a face so altered that Roger lowered the glasses.

“The ring,” Ainsley said.

“What ring?”

“The bird on Polly’s ring.”

“Oh, no.”

“Six birds.”

“Ainsley—”

“Six months.”

“That proves nothing.”

“White. From the west. Falling at my lake.”

“Also nothing.”

“From the Nile.”

“I expressly said they could not be from the Nile.”

“You said they were sacred ibises.”

“I said they resembled them.”

Ainsley seized Roger by both shoulders.

“She has sent them.”

“She has done nothing of the kind.”

“She promised a sign I must read in my heart. Well, I read it!”

“You are reading it with your imagination.”

“My imagination has been wrong five months. It is entitled to one correct evening.”

He released Roger and ran toward the house.

Up the slope he went, through the iris again, across the lawn, over a low stone wall rather than around it, and onto the terrace in a succession of movements which caused Griggs to abandon dignity and pursue him.

“The trunks!” Ainsley shouted.

“Yes, sir.”

“Get them down.”

“They have been down since November, sir.”

“Then get them UP—into the motor! Telephone the station. Telephone the steamship office. Telephone New York. I want the captain’s cabin on the first vessel sailing.”

“It may be occupied, sir.”

“Then buy the captain another cabin.”

Roger reached the terrace.

“You cannot cross the Atlantic because six escaped birds landed in your pond.”

“I can cross it because I have a passport and money.”

“What will you say to Polly?”

“I shall say I received her message.”

“And when she denies sending it?”

“She won’t.”

“She will.”

“Then,” Ainsley declared, “I shall drown myself in the Nile.”

Griggs paused in the doorway.

“Shall I pack the revolver, sir?”

“No. I am going to be married.”

“Yes, sir. The light linen or the heavier?”

“All of it!”

Within forty minutes the motor was at the door. Within fifty, the trunks were tied behind it. Within an hour, Ainsley had said good-bye to the six birds, which continued to stand in the reeds with their drooping heads and folded wings, as indifferent to the destiny they had altered as stars.

Roger climbed into the motor beside him.

“I am coming to the station.”

“To stop me?”

“To make sure you go. If you remain here another week, I shall drown you in the lake myself.”

As they started, Ainsley looked back. The storm had moved north without breaking. A strip of clear sky burned above the hills, and on the dark water the six birds shone like a message written in an unknown alphabet.

IV

Ainsley crossed the Atlantic with the speed ordinarily attainable only by people who have missed an appointment.

The steamer took the usual number of days, but he objected to each of them. He offered the chief engineer a substantial sum to increase the vessel’s speed, and when told that this would endanger the engines, replied that engines were replaceable. He walked the decks at night, sent wireless messages ahead, and quarrelled with a steward who insisted that Alexandria could not be seen from Sicily.

At Naples he received a reply from Mrs. Kirkland:

POLLY WITH US LUXOR WELL WHY ARE YOU COMING

Ainsley answered:

MESSAGE RECEIVED EXPLANATIONS LATER

There was a train, then another train, then Cairo, blazing and noisy beneath a white sun. Ainsley remained there only long enough to discover that all Egypt seemed engaged in preventing him from travelling through Egypt. Porters seized the wrong trunks. A dragoman offered to show him the pyramids. A hotel manager explained railway timetables as though initiating him into a mystery religion.

At last he travelled south.

The Nile accompanied the railway, appearing and disappearing between fields of vivid green. Beyond cultivation the desert rose, bare and golden, holding distance without mercy. Villages of mud brick crouched beneath palms; children ran beside the train; white-sailed boats leaned upon the river. Everything was strange, ancient, and fiercely illuminated, while Ainsley’s one concern was whether Polly would be wearing a hat when he found her.

Luxor received him in the late afternoon.

He learned that the Kirklands’ dahabiyeh lay moored a mile above the town. Refusing rest, food, and the assistance of an intelligent hotel clerk, he secured a donkey, discovered within five minutes that his steamer trunks could not accompany him upon it, abandoned both donkey and trunks, and proceeded in a hired carriage.

The dahabiyeh floated beside a bank where acacias threw thin shadows on the water. It was long, low, and painted white, with rugs upon the deck and flowering plants arranged in brass pots. Beyond it the Nile moved broad and bronze toward the evening. The western hills had begun to take the colour of amethyst.

Ainsley saw Mrs. Kirkland first. She reclined beneath an awning, attended by tea, cushions, and two English women. Her astonishment, though genuine, remained perfectly social.

“My dear Ainsley! We heard you were coming, but one never believes cablegrams. They are so often exaggerated.”

“Where is Polly?”

Mrs. Kirkland pointed with a teaspoon.

Polly stood at the far end of the deck.

She wore white. Ainsley had anticipated blue, but forgave her instantly. One hand rested on the rail, and the late light lay along her hair. For a moment she did not move. Then she came toward him—not rapidly, not slowly, but with an openness in her face that deprived him of speech.

“Ainsley,” she said.

“Polly.”

“You came.”

“I came.”

“You understood.”

“Perfectly.”

This was not the confession he had intended. He had intended to explain the birds at once, to describe their curved beaks, their black-tipped wings, their exhaustion, the green ring, Roger’s identification, and the sunset on Lone Lake. But Polly’s eyes shone with such happiness that truth, normally a sturdy traveller, missed its connection.

She gave him both hands.

“Oh, Ainsley, I was afraid you never would.”

“You were afraid?”

“Every week I thought, now he will stop waiting for something impossible and come. Every steamer from the north—I wondered. Every footstep on the landing—” She laughed softly. “It was becoming humiliating.”

“But your sign—”

“Yes.”

Ainsley swallowed.

“Your very remarkable sign.”

“You read it in your heart.”

“In my heart,” he repeated.

Mrs. Kirkland, observing that the moment threatened to become private, gathered the English women and took them below to inspect a scarab. A servant removed the tea. Presently Ainsley and Polly stood alone beside the rail.

The river carried little islands of reflected flame. Across the water, palms and temple columns were drawn in purple against the west.

“Tell me,” Polly said. “When did you know?”

Ainsley considered several answers, all honest and all disastrous.

“I knew when I could no longer stay away.”

“That is what I meant.”

“Of course.”

“I did not send anything.”

He looked at her.

“Nothing?”

“No literal signal. You didn’t suppose I meant a cable?”

“No,” said Ainsley with dignity. “Certainly not a cable.”

“Or some absurd parcel?”

“Absurd parcels never entered my head.”

She slipped her hand through his arm.

“I meant that if you truly loved me, waiting would become impossible. You would understand that love itself was the message.”

“Yes,” he said faintly. “That is substantially how I reasoned.”

“Did it happen suddenly?”

“Very suddenly.”

“Where were you?”

“By the lake.”

“I knew you would think of me there.”

“I did.”

“Was it beautiful?”

“Extraordinarily.”

“And then you simply ordered your trunks and came?”

“I cleared a flower-bed first.”

“What?”

“Nothing. I came at once.”

She leaned her head against his shoulder. The gesture restored both his courage and his dishonesty.

“Polly,” he said, “does this mean—”

“That I have discovered you are the man I cannot live without?”

“Yes.”

“It means precisely that.”

“Then you will marry me?”

“I suppose I must.”

“When?”

She lifted her head.

“Ainsley, you have crossed an ocean and half a continent. Surely you can endure a week.”

“A week! Polly, I have endured six months. Another week would be ostentation.”

“Mother will want Cairo.”

“Your mother can have Cairo.”

“For the wedding.”

“She can have the pyramids, the museum, the Sphinx, and all the hotels. I want Thursday.”

“Friday.”

“Thursday afternoon.”

“Friday morning.”

“Agreed.”

They shook hands upon it; then, as the contract concerned them intimately, they ratified it by other means.

Afterward they stood watching the river darken. Lamps appeared on distant boats. A muezzin’s call rose from the town, remote and melancholy, and the first star trembled above the desert.

Ainsley’s happiness was perfect except for the truth.

The truth waited close beside him, patient and slightly amused. He would have to tell her about the wireless message ordering six dozen white basins. He would have to mention the intaglio ring and the learned gentleman at the museum. The arrival of Polly Faber could not honourably be omitted. Above all, he would have to confess that he had crossed the world not because love itself had spoken clearly, but because Roger Merritt had made an uncertain zoological observation.

He decided to confess after dinner.

Then he decided that such revelations were cruel before marriage.

Polly sighed contentedly.

“I should like to have seen your face when you understood.”

“You will,” Ainsley said, “when I tell you about it.”

“Tell me now.”

He drew breath.

At that moment a shadow passed over the deck.

Polly looked up.

From the north, low above the Nile, came six white birds.

They flew in a close and wavering line. Their long necks were bowed, their legs streamed behind them, and their broad wings moved with the same heavy, failing rhythm Ainsley had watched above Lone Lake. Against the violet sky they shone; against the last red light they became briefly golden. One faltered, and the others circled with it.

Ainsley’s hand tightened on the rail.

The birds descended toward the river.

Their feet touched the bronze surface, drawing six slender paths through the reflection of the sunset. They settled near the reeds below the dahabiyeh and stood together, grave and pale, their curved beaks lowered as though in ritual or fatigue.

Polly watched them with delight.

“How beautiful! What are they?”

Ainsley did not answer.

A boatman on the lower deck crossed himself according to his own faith, then called to another man. Mrs. Kirkland came up with the English women. One of them produced glasses.

“Ibises,” she announced. “How extraordinary to see them here in such a little company.”

“Sacred ibises?” Polly asked.

“Something very like them.”

Polly turned to Ainsley.

“You look as if you had seen a ghost.”

“Six ghosts,” he said.

The birds lifted their heads together.

For a moment Ainsley saw again the northern lake beneath storm clouds, the black oaks, the silver water, the servants running with baskets, and Roger holding the glasses. He saw the ring cut with its ancient bird. He heard Polly’s voice above the steamer whistles: You must read it in your heart.

Coincidence, said reason.

Providence, said love.

Nonsense, said that practical portion of his mind which had failed to make potatoes remunerative.

Yet there they stood—six white birds upon the Nile, after six white birds had come to him across a darkening sky and sent him where he ought to have gone without them.

“What were you going to tell me?” Polly asked.

Ainsley looked at her, at the birds, and at the first lamps trembling upon the water.

“That,” he said slowly, “is a long story.”

“I like long stories.”

“You may not admire the hero.”

“I am going to marry him on Friday.”

“Thursday.”

“Friday.”

He smiled, but his astonishment remained. The world, which had appeared for six months to be a machine contrived for delaying him, had revealed itself suddenly as something less reliable and more generous. Whether the birds had flown from a private collection, from an unknown marsh, from his longing, or from some region not marked upon maps, he could not decide. He knew only that he had mistaken their meaning and obeyed it correctly.

Below them, the six birds rested upon the dark Nile.

Ainsley postponed his confession until after dinner, then until morning, and afterward until a suitable moment which, during the first years of marriage, failed to present itself. But he knew that one day he must tell Polly everything: the trunks, the basins, the ring, the wrong Polly at the station, the birds on Lone Lake, and the lie upon the dahabiyeh.

He also knew, with that rare certainty which comes not from the head or the heart but from being happily trapped between them, that Polly would laugh, forgive him, and insist she had been right.

And perhaps she had.

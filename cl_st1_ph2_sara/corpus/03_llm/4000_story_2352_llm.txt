The Oracle was standing beyond the firelight with his hands hooked behind him and his old felt hat pushed back, looking across the creek-flat as if there was something on the other side besides ringbarked trees and a boundary fence.

“What’s he staring at now?” asked Mitchell.

Sympathy Joe lifted the tea-billy off the coals and set it where it could settle.

“Nothing that you could see,” he said. “Nor me neither, perhaps. He looks about a thousand miles across country when he stands like that—and about twenty years back.”

“He’s ratty,” said Mitchell.

“That’s what you young chaps always say. A man don’t grin when you grin, nor swear when you expect him to, nor run after every new rush with his tongue out, so he’s ratty. You fellers of nineteen or twenty know this world from the top rail down, of course. My oath! You’ve been cheated in a horse deal, and perhaps a barmaid’s laughed at you, and so you’ve got no more illusions. You understand women, sorrow, drink, mateship, death, insolvency, and the price of flour at every store from here to the Gulf.”

Mitchell grinned.

“Go on, Joe.”

“You called him an old ass this morning.”

“Well, he was walking to and fro behind the surveyor’s tent, talking to himself.”

“He wasn’t talking to himself. He was trying to recollect a tune.”

“Same thing.”

“No, it ain’t. There’s tunes that’ll fetch a man farther than a bullock-team could drag him. You mind how he went deaf last night when little Harry played that German air on the concertina?”

“I thought he was sulking.”

Joe poured the tea carefully.

“You think too cheap. The Oracle wasn’t always called the Oracle, and he wasn’t always grey and stooped, neither. His name’s Tom Marshall. When I first knew him he was straight as a sapling, broad in the shoulders, and had a laugh that made other chaps laugh whether they’d heard the joke or not. He could lift the tail of a loaded dray out of a rut, ride anything with hair on it, and put two fighting men apart without making a speech over the business. He was the best-liked young fellow on the southern fields.”

“Who told you?”

“Uncle Bob, mostly. Him and Aunt Bob knew the rights of it. I picked up pieces elsewhere, too. Some from Tom himself, though he never told a yarn straight through, and some from people who didn’t know I was listening. You can generally build the truth out of lies if you’ve got enough of ’em.”

Mitchell drew his knees up.

“Was there a woman?”

“There generally is, when a good man goes queer.”

Joe filled his pipe, though he did not light it.

“Tom was digging near Bendigo then, or perhaps farther north—I forget which lead, and it don’t matter. The place was one of them roaring canvas towns that comes up in a fortnight and goes down quicker than a drunk at closing-time. There were stores made of calico, shanties made of slabs, sly-grog shops made of anything that’d keep the rain off the whisky, and thousands of chaps sinking holes as if the devil had buried their fortunes and promised to wait below for ’em.

“She came there with a travelling concert lot. Clara Vane she called herself, though no doubt she’d had other names. She could sing. That was the mischief of her. A woman may have a false heart, false hair, false teeth and a false account of her father, but if her voice is true some honest fool will trust all the rest.

“She wasn’t what you’d call handsome after you’d looked twice. But most young fellows never look twice in them matters. She had black eyes, a white face, and a way of seeming helpless whenever a strong man came near. She’d tell Tom how rough the world had used her, how she’d been left without friends, and how she hated the life she was living. She was always just going to leave it, you understand—next week, after one more engagement, or when somebody paid her money owing, or when she got news from a sick aunt that perhaps never existed.

“Tom believed her. He was young, and he’d got that dangerous notion that because he meant fair everybody else must mean fair when they cried. He spent money on her, fought one chap on her account, and would likely have married her if the rush hadn’t broken up and scattered the camp.”

“What became of her?”

“She cleared out with a red-headed horse-dealer named Redmond. Tom heard she’d gone to Melbourne. It hit him hard, but pride helped him then. Pride’s a poor crutch, but it’ll carry a man over some nasty ground. He packed his swag, went south, and worked here and there till he got into Gippsland.”

Joe paused to listen. The Oracle had moved farther along the fence and was now only a dark mark in the gloaming.

“In Gippsland Tom stopped with Uncle Bob. Bob wasn’t my uncle, properly speaking. Everybody called him that because he was the sort of old chap that feeds you first and asks your name afterwards. Aunt Bob was his wife, a little woman with a tongue like a stockwhip and a heart you could camp in.

“They had a place among the hills—cattle, a few acres under crop, pigs under the kitchen floor nearly, dogs everywhere, and hospitality enough to ruin ’em. Bertha Bredt lived with her people on the next selection.

“Now, I ain’t going to tell you Bertha was an angel. Whenever a woman dies young the district turns her into an angel, though it may have found faults enough while she was alive. But Bertha was good without making a business of goodness. That’s uncommon. She was fair-haired and quiet, and she had steady grey eyes that made a liar uncomfortable and an honest man contented. She worked, too. She could milk, bake, nurse a sick child, sit up with a woman in trouble, and play old tunes on a square piano that had come up from Melbourne in a bullock-dray and never been in tune since.

“Tom and her went together as natural as creek-water downhill. There wasn’t any mooning about it at first. He helped her father with a fence; she mended Tom’s shirt after he tore it at a branding; they rode to a picnic; he fetched medicine when her little brother had the fever. Then one evening she played that same German air Harry struck last night.”

“The one that upset him?”

“That’s it. Tom stood by the window listening. Aunt Bob said his face changed while Bertha played—not sad, mind you, but as if he’d suddenly seen the road ahead and found it pleasant. After that there was no doubt.

“They planned quiet. Tom was to take up land. He’d saved money, and Uncle Bob promised cattle on terms. There was a little weatherboard place empty near the river. Bertha had picked curtains in her mind, I suppose, and Tom had measured the paddock for a fence. A man’s married long before the parson gets at him, if he’s measured paddocks and a woman’s planned curtains.

“Then the letter came.”

Joe struck a match, watched it burn close to his fingers, and dropped it without lighting the pipe.

“It came from Clara. She said she was in Sydney, ill and destitute. She said Redmond had deserted her. And she said she was going to have a child—Tom’s child.”

Mitchell swore softly.

“Quite so,” said Joe. “That’s about what Uncle Bob said, only stronger.

“Tom walked all night. To and fro along the creek, to and fro by the stockyard, and once Aunt Bob saw him standing at the sliprails with his forehead on his arm. In the morning he looked as he looks now, only his hair was black.

“Uncle Bob told him to burn the letter. He said the woman had deceived him once and would do it again. Aunt Bob said much the same, though she put it plainer. But Tom asked one question: ‘Suppose it IS mine?’

“That stopped ’em.

“You see, Mitchell, the world’s got a handy rule for these cases. The woman bears the child, bears the shame, bears the hunger, and bears every respectable female’s stare in church; the man goes to another district and grows whiskers. If she steals a loaf they jail her. If she leaves the baby somewhere they call her unnatural. If she keeps it they call the child worse. Tom had seen enough of that. He said if there was one chance in ten that the child was his, he’d no right to put all the payment on her.

“Then he went to Bertha.”

“Did he tell her everything?”

“Every blanky thing. That was Tom’s way when lying might have served him better. He told her what Clara had been, what he’d been, what the letter said, and what he meant to do.

“Bertha went white, Aunt Bob said, but she never made a scene. She asked whether he loved Clara. He said no. She asked whether he believed the child was his. He said he didn’t know, but he COULD not gamble with it. Then she put both hands on his shoulders and said, ‘You must go, Tom. If you stayed now, you wouldn’t be the man I loved.’

“That sounds like something out of a book, perhaps. But Aunt Bob heard it through the half-open door, and Aunt Bob never read anything except the Bible, almanacs and labels on medicine bottles.

“Tom left next morning. Bertha came to the gate. He kissed her once, got on his horse, and rode away without looking back. She stood there till he was out of sight.”

“And married the other?”

“He did. Found her in a room over a public-house, with no money except what she’d got by pawning things, and the landlady threatening to put her out. Whether she was as bad off as she pretended I don’t know. Perhaps she was. Even deceitful people get hungry, and a lie don’t keep rain out.

“Tom married her. He got work at a timber yard, rented two rooms, and did his duty by her. She cried, promised, reformed, broke promises, made new ones, and could always make him pity her just when he’d made up his mind not to. Some women ARE weak, and some only know how to use weakness like a knife. Clara was both sorts by turns.

“Then the baby was born.”

Joe leaned forward and spoke more quietly.

“It wasn’t his.”

Mitchell stared.

“How did he know?”

“The child settled that. Red hair—bright as sealing-wax—and a mark beside the ear that ran in Redmond’s family. Clara confessed, too, when she was frightened and feverish. She’d known all along, or near enough. Redmond had cleared out after losing money, and Tom was the safer man. She knew his conscience better than he did.

“What did Tom do?”

“What you or I mightn’t have done. He stayed.”

“Stayed!”

“The woman was ill, and the baby hadn’t cheated him. He said neither of ’em could be turned into the street. Perhaps he told himself he’d leave when she recovered. Then the child got sick. Then work was slack. Then there was rent owing. There’s always a next reason when a man’s tied by pity.

“He grew fond of the youngster, too. A baby don’t inquire into legal ownership before it grips a finger. Tom nursed it, walked the floor with it, bought milk when he went without meat, and sat up nights while Clara slept. I reckon that child had more fathering from Tom Marshall in one year than Redmond could have given in fifty.

“For a while Clara behaved. Then Redmond came back.”

Joe at last got his pipe alight and drew on it savagely.

“He was a big, noisy brute, Redmond—one of them men who mistake other people’s fear for their own courage. He wore a red beard, flash boots and a horsey waistcoat, and was always talking about men he’d knocked out. Jimmy Nowlett knew him. Jimmy was a little nugget of a chap, hard as cast iron and twice as sudden. Redmond once called him a cockroach in Duigan’s shanty, and Jimmy went at him with a pewter pot, a chair-leg, and afterwards, when they’d taken them away, a pickle bottle. They had to roll Jimmy in a blanket to stop him. That’ll give you an idea of Redmond’s manners—and Jimmy’s.”

Mitchell laughed.

“Well, Redmond found Clara. Tom suspected nothing at first. He was working long hours, and she told him Redmond had only called about money. Then neighbours began looking away too quick when Tom passed. A man hears most bad news last when it concerns his own house.

“One evening he came home early and found Redmond there.

“There wasn’t much talk. Clara screamed and put the child behind her. Redmond laughed. He had his coat off and seemed comfortable.

“Tom said, ‘Get out.’

“Redmond said, ‘This is more my home than yours, by rights.’

“Tom said, ‘Get out before I shift you.’

“Redmond stood up. He was heavier than Tom and fancied himself with his hands. He said something filthy about Bertha, too—Clara must have told him—and that finished the talking.

“Tom hit him.”

Joe’s own shoulders moved as though the fight were before him.

“He hit him straight and hard and drove him across the table. Redmond came back swinging. They smashed a chair, knocked the lamp down, went through the bedroom partition, and landed in the passage among the plaster. Clara ran outside yelling murder, which fetched half the street.

“Redmond tried wrestling. Tom threw him against the door. Redmond bit his cheek. Tom pulled him away and struck him three times—left, right, left—so quick the chaps outside said it sounded like one blow. Then Redmond got a poker.

“Jimmy Nowlett arrived just then and shouted, ‘Give me a go at the red cow!’ Nobody let him.

“Tom took the poker on his arm, went in under it, and drove Redmond through the front doorway. They fought in the yard, then over the fence, and then in a patch of mud beside the lane. Redmond was roaring and cursing. Tom never said a word. That frightened the crowd more than Redmond’s noise.

“At last Tom caught him by the waist, lifted him—and Tom was terrible strong in those days—and threw him flat on the road. Then he knelt on him and said, quite calm, ‘If you come near her again while she is my wife, I’ll kill you.’

“Redmond believed him. Everybody did.

“Jimmy Nowlett danced round asking whether he might have what was left. When Redmond crawled off, Jimmy followed for half a street, throwing bits of blue metal and calling him endearing names.

“But Tom went back into the house. Clara was crouched with the child in her arms. He washed the blood off his face, wrapped up his arm, and packed a small swag.

“She asked what she was to do.

“Tom said, ‘Go with Redmond, if he’ll keep you. I’m done.’

“She began crying and tried to catch hold of him. Perhaps she was sorry then. Perhaps she was only scared. A person may be sincere for five minutes and false for the rest of the year. Anyway, Tom left.”

“And the child?”

“He sent money.”

“After all that?”

“Regular. Through a solicitor, so she couldn’t write back. When work was good he sent more. When work was bad he went short. The child wasn’t to blame—that was all he’d say.”

Mitchell poked a coal into the fire.

“What about Bertha? Why didn’t he go back?”

“Because men can be fools in more honourable ways than one. He reckoned he’d spoilt her life enough. He thought she’d marry somebody steady. Also, I fancy, he was ashamed—not of doing right, but of having been deceived. A man’ll show you a wound sooner than he’ll show you where he’s been made ridiculous.

“He wrote once to Uncle Bob saying the marriage had failed and that he was moving north. He didn’t explain. Uncle Bob, being a man, supposed Tom wanted silence. Aunt Bob, being a woman, guessed there was more and told Bertha.

“Bertha wrote to him. No answer.

“She wrote again. He answered that she must forget him.

“She wrote a third time, and the letter followed him from camp to camp until it came back stained and creased. By then Tom had gone prospecting inland.

“Years slipped. Bertha didn’t marry. There were men willing enough—a storekeeper, a selector, and a widower with two children—but she said no without giving reasons. People talked, of course. Country people’ll leave a gate open all winter but never a private matter. Some said Tom had deserted her; some said she’d jilted him; some invented a secret marriage, a murder, and a fortune in England. Bertha kept quiet.

“Then Redmond got killed in a drunken fall from a horse, and Clara, perhaps from fear of judgment or one of them sudden fits of truth that come over the worst people, wrote to Aunt Bob. She told the whole business. She said Tom had known the child wasn’t his and had supported them nevertheless.

“Aunt Bob showed Bertha.

“Bertha wrote again, and this time she said she would come to him if he would not come to her. There was no answer. Tom was working near Sydney then, though she didn’t know exactly where. So she took what money she had and travelled up.”

“Alone?”

“Alone. She wasn’t the helpless sort. But she’d been coughing before she left, and the journey finished what the illness had started. She searched boarding-houses, yards and camps in cold rain. She went to the address on an old letter and found Tom had shifted. Then she took a room near the wharves and fell sick.

“A landlady found Aunt Bob’s address among her things and telegraphed. Aunt Bob wired to every place where Tom might be. Uncle Bob knew a carrier who knew Jimmy Nowlett, and Jimmy knew a chap at the timber yard. Bush messages travel crooked, but sometimes they travel fast.

“Tom was at a camp west of the city when word reached him. He rode through the night, changed horses once, and got there in the morning.”

Joe’s pipe had gone out again. Neither man noticed.

“She was near finished. The doctor had said so. Tom came into the room with mud to his knees and his face grey from riding. For a second she didn’t know him. Then she smiled.

“He dropped beside the bed and tried to speak, but no words came. The Oracle can talk by the hour about nothing, Mitchell, but at the big places in his life he’s always been struck dumb.

“Bertha put her arms round his neck. She said, ‘You came, Tom.’

“That was all she wanted.

“She held him close, as if they’d only been parted since yesterday and there was no need to explain anything. Then she died in his arms.”

The coals settled with a soft fall, and somewhere down by the creek a horse shook its bridle.

Mitchell cleared his throat.

“And that made him like he is?”

“No one thing makes a man. It’s the weight added ounce by ounce—the woman he couldn’t save, the woman he gave up, the child that wasn’t his, the years wasted by other people’s lies, and his own blanky conscience driving him where an easier man wouldn’t go. Put enough behind a man’s eyes and he’ll stare across a clearing at things you can’t see.

“So when he goes deaf, let him. When he walks to and fro, don’t laugh. When an old tune catches him, play something else—or play it through properly. And don’t call him soft. He thrashed Redmond till Redmond couldn’t crawl straight, but that wasn’t his strength. Any bullocky can fight when he’s mad. Tom’s strength was staying with a sick woman who’d cheated him, feeding another man’s child, and leaving the woman he loved because he thought duty pointed the other way.”

Joe glanced beyond Mitchell.

“Here he comes. Shift the billy closer.”

The Oracle entered the firelight with his hands behind him and his head bent. He looked at the two men, then at the tea.

“Water boiled?” he asked.

“Just on,” said Joe.

The Oracle sat down between them, humming under his breath the first few notes of an old German air.

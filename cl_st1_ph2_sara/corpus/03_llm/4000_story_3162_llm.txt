## The Covenant of the Valley

Whether madness be indeed the eclipse of Reason, or only that excessive illumination by which the common shapes of things are lost in intolerable glory, I have never dared determine. Ye who possess calm hearts, and whose lives flow between familiar banks, may call me visionary; yet there are truths which enter not by the gates of sense, and memories more substantial than marble, though they leave upon the earth neither shadow nor footprint. Judge me, therefore, as ye will. I ask neither pardon nor belief. I relate only what befell me in the two epochs of my existence—the first clear as a star reflected in deep water, the second confused by the winds of Humanity.

My earliest years were passed in a region which no geographer hath traced. It was an enclosed and secret domain, named by those few who dwelt there the Valley of the Many-Colored Grass. A ring of mountainous crags shut it from the world; their summits leaned inward like gigantic wardens, and no path crossed them save one narrow fissure through which, at certain hours, the western sun flung a spear of amber light. At the valley’s heart wandered the River of Silence, so called because its waters, though rapid, uttered no murmur against their banks. It seemed less a river than a long mirror moving mysteriously beneath the sky.

There I lived with my aunt and with her daughter Eleonora, who was my cousin in the flesh, but in Spirit—ah! what language shall contain that affinity?—my companion before memory, my sister in solitude, and afterward the sovereign of my soul.

Our dwelling stood among trees whose trunks were dark as polished ebony and whose leaves shone silver beneath the moon. Around it sprang flowers of prodigious hue: saffron lilies marked with purple veins; asphodels redder than rubies; pale bells holding drops of perfume like imprisoned tears; and low, starry blossoms which opened only when Eleonora sang. Birds were few in those first days, and the silence was deep, but it was not desolate. The valley appeared to sleep, awaiting some command of Love.

We knew nothing of cities, of courts, of merchandise, war, ambition, or the deceitful commerce of men. Books were our only ambassadors from Humanity, and even these we read as one reads inscriptions upon tombs—wondering, remote, half incredulous. The heroes and sinners of history seemed to us creatures of a coarser element. We believed ourselves secure beyond Time.

Eleonora and I wandered from dawn until the evening star. We gathered flowers beside the River of Silence; we watched the crimson clouds burn themselves away behind the western gap; we lay together in the Many-Colored Grass and spoke of angels, of dreams, and of the possibility that each human soul had once been a note in the music of Heaven. She possessed that rare and mournful beauty which awakens reverence before desire. Her hair was brown with golden shadows; her brow had the serenity of moonlight; and her eyes—large, luminous, and darkly blue—seemed less organs of earthly sight than windows through which some exiled seraph looked homeward.

Years passed without announcement. Childhood departed as a spirit departeth—leaving the chamber unchanged, yet empty of its former presence. Then, in one enchanted season, Love arose between us.

I know not the hour of his coming. Perhaps he had always inhabited our hearts, silent and folded-winged; perhaps a glance awakened him; perhaps the valley itself breathed him into being. I remember only that one evening, while the sky was filled with clouds of crimson and gold, Eleonora bent above the river, and her face trembled in the moving glass. She turned toward me. Our eyes met; and the whole visible creation became altered.

Thenceforward Nature shared our passion. One by one, and then ten by ten, strange flowers awakened in the grass. Vast birds descended from unknown climates, their plumes green, scarlet, and imperial violet, and their voices filled the groves with liquid rejoicing. Golden fish appeared beneath the transparent river, and wherever they passed there ran a faint chiming, as of little silver bells heard across a dream. The trees put forth new leaves, paler than pearl by day and phosphorescent at night. From the southern cliffs flowed perfumes for which no blossom could be found.

Above all, a cloud of singular brightness gathered over the valley. It rested there continually—a gorgeous pavilion of rose, purple, and molten gold—and from its depths descended beams that trembled like the lances of celestial armies. Under that canopy we loved.

To say that I loved Eleonora would be poverty of speech. I worshipped her, yet not as idolaters worship dead matter; for she answered my devotion with a living tenderness so complete that our two spirits seemed but divided flames striving to reunite. Her breath was my atmosphere. Her happiness was my religion. When she smiled, Death himself appeared an incredible fable.

But Death is most vigilant where men deny him.

A change came first into her voice. Its melody remained, but beneath the melody trembled a distant weariness, like a sigh imprisoned under music. Then the delicate rose withdrew from her cheek. She no longer climbed with me to the high meadows, but rested beneath the silver trees, watching the River of Silence with an attention that filled me with terror. Physicians there were none; nor would the rude wisdom of physicians have availed against that invisible hand which had touched her.

At last she spoke of dying.

I rebuked the word as blasphemy; I laughed, I wept, I clasped her, I called upon Heaven to preserve its fairest work. Yet she smiled with an unearthly composure and laid her transparent fingers upon my brow.

“It is not Death that troubleth me,” she said. “For I shall not cease to love thee. But thou wilt remain among these flowers, and the years will grow heavy. Thou wilt cross the mountains. Thou wilt behold women of the world—women beautiful and wise—and thy heart, being human, may incline toward another.”

At this her calmness failed. Her eyes darkened with mortal anguish, and she hid her face against my breast. Then, possessed by grief and by the proud madness of fidelity, I uttered the covenant that hath haunted all my later days.

Before the river, before the mountains, before the bright cloud that seemed the visible throne of the Almighty, I vowed never to bind myself in marriage to any daughter of earth. I called upon the Spirit of Eleonora, if spirits retain knowledge after death, to witness my constancy. I invoked upon my soul a punishment nameless and eternal should I become recreant. Let my guardian angel abandon me; let every joy become corruption in my grasp; let sleep bring demons and waking bring remorse; let Heaven close against me—so I swore—if another should receive the love consecrated to Eleonora.

She listened; and a light, neither of health nor of this world, arose within her countenance.

“I am at peace,” she whispered. “I shall watch over thee. If it be permitted, I shall return in signs—in fragrance, in wind, in dreams. And when thy trial is ended, I shall make known that I have not forgotten.”

Not many days thereafter, while evening gathered violet among the trees, she died in my arms. Her departure was gentle. A sigh came from her lips; the river gave one low musical sound; and the great cloud above us was suddenly extinguished, as though a lamp had been covered by an unseen hand.

For a time grief made no change in the valley, because grief had made no change in me: I was not yet a man, but a stone retaining the shape of a man. I sat beside her grave beneath the ebony trees and awaited some fracture of the universe. None came. The sun rose with monstrous indifference. The river continued its noiseless pilgrimage.

Then, gradually, the valley began to mourn.

The radiant birds departed, first singly, then in flocks, until the groves were mute. The golden fish sank into unseen caverns. The ruby asphodels bowed, faded, and returned no more. The silver foliage darkened; the strange flowers closed their eyes; the perfumes diminished. Even the Many-Colored Grass surrendered hue after hue, becoming first green, then gray, then nearly white, like the hair of one aged by sorrow. The River of Silence grew black beneath the diminished heavens.

Yet Eleonora was not wholly absent.

At twilight, when no breeze stirred, a warm breath would pass over my cheek, laden with the fragrance that had belonged to her hair. From empty recesses among the hills came sighs too articulate for wind. Once I heard my name murmured from the middle of the river, though no creature stood upon either bank. Often, in the profoundest night, a pale brightness moved behind the curtains of my chamber; and once—but once—while I lay between sleep and waking, lips invisible pressed mine with a cold and solemn tenderness.

Was this madness? I cannot answer. If madness, it was sweeter than wisdom.

For many years I remained faithful to the valley and to the dead. But solitude, which at first is a sanctuary, becometh by slow degrees a prison-house. The mountains seemed to move inward. Every path ended at a grave. The silence accumulated until I feared that it would crush thought itself. My aunt had long since passed into her final sleep; servants departed; the house decayed. I alone endured, the melancholy sovereign of a kingdom without subjects.

Then there arose in me a desire for the world beyond the mountains—not a gentle curiosity, but a fever. I resisted it as temptation; I prayed; I flung myself upon Eleonora’s grave and renewed the awful vow. Nevertheless the fever increased. Voices seemed to call from beyond the cliffs: voices of multitudes, of trumpets, of women, of Life. At last I fled.

The city into which I entered appeared to me more marvellous than the fabled Bagdad of Haroun. Towers glittered with lamps; fountains leaped in courts of porphyry; chariots thundered along avenues populous as ant-hills; banners streamed above battlements; and through halls of gold moved nobles, soldiers, ambassadors, dancers, philosophers, flatterers, priests, and queens. After the colorless repose of the valley, this splendour struck my senses like strong wine. I became attached to the household of the king, who found entertainment in my strange learning and stranger silences.

Yet amid music and festival I remained alone. The memory of my covenant followed me. In every beautiful face I discerned danger; in every tender voice, accusation. I believed the curse waiting, patient as a crouching beast.

Then came Ermengarde.

She entered the court from a northern province during a winter festival. I saw her at the far end of a hall whose thousand candles were reflected in mirrors from floor to dome. Jewels burned upon every side; musicians breathed through silver instruments; dancers moved like bright phantoms across the marble. Yet when Ermengarde appeared, all else receded into unreality.

Her beauty was unlike Eleonora’s. Eleonora had been the soul of moonlight and enclosed gardens; Ermengarde was morning upon mountains, the splendor of banners in a pure wind, the first rose seen after a winter of many years. Her hair was black, but lights of blue wandered through it; her eyes were gray and fearless; her mouth seemed formed equally for command and compassion. She walked not as one conscious of admiration, but as one whom invisible powers attended.

Mine eyes met hers.

In that instant the dead valley, the solemn river, the grave beneath the ebony trees, and even the vow pronounced beneath Heaven arose before me. I trembled. I turned away. I fled from the hall as though the curse already pursued me.

But Love, who had once ruled me through innocence, now assailed me through despair. I avoided Ermengarde, and encountered her everywhere. Her name sounded in corridors before she came; her image visited sleep; her kindness, when at last she spoke to me, entered the desolate chambers of my heart and kindled lamps where I had believed darkness eternal.

I struggled long. I accused myself before God. I called myself false, perjured, unworthy of the dead. Yet the more I struggled, the more absolute became the new dominion. I did not love Eleonora less. This was the mystery that confounded me. Her memory remained pure, remote, and sacred; but beside it, not upon its ruins, grew my love for Ermengarde—a living tree beside a marble shrine.

At length I yielded.

We were married beneath the vaulted roof of the royal chapel. While the priest pronounced the blessing, I listened for thunder. When Ermengarde placed her hand in mine, I expected it to become cold in death. When I raised the veil from her face, I looked for the avenging countenance of Eleonora beyond her shoulder.

Nothing came.

The organ sounded; the candles burned tranquilly; the courtiers smiled. Day followed day, and no demon crossed our threshold. Yet peace did not immediately enter me. Each happiness sharpened dread. At midnight I would awaken and watch Ermengarde sleeping, fearful that dawn would reveal punishment written upon her brow.

One such night, when the palace lay silent and the moon made the tapestries resemble forests of frost, I rose and stood at the open window. Far below, the city slept beneath a mist. Then there stole into the chamber a fragrance impossible to mistake—the breath of those vanished flowers which had grown only in the Valley of the Many-Colored Grass.

Ermengarde did not stir.

The curtains lifted, though the air was still. A pale radiance gathered near me; not a form, not wholly a light, but something tender and conscious. My heart ceased its beating. From within that radiance—or from the depths of mine own spirit—came the voice of Eleonora.

It spoke once, and only once:

“Sleep peacefully. The Spirit of Love reigneth above the letter of thy covenant. Thou art absolved—for causes which shall be revealed to thee in Heaven.”

The light faded. The fragrance lingered until dawn.

Since that hour I have known tranquillity. Whether the voice descended from the blessed dead, or arose from some merciful chamber of my divided mind, I leave to those who delight in boundaries. I know only that Love did not betray Love; that Eleonora and Ermengarde dwell together within my soul without rivalry; and that, beyond the last mountain, beyond the last silence, all vows broken by tenderness may yet be fulfilled in God.

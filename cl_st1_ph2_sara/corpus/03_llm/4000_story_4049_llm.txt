Among the kingdoms of Christendom there were few in which the visible instruments of sovereignty possessed so awful an authority as in Hungary; for there the crown was not merely an ornament placed upon the head of a fortunate prince, but, in the imagination of the people and almost in the language of the law, a sacred personage in itself. It had belonged, according to ancient belief, to St. Stephen, the first Christian king of the Magyars; it had been blessed by Rome, guarded by prelates and magnates, and carried before armies as the sign of the nation’s unity. A claimant might have royal blood, the consent of powerful nobles, and a sword sufficient to enforce both; yet if he had not been crowned with that particular diadem, in the ancient city of Weissenburg, or Székesfehérvár, his title remained defective.

The Crown of St. Stephen was therefore enclosed within strong chests, behind locks, chains, seals, gates, and armed men, in the castle of Vissegrad, which rose above the Danube. Its bent cross, its plates of gold, and its solemn images of saints and emperors were familiar to few eyes; nevertheless, the humblest peasant knew that where the crown went, there went a formidable portion of lawful kingship.

King Albert of Hungary died in the year 1439, leaving his country exposed to enemies without and factions within. The Ottoman power pressed upon the southern frontier; the great lords distrusted one another; and Queen Elizabeth, daughter of the Emperor Sigismund and widow of Albert, had no living son. She was, however, with child.

The principal nobles, unwilling to entrust the defence of Hungary to the uncertain issue of a royal confinement, resolved to invite Wladislas, King of Poland, to accept both the Hungarian throne and the widowed queen’s hand. The proposal was not destitute of political wisdom. Wladislas was young, warlike, and able to bring Polish strength against the Turks. Elizabeth herself, they reasoned, would retain dignity as his consort, while the kingdom obtained a captain.

But political wisdom, as commonly understood by assemblies of men, appears very different when viewed by a mother whose unborn child may be deprived of an inheritance. Elizabeth listened, temporised, and considered. If she bore a daughter, the question might be settled as the nobles desired; if she bore a son, that infant would be, in her eyes, the true King of Hungary. Yet a baby in a cradle could scarcely contend against an armed prince unless he possessed the one weapon more potent than age, eloquence, or a Polish army—the holy crown.

Among the queen’s attendants was Helen Kottenner, a German gentlewoman of approved fidelity, married to a burgher of Oedenburg and accustomed to the domestic service of the royal household. She had no command of soldiers, no skill in statecraft, and no pretension to heroic enterprise. Her duties concerned chambers, garments, jewels, children, and the thousand unrecorded necessities by which courts, like cottages, are kept from disorder. It was for this reason, perhaps, that Elizabeth trusted her. A great lord might betray a conspiracy to increase his power; a faithful waiting-woman, having once given her heart, could be harder to purchase.

Helen afterwards wrote an account of these events, and it is from her plain testimony, with its prayers, alarms, and careful mention of cushions, keys, boats, and meals, that the adventure derives much of its authority. She does not represent herself as fearless. On the contrary, she was so much afraid that a modern physician might have found many learned names for her condition; but “nervous agitation” had not then been invented to excuse either obedience or treason, and Helen understood her distress in the more serious language of conscience.

The queen sent for her privately.

The crown, she said, must be taken from Vissegrad and brought to her before the Polish party could obtain it. Helen was to accomplish this.

The poor lady was confounded. To steal a kingdom’s most sacred treasure from a guarded fortress was a crime for which no indulgent judge was likely to prescribe a pilgrimage and a few prayers. If discovered, she might be tortured, imprisoned, or put to death; if she succeeded, civil war might follow. She feared for her husband and children, for her country, and, above all, for her soul.

Yet she also believed that her dead king’s possible son had a just claim upon her service. The queen commanded; fealty urged; maternal pity added its voice; and after much inward conflict Helen consented, though she prayed that Heaven would prevent the deed if it must bring ruin upon Hungary.

The first man approached as an accomplice was a Croat, reputed bold in ordinary affairs. He heard enough of the design to understand its danger, and then disappeared with such completeness that his discretion could not be distinguished from cowardice. A second helper proved of another temper. His name Helen, prudently or gratefully, withheld. He possessed courage, mechanical ingenuity, and that useful indifference to appearances which permits a man to carry files and iron tools hidden about his person without behaving as though every buckle proclaimed high treason.

In February 1440, Helen went to Vissegrad under a lawful pretext connected with the removal of the queen’s household goods. The castle was occupied by persons who knew her and had no reason to suspect that a waiting-woman, moving among chests and bedding, intended to alter the succession of a kingdom. Yet the chamber containing the crown was heavily secured; the doors were sealed, the locks strong, and the keys not in Helen’s possession. Worse still, ladies of the household slept near the place, guards passed through the passages, and every stroke of a hammer or rasp might awaken inquiry.

Night came. The castle grew quiet, though to those engaged in a forbidden enterprise no building is ever wholly silent. Beams crack, wind presses against casements, sleepers turn in their beds, and distant footsteps seem always to be approaching the guilty door.

Helen’s accomplice entered the inner room with his tools. She remained where she could watch and, if necessary, give warning. The outer fastenings were overcome; then came the sealed door, the iron bands, and the chest within. The man filed, forced, and broke. Each sound appeared to Helen loud enough to rouse the whole fortress. Once a movement outside caused them to stop, scarcely breathing. No one entered. The work began again.

A lock resisted with that peculiar obstinacy by which lifeless objects sometimes seem to take the side of authority. The accomplice laboured until sparks and fragments fell near the stove. Chains had to be divided; seals must be removed without rendering their violation immediately visible; and every obstacle conquered revealed another behind it. Helen prayed to the Virgin and listened for the guard.

At length the final chest was opened.

There, in the wavering light, lay the Crown of St. Stephen.

Even at such a moment the sight imposed reverence. They were not plunderers admiring gold. Before them was the object at which queens, bishops, generals, and ambassadors had looked with desire or fear; the sign under which the dead king had ruled and for which living men might soon shed blood. Helen took it with trembling hands.

The damaged locks and seals were arranged as nearly as possible in their former condition. This deception could not survive a close examination, but every hour was precious. One fragment of a broken fastening escaped notice and remained near the stove—a small witness capable, had it been observed too soon, of defeating the whole enterprise.

The crown was placed within a velvet cushion, which Helen sewed so carefully that no one examining the outside would imagine it contained anything more dangerous than feathers. She then returned among the sleeping household. The accomplice concealed his implements, though not, one imagines, with perfect comfort, and the two waited for morning.

When daylight came, Helen expected accusation in every greeting. Servants entered and departed; baggage was assembled; questions were asked concerning boxes and coverings. The cushion attracted no notice. The party left Vissegrad, and the crown of Hungary passed through its own guards in an article of household furniture.

For additional concealment the baggage was covered with an ox-hide, the tail of which had not been cut away. Thus the sacred regalia of St. Stephen travelled beneath a hide whose tail swung at the rear of the wagon—a circumstance which would have seemed undignified had the safety of a dynasty not depended upon it. History, being conducted by human beings, seldom maintains the splendour represented in paintings.

Their first halt brought little relief. Helen was exhausted, hungry, and tormented by the conviction that horsemen must already be following. The breakfast offered to her consisted chiefly of herrings. Under happier conditions these might have been acceptable; after a night spent committing high treason, they appeared a poor reward. She ate what she could, watched the doors, and continued her journey.

At one lodging the cushion was placed so near ordinary domestic objects that a spoon lay across it. Helen contemplated that spoon with almost superstitious uneasiness. A servant had only to move it, press the cushion, or remark upon its unusual weight, and beneath his hand he might feel the hard curve of a kingdom. But servants, happily, have their own concerns. The spoon was removed, the cushion remained unopened, and the travellers went on.

The Danube presented a more substantial danger. Winter was breaking; great pieces of ice moved upon the swollen water, striking one another and turning in the current. The passage was made in a boat ill-fitted to inspire confidence even in travellers burdened only with their lives. Helen carried, besides her life, the crown, and believed that if the vessel overturned she must answer not merely for drowning herself, but for casting Hungary’s sacred treasure into the river.

The boatmen watched the ice and pulled hard. Once the craft was forced aside; once a floe came so near that those aboard expected the thin planks to split. The horses and baggage shifted dangerously. Helen held the cushion and commended herself, the queen, and her country to God. At last the boat touched the farther bank. No pursuit appeared behind them; the cushion had not fallen into the Danube; and for the first time since leaving Vissegrad she permitted herself to believe the theft might succeed.

She reached Komorn—Komárom, as the Hungarians called it—on the very day when Queen Elizabeth was brought to bed. Before Helen could present the treasure, news came from the women’s chamber.

The child was a son.

He was named Ladislas, and his adherents immediately styled him Ladislas the Posthumous, because he had entered the world after his father’s death. The coincidence seemed to Elizabeth a declaration of Providence. While her son was being born, the crown necessary to make him king had crossed the winter roads and the breaking river toward him.

Helen opened the velvet cushion in the queen’s presence. Elizabeth received the diadem with emotion, gratitude, and triumph. Whether she thanked her attendant as warmly for having imperilled her soul as for having saved the inheritance cannot now be determined; sovereigns are apt to see the successful issue of a command more clearly than the anguish of the person who obeyed it. Yet Helen’s fidelity was never forgotten by her mistress.

There was little time for rejoicing. Wladislas of Poland advanced toward Hungary, supported by nobles who declared that the kingdom required a man, not a child twelve weeks old and a woman acting in his name. Elizabeth’s party replied that hereditary right and lawful coronation could not be set aside for convenience. Both arguments had force; and, as frequently happens when both sides possess some justice, swords were prepared to supply what reasoning could not settle.

The queen resolved that her infant must be crowned before her adversaries could gain possession of Weissenburg. The ceremony required not only St. Stephen’s Crown but the accustomed vestments. No royal tailor had prepared robes for a sovereign scarcely larger than a bundle of linen. Helen therefore became seamstress to a king. From rich materials she fashioned a tiny tunic, mantle, and other garments suited as nearly as possible to the coronation usage, reducing the magnificence of centuries to the dimensions of a baby.

The crown itself presented another difficulty. It must be carried through uncertain country where the queen’s baggage might be stopped and searched. A velvet cushion had served once, but suspicion was now abroad. Helen concealed the diadem beneath the mattress of Ladislas’s cradle.

Thus the little king lay above the evidence of his kingship, crying when cold, hungry, tired, or merely offended by the roughness of fifteenth-century travel. The queen’s company moved toward Weissenburg through bitter weather. Scouts reported enemies; strange riders were watched; every bridge and narrow road appeared suitable for an ambush. Helen remained near the cradle, knowing that a soldier who thrust his spear into the bedding might at once injure the child and strike the crown.

Storms delayed them. Wheels sank in mud where the frost had yielded, and hardened roads jarred the cradle so violently that Ladislas protested with all the power of his lungs. The crown pressed from below, blankets were arranged above, and anxious attendants attempted to soothe him without disturbing the hiding-place. There can have been few monarchs who travelled to their coronation in so uncomfortable a relation to their regalia.

Once alarm spread that an armed party was near. The baggage was drawn together, men reached for weapons, and the queen prepared for capture or flight. The riders proved harmless. On another occasion an officer questioned the escort and looked closely at the wagons. The infant began to scream at the most inconvenient moment. Perhaps the noise persuaded the officer that no political mystery could be concealed in a cradle so plainly occupied by a cross baby; at any rate, he allowed them to proceed.

Weissenburg remained loyal and received the queen. The city, ancient burial and coronation place of Hungarian kings, possessed that solemn authority which old capitals retain even after much of their ordinary power has passed elsewhere. The clergy prepared the church; magnates favourable to Elizabeth assembled; the regalia were brought forth; and on the appointed day, in May 1440, Ladislas, being about twelve weeks old, was carried to his coronation.

The rites devised for grown warriors did not fit the little creature with convenience. He was anointed according to custom and invested with garments which Helen had made. A sword was placed in hands unable to grasp it with purpose. Since a Hungarian king was expected to be a knight, a noble performed the ceremonial stroke with such vigour that Helen feared he might injure the sovereign whom the rite was intended to honour.

She held Ladislas during much of the service. Bishops chanted, incense rose, nobles stood in state, and the immense meaning of the crown was proclaimed in prayer and acclamation. The child, unimpressed by antiquity, music, politics, or maternal ambition, cried steadily. He had little pleasure in his coronation.

At last the Crown of St. Stephen was placed upon his head, though attendants were obliged to support its weight. The gold diadem which grown men coveted was too large and heavy for him; yet in the eyes of his party the act transformed his claim. He was no longer merely Albert’s posthumous son. He had been anointed at the proper place, by the proper authority, with the one true crown. Bells rang, voices shouted for King Ladislas, and the infant continued his lamentation as if giving the only candid judgment upon the burdens of monarchy.

The ceremony did not end the struggle. A crown can bestow legitimacy upon those who believe in its authority, but it cannot compel obedience from those who have already chosen another king. Wladislas entered Hungary and was himself crowned with another object in the absence of the sacred diadem; civil war and divided allegiance followed. Elizabeth, fearing for her son, carried him into Austria, where stronger hands might protect what Helen’s courage had preserved.

It would be easy, after the fashion of partisan chronicles, to declare the queen wholly right or wholly guilty. She defended her child’s inheritance, but she also placed private and dynastic interest against the judgment of many who feared for the kingdom. The theft secured a lawful symbol and helped to deepen a dangerous division. History seldom permits a crown to be stolen without sending the account, in blood or sorrow, to persons who had no share in the design.

Helen’s case is clearer. She did not devise the policy, summon armies, or seek advancement. She was a servant placed between obedience and terror, and she performed what she believed to be her duty with extraordinary resolution. Her courage consisted not in ignorance of danger, but in seeing each danger distinctly—the prison, the executioner, the river, the searching hand, the judgment of God—and continuing nevertheless.

The crown survived further wars, journeys, concealments, and dynastic revolutions; kingdoms changed, families perished, and the sacred diadem passed through adventures scarcely less remarkable than its escape from Vissegrad. Yet among all who guarded, wore, coveted, or carried it, few served it in a stranger fashion than Helen Kottenner, who broke into a royal vault by night, hid the sovereignty of Hungary in a cushion, bore it across the thawing Danube beneath an ox-hide, laid it under a crying child’s mattress, and at last held that child upright while bishops crowned him.

Her mistress’s political wisdom may still be questioned. Her own fidelity cannot.

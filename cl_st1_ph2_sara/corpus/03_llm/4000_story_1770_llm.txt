“Papa,” said Ruth, leaning both elbows upon his desk, “I want a story.”

Her father looked over a wall of letters, bills, correspondence cards, and packages tied with red string.

“My dear, I am perfectly occupied.”

“You have been perfectly occupied since breakfast.”

“That is how I became so perfect at it.”

“Papa!”

“Well, what?”

“A Christmas story. A new one.”

“It is June.”

“That will make it cooler.”

Her father laid down his pen. “Very well. Once there was a little girl—”

“Was she good?”

“Not inconveniently good. Her name was—”

“Ruth?”

“Certainly not. Her name was Euphemia.”

“I sha’n’t listen to anyone called Euphemia.”

“Then her name was Polly, and she lived in the United States and Canada.”

“How could she?”

“She had one foot on each side of the boundary.”

“Papa!”

“Well, then, she lived in a town not far from here, in a white house with green blinds, a furnace that smoked, and a front door which swelled in damp weather. And Polly loved Christmas more than anything in the world.”

“More than her mother?”

“No comparisons, please. They cause trouble in families. She loved her mother every day, but she loved Christmas with bells, raisins, tissue paper, candles, oranges, drums, dolls, sleigh rides, plum pudding, and a great many things that must be paid for by other people.”

Ruth settled comfortably against the desk. “Go on.”

Polly began expecting Christmas on the Fourth of July. In August she made lists. In September she revised them. In October she added footnotes. By November she had prepared a catalogue seven feet long, naming a doll with real hair, a doll’s carriage, skates, books, paints, a music box, a gold bracelet, a pony, a camera, a writing desk with her monogram upon it, and a white bear from the North Pole.

“A live one?” asked Ruth.

“Stuffed.”

“That is cruel.”

“Then it was alive.”

“That would eat her.”

“Then it was a nice bearskin rug which had died of old age and left a letter requesting to be put beside her bed.”

“That will do.”

When Christmas came, Polly sprang up before daylight and found her stocking so full that it had stretched from the mantel to the floor. There were oranges and nuts and chocolates in it, and a silver thimble she did not want, and a handkerchief embroidered by an aunt, and a small book about improving children, which she put under the oranges.

Downstairs stood a tree sparkling with glass balls, sugar birds, gilt walnuts, candles, chains, angels, and parcels enough to occupy two expressmen and a horse. There were presents at breakfast and presents after breakfast, and presents sent by post, and presents brought by cousins, and a mysterious parcel which nobody remembered buying and everybody wished somebody else would claim.

At dinner there were turkey, stuffing, gravy, potatoes, sweet potatoes, celery, cranberries, three kinds of pie, pudding, nuts, raisins, candy, and ice cream molded like Santa Claus. Polly ate until she could not look pleasantly at a raisin.

In the evening they sang songs, pulled crackers, wore paper crowns, upset a lamp, quarreled mildly over a game, and went to bed dreadfully tired.

“It sounds first-rate,” said Ruth.

“It was. Polly lay awake wishing that Christmas came every day.”

“That was foolish.”

“You know it was foolish because I have got only as far as the wish. She did not.”

Just then a pale green light shone at the foot of Polly’s bed, and in it appeared the Christmas Fairy, wearing a frock trimmed with holly and a cap topped by a silver star. She carried a wand resembling a peppermint stick.

“Polly,” said the Fairy, “I have heard your wish.”

“Do fairies listen at windows?” Ruth interrupted.

“Only when windows are insured. This fairy may have heard through the chimney.”

“That is better.”

“Would you truly like Christmas every day?” asked the Fairy.

“Oh, yes!” cried Polly. “Every single day for a whole year.”

“Consider,” said the Fairy.

“I have considered.”

“For how long?”

“Nearly a minute.”

“That is not much considering.”

“It is for a wish,” said Polly.

The Fairy sighed. “Very well. From tomorrow until a year from tomorrow, every day shall be Christmas. But you must not complain.”

“I never complain,” said Polly, which was such a remarkable statement that the clock stopped for two seconds.

The next morning Polly awakened to bells, stockings, parcels, oranges, drums, dolls, and another book about improving children. The tree had appeared fresh and glittering. Her mother looked surprised, her father looked alarmed, and the cook looked cross.

“Christmas again?” everyone cried.

Polly clapped her hands.

It was a magnificent day. They had another enormous dinner, more cousins came, more presents arrived, and the postman brought seventeen cards showing robins standing in snow, though no robin with ordinary judgment would have stood there.

On the third morning it happened again.

On the fourth, again.

By the seventh Christmas Polly possessed nine dolls, because two relatives had sent duplicates; five sleds, though there was no snow; four workboxes; eleven storybooks; three pairs of skates; a trumpet; a toy stove; six boxes of candy; a china dog; a live canary; and a machine for making a noise like a cow.

“Why would anyone want that?” asked Ruth.

“To remind them how quiet the trumpet was.”

At first the grown people tried to keep cheerful. They said “Merry Christmas!” heartily every morning. Then they said it faintly. Then they merely nodded. By the twentieth Christmas, half the town had lost its voice from wishing the other half a merry Christmas, and the other half had lost its temper from hearing it.

Business suffered dreadfully. Shops were always closing for the holiday and opening to sell holiday goods and closing again. Clerks tied ribbons day and night. The telegraph office sent nothing but “BEST WISHES STOP SAME TO YOU STOP PLEASE DO NOT SEND PRESENT STOP.” Express wagons blocked the streets. Postmen wore out their shoes, their satchels, and finally their dispositions.

Polly’s house filled from cellar to attic. Dolls sat on the stairs and cried “Mama” whenever stepped upon. Drums occupied the bathtub. Books were stacked in the oven, after the cook swore she would never bake another fruit cake. Mechanical bears walked out of cupboards at inconvenient moments. A rocking horse stood on the dining table because there was no other vacant place, and the family ate beneath it with the horse’s tail in the gravy.

“Couldn’t they give the things to poor children?” asked Ruth.

“They did. The poor children’s houses became full too. Then the poor-houses became so rich in presents that they had to build poor-houses for the poor-houses.”

Ruth considered this and permitted him to continue.

By February, people had eaten so much turkey that the sight of a feather made them ill. Turkeys grew scarce and cost forty dollars apiece. Cranberries were sold in jewel boxes, one berry to a box, with a certificate of genuineness. A pound of sugar cost more than a piano, and a piano cost less than a pound of candy, because everybody had received pianos and nobody had any room for them.

On Valentine’s Day the children hoped for red hearts and lace envelopes. But the postman brought Christmas cards, the church bells rang Christmas hymns, and everybody received stockings.

“That isn’t fair,” said Ruth.

“Fairies are not always fair. That is why the word confuses them.”

Polly herself had grown tired of opening parcels. At first she had torn off the paper with shrieks. Now she read the labels and groaned.

“Another doll!”

“Another game!”

“Another silver-backed brush!”

Her initials were P. M., and so many monogrammed objects came marked P. M. that her room looked like a postmaster’s private warehouse.

She tried leaving her stocking down. It filled on the floor. She hid it in a drawer. The drawer burst. She threw it away, but it returned before dawn with two oranges, a whistle, and another improving book.

Then Polly remembered the Fairy.

That night she watched beside the chimney until the green light appeared.

“Please,” Polly said, “I should like to beg off.”

No answer came.

“Christmas Fairy! I have had quite enough Christmas.”

The green light proved to be the reflection of a street lamp on a pickle bottle.

“Papa,” said Ruth, “that is mean.”

“It was mean of the pickle bottle. But it had never been properly brought up.”

March came, and still it was Christmas. Coal was almost gone because every fireplace had to blaze festively. People burned old chairs, packing cases, Christmas cards, and eventually the improving books. The price of firewood rose so high that a man who owned three sticks was invited to join the leading club.

Worse still, every day required a Christmas tree.

At first the hills around the town had been dark with firs. Then whole slopes stood bare. Axemen went farther and farther, railroads carried trees from northern forests, ships brought them from distant coasts, and still every parlor demanded one fresh tree every morning. After a while there were scarcely any evergreens left in the country except two in a cemetery and one artificial tree in a shop window, which was guarded by policemen.

Birds flew about holding meetings and complaining that their houses had been turned into parlor ornaments. Squirrels sent a delegation to Washington. The government appointed a committee, which wrote a report of nine hundred pages and recommended waiting until after Christmas.

“But it was always Christmas,” said Ruth.

“Exactly. That is what made the committee so successful.”

In April, spring flowers came up, but they were immediately tied into wreaths. In May, picnics became Christmas dinners on the grass. Everyone had to carry turkey, pudding, plates, parcels, a tree, and a fireplace. Nobody enjoyed the country because the expressmen arrived before them.

By June the heat was terrific. Santa Claus came down chimneys in a linen suit, with a palm-leaf fan. Reindeer refused to work before sunset. Chocolate Santas melted in shop windows and ran into the gutters, where small boys followed them with spoons.

Polly was perfectly miserable. She no longer liked dolls or drums or candy. She longed for plain bread, a quiet morning, and a package containing nothing.

“Nothing isn’t a present,” said Ruth.

“It was the most expensive present obtainable that year. Empty boxes sold for five dollars, and boxes with things in them were given away.”

Polly wrote to the Fairy on correspondence cards:

DEAR FAIRY, PLEASE STOP CHRISTMAS. I MEAN IT.

She posted one up the chimney, sent one by express, one by registered mail, one by telegram, and one tied to a balloon. No reply came. The balloon card landed in the yard of a senator, who introduced a bill against it.

Then came the Fourth of July. The children had saved firecrackers, rockets, torpedoes, pinwheels, and Roman candles. At sunrise they hurried into the streets. But when they opened the boxes, every firecracker had turned into a stick of candy, every rocket into a striped sugar cane, every torpedo into a chocolate cream, and every Roman candle into an ordinary candle for an evergreen tree.

There were, according to the newspapers, four million six hundred and three crying boys that morning, besides girls who were too angry to be counted.

“Girls like fireworks too,” Ruth said severely.

“Of course. The newspaper printed a correction the next day, but it was wrapped around a fruit cake and nobody saw it.”

By August the country was nearly bankrupt. Fathers had spent all their money on toys, mothers had worn their fingers out writing cards, and shopkeepers had become so rich that money was no use to them. Factories made nothing but drums, dolls, ribbon, candles, and small silver articles whose purpose nobody understood.

Nobody repaired roofs or shoes. Nobody planted enough wheat. Farmers had been too busy raising turkeys, and turkeys had become so nervous about Christmas that they laid no eggs and hid whenever anyone hummed a carol.

Then people began asking how the trouble had started.

A committee questioned astrologers, professors, grocers, chimney sweeps, and a lady who understood fairies because she had once written a poem about one. At last a small boy remembered hearing Polly wish for Christmas every day.

The news ran through town, over telephone wires and newspaper presses and postals:

POLLY DID IT.

After that, people were cross to her. Children would not let her join their games. Shopkeepers hid when she entered. The postman left her parcels at the gate and ran. Even her cousins said, “There goes the girl who spoiled the year.”

“That was horrid of them,” said Ruth.

“It was. People are often most severe with the person whose foolish wish resembles one they might have made themselves.”

Polly tried to explain.

“I only thought it would be lovely.”

“So did we, for three days,” they answered.

“I asked the Fairy to stop.”

“Ask louder.”

So Polly stood beneath the stars night after night and called until she was hoarse. She went into the woods, though there were scarcely any woods left. She searched beneath holly bushes, though all the holly had been used for decoration. She left bowls of milk, saucers of sugar, and polite notes.

The milk turned into eggnog, the sugar into candy, and the notes came back stamped ADDRESS UNKNOWN.

Thanksgiving arrived at last. Everyone hoped desperately for one sensible, quiet holiday. They planned to eat a modest dinner and give thanks that it was not—at least by the calendar—Christmas.

But at dawn the bells rang, trees appeared, stockings bulged, carolers sang, and turkeys doubled in price before breakfast.

The people rebelled. They would not roast a turkey. They ate beans. They would not open gifts. They sat upon them. They would not say “Merry Christmas.” They said “Good morning” in grim voices.

At noon the unopened parcels burst by themselves, covering the town with tissue paper, ribbon, dolls, skates, nutcrackers, neckties, albums, slippers, perfume, puzzles, and twelve thousand silver thimbles.

“Why so many thimbles?” asked Ruth.

“Nobody ever knows what to give an aunt.”

December came again. The last days of the enchanted year crept by. Christmas Eve was particularly confusing, because it was Christmas already and also the evening before Christmas. People hung two stockings, put up two trees, ate two dinners, and became twice as cross.

Polly did not sleep. She sat at her window and watched the clock.

At midnight a pale green light entered the room. The Christmas Fairy stood beside her, looking just as fresh as she had a year before, which Polly thought inconsiderate.

“Well?” said the Fairy.

“Well!” cried Polly. “Where have you been?”

“Attending to your wish.”

“I called you hundreds of times.”

“A year was a year. You were quite definite.”

“Then stop it now! Please make Christmas never come again.”

The Fairy raised her peppermint wand. “Never?”

“Never, never, never!”

“That sounds greedy.”

“Greedy? I don’t want anything.”

“You want all the future arranged to suit your present temper. That is another kind of greed.”

Polly was silent.

Outside, people were throwing unwanted candy into wagons. Bells had begun to ring, and somebody was shouting that the final Christmas tree had fallen through a roof.

“What ought I to wish?” Polly asked.

“I do not advise,” said the Fairy. “Advice is wasted almost as often as Christmas presents.”

Polly thought longer than a minute. She thought of the first Christmas, when the candles had been beautiful because they were not always burning, and the candy sweet because breakfast was usually oatmeal, and the presents delightful because they were surprises instead of a daily duty. She thought of spring picnics, Fourth of July fireworks, Thanksgiving dinner, ordinary mornings, and trees standing where birds could use them.

“At last,” she said, “I wish Christmas would come once a year—only once—at its proper time.”

“Just as it used to?”

“Yes, please.”

“That,” said the Fairy, “is an extremely unoriginal wish.”

“But is it a good one?”

“It has done very well for a great many centuries.”

She touched the clock with her wand and vanished.

The next morning was not Christmas.

Nobody had ever been so pleased with an ordinary day. People ran into the streets shouting, “It’s over!” They rang no bells, sang no songs, and wished one another a happy Wednesday.

Great bonfires were built from broken toys and correspondence cards. Candy was shoveled out of houses and buried in old gravel pits. Drums were flattened, trumpets silenced, and several thousand improving books were sent to the committee in Washington. The remaining presents were packed into warehouses, where they lasted until the grandchildren of the original receivers were old enough not to want them.

Forests slowly grew again. Turkeys came out of hiding. Cranberries ceased to be worn as jewelry. The price of sugar fell, and empty boxes became worthless.

When Christmas returned at the end of the next year, everybody welcomed it. There was one tree in each house, one dinner, one stocking apiece, and only as many presents as people could enjoy and expressmen could carry without weeping.

Polly received a book, a sled, and a small box of chocolates.

“Was it an improving book?” asked Ruth.

“No. She had improved.”

“And did she see the Fairy again?”

“Perhaps. Fairies are careful about appearing after they have proved a point.”

Her father picked up his pen.

“Is that all?” Ruth asked.

“That is all. It is just a moral tale.”

“What is the moral?”

“That people should be careful what they wish for; that holidays require weekdays; that too much pleasure becomes hard work; that forests are better standing up; that committees are useless during enchantments; and that no child should ask her busy father for a story in June.”

“The last one isn’t a proper moral.”

“It is the best one.”

“Papa!”

“Well, what?”

“Did Polly truly live near here?”

“Very near.”

“And did all those things truly happen?”

“Every one of them, except possibly the senator.”

Ruth looked solemnly toward the fireplace, though it was empty and clean and there was no green light anywhere.

“I won’t tell,” she whispered.

“Tell what?”

“About Polly, and the Fairy, and the cranberries costing diamonds, and the poor-houses for poor-houses. Those details shall be our secret.”

“Excellent,” said her father, returning to his letters. “Especially from the newspapers.”

Ruth went to the door, then turned.

“Papa?”

“Well, what?”

“When Christmas does come, may I have a pony?”

Her father dipped his pen.

“Once a year,” he said, “is quite often enough to ask.”

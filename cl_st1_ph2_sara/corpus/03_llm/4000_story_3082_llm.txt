I

There were three of them in the back room of Ryder’s office above a chandlery on California Street: Hardenberg, who had commanded ships in every ocean where ships were not wanted; Strokher, an Englishman of solemn countenance and no discoverable occupation; and Ally Bazan, a little, lean, combustible colonial, whose pockets were as empty as his conversation was full.

In the water-front saloons, where reputations are made from rum and lost through accuracy, these three were known as the Black Crows. The name had nothing to do with the colour of their clothes, which varied, nor with their morals, which did not. It arose from their habit of appearing wherever there was carrion—or, to speak commercially, an opportunity.

Ryder sat behind a desk broad enough to suggest solvency. He was bearded like a minor prophet, wrinkled like an orang, and buttoned into a frock coat whose blackness advertised respectability as a pirate flag advertises piracy.

“Gentlemen,” he began.

“Hold on,” said Hardenberg. “Before you call us gentlemen, what’s the percentage?”

Ryder opened his hands.

“You are practical.”

“I’m broke.”

“I put it to you,” observed Strokher, crossing one knee over the other, “that the terms are the essence of the undertaking.”

“Terms!” cried Ally Bazan. “I s’y, let’s ’ave the bloomin’ terms. If there’s cold weather in it, I want double.”

“There is,” Ryder admitted, “a certain amount of latitude involved.”

“How much latitude?” asked Hardenberg.

“Point Barrow.”

Ally’s chair came down on all four legs.

“Point Barrow! ’Ere, what d’you tyke me for—a walrus?”

Ryder smiled patiently. He had the promoter’s smile, which acknowledges every objection and answers none.

“Near Point Barrow,” he corrected. “There is a Russian trading post on the coast. Six men, perhaps seven. They have accumulated sea-otter pelts.”

“How many?” Hardenberg asked.

“Forty, perhaps fifty.”

There was a silence.

Sea-otter was not fur in the ordinary sense. It was portable wealth, folded velvet, black money with silver tips. A first-rate skin could be sold in San Francisco for a sum at which a bank clerk would remove his hat. Forty of them made virtue look provincial.

Strokher slowly uncrossed his legs.

“I put it to you,” he said, “that the Russians may object.”

“They must not know.”

“Ah,” said Hardenberg. “So we ain’t buying.”

“Certainly you are buying. The Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company will furnish invoices, permits, bills of sale, transfer certificates and consular attestations.”

“Beautiful,” said Hardenberg. “Will it furnish money?”

Ryder looked pained.

“My dear captain, if the company had ready money, would it require men of initiative?”

“No. It would require accountants.”

“The documents,” Ryder continued, “will be beautifully precise.”

“Which means fake,” said Hardenberg.

“Which means sufficient.”

“And the Russians?”

Ryder leaned across the desk.

“You will put in under the pretence of searching for survivors from a wrecked whaler. While there, you will establish friendly relations. I am providing six cases of champagne.”

Hardenberg stared at him. Strokher’s eyebrows rose. Ally Bazan uttered a small, appreciative whistle.

“Champagne,” Ryder repeated, “of an excellent appearance.”

“Appearance?” said Hardenberg.

“The bottles, labels, corks, wire and foil are of the first quality.”

“And what’s inside?”

“A sound California white wine, fortified with neutral spirits and carbonated.”

“Counterfeit champagne.”

“Commercial champagne.”

“Poison,” said Hardenberg.

“Hospitality.”

It was thus that the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company entered the Arctic fur trade: with forged papers, fraudulent wine, no capital and three men whose principal qualification was an elastic interpretation of property.

There remained the schooner.

She lay at Oakland Creek, a hundred-and-ten-ton vessel called the Petrel, though nothing in her build suggested that light-footed bird. She was broad in the waist, blunt in the bow, patched in the topsides and redolent of tar, bilge, onions, damp sailcloth and prior bankruptcy. Her papers were recent; her timbers were not. She had once carried lumber, twice gone ashore, and for six months served as collateral for a debt which nobody could collect. Ryder now described her as “specially adapted for northern service.”

“What’s she ballasted with?” Hardenberg demanded.

“Stone.”

“Good,” said Hardenberg. “Stone’s cheap by the ton.”

“The company’s agent will sail with you,” Ryder added.

“The company’s what?”

“Agent. Mr. Nickerson.”

The door opened, and Nickerson entered.

He was a vast man, six feet three in his stockings and apparently broader than the doorway. His beard was red, his fists knotted, his nose flattened, his left ear divided by an ancient knife cut. He wore sea-boots, a blue jersey and a leather belt containing a clasp knife, a belaying pin and—through some private whim—a Methodist hymn-book with the covers torn off.

“That him?” said Hardenberg.

“That’s me,” Nickerson roared in a voice like a steam siren. “And if any son of a gun doubts it, I’ll drive his head through the bulkhead!”

Ryder nodded, as though this completed the introduction.

“Mr. Nickerson is familiar with the north.”

“Are you?” Hardenberg asked.

Nickerson struck himself on the chest.

“Three seasons in the ice, two winters in Nome, six months sealin’, four months whalin’, and once drunk from Unalaska to Dutch Harbor without drawin’ a sober breath.”

“A useful man,” Ryder remarked.

“Where’d you get him?”

“That is a peculiar story. Five years ago he was found unconscious beside a railway embankment in Oregon. He had no memory of his name, occupation or connections. Since then he has called himself Nickerson.”

“Why?”

“He saw it on a packing case.”

“It was a good case,” said Nickerson. “Held whisky.”

Later, as the four adventurers—or, rather, the three adventurers and Nickerson—went down the narrow stairs, Hardenberg detained Ryder.

“Shares,” he said.

“Company takes half after expenses.”

“The company takes a quarter.”

“Forty per cent.”

“A third.”

“Agreed.”

“Crew gets the rest?”

“You four divide it.”

Hardenberg buttoned his coat.

“And if the proposition busts?”

Ryder spread his hands with the serene gesture of a financier distributing misfortune.

“Then, my dear captain, you will have had the voyage.”

II

They sailed on a gray morning in May.

The Petrel passed through the Golden Gate under shortened canvas, dragging her patched hull into the long Pacific swell. San Francisco diminished behind them—a white confusion upon its hills, noble at a distance, expensive at close range—and the four watched it go with different emotions. Strokher smoked. Ally Bazan waved his cap at no one in particular. Nickerson spat overside. Hardenberg, at the wheel, estimated how long the main topmast would last.

The crew consisted of seven men besides themselves: a Finnish mate, two Norwegians, a half-breed cook, an elderly sailmaker and two beach-combers taken aboard while drunk and retained because they awoke at sea. They were paid partly in advance, partly in promises, and wholly in ignorance.

In the hold were salt beef, flour, beans, kerosene, ammunition, spare cordage, six cases of the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company’s champagne, and documents of such beautifully precise illegality that Hardenberg preferred not to read them.

For eleven days the wind served. The Petrel wallowed northward, lifting her clumsy bows over green miles of water and falling into the hollows with a concussion that started dishes from the racks and profanity from the forecastle. After that the weather hardened.

The Pacific, amiable only upon maps, came down upon them from the northwest. It buffeted and mauled and man-handled the schooner; it slapped her broadside, filled her waist, tore at her jibs, found every seam in her decks and every defect in her crew. The wind screamed in the rigging like wire dragged over glass. The Petrel, unable to run and unwilling to sink, continued.

For six days they lived in oilskins.

They ate standing; slept wet; swore without enthusiasm. Ally Bazan was flung from his bunk so regularly that he took to sleeping on the floor.

“I s’y,” he complained, “this ’ere ship don’t sail. She falls in sections.”

“She’s making northing,” Hardenberg told him.

“She’s myking kindling.”

Strokher appeared at meals with his beard wet and his manner unchanged.

“I put it to you,” he said over a plate of beans which migrated from one side of the table to the other, “that our host Ryder has remained in San Francisco by design.”

“You just discovered that?” said Hardenberg.

“I have suspected it from the first.”

“Suspect faster.”

Nickerson alone flourished. He went aloft in weather that kept the Norwegians on deck, bellowed chanteys into the gale, and carried two men’s weight as easily as another carried a coil of rope. When a block tore loose from the foremast and swung murderously across the deck, Nickerson caught the tackle in both hands, took the blow on his shoulder and laughed.

“Ain’t enough wind!” he shouted. “Give us wind!”

“The bloke’s a bloomin’ mad cathedral,” Ally said.

But the madness had its uses. The big man knew ships, ice, weather and men. He could splice a hawser in darkness, smell fog before it formed and strike obedience out of a mutineer with one open hand. Hardenberg, who trusted ability wherever he found it and character nowhere, valued him.

At Unalaska they took water, repaired a sprung plank and heard rumours of early ice. Beyond the Aleutians the world altered. The sea turned lead-coloured; the daylight lengthened; the wind acquired a knife edge. Islands rose naked from the water, black, volcanic and deserted. The coast, when it showed itself, appeared not as land but as resistance—a low iron line barring the north.

They entered Bering Strait under a pale midnight sun.

Here the Arctic waited: treacherous, nursing hurricanes under flat horizons, breeding fog out of stillness and thrusting ice into open water as a clenched hand thrusts chips across a table. Floes scraped the Petrel’s sides. Seals lifted round heads to watch her pass. Once, far off, a whale blew, the vapor hanging for an instant like smoke from a gun.

The men grew quiet.

There is a loneliness peculiar to northern seas. It is not the loneliness of an empty room, an abandoned road or a forest at night. It is planetary. The land lies barren, bleak as a dead world, inhospitable as the moon; the water extends without warmth, without promise, without the least human concern. A ship there seems not merely distant from civilisation but forgotten by it.

Then somebody found the champagne.

It was the elderly sailmaker, seeking lamp oil. He brought one bottle into the forecastle, and the counterfeit hospitality of the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company was sampled by experts.

“It bites,” said one beach-comber.

“It kicks,” said the other.

“It’ll do,” said the Finnish mate.

Hardenberg arrived in time to save the remaining bottles and to hit three men with the same rope’s end.

“That wine,” he announced, “is medicine.”

“What for?” asked the sailmaker.

“Russians.”

III

They sighted the post two weeks later.

It stood on a low reach of coast some miles east of Point Barrow: six log huts, a storehouse, a mast bearing a weather-stiffened flag, and a wooden landing stage thrust into shallow water. Behind it stretched the tundra, flat and dun beneath a lid of cloud. No tree, no hill, no road softened the desolation. The settlement might have been dropped there by accident and abandoned from embarrassment.

“A cheerful little suburb,” said Strokher.

“Seven men on the beach,” Hardenberg answered. He lowered the glass. “Two rifles. One dog. Storehouse has a new padlock.”

“Furs,” said Ally Bazan.

“Maybe.”

“Furs,” Ally repeated with conviction. Poverty had made him clairvoyant.

The Petrel entered under reduced sail, flying the American ensign above a company flag Ryder had designed himself. It displayed a polar bear, an anchor, a cornucopia and the initials N.C.E. & S.C.; taken together, these symbols meant nothing, which made them admirably corporate.

Hardenberg went ashore with Strokher, Nickerson and a boat’s crew. Ally remained aboard to maintain discipline and, more importantly, to prevent discipline from drinking the cargo.

The Russian in charge was named Kropotkin—not the celebrated Kropotkin, as he explained several times, but a cousin of somebody who had once seen him. He was short, square, yellow-bearded and hospitable in the manner of lonely men who have not received news for eleven months.

Hardenberg presented the papers.

Kropotkin read English slowly, French not at all and official seals with respect. The papers described the Petrel as a chartered auxiliary of an international relief expedition searching for thirty-two survivors of the wrecked steam-whaler Hesperus.

“A ship?” Kropotkin asked.

“Lost in the ice,” said Hardenberg. “Forty men.”

The papers said thirty-two; but precision, though beautiful in documents, is burdensome in conversation.

“We have seen no men,” Kropotkin replied.

“Bodies?”

“No bodies.”

“Boats?”

“No boats.”

“Then we must search eastward.”

Kropotkin shook his head.

“Ice comes. You do not go east.”

“We got orders.”

“You have death.”

“Had that before.”

Nickerson pushed forward.

“We got champagne,” he thundered.

This produced a result which the humanitarian mission had not. The Russians looked at one another. Kropotkin’s beard opened in a smile.

“French?”

“French enough,” said Hardenberg.

That evening the officers of the Petrel entertained the employees of the Russian trading post aboard the schooner.

The table in the after-cabin was covered with a white sheet. There were tins of lobster, salt pork disguised with onions, ship’s biscuit, preserved peaches and six candles in bottles. Strokher wore a collar. Hardenberg wore authority. Ally Bazan, released from guardianship of the cargo, wore a red sash and called himself steward.

At seven o’clock the first case of champagne was opened.

The cork struck the ceiling.

“Hear that?” said Nickerson. “That’s Paris!”

It was not Paris. It was carbonic gas under pressure. But the Russians cheered.

They drank to the Czar, the President of the United States, the relief expedition, the absent survivors of the Hesperus, the Russian post, the Petrel, the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company, the friendship of nations and Kropotkin’s mother. By the time they reached the health of the dog ashore, international relations had become cordial in the extreme.

The commercial champagne behaved with efficiency. It sparkled, foamed, burned and descended. Its flavour was suggestive of cider, kerosene and a dentist’s cupboard; but flavour, like morality, weakens after the third bottle.

Strokher sat beside Kropotkin and listened gravely to an account of fur prices in Vladivostok.

“I put it to you,” he said, refilling the Russian’s glass, “that the winter must be excessively tedious.”

“Terrible,” Kropotkin agreed, and drank.

“I put it to you further that only a man of remarkable constitution could survive it.”

Kropotkin struck his chest.

“I am iron.”

“Precisely.”

He drank again.

At the other end Nickerson competed with two trappers in a series of toasts, each more murderous than the last. Ally Bazan danced a hornpipe on the cabin table, kicked a preserved peach into the lamp and sang a song concerning the governor of New South Wales. Hardenberg, who drank nothing, watched.

At ten o’clock the dog’s health was proposed a second time.

At ten-fifteen Kropotkin attempted a speech in French, forgot the language, embraced Strokher and slid under the table.

At ten-thirty the last Russian was unconscious.

Nickerson wiped his mouth.

“Good liquor,” he declared. “Hits low and spreads.”

“Now,” said Hardenberg, “we go ashore.”

They left Nickerson to guard their guests. He objected; but Hardenberg pointed out that a Russian might awake.

“If he does,” said Nickerson, doubling one tremendous fist, “I’ll put him back to sleep.”

Hardenberg, Strokher and Ally Bazan pulled for the landing stage under the colourless midnight light.

The storehouse padlock yielded to a cold chisel. Inside were barrels of flour, bales of cloth, traps, tools, dried fish, lamp oil, tobacco and shelves of ordinary skins: fox, seal, mink and bear.

“No otter,” whispered Ally.

“Look,” said Hardenberg.

At the rear of the building, stacked against the logs, were cases marked MACHINE PARTS. Their boards were new. Their nails had been recently driven. Hardenberg prized one open.

Within lay sea-otter pelts.

For a moment none of the three spoke.

They were packed fur inward, each skin long, dark, dense and lustrous, the silver-tipped guard hairs glimmering in the dimness. Forty-eight pelts lay in the cases, and each was worth a little fortune. Their combined value exceeded every honest wage the three men had ever earned or were likely to earn if longevity became a curse.

Ally Bazan sat down hard on a barrel.

“Gawd,” he whispered. “We’re capitalists.”

Strokher lifted one skin, weighed it in his hands and replaced it with reverence.

“I put it to you that we should lose no time.”

They lost none.

The boat made four journeys. The cases were broken up, the pelts bundled in sailcloth and transferred to a concealed locker beneath the Petrel’s cabin floor. By two in the morning the storehouse was closed, the lock hung in place, and the Russians remained prostrate among empty bottles.

At three the schooner warped from the landing stage.

At four the breeze freshened from the south.

At five Kropotkin awoke, crawled on deck and waved.

Nickerson waved back.

The Petrel stood westward under all plain sail.

IV

They were sixty miles from the post before Nickerson learned what had happened.

He came aft after breakfast and found Hardenberg, Strokher and Ally Bazan dividing figures upon the cabin table. The calculation was pleasing. Even after Ryder’s share, expenses, commissions, bribes and the natural shrinkage attaching to every large commercial transaction, each man stood to clear more money than he had ever possessed at one time.

“What’s that?” Nickerson asked.

“Salvage,” said Hardenberg.

“What salvage?”

“Forty-eight sea-otter skins.”

Nickerson stared.

“You found ’em?”

“We located them.”

“Where?”

“In the storehouse.”

Nickerson considered this. His moral mechanism, such as it was, worked slowly but not altogether inaccurately.

“You lifted ’em.”

“Don’t use ugly words,” said Hardenberg. “They were Russian pelts collected in American territory under questionable concession.”

“You stole ’em.”

“We adjusted the concession.”

Nickerson’s grin widened.

“My share?”

“One quarter of what’s left after Ryder’s third.”

“B-u-s-t!” Nickerson shouted, striking the table so that the pencil leaped. “I’ll buy a saloon. No—I’ll buy two saloons. One to drink in and one to get throwed out of.”

“You’ll keep quiet first,” Hardenberg said.

Nickerson slapped him between the shoulders with affection enough to damage a smaller man.

“Captain, I’m as quiet as a corpse.”

That afternoon the wind hauled east. Hardenberg wanted distance and drove the schooner hard. The Petrel plunged through a short sea, carrying topsails, jib and flying jib when prudence suggested less. Ice showed to leeward. Fog streamed in ragged bands across the water.

Near midnight a squall came down.

“Hands shorten sail!” Hardenberg shouted.

The deck sprang into confusion. Men ran to sheets and halyards. Canvas thundered. The schooner heeled until the lee rail buried itself and water rushed aft in a white sheet.

Nickerson was already aloft.

He moved through the rigging with his usual brutal confidence, a dark mass against the pale sky. Reaching the foretop, he leaned outward to clear a fouled line.

The schooner lurched.

A block swung.

Whether the block struck him, whether his foot slipped, whether the ratline parted was never determined. They saw his arms fly wide. For an instant he hung against the sky like a man crucified upon the rigging; then he fell.

He struck the edge of the foretopsail yard, turned over and came down upon the deck.

The sound was appalling.

Ally Bazan cried out. Strokher ran forward. Hardenberg left the wheel to the mate and knelt beside the body.

Nickerson breathed. There was blood at his temple, but no bone showed. They carried him below, laid him in a bunk and bandaged his head. The cook, who claimed surgical experience because he had once assisted at the butchering of a steer, declared the skull intact.

For eighteen hours Nickerson did not move.

Toward evening of the next day he opened his eyes.

Hardenberg sat beside him.

“How d’you feel?”

Nickerson looked at the cabin roof, then at Hardenberg. His face seemed altered. The ferocity had gone out of it; even the broken nose appeared less aggressive.

“Where am I?” he asked.

His voice was low.

“Aboard the Petrel.”

“The what?”

“Our schooner.”

Nickerson touched the bandage.

“I have been injured.”

“You fell from aloft.”

“From aloft?”

“You were furling the foretopsail.”

The big man closed his eyes. His lips moved.

“What’s that?” Hardenberg asked.

“A prayer.”

Hardenberg straightened.

Nickerson opened his eyes again.

“My name is not Nickerson.”

“No?”

“It is the Reverend Silas Peabody.”

Hardenberg said nothing.

“I am a minister of the Methodist Episcopal Church,” the man continued. “I was travelling to Portland to assume charge of a parish. There was an accident—a train—darkness.” His hand pressed his forehead. “How long ago?”

“Five years.”

The Reverend Silas Peabody looked at him in horror.

“Five years!”

“Easy now.”

“What have I done?”

Hardenberg considered several possible answers and chose the safest.

“You’ve sailed.”

“Only sailed?”

“Well—mostly.”

Peabody struggled upright.

“My clothes. My hands.” He regarded the scars upon his knuckles. “This language in my mind—these dreadful recollections—saloons, fights, profanity. Sir, I remember striking a man with a chair.”

“He had a knife.”

“That does not excuse it.”

“It excused it where we were.”

Peabody gripped Hardenberg’s arm.

“Tell me everything.”

“No sense exciting yourself.”

“Everything.”

The second blow had done what physicians, detectives, advertisements and five years of time had failed to do. It had restored the Reverend Peabody to himself—memory, conscience, vocabulary and all. Unfortunately, it had not erased Nickerson. The minister remembered both lives: the church suppers and the waterfront brawls, the sermons and the chanteys, the laying on of hands in benediction and the laying on of hands for other purposes. These two existences met in him with a shock more violent than the fall.

Hardenberg tried commercial phrasing.

“We came north on a fur proposition.”

“What kind of proposition?”

“A transfer.”

“Was it lawful?”

“Law’s a matter of location.”

“Captain!”

“We lifted some skins.”

“You mean stole them.”

There are words which clarify an enterprise disastrously. “Acquisition” may be defended; “adjustment” explained; “salvage” documented. But theft is theft, especially when pronounced by a Methodist minister with restored memory and a bandage around his head.

Peabody rose from the bunk. He swayed, caught the bulkhead and steadied himself.

“How many skins?”

“Forty-eight.”

“From whom?”

“Russians.”

“By violence?”

“No violence. Champagne.”

“You corrupted them with drink?”

“It wasn’t good enough to corrupt anybody.”

“And I assisted?”

“You drank with ’em.”

The minister groaned.

“Oh, Lord, have mercy upon me.”

“He may,” said Hardenberg. “Ryder won’t.”

The others were called.

Strokher received the transformation with grave attention. Ally Bazan received it with open-mouthed terror.

“You mean to s’y,” Ally demanded, “that one crack on the ’ead ’as turned Nickerson into a parson?”

“Restored,” Peabody corrected.

“I put it to you,” Strokher said to Hardenberg, “that another crack might restore Nickerson.”

“No!” Peabody exclaimed.

“I merely state the physiological possibility.”

Hardenberg took Strokher aside.

“Don’t even think it.”

“I have not proposed action.”

“You’re looking at that belaying pin.”

“I am looking beyond it.”

Peabody heard them.

“Gentlemen, if you imagine that you may silence my conscience by inflicting a further injury—”

“Sit down,” Hardenberg said. “Nobody’s inflicting anything. We ain’t murderers.”

“No,” said the minister bitterly. “You are thieves.”

Ally Bazan flared.

“Now see ’ere! There’s no call to be insultin’. We stole them pelts fair and square.”

For the remainder of the voyage south, Peabody lived apart. He read the damaged hymn-book, prayed, and attempted to persuade the crew to repentance. The crew listened respectfully because they remembered his fist. He refused his share of the pelts and demanded that the schooner return them.

“That post is behind the ice,” Hardenberg told him. “We couldn’t get back if we wanted.”

“Then we must surrender the stolen property to the lawful authorities.”

“We must do nothing of the sort.”

“It is my duty.”

“Your duty’s to recover.”

“My health is of no consequence.”

“Maybe not to you. We’re short-handed.”

They were indeed. The Finnish mate had developed fever; one beach-comber had crushed two fingers; the sailmaker was too old for heavy work; and Peabody, though restored morally, was still six feet three and capable of hauling a line. Hardenberg therefore dared neither confine him nor set him ashore on the empty coast.

He watched him.

Strokher watched him.

Ally Bazan watched everybody.

The Petrel crawled southward through fog and adverse wind, her stolen fortune beneath the cabin floor and a functioning conscience in the forecastle—two forms of cargo equally dangerous, though only one was insured by prayer.

V

By the time they reached Southeast Alaska, water casks were low, provisions mouldered and the Finnish mate could no longer stand.

“We put into Juneau,” Hardenberg decided.

Peabody looked up sharply.

“For supplies,” Hardenberg added. “Nothing else.”

“I understand.”

“No, you don’t. There’s a revenue cutter in those waters.”

“Then Providence has simplified my duty.”

“Providence didn’t charter this schooner.”

They entered Juneau in rain.

The town climbed from the waterfront in wet tiers of warehouses, saloons, stores, offices and houses, all roofed in mist. Steam curled from stacks. Small craft moved among the pilings. Beyond, the mountains rose dark and immense, their flanks striped with snow.

At anchor in the channel lay the United States revenue cutter Mohican, trim, black-hulled and official. Her brasswork shone even in the rain. A launch moved between her and the customs wharf.

Ally Bazan saw her and became profane.

“Keep your shirt on,” Hardenberg said. “They ain’t looking for us.”

Peabody stood at the rail, staring at the cutter.

Hardenberg moved beside him.

“If you go ashore, you go with me.”

“I will not be restrained.”

“You’ll be watched.”

“Captain, you may watch my body. My duty is beyond your authority.”

“That sounds pretty, but if you make trouble I’ll put you aboard and sail without stores.”

“You cannot sail without men.”

It was an unanswerable objection. Hardenberg hated him for making it calmly.

The schooner tied up at a wharf. Hardenberg went ashore to arrange water, provisions and medical aid. Strokher remained aboard. Ally Bazan took charge of Peabody with instructions not to let him out of sight.

For twenty minutes this arrangement held.

Then the cook reported smoke in the galley; a kettle had overturned upon the stove. Ally ran below. Peabody stepped onto the wharf, turned the corner of a warehouse and disappeared.

When Ally returned, he emitted a noise like a punctured concertina.

“Gone!”

Strokher looked over the rail.

“Who?”

“The parson!”

“I put it to you that you had one task.”

“I put it to you that the bloomin’ stove was afire!”

They found Hardenberg at the provision merchant’s.

The captain listened, said nothing and ran.

They searched the customs house, the marshal’s office, the post office, two churches, three docks and, through force of habit, four saloons. At last a boy selling newspapers told them that a large man with a bandaged head had asked the way to the American consul’s residence.

“Consul,” said Hardenberg. “That’s done it.”

They hurried uphill through the rain.

The consul’s house stood behind a white fence. The gate was open. From within came raised voices. A woman screamed. A chair overturned.

Hardenberg stopped.

“Too late.”

The front door burst open. Two servants and a policeman emerged, struggling with Peabody. The minister’s coat was torn, his bandage awry, his face radiant with terrible purpose.

“You must hear me!” he cried. “Robbery upon the high seas! Stolen furs! I am an accomplice and demand arrest!”

The policeman ducked as Peabody’s arm swung free. The movement was innocent—the Reverend meant only to point toward the harbour—but Nickerson’s muscles remained attached to Peabody’s intentions. The policeman went backward into a rosebush.

“Seize me!” Peabody shouted.

The servants seized him.

“Confiscate the schooner Petrel!”

“Gag him,” said the policeman from the roses.

Hardenberg pulled the others behind a fence before they were seen.

They returned to the schooner by separate streets.

No one spoke until they were in the cabin.

Then Ally Bazan collapsed upon a locker.

“It’s all up. Finished. We’re ’anged.”

“They don’t hang for fur theft,” said Strokher.

“They’ll myke an exception.”

Hardenberg peered through the rain-blurred port at the revenue cutter.

The Mohican’s launch had changed course. Instead of returning directly to the cutter, it was heading toward the customs wharf.

“See that?”

Strokher saw.

“They’ll get a warrant,” Hardenberg said.

“Perhaps.”

“No perhaps. Peabody gave ’em the ship’s name. He gave ’em the furs. He gave ’em us.”

Ally’s face changed.

“The pelts.”

All three looked down at the cabin floor.

Forty-eight sea-otter skins lay beneath their boots. A fortune. Evidence. Ten years apiece.

Hardenberg’s practical intelligence, which had survived storms, frauds, drunken Russians and restored religion, now worked with desperate speed.

“We can’t sail. Tide’s wrong, crew scattered, water not aboard. They search us, we’re gone.”

“Hide them ashore,” Strokher proposed.

“Where?”

“Warehouse.”

“Customs men everywhere.”

“Sink them,” said Ally.

“Fur floats.”

“Ballast ’em.”

“And have divers bring ’em up tomorrow? No.”

The launch reached the wharf.

Two uniformed men stepped ashore.

Hardenberg stood.

“Burn them.”

The others stared.

“I put it to you,” said Strokher slowly, “that the pelts are the object of the voyage.”

“They’re evidence now.”

“They’re worth sixty thousand dollars.”

“They’re worth ten years.”

Ally Bazan clutched the edge of the table.

“Burn ’em? Burn all that lovely money?”

“Overboard with that loot!” Hardenberg snapped. “No—bring it to the galley. Move!”

They tore up the cabin floor.

The bundles came out, heavy, soft, magnificent. Ally held one to his chest like a rescued child.

“Couldn’t we keep just a little one?”

“You can wear it in prison. Move.”

In the galley the stove glowed red. They fed it kindling, coal oil and split wood until the iron pipe roared.

Then they began to burn the pelts.

Sea-otter fur does not burn willingly. It curls, smokes, chars, stinks and must be forced into the flame. They cut the skins into strips with sheath knives, thrust them into the stove and packed them down with pokers. Black smoke poured from the chimney. The galley filled with an odour compounded of scorched hair, grease and ruined ambition.

Ally wept openly.

“Three thousand dollars,” he said as each pelt went in. “Another three thousand. There goes me ’ouse. There goes me carriage. There goes the little place in Sydney with the verandah.”

“Shut up and cut,” Hardenberg ordered.

Strokher worked with rigid dignity, but after the twentieth skin he paused.

“I put it to you that this is the most expensive fuel ever employed aboard a merchant vessel.”

“Cut.”

The cutter’s launch left the customs wharf and headed toward the Petrel.

“Faster!”

They crammed the stove. Flame leaped from the cracks. The stovepipe reddened. Grease ran across the galley floor. The cook, seeing what they did, crossed himself and demanded whether rich men had gone mad.

The thirty-ninth pelt burned.

The fortieth.

The forty-first.

The launch drew nearer.

Ally attempted to conceal the forty-second inside his shirt. Hardenberg removed it.

“Traitor!” Ally cried.

“Idiot!”

The forty-eighth and last pelt would not fit. Strokher hacked it into quarters. Hardenberg forced the pieces through the stove door. A tongue of flame rolled out and singed his eyebrows.

Then it was done.

Forty-eight sea-otter pelts, the object of a four-thousand-mile voyage, the capital of the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company, the future comfort of the Black Crows, had become smoke over Juneau.

The three stood blackened, sweating and speechless.

A boat bumped alongside.

Boots sounded on deck.

Hardenberg wiped his hands and went out to meet the law.

VI

There were two men: a lieutenant from the Mohican and a deputy marshal.

The lieutenant saluted.

“Captain Hardenberg?”

“That’s me.”

“You’re master of this vessel?”

“So the papers claim.”

“We’ve come about a man named Nickerson.”

Hardenberg’s mouth went dry.

“Nickerson?”

“Large fellow. Head bandaged. Says his name is Peabody.”

Strokher and Ally had appeared behind him, both smelling strongly of burnt fur.

“He has made a statement,” the deputy marshal added.

Hardenberg said nothing.

The lieutenant looked toward the galley chimney.

“Fire aboard?”

“Cook burned the dinner.”

“Smells queer.”

“Cook’s queer.”

The deputy laughed.

“Well, your man’s caused quite a disturbance at the consul’s house. Broke a chair, knocked down a policeman, accused half the territory of piracy and demanded that the United States arrest the Czar of Russia.”

“The Czar?” Hardenberg repeated.

“He was excited,” said the lieutenant. “Claims you have a hundred thousand stolen otter skins aboard.”

“Forty-eight,” Ally Bazan said automatically.

Hardenberg stepped on his foot.

“Forty-eight thousand,” Ally corrected with a gasp. “He said forty-eight thousand. Mad as a bloomin’—as a very mad person, sir.”

The deputy marshal nodded.

“That’s our view. Doctor says concussion. Fellow appears to have two identities and can’t keep either one quiet. We don’t want him in the lockup, and the consul’s wife says she’ll leave Alaska if he remains in town.”

The lieutenant handed Hardenberg a folded paper.

“You are requested to take charge of him and remove him aboard your vessel.”

Hardenberg looked at the paper, then at the lieutenant.

“That’s all?”

“That’s all.”

“No search?”

“Search? For what?”

Behind Hardenberg, something in the galley stove collapsed with a soft, expensive sigh.

The captain leaned against the rail.

“Nothing,” he said.

“We’ll bring him down in half an hour. Keep him confined until you sail.”

“We’ll keep him.”

The lieutenant sniffed again.

“You might inspect that stovepipe. Burning hot.”

“It is,” said Strokher. “Extremely.”

The officials returned to their launch.

The three adventurers watched it pull away.

Nobody moved.

At length Ally Bazan went into the galley and opened the stove. He returned with a handful of gray ash and one twisted scrap of blackened hide no larger than a postage stamp.

He laid it upon the cabin table.

“There,” he said. “Our fortune.”

Strokher sat down.

“I put it to you,” he began; then words failed him.

Hardenberg read the paper twice. The first reading established its contents. The second was an act of self-punishment.

Peabody had confessed. He had named the ship, the men, the post, the counterfeit champagne and the precise number of skins. But he had begun his confession by declaring that for five years he had been another man, that his soul had returned through a blow from a block, and that divine guidance had led him to the consul. When the consul attempted to calm him, Peabody had overturned a table, quoted the Book of Amos, seized a brass umbrella stand and demanded a detachment of marines. His truth, being surrounded by lunacy, had been dismissed with the rest.

The thieves had destroyed themselves through excessive confidence in honesty.

Half an hour later Peabody was brought aboard between two policemen. He had a new bandage and an expression of chastened dignity.

“Captain,” he said, “the authorities refuse to act.”

“So I heard.”

“They consider me insane.”

“Can’t imagine why.”

“But I shall persist. Truth must prevail.”

Hardenberg looked at him for a long time.

Behind the minister stood Strokher, calm but pale, and Ally Bazan, clutching the charred scrap of fur. The stove continued to radiate the heat of sixty thousand dollars.

“Truth,” Hardenberg said, “has prevailed enough for one day.”

Peabody sniffed.

“What is that odour?”

“Our evidence,” Ally whimpered.

The minister saw the open floor, the empty locker, the knives, the ash upon their clothing. Understanding came slowly.

“You burned the pelts?”

“All forty-eight,” said Strokher.

Peabody put a hand to his breast.

“Oh.”

It was not triumph. It was not relief. Even the Reverend Silas Peabody, whose conscience was new from five years’ storage, understood the magnitude of the destruction. For one instant Nickerson’s old face showed through his own.

“All of ’em?” he whispered.

“All,” said Hardenberg.

Peabody sat down heavily.

There was silence in the cabin.

Rain tapped the skylight. From the waterfront came the rattle of trucks, the cries of stevedores, the thin whistle of a departing steamer. The Mohican lay in the channel, black, trim, indifferent.

At last Ally Bazan sprang to his feet.

“Ryder!”

The others looked at him.

“That bearded old shark sent us. ’Is champagne, ’is papers, ’is agent. ’E knew about the parson.”

“He knew Nickerson had lost his memory,” Hardenberg said.

“Then ’e knew it might come back.”

“Perhaps,” said Strokher, “he did not anticipate the precise therapeutic action of a topsail block.”

“He stayed in San Francisco,” Ally continued, gathering force. “Warm, dry, respectable, while we froze, stole, burned and got preached at. And ’e gets a third!”

“A third of the ashes,” said Hardenberg.

“I put it to you,” Strokher said, “that our agreement with Ryder requires renegotiation.”

Hardenberg folded the deputy marshal’s paper and placed it carefully in his pocket.

His burned eyebrows gave him an expression of perpetual astonishment, but his eyes had recovered their practical light.

“We take water,” he said. “We take provisions. We get the mate to a doctor. Then we sail south.”

Peabody raised his head.

“To surrender ourselves?”

“To San Francisco.”

“And then?”

Hardenberg smiled without warmth.

“We call on the president of the Northern Commercial Exploration and Salvage Company.”

Ally Bazan brightened.

“With a lawyer?”

“With Nickerson.”

Peabody stood.

“I cannot lend myself to violence.”

“Nobody asked the Reverend Peabody.”

They all considered this.

Peabody touched the bandage upon his head.

Far beneath the formal coat borrowed from the consul, beneath the recovered vocabulary, the conscience and the clerical dignity, something large, red-bearded and sailorly appeared to stir.

“I shall remonstrate with Mr. Ryder,” he said.

“Remonstrate hard,” said Hardenberg.

That evening the Petrel took on water. The following morning she warped from the wharf and stood down the channel beneath a clearing sky. Juneau receded astern; the revenue cutter dwindled to a black toy; the mountains opened, rank beyond rank, magnificent and useless.

The four men stood by the rail.

They possessed no pelts, no profit and no plausible account of their enterprise. They had crossed northern seas, deceived Russians, robbed a post, restored a minister and burned a fortune to avoid an arrest nobody intended to make. In every practical sense they had failed.

Yet the Petrel was headed south, Ryder was waiting in San Francisco, and Nickerson—or, rather, the Reverend Silas Peabody—had begun, quite unconsciously, to crack his knuckles.

For the Three Black Crows, this was not wealth.

But it was something.

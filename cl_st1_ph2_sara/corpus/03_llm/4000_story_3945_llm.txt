# The Rabbit Who Learned to Run

There was once a Rabbit made of soft brown plush, with a cream-coloured breast, long ears lined with pink sateen, and two shining eyes as black as currants. His whiskers were of stiff silver thread, his paws were neatly stitched, and inside him he was packed so firmly with sawdust that he could sit upright without leaning against anything.

He was a splendid Rabbit.

On Christmas morning he lay in the very middle of the Boy’s stocking, with a sprig of holly caught between his paws. The Boy pulled him out at once and cried, “A rabbit!” and kissed the pink inside of one ear. For two whole hours the Rabbit was happier than he had ever imagined it possible to be.

But Christmas morning is a busy time.

There were drums to beat and picture-books to open, and a tin railway that went round and round when one turned a key. There was also a boat with a real little sail, a box of soldiers in scarlet coats, and a clockwork lion which could open its mouth and roar if the nursery floor was level enough. By dinner-time the Rabbit had slipped between the bedclothes, and no one remembered him until Nana made the bed.

“What’s this old thing doing here?” she said, though he was not old at all, and she set him upon the toy cupboard.

The cupboard was crowded and rather dark. The best toys lived on the upper shelf, where they could look down upon everybody else. The Model Engine had brass wheels and a label fastened underneath him to prove that he was made by a famous firm. The Lion possessed a mane of real horsehair and spoke continually of his machinery. Even the wooden soldiers held themselves stiffly, as if a Rabbit stuffed with sawdust were hardly fit to be in the same regiment.

“Have you a patent?” asked the Engine.

“I don’t think so,” said the Rabbit.

“Can you move by clockwork?” asked the Lion.

“No,” said the Rabbit. “But I can sit upright.”

“Anyone can do that if he is stuffed tightly enough,” said the Lion.

The Rabbit looked down at his paws. Until then he had thought them charming. Now they seemed very plain.

Only the Skin Horse was kind to him. The Skin Horse had belonged to the Boy’s uncle long ago, before the Boy was born. Most of the hair had been loved from his sides, and one ear hung lower than the other, and his leather bridle was cracked. Yet there was something quiet and important about him which made even the Engine cease puffing when he spoke.

The Rabbit sat beside him one evening after Nana had turned down the gas and gone away.

“What is Real?” the Rabbit asked.

The Skin Horse looked at him for a long moment.

“Real is what you become when someone loves you,” he said.

The Rabbit considered this.

“Does it happen all at once, like being wound up?”

“No.”

“Is there a click?”

“Not usually.”

“Do you feel different inside?”

“Yes,” said the Skin Horse. “But often not until it has been happening for a long, long time.”

The Rabbit edged closer.

“How long?”

“Sometimes years. A child must love you not merely on birthdays, or while your paint is fresh, but in bed and at breakfast, and when he is cross, and when he is frightened. He must carry you by one ear and leave you beneath chairs and whisper secrets into your fur. Then, little by little, you become Real.”

“Does it hurt?” asked the Rabbit.

“Sometimes.”

The Rabbit was silent. Hurt sounded unpleasant.

“When you become Real,” the Skin Horse went on, “your fur may rub away. Your stuffing may settle, and your seams may loosen. You may lose your whiskers. But these things do not matter, because once you are Real, you cannot be ugly except to people who do not understand.”

“Are you Real?” whispered the Rabbit.

The Skin Horse’s old face grew gentler.

“The Boy’s uncle made me Real,” he said. “That was many years ago. It lasts.”

The Rabbit thought about this after the others were asleep. He wanted more than anything to become Real, but he wished it might happen without his pink ears fading or his whiskers coming out. He was very proud of his whiskers.

For some time nothing happened.

The Boy played with the Engine, and the Engine rushed along his rails with a tremendous clattering. The Lion roared until his spring broke, after which he was sent away to be mended and came back more conceited than before. The Rabbit remained in the cupboard and waited.

Then, one evening, the Boy could not find the china dog he always took to bed.

“Nana,” he called, “I want Hector!”

“Hector is nowhere to be seen,” said Nana, who was tired. She opened the cupboard, put in one hand, and pulled out the Rabbit by a hind leg. “Here. Take this. A soft toy is better in bed than all that hard china.”

The Rabbit landed upon the pillow.

At first the Boy pushed him under the blankets, and the Rabbit could scarcely breathe for the heat. Then the Boy drew him up beneath his chin and folded both arms around him.

“You shall sleep here,” said the Boy. “Don’t be afraid.”

The Rabbit had not known that he was afraid. But after that he was not.

Every night he slept in the Boy’s bed. At first he found it uncomfortable, for the Boy tossed about, and sometimes the Rabbit was flattened beneath an elbow. Once he fell between the bed and the wall and remained there until morning. Yet he soon discovered that being held was far more wonderful than sitting perfectly upright in a cupboard.

The Boy told him secrets. He told him which puddle in the garden was deep enough for ships, why he disliked rice pudding, and how he intended to become an explorer when he was grown. He also explained that the dark under the bed was full of wolves, but that no wolf would come near while the Rabbit was there.

This was a great responsibility for a Rabbit who could not move by clockwork. Nevertheless, no wolf ever came.

And so the days passed, and the Rabbit was forgotten no longer.

In the mornings he attended breakfast tucked under the Boy’s arm. His paws sometimes dipped into the porridge, and Nana wiped them briskly with a corner of her apron.

“This creature gets filthier every day,” she said.

“He isn’t a creature,” said the Boy. “He’s my Rabbit.”

The Rabbit did not mind being wiped, though it pulled his whiskers. He went out in the perambulator and rode in the toy cart. He was carried downstairs to hear stories and upstairs to watch the Boy splash in his bath. When the Boy built a tent from two chairs and a shawl, the Rabbit lived inside it and guarded a treasure made of buttons.

In spring they began to play in the garden.

The Boy made the Rabbit a burrow beneath the raspberry canes. To the Boy it was a house, but to the Rabbit it seemed an enormous palace in a green jungle. Tender leaves spread above him, and striped shadows moved across the earth. Ants marched past carrying burdens, beetles shone like polished toys, and once a snail looked at him so solemnly that the Rabbit wondered whether it was an old toy which had become Real.

The Boy brought acorn cups for plates and laid dandelion leaves upon them.

“You must eat your supper,” he said.

The Rabbit could not truly eat, but he listened while the Boy made suitable nibbling noises for him, and that was almost the same thing.

They hunted tigers among the gooseberry bushes. They crossed deserts no wider than the gravel path. They sailed to islands in a wheelbarrow and escaped from pirates behind the fowl-house. When the Boy ran, he carried the Rabbit pressed against his chest, and the Rabbit imagined that he too was running, faster and faster, with the wind flowing past his ears.

Weeks passed, and his beauty began to change.

His fur grew thin where the Boy held him. The cream upon his breast turned greyish, and the pink sateen in his ears became pale. One whisker bent, another disappeared, and his firm, round body grew soft and crooked. The Engine laughed when he saw him.

“You look as if you have been through the washing machine,” he said.

The Rabbit did not answer.

The Lion said, “No one could tell what sort of animal you are.”

But that night the Boy kissed the worn place upon his nose and said, “You are the loveliest Rabbit in the world.”

After that the Rabbit did not care what the Lion thought.

One warm evening, when the windows stood open, the Boy was called away to his supper and left the Rabbit in the garden. The Rabbit lay among the bracken, watching moths flutter above the grass.

Presently two strange rabbits appeared.

They were brown and quick, with bright eyes and whiskers that trembled. Their ears turned this way and that without anybody touching them. They sprang over one another, kicked up their heels, and darted round the bracken in a wonderful dance.

The Rabbit stared.

They must be very expensive toys indeed, he thought, for their clockwork made no noise at all.

One of them stopped and looked at him.

“Why don’t you come and play?” it asked.

“I am playing,” said the Rabbit. “I am waiting for the Boy.”

“You are sitting still.”

“I often sit still.”

The second rabbit hopped nearer and sniffed him.

“He smells of the nursery,” it said.

“I live there,” said the Rabbit proudly.

“Can you hop?”

“Of course,” said the Rabbit, though no one had ever asked him before.

“Then hop.”

The Rabbit tried. He strained inside with all his might, but his legs remained where they were.

“I do not feel like hopping just now,” he said.

“Can you dig?”

“I have a burrow.”

“Did you dig it?”

“The Boy did.”

The wild rabbits looked at each other.

“He hasn’t any proper hind legs,” said one.

“I have,” cried the Rabbit. “They are under me.”

“Those are cushions.”

“They are legs!”

The rabbits danced around him, light as brown leaves.

“He isn’t a rabbit at all,” they said. “He is only a toy.”

“I am not!” cried the Rabbit. “I am Real! The Boy says so!”

Just then the Boy came running back.

“There you are!” he said, and caught the Rabbit up. “I thought I had lost you.”

The wild rabbits vanished beneath the hedge. The Rabbit pressed his face against the Boy’s jacket, but he could not forget what they had said.

That night, when the moon lay across the bed, he asked, “Am I truly Real?”

The Boy did not understand the words, for the Rabbit spoke only in the quiet way toys speak. But he seemed to know the question.

“You aren’t a toy,” the Boy whispered. “You’re Real. You are my real Rabbit.”

Then he kissed him and went to sleep.

A warm and wonderful feeling spread through the Rabbit, even to the tips of his faded ears. He remembered the Skin Horse and understood at last. It did not matter that the wild rabbits could not see it. It did not matter that he had no clockwork, nor that his fur was rubbed and his stuffing lumpy.

The Boy loved him.

He was Real.

Summer came, and they were together every day. They lay beneath the trees where sunlight trembled among the leaves, and the Boy read aloud from picture-books, making up the difficult words. They climbed the hill beyond the orchard and watched the clouds become castles, elephants, and ships. The Rabbit’s remaining whiskers disappeared, and his seams showed at the paws, and once Nana was obliged to mend his side with brown thread.

“What a shabby old fellow,” she said.

“He’s not shabby,” said the Boy. “He’s loved.”

The Rabbit would have smiled if his mouth had not been made of stitching.

Autumn came after summer, with red berries and cold evenings and leaves that skittered along the paths. The Rabbit enjoyed being tucked beneath the Boy’s coat, where it was dark and warm. At night they listened to rain upon the nursery window and planned what they would do when spring returned.

And then, one day, the Boy was ill.

At first everyone said it was only a cold. Nana brought hot milk, and the Rabbit lay beside the Boy’s cheek. But the Boy grew hotter and stranger, and he did not ask for stories or speak of wolves. His hands moved restlessly over the bedclothes, searching until they found the Rabbit.

The doctor came with a black bag. He frowned and spoke quietly to Nana outside the door. Lamps burned all night, and bowls of water stood upon the washstand. The Boy’s mother sat by the bed, and the nursery smelled of medicines and coal smoke.

It was scarlet fever.

During those long days the Rabbit never left the Boy. Sometimes he was pushed beneath the pillow; sometimes he lay on the floor until Nana picked him up and placed him back within reach. The Boy’s arms no longer hugged him tightly, but one hot hand often rested upon his worn fur.

The Rabbit wished he could do more. He wished there were a key inside him which someone might turn, so that he could fetch water or call Nana. He wished he could draw the fever into his own sawdust and keep it there.

But he could only stay.

So he stayed through the bright, uneasy mornings and the dreadful quiet nights. He listened to the Boy breathing, and whenever the Boy stirred, the Rabbit was beside him.

At last the fever broke.

The Boy slept peacefully, and when he woke he knew everybody again. His mother cried, and Nana laughed while pretending she had something in her eye. The doctor nodded and shut his black bag.

“He will do now,” he said. “Fresh air as soon as he is strong enough. Take him to the seaside, perhaps.”

The Rabbit heard this and was filled with delight. He pictured the sea as an enormous blue bath, and he wondered whether there would be pirates.

Then the doctor looked around the room.

“Everything he has used must be burned,” he said. “Bedclothes, books, toys—all of it. We cannot risk the infection.”

Nana began opening cupboards.

The next morning the Boy was carried into another room wrapped in a clean blanket. He was too weak to notice that the Rabbit remained behind.

The Rabbit waited all day.

Surely, he thought, the Boy would ask for him at bedtime. Surely Nana would return and say, “Here is your old Rabbit.” He lay beneath the crumpled sheet and listened to the house moving around him.

But near evening Nana came with a large sack.

Into it went the sheets, the pillows, two picture-books, the paper circus, the small woollen shawl, and the Rabbit.

“There,” said Nana. “That is the last of the fever things.”

She tied the sack and carried it outside.

The gardener set it behind the fowl-house. “I’ll burn the lot tomorrow,” he said. “Too damp tonight.”

The Rabbit fell through a hole in the sack and landed among dead leaves. For a moment he could see the nursery window shining above the garden. Then darkness came, and a cold wind stirred his thin fur.

He thought of the seaside and the adventures he and the Boy had planned. He thought of spring under the raspberry canes and the tent made from chairs. He thought of the Boy saying, You are my real Rabbit.

Was this what happened after one became Real?

The Skin Horse had said that it sometimes hurt. He had not said that it could hurt so much.

The Rabbit tried to comfort himself. Perhaps tomorrow the Boy would remember. Perhaps he would run into the garden, untie the sack, and carry him away. But the Rabbit knew the Boy was weak and far off, and he knew the gardener would come in the morning.

“If I had stayed splendid,” he thought, “I might have sat upon a shelf for years. I should still have my whiskers and my pink ears. But I wanted to be Real, and now I am to be burned.”

He remembered the wild rabbits dancing upon the grass. They were Real to everyone. They could run from danger, and burrow beneath roots, and leap through hedges. The Rabbit could do none of these things.

A great loneliness filled him, and from his black currant eye there slipped one tear.

It was the first real tear he had ever cried.

The tear fell upon the ground.

Where it touched, a green shoot pushed through the dead leaves. It grew taller and taller, and leaves opened upon it like little emerald hands. Then a golden flower appeared, shining so brightly that the darkness drew back.

The flower unfolded, and in its cup sat a Fairy.

She wore a cloak the colour of moonlight and a crown no larger than a dewdrop. Her wings were thin as pearl, and when she stepped down, the grass beneath her feet glittered with frost.

The Rabbit stared at her.

“Are you a new toy?” he asked.

The Fairy laughed, but not unkindly.

“No, little Rabbit.”

“You move without clockwork.”

“So do many things.”

She bent and lifted him from the leaves. Her hands were warm.

“Why are you crying?” she asked.

“Because I became Real,” said the Rabbit, “and now I am no use to the Boy, and tomorrow I shall be burned.”

“You were of great use to him,” said the Fairy. “You stayed when he was ill.”

“But he has gone away.”

“Children must go away from many things. That does not make the love untrue.”

The Rabbit looked at her shining face.

“Who are you?”

“I am the Fairy of Things That Have Been Truly Loved,” she said. “I care for toys when their work in the nursery is finished.”

“Will you mend me?”

“I shall do something better.”

The Rabbit trembled.

“The Boy made you Real to himself,” the Fairy said. “Because he loved you, you became alive in the place where love sees clearly. But now I will make you Real to everyone.”

“Will I have machinery?”

“No machinery.”

“New stuffing?”

“No stuffing.”

“Then how shall I move?”

“You shall see.”

She kissed him upon his worn nose.

The Rabbit felt a curious tingling inside. At first he thought his sawdust was shifting. Then his seams seemed to open without hurting, and his flat paws stretched, and bones and muscles appeared where stuffing had been. Soft warm fur flowed over his shabby coat. Whiskers sprang from his cheeks, not silver thread but living whiskers that quivered at every breath of wind.

His ears rose by themselves.

His heart began to beat.

Thump-thump, thump-thump—it went, faster and more wonderful than any clockwork.

The Fairy set him upon the ground.

For one astonished moment he crouched among the leaves. He could feel the cold earth beneath four separate paws. He could smell moss, smoke, damp bark, and a wild sweet scent drifting from the hedge. The night was full of tiny sounds he had never heard before.

Then his hind legs gathered beneath him.

He sprang.

He rose over the sack, landed beyond it, and sprang again. He raced around the fowl-house, through the grass, beneath a currant bush, and out again into moonlight. The wind streamed past his ears exactly as he had always imagined, only a hundred times better.

“I can run!” he cried. “I can run by myself!”

The Fairy stood beside the golden flower, smiling.

From beneath the hedge came the two wild rabbits. They stopped when they saw him.

“Who are you?” one asked.

The Rabbit sat upright. It was easy now.

“I am the Boy’s Rabbit,” he said.

The other sniffed him.

“You smell like a proper rabbit.”

“I am a proper rabbit.”

“Can you hop?”

The Rabbit leaped so high that he cleared a clump of bracken.

The wild rabbits twitched their noses with admiration.

“Come to the wood,” they said. “There are roots to dig beneath and ferny places to sleep, and velvet grass where we dance in the evenings.”

The Rabbit looked back once at the nursery window. Somewhere far away, the Boy was sleeping in clean sheets, growing stronger. The Rabbit loved him still, and always would.

Then he followed the others through the hedge.

All winter he lived in the wood. He learned to nibble clover and strip bark from young twigs. He discovered how to dig a warm hollow beneath the roots of an oak and how to remain still when an owl passed overhead. He raced with the other rabbits across silver bracken, and no one laughed at his legs.

Sometimes, in the quiet before dawn, he remembered the nursery: the cupboard, the Skin Horse, the Boy’s arms, and the sound of rain upon the window. These memories made him sad, but they made him glad too, and the two feelings rested together in his heart.

For being Real does not mean forgetting.

Winter passed, and the days grew mild. Buds opened, birds began building nests, and pale primroses shone beneath the trees. One morning the Rabbit went back to the garden.

The Boy had returned from the seaside. He was thinner than before, but colour had come into his cheeks, and he ran along the path with a wooden hoop.

The Rabbit watched from beneath the raspberry canes.

The Boy stopped.

A brown rabbit sat at the edge of the lawn, with a cream-coloured breast and ears touched delicately with pink. There was something about the shape of its face, and about the way it sat looking at him, that stirred an old memory.

“Look!” the Boy called to Nana. “There’s a rabbit! It looks almost like my old Bunny used to look before he got lost.”

Nana glanced from the doorway.

“Wild rabbits are all alike,” she said. “Come in now. Your tea is ready.”

The Boy remained for another moment.

The Rabbit wished he could tell him everything: how the tear had fallen, how the flower had grown, how the Fairy had kissed him, and how he could run beneath the moon. But the language of wild rabbits is not the language of children, except perhaps in dreams.

So he only twitched his nose.

The Boy smiled.

Then the Rabbit turned, flashed his white tail, and bounded through the hedge into the bright spring wood.

The Boy watched until he was gone.

And though he never knew that the rabbit in the garden was the shabby toy who had slept beside him through so many nights, something warm and familiar remained with him for the rest of that day.

As for the Rabbit, he returned often in the seasons that followed. Sometimes he saw the Boy beneath the apple trees or heard him laughing beyond the wall. He never went too near, for he belonged to the woods now, where the grass was soft and the paths ran on for ever and ever.

But he did not belong any less to the Boy.

For the Skin Horse had spoken truly. Once a child has loved you for a long, long time, that love does not vanish when fur is rubbed away, or when rooms are emptied, or when one spring follows another.

It changes you.

And the Rabbit, racing beneath the green leaves on strong living legs, was Real at last.

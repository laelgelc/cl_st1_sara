I shall be dead before dawn, provided the trembling of my hand permits the proper employment of the razor. There remains no morphia in the little brown vial beside me; the chemist, having at last discerned the nature of my dependence, refused me further surcease, and my purse contains only two tarnished pennies. Thus necessity completes what reason began. Yet before I pass beyond the dominion of men, I must commit to paper the circumstance which destroyed my sleep, my fortune, and—if such a faculty still abides in me—my sanity.

Let the physicians call it delirium. Let the learned gentlemen of the university speak of war-neurosis, isolation, fever, and narcotic degeneration. Their explanations are merciful, and therefore false.

In April of 1917 I served as supercargo aboard the merchant vessel *Alecto*, then conveying machinery from Wellington to a port in Chile. The Pacific was troubled by reports of an armed German raider, but our captain, a stubborn Cornishman, trusted in distance and an old gun mounted uselessly at the stern. At sunset on the twenty-third day we sighted smoke upon the northern horizon. Before midnight our wireless apparatus had been shattered, two men killed, and the *Alecto* brought to beneath the guns of a black, unmarked cruiser.

Our captors behaved with that formal courtesy which occasionally disguises the operations of war. We were transferred to the raider, whilst a demolition party sank our vessel. I remember the sight of her mast descending into the moonlit water, and the absurd grief I felt at it; for I did not then understand that the sea conceals losses infinitely older than any fashioned by human hands.

Two nights afterward I escaped.

The raider lay nearly motionless in a warm, breathless calm. A small cutter trailed astern, provisioned against emergency, and through negligence or providence its securing line remained accessible from the deck. I descended unseen, severed the rope, and drifted into darkness. By dawn the enemy had vanished. I possessed water, ship’s biscuit, a compass, several tins of meat, and no means of propulsion save two oars and a ragged square of canvas. I calculated that currents might eventually carry me within reach of some island route, though I knew that vast region to be among the loneliest upon the globe.

For many days nothing occurred.

The sky remained a brazen vault; the ocean, a polished and immeasurable plain. No sail disturbed the circle of the horizon, no bird crossed the intolerable heavens, and no fish broke the leaden surface. At times I fancied that the world had ended whilst I slept, leaving only myself, a cockleshell, and that eternal accumulation of waters. I rationed my provisions rigorously, recorded my position so long as I retained the strength to do so, and sought comfort in rational conjecture. Rescue was improbable, but not impossible. Death was likely, but familiar.

On the ninth or perhaps tenth day the weather altered. Clouds assembled with prodigious rapidity, blotting out both sun and horizon, and a wind arose whose heat was like the breath issuing from an opened furnace. During the ensuing tempest I could neither observe nor think. Rain beat me insensible; waves lifted the cutter until it seemed suspended above abysses of liquid night, then hurled it downward amid a tumult resembling the collapse of mountains. I bound myself to a thwart and commended my soul to a God in whom, at that time, I retained a conventional belief.

When consciousness returned, the storm had ceased.

I lay face downward in a substance warm, adhesive, and unspeakably foul.

At first I supposed the cutter had capsized upon some muddy reef. Then, raising my head, I perceived the boat resting upright several yards away, half embedded in a glistening black ooze. Beyond it extended—not water, not beach, not any wholesome terrestrial formation—but an unending waste of slime beneath a white and scorching sun.

The smell nearly overcame me. It was the concentrated exhalation of a million opened graves, mingled with brine, sulphur, and the sweetness of corruption. All about me lay the inhabitants of the abyss, delivered nakedly to the air: bloated fish with luminous organs already dimming; pale crustaceans whose multitudinous limbs moved feebly in the mire; transparent things resembling sacs of diseased jelly; and huge, discoloured carcasses of forms unknown to any naturalist. Some were armoured, some flabby, some furnished with mouths so extravagantly toothed that I could not comprehend how their bodies had contained them. Among these relics twisted long ropes of weed, white from want of sunlight and thick as a ship’s hawser.

The sea itself was nowhere visible.

Only after hours of stupefied observation did a possible explanation occur to me. Some volcanic or tectonic convulsion, stupendous beyond recorded precedent, had raised an enormous tract of the ocean floor. The tempest must have coincided with that upheaval and cast my cutter upon the newborn plain. This hypothesis, however prodigious, belonged at least to geology. I clung to it as a drowning man clings to wreckage.

Yet no ridge of ordinary land bounded the waste. The black level rolled outward until vapour consumed it, and the silence possessed a weight almost material. There were no waves, no wind, no cries of scavenging birds—nothing within hearing, and nothing in sight, save death exposed beneath an alien heaven.

The sun rendered travel impossible for two days. I sheltered beneath the tilted boat, drinking sparingly and watching gases burst in oily bubbles from the mire. By the third morning the surface had hardened sufficiently to bear my weight. Far to the west rose a low elevation, scarcely more substantial than a shadow, but it offered the only variation in that hellish monotony. Curiosity, which survives in man after wisdom has perished, compelled me thither.

I carried water, biscuit, a revolver, and a length of rope. Each footstep broke a grey crust and released putrid vapours. The dead forms became less numerous as I advanced, though occasionally I encountered trenches filled with enormous bones. One curving rib rose higher than my shoulder. Another skeleton, half concealed in slime, possessed a skull both piscine and vaguely anthropoid, with sockets of detestable magnitude. I told myself it belonged to a whale distorted by decay.

The elevation proved farther away than I had judged. Scale had become treacherous upon that featureless expanse, as though distance itself obeyed unfamiliar laws. I reached the slope near evening and ascended whilst the sun descended behind me in crimson deformity. At the summit I discovered not another plain, but a tremendous declivity plunging into a canyon of such depth that its lower recesses were already Stygian.

A narrow channel of water wound along the bottom.

The sight of it awakened both relief and apprehension. Here, presumably, the withdrawn ocean survived in some fissure, black and slow beneath the lifted bed. The farther wall rose sheer from the channel, its surface curiously pale amid the surrounding basalt. Something upon that wall caught the dying light: a regular edge, a plane, an angle too exact for nature.

I waited for the moon.

It ascended large and yellow, spreading a cadaverous illumination over the abyss. By that light I descended a path formed from terraces of cracked stone. The water below gave forth no murmur. When at last I reached the margin, I saw that the pale object stood partly within the channel and partly against the cliff—a monolith fashioned from greenish material, higher than a cathedral door and covered from base to summit with carvings.

I forgot my terror.

Even now I confess to a moment of intellectual rapture. Here was evidence of workmanship where no human settlement could ever have existed; an archaeological enigma immeasurably older than Egypt, Chaldea, or those conjectural tribes from whom our species inherited its earliest fears. I approached until my hands touched the stone. Its surface was greasy and cold, though the air remained warm.

The carvings were executed in a manner at once crude and powerful. They represented processions beneath stylised waves: cities of conical towers, vaulted grottoes, and temples ornamented with shells and the coiled bodies of marine beasts. Through these scenes moved figures which I initially assumed to be gods. They stood upright, but bore flattened heads, pendulous lips, and staring lateral eyes. Scales covered their torsos. Webbed extremities terminated their long arms, whilst their lower limbs combined the suggestions of man, seal, and some primitive batrachian.

They bowed before shapes descending from the stars. They offered fish, whales, and beings unmistakably human. In one panel they swarmed over a shoreline beneath a swollen moon; in another they dragged carved stones into the sea whilst cities burned behind them.

Their proportions offended me most. The sculptor had shown whales no larger than the fishlike priests who carried them, and human captives scarcely exceeding the length of one webbed hand. I attributed this to the hieratic conventions of a savage art, whereby divinity is depicted as physically greater than its worshippers. Yet the longer I examined the scenes, the less allegorical they appeared. Certain details—the articulation of joints, the folds about the gills, the careful correspondence of hands to tools—possessed the fidelity of direct observation.

I recalled legends of Oannes and Dagon; the marine deities of Phoenicia; Polynesian tales of kingdoms beneath the reefs; even those fantastic speculations by which certain ethnologists explain recurring fish-symbols among widely separated peoples. Such parallels had amused me in calmer years. Beside that moonlit channel, with the slime of the primordial seabed upon my boots, they assumed another and more pernicious significance.

The water stirred.

Not with a wave, for the channel was confined and windless, but with a slow displacement as of some great mass ascending. Concentric ripples spread toward my feet. Bubbles rose, each larger than a man’s head, and burst with a sound indecently soft.

I stepped backward.

A rounded darkness emerged. For one merciful instant I mistook it for a boulder thrust upward by subterranean pressure. Then two vast eyes opened upon its surface.

The remainder rose deliberately.

I shall not degrade the horror by attempting completeness. Language was fashioned for the dimensions and categories of human experience; it cannot contain that which preceded humanity. I saw flabby folds shining beneath the moon, gills opening and closing like wounds, and arms whose webbed extremities disturbed the channel from bank to bank. Its head swayed above me at a height that made the monolith seem no longer colossal.

Then I understood the carvings.

They were not gods enlarged by reverence. They were portraits.

The creature turned toward the stone. Whether it saw me I cannot say; to suppose that its gaze rested upon my person is a thought from which even morphia has never granted complete release. It extended its prodigious arms around the monolith and pressed its monstrous face against the carvings. From its throat issued a series of deep, liquid ululations, ceremonious and mournful, which vibrated through cliff and marrow alike.

It worshipped.

At that spectacle something essential within me broke. I remember firing the revolver, though whether at the being, the stone, or the moon I do not know. The reports sounded pitiful, like the snapping of twigs before a titan. The head turned. A hand loosened from the monolith.

Thereafter come only fragments: the ascent of the canyon; black mud flying beneath my feet; a laughter which may have been my own; and behind me a sound of wet enormity dragging itself across stone.

I regained the cutter. In blind frenzy I pushed, hauled, and rocked it until the softened plain released the keel. How the waters returned I cannot explain. Perhaps another convulsion lowered that accursed tract; perhaps the sea, waiting beyond the horizon, poured back whilst I lay senseless. My next coherent memory is of a white room in San Francisco, where a naval surgeon informed me that an American freighter had found me drifting alone. No land had been reported in that region. Of my revolver, journal, and remaining provisions there was no trace.

The physicians listened politely. One suggested sunstroke. Another spoke of nightmares induced by starvation. A professor of geology assured me that no elevation on the scale I described could rise and sink without detection. When I showed him the black residue beneath my fingernails, he called it ordinary marine mud.

For a time I accepted their charity. Then the dreams began.

I saw moonlit towers beneath miles of water. I saw multitudes moving through avenues where human bones formed the pavement. Sometimes the ocean drained from every coast, revealing an empire that girdled the earth; and from the exposed gulfs arose those who had slept before the first ape shivered in the forests.

Morphia alone closed that inward eye.

Now the drug is gone, and throughout this night there have been sounds upon the stair. The house is quiet; my landlady sleeps below. Nevertheless something ascends, pausing after each dragging step. A smell of brine and opened graves enters beneath the door.

They will say this manuscript is the final extravagance of a ruined addict. Let them. I know now why the oceans are deep, and why mankind has been permitted only the narrow continents. We are not masters of the earth, but tenants upon its driest crust; puny, recent things sheltered by a few miles of concealing water.

The boards outside are bending.

There—a stroke against the door.

I had meant to use the razor, but the window is nearer. Six floors should suffice. Better the pavement than the embrace of that glistening arm now forcing through the splintered panel.

The lock is breaking.

That eye—

The window. I must reach the window!

# RIGHTS AND DUTIES

There are few words in our language more frequently upon the lips, and less frequently within the understanding, than the word *rights*. Men claim their rights against society, children against parents, servants against employers, and husbands and wives against each other; yet it is worthy of remark that those who speak most loudly of what is due to themselves are not always equally clear-sighted concerning what is due from them. Rights and duties are twins—born in the same hour, nourished at the same breast, and incapable of separation without injury to both. He who insists upon the first while rejecting the second may call himself independent; but we must utter the word—he is selfish.

Mrs. Barbara Uhler had not arrived at this conclusion.

She was a woman of no mean understanding, of quick perceptions, strong affections, and uncommon energy; but all these excellent gifts were overshadowed by an excessive jealousy of personal control. She could be persuaded where she would not be directed, and often resisted a reasonable request merely because it bore, to her sensitive ear, some resemblance to a command. During the first years of her marriage this unhappy tendency had slept beneath the warmth of affection. Herman Uhler was proud of his handsome, spirited wife; Barbara was fond of her grave, industrious husband; and the small house in Mulberry Street, though furnished without elegance, was bright with good order, pleasant words, and the laughter of two children.

But prosperity brought leisure; leisure brought acquaintances; and acquaintances brought theories.

Among the ladies whom Mrs. Uhler met at a literary circle were several who had discovered that the chief obstacle to human advancement was the domestic authority of man. They spoke eloquently—no one could deny them eloquence—of individuality, self-development, social emancipation, and the degradation involved in preparing a husband’s dinner at a stated hour. If a woman watched over her children, they called her enslaved by maternity; if she consulted her husband, they lamented the extinction of her personality; if she made her home attractive, they hinted that she had stooped to become the ornamental keeper of a masculine lodging-house.

These ladies were pleased to call themselves “strong minded.”

We cannot say that Barbara accepted all their doctrines. The natural tenderness of her heart recoiled from some of their bolder propositions; but they flattered her pride, gave imposing names to her impatience, and dignified every petty domestic disagreement into a struggle for liberty. Before long, if Herman requested that dinner should be served at six, Barbara heard in the request the clanking of a chain; if he expressed concern because the children had been left with a careless nurse, she saw in his face the countenance of a tyrant.

“Your wife is not your servant,” said Mrs. Elvira Pomeroy, the most uncompromising of Barbara’s counsellors.

“I never said she was,” replied Barbara, with a slight uneasiness.

“My dear, men do not always say these things. They assume them. The assumption is more insulting than the declaration.”

That sentence remained in Mrs. Uhler’s mind; and, like many sentences which are half false and half true, it did more mischief than an open absurdity could have accomplished.

One afternoon Herman returned from his counting-room with the fatigue of a difficult day upon him. A large payment was due on the morrow; two remittances had failed to arrive; and an important customer had threatened to transfer his business to a rival house. Herman had eaten little since morning, and the thought of home—of a quiet room, a warm dinner, and the faces of his wife and children—had accompanied him through the chill November streets like a promise.

When he entered, the hall was cold and littered with muddy shoes. In the parlor he found little Margaret upon the carpet, crying over a broken doll, while five-year-old Harry was cutting pictures from one of his father’s valuable books.

“Where is your mother?” he asked.

“Gone out,” said Harry, without looking up.

“And Hannah?”

“She went to see her cousin.”

“Who is taking care of you?”

Harry considered this question as if it involved a point of philosophy.

“Margaret takes care of me, and I take care of baby.”

At that instant a wail arose from the chamber above.

Herman ran up-stairs and found the youngest child awake, cold, and hungry in her cradle. He carried her down, wrapped in a blanket, gathered the other two beside him, and waited. Six o’clock passed; then seven. At half-past seven Hannah returned, full of apologies which explained every thing except why she had left the house. She went into the kitchen, where the fire was nearly out, and after a prolonged clatter produced coffee of doubtful character, scorched potatoes, and a steak burned on one side and raw upon the other.

At eight Barbara came home, animated and smiling.

“We had the most remarkable address!” she exclaimed, laying aside her cloak. “Mrs. Pomeroy spoke upon the right of woman to a complete existence.”

“I am glad the address was remarkable,” said Herman. “Nothing else in the evening has been so.”

Barbara detected the reproof.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean that the children were alone, the baby neglected, and dinner not fit to be eaten.”

“Hannah was here.”

“She was not.”

“She had no permission to go out.”

“Servants do not always wait for permission.”

“Then blame Hannah. I cannot be responsible for her misconduct.”

“You are responsible for employing her, and for knowing whether she performs her duties.”

Barbara drew herself up.

“I did not understand, when I married, that I was to spend my life overlooking servants.”

“Nor did I expect you to do so. But some oversight is necessary in every department of life.”

“My department, I suppose you mean?”

“Our home, Barbara.”

“The distinction is without a difference. You go into the world, cultivate your faculties, transact business, and enjoy the society of men; I am to remain here, tied to a cradle and a cooking-stove.”

“I have never tied you to either.”

“No—with cords, perhaps not.”

Herman looked at her steadily. He was a patient man, but patience is not insensibility.

“I ask only that our children shall be safe, and that, when practicable, the family may have wholesome food at a reasonable hour.”

“How modestly tyranny can speak!” said Barbara, with a short laugh. “It asks only this, and only that, until a woman has surrendered every preference.”

“You call my wishes tyranny?”

“When they interfere with my liberty—yes.”

“And have I no liberty? no right to comfort in my own house?”

“You have precisely the rights I have.”

“Then,” said Herman, after a pause, “I presume I possess the right to pursue my own pleasure without considering whether you or the children suffer by it.”

“You distort every thing.”

“No, Barbara. I carry your principle to its conclusion.”

She turned away. The phrase irritated her because it touched the truth; and truth, when it enters an unwilling mind, is seldom welcomed as a guest.

For a few days Mrs. Uhler was more attentive; but the improvement came from wounded pride rather than conviction, and therefore had no permanence. Hannah was dismissed and another servant engaged—a heavy, good-natured girl named Susan, who possessed an extraordinary capacity for breaking dishes and forgetting instructions. Barbara gave orders in the morning and assumed them to be obeyed throughout the day. If the bread was sour, Susan was blamed; if Margaret escaped into the street, Susan was threatened; if the baby’s face became inflamed through neglect, Susan was pronounced the most useless creature alive. Yet Mrs. Uhler would not condescend—so she expressed it—to that personal vigilance by which alone an ignorant servant could have been made useful.

Meanwhile the meetings increased. There was a committee on education, a society for enlarged opportunity, a conference upon individuality, and, at last, an association to protect wives from domestic exactions. Barbara attended them all.

We are not among those who believe that a woman’s mind is injured by exercise, or that every hour spent beyond her own threshold is stolen from her family. Such opinions are refuted alike by reason and experience. But there is a difference, which our “strong-minded” friends did not care to observe, between enlarging one’s usefulness and abandoning one’s obligations. The mother who returns from benevolent labor to find her own child unfed has merely carried charity too far from home.

One bitter evening in January, Herman came in earlier than usual.

“Barbara,” he said, “I have asked Mr. Ellison to dine with us to-day.”

“To-day!”

“I mentioned it on Monday.”

“I had forgotten.”

“He may be of great service to me. The contract we have discussed depends largely upon his judgment.”

“And what do you expect me to do?”

“Nothing extraordinary. See that Susan prepares a proper dinner, and remain to receive him.”

Barbara’s face changed.

“This is unfortunate. Mrs. Pomeroy speaks at seven.”

“Can you not omit one meeting?”

“It is the most important of the series.”

“Important to whom?”

“To every woman who values her dignity.”

“And is my business of no importance?”

“I do not interfere with your business.”

“Your absence may interfere with it to-night.”

Barbara’s cheeks flushed.

“So I am to entertain a man whom I scarcely know because you hope to make money through him?”

“You are to receive a guest whom your husband has invited to his house.”

“*His* house!”

“Our house, if you prefer accuracy.”

“But governed by your pleasure.”

“Barbara,” said Herman, with evident effort, “do not turn a simple request into an accusation. I seldom ask you to sacrifice an engagement. This dinner is important. I beg you to remain.”

She might have yielded then. His voice had lost its severity and contained an appeal which, in earlier days, would have reached her heart. But Mrs. Pomeroy’s maxim concerning masculine assumptions sounded in her memory.

“I cannot,” she replied. “You had no right to make the arrangement without consulting me.”

“I told you four days ago.”

“You informed me; you did not consult me.”

“Will you stay?”

“No.”

“Very well, Mrs. Uhler.”

It was the first time since their marriage that he had addressed her in this manner. She felt the change, but would not acknowledge it.

“Very well, Mr. Uhler.”

At half-past six Barbara went out. Mr. Ellison arrived soon afterward, and found Herman endeavoring to pacify the baby while Susan ran wildly between dining-room and kitchen. The soup was cold, the fowls half cooked, the pudding forgotten. Harry upset a glass of water over the guest; Margaret appeared with jam upon her dress; and Susan, in carrying away the plates, dropped the gravy-boat upon the carpet.

Mr. Ellison was polite—men of the world are often most polite when most uncomfortable—but he departed early. The contract was not obtained.

Herman did not reproach his wife when she returned. He merely told her that Mr. Ellison had called and gone.

“I hope your dinner was agreeable,” said Barbara, defensively.

“It was instructive.”

“Was Susan careless?”

“As usual.”

“You should have dismissed her.”

“I did not suppose it my province to overlook servants.”

Barbara glanced quickly at him. He stood with his back toward her, warming his hands at the dying fire.

“Herman—”

“Well?”

She had meant to say something conciliatory; but there are moments when pride, like a malicious servant, closes the door upon our better impulses.

“Nothing,” she answered.

From that evening Mr. Uhler’s conduct underwent a change—not sudden enough to excite immediate alarm, but too regular to be accidental. He had reflected bitterly upon his position. Whether he formed a deliberate plan to cure Barbara by giving her a visible image of herself, or whether resentment merely drove him into the same selfishness which he condemned in her, we cannot say. It is certain that his example was not defensible because it was retaliatory. One transgression does not become virtue by putting on the garments of reproof.

Formerly Herman had returned home soon after the closing of his counting-room. Now he came at eight, nine, or ten o’clock. Formerly he had examined every important account and personally supervised each shipment. Now he left much of this labor to young clerks who were pleased with his confidence and careless of its consequences. He joined a commercial club, supped with acquaintances, played cards, and learned to seek in wine that temporary relief from vexation which leaves a heavier vexation behind.

At first Barbara affected indifference.

“You are late,” she said one night.

“I am aware of it.”

“Where have you been?”

“At the club.”

“Again?”

“Again.”

“I should think a married man might find his home sufficiently attractive.”

Herman raised his eyebrows.

“Must I be tied down to a particular place?”

“You have duties here.”

“And I have individuality elsewhere.”

“That is absurd.”

“Is it? I have heard something like it before.”

Barbara bit her lip.

“A business-man who spends every evening in pleasure will soon discover that his affairs suffer.”

“My clerks attend to my affairs.”

“They are careless boys.”

“Then blame the clerks. I cannot be responsible for their misconduct.”

The words struck her with unpleasant familiarity.

“You are mocking me.”

“By no means, Mrs. Uhler. I merely claim the right to a complete existence.”

He went up-stairs, leaving her pale with anger. Yet even then she did not see the whole truth. She perceived the insult in his imitation, but not the justice of the lesson; she saw that he had become negligent, but would not admit that her own negligence had supplied the pattern.

Thus the unhappy contest proceeded. Barbara was absent because Herman stayed away; Herman stayed away because Barbara made home comfortless. Each waited for the other to reform; each counted the other’s offences and forgot his own; each spoke much of rights and little of love. The children, unable to understand principles, understood cold rooms, irregular meals, sharp voices, and absent faces.

The decline in Herman’s business was at first concealed by its former prosperity. A man may live for some time upon the capital of a good reputation; but reputation, like money, is exhausted when constantly drawn upon and never renewed. Orders were entered incorrectly. A valuable consignment was sent to Albany instead of Baltimore. One clerk used funds which did not belong to him, hoping to replace them before discovery. Another neglected an insurance, and a warehouse fire consumed goods for which Mr. Uhler remained liable.

Herman knew enough to be anxious, but pride forbade him to acknowledge the danger. He drank more freely, came home more irregularly, and met Barbara’s complaints with a colder repetition of her own arguments.

“You promised to bring me money to-day,” she said, when the grocer refused further credit.

“I forgot it.”

“We have almost nothing in the house.”

“Susan can manage.”

“Susan has left.”

“Then engage another servant.”

“With what am I to pay her?”

“Really, Barbara, I cannot descend to every detail.”

She looked at him in amazement.

“You call the support of your family a detail?”

“And you called the care of it a degradation.”

The shot was cruelly aimed, and he saw that it wounded; nevertheless, he walked away. A victory in domestic warfare is often the most miserable form of defeat.

Before spring the pleasant house in Mulberry Street was surrendered to creditors. The piano went first, then the silver, then the better furniture. Barbara wept when the cradle in which all three children had slept was carried away; yet tears could not redeem it. The family removed to four small rooms in a narrow court, where sunshine entered briefly at noon and smoke remained all day.

Even now Mrs. Pomeroy and her associates did not forsake Barbara. Indeed, adversity made her case more attractive to them, for they could now point to her faded dress and altered face as evidence of masculine oppression.

“My dear,” said Mrs. Pomeroy, seated one afternoon beside the poor little stove, “this must end.”

Barbara had been mending Harry’s coat. She lifted her tired eyes.

“What must end?”

“Your bondage. Herman Uhler has ruined you.”

“He has ruined himself also.”

“That is nothing. Men always involve innocent women in their failures.”

“He was not always careless,” Barbara said slowly.

“You are defending him!”

“No. I only said he was not always so.”

Mrs. Pomeroy exchanged a significant glance with Miss Lavinia Sharp, another advocate of complete independence.

“This is the fatal weakness of our sex,” said Miss Sharp. “We excuse the hand that crushes us.”

“Herman has never struck me,” said Barbara.

“There are blows which leave no mark upon the flesh.”

“And chains,” added Mrs. Pomeroy, “which make no sound.”

Barbara looked from one solemn face to the other.

“What do you advise?”

“Leave him.”

The words, though not unexpected, produced a shock.

“Leave my husband?”

“Certainly. Take the children, if you choose—though children greatly complicate a woman’s development—and begin an independent life.”

“How am I to support them?”

“There are many careers.”

“Name one for which I am prepared.”

Mrs. Pomeroy hesitated.

“That is not the present question. The first step is freedom.”

“Freedom to starve?”

“My dear Barbara, practical objections are always advanced against great principles.”

Miss Sharp leaned forward.

“Besides, why should you take the children? They are his as much as yours. Let him feel the burden he has imposed upon you.”

“The burden!” Barbara repeated.

“Certainly. Three children, endless household care, no opportunity for self-culture—why, your whole youth has been sacrificed to his selfishness.”

A strange pain moved through Barbara. She looked toward the next room, where Margaret was singing softly to the baby. Was that tender voice a chain? Were those little garments beneath her needle emblems of servitude? Was Harry’s arm, flung so trustingly about her neck in sleep, an instrument of masculine oppression?

Mrs. Pomeroy continued:

“Mr. Uhler is plainly a coarse, unprincipled, intemperate man, without affection, conscience, or honor. He has robbed you, deceived you, and dragged you into poverty. Were I in your place, I would abandon him this very night and never speak his name again.”

“Stop!” cried Barbara.

Her visitors stared.

“You do not know Herman as I know him. He was industrious—patient—kind. For years he denied himself every thing for us. If he is changed—”

She stopped. A dreadful question had risen before her.

“If he is changed?” repeated Miss Sharp.

Barbara stood up.

“I must go home.”

“You are at home,” said Mrs. Pomeroy.

“I mean—I must go to the children.”

She put aside her sewing, wrapped a shawl around her shoulders, and hurried into the street. Her friends had intended to strengthen her resentment; they had, without knowing it, broken the crust of self-justification beneath which conscience had long been imprisoned. Their picture of Herman was so black, so false to the man he had once been, that her memory rose in rebellion. She saw him carrying Harry through a night of fever; saw him returning from work with fruit hidden in his pocket for her; saw him sitting beside the cradle, his strong hand resting gently upon the coverlet. Then another image appeared—Herman alone with the children while she listened to an oration upon maternal independence; Herman humiliated before his guest; Herman asking, almost pleading, for the common comforts of his home.

“What have I done?” she whispered.

The question pursued her through the dusk.

She had left the children with a girl named Betsy, lately engaged because she asked lower wages than a competent servant. As Barbara approached the court, she heard screams. Smoke was rolling from the open window of her rooms.

She flew up the stairs.

Harry stood in the passage, white with terror. Margaret was crying near the door. On the floor lay the baby, wrapped in a smouldering blanket, while a neighbor beat out the sparks with her apron.

“My child!”

Barbara snatched the little one into her arms. A blistered patch showed upon the tender arm, and the hair was singed above one temple.

“How did it happen? Where is Betsy?”

“Gone,” sobbed Margaret. “She went away after dinner. Baby was cold, and Harry put wood on the fire, and it fell out—and—and—”

Barbara sank upon a chair.

The burn was painful but not dangerous. A physician was called; the arm was dressed; the frightened children were soothed. When quiet had in some degree returned, Mrs. Keene, the neighbor who had extinguished the blanket, remained to help put the rooms in order.

“It is a mercy I heard them,” she said. “Five minutes more, and the Lord alone knows what might have happened.”

Barbara shuddered.

“Did Betsy say where she was going?”

“She took her bundle. I doubt she means to return.”

“And I trusted my children to her!”

Mrs. Keene looked at her, but made no answer.

“Has Mr. Uhler been here?” Barbara asked after a time.

“He came in while you were out and asked for the little ones. He looked badly, poor man.”

“What did he say?”

The neighbor hesitated.

“Tell me—every word.”

“Well, ma’am, he saw the rooms as they were, and Betsy scolding the children. He said—though I suppose he was beside himself—‘I wish they had no mother!’”

Barbara covered her face.

“He said more,” continued Mrs. Keene gently. “He said, ‘If they had no mother, I should know they depended upon me, and I might save them. But while she lives, I keep trusting that nature will awaken in her heart.’ Then he went away.”

No accusation that Herman had ever uttered equalled these despairing words. Barbara bent over the injured child, and tears fell upon the little bandaged arm.

“Nature awaken!” she murmured. “Am I, then, so unnatural?”

Conscience answered—not with the extravagant denunciations of Mrs. Pomeroy, but with the calm recital of neglected hours. Who had left the children to ignorance and chance? Who had called personal care servitude? Who had hardened a faithful man by meeting affection with controversy and reasonable expectation with defiance? Herman had sinned—grievously; but his sin did not make hers less. She had demanded that he honor every right belonging to her while she forgot those belonging to him, to the children, and to the helpless life committed to her keeping.

All night Barbara watched beside the baby. Once Herman entered. His step was unsteady, and the odor of wine clung to his clothes; but when he saw the bandage, every trace of intoxication seemed to leave his face.

“What has happened?”

Barbara told him.

He grew deathly pale, knelt beside the child, and touched her cheek.

“My poor little Grace!”

The baby opened her eyes and held out her uninjured hand. Herman caught it and bowed his head.

Barbara waited for reproach. None came. His silence was more terrible than anger.

“Herman,” she said, “do not go out again.”

He rose slowly.

“Why not?”

“Because—I need you.”

“You have friends.”

“Not such friends as I believed.”

“And meetings.”

“I shall attend no meeting to-night.”

He smiled bitterly.

“The occasion is sufficiently important, then?”

She accepted the deserved rebuke.

“Yes,” she answered. “More important than I understood.”

He looked at her with suspicion, as one who fears that hope may be another form of pain.

“I cannot talk now,” he said. “I will remain until morning.”

He lay down without undressing upon a broken lounge, and slept heavily. Barbara kept her vigil; but beneath the anguish of the night, a new purpose was forming—humble, practical, and therefore more valuable than a hundred brilliant resolutions.

At dawn she rose, washed, and put the rooms in order. There was little fuel, scarcely any food, and no money except a few coins in her purse. Yet poverty, when confronted by willing hands, loses something of its terror. Barbara kindled the fire, borrowed a kettle from Mrs. Keene, prepared gruel for the baby, and sent Harry for a small loaf. She brushed the crumbs from the table, warmed the cracked teapot, and contrived from an egg, two slices of ham, and the remains of yesterday’s bread a breakfast which, if not abundant, was at least clean, hot, and inviting.

When Herman awoke, the room was filled with the fragrance of tea and toasted bread. Sunlight, entering through the smoky window, found a polished cup upon the table and reflected itself from that humble surface as cheerfully as if it had fallen upon silver.

He looked about in surprise.

“Where is Betsy?”

“She will not return.”

“Who prepared breakfast?”

“I did.”

“You?”

The single word contained disbelief, reproach, and an old tenderness which he could not wholly suppress.

“Yes, Herman. Come while it is warm.”

He sat at the table. Harry and Margaret, subdued by the previous night’s alarm, watched their parents with solemn eyes. Barbara poured the tea. Her hand trembled, and some fell into the saucer.

“Herman,” she said, “I have been wrong.”

He made no reply.

“I thought only of my claims, my preferences, my liberty. I called every obligation a chain, and every request an act of tyranny. I left to careless hands what no hands but mine could rightly perform. I made your home wretched; and when you became what my conduct taught you to become, I accused you as if I were innocent.”

“Barbara—”

“No; let me speak. You have done wrong also. You know it.”

“I do.”

“But your wrong does not excuse mine, nor mine yours. If we continue measuring guilt, our children will pay the whole account.”

Herman looked toward the cradle.

“What do you ask of me?”

“Nothing this morning—except that you eat, and that you come home to-night.”

“Why?”

“Because I shall be waiting.”

There was no rhetoric in this answer, no declaration of rights, no lofty phrase concerning self-sacrifice. Yet it reached where argument had failed.

“I will come,” he said.

That day began the restoration of the Uhler household.

Let no reader suppose that repentance completed the work in an hour. Tears may wash the eyes, but hands must repair the ruin. Barbara found household labor more difficult than she had remembered and far more dignified than her advisers had represented it. She dismissed no duty as beneath her merely because it was small. She learned that comfort is made of trifles faithfully attended to—of a fire renewed before it dies, a child’s wet shoe removed in time, a pot watched before it boils over, a garment mended ere the rent enlarges. Economy became not a humiliating necessity but an exercise of intelligence. With little money she produced wholesome meals; with poor furniture she created order; with no servant she accomplished more than three servants had performed without direction.

The children changed under her personal care. Harry lost his wild, defiant manner; Margaret’s face grew clean and tranquil; little Grace, recovering from her burn, followed her mother with trusting eyes. The rooms, though narrow, acquired that indefinable atmosphere which expensive upholstery cannot purchase. At evening the lamp was trimmed, the hearth swept, and the kettle simmered with a domestic music more persuasive than the speeches of Mrs. Pomeroy.

Herman came home the first night at seven.

Barbara met him without question.

On the second night he came at six.

On the third, he brought a small package of tea and a wooden horse for Harry. After supper he took Grace upon his knee; and when the child fell asleep against his breast, Barbara saw tears in his eyes.

A week passed without his visiting the club. His hand became steady; the cloud upon his face began to lift.

One evening he said:

“Barbara, I have been offered a position.”

She looked up quickly.

“It is not equal to my former business. The salary is small.”

“Is it honorable work?”

“Yes.”

“Then accept it.”

“You will not be ashamed?”

“Ashamed! Herman, if you sweep a warehouse faithfully, I shall honor you more than if you own it carelessly.”

He leaned across the table and took her hand.

“My brave wife!”

It was the first affectionate name he had given her for many months. Barbara bowed her face upon their joined hands and wept—not with the loud sorrow of wounded pride, but with that tender grief which already contains the beginning of joy.

Herman accepted the position. He entered upon his duties with the energy which had distinguished his earlier years, and adversity, having punished his negligence, now sharpened his judgment. He remained after hours when necessary, examined every account entrusted to him, corrected disorders which older employees had ceased to notice, and won first the confidence, then the friendship, of his employer.

At home he no longer treated domestic comfort as something produced by invisible agencies for his exclusive use. Poverty had taught him also. He brought wood up the narrow stairs, minded the children while Barbara sewed, and on Sundays carried Grace into the sunshine. We must not imagine that because husband and wife had separate responsibilities, they possessed separate interests. Duty had divided their labors; love united their purpose.

Mrs. Pomeroy called once more.

She found Barbara kneading bread, with white flour upon her arms and a song upon her lips. Grace sat safely near the table; Margaret was learning her letters; and a savory broth gave promise of the evening meal.

“My dear,” said the visitor, glancing about with compassionate severity, “I had hoped you would rise above this.”

“Above what?”

“This servile routine.”

Barbara smiled.

“I once thought it servile to perform necessary things for those I loved. I then left them to a servant, who deserted my children beside an open fire.”

“But your individuality!”

“I have not lost it. I have found it useful.”

“Does Mr. Uhler permit you—”

“Herman and I no longer talk much of permission.”

Mrs. Pomeroy stiffened.

“Then you have submitted.”

“No. We have both repented.”

To this answer the “strong-minded” lady could find no immediate reply; and as the bread required Barbara’s attention, the visit soon ended.

Within a year Herman was made manager of the establishment. Two years later he entered the firm as a junior partner. His former creditors, observing his renewed diligence and honorable conduct, offered terms by which the old obligations were gradually discharged. Prosperity returned—not by miracle, although to grateful hearts all honest restoration appears providential, but through industry, economy, mutual confidence, and the good reputation which these things inevitably create.

In time the Uhlers returned to a comfortable house—not the old one in Mulberry Street, nor one chosen to excite envy, but a bright dwelling with trees before it and a broad hearth in the sitting-room. Upon winter evenings Herman might be seen there with a child upon either knee, while Barbara sewed near the lamp. Books were not forbidden her; society was not closed against her; nor was her mind imprisoned because her affections had learned their proper order. She read, thought, visited, and occasionally assisted in works of public usefulness. But she no longer mistook excitement for duty, or imagined that care bestowed upon strangers could atone for care denied to her own.

Herman, on his part, never again claimed comfort without considering the labor by which it was secured. If business required his absence, he explained the necessity; if Barbara was weary, he observed it; and if differences arose—as differences will arise wherever two human wills inhabit one house—they discussed them without converting every preference into a principle and every concession into a defeat.

Years afterward, when Margaret was about to marry, Barbara spoke to her beside the same cheerful hearth.

“My child,” she said, “guard your self-respect, but do not make an idol of self. Ask what is rightfully yours; yet ask more often what is rightfully due from you. The world may praise independence, and independence is good when it means strength to perform duty. But independence which refuses obligation is only selfishness wearing a crown.”

Herman, who had entered unheard, laid his hand upon his wife’s shoulder.

“And tell her,” he added, “that a husband has no privilege to neglect his home because his wife has neglected hers. I tried to teach by retaliation, and almost destroyed what I meant to save.”

Barbara looked up at him.

“We both learned dearly.”

“But we learned together.”

The fire burned clearly; the tea sent up its familiar fragrance; and from another room came the voices of their children, no longer crying in loneliness, but mingling in the happy confusion of a well-ordered home.

Thus comfort returned where duty had returned before it; affection revived where selfish contention had ceased; and prosperity followed, not as the reward of one person’s submission to another, but as the natural fruit of two lives brought again under the law of mutual obligation.

For rights may build a barrier around the individual; duties alone build a home.

Peduzzi waited behind the kitchen yard of the hotel with his hands in the pockets of an old blue military coat. One pocket had no bottom. His fingers went through and touched the lining near his knee. There was rain on the woodpile and the split logs were dark.

He had drunk two grappas before breakfast. The cook had given him the second because he carried out the ashes. It was not much work. He had carried the bucket slowly and afterward stood where the steam came from the kitchen window.

The young gentleman came down the steps carrying two new rods in green cloth cases. He wore a brown hat and a rain cape folded over one arm.

Ready, Peduzzi said.

The gentleman looked toward the hotel door.

My wife is coming.

Good. We go now. Best time.

It is nearly three.

Best time, Peduzzi said. For trout, always best time.

The young wife came through the door. She had no hat. Her fair hair was pinned flat at the sides, and she wore a gray coat and walking shoes with clean soles. She looked at the rods and then at her husband.

You said we were only going for a walk.

We are walking.

With those.

Peduzzi smiled. He gave a small bow.

Signora, very fine trout. Grande. Like this.

He held his hands apart. The sleeves of the coat came down over his knuckles.

She looked at her husband.

I am not carrying anything.

Of course not, he said.

Peduzzi took one of the rod cases and placed it over his shoulder like a rifle. He wished he had both. One was enough for people to see. They went out through the arch into the road.

The clouds were low over the mountains. Snow lay in dirty strips under the pine trees above the town. Water ran in the gutter. Peduzzi walked ahead, greeting everyone.

Buon giorno, dottore.

The man was not a doctor. He sold coal.

Buon giorno, maresciallo.

The policeman at the corner looked at Peduzzi and then at the rod case. He said nothing.

You see, Peduzzi said to the young gentleman. All friends.

Is fishing permitted now?

Peduzzi turned and walked backward.

For me, permitted. I know everyone. Municipio, carabinieri, guardia. No trouble.

I understood there was a closed season.

Closed for strangers. Not with me.

He faced forward again. Two boys watched them from under the roof of a bicycle shop. One boy said something and the other laughed.

What did they say? the wife asked.

Nothing, the gentleman said.

They were speaking about the fishing, Peduzzi said. They know me.

The wife walked on the far side of her husband. At the square the wind came hard between the buildings. Flags over the post office snapped on their wet ropes. Peduzzi stopped outside a wine shop.

We take marsala, he said. Necessary.

We have wine at the hotel.

Hotel wine no good for river. Marsala keeps warm. Also for bait maybe bread.

We have bait?

Peduzzi touched his coat in several places. There was nothing in the pockets except a bent nail and a small cork.

We buy. Very little.

The gentleman took out his purse.

How much?

Four lire. Maybe five.

The wife turned toward the campanile.

You had money, she said.

Not small money.

You gave it away.

The gentleman handed Peduzzi five lire.

Marsala, he said.

And bread, Peduzzi said. Perhaps cheese.

Marsala and bread.

Naturally.

Peduzzi went into the shop. There were three men at the counter. He ordered a half bottle of red wine and drank one glass while it was being corked. Then he ordered a second glass. The woman behind the counter said he must pay first.

I am guiding foreigners, he said.

She held out her hand.

He paid and drank. The wine was thin, but it settled well below the grappa. He bought a heel of bread. There was not enough for cheese. When he came outside, the couple had moved under the awning of a tobacco shop.

Marsala, Peduzzi said, holding up the bottle.

That is red wine, the gentleman said.

Better than marsala today. Weather requires it.

The wife laughed once. It was not a happy laugh.

He spent your money on ordinary wine.

It makes no difference, the gentleman said.

No, she said. That is what you always say.

They began walking again. Peduzzi carried the bottle under his coat and the bread in his hand. He knew a way past the new hotel where they would not meet the gamekeeper. He also knew the gamekeeper had gone to visit his brother in Belluno, or perhaps it was the brother who had come to visit him. In either case there would be no difficulty.

At the edge of town they passed men working on a stone hotel. The men stood on the scaffolding in caps and old jackets. A pulley creaked above them. One called down to Peduzzi.

Where are you taking the English?

To catch their supper.

In the fountain?

The men laughed.

Peduzzi waved.

Friends, he told the gentleman. Always jokes.

We are not English.

All forestieri are English here.

I see.

The road narrowed beside a heap of broken tiles, tins, cabbage leaves, and gray snow. Beyond it the valley opened. The river was not visible, but they could hear it below the road.

The wife stopped.

How far is it?

Twenty minutes, Peduzzi said.

You said twenty minutes before we left.

From here twenty. Before, from hotel, also twenty. Different walking.

I am going back.

Tiny, the gentleman said.

Do not call me that.

Peduzzi examined the bread. He tore off a piece and chewed it.

Come, signora. Wonderful water. Sun soon.

There is no sun.

Soon, Peduzzi said.

The gentleman stepped close to his wife. They spoke in English. Peduzzi understood only his name and the word ridiculous. The wife’s face was white around the mouth.

You wanted to come, the gentleman said finally.

I wanted to walk with you.

We are walking.

With a drunken old man through rubbish.

Peduzzi looked toward the mountains. There was a patch of pale sky beyond the ridge.

Not drunken, he said. Poco. For cold.

She stared at him, then at her husband.

You see? she said.

I see.

And you do nothing.

What do you want me to do?

Nothing. Anything.

She turned and started back toward town.

Tiny.

She did not stop.

Let her go, Peduzzi said. Women do not like fishing when rain.

The gentleman watched until she passed behind the dump heap.

We should go back.

Only ten minutes. River there. We fish one hour. Then hotel. Big trout makes all good.

The gentleman rubbed the side of his face. The wind lifted his hat and he caught it.

Is it truly safe?

Safe? With Peduzzi? Completely. I speak to guard. I say guests of municipio. Important people.

That would make it worse.

No. Better. Everything better.

The gentleman looked at the road behind them. Then he took the second rod case from under his arm and started downhill.

One hour, he said.

Forty minutes, Peduzzi said. Enough.

They left the road by a path between wet alders. Mud clung to their shoes. Peduzzi slipped twice but did not fall. The bottle knocked against the buttons of his coat.

At the bottom was a flat field where last year’s grass lay pressed beneath the rain. The river moved brown and quick between banks of black stones. Branches came down in the current and turned in the eddies.

Good, Peduzzi said. Very good water.

It looks high.

Trout love high water.

He set down the bread and bottle. The gentleman unfastened the cases. The rods were jointed bamboo with bright metal fittings. Peduzzi handled one carefully. It was finer than any rod he had owned. He had once owned two rods, before the bad winter, and a net with a chestnut handle.

Beautiful, he said. English.

French.

English work, Peduzzi said. Best.

The gentleman fitted the sections together. Peduzzi opened a small metal box of hooks, gut leaders, flies, and swivels. Everything was clean and arranged in rows.

Where is piombo? Peduzzi asked.

What?

Piombo. Lead. Small lead.

The gentleman looked through the box.

There are weights here, aren’t there?

No lead. For sink. Water strong.

Can we use a fly?

Not this water. Bait down deep. Must have piombo.

Peduzzi searched the box again. He emptied part of it onto his palm and put everything back.

Perhaps a stone.

No. Bad. Frightens fish.

We came all this way and cannot fish?

Peduzzi looked along the bank. Near the alders was a rusted tin, but it was light. He kicked at the mud. There might be wire or a piece of metal under the rubbish. Nothing showed.

Lead necessary, he said.

The gentleman’s shoulders went down. He looked across the river. On the opposite bank a path ran among the trees.

Does the gamekeeper use that path?

Sometimes.

Peduzzi saw the gentleman look quickly upstream and downstream.

No danger, he said.

The gentleman began taking the rod apart.

We may as well go.

Tomorrow, Peduzzi said. Tomorrow with piombo. Early. Six o’clock. Water lower, trout hungry.

The rain stopped. A band of sun came under the clouds and showed on the wet field. The brown water flashed in places. Peduzzi picked up the bottle.

We drink, he said.

He pulled the cork with his teeth and gave the bottle to the gentleman. The gentleman wiped the mouth with his thumb and drank.

It isn’t marsala.

Good wine.

It is terrible wine.

For river, very good.

The gentleman drank again and passed it back. Peduzzi took a long drink. Gray hairs at the open neck of his coat stirred in the wind.

The sun is here, Peduzzi said. Wonderful day.

The gentleman sat on a stone. His trousers darkened where they touched it.

My wife hates me, he said.

Peduzzi did not understand all the words, but he understood moglie. He nodded.

Women become angry. Then not angry. Trout the same. Today no bite. Tomorrow all bite.

The gentleman laughed.

You are an expert on both?

Many years.

Peduzzi offered the bread. The gentleman broke off a piece.

What do we need tomorrow?

Everything ready, Peduzzi said at once. Lead, worms, cheese, polenta, more wine. Perhaps salami. We leave six. No people. I come hotel.

Not to the hotel.

Where then?

By bridge. Small chapel near bridge.

The gentleman shook his head.

Not chapel. Behind the skating rink.

Good. Six.

Seven, the gentleman said.

Six better.

Seven.

Peduzzi considered it.

At seven sun on water. Still good. I buy all tonight.

How much?

Ten lire.

The gentleman looked at him.

That is a lot for worms and lead.

Also food. We stay long. Trout up to this.

He held his hands apart wider than before.

Five lire.

Eight.

Five.

Peduzzi nodded slowly, as if granting something important.

Five now. Tomorrow, after fish, five more.

The gentleman gave him a five-lire note. It was dry and clean. Peduzzi folded it and placed it inside his shirt, not in the broken pocket.

At seven, Peduzzi said. Behind rink. I bring lead, worms, salami, bread, wine. Everything.

I may not come.

You come. Wonderful fishing.

I said I may not.

Peduzzi smiled.

If signora tired, you come alone.

Probably I won’t come at all.

Yes, Peduzzi said. Seven.

They climbed back toward the road. The gentleman carried both rods. Peduzzi carried the emptying bottle and the bread. The mud was worse going up, but he felt strong. With five lire he could buy lead for half a lira, worms for nothing if he dug behind the stable, bread for one, and a little salami. There would be enough for wine. Perhaps marsala if the shopkeeper gave credit.

At the road the young gentleman stopped.

Listen, Peduzzi. Do not buy too much. I do not think I will come.

No need buy much. Only necessary things.

I’m serious.

Naturally.

They walked past the dump. The hotel builders had gone inside, and the pulley turned loose in the wind. In town the wet paving stones shone. Evening light touched the upper part of the campanile.

At the square the gentleman took the rod case from Peduzzi.

Tomorrow, Peduzzi said.

Perhaps.

Seven.

Perhaps.

The gentleman went toward the hotel without looking back.

Peduzzi stood under the tobacco-shop awning. He touched the note through his shirt. Then he went into the wine shop and ordered a glass.

Pay first, the woman said.

He laid one lira on the counter.

Tomorrow there will be trout, he said. Foreign gentlemen. Very important.

The woman poured the wine.

Peduzzi drank it slowly. He would need to find lead before morning. The plumber near the municipio would have scraps. The stable yard would have worms. He could borrow a basket. If there were trout, everything would be settled.

Outside, the last sun was on the mountain and rain still fell in the square. It was fine weather for tomorrow.

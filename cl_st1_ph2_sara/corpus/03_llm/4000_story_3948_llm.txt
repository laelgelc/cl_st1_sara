## The Finch Method

There are men who fall in love as they fall downstairs—suddenly, noisily, and with no clear recollection afterward of how the thing began. Others approach the emotion as a corporation approaches the erection of a railway terminus. They survey the ground, prepare estimates, appoint subcommittees, and issue a prospectus.

Rollo Finch belonged to the latter class.

He was a pleasant young man, pink, well dressed, and adequately supplied with money. Nature, having bestowed upon him an agreeable disposition and a profile which photographed nicely from the left, had perhaps felt that she had done enough. His brain was not so much an organ as a small, tastefully furnished apartment which he seldom visited. But Rollo had no suspicion of this. Like many men whose thoughts are few, he valued them enormously.

On the morning when this story begins, he was seated in the sitting-room of his flat in Half Moon Street with a sheet of foolscap before him. He had ruled the paper into columns and decorated it with arrows, stars, and Roman numerals. At intervals he sucked the end of a pencil and smiled the quiet smile of one who is rendering civilization a service.

Wilson, his valet, entered with a tray.

“Breakfast, sir.”

“Put it down, Wilson. I’m busy.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“Brain work.”

“Yes, sir.”

Wilson placed the tray upon the table. He was a lean, self-contained man of thirty or thereabouts, with a face upon which professional discretion had laid a permanent glaze. Earthquakes, revolutions, and the discovery of a hippopotamus in his master’s bath would have drawn from him, at most, a slight elevation of the left eyebrow.

Rollo flourished the foolscap.

“Want to know what this is?”

“If you wish to tell me, sir.”

“It’s a schedule.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“A courtship schedule.”

Wilson’s hand, which had been adjusting a fork, paused for the fraction of a second. To the untrained observer the movement would have conveyed nothing. A skilled student of valets might have detected emotion.

Rollo leaned back.

“You remember Miss Marguerite Parker?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Pretty girl in the chorus at the Regal.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Blue eyes. Fair hair. Small chin. Laughs as if somebody had just told her that the income tax had been abolished.”

“I recall Miss Parker, sir.”

“Well, I’m going to marry her.”

“Indeed, sir?”

Rollo was slightly disappointed. When a man announces his approaching marriage, he likes the world to sway visibly. Wilson merely moved the marmalade nearer the toast.

“You don’t seem surprised.”

“No, sir.”

“Why not?”

“I had observed that you had attended *The Girl from Granada* on eleven occasions.”

“Twelve.”

“Just so, sir.”

“And you guessed?”

“It occurred to me as a possibility.”

Rollo tapped the paper.

“The whole thing is practically settled.”

“Has Miss Parker consented, sir?”

“Not yet. That comes in Week Six.”

“Week Six, sir?”

“Exactly. You see what most fellows do wrong, Wilson? They rush things. They go charging in, proposing before the girl has had time to form a proper estimate of their qualities. Fatal. Women have to be led up to a fellow gradually.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“Scientific courtship. That’s my idea.”

He turned the foolscap so that Wilson could inspect it.

“Week One: flowers, anonymously.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Week Two: flowers with card.”

“Very good, sir.”

“Week Three: chocolates and light correspondence.”

“Light, sir?”

“Nothing emotional. Wit, you know. Anecdotes. A few bright thoughts about the weather.”

“I understand.”

“Week Four: lunches. Week Five: theatres and suppers. Week Six: steady pressure, ending with proposal on Friday.”

“Why Friday, sir?”

Rollo looked at him with the indulgence which great organizers reserve for minor employees.

“Because Friday gives her the week-end to think it over.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Though, of course, there’ll be nothing to think over. Still, it shows consideration. The system is practically flawless.”

Wilson glanced at the schedule.

“It appears comprehensive, sir.”

“That’s the word. Comprehensive. You don’t believe in rushing a woman, do you?”

“In Market Bumpstead, sir, the procedure was somewhat different.”

Rollo sighed.

Wilson had been born in Market Bumpstead, a small town in the eastern counties which he mentioned in moments of philosophical retrospect. According to him, Market Bumpstead was a place where servants were honest, beer was beer, trains arrived when advertised, and the human heart conducted its affairs with a simplicity unknown in London.

“What did you do in Market Bumpstead?”

“A gentleman who admired a young lady generally told her so, sir.”

“At once?”

“Usually, sir.”

“Good heavens! Without groundwork?”

“Without much groundwork, sir.”

“No schedules?”

“No, sir.”

Rollo shook his head compassionately.

“That might do in Market Thingummy, but not in London. A modern girl expects finesse.”

“Possibly, sir.”

“Of course she does. Look at advertising. You don’t see a successful firm saying, ‘Here’s our soap. Buy it.’ They create atmosphere. They establish confidence. They prepare the public mind.”

“And then propose on Friday, sir?”

“Precisely.”

He beamed upon Wilson. Rollo was fond of Wilson. He often felt that the man’s conversation, though lacking in sparkle, offered admirable opportunities for Rollo himself to shine.

“You ought to marry, Wilson.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Settles a man. Gives him an interest in life.”

“I was once engaged, sir.”

Rollo lowered his coffee-cup.

“You? Engaged?”

“Yes, sir.”

“What happened?”

“There was a misunderstanding.”

“What sort of misunderstanding?”

“A personal one, sir.”

“But what about?”

“The matter was private.”

Rollo frowned. He considered privacy a form of selfishness. If a man possessed an interesting story, common humanity demanded that he place it in circulation.

“Did she throw you over?”

“No, sir.”

“You threw her over?”

“No, sir.”

“Then what happened?”

“We ceased to correspond.”

“But why?”

Wilson’s features acquired an additional coat of varnish.

“As I mentioned, sir, there was a misunderstanding.”

Rollo regarded him sadly.

“That’s where your Market Bumpstead methods lead. No system. No organization. You go in with a rush, and the next thing you know there’s a misunderstanding and everybody stops corresponding. Contrast that with this.”

He lifted the schedule.

“Week One begins today.”

“Very good, sir.”

“I want two dozen roses sent to Miss Parker at the Regal Theatre. No card.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And keep this carefully. It’s the master plan.”

“I shall place it in the left-hand drawer of your desk, sir.”

“Splendid. In six weeks, Wilson, I shall be engaged.”

“I hope so, sir.”

Rollo attacked a kidney with the confidence of a man whose future has been reduced to columns and dates.

We all make schedules. Some of us call them resolutions, some budgets, and some five-year plans. They have this in common: the universe reads them with interest and then does something else.

* * *

Andrew Galloway was Rollo’s uncle on his mother’s side, and from a financial point of view he was an uncle of the finest quality.

He had made a large fortune in Pittsburgh out of a device known as the Tried and Proven Self-Regulating Trouser Support. Before Mr. Galloway’s invention, the American trouser had depended for its position upon methods crude, fitful, and unworthy of a great republic. Galloway had changed all that. From Maine to California, men who had once moved nervously through life now strode with security, upheld by Tried and Proven.

The contemplation of this achievement had produced in Andrew Galloway a deep sense of moral worth. He had not merely accumulated millions. He had rendered a public service.

Rollo expected eventually to inherit the bulk of these millions. It was his profession. Other young men rose early and went to offices; Rollo preserved his health and waited for Uncle Andrew. He performed the duties of expectancy with care, remembering birthdays, listening to Pittsburgh anecdotes, and never contradicting his uncle except when too excited to help it.

Two days after the drafting of the schedule, Andrew Galloway arrived in London and summoned his nephew to luncheon at his club.

The club was one of those establishments in which sound dies at the threshold. Its hall had the sanctity of a cathedral, the temperature of an unsuccessful Turkish bath, and the furniture of a dentist who has prospered but remains conservative. Members sat in leather chairs looking as if they had been stored there by mistake.

Galloway dominated the dining-room.

He was a large man with a large head, large hands, and a voice which had been trained to cross factory floors while machinery was in action. His waistcoat preceded him into a room like a herald. He ate with the concentration of a man who knew that meals, like business opportunities, must be grasped.

“Rollo!” he boomed.

A bishop at the next table dropped his spoon.

“Hallo, Uncle Andrew.”

“You’re late.”

“Only eight minutes.”

“In Pittsburgh a man can make eight thousand dollars in eight minutes.”

“Can he really? Dashed exhausting place.”

Galloway inspected him sternly, then returned to his soup.

“What are you doing with yourself?”

“Oh, this and that.”

“What this?”

“Well—things.”

“What that?”

“Other things.”

Galloway grunted. He had long believed that Rollo’s principal occupation was avoiding nouns.

“You ought to work.”

“I’m thinking of writing a book.”

“About what?”

“I haven’t got as far as that.”

“Then you haven’t started.”

“Oh, I’ve bought the paper.”

The arrival of a turbot spared Galloway the necessity of replying. For some minutes he attended to it with grave intensity. Then, leaning back, he breathed heavily and fixed Rollo with an eye that had caused strong men in conference rooms to revise their estimates.

“Rollo, I’m going to get married.”

The words struck Rollo at a vulnerable point. His fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

“What!”

“Married.”

“But you can’t.”

Galloway’s eyebrows descended.

“Why not?”

“Well, you’re—”

Rollo paused. There are remarks which, though logically sound, lose value when spoken to rich uncles. He had been about to say “old.”

“You’re so busy,” he concluded.

“I’ve retired.”

“You said you’d never marry again.”

“I changed my mind.”

“But you told me woman was a distraction invented to undermine the commercial man.”

“I was wrong.”

“You said romance was a softening of the brain.”

“I was wrong.”

“You said the heart should be held in check by the firm braces of self-control.”

Andrew Galloway looked annoyed. The phrase had been his, and until three nights ago he had considered it excellent. For twenty years he had worn emotional restraint as proudly as the public wore Tried and Proven. The trousers of passion, if they existed at all, had been kept rigidly in place.

Now the braces had snapped.

“I’ve met someone,” he said.

Rollo swallowed.

“Who?”

“Girl at the Regal Theatre.”

The bishop at the next table, who had resumed his soup, dropped the spoon again.

Rollo felt the room grow misty.

“Not—not *The Girl from Granada*?”

“That’s the show.”

“What’s her name?”

“Marguerite Parker.”

There are moments when the human system, unable to express itself through ordinary channels, seeks relief in some involuntary action. Rollo put an entire bread roll into his water glass.

Galloway stared.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“You put bread in your water.”

“Did I? I thought it was the butter.”

“Why would you put bread in the butter?”

Rollo removed the roll.

“Absent-mindedness. Literary fellows are like that.”

Galloway’s expression suggested that he found the literary life increasingly unattractive.

“When did you meet her?” Rollo asked.

“Monday. Went behind after the show with a man who knows the manager. We had supper.”

“Just you and Miss Parker?”

“There were others.”

“And you’ve seen her since?”

“Every day.”

Rollo’s mind turned slowly but desperately. His beautiful six-week campaign had been proceeding for forty-eight hours. During that time the opposing army, scorning reconnaissance and supply lines, had occupied the central position and ordered dinner.

“What are your plans?” he asked faintly.

“I’m dining with her tomorrow. I shall propose.”

“Tomorrow!”

“Why wait?”

Why wait? The question was barbarous. It swept aside all those finer things for which Rollo stood: atmosphere, confidence, anonymous roses, light correspondence. It was Market Bumpstead with a Pittsburgh accent.

Galloway took a vigorous mouthful of turbot.

“She’s the finest girl in London. Pretty, sensible, no nonsense. I’ve wasted enough time. A man who knows his own mind acts.”

Rollo gazed at his uncle’s waistcoat. It seemed to advance upon him across the table, bearing banners.

A terrible vision appeared before him. He saw Marguerite as Mrs. Andrew Galloway. He saw little Galloways arriving in battalions, each equipped by heredity with a booming voice and an appetite. He saw the fortune which had hovered above him like a benevolent cloud descending upon strangers. The profession of heir presumptive is precarious. Its practitioners depend entirely upon other people continuing to behave as expected, and other people so rarely do.

“Are you sure she likes you?”

Galloway laughed. The glasses trembled.

“She’ll like being Mrs. Andrew Galloway.”

“Girls aren’t always influenced by money.”

“Who said anything about money? I’ve got personality.”

Rollo looked at him. This was undeniable. Andrew Galloway had enough personality for three men, provided they were not particular about the kind.

“Tomorrow, eh?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Suppose she refuses?”

“She won’t.”

“But suppose.”

Galloway speared a potato.

“Then I ask her again Friday.”

Rollo perceived that the situation called for action. The comfortable express train of his courtship had been proceeding according to timetable through pleasant country, and now a porter had leaned through the window to mention that the bridge was down.

He excused himself before dessert.

“Where are you going?”

“Important appointment.”

“Business?”

“In a sense.”

“What business?”

Rollo considered.

“Floristry,” he said.

* * *

He reached the flat at three minutes past two and shouted for Wilson before the front door had closed.

“Wilson!”

“Yes, sir?”

Wilson emerged from the bedroom carrying a coat-brush.

“The schedule!”

“Sir?”

“Produce it. Tear it up. Burn it. Dance on the ashes.”

“Very good, sir.”

“No, wait. Don’t burn it yet. I may need to refer to it.”

Wilson opened the left-hand drawer and handed over the foolscap.

Rollo spread it upon the table. Its calm columns mocked him. According to the document, he was still in the anonymous-flower phase and would not be entitled to speak affectionately until the following Thursday fortnight. Meanwhile Andrew Galloway intended to propose tomorrow.

“Wilson, the position is dashed serious.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“My uncle is in love with Miss Parker.”

For the second time that week, Wilson’s hand paused. This time he was holding the coat-brush, and two bristles fell silently to the carpet.

“He intends to marry her,” Rollo continued.

“I see, sir.”

“No, you don’t. If Uncle Andrew marries, what happens to me?”

“I could not say, sir.”

“I’ll tell you. Ruin. Penury. Work.”

“A distressing prospect, sir.”

“I may have to go to an office.”

Wilson inclined his head in sympathy.

“And that isn’t all. I love her.”

“Yes, sir.”

“So we have to accelerate.”

“Accelerate, sir?”

“Compress six weeks into twenty-four hours.”

Wilson looked at the schedule.

“That would appear difficult.”

“Not if we combine the stages. Flowers, card, chocolates, correspondence, lunch, theatre, supper, proposal. We administer them simultaneously.”

“Rather like a tonic, sir?”

“Exactly. A concentrated emotional tonic.”

Rollo strode about the room.

“Get on the telephone. Order four dozen roses. No—six dozen. A girl can resist two dozen roses, but six dozen create a sort of jungle effect. Also orchids.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And chocolates. The large gold box.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And a bracelet.”

Wilson’s eyebrow moved.

“From where, sir?”

“Bensons. Something refined but unmistakably expensive. Diamonds. Not enormous diamonds, because that might seem vulgar. Medium diamonds. The kind that suggest a fellow has deep feelings and an excellent account.”

“Very good, sir.”

“Everything is to go to Miss Parker at once.”

“Would you wish me to deliver the flowers personally?”

“Yes. No. Yes, better. Florists are careless. A crisis like this needs a man on the spot.”

“Just so, sir.”

“And tell her I’m giving a little supper tonight at the Carlton. Midnight. Say I implore her to come.”

“Would a note not be preferable?”

Rollo seized paper.

“Of course. A note. Light correspondence becomes heavy correspondence under emergency conditions.”

He sat and wrote:

> My dear Miss Parker,  
> Circumstances have arisen which make it essential that I should see you without delay. Please come to supper at the Carlton tonight after the performance. I shall be waiting from eleven-thirty onward. Do not fail me. There is much at stake.  
> Yours with profoundest admiration,  
> Rollo Finch.

He read it aloud.

“How does that strike you?”

“It conveys urgency, sir.”

“Romantic?”

“Somewhat, sir.”

“Not too romantic?”

“No, sir.”

“Good. Give it to her personally. And, Wilson—”

“Sir?”

“If you happen to see my uncle hanging about, trip him.”

“Trip him, sir?”

“Discreetly.”

“I fear that might attract notice.”

“Then engage him in conversation.”

“On what subject?”

“Braces. He’ll talk about braces until the theatre closes.”

Wilson took the note.

“Very good, sir.”

He had reached the door when Rollo called him back.

“Wilson.”

“Yes, sir?”

“What would you do?”

“In what respect?”

“If you loved a girl and another man was going to propose tomorrow?”

Wilson was silent for a moment.

“I should tell her at once, sir.”

“Market Bumpstead.”

“Yes, sir.”

“No subtlety.”

“No, sir.”

“No schedule.”

“No, sir.”

Rollo smiled tolerantly, though his smile was not quite as robust as it had been at breakfast two days earlier.

“Well, off you go. Deliver the flowers. I’ll handle the subtle part.”

“At the Carlton, sir?”

“At the Carlton.”

Wilson departed.

It was then twenty minutes past two. Between that hour and eleven-thirty, Rollo experienced the peculiar expansion of time known only to lovers, prisoners awaiting verdict, and men who have ordered dinner too early.

He went to his club, left it, visited a tailor, forgot why he had gone, returned home, changed his tie four times, and composed several proposals. The first was eloquent but sounded like a speech at the opening of a public library. The second was brief but, on rereading, appeared to demand payment of a debt. The third began, “Marguerite, life is a sea,” and stranded almost immediately.

At seven, Wilson had not returned.

At eight, there was still no Wilson.

At nine, Rollo rang the Regal Theatre. A gentleman in the box office said Miss Parker was in the building but could not be disturbed. Asked whether six dozen roses had arrived, he replied that the stage door resembled Covent Garden and he was not in a position to identify individual consignments.

At ten-thirty, Rollo dressed for supper.

At eleven, he left for the Carlton, carrying in his breast pocket the bracelet which, owing to some confusion at Bensons, had been delivered to the flat instead of the theatre.

He felt nervous, but hopeful. There is a point in every crisis when the human mind, exhausted by anxiety, begins to manufacture optimism as a kind of emergency ration. Rollo pictured Marguerite entering the restaurant, overwhelmed by roses and jewelry. He would rise. She would smile. He would say something simple and manly. She would accept. Andrew Galloway would be informed in the morning and would bear the blow badly but, one hoped, without altering his will.

At eleven-thirty, Rollo was seated at a corner table.

At eleven-forty-five, he was still seated.

At midnight, he ordered oysters.

At twelve-fifteen, he explained to the waiter that the lady had been detained.

At twelve-thirty, he ordered soup in order to preserve the table.

At twelve-forty-five, the waiter began to treat him with the respectful tenderness accorded to the bereaved.

At one, Rollo ate a cutlet which he did not want.

A supper restaurant after midnight is a cruel place for a man waiting alone. Every table seems populated by persons whose friends have arrived with almost indecent punctuality. Laughter breaks out on all sides. Corks emerge from bottles like artillery fire celebrating somebody else’s victory. Even the orchestra, which under ordinary circumstances merely makes conversation difficult, appears to be playing a personal commentary.

At one-fifteen, Rollo sent the waiter to the entrance.

“No lady has asked for you, sir.”

“She may have asked for Mr. Finch.”

“No, sir.”

“Or Mr. R. Finch.”

“No, sir.”

“Or possibly Lord Finch.”

“Are you Lord Finch, sir?”

“No. But she might have got confused.”

“No lady has inquired for any Finch, sir.”

At one-thirty, the orchestra packed its instruments.

At one-forty, a man turned off half the lights.

At one-forty-five, Rollo paid the bill.

Hope is a sturdy little plant, but even it requires occasional watering. Rollo’s had now become a brown stalk in a cracked pot. He left the Carlton with the bracelet in his pocket and the sensation that every taxi-driver in London knew what had happened.

He walked home.

The night was soft and clear. The moon hung over Piccadilly with the bland detachment of a relative who has advised against the whole affair. Rollo moved beneath it heavily.

His thoughts were dark.

Marguerite had rejected him. She had accepted Andrew Galloway. There could be no other explanation. At that very moment she was probably discussing wedding arrangements with his uncle. Perhaps Galloway had proposed after the performance. Perhaps she had said yes before Rollo’s roses had finished arriving. Perhaps she had shown him the note, and they had laughed.

The vision grew.

He saw himself turned out of Half Moon Street. He saw the motorcar sold, the clubs resigned, the tailor requiring cash. He saw himself behind a desk, answering correspondence about goods. He did not know what goods, but they were unquestionably disagreeable.

A policeman observed him with concern.

“Feeling all right, sir?”

“No.”

“Can you get home?”

“I suppose so.”

“Better do that, sir.”

It was advice of a Market Bumpstead simplicity, and Rollo followed it.

* * *

He reached the flat shortly after two.

A light showed beneath the sitting-room door. From within came the sound of voices and, more remarkable still, laughter.

Rollo stopped in the hall.

The laughter was feminine.

For one bright instant hope flared again. Marguerite had missed him at the Carlton and come to the flat. She was waiting. All could yet be explained. He straightened his tie, smoothed his hair, and entered with a smile which he had intended to be wistful but which, owing to emotional strain, became slightly idiotic.

Three people were in the room.

Marguerite Parker stood by the fireplace. She was small and fair, with bright eyes and the composed air of a young woman who knew what she wanted and had recently obtained it. Beside her stood Wilson.

The third person was the remains of a supper laid upon the table.

Rollo stared.

“Miss Parker!”

“Good evening, Mr. Finch.”

“Wilson!”

“Good evening, sir.”

“What are you doing here?”

“I live here, sir.”

“I know you live here. I mean—what’s all this?”

Marguerite smiled.

“We were waiting to tell you.”

“Tell me what?”

Wilson cleared his throat. It was the first thoroughly human noise Rollo had ever heard him make.

“Miss Parker and I were married this evening, sir.”

Silence entered the room and sat down.

Rollo looked at Wilson. He looked at Marguerite. He looked at Wilson again, as if hoping that on the second inspection the man might turn into somebody else.

“Married?”

“Yes, sir.”

“To each other?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Tonight?”

“Yes, sir.”

Rollo sank into a chair.

The human brain, even when not lavishly equipped, can deal with astonishing quantities of information if given time. Rollo’s, however, had received the equivalent of a grand piano through the letter-box.

“But you—she—I—flowers—Carlton—”

“Would you like a whisky-and-soda, sir?” asked Wilson.

“Yes. No. Make it large.”

Wilson performed the operation.

Marguerite sat opposite Rollo.

“I’m afraid you must think me very rude for not coming to supper.”

“My dear Miss Parker, I’ve gone beyond thinking. I’m in an area where thought doesn’t seem to function. Wilson?”

“Sir?”

“You married Miss Parker.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You were supposed to deliver flowers.”

“I did so, sir.”

“Then how did marriage break out?”

Wilson handed him the glass.

“It is a somewhat long story, sir.”

“I’ve had a long evening.”

Marguerite laughed.

“You see, Mr. Finch, Henry and I knew each other before.”

“Henry?”

“Wilson,” she explained.

Rollo looked wounded.

“Your name is Henry?”

“Yes, sir.”

“You never told me.”

“You never inquired, sir.”

Rollo drank.

“And you knew Miss Parker?”

“We were engaged, sir.”

The whisky went the wrong way. For some moments Rollo was occupied with coughing.

“The misunderstanding?” he said at last.

“Yes, sir.”

Marguerite’s expression became momentarily severe.

“It was all Henry’s fault.”

Wilson bowed.

“Yes, Marguerite.”

“He saw me speaking to a gentleman outside the theatre in Norwich and assumed I was flirting with him.”

“The gentleman had his arm about your waist.”

“He was my cousin Bert.”

“I was not aware of that.”

“You never asked.”

“I considered the evidence conclusive.”

“And instead of speaking sensibly, he sent me back my letters.”

“You returned mine.”

“Naturally.”

Wilson bowed again.

“Just so.”

Rollo looked from one to the other.

“How long ago was this?”

“Two years,” said Marguerite.

“And you haven’t communicated since?”

“No.”

“Then today Wilson walked in under six dozen roses?”

“That describes it rather well.”

“What happened?”

Marguerite leaned back.

“At first I was furious.”

“She struck me with the flowers, sir,” said Wilson.

“Only once.”

“With the larger bouquet.”

“You deserved it.”

“Yes, Marguerite.”

Rollo passed a hand over his forehead.

“Proceed.”

“Then Henry explained that he had been an idiot.”

“I believe the phrase was ‘suspicious beast,’ sir.”

“And I explained about Bert.”

“After which,” said Wilson, “the misunderstanding was removed.”

“In the stage-door passage?”

“Initially, sir.”

“And you decided to get married?”

“After tea.”

Rollo blinked.

“After tea?”

“The Market Bumpstead method, sir.”

“Where did you find anywhere open for marriages?”

“Henry knew a clergyman,” said Marguerite.

“A gentleman formerly curate at Market Bumpstead,” Wilson explained. “He is now attached to a church in Bloomsbury. The legal requirements had already been attended to some time ago.”

“What do you mean, already?”

Wilson permitted himself the faintest suggestion of embarrassment.

“I had never quite abandoned hope, sir.”

There are occasions when the superiority of another man becomes so gross that resentment is impossible. Rollo contemplated Wilson as a minor general might contemplate Napoleon unexpectedly revealed beneath the uniform of a footman.

“You carried the special licence about with you?”

“Not precisely, sir. But it was obtainable.”

“And you got married between the evening performance and two o’clock?”

“Yes, sir.”

“What about the performance?”

“I don’t appear in the last act,” said Marguerite. “Nobody notices. They’re all watching the fountain.”

Rollo took another drink.

“But why come here?”

“We wished to tell you,” said Wilson. “And to thank you.”

“Thank me?”

“For sending me with the flowers, sir.”

The words stirred painful memories.

“Those flowers were from me.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I was courting Miss Parker.”

“I gathered as much, sir.”

“I had a schedule.”

“Yes, sir.”

“A comprehensive schedule.”

“Extremely, sir.”

Marguerite looked interested.

“What schedule?”

Rollo hesitated. Plans which appear splendid in the morning sometimes develop shyness after midnight. Nevertheless, honour compelled him to explain.

“Six weeks,” he said. “Flowers, chocolates, lunches, theatres, and so forth. Gradual approach. Proposal on Friday.”

Marguerite considered this.

“Which Friday?”

“Friday week after next.”

“I see.”

“What do you mean, you see?”

“I should probably have married Henry before then.”

Rollo winced.

Wilson coughed discreetly.

“Sir, there is a further matter.”

“What now?”

“Mr. Galloway.”

At the name, the icy waters of practical concern closed over Rollo once more.

“Uncle Andrew! Good Lord! He proposes tomorrow.”

“So Miss Parker informed me, sir.”

Marguerite frowned.

“He has been very kind, but really, he’s absurd.”

Rollo sat upright.

“You wouldn’t have married him?”

“Of course not.”

“Not even with all his money?”

“My dear Mr. Finch!”

“I only asked.”

“He talks while eating asparagus. Besides, I love Henry.”

Wilson’s professional mask trembled at the edges.

Rollo stared at them.

Gradually a new train of thought pulled out of the station.

Marguerite had not married Andrew Galloway.

She could not now marry Andrew Galloway.

Andrew Galloway’s millions were safe from matrimonial diversion.

The expected inheritance, recently seen vanishing into a mist, returned like a liner entering harbour.

At the same time, Marguerite—whom Rollo had sincerely admired but had known for less than a month—was no longer a problem requiring flowers, correspondence, lunches, theatres, suppers, and a Friday proposal. She had been removed from the schedule by lawful authority.

The more Rollo examined the situation, the brighter it became.

A smile spread over his face.

“Wilson, old scout.”

“Sir?”

“You’re a genius.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“You’ve saved me.”

“I am glad, sir.”

“You’ve saved Uncle Andrew too, though he won’t know it.”

“Very possibly, sir.”

“And you’ve saved Miss Parker.”

Marguerite raised her eyebrows.

“From what?”

Rollo considered saying “the schedule,” but checked himself.

“From uncertainty.”

“I wasn’t uncertain.”

“Exactly. All the better.”

He sprang up and began to pace the room, buoyancy returning to his step.

“This is magnificent. Uncle Andrew arrives tomorrow, swelling with proposal, and I tell him the bird has flown.”

“I’d rather you didn’t call me a bird,” said Marguerite.

“Figure of speech. I tell him the position is closed. Applications no longer received. He’ll be upset, of course, but grief passes. Money remains.”

Marguerite looked at Wilson.

“Is he always like this?”

“Not always, Marguerite.”

“I’ve had a trying night,” said Rollo with dignity.

His hand encountered the bracelet in his pocket.

“By George!”

He drew out the case and opened it. The diamonds glittered with the innocent cheerfulness of objects which have caused no trouble and expect none.

“This was for you,” he said to Marguerite.

“It’s beautiful.”

“Wedding present.”

Marguerite looked doubtful.

“I couldn’t.”

“You must. Absolutely. I insist. I bought it for you, and returning things is a nuisance. There are forms.”

He placed the case in her hands.

“And the chocolates. Where are the chocolates?”

“On the sideboard, sir,” said Wilson.

“Wedding present.”

“You’ve already given us one.”

“Then the chocolates are for Wilson.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“And the roses?”

“They are in the kitchen, the hall, the bathroom, and part of the bedroom, sir.”

“Take them. Take everything. Consider the flat looted.”

“That is most generous, sir.”

“Not at all. I owe you a great deal. You have no conception what office work is like.”

“No, sir.”

“Neither have I, and I want to keep it that way.”

Marguerite fastened the bracelet about her wrist. For a moment her expression softened.

“You’re being very sweet, Mr. Finch.”

Rollo flushed pleasantly. One cannot be called sweet by a pretty woman without feeling that, schedule or no schedule, the campaign has yielded some decoration.

“I say, though,” he said. “Where will you live?”

“I had considered taking a small house, sir,” said Wilson. “If you have no objection, I should wish to leave your service.”

“Objection? My dear fellow, after this you can leave, stay, stand on your head—anything you like. Though I shall miss you.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“Who will look after me?”

“I know an excellent man, sir.”

“Market Bumpstead?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Married?”

“No, sir.”

“Good. I don’t want this happening every week.”

They drank champagne left over from the improvised supper. Rollo proposed the health of the bride and bridegroom in a speech which began badly, lost its way in the middle, but finished strongly on the subject of inheritance. Marguerite kissed him on the cheek. Wilson shook his hand with a warmth which, in a less controlled man, might have become enthusiasm.

At three o’clock the newly married pair departed.

Rollo stood in the doorway watching them descend the stairs. Wilson carried the chocolates under one arm and an enormous bouquet under the other. Marguerite’s diamonds winked beneath the hall light.

“Wilson,” Rollo called.

The valet turned.

“Sir?”

“That Market Bumpstead business.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Telling a girl at once.”

“Yes, sir.”

“No groundwork.”

“No, sir.”

“No schedule.”

“No, sir.”

Rollo nodded gravely.

“There may be something in it.”

“I have sometimes thought so, sir.”

“Still, my system had results.”

Wilson glanced at his wife.

“Undoubtedly, sir.”

Rollo closed the door and returned to the sitting-room.

On the table lay the courtship schedule. He picked it up and read the first entry:

**Week One: Establish an atmosphere of mystery.**

Rollo considered the events of the evening. Mystery, at any rate, had not been lacking.

He tore the paper in half, then paused. It was, after all, an important historical document, and the reverse side was blank. He placed it carefully back in the drawer.

The next morning Andrew Galloway telephoned at nine.

“Rollo!”

Even through several miles of wire, the voice caused a vase to vibrate.

“Hallo, Uncle Andrew.”

“I’ve had bad news.”

“Really?”

“Miss Parker has married somebody.”

“No!”

“A valet.”

“You astonish me.”

“Your valet.”

“This is extraordinary.”

“Do you know anything about it?”

Rollo gazed at the roses crowding the hall, the discarded ribbon from the chocolate box, and a champagne cork resting in his coffee-cup.

“Not a thing,” he said.

Galloway breathed like a furnace.

“Women!”

“Quite.”

“Romance!”

“Exactly.”

“Distraction invented to undermine the commercial man!”

“You’ve always said so.”

There was a pause.

“I sail for Pittsburgh Saturday,” said Galloway. “I’ve had enough of London.”

“Very wise.”

“A man’s place is in business.”

“Absolutely.”

“I’m considering a new model of support. Something with reinforced lateral webbing.”

“Topping.”

“You should come into the firm.”

The smile faded from Rollo’s face.

“I shouldn’t like to interfere with your methods.”

“You wouldn’t.”

“That’s what I was afraid of.”

“What?”

“Nothing. Bad line. Goodbye, Uncle Andrew.”

He replaced the receiver and rang for the temporary valet.

As he waited, his eye fell upon the drawer containing the schedule. For a moment he meditated upon human cleverness, a subject which offers endless entertainment provided we examine chiefly the cleverness of others.

Rollo had planned for six weeks and achieved nothing he intended. Wilson had delivered one bouquet and acquired a wife. Andrew Galloway had discarded twenty years of philosophy and recovered it inside three days. Marguerite had avoided two unsuitable proposals by accepting the one suitable man before either could be made.

The facts seemed to point toward a conclusion.

When the temporary valet entered, Rollo was still thinking.

“You rang, sir?”

“Yes. What’s your name?”

“Meadows, sir.”

“From Market Bumpstead?”

“Yes, sir.”

Rollo looked at him with new respect.

“Meadows, if you ever fall in love, what will you do?”

The man seemed surprised.

“I suppose I should tell the young lady, sir.”

“At once?”

“I expect so, sir.”

“No flowers first?”

“Perhaps a small bunch, sir.”

“No six-week schedule?”

“No, sir.”

Rollo sighed.

“Very well. Bring breakfast.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And, Meadows.”

“Sir?”

“If I ever start ruling foolscap into columns again, stop me.”

“Very good, sir.”

Rollo went to the window. Below, London hurried through its morning business without a schedule worth mentioning. Taxis darted, pedestrians crossed where they should not, and somewhere in the great city Henry Wilson and his bride were beginning married life with six dozen roses, a gold box of chocolates, and a diamond bracelet supplied by the Finch Method.

Rollo smiled.

The system, he felt, had merely been misunderstood.

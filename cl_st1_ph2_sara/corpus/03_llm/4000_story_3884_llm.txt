## The Closed Berth

Until the doctors sent Daniel Easton home to die, Marion had believed that unhappiness must have some dramatic cause: infidelity, poverty, a slammed door, a letter found where no letter should have been. She had not imagined that two people might be separated by the slow accumulation of necessary tendernesses, by broth held patiently to unwilling lips, by pillows shaken at two in the morning, by the daily question of whether the window might be raised an inch and the daily answer that it must not.

Illness had not made Daniel cruel. That would have been simpler. It had made him grateful.

His gratitude met her wherever she turned. It lay in the look he gave her when she warmed his milk; it spoke in the weak pressure of his fingers after she buttoned his shirt; it followed her from the sickroom into the bright Colorado streets, where she walked too rapidly, filling her lungs with the cold, thin air as if health were an illicit pleasure. He thanked her for every small service until kindness itself became a debt she could never finish paying.

At first she had protested—“Don’t be absurd, Dan; what else should I do?”—and had kissed him with deliberate cheerfulness. Later she merely smiled. Later still, she sometimes pretended not to hear.

They had gone west because the specialist had said altitude, dryness, rest. The words had shone before them like three immaculate stepping-stones across a black stream. On the farther bank Daniel would be well again: broad-shouldered, impatient, amused by her extravagance, able to lift both suitcases at once. They had gone west in April. In November the specialist, no longer speaking of altitude, had advised them to return to New York while the journey remained possible.

“While the journey remains possible” meant before Daniel died, but no one had pronounced the more serviceable word.

On the night train eastward, Marion watched the mountains recede behind wet glass. Their black flanks passed and passed, immense, indifferent, already withdrawing from the hopes she had fixed upon them. Daniel lay in the lower berth, his face turned to the wall. Each time the train curved, the green curtain bellied inward and fell back, breathing more deeply than he did.

“You should try to sleep,” he told her.

His voice had become so slight that the wheels devoured half of it.

“I’m not tired.”

“You’ve done everything.”

The sentence, intended as absolution, afflicted her like an accusation. She bent over him, pulled the blanket higher, and found his skin damp beneath the jaw. There had been a time when the touch of his throat brought to her a quick sensation of secrecy and possession. Now she withdrew her fingers before he could feel the recoil in them.

“You’ll be stronger tomorrow,” she said.

He looked at her with the composed incredulity of the dying. “Yes.”

The porter made up the upper berth for Marion, but she could not remain in it. Its roof hung close above her face, and every sway of the car rolled her toward the narrow leather guard. Below, Daniel coughed at irregular intervals, each cough a dull blow upon her nerves. She counted them until counting became unbearable; then she counted the spaces between them. At last she climbed down and sat beside him.

The car had subsided into the intimate ugliness of communal sleep. Behind curtains, strangers grunted, sighed, muttered, and released long greasy snores. Shoes stood beneath the berths like ownerless feet. A night-light burned at the end of the aisle, coating brass fittings and water cooler with a yellow, medicinal glaze.

Daniel seemed asleep. Marion leaned back, closed her eyes, and tried to think of New York: her mother waiting at the apartment, the spare room prepared, the familiar noises in the street. But each image opened upon another duty. The stairs. The doctor. Clean linen. The people who would come softly and ask how he was. The long extinction repeated in another room.

She slept without knowing it.

When she woke, the train was motionless.

No sound came from Daniel. This in itself was not strange; nevertheless, before she opened the curtain farther, before she touched him, before anything had happened which knowledge could legitimately fasten upon, terror entered her complete and fully armed.

His hand lay outside the blanket. She took it.

The cold was not severe. That was what deceived her for an instant. It was merely the absence of the answering warmth she expected, a polished coolness like the surface of a stone kept in shadow. She pressed his wrist. She pressed harder. Under her fingertips there was nothing but the structure of him—skin, tendon, bone—things which until that moment had been concealed by life as wires are concealed in a wall.

“Dan?”

Her whisper made no disturbance. She bent close and smelled medicine, stale breath, wool, the faint sourness of exhausted flesh. His mouth was open. One eyelid had not completely closed, and within the narrow aperture the eye showed not white but a dim mineral color, like agate under water.

“Dan!”

The train gave a preliminary jar. Somewhere outside, a man called a station name she did not understand. The sound came long and melancholy through the glass.

Marion’s first clear thought was not that Daniel was dead.

It was that they would put her off the train.

She saw it instantly: the porter summoned, the conductor bending into the berth, the sudden official faces; Daniel carried out beneath electric lamps; herself following onto a platform in a town whose name she had not even heard. There would be a hotel—or no hotel. Questions. A coroner. Telegrams. Men lifting Daniel as though he were freight. The train would move on without her, taking trunks, tickets, plans and every person who knew where he was going, while she remained beside the body in a raw little station surrounded by darkness.

No. She could not.

A bell clanged. The wheels moved. The unknown platform began to slide past the window: a row of milk cans, a red lantern, a man with his collar raised. Then it was gone.

Relief, enormous and shameful, weakened her knees. They were moving again. Nothing need happen yet.

She drew the curtain across Daniel’s face and stood in the aisle, holding its edge.

The porter came toward her, his white jacket gray in the night-light.

“Everything all right, ma’am?”

She heard herself answer with astonishing calm. “My husband has just fallen asleep.”

The porter glanced toward the berth.

“He was pretty restless earlier.”

“Yes. He needs sleep. Please don’t wake him.”

“Breakfast call about seven.”

“He won’t want breakfast.”

The porter’s gaze rested on her for no longer than politeness required, yet she felt it travel over her loosened hair, her bare feet thrust into shoes, the hand clenched upon the curtain.

“He been mighty sick, ain’t he?”

“The journey tires him.”

“Yes’m. You let me know you need anything.”

When he had gone, Marion remained standing. Her heart pounded with such violence that she thought the green curtain must tremble from it.

She had lied. The astonishing thing was not the lie itself but its efficacy. Words had covered death. Four feet away Daniel lay with his mouth open and his strange dulled eye, yet because she had said sleep, the porter had accepted sleep and walked away. It seemed for one giddy instant that the name of a thing might still govern the thing itself.

She climbed into the berth and closed the curtain behind her.

Dawn entered gradually through the window, revealing what darkness had mercifully simplified. Daniel’s face was not peaceful. People always said that: peaceful, released, at rest. But his features had stiffened around the final deprivation. The cheeks were hollow, the nostrils pinched, the lips drawn back from the teeth with an expression almost peevish in its incompleteness. It was not Daniel’s face, and yet it wore every mark by which she knew him. It was as if some clumsy sculptor had made a mask from memory and left it on the pillow to mock her.

She touched the eyelid, meaning to close it, but the skin resisted and slid beneath her finger. A small sound escaped her. She wiped her hand on her skirt, then hated herself for doing so.

“Oh, Dan,” she whispered. “I’m sorry.”

For what? For the touch, the lie, the relief? For all the afternoons when she had sat beside his chair and felt his illness eating not only him but the room, the daylight, her youth? Compassion and revolt had grown in her so intricately that she could no longer separate their roots. She had loved him; she had served him; she had sometimes wished, with a violence that frightened her, for the conclusion toward which every service tended. Now the conclusion had come, and she crouched beside it like a criminal surprised by her own desire.

The car began to wake.

Metal clasps snapped. Curtains scraped upon rods. Water ran into the washbasin at the end of the aisle. Voices, at first hushed by the lingering authority of night, became brisk and proprietorial. Marion slipped out, carrying her toilet case, and washed in cold water while a woman in a pink wrapper waited behind her, breathing impatience.

When Marion returned, she found a child standing beside Daniel’s berth.

He was a freckled boy of eight or nine, with one stocking fallen around his ankle and a pale wad of chewing-gum moving between his teeth. He had inserted two fingers beneath the curtain.

“What are you doing?” she said.

The boy withdrew them. “Nothing.”

“My husband is asleep.”

“I was only looking for my ball.”

“It isn’t there.”

He stared at her with the direct, unembarrassed curiosity of children and lunatics. “Is he the sick man?”

“He’s tired.”

“My uncle died on a train.”

The gum clicked between his molars.

Marion gripped her toilet case. “Go back to your mother.”

“He turned purple. They had to stop.”

“Go!”

The boy fled, more surprised than frightened. His mother, the woman in the pink wrapper, intercepted him halfway down the aisle and drew him close with the offended protectiveness of one whose child has been denied the universal right to intrude.

Marion returned behind the curtain. She felt now that all the passengers knew. They would not know consciously, perhaps, but the fact had escaped her and was moving among them like an odor. They would glance, whisper, consult. Curiosity would dress itself as concern and come tapping at the curtain.

She must be prepared.

She did not know. That was the essential thing. She had left Daniel sleeping. She had gone to wash. She had eaten breakfast, perhaps. Upon reaching New York she would try to wake him. Then she would discover—

No, the porter might try first.

She would prevent him. She would say Daniel must not be disturbed until they arrived. Then, when the train was safely in the station, when her brother and mother were perhaps beyond the gate, she would draw the curtain and cry out. Surprise. Grief. She attempted to imagine the proper cry, but every sound she invented seemed theatrical. Would a wife scream? Would she collapse? There was no rule. People described grief afterward but never its etiquette at the instant of discovery.

She did not know. She did not know yet.

The formula steadied her. It placed a thin partition between herself and the morning’s knowledge. She repeated it until the words became smooth and meaningless.

At eight the porter appeared with his whisk broom.

“Make up your berth, ma’am?”

“Not yet.”

He looked toward the closed lower section. “Gentleman still sleeping?”

“Yes.”

“Dining car serving.”

“He doesn’t want anything.”

“How about you?”

“I’m not hungry.”

The porter shifted the broom from one hand to the other. “Man in his condition ought to take nourishment.”

The phrase “his condition” seemed to expand in the narrow aisle.

“I know what he ought to take.”

“Yes’m. Didn’t mean nothing.”

His withdrawal offended her almost as much as his persistence. She had spoken sharply; he would remember it. Sharpness was suspicious. Calmness might be suspicious too. Everything she did cast a shadow shaped like guilt.

She bought coffee and toast from the buffet attendant and carried them to her seat beside the berth. The coffee was weak and scalding. She drank greedily, grateful for the pain of it. The toast broke into dry fragments that swelled in her mouth and would not go down. She wrapped the remainder in a napkin, making a little packet whose neatness seemed obscene.

Outside, morning spread over level fields glazed with frost. Farmhouses passed, each complete in its own stillness: smoke standing above a chimney, wash stiff upon a line, a dog halted beside a gate. The world’s solidity astonished her. Daniel had ceased, and yet fences continued to divide one property from another; women would sweep kitchens; clocks would be wound. Even the train, containing his fixed body, rushed east with an energy that seemed not indifferent but insulting.

The woman with the child approached.

She had dressed now in a traveling suit and wore two glossy brown braids coiled above her ears. Their color did not correspond to the thin hair at her temples.

“I hope Percy didn’t disturb your husband,” she said.

“No.”

“He’s a very imaginative child. Death seems to fascinate him. I tell him it isn’t wholesome, but boys—” She smiled, inviting Marion to pardon both motherhood and boyhood. “Your husband is consumptive, isn’t he?”

Marion’s hand tightened around the paper cup.

“He has had trouble with his lungs.”

“I thought so. My sister’s husband had it. We sent him to Arizona, though personally I’ve always believed Colorado is too cold. Does he sleep with the window open?”

“No.”

“He should. Fresh air is the great thing. Even in winter. My sister kept every window open, and she says if they had begun it sooner—”

“Did he recover?”

The woman’s smile faltered. “No. But he had gone too far.”

Marion turned toward the window.

“You mustn’t lose hope,” the woman continued quickly. “And you must take care of yourself. Wives wear themselves out unnecessarily. If I were you, I’d insist that he eat an egg.”

“He can’t eat an egg.”

“Perhaps if I spoke to him? Invalids often accept things from a stranger.”

For one monstrous second Marion imagined the woman’s head passing through the curtain, the false braids suspended over Daniel’s dead face.

“No!”

Several passengers looked around.

Marion lowered her voice. “He has only just gone to sleep. The doctor said sleep was more important than food.”

This invocation succeeded. The woman retreated before professional authority, though with an expression suggesting that she hoped the doctor would eventually recognize her superior judgment.

By noon the closed berth had become conspicuous. All the others were made up, their linen and pillows vanished, their sections transformed into democratic pairs of seats. Only Daniel’s curtain remained drawn, a dark green wound in the orderly car. Marion sat before it like an attendant at a forbidden shrine.

Passengers passed more often than necessity explained. A lantern-jawed man in a checked waistcoat walked to the water cooler five times, though Marion never saw him drink. Percy lost another ball. An elderly gentleman asked whether the berth number was eleven, then apologized when she told him it was nine, although the brass numeral shone beside his sleeve. Each glance touched the curtain and recoiled.

If she could open it a little, show Daniel’s shoulder, perhaps suspicion would diminish. But the motion of the train had altered him. She had looked once, ten minutes earlier, and seen that his jaw had sunk lower. A dark line marked the open mouth. The visible eye had dried to a lusterless bead.

She could not let them see.

Nor could she stop seeing him. With the curtain closed, his face became more vivid. It hovered against the green cloth, white and sharply detached, like a moon glimpsed through deep water. When she turned to the window, it appeared in the fields beside the train, keeping pace without effort. Once she saw it reflected above her own shoulder in the glass and spun around, striking her knee against the seat.

The lantern-jawed man was watching.

“You all right, ma’am?”

“Perfectly.”

“You look peaked.”

“I’m tired.”

He sat opposite without invitation. His knees, pointed and durable, nearly touched hers.

“Traveling with a sick party is a strain. I know. I’ve traveled with ’em.” He lowered his voice. “Tuberculosis?”

She said nothing.

“My line takes me through every climate in the country. Denver’s overrated. Too many fly-by-night institutions. What a man needs is a low altitude and plenty of beefsteak. You folks going through to New York?”

“Yes.”

“Got relatives meeting you?”

“Yes.”

“That’s good. He able to walk?”

The questions dropped one by one, small hooks seeking purchase.

“Why do you ask?”

“Well, they’ll need a chair for him at the terminal. I can tell the conductor.”

“That won’t be necessary.”

“Nothing to be ashamed of. Railroad supplies ’em.”

“He will not need a chair.”

The man leaned back, studying her. “You know best, of course.”

She heard in the phrase the full weight of his disbelief.

After he left, Marion considered moving to another car, but the idea of leaving Daniel unattended was intolerable. Someone would draw the curtain. Percy would crawl underneath. The porter, exercising a railway authority greater than any law of marriage, would begin dismantling the berth and expose its secret among folded sheets.

She remained.

The hours lost their edges. Stations arrived, bringing blasts of cold air and new faces, then slid backward into the wake of the train. Marion measured time not by the hands of her watch but by invasions. The porter. The child. The mother. The traveling man. Between them stretched intervals in which she listened for a sound from the berth, though she knew no sound could come.

At one o’clock she remembered brandy.

The doctor had told her to carry a flask. “If he has one of the attacks, a teaspoonful—no more.” She found it in her bag and poured some into the little silver cup. The smell brought back their last night in Colorado: Daniel coughing into a basin while she stood barefoot on the cold floor, waiting for the spasm to end. He had apologized afterward. Even then, choking on his own blood, he had apologized for troubling her.

She swallowed the brandy herself.

It burned down her throat and spread a false warmth through her chest. For several minutes she felt almost competent. There would be no difficulty. People transported dead men every day; only they did so knowingly, in proper boxes, with certificates. Her offense was merely one of arrangement. Daniel wanted to reach home. She was carrying out his wish. If officials objected, it was because officials preferred procedure to human feeling.

But had Daniel wanted this? Had he wanted to lie all day behind a curtain while strangers discussed eggs and climates outside? She remembered his horror of indignity, the care with which he had once shaved, knotted his tie, brushed dust from his sleeve. During the illness he had surrendered these rituals one by one, each surrender visible to her as a private humiliation. Now she had made of him a parcel.

“I had no choice,” she murmured.

The curtain stirred.

She froze.

It stirred again, swelling outward as the train rounded a curve, then settling against the dead shape within. Yet for the space of two heartbeats she had believed Daniel’s hand was reaching through.

Her competence vanished. The brandy had left only nausea.

Toward the middle of the afternoon, a large woman in violet approached. She wore a hat burdened with artificial grapes and smiled with such comprehensive sweetness that Marion recognized danger before a word was spoken. When she sat down, gold flashed among her front teeth.

“My dear,” she said, “I have been guided to speak with you.”

Marion looked toward the aisle for assistance, but the other passengers had assumed the absorbed expressions of people listening.

“I would rather be alone.”

“Of course you would. Mortal thought seeks isolation. It clings to its little dark room and says, ‘Here is sorrow; here is disease; let me shut the door.’ But there is no disease.”

“There is.”

“No, dear. There is only the belief in disease.”

Marion almost laughed. The woman’s serene denial seemed suddenly more outrageous than her own. Behind them Daniel lay dead; before them the woman sat denying not merely sickness but the whole massive fact of the body—its sweat, its blood, its humiliating dependence, its final coldness.

“My husband is sleeping,” Marion said.

“Sleep too is a mortal misconception. True consciousness never sleeps.”

The phrase penetrated her with a peculiar terror. Daniel’s half-open eye appeared in her mind.

The woman leaned nearer, breathing peppermint. “May I treat him?”

“No.”

“I need not touch him.”

“No.”

“Truth can pass through any material barrier.”

Her gaze moved to the curtain. Marion rose and placed herself between them.

“You must go away.”

“My dear, fear is making you hostile.”

“Yes,” Marion said. “It is.”

The answer, stripped of evasion, startled them both.

The woman stood with dignity. “When you are ready to release your error, you may find me in section four.”

After she had gone, the car was filled with muffled movements. Newspapers lifted. Throats were cleared. Percy’s gum clicked. Marion felt their curiosity gathering, no longer a collection of separate glances but a single many-eyed presence.

She entered the berth and pulled the curtain shut behind her.

The air inside was warmer now, imprisoned and faintly corrupt. The glass was wet at the corners. Daniel’s face had acquired a waxen translucence, as though the light no longer struck its surface but entered and died within it. Marion crouched beside him, scarcely breathing.

“You must help me,” she whispered.

The appeal was absurd. For months he had asked everything of her because he could do nothing; now she asked the one thing he was less able than ever to give. Yet the old resentment rose instinctively. Even dead, he left her to manage. Even now, when pity should have purified her, there remained this mean domestic anger: that the difficult thing was hers, always hers, while he lay beyond embarrassment.

Then she saw him as he had been before the illness, running down the steps of their first apartment because he had forgotten to kiss her goodbye; standing on a beach with his trousers rolled, shouting at the cold water; asleep with one arm flung heavily across her waist. Those images did not soften the face on the pillow. They made it more alien. The man she had loved seemed not to inhabit it, nor to have departed from it, but to have been destroyed by it long before death.

She put her hand upon the blanket where his arm lay. The contact was separated by wool, and therefore bearable.

“I did love you,” she said. “You know I did.”

The wheels hammered beneath them: did-love-you, did-love-you, did-love-you. The repetition became accusation, defense, meaningless rhythm.

A knock struck the partition.

Marion jerked upright.

“Ma’am?” the porter called. “We got to make this berth sometime.”

She emerged so quickly that the curtain caught in her hair.

“Not yet.”

He looked at her, then at the watch chain across his vest. “Be New York this evening.”

“I know.”

“Regulations say I got to have the beds up before we get in.”

“My husband is too ill to be moved.”

“Then maybe we better get the conductor.”

“No!”

There it was again, too loud, naked with fear.

The porter’s face changed. Until then he had been patient, perhaps suspicious, perhaps only annoyed. Now professional concern settled upon him.

“What’s the matter with the gentleman?”

“Nothing is the matter. He is resting.”

“You just said he’s too ill to move.”

“He is ill. That is why he must rest.”

“Maybe I ought to look at him.”

Marion held the curtain closed with both hands. “The doctor ordered that he not be disturbed.”

“What doctor?”

“Our doctor in Colorado.”

The porter hesitated. Behind him, the lantern-jawed man had lowered his newspaper. The woman with false braids watched openly. Even the spiritual healer’s purple hat was visible above a seat.

The porter spoke more softly. “You sure he don’t need help, ma’am?”

For one instant she nearly told him. The truth rose into her throat with the force of sickness. She imagined the relief of stepping aside, of letting other hands assume the burden, of no longer guarding the monstrous little boundary between what everyone believed and what she knew.

Then the train slowed.

Outside stood another unfamiliar platform, another station roof, another set of lamps waiting to receive whatever the railway cast out.

“He is asleep,” she said distinctly. “You may make up the berth when we reach New York.”

The porter looked at her for a long moment.

“All right,” he said at last. “New York.”

When he left, she understood that he knew—or that he suspected enough to be dangerous. Yet perhaps he, too, preferred New York. A dead passenger meant reports, delays, explanations. Human kindness and institutional convenience might wear the same face.

Evening gathered early. Lamps came on in the car, and the windows turned to black mirrors. Reflected passengers floated beside Marion: pale ovals, hats, lifted hands, mouths working soundlessly beneath the roar of the wheels. At the center of the glass she saw the closed berth behind her. The curtain appeared transparent. Through it Daniel lay exposed, his head turned toward the aisle, his open eye fixed on her reflection.

She swung around.

The curtain was opaque.

When she looked back at the glass, his face remained.

She shut her eyes, but then it hung within the darkness of her lids, larger and whiter. She opened them again. Better the ordinary world, even populated by staring strangers, than the inward country where his face could not be escaped.

The train ran through deepening suburbs. Rows of houses multiplied; factory windows burned in long tiers; signal lights approached like red eyes and sprang green as they passed. The car’s motion grew more violent, checked and renewed by crossings and switches. Each jolt seemed to loosen something in Marion’s mind. Thoughts no longer followed one another; they collided.

New York. Her mother. The platform. Wake him. Surprise.

She did not know.

She must not know until the right moment.

But what was the right moment? Before they stopped? After? Suppose the porter opened the curtain while she gathered her bags. Suppose Daniel fell when the berth was dismantled. Suppose his body, stiffened in its narrow enclosure, would not pass through. Suppose he spoke.

No—no, that was the one thing he could not do.

She began to tremble. The shaking started in her thighs and rose through her body until her teeth touched. She gripped the armrests. Around her the passengers prepared for arrival with hideous normality. Brushes appeared. Collars were adjusted. Bags came down from racks. The false-braided woman powdered her nose. The lantern-jawed man snapped shut his sample case. Percy pressed his face to the window and announced every light.

A tunnel swallowed the train.

Darkness erased the outer world, and the windows filled with the car’s interior. Marion saw herself sitting rigidly before the curtain: a young woman in a gray traveling dress, hair untidy beneath her hat, eyes enormous. She looked guilty. She looked mad. She looked healthy.

That was the worst of it—her health. It burned in her cheeks, beat in her wrists, demanded food, air, movement, tomorrow. Beside Daniel’s stillness it seemed indecent, an animal dragging at a leash. She had thought his illness imprisoned them both, but only he had been imprisoned. She had stood outside the bars, weary from watching, secretly certain she could walk away.

Now the train bore her toward freedom, and she was terrified of its arrival.

They burst out of the tunnel among walls, roofs and multiplied tracks. The porter passed through, calling that they would be in the terminal in ten minutes.

Ten minutes.

Marion rose. Her legs seemed jointed incorrectly. She gathered the flask, the napkin of dry toast, Daniel’s medicine bottle, and placed them in her bag. The trivial objects demanded an impossible exactness. The clasp would not close. She pressed it, opened it, rearranged the bottle, pressed again.

Five minutes.

People put on coats. Goodbyes began among those who had not known one another yesterday and would not remember one another tomorrow. The spiritual healer paused beside Marion and touched her sleeve.

“Hold to the truth, dear.”

Marion recoiled.

The woman moved on, wounded but forgiving.

The train crept between platforms, stopped, crept again. Vast ironwork appeared overhead. The noise changed, becoming enclosed and thunderous. Marion could see figures waiting beyond pillars, porters with carts, women lifting their faces toward the arriving windows.

Her mother might already be there.

At the thought of that known face, something in Marion nearly broke. She wanted to be a child again, to step down and say simply: It happened; I was frightened; take care of it. But first there remained the performance. She must discover Daniel. She must make the cry.

She practiced silently: Dan? Daniel, wake up. Daniel—

The porter came toward her.

His cap sat straight upon his head; his jacket was buttoned. Behind his official composure she saw apprehension, or pity, or only the fatigue of a man whose duties had been obstructed all day.

“Here we are, ma’am,” he said.

“Yes.”

“We’ll be stopped in a minute.”

“Yes.”

“Better wake your husband now.”

The words were ordinary. That was their violence.

Marion turned to the curtain. Its folds hung motionless, green and heavy. Beyond it the face waited—marble, wax, wet agate; Daniel and not Daniel; husband, burden, accusation, release.

She lifted her hand.

She did not know. Not yet. She knew nothing. She had slept; he had slept; they had traveled together; they had come home—

“Ma’am?” said the porter.

The train gave its final lurch.

Marion’s fingers caught the curtain, but instead of drawing it back she felt the floor rise softly and strike through her knees. The porter reached for her. She heard someone exclaim, heard Percy laugh and then stop, heard the iron shriek of brakes filling the terminal.

She fell sideways against the closed berth.

For an instant it upheld her, that narrow curtained tomb moving no longer; then her hand slid down the green cloth, and the waiting faces, the station lights, the porter’s white sleeve and all the laboriously preserved distinctions between sleep and death flowed together into darkness.

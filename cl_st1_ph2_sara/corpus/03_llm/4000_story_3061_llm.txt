The afternoon train had been travelling for two hours through a landscape that seemed to have fallen asleep from heat. Dust filmed the windows, the cushions gave out a smell of warm cloth, and every station appeared to be a repetition of the last, with the same discouraged porter and the same row of sun-stricken milk cans.

In one compartment were an aunt, three children, and a bachelor whose expression suggested that he had selected solitude and been given its opposite.

The children consisted of two girls and a boy. The elder girl occupied herself by asking questions; the boy asked the same questions more loudly; and the younger girl repeated, in a tuneless murmur, the first line of a poem about a little silver fish. She had forgotten the second line and showed no anxiety to recover it.

“Why are those sheep under the bridge?” asked the boy.

“To get out of the sun,” said the aunt.

“Why?”

“Because the sun is hot.”

“Why is it hot?”

The aunt looked through the window as though the answer might be posted on a signal box.

“It is always hot in summer.”

“Why?”

The bachelor shifted one knee away from the boy’s swinging shoe.

Presently the elder girl discovered a field containing no sheep.

“Why aren’t there sheep in that field?”

“Perhaps the farmer doesn’t keep sheep.”

“Why doesn’t he?”

“He may prefer cows.”

“There aren’t any cows.”

“Then perhaps the field is resting.”

The children considered this.

“Does a field get tired?” asked the boy.

“No, of course not.”

“Then why does it rest?”

The aunt’s lips tightened in a manner intended to announce that reasonable inquiry had ceased and wilful provocation had begun. The younger girl continued to inform the carriage that a little silver fish had swum beneath a tree. The bachelor glanced briefly toward the communication cord, but did not pull it.

“Come and look out of the window,” said the aunt, with artificial brightness. “See the telegraph poles running past.”

“They aren’t running,” said the elder girl. “We are.”

“And there’s a cow,” added the boy.

“Where?”

“It’s gone.”

This failure of the cow to remain available for educational purposes was received as a personal injury.

The children resumed movement. The boy opened and shut the ashtray. The elder girl knelt on the seat and pressed her face to the glass. The younger girl gave the silver fish a louder and more accusatory rendering.

The bachelor sighed.

The aunt turned to him with the severity of a person whose public difficulties have been noticed.

“Children are very hard to amuse on a long journey,” she observed.

The bachelor looked at the three faces, each alert for the next weakness in adult authority.

“I should not have thought it especially difficult,” he said.

The aunt bristled. “Perhaps you would like to try.”

There was a silence of challenge. The children, sensing that some alteration in government was proposed, became attentive.

“Very well,” said the bachelor. “I will tell you a story.”

“What about?” asked the boy.

“A girl.”

“Was she pretty?” asked the elder girl.

“No,” said the bachelor.

The aunt looked offended on behalf of girlhood.

“Then what was she?” asked the boy.

“She was good.”

The children’s interest visibly declined.

“Horribly good,” added the bachelor.

The interest returned.

Her name, he told them, was Bertha, and her goodness was not the ordinary, occasional kind that appears when grown-up people are watching. It was continuous, polished, and oppressive. She never tore her clothes, never stole jam, never told untruths except by grammatical accident, and never pushed smaller children into ponds, even when there was every appearance that they would float.

“Could they swim?” asked the boy.

“Some could. That made it less tempting.”

Bertha learned her lessons perfectly, ate boiled vegetables without complaint, and took medicine without first asking whether it was necessary. For her obedience she received a bronze medal. For punctuality she received a silver medal. For exceptional good conduct she received a gold medal, nearly as large as a biscuit and considerably less useful.

“Where did she wear them?” asked the elder girl.

“Pinned to the front of her white pinafore.”

“All three?”

“All three. When she walked they struck together—clink, clink, clink—so that people could hear her virtue approaching before they had the misfortune to meet it.”

The aunt gave a small disapproving cough. The children ignored it.

Bertha’s fame reached the Prince of that country, who owned a private park outside the town. No children were admitted to it, because the Prince disliked children in the collective and distrusted them separately. But when he heard of Bertha’s extraordinary goodness, he permitted her to walk in the park once a week.

“Was the Prince there?” asked the boy.

“No. Princes are seldom present when privileges become dangerous.”

“Were there flowers?” asked the elder girl.

“There were no flowers.”

“Why not?”

“Because the Prince’s pigs had eaten them.”

This was satisfying. A park without flowers, but with pigs, was plainly superior to the usual arrangement.

There were seven pigs: a black pig with white feet, a white pig with black ears, two reddish pigs with grey noses, a yellowish pig, a small spotted pig, and a large mud-coloured pig which looked as if it had been designed to disappear in bad weather. They roamed among the trees eating roots, acorns, beetles, and anything else that had neglected to escape.

“Were they good pigs?” asked the younger girl, abandoning the silver fish for the first time.

“They had no medals,” said the bachelor, “so there was no means of knowing.”

Bertha entered the park in her spotless pinafore, and the three medals sounded clink, clink, clink as she walked. She admired the pigs, though they did not admire her. She saw ponds full of golden fish, blue fish, and one very fat green fish that spent its time pretending to be a stone. She saw peacocks, lizards, and fruit trees covered with fruit which the Prince had forbidden anyone to pick.

“Why?” asked the boy.

“He enjoyed forbidding things.”

The aunt moved impatiently, but the story had passed beyond her control.

Now, on that same afternoon, a wolf came into the park.

It was a large wolf, lean from unsuccessful hunting and cross from being lean. Its fur was the colour of storm clouds; its tongue was black; its eyes were a pale, unpleasant grey. It came softly through the bushes, smelling the pigs.

“Did it eat one?” asked all three children.

“Not at first.”

The pigs saw it and fled with remarkable speed. The white pig with black ears rushed into a shed. The reddish pigs hid behind a stone wall. The spotted pig squeezed through a gap in the railings, and the mud-coloured pig lay down in a muddy ditch and immediately became difficult to distinguish from local scenery.

Bertha saw the wolf and ran.

Her clean white pinafore flashed between the dark trees, and her medals went clink, clink, clink as she hurried. For the first time in her life, she regretted having obtained so many official proofs of goodness.

She reached a thick clump of myrtle bushes and crawled underneath. The branches concealed her white clothes. She pressed herself against the earth and held her breath.

The wolf came nearer.

It sniffed beside the pond.

It sniffed beside the fruit trees.

It sniffed around the myrtle bushes.

Bertha trembled, but she remained silent. The wolf’s pale eyes stared between the leaves and saw nothing. After a while it turned away.

The three children leaned forward. The aunt wore the hopeful expression of one who anticipates a rescue and intends afterward to claim it for morality.

Bertha began to feel safer. She thought of her perfect lessons, her boiled vegetables, her truthful answers, and all the many occasions on which she had not pushed smaller children into ponds. Surely, she reflected, a person of such goodness could not possibly be eaten.

At that moment she trembled with relief.

The three medals knocked together.

Clink.

The wolf stopped.

Bertha became still.

The wolf listened. Nothing moved except a leaf.

Then Bertha’s knees shook again.

Clink, clink.

The wolf turned and came back.

It pushed its long nose into the myrtle branches. Bertha tried to crawl deeper, but the gold medal caught on a twig. There was a brisk metallic rattle, a tearing of white cloth, and one short cry.

The wolf dragged her out and devoured her to the last useful mouthful.

For several seconds the railway compartment was perfectly quiet.

“What happened to the medals?” asked the boy.

“They were left on the ground,” said the bachelor, “along with her shoes and a few fragments of ribbon.”

“And the pigs?”

“They all escaped.”

“Even the yellowish one?”

“Especially the yellowish one.”

“Why?”

“It had gone home early.”

The children settled back with expressions of profound satisfaction.

“That is the best story I have ever heard,” said the elder girl.

“It began badly,” said the boy, “but the ending was splendid.”

The younger girl considered the matter. “She was eaten because she was good.”

“She was discovered because she wore the rewards of goodness,” corrected the bachelor.

“That is nearly the same thing,” said the child.

The aunt drew herself up.

“I think it was a most improper story,” she said. “It suggests that good behaviour brings misfortune. You have undone years of careful teaching.”

The bachelor looked mildly surprised. “Your teaching appeared to be surviving with some difficulty before I began.”

“It is wrong to tell children tales in which wicked animals triumph.”

“The wolf did not triumph entirely. It failed to eat the pigs.”

“That makes it worse.”

The train began to slow. The bachelor collected his gloves and walking stick.

“At any rate,” he said, “they have been quiet for ten minutes.”

The aunt could find no immediate answer that did not concede the point.

The train entered a small station, apparently the same station as before but equipped with a different porter. The bachelor stepped onto the platform.

As he passed the compartment window, he heard the boy say, “Tell us another story about a very good child.”

“One with four medals,” said the elder girl.

“And two wolves,” added the younger.

The aunt’s reply was lost beneath the renewed movement of the train, but her face supplied its general character.

The bachelor walked away smiling. He had no particular ill will toward the aunt; nevertheless, it pleased him to think that for the rest of the journey, and probably for many journeys afterward, those children would demand stories that were improper, logical, and furnished with wolves.

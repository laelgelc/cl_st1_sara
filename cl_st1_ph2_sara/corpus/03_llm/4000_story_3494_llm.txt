# The Kingdom of Cards

Once upon a time, beyond seven grey channels and a sea where no prudent mariner desired to sail, there lay an island as smooth and square as a gaming table.

The sea crooned about it day and night. With wave’s white hands it stroked the long yellow beach, saying, “Sleep, little island, sleep.” Above it the sky brooded like a great blue mother-bird; around it the winds passed softly, for even the winds had learned that noise was contrary to the Rules.

This island was called the Kingdom of Cards.

There the people had lived from time immemorial according to their proper suits and stations. The Kings stood foremost, the Queens beside them, and the Knaves at a respectful distance. The Aces possessed a dignity not altogether understood, though universally acknowledged. The Tens looked down upon the Nines, the Nines upon the Eights, and so downward through the solemn ranks until the poor Twos had no one beneath them and were therefore obliged to look down upon the ground.

The Hearts dwelt in the eastern quarter, the Diamonds in the western, the Clubs in the north, and the Spades in the south. None crossed into another’s district except on Ceremonial Occasions, and even then only by the Appointed Road.

Everything had a Rule.

There was a Rule for rising and a Rule for lying down. There was a Rule determining which direction one’s face should turn at noon. There was a Rule concerning the proper angle of the elbow at supper. There were Rules for sneezing, Rules for yawning, and twelve ancient Rules governing the unfortunate occurrence of a hiccup in the presence of a Court Card.

No one asked why. No one chose when. No one wondered whether things might be otherwise.

The people neither laughed nor wept. Their painted faces were always correct. If a Queen stood beside a King on the first day of spring, she stood beside him because the Rules placed her there, not because spring had entered her blood. If a Knave bowed, he bowed at the prescribed degree. If a Seven passed an Eight, he withdrew exactly three paces and waited, even though rain fell or night came.

Thus there was never a quarrel. There was never an embrace. There was never a mistake.

And nothing happened.

Now, far across the sea, there lived a young Prince with his mother in a little palace that had forgotten the sound of trumpets.

The Prince’s father was dead, and his elder half-brothers had taken the kingdom. The widowed Queen had been sent away with a few servants, three rooms, and a garden where the roses bloomed as though they remembered happier days. She bore her exile with gentleness, but sorrow sat beside her at meals and slept near her pillow.

The Prince grew tall. His eyes were bright, his hands restless, and the world called to him from beyond the garden wall.

One morning he came to his mother and said, “Mother, I cannot remain here counting the roses while your heart grows pale. I shall go abroad, gather fortune, and return with such honour that sorrow itself will be ashamed to enter our gate.”

The Queen tried to smile.

“The sea is wide,” she said, “and fortune has many doors, most of them shut.”

“Then I shall knock loudly,” replied the Prince.

She placed her hand upon his head and blessed him. And because mothers must sometimes open the door through which all their happiness departs, she watched him go without calling him back.

Two companions travelled with him: the son of a rich Merchant and the son of the Kotwal, who was guardian of law and keeper of the city prison.

The Merchant’s Son understood weights, measures, bargains, pearls, pepper, silk, ivory, sandalwood, and the exact point at which friendship became unprofitable. The Kotwal’s Son knew every road by which a thief might escape, every knot by which he might be secured, and every official phrase suitable to occasions of public alarm.

The Prince knew none of these useful things. But he could hear adventure breathing behind a closed door, and that gift proved no less valuable.

For years the Three Companions sailed from port to port. They traded indigo for silver, silver for spices, spices for amber, amber for horses, and horses for jewels. Their ships grew heavy with treasure. Sailors sang beneath strange stars; merchants bowed to them in foreign bazaars; rulers invited them to dine beneath lamps of beaten gold.

At last the Merchant’s Son said, “Our profits are sufficient.”

The Kotwal’s Son said, “Our papers are in order.”

But the Prince looked toward the horizon and said, “Then let us go home.”

They turned their ships westward.

For seven days the sea lay peaceful. On the eighth, a black cloud arose like a mountain suddenly remembering its anger. The wind tore the sails from the masts. Thunder rolled its iron chariots over the sky. Waves leapt aboard and seized caskets, bales, sailors, ropes and spars with equal appetite.

The Merchant’s Son attempted to calculate the loss, but the rain washed away his figures.

The Kotwal’s Son commanded the tempest to halt in the name of the Law, but the tempest proved contemptuous of jurisdiction.

The Prince laughed once into the storm—not because he was unafraid, but because terror and splendour had filled him beyond speech.

Then the ships broke.

At dawn the Three Companions awoke upon a beach. Their treasure was gone, their fleet had vanished, and the sea was again crooning as innocently as a nurse beside a cradle.

Before them stood rows of people.

The Hearts had come by the eastern road, the Diamonds by the western, the Clubs from the north, and the Spades from the south. The Kings stood in front, then the Queens, Knaves, Aces and numbered ranks, all in faultless order. Every face was prim. Every limb was still.

The visitors stared at the Cards.

The Cards stared at the visitors.

At length the King of Spades spoke.

“What are you?”

“We are hungry,” said the Prince.

This answer caused uneasiness. Hunger was a Condition, not a Rank, and therefore supplied no guidance concerning precedence.

The Queen of Diamonds whispered, “Perhaps they are Court Cards of an unknown suit.”

The Ten of Clubs objected. “They bear no proper emblems.”

“Then they may be low cards,” said the Knave of Spades.

The Twos, who had hoped for someone beneath them, trembled with expectation.

A Great Debate immediately began.

Were strangers washed ashore superior to inhabitants born upon dry land? Should a man without a number stand above an Ace or below a Two? Was salt water a qualification? Did wet garments constitute ceremonial dress? And, most vital and critical of all, in which quarter ought an Unclassified Person to sleep?

The Cards spoke slowly, consulted precedents, formed committees, dissolved them on points of rank, and formed them again.

Meanwhile the Three Companions smelled food.

Without awaiting the Judgment of Classification, they walked into the town, entered a public dining hall, sat wherever they pleased, and ate from dishes intended for several distinct ranks.

The Cards followed in horror.

The Prince took sweet cakes from the tray of the King of Hearts. The Merchant’s Son sampled the Queen of Clubs’ preserves. The Kotwal’s Son, though descended from a guardian of laws, drank from a cup reserved for Aces.

A murmur passed through the assembly.

“They are breaking the Rules!”

“Which Rules?” asked the Prince, his mouth full.

“All of them,” replied the Cards.

The Prince looked at his companions. His companions looked at him. Then all three burst into such tremendous laughter that the windows shook, the spoons rang, and a flock of white birds rose startled from the palace roof.

Never had laughter been heard in the Kingdom of Cards.

The inhabitants stepped backward. Several Fives changed places with Sixes. A Nine of Diamonds sat down without permission. The Queen of Hearts raised one hand to her breast, though no Rule required it.

The sound seemed to roll across the island, enter the empty houses, disturb the sleeping gardens and awaken some old creature beneath the earth.

That evening the authorities commanded the strangers to appear before the Court Cards.

The King of Clubs asked, “By whose order did you eat?”

“Our own,” said the Merchant’s Son.

“Under which regulation did you laugh?” asked the Queen of Spades.

“Under none,” said the Kotwal’s Son.

The King of Hearts turned gravely to the Prince.

“According to what authority do you stand where you stand?”

“According to Ichcha,” replied the Prince.

At this word the Court fell silent.

They searched the Tables of Rank. They examined the Book of Conduct, the Appendix of Exceptions, and the Thirty-Seven Sacred Footnotes. No Ichcha was found.

At last the Queen of Hearts asked, very softly, “And pray who is Ichcha?”

“Ichcha is wish,” said the Prince. “She is the little sovereign within us who says, ‘Go,’ though all the roads forbid us; ‘Speak,’ though silence has been appointed; ‘Choose,’ though a place was chosen for us before we were born.”

The Cards gazed at him.

“Where does this sovereign dwell?” asked the Knave of Diamonds.

“In the heart.”

At once all the Spades, Clubs and Diamonds glanced suspiciously at the Hearts.

“Not that suit,” said the Prince, laughing. “I mean here.”

He struck his breast.

A strange disturbance followed. The Cards had always possessed painted hearts, painted eyes and painted mouths, but now some imagined that beneath those surfaces something stirred.

The next morning, the Three Companions walked through the island without observing the Appointed Roads. The Prince climbed a hill reserved for Kings. The Merchant’s Son entered the Diamond market and bargained with a Three. The Kotwal’s Son lay beneath a tree at noon with his head pointing east, though the Rule demanded west.

The Cards gathered around them.

“Why do you do this?” they asked.

“Because we wish to,” replied the companions.

The phrase travelled from mouth to mouth.

“I wish to.”

At first the Cards repeated it as children repeat a charm without knowing its power.

A Five of Clubs whispered, “I wish to stand beside that fountain.”

He stepped from his row.

Nothing struck him dead.

A Two of Spades said, “I wish to sit.”

She sat, although an Eight was present.

The sky did not fall.

Then the Queen of Diamonds removed her crown because it was heavy. A Knave of Hearts sang three notes for which there existed no official tune. The Seven of Clubs plucked a yellow flower from the royal garden and wore it behind his ear.

By sunset the Kingdom was full of dreadful irregularities.

The authorities rang the Bell of Correction, but the appointed bell-ringer had gone to watch the sea. They ordered the guards to restore the rows, but one guard wished to dance and another wished to sleep. They opened the Book of Punishments, only to find that the Clerk had drawn a bird in the margin.

Confusion entered the island like a spring flood.

At breakfast, a Four sat above a Six. At noon, two Queens disagreed. By evening, a King discovered that he disliked the colour of his own robes. People walked swiftly, slowly, sideways, together and alone. They interrupted speeches. They changed their minds. They made promises and regretted them. They gave gifts, stole kisses, told lies, confessed truths, and arrived late.

And, little by little, their painted faces altered.

Eyes became bright or troubled. Mouths trembled. Brows grew proud. Cheeks flushed. The Cards looked into mirrors and saw, for the first time, not ranks but selves.

Some were delighted with what they saw. Some were disappointed. Some gazed so long that vanity was invented before supper.

The change did not make everyone good. Indeed, goodness, which had previously been unnecessary, now became difficult. The King of Diamonds coveted the King of Clubs’ garden. The Nine of Spades grew jealous of the Ten. Two Knaves quarrelled over a blue ribbon and refused to speak for three days, though each spent those days speaking continually about the other.

But there was kindness too.

A Queen comforted a weeping Two. An Ace gave away his supper. A Six remained awake beside a sick Seven, not because the Rules commanded it, but because she could not bear to leave.

Life was throbbing; hearts were breaking; passions were kindling.

And the island, which had been flawless as a painted picture, became untidy and beautiful.

Spring came.

Birds nested in the palace eaves and sang without licence. The south wind ran laughing through the streets, loosened veils, disturbed carefully arranged hair and whispered words no Council could record. Leaves rustled in secret conference. The sea no longer seemed to croon of sleep; it called, retreated, returned and called again.

Music appeared everywhere.

The Knave who had once sung three forbidden notes discovered a fourth and then a hundred more. Drums answered flutes; strings trembled under uncertain fingers; voices rose after moonset from gardens where no one admitted having gone.

Among all who awakened, none awakened more deeply than the Queen of Hearts.

She had always stood in her appointed place beside the King of Hearts. She had never disliked him, for dislike had not been included in the arrangement. She had never loved him either. He was simply there, as the palace wall was there.

But now, whenever the Prince entered the Hall, she felt a fluttering beneath her breast.

At first she consulted the Rules. They said much concerning posture and precedence, but nothing about breath that came too quickly.

She consulted the royal physician. He advised her to stand more rigidly.

She tried. The fluttering grew worse.

During a public ceremony she left her place and stood near the Prince.

The whole assembly gasped.

The King of Hearts looked offended, then thoughtful, then unexpectedly relieved. For he had lately discovered that he preferred the Queen of Spades, who told amusing stories and had a small dimple in her left cheek.

The Queen of Hearts returned to her station. The next day she left it again. Each time she stood a little nearer to the Prince, and each time colour rose to her face like dawn climbing the sky.

The Prince saw her, yet said nothing.

He had awakened wish in the Kingdom, but he knew that a wish named by another becomes an order in disguise.

So the Queen struggled alone with hope, shame, jealousy and delight. If the Prince spoke to the Queen of Diamonds, she grew cold. If he smiled at her, the world became too bright to look upon. She listened for his footstep in every corridor and heard it even when he was far away.

Soon the entire kingdom knew.

Some approved. Some disapproved. Some approved in the morning and disapproved after hearing new gossip at noon. The old authorities called the matter vital and critical. Young Cards embroidered hearts upon their sleeves. Poets appeared in alarming numbers.

At last the Prince summoned the people to the Great Square.

“You have learned to leave your places,” he said. “You have learned to speak, to sing, to quarrel and forgive. Yet one chain remains. Your marriages are still arranged by suit and rank. Let each choose freely, and let none be given like a card from one hand to another.”

The old Cards trembled, but the young ones shouted.

A Ceremony of Choice was proclaimed.

Pavilions were raised beside the sea. Garlands hung from towers and balconies. Lamps were set in long rows along the beach, so that at twilight the island seemed to wear a necklace of stars. Drums sounded. Conches cried. All ranks and suits assembled without order, which caused much inconvenience and greater happiness.

The Queen of Hearts came dressed in crimson, bearing a garland of jasmine.

Before her stood Kings, Princes, Knaves, Aces and common numbered Cards. The King of Diamonds straightened his crown. The Knave of Clubs displayed a newly composed smile. The Ten of Hearts tried to appear taller than his number.

The Prince stood among them without crown or jewel.

The Queen’s hands trembled. The jasmine flowers shook, releasing their fragrance. For one moment the old silence returned. The island waited. The sea waited. Even the south wind seemed to hold its breath.

Under the ancient Rules, the Queen would have had no need to fear, because nothing would have depended upon her.

Now everything depended upon her.

She walked past the Kings.

She walked past the Aces and Knaves.

She came to the Prince and stopped.

Then, raising her arms, she placed the jasmine garland around his neck.

A cry rose from the people. Drums thundered, flutes leapt into song, and laughter flew upward among the stars. The sea flung white flowers of foam upon the shore. The Queen covered her face, but the Prince took her hand before all the assembly.

“I choose you,” she whispered.

“And I choose you,” he replied.

That night no one slept according to the Rules.

The people danced on roads where once they had marched in silence. Hearts joined hands with Spades, Diamonds with Clubs, Twos with Queens and Sevens with Aces. The former King of Hearts danced with the Queen of Spades and discovered that her dimple appeared whenever he forgot his dignity.

At dawn the islanders brought forth a crown.

“Be our King,” they said to the Prince, “not because an old book commands it, but because we wish it.”

“And the Queen of Hearts shall reign beside you,” cried the people, “because she chose, and was chosen.”

So the Prince and Queen were crowned beneath the open sky.

Thereafter ships were built, and messengers crossed the sea to the little palace where the Prince’s mother still counted roses in her lonely garden.

They brought her a letter, a crown and a branch of jasmine.

The exiled Queen came over the sea. When she stepped ashore, her son knelt before her, and all the Kingdom bowed—not in prescribed degrees, not by rank or regulation, but out of love.

She looked upon the laughing crowds, the mingled suits, the untidy processions and the Queen of Hearts standing beside her son.

“Is this kingdom well governed?” she asked.

The Prince considered.

“Not always,” he said. “The people make mistakes.”

“Are they good?”

“Sometimes.”

“Are they bad?”

“Sometimes.”

“And do they know what they are doing?”

“Almost never.”

Then the old Queen smiled.

“Ah,” she said, “they are alive.”

And so the Kingdom of Cards lost its perfect order forever.

Its people chose wisely and foolishly, loved faithfully and inconstantly, worked, idled, sang, sulked, forgave and remembered. They became generous, greedy, brave, vain, tender and troublesome. They were good or bad, or both, according to their own Ichcha.

The Rules remained in an old palace room, where scholars occasionally dusted them and argued over their meaning.

But outside, the sea called, the south wind laughed, and every road led in more than one direction.

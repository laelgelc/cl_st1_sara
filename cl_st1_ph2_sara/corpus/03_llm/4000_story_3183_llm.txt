### The Minister of Misadventure

It has long been my custom, whenever the malicious world accuses me of an error in judgment, to preserve a philosophical composure and demonstrate, by an exact recital of circumstances, that the error belonged not to me but to the world. In this manner I have maintained, through calamities that would have unsettled a less contemplative understanding, both the integrity of my reason and the tranquillity of my conscience.

I mention this lest the extraordinary incidents I am about to relate should induce some inconsiderate person to suppose that I was, at their commencement, confused by wine. I had dined heavily, it is true. A duck of unusual amplitude, supported by peas, potatoes, and a pudding whose interior architecture was rich in raisins, had occupied my attention for nearly two hours. Nor shall I deny that I drank afterward three bottles of claret and a quantity of brandy which, in a person of inferior constitution, might have produced drowsiness. With me it produced reflection.

Having drawn my chair toward the fire, elevated my feet upon the mantelpiece, and placed within reach a decanter of rum, I betook myself to that miscellaneous intellectual nourishment offered by the Sunday newspaper.

My eye was presently arrested by a report entitled:

**MELANCHOLY AND UNACCOUNTABLE FATALITY**

The account concerned a certain Mr. Silas Pymble, a respectable dealer in buttons, who, while passing beneath the window of a confectioner, had opened his mouth to cough. At that precise instant a workman above dropped a needle; the needle entered Mr. Pymble’s mouth, lodged crosswise in his throat, and caused him to leap backward against a fruit cart. The cart rolled downhill, overturned a barrel of treacle, frightened a horse, and drove the unfortunate gentleman into a shop where a mechanical rocking-horse, set violently in motion by his fall, struck him repeatedly upon the head. Death, said the report, was instantaneous.

“Fabrication!” I exclaimed. “An insult to the understanding! No man possessing the smallest acquaintance with probability could swallow such nonsense.”

Scarcely had I spoken when a deep voice near my elbow replied:

“Vy should he not svallow it? He svallowed te needle, did he not?”

I lowered my newspaper.

Directly opposite me, where no visitor had stood an instant before, there now existed a personage so grotesquely constituted that I hesitated whether to regard him as a being, a piece of furniture, or an assemblage of vessels accidentally piled by an intoxicated cooper.

His body was a broad wine barrel. Two small kegs, attached at the shoulders, served him for arms, while his legs were long bottles of a greenish color, terminated by little casks in the office of feet. A canteen occupied the position ordinarily assigned to the head; in its front were two round apertures, from which glared eyes of a fiery copper hue. His nose was a corkscrew, and his mouth consisted of the wide end of a tin funnel, through which his speech issued with the mingled roar and gurgle of liquor descending a drain.

In one keg-hand he carried a squat black bottle; in the other, a glass.

“Sir,” said I, after contemplating him with that calmness which good breeding preserves even before monstrosities, “you have entered my apartment without announcement.”

“Und you haf read te paper mitout pelief,” he answered.

“Belief is not due to impossibilities.”

“Impossipilities!” cried the figure. “Vat you calls impossipilities is mein regular pizziness.”

“Your business?”

“I am te Angel of te Odd.”

At this declaration I could not restrain a smile. The creature stamped both cask-feet, causing the glasses upon my table to ring.

“Te Angel of te Odd!” he repeated. “Te boss of plunders! Te minister of misadventures! Ven te man slips on a grape und marries his cook; ven te lady sneezes und drops her diamond into te soup; ven te horse eats te vill, te mouse burns te house, und te umbrella pokes te mayor—dat is me!”

“I perceive,” said I, “that you are an allegorical consequence of indigestion.”

“Vat?”

“A gastric symbol—a vapor arising from duck, pudding, and journalism. In short, you are not present.”

The Angel thrust the black bottle into his funnel and took a prodigious draught.

“Not bresent, eh? I tells you vot: you haf too much pride in your little logics. You tinks noting odd can happen because you haf not arranged it before breakfast.”

“On the contrary, accidents happen continually. But they follow natural laws. They do not require the supervision of a barrel.”

His copper eyes glowed.

“You call me a parrel?”

“I call you an absurdity.”

“You pelief te newspaper?”

“Not one syllable.”

“You pelief in me?”

“Less than one syllable.”

Hereupon the Angel poured the remainder of his bottle upon my head. The liquor possessed a pungency inconsistent with incorporeal existence, but I attributed the sensation to nervous sympathy.

“Very well!” he bellowed. “I teaches you te troof! I makes you pelief in accidents vat is too natural for you! I makes you respect te Odd!”

He raised one bottle-leg, kicked me ceremoniously in the chest, and vanished up the chimney.

I confess that this departure surprised me; nevertheless, on examining the room and discovering the decanter empty, I arrived at the obvious explanation. I had fallen asleep, dreamed the monster, and unconsciously spilled the rum. Secure in this conclusion, I retired.

On the following morning I was to meet Mr. Gripps, my insurance agent, at ten o’clock precisely, for the purpose of renewing the policy upon my house, which expired at noon. My clock, though recently injured by the accidental fracture of its glass, had always been punctual. I therefore wound it, placed it beside my bed, and abandoned myself to repose.

When I awoke, the sun stood at an alarming altitude. My watch declared the hour to be twelve minutes past one. I sprang up, overturned the washstand, dressed with a rapidity derogatory to dignity, and hurried to the office of Mr. Gripps.

That gentleman had departed.

His clerk informed me that Mr. Gripps, having waited until eleven, had gone into the country; furthermore, the policy had expired and could not be renewed before the next day.

Returning home, I investigated my clock. The mechanism was sound. Between two teeth of the smallest wheel, however, I discovered the stem of a raisin, which had evidently flown from my pudding on the previous evening, passed through the aperture in the broken glass, and stopped the works at seventeen minutes before nine.

This was certainly unusual, but in no degree supernatural. Raisins possess stems; clock glasses are sometimes broken; and openings admit foreign substances. To invoke a celestial officer of irregularity would have been childish.

That night, as fatigue had disposed me to sleep, I lay down with a book and a candle. I had not read six pages when the candle disappeared.

I heard a scrambling beneath the bed and, looking down, saw a large rat dragging the lighted candle by the wick. The animal had perhaps mistaken the tallow for food. Before I could seize him, he carried it through a hole in the wainscot.

I obtained another candle, inspected the wall, perceived no smoke, and concluded that the flame had been extinguished in transit. Being of a composed temperament, I returned to bed.

At two in the morning I was awakened by a suffocating heat and a crackling noise. The curtains were on fire; the wall was on fire; the floor was on fire; indeed, the whole house appeared to have adopted fire as its ruling principle.

I wrapped myself in a blanket and rushed to the window. The street below was crowded. A ladder was raised, and a courageous fireman ascended until his hands nearly touched the sill.

“Steady!” I cried.

At that instant a hog, having escaped from a neighboring yard and become agitated by the engines, ran between the supporting legs of the ladder. The ladder swung sideways. The fireman fell into a basket of turnips, while I, attempting to follow him, descended upon the pavement with more velocity than grace.

My life was preserved by an awning. My left arm, however, was broken in two places, my eyebrows were consumed, and my house—with its furniture, library, wine, silver, and unpaid insurance premium—was reduced to a smoking cavity.

I heard, or imagined I heard, from somewhere among the engines, a liquid chuckle followed by the words:

“Only natural accidents, yah?”

The speaker could not be identified.

My recovery occupied six weeks. During this period my affairs became embarrassed, for although I possessed investments adequate to the habits of a gentleman, the destruction of my house had deprived me of ready money. My physicians recommended quiet, cheerful society, and marriage.

The last prescription appeared especially agreeable, since I had for some months admired the Widow Crumble, a lady of property, sentiment, and abundant hair. Her estate would restore my finances; her conversation, being infrequent, would not disturb my reflections.

The fire had deprived me of my own hair, which, before the accident, had been of considerable elegance. I therefore purchased a wig of the most convincing manufacture and presented myself at the widow’s residence.

She received me in a small garden pavilion. The evening was fragrant, the moon discreet, and Mrs. Crumble more than ordinarily receptive. I had conducted the conversation from meteorology through bereavement to the insufficiency of solitary existence, and was preparing to declare myself, when she lowered her eyes.

“Dear madam,” I said, kneeling, “there are wounds of the heart which only a sympathetic hand can—”

At this tender crisis a breeze entered the pavilion. A loose fastening within my wig caught in one of the widow’s curls. Unaware of the union, I rose; she turned away; and our heads were drawn violently together.

She screamed.

“Be calm,” I said. “A temporary conjunction—”

“Let me go!”

“I am endeavoring to do so.”

She fled toward the door; I, being attached, followed in a stooping posture. Our confused rotation overturned a table, two flowerpots, and a cage containing a parrot. The bird escaped and circled overhead, crying, “Shame! Shame! Naughty old man!”

At length my wig came away. The widow rushed into the house carrying it entangled in her hair, while I remained bareheaded before three servants and the parrot.

The next morning I received my wig in a parcel, accompanied by a note declaring that Mrs. Crumble could never unite herself to a person capable of such duplicity.

It would be unjust to call this event anything more than an ordinary romantic misunderstanding. Artificial hair must be secured by fastenings; natural hair is liable to entanglement; and widows are proverbially sensitive.

Notwithstanding these reflections, I was distressed.

My second matrimonial prospect was Miss Amabel Vane, a young lady possessing less property than Mrs. Crumble but considerably more beauty. She had long shown a partiality for me, and I resolved to seek consolation in her affection.

On the morning appointed for my visit, a minute particle of soot entered my right eye. This was unsurprising, since my temporary lodgings stood opposite the ruins of my house, where workmen were clearing the chimneys. The irritation caused a continual involuntary contraction of the eyelid.

As I approached Miss Vane’s residence, I perceived a female figure at an upper window. Because of the watering of my eye, I could not distinguish features and therefore passed without saluting. A second figure appeared at the next window; then a third. I judged these to be servants shaking curtains.

Upon entering, I found Miss Vane pale with anger.

“You saw me,” she said.

“Madam?”

“You looked directly at me three times. I bowed, waved my handkerchief, and threw you a rose. You winked and walked on.”

“My dear Amabel, I suffered from an ocular intrusion.”

“You winked at my aunt also.”

“A muscular necessity.”

“And at the maid.”

“The same necessity, democratically extended.”

“You then trod upon my rose.”

“I did not see it.”

“That,” she said, “I believe.”

She commanded me to leave and later accepted the hand of a military gentleman whose principal intellectual accomplishment consisted in never winking.

Thus did a speck of soot destroy my happiness.

The accumulation of perfectly natural occurrences had by this time produced in me a weariness of existence. My house was gone, my arm remained stiff, two advantageous marriages had failed, and creditors—persons ordinarily incapable of punctuality—visited me with mechanical regularity.

I considered it high time to die.

After examining the various methods with scientific impartiality, I rejected poison as dyspeptic, drowning as damp, and firearms as noisy. I selected a picturesque cliff beyond the town, intending to cast myself from its summit at sunset. There was in this design a classical simplicity which recommended it to my taste.

I went there one afternoon carrying a bottle of cordial, for a gentleman should not approach even the extinction of consciousness with a dry throat. The weather being warm, I removed my coat and the most indispensable portion of my lower apparel, lest these valuable articles be injured by contact with the rocks. I folded them neatly beside a tree, retaining only a long undergarment and one stocking, the other having been mislaid in the fire.

Before consummating my purpose, I drank the cordial.

A crow descended to a branch above me. He was an old, disreputable bird with a bluish beak and an uncertain manner of balancing. Near the tree lay a broken flask, from which he had evidently been drinking. He regarded my clothes, lurched downward, seized the indispensable portion, and flew away with it.

I shouted.

The crow alighted twenty yards distant and dropped the garment. When I approached, he snatched it up and flew farther. In this manner he led me across the hilltop, hopping, flapping, and occasionally falling upon his beak. My indignation overcame my intention of suicide. Death might wait; modesty could not.

“Stop, thief!” I cried.

The bird staggered toward the cliff. I pursued him. He dropped the garment at the very edge. I made a final spring—and discovered, too late, that the grass concealed the termination of the earth.

I ran into vacancy.

For one appalling instant I descended without obstruction. Then my right hand encountered a rope. I grasped it with such energy as remained to me and found myself swinging several hundred feet above the valley.

The rope hung from a balloon.

It was a vast yellow globe, moving slowly across the sunset. In the basket above leaned the barrel-shaped figure whose reality I had so often and so reasonably denied.

“Mein Gott!” cried the Angel of the Odd. “Vat a costume for traveling in te air!”

“Sir,” I replied, endeavoring to preserve decorum while revolving at the end of the rope, “this is no occasion for criticism. Draw me up.”

“Vy?”

“Because the rope belongs to your balloon, and common humanity—”

“I am not common humanity. I am te Odd.”

“My broken arm prevents me from climbing.”

“Ah! Te arm vat te hog proke?”

“That injury arose from a defectively positioned ladder.”

“Und te house?”

“A rat and an unrenewed policy.”

“Und te clock?”

“A raisin stem.”

“Und te vimmins?”

“A fastening and a speck of soot.”

“Und te drunken crow vat takes your—how you says?—your dignity?”

“The bird’s conduct, though reprehensible, may be explained by fermented spirits.”

At this the Angel shook so violently with laughter that the basket tilted, showering me with corks.

“Still you don’t pelief! You hangs in te sky mitout breeches, und you talks explanations!”

“Explanations distinguish philosophy from superstition.”

“Den I cuts te rope.”

He produced an enormous knife.

“Wait!” I exclaimed.

“Pelief in te Odd?”

“I acknowledge the possibility of irregular occurrence.”

“Not enough.”

“I concede the existence of highly improbable accidents.”

“More!”

“I concede that such accidents may appear to be maliciously arranged.”

“More!”

The knife touched the rope.

“I believe in the Angel of the Odd!” I cried. “I believe in his office, authority, activity, and personal presence. I believe that he governs raisin stems, rats, hogs, wigs, soot, intoxicated crows, defective ladders, insurance agents, and every other instrument of human mortification!”

“Goot!” said he. “Now ve make a little ceremony. Put your right hand in your left trousers pocket.”

“I have no trousers pocket.”

“Vat?”

“The garment in question was stolen by the crow.”

“Den put your left hand over your heart.”

“My left arm is broken.”

“Always excuses! You make te sign, or down you goes.”

“I assure you, the physical impediments are insuperable.”

“Den,” said the Angel, “te ceremony is insuperable too.”

He cut the rope.

I fell.

The balloon shot upward; the valley rushed toward me; the air roared in my ears. I had time to reflect that belief, adopted at so late a period, possesses limited practical value.

Yet I did not strike the valley.

A current of wind had carried the balloon over the town, and when the rope was severed I was directly above my rebuilt house—reconstructed, unknown to me, by a speculative purchaser. I descended feet foremost into the chimney.

My passage was narrow, rapid, and excessively sooty. I dislodged bricks, terrified pigeons, extinguished a kitchen fire, and emerged from the lower aperture in a cloud of ashes.

Or rather—I awoke.

I was lying upon the floor of my original dining room.

The fire had burned low. The newspaper covered my face. Around me lay broken plates, overturned books, corks, fragments of pudding, and the remains of the decanter. My chair was on its back; my wig—although I had never knowingly owned one—hung from the chandelier. A candle guttered beside a mouse hole. My left arm, upon which I had slept, was numb; my right hand clutched the stem of a raisin.

Near my head stood the great stoneware jug that had contained the rum.

It was empty.

A superficial observer might infer from these circumstances that I had never left the room; that the fire, the hog, the widows, the crow, the cliff, and the balloon had been the disordered productions of sleep induced by excessive food and spirits. Such an explanation has the advantage of vulgar simplicity.

It has also the disadvantage of being false.

For as I raised myself from the ashes, there issued from the mouth of the empty jug a low, cavernous gurgle; and although no person of reflective intelligence could mistake the voice, I heard it say:

“Now, perhaps, you pelief te newspaper.”

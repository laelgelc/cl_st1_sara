### THE MAN WHO LEFT POLITICS

In Cadgers, a city whose citizens boasted of honest government whenever no indictment was pending, Robinson Asbury began his public career with a blacking brush.

He was fourteen then, narrow of shoulder and grave of face, with a wooden box that he carried from the Union Depot to the courthouse square. A nickel was a nickel in those days, before municipal progress had discovered how to make it cost the taxpayer seventeen cents, and Robinson collected his nickels with care. He spoke little, listened much, and learned early that the gentleman who talked most loudly of principle generally wished his shoes polished on credit.

Cadgers was divided by custom into three parts. Uptown possessed the banks, the clubs, the newspapers, and most of the virtue. The river ward possessed the factories, the saloons, and most of the votes. Between them lay the Black district, which possessed churches, barber-shops, benevolent orders, funeral associations, debating societies, and political leaders in a proportion that would have astonished Plato.

Robinson knew them all. While his customers elevated one foot and then the other, he heard aldermen curse reformers, reformers bargain with aldermen, lawyers estimate juries, and ministers estimate collections. He discovered that public affairs were conducted by men who needed their boots cleaned like everybody else. This was his first political education, and perhaps his soundest.

At nineteen he rented a chair in Elijah Cobb’s barber-shop. At twenty-three he owned the establishment and renamed it the Equal Rights Barber-Shop. The name was much admired. White men did not patronise it, and Black men could not patronise the white shop uptown, but equality, like many excellent institutions, flourishes best upon a signboard.

The shop had four chairs, two mirrors, a stove that smoked in mild weather and failed entirely in cold, and a rear room where cigars, arguments, and candidates were consumed. Robinson stood behind the second chair with his sleeves white, his razor bright, and his eyes half shut. He had learned that a barber may contradict a customer or shave him, but should not attempt both at once.

“Rob,” said old Deacon Sykes one Saturday evening, “you got more sense than half these fellows runnin’ the ward.”

“Then I’ve got half enough to keep out of it,” said Robinson.

The remark was repeated, and within a month he had been appointed secretary of the Colored Citizens’ Improvement League.

Men are often promoted because they decline promotion in a manner suggesting that they expect it. Robinson served the league well. He wrote resolutions demanding street lamps, school repairs, equal treatment at the public hospital, and recognition by the Republican Committee. The first three demands were ignored; the fourth brought a visit from Mr. Gideon Rusk, chairman of the city organisation.

Rusk was a large, pale man whose waistcoat seemed to govern his body as firmly as he governed his party. He came to the Equal Rights Barber-Shop after closing time and entered by the rear door, for equality, though useful in elections, was not yet fashionable on Main Street.

“How many men can you influence, Asbury?” he asked.

Robinson wiped a razor slowly.

“How many do you need?”

Rusk smiled. Politicians are pleased to meet a man who speaks their native tongue without having been introduced to it.

“Two hundred in the Fifth Precinct.”

“For what?”

“For the regular ticket.”

“What does the Fifth Precinct get?”

“Good government.”

“It’s had that promised already.”

Rusk’s smile became thinner. “What do your people want?”

“A lamp on Carver Street. Two coloured policemen. Mr. Cobb’s boy on the paving gang. And the school roof fixed before it falls on the children.”

“You drive a hard bargain.”

“No, sir. A hard bargain would include the children.”

Rusk laughed, and the compact was made. The roof remained defective until December, but the lamp appeared, one policeman was appointed, and Cobb’s boy received a shovel bearing the city’s initials. Robinson delivered two hundred and forty-three votes. From that hour Mr. Rusk called him “our representative among the coloured people,” while the coloured people called him “our representative with the white folks.” There is a whole system of government in the distinction.

His rise was rapid because his ambitions wore working clothes. He organised clubs, arranged picnics, found bail for unlucky constituents, secured small jobs for deserving men and larger jobs for useful ones, attended weddings, subscribed to funerals, and never forgot a face that possessed a vote. If a widow’s coal was low, he knew it. If a labourer had been dismissed, he heard of it before the labourer’s wife. He could not remedy every misfortune, but he listened to all of them, and the poor will sometimes forgive failure sooner than inattention.

In due course Robinson sold the barber-shop to his chief assistant and studied law. This caused uneasiness among those already practising it.

The two most eminent Black attorneys in Cadgers were Mr. Julius Bingo and Mr. Napoleon Latchett. Bingo was round, shining, and eloquent. He had a gold watch, a frock coat, and the conviction that these accessories proved his fitness for leadership. Latchett was thin, suspicious, and learned in objections. Between them they possessed six diplomas, three secret-society titles, a carriage purchased on instalments, and very few clients.

They had tolerated Asbury the barber. They disliked Asbury the politician. Asbury the lawyer was an offence against nature.

“Law is a learned profession,” Bingo observed at the Athenaeum Literary Circle. “It cannot be entered by every person who has acquired popularity in a shaving establishment.”

“No,” said Latchett. “There must be standards.”

Their concern for standards increased when Robinson passed his examination. It was whispered that questions had been supplied to him by friends at the courthouse. It was also whispered that Bingo had taken the examination three times and Latchett four. The first whisper proved nothing, and the second explained much.

Robinson opened an office above a druggist in the Black district. Bingo advised him to move uptown.

“A lawyer needs dignity,” he said. “You can’t preserve dignity over Haskins’s cough syrup.”

“My clients live down here,” Robinson replied.

“The best people won’t come.”

“The best people don’t get arrested.”

Within a year he had more business than both rivals. His practice was not distinguished, but it was abundant. He defended gamblers, collected wages, settled quarrels, drafted wills for men whose possessions consisted of a feather bed and an insurance certificate, and extracted small justice from institutions that had not intended to surrender any. He charged those who could pay and remembered those who could not. Gratitude may be an uncertain currency, but in politics it bears compound interest.

Bingo and Latchett therefore established the Progressive Colored League, whose declared purpose was to uplift the race and whose immediate purpose was to lower Robinson Asbury.

They invited physicians, teachers, undertakers, prosperous grocers, and ladies with social aspirations. No barber was admitted unless he had retired from shaving. The league announced that leadership must pass from “ward manipulators” to “men of education, property, and character.” This phrase was printed in the uptown newspapers, where it gave much satisfaction. Nothing pleases the powerful more than discovering that the best people among the powerless agree with them.

The annual Emancipation Day celebration provided the first trial of strength. For years Robinson’s Citizens’ Club had conducted the parade. Bingo’s league now declared that the occasion should be rescued from vulgar political control and placed upon a higher plane. It selected Bingo as grand marshal, Latchett as chairman of resolutions, and a white reformer from uptown as principal speaker.

Robinson made no protest.

“You goin’ to let ’em take the day?” asked Deacon Sykes.

“It belongs to everybody,” said Robinson.

“You ain’t doin’ nothin’?”

“I’m having a parade.”

That was all.

On the morning of the celebration, Bingo’s procession assembled before the Athenaeum Hall. It consisted of one brass band, two carriages, forty-seven league members, twelve schoolchildren dressed as states, and a banner bearing the words INTELLIGENCE, MORALITY, ADVANCEMENT. Arizona was absent with the measles, and Rhode Island had lost a shoe.

Bingo sat in the first carriage wearing a sash broad enough for a national emergency. He had just ordered the band to begin when a distant thunder rolled across the district.

From Carver Street came Robinson’s parade.

First marched three bands, each playing a different patriotic tune with equal determination. Then came the veterans, the church lodges, the washerwomen’s union, the porters’ association, the barbers, the bricklayers, the cooks, the coachmen, the Republican clubs, the independent clubs, the women’s auxiliary, six floats, nine carriages, two hundred schoolchildren, and an aged white horse disguised as Liberty. Banners filled the street. Flags leaned from windows. The crowd stretched so far back that its rear ranks were still forming when its leaders reached the courthouse.

Robinson rode neither horse nor carriage. He walked beside the veterans, wearing an ordinary black coat.

When Bingo’s band perceived the multitude, it stopped playing from motives that may have been musical or political. His forty-seven men joined Robinson’s procession by natural stages. First the schoolchildren wandered over to see Liberty. Then the band accepted a better position. Finally the league members discovered old acquaintances in the passing clubs and fell in beside them. Bingo and Latchett were left with their carriage, their sash, and the white reformer, who asked whether lunch would be served.

Thus ended the Progressive Colored League. Organisations based on principle are frequently destroyed by a larger organisation with a band.

Robinson’s authority was now undisputed. He became a delegate to conventions, an assistant prosecutor for a season, and at last the acknowledged distributor of patronage in the Black district. Men said he carried two thousand votes in his vest pocket. This was an exaggeration; a number of them were in his coat.

Success, however, exposes a man to friendship, and friendship in politics is merely hostility awaiting information.

Bingo submitted. He praised Robinson in public, consulted him in private, and spoke of their former differences as “the natural misunderstandings of ambitious young men.” Latchett remained sour and was therefore harmless. Bingo became pleasant and was consequently dangerous.

Robinson knew this. Yet continual victory produces in wise men the same defect that it produces in fools: they begin to believe themselves exceptions. When a close city election approached, he admitted Bingo to meetings that had formerly been reserved for Rusk, Judge Amos Davis, two contractors, and men who were never named in newspapers except as “prominent citizens.”

The party was in difficulty. Its mayor had been renominated despite an administration so economical that the city had paid twice for nearly everything. The opposition promised reform, lower taxes, cleaner streets, and an investigation. Such promises are alarming because, though no sensible person expects all of them to be kept, one can never tell which may be attempted.

Three precincts had to be managed. Registration lists were adjusted, repeaters were imported from the river ward, and certain election officers received envelopes in which the party’s appreciation took a negotiable form. Robinson directed the work in his district. Bingo assisted with enthusiasm.

“Is it safe?” Bingo asked.

“Nothing is safe,” said Robinson. “That’s why men ask whether it is.”

“But everybody’s in it?”

“In politics we are all lambs. The wolves are only to be found in the other party.”

Bingo laughed and accepted another envelope.

The regular ticket won by one hundred and twelve votes. Church bells rang, saloons remained open, and Chairman Rusk declared that the people had vindicated honest government.

Three weeks later reform fever broke out.

Its immediate cause was a disappointed contractor who had kept copies of letters. The newspapers published them beneath headlines usually reserved for war. Citizens who had voted the regular ticket discovered that they had always distrusted it. Clergymen demanded purification. Businessmen demanded stability. The mayor demanded time. A committee of one hundred demanded everybody’s resignation except its own.

Reform fever resembles other civic epidemics in attacking memory first. Rusk forgot the rear room of the Equal Rights Barber-Shop. The mayor forgot Rusk. Contractors forgot the mayor. Bingo forgot the envelopes. Only Robinson remembered everything, which made him unsuitable for continued liberty.

The party leaders met in Judge Davis’s library. Davis had been raised from a poor family in the river ward and had not forgotten that Robinson’s influence helped put him on the bench. He was neither innocent nor ungrateful, two qualities that rarely flourish together in public life.

“The grand jury wants someone,” Rusk said.

“It wants facts,” replied Davis.

“My dear Judge, facts are someones.”

Rusk proposed that the organisation separate itself from “unauthorised irregularities in the coloured precincts.” The phrase had already been prepared for the newspapers. Robinson listened with his eyes half closed.

“So I acted alone?” he asked.

“No one says that,” Rusk answered.

“You’re saying it now.”

“The party must be protected.”

“From what?”

“From public suspicion.”

Robinson looked around the library. There were six men present. He had bought votes for four, collected votes for one, and helped elect the sixth.

“Suspicion has made a good selection,” he said.

Judge Davis objected. “If Asbury goes down, others go with him.”

Rusk lowered his voice. “Unless others testify.”

There it stood. Municipal reform had built its altar and required a sacrifice. Since the white leaders were essential to civic stability, and the Black leader was essential only on election day, Providence indicated the victim.

Bingo supplied the knife.

Called before the grand jury, he admitted with sorrow that Robinson had directed illegal payments. He himself had been present, but only, he said, to dissuade his old friend from a dangerous course. He produced notes, dates, and the names of election officers. His memory, recently so infirm, recovered under oath with almost indecent vigour.

Robinson was indicted for conspiracy and election fraud.

The trial filled the courthouse. Uptown reformers occupied the front benches, the Black district crowded the galleries, and reporters sharpened pencils beneath portraits of deceased judges who had escaped investigation by dying at appropriate times.

Bingo testified for two days. He wore black and spoke sadly.

“Mr. Asbury had great influence over me,” he said. “I regarded him as a leader.”

“Did you accept money from him?” asked Robinson’s counsel.

“I accepted what I believed to be campaign funds.”

“And distributed it?”

“Under protest.”

“How was the protest expressed?”

“I cautioned him privately.”

“Before or after you distributed the money?”

Bingo hesitated. “Both.”

This reply satisfied the jury more than it deserved.

Rusk testified that he had demanded an honest election. The mayor testified that he knew nothing of details. Contractors testified that their payments were for legitimate services. Party officials became, one after another, spotless spectators at a crime that had somehow elected them.

Robinson sat impassive.

He was convicted.

Before sentence was passed, he rose and asked permission to speak. The judge presiding was not Davis; reform had prudently selected a man unembarrassed by gratitude.

Robinson began quietly.

“I have been found guilty of corrupting an election. The verdict is lawful. Whether it is lonely is another matter.”

Then he opened a small notebook.

He named the ward captain who ordered false registrations. He named the contractor who supplied money. He named Rusk’s messenger. He gave the sum paid to each precinct officer, the room where it was counted, the hour when the mayor’s secretary telephoned, the number of repeaters brought from the river ward, and the saloon where they were fed. He described Bingo’s part without ornament. He described Rusk’s part with dates. He described the mayor’s part with copies of letters.

The courtroom stirred, groaned, and finally erupted. It was as if Samson, finding himself tied to the altar of reform, had pulled down the roof and pillars upon the congregation.

Only one name Robinson did not speak.

Judge Davis sat behind a screen in his chambers, hearing every word. He waited for his own name. It did not come.

The court sentenced Robinson to one year. With good conduct, he served ten months.

The party congratulated itself upon purification until further indictments made congratulations unsuitable. Rusk resigned for reasons of health and went into the insurance business. The mayor completed his term but was not renominated. Bingo prospered briefly. He was praised as a courageous representative of a new and moral leadership and received a minor appointment from the administration. Men who had once passed him in the street now invited him to lunch, though seldom at clubs.

Meanwhile Robinson became a martyr.

The Black district had not supposed him innocent. Innocence was not the point. It knew that many had feasted and one had been handed the bill. Mothers took children to see the prison walls. Ministers referred to “our persecuted brother” without inquiring too closely into the envelopes. A relief committee supported his wife. His photograph appeared in barber-shops, lodge halls, and parlours, sometimes beside Lincoln and sometimes above the mantel clock.

When he returned to Cadgers, five thousand people met him at the depot.

There were bands, banners, flowers, carriages, resolutions, and a delegation of children who presented him with a Bible. Robinson thanked them from the station steps.

“I have made mistakes,” he said. “I have paid for them. I’m grateful to my friends. I ask only to be allowed to earn an honest living.”

The crowd cheered for ten minutes.

He opened a newsstand opposite the courthouse.

It was a modest establishment containing newspapers, magazines, tobacco, sweets, and a rear room with three chairs. Robinson sat near the entrance in shirtsleeves, sold the reform newspapers that had denounced him, and answered political inquiries with one sentence.

“I am not in politics.”

The statement was accepted uptown with relief and downtown with amusement.

Judge Davis visited him one evening.

“Why did you spare me?” he asked.

Robinson arranged a row of cigars.

“You objected.”

“Not enough.”

“You objected when the rest were agreeing.”

“I was in those meetings. I knew the arrangements.”

“Yes.”

“You could have ruined me.”

“Yes.”

Davis waited, but Robinson offered no moral decoration.

“I owe you my place on the bench,” the judge said at last.

“You’ve been a fair judge.”

“Not always.”

“Nobody’s always anything.”

They shook hands. It was one of the few transactions in Cadgers for which neither man requested a receipt.

Two years passed. Bingo’s appointment ended, and the administration, having extracted the full value of his courage, neglected to renew it. He returned to law and public leadership, but neither prospered. The best people still praised him; unfortunately, the best people were not numerous enough to provide clients.

Then came another Emancipation Day.

Professor Isaac Morton, principal of Douglass School, was chosen by church and educational groups to direct the celebration. Morton was a tall, earnest man who believed that public occasions should elevate the mind. He planned historical tableaux, children’s choruses, and speeches on citizenship. He had little money, no organisation, and an unfortunate trust in the reasonableness of mankind.

Bingo announced a rival parade.

He declared that Morton was an impractical schoolmaster controlled by hidden politicians. The phrase “hidden politicians” was thought to refer to Robinson, though Robinson had not spoken to Morton in months.

Bingo formed the United Emancipation Committee, selected himself as grand marshal, collected funds, hired two bands, and secured promises from nearly every lodge in the district. Latchett, faithful to lost causes, became secretary.

“Bingo’s going to crush Morton,” Deacon Sykes observed at the newsstand.

Robinson handed him a newspaper.

“I’m not in politics.”

A week later Bingo entered the rear room.

He closed the door and sat down with the air of a man who has forgiven another for being betrayed by him.

“Rob,” he said, “we’ve had our differences.”

“Have we?”

“Let the dead past bury its dead.”

“Some of it’s alive.”

Bingo cleared his throat. “This Morton affair is bigger than personalities. He’s dividing the people.”

“With one parade?”

“He’s being used. I need your friends to prevent a disaster.”

“My friends belong to themselves.”

“Of course. But they listen to you.”

“They buy papers.”

Bingo leaned forward. “You don’t want Morton built up as the new leader.”

Robinson’s face did not change, but Bingo thought he detected interest. Vanity has excellent eyesight for objects that do not exist.

“What do you want?” Robinson asked.

“The clubs. The lodge captains. Sykes’s men. The women’s auxiliary. If you quietly tell them to come with us, Morton won’t have two hundred people.”

Robinson considered.

“I am not in politics.”

“This isn’t politics. It’s a celebration.”

“That’s different.”

“Then you’ll help?”

“I’ll speak to some friends.”

Bingo departed radiant.

Robinson did speak to friends. He spoke to Deacon Sykes, Mrs. Amanda Green of the women’s auxiliary, the three bandmasters, twelve lodge officers, seven ward workers, four ministers, and the owner of Liberty, who was now a grey horse of advanced years and fixed opinions.

To each he said nearly the same thing.

“Bingo expects you at his assembly point. Take your people there. Wear his badges. Let him arrange you. When the Douglass School bell rings at ten, march to Professor Morton.”

No one asked why. A leader is not the man who gives the best reasons, but the man whose reasons have ceased to be required.

On Emancipation morning, Bingo stood in Jackson Square surrounded by triumph. Clubs arrived from every street. Bands tuned their instruments. Lodge officers saluted him. Women pinned on badges. Children climbed floats. His parade was larger than Robinson’s old procession had been.

Latchett ran about with a clipboard.

“We have three thousand at least,” he gasped.

“Four,” said Bingo. “The people know their leader.”

At nine forty-five, Bingo climbed into a decorated carriage. He wore a white sash and carried a silver-headed baton purchased for the occasion. A photographer recorded him in the attitude of command.

At ten, the bell of Douglass School began to ring.

The first band stopped in the middle of a march. Its leader turned east.

“What are you doing?” Bingo called.

The band struck up “John Brown’s Body” and moved toward the school.

Behind it went the Veterans’ Lodge. Then the women’s auxiliary wheeled its float. The church clubs followed. One by one, section after section turned away from Jackson Square and poured into Morton’s route.

“Hold your places!” shouted Bingo.

No one held them.

Latchett seized a lodge captain by the sleeve. “You’re assigned to Division Three!”

“I been reassigned.”

“By whom?”

The captain glanced toward the courthouse, where Robinson sat beneath the awning of his newsstand reading a paper.

“Circumstances,” he said.

Within fifteen minutes Bingo’s mighty organisation had vanished. It had not dispersed; that would have been a misfortune. It had transferred itself entire to his rival, which was a judgment.

Professor Morton stood before Douglass School bewildered as bands, banners, floats, lodges, children, veterans, ministers, and several thousand citizens arrived to place themselves under his direction. Robinson appeared at last, walking quietly beside Deacon Sykes.

Morton grasped his hand.

“Mr. Asbury, I don’t understand this.”

“Then you’re qualified to lead it,” said Robinson.

“What shall I do?”

“Start the parade.”

Morton did. It was the largest Emancipation celebration Cadgers had ever seen. Bingo remained in Jackson Square with Latchett, the decorated carriage, the photographer, and seven small boys who had stayed because they expected refreshments.

His ruin was complete.

The administration now became anxious. A man who could destroy an organisation without appearing to possess one was a public danger. Delegations called at the newsstand.

The new party chairman offered Robinson control of district patronage.

“I am not in politics,” Robinson said.

A councilman suggested an appointment to the board of charities.

“I’m an ex-convict.”

“That needn’t prevent—”

“It prevented you from speaking to me last year.”

A businessman offered support for a pardon.

“I served my time.”

Bingo came once, pale with fury.

“You lied to me.”

“No.”

“You said you’d speak to your friends.”

“I did.”

“You promised they’d come to my parade.”

“They came.”

“You stole my whole organisation!”

Robinson looked up from a magazine.

“If it was yours, how could I?”

“You planned this from the beginning.”

“You give me too much credit.”

“I’ll expose you.”

“As what?”

Bingo opened his mouth, but no convenient crime presented itself.

“A politician!” he cried.

Robinson smiled.

“I am not in politics.”

The city election approached. The regular party had recovered from reform by adopting all its phrases. Its platform promised integrity, economy, justice, progress, and continuation in office. The opposition promised the same things in a different order.

The contest was expected to be close. Both sides sought Robinson.

He refused them equally.

“I have no organisation,” he told reporters. “I sell newspapers. I advise every citizen to vote according to conscience.”

This statement caused grave concern, for conscience is political language for an influence the speaker does not care to identify.

Chairman Rusk, restored to unofficial usefulness, came to the newsstand by the rear door.

“We treated you badly,” he admitted.

“You treated me politically.”

“Let’s not dwell on the past.”

“I did ten months in it.”

Rusk offered money, appointments, and control of the Fifth Precinct. Robinson shook his head.

“What do you want?” Rusk demanded.

“Nothing.”

No politician believes that another man wants nothing. It suggests either insanity or a price beyond calculation.

“We can’t undo what happened.”

“No.”

“We can make it right.”

“No.”

“Then what are you going to do?”

“Vote.”

“Your vote only counts one.”

“Just the same,” Robinson said.

Two nights before the election, visitors entered the rear room of the newsstand singly. Deacon Sykes arrived at seven. Mrs. Green came at seven fifteen. Bandmasters, ministers, lodge captains, barbers, porters, cooks, labourers, schoolteachers, saloonkeepers, and ward workers followed. No minutes were kept. No resolutions were adopted. No chairman was elected. Robinson had learned that formal organisations are chiefly useful for providing traitors with a membership list.

His instructions were brief.

“Vote the regular party out.”

“What about Judge Davis?” Sykes asked. Davis was standing for another term on the same ticket.

“Keep him.”

“Anybody else?”

“No.”

“How many can we bring?”

“All.”

Before dawn on election day, a dark bank of clouds hung over Cadgers. The regular leaders looked at the sky and hoped for rain. Bad weather, they believed, would reduce the Black vote. The belief was one of many for which they had no evidence beyond desire.

At six, the polls opened.

The voters came.

They came from Carver Street and the alleys behind it, from the factory road, the kitchens uptown, the railway yards, the barber-shops, laundries, churches, classrooms, stables, and boardinghouses. Old men arrived leaning on canes. Women came before domestic work. Porters came between trains. Labourers came in work clothes. Wagons carried the infirm. Lodge captains checked names from memory. Ministers watched precinct doors. Lawyers challenged suspicious ballots, though neither Bingo nor Latchett had been retained.

By eight o’clock the Black district was voting like steady rain.

By noon it was a flood.

Rusk telephoned Robinson.

“What are you doing?”

“Selling newspapers.”

“Your people are knifing us.”

“They’re citizens.”

“Call them off.”

“I’m not in politics.”

“We’ll give you anything.”

“I don’t need anything today.”

That was true, and therefore terrible.

The regular party lost the mayoralty, the council, the prosecutor, the sheriff, and every contested county office. Its candidates went down one after another like dignified gentlemen stepping through rotten floorboards.

Judge Davis survived by six hundred and eleven votes.

The newspapers called the result a tidal wave, a moral awakening, a popular revolt, and an unexpected manifestation of independent voting in the coloured wards. None mentioned the newsstand until the defeated chairman, deprived by grief of discretion, declared that Robinson Asbury had engineered the catastrophe.

Reporters crowded around Robinson the next morning.

“Did you order the vote against the regular ticket?”

“No.”

“Did you organise the Fifth Precinct?”

“No.”

“Did you work for the opposition?”

“No.”

“Why was Judge Davis spared?”

“He’s a good judge.”

“Are you returning to politics?”

Robinson folded a newspaper and placed it on the counter.

“I am not in politics.”

Cadgers laughed when it read the words, but it did not laugh comfortably.

The new mayor called upon him within a week. So did the new chairman, three councilmen, two contractors, five ministers, the superintendent of schools, and a delegation of citizens interested in municipal harmony. They entered by the front door.

Robinson accepted no office. He requested no title. He remained at the newsstand, where newspapers passed through his hands in the morning and men passed through the rear room at night. Professor Morton retained leadership of the Emancipation celebration but wisely consulted Robinson about the route. Judge Davis continued on the bench and became notably careful in election cases. Bingo moved to another city, where he announced that Cadgers had never appreciated educated leadership. Latchett remained and explained Bingo’s failure to anyone who would listen.

As for the regular party, it discovered respect for the Black electorate. Respect in politics is what fear calls itself when asking for votes.

Thereafter candidates did not merely send messages into Robinson’s district. They came. They climbed the worn step, stood beside the tobacco case, and waited while he served customers. Some offered programmes, some apologies, and some promises. Robinson listened with his half-shut eyes and inscrutable face.

He had risen by polishing the shoes of powerful men, and he ended by making those men wipe their own before entering his shop.

Be it said that he never again accepted an official political position. The statement is literally true, which is the form of truth most convenient to politics. He held no chairmanship, signed no orders, kept no membership roll, and appeared on no platform. Yet parades turned when his bell sounded; candidates survived when his silence permitted it; and parties learned that a man may possess an army without uniforms, an office without a sign, and authority without a name.

Cadgers at last understood him. It did not make the mistake of calling him virtuous. Political virtue is always in a minority and usually unable to carry its own precinct. Nor did the city call him innocent, for it remembered the envelopes, the trial, and the ten months. But it knew that guilt shared by many had been fastened upon one, that betrayal had mistaken patience for defeat, and that a quiet man behind a counter could be more dangerous than a shouting man upon a platform.

Robinson Asbury had left politics.

Politics, however, had not been able to leave Robinson Asbury.

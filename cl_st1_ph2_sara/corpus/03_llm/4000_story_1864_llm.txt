### The Weighty Administration of Wouter Van Twiller

In the year of our Lord 1629, when the Hudson ran as tranquilly between its wooded shores as though it had not yet heard of commerce, litigation, or other inventions by which mankind disturbs the peace of Providence, there arrived at New Amsterdam a governor whose virtues were peculiarly adapted to that innocent age.

The spring had opened with uncommon ceremony. The peach trees put forth their blossoms; the wild geese held consultations in the marshes; the frogs, those hereditary burgomasters of every Dutch ditch, delivered nightly orations; and the sun, after lingering behind the hills of New Jersey, rose at last upon the broad roofs and weathercocks of the settlement. At this auspicious moment Mynheer Wouter Van Twiller came into office under the commission and control of their High Mightinesses, to administer the laws, protect the trade, preserve the dignity, and consume the provisions of the colony.

Concerning the ancestry of this renowned personage, I have collected several biographical anecdotes from old women, retired constables, and the margins of worm-eaten account books. Some affirm that his grandfather was a dealer in wooden shoes at Rotterdam; others that he descended from a famous timmerman who built a henhouse for the Stadtholder. There is likewise a tradition that one of his forefathers slept through the siege of a certain town and thereby escaped being killed. As this last circumstance bears a striking resemblance to the genius of the family, I am inclined to credit it; nevertheless, in obedience to the severity of historical truth, I shall leave the matter undecided.

Wouter himself possessed, from his earliest years, that profound gravity which common men often mistake for wisdom, and which wise men, having discovered the advantage of the mistake, are careful never to correct. He was not talkative. Indeed, it was doubted whether his silence arose from the multitude of his thoughts or from their absence; but as either condition rendered speech inconvenient, the result was equally admirable.

There are men who deliver opinions before they have considered them, and men who consider so long that the necessity for an opinion passes away. Wouter belonged to the latter and more dignified order. If asked whether the moon were round, he would smoke a pipe, close his left eye, open his right, and reply that much might be said upon both sides. If informed that it rained, he would look solemnly from the window, observe the wetness of the street, and suspend his judgment until the weather cleared. Thus did he avoid error—not by arriving at truth, but by never arriving anywhere.

His person furnished an indubitable sign of the repose within. His head was nearly spherical, though somewhat flattened where it rested upon the cushion; his body proceeded downward with the regular amplitude of a prosperous pudding; while his short legs appeared less intended for locomotion than for supporting him when a chair was unavailable. His cheeks, broad and mottled, shone like two winter apples kept in a warm cellar. Taken altogether, he resembled a municipal beer-cask which, by some caprice of the cooper, had been furnished with buttons, breeches, and a pipe.

Yet let no malicious reader infer from this description that the excellent Wouter was gross or inactive. I record only appearances, which are notoriously deceitful, especially when they happen to be accurate.

His daily occupations were distributed with a precision worthy of Apollo regulating the celestial courses. Four hours he devoted to eating, in which duty he was punctual; eight to smoking, listening, and doubting; and twelve to sleep, that mysterious condition wherein great rulers are delivered from flatterers and their subjects from government. On extraordinary occasions, such as public thanksgiving or the arrival of a cheese from Holland, the hours of eating were enlarged, and those of doubt proportionately diminished.

Every morning he seated himself in a carved oak chair before the door of the government house. There, with an amber pipe in one hand and a silver tobacco-box in the other, he contemplated the affairs of the province. The smoke rose in tranquil clouds about his head, veiling his countenance like the mist that concealed the counsels of the ancient oracles. Petitioners, seeing him thus enveloped, waited respectfully for wisdom to emerge; but the smoke commonly cleared before the wisdom did.

When his council assembled, the governor occupied the largest chair and consequently appeared to possess the largest understanding. The burgomasters argued, the schepens contradicted them, the secretary sharpened his quill, and the constable stood by with that expression of legal acumen which is produced by a tight belt and complete ignorance. Throughout these commotions Wouter remained immovable. At intervals he laid a finger beside his nose, shook his head, and uttered a guttural sound. These signs were variously interpreted. One councillor believed them to indicate approval; another, objection; a third, digestion. Thus every man departed satisfied that the governor agreed with him.

Such was the tranquillity of this administration that no enemies were punished, no abuses corrected, and no questions settled. The charitable attributed this to mercy; the prudent to caution; and those who had business before the government to sleepiness. I shall not presume to decide among opinions so respectable.

It is related that once, on being warned of possible hostilities from a neighboring people, Wouter smoked three pipes and fell asleep over the dispatch. When he awoke, the danger had passed. This fortunate result established his reputation for military foresight. Even Haroun Alraschid, who wandered by night to discover the grievances of Bagdad, could not have served his capital more effectually; for while that restless monarch exposed himself to assassins, Wouter remained safely indoors and allowed grievances to discover themselves.

But the brightest jewel in the crown of his renown was a judgment delivered soon after his accession, which I shall set down with all the solemnity due to the only decisive act of his government.

There lived in New Amsterdam two reputable burghers, Wandle Schoonhoven and Barent Bleecker. Wandle dealt in flour, dried fish, and such other articles as gain weight in damp weather; Barent traded in timber, nails, and opinions. Between them had existed for years a complicated traffic. Wandle supplied Barent with three barrels of meal; Barent furnished Wandle with planks for a pigsty; one charged cartage, the other storage; both charged interest; and neither remembered what had been paid.

At length each discovered, by the clearest computation, that the other owed him money.

Their dispute first occupied the tavern, where it was argued over beer; then the street, where it was argued without beer and therefore with less moderation; and finally the court, where men are permitted to quarrel at public expense. Each appeared bearing an account book of formidable dimensions, tied with string and fortified by scraps of paper. Wandle’s book was bound in brown leather and smelled faintly of herring. Barent’s was covered in calfskin and ornamented with a brass clasp that had long ceased to clasp.

The court convened. The secretary prepared a fresh quill; the constable cried for silence in a voice that produced the contrary effect; and Governor Van Twiller sat in judgment, looking as immovable as King Log after a substantial dinner.

Wandle opened the proceedings. He recounted barrels, half-barrels, nails, boards, cartloads, days of labor, spoiled flour, borrowed rope, and one jack-knife never returned. He cited dates going back to seasons whose existence no living person could confirm. Whenever he arrived at a total, Barent groaned; whenever Barent groaned, Wandle increased the total.

Barent then delivered his defense, which was indistinguishable from an accusation. He referred to rafters, shingles, hinges, damages, counter-damages, and a pig alleged to have eaten part of a receipt. He produced witnesses who remembered nothing with impressive exactness. One testified that Wandle had once said something near the ferry; another was certain Barent had nodded in a suspicious manner on a Tuesday.

During all this, the sage governor smoked.

At the end of the second hour he turned Wandle’s book upside down. At the end of the third he opened Barent’s in the middle and regarded a column of figures as an owl regards daylight—with solemn attention and no visible benefit. At the fourth hour he ate a slice of pudding, justice being exhausting even when not administered.

The burgomasters whispered. The litigants perspired. The constable, overwhelmed by the majesty of the law, fell asleep standing.

At last Wouter removed his pipe.

“Bring me the books,” said he.

A silence descended upon the chamber. The volumes were placed before him. He examined their bindings, counted their pages, measured their thickness with his thumb, and held each at arm’s length. He then requested a pair of scales from the nearest merchant. These being produced, he laid Wandle’s ledger in one pan and Barent’s in the other.

The scales trembled.

The court leaned forward.

The governor added to Barent’s side a loose receipt. That pan sank. He removed it and placed upon Wandle’s side a paper concerning the jack-knife. The balance wavered once more, then came to rest with both books suspended at an equal height.

Wouter contemplated this phenomenon for the duration of half a pipe. Finally he raised his hand.

“Inasmuch,” said he, “as the accounts are of equal thickness, contain an equal abundance of writing, and weigh precisely alike, it is the final opinion of the court that they are balanced.”

The secretary’s quill stopped in midair.

“Therefore,” continued Wouter, gathering confidence from the unusual experience of having begun a sentence, “the said Wandle shall give a receipt to the said Barent, and the said Barent shall give a receipt to the said Wandle. And as the constable has slept through the hearing, he shall pay the costs.”

Having delivered this judgment, the governor replaced his pipe and resumed his ordinary uncertainty.

For one moment the assembly remained astonished. Then the burgomasters nodded. The schepens declared that Solomon himself could not have reasoned more equitably. Wandle, reflecting that he had escaped paying Barent, was satisfied; Barent, perceiving that he had escaped paying Wandle, was equally pleased; and the constable, who understood neither the suit nor the judgment, paid the costs rather than confess that he had been asleep.

News of the decision traveled through New Amsterdam. It was repeated at firesides, warehouses, docks, and taverns. Some praised its mathematical precision; others its merciful spirit; all admired the marvelous simplicity by which accounts, being too difficult to understand, had been prevented from causing further inconvenience.

Its effects were immediate and beneficent. Men who contemplated lawsuits first weighed their ledgers at home. Finding them commonly equal—for Dutch accounts of that age were all written upon heavy paper—they compromised their quarrels. Attorneys, having nothing to do, returned to honest trades. The court grew silent, the constable slept without interruption, and the colony entered upon a golden age of jurisprudence.

This remained the sole decisive judgment of Governor Wouter Van Twiller. He never hazarded another, perhaps from modesty, perhaps from fear of diminishing its glory. For the rest of his administration he ate, smoked, slept, and doubted with unaltered dignity; and the people, enjoying peace beneath a ruler who did almost nothing, wisely concluded that nothing could have been better done.

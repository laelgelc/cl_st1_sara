# The Adventures of Oliver Bowman

Oliver Bowman had arrived at the age of thirty-two without ever having been thoroughly wet.

Rain had touched him, certainly. On several occasions a drop had fallen between his collar and his neck, producing a sensation so immediate and personal that he had remembered it for days; but he had never been soaked, drenched, buffeted, or otherwise admitted into that rude fraternity of persons who return home with their trousers adhering to their legs. He possessed waterproof boots, an umbrella of exceptional circumference, and a cab allowance which his father’s trustees regarded as extravagant. Between these three defences and a constitutional caution, the weather had been reduced for him to an agreeable spectacle observed through glass.

His sister Alice considered this deplorable.

“You have never experienced anything,” she said one evening, cracking a walnut with a severity that seemed directed less against the nut than against Oliver’s character.

They sat in the smaller drawing-room of their house in Kensington. Oliver occupied the sofa beneath the shaded lamp and was embroidering, in silks of a very subdued brilliance, a shepherdess who had lost her way among lilac bushes. Alice, who had spent the afternoon turning the leg of a table on a small lathe in the conservatory, wore a rough tweed skirt, smoked a cigarette, and drank port.

“I experience a great deal,” said Oliver, selecting a green fine enough for a distant willow. “Only I prefer that it should happen at a reasonable distance.”

“That is not experience.”

“It is the civilised form of it.”

Alice tossed the shell into the fire.

“You read about storms, voyages, duels, revolutions and love affairs. You would dislike every one of them.”

“Undoubtedly. That is why I read about them. A storm at sea is superb in Conrad and intolerable in the Channel. Revolutions are magnificent until somebody breaks a window. As for duels, the objection is obvious.”

“And love affairs?”

Oliver paused over the shepherdess.

“They appear to require late hours and an excessive correspondence.”

“You are an old maid.”

“My dear Alice, if masculinity consists of eating walnuts with port, I willingly resign it to you.”

Alice laughed. She had an honest, square laugh which made the lamp-shade tremble.

“One day,” she said, “I shall see you doing something. Riding, perhaps. Or shooting. You may even play golf.”

“Only if the ball remains perfectly still.”

“It does.”

“That makes the game less excusable.”

Their antagonism was an old one and, because each secretly found the other useful as an example of how not to live, a comfortable one. Alice went out in frost, handled tools, visited stables, knew how to bandage a dog’s paw, and had once put out a chimney fire before the servants had finished disagreeing about the extinguisher. Oliver disliked dogs when they were moist, horses when they moved, and servants when they had news. His health was delicate chiefly because he attended to it. He took hot milk at eleven, rested after luncheon, and possessed three thermometers, none of which had ever predicted anything serious.

His pleasures were selected for their immunity from consequences. He liked music when performed in another room, gardens when printed in colour, and rustic life when represented upon china. He owned a set of plates on which red-cheeked peasants danced beneath an elm; the fact that real peasants perspired, had political opinions, and occasionally came to mend the drains did not diminish his affection for them, because he never confused the plates with agriculture.

Yet Alice’s accusation troubled him. Not because he desired action, but because he disliked being thought deficient. He went to bed that night with a volume of Pierre Loti and read of coral lagoons, white sails, tattooed sailors, and blue distances until the hot-water bottle cooled at his feet. Then, during that suspended interval when the room is dark but the mind still retains the furniture, an idea came to him.

It was not necessary, he perceived, that experience should be complete.

The ordinary person, travelling to the South Seas, was subjected to tedium, expense, insects, quarrels, heat, seasickness, and the society of other travellers. These were not the adventure; they were the tax imposed upon it. The essence lay in a few chosen sensations: the odour of salt and tar, a blue-green light, the plash of water, a native melody imperfectly remembered. If these could be obtained separately, under favourable conditions, one might enjoy the imaginative profit of travel without paying its physical liabilities.

Oliver sat up in bed.

Destinies are sometimes determined by cannon, earthquakes and declarations of war; but quite as often they depend upon a cold hot-water bottle and a sensitive gentleman’s unwillingness to ring for the maid.

The next morning he began his experiments.

He first attempted Venice. A book of photographs supplied the architecture; a bowl of water, placed where the sun could touch it, furnished the tremulous reflections; and from the street came the intermittent cries of a man selling fish. Oliver added a sprig of rosemary, because he had once read that Italy smelt of herbs, and reclined upon the sofa while a musical box played an air which might have been Venetian if one did not listen closely. Within ten minutes he was gliding beneath marble balconies. Black gondolas nosed the green water; a masked lady dropped a carnation from a bridge; the bells of the Salute floated through the dusk.

Unfortunately, the fishmonger began to quarrel with a cook, and Venice dissolved into Hammersmith.

Still, the experiment had succeeded.

Paris proved simpler. Coffee, scent, a fashion paper and the distant grinding of a barrel-organ created the boulevard with almost vulgar promptitude. For the Arctic, Oliver stood before the open ice chest wearing Alice’s white fur motor-coat and reading an account of Nansen. India required sandalwood, a brass tray, and a yellow curtain illuminated from behind. He achieved Japan with a paper parasol, a pot of chrysanthemums, and three lines from a travel essay by Mr. Pater, whose prose could Orientalise even a bathroom.

The new method altered his reading. Formerly he had accepted the author’s imagery with gratitude; now he collaborated. When a novelist mentioned a rose garden, Oliver uncorked attar. When cavalry crossed a plain, he beat two fingers upon a cushion. A murder in a chemist’s shop was greatly improved by opening the medicine cupboard. He kept lemons for Sicily, pine shavings for Canada, pepper for the bazaars, and a length of blue silk which, according to its disposition, became lake, sky, river, or the melancholy eye of a Balkan princess.

Books soon seemed unnecessarily restrictive. Their authors had a tiresome habit of introducing motives where atmosphere would have been sufficient, and characters where one desired only scenery. Oliver began composing adventures directly from the world.

London, properly handled, contained every continent.

In the Strand, the damp fragrance outside a fish shop carried him aboard a schooner rounding Cape Horn. The lemons piled in a grocer’s window turned the neighbouring pavement into a terrace above Amalfi. A chemist’s display of blue bottles and white bandages produced a fever hospital in Alexandria, where Oliver, pale but distinguished, directed heroic operations while the city rioted beyond the shutters. Coffee-roasting in Soho gave him Arabia; wet rope beside the docks gave him Singapore; a dark archway and a policeman’s lantern supplied the opening chapter of a Russian conspiracy.

He roamed farther each day. His limbs, surprised by use, offered a little resistance and then improved. His complexion acquired colour. He reduced his afternoon rest from forty minutes to twenty-five, a reform Alice regarded with suspicion.

“You have been out again,” she said.

“I have returned from Sumatra.”

“You went to Bloomsbury.”

“That was the geographical pretext.”

Alice stared at him.

“You look better.”

“Imagination is an excellent climate.”

“So is Hampstead Heath.”

“Hampstead has dogs.”

He did not tell her everything. There were experiences which the direct-minded could only injure by understanding.

For example, one wet Thursday in November, Oliver fell in love.

The lady stood in the window of a dressmaker’s near Hanover Square. She was tall, golden-haired and perfectly still, with the grave serenity of one who has suffered without becoming untidy. Her head inclined very slightly to the left. Her hands, narrow and white, were held before her with a simplicity that Leonardo might have admired, although Leonardo would perhaps have corrected the thumbs. She wore an orange gown, not the vulgar orange of marmalade or railway advertisements, but the deep luminous colour of a sunset seen through Venetian glass.

The rain shone upon the pavement. Cabs rolled behind Oliver. A boy carrying hatboxes collided with his shoulder and made an unliterary remark. Oliver noticed none of these things.

She understood him.

This conviction, which in relation to a living woman would have required evidence, arose complete and splendid in relation to wax. Her silence contained no contradiction; her expression, being fixed, could not harden at an unfortunate phrase. She did not laugh loudly, eat walnuts, discuss drains, breed terriers, or demand that one go out in the east wind. She represented femininity purified of incident.

Oliver passed the window twice, then a third time. On the fourth passage he permitted himself to look directly into her painted blue eyes.

That evening the shepherdess remained unfinished.

His beloved required a history, and Oliver supplied it with the prodigality natural to a man spending imaginary money. She was at first the daughter of an impoverished duke. This seemed insufficiently perilous, so her father became a Russian prince exiled for liberal sympathies. By Saturday she had inherited a villa on Lake Como and the secret of a diplomatic treaty. On Monday she was the widow of an Austrian cavalry officer, though Oliver removed the husband on Tuesday because widowhood introduced a trace of physical experience which he found disquieting.

Her name changed with her circumstances. She was Bianca in Italy, Tatiana in Russia, Claire in Paris, and, during a brief American episode suggested by a display of travelling trunks, Miss Aurelia Van Doren of Newport. Oliver himself was correspondingly transformed. He rescued her from a burning palace, steered her yacht through reefs, defeated her enemies at baccarat, and renounced a throne for her sake. The renunciation pleased him particularly, since thrones looked draughty.

He visited every afternoon.

Their courtship was conducted with the refinement possible only when one party neither moves nor answers. Oliver brought no flowers; actual flowers fade, smell too strongly, and create awkwardness with shop assistants. Instead he imagined bouquets: white orchids in Cairo, violets in Vienna, orange blossom in Seville. He addressed her silently through the glass. Her replies were conveyed by the inclination of her head, which varied in significance according to his mood.

At first she was reserved. Then she became tender. Once, when sunlight broke suddenly through the clouds and spread across the orange silk, she seemed to blush with such radiant ardour that Oliver had to sit down in a tea-shop.

Love performed upon him what cod-liver oil, electric treatment and four expensive physicians had failed to achieve. He walked briskly. He forgot his milk. He ate a mutton chop without examining it. His shoulders straightened, and he purchased a blue necktie of almost adventurous brightness.

Alice observed these changes with the alarm of a practical woman confronted by a result she cannot attribute to exercise.

“You are in love,” she announced at breakfast.

Oliver coloured.

“I hope I am never so indiscreet as to confirm a personal remark.”

“Who is she?”

“A woman whose nature is not expressed in questions.”

“Then she must be dead.”

“She is more responsive than many persons whose circulation is beyond dispute.”

Alice buttered toast.

“Bring her to dinner.”

“The conditions of our intimacy make that impossible.”

“Married?”

“Certainly not.”

“Actress?”

Oliver shuddered.

“My dear Alice, she is incapable of exhibiting herself.”

Alice looked at the bright tie, the improved appetite and the spot of rain on his sleeve.

“Well, whoever she is, she has done you good. I hope she makes you play golf.”

Oliver smiled with the forbearance of a man secretly accustomed to crossing Central Asia in winter.

His felicity continued for three weeks. Then, on an afternoon of clear, brittle cold, he turned the corner near Hanover Square and found the window occupied by two headless figures in grey walking-suits.

She was not there.

Oliver stopped so abruptly that an elderly lady behind him struck his ankle with a furled umbrella. He apologised mechanically and stared through the glass. The orange gown had vanished. The background, formerly a pearl-coloured chamber fit for confidence and renunciation, had been rearranged as a railway platform. One grey figure held a leather valise; the other, without head or hesitation, appeared prepared to depart for Scotland.

Oliver entered the shop.

A young woman approached him. She wore black, had competent wrists, and seemed precisely the kind of person who knew where things had gone.

“The figure in the orange dress,” Oliver said. “The lady who was in the window.”

“The model, sir?”

He disliked the noun, but necessity compelled him.

“Yes. The model.”

“We changed the window yesterday.”

“So I perceive. Where is she?”

The young woman considered.

“The orange gown was sold.”

“I did not ask about the gown.”

“The figure will have gone downstairs, sir. Or to our Kensington branch.”

“Could you ascertain?”

Her expression suggested that no gentleman had previously attempted to trace a mannequin’s movements with the seriousness usually reserved for missing heiresses.

“I’m afraid I couldn’t say, sir. They shift them about.”

“Shift them?”

“Between the branches.”

The perfidy of commerce revealed itself to him. What he had taken for constancy was merely window arrangement; what he had interpreted as silent devotion was a temporary use of floor space. She might at that moment be displaying waterproofs in Kensington, underlinen in Bayswater, or seaside costumes at some branch beyond the reach of decent omnibuses.

For eight days Oliver searched.

He visited establishments he had never before entered and interrogated assistants with a persistence that astonished himself. He inspected waxen blondes, brunettes, widows, débutantes, shepherdesses, tennis players and creatures in tea-gowns who appeared to possess no social position at all. One had her hands; another the noble tilt of her head; a third, seen from across Regent Street at dusk, caused his heart to leap, but proved upon approach to have a green complexion and no left arm.

The search exhausted him. London, formerly a treasure house of manageable sensations, became an enormous warehouse of counterfeit women. Their resemblance accused his beloved. Her beauty, which he had supposed unique, was evidently manufactured in several sizes.

He returned home late one evening, chilled, hungry and embittered. Light shone beneath the drawing-room door. He entered.

Alice stood before the fire wearing the orange gown.

She had bought it that afternoon. On her vigorous frame the dress looked less like sunset through Venetian glass than like a flag displayed during military occupation. She had loosened her hair, put on amber beads, and was smoking a cigarette through a long ivory holder.

“What do you think?” she asked. “They called it a tea-gown, though it seems too grand for tea. I found it absurdly cheap.”

Oliver gripped the door.

“Where did you get it?”

“At Marlow and Finch’s. Their Hanover Square shop.”

The room became very quiet.

Alice turned before the mirror.

“It was in the window, apparently. The assistant said some odd little man had been asking about the dummy.”

Oliver sat down.

His tragedy was complete, not because Alice wore the gown badly—though she did—but because she wore it naturally. The sacred orange folds moved when she moved. They creased at the waist, caught on the fender, and received a flake of cigarette ash near the sleeve. The robe that had belonged to moonlit terraces, royal conspiracies and immortal fidelity was now discussing whether there was enough hot water upstairs.

“Are you ill?” Alice asked.

“No.”

“You look dreadful.”

“I have merely witnessed the commercial basis of passion.”

“What?”

“Nothing. The colour does not suit you.”

Alice inspected herself cheerfully.

“It suits the room.”

That night Oliver revised his philosophy. Imagination, he concluded, remained the highest form of existence, but emotional concentration was dangerous. To fasten one’s entire visionary apparatus upon a single object was as foolish as investing one’s fortune in a South American railway. Henceforward his fancies should be diversified, mobile and light. He would admire, but never adore; visit, but never settle; sip at sensation rather than drown in it.

For several months he practised this prudence successfully. A pearl necklace made him the favourite of an empress for seven minutes. A pair of fencing foils engaged him in a duel before luncheon. Three tropical birds in a dealer’s window provided a Brazilian expedition from which he returned in time for tea. He became a connoisseur of fugitive ecstasies.

Then he discovered Bellamy’s Universal Stores.

Bellamy’s occupied a whole block between two streets and seemed, from outside, less a shop than a nation with plate-glass frontiers. Above its entrances flew flags. Uniformed men opened doors with diplomatic gravity. Its windows contained furniture, furs, fruit, fishing tackle, French clocks and mechanical toys, all arranged with that costly simplicity by which abundance pretends to be taste.

Oliver entered to buy a handkerchief.

He emerged six hours later from the wrong side of the building, believing that he had visited six countries, conducted a war, become engaged twice, and eaten dinner with a cardinal.

Bellamy’s offered what London had hitherto supplied only in scattered portions: the whole universe beneath a roof and protected from rain. It was climate, geography, society and destiny centralised for the convenience of the customer. Lifts abolished distance; staircases connected epochs; a turn of the corridor transported one from a nursery in Mayfair to an encampment in Somaliland. The air itself was composite. Perfume crossed coffee; leather contended with flowers; hot pastry drifted into the colder breath of silver and glass. To a normal shopper these odours indicated departments. To Oliver they were the winds of creation.

He began in Travel.

Trunks stood open like invitations. Their pale silk linings suggested Parisian bedrooms, shipboard cabins, diplomatic missions and elopements conducted with excellent luggage. Labels upon specimen cases named Cairo, Brindisi, Bombay, Yokohama. Oliver had only to rest one gloved hand upon a crocodile dressing-case and he was aboard the Orient Express, watching the Balkans slide by beneath snow while a beautiful spy slept in the adjoining compartment.

Camping Equipment gave him sterner adventures. Canvas, paraffin, cork helmets and coiled rope carried him to the African interior. He crossed swamps without mud, endured thirst beside a drinking fountain, and faced lions within convenient reach of the confectionery department. A shopwalker once asked whether he required assistance at the precise moment when he was negotiating with a hostile chief.

“Not yet,” said Oliver. “But remain within call.”

Sporting Goods fulfilled Alice’s prophecy. Among polished clubs, white balls and green baize, Oliver became a golfer of international renown. He won championships with noiseless strokes, accepted silver cups, and declined to endorse a patent niblick because commercial vulgarity must not invade the ancient game. In the cricket section he scored two hundred runs at Lord’s. A salesman’s demonstration of an exerciser enabled him to defeat a Canadian heavyweight, though he considered the episode coarse and moved quickly into Stationery.

There he governed Europe.

The grave splendour of blotting-paper, sealing-wax, dispatch boxes and fountain pens awoke his administrative genius. Treaties multiplied beneath his hand. He warned ambassadors, pardoned rebels, rejected ultimatums and wrote a private note to the Tsar. Red tape, which in actual government delays action, accelerated it wonderfully in imagination. Oliver could rearrange the Balkans before the assistant finished wrapping a memorandum book.

The Music Department gave concerts twice a day. When a pianist played Chopin, Oliver did not merely listen; he performed. He sat in a white salon before princes, artists and women whose beauty had ruined less disciplined men. His long hands descended upon the keys. Paris held its breath. At the final chord, a duchess removed a single red rose from her bodice. In reality a child dropped a sticky sweet upon his shoe, but Oliver had learned to incorporate accidents. The child became an infant prodigy overcome by genius.

At Perfumery, romance grew almost embarrassingly abundant. Each bottle released a different woman. Violets produced a pale girl in a convent garden; amber summoned a dark-eyed dancer from Granada; verbena belonged to a married Frenchwoman who had read too much Byron; jasmine arrived with warm nights, white balconies and a difficulty involving honour. Oliver kept these ladies distinct with the conscientiousness of a man maintaining several imaginary households. None was permitted an orange gown.

He lunched at Bellamy’s restaurant, where pyramids of fruit, shining silver and folded napkins allowed appetite to become pageantry. He was alternately a Roman proconsul, a Russian grand duke, an American financier and a poet entertained by admirers. The food itself mattered less than its implications. A sole carried him to Dieppe; caviar placed him beside the Neva; an ice shaped like a swan transformed the room into a ball at Schönbrunn. He tipped moderately, since even fantasy ought not to disturb one’s income.

At first Oliver visited Bellamy’s three afternoons a week. Soon he went every day except Sunday, which he regarded as a blank in civilisation. His acquaintances ceased inviting him to luncheon because he accepted and did not appear. His tailor wrote twice. His doctor, meeting him in the street, declared him surprisingly well; Oliver thanked him as one monarch might acknowledge the medical opinion of another.

Alice protested.

“You live in that shop.”

“Alexander lived in Asia.”

“Alexander conquered it.”

“Occupation is the sincerest form of conquest.”

“You do not buy anything.”

“I confer value by appreciating it.”

“The attendants think you mad.”

“Only because they think themselves sane.”

There was justice in this. Bellamy’s employees had begun to classify him. The younger assistants laughed; the older ones, accustomed to wealthy peculiarities, bowed. A floor manager believed him to be a harmless eccentric with private means. One perfumery girl thought he was a poet. The lift attendant, who saw him ascend to Furniture and descend from Toys with the solemn fatigue of a traveller returning from empire, considered him a retired actor.

Oliver himself thought he had at last solved the problem of life. He enjoyed labour without exhaustion, danger without injury, love without obligation, travel without foreign plumbing, and food without indigestion. Every costly inconvenience had been removed by commerce. Bellamy’s was Plato’s world of forms revised by a board of directors.

But a subtle change had occurred.

In the early days Oliver had selected his sensations. A lemon had been Sicily because he chose Sicily; blue silk became a lake at his command. At Bellamy’s, where impressions arrived in battalions, choice became difficult. The objects began to command him.

A display of revolvers forced him into a frontier quarrel when he had intended to visit the Riviera. The sight of baby clothes made him the anxious father of seven before he could escape to China. Passing the kitchens, he was compelled by garlic into a Neapolitan vendetta; three steps later, furniture polish converted him into a conscientious housemaid. The transitions grew rapid and humiliating.

He attempted discipline. Before entering the store he would announce, “Today I shall be a Venetian nobleman.” Yet an umbrella display made him an explorer in the monsoon; a row of boots enlisted him in the Prussian army; and a mechanical duck in Toys reduced him to a child at a village pond. By luncheon he had forgotten Venice.

The condition was painless and, for that reason, advanced unchecked. Had Oliver suffered headaches, he would have consulted a specialist. Had he stolen or shouted, Alice would have intervened. But he merely wandered through a respectable establishment, quiet, well dressed and entranced. His insanity was agreeable. It demanded no padded room, only opening hours.

The whole store scintillated with instructions.

In Jewellery he became a sultan, a thief, a lover, a Rothschild and an Indian god. In China and Glass, he gave banquets, founded dynasties and smashed ancestral services during domestic quarrels. The Garden Department made him a country gentleman, then a hermit, then Adam. At Ladies’ Shoes he pursued fleeting women through casinos, railway stations and moonlit parks. At Furs he travelled by sleigh, attended the opera in St. Petersburg, and embraced an actress whose morals were said to be Continental.

Even the mundane departments acquired power. Hardware turned him into Vulcan among hammers and brass fittings. Linoleum filled him with visions of suburban marriage. A display of patent dusters compelled him to reorganise the servants of an imaginary castle. He could no longer pass a bed without falling ill, falling in love, or inheriting a haunted estate.

His body began to imitate these adventures. He ducked beneath invisible branches in Furnishings. He raised his hat to women who existed only as perfume. At Sporting Goods he swung an imaginary club and nearly struck a clergyman. While brokering peace beside the writing desks, he murmured, “The Adriatic clause is unacceptable,” within hearing of an assistant who replied that the desk was also available in mahogany.

Alice followed him one afternoon.

She found him in the Grocery Hall, motionless before a pyramid of tinned pineapple.

“What are you doing?”

Oliver blinked. He had been standing upon the deck of a mutinous ship under a tropical moon.

“Preventing bloodshed.”

“You are staring at tins.”

“The two activities are not incompatible.”

She took his arm.

“Come home.”

He resisted with surprising firmness.

“I am expected in Vienna.”

“You are expected at the dentist.”

“Empires have fallen because their rulers attended to trivial engagements.”

“Teeth have fallen for the same reason.”

She dragged him away, but outside Bellamy’s he became weak and dispirited. The street was badly arranged. Its people had no price labels, its smells were unclassified, and its noises suggested nothing coherent. A real cab was merely a cab, whereas the toy carriage on the fourth floor could become the equipage of any crowned head in Europe. He went home, drank his milk, and slept poorly.

The next morning he returned before the doors opened.

Winter passed. Bellamy’s prepared for spring. Artificial apple blossom appeared in the windows; tennis racquets displaced skates; pale dresses succeeded furs. Oliver underwent the seasons as a series of commercial revelations. He spent April on Mediterranean terraces, May in English gardens and June among white shoes, picnic baskets and striped bathing tents. He was brown from no sun and exhausted by no exertion.

By midsummer he had become one of the store’s fixtures. Assistants used him to judge the hour.

“Mr. Bowman has gone up to Carpets,” they would say. “It must be nearly three.”

He never heard them. He was by then a harmless monomaniac moving inside a universe whose gods were window dressers.

It was a small accident that killed him.

Bellamy’s was undergoing alterations. Departments migrated overnight; familiar passages ended in temporary screens; arrows directed customers through regions previously reserved for staff. Oliver disliked these changes because they disturbed geography, but he had learned to trust the store’s abundance. If one empire closed, another opened beside it.

On a dark November afternoon he left the lift at the fifth floor intending to visit Musical Instruments. A harp had lately enabled him to become a Welsh bard, Orpheus, and the favourite virtuoso of a countess in Prague. But a partition blocked the usual corridor. Upon it a card announced:

MUSIC DEPARTMENT—THIS WAY.

The arrow pointed through a narrow black door.

Oliver entered.

He found no pianos.

The room beyond was long, hushed and softly illuminated. Black dresses hung in a row. Veils descended from pale heads. Gloves, crepe bands and sombre hats lay under glass. White wreaths stood against the walls, their artificial lilies shining with the waxen purity of flowers that had never lived.

Bellamy’s Funeral Department had been temporarily moved from the basement.

Oliver stopped.

Ordinarily, an unwelcome suggestion could be replaced by a pleasant one. Illness yielded to perfume, battle to confectionery, old age to Toys. But the black door had closed behind him, and every object in the room repeated a single idea.

A wreath became a grave. Crepe became grief. The lilies exhaled, not scent, but the cold odour of stone and damp earth.

He turned toward the exit. Before he reached it, two attendants wheeled a coffin from behind a curtain.

It was polished, satin-lined, and admirably finished. A small card stated that the model could be supplied in oak, elm or mahogany.

To another customer it was merchandise. To Oliver it was destiny made visible.

His imagination, trained through years of indulgence and lately released from all authority, seized upon the coffin with perfect obedience. He saw the shaded room, the closed curtains, Alice standing awkwardly in black. He heard the subdued servants, the carriage wheels, the church bell. He felt the narrowing satin, the lid descending, the screws turning with neat professional patience.

An undertaker’s assistant approached. He was a mild young man with pink ears.

“Can I help you, sir?”

Oliver stared at the coffin.

“No,” he whispered. “No one can.”

He put one hand to his heart, as heroes, invalids and elderly relatives had done in a thousand books. This time the gesture suggested nothing beyond itself.

He collapsed beside a display of mourning handkerchiefs.

The assistant ran for help. The floor manager came, then the store physician, then Alice, who had been summoned from Kensington and arrived without hat or gloves. She pushed past the attendants, knelt, loosened Oliver’s collar and called his name with all the force that had once extinguished a chimney fire.

But direct action had at last encountered an event that would not yield to it.

Oliver was dead.

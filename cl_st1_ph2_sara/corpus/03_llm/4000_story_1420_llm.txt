# The Indivisible Gentleman

There has attended me, from the first hour at which memory begins to distinguish its own small candle from the general daylight of existence, a certain gentleman whose intimacy I have never solicited, whose fidelity I cannot reward, whose origin no herald has yet discovered, and whose character—if character may be predicated of one who has never uttered a syllable—has furnished me with more matter for reflection than all the philosophers from Socrates downward.

I shall call him Monsieur du Verre.

This appellation is not his own; for he has never presented a card, subscribed a letter, answered to a name, nor, indeed, given the least audible intimation that he possesses a tongue. It was bestowed upon him by myself, partly because there is something foreign in the elegance of his motions, partly because he seems to inhabit the smooth province of glass, and chiefly because an uncertainty of nomenclature is intolerable to a sober investigator. Let the reader, therefore, dismiss from his imagination all legends of spectres, familiars, guardian demons, and enchanted twins. I relate facts. If those facts incline toward the marvellous, the fault lies not in my veracity, but in Nature, who has always shown herself less fearful of ridicule than her commentators.

My earliest recollection of Monsieur du Verre is associated with a nursery looking-glass, framed in black wood and surmounted by a gilded eagle whose wings, though permanently spread, never carried him an inch toward liberty. I was lifted before this instrument by my nurse, a broad, honest woman, who smelt agreeably of lavender and toast. There, in an apartment which appeared to continue our own and yet was inaccessible by every door in the house, I beheld an infant held by another nurse, in another world, beneath another eagle. The infant resembled me with a precision that would have astonished a painter and disgraced an original genius. When I laughed, he laughed; when I thrust out my tongue, he exhibited an organ neither longer nor more civil; when, moved by the generous impulse of childhood, I offered him a sugar-plum, he made a corresponding offer, but would not surrender his sweetmeat.

This last circumstance first awakened my distrust.

A child forgives imitation, because his whole education consists of it; but he understands property. I struck at the little miser. He struck also. Our fists met a cold resistance, and I retired howling, while he appeared to howl with equal energy but without producing the smallest sound. My nurse declared that I had hit the glass. This was an explanation suitable to her station, and perhaps to mine; yet it left untouched the more delicate question of the person behind it.

From that day Monsieur du Verre became my companion.

He grew with me; or, to speak with scientific caution, he presented, at every subsequent interview, an enlargement exactly proportioned to my own. Whether he accomplished this by food, exercise, or some secret expansion peculiar to his species, I have never learned. He was not visible at breakfast, except occasionally in the silver coffee-pot, where his countenance appeared compressed by circumstances; but at the great mirror upon the landing he awaited me with unfailing punctuality. I ran, and he ran; I halted, and he halted; I approached, and he advanced with a forwardness that might have ended in collision had not the transparent barrier intervened.

In dress his dependence upon me was abject. Whatever coat I put on, he wore its fellow; every button of mine implied a button of his; a torn cuff on my arm became, without tailor, needle, or delay, a torn cuff upon his. No sooner had my mother invested me in a blue jacket with brass fastenings than he appeared in a garment of the same cut, hue, and splendour. When I was condemned to an elder cousin’s discarded trousers, he submitted at the same instant to an equal humiliation. This might be called sympathy, were it not that sympathy usually has some choice in the matter.

His plagiarism extended beyond clothes. He appropriated my smiles, frowned with my forehead, yawned with my mouth, and seemed incapable of an original embarrassment. Yet there was one curious disagreement between us: he invariably preferred the opposite hand. If I saluted with my right, he returned the civility with his left. If I held up a school prize, he held his on the contrary side. If I placed my hand upon my heart, as young gentlemen do when reciting patriotic verse, he laid his upon a heart which, by all anatomical calculation, must have been misplaced.

Was this perversity? Was it a silent declaration of political opposition? Or did he dwell in a country where left and right had exchanged dignities, where men mounted horses from the wrong side, read books backward, and accused their neighbours of being sinister when they were merely dexterous? I asked him. He repeated the movement of my lips, but withheld the answer.

Is he really dumb?

I have sometimes stood before him and pronounced, very slowly, those simple questions by which travellers test the intelligence of savages.

“Who are you?”

His mouth formed the same inquiry.

“Whence came you?”

He appeared equally desirous of information.

“What do you want?”

He demanded as much of me.

Thus every interrogation was returned like a letter insufficiently addressed. I could not determine whether he mocked my ignorance, confessed his own, or belonged to some philosophic sect which teaches that a question is most wisely answered by its duplicate. Certain professors have done no more and acquired reputations.

As I advanced toward boyhood, his habits became stranger. I discovered that he was not confined to the family looking-glasses, as I had at first charitably supposed, but possessed a mobility approaching witchcraft. He appeared in the polished case of my father’s watch, in the brass knob of the parlour door, in the bowl of a spoon, and once, with indecorous levity, upon the convex belly of a warming-pan. In these minor habitations his figure underwent monstrous alterations. His nose expanded, his forehead fled backward, his cheeks curved around a metallic horizon; nevertheless, beneath every distortion, I recognized the fellow.

What conveyance transported him?

I would leave him upstairs in the tall mirror, descend two flights with speed, and find him already crouching in the hall barometer. I would cover the mirror with a shawl, imprisoning him, as I believed, in darkness; yet at the very moment of his supposed captivity he peeped from a silver tray in the dining-room. Either he had the power of multiplying his person, like certain heroes of Eastern romance, or he travelled through walls more swiftly than thought. Both hypotheses contain difficulties. The first would populate the world with an embarrassing number of Monsieurs du Verre; the second would render bolts, property laws, and private apartments equally ridiculous.

His aquatic accomplishments were not less surprising.

Whenever I leaned over a quiet pond, he rose from beneath to meet me, forehead to forehead, as if he had been waiting all morning at the bottom among weeds and philosophical fishes. In a rain puddle he lay comfortably under my boots, without concern for mud, dampness, or passing wheels. At the village well he inhabited an abyss; at the lake he grew to natural stature; in a cup of water he reduced himself to a miniature. I tested his courage by dropping a pebble upon him. He was shattered into circles, but reassembled without complaint.

Here, surely, was a being of amphibious constitution! Yet no fisherman drew him in his net; no swimmer brushed against him; no bubble announced his breathing. He lived in water and remained dry, drowned daily and never died. Narcissus, according to the classical authority, fell in love with a countenance similarly situated; but Narcissus was fortunate in finding a face that did not grow freckled in summer or swollen with toothache. My own submarine acquaintance had no such immunity.

For it is one proof of our mysterious alliance that Monsieur du Verre has shared all my bodily afflictions while refusing me the slightest assistance in bearing them. When measles reddened me, he was spotted from brow to chin. When a schoolboy’s stone adorned my temple with a bruise, his temple bore its violet flower. When a villainous molar raised my left cheek to the dimensions of a winter apple, the corresponding side of his face—though, as usual, contrary in position—became equally magnificent. He looked as miserable as I felt, and thereby increased my misery; for there are hours when companionship in suffering is merely suffering doubled.

Once, during a fever, I was carried past a mirror and saw him stagger by, white-lipped, hollow-eyed, and wrapped in my dressing-gown. His appearance alarmed me beyond the physician’s Latin. I requested that he be sent away, since the chamber already contained one sick man and could not conveniently support another. The mirror was covered. He vanished. But whether he departed or only stood behind the curtain, listening to the doctor’s prognosis with his accustomed secrecy, I cannot affirm.

In joy he has been no less parasitical. If good news brightened my face, his brightened; when I first received a letter signed by a feminine name and perfumed beyond the necessities of correspondence, he read it over my shoulder with an ardour equal to mine. This intrusion I resented. A gentleman may forgive another for borrowing his coat, but hardly for sharing his blushes. I turned away from the glass. Instantly he disappeared from that quarter, only to re-emerge faintly in the black varnish of a bookcase.

“Sir,” I said, “there are limits.”

He silently agreed, with so perfect an imitation of my severity that I could not decide which of us was rebuked.

In those youthful years I confess that his society often pleased me. He had the admirable quality of confirming every favourable opinion I formed of my appearance. Before a ball he approved my neckcloth, admired the wave of my hair, and smiled upon a waistcoat whose colour less partial observers compared to mustard. If I practised a Byronic gloom, he became Byronic; if I lifted my chin in the manner of a conquering general, he conquered also. Between us we might have subdued Europe, had Europe been made of dressing-rooms.

He never laughed at me except when I laughed first. He never looked astonished at my folly unless astonishment had already seized me. He contradicted no boast, exposed no affectation, and repeated no secret. What companion could be safer? What flatterer more economical? He consumed neither wine nor tobacco, yet attended every supper at which those articles improved my conversation. He never asked to borrow money. He was content with the outward show of a feast and left me the indigestion.

Still, certain objections attached to his character. His loyalty depended upon light. At noon he was devoted; at dusk uncertain; in absolute darkness he abandoned me altogether. A friend who disappears when the candles are extinguished cannot be wholly trusted. Nor would he accompany me when I shut my eyes, though that act placed me in greater need of guidance. I have walked on moonless roads calling inwardly for him, but he gave no sign. The first inn lamp, however, restored his courage, and there he was in the window, as composed as if he had kept beside me all the way.

His social position was equally equivocal. He entered houses without being announced. He attended ladies at their toilets, statesmen in their cabinets, misers before their gold, actors in their tiring-rooms, and criminals—so I am informed—during the last dreadful preparation before execution. No privacy restrained him, no rank dazzled him, no poverty excluded him. He was democratic in access and aristocratic in silence. Yet, though admitted everywhere, he could touch nothing; though apparently present, he displaced no air; though dressed like a gentleman, he had not a pocket capable of containing a coin.

I once endeavoured to shake his hand.

The attempt occurred after a period of absence. I had travelled through a region where mirrors were scarce, spoons wooden, windows dusty, and streams agitated by wind; consequently I saw little of him and began, with that tenderness which distance sometimes produces toward troublesome acquaintances, to regret his company. On returning to my room in the city, I found him full before me. His eyes expressed—because mine expressed—a grave affection. Moved beyond prudence, I extended my hand.

He extended his.

Our palms approached.

A pane of glass, cold as etiquette, lay between us.

This failure affected me. Here was a creature who had attended every stage of my life, borne my appearance, imitated my grief, and witnessed my most private hours, yet whom I could never embrace, assist, strike, detain, or rescue. If fire consumed his apartment, I could not pull him forth; if he drowned in his favourite well, no rope would reach him. On the other hand, he could not lift me when I fell, carry a message, lend a shoulder, or hand me my hat. Our friendship possessed all the sentiment and none of the practical advantages.

It was about this time that I began a systematic inquiry into his origin. The resemblance suggested kinship, though my family, after a diligent examination of registers, denied knowledge of any such relation. An aged aunt remembered twins upon my mother’s side, but both had died before learning to imitate anybody. A portrait of my great-grandfather showed a long nose which I inherited and Monsieur du Verre appropriated; nevertheless, that worthy had been buried for sixty years, whereas my companion showed no preference for churchyards.

Could he be my double—the being whom northern superstition appoints to precede death? The thought was Gothic, and for three nights I removed the mirror from my chamber. Yet no funeral followed. On the fourth morning vanity proved stronger than apprehension, and Monsieur du Verre returned while I shaved.

Could he be a demon? If so, his temptations were feeble. He had never urged me toward anything worse than admiration of my own countenance, and in that enterprise he found me already advanced. Could he be an angel? His record was no better; he warned me of no danger, corrected no vice, and once allowed me to go into public with ink upon my nose—though, in justice, he displayed the stain beforehand, and I mistook it for a blemish in his complexion.

A more learned acquaintance proposed that he was an optical phenomenon.

There are persons who explain a mystery by giving it a Greek name and then expect gratitude. I asked whether optical phenomena commonly purchased coats of my tailor, cut themselves shaving whenever I did, and aged year by year in familiar company. He replied with rays, angles, surfaces, and other pieces of intellectual hardware. I listened respectfully. When he had finished, Monsieur du Verre remained where he had been, no less personal for having been reduced to geometry.

But the years, which solve certain questions by making others urgent, continued their march.

I had once regarded time as a road stretching magnificently before me, bordered with achievements, affections, and noble enterprises, each waiting only until I should condescend to arrive. Monsieur du Verre then wore a smooth brow. His eyes were quick with expectation; his lips seemed on the verge of pronouncing the password to fortune; his hair, abundant and somewhat unruly, caught the candlelight like a dark flame. We were young together, and youth, that credulous alchemist, transformed every pane into crystal and every outward show into prophecy.

I cannot identify the morning when the alteration first became undeniable. Time does not always knock; sometimes he enters while one is occupied with smaller visitors. I stood before the glass knotting my neckcloth and observed that Monsieur du Verre had acquired a line across his forehead. I accused him silently of fatigue. He accused me of the same. A silver thread appeared near his temple. I attributed it to the light, but the light followed him into every room. Soon other witnesses assembled: a slight descent at the corners of the mouth; a less courageous fullness beneath the chin; small hieroglyphics about the eyes, in which laughter and sleeplessness had written alternate chapters.

“Sir,” I demanded, “what have you done with your youth?”

He put the question back to me.

This was unfair. His whole occupation had been to follow, while I had lived, chosen, laboured, loved, failed, delayed, begun, abandoned, promised, forgotten, and consumed innumerable mornings upon business which, by evening, appeared not to have deserved them. Yet he looked as disappointed as I. He had ventured nothing and lost everything.

There were days when I disliked him. He ceased to be the flattering companion of my youth and became a creditor presenting old accounts. The neglected duty stood in his stoop; the unspoken apology in the compression of his mouth; the generous intention, postponed until it perished, looked reproachfully from his eyes. I could escape the accusations of acquaintances by changing the subject, of society by changing my address, and of conscience by changing my occupation; but Monsieur du Verre changed with me and arrived first.

If I sought cheer in a tavern, he floated darkly in the wineglass. This I considered an abuse of reflection, for liquor should encourage forgetfulness, not multiply the drinker. If I walked abroad after rain, he lay in every puddle, a moralist underfoot. If I entered a shop, he confronted me in polished kettles, sometimes stretched absurdly tall, sometimes crushed into the dimensions of a guilty dwarf. If I raised my eyes from earthly annoyances to the quiet night, I found no face among the stars; yet the instant I turned toward a window, there he stood between me and infinity.

Ha! What is yonder?

Only Monsieur du Verre, pale in lamplight, with darkness behind him.

At such moments his appearance carried a terror disproportionate to the circumstances. He seemed less an inhabitant of glass than a prisoner upon its further side, condemned to perform my gestures until one of us should be released. Which of us was the prisoner? I walked where I pleased, certainly; yet desire, habit, duty, fear, and memory made walls no less real because no glazier had set them. He could not leave his luminous surface; I could not leave myself.

This consideration brought me at last to a discovery which the reader, with perhaps premature cleverness, may suppose he made in the nursery. Monsieur du Verre was not merely like me. He was—within the severe limits of his insubstantial nature—my own reflection.

I record the conclusion without shame. Great truths are often obvious after they are found; Columbus required an ocean to demonstrate what an egg could afterward illustrate. Yet I beg the reader not to imagine that by naming the mystery I had exhausted it. To say that this silent gentleman was my reflection explained his resemblance, his reversals, his watery habits, and his dependence upon light; but what, precisely, was the self that he reflected? My coat? My countenance? My momentary passion? The accumulated consequence of my years? The visible mask of an invisible tenant? A word may close a door while leaving the house unexplored.

I therefore resolved to make him useful.

Until then I had employed the mirror chiefly to discover whether my collar sat properly and whether the world would be justified in admiring me. This was to consult an oracle concerning buttons. I now determined that each interview should become an examination—not of complexion, but of conduct; not of the hair, which was retreating beyond negotiation, but of the mind, which might still be rallied; not of outward blemishes, which time would soon erase with the face itself, but of those stains which, if theologians speak truly, survive the ruin of flesh.

Every morning I stood before Monsieur du Verre and asked three questions.

“What falsehood did you permit yesterday?”

“What kindness did you postpone?”

“What portion of your life did you waste?”

He asked them in return.

At first I found this irritating; later I perceived its justice. He could answer only through me. If his face grew ashamed, mine supplied the shame; if resolution brightened it, the resolution must originate on my side of the glass. He possessed no conscience independent of my own, yet by displaying its effects he rendered conscience visible. A blush, formerly an inconvenience, became testimony. A furtive eye became evidence. The tranquillity of an honest morning—rare, but not impossible—appeared upon him like clear weather over a familiar landscape.

Did this discipline reform me? Let no man claim too much. I continued to prefer ease to duty whenever the preference could be disguised as prudence. I still mistook postponement for preparation and good wishes for good deeds. I still composed admirable speeches to persons whom I lacked courage to address, and bestowed imaginary fortunes upon the poor while guarding my actual purse from every draught. But Monsieur du Verre made concealment laborious. I could deceive a neighbour with words; him I must deceive with a face, and the face, being an ancient conspirator with truth, sometimes betrayed me.

By degrees I regarded him less as a spy and more as an emblem.

Humanity, I reflected, has always sought the ultimate wisdom in its own likeness. We carve gods with human brows, people the heavens with our passions, describe Providence as a king, judgment as a court, eternity as a city, and the soul as a winged version of the body. The savage bends over a dark pool and trembles before himself; the philosopher bends over consciousness and gives his own reason the title of universal law. We gaze into Nature as into an immeasurable mirror, and, when a face appears, cry out that we have discovered the Infinite.

Perhaps all our systems are Monsieurs du Verre—majestic repetitions of the questioner.

The sun writes his burning image upon the sea; the moon lays a silver road across the lake; northern lights shake spectral banners over the snow; the Alpine traveller beholds a giant of himself projected upon vapour and bows before the Brocken apparition. At Niagara, every broken fragment of water catches heaven and loses it in the fall. The Nile carries palaces, palms, and stars upon its breast, though its depths know none of them. Everywhere substance is accompanied by image, being by appearance, light by a bright deception.

Yet is an image therefore nothing?

Monsieur du Verre cannot grasp my hand, but he has altered my thoughts. He has no weight, yet he has burdened me; no voice, yet he has reproved me; no life apart from mine, yet he has been present at more of my existence than any living friend. Men of flesh have forgotten my childhood; he remembers it, not in words, but in the continuity of his attendance. Every former face he wore is gone, as mine is gone, yet each has somehow contributed to the face now before me.

And when I die?

Here our companionship reaches its darkest question. Will Monsieur du Verre die also? If they carry a mirror to my bed, will he lie opposite, breathing when I breathe, fading as I fade, until my final motion receives its final counterfeit? When the eyes close, his will close. When the sheet is drawn upward, he will be hidden. But if another hand uncovers the glass, what remains of him? Nothing. The mirror will contain the bed, the curtains, the grieving figures, the indifferent daylight—everything except the gentleman who attended me for a lifetime.

Thus his fidelity is absolute and his survival impossible.

I had once feared that he might abandon me; now I know that he cannot remain. He is chained not to my soul, perhaps, but to my visibility. If some spiritual essence escapes the failing body, Monsieur du Verre gives no evidence of it, for spirits cast neither face upon glass nor shadow on the road. He belongs to the perishable arrangement of features—to eyes that will cloud, lips that will stiffen, and a brow that will return to dust. At the threshold of eternity he must leave me, or I him.

This thought should humble vanity. We devote absurd labour to the image that cannot cross the grave. We polish the frame, decorate the chamber, adjust the light, and forget to ask whether there is an inhabitant behind the show. Yet contempt for appearance is another vanity. The face is not the soul, but it is the soul’s weather. Kindness visits the mouth; cruelty makes lodgings about the eyes; long disappointment draws its blinds; peace, when genuine, illuminates without ornament. Monsieur du Verre may not reveal the invisible tenant, but he shows what that tenant has done to the windows.

Last evening I met him again.

The hour was late. Rain moved softly against the panes, and the fire, sinking among its ashes, gave the room that uncertain illumination in which familiar furniture appears to meditate upon its owners. I had been reading theology, though whether for instruction or defence I cannot say. Opposite my chair stood the old mirror, its silver darkened at the edges, its frame scarred by years of removal from house to house. Within it another room receded into gloom, another fire expired, and another solitary man sat with an open book.

I rose.

He rose.

I approached until our faces were separated by no more than the thickness of glass and the immeasurable interval between substance and appearance. He looked older than I remembered, but so did I. His hair was thin, his cheeks somewhat fallen; about his eyes rested neither despair nor contentment, but the tranquil gloom of one who has expected many answers and learned to respect silence.

“Monsieur,” I said, “have we lived wisely?”

His lips repeated the inquiry.

“What part of us has been real?”

Again the voiceless words.

“Is there anything in man that no mirror can contain?”

He asked me with my own earnestness.

“And when this face is gone, shall I find you nowhere—or everywhere?”

The fire shifted. Light trembled across the glass. For one instant he seemed to withdraw into a depth beyond the reflected room, beyond darkness, beyond all surfaces capable of returning a human likeness. I lifted my hand.

He lifted his.

I waited.

He waited.

Then the ember failed, and Monsieur du Verre disappeared without explanation.

I remained before the darkened pane, in which there was now neither companion nor countenance, but only an obscurity deep enough to contain all my questions. Whether he had taught me anything, I could not decide. His entire existence had been devoted to reflection; yet, as is the case with many gentlemen who spend their lives in that occupation, it was doubtful whether reflection had made him wise.

## The Afternoon of the Wax Lady

In all Fairyland there was no more troublesome fellow than Tanko-Mankie the Yellow Ryl. He was not wicked, exactly, for a wicked fairy does harm with a solemn face and means every bit of it; but Tanko-Mankie played tricks merely to discover what would happen, and afterward flew away before anyone could ask him to put matters right.

One rainy night he came to the city, where the lamps shone in long rows and the shop windows glittered like palaces. Tanko-Mankie had never cared for cities, because their people were always hurrying and seldom looked high enough to see a fairy perched upon a clock or swinging from a telegraph wire. But he liked the great department store of Silas Floman, for its plate-glass windows contained so many things that did not belong together—silver teapots, rubber boots, rocking horses, lace curtains and mechanical frogs.

In the largest window stood a wax lady.

She was tall and pink-cheeked, with fluffy yellow hair, blue glass eyes and a pleasant smile which the window dresser had fastened upon her forever. She wore a plain gray gown so that customers might attend to the marked-down parasols around her.

Tanko-Mankie pressed his nose to the glass.

“You look exceedingly dull,” he said.

The wax lady did not deny it.

“Would you like to walk about?”

She made no answer, being at that time mostly wax, with a framework of wood and several iron screws.

“Then you shall,” declared Tanko-Mankie, who always took silence for consent.

He slipped through a keyhole, tapped her three times upon the funny bone—which was wood and therefore not at all funny—and whispered a word that is known only to Yellow Ryls and certain educated butterflies.

At once the wax lady blinked.

“Good evening,” said Tanko-Mankie.

“Good evening,” she replied.

These were the first words she had ever heard and consequently the only ones she knew.

Tanko-Mankie laughed so loudly that he rolled into a box of gloves. By the time he climbed out, the lady had moved one stiff foot.

“Good evening,” she said again.

“Go and be alive,” commanded the Ryl. Then he darted up the chimney, leaving her alone.

This was rather thoughtless of him. For you must consider, dear reader, that the poor creature possessed the mind of a newborn child, although she appeared to be a fashionable woman of twenty-seven. She knew nothing about doors, money, meals, traffic, policemen or the necessity of returning borrowed goods. She had, however, spent many months looking through glass at ladies in the street, and she believed she could become a real person by doing whatever they did.

Her first concern was clothing.

The store window held a placard reading:

LADIES’ AFTERNOON GOWNS  
THIS WEEK ONLY—$19.98

The wax lady could not read, but she understood that gowns were worn, having observed the matter through the window. She therefore stepped out of her gray dress and selected a purple gown trimmed with twenty-four brass buttons. It did not fit her properly, because it had been pinned to look slender upon a stand, but she pulled until three stitches broke and was satisfied.

Next she found a hat with a green feather, price $4.23; a pair of pea-green gloves, 76 cents; a beaded handbag, $2.49; and patent-leather shoes, reduced from $6.00 to $3.97. She wore all of them. The handbag was empty, yet many handbags she had seen were carried as if their emptiness were important, so she tucked it beneath her arm with confidence.

Then she went to a mirror.

“Good evening,” she told her reflection.

“Good evening,” replied the lady in the mirror without making a sound.

The wax lady admired this polite person and smiled. As her own smile had been manufactured, it was already admirable and required no practice.

At six o’clock Mr. Dobbs, the night janitor, entered the ladies’ department with a broom. He saw a splendid customer standing before the mirror and hurried forward.

“Store’s closed, ma’am,” said he.

“Good evening,” answered the wax lady.

“You’ll have to go out.”

She had seen customers go out through the revolving door, so she walked toward it. But a revolving door is confusing even to people who have known doors all their lives. The poor thing entered one compartment, walked around twice, came back into the store, and said, “Good evening,” to Mr. Dobbs.

“This way!” he cried impatiently, seizing her wrist.

His fingers sank slightly into the wax.

Mr. Dobbs stared at the little dents he had made. Then the lady turned her blue glass eyes upon him and smiled her fixed smile.

The janitor gave a faint squeak, sat down in a basket of marked-down stockings and whuckered himself completely unconscious.

Having learned from this that janitors commonly sat in baskets, the wax lady would have done so too, but she noticed that the revolving door was still moving. She stepped into it and was carried out upon the pavement.

The city delighted her. Electric signs blinked red and gold, motorcars honked, newsboys shouted and umbrellas bumped one another in the rain. Everyone appeared to know where to go. The inexperienced lady decided that she must also go somewhere, and therefore followed a plump woman in a sealskin coat.

The plump woman entered a restaurant.

At the lunch counter she said, “Ham sandwich, coffee and apple pie.”

The wax lady sat upon the next stool.

“Wot’ll ye ’ave?” asked the waiter.

“Ham sandwich, coffee and apple pie,” she repeated.

The waiter brought them and pushed the check toward her. The wax lady watched the plump woman bite her sandwich, so she raised her own sandwich to her mouth. But her teeth were painted upon one solid strip of porcelain and would neither open nor bite.

She pressed the sandwich against them several times.

“Somethin’ wrong with it?” asked the waiter.

“No,” said the wax lady, having now acquired another useful word.

She poured the coffee against her lips. It ran down her chin, entered the neck of her purple gown and softened a small patch below her collar. This did not hurt, because wax ladies are not sensitive to coffee, although hot weather may distress them severely.

The plump woman paid and left. The wax lady rose to imitate her.

“Here!” cried the waiter. “You forgot your check.”

She looked back.

“Twenty-eight cents,” he explained.

“No.”

“No what?”

“No twenty-eight cents.”

“Then why’d you order?”

The poor creature considered this. “Ham sandwich, coffee and apple pie.”

Several customers laughed. The waiter did not, for he had worked since morning and had lost his appreciation of mystery.

“You got money in that bag?”

He reached for the beaded handbag. The wax lady, believing this another social custom, handed it to him. He opened it, found nothing, and demanded her name.

She had no name.

“Lady,” she said, because Mr. Dobbs had called her “ma’am” and the waiter had called another customer a lady.

“Where d’you live?”

“Store.”

The waiter looked at the coffee running from her sleeve, the stiff smile and the unblinking glass eyes. He decided there were some debts not worth collecting.

“Go on,” he said. “You give me the creeps.”

“Good evening,” she replied, and went out.

A newsboy waved a newspaper under her nose.

“Yer evenin’ paper! All the news!”

The wax lady took it because she had seen others take papers. Then she walked away.

“Hey! Two cents!”

“No two cents,” she said pleasantly.

“Can’t ye read?”

“No.”

“Then wot d’ye want a paper for?”

She observed a gentleman fold his paper and sit upon it to keep a bench dry. Accordingly she laid hers in a puddle and sat upon it. The newsboy watched her for a moment, decided she was either very grand or very foolish, and hurried away to sell the next copy more carefully.

Presently a taxicab stopped.

“Cab, ma’am?” called the driver.

“No,” she answered. “I’m wax.”

The driver had not expected this information. He stared after her, scratched his cap and said that city women were getting funnier every year.

At the corner several people waited beside a pole. The wax lady waited also. When a streetcar arrived, everyone climbed aboard, so she climbed aboard and sat beside a little old gentleman carrying flowers.

The conductor came jingling through the car.

“Fares, please!”

Passengers gave him coins. When he reached the wax lady, she gave him one brass button from her gown, having seen things pass from hand to hand but not understanding their value.

“What’s this?” he demanded.

“Fare,” she said.

“This ain’t money.”

“No money.”

“Then you can’t ride.”

The lady remained seated. Patience was one quality she possessed in abundance, for she had formerly stood twelve hours a day in a window without shifting so much as an eyelash.

“Off you go,” ordered the conductor.

“No.”

He took her by the shoulder. His thumb pushed into her neck and came away with a smear of pink wax.

The conductor’s face turned pale.

“What are you?” he whispered.

“Lady.”

“You ain’t!”

“I’m wax.”

The little old gentleman dropped his flowers. A girl screamed. Someone cried that the passenger was melting, although she was not; and another person shouted that she had no pulse, which was perfectly true and no cause for alarm.

The conductor backed away until he sat suddenly upon the little old gentleman’s lap.

“Stop the car!” he yelled.

The motorman stopped. Passengers tumbled through both doors, leaving hats, parcels and one small dog behind. The conductor followed them, and even the motorman abandoned his post after taking a single look at the wax lady’s smiling face.

The poor creature supposed they had all arrived at their destination. She stepped down and crossed the track directly before another streetcar.

There came a clang, a shout and a tremendous bump.

The wax lady flew through the air, lost her green-feathered hat, struck a lamppost and landed in the street with one ear broken off, her left hand twisted backward and the top of her head caved in like a pudding mold.

A crowd gathered.

“Dead!” cried a woman.

“Get the police!”

“She stepped straight in front of it!”

“She never even screamed!”

The wax lady sat up.

“My hat,” she said.

The crowd moved backward as one person. A policeman named Officer Brindle pushed through, twirling his club with professional bravery. When he saw her damaged head and cheerful expression, his knees knocked together; but because he wore a uniform, he pretended the sound came from the streetcar bell.

“Madam,” he said, “you have caused an obstruction.”

“My hat.”

Officer Brindle retrieved the hat and placed it over the hollow in her head.

“You’d better come with me.”

“Good evening.”

He led her to the police station, keeping a careful distance from her waxen arm. There Inspector Mugg sat behind a high desk, looking level-headed. Inspector Mugg was proud of his level head and often announced that nothing could astonish him, although a mouse in his wastebasket had once made him stand upon a chair.

“What’s the charge?” he asked.

Officer Brindle saluted. “Riding without fare, obstructing traffic, and maybe being dead, sir.”

“People cannot be charged with being dead.”

“She says she’s wax.”

Inspector Mugg examined the prisoner. He touched her broken ear, peered into the dent beneath her hat and rapped her wooden elbow with a pencil.

“Clearly a woman in disguise,” he declared.

“I’m a lady,” she corrected him.

“Name?”

“Lady.”

“Residence?”

“Store.”

“Occupation?”

“Good evening.”

Inspector Mugg frowned. “Lock her up until she chooses to answer sensibly.”

Thus the wax lady was placed in a cell. She did not object, because a cell was not much smaller than the window in which she had spent her former existence, and it contained a bench upon which she could stand very comfortably.

Meanwhile, Silas Floman had arrived at his department store and discovered Mr. Dobbs in the stocking basket. After being restored with cold water, the janitor told a confused account of a customer whose wrist had dents in it.

Mr. Floman ran to the window.

“My mannequin!” he screamed. “My imported mannequin! And she has stolen a $19.98 gown, a $4.23 hat, 76-cent gloves, a $2.49 handbag and shoes reduced to $3.97!”

He hurried to the police station with the price tags in his fist.

“That is my property!” he cried upon seeing the prisoner. “Altogether, including sales tax and damage to the window display, she has taken $31.43!”

“She claims to be a lady,” said Inspector Mugg.

“She is a dummy!”

“Then can a dummy commit theft?”

“No, but somebody must pay me $31.43.”

“Can a dummy ride without fare?”

“Not in a $19.98 gown!”

“Can we imprison merchandise?”

“You may imprison it provided you return the $4.23 hat.”

The officials argued, and Mr. Floman repeated the prices until even the station clock seemed to tick, “Nineteen-ninety-eight, four-twenty-three, seventy-six.”

At that moment Tanko-Mankie the Yellow Ryl peeped through the barred window. He had returned from Fairyland to discover what had happened, and the sight of policemen, a store owner and an animated mannequin in jail pleased him immensely.

Still, even a mischievous Ryl understands that every joke must end before supper.

He fluttered invisibly into the cell, perched upon the wax lady’s crooked shoulder and whispered, “Have you enjoyed being alive?”

“My hat,” she answered, for it was the latest thing she had wanted and no one had taught her how to forget.

Tanko-Mankie sighed, tapped her three times upon the wooden funny bone and spoke the enchantment backward.

At once the light left her glass eyes. Her raised hand dropped. Her manufactured smile remained, but it no longer seemed hopeful or puzzled or brave. She was only a battered dummy in a torn purple gown.

When Inspector Mugg, Officer Brindle and Mr. Floman entered the cell, they found her leaning stiffly against the wall.

“There!” said Inspector Mugg triumphantly. “Exactly as I maintained. Wood, wax, paint and screws. It never lived.”

Mr. Floman groaned over the missing ear, the ruined gown, the coffee stain and the caved-in head. “Thirty-one dollars and forty-three cents, not counting repairs!”

Officer Brindle said nothing until the inspector had gone. Then he lifted the green-feathered hat and looked into the poor lady’s still-smiling face.

“Maybe she’s a dummy now,” he whispered, “but this one were a dummy as lived.”

And because Tanko-Mankie was listening outside the window, he laughed softly all the way home to Fairyland.

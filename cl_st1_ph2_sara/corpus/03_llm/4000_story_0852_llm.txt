The night had settled heavily upon the low country. It was not a darkness that cooled or refreshed, but one that seemed steeped in the day’s heat and laid like black velvet over the roofs, gardens and shining reaches of the bayou. No breath moved among the orange leaves. The magnolias held their pale blossoms rigid, and the odor rising from them was too sweet, almost oppressive, as though the flowers themselves had grown faint.

Beyond the levee a lugger drifted down with the current, its brown sail hanging loose and its lantern making a sleepy star upon the water. From somewhere across the marais came the thin complaint of a violin. It was answered presently by a man’s voice, then by laughter so remote that it sounded scarcely human. The music floated nearer, faded, returned again, and was lost at last among the cypresses.

Madame Delisle lay beneath the gauze of her bed, turning her face from one warm pillow to the other.

“Manna Loulou,” she said, “you fan as though your arm were dreaming.”

The old woman, seated beside the bed, roused herself and moved the palm-leaf fan with greater vigor. Her white tignon shone dimly in the candlelight; her broad face, dark and wrinkled, wore the patience of one who had watched many nights pass and many griefs exhaust themselves.

“It is the heat, Madame,” she answered. “Even my bones are asleep.”

“Then tell me something. A little story, Loulou—one from the old time.”

“Ah, the old time!” Manna Loulou’s fan slowed again. “Those stories are not always good for sleeping.”

“I do not wish to sleep. Tell me of love.”

At that the old woman gave a soft sound, half laughter, half sigh.

“Love? Then I will tell Madame about Zoraïde. But afterward Madame must not say Manna Loulou put bad dreams beneath her pillow.”

And while the lugger slipped unseen into the darkness and the violin began once more beyond the bayou, Manna Loulou told the story.

Zoraïde belonged to Madame Delarivière, who was also her nénaine. Her mother had died when she was very small, and Madame, having no daughter of her own, brought the child into the great house and reared her there among mirrors, lace curtains and silver dishes.

Nothing was thought too fine for Zoraïde. She learned to stitch flowers upon linen, to arrange Madame’s hair, to dance without disturbing the candle balanced on her head. She wore little slippers upon feet that had never crossed a cane-field barefoot. Her gowns came from the same dressmaker who sewed for the young ladies of the quarter, though of course they were made plainer, as Madame reminded everyone.

Zoraïde grew beautiful—so beautiful that people turned in church to look at her. Her skin had the warm color of coffee softened with cream; her eyes were dark and grave until she laughed, when they sparkled like water struck by sun. Her hair fell in smooth waves, black and lustrous, and she had a proud way of carrying her head which Madame sometimes praised as elegance and sometimes reproved as vanity.

“My little Zoraïde will make a fine marriage,” Madame Delarivière often said.

She had already chosen Ambroise, her own valet, for the husband. Ambroise was careful with his clothes, respectful in his speech and light enough in complexion to satisfy Madame’s ideas of what was suitable. He could read a little, write his name handsomely and calculate household accounts. He possessed a gold watch that did not run, which he displayed on Sundays.

But Zoraïde could not endure him.

“He smells of pomade,” she told Manna Loulou, “and he admires himself in every window.”

“Piti, a husband may have worse faults.”

“Then let him carry his faults to another woman.”

It was not Ambroise whom Zoraïde watched when the people gathered on feast days beneath the pecan trees. It was Mézor.

Manna Loulou had seen him dance the Bamboula and the Calinda upon packed earth while the drums spoke under the hands of old men. Mézor was a field laborer from a plantation above the city. He was tall and erect, with shoulders like a carved figure at the prow of a ship, and skin so deep in color that lamplight lay blue upon it. When he danced, strength and grace passed through him together. His feet struck the ground, his body bent, sprang and turned; yet his head remained proud, and his eyes sought Zoraïde among all the faces.

“Ah, Madame,” said Manna Loulou, “there are men who ask for love with songs, and men who ask with jewels. Mézor asked only by looking.”

One evening Zoraïde disappeared from Madame’s side during the dancing. She went beyond the torchlight to where the banana leaves whispered beside a wall. Mézor waited there.

“You should not have come,” she told him.

“You came also.”

“I came to tell you not to look at me.”

“Then do not look at me, Zoraïde.”

But she looked; and after that they met whenever love could steal an hour from those who believed they owned every hour of their lives.

Presently Zoraïde went to Madame Delarivière and knelt beside her chair.

“Nénaine,” she said, “I do not want Ambroise. Let me marry Mézor.”

Madame was at first astonished, then amused, then angry.

“Mézor? That black field hand?”

“He is a man, nénaine.”

“A man! There are a thousand men. One does not marry any creature who beats a drum and shows his teeth at a dance.”

“He does not beat the drum. He dances.”

“That makes the matter far more respectable, no doubt.” Madame folded her hands and regarded Zoraïde with a tenderness sharpened by displeasure. “I have raised you differently. I have protected you from coarse people and coarse ways. You would throw yourself away upon a negro of the fields?”

“I love him.”

Madame’s face hardened.

“You speak like a fool. Ambroise is of your own class and color. He will have a position in this house. Mézor will have mud on his legs and cane leaves in his hair until he dies.”

Zoraïde rose. The pride that Madame had once admired was in every line of her.

“The mud does not frighten me.”

“It should,” answered Madame. “And you will not marry him.”

No more was said then; but a command does not extinguish love. It only teaches love secrecy.

Weeks passed, and Zoraïde became quiet. She avoided Ambroise, neglected her embroidery and stood long at windows looking toward the road. Madame believed the foolishness was leaving her. Then one morning Zoraïde fainted while fastening her nénaine’s gown, and before many days the truth could no longer be concealed.

Madame Delarivière did not strike her. She did not raise her voice. Her anger was colder than that.

“You have disgraced my house,” she said. “After all I have done for you.”

Zoraïde answered nothing.

“Did you imagine I would permit this Mézor to boast that he had taken what belonged to me?”

At this Zoraïde lifted her head.

“I belong to God.”

Madame turned pale. “You belong where the law has placed you.”

That same afternoon she sent a message to Mézor’s master. Money was owed between the families; favors could be exchanged. Within the week Mézor was taken away in chains with a group bound toward the Red River country—or perhaps to Texas, perhaps farther. No one could tell Zoraïde where he had gone. The men who sold him did not consider a direction part of what she was entitled to know.

She ran down the road when she heard. Dust rose around her skirts, and the sun beat upon her uncovered head. But the wagon had already passed beyond the bend.

For days she cried out Mézor’s name. Afterward she never spoke it. Her child turned beneath her heart, and she lived only for that movement.

Madame Delarivière watched her with an anxious severity. She had decided what must be done. The child, she told herself, was the knot binding Zoraïde to her disobedience. Remove it, and gratitude, good sense and old habits would return. Zoraïde might yet marry Ambroise. She might yet become again the graceful girl who stood behind Madame’s chair and sang while arranging flowers.

The baby was born during a night of rain. It was a girl, strong and perfect, with small clenched fists and a cry that filled the room.

“Let me see her,” whispered Zoraïde.

The midwife laid the child against her. Zoraïde smiled, and those who saw that smile remembered it afterward with pain. She touched the dark silk of the baby’s hair, counted its fingers and murmured a name no one heard.

But before dawn, while exhaustion held her senseless, the child was taken away. Madame sent it to one of her plantations, with orders that it be fed, clothed and never brought to the house.

When Zoraïde awoke, her arms searched the bed.

“My baby?”

Madame sat beside her. She had prepared words of pity, words of religion, words about Providence and submission.

“The child did not live, Zoraïde.”

The poor mother stared.

“No.”

“It is true. She was too weak.”

“She cried.”

“For a little while.”

“I held her. She was strong.”

Madame lowered her eyes and committed the wicked falsehood again.

“She is dead.”

Zoraïde turned her face toward the wall.

“Li mouri,” she said.

All that day she repeated it. Li mouri. She is dead. Through fever and weakness, through sunlight and night, the words came with the same dull cadence. Sometimes she asked where the little body had been buried; sometimes she begged them to dig it up because the baby might be cold. No answer contented her.

Then, one morning, Zoraïde became quiet.

She tore a strip from a petticoat and rolled it tightly. Around this she wrapped a handkerchief, then a piece of blue cloth. She cradled the bundle against her breast and rocked it.

“My little one was not dead,” she announced. “You wished to deceive me, nénaine, but I found her.”

Madame tried to take the rags.

Zoraïde screamed so terribly that the servants came running. She crouched in a corner, guarding the bundle with her body, her eyes wide and savage.

After that they let her keep it.

Years passed. The beauty did not leave Zoraïde at once; it faded slowly, like color from cloth laid too long in sunlight. She wandered through the rooms singing to her rag child. She fed it from an empty spoon, bathed it in an empty basin and hushed those who spoke above a whisper.

People no longer called her la belle Zoraïde.

They called her Zoraïde la folle.

Madame Delarivière grew old, and remorse began to trouble the prayers she said each morning. She dreamed of the real child on the distant plantation. Reports came that the girl was healthy, obedient and much like her mother. At last Madame persuaded herself that truth might cure what falsehood had destroyed.

The child was brought to the house. She was perhaps six years old, slender and solemn, in a clean white dress. She stood before Zoraïde with frightened eyes.

“Look,” Madame said, weeping. “Here is your daughter. Your true daughter. I lied to you, Zoraïde. She did not die. Take her now, and forgive me.”

For one instant everyone waited.

The little girl stretched out her hands.

“Maman?”

Zoraïde drew back. She clutched the rag bundle beneath her chin.

“No,” she whispered.

“It is your child!”

“No! You want to trick me. You brought that girl so I would put down my baby, and then you would steal her again.”

She fled through the house, laughing and sobbing, while the living child remained where she was, her hands still extended.

They returned the girl to the plantation. She grew there without Mézor, who was never heard from, and without the mother who had chosen madness because truth had been made unbearable. Zoraïde lived many years. White crept into her hair; her back bent; her songs lost their words. Yet always she carried the rags, wrapped and rewrapped, tenderly guarded from every hand.

Manna Loulou ceased speaking.

The violin beyond the bayou had fallen silent. Madame Delisle lay very still beneath the netting, and the candle beside her bed burned low.

“And the daughter?” she asked at last.

“She lived, Madame.”

Madame Delisle sighed and turned her face into the pillow.

“It would have been kinder if she had died.”

Manna Loulou’s dark hand paused above her. Then the fan resumed its slow passage through the heated air.

And this is what was truly said between them, in the soft tongue of that country:

“Et la piti, Loulou?”

“Li viv’, Madame.”

“Ah, pauv’ piti. Mieux si li té mouri.”

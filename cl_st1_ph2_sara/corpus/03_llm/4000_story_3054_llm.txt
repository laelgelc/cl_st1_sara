Rupert Harborough’s objection to his prospective brother-in-law was not founded on any of those prejudices which, being indefensible, are usually described as instinctive. He had watched the young man closely, heard him often, and suffered by him repeatedly; his dislike was therefore the ripe product of observation. Kathleen called the young man Hugh, his mother called him her “wandering boy,” and several charitable acquaintances called him well-meaning. Rupert called him, in the privacy of his mind, the Sheep.

There are human sheep who possess the amiable, woolly qualities of their kind, and there are others who combine its expression with the destructive mobility of a landslide. The Sheep belonged to the latter order. He had a pink, apologetic smile, an immense confidence in the eventual rightness of whatever he had just done, and a genius for entering situations at the precise point where ignorance became expensive.

Kathleen loved him. This circumstance, which would have softened a more sentimental man, merely made Rupert regard him as an invading power.

“I don’t ask you to admire Hugh,” she said. “Only to be fair to him.”

“I am scrupulously fair,” said Rupert. “I dislike him for reasons which he renews almost daily.”

The first substantial renewal occurred at bridge. Rupert cared for cards as he cared for shooting, speeches and machinery: not passionately, perhaps, but with a conviction that they should be handled by people who understood them. The Sheep played bridge as though the rules had been explained to him during a railway collision.

On the evening in question Rupert and the Sheep opposed Lady Bastable and Colonel Meryweather. With a modest contract safely established, the Sheep conceived the notion that Rupert’s emphatic lead was an appeal for quite another suit. Nothing in the bidding, the cards, or Rupert’s expression supported this revelation, but inspiration is notoriously independent of evidence. The Sheep acted upon it with sunny courage and lost the rubber by a margin that startled even Lady Bastable.

“I’m so sorry,” he said, producing the smile. “I thought you wanted diamonds.”

“I wanted you to notice that I had bid no diamonds,” said Rupert.

“Yes, of course. I see that now.”

The wisdom that arrives after its subject has been buried is one of the least valuable forms of enlightenment.

Kathleen, who had been reading by the fire, said that everybody made mistakes at bridge.

“Certainly,” said Rupert. “But most people distribute them. Hugh concentrates.”

The Sheep laughed with perfect good humour. He was impervious to irony, as a duck is impervious to weather, and for much the same reason.

The shooting incident was less easily enclosed within the category of social misfortune. A rare honey-buzzard had taken to visiting the western woods of Harborough. Rupert’s son Robert, before his death, had first identified the bird and had spent patient mornings watching its uncertain flight above the beeches. The boy had called it the Friar, on account of its hooded head, and after Robert was gone the occasional reappearance of the bird had become for Rupert one of those private observances which grief establishes and strangers are expected not to profane.

Everyone on the estate knew that the honey-buzzard was protected. The keepers knew it, the children in the village knew it, and the Sheep was told it twice before luncheon and again while the guns were being handed out.

Late in the afternoon a shot sounded from the lower covert, where no shot should have sounded. Rupert found the Sheep standing over the crumpled bird with the expression of a man who has performed a doubtful public service.

“I thought it was a large hawk,” he explained.

“It was a large hawk,” said Rupert with polite bitterness. “It was also the one bird you had been particularly instructed not to shoot.”

“I’m frightfully sorry. I suppose there are others?”

“There was this one.”

The Sheep looked down at the barred feathers.

“It came over so suddenly.”

Rupert reflected that this defence might be made by a pedestrian who had shot an archbishop in Piccadilly. Sudden appearance does not ordinarily cancel identity.

Kathleen wept when she heard. She wept partly for the bird, partly for Robert, and partly because Rupert’s silence had the hard brilliance of frost. The Sheep sent him, next morning, a volume on British birds, inscribed “With sincere regrets.” It contained a coloured plate of the honey-buzzard and several inaccuracies. Rupert put it in the waste-paper basket, where at least its classification was beyond dispute.

Even then the engagement continued. Betrothals, like minor governments, may survive errors which would have destroyed a more useful institution.

The Sheep’s third great intervention took place in public life. Rupert had represented his division in Parliament for nine years and proposed to do so for a tenth, in spite of a troublesome boundary alteration and a Radical opponent with a talent for remembering the price of bread. The contest required discipline, punctuality and an avoidance of gratuitous offence. The Sheep offered assistance.

“No,” said Rupert.

Kathleen protested that Hugh spoke very nicely at dinners.

“He speaks very nicely because dinners cannot answer back.”

Nevertheless, through the treachery of a local committee-woman who believed that youth must be encouraged, the Sheep was invited to address a meeting in the manufacturing town of Lembury. Rupert arrived late from another engagement and heard, while still in the passage, the buoyant tones of his husband-elect brother-in-law.

The Sheep had chosen unemployment as his theme. Having never been unemployed, nor employed in any recognisable sense, he approached the subject with freshness. He informed an audience containing two hundred dismissed mill-hands that honest work was always available to those who looked for it; he mentioned his own difficulty in finding a satisfactory second gardener; and he concluded by suggesting that periods of enforced leisure might profitably be devoted to self-improvement.

There are remarks which fall upon a meeting like sparks upon dry heather. Rupert entered in time to see an army with banners become a furniture-breaking multitude. The chairman’s bell disappeared, three windows suffered political martyrdom, and the Sheep was removed through the scullery of an adjoining temperance hotel.

“I’m most awfully sorry,” he said later. “They misunderstood me.”

“They understood you with a completeness I should not have thought possible,” said Rupert.

The seat was lost by three hundred and eleven votes. Lembury gave the opposing candidate a majority of four hundred and six.

Kathleen maintained that the national swing was responsible.

“The national swing,” Rupert observed, “did not mention the second gardener.”

Winter found them all at a small Alpine resort where the snow was excellent, the hotel overheated and the English colony numerous enough to make foreign travel unnecessary. Rupert had gone because Kathleen wished it; the Sheep had gone because Kathleen was there; and a young yard-dog named Bisket had gone because Rupert, having bought him from the hotel proprietor, trusted nobody else to train him.

Bisket was a tawny, long-legged creature, still uncertain whether human beings existed to command him or to be joyfully knocked down. He attached himself to Rupert with immediate violence and regarded ladders, skis and fur rugs as enemies of the household.

Beyond the hotel lay a lake which appeared, under its white covering, to be frozen. Notices in German, French and an English of almost military directness declared that it was not safe. The hotel porter repeated the warning each morning. Rupert tested the edge and forbade Kathleen to venture on it.

The Sheep, who had brought skates, considered these precautions excessive.

“I saw some boys crossing yesterday,” he said.

“They were on the northern inlet,” said Rupert. “The spring runs beneath this side.”

“It looks solid enough.”

“So did your speech.”

The Sheep smiled defensively and went to oil his skates.

That afternoon Rupert was in the yard teaching Bisket not to assault a broom when Kathleen came running from the terrace.

“Hugh is on the lake.”

The white surface stretched placidly beneath the grey sky. Far out, near a patch where the snow had darkened, the Sheep moved in large, uncertain curves, flourishing one arm to show that warnings, like election figures, could be misinterpreted by timid people.

Rupert shouted. The Sheep waved.

Then the ice broke.

For a moment he disappeared altogether; then his head and shoulders rose in a black circle of water. He struck at the edge, which splintered beneath his gloves.

“Don’t struggle,” Rupert called. “Keep your arms on the ice.”

“I’m all right,” gasped the Sheep, with astonishing fidelity to his character.

Rupert ran for the long ladder kept against the shed. Whatever secret solutions he might have entertained during darker reflections, they had never included standing idle while Kathleen’s chosen husband drowned. Hatred is often a leisurely emotion; emergency makes technicians of us all.

He dragged the ladder from the wall and turned towards the lake.

Bisket, observing the ancient enemy in motion, flung himself upon it with delighted guardian zeal. He seized a rung, growled magnificently and pulled in the opposite direction.

“Leave it!” shouted Rupert.

Bisket interpreted this as encouragement. The ladder slewed sideways, struck a water butt and sent Rupert to one knee. He rose, kicked free, and hauled again. The dog bounded after the trailing end, biting, barking and placing his entire young body where it would cause the maximum delay.

It occupied perhaps forty seconds. In ordinary life forty seconds is a negligible fragment, insufficient for a speech, a rubber, or a satisfactory apology. On unsafe ice it can acquire the dimensions of an era.

When Rupert reached the bank, the black circle was empty.

Men came with ropes; a boat was broken from its winter moorings; the porter crossed himself and gave instructions nobody followed. They recovered the Sheep after dusk. His apologetic smile had finally deserted him.

Kathleen’s grief was genuine and decorous. She remembered his kindness, his cheerfulness and the many things he had intended to do. Rupert remembered these also, though without finding that intention improved them. He did not tell her about Bisket’s intervention. The dog’s share in the afternoon remained between Rupert, Bisket and an overturned water butt.

In the spring Rupert took the animal back to Harborough. Bisket matured into a splendid guard, suspicious of strangers, respectful of game and entirely indifferent to political theory. Rupert was never seen to strike him, even when correction might have been technically justified.

Two years later Kathleen married Malcolm Athling, a man who could play a sound hand, distinguish protected birds, and address working men without offering them moral instruction from the shrubbery. Their first child was christened Robert, and called Robbie before the water had dried on his forehead.

Rupert became extravagantly devoted to the boy. On summer mornings Robbie rode before him on the old pony, while Bisket trotted at their heels with sober proprietorship. Sometimes a honey-buzzard circled above the western beeches, whether the old bird’s mate or some unconnected visitor nobody could say.

Rupert would uncover his head and watch it pass. Then Robbie would laugh, Bisket would press against his knee, and the three of them would go home to luncheon.

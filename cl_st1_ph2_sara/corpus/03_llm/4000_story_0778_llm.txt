### THE OTHER PROFILE

At the autumn exhibition of the Société Nouvelle there hung, between a pallid Manet and a panel of Breton girls, a picture which brought the critics to a halt. It represented a herd of creatures—women to the waist, below it wolves, swine, or scaled and sinuous beasts—issuing from a violet wood toward a marble terrace on which Circe stood with uplifted cup. The enchantress herself was grave and almost maternal; the horror belonged to the transformed faces, where human recollection seemed imprisoned behind muzzles, tusks, and wet, imploring eyes.

“The painter has mistaken pathology for imagination,” observed M. de Salignac, who wrote on art with the authority of one who had never practiced it.

“On the contrary,” said another, “he has understood that the grotesque begins where memory survives form.”

A young sculptor objected that deformity could not be beautiful, and therefore could have no legitimate place in art.

“Then neither Rheims nor Amiens would be legitimate,” said Aaron Dunlap. “Their devils and tortured saints are as necessary as their angels.”

“Necessary to architecture, perhaps. But would you hang a mutilated face above your mantel?”

“If it had a soul worth painting.”

This answer, uttered without emphasis, was repeated afterward as a profession of faith. Dunlap was then thirty-six, an American long resident in Paris, sufficiently successful to be discussed and not sufficiently fashionable to be despised. His portraits were praised for tenderness, though certain sitters complained that tenderness was not always flattering. He painted women as if their beauty were an accident which time might presently correct, and old men as if age had merely removed an encumbering mask. In both cases he sought, beneath the polished physiognomy, some hesitating confession.

Paris had educated this instinct in him. He had arrived from Ohio at twenty-two, with the angular body, excellent conscience, and wounded vanity of a provincial youth; and the city, indulgent as a cultivated woman toward a sincere barbarian, had softened his manners without destroying his gravity. She had taught him that gray may contain a hundred colors, that wit may be kind though it refuses consolation, that bread, wine, linen, and conversation have each a ceremonial value. In return he loved her quays, her rain-darkened gardens, her tawny afternoons, even the sour exhalations of narrow streets behind the Panthéon. When disappointed, he walked beside the Seine; when successful, he went to the Louvre. Thus he had acquired that dangerous confidence of the artist who imagines that because he has learned to see, he has also learned to judge.

A week after the discussion at the Société Nouvelle, Mrs. Gilbert, an American widow with a large income and a small, vigilant mouth, visited his studio on the rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs. She wished him to paint her daughter Virginia.

“She has refused three painters,” Mrs. Gilbert said. “One made her too brilliant; another made her sentimental; the third—well, he was impossible. But she has seen your Madame de Rancy.”

“And approved?”

“She said the hands were alive.”

This, Dunlap knew, was an exact compliment. He agreed to call.

The Gilberts occupied an apartment near the Parc Monceau, sumptuous without warmth: pink marble, pale tapestries, cabinets bright with Sèvres, and an abundance of flowers which seemed selected less for fragrance than for social position. Virginia entered late. Dunlap, standing beside a window, saw first the right profile—a young, flower-tinted face, narrow at the temple and full at the lips, the eye darkly blue beneath a sweep of lashes, the hair tawny and fine, twining about the ear in silken disorder. It had the grave sweetness of a Botticelli head, modified by an American alertness about the mouth.

Then she turned.

There was something on the left cheek. His first impression was not of shape but of color: a glazed crimson irregularity stretching from the temple toward the jaw, as though fire had laid a violent hand upon the flesh and withdrawn before completing its work. The eyebrow was partly gone; the eyelid, though uninjured, appeared unnaturally exposed; near the ear the skin gathered in pale, rigid seams. It was not hideous—her youth forbade that—but it was unmistakable, and the contrast with the unmarred side gave to the whole countenance the painful doubleness of a broken image.

Neither mother nor daughter referred to it.

Virginia spoke of sittings, gowns, and the probable destination of the portrait. Her voice was low and pleasantly negligent. She wore no veil. Once, as she crossed the room, the full scarred profile was presented to him beneath the impartial light, and he felt the involuntary contraction of pity which he would have concealed even from himself. What courage! She offered the wound to the world without apology, without theatrical humility. There must be, beneath that composed manner, a history of suffering, perhaps a fastidious terror of being pitied. Her silence seemed more moving than lamentation.

He chose to paint her in pearl-gray satin, with one shoulder turned from the spectator and the head looking back. Mrs. Gilbert protested.

“It shows the left side.”

“I know,” said Virginia.

She met Dunlap’s eyes. The look was brief, almost amused, but he interpreted it as gratitude.

I

The sittings began in November. Virginia came with her mother, an elderly companion, or, more often, her cousin Eleanor Vane, who had recently arrived from Boston. Eleanor was twenty-eight, slender, colorless, and possessed of that quiet competence which fashionable people reward by imposing upon it. She brought books, remembered appointments, found mislaid gloves, and knew when Virginia was tired before Virginia knew it herself.

Dunlap scarcely noticed her at first. His whole imaginative energy was occupied by the problem before him. He would not disguise the scar, nor would he give it brutal prominence. To soften it was cowardice; to insist upon it, vulgarity. It must be rendered as a fact subdued to the unity of the face, like grief in a noble life.

Virginia sat admirably. She could remain motionless for forty minutes, one white hand lying along the arm of the chair, the gray folds breaking from her waist in large, tranquil planes. The studio light passed over her sound cheek, rested upon the bridge of her nose, and fell more coldly on the altered flesh. Sometimes Dunlap became conscious of the grotesque distinction between the two profiles: one seemed made for kisses and roses, the other for secrecy, endurance, and midnight thought. He fancied that in painting both he was painting youth and fate.

“You are tired,” he said one afternoon.

“No.”

“The light is nearly gone.”

“Then it is the light that is tired.”

She smiled with the unscarred side toward him. He was touched by what he supposed to be her evasion.

He waited for confidence. It did not come. Mrs. Gilbert once alluded vaguely to “the accident,” and Virginia changed the subject to a dress at Worth’s. Eleanor, when alone with him for a moment, seemed about to speak, but merely asked whether the studio fire smoked.

The omission enlarged the event. Dunlap imagined a nursery flame, an overturned lamp, perhaps an act of rescue. He saw the child Virginia standing amid curtains of fire, already beautiful, already doomed to bear on her face the autograph of pain. Every small coldness in her manner became reserve; every irony, a defense against curiosity. His pity, refined by aesthetic contemplation and flattered by its own delicacy, passed almost without his knowledge into love.

The portrait, when exhibited, was much praised. Some called it cruel, others merciful. A critic declared that Dunlap had painted “beauty reconciled with destiny.” Virginia herself studied it for several minutes at the private view.

“You have made it very red,” she said.

“It was necessary.”

“Is necessity always so ugly?”

He answered with some solemnity that truth was not ugly.

“No,” she said, adjusting a bracelet, “but it is frequently ill-bred.”

Yet she returned twice to stand before the picture. The second time Dunlap saw tears in her eyes. He did not perceive that she had been reading, just before, a newspaper paragraph describing her as “the tragically scarred American beauty.”

Their engagement astonished the American colony and disturbed Mrs. Gilbert, who had intended beauty—even damaged beauty—to purchase a title. Virginia accepted him without visible emotion. In the small blue drawing-room, under the cold regard of a Pompadour shepherdess, he tried to speak of devotion, protection, and the sacred generosity of trust.

“I shall never question you,” he said. “Whatever you wish forgotten—”

She looked at him sharply.

“What should I wish forgotten?”

He faltered. The scar was between them, immense because unnamed.

“All of us have memories that wound us.”

“I have an excellent memory,” she replied. Then, softening as he thought, she held out her hand. “You are very good, Aaron.”

He kissed it, and felt that goodness had been accepted as love.

They were married in the spring and went south. At Avignon, Virginia complained of the mistral; at Arles, of the hotel; at Les Baux, of the dust. The austere landscape, which to Dunlap appeared washed in antique gold, left her indifferent. She preferred Monte Carlo, where men watched her enter the dining-room and women calculated the price of her gowns. She had learned to arrange her hair so that, from the right, the scar was invisible; she placed herself at tables with strategic instinct and received admiration as a tribute unjustly delayed.

At first Dunlap admired this also. Was it not the instinct of life asserting itself against injury? If she delighted in jewels, music, suppers, and the rustle of her own train through brilliant rooms, these were perhaps reparations owed to her. He became her expiator, paying a debt contracted by fire.

Only slowly did he discover that Virginia’s courage consisted less in accepting her disfigurement than in refusing it any reality. Mirrors were placed according to her instructions; photographs were taken only from one angle; servants learned, without being told, on which side to offer a cloak. She spoke contemptuously of ugly people. A singer at the Opéra-Comique had “the mouth of a fish”; a celebrated duchess resembled “a moulting bird”; a limping child in the Tuileries was “a little medieval grotesque escaped from a waterspout.”

Dunlap was shocked, then indulgent. Her severity was the recoil of wounded sensibility. The scar had made beauty tyrannical to her; she condemned in others what she feared in herself. He continued to wait for the confession that would explain and transfigure everything.

Their daughter was born after two years of marriage.

The child, named Margaret, was frail, large-headed, and distressingly plain. A whitish birthmark covered part of her neck; her eyes were too near together; her thin hair grew in colorless wisps. Dunlap, seeing her in the nurse’s arms, felt the profound, almost animal tenderness which weakness awakens in the compassionate. Virginia turned her face away.

“She will improve,” he said.

“Will she?”

“All babies are crumpled at first.”

“Not all.”

She would not nurse the child. This was easily explained to acquaintances by her health, but no physician had forbidden it. Later, she objected to the cradle in her bedroom, the medicines on her dressing-table, the nurse’s shoes in the corridor. When Margaret cried, Virginia’s mouth tightened with personal offense.

One afternoon Dunlap entered the nursery and found the child, then nearly three, kneeling beside Eleanor Vane, who had come to make a long visit. Eleanor was tying a blue ribbon around the neck of a wooden lamb. Margaret leaned against her with the complete bodily confidence children reserve for those who love them. The northern light, filtered through muslin curtains, lay softly on Eleanor’s brown hair and capable hands; her face, though without brilliance, had a fine serenity, as if every lineament had consented to the service of something beyond itself.

“Papa,” Margaret said, “Nell’s lamb is beautiful.”

“It has only one ear,” Dunlap observed.

“That is why,” Eleanor said.

Their eyes met. He felt, with a discomfort almost physical, the contrast between this quiet tenderness and Virginia’s fastidious aversion.

That evening Virginia appeared at dinner in pink chiffon embroidered with gold beetles. The scar, reddened by the cold, showed beneath a dusting of powder. She spoke animatedly of a ball at the Austrian embassy and of a prince who had begged three dances.

“Margaret has been feverish,” Dunlap said.

“Has she? Eleanor did not tell me.”

“I thought you knew,” said Eleanor.

“How should I know? The nursery is your kingdom.”

There was laughter in the phrase, but Eleanor colored.

Dunlap looked across the table at his wife. For the first time the scar seemed not imposed upon her face but generated from within it. The damaged skin, once tragic in his eyes, appeared to spread invisibly over the smooth cheek, the lovely mouth, the clear blue eye. It was an unjust perception, and some remnant of the artist in him protested; yet perception, once morally corrupted, is not recalled by justice. He had sought a symbol and found one. Only its meaning had altered.

II

The Dunlap household became one of those elegant interiors in which every object is harmoniously placed and every affection displaced. Virginia entertained, traveled, bought clothes, and was much photographed from the right. Dunlap worked with increasing success and diminishing pleasure. His later portraits were considered stronger; tenderness had acquired an edge. He painted a minister whose polished face revealed, at the corners of the mouth, the minute fatigue of hypocrisy, and an actress of the Comédie-Française whose famous laughter he rendered as a muscular habit independent of joy.

At home he spoke little. He was ashamed of having been deceived, more ashamed that the deception had been largely his own. Virginia had never claimed spiritual nobility. She had merely sat in pearl-gray satin and permitted him to invent it. The silent tragedy he had worshiped was perhaps only silence; the hidden sensitivity, only an unwillingness to discuss an unbecoming fact.

Yet his disappointment demanded a culprit. The generosity with which he had loved her now seemed to him a treasure fraudulently obtained. He had offered pity as a gift and resented her for failing to deserve it.

Eleanor remained with them because Margaret’s health was uncertain and because Virginia, who mocked her dull gowns and provincial phrases, found her useful. She ordered flowers, corrected household accounts, read aloud to the child, and accompanied Dunlap and Margaret on Sunday walks when Virginia was sleeping after a ball.

These walks became the clearest hours of his week. They went to the Luxembourg, where Margaret sailed a red boat under the stone regards of queens, or along the gray quays, where Eleanor searched the boxes for children’s books. Her pleasure was without display. She could be silent without making silence an accusation; she listened not as a social accomplishment but as if another person’s words altered the actual composition of the world.

Dunlap began a drawing of her. He made it secretly and destroyed it. The gesture warned him too plainly.

Virginia, meanwhile, had noticed. Vanity possesses its own acuteness; it may be blind to love yet infallible in detecting the withdrawal of admiration. She began to interrupt their conversations, to summon Eleanor for trivial errands, to complain that Margaret was being made tiresome by excessive attention.

One wet March evening, after dinner, Virginia announced that she intended to spend Easter at Nice with friends.

“Margaret cannot travel,” Dunlap said. “The doctor was explicit.”

“I did not propose taking Margaret.”

“Then I shall remain with her.”

Virginia lifted her shoulders. “Naturally. Eleanor will remain too, I suppose.”

“If she is willing.”

“How idyllic.”

Eleanor rose. “I think I shall go to my room.”

“No,” Virginia said. “Why should you? We are discussing arrangements, not emotions.”

Dunlap’s restraint, practiced through years, had become brittle. “You make every arrangement an emotion and every emotion an inconvenience.”

She turned toward him. The room was lit by two lamps shaded in amber silk; behind her, the windows showed a black pane streaked with rain. She had dressed for the evening though they were alone, in white velvet with emeralds at her throat. Beauty still attended her with humiliating fidelity.

“You are obscure tonight, Aaron.”

“I am tired of being clear only in my own mind.”

“How unfortunate for your sitters.”

“Virginia,” Eleanor murmured.

But Virginia’s eyes remained on her husband. “Is this about the child? You have become sentimental on that subject. One would think unattractiveness a moral distinction.”

“She is your daughter.”

“I am not responsible for her appearance.”

“No,” he said. “You are responsible only for your own.”

The pause which followed was small, almost courteous. Virginia touched one emerald with her forefinger.

“What do you mean?”

He could still have withdrawn. He saw the path back into pretense and hated it because it was open.

“You have made beauty your religion,” he said, “as though you had never learned what fire can do to a face.”

Eleanor gave a low cry.

Virginia did not move.

“Or what it cannot do,” he added.

It was spoken. The scar, banished from language for years, entered the room with the violence of an intruder. Her hand rose—not to conceal it, but as if she had been struck there.

“You mean this?”

The words were barely audible.

Dunlap’s anger vanished at once. He saw only the white velvet, the exposed red seam of flesh, the frightened eye. Pity returned, but degraded now by remorse.

“Virginia—”

“This?”

She drew her fingers down the scar. Her face changed with a rapidity more terrible than weeping. Contempt, incredulity, naked hatred passed across it; then came an unnatural laugh.

“So that is what you married.”

“No.”

“Yes. Your beautiful sorrow. Your little masterpiece of charity.”

“I loved you.”

“You pitied me. There is no more insolent form of love.”

The justice of the phrase wounded him because it was incomplete.

“And you accepted it,” he said.

“I accepted a husband. I did not know I was accepting a curator.”

She turned to Eleanor. For an instant her expression became calm—too calm, and concentrated as the still head of a snake before it strikes.

“You may have him now,” she said. “You understand damaged things.”

She left the room.

The following morning Virginia departed for Nice. She took two maids, six trunks, and a jewel-case; she did not see Margaret before leaving. To Eleanor she sent a note requesting that several gowns remaining in the wardrobe be packed and forwarded. The household received no further explanation.

That evening Dunlap dined at his club. He had wished to remain at home, but Eleanor urged him to go.

“You are both overwrought,” she said. “An empty house will make you imagine things.”

“And you?”

“I shall put Margaret to bed and finish the packing.”

He walked through the rain with a sensation of unreality. Paris, which had once seemed to gather his troubles into her maternal dusk, now appeared merely wet, sordid, and indifferent. Omnibuses splashed the curbs; lamps shook in black puddles; the faces beneath umbrellas were flattened into masks. He ate nothing, drank two glasses of brandy, and returned shortly after ten.

The street before his house was full of people.

At first he saw only smoke issuing from an upper window, not thickly, but in gray coils made silver by the rain. Then a servant ran toward him, apron over her head.

“Monsieur—the lamp—the nursery—”

He pushed past her.

The fire had been extinguished before it reached the stair. A scorched smell—oil, wool, wet plaster, burned hair—filled the apartment. In the corridor outside the nursery lay broken glass and a blackened strip of carpet. Margaret, wrapped in a blanket, was crying in the arms of the cook.

“Where is Mademoiselle Vane?”

No one answered.

He found Eleanor in the small dressing-room, seated upright while a physician cut away the remnants of her blouse. Her hands were blackened and blistered. One side of her hair had burned almost to the scalp. The right half of her face was hidden beneath cloths soaked in oil and limewater; beneath them, along the jaw and temple, the flesh showed raw and darkly crimson.

She recognized him.

“Margaret is safe,” she said.

Then she fainted.

The servants’ account was simple. Eleanor had packed Virginia’s gowns and afterward gone to the nursery, carrying the little alcohol lamp she habitually used to heat milk when Margaret woke at night. She had set it on the table and struck a match. The reservoir exploded. Burning spirit ran across the carpet toward the bed. Eleanor, herself on fire, lifted Margaret through the flames and carried her into the corridor before collapsing. The nurse, who had been supping below, returned in time to smother Eleanor’s dress and raise the alarm.

“But lamps do not explode,” Dunlap said.

The physician looked at him without reply.

The next morning, when the wreckage had cooled, Dunlap examined the twisted reservoir. The brass cap lay several feet away. A sticky residue clung to the thread; the air about it retained a faint chemical sharpness unlike alcohol. Eleanor’s maid remembered that the lamp had been on Eleanor’s dressing-table before dinner. Virginia had entered the room that afternoon, she said, to ask about the packing. She had remained alone there for perhaps ten minutes.

Nothing could be proved. The servants contradicted themselves; the lamp was old; the police, discreetly consulted, spoke of pressure, defective fittings, and regrettable domestic carelessness. Virginia was already in Nice. A telegram announcing the accident brought no answer for three days.

Then came a letter in her large, sloping hand:

> I am horrified, though not surprised, that Eleanor should have managed a simple lamp with her usual incompetence. Naturally, every expense must be paid. I hope the child is not marked.

Dunlap read the final sentence many times. It was possible that only his hatred supplied the emphasis. It was possible that Virginia was innocent, that resentment had joined chance to create a shape of guilt. He knew how eagerly the mind completes an unfinished composition; had he not once painted nobility into a scar because the balance of his picture required it? Now he painted malice there. The evidence remained circumstantial, but imagination, that old betrayer, performed its office with murderous ease.

III

Eleanor lived. For weeks the doctors were uncertain whether she would retain the sight of her right eye; at last it was saved, though the lid contracted slightly. Her hands healed with shining patches. Her face did not.

The burn extended from the brow to the throat. Where the skin closed, it formed ridges and shallow hollows, some livid, some unnaturally white. The corner of the mouth was drawn upward, giving her repose an expression of faint, involuntary bitterness. Her ear had been badly injured; the hair above it grew only in sparse brown threads.

Dunlap visited each morning. At first she would not allow Margaret to see her.

“The child will be frightened.”

“She asks for you every hour.”

“Then tell her I am away.”

He obeyed for two days. On the third Margaret slipped past him into the darkened room. Eleanor turned toward the wall, but the child climbed upon the bed and pressed her face against Eleanor’s bandaged shoulder.

“Nell,” she said, “does it hurt?”

“Not now.”

“Papa says you carried me.”

Eleanor began to weep. Margaret, perplexed, patted the uninjured side of her cheek.

The simplicity of the child’s acceptance exposed the elaborate falsities of the adults. Margaret did not find Eleanor less beloved because she was less pleasing to look at; nor did she convert the wound into a moral emblem. It was a hurt. She wished it well. Dunlap, watching them, experienced a humility more painful than remorse. He had made physiognomy into destiny, reading virtue and corruption in flesh as priests once read divine anger in plague. Even compassion had been, with him, an aesthetic appetite.

Virginia did not return. From Nice she went to Florence, then to Vienna. Her letters concerned money, trunks, and the lease of the apartment. No message was addressed to Margaret. Through acquaintances Dunlap heard of her at dinners and at the opera, exquisitely dressed, her scar concealed by a new arrangement of hair and a fall of black lace. She was seen at Carlsbad with a Hungarian count; in Madrid with the wife of an attaché; in Cairo wearing silver tissue and a necklace of carved emeralds.

Society, which forgives almost anything when it is well clothed, admired her resilience.

Legal negotiations began the following year. Virginia alleged cruelty. Dunlap did not defend himself publicly; to have spoken of the lamp would have required either accusation or forgiveness, and he was capable of neither. There were consultations in offices smelling of leather and dust, affidavits, sealed letters, journeys to jurisdictions where marriage could be dissolved with less moral inconvenience. In the end Virginia obtained her divorce abroad.

Eleanor had returned to Boston, taking Margaret with her at Dunlap’s request. He remained in Paris and worked. During those two solitary years his art altered again. He ceased to seek the hidden drama which would explain a face. He painted what was present: fatigue without inventing martyrdom, hardness without deducing crime, beauty without worship. Critics complained that his new portraits were austere. One called them “morally undecided,” which pleased him more than praise.

He wrote to Eleanor every week. Her replies were affectionate and brief, filled with Margaret’s lessons, illnesses, sayings, and growth. She never mentioned her own appearance. When he proposed marriage, she refused.

“You do not owe me a life because I nearly lost mine,” she wrote. “Nor should Margaret’s need decide what concerns us. You have mistaken pity before. Be certain that gratitude has not put on the same mask.”

He was angry when he read this, then grateful. After six months he crossed the Atlantic.

He found Eleanor in a garden at Cambridge, seated beneath an apple tree while Margaret, now nine, read aloud from a book of legends. The afternoon light fell without mercy upon the scarred cheek. Dunlap had prepared himself for shock, and was ashamed of the preparation. The alteration was severe; memory, softened by distance, had lied. Yet within minutes the face resumed its expressive unity. The scar did not vanish, nor did it reveal some compensatory holiness. It was simply one fact among many: the attentive eyes, the fine brow, the humorous patience of the mouth, the quick color which still rose in the uninjured cheek.

Margaret ran to him. Eleanor remained seated.

“You have become gray,” she said.

“You have become severe.”

“I always was. You failed to notice.”

They smiled, and the smile released him from the need to be noble.

He remained in America through the summer. Nothing was decided in gardens, under moonlight, or beside a sickbed. Their intimacy advanced through ordinary days: meals, rain, Margaret’s quarrels with arithmetic, Eleanor’s dislike of his tobacco, his impatience with Boston callers. He saw her vain about her handwriting, unjust to a neighbor, absurdly timid in thunderstorms. These discoveries comforted him. He had no wish to marry another symbol.

In October she accepted him.

They returned eventually to Paris, where Dunlap reopened his studio. Margaret grew strong, though never pretty, and developed a frank intelligence which made prettiness seem beside the point. Eleanor managed the household without effacing herself in it. At receptions she wore her hair plainly and never veiled the burned side of her face. Some people praised her courage, which amused her; others avoided looking at her, which she preferred to being looked at heroically.

Virginia continued to traverse Europe like a brilliant, wounded comet. Her gowns were chronicled from Deauville to St. Moritz; one season she introduced sleeves of green ostrich feathers, the next a jeweled net worn over the scarred cheek. No charge was ever brought concerning the lamp. Dunlap never saw her again, and in later years he sometimes wondered whether his certainty of her guilt had been merely the final work of that imagination which had first made her innocent. The question remained without an answer.

Parisian society preserved little of these distinctions. It remembered that Virginia Gilbert had been beautiful, extravagant, and unfortunate; that Eleanor Vane had saved a child from a fire; that Aaron Dunlap’s portraits had grown rather stern after middle age. Above all, it delighted in the coincidence, so neat that no hostess could resist it, that the American painter who had once declared deformity a proper subject for art had married twice—and that both his wives had borne the mark of fire upon the face.

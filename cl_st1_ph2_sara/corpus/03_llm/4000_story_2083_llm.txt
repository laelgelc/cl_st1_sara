By four o’clock the light over the office had turned the colour of dirty wool. It pressed against the high windows and showed every smear upon them. Beneath the green shades the clerks bent over their books, ruling, copying, blotting, turning pages; and the scratch of their pens went on with the steadiness of mice behind a wall.

Farrington sat at the last desk but one, a broad man in a shiny coat. The collar of the coat was grey with wear, and a dark crescent of sweat had dried between his shoulders. His face, heavy and inflamed, seemed darker than usual in the failing light. He had copied seventeen lines of the contract since dinner.

He lifted the sheet and stared at it fixedly.

Seventeen lines. There were five full pages to be done before half-past five.

His tongue moved over his dry teeth. He could feel the thirst in the roof of his mouth, a rough hot patch which no swallowing eased. All afternoon he had tried not to think of porter. But the thought came back each time his pen stopped: a black pint standing on a wet counter, the foam settling slowly, the first cold mouthful.

The bell in the inner office rang.

Nobody moved. The bell rang again, sharply.

“Farrington!” called the chief clerk.

The man pushed back his chair and rose heavily. As he crossed the room he became aware that the other clerks had lowered their heads. He opened the glass door and went in.

Mr. Alleyne stood beside the fireplace with his hands beneath the tails of his coat. He was a small, pink, hairless man. Under the gas his bald head shone smooth and pale, like an egg left upon a plate. Miss Delacour sat by his desk, drawing on one glove finger by finger. A blue feather trembled over the side of her hat, and the perfume about her made the air seem close.

Mr. Alleyne looked up.

“You wished to see me, sir?” Farrington said.

“Did I wish to see you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Did I wish to see you?” Mr. Alleyne repeated. “I rang twice.”

“I came as soon as I heard, sir.”

“As soon as you heard. Quite. And perhaps you will tell me where you have been during the last twenty minutes?”

Farrington looked at the strip of carpet between them.

“I was in the lavatory, sir.”

“For twenty minutes?”

“No, sir.”

“No? Then where were you?”

“I was downstairs for part of the time.”

“Downstairs. On business, I presume?”

The man said nothing. He could feel Miss Delacour looking at the side of his face.

Mr. Alleyne took a paper from his desk and tapped it against his palm.

“This contract was given to you at two o’clock. It is required this evening. Mr. Crosbie is waiting for it. Yet I am informed that you have copied scarcely a page.”

“I’ll have it done, sir.”

“You’ll have it done?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And when, may I ask?”

“By half-past five.”

Mr. Alleyne gave a small laugh and glanced towards Miss Delacour. She lowered her eyes, but her mouth had bent at one corner.

“You have been absent from your desk three times since dinner,” he said. “Three times. You arrive late, you disappear without permission, and when urgent work is placed before you, you neglect it. Do you imagine this office is maintained for your convenience?”

“No, sir.”

“Do you imagine I pay you to stroll in and out as you please?”

“No, sir.”

“Then perhaps you can explain your conduct.”

Farrington’s right hand closed slowly. The nails pressed into his palm. If he took the little man by the neck he could lift him clear of the carpet. One shake—that was all. The smooth head would wobble on the narrow shoulders like the knob of a machine.

But Miss Delacour’s perfume lay between them, and beyond the glass door the pens scratched.

“I have no explanation, sir,” he said.

“That is evident. You will finish the contract before leaving. And let me warn you, Farrington: if I have another complaint, I shall place the matter before Mr. Crosbie.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You may go.”

The man returned to his desk. A red mist had gathered before his eyes. He dipped his pen, made two strokes and tore the paper with the nib. The clerk opposite him coughed into his hand.

So that was it. Threats before a woman. The little bald swine had waited until she was there. Of course he had. He wanted to display himself to her, to show her who gave orders in the office. And she with her feather and scent, sitting there as if she owned the building. It was known that her people had influence with the partners. Alleyne would lick the pavement for her.

Farrington blotted the tear in the paper and began again.

At ten minutes past four the thirst became unbearable. His stomach seemed lined with hot dust. He looked towards the chief clerk, then towards the glass door. Mr. Alleyne’s silhouette passed once across the blind and disappeared.

He bent over his work for another minute.

Could a man not take one half-pint? Five minutes there and back. The contract would go quicker afterwards. His hand was no use to him in this state. It was common sense.

He slipped from his chair, took his hat from the peg and went down the stairs.

Outside, the evening was raw and yellow. Carts jolted through the traffic, their wheels grinding mud against the kerbs. A tram bell clanged beyond the bridge. Farrington walked rapidly, though his bulk made the motion clumsy, and entered a narrow public house where a gas flame burned above bottles of red and green cordial.

“Half-one,” he said.

The barman drew the porter. Farrington laid down threepence and watched the glass fill. He drank deeply. The first mouthful cooled his gums and throat; the second spread warmth through his chest; after the third he set the glass down and drew breath through his nose.

That was better.

He finished it, wiped the foam from his moustache and looked at the clock.

Four seventeen.

“Another half?” asked the barman.

Farrington stared at the wet rings on the counter. Another would settle him properly. But he shook his head and went out.

When he returned, the chief clerk was standing beside his desk.

“Mr. Alleyne has asked for the contract.”

“It’s not ready yet.”

“I can see that.”

“I’ll bring it in when it is.”

The chief clerk lifted the copied sheets, counted them and put them down.

“You had better make haste.”

Farrington worked. The porter had soothed his throat but loosened his thoughts. Each sentence seemed to lengthen as he copied it. The words indemnity, aforesaid and hereinafter marched across the page and then returned upon themselves. Twice he omitted a line and had to scrape the ink with a blade. His wrist ached. The office grew darker; one by one the gas jets were lit, making yellow pools above the desks.

At five o’clock the bell rang again.

Mr. Alleyne came out in person.

“Well?” he said.

Farrington did not rise.

“I’m doing it, sir.”

“Doing it? Where is it?”

“It isn’t finished.”

“Not finished!”

Every head in the office bent lower. Mr. Alleyne took up the pages and flicked through them.

“Two pages and a quarter. Is this your notion of urgent work?”

“No, sir.”

“What is your notion, then?”

Farrington looked at the little man’s smooth head. The porter in his stomach had become sour.

“I asked you a question,” Mr. Alleyne said. “What is your notion of urgent work? Perhaps I am to copy the remainder myself?”

The man heard a chair creak somewhere behind him.

“Do you think,” Mr. Alleyne continued, raising his voice, “that I am here to perform your duties?”

“I never said you were, sir.”

“Then what did you say?”

“I said nothing, sir.”

“Exactly. You have nothing to say. You never have anything to say when called to account.”

Farrington raised his eyes. The words came before he had judged them.

“I thought, sir, you said I had too much to say.”

For an instant there was no sound. Then a clerk at the far desk choked. Another gave a short snort and turned it into a cough. Mr. Alleyne’s face changed from pink to crimson.

“What did you say?”

“I meant no offence, sir.”

“What did you say?”

“I was only answering, sir.”

“Answering! You call insolence an answer?”

Mr. Alleyne looked around the office and saw the bowed shoulders trembling. His mouth opened and shut.

“You will apologise at once.”

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

“That is not an apology.”

“I’m sorry for what I said.”

“You will say that you were insolent and that you regret it.”

Farrington’s fist closed beneath the desk.

“I was insolent, sir, and I regret it.”

“Louder.”

“I was insolent, sir, and I regret it.”

Mr. Alleyne held his gaze for a moment, then threw the pages down.

“You will hear more of this. Finish what you can and leave the remainder for the morning.”

He turned and entered his office, shutting the door hard.

The chief clerk waited half a minute before saying:

“You had better mind yourself.”

Farrington did not answer. His face had darkened to the colour of raw liver. Yet beneath the rage a small pulse of pleasure beat. They had laughed. Every man in the room had heard him take Alleyne up. The story would be worth telling. If only he had money enough for the evening.

He counted what was in his pockets: one shilling and twopence, a halfpenny, a tram ticket, two keys. Not enough. He owed Jenkins fourpence and had promised Nosey Flynn a drink. Payday was not until Friday.

His watch lay heavy against his waistcoat.

At half-past five he left the office. The fog had thickened over the quays, blurring the lamps and gathering orange about each flame. He turned into a lane off Dame Street and stopped before a pawnbroker’s shop. Three brass balls hung above the door, dark with damp.

Inside, wire partitions divided the counter into narrow boxes. A woman with a bundle stood before him, arguing in a whisper. The assistant shook his head, pushed the bundle back and called, “Next.”

Farrington entered the box and unhooked his watch.

“How much on this?”

The assistant opened the case, examined the works and clicked it shut.

“Five shillings.”

“It’s worth two pound.”

“Five.”

“I got eight on it last time.”

“Then you were fortunate last time.”

“Make it six.”

The assistant slid the watch back.

Farrington’s fingers rested on it. Alleyne’s crimson face appeared before him; then the wet counter, the black pints, the circle of men listening.

“Five, then.”

The ticket and coins were pushed through the grille. Farrington counted them twice, placed the ticket in his waistcoat pocket and went out.

He met Higgins and O’Halloran in a house near Fleet Street. They were standing at the bar with their hats pushed back, and Higgins had just ordered two glasses of stout.

“Here’s the very man,” O’Halloran said. “We heard there was murder done in Crosbie’s.”

“Murder?” Farrington said. “Who told you?”

“Little Leonard. Says Alleyne near burst a blood vessel.”

Farrington smiled and struck the counter with a coin.

“Name your poisons, lads.”

“That sounds healthy,” Higgins said.

They took three pints to a table under the window. The glass was clouded by steam and rain. Men moved past outside as shadows. Inside, the gas hissed, boots scraped sawdust and the air smelled of stout, wet wool and tobacco.

Farrington drank half his pint before beginning.

“He comes out to my desk,” he said, “with his head shining like a chapel lamp, and starts at me before the whole office. ‘Do you think I’m here to do your work?’ says he. ‘I never said you were,’ says I. Then he says I’ve nothing to say for myself.”

Higgins leaned forward.

“And what did you give him?”

Farrington paused, enjoying it.

“I says: ‘That’s queer, because a minute ago you said I had too much to say.’”

O’Halloran slapped the table.

“By God, that’s the one!”

“You said it to his face?”

“To his face. He went purple. The fellows were fit to split themselves.”

Farrington made Mr. Alleyne’s voice thin and pompous, repeating the demand for an apology. He enlarged the silence before his answer and made Miss Delacour witness the entire encounter. In his telling he did not lower his eyes. He stood square before the little tyrant, hands in his pockets, while the whole office waited upon his words.

“I’d have told him where to put his apology,” Higgins said.

“You would, would you?” O’Halloran said. “And be looking for a situation in the morning.”

Farrington frowned.

“A man has to watch himself with the likes of him. He has the partners’ ears poisoned.”

“Stand another?” Higgins asked.

Farrington called for three more.

By the time the second pint was gone, the stiffness had left his shoulders. Warmth filled his body, and the office seemed far away and contemptible. Let Alleyne report him. Let him report to Crosbie, to the Castle, to the Lord Lieutenant himself. There were other offices in Dublin. A man with Farrington’s experience would not be long without a berth.

Two more men joined them, Paddy Leonard and a young fellow named Weathers who had lately returned from Belfast with a travelling company. Weathers wore a brown coat cut close at the waist and a blue cravat fastened with a horseshoe pin. His cheeks were smooth, and he had a habit of turning his face sideways when he spoke, as though an audience sat beyond his shoulder.

“Is this the celebrated Farrington?” he asked.

“The same,” O’Halloran said. “He assassinated his employer this afternoon.”

Farrington told the story again. This time Alleyne had threatened to dismiss him on the spot, and Farrington’s answer came coolly, after he had looked the man up and down. Weathers laughed and tapped ash from his cigarette.

“Capital,” he said. “You fellows in the law offices have all the comedy.”

“He had no answer,” Farrington said.

“Couldn’t have. You pinned him.”

The phrase pleased Farrington. He ordered a round, though Leonard said it was his turn. Five drinks cost more than he expected. When the change came, he spread the coins in his palm and closed his hand quickly.

They went next to a brighter house beyond the river, where mirrors made the room seem crowded twice over. A girl played a cracked piano in the back lounge, and two soldiers sang part of a song without knowing the finish. The men stood shoulder to shoulder at the counter.

Farrington was telling the story for the third time when he saw the woman.

She stood near the partition with an older companion. She wore a large hat and a fur collar, though the evening was not bitter. Her hair showed beneath the brim in a copper coil. Once she looked towards Farrington, not quite at his eyes but at his broad chest and red face. Then she smiled at something her companion said.

He became conscious of his size. He straightened, drew in his stomach and spoke more loudly.

“So I looked at him,” he said, “and I says, ‘You’ll have to decide whether I say too much or nothing at all.’”

The men laughed. The woman turned again. Farrington lifted his glass and held her gaze, or thought he did. Her eyes were bright and pale. She whispered to her companion.

There now. She had noticed him.

He moved a little apart from the group, pretending to examine the bottles behind the bar. If she passed he would speak. Not roughly—just a word. Ask whether she had dropped her glove, perhaps. A woman like that would understand a man.

But the older companion gathered her cloak. The two women moved towards the door. Farrington took one step after them.

The younger woman looked back. Her glance passed across him without stopping. Then she said something to the older woman, and both laughed as the door closed.

“What’s the matter with you?” Leonard asked.

“Nothing.”

“You look as if you’d lost a sovereign.”

“I was only seeing who went out.”

Weathers was smiling into his glass. Farrington disliked the smoothness of his face, the neat cravat, the small clean hands.

“Women in Belfast finer than our Dublin girls?” he asked.

“There are fine women everywhere, if you know the proper door to knock at,” Weathers said.

The others laughed.

Farrington ordered whiskey. His money had diminished to two shillings and a few coppers. It was impossible. Five shillings did not go anywhere among a crowd. Somebody else should stand a drink. Leonard had promised.

But when the glasses were empty, Leonard began searching his pockets with great ceremony and found only a penny and a theatre counterfoil.

“My funds are temporarily depressed,” he said.

“You’ve a neck on you,” Farrington said.

“Come now, Tom. I stood two last Saturday.”

“That was last Saturday.”

“I’ll make it right on Friday.”

Farrington cursed and paid. He watched the barman sweep away the coins. Only ninepence remained.

They moved to a snug where Weathers knew two players from the theatre. One was a pale comedian with a green muffler; the other, an old man whose eyebrows were blackened for the stage. Drinks appeared, though Farrington did not see who paid for them. The talk turned to strength, prizefighters and a policeman in Belfast who could bend a poker around his neck.

“I’d like to see him bend mine,” Farrington said.

Weathers glanced at his arm.

“You carry weight, certainly.”

“Weight? There’s more than weight there.”

Farrington rolled up his sleeve. His forearm was thick and covered with reddish hair. He placed his elbow on the table and clenched his fist. The flesh shook slightly from drink.

“No doubt about it,” Higgins said. “Tom’s strong as a bull.”

Weathers removed his coat.

“Will we try a friendly pull?”

The table was cleared. Men gathered around. Farrington looked at Weathers’s arm. It was smaller than his own, but hard and white, with a cord showing from wrist to elbow.

“You’ll want both hands,” Farrington said.

“One will do for a beginning.”

They gripped. Weathers’s hand was dry and narrow inside Farrington’s broad fist.

“Fair now,” said Leonard. “Elbows steady. When I give the word. One—two—three!”

Farrington drove at once. He expected the younger man’s wrist to give, but it remained upright. His own shoulder pressed forward. The table creaked. Weathers looked aside with the faint stage smile still on his lips.

Farrington gathered himself and forced harder. A humming passed through his fist and up his arm. The faces around the table blurred. For a moment Weathers’s hand yielded an inch.

“Go on, Tom!”

“Have him!”

Then Weathers’s wrist turned. The pressure changed. Farrington felt his arm being lowered slowly, steadily, as if a weight had been hooked to it. He strained, showing his teeth. His elbow slipped.

The back of his hand struck the table.

There was laughter and a burst of applause.

“His elbow moved,” Farrington said.

“It did not,” Leonard answered.

“It slid. The table’s wet.”

“We’ll try again,” Weathers said pleasantly.

Farrington looked at him fixedly. His face was dark and swollen.

“Try now.”

They set their elbows again. This time Farrington gripped until the young man’s knuckles whitened.

“One—two—three!”

He held back at first, meaning to save his force. Weathers did the same. Their joined fists stood upright between them like a single ugly instrument. Then Farrington lunged.

Again the smaller arm did not give. Sweat broke under Farrington’s hair. The room had grown silent except for the piano beyond the partition and the short gasps coming from his own throat. He imagined Mr. Alleyne’s head, the woman’s pale eyes, the pawnbroker’s coins sliding away.

He pushed with all his strength.

Weathers’s smile disappeared. His lips narrowed. His arm moved suddenly, not violently but with exact control, and Farrington’s hand went down a second time.

The comedian whistled.

“Two straight!”

Weathers shook out his wrist and put on his coat.

“You’re a powerful man,” he said. “But you use yourself badly. It’s knack.”

“Knack be damned,” Farrington said. “You had your body against the table.”

“No.”

“You did.”

“Everyone saw it.”

The old actor raised both black eyebrows.

“It was a fair trial, my friend.”

Farrington stood. The snug seemed too small for him. He wished someone would touch him or laugh openly. He would smash a chair across Weathers’s neat head. But Higgins took his arm.

“Leave it, Tom. There’s no harm done.”

“No harm? I’m saying it wasn’t fair.”

“Another night you’ll beat him.”

Another night. They were all against him now, admiring that play-acting pup. Because he had a fancy pin and knew how to twist a wrist they thought him a great man.

Weathers called for drinks.

“What will you take, Farrington?”

The offer was worse than laughter.

“Nothing from you.”

“As you please.”

Farrington searched his pocket. Ninepence. He ordered a small whiskey for himself and paid. The last threepence remained in his palm, damp and warm.

When they left the house the fog had changed to a fine rain. Leonard and Higgins were going towards the south side. Weathers walked between them, talking of a rehearsal. Nobody pressed Farrington to accompany them.

“So long, Tom,” Higgins called.

The man stood beneath a lamp and watched them go. Their shoulders became shapeless in the rain.

He was still thirsty.

Threepence would buy a half-pint, but then there would be no tram fare. He could walk home. It was a long road, but the drink would warm him. He entered another public house and spent the threepence.

The porter tasted thin. He drank it slowly, trying to prolong the coldness. When the glass was empty, he tilted it and waited for the last brown thread to reach the rim.

Outside again, he walked heavily. The city had thinned. Shop shutters were down; lamps shone on wet rails; a cab passed with the horse’s head hanging low. At the bridge the wind came up from the black water and entered his coat. The warmth of the drink faded.

He put his hand to his waistcoat for the watch and touched only the paper ticket.

Five shillings gone. The watch gone. And what had he got for it? A few drinks for fellows who let him stand alone in the rain. Leonard, with his empty pockets. Higgins holding his arm as if he were a troublesome child. Weathers smiling, twisting, cheating. The woman laughing as she left.

And tomorrow Alleyne would begin again.

He saw himself entering the office without the contract complete. The little man would be waiting. Perhaps Crosbie would send for him. Perhaps there would be a week’s notice. His wife would ask about the watch. She had already warned him after the last time.

He walked faster. Anger moved inside him without direction, striking against his ribs. His right hand opened and closed.

At home the hall was dark. He pushed the door and stumbled over something on the floor.

“Who’s there?”

A child’s voice answered from the kitchen.

“It’s me, da.”

Farrington entered. The fire had sunk to a red hollow beneath grey ash. His youngest son, Willie, knelt on the tiles, trying to coax a flame from a twist of newspaper. The room was cold. Two cups stood unwashed on the table.

“Where’s your mother?”

“She went out, da.”

“Out where?”

“To the chapel.”

“What chapel?”

“She said she was going to the mission with Mrs. Byrne.”

Farrington stared at the fire.

“At this hour?”

The boy rose. He was thin, with fair hair that would not lie flat. One stocking had fallen about his ankle.

“She said she’d be back soon.”

“And the dinner?”

“It’s in the oven, da.”

Farrington opened the oven. A plate of potatoes and bacon stood inside, cold and filmed with grease.

“Is that what she left?”

“Yes, da.”

“Cold?”

“I tried to keep the fire, but it went down.”

“You tried.”

“Yes, da.”

The man turned. The boy’s eyes widened.

“What have you been doing all evening?”

“My lessons.”

“Lessons! And you let the fire die?”

“I put coal on it.”

“How much?”

“Two pieces.”

“Two pieces! Where’s the coal now?”

“There’s none in the box.”

“None?”

“Mammy said to tell you the coalman wouldn’t leave more till Saturday.”

The words seemed prepared against him. No money, no fire, no hot dinner. Even in his own house they had arranged a welcome of darkness and cold. His wife away praying, leaving him to eat grease from a plate. The child looking at him with those frightened, spying eyes.

“Why didn’t you go for coal?”

“I had no money, da.”

“You could have got a few sods on tick.”

“Mammy said not to.”

“Mammy said. Mammy said. Are you master here, or am I?”

“You are, da.”

“What?”

“You are.”

Farrington stepped towards him. The boy retreated against the dresser.

“And don’t answer me back.”

“I didn’t, da.”

“You did.”

“No, da.”

The man’s hand shot out and caught the boy by the shoulder. The small bone seemed to disappear beneath his fingers.

“You’re a liar as well.”

“I’m not, da! I’m not!”

Farrington shook him. A cup rattled on its hook. The boy’s face puckered, and the sound of his crying sharpened the man’s fury. That cry—thin, helpless, accusing. He had heard laughter all evening; now this whine met him at his own hearth.

“I’ll teach you to mind the fire.”

He looked around and saw a walking stick behind the door. It was a light ash plant his wife used to close the upper window. He released the boy and reached for it.

Willie sprang sideways.

“Don’t, da!”

“Come here.”

“I’ll light it now. I’ll get wood.”

“Come here when I tell you.”

The boy ran around the table. Farrington followed, knocking a chair aside. His movements were heavy, but the room was small. He caught the back of the boy’s shirt and dragged him close.

The first blow fell across the legs. Willie screamed.

“Stop that noise!”

Another blow. The stick made a flat sound against cloth and flesh.

“O da! Please, da!”

“You’ll mind me next time.”

“I will! I will!”

The child twisted, shielding his head with both arms. Farrington struck the arms, the shoulders, the thin back. Each blow loosened something inside him and yet left the thing there, hotter than before. Alleyne’s bald head, Weathers’s white wrist, the woman’s smile—all rose and vanished with the stick.

“Please, da! Don’t beat me!”

“You’ll let the fire out, will you?”

“No, da!”

“You’ll give me impudence?”

“No!”

The boy fell to his knees. Farrington seized his collar and lifted him halfway. His own breath came loudly. His fist trembled as it had trembled upon the public-house table.

Willie pressed his hands together. Tears and mucus shone on his face.

“O da, don’t! Don’t hit me again!”

The stick rose.

The child looked up at him in terror.

“I’ll say a prayer for you, da,” he cried. “I’ll say a Hail Mary for you. I’ll pray for you, da, if you don’t hit me.”

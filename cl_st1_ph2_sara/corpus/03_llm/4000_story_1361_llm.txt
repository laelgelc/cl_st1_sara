## COLONEL STARBOTTLE AND THE TESTIMONY OF PEPPERMINT

It was conceded, even by those citizens of Laurel Run who had suffered professionally, politically, or physically from Colonel Culpeper Starbottle’s peculiar methods, that his defense of the Eureka Ditch Company was a triumph. Whether this triumph belonged properly to his personality, his oratorical abilities, or his legal function was a question upon which local opinion divided; but, as the Colonel himself claimed all three departments, and had already challenged one gentleman who suggested a fourth, I prefer not to speak dogmatically.

The case itself was simple. The Eureka Ditch Company had diverted water from a creek which, according to the plaintiff, Providence, the statutes of California, and several indignant farmers had intended to flow elsewhere. There were maps, surveys, water measurements, engineering reports, and a deposition from a man who had known the creek intimately for seventeen years. The Colonel ignored them.

For three hours and forty minutes he addressed the jury upon the sacred right of American enterprise to pursue its manifest channel; upon the tyrannical hostility of monopolistic mud to the onward progress of civilization; upon Thomas Jefferson; upon the American eagle; and, finally, upon a hypothetical widow in Missouri whose youngest child, deprived of irrigation, was stretching its innocent hands toward the sunset and asking why the Constitution had forsaken it.

No widow in Missouri had been mentioned in evidence. Indeed, the Eureka Ditch Company’s operations lay several thousand miles from that State, and it was afterward objected that irrigation was not commonly required there in the manner suggested. Yet when the Colonel, loosening his broad Byron collar and extending one fat but faultlessly kept hand toward the jury, demanded whether they would “turn back the luminous and fertilizing tide of Anglo-Saxon advancement at the bidding of a diagram in red ink,” the foreman burst into tears. The jury returned a verdict for the company without leaving their seats.

Colonel Starbottle received the decision with that grave composure which distinguished him at moments when less remarkable men might have shown surprise. He bowed first to the court, then to opposing counsel, then to the jury, and lastly—though perhaps most profoundly—to himself as reflected in the dark window beyond the clerk’s desk. He buttoned his blue frock coat over a spotless shirt frill, settled his Panama hat upon his white hair, and left the courtroom with the slow, slightly rolling gait of one who had vindicated not merely a corporation, but a continent.

Outside, the crowd divided before him. Several admirers pressed forward. A gentleman from Red Dog requested the privilege of shaking the hand that had “knocked engineering galley-west.” The Colonel granted it. Another asked whether the Missouri widow was known personally to him.

“Sir,” returned the Colonel, with a slight recession of his chin into his collar, “there are moments in the life of an advocate when humanity itself becomes—er—personal.”

This answer, which appeared to satisfy everybody except the gentleman who had asked the question, was repeated that evening in three saloons.

The Colonel’s immediate destination was the office of the Eureka Ditch Company, where its president, Mr. Adoniram Hotchkiss, awaited him with a check. Mr. Hotchkiss was a tall, narrow, dry man, whose black clothes seemed less worn than admonitory, and whose face suggested that he had detected pleasure in the human race and intended to put a stop to it. He was the superintendent of the First Reformed Temperance Sabbath School, the author of a pamphlet against dancing, and the only man in Laurel Run known to have refused brandied peaches on theological grounds.

“You won,” said Hotchkiss.

“My dear sir,” replied the Colonel, expanding gently, “the principles of constitutional liberty won. I was merely their instrument.”

Hotchkiss looked at the amount written upon the check.

“A costly instrument.”

The Colonel took it without resentment. He held the paper at arm’s length, as if verifying not the sum but the penmanship, folded it, and placed it in a morocco pocketbook.

“Mr. Hotchkiss,” he said, “genius has in all ages suffered from the inability of commercial persons to distinguish between price and value. I accept this compensation—not for myself, sir, but for that dignity of the profession which, in my person, has perhaps to-day been—er—conspicuously maintained.”

“You talked about a widow who didn’t exist.”

The Colonel drew himself up.

“Exist, sir? She existed in the moral apprehension of twelve sovereign citizens.”

Hotchkiss closed the company ledger. “I don’t pay for moral widows twice. Good day.”

The Colonel bowed with punctilious coldness and withdrew. It may be observed that he did not at once cash the check. He first exhibited it at the Union Hotel bar, where, declining to drink on account of the fatigue of his exertions, he took only a small restorative of whiskey. He then passed to his office.

This apartment occupied the second floor above a harness shop and was approached by a stairway steep enough to discourage ordinary litigation. Upon its frosted glass door appeared the words:

**CULPEPER STARBOTTLE  
ATTORNEY AND COUNSELOR AT LAW  
AND COMMISSIONER FOR KENTUCKY**

The last office had produced neither business nor emolument in seven years, but the Colonel retained it from a belief that Kentucky would eventually remember him.

Within were two chairs, a desk, a sofa with a broken spring, a rack of canes, and certain objects belonging less to jurisprudence than to history. A gold watch with a bullet mark lay beneath a glass shade. Beside it was a silver-mounted pistol, unloaded by order of the fire commissioners, and a card bearing the name of Major Evander Chubb, deceased. Over the fireplace hung a portrait of the Colonel at thirty-two, painted at New Orleans, in which his waist was narrower, his hair darker, and his confidence—if possible—more radiant. A whiskey bottle stood behind a volume of California statutes. I mention these arrangements because they were not accidental. The Colonel’s office was not merely the place where he conducted his affairs; it was the place where his affairs had been composed for public inspection.

He had scarcely seated himself and removed his boots from the immediate tyranny of walking when there was a knock.

“Enter,” he said, in the mellow voice with which he had lately summoned the American eagle.

Two women came in.

The elder was stout, sun-browned, and dressed in black. She carried an umbrella, although the day was cloudless, and approached the Colonel with the practical air of one who had come to buy a hinge. The younger lingered behind her.

“We’re lookin’ for Lawyer Starbottle,” said the elder.

The Colonel rose. “Madam, you behold him.”

She looked him over, from the snowy crest of his hair to the varnished toes of his boots.

“You the one that made that speech?”

“I had, madam, the honor of addressing the court in the matter of the Eureka Ditch Company.”

“Zaidee,” said the woman, turning, “that’s him.”

The young woman came forward.

She was perhaps nineteen, perhaps twenty-two; rustic estimation in such matters is notoriously charitable, while legal estimation is inclined to be exact only when damages depend upon it. She wore a plain gray gown, blue ribbons at her throat, and a little straw bonnet beneath which her fair hair showed with an effect of deliberate innocence. Her figure was slight and almost childish. She carried a green parasol, which she kept closed and employed in tracing an invisible boundary on the carpet.

But her eyes contradicted the rest of her. They were dark, steady, and possessed of a curious, unhurried penetration. They did not challenge the Colonel; they seemed already to have heard his answer. What precisely he discovered in them I am not sure. He afterward called it “the mute appeal of betrayed womanhood.” At the moment, however, he buttoned his coat.

“This is my darter, Zaidee Hooker,” said the elder woman. “I’m Mis’ Hooker. We come over from Angel’s Crossing.”

The Colonel placed his best chair for Mrs. Hooker and the second-best for Zaidee, leaving himself the sofa. The broken spring received him with an intimate sound, and he rose again.

“Madam,” he said, standing, “any service which my professional abilities—or my personal devotion to the sex whose purity hallows our existence—may render, is already yours.”

“We want damages,” said Mrs. Hooker.

The Colonel paused.

There are words which, though innocent in themselves, possess an unfortunate power of stripping ceremony from a situation. “Damages” was such a word. It reduced the Colonel’s temple of feminine purity to a collection office.

“Damages,” he repeated slowly. “For—er—injury to property? Trespass? Depredation by stock?”

“Breach o’ promise.”

The Colonel’s expression changed. He had never approved of breach-of-promise suits. He considered them vulgar, northern, and hostile to the delicacy of women. His own code held that a gentleman who trifled with a lady should marry her, leave the country, or meet a male relative at sunrise. The interposition of a sheriff and a schedule of pecuniary compensation offended him.

“My dear madam,” he began, “the sacred relations between the sexes—”

“Zaidee was promised,” said Mrs. Hooker, “and then he quit. I says she ought to ketch him and make him pay.”

“Who,” asked the Colonel, “is the—er—gentleman?”

“Adoniram Hotchkiss.”

The Colonel’s hand, which had been resting in the breast of his coat, emerged slowly. His face became judicial.

“Mr. Hotchkiss,” he said.

“That’s him.”

“President of the Eureka Ditch Company?”

“That’s him.”

“The gentleman whose cause I have, within the last hour, carried to a successful termination?”

Mrs. Hooker nodded. “We heerd you carry it. That’s why we come.”

The Colonel turned toward Zaidee. She was looking at him beneath lowered lashes, though not so low that he failed to see the intelligence behind them.

“My dear young lady,” he said more gently, “this is a grave accusation. Mr. Hotchkiss is—whatever his limitations—an individual of established public position, mature years, and, I may say, a somewhat conspicuous austerity.”

“He’s fifty-four,” said Zaidee.

“Precisely. A somewhat conspicuous austerity of fifty-four. You understand that a proceeding of this nature requires proof?”

“That’s what maw said.”

“Documentary evidence, for example. Letters.”

“Nary letter.”

“Notes?”

“No.”

“Written declaration of affection?”

“No.”

The Colonel glanced at Mrs. Hooker.

“She burned ’em,” said that lady confidently.

Zaidee looked up. “There wasn’t none to burn.”

Mrs. Hooker accepted this correction without embarrassment.

“Then why’d you tell me there was words?” she demanded.

“There was words. They wasn’t wrote by him.”

The Colonel drew his chair closer and sat carefully upon its forward edge.

“Let us proceed with order. Mr. Hotchkiss made use of words not written by himself?”

“In hymn-books,” said Zaidee.

The Colonel waited.

“And prayer books,” she added.

“Would you be good enough, Miss Hooker, to explain?”

Zaidee put both hands upon the handle of her parasol.

“It began at Wednesday meetin’. He set two benches front of me. When we come to the hymn, he turned around like he was lookin’ for the number, and he looked straight at me. Then he set his finger on a word.”

“What word?”

“‘Love.’”

The Colonel coughed.

“It might be urged,” he said, “that the word occurs with some frequency in religious compositions.”

“He marked ‘thee’ next.”

“In the same hymn?”

“No. Week after.”

The Colonel became attentive in spite of himself.

“And after that?” he asked.

“‘Forever.’ Then ‘mine.’ Then ‘bride.’”

“Always,” said the Colonel, “in hymns?”

“Sometimes the prayer book. Once in a tract against card-playin’.”

Mrs. Hooker leaned forward. “He’s artful.”

The Colonel ignored her. “Did he accompany these indications with any spoken communication?”

“He’d cough.”

“Cough?”

“Twice for yes, once for no.”

“Did you and he arrange that code?”

“No.”

“Then, my dear young lady, how could you be certain of its significance?”

Zaidee regarded him with those composed, disconcerting eyes.

“I coughed back.”

The Colonel shifted his position.

“Ah,” he said. “Mutual interpretation.”

“That’s what maw said.”

Mrs. Hooker had not said it, but she nodded as if she might have done so under favorable circumstances.

The Colonel rose and paced before the fireplace. His thumbs entered the armholes of his waistcoat. He was already exhibiting the first symptoms of an argument.

“Permit me to understand the alleged progression. Mr. Hotchkiss directs your attention, through a series of devotional publications, to certain isolated expressions—love, thee, forever, mine, bride. He then confirms or modifies their application by—er—bronchial signals.”

“He whistled, too,” said Zaidee.

The Colonel stopped.

“Whistled?”

“Like a meadowlark.”

“In church?”

“Outside.”

“Ah! Outside. That distinction may become important.”

Mrs. Hooker sniffed. “It was courtin’, wherever he done it.”

“Madam,” replied the Colonel, “the law, unlike the affections, is greatly influenced by locality. Continue, Miss Hooker.”

Zaidee traced a circle on the carpet with her parasol.

“He’d pass our place Tuesdays and Fridays on his bay mare. If I was by the window, he’d whistle. If I come to the gate, he’d take off his hat. If maw was there, he’d scratch his ear instead.”

“Why?”

“Maw was agin him.”

“I mistrusted his whiskers,” explained Mrs. Hooker.

Hotchkiss wore two narrow strips of iron-gray beard descending from his ears to the corners of his mouth. They gave him the appearance of having placed his face in brackets.

“Was there,” asked the Colonel, “any gift? Any article transferred from his possession to yours under circumstances suggestive of attachment?”

Zaidee hesitated.

The Colonel saw it. His manner softened; his voice dropped from forensic resonance to paternal persuasion.

“My child, concealment can serve only the wrongdoer. Speak without fear. In this room, at least, you are protected by the usages of advocacy and the honor of Culpeper Starbottle.”

“He give me a peppermint.”

The Colonel’s brows rose.

“A—what?”

“A peppermint lozenger. Stamped.”

Mrs. Hooker added triumphantly, “With words onto it.”

“What words?”

Zaidee’s parasol ceased moving.

“‘I love you.’”

There was a pause. The Colonel’s professional imagination, which had survived without nourishment through hymn-books, coughs, and meadowlarks, fixed itself gratefully upon the peppermint.

“Where,” he asked, “is that object?”

“I ate it.”

The broken spring in the sofa gave a faint report as the Colonel resumed his seat.

“You—consumed the evidence?”

“I didn’t know it was evidence then.”

Mrs. Hooker shook her head. “I told her she hadn’t no business eatin’ sentiments.”

The Colonel passed his hand over his face. Before him lay a case with no letters, no witnesses, no direct statement, and one material exhibit digested beyond subpoena. Yet the young woman’s eyes remained upon him, neither pleading nor ashamed. If she was foolish, she possessed an embarrassing confidence in her foolishness. If she was artful—and some small voice, less romantic than the Colonel’s other voices, suggested the possibility—her art had chosen him as its instrument with notable intelligence.

“Did Mr. Hotchkiss ever say,” he asked at last, “‘I will marry you’?”

“Not right out.”

“Did he ask you to become his wife?”

“He asked if I’d be ready when he was.”

“Where?”

“By the elder bushes.”

“Were you alone?”

“Yes.”

“What did you answer?”

“I said I reckoned.”

“Did he specify what you were to be ready for?”

Zaidee looked surprised. “Marriage.”

“Did he say marriage?”

“No.”

“Then pardon me—how did you know?”

“He had his Sunday coat on.”

The Colonel rose again, this time with some violence.

“Miss Hooker,” he said, “the law is not blind to the natural and reasonable interpretations of feminine perception. I would be the last man—sir, the last man—to deny that a lady may gather from tone, gesture, costume, propinquity, and the mute but eloquent avowal of the eye, a pledge which the craven tongue withholds. But courts—our courts, as presently constituted—demand a certain brutal explicitness.”

Zaidee’s eyes brightened.

“You could make ’em see it.”

The Colonel stopped pacing.

“My abilities,” he said modestly, “have occasionally enabled twelve citizens to perceive truths obscured by the mechanical restrictions of testimony.”

“We heerd you,” she said. “You made that ditch sound like the whole country.”

Mrs. Hooker added, “Folks say there wasn’t much law in it.”

The Colonel waived the latter tribute and retained the former.

Zaidee leaned forward. For the first time her monotonous recital gave way to animation.

“You could tell it—the meetin’ nights, and him turnin’ the pages slow, and the words comin’ one after another. You could tell how he rode past and whistled, and how I waited by the gate. You could make ’em hear it. You could make ’em know what he meant.”

The Colonel looked at her.

There are flatteries addressed to vanity and flatteries addressed to identity. The first delight us; the second enlist us. Zaidee had not merely praised the Colonel’s eloquence. She had proposed that his eloquence was a superior form of evidence—that reality, incomplete and commonplace in itself, awaited the touch of Culpeper Starbottle to become intelligible. No doctrine could have been more congenial to him.

Nevertheless, he made a final effort toward prudence.

“You say he abandoned the understanding abruptly?”

“He just quit.”

“When?”

“Three weeks ago.”

“No quarrel?”

“No.”

“No explanation?”

“No.”

“No later communication?”

“He changed his pew.”

The simplicity of this desertion affected the Colonel. To change one’s pew after organizing a romance among the hymns seemed not only heartless, but profane.

“And what,” he asked, “do you desire now? Marriage?”

Mrs. Hooker said, “Damages.”

The Colonel addressed Zaidee. “I ask the injured party.”

Zaidee lowered her face and returned to the carpet with her parasol.

“I wouldn’t marry him now.”

“Quite right,” said Mrs. Hooker.

“But,” Zaidee continued, “I want him made to know he can’t do so.”

“Damages,” repeated Mrs. Hooker.

The Colonel walked to the window and looked down upon the street. Hotchkiss’s office was visible across the way, its blind drawn halfway against the afternoon glare. The president of the Eureka Ditch Company was at that moment probably entering the verdict in his ledger and subtracting the Colonel’s fee as an expense of moral enterprise.

“I have,” said Starbottle slowly, “certain professional relations with Mr. Hotchkiss.”

“We waited till his ditch case was done,” said Zaidee.

The Colonel turned. The remark was quiet, almost innocent; but it showed that she had considered the point. Again he had the sensation that her intelligence preceded his own and courteously left footprints for him.

“I could not,” he continued, “appear against a client in the same matter in which I represented him.”

“This ain’t water,” said Mrs. Hooker. “It’s Zaidee.”

“Legally, madam, the distinction is perhaps sufficient. Professionally, however, there remains an affair of some delicacy.”

“We ain’t askin’ you to be delicate,” said Mrs. Hooker. “We’re askin’ you to git damages.”

The Colonel’s face reddened. Then Zaidee looked up.

“Maw means,” she said, “we come because there ain’t nobody else could do it.”

That decided nothing, of course. But ten minutes later, after the women had gone, the Colonel was standing before his New Orleans portrait, buttoning his frock coat and telling the younger man in the frame that certain duties could not be measured by professional convenience.

He crossed the street to Hotchkiss’s office.

I should explain that Colonel Starbottle had not yet accepted the case. He was merely going, as he afterward stated, “to explore the possibility of sparing all parties the painful publicity of litigation.” Yet he carried his sword-stick. He generally carried it when sparing people publicity.

Hotchkiss sat behind a high desk beneath framed texts warning against wine, vanity, and the wages of sin. A glass jar of peppermints stood upon a shelf near his elbow.

The Colonel saw the jar at once.

Hotchkiss saw him seeing it and frowned.

“What do you want now?”

The Colonel closed the door with care, placed his hat upon a chair, and stood erect before the desk.

“Mr. Hotchkiss, I come upon a matter which, touching as it does the honor of a young and defenseless female, the sanctity of the domestic relation, and your own public standing, demands the utmost—er—circumspection.”

“If it’s another bill, I won’t pay it.”

“It is not a bill.”

“What is it?”

“The name of Miss Zaidee Hooker may not be wholly unfamiliar to you.”

The effect was small but distinct. Hotchkiss’s pen stopped. A bead of ink widened upon the ledger.

“What about her?”

The Colonel noted the absence of denial and naturally considered it evidence.

“I have this afternoon been consulted,” he continued, “by that young lady and her estimable mother concerning representations alleged to have been made by you—representations of an affectionate, matrimonial, and binding character.”

Hotchkiss stared.

“Binding?”

“In the legal sense, sir.”

“Zaidee Hooker says I promised to marry her?”

“She does.”

“That’s a lie.”

The Colonel’s hand moved toward his shirt frill. “Mr. Hotchkiss, I must request that in referring to a lady in my presence you select language compatible with your continued personal security.”

“I said it’s a lie, and I’ll say it again.”

“Then,” replied the Colonel, very softly, “you shall answer for it.”

Hotchkiss pushed back his chair and stood.

“I’ll answer in court. I never courted that girl. I never offered marriage. I never touched her hand.”

“The absence of physical display,” said the Colonel, “may credit her modesty rather than your innocence.”

“I never wrote to her.”

“She does not claim a letter.”

“I never gave her anything.”

The Colonel looked at the jar.

Hotchkiss followed his glance. “Those are for bad breath.”

“Ha!”

“What do you mean, ‘ha’?”

“I mean nothing, sir—nothing beyond the natural exclamation of a professional mind upon discovering material corroboration.”

“Corroboration of what?”

“A peppermint lozenge bearing the legend, ‘I love you.’”

Hotchkiss’s face, usually the color of old paper, became gray.

“She told you that?”

“She did.”

“And she’s got it?”

The Colonel smiled.

“Your anxiety regarding the preservation of the article is significant.”

“I’m not anxious. Has she got it?”

“No, sir.”

Hotchkiss exhaled sharply.

“She ate it,” said the Colonel.

For the first time the president’s face showed something almost like relief. He recovered immediately.

“Then there’s no proof.”

“Proof, Mr. Hotchkiss, is not always a portable commodity. It may perish in the body while surviving in the conscience.”

“That kind of talk may fool a jury. It doesn’t fool me.”

“My purpose is not to fool you, sir, but to afford you an opportunity to make honorable reparation without dragging a sensitive young lady through the ordeal of public examination.”

“Reparation! This is blackmail.”

The Colonel’s sword-stick struck the floor.

“You have now insulted two ladies.”

“I have named their business.”

“You have named what your own apprehension suggests. I named only an honorable settlement.”

“How much?”

This abrupt question might have warned a less imaginative advocate. To the Colonel, it was confession.

“I have not been authorized to state a sum.”

“You’re not their lawyer?”

“Not yet.”

Hotchkiss pointed toward the door. “Then get out.”

The Colonel did not move.

“Before complying with that hospitable suggestion,” he said, “I shall ask you one question. Did you, during divine service, direct Miss Hooker’s attention to certain words in hymns and other religious publications?”

“No.”

“Did you signal to her by coughing?”

“I have catarrh.”

“Did you whistle in passing her residence?”

“I whistle to my mare.”

“Like a meadowlark?”

Hotchkiss hesitated. “I don’t know what it sounds like.”

“Did you give her a peppermint?”

“I’ve given peppermints to half the congregation.”

“Bearing amorous inscriptions?”

“I don’t stamp them!”

“Yet you distribute them.”

“They’re mixed in the jar.”

“Providence, then, selected the message?”

Hotchkiss struck the desk with his palm.

“This is lunacy! I treated that girl with Christian kindness. She came to the meetings. She asked questions. I lent her books.”

“What books?”

“Religious books.”

“Hymn-books?”

“Yes—but not—”

“Prayer books?”

“Maybe.”

“Tracts?”

“Yes!”

The Colonel bowed. “Thank you.”

“For what?”

“For completing the chain.”

“There is no chain.”

“There is a chain, sir, forged link by link in the sacred precincts you desecrated—love, thee, forever, mine, bride!”

Hotchkiss came around the desk. “You’ve got no case, and you know it. Not a letter. Not a witness. Not a word I ever spoke. If you bring this into court, I’ll expose the whole fraud.”

The Colonel’s florid face became calm.

“Mr. Hotchkiss, I have carried causes with less evidence.”

“I believe you.”

The insult was slight in construction but comprehensive in effect.

The Colonel drew himself up. His right hand closed around the head of his cane.

“In another community,” he said, “and under usages more consonant with the traditions in which I was reared, your observation would receive an immediate and final reply. Unfortunately, the commercial timidity of this State has surrounded personal satisfaction with technical impediments.”

“I’m not going to duel you.”

“I have not invited you, sir.”

“You came in here with that sticker and tried to scare me.”

The Colonel’s nostrils dilated. “This cane?”

“I know what’s in it.”

“With your customary moral penetration, you are mistaken.”

Hotchkiss suddenly seized the cane and pulled. Three inches of polished steel flashed between them.

There followed a silence.

The Colonel looked down at the exposed blade with pained surprise, as though the weapon had betrayed a confidence.

Hotchkiss released it. “Get out.”

Colonel Starbottle replaced the sword with dignity.

“Mr. Hotchkiss,” he said, taking his hat, “I shall not permit the accidental mechanism of a walking appliance to distract us from the issue. You have refused honorable accommodation. You have charged two respectable women with crime. You have questioned my professional integrity and laid a violent hand upon my person. From this moment I regard the matter as committed to the enlightened judgment of a California jury—and, subject to the restrictions you have so prudently invoked, to my own.”

“Bring your suit,” said Hotchkiss. “I’ll break you.”

The Colonel bowed. “Others have made that prediction. Their monuments, sir, are distributed through several Southern States.”

He departed, leaving Hotchkiss beside the desk, the jar of peppermints, and the spreading stain upon the ledger.

By the time the Colonel reached the street, he had accepted the case.

His first act was to return to his office and draw the blind. His second was to take a restorative from behind the statutes. His third was to remove his coat, loosen his collar, and dictate a complaint to himself in a voice which shook the harness hanging in the shop below.

He alleged that Adoniram Hotchkiss, “moved and instigated by a false, fraudulent, malicious, and matrimonially evasive purpose,” had sought and obtained the confidence of Zaidee Hooker; that he had employed signs, tokens, sacred literature, confectionery devices, avian imitations, and other instruments too numerous to mention; that in consequence Miss Hooker had suffered anguish of mind, loss of social expectation, impairment of matrimonial opportunity, and general damage in the sum of twenty-five thousand dollars.

The figure came to him naturally. He had no authority for it, but believed that large principles required large numbers.

At six o’clock he sent a note to Mrs. Hooker requesting that she and her daughter return the following morning. The boy who carried it read the outside, saw the Colonel’s hand, and concluded that it was a challenge. Within half an hour the Union Hotel bar knew that Starbottle had demanded satisfaction from “a party at Angel’s Crossing.” By seven, the party had become three brothers. By eight, it was reported that the Colonel would meet the entire Hooker family at sunrise with dueling pistols.

The Colonel, meanwhile, dined alone on steak and onions, entirely unaware that the town had arranged his death.

Mrs. Hooker and Zaidee arrived at nine the next morning. A crowd of twelve men, having followed them from the stage office, waited below. When no pistol shot occurred within ten minutes, two went upstairs on the pretense of needing legal advice and were dismissed by the Colonel for having no actionable grievance.

Mrs. Hooker read the complaint, moving her lips over the longer words.

“Twenty-five thousand,” she said at last. “You reckon we kin git it?”

“Madam, the sum is intended to express, in terms necessarily inadequate, the injury sustained.”

“You said yesterday the proof was poor.”

“The interview which I have since had with Mr. Hotchkiss,” replied the Colonel, “has altered the moral complexion of the case.”

“He owned up?” asked Zaidee.

“He denied everything.”

Mrs. Hooker looked disappointed.

“But,” continued the Colonel, “he denied it with a degree of agitation incompatible with innocence.”

Zaidee’s eyes rested on him. “You made him mad.”

“He forgot himself,” said the Colonel.

“You made him,” she repeated.

The admiration in her voice was unmistakable. The Colonel turned toward the window and adjusted his collar. He had been admired by juries, saloon keepers, political committees, and, on one memorable occasion, a militia company; but this quiet certainty from a young woman whose intelligence had already unsettled him possessed another quality. It was not precisely affection, and he was too honorable to call it so. It was rather recognition—the homage of instinctive genius to trained power.

Mrs. Hooker tapped the complaint.

“How much do you git?”

The Colonel came back to earth.

“My professional compensation may be arranged upon the contingent principle. One-third of any recovery, together with necessary costs.”

“Too much.”

“Madam!”

“You don’t pay nothin’ if we lose.”

“I contribute my time, my reputation, my abilities, and perhaps”—he looked solemnly toward his sword-stick—“my personal safety.”

“Two bits for filin’, ain’t it?”

The Colonel looked at Zaidee, but Zaidee had readdressed herself to the carpet.

After a negotiation which lowered the majesty of the law without appreciably lowering its fee, Mrs. Hooker agreed. Zaidee signed the complaint. Her handwriting was unexpectedly neat and firm.

As she gave the pen back, the Colonel said, “Miss Hooker, I must impress upon you the solemn obligation of absolute candor. If there is any circumstance—any act, conversation, prior attachment, innocent indiscretion, or fact capable of hostile distortion—you must tell me now.”

Zaidee considered.

“There ain’t.”

“No other suitor?”

“No.”

“No one to whom you repeated these alleged communications?”

“No.”

“No witness of Mr. Hotchkiss’s conduct?”

“Nary one.”

The Colonel sighed. “You have at least been consistent in withholding assistance from your counsel.”

She smiled faintly.

“You don’t need much.”

The Colonel’s sigh ended in a cough.

At ten-thirty he filed the action.

The clerk read the title twice.

**ZAIDEE HOOKER, PLAINTIFF,  
v.  
ADONIRAM HOTCHKISS, DEFENDANT.**

“Breach of promise?” he asked.

“Aggravated breach,” said the Colonel.

The clerk glanced through the pleading. “What’s an ‘avian imitation employed with seductive intent’?”

“A meadowlark,” said the Colonel coldly. “The technical phrase is preferable.”

The sheriff received the summons at eleven. At half past eleven, having no wish to walk to the Eureka office in the heat, he handed it to his deputy. The deputy, who owed Hotchkiss money, gave it to a constable. The constable found the president addressing a temperance committee in the Methodist basement and served him between a resolution against lager beer and a motion concerning cider.

Hotchkiss read the paper in silence. Then he looked up at the assembled committee.

“This,” he said, holding it aloft, “is an attack upon Christian character, organized by wickedness and defended by rhetoric.”

As several members had heard the Colonel’s ditch speech and admired it, this statement did not produce the unity he expected.

By noon, Laurel Run knew.

It knew more than had happened, less than was true, and exactly as much as it desired. The story passed from the courthouse to the saloons, from the saloons to the stores, from the stores to the church sewing circle, acquiring at each transfer the moral complexion of its surroundings. At the Union Hotel it was believed that Hotchkiss had maintained a secret harem through coded hymnals. At the temperance rooms, Zaidee was described as an adventuress trained from infancy to compromise religious men. At the barbershop, the peppermint had become a diamond brooch; at the drugstore, it became poison. The meadowlark survived every version.

Public opinion at first favored Zaidee because she was young, pretty, and had asked twenty-five thousand dollars. It shifted briefly toward Hotchkiss when he announced that he would not pay a cent. It shifted back when Colonel Starbottle declared in the courthouse corridor that “no treasury, however fortified by corporate greed, could purchase immunity from outraged womanhood.” By evening, three boys had been arrested for whistling like meadowlarks beneath the Sabbath school window.

The Colonel’s position became the subject of particular interest. Those who had yesterday praised his defense of the Eureka Ditch Company now praised the independence with which he attacked its president. Those who had condemned his defense praised his late discovery that Hotchkiss was a villain. Nobody was troubled by consistency; Laurel Run respected vigor more.

Hotchkiss retained Mr. Peabody, a dry and competent attorney from Sacramento, and filed an answer denying every allegation in language so plain that the town found it disappointing. The Colonel read the answer at his desk while Zaidee sat opposite him.

“Observe,” he said, striking the page, “the cowardly refuge of monosyllabic negation. ‘Denies that he courted.’ ‘Denies that he promised.’ ‘Denies that he whistled.’ Not one generous phrase—not one flash of human feeling!”

Zaidee turned her parasol between her fingers.

“Kin he win?”

“My dear young lady, cases are not won by denials.”

“What are they won by?”

The Colonel started to say “evidence,” reflected, and answered, “The intelligent comprehension of motive.”

“You mean tellin’ it right.”

“Precisely.”

She nodded, satisfied.

In the days that followed, the Colonel devoted himself to preparation with a fervor not always observed in his better-supported causes. He visited Angel’s Crossing, inspected the Hooker cottage, measured the distance from the road to the gate, stood beneath the elder bushes, and listened to three meadowlarks, none of which, in his opinion, possessed Hotchkiss’s probable tone. He attended Wednesday meeting and examined the hymnals. He found “love” on eighty-seven pages, “thee” on one hundred and twelve, “forever” on nineteen, “mine” on eight, and “bride” on two.

This abundance disturbed him briefly. He recovered upon observing that the words, when arranged in Zaidee’s order, formed an unmistakable declaration.

Mrs. Hooker followed him through the church.

“Kin you make anything out of it?” she asked.

“Madam,” he replied, closing the hymnal, “I can make the language of sacred song reveal the very footsteps of duplicity.”

“Will it git damages?”

“It will assist.”

Zaidee waited outside beneath a pepper tree. The blue ribbons at her throat stirred in the warm wind. When the Colonel approached, she did not ask what he had found.

“You stood where he stood?” she said.

“I did.”

“And you seen where I was?”

“I did.”

“Then you know.”

The Colonel looked from her to the church window. Framed there in his imagination was the severe profile of Adoniram Hotchkiss; behind it, the printed hymn; beyond it, the waiting face of the girl. The arrangement was theatrical, improbable, and touching. He believed in it immediately.

“My child,” he said, “I know that the heart may employ humble instruments when fear restrains the tongue. A word beneath a finger, a note of a bird, a trivial confection—these may become eloquent when addressed to innocence.”

Zaidee studied him.

“You’ll say that to the jury?”

“With greater fullness.”

A smile moved over her face and vanished.

On the return journey, the Colonel composed his opening speech aloud. He began with the earliest use of song in human courtship, passed through chivalry, psalmody, and the religious liberties of the republic, and arrived at peppermint as “the crystallized and saccharine repository of a pledge.” The stage driver, after listening for nine miles, later declared that he would have awarded the girl thirty thousand himself.

Hotchkiss did not share this susceptibility. He avoided the Colonel in public and denounced him in private. Yet he also purchased every jar of stamped peppermint in Laurel Run and had them destroyed behind the company office. This act, intended to prevent further fraud, became known within the hour and was regarded as suppression of evidence.

Then he changed his tactics. Through Mr. Peabody, he offered five hundred dollars “to terminate vexatious proceedings without admission.”

Mrs. Hooker wanted to accept.

“Five hundred’s money,” she said.

“It is also,” replied the Colonel, “an insult disguised as arithmetic.”

Zaidee sat quietly beside the desk.

“What do you think?” the Colonel asked her.

She raised her eyes.

“You said twenty-five thousand.”

“I did.”

“You said you could make him pay.”

“I did.”

“Then I reckon five hundred ain’t enough.”

The Colonel turned to Mrs. Hooker.

“Madam, the plaintiff rejects the proposition.”

“Mebbe the plaintiff don’t buy flour,” said Mrs. Hooker.

But Zaidee’s gaze remained on the Colonel, and he felt again that curious mingling of trust and command. She believed in his power with such completeness that any retreat would be not caution but betrayal. He tore the offer in two.

It was at this point, perhaps, that the case ceased to be hers and became his.

The trial was still weeks away, but the Colonel already lived within its future triumph. In the saloon he tested phrases upon listeners. In his office he arranged hymnals in chronological order. He ordered a large diagram of the church, showing Zaidee’s pew, Hotchkiss’s pew, the pulpit, and the probable trajectory of the defendant’s glances. He subpoenaed the keeper of the candy store, the stage driver, two deacons, Mrs. Hooker, and a naturalist reputed to distinguish the call of the western meadowlark.

The naturalist, when informed of his purpose, said that men could not reproduce the call exactly.

“Sir,” replied the Colonel, “the imperfections of imitation may themselves identify the imitator.”

At night he sat beneath the portrait of his younger self, the complaint open before him, the gold watch and silver pistol glimmering under the lamp. His own past—its duels, speeches, campaigns, loves, and generous revisions—seemed to gather around the new cause. He had once inhabited a world in which a lady’s dropped handkerchief could alter destinies, in which a look was a compact, an insult a summons, and honor more tangible than property. That world had become antique in the republic. Railroads, corporations, temperance committees, civil pleadings, and men like Hotchkiss had replaced it with ledgers and denials.

Yet here was Zaidee Hooker, making a romance from words touched in a hymn-book; and here was Culpeper Starbottle, perhaps the last man in California willing to insist that such invisible things had legal weight.

Whether Zaidee had truly understood Hotchkiss, whether Hotchkiss had trifled with her, whether both had participated in some timid half-courtship which neither could now confess, I cannot say. The facts remained obstinately small. But the Colonel’s faith was large, and in Laurel Run faith of that dimensions was itself a public event.

One evening, as sunset burned red along the courthouse windows, Zaidee came alone to his office.

The Colonel was practicing his address. His coat lay across a chair, his collar was open, and one hand was extended toward an imaginary jury.

“—shall this defendant,” he was saying, “after invading the sacred citadel of a maiden’s trust beneath the very banner of religion, retire behind the miserable earthworks of technical denial—”

He saw her and stopped.

“My dear Miss Hooker! You find me engaged in—er—preliminary arrangement.”

“I heerd you from downstairs.”

The Colonel put on his coat.

“Your mother is well?”

“She’s mad about the five hundred.”

“Natural maternal prudence. History abounds in such temporary failures of vision.”

Zaidee moved to the desk and touched one of the hymnals.

“You believe me, don’t you?”

The question was direct. The Colonel’s expression lost, for a moment, its professional fullness.

“My child,” he said, “I have staked my reputation upon your truth.”

“That ain’t what I asked.”

He looked at her. The room seemed suddenly smaller, stripped of audience and ceremony. Even the painted Colonel over the fireplace appeared to wait.

“I believe,” he said slowly, “that Mr. Hotchkiss permitted you to believe yourself beloved; that he awakened expectations which an honorable man should either fulfill or answer for; and that, whatever cowardly reservation he may now shelter behind, he is responsible for the wound he inflicted.”

Zaidee considered this elaborate reply.

“You believe me,” she said.

The Colonel bowed.

She turned toward the door, then paused.

“You’ll tell it good?”

“My dear young lady,” he replied, and his hand rose once more to his breast, “I shall tell it truly.”

She went downstairs.

The Colonel remained by the desk. After a little while he crossed to the window. In the street below, Zaidee opened her green parasol and walked toward the stage office, slight, demure, and self-possessed. Two men turned to look after her. Across the way, behind the blind of the Eureka office, a shadow moved and disappeared.

The Colonel drew himself upright.

Then, alone in the fading light, with no jury before him and no applause behind, he began again:

“Gentlemen—there are promises written in ink, promises uttered before witnesses, promises sealed by ring and bond; but there are others, no less sacred, which timid affection entrusts to a glance, a song, the call of a bird, or the perishable sweetness of a child’s confection—”

The harness maker below ceased hammering to listen. A passerby stopped beneath the window. Another joined him. Within five minutes a small crowd had gathered in the street, though the trial was weeks away and the evidence, strictly speaking, had not improved.

But Colonel Starbottle’s case had begun.

## THE ASCENT OF HORACE NIMMS

For twenty-one years Horace Nimms had occupied the cashier’s cage of the Amalgamated Soap Corporation, on the ninth floor of the Pemberton Building, lower Manhattan, and during that period no dollar entrusted to him had escaped without leaving its name, destination, and exact time of departure.

Horace was forty-seven, five feet four and three-eighths inches in his stocking feet, and weighed one hundred and twenty-six pounds before breakfast. His brown hair had retreated from bookkeeping but had not yet abandoned the ledger entirely. He wore sleeve garters, a high collar softened by washing, and black shoes which cost six dollars in 1919 and had since received three soles, two sets of heels, and more personal attention than many marriages.

Cashiers are commonly suspected of dryness. This is unjust. Put any man for two decades behind brass bars with thirty-eight thousand dollars a week passing under his nose and presently he will develop either imagination or a criminal record. Horace developed imagination.

His cage was his principality. There stood the adding machine, black and nickel-plated, with its upright handle; the red cashbook; the green cashbook; the petty-cash drawer; the drawer containing receipts; the drawer containing luncheon; and a small, square mirror in which Horace examined himself before the semiannual visit of Mr. Oren Hammer, president of Amalgamated Soap.

At nine each morning he entered the cage, removed his coat, arranged his pencils by length, and became cheerful.

Figures did that to him.

Outside the cage there were rents, dentists, coal bills, rheumatic uncles, and the stubborn fact that a cottage at Babylon, Long Island, with two bedrooms, a screened porch, and a strip of garden adequate for six tomato plants, cost no less than four thousand eight hundred dollars. Within the cage, seven added to eleven made eighteen every time. No landlord could increase it. No dentist could drill it. No efficiency man could explain that, according to modern research, it ought to make nineteen.

At least, no efficiency man had yet tried.

Horace received forty dollars a week. He gave thirty-two to Mrs. Nimms, spent one dollar and seventy cents on commutation from East New York, sixty cents on lunches, thirty cents on cigars of an extremely theoretical nature, and recorded the remainder in a private expense book which began each January with a ruled page headed:

**COTTAGE FUND—BABYLON—DO NOT TOUCH.**

It was frequently touched. Influenza touched it in 1920. The landlord touched it in 1922. Mrs. Nimms’s sister Agnes touched it for ninety-five dollars and never returned the fingerprints. Still, the fund stood at six hundred and fourteen dollars and twenty cents, and Horace continued to inspect advertisements showing bungalows with white fences, although in most cases the white fence was the only portion obtainable for his capital.

He was a timid man. This should not be confused with weakness of mind. Horace could discover a two-cent error in a column of nine hundred items while the office boy was whistling, the sales manager was dictating, and a steam riveter across the street was attempting to construct a battleship in his left ear. But if the sales manager asked, “Who took my blue pencil?” Horace’s heart confessed to burglary.

For twelve years he had been developing a cost-accounting system for Amalgamated Soap.

He did it at night on the dining-room table after Mrs. Nimms had cleared away the beef liver. The system occupied fourteen ledgers, three hundred and eighty-six loose sheets, and a cigar box containing colored index cards. It traced every cake of soap from tallow, alkali, perfume, wrapper, labor, steam, storage, breakage, freight, commissions, advertising, and bad debts to the ultimate drug-store counter. It revealed that Amalgamated lost one-fifth of a cent on every cake of Carnation Complexion Soap sold west of Omaha, made an excellent profit on Giant Scourer, and paid enough unnecessary freight on hotel soap to furnish all the hotel guests with towels.

Horace knew these things because he had eyes.

Every voucher came through his cage. He saw the bills for coconut oil from Savannah, wrappers from Newark, cartons from Bridgeport, perfume from an importer who spelled Paris with unusual frequency, and railroad charges from every freight agent between Hoboken and San Francisco. Other employees saw paper. Horace saw soap marching in battalions, each small pink soldier carrying its cost upon its back.

He had intended to tell Mr. Hammer.

In Horace’s imagination the interview occurred regularly, usually on the 8:17 train.

“Come in, Nimms,” said Mr. Hammer, standing beside a mahogany desk large enough for treaty-making. “I understand you have given some thought to our costs.”

“A little, sir.”

“Sit down. Have a cigar.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“This is remarkable, Nimms. You have saved the corporation one hundred and eighty thousand dollars a year.”

“I only did my duty, sir.”

“Nonsense! We need men like you at the top. How would twelve thousand a year strike you?”

Horace would hesitate, because a gentleman did not grab at twelve thousand dollars like a dog at liver.

“I think I could manage on that, sir.”

Then Mr. Hammer would slap his shoulder and inquire about the Babylon cottage.

Actually Horace had spoken to Mr. Hammer twice in twenty-one years. Once he said, “Good morning, sir,” and received no answer because Mr. Hammer was discussing lard. The second time Mr. Hammer dropped a glove near the cage.

“Your glove, sir,” said Horace.

“Eh? Thanks.”

The conversation had never reached costs.

Oren Hammer was a thick, erect man with silver hair, a decisive black mustache, and the air of having personally authorized gravity. He arrived at ten-thirty in a cutaway coat and a top hat, carrying a perfecto at an upward angle. Doors opened in front of him. Vice-presidents detached themselves from walls. The office boy, who feared neither God nor the assistant treasurer, ceased whistling.

Horace feared him exquisitely.

Each time Horace resolved to carry his ledgers into the presidential office, he pictured Mr. Hammer looking up and saying, “Who are you?” This question had in fantasy a killing power not usually associated with three monosyllables.

Therefore the accounting system remained beneath the Nimmses’ winter blankets, and Amalgamated Soap continued to manufacture eleven varieties of cleanliness without knowing precisely what any of them cost.

Then S. Walmsley Cowan arrived.

His title was Director of Personnel Analysis and Coordinated Efficiency. He had been retained at one thousand dollars a week for six weeks, which was more than Horace received in six months and caused Horace, despite his mildness, to calculate several unpleasant ratios.

Cowan’s head was broad at the temples, narrow at the chin, and shiny throughout, resembling a cheese pressed for some scientific banquet. His eyes were pale, round, and damp. His mouth was small and pink. His hands were white, soft, and tapered, the sort of hands which have never lifted anything heavier than an opinion.

He wore a gray suit without a wrinkle, lavender spats, and a watch chain from which dangled a gold caliper no larger than a grasshopper. When he spoke, his voice came through his nose with the purring patience of a man explaining civilization to a chair.

“My work,” he informed the department heads, “is to place every human unit in the function for which Nature has psychophysically designed him.”

The department heads nodded. Department heads will nod at anything costing one thousand dollars a week.

Cowan established himself in the directors’ room. He imported charts, stop watches, colored blocks, cards bearing inkblots, a machine for measuring grip, and a young woman named Miss Cleek who wrote down everything with the speed of a court reporter at a scandal.

Employees entered the directors’ room one by one and emerged offended.

Mr. Pritchard, chief shipping clerk for sixteen years, was asked whether he preferred roses to triangles. Miss Cleek recorded that he hesitated.

Miss Dobbins of the stenographic pool was shown an inkblot and asked what it suggested.

“An inkblot,” she said.

Cowan looked sorrowful.

Young Peavey, the office boy, was instructed to put twelve wooden shapes into twelve corresponding holes. He finished in eleven seconds and was classified as a Potential Executive. Mr. Duckett, assistant treasurer, required four minutes and put the purple star in his pocket.

The corporation became psychophysical.

Cowan walked through departments with Miss Cleek behind him, observing, measuring, purring. He watched typists type and advised them that efficiency was speed multiplied by personality. He timed men going to the water cooler. He studied the angle at which salesmen wore their hats. He informed the advertising manager that his ears showed excellent imaginative projection but inadequate resistance to green.

One Tuesday he stopped before the cashier’s cage.

Horace, balancing the branch-office remittances, felt the damp eyes settle on him.

“Your name—ple-e-ese?”

“Horace Nimms.”

“Age?”

“Forty-seven.”

“Married?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Children?”

“No, sir.”

“Desires?”

Horace blinked.

“Sir?”

“Your dominant desire. Money? Power? Beauty? Service? Escape?”

Horace thought of the Babylon cottage, its imaginary porch, Mrs. Nimms in a wicker chair, and six orderly tomato plants.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Cowan made a mark. It was probably not a cottage.

“Hold out your hands—ple-e-ese.”

Horace extended them through the bars. Cowan turned them palm upward, then downward, pressed the fingertips, measured the spread between thumb and forefinger, and examined the nails as if one of them might confess.

“Interesting,” he murmured.

Miss Cleek wrote this down.

“Very interesting.”

She underlined it.

Cowan placed three brass washers, a steel nut, and a wooden cube on the counter.

“Separate according to instinct.”

“Separate what from what?”

“Precisely.”

Horace waited.

Cowan waited.

Miss Cleek waited with her pencil poised.

Horace put the steel nut beside the brass washers and left the wooden cube alone.

“Ah,” said Cowan.

It was one of those “ahs” from which no employee can profit.

The following afternoon Horace underwent his full examination. His skull was measured in seven directions. He squeezed the grip machine. He copied a design of intersecting squares. He was asked to say the first word occurring to him after “ocean,” “mother,” “red,” “authority,” and “Thursday.”

To “authority” he replied, after an unfortunate pause, “Mr. Hammer.”

Cowan leaned forward.

“Why?”

“He is president.”

“Do you always answer questions literally?”

“I try to answer them correctly.”

“Accuracy compulsion,” Cowan murmured to Miss Cleek.

Horace was shown twenty cards and asked to sort them rapidly. Since the cards bore account numbers, he arranged them numerically.

“No, no,” Cowan said. “Not intellectually. Emotionally.”

Horace attempted emotion. He placed a yellow card beside a blue one and felt idiotic.

Next came the inkblots.

The first looked like two totals carried forward. The second resembled a freight map of Ohio. The third looked to Horace like Cowan himself after being dropped from a moderate height, but he lacked the courage to mention this.

“A butterfly,” he said.

Cowan sighed.

“Another conventional response.”

“I could call it a bat.”

“Also conventional.”

Horace studied the blot helplessly. “A deficit?”

Miss Cleek’s pencil became excited.

Finally Cowan examined Horace’s hands again. He bent each finger, tapped the knuckles, tested wrist rotation, and asked him to pick pins out of a saucer.

“Extraordinary manipulative aptitude,” he announced. “Strong machine orientation. Weak social projection. Low aggressive initiative. Marked dependence upon confinement.”

Horace looked around. They had put him in a cage for twenty-one years. Dependence may sometimes be merely punctuality with walls.

Three days later Cowan called a conference in Mr. Duckett’s office. Horace stood before Cowan, Mr. Duckett, and a sheet of paper titled **FUNCTIONAL HUMAN REDISTRIBUTION—PRELIMINARY**.

“Nimms,” Cowan purred, “you have been misassigned.”

“Misassigned?”

“For twenty-one years.”

Horace swallowed. Twenty-one years was a long error even for Amalgamated Soap.

“You possess the digital-mechanical pattern. Your hands demand levers, controls, repetitive vertical coordination. Figures, on the other hand, require aggressive abstraction.”

“I like figures.”

Cowan smiled as physicians smile at patients who enjoy their symptoms.

“Conscious preference is frequently the disguise of functional maladjustment.”

“But I’ve handled the cash since—”

“Exactly. Habit. Fear. Inertia. We are freeing you.”

Horace did not feel free. A man falling from a roof is technically free, but the distinction brings little comfort.

Cowan continued. “Beginning Monday, you will operate elevator number four.”

Horace’s mouth opened.

“Mr. Rocco, the present operator, will take your position as cashier.”

“Rocco?”

“His tests reveal bold monetary imagination, excellent acquisitive reflexes, and a dynamic attitude toward negotiable paper.”

Mr. Duckett coughed. Perhaps he had met Rocco.

Horace had ridden in Rocco’s elevator. Rocco was a large young man with blue jaws, a green uniform, and a grievance against passengers. He started before people entered, stopped after they wished to leave, and regarded floors as suggestions. On quiet trips he read racing sheets. On busy trips he read them with annoyance.

“He knows nothing about accounts,” Horace said.

“Knowledge,” replied Cowan, “is often an obstacle to native capacity.”

That evening Horace carried home his red expense book and forgot his umbrella. He had forgotten an umbrella only once before, in 1908, and then because a horse had entered the office.

Mrs. Nimms was a narrow, practical woman who had learned during twenty-three years of marriage that Horace’s silences came in denominations. There was the five-cent silence of a missed train, the two-dollar silence of a plumbing bill, and the fifty-dollar silence connected with her sister Agnes.

“What happened?” she asked.

“They made me an elevator man.”

Mrs. Nimms set down the liver.

“They did what?”

Horace explained Cowan, the tests, the washers, the inkblots, and his alleged intimacy with machinery.

“What will you get?”

“Thirty-four dollars.”

This was the important obscenity.

Mrs. Nimms looked toward the cupboard containing the cottage ledgers. “You go tell the president.”

“I can’t just—”

“You’ve worked there twenty-one years.”

“That’s exactly why.”

The logic was Horace’s and therefore clear only to him. The longer one has failed to approach a president, the more enormous the approach becomes. By twenty-one years it is not an interview but an expedition requiring ropes, oxygen, and burial instructions.

Monday morning Horace put on Rocco’s spare uniform. It was too large in the chest, too long in the sleeves, and carried upon it a smell compounded of brass polish, cigars, and democratic resentment. A peaked cap rested on his ears.

Rocco entered the cashier’s cage wearing a scarlet tie and Horace’s sleeve garters.

“Some aquarium,” he said, rattling the bars.

Horace showed him the drawers, the voucher procedure, the signature file, the daily reconciliation, the branch-office deposits, and the emergency key.

“Yeah, yeah,” said Rocco. “Where’s the big money?”

“All money is accounted for.”

Rocco grinned. “You don’t say.”

At nine-fifteen Horace made his first trip.

“Going up,” he called weakly.

“Put some life in it,” said Cowan, who happened to be passing. “Sell the ascent!”

Horace tried again. “Going up!”

A stenographer stepped aboard and asked for seven. Horace overshot by half a floor, descended to six, ascended to eight, and finally delivered her to seven with the expression of a man returning a kidnapped child.

By noon he had learned the controller. By three he stopped within six inches of the mark. His hands, traitorously, were indeed good at levers.

Up went Horace: lobby, three, five, nine, twelve.

Down went Horace: eleven, eight, four, lobby.

He carried salesmen, clerks, samples, directors, bundles of wrappers, two drums of violet perfume, and Cowan’s coordinated efficiency. The cage rose and fell all day, a steel pendulum measuring his disgrace.

From the elevator Horace saw more of Amalgamated than he had seen from the cashier’s cage, but understood less. Conversations entered in fragments.

“—Chicago territory says—”

“—if Palm Lily falls another point—”

“—Hammer’ll skin Duckett—”

“—twelve thousand gross of hotel size—”

Doors closed. People vanished. Floors slid by like ruled lines without totals.

Cowan assembled the entire office on Wednesday at five for an Inspirational Coordination Address. Horace was ordered to leave the elevator at nine and stand in the rear.

Cowan mounted a packing case beneath a banner reading **ABILITY PLUS ACTION EQUALS ACHIEVEMENT**. Miss Cleek sat beside him.

“My friends,” he began, “modern business demands initiative. Do not wait for opportunity to knock. Knock first. Knock harder. If the door is locked, create a key. If there is no keyhole, become the keyhole.”

The employees listened with the grave expressions of people being paid by the week.

“The timid man says, ‘I cannot.’ The dynamic man says, ‘Why not?’ The successful man says, ‘Stand aside!’ Break your mental shackles. Leap the chasm. Grasp the ladder. Burn your bridges. Keep your powder dry. Hitch your wagon to the morning star and then step on the accelerator!”

Horace, standing in the rear in Rocco’s oversized uniform, felt a slight heat under his collar.

“Too many employees,” Cowan continued, “hide their gifts. They bury ideas. They fear authority. Authority is not a wall—it is a window! Speak up. Push forward. Make yourself heard. No man rises by remaining on the ground floor.”

Cowan had probably used this sentence before, but never to an elevator operator.

Thursday morning Rocco disappeared.

He also caused six hundred dollars to disappear.

The facts developed rapidly. A messenger had delivered eight hundred and forty dollars for the payroll account. Rocco gave a receipt, placed two hundred and forty dollars in the drawer, put six hundred dollars somewhere with better ventilation, and departed for lunch at ten-twenty.

Mr. Duckett ran through the offices pale and moist. Detectives arrived. Miss Cleek declared that Rocco’s acquisitive reflex had been correctly diagnosed but improperly integrated. Cowan said no scientific system could eliminate temporary deviations.

Horace sat on his stool in elevator four and stared at the floor indicator.

Six hundred dollars.

He had handled sums ten times as large without losing a nickel. They had taken him from the money because his hands were suitable for machinery, and put Rocco there because his personality was suitable for money. Rocco’s personality was now on its way to Montreal.

For the first time in his career Horace felt anger without immediately apologizing to it.

At eleven-forty he took Mr. Duckett and two vice-presidents from nine to twelve. They spoke over him because elevator men, like office furniture, are not expected to retain language.

“Old man’s boiling,” said one vice-president.

“He wants the real cost on Lily White,” said Duckett. “Manufacturing says four-point-six cents. Sales says five-point-two. Advertising won’t allocate premiums. Freight has three schedules. Nobody knows.”

“He wants it by tomorrow?”

“He wants it by noon. Says we’re selling ten million cakes and may be losing money on every one.”

“He’ll fire the lot of us.”

“He ought to fire Cowan.”

The doors opened. They departed.

Horace remained very still.

Lily White Toilet Soap cost, in eastern territory, four and seventy-three hundredths cents per cake before sales commission; five and eighteen hundredths including national advertising; five and thirty-one hundredths with breakage, returns, and dealer allowances. In western rural territory it cost five and sixty-four hundredths. The current wholesale realization was five and one-half cents. At ten million cakes—

“Lobby!” shouted a passenger.

Horace descended.

All afternoon numbers rose around him more vividly than floors. Freight allocations, steam costs, perfume losses, wrapper spoilage, seasonal rebates: they assembled in their proper columns. At five Cowan’s phrases returned.

Speak up.

Push forward.

Make yourself heard.

No man rises by remaining on the ground floor.

Horace disliked Cowan’s philosophy, but a drowning man does not inquire where the rope was educated.

The following morning Mr. Hammer entered the elevator at ten-thirty-two. He wore his cutaway coat, top hat, and a red carnation, and held the perfecto in his right hand. Mr. Duckett followed him.

“Nine,” said Hammer.

Horace closed the doors.

His heart began beating at approximately one hundred and forty per minute. He knew because he counted eleven beats during a measured portion of the ascent.

At the seventh floor he made his decision.

At seven and one-half, he stopped the elevator.

It stopped sharply enough to disturb Mr. Hammer’s cigar ash.

“What’s this?” he demanded.

Horace gripped the controller.

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

“Then move.”

“Yes, sir. That is—I wanted to speak to you.”

Mr. Duckett stared. “Nimms, have you gone crazy?”

“No, sir. I don’t believe so.”

“Start this car!”

Horace looked at Mr. Hammer. For twenty-one years he had imagined that face above a mahogany desk. He had never imagined it suspended between Ladies’ Handbags on seven and Amalgamated Soap on nine.

“Mr. Hammer,” he said, and his voice shook, “Lily White costs five and eighteen hundredths cents a cake in eastern territory, including advertising but excluding dealer allowances.”

Hammer’s eyes narrowed.

“What?”

“In western territory it costs more than you receive for it. The freight allocation is wrong. Manufacturing charges all perfume evaporation against the quarter where the loss is discovered instead of the batches where it occurs. And sales commissions are calculated on gross billings without deducting returned goods.”

Mr. Duckett turned from pink to gray.

Hammer removed the perfecto from his mouth. “How do you know?”

“All the vouchers passed through my cage.”

“You’re the cashier?”

“I was, sir.”

“Why aren’t you now?”

“Mr. Cowan says I have mechanical hands.”

Hammer glanced at Horace’s white-knuckled grip upon the controller. “Have you?”

“I can stop an elevator.”

“That’s evident. Can you prove those costs?”

“Yes, sir.”

“How?”

“I have a system. Fourteen ledgers. It assigns raw materials, labor, steam, spoilage, storage, selling expense, freight, advertising, premiums, returns, and bad debts by brand and territory. I’ve worked on it twelve years.”

Mr. Duckett found his voice. “On company time?”

“No, sir. At home after supper.”

Hammer looked at Duckett. Duckett’s voice went away again.

“What does Giant Scourer cost?” Hammer asked.

“Three and nine-tenths cents per cake in lots of one gross, eastern delivery.”

“What do we get?”

“Five cents, less two percent discount.”

“Carnation Complexion?”

“We lose approximately two-tenths of a cent west of the Missouri when the free washcloth premium is included.”

“Hotel soap?”

“The special wrapper costs more than the soap. If the Cleveland contract is routed through Buffalo, as it has been for nine months, the excess freight is one-ninety-second of a cent per cake.”

Hammer stared at him with the concentrated expression of a python reconsidering a rabbit.

“How much would your system save?”

Horace had dreamed of this question. In dreams he answered modestly and Mr. Hammer supplied the larger figure. Reality required more backbone.

“Direct savings, perhaps ninety thousand dollars the first year. More if you discontinue unprofitable territories or alter the price schedules.”

“How much more?”

“Between forty and sixty thousand.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

There it was: the terrible question. Curiously, once it arrived, it seemed smaller than anticipated.

“I was afraid of you, sir.”

Hammer blinked. Mr. Duckett studied the ceiling.

“Afraid?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Why?”

Horace considered. “You’re president.”

For one second Oren Hammer’s mustache twitched. Then he laughed—a short, explosive laugh that filled the elevator and startled Horace more than anger would have done.

“You trap me between floors, hold up the assistant treasurer, and tell me I’m losing a hundred and fifty thousand dollars because you’re afraid of me?”

“Yes, sir.”

“That’s the damnedest kind of timidity I ever saw.”

“I had encouragement.”

“From whom?”

“Mr. Cowan. He told us to make ourselves heard and not remain on the ground floor.”

Hammer laughed again. Mr. Duckett joined him carefully, as assistant treasurers do.

“All right, Nimms. Take us to nine before your courage improves further.”

Horace moved the lever. The elevator rose the remaining floor and a half with the smooth dignity of a presidential yacht.

As the doors opened, Hammer turned.

“My office. Nine tomorrow. Bring the ledgers.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Nimms—”

“Yes, sir?”

“Don’t stop the elevator next time.”

“No, sir.”

Horace reached home that evening at six-thirty carrying no liver. He had purchased two lamb chops at forty-eight cents a pound, a bunch of celery, and a small bottle of ginger ale. Extravagance? Certainly. But revolutions have always cost money.

Mrs. Nimms heard the story without interruption.

“When he said come at nine,” she asked, “did he mean it?”

“He looked as though he did.”

“Then wear your blue suit.”

“The elbows shine.”

“So does the president’s head, probably. Brush it.”

That night Horace slept for intervals of three minutes and calculated during the spaces between. At seven he rose, shaved twice, tied his best tie, untied it, tied the second-best tie, returned to the first, and packed the fourteen ledgers in two valises. Mrs. Nimms accompanied him to the door.

“Speak loud,” she said.

“I’ll try.”

“And ask for enough.”

“I’m not going to ask—”

“Then speak louder.”

At eight-fifty-seven Horace stood outside the presidential office. He wore the blue suit, the repaired shoes, and a collar so high that it gave moral support to his chin.

At nine precisely the door opened.

Mr. Hammer stood beside the mahogany desk.

“Come in, Nimms.”

The room was exactly as Horace had imagined, except that the desk was smaller and the rug had a cigar burn near the window. Sunlight shone on framed factories, a bronze bull, and a silver model of a cake of Lily White.

“Sit down,” said Hammer.

Horace sat.

“Cigar?”

The word descended from twenty-one years of fantasy.

“Thank you, sir.”

Hammer pushed the box toward him. Horace selected a cigar with a band broad enough to serve as a napkin ring. He did not light it. It seemed more prudent to preserve the evidence.

For two hours they went through the ledgers. Hammer asked short questions. Horace answered with figures, freight zones, production runs, commission rates, and decimals. Once begun, he forgot to be afraid. He opened the cigar box of index cards. He demonstrated how a blue card represented direct labor, red indirect expense, yellow selling cost, and green avoidable waste. He showed how warehouse charges had been spread equally over fast-moving and slow-moving brands, thereby permitting six-year-old peppermint soap to live comfortably at the expense of Giant Scourer.

Hammer sent for Duckett, the manufacturing chief, and the sales manager.

They came in prepared to defend themselves. Within fifteen minutes they were leaning over Horace’s ledgers.

“Where’d you get that figure?” demanded the manufacturing chief.

“Your steam invoices for March, April, and May.”

“That perfume loss can’t be right.”

“Plant inventory, page forty-seven.”

The sales manager objected to the western commission allocation. Horace produced seventeen vouchers and ended his objection at voucher six.

At eleven-thirty Hammer leaned back.

“Gentlemen, we’re installing this system.”

The three executives looked at Horace.

“Nimms will take charge. He’ll report directly to me until it’s running.”

Horace felt the office become unreal around the edges.

“What salary are you getting?” Hammer asked.

“Forty dollars a week—before the transfer.”

“You’ll get a hundred to start.”

A hundred dollars a week. Five thousand two hundred a year. A Babylon cottage with two bedrooms, a porch, a garden, and perhaps seven tomato plants.

“Is that satisfactory?”

Horace remembered Mrs. Nimms at the door: ask for enough.

“If the system saves what I believe it will,” he said, carefully but audibly, “I should like the matter reviewed in six months.”

Hammer’s eyebrows rose. Then the mustache twitched.

“It will be.”

Horace nodded. “Thank you, sir.”

There are moments when a timid man astonishes everybody except himself. The courage has not appeared suddenly; it has merely completed twenty-one years of compound interest.

A soft knock sounded.

Cowan entered, followed by Miss Cleek. He wore his gray suit, lavender spats, and professional smile.

“You sent for me, Mr. Hammer?”

“Yes. Shut the door.”

Cowan shut it.

Hammer picked up one of Horace’s ledgers. “Did you transfer this man from cashier to elevator operator?”

Cowan glanced at Horace as if discovering an insect in a salad.

“A psychofunctional reassignment, yes. His manipulative profile—”

“And you put Rocco in the cashier’s cage?”

“His dynamic acquisitive pattern indicated financial—”

“He stole six hundred dollars.”

“A temporary maladjustment under environmental—”

“You’re through.”

Cowan’s pink mouth remained open.

“I beg your pardon?”

“You’re fired. As of now.”

“But, Mr. Hammer, ple-e-ese. No scientific evaluation can be judged by an isolated—”

“It isn’t isolated. It’s accompanied by you.”

Cowan’s damp eyes moved from Hammer to Horace, from Horace to the fourteen ledgers, and from the ledgers to Miss Cleek, whose pencil had ceased to move. Science is admirable in success, but in failure it generally needs a train.

“You cannot dismiss a program of this magnitude without a terminal integration conference,” Cowan protested.

“I can.”

“My contract—”

“Duckett will settle it. Go to the cashier’s cage and collect your final salary.”

For the first time Horace saw Cowan without a phrase ready. The efficiency expert’s hands fluttered at his sides, beautifully adapted to nothing.

Hammer turned to Horace.

“Nimms, we’ll need an office for you. Take Cowan’s.”

Horace looked at Cowan. Cowan looked at Horace. Between them passed the entire doctrine of coordinated human efficiency, measured, classified, and found six hundred dollars short.

“Ple-e-ese,” said Horace, with unconscious politeness, “the cashier’s cage is on nine.”

Cowan departed.

By noon, a sign painter had removed **S. WALMSLEY COWAN—DIRECTOR OF PERSONNEL ANALYSIS** from the frosted glass. At two, Horace’s name appeared in plain black letters:

**HORACE NIMMS  
COST ACCOUNTING**

He stood in the doorway looking at it.

The office contained a desk, three chairs, a telephone, and Cowan’s abandoned chart showing the seven principal shapes of commercial skulls. Horace put the chart in the wastebasket. He arranged his pencils by length. Then he telephoned Mrs. Nimms.

“It’s a hundred,” he told her.

“A week?”

“A week.”

There was a silence on the line—a new denomination, not previously recorded.

“Horace,” she said at last, “we can look at cottages Sunday.”

“Yes.”

“With a porch?”

“Yes.”

“And not too far from the station.”

“Within twelve minutes’ walk,” said Horace, who had already calculated it.

He hung up and went to the window. Below him Broadway shone in the afternoon, crowded with trucks, hats, streetcars, messengers, salesmen, and small determined people carrying private systems nobody had yet asked to see.

Across the outer office, Cowan waited at the cashier’s cage while Mr. Rocco’s temporary replacement counted out the final salary twice.

“Sign here,” said the cashier.

Cowan signed.

“Initial here.”

Cowan initialed.

“Count it before leaving.”

Cowan counted it. His soft mechanical fingers performed tolerably well.

Horace watched for a moment, then returned to his desk. There were costs to allocate, territories to compare, wastes to expose, and eleven varieties of soap to lead out of financial darkness. He opened the first ledger and dipped his pen.

Down in the elevator shaft, car number four went up, stopped neatly at nine, and went down again.

Horace did not hear it.

He was already higher.

### The Step That Did Not Break

June had come to Washington with brass music in its breath.

All week the pavements near the high school rang beneath the measured tread of cadets. Blue-coated companies crossed the yards in columns of four; captains called commands until their voices grew hoarse; rifle barrels flashed above young shoulders; and small boys, flattening themselves against iron fences, marched in imitation with broomsticks, laths, and such other weapons as an obliging alley might provide. Everywhere there were buttons to polish, leggings to whiten, belts to tighten, and mothers to remind their sons that glory was no excuse for mud upon the carpet.

To Bud Carter, the competitive drill was the end toward which the whole year had been marching.

He had studied his geometry and Latin, worked afternoons at a grocer’s when work could be had, and reported faithfully for drill in rain that soaked the plume of his cap and cold that made the rifle burn against his bare fingers. Yet now lessons, wages, and weather seemed only the scattered notes before the great chord. Company A was to compete on Friday, and every member believed—or said loudly enough to compel belief—that Company A would win.

Bud’s mother believed it too.

Hannah Carter had made the uniform possible by a series of economies so small that no one but herself could have counted them and so severe that Bud, had he known them all, would have refused the coat. She turned an old winter skirt, gave up meat on Tuesdays, and sat sewing after the lamp ought to have been extinguished. The shining buttons cost more than buttons had any Christian right to cost. The white gloves were bought with money intended for a new pair of shoes, whereupon Hannah put paste upon the cracked leather of the old pair and told her feet to remember that education was walking forward, whether the shoes agreed or not.

She was a widow, stout-hearted and slender-pursed, with two children and an unshaken conviction that both had been lent to her for improvement.

Little Sister—who was ten, though illness had left her no larger than seven—helped by lying very still while Hannah stitched. She also admired Bud’s uniform at regular intervals.

“Turn ’round again, Buddie,” she would say from the couch.

Bud would turn.

“Now walk.”

He would take six grave steps across the room, wheel before the stove, and return with the solemnity of a general reviewing his troops.

“You look finer’n de President,” Little Sister declared.

“Presidents don’t wear cadet coats,” Bud answered.

“Den dey ought to.”

Hannah bit off a thread. “Don’t you fill dat boy’s head wid foolishness, Baby. A uniform look mighty grand, but it ain’t de cloth dat makes de man inside it.”

Still, when Bud stood before her on Friday morning, the dark coat fitting his straight young body and the white belt crossing his breast, her eyes shone despite her wisdom.

“Company A’s going to take that medal, Mammy,” he said.

“Mebbe so.”

“Mebbe! Why, we’re better than Company B.”

“Boy, ain’t nobody ever been so good dat somebody couldn’t be bettah. You keep yo’ chin up, yo’ shoulders square, an’ yo’ mind on de command. De medal can look after itself.”

Little Sister reached from the couch and touched one bright button.

“You going to see us, Lammie?” Bud asked.

“Sho’ I is.”

Hannah glanced at the pale little face, then at the warming sky. “If I got to carry her all de way.”

By noon the drill grounds had become a republic of colours. Blue and white ribbons streamed from one stand, crimson and gray from another, while black and yellow pennants fluttered above a third like a flock of quarrelsome birds. Girls waved banners; boys shouted school cries across the field; vendors carried lemonade through the crowd; alumni displayed old badges and younger waistcoats. The band thundered a march, ceased, began another, and blew with such heroic vigour that the trumpets seemed prepared to summon not merely cadets but all the sleeping armies of history.

On the reviewing stand sat school officials, military officers, dignitaries, and men important enough to consult their watches repeatedly. They arranged papers, changed chairs, whispered, coughed, and looked toward the entrance. Altogether accomplishing delay with admirable discipline.

Hannah and Little Sister found seats low in the stand. Around them the crowd moved and broke like restless water, but Hannah planted her shoes, settled the child close beside her, and would not be shifted.

“Can you see, Baby?”

“I can see de gate.”

“Bud ain’t come through yet.”

“I know his step.”

Presently the megaphone announced Company C.

The gates opened. The band struck a shining blast, and the first cadets entered beneath their colours. They marched well. Rifles rose and fell together; feet struck the earth in a single pulse; commands flew sharp as stones from the captain’s mouth. The crowd applauded volley firing, cheered a difficult wheel, and groaned when the rear rank lost alignment.

Company B followed and did better. Its blue and white streamers tossed proudly in the sun. At the command “Right oblique!” the whole company changed direction as though one mind had turned within eighty bodies. Their manual of arms was clean; their columns of four opened and closed without a jar. The blue-and-white stand became a storm of handkerchiefs.

“Dey’s good,” Little Sister whispered.

Hannah clasped her hands upon her lap. “Ain’t seen Company A yet.”

Then the black and yellow appeared at the gate.

“Bud! Bud!” cried Little Sister.

Hannah saw him before she found breath to answer. There he was in the second platoon, taller than the boy before him, his chin drawn in, his rifle true against his shoulder. In that instant she did not see the groceries carried, the Latin declined, the tired shoes beneath the table, or the lamp burning past midnight. She saw only her son moving forward among his fellows, disciplined, educated, belonging to something ordered and splendid. Pride rose in her until it hurt.

Company A entered like a dark machine set to music. Its lines seemed fastened by invisible rods; its turns snapped square; its rifles came to present arms with one metallic clap. Black-and-yellow streamers leaped overhead and kissed the light. The captain’s voice rang out, the band fell silent, and even the people who had cheered Company B leaned forward.

“Fix bayonets!”

Steel sprang from scabbards.

The cadets fixed the blades with crisp precision. Under the June sun the bayonets became a field of narrow fires. Hannah felt Little Sister’s fingers creep into hers.

Company A advanced, wheeled, halted, and dressed ranks. It moved from line into columns of four, executed left oblique, formed again, and passed through the manual with a sureness that made rival colours droop. Each success drew a roar from the black-and-yellow stand. Yet to Hannah and Little Sister there were not eighty cadets. There was Bud, Bud—Bud’s shoulder, Bud’s foot, Bud’s grave face under the visor.

The final movement was difficult: a change of front, followed at once by a wheel and advance. Hannah knew nothing of its name, but she understood from the sudden quiet that the danger of error was great.

The command came.

The company turned.

A bayonet flashed loose.

For the smallest part of a second it seemed to hang beside Bud like a stroke of light. Then it struck the ground with a sharp ring and lay behind him in the dust.

Little Sister gasped.

Bud had felt the socket slip. Every nerve in him cried out to stop, stoop, seize the weapon, repair the shame. But the cadet at his back was one pace behind; the rank beside him was wheeling; the whole company pressed onward under the command. To halt would mean collision. To bend would break the line.

So Bud marched.

He marched with an empty muzzle above his shoulder and fire in his face. The fallen steel glittered farther and farther behind. To him its little ringing fall grew louder than the band, louder than the captain, louder than the shouts from the stands. He saw nothing but that blade in the dust.

The company finished. Its line wavered slightly in the last formation, recovered, and stood at attention. Applause came, but not with the confident thunder of a moment before. Black-and-yellow flags still waved, though here and there one streamer hung limp.

The judges consulted.

Company B was awarded first place.

Its supporters burst into joyful confusion. Blue-and-white banners poured over the railings; hats flew; the band began a victorious march several measures before anyone had asked it. Company A stood rigid while the announcement passed above their heads like a sentence.

Bud did not look toward the stand.

After dismissal his comrades gathered in knots, some angry with the captain, some with the judges, some with the sun, the dust, the regulations, or any other thing large enough to bear defeat. One boy called Bud’s name, but Bud slipped behind the equipment shed. He waited until the crowd had begun to leave, then took a side street home.

Hannah and Little Sister returned more slowly.

“Was it Bud made ’em lose?” Little Sister asked.

Hannah’s mouth tightened. “I don’t know what made ’em lose.”

“He didn’t stop.”

“No.”

“He looked so lonesome marching on.”

Hannah squeezed the child’s hand. She had seen the empty rifle; she had seen the colour rise above Bud’s collar; and she had known, with the terrible knowledge of a mother, exactly how much courage it had cost him not to run from the field.

They found him sitting in the dark kitchen with his uniform coat unbuttoned and his cap on the table. The supper was ready, but he had not touched it. His shoulders, which had been held so square before thousands, bent beneath the gaze of two loving people.

Little Sister went directly to him and put both thin arms around his neck.

“You was the best one, Buddie.”

He swallowed. “Don’t say that.”

“You was.”

“I dropped it. Everybody saw. We lost.”

Hannah removed her hat and laid it beside his cap. “Company B drilled fine.”

“They wouldn’t have beaten us if I hadn’t—”

“How you know dat?”

“I know.” His voice broke in spite of him. “All year we worked. Captain trusted me. The fellows—Mammy, I disgraced the whole company.”

Little Sister pressed her cheek against his sleeve. “You ain’t disgraced me.”

He shut his eyes.

“Nor me,” said Hannah.

“You don’t understand.”

Hannah’s hand stopped over the supper plates. For a moment weariness showed in her face—the work, the saving, the walking, the hours spent fastening pride into every seam of that coat. Then she drew herself upright.

“Mebbe I don’t understand all dem commands. Mebbe I can’t tell a right oblique from a left-handed somersault. But I understand a boy marchin’ when he feel like he want de earth to open up an’ hide him. I seen you keep yo’ place.”

“What else could I do?”

“Plenty folks finds plenty else to do when trouble comes.”

Bud turned away. “I’m not going back tomorrow.”

The room grew very still.

Little Sister loosened her arms and stared at him. Hannah crossed to the window, lowered the shade, and returned. Her movements were quiet, which Bud knew to be more dangerous than noise.

“What you say?”

“I can’t face them.”

“Can’t?”

“They’ll all be talking about it.”

“Most likely.”

“They’ll blame me.”

“Some might.”

He looked at her, surprised by the absence of rescue in her answers.

Hannah sat opposite him. “Buddie, courage ain’t just marchin’ when de band playin’ an’ folks hollerin’. Courage is gettin’ up next mornin’ when de music done stopped. You made one mistake. Now you fixin’ to make another, bigger one, by backin’ down.”

“I’m ashamed.”

“Carry yo’ shame to school, den. It ain’t too heavy. You carried groceries heavier’n dat.”

Little Sister climbed into the chair beside him. “I’ll keep being proud till you come home.”

At last Bud gave a miserable little laugh. Hannah served supper without another sermon. She understood that some truths, like medicine, did not improve when the dose was doubled.

The next morning his uniform hung on its hook, but Bud wore his ordinary school clothes. He ate little. At the door Hannah straightened his collar.

“Shoulders,” she said.

He braced them.

“Chin.”

He raised it.

“Now go get yo’ edgication.”

“Yassum.”

Every block seemed shorter than Bud wished. By the time he reached the school, groups of boys already crowded the steps. Conversation dipped as he approached—or perhaps he only imagined it. Someone called, “Morning, Carter.” Someone else asked whether he had heard that Company B’s captain had celebrated by falling into a fountain. Bud managed a smile and went inside.

At assembly the whole school gathered in the hall. Company A occupied the front rows, no longer a unit of flawless motion but a collection of tired boys with ink on their fingers and defeat upon their spirits. Bud took his accustomed seat. The cadet beside him moved his books to make room, neither warmly nor coldly.

The principal rose, made announcements, and then introduced Major Wainwright, the army officer who had served as chief judge.

Bud’s stomach tightened.

The major stood tall and gray before the students. He spoke first of the excellence of the competition, the labour of the instructors, the credit due every company. Then he paused.

“There was an incident yesterday,” he said, “which deserves particular notice.”

Bud stared at the floor.

“During Company A’s change of front, a cadet lost his bayonet. Some spectators believed the accident responsible for the company’s defeat. It was not. The scoring record will show that the decisive deductions occurred elsewhere.”

A stir passed through the hall.

“I watched that cadet closely. Had he halted to recover the weapon, he would have obstructed the man behind him, broken the wheel, and perhaps thrown an entire platoon into confusion. Instead, though plainly aware of the accident, he maintained interval, direction, and step. He sacrificed his own appearance to preserve the formation.”

Now Bud looked up.

The major’s eyes found him.

“Cadet Carter, will you stand?”

For one dreadful second Bud’s legs refused. Then Hannah’s voice seemed to come from the kitchen: Shoulders. Chin. He rose.

“Discipline,” said the major, “is not the art of never meeting an accident. It is the power to perform one’s duty after the accident has occurred. Yesterday this young man showed self-command under the hardest circumstance a cadet commonly faces: public embarrassment. I commend him.”

The major stepped down and held out his hand.

Bud walked forward through a silence so complete that he heard the scrape of his own shoe. He took the offered hand. The major’s grip was firm, official, and brief.

Then Company A began to cheer.

The sound struck Bud almost physically. Boys rose; hands clapped; someone shouted his name; then the whole hall caught it and gave it back to him—not in mockery, not in blame, but in fellowship. Even Company B joined, magnanimous in victory and relieved to cheer something that did not require another drill.

Bud returned to his seat with his face burning. The cadet beside him punched his arm.

“Why’d you dodge us yesterday, fool?”

Bud could not answer. He was too busy blinking at the black-and-yellow ribbon pinned inside his classmate’s coat.

He ran much of the way home.

Hannah was washing clothes in the kitchen, her sleeves rolled above her elbows. Little Sister lay near the window with a book open upside down on her lap. Both looked up when the door burst wide.

“Well?” Hannah asked.

Bud told them. He began with the assembly, went back to the scoring, repeated the major’s words as nearly as he could, and demonstrated the handshake twice because Little Sister demanded exactness.

“I told you,” she said. “You was the best one.”

“Not the best,” Bud answered, smiling now. “But I didn’t break the line.”

Hannah turned toward the washbasin, though no soap required such close attention. She wiped one wet hand across her cheek and left a little streak of suds there.

“Dat’s what I seen,” she said. “Dat’s what mattered.”

That evening Bud took his uniform from its hook. Dust still marked the trouser knees, and one sleeve bore the faint pressure of Little Sister’s embrace. He spread the coat carefully across the table, fetched the brush, and began to work from collar to hem.

Tomorrow Company A would drill again. The medal belonged to Company B; the bayonet had truly fallen; no praise could lift it backward into his hand. Yet defeat no longer seemed a door closed upon him. It was only a hard place in the road over which he had kept step.

Hannah watched from the stove. Little Sister watched from the couch. Under Bud’s steady brushing, the dark cloth regained its lustre, the buttons gathered the lamplight, and the uniform waited, ready to be worn.

Jan would not hang properly.

There were four men trying to persuade him, and still he would not hang. He clawed, kicked, butted, bit, and twisted among them with such demoniacal industry that the five bodies rolled over the snow like a many-legged beast fallen from some elder and less orderly creation.

“Lay hold o’ his feet!” Red Bill shouted.

“I have his feet,” Lawson answered from beneath the confusion. “Which is to say, they have me.”

One of Jan’s boots drove into a kettle. The kettle sailed ringing across the ice, struck the frozen bank, and came spinning back as if unwilling to miss any part of the entertainment.

Mr. Taylor, who had been born in Kentucky and had preserved through famine, frost, and five northern winters a large remnant of ceremonial dignity, clung to Jan’s right arm.

“Sir,” he panted, “this conduct is unseemly. We are attempting to execute you with every consideration compatible with the circumstances.”

“Den I make de circumstances not compatible!” Jan roared.

He doubled himself, flung Mr. Taylor over his shoulder, and sat down violently upon Red Bill. Lawson, rising at the same instant, received the back of Jan’s head under the chin. His teeth snapped together. For half a second he saw green seas, red moons, and the entire British navy sailing bottom upward.

There had been a time when all five men would have agreed that hanging a companion was not a proper occupation for the hour before breakfast. That time lay southward, beyond two thousand miles of spruce, muskeg, mountain, and frozen river. It belonged to streets, policemen, Sunday bells, and women who poured coffee from white china. Here there was snow, and under the snow more snow; there was darkness, and behind the darkness the promise of another darkness; there was cold, steady and profound, pressing upon body and brain until the little laws men carried inside them cracked like thin ice.

For five years they had endured one another.

Five years of Jan’s snoring; five years of Bill’s tobacco smoke; five years of Lawson’s tales of the sea; five years of Mr. Taylor shaving on Sundays in order, as he explained, to maintain the distinction between man and brute. They had eaten fish till fish seemed a personal insult. They had mended harness by candle-stub, buried dogs in iron ground, dragged boats over mountains, and watched the same faces across the same grease-blackened pan.

Civilization is often no more than sufficient elbow room. Take away the room, lower the temperature, repeat every annoyance through sixty months, and a man may kill because another man pronounces his name incorrectly.

John Gordon had done worse. He had called Jan a liar.

It happened in the tent while the storm worried the canvas and the smoke refused to rise. Gordon had laughed at Jan’s account of a bear killed in Manitoba, adding that no bear of such dimensions had ever lived outside a German beer barrel.

Jan had risen slowly.

“You say I lie?”

“I say,” Gordon replied, “that your bear grows two feet every winter.”

The revolver came out. There was one shot, deafening within the low canvas. Gordon pitched backward, bringing down the center pole, the stove pipe, and most of the tent. Blood ran from his neck into his collar. He did not move.

For perhaps ten seconds the others stood beneath collapsed canvas, coughing in smoke and staring.

Then Red Bill said, “Well, we got to hang him.”

This conclusion had possessed the merit of simplicity. It spared them thought, and men in the North frequently mistake that which spares thought for justice.

Now the murderer, if murderer he was, declined to assist.

“Take it decent, Jan,” Bill complained, struggling to free himself from under Jan’s weight. “A man’s got a right to be hung clean. Don’t make a dog-fight of it.”

“I make vat I please!”

Jan lunged up. Mr. Taylor caught his coat; the coat tore from collar to hem. Jan came out of it like a shaggy animal shedding a damaged hide. He struck Lawson with one fist, Bill with the other, and sent Mr. Taylor staggering into the wreckage of the tent.

Then he ran.

He ran without coat, cap, mittens, or plan. He ran because every cell in him cried the one command older than law, honor, religion, or friendship.

To live! To exist!

Behind him Bill shouted. Lawson cursed. Mr. Taylor called upon him to halt in the name of organized society, though organized society at that moment consisted of three breathless men, a rope, and a dented kettle.

Jan floundered through knee-deep snow toward the river. The cold bit instantly into his hands and scalp, but he did not feel it. His heart hammered magnificently. The white waste opened before him, blank and immense. There were no trees on that high barren, no cabins, no rocks larger than a sleeping dog. The wind had shaved the land down to snow and iron.

Freedom lay everywhere, and shelter nowhere.

“After him!” Bill cried.

“After him yourself,” Lawson said, spitting blood. “My jaw’s gone adrift.”

The dogs solved the argument. The team had watched the struggle with bright-eyed interest, accepting it as an improvement in camp routine. At Bill’s call they plunged forward, traces tangling, bells jangling. Bill seized the leader’s harness and pointed.

“Fetch Jan!”

The leader knew Jan. Jan fed him, cursed him, and had once pulled him from river ice by the tail. But a command was a command, and pursuit was warmer than waiting. The dog bounded after the fleeing man, the team streaming behind.

Jan heard them and looked back. That look ruined him. His foot caught a ridge of wind-packed snow, and he went headlong. Before he could rise, the leader crashed upon him. Dogs boiled around his body, barking, licking, snapping in excitement. One seized his shirt. Another planted both forefeet between his shoulders. Jan roared and rolled, but tangled traces bound his legs more effectively than rope.

The men arrived in stages. Bill came first, wheezing; Mr. Taylor followed with one hand pressed to his ribs; Lawson advanced last, carrying the rope and the expression of a sailor who has discovered that the cargo is both dangerous and badly stowed.

They fell upon Jan again.

This time the dogs assisted. So did the traces. After a struggle that plowed a red-streaked circle in the snow, Jan lay face downward with wrists tied and ankles crossed.

“Now,” said Bill, sitting on his back, “maybe you’ll hang peaceable.”

Jan spat snow.

Mr. Taylor straightened what remained of his collar. “Before proceeding, gentlemen, I submit that the accused is entitled to a hearing.”

“Hearing?” Bill said. “We all heard the shot.”

“A hearing,” Mr. Taylor repeated, “is the ornament of justice.”

Lawson felt his swelling jaw. “Make it short. I’m freezing through.”

They hauled Jan upright and marched him back to camp. The tent lay flattened over Gordon like a dirty canvas grave. From beneath one fold protruded a boot. No one examined it. They had examined Gordon once, and in their present state one examination seemed almost professional.

The prisoner sat upon an overturned provision box, bound from wrists to elbows. Bill stood behind him with the rope. Lawson leaned against the upturned boat. Mr. Taylor took a position near the dead fire and assumed the gravity of a judge, though his left eyebrow was bleeding and his trousers were split at the knee.

“Jan,” he began, “you stand charged before this tribunal with the unlawful slaying of John Gordon, lately our companion and, whatever his faults, a white man.”

“Idiot man,” Jan corrected.

“The court will not debate the deceased’s intelligence.”

“He say I lie.”

“That may be entered as provocation.”

“He say my bear vas in a beer barrel.”

“Also provocation,” Mr. Taylor conceded. “But not, sir, justification.”

Bill scratched his beard. “Seems pretty plain. He shot Gordon. Gordon’s dead. We hang him.”

“Upon what law?” Jan demanded.

The question troubled them.

Law, in settled countries, is kept in books and courthouses and in the habits of millions. On the frontier it is carried in fragments: a Bible verse remembered by one man, a sailor’s rule remembered by another, a sheriff’s threat heard twenty years before by a third. These fragments are sharp enough to wound and too small to build with.

“The law against murder,” Bill said at last.

“Vere is he?”

“Who?”

“De law.”

Bill looked around the white horizon, perhaps expecting to see it approaching.

Mr. Taylor cleared his throat. “Law, sir, is an invisible principle.”

“Den hang me mit an invisible rope.”

Lawson grinned despite his jaw. Bill did not.

“We got a visible rope.”

Jan turned to look at it. His eyes narrowed. The instinct that had driven his legs now entered his brain and began rummaging there for tools.

“You cannot hang me.”

“Watch us,” Bill said.

“You need t’ree t’ings. You need a man.”

“We got one.”

“You need a rope.”

“Got that.”

“You need some place to hang him from.”

Bill’s eyes traveled over the barren.

There was no tree. The last spruce had been seen three days south, a twisted dwarf no higher than a rifle. There was no cliff, no cabin beam, no bridge, no projecting rock. The river lay under four feet of ice. The camp contained a flattened tent, an overturned boat, three sleds, cookware, bedding, dog harness, and two long oars.

Jan saw their faces and laughed.

It was a large laugh, extravagant and triumphant. It rolled over the snow and startled the dogs.

“Dere!” he cried. “Your invisible law forget somet’ing. You got no gallows. You cannot hang a man from de sky.”

Bill stared at the rope in his hands with personal disappointment.

“Could drag him,” he suggested.

“That ain’t hanging,” Lawson said.

“Could choke him by hand.”

“That is not the sentence,” Mr. Taylor objected. “The judgment, once adopted, should be executed with precision.”

Bill threw down the rope. “Ain’t this a remarkable country! A man can shoot another man and get off because there ain’t timber.”

Jan leaned back against the box, beaming. “De Nort’ has good law.”

For a minute the condemned man became the only cheerful member of the party. In civilization, technicalities are hated because they impede justice; in the wilderness, they may be the last thin thread by which civilization admits it is uncertain. Jan had found such a thread and wrapped both fists around it.

Lawson, however, was looking at the oars.

His life before the North had been passed among ships, where a missing mast, a broken spar, or a dead captain did not excuse a man from getting work done. He walked to the boat, pulled out one oar, then the other, and laid them upon the snow.

Jan ceased laughing.

“What you do?”

“Rigging,” Lawson said.

He lashed the blades together near the top, spread the handles apart, and braced them against two loaded sleds. The crossed oars rose into the gray morning like the legs of a gigantic compass. He added a short cross-lashing, tested the structure, tightened it, and tested again.

“There,” he said. “She’ll bear his weight.”

Bill’s spirits revived. “Knew the sea was good for something.”

“It vill break,” Jan said.

“Then you’ll fall down,” Lawson replied, “and we’ll rig it better.”

Jan looked from the oars to the men. The gallows was absurd—too low, too narrow, and built from implements that yesterday had propelled them through open water. But death has never insisted upon dignity. A falling stone, a fishbone, a fever, a pair of oars: the body is not particular about the tool that ends it.

The men moved toward him.

Jan gathered his legs beneath him and sprang.

The box shot backward. His tied arms struck Bill in the stomach. Bill folded with a grunt. Jan rammed his head into Mr. Taylor’s chest and sent him sprawling across the stove. Lawson seized the prisoner’s bindings, was dragged three yards, and clung like an anchor.

“Dog!” Bill gasped. “Fetch!”

The leader bounded in, seized Jan’s trouser leg, and pulled the opposite way. Jan hopped, kicked, crashed upon his side, rolled over Lawson, and came up on his knees. Mr. Taylor joined from behind, wrapping both arms around Jan’s neck.

“Sir! Submit!”

“I submit you into de snow!”

Jan surged upright with Mr. Taylor hanging from him. Bill tackled his legs. Lawson caught his waist. For a moment the four men swayed together, snorting steam, while the dog hauled steadily at Jan’s trousers.

Then they fell.

The gallows trembled. A pan dropped from a sled and struck Bill on the ear.

“Who threw that?”

“No one threw it,” Lawson said.

“Then this camp’s against me too.”

They bound Jan’s ankles more thoroughly, passed another line around his chest, and dragged him beneath the crossed oars. He fought every inch, clawing furrows with his heels.

“Five years!” he shouted. “Five years I cook for you! I mend your boots! I save Lawson in rapids! I pull Bill from overflow! Now you hang me because Gordon has a soft head!”

“Hard head,” Bill corrected. “Bullet didn’t go through.”

“It went through enough,” Mr. Taylor said.

Bill shaped the noose with care. He took pride in practical tasks and was offended by poor knots, loose loads, blunt axes, and executions likely to require repetition.

“Hold still,” he said. “You’ll get it crooked.”

“I vant it crooked!”

“A man ought to have some respect for the work.”

The loop passed over Jan’s head and settled against his neck. His face changed. Rage remained, but beneath it lay naked comprehension. The rope was real. The oars were real. These battered companions, whom he had seen drunk, sick, foolish, afraid, and once weeping over a dead dog, were about to pull him out of the world.

To live! To exist!

He strained until the cords cut his skin.

“Bill,” he said quickly, “I give you my rifle.”

“Don’t bribe the hangman.”

“Lawson, I owe you t’ree ounces.”

“You owe me five.”

“Mr. Taylor, you are gentleman. Tell dese savages—”

“Had you expressed remorse,” Mr. Taylor said sadly, “I might have addressed the court in mitigation.”

“I express it. I am sorry.”

“For killing Gordon?”

“For not killing Bill first.”

Bill jerked the rope snug.

“All ready.”

Lawson and Mr. Taylor took hold of the hauling line. The crude frame creaked. Snow hissed across the ice in long pale ribbons. The dogs sat in a half-circle, tongues out, attentive to this new and puzzling labor of men.

“On three,” Lawson said. “One—”

The collapsed tent moved.

No one noticed except the leader dog, whose ears rose.

“Two—”

Canvas heaved. A mittened hand emerged, groped blindly, and seized the fallen stove pipe.

“Three!”

“Hold on,” said a muffled voice. “What in thunder are you doing?”

The hauling line dropped.

The tent rose in the middle. Canvas slid aside, and John Gordon crawled from beneath it on hands and knees. Blood covered one side of his face. His hair was scorched. A purple furrow ran along his skull and down into his collar, where the bullet had grazed, stunned, and abandoned him to darkness.

He sat in the snow, blinking.

Four men and one condemned prisoner stared at him.

Gordon touched his head. “Who knocked the tent down?”

No one answered.

He looked at the oars, the rope, and Jan standing beneath the noose.

“Are you hanging Jan?”

“We was,” Bill said.

“For shooting me?”

“That entered into it.”

Gordon considered this. “I ain’t dead.”

“We perceive that now,” Mr. Taylor said.

“Didn’t perceive it earlier, apparently.”

Lawson crossed to him and inspected the wound. “Skull’s sound. More or less.”

“I told you he had a hard head!” Jan shouted.

Bill removed the noose with profound reluctance. “All that work wasted.”

“Not wasted,” Lawson said. “Good rig. Might dry meat on it.”

Mr. Taylor untied Jan’s wrists. “Sir, in view of the resurrection of the injured party, the court has no alternative but to vacate its judgment.”

“Your court is fools.”

“That observation,” Mr. Taylor replied, “may be accurate without being courteous.”

Gordon got to his feet. He swayed, steadied himself, and looked at Jan. The northern cold had not left him mild. Nor had resurrection improved his temper.

“You shot me.”

“You insult my bear.”

“I said your bear was a lie.”

Jan stepped forward, ropes falling from his arms.

“Say it again.”

Gordon wiped blood from his cheek. “Your bear was a lie, your shot was poor, and your apology is due.”

“I apologize to no man.”

“Then fight.”

Bill brightened. Lawson dragged the kettle out of the way. Mr. Taylor lifted both hands.

“Gentlemen, surely the morning’s judicial errors suggest restraint.”

Jan rolled his shoulders. “I t’rash Gordon. Den I t’rash Bill for his bad rope. Den Lawson for his gallows. Den, Mr. Taylor, I t’rash you polite.”

Mr. Taylor sighed and stepped backward.

The two men circled upon the trampled snow, one bloodied from the bullet and the other striped by rope and dogs. Around them stood the dismantled machinery of justice: crossed oars, loose line, overturned box, scattered pans, and a tent still breathing smoke.

Nothing had been learned. Nothing had been forgiven. Law had appeared, delivered a speech, tied a knot, and collapsed when the corpse objected. Yet masculine pride, older and less complicated, remained ready for duty.

Gordon raised his fists.

Jan lunged.

And across the white emptiness, beneath a sky as cold and impartial as iron, the dogs began to bark.

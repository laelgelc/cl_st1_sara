### THE RING OF THE PYRENEES

There are certain passages in the infancy of nations concerning which the historian, if he be a prudent man, will say little, and the poet, if he be an honest one, will say whatever pleases him. Between the two lies the ancient chronicler, who, having neither the caution of the first nor the liberty of the second, commonly relates miracles with the gravity of a magistrate and battles with the particularity of one who arrived after the dead were buried.

Thus it is with the youth of Don Pelayo. The monk of San Millán says that eagles watched above his cradle; the Moor Ben Abenazar, who could have known nothing of the matter, declares that his first cry was heard across three valleys; while an anonymous writer of Oviedo, more temperate than either, merely assures us that the child bit through a silver whistle before he had teeth. From these testimonies we may safely conclude that Pelayo was born, that he possessed lungs, and that his later fame rendered his infancy interesting.

His father, Fafila, was of the ancient Gothic blood, a stern cavalier who held that virtue was best taught by hardship, and who mistrusted courts, perfumes, soft mattresses, trained musicians, and all meats requiring more than one sauce. He therefore sent his son, while still a stripling, among certain kinsmen who guarded the passes of the Pyrenees. There Pelayo learned to endure hunger without lamentation, frost without a cloak, and counsel without impatience; to climb where the chamois hesitated; to follow the bear through alder thickets; to cast a javelin from horseback; and to hear Mass before breaking his fast. His tutors were old warriors, mountain priests, hunters scarred by boars, and women of severe piety, who praised him seldom and fed him abundantly whenever he returned wounded.

At eighteen he was tall, broad in the shoulder, and spare at the waist; his hair was brown and somewhat unruly, his eyes grey, and his countenance open, though grave when occasion required. He had never been vanquished in wrestling, had never broken his word, and—if our chronicler is to be credited—had reached manhood without sighing once for any donzella. This last achievement, being more difficult than the others and less useful in war, is recorded with particular admiration.

Now it happened one day, toward the decline of summer, that Pelayo rode forth with four young hunters in pursuit of a great dun-coloured stag which had long mocked the valleys. They were clad in leathern jerkins, with short swords at their sides, hunting-spears in hand, and small round bucklers slung behind their shoulders. Pelayo rode a black mountain horse called Bravonel, whose sure feet were more valuable among precipices than the pedigree of any palfrey in Toledo.

The stag led them across a torrent, through a forest of beech and chestnut, and at length into a rocky defile so narrow that two horsemen could scarcely pass abreast. There the beast vanished, as famous stags are accustomed to do when they have discharged their duties toward romance. Pelayo had dismounted to examine a broken fern when there came from below the clatter of stones, the crashing of branches, and the gasping of a man who runs with death behind him.

Presently a fellow burst into view, bareheaded, with torn sleeves, one shoe missing, and blood upon his cheek. Seeing armed men, he cried out, crossed himself, turned about, and would have fled the way he had come had not Pelayo caught him by the girdle.

“Stand, friend,” said the young cavalier. “We are hunters, not enemies.”

“Hunters!” exclaimed the fellow, rolling his eyes upon their spears. “So called the last company, Señor, and they hunted my master’s purse.”

When he had recovered sufficient breath—and this required some patience—he declared himself to be Étienne, servant to Master Guilhem de Roquefort, a wealthy merchant of Bordeaux, who was travelling with his wife, his daughter, two clerks, six attendants, and three laden mules toward Jaca. They carried fine cloth, spice, silver plate, and certain strong wines, to which Étienne attributed virtues scarcely inferior to those of relics. At the lower end of the defile they had been surprised by Gascon Bandoleros, who killed one guard, wounded another, seized the party, and were then disputing whether the merchant should be ransomed, robbed, or hanged.

“How many villains?” asked Pelayo.

“Twenty at least.”

The ancient chronicler says fourteen; a later copyist, either more patriotic or worse at figures, increases them to forty. We shall content ourselves with twelve, which are quite enough for any reasonable ambuscade.[1]

Pelayo turned to his companions.

“Ride back to the valley. Sound the horn and gather our men.”

“And you?” asked the eldest.

“I will keep the pass.”

The servant clutched his sleeve. “Señor, you have not understood. They are Gascons.”

“I understood that they were robbers.”

“But Gascons, Señor! Men of desperate pride. Each thinks himself descended from Charlemagne.”

“Then their descent has been rapid,” said Pelayo, and mounted Bravonel.

He ordered two hunters to hasten for aid and two to climb the ridge and show themselves among the trees, as if they were the advance of a greater company. Then, taking Étienne as guide, he descended the pass at a trot.

Below, the defile widened into a stony basin shaded by black pines. The travellers had been driven against the face of a cliff. Master Guilhem, a round and richly dressed burgher with a beard divided into two careful points, stood between his family and the robbers, endeavouring at once to protect his daughter, conceal his purse, and preserve his dignity. His wife prayed aloud. His clerks sat bound beside the baggage. The surviving attendants had been disarmed; while the robbers, in patched cuirasses and red caps, searched the bales with that contempt for commerce frequently displayed by men who live upon it.

Their captain was a powerful Gascon named Arnaud le Roux, distinguished by a scar across his nose, a wolfskin mantle, and a steel skull-cap. He held in one hand the merchant’s rosary and in the other a small casket.

“You call this all?” he demanded. “By my faith, burgher, the mules groan like bishops, yet your coffers preach poverty.”

“My wealth is invested,” answered Guilhem, with the mournful courage of a man compelled to discuss business at sword-point.

“In what?”

“In honourable enterprises.”

“Then honour shall pay your ransom.”

At this moment Pelayo rode into the basin.

“Set down what is not yours,” he called.

The robbers turned. Arnaud looked beyond him, first toward the pass and then toward the wooded ridge, where two figures moved conspicuously among the pines.

“Who are you,” he demanded, “to give laws in Gascon country?”

“This is not Gascon country.”

“Where a Gascon stands armed, there is Gascon country.”

It was an opinion popular among Gascons and accepted nowhere else.

Pelayo lowered his hunting-spear. “Release the travellers.”

Arnaud laughed and drew his sword.

The robbers spread across the path; yet the narrowness of the basin, which had assisted their ambush, now prevented them from surrounding the young cavalier. Pelayo touched Bravonel with his heel. The horse sprang forward. A javelin flew past his shoulder and splintered against the rock; another struck his buckler. Before Arnaud could step aside, the spear-point smote him upon the breast and hurled him backward over a bale of Flemish cloth.

The shaft broke. Pelayo cast it away, drew his sword, and reined his horse between the captives and their assailants.

“Cut the cords!” he cried to Étienne.

Three robbers rushed him together. He struck the first upon the wrist, thrust the second beneath the arm, and received the third man’s blow upon his buckler with such force that the rim was shorn. Bravonel wheeled, kicking one villain into the torrent. At that instant Arnaud rose from behind the bale. His cuirass had turned the spear, but blood ran from his mouth, and his pride—which was better tempered than his armour—had suffered grievously.

“Boy!” he roared.

He sprang at Pelayo’s bridle. Pelayo leaped from the saddle. Their swords met once, twice, and a third time; sparks flew in the shadow of the pines. Arnaud was stronger, but rage made him lavish of his strength. He cut downward with both hands. Pelayo stepped within the stroke, caught the descending arm upon his buckler, and cleft the Gascon’s skull-cap. The captain fell without a word.

At once a horn sounded from the ridge. It was answered by another farther up the valley, then another, until the mountains seemed filled with armed men. In truth, the second blast came from Pelayo’s returning hunters and the third was merely an echo; but echoes have decided affairs in which counsellors would have failed.

“The mountaineers!” cried one of the Bandoleros.

“They are behind us!”

“Save yourselves!”

Some fled among the pines; some scrambled over the rocks; two threw down their weapons and begged mercy. A young robber who had been guarding the captives attempted to strike Master Guilhem’s daughter before he ran, whether from spite or awkwardness is uncertain. Pelayo caught the blow upon his sword and struck the fellow flat with the pommel.

By the time the summoned hunters arrived, the victory was complete. One wished to hang the prisoners from an oak, another to cast them into the torrent, and a third—who had lost a cousin to bandits—proposed first the one and then the other.

“No,” said Pelayo. “Bind their hands and take their weapons. Let them carry the wounded man to the nearest village. If they return to robbery, the gallows will not forget them.”

Thus mercy followed valour, as it ought in chronicles, though it does not always find the road in life.

Master Guilhem embraced his wife, counted his mules, embraced his daughter, counted his caskets, gave thanks to Saint James, and then counted the mules again. Finding his family living and his merchandise substantially whole, he approached Pelayo with tears upon his cheeks.

“Most noble Señor,” said he, “you have restored to me my wife, my child, my servants, six bolts of cloth, four packets of cinnamon, a chest of silver, and two casks of wine such as are not made south of the Garonne. The debt is beyond calculation.”

“Then do not calculate it,” replied Pelayo.

Guilhem nevertheless opened the casket Arnaud had taken and displayed a quantity of gold pieces.

“Accept these.”

“I did not fight for gold.”

“Then accept them because you did not.”

Pelayo smiled and closed the lid. “Give something to the family of your slain guard. Pay the physician of the wounded. If anything remains, offer prayers for those who died badly.”

The merchant stared at him. It is doubtful whether Pelayo’s courage or his refusal of payment seemed the greater prodigy. A rich burgher can comprehend a man who risks his life for a treasure; but one who rescues treasure and will not touch it approaches the supernatural.

The merchant’s daughter had meanwhile remained close beside her mother. Her name was Alix, though the Spanish manuscript calls her Alicia and an otherwise careful Moorish translation refers to her throughout as “the Frankish pearl.” She was in her seventeenth year, slender and fair, with dark-blue eyes and hair the colour of ripe chestnuts. Terror had left her pale; gratitude now brought a soft colour to her cheek. During the combat she had watched Pelayo with hands clasped upon her breast, and when he refused her father’s gold, she lowered her eyes, for something more perilous than robbers had taken possession of her peace.

Pelayo, who could read a broken trail upon granite, did not read this.

The company could not continue that day. One mule had gone lame, dusk approached, and the surviving attendants started whenever a pine cone fell. Pelayo therefore led them to a grassy glen known to the hunters, where a spring flowed beneath an ancient oak and a hermit had built his cell against the shelter of the rock.

The holy man was called Brother Aurelio. He had a white beard, a patched brown habit, and eyes of surprising brightness. He received the travellers without question, as men who have renounced the world commonly receive the world when it comes hungry to their door. His dwelling contained a chapel, a rude chamber, a shed, and a garden in which cabbages struggled heroically against stones.

The hunters raised shelters of boughs. Brother Aurelio produced black bread, goat’s cheese, onions, and honey. Master Guilhem, recovering his spirits with a promptitude worthy of Bordeaux, contributed a ham, two capons, a jar of olives, sugared almonds, and one of the rescued wine casks. Before an hour had passed, the scene of peril had become a feast; for grief may travel on an empty stomach, but gratitude in Gascony prefers supper.

Pelayo ate little. He saw that the wounded were tended, that the women had blankets, and that watches were posted along the paths. Alix observed all these things; indeed, she observed whatever he did, whether he carried wood, spoke with the hermit, tightened Bravonel’s girth, or merely stood beneath the oak looking toward the darkening mountains. The smallest action of an indifferent man is a weighty text to a loving maiden.

After the meal, she found courage to approach him by the spring.

“Señor,” she said, “my father tells me you will take no reward.”

“I have already received one, donzella.”

“What reward?”

“To see fear depart from innocent faces.”

This answer, which Pelayo offered simply, was precisely the answer a cavalier in a chanson ought to make; and Alix, who had read several chansons, was lost beyond rescue.

“You speak as one already knighted.”

“I am not yet worthy of that honour.”

“You faced twelve armed men.”

“Had I counted them first, perhaps I should have been wiser.”

She smiled, then grew grave. “Were you not afraid?”

“Yes.”

This confession surprised her.

“Yet you came.”

“Fear warns the body. Duty commands the man.”

Having said this, Pelayo bowed and departed to inspect the watch, leaving Alix beside the spring with a heart at once comforted, troubled, exalted, and very young.

The moon rose above the glen. The merchant slept loudly beneath an awning; his wife and daughter rested within the hermit’s chamber; the servants lay near the embers; and the hunters kept watch by turns. Pelayo, unable to sleep, knelt in the small chapel, where a lamp burned before a wooden crucifix.

When he came forth, Brother Aurelio sat beneath the oak.

“Sit beside me, hijo-dalgo,” said the hermit.

Pelayo obeyed. For a while neither spoke. The moon silvered the spring, the rocks, and the distant summits; an owl cried from the forest; the wind moved through the branches like the murmur of an unseen congregation.

“You have learned to conquer men,” said Aurelio at last. “Have you learned to conquer yourself?”

“No, father.”

“A good answer. He who says yes has not begun.”

The hermit took Pelayo’s right hand and examined it, not in the manner of those women who read fortunes at fairs, but with the solemnity of a priest considering some inward sign.

“Dark years approach,” he said. “Beyond the sea, armies are gathering. Crescent banners shall cross the water; cities shall open or be broken; palaces shall burn; bells shall fall silent; churches shall become stables; princes shall betray princes, and the pride of the Goths shall be scattered like chaff before the south wind.”

Pelayo looked toward the sleeping camp. “When?”

“Soon. So soon that men now feasting shall hunger, men now crowned shall hide their names, and children now unborn shall inherit exile.”

The hermit rose. His voice strengthened until it seemed no longer the voice of a solitary man, but that of the mountain itself.

“Yet Spain shall not perish! From caves, forests, crags, and northern rains shall rise a remnant. The cross shall be lifted where all believe it cast down. A handful shall withstand a multitude; faith shall make a fortress of the rock; and from one small victory shall grow a labour of generations.”

He laid his hand upon Pelayo’s head.

“You shall have part in this redemption.”

Pelayo bowed beneath the benediction, trembling now more deeply than he had trembled before the bandits.

“I am young, father, and know little.”

“Therefore prepare. Go first as a pilgrim. Kneel at holy shrines; sleep among the poor; learn how wide the Christian world is, and how small a man appears within it. Then take service under honourable captains. Learn the ordering of horse and foot, the keeping of a stronghold, the temper of nobles, the griefs of peasants, the cost of victory, and the danger of ambition. Keep your hand clean of cruelty, your heart clean of pride, and your soul clean of despair.”

“What sign shall tell me the hour?”

“You will need none. Calamity carries its own trumpet.”

The wind passed suddenly through the oak, scattering pale leaves around them. Brother Aurelio lifted both hands.

“Go forth, Pelayo! Gold will be offered you; refuse bondage to it. Women will love you; do not purchase pleasure with broken faith. Kings will command you; obey while honour permits. Enemies will mock you; endure until endurance becomes sin. And when your country lies wounded, remember these mountains. They have taught you that what stands highest must endure the storm.”

Pelayo knelt upon the grass.

“With God’s grace,” he said, “I will remember.”

At dawn the glen was bright with birdsong and the bustle of departure. The servants loaded the mules; Master Guilhem supervised every knot; his wife received the hermit’s blessing; and Pelayo’s hunters brought news that the road westward was clear. Brother Aurelio advised the travellers to lodge that night with a brotherhood at a fortified priory beyond the next pass. Pelayo undertook to conduct them there.

The journey was peaceful. They crossed slopes purple with heather, traversed pinewoods where shafts of sunlight fell like golden lances, and climbed a high ridge from which the merchant could see, far below, the safer country toward which they journeyed. He praised the prospect chiefly because no robber was visible upon it.

Alix rode a grey palfrey beside her mother, speaking little. Once, where the path narrowed along a precipice, Pelayo took her bridle and guided the animal across. His hand rested only a moment near hers, but she remembered that moment for more years than he remembered the road.

By afternoon they reached the priory, a square, strong building girdled with orchards and a low wall. The prior welcomed them; the gates stood open; armed lay brothers guarded the road; and it was plain that Pelayo’s protection was no longer required.

Master Guilhem drew him aside.

“Come with us to Bordeaux,” he urged. “My house shall be yours. I have ships, warehouses, friends at court, and cooks whom princes envy. You shall want for nothing.”

“That is what I fear,” said Pelayo pleasantly. “I must go elsewhere.”

“Then name some service.”

“Remember the dead guard’s household.”

“I shall.”

“And pray for Spain.”

Guilhem promised both, though he understood the first obligation better than the second.

Pelayo had turned toward his horse when Alix approached. She wore a blue mantle, and though her face was composed, her hands betrayed her by their trembling. Her mother remained at a distance, watching with the grave compassion of one who knows that certain griefs cannot be prevented without making them greater.

“Señor Pelayo,” said Alix, “will you not take one remembrance from those whose lives you saved?”

He began to repeat that he desired no reward; but she drew from her finger a slender ring of gold, set with a small green stone.

“It is not payment,” she said quickly. “It belonged to my grandmother. Take it only as a token that somewhere beyond these mountains there are those who will pray for you.”

Pelayo accepted the ring.

“I will keep it,” he said, “in remembrance of you, your father, and all who shared danger with us.”

The answer was innocent, fraternal, and fatal.

“Always?” she whispered.

“Always, Amiga, unless death takes it from me.”

He placed it upon a cord beneath his tunic, bowed, and mounted Bravonel. Then, with a final salutation to the family, he rode away beside his hunters, his figure diminishing along the mountain road until the pines received him.

Alix remained at the gate long after he had vanished.

Here the adventure ends; but the chroniclers, who are seldom willing to release a beautiful damsel at the proper moment, have preserved something of her later history. She returned to Bordeaux and refused, in due season, the addresses of merchants, magistrates, one minor count, and a troubadour who composed thirty-seven stanzas upon her cruelty. She did not enter a convent, nor did she retreat altogether from the world. She cared for her parents, relieved the poor, instructed young women, and maintained throughout her life that fidelity of the soul may exist without possession, bargain, or hope.

In her mature years she composed a small book entitled *The Mirror of the Faithful Heart*, in which were meditations upon love, chivalry, temptation, mercy, and the danger of mistaking gratitude for destiny. Certain passages have a tenderness uncommon in theological works; others contain such stern warnings against handsome young cavaliers that one suspects the author had not forgotten the original occasion.

As for the ring, the monk of San Millán affirms that Pelayo wore it beneath his cuirass in every battle. The historian of Oviedo says it was found upon him in old age. Ben Abenazar declares that it shone like an emerald star on the morning of Spain’s first deliverance. Whether any of these reports be true, I cannot determine. It is enough that Alix believed him faithful to his promise, and that Pelayo, who remembered the gift with gratitude but never understood its full confession, went onward toward pilgrimage, service, calamity, and renown.

Thus Providence sometimes joins two hearts only in memory: the one to be strengthened for the deeds of history, the other to be refined by a love which asks no earthly return. And if the tale appears too perfect for fact, we must recall that facts themselves, after lying some centuries in monastic ink, acquire a marvellous inclination toward perfection.

## THE IRON CANVASSER

The publishing firm of Sloper and Dodge had made its reputation by selling books which nobody wished to read to persons who could not afford to buy them. This is the foundation of the publishing trade, and explains why Mr. Sloper occupied a large office furnished in red morocco while many of his customers slept on flour-bags.

Sloper was a broad, bald man with a sanctimonious chin and a watch-chain like a ship’s cable. Dodge, his partner, was small, sharp, American, and so continuously surrounded by cigar-smoke that clerks entering his room frequently suffered temporary paralysis. Between them they had issued *The Imperial, Universal, Historical, Geographical, Geological and Domestic Atlas of the Civilised and Uncivilised World*, in six volumes, with an index, a supplement, and portraits of eminent subscribers.

The work was magnificent. It contained maps of countries that no longer existed, coloured pictures of fishes that never had existed, and statistics prepared with such care that every total disagreed with every other total on the same page. The retail price was twelve guineas, payable in weekly instalments extending far beyond the probable life of the purchaser. With each set went a presentation engraving of Her Majesty, a family medicine chest, a certificate of intellectual distinction, and, for immediate orders, a patent corkscrew.

Unfortunately, the Australian public declined to purchase it.

Sloper and Dodge employed twenty-seven canvassers to carry the gospel of the Atlas through the continent. Of these, three returned within a month. One had been tarred and feathered near Wagga; one had been attached to a stump-jump plough and harrowed across forty acres of light loam; and one had escaped from a deserted mine shaft by climbing a rope composed of his own order forms. The remaining twenty-four were variously missing, married, converted to honest labour, or lying low under assumed names.

The reports that reached the firm were discouraging.

“Dear Sirs,” wrote a canvasser from the Darling, “I regret I cannot continue in your service, having been bitten by the local vigilance committee.”

Another stated that the inhabitants of an inland township had listened patiently to the full description of the work and then escorted him seventeen miles into the bush without horse, boots, compass, or trousers.

A third had induced a selector to sign an order, but the selector subsequently discovered that the signature was binding. He pursued the canvasser with a shovel, recovered the order, and inserted it, together with portions of the canvasser’s coat, into the hollow of a burning tree.

“It is prejudice,” said Sloper.

“It is assault and battery,” said Dodge. “And it’s cutting into profits.”

They sat opposite one another in the office, separated by an account-book showing that forty thousand copies of the Atlas had been printed and eleven sold. Of the eleven, four had been returned, two had been exchanged for groceries, and one subscriber was suing the firm on the ground that the map of Queensland had frightened his children.

“We require,” said Sloper, “a canvasser of courage, endurance, address, persuasive eloquence, and Christian fortitude.”

“What we require,” said Dodge, “is a canvasser they can’t kill.”

At that instant the door opened and a little man entered carrying a black bag.

He had long hair, bright eyes, an alpaca coat, and the expression of one who has just invented perpetual motion and requires five shillings to complete it. He bowed, struck his bag against the doorpost, apologised to the bag, and placed a soiled card before the partners.

It bore the words:

**PROFESSOR SEPTIMUS WOMBATT**  
**Mechanical Genius, Electro-Phrenologist, Inventor**  
**Confidential Experiments Undertaken**

“I have solved your difficulty,” he said.

Dodge looked at him through the smoke.

“Which difficulty? We’ve got a selection.”

“The hostility of the public toward the travelling representative.”

Sloper folded his hands upon his waistcoat.

“You propose to soften the public?”

“No, sir. I propose to harden the representative.”

The Genius opened his bag and produced springs, brass wheels, a small hammer, several screws, and a sketch of an iron man. He spread the sketch on the table and tapped it with an enthusiastic forefinger.

“My Patent Automatic Commercial Advocate,” he said, “combines eloquence with invulnerability. It can traverse eighty miles per day, deliver a persuasive address of four hours and thirteen minutes without repetition—except where repetition is commercially desirable—resist dogs, stones, sticks, boiling water and ordinary firearms, secure the subscriber’s signature, detach the order, place it in a locked receptacle, and continue to the next residence.”

“Can it take a punch?” asked Dodge.

The Genius smiled pityingly.

“The frame is cast iron; the exterior is sheet steel; the joints are manganese bronze; and the trousers are padded with tarred rope. A man striking it would experience sensations comparable to kicking a municipal bridge.”

“Can it give a punch?”

The Genius became solemn.

“In self-defence only. Pressure upon the left breast-plate releases an eight-horse spiral spring operating the right arm. The return blow has been calculated at one ton, three hundredweight, less friction.”

Sloper glanced toward the door, as if expecting a procession of ruined debtors.

“Would that be legal?”

“Perfectly,” said the Genius. “The machine has no criminal intention.”

Within a week the partners visited an abandoned coach factory at the edge of the city, where Professor Wombatt had constructed the Automatic Commercial Advocate. It stood seven feet high and weighed nearly half a ton. Its head was painted pink, its eyes were blue glass, and its black iron moustache curved upward with a confidence rarely met outside patent medicine advertisements. It wore a frock-coat of boiler plate, steel boots, and a bell-shaped hat riveted to the skull.

Across its chest appeared the words:

**SLOPER & DODGE—KNOWLEDGE BROUGHT TO THE HOME**

A speaking trumpet occupied the place ordinarily assigned to the mouth. In its right hand it carried a specimen volume; in its left, an order-book and self-inking pen.

The Genius wound the machine with a windlass and pressed a stud behind its ear.

The iron eyes rolled. The chest clicked. One foot advanced.

“GOOD MORNING, SIR OR MADAM,” it proclaimed in a voice capable of addressing a public meeting during artillery practice. “HAVE YOU CONSIDERED THE MORAL, SOCIAL, COMMERCIAL AND MATRIMONIAL ADVANTAGES OF ACCURATE GEOGRAPHICAL INFORMATION?”

The windows shook.

“Reduce it,” said Sloper.

Professor Wombatt adjusted a screw.

The machine continued in a confidential roar.

“THIS UNRIVALLED WORK, PREPARED BY NINETY-SEVEN DISTINGUISHED SCHOLARS, NINE EXPLORERS, THREE BISHOPS AND A PRACTICAL TAXIDERMIST, PLACES THE ENTIRE GLOBE WITHIN REACH OF THE HUMBLEST HOME.”

It extended the order-book. Dodge took the pen experimentally. The iron fingers closed upon his wrist and guided his hand toward the dotted line.

“Here!” he cried.

“The collecting gear,” explained the Genius. “It assumes reluctance to be muscular hesitation and assists the subscriber.”

Dodge signed. The machine released him, tore out the order, posted it through a slot in its own ribs, and resumed:

“TO THE FIRST FIVE THOUSAND SUBSCRIBERS WE PRESENT, WITHOUT EXTRA CHARGE, A MAGNIFICENT STEEL ENGRAVING OF THE BRITISH EMPIRE, A HOUSEHOLD GUIDE TO ETIQUETTE, AND A COMPLETE SET OF FALSE TEETH, SIZES TO BE SELECTED AFTER DELIVERY.”

Sloper’s eyes shone.

“It is Providence,” he said.

“It is business,” said Dodge. “Where do we try it?”

Now, the prudent man tests a new gun where there is nobody to shoot, and the experienced publisher tests a canvasser where the population is too scattered to combine against him. They selected Ninemile.

Ninemile lay beside a brown creek in a fold of the western plains. It possessed a hotel, a police station, a store, a blacksmith’s shed and thirty-one inhabitants, counting a Chinese gardener who denied responsibility for the census. The town was named Ninemile because it stood eleven miles from everywhere. In summer the heat melted candles inside locked cupboards; in winter the wind removed roofs and distributed them among the neighbouring districts.

The inhabitants were celebrated for their repose. The publican’s clock had stopped in 1889, and nobody had yet found the energy to wind it. Smoke from the chimneys rose ten feet, reconsidered the matter, and lay down. Mosquitoes approaching the town fell asleep before biting and had to be carried home by relatives.

In this favourable market resided Angus Macpherson, land agent, commission broker, auctioneer, breeder of dogs and enemy of canvassers.

Macpherson stood six feet seven in his socks, though no man had ever seen him in his socks and lived to measure him. His hands resembled uncooked hams; his freckles were as large as half-crowns; and his red moustache projected on either side of his face like the horns of an irritated bullock. His ancestry, according to himself, went back through Robert Bruce, Julius Caesar and a second cousin of Noah who missed the Ark through a misunderstanding about the sailing date.

Macpherson had once purchased a twelve-volume history from a canvasser. On discovering that the last eight volumes consisted mainly of footnotes to the first four, he swore an oath extending from sunset until breakfast. Since then he had received commercial travellers with a stockwhip.

“He is the severe test,” said Sloper.

“He is the sale we need,” said Dodge.

Professor Wombatt and the two publishers arrived at Ninemile by goods train with the machine packed in a crate labelled AGRICULTURAL IMPLEMENT—THIS SIDE UP. It had travelled from the city upside down, but iron constitutions are less sensitive to railway management than human ones.

They lodged at the hotel under fictitious names. Next morning, before the population had begun considering whether to get out of bed, they wheeled the Advocate to the road leading to Macpherson’s house.

The Genius wound it, pointed it at the gate and pressed the stud.

“GOOD MORNING, SIR OR MADAM,” bellowed the machine, and set off at a steady march.

The three experimenters followed at a prudent distance behind a row of pepper trees.

Macpherson’s dog was named Presbyterian because it distrusted strangers and believed firmly in punishment. It was a brindled brute with the chest of a bull, the jaws of a rabbit trap and the private habits of a crocodile. Hearing the Advocate, it came through the fence without troubling the gate and seized the machine by the calf.

There was a noise like a saw entering hardwood.

Presbyterian bit through the steel cloth, removed six inches of tarred rope padding, and retired with its mouth stuck together. It rolled upon the ground, sneezed, and looked at the machine with astonished reproach.

The Advocate did not vary its pace.

“WITHIN THESE SUMPTUOUSLY ILLUSTRATED PAGES,” it announced, “THE READER MAY VISIT THE SNOWS OF THE ARCTIC, THE JUNGLES OF AFRICA AND THE PRINCIPAL RAILWAY REFRESHMENT ROOMS OF AUSTRALASIA WITHOUT LEAVING THE DOMESTIC FIRESIDE.”

Macpherson appeared on the veranda in shirt-sleeves.

“Clear out!” he said.

“THE CLIMATE OF PATAGONIA—”

“Clear out, ye boiler-plated nuisance!”

“—IS FULLY DESCRIBED, TOGETHER WITH THE HABITS OF THE LLAMA, THE CUSTOMS OF THE NATIVES, AND RELIABLE INSTRUCTIONS FOR REMOVING INK FROM CARPETS.”

Macpherson descended the steps. The earth moved slightly under him. He walked round the machine, observed the order-book, and understood its profession.

“A canvasser,” he whispered.

There are moments when an old wrong rises before a man with terrible clearness. Macpherson remembered the twelve-volume history, the instalments, and the footnotes. With a roar he struck the Advocate upon the left breast-plate.

It was a fatal mistake.

A spring snapped inside the machine. The right arm vanished and reappeared. There was a sound resembling a horse kicking an empty tank, and Macpherson travelled backward through the veranda rail, across a cane chair, and into a rainwater barrel. The barrel burst. Macpherson emerged briefly amid hoops and water, looked surprised, and fell like a fowl shot during an argument.

The Advocate extended its order-book toward him.

“THE PRICE OF THIS IMPERISHABLE MONUMENT OF HUMAN LEARNING IS LESS THAN THE WEEKLY EXPENDITURE OF THE AVERAGE FAMILY UPON PICKLES.”

Behind the pepper trees, Sloper removed his hat.

“Powerful,” he said.

“Too powerful,” said Dodge.

Professor Wombatt was pale.

“The spring requires moderation.”

Presbyterian, having freed its jaws by swallowing most of the padding, crawled beneath the house and abandoned public life. The three men hurried toward the machine, intending to switch it off. Then Sergeant O’Grady appeared at the gate.

O’Grady was a round, persuasive policeman whose courage was chiefly conversational. He could pacify two fighting men by explaining that Constable Dooley was coming, and could pacify Dooley by explaining that the fighting men had gone. Through these arrangements he had preserved the King’s peace for fourteen years without doing anything calculated to disturb his own.

He surveyed the wrecked veranda, the unconscious Macpherson, and the iron stranger.

“What’s this, now?”

The Advocate marched toward the road.

“GOOD MORNING, SIR OR MADAM.”

“Drunk,” said O’Grady. “And disorderly.”

Sloper, Dodge and the Genius silently withdrew behind a haystack.

O’Grady approached the machine with official majesty.

“Come along quiet, me fine fellow. There’s no use makin’ a noise about it.”

“DO YOU KNOW THAT THE AMAZON RIVER DISCHARGES A VOLUME OF WATER SUFFICIENT TO SUPPLY EVERY HOUSEHOLD IN THE EMPIRE WITH THREE BATHS DAILY?”

“Canvasser!” said O’Grady, stepping back.

He had faced drunken shearers, runaway horses and a deputation of ratepayers, but the travelling book agent touched an older fear. Still, Macpherson lay among the ruins, and the law must occasionally be seen from the hotel veranda to be doing something.

“In the King’s name,” said O’Grady, “I arrest ye for assault, drunkenness, damage to property, and speakin’ without reasonable excuse.”

He took hold of the Advocate’s left arm.

The Genius, watching from the haystack, made a small choking sound.

“What?” whispered Dodge.

“That operates the subscription grip.”

The iron arm swung outward, circled O’Grady’s waist and drew him against the machine’s ribs. His helmet fell over one eye. His toes barely touched the ground.

“Release me!” he gasped.

The Advocate marched on.

“NO HOME IS COMPLETE WITHOUT THIS TREASURY OF KNOWLEDGE. NO PARENT CAN NEGLECT IT WITHOUT IMPERILLING THE FUTURE OF THE CHILD.”

“Ow! Murder! Put me down, ye iron lunatic!”

O’Grady struggled, thereby increasing the pressure. The arm tightened until his uniform buttons left the force in several directions. One struck the hotel sign; another broke a window; a third was afterward found embedded in a pumpkin.

The machine turned toward the creek.

“Sign the order!” cried Professor Wombatt from concealment. “It may release you!”

“What order?”

“The Atlas!”

“I’ll buy it! I’ll buy two! Take the whole of me pay!”

But the order-book was in the Advocate’s other hand, beyond O’Grady’s reach. The machine’s internal pen waved cheerfully.

By this time Ninemile had awakened. Men emerged from the hotel carrying glasses they had not had time to put down. Women leaned over fences. Children followed at a distance, throwing stones until informed that the object was a canvasser, whereupon they found heavier stones.

Constable Dooley was asleep in the police station.

Dooley weighed seventeen stone and possessed shoulders broad enough to advertise on. In his youth he had thrown a professional wrestler through the wall of a tent; in middle age he preferred not to revive the habit, as violence made him thirsty and the Government allowance did not cover consequences. His courage was sound when fully awake, but he required forty minutes to attain that condition.

A boy rushed in shouting that Sergeant O’Grady had been captured by a talking boiler and was proceeding toward the river.

Dooley opened one eye.

“Tell him I’m busy.”

“It’s squeezin’ him to death.”

“Tell it to stop.”

“It won’t.”

Dooley sat up, buckled on his belt, put his boots upon the wrong feet, corrected the error, and came into the street carrying a baton.

The Advocate approached with O’Grady pinned to its side.

“THIS SUPERB ATLAS CONTAINS FOUR HUNDRED MAPS, EIGHT HUNDRED PLATES, NINE THOUSAND ENGRAVINGS, AND A COLOURED DIAGRAM EXPLAINING THE BRITISH CONSTITUTION TO THOSE WHO HAVE NEVER SEEN ONE.”

“Dooley!” shrieked O’Grady. “Save me!”

Dooley planted himself in the road.

“Release that officer.”

“THE MAPS ARE PRINTED IN COLOURS OF UNEXAMPLED BRILLIANCE—”

“Release him, or I’ll use force.”

“Use diplomacy!” cried O’Grady. “Force is what started it!”

Dooley struck the machine’s arm with his baton. The baton broke. Half flew into the store; the other half remained in his hand and gave him the appearance of a man threatening a locomotive with a clothes-peg.

The Advocate kept marching.

Dooley seized it around the waist. His muscles rose like sacks beneath his tunic. For one glorious second the machine stopped. The townspeople cheered. Dooley heaved; the road cracked under his boots; perspiration issued from him in quantities sufficient for irrigation.

Then his hand slipped upward and pressed the left breast-plate.

It was the same fatal mistake, at a more public moment.

The right arm delivered its commercial rejoinder. Dooley rose from the road, described a curve of uncommon scientific interest, passed through a stack of empty kerosene tins and came to rest beneath the blacksmith’s horse trough. The horse looked in, decided against drinking, and went away.

“Good man, Dooley,” murmured O’Grady. “Try again when ye wake up.”

Nobody else offered assistance. Ninemile courage was not deficient, but it was selective. The blacksmith proposed shooting the machine, until the storekeeper reminded him that the sergeant was attached. The publican suggested a rope, then recollected that his rope had been lent to the Progress Association in 1894. Macpherson, recovering sufficiently to crawl to the gate, shouted advice which was impressive in tone but unfit for mechanical application.

Behind the haystack the publishers held a conference.

“Switch it off,” said Sloper.

“We must reach the stud,” said the Genius.

“You invented it,” said Dodge. “Reach.”

“My life belongs to science.”

“Our money belongs to that machine.”

The Advocate came to the slope above Ninemile Creek. The creek was neither wide nor handsome, but a recent storm upstream had filled it with twelve feet of muddy water, drowned timber and objects which no respectable stream would admit carrying.

O’Grady saw the river.

“Turn round!” he cried. “Station’s the other way!”

“THE WORK IS STRONGLY BOUND IN MOROCCO-GRAINED CLOTH, RESISTANT TO DUST, MILDEW, CHILDREN, TERMITES AND ORDINARY DOMESTIC UNHAPPINESS.”

The iron feet entered the mud.

Professor Wombatt ran from concealment at last.

“Stop!” he shouted. “You’re exceeding the demonstration!”

The machine descended the bank. O’Grady clutched its neck, its hat and finally its speaking trumpet.

“Holy saints,” he said, “I’ll sign anything!”

“THE FIRST PAYMENT OF FIVE SHILLINGS SECURES IMMEDIATE DELIVERY.”

Water reached its knees.

Sloper and Dodge arrived at the top of the bank, but commercial instinct restrained them from going farther. The Genius slid halfway down, grasped a bush and called technical directions which the machine disregarded.

“Pull the lever beneath the right shoulder!”

“I can’t reach it!”

“Press the red stud!”

“They’re all mud-coloured!”

Water reached the Advocate’s waist. O’Grady’s helmet floated away. The current took his boots next, which was remarkable, as they had been issued two sizes too small.

“Tell me wife,” he called, “that I died on duty!”

“You’re not married,” shouted Dooley faintly from beneath the trough.

“Then tell nobody!”

The machine marched into the deepest channel.

“AS A FINAL INDUCEMENT, EACH SUBSCRIBER RECEIVES ABSOLUTELY FREE A HANDSOME CERTIFICATE SUITABLE FOR FRAMING—”

Its iron hat disappeared. For several seconds the speaking trumpet continued beneath the water, producing bubbles of salesmanship. The bubbles moved downstream, still numerous and persuasive, and then ceased.

Sergeant O’Grady and the Automatic Commercial Advocate were seen no more.

There was some talk of recovering them. Dooley declined to dive on medical grounds. Macpherson declared the creek bottom Crown land under agency to himself and demanded a search fee. The publican organised a rescue committee, which met in the bar and remained there until its funds were exhausted.

Sloper, Dodge and Professor Wombatt left Ninemile that night on a hand trolley. They took the order box with them, the machine having ejected it automatically before entering the creek. It contained Macpherson’s involuntary mark, O’Grady’s fingerprints, and an order signed by Dodge during the factory demonstration. The firm delivered all three sets of the Atlas and pursued payment with its customary devotion to principle.

No complete report of the disaster reached the newspapers. Dooley prepared one, but fell asleep over the second page. The first page merely stated that the sergeant was absent from duty under circumstances requiring explanation, and the Department returned it for additional particulars. Before these could be supplied, Ninemile began a campaign to become the Federal Capital. Public attention was diverted, and O’Grady’s disappearance passed into municipal tradition.

For years afterward children were warned not to swim in the creek, because an iron man lived at the bottom and would sell them six volumes of geography. During floods, settlers sometimes heard a hollow voice beneath the bridge discoursing upon the mineral resources of Bolivia; but sensible persons attributed this to frogs, whisky, or politics.

Professor Wombatt attempted to construct a mechanical temperance lecturer, but made the unhappy mistake of testing it in a public house. The machine was destroyed, and the Genius, finding science ungrateful, took to drink. He died some years later while explaining to a lamp-post an invention for converting seawater into brandy.

Constable Dooley was promoted for conspicuous gallantry in attempting the rescue. He ultimately became an inspector and was respected throughout the service as a man who never rushed into danger until it had been officially reported over.

Sloper abandoned publishing, entered the ministry, and acquired a reputation for sermons of extraordinary length. Those who heard him preach declared that his gestures occasionally resembled the action of a powerful spiral spring.

Dodge entered Parliament, where his experience with automatic speech proved invaluable. He became a Minister, survived three scandals, and was eventually knighted for services which nobody could precisely remember.

As for the Atlas, it sold out within ten years. Every police station in the colony was supplied with a set, the Government having decided that accurate geographical knowledge was essential to the preservation of public order.

Ninemile received two copies.

One was used to raise the sergeant’s desk to a comfortable height.

The other was placed in the local museum, beside O’Grady’s recovered helmet, under a card stating that he had been drowned while heroically saving an unknown traveller from the creek.

Thus history is made accurate, provided it is strongly bound, handsomely illustrated, and sold to the public by persons whom the public cannot conveniently knock down.

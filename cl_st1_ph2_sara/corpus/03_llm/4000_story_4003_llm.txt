The Woman in the Green Gloves

Just beyond Croydon, when the backs of houses had begun to loosen their grip on the railway and allotments appeared—tin roofs, black beans, a child’s red pail overturned beside a row of cabbages—the woman opposite me lifted one shoulder, sharply, as if an invisible finger had touched her there.

It was not a shrug. A shrug belongs to conversation; it requires witnesses, even when it denies them. This was private. The shoulder rose, the mouth tightened, the chin gave a little answering jerk. Then all was still.

She had green gloves on. Not bright green: the exhausted colour of leaves pressed years ago between sermon pages. Her coat was brown and too heavy for the mildness of the morning. From beneath a narrow hat, several colourless hairs had escaped and lay across her forehead in damp, separate threads. She watched the window without seeing fields, roofs or telegraph poles. Or perhaps she saw them with unnatural intensity. That is the difficulty. The vacant eye may contain multitudes; the fixed eye may be empty.

Again the shoulder lifted.

There. Guilt.

No—rheumatism.

No—one of those minute bodily rebellions which begin in childhood and survive marriage, childbirth, funerals, and all the grand reforms of the soul. A twitch; no more. Yet it disturbed me. It made a crack in her composure, and through the crack something dark seemed to move.

I put up my newspaper.

Newsprint makes an admirable wall. Ministers may resign across it, earthquakes open, aviators fall flaming into fields; yet two strangers, each holding a grey sheet before the face, are saved from the naked indecency of being alive together. I read three times that there had been a decline in the price of eggs. Eggs down a penny. Housewives rejoicing. Farmers protesting. Thousands of smooth white ovals descending together through the columns, rolling under editorials, knocking against the King’s engagements.

When I lowered the paper she was looking at me.

Not looking, exactly. Her eyes had struck mine in passing and recoiled. She rubbed the thumb of one green glove against the forefinger. The gloves were mended at the seams. Poverty, then—not destitution, because destitution has no return ticket to Eastbourne and seldom owns matching gloves, but the respectable narrow corridor in which a penny saved from butter is spent on shoe leather, and a cracked saucer is kept for six years because Aunt Ellen gave it, and every knock at the door announces a bill.

Minnie Marsh, I thought.

The name came complete. One must distrust names that come complete; nevertheless there she was: Minnie Marsh. Miss, probably. No, Mrs. Widowhood clung to the coat somehow, to the black buttons, to the way she guarded the small handbag on her lap. Mr. Marsh had been dead eleven years. Consumption? Too romantic. A railway accident? Unfair to the railway, which was behaving impeccably. He had died of an illness that took eight months and required broth, thermometers, whispered consultations on the landing, and six guineas still owing to a doctor in Balham.

“Don’t trouble about me, Min,” he had said.

Naturally she had troubled. It was the one command she could never obey.

The train entered a cutting. Brick walls rushed up on either side, close enough to scrape away conjecture, and our reflections floated on the window: my hat superimposed upon a signal box; her face carried over dark brick; the green gloves resting in a cloud. A black speck on the glass settled exactly beneath her lower lip. It looked like a beauty mark, then a fly, then a drop of ink placed there by some censor to blot out a confession.

What had she done?

Because suffering alone does not produce that twitch. Suffering may make the hands heavy, the eyelids red, the body sag as if its strings had been cut. But this—shoulder, mouth, chin—was the body answering an accusation. She had done something, or failed to do it. Most guilt comes from failure. We do not murder; we omit the visit. We do not steal; we forget the letter. We leave the kettle on, the latch undone, the hard word unsoftened. Then twenty years pass, and still the shoulder rises when the train goes through Croydon.

She was going to visit her sister-in-law.

At once the house appeared: a semi-detached villa named The Laurels though no laurel grew there, only privet clipped into moral severity. Its windows shone with that hostile brightness peculiar to houses where cleaning is considered a virtue superior to mercy. On the front step stood Hilda—Mr. Marsh’s elder sister, broad in the bosom, pale at the lips, wearing a chain of jet beads that clicked when she breathed.

“You’ve come, then.”

“Yes, Hilda.”

“We expected the earlier train.”

“I couldn’t—”

“No matter. Wipe your feet.”

The door opened; linoleum; umbrella stand; the smell of furniture polish, boiled mutton and last Sunday’s lilies. A clock with brass pillars struck the quarter, each stroke a small official condemnation. Minnie, entering, would become at once too damp, too late, too apologetic. Her suitcase would strike the skirting board.

“Mind the paint.”

“Oh, I’m sorry.”

“You always swing things.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“No. You never mean.”

Excellent, Hilda. Almost too excellent. A sister-in-law should not announce her cruelty so efficiently. Cruel people seldom know their lines. They talk of weather, digestion, curtains. Revise her. Hilda kisses Minnie. Hilda says, “How well you look,” while observing the frayed cuff. Hilda has put fresh flowers in the spare room and later mentions what flowers cost. There: truth enters on quieter shoes.

The woman opposite opened her handbag.

Inside were a purse, a folded handkerchief, two keys tied with blue thread, a paper packet and a small book with gilt edges. Bible? Prayer book. Of course. Guilt had its equipment. She took out the paper packet and examined it, not opening it; perhaps a gift—sweets for Hilda’s children, though Hilda’s children must be grown by now. Twenty years old, twenty-three. One son in a bank, one daughter engaged to a dentist. Or the packet contained stockings, carefully mended, being returned. Or—

She put it back.

The prayer book remained visible.

Minnie went to church every Sunday, not because she found peace there but because she feared what might happen if she stayed away. God, in her mind, kept a ledger more exact than Hilda’s housekeeping book. Attendance: one mark. Wandering thoughts: seven marks against. Envy during hymn: two. Pride when complimented on the jam: three. Failure to feel properly repentant: infinite.

At night, kneeling beside the narrow bed in Balham, she tried to pray.

Forgive me.

The phrase opened like a trapdoor.

Forgive what?

She could not name it. If named, perhaps the thing would shrink. But unnamed it swelled, filled the wallpaper roses, sat upon the washstand, breathed behind the curtains. Her dead husband’s cough came from the grate. She had been impatient with him. There it was. One February night, after three months of broken sleep, she had wished—only for a second—that he would die.

And he had.

Nonsense, I told her. Wishes do not kill.

But she did not hear me. The dead are tyrants when the living lend them power. Each time she laughed, each time she bought herself a cake, each morning she woke refreshed, she had stolen pleasure from the man who had stopped breathing because, on a February night, she had wanted silence.

The train burst into light. A meadow flashed open, sheep arranged on it with the preposterous deliberation of ornaments; then a farm, a pond like a dropped mirror, two women hanging sheets. The sheets ballooned between them, dazzling, and for one instant I saw Minnie’s entire life washed and hung there—petticoats, apologies, widow’s black, the secret wish—flapping shamelessly over Sussex.

She twitched again.

“Do you find the window draughty?” I asked.

The words, once escaped, seemed monstrous. They crossed the compartment, stumbled over her knees and demanded lodging.

“No. Thank you.”

A pleasant voice. Younger than the face.

She glanced at the strap above my head. Conversation closed. My question had changed nothing, yet it had damaged Minnie. Her voice did not fit. I had given her a pinched little instrument, apologetic and dry; the real voice contained a roundness, perhaps humour. Very well, Minnie had humour. Why should grief forbid it? She laughed at cats. She imitated preachers privately. Once, alone in the kitchen, she placed a saucepan lid on her head and saluted her own reflection. No—too decorative. Remove the saucepan. Leave humour hidden, like a silver coin sewn into a hem.

The train slowed before a station whose name I missed. Doors slammed; feet hurried; a guard called something that broke in the air. A commercial traveller entered our compartment.

At least, he carried the proper case: brown leather, swollen at the hinges, initials stamped in gold. His moustache was so carefully shaped that one felt his domestic life must be untidy by contrast. He sat beside Minnie, breathed hard from his haste, wiped the inside of his hat, and said to nobody:

“Close thing.”

His knees opened over an extensive territory. From him came scents of rain, tobacco and hair lotion. He unfolded a newspaper, struck it twice to discipline it, and disappeared behind the financial page.

Now, obviously, he was temptation.

Not romantic temptation. Let us be sensible. Minnie was nearly fifty; he had a wedding ring and a small sore beside his nose. But temptation assumes many forms. Conversation, for example. Recognition. The bliss of saying to a stranger what cannot be said to a friend. He would lower the paper and remark upon the delay. Minnie would answer. By Haywards Heath he would know about Hilda; by Lewes, the February wish; before Eastbourne he would say, “My dear lady, everyone thinks such things,” and absolve her more effectively than the Church.

But he did not lower the paper.

Could I make him? Stories require cooperation, and human beings are disgracefully independent. His name was Mr. Pritchard. He represented—what? Brushes. Too comic. Surgical appliances. Too sinister. Biscuits, surely; solid, harmless tins of biscuits, illustrated with castles and Highland cattle. He travelled three days a week, loved his youngest daughter, disliked cucumber, and kept in a drawer a photograph of a woman not his wife. That last is cheap. Strike it out. He kept invoices in the drawer. Life is often cheaper than invention, but not in the same currency.

Mr. Pritchard turned a page.

Minnie’s shoulder jerked.

The newspaper trembled; he peered over it. Their eyes met.

“Beg pardon,” he said, though no offence had occurred.

“Not at all,” she said.

There! A dialogue, two doors opening—then closing.

Yet in the space between “beg” and “pardon” she had seen his kitchen: the blue teapot with a broken spout, his wife spreading margarine, the youngest daughter practising scales while rain crawled down the window. And he had seen Minnie’s room, its gas ring, its single armchair, the cup left unwashed overnight. For we do see such things, perhaps, in flashes too swift for acknowledgement. The soul travels faster than politeness; returns before the lips have finished their sentence; denies it went anywhere.

Or else they saw nothing. A woman twitched; a man apologized. Keep to facts.

Facts: her hat had a pin with a dull pearl head. The left glove bore three dark spots near the wrist. Her shoes were new enough to hurt. On the floor beside her rested a square parcel tied with white string. I had not noticed it before. There was writing on the brown paper, but her hem obscured it.

A present for Hilda.

No, for a child.

Whose child?

Hilda’s daughter, Rose, was to be married. That explained Eastbourne, the visit, the better shoes, the parcel. Minnie had been invited not from affection but necessity: families summon their neglected members at weddings as cupboards release ceremonial china—chipped perhaps, unfashionable, yet required to complete the set.

At The Laurels, Rose would receive her in the drawing room.

“Aunt Minnie! How sweet of you to come.”

Rose was smooth and pink, with the slightly glazed surface of an iced cake. Her fiancé, Douglas, had excellent teeth and no use for metaphysics.

“We’re so pleased,” Rose said, looking over Minnie’s shoulder to see whether the florist’s boy had arrived.

“I brought you something.”

“Oh, you shouldn’t!”

This phrase, universally understood to mean Let us see whether you should, would be followed by the cutting of string. Inside: six teaspoons. Electroplate, not silver. Minnie had saved for them since Christmas, setting aside sixpence when eggs fell, threepence when coal rose, nothing when the sole came off her boot.

“How useful,” Hilda would say.

Useful! The blade wrapped in velvet. The six spoons lay in their box with their narrow faces turned upward, reflecting six distorted Minnies.

At luncheon she would be seated beside Douglas’s uncle, who dealt in property and chewed with inward concentration.

“Do you live near here, Mrs.—?”

“Marsh.”

“Ah. Mr. Marsh with you?”

“My husband is dead.”

“Dear me.”

Then the uncle would look at his plate, inconvenienced by mortality. Hilda, at the head of the table, would ask whether anyone wanted more potatoes. Rose would say the church decorations must not include lilies because lilies suggested funerals. Everyone would agree too quickly.

Minnie’s hand would shake; gravy would cross the rim of her plate.

A spot. Always the spot.

On the train window it remained beneath her lip, though landscape streamed behind it. A stationary blemish through which England passed: woods poured into it, white houses, a horse, whole skies. I leaned slightly. The spot moved from her face to her throat.

Suppose the guilt was not a wish but a theft.

She had taken money from Hilda. Years ago. Five pounds from a desk while Mr. Marsh lay ill upstairs. She needed medicine—or believed she did; perhaps the doctor had already said medicine was useless. Hilda had suspected the maid, dismissed her, ruined the girl’s character. Minnie kept silent.

Too melodramatic. Five pounds, a dismissed servant, ruined character: they arrive carrying labels. Fiction likes consequences visible from the road. Life buries them behind sheds.

Make it smaller.

She took a brooch. No.

She lied about a letter. Better. A letter came for her husband from his brother, asking forgiveness after a quarrel. Minnie, fearing excitement, hid it. The brother died before they reconciled. She had meant to produce it later; later became impossible; the envelope remained for years beneath folded tablecloths. One day she burned it, and the flame went green.

Yes, that was the thing. Not murder, not theft: interference disguised as care. The most respectable sin.

“Let him rest,” she had thought.

Now no one rested.

The commercial traveller sneezed. Minnie started so violently that her parcel tipped. She caught it with both hands.

“Sorry,” he said.

“You can’t help sneezing.”

This time she smiled.

It was a quick smile, but astonishing. It did not illuminate her face—that weary phrase, as if faces were lodging houses with lamps—but altered its architecture. The guarded mouth widened; two fine lines sprang from each eye; for a second the years rearranged themselves, and she was not young, exactly, but continuous with youth. A girl had not vanished; she had folded herself inside the woman.

Mr. Pritchard smiled too.

“Hay fever,” he said. “Always get it this way.”

“Even this late?”

“Soon as I come south.”

“Odd.”

“Very.”

Finished.

But her “Odd” had been almost playful. Hilda receded. The hidden letter curled into ash. Was Minnie perhaps going to Eastbourne for pleasure? A boarding house; a sea view; shrimps eaten from a paper bag? No. The shoes, the parcel, the prayer book—one does not carry a prayer book to the pier unless one intends to drown, and she did not look theatrical enough for drowning.

She rubbed her shoulder.

An itch. It might simply be an itch between the shoulder blades, unreachable beneath coat and blouse, maddening in its persistence. How many tragedies are founded upon an itch misread by a stranger? Entire marriages, betrayals, nights of prayer—all because a label scratches the skin.

Scratch it, woman! I commanded silently. Put an end to us both.

She did not.

Instead she looked out, and I followed her gaze. The country had changed. Hedges ran lower; the sky widened; chalk showed white where earth had been cut. Rows of roofs curved over a rise like the jointed back of some enormous sleeping creature. On a road parallel to us, a cyclist laboured, vanished behind trees, appeared again, faithfully pursuing our speed with no hope of matching it. I loved him at once. He became a messenger. He carried the lost letter. If he could reach the next crossing before us, Minnie would be saved.

Faster! The wheels took it up: faster, letter, later, never; faster, letter, later, never.

Then the road swerved and abandoned him.

One grieves ridiculously for these disappearances. A face at a cottage window, a coat in a field, a dog running beside a fence—all are offered, snatched away, and continue without us. The dog reaches the gate. The coat goes home. The woman at the window lifts a kettle. But because we do not see it, their lives seem broken off, while ours, by favour of consciousness, pretends to be whole.

Minnie’s was not whole. I would complete it.

After luncheon at The Laurels there would be a walk. Hilda believed walking essential after meat. They would proceed in pairs along the promenade, the sea hammered flat and bright beside them. Rose and Douglas ahead; Hilda with the property uncle; Minnie behind, carrying an unnecessary umbrella.

“You must make an effort,” Hilda would say before they left.

“What sort of effort?”

“To be cheerful.”

“I am cheerful.”

“My dear Minnie.”

That “my dear”—a thimbleful of poison.

On the promenade, strangers passed: nurses with perambulators, old men polished by sea air, girls whose scarves streamed like declarations. Minnie would watch a gull hang motionless above the railings. How easily it did what she could not: remain suspended in the rushing world, held by invisible currents. Then it would turn, drop, become merely a bird fighting over paper.

Douglas’s uncle would fall back to join her.

“Fine air.”

“Yes.”

“Bracing.”

“Yes.”

“Live alone, do you?”

“Yes.”

“Don’t fancy it myself.”

Nor do I, Minnie would think. But aloud: “One becomes accustomed.”

At this point the uncle might propose marriage. No, really! Narrative greed had overreached itself. Because a woman sits opposite on a train, must every unattached man tumble into her path with an offer? Dismiss him. Let him inquire about rates and return to Hilda.

Minnie remained alone. That was important—not because loneliness ennobled her, but because it had become her climate. She carried it as towns carry weather: fog in the alleys, damp on the sill, a pale pressure over everything. In shops she spoke eagerly to assistants. At home she repeated their remarks.

“Cold for the time of year,” the butcher had said.

“Yes, very,” Minnie answered later, placing one plate upon the table.

She listened to wireless voices and sometimes disliked them for not knowing she was there. She left the hall light burning when she went to bed so that, waking, she might see proof of another room. Once a moth entered the lampshade and battered itself all evening against the glass. Its shadow enlarged upon the ceiling—a black, frantic angel. She could not bear to kill it. She could not bear the noise. At last she turned out the lamp and sat in darkness.

The woman opposite bent to adjust the parcel. Her hat brim hid her expression. The nape of her neck appeared, pale and vulnerable, with three little horizontal folds. Compassion seized me then—not the pleasure of pity, which warms the giver and freezes the recipient, but something harsher, almost panic. Here she was, enclosed. Blood beat in her ears; memories moved behind her forehead; somewhere within, perhaps, a child called, a door slammed, God leaned over a ledger—and I could reach none of it. Six feet of compartment were wider than death.

I wanted to say: You are not condemned. Whatever the letter, the wish, the five pounds, the unwashed cup—none of it has sealed you. Look, the sky has opened over the downs. Look, a boy is flying a blue kite. Look, even Mr. Pritchard has fallen asleep with his mouth open, and is therefore innocent.

But one cannot offer absolution to a stranger without causing alarm.

The traveller had indeed fallen asleep. His newspaper sagged over his stomach; his head nodded, recovered, nodded again, like a spinning toy making its last brave circles. Each time it fell, his moustache approached the print. Minnie watched him. She was amused.

So perhaps she was not unhappy.

Here the whole structure trembled. Hilda’s villa, Rose’s spoons, the February room—all balanced upon a mouth I had called guarded, a glove I had called poor, a shoulder I had called guilty. Pull out one gesture and the house collapses. But I propped it again. Unhappy people smile. Condemned prisoners laugh. Besides, sympathy once invested becomes stubborn; it dislikes a change in the evidence.

She took out the prayer book.

A photograph slipped from it and fell face downward.

She moved quickly, but not before I saw the white border, the grey shine. She picked it up and held it for a second. A man? The shape looked vertical, dark. Her husband, of course. Or Christ. Or a seaside hotel.

Her gloved thumb passed over the hidden face.

Then she returned the photograph to the book.

Proof!

Of what?

Of a photograph.

The mind is a shameless magistrate: it fabricates the testimony, browbeats the witness, then retires to consider its own verdict.

We were nearing Lewes. The castle ruins appeared and vanished; chimneys multiplied; washing hung above yards. Mr. Pritchard woke with a snort, consulted his watch and gathered his case.

“Nearly missed it,” he said.

Minnie moved her parcel to let him pass.

“Good day.”

“Good day.”

At the door he looked back, perhaps at her, perhaps to ensure he had not left his umbrella. Then he was gone, taking with him his biscuits, daughter, blue teapot and nonexistent invoices.

We had lost a character. One feels such losses physically. The compartment enlarged around his absence. His seat retained a shallow dent. Minnie and I sat opposite again, restored to the original problem.

At Lewes, a schoolgirl entered carrying a violin. She wore a navy cape and had rain-bright cheeks. The violin case lay across her knees like a small black coffin. Minnie looked at it.

“Do you play?” she asked.

The girl nodded.

“Is it very difficult?”

“Scales are.”

“I used to play the piano.”

“Did you?”

“A little.”

The girl, having exhausted her interest in adult history, turned to the window.

The piano! Immediately it stood against Minnie’s childhood wall, walnut, one ivory missing. Saturday lessons with Miss Crabb, whose breath smelled of violets. Minnie at twelve, hands hovering over scales while summer called through an open window. Her younger brother rolled a hoop in the lane. Her mother beat carpet dust from the curtains. Life had not yet chosen its narrow channel; it spread around her in shining shallows, possible in every direction.

She might have been a musician.

No, she played “The Blue Bells of Scotland” badly and stopped at fourteen. Must every abandoned lesson become a murdered destiny? We honour ourselves absurdly with lost possibilities. The book unwritten would have been a masterpiece; the voyage untaken reached islands; the man refused loved us forever. Perhaps the piano was gladly abandoned for hockey.

Yet Minnie’s fingers moved on the parcel: one, two, three, four, five. A scale remembered by the body.

The schoolgirl opened a book and began to read. Minnie watched her for another moment, then turned away. Was there envy? Tenderness? Nothing? Her face had resumed its first guarded stillness, but now I knew the smile hidden in it, the piano, the round voice. My Minnie had become crowded. She could no longer walk cleanly into Hilda’s house; too many alternatives tugged at her sleeves.

Perhaps Hilda did not exist.

This possibility was intolerable.

We passed a level crossing where cars waited in a neat, submissive line. Beyond it, the land opened toward the sea, though the sea itself remained invisible. One could feel it: light sharpened; distance turned metallic; gulls wheeled over a field with no business there. The compartment seemed to inhale.

Minnie checked her watch.

Anxiety returned. She would be met. By Hilda? No, Hilda never met anyone; she believed timetables a private responsibility. Rose might come, late and flustered. Or nobody would come, though Minnie had written twice. She would stand on the platform while families joined around her, kisses striking left and right like matches, until a porter asked whether she wanted a cab.

She would say, “No, thank you. I’m expected.”

The platform would empty.

Five minutes.

Ten.

At last she would carry the parcel out herself, refusing taxis because of the expense. The address—The Laurels, St. Anne’s Road—would prove farther than she remembered. Her new shoes would burn. At each crossing the parcel string would cut deeper into her fingers. By the time she reached the villa, Hilda would say:

“We thought you’d changed your mind.”

“You said someone would meet me.”

“Did I? Rose, did I?”

“I don’t remember, Mother.”

“I waited.”

“My dear, you should have taken a cab.”

And Minnie’s shoulder would rise. Not guilt now, but anger forbidden from becoming action; a little wave striking the wall of the body and thrown upward through the shoulder.

Yes! Anger explained it better. All those years she had been angry. At Hilda, at the dead husband, at God, at eggs, at the cheerful brutality of other people’s arrangements. Yet because she had been taught that anger made women ugly, she pressed it down, where it lived as a twitch.

The train began its final long run. Houses thickened. Gardens shrank behind walls. The girl closed her book and secured the violin. Minnie smoothed her coat, touched her hat, opened her bag, closed it, checked the parcel string. Preparations. The curtain rising.

I too gathered my things, but clumsily, watching her. My gloves fell; the newspaper slid under the seat. The headline about eggs stared upward between our shoes. Down a penny—how absurdly faithful it had been, travelling all this way with us, while sin and widowhood and Hilda rose and perished above it.

The brakes caught. Metal cried along metal. The compartment trembled; roofs slowed; a platform edged into view.

Now, Minnie.

Face it.

The train stopped.

Doors opened all at once. Steam, whistles, voices, porters’ barrows. The schoolgirl sprang out with her violin. Minnie rose, lifted the parcel, and for one instant stood uncertain in the doorway.

There—the abandonment I had foreseen.

Then a young man came running along the platform.

He was perhaps twenty-five, bareheaded, tall, his fair hair disordered by haste. He waved with extravagant relief.

“Mum!”

The word struck clean through Hilda, The Laurels, the dead husband’s hidden letter, the lonely plate, the moth, the February wish. Walls fell soundlessly. The six spoons scattered. God closed His ledger.

Minnie—if Minnie she still was—laughed.

Not the quick social smile offered to Mr. Pritchard. She laughed openly, and the guarded face I had studied for an hour broke into welcome.

“You’re late,” she called.

“Only two minutes.”

“Seven.”

“Train was early.”

“It was not.”

He took the parcel from her, then bent and kissed her cheek. She touched his sleeve with the simple proprietary tenderness of a mother verifying that her child is solid.

“What have you brought?” he asked.

“None of your business.”

“It’s for me.”

“It may be.”

“I knew it.”

“You know nothing.”

They began to walk away.

Her son.

Of course she could still be widowed, poor, guilty, commanded by a sister-in-law. A son does not disprove sorrow. Yet this son, with his easy arm briefly around her shoulders, altered every proportion. She was expected. She was loved. The parcel was perhaps a cake. The photograph—a boy in uniform, a school portrait, this young man at twelve holding a cricket bat. The prayer book might have belonged to her mother. The twitch might be an itch.

Bare facts rushed in, victorious and empty.

Woman travels to Eastbourne. Son meets her.

What a miserable little statement! Accurate, perhaps, and dead as a label tied to a toe. I stood on the platform while people streamed around me, ashamed not because I had been wrong—wrongness is common enough—but because I had mistaken possession for sympathy. I had taken her face, opened it like a cupboard, stocked it with my own stale provisions: widowhood, guilt, teaspoons, God. I had called the arrangement compassion.

She had known nothing of it. She had sat six feet away, carrying a life whose least morning I could not guess.

The young man said something. She struck his arm lightly with one green glove. They laughed again.

I followed.

Not deliberately—or only as one deliberately moves with a crowd when the exit lies ahead. They passed beneath the station clock, he carrying her parcel, she carrying the handbag. Around them flowed soldiers, children, women with flowers, men holding folded coats; each face sealed, each gesture opening for a second and closing. A porter wheeled trunks toward a cab. Two sisters quarrelled beside a timetable. An old man stood motionless while everyone avoided him as water avoids a stone.

Outside, the town received us in brightness.

Buses groaned; gulls cried over chimneys; shop windows flashed with shoes, clocks, oranges piled into pyramids. The air held salt, petrol, warm bread. Minnie and her son paused at the kerb. He looked left, she right, and this tiny disagreement delighted them. Then they crossed between a taxi and a delivery van.

Who were you? Not Minnie, perhaps. Not Marsh. You might be Mrs. Vale, Mrs. Cooper, Miss someone greeted in jest as Mum. He might be a nephew, a lodger, a former pupil. Even “Mum,” that simple hammer which demolished my palace, might be playful. Reality, having corrected me, refused to remain corrected.

They turned into a street where strips of sea shone between buildings.

I could have hurried. I could have passed them, seen the label on the parcel, heard a name. But knowledge gained by pursuit would be another kind of theft. So I let a bus come between us, enormous and red, bearing an advertisement for soap. When it moved on, they were farther away.

The woman lifted her shoulder.

The twitch again—guilt, anger, wool against skin, a message from some private depth. Her son did not notice. Or noticed and had always noticed. Or knew its origin and loved it as one loves the familiar crack in a cup.

They walked on.

And suddenly the town seemed full not of strangers but of interiors—thousands upon thousands of hidden rooms moving upright through the streets. Behind one face, a breakfast quarrel still rang; behind another, a tune repeated; behind another, a field in summer remained forever green because someone had died there. The flower seller remembered a red scarf. The policeman feared water. The child dragging a wooden horse had discovered yesterday that adults could lie. Or none of this was true. Better: none of it was mine, yet all of it burned there, inaccessible, prodigal, alive.

How poor the world would be if faces yielded at once, if the glass were clean, if every spot declared itself merely soot.

They reached the far corner. The young man shifted the parcel under his arm. She looked up at him as he spoke, and whatever passed between them changed her mouth again. Then they turned.

Gone.

But not ended. Never ended. Beyond the corner, the street descended toward the sea; doors opened; bells rang in shops; cups touched saucers; names were spoken which I would never hear. The parcel string was cut. Perhaps cake appeared. Perhaps spoons. Perhaps nothing I had imagined or could imagine.

Above the roofs, gulls tilted in the white air, vanishing whenever the light took them, returning dark-winged an instant later. The sea at the end of the street rose like a sheet of moving metal. People crossed before it—brief, black figures, each carrying the immeasurable cargo of a life.

Go on, then, unknown woman. Go with your green gloves, your twitch, your son who may not be your son. Take your sealed history into the shining town. Keep it. Spill it in fragments. Conceal it in laughter, in parcels, in the ordinary words—late, train, business, seven—which are trapdoors over infinity.

And you, city, swallow her; not cruelly, but as water takes light, breaking one brightness into ten thousand.

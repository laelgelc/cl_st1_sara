# HIS MAJESTY’S STAR

*“Where the word of a king is, there is power.”*

At six o’clock each evening His Majesty the King dismissed his court.

The ceremony took place in the nursery, a province measuring twenty feet by sixteen, bounded on the east by the bathroom, on the west by Miss Biddums’s chamber, and on the south by a white door beyond which the King’s authority ceased as abruptly as British law at a disputed frontier. Within his dominion he could order the wooden horse to be saddled, condemn a woolly bear to the corner, grant pardons to lead soldiers, and require Miss Biddums to tell the story of the mahseer that swallowed a General Sahib’s watch. Beyond the white door he was merely Toby, aged six, expected to wash his hands and make no noise.

“Now say your prayers, dear,” said Miss Biddums.

“I aren’t dear. I’m ve King.”

“Kings say their prayers too.”

“Not all kings.”

“All good kings.”

This theological thrust drove His Majesty to his knees. He knelt beside the cot in a nightshirt so large that it gave him the appearance of a very small penitent enclosed in a very considerable white flag, and petitioned Heaven for blessings upon Papa, Mamma, Miss Biddums, Patsie, the pony, the bearer, and the red ant whose hind leg he had injured with a match-box that afternoon.

“An’ don’t let Papa be cwoss,” he concluded; “an’ don’t let Mamma look at me like vat; an’ make me good, but not too quick, ’cause I wants to finish ve fort to-morrow. Amen.”

Miss Biddums turned her face toward the mosquito-curtain.

Children are selfish little grubs, as every mother declares when another woman’s child paws her new dress; but they are also detectives working without evidence, judges ignorant of law, and theologians who must construct Providence out of fragments overheard behind doors. Toby knew that Papa went to duftar, that Mamma went to dinners, that both belonged to the tremendous outer world of horses, uniforms, calling-cards, silver dishes, and voices lowered when he entered. He knew also that they did not like each other. He could not have explained this knowledge. No one had told him. No one tells a thermometer that the room is cold.

Papa was a District officer of some consequence, with a square brown beard and a manner that caused chuprassies to stand more upright. Mamma was beautiful in a pale, weary fashion, and possessed dresses which hung in the almirah miles and miles above Toby’s head. Between them lay a history of jealousy, injured pride, accusations half made and wholly remembered, and those small daily cruelties by which civilised persons avoid the vulgarity of striking one another.

How should a six-year-old understand that his father’s silence was not strength but resentment, or that his mother’s laughter at Burrakhana had been sharpened for one particular listener? He knew only that Papa’s mouth became thin when Mamma’s carriage returned late, and that Mamma sometimes tapped her foot when Papa spoke. Once, attempting to improve matters by placing both their hands upon his Noah’s Ark, he had said, “Now you must pway togevver.”

Papa had withdrawn his hand.

“Don’t be absurd, Toby.”

Mamma had laughed—not kindly.

He never repeated the experiment.

The executive government of His Majesty’s territories was therefore entrusted to Miss Biddums, a little governess with a faded complexion, a faded brown gown, and an inexhaustible treasury of affection. She had come to India with photographs of two sisters, a pressed fern, and the belief that patience received its wages in another world. In this world it received seventy-five rupees a month, prickly heat, and Toby.

She brushed his hair, repaired his morals, mediated disputes between him and the ayah, and understood that when he demanded the immediate execution of the nursery lizard, he was generally tired and required bread-and-milk. She alone knew that His Majesty feared the dark patch under the bath, where a dak-bungalow ghost was supposed to live, and that he must not be laughed at for pronouncing rhinoceros “winosserus.” She kissed him openly. This was of importance.

Papa kissed him once each morning if Toby happened to be present and clean. The kiss was laid upon the top of his head with the air of signing a file. Mamma sometimes remembered him before going to a Nautch or a dinner, when she would bend in a mist of scent and diamonds and say, “Good-night, small boy,” as though he were an amusing object borrowed from another household.

Miss Biddums did not ill-treat the child. Mamma herself had said so.

“I suppose he is comfortable?” she asked one afternoon, turning the pages of a novel.

“Quite comfortable, ma’am,” Miss Biddums replied.

“And Miss Biddums does not ill-treat him?”

“No, ma’am.”

“Then I cannot see what more he wants.”

Toby, who had been building a fort behind the sofa and was not supposed to comprehend grown-up speech, considered the matter gravely. He wanted Mamma to look for him when she came into a room. He wanted her to know that he disliked fat in mutton. He wanted to climb upon her bed during thunderstorms. He wanted—though the desire was too large and shapeless to be named—to be necessary.

Since none of these things could be placed in words, he added another gun to the fort.

His friend Patsie lived in the Commissioner’s compound, where there were two croquet-hoops, a guinea-pig, and a mother who behaved with a scandalous lack of dignity. Mrs. Larkyn would run down the veranda steps to meet Patsie, kneel regardless of dust, and say, “Where has my darling been?” She knew the whereabouts of Patsie’s bruises, the names of her dolls, and the day on which a particular loose tooth had first become loose.

Toby witnessed these indecencies with astonishment.

One morning Patsie appeared in a white frock crossed by a broad blue sash. It was tied behind in a bow large enough to govern a minor province. Mrs. Larkyn caught her as she ran past, kissed one cheek, then the other, and declared her the sweetest blue-belted person in India.

Toby looked at the sash. The world, hitherto confused, became plain.

“Vat’s why,” he said.

“Why what?” asked Patsie.

“Why your mamma loves you.”

Patsie, who was seven and possessed the cruel common sense of that advanced age, stared at him.

“She loves me when I’ve got my pink frock.”

“Not so much.”

“Just the same.”

“No. It’s ve blue wiban.”

“It isn’t.”

“It is. Mamma likes pwetty fings. I’ve seen her. If I had a blue wiban, she’d kiss me bof sides too.”

Patsie suggested that boys did not wear sashes. His Majesty replied that kings wore anything they chose, and the interview ended in a brief but spirited contest over the guinea-pig.

That afternoon Toby invaded Miss Biddums’s work-basket and found a length of blue tape. It was not broad, it was not silk, and it had been intended to bind the edge of a flannel petticoat; but faith, whether exercised by saints or children, has made more inadequate materials serve greater purposes. He wound it three times round his waist, tied the ends in front, and presented himself at the drawing-room door.

Mamma was within, lying on a long chair. The chick filtered the hot light into yellow bars. On the table stood a bowl of roses dying with expensive grace. Papa, near the window, held a letter.

“I do not question your right to correspond with whom you please,” he was saying. “I merely observe that Colonel Harcourt’s attentions are becoming conspicuous.”

“And I merely observe,” said Mamma, “that your observations are intolerable.”

Toby halted. The blue tape tightened round his middle.

Papa saw him first. “Well?”

His Majesty advanced through the great reflected wilderness of mirrors and polished furniture. His knees trembled, but sovereigns have gone to execution with no steadier gait.

“I’ve got a wiban.”

“So I see,” said Mamma.

“A blue one.”

“It is certainly blue.”

There should now have been an embrace, two kisses, and a public declaration that he was the sweetest blue-belted person in India. Nothing followed.

Papa’s eyes returned to the letter. Mamma said, “Run away, Toby. You look ridiculous.”

He stood still for one second. Then the blue tape, which had been a sash, became a fool’s cap tied around his body.

Miss Biddums found him behind the nursery door trying to cut it into very small pieces with blunt scissors. He explained that it was bad tape. She asked no more, because some women are born with the wisdom which others acquire only after they have lost the persons who needed it.

But two days later Mrs. Larkyn heard the story from Patsie.

“Why did you want the sash, Toby?” she asked when he came to tea.

His Majesty kicked the veranda railing.

“Did you think it would make you handsome?”

“No.”

“Important?”

“I’m important now. I’m ve King.”

“Then why?”

His mouth puckered. “Patsie’s mamma kisses her when she has it.”

Mrs. Larkyn ceased smiling.

“Mammas kiss children without sashes,” she said gently.

“Not all mammas.”

She drew him toward her, but Toby resisted. Sympathy, when it touches a wound too directly, has the first effect of salt.

“I don’t want *you*,” he said. “I want—”

He stopped, delivered of a few inarticulate sounds, and buried his face in the guinea-pig’s hutch.

Mrs. Larkyn spoke to Miss Biddums that evening. Miss Biddums listened, twisted her handkerchief, and said there was little she could do. To speak to Papa would be presumption. To speak to Mamma would be impertinence. Rank and salary are admirable devices for preventing the right words from reaching the right ears.

Soon afterward temptation entered the King’s dominions by way of a pink string.

A parcel lay upon the table in Mamma’s dressing-room. Toby had gone there in search of sealing-wax for a treaty with the nursery bear, and the room was empty. In daylight it appeared even larger than by evening: an acreage of toilet-table covered with silver-backed brushes, bottles, powder-boxes, rings, pins, and implements of mysterious use; a great, reflected wilderness in which a six-year-old might lose both his way and his virtue.

The parcel was small, wrapped in brown paper, and tied with pink string. Upon it was written Mamma’s name.

Toby touched the knot.

A servant had once told him that certain sadhus could send jewels through the air. Miss Biddums had told him that opening another person’s letters was wicked. The two doctrines met in his mind and fought. Curiosity reinforced the sadhus. Duty had only Miss Biddums. The result was foreordained.

He pulled the string.

Inside lay a velvet case, and inside the case a diamond star.

It caught the hard Indian sunlight and shattered it into points of white fire. Toby gasped. This was no brooch. It was a royal ornament, a fragment of the firmament, a sparkle crown intended—surely, unmistakably—for a king.

Under the case was a folded note. Toby could read printed words and certain written ones, but the sloping hand defeated him after “To-night,” “garden gate,” and a name he did not know. The note possessed no brilliance. He dropped it behind the toilet-table.

The star he carried away.

For one glorious hour His Majesty wore the sparkle crown pinned crookedly to his nightshirt. He held durbar before the wooden horse, created the bear Duke of Bath, and commanded Miss Biddums’s pincushion to pay tribute. Then conscience entered without knocking.

“Where did you get that?” Miss Biddums called from the adjoining room.

Toby tore the star away and thrust it into a crack behind the battered tin trunk that contained discarded toys.

“Nowhere.”

The answer frightened him more than the theft.

At prayers he stumbled over “forgive us our trespasses.” Hell, according to the bearer, was a place where liars were boiled in oil by black devils with green tongues. According to Miss Biddums, it was separation from God. Toby preferred neither account. He lay awake listening for celestial police.

No one asked about the parcel.

The next morning Mamma’s dressing-room was swept. The brown paper vanished. The note remained where it had fallen, and the star remained behind the trunk. No cry arose, no inquiry was made, no sepoy surrounded the nursery. This neglect, far from comforting His Majesty, increased the acuteness of the discomfort. If the Sirkar had marched in and demanded the jewel, he might have confessed, been sentenced, and perhaps pardoned before tea. Instead, the machinery of justice slept, leaving him alone with omniscience.

He ceased to play with the trunk. He disliked Miss Biddums’s stories about thieves. When Patsie accused him, quite falsely, of stealing her red marble, he struck her and then wept for twenty minutes. He hid bread under his pillow in preparation for exile. He asked whether people who stole diamonds could still go to Heaven if they were only six.

“Why do you ask?” said Miss Biddums.

“Because ve Bible doesn’t say six.”

“Have you taken anything, Toby?”

“No!”

The lie came quickly now, but each lie was a brick added to the prison.

Weeks passed. The hot weather settled upon the station. Punkahs flapped with the languor of exhausted birds; the compound baked white; crows sat with open beaks beneath the tamarinds. Papa worked longer at duftar. Mamma accepted more invitations. Colonel Harcourt’s name was not mentioned, which meant that it occupied a great deal of space.

The note behind the toilet-table had invited Mamma to meet its writer at the old garden gate on the night the parcel arrived. Had she received the star, pride, loneliness, anger, or mere curiosity might have carried her there. She had not received it. Colonel Harcourt waited in the dark, interpreted absence as a refusal, and within a fortnight departed on shooting leave after mahseer and forgetfulness.

Thus His Majesty, having committed theft, obstructed an intrigue. Providence is not always careful in the choice of its instruments.

But the instrument suffered.

Toby grew quiet. A quiet child is praised by adults who have forgotten that stillness in childhood is often no more natural than silence in a kennel. Mamma observed at dinner that the boy seemed improved.

“He no longer rushes about the passage,” she said.

Papa answered, “Miss Biddums is perhaps earning her salary.”

Miss Biddums, in the nursery, touched Toby’s forehead and found it hot.

By midnight the fever had taken command.

It swept through the King’s territories, abolished law, dissolved ceremony, and flung down the walls between memory and fear. Toby fought green-tongued devils under the cot, ordered Colonel Harcourt away from Mamma’s gate, demanded a blue sash, and begged Miss Biddums not to tell the police. The doctor came, smelled of tobacco and carbolic, and spoke in the veranda with a gravity that penetrated even the parents’ armour.

Mamma entered the nursery before dawn.

She had not been there at that hour since Toby was a baby. The familiar room looked altered: poorer, smaller, and more sacred. Miss Biddums sat beside the cot, her hair untidy, one hand imprisoned in Toby’s burning fingers.

“What does he mean about a crown?” Mamma whispered.

“I don’t know.”

“And the police?”

“I don’t know that either.”

Toby opened his eyes. Fever made Mamma’s face swim above him like the moon in disturbed water.

“Don’t let ’em boil me,” he said.

“No one shall hurt you.” She bent nearer, awkwardly at first. “Toby, darling, what is it?”

He heard the word *darling*. It might have purchased silence from a stronger criminal.

“I’m a fief.”

“A what?”

“A fief—a wicked fief. I took ve sparkle crown.”

Papa had entered behind her. “What crown?”

“Ve star. Mamma’s star. Pink string. I opened it, an’ I lied, an’ God knows, an’ ve devils know, an’ Miss Biddums didn’t but now she does.”

His breath caught in a sob. Mamma sat upon the bed.

“Where is it, Toby?”

“Behind ve twunk. Don’t send me away. I’ve got bwead for going away, but it’s old now.”

Miss Biddums moved the tin trunk. Her hand found the crack; from dust, dead beetles, and the abandoned foundations of three childish empires she drew a diamond star.

Papa took it.

Recognition came first to Mamma, then terror. “Where is the note?”

“Behind your big table,” Toby murmured. “It wasn’t shiny.”

Papa went himself to the dressing-room. He returned with the paper, grey with dust along one edge. He read it near the lamp.

There are moments when a whole marriage, with its first tenderness, its later evasions, its unspoken injuries, and its final dreadful possibilities, may be compressed into a sheet no larger than a man’s hand. Papa read the invitation once and then again.

“You meant to go?” he asked.

Mamma’s face had become colourless.

“I never saw it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

She looked at Toby, tossing beneath the net.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Perhaps. I was angry enough. Lonely enough.”

“Lonely?”

“Yes—lonely. You had condemned me before I had done anything. After a time condemnation becomes an instruction.”

Miss Biddums rose. “Shall I take the child—”

“No,” said Mamma and Papa together.

That small unanimity startled them.

Papa folded the note, but his hands were not steady. “I thought you despised everything I cared for.”

“I thought you cared only for being obeyed.”

“I was jealous.”

“You were cruel.”

“So were you.”

“Yes.”

The feverish child between them began to cry because the devils had returned. Mamma lifted him. For the first time within his memory he found himself held against her, not displayed, not greeted, but held. Her cheek was wet.

“You shan’t go away,” she said. “Do you hear? Never for this.”

“I’m black wiv sin.”

“Then we are all black with it,” Papa said.

This was bad theology perhaps, but good confession.

Toby struggled to understand. He had stolen the star; Mamma had lost the note; Papa had found both; yet no one spoke of prison. Instead Mamma pressed her lips to his forehead again and again, while Papa knelt beside the cot and hid his face for a moment in the little hot hand.

“Are you bof cwoss?” Toby whispered.

“Not with you,” said Papa.

“Wiv each uvver?”

A long silence followed. Adults can maintain wars for years rather than answer a direct question from a child.

Papa looked at Mamma. “I don’t want to be.”

“Nor I,” she said.

“Zen say sorry.”

His Majesty had spoken from the bed of judgement.

Papa’s mouth moved oddly. “I am sorry.”

Mamma gave a broken laugh. “So am I.”

“An’ kiss,” Toby commanded faintly, remembering the treaty of the Noah’s Ark.

This time they obeyed.

The secret was out.

For three days the fever held him. For three nights Mamma divided the watch with Miss Biddums, refusing dinners, callers, music, and sleep. Papa neglected files that would once have seemed weightier than the happiness of nations. He carried ice, bullied the doctor, and learned the exact manner in which Toby preferred his pillow turned.

The fever burned itself out.

His Majesty awoke one pale morning to find the punkah moving, a koel calling beyond the chick, and Mamma asleep in the chair with her hand upon his blanket. Her hair was untidy. She had no diamonds, no scent, no rustling dress. She looked, Toby thought, much prettier.

“Mamma,” he said experimentally.

She woke at once.

“What is it, my darling?”

The word was still there.

“I wants water.”

She gave it to him herself. Then she kissed one cheek and the other.

Toby lay very still, lest movement disturb the miracle.

Convalescence established a new constitution. Papa came to the nursery without requiring cleanliness. Mamma learned the names of the animals in the wooden ark and distinguished, after some instruction, between the Duke of Bath and the common bear. His Majesty was carried to the veranda, fed jelly in quantities condemned by the doctor, and allowed to issue decrees from a cane chair piled with cushions.

Miss Biddums watched this revolution with satisfaction and a certain private ache. A faithful regent must surrender power when the rightful government returns. Toby no longer called first for her in the night. She had prayed for that loss and bore it bravely.

The diamond star was locked away. Colonel Harcourt did not return to the station. The note was burned. Papa and Mamma did not become newly married lovers in a day, for fairy-tales end where difficult virtues begin; but they spoke without weapons, listened without preparing replies, and sometimes walked together after sunset. This was enough for a beginning.

As for Toby, he recovered with the indecent speed of childhood. Within a fortnight he was again persecuting lizards, issuing frontier orders to the ayah, and refusing bread-crusts upon constitutional grounds. Remorse faded. The devils withdrew. He even approached the tin trunk without trembling.

One afternoon Mrs. Larkyn brought Patsie to visit him. Patsie wore the celebrated blue sash, now somewhat faded and stained with mango.

“You look ever so fat after being ill,” she told him.

“I’m not fat. I’m stwong.”

Mrs. Larkyn laughed and held out her arms. “Come and kiss me, Your Majesty.”

Toby permitted one kiss upon his right cheek. When she turned him for the other, he covered the left cheek with his hand.

“Not vere.”

“Why not?”

He looked toward the veranda, where Mamma sat sewing something extremely crooked for him.

“Vat’s Mamma’s place,” he said.

Mrs. Larkyn’s eyes softened. She kissed the top of his head instead.

Patsie, bored by these ceremonies, had discovered a lizard’s severed tail twitching near the step. In a moment both children were prodding it with a twig, disputing whether it was alive, dead, or bewitched. His Majesty forgot stars, sin, and salvation.

Thus black wickedness brought peace to a house, theft made restitution, and a stolen crown restored a kingdom.

But children are notoriously unsound moralists.

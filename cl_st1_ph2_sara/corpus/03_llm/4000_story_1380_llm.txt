At noon, Red Gulch lay stretched beneath the Californian sun with that appearance of moral exhaustion which is sometimes observable in communities that have sat up all night.

The road, white with dust and diversified by empty bottles, playing-cards, and the recent impress of a mule, wound between a saloon, a blacksmith’s shed, three log cabins, and the schoolhouse. Beyond these rose the scarred shoulder of the hill, where derricks creaked, sluices glittered, and certain patient men washed a great deal of earth in the expectation of finding very little gold. A belt of pines darkened the higher ridge; lower down, where a spring crossed the road and then modestly disappeared, an azalea bush flamed against the dun hillside with a prodigality quite foreign to the commercial habits of the settlement.

Beneath this bush Mr. Alexander Morton—known to Red Gulch as “Sandy”—was sleeping.

I should be glad, for the sake of romance, to say that he resembled a fallen Viking. He was certainly large, blond, and bearded, and one of his boots had a heroic breadth. But the other boot was missing; his hat covered his face; and an overturned demijohn, resting affectionately against his shoulder, gave the composition a domestic rather than an epic character. His repose was profound. He had reached that tranquil philosophy in which dust is a pillow, sunlight a blanket, and the contempt of mankind a circumstance unworthy of notice.

Upon this scene Miss Mary Vale presently entered.

She had been six months the teacher of Red Gulch School, and was still known as “the new schoolma’am,” the settlement having no very exact sense of time except as measured by floods, fires, killings, and the arrival of the stage. Miss Mary was small, erect, and Boston-bred. Her gray dress had survived the Gulch by means of daily miracles; her cuffs were white; her collar was uncompromising; and the little sunshade she carried seemed less a protection against heaven than a protest against California.

Seeing the boot in the road, she paused. Seeing the man attached to it, she lowered her eyelids. Seeing the demijohn, she raised them again.

“Mr. Morton!”

The azalea shed one scarlet petal upon the sleeper.

“Mr. Morton!”

Sandy moved his hat sufficiently to expose a blue and troubled eye.

“School’s out?” he inquired.

“It is noon.”

“S’ame thing,” said Sandy, and closed the eye.

Miss Mary gathered her skirts so closely about her that she became, as it were, an embodied parenthesis.

“You are lying in the public road.”

“Road’s lyin’ too,” returned Sandy. “Been lyin’ thar all mornin’.”

“You are intoxicated.”

Sandy considered this accusation with gravity. “Mebbe road is.”

Miss Mary’s color rose. She was conscious that behind the windows of the Magnolia Saloon several gentlemen were enjoying the interview with an attention not wholly educational. Red Gulch, though commonly indifferent to sin, was keenly interested in correction.

“Get up,” she said.

Sandy slowly sat upright. His hat fell backward, disclosing a broad, sunburnt face, a tangle of yellow hair, and an expression of such obedient misery that Miss Mary’s severity was for an instant embarrassed.

“You are disgraceful,” she continued. “You are filthy. You should be ashamed to be seen by—by any decent person.”

“I am,” said Sandy humbly. “That’s why I was hidin’ under the flowers.”

A subdued sound came from the saloon windows. Miss Mary turned toward them, and the sound ceased with that promptitude which showed how deeply her school discipline had affected even the adult mind.

“You need water,” she said.

“Don’t like it.”

“You need a bath.”

At this word Sandy’s expression altered. It is possible that some ancestral memory of persecution by soap stirred within him. He stared at Miss Mary, at the spring, and finally toward the river, which flashed through the cottonwoods half a mile below.

“A bath,” repeated Miss Mary inexorably.

Sandy rose. He swayed once, obtained an imperfect adjustment with the earth, and suddenly started down the road at a run. His remaining boot struck up the dust; his coat tails streamed behind him; and before Miss Mary or the spectators could comprehend his intention, he had crossed the flat, plunged through the willows, and disappeared in the river.

A moment later his head emerged.

“Is this enough?” he called faintly.

I grieve to say that Miss Mary laughed.

It was not the laugh she allowed her pupils when the youngest confused Nebuchadnezzar with George Washington. It was a clear, girlish sound, wholly unregulated by Boston, and it traveled across the flat with astonishing freedom. Sandy heard it. The men at the Magnolia heard it. Even Yuba Bill, asleep upon the stage box, opened one eye and remarked that education was “lookin’ up.”

Miss Mary immediately recovered herself and walked away, carrying her sunshade with unusual rigidity.

For three days Sandy was not seen in Red Gulch.

This did not alarm the settlement. Sandy’s disappearances had formerly been associated with poker, prospecting, or a temporary preference for some neighboring camp’s liquor. On the fourth morning, however, Miss Mary found a branch of azalea blossoms lying across her desk.

There was no card. Red Gulch did not much affect cards outside the gaming-table, and Sandy’s literary resources were believed to end with his name. Miss Mary lifted the flowers, looked toward the open door, and put them in a cracked blue pitcher.

The next morning there were more.

On the third morning a bucket of clear spring water stood beside the schoolhouse, the loose step had been mended, and the azaleas occupied a tin cup from the Magnolia, whose proprietor afterward denied having donated it.

These attentions continued. A broken shutter was fastened. Firewood appeared in exact, almost penitential lengths. A dangerous branch above the children’s path vanished overnight. Miss Mary never saw the agent of these improvements, though she occasionally discovered in the dust the print of an immense boot and beside it the lighter mark of its recently purchased fellow.

Red Gulch saw him.

“He’s reformed,” observed Colonel Starbottle, who had no military title and viewed reform as a disease peculiar to other men.

“He’s skeered,” said Tennessee Joe.

“He’s courtin’,” said Yuba Bill, and the company, recognizing a natural phenomenon, treated Sandy thereafter with a delicacy which took the form of never addressing him upon the subject when sober.

Sandy had indeed changed. He passed the Magnolia by walking upon the opposite side of the road; and as the road was not broad, this moral distance might appear insufficient to my fairest reader, though in Red Gulch it represented a considerable journey. He washed with a frequency that excited comment. He purchased a red necktie, wore it once, and afterward kept it folded in his pocket, where its virtues were less conspicuous. He worked steadily at the Eureka claim and spoke little.

Miss Mary, meanwhile, preserved an admirable ignorance. If she noticed that Sandy attended Sunday service whenever the itinerant preacher came through the Gulch, she did not show it. If she observed that his hands were scrubbed, his beard trimmed, and his blue eyes no longer troubled by the immediate past, she merely adjusted her cuff. But when, one afternoon, she found him standing outside the schoolhouse with a new slate under his arm, she stopped.

“It’s for Tommy,” he said.

Tommy was the smallest and quietest of her pupils, a dark-eyed boy who lived with his mother in a cabin beyond the abandoned shaft. The mother did washing for the camp, appeared rarely by daylight, and received from respectable women the punishment usually allotted to sins in which respectable men have participated.

“Why did you not give it to him?” asked Miss Mary.

Sandy looked through the doorway, where Tommy sat over a battered slate divided by a crack.

“Reckoned he mightn’t take it.”

“Why not?”

“Mebbe his mother wouldn’t like it.”

Miss Mary regarded him more closely. His face had reddened beneath the tan.

“I will give it to him,” she said.

“Thank you, miss.”

He moved away, stumbled over level ground, recovered himself with violence, and went up the road without looking back.

The slate bore no inscription. Yet Miss Mary, whose profession made her suspicious of blank surfaces, held it for some moments before calling Tommy. When the child came, he accepted it solemnly.

“Who gave it?” he asked.

“A friend.”

Tommy ran one finger along its smooth wooden frame.

“Was it Sandy?”

Miss Mary was surprised. “Why do you think so?”

But Tommy had already bent his head. Children, I imagine, learn silence earlier in settlements where grown people have much to conceal.

Summer ripened. The dust grew deeper; the pines drew their shadows close at noon; and the azalea blossoms, scorched at the roadside, lingered in cooler recesses of the wood. In July Miss Mary announced a school picnic.

The event produced in Red Gulch a civic emotion. The Magnolia contributed lemons of uncertain ancestry. Colonel Starbottle lent a white tablecloth bearing evidence of previous diplomatic service. Yuba Bill placed the stage at the disposal of the children and drove them to the pine grove with such moderation that only two were thrown out, both into soft bushes. Sandy followed on foot, carrying a basket whose contents had been entrusted to him under seal.

The grove lay beyond the mines, where a clear stream moved over brown stones and the resinous air seemed to have forgotten the existence of whisky. The children scattered among the ferns. Miss Mary, relieved of six sums, four disputes, and one nosebleed, sat beneath a pine and watched the sunlight drift across the grass.

Sandy stood at a distance.

“You may sit down, Mr. Morton,” she said.

He sat at once, but on the farther side of the tree.

“I did not mean there.”

He came nearer by several inches.

Miss Mary smiled. “Are you afraid of me?”

“Yes, miss.”

“Why?”

“Because you’re good.”

This answer, being inconvenient, was followed by silence. Miss Mary folded her hands. Sandy pulled a blade of grass into fragments so minute that their future usefulness was doubtful.

“I am not very good,” she said at last.

Sandy looked up, plainly unconvinced.

“I have been proud,” she continued. “I have judged people without understanding them. When I came here, I thought Red Gulch wholly dreadful.”

“It is,” said Sandy.

“No. Not wholly.”

A shout from the stream showed that Tommy had entered it beyond the depth allowed by Miss Mary. Sandy rose, crossed the intervening space in three strides, and lifted the boy bodily to the bank. Tommy clung for one instant about his neck. Sandy set him down quickly and returned.

“You are fond of children,” said Miss Mary.

“Never had none to be fond of,” he replied.

The sentence was simple; yet something in his manner caused her to look away.

“Nor I,” she said. “I was an only child. My mother died when I was born, and my father when I was twelve. My aunt raised me.”

“Back East?”

“In Boston.”

“I never knowed mine,” said Sandy. “Folks said my mother was an actress. Dad might’ve been anybody. I got passed from camp to camp till I was big enough to work. After that I mostly raised myself.”

He said it without complaint, almost apologetically, as if his lack of ancestry were a social omission for which Miss Mary might properly hold him responsible.

“And yet,” she said softly, “you have been kind.”

Sandy’s rough hand closed over the scattered fragments of grass.

“Not always.”

“No one is always kind.”

“I’ve been bad, Miss Mary.”

She waited.

“I’ve drunk. I’ve gambled. I’ve done things—” He stopped. Through the trees came the children’s laughter and the liquid monotony of the stream. “Things I’d give my right hand not to’ve done.”

“Are you trying to repair them?”

“I’m tryin’ to quit bein’ what I was.”

“That is not quite the same thing.”

“No, miss.”

He accepted the correction humbly, but his face had grown pale. Miss Mary, perhaps conscious that virtue may become cruelty when too precisely applied, softened her voice.

“One cannot change the past. One can be honest in the present.”

Sandy raised his eyes. “Do you reckon a man can ever start fresh?”

“I believe he can—if he tells the truth, and bears the consequences of it.”

Again there was silence. A breeze passed through the upper branches, moving them like dark water against the sky.

Sandy seemed about to speak. Then Tommy came running with his trousers wet to the knee and a bunch of late azaleas in his hand.

“These are for Teacher,” he said.

Sandy took the flowers from him and gave them to Miss Mary. For one brief moment the three stood together in the green light: the woman with the blossoms against her gray dress, the man bending above her, and the child between them.

It was an arrangement so pleasing to sentiment that I distrust it. Nature, who has no reputation to preserve, frequently composes such pictures at the least defensible moments.

After the picnic, Miss Mary and Sandy were often seen together. He walked beside her from the schoolhouse to Mrs. Dodd’s boarding cabin, preserving a respectful interval which narrowed as the weeks advanced. She lent him books. He read them laboriously, beginning with “The Vicar of Wakefield,” whose domestic sorrows impressed him, and abandoning a volume of Emerson after declaring that the gentleman “had struck a mighty poor lead and kept on workin’ it.”

Sometimes Miss Mary laughed. Sometimes Sandy did. His laughter, at first rusty from disuse, became younger; hers lost something of its guarded precision. Red Gulch watched them with benevolent discretion. The men removed certain pictures from the Magnolia parlor when she entered to collect money for the school roof. Profanity diminished in her neighborhood, though it burst forth with accumulated force after her departure. Once, when a stranger made a jest concerning the teacher, he was requested by six citizens to select the weapon in which he felt least incompetent.

Yet no word passed between Miss Mary and Sandy that could be called a declaration. Their affection lived in small services, altered paths, pauses at gates, and azaleas laid freshly upon the desk. Perhaps each believed there was time.

But in September a letter came from the East.

Miss Mary read it twice beneath the schoolhouse window. Her aunt was ill. A position awaited her in a girls’ academy near Boston. The letter spoke of duty, opportunity, family, and climate; four respectable powers against which Red Gulch could oppose only a blond-bearded miner, a dusty schoolhouse, and a bush of scarlet flowers.

She gave notice that she would leave in ten days.

The settlement received the announcement badly. The trustees offered increased wages, though the treasury contained only seven dollars and a claim against a deceased muleteer. The children cried. Yuba Bill swore privately and then denied it publicly. Sandy said nothing.

For nine days he did not come to the schoolhouse.

On the evening before her departure, Miss Mary remained late, arranging copybooks, labeling primers, and leaving instructions for a successor not yet discovered. The sunset entered through the western windows and lay in red bars across the benches. Outside, Red Gulch glittered harshly—the tin roofs, the saloon windows, the wet boards of the sluice—while above it the pines darkened against a sky of faded gold.

There were no azaleas on her desk.

At last she put on her bonnet. Her fingers trembled at the strings, and she was obliged to begin again. She looked toward the door once, then lowered her eyelids with something of the old defensive movement.

A shadow crossed the threshold.

It was not Sandy.

A woman stood there, thin and dark, with a shawl drawn over her head. Miss Mary recognized Tommy’s mother, Mrs. Lyle, though the title was one Red Gulch bestowed out of courtesy rather than evidence. The woman’s face, ordinarily hard with watchfulness, was colorless.

“You’re goin’ tomorrow?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Take Tommy.”

Miss Mary thought she had misunderstood. “What did you say?”

“Take him with you. East. Anywhere. Don’t leave him here.”

The woman came forward. Her hands moved beneath the shawl and clasped each other with desperate pressure.

“I cannot take another person’s child,” said Miss Mary. “You are distressed. Sit down.”

“I don’t want to sit. I want you to hear me before I lose my nerve. He’s a good boy. You know he is.”

“Yes.”

“He learns quick.”

“Yes.”

“He ain’t like me yet.”

Miss Mary’s expression changed.

“No,” she said gently. “He is like you in many things.”

The woman gave a sharp, unbelieving laugh.

“You don’t know me.”

“I know you love him.”

At this, Mrs. Lyle sank upon the nearest bench. Her shawl slipped back. She was not old; indeed, in the dying light there remained about her mouth the wreck of a prettiness that had perhaps been her chief misfortune.

“I’ve tried,” she said. “God knows I’ve tried. But what can I make of him here? He’ll grow up hearin’ what they call me. He’ll learn cards before letters and whisky before work. Some day a man’ll tell him about me, and then he’ll either hate me or be like the men that ruined me. Take him where nobody knows.”

“You ask a great deal.”

“I’m givin’ everything.”

The quiet words struck more deeply than any cry. Miss Mary sat beside her.

“Where is his father?”

Mrs. Lyle did not answer.

“Is he living?”

“Yes.”

“Does he help you?”

“Sometimes. When I let him.”

“Does he know you mean to send the child away?”

“No.”

“Then he has a right to know.”

The woman lifted her face. Fear—not shame, but fear—had widened her eyes.

“No!”

“Why not?”

“Because he’ll stop you. Or follow. Or—” Her voice failed.

Miss Mary rose. The last sunlight had left the floor; only the high western panes still burned.

“Who is he?”

Mrs. Lyle covered her face.

“Tell me.”

There was a long interval. Outside, a wagon rattled past; someone called from the Magnolia; a hammer rang once upon iron and was still.

“Sandy.”

Miss Mary did not move.

The woman lowered her hands. “Sandy Morton.”

It is easy, at a safe distance and in a comfortable chair, to demand greatness from the human heart. We may require forgiveness, renunciation, maternal devotion, and perfect justice, all before the turning of a page. Miss Mary had no such leisure. The name entered the little schoolroom and seemed to alter every familiar object: the mended shutter, the water bucket, Tommy’s slate, the empty place upon the desk where the azaleas had lain.

“You are certain?” she asked.

Mrs. Lyle stared at her.

“Forgive me,” said Miss Mary. “Of course you are.”

“He was drunk half the time then,” the woman went on rapidly. “He’d just struck money. There was promises—then there wasn’t. I ain’t sayin’ I was innocent. I was a fool. When the boy came, Sandy offered to marry me.”

Miss Mary’s hand tightened on the desk.

“You refused?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because he offered from shame, not love. Because I hated him. Because I hated myself more when I saw him. Choose any reason—you’ll find a bad one.”

“And since then?”

“He’s brought money. I sent it back till Tommy needed things. He watches the boy. Tommy don’t know. Sandy thinks maybe I’ll let him speak some day.”

The new slate flashed in Miss Mary’s memory; the child’s arms about Sandy’s neck; the man’s broken sentence beneath the pines.

“I’ve been bad, Miss Mary.”

The truth had been near her, asking to be told, and she had supplied courage only in the abstract.

Mrs. Lyle slipped from the bench to her knees.

“Take him! You’re clean. You’re educated. You can make him forget this place. I’ll never come after him. I’ll tell him I’m dead if that’s better. Only take him!”

Miss Mary bent quickly and lifted her.

“Do not kneel to me.”

“You don’t know what I am.”

“I know you are his mother.”

For the first time the woman began to weep. She did so silently, with her face turned away, as if tears also were an indecency for which Red Gulch might punish her.

Miss Mary stood beside her while the darkness gathered. She saw, or believed she saw, the life she had almost chosen: Sandy at her gate, Sandy with books beneath his arm, Sandy’s altered laughter, the azaleas renewing themselves each spring. Then behind these came the child, carrying a cracked slate; and behind the child, the kneeling woman.

“I will take Tommy,” she said.

Mrs. Lyle caught her hand.

“But you must tell his father tonight.”

The hand fell.

“I can’t.”

“You must. Tell him I know. Tell him Tommy goes with me.”

“He’ll follow.”

“Tell him he is never to follow us.”

Mrs. Lyle looked at her with something like pity.

“And never see the boy?”

Miss Mary’s face was very pale.

“Tell him that is my condition.”

She turned toward the window. The sunset was gone. Red Gulch had sunk into shadow, and one by one the lamps of the saloons came out along the road.

The stage left at six the next morning.

Long before that hour the settlement had gathered to witness Miss Mary’s departure. Her trunk was strapped behind; a carpetbag occupied the roof; parcels of impractical food filled the interior. Yuba Bill sat on the box in funereal dignity. The children stood in a miserable row, scrubbed beyond recognition.

Tommy, bewildered but silent, sat beside Miss Mary. His mother had not come.

Neither had Sandy.

Colonel Starbottle made a speech, lost its central idea, and concluded by removing his hat. The trustees shook Miss Mary’s hand. The children cried openly. Miss Mary kissed each one, adjusted Tommy’s collar, and entered the coach.

Yuba Bill gathered the reins.

As the stage moved up the road, Tommy pressed his face to the window. They passed the schoolhouse, the spring, and the turn where the azalea bush leaned over the dust.

“Stop!” he cried.

Yuba Bill pulled the horses back with an oath softened for the occasion.

Tommy jumped down. Before Miss Mary could speak, he ran to the bush and tugged at a flowering branch. It resisted him. He pulled again, and a large hand, reaching from behind the leaves, bent the branch within his grasp.

Tommy did not see the hidden face. Perhaps Miss Mary did.

The branch broke. Tommy climbed back into the coach with its scarlet blossoms against his breast.

The stage rolled on, carrying the teacher and the child toward the high road, the pines, and the East. Miss Mary held the azalea branch in both hands. She neither turned nor spoke.

Behind them, beneath the brightening flowers, a man remained kneeling in the dust.

### The Room Beyond the Armour

It was after dinner on Christmas Eve, when the younger children had been sent upstairs and the snow, which had threatened all afternoon, began at last to whiten the black panes of Harcourt House.

We sat in the old dining-room because the drawing-room chimney smoked in an east wind. The inconvenience was one for which nobody professed regret. There are rooms which become more agreeable by candlelight, and others which seem actually to require the night before they will disclose their character; the dining-room at Harcourt was of the latter kind. Its oak panels, blackened by age and by the smoke of innumerable winter fires, reflected no more than a dull lustre from the lamps. Above the long sideboard hung three portraits whose names had been forgotten, though each face preserved that air of injured consequence which survives genealogy. A suit of armour stood in the angle beside the door. In daylight it was a shabby object, with a dent in the breastplate and one gauntlet missing; but after ten o’clock, when the lamps were lowered, one noticed that its empty visor commanded the room.

The fire made a more cheerful centre. Chairs had been drawn about it without much regard to precedence, and the ladies’ skirts, the men’s polished shoes, the brass tongs and the decanters upon a little table shone and disappeared as the flames rose or sank. Beyond that circle all was uncertain. One could not have said where the panels ended and the shadow began.

There were perhaps twelve of us. Our host, Sir James Harcourt, had the comfortable breadth and pink complexion proper to a man whose house has descended to him without litigation. Lady Harcourt sat near him, working at a strip of embroidery which, so far as I observed, advanced not at all during the evening. There were two young officers, a clergyman, Mrs. Marden and her daughter Elinor, old Miss Vale, myself, and George Grady.

Grady was then a man of fifty-five or thereabouts, bald above the temples, with a narrow pointed beard already iron-grey. Ordinarily his conversation was precise and somewhat dry. He had travelled, practised medicine for a time, and afterwards occupied himself with certain researches into abnormal states of memory, though he disliked being called a psychologist. On this evening he had spoken less than usual. Once or twice I found him watching Elinor Marden.

She was a curious-looking girl—not beautiful, but arresting. She had lately put up her hair, and the alteration seemed to have been made before her childhood had consented to it. Her face was pale and narrow; her eyes, when at rest, had a fixed, dark brightness. She laughed readily enough, but always a moment after the others, as though laughter were a social direction which she had first to understand and then obey.

The talk had fallen, as talk will on such a night and in such a house, upon apparitions. The clergyman had explained that most supernatural experiences could be attributed to fatigue, suggestion, or the imperfect circulation of air in old buildings. One of the officers had replied that imperfect circulation was an odd name for a white woman who had followed him along a road in India and vanished at a locked gate. This led to several narratives, all confidently remembered and indifferently told. At length Sir James turned to Grady.

“You have been sitting there like a judge,” he said. “It is your turn. You must have one story at least.”

Grady looked up quickly.

“I?”

“You know this house better than most of us,” said Lady Harcourt. “You stayed here when my father was alive.”

“Yes,” Grady answered. “I stayed here once.”

There was something in the way he said it which quieted the little murmur about the fire. He placed his glass upon the table, but not securely; its foot touched the edge, and he moved it twice before he appeared satisfied.

“It happened here,” he said, “on Christmas Eve.”

“Then you cannot possibly refuse us,” cried Miss Vale.

He did not answer her. His eyes had passed again to Elinor Marden. She was leaning back in the great carved chair beyond Mrs. Marden, and the firelight showed only the lower half of her face. Her lips were parted.

“I do not know,” said Grady slowly, “whether it is a story that ought to be told.”

“My dear fellow,” said Sir James, “that is the proper beginning to every story that must.”

A little laughter followed, but it died away almost immediately. Grady drew his chair nearer the hearth.

“Very well. You must allow me to tell it in my own fashion. It is not long, though it may seem so. There were intervals in it—moments, you understand, in which nothing happened, but in which the nothing was of greater importance than any event. If I leave those out, I shall give you a false account.”

He paused, listening perhaps to the snow against the glass.

“It was twenty-eight years ago,” he began. “Sir James’s uncle, Edward Harcourt, had the house then. I was a young man and had come down for a week’s shooting, though a thaw spoiled most of it. The company was larger than this one, and there were many children. Among them was a boy called Joseph Fane.

“Joseph was eight years old. Remember that. He was not sickly, precocious in the common sense, or given to those performances by which clever children make themselves intolerable. In the daytime he was a simple, active boy. He ate enormously, tore his stockings, and cheated at forfeits. But at night—or perhaps I should say in relation to one particular room—he became another person.

“I first noticed him on the staircase at about one in the morning.

“We had played cards late. The others had gone up, and I remained below to finish a letter. When I left the library, the hall lamps had been extinguished. There was a candle on the table for me, but I did not take it, because I knew the house and because the fire in the hall still gave a little light.

“You remember the staircase as it was before the alterations, James? It rose straight to the landing and divided there. The armour, which is now in this room, stood beneath the stained-glass window. Well, as I crossed the hall, I saw what I supposed to be a white rag lying on the third stair.

“It moved.

“I stopped. The object was Joseph, seated in his nightshirt, with his arms round his knees. He was looking up the staircase—not down at me, not toward anything visible, but up into the darkness above him.

“‘What are you doing here?’ I asked.

“He put one finger to his lips.

“Children delight in mystery, and I thought at first he was playing some game. I went nearer. Then I saw his face. He was not frightened as a child is frightened. There were no tears, no trembling, no appeal to be carried back to bed. He looked watchful and very old.

“‘You must go upstairs,’ I said.

“‘Not yet,’ he whispered.

“‘Why not?’

“‘She has only just gone up.’

“I listened. There was no sound at all anywhere.

“‘Who has gone up?’

“He considered the question, and then said, ‘The person who killed me.’

“Naturally I supposed he had been dreaming. I sat beside him and asked where his nurse was. He said she was asleep. I told him he too had been asleep, and that he had brought his dream downstairs with him.

“‘No,’ he said, ‘I came down before she began.’

“‘Before who began?’

“‘The woman.’

“Now, there was nothing melodramatic in his manner. That is important. He did not try to alarm me, and he showed some impatience at my questions. Murder, to him, appeared less terrible than my inability to understand it.

“‘What woman?’ I asked again.

“‘I don’t know her name now. I knew it then.’

“‘And when was then?’

“He turned and looked at me. ‘Before I was Joseph.’

“I confess that answer disturbed me. Not because I believed it; I was too young to believe anything which had not first received a respectable Latin name. But the boy’s certainty had an effect out of all proportion to his words. One felt—not reasoned, but felt—that he was speaking of a fact complete in itself, a fact which did not depend upon our acceptance.

“I asked him where it had happened.

“He pointed upward.

“‘In my room.’

“His room was at the end of the west passage, beyond the small stair and directly over this dining-room. It is the room with the faded blue paper.”

At this Mrs. Marden shifted in her chair. The embroidery frame upon Lady Harcourt’s knee gave a faint click.

“Elinor occupies the blue room,” she said.

“Does she?” Grady replied.

His tone was perfectly ordinary, yet Elinor turned her face fully toward him. Her hands, which had rested upon the carved arms of the chair, closed upon them.

Grady continued.

“I told Joseph that no one had been murdered in that room. He said, ‘Not lately.’

“There was a dryness in the answer which might have been comic under other circumstances. I asked what he remembered.

“‘Not all of it,’ he said. ‘It goes away when the sun comes. But I remember more here.’

“‘Why here?’

“‘Because the room remembers too.’

“That was his phrase. The room remembers too.

“I persuaded him to come down to the hall fire, where there were still some embers. He sat on the rug. Presently he began to speak—not eagerly, but with pauses, as if translating impressions which had no natural relation to a child’s vocabulary.

“He said that once he had been older, though not old. He had slept in the blue room, but the bed had then stood against the opposite wall. There had been curtains around it, a heavy counterpane, and a bell-rope near the pillow. He was ill, or had been injured; on this point his memory failed. He could not move his legs, and his left arm had no strength. He heard people below and knew that, if he could summon them, he would be safe.

“‘Safe from whom?’ I asked.

“‘From her.’

“He remembered a woman sitting in the chair beside the window. He could not see her face because the room was dark, but he knew she was watching him. For a long while neither of them moved. Then a coal fell in the grate. At that sound she rose.

“Here Joseph stopped. He bent forward and spread his fingers to the embers, though the hall was not cold.

“‘She did not walk straight to the bed,’ he said. ‘That would have been easier. She went first to the door.’

“‘Why?’

“‘To listen.’

“He described how she crossed the room without shoes. The boards gave a slight complaint beneath her weight; she waited after each sound, and then went on. At the door she listened to the passage. Someone laughed far away. There was music below—fiddles, he thought—and the noise of many people. It was Christmas Eve.

“Then she returned to the chair and sat down.

“‘Perhaps she did not mean to hurt you,’ I said.

“Joseph looked almost offended.

“‘She had been thinking of it all day.’

“‘How could you know that?’

“‘Because I was thinking of her thinking it.’

“You see the distinction? I did not, at the time. He seemed to mean that his fear entered her intention, or her intention entered his fear, until they were one knowledge shared between them. Sight had very little to do with it.

“After sitting by the window, the woman put out the candle.

“Joseph remembered the smell of it—the warm tallow, and then the thin smoke. He remembered a bar of moonlight across the floor, though he could not say whether there had truly been moonlight or whether memory had supplied it afterward. The woman passed through this pale bar and disappeared beside the bed.

“He waited for her to rise.

“She did not rise.

“She was moving on the floor.

“At that point, Joseph told me, he ceased to be frightened. The force of his fear had become too great for the body that contained it. It escaped from him all at once, like a congregation rising together to pray; and what remained was quiet, detached, almost curious.

“He could hear the woman’s sleeve touch the carpet. Then her hand reached the hanging edge of the counterpane.

“It searched along it.

“‘I watched the cloth move,’ he said. ‘Her hand made little hills underneath.’

“He tried to reach the bell-rope with his right arm. It was six inches beyond his fingers. He tried to call, but his voice produced only a breath. The music below had stopped. Somewhere a door shut. Then the hand found the top of the bed.

“It came over the mattress slowly, pressing here and there, uncertain of where his body lay.

“‘Why did she not see you?’ I asked.

“‘The curtains were closed.’

“‘Then how did you see her hand?’

“‘I did not. I felt where it was.’

“There it is again. He knew the movement without seeing it. The hand passed his feet, his knees, his side. It went away once, and he thought she had abandoned him. But after a moment the mattress sank near his shoulder.

“He smelled her hair. He remembered that with extraordinary distinctness. It had the scent of damp violets and wood smoke.

“Then her fingers touched his throat.

“He said the first touch was gentle. Indeed, it was so light that for an instant he wondered whether she meant merely to discover that he was there. The fingers travelled upward beneath his jaw, withdrew, and returned with the other hand.

“‘Did you see her then?’ I asked.

“‘No.’

“‘Did she speak?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘What did she say?’

“The boy looked toward the staircase. ‘She said, “You must not come back.”’

“Then the hands tightened.

“He described no pain. He spoke instead of the noises: the faint knocking of the bed against the wall; a ticking clock which became louder and louder; the blood in his ears; and, last of all, a long inward rushing, like a wave drawing stones down a beach. He believed he heard the woman weeping, though he was not certain. The darkness changed its nature. It ceased to be the darkness of a room. Then he was no longer there.

“‘Where were you?’ I asked.

“‘Everywhere for a little while,’ he replied. ‘After that I was Joseph.’

“Now, do not run away with the idea that I accepted all this as a revelation. There might have been a dozen explanations. The child could have overheard a servant’s tale; he might have possessed an unusual imagination; he might even have seen some illustrated account of a murder and transferred it to the room. I told myself these things while he sat before me.

“Then we heard a movement overhead.”

Grady paused.

No one stirred in our dining-room. The fire gave a soft collapse, and a little red tower of coal fell inward. Outside, snow brushed the windows with a dry, persistent whisper.

“It was not a footstep,” Grady resumed. “At least, I cannot call it one. It was more like the communication of weight through timber—a pressure beginning above the hall and passing slowly toward the west passage.

“Joseph stood up.

“‘She is going back,’ he said.

“I told him to remain where he was. I took the candle from the table and went upstairs.

“The flame burned badly. I remember sheltering it with my hand, though there was no draught. At the first landing I stopped. The stained glass made the snowlight green upon the floor. Beyond it the two passages ran into darkness.

“Something was in the west passage.

“I saw nothing. Let me be exact: there was no shape, no shadow independent of other shadows, no sound that could not have been made by the settling of an old house. Nevertheless, I knew where it was. I knew its height, its pace, even the horrible caution with which it moved. One may know, in a dark room, that another person has entered before he speaks or touches you. Magnify that sensation until it becomes almost physical, and you will understand me.

“It passed the small stair.

“It passed the linen cupboard.

“It reached the door of the blue room.

“The door opened.”

“By itself?” asked one of the officers.

“I did not say that. I said it opened. Whether a current of air moved it, whether it had been imperfectly latched, I cannot tell you. But it opened inward, very slowly, while I stood at the far end of the passage.

“At the same instant my candle went out.

“I remained where I was. Pride, I think, prevented me from retreating; fear prevented me from advancing. Then Joseph came up behind me and took my hand.

“His hand was warm. That ordinary warmth gave me courage. I relit the candle, and we went together to the room.

“It was empty.

“The bed stood near the window, not against the wall Joseph had indicated. There were no curtains, no bell-rope, and no chair except a little painted one beside the fireplace. Yet the boy crossed directly to the opposite wall and touched a panel in the oak dado.

“‘The bed knocked here,’ he said.

“There were marks upon the wood—three small crescent-shaped bruises in a vertical line. They might have been made by furniture. Most likely they were. Beneath them, half hidden by later varnish, someone had scratched a date: 1811.

“Joseph then went to the fireplace. He knelt and put his hand beneath the edge of the fender. From the dust he drew a small brass button, green with age. It bore the device of a running stag.

“I asked how he knew it was there.

“He said, ‘It came off her sleeve.’

“Then, for the first time, he became a child again. He began to cry. Not violently; he seemed ashamed of it. I carried him to his nurse and returned downstairs.

“The next morning I questioned Edward Harcourt. He knew of no murder. Old families conceal much, but they also forget much, and there is no gainsaying the fact that forgetfulness is often the stronger lock. We searched some papers after Christmas. In a bundle of household accounts we found a letter dated January 1812. It referred obscurely to the death, during the previous Christmas, of a young man named Stephen Vale, a distant connection who had been confined to his room after a riding accident. The letter stated that he had died suddenly in the night.

“No inquiry had been held. His elder sister, who inherited a small property through his death, left England three months later.

“Her name was Honoria.

“We found a miniature of her. On her sleeve were brass buttons stamped with a running stag.”

The clergyman cleared his throat.

“Family evidence,” he said, “is notoriously adaptable after the event.”

“Quite,” said Grady. “And I have not asked you to adapt yourself to it.”

“What became of the boy?” Lady Harcourt asked.

But it was Elinor who moved first. She leaned so far forward that the firelight caught her whole face. There was no colour in it now. A small pulse beat rapidly at her temple.

“Yes,” she said. “What became of Joseph?”

Her mother glanced at her in surprise.

Grady turned the brass poker between his fingers.

“He went away after Christmas. I saw him from time to time.”

“Did he remember?” Elinor asked.

“Less as he grew older.”

“That is usual,” said the clergyman, perhaps too readily. “Children’s fantasies lose their hold.”

“Not entirely,” Grady said. “Certain sensations remained. The scent of violets made him ill. He could not endure a hand near his throat. And he was troubled, though he could not explain why, by the conviction that the woman had returned.”

Elinor’s breathing had become audible.

“Returned where?”

“To life.”

Mrs. Marden laid a hand upon her daughter’s arm. Elinor shook it off with an abruptness that startled us all.

“How could he know?”

“He did not know. He felt it. There is a difference, but not always a great one.”

“And did he see her?”

Grady waited. He had ceased to appear nervous. Looking at him then, one perceived that his earlier hesitations had not been weakness but measurement. He had watched the room as a surgeon watches the effect of an anaesthetic.

“Yes,” he said. “He believed he did.”

Elinor rose.

The great chair behind her seemed suddenly enormous and empty, its carved points rising on either side of her head. Her eyes went not to Grady but to the darkness beyond the armour.

“Where?” she whispered.

“In this house.”

Mrs. Marden also rose. “Elinor, you are overtired. Come upstairs.”

“No!”

The word struck the panels and appeared to return from them. Elinor pressed both hands to her throat.

Grady said very gently, “Joseph is not here tonight.”

She stared at him.

“Not here?”

“No.”

“Then—then he is dead?”

“I did not say that.”

The girl’s lips twitched. “Alive?”

“Alive,” said Grady. “He is thirty-six years old.”

For several seconds there was no movement in the room. One had the strange fancy that our bodies remained seated around the hearth while our more essential selves had streamed across the carpet and gathered about the girl.

She made a sound like a breath drawn through water.

“He must not come back,” she said.

Mrs. Marden caught her wrist. “What are you saying?”

Elinor looked down at her own hands as though they were objects newly placed before her. The fingers bent slowly, gripping an invisible softness.

“I only meant to stop him,” she said. “He would have told. He knew about the papers. I waited until the music ended. I listened at the door. I put out the candle because he watched me. But he still watched me in the dark.”

Nobody spoke.

Her voice had altered. It was lower, older, and possessed an exhausted bitterness wholly foreign to her youth.

“He could not reach the bell. I moved the rope before dinner. He tried—I heard his hand upon the wall. Then the bed knocked. Once, twice—”

She turned toward Grady with a look of terrible recognition.

“You said he did not see me.”

“No,” Grady answered.

“Then how did he know?”

“I think you have answered that yourself.”

She drew back. “I did it,” she said. “I did it. And he came back.”

Her knees failed. Grady reached her before she struck the fender, and Mrs. Marden gave a cry which seemed, after that controlled and dreadful speech, almost a relief. The room broke into movement. Someone rang for the servants; Lady Harcourt brought water; the clergyman bent over the girl and called her name.

Presently she opened her eyes, but there was no recognition in them. She began to weep with the helpless bewilderment of a child awakened in a strange room.

It was later, when Elinor had been carried upstairs and the company had separated into whispering groups, that Sir James drew Grady aside.

“You knew,” he said.

Grady stood before the dying fire. His face had grown grey.

“I suspected.”

“On what possible ground?”

“Joseph saw her last spring. He knew nothing of Miss Marden’s family and had never met her before. Yet when she passed him at a concert, he fainted. Afterwards he described the scent of damp violets and smoke. He also said—though he was ashamed of the absurdity—that he heard a bedstead knocking against a wall.”

“And the girl?”

“I questioned her mother. Elinor has had dreams of this house since infancy, though she had never visited it until today. This afternoon I found her alone in the blue room. She had moved the chair from the window to the precise place Joseph described. When I entered, she was searching beneath the fender.”

Sir James looked toward the door.

“Then you told that story deliberately.”

“Yes.”

“To force a confession?”

“To discover whether there was anything to confess.”

There was, I thought, more horror in that distinction than in all the rest.

“But what has she confessed?” Sir James demanded. “A murder committed a century before she was born? What can one do with such a confession?”

“Nothing,” said Grady. “That is what makes it perfect.”

He stooped and separated two coals with the poker. A small flame sprang up between them and illuminated the empty visor of the armour.

From somewhere overhead came the measured creak of a board.

Sir James looked up.

“It is an old house,” he said.

“Of course,” Grady replied.

Neither of them moved until the sound had crossed the ceiling and stopped directly above us, in the room where the girl lay sleeping.

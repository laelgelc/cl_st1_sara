**A CONFUSION OF PERSONS**

My Dear Ellison,

You ask whether I believe that one mind may communicate with another otherwise than by the ordinary offices of speech, writing and gesture. I answer that belief is not evidence; yet disbelief, when too confidently entertained, is merely ignorance in ceremonial dress. There are laws of nature whose effects we observe without knowing their terms, as a savage may see the needle turn northward and attribute its obedience to a spirit. What I shall relate is true as nearly as memory, impaired by illness and troubled by doubt, permits me to make it. If you can explain it, you will have relieved me of a burden which philosophy has only taught me to carry with composure.

You knew my brother John, though you met him, I think, but twice. You have met me more frequently; nevertheless, had we three stood together, I doubt if you could have said which of us was Henry Stevens.

We were identical twins—not merely alike in the broad and charitable sense in which relatives discover resemblance between any two infants equally red, shapeless and objectionable, but alike in every feature, motion and intonation. Our mother, whose affection should have sharpened her discernment, confessed that she distinguished us chiefly by our clothing; and this resource failed whenever, in the exercise of a harmless depravity, we exchanged coats. Our father once tattooed a small blue point upon John’s left arm. By morning an equally blue point appeared upon mine, placed there by John with a needle and indigo. The experiment was not repeated.

In the family we came to be known collectively as “Jehnry,” a name compounded with more economy than elegance. If one of us were wanted, both were summoned; if one offended, both were punished, our parents holding, with some justice, that the innocent one had probably escaped correction for an undiscovered crime. We learned early that identity is less a possession than a convention. I have sometimes thought that this lesson, received in childhood as a jest, prepared me badly for the darker confusion of later years.

Our tastes and tempers were not, however, identical. John was the more social, candid and hopeful; I was reserved, inquisitive, and perhaps unduly pleased by whatever resisted explanation. These differences, being inward, afforded no assistance to strangers. After our parents died and we removed to California, we lived apart in San Francisco and followed separate employments—John in the office of an importer near the water front, I with a firm of attorneys in Montgomery Street. Our acquaintances were mostly distinct, and each was therefore compelled at times to answer courteously to the other’s name.

One wet afternoon in November I was crossing Market Street near Kearney when a gentleman seized my hand and said:

“My dear John, I had begun to think you avoided me.”

He was a man of about sixty, strongly built, carefully dressed and wearing iron-gray whiskers. I had never seen him. Yet before he spoke again I knew his name.

“Mr. Margovan,” I replied, “you do me too much honour.”

The name escaped me without reflection; indeed, it seemed less spoken than remembered. The stranger’s face cleared.

“Then you have forgiven my persistence. Come to dinner tomorrow; Julia will be offended if you refuse again.”

I ought, of course, to have corrected his error. Instead, moved by that spirit of inquiry which has led better men to worse actions, I accepted. He gave me his address, though I already knew it—or thought I knew it—the number and street standing in my mind with the distinctness of a written memorandum. We parted, and I walked home in a state not so much of alarm as of intellectual irritation.

John came to my rooms that evening.

“Henry,” he said, before removing his gloves, “do you know a man named Margovan?”

The question affected me so strangely that I delayed my reply.

“I was about to ask the same of you.”

He laughed. “Then one of us is haunted. All day I have felt an absurd necessity to obtain his address. I do not know why. The name occurred to me at breakfast and has pursued me like a creditor.”

I gave him the card which the stranger had pressed upon me. John glanced at it, became pale, and then laughed again, though less naturally.

“That is the address,” he said.

“How can you know?”

“I cannot. Yet I do.”

We discussed the matter at some length and reached those ingenious conclusions by which reasonable men conceal from themselves that they have reached none. John finally declared that he must keep the appointment.

“But it was made with me,” I said.

“It was made with my face and name.”

“You are welcome to both. Return them in good condition.”

Thus it happened that John dined at the Margovans’ and I did not. His deception, if deception it may be called, was merely a restoration of error to truth. Mr. Margovan had intended to invite him; I had accidentally intercepted the invitation. From that evening John became a frequent visitor at the house. Within six months he informed me that Miss Julia Margovan had heartlessly accepted him in marriage.

I had not yet seen her—or rather, I did not know that I had.

Some weeks before the engagement was announced, I had observed in Union Square a man whose appearance was sufficiently disagreeable to command attention. He was young, but dissipation had written its age upon him in a bold hand. His clothing had once been fashionable; his face, handsome in its lines, was colorless and loose; his eyes had the watchfulness of one who expects pursuit. He passed me twice, each time looking back, though I am certain he did not know me.

Without assignable motive I followed him.

I do not defend the act. Curiosity is a plea which the moral law receives with proper contempt; but at the time I seemed to have no volition in the matter. The man went westward through streets shining with recent rain, turned abruptly more than once, and finally entered a narrow lane behind a row of respectable houses. There he waited beneath a lamp which cast upon his face a wan and ghastly light.

Presently a woman came from a side gate. She was closely veiled, but the movement with which she approached him had the certainty of habit. He took both her hands.

“You should not have sent for me,” she said.

Her voice was low, yet I heard it distinctly from the shadow where I stood.

“You would not have come otherwise,” he replied.

“I had resolved never to see you again.”

“You have resolved that often.”

She drew back. “Do not speak so loudly. My father—”

“Your father knows nothing. And your excellent Mr. Stevens knows less.”

At the sound of my name—or John’s—I felt an inward shudder, different from the physical trembling caused by the cold. The woman turned her head, and for one instant the wind lifted her veil. Her face was singularly beautiful; but beauty seen in fear is an accusation. I had never beheld her before. Of that I was certain.

Their remaining words were too faint for me to hear. The man appeared to demand something; the woman refused, then yielded him a small packet. He caught her wrist with needless violence, bent toward her and spoke close to her ear. She left him immediately. I did not follow either of them. Whatever compulsion had led me there was withdrawn, and I returned home ashamed, perplexed and angry with a person whom I could not name.

Three weeks later John presented me at the Margovan house.

The drawing room was bright with fire and shaded lamps; the windows, blackened by fog, reflected the interior so perfectly that we seemed enclosed among duplicates of ourselves. Mr. Margovan greeted me with loud amusement.

“Julia,” he said, “here is another John—or perhaps the same one twice.”

She entered as he spoke.

It was the woman of the lane.

Her composure did not fail, but the blood left her face. John, observing this, attributed it to astonishment at our resemblance and was delighted.

“Which will you marry?” he asked.

“The one who asks me,” she replied.

“I have asked you.”

“Then the difficulty is solved.”

The levity of her answer was faultless; only her eyes betrayed her. They rested upon me with the fixed attention one gives to a weapon whose use is uncertain.

I found an opportunity, later in the evening, to speak with her alone in the conservatory. Beyond the glass the fog pressed like a gray hand; drops of moisture slid down the panes with a stealthy persistence.

“Miss Margovan,” I said, “we have met before.”

“No.”

“I saw you near Union Square, at night.”

She touched the back of a chair but did not sit.

“You followed me?”

“I followed the man.”

“That distinction may satisfy you.”

“It does not.”

She regarded me for a moment in silence. “How much do you require?”

The question, quietly spoken, was more shocking than an outcry.

“You believe I mean to blackmail you?”

“I do not presume to know what you call it.”

“I require nothing. If you are in danger, I offer assistance.”

At this her expression altered, though whether toward hope or suspicion I could not determine.

“You cannot assist me.”

“Then I shall at least prevent your marriage to my brother.”

A faint flush came into her cheeks.

“By telling him?”

“If necessary.”

“You would destroy his happiness upon the evidence of an overheard conversation?”

“Upon the evidence of your fear.”

“That is not evidence, Mr. Stevens; it is an interpretation.”

Her precision disconcerted me. “Who is the man?”

She looked past me toward the drawing room, where John was laughing with her father.

“I shall not tell you.”

“Does he possess some claim upon you?”

“No lawful claim.”

“Does he threaten you?”

She hesitated. “I have said too much.”

“You have said almost nothing.”

“And you have understood too much. Let the matter rest. I accept your terms.”

“I have offered no terms.”

“Every silence has them.”

John approached before I could reply. Julia turned to him with a smile so tender that for an instant I doubted my own senses and almost my own honesty. He placed her hand upon his arm and led her away. She looked back once—not appealingly, as I had expected, but with an expression of grave finality.

The next day I called upon John and urged him to postpone the marriage. I did not reveal what I had seen; I had, after all, no knowledge of its meaning, and the honour of a woman is too serious a thing to be handled with conjecture. I spoke instead of haste, incompatibility, and every other respectable falsehood available to a brother in distress.

John listened with increasing impatience.

“You dislike her,” he said.

“I fear for you.”

“On what ground?”

I could not answer without betraying her.

“You have none,” he continued. “Henry, your affection has become officious.”

We parted coldly. It was our first quarrel and our last.

On the following night, shortly after eleven, I sat alone in my rooms attempting to read. Rain struck intermittently at the window; in the street below, wheels passed with a muffled sound. For perhaps an hour I had been oppressed by a sense of impending calamity—no definite apprehension, but a pressure upon the mind, as if some vast event approached from behind me.

Suddenly I heard John cry my name.

“Henry!”

The voice was close, distinct and charged with mortal agony. I sprang up, overturning the lamp. The room was empty.

I called to him, opened the door, and searched the passage. No one was there. My servant, roused from sleep, had heard nothing. I returned to the room ashamed of my agitation; yet before I had relighted the lamp the cry came again:

“Henry—help me!”

It cut through thought as a taut cord is parted by steel. With it came—not knowledge, perhaps, but irresistible direction. I seized my coat and ran into the street.

Of my course through the city I retain only broken impressions: wet pavements flashing beneath lamps; the startled face of a cabman whom I thrust aside; a steep street rising into fog; my own breath loud in my ears. I did not consciously choose the Margovan house, yet I arrived there as a sleepwalker arrives at a precipice.

The front door stood open.

In the hall a servant crouched against the wall, incapable of speech. Somewhere above, Mr. Margovan was calling his daughter’s name in a voice already bereaved. I ascended the stairs.

Julia lay upon a couch in her chamber, dressed in white. A small bottle stood uncorked upon the table beside her. Her face was composed, almost disdainful; only the contracted fingers of one hand declared the violence of her death. Mr. Margovan knelt near her, striking his forehead with his fist.

John was in the adjoining room.

He sat on the floor with his back against the bed. A pistol lay between his feet, and blood darkened his shirt. When I entered, he lifted his head. For one terrible moment I had the sensation of seeing myself murdered.

“Henry,” he whispered.

I knelt and took him in my arms.

“What has happened?”

His lips moved. I bent nearer, but what he said—if he said anything—was lost beneath the confused cries from the other room. His eyes turned toward the doorway through which Julia could be seen; then they returned to mine with an expression neither of accusation nor forgiveness, but of immense astonishment.

He died so.

What followed is not wholly within my recollection. I am told that I uttered a cry, fell across his body, and could not be removed without force. Fever succeeded. For several weeks I lay between life and death, at times insisting that I was John and demanding Julia; at others declaring myself guilty of crimes which, being carefully investigated, proved to have no existence. The physicians called these fancies delirium. Their word is no doubt correct; it explains nothing, but it enables the mind to label the drawer in which the mystery is kept.

No letter was found upon Julia, nor any message from John. The poison had been taken by her own hand; the shot had been fired by his. Whether he saw her die, whether she confessed anything, whether the unknown man of the lane had driven her to the act—these questions remained unanswered. Mr. Margovan, broken by grief, refused all inquiry and soon afterward left California. I never saw him again.

Years passed. Time, which is said to explain all things, explained only that grief may become habitual without becoming intelligible.

One evening I was crossing Union Square when a man rose from a bench and confronted me. The place was nearly deserted, and the trees moved restlessly in a cold wind. I recognized him with difficulty: he was the man whom I had followed to the secret meeting.

Dissipation had completed its work. His body was bent, his hair white in patches, his lips tremulous. Yet his eyes burned with the same hunted light.

He stared at me and recoiled.

“John Stevens!” he said.

I made no reply.

“You are dead.”

“So I have been told,” I answered.

He uttered a sound between a curse and a sob. “You drove her to it. You came between us—then you followed me. Do you think I have forgotten?”

“Who are you?” I demanded. “What was she to you?”

At Julia’s name, which I then spoke, he raised his arm and struck at my face. The blow scarcely touched me. He staggered, clutched his breast, and fell backward upon the pavement.

I summoned assistance, but before anyone arrived he was dead.

Nothing upon him established his identity. His pockets contained a little money, a key without a number and a scrap of paper on which were written two words, blurred beyond certainty. One may have been “Julia”; the other may have been “John.” The authorities recorded the death as heart failure, which was medically sufficient. They did not record that terror had preceded it.

You ask whether he saw in me only a resemblance, or whether the dead for one instant looked through my eyes. I do not know. Nor can I tell you why I knew Margovan’s name, why John desired the address, why I followed the stranger, or by what faculty I heard my brother’s dying appeal across half the city. Perhaps between twins there exists a channel which ordinary birth does not provide; perhaps fear and coincidence, acting upon a disordered memory, have constructed the whole design after the event. I submit the facts—not the interpretation.

As for the ruined man’s declaration that John was dead, it should have reassured him. To know of a man that he is dead should be enough.

Your friend,  
**Henry Stevens**

It really was most unfortunate.

Peggy’s temperature had risen to a hundred and three; she had been sick twice, and complained of a pain in her right side. Mrs. Workington Bancroft, who had once passed six weeks in a nursing home and therefore possessed medical instincts, pronounced the case appendicitis.

“If only Sir James had not gone back to Liverpool!” she said.

Sir James Pritchard had been staying at Brackenrigg Farm, seven miles across the moor. Whether he had gone that morning or intended to go on the morrow nobody knew. The telephone, installed chiefly to impress visitors, had ceased working in the rain.

Mr. Bancroft had taken the jaunting-car to the station. The chauffeur was in York with the motor. The groom had lamed himself at football, and the butler suffered from bronchitis whenever asked to leave the house after dusk.

There remained Miss Craig.

Mrs. Bancroft had a graceful manner of allowing inferiors the privilege of offering the services which it was her right to command.

“My dear Miss Craig,” she said, entering the schoolroom, “I would never dream of asking you to walk to Brackenrigg on such an evening.”

Miss Craig looked at Peggy, whose cheeks were unnaturally red above the white sheet.

“Of course I shall go.”

“But the moor!”

“It is not yet six.”

“And you know the way?”

“Past the shooting butts, over Graywife Beck, and along the glebe road.”

“How brave of you! Cook shall put up some sandwiches. You may take Caesar.”

Caesar was a large retriever whose courage had never been tested except by the cat. He followed Miss Craig willingly as far as the servants’ hall, where the cook was cutting bread.

“You’ll not be crossing by Graywife Beck after dark?” the cook asked.

“I hope to return before dark.”

“Hope’s a poor lantern on t’moor.”

Mrs. Bancroft, who had followed to see that no unnecessary quantity of chicken was put into the sandwiches, laughed delicately.

“Cook believes every rustic tale she hears.”

“I’ve heard them as has seen Parson Lyle,” said the cook. “Wet nights, he walks from t’stone trough to t’old butts. At nine o’clock he’s gone.”

“Gone where?” asked Miss Craig.

“Where dead men go, miss.”

Mrs. Bancroft’s laugh became firmer.

“Really, Cook! You will frighten Miss Craig.”

“No, madam,” said Miss Craig, though this was untrue.

She buttoned her mackintosh, took the lantern, and set out with Caesar.

For the first mile the rain was merely tiresome. It hung in the air as a fine grey dust, obscuring the hills without seeming to fall. Behind her, Bancroft Hall showed a row of yellow windows; before her, the moor rose blackly beneath a colourless sky.

Caesar trotted ahead, investigating heather and road metal with equal gravity. His companionship was comforting. A governess might be socially negligible to a dog, but she was at least a person.

At the fork by Carter’s Plantation, however, Caesar stopped.

“Come along.”

He looked at the road to Brackenrigg, then at the road home. The latter led toward supper.

“Caesar!”

He wagged his tail apologetically and departed at a gallop. It appeared that, upon consideration, she was only the governess.

Miss Craig continued alone.

Darkness came without sunset. The rain thickened, and the familiar landmarks dissolved. A grouse burst from the heather almost under her feet, crying as it flew:

“Go back, go back, go back!”

She told herself that the human ear imposed words upon senseless noises. Nevertheless, when another bird repeated the warning farther off, she walked more quickly.

At Graywife Beck the water covered the stepping-stones. She crossed with one hand against the rough stone trough which stood beside the ford. Beyond it the path became a pale strip between the heather. Once she mistook a broken post for a crouching man; once a sheep stared from behind a wall with eyes like green lamps.

Brackenrigg Farm appeared at last as three squares of light.

The farmer’s wife opened the door, wiping her hands upon her apron.

“Sir James? Nay, miss. He left by the noon train. Got word there was an operation waiting.”

Miss Craig leaned against the doorpost.

“Is there no other doctor?”

“Dr. Bell’s at Settle, and that’s twelve miles if it’s one. Come in and dry yourself.”

But Peggy’s burning face had become fixed in Miss Craig’s mind. She wrote a note asking the farmer to send a boy on horseback to Settle, accepted hot tea, and refused a bed.

“You cannot go back alone,” said the farmer’s wife.

“There is a sick child.”

“There’s always sick children. There’s not always foolish young ladies.”

Miss Craig, being a governess, could not claim the latter description, but she thanked her and went.

The wind had turned. It drove the rain directly into her face and extinguished the lantern before she reached the glebe land. Matches were useless. The darkness was complete except where the clouds occasionally thinned over a watery moon.

She began to think of the cook’s ghost.

She wondered what the bark of a ghost might be like, and whether Caesar, confronted with one, would bark at all. This reflection was so ridiculous that she almost laughed. The laugh did not come. There was a footstep behind her.

It ceased when she ceased.

She walked again.

So did it.

A man’s voice said, quite near, “A poor night for walking.”

Miss Craig turned sharply.

The speaker was tall and thin, wearing a broad-brimmed hat and a long dark coat. The pale band at his throat showed him to be a clergyman.

“I fear I startled you,” he said.

“A little.”

“You are going toward Bancroft Hall?”

“Yes.”

“So am I, for part of the road. May I accompany you?”

His voice was cultivated and gentle. Miss Craig felt an immense relief.

They walked side by side. Curiously, she could hear only her own footsteps, but the road was soft with rain and his tread might have been lighter.

“You are afraid of the moor,” he said.

“Not ordinarily.”

“Night gives common things an uncommon authority. A pool becomes a pit, a bush a conspirator. Yet the things we imagine are often kinder than the things we know.”

They had reached the stone trough. The grouse called once from the darkness.

“Go back, go back, go back!”

The clergyman paused.

“Eleven years ago tonight,” he said, “I passed this place at almost this hour. That walk was the turning point of my life.”

Miss Craig glanced at him. “Were you lost?”

“In a manner of speaking.”

He rested one hand on the trough.

“I had been visiting a dying labourer beyond Brackenrigg. He was a good man, according to the easy language we use when goodness has been made inevitable by poverty. His wife had seven children. There was no food in the house, yet he asked me whether God would forgive him for having doubted.”

The clergyman resumed walking.

“I had preached upon Job the Sunday before. As I crossed the moor I found myself considering that unfortunate man—not as an emblem of patience, but as a piece upon a chessboard. God and Satan contend; the children die, the herds are destroyed, the body is tormented; and commentators admire the game.”

The rain hissed upon the heather.

“I was young enough to demand justice from the universe. The darkness seemed infinite, and every human habitation no more than a lamp set afloat upon it. I wondered whether evil were not the ocean and goodness merely those few twinkling lights.”

Miss Craig wished he would choose a more cheerful subject. Yet his measured voice kept the darkness at a distance.

“At this very spot,” he continued, “a man came out from behind the trough.”

Miss Craig looked back involuntarily.

“He wore a torn soldier’s coat. His boots were tied with string. In his hand he carried an ash stake, sharpened at one end.”

The clergyman’s tone remained calm.

“He said, ‘Give us a copper, guv’ner.’

“I gave him sixpence. He looked at it and laughed.

“‘You’ve more than that.’

“I told him I had very little. He replied that gentlemen always said so. He walked beside me, as I am walking beside you now.”

Miss Craig became aware that they were approaching the abandoned shooting butts. Their earthen banks rose like graves against the sky.

“I attempted conversation. I asked whether he had served in the army. He said he had served everybody and been paid by nobody. Then he demanded my purse.

“I refused—not courageously, I think, but from an instinctive absurdity. The purse contained fourteen shillings and a railway ticket. I told him he ought to be ashamed.

“That amused him. He drew a clasp knife, though he did not open it. Then he asked the time.”

The clergyman looked toward the hidden sky.

“I had a watch. It was eight minutes to nine.”

Miss Craig’s hand went to the little watch pinned beneath her collar, but she did not take it out.

“We had reached the farther butt. There was a gap in the wall and a heap of broken road metal. He said nobody would find me there before morning.

“I understood him then. It is strange how reluctantly the mind accepts its own extinction. One thinks first of appointments, letters unanswered, boots left to be mended. I thought of my sermon for Sunday.

“I gave him the purse.

“He weighed it in his hand. ‘Too late now, guv’ner,’ he said.”

A sudden gust moved across the moor. Miss Craig heard something beat softly against the clergyman’s coat.

“He struck from behind. There was no pain—not what we call pain. Only a flash of lightning running downward through the spine, and then the sensation of falling an immeasurable distance into light.

“I lay with my face against the wet stones. I heard him breathing as he searched my pockets. The sound grew faint. Everything grew gentle and distant: the rain, his boots, the patter of my watch where it had fallen and still continued ticking.

“And then there was an extraordinary happiness. The lamps I had imagined scattered upon darkness were no longer small or distant. I saw that the darkness lay only between them because I had mistaken my own blindness for the shape of the world.”

Miss Craig stopped.

The clergyman went two paces farther and turned.

“How do you know?” she whispered.

“Know what?”

“What it was like—after he struck you?”

For the first time his face became distinct. It was a narrow, kindly face, colourless in the faint moonlight. Above the left temple appeared a dark, crushed hollow.

He smiled with great sadness.

“My dear young lady,” he said, “how else should I tell you?”

Somewhere beyond the moor, Bancroft Hall’s clock began to strike.

At the first stroke, the clouds passed from the moon.

Miss Craig stood alone beside the shooting butt.

At the second, she saw the wet road running empty behind her.

At the third, something rustled in the heather.

She ran.

The rain whipped her eyes. Her shoes slipped on the stones. Behind her the clock continued its deliberate counting, each note spreading across the moor.

Six.

Seven.

The gates of Bancroft Hall appeared.

Eight.

She stumbled through them, crossed the lawn, and struck the front door with both hands.

Nine.

The butler opened it. His candle cast a solid, blessed shadow upon the wall.

Miss Craig fell into the hall, her teeth chattering, while far away on the empty moor the grouse cried once more.

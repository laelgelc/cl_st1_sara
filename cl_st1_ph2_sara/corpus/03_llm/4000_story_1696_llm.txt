Purple, you will perceive, is not merely a color. It is a proclamation.

Emperors have employed it to distinguish themselves from grocers; bishops have found it useful; and ladies with accounts at fashionable establishments know that a purple gown, properly cut, can convert an ordinary entrance into a historical event. But emperors do not save ten cents by declining pie at luncheon, and bishops seldom stitch shirtwaists ten hours a day beneath electric lights. Therefore neither can understand what purple meant to Maida Flynn.

You, madam, who summon six gowns and dismiss five because the sleeves possess insufficient soul, may listen—but you cannot know. Not quite.

Maida worked in Bachman’s store, in the ready-made waists, at six dollars a week. Mr. Bachman paid his clerks with a caution that suggested every dollar had been personally entrusted to him by a dying relative. Once a year, however, he gave them a Thanksgiving dinner. For eleven months afterward he referred to the dinner whenever anyone mentioned wages.

“Didn’t we have turkey?” he would demand.

Turkey, in Mr. Bachman’s philosophy, was a form of profit-sharing.

The head clerk was Mr. Ramsay. He had smooth brown hair, a grave chin, and theories concerning health. These theories were numerous enough to furnish a medical college and sufficiently original to close one. He believed rain invigorating, draughts wholesome, breakfast harmful, and overshoes the invention of weak-minded rubber manufacturers. The girls admired him. Men with opinions are often admired by those who do not have to live with them.

Maida admired him most quietly.

Grace Burrows admired him most audibly.

Grace sold ribbons at the next counter. She had cinnamon-colored hair, quick blue eyes, and a rhinestone brooch that flashed like an unpaid bill. Her conversation had the medicinal briskness of peppermint drops.

“Ramsay’s awful swell,” she said. “And he likes red. Any girl can see that.”

“How?” asked Maida.

“He looked twice at my red bow Tuesday.”

“Maybe it was crooked.”

Grace stared. “You’re a sly one, Maida Flynn.”

Maida was not sly. She was twenty, pale from indoor work, and had brown eyes which seemed too large because the rest of her was so slight. But she had determination. Determination, when it inhabits a small shopgirl, receives no statues and very few notices. Nevertheless, it can perform miracles with six dollars a week.

For four months Maida saved.

She saved nickels, dimes, streetcar fares, desserts, gloves, and the privilege of being warm. She walked to work unless the snow rose above the tops of her shoes. She drank coffee without milk and told the boarding-house landlady that she preferred it so. She mended stockings until they consisted chiefly of moral character.

The object of these economies hung unfinished in the shop of Schlegel, the tailor: a purple dress.

Not a timid violet, mind you. Not lavender, lilac, plum, mauve, orchid, grape, or any of those apologetic relatives of purple. It was the royal tint itself—a deep, triumphant purple that would have caused Cleopatra to telephone her dressmaker.

It had a fitted bodice, a narrow panel, six smoked pearl buttons, gathered sleeves, a collar of imitation lace, and a skirt that fell in lines which Schlegel declared were “so graceful like music.” The entire creation cost eighteen dollars, alteration included.

Grace bought red.

Her dress was scarlet, with black braid, brass buttons, a pointed belt, and enough confidence for three women.

“It’s me for red,” she announced at luncheon. “Purple makes a girl look like a funeral wreath.”

Maida continued eating her bread.

“My dress is nearly done,” she said.

“Mine’s done now. Ramsay’ll notice it at the dinner.”

“He may.”

“He likes cheerful colors.”

“Maybe.”

Grace frowned. Maida’s soft replies were troublesome. You could not quarrel successfully with them; they lay down before attack and permitted it to fall over.

The night before Thanksgiving, Maida went to Schlegel’s with fourteen dollars in her purse. Four more dollars would be due before the dress could leave the premises, but Friday was payday, and Schlegel had promised to finish the gown by six.

She had arranged everything. At half past six she would take the dress, at seven she would dress in the back room at the store, and at seven-thirty Mr. Bachman’s annual benevolence would begin with oyster soup. Mr. Ramsay would see her in purple. The universe would then proceed as it thought best.

At five o’clock Grace came to Maida’s counter.

Her red dress was in a paper box beneath her arm, but her face had lost all its peppermint. Her eyes were redder than the gown.

“What is it?” Maida asked.

“Nothing.”

Grace sniffed.

“Tell me.”

“My landlady. She says out I go tonight if I don’t pay four dollars.”

“Four?”

“I spent it.” Grace touched the dress box. “I thought I could put her off. She says she’ll set my trunk in the hall. I haven’t anywhere to go.”

Sniffle, gulp, sniffle—oh, bother! Tears are awkward things in a department store. They spot ribbons, alarm customers, and expose the fact that young women who sell holiday luxuries may themselves lack four dollars’ worth of shelter.

Maida opened her purse.

Inside lay two five-dollar bills and four ones—the final treasury of months of denial. The four dollars were Schlegel’s. They were also a roof, a room, and Grace’s battered little trunk safe from public disgrace.

Here, madam with the six gowns, is the place where you may misunderstand Maida. You may say she ought to have offered two dollars, or demanded the return of the red dress, or proposed an installment arrangement. But poverty does not always make people calculating. Sometimes it makes them magnificent.

Maida gave Grace the four one-dollar bills.

“Pay her.”

Grace stared.

“But your dress?”

“I’ll get it later.”

“You’ll miss the dinner.”

“I suppose so.”

“And Ramsay—”

“Go pay your rent.”

Grace embraced her with one arm, preserving the red dress with the other. Gratitude is sincere, but a new gown must not be crushed.

At six-fifteen Maida stood in Schlegel’s shop while rain worried the windows.

“Four dollars yet,” said Schlegel, holding out his hand.

“I haven’t it.”

“No?”

“I gave it to somebody.”

The tailor’s eyebrows rose. He was a square little man with gray bristles and spectacles through which the world had apparently disappointed him for forty years.

“You gif away my four dollars?”

“I gave away mine. The dress can stay.”

“Stay! After I finish him? After buttons, hooks, pressing, two alterations because somebody is growing thinner from foolishness?”

“I’m sorry.”

“Sorry!” cried Schlegel. “Sorry buys silk now, perhaps? Sorry pays gas? You take him off. I sell him to a duchess.”

“There aren’t many duchesses my size,” said Maida.

Schlegel glared. Then he thrust the dress behind a curtain.

“Go. You make me nervous.”

So Maida went home in her old brown skirt and black jacket while Mr. Bachman’s clerks gathered beneath paper garlands.

Grace came in red, brilliant and frightened, like a poppy that suspects the gardener. Mr. Ramsay looked at her twice, perhaps three times. Mr. Bachman carved the turkey thinly. Speeches were made concerning loyalty, prosperity, and the family spirit existing between employer and employees. No one remarked that families generally provide enough food on more than one evening per year.

Maida sat in her boarding-house room and heard the rain.

She did not cry. This is important. Her disappointment was beyond the convenience of tears. She laid out the old blue waist she would wear to work, repaired a loosened button, and imagined the purple dress hanging in darkness.

At nine came a pounding below.

“Miss Flynn!” called the landlady. “A parcel!”

It was enormous, wrapped in brown paper, and accompanied by a note written in violent pencil:

You take the dress. Pay one dollar each week. If you spoil it in rain, you are a crazy girl. Schlegel.

Maida tore the paper.

Purple flowed into the room.

There are moments when the daughters of man become queens without the assistance of crowns, inheritances, or newspaper photographers. Maida held the dress against her and saw in the cracked mirror not silk, not buttons, not eighteen dollars—but the lunches she had refused, the miles she had walked, the cold mornings, the quiet hopes. She saw patience made visible.

In ten minutes she wore it.

The dinner would be nearly over. The streets were streaming. Sensible persons were indoors, and Maida possessed neither umbrella nor overshoes.

But the dress had waited four months to exist upon her. Must purple submit to weather?

She put on her poorest coat, lifted her skirt, and went out.

The rain descended with the enthusiasm of a municipal contractor paid by the gallon. It drummed on awnings, polished sidewalks, filled gutters, and drove citizens into doorways. Maida walked through it radiant. Beneath her coat the purple skirt flashed at every step—a banner, a secret sunset, a little private rainbow travelling east.

Rich ladies, avert your eyes. You cannot comprehend the happiness of wearing at last the one beautiful thing for which you have paid in hunger, cold, and self-command. Your jewels may be finer; your joy is ready-made.

At the corner of Eighth Street a tall figure emerged from the rain without an umbrella.

It was Mr. Ramsay.

“Miss Flynn!”

Maida stopped.

He looked at her—first at her wet hair, then at the purple beneath her open coat, and then again at her face. Approval softened his grave chin.

“Why, you look—upon my word, you look wonderful.”

“Thank you,” said Maida.

“And out in weather like this! I’m glad to find one young woman with sense. Rain is nature’s tonic. The pores respond to it. Most people wrap themselves in rubber and practically pickle their constitutions.”

“Yes,” said Maida, though water was travelling down her neck.

“I’ve often noticed your intelligence at the store,” he continued. This was not strictly true, but romance has never applied for a position in the auditing department. “The others laugh at my health principles. You understand them.”

Maida thought of Grace in red beneath the paper garlands; of four dollars; of Schlegel’s angry pencil; of purple moving bravely through rain.

“Yes,” she said again.

Mr. Ramsay offered his arm.

“May I walk with you?”

Maida looked up. Her brown eyes shone; her cheeks, which the rain had left pale, suddenly took the rival color from Grace’s victorious gown.

She put her hand upon his sleeve.

Then Maida blushed—and sneezed.

### THE HOME EDITION

When a writer has worn out three pencils, two editors, and the patience of her landlady without finding a story, she goes down into the city and hunts one.

At least, I do.

There are authors, I know, who sit before a rosewood desk and wait for a winged idea to flutter in through the casement. They generally have casements. Also rosewood desks. Their ideas wear pale Greek garments and arrive to the sound of invisible harps.

Mine come up South Clark Street, usually with mud on their shoes.

So on a gray afternoon in November I went to Tony Baldassaro’s newsstand, where the printed troubles of the world were hung out like family washing. Tony kept newspapers from everywhere that had discovered movable type. Paris leaned against Peoria. Stockholm lay cheek by jowl with Springfield. A Polish weekly flapped over a racing sheet, and a newspaper from Naples, yellow and voluble-looking, nudged the London Times with the familiarity of a man who has met a duke in a lodging-house kitchen.

Clark Street flowed past Tony’s in both directions and several moral dimensions. It was a street with diamonds in its ears and holes in its stockings. Marble hotel fronts looked down on pawnshop windows; a five-cent soup sign steamed beside an automobile agency; a tailor displayed evening coats over a basement where one might sleep for a quarter, provided one brought neither luggage nor expectations. There were smells of coffee, wet wool, frying onions, bananas, printer’s ink, damp pavements, and the everlasting breath of the elevated trains.

Men hurried through it with samples, subpoenas, carnations, and schemes. Women passed in sable, imitation sable, and no sable at all. A recruiting sergeant stood beneath a sign promising adventure, while across the way a lodging-house lounger demonstrated its probable conclusion.

There were chop-suey bowls painted on windows, spaghetti words curling over doors, Hebrew letters, Greek letters, and English letters that said:

MONEY LOANED

BEDS 25 CENTS

EAT HERE

The last was plainly the most reckless invitation.

Tony himself stood in the middle of this international republic, selling home to exiles at five cents a copy.

“Got a story for me, Tony?” I asked.

“Sure,” he said. “Fellow got his pocket picked here Tuesday.”

“That isn’t a story.”

“Was twelve dollars in it.”

“Not enough.”

“Plenty for him.”

Tony handed change to a small dark man who tucked a Spanish paper inside his coat as carefully as if it were a legal document restoring his kingdom.

“What I need,” I explained, “is two people, one coincidence, a little heart interest, and a finish that will make an editor say, ‘Well, now, there’s a human thing.’”

“Try the marriage-license bureau.”

“Too much heart interest.”

“Stockyards?”

“Too human.”

Tony shrugged. He had the contempt of commerce for art. Then he called to a customer, “Tribune? Two cents. Nix, that’s yesterday’s. You want history, go to the library.”

I lingered. A copy-hunter must have patience. The city does not ring a bell before performing.

That was when Mercedes Meron came up from the north and Guy Peel came down from the south.

They arrived at Tony’s counter together, which is the sort of thing that makes a writer forgive Providence for the slow opening.

Mercedes came first by half a high-heeled step. She wore a coat with enough fur on it to suggest that several animals had made serious errors in judgment. Her hair was the color of new copper, her cheeks had been assisted beyond nature’s original intention, and from her hat rose a black willow plume that drooped over one shoulder with the exhausted grandeur of a prima donna after the sixth curtain call.

The plume entered a room before Mercedes and left after her. It bowed to policemen, swept menus, threatened babies, and caused persons seated behind her in theaters to doubt the existence of the stage.

She was not more than twenty-eight, though electric signs, midnight suppers, cold cream, rehearsal rooms, and men named Izzy had each tried to put in a claim. Chicago had taken the country girl out of her, hammered her thin, plated her brightly, and returned her to the world in a metallic cast.

Guy Peel was forty, perhaps, and constructed on a different architectural plan. He was long in the leg, brown in the face, broad in the shoulder, and narrow where his clothes considered it appropriate. His hat was western, his overcoat English, and his boots had known distances. He moved with that loose economy acquired by men who spend much time on horseback and very little in drawing-rooms.

His most conspicuous feature was an Adam’s apple which rose and fell in conversation like a man signaling from a well.

Mercedes laid a gloved finger on the counter.

“Say, Tony, got the Kewaskum Courier?”

At the same instant Guy said:

“The London Times, if you please.”

Tony reached backward with both hands. He could find a newspaper from any town in darkness, during an earthquake, while arguing about baseball.

“Courier come this morning,” he said. “Times three days old. Ocean ain’t paved yet.”

Mercedes glanced at Guy’s paper. Guy glanced at hers.

Now, simultaneous glances do not constitute a story. I mention this because young writers are too hopeful. Simultaneous glances become a story only when one of the owners says something indiscreet.

Mercedes did.

“London,” she said. “That your home town?”

Guy’s Adam’s apple made one complete trip.

“Not exactly. My people live in Sussex.”

“Sussex.” She tasted it doubtfully. “That near Milwaukee?”

“Not very.”

“Oh. England, huh?”

“Yes.”

“Thought so. You talk like the swell heavy in an English bedroom farce.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Don’t. It ain’t your fault.”

She opened the Courier. Her manner changed at once. The bright, wary look went out of her face. Beneath the rouge something young and defenseless appeared, looked around, and was frightened back.

Guy observed it. So did I. Tony did not; he was selling a racing sheet to a man who intended to improve the breed of horses by losing rent money on them.

Mercedes ran her finger down a column.

“‘Mrs. E. H. Densmore entertained the Lutheran Ladies’ Aid Thursday afternoon,’” she read softly. “‘Refreshments consisted of coffee, sandwiches, pickles, and angel cake.’ Angel cake! My mother makes one high enough to hide behind.”

She laughed, but the laugh broke in the middle.

Guy unfolded the Times with excessive attention. Englishmen are supposed to be unable to recognize emotion unless it has been formally presented. This is nonsense. They recognize it and then look for a fireplace.

Mercedes turned a page.

“Here’s my kid sister. ‘Miss Cora Hayes is home from normal school for the Thanksgiving holidays.’ Cora. She had two pigtails when I beat it. Used to trail after me, bawling because I wouldn’t let her wear my blue beads.”

“Hayes?” Guy asked.

“That’s right.”

“I thought your name was Meron.”

“It is, professionally.”

“You are on the stage?”

“Near it. I do a specialty at the Golden Pheasant. Two songs, one dance, and a costume that wouldn’t keep a mosquito warm. Mercedes Meron looks better on a bill than Sadie Hayes. You can’t put SADIE HAYES in electric lights. Sounds like somebody that wins a prize for canned tomatoes.”

She looked at him sideways.

“What’re you doing in this town, Sir Walter Raleigh?”

“Buying cattle.”

“For a packing house?”

“For myself. I have a ranch in Texas.”

That interested her. Ranches possess a dramatic value even for ladies whose outdoor experience is limited to crossing streets.

“No fooling? Cowboys and broncs and all?”

“And a great many bills.”

“You don’t look like a regular ranch guy.”

“I have been one for fourteen years.”

“How’d an Englishman get mixed up in Texas?”

Guy folded his paper. Some persons guard their private affairs with locks and dogs; others surrender them upon being asked the right question by a woman with an outrageous plume.

“My elder brother inherited the family property,” he said. “There was little for me, and less that I cared to do. I went to Canada first, then south. I worked for wages, learned cattle, saved money, borrowed more than was wise, and finally bought land of my own.”

“Made good, did you?”

“I have been fortunate.”

His clothes said he had been fortunate. So did the quiet way he spoke of borrowing money. A poor man mentions the amount.

Mercedes tapped the London paper.

“Then what’s eating you?”

“I don’t understand.”

“Yes, you do. You’re standing here in Chicago buying a three-day-old paper to read what some duke said about turnips. You got the home-town fever, same as me.”

A little color moved under Guy’s brown skin.

“Perhaps.”

“That means yes in English.”

“It is some years since I have seen my people.”

“Fourteen, you said.”

“Nearly fifteen.”

“Lord! Ain’t you ever wanted to go back?”

“Frequently.”

“Why don’t you?”

This is one of the great questions of the world, and it is usually asked by someone who has not yet paid for the answer.

Guy looked beyond her into Clark Street. Rain had begun. Under the elevated structure the lamps came on prematurely, making pools of dirty gold. A newsboy ran past calling disaster in three syllables. Taxis complained. A woman argued with an umbrella. Chicago had reached that hour when it takes off its business collar and puts on its electric jewelry.

“I hardly know,” he said. “There is always the ranch. Men depend upon me. Stock must be moved. A well goes wrong. Prices change. One postpones.”

“Sure. One’s yellow.”

“Possibly.”

Mercedes bent over her little paper.

“I’m yellow too,” she said. “That’s why I know.”

Guy waited.

The writer who sees two people becoming confidential should retire. She should respect the sacred privacy of the human soul.

I moved six inches nearer.

Mercedes seemed to be reading the Courier, but what she saw was not print.

“My father kept the hardware store,” she said. “He wanted me to marry a fellow named Ed Bruckner. Ed had a chin like a doorstop and all the romance of a bag of nails. I wanted music and lights and people clapping. A show came through Fond du Lac, and I went away with it. Not the way you’re thinking, either. The leading lady hired me to mind her kid and press skirts.”

“I was not thinking—”

“Oh, yes, you were. Everybody does. Saves time.”

She smiled without pleasure.

“After that I got into the chorus. Then vaudeville. Then this specialty. My old man wrote once: ‘You have chosen your life. Do not bring disgrace to your mother’s door.’ I wrote back that I wouldn’t bring anything to his door, not even a Christmas card. That was eight years ago.”

“And your mother?”

“She sent me two letters on the sly. First one said they prayed for me. Second one said Cora had the measles. Funny, ain’t it? Eight years, and what I remember is bread dough rising behind the stove, the red geranium in the kitchen window, and Ma shaking the tablecloth off the back porch. I can see every crumb go down to the chickens.”

The plume trembled. An author of the old school would now have arranged one large tear upon Mercedes’ cheek, where it would have glittered through rouge like a diamond in a sunset.

Mercedes sniffed instead.

“Gee! Listen to me. Next I’ll be singing ‘The Old Oaken Bucket’ and passing the hat.”

Guy’s face had softened.

“I remember a yew hedge after rain,” he said. “And the sound of rooks in the trees before breakfast. My mother’s silver tea service was always placed at the same end of the table. There is a long slope behind the house, and in spring it is covered with daffodils.”

“Any bread pudding?”

“An excellent one.”

“That settles it. Home is home.”

“Is it?”

“Sure. We’re a couple of boobs, standing here buying papers when we could buy tickets.”

Guy smiled.

“To Kewaskum and Sussex?”

“You said it.”

“Would they welcome us?”

Mercedes shut the Courier sharply.

“Listen, mister. Every story I ever saw says they would. The prodigal kid blows in, the old mother stretches out her arms, father forgives everything, the dog remembers, and somebody kills a fatted calf. Maybe we don’t rate a calf, but we ought to take a chance on sandwiches and angel cake.”

“You place considerable confidence in stories.”

“I work in show business. I got to believe something.”

“And if reality proves less obliging?”

“Then we beat it again. Trains run both ways.”

She held out her hand.

“I’m going. Christmas. Kewaskum, Wisconsin. Sadie Hayes walks up the front path and takes what’s coming. How about you?”

Guy looked at the hand. It was gloved in pale gray and carried too many rings. Yet there was something solemn in the gesture.

He took it.

“I shall go to England.”

“When?”

“As soon as I can arrange matters.”

“Nix. That means never. Pick a date.”

“Christmas,” he said.

“Shake again.”

They shook again.

Tony looked over from his cash box.

“You folks buying them papers or getting married over ’em?”

Mercedes tossed down a coin.

“Keep the change, Tony. I’m going home.”

Guy paid for the Times.

“So am I.”

They walked away in opposite directions, she north beneath the swaying plume, he south beneath the western hat.

And there, I thought, was my story.

I saw it complete: two exiles, two returns, two doors flung open, two mothers crying, perhaps one father stern at first but melting by paragraph thirty-seven. Editors enjoy parallel construction. It gives them the feeling that life has been properly proofread.

I went home and wrote the opening.

Then I stopped.

You cannot finish a return before the traveler arrives. Fiction may gallop, but truth demands railroad time. I put the pages in a drawer labeled “Home People,” which also contained a lady who had married a burglar, an honest alderman for whom I had never found a plausible city, and three promising orphans.

Christmas passed. Snow turned Clark Street respectable for nearly twenty minutes. Spring arrived with soot in its eye. Summer baked the pavements and made every restaurant smell of yesterday’s cabbage. Autumn blew theater posters along the gutters.

A year went by.

You may object that this is a lazy way to handle time. Very well. You sit down and describe three hundred and sixty-five days on South Clark Street. I will wait.

At the end of the year, on an afternoon so like the first that the weather itself seemed short of new material, I returned to Tony’s stand.

“You owe me the finish of a story,” I told him.

“Put it on my bill.”

“Red-haired woman. Big plume. Englishman with an Adam’s apple.”

Tony considered.

“I sell papers, not spare parts.”

“They met here last November. Both went home.”

“Everybody goes home.”

“No, Tony. Everybody goes somewhere to sleep. It isn’t the same thing.”

He was about to answer when he looked past me.

“Here’s your feather.”

Mercedes came up from the north.

The plume was there, but altered. It was smaller, firmer, and perched at a businesslike angle. Mercedes herself had changed. She still wore cosmetics, but they no longer seemed to be fighting for possession of her face. Her coat was handsome without requiring a census of fur-bearing animals. On one finger was a plain wedding ring, which looked surprised at its surroundings.

“Hello, Tony,” she said. “Got the evening Chicago papers?”

Tony stared at her.

“No Kewaskum Courier?”

“Not if you wrap fish in it.”

Before I could pounce, Guy Peel came down from the south.

He was browner than ever, though the season did not account for it. His English overcoat was gone. He wore a serviceable western jacket, and his boots carried pale dust in the seams. The Adam’s apple remained. Time had been unable to persuade it to retire.

He saw Mercedes.

“Miss Meron!”

“Hold it,” she said, displaying the ring. “Mrs. Ike Rosenthal.”

“Indeed? My congratulations.”

“Thanks. Ike was my manager. He says he married me to keep me from signing contracts without reading ’em.”

“You went home, then?”

“Did you?”

They stood before Tony’s papers as they had stood a year earlier. But now neither reached for the sheet of childhood.

I leaned against the counter with the innocent expression of a detective hidden behind a curtain.

Mercedes spoke first.

“I went Christmas Eve. Snow all over the place. Looked just like a holiday postal card—white church, yellow windows, sleigh bells, the whole works. I nearly cried when the conductor called the station.”

“And your family?”

“Ma cried enough for both of us. Cora cried. Even Pa’s eyes got damp, though he said it was the cold. They had my room ready. Same patchwork quilt. Same picture of a little girl crossing a bridge with an angel behind her. I used to think the angel was following to push her off.”

“You were welcomed.”

“Like the lost sheep, calf, coin, and umbrella all rolled together. Pa made a speech. Said the past was buried. Then he looked at my dress as if he hoped it was buried deep.”

Guy’s mouth twitched.

“It was difficult?”

“Not at first. At first it was swell. Ma made chicken and dumplings. Cora showed me her school books. Neighbors came in and squeezed my hands and said, ‘Sadie, Sadie,’ in the voice people use at funerals. Pa asked me to sing. I sang a hymn.”

“You?”

“Don’t get fresh. I knew two verses.”

“And afterward?”

Mercedes sighed.

“Afterward came morning.”

There is always a morning after the prodigal returns. Stories frequently omit it.

“I woke up at six because the house was so quiet,” she continued. “No elevated banging, no milk wagons rattling, no fellow in the alley hollering about junk, no piano pounding through the wall. Just a clock going tick—tick—tick, like it was counting how long before I screamed.

“I went downstairs. Ma was in the kitchen wearing the same kind of gray wrapper she wore when I was sixteen. She was making bread. There was the geranium. There were the chickens. Every blessed thing I’d remembered. Only it had shrunk. The kitchen was little. The porch was little. The whole town looked like somebody had folded it up to fit a trunk.”

“Yet surely the affection—”

“Oh, they loved me. That was the trouble. They loved Sadie Hayes, aged eighteen, before she got ideas. They kept trying to fit me back into her clothes.”

She touched the wedding ring.

“Cora wasn’t a kid. She was a schoolteacher with opinions. She said the theater was degrading, then asked if I knew any movie stars. Pa wanted me to help in the hardware store. Ed Bruckner came around. He’s got the same chin, only more of it, and four children with runny noses. His wife stared at my rings until I wanted to give her one.”

Guy laughed outright.

Mercedes nodded.

“Go ahead. It gets better. The minister called and talked about the joy of rescue. Ladies invited me to meetings so I could tell how I had been saved. Saved from what? Good pay, decent health, and my name bigger than the acrobats’? They didn’t want facts. They wanted me barefoot in a blizzard with a villain chasing me.”

“And your father?”

“He meant well. He offered to buy me a dark wool dress. Said folks would forget if I behaved quiet. Me—quiet!”

Clark Street answered for her with a train overhead, a taxi horn, a newsboy, and the crash of dishes from a lunchroom.

Mercedes listened as a countrywoman might listen to birds.

“I lasted seven weeks,” she said. “Then Ike wired that a girl had walked out of the new act and would I come back. I read the telegram at breakfast. Ma was pouring coffee. Pa was reading the Courier. The clock was ticking.

“I said, ‘I’m going to Chicago.’

“Pa said, ‘Sadie, this is your home.’

“And I looked at that little room and knew it wasn’t. Not anymore. Maybe it had been once. Maybe I carried a piece of it around so long I polished it into something else. But I wasn’t needed there except as a repentance exhibit. Ike needed me. The act needed me. I needed the lights to go on at eight fifteen.”

“So you returned.”

“On the noon train. Ma packed sandwiches. Pa didn’t speak till we got to the station. Then he gave me twenty dollars.”

Her voice softened.

“He slipped it into my glove like I was still a kid. I didn’t tell him I had eight hundred dollars sewed inside my coat. That twenty nestled against my wrist all the way to Chicago.”

“And you married your manager?”

“Three months later. Ike said, ‘Sadie, you’re a headache, but you’re my headache.’ Ain’t that romance?”

“For some people,” I said, forgetting to remain invisible, “it is practically poetry.”

Mercedes looked at me.

“Who’s the lady?”

“Writes stories,” Tony said. “Can’t mind her own business.”

“Then she’s no lady,” said Mercedes. “What happened to you, cowboy?”

Guy had been fingering the edge of a London newspaper. He withdrew his hand.

“I, too, kept the agreement. I sailed in December.”

“And did the old homestead throw its arms around your neck?”

“In a manner of speaking. My mother is living. My brother has two sons and a daughter. They received me most warmly. The house had scarcely changed. The yew hedge was there. The rooks. The silver service. Even the daffodils appeared in due course.”

“Bread pudding?”

“Excellent.”

Mercedes waited.

“But?” she prompted.

“But I found England rather small.”

“Attaboy.”

“I do not mean the country itself. I mean my place in it. My brother manages the estate. His sons will follow him. My mother wished me near her, and I was glad to be there, but there was nothing for me to do. They had arranged a pleasant room, a pleasant routine, pleasant neighbors. Everything was exceedingly pleasant.”

“Sounds terrible.”

“It became so.”

He smiled, but his eyes had gone beyond us to a harsher country.

“I had forgotten the damp. It entered one’s bones. I had forgotten how early darkness falls in winter, and how hedges shut in the roads. People discussed families I scarcely remembered and events which had occurred before I left. They treated Texas as an unfortunate fever from which I had recovered.”

Mercedes grinned.

“One lady asked whether Indians still attacked my house. Another supposed I raised cattle as a gentlemanly amusement. My brother advised me to sell. He said I could live comfortably near them.”

“Could you?”

“Comfortably, yes.”

“And useless?”

Guy’s Adam’s apple moved once.

“Precisely.”

He picked up the Times, stared at its folded front, and laid it down again.

“At the ranch, before daylight, someone knocks because a horse is sick, or a fence is down, or cattle have broken toward the river. Men wait for my decision. Not because I am admirable, but because I pay them and the land is mine. There are a cook who murders coffee, a foreman who contradicts me, two hounds who sleep on my porch, and a gray mare who kicks anyone else attempting to saddle her.”

“That your family?”

“I suppose it is.”

He spoke now with less Sussex in his voice and more open country.

“I missed the bunkhouse lamps at night. I missed dust, and the dry smell of mesquite after heat. I missed riding until the house disappeared and there was nothing between me and the horizon. In England every field appeared to have been folded, labeled, and placed in a drawer.”

“How long did you stick?”

“Until May. Then my foreman wrote that the north well had failed, three horses had contracted fever, and a neighbor proposed buying my eastern pasture.”

“Music!” said Mercedes.

“I took the next ship.”

“And when you got back?”

“The cook had burned supper. The foreman said, ‘About time.’ The mare bit my sleeve.”

Mercedes held out her hand as she had a year before.

“Welcome home.”

Guy took it.

“The same to you.”

There we were: no fatted calves, unless one counts Texas livestock; no permanent return to ancestral doors; no moral rescue; no villain punished; no heroine purified by a dark wool dress. Merely two people cured of homesickness by going home.

It is poor doctrine for ballads, but excellent medicine for exiles.

Tony cleared his throat.

“You buying something?”

Mercedes turned to the rack.

“Give me the Chicago evening paper. The one with the theatrical notices.”

Guy reached past the London Times.

“And the San Antonio paper for me.”

Tony handed them over.

The Kewaskum Courier remained beneath its wire clip. The London Times rustled beside it. Two old homes lay there in black type, available at the usual price and unwanted.

Mercedes tucked Chicago under her arm. Guy folded San Antonio into his pocket.

Then the willow plume went north, the Adam’s apple went south, and this time each was headed home.

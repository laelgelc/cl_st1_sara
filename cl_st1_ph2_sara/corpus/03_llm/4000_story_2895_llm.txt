## SKETCH FIRST  
### THE CINDER ARCHIPELAGO

> Where neither dewy morn nor evening mild  
> Makes green the ribèd hill or blasted plain,  
> But elder Fire, of his own rage beguiled,  
> Hath left black monuments of ancient pain.

Take, reader, some five-and-twenty heaps of furnace refuse—clinkers, slag, extinguished brands, and the coarse ash which a smith sweeps from beneath his forge; magnify each heap into a mountain, scatter the whole upon a sea bright as molten glass, and, lest any tenderness of Nature mitigate the prospect, forbid the clouds to rain, the soil to deepen, the trees to rise, or the birds of cheerful woodland to sing—and you shall have no unfaithful image of those islands which old pilots called the Encantadas.

They lie under the Equator, but have none of his luxuriance. Elsewhere that mighty girdle of the globe is embroidered with forests; here it seems scorched through. The sun, who in Brazil is a gardener, in these isles is an executioner. Day after day he searches every fissure with a spear of white flame; yet from the sterile wound comes neither sap nor blossom. A few gray shrubs, twisted as though they had died in the act of imploring mercy, adhere to the slopes; cacti lift their distorted arms; and here and there a vine, without leaf or grape, trails over the lava like a cord left after strangulation.

Nor is the barrenness smooth and passive. The land has teeth. Everywhere rise jagged combs of basalt, shattered chimneys, caverns with black throats, and vast congealed streams which look as if the infernal metal had but yesterday run hissing toward the sea. In some places the lava is wrinkled like the hide of a monstrous elephant; in others it is bubbled and burst, as though imprisoned vapors still labored below. Walk heedlessly, and your boot is cut; fall, and the island takes blood. Thus it refuses alike the husbandman’s plough and the pilgrim’s knee.

I first saw the group from the masthead of a South-Seaman, after weeks in which the Pacific had shown us nothing but its circular horizon, its flying-fish, and the occasional black sermon of a whale’s back. At dawn the main-royal-man called down that land stood westward. But land!—the word was too hospitable. What we beheld resembled the cooled fragments of a demolished world. The summits, dim in vapor, seemed smoke-stacks of Tartarus; the lower ridges, red and brown, appeared newly raked from a penal conflagration. No beach invited us. The surf leaped among splintered rocks, not with the regular thunder heard on generous shores, but with short, spiteful reports, as if hidden musketeers fired upon every boat.

Our old captain, who had rounded the Horn seven times and believed himself incapable of surprise, examined the islands through his brass glass, then shut it with an oath of unusual gravity.

“Those countries,” said he, “were made on a Saturday night, when materials had run low.”

The carpenter, a Scot and therefore theological, answered that they were made earlier, but cursed afterward. Between these opinions I shall not adjudicate. It is enough that the curse—whether contemporary with creation or imposed by subsequent decree—appears still operative.

Observe, too, the deception of their waters. One might suppose that islands so naked would at least be honestly approached; but the sea about them is full of covert treacheries. Currents run at variance with wind and compass; a ship steering north finds herself carried west; a boat pulling for a headland beholds it sliding away, while another promontory, which all hands had sworn was astern, advances with spectral deliberation upon the bow. Tide-rips wrinkle the calm at noon; black eddies revolve where no rock is charted; and a vessel, becalmed under a burning sky, may drift all night as rapidly as if towed by drowned Titans.

Hence the Spanish mariners, who first blundered among these shores, believed the islands enchanted. Their charts placed them here, there, and nowhere. A cape seen at sunset was absent at dawn; a mountain altered shape; a channel narrowed into a cul-de-sac; and the very stars, those incorruptible witnesses, seemed bribed by the archipelago. “Las Encantadas!” cried the bewildered pilots; and the name, born of bad reckoning, terror, and perhaps a little Canary wine, was just.

No settled voice disputes the wind there. No village bell tells noon. The tortoise crawls; the lizard watches; the serpent, if serpent there be, keeps his counsel under stones. Reptile reigns over reptile territory. Man comes as pirate, castaway, hunter, or transient butcher; builds a fire; carves a date; leaves a broken cask; and departs, generally with gratitude. The islands remain—black, silent, immutable—as though Time himself, having begun some gigantic inscription, had broken his chisel and abandoned the task.

Yet I confess that their desolateness drew me. Fertile countries welcome and dismiss us; such places seize the imagination by refusal. A green valley asks to be loved: a blasted island demands to be interpreted. Looking upon those cinders, I fancied that they preserved the earliest morning of the earth, before the blessing—or its latest evening, after the judgment. Which of the two? Reader, choose if you can. In the Encantadas, beginnings and endings wear the same face.

## SKETCH SECOND  
### ALBEMARLE AND THE THREE PILGRIMS

> Three ancient shapes from out the hollow stone  
> Came burdened, mute, and steadfast in their woe;  
> No trumpet called, no guiding star was shown,  
> Yet still they pressed where none might bid them go.

We were cruising under the lee of Albemarle, the largest and, to my eye, the most tyrannically shaped of the group. Its northern cape rose in terraces like the ruined seats of a coliseum constructed for demons; inland, volcanic cones stood rank behind rank, some breached upon one side, some perfectly round, all bare. In the slant afternoon light they acquired a mournful magnificence. Their shadows stretched across the ash-plains like the shadows of extinguished pyramids.

Water was low aboard. This, among seamen, converts speculation into business. The most metaphysical island becomes at once a question of casks. We lowered two boats and pulled toward a little inlet where, according to an old manuscript chart, a spring had once been found by a buccaneer, though whether the buccaneer drank from it or merely died looking, the chart did not specify.

The landing was achieved with wet shirts, bruised knees, and language which I shall not preserve. Beyond the surf lay a shelf of black rock, hot enough to discomfort the palm. From it a ravine led inland. There was no spring. There was, indeed, the memory of water—a dry channel, polished stones, and a shallow basin cracked into pentagons like an old man’s heel. Some bitter drops collected beneath an overhang, but they tasted of copper, salt, and lizard. Even thirst paused over them.

While the cooper and two hands explored farther, I climbed a ridge. There, among dwarf bushes, I heard a sound as of a heavy chest dragged over gravel: scrape—pause—scrape. Expecting one of my shipmates, I rounded a block of lava and encountered instead an enormous tortoise.

He stood with one forefoot lifted, his neck stretched, his small head turned toward me. There was nothing rapid in the encounter; nevertheless, I started. A lion springs and affords no leisure for interpretation. The tortoise, by withholding action, imposes thought. He looked antediluvian—not merely old, but anterior to the institution of youth. His shell, cracked, scarred, and overgrown in places with a pale powder, resembled a shield dug from beneath the ruins of Nineveh. The folds about his throat hung like the withered ornaments of a judge. His eye, though minute, was black and lucid; it contained neither alarm nor welcome, but a patience so profound that my own brief human life seemed, under its inspection, an impertinence.

Soon another was discovered; then a third. One had lodged himself between two stones and continued pushing directly forward, though an open passage lay a yard to the left. His forelegs labored, his shell grated, his long neck advanced and withdrew; but to turn aside appeared no part of his philosophy.

“Give him room,” said I, attempting to shift a stone.

“Give him an axe,” said the boat-steerer, “and room in the long-boat.”

Practicality prevailed. These creatures, which on land wore the solemnity of patriarchs, were at sea esteemed chiefly as fresh provisions. Ropes were fetched. With much grunting—ours, not theirs—the three were rolled, levered, hauled, and conveyed toward the beach.

Here arose one of those grotesque processions which no court painter, being anxious for reputation, would willingly depict. First went the cooper with an empty breaker on his shoulder; then four sailors bearing a tortoise slung upon an oar; then two more dragging another over a bed of brush; last came the largest, resisting without haste and prevailing without movement, while five profane Christians disputed his weight, his ancestry, and the probable excellence of his calipee. Above us circled a hawk, perhaps the sole herald permitted by that court of ashes.

At the steep descent our eldest captive extricated one leg from the sling and touched the ground. Instantly he began moving inland. Not hurriedly—not rebelliously—but with the same grave intention as before, as if our ship, our ropes, our appetites, and the Pacific Ocean itself were no more than trifling obstructions accidentally set across an ordained pilgrimage.

Whither went he? Toward water, said the mate. Toward the uplands, said a hunter. Toward the place of his hatching, said a sentimental boy. Perhaps toward nothing. Yet his persistence oppressed me. In a tangled world he carried a straight road within him; and because creation had neglected to lay that road outside, he pressed against stones.

We lifted him again.

At sunset the three lay inverted in the long-boat, their dark legs making slow, astronomical motions in the air. The island receded behind us. I watched its burnt peaks grow purple, then black. From the boat came an occasional scrape of shell against plank.

It sounded like old armor shifting in a tomb.

## SKETCH THIRD  
### THE TORTOISES AT NIGHT

> Full bright beneath, though sable be the shield;  
> The star lies hid where outward night is spread.  
> Judge not the secret by the face revealed,  
> Nor call all darkness wholly born of dread.

The tortoise is a creature of contradictions, and therefore better fitted for emblematic use than many a handsomer beast. Above, he is black, battered, sepulchral; below, his shell may show amber, yellow, and warm brown, as if a fragment of sunset had been shut beneath a thundercloud. Viewed crawling, he seems the poorest slave of gravity; turned upon his back, he becomes a strange celestial instrument, presenting to heaven a polished oval wherein dusky constellations appear congealed.

Our three were placed on the quarter-deck until a more convenient disposition could be made. They neither complained nor visibly despaired. A captured goat protests; a pig impeaches the universe; but these victims conducted themselves like deposed monarchs who had survived too many revolutions to remonstrate against one more.

That night I had the middle watch. The moon, thin as a shaving from a silver spar, hung low; the trade-wind had fallen; and our ship rolled uneasily upon a long swell. Every block creaked. The idle canvas breathed in and out. Astern, the phosphorescent wake opened like a green wound and slowly closed.

About one bell I descended for a coat, and, returning, found the tortoises awake. Or perhaps they had never slept. Their heads were extended; their eyes caught the moon; their feet moved with that measured, futile regularity which, in darkness, seemed less animal than mechanical. One shell struck another: hollowly—solemnly. I stopped.

Reader, ridicule the confession if you choose, but I then experienced a fancy so absolute that for some moments it overmastered sight. The deck widened; the bulwarks vanished; our masts became pillars without summit. The three creatures enlarged into gigantic bearers of the earth, condemned to march beneath its cities, wars, temples, debts, coronations, and graves. On their backs rested Babylon and London, Delhi and Lima; every cathedral pressed upon one scarred plate; every prison upon another. Yet they advanced nowhere. Their feet labored in vacancy.

Then the fancy altered. They were not bearers, but judges—three black-robed assessors sent from some reptilian antiquity to examine mankind. Their accusation was silence. Their evidence was endurance. What defense could we offer? We had dragged them from their path, inverted them, appraised their flesh, and debated the best sauce; they, having perhaps seen two centuries of suns rise over Albemarle, did not so much as hiss.

“Ugly old coffins,” muttered the helmsman behind me.

The words broke the enchantment, though not entirely. Coffins, indeed—but coffins carrying their occupants alive; tombs walking about with patience enclosed.

A squall came near dawn. We shortened sail, and in the confusion one tortoise slid several feet across the wet planks, fetching up against a gun-carriage. There he resumed his motions as calmly as though traversing an island. Neither thunder nor our stamping altered his rate. A man may be brave because danger inflames him; the tortoise seems brave because danger cannot revise his schedule.

With morning, philosophy yielded to appetite. The cook appeared with knives. Sailors gathered, discussing steaks. The sentimental boy who had supposed that one captive sought the place of his hatching now asked whether the yellow fat would fry well. Thus rapidly does sympathy change watches aboard ship.

I will not embellish the slaughter. The creatures died; we ate them. The flesh was rich, delicate, and abundant, and the same men who had mocked their appearance praised their flavor with tears of grease in their eyes. Soup steamed in the forecastle; steaks hissed in the galley; portions were salted down. For two days the ship, lately rationed on hard biscuit and ancient pork, became a floating banquet hall.

But observe the economy of seafaring life, which permits no grandeur to survive without employment. The shells, cleaned and scraped, were not committed to the deep. One became a basin for washing clothes; another held potatoes; a third, inverted upon a cask, served as a table from which the mate ate duff on Sundays. Those shields which my midnight reverie had placed under empires now supported onions, stockings, and molasses.

I once saw the carpenter sit in the largest, drawing oakum from a coil, like a gray child in an enormous cradle.

“Comfortable?” I asked.

“Ay,” said he. “Better than the owner found it.”

There is the human epitaph.

Yet the underside of that shell remained bright. No scraping dulled it. Whenever the vessel rolled and the shell shifted, a mellow gleam appeared beneath its rim. I came to regard this as a small rebuke to summary judgment. Black above, golden below; monstrous in motion, excellent in soup; venerable as Saturn, useful as a wash-tub—the tortoise refuses singular interpretation.

And may not men do the same? The officer most polished outwardly may conceal a cellar of cruelty; the tar whose face resembles a badly repaired boot may harbor charity. We are all shells, though few of us bear our contradiction so visibly.

Still, on quiet nights, when the empty carapaces knocked together in the hold, I remembered the three heads lifted to the moon; and sometimes, between sleep and waking, heard a slow scraping beneath the ship, as if the slaughtered pilgrims had resumed their journey along the floor of the sea.

## SKETCH FOURTH  
### OF REPTILIAN MELANCHOLY

> No lash pursues, no tyrant bids him strain;  
> His will itself becomes the captive’s chain.  
> Through thorn and stone he holds the fatal line,  
> And makes his prison by a law divine.

It has been asserted by persons who prefer a universe of labels that melancholy belongs only to man. I would advise such philosophers to spend an afternoon with an ancient tortoise upon a waterless island.

Not that the creature sighs, quotes elegies, or refuses dinner. His sadness, if sadness it be, lies deeper than conduct. It is architectural. Nature has built sorrow into his form: a low head issuing from a high burden; knees perpetually bent; eyelids wrinkled before birth; a mouth shaped neither for laughter nor defiance; and over all, the house which he must carry yet can never enter wholly. Other animals inhabit the world; the tortoise appears sentenced to transport his own little prison through it.

Some sailors maintain that wicked commodores are after death converted into these reptiles and dispatched to the Encantadas, there to practice upon lava the stubborn courses they once forced upon ships. The belief has conveniences. It explains the imperious neck, the leathery complexion, the ancient eye, and the refusal to alter direction. I knew a lieutenant whose reincarnation, upon this doctrine, must even now be pushing against the same basalt wall with which he first collided in 1819.

But this is forecastle theology. Let us be grave—if gravity can improve the truth.

The gait of the tortoise suggests hopeless labor, yet his very hopelessness contains dignity. He advances not by confidence in arrival, but by obedience to impulse. No prospect cheers him. The hill he climbs reveals another hill; the water-hole may be dry; the cactus-pad may hang beyond reach. Still he lifts the foot, sets it down, and draws the world backward by half an inch.

Compare him with the butterfly, that painted spendthrift of an afternoon, which wanders wherever perfume tempts and dies before disappointment can become a habit. Compare him with the albatross, whose road is the wind and whose mile costs scarcely a feather’s tremor. The tortoise earns every yard. He purchases distance with his body.

Yet do not sentimentalize too confidently. Our pilgrims, for all their martyr-like deportment, were not apostles of gentleness. When two met upon a narrow track, the larger sometimes thrust the smaller aside with a slow brutality more dreadful for lacking passion. At a pool they crowded one another; at food they bit. Antiquity does not abolish appetite. Methuselah, had his dinner been delayed, may have elbowed as vigorously as a cabin-boy.

I remember one ash-colored giant seen upon a later visit. He had fallen into a shallow volcanic pit and could not climb out. The pit was perhaps twelve feet across, and its sloping side presented many possible exits. But he had selected one place, the steepest, and there persisted. He rose; his claws slipped; he descended upon his breast. Again he rose. Again the ash betrayed him. The ground below was scored with the record of his attempts.

We cut a path at an easier angle and tried to turn him toward it. He resisted. We pushed; he withdrew his head. We dragged; he braced his legs. At length, by united force, we set him facing the passage. He considered it, advanced two paces, then wheeled—with surprising decision for so deliberate a being—and returned to his impossible ascent.

“Let the fool alone,” said a seaman.

But what if folly was his one freedom? We imagine that assistance is good because it conforms the sufferer to our map. Perhaps he preferred failure of his own choosing to rescue by ours.

At last we rolled him bodily over the lower rim. He landed upright outside the pit. There he remained some minutes, motionless. Then, instead of departing, he turned and approached the edge, looking down into the hollow whence we had delivered him.

Was it gratitude? Reproach? A wish to re-enter? I know not. His face had been made before expression was invented.

Presently he went away among the cinders. Long after his body disappeared, the top of his shell showed intermittently above the broken ground, like a dark moon setting several times.

Since then, whenever I have observed men laboring at some chosen impossibility—office, fortune, revenge, reputation, a perfect poem, a hopeless love—I have thought of that tortoise and his volcanic pit. We cut easier paths for one another and are offended when they go unused. Each carries his inward north; each believes his steep place the appointed one.

But the beast had one advantage over us: he made no speech about perseverance.

## SKETCH FIFTH  
### ROCK RODONDO

> Amid the watery waste a tower is set,  
> Unbuilt by hand, unblessed by priestly word;  
> Its stairs are storm, its bells with salt are wet,  
> Its windows darkened by the wheeling bird.

Sail ho!

So cries the masthead upon spying a distant canvas; and so I nearly cried on first beholding Rock Rodondo, for at fifteen miles it rose from the sea with the narrow whiteness of a ship under topsails. But as we approached, the supposed vessel neither altered bearing nor betrayed a mast. It broadened, darkened, and at length stood confessed—a solitary tower of stone, thrust sheer from the Pacific.

Rodondo lies apart, a sea-stack rather than an island, though its dignity deserves the latter title. Imagine a cathedral bell-tower shorn from its church and planted in deep water; imagine, further, that earthquake has cracked its walls, surf undermined its base, and ten thousand birds appropriated every cornice; then color it white with guano, black with wet rock, green at the lower ledges with weed, and red where iron stains bleed downward—and you have its portrait, wanting only the scream, the stench, and the incessant flashing of wings.

Our ship passed under its lee in weather so moderate that the captain permitted a boat to sound near the rock. I joined her. As we pulled closer, the tower appeared to rise, not from the sea, but from the bottom of the sky. Its summit overhung us. Caverns opened at the waterline, receiving the swell with booming respirations. Each retreating wave sucked pebbles and shells through hidden passages, producing a rattle like chains dragged under a dungeon floor.

The fish! I must pause for the fish.

In most waters, creatures below regard the boat as a hostile cloud. Here they displayed an innocence almost insolent. Blue, yellow, silver, striped, spotted, blunt, needle-shaped, and fan-tailed, they swarmed beneath us in such profusion that the sea seemed less a medium than a moving mosaic. Some followed the oars; some pecked at the descending lead-line; one green fellow, broad as a platter, stared upward with an expression of comic suspicion, then permitted the blade to graze his fin. We lowered a hook without bait and nearly caught two.

The boat-steerer declared that no fisherman had yet taught them theology.

By this he meant the fall of fish.

Around the base seals lifted polished heads. Above them sat penguins, ranged upon a ledge with the solemn disorder of badly drilled infantry. Higher still, pelicans occupied niches like pouches hung upon a wall; gulls disputed the projections; petrels flickered in and out of the shadows; while at the summit great albatrosses wheeled with so little motion that they appeared not birds but white ideas revolving around a black fact.

Rodondo is an enchanted frigate of the air. Its crew numbers thousands, yet no captain is obeyed; its decks rise vertically; its anchors descend to the center of the globe. Every species keeps a quarter and trespasses upon another. The penguin owns the lower battery, the pelican the waist, the gull the rigging, the albatross the royal masthead. But mutiny is perpetual.

We found a possible landing on the eastern side, where a sloping shelf emerged between surges. Possible, I say, because sailors use that word where landsmen employ suicidal. Watching the swell, we pulled in; at the command we leaped, dragging the boat’s painter after us. One man fell to his waist; another embraced a rock ornamented with crabs; a third lost his hat, which a gull immediately investigated.

Above the shelf rose a broken chimney of stone. A narrow cleft zigzagged upward, choked with loose fragments and generations of avian refuse. Here was our staircase.

“But ropes now,” said the mate, “and let us ascend.”

A line was passed around a projection. The lightest hand climbed first, driving iron pins where the rock permitted. I followed, though I had eaten unwisely at noon and soon regretted both the meal and the ambition. At every ledge birds confronted us. Some fled screaming; others struck with beak or wing; a few merely opened their mouths in silent astonishment, as if human ascent were a breach of natural law.

The rock was hot where dry, slippery where shaded, and everywhere perfumed beyond civility. Feathers entered the eyes; dust entered the throat; eggs rolled underfoot with consequences at which I shall only hint. Yet upward we went, pulled by rope, pushed by pride, and accompanied by a parliament of indignant wings.

At halfway I looked down.

Our boat had diminished to a chip; the men in her were buttons; the ship beyond, with sails furled, resembled a white insect pinned upon blue silk. The sea encircled us without seam or boundary. For an instant the tower seemed to lean, and the ocean to rise like a wall.

I clung more lovingly to the rock.

Above, a penguin waited.

## SKETCH SIXTH  
### THE AVIARY OF OCEAN

> Some wear the cowl, some bear the senator’s air;  
> Some thieves do chatter, some pale angels glide;  
> Yet all alike inherit wind and glare,  
> And build their Babel on the tower’s side.

The penguin is proof that Nature, though commonly majestic, occasionally drafts in a comic mood.

On land he stumps like a merchant late for an appointment; on the water he sculls with the dexterity of a smuggler; between the two elements he flops, scrambles, bows, and recovers with an offended dignity which seems to accuse the planet of improper construction. His little wings, excellent as fins, are ridiculous as wings; his black coat and white waistcoat suggest formal society, but his feet betray the tavern. Place a row of penguins on a ledge, and you behold a committee of aldermen debating a sewer.

The individual who barred my ascent was tall for his tribe and had lost feathers above one eye, which gave him the look of a scarred duelist. I shouted. He did not move. I waved my cap. He pecked it. I advanced; he struck my shin with a beak of astonishing emphasis.

“Sir,” said I, “your island is large.”

He answered by making himself larger, an effect achieved chiefly by indignation.

At last I attempted to step over him. He seized my trouser-leg and held fast while I, suspended between terror and laughter, clung to the rope. The mate below, instead of assisting, offered counsel in several varieties, none practicable. Only when a pelican, disturbed from above, descended like a collapsed umbrella between us did the penguin relinquish his claim.

Pelicans.

A pelican at rest resembles an elderly ecclesiastic burdened with private doubts. His eye is grave, his head bald, his bill preposterous; beneath it hangs that famous pouch, which irreverent sailors compare to a provision-bag and theologians to charity. Seen flying, however, he loses much of his comedy. The broad wings labor slowly; the long bill points with sacerdotal authority; and a file of pelicans crossing sunset might be monks migrating from a drowned abbey.

The gulls possessed no such sanctity. They were pickpockets, fishwives, prosecutors, and street-boys of the air. They stole from one another, screamed scandal, dropped shells upon our heads, and followed each slip of the climber with hopeful attention. A white gull with one black feather in his tail attended me for fifty feet, uttering comments whenever I paused. Had he been a boatswain, his vocabulary could scarcely have been more expressive.

Petrels darted about like fragments of burnt paper lifted from a fire. Boobies sat so tamely that we touched them, their mild faces incapable of comprehending malice. Frigate-birds hung overhead, forked tails steering, long wings making almost no visible stroke. These were the pirates: lean, black, and elegant, they waited until another bird caught a fish, then harried him through the air until he surrendered it. No cutlass, no skull flag, no scarlet sash—yet the true buccaneering principle was there.

And the albatross? He belongs to another order.

Far above the quarrels, awkwardness, hunger, and guano, the albatross sailed. His wings seemed not supported by the air but suspended from heaven. He turned without effort, rose without ascent, descended without fall. Looking up from our filthy ledge, with penguins biting at our boots and gulls rehearsing our funeral, I thought him an angel condemned to supervise a market.

Yet even angels must nest. On a broad shelf near the summit we found several albatrosses seated upon rude mounds. They permitted our approach with more composure than courage. One snapped his bill; another rose and revealed an egg of marble whiteness. The bird, standing beside it, became suddenly ungainly—too much wing, too little leg. Sublimity had landed and did not know where to put its feet.

Thus Rodondo teaches no simple hierarchy. The ridiculous bird is master underwater; the celestial bird becomes a scarecrow on stone. The pelican, august in flight, is absurd at dinner; the thieving frigate-bird is beautiful while committing robbery. As with tortoises—and men—the judgment alters with the element.

Below us the fish continued their festival. Looking over an edge, I could see them flashing through clear water fifty fathoms down. Large shadows moved among them, perhaps sharks, perhaps merely the magnification of fear. The shoals divided and reunited, forming wheels, arrows, banners, and bright rivers within the darker river of the sea. No drought afflicted that submerged country. While the islands above seemed abandoned after judgment, the waters beneath teemed as on the fifth morning of Genesis.

What perversity had concentrated barrenness on the land and abundance around its feet? The rock supported no tree, yet nourished a nation of wings. Its crevices held no spring, yet beneath them swam a treasury of living jewels. Death-white with droppings, black with volcanic birth, deafening with cries, Rodondo appeared at once cemetery and capital.

We climbed through this contradiction. At one place the path narrowed to a rib no broader than a handspike. On the left yawned a fissure; on the right, open air. The rope trembled. Birds rose around me in a storm, their wings striking cheek and shoulder. Blinded by feathers, I pressed myself to the wall and for several seconds could neither advance nor retreat.

Then, through the tumult, I heard a single albatross cry from above—a long, silver, melancholy note.

All at once the confusion seemed ordered. The gulls wheeled like sparks around an invisible axis; the sea breathed below; the tower stood rooted in abysses; and I, a small adhesive animal upon its side, felt the whole vast mechanism turn.

The sensation passed.

A gull dropped something upon my collar.

Philosophy, on Rodondo, is seldom permitted the final word.

## SKETCH SEVENTH  
### THE SUMMIT

> Who gains the height finds not the world made small,  
> But sees how wide the wilderness may be;  
> The isles are ashes, and the nations all  
> Mere names adrift upon a nameless sea.

The summit was not a point, as distance had promised, but an uneven platform some sixty paces in length, broken by pits and ridges. Here the birds had established their highest republic. Nests occupied every depression; eggs lay among stones; young albatrosses, clad in gray down, sat like dusty magistrates awaiting robes. Our arrival produced a widening circle of disturbance. Wings rose, bills clattered, voices multiplied; but after some minutes the citizens, perceiving that we neither laid eggs nor stole many, resumed their affairs.

We crawled to the western brink.

Now, reader, if you have never looked from an isolated sea-tower upon an ocean without visible continent, you may suppose that height makes immensity comprehensible. It does not. Height merely reveals how little there is to comprehend it with.

The horizon formed a perfect ring. Within it, the Pacific heaved in immense blue provinces, each changing under cloud and sun. Near the tower the water was emerald, then purple, then steel; farther out it shone with that merciless azure which promises nothing because it conceals everything. A few islands broke the circuit: Albemarle, long and dark; Narborough, crouched beside it; others farther south, reduced by distance to low smudges, like thumbprints left by the hand that had arranged the globe.

Our ship lay below, absurdly minute. Her yards were threads; her boats, seeds. Yet in that seed were barrels, charts, quarrels, debts, tobacco, knives, hymns, fever, and the captain’s authority. A whole human polity floated there, invisible in detail, complete in anxiety.

To the east—far beyond sight—stretched the coast of South America: Peru with its rainless cliffs, Ecuador under equatorial cloud, Chile drawn like a narrow blade between Andes and sea. To the west, nothing accessible to the eye, though the mind placed island after island across the waste: the low coral rings of the Paumotus; green Tahiti; the Marquesas, lifting fierce valleys from the deep; Pitcairn, haunted by mutiny; Easter Island, with its stone faces staring inland as though the sea had disappointed them; and beyond, innumerable groups scattered like crumbs from some celestial table.

What is the Pacific? On a chart, blue paper with names. From Rodondo, it became a boundless watery Kentucky—a wilderness without trees, roads, inns, or Daniel Boone; crossed not by footprints but wakes, which close behind the traveler. Its mountains are waves; its clearings, calms; its wild beasts, whales and storms. Men enter it with compass and chronometer, those small metallic boasts, yet a current unmarked in any book may carry them two hundred miles from intention.

The Spanish first approached these regions not as tourists of desolation but as men driven by gold, empire, salvation, and hunger—the four principal winds of history. Their ships were high-sided, clumsy, valiant; their pilots guarded latitudes as misers guard coin; their priests blessed departures; their soldiers dreamed of cities plated in treasure. From Panama and Peru they pushed into the western sea, where no familiar cape arose and no Christian bell answered the watch.

Imagine their first sight of the Encantadas. Weeks of water; scurvy in the forecastle; saints invoked until heaven itself seemed deaf; then at dawn black summits on the rim. Land! But landing gave no river, fruit, Indian village, or cross—only lava, lizards, tortoises, and a sun which drank the sweat before it cooled the skin. Worse, the currents seized their vessels. A ship entering between two islands at noon found herself at midnight near a third; fog concealed the stars; soundings failed; the wind died while the sea continued secretly to move.

No wonder the pilots called the group enchanted. Enchantment is often ignorance frightened into poetry.

One old account, repeated to me by a half-blind navigator in Callao, told of a Spanish vessel that sailed three days toward a mountain without nearing it. The crew believed the peak to be a demon’s palace. A friar displayed a relic; a gunner fired a cannon; a sailor jumped overboard in terror and was recovered by accident. On the fourth morning the mountain lay astern. The current had been setting sideways all along.

The explanation is plain. Yet does plain explanation entirely banish strangeness? Name the current, calculate its knots, draw its arrow on the chart—and still the mountain advances while the ship sails toward it. Science describes the enchantment’s habits; whether it dismisses the enchantment is another matter.

From our summit we saw such currents written upon the sea. Long bands crossed one another, smooth and rough, pale and dark. Here a tide-rip sparkled; there an oval of calm revolved within ripples. The ocean looked like a vast piece of watered silk slowly twisted by invisible hands.

Cloud-shadows traveled over Albemarle. Under one shadow the volcanic cones vanished; emerging, they seemed to have changed position. Narborough detached itself, joined again, altered color. For several minutes a low island appeared westward where our chart showed none. We debated it. The mate swore to land; I suspected cloud; another called it a whale-spout magnified by refraction. Gradually it thinned and became sky.

Thus, upon the highest seat of observation, uncertainty sat beside us.

We had ascended to enlarge knowledge and discovered how spacious ignorance was.

## SKETCH EIGHTH  
### DESCENT AND AFTER-VISION

> The climber down from airy kingdoms fares,  
> With feathered tumult beating round his head;  
> He bears no crown, but in his bosom bears  
> The doubtful speech of islands deemed dead.

Descent, in moral essays, is commonly easier than ascent. On Rodondo it was merely different in terror.

Going upward, one sees the next hold; going down, one sees the consequence of missing it. The sea waited below with an appearance of patience inherited from tortoises. Our rope, so stout when viewed from the landing, had become offensively slender. Loose stones, dislodged by the first man, bounded past the others and disappeared without report.

We descended backward, embracing the tower. The birds, having failed to prevent our invasion, now appeared determined to tax our departure. Gulls struck at our hats; penguins occupied the shelves; a booby, seized by panic at the wrong instant, flew directly into the mate’s chest, and bird and man remained for a moment mutually astonished before separating with cries.

Halfway down we paused where a projecting stone offered rest. The afternoon sun had lowered. Albemarle burned red along its ridges, while the ravines were already black. The surrounding water had lost its noonday glitter and taken on the color of tempered metal. Flocks returning to Rodondo crossed the light in ordered streams. Each species arrived at its own elevation, as citizens entering separate gates of a many-storied city.

I listened.

From below came the boom of caves; from above, the clatter of bills and endless cries; from far out, faint through distance, our ship’s bell sounded. That little man-made note, regular and bronze, passed through the wilderness like a remembered law.

For an instant I felt the tower itself to be a bell, sunk root-down in the ocean and rung by tides. Its birds were sparks shaken from it; its fish, colored vibrations below; the islands around, lesser bells long cracked and silent. Who was the ringer? Wind? Moon? Some submerged hand of the planet?

“Lower away!” shouted the mate.

Down we went.

At the last ledge, timing the swell, we jumped into the boat. One oar was nearly lost; two men shipped water; my left boot remained upon Rodondo, wedged in a crevice and perhaps subsequently adopted by a penguin. Barefoot and triumphant, I helped shove off.

As we pulled around the base, the rock assumed new shapes at every angle. From the south it was a fortress with ruined battlements; from the west, a mitred bishop; from the north, the black hull of a frigate standing on end. Distance simplified it. The birds vanished into a white haze around the summit. Soon Rodondo was again a tower, then a sail, then a pale tooth at the horizon.

The fish followed us for a time. Their bright backs flashed beneath the boat, fearless as before. Gradually the water deepened, the colors faded, and one by one our escort disappeared. Last remained the broad green fellow—or his cousin—who turned sideways below my hand, showing a golden eye. Then he sank into blue.

Night came while we were still between rock and ship. Albemarle’s peaks darkened against a copper sky. No fires showed there, no house-lamps, no signal. Venus appeared above one crater; another star rose over Rodondo. The first mate, steering, hummed a tune without words. Each oar dipped and shone.

When we reached the vessel, supper had been held for us. It was served, by one of those ironies which the sea arranges without smiling, in the polished shell of our largest tortoise. Potatoes filled the center; strips of salted meat lay around them; a ladle rested against the rim. The shell had been set upon two boxes, making a low communal table.

We crouched about it, hungry from the climb. Above us the new moon sharpened; below, the sea touched the planks. The carpenter remarked that my missing boot improved the flavor of the meal. The mate described his collision with the booby, enlarging the bird at every telling until it had the dimensions of a condor. Laughter went around.

Yet I looked at the shell.

In its yellow underside the lantern-light gathered warmly. Scratches made during the creature’s capture crossed older scars whose history no man knew. Perhaps the tortoise had carried that shield through a hundred seasons of drought. Perhaps, when young—if so ancient an object had ever consented to youth—it had moved beneath shrubs long since burned, drunk from pools now filled with ash, and watched ships unlike ours anchor where charts were blank. Now it bore potatoes.

Was this degradation? Utility? Transformation? The sailors saw a dish; I saw a relic; the cook saw something to be scrubbed. None possessed the whole truth. The tortoise itself, had it survived, might have regarded all three opinions with the same black, unanswering eye.

Later I took the first watch. The wind freshened from southeast, and we made sail. Rodondo dwindled astern. The Encantadas lay about us, unseen but present: Albemarle to leeward, Narborough beyond, the lesser cinder heaps spread under darkness. Currents worked beneath our keel. Though the compass held steady and the stars remained clear, I could not resist the feeling that the ship stood still while the islands moved—slowly revolving, exchanging places, drawing invisible circles around us.

A cry rose from the lookout.

Not “Sail ho,” but only the warning of broken water ahead.

We altered course. White foam appeared under the bow, passed along the side, and vanished. Nothing followed. Still, for an hour afterward, every dark patch seemed a reef; every low cloud, an island; every bird, a signal from some unseen shore.

By dawn we were clear of the group.

The eastern sky brightened behind us, and the islands emerged one after another in reverse order: first a peak, then a ridge, then several cones hovering above haze. The sun touched them. Their blackness became purple, their purple bronze, their bronze a momentary gold. Even those condemned rocks received transfiguration—but only at distance, and only for a minute. Soon the haze thickened. One summit remained, like the top of a buried pyramid; then that also sank.

The ocean resumed its vacant circle.

Years have passed since that voyage. Ships on which I served have foundered, been sold, or rotted at obscure wharves. Captains who cursed the Encantadas now keep quieter watches. The sentimental boy who helped eat the tortoises became, I heard, a magistrate. The carpenter was lost off Cape Horn. Of our charts, perhaps some lie worm-eaten in a warehouse; perhaps another hand has corrected their errors and drawn confident arrows for the currents.

But the islands remain in me less corrected than before.

I remember their drought, yet also the multitude of fish beneath Rodondo. I remember the tortoise’s black shell, yet also its brightness below. I remember the penguin’s absurdity in air and mastery in water; the albatross’s majesty aloft and awkwardness beside its egg. Everywhere contradiction: the dead land crowded with life, the treacherous sea radiant with beauty, the ancient creature turned into supper, the high lookout enlarging ignorance.

What, then, is the moral of the Encantadas?

Beware of morals gathered too neatly from broken countries.

One may call the islands cursed; but birds make cities upon their rocks. One may call the tortoise miserable; but perhaps his purpose is more entire than ours. One may call the ocean empty; but beneath its blue floor nations flash and wheel. One may call superstition folly; yet the accurately measured current can still make a mountain appear enchanted.

No; let the group keep its doubleness. Let it stand black above and bright below, like the tortoise. Let it be furnace and sanctuary, grave and aviary, fact and fable. The world is not obliged to choose one face for our convenience.

Sometimes, in inland towns, when rain freshens the roofs and trees shake green light over the road, I awake before dawn believing I hear shells knock together in a ship’s hold. Then comes the long boom of water in the caverns of Rodondo; then the silver cry of an albatross; then the scrape—pause—scrape of an ancient foot advancing over cinders.

I rise and go to the window.

There is no sea. A cart passes; a lamp burns; somewhere a baker opens his door. Yet beyond the roofs, beyond the hills, beyond the mapped coast and the prosperous traffic of men, those dark islands keep their station—or seem to keep it—while secret currents turn about them.

And on some barren slope, beneath an executioner sun, a tortoise perhaps presses against a stone. An easier passage lies near. He does not take it. Century upon century may rebuke him; drought may whiten his bones; ships may come, capture his companions, and depart. Still he leans forward, bearing his contradictory shield, obedient to that inward line which no chart records.

Poor fool! Grave pilgrim! Brother!

Advance.

### The Road Back to Chiswick

Robert Turner had not seen Chiswick for forty years; and since in those forty years he had accumulated a fortune, broken several competitors, outlived two partners, and acquired a reputation for never forgiving either a debt or an injury, it may be supposed that his return was not prompted by sentiment.

Indeed, sentiment was a commodity for which Mr. Turner professed no use. He had begun life with none of the advantages that induce a man to think tenderly of Providence, and had continued it in a manner which confirmed his earliest impressions. At sixty he was tall, spare, and erect, with iron-grey hair, a clean-shaven mouth, and a face whose harsh, regular lines seemed to have been cut in granite by an impatient sculptor. His clothes were excellent without being fashionable; his boots were too well made to creak; and his gold watch kept time with that severe accuracy which he demanded from clerks, trains, and debtors.

He had come to Chiswick upon business.

The Jameson mortgage, long overdue, had passed into his hands six months before. There had been appeals for delay, first from an attorney and afterward from Mrs. Jameson herself—appeals which Mr. Turner had answered with formal brevity. He was now to inspect the property before foreclosure.

Ordinarily he entrusted such matters to agents. In this instance, however, he had chosen to attend personally.

Neil Jameson was dead, and had been dead three years; but Robert Turner had hated him for nearly half a century.

It had been a schoolboy hatred in the beginning, born of ridicule and nourished by humiliation. Neil, handsome, confident, the son of a prosperous farmer, had possessed that careless authority which is often more cruel than deliberate malice. Robert had been poor, awkward, and painfully proud. His jacket had been patched, his lunch scanty, and his ambition ridiculous in the eyes of boys whose fathers owned broad fields and red cattle. Neil had mimicked his solemn speech, exposed his worn stockings, and once, before the whole school, read aloud a page from Robert’s private copybook in which the lonely boy had set down his intention of becoming a great merchant.

The laughter had never quite died in Robert Turner’s ears.

He had become a great merchant. Neil Jameson had inherited a good farm, mismanaged it, mortgaged it, and finally died with little besides debts to bequeath his family. That the mortgage should have come, by a series of ordinary business transactions, into Turner’s possession seemed to him one of those exquisite adjustments which Fate occasionally makes when she has leisure to concern herself with justice.

He meant to foreclose.

The road from the station disappointed him by being lower and smaller and narrower than the road of his recollection. The hills had shrunk; the houses stood nearer together; the old Presbyterian church, once a commanding edifice, was merely a grey and faded building with a modest spire. Even the village store, before which he had formerly paused in awe of inaccessible sweets and shining jackknives, looked cramped and dusty.

“Everything is insignificant,” thought Mr. Turner, with satisfaction. “It is memory that magnifies.”

Yet he did not order the driver to proceed directly to the Jameson farm. At the bridge he dismissed the hired conveyance, saying that he preferred to walk the remaining two miles. The driver glanced at the smooth leather shoes and the threatening clouds, but Mr. Turner was not a man to whom advice offered itself readily.

The April afternoon was pale and mild. Beyond the bridge the road curved between low stone walls, its brown ruts edged with young grass. White birches lifted delicate branches against the sky; farther off, the spruce woods lay dark and still, and through their openings appeared brief, misty gleams of the sea. On a sunny bank, violets made little pools of purple. Cherry trees beside an abandoned cottage were in bloom, and their loosened petals drifted over the broken doorstep like belated snow.

Mr. Turner began with his usual businesslike stride. Insensibly, however, it slackened. At one turning he stopped altogether.

Below the road stood an orchard.

The trees were older, contorted by wind and silvered with lichen, but the slope was unchanged. At its foot a brook ran between mossy stones, and over the brook lay a plank bridge. Robert remembered crossing that bridge barefoot, with his shoes tied around his neck and a fishing rod over his shoulder. He remembered lying among the long grasses while clouds sailed above him, and believing that the summer day could never end.

A singular error. It had ended forty years ago.

He went on, a little impatient with himself. Nevertheless, his stride did not recover its former precision. The road, conspiring with the air and the sea and the foolishly persistent birds, drew him into an unaccustomed saunter.

He passed the field where the schoolhouse had stood. Only a hollow remained, half filled with alder bushes. There Neil Jameson had laughed at him; there the other boys had joined in; there Robert, white with rage, had sworn that one day he would be richer than all of them.

He had kept the oath. Why, then, did the recollection bring no triumph? He saw, not Neil’s mocking face, but a thin, dark-haired boy gathering up scattered papers with hands that shook.

Then another figure came into the picture—a little girl in a blue checked apron, her brown curls flying, her eyes bright with indignation.

“Shame on you, Neil Jameson! Shame on every one of you!”

Lisbeth Miller.

Mr. Turner’s mouth altered slightly, though scarcely enough to be called a smile. In the offices of Turner and Company such an alteration was regarded with distrust; experienced clerks, like weather prophets watching a narrow streak of brightness under a black cloud, generally predicted a squall.

But no squall followed now.

The Jameson farm lay beyond the village, near the shore. Turner might have reached it by the main road, but at the next crossroads he chose, almost without conscious intention, a lane leading downward between spruces. The lane ended at a cove he had known in childhood—a small, sheltered indentation where red sandstone cliffs curved about a strip of grey shore.

The place seemed untouched by mortal reach. Creamy foam slid over the stones and withdrew with a whispering sound; gulls wheeled above the misty water; and a single fishing boat, far out, was no larger than a dark leaf. On the landward side, juniper and wild rose clung to the bank.

A boy sat upon a flat rock, mending a lobster pot.

He looked up as Turner approached. He was perhaps twelve, delicately made but wiry, with sun-browned hands, reddish-brown curls, and clear hazel eyes. His clothes were clean and carefully patched. Something in his appearance troubled Turner, although he could not have said why.

“Good afternoon, sir,” said the boy.

“Good afternoon. Does this path lead to the Jameson farm?”

“Yes, sir. You can go up by the pasture, though the hill is steep.”

“You know the place?”

“I live there.”

Turner looked at him more sharply.

“Then you are a Jameson?”

“My name is Paul Jameson, sir.”

The boy bent again over his work. His mouth, in profile, had a certain proud curve which Turner remembered unpleasantly. Neil’s mouth. Yet the eyes were not Neil’s; nor were the curls, nor the candid, friendly glance. Nature, thought Turner, had perpetrated some flagrant forgery and then spoiled it by mixing the signatures.

“You are Neil Jameson’s son?”

“Yes, sir. Did you know Father?”

“I knew him when we were boys.”

Paul’s face brightened.

“Then you must have lived here long ago. Father used to say Chiswick was the best place in the world, though he never had much luck farming in it.”

Mr. Turner, who considered bad management a more accurate phrase than bad luck, made no reply.

The boy tested a strip of netting and frowned over a broken knot. He had competent fingers, but the work was stiff, and presently Turner said:

“That is a large task for you.”

“I have to do what I can. Mr. Bell pays me for mending them. Not very much, of course, because I’m not big enough to work fast.”

“You are anxious to be big?”

“Oh, yes, sir—bigger and older. If I were sixteen I could hire out properly, and if I were twenty I could take the farm and make it pay.”

His earnestness was so complete that it admitted no smile.

“Can the farm be made to pay?”

“I know it can. The south field needs draining, and we ought to keep more cows instead of trying grain. Father meant to change things, but he was ill the last two years. Since he died, Mother and Ellen and I have managed as well as we could.”

“Ellen is your sister?”

“She is nine. She feeds the hens and gathers eggs, but Mother says she is too little for heavy work.” Paul sighed. “We are all too little, except Mother, and she works too hard.”

There was no complaint in the statement; only the grave recognition of an unfortunate fact. Turner seated himself on another rock, an action which would have astonished his board of directors, for the stone was damp and his overcoat new.

“You spoke of taking the farm,” he said. “Is it not mortgaged?”

The boy’s hands became still.

“Yes, sir.”

“And overdue?”

“I suppose so. Mother doesn’t tell us everything, but I know. A man in the city owns the mortgage now. His name is Turner.”

“What have you heard of him?”

Paul glanced up, uncertain whether such a question might be improper.

“Only that he’s very rich.”

“That is not usually considered confidential.”

“And that he is going to take the farm.”

“Foreclose upon it,” corrected Turner.

“Yes, sir. Mother got a letter yesterday. She cried after she read it, though she said she had smoke in her eyes. There wasn’t any smoke.”

The waves crept over the stones and fell back. Turner watched a thread of foam cling for an instant to a black rock, then vanish.

“What will you do if the farm is taken?”

“I don’t know. Mother says we’ll go somewhere and manage. She always says we’ll manage. But”—his voice faltered and recovered—“it would be hard for her to leave. She has lived there since she married Father. And I’d never get it back if it were sold. If I were only bigger and older, I could earn the money. I could pay Mr. Turner every cent.”

“That might require a considerable number of lobster pots.”

“I know.” Paul flushed. “But I would do anything honest. I don’t see why Mr. Turner can’t wait. He has so much, and we have only the farm.”

The plain arithmetic of childhood has no standing in a court of finance; nevertheless, it sometimes finds jurisdiction elsewhere.

“Perhaps Mr. Turner needs the money,” said Turner dryly.

Paul considered this.

“Perhaps he does. Mother said we mustn’t judge him, because we don’t know. She said maybe he had troubles of his own.”

This unexpected charity annoyed Turner.

“Your mother appears disposed to think well of strangers.”

“She thinks well of everybody. Father used to say she could find a good word for the devil if he came to the kitchen door on a wet night.”

There was the curve of Neil’s mouth again, but the eyes softened it; and suddenly, from some association too swift for warning, Turner saw a little girl standing in summer grass, holding out half an apple to him.

“What is your mother’s name?” he asked.

“Lisbeth. Lisbeth Miller before she married Father.”

Lisbeth Miller!

The cove, the boy, the grey shore—all receded for a moment under the rush of that name.

Little Lisbeth, pretty and merry and roguish; little Lisbeth, who had never laughed when the others laughed, except from happiness; little Lisbeth, who could climb the old pine almost as high as a boy, bait her own hook, whistle through an alder leaf, and invent splendid kingdoms among the shore rocks. She had been his playmate during the few bright years before poverty drove him from school to work. When his mother died, Lisbeth had brought him a bunch of wild roses and cried because he did not. When Neil scattered his copybook, she had gathered the pages, smoothed them, and declared that Robert would indeed be a great merchant, and that she would not be a bit surprised if he owned ships and houses and wore a gold watch.

He remembered the last afternoon before he left Chiswick. They had sat in the orchard beside the brook. He was fifteen, fierce with grief and ambition; she was fourteen, slender and brown, with a blue ribbon in her hair.

“You’ll forget me,” he had said, desiring passionately to be contradicted.

“I won’t.”

“You’ll marry Neil Jameson.”

“I won’t! I don’t even like Neil—not much.”

“Then wait till I come back.”

She had laughed, though there were tears in her eyes.

“Forty years, perhaps?”

“I’ll come before that.”

He had not come.

At first he wrote. Then work swallowed the days, ambition the years. Her second letter went unanswered for six months; her third remained in a drawer until shame made answering difficult. Later he heard that she had married Neil Jameson. The news hardened something already hardening within him, and he told himself that she had been false—as though a girl’s promise beneath an apple tree were a bond he might neglect but she must honour forever.

Yet what else could she have done? Wait through silence, youth, and middle age for a boy who worshipped success more faithfully than he had ever worshipped her?

Turner looked at Paul. The hazel eyes were Lisbeth’s. He wondered that he had not known them at once.

“Does your mother ever speak of her childhood?” he asked.

“Often. She tells us about the cove and the orchard, and a boy she used to play with—a boy named Robert.”

“Indeed?”

“He was poor and very clever. The other boys teased him, but Mother said he would make something of himself. She wonders what became of him.”

A queer ache, unwelcome and almost sweet, stirred under Robert Turner’s ribs.

“Did she never hear?”

“I don’t think so. She said once that perhaps he had forgotten Chiswick. But she didn’t blame him. Mother never blames people long.”

No, Lisbeth had never blamed people long. Her anger flashed gallantly and vanished; her sympathy remained. How many small kindnesses had she lavished upon the lonely, arrogant boy—kindnesses accepted then as naturally as sunshine, remembered now with an old man’s humiliating gratitude.

And he had come to turn her out of her home.

The naked fact stood before him, stripped of legal phrases and old resentments. Neil was beyond his revenge. No foreclosure could humble him, no notice of sale disturb his sleep. The blow would fall upon Lisbeth, upon this earnest boy with his patched sleeves, upon a little girl who fed hens and was considered too small for heavy work.

For forty years Turner had cherished an injury done him by a dead schoolboy; for forty years he had forgotten the affection given him by a living girl.

He rose abruptly.

Paul looked alarmed, as though fearing he had said too much.

“Will you tell your mother,” said Turner, “that the owner of the mortgage will not foreclose?”

The boy stared.

“Sir?”

“The Jamesons may remain on the farm. The debt can wait until you are bigger and older—considerably bigger, if necessary.”

“But how do you know?”

“Because I am Robert Turner.”

Paul’s face changed first to astonishment, then apprehension. He scrambled to his feet.

“Oh! I’m sorry, sir. I didn’t mean to say anything disrespectful. I didn’t know it was you.”

“You merely stated the case with an accuracy some lawyers might envy.”

“Then—you truly won’t take the farm?”

“I truly won’t.”

Paul searched the granite face, perhaps for mockery; finding none, he drew a long, trembling breath.

“Mother will be so glad. I don’t know how to thank you.”

“You need not. Pay me when you can. Drain the south field first.”

“I will, sir! I know I can make it pay. And you’ll come up to the house, won’t you? Mother will want to thank you herself. When she hears you are the Robert she knew—”

“No.”

The word was sharper than Turner intended. He softened it with difficulty.

“No, sonny. I have not time today.”

“But the house is only over the hill.”

Turner looked toward the path. Beyond the bank he could see the upper branches of the Jameson orchard, lightly veiled in blossom. Somewhere among them was Lisbeth—not the little girl of his memory, but a woman near sixty, worn by work and sorrow, Neil Jameson’s widow.

He had no wish to measure the years upon her face, nor to have her measure them upon his. Let her remain as she had been: brown curls, roguish eyes, blue ribbon, bare feet on the plank bridge. Time had taken enough liberties with Chiswick; it should not be permitted this one.

“Tell her,” he said slowly, “that an old friend remembered a debt.”

“What debt?”

“She will understand. Or perhaps she will not; it does not matter.”

Paul held out his hand. Turner hesitated only an instant before taking it. The boy’s grasp was firm and warm.

“I’ll never forget you, Mr. Turner.”

“Do not make rash promises about forty years,” said Turner; but his voice was not severe.

He watched Paul run up the path, stumbling once in his haste, then recovering and disappearing beyond the junipers. Presently a clear halloo floated down from the hill, youthful and exultant.

Robert Turner remained at the cove.

The mist was lifting. Across the water a pale width of gold had opened beneath the clouds, and the wet stones shone richly—red, brown, and purple. A gull dropped to the surface and rose again, scattering silver. The world had not grown younger; no world does. Yet something within him, long cramped and silent, seemed to stretch toward the light.

He had thought himself content because he had been successful. He perceived now, with some surprise, that contentment was a gentler thing. It did not resemble victory. It resembled a little girl defending a ragged boy; an apple divided exactly in half; wild roses laid upon a grave; faith offered before there was any evidence that it had been deserved.

He turned back toward the village, leaving the Jameson farm uninspected.

His step was lighter than it had been upon the outward road. When he came to the old orchard, he paused at the stone wall. The brook still ran below, making the same confidential murmur among the mossy stones, and the cherry petals drifted through the quiet air.

Almost without knowing it, Robert Turner began to hum a song which had been popular in his boyhood. His voice was rusty and the tune uncertain, but he persisted through several lines. Then he smiled.

It was not the narrow, ominous alteration which disturbed the offices of Turner and Company. It was an awkward, unguarded smile, astonishingly like that of the poor boy who had once crossed a plank bridge barefoot and dreamed of owning the world.

He had owned much of it, in the end, and found the possession barren.

But little Lisbeth’s kindness, preserved beyond pride, resentment, distance, and time, had remained outside all markets and beyond all mortal reach. Now, at last, he had repaid a fraction of it; and as he walked beneath the flowering trees, Robert Turner knew that the mercy he had granted had not been given to Lisbeth and her children alone.

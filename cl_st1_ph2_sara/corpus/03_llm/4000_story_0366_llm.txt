The last grouse had fallen into the heather with a soft, almost apologetic thud, and Sir Hugh Tredennis, lowering his gun, observed that the light was going.

This was not strictly true. A long sulphurous gleam still lay above the moors, and the western windows of Bramley Chase burned as though the old house contained a furnace instead of twenty-seven bedrooms, six indifferent portraits by Lely, and a rent-roll which diminished with every parliamentary session. But Sir Hugh’s statements, like his hospitality, were not intended to be examined. The guns turned homeward.

Weigall followed them in the detached temper of a man who had crossed half Europe in search of novelty and found everywhere the same dinner table differently dressed. He had endured three days of shooting, two county ladies, an American widow with literary intentions, and a Cabinet minister who spoke of empire as if he had invented geography. Under ordinary circumstances he would have regarded Bramley as tolerable. But Wyatt Gifford had disappeared.

He had vanished two nights before, after dinner, without hat, coat, or message.

At first the household had treated the matter lightly. Gifford was notorious for sudden expeditions and elaborate jokes; he had once abandoned a yacht at Malta because a German metaphysician had assured him that Sicily was indispensable to the understanding of free will. Sir Hugh supposed him gone to Skipton. Lady Tredennis suggested a clandestine meeting, with the pleased severity of a woman who believed every bachelor governed by a woman. The American widow hinted at suicide and looked disappointed when no note was found.

By the following afternoon the servants had searched the outbuildings, the stationmaster had been questioned, and two keepers had gone over the nearer woods. No one had seen him. His boots and travelling coat remained in his room. On his dressing table lay the watch which he carried habitually, beside a little heap of sovereigns and a letter begun to Weigall himself.

“My dear Anthony,” it said, “your objection is less scientific than theological. If you insist that the soul must vacate—”

There the writing ended.

Weigall had shown the fragment to no one. It seemed to him private, absurd, and somehow ominous.

That evening dinner was more brilliant than usual, because anxiety in a country house must either extinguish conversation or refine it into cruelty. The minister related an anecdote concerning the Prince of Wales. Lady Tredennis discussed a forthcoming marriage. Sir Hugh drank too much claret and repeatedly assured Weigall that Gifford would appear before breakfast with a Turkish dervish or an escaped actress.

Weigall answered properly. His face, schooled by embassies, clubs, and women, betrayed nothing; yet beneath his composure apprehension moved with the slow persistence of an underground stream.

Gifford was his oldest friend. They had been boys together, had quarrelled at Oxford over a horse, had met by accident in Cairo and resumed their intimacy without explanation. Weigall had admired many women, desired a few, and loved none sufficiently to disturb his habits. For Gifford he possessed that rarer affection which men conceal even from themselves until danger strips life of its ornamental sentiments.

After dinner he refused cards and went out alone.

The moon had not risen. The stars were innumerable and cold, but their light seemed arrested above the woods, whose interlacing branches formed caverns of palpable blackness. A frost had stiffened the paths. Now and then some small creature fled through the dead fern, and the sound, magnified by stillness, followed him like a furtive step.

He intended only to walk as far as the lower bridge. Instead, upon reaching the path which descended to the Wharfe, he turned toward the Strid.

The name had occupied much of the luncheon conversation on his first day at Bramley. The river, broad and amiable above Bolton, contracted there into a rocky throat so narrow that tradition invited the foolhardy to leap it. Under the dark cleft the whole weight of the Wharfe was driven through invisible channels, striking shelves, revolving in hollows, pouring into chambers which no plummet had measured. Men who fell there were seldom recovered. One keeper maintained that beneath the ledges lay a natural vault, crowded with the bodies of centuries, their bones rocked patiently by the current.

The tale had amused Gifford.

“A respectable immortality,” he had said. “To be preserved under a Yorkshire estate is almost as good as being buried in Westminster Abbey.”

“You assume preservation,” Weigall replied. “Water has appetites.”

“So has immortality.”

The words came back now with disagreeable distinctness.

The path descended sharply. Here the river could be heard before it was seen: not the continuous murmur of ordinary water, but a succession of voices—scolding, protesting, subsiding into whispers, then rising with furious and untiring energy. Between the trunks Weigall caught momentary patches of white. The foam appeared and vanished like torn linen waved by an imprisoned hand.

He stopped.

There was nothing rational in his uneasiness. Gifford had known the danger of the place, and possessed neither the melodramatic disposition necessary for suicide nor the clumsiness favourable to accident. Yet Weigall remembered that the unfinished letter concerned their discussion of death.

It had begun in the library after the others had gone to bed. Gifford, stretched before the fire, had asked whether death must necessarily be instantaneous merely because the heart ceased.

“The machinery stops,” Weigall had said. “That is death.”

“The machinery may stop while the engineer remains.”

“Doing what?”

“Waiting, perhaps. Or trying the levers.”

Weigall had laughed. “You would make a haunted factory of every corpse.”

“Why not? We know nothing of the vital principle except its effects. Suppose the soul is not expelled, but detained. Suppose a violent death leaves it entangled in damaged machinery.”

“Then cremation is an act of mercy.”

“Or murder,” Gifford had answered. “Consider a body under water. The lungs are useless, the heart is still, the brain extinguished; but some last province of the organism may remain obedient. A finger may preserve the memory of command after the sovereign power has fallen.”

“You confuse muscular irritability with consciousness.”

“We give long names to our ignorance in order that it may pass for knowledge.”

It had been the sort of conversation at which Gifford excelled—witty, perverse, polished until folly wore the lineaments of philosophy. Yet his eyes, Weigall now remembered, had not been amused.

The path ended upon a shelf of stone above the river. Here the Wharfe, compressed between black walls, lost all resemblance to pastoral water. It boiled, recoiled, twisted upon itself, and hurled its foam against the rocks as if in rage at an immutable restraint. A few yards lower down it spread suddenly into a quiet pool, smooth on the surface, though the current beneath moved with great force toward another narrow channel.

Weigall advanced to the edge.

Something pale showed amid the foam.

He bent forward. It vanished, returned, vanished again. A branch, he thought; the stripped root of a tree, caught under the opposite ledge. Then five white lines opened distinctly against the black water.

A hand.

For one paralysed second he watched it. The fingers curved, flattened, and clutched at the rock. They did not drift loosely with the current. They struggled.

“Hold on!” he shouted.

The absurdity of the words did not reach him. A man had fallen in—perhaps that instant, perhaps while Weigall descended the path—and had been swept beneath the shelf. The hand appeared again. Water streamed from the wrist. A cuff flashed white, and above it gleamed two dark stones joined by gold.

Gifford’s cuff links.

Weigall flung himself upon the rock. Lying flat, he thrust his arm into the torrent and caught the hand.

It closed upon his.

The grip was weak but unmistakable. A violent joy drove the blood to his head.

“Wyatt! I have you!”

The river wrenched at them. Weigall braced one knee against a spur of stone and pulled. Nothing yielded. Gifford’s arm was wedged beneath the ledge, or his clothing had caught on one of the submerged projections. The hand slackened, then tightened convulsively around Weigall’s fingers.

He drew back, dragged again, twisted the wrist. The arm rose by an inch. Cloth tore somewhere below. A shoulder struck the underside of the rock with a dull sound.

Weigall released the hand, scrambled down to a lower foothold, and plunged both arms into the water. The cold bit like metal. He found the sleeve, followed it to the shoulder, and tugged with all the strength of back and thigh. The current fought him, not blindly but with a horrible, varying intelligence—now pressing the body deeper, now allowing it to rise, then snatching it sideways as if determined to retain its prey.

The head was still below. Weigall could see only the hand turning in the foam.

Again the fingers opened and shut.

That gesture destroyed his remaining caution. He sprang waist-deep into the channel.

The shock emptied his lungs. His boots slipped upon weed; the river seized his legs and nearly swept him under. He caught the ledge, recovered himself, and thrust one foot into a fissure. Then, reaching down, he grasped a mass of cloth and hair.

“Now, Wyatt!”

He pulled.

The body came free with appalling suddenness. Weigall fell backward, dragging it upon him. For an instant they were both submerged; black water filled his mouth, and the thunder of the Strid became a universe without air or light. He kicked upward, found stone, and forced his head above the foam.

One arm around Gifford’s chest, he let the current carry them toward the lower pool.

There the water recovered its equanimity. It bore them in a slow circle beneath the stars, as indifferent as a lake. Weigall swam heavily toward a shelving bank, pushed the body ahead, and felt mud under his knees. He crawled out, dragged Gifford after him, and collapsed upon the frozen grass.

For several moments he could hear only his own breath.

Gifford lay on his side, one arm bent beneath him. His evening clothes were sodden and torn. One shoe had gone. The exposed hand was very white, but Weigall saw with a renewal of hope that the fingers were crooked as if they had only just released their hold.

He had rescued him. The conviction, passionate and unreasonable, filled him. Men had survived longer immersion; medical records contained prodigies of suspended animation. The icy water itself might have preserved the vital spark. What mattered was immediate action—warmth, friction, artificial respiration. Sir Hugh’s house stood less than a mile away. There were servants, blankets, brandy, a telephone.

“We shall make a scandal of this,” he said, and the familiar tone broke in his throat. “You will have to explain yourself to Lady Tredennis.”

He rolled the body onto its back.

Its head fell loosely aside.

Weigall placed both hands upon the chest and pressed, released, pressed again. Water came from the mouth—or he thought it did, for the night confused everything, and the wet hair had fallen forward over the features. He tore open the waistcoat. The shirt beneath was cold and clinging. No movement answered his efforts.

Still he worked.

He remembered Gifford at nineteen, laughing from the far side of a hedge; Gifford on a balcony in Rome, arguing that ruins were more alive than modern buildings; Gifford leaning across a gaming table at dawn; Gifford beside the library fire, saying that a finger might retain the memory of command.

Weigall pressed harder.

“Breathe, damn you.”

The words were not urbane. Nothing civilized remained in him but his refusal to despair. He lifted the body’s shoulders, forced the arms upward, brought them down against the ribs. Again. Again.

Then he noticed the smell.

At first he supposed it came from the river—that ancient exhalation of mud, roots, drowned leaves, and hidden caverns. But this odour was sweeter, thicker, intimate. It issued from the clothes beneath his hands.

He recoiled, then bent forward in disbelief.

The cuff had slipped back from Gifford’s wrist. Above the cuff link the flesh was mottled and loose. A narrow strip moved under Weigall’s thumb.

Not life. Water.

The hand had not gripped him. The current, tightening dead fingers around his own, had performed the gesture—or else—

He could not complete the thought.

A wind moved along the pool and lifted the wet hair from the head.

Weigall stared.

The river had spared the hair, the ears, and the pale curve of the jaw. Between them it had eaten everything else away.

There was no face.

### The Affair of the Serene Prince

If you have ever spent a spring week at Monte Carlo with nothing to do but watch the blue Mediterranean, lose money at the tables, and prevent your friends from marrying the wrong people, you will understand that life can become fairly strenuous.

I was staying on the *Circe*, old Vanderley’s yacht, which lay in the harbour looking like a white palace that had taken to drink. Blue sea, gleaming Casino, cloudless sky, and all the rest of the picture-postcard business. The cook was sound, the cellar better, and my valet, Voules, could press a pair of trousers so that you could have shaved with the crease.

In short, there seemed no reason why Fate should not leave me alone.

It didn’t.

The first indication that the machinery was warming up came one morning when George Lattaker entered my cabin while I was finishing breakfast. He had the air of a man whose nerves were sticking a yard out of him and waving in the breeze.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’m going to propose to Stella.”

I lowered the coffee-cup.

“Again?”

“For the first time.”

“You’ve been proposing to her with your eyes for three weeks.”

“That is different.”

“Not to the casual observer.”

George prowled about the cabin. He was a large, pleasant, loose-jointed specimen, with hair that refused to lie down and a face which generally suggested that someone had just asked him a question about the Schleswig-Holstein affair. Ordinarily he was one of the cheeriest old scouts in existence. Love had reduced him to a sort of haunted giraffe.

“Do you think she’ll accept me?” he asked.

“I shouldn’t wonder. Women do extraordinary things.”

“Be serious.”

“I am serious. Look at Cleopatra.”

“What about Cleopatra?”

“I don’t know. But she always seems to come in useful when one is discussing women doing extraordinary things.”

He stopped by the porthole.

“If Stella refuses me, I shall go straight ashore and blow my brains out.”

“You haven’t got a pistol.”

“I shall buy one.”

“The shops may be shut.”

“Then I shall drown myself.”

I reflected. Suicide is always an awkward topic before lunch. There seems nothing suitable to say. Besides, George was my guest, and a man drowning himself from one’s yacht creates talk among the crews of the neighbouring vessels.

“Why not wait till your uncle arrives?” I said. “You tell me he is bringing the glad news about the inheritance.”

“He comes this afternoon.”

“Exactly. Propose after you have heard that you are rolling in doubloons. There is a lot of sound common sense in money.”

“It wouldn’t make any difference to Stella.”

“Possibly not. But it would make a difference to you. A rejected man with fifty thousand a year can go to Paris. A rejected man without it has to sit in his bedroom.”

George struck the table.

“I’m going to do it now.”

“Just as you like, old top. Don’t say I didn’t give the question concentrated thought.”

He went out.

Voules came in to remove the breakfast things. He was a tall, grave fellow with the face of a bishop who has discovered a typographical error in the Athanasian Creed. Nothing shook Voules. I had once seen a deck-chair collapse under him during a reception, and he had risen from the wreckage with the manner of one who considered the occurrence regrettable but not unprecedented.

“Mr. Lattaker appears disturbed, sir,” he said.

“He is about to propose marriage.”

“Yes, sir.”

“That is why he looks as if he had swallowed a live ferret.”

“Yes, sir.”

Voules lifted the coffee-pot.

“Love,” I said, because the subject seemed to call for a broad, philosophical treatment, “is a dashed rum thing, Voules.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You have felt its sting yourself, no doubt?”

For one instant his left eyebrow moved. With Voules, this was equivalent to falling on the floor and beating the carpet.

“I am engaged to Miss Pilbeam, sir.”

“Stella’s maid?”

“Yes, sir.”

“By Jove. I had no notion.”

“No, sir.”

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you, sir.”

He departed with the tray, and I went on deck.

Stella Vanderley was standing near the rail in a white dress. She was one of those cool, self-possessed girls who make a man feel that he has entered a room with his waistcoat unbuttoned. She had grey eyes, dark hair, and a way of looking at George which made it obvious, even to an intellect like mine, that his chances were not negligible.

Old Vanderley, her father, was below with a cigar and the financial pages. He owned mines, railways, steamship lines, and several newspapers which told the public what to think about mines, railways, and steamship lines. He had invited us to cruise with him because rich men become restless unless surrounded by young fellows who can be beaten at piquet.

I was just considering whether tact demanded that I retire when George appeared.

He had changed his collar.

This showed the depth of the crisis.

“Stella,” he said.

“Good morning, George.”

“I want to speak to you.”

“You are speaking to me.”

“Alone.”

She glanced at me.

“Reggie,” she said, “go away.”

There are moments when one sees with perfect clearness how little one is valued by one’s associates. I went aft and leaned over the rail, preserving a dignified profile. I could not hear everything, but the sea carries sound in a peculiar fashion, and every now and then a sentence floated to me.

“I have loved you since—”

Then something about his uncle.

Then Stella said, “Oh, George!”

Then George said, “Do you mean it?”

Then there was a silence.

I deduced success. I have always had a gift for piecing evidence together.

Presently George came round the corner of the deck. He was walking three inches above the boards.

“She has accepted me,” he whispered.

“Splendid.”

“I am the happiest man alive.”

“Capital.”

“Reggie, I feel I want to kiss the whole world.”

“Don’t start with me.”

At that moment Pilbeam emerged from the companionway.

She was a pretty, golden-haired girl with blue eyes and a neat figure. I had always admired Voules’s judgment, and I admired it more than ever now. She was carrying a wrap which Stella had left below.

George gazed at her.

I saw something flicker in his face. It was the same expression he wore at the Casino immediately before putting the family jewels on red.

“Good morning, Mr. Lattaker,” said Pilbeam.

“Good morning,” said George thickly.

Then he seized her and kissed her.

There are situations for which etiquette books provide no guidance. When a man who has just become engaged to one girl embraces another within twelve feet of his fiancée, the onlooker is placed in a delicate position. He may cough, but coughing seems inadequate. He may look at the view, but the view loses its grip.

I was still searching for the right move when Stella came round the corner.

She stopped.

George released Pilbeam.

Pilbeam uttered a small cry and dropped the wrap.

Stella looked at George. She looked at Pilbeam. Then she looked at me, as if feeling that the whole thing was probably my fault.

“So this,” she said, “is how you celebrate an engagement.”

George opened his mouth.

“Stella—”

“The engagement is over.”

“My dear girl—”

“Kindly do not speak to me.”

“But I can explain.”

“Can you?”

George considered.

Apparently he could not.

Pilbeam had turned scarlet.

“I’m sure, miss, I never—”

“I do not blame you, Pilbeam.”

I thought this severe on George, though technically defensible.

Stella walked away.

George stood looking after her. His joyous glow had vanished. Fate had swatted him all over the ring.

“Why did you do that?” I asked.

He passed a hand over his forehead.

“I told you. I felt I wanted to kiss the whole world.”

“You might have begun with something less local. Italy, for instance.”

“I didn’t know what I was doing.”

“Stella did.”

He groaned.

Pilbeam burst into tears and fled below.

From the companionway there emerged Voules.

His face was as calm as ever, but the temperature of the immediate neighbourhood appeared to drop several degrees.

“You kissed Miss Pilbeam, sir,” he said.

George stared at him.

“I’m terribly sorry, Voules. It was an impulse.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I didn’t mean anything by it.”

“No, sir.”

“You understand?”

“Yes, sir.”

He moved past us and picked up the wrap.

There was something about the back of his neck as he went below that caused me uneasiness. It was not an agitated neck. Voules would never have allowed himself that. But it was a neck which had filed the facts away for future reference.

George clutched my arm.

“I must go after Stella.”

“She told you not to speak to her.”

“I shall write.”

“She may tell you not to spell at her.”

He stared wildly over the harbour.

“There is only one thing left for me.”

“If you mean drowning, bear in mind that we lunch in twenty minutes.”

“I’m going ashore.”

“What for?”

“To forget.”

Monte Carlo offers an admirable selection of methods for forgetting, all expensive and most of them headache-producing. Before I could stop him, George had gone.

I did not see him again that day.

He returned at six the next morning.

I was awakened by a noise in my cabin. Sitting up, I saw a ruin in evening dress leaning against the door. It had a torn collar, one shoe, a bruise over the left eye, and seaweed on its sleeve.

“George!”

“Is that you, Reggie?”

“It had better be. This is my cabin.”

He advanced unsteadily and sat on the bed.

“Have you a head?”

“Certainly.”

“Mine has gone wrong.”

“Where have you been?”

“I don’t know.”

“What have you done?”

“I don’t know.”

“Why are you wearing seaweed?”

“I don’t know.”

The conversation threatened to become monotonous.

Voules appeared behind him, fully dressed, smooth, and dignified. I have never discovered when valets sleep. Mine seemed always to be waiting just outside the door for civilization to collapse.

“Mr. Lattaker’s bath is prepared, sir,” he said.

“Excellent. Put him in it.”

“Yes, sir.”

George clung to the bedpost.

“No water. Water is an enemy.”

“Get into your bath, old top.”

“I have seen terrible things.”

“You’ll see worse in the mirror.”

Voules detached him and led him away.

At breakfast, two hours later, George looked less like a shipwreck but more like a man awaiting execution. He drank four cups of coffee without result.

Old Vanderley entered carrying a newspaper.

“Extraordinary affair last night,” he said.

George’s cup rattled.

“What affair?”

“Prince Otto of Saxburg assaulted on the quay. Found unconscious at three in the morning.”

I looked at George.

George looked at me.

Old Vanderley sat down.

“Masked ruffian attacked him. Robbery, apparently. The fellow got away. Prince is badly knocked about, though the doctors say he’ll recover.”

“That,” said George, “doesn’t matter.”

“It does to him,” I said.

Old Vanderley rustled the paper.

“There appears to have been a second man involved. Witnesses heard a fight, but nobody saw it clearly.”

George’s face had become the colour of hotel fish.

“Reggie,” he said faintly, “I should like a word with you.”

“You haven’t eaten your egg.”

“I shall never eat again.”

We went to my cabin.

George shut the door.

“I did it.”

“Did what?”

“Slugged the prince.”

“Nonsense.”

“I must have.”

“Why must you?”

“I remember fighting someone.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know.”

“What about?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere near the quay. I remember a chap with a cloak. Or perhaps it was a curtain. Then there were stars. Then I woke up beside a fishing-boat.”

“This is pretty thin evidence.”

“My hat is missing.”

“You can’t assault royalty with a hat.”

“It may have been found at the scene.”

“Did it bear your name?”

“My name is inside it.”

I saw the difficulty. George had had the hat made in London by a conscientious firm which put the customer’s name in gold letters beneath the crown, apparently to assist the police.

“Besides,” he said, “I had motive.”

“What motive?”

“I was miserable.”

“That is not a motive for slugging a Serene Highness.”

“I was drunk.”

“That helps a little.”

There was a knock.

Voules entered.

“A gentleman to see Mr. Lattaker, sir.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Denman Sturgis, sir.”

George caught my eye.

The name had weight. Denman Sturgis was a private investigator of international reputation. His picture appeared in the newspapers whenever some countess mislaid her pearls or a South American republic misplaced its president.

“Where is he?” I asked.

“In the saloon, sir.”

“What does he look like?”

“Like a gentleman who has found something, sir.”

George made a choking noise.

We went to the saloon.

Sturgis was a small, dry man in a grey suit. He had narrow eyes and a pointed nose, giving him the appearance of a thoughtful rat. On the table before him lay George’s hat.

“Mr. Lattaker?” he said.

George swallowed.

“I am Lattaker.”

Sturgis touched the hat.

“This belongs to you?”

George looked at me.

At such moments one’s friends expect assistance. I supplied it.

“Never seen it before,” I said.

Sturgis looked inside.

“George Lattaker,” he read.

“A coincidence.”

“The maker confirms it was purchased by Mr. George Lattaker.”

“Another coincidence.”

George broke.

“It is mine.”

Sturgis nodded.

“Where were you between midnight and three o’clock this morning?”

“In bed,” I said.

Sturgis looked at George’s eye, his torn collar, and the cut on his chin.

“An uneasy bed.”

George swayed.

At that instant Voules entered with a silver salver.

“A letter for Mr. Lattaker, sir. Delivered by hand.”

The address was in a bold, sprawling fist. George opened it. He read. His jaw fell.

“What is it?” I asked.

He handed me the sheet.

> My dear George,  
> Prepare yourself for a shock. Documents recently discovered in Australia establish that you have a twin brother, separated from you in infancy. His name is Alfred. He reaches Monte Carlo today. I shall explain all when I arrive.  
> Your affectionate uncle,  
> Frederick Lattaker.

For perhaps three seconds no one spoke.

Then my brain flared like a beacon.

“Mr. Sturgis,” I said, “you have made a natural mistake.”

“Oh?”

“This gentleman is not George Lattaker.”

George looked at me as though I had bitten him.

“He said he was.”

“Family habit. They are very close.”

“Who is he, then?”

“Alfred.”

“Alfred?”

“George’s twin brother. Newly discovered. From Australia.”

Sturgis turned to George.

“Are you Alfred Lattaker?”

George’s lips moved.

I kicked him.

“Yes,” he said.

“Why did you say you were George?”

“Jet lag,” I said.

This was perhaps ahead of the period, but it expressed the thing.

Sturgis examined him.

“The resemblance is exact.”

“That is twins for you.”

“And the injuries?”

“Australia,” I said. “Very rough place.”

Sturgis picked up the hat.

“Where is George?”

“We do not know.”

“He may return?”

“Possibly. George is a restless spirit.”

Sturgis put the hat under his arm.

“I shall remain nearby.”

He left.

George sank into a chair.

“What have you done?”

“Saved you from the dungeon.”

“I haven’t a twin brother.”

“You’ve got a letter saying you have.”

“My uncle is mad.”

“Possibly. But his madness has arrived at the psychological moment. Until we clear up last night’s proceedings, you are Alfred.”

“How long?”

“The *Circe* sails tomorrow evening. Keep out of Sturgis’s way till then.”

“But everybody on board knows me.”

“Only as George. They do not know Alfred.”

“That is the point.”

“You will be introduced as Alfred. You have spent your life in Australia. You know nothing of George’s actions.”

George stared.

“And Stella?”

It was then that the scheme, already good, became magnificent.

“As Alfred,” I said, “you can plead George’s cause.”

“I can?”

“You can tell Stella what a splendid fellow your brother is. You can explain that the kiss meant nothing. Coming from George, that sounds feeble. Coming from his long-lost twin, it has the ring of independent testimony.”

A little colour returned to his face.

“Reggie, you’re a genius.”

There are days when one’s true worth is recognized.

“I have my moments.”

The next item was to inform the yacht.

Old Vanderley accepted the twin without much emotion. Men who own mines are accustomed to new relatives appearing whenever the market is firm. Stella was more difficult.

We found her on deck.

“Stella,” I said, “this is Alfred Lattaker.”

She looked at George.

“Don’t be absurd.”

“George’s twin brother.”

“George has no twin brother.”

“So everybody thought. He was misplaced in Australia.”

George bowed stiffly.

“How do you do?”

Stella stared at him.

“Is this a joke?”

“Do you notice,” I said, “the subtle differences? George slouches. Alfred stands upright. George’s hair goes east. Alfred’s goes west.”

“It is going in precisely the same direction.”

“Superficially.”

She walked around him.

George quivered.

“The resemblance,” she admitted, “is extraordinary.”

“Twins,” I said again. It was becoming a useful word.

Stella’s eyes narrowed.

“Is there no distinguishing mark?”

“Certainly,” I said. “Alfred has a mole.”

George looked alarmed.

“Where?” asked Stella.

“On his—”

I paused. A mole on the face would have been visible. A mole on the hand equally so. I selected a region which good breeding would protect from investigation.

“On his left shoulder-blade.”

George relaxed.

Stella did not.

“George has a mole on his left shoulder-blade.”

I had not known this.

“The family is riddled with moles,” I said.

“When did you arrive?” she asked George.

“This morning.”

“From Australia?”

“Yes.”

“Which part?”

“The central part.”

“What do you do there?”

“Wool.”

“What about wool?”

“I attend to it.”

She continued to look at him, but even the most suspicious girl cannot easily reject documentary evidence combined with a mole. At last she inclined her head.

“How do you do, Mr. Lattaker?”

George breathed again.

“Alfred,” I said, “has heard much about you from George.”

“I hope not.”

“All good.”

“That would hardly fill a postcard.”

She turned away.

George waited until she was out of earshot.

“You see?” he said. “She hates me.”

“She didn’t throw anything.”

“Give her time.”

That afternoon George’s uncle arrived.

Frederick Lattaker was a stout, pink man with white side-whiskers and an expression of permanent good fellowship. He looked like a Christmas card advertising port. On seeing George, he rushed forward with both hands extended.

“My dear boy!”

“Which one?” I said quickly.

He checked himself.

“Ah. Alfred.”

“Correct.”

“Of course. The Australian sun has browned you.”

“It hasn’t,” said George.

“Then the voyage has bleached you. Travel is full of these effects.”

We went into the smoking-room. The moment the door closed, George advanced on him.

“What is the meaning of this?”

Uncle Frederick’s good fellowship weakened.

“My boy—”

“I have no twin brother.”

“No.”

“Then why did you say I had?”

His uncle lowered himself into a chair.

“I found myself in a position of some embarrassment.”

“What position?”

“A financial one.”

George became still.

“My inheritance?”

Uncle Frederick sighed.

“I was trustee.”

“I know.”

“I invested the capital.”

“In what?”

“A series of promising enterprises.”

“What enterprises?”

“There was a folding top-hat company.”

George shut his eyes.

“Also a device for extracting gold from seawater. And a theatrical production based on the Book of Leviticus.”

I had to admit that the last showed vision.

“You lost it?” said George.

“Temporarily.”

“How much?”

“All.”

George opened his eyes.

“All?”

“There may be three hundred pounds left. Or it may be thirty. The accounts are complex.”

“And Alfred?”

“I invented him.”

“Why?”

“I needed time. You expected me to arrive today and hand over the money. I felt the emergence of a brother might distract you.”

“It has.”

“I am delighted.”

George made a movement toward him. I intervened. One should not permit a fellow to assault an uncle before tea, particularly when there is already a prince on the police books.

“You old scoundrel!” said George.

Uncle Frederick looked pained.

“Scoundrel is a harsh word.”

“What would you prefer?”

“Optimist.”

“You stole my money.”

“I invested it unwisely.”

“In a musical Leviticus!”

“There were to have been dancing priests.”

George collapsed into a chair.

“I’m ruined.”

“For the moment.”

“I shall expose you.”

Uncle Frederick brightened.

“Then you will expose yourself.”

George stared.

“You are at present pretending to be Alfred. If you announce publicly that there is no Alfred, the detective will conclude that you are George.”

This was true. The old gentleman, though weak in the matter of seawater, had a clear grasp of tactics.

“You see my difficulty,” he continued. “And yours. We are, if I may put it so, twins in adversity.”

George uttered a low growl.

“What am I to live on?”

“Hope.”

“There isn’t much nourishment in hope.”

“I have often found it sustaining.”

“You weigh sixteen stone.”

Uncle Frederick rose.

“I shall return to my hotel. We can discuss everything quietly after the yacht sails.”

“You are not leaving Monte Carlo.”

“I thought it better to sleep ashore.”

“You thought correctly,” I said.

He went out.

George sat silent.

I poured him a whisky-and-soda.

“So that is that,” he said.

“Not necessarily.”

“I have lost Stella, my money, and my name.”

“You still have your health.”

He touched his black eye.

“Some of it.”

“Buck up, old scout. As Alfred, you have made a favourable start.”

“She despises George.”

“You must alter her view.”

“How?”

“Talk to her.”

He did.

I found them half an hour later on the afterdeck. George was speaking with feeling.

“My brother is impulsive,” he was saying. “But his heart is pure.”

Stella leaned against the rail.

“Are you always so loyal to people you have never met?”

George hesitated.

“Blood is thicker than water.”

“So is porridge.”

“That kiss meant nothing.”

“You were not there.”

“I know George’s mind.”

“Telepathy?”

“Twinship.”

She looked out at the sea.

“I thought he loved me.”

“He does.”

“He has an odd way of showing it.”

“He was intoxicated by happiness.”

“He was intoxicated by something.”

“No. That came later.”

Stella’s mouth twitched.

“Has he told you what he did later?”

“Nothing definite.”

“The police are looking for him.”

“He may be innocent.”

“He may be. But innocence has not been his most conspicuous feature lately.”

George stepped closer.

“Suppose he came to you and said he was sorry.”

“He did say he was sorry.”

“Suppose he said it better.”

“He had his opportunity.”

“Suppose he explained that, when you accepted him, the universe seemed so radiant that he lost control and embraced the first convenient symbol of creation.”

“That sounds like Reggie.”

I coughed.

They looked round.

“Sorry,” I said. “I was searching for a cushion.”

“There are no cushions here,” said Stella.

“That explains why I haven’t found one.”

I withdrew.

The campaign continued through dinner. George, as Alfred, told anecdotes illustrating George’s nobility. Most were invented, and the rest had happened to other people. By coffee, George had saved a child from a burning house, restored a widow’s purse, refused a peerage, and carried a wounded missionary across the Australian desert.

“You seem to know a great deal about him,” Stella said.

“Twins correspond.”

“I thought you had only just discovered each other.”

George paused.

“The letters crossed.”

Later, Stella found me alone.

“Reggie.”

“Hullo?”

“Do you believe in this Alfred?”

“My dear girl!”

“Do you?”

“I have seen his documents.”

“What documents?”

“The letter.”

“Written by his uncle?”

“Exactly.”

“The uncle who invested in a musical version of Leviticus?”

News travelled rapidly aboard the *Circe*.

She looked toward the saloon, where George was losing at piquet to old Vanderley.

“George cheats at cards,” she said.

“Never.”

“He always scratches his ear when he has a good hand. Alfred does the same.”

“Family gesture.”

“And Alfred knows I dislike sugar in coffee.”

“George may have written it.”

“In the letters that crossed?”

“Probably a postscript.”

She smiled suddenly.

“Reggie, you are a dreadful liar.”

“I prefer strategist.”

“Is George in danger?”

This was a direct question, and direct questions are the enemy of strategy. I assumed a grave expression.

“There are powerful forces at work.”

“The police?”

“Among others.”

“Did George attack the prince?”

“I don’t know.”

Her face changed.

“You really don’t?”

“He remembers a fight. His hat was found.”

“Oh.”

It was a small sound, but it told me more than one of George’s forty-minute speeches. She was worried about him.

“Stella,” I said, “he is an ass, but not a criminal.”

“I know he is an ass.”

“He loves you.”

“I know that too.”

“Then everything is practically settled.”

“Nothing is settled.”

Women like keeping matters in suspense. It gives them a sense of management.

Unknown to us, another powerful force had entered the arena.

Voules had discovered the truth.

How he did so I learned later. He had passed the smoking-room while George was throttling Uncle Frederick and had heard enough to fit the pieces together. A good valet is trained to collect information while appearing to collect hats.

His manner toward George remained perfect. Indeed, it became more perfect. He addressed him as “Mr. Alfred” with a respectful emphasis which should have warned us. But we were busy watching Sturgis, who had installed himself at a café opposite the quay and appeared to have taken root there.

At nine that evening Voules asked permission to go ashore.

“Certainly,” I said. “Taking Miss Pilbeam?”

“No, sir.”

“Ah.”

“I shall not trouble Miss Pilbeam again.”

There was a sombre note beneath the words.

“You haven’t quarrelled?”

“Miss Pilbeam has expressed admiration for Mr. Lattaker, sir.”

“George?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Because he kissed her?”

“She considered the incident romantic.”

“Women are odd.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Still, you mustn’t let a small thing like that wreck your happiness.”

“No, sir.”

“Talk it over.”

“No, sir.”

I looked at him.

“Was that a no meaning yes, or a no meaning no?”

“No, sir.”

He left.

Pilbeam appeared soon afterward and asked where he had gone.

“Ashore.”

“Alone?”

“So he indicated.”

She tossed her head.

“He can go where he likes.”

“This seems to be the popular view.”

“I never encouraged him.”

“You were engaged to him.”

“That was before he became jealous and overbearing.”

“Overbearing?”

“He said I ought not to admire Mr. Lattaker.”

“Voules has old-fashioned ideas.”

“He said no respectable woman would be pleased to be kissed against her will.”

“Quite medieval.”

Her eyes flashed.

“I wasn’t pleased.”

“Of course not.”

“I merely said it showed spirit.”

“Undoubtedly.”

“And Mr. Lattaker is handsome.”

“From certain angles.”

“And if Voules chooses to sulk, he may.”

She marched out.

The next morning Voules returned at breakfast.

He had gone ashore with fifty francs. He returned with four hundred thousand.

Roulette had done it. He had placed the first fifty on seventeen because seventeen was Pilbeam’s birthday. Seventeen had appeared. He had moved the winnings to black, then red, then odd, then a number suggested by George’s hat size. By two in the morning he had accumulated a chunk of o’ goblins sufficient to purchase a modest estate and several respectful neighbours.

He told me this while laying out my socks.

“Four hundred thousand francs?” I said.

“Yes, sir.”

“You won it?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Good heavens.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And you are laying out my socks?”

“I have not yet resigned, sir.”

The fellow’s professionalism moved me deeply.

“What will you do?”

“I may acquire property, sir.”

“Excellent.”

“And perhaps enter public life.”

“Why?”

“I understand it requires no previous experience.”

There was logic here.

“Have you told Pilbeam?”

“No, sir.”

“She will be delighted.”

“I no longer intend to marry Miss Pilbeam.”

“Nonsense.”

“I have been deceived in her, sir.”

“You haven’t been deceived. You’ve been jealous.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You are making a mistake.”

“Possibly, sir.”

He smoothed a handkerchief.

“Before leaving your service, sir, there is a matter I feel obliged to clarify.”

“What matter?”

“Mr. Alfred, sir.”

I felt a chill.

“What about him?”

“He is Mr. George.”

“You amaze me.”

“No, sir.”

“Twins can be remarkably alike.”

“There is no twin, sir.”

“What makes you say that?”

“I heard Mr. Frederick Lattaker admit it.”

“Eavesdropping, Voules?”

“I was passing with a clothes-brush, sir.”

There is no defence against a man with a clothes-brush.

“Look here,” I said. “You won’t mention this?”

“I fear I must, sir.”

“Why?”

“I consider Mr. Lattaker’s conduct toward Miss Pilbeam dishonourable.”

“He kissed her once.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Without meaning anything.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And she is in love with you.”

“She was impressed by his spirit.”

“You have four hundred thousand francs. Be magnanimous.”

“I intend to be just, sir.”

Justice is one of the most dangerous passions. I tried argument, sentiment, bribery, and an appeal to his professional pride. Nothing moved him.

“At what time do you plan this ghastly act?” I asked.

“When all concerned are assembled, sir.”

“Why?”

“It will avoid repetition.”

He withdrew.

I hurried to George.

“We are sunk.”

He was dressing for lunch.

“What now?”

“Voules knows.”

George dropped a hairbrush.

“How?”

“Clothes-brush.”

“What is he going to do?”

“Expose you publicly.”

George sat on the bed.

“Then I shall be arrested.”

“Possibly.”

“Stella will know I lied.”

“Definitely.”

“My uncle will escape.”

“Almost certainly.”

He put his head in his hands.

“I might as well jump overboard.”

“I wish everyone would stop suggesting that. It is bad for the tone of the yacht.”

There came a knock.

Sturgis entered without waiting.

He had lost some of his dryness. His eyes glittered.

“Prince Otto has regained consciousness,” he said.

George rose.

“What does he say?”

“Very little. He asks to come aboard.”

“Aboard?”

“He has learned that a person connected with this yacht was present during the attack.”

George gripped the bedpost.

“When?”

“Within the hour.”

Sturgis studied him.

“I suggest, Mr. Alfred Lattaker, that you remain available.”

He went out.

George turned to me.

“This is the end.”

“It may be.”

“Thank you.”

“I mean, it may also clear you.”

“How?”

“Perhaps the prince saw his attacker.”

“Then he saw me.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I remember hitting someone.”

“People are always hitting the wrong person in night fights.”

“I shall confess.”

“Not before lunch.”

“Why do you keep talking about lunch?”

“Because it is sole meunière, and emotional scenes make it leathery.”

We ate little. At least George ate little. I have always believed that one should keep up one’s strength in a crisis.

At two o’clock the principal cast assembled on deck.

There were George, Stella, old Vanderley, Uncle Frederick, Sturgis, Pilbeam, Voules, and myself. Voules had arranged it with the precision of a stage manager. Even the weather seemed to understand that something was expected of it. The sun shone, the sea sparkled, and a launch approached bearing Prince Otto of Saxburg.

The prince was a tall, pale young man with a bandaged head. Beside him sat a severe gentleman in uniform, all braids, spurs, and indignation. This was Count Blankenberg, the prince’s equerry.

They came aboard.

Count Blankenberg clicked his heels.

“His Serene Highness,” he announced, “has expressed the wish to identify the gentleman who was present at the deplorable outrage.”

The word “Serene” has always seemed to me ill-chosen for a man with his head in bandages, but I kept this to myself.

Prince Otto looked around.

His gaze reached George and stopped.

George made a faint noise.

Count Blankenberg pointed.

“You recognize this person, Highness?”

“Yes,” said the prince.

Sturgis stepped forward.

“As George Lattaker?”

Before anyone could reply, Voules raised his voice.

It was the first time I had heard him raise it. The experience was impressive. Trumpets could have taken lessons from him.

“That gentleman,” he said, “is George Lattaker.”

Silence fell.

Stella turned slowly.

George went grey.

Uncle Frederick edged toward the companionway.

Voules continued.

“There is no Alfred Lattaker. The supposed twin brother is a fabrication.”

Count Blankenberg stared.

Sturgis smiled.

Stella said, “I knew it.”

George looked at her.

“You knew?”

“I suspected.”

“Then why—”

“I wanted to see how long you would continue being an idiot.”

“That,” I said, “might take years.”

She ignored me.

Sturgis moved toward George.

“So. You are George Lattaker.”

“Yes,” George said.

“Your hat was found near the unconscious prince.”

“Yes.”

“You were involved in the fight.”

“I think so.”

“And you concealed your identity.”

“Yes.”

Sturgis put a hand on his arm.

“In that case—”

Prince Otto interrupted.

“Why are you arresting him?”

Sturgis paused.

“He attacked Your Highness.”

“He did not.”

Everyone spoke at once.

Count Blankenberg restored order by clicking his heels more loudly.

Prince Otto pointed to George.

“This gentleman saved my life.”

George stared.

“I did?”

“You did.”

“Are you sure?”

“I was there.”

This was sound evidence.

The prince lowered himself into a chair.

“I was returning from the Casino shortly before three. A man in a mask followed me from the gardens. He struck me from behind and attempted to take a leather case containing state papers.”

“Not jewels?” I asked.

“State papers.”

“Ah.”

Jewels would have been more romantic, but one cannot have everything.

“The masked man knocked me down,” continued Prince Otto. “Then this gentleman appeared. He grappled with the assassin. There was a fierce struggle.”

George touched his bruised eye.

“I knew I had been busy.”

“You struck the man several times. He fled. Unfortunately, in pursuing him, you tripped over me.”

George’s expression changed.

“Was that when you were injured?”

“No. But you fell upon me.”

“I’m terribly sorry.”

“I was already unconscious.”

“That is something.”

Count Blankenberg frowned.

“His Highness’s condition cannot be described as something.”

“No,” I said. “More a general state.”

The prince continued.

“The police found your hat, but the masked attacker had taken your coat during the struggle. It was therefore assumed that the owner of the hat might be the criminal.”

Sturgis released George’s arm.

“Why did Your Highness not say this before?”

“I had concussion.”

Count Blankenberg glared.

“Concussion, Mr. Sturgis, is not conducive to sustained narrative.”

“No,” I said. “It breaks up the flow.”

The prince smiled at George.

“You defended me with great courage.”

George blinked.

“I don’t remember.”

“That makes it more courageous,” I said. “Pure instinct.”

“I remember,” said the prince, “that before charging the assassin you shouted, ‘Nobody shall say George Lattaker stands by while a fellow gets slugged.’”

George looked pleased.

“Did I really?”

“You then attempted to remove your waistcoat and became entangled in it.”

“That sounds like me.”

“You fought magnificently nevertheless.”

Stella was looking at George in a new way. Or perhaps an old way which had been away for repairs.

Uncle Frederick stopped edging toward the stairs and came forward beaming.

“My nephew has always been heroic.”

George gave him a look.

The prince rose.

“My government is anxious to express its gratitude. The papers you saved were of great importance. I have authority to offer you a reward.”

George straightened.

“A reward?”

“A substantial one.”

“How substantial?” asked Uncle Frederick.

Prince Otto named a sum.

It was enough to replace the lost inheritance twice over and leave a bit for hats.

George sat down abruptly.

Uncle Frederick clasped his hands.

“Providence!”

“Keep quiet,” said George.

The prince seemed surprised.

“That gentleman is your uncle?”

“Temporarily.”

Count Blankenberg announced that further ceremonies would follow at the palace. Prince Otto shook George’s hand. Sturgis apologized with the air of a man reserving the right to suspect him again later. Then the royal party departed.

For a moment nobody spoke.

Then Voules coughed.

Pilbeam turned on him.

“Well?”

“Miss?”

“You exposed Mr. Lattaker.”

“Yes, miss.”

“You tried to have him arrested.”

“I believed him guilty, miss.”

“You did it because you were jealous.”

Voules’s composure wavered.

“I considered his conduct—”

“You were jealous.”

“Yes, miss.”

“And now you’re rich, I suppose you think you’re too good for me.”

“No, miss.”

“You said you wouldn’t marry me.”

Voules looked at me.

I looked at the sky. A gentleman does not intrude upon servants at moments of emotional stress, especially when he knows they can resign without notice.

“I was hasty,” he said.

“You were hateful.”

“Yes, miss.”

“And suspicious.”

“Yes, miss.”

“And pompous.”

There was a pause.

“Yes, miss.”

Pilbeam’s eyes softened.

“How much did you win?”

Voules told her.

She drew a quick breath.

“Is that a lot?”

“It is sufficient to purchase a house in the country, miss.”

“With a garden?”

“Yes, miss.”

“And chickens?”

“If desired, miss.”

She considered.

“I have always liked chickens.”

Voules took her hand.

“Then perhaps, miss—”

“Oh, stop calling me miss.”

“Yes, P—”

She kissed him.

Voules, who had faced collapsed deck-chairs and sudden riches without a tremor, turned purple.

I was glad for him, though the thing meant that I should lose the only man on the Riviera who understood the proper treatment of a white waistcoat.

George approached Uncle Frederick.

“As for you—”

His uncle backed away.

“My dear boy, surely this happy outcome wipes out the past?”

“It does not.”

“Your fortune is restored.”

“By a prince, not by you.”

“The source is immaterial. Money has no memory.”

George advanced.

Uncle Frederick retreated behind old Vanderley.

“I have been thinking,” he said rapidly, “that the folding top-hat concern might yet revive. With a small infusion of capital—”

George made a noise like a bulldog discovering an unpleasant visitor.

Uncle Frederick left the deck at speed.

This disposed of everyone except George, Stella, and myself.

Stella stood by the rail. George approached her.

“Stella.”

“Yes, Alfred?”

He winced.

“I suppose I deserved that.”

“You deserved several things.”

“I only pretended because I was frightened.”

“Of the police?”

“And of losing you.”

“You had already lost me.”

“I know.”

“You lied to me.”

“Yes.”

“You invented an Australian childhood.”

“Yes.”

“You said you had raised sheep.”

“I said wool.”

“You told me George once saved a missionary.”

“He may have done.”

“He hasn’t.”

“No.”

“And you made your hair go west.”

“That was Reggie.”

Stella glanced at me.

I smiled modestly.

George moved closer.

“But everything I said about loving you was true.”

“You said George loved me.”

“I am George.”

“So you are.”

“And the kiss meant nothing.”

Pilbeam, passing toward the companionway with Voules, heard this.

“Thank you, sir,” she said.

“I mean—dash it—”

“She knows what you mean,” said Stella.

Pilbeam smiled and went below.

George took Stella’s hand.

“Can you forgive me?”

Stella did not answer at once. She had the look of a magistrate deciding whether to impose a fine or send the fellow down for six months.

“You behaved abominably,” she said.

“I know.”

“You are impulsive, deceitful, and absurd.”

“Yes.”

“You got drunk.”

“Yes.”

“You tried to make me jealous of a man who did not exist.”

“Yes.”

“You allowed Reggie to manage the whole affair.”

George looked doubtful.

“I was desperate.”

“Am I to take it,” I said, “that there is criticism of my handling?”

They both turned.

“Go away, Reggie,” said Stella.

“Certainly. Only I should like it recorded that without my strategy—”

“Go away.”

I went aft.

Behind me there was silence, followed by a sound which indicated that the engagement had resumed.

I found old Vanderley opening champagne in the saloon. He offered me a glass.

“Everything settled?” he asked.

“Practically.”

“George and Stella?”

“Reconciled.”

“Voules and Pilbeam?”

“Also reconciled.”

“The prince?”

“Still Serene.”

“And Frederick?”

“At large.”

Old Vanderley filled my glass.

“You’ve managed the business well, Pepper.”

I accepted the tribute with becoming gravity.

“One does what one can.”

At that moment George put his head through the doorway.

“Reggie.”

“Yes, old top?”

“If you come within fifty yards of the afterdeck during the next hour, I shall throw you into the harbour.”

Then he disappeared.

Old Vanderley regarded me.

“Popular fellow, aren’t you?”

“I have always found,” I said, taking a restorative sip, “that gratitude is the rarest of the virtues.”

From above came Stella’s voice.

“George, were you really going to drown yourself?”

“No.”

“You told Reggie you were.”

“I was upset.”

“And did you truly feel you wanted to kiss the whole world?”

“No.”

“You told him that too.”

“He exaggerates.”

This wounded me. I had reported the affair with scientific accuracy.

Old Vanderley raised his glass.

“To twins,” he said.

“To twins,” I agreed.

“Even imaginary ones.”

“Especially imaginary ones. Real twins would have complicated everything.”

And with that we drank, while outside the Mediterranean glittered, the Casino waited for Voules to return his winnings, and Fate, having done a hard week’s work, appeared at last to have knocked off for lunch.

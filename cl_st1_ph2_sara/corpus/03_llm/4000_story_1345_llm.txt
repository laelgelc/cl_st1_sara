I ought to say at once that nothing happened to me between Wingdam and Slumgullion.

No masked horseman presented a pistol at the window; no pale child, wrapped in a cloak of suspicious fineness, was discovered beneath the seat; no murdered man rode upon the box beside the driver; and no young woman of extraordinary beauty subsequently proved to be the lost heiress of a Spanish grandee. I mention these deficiencies because the road, the hour, and the vehicle were otherwise admirably fitted for them, and the experienced reader might justly feel himself lured under false pretences.

I had spent the evening at the Wingdam Hotel, an establishment which seemed to have been built during a season of public remorse. Its parlour contained seven men, one woman, a stove, and a chromolithograph of Niagara Falls; but the stove alone exhibited warmth, and even that with an air of official reluctance. The men sat against the wall in various attitudes of judgment. They did not converse; they appeared to have already conversed, to have found the practice unsatisfactory, and to be awaiting death with increased respect for silence.

The woman, who was elderly, was telling the clerk about her daughter Maria.

“I sez to Mariar,” she observed, for perhaps the eleventh time, “‘Mariar, praise to the face is open disgrace.’”

The clerk, who had been trying for half an hour to insert a pen behind his ear and had not yet abandoned hope, replied that this was so.

“Praise to the face is open disgrace,” repeated the woman, with the calm of one announcing a law of gravitation which she had personally arranged.

I had no objection to the sentiment. Indeed, it was probably sound; but there are truths which become crimes through repetition. Before the stage arrived, the maxim had entered my consciousness, removed its boots, and established itself there for the winter.

At eleven o’clock the driver appeared in the doorway and said, “Slumgullion.”

He did not say that the stage was ready, or that persons desiring transportation should prepare themselves, or even that Slumgullion lay in any particular direction. He merely pronounced the name, much as a Roman executioner might have pronounced a sentence, and withdrew.

I followed him.

The coach stood in the road under a lamp whose principal achievement was to illuminate the dust immediately beneath it. Beyond that small circle all was obscurity. The horses were visible chiefly as a collective restlessness—bits of harness moving without apparent support, an occasional hoof striking sparks, and two clouds of vapour where I judged their heads to be. The stage itself resembled a large black wardrobe which had taken to drink and fallen upon wheels.

Three citizens of Wingdam had assembled to witness my departure. None offered assistance or farewell. The first leaned toward the forward wheel and spat upon it with deliberation. The second, apparently unwilling to imitate him too closely, bestowed a similar attention upon the road beneath the door. The third waited until I had mounted, then projected his contribution toward the rear axle with a graceful curve that suggested long practice.

Whether this was benediction, contempt, or merely local deportment, I could not determine. But as the stage moved off and these figures sank into darkness, I felt that I had been initiated into some rough mystery of the mountains—that coach, horses, luggage, and passenger had received a weird baptism, and were henceforth committed to whatever gods preside over mud, darkness, and defective springs.

The interior was empty. This promised comfort, but did not provide it.

An empty stagecoach at night possesses an amplitude wholly illusory. Its corners invite the limbs with specious hospitality; its cushions suggest repose; its hanging straps extend themselves like spectral hands, offering aid. But no sooner has the traveller committed his body to one of these arrangements than the vehicle, with malignant intelligence, removes it. The seat rises to strike him, the roof descends to receive his hat, and the opposite side advances briskly to meet his shoulder. Solitude merely permits him to be assaulted in several directions without witnesses.

I placed my valise beneath my feet, arranged my coat as a pillow, and lay across the seat. The stage immediately descended into a hollow, and I was folded at the waist. It then climbed a stone and unfolded me with unnecessary emphasis. A loose bolt beneath the floor kept up a small metallic objection; the leather braces groaned; the windows chattered in their frames; and the axle, evidently dissatisfied with the whole enterprise, uttered at intervals a long, expiring complaint.

Sleep was impossible.

“Praise-to-the-face-is-open-disgrace,” said the wheels.

I changed my position.

“Praise-to-the-face-is-open-disgrace,” replied the springs.

It was of no use. I counted the jolts, recited fragments of verse, attempted to remember the kings of England, and composed an argument proving that travel enlarged the mind chiefly by making it anxious to be elsewhere.

It was of no use.

The old woman’s maxim had adapted itself perfectly to the mechanism of the coach. On smooth ground it trotted; on steep ground it laboured; in the hollows it was compressed into one violent moral thump.

Praise—to—the—face—

A stone intervened.

—is open disgrace.

I sat upright and listened.

How quiet the driver was!

Until that moment I had not considered him. His silence now became immense. He had not spoken to the horses; he had not cracked his whip; he had not coughed, shifted his weight, or indulged in any of those modest noises by which a human being certifies his continued existence. Above me there was only the creak of the box and the dull beat of hoofs.

Was there any driver?

The question, once admitted, found the interior singularly congenial. Perhaps, at the last turn, highwaymen had stopped the stage. Perhaps the driver lay bound in the brush, while one of the robbers, wearing his coat and hat, guided us toward a ravine where his companions waited. That I had heard no command, struggle, shot, or change of pace did not weaken the theory. On the contrary, it proved the professional excellence of the criminals.

I drew back the curtain a fraction. Nothing was visible except a darkness so complete that it seemed less an absence of light than a positive substance pressed against the glass.

Then, naturally, I ceased to be an American traveller in an ill-sprung stage and became the Marquis Henri de Valcour, youngest colonel in the armies of His Most Christian Majesty, bearer of a packet upon which depended the fate of France.

I wore lace at my wrists. My sword, which had hitherto escaped my notice, lay across my knees. In the breast of my embroidered coat reposed the royal dispatch, while beneath it—nearer the heart, naturally—was the miniature of a woman whose name I had sworn never to pronounce.

The coach halted in a forest of gigantic oaks.

“Descend, monsieur,” said a voice.

I descended with that negligent elegance which aristocrats preserve under circumstances fatal to common men. Twelve brigands surrounded me. Their chief was a Corsican of sombre aspect, scarred from temple to chin, and dressed with more taste than his profession warranted.

“The packet,” he demanded.

“France has no packet for thieves,” I replied.

This was not perfectly clear, but in such encounters clearness is less important than deportment.

They bound me to a tree. The Corsican raised his pistol. At that instant a woman in a black velvet mask sprang from the shadow, struck up the weapon, and cried, “Hold! You do not know whom you are about to kill!”

Neither did I, precisely, but I felt that her information would be interesting.

She removed her mask.

It was not the face of any woman I had known. It was, however, the face of a woman whom any marquis would have been gratified to know at once: dark eyes, proud lips, a single diamond trembling at the throat, and that pallor which in romance indicates either noble birth or insufficient circulation.

“Henri!” she cried.

“Isabelle!”

At this point the driver spat.

The sound came distinctly from above—an honest, fatigue-laden frontier expectoration, followed by the faint impact of its result upon the road. Never did trumpet from a friendly citadel bring greater reassurance. The driver existed. More than that, he was free in the use of his mouth and therefore had probably not been gagged. The Corsican vanished; France recovered her packet; Isabelle, with all her diamonds, dissolved into the upholstery.

Presently the moon rose.

At first a thin brightness touched the upper edge of the window; then a pale country unfolded itself—low hills, scattered pines, and rocks that looked like sheep lying down. Far off, above a black ridge, one star burned with a patient sorrow. Another stood beyond it, remote and solitary, and for a little while I was moved by the immeasurable loneliness of those fires, each suspended in silence, each separated from its companion by distances beyond thought.

Then the coach rounded a shoulder of the mountain, the full moon appeared between two pines, and the scene became theatrical. I had seen that moon before. It belonged to an opera. A tenor in boots of improbable softness should have entered from the left, placed one hand upon his chest, and sung of exile; a soprano should have answered from a cardboard balcony; the pines should have trembled under the efforts of unseen stagehands.

Framed by the coach window, the wilderness became a private box of cheap enchantment. I regretted only the absence of a programme.

Something glittered upon the seat opposite. I picked it up.

It was a hairpin.

In daylight a hairpin is a humble instrument, without history or moral intention. At midnight, in an empty stagecoach crossing a desolate range, it becomes evidence.

I examined it in the moonlight. It was bent, but whether by accident, agitation, or a struggle could not be said. One end bore a thread—possibly black, possibly brown, possibly merely dirt. Had a woman occupied this seat before me? Assuredly. Had she been young? The pin was not communicative. Beautiful? Its silence deepened. Had she dropped it while fleeing from persecution, or while adjusting her hair after an ordinary journey? Here the mind, trained upon superior literature, naturally rejected the ordinary journey.

I constructed its owner with care. She was slender, self-possessed, and travelling under an assumed name. She had overheard two men conspiring at Slumgullion; she had concealed a document in the lining of the coach; she had—

I awoke with the hairpin in my hand.

At least I believed I awoke. The stage was still moving, but with a new smoothness that seemed almost supernatural. The moon had shifted. My neck was stiff, my feet were numb, and my valise, formerly beneath them, now rested on my chest like a small but determined creditor.

I had not slept. I was certain of that. I had merely withdrawn for a moment into profound contemplation.

The road began to descend. The horses quickened their pace; the wheels, released from their moral duties, abandoned “Praise-to-the-face-is-open-disgrace” and took up a more reckless vocabulary. We swung round bends with a freedom that made the interior appear undecided which side should be floor. The straps danced, the windows applauded, and every bolt in the body of the coach contributed an independent warning.

Once I was lifted bodily from the seat and set down in another place. I accepted the transfer without protest. Civilisation is largely the knowledge of when protest will not improve matters.

Lights appeared below us, scattered in the hollow—first three, then a dozen, then a wavering congregation of lamps. Slumgullion! The name, which in Wingdam had sounded like a threat, now possessed the sweetness of home. A dog barked. Another answered. We crossed a bridge with a thunderous emphasis wholly disproportionate to its size, climbed a final street, and stopped before a hotel whose porch contained several men apparently appointed to receive arrivals by not looking at them.

The driver climbed down. In the lantern light he proved to be a small, weary man with a red beard and an expression of having driven not merely from Wingdam but from the beginning of time.

I descended with some difficulty and restored my limbs to their customary relations.

“You made pretty good speed toward the end,” I said.

“Yep.”

“I had supposed we should change horses somewhere on the road.”

“We did.”

I looked at the four animals, steaming beneath the lamps. They were certainly not those with which we had departed; these were darker, or lighter, or perhaps merely horses seen under improved conditions.

“When?”

“Two hours back. At Black Creek.”

I smiled with the indulgence one gives to an obvious mistake.

“My good man, I was awake the entire journey.”

He considered me without hostility.

“You got out,” he said, “walked round while we changed, asked for a drink of water, climbed back in, and went to sleep afore we’d hit the grade.”

He removed my valise from the boot and set it at my feet.

I searched my memory. There was Wingdam, the ceremonial citizens, the maxim, the brigands, Isabelle, the moon, the hairpin, and the descent into Slumgullion. Of Black Creek, four horses, water, or voluntary locomotion, there was nothing.

The driver spat thoughtfully into the dust.

I did not argue. There are occasions when praise to the face may be open disgrace; but contradiction in the presence of a witness is merely wasted exertion.

I entered the hotel carrying the hairpin, the valise, and the chastened conviction that a man may remain awake all night with remarkable vigilance, provided he is asleep during the uninteresting portions.

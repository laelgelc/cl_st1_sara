## THE LAST THROW

There are ball games a man remembers by a score in the record book, and there are others that come back to him in the night with every face, every shout, every white flash of the ball as plain as on the day they were played. That last game with Buffalo belongs to the latter kind.

We had climbed all season for it. We had climbed lame, sick, patched with tape, and weary enough to sleep standing. One more victory meant the pennant. Defeat meant that the long fight—the broken fingers, split hands, twisted ankles, hot railroad journeys, and bitter double-headers—had gone for nothing.

And for me there was more than a flag at the top of the pole.

Milly had said, ``Win it, Con, and then perhaps we can talk about that little white cottage.''

I had seen the cottage every night for a month. It stood in a green place outside Worcester, with vines over the porch and one upstairs window looking east. Sometimes, when the team was beaten and the papers called me a blunderer, that window faded. Sometimes, after the Rube had pitched us to victory, it shone like sunrise.

On the morning of the Buffalo game I could see neither cottage nor pennant. I could see only nine worn-out players dressing in a close, smoky clubhouse.

Spears sat on the rubbing-table, winding a strip of cloth about his left thigh. He was our captain, third baseman, old war-horse, and chief mischief-maker. His hair was gray at the temples, his nose had been broken twice, and one knee was so stiff that he went down-stairs sidewise. Yet he could read a pitcher’s thoughts from the set of his shoulders.

McCall, our shortstop, had a black bruise from hip to knee.

Ashwell, at second, had two cracked fingers taped together and a swollen ankle that barely went into his shoe.

Stringer, in center, had not spoken ten words in three days. He was a long, dark fellow, tremendous through the shoulders, with the nervous hands of a violin-player and the speed of something wild.

At the far end sat the Rube.

He had pitched four games in eight days. His right arm hung heavy. He moved slowly, as he always did, but there was an unnatural droop to him now. He was big as a barn door, sunburned, mild-eyed, and about as talkative as a stone fence.

I went to him.

``How is it?'' I asked.

He lifted the arm, bent it once, and let it fall.

``It’s there,'' he said.

``Can you go nine?''

``Reckon so.''

That was the Rube. Had his arm been tied on with string, he would have reckoned he could go nine.

The noise outside rolled through the wooden walls. Buffalo had brought down half its town. Worcester had brought the other half. Every seat was packed, the aisles were jammed, boys hung from poles and roofs, and men without tickets stood beyond the outfield ropes. The grand stand trembled under a steady pounding of feet.

Spears finished wrapping his leg and limped into the middle of the room.

``Listen, fellers,'' he said.

They looked up.

``We’re a bunged-up lot of stiffs. No use lyin’ about it. The Rube’s got no more arm than a wooden Indian. Ash can’t pick up a quarter. Mac’s leg looks like spoiled beef. I can’t run from here to that door without stoppin’ for lunch.''

Nobody smiled.

Spears shoved out his jaw.

``But Buffalo’s got to beat us before they get that rag. An’ they ain’t done it yet.''

``They won’t,'' said Stringer quietly.

Spears turned on him.

``That’s talk! This game’s our’n. It’s been our’n since April. All we got to do is go out an’ collect it.''

He looked at the Rube.

``Save nothin’. If your arm comes off, pitch the last one left-handed.''

The Rube nodded.

Then Spears glanced at me. He knew I was near the breaking point. For two weeks I had managed by guesses, prayers, and the courage of those men.

``Con,'' he said, ``you sit on the bench an’ don’t worry. I’ll run the club on the field.''

That was relief and humiliation together. But he was right. Spears could smell a play before it started. I took his hand.

``Bring it home,'' I said.

He grinned.

``Already packed.''

The moment we ran out, the crowd rose in one body. Sound struck us like wind. Hats sailed. Canes beat the boards. Tin horns blared. Thousands were yelling, but I could distinguish no words—only the immense, ragged thunder of hope.

The Buffalo players came from their bench loose and confident. They were a strong club, clean-fielding, hard-hitting, patient at bat. Their manager, Hanley, was a cold-eyed fox who had watched the Rube weaken in the series.

He walked near our bench.

``Going to work the farmer again, Con?'' he asked.

``You’ll know soon enough.''

He looked toward the Rube warming up.

``We’ll make him pitch.''

That sentence chilled me, for I knew what he meant.

Buffalo would not swing at a doubtful ball. They would foul good ones, step out, delay, bunt, threaten to steal, and compel the Rube to use his strength on every pitch. They did not have to hit him early. They only had to keep him working until his arm died.

The umpire called the captains. The crowd hushed, then broke into cheers again as Spears hobbled to the plate.

We batted first.

Buffalo’s pitcher was Mullaney, a lean sidewheeler with a quick motion and a tantalizing change of pace. He had beaten us twice that season. McCall led off. Mullaney whipped over two strikes before Mac had fairly settled. Then he wasted one outside.

Spears, coaching at third, clapped his hands.

``Make him give it to you, Mac!''

The next came breast-high. McCall slashed it over short. He rounded first like a streak and would have tried for second had Spears not roared him back.

Ashwell limped to the plate.

Every Buffalo infielder knew we would sacrifice. Mullaney knew it. The whole park knew it. Still Ash deadened the first pitch so perfectly that it rolled along the third-base line as though pulled by a thread. The third baseman charged, pounced, and threw him out. McCall reached second.

One out.

Burke, our left fielder, drove a long fly to right. McCall tagged, dashed to third, and slid under the throw in a cloud of dust.

Spears came up with two out.

He could scarcely plant his left foot, but there was no man I would rather have had at bat. Mullaney fed him a low curve. Spears watched it.

``Ball!''

A fast one cut the inside edge.

``Strike!''

Spears turned slowly toward the umpire.

``That one had whiskers on it.''

``Get in there,'' snapped the umpire.

Mullaney tried the same place. Spears met it with a short, chopping swing. The ball shot between first and second. McCall scored standing, and Worcester’s roar tore the air.

Stringer followed. Mullaney, disturbed by the noise, gave him a pitch too far in. Stringer turned, and the crack was like a board splitting. The ball lined past first, Spears limped to third, and Stringer took second on the throw.

Two runners in scoring position!

Our catcher, Pete Doran, came up swinging. Mullaney crossed him with a slow ball, then another. Pete fouled off a fast one and finally bounced sharply to short. The Buffalo shortstop smothered it, but his hurried throw pulled the first baseman off the bag.

Spears scored.

Stringer rounded third. Spears, already across the plate, threw up both hands.

``Hold it!''

Stringer stopped and went back. The first baseman recovered, and further risk would have been madness.

The next batter flied out.

Two runs! Worcester 2, Buffalo 0.

The Rube walked to the box amid a roar that seemed to lift him bodily. He pawed the dirt, stared at Doran’s sign, and swung his arm once. The first Buffalo batter took a strike straight through the heart of the plate.

He took the second pitch, too.

It missed by a finger.

Then he fouled one into the stand, fouled another behind first, let two wide ones pass, and tipped off one more.

Seven pitches!

He finally lifted a fly to Burke.

The next Bison followed the same plan. He stepped out twice. He rubbed dirt on his hands. He let the Rube throw three balls and two strikes. Then he fouled off four.

``Hit the blamed thing!'' yelled somebody from the bleachers.

The Buffalo batter grinned and spoiled another good pitch.

I saw the Rube’s shoulder sag.

Not much. Perhaps no one else noticed. But I had watched every ball he had thrown that season. Hanley’s cold strategy was already at work.

On the twelfth pitch the batter grounded to Ashwell. Ash moved like a man stepping on knives, scooped the ball with his sound hand, and got it to first.

Two out.

The third hitter singled on the first pitch. The fourth waited him out to three and two, then sent a hard roller toward McCall. Mac lunged, knocked it down with his chest, scrambled after it, and forced the runner at second.

No score, but the Rube had thrown twenty-seven pitches.

He came to the bench breathing through his mouth.

``Easy,'' I said. ``Don’t waste yourself.''

He reached for the water bucket.

``They won’t hit,'' he answered.

``That’s just it. They’re waiting.''

Spears leaned close.

``Let ’em wait, Rube. Slip over the little one when you can. Don’t try to knock Doran down.''

The Rube drank, wiped his lips, and said nothing.

We failed to score in the second. Mullaney had steadied. His side-arm ball skimmed in like a flat stone over water, and our tired hitters could not get hold of it.

Buffalo began its half with a bunt.

The Rube was slow from the box. The ball trickled toward third, Spears hobbled in, bare-handed it, and threw from an impossible angle. The runner was out by half a stride.

Spears came up limping worse than before.

``You all right?'' I called.

He waved me back to the bench.

The next batter let nine pitches go before drawing a walk. Then Buffalo started its inside work. The runner broke for second, stopped, danced back, and broke again. Doran signaled for pitch-outs. Twice the Rube wheeled without throwing. On the third move the runner went, and Doran’s throw nailed him.

The crowd howled with delight.

But the damage was being done, not on the bases, but in the Rube’s arm.

He struck out the next man with a high fast ball. When he returned to the bench, his face had gone pale beneath the tan.

For three innings we held the 2–0 lead. Yet I felt no security. Each Buffalo batter worried the Rube like a terrier at a rope. They fouled close pitches, refused to bite at curves, and made him cover first on ground balls. They shouted from their bench.

``Get at him!''

``Make the big feller work!''

``He’s breathin’ hard now!''

The Rube heard. If the taunts angered him, he gave no sign. He merely wound up and pitched.

In the fourth their leadoff man drew a walk after eleven pitches. The next man bunted foul twice, then pushed a slow one past the Rube. Ashwell raced in, but his bad ankle buckled. He recovered the ball too late.

Two on, none out.

Their strongest hitter, Bell, came up.

Bell was built low and solid, with forearms like clubs. He had hit the Rube harder than any man in the league. Doran called for a curve outside. The Rube threw it, but the snap had gone from the pitch. Bell drove it on one hop past Spears.

One run scored. Burke’s throw held the others at first and third.

Worcester 2, Buffalo 1.

The Buffalo crowd found its voice. The section behind their bench rose, waving coats and hammering the railing.

``Get him! Get him!''

The next batter lifted a foul near our dugout. Doran tore after it, crashed against the bench, and caught it with one hand.

One out.

The Rube then tried to pick Bell off first. His throw sailed wide. Our first baseman, Cary, stretched full length and saved it, but the runner from third came home before the ball returned.

Score tied, 2–2.

I could feel the pennant slipping.

Spears called time and walked in from third. The infield gathered around the Rube. I could not hear all he said, but I saw him point once at the Buffalo bench, then toward our own crowd.

Presently he shouted loud enough for me to catch it:

``Let ’em yap. They got to hit the ball yet.''

The Rube nodded.

With runners on first and second, he made his first truly great pitch of the day—a sudden, lifting fast one that Bell’s successor swung under. Strike three.

Two out.

Then he crossed the next hitter with a change. The ball dribbled toward short. McCall charged, snatched it on the run, touched second, and went sprawling over the bag.

The inning was done.

Mac rose slowly, his bruised leg trembling. Ashwell put an arm beneath him.

``Come out!'' I called.

McCall glared at me.

``Not if they bring a wagon.''

He limped to the bench.

Spears followed and dropped beside me.

``How bad?'' I asked.

``Bad enough.''

``The Rube?''

``Worse.''

``Can he last?''

Spears looked out at the packed stands.

``He’s got to.''

In our fifth, McCall beat out a roller by throwing himself headlong at first. Ash sacrificed him to second. Burke struck out, but Spears drew a walk.

Stringer stepped in.

Mullaney had not forgotten the line drive in the first. He worked Stringer outside, farther outside, then tried to slip a fast ball across the inner corner. Stringer drove it into left. McCall came home, and Spears, moving on sheer will, reached third.

We led 3–2.

Doran slapped a single over second, bringing Spears in.

Worcester 4, Buffalo 2!

The Worcester crowd went mad. Men embraced strangers. Women waved handkerchiefs. A boy climbed the grand-stand supports and nearly fell in his excitement. The bleachers rocked until I thought the timbers would split.

Spears came across the plate with a hard, fierce grin.

``Told you, Con. It’s our game.''

I wanted to believe him. But the Rube sat at the end of the bench rubbing his right forearm with his left hand.

Buffalo answered at once.

The first hitter in the fifth singled. The second doubled down the line. Burke got the ball in fast, holding the lead runner at third.

None out. Two in scoring position.

Hanley sent up a pinch hitter, a crafty veteran named Doyle, who had not swung at a bad pitch since the Spanish War. Doyle let two close ones pass. The Rube grooved the next, and Doyle lined it toward center.

Stringer came tearing in. For an instant I thought he could catch it. Then he checked, slid on one knee, and blocked the ball with his body. One run scored. The other runner held at third.

4–3.

Buffalo tried a squeeze.

The batter squared early. The Rube rushed the pitch, throwing it high. Doran leaped, stabbed it, and whirled toward third. The runner had come too far. Doran fired to Spears, who chased him back toward the plate.

The runner twisted past him.

Spears flung himself forward, one hand sweeping through the dust. The umpire’s arm went up.

``Out!''

The Buffalo bench exploded. Hanley stormed onto the field, but the call stood.

In saving the run, Spears had hurt his thigh again. He remained on one knee, head down.

I started out.

He looked up, saw me, and roared:

``Stay where you are!''

His voice stopped me as if I had run against a wire.

The next batter walked. Bell followed with a double into the right-field corner. Two runs crossed.

Buffalo 5, Worcester 4.

Bell stood on second, pounding his fists together. The Buffalo part of the park shook beneath a tremendous roar. The Bisons had done exactly what they planned. They had drained the Rube, waited for his speed to flatten, and struck.

I went to the box.

The Rube stared past me at Doran.

``That’s enough,'' I said.

``No.''

``Your arm’s gone.''

``Still got some.''

``I’ve got Lacey ready.''

The Rube glanced toward our bullpen. Lacey was a willing youngster, but Buffalo had hammered him the day before. We both knew what would happen if I sent him in.

``I started it,'' said the Rube. ``I’ll finish.''

There was no drama in his tone. He spoke as he might have said he would finish mending a fence.

Spears hobbled in.

``Let him alone, Con.''

``He can hardly raise his arm.''

``Then he’ll pitch low.''

I returned to the bench.

The Rube got the third out on a weak fly. As he walked in, Buffalo’s players shouted after him:

``You’re through, farmer!''

``Bring out the undertaker!''

The Rube sat down and pulled his cap over his eyes.

The sixth inning passed without a run for us. Mullaney struck out two and made Cary lift a foul. Buffalo put two men on in its half, but Stringer ran down a drive at the ropes and McCall turned a grounder into a double play. Each defensive effort cost us pain. Each out seemed bought with a strip of flesh.

Before the seventh, Spears gathered the men at the bench.

``They think they got us,'' he said. ``That’s where they’re foolish.''

Ashwell looked down at his swollen ankle.

``Mebbee they got good eyes.''

``Shut up, Ash. We need one run. Just one. Push it over an’ start again.''

The bottom of our order failed quickly. Two out, none on.

McCall came up.

Mullaney tried to waste no strength on him. He threw a fast strike, then a curve. McCall fouled it off. Another fast one came inside and struck Mac on the shoulder, spinning him around.

The umpire sent him to first.

Ashwell followed. With McCall barely able to run, the sacrifice looked useless. But Spears gave the sign.

Ash pushed the bunt toward first. Mullaney took it, looked at second, saw McCall struggling, and threw there.

Mac hurled himself at the bag. The throw beat him, but he slid wide, hooked the base with one hand, and somehow twisted beneath the tag.

``Safe!''

Two on!

Burke came up, and the crowd began chanting his name. He was our cleanest hitter, but Mullaney fooled him completely. Three pitches—two fast, one slow.

Strikeout.

Two out.

Spears to the plate.

He dragged his left leg behind him. His face was gray. I knew he could not play another inning. Hanley knew it, too. He shouted from the Buffalo bench:

``Pitch to the cripple!''

Spears heard and smiled.

Mullaney’s first pitch was outside.

The second broke low.

``Two balls!''

Mullaney stepped off the rubber, rubbed the ball, and studied Doran’s sign. He did not want to walk Spears and face Stringer with the bases crowded. He had to come nearer.

Spears knew it.

The pitch was a fast ball, waist-high, on the outside half. Spears stepped into it with his sound leg and swung.

For one instant there was only the crack.

Then the ball soared toward right.

The right fielder went back. Farther. Farther still. He reached the warning path, turned, and looked up.

The ball dropped beyond the fence.

Home run!

I remember no sound at first. My own senses had been struck dumb. Then the field, the stands, the sky itself burst apart in uproar.

McCall crossed the plate.

Ashwell followed.

Spears rounded first slowly, dragging his leg, while hats rained onto the grass. He touched second, then third. The crowd yelled itself hoarse at every painful stride.

He came home and stepped squarely upon the plate.

Worcester 7, Buffalo 5.

But the umpire was waving his arms.

In the storm of hats and spectators along the lines, Ashwell had been ordered back to second. The right fielder had caught the ball’s rebound from a sign beyond the temporary fence, and the ground rules allowed only a double.

Confusion swept the field. Buffalo protested that the ball was in play. Worcester screamed that it was a home run. The umpires consulted.

At last the chief umpire raised two fingers.

``Ground-rule double! Two runs score. Runner holds second.''

Only McCall and Ashwell counted. Spears had to return to second.

Score tied, 5–5.

The crowd hissed and howled. Spears stood on second, staring toward the plate. Then his expression changed. I knew that look. He had conceived some crooked, splendid little plan.

He walked off the bag and shouted:

``You blind old burglar! That ball was in the next county!''

The umpire warned him.

Spears advanced.

``You couldn’t see a wash-tub if you fell into it!''

``Get back to second.''

``Make me!''

The Buffalo infield laughed. I did not. Spears was too hurt to continue, and he knew I would not willingly remove him with the game tied. So he was arranging it himself.

He limped toward the umpire, flourishing both arms.

``You been robbin’ us since Decoration Day!''

``That’s enough!''

``Ain’t started yet!''

The umpire pointed toward the clubhouse.

``You’re out of the game!''

Spears flung down his cap, kicked dust with his good leg, and gave a magnificent imitation of murderous rage. The crowd shrieked approval.

As he passed our bench, he bent to pick up a bat.

``Put Kane on third,'' he muttered to me. ``My leg’s finished.''

Then, louder, he bellowed at the umpire:

``I’ll have your job for this!''

He disappeared beneath the stand while two policemen pretended to escort him.

Even in that extremity I laughed. That was Spears—cunning to the last inch. He had tied the score with his blow and saved me from the choice of ordering him out.

Stringer now batted with a runner on second. Buffalo wisely walked him. Doran grounded out.

The score remained 5–5.

In the eighth the Rube made his last show of power. Perhaps Spears’s blow revived him. Perhaps he reached down into some reserve no man knew he possessed. He struck out Bell on three pitches, made the next hitter pop to Ashwell, and fanned the third with a curve that broke a foot.

The Worcester rooters stood and screamed his name.

``Rube! Rube! Rube!''

He walked from the mound without looking at them.

When he reached the bench, Doran had to help him sit down.

``One more,'' I said.

The Rube flexed his fingers.

``One more.''

We came to bat in the eighth with the bottom of our order leading. Cary scratched a single through the box. Kane, replacing Spears, bunted him to second.

One out.

McCall hit a slow roller to short. The Buffalo shortstop charged, but the ball took a bad hop and struck his wrist. Mac reached first, Cary holding second.

Ashwell limped in. He had been one of our best hitters all year, a vicious puller of inside pitching. Mullaney gave him nothing inside. Two curves crossed the outer edge. Ash fouled one, took a ball, then swung over the sidewheeler.

Strike three.

Two out.

Burke came next, and all Worcester seemed to rise with him. He had driven in more runs than any man on the club. The situation was made for him.

Mullaney rubbed the ball against his dark trousers. His face had lost its confidence. He glanced at Cary, at McCall, then at Burke.

The first pitch was low.

The second missed outside.

Burke waited.

Mullaney put the next over, and Burke swung through it. A change of pace followed. Burke lunged, checked too late.

Two and two.

The crowd became still. Not quiet, but still in that unnatural way of thousands holding breath together.

Mullaney fired high and tight. Burke jerked back.

Full count.

The runners would go with the pitch.

Mullaney wheeled. Cary broke for third, but the pitcher only feinted and held the ball. Buffalo’s bench jeered.

Again Mullaney set. Again the runners leaned.

The pitch came—a beautiful, sweeping side-arm curve. Burke swung. The catcher held it.

``Strike three!''

For a second no one moved.

Then Buffalo burst into cheers.

Our two best opportunities had died on strikeouts. Cary and McCall trudged back from the bases. Burke hurled his bat toward the rack and sat with his face in his hands.

But the catcher was arguing with the umpire.

I stared.

Mullaney’s curve had struck the dirt before it reached the plate. The catcher had trapped it, not caught it cleanly. Burke, believing himself out, had left the batter’s box.

``Run!'' I screamed.

Burke looked up.

The catcher snatched for the ball. Burke lunged toward first. The throw struck him in the back and shot into right field.

Cary scored.

McCall tore around third, dragging his bad leg. The right fielder fired home. Mac slid beneath the catcher.

Safe!

Burke reached second.

Worcester 7, Buffalo 5!

The stands became bedlam. Buffalo protested. Hanley ran to the plate, shouting that Burke had abandoned the base line. The umpire drove him back.

Then I saw Mullaney appeal at first.

Burke had stepped outside the running lane and interfered with the throw. The umpires met once more amid an ocean of noise. The chief wiped sweat from his face.

At last he called Burke out.

The runs did not count.

Score still 5–5.

Hope had leaped up and been clubbed down within half a minute. I sank upon the bench. Never had the game seemed so cruel. Two of our strongest men had failed, and the ninth waited with the Rube scarcely able to move his arm.

Then Hanley made an error.

Believing Burke’s interference to be the third out, Buffalo left the field. But Ashwell’s strikeout had been only the second out; Burke’s interference made the third—except the umpire ruled that the uncaught third strike had allowed play to continue before the interference. After another conference, Burke was declared out, while Cary and McCall were ordered back to second and first. The inning was not over because the interference call replaced no preceding out against Burke until the play ended.

The decision was tangled enough to make lawyers fight for a year, but it left us two out and two men on.

Hanley shouted until the umpire threatened him.

Stringer came to the plate.

Mullaney wanted no part of him. He delivered four wide pitches.

Bases full.

Doran was next, our second dangerous hitter after Burke. He had caught the whole season, his fingers were split, and his legs had gone stiff. Still he could lam a fast ball farther than any catcher in the circuit.

Mullaney struck him out.

Or so I thought.

The umpire held up two fingers. The earlier confusion had deceived even me: Burke’s interference had been ruled null when the first baseman failed to secure the ball, leaving his strikeout recorded but no out on the interference. Doran’s strikeout was the second out.

The scorekeeper shouted the count from behind the wire.

Two out. Bases full.

And now Stringer should not have been at bat, for I had seen him walked. Yet the official conference wiped away the walk when Buffalo’s catcher appealed that Mullaney’s fourth delivery had been called a strike before time was granted. Stringer returned with a full count, and Doran’s supposed appearance was erased as having occurred out of turn.

It was the wildest muddle I had ever witnessed. Buffalo raged; Worcester roared; the umpires stood by their ruling.

Score tied, bases full, two out, Stringer at bat!

He came back without a word.

Mullaney looked shaken, but his nerve did not break. He fired a fast one on the corner.

``Strike!''

Stringer never moved.

The next pitch swept outside.

``Ball!''

Mullaney tried inside. Stringer fouled it straight back.

Two strikes.

The Buffalo infield crept toward the plate. Their outfielders moved deep. Every runner took a long lead.

``Put it over!'' yelled Hanley.

``Lam one out, String!'' cried McCall from first.

Mullaney wound up and threw his change. Stringer started too soon, checked, and got only the end of the bat upon it.

The ball trickled foul by inches.

A groan went over our crowd.

Again Mullaney set. He threw high.

Two and two.

Then low.

Full count.

Now the runners would move. Cary danced at third. Ashwell crouched at second. McCall, at first after the rearranged ruling, bent over his injured leg.

I could not breathe. In my mind I saw the pennant come down, saw Buffalo carry it away, saw the white cottage darken and vanish.

Mullaney delivered.

The pitch was a fast ball, letter-high, cutting toward the outside corner. Stringer stepped across and drove it.

The ball shot between short and third so hard that neither man got a glove near it.

Cary scored.

Ashwell came behind him.

Burke—restored to second under the ruling—rounded third like a madman. The left fielder charged and threw home.

Stringer never stopped. He tore past first, his long legs devouring the ground.

Burke slid. Doran waved him down. The catcher caught the throw and lunged.

Safe!

Three runs!

Stringer reached second standing, his fists clenched, his dark face blazing for the first time all day.

Worcester 8, Buffalo 5.

No noise I have heard before or since equaled the sound that followed. The grand stand became a living thing. Men pounded one another, women wept, boys shrieked themselves voiceless. Dust rose from the bleachers. The team poured from our bench and met Stringer at second until the umpire drove them away.

Spears appeared at the clubhouse door in his undershirt, hopping on one leg and waving a towel.

``It’s our game!'' he bawled. ``Didn’t I tell you? It’s our game!''

Stringer was left stranded, but those three runs stood.

Only the last of the ninth remained.

The Rube rose.

A silence fell around our bench as he walked toward the box. His right arm hung almost straight. Doran went with him, talking close to his ear.

``Three outs,'' said Ashwell.

``Three,'' answered McCall.

Spears, from the doorway, repeated his refrain:

``It’s our game.''

But I had managed too many innings to trust three runs against Buffalo.

The first batter was Doyle, the patient veteran. He took two balls. The Rube laid in a slow strike. Doyle fouled another, then waited through a pitch that missed low.

Full count.

The Rube threw. Doyle chopped toward short.

McCall sprang to his left. His bad leg gave way, but he flung himself flat and stopped the ball with his bare hand. From his knees he snapped to first.

Out!

Mac remained on the ground.

The trainer and I ran to him. His face was white and wet.

``Something tore,'' he whispered.

``You’re through.''

He gripped my sleeve.

``One out, Con. Don’t waste time.''

We carried him to the bench. Little Ford went to short.

The second Buffalo hitter drove the Rube’s first pitch into right for a single.

The next batter doubled over Cary’s head. The runner from first raced to third.

One out. Two runners in scoring position.

The Buffalo crowd thundered. Their bench hammered bats on the roof.

``Now! Get him now!''

Bell came up.

I considered walking him, but that would load the bases and bring the winning run to the plate. Besides, the Rube could no longer throw four deliberate balls with certainty.

Doran went out to him. The Rube listened, then shook his head.

Bell dug in.

The first pitch floated high. Bell smashed it toward third. Kane knocked it down, but the ball rolled away. One run scored, and Bell reached first.

Worcester 8, Buffalo 6.

Runners at first and third. One out.

The next Bison lifted a fly to Burke. It was deep enough to score the runner from third. Burke caught it and whipped home, but the runner crossed ahead of the throw.

Worcester 8, Buffalo 7.

Two out.

Bell stood on first, representing the tying run.

The Buffalo stands had gone insane. Their rooters were on the field ropes. Hanley clapped his hands and shouted toward the batter:

``One drive, Eddie! One drive wins it!''

Eddie Marsh came to the plate.

Marsh was a left-handed hitter with a long swing and the strength to put a ball over any fence in the league. The Rube had struck him out twice early, but those pitches belonged to another arm.

I looked toward the bullpen. Lacey was throwing. His face was frightened.

No. This belonged to the Rube.

Doran signaled for a curve. The Rube tried it. The ball slipped from his fingers and bounced in front of the plate. Bell started, then scrambled back.

``Ball one!''

The next pitch came straight and slow.

Marsh swung too soon.

Strike one.

The third missed outside.

Two and one.

The Rube stepped from the box. He rubbed his hand against his shirt. The whole field waited. No shouting now. No horns. Twenty thousand people held silence like a weight.

Doran crouched and gave the sign for the inside fast ball.

The Rube nodded.

He lifted his arms. His body turned. For one final time he drove off the rubber and brought his right arm over.

The pitch had no speed. It came waist-high and inward—the ball Marsh had waited for all afternoon.

He swung.

The crack was dreadful.

The ball rose toward deep center, high and hard. Bell raced from first. Marsh flung away his bat and followed.

Stringer turned and ran.

For three steps he did not look up. Then he glanced over his shoulder, changed direction, and sped toward the fence. The ball carried beyond him.

The crowd came out of its silence with one vast scream.

Home run!

That was my thought. Buffalo 9, Worcester 8. Pennant gone.

Bell rounded second. Marsh had crossed first. Stringer still ran, his cap flying.

The ball began to descend beyond the center-field rope.

Stringer reached the fence.

He did not slow.

He planted one foot against the boards and leaped. His body rose like a dark spring uncoiled. One long arm stretched above the fence. The ball struck his glove, glanced out, hit the top board, and fell back into the field.

Not a home run!

Stringer crashed against the fence and dropped.

Bell was beyond third.

The Buffalo coaches waved him home. Marsh flew toward second.

For one terrible instant Stringer lay motionless.

``Get up!'' I screamed, though he could not hear me.

He rolled, pushed to one knee, and found the ball in the grass. There was no chance for him to throw home. Bell was already halfway down the line.

Ashwell came racing into short center.

His ankle was gone, his fingers were broken, but he came like a tiger.

``Here!'' he yelled.

Stringer, still on one knee, whipped the ball underhand. It traveled low and true.

Ashwell caught it near second base.

Bell was twenty feet from home.

Doran blocked the plate.

Ashwell had to throw across his body. He planted the swollen ankle. I saw his face twist. His arm flashed.

The ball shot toward home.

Bell plunged.

Doran reached.

The throw came in waist-high, a shade toward first. Doran snatched it and swung his glove down as Bell’s feet cut into the dirt.

Dust covered them both.

The runner slid across the plate.

Doran rolled over him, holding the ball.

The umpire leaped aside.

For a fraction of a second his arms remained still.

Then his right fist went up.

``Out!''

The game was over.

Worcester had won, 8–7.

For a heartbeat no one understood. Then the Worcester crowd broke through the ropes. Men, boys, women, policemen, and players rushed together. The diamond disappeared beneath a storm of bodies. Hats flew. Horns shrieked. The grand stand shook as if it must collapse.

I tried to reach Doran, but a dozen arms seized me. Somebody struck me on the back. Somebody else kissed my cheek. I saw McCall hopping from the bench, supported by two substitutes. Spears came charging on one leg, brandishing his towel like a flag.

The Rube stood alone beside the pitcher’s box.

His shoulders drooped. His right hand hung open. He looked not at the crowd but toward center field, where Stringer was being carried in.

I fought through to him.

``Rube!''

He turned his mild eyes upon me.

``Did we get him?'' he asked.

I laughed and cried at the same time.

``We got him. We got the pennant.''

He nodded once.

``Reckoned we might.''

Then his knees folded, and I caught him.

They carried us all from the field piecemeal. McCall had torn a muscle. Ashwell could not put his foot down. Spears’s thigh was black from hip to knee. Doran had a spike cut across his forearm. Stringer’s shoulder was bruised where he struck the boards, and the Rube’s arm swelled so badly that the doctor ordered it bound to his side.

Yet in the clubhouse they sang.

They sang badly, loudly, foolishly. They pounded lockers and threw towels. Buffalo’s captain came in, shook the Rube’s left hand, and said:

``That was the gamest stand I ever saw.''

The Rube looked embarrassed.

``String caught it,'' he said.

``Knocked it down,'' Stringer corrected from the rubbing-table.

Ashwell lifted his taped hand.

``An’ I threw it.''

Doran held up the ball.

``An’ I put it on him.''

Spears hobbled into the center.

``That’s why it’s our pennant. Every stiff in the room had a hand on that last ball.''

For once no one joked at his speech.

He was right. The Rube had thrown it. Marsh had driven it. Stringer had pulled it back from beyond the fence. Ashwell had made the relay on a ruined ankle. Doran had held the plate. McCall’s stop had given us the first out. Burke, Cary, Kane, and all the rest had pushed runs across by hits, bunts, sacrifices, and desperate slides.

No one man had won the game.

They had won it together.

Late that evening, after the streets quieted a little, I went outside alone. The park lay dark behind me. Torn paper and broken hats littered the road. Somewhere downtown the celebration still rolled and burst in waves.

In my pocket was a telegram from Milly.

It said only:

``I knew you would. The cottage is waiting.''

I stood under the yellow station lamp and read it three times.

Then I looked back at the black outline of the grand stand. I could still see the last play—the soaring ball, Stringer against the fence, Ashwell whirling, the throw cutting the diamond, Doran’s glove dropping through the dust.

All season we had fought uphill toward that one throw.

And it had come true and straight.

The pennant was ours. The white cottage shone again, its eastern window bright. And beyond the shouting, beyond the pain and dust of the final stand, I could see Milly waiting on the porch.

In the reign of King Louis—whether the Eleventh, Twelfth, or some other Louis I shall not determine, kings of that name having been as plentiful in France as blackberries beside a country road—there lived in the Rue des Lombards a goldsmith called Master Anselme Corbeil, who possessed three things much esteemed in Paris: a strong coffer, a weak conscience, and a daughter of such beauty that men stopped beneath his windows and forgot why they had come into the street.

This daughter was named Isabelle. She was twenty-two years of age, tall, white of skin, black of hair, and carried her head as proudly as though she had been born beneath a canopy instead of behind a shop filled with scales, crucibles, chains, brooches, chalices, rings, and pledges which their owners seldom redeemed. Her eyes were gray, but changed colour according to her temper, being silver when she laughed, steel when she was crossed, and winter water when a fool addressed her as if she were already conquered. Her mouth was red and wilful, her hands delicate, and her wit so sharp that many a gallant, approaching her like a hunter, went away feeling himself the game.

Master Anselme loved his daughter as misers love the key of their treasury: not for itself alone, but for what it may open. He had refused merchants, captains, younger sons, and two rich widowers, always saying that Isabelle deserved something better; by which he meant that no offer yet made had sufficiently fattened his ambition.

Now there practised before the Parliament of Paris an advocate named Feron de Villiers, whose reputation was magnificent and whose person was deplorable. He could make a crooked inheritance appear straight, a stolen field seem honestly purchased, and a perjured witness smell of sanctity; but no art of pleading could persuade the eye that he was handsome. He was near sixty, though he confessed only forty-eight; bent in the shoulders, long in the arms, short in the neck, yellow about the eyes, and furnished with a nose which entered a room before the remainder of him. His sparse beard grew in gray tufts like grass between paving stones. One of his ears was larger than the other, his lower lip hung pensively, and his breath had the authority of an edict, compelling all who encountered it to retire three paces.

Yet Feron was wealthy. He had acquired money in the law, wherein coins breed faster than rabbits if clients are frightened and proceedings prolonged. Moreover, he owned beyond Saint-Denis a fine estate called Vauvert, with vineyards, mills, meadows, woods, ponds, a stone house, and peasants enough to curse their landlord in chorus on feast days.

Seeing Isabelle at Mass, our advocate fell in love—or into appetite, which old men often rename so they may carry it respectably in public. He stared at her throughout the service, prayed to no saint, missed the elevation of the Host, and followed her home at such haste that his clerk supposed a debtor had escaped.

That very afternoon he presented himself to Master Anselme.

“Your daughter,” said Feron, “is a pearl.”

“Beyond price,” replied the goldsmith, who had priced her a hundred times.

“I would marry her.”

“That is a grave proposition.”

“I am a grave man.”

“Indeed,” said Anselme, looking at him, “you are nearly grave enough.”

The advocate did not hear, being busy unfolding documents. He offered marriage settlements, rents, plate, jewels, and, as the crown upon this heap, the estate of Vauvert, to pass eventually into Anselme’s hands under certain conditions so artfully phrased that three notaries afterwards disputed what they meant. But the goldsmith understood the chief point: he would have the estate.

He clasped Feron’s hand.

“You shall be my son.”

“My father!”

Thus affection leapt between them as readily as a spark between flint and steel, though the spark in this case was struck from title deeds.

When Isabelle was summoned and informed of the bargain, she listened without interrupting. This silence delighted her father, who mistook it for obedience, and Feron, who mistook it for maidenly confusion. Men commonly interpret a woman’s silence as saying whatever they most desire to hear.

At last she turned to the advocate.

“Do you truly wish to marry me?”

“More than I wish salvation.”

“Then you shall likely lose both. Look at me, Master Feron.”

“I have done little else since Sunday.”

“And now look at yourself.”

His smile faltered.

“I know what I am,” he said. “A man of position, fortune, and learning.”

“You are an old, ugly, malodorous ape wrapped in a lawyer’s robe.”

“Isabelle!” cried her father.

“Nay, let her speak,” said Feron, attempting a laugh. “Young girls scratch before they purr.”

“I shall never purr for you. If my father forces me to the church, I will go; if the priest asks, I will answer; if you place a ring upon my finger, I may not prevent it. But hear me plainly: I despise you. You shall never possess me willingly, and I shall never be faithful to you.”

There was silence.

Master Anselme became purple. Feron became pink, then pale, then pink again, like a ham being alternately dipped in milk and wine.

“My dove,” said he at last, “these fierce words are but the little fortifications of modesty.”

“They are cannon.”

“Marriage will disarm you.”

“Try it, and marriage may bury you.”

Here the father seized her wrist and declared that daughters were not born to lecture their parents, that obedience was the first jewel of womanhood, that Feron was an honourable advocate, and that the wedding would take place within the month.

Isabelle merely smiled.

There are smiles which invite a kiss, smiles which forgive an injury, and smiles which announce that the executioner has measured your neck. Hers was of the third kind.

Before the wedding, Master Anselme carried his daughter to court, desiring to display his triumph among persons who had laughed at the notion of a goldsmith’s girl becoming mistress of Vauvert. Isabelle wore blue velvet sewn with seed pearls, the which had once belonged to a duchess who pawned it to pay a gaming debt; and since garments, like reputations, often rise when their first owners fall, the gown appeared nobler upon Isabelle than it ever had upon the duchess.

The king saw her.

A king’s eye, as you know, is a falcon for beauty and a mole for expense. His Majesty, standing among ambassadors, captains, bishops, and gentlemen whose grandfathers had all done something violent enough to earn nobility, asked who she was.

“The daughter of Anselme Corbeil, sire.”

“The goldsmith?”

“The same.”

“And the old scarecrow beside her?”

“Feron de Villiers, who is to marry her.”

The king examined Feron and laughed.

“Then justice is blind indeed.”

He sent for Isabelle that evening. Whether she went willingly I need not inquire too strictly; certainly she did not go weeping, and if her heart beat faster upon entering the royal chamber, there are palaces capable of alarming even those who expect good fortune.

His Majesty spoke courteously, for kings begin with poetry where lesser men begin with bargaining.

“Madame,” said he, though she was not yet entitled to the name, “Paris tells me you are to be imprisoned.”

“I am to be married, sire.”

“’Tis often the same sentence with different seals.”

“To an advocate.”

“Then your prison comes with its own gaoler, accuser, and judge.”

“He believes himself also executioner.”

The king smiled. “And what will you do?”

“What I can.”

“Ask a favour.”

She looked about the chamber. On a table lay a small dagger in a sheath of crimson leather, its pommel set with a ruby, its blade slender, keen, and fit for a lady’s hand.

“That,” said she.

The king raised an eyebrow.

“Most women ask jewels.”

“This has a jewel.”

“And something more persuasive.”

“The gift suits my need.”

He gave it to her, but retained her hand an instant after placing the dagger in it.

“If you require refuge after employing my gift,” said he, “remember that the Louvre has many chambers.”

“And each chamber many keys?”

“The king carries the master key.”

“Then I shall remember, sire.”

Of what else passed I cannot report, seeing I was not beneath the tapestry, behind the arras, or in the confidence of the royal laundress. It is enough that Isabelle departed with the dagger hidden in her sleeve and a promise hidden in her smile.

The wedding was celebrated with great magnificence. Feron wore cloth of gold, though cloth of sack would have improved him as much. Master Anselme gave a banquet of twelve courses, at which lawyers, aldermen, merchants, and minor nobles devoured capons, pike, venison, sweetmeats, and one another’s characters. The bride sat pale and splendid beneath a wreath of orange blossoms. The bridegroom drank heavily, boasting that before dawn he would tame the proudest mare in Paris.

“A wise horseman first learns whether the mare has teeth,” said one guest.

Feron replied that law and matrimony both gave a husband remedies. At this the company laughed, for men always laugh at a husband’s confidence before marriage and at his complaints afterward.

At last came the hour when the bride was conducted to the nuptial chamber. The matrons undressed her amid whispering, giggling, advice, and prayers not all of which were directed to saints. She was placed in the great bed, whose curtains were red damask and whose sheets smelled of lavender. Then the women withdrew, leaving candles burning.

Feron entered in a linen shirt and cap, displaying legs so thin that they appeared punctuation marks beneath the sentence of his body. He had perfumed himself extravagantly, but musk, rose water, and ambergris, battling with his natural odour, created such confusion in the air that a moth fell dead from the canopy.

“My wife,” said he.

“My gaoler,” said she.

He advanced, grinning.

She drew the royal dagger and laid its point against his throat.

He stopped so abruptly that his nightcap slid over one eye.

“What jest is this?”

“The last warning before earnest.”

“You are my lawful wife.”

“And this is lawful steel, made by the king’s armourer.”

“I have rights.”

“Bring them one step nearer and I shall open a passage through which they may escape.”

Feron retreated.

“My dear child, you misunderstand marriage.”

“I understand it perfectly. You have purchased ceremony from my father, words from a priest, and signatures from a notary. You have not purchased me.”

“I will call the household.”

“Call them. They shall find you dead in your shirt, and I shall say you attacked me. Who will contradict the king’s dagger?”

At this Feron’s courage, which had survived many hostile judges and several furious clients, retired behind the bed curtains. He attempted reasoning, entreaty, reproach, flattery, command, and a quotation from Roman law. Isabelle answered each by pressing the blade a little more firmly against him.

“Wife—”

“No.”

“Isabelle—”

“No.”

“My angel—”

“Another word, and you may speak with angels.”

Thus the passage of arms concluded without passage, without arms, and with the champion seated in a chair until morning, wrapped in a coverlet and shivering from cold, terror, and disappointed ownership. Isabelle slept soundly, one hand beneath her cheek and the other around the dagger.

At sunrise she rose, dressed herself, took what jewels belonged to her, and called for a litter.

“Where are you going?” asked Feron.

“To one whose gifts are better defended than yours.”

“You cannot leave me!”

“Observe.”

“You are Madame de Villiers.”

“A title may follow if it can keep pace.”

“I will appeal to the courts.”

“You will plead before judges who dine at the king’s table. Prepare a long speech.”

She departed, and before noon was lodged in the royal household.

Now behold the wheel of Fortune, the which crushes one man beneath it and lifts another so high that she forgets the ground. Isabelle, who had entered court as a goldsmith’s daughter with an unwanted husband, soon became the most admired woman in France. The king called her his white falcon; poets compared her brow to ivory, her lips to roses, her neck to a swan, and her bosom to various celestial objects which, if examined by astronomers, would doubtless prove less firm. Painters painted her, musicians sighed beneath her windows, and ambassadors learned her preferences before presenting their credentials.

She took apartments near the Louvre, servants in silver livery, horses from Spain, tapestries from Flanders, pearls from Venice, and emeralds from wherever emeralds come—I know not, being no lapidary. His Majesty gave her the manor of Bellac, the revenues of two bridges, and a house in the Rue Saint-Antoine. Yet royal generosity, like royal taxation, is rarely sufficient by itself; and Isabelle soon discovered that every nobleman seeking a captaincy, pardon, bishopric for a cousin, favourable lawsuit, hunting privilege, remission of debt, or smile from the throne believed a present to her the shortest staircase to the king’s ear.

She did not disabuse them.

A duke sent six silver dishes and obtained command of a province. A count presented a vineyard and saw his brother released from prison. A bishop offered a reliquary containing, so he swore, a finger of Saint Anne; Isabelle returned the relic but retained the gold around it. One lord gave ten thousand crowns for a whispered recommendation. Another gave an estate for a sealed letter, and a third, having nothing left but a handsome person, offered that, which she accepted or declined according to her humour and the king’s engagements.

Thus favours circulated through Paris like counterfeit money, each hand knowing the coin false and passing it eagerly to the next. The king knew something of these affairs, suspected more, and ignored most, for a monarch in love is like a merchant whose clerk steals only after increasing the business. Besides, Isabelle amused him, opposed him without fear, and could send three factions away believing each had obtained her promise.

As for Feron, he remained her husband before God, law, and every person who wished to laugh at him. He brought actions, petitions, memorials, and protests. He cited canon law, civil law, custom, Scripture, and a judgment rendered at Toulouse in the case of a runaway miller’s wife. Nothing profited him. His suits vanished in clerks’ drawers; witnesses forgot what they had seen; judges postponed hearings; and one decree, obtained after six months, was reversed before the ink dried.

The advocate grew thinner. He ceased attending banquets because someone always asked after Madame de Villiers. In court, opposing counsel called him “the defender of marital possession,” whereupon the judges coughed into their sleeves. Boys sang beneath his window:

Master Feron bought a bride,  
Locked the door and stayed outside.

This rhyme wounded him more than all Isabelle’s refusals, for a man may endure private misery if the public praises his courage, but ridicule salts the wound and charges admission.

Yet his desire did not weaken. Rather it became an obsession, as hunger grows in a miser who owns the bakery but cannot taste the bread. He kept her portrait in a cabinet, questioned servants about her gowns, bribed pages for news of her movements, and swore to himself each night:

“She is mine. Mine by sacrament, mine by contract, mine by the seal of Parliament, mine by every law in France.”

Then he would remember the dagger and add:

“But not yet.”

Years might have passed thus, had not Fortune, who adores a fool’s last coin, placed before Feron an opportunity.

Among the courtiers orbiting Isabelle was the young Seigneur de Brissac, handsome, vain, smooth-cheeked, and so carefully dressed that he seemed born each morning from his tailor’s needle. He had long petitioned for a private audience of a nature not concerning state affairs. Isabelle, amused by his persistence and perhaps by his shoulders, at last granted him a night.

The arrangement was made through her chambermaid, Perrine, a round-faced, quick-fingered woman from Normandy, who possessed the loyalty of a cat and the financial instincts of a tax collector. Brissac was to come by a garden gate after midnight, wrapped in a dark cloak; he would be admitted without torchlight, led by a private stair, and received in a bedchamber where the curtains should be drawn. Such precautions were less for the king, who was hunting at Compiègne, than for the court, where news travels through stone walls and arrives embroidered.

Feron learned everything.

How? Through servants, who carry secrets as cooks carry onions, dropping some in every passage. He went at once to Perrine and found her purchasing ribbons near Les Halles.

“My good woman,” said he, “I desire a word.”

“A crown purchases many.”

“Twelve thousand crowns may purchase one night.”

Perrine stared.

“Whose night?”

“Brissac’s.”

“Those are dangerous words.”

“Then they merit dangerous wages.”

He drew her into the porch of a church, where bargains are often made under the eyes of saints because men suppose holy witnesses less troublesome than living ones. Feron explained that Brissac must be turned away and he himself admitted in the courtier’s place. The chamber would remain dark. Isabelle, expecting Brissac, would not know the substitution until too late; and once the marriage had been consummated, his honour would be restored, his legal position strengthened, and his heart—he used the word heart, though other organs were plainly conducting the argument—satisfied.

“Twelve thousand crowns?” said Perrine.

“In gold.”

“Half before.”

“Half after.”

“Three parts before.”

“You bargain like an advocate.”

“I am one.”

“Then I bargain like your equal.”

They agreed on eight thousand beforehand and four thousand at dawn. Feron paid the first sum that afternoon, groaning as each bag departed his hands. The coins marched from his coffer like nuns going to vespers, two by two, never to return.

“Remember,” said he, “absolute darkness.”

“My mistress dislikes being seen in treason.”

“And Brissac?”

“I shall tell him she is ill.”

“Tell him she is dead.”

“He would send flowers and seek another before supper. Illness is safer.”

Feron left jubilant, rubbing his hands and whispering that patience conquers all, though money had plainly done the conquering.

Perrine watched him go, then carried the gold directly to Isabelle.

Her mistress heard the account while seated before a mirror, having her hair combed.

“How much?”

“Twelve thousand crowns.”

“And he paid eight?”

Perrine displayed the bags.

“He values me highly,” said Isabelle.

“He values himself more. What shall we do?”

“Take the remainder.”

“How?”

“By earning it.”

Perrine crossed herself. “Madame!”

“Do not be alarmed. You shall be Madame for a night.”

The maid’s mouth opened.

“Me?”

“You.”

“With him?”

“The real one.”

“Brissac?”

“The husband.”

Perrine considered. Twelve thousand crowns can make an old advocate appear younger at a distance, and in absolute darkness the distance is whatever imagination requires.

“What if he knows me?”

“He has never known me. How should he know you?”

“My hair is black.”

“Mine is fair.”

“He may touch it.”

“Wear my nightcap.”

“There are other differences.”

“In darkness all cats claim the same ancestry. Besides, he comes to prove a legal point, not conduct an inventory.”

Perrine laughed until tears shone in her eyes.

“And you, Madame?”

“I shall wait in the next room. Near dawn, when he is exhausted, you will leave and I shall take your place. He may then see enough to certify his triumph.”

This plan pleased the maid both for its wickedness and its profit. Brissac was informed that Madame had been seized by a fever and would receive no one. He cursed physicians, weather, women, and bad luck, then went gaming. The household was dismissed early. Curtains were drawn, lamps extinguished, doors unbolted, and Perrine entered Isabelle’s bed wearing her mistress’s lace cap and perfume.

At midnight Feron arrived.

He wore Brissac’s style of cloak, though not Brissac’s shape, and carried the remaining four thousand crowns under his arm. At the garden gate Perrine’s cousin, instructed in the business, took the money and led him upstairs.

“No light,” whispered Feron.

“Madame commanded it.”

“Excellent.”

“You must not speak.”

“I understand.”

“She fears recognition by your voice.”

“Natural.”

“And whatever happens, you must leave before sunrise.”

“Certainly.”

Thus warned against every act which might uncover the trick, he believed the precautions designed entirely for its success. A man approaching his desire sees road signs backward.

The chamber was black as the conscience of a court confessor. Feron crept toward the bed, striking his knee against a stool and swallowing a cry. From behind the curtains came a whisper.

“My lord?”

He trembled with delight. Perrine had softened her country accent and spoke through the sheets.

“Madame,” he breathed.

“No names!”

He climbed into bed.

Here modesty, history, and the limits of useful description advise us to draw a curtain, though Feron himself was eager to open several. Let it suffice that our advocate, having waited years to take possession of what he called his property, proceeded like a creditor entering a seized house: inspecting nothing carefully, claiming everything loudly, and tiring himself before the furniture could protest.

Perrine bore the assault with professional resolution. Whenever he whispered some endearment, she stopped his mouth. Whenever he sought conversation, she reminded him of danger. Whenever he grew suspicious at the softness of her laughter or the coarseness of one hand, she embraced him with such energy that suspicion fled, being a timid bird when appetite claps its wings.

Feron was transported. He imagined Isabelle conquered by the supposed youth of Brissac, and this very error doubled his pleasure, for he believed himself stealing from the thief who had stolen from him. He thought of the king, the court, the judges, the street boys, and every man who had mocked him.

“Mine,” he murmured.

Perrine pinched him.

“No words.”

“Mine,” he repeated more quietly.

So passed the night, with our champion charging, retreating, negotiating, adjourned, and reopening proceedings, until at length age obtained a continuance. Toward dawn he slept on his back, breathing like damaged bellows, his mouth open and his nightcap lost somewhere near the foot of the bed.

At this point Isabelle entered from the adjoining chamber. A faint gray light showed at the shutters. She wore a linen shift and had loosened her fair hair over her shoulders. Perrine slipped from the bed, wrapped herself in a robe, and whispered:

“He has argued the case to exhaustion.”

“Did he suspect?”

“He suspected he was irresistible.”

“Then he is safe.”

The maid departed. Isabelle lay down beside Feron, arranged the sheets, and waited.

Presently the advocate stirred. He opened one eye, saw the pale curve of her shoulder, opened the other, and nearly cried aloud. There she was—Isabelle, his wife, the king’s white falcon, the jewel of France—lying where he had dreamed of seeing her since their wedding night.

He raised himself and peered at her face.

She opened her eyes languidly.

“My lord,” she murmured, as though surprised and ashamed.

Feron’s heart struck his ribs like a bailiff at a debtor’s door.

“You!” he whispered.

She turned away, drawing the sheet over her breast.

“You must go.”

“But—last night—”

“No names, you fool. If the king learns—”

Feron almost laughed. Her warning proved everything! She believed him Brissac still; therefore he had deceived her completely. He bent nearer, hoping to savour his victory, and saw upon the pillow one long fair hair.

He plucked it up and kissed it.

“A trophy,” said he.

At that moment his fingers, searching idly beneath the sheet, encountered another hair, shorter and dark. He held it toward the narrow seam of daylight.

“What is this?”

Isabelle’s blood chilled, though her face remained composed.

“What?”

“This hair. Black.”

She glanced at it.

“It is nothing.”

“Your hair is fair.”

“Must every hair in my chamber be mine?”

Feron frowned. Desire believes quickly; triumph, having more to lose, becomes a notary and asks for documents.

“Whose is it?”

Isabelle sat upright with a look of offended danger.

“If you remain to interrogate me, the servants will find you.”

He hesitated.

“Go to Perrine. Ask her whether a fair hair may darken when plucked. She knows these matters.”

This was absurd, but Feron had spent twelve thousand crowns and one strenuous night upon believing absurdities; he was not disposed to economise at the last minute.

He dressed hurriedly and crept down the stair. Perrine awaited him near the gate, rosy, composed, and considerably richer.

“My friend,” said he, displaying the hair, “answer honestly. Can a fair hair turn dark after being plucked?”

“Assuredly.”

“Assuredly?”

“Everyone knows it.”

“I did not.”

“You are a lawyer, not a barber. A hair lives by nourishment from the head. Once removed, it dies, and in dying discolours, just as a rose blackens when cut, a fish fades when taken from water, and a client changes countenance when shown your fee.”

Feron examined the hair.

“So it died?”

“In your fingers.”

“And turned black?”

“Of grief, perhaps.”

He smiled with immense relief.

“I thought as much.”

No man is so learned that a servant cannot teach him what he has paid dearly to believe.

Feron returned home in triumph. He bathed, perfumed himself, dressed in his finest robe, and appeared that morning before Parliament. He walked upright for the first time in years. When counsel smiled, he smiled more broadly. When a clerk whispered, he tapped his nose as though holding a secret capable of overturning the monarchy.

By noon he had hinted to three advocates that marriage rights, though delayed, were not extinguished. By afternoon he had informed a judge that certain fortresses yielded best to disguise. At supper he told two friends that a man must sometimes enter his own house through another’s door. Within a day Paris understood he claimed to have slept with Isabelle.

Within two days Paris knew with whom he had actually slept.

For Perrine, though loyal to gold, was not loyal to silence, which brings no interest. She told her cousin at the gate; the cousin told a cook; the cook told Brissac’s valet; the valet told Brissac; Brissac, furious at first, became delighted when he perceived that the jest injured Feron more than himself. He carried it to court, adding gestures and improvements. Isabelle denied nothing. Indeed, when asked whether the advocate had enjoyed his wife at last, she replied:

“He enjoyed twelve thousand crowns’ worth of certainty.”

The king laughed until he was obliged to sit down. The courtiers repeated the tale in galleries, guardrooms, stables, chapels, and privies. New verses appeared:

Feron pleaded through the night,  
In the dark his cause was right;  
Dawn produced a different brief—  
Black the proof and gold the grief.

Others sang that the greatest advocate in Paris could distinguish truth from falsehood in every bed but his own. Women plucked dark hairs and sent them to him in sealed packets. Someone left a dead raven at his door with a note: “This hair was once fair.” Even grave magistrates asked whether evidence changed colour after removal.

Feron denied all.

Then he threatened actions.

Then he brought actions.

Then, perceiving that each action created ten new jokes, he withdrew them.

His triumph curdled into horror. He recalled the night in every detail: the muffled voice, the strong hands, the laughter beneath the sheets, the perfume laid too thickly, the mysterious hair. What had seemed proof of conquest became testimony against him. His professional mind, returning too late from its holiday, examined the facts and found him guilty of folly beyond appeal.

He shut himself indoors. Clients deserted him, not because he had been deceived—for clients expect that of advocates—but because he had been deceived cheaply in judgment and extravagantly in money. He stared at Isabelle’s portrait until beauty itself seemed to mock him. He counted the twelve thousand crowns in imagination, each one ringing like a little bell at his funeral.

Worst of all was the phrase repeated everywhere: he had been the only man in France unable to enjoy his own wife.

Whether literally true mattered nothing. Public opinion is a judge who hears no evidence after discovering a good epigram.

Within three months Feron fell ill. Physicians bled him, purged him, wrapped him in hot cloths, prescribed broths, powders, prayers, and bills; but shame had entered deeper than medicine. He died one rainy morning, calling alternately for Isabelle, Perrine, his money, and the dark hair, which he had kept in a little legal envelope marked “Exhibit A.”

His funeral was respectable. Many lawyers attended, partly from fellowship and partly to ensure he was truly dead. The priest preached upon the vanity of worldly possession, a subject which made Master Anselme uncomfortable, since he had by then taken control of Vauvert according to one interpretation of the marriage articles.

As for Isabelle, she mourned in black velvet for the exact period required by custom, during which she looked so beautiful that mourning became fashionable. She remained with the king some years more, increased her estates, settled friends in offices, ruined enemies with smiles, and learned that royal affection, like spring weather, is warmest just before it changes.

When His Majesty’s attention turned toward a younger lady with red hair and no husband at all, Isabelle did not rage, plead, or retire to a convent. She negotiated. In exchange for withdrawing gracefully, she obtained confirmation of her lands, a pension, jewels, and protection from several lawsuits revived by Feron’s distant relatives. Thus she left the royal service like a prudent merchant closing a profitable shop before the street floods.

Not long afterward she married the young Count de Montreuil, who was handsome, cheerful, ten years her junior, and wise enough never to speak of ownership. Some said he married her for love, others for her wealth, and most for both, which is the strongest mortar in aristocratic houses. They lived agreeably, hunted, entertained poets, quarrelled only over horses, and had children who inherited their mother’s eyes and their father’s uncomplicated nose.

Many years later, at a supper in her country house, the company began telling stories of foolish husbands. One spoke of a merchant who hid beneath his own bed; another of a knight who challenged himself in disguise; a third mentioned Feron, not knowing—or pretending not to know—that the countess had been his wife.

Isabelle laughed.

“You have not the tale correctly.”

“Madame,” said a guest, “they say he at last succeeded by trickery.”

“He succeeded in paying Perrine twelve thousand crowns.”

“And you never—?”

“Never.”

“Not even that morning, when you lay beside him?”

She raised her wine cup.

“I endured his snoring for seven minutes. That was the whole of my marriage.”

The Count de Montreuil kissed her hand.

“A grievous sacrifice.”

“The worst,” said she, “was resisting the desire to wake him and show him the maid.”

“And the black hair?” asked another.

“Perrine’s.”

“They say it changed colour when plucked.”

“So it did,” replied Isabelle. “It changed from evidence into belief.”

At this the company laughed, and the countess laughed loudest.

Therefore, gentle listeners, let us draw from this history the moral it plainly refuses to supply. A father may sell a daughter, but cannot deliver her heart with the title deeds; a husband may possess a contract, yet find the property armed; a lawyer may deceive judges, witnesses, clients, and himself, but the last is commonly the costliest deception. Above all, when a chambermaid tells you that fair hair turns black from grief after being plucked, believe her if you must—but first count your crowns, look well at your bedfellow, and open the shutters.

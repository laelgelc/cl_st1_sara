In a village there once lived two men who were both named Claus. One owned four strong horses, a broad field, a red barn, and a house with glass windows; and because he possessed so much, the villagers called him Great Claus. The other owned only one thin horse and a little cottage with a roof through which the rain sometimes fell; and therefore he was called Little Claus.

Now we shall hear what happened to them.

All the week Little Claus was obliged to lend his one horse to Great Claus, who used it with his own four at the plough. In return Great Claus lent all four of his horses to Little Claus every Sunday, for on that day no one worked in the fields, and Little Claus might drive them wherever he pleased.

This seemed a fair bargain to Great Claus, because he had five horses at work for six days and lost nothing on the seventh. Little Claus was less pleased, but he was poor, and a poor man must often accept the bargain made by a rich one.

Every Sunday morning Little Claus put on his best coat, climbed into his little cart, and drove all five horses along the road. The bells on their harness jingled splendidly, the dust rose beneath their hoofs, and Little Claus cracked his whip so loudly that every one came to the doors to look.

“Gee-up, all my horses!” he cried. “Make haste, my five beautiful horses!”

But Great Claus had warned him most particularly against saying this.

“Four of them are mine,” said Great Claus, “and you must cry, ‘Gee-up, my horse and Great Claus’s four horses!’ If you call them all your own, I shall strike your horse dead.”

Little Claus promised; but promises are soon forgotten when five horses trot merrily before a man who generally owns only one.

The next Sunday he passed the church just as the people were coming out, and he wished them to think him a very great farmer.

“Gee-up, all my horses!” he cried.

Great Claus was standing by the churchyard wall.

“Mind what you say!” he shouted. “Only one is yours.”

Little Claus nodded and drove on. But presently he met the schoolmaster, and again his heart swelled with pride.

“Gee-up, all my horses!” he called.

Great Claus had followed him and heard it.

“If you say that once more,” he said, “I shall kill your horse, as surely as I stand here.”

“Oh, no; oh, no,” replied Little Claus. “I shall remember.”

Toward evening he drove through the village again. The sun shone upon the harness, the bells rang, and several children ran beside the cart. Little Claus forgot both warning and promise.

“Gee-up, all my five horses!” he cried, cracking the whip.

Great Claus came running with a heavy wooden mallet. He struck Little Claus’s horse on the forehead, and the poor beast dropped dead between the shafts.

“There!” said Great Claus. “Now you will never again call five horses your own.”

Then he took his four horses and went home.

Little Claus stood looking at the dead animal. It had been lean and old, but it was all he had possessed, and without it he could neither plough nor carry wood.

“You have left me poorer than before,” he said, “yet perhaps the hide may be worth something.”

He skinned the horse, dried the hide as well as he could, put it into a sack, and set out for the nearest town. The road was long, and before he had travelled halfway, night overtook him in a dark, gloomy forest. The wind shook the branches, an owl cried above his head, and Little Claus could scarcely see the path.

Presently he saw a light between the trees. It came from a large farmhouse, and he went to the door and knocked.

The farmers wife opened it. She was dressed in her best gown, though it was a weekday, and the smell of roast meat came from the kitchen.

“Good evening,” said Little Claus. “Will you give a poor traveller a nights lodging? I can sleep upon the floor and ask for nothing more.”

“No,” answered the woman quickly. “My husband is away, and I will admit no stranger.”

Little Claus begged again, but she shut the door in his face.

Near the house stood a haystack, and as the night was cold, he climbed upon it and crept beneath the loose hay. From there he could see through the kitchen window.

A splendid supper had been set upon the table. There was a great roast goose, a dish of fish, white bread, cheese, cakes, and two bottles of red wine. Beside the table sat the village sexton, rubbing his hands and smiling at the farmers wife.

Little Claus had not eaten since morning, and the sight made him hungrier than ever.

“So there was no room for a poor traveller,” he whispered, “but there is room enough for the sexton and a roast goose.”

The woman filled the sextons glass.

“My husband will not return until tomorrow,” she said. “We shall have the whole evening in peace.”

At that very moment the sound of wheels came along the road.

“It is my husband!” cried the woman. “He has returned before his time. Heaven preserve us!”

She seized the food and hid it in the oven, where no fire had been lighted. She pushed the wine beneath the bench, thrust the cakes into a cupboard, and commanded the sexton to creep into a large chest in the corner.

“Be silent,” she whispered, shutting down the lid. “If he finds you here, I am undone.”

The farmer entered, wet and weary.

“What a dreadful night!” he said. “I am glad to be home. Have you nothing to eat?”

“Only porridge,” said his wife. “You were not expected until tomorrow.”

Then the farmer noticed Little Claus lying upon the haystack, for his legs stuck out from beneath the hay.

“Who is that fellow?” he asked.

“A traveller,” replied the woman. “He begged for shelter, but I sent him away.”

“That was unkind,” said the farmer. “No Christian man should sleep outdoors in such weather. Come down, friend, and share our poor supper.”

Little Claus entered the kitchen and sat at the table. The farmers wife brought a bowl of cold porridge, and the farmer ate from it without complaint; but Little Claus knew that roast goose and wine were close at hand.

The horsehide in his sack had become stiff. Whenever he moved his foot against it, it gave a dry creak.

“What have you in that sack?” asked the farmer.

“Oh, it is a conjuror,” said Little Claus.

“A conjuror?”

“Yes. He knows what is hidden in every house, and if I tread upon the sack, he speaks to me.”

Little Claus pressed the hide with his heel.

Creak! creak! went the dry skin.

“What does he say?” asked the farmer, staring.

“He says that a roast goose, a dish of fish, and several good cakes are hidden in the oven.”

The farmers wife turned pale.

“Nonsense!” she cried. “The sack is lying.”

“We shall soon know,” said her husband.

He opened the oven, and there stood the goose, the fish, the white bread, and all the rest.

“Wonderful!” exclaimed the farmer. “Why, wife, you must have prepared this as a surprise.”

“Yes, certainly,” she said. “I wished to surprise you.”

They placed the food upon the table, and Little Claus ate until he could eat no more. The farmer was delighted with the conjuror and wished to hear it speak again.

Little Claus stepped upon the sack.

Creak! creak!

“What does he say now?” asked the farmer.

“He says there are two bottles of excellent wine beneath the bench.”

The wine was found, and the farmer drank freely. Soon his cheeks were red, and he began to think Little Claus’s conjuror the most useful thing in all the world.

“Can it also show spirits?” he asked. “I should dearly like to see a devil, for I have often wondered what one looks like.”

“Yes,” replied Little Claus, “but that may be dangerous.”

“I am not afraid,” said the farmer. “Show me one!”

Little Claus pressed the hide.

Creak! creak! creak!

“The conjuror says,” he whispered, “that a devil is sitting in the chest in the corner.”

The farmers wife dropped a plate.

“In that chest?” said the farmer. “Let us open it.”

“No, no!” cried Little Claus. “The sight might kill you. Devils are dreadful creatures. Yet perhaps you may look at him through the crack, if you promise not to lift the lid.”

The farmer went to the chest and peered through a little split in the wood. Inside he saw the sextons round face, grey with fear. The sexton’s eyes rolled, and his teeth chattered.

“Heaven preserve us!” whispered the farmer. “The devil looks exactly like our sexton!”

“That is how devils deceive honest people,” said Little Claus. “They take the shape of those whom we trust.”

The farmer hurried away from the chest.

“You must sell me that conjuror,” he said. “I will give you a whole bushel of money for it.”

“A bushel!” exclaimed Little Claus. “It is worth much more. Think how it finds food and wine and discovers devils.”

“Then I will fill the bushel to the top.”

Little Claus pretended to consider. At last he sighed.

“You have treated me kindly,” he said, “and so you shall have it. But you must do me one favour. I cannot carry both the money and that heavy chest. Let me take the chest away, for I have always wanted one.”

The farmers wife quickly begged her husband to agree.

“Yes, yes,” she said. “Give him the old chest. It is of no use to us.”

The farmer filled a grain measure with silver and copper coins. Little Claus tied the money securely upon his back, loaded the chest onto a small handcart, and set off before sunrise. The farmer remained behind with the horsehide, waiting for it to tell him where more wine was hidden.

When Little Claus reached the middle of a bridge over a deep river, he stopped.

“I am tired of dragging this chest,” he said loudly. “I shall throw it into the water.”

“Do not!” cried the sexton from within. “Let me out, good friend!”

“Oh, are you still there?” said Little Claus. “I had almost forgotten.”

“Open the lid. I will give you money.”

“How much?”

“A sackful.”

“That is not enough. You are a heavy devil, and I have dragged you many miles.”

“Then a bushelful! I have money hidden near the church. Spare me, and you shall have it.”

Little Claus opened the chest, and the sexton climbed out, stiff and trembling. Together they went to a hollow tree behind the churchyard, where the sexton kept his savings. He measured out a full bushel of money, and Little Claus allowed him to run away.

Thus Little Claus returned home with two bushels of money, although he had set out with nothing but the hide of a dead horse.

When Great Claus saw so many coins, his eyes became as round as plates.

“Where did you get all that?” he asked.

“I sold my horsehide,” replied Little Claus.

“How much did they pay?”

“A bushel of money, and more besides.”

Great Claus did not stop to ask why any one would pay such a price. He ran home, took his sharpest hatchet, and killed all four of his fine horses. Then he skinned them and loaded the hides upon a cart.

“My fortune is made!” he cried. “Four hides will bring four bushels, and perhaps eight.”

He went to the town and walked through every street, shouting:

“Skins, skins! Who’ll buy skins? A bushel of money for each!”

The shoemakers laughed at him. The tanners offered a few small coins, which was the proper price, but Great Claus grew angry.

“A few coins!” he roared. “Do you take me for a fool? Little Claus received a bushel for one hide, and mine are larger and better.”

“Then sell them to the man who bought his,” said a tanner.

Great Claus continued shouting until a crowd followed him. He pushed into shops, demanded bushels of silver, and called every one a cheat. At last the townspeople lost patience. The apprentices seized broom handles, the butchers brought their switches, and the women threw cabbage stalks.

“Give him the price of his skins!” they cried.

They beat Great Claus from one end of the town to the other. His coat was torn, his hat was crushed, and he was obliged to leave the four hides lying in the mud.

“This is Little Claus’s doing,” he said as he limped home. “He has killed my horses by his wicked lies, and tonight I shall kill him.”

Little Claus had guessed that Great Claus would return in a rage. Now his old grandmother lived with him, but that very day she had died peacefully in her chair. Little Claus was sorry, for she had been good to him; yet he knew Great Claus was coming, and a clever man must use what lies before him.

He laid his grandmother in his own bed, placed his nightcap upon her head, and drew the blanket up to her chin. Then he hid beneath a pile of empty sacks in the corner.

During the night Great Claus entered with his hatchet.

“You shall not cheat me again,” he whispered.

He crept to the bed and struck the figure beneath the blanket. The old grandmother did not move.

“There,” said Great Claus. “Now you are dead.”

He went home, believing he had murdered Little Claus.

In the morning Little Claus came out from his hiding place.

“My poor grandmother,” he said. “You were dead already, but Great Claus has treated you very rudely. We must see whether his wickedness can be made to pay for your burial.”

He dressed the dead woman in her best clothes and placed her upright in a cart. He tied a cord behind her so that she would not fall, put a basket upon her arm, and drove toward the town.

At noon he reached an inn. He stopped outside and called to the innkeeper.

“My grandmother is old and deaf,” he said, “but she has money and wishes for a glass of sweet wine. Pray carry it to her while I go to the market.”

The innkeeper was a hot-tempered man. He filled a glass and took it to the cart.

“Here is your wine,” he said.

The grandmother made no reply.

“Do you hear me?” he shouted.

Still she said nothing.

The innkeeper held the glass beneath her nose.

“Pay me first, old woman.”

She remained silent and stared before her.

“Are you mocking me?” cried the innkeeper. “Do you think my wine costs nothing?”

He struck her under the chin with the back of his hand. The cord broke, and the dead grandmother fell from the cart onto the road.

At that moment Little Claus returned.

“What have you done!” he cried. “You have killed my grandmother!”

“I hardly touched her,” said the innkeeper, turning pale.

“She was alive when I left her. Look! She neither breathes nor speaks. Murder! Murder!”

“Be quiet,” begged the innkeeper. “If the magistrate hears of this, I shall be hanged.”

“You struck a poor old woman because she did not hear you.”

“I did not know she was deaf.”

“That will not bring her back.”

The innkeeper looked up and down the road. No one had seen them.

“If I give you a bushel of money, will you say she died upon the journey?”

Little Claus shook his head.

“My grandmother was worth more than that.”

“A bushel filled to the rim, and I shall also give you food for the road.”

Little Claus allowed himself to be persuaded. The innkeeper fetched his money, and Little Claus drove home with the dead grandmother and another bushel of coins.

Great Claus was watching from behind his hedge.

“What is that money?” he demanded.

“I sold my grandmother,” said Little Claus. “Dead grandmothers are fetching excellent prices in town.”

“How much?”

“One bushel of money. If the grandmother is large and well dressed, she may bring even more.”

Great Claus hurried into his house, where his own grandmother sat beside the fire knitting a stocking.

“Grandmother,” he said, “I am in need of money.”

“So are we all,” replied the old woman.

Great Claus took a mallet and struck her dead. Then he dressed her in her Sunday gown, placed her upon his back, and carried her to town.

The first shop he entered belonged to an apothecary.

“What do you want?” asked the apothecary.

“I have brought my dead grandmother,” said Great Claus. “How much will you give for her?”

The apothecary stared at him.

“Give for her?”

“Yes. She is freshly killed, and I will not accept less than a bushel of money.”

“You killed her?”

“Certainly. Do you suppose she would have died at the proper time merely to oblige me?”

The apothecary rushed to the door.

“Help! Murder! Fetch the constable!”

Great Claus was terrified. He dropped his grandmother in the shop, knocked over a shelf of bottles, and fled from the town. Dogs barked after him, boys threw stones, and the constable chased him nearly to the village.

When he reached home, breathless and empty-handed, he saw Little Claus counting his money.

“You have cheated me once too often,” he cried. “I have lost my horses, my hides, my grandmother, and nearly my liberty because of you.”

“I never told you to do any of those things,” said Little Claus.

“Silence! Tomorrow you shall be drowned.”

Great Claus seized him, thrust him into a large sack, and tied the mouth with a strong rope.

“I shall carry you to the river tonight,” he said, “and there will be no trickery this time.”

The river lay several miles away, and Little Claus was heavy, for he had eaten a good supper. Great Claus carried the sack over one shoulder, but soon his back ached and sweat ran down his face.

The road passed a church, and the organ was playing within.

“I have worked hard,” said Great Claus. “I may as well rest and hear one hymn before I drown you.”

He placed the sack beside the church wall and went inside.

Little Claus lay quietly and listened. Presently he heard the bells of cattle and the slow steps of an old man. It was a cattle drover returning from market with a fine herd of cows and oxen.

“Oh, dear! Oh, dear!” cried Little Claus from the sack. “I am too young to go to heaven! I do not wish it!”

The drover stopped.

“Who is in that sack?” he asked.

“It is I,” replied Little Claus. “I am being sent straight to heaven, but I have no desire to leave this world.”

“How can a sack send you to heaven?”

“When the church service ends, a holy man will carry the sack to the river and throw it in. Then I shall rise through the water and clouds and arrive at heavens gate before morning.”

“That is wonderful,” said the drover. “I have always wished to go to heaven, for I am old, my knees pain me, and driving cattle is wearisome.”

“You may take my place if you like.”

“Will it truly send me there?”

“Certainly. But you must remain very quiet. If you cry out, the holy man may think you unworthy.”

The drover untied the sack, and Little Claus climbed out.

“Quickly,” said Little Claus. “The hymn is nearly finished.”

The old man crept into the sack, and Little Claus tied it securely. Then he took the drovers staff and drove the cattle away by a path behind the church.

Presently Great Claus came out. He lifted the sack and was surprised at its weight.

“You have grown heavier,” he said.

The old drover remembered that he must remain silent.

Great Claus carried him to the river. The night was black, and the water ran deep and rapidly between steep banks.

“You cheated me with the hide,” said Great Claus, “and with the grandmother, but you shall cheat me no more. Into the river you go!”

The drover said nothing.

Great Claus swung the sack once, twice, three times, and threw it far into the stream.

Splash!

The sack disappeared beneath the dark water.

“There,” said Great Claus. “Now Little Claus is at the bottom, and I shall live in peace.”

He walked home and slept soundly.

The next morning he went to his field, though he had no horses left to plough it. As he stood by the gate, he heard the lowing of cattle. Along the road came Little Claus, driving a splendid herd. There were red cows, black cows, white oxen, spotted calves, and a great bull with a brass ring in its nose.

“Good morning, Great Claus!” he called.

Great Claus turned as pale as flour.

“Were you not drowned last night?”

“Oh yes,” replied Little Claus. “You threw me into the deepest part of the river.”

“Then how have you returned?”

“The river people released me.”

“What river people?”

“Why, those who live beneath the water. There are whole villages down there, with church towers, houses, gardens, and meadows. The grass is greener than any grass upon earth, and the flowers shine like silver. These cattle came from the river meadows.”

Great Claus looked greedily at the herd.

“Were they given to you?”

“Yes, though I received only a small number because you threw me too near the bank. Had I fallen into the deepest place, I should have found thousands.”

“Thousands of cattle?”

“Certainly. The bottom is covered with them. Sea-cattle have broad backs, rich milk, and horns that never break. I was told to return tomorrow for another herd, but I hardly have room for these.”

Great Claus walked around the cattle and examined them. They were fat and healthy, and the bell on the leading cow was made of good bronze.

“How does one reach this underwater country?” he asked.

“One must be tied in a sack and thrown into the river. Otherwise the current carries a man away before the river people can find him.”

Great Claus considered. He had no horses, no money, and no grandmother, but if he owned a thousand sea-cattle, he would be the richest farmer in the district.

“You must take me there at once,” he said.

“I am busy,” replied Little Claus. “These cattle need feeding.”

“I shall give you half of those I bring back.”

“No. The journey is dangerous.”

“Then you may keep one hundred.”

Little Claus shook his head.

“Two hundred!”

“I do not know.”

“Five hundred!” cried Great Claus. “Only tie me in the sack and throw me into the deepest place.”

“Well,” said Little Claus, “as you ask it so earnestly, I shall do my best.”

They went to Great Claus’s barn and found the same large sack. Great Claus climbed inside, but before Little Claus tied it, he said, “We must take a heavy stone.”

“What for?” asked Great Claus.

“To make certain that you sink quickly. Yesterday I went down slowly and nearly missed the finest meadows.”

“Then choose the heaviest stone you can carry.”

Little Claus rolled a great stone into the bottom of the sack. Great Claus squeezed in beside it, though there was hardly room.

“It is uncomfortable,” he said.

“The road to riches is not always soft,” replied Little Claus.

He tied the sack with two ropes, placed it upon a cart, and drove toward the river. The cattle followed behind, their bells ringing.

As they approached the bridge, Great Claus called from the sack, “Are we nearly there?”

“Yes.”

“Remember the deepest place!”

“I shall.”

“And if you see me rising, push me under again, or I may lose the thousand cattle.”

“I shall remember that also.”

At the middle of the bridge Little Claus stopped the cart. The river roared below, swollen by rain, and white foam curled around the rocks.

“This is the place,” he said.

“Are you certain it is deep?”

“No one has ever found the bottom.”

“Excellent! Throw me in.”

Little Claus dragged the sack to the edge.

“Do not forget,” cried Great Claus. “A thousand cattle, perhaps two thousand!”

“You were always fond of having more than other people,” said Little Claus.

He tipped the sack over the edge.

Down it fell, struck the water, and vanished.

Plump!

For one moment a few bubbles rose to the surface; then the current swept them away. The heavy stone carried Great Claus to the muddy bottom, and neither the river people nor the sea-cattle came to meet him.

Little Claus stood upon the bridge and looked at the water.

“You killed my horse because I called five horses mine,” he said, “and you tried to kill me because you could not learn that another mans good fortune cannot be copied with a hatchet. Now the river may teach you, though I fear you are a poor scholar.”

Then he drove the cattle home.

With part of the money he repaired his cottage and gave his grandmother a proper burial. With the rest he bought fields, a warm stable, and a cart painted blue. He cared well for the herd, and before many years had passed he possessed more cattle than Great Claus had ever dreamed of owning.

Whenever Little Claus drove through the village, the children ran after him, and the bells rang merrily upon the harness. Yet he no longer cried that every horse and cow was his, for he had learned that boasting may cost a man dearly, even when his wit afterwards repays him.

As for the villagers, they often spoke of Great Claus and wondered how a man who had owned four horses, a broad field, and a red barn had lost everything.

“He was strong,” said one.

“He was rich,” said another.

“But he was greedy,” said a third, “and greed can put a heavier stone in a sack than any man can lift.”

And that was the truest word of all.

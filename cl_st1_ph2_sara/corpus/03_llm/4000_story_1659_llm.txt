## THE PEOPLE AGAINST LIZZIE VALE

At seven o’clock on a November evening, Amos Vale sat beside the kitchen stove in carpet slippers, with a newspaper spread before his face like a private wall.

He was a red-haired, unshaven, untidy man. His blue shirt lacked a button at the throat; his trousers bagged at the knees; one suspender had been repaired with string. On the table stood the remains of bacon, a cup holding half an inch of black coffee, and a saucer wherein his pipe rested amid a congregation of ashes.

“Pa,” said Lizzie, “can I stay in with you?”

The newspaper stirred.

Lizzie was twelve. She had a narrow face, solemn gray eyes, and two braids tied with different-colored ribbons. Her dress had descended through three cousins and arrived upon her with its hem let down and its elbows fortified. She stood by the table twisting a bit of cord around her fingers.

“Pa?”

“What?”

“Can I stay in?”

Amos lowered the paper an inch. “What for?”

“I dunno. It’s cold outdoors.”

“Then run. Runnin’ keeps you warm.”

“There’s boys by the steps. They throw things.”

“Throw back.”

“They’re bigger’n me.”

“Don’t bother me, Lizzie. I worked all day.”

There are men who labor ten hours and consider the remaining fourteen sacred to repose. Having carried bricks, driven wagons, punched tickets, or supervised the mysterious progress of a shovel, they return home crowned with the martyrdom of employment. A child may have waited all day for one word from them, but that word is an additional tax, and the exhausted citizen declines to pay it.

Amos raised his paper.

Lizzie approached the stove. “Couldn’t we play somethin’?”

“Play by yourself.”

“I don’t know nothin’ to play.”

“Then go out and learn.”

Thus did Amos Vale, without diploma, salary, or public appointment, become an instructor of youth. The street was to be his assistant teacher, the gutter its slate, the saloon its museum, and every loafer on the corner an ex-officio member of the faculty.

“Go on,” he said. “You make me nervous.”

Lizzie put on a thin coat. At the door she paused. “Will you be here when I come back?”

“Where’d I go?”

She stepped into the hallway.

The door shut, and Amos drew a comfortable breath. He filled his pipe, planted his slippers upon the stove rail, and entered the editorial columns, where the decay of the nation was traced to foreign ideas, loose morals, and the failure of parents to exercise discipline.

Outside, the East Side received the child.

The street glittered wetly beneath gas lamps. Pushcarts crouched along the curb; fire escapes clung like black skeletons to the tenements; windows exhaled steam, curses, lullabies, onions, medicine, and the immortal odor of cabbage. Boys battled over a barrel hoop. Two girls, painted far beyond their years, went by laughing. A drunk lay against a lamppost and lectured the moon.

The street was a vast House of Sin, but unlike better-managed houses it charged no admission. Its doors stood open to every child whose home had closed one.

“Hey, Lizzie!”

A boy named Kid Mullaly leaned in the doorway of Dugan’s saloon. He was fifteen, wore his cap over one eye, and possessed the magnificent assurance of a statesman who has never examined a fact.

“Come on in back,” he said. “We’re havin’ sport.”

“I ain’t allowed.”

“Who says?”

Lizzie looked up at the dark windows of her building.

“Nobody,” she said.

Kid grinned. “That’s the girl.”

Behind the saloon, among barrels smelling of beer, he gave her the bottom of a glass.

“What is it?”

“Medicine for bein’ cold.”

She tasted it, coughed, and wiped her mouth.

Kid laughed. “You’ll learn.”

And she did.

* * *

Seven years later, Liz Vale stood before a spotted mirror fastening a glass emerald at her throat.

She wore a green silk skirt purchased from a woman whose explanations concerning its former ownership had been more fluent than persuasive. Her shoes were cracked, but she had blackened the cracks. Her hat carried a bent white feather, and her hair, piled high, shone like dark copper under the gas.

On the bed behind her sat Minnie Doss, making a cat’s-cradle for Minnie’s little sister.

“Kid’s takin’ Nell Corby to the dance,” Minnie said.

Liz’s hands became still.

“Who told you?”

“Everybody knows.”

“Everybody’s a liar.”

“I seen ’em.”

Liz turned. “Where?”

“On Hester. He bought her violets.”

Liz laughed loudly. “Let him. Think I care?”

Minnie transferred the string from one hand to the other. “He done it to freeze to you.”

“He can freeze till he cracks.”

Her voice was hard, but she took a bottle from the washstand and drank. Kid Mullaly had first offered her liquor as medicine for cold. It had since been promoted to physician-in-chief, spiritual adviser, family friend, and Grand Vizier over all disorders of the heart.

The child beside Minnie held out the tangled string.

“Make the diamonds, Liz.”

“Nothin’ doin’.”

“Please.”

Liz looked at the small fingers. For an instant the hard paint of her face seemed to loosen. She sat down, took the cord, and moved it deftly from thumb to thumb.

“See? You cross here. Then you pull.”

The child smiled.

There she was: the twelve-year-old girl in the poor dress, waiting beside a stove with a game in her hands. She appeared for perhaps ten seconds. Then Minnie said, “Nell’ll be wearin’ red,” and the child vanished.

Liz flung the string aside.

“I’ll show her red.”

The Imperial Assembly Hall occupied the second floor above a pawnshop. Its proprietor had decorated it with paper garlands, three flags, and portraits of men whose connection with dancing or empire could not be discovered. A cornet, violin, and piano waged separate wars from a platform. Couples stamped upon the floor with the grave fury of citizens extinguishing a fire.

Kid Mullaly entered with Nell Corby on his arm.

He was broad-shouldered now, with a checked suit and a yellow necktie. His hair had been plastered until it possessed the architectural firmness of a roof. Nell wore red, as prophecy had announced, and looked over the room with the calm of a duchess inspecting purchased territory.

Liz saw them.

Kid saw Liz see them.

He grinned.

That grin was the little match presented by society to the powder magazine it had spent nineteen years filling.

Liz went to the refreshment table.

“Whiskey,” she said.

The man behind the bottles winked. “Lady’s drinkin’ hard tonight.”

“Lady’ll drink how she likes.”

She swallowed one glass, then another. Respectable persons will tell you that the second glass was her choice. They are correct. The first had been Kid’s, years before. The thirst had been the street’s. The ache beneath it had been manufactured at home. But the second glass—ah, that belonged wholly to Liz, and Justice, delighted to find some portion of the case simple, sharpened her pencil.

Kid danced with Nell. He swung her past Liz once, twice, three times.

“Enjoyin’ yourself?” he called.

“For fair,” Liz answered.

On the fourth turn she stepped before them.

“Kid, I want a word.”

“I’m busy.”

“You’re my feller.”

“Was. Maybe.”

Nell laughed.

Liz struck her.

The music broke apart. Nell shrieked and clawed at Liz’s hat. Men gathered with the instant enthusiasm of a crowd discovering violence for which it cannot be blamed. Kid seized Liz by the shoulders.

“Cut it out!”

“You brought her to spite me!”

“What if I did?”

“You said we was gettin’ married.”

Kid pushed her away. “I ain’t marryin’ no soak.”

This judgment, delivered by the gentleman who had first put a glass into her childish hand, was admired for its moral elevation.

Liz stared at him. Something in her face frightened Nell, who retreated behind a large man with a watch chain.

“You don’t mean it,” Liz said.

Kid shrugged. “I’m through.”

She reached into her skirt.

The knife flashed once.

Kid looked surprised. He placed a hand against his chest, as though feeling for an absent button. Then he fell.

For one second the hall was silent.

Afterward everyone shouted.

“She killed him!”

“Grab her!”

“Stop that girl!”

Liz ran.

She flew down the stairs, losing one shoe. She struck the pavement and turned east. Behind her poured the dance hall: men in derbies, women lifting their skirts, boys who had not seen the stabbing but intended to remember it most accurately. A policeman joined the chase, swinging his club. From saloons, doorways, lunch wagons, and lodging houses came reinforcements.

The mob grew because a mob, like a charitable subscription, permits each contributor to feel virtuous at small personal expense.

“She murdered Kid!”

“Lynch her!”

“Head her off!”

Liz ran through alleys, over gutters, beneath the elevated tracks. Her breath tore at her throat. Blood from her bare foot marked the stones. Windows flew open above her; heads appeared; questions descended; answers ascended improved.

At the river she stopped.

The East River lay black under the winter sky, carrying scraps of light upon its back. Tugs moaned. The farther shore glittered with a thousand windows, each perhaps containing a stove, a chair, a newspaper, a father.

The mob rounded the corner.

“There she is!”

Liz looked behind her and saw humanity coming to correct its mistake.

She looked ahead.

The river opened its arms.

When no mother had called her home, when no father had kept a lamp for her, when lover, dance hall, street, police, and righteous public had completed their several duties, the river undertook the neglected office.

Liz jumped.

The black water closed over the green silk skirt, the glass emerald, the cracked shoe that remained, the knife, the whiskey, the anger, the cat’s-cradle, and the child.

The mob stopped at the edge.

A man said, “Well, that’s done.”

The policeman wiped his forehead.

Then they went back to the dance.

* * *

Liz awoke in a large courtroom where the walls appeared to be made of pale morning.

A clerk with silver spectacles consulted a ledger.

“Elizabeth Vale,” he called. “Murder and self-destruction. Case number eighty-seven million, four hundred and—never mind. Step forward.”

Liz stood before the bench. She wore no hat, and her hair hung wetly down her back.

The Judge looked tired. He had perhaps been hearing human explanations since Cain discovered the passive voice.

“How do you plead?” asked the clerk.

“I stabbed him,” Liz said.

“And afterward?”

“I jumped.”

“So noted. Officer, where are the witnesses?”

A celestial detective entered, leading Kid Mullaly, Nell Corby, the saloonkeeper, two policemen, three reformers, a clergyman, the dance-hall proprietor, and forty-seven members of the mob.

“These all claim an interest, Your Honor.”

“Of course they do,” said the Judge. “They always arrive after the event.”

Kid pointed at Liz. “She done me.”

“Who taught her to drink?”

Kid studied the floor. “Just foolin’, the first time.”

The saloonkeeper cleared his throat. “I merely supplied a demand.”

The reformers announced that they had issued pamphlets.

The clergyman said the district had not attended worship in satisfactory numbers.

The police declared they could not be everywhere.

The mob explained that it had pursued Liz solely from devotion to law.

The Judge listened.

“Clerk,” he said, “discharge the prisoner.”

A murmur swept the court.

“But murder!” cried the reformers.

“And suicide!” added the clergyman, not wishing his department overlooked.

“Wrong defendant,” said the Judge. “Detective, bring in the party responsible.”

The detective departed.

Presently he returned with a red-haired, unshaven, untidy man in carpet slippers. The man carried a newspaper and smelled faintly of bacon, coffee, and pipe smoke.

Amos Vale blinked at the court.

“What’s this?” he demanded. “I ain’t done nothin’.”

The Judge leaned forward.

“Precisely.”

Amos looked at Liz. “Her? She was always wild.”

“At twelve?”

“She wouldn’t mind.”

“She asked to remain with you.”

“I was tired.”

“She asked you to play with her.”

“I worked all day.”

“You sent her into the street.”

“I had to have some rest.”

The Judge lifted the newspaper from Amos’s hand. On its front page was an editorial concerning the alarming wickedness of young women.

“Amos Vale,” he said, “you are charged with preferring this paper to a child; with closing your door and opening the city’s; with appointing the gutter guardian, the saloon tutor, and the mob executioner; with doing nothing at the exact hour when a little something was required.”

Amos scratched his unshaven chin.

“You can’t jail a man for readin’ the news.”

“Not in your courts,” said the Judge.

Liz stared at her father. Her face was neither hard nor painted now. She was twelve again, and a cord lay between her fingers.

“Pa,” she said softly, “I only wanted somebody to play with.”

Amos opened his mouth.

No answer came.

At this point I awoke, for the whole proceeding was plainly absurd. Earthly courts do not arrest causes. They arrest results. They do not indict the locked door, the tired selfishness, the neglected hour, the first glass, the approving crowd, or the respectable citizen behind his newspaper. Such defendants have excellent connections and are seldom at home when the warrant arrives.

So the dream was silly.

Nevertheless, somewhere near the river, a red-haired, unshaven, untidy man sat in carpet slippers, reading with indignation about the wickedness of a girl who had drowned.

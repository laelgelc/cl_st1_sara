Lucy had been a cook at the vicarage when Willy Horsepool first saw her carrying a basin of white cloths across the yard. She was a fair, still girl, with smooth hair and quiet grey eyes, and she walked as though nothing must be spilled. Willy, coming from the pit at noon, black to the rims of his eyelids, stopped by the wall and watched her.

“I’m havin’ that lass,” he told his mate.

His mate laughed, because Willy spoke as if he had picked out a new pair of boots.

Nevertheless, he had her. He was twenty-four, short and strongly made, with a round, close-cropped head that seemed driven forward upon his thick neck. His body was compact and hard. Even on Sundays, in his blue suit, there was something of the pit pony in him: a contained, impatient strength, ready to push. Lucy was at first frightened by the pressure of his courtship. He did not ask so much as announce. Yet he was clean in his intentions, earned good money, and looked at her with an admiration which warmed her against herself.

After they were married they took a little end house in Colliers Row. Lucy made the front room white and precise. The lace curtains were washed every fortnight; the brass fender shone; the china dogs upon the mantelpiece stared across at one another without a grain of dust between them. Willy was enormously proud.

“Tha’rt set up like a lady,” he would say, standing in the doorway with his dinner tin under his arm. “There’s none on ’em got a house like our’n.”

But he brought the mine in with him. It came in his boots, in the seams of his clothing, beneath his nails and behind his ears. It came as a wet, used-up smell when he returned from the afternoon shift, his face black and his lips astonishingly red. He would stamp upon the flags and shout:

“Wesh us, Luce! I’m fair clommaxed.”

To be clommaxed was to be exhausted past complaint, though the men generally complained while saying it.

Lucy set the zinc bath before the fire and poured the steaming water. Willy stripped without modesty, flinging each black garment where she must pick it up. His flesh, emerging in pale bands from the grime, had a muscular fullness that disturbed and pleased her. He splashed, snorted, and shook his head like a dog. Sometimes, catching her watching, he laughed.

“Tha got a good ’un, tha has.”

Then he drew her against his damp chest, though she protested about the water on her apron.

He was lordly in small things. His tea must be strong, his bacon crisp, his pit clothes warmed before the early shift. If the snap in his tin was not to his liking, he mentioned it. Yet he gave her his unopened wage packet and never spent beyond a few pence on beer. On Saturday nights he sat in stockinged feet beside the polished fender while Lucy read the newspaper aloud, and his face took on a solemn importance at the sound of Parliament, coal owners, wages, and foreign wars. He understood little beyond the price of coal and the strength of a man’s back, but he listened as though the nation depended upon his judgment.

They were happy. Lucy knew it in the orderly progress of days: the alarm in darkness, the fire newly roused, the heavy boots going away, the dinner at two, the washing hung like surrendered flags in the yard. She knew it also in Willy’s boastfulness. He told the men she could cook anything, that she never ran into debt, that she was “as clean as a pin.” His pride laid claim to her, but it also sheltered her.

Then came the strike.

The pit wheels stopped, and the silence over the village was at first almost festive. Men stood in groups where they had formerly hurried. They leaned against walls, smoked, argued, and found leisure in one another’s faces. The union money was small; then smaller. Lucy learned to make a stew from a bone and two onions. Her housekeeping purse became flat as an empty glove. Willy grew restless, but he did not blame her.

“Dunna fret,” he said. “We’ll best ’em yet.”

They did not best anybody. After many weeks the men went back, thinner and louder, and with debts tied to them like cans to a dog. Willy worked every shift offered. He took overtime, came home grey beneath the black, ate with his eyes half shut, and fell asleep before the fire. Slowly the grocer’s book was reduced. Slowly Lucy breathed again.

It was only six weeks after the last debt had been paid that Willy was hurt.

A fall had occurred in the east heading. No one was killed. Willy walked out with help, his face bloodless under the coal, one hand pressed to his side. The doctor found no broken bone and spoke of bruising. For three days Willy lay upstairs; then he rose, declaring he would go back.

He reached the bottom of the stairs and folded suddenly over the banister.

“The peen,” he gasped. “Eh, Luce—the peen!”

It was not fixed in one place. At times it burned below his ribs; at times it drove through his back or gripped his groin. One doctor called it muscular, another nervous. A specialist in town tapped Willy’s knees, pressed his belly, and asked questions until Willy reddened with humiliation.

“There’s summat there,” he insisted. “I know my own inside.”

He was put upon compensation, a portion of his wage, paid while his injury prevented work. Thus began his captivity.

Through autumn and winter he sat by the window, watching other men go to the pit. The strong body softened slightly, yet seemed no less charged. His head remained thrust forward. He listened for the morning clatter of boots and the distant winding gear, and shame moved over him in waves.

Lucy tended him patiently. She carried his meals, rubbed the painful places, washed the sweat from him after bad nights. But the complaint had no visible face. A wound she could have cleaned; a broken limb she could have set upon cushions. This hidden thing entered every hour and made her wary.

“Is it bad now?” she would ask.

“How should it be?” he snapped. “Tha thinks I’m makin’ it up.”

“I never said so.”

“Tha looks it.”

Afterward he might call her to him and lay his round head against her breast.

“Dunna take notice, duck. I’m drove wild wi’ it.”

In the spring another national strike began. Once more the wheels stopped. Once more the street filled with men, but now they were excited rather than defeated. They had whistles, union badges, bicycles, and time. Voices travelled through the thin walls from morning until night. Traction engines carrying goods to the town shook the crockery upon Lucy’s dresser, and crowds ran after them shouting at the drivers.

Willy could not bear it. When men had been working, he had envied them secretly; now that they were idle, they appeared to be enjoying a liberty denied to him. They marched, held meetings, played quoits, and crowded the public houses. He remained in his chair with a rug over his knees.

On a bright Saturday, a group gathered beneath the Horsepools’ window.

“Kick-off’s at three!”

“We’ll walk it in an hour.”

“Nay, tha’ll want two, wi’ them legs.”

There was laughter, the scuffle of boots, someone striking up a song. They were going across the fields to see the cup match at Staveley.

Willy lifted his head. For two nights he had scarcely slept. His skin was yellowish and tight around his mouth.

“Fetch my boots,” he said.

Lucy, cutting bread at the table, did not turn.

“What for?”

“I’m goin’ wi’ ’em.”

“You can’t walk to the corner.”

“Fetch ’em.”

She put down the knife. “Willy, be sensible.”

His eyes came upon her with a startled brightness.

“Tha heard me.”

“You were crying with the pain half an hour ago.”

“It’s gone.”

“It hasn’t.”

He pushed the rug from his knees and stood. At once his right hand went to his side, but he smiled fiercely, as though he had caught her in a lie.

“See? I’m all reight.”

Outside, a man shouted: “Are we off, then?”

“Ay! Come on!”

Willy lurched toward the peg where his coat hung. Lucy moved between him and the door.

“No.”

The word was quiet. Its quietness offended him more than a shout.

“Shift.”

“You’ll get halfway and drop. What will they do with you then?”

“I said shift, woman.”

She put her back against the door. For an instant they regarded one another. Willy’s nostrils widened. The noise outside swelled, boots passing, men calling farewells. He could hear the life of the street moving away from him.

“Tha wants to keep me in,” he said.

“I want you not to harm yourself.”

“Liar.”

His face altered. The lips drew back a little, and Lucy saw, with a coldness in her stomach, that he did not know her. He knew only an obstacle shaped like his wife.

“Willy—”

“Tha’s done it.”

“Done what?”

“This.” He struck his side. “This peen. Tha’s put it in me.”

She stared at him.

“I niver had it afore I had thee.”

“That isn’t true.”

“It is! It is!” His voice rose shrilly. “Tha poisons me. Tha sits there, watchin’, watchin’—”

He seized her wrist. His fingers, though he had been ill so long, closed with the old pit strength. She felt the bones press together.

“Let go.”

“I’ll kill thee.”

He said it almost conversationally. Then, hearing the words himself, he became excited by them.

“I’ll kill thee! Kill her—that’s what I’ll do. Kill her!”

Lucy pulled back. The bread knife lay upon the table, bright in the sunlight. She saw his glance strike it and pass. With her free hand she reached behind her for the latch, but he dragged her forward.

“The peen!” he cried. “Tha knows! Tha knows where it is! Take it out or I’ll kill thee!”

His breath was hot and sour. His compact body trembled against hers like a machine running too fast. For one second she was not his wife but a small fair creature held by a suffering animal.

Then the door shook beneath her back.

“Lucy? What’s up?”

It was Ethel Brough from next door.

“Come in!” Lucy called.

Willy clapped a hand over her mouth. She tasted coal ingrained in his palm, though he had not been underground for months.

“I’ll do thee in,” he whispered. “Both on yer.”

Ethel tried the latch.

“Open this door!”

Willy turned his head. Lucy wrenched free and lifted the latch. Ethel came in broadside, a stout woman in a brown apron, smelling of soap and fried onions.

“What’s all this?”

“He wants to go to Staveley,” Lucy said. Her voice sounded absurdly calm.

“I am goin’!” Willy shouted. “And she’s stoppin’ me. She’s killin’ me wi’ peen, Ethel. I’ll kill her first.”

Ethel did not retreat. She had helped bring six children into the world, buried two, and once pulled her husband from beneath a runaway cart. Madness, if this was madness, was only another domestic emergency.

“Now then, Willy Horsepool. Sit thee down.”

“I’ll kill her!”

“No, tha won’t.”

“I will!”

“Tha’ll sit down, or I’ll fetch Bill and have thee held.”

At the name of Bill, a hewer larger than Willy, his certainty faltered. He looked from Ethel to Lucy, and then toward the window. The football party had gone. The street was nearly empty.

A spasm crossed him. He bent double with a groan.

“The peen—the peen—oh, God, it’s got me!”

Ethel caught one arm and Lucy the other. Together they lowered him into the chair. He writhed, his boots beating the fender. Lucy knelt, though terror still moved coldly along her arms, and loosened his collar.

“Where is it now?”

“Everywhere. I’m full on it. Luce, dunna let it—”

“I’m here.”

“Dunna let ’em take me.”

“No one’s taking you.”

His eyes rolled toward her. Slowly recognition returned, first as bewilderment, then as horror. He saw the red marks upon her wrist. He saw Ethel standing guard by the table, one hand deliberately covering the bread knife.

“What’ve I done?”

Neither woman answered.

“Luce?”

“You were bad,” she said.

“Did I—did I say owt?”

Ethel’s expression softened in spite of herself. “Tha said plenty.”

He looked again at Lucy’s wrist.

“I hurt thee.”

“It’ll pass.”

“I threatened thee.”

“You didn’t know.”

He began to cry. It was not the quiet weeping of a sick man but the abandoned sobbing of a child, his bullet head bowed, his hands clutching his hair. The sound appalled Lucy more than his threats. She put her arms around him. He pressed his face into her clean apron and soaked it.

“I’d niver hurt thee,” he said. “Not thee. Tha knows I wouldn’t. I’m a good husband, Luce. Aren’t I? Tell her I’m a good husband.”

“You are,” Lucy murmured.

Ethel turned away and wiped her own eyes with the corner of her apron.

For some time the three remained in the small bright room, crying without speaking. Outside, a boy bowled an iron hoop along the pavement. Farther off, the strike crowd gave a cheer. The sound came thinly over the roofs, belonging to another world.

At last Willy slept, exhausted, his head against Lucy’s shoulder. Ethel covered him with the rug.

“Doctor ought to know,” she whispered.

Lucy looked quickly at her.

“Must he?”

“He’s bad, duck.”

“It was the pain.”

“Ay.”

“And no sleep.”

“Ay, happen.”

Lucy watched Willy’s face. In sleep it was slack and young, the damp lashes lying black upon his cheeks. One hand remained curled in her skirt.

“If they say it’s his mind,” she whispered, “they’ll say it isn’t the accident.”

Ethel said nothing.

“They’ll stop the compensation. They’re only waiting for reason. Doctor, company, all of ’em. If they hear he’s gone queer—”

“I shan’t tell.”

“But the street hears everything.”

As if in answer, a curtain moved in the opposite house. Lucy saw the pale oval of a neighbour’s face withdraw.

She tightened her arm around Willy. The room was still clean: the brass bright, the hearth swept, the bread neatly cut upon the table. Only the overturned chair and the red band around her wrist showed that anything had happened.

Presently Willy stirred and clung to her in his sleep.

Lucy sat without moving, listening to the idle village beyond the thin walls. There was coal enough beneath them to darken every house in England, and yet no fire could be made from it that day. She looked at her husband, at the strong hands made useless, at the mouth that had kissed and threatened her, and wondered how long love could be expected to keep them when wages, work, health, and even a man’s own mind had all slipped loose.

Above the mantelpiece the clock ticked loudly.

“If they stop it,” she said, scarcely making a sound, “what are we to live on?”

The meadow had the innocent colour of spring.

Long grass leaned beneath the warm wind, recovered itself, and leaned again, sending silver ripples toward a farmhouse that had been broken open by artillery. Half its roof sagged into the rooms. A chimney stood alone at one end, ceremonious and absurd, like a black-hatted mourner who had forgotten the funeral. Beside the house, beneath two ragged willows, there was a well.

Across this pleasant interval the guns disputed.

From the low ridge where the infantry crouched, a battery threw yellow flame over their heads. The reports struck the men in the back and seemed to drive their hearts forward against their ribs. Farther away, beyond a strip of woods, enemy cannon replied with grave red winks. Shells came hunting over the meadow. They howled, paused in that high blue cathedral, and descended with iron judgment. The green field sprang up in brown columns. Earth, roots, stones and fragments of innocent flowers whirled together in brief, filthy fountains.

The regiment lay behind a shallow line of earth. It had been commanded to hold this ridge, and so it held it with elbows, knees, curses and the blind fidelity of a dog chained near a burning barn.

Men fired when they were told. They loaded when no one told them. They peered over the dirt and saw smoke moving in vast sheets among the trees. At times a dark enemy line appeared, delicate as stitching along a distant slope; then rifles crackled, the stitching vanished, and every man declared that he had seen thousands.

Fred Collins sat with his back against the breastwork and licked his lips.

“I’m dyin’ of thirst,” he said.

No one answered. A shell struck the meadow, and its crimson belly opened. Black smoke curled upward, spreading with lazy majesty.

Collins watched the smoke. “I tell yeh, I’m dyin’ of thirst.”

A soldier named Briggs, whose face was the colour of flour except where powder had made black commas on it, bent over his rifle.

“Then die quiet,” he said. “Some of us is tryin’ t’ enjoy the battle.”

The men nearby laughed. Their laughter was thin and quick. It ran along the ditch like mice.

Collins wiped his mouth with a dirty sleeve. His tongue seemed covered with wool. For an hour he had been thinking of water in various forms: water in a tin cup, water falling from a pump, water shining in a horse trough, water running cold over white stones. The thought of it had become tyrannical. He could feel the shape of a swallow in his throat, but there was nothing to swallow.

“There’s a well over by that house,” he said.

Briggs raised his head. “Is there, now?”

“I seen it when we come up. Right there by them willows.”

A little corporal squinted across the meadow. “That well belongs t’ the rebs at present.”

“It don’t belong to nobody.”

“Go tell the shells.”

Another explosion planted a giant brown hand in the grass. Clods pattered upon the roofless house. One willow shook its pale leaves as if protesting the bad manners of heaven.

Collins stared at the well. In the violence of the field it had acquired a supernatural clearness. He could see the round stone lip, the crooked frame above it, even the bucket hanging from a pole. It seemed near enough for a boy to throw a ball against, yet the space between was crowded by invisible knives.

“There’s water there,” he persisted.

“Sure,” said Briggs. “An’ lemonade in Richmond.”

“I could git it.”

This sentence made a small silence. The men turned toward him with that alert pleasure which soldiers feel when one of their number approaches folly.

The corporal grinned. “Yeh could?”

“I didn’t say I was goin’.”

“Course not. Fred’s merely discussin’ geography.”

A young private with a bandage tied around his head leaned forward. “Go on, Collins. Bring us a drink. I’ll lend yeh my canteen.”

“Mine, too,” said Briggs. “Seein’ as how it’s such a nice walk.”

Canteens began to rattle. The men laughed again, more loudly. They called to soldiers farther down the line, announcing that Collins had discovered a hotel across the meadow and would shortly serve refreshments. Each shout enlarged the boast he had not intended to make. He tried to retreat from it, but words crowded behind him like troops closing a road.

“I only said there was a well.”

“An’ yeh could git to it.”

“I said maybe.”

“Naw. Yeh said yeh could.”

Collins felt a heat rise beneath the grime of his face. He looked from one companion to another and saw no mercy. They were not cruel; that made it worse. Their pale, strained faces had found amusement, and they intended to keep it. Death was firing from two ridges, but for the moment Collins’s embarrassment was the regiment’s liveliest event.

“I’ll go,” he said suddenly.

The little corporal ceased smiling. “Aw, don’t be a fool.”

“I said I’ll go.”

“You’ll git split in two.”

“Mebbe. Gimme the canteens.”

The men studied him. The joke had stepped beyond them and now stood upright with a loaded rifle. Briggs muttered that nobody had asked him to do anything. The bandaged private began to untie his canteen, then held it uncertainly.

Collins snatched it. “C’mon. Who wants water?”

They passed him six canteens, though several men advised him to sit down and forget the matter. He looped the straps across his chest. The vessels bumped and tinkled against his body with a cheerful domestic sound. It was the sound of spoons in a kitchen drawer.

He went toward the captain.

The captain crouched behind the earthwork with one hand pressed to his cheek. A splinter had laid open the skin beneath his eye, and blood ran through his beard in a narrow, dignified stream. He was studying the opposite woods as though they had insulted him personally.

“Captain,” said Collins.

“What?”

“I’d like permission to go over to that well.”

The captain turned. “What well?”

“By the house. The boys are out of water.”

For a moment the officer’s face expressed nothing. Then he looked at the farmhouse and back at Collins.

“You want to cross that field?”

“Yes, sir.”

“For water?”

“Yes, sir.”

A rifle volley rolled from the enemy line. The sound was like a long board being torn apart. Bullets flicked through the grass and snapped in the air above the ridge.

The captain gave a short laugh. “Ask the colonel. If he lets you, I won’t stop you.”

The colonel stood twenty yards away behind a shattered wagon. He was a thick man with white trousers absurdly clean below his dark coat. He shouted orders at an aide, at the battery, at the enemy and occasionally at God. Collins waited until the colonel noticed him.

“Well? Well? What is it?”

Collins repeated his request.

The colonel peered toward the well. A gun behind them discharged, and its smoke rolled over the ridge, turning all men into ghosts.

“Water?” the colonel said.

“Yes, sir.”

“Do you think you can make it?”

Collins wished then that the colonel would forbid him. He wanted a powerful voice to call him sensible, to release him from the jaws of his own statement. He imagined himself returning to the company with a shrug: Colonel says no. Nothing I can do. He waited eagerly for this rescue.

The colonel rubbed his chin. “Go ahead, if you’ve got the stomach.”

Collins saluted.

He had been condemned by permission.

When he returned to the breastwork, the news had spread. Faces appeared along the ridge. Men from another company asked who was going. An artilleryman stood upon an ammunition chest for a better view until a sergeant kicked him down. Even the wounded seemed interested. One man, sitting with his hand wrapped in a red-soaked cloth, raised his eyes and smiled.

Collins climbed over the works.

“Bring it cold, Fred!”

“Don’t spill none!”

“Tell the landlord we’ll be over after supper!”

He stood a moment on the outer slope, adjusting the canteens. Before him the meadow extended in its impossible beauty. The grass nodded. Buttercups made small yellow stars near his boots. Above, the sky was blue, spacious and untouched, as though the battle were occurring beneath a painted ceiling that could not hear it.

Then a shell screamed.

Collins ran.

At once the canteens leaped and hammered against his chest. Their straps cut his neck. He bent forward, arms pumping, boots crushing the grass. He had meant to run steadily, with the grave economy of a man who knew danger and despised it. Instead he sprinted like a thief with dogs behind him.

A shell struck to his left. The ground slapped him with heat. Brown dirt rushed across his eyes. For three strides he could see nothing, and in those blinded seconds he felt his body being torn into many pieces. When the air cleared, he discovered himself whole.

This surprised him.

He ran harder.

The battlefield seemed suddenly to have noticed him. Until now the great guns had spoken to armies, ridges, batteries and flags. Now every roar named Collins. The shells curved toward him with terrible intelligence. Bullets whispered at his ears. One clipped a stalk in front of him, and the severed green head turned slowly as it fell.

He wondered what the regiment saw.

Perhaps from the ridge he looked heroic. This idea came to him with an almost comic bitterness. They would see a lone figure crossing the fire-swept meadow, a man carrying water for his thirsty comrades. They would not see the panic beating inside his coat. They would not know he wished to throw away the canteens, flatten himself in the grass and claw a burrow into the earth.

A hero! The word strutted before him wearing gold braid.

Collins had known himself for twenty-two years, and this knowledge made the word ridiculous. Heroes were men whose souls were white and steady, who gave away their last coins, spoke gently to mothers and met death with calm eyes. Collins owed money to a storekeeper in his town. He had once cheated a cousin in the trade of a watch. Before leaving home he had shouted at his mother because she cried over his socks. He remembered her standing at the gate, small and grey, while he pretended not to look back.

Could a man with such accounts against him become noble merely by running through a field?

No. There had been an error.

He was an impostor trespassing among fine actions, and at any instant some shell would expose him.

The farmhouse grew larger. Its broken windows looked down like empty sockets. A dead horse lay near the doorway, its belly enormously swollen, its legs stiff and delicate. Flies rose from it in a blue cloud when Collins passed. The smell struck him—sweet, hot, corrupt—and for a moment overcame even the sulphur of the guns.

He reached the shelter of the house and fell against a wall.

The bricks trembled. Dust sifted from the mortar onto his hair. He opened his mouth and dragged at the air. His heart had become a savage creature trying to escape through his throat.

Still alive.

He looked back.

The ridge was far away now, a brown crease occupied by tiny dark heads. Smoke wandered between. He could not tell whether the men were watching him. The whole regiment appeared smaller than his hand.

A shell burst above the meadow. Its smoke formed a black flower.

Collins turned to the well.

The stone rim was cool where the shade touched it. A wooden cover hung crookedly from one hinge. He pulled it aside and looked down. Far below, a circle of water held a trembling patch of sky. The sight of it gave him joy so sharp that he nearly laughed.

He untied a canteen and lowered it by its strap.

The opening was narrow. The canteen struck the stones, spun, floated and filled with insulting slowness. Bubbles rose one at a time. Collins jerked it up half full, spilling much of it against the wall.

“Come on,” he snarled at the well.

He tried a second. It swung beyond his reach, struck the side and almost slipped free. Shells continued to pass overhead. One exploded behind the house, throwing boards from the roof. A plank fell into the yard and broke with a dry crack.

The well remained cool and deliberate. Its water had waited under the earth through years of rain, births, harvests and quarrels; it could not understand why Fred Collins wished it to hurry.

He saw the old bucket.

It lay on its side among weeds, grey with age, one hoop loose and its handle orange with rust. He seized it, hooked it to the pole and plunged it downward. The rope hissed over the wheel. There was a distant splash.

“Now, yeh old devil.”

The bucket came up heavy and shining. Water spilled over its edge and struck the stones in silver coins. Collins tore one canteen from his shoulder, drank two great swallows, then another. The water tasted of iron, leaves and heaven. It ran down his chin, streaking the dust.

He filled the canteens rapidly, but the guns seemed to draw closer. Their voices crowded the ruined rooms. The farmhouse shook under successive blows. Plaster slid from an inner wall, revealing faded blue paper patterned with roses. On the floor beneath it stood a child’s wooden chair.

Collins stared at the chair. The little object, waiting among wreckage, frightened him more than the dead horse.

He filled the final canteen, then looked at the bucket. The men would want more than six canteens. They would cheer the bucket. He imagined carrying it back full, imagined the shining water and the astonished faces.

He dipped it once more.

“Damn fool,” he told himself.

He lifted the bucket and ran from the yard.

The return seemed instantly worse.

Before, the house had been a destination, and a destination possesses the power of a promise. Now there was only the ridge, distant behind smoke, while the bucket dragged at his arm and sloshed against his leg. Each leap of water over the rim appeared criminal. He tried to run smoothly, but the earth bucked beneath explosions.

The battle had swollen while he was at the well. The two armies seemed no longer composed of men. They were enormous animals locked behind the woods, tearing at each other with teeth of flame. The batteries bellowed like iron bulls. Smoke pushed over the land in dirty tides, and through it flags rose and dipped like coloured fins above a mad sea.

Collins dashed through this argument of monsters carrying a bucket.

Water flew upon his trousers. The canteens clattered. His breath came in dry cries. He fixed his eyes on the ridge and refused to look at the shell bursts. If he did not see death, perhaps death would be unable to see him.

Then he heard a voice.

“Water!”

It came from his right, thin but distinct.

Collins turned his head. Near a wrecked artillery limber lay three horses tangled in harness. One kicked weakly, making the chains ring. Beyond them, an officer reclined against a wheel. His uniform was blue-black with dust. One hand pressed his side. Beneath it the cloth had become a broad, wet purple.

“Water,” the officer said.

Collins swerved away.

“I can’t!” he shouted.

The officer’s eyes followed him. They were not accusing eyes. That was intolerable. They merely watched, exhausted and strangely polite, as if Collins were a passer-by on a town road who had failed to hear a greeting.

“Just a drink.”

A shell passed so low that its wind struck Collins’s cap crooked. He ran. The ridge drew him like a rope tied around his chest. Men were waving there. He could hear distant shouts. Safety opened its arms.

Behind him the officer coughed.

Collins continued for perhaps twenty yards.

In that distance his mind became a courtroom crowded with noisy witnesses. One declared that the officer was dying and water would not save him. Another said the bucket belonged to the regiment. Another screamed that to stop was death. A fourth, quiet and hateful, reminded him of his mother at the gate.

He had crossed the meadow for a jeer. He had stood at the well for pride. If he now left the man, what was the whole adventure? A circus trick. A mad dog’s dash.

He cursed.

“But Collins turned.”

The movement astonished him even as he made it. His legs had chosen before his courage could object. He ran back toward the shattered limber, crouching as though the sky had lowered.

The artillery officer smiled faintly.

“Knew you would,” he whispered.

“Don’t talk,” said Collins angrily. “Here.”

He set down the bucket. Water rocked near the brim, reflecting for a second the bright sky and the officer’s grey face. Collins lifted it with both hands and tilted it.

The wounded man drank badly. Most of the water ran over his mouth and into his collar. His teeth struck the bucket’s edge. He clutched at the handle, and Collins saw that his fingers were already cold-looking, pale at the nails.

“That’s enough,” Collins said. “Yeh’ll spill it.”

The officer tried to laugh. The sound was a bubble in his throat.

“Good water.”

A blast shook the limber. One dead horse moved as though suddenly alive, rolled by the force of the explosion, and its harness bells gave a merry jingle.

Collins dropped the bucket. For an instant he was on his knees, arms over his head. Dirt rained down. Something struck his shoulder and bounced away.

When he looked up, the officer had sagged against the wheel.

“Hey,” Collins said.

The man’s eyes were open, but they no longer watched anything in the meadow.

Collins seized the bucket. Less than half the water remained.

He ran.

There were no thoughts now. His mind had become white noise. His feet struck the ground, his lungs burned, and the bucket swung from one stiff hand. Rifle shots cracked around him. He passed a crater whose raw earth smoked like fresh meat. He leaped over a fallen knapsack, saw a family photograph lying face upward in the grass, and lost it behind him.

The ridge approached. Faces separated from the brown line—wide mouths, waving arms, caps lifted into the air.

“Here he comes!”

“Collins!”

“Git down, yeh fool!”

A bullet struck the bucket with a bright metallic knock. Collins felt the handle jerk, but the ball had only dented the side. He stared at the small new hollow as if it were an eye.

Then he reached the earthwork.

Hands caught his sleeves and hauled him over. He fell among boots, rifles and shouting men. The bucket landed upright. Water leaped, but some remained.

For a few seconds Collins lay on his back, gazing at the serene blue sky. His comrades bent above him in a circle, their faces dark against the light.

“Fred, yeh old devil!”

“Did yeh git hit?”

“Look at him! Ain’t even scratched!”

“Where’s my canteen?”

Briggs dragged him upright and struck him joyfully between the shoulders. The little corporal was grinning, though tears had made two clean tracks through the dirt on his cheeks.

“I said you’d git split in two,” the corporal cried. “I always knowed you was too mean t’ die.”

The men crowded around. They touched Collins’s arms and examined the canteens as if he had brought back treasures from another planet. Someone began to cheer. The cry travelled down the line, gathered other voices, and rose above the rifles.

Collins heard his name pass from company to company.

He stood stupidly amid the noise. Praise embarrassed him more deeply than mockery had. The men saw his crossing but not his fear. They saw the bucket but not the moment when he had refused the dying officer. They did not know how far he had run before turning back.

He wanted to explain.

“I wasn’t—I only—there was a feller by the guns—”

No one listened. Briggs held up a canteen.

“Water! Real water, boys!”

Two lieutenants came pushing through the men. They were young officers, bright-eyed from battle and from the strange gaiety that sometimes seizes men whom death has repeatedly missed. One had lost his sword. The other wore a torn white glove on his left hand.

“What’s this?” cried the first. “Collins raided the enemy’s spring?”

“He brought the whole well, sir,” said Briggs.

The lieutenant snatched up the bucket. “Then the officers drink first. Regulations.”

A groan rose from the soldiers.

“Thief!”

“Put that down!”

The second lieutenant seized the opposite side. “Share equally, Henry.”

“Let go.”

“You let go.”

They pulled the bucket between them, laughing. The men shouted advice. For one absurd instant the bucket became a trophy in a schoolyard game, jerking from side to side above the trench while shells screamed and wounded men groaned a few yards away.

Collins reached out.

“Careful, sir—”

The first lieutenant twisted. The other slipped on a loose cartridge box. Their hands separated.

The bucket fell.

It struck the breastwork, turned once in the air and landed upon its side. Water rushed into the dust. A bright stream ran between the men’s boots, carrying splinters, powder grains and a small red thread of blood. The thirsty earth drank quickly. Within moments there remained only dark mud and the dented bucket rocking gently on its rim.

The soldiers stared.

Beyond them the meadow bent beneath the breeze, green and beautiful, while the guns continued their immense, senseless argument.

## The Charlett Hangings

It was in the spring of 1896, as nearly as I can determine—for Denton himself, though exact upon the dates of the Plantagenet charters, was reprehensibly vague about those of his own proceedings—that the late Mr. James Denton purchased the manuscript which was to occasion him so considerable an inconvenience.

I use the word inconvenience because it was his own. Had the experience been mine, I should perhaps employ a term of greater dignity; but Denton, in relating the matter to me at the library of the Kentish Archaeological Society some twelve years later, persisted in describing it as “that wretched curtain business.” This was characteristic. He possessed the scholar’s unwillingness to exaggerate any occurrence not supported by at least two independent authorities, and would, I think, have called the Last Judgment an irregular sitting of the court.

The purchase was made at Mr. Naylor’s rooms in Barchester, on Thursday, the 16th of April. The sale consisted chiefly of the library and miscellaneous collections of the Rev. Edmund Poynter, formerly rector of North Wraxall, who had died at the age of eighty-seven after devoting sixty-one years to an exhaustive history of the county. Of this history only the account of three parishes had been printed; the remainder was represented by thirty-two folio bundles, tied with green tape and labelled, in the author’s hand, “For further verification.”

Denton did not buy these. His interests, though multifarious, were not indiscriminate. What attracted him was Lot 214:

> “POYNTER (Edm.). Commonplace Book and Diary, 1716–1741, MS. on paper, with drawings of arms, seals, churches, inscriptions, &c.; original calf, broken.”

He had heard of Poynter as a diligent transcriber of inscriptions subsequently “restored” by churchwardens, and hoped the volume might contain particulars of several monuments then under discussion. It was a thick quarto, unlovely in appearance, and had suffered both from damp and from the Rev. Edmund’s habit of using it as a receptacle for anything too flat to stand on a shelf.

The bidding began at five shillings. A bookseller from Worcester carried it to nineteen; Denton said one guinea; the bookseller, discovering that the back board was detached, shook his head with the air of a man preserved from ruin; and Denton secured the lot.

He had scarcely resumed his seat when an elderly gentleman behind him observed:

“You have bought more than you suppose, sir.”

Denton turned. The speaker was unknown to him: a thin person with a yellowish beard, dark spectacles, and a catalogue so liberally annotated that very little of the original printing remained visible.

“I hope so,” said Denton. “What in particular?”

But at this point Lot 215 was announced, being six sermons by the Rev. Edmund Poynter on the Mosaic Dispensation, and the old gentleman raised one finger imperatively. Denton, who had no intention of competing for the sermons, waited until they had been knocked down for two shillings; when he looked again, the seat behind him was empty.

There is nothing remarkable in this. Auction rooms have exits, and elderly gentlemen occasionally use them. I mention the circumstance because Denton did, though he admitted that he might have misunderstood the words, and that the speaker may merely have congratulated him upon the unsuspected abundance of Poynter’s topographical information.

At that time Denton was building a house at Longcombe, about four miles from his native town of Wraybourne. His old house had been damp, inconvenient, and Tudor in precisely those portions which the surveyor most desired to remove. After a controversy prolonged through two winters, Denton surrendered it to a wealthy brewer, who promised to preserve its ancient character and at once installed a billiard-room where the chapel had been.

The new house, Longcombe Manor, stood on rising ground above the river Lark. It was of red brick, commodious, hygienic and, as Denton’s aunt remarked, utterly devoid of repose. Miss Denton had kept house for her nephew since the death of his mother. She regarded this service not as an occupation voluntarily undertaken, but as a sentence inflicted upon her by the improvidence of Providence and the helplessness of men.

“I do not complain, James,” she would say, generally in the preface to a complaint of twenty minutes’ duration. “No one has ever heard me complain. But when I reflect that at my age I am expected to choose between seven kinds of bedroom paper, all objectionable, and to explain to a young man from Birmingham why a linen cupboard requires shelves, I confess I sometimes wonder whether a convent might not have been wiser.”

Denton, who had been known to read a papal register during one of these addresses, usually said, “Quite so, Aunt Anne,” at intervals which he hoped were appropriate.

The completion of the house was promised for July, and consequently occurred in October. Meanwhile the furniture accumulated in warehouses; carpets came home in the wrong sizes; a marble chimneypiece intended for the drawing-room was discovered in Scarborough; and Miss Denton developed a personal hostility toward the architect, whom she accused of having placed the morning-room on the east side merely to annoy her.

It was during this period that Denton examined his purchase.

The Poynter diary proved disappointingly barren of inscriptions. Its author recorded visits to every house of consequence in the county, but was more interested in the quality of the wine than in the antiquity of the buildings. He copied pedigrees, noted monstrous births, disputed the age of baptismal fonts, described storms, elections and executions, and quarrelled in writing with most of his contemporaries. Quarrelling was not uncommon in Poynter’s career, though it does not appear that the other party was always aware of it.

The volume contained a great many loose papers: receipts for medicines, sketches of windows, pressed leaves, a broadside against the Pretender, two sermons in another hand, and a lock of grey hair, which Denton restored to its envelope with some precipitation. Toward the back he found a piece of fabric, roughly seven inches square, sewn by one corner to a blank leaf.

It was a stout silk-and-linen stuff, originally of a pale colour but now yellowed. Upon it had been printed, in a reddish brown, a pattern of extraordinary intricacy. At first Denton took it for a conventional arrangement of leaves. Longer inspection suggested streams of water flowing downward and caught at intervals by knots or bands. Yet neither leaves nor water quite accounted for the design. The narrow lines had a fibrous quality; they divided, crossed and gathered themselves into heavy masses, from which smaller tendrils escaped. The bands resembled bows of ribbon, but the things they confined looked less like vegetation than like long locks of hair.

Under the sample Poynter had written:

> “A piece of ye Hangings at Charlett Hall, being of a very singular and, as I conceive, artful Contrivance, tho’ wherein the Subtilty lies I have not yet discover’d. Given me by Mrs. M. H., 20 Aug. 1731.”

Denton carried the diary downstairs after luncheon and showed the fragment to his aunt.

Miss Denton put on her spectacles.

“At last,” she said.

“At last what?”

“At last you have brought me something of practical use. For fifteen years you have purchased objects which cannot be sat upon, slept in, eaten from, or even dusted without professional advice. This is a curtain pattern.”

“It may have been.”

“It is.”

“It is rather gloomy, surely.”

“The colour has faded. Done in a soft green upon cream linen, it would be charming for the bedrooms.”

Denton looked more attentively at the design.

“It seems to be hair.”

“Nothing of the kind. It is a running pattern.”

“Hair may run, I suppose.”

“If you are determined to be disagreeable, James, I will say no more. I merely thought that, since the patterns supplied by Mr. Trask are either bunches of cabbages or arrangements of pink objects which he calls Louis Seize, an old English design would give the rooms some individuality. But naturally I have no opinion.”

This last statement, when made by Miss Denton, indicated that her opinion had become immutable.

The next step was to discover whether the pattern could be reproduced. Denton, who regarded the whole matter as belonging to that region of domestic government into which a prudent man does not carry appeals, took the fragment to Cattell and Sons, decorators and furnishers, of Wraybourne High Street.

The Sons were perhaps anticipatory. Mr. Cattell himself, a round, carefully brushed man with a watch-chain resembling a civic decoration, received Denton in a room lined with wallpapers. Here crimson parrots climbed upon gilt trellises, blue irises contended with yellow suns, and Japanese children of a discouraging uniformity crossed bridges which could not, in the nature of things, have supported them.

Mr. Cattell was delighted with the fragment.

“It’s unique, sir; that’s what it is. Quite unique. And old. You can see the age in it, if I may so express myself, without any disrespect to the material. We can reproduce it, certainly. Not by machinery—not satisfactorily. ’And-blocked, sir, in two colours, with a third for the ties. Now, I should say a sage ground—”

“Cream ground,” said Denton, remembering his instructions.

“Cream, by all means. There’s nothing like cream where there’s a sufficiency of servants. The pattern in moss-green, and the little bands in a dull gold. Very chastened. Very ’armonious. You might call it the Charlett pattern, sir, if that’s the name connected with it. Customers appreciate a name.”

Denton inquired about the cost. Mr. Cattell mentioned a sum which caused him to remove his spectacles and inspect them.

“You see, sir,” said the decorator, “the block is the difficulty. Everything depends upon the block. I don’t say a lesser man couldn’t cut it cheaper. A lesser man can always do a thing cheaper, particularly if it’s done wrong. But our cutter, Mr. Pilbrow, is an artist. Temperament, unfortunately, but an artist.”

Miss Denton approved the estimate after observing that extravagance was apparently expected of her, and the work proceeded.

Some three weeks later Mr. Cattell called at Longcombe Manor, which was then inhabited by Denton, his aunt, eight workmen, two maids and an odour of varnish.

“I thought I’d mention a curious circumstance, sir,” he said. “Not that there’s any importance in it. A tradesman who understands ’is position doesn’t attach importance to the fancies of employees. But Pilbrow’s objected to the pattern.”

“Objected to cutting it?”

“Not on artistic grounds. On personal grounds, if you follow me. He says it’s a bad pattern.”

“I thought he was an artist.”

“He is, sir, and that’s where the difficulty arises. An ordinary workman cuts what he’s told and thinks about Saturday. An artist looks into a design. Pilbrow looked into this one, and after he’d looked he said he didn’t like what was looking out.”

Denton set down the paper-knife with which he had been opening an invoice.

“What did he mean by that?”

“Nothing rational, sir. Nothing that would bear repetition in a business interview. He said the strands—what you might call the branches—went together in ways he didn’t care for. Faces, he said. Figures. People hiding behind hair.”

“Was he drunk?”

“No more than usual, sir, if I may put it so. He’s cut the block. Beautiful piece of work. But when it was finished he took it out into the yard with the intention of breaking it. My foreman stopped him. We have to consider property.”

“And Pilbrow?”

“Left our employment. Mutual arrangement. He said he’d had dreams, and I said dreams were not included in the wages. There was some ’eated language. But the block is unharmed, and the first lengths will be ready on Monday.”

Denton laughed. He told Cattell that artists were privileged persons and repeated the story to his aunt at dinner.

Miss Denton did not laugh.

“I hope,” she said, “that you did not allow this absurdity to affect the price.”

“I did not.”

“Then it is merely another example of the lower classes being encouraged to have nerves. In my father’s time a man who cut blocks cut blocks. He did not look for people in them.”

The curtains were delivered in December. No one could deny that they were handsome. Against the warm cream ground, the green lines descended in sinuous masses, crossed at intervals by bands of dull yellow. Repetition had altered the effect of the original scrap. What had seemed there a mere tangle now extended from ceiling to floor in an ordered profusion; and though the motif might still be interpreted as foliage, water or hair, the eye was repeatedly drawn to certain conjunctions where the lines widened about an oval vacancy, narrowed below it, and spread again like shoulders draped in a cloak.

When Denton pointed this out, Miss Denton replied that the human eye was notorious for finding faces in carpets, clouds, fireplaces and indecorously shaped vegetables. She ordered curtains for five bedrooms, including Denton’s own, and for the long passage window on the first floor.

It may be asked why Denton permitted this. The answer is that he saw no reason not to permit it, and several excellent reasons for avoiding a dispute. It is easy, after an occurrence, to discover the precise instant at which wisdom ought to have intervened. Beforehand, wisdom commonly has another engagement.

The housewarming took place on the 19th of December. The guests were Colonel and Mrs. Maberly; Canon Rooke, whose conversation was limited to cathedral finance; Miss Henslow, an enthusiast for village industries; and Mr. Fleet, a young solicitor related to the Dentons on a branch which no one had thought it worth while to trace. Their arrival engaged Miss Denton’s whole attention and supplied James with opportunities for escape which he employed in examining the Poynter diary.

Nothing further concerning the Charlett fabric was readily discoverable. The entry of August 1731 stood alone. There were, however, several places near the end of the book where two or three leaves had been pasted together. Denton supposed that Poynter had thus suppressed matter which he later considered libellous, indecent or erroneous—a classification embracing much that antiquaries most wish to read. He did not then attempt to separate them.

The first small incident occurred on the second evening.

Denton had gone upstairs before dinner to fetch a letter. As he passed the long window at the turn of the staircase, he became aware that someone stood behind the curtain.

The impression was perfectly definite. A head was thrust forward at about the height of his own shoulder; beneath it was the suggestion of a narrow body, and one hand appeared to clutch the inner edge of the fabric. He supposed Mr. Fleet had concealed himself there in some juvenile attempt at humour and pulled the curtain aside.

There was no one. The recess was shallow and bare. The window was shut and fastened.

As the curtain fell back, Denton saw how the mistake had arisen. One of the repeated forms in the design had folded upon another, producing a tolerable representation of a face peeping through a mass of hair. He adjusted the stuff, and the figure vanished.

At dinner he asked whether Mr. Fleet had been on the stairs.

“Not I,” said Fleet. “I was being shown the hot-water system by Miss Denton.”

“You requested to see it,” said Miss Denton.

“I was too timid to refuse.”

“Young people,” observed Canon Rooke, “take warmth very much for granted.”

The conversation then entered upon the heating of the cathedral chapter-house, from which no supernatural agency could have rescued it without attracting remark.

On the following afternoon Mrs. Maberly complained that the curtains in the Blue Room rustled during the night.

“There was no wind,” she said, “and the windows were closed. I thought at first it was mice.”

“Not mice,” said Miss Denton firmly. “Mr. Cattell has certified the house.”

“What did he certify it as?” asked Fleet.

Miss Denton ignored him.

“New curtains are stiff,” she explained. “They settle.”

Mrs. Maberly looked doubtful. “These did not settle. They seemed to be rubbed together, slowly. And once I could have fancied that someone was drawing a handful of hair through the rings.”

Colonel Maberly, who had spent the night in the adjoining dressing-room because he snored, remarked that his wife’s hearing was extraordinarily acute when there was nothing to hear.

That night Denton woke at half past two. His fire had sunk low, but the room was not dark; a winter moon shone through the uncurtained upper panes and laid a pale quadrilateral upon the carpet.

The curtains were moving.

There was no violent disturbance. They swayed inward by perhaps an inch, remained suspended, and sank back. Then the left curtain stirred along its whole length, not from top to bottom as a draught might have moved it, but laterally, as if some large body behind it were cautiously changing position.

Denton sat up and listened.

The house was silent. Far off, a clock struck the half-hour. His spaniel, Toby, slept as usual on a rug beside the bed.

“Toby,” Denton whispered.

There was no answer. The curtains hung still.

He got up, crossed the room and pulled them apart. The window was secure. Nothing occupied the recess, and the air there was no colder than elsewhere. Feeling foolish, he returned to bed. As he lay down he heard a faint sound from the fabric—not a rustle now, but a soft, continuous whispering, comparable to the friction of dry hair under a brush.

He lit a candle. The sound ceased.

In daylight the thing was negligible. Denton told himself that the curtain had retained currents of warm air from the fire, that the rings were new, that heavy fabric moves for reasons not immediately apparent to those who have never studied upholstery. He did not mention the occurrence.

The guests departed on Monday, except Mr. Fleet, who remained to attend a county ball at Wraybourne. Miss Denton went with him, having declared that a young man without a competent chaperon was exposed to women of every description. Denton pleaded a headache and was left at home with two servants, both of whom retired early to rooms in the opposite wing.

I now approach the part of his account which I have most hesitated to set down. Denton told it without rhetorical embellishment; indeed, his chief difficulty was to convey a sequence of sensations for which ordinary language is ill provided. I shall not improve upon his phrases more than is necessary to make them intelligible.

He spent the evening in the library with the Poynter manuscript and went upstairs shortly after eleven. Toby accompanied him. The night was still and damp, with no moon. Denton read in bed for half an hour, put out his lamp and, as he believed, fell asleep.

He was awakened by a slight pressure upon the coverlet near his left hand.

Toby sometimes rose from the rug and laid his head upon the bed when he wished to be admitted under the blankets—a liberty Denton discouraged in theory. Without opening his eyes, he put down his hand and touched something soft.

He stroked it once.

It was hair: thick, long and human in texture.

His hand moved over a rounded surface which might have been a head, but the head was lying too high for the dog and too close to his shoulder. Moreover, it was perfectly motionless. No warmth came from it; no breath disturbed the sheet.

Denton opened his eyes.

At first he saw nothing. Then, as his sight accommodated itself to the darkness, he perceived that a shape was stretched upon the bed beside him.

It had the posture of a man crawling on his belly. One arm—or something occupying the position of an arm—lay forward across the counterpane. The head was raised slightly under Denton’s hand. From the crown, from the cheeks, from every part of it that should have presented skin or feature, there hung a mass of hair.

He could distinguish no eyes. Yet he knew that the face was turned toward him.

For perhaps two seconds neither moved. Then the head began to rise.

The motion was not the motion of one who lifts himself by neck and shoulders. The whole front of the shape appeared to draw upward soundlessly, as a fold of cloth is lifted by a concealed hand. The hair slid over Denton’s wrist. Beneath it, where a mouth should have been, something opened—a separation, not visibly formed, but perceptible by the colder darkness within.

Denton struck at it and sprang from the bed.

His feet caught in the blankets. He fell against a chair, overturned it, and reached the door with the thing moving behind him. He did not hear footsteps. He heard instead the rapid dragging of a heavy soft substance across the carpet and, mingled with it, the dry agitation of innumerable strands.

The bedroom door opened inward. As he pulled it, something caught the skirt of his nightshirt. He felt no fingers. What closed upon the linen was broader, more yielding, and for that reason infinitely worse than a hand.

The cloth tore.

Denton gained the passage. There was a night-light burning at the head of the stairs, and in its feeble circle he saw the long-window curtains stirring wildly. Figures crowded in their folds: green, elongated forms with pale vacancies for faces, thrusting themselves forward as though the printed pattern had acquired depth and was endeavouring to emerge.

Behind him came the dragging sound.

He ran toward the guest-room occupied by Fleet. The distance was perhaps twenty yards. Halfway along, something swept the back of his neck, light as a lock of hair and cold as wet thread. He flung himself against Fleet’s door, found it locked, and struck with both fists.

“Open! For God’s sake, open!”

A bolt grated. Denton fell into the room, knocking Fleet backward. As the door swung inward, Fleet saw—or said afterward that he saw—a low, black mass at the farther end of the passage. It rose nearly to the ceiling, contracted, and slipped behind the curtains of the staircase window.

They locked the door and remained with the lamps burning until Miss Denton returned at three.

Toby was found next morning under a table in the breakfast-room. He had torn the baize from the door in his effort to get out of Denton’s bedroom and could not be induced to pass the staircase window for several days.

Miss Denton’s reception of the affair may be imagined.

“You have overtired yourself,” she said. “You read in bed, you eat no proper dinner, and then you wake the entire house because you have put your hand on the dog.”

“It was not the dog.”

“You have just told me that the dog was in the room.”

“It left the room.”

“Dogs do. Particularly when chairs are thrown at them.”

Fleet attempted to intervene, but Miss Denton regarded bachelors under thirty as persons whose testimony had not yet acquired legal standing.

“And as for shadows in the passage,” she continued, “if Mr. Fleet chooses to unlock his door without putting on his spectacles—”

“I don’t wear spectacles,” said Fleet.

“Then perhaps you should.”

Denton did not argue. At eight o’clock he ordered the dogcart and drove to Wraybourne, taking the Poynter diary with him. He spent the day at the Crown Hotel, where a commercial traveller occupied the adjoining sitting-room and practised a cornet, an arrangement which Denton afterward praised as reassuring.

His first act was to send a note to Cattell requiring that every curtain of the Charlett pattern be removed from Longcombe before nightfall. His second was to examine the pasted leaves.

The operation demanded care. Warm water, a thin ivory folder and three hours’ patience enabled him to separate two pairs of pages with little injury to the writing. The first contained merely an uncharitable account of a neighbouring clergyman. The second supplied what follows.

I preserve Poynter’s spelling, though I omit a digression upon the Charlett arms and another concerning the probable site of a Roman road. That is all we need know of the antiquary’s conscience.

> “Sept. 3, 1731. At Mrs. Harbottle’s, where was much Discourse of Sr Everard Charlett, deceased now above xv Years, but still remember’d in those Parts, not with Love. He was the only Son of Sr Josias C., and in his Youth so extraordinary comely that Pictures of him were sold at Wraybourne Fair, which Vanity he did encourage. His Hair was of a dark shining Colour and came, when unbound, below the Middle; nor would he suffer it to be cut save by his own Man, preserving every Lock in Cabinets of Cedar.
>
> “He was otherwise of a vicious and cruel Disposition, given to Gaming, Women and divers unnatural Jests. One Maid of the House, having laugh’d at him when his Hair was caught by a Thorn, was turn’d out in Snow and lost two Toes. Another, who found one of his preserved Locks and threw it on the Fire, was said to have been whip’d, tho’ this the Family deny.
>
> “In the Year 1714 he caus’d Hangings to be made for the great Bedchamber at Charlett Hall, the Figure being taken from his own Hair, twisted with Ribbands; saying it was meet that they who slept there should be encompass’d with what all admir’d. The Weaver at first refus’d, alleging that the Pattern shifted under his Eye, but was compell’d by Threats. A Parcel of Sr E.’s Hair was mix’d (so Mrs. H. affirms) with the Threads or Stuff of the first Piece.
>
> “On the Night of 11 November 1716 Sr E. came late from Mr. Vane’s House, being in Liquor and having quarrell’d there. He was not seen alive thereafter. Next Morning he was found in the Ditch by Monk’s Field, his Neck broke, his Hands fill’d with Earth, and all the Hair torn from his Head, not shaven but pluck’d away to the last Root. No Hair was discover’d near the Body, tho’ Search was made.
>
> “He was laid in the Charlett Vault. At the Funeral a Woman unknown cry’d that they should bind the Coffin with Iron, else he would fetch back what was his. She was remov’d as disorderly.
>
> “In March following, the Vault being open’d for old Lady C., it was found that Sr Everard’s Coffin had burst at the Side. Those present suppos’d the Corpse swollen, but looking in perceiv’d no Corpse at all, only a great Quantity of black Hair, fresh and shining, pack’d therein and issuing from the Split. The Vault was clos’d by Order of Sr Josias, and the Matter suppress’d.
>
> “The Hangings were afterwards taken down. Mrs. Harbottle, then a young Companion to Lady C., sav’d a Piece before they were burn’d. She says that when the Cloth was cut, something within it gave a Cry, very low, such as a Child makes in Sleep. I cannot credit this, she being aged and much inclin’d to Romish Superstition; yet the Design hath in it a notable Artifice, for the longer one regards it, the more it seems not Hair represented but Hair disguis’d.”

Below this entry, in ink more faded than the rest, was added:

> “N.B. Have stitch’d the Piece herein, lest it be lost. But I like it not near my Bed, and twice dream’d of a Man without a Face, who ask’d me for his Peruke.”

One cannot admire Poynter’s judgment in preserving what he disliked, though museums and private libraries owe much to this amiable defect in collectors.

Denton read the passage twice. At five o’clock he returned to Longcombe, accompanied by Mr. Cattell and three workmen. Miss Denton objected to the removal of the curtains on the grounds of expense, appearance, inconvenience to guests, insult to the manufacturer and the absence of any spare material. Her nephew remained firm.

Mr. Cattell was less difficult.

“I won’t say I’m surprised, sir,” he remarked as they stood in the passage. “Business principles prevent me saying so. But Pilbrow did use a phrase which has come back to me. He said, ‘Once you stamp that thing, you don’t copy it—you give it children.’ Very unprofessional language, but pointed.”

The curtains were taken down before sunset. The men disliked handling them. One complained that the fabric clung to his wrists; another declared that a length removed from the Blue Room was warm, though no fire had been lit there. Cattell himself discovered, on folding the staircase pair, a quantity of dark filaments between the layers.

“It’ll be horsehair from the interlining,” he said.

There was no interlining.

Denton ordered everything—the curtains, unused lengths, trial impressions and printing block—to be carried to the kitchen garden and burned. Cattell protested faintly at the destruction of the block, on which he had entered three pounds ten in his books, but allowed professional sentiment to yield to cash reimbursement.

The fabric burned reluctantly. It blackened and writhed before taking flame, and several folds unrolled themselves from the pile with a motion which the gardeners attributed to trapped air. When the block split, a dense coil of smoke rose from it and hung for some seconds without dispersing. Toby, watching from the terrace, howled and fled into the house.

By midnight nothing remained but grey ash, metal curtain rings, and a number of small greenish clinkers. These Denton had buried. I asked him where.

“My dear fellow,” he said, “if I had thought it desirable that antiquaries should dig them up, I should have labelled the spot.”

The original fragment was not burned with the copies. It had disappeared from the diary.

This loss disturbed Denton more than he admitted. The stitch remained in the page, broken but not cut, and a faint brown outline showed where the fabric had lain. A search of the library, hotel and carriage produced nothing. Miss Denton denied having touched it. Cattell had never possessed it after the block was cut, and Pilbrow could not be found.

No further manifestation occurred at Longcombe Manor. The bedrooms were furnished with plain crimson curtains, which Miss Denton described for the remainder of her life as “safe, expensive and hideous.” Denton himself continued to sleep there, though not, I believe, without a lamp in the passage. He abandoned neither antiquarian study nor auctions, but acquired a prejudice against textile specimens and would pass them in catalogues with unusual rapidity.

The Poynter diary was eventually presented to the Wraybourne Municipal Library. The pasted leaves have been repaired and mounted, and the volume may be consulted upon application to the librarian, provided the applicant supplies a reference and does not call on Wednesday afternoons.

As for Charlett Hall, it was demolished in 1823. The vault survives beneath a plantation known as Monk’s Copse, but has not been opened in modern times. A proposal to investigate it was made in 1907 by the Wraybourne Field Club. Denton, who was then treasurer, observed that the funds would not permit it; and, for once in the history of archaeological enterprise, financial caution may have been no bad thing.

There remains only the question of the missing fragment. I have no explanation to offer. Denton had none. He once told me, however, that several months after the burning he attended a sale in Oxford and noticed, protruding from a portfolio of seventeenth-century brocades, a strip of yellowed stuff printed with reddish-brown lines.

He did not examine it.

This restraint, though late, was perhaps commendable. There are survivals of the past which instruction requires us to preserve, and others concerning which the wiser text is that of the melancholy Prince: let them be, as indeed they ought always to have been, “too much i’ the hair.”

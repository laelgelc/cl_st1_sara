“There is one department of folklore,” said Harcourt, settling himself rather deeply into the leather chair by the fire, “which has never, so far as I know, been properly collected.”

We had been talking of folklore in a desultory way—wishing-stones, corpse-candles, the appearances supposed to precede a death, and so forth. Harcourt’s remark was received with the caution commonly accorded to his discoveries.

“What department is that?” I asked.

“The folklore of private schools. Not public schools: they have traditions, of course, but traditions of a recognised and almost official kind. I mean the small private school in a large old house, where forty or fifty boys spend three parts of the year and tell each other stories after lights-out.”

“Stories of murdered ushers?”

“Among other things. There is generally a room which no boy will enter alone; a staircase where something comes up behind you; an old woman seen in the shrubbery; a master who died in a cupboard; and, almost invariably, a boy who looked through a keyhole and went mad.”

“And did you believe any of them?”

“Not in the least. That is why the one I am going to tell you remains troublesome. It was not traditional, and I was present at most of it.”

Harcourt stirred the fire with the toe of his slipper, and for a little while watched the sparks ascend.

“The school,” he began, “was near London—near enough for our parents to imagine it convenient, and far enough for us to feel exiled. The house had been built, I suppose, in Queen Anne’s time, though the schoolrooms added at the back were hideous enough to be much later. There were cedars before the drawing-room windows, ancient elms along the drive, two fields for games, and beyond them a neglected piece of ground which we called the Wilderness. I don’t remember that we were forbidden to go there, but nobody often did. It was full of laurels, nettles, and old trees clipped at some remote period and afterwards abandoned to their own devices.

“My particular friend was McLeod. You must not suppose him visionary, or bookish, or subject to fits. He was a fair, square-built boy, not clever except at figures, reasonably fond of cricket, and capable of eating anything. If he had a fault, it was an excessive satisfaction with perfectly ordinary jokes. I mention this because, when curious events are connected with a person, it is usual to discover afterwards that he had Celtic blood, second sight, an aunt in an asylum, or some rot of that kind. McLeod had none of it, so far as I know.

“The event happened in our second year, after the arrival of a new classical master named Sampson.

“Sampson was not an unpopular man. He was perhaps forty, with black hair and a short black beard, and a face which looked pale even in summer. He had travelled a good deal in the East, and could make his lessons interesting when he chose. He would bring out a coin, a bit of pottery, or an engraved stone, and tell us where he had bought it. Boys are not exacting judges of scholarship; but we knew when a man knew his business, and Sampson did.

“There was one little incident concerning him which meant nothing at the time. He showed McLeod and me a Byzantine coin, or what he called Byzantine, which he wore on his watch-chain. On one side there was an emperor’s head, and on the other a cross with cramped letters round it.

“‘You collect these things, sir?’ McLeod asked.

“‘A few,’ said Sampson. ‘This one has a private association.’

“He turned it over quickly, but not before I saw that its rim had been engraved with two initials and a date: 24 July 1865. The initials were not his. I think they were G. W., though I will not swear to it.

“‘Whose date is that, sir?’ I asked.

“He looked at me in a way that made me feel I had been impertinent.

“‘A date is not necessarily a birthday, Harcourt,’ he said, and put the coin away.

“That was all. We forgot it until later.

“The first really odd occurrence took place during a Latin exercise. Sampson’s method was to dictate English sentences, which we translated and wrote in our books. Sometimes he walked between the desks; more often he sat at the high table, correcting papers while he dictated. I can see the room now: the long windows, the maps rolled above the blackboard, the ink-stained desks, and the dusty light coming through the cedars.

“He had given us perhaps eight sentences when McLeod suddenly stopped writing.

“‘What is it?’ I whispered.

“‘Nothing. Shut up.’

“He looked puzzled, rubbed out something, and began again. Sampson dictated two more sentences and then collected the books. As he passed ours, McLeod held his down.

“‘Hand it up,’ said Sampson.

“‘Please, sir, I’ve made rather a mess.’

“‘Then I shall have the pleasure of observing it.’

“McLeod gave him the book. Sampson turned the pages with his pencil. When he reached the last one, he became absolutely still. I was looking directly at him. His face did not merely change colour; it seemed for a moment to lose its expression altogether. Then he shut the book.

“‘McLeod, what does this mean?’

“‘I don’t know, sir.’

“‘You don’t know?’

“‘No, sir.’

“Sampson read aloud: ‘Remember the well among the four yews.’

“There was a laugh, naturally. Someone at the back said, ‘Very good, McLeod,’ and Sampson called for silence with unusual sharpness.

“‘Why did you write it?’

“‘I don’t know, sir. I thought you dictated it.’

“‘You thought I dictated that?’

“‘Yes, sir—I mean, it was in my head as if you had said it. Then I saw it wasn’t Latin exercise, so I tried to scratch it out.’

“Sampson stood with the book in his hand, looking over McLeod’s head. I remember hearing a cart go along the road outside; the sound seemed extraordinarily loud.

“‘Have you heard of such a place?’ he asked.

“‘No, sir.’

“‘Has anyone spoken to you about a well?’

“‘No, sir.’

“‘Or four yew trees?’

“‘No, sir.’

“At this Sampson turned his back upon us and went to the window. He remained there for half a minute, perhaps longer, and when he faced the room again he looked ill.

“‘You will stay behind after class, McLeod.’

“McLeod did so, and I waited for him in the corridor. When he came out, Sampson followed as far as the door.

“‘If you remember anything further,’ he said, ‘anything whatever about those words, you will come to me at once.’

“‘Yes, sir.’

“‘I mean at once.’

“He shut the door.

“‘What did he do to you?’ I asked.

“‘Nothing. Asked me about twenty times whether I’d heard the sentence before.’

“‘Had you?’

“‘No. That’s the jolly odd part. I can’t tell you where it came from. I could see the place, though.’

“‘What place?’

“‘The well. It was all dark and green, with the four trees close round it. I had a notion there was water at the bottom, and something else. But that’s gone now.’

“He said this quite calmly and was chiefly concerned because he expected to lose marks for the spoiled exercise. Within a few days he seemed to have forgotten the matter; so did I, almost. Sampson was absent from supper that evening, but came to lessons next morning and behaved as usual.

“Several weeks passed. The term went on with football, impositions, cold baths, and the other institutions which then appeared permanent. If Sampson was quieter than he had been, we did not greatly notice it. Once I saw him walking near the Wilderness, and thought he was searching among the trees; but he returned when he observed me.

“The second incident happened on a wet afternoon toward the end of November. We were again doing Latin, though this time the exercise was written on loose sheets. Sampson had set a passage and left us for ten minutes while he fetched a reference book. There were sixteen boys in the division and, when he returned, sixteen papers lay upon the end of his desk.

“He counted them.

“‘Who has handed in two?’

“No one answered.

“He counted again, more slowly.

“‘There are seventeen sheets.’

“‘Perhaps one was there before, sir,’ someone suggested.

“‘There was nothing on the desk.’

“He began sorting the papers by name. Each boy’s sheet was present, and there remained one more. It was of the same school paper, ruled with a blue margin, but it bore no name. Sampson picked it up gingerly, as though the ink might not be dry.

“What followed I saw clearly, because I sat in the front row. The writing was large and uneven, in red ink. We were not allowed red ink, and none was kept in the room except in Sampson’s marking bottle, which stood corked beside him.

“He read the words. His lips moved before any sound came.

“‘If you do not come to me, I will come to you.’

“He made a noise in his throat, dropped the sheet, and went out of the room.

“That caused more excitement than the message itself. A master did not run from a class. Yet Sampson had very nearly run. We sat quiet for perhaps ten seconds; then everyone began talking.

“‘Whose is it?’

“‘What does it mean?’

“‘It’s McLeod again.’

“‘It isn’t my writing,’ said McLeod. ‘And I haven’t any red ink.’

“Dawson, who had a taste for evidence, examined the desks and waste-paper basket. I bagged the extra paper. That was not very honest, perhaps, but we feared Sampson would destroy it, and by then we felt the thing belonged partly to us.

“The head boy tried the door. Sampson was nowhere in the corridor. After some discussion we completed the exercise, though nobody attended much to the Latin. Another master came twenty minutes later and said that Mr. Sampson was indisposed.

“At tea I took out the sheet to show McLeod. The writing had gone.

“I don’t mean that it had faded so that one could barely distinguish it. There was no red writing whatever. The sheet was blank except for a brown mark near the corner, which might have been made by a wet finger. We held it against the lamp, rubbed it, breathed on it, and tried water from the washbasin. Nothing appeared.

“‘You saw it,’ I said.

“‘Of course I saw it.’

“‘It said—’

“‘I know what it said. Don’t say it again.’

“His manner was so unlike his usual one that I stopped. We put the paper into my locker, where, for all I know, it remained until the end of term. I did look for it years afterwards, when my mother sent me a box of school rubbish, but it was not there.”

Harcourt paused to refill his pipe. I asked whether anyone else had preserved a copy of the message.

“No. Several boys remembered it, but not for long, or not accurately. One said the words were, ‘You must come to me’; another, ‘I am coming for you.’ That is how legends are made. McLeod and I had particular reason to remember the exact form.

“Sampson did not appear again that day. We heard that he had a fever and was lying down in his room. His room was on the first floor in the older part of the house. It looked over a narrow stone terrace toward the cedars. The window was perhaps twelve feet from the ground; there was no balcony, no ivy, and no convenient pipe by which anyone could climb to it.

“Our dormitory was on the floor above and at the opposite end. McLeod slept next to me. It must have been about two in the morning when he shook me awake.

“‘Harcourt,’ he whispered. ‘Come here.’

“‘What’s the matter?’

“‘Don’t make a row. Come to the window.’

“The moon was full, and the rain had cleared. I remember the floor was icy under my feet. McLeod had pulled aside the blind and was staring toward the old wing.

“‘Sampson’s room,’ he said. ‘Look.’

“At first I saw the white wall, the black squares of windows, and the cedar branches moving over them. Then something moved where nothing ought to have been.

“There was a figure outside Sampson’s window.

“It was not standing on the ground. It seemed to be perched upon the stone sill, crouching sideways, with one arm pressed against the glass. How it supported itself I cannot say. The moon showed it quite distinctly, yet without giving me any detail I was anxious to retain.

“It appeared to be a man—or the remains of one—very thin, bareheaded, and dressed in clothes that clung close to the body. Water streamed from it. I could see the drops falling from its elbows and from the ragged ends of its coat, shining for an instant before they vanished against the wall. The face was turned partly toward us. It was pale, but not with the ordinary paleness of illness; there was something swollen and wasted in it at once, if that is possible. The hair lay across the forehead like wet weed.

“‘What is it doing?’ I said.

“‘Beckoning.’

“It was. The hand nearest the glass opened and closed slowly. Then one long finger tapped the window.

“We could not hear the tap at that distance, but I knew exactly what sound it made.

“A light appeared in Sampson’s room. Not a lamp, I think; probably a candle. His shadow crossed the blind, stopped, and returned. The figure raised its head. For a moment its mouth looked open.

“Then the candle went out.

“McLeod caught my arm hard enough to bruise it. The wet thing upon the sill turned, not toward us, but toward the darkened room. It drew itself upright in a movement that seemed much too slow, and passed forward.

“The window had not opened. Nevertheless, it was no longer outside.

“We remained where we were for some time. I cannot tell you how long. Neither of us proposed going to Sampson’s room. Boys are said to be adventurous, but there are occasions when they know better.

“And next day Mr. Sampson was gone.

“His bed had been slept in. His clothes, watch, money, and travelling bag were in the room. The window was shut and fastened. There were some drops of water on the floor between the window and the bed, but these were explained by a cracked wash-jug, though the jug stood upright and was nearly full. The coin with the engraved date was not found.

“They searched the house, grounds, pond, railway station, and surrounding roads. Police came and questioned everyone. It was supposed that Sampson had left in a state of delirium, though no one could show how he had passed the locked doors or why he had taken neither coat nor boots. The Wilderness was searched too. I remember seeing men among the laurels with sticks.

“Whether they found four yews, I cannot tell you. I never asked.

“The curious thing is that McLeod and I said nothing. We intended to. We agreed before breakfast that we must tell the headmaster what we had seen; but when we stood outside his study, neither of us could speak of it. This was not ordinary fear of ridicule. The words simply would not come. McLeod became sick, and I felt, as I remember, a pressure at the base of my throat whenever I tried to say ‘window’ or ‘well.’

“By the time that constraint passed, years had gone by and the affair had become an old scandal which respectable people preferred not to revive. McLeod died in India. I have told no one until tonight.”

Harcourt knocked the ash from his pipe.

“So that,” he said, “is my contribution to the folklore of private schools. I do not ask you to believe that a dead man climbed to Sampson’s window, or that anyone had once been put into a well among four yews. I only know that McLeod wrote a sentence he did not understand; that another sentence came into the classroom upon a paper no boy owned; that we saw something wet outside a first-floor window; and that Sampson was not there in the morning.

“What came for him, and why, I really don’t know.

“But I think he knew.”

## The Practical Uses of Golf

The young man came into the clubhouse at half-past six with the expression of one who has discovered that civilization is a fraud and that the universe, considered as a going concern, pays no dividend.

He carried beneath his arm a driver broken neatly in two. Behind him, at an interval of some twenty yards, came a caddie bearing the remainder of the instruments with which the tragedy had been performed. The caddie’s face was calm. Caddies see too much of human sorrow to allow it to disturb them.

The young man flung himself into a chair.

“Waiter,” he said, “bring me something long and cold. Also send word to Perkins in the professional’s shop that he can have my clubs for what they’ll fetch. I am through with golf.”

In the deep leather chair by the window, the Oldest Member opened his eyes. He had been asleep, but only in the technical sense. The Oldest Member never surrendered consciousness altogether while there was a possibility that somebody in the neighbourhood might begin talking about golf.

“Through with golf?” he repeated.

“Absolutely.”

The Oldest Member looked at him with pity. It was the sort of look which bishops reserve for promising young curates who have announced an intention of joining a travelling circus.

“What happened?”

“I went round in a hundred and twenty-seven.”

“The wind was troublesome.”

“There was no wind.”

“The greens were fast.”

“They were not.”

“Your caddie misclubbed you?”

“My caddie,” said the young man, “spent most of the afternoon hiding behind trees and making his peace with his Maker. No, golf has had every chance with me, and it has failed. I have devoted three of the best years of my life to it. I have bought books, balls, waistcoats, patent putters, ribbed stockings, and a small machine which was guaranteed to keep my left heel down. And what has golf given me in return? Nothing. It has no practical value.”

The Oldest Member sat up.

“No practical value?”

“None whatever. You hit a ball. You walk after it. You hit it again. Sometimes you don’t hit it. What earthly good does it do?”

Outside, on the terrace, a stout man dropped a glass.

The Oldest Member’s brows contracted. He appeared to be listening to a voice from another world.

“My boy,” he said gravely, “you remind me of Cuthbert Banks.”

“Who was he?”

“The man who owed all the happiness of his life to the fact that he could play a mashie shot over a bunker and make it stop dead.”

The young man moved uneasily.

“If this is going to be a golfing story—”

“It is not a golfing story. It is a story of Love, Art, Destiny, and the municipal water supply. Golf merely enters into it as the handmaiden of Providence.”

The waiter arrived with the long, cold restorative. The young man drank deeply.

“Well,” he said, “go on.”

The Oldest Member folded his hands across his waistcoat and looked dreamily out at the eighteenth green.

“The incidents which I am about to relate,” he said, “occurred some years ago in that singularly attractive residential suburb, Wood Hills.”

Wood Hills was one of those agreeable spots which have everything except a past. Its gravel soil ensured perfect drainage; its electric-lighting arrangements were of the most modern description; each house possessed not only a bathroom but, in many cases, a little room adjoining it in which golf clubs, perambulators, and objects whose use nobody could remember might be stored. The railway service to town was frequent, the rents moderate, and the company’s water beyond reproach. Estate agents, in their moments of enthusiasm, spoke of Wood Hills as Nature’s Paradise within thirty minutes of the City.

It had also Culture.

Culture had arrived comparatively late, but it had made up for lost time. For many years Wood Hills had been content with its golf club, its tennis club, and its annual flower show. Then Mrs. Smethurst took a house in Hawthorn Road, and almost immediately the spiritual life of the neighbourhood began to sit up and take nourishment.

Mrs. Smethurst founded the Wood Hills Literary and Ethical Society.

The society met on alternate Wednesdays in the drawing rooms of its members. Papers were read. Questions were asked. Tea was drunk. Great Thoughts circulated in an atmosphere mildly charged with buttered toast. Before long, nobody who wished to be considered anybody could admit that he had not read the more recent Scandinavian dramatists or formed a considered opinion on the neo-symbolists of southern Lithuania.

The golfers did not join.

This was unfortunate. Had they joined, the Literary and Ethical Society might have broadened its mind; and had the members of the society occasionally played nine holes, they might have ceased to use the word “soul” as if they had invented it. Instead, the two camps drew apart.

The Golfers spoke of the Cultured as highbrows, windbags, and people who needed exercise. The Cultured referred to the Golfers as materialists, Philistines, and—when greatly moved—bourgeois. There came into being what historians, if historians had ever heard of Wood Hills, would undoubtedly have called the Great Schism.

Across this gulf there drifted, one pleasant afternoon in May, a golf ball.

Cuthbert Banks had reached the fourteenth hole one under fours. He was a broad-shouldered, pleasant-faced young man whose handicap was plus two and whose conversation, though not sparkling, was considered sound at the nineteenth hole. He possessed a private income sufficient for his simple needs, a neat semi-detached house, and a collection of putters which he believed to be the largest in Surrey.

At the fourteenth he drove.

A more perfect shot, judged by its first two hundred yards, could hardly have been imagined. It flew straight, low, and strong, with that clean singing note which causes the true golfer’s heart to leap like a gaffed salmon. Unfortunately, at the two-hundred-and-first yard it struck the branch of an oak tree, swerved sharply to the left, cleared a privet hedge, and passed through the open french window of a house adjoining the course.

There was a crash.

Cuthbert winced.

“Gone in somebody’s tea,” said his caddie.

It had not, as it happened, gone into the tea. It had gone into Culture.

The drawing room belonged to Mrs. Smethurst, and the Wood Hills Literary and Ethical Society was in session. A thin gentleman from Bloomsbury had just reached the most delicate portion of his address on “The Future of the Inner Consciousness in Post-War Prose” when the ball entered. It struck a plaster bust of the poet Shelley immediately above the left eyebrow, glanced from him to a small table, demolished a cup, and came to rest in a dish of macaroons.

There are moments when the most experienced lecturer loses the thread of his argument. The gentleman from Bloomsbury stood silent, with his mouth open.

Cuthbert climbed through the window.

“Terribly sorry,” he said. “My ball, I think.”

Several ladies uttered little cries. A man with flowing hair moved behind the piano. Mrs. Smethurst drew herself up.

“Do you habitually enter private houses in this fashion?”

“No,” said Cuthbert. “Generally I come in through the door. But I’m playing in the monthly medal.”

He approached the dish and picked out his ball, which had become attached to a macaroon.

“Would anybody mind if I dropped without penalty?”

The meeting regarded him coldly. He became conscious of being in the presence of an alien civilization. It was as if a Roman legionary, looking for a short cut to the barracks, had blundered into a convention of vestal virgins.

Then he saw Adeline Smethurst.

She was standing near the mantelpiece, and to Cuthbert she seemed to shine among the assembled intellectuals like a diamond in a scuttle of coal. She was slender, fair, and grave, with large eyes which gave the impression that she had just been reading something improving and had not enjoyed it but intended to persevere. In one hand she held a notebook. In the other was half a macaroon.

Love came to Cuthbert not as a gradual dawning but as a topped drive comes to a man standing unwarily thirty yards ahead of the tee. It struck him suddenly and deprived him of coherent speech.

He gazed at Adeline.

Adeline gazed at the golf ball.

“You have damaged Shelley,” she said.

Cuthbert looked at the bust.

“Only a small chip,” he said reassuringly. “A dab of plaster and he’ll be as good as ever.”

The thin lecturer made a distressed sound.

“Sir, we were discussing the function of art.”

“Were you?” said Cuthbert. “I wondered what was going on.”

He became aware that this remark had not helped. Even Adeline appeared chilled by it.

“I had better be getting along,” he said. “I’m sorry about the cup. Send the bill to the club.”

He climbed back through the window and continued his round. It is evidence of the extraordinary effect which Adeline had produced upon him that he played the remaining holes in level fours and won the medal by three strokes.

Love affects golfers in different ways. There are men whom the tender emotion reduces to a state in which they cannot hole a six-inch putt without first addressing the ball six times and breathing like a broken bellows. Others are stimulated by it. Cuthbert belonged to the latter class. For the next fortnight his approaches nestled against the pin as if tied to it by a string.

But though his golf prospered, his heart knew no peace.

He discovered Adeline’s name, address, habits, ambitions, and favourite authors. The last item gave him considerable trouble. Hitherto his reading had consisted chiefly of detective stories, the rules of golf, and a pamphlet entitled *How I Lowered My Handicap*, by a man who, as was revealed in the final chapter, had accomplished this miracle by joining a club where everybody played badly.

Adeline’s literary tastes were of a sterner kind.

Her supreme admiration was reserved for Raymond Parsloe Devine.

Raymond Parsloe Devine was Wood Hills’ resident novelist and its principal intellectual ornament. He was a long, pale young man who wore black ties, allowed his hair to droop over his forehead, and had the air of one who has been selected by Fate to bear the sorrows of humanity and finds the weight trying.

His first novel, *The Grey Nausea*, had been published at his own expense and reviewed by a cousin in a weekly paper. His second, *Whither? A Study in Ultimate Things*, had sold seventeen copies, six of which had been purchased by Mrs. Smethurst for presentation to friends. His third, *Ashes of Dawn*, had been called “curiously significant” by a critic whom Raymond had once lent five pounds.

In Wood Hills this was fame.

Raymond spoke frequently at meetings of the society. His principal themes were the futility of existence, the decay of civilization, and the influence upon his work of obscure foreign writers. He could never mention a loaf of bread without tracing its spiritual ancestry to a Bulgarian pessimist whose name sounded like a sneeze. Adeline listened to him with parted lips.

Cuthbert, having met her several times on the road between her house and the station, lost no time in proposing marriage.

“I love you,” he said. “Will you marry me?”

He had rehearsed a rather longer speech, but the opening seemed to cover the ground.

Adeline looked at him sadly.

“No, Mr. Banks.”

“Why not?”

“Our souls do not meet.”

“We could have lunch somewhere and talk it over.”

“You misunderstand me. You and I belong to different worlds. I could never marry a man without ambition.”

Cuthbert was surprised.

“I’ve got ambition. I want to win the club championship again this year.”

“I mean real ambition. The desire to achieve something permanent—to enrich humanity—to make your life a contribution to the progress of the race.”

Cuthbert considered.

“I won the French Open last spring.”

Adeline sighed.

“You see? You think only of games.”

“It took a good deal of thinking to get out of the rough at the seventeenth.”

“What is golf compared with Art?”

“You can’t compare them,” said Cuthbert. “Golf’s much harder.”

She shook her head.

“You are a nonentity.”

“A what?”

“A man who has done nothing and is known to nobody. Contrast yourself with Raymond Parsloe Devine.”

Cuthbert did so. He found the process unpleasant.

“Parsloe Devine,” continued Adeline, “is a force. His name is familiar in the highest literary circles.”

“It may be,” said Cuthbert, “but I’ve never heard it in the locker room.”

“He is influenced by Sovietski.”

“Who?”

“Sovietski, the great Russian master.”

“What did he win?”

Adeline’s eyes grew colder.

“And by Nastikoff.”

“Another Russian golfer?”

“They are novelists.”

“Ah,” said Cuthbert. “That explains it.”

“No,” said Adeline, “I cannot marry you.”

Cuthbert went home and sat among his putters till midnight. At twelve-fifteen he formed a resolution. Love had placed him in a bunker, but he would not take an unplayable lie. He would join the Literary and Ethical Society and become a man whose soul could be seen without field glasses.

Mrs. Smethurst welcomed him cautiously. The conversion of a Golfer was an event of some importance, but she had not forgotten Shelley’s eyebrow.

“You understand,” she said, “that ours is not a merely social body?”

“Quite.”

“We are seekers.”

“Right.”

“We endeavour to pierce through appearances to the fundamental reality beneath.”

“I see.”

“What are you reading at present?”

Cuthbert had prepared for this.

“The Russians.”

Mrs. Smethurst beamed.

“Which Russians?”

“All of them,” said Cuthbert.

His membership began the following Wednesday.

I will pass lightly over the next few months. There are sufferings which should not be described in detail, particularly when dinner is approaching. It is enough to say that Cuthbert attended every meeting and sat through papers on “The Neo-Primitives,” “Sex Repression in the Younger Icelandic School,” “The Ethical Significance of Free Verse,” and “Is the Novel Dead?”

At the last of these he was tempted to reply in the affirmative, but restrained himself.

He bought books. He opened books. On two occasions he read as far as page nine. He learned to say “subconscious urge,” “essentially dynamic,” and “the reaction is purely subjective.” These phrases, properly distributed, enabled him to take part in general conversation without exposing the fact that he had no idea what anybody was talking about.

The strain told upon his game. A man cannot spend Tuesday night wrestling with a three-hundred-page essay on the soul of modern Poland and expect to drive sweetly on Wednesday morning. Cuthbert began to press. His left shoulder dipped. His putting became nervous. Once, in a mixed foursome, he laid himself a stymie and laughed bitterly for nearly an hour.

His love, meanwhile, increased.

So did his dislike of Raymond Parsloe Devine.

Raymond was everywhere. If he was speaking, Adeline watched him. If he was not speaking, she watched him prepare to speak. If he was merely sitting in a corner eating a cucumber sandwich, she studied him as though the manner in which he removed the crust contained a message for future generations.

Raymond accepted this devotion as his right. He spoke to Cuthbert with kindly contempt.

“Still amusing yourself with golf, Banks?”

“Yes.”

“Extraordinary. I suppose it supplies a certain physical outlet.”

“It does if you hit somebody with a niblick.”

Raymond smiled faintly.

“I fear you have not yet escaped from the literal mind.”

“I haven’t tried.”

“You should cultivate the symbol. To me, the golf ball is Man, the club is Destiny, and the bunker is the Inescapable.”

“To me,” said Cuthbert, “the golf ball is a new one-and-sixpenny object which I don’t like losing.”

Raymond’s smile became still fainter.

The position might have continued indefinitely had it not been for Vladimir Brusiloff.

Vladimir Brusiloff was the most celebrated Russian novelist then living—or, at any rate, the most celebrated Russian novelist who had recently arrived in England and whose publishers were advertising a new translation. He had written novels about peasants, revolutionaries, princes, convicts, mystics, assassins, and one memorable veterinary surgeon who strangled a tax collector in a snowstorm and then spent four hundred pages regretting it.

Nobody in Wood Hills had read him from beginning to end. This only increased his reputation.

When Mrs. Smethurst learned that he had consented to attend a reception at her house, she experienced emotions which can come only once in a suburban hostess’s life. Napoleon may have felt something similar on entering Moscow, though his catering arrangements were less satisfactory.

Invitations were issued. Flowers were ordered. The best drawing room was opened and aired. Shelley’s forehead was repaired. Mrs. Smethurst purchased a special tea whose label suggested that it had been plucked at dawn by thoughtful monks on a Himalayan precipice.

Raymond Parsloe Devine was in a state of feverish excitement. For years he had described himself as a disciple of Brusiloff. He had written an essay, “Brusiloff and the Ultimate Void,” which had appeared in the parish magazine because the editor was ill and the assistant editor careless. The approaching meeting was to be the summit of his career.

Cuthbert had not been invited.

The omission was not accidental. Mrs. Smethurst felt that on such an occasion no risks should be taken with a man who might begin discussing mashies. Adeline did not protest.

On the evening of the reception, therefore, Cuthbert remained at home attempting to read *The Cow of the Steppes*, Brusiloff’s most symbolic novel. He had reached the passage in which the cow represented the failure of parliamentary government when his concentration broke down.

From across the road came the lights of the Smethurst house. Motorcars arrived. Figures moved behind curtains. Somewhere within, Raymond was probably clasping the hand of the Master and saying that his own poor work owed everything to *The Cow of the Steppes*.

Cuthbert put down the book.

Jealousy is bad for the figure. During his months of literary discipline, Cuthbert had lost three pounds through skipped meals and gained nine through sitting indoors. His waistcoats no longer met comfortably, and his soul resembled a golfer who has just found his ball in a rabbit scrape.

He crossed the road.

What exactly he intended to do he did not know. Men in love seldom prepare a card for the course. They take a driver and hope.

The kitchen door stood open. Cuthbert entered. Hearing footsteps, he concealed himself in a small room which proved to be the coal cellar. A few moments later a maid came in for fuel, and he was obliged to flatten himself behind the door in a manner more suitable to a sardine than to a former champion of France.

When the maid had gone, he emerged, dusty but resolute, and made his way upstairs. He reached the drawing-room door just as Mrs. Smethurst was presenting Raymond to Vladimir Brusiloff.

The great man was short, square, and abundantly bearded. He looked less like the conscience of Europe than like a prosperous pirate who had recently bought a fur coat. His eyes were small and piercing. His manner suggested that he had been disappointed in mankind and was prepared to say so.

Raymond bent reverently.

“Master,” he said, “this is the greatest moment of my life.”

Brusiloff inspected him.

“So?”

“I cannot tell you what your work has meant to me.”

“No?”

“I owe you everything. It was through you that I came to Sovietski.”

Brusiloff’s beard moved.

“Sovietski?”

“The immortal Sovietski.”

“Sovietski no good.”

There was a pause.

It was not a long pause, but during it Raymond Parsloe Devine’s stock fell twenty points on the Wood Hills exchange.

Raymond blinked.

“No good?”

“Very bad,” said Brusiloff. “Sovietski write like cabbage. All fog. No life. No blood. No pipovitch.”

“Quite,” said Raymond faintly.

Mrs. Smethurst moved a vase half an inch to the left.

Raymond rallied. Great thinkers are not crushed by a single reverse. They issue new shares.

“Of course,” he said, “there are weaknesses. It is Nastikoff whom I regard as the supreme—”

“Nastikoff!” cried Brusiloff.

His voice shook the teaspoons.

“Yes,” said Raymond, less confidently. “Nastikoff.”

“Nastikoff worse. Nastikoff is donkey. Nastikoff is what you call him—hot potato.”

“Hot potato?”

“Bad potato. Rotten. Pah!”

He made a gesture as of throwing Nastikoff into the dustbin.

The members of the Literary and Ethical Society stared at Raymond. Five minutes earlier, his intimacy with Sovietski and Nastikoff had seemed to place him among the elect. Now those distinguished Russians lay about the room in fragments, and Raymond’s preferred shares were in the cellar with no takers.

Adeline was looking at him strangely.

“I thought,” she said, “you considered Nastikoff the central genius of the age.”

Raymond moistened his lips.

“One’s judgments,” he said, “are necessarily provisional.”

“You said anyone who failed to understand him was spiritually dead.”

“Did I? I was speaking paradoxically.”

Brusiloff had turned away. Literary assassination made him thirsty. He accepted a cup of tea and drank it with a grimness which suggested that he had expected vodka and suspected treachery.

“England,” he said, “very strange country. Many great men.”

Mrs. Smethurst glowed. This was better. She prepared to hear Shakespeare praised on her carpet.

“Ah, yes, indeed,” she said. “Our literary tradition—”

“You know Meetch?”

She hesitated.

“Meetch?”

“Great English Meetch.”

Nobody spoke. The room was full of Culture, but Meetch had somehow escaped it.

Brusiloff frowned.

“Also Vardoon.”

Mrs. Smethurst looked imploringly at Raymond. Raymond, though bruised, still represented the home side.

“Vardoon,” he said thoughtfully. “The name is familiar. A minor Georgian poet, perhaps?”

Brusiloff uttered a sound of disgust.

“No poet. Vardoon! Great Vardoon! Great gameovitch man. You not know?”

The literary society looked at one another. They resembled a collection of cats which has discovered that the committee arranging the animal show is composed entirely of dogs.

From the doorway came a voice.

“He means Abe Mitchell and Harry Vardon.”

Every head turned.

Cuthbert stood there, blackened with coal dust and with a cobweb across his collar. His appearance was not formal, but his eye was bright.

Brusiloff set down his cup.

“You know Meetch?”

“Rather.”

“You know Vardoon?”

“I’ve played with them both.”

The great novelist advanced upon him.

“You play golf?”

“Yes.”

“What handicapovitch?”

“Plus two.”

Brusiloff seized his arm.

“Plus two! Golly!”

The word fell upon the room with tremendous effect. It was as if Sophocles, invited to address the Athenian Academy, had opened with a cry of “What ho!”

“You good player?”

“Fair.”

“Name?”

“Banks. Cuthbert Banks.”

Brusiloff stared. Then his face changed. The sternness vanished; the beard seemed to bloom.

“Banks! Cootaboot Banks?”

“More or less.”

“Cootaboot Banks who win French Open?”

“Yes.”

Brusiloff embraced him.

There are social moments which cannot be adequately rehearsed. Cuthbert, who had expected at most to be asked why he was covered in coal, found himself locked against the bosom of the most famous living Russian novelist while the intellectual aristocracy of Wood Hills watched in stupefaction.

Brusiloff released him and shouted with joy.

“Cootaboot! Marvelous! Great iron player! At Saint Cloud, last round, you make niblicksky from sand—zip!—dead by hole.”

“It came off rather well.”

“Rather well! It is colossal! It is art! It is soul!”

The society shivered. For months its members had been explaining to Cuthbert that golf was neither art nor soul. Brusiloff had disposed of the matter in three sentences.

“You play with Meetch?”

“Several times.”

“And Vardoon?”

“Yes.”

“Tell me. Sit. All peoples go away.”

Mrs. Smethurst started.

“But, Mr. Brusiloff, we had hoped that you would speak to us about the future of the novel.”

“No future,” said Brusiloff. “Novel dead.”

The thin gentleman from Bloomsbury, who had once lectured on that very subject for an hour and a quarter without reaching so definite a conclusion, looked wounded.

Brusiloff drew Cuthbert to a sofa.

“You use interlocking grip?”

“Overlapping.”

“Ah! I also overlapping. But my right elbow—bad. At top I make chicken.”

“You’re letting it fly out?”

“Always fly. Like revolutionary. Cannot control.”

“You want to keep a handkerchief under your right arm.”

“Handkerchief?”

“Or a glove. Swing without dropping it.”

Brusiloff slapped his knee.

“Great! Simple! This is genius.”

Raymond Parsloe Devine stood near the fireplace. His evening, which had opened with the promise of international recognition, had developed into a nightmare in which an uninvited golfer from across the road was teaching Vladimir Brusiloff to keep his elbow in. Raymond attempted one last recovery.

“Master,” he said, “might I ask your opinion of my own work?”

Brusiloff looked at him vaguely.

“You write?”

“I am the author of *The Grey Nausea*.”

“Never hear.”

“*Whither?*”

“No.”

“*Ashes of Dawn*?”

“No. You play golf?”

“No.”

“Then go away,” said Brusiloff.

Raymond went.

He did not literally leave the house. That would have been too dignified. He retreated behind a group of ladies and remained there, rather like a hat stand which has realized that it is not wanted.

Cuthbert found himself the centre of the gathering. People who had previously addressed him, when compelled to do so, in the tone used to request a tradesman to call at the back door now clustered about him.

Had he really played with Harry Vardon? Was Abe Mitchell’s drive as long as report made it? What did he consider the proper club for a half-shot into a crosswind? One elderly lady, who had spent thirty years without touching anything more athletic than a paper knife, expressed a passionate desire to know whether the modern rubber-cored ball had impaired the spiritual discipline of the game.

Cuthbert answered modestly.

Adeline stood apart.

The rapid collapse of Raymond’s intellectual capital had affected her profoundly. She looked at him and saw, not the force whose name was familiar in the highest circles, but a pale young man hiding behind an aunt in a mauve dress. She looked at Cuthbert and saw Vladimir Brusiloff hanging on his words as a novice hangs on the professional’s advice before the spring meeting.

The facts rearranged themselves.

It was not that Adeline was mercenary. She cared nothing for money. It was distinction that attracted her, and distinction is a curious bird which frequently requires somebody else to point it out. She had been informed that Raymond was great, and she had admired him. Brusiloff had now informed her that Cuthbert was great. The stone rejected by the builders had turned out to be the clubhouse champion.

She approached the sofa.

“Mr. Banks.”

Cuthbert looked up.

During the last quarter of an hour he had scarcely noticed her. This alone was a new experience for Adeline.

“Good evening,” he said.

“I did not know you were coming.”

“Neither did your mother.”

“I have been listening to what Mr. Brusiloff says of you.”

“He knows a lot about golf for a novelist.”

“Why did you never tell me that you were famous?”

Cuthbert was puzzled.

“I told you I won the French Open.”

“Yes, but you said it as if it were nothing.”

“Well, one doesn’t like to swank.”

Brusiloff nodded approvingly.

“Great man never swank. Only bad novelist swank.”

From behind the aunt in mauve came a faint choking sound.

Adeline sat beside Cuthbert.

“Would it be very difficult,” she asked, “for someone like me to learn golf?”

The room appeared to recede from him. Hope, which for months had been lying at the bottom of his heart with a sack over its head, sprang up and waved both arms.

“You want to learn?”

“I feel that I may have judged the game superficially.”

“You called it an aimless pastime for the physically obsessed.”

“One’s judgments,” said Adeline, borrowing from the ruined Raymond, “are necessarily provisional.”

Cuthbert looked at her.

“Would you come to the course tomorrow morning?”

“I should love to.”

“I could show you the grip.”

“Thank you.”

“And the stance.”

“Thank you.”

“And perhaps, after that, we might talk about what I asked you before.”

Adeline lowered her eyes.

“The proposal?”

“Yes.”

“I think,” she said softly, “that my answer might also be provisional.”

This was not entirely clear, but Cuthbert, who had spent many months among literary people, had acquired a useful faculty for extracting plain meaning from complicated language.

“Do you mean yes?”

“Yes.”

Brusiloff leaped to his feet.

“Good! Fine! Marriageovitch! I give blessing. Also I come tomorrow. Nine o’clock. Cootaboot fix elbow.”

Mrs. Smethurst, who had by now adjusted herself to the altered social market, hurried forward and kissed her daughter. She had always liked Cuthbert, she said. Even when he broke Shelley, she had sensed something elemental in him.

Raymond Parsloe Devine slipped from the room.

His departure was noticed only by the elderly lady interested in rubber-cored balls, and she assumed that he had gone to fetch her another sandwich.

Cuthbert and Adeline were married in June.

The honeymoon was spent in Scotland, where Adeline learned that there are deeper emotions than those described by Sovietski and more searching tragedies than any devised by Nastikoff. On the third morning she took fourteen strokes in a bunker and came out a changed woman.

Within a year she had a handicap of twenty-four. Within two years it was sixteen. She ceased to speak of golf as physical symbolism and began to speak of it as golf, which is the final stage of intellectual development. She developed a low running approach of considerable merit, acquired three putters, and became capable of discussing for forty minutes the relative wickedness of those who talk during the swing and those who stand on the line of a putt.

The Smethurst drawing room changed, too. The bust of Shelley disappeared, and in its place there hung a framed photograph of Harry Vardon. The Literary and Ethical Society continued for a time, but attendance declined after Mrs. Smethurst joined the ladies’ section of the golf club and discovered that Wednesday was the day of the weekly medal.

As for Raymond Parsloe Devine, he left Wood Hills soon afterward. He settled in Chelsea and wrote a novel called *The Empty Fairway*, in which golf was treated as a symbol of man’s hopeless attempt to reach an unattainable green. It was praised by three critics who did not play the game and condemned by everybody who did.

Vladimir Brusiloff remained Cuthbert’s friend. He never succeeded in keeping his right elbow entirely under control, but the handkerchief treatment improved him sufficiently to reduce his handicap by six strokes. His next novel contained a long scene in which a nihilist, awaiting execution at dawn, explains the overlapping grip to the prison governor. The critics called the passage baffling but tremendous.

The Oldest Member paused.

Evening had settled over the course. Through the windows could be seen the final pair coming up the eighteenth, one of them in the rough, the other offering advice.

“You see, my boy,” said the Oldest Member, “golf is not the hollow and unprofitable pursuit which the ignorant suppose. It trains the character. It disciplines the emotions. It opens doors which remain closed to ordinary men. It brings nations together. It may even—”

The chair opposite him was empty.

The young man who had gone round in a hundred and twenty-seven was already halfway across the hall.

“Perkins!” he shouted. “Perkins! Don’t sell those clubs! I’ve changed my mind!”

The Oldest Member smiled gently and closed his eyes.

He had not finished the moral, but this rarely worried him. In golf, as in life, the important thing is to get the result.

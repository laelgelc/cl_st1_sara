## THE STRANGE BIRD IN THE REEDS

It was delightful in the country. Summer had come; the corn stood golden in the fields, the oats were green, and in the meadows the hay lay in sweet-smelling heaps. Beyond the fields rose a dark wood, and in the midst of the wood were deep, quiet pools, over which the dragon-flies darted like little blue sparks.

Close beside one of these pools stood an old farm-house. Burdock leaves grew so tall around it that a child might have hidden beneath them; and under the broadest leaves a duck had made her nest.

For many days she had sat patiently upon her eggs. It is tiresome work, even for the best-tempered duck, and she sometimes wondered why no one paid her a visit.

“At least they might come and say, ‘How do you do?’” she murmured. “But every one thinks only of himself.”

At length one egg cracked.

“Peep, peep!” cried a little yellow head, pushing its way through the shell.

Then another cracked, and another.

“Peep, peep! How large the world is!” cried the ducklings, looking about them beneath the green leaves.

“You imagine this is all the world?” said their mother. “It reaches far beyond the garden, and even beyond the clergyman’s field; though I have never been so far myself.”

Soon all the eggs were open except one, which was larger than the rest and of a strange grey colour.

The mother duck looked at it uneasily.

“This egg takes a terrible time,” said she. “Perhaps it is a turkey’s egg. I was once deceived in that manner, and the young turkey would not go into the water. I pushed and scolded, but all to no purpose.”

An old duck, who had come waddling by, bent her neck over the nest.

“Let me see the egg,” said she. “Ah! It is certainly a turkey’s egg. Leave it where it is and teach the other children to swim.”

“I have sat so long already,” replied the mother duck, “that I may as well remain another day.”

And so she remained.

The next morning the great egg cracked.

“Peep, peep!” said the young creature, tumbling out upon the leaves.

He was large, long-necked, grey, and awkward; his feet appeared too big for him, and his wings looked as though they had been fastened on in haste.

“What an enormous duckling!” exclaimed his mother. “He is not like the others. Still, perhaps he only needs time to improve.”

On the following day she led the whole family to the pool.

“Now, children, into the water!” she cried, and splash, splash! in they went.

The little yellow ducklings floated prettily. The grey one plunged in after them, and he swam more strongly than any of the rest. He could dive, turn, and glide through the water as though the pool had been his home for a hundred years.

“He is no turkey,” said his mother with satisfaction. “See how well he uses his legs! Indeed, if one does not look too closely, he is almost handsome.”

After their bath she led them across the yard, for the ducklings must be introduced into society.

The farm-yard was crowded and noisy. Two families were quarrelling over an eel’s head; hens scratched in the dust; pigeons strutted upon the roof; and a great turkey cock walked to and fro, swelling himself until his head became quite red.

“Keep together,” said the mother duck. “Bow politely to the old duck with the scarlet ribbon round her leg. She has Spanish blood and is the grandest person here. Do not turn your toes inward. A well-bred duckling carries his feet apart, like his father and mother. Now bow your necks and say, ‘Quack!’”

The ducklings obeyed.

The farm-yard creatures stared at them.

“More mouths!” cried one duck. “As if there were not enough of us already.”

“They are a pretty family,” said the old Spanish duck. “All except that large grey one. He is badly made.”

“He swims beautifully,” said the mother.

“What has swimming to do with appearance?” replied the grand duck. “He is much too big, and his feathers have no proper colour.”

Then a young drake ran forward and bit the grey duckling in the neck.

“Leave him alone!” cried his mother. “He has done no harm.”

“No,” said the drake, “but he is so ugly that he deserves to be teased.”

The turkey cock spread his tail, gobbled, and rushed at him. The hens pecked him; the ducks pushed him away from the food; and even his brothers and sisters whispered together.

“How unfortunate that you belong to us!” said one.

“I wish the cat would catch you,” said another.

The poor duckling did not understand what crime he had committed. He tried to be gentle. He stood aside when others wished to eat, and he bowed to those who were older; yet the more humbly he behaved, the worse they treated him.

The girl who fed the poultry kicked at him with her wooden shoe. The hens screamed when he came near. The turkey cock chased him round the water-trough, and his own brothers snapped at his wings.

At first his mother defended him.

“He may not be pretty,” she said, “but he has a good heart, and he swims as well as his father.”

But day followed day, and even she grew weary of the noise.

“I wish you had never come out of that egg,” she said at last.

Those words hurt him more than all the bites and blows.

That evening, when the sky was red behind the barn and the other birds had gone to rest, the poor ugly creature crept through a hole in the hedge and ran away.

The little birds in the bushes flew up in alarm.

“They are frightened because I am so hideous,” he thought.

He ran across the fields until his weak legs trembled. The wind shook the corn, the clouds covered the moon, and the whole great world seemed dark and lonely. At length he reached a wild moor, where rushes and reeds grew around a broad marsh. There he hid himself through the night.

Early in the morning two wild ducks discovered him.

“What sort of bird are you?” asked one.

The duckling bowed in every direction, for he wished to be polite.

“You are remarkably ugly,” said the other, “but that is of no concern to us, provided you do not marry into our family.”

The poor creature had never thought of marrying any one. He desired only to lie among the reeds and drink a little marsh water.

For two days he remained there. Then two young wild geese came to visit him. They were cheerful fellows, and they had not been long in the world.

“Listen, comrade,” said one. “You are so odd-looking that we like you. Come with us to another marsh. There are several charming young geese there, and not one of them is engaged. A bird with your face may make his fortune.”

At that instant there came a terrible sound.

“Pop! pop!”

One of the geese fell dead among the reeds, and the water turned red around him.

“Pop! pop!” sounded again.

Flocks of wild birds rose into the air. Guns cracked from every side, smoke drifted between the dark trees, and great dogs bounded through the mud with their tongues hanging out.

The duckling pressed himself flat against the ground. He dared not move; he scarcely dared to breathe.

A huge hunting dog pushed through the reeds directly before him. Its teeth were white and sharp, and its eyes were fierce. It opened its mouth, sniffed the duckling from head to foot—and then went away without touching him.

“Thank Heaven!” whispered the duckling. “I am so ugly that even a dog will not bite me.”

All day the shooting continued. It would be very sad, were I to relate every dreadful thing that happened upon the marsh. Not until evening did the hunters depart. Then the poor bird crept out from his hiding-place and hurried away as fast as the wind would permit.

A storm arose. The trees bent low, the rain beat upon the earth, and the duckling was blown over and over. Towards night he saw a little cottage with a crooked roof and one small window. The cottage leaned so much to the left that it seemed ready to fall, yet perhaps it could not decide in which direction to fall, and therefore remained standing.

The door had slipped from one hinge, leaving a narrow opening. Through this the duckling crawled.

In the cottage lived an old woman, a cat, and a hen. The cat was called His Grace because he carried himself with great dignity. He could arch his back, make sparks when his fur was stroked the wrong way, and purr so loudly that the cups rattled upon the shelf.

The hen was called Short-Leg. She laid excellent eggs and believed herself one of the most useful creatures in the world.

In the morning the cat saw the duckling.

“What is this?” he cried.

The hen clucked, and the old woman peered about; but her eyes were not good, and she thought the visitor might be a fat young duck who would one day lay eggs.

“This is fortunate,” said she. “Now we shall have duck’s eggs, unless it proves to be a drake. We must wait and see.”

So the duckling was allowed to remain for three weeks. No eggs appeared.

The cat and the hen considered themselves master and mistress of the house, and they always said, “We and the world,” for they imagined that they formed the better half of it.

“Can you lay eggs?” Short-Leg asked the duckling.

“No.”

“Then you should keep silent when sensible persons are speaking.”

The cat said, “Can you arch your back, purr, or send sparks from your fur?”

“No.”

“Then you have no right to hold an opinion before those who understand the world.”

The duckling sat sadly in the corner. One day the sunshine entered through the little window, and he remembered the broad marsh, the cool water, and the reeds whispering under the sky.

“How pleasant it would be to swim again!” he said. “To float upon the water, and to dive down until it closes over one’s head!”

“What strange ideas!” cried the hen. “You have nothing to do, and therefore foolish thoughts enter your mind. Lay an egg, or learn to purr, and they will pass.”

“But it is delightful to swim,” said the duckling, “and delightful to feel the water above one’s head.”

“Delightful!” repeated Short-Leg. “You must be mad. Ask His Grace, who is the wisest creature I know, whether he likes to dive. Ask our mistress. No one in the world knows more than they do.”

“You do not understand me,” said the duckling.

“If we do not understand you, who will? Surely you do not imagine yourself cleverer than a cat and a hen—not to mention the old woman! Be grateful, child. You have a warm room and excellent society.”

Yet the duckling could not forget the water. At length he crept out beneath the crooked door and went away.

Autumn had come. The leaves in the wood were yellow, crimson, and orange; the wind seized them and whirled them over the fields. The clouds hung low, and the ravens cried, “Croak, croak,” as if they knew the cold days were near.

The poor bird wandered from pool to pool. He swam whenever he could, and while he floated he forgot for a little time that no one loved him. But when other birds saw him, they cried, “What an ugly creature!” and flew away.

One evening, as the sun sank behind the dark trees, a sound came from the sky—a deep, musical cry, unlike the clucking of hens or the harsh quacking of ducks.

The duckling lifted his head.

From the reeds rose a flock of great white birds. Their necks curved gracefully, their broad wings shone in the red light, and they flew higher and higher toward the radiant clouds.

They were swans.

The duckling did not know their name. He only knew that he had never seen anything so beautiful. A strange joy and sorrow filled his heart together. He stretched his neck toward them and gave a cry so loud that he frightened himself.

The glorious birds flew on toward warmer countries and open waters, while he remained alone upon the dark pool.

Why did he love them? Why did he long to follow them? He could not tell. He did not envy their beauty; he would never have dreamed that he might be like them. He would have been thankful if only the farm-yard ducks had allowed him to live among them without blows.

Winter came.

The air grew colder and colder, and the water upon the pools began to freeze. The duckling was obliged to paddle continually to keep a little circle open around him. Each night the circle became smaller. The ice cracked beneath the frost, and the little bird’s legs grew weary.

At last he could move no longer. The water froze around him, and he lay helpless in the ice.

Early the next morning a peasant passed that way. He saw the bird, broke the ice with his wooden shoe, and carried him home beneath his coat.

The peasant’s children rejoiced.

“A duck!” they cried. “He shall play with us.”

Warmth returned to the bird’s body. But when the children reached for him, he believed they meant to hurt him. He flew in terror into the milk-pail, splashing milk over the floor. The peasant’s wife screamed and clapped her hands. Then he fluttered into a tub of flour, and came out as white as a miller; after that he knocked down a bowl, upset a stool, and flew against the window.

The children laughed and chased him.

“Catch him! Catch him!”

The woman came with the fire-tongs; the cat sprang upon the table; plates rattled, the baby cried, flour rose in clouds, and the duckling, not knowing where to turn, darted through the open door and escaped into the snow.

There he lay, exhausted and half frozen.

It would be too miserable to describe all the hardships of that winter. He sheltered beneath thorn bushes, in hollow trees, and once beneath an overturned boat. Often he had nothing to eat but a few frozen berries or a blade of grass uncovered by the wind. The snow fell deeper and deeper; the nights became longer; and sometimes he thought with longing of the farm-yard, though it had been so cruel, and of the crooked cottage, though no one there had understood him.

Yet life remained in him.

At length the sun grew warmer. Drops of water fell from the icicles; larks sang above the fields; and little green shoots pushed through the brown earth. Spring had returned.

The bird lay among the rushes, weak and sleepy, when a warm breeze passed over him. He spread his wings—and was astonished. They were no longer short and feeble. They were broad, strong, and bright. Before he knew what was happening, they lifted him into the air.

Up he rose, higher and higher, over the marsh, over the fields, and above the budding trees.

“How wonderful!” he cried. “The world is not closed to me any longer.”

He flew until he came to a beautiful garden. Apple-trees covered with white and pink blossoms bent over a clear stream; lilacs filled the air with sweetness; and upon the green banks children were scattering crumbs.

From behind a little island came three magnificent white swans.

They ruffled their feathers, raised their graceful necks, and glided over the water.

The poor bird knew them at once. They were the splendid travellers he had seen in autumn. At the sight of them the old longing returned with such strength that his heart seemed ready to break.

“I will go to them,” he thought. “They may strike me because I am ugly, but it is better to be killed by such noble birds than to be bitten by ducks, pecked by hens, scolded by a woman, and frozen alone in winter.”

He flew down to the stream.

The swans saw him and came toward him, their wings half lifted.

“Kill me,” said the poor bird, and bowed his head over the clear water.

But what did he see?

Not the clumsy grey creature whose reflection had so often troubled him. In the water appeared a white bird with shining feathers, a slender neck, dark gentle eyes, and broad, beautiful wings.

He looked again. The reflection moved when he moved. It bowed when he bowed.

He was himself a swan!

It matters little to be hatched in a duck’s nest, had one only lain in a swan’s egg.

The other swans surrounded him. One touched his neck kindly with its beak; another welcomed him with a soft cry; and the oldest said, “You have come late, young brother, but you are welcome.”

“Brother!” repeated the young swan. No one had ever called him that with love.

He remembered the farm-yard, the eel’s head, the proud duck with Spanish blood, the turkey cock, the hunting dog, the cat who could purr, and the hen who believed egg-laying to be the highest wisdom. He remembered the frozen pool and the long white fields.

Then he looked at the clear water, the blossoming trees, and the noble birds beside him.

He felt almost too happy.

The children upon the bank clapped their hands.

“There is a new swan!” cried the youngest.

The others ran to fetch more bread and cake.

“Look at him! Look at him!”

“He is the whitest!”

“No, the most graceful!”

“The new one is the loveliest of all!”

Their parents came to the water’s edge and agreed that the young swan was very beautiful. Even the old swans bowed before him, and the branches above stirred in the warm wind as if they too offered their greeting.

The young swan hid his head beneath one wing. He did not know what to do. He had suffered so much that praise made him shy. He was happy, but not proud; for a heart that has known true sorrow does not easily become vain.

He thought, “When I was the ugly bird, I never dreamed of such happiness.”

The sun shone warmly upon his feathers. The lilacs bent over the clear stream, and the apple blossoms drifted down like soft snow. He raised his strong wings, curved his graceful neck, and from the depths of his heart came a joyous cry.

Meanwhile, far away, the farm-yard continued as before.

The old duck with the scarlet ribbon still spoke of her Spanish blood. The turkey cock still swelled whenever a small bird passed him. The hens still quarrelled over scraps, and the ducklings—now grown—still believed that whatever looked strange must surely be worthless.

One morning a flock of swans crossed the sky above them.

The farm-yard creatures stopped and stared.

“How magnificent!” cried the ducks.

“What noble wings!” said the hens.

“I could fly in precisely that manner if I chose,” declared the turkey cock, though he remained firmly on the ground.

Among the swans was the youngest and most beautiful of the flock. He glanced down and recognized the old roof, the pond, the burdock leaves, and the yard in which he had once been chased.

For one moment the memory of his unhappiness returned. Then he looked at his companions flying beside him, and he felt no anger. The farm-yard had judged him according to the little knowledge it possessed; he had once judged himself in the same manner.

Had he remained there for ever, he might have believed them.

But the wind carried him onward over green meadows and golden fields, over dark woods and shining lakes. His wings beat strongly; the white flock called to one another; and all the wide blue heaven lay open before him.

He was no longer the creature who crept beneath hedges or hid among reeds. He had found his own kind, and, what was more, he had found himself.

Yet he never forgot those who were lonely.

When he saw a small bird driven from a feeding-place, he made room beside him. When a young swan was awkward and plain, he did not laugh, for he knew that youth wears many disguises. And when the older birds praised his beauty, he listened modestly, remembering that beauty had not fed him in winter, nor comforted him when he was despised; courage had carried him through, and hope—though he had scarcely known its name—had kept his heart alive.

Thus his sorrow was not lost. It had taught him tenderness, and tenderness made his happiness greater.

The children visited the garden every day. They brought grain and white bread, and in time the young swan came when they called. They named him Silver-Wing, though in the sunshine his feathers were whiter than silver.

“Do you think he knows he is beautiful?” asked one little girl.

“Certainly,” said her brother. “He can see himself in the water.”

But the youngest child shook her head.

“I think,” she said, “he looks into the water to be sure the lonely grey bird is not there.”

The young swan heard her, for birds in fairy tales understand more than people suppose. He looked down into the stream. The beautiful white image looked back at him, but behind it, deep in his memory, he saw the frightened duckling he had been.

He did not despise that poor grey creature. Without him there could have been no Silver-Wing; without the dark winter, spring would not have seemed so radiant; without rejection, welcome could never have felt so sweet.

Then the other swans called him, and he joined them upon the stream.

They sailed beneath the apple-trees, while the water shone blue between their white forms. The clouds were reflected below, the birds were reflected among the clouds, and it seemed as though they floated both upon the earth and in the sky.

The young swan lifted his head.

Never had the country appeared so delightful. Never had the reeds whispered so gently, the sun shone so brightly, or the world seemed so wide.

And this time, when he cried aloud, it was not from fear or loneliness, but from pure happiness.

For it matters not where a noble nature first opens its eyes, nor what foolish tongues may say of it. The little grey bird had been a swan from the beginning, though no one—not even he himself—had known it.

Now he knew, and he was loved.

What more could any heart desire?

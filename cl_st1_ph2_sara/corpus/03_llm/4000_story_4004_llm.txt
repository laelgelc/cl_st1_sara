### THE CONTINUATION COMMITTEE

It began, so far as I can remember, with apricots.

We were sitting in Poll’s garden on a June afternoon, and the apricots, which had been arranged upon a blue dish in order of ripeness, gave us that sensation of wealth which comes to young women when the weather is fine, the rent has somehow been paid, and no one has yet asked them what they intend to do with their lives. There were six of us then: Poll, Jane, Castalia, Helen, Rose, and myself, Cassandra. We had read very little and believed a great deal.

We believed, for instance, in Men.

This belief had not been taught to us exactly. It had been breathed upon us. It was in the curtains, the sermons, the biographies with gilt edges, the remarks made after dinner, the statues in public squares, the fact that fathers shut their study doors, and the tone in which mothers said, “Your father is tired.” Men, we understood, made laws, discovered continents, wrote books, commanded ships, built bridges, and thought. Women—our mothers especially—admired these activities, prepared meals for those engaged in them, and produced fresh men when the supply ran low.

The division seemed at once natural and magnificent.

“Think of Shakespeare,” Rose said, taking the largest apricot.

“And Newton,” said Helen.

“And Napoleon,” said Castalia.

“Napoleon?” Jane objected.

“He did a great many things,” said Castalia.

“Killed people,” said Poll.

We turned to her, for Poll had lately become difficult. Her father, who had died the winter before after a life spent acquiring obligations for others, had inserted into his will a clause requiring his daughter to read every book in the London Library. Why he did this no one knew. Perhaps he had wished to educate her. Perhaps he had wished to punish the Library. He had been an original man, and original men, we had been told, are not required to explain themselves.

Poll had begun obediently with the letter A.

She now came into rooms with dust on her cuffs and an expression of nausea. While we praised the genius of men, she scraped her apricot stone with a thumbnail.

“Have you read them?” she asked.

“Read whom?”

“The men.”

We laughed. It seemed an absurd question. One did not read Men; one revered them. They existed collectively, like the Alps or the Navy.

“I have read some Shakespeare,” Rose said.

“Which?”

“Parts.”

“And Newton?”

Helen admitted that she had not read Newton, but had seen his tomb.

“That proves nothing,” said Poll. “There are very fine tombs for dreadful people.”

“What do the books say?” I asked.

Poll looked toward the house. The windows of her father’s study shone with a grave and inherited light. Shelves rose there from floor to ceiling, and books stood in double ranks like soldiers concealed behind soldiers.

“Most of them,” she said, “are rubbish.”

There was a silence. A bee pushed its head into a foxglove.

“Rubbish?” Rose repeated.

“Dreadful rubbish. Men have written twelve volumes about the use of the semicolon in provincial charters. They have written lives of admirals who never won battles, answers to answers to replies concerning baptism, and poems about women whom they plainly never met. They contradict one another. They quote one another. Some have no facts; the rest have too many. And all expect to be read.”

“But perhaps you began badly,” Jane suggested. “A may be an inferior letter.”

“I tried M yesterday.”

“Milton!” cried Helen.

“Military Buttons, Manufacture of,” Poll replied. “Three hundred and twelve pages.”

This disturbed us. Yet youth is resourceful in defending what it has been told, and we immediately pointed out that bad books were surely the by-products of civilisation, just as smoke was the by-product of trains. If men produced some nonsense, they also produced Paradise Lost, parliamentary government, vaccination, oil painting, moral philosophy, the steam engine, and the British Empire.

Poll said she had reached a book which proved that parliamentary government was a failure, another which proved vaccination was murder, and four which disagreed about moral philosophy.

“But they cannot all be right,” I said.

“No,” said Poll. “That is what makes the shelves so long.”

Castalia, who had been lying on the grass with her shoes off, sat up.

“Then what are they doing?”

“Writing books.”

“But why?”

“To prove that the other books are wrong.”

This answer, though simple, had a curious effect upon us. The garden changed. The blue dish, the foxgloves, Poll’s sensible shoes, even the white clouds above the elms appeared to be awaiting an explanation. We had always supposed that men thought in order that civilisation might advance. Now it seemed possible that civilisation advanced chiefly in order to give men something to think about.

Jane, who could never endure uncertainty without appointing officers, fetched a notebook.

“We must investigate,” she said.

“Investigate what?”

“Men.”

“How?”

“Systematically.”

This word reassured us. System had protected many enterprises from their results.

We drew our chairs into a circle. The first question, Jane said, was what men were for. We objected that such language was indelicate. Women were for something—beauty, consolation, maternity, the home—but men simply were. Yet, once asked, the question would not go back into silence.

“Men produce good books,” Helen proposed.

“And good people,” said Rose.

“Women produce the people,” Poll said.

“But men make them good.”

“Do they?”

Here we became confused, since all our knowledge of good people came from books written by men, while most actual men of our acquaintance spent their evenings asleep, irritated, or discussing money.

At length Jane wrote upon the first page:

**THE SOCIETY FOR DETERMINING WHETHER CIVILISED MEN PRODUCE GOOD PEOPLE AND GOOD BOOKS**

The title occupied four lines and looked so authoritative that for a moment our inquiry seemed complete.

“We must pledge ourselves,” Jane said.

“To what?”

“To impartiality.”

Poll objected that impartiality was what people claimed when they wanted their prejudices to sit in the judge’s chair. So we pledged ourselves instead to doubt everything equally.

Then Castalia, whose ideas came like swallows and often flew into windows, said, “We ought not to have children until we know.”

“Know what?”

“Whether the human race should continue.”

Rose dropped her apricot.

It was one thing to question books and another to interfere with the human race, which we had imagined as a vast procession carrying banners through history, with women standing at intervals to provide refreshments. But Castalia insisted. If women bore children merely because men told them civilisation was splendid, and civilisation consisted of bad books, bad laws, battles, buttons, and explanations of explanations, then maternity was not a duty but an endorsement. One should not sign a document without reading it.

We were unmarried, which made renunciation easier. Nevertheless we stood, joined hands, and swore that until our inquiry was completed none of us would bear a child.

The bee came out of the foxglove covered in pollen. I remember this because later Poll called it a hostile witness.

Our first difficulty was access. Men’s institutions, though built for the benefit of mankind, admitted only selected portions of mankind, generally those wearing trousers, uniforms, wigs, ribbons, or the correct school tie. We could not all enter Parliament, the universities, the courts, the clubs, the exchanges, the laboratories, the galleries after closing time, or the private rooms in which public affairs were arranged. We therefore divided the world among us and adopted disguises.

Jane took Law and Government because she liked rules. Helen took Art and Scholarship because she had beautiful handwriting. Rose took Commerce and Empire because an uncle owned ships. Poll retained Literature, having no choice. Castalia chose Science, Religion, and the Army, which was considered excessive, but she said they all concerned invisible forces. I took Education and the Navy, since my cousin Edward knew a schoolmaster and an admiral’s secretary.

We agreed to report monthly and to accept no general statement unsupported by particulars, except where particulars were confidential, imperial, or too expensive to obtain.

For five years we investigated.

I cannot give you all our findings. They filled thirteen ledgers, nine portfolios, a hatbox, and the back of a menu from the Criterion. Some were lost when Rose’s aunt mistook them for waste paper and wrapped pears in them. Others became illegible after Castalia left a window open during a storm. Perhaps this was fortunate. Knowledge, like pastry, is sometimes improved by being incomplete.

I began with Education.

My cousin’s schoolmaster, Mr. Pritchard, received me in a room containing maps, canes, and the busts of dead Romans. He explained that boys were educated to become men of character.

“What is character?” I asked.

“It is what the Empire rests upon.”

“What does it rest upon?”

“Character.”

I wrote this down.

He showed me forty boys translating a Latin account of a battle. None knew where the battle had taken place, why it had occurred, or whether the account was true, but three knew that a verb in the second sentence was irregular. Mr. Pritchard said this trained the mind.

“For what?”

“For life.”

“What part of life?”

“The important part.”

At luncheon the boys ate boiled meat and struck one another with spoons. One small boy, who wrote poetry, had been made to sit on the floor because he had cried when a blackbird was killed. Another, who had broken a cat’s leg, was captain of the house. Mr. Pritchard explained that sentimentality must be discouraged, while leadership should be recognised early.

I asked whether girls were trained in character.

“No,” he said, smiling. “Girls have influence.”

“What is influence?”

“What the home rests upon.”

“And what does the home rest upon?”

“Women.”

I noted that civilisation rested upon character, character upon civilisation, the home upon women, women upon influence, and influence, so far as I could discover, upon never asking what influence was.

My naval inquiry proved more technical.

Admiral Sir Harley Tightboots had spent forty years preserving peace by preparing for war. He had blue eyes, a red face, and an honourable inability to sit comfortably. When I asked what naval honour meant, he replied that it could not be defined but must be instantly defended.

“Against whom?”

“Anyone who questions it.”

“How is it defended?”

“With force, if necessary.”

“Then honour is something which becomes stronger when another person is injured?”

He said I had the female mind.

To make matters clearer, he told me a story about a cadet who had failed to report another cadet for stealing marmalade. The first cadet was caned for disloyalty, the second for theft, and a third for knowing of the incident but refusing to say whether he knew of it.

“Which was honourable?” I asked.

“The Service.”

He then showed me a gun capable of sending a shell through twelve inches of armour at a distance of eight miles. It was a marvel of civilisation. The sailors who polished it earned less in a week than the admiral spent upon wine at dinner, but this, he said, belonged to Economics and was outside the naval sphere.

Jane’s account of Law was more impressive. She attended trials in a hat so large that even guilt hesitated before it.

Judges, she reported, sat high above everyone else and were treated with the respect accorded in ancient times to speaking animals. Their utterances were recorded, their mistakes became precedents, and their jokes caused barristers to laugh before they understood them.

One case concerned a woman who had taken bread because her children were hungry. The judge explained that property was the basis of civilisation and sentenced her to prison.

Another concerned a gentleman who had taken money entrusted to him by widows. His counsel explained that he had been under severe financial strain owing to the upkeep of two houses and a racehorse. The judge said temptation increases with responsibility and gave him six months in the country for his health.

“Was that prison?” Rose asked.

“No,” said Jane. “His doctor recommended travel.”

Jane asked a barrister whether laws were just.

“They are the law,” he replied.

“But are they just?”

“If they were not just, Parliament could change them.”

“Does Parliament change unjust laws?”

“When public opinion requires it.”

“Who forms public opinion?”

“Responsible men.”

“Who decides which men are responsible?”

“The law.”

Jane said she had filled seventeen pages before she realised they had returned to the beginning.

Her Parliamentary investigation was interrupted by a debate on national economy. A member spoke for two hours to prove that expenditure must be reduced, after which the House voted money for a monument commemorating economy. Another member declared that the nation’s prosperity depended upon cheap food, but opposed cheap foreign wheat because it would ruin agriculture, and cheap domestic wheat because it would ruin farmers. Lord Bunkum, speaking from a red bench, said the poor must learn thrift; his estate, we later discovered, had been rescued three times by public funds.

“What is government for?” Jane asked him at dinner.

“To safeguard liberty.”

“Whose?”

“British liberty.”

“Does everyone possess it?”

“Certainly.”

“Can women vote?”

He looked pained. “My dear young lady, liberty must not be confused with power.”

We debated this distinction for most of July and failed to find it.

Helen meanwhile entered Scholarship by becoming secretary to Professor Amelius Wort, a celebrated authority on Sappho. For twenty-three years Professor Wort had been preparing a definitive study proving that Sappho was perfectly respectable.

“Why does it matter?” Helen asked.

“My dear Miss Helen,” he said, “nothing matters more than the moral reputation of great literature.”

“Does the poetry improve if she was respectable?”

“The poetry is immortal.”

“Then how can her reputation harm it?”

Professor Wort removed his spectacles and polished them. This action, Helen observed, served scholars as smoke serves the cuttlefish.

The whole argument turned upon a small bronze object found on an island and described variously as a hairpin, a religious emblem, a fishhook, or part of a door. Professor Wort believed it was a hairpin and that its position proved Sappho wore her hair in the manner of an honourable woman.

“Where was it found?” Helen asked.

“In a trench.”

“Near Sappho?”

“Near a structure conjectured to occupy the site of a house mentioned by a writer who may have seen a copy of a lost inscription.”

“And this proves—?”

“Chastity.”

He had six assistants, two of whom doubted the hairpin privately but hoped for university appointments and therefore doubted it without publication.

In the galleries Helen found men explaining why great painters had painted women without clothes. The reason was Beauty. When women removed their clothes outside paintings, however, the reason was Immorality. A sculptor told her that the female body was the most perfect form in nature, though women’s minds were incapable of understanding form. A critic, confronted with a canvas composed of a brown square and three yellow streaks, called it a courageous assault upon convention. Asked whether it was good, he said goodness was a bourgeois category.

“But you recommend people buy it,” Helen said.

“Certainly.”

“Why?”

“Because it will be important.”

“How do you know?”

“Because people will buy it.”

Rose’s investigation of Commerce began at her uncle’s shipping office, where a large map showed red routes running over blue seas to countries painted green, pink, ochre, and yellow. Small brass flags indicated mines, plantations, ports, and disturbances.

“What are disturbances?” she asked.

“Resistance to order,” her uncle replied.

“What order?”

“Trade.”

He explained that trade carried civilisation to backward peoples. It carried rifles, spirits, cloth, machines, missionaries, and account books outward, and carried rubber, copper, tea, cotton, gold, and profit homeward. The backward peoples received the benefits of labour and taxation.

“What if they do not want them?”

“They don’t understand their interests.”

“And if they refuse?”

“We protect our investments.”

“With rifles?”

“Only in the last resort.”

Rose asked what the first resort was.

“Firmness.”

“And what is firmness?”

“The knowledge that rifles are available.”

At the factory attached to the enterprise, women worked ten hours a day making shirts they could not afford to buy. The owner said wages must remain low because the market was competitive. His own income had risen because the market rewarded initiative. When Rose asked whether the women showed initiative by asking for higher wages, he said they had been misled by agitators.

The foreman dismissed a girl for arriving late after her child fell ill. Punctuality, he explained, was the soul of business. The owner arrived at noon.

Rose wrote in her report: “Commerce appears to be the art by which one man’s necessity becomes another man’s merit.”

Poll’s findings were less orderly because books, unlike factories, multiply in the dark.

She read histories in which every nation was brave, every defeated general unlucky, every invasion necessary, and every massacre an unfortunate excess committed by subordinates. She read biographies showing that genius was proved by neglecting one’s wife, borrowing from one’s friends, and dying in debt. She read novels in which women were angels until they spoke, temptresses when they moved, and symbols when they died.

“What do they want us to be?” Castalia asked.

“Everything they are not,” Poll replied, “and nothing that inconveniences them.”

She read reviewers too. One praised a book because it was unlike life; another condemned it for the same reason. A third declared that judgment must be left to posterity, while advising posterity to purchase the second edition immediately.

Poll invited a famous reviewer, Mr. Basil Quoin, to tea.

“What makes a book good?” we asked.

“Style.”

“What makes style good?”

“Personality.”

“What makes personality good?”

“Experience.”

“What experience?”

“Life.”

“What is life?”

“Material for books.”

He drank three cups of tea and afterward wrote that modern young women possessed curiosity but lacked charm.

Poll’s father’s will drove her from theology to travel, from beetles to banking, from elegies to encyclopedias. At meetings she quoted fragments until they collided:

“Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, and government of the people by the people, to strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s, in Xanadu did Kubla Khan take up the white man’s burden—”

“Stop!” we cried.

“I can’t,” said Poll. “They continue in my sleep.”

Yet there were wonderful books. That troubled her more than the dreadful ones. For every hundred pages of vanity, dullness, spite, and furniture, a sentence opened suddenly like a door in a wall and showed the sea. Men had imagined pity, justice, courage, freedom, and love with a splendour they seldom displayed at breakfast.

“How can they write what they cannot do?” Rose asked.

“How can we admire what we cannot trust?” said Helen.

We recorded both questions and answered neither.

Castalia’s enquiries were naturally the most dangerous.

She visited a laboratory where Dr. Septimus Crank had discovered a method of killing rats by exposing them to a violet ray. He hoped, with increased funding, to adapt the process to moths, microbes, and enemy personnel. On one bench a mouse trembled inside a wire cage.

“Why hurt it?” Castalia asked.

“For knowledge.”

“What knowledge?”

“The effect of pain upon the nervous system.”

“What is the effect?”

“Pain.”

Dr. Crank said she misunderstood science, which was morally neutral.

“If you discovered a way to destroy a city by pressing a button, would that be morally neutral?”

“Entirely. Pressing it would be a political decision.”

“Who advises politicians?”

“Scientists.”

This seemed to Castalia an elegant arrangement, since it allowed intelligence to supply the button and morality to take responsibility for the finger.

Yet science also purified water, prevented fever, restored sight, measured stars, and made invisible things visible. Castalia watched a surgeon save a child whom everyone had expected to die. She came to our meeting radiant.

“They are magnificent!” she cried.

The next month she visited a hospital where poor women waited twelve hours for treatment because there were not enough beds. A donor had given ten thousand pounds for a marble entrance bearing his name, but nothing for nurses, since corridors were permanent and nurses might marry.

“They are monsters!” she cried.

We reminded her that an investigation required consistency.

“Then reality should provide it,” she said.

Religion fared no better. One clergyman told her poverty purified the soul; another collected subscriptions to relieve it. One preached that women’s highest vocation was motherhood but opposed public assistance for mothers because dependence weakened character. A bishop explained that war was contrary to Christian teaching, though soldiers dying in a just war went directly to heaven.

“How do we know a war is just?” Castalia asked.

“Our leaders determine it.”

“Are leaders always just?”

“We must pray that they are.”

“And if they are not?”

“Prayer is especially necessary.”

At an officers’ dinner she asked a colonel why nations fought.

“For honour.”

“What is honour?”

“That which no nation can surrender.”

“What do nations want from the enemy?”

“Surrender.”

“But if surrender is dishonourable, how can you ask honourable men to surrender?”

The colonel said women had no sense of logic.

During the fourth year our meetings became less cheerful. Evidence accumulated without forming conclusions. Men were brave and cowardly, truthful and fraudulent, tender and brutal; they composed symphonies and prospectuses, cured disease and invented improved explosives, gave their fortunes to colleges and paid seamstresses starvation wages. The same man might admire liberty before luncheon, exploit a clerk after it, and weep at the opera in the evening. Civilisation appeared not as a temple, which was how we had imagined it, but as a drawing room connected by hidden pipes to a slaughterhouse.

Still, we laughed. It was our method of breathing.

Then Castalia broke the vow.

She arrived late one November evening, shook rain from her coat, refused tea, accepted cake, refused cake, and at last said, “I am going to have a child.”

Jane dropped the minutes.

Poll said, “Oh, bother.”

Helen kissed her.

Rose asked who the father was.

These three actions occurred almost simultaneously, and perhaps represent the principal schools of moral philosophy.

Castalia told us she had fallen in love with William Dace, a young astronomer who played the flute badly and believed the universe was expanding. They had intended to be careful. They had also intended to marry, though not necessarily in that order. Now, she said, the facts had rearranged the principles.

“You swore,” Jane said, touching the notebook.

“Yes.”

“Then the Society must decide what is to be done.”

“Nothing can be undone,” Castalia replied.

We spoke of chastity, though none of us knew exactly what it was. Men praised it in women, mocked it in men, represented it in marble, guarded it with laws, and destroyed it in private. Was chastity a physical condition, a moral quality, a reputation, an absence, a form of obedience, or simply whatever prevented neighbours from talking?

Professor Wort, when consulted anonymously, said chastity was an inward spiritual state evidenced by outward conformity.

Mr. Quoin said it was no subject for ladies.

Sir Harley Tightboots said the Navy upheld it.

Lord Bunkum said civilisation depended upon it, though Rose had seen him leaving a hotel with an actress.

Poll turned to Castalia. “Do you feel impure?”

“No,” she said. “I feel sick before breakfast.”

“Do you love him?”

“At present.”

“Does he love you?”

“He says eternally, but he is an astronomer.”

“Will you marry him?”

“If marriage is to protect the child. Not if it is to punish us.”

Jane insisted that a vow was a vow. Castalia replied that vows made in ignorance should not be worshipped merely because ignorance had signed them. Helen said chastity without truth was vanity. Rose said truth without kindness could become cruelty. I said we must distinguish between principles governing conduct and principles used to govern other people’s conduct.

Poll closed the ledger.

“We began by refusing to accept men’s words without evidence,” she said. “Now we are using their words against Castalia.”

So we abolished chastity.

Not the practice, you understand; only the word. We could find no definition which did not make honesty shameful, desire criminal, marriage a receipt, or women responsible for everything men wished to forget. Jane entered in the minutes: “Members shall henceforth be judged, if judgment is unavoidable, by candour, kindness, and consequences.”

Castalia married William in a plain dress. He was charming, absent-minded, and poor. At the reception he explained that the light now reaching us from certain stars had begun its journey before human beings existed.

“Then the stars do not know about us,” Rose said.

“Not yet.”

This comforted us.

The child was to be born in August.

But before August, a statesman was murdered in a country several railway changes away, an ambassador sent a note to another ambassador, newspapers printed maps, ministers cancelled holidays, crowds gathered under lamps, and men who had spent years explaining that commerce made war impossible began explaining why honour made peace impossible.

“What war?” Castalia asked when William came in pale from the street.

“The war.”

“Which war?”

“The European war.”

“What is it about?”

He mentioned alliances, frontiers, mobilisation, ports, treaties, an archduke, and necessity.

“Who wants it?”

“No one.”

“Then why will it happen?”

“Because no one can stop it.”

We called an emergency meeting. Jane wrote to Parliament. Rose telephoned her uncle. I sought Sir Harley. Poll searched the newspapers for a reason. Helen asked Professor Wort what civilisation required. Castalia, seven months pregnant, sat beside the window while boys marched below shouting.

Parliament said the nation had been forced to defend peace by declaring war.

Rose’s uncle said war was disastrous for trade but admitted that shipping contracts might improve.

Professor Wort suspended his work on Sappho to compose a pamphlet proving that classical civilisation supported our side.

Sir Harley’s secretary said the admiral was delighted—professionally, of course.

The newspapers spoke of destiny, duty, sacrifice, the cleansing flame, the hour of trial, the rights of small nations, the perfidy of large nations, civilisation, liberty, honour, necessity, and God. Each word had appeared in our ledgers. None explained why William must leave his telescope and learn to kill a man whose name he did not know.

“Ask them what victory means,” Poll said.

We asked.

It meant the defeat of the enemy.

“What does defeat mean?”

“That they can no longer resist.”

“What do we want them to accept?”

“A just peace.”

“Why can we not offer it now?”

“Because they would think us weak.”

So strength required war in order to obtain the peace which weakness was not permitted to propose.

William enlisted. He said everyone was going and he could not remain behind.

“Why not?” Castalia asked.

“Honour.”

We groaned.

“I know,” he said. “But there it is.”

The baby, Ann, was born while guns were firing across Belgium. Castalia laboured for thirty hours in a room where the curtains would not close properly. The doctor arrived late because his motorcar had been requisitioned. William received leave for one afternoon and held the child as though she were a star whose light had unexpectedly reached him.

“She is extraordinary,” he said.

“She is ordinary,” Castalia replied. “That is extraordinary enough.”

He returned to France the next morning.

Our Society ceased meeting regularly. There was no need to investigate institutions; institutions came to us with forms. We filled forms for hospitals, ration books, separation allowances, missing men, dead men, damaged men, men whose bodies returned without their minds, and women whose minds continued after everything else had been removed.

Rose organised a canteen. Helen drove an ambulance. Jane worked in an office which counted casualties and called them returns. Poll wrapped books for soldiers, though she tried to exclude the dreadful ones and was reprimanded for discrimination. I visited families with official envelopes. Castalia raised Ann beneath the expanding map of the front.

Men performed acts of astonishing courage. They carried wounded strangers through fire, shared water, sang in mud, and died protecting friends. Men also ordered assaults they knew would fail, falsified reports, sold defective boots, imprisoned objectors, and wrote poems about the beauty of sacrifice from hotels twenty miles behind the line.

Civilisation produced poison gas, artificial limbs, blood transfusion, improved artillery, canned milk, propaganda, mobile surgery, barbed wire, and statistics. Science saved the wounded so that strategy might wound them again. Commerce supplied both sides when it could. Religion blessed flags. Scholarship discovered that the enemy had always been barbarous. Art designed posters of radiant women pointing men toward death.

Once Poll read aloud from a newspaper: “Our losses were comparatively light.”

“How many?” Castalia asked.

“Eight thousand.”

“Compared with what?”

No one answered.

William survived two years. Then a telegram said he was missing. Another said presumed dead. A third corrected the spelling of his middle name.

Castalia did not cry when the telegrams came. She folded them and put them beneath Ann’s socks. Several months later, when Ann broke a blue cup, Castalia sat on the kitchen floor and wept until there were no tears left for the cup or William or civilisation.

I will not describe the remainder. You know it, or think you do. There was rain, mud, influenza, queues, black dresses, white faces, speeches, offensives, retreats, victories which resembled defeats, defeats called strategic adjustments, and finally bells.

Peace was signed in a hall of mirrors.

This seemed suitable.

Five years after the bells, Castalia and I found the Society’s papers in a cupboard. We had gone to Poll’s old house to clear it, for Poll had at last completed the London Library—or as much of it as could be completed before new books arrived—and had departed for a cottage where she intended to read nothing but seed catalogues.

The garden was wet. The foxgloves had spread beyond their bed. Inside, dust lay upon the table where we had once set down apricot stones and decided the fate of the human race.

Castalia opened the first ledger.

“‘Whether civilised men produce good people and good books,’” she read. “What children we were.”

“We were not entirely wrong.”

“No. That was the trouble.”

We went through the reports. Here was Sir Harley’s honour, Professor Wort’s hairpin, Lord Bunkum’s liberty, Dr. Crank’s neutral button, Mr. Pritchard’s character, the judge’s property, the critic’s yellow streaks. We laughed, but not as before. Jokes age strangely after graves have been dug. Some become cruel; others become evidence.

“Our mistake,” Castalia said, “was thinking men did it alone.”

“Did what?”

“All of it.”

She waved toward the ledgers, the books, the world outside.

“We treated them as a separate species—as though they sprang at twenty-one from university courtyards, fully clothed and carrying dispatch boxes. But women bore them. Women fed them, washed them, praised them, excused them, believed them. We taught boys that courage meant not crying and girls that goodness meant making boys comfortable. Then we wondered why the men could not grieve and the women could not act.”

“That hardly makes women responsible for war.”

“Not responsible in the same way. But useful to it. We made the bodies. We made the homes from which they escaped to become important. We listened when they spoke nonsense and called it intellect because they were tired and we loved them. We freed them from the harmless work.”

“What harmless work?”

“Children.”

She said men should bear them.

The suggestion was not anatomical, she explained, or not entirely. She proposed that every statesman, general, professor, industrialist, bishop, and critic be required to spend nine months carrying the consequence of one public decision beneath his waistcoat. Cabinet meetings would be shorter. Declarations of war would be postponed by nausea. Admirals would calculate bombardments between contractions. Professors would abandon chastity to discuss feeding times. Economists would discover that dependence is not idleness when it wakes screaming at three in the morning.

“And if they actually had the children?” I asked.

“They might be occupied for twenty years.”

“Harmlessly?”

“Well,” she admitted, “comparatively.”

We pictured Lord Bunkum nursing twins on the red benches; Sir Harley pausing an engagement because the baby had swallowed a button; Dr. Crank defending the moral neutrality of porridge while it ran down his sleeve. We laughed until Castalia wiped her eyes.

Then Ann came in.

She was five years old, with one stocking falling down and a doll carried upside down by the leg. The doll’s face had been coloured purple with pencil, and one arm was missing.

“What happened to her?” I asked.

“She went to war,” Ann said.

Castalia looked at me.

Children, we discovered, inherit civilisation before they can spell it.

We spread the papers over the floor. Ann sat among them and gave her doll tea from an empty thimble. She could read simple words already. Poll had taught her, asserting that if children must enter the world, they should at least be issued weapons.

“What shall we do with all this?” I asked.

“Burn it,” Castalia said.

But she did not mean it. To burn evidence because it had failed to save us would be to imitate the men who destroyed inconvenient records and then wrote history from memory.

“Publish it?”

“Who would review it?”

“Mr. Quoin is dead.”

“There are always more Quoins.”

We considered depositing the papers in a university, but Professor Wort’s successor had recently published eight hundred pages proving that Wort’s hairpin was probably a buckle. We considered Parliament, but Parliament was busy preserving peace by enlarging the air force. We considered the Library, but Poll had forbidden us to increase its suffering.

Ann held up a page.

“What does ‘civilised’ mean?”

Castalia and I looked at one another.

“It means—” I began.

“Don’t tell her unless you know,” Castalia said.

I did not know.

Ann sounded out the Society’s title, stumbling over “determining.” Then she asked why we wanted to know whether people were good.

“So that we could decide whether to have children,” Castalia said.

Ann accepted this with the calm of one who has not yet learned which facts adults consider improper.

“Did you decide?”

“No.”

“But you had me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Castalia took the doll and turned it right side up.

“Because people do not always wait for conclusions.”

This satisfied Ann less than we hoped.

It occurred to us then that our Society had failed because it sought a final answer, as men’s books did. Good or bad, honourable or dishonourable, civilised or savage, chaste or impure: we had borrowed their boxes and been astonished when life would not lie still inside them. Perhaps inquiry should not end in a verdict. Perhaps it should end in another person capable of asking better questions.

“Ann shall have the papers,” I said.

Castalia stared at me.

“She is rather young.”

“All presidents are inadequate at first.”

Ann looked up. “President of what?”

“The Continuation Committee,” I said, inventing it.

Jane would have objected to the title, but Jane was in Geneva attending a conference designed to abolish future wars by arranging the chairs alphabetically, and could not stop us.

We explained that the Committee’s purpose was to discover how people might think without using thought as a weapon, work without turning necessity into slavery, love without ownership, raise children without teaching them contempt, and make books that did not conceal life beneath reputation.

This was perhaps too much for a child who still believed chairs suffered when sat upon roughly.

“Must I?” Ann asked.

“No,” said Castalia at once.

“Then why give it to me?”

“Because you can read,” I said.

That was the whole danger. Once a girl could read, not merely pronounce the sentences approved for her but compare one sentence with another, place the sermon beside the wage book, the patriotic speech beside the casualty list, the poem beside the poet’s life, the law beside the hungry child—once she could do that, belief ceased to be obedience. Letters were small things, black marks, hardly more formidable than ants; yet they crawled into inherited certainties and carried them away crumb by crumb.

Literacy had led Poll into the Library and us into doubt. It had not made us wise. It had made ignorance uncomfortable.

Castalia gathered the ledgers and tied them with faded ribbon. She placed them in Ann’s arms. The pile reached from the child’s waist to her chin.

“You are to believe in yourself,” Castalia said.

This was a phrase mothers had lately begun to use when they could no longer believe in the world.

Ann’s eyes widened above the papers.

“Always?”

“As often as possible.”

“What if I am wrong?”

“Then change your mind.”

“What if everyone says I am wrong?”

“Ask for evidence.”

“What if they have evidence?”

“Read it.”

“What if it is dreadful?”

“Most of it will be,” I said.

Ann considered this. Her doll slipped from beneath her elbow and fell headfirst upon the carpet. One ledger followed, then another; Parliament struck the Navy, Scholarship slid under Commerce, Chastity opened face down upon the war, and the minutes of our first apricot meeting scattered beneath the table.

Ann burst into tears.

Whether she cried because the doll had fallen, because the papers were heavy, because we had made her president without consent, or because children understand sooner than adults suppose when they are being handed an unfinished world, I cannot say.

Castalia knelt and gathered her up.

Outside, rain began softly on the leaves. The foxgloves bent, recovered, and bent again. In the cupboard the books stood waiting to be opened; in the street men hurried to offices where new plans were being made; somewhere a machine, recently perfected, flew over the rooftops with a noise like distant guns.

We left the papers on the floor.

Ann kept the doll.

I am a ten-dollar bill, which is the same as being ten men in one suit, provided the men are cheap enough.

I have no ancestry worth boasting of. My father was a printing press, my mother was the Treasury Department, and my first cradle was a package tied so tight that none of us could turn over without permission from Washington. As for character, I came into New York clean, crisp and green, with a portrait on my face that looked as if it had just refused a second helping of pie.

But don’t imagine innocence lasted. A ten-spot in Manhattan sees more life before luncheon than a bishop’s chauffeur sees in Lent.

I left a bank in the pay envelope of a bricklayer named Mike, who had been arranging red clay in straight lines fifty feet above the municipal conscience. Mike drew me out, looked at me affectionately and said:

“Little Willie, you go home to the missus.”

That was family government in theory. In practice I went to a saloon.

Mike laid me on a mahogany bar and ordered refreshments for six members of the Hod-Carriers’ Philharmonic Society, all in overalls and all singing different words to the same song. The bartender gave Mike change, put me in a drawer beside two fivers, a gold certificate and a Canadian quarter that claimed to be travelling for its health.

“You’re new,” said one of the fivers.

“Fresh from the mint,” I replied.

“Cut out yer society manners. You’ll be buying onions in Avenue B by sundown.”

He was wrong by three hours.

The bartender used me to pay an iceman, the iceman gave me to a butcher, and the butcher handed me to a lady in sealskin who had bought two pounds of steak for a dog with a digestion delicate enough to require a consulting physician. She tucked me into a handbag containing smelling salts, a silver pencil, three unpaid bills and enough powder to fresco a courthouse.

At a department store she exchanged me for a pair of gloves marked down from twelve dollars to eight dollars and marked up from four dollars to eight before that. The floorwalker carried me to the cashier as though I were evidence in a breach-of-promise suit. From there I went into the change of a contractor who sold paving stones to the city.

Now, I never inquire into a gentleman’s private affairs. I am legal tender, not a district attorney. Still, this contractor smelled of automobiles, carnations and inside information. He put me into a wallet as thick as a boarding-house steak is thin.

That evening he dined with an alderman and a person called Professor Bellows, who had no college but knew how to operate three shells and a pea. The contractor paid for dinner with me. The waiter—one of a hod-carriers’ union in jackets and aprons—carried me away on a plate and later slipped me to a policeman in the vestibule.

“What for?” I asked.

“Hush,” said a one-spot beside me. “Money should be seen and not subpoenaed.”

The policeman spent me at a cigar shop and received nine dollars and sixty cents’ worth of smoke, conversation and campaign news. The cigar dealer passed me to a bookmaker, the bookmaker paid me to a messenger boy, and the messenger boy, being of an executive temperament, invested me at the racetrack on a horse named Moral Principle.

Moral Principle finished seventh.

That night I was in the pocket of a gambler who had backed a horse named Easy Money. You observe that language is a great prophet if you select your facts afterward.

For two days I circulated among the brethren of chance—men with diamond horseshoes, pearl waistcoats and faces that seemed to have been slept in. I lay upon green tables, under red chips and beside stacks of yellow certificates. The yellow boys were proud, but I never trusted them. Every one had a streak of yellow right through him.

At last I reached Jack Marlowe.

Jack owned the Golden Ace, a gambling establishment situated discreetly above a place that advertised Turkish baths but never seemed to wash anybody. He was a broad man with quiet hands, iron-gray hair and eyes that could add a column of figures while appearing to forgive an enemy. Men called him Black Jack when he was absent and Mr. Marlowe when he could hear them.

I found him in his office, looking at a letter.

Now, a letter is only a bill that hasn’t learned its value, but this one had learned to sting. Jack read it twice, folded it once and threw it upon his desk. I could see the heading: Church of the Redeeming Light.

A week earlier a committee had been collecting funds for a new parish house. It was to contain a gymnasium, a reading room and facilities for removing the young from temptation on weekdays between seven and nine. Jack had offered five thousand dollars.

The committee declined.

The Reverend Doctor Silas Wembly had written that, while appreciating the generous impulse, the trustees could not accept money derived from gambling. Such funds, he said, were tainted.

“Tainted,” Jack repeated.

His cashier, Eddie, leaned against the desk.

“That’s church Latin for ‘not enough,’” said Eddie.

Jack did not smile.

“They took eight hundred from their bazaar,” he said. “Mrs. Pottle raffled a lace pillow. Twenty men paid a dollar each for chances on a ham. They had a wheel of fortune in the Sunday-school room.”

“Sure,” said Eddie. “But they prayed over the wheel before spinnin’ it.”

Jack picked up the letter again.

“They sold votes to choose the prettiest baby.”

“That’s reform,” Eddie explained. “The babies don’t vote themselves.”

Jack rose. “So my money’s dirty.”

He opened his safe. Inside lay enough currency to make a bank examiner develop poetry. He pulled out a thick wad, and I was near its middle, pressed between a fifty with social ambitions and a one-spot who had been folded into the shape of a necktie.

“Where are we going?” I asked.

“From his expression,” said the one-spot, “we’re going to demonstrate something expensive.”

We did.

Jack began with dinner at the Imperial Peacock. He occupied a private room and invited everybody who had ever borrowed money from him, which made the room look like a convention. Champagne marched in by platoons. Lobsters arrived wearing red uniforms. A string orchestra played selections from an opera in which nobody had ever paid cash for anything.

Jack tipped the waiters, the hat boy, the flower girl, the fiddlers, the doorman and a gentleman who had merely stood near the doorman with an expectant expression. He bought cigars shaped like artillery and sent roses to actresses he had never met.

“Here,” he said whenever a hand appeared. “Have some tainted money.”

Nobody refused.

A theatrical manager accepted two hundred dollars and said vice was ruining the nation. A reform politician took fifty for campaign expenses and warned Jack against evil companions. A lady in a diamond collar declared gambling perfectly horrid while wagering twenty dollars that Jack could not toss a grape into the orchestra leader’s horn.

He could not. The orchestra leader kept the twenty.

You may talk about polluted currency, but I noticed that no one asked us to take a bath. We passed from Jack’s hands into pockets fragrant with violet water, tobacco, musk, mothballs and political principle. The cleaner the collar, the quicker the fingers.

Near midnight Jack came down Broadway alone. The electric signs winked like chorus girls with insomnia. Cabs rolled, restaurants glittered and things with cold noses and hot gullets stood near doors hoping prosperity would come out dizzy.

Jack still had me and several of my relatives. His anger had cooled into the sort of silence that breaks furniture if disturbed.

At the corner of a side street a woman spoke to him.

She did not step dramatically beneath a lamp, nor clasp her hands like a lady on a soap advertisement. She simply came from a doorway and said:

“Mister, could you help me?”

Her coat was too thin for the weather and too proud to admit it. One shoe had lost a button. Her face was young except around the eyes, where worry had been doing overtime.

Jack stopped.

“What’s the story?”

“My boy’s sick. I haven’t had work for three weeks. They lock us out in the morning if I can’t pay four dollars. He hasn’t eaten since yesterday.”

Jack looked at her hard. He had heard stories before. Broadway manufactures them wholesale, with tears put in at the factory.

“What kind of work?”

“I sew. Buttonholes mostly.”

“Where’s the boy?”

She gave him an address on West Forty-Ninth—a rear tenement where the sunshine had to know somebody to get in.

Jack took me out.

“Here’s ten dollars,” he said. “But I’m warning you. It came out of a gambling house. The church says it’s tainted.”

She stared at me, then at him.

“Will it buy milk?”

“I suppose so.”

“Will it pay rent?”

“It has before.”

“Then,” she said, closing her fingers around me, “it’s clean enough for Tommy.”

There are times when even a ten-spot dislikes to talk. I lay quiet in her glove, though the stitching scratched my portrait.

She hurried first to a bakery. The baker was pulling brown loaves from an oven, and the smell could have converted a restaurant critic.

“Bread, please,” she said. “And those rolls. And—how much is milk?”

She bought bread, milk, eggs, a little butter and a piece of beef. The baker changed me, sending me into his till while six silver dollars, three ones and some small fry went back to her.

I objected.

“Hold on! I want to see the finish.”

“Can’t,” said a nickel. “Circulation is circulation.”

Luckily the baker gave me next morning to his landlord’s agent, who gave me to a coal dealer, who passed me at luncheon to a druggist. There, by one of those coincidences that fiction uses because Providence invented them, I met a one-dollar bill who had spent the night in the woman’s purse.

He was creased, greasy and full of news.

“Well?” I demanded. “What happened?”

“What didn’t?” said the one-spot. “She flew upstairs like a rent notice with wings. Kid was on a cot, hot as a griddle and thin enough to hide behind a thermometer. She warmed the milk, soaked bread in it and fed him slow. Then she fried the beef and ate some herself after pretending she wasn’t hungry.”

“The rent?”

“Paid at eight this morning. Landlady made a speech about rules, widows and the price of coal, but she took four dollars. Doctor came—two dollars. Said pneumonia hadn’t got a good start and might as well pack up. She bought medicine with me and got change. That’s how I landed here.”

“Did the boy improve?”

“He asked for another roll and wanted to know if there was jam. In medical circles that’s called a favorable bulletin.”

The one-spot gave a dry little cough. He had spent the night near the stove and was feeling crisp around the edges.

“So,” I said, “the tainted money paid rent, bought food and hired a doctor.”

“Yep.”

“And the church wouldn’t take it.”

“Maybe their parish house wasn’t hungry.”

I considered my career. Mike had spent me on beer; the contractor on dinner; the policeman on cigars; the messenger on a horse; gamblers had risked me; fashionable citizens had taken me in tips and wagers. Not one had asked where I was born yesterday. Then Jack, marked unclean by respectable gentlemen, had put me where bread, shelter and medicine stood waiting.

Humans are peculiar accountants. They keep two ledgers—one for deeds and one for reputations—and when the totals disagree they audit the ink.

The druggist took me that afternoon to pay a wholesaler. I was on my way again—perhaps to a bank, a bar, a bridegroom, a burglar, a hospital box or a church collection plate that had not been properly introduced to me. It made no difference. I had learned my profession.

“Are you clean money or dirty money?” asked the one-spot as we parted.

I turned my printed face toward him.

“Read the notice,” I said. “It doesn’t say, ‘Good for all debts, public and private, unless some gentleman objects to the company it has kept.’”

The one-spot squinted.

“No,” he admitted.

“Then there’s your moral. Money doesn’t carry a conscience. It only carries value. The conscience belongs to the hand that spends it.”

And off I went—legal tender, ten dollars, no questions asked.

### The God Beneath the Viaduct

James Holroyd was a man of convictions, though they were not always sober convictions, and seldom improved the comfort of anyone within reach of his hands. He denied Providence, maintained that Shakespeare knew nothing of electricity, and considered mercy a defect peculiar to clergymen, women, and improperly hardened steel. Yet he believed in Carnot’s cycle, in the British Empire, and in the chastening virtue of a leather strap; and upon these three articles he had founded a practical theology quite sufficient for his needs.

He was chief electrician in the power-house beneath the eastern railway viaduct, where three dynamos supplied current to a suburban line. Two were elderly machines with narrow belts that flapped and smacked as though perpetually applauding some private jest. The third was new, enormous, and of a Continental design. Its polished contact rings revolved with so deceptive a smoothness that they seemed motionless; its brushes spat blue fire; and the broad belt that descended from the shaft overhead came down in a swift grey sheet, carrying a dark patch round and round—appearing, vanishing, and returning with the punctuality of a planet.

Holroyd’s assistant was called Azuma-zi.

Whether that was all his name, or merely the portion that survived the immigration clerk, it is hard to say. He had come by way of Aden and Marseilles, had worked in the stokehold of a steamer, in a sugar warehouse, and for six weeks as the ornamental servant of a gentleman who admired foreign faces but objected to foreign appetites. London received him finally, as it receives so much picturesque material, by putting him in a dark place, covering him with coal dust, and paying him eighteen shillings a week.

His English consisted chiefly of nouns, imperatives, and the names of tools. Holroyd supplied the imperatives.

“Oil-can!”

Azuma-zi brought it.

“Spanner!”

He brought that also.

“Not that one, you black fool!”

Then the lesson became physical.

Holroyd called this “making a man useful.” It was, in his opinion, a labour of love; and because love fatigued him, he commonly refreshed himself afterwards with gin.

The shed was a long brick cavern ribbed with iron. By day a begrimed skylight admitted a pale dilution of heaven; by night the arc lamps cast a livid brilliance upon the machines and left the spaces between them in profound blackness. The air was hot with oil, copper, dust, and the sour exhalations of labouring men. We would have the noises of that shed always about the reader if print could carry sound: the light machines buzzing and rattling, the overhead shafting crying out at every bearing, the belts going slap-slap, suck and thud, and beneath all these the vast sustained note of the great dynamo—

Whoo-oom—whoo-oom—whoo-oom—

It was not loud in the ordinary sense. It entered through the ribs rather than the ears. Now and again some change of load on the railway altered its voice, and then the steady breathing rose into a heavier complaint, or sank to a muttering so deep that the floor itself appeared to meditate. Trains began, gathered speed, and stopped miles away; lamps sprang alight in distant carriages; points shifted under the rain; and here, in the cavern under the viaduct, the great armature turned without haste.

Holroyd understood the dynamo as a collection of parts, which is the proper and economical way to understand a machine. He could tell you the resistance of its field coils, the permissible temperature of its bearings, and the consequences of short circuiting. He had helped to erect it and therefore despised it. Familiarity, in coarse minds, is not far removed from contempt.

To Azuma-zi it was another matter.

He had seen engines before—ship engines that heaved with iron knees in the red twilight of stokeholds, little pumping engines beside fields, and cranes that turned their long necks against the sky. But those had confessed their machinery. Their pistons came and went; their rods thrust visibly; their steam escaped in white surrender. This great machine concealed its strength. It revolved too swiftly for movement to be followed, and its smoothness was more alarming than violence. There was power without effort, fire without fuel, and command without speech. The railway obeyed it beyond the walls.

At first he watched it from the corner of his eye.

The dark patch on the belt came down.

It vanished below.

It sprang upward behind.

It returned.

After many hours it seemed to Azuma-zi that the patch was not passing but observing him, peeping at each revolution from the grey rush of leather. The polished rings shone like pale eyes. Blue sparks crawled under the brushes and snapped away into darkness. And always the note continued—

Whoo-oom—whoo-oom—

Perhaps the thing said nothing. A properly educated listener would insist upon this, and would produce diagrams to make his silence convincing. But Azuma-zi had little education and much suffering; and there are moments when a repeated sound, though made by brass and copper, acquires grammar.

The machine said: I endure.

It said: I am strong.

And presently, when Holroyd struck him across the mouth with a doubled belt because a bearing had run hot, it seemed to add: I see.

Azuma-zi wiped his lip and looked at the blood upon the back of his hand. Holroyd had gone to the bench for his bottle. The two small dynamos buzzed foolishly; the shafting clattered; but the great one deepened its note, throb, throb, throb, until the bolts in the floor trembled.

That night Azuma-zi bowed to it.

He did this secretly, in the shadow behind the casing, where Holroyd could not see him from the little glass-fronted office. He crouched with his forehead near the greasy concrete and placed before the machine three objects: a bright copper washer, a twist of tobacco, and a small yellow flower purchased from a woman outside the station. They were not, perhaps, the gifts an electrical engineer would have selected, but gods have accepted stranger offerings.

He whispered in his own language. He spoke of Holroyd’s fists, of the strap, of the loneliness of the vast city, of cold rain and the flavourless bread of England. He asked the machine to look upon him. As he prayed, a distant train pulled away, the load increased, and a spray of violet light danced at the brushes.

Azuma-zi lifted his head.

The Lord had answered.

From that night his duties acquired a ceremonial character. He polished the brass not because Holroyd ordered it, but because the brass belonged to the Lord. He fed oil to the bearings as a priest might pour wine. When he brushed away the black dust that settled on the casing, his hands were reverent. He learned the changes of the great note: its indifference at dawn, its burden during the evening traffic, its long, contented murmur after midnight. He could distinguish the faint hiss that preceded sparking from the heavier growl of an overloaded armature. Knowledge came to him not as science, but as liturgy.

Holroyd observed the improvement and claimed the credit.

“See?” he told a fitter who visited the shed. “There’s nothing like a bit o’ leather for teaching these foreign chaps. Reason’s wasted on ’em.”

The fitter laughed, because Holroyd was a man one laughed with when one could not conveniently knock him down.

The worship might have continued for months had Azuma-zi not become bold. One Sunday morning, when the traffic was light and Holroyd slept in the office with his boots on the desk, Azuma-zi made a little shrine beneath the shadow of the main bearing. He arranged bits of polished metal in a half-circle, set the yellow remains of a candle between them, and laid down a strip of red cloth that had once been part of a porter’s scarf.

Then he bowed, and the candle flame leaned towards the revolving belt.

Holroyd awoke thirsty.

For several seconds he watched through the dirty glass, his heavy face gradually acquiring intelligence. Then he came out with the noiseless deliberation of a man approaching a comic opportunity.

“Well, I’m damned,” he said. “Here’s our heathen praying to the company plant.”

Azuma-zi rose quickly and placed himself before the little shrine.

Holroyd peered over his shoulder. “What’s this rubbish? Is that your idol, then?”

“No touch,” said Azuma-zi.

“No touch?” Holroyd repeated, with great enjoyment. “Why, it belongs to me more than it belongs to you.”

Strictly speaking it belonged to a syndicate, several directors, and a quantity of borrowed capital; but possession is a flexible idea in the hands of authority.

Holroyd kicked the candle away. It rolled under a bench and went out. He stamped upon the metal pieces, tore up the red cloth, and flung the tobacco into an oil drain.

Azuma-zi made a sound in his throat.

“None o’ that,” said Holroyd, and struck him.

Azuma-zi staggered against the casing. His cheek touched warm iron. The great dynamo was turning at full speed now, for an excursion train had entered the line; its note rose, broad and tremendous, until every loose pane vibrated.

Whoo-oom! Whoo-oom! Whoo-oom!

Holroyd seized him by the collar.

“Where’s your Lord now?”

The words were scarcely audible, but Azuma-zi heard them. More than that, he believed the machine heard them.

Holroyd dragged him away, beat him with the strap, and finally dismissed the ceremony from his mind. He had demonstrated Western enlightenment, defended engineering from superstition, and enjoyed himself. After that he drank until his face grew purple and retired to the office.

Azuma-zi sat behind the second dynamo, breathing through his teeth.

The great machine laboured in the darkness. The patch descended, vanished, and returned. Blue flame fluttered at the contacts. Each pulse of the note appeared to strike the same thought into his bruised head.

Bring him.

Azuma-zi looked up.

The machine continued.

Bring him.

It may be that the phrase was no more than the recurrent jar of the belt fastener. It may be that pain and hatred translated an accidental rhythm. Yet one ought not to be too proud of one’s rationality in these matters. A cathedral organ, which is only pipes, leather, wind, and keys, has persuaded educated men of immortality; and surely a dynamo, with the strength of a thousand horses in its coils, may be allowed some influence upon an untutored mind.

For two days Azuma-zi waited.

Holroyd noticed his silence only to approve it. On the third evening the rain fell heavily, and the passing trains flashed wet yellow windows through the openings of the viaduct. Water drummed on the roof. The shed was empty except for the two men and the machinery.

Holroyd had been drinking since four.

At nine he came out of the office singing a fragment of a music-hall song. He lurched between the dynamos, inspected a gauge without seeing it, and discovered Azuma-zi beside the great machine.

“What are you hanging about here for?”

Azuma-zi did not answer.

Holroyd swayed closer. “Praying again?”

The note of the dynamo altered. Somewhere on the line a train gathered speed.

Bring him.

Holroyd grinned and reached for Azuma-zi’s ear.

Azuma-zi struck upward with both hands.

The blow took Holroyd beneath the chin and snapped his head back. Surprise, rather than pain, appeared upon his face. He stumbled, recovered, and swung a fist. Azuma-zi ducked. The fist glanced from his temple, and they closed together.

Holroyd was heavier and ordinarily the stronger, but gin had loosened the fastenings of his body. He gripped, cursed, and drove Azuma-zi against a railing. Azuma-zi twisted free. They went round once, stamping and slipping in oil, grotesque dancers beneath the purple lamps. The light dynamos buzzed on; the belts smacked; rain hammered the roof.

Holroyd caught Azuma-zi by the throat.

“You devil!”

Azuma-zi’s hand found the leather strap hanging from Holroyd’s belt. He jerked it loose, flung it round Holroyd’s wrist, and pulled. Holroyd pitched forward. Azuma-zi turned, set his back beneath the other’s chest, and heaved with all the strength that fear and worship could lend him.

Holroyd struck the guard rail.

For a second both men were still.

Then Azuma-zi saw the exposed copper contact beyond Holroyd’s shoulder—two live points left uncovered after an earlier adjustment. He heard the command in one vast descending roar.

Now.

He gripped Holroyd’s coat and swung him sideways.

Holroyd’s hands touched the terminals.

There was a sharp blue flash, insignificant beside the size of the machine. Holroyd became rigid. His mouth opened, but whatever final criticism he intended upon Azuma-zi, foreign religion, or electrical design never attained expression. A smell of scorched cloth entered the oily air.

Azuma-zi released him.

The body fell heavily and rolled onto its back. The dynamo did not pause. Its armature turned; its belt descended; its brushes murmured. A human death had disturbed its current less than the starting of a train.

For a while Azuma-zi knelt beside Holroyd. Then he crawled to the shadow beneath the casing and bowed until his forehead touched the floor.

“Lord,” he whispered.

The machine’s voice had softened.

Accepted, it seemed to say. Accepted.

At ten a linesman entered and found Azuma-zi sitting quietly by the wall. Holroyd lay near the contacts. There was immediate shouting, then running, then the abrupt stopping of machinery. The great note sank, wavered, and ceased. In the silence that followed, the rain sounded enormous.

The railway lost power over a section of the line. Two trains stopped between stations. Passengers leaned out into the wet night and offered opinions. Telegraph instruments clicked, inspectors arrived, and a doctor certified that Holroyd was dead—a conclusion already reached by everyone except Holroyd.

The exposed terminals explained the matter neatly. Holroyd had been drinking; Holroyd had often handled live apparatus recklessly; Holroyd had, beyond dispute, been electrocuted. Whether his death was accident or suicide became for half an hour an absorbing question. Then the traffic superintendent demanded to know when the current would be restored, and philosophy yielded to timetables.

Azuma-zi was questioned.

He understood little, appeared terrified, and repeatedly pointed at the great dynamo.

“Him kill,” he said.

“Yes, yes, the machine killed him,” said the inspector. “We can see that.”

Azuma-zi bowed.

To the inspector this was gratitude at being understood. To Azuma-zi it was confirmation of a miracle. The officials, the doctor, the engineers—all acknowledged the Lord’s deed. Holroyd had mocked, and the machine had slain him. Civilisation itself had examined the sacrifice and declared it lawful.

Before midnight the body was removed. The contacts were covered. The dynamos were restarted.

The broad belt gathered speed: flap, flap, flap—then a continuous rushing. The rings brightened. Sparks awakened under the brushes. The mighty note rolled once more beneath the viaduct.

Whoo-oom—whoo-oom—

Azuma-zi smiled.

The scientific manager arrived the following afternoon.

He was a thin, clean-shaven man named Mr. Phelps, with cool grey eyes and the habit of touching machinery as another man might take a pulse. His clothes were plain, his hands well kept, and his mind furnished with those admirable compartments in which facts remain separate from emotions. He regretted Holroyd’s death, disapproved of uncovered terminals, and was chiefly anxious about a vibration reported in the main shaft.

Azuma-zi watched him approach the great dynamo.

Phelps examined the bearings, tested the brushes, and listened to the note. He placed his palm against the casing.

To Azuma-zi the gesture was profanation.

The Lord had accepted Holroyd, yet its voice remained restless. During the night it had spoken again—not with the fierce command of before, but with a low continual hunger. The first sacrifice had not completed the matter. Perhaps Holroyd, being drunk, cruel, and generally inferior, had proved unacceptable nourishment. Perhaps a greater victim was required: a man with authority, clean hands, and knowledge of the Lord’s hidden parts.

Phelps walked behind the machine.

Azuma-zi followed.

“What is it?” Phelps asked.

Azuma-zi pointed to the casing. “Lord.”

“Yes, yes,” said Phelps absently. “Large machine. Keep clear of the belt.”

He stooped to inspect the fastening on the guard.

Azuma-zi glanced towards the office. The linesman had gone to fetch a tool. No one else was in the shed. The light dynamos rattled; the shafting shrieked; the returning patch on the belt looked down and vanished.

Now, said the machine.

Azuma-zi sprang.

He caught Phelps about the waist and drove him towards the contacts. Phelps struck the iron guard with one shoulder. For an instant he was too astonished to resist; then his professional composure gave way to a very natural energy.

“Let go!”

Azuma-zi pushed harder. Phelps saw the blue flicker at the brushes, saw the foreigner’s fixed eyes, and comprehended—not the theology, perhaps, but the destination. He planted one foot against the base of the machine and twisted violently.

They swung apart.

Azuma-zi seized his coat. Phelps struck him across the face. Azuma-zi returned with his head lowered and drove him back. The manager’s sleeve brushed the insulated cable. Sparks leapt nearby like tiny malignant insects.

Phelps got a hand beneath Azuma-zi’s chin and thrust. They reeled into the open space, toppled over an oil can, and fell together. Azuma-zi rose first. Phelps gripped his ankle; Azuma-zi kicked free and darted again towards him.

Then a voice sounded at the shed entrance.

“What the devil’s going on?”

The linesman had returned with the traffic superintendent behind him.

Azuma-zi stopped.

In that instant his religion reached its final problem. Before him stood the intended sacrifice, alive and guarded. Behind him the Lord revolved, waiting. The officials would seize him, question him, perhaps beat him as Holroyd had done. They would shut him from the shed. They would leave the hungry god without a servant.

Or perhaps the command had never concerned Phelps.

Perhaps the second victim had always been himself.

He turned. Phelps, rising on one knee, saw his intention and shouted.

Azuma-zi leapt over the low rail.

For a fraction of a second he stood outlined against the polished rings, his arms spread, his face lifted into the violet glare. Then he thrust both hands upon the live terminals.

The flash filled the shed.

It was a hard white-blue blossom, instantaneous and without mercy. Azuma-zi’s body arched. The great machine uttered a sudden deeper note as the current found its path through him. Then he dropped beneath the brushes, one hand blackened, his clothes smouldering at the cuffs.

The dynamo continued to turn.

No thunder followed; no heavens opened. A train emerged from a tunnel three miles away and accelerated precisely as scheduled.

Phelps stared at the body. He was breathing rapidly, and one cheek was bleeding where Azuma-zi’s nail had cut it. The traffic superintendent called for the current to be shut off. The linesman ran to the switches.

“Don’t touch him yet,” Phelps said automatically.

Even under these circumstances his technical reflexes remained admirable.

When the machine had slowed and the note descended through a long moan into silence, they drew Azuma-zi away. Phelps looked from the dead man to the insulated contacts, then to the place where Holroyd had been found.

His face changed.

“He tried to put me against them,” he said.

The superintendent frowned. “What?”

“The contacts. That was what he was doing.”

They looked together at the marks on the floor, at the guard rail, and at the terminal cover that could be lifted by hand. Phelps recalled Holroyd’s position, the bruising noted by the doctor, and Azuma-zi’s strange declaration: Him kill.

“Holroyd didn’t take hold of those terminals,” he said slowly. “He was put there.”

Outside, the rain had cleared. Through the high begrimed panes a few pale stars showed above London, remote and cold. In the shed the machines stood temporarily dead, their belts hanging slack, their polished surfaces reflecting the arc lamps like black water.

There were inquiries afterwards, of course. Reports were written, corrected, copied, and filed. Holroyd’s death changed from probable suicide to murder; Azuma-zi’s from industrial accident to self-destruction while evading arrest. The railway company denied responsibility for both, strengthened the guards around the contacts, and recommended closer supervision of foreign labour. A newspaper printed six inches on “A Strange Tragedy at the Power-House,” though it misspelled Azuma-zi’s name and gave the dynamo twice its actual voltage.

By the end of the week a new electrician had been appointed.

The blood was washed away. The scorched mark near the terminals was scraped and painted. Trains ran, lamps shone, tickets were clipped, shareholders received their reports, and the great dynamo turned beneath the viaduct as before—whoo-oom, whoo-oom—patient, puissant, and innocent according to every principle of electrical science.

Yet if gods are measured by worship, commandments, mysteries, and death, then for three days that machine had been a god. Its religion was brief, but not incomplete. It had possessed a shrine, a priest, a revelation, a Human Sacrifice, and at last a Martyrdom.

Few religions, in so short a time, have accomplished more.

The storm had been mustering behind the western mountains since noon; but toward evening, having marshalled its black battalions ridge above ridge, it advanced upon my valley with banners of indigo, artillery of thunder, and such glittering crooked lances as seemed forged in the furnaces beneath Olympus. The first peal rolled ponderously among the peaks; the second split into a dozen detonations; the third came tumbling down the gorge like Himalayas of concussion, till every plate upon my dresser rang, the shutters clapped their hands, and my small cottage—built stoutly of stone, though no Gibraltar—appeared to shrug beneath the celestial bombardment.

I sat by the hearth, not timidly, but with that grave delight which a reasonable man may feel when Nature, tired of whispering through grass and brook, lifts her brazen trumpet. Rain struck the roof in spear-points. The chimney roared. Now and again the room sprang white as a chapel at judgment-day, and the clock, the kettle, the brass candlestick, and my own two hands showed with preternatural distinctness; then all sank back into red firelight and shadow.

At the very crown of this majesty there came a knock.

Not the bluff summons of a neighbour, but three hollow taps, deliberate and metallic.

“Enter!” I called.

The door opened inward against the gale, and with the gale there entered a tall, drenched man bearing an elaborate staff of copper. It was six feet long, perhaps more, and terminated above in three needle-like points; below, it broadened into a green-glass handle bound about with brass wire. Rain streamed from the stranger’s hat. His narrow coat clung to him like a burial cloth, and his sunken eyes contained a pale, innocuous sort of lightning, as if the storm had appointed him its minor clerk.

“Shut the door, sir,” said I, “or bring in the mountain with you, if that be your design.”

He thrust it closed, looked neither at me nor the fire, but surveyed the room with frantic calculation.

“The centre!” he cried. “Where is the exact centre?”

“Possibly beneath the table; but you are welcome to the chair.”

“No chair! Wood attracts less than iron, yet nails—nails, sir! And the table may stand above a buried vein of ore.”

He planted himself upon the rug midway between hearth and wall, holding his copper staff upright. Water descended from every hem and gathered beneath him in a melancholy pool.

“Come to the fire,” said I. “You are wet enough to irrigate Egypt.”

“The fire!” He recoiled as though I had invited him into a cannon. “Know you not that chimneys are highways for the electric fluid? The soot conducts; the heated vapour invites; the iron crane receives; and there”—pointing with horror—“your kettle completes the murderous arrangement!”

I glanced at the kettle, which continued its domestic hymn without shame.

“Then take the bench by the wall.”

“The wall! Never in a storm. Stone retains moisture; moisture solicits the current; the current descends; the man becomes carbon.”

“An unceremonious conclusion.”

“Hark!”

A blue blade cleft the panes; thunder crashed so directly overhead that the cottage seemed for one instant to have been struck flat and restored.

The stranger ducked. His copper sceptre shook in his hands.

“Awful!” he whispered. “There—did you mark the interval? Scarce one second. The discharge was within a quarter mile. Stand away from the chimney! Away, I say!”

I moved a little nearer it.

He stared at me. “Are you mad?”

“I am cold.”

“You trifle with death.”

“Death,” I replied, “has endured worse trifling and never yet missed an appointment.”

Another flash spread over the windows like molten silver. The visitor bounded sideways, then sprang back.

“The rug!” he cried. “The rug, the rug!”

“What crime has it committed?”

“Wool is an imperfect conductor. Stand here. Precisely here. But not too near me.”

“You occupy the centre so imperially that I must remain a province.”

“Sir, this is no hour for wit. Have you metal about your person?”

“A buckle, two buttons, a watch, and perhaps an iron constitution.”

“The watch—remove it!”

“Time has frequently oppressed me; nevertheless, I keep it.”

He swept off his hat. His hair, dark and rope-like with rain, fell over his forehead. “Your levity is criminal. Last June, in a house beyond the north pass, a gentleman stood during a storm with a silver watch in his waistcoat. The bolt entered through the roof, followed the chain, melted the case, fused the works, and killed him where he stood.”

“Did it spare the chain?”

“What?”

“If your history be so exact, I would know whether the chain survived.”

“It killed the man!”

“That I apprehend. Yet perhaps the man, and not the watch, attracted it. Was he tall?”

“Tall? Yes—uncommonly tall.”

“Then why accuse the watch? Height, you will presently tell me, is perilous.”

“It is! Eminence invites the thunderbolt. Trees, towers, steeples, masts, giants—all court destruction.”

Being myself somewhat above ordinary stature, I straightened until my head nearly touched a smoke-blackened beam.

He uttered a strangled sound. “Crouch!”

“I dislike bowing without introduction.”

“Crouch, man! The electric fluid selects the highest object.”

“Then raise your staff another foot. It appears ambitious.”

At this he clutched the copper implement to his breast, then, remembering its nature, thrust it from him, yet dared not set it down.

I examined him more narrowly. His boots were excellent, though muddy; his coat, under its baptism, showed good cloth; and across his waistcoat, concealed until now by the folds of his cloak, ran a chain from which depended several small brass labels. On one I read, in tiny stamped letters: CELESTIAL SECURITY COMPANY.

“Sir,” said I, “you have not yet declared whether you are fugitive, philosopher, prophet, or envoy extraordinary and minister plenipotentiary from Jupiter Tonans.”

A transformation, slight but significant, passed over him. His fear did not leave him; it rearranged itself. His eyes still watched the windows, yet calculation crept behind their terror.

“I am,” he said, “an agent for the most approved system of atmospheric protection ever offered to householders.”

“Ah. You sell lightning rods.”

“I preserve lives.”

“At what rate?”

“The rod is furnished, mounted, connected, and warranted for twelve sovereigns—fifteen where the chimney exceeds twenty feet.”

“Providence, then, is cheaper in low houses.”

“Hark!”

The thunder, obedient to his trade, delivered a magnificent endorsement. He lifted one finger.

“You hear? That voice is my advertisement. Every concussion cries, Prepare!”

“To my untutored ear it cried only, Boom.”

He ignored me. “Observe this instrument.” He held up the staff, and the fire ran along its wet copper coils. “Pure metal, no adulteration; triple point, after the latest continental principles; insulated grip of vitrified green glass; spiralled conductor; earth-clasp beneath. Properly fixed above your roof and carried down into moist soil, it receives the discharge, persuades it from your dwelling, and conducts it harmlessly into the ground.”

“You travel with it in your hand?”

“For demonstration.”

“During thunderstorms?”

“Thunderstorms are the season of demand.”

“How industrious of you to seek customers when they are least capable of reflection.”

His face tightened. “Danger does not wait upon reflection.”

“Nor, apparently, does commerce.”

He paced half a step, remembered the nails beneath the floor, and resumed his position on the rug.

“You mock because you do not understand. Three cottages in the eastern valley were struck this summer.”

“How many were not?”

“Numbers prove nothing to the dead.”

“But much to the living. Had those cottages rods?”

“One had a defective rod installed by an ignorant rival. It terminated in dry clay. The bolt leaped from the conductor to a bedstead.”

“So the remedy helped the disease find the patient.”

“A defective remedy!”

“And the second?”

“No rod.”

“The third?”

“No rod.”

“Then of your three witnesses, one speaks against you and two are silent.”

His brow darkened like a lesser weather-front. “I could tell you of a mother and five children destroyed beneath an oak.”

“Did you sell the oak?”

“Sir!”

“You arraign watches, walls, fires, trees, beds, chimneys, tall men, and tables. Ere long you will condemn the whole creation as improperly constructed.”

“Animals too,” he continued, driven by habit past indignation. “Never shelter near cattle. Their bodies exhale vapours attractive to electricity. Dogs are dangerous. Cats—less so, though their fur accumulates charge.”

My old hound, Brutus, slept beside the hearth. At the mention of dogs he opened one eye, judged the speaker beneath concern, and closed it again.

“The creature must be removed!” cried the agent.

“He was here first.”

“Then you are both imperilled.”

“Excellent. Company sweetens calamity.”

“And bell wires!” He pointed toward the braided wire that ran from my door-pull along the ceiling. “Fatal conductors. Last year a bolt followed a bell wire through six rooms, shattered a mirror, entered a portrait frame, and passed out through the kitchen pump.”

“A bolt of remarkable domestic curiosity.”

“It killed a maid.”

“Before or after admiring the portrait?”

He stamped, then instantly looked down in alarm, as though his heel might have awakened a subterranean battery.

“You are proud,” he said. “Pride is the mast upon which judgment descends.”

“And fear is the purse into which your hand ascends.”

For a moment we heard nothing but the rain’s furious tattoo. He lowered his voice, giving it the solemn weight of a pulpit.

“Would you wager your wife’s life against twelve sovereigns?”

“I have no wife.”

“Your children’s?”

“None.”

“Your servant’s?”

“I serve myself poorly but without wages.”

“Your own, then.”

“That is already staked, sir, from birth.”

He came nearer despite his rule about proximity. “Consider: every hair of your head is numbered.”

“Then Heaven keeps a more exact inventory than your company.”

“And will you not protect what Heaven has given?”

“Against what Heaven may send?”

“Against physical law!”

“Is physical law outside divine ordination?”

He hesitated. Theology had led him onto ground less neatly wired than a rooftop.

“The Almighty,” he said at last, “has supplied man with reason, metals, and scientific progress.”

“He has also supplied charlatans with tongues.”

His cheeks went black-red. A flash illuminated him so that for one grotesque instant he appeared forked above by his rod, dripping below, some itinerant demon of commerce cast out of the storm and eager to insure me against his master.

Then came the thunder. He fell to his knees.

Not figuratively. Not prudently. He dropped upon the rug, embraced his copper staff, and cried, “Mercy!”

I confess I laughed.

He rose with the stiff wrath of humiliated fear. “You laugh at death?”

“No. I laugh at its salesman. Behold yourself! You announce dominion over lightning, yet every flash jerks your limbs like wires on a puppet. You bear a wand that promises security, but dare touch neither chair nor wall. You offer me calm at fifteen sovereigns, being unable to keep a pennyworth for your own use.”

“I know the danger—that is why I fear it.”

“The sailor knows the sea and does not shriek at every wave.”

“The prudent sailor carries a compass.”

“Which he does not brandish while begging the ocean to spare him.”

“Hark! There again. Nearer!”

“In truth, farther.”

“You cannot know.”

“I counted the interval.”

“Intervals deceive among mountains.”

“Then why did you use one five minutes ago?”

He looked at me with hatred pure enough to be almost tranquil. His salesmanship, battered at every gate, now sought one final entrance.

“Ten sovereigns,” he said. “A concession made only because the road is severe.”

“No.”

“Eight, without the ornamental weather-vane.”

“No.”

“Six, if you dig the trench yourself.”

“I would sooner dig for potatoes. They make fewer claims.”

“Four! The bare conductor at cost. Think of your neighbours. Should your house be struck, burning fragments may set the valley aflame.”

“Now you insure the neighbourhood through my roof.”

“You refuse responsibility.”

“I refuse extortion.”

The storm, meanwhile, had spent its chief rage. The thunder withdrew from the cottage and spoke among distant hills. Rain slackened to separate drops. A pale radiance entered the western panes, and when I opened the door—despite the agent’s shout that hinges were metal—the valley shone beneath departing clouds. Across the eastern mountain there arched a rainbow, full and lucid: violet rooted in the pines, red dissolving above the crags, the whole bright covenant suspended over drenched earth.

“Look,” said I.

“Close the door! The threshold may be charged.”

“Look, man. Yon sign asks no subscription, bears no company seal, and descends alike upon the insured and uninsured.”

“Superstition.”

“Perhaps. Yet it makes me brave, whereas your science has made you contemptible.”

“My science saves lives!”

“Then sell it honestly in sunlight. Speak of probabilities, not certainties; of prudent measures, not enchanted guarantees. Do not stalk thunder like Tetzel after a church bell, vending pardons from fire. Do not wait until the sky shouts and children tremble, then knock with copper knuckles and put a price upon their terror.”

“You blaspheme knowledge.”

“No. I rebuke its masquerade. Knowledge stands upright. You crawl. Knowledge admits the unknown. You turn it into merchandise. Your rod may conduct lightning—I do not dispute the metal—but your true conductor is fear, and through it you draw gold from the hearts of men.”

He raised the staff. His face, which had mirrored the storm in pallor and convulsion, now became darker than the retreating cloud.

“You will buy,” he said.

It was spoken softly.

“I will not.”

“You will sign.”

“I will throw you out.”

With a cry he lunged, driving the copper point toward my breast. I stepped aside, caught the shaft beneath its glass handle, and twisted. He clung to it with both hands. For an instant we revolved together in the middle of the room—buyer and seller, scepticism and terror, each absurdly disputing possession of Jupiter’s sceptre.

Then the green glass cracked.

I wrenched the staff free, set it across my knee, and broke it. Copper coils sprang loose like serpents. I snapped the splintered pieces again, flung them on the floor, and trampled point, clamp, warranty plate, and ornamental vane beneath my boots.

He stared as though I had fractured the axis of the world.

“There,” said I. “Your guarantee is cancelled.”

He rushed at me barehanded. I seized him by collar and waistband, conveyed him through the doorway with less ceremony than he had entered, and deposited him in the mud. The pieces of his instrument followed, ringing around him.

“Go,” I said. “And when next you come hither, bring either courage or an umbrella.”

He gathered his copper relics, casting upon me a look compounded of prophet, murderer, and disappointed shopkeeper. The sun struck the wet brass labels on his waistcoat; they flashed once, harmlessly.

“You will be struck!” he cried.

“Then I shall not require a receipt.”

He descended the road, slipping among puddles, and before long was a black mark beneath the rainbow.

I warned my neighbours of him. Some laughed; some thanked me; three purchased rods before the month was out. For the man still dwells in the land. Whenever clouds assemble, he walks abroad; whenever thunder speaks, he translates it into a price; whenever fear opens a door, he enters carrying his bright counterfeit of providence. His staff is mended, his stories enlarged, his tariffs improved. And though lightning seldom strikes a house, terror strikes the human heart daily—wherefore the merchant prospers.

There was once a little girl named Karen, who was so poor that in summer she went barefoot, and in winter she wore heavy wooden shoes which rubbed her ankles until they bled. Yet she was a beautiful child, with dark eyes and soft hair; and because people praised her beauty, she learned early to look into every clear pool and every shining window.

Her mother was poor and sickly. When she died, the neighbors laid her in a plain coffin, and Karen followed it in an old black dress. Upon her feet she wore shoes made by the village shoemaker’s wife from scraps of red cloth. They were clumsy shoes, and one was larger than the other; but Karen thought them splendid because they were red.

As she walked behind the coffin, a carriage came along the road. In it sat a wealthy old lady, who looked out and saw the beautiful child in the clumsy red shoes.

“Who is that little girl?” she asked.

When she heard that Karen had neither father nor mother, she said, “I will take her into my house and bring her up properly.”

So Karen was given clean linen, warm food, and a room with white curtains. Her hair was combed, her hands were washed, and she was taught to read from a great Bible with golden clasps. The old red shoes were burned, for the lady said they were ugly and unsuitable.

Karen wept when she saw the flames consume them; not because the shoemaker’s wife had made them kindly, but because they were red.

As the years passed, Karen grew more beautiful. Everyone who came to the house said so.

“What a lovely face!”

“What graceful hands!”

“What fine dark eyes!”

And Karen heard every word. She became proud, though pride sits poorly upon the young, and worse upon those who have been rescued by mercy.

One day a princess rode through the town. She wore a white dress, and upon her feet were red morocco shoes, delicate as rose petals and bright as berries beneath the snow. Karen saw neither the princess’s gracious smile nor the poor people whom she greeted. She saw only the shoes.

“Such shoes,” thought Karen, “would make even an ordinary girl look royal; and upon my feet they would be more beautiful still.”

The time came for Karen to be confirmed. The old lady ordered a respectable black dress and took Karen to the shoemaker so that she might have proper shoes.

In the shoemaker’s window stood a pair of red shoes. They were of shining leather, with narrow straps and small heels. They had been made for a nobleman’s child, but they did not fit her.

“Those!” cried Karen. “I must have those!”

The old lady’s sight was weak, and she did not perceive how brightly red they were. The shoemaker hesitated.

“Red shoes are scarcely suitable for confirmation.”

“They fit me,” said Karen, slipping them on. “See how perfectly they fit!”

And indeed they fitted as though they had been made upon her feet.

At church Karen knelt before the altar. The minister spoke of baptism, obedience, humility, and the putting away of childish vanities. Karen heard the solemn words, and yet she did not think of them. She wondered whether the people behind her could see the red shoes. When she walked to the rail, she stepped carefully, that the shoes might appear to advantage; and when she returned to her place, she looked down at them again.

Outside the church stood an old soldier with a red beard. He carried a crutch, and his coat was faded and patched. He stooped and brushed the dust from Karen’s shoes.

“Look!” he said. “What beautiful dancing shoes! Hold fast when you dance!”

He tapped each sole with his hand.

The shoes tingled upon Karen’s feet.

At home the old lady saw their color clearly.

“Those are not fit for church,” she said sternly. “You must never wear them there again.”

Karen bowed her head and answered, “No.”

But on the following Sunday she looked at the black shoes and then at the red shoes. She looked once, and she looked twice, and at last she put on the red ones.

“The old lady will not notice,” she thought.

At the church door stood the soldier.

“Look, what beautiful dancing shoes!” he cried.

When Karen came out, she lifted one foot as if to show the shoe. At once the other foot sprang after it. She took three dancing steps, then seven, then spun around so quickly that her skirts flew out.

“Stop!” cried the coachman.

But the shoes carried her past the carriage and along the road. She danced down the hill and up again; she danced around the churchyard wall; she danced until the coachman ran after her, caught her, and lifted her bodily into the carriage. Even there her feet continued beating against one another.

The shoes were pulled off and locked away in a cupboard.

Not long afterward the old lady became ill. She lay in a great bed under heavy curtains, and her breathing was weak. Karen was to sit beside her, bring her medicine, and read aloud from the book of psalms.

But there was to be a ball in the town.

“I am young,” Karen thought. “Must I sit here while others dance?”

She opened the cupboard. There stood the red shoes, brighter than before.

“I shall wear them only this once.”

She put them on, and at first they were quiet. At the ball all eyes turned toward her. The music played, and Karen danced. She forgot the sick woman; she forgot the medicine and the psalms. She thought only of how light she seemed and how prettily the red shoes flashed.

Then the shoes began to dance of themselves.

They carried her through the ballroom, down the stairs, and into the street. Karen cried out, but the musicians believed she was merry, and the guests clapped their hands.

She danced out of the town and into the dark wood.

Rain fell, and branches struck her face; but she danced. She wished to turn homeward, yet the shoes bore her deeper among the trees. She wished to sit down, yet her feet sprang higher.

The old soldier appeared beside the road.

“Look, what beautiful dancing shoes!” he said.

“Take them off!” cried Karen. “Oh, take them off!”

But he vanished, and she danced on.

At midnight she came to a churchyard. The moon shone upon the stones, and the dead lay quietly beneath them, for the dead had something better to do than dance.

Before the church door stood an angel in white garments. His wings reached from the earth toward heaven, and in his hand was a broad sword that shone like lightning.

Karen tried to kneel, but the shoes made her leap.

“Dance shalt thou,” said the angel, “dance in thy red shoes, thou proud and vain child! Thou shalt dance through darkness and daylight, through joy and sorrow. At doors where thou wouldst enter, the shoes shall go before thee. Thou shalt dance until thy heart learns what thy eyes refused to see.”

“Mercy!” cried Karen.

But the angel pointed with the sword, and the shoes carried her away.

She danced over fields and meadows, in rain and sunshine, by night and day. She danced across a bridge while the river roared beneath her. She danced past cottages where children hid from her pale face. She danced over sharp stones, through thorns, and across the lonely heath.

At last she came to the house of the old lady. The shutters were closed. A coffin was being carried through the gate, and mourners walked behind it.

“The old lady is dead,” someone said. “The girl she sheltered deserted her.”

Karen stretched out her arms.

“Forgive me!”

But the coffin went one way, and the red shoes took her another.

Now Karen’s pride was broken, but the punishment did not cease. She danced until she came to a small house beside the forest. Over the door hung a bright axe, for there lived the executioner.

She beat upon the door.

“Come out! Come out! I cannot enter, for I must dance.”

The executioner opened the door. He was a grave man, with gray hair and powerful hands.

“I know thee,” he said. “Thou art the girl with the dancing shoes.”

“Do not strike off my head,” Karen pleaded, “for then I could not repent. Cut off my feet instead, with the red shoes upon them.”

“Dost thou understand what thou askest?”

“I understand only that I cannot stop.”

He raised the axe, and with one blow severed the feet in the red shoes.

Away they danced across the heath, the little feet still moving, the bright shoes still flashing, until they disappeared into the gloomy wood.

The executioner bound Karen’s wounds and made wooden feet for her. He gave her crutches and taught her to walk. Then he spoke to her of sin and mercy, and Karen listened. She believed that because she had suffered greatly, she must now be good.

“I have paid dearly,” she thought. “Surely I am worthier than those who have never been punished.”

She wished to go to church, that all might see how humbly she walked upon wooden feet.

But when she reached the churchyard gate, the severed feet came dancing before her in the red shoes. They danced up the path and stood between her and the door.

Karen turned away in terror.

The next Sunday she tried again. She heard the organ and the congregation singing. She longed to enter; but again the red shoes danced before her, and she fled.

Then Karen wept, not yet because she had offended God, but because she could not sit among respectable people and be seen to suffer piously.

Many days passed. She went to the parsonage and begged for service.

“I am strong enough to work with my hands,” she said. “Do not pay me. Give me only a corner in which to sleep, and teach me to do what is right.”

The clergyman’s wife received her kindly. Karen carried water, swept the rooms, and cared for the children. She read to them from the Bible, and when they spoke of fine clothes or grand festivities, she warned them gently; but she did not tell her story to make herself important.

She no longer looked into windows to see her face. She no longer wished for admiration. When she heard music in the distance, she prayed for steadiness; and when she saw roses, she remembered the red shoes, yet roses did not fill her with longing, for God had made them red, and they were beautiful without pride.

Still she dared not enter the church.

“The red shoes will be there,” she thought. “I am not worthy.”

One Sunday the whole household went to worship. Karen remained alone in her little room. Through the open window came the sound of the organ, faint and solemn, and the singing of the psalm rose over the roofs.

Karen took the Bible upon her knees.

“O Lord,” she prayed, “I have nothing to give Thee. My beauty was never mine, and I made it an idol. My suffering was not goodness, yet I wore it like a crown. I do not ask to be seen among the righteous. Only let me be Thy servant, though I remain outside the door forever.”

Then the room grew bright.

Karen lifted her eyes.

Before her stood the angel. But now he bore no glittering sword. In his hands were branches covered with roses, white and red together. His face was mild, and his wings shone like the morning.

The angel touched the low ceiling, and it rose high above her. He touched the narrow walls, and they widened. The little room opened like a flower, and Karen saw the church with its arches and candles. She heard the organ, no longer far away but full and mighty, and she heard the voices of the people singing mercy and peace.

The clergyman stood at the altar; the sunlight streamed through the windows; and upon the light there seemed to shine a golden star.

Karen wished to rise, but she could not. She bowed her head.

“Mercy!” she whispered, and this time she did not ask mercy from pain, nor from shame, nor because others judged her. She asked it because she loved the goodness she had once despised.

The angel smiled.

The music swelled, and Karen’s heart became so full of peace and joy that it could hold no more. There was no pride in it, no thought of dresses, faces, shoes, or watching eyes. There was only gratitude.

When the household returned from church, they found Karen sitting with the Bible open upon her knees. Her face was calm, and beside her lay a branch of fresh roses, though it was winter.

She was dead.

But her soul had flown upon the sunshine to God, where no one asked about the red shoes, and where mercy had the final word.

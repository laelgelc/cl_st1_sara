### The Open Floor

Cobham Stilling was, by every computation acceptable to himself, the largest man in Westmere. His house had thirty-seven rooms (excluding offices, passages, and a glazed corridor which he called the orangery whenever strangers were present); his stable held six horses, though only two could safely be ridden; his garages contained three motor-cars in successive stages of obsolescence; and his lawns, terraces, forcing-houses, tennis-courts, kennels, peach-walls, poultry-runs and ornamental water required, on a conservative estimate, the divided energies of fourteen men and three boys.

That these accessories had been paid for chiefly out of his wife’s fortune did not affect his sense of proprietorship. Isabel’s money, like Isabel’s beauty, her composure, and the old French furniture inherited from her aunt, had become by marriage evidence of his discrimination.

“You see, my dear fellow,” he explained to Austin Wrayford one evening after dinner, “I don’t believe in doing things by halves. Never did. If a man’s got a place, let it be a place. If he keeps a horse, let it be a horse. Gad, what’s the use of crawling through life apologizing for your existence?”

They were standing at one of the long windows of the drawing-room, looking across the twilight gardens toward the river. The room behind them was full of flowers, lamps, cigarette smoke, and the subdued expectancy which descended upon Cobham’s guests whenever he began to expound his philosophy. He had been expounding it for some time.

Beyond the lower lawn, where the last borders melted into dusk, a new motor-launch lay moored at the landing. Its white hull retained, even at that distance, an air of aggressive expense. Cobham had brought it up from town that afternoon and had spent the first part of dinner describing its speed, the second its fittings, and the third the stupidity of the man from whom he had bought it.

“Fellow didn’t know what he had,” he continued. “No eye. No sporting instinct. These moneyed men are all alike—pile up fortunes and haven’t the faintest notion how to spend ’em. Now I may have my faults—”

His wife, bending over an embroidery-frame on the far side of the room, did not lift her head.

“—but no one can say I haven’t taste. Or courage. Taste without courage is no earthly use. You see a thing’s right, you go in and get it. That’s my principle.”

Wrayford watched the river darken between the clipped yews. He had heard Cobham’s principles before, usually in connection with an unpaid account.

Mrs. Swordsley, the rector’s wife, had moved nearer the window. She possessed the long, solicitous features of her husband, softened by the expression of one who knew that Providence, though well-intentioned, was perpetually mislaying parish subscriptions.

“Will you take people out in it tomorrow?” she asked, indicating the launch with a reverential motion.

“Take you all out? Why, of course! That’s what it’s for. I’m not one of your fellows who buys toys to hide ’em under covers. Everything I have is at my friends’ disposal.”

Mrs. Swordsley’s face brightened. She had never been in a motor-launch and had already conceived a vision of gliding past the houses of two ladies who had omitted her from their garden party.

Young Harcourt Bell, who had come down chiefly because Cobham had hinted that a syndicate was being formed to acquire certain copper properties, wondered whether confidential financial talk might occur on the water. Miss Althea Venn, who had lately decided that speed was romantic, wondered if Mr. Bell would sit beside her. General Torby, who distrusted machinery and had lost money in one of Cobham’s companies, wondered whether the launch was paid for. Lady Torby wondered the same thing, but with less hope of amusement.

Each guest, in short, saw in the white object below the lawn some private extension of desire; and Cobham, perorating before the window, saw only the reflection of his magnificence.

“Forty horse-power,” he said. “She’ll do eighteen miles against the stream. Electric light, refrigerator, patent lockers, silver-plated gear—everything complete. There’s a fellow living below Bray who has one nearly as good, and gives himself the airs of an admiral. I shall run past him as if he were anchored.”

“You told me last year,” Lady Torby observed, “that you disliked the river.”

Cobham laughed with exceptional heartiness, to show that she had contributed something diverting.

“My dear lady, I disliked crawling about in a punt. That’s not the river; that’s aquatic invalidism. Put an engine under me and I’ll show you whether I dislike it.”

“And the boathouse has been made ready?” Wrayford asked.

The question was casual, but Isabel’s needle paused above the silk.

“Ready? It’s been transformed. New cradle, new davits, runners greased, floor overhauled—the whole box of tricks. Cost me the devil of a sum, naturally. These local workmen think a man with a decent place is public beneficence in trousers.”

“The sliding floor still opens?”

“Still opens? My dear Austin, it slides like a card-table drawer. Had it all repaired. Boat comes in underneath, floor rolls back, you hoist her clear. Simple as daylight, if one has the intelligence to manage it.”

General Torby grunted. “Deep under there.”

“Deep enough. Twelve feet at the wall, sixteen in mid-channel.”

Mrs. Swordsley looked agreeably alarmed.

“Suppose someone walked in when the floor was open?”

“Then he’d get wet,” said Cobham, and his laugh released the others from the obligation of considering the matter.

Only Isabel did not smile. Her needle descended again, drawing through the fabric a thread of dark red silk.

Wrayford turned from the window. The sight of her in the luminous room—her head lowered, her pale hands occupied with their small, exact labour—gave him the impression of a person maintaining, by the measured repetition of an act, the integrity of a world that had already fallen. The others thought her cold. Cobham called her “a saint, but damned unresponsive.” Her mother-in-law praised her goodness with tears. Wrayford alone knew the amount of passion required for her stillness.

He had known it for ten months.

Their intimacy had begun, as such intimacies sometimes do among people trained to evade obvious hazards, with a discussion of another person’s suffering. Cobham’s mother, old Mrs. Stilling, lived in a small house near Richmond and adored her son with the devouring credulity of the long-widowed. She had sacrificed to him first her judgement, then her comfort, and finally, though she did not yet know it, the greater part of her income. Isabel’s concern for her had brought her repeatedly to Wrayford’s office, where inquiries about settlements and securities had led by imperceptible degrees to the exchange of those confidences which, between a man and a woman already thinking constantly of each other, are less the cause of surrender than its decorous notation.

Nothing visible had followed. They had met in drawing-rooms, at dinners, beneath the supervision of servants and flowers. Once, in a crowded railway carriage, their hands had touched beneath a folded newspaper. Twice Isabel had come to his office after dusk, and on the second occasion he had kissed her. The austerity of their opportunities had given each one the disproportionate intensity of a crime contemplated through a keyhole.

Cobham trusted Wrayford completely. He called him “my dry old stick,” entrusted him with impossible negotiations, ignored his advice, and cited the persistence of their friendship as evidence of his own loyalty.

“Austin knows me,” he would say after some particularly reckless disclosure. “He knows I’ve got a heart.”

Wrayford did know him; and knowledge had proved less a safeguard than Cobham supposed.

When the guests went up to dress, Wrayford remained in the drawing-room under pretence of finishing a cigar. Isabel had left her embroidery on the chair. He stood above it, looking at the half-worked pattern: a spray of leaves enclosing a red flower so tightly designed that it seemed pinned beneath the stitches.

A light step sounded in the corridor. He turned, but it was old Mrs. Stilling.

She had arrived unexpectedly that afternoon, attended by a maid almost as ancient as herself, and had been established in the south bedroom after a fluttering reunion during which Cobham kissed her forehead, reproached her for travelling alone, rang violently for tea, and forgot her existence until dinner.

Now she entered in a lavender dressing-gown, a lace scarf over her silver hair.

“My dear Austin, are you alone?”

He offered his arm and led her to a chair.

“I came to look for Cobham. He said he would come up after dinner, but young Bell tells me they are playing billiards.”

“He’ll come presently.”

“Yes. Of course. He is so much occupied when the house is full.” Her eyes moved fondly around the room, as if the lamps, the Sèvres bowls, and the banks of roses were emanations of her son’s nature. “How beautifully he does everything.”

Wrayford murmured assent.

“And that wonderful boat! So generous of him to think of amusing his friends. He always had generous impulses. As a child, if one gave him sixpence he spent it at once.”

There were facts which satire itself could not improve.

Mrs. Stilling smoothed the lace over her knees. “Austin, I wanted to ask you—Cobham tells me there is some little delay about the interest on the Haversham bonds.”

Wrayford looked down at her.

“A temporary delay, he said. Some transfer. I hope I signed the papers properly?”

“You signed what was put before you.”

“I am so stupid about business. Cobham says women only confuse things by trying to understand them.” She smiled. “He takes all that trouble off my hands.”

The billiard-room door opened across the hall, and Cobham’s voice rolled out in jovial denunciation of someone’s stroke.

Mrs. Stilling listened with pleasure.

“He has had anxieties lately, I fear. You must stand by him, Austin. There is no one he relies on as he does on you.”

Wrayford answered after a moment: “I shall do what I can.”

“Oh, I knew it.” She rose, reassured. “And you must persuade Isabel not to worry. She is devoted to him, but she takes things so gravely.”

At the doorway she turned.

“You lawyers see the darker side, perhaps. But those who love Cobham know that everything comes right for him.”

Everything had, so far; but Wrayford, watching her pass into the hall, reflected that this was chiefly because the consequences had always come wrong for someone else.

At midnight the house settled slowly into silence.

The Torbys had retired early. Mrs. Swordsley had carried to bed a novel concealed beneath a devotional magazine. Harcourt Bell and Althea Venn had prolonged their conversation on the upper landing until the approach of a footman separated finance from romance. Cobham, after billiards, had taken Bell to the smoking-room and opened a second bottle of brandy on the ground that the first had been “poisonously mild.”

Wrayford sat in his room with the lamp extinguished. His window looked over the lawn to the river, where a few broken reflections trembled between banks of cloud. The wind had risen. Now and then it pressed a branch against the glass with a dry, admonitory finger.

At a quarter past twelve he saw a light pass along the lower terrace and disappear behind the yews.

He waited five minutes, descended by the service staircase, and left the house through the garden door.

The air was close and metallic. Behind the woods, the gathering storm showed at intervals as a colourless dilatation of the sky. He crossed the damp lawn, found the narrow path between the laurels, and went down toward the boathouse.

It stood on piles beyond the landing-stage, an ambitious little structure of red brick and black timber built during Cobham’s brief enthusiasm for rowing. That enthusiasm had left behind two sculls warped in their racks, a racing-shell split along the seam, seven club blazers, a silver challenge cup never challenged for, and a wall of photographs recording the host in nautical attitudes. Later passions had added fishing-rods, water-skis, a canoe imported from Canada, a punt-pole with an ivory handle, and the oily accessories of the new launch.

The door was unlocked. Wrayford entered and found Isabel waiting beside a shuttered window.

She wore a dark cloak over her evening dress, and in the uncertain light from a small electric torch her face seemed detached from the obscurity, grave and almost bodiless.

“You came,” he said.

“You asked me.”

Her voice was low, without reproach; yet its fatigue made the words sound like an account of all she had done because someone had asked it of her.

He closed the door. Beneath the planked floor, water moved among the piles with a recurrent sucking sound. The whole building appeared to breathe uneasily.

“Is he in the smoking-room?”

“He was when I came down. With Mr. Bell.”

“He may keep him for hours.”

“Or dismiss him in five minutes.”

They listened.

From the house came no sound. A gust passed over the roof and subsided in the reeds.

Wrayford had imagined taking her in his arms as soon as they were alone; but the knowledge he carried had made every gesture difficult. She saw the inhibition and drew her cloak more closely around her.

“It’s worse,” she said.

He did not ask what she meant.

“How much worse?”

“Nearly everything is gone.”

Her face did not change. He wondered whether she had rehearsed all possible figures until number itself had ceased to terrify her.

“The North Andean shares?”

“Worthless. The land company is in liquidation. The advances on the mills can’t be renewed. There are mortgages on the house and farms—first and second charges in some cases.”

“My money?”

“What remains of the capital is tied to the boy. He can’t touch that. But the income has been anticipated for three years.”

She looked toward the floor.

“And his mother?”

Wrayford felt the cruelty of answering, yet silence had already answered for him.

“He obtained her signature last winter. She thought the papers concerned a change of trustees. The Haversham bonds were sold. So were the railway debentures. He has used the proceeds as security.”

“All of them?”

“Almost.”

The water struck a crossbeam below with a smothered thud.

Isabel walked to the end of the boathouse, where the launch lay hidden under canvas behind a waist-high rail. Her torch moved across brass fittings, coiled rope and newly painted boards. Even concealed, the machine exhaled its recent cost.

“What can be done?”

“I can try to protect the residue. There may be an arrangement with the principal creditors. The house will probably have to go.”

“He won’t consent.”

“Consent will cease to matter.”

“He will say he has been badly treated.”

“Yes.”

“That the market turned against him.”

“Yes.”

“That he did it all for us.”

Wrayford did not answer.

She rested a hand on the rail. “He told me tonight that the launch was a bargain.”

“It cost eleven hundred pounds.”

A brief sound escaped her; it was not laughter, but it had passed too near laughter to be mistaken for a sigh.

“He ordered emerald studs last week. He said a man in his position owed it to his wife to be properly dressed.”

“Isabel—”

“And she loves him.”

“His mother?”

“She would give him the last chair in her room and thank him for relieving her of it. When she came this afternoon, she whispered that I must be patient because great men need understanding.”

The controlled accuracy of her tone frightened him more than grief would have done. He crossed the space between them.

“You cannot stay through this.”

“I shall stay.”

“You have said that before. You didn’t know then what he had done.”

“I knew enough.”

“Not about his mother’s money.”

“That is one reason more.”

“One reason to leave him.”

“One reason not to leave her alone with him.”

He took her hand. It was cold, but it remained in his.

“Come away with me.”

The words, so often suppressed that he had thought them refined out of existence, sounded crude and youthful in the dark building.

She answered quietly: “And my son?”

“Bring him.”

“He is twelve. Do you think Cobham would let me?”

“We could fight him.”

“In public?”

“If necessary.”

“And his mother? When it all comes out? When he says that my conduct ruined his credit, destroyed his career, drove him into speculation—”

“Must your life be governed by what he will say?”

“It has been governed by it for fourteen years.”

“Then end it.”

The light in her hand sank. He could no longer see her eyes.

“It would not end. It would only change its name.”

A loose shutter rattled. Wrayford put his hands on her shoulders and felt, beneath the cloak, the fine rigidity with which she sustained herself.

“You love me.”

“Yes.”

“You trust me?”

“Yes.”

“Then come.”

She shook her head.

He drew her to him. For an instant she remained unyielding; then her forehead fell against his chest, and all her stillness seemed to pass into him as a violent trembling. He held her without speaking. The dark, the moving water, the smell of damp timber and machine-oil enclosed them in a world temporarily stripped of household, law, money, child and age—a world reduced to the fact that they had found each other too late and could not release each other soon enough.

At length she drew back.

“I do my duty,” she said.

“Don’t say that.”

“I must say it. I have to hear what it sounds like.”

“It sounds like something Cobham would praise in an after-dinner speech.”

“Yes. Perhaps that is why I hate it.”

Her hand went to the latch of her cloak, then dropped.

“I sit opposite him. I order what he likes. I ask whether the room is warm enough for him. When he has a headache I lower the blinds. When he is ill I am afraid.”

“That is habit, not duty.”

“Perhaps. But I do it.”

She turned toward him, and the beam of the torch slipped across her face. What he saw there was neither hysteria nor the abandonment he had desired; it was the nakedness of a mind that had kept one thought imprisoned until the thought had consumed the prison.

“All day,” she said, “while I do these things, I wish that he were dead.”

Wrayford’s hands fell from her.

She went on in the same level voice: “Not when he is cruel. Cruelty passes; one can resist it. I wish it when he is pleased with himself. When he praises his generosity. When he tells the boy that honour is a man’s most valuable possession. When his mother looks at him. When he kisses me before people. I think: if he were dead, the room would be empty of him. The chair, the air, the future—empty.”

“Isabel.”

“I have tried to call it anger. It is not anger. It is a wish. It is always there.”

He stared past her at the covered launch. Thunder moved, still distant, beyond the hills.

“You mustn’t remain with him.”

“Because I wish him dead?”

“Because he is killing you.”

“No. That would be simpler. He leaves me alive.”

She extinguished the torch.

Darkness closed over them, complete except for a thin line under the door.

Then, from beyond the laurels, came a voice.

“Hallo there!”

Isabel’s fingers seized Wrayford’s sleeve.

Cobham called again, nearer: “Who’s down at the river?”

They stood motionless.

A wandering light appeared between the slats of the wall, vanished, and reappeared. Cobham was crossing the landing with a lantern.

“Damn these paths,” he said aloud. “Why the devil can’t they keep the gravel rolled?”

The door shook under his hand.

“Locked? Hallo!”

It was not locked. In another second he would discover that.

Wrayford’s mind received the surrounding objects with a sudden, unnatural precision: the rail, the canvas-covered hull, the chain hanging from the roof, the racks of oars, the iron handle set flush in the floor beside him. He remembered Cobham’s demonstration that afternoon. The central planking, mounted on greased runners, could be drawn aside to admit the launch. The mechanism was fitted with a counterweight; one strong pull would open a black rectangle of water between the door and the inner platform.

There was no other exit. The shuttered window was too narrow; behind the launch the wall stood directly above the river.

The latch lifted.

Wrayford gripped the iron handle and pulled.

The floor moved with appalling ease. Planks slipped under the fixed platform; darkness widened at their feet; the sound of water rose abruptly from below. He drew Isabel behind the rail beside the launch.

The door opened.

Cobham entered holding a storm-lantern. The light shone upward into his flushed face. He had no hat, and his evening coat was unbuttoned. His gait, though careful, possessed the ceremonious instability of a man determined that the night should not suspect him of drunkenness.

“Who’s there?” he demanded.

Neither answered.

The wind forced the door shut behind him with a bang. Cobham turned, swore, and raised the lantern.

“Thought I saw a light. Austin? Is that you, my boy?”

He took a step forward.

Wrayford saw at once that Cobham could not see the open floor. The lantern dazzled him; beyond its small circle the darkness appeared solid. One more step would bring him to the edge.

“Stop!” Wrayford said.

Cobham peered into the blackness.

“Who the devil—?”

His foot came down where the planking had been.

There was an instant in which his body seemed to remain upright after its support had vanished. The lantern flew from his hand and struck the wall. Then he plunged downward, hit the edge with a heavy crack, and disappeared.

The lantern went out.

A tremendous splash struck the boards and walls. Water surged below them; something battered against a pile.

“Cobham!” Isabel cried.

Wrayford was already over the rail. He kicked off one shoe, caught the edge, and lowered himself into the opening.

“Where are you?”

A choking shout answered from under the platform.

Wrayford dropped.

The water, colder and deeper than he had expected, closed above his head. He came up striking against timber. Somewhere to his left Cobham thrashed and cursed. There was no light; the boathouse roof and the storm-black sky were one darkness.

“Keep still!” Wrayford shouted. “I’m coming.”

“You did this!” Cobham gasped. “By God—you—”

A wave broke into his mouth.

Wrayford swam toward the voice. His hand encountered cloth, slipped, then closed on Cobham’s collar. Cobham twisted around and seized him with both arms.

“Let go! You’ll drown us both.”

“Get me out!”

“Let go, damn you!”

Cobham’s weight dragged him under. Wrayford drove a knee upward, broke the hold, surfaced and seized a hanging chain. It ran from the hoisting gear above; slimy links cut into his palm.

Cobham emerged beside him, coughing.

“The chain,” Wrayford said. “Take hold.”

“I can’t.”

“It’s against your shoulder.”

“I’ve hurt my arm. Austin—don’t leave me.”

Wrayford found his wrist and forced his hand around the chain.

“Hold it.”

The chain swayed under their combined weight. Wrayford groped for a beam with his free hand. His fingers touched wet wood and slid off.

Above them Isabel called: “Where are you?”

“Here!” Wrayford answered. “Bring the lantern—anything.”

“It’s broken.”

“An oar. Lower an oar.”

He heard her moving on the platform. Wood clattered against wood; an oar struck the rail and fell lengthwise across the gap.

“Cobham!” she called.

Her voice had changed. It was high and clear, a voice made for carrying over danger.

There was no answer but his gasping.

“Cobham, can you hear me?”

“Yes!”

“Put out your hand. I have the oar.”

“Where?”

“Toward my voice.”

The blade descended, striking the water some feet away. Isabel drew it back and lowered it again.

Wrayford felt Cobham release the chain with one hand.

“Don’t let go yet.”

“I can reach it.”

“You can’t. Hold still.”

The blade swept closer. It touched Wrayford’s shoulder, passed across his chest, and knocked against Cobham’s upraised arm.

“Got it!” Cobham cried.

“Both hands,” Isabel said.

“I told you—my arm—”

“Take it.”

Cobham shifted his grip. The chain lurched violently. Wrayford’s numbed fingers began to open.

For one confused instant he saw, not with his eyes but with the whole intelligence of his body, the moral diagram in which they were suspended: Isabel above, holding out deliverance to the man whose death she had wished; Cobham clutching at the offered instrument; himself beneath them, keeping afloat the life that imprisoned hers. The thought was without eloquence and passed more swiftly than pain.

Cobham caught the oar with both hands.

“Pull!” he screamed. “For God’s sake, pull!”

Isabel braced the shaft against the rail. Cobham kicked upward. His shoes beat Wrayford’s side; his elbow struck Wrayford’s mouth. Wrayford lost the chain, recovered it, lost it again.

The oar scraped over the boards.

“I’m slipping!” Cobham shouted.

“You’re safe,” Isabel said. “Move your hand higher.”

“Pull me up!”

The blade rose. Cobham’s head and shoulders appeared level with the platform. He flung one arm over the edge.

Wrayford reached for the chain.

It swung beyond his fingers.

He went under.

The water was full of noise: Cobham’s boots hammering against the wall, Isabel’s breath, the grind of the oar, thunder breaking at last above the roof. Wrayford struck downward, found no bottom, and rose beneath the fixed part of the floor. His head hit planking. He thrust sideways, but one shoulder caught between two piles.

For a second he was calm. He knew the direction of the opening; he knew he must sink, turn, and swim outward. He filled his lungs with what air remained and pushed himself down.

A current took him crookedly. Rope or weed twisted around his ankle. He bent to tear it loose, lost his sense of the piles, and came up again beneath timber.

Above, Cobham was sobbing and swearing.

Wrayford beat both hands against the underside of the floor.

“Isabel!”

Whether he spoke or only formed the name he could not tell. Water entered his mouth. The blackness pressed upon his face with a soft, deliberate weight.

On the platform Isabel had dragged Cobham high enough for him to hook his sound arm around the rail. He rolled sideways, bringing one knee onto the boards, and collapsed at her feet.

“My shoulder,” he groaned. “It’s broken. Austin—where’s Austin?”

She dropped the oar into the opening.

“Austin!”

No answer came.

She knelt at the edge, reaching into darkness.

“Austin!”

Cobham raised himself on one elbow. “He was there. He had hold of the chain.”

“Call him.”

“What?”

“Call him!”

Cobham crawled to the edge.

“Austin, old man! Where are you?”

They listened.

The storm had reached the river. Rain began to strike the roof in separate heavy drops. The water below heaved against the piles, making the building tremble.

Isabel seized the oar and swept it through the opening. The blade struck a beam, a chain, the side of the launch. Nothing answered.

“I’m going in,” she said.

Cobham caught her cloak.

“Don’t be a fool.”

She tore herself free.

“You’ll only drown too. The current runs under the floor. He may have been carried out.”

“Then go after him.”

Cobham stared up at her. His face, barely visible when the lightning whitened the shutters, was naked with incredulity.

“My arm’s smashed. I can hardly breathe. I went under twice.”

She stood without moving.

“Austin can swim,” he added. “He’s a strong swimmer. He’ll come up outside.”

They waited for him to come up.

Cobham began calling again, at first loudly, then with increasing intervals, as though each silence entitled him to reduce his effort.

“Austin! Hallo, Austin! Answer, my boy!”

Rain poured across the roof. Isabel found the switch near the door and pressed it. For a moment nothing happened; then two electric bulbs flickered into yellow life.

The opened floor lay between them like a rectangular grave. Brown water churned through it, littered with leaves and splinters. The chain swung slowly above the surface.

There was no sign of Wrayford.

Cobham looked from the water to his wife.

“What in God’s name happened? Why was the floor open?”

She did not answer.

He sat back, nursing his shoulder.

“I came down because I was uneasy about the launch. Bell said the storm was moving in, and I remembered these damned fools had left the river doors unfastened. Thought I saw someone with a torch.” His eyes sharpened despite the brandy, the pain and the water streaming from his hair. “You were here.”

“Yes.”

“With Austin.”

“Yes.”

The answer seemed to affect him less than the fact that it was made without apology. He examined her face as though searching for the familiar arrangement of things.

“What were you doing?”

She looked into the water.

“Austin came to tell me about the money.”

“What money?”

“Your mother’s securities. Mine. The mortgages.”

He gave a harsh laugh, then winced and clutched his shoulder.

“So that’s it. Professional confidences! By George, that explains a good deal.”

“He told me the truth.”

“The truth? He told you his version. Lawyers! A set of undertakers measuring a man before he’s dead.” He shifted painfully against the rail. “And then he opened the floor.”

“He opened it because you were coming.”

Cobham’s face altered.

“Because I was coming?”

She remained silent.

“He meant me to fall.”

“No.”

“Then why open it? Answer me that.”

“There was nowhere for us to go.”

Cobham looked at her with an almost childlike concentration. Gradually the separate facts arranged themselves in the only form his nature could receive: a form in which he stood at the centre, magnificent in injury.

“You and Austin,” he said.

She closed her eyes.

“My oldest friend.”

The phrase emerged with such automatic fitness that she knew he had already entered the future—had already found the words with which the event could be recounted.

“My wife and my oldest friend.”

“He went into the water after you.”

Cobham appeared not to hear.

“I trusted him. There wasn’t a man living I trusted as I trusted Austin. Brought him into the house. Made him one of us. Told him everything.”

“He knew everything.”

“And this is my reward.”

“He died for you.”

The words arrested him.

Neither moved. Beneath them the water continued its dark mechanical labour, entering, recoiling, entering again.

Cobham lowered his eyes. When he spoke, his voice had softened.

“We don’t know that he’s dead.”

Isabel knelt once more and called Wrayford’s name.

Only the rain answered.

Servants came at last. Cobham’s shouting, less effective than the electric bell he eventually remembered beside the door, brought the butler, two footmen, the chauffeur and Harcourt Bell running through the flooded gardens. Lanterns were fetched; ropes dragged the water; the launch was shifted; men went out in a punt with boat-hooks while the storm passed eastward in diminishing detonations.

They found Wrayford at dawn, wedged beneath the outer platform.

By then Cobham had been carried to the house, examined by a doctor, bathed, bandaged and fortified. His shoulder was dislocated, not broken. There was a cut above his temple and a dark bruise along his jaw, injuries serious enough to gratify his instinct for evidence without compromising his recovery.

Isabel remained below until they brought the body in.

The storm had washed the sky pale. Morning lay over the lawns with a heartless freshness; wet roses shed drops along the terrace, and the new launch, raised at last in its cradle, glittered under the boathouse lamps.

Old Mrs. Stilling met Isabel in the hall.

The little woman had dressed hastily in a black gown, as if death, however unexpected, required the compliment of immediate conformity. Her hands fluttered toward Isabel and stopped.

“They say Austin— They say poor Austin—”

Isabel put an arm around her.

“And Cobham?” the mother whispered. “My boy?”

“He is safe.”

Mrs. Stilling began to cry.

“Oh, my dear—my dear! How merciful!”

Her head sank against Isabel’s wet cloak. Behind her, through the open library door, Cobham’s voice could be heard addressing the doctor, General Torby, and anyone else whom anxiety or curiosity had drawn from bed.

“Never thought of myself for an instant. Not for one instant. ‘Austin,’ I called, ‘save yourself, old man.’ But he wouldn’t leave me. That was Austin all over—loyal to the core. We may have had our differences; what close friends don’t? But at the last— Gad, I can’t speak of it.”

There was a pause during which he appeared to overcome what could not safely be expressed.

“Terrible loss. Terrible. One of those mysterious dispensations no man can understand. There we were—the floor open, black as pitch, the river in flood. He goes down; I’m spared. A miraculous escape, the doctor calls it. Simply miraculous.”

Mrs. Stilling lifted her tearful face to Isabel’s.

“He is so brave,” she murmured. “And so tender-hearted. Listen—he can hardly speak.”

Isabel listened.

Cobham’s voice, lowered now to the register he used for intimate grief, flowed steadily from the library. Already the rough edges of the night had been smoothed; already accident, friendship and providence had assumed their appointed places around the central marvel of his survival. By luncheon he would have found a phrase for the plunge, by dinner an exact description of the instant when he had urged Wrayford to save himself. In a week he would believe it.

His mother trembled against Isabel, asking to be taken to him.

Isabel led her across the hall.

At the library threshold Cobham looked up from the sofa. His bandaged arm lay in a sling; a rug covered his knees; a glass of brandy and milk stood at his side. The assembled faces turned toward Isabel with that involuntary expectation which a room reserves for the wife of a rescued man.

Cobham extended his uninjured hand.

“My dear,” he said brokenly, “we must bear it together.”

Behind his head the long windows stood open on the brilliant rain-washed garden. Far below, beyond the roses and the motionless yews, the river passed the boathouse without a sign, carrying the last leaves of the storm toward the sea.

Isabel crossed the room and took her husband’s hand.

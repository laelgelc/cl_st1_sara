### The Morning After

“So you still maintain,” said Sergei Nikolaevich, “that a man’s whole life may be altered by his surroundings—that he is kind or cruel, useful or useless, according to the conditions into which he is put?”

“No, I maintain nothing of the sort,” replied Ivan Vasilievich. “I say only that you cannot judge a life by theories. You speak of surroundings, education, convictions. Very good. But sometimes it is neither surroundings nor convictions. Sometimes it is chance.”

“Chance is only a name for conditions we have failed to examine,” said Sergei Nikolaevich.

“There! That is exactly the sort of thing young men say now. Everything must be examined, labelled, accounted for. But suppose a man’s whole course is changed in one night—by something he sees accidentally?”

“Then the thing he sees changes his convictions.”

“Perhaps. Yet why does he see it? Why that night? Why not an hour earlier or later? No, my dear fellow, you may arrange it as scientifically as you please, but chance remains chance.”

We were sitting after supper in the library of Pyotr Alexandrovich’s country house. Ivan Vasilievich, though no longer young, was one of those men whose presence is welcome everywhere. He had never held any important position, had acquired neither rank nor fortune, and had even earned among practical people the reputation of having wasted his abilities. Yet no one called him a failure to his face. His honesty, his warmth, his almost youthful readiness to be interested in other people made the word seem absurd when applied to him.

“You are speaking of yourself, of course,” said one of the ladies.

“Of whom else should I speak? Other people’s lives I do not know.”

“And was yours changed in a single night?”

“Entirely.”

“By love?” asked the youngest lady.

“By love—and by something else.”

“There must have been a woman.”

“Certainly there was a woman. When I was young there was always a woman. But in this case there was one woman only. Or, at least, there seemed to be only one.”

“You see,” said Sergei Nikolaevich. “Not chance, then, but love.”

“You interrupt before I have begun. That is the modern method of inquiry, I suppose. First decide, then listen. Very well. I shall tell you, though it is not a pleasant story.”

He moved his chair nearer the table, rested his hands on his knees, and for a moment smiled to himself.

“I was twenty-two,” he began, “and a student at the provincial university. I studied little, I confess, but that does not matter. I was healthy, rich enough for my needs, fond of dancing, fond of horses, and pleased with everybody—most of all with myself. I drank champagne, but was never drunk; I played cards, but without passion; I had debts, but not serious ones. In short, I possessed all the virtues that come from having no reason to be unhappy.

“At that time I was in love with Varvara B——, whom we called Varinka. Even now, when I close my eyes, I can see her as she was then: tall, slender, always holding herself very straight, though without stiffness, with a small proud head, a smooth forehead, bright eyes and a smile that seemed not merely to appear on her lips but to illuminate her whole face.

“She was not what is now called a beauty.”

“Then she was a beauty,” said the youngest lady.

“Yes, precisely. You understand. Nowadays every pretty girl is photographed from six directions, and her beauty is measured, discussed and explained until nothing remains of it. Varinka could not have been explained. When she entered a room, the room became different. People spoke more softly, men stood straighter, and even women who disliked her looked at her.

“This happened during the last week before Lent. The provincial marshal of the nobility gave a ball. He was a good-natured old man, enormously stout, with a red face and silver hair brushed over his temples. His wife was even stouter and received the guests in a puce velvet gown, with diamonds on her broad bosom and a white feather trembling in her hair. Their house was the finest in the town. There were mirrors between the windows, crystal chandeliers, musicians from the estate, supper enough for a regiment and champagne enough for two.

“I arrived early. I wore a swallow-tailed coat made by the best tailor in the town, a white waistcoat, new gloves and boots so highly polished that I looked at them with pleasure whenever I could do so unnoticed. You laugh, but I was young, and one’s boots matter greatly at twenty-two. I intended to dance every dance with Varinka that propriety allowed.

“She wore a white muslin dress over a white satin slip, a pink sash with long ends falling almost to the floor, white gloves that reached scarcely to her thin elbows, and white satin slippers. In her fan there was one pink feather. Nothing could have been simpler, but I remember every detail because that white and pink seemed to me then the colours of innocence, happiness, spring—of everything good that existed or could exist.

“I had already danced the first quadrille with her and hoped to secure the mazurka, but a certain engineer named Anisimov forestalled me. I disliked him for it. That is not true—I hated him. Yet I loved everybody so much that evening that even my hatred was benevolent. I thought him clumsy, vain and objectionable, but I was ready to forgive him because he too could not help admiring Varinka.

“During the mazurka I stood near the door, pretending indifference, though I followed her with my eyes. She danced beautifully, not because she made striking movements, but because nothing in her movement seemed difficult. She glided, turned, paused, lowered her eyes and smiled; and the pink ends of her sash floated behind her. Whenever she approached, I imagined she had come for me. Whenever she moved away, I felt that something had been taken from the room.

“Anisimov did not dance the whole mazurka with her. In those days the mazurka was not the exercise you know now, two turns round a room and then an exhausted collapse. It lasted an hour, sometimes longer, with figures, choices, crossings and all sorts of inventions. A man might dance with several ladies, and a lady with several men. Thus I succeeded in dancing with Varinka often enough, though never enough for me.

“Once, after a figure, she came toward me instead of the gentleman who stood beside me.

“‘Pardon,’ she said, laughing. ‘I mistook you for someone else.’

“‘Then do not correct the mistake.’

“‘That would be dishonest.’

“‘Toward whom?’

“‘Toward him.’

“‘And toward me?’

“‘Toward you too, perhaps.’

“She gave me her hand, and we crossed the room. I scarcely felt the floor. I had no body; I was only joy wearing a white waistcoat.

“When the figure ended, I begged for the first waltz after supper.

“‘If Papa does not take me away.’

“‘Do not let him.’

“‘I always obey Papa.’

“‘Then I shall persuade him.’

“She smiled and opened her fan. One of the pink feathers, already loose, came away in her gloved fingers.

“‘Take it,’ she said. ‘But only because you danced the figure well.’

“I took the feather and slipped it inside my glove, where it touched my palm. That touch seemed to pass through my whole body. A ridiculous thing, of course, but there is no use telling of youth if one is ashamed of its ridiculousness.

“Just then the master of the house approached us with Varinka’s mother, a stately lady with a diamond clasp in her hair.

“‘Where is your father, my dear?’ she asked. ‘Bring him here. We must make him dance.’

“Varinka looked across the room.

“‘Papa does not dance now.’

“‘Nonsense! He used to dance wonderfully. Go and fetch him.’

“Everyone turned toward the far end of the ballroom, where Varinka’s father stood among several elderly gentlemen. He was a colonel, tall and broad-shouldered, with a fresh ruddy face, grey moustaches, white whiskers and hair combed forward over his temples. He resembled his daughter around the eyes and mouth, though his features were larger and more severe. His uniform was old-fashioned but splendidly kept: silver epaulettes, a high collar, decorations upon his breast, and a sword at his side. He wore a soft suede glove upon his right hand.

“When Varinka led him forward, he protested.

“‘No, no, gentlemen. I have forgotten everything.’

“‘You have forgotten nothing,’ cried the hostess. ‘We shall see whether the old school can still put us to shame.’

“The colonel smiled, shrugged his shoulders and unfastened his sword. A young man took it from him. Then he drew on his other glove carefully, finger by finger.

“‘Everything must be done according to rule,’ he said.

“I remember those words distinctly.

“He stood beside his daughter and waited for the music. At that moment I noticed his boots. They were not fashionable boots with pointed toes and high heels, such as we wore. They were plain calfskin boots, well blacked, but square at the toes and made, evidently, by the regimental shoemaker.

“I was moved almost to tears by those boots. I thought: he does not buy elegant boots for himself because he spends everything on dressing his beloved daughter. He is a noble, devoted father, denying himself for her sake. Whether this was true I do not know. Most likely it was not. But that does not matter—or rather, it matters very much, because it shows how I interpreted everything that evening.

“The music began. The colonel stamped one foot, stretched the other, and moved off with surprising lightness. He was heavy, and his movements were not always graceful; once his foot came down too firmly and his spur caught the floor. But he knew the old manner of the mazurka, stately and bold. He advanced, retreated, struck one boot against the other, dropped suddenly upon one knee, and Varinka, smiling and adjusting her step to his, circled around him. Her white dress rustled, the pink sash streamed, and he looked up at her with a tenderness so open and so proud that everyone applauded.

“When he rose, he drew her toward him, placed his hands on either side of her face, and kissed her forehead. Then, instead of leading her to the gentleman with whom she had been dancing, he brought her to me.

“‘Here, sir,’ he said. ‘You must dance with her now.’

“‘But I am not her partner.’

“‘That does not matter. Dance with her.’

“He smiled and replaced his sword.

“I cannot tell you how I loved him at that moment. I loved his red face and grey moustaches, his unfashionable boots, his firm voice, his tenderness for Varinka. I loved the hostess with her diamonds and her exposed old shoulders; I loved Anisimov, though he had stolen my mazurka; I loved the footmen, the musicians, the servants carrying ices; I even loved a young man who had stepped upon my boot and failed to apologise. I was filled with a happiness so abundant that it had to overflow upon everyone.

“After supper we waltzed and waltzed. The musicians repeated the same tunes until the candles burned low and the windows began to pale, but I did not feel tired. Varinka’s face had grown flushed, and little curls had loosened near her ears. Each time she passed me, the air stirred by her movement seemed warm and fragrant. Once I asked whether she was weary.

“‘Not in the least.’

“‘Will you dance again?’

“‘Again.’

“‘And after that?’

“‘If Papa permits.’

“The colonel was standing near the card room. When I approached him, he looked at me with good-humoured amusement.

“‘You young people would dance through Judgment Day,’ he said.

“‘Only if the music were good, Colonel.’

“‘And the partner pretty?’

“‘That is indispensable.’

“He laughed and gave permission for one more waltz.

“At last, after four in the morning, the ball ended. Varinka wrapped herself in a white shawl. Her mother put a lace hood over her hair, and the colonel, already in his military overcoat, supervised everything: the carriage, the cloak, the maid, the foot-warmer. Before Varinka entered the carriage, she turned to me.

“‘You have not lost my feather?’

“I showed it inside my glove.

“‘Guard it,’ she said.

“The colonel helped her into the carriage and tucked the rug about her knees. Then he looked back at me and nodded, almost as one friend to another. The carriage drove away.

“I returned home, but there was no possibility of sleep. My elder brother was asleep in the adjoining room, breathing evenly, with one hand under his cheek. Our servant Petrusha came in to help me undress, but his sleepy face seemed so gentle and touching that I sent him away. I removed my coat, then put it on again. Whenever I lay down, music began sounding in my head, and I saw Varinka coming toward me in her white dress; or I saw the colonel dropping on one knee, his square-toed boot extended, while everyone applauded.

“The rooms were warm, close and unbearably still. I went outside.

“It was near dawn. The town lay under a wet grey mist. Snow remained on the roofs and in patches beside the road, but it was melting; water dripped from eaves, and beneath the blackened snow the earth showed through. Carts were already moving toward the market. The horses’ flanks steamed. Drivers sat hunched in their sheepskins, and the runners grated where the snow was thin.

“Varinka’s family lived near the edge of town, beyond a large open field. Without deciding to go there, I found myself walking in that direction. I wanted only to pass her house, to see the shutters behind which she slept, and perhaps the carriage that had brought her home. You know the foolish occupations of a lover.

“Across the field something dark appeared through the fog. At first I thought it was a line of trees. Then I heard music—not the mazurka, not a waltz, but a sharp, disagreeable sound: fifes shrilling and a drum beating the same hard measure over and over.

“‘What can that be?’ I thought.

“A blacksmith in a greasy sheepskin was walking ahead of me. I caught up with him.

“‘What are they doing there?’

“He looked toward the dark lines.

“‘Driving a Tartar through the ranks. Tried to desert.’

“His voice was ordinary, almost bored.

“I went nearer.

“Two rows of soldiers stood facing one another, their rifles held upright. They wore dark uniforms and tall caps, and in the fog their faces seemed all alike—grey, sleepy, expressionless. Behind them stood the fifer and drummer. The fifer’s cheeks swelled; the drumsticks rose and fell. The music was lively in tempo, but cruel, without melody, as if it too were an order.

“Between the rows something moved toward us.

“It was a man stripped to the waist, his wrists tied to the rifles of two soldiers who walked beside him. I could not understand at first what I was seeing. His trousers hung low upon his hips; his bare feet slipped in the wet snow. His head drooped, then jerked upward. On one side a blow fell; his body sprang away from it. A blow struck from the other side; he sprang back. The soldiers supporting him dragged him forward.

“Beside them walked a tall officer in a military greatcoat and cap.

“I recognised the square, ruddy face, the grey moustaches and white whiskers.

“It was Varinka’s father.

“My God!

“The prisoner came nearer. Each soldier, as he passed, stepped forward and brought down a stick upon the man’s back. The sticks whistled, struck, and rose again. Whistle, blow; whistle, blow. The drum beat. The fife shrieked. The colonel marched firmly beside the prisoner, looking first at his feet, then at the soldiers, breathing out through pursed lips.

“The Tartar turned his face from side to side. It was a narrow, exhausted face, glistening with tears.

“‘Brothers, have mercy! Brothers, have mercy!’ he kept saying. ‘Brothers, have mercy!’

“But the brothers did not have mercy.

“He stumbled. The soldiers holding the rifles pulled him upright, and another blow fell. His knees bent, his head dropped, and again they dragged him forward. From far off I had imagined his back to be covered by some red and grey cloth. When he was near, I saw that it was bare flesh. Or rather, it no longer resembled a back. It was a wet, swollen, striped, unnatural thing, red in places, dark in others, with blood running down toward the waistband.

“I remember thinking, absurdly: Can a man’s body become like that? I had seen injuries, but this was not an injury. It was as though the person had been erased and only a suffering object remained.

“Suddenly the colonel stopped.

“One of the soldiers had struck too lightly. Perhaps he was tired; perhaps he had pity; perhaps the stick had merely glanced aside. The colonel sprang toward him.

“‘What are you doing?’ he shouted. ‘Is that how you strike?’

“The soldier, a small young fellow, stood rigid.

“‘I’ll teach you!’

“The colonel struck him across the face with his suede-gloved hand.

“‘Hit properly! Do you hear? Properly!’

“He turned to an orderly.

“‘Fresh sticks!’

“Then he resumed his place beside the prisoner.

“I saw the glove distinctly—the same kind of glove he had drawn on finger by finger before dancing with his daughter. I saw the sword at his side, the firm step, the straight back. Everything must be done according to rule.

“At that moment he looked toward me.

“Whether he recognised me I do not know. I think he did. His eyes rested on me for an instant; then he frowned slightly and turned away, as though I were a stranger, or as though what was happening did not concern anyone outside the ranks.

“The procession passed.

“‘Brothers, have mercy!’ came the voice again.

“The drum beat. The sticks whistled. The colonel called something out. Then the fog swallowed them, though for some time I could still hear the drum and that same thin, tearing plea.

“I stood beside the blacksmith.

“‘They’ll kill him,’ I said.

“The blacksmith looked at me and shrugged.

“‘It is the regulation.’

“He took off his cap, scratched his head and walked away.

“I returned home by the same road. The town had awakened. Shop shutters were being opened; women carried baskets; a boy drove geese through the mud. Everything appeared ordinary, and that was perhaps the most terrible thing. If the streets had been deserted, if bells had sounded an alarm, if people had run from their houses crying out, I might have understood. But life proceeded calmly. Everyone seemed to know that such things happened and that breakfast must nevertheless be prepared.

“At home I tried again to sleep. Each time I closed my eyes, Varinka appeared in white with her pink sash, and at once the white dress became the wet field, the pink sash became that red, mangled back, and the waltz changed into the fife and drum.

“I rose and drank water. I felt ill.

“Now you will say that I understood the colonel to be a wicked man and the punishment an evil deed. No. That would have made matters easier. The colonel did not look wicked. He performed his duty calmly, confidently, even conscientiously. The soldiers obeyed. The blacksmith said it was the regulation. The thing was done openly, in the presence of many men, at dawn, near the town. Therefore, I told myself, they must know something I do not know. There must be some necessity, some law, some wisdom belonging to authority and inaccessible to me.

“I tried to discover it. I could not.

“Until that morning I had intended to enter military service. My family expected it, and I myself had imagined the uniform, the comradeship, the honour of command. Afterward, whenever I thought of it, I heard the colonel shout, ‘Hit properly!’ and saw his suede glove strike the young soldier’s face.

“I considered civil service instead. But the same question followed me there. If rules required one thing in one uniform, might they not require another in another uniform? Would I understand when obedience became cruelty? Would I, too, one day say that everything must be done according to rule?

“So I entered no service at all.”

“And you think that was wise?” asked Sergei Nikolaevich.

Ivan Vasilievich smiled.

“No. I told you people consider my life useless. I am not praising myself. Perhaps if I had been wiser, I should have understood what the colonel understood and served with distinction. But I could not.”

“And Varinka?” asked the youngest lady.

“Yes—Varinka.”

He was silent for a moment.

“I continued to see her. At first I loved her as passionately as before. She was innocent of what had happened; she knew nothing of it, perhaps. When she smiled, I wanted to forget. Yet whenever she inclined her head in that proud, graceful way, I saw her father’s head beneath the military cap. When her white glove touched my hand, I remembered the suede glove. When music began and she moved toward me, I heard the drum.

“My love did not die at once. Such feelings rarely obey a command. It diminished little by little. I visited less often. I missed dances. Then she went away for the summer, and before she returned the thing was over. She later married, and I believe she was happy. She remained a charming and excellent woman.

“But I did not marry her. I did not become an officer. I did not become an official. One morning had set me on another road, though I did not know where it led.

“So you may speak to me of character, surroundings, historical necessity and all the rest. Perhaps you are right. Perhaps the ball and the punishment were not opposites at all but two parts of the same order—the same polished boots, the same straight back, the same rule. I cannot explain it. I only know that before that morning I saw one world, and afterward I saw another.

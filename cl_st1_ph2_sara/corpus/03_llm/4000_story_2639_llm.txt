# The House Above the Mists

In Kingsport the sea-mists are older than the steeples, and wiser, perhaps, than the men who ring their bells. They come in from the outer waters at evening, white and soft-footed, passing the black reef where drowned spars rise at low tide, and creeping upward through crooked lanes whose overhanging gables seem to whisper across the cobbles. They curl about the warehouses, dim the lanterns of Shipwright Street, and lay pale fingers upon gravestones so weathered that no living antiquary can read the names. Sometimes they climb higher still, beyond the clustered roofs and the last twisted pasture-fence, to that tremendous northern crag which the townsfolk call the Rim.

There, at a height no prudent climber would attempt, stands a house.

Ancient it is, and gray; low-eaved, narrow-windowed, and perched upon the bare rock as if planted there before the cliff rose from the primeval sea. From the town one may see only its western wall and one small bull’s-eye pane, yellow by night when all Kingsport sleeps. No path approaches it. The cliff beneath falls sheer for hundreds of feet, save where furtive ledges wrinkle its face; and behind it the higher crags lean outward, barring descent from the inland hills. Yet smoke has sometimes risen from its chimney, and once or twice each year fishermen far offshore have sworn that a tall black-bearded figure stood against the sunset at its door.

The old people dislike that house. They seldom point toward it, and never after dark.

Thomas Olney first beheld it in the autumn of a year which Kingsport records chiefly for an uncommon run of mackerel and the collapse of the old Customs House cupola. He had come from the inland town where he lectured upon philosophy, bringing with him a stout, practical wife, three noisy children, and a manuscript concerning the ethical foundations of civic duty. His physician had ordered sea air and idleness; but since Olney had never acquired the art of idleness, he spent his mornings revising footnotes and his afternoons asking questions which the residents considered impolite.

He lodged in a gambrel-roofed house near the harbour, where the windows were thick with greenish bull’s-eye glass and the hearthstones had been brought, rumour said, as ballast from Bristol before the Revolution. His wife praised the drains, distrusted the fishmongers, and complained that the fog spoiled the children’s stockings. Olney himself listened to the buoy-bells beyond the breakwater and wondered what dim coasts had known those waters before the first Indian watched from the hills.

He was not, by profession or temperament, a credulous man. His books had taught him to arrange mystery under headings, and his lectures reduced the hungers of the soul to useful propositions. Nevertheless, Kingsport troubled some chamber of his nature which had long lain sealed. At dusk he would wander alone through lanes where Tudor chimneys leaned above carved doorways, and where captains’ houses displayed ivory idols from ports omitted by modern charts. He heard fragments of strange lore in sail-lofts and chandlers’ shops: tales of reefs that sang under certain moons, of tritons glimpsed in deep green water, and of a black galley whose oars made no ripple.

Always his eyes returned to the house upon the Rim.

The younger townsfolk pretended ignorance when he spoke of it. Middle-aged merchants advised him that climbing spoiled good boots. Only the very old answered at all, and they answered obscurely. A centuried widow living beside the burying-ground said that the house had been old when her grandfather’s grandfather was a boy. A former whaler remembered seeing, from far at sea, lights moving above it where no earthly sky should have been. The sexton of St. Erasmus declared that no house stood there, though he crossed himself afterward.

Most singular was the warning of the Terrible Old Man.

That bent and yellow-bearded relic dwelt near Water Street among bottles which trembled when he addressed them, and little lead pendulums that swung without wind. Children feared him, sailors saluted him, and tax collectors approached his gate reluctantly. When Olney mentioned the cliff-top dwelling, the old man ceased smiling. He said only that certain doors opened both ways, and that a man might return through one without bringing back all that had entered.

This statement, being cryptical, increased Olney’s interest.

On a gray morning when the children were occupied in hunting shells and his wife had gone to dispute the price of laundry, he set out toward the northern cliff. He carried a stout stick, a packet of sandwiches, and a folded sheet of notes for his unfinished chapter on rational contentment. The last item he abandoned halfway up, when a sudden gust tore it from his pocket and scattered its numbered arguments among the gulls.

There was no path. Yet the rock offered narrow shelves where none appeared from below, and handholds emerged beneath lichens of an unfamiliar golden hue. Olney climbed through belts of mist, now seeing the red roofs of Kingsport diminished beneath him, now seeing nothing but wet stone and a white illimitable void. Once he heard laughter below, though the children on the beach were too distant to be heard. Once a bell tolled above him, where neither church nor buoy could be.

At noon he reached a ledge no broader than a grave-board. The wall of the house rose directly overhead, its shingles green with age and its angles queerly opposed to those of the cliff. He could discover no door on that side. To go farther required him to round an outward-jutting spur above an abyss whose bottom was concealed by cloud.

Then the mist opened.

For one instant he saw all Kingsport beneath him: the clustered roofs, the white steeples, the wharves like black fingers, and the sea stretching eastward beneath a pale autumn sun. But beyond the accustomed sea lay another horizon, immeasurably remote and strangely curved, where towers shone beneath waters of translucent green and winged shapes moved among stars visible at midday. The vision closed before he could decide whether he had seen it.

He rounded the spur.

The eastern side of the house possessed a door, but no ground before it. Threshold and sill projected over emptiness, facing the sea and the sky, as though its builder expected visitors who came by no terrestrial road. A narrow window stood open beside it, and through this Olney perceived a room shadowed by massive beams, sea-chests, dusky hangings, and furnishings older than any colonial house below. He called out. The gulls wheeled without crying.

Presently a face appeared at the window.

It was a strong, dark face, framed by a black beard in which no gray showed. The eyes were grave and hospitable, though their look carried an immeasurable weariness. The man wore a garment like a sailor’s coat of olden cut, with brass clasps green from salt; and those who have pondered the matter think he may have seemed thirty-five years of age, though he had certainly watched from that window for centuries.

He extended one hand and drew Olney through.

The room within was larger than the outer walls allowed. Its low ceiling vanished in brown rafters, and the walls were lined with shelves bearing scrolls, tablets, conchs, astrolabes, and jars wherein dim blue flames floated without consuming oil. A fire burned on the hearth, smelling not of wood but of cedar islands lost beneath the antediluvian ocean. The eastern door was barred with beams of black timber, although no ledge lay beyond it and nothing human could have knocked there.

The host offered wine from a metal cup figured with dolphins. Then, as the mist thickened about the window and the mortal coast disappeared, he began to speak.

No living tongue could preserve all that was told in that high and lonely room. Olney afterward remembered nothing; yet perhaps the winds remembered, and perhaps some fragments descended through Kingsport’s chimneys that night, for sleepers dreamed of elder things. The black-bearded man spoke of the days when the peaks of Hatheg-Kia were young, when white ships sailed above the clouds to Ulthar, and when priests crowned with coral descended stairways beneath the moon. He told of cities whose basalt walls lie under the outer Atlantic, where pale processions still move through drowned avenues and strike silver gongs at the turning of the tide.

He spoke of earth before mankind, when the poles bore gardens and the equator was veiled in ice; of creatures that built domes in the western deserts and departed toward dim Aldebaran; of the caverns beneath Arkham, whose black rivers know outlets in other worlds. He named gods remembered only by waves, and gods never known to earth at all. Some were gentle, some indifferent, and some so malign that the host lowered his voice lest the shapes of their names pass through the barred door.

All afternoon Olney listened. The room darkened, though beyond the window no sun could be seen setting. He forgot his manuscript, his lectures, his modest house inland, and even, for intervals afterward shameful to recall, the wife and children waiting below. He felt that all his correct and useful years had been a corridor leading to this chamber; that every argument he had published was but the dry husk of a wonder he had feared to taste.

Then came a knocking at the door.

Three measured strokes sounded from beyond the threshold which hung over the abyss.

The black-bearded man stopped speaking. The blue flames in the jars dwindled, and the fire turned green. Again came the knocking, soft but prodigious, as if some gigantic hand tapped from a distance greater than the stars. Olney thought he heard wings beating under the sound, and a low piping such as no shepherd would make.

The host went to the door but did not unbar it. He asked a question in a language which hurt the ear, and from outside came an answer deeper than thunder beneath the sea. At that answer his face hardened. He shook his head.

For a while there was silence. Then something moved against the bull’s-eye pane.

Olney saw no definite shape—only a shadow too vast for the narrow glass, a curve that might have been a horn, and three points of violet radiance arranged like eyes. The house trembled. Far below, though the hour was windless, every bell in Kingsport began to toll.

The visitor knocked once more.

The host made a sign with his right hand and uttered a name which Olney could never afterward repeat. The shadow withdrew. A smell of cold stone, nighted planets, and graves opened before burial lingered in the chamber; and from very far away came a thin, disappointed crying. The barred door remained shut.

Darkness had gathered when other sounds arose from the mist: conchs in grave harmony, the splash of immense yet graceful bodies, and voices singing in a mode older than human sorrow. The host smiled for the first time. He lifted the bars.

No terror waited outside now.

Through the open doorway poured a soft aqueous light, green and silver, revealing no precipice but a rolling field of cloud. Upon it rested a shell vast as a king’s pavilion, pearl-white and iridescent, drawn by creatures whose equine heads bore fins and streaming manes. Tritons sounded spiral horns from its rim. Nereids, pale-haired and laughing, scattered drops that became stars wherever they fell.

Behind them stood a mighty bearded form with a trident, sea-green robes, and a crown of dark weed. Some would call him Neptune, though the gods bear many titles and are not diminished by the forgetting of men. Beside him, wrapped in the blue gloom of abysses deeper than any plummet has found, sat hoary Nodens, Lord of the Great Deep, whose chariot is drawn over gulfs where the oldest nightmares dare not descend.

The black-bearded dweller bowed, and the gods answered as equals greeting one long absent.

What followed must be guessed.

Olney and his host entered the shell, or seemed to enter it; and the pearly vessel moved outward upon the mist while horns sang over Kingsport. It passed beyond the cliff, beyond the sea, and beyond the low roof of the world. The stars came near and wheeled beneath it. They sailed among luminous shoals that swam through the aether, past cloudy citadels upon which no sun had ever risen, and over black gulfs where chained shapes stirred at the music of the tritons.

Perhaps they descended beneath the ocean and saw the palaces of the sea gods, with columns of red coral and pavements where monstrous pearls gave light. Perhaps they crossed the lunar mountains and looked upon the hidden side, where gray gardens grow around the ruins of forgotten visitors. Perhaps they approached Kadath in the cold waste, though prudent dreamers avoid that summit and the powers that brood above it.

Rumour has added much. It says Olney danced upon a bridge of moonbeams with nereids whose eyes held the dawns of vanished worlds. It says he drank from a cup that contained every dream he had renounced. It says the black-bearded man showed him a gate standing alone in interstellar night, and that through it Olney glimpsed his own soul as a bright youth wandering toward an endless festival.

At sunset, those below saw lights on the cliff.

Olney’s wife, discovering his absence, first expressed annoyance, then alarm. Searchers climbed the inland hills and probed the lower ledges with ropes, but no one approached the house. The fishermen refused even to launch a boat. Through the night the mist lay thick around the Rim, glowing at intervals with rose, green, and violet radiance; while music drifted downward, so lovely that several young men in the tavern wept without knowing why.

Near dawn there came a single peal of laughter from the heights.

The mist parted after sunrise. Olney was found on the road above the burying-ground, walking toward his lodgings with wet shoes and an expression of mild perplexity. He could not say where he had been. He remembered climbing, a narrow ledge, and perhaps a window; beyond that lay only a blank region which questioning could not penetrate. His clothes smelled faintly of salt, though no spray could have reached the cliff, and in one pocket was a small opalescent scale unlike that of any known fish. His wife threw it away.

The family departed Kingsport that afternoon.

Most people accepted the natural explanation: exhaustion, fog, a fall without serious injury, and temporary loss of memory. The local physician spoke of nervous strain. Olney himself agreed readily and thereafter discouraged discussion of the event. Only the Terrible Old Man denied that the professor had returned entire.

He watched Olney’s departure from a doorway in Water Street, and later told certain captains that the shape walking beside the stout wife was respectable, breathing, and legally Thomas Olney, but that its shadow fell too faintly. What had come down from the Rim possessed the man’s habits, duties, recollections, and useful virtues; what it lacked was precisely that which had once looked upward.

In the years afterward Olney prospered.

He completed his ponderous volume, received promotion, purchased a square modern house with excellent drains, and saw his children educated according to reputable methods. He became known as a dependable citizen, punctual at committees and temperate in opinion. His lectures lost the rare flashes of poetry which had formerly embarrassed his colleagues. He never again wandered at dusk, never paused at strange music, and never asked what lay beyond the horizon. When a student once spoke passionately of dreams, Professor Olney advised regular sleep and practical employment.

His wife considered the Kingsport holiday beneficial.

Yet changes came upon the high house.

Before Olney’s ascent its one dim window had seldom shone, and no sound had issued from it save, perhaps, the low speech of its solitary keeper. Afterward the bull’s-eye pane burned often with a golden radiance brighter than any lamp. Shadows moved within, sometimes one, sometimes two; and those who watched from the harbour declared that the second was slighter, clean-shaven, and inclined to gesture with the eager grace of a young scholar freed from restraint.

Laughter became frequent on the Rim.

It was not cruel laughter, and for that reason Kingsport feared it more. It rang over the roofs on foggy nights with such unearthly gladness that sleepers awoke mourning lives they had never lived. Music followed—pipes, conchs, viols, and deep-throated horns—while the mist climbed in great white armies from the sea. At times an aurora streamed above the house, though no such lights appeared elsewhere along the coast; and through its cold green curtains moved silhouettes crowned, finned, winged, or magnificently human.

The elders remembered older warnings.

They recalled youths of previous centuries who had vanished on the northern cliffs, and sober men who returned from sea unable to care for gold or kin. In worm-eaten diaries were found references to a “house at the world’s edge,” and to revels which increased whenever some ardent mortal climbed too high. The ancient widow by the burying-ground said the black-bearded keeper did not lure men upward; rather, he guarded them from something worse, and opened his window only to those already summoned by their own unsatisfied hearts.

Even the Terrible Old Man seemed uncertain.

He feared neither Neptune nor the mild tritons, and spoke of Nodens with grave respect. The danger, he said, lay in the knocking visitor who had been refused. Every new voice in the house made the revel stronger, but every revel cast farther echoes through the outer dark. Joy might guard the threshold—or weaken it. No man could tell. Gods benevolent to dreamers are not always mindful of towns.

Therefore the fathers of Kingsport warned their sons against the Rim. They mended the fences on the northern pastures and posted signs concerning unstable rock. Ministers preached against idle curiosity, and schoolmasters emphasized geography of the approved terrestrial kind. But prohibitions are feeble things when set against music descending through moonlit mist.

Young men still looked upward.

A shipwright’s apprentice once climbed as far as the golden lichens before friends dragged him back. A melancholy clerk disappeared for a night and returned smiling, after which he abandoned bookkeeping and went to sea. Two students from Arkham claimed to have seen a stairway of cloud reaching from the eastern door to a constellation unknown to astronomers. They were mocked publicly and questioned privately.

Meanwhile the correct and useful shell of Thomas Olney grew old inland.

He became dean, then emeritus professor, and finally a white-haired ornament at academic dinners. He spoke calmly of mankind’s duty to remain within the limits of demonstrable knowledge. He died at a respectable age, surrounded by descendants, papers, medicines, and clocks. Those present said his passing was quiet. At the same hour, however, Kingsport heard such laughter from the cliff that windows shook along Water Street, and a new star flared above the sea.

After that, the second shadow in the high window no longer seemed young.

Whether this means that the lost spirit aged in its freedom, or merely assumed at last the aspect appropriate to wisdom, none can say. Perhaps it was never Olney’s spirit at all, but some bright counterfeit made from his discarded longing. Perhaps the man who returned had been the true one, and the joyous figure aloft only a dream granted substance by the old gods. Kingsport, which has learned caution from both sea and grave, does not choose among these possibilities.

It knows only that the house remains.

On certain evenings, when the sunset lies red behind the western steeples and the tide breathes heavily in the caves, the mist comes ashore with more than mist within it. Bells toll where no buoys float. Conchs answer from beyond the horizon. White nereids are glimpsed upon the reefs, combing hair that gleams like drowned moonlight, and enormous bearded faces seem to form in the clouds before melting into rain.

Then the gray, ancient, low-eaved house shines at the rim of all earth, and voices call from its impossible seaward door.

The old people bolt their shutters. Mothers count their sons. Sailors make signs learned in remote ports, while ministers pretend not to hear the horns above their prayers. For there are nights when the laughter is joined by another sound: three slow knocks, vast and patient, coming not from the sky or sea but from whatever black gulf lies behind both.

Thus far, the door has remained barred.

But the mist has many brothers, and the cliff consorts with celestial things. The gods of the green deep still ride their pearly shells between worlds; hoary Nodens still hunts through abysses where nameless powers flee before him; and high above the sleeping roofs of Kingsport, two shadows—or perhaps many—move merrily behind the yellow bull’s-eye pane.

So the elders watch, and remember, and fear the day when another weary philosopher, another poet stifled by prudence, or another youth sick of the narrow streets of men shall look too long toward the Rim. For the ascent is hard, but longing discovers ledges invisible to caution; and he who reaches that window may behold wonders sufficient to empty a lifetime of desire.

Whether he returns blessed or diminished will not be known.

And whether the laughter overhead is the music of liberation, or only the sweet prelude by which elder darkness summons the human soul beyond recall, is a secret kept by the sea, the stars, and the white, upward-wandering mist.

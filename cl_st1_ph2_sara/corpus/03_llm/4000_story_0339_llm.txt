## THE SHADOW UPON THE HEARTH

There are homes upon which the morning seems always to rest.

The house in which Madeline Henry passed her childhood was one of these. It was neither stately nor adorned with costly things; no marble steps led to its modest door, no glittering equipage waited beneath its elms. Yet Love dwelt there—and where Love has taken up her abode, she can make a palace of the humblest room, spread a feast upon the plainest board, and draw from common hours a music which wealth may hear, but cannot purchase.

Madeline’s father was a man of moderate means and cheerful temper; her mother, one of those gentle beings whose influence is felt like sunshine—not dazzling, not commanding observation, but warming every living thing within its reach. Their children never doubted that they were loved. A little grief brought the mother’s arm about the sufferer; a childish triumph called brightness into the father’s eyes. There were kisses at morning and evening; there were kind words over daily tasks; there were pleasant gatherings around the winter fire, and long summer twilights beneath the garden trees.

Thus Madeline grew with an open heart. Affection was to her not an ornament of life, but its atmosphere. She knew no more how the spirit could live without tenderness than the flower knows how it may bloom without light. If she sometimes saw sterner households, where duty moved with measured step and children trembled beneath cold authority, she pitied their inmates, but did not understand them. She had yet to learn that the world often calls a dwelling happy because its windows are bright and its furniture costly, though hearts sit shivering within.

Among the young men who visited at her father’s house was Edward Leslie.

He was prosperous, grave, and universally respected. Though scarcely thirty, he had already obtained a position in business which older men regarded with envy. His word was as good as his bond; his accounts were exact; his dress was without ostentation, yet always becoming; and no one had ever accused him of a reckless act or an unworthy bargain. He neither drank, gambled, nor frequented disreputable company. Careful mothers spoke of him with approval, and prudent fathers pointed to him as a model.

How little does the world, in making up its judgment of character, look beneath the surface! It sees the vice that wastes money, but not the selfishness that withholds love; it condemns the hand that strikes, while it excuses the tongue that chills; it notes the public obligation faithfully discharged, but does not enter the home-sanctuary to ask whether the husband has given sympathy with his bread, or tenderness with his protection.

Edward admired Madeline. Her sweetness refreshed him; her animation relieved the severe pressure of his thoughts; her beauty pleased both his eye and his pride. He loved her, so far as a man may love who has never learned that love is less a possession than a ministry. He wished to call her his wife, to place her in a handsome home, to surround her with comforts, and to feel that the gentle creature who brightened every circle belonged to him.

Madeline, on her part, mistook his gravity for depth, his reserve for strength, and his attentions for the outward sign of an affection as warm as her own. He was never ardent; but because she loved, imagination supplied what manner withheld. If his words were few, she believed them weighty; if his caresses were rare, she treasured them the more. Thus does a trusting heart often weave a golden garment for the object of its devotion, and then worship the brightness which itself has bestowed.

They were married on a fair spring morning.

Madeline wept when she left her mother; but they were not tears of sorrow. Edward’s home stood before her, touched by hope into beauty. She would make it glad, she thought. She would carry thither the warmth of the old hearth, the songs, the smiles, the thousand little endearments which had made childhood blessed. And he—her husband—would be her nearest friend, her companion, her shelter from every wind.

Alas! she had not yet discovered that one may be sheltered from poverty, scandal, and outward misfortune, and yet be left defenceless against the daily frost of an unresponsive heart.

For some weeks all went well. Edward was proud of his young wife and pleased by the admiration she awakened. He gave her jewels, ordered whatever he believed suitable to her station, and smiled when visitors praised the elegance of his establishment. But when the novelty of marriage had passed, he returned wholly to the habits of business. His office absorbed his strongest interest; its perplexities followed him home; its victories stirred him more deeply than Madeline’s joy, and its reverses rendered him inaccessible to her sympathy.

One evening, early in their married life, she waited for him with more than usual impatience. It was the anniversary of the day on which he had first asked her to become his wife. Madeline remembered it as loving natures remember all dates made sacred by feeling. She placed flowers upon the table, wore the pale blue dress he had once admired, and arranged the lamp so that its softened light fell upon the chair beside her.

He came an hour later than she expected.

“Dear Edward,” she said, going quickly to meet him, “I began to fear that something had detained you unpleasantly.”

“Business detained me,” he replied, laying aside his coat. “There was no occasion for fear.”

She put her hand upon his arm.

“Do you know what evening this is?”

“Tuesday, is it not?”

The smile upon her lips trembled.

“It is a year since—since you told me that you loved me.”

Edward looked at her, then at the papers in his hand.

“Indeed? I had forgotten the date.”

Only that! Yet the words fell with the weight of a heart-stroke.

She tried to laugh. “I suppose men do not remember such things.”

“Men who have responsibilities cannot retain every sentimental circumstance,” said Edward. “But you have no cause for complaint, Madeline. I trust you have everything you need.”

Everything she needed! There were rich curtains at the windows, silver upon the table, servants to anticipate her wishes; but the one small coin of tenderness for which her heart pleaded, he did not give.

“I did not mean to complain,” she answered softly. “I only thought—we might be happy together this evening.”

“And are we not happy?” There was impatience in his voice. “What more would you have?”

She looked into his face, yearning to say, “Yourself!” But something in its composed coldness restrained her.

“Nothing,” she murmured, and withdrew her hand.

Edward unfolded his papers and sat beneath the lamp. Madeline took the chair opposite, though the flowers between them seemed now like blossoms laid upon a grave. Once she raised her eyes, hoping to meet his; but he was calculating columns. The clock ticked loudly. At length she rose and went to the window, where her tears might fall unseen.

He neither followed nor perceived them.

From that hour a shadow entered Madeline’s heart. At first it was slight, no larger than the little cloud which hangs upon a summer horizon; but neglect fed it, silence deepened it, and disappointment spread it over the inward sky.

Let it not be supposed that Edward was openly cruel. Had he been so, the world might have condemned him, and Madeline herself might have understood the source of her suffering. He never denied her money. He did not forbid her pleasures, provided they did not interfere with his order. He spoke no coarse word; he inflicted no visible wound. But affection, when it sought him, encountered dignity; sorrow, when it appealed to him, was instructed to be reasonable; and joy, when it danced before him, was requested not to disturb his thoughts.

If Madeline sat near him and laid her hand in his, he sometimes withdrew his own because he wished to turn a page. If she asked whether he had missed her during an absence, he answered that his time had been much occupied. If she met him at the door, her face bright with welcome, he gave her his hat instead of a kiss.

Small things! Who will wonder that a strong man should disregard them? Yet of small things is domestic happiness composed. A drop of oil keeps the lamp alive; a word nourishes hope; a pressure of the hand may strengthen the weary spirit for another day. And, on the other side, it is not always the thunderbolt that destroys. The continual dropping wears away the stone; the repeated frost kills the root; the daily heart-strokes, unfelt by those who deal them, may at last break the life-chords one by one.

When Madeline’s first child was born, new joy stirred within her. It was a daughter, with soft dark eyes and a mouth that seemed fashioned for smiles. As Madeline held the infant against her breast, she thought surely Edward’s heart must now open. Surely the helpless little being, their’s alike, would draw them together.

He was pleased. He stood beside the bed and regarded the child with grave satisfaction.

“She is a fine, healthy infant,” he said. “We must see that every proper provision is made for her.”

Madeline lifted the little face toward him.

“Kiss her, Edward.”

He obeyed, almost awkwardly. Then Madeline turned her own pale cheek, but he did not understand the mute petition. He spoke to the nurse concerning the physician’s directions and left the room.

The mother pressed her lips upon her baby’s brow and wept.

Years passed, and other children gathered around the table—two boys and another little girl. To them Madeline gave the wealth of feeling which found no welcome elsewhere. Their arms clung about her; their griefs, however trifling, were brought to her knee; their laughter filled the chambers from which her own gaiety had long since departed.

Edward loved his children after his fashion. He meant to educate them well, to secure their future, and to command their obedience. But noise annoyed him, childish blunders offended his sense of order, and childish fears appeared absurd. At his step, merry voices were lowered; playthings disappeared; small feet moved cautiously. The father mistook silence for discipline, not knowing that fear may produce the outward form of obedience while it withers confidence at the root.

One winter afternoon, their eldest boy, Henry, broke a small porcelain ornament in the library. The child stood white and trembling amid the fragments when Edward entered.

“Who did this?” he demanded.

“I—I did, father.”

“Carelessness again. How often have you been warned not to touch what does not belong to you?”

“I did not mean—”

“Intention does not repair damage.”

Madeline came quickly from the adjoining room. She saw the child’s quivering lip and drew him gently toward her.

“He was reaching for a book,” she said. “The figure stood too near the edge.”

Edward’s brow contracted.

“You will ruin the children by excusing every fault.”

“And severity will not mend the figure,” she replied, with a courage born of maternal love.

“It may prevent another from being broken.”

Henry began to sob. Edward ordered him from the room, and the child fled, clinging for one instant to his mother’s dress as he passed.

“You contradict me before them,” said Edward, after the door closed.

“I could not bear to see him frightened.”

“He should be frightened when he does wrong.”

“No, Edward—not frightened of his father.”

The words were gentle, but they touched that self-love which is often more sensitive than conscience.

“You are becoming fanciful and morbid,” he said. “I provide for these children; I labor for them every day; yet you would represent me as a tyrant because I require proper conduct.”

Madeline’s face grew very pale.

“I know that you labor for us.”

“Then let me hear no more ingratitude.”

He took up his book. The conversation was ended—not reconciled, not softened, not healed; merely ended. And how many heart-troubles are thus shut into silence, there to gather poison!

That night Madeline lay wakeful beside him. Once she whispered, “Edward?”

He made no answer.

Whether he slept or chose not to reply, she could not tell. She turned her face toward the darkness, and the darkness seemed kinder than he.

Her health, always delicate, now visibly declined. The physician spoke of weakness, prescribed air, change, and freedom from excitement. Edward spared no expense. Rooms were engaged at the sea-shore; a carriage was placed at her disposal; the best advice was obtained. In all this he felt himself an exemplary husband.

But when Madeline said, “Will you come with us?” he answered that business rendered it impossible.

“Only for a week?”

“I cannot sacrifice important interests to a whim.”

“The physician thought your company might benefit me.”

“The physician understands medicine, not my affairs. You will have the children and their nurse.”

She went; and every day the sea spread its splendid freedom before her, while strangers remarked upon the comfort of her arrangements. Yet she watched other husbands walking beside their wives, drawing shawls around their shoulders, bending to catch low words; and a yearning, deep and almost shameful in its intensity, consumed her.

To be loved—not maintained.

To be understood—not managed.

To be held dear, not merely held in honorable possession.

Was the desire unreasonable? Oh! there are those who would call it weakness; but the Creator who made the flower to turn toward the sun made also the human heart to turn toward affection. Deny it light, and though it may stand awhile in outward beauty, its color fades, its strength departs, and decay begins where no common eye can see.

When Madeline returned home, Edward observed that the sea air had not accomplished much.

“You appear as languid as before,” he said.

“I missed you.”

“You must conquer this dependence upon others for your spirits.”

She gazed at him for a moment. Then something like despair passed over her face.

“Yes,” she answered; “I must.”

From that hour she ceased, almost entirely, to ask tenderness of him.

At first Edward was relieved. There were no more questions as to whether he would return early, no gentle reproaches when he forgot a promised walk, no tears which he could not comprehend. The household moved with greater quietness. Madeline received him courteously, attended to his comfort, and spoke little of herself.

He thought her improved.

Alas! the resignation which gives peace is not the same as the hopelessness which ceases to plead. A wife may become very still, not because the tempest has passed, but because it has swept away all that once cried out beneath its fury.

For a time Madeline seemed sinking rapidly. Her cheek grew transparent, her step slow; even the children sometimes paused in their play to look anxiously upon her. One evening she heard them speaking together in the passage.

“Mamma is always tired now,” said little Rose.

“Do people die when they are tired a great while?” Henry asked.

“Hush!” whispered his sister. “She must not hear.”

But Madeline had heard. Their words pierced through the stupor of her grief.

“My children will feel my loss,” she murmured.

The thought returned again and again.

My children will feel my loss.

Until then she had regarded death with something almost like longing—not as self-destruction, which her pure faith abhorred, but as the opening of a door from a cold room into everlasting warmth. Now she saw four young beings left beneath a discipline unsoftened by her intercession; little griefs mocked, little faults severely judged, little arms reaching in vain for the shelter they had known.

“No,” she said, rising from her chair with sudden energy; “I must live for them.”

From that hour a new purpose animated her. She no longer wasted her remaining strength in seeking to awaken what seemed asleep beyond recall. She spent it upon the children. She made their home as cheerful as she could; she taught them that their father’s coldness was not hatred; she softened his commands in the execution, defended his motives, and carefully restrained them from returning fear with resentment.

“Your father works very hard for you,” she would say. “You must remember the good he does, even when his manner pains you.”

Thus she placed herself, like a tender veil, between their young hearts and his severity. Was there not heroism in this? The world gives crowns to those who endure a single hour of visible danger; but how many women pass through years of secret martyrdom, smiling that their children may not learn despair!

Her renewed activity deceived Edward. Because she complained less and occupied herself more, he believed her health restored.

“You see,” he remarked, “that resolution is better than indulgence. The mind has great power over the body.”

Madeline looked at him with a mournful sweetness.

“Yes, Edward. Sometimes the heart can command the body to walk a little farther.”

He did not perceive the meaning of her words.

For several years she walked that farther distance. She marked birthdays, comforted disappointments, taught prayers, mended quarrels, and filled memory with those small sacred images which become, in later life, the guardian angels of childhood. Her children never forgot the sound of her voice at twilight, nor the touch of her hand upon a fevered forehead, nor the smile with which she welcomed confessions that fear would have concealed from another.

But the flame fed upon a failing supply. Love may sustain life; it cannot always rebuild what prolonged heart-sickness has destroyed. A cough settled upon her; faintness often compelled her to sit in the midst of household duties; and one autumn, when the leaves fell early, Madeline took to her bed.

The physician spoke privately with Edward.

“Mrs. Leslie’s strength has been undermined for years,” he said. “We may alleviate, but I fear we cannot restore.”

“For years?” repeated Edward. “She has had every attention.”

The physician hesitated.

“Every physical attention, perhaps.”

Edward’s face hardened. He disliked implications which could not be entered in an account and disproved by receipts.

“I have spared neither means nor care.”

“I do not question it,” replied the physician quietly. “But there are ailments for which means purchase no remedy.”

For the first time, a faint uneasiness entered Edward’s mind.

He went to Madeline’s chamber. The children were gathered around her, and her wan face was bright with a tenderness which illness had rendered almost unearthly. Rose sat upon the bed, clasping one thin hand. Henry, now a tall youth, stood near the pillow, struggling to restrain his grief.

“Do not be afraid for me,” Madeline was saying. “Whatever comes, love one another. And love your father. He has many cares which you do not understand.”

Edward stopped at the threshold. Even then she shielded him!

When the children had gone, he approached the bed.

“Madeline,” he said, “the physician thinks you very ill.”

“Yes.”

“Why was I not told sooner?”

A slight smile touched her lips.

“I think I have been telling you, Edward, for a great many years.”

He drew back as though struck.

“I have given you everything in my power.”

“Everything you understood.”

“Have I been unkind?”

She was silent.

“Madeline, answer me.”

Her eyes filled slowly. “You never meant to be.”

It was a merciful reply; but mercy sometimes reveals guilt more terribly than accusation. Memories, long dismissed as trifles, came thronging upon him—the anniversary forgotten, the hand withdrawn, the solitary journeys, the tears treated as weakness, the nights of punitive silence. He saw them now, not separately, but as a chain; not as moments passed and ended, but as repeated strokes descending upon one faithful heart.

He sat beside her and took her hand. How light it was! Once that hand had sought his with timid eagerness. He had withdrawn it to turn a page.

“Can you forgive me?” he asked.

“I forgave you always.”

“No—do not speak so. It is more than I can bear.”

For several days he scarcely left her. Business, which had so often seemed imperative, now waited unanswered. He adjusted her pillow, carried the medicines, watched every change in her breathing. He would have poured out affection like treasure, but the impoverished hour could receive so little! Thus do men sometimes hoard tenderness until death stands at the door, and then offer in anguish what would once have made a life rich.

The final evening came softly. A pale sunset lingered upon the curtains, and the children knelt in the adjoining room. Madeline had spoken to each, blessing them with broken words. Edward bent over her, his stern face altered by grief beyond the power of years.

She opened her eyes and looked at him—not reproachfully, but with the old yearning which had survived every winter of neglect.

“Kiss me, Edward, once, before I die!”

With a cry he clasped her and pressed his lips to her forehead, her cheeks, her mouth.

“Madeline! My wife! Do not leave me. I love you—I have always loved you!”

“I know,” she whispered. “But you should have told me—before.”

Her breath fluttered. For one instant her head rested against his breast, where in the morning of marriage she had hoped to find her home. Then the tired heart was still.

The children’s sobs arose from the doorway. Edward did not move. He held her as though warmth might return through the very violence of his remorse; but no answering pressure came from the hand in his, no smile rewarded the kisses now lavished so freely.

The house remained splendid after Madeline’s burial. Its carpets were soft, its silver bright, its order undisturbed. Edward’s credit stood high; his business prospered; men continued to speak of his integrity and success.

Yet the sunshine had gone out.

He would enter at evening and look instinctively toward the place where she had once waited. He would hear a light step upon the stair and remember how often he had received its approach without welcome. He found, among her possessions, little faded flowers tied with ribbon—the flowers from that forgotten anniversary—and beside them a paper on which she had written, years before, only these words: “My children will feel my loss.”

Then the proud man bowed his head and wept.

No accusation followed him through the silent rooms; none was needed. The absence of her voice accused him. The empty chair accused him. His children’s subdued faces, affectionate but never wholly confident, accused him. And memory, that stern accountant who omits no item and accepts no false balance, placed before him the long record of neglected tenderness.

He had loved her, he said.

Yes—but love unspoken, unshown, ungiven, may become in its effects scarcely distinguishable from indifference. The bread was provided, but the heart hungered; the roof was secure, but the spirit shivered; the world called her fortunate, while shadow after shadow gathered over her life.

Reader! is there one beside you who waits for the kind word you are too busy to utter? Is there a hand that has ceased to seek yours because it was so often unanswered? Do not trust to-morrow. Wealth may be restored, lost opportunities regained, errors of business corrected; but the heart has seasons which do not return. Give the kiss before the lips are cold—the tenderness before yearning has become despair—the sunshine before the flower has faded.

For Madeline the shadows had passed. She had entered, let us trust, that eternal Home where love is neither measured nor withheld.

But they rested now upon Edward Leslie; and from his heart, they never departed.

## The Garland of Victory

She was the Princess Ajita.

Shekhar, the King’s poet, had never beheld her face. Yet, in his secret heart, he had fashioned it from moonlight, music, and the dreams which visit a lonely man before dawn. During the hours of recitation she sat behind the latticed screen above the royal hall. Sometimes the lamps cast upon the silk curtain the shadow of a slender head; sometimes, when his song ended, there came from that hidden place the faint tinkle of golden anklets.

That shadow was his heaven, and those anklets its answer.

King Narayan would sit upon his ivory throne, grave and magnificent, while courtiers, captains, pandits, and merchants gathered in their appointed ranks. Shekhar stood below them with no jewelled robe and no scholar’s chain. He was slight, and his eyes had the distant look of one who sees another world through this one. When he began to sing, his voice at first was low, as though asking leave of silence; then it rose like a quivering flame of fire.

His songs told of the old kings who had guarded the land, of mothers waiting at twilight for sons returning from battle, of the river carrying the prayers of villages to the sea. Yet beneath them all ran another song, unnamed and unheard by none save one.

“O thou who dwellest behind the veil,” he sang, “thy mercy falls unseen, like dew upon a sleeping flower. I know not thy face; therefore the whole sky becomes thy countenance. I touch not thy feet; therefore the earth beneath me is sacred.”

At such times the royal hall grew still. The old guards stared before them, remembering vanished springs. The King’s stern mouth softened. From behind the screen came the anklets, once—only once—and Shekhar’s heart received the sound as a kingdom receives the trumpet of deliverance.

Afterward Manjari, the Princess’s maid, would visit him.

She came without solemnity, laughing through the garden as if all the birds were her cousins. She wore no heavy jewels, but wound fresh jasmine in her hair. Because she passed freely between the women’s chambers and the outer courts, she knew every secret except those which mattered; and those she pretended not to know.

“Poet,” she said one morning, leaning against his doorway, “the bees accuse thee.”

“Of what crime?”

“Thou stealest the honey from the flowers and hidest it in thy songs.”

“Tell the bees,” answered Shekhar, “that the flowers gave it willingly.”

“And if the flowers deny this?”

“Then I shall confess myself a thief.”

Manjari clapped her hands. By evening the exchange had travelled through the bazaar. Before the week was done, boys sang that Manjari was the flower and Shekhar the bee. The palace women teased her; the courtiers smiled knowingly; even the King once asked Shekhar whether his next epic would concern the sorrows of a honey-gatherer.

Thus truth and falsehood mingle in life, and to what God builds man adds his own decoration.

Shekhar laughed with the rest, but his heart remained beyond their ken. Manjari was dear to him as sunlight upon a familiar path. Ajita was the distant ruling planet by which that path was known. Of Manjari he could speak; of Ajita he could only sing.

His verses passed from the court into the kingdom. Herdsmen played them upon reed flutes. Boatmen fitted them to the stroke of their oars. Women sang them over sleeping children, altering a word here and a cadence there, until no man knew what belonged to Shekhar and what had arisen from the people’s own sorrow. From fields and groves, from the shady lanes and lonely roads, from ferries bright beneath the moon and courtyards fragrant with evening rice, his music returned to him changed, enlarged, and made immortal by unknown lips.

Then Pundarik came.

The trumpet sounded at noon. Through the northern gate entered a procession of disciples bearing palm-leaf MSS., copper scrolls, sandalwood boxes, and banners inscribed with verses in six languages. In their midst rode Pundarik upon a white horse. His beard was perfumed, his robe shone like the wing of a kingfisher, and his forehead bore so many learned marks that it seemed a battlefield of philosophies.

Before the throne he lifted both arms.

“Sire,” he cried, and his voice crashed through the hall like sea-breakers against stone, “I have defeated the poets of seven kingdoms. I have measured grammar, mastered rhetoric, crossed the oceans of scripture, and plucked the secret syllable from the closed mouth of antiquity. I ask leave to contend with thy court poet.”

King Narayan looked at Shekhar.

“What sayest thou?”

Shekhar bowed. “The tree does not challenge the storm, sire. Yet if the storm comes, the tree must answer with such leaves as God has given it.”

Pundarik smiled. “A pleasing sentence. We shall discover whether it is poetry.”

The contest was proclaimed for two days. Messengers rode through the city. Shops closed before noon, scholars arrived carrying ink-pots, and noblemen quarrelled over places near the throne. Behind the screened balcony there burned an extra row of lamps.

On the first day Pundarik spoke of the majesty of King Narayan. He traced the King’s name through roots, suffixes, hidden numerals, and sacred correspondences. “Na,” he declared, signified negation, which proved that no fault existed in the sovereign. “Ra” was fire, for the King consumed his enemies. “Yana” was the path, because all roads of virtue led to him. Then Pundarik quoted twelve scriptures, contradicted eight commentators, reconciled six schools, and showed that Narayan was at once monarch, sacrifice, sacrificer, altar, flame, clarified butter, and reward.

The pandits struck their knees in delight. Each discovery drew forth a murmur. Pundarik’s disciples cried, “Excellent! Unanswerable!” and the court, fearing to appear less wise, nodded until its turbans trembled.

Then Shekhar rose.

He carried no manuscript. For a moment he stood with lowered head. Pundarik’s thunder still rolled among the pillars, and Shekhar’s first note seemed as frail as a lamp taken into the wind.

He sang of the ancient bond between ruler and people.

“Before thy fathers wore the crown,” he said, “our fathers loved this soil. When famine came, the palace granary opened; when enemies crossed the river, the village dead lay beside the royal dead. O King, we do not praise thee because thy name conceals the sun and scripture. We praise thee because, when we cry in darkness, thou art awake.”

His voice strengthened. He recalled the boy Narayan, who had once walked among flood-stricken huts; the old Queen Mother giving her ornaments for seed; soldiers whose names had vanished, though the land purchased by their blood remained. His words were simple. They did not glitter; they opened.

A sound passed through the assembly like wind through ripened corn. Men who had come to judge forgot judgement. The King covered his eyes. Even the scholars sat silent, though some afterward explained that they had merely been considering defects of metre.

Behind the balcony screen the anklets rang.

Shekhar looked up. For one breath the curtain moved, and upon it appeared that beloved shadow. His song flew toward it and ended.

Pundarik applauded delicately.

“Emotion,” he said, “is the ornament of poetry. Tomorrow, perhaps, we may discuss its substance.”

That evening the city sang Shekhar’s song. Yet in the houses of learning men repeated Pundarik’s derivations and marvelled at their depth. A flower enters the heart without argument; but many distrust what they have understood too easily.

On the second day Pundarik advanced into higher regions.

“What is greater than the word?” he demanded.

He explained that creation itself began in sound; that gods were bound by mantras; that things perished, while their names endured; that love without a word could neither be declared nor proved. He divided speech into manifest, unmanifest, subtle, causal, celestial, and supreme forms. He demonstrated that silence also was but a species of language and therefore a tributary province of his theme.

“What is greater than the word?” he repeated.

No one answered. His question seemed to fill the hall, enormous and invincible.

Shekhar rose slowly. All night he had heard within himself the flute of an old song. Now he began the legend of a maiden who waited by a forest shrine for the Eternal Beloved. Spring came and strewed flowers at her feet. Summer burned the road white. The rains filled the river until no traveller could cross. Year followed year; her hair grew grey; but each evening she lit a lamp.

The Beloved never came in visible form.

Yet the deer lost their fear beside her. The hungry found food at her door. Men burdened with grief rested beneath her tree and departed comforted. At last, when death approached, she said, “Why should I lament? He whom I awaited has looked from every face that needed me.”

Shekhar sang no doctrine. He offered no proof. His melody wandered beyond court and city, over moonlit water, through groves where unseen flowers surrendered their perfume, into those desolate chambers of the soul where each man waits alone.

Then he turned toward the screened balcony.

“Words are the lamps, my lord,” he said softly. “But love is the flame. What profit is there in counting lamps when the flame has gone?”

For an instant no one moved.

Pundarik sprang up.

“An ingenious confusion!” he cried. “Flame itself is a word. Love is a word. Lamp is a word. By words alone has our friend denied the supremacy of words. Therefore his denial establishes my argument.”

The pandits exclaimed. One found the logic subtle; another declared it irresistible. Pundarik pursued his triumph through definitions, quotations, antitheses, and puns. He seized Shekhar’s images one by one, opened them like mechanical toys, and displayed their springs to the assembly. That which had been alive in song lay dismembered in analysis.

The crowd, ashamed of its former tears, began to laugh when the scholars laughed. A court fears ignorance more than falsehood. Presently even those who had been most deeply moved whispered that Shekhar’s poem lacked learning.

King Narayan’s face was troubled. He loved Shekhar, but kings too are prisoners of public judgement. He consulted the pandits. They bowed toward Pundarik.

The King raised the garland of victory.

“Pundarik,” he said, “thy mastery is beyond dispute.”

The garland fell upon the conqueror’s shoulders. Drums sounded. Pundarik stood immense beneath his flowers and glanced at Shekhar with the benevolence of an elephant toward a fallen reed.

Shekhar bowed to the throne. His eyes went once to the balcony. The lamps burned behind the screen, but no shadow appeared, and the anklets gave no answer.

He returned home at sunset.

Manjari came running after him, but he closed the garden gate. She called his name. He did not reply. At last she went away, weeping and angry, for tears and anger are sisters when the heart is helpless.

Within his room lay the labour of many years: hymns written beside the river, songs born in the spring woods, fragments composed after hearing anklets in the night, and verses in which a woman without a face had become mercy, beauty, homeland, and God. Shekhar gathered the MSS. and carried them to the courtyard.

He lit a fire.

One by one he cast the manuscripts into it. The leaves curled; the ink blackened; little wings of ash flew upward. Here burned a song sung by thousands. There vanished a line which had once made Ajita’s shadow lean nearer to the screen. Fire is an ignorant critic. It refuses neither wisdom nor folly.

“If words without learning are empty,” Shekhar said, “let emptiness return to emptiness.”

When all was consumed, he bathed and dressed in white. He filled his chamber with jasmine, champak, roses, and night-flowering tuberose. Upon the floor he scattered petals until it seemed that spring, slain in its youth, had been laid there for burial. He lighted lamps at the four corners and opened the lattice to the moon.

Then he poured poison into a silver cup.

“To thee,” he whispered, though whether he spoke to Ajita, poetry, or death he did not know, “I give my last song, which has no word.”

He drank.

For a little while there was peace. Perfume moved through the room with the night breeze. The lamps bent and straightened. Far off, a flute played one of his own melodies, imperfectly remembered. He smiled to hear the error; it made the tune human.

Pain came softly, then tightened its hand. He lay among the flowers and watched the moon dissolve at the edges.

Suddenly he heard anklets.

At first he believed memory had put forth music, as a wounded tree puts forth its final blossom. But the sound came nearer—through the garden, across the threshold, into the flower-filled room.

A woman stood before him.

She had cast aside her veil. Her face was pale beneath the moon, and sorrow had made her eyes more royal than any crown. She wore no ornaments save the golden anklets and a garland of white flowers hanging from her hands.

Shekhar struggled to rise.

“Who art thou?”

She knelt beside him.

“I am Ajita.”

The name entered him more swiftly than the poison. He gazed upon her as one who, worshipping all his life toward an unseen altar, beholds at last the god descend.

“Then—it was thy shadow?”

“Mine.”

“And the anklets?”

“They answered thee when I had no courage to speak.”

He closed his eyes. “Too late.”

“Nay,” she said. “The court was late. The King was late. I also was late. But truth does not become false because men discover it at sunset.”

She lifted his head upon her arm.

“I heard every song. When thy voice trembled, my heart trembled behind the screen. When the people sang in the roads, I knew their love had chosen rightly. Pundarik conquered the ears of the learned; thou didst conquer that which listens when the ears are dust.”

She placed the white garland upon his brow.

“By the right that hidden love gives to its hidden judge, I declare thee victor. This is my garland, Shekhar. Take it from me.”

He looked at her then—truly looked—and all the faces imagined through the years faded before the mortal tenderness of this one. She was not moonlight, nor goddess, nor distant planet. Tears had reddened her eyes. A loosened strand of hair clung to her cheek. Her hands shook as they held him.

Thus the seen may be less perfect than the dream, yet infinitely more dear.

The anklets sounded when she bent over him. That little music, which had crossed the gulf between balcony and court, now trembled beside his failing heart.

Shekhar smiled.

“At last,” he whispered, “the poem is complete.”

The eastern sky was still dark when his breath ceased. Ajita remained beside him, her hand beneath his head, while the lamps burned low and the flowers released their final fragrance.

Upon his brow rested the garland of victory.

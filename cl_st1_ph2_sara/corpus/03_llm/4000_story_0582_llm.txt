The ledge had pinched down to a streak no wider than a bootlace, and after three days of powder, smoke, and language we had not raised enough color to gild the tooth of a sick grasshopper. I suggested to Bill Joyce that perhaps the mountain had been salted backward.

Bill sat on an ore sack outside the tunnel, sharpening a drill with a file he had found in a deserted camp and distrusted on moral grounds.

“A mountain,” said he, “is like a faro bank. It shows you enough to compromise your judgment, then removes your pants by gradual legislation.”

“You’ve prospected worse country.”

“Arizona,” said Bill, and stopped filing.

The evening was cooling off. Down in the draw our burro browsed on a piece of canvas, having already eaten everything more digestible. I tossed another stick on the fire and asked Bill what had happened in Arizona.

He looked at me as if measuring whether I possessed room for the information.

“Well,” he said, “if you’ll agree not to improve the facts, I’ll tell you. This happened when prospectin’ fever had me so bad I’d assay a brick chimney if the mortar looked encouraging. My partner was Kink Martin. Kink was a tolerable miner, a superior shot, and the poorest social ornament ever buttoned into a shirt.

“He got the name Kink because he had one in him. Most men’s dispositions run straight till circumstances bend ’em. Kink’s had been bent early and cooled that way.

“When he was eight years old, his folks was travelin’ through southern Arizona with a little outfit of emigrants. A raid struck ’em at a water hole. His father and mother were killed before his eyes, and Kink was found two days later under a wagon, without water and with enough hate in him to supply a territorial election. He never got over it. He made one nation answer in his mind for what a handful of raiders done, which was ignorant, unjust, and dangerous; but tryin’ to argue him out of it was like explainin’ mercy to a bear trap.

“I argued, though. I was young then, and liable to attempt improvements on anything from human nature to a jackass.

“‘Kink,’ I says, ‘you’re condemnatory beyond the evidence. Them that killed your folks was guilty. Every other man with the same skin ain’t.’

“He’d look off across the desert and say, ‘I know what I seen.’

“That was the whole courthouse, jury, and Supreme Tribunal of Kink Martin.

“Still, he was my partner, and there wasn’t anything he wouldn’t do for me. Once in the Bradshaws a shaft caved and put me under six foot of assorted geology. Kink dug eighteen hours without food, water, or profanity—which last showed the serious condition of his mind—and pulled me out by one boot. Another time I got mountain fever near old Camp Grant, and he packed me sixty miles on a mule that disliked invalids. When the mule tried to scrape me off under a mesquite, Kink whipped the mule, cut down the mesquite, and apologized to me for the climate.

“So we was tied. Not by paper, nor church, nor commercial corporation, but by them little items a man don’t put in an account book because they’re wrote somewhere under his ribs.

“We went into the country southeast of Tucson because a man named Doolan had showed us a lump of quartz with wire gold in it. Doolan said he picked it up beside a hill shaped like a kneelin’ camel, near three white rocks and a spring with a cottonwood tree. That description covered approximately eight thousand square miles of Arizona, but we was excited and didn’t notice its generality.

“We bought two burros, one mule, bacon, flour, beans, coffee, powder, fuse, and a skillet whose bottom was convex from previous suffering. We traveled twelve days through country so dry the lizards carried canteens. Every hill was a kneelin’ camel till you climbed it; then it became a crouchin’ bankruptcy. We found white rocks enough to build a courthouse, but no spring, no cottonwood, and no gold.

“On the thirteenth day we struck a little gulch with iron-stained float and made camp. I went up the wash with a pan while Kink commenced supper. Kink could cook two things—bacon and resentment—and frequently got ’em mixed.

“When I come back, smoke was boilin’ out of camp in a manner that looked organized. The bacon had caught fire. Kink had tried to put it out with flour, then mistook the coffee sack for more flour, and followed that with beans under the theory that any quantity of food ought to smother a smaller quantity. The skillet contained a black architectural compound capable of repairing forts.

“‘Supper,’ says Kink.

“‘It appears to have already had supper,’ I says.

“He handed me a plate.

“Now, partnership imposes obligations. I set down and tasted that composition. It took possession of my mouth like a sheriff. The coffee grounds commenced prospectin’ my gums, the beans was only frightened on one side, and the bacon had passed through burning into mineralization.

“‘How is it?’ Kink asks.

“‘Remarkable,’ says I, when I got a passage open. ‘It has range. It starts as food and finishes as evidence.’

“Kink didn’t laugh. He seldom did. His face had about as much play to it as the door of a powder magazine.

“‘You’re makin’ fun,’ he says.

“‘No,’ says I. ‘I’m makin’ every effort not to.’

“He drew his gun and laid it across his knee.

“‘Laugh,’ he says.

“‘At what?’

“‘At your own joke.’

“‘Kink,’ says I, ‘you can’t compel spontaneous amusement by artillery.’

“‘Laugh.’

“So I laughed. I laughed careful at first, then he raised the muzzle and I enlarged operations. I laughed till the burros pulled their picket ropes and the mule backed into a cactus. I laughed scientific, hysterical, benevolent, and confidential. I laughed in soprano, bass, and one note not since used outside a sawmill. Kink watched till he was satisfied my good humor was genuine, then holstered his gun.

“‘Don’t be sarcastic about my cookin’,’ he says.

“‘I wouldn’t dare,’ says I. ‘It might hear me.’

“That was Kink. His friendship was absolute, but his etiquette sometimes required armed enforcement.

“Next morning we found a vein. It wasn’t the lost treasure Doolan had advertised, but it showed copper and a little silver, enough to keep two fools interested. We sank a hole eight feet, then twelve, then twenty. The deeper it got, the poorer it got, which is a habit ore has when it learns you’re dependin’ on it.

“We’d been there near three weeks when two men rode into camp from the north.

“They were reservation men, peaceful as rain barrels, each with a pass from the agency and a bundle of willow baskets to trade. One was old, with gray in his hair; t’other was maybe twenty. They stopped a proper distance out, raised empty hands, and asked for water.

“I was at the shaft collar windin’ up a bucket. Kink was by the fire.

“I seen his whole body change. He didn’t jump nor holler. Kink got quieter when danger—or what he believed was danger—come along. The life went out of his face and left that old childhood picture lookin’ through.

“‘Kink,’ I says.

“He picked up his rifle.

“The older man spoke again, pointin’ toward the water barrel. He had no weapon in his hands. Neither did the young one.

“‘Don’t,’ I says.

“Kink fired.

“The old man fell beside his horse. The younger wheeled his pony. Kink fired again, and horse and rider went down in a twist of dust beyond the mesquites.

“I hit Kink from the side before he could work the lever again. We rolled through the fire, improved the supper with sand, and arbitrated among the rocks till he got the rifle barrel across my throat.

“‘Turn loose,’ he says.

“‘Not till your fit passes.’

“‘He’s gettin’ away.’

“I looked. The young man had got clear of the fallen pony and was staggerin’ toward the ridge. He went over it before Kink could reach his gun.

“Kink stood still. His lip was bleeding where I’d introduced it to a stone.

“‘They asked for water,’ I says.

“‘He’ll bring soldiers,’ says Kink.

“‘You murdered a man.’

“He looked toward the body, but he didn’t answer me. There wasn’t triumph in him, nor anger now. Only that dead, fixed look. It was as if the eight-year-old boy had pulled the trigger and the grown man had arrived too late to prevent it.

“I went to the older man. He was dead. The pony beyond the brush was dead too, but there was a blood trail leading south from it, light at first and then scattered. The young man had been hit, but he could travel.

“The nearest army post was less than thirty miles north. If he could circle back to the reservation road or meet a patrol, the soldiers would be on us by morning.

“‘We got to bury him,’ Kink says.

“‘We ain’t buryin’ anything except our prospects,’ I says. ‘You’re goin’.’

“‘You comin’?’

“I looked at the shaft, the tools, the burros, and that dead man beside the fire. Then I looked at Kink.

“‘No.’

“He nodded once, as though I’d told him the assay run low.

“‘I reckon that squares us,’ he says.

“‘It don’t square a thing. You ride for Mexico. Take the mule. Leave the burros—they’ll slow you. Keep west of the road and don’t stop at water till dark.’

“‘What’ll you do?’

“‘I’ll think up something unconstitutional.’

“Kink rolled his blanket, filled two canteens, and saddled. Before he mounted, he come over to me.

“‘Bill, I didn’t ask you to stand in.’

“‘No. But if they catch us together, they’ll hang us conversationally. If I stay, they’ll spend time questionin’ me. Time is what you need.’

“He held out his hand.

“‘You remember the Bradshaws?’ he asks.

“‘I remember.’

“‘Camp Grant?’

“‘I remember that too.’

“‘Then forget this.’

“‘Ride,’ I says.

“He rode.

“I dragged the dead man into the shade and covered him with our spare canvas. I gathered the baskets that had spilled when he fell. Fine work they was, woven tight and clean, made for flour or fruit. That detail bothered me worse than anything. A man on the warpath don’t generally carry six market baskets.

“Then I considered my legal situation.

“If I told the truth, I’d send Kink to the gallows. If I lied, any corporal with two ounces of curiosity would sift me. If I ran, they’d know we was both guilty. At last I conceived a plan so simple and idiotic it shone like genius.

“I would be dumb.

“Not foolish. I had that department staffed already. I mean speechless. A mute can’t inform, confess, contradict, perjure, or accidentally pronounce the name of a departing partner. I’d point, shrug, shake my head, and let the United States Army exhaust itself against my silence.

“I practiced for an hour. It’s surprisin’ how many remarks a man wants to make when he’s decided he can’t. First I dropped a hammer on my toe and nearly resigned the plan. Then a burro got into the beans, and I had to correct him by gestures, which he interpreted as religious enthusiasm.

“Near sunset I heard horses.

“Twelve cavalrymen came down the wash with a lieutenant in front and the wounded young man tied upright on a saddle between two troopers. He was pale and bloody, but alive. He pointed at me, then at the covered body, then toward the south.

“The lieutenant dismounted.

“‘Who shot these men?’

“I opened my mouth and made a noise like a door hinge.

“‘What?’

“I touched my tongue, shook my head, and spread my hands.

“‘You can’t speak?’

“I shook my head.

“‘Were you born that way?’

“I nodded. That was my first false statement, and I delivered it with such purity I near believed it myself.

“They searched camp. They found two bedrolls, two cups, two sets of tools, and Kink’s tracks. The lieutenant pointed south.

“‘Your partner?’

“I nodded.

“‘He did the shooting?’

“That was delicate. I looked mournful and waggled my hands in a manner which might’ve signified murder, weather, or an interest in poultry.

“The wounded man said something in his own language. A scout translated.

“‘He says the other one fired. Says this man tried to stop him.’

“The lieutenant studied me.

“‘What’s your name?’

“I pointed to myself and waited.

“‘You understand English?’

“I nodded.

“‘Can you write?’

“Now there was a trap with teeth. I could write Bill and sometimes Joyce if allowed to spell the last part privately. But a man born mute might’ve learned, and then they could ask me every question with a pencil. I shook my head hard.

“‘Read?’

“Another shake.

“‘Convenient,’ says the lieutenant.

“I smiled in an innocent, defective fashion.

“They buried the dead man, treated the survivor, tied my hands, and took me north.

“I tell you, silence sounds easy till you try wearin’ it all day. Words began accumulatin’ in me like tailings behind a busted dam. Every time a soldier called me Dummy, seven replies loaded themselves and waited. When a trooper tied my bedroll wrong, I produced internally a lecture on knots which would’ve educated the navy. At supper they gave me army coffee, and I had an entire sermon rise up concerning burnt beans, public corruption, and the decline of civilization, but I swallowed it. It stuck crossways like a toothpick.

“We reached the fort next afternoon.

“The colonel was a long, gray man with a clipped mustache and eyes like two clean gun barrels. His name was Colonel Hannaford. I knew the name when I heard it, though I couldn’t place him. He sat behind a desk while the lieutenant reported.

“The wounded man had told the scout what happened. He said Kink fired without warning; said I knocked Kink down and argued with him; said Kink escaped. That cleared me of the actual killin’, but the colonel wanted to know who Kink was, where he’d go, and why I had helped him.

“They brought me in.

“‘Sit down,’ says the colonel.

“I sat.

“‘Your name?’

“I gave him my hinge noise.

“‘Yes, I’m told you cannot speak. Your name?’

“I patted my chest, then looked helpless.

“‘Can you write?’

“I shook my head.

“‘Read?’

“I shook it again.

“He held up a printed order upside down.

“‘What is this?’

“I studied it with the grave bewilderment of a calf inspectin’ algebra.

“He turned it right side up so sudden he nearly caught me recognizing a capital A. I let my eyes remain vacant.

“‘How long were you with the fugitive?’

“I counted on my fingers, got to ten, borrowed the other hand mentally, and then gave up.

“‘Months?’

“I nodded.

“‘Years?’

“I nodded again.

“‘Both?’

“I smiled.

“The colonel sighed.

“‘Lieutenant, either this man is a natural idiot or he has raised deceit to a branch of pantomime.’

“I bowed. Compliments was scarce in them days.

“They kept at me for two hours. They drew maps. They showed me the border. They named towns. When they said Kink Martin, I let my face remain peaceful, though inwardly every nerve stood up and saluted. Finally the colonel dismissed me.

“They put me in the guardhouse, but not in irons. The young reservation man was in the infirmary, and I was glad to hear he would live. That eased one corner of my conscience, though the rest remained furnished.

“For two days they interrogated me. The chaplain tried kindness, the lieutenant tried threats, and a sergeant tried surprise.

“He walked behind me and yelled, ‘Rattlesnake!’

“I jumped onto a bench, but I did it silent.

“Another time they set a bottle of whiskey on the table and left me alone with paper and pencil. I drank the whiskey, drew a picture of a buffalo, and labeled it in my mind.

“The colonel himself attempted scientific procedure. He had the surgeon examine my throat.

“‘Nothing visibly wrong,’ says the surgeon.

“I pointed to my head.

“‘He indicates the defect is mental,’ says the colonel.

“I nodded enthusiastically.

“‘That,’ says he, ‘I am prepared to believe.’

“By the third morning I figured Kink had crossed into Mexico. I only needed hold my tongue another day or two, then maybe they’d release me, or charge me with bein’ uninformative, which ain’t generally punished west of Congress.

“But Providence had six mules and an incompetent teamster.

“The fort received supplies by wagon from the railroad. That morning a government wagon stood in the yard loaded with flour, ammunition, harness, mail, and a small barrel of molasses. Six mules was hitched to it—wheel pair, swing pair, and leaders—and every one had a private grievance against organized labor.

“The teamster was a new man named Larkin. He wore yellow gloves.

“No man can drive mules in yellow gloves. Mules regard gloves as proof you got secrets in your hands.

“I was sweepin’ outside the guardhouse under escort when Larkin climbed aboard. He gathered the lines as if pickin’ up wet string and said, ‘Proceed.’

“Mules don’t proceed. They start, balk, bolt, sidestep, reverse, deliberate, or enter litigation. But they do not proceed.

“The off leader turned around to inspect the wagon. The near swing mule tried to step over the tongue. One wheel mule sat down. Larkin clucked.

“I felt a word rise in my chest and strangled it.

“He slapped the lines.

“The leaders jumped, the swing pair crossed, the wheelers surged, and the wagon moved sideways into a hitching rail. Soldiers scattered. The molasses barrel rolled against the tailgate. Larkin stood up and shouted, ‘Whoa!’

“This encouraged speed.

“The team straightened by accident and headed for the main gate. Beyond that gate the road dropped steep along the bluff, made a sharp turn above the dry wash, then crossed a narrow bridge. If they missed the turn, six mules, one teamster, and the quartermaster’s arithmetic would arrive in the wash at the same time.

“Larkin had all six lines under one boot and was pullin’ on the brake handle, which he had mistaken for a devotional instrument.

“I gripped the broom.

“‘Stay here,’ says my guard.

“The wagon hit the gatepost. One rear wheel bounced three foot. Larkin lost his hat and what remained of his education.

“Something broke inside me.

“It might’ve been conscience. More likely it was pressure.

“I threw down the broom and commenced to speak.

“I began with Larkin’s ancestry, taking it back through several doubtful mammals, and proceeded to his gloves, his posture, his knowledge of harness, his right to possess thumbs, and the astonishing negligence of a republic that allowed him near movable property. I then addressed the mules separately by name, color, position, probable vice, and future destination.

“The guard dropped his rifle.

“Every window in headquarters opened.

“I ran.

“The wagon was through the gate and gatherin’ velocity. I caught the tailboard, got one foot on the axle, and climbed over the flour sacks while the ammunition boxes danced under me like dice. Larkin turned and seen me.

“‘Help!’ he says.

“‘That,’ I told him, ‘is the first intelligent proposal you have advanced!’

“I crawled forward, removed him from the seat by the collar, and deposited him among the mail. Then I got the lines untangled.

“Now I ain’t boastin’. A man should be modest regarding gifts bestowed by nature. Some can sing. Some can calculate eclipsements. I can drive mules and use corrective langwidge.

“I wrapped the wheel lines round my wrists, took the leaders between two fingers, stood on the brake, and opened negotiations.

“I spoke to them mules in a style adapted to their nervous construction. Ordinary profanity only dusts a mule’s surface. You got to use language with soak to it, language that enters the pores and reminds the animal of errors committed before birth.

“The wheelers lifted. The swings uncrossed. The leaders quit attemptin’ to exchange sides. We come to the turn with one wheel over the edge and the whole wagon leanin’ toward eternity.

“‘Haw!’ I yelled, followed by a paragraph whose publication would’ve melted type.

“The near leader hauled left. The off leader considered mutiny, but I mentioned a private matter concerning his ears. He yielded. The swing pair bent. The wheelers dug in. The wagon slewed broadside, clipped a boulder, and threw Larkin through three sacks of mail.

“The molasses barrel broke loose.

“It rolled forward between my legs, struck the dashboard, burst, and distributed sweetness over the machinery of government.

“We made the bridge.

“One wheel missed the planks, climbed the rail, and traveled ten yards with daylight under it. I kept the team straight by insultin’ them equally, for a mule is sensitive to favoritism. At the far end the wheel dropped back, the wagon struck level ground, and all six animals stopped together as meek as Sunday-school children.

“For a minute there wasn’t a sound except molasses drippin’ off Larkin.

“Then somebody laughed.

“I looked back. Half the garrison stood along the road. The colonel was in front, hatless, starin’ at me.

“I remembered I was mute.

“I touched my throat and made the hinge noise.

“The colonel walked down the hill slowly.

“‘Sergeant,’ he says, ‘take this miraculous person to my office.’

“In the office he shut the door. I stood before the desk, damp with sweat and molasses, preservin’ what little dignity adhered.

“The colonel sat and looked at me.

“‘Your recovery,’ says he, ‘is medically impressive.’

“I nodded.

“‘Sudden, too.’

“I nodded again.

“‘Would another runaway team cause you to become literate?’

“I gave the hinge.

“He leaned back.

“‘Stop that noise, Joyce.’

“My stomach dropped.

“‘You know me?’ I says.

“‘I knew you when you told that off leader his hide would be made into a congressional briefcase.’

“Then I remembered.

“Seven years before, up near the Mogollon Rim, I’d been freightin’ for an army contractor. A detachment got ambushed in a narrow canyon. Their officer was wounded, their driver killed, and the team bolted with a wagon full of cartridges. I’d come along behind with another outfit.

“I jumped to the army wagon, turned it under fire, and drove the officer and two wounded soldiers out. I hadn’t seen his face clear through the dust and blood.

“‘You was that lieutenant,’ I says.

“‘I was. And you were the teamster who drove six terrified mules through Apache fire while expressing opinions that permanently altered the vocabulary of my command.’

“‘I was younger,’ says I. ‘My language had more elasticity.’

“He almost smiled.

“‘Sit down, Joyce. Tell me the truth.’

“So I told him.

“I told about Kink’s folks, and what the memory had done to him—not as excuse, because there ain’t excuse for shootin’ peaceful men, but as explanation of how a human mind can warp till it mistakes hate for justice. I told how Kink fired, how I fought him, how the young man escaped, and how I sent Kink south.

“The colonel listened without interruptin’.

“‘Why protect him?’ he asked.

“‘Because he’s my partner.’

“‘He killed an innocent man.’

“‘Yes.’

“‘You know he deserves trial.’

“‘Yes.’

“‘Then why?’

“I looked at the floor.

“‘Because when that shaft come down in the Bradshaws, Kink heard the timbers crack and jumped in after me. He could’ve run. He didn’t. When I had fever, he used the last water to cool my head and went thirsty himself. Three times he stood where a sensible man wouldn’t stand because I was there. I couldn’t hand him over without givin’ him one chance to run. Maybe that ain’t law, Colonel, and maybe it ain’t virtue. But it’s the way I’m built.’

“The colonel tapped one finger on the desk.

“‘Had you remained mute, I might have released you for lack of evidence.’

“‘I calculated along them lines.’

“‘But the entire post has now heard you speak. The lieutenant knows you lied. The surgeon knows you deceived him. The sergeant wants to arrest you for making him look foolish, though nature preceded you in that offense.’

“‘What do you aim to do?’

“He stood and went to the window.

“‘My sorrel horse is tied behind headquarters,’ he said. ‘He is fresh. The south gate is temporarily unguarded because everyone is scraping molasses off the mail wagon. I shall remain facing this window for five minutes.’

“I didn’t move.

“‘Colonel—’

“‘You saved my life, Joyce. I cannot clear you. I cannot officially assist you. And I strongly advise that you do not waste the first minute in gratitude.’

“I got up.

“‘Kink ain’t a bad man all through,’ I says.

“‘Most dangerous men aren’t. Go.’

“I went out the back, found the sorrel, and rode south.

“They fired two shots after me from the wall, but both was broad, and I have always believed the colonel selected the marksmen. I crossed the border after dark and found Kink three days later in a mining camp where he was cookin’ supper for two.

“I forgave him for Arizona sooner than I forgave him for that supper.

“As for the colonel’s sorrel,” Bill concluded, testing the edge of his drill with one thumb, “I sold him in Sonora.”

“Wasn’t that ungrateful?” I asked.

“No,” said Bill. “By then the horse was too tired to carry gratitude.”

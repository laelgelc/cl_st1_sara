## The Little Judge

Larry brought the clock home on a wet Thursday evening.

He carried it under one arm, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. Rain shone on the shoulders of his gray topcoat. He set the parcel on the kitchen table and looked at Doris as though he expected applause.

“Well?” he said.

“What is it?”

“If you’d open it, you’d find out.”

Doris dried her hands and untied the string. The paper fell away from a dark wooden cuckoo clock, old and heavily carved. Wooden leaves curled around its face. A little door sat above the hands. The case was scored along one side, and one of the brass weights had a dent in it.

Doris stared.

“Oh, Larry.”

“Fellow at the shop said it was antique.”

She touched the carved leaves. “It’s beautiful.”

“It ought to be. Cost me thirty-eight dollars.”

That was Larry. He could not give anything without fastening the price to it.

Doris lifted the clock carefully. “Where did you find it?”

“Place on Fulton. You’re always looking in those dusty windows at old junk.”

“It isn’t junk.”

“I know what it is. It’s thirty-eight dollars.”

He laughed, but Doris did not. She carried the clock into the living room and held it against the wallpaper. The room had seemed dull for years. The clock changed it at once.

“There,” she said. “By the bookcase.”

“You want it, you can wind it.”

Larry drove a nail into the wall. When the clock was hung, Doris pulled the chains until the weights rose. The pendulum began to swing.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The sound was small and patient.

They waited for eight o’clock. Larry sat with the newspaper spread across his stomach. Doris stood near the clock, watching the minute hand.

Something whirred inside the case. The tiny door snapped open.

A wooden bird shot out.

“Cuckoo,” it called. “Cuckoo. Cuckoo.”

It called eight times. Then it remained outside, balanced on its narrow perch.

Doris leaned closer. The bird was painted brown and cream, with a red patch under its beak. Its glassy eyes were black and unusually bright.

“Why, hello,” she whispered.

The cuckoo turned its head.

Doris laughed. “Did you see that?”

“See what?”

“He looked at me.”

“It’s hooked to a wire.”

The bird turned toward Larry. For a moment it appeared to examine him. Then it backed into the clock, and the little door shut.

Larry rustled his paper. “Marvel of modern science.”

“It likes me.”

“It’s wood.”

Doris rested one finger against the closed door.

“Maybe,” she said, “but it likes me.”

* * *

During the next few days Doris spoke to the cuckoo whenever Larry was out.

She knew it was foolish. She also knew there was no harm in foolishness when no one was there to sneer at it.

“Good morning,” she would say while dusting the clock. “Did you sleep well?”

Sometimes the little door opened though the clock had not reached the hour. The cuckoo would look out at her without calling.

Doris gave it a name. She called it Mr. Pip.

“You’re a solemn little fellow, aren’t you, Mr. Pip?”

The cuckoo tilted its head.

She told it about the woman upstairs who wore high heels at midnight. She told it about the geranium that would not bloom. Once, without meaning to, she told it about Larry.

“He didn’t used to be so cross,” she said. “At least I don’t think he did.”

Mr. Pip watched her.

“Maybe I used to be different, too.”

The clock ticked steadily. It was pleasant to talk when no answer could hurt.

One Saturday Larry caught her.

He came in while she was standing before the clock with a saucer in her hand. On the saucer were crumbs from a sweet roll.

“What are you doing?”

Doris jumped. “Nothing.”

“You feeding that thing?”

“Don’t be ridiculous.”

“I saw you.”

“I was dusting.”

“With coffee cake?”

Larry put down his hat. The cuckoo stood outside its door, looking at Doris.

“Get back in there,” Larry said.

The bird did not move.

Larry came nearer. “Go on. Show’s over.”

He placed his thumb against the bird’s painted breast and pushed.

The cuckoo’s head darted forward. Its beak struck the soft part of his thumb.

Larry swore and pulled away. A bright bead of blood appeared.

“Larry!”

“The miserable little trap!”

He seized the sides of the clock.

“Don’t!” Doris caught his arm. “You’ll pull it off the wall.”

“It bit me.”

“You pushed it.”

“It’s a machine. Machines don’t bite unless something’s wrong with them.”

The cuckoo slipped backward. The door snapped shut.

Larry stared at the blood on his thumb.

Doris tried not to smile.

“You think that’s funny?”

“No.”

“You do.”

“I told you he didn’t like being pushed.”

“He?”

She turned away. “I didn’t mean anything.”

Larry sucked his thumb and glared at the clock. Its pendulum swung calmly beneath the face.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

That evening the cuckoo performed for Doris when she entered the room alone. When Larry followed, the door closed in the middle of the call.

He stood beneath it, listening to the last note die inside the case.

“Cheap piece of junk,” he said.

“You said it cost thirty-eight dollars.”

“That’s what makes it worse.”

* * *

Bob Chambers began stopping by on Tuesday afternoons.

He worked in the insurance office across from the drugstore, and Doris had known him before she married Larry. At first he came to return a book. The next time he brought a record. After that there was no excuse except that Doris was lonely and Bob understood how to listen.

He was a broad, mild man with thinning blond hair. He laughed quietly and never announced the price of anything.

The cuckoo approved of him.

On Bob’s third visit, the little bird came out and called twice though it was twenty minutes past three.

Bob looked up from the sofa. “That thing always do that?”

“Only when it wants to.”

“You’re serious.”

“Of course not.”

She was serious enough to blush.

Bob went to the clock and studied the bird. “Hello there.”

The cuckoo looked at him, then at Doris.

“I think he’s inspecting you,” she said.

“Do I pass?”

“So far.”

Bob smiled. “I’d hate to get on his bad side.”

Doris looked toward the front door. “Larry has.”

The smile faded.

“Doris, you don’t have to stay unhappy forever.”

“Please.”

“I only mean—”

“I know what you mean.”

“Do you?”

She did. That was the trouble.

He touched her hand. She let him keep touching it. The clock ticked above them with the steady sound of a small heart.

The following Thursday Larry came home at four-thirty.

His supervisor had gone to a funeral and closed the office early. Larry considered stopping for a beer, but the rain had started and he remembered there was a bottle in the kitchen cupboard. He let himself into the apartment without calling out.

He heard voices in the living room.

Doris said, “No, Bob. Not yet.”

Then Bob said something too low to hear.

Larry stepped through the doorway.

Doris and Bob were on the sofa. They were not kissing. They were sitting close enough that Larry did not care about the distinction.

For a second nobody moved.

The clock ticked.

Bob stood. “Larry.”

“That’s my name.”

“You’re home early,” Doris said.

“I can see how inconvenient that is.”

“It isn’t what you think.”

Larry laughed once. “Every cheap picture show in town has that line.”

Bob held up a hand. “Let’s keep this sensible.”

“Sensible? You’re in my house with my wife, and you want sensible?”

“Nothing happened.”

“Get out.”

“Larry, listen—”

“Get out before I put you through that window.”

Bob looked at Doris. She gave the smallest shake of her head. He took his coat from the chair.

At the door he said, “If you need me, call.”

Larry moved toward him, but Bob was already gone.

Doris stood beside the sofa, white-faced.

“How long?”

“Don’t.”

“How long has he been coming here?”

“A few weeks.”

“A few weeks.” Larry nodded. “While I’m out earning money.”

“You’re always earning money. Or talking about earning it. You don’t do anything else.”

“I bought you this apartment. I bought that clock. I buy every dress on your back.”

“Yes. You buy things.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means you think the receipt is the same as loving someone.”

The cuckoo door opened.

The bird appeared silently.

Larry looked up. “Stay out of this.”

Its head turned toward Doris.

She began to cry.

Larry pointed toward the bedroom. “Pack a bag.”

Her eyes widened. “You don’t mean that.”

“I don’t keep another man’s leftovers.”

She slapped him.

The sound was flat and sharp. From the wall came a quick wooden chirp.

Larry raised his hand, then lowered it.

“Go,” he said.

Doris packed one suitcase. She moved through the bedroom blindly, taking stockings, a blue dress, her toothbrush. When she came out, Larry stood by the window with his back to her.

She stopped beneath the clock.

“Goodbye, Mr. Pip,” she whispered.

The cuckoo leaned from its door. It made a low sound unlike its usual call.

Doris touched the red patch beneath its beak.

“You keep the clock,” Larry said. “I don’t want it.”

“No.”

“You’re the one who talks to it.”

“You paid thirty-eight dollars for it. You’d never forgive me.”

She picked up her suitcase.

The cuckoo began to call as she crossed the room.

“Cuckoo. Cuckoo. Cuckoo.”

It went on after the door had closed. Larry stood listening until the call became unbearable.

“Shut up!”

The bird withdrew.

The apartment was quiet except for the ticking.

* * *

At first Larry liked having the place to himself.

He left dishes in the sink. He smoked in bed. He kept the radio on during supper and did not have to hear Doris complain about cigar ash.

But the rooms changed at night.

They seemed larger. The furniture stood in stiff, separate shapes. The refrigerator clicked on in the kitchen and startled him. The clock ticked in the living room with a noise that carried through the apartment.

The cuckoo no longer kept proper time.

At seven in the morning, when Larry was shaving, it called three times. At noon on Sunday it did not appear at all. Sometimes he heard movement inside the case—a faint scrape, a rustle—yet the door remained shut.

He took it to a repairman.

The repairman was an old German with rimless glasses. He examined the works, oiled two gears, and said there was nothing wrong.

“Then why doesn’t it come out?”

“It comes out for me.”

As if to prove it, the cuckoo emerged and called once.

Larry stared at it.

On the way home, the clock lay wrapped on the seat beside him. Something knocked from inside the paper.

Larry tightened his hands on the steering wheel.

After that he treated the cuckoo as an enemy.

He wound the clock because he had paid for it and because a clock had a job. Every hour he waited. More often than not, the little door opened and closed without a call. Sometimes the bird put out only its head, saw him, and vanished.

“You did it for her,” he said. “You’ll do it for me.”

The clock ticked.

One evening he telephoned Doris at her sister’s apartment.

He had meant to tell her to come for the rest of her clothes. Instead he said, “That clock’s broken.”

There was a pause.

“No, it isn’t.”

“It won’t perform.”

“Maybe it misses me.”

Larry almost hung up. “You think everything’s alive. Clocks, flowers, that marriage we had.”

“Is that why you called?”

“No.”

“Have you heard from Bob?”

“I haven’t asked.”

“You’re lying.”

“So are you.”

He put down the receiver.

At once the cuckoo called from the living room.

One clear, mocking note.

Larry walked in and stood beneath it. The little door was closed.

* * *

The end came on a hot night in August.

Larry had been drinking. Not enough to make him helpless, he would later have said, but enough to make old insults feel new.

At eleven the mechanism whirred.

The door opened.

Nothing came out.

Larry waited.

“Go ahead.”

The dark square remained empty.

“You know I’m here.”

A faint scrape sounded inside.

Larry dragged a kitchen chair to the wall and climbed onto it. The chair rocked slightly on the rug. He steadied himself with one hand against the clock.

“Come out.”

The mechanism gave a soft click.

Larry put his eye near the opening. He could see only darkness.

“You’ve got a job. I paid for you. You understand that?”

Tick.

Tick.

“You’re mine.”

He reached into the little doorway with one finger. Something struck it hard. He jerked back and nearly lost his balance.

“All right.”

He climbed down and went to the hall cupboard. In the bottom drawer of his tool chest was a claw hammer.

He returned with it hanging at his side.

The clock had begun to whir again.

Larry smiled without humor. “Now you’re interested.”

He placed the chair beneath the clock and climbed up. Sweat stood on his forehead. The hammer felt solid and reasonable in his hand.

“I’m giving you one chance.”

The door stayed closed.

“Come out.”

Nothing.

“Come out and do your job.”

The pendulum swung below him.

“Come out!”

He raised the hammer.

The tiny door exploded open.

The cuckoo shot forward with a force no spring should have possessed. Its pointed beak struck Larry squarely in the left eye.

He screamed and flung both arms upward. The hammer flew from his hand. One chair leg slipped off the rug.

For an instant Larry hung crookedly in the air.

Then he fell.

His head struck the edge of the table. His body turned once and landed beside the chair with the neck bent at an ugly angle.

The hammer bounced and came to rest near his open hand.

Above him, the cuckoo remained on its perch.

It looked down.

The room was quiet now except for the ticking. After a while the bird backed into the clock. The door closed with a neat wooden snap.

* * *

The doctor said death had been immediate.

“Unfortunate accident,” he told the policeman. “He climbed onto the chair with a hammer, probably intending to adjust the clock. Lost his balance.”

Bob Chambers stood near the living-room doorway with Doris. She had one hand pressed to her mouth.

The policeman looked at the overturned chair. “Funny way to adjust a clock.”

“People do foolish things in their own homes,” the doctor said.

Doris began to tremble. Bob put an arm around her.

At that moment the mechanism in the clock started to turn.

Everyone looked up.

The little door opened. The cuckoo came out and called midnight, though Bob’s wristwatch showed twelve minutes past.

Its black eyes passed over the doctor and the policeman. Then they settled on Doris.

The bird gave one final, softer call.

Doris whispered, “Mr. Pip.”

The cuckoo turned toward the body on the floor.

Bob followed its gaze. He saw the small puncture in Larry’s ruined eye. Then he looked at the hammer, the clock, and the calm wooden bird above them.

“Accident,” the doctor repeated.

Bob said nothing.

The cuckoo looked at him for another second, solemn and bright-eyed. Then it withdrew into the darkness, and the little door snapped shut.

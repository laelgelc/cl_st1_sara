# The People of the Rocks

The first time Miss Trevor saw Paul Hubert the whole western sea was paved with gold.

It was not the pale, respectable gold of a drawing-room ornament, but a fiery, living splendour, spreading from the low sun to the shore in a broad pathway that quivered and broke and reunited with every movement of the waves. Above it the sky was daffodil and rose; below, the quiet water deepened through violet into purple, save where little ripples crisped and silvered about the black rocks of Noel’s Cove. Far out, two fishing schooners drifted homeward, their brown sails turned to copper, their masts nodding gravely against the horizon.

Miss Trevor, who had come to Noel’s Cove because her physician had commanded sea air and because an acquaintance had assured her that there was absolutely nothing to do there, sat on a ledge of red sandstone and congratulated herself upon having discovered the one place in the world which had not yet learned to be tiresome.

Then she saw Paul.

He was sitting on a lower rock, with his chin in his hands and his elbows on his bare brown knees, gazing along the golden road as if he expected some royal procession to come over it. He wore a faded blue jersey, patched trousers, and a fisherman’s cap pushed back from his forehead. There was nothing princely in the garments; nevertheless, Paul looked as young princes should—and seldom do.

He had a slender, vivid face, browned by sun and wind; a mouth that seemed always on the verge of laughter or wonder; and dark eyes in which thoughts came and went like swallows across a bright sky. His hair, where the cap had not imprisoned it, curled in damp rings of bronze.

Miss Trevor watched him for several moments before speaking. She was not accustomed to watching any person long without presently arranging him in some convenient little compartment of her mind. But Paul declined to be arranged. He belonged to the shore, certainly; yet there was something about him that belonged equally to the sunset.

“What do you see out there?” she asked.

Paul turned. He did not start, for he had known all the while that she was there; the rocks told him most things that happened on them.

“I’m watching the Golden Lady go home,” he said.

Miss Trevor looked obediently at the sea.

“I see a fishing boat, two gulls, and a great deal of extremely beautiful water. Which is the Golden Lady?”

“You can’t see her now,” said Paul, with a regretful shake of his head. “She went through the door just when you spoke. She don’t like strangers looking at her.”

“What door?”

“That yellow place under the sun. It’s shut now. It only opens for half a minute, and if you aren’t watching particular you miss it. The Golden Lady lives in Sunset Land.”

“How inconvenient of her. Does she never come here?”

“Oh, yes, but only at night. She has a little boat made out of a pearl shell, with seven white moths harnessed to it. She comes to ask Nora what people have been saying about her.”

“Nora?”

Paul pointed to a tall, narrow rock standing apart from the others. A fringe of green weed trailed about its foot, and on its summit a small white stone gleamed like a cap.

“That’s Nora. She was a girl once, but she got bewitched. I come and talk to her, because it’s lonesome being a rock. She can’t answer me aloud, of course, but I know what she says.”

Miss Trevor moved down to the lower ledge.

“Will you introduce me?”

Paul considered her candidly.

“Nora’s kind of shy. She don’t like people that laugh.”

“I shall be as solemn as the minister.”

“That might frighten her worse,” said Paul. “But you can sit here. This is the best place, only you mustn’t step on that crack. The Twin Sailors live under it, and they’re asleep in the daytime.”

Miss Trevor avoided the crack with due care. She had been told that the shore children were ignorant, uncouth little creatures who stared at visitors and fled from questions. This one neither stared nor fled, and ignorance, if he possessed it, sat oddly on him.

“What are the Twin Sailors like?”

“They’re twins,” Paul explained, “only one is very old and the other is quite young. That happened because one of them drank from the Morning Spring and one drank from the Evening Spring. They sail a green boat under the island. Some nights, when the tide’s high, I hear them knocking with their oars. But none of this is true, you know,” he added anxiously.

“I am relieved to hear it.”

“I just make it up. Stephen says it’s wrong to tell what isn’t true, so I always explain. There isn’t really any Golden Lady, nor Nora either—not that way. Nora’s just a rock. But when you’ve known her a long while, she seems more than just a rock.”

“Many persons never become more than just persons, however long one knows them,” said Miss Trevor. “Your name, please?”

“Paul Hubert. I’m eleven. How long have you been yourself?”

Miss Trevor laughed; then, remembering Nora’s prejudice, she turned the laugh into a cough.

“Thirty-five years, more or less.”

“That’s a long time,” said Paul compassionately. “You must know nearly everything.”

“Far less than I did at eleven. Where do you live?”

He pointed eastward, where a small grey cottage crouched above the cove. It had been weathered by so many storms that it seemed less built than washed ashore—a great seashell, faded and roughened, which the tide had cast beyond its reach and forgotten.

“With Stephen Kane. He’s getting the nets in. That’s his boat—the Mary Ellen.”

“Is Stephen your father?”

“No. My father was lost before I was born, and Mother died when I was five. Stephen took me. He used to know Mother before she married Father.”

The words were spoken without self-pity. Grief, to Paul, belonged to the dim backward country of childhood; Stephen, the cottage, and Noel’s Cove were the present world, solid and sufficient.

“Is Stephen kind to you?”

Paul looked surprised.

“Why, he’s Stephen.”

This appeared to him a complete answer. Before Miss Trevor could demand a translation, a man came up the path from the beach carrying a coil of rope. He was tall, stooping a little from years of bending against wind, with a lean brown face and quiet grey eyes. His clothes smelled pleasantly of salt, tar, and fish. At sight of Miss Trevor beside Paul, some reserve, habitual and almost impersonal, closed over his expression.

“Tea’s ready,” he said.

Paul sprang up.

“This is Miss Trevor, Stephen. She’s been here thirty-five years, and she knows about Nora and the Golden Lady.”

Stephen removed his cap.

“Evening, ma’am.”

“I have been trespassing upon Paul’s kingdom,” said Miss Trevor. “He is a most entertaining sovereign.”

A faint smile moved under Stephen’s moustache.

“He talks considerable.”

“And thinks more. May I come to see your cottage some day?”

“If you’ve a mind to.”

Paul slipped his hand into Stephen’s. They went up the path together, the man shortening his stride without seeming to do so, the boy looking back to wave from the turn. Miss Trevor watched them until the cottage swallowed them.

Behind her the sunset door had closed. The sea was purple now, breathing softly against the shore, and Nora stood black and solitary in the gathering dusk. Miss Trevor, who had intended to remain at Noel’s Cove for a fortnight, decided then that she might endure it for a month.

* * *

She met Paul often after that, sometimes by accident and sometimes by the intelligent management of accident.

He showed her the hollow where the Little Queen kept imprisoned winds in blue bottles; the flat stone upon which the Moon Man dried his silver cloaks; and a twisted juniper which, in certain lights, was plainly an old woman stooping over a green fire. Every tale was related with abundance of detail and with a scrupulous disclaimer at the end.

“You understand it isn’t true, Miss Trevor. Not really true.”

“Of course. Merely as true as most interesting things.”

Paul did not understand this, but he liked the sound of it.

He was an odd blending of wildness and delicacy. He could row a boat through rough water, clean a fish, mend a small rent in a net, and predict rain from the sea’s colour; but he apologised to limpets before prying them from rocks and would not disturb a gull’s feather that had fallen into Nora’s pool because, he said, she had been saving it for a letter. He had never seen a railway train. He knew little of kings, dates, fractions, or geography, but he could distinguish twenty shades in the evening sky and had names for each mood of the sea.

Miss Trevor, who possessed more books than she had ever read and had crossed the Atlantic three times without once imagining that the ocean might have moods, was captivated.

She went one afternoon to the grey cottage. Its two rooms were bare but shiningly clean. A red geranium burned at the window; bunches of dried herbs hung from the rafters; shells stood in rows upon the mantel, and above them was a faded likeness of a young woman with large, wistful eyes.

Paul followed Miss Trevor’s glance.

“That’s Mother. Stephen says I have her eyes, only mine are darker.”

Stephen, mending a net by the door, pulled one knot needlessly tight.

“You’ve her look at times,” he said.

Miss Trevor saw then—or believed she saw—the whole history. Stephen had loved Paul’s mother, quietly and hopelessly; she had chosen another, and when death took both parents, he had accepted their child as naturally as he might have taken a storm-driven bird into shelter. He would never have told this. His silence had grown over his feeling as grey lichen grows over stone, concealing it from careless eyes and preserving it beneath.

Paul brought out a packet of foolscap tied with blue yarn.

“My book,” he announced.

The writing wandered up and down the pages in a large, uncertain hand. Words were misspelled, commas appeared where no commas could reasonably be expected, and capital letters flourished according to Paul’s affection rather than grammar. Yet Miss Trevor had not read three pages before her amusement changed to astonishment.

There was a story of a drowned bell that rang under the sea whenever a lonely child cried on shore; another of a boy who sailed to the north in a boat made from a gull’s shadow; and a third, unfinished, concerning Nora before enchantment, when she had been the lighthouse-keeper’s daughter and wore a violet gown because she pitied the evening.

“Did you write all these yourself?”

Paul flushed with pleasure.

“Yes. Only Stephen helped me spell ‘magnificent.’ It’s an awful long word. Do you like them?”

“Like them!” Miss Trevor laid a hand dramatically upon her heart. “Paul, I am overcome.”

“In the end Nora finds the key,” he said eagerly. “It’s inside a whale’s song, and the Twin Sailors have to catch the song in a net. But I haven’t worked out how they get it open. Prob’bly with moonlight.”

“Undoubtedly with moonlight. Nothing else would do.”

Stephen glanced across at them. He did not comprehend why a lady who wore silk gloves and spoke as though every sentence had been polished beforehand should make so much of the boy’s scribbling. Paul’s tales were part of the weather of the cottage. They blew in at breakfast, wandered through supper, and sometimes kept one awake after bedtime. Stephen valued them because they were Paul’s, but it had never occurred to him that the world might value them too.

“Has he schooling?” Miss Trevor asked.

“Winter school at the harbour when weather’s fit,” said Stephen. “He reads well. Does sums some.”

“I hate sums,” Paul confessed. “They always come out wrong even when you coax them.”

“He should have regular lessons.”

Stephen bent over the net.

“Maybe.”

“He has a remarkable imagination.”

“Has he?”

The two words were not sceptical; they merely held the fisherman’s distrust of terms that classified what he loved. To Stephen, Paul was not a remarkable imagination. He was the small, warm presence across the table, the voice in the boat, the tousled head on the pillow in the adjoining room.

Miss Trevor left the cottage in a meditative mood. She walked along the headland while the wind, fragrant with clover and salt, played capriciously with her veil. Below, Paul had run down to Nora and was telling her something with animated gestures. His words rose and fell too faintly to be understood, but his happiness was visible about him like light.

It seemed to Miss Trevor both beautiful and insufficient.

For Miss Trevor believed in opportunity with the ardour of one who has always possessed it. Beauty should be cultivated, talent trained, intelligence furnished with all the shining instruments of knowledge. It was intolerable that Paul should grow up hauling nets, with no books but a Bible, an almanac, and three damaged volumes of voyages; intolerable that his prism of an imagination, turning every common ray into rainbows, should be left to chance and weather.

She began, therefore, by sending him books.

There were tales of knights and voyages, poems with pictures, a map of the world, and a volume of mythology which Paul loved because it proved that grown persons had once made rock people too. He received them with rapture. For a week he walked about in a cloud of Greeks, dragons, islands, and enchanted swords.

“Stephen,” he cried one evening, “did you know there was a man who had to hold up the whole sky?”

“Sounds a foolish job,” said Stephen. “Couldn’t he set a post under it?”

Paul considered this practical amendment and rejected it reluctantly.

Miss Trevor taught him things as they sat on the shore. She showed him where Noel’s Cove lay upon the map, and Paul was amazed to find it represented by no more than a nameless indentation.

“They’ve made it very small.”

“It is small, darling.”

“Not when you’re in it.”

She told him of cities where lamps shone all night, museums filled with treasures, libraries with miles of books, schools where clever boys learned Latin and astronomy, and countries where the flowers and stars were different. Paul listened breathlessly.

“Could a person see all those things?”

“If that person had money, education, and a benefactress of unusually resolute character.”

“What’s a benefactress?”

“I am,” said Miss Trevor.

* * *

The idea came to her gradually and then, as her ideas often did, presented itself as a moral necessity.

She had no children. Her house in town was large and absurdly quiet; her income exceeded her reasonable wants; and here was Paul, gifted, beautiful, affectionate, growing like a sea-pink between stones when he might be transplanted into a garden. She pictured tutors, travel, music, companionship, and, beyond them, a splendid future in which Paul wrote books that everyone read and acknowledged Miss Trevor in the dedication.

She spoke first to Stephen.

He was drawing his boat higher on the beach at the close of a grey day. The sky hung low, pearl-coloured, and the sea rolled in long, subdued lines, as if listening.

“Stephen,” said Miss Trevor, “I want Paul.”

The rope stopped in his hands.

“Want him?”

“To adopt him—legally, properly, altogether. I would give him every advantage. He should attend the best schools, travel, have books and masters and boys of his own age. He should lack nothing.”

Stephen resumed pulling the boat. Once it was secure, he coiled the rope very slowly.

“He lacks much now, you reckon?”

“Materially, he lacks nearly everything.”

“He’s warm and fed.”

“I don’t mean that. He has talent. Surely you must see he is not an ordinary shore boy.”

The phrase was unfortunate. Stephen’s face did not alter, but his hand went into his pocket and closed about his pipe until the stem pressed hard into his palm.

“No,” he said. “He ain’t ordinary.”

“He ought not to spend his life fishing.”

“Why not?”

“My good Stephen, because he is capable of more.”

“Fishing’s honest.”

“Naturally. But honesty is not the only consideration. There are worlds of beauty, knowledge, and experience to which he has a right.”

Stephen looked out over the cove. Paul was far along the rocks, a blue speck beside Nora, his voice carried shoreward in fragments by the wind.

“He’s got a world,” said Stephen.

“A very little one.”

“Might be big enough for him.”

Miss Trevor drew her cloak closer. She was conscious of impatience, but also of pity. Stephen loved the child according to his lights; no doubt of that. Yet love, unguided by judgment, could be selfish.

“Do you think he will always be content here?”

“Dunno.”

“Do you think you can educate him?”

“No.”

“Can you show him other countries? Give him masters? Put him among cultivated people?”

“No.”

“Then you must admit—”

“I don’t admit money’ll make him happier.”

“It will give him the means to become himself.”

Stephen’s eyes came back from the sea and rested upon her.

“He is himself.”

For a moment Miss Trevor had no answer. Then she smiled, a little sadly and a little triumphantly, as one smiles upon a worthy but defeated opponent.

“We need not decide for him. Let Paul choose.”

At that the blood left Stephen’s face under its brownness. He turned away, ostensibly to examine the knot on the boat rope.

“If he wants to go,” he said at last, “I’ll not hold him.”

“You will say nothing to influence him?”

“No.”

“And you will consent?”

“If he chooses.”

Miss Trevor offered her hand. Stephen looked at it as if it were some delicate implement whose use he had forgotten, then took it.

“You are doing what is best,” she said gently.

“Maybe.”

That evening Stephen sat long after Paul had gone to bed. The lamp burned low, filling the cottage with amber shadow. Across the room lay Paul’s cap, a book face downward, and one small wet boot which had been left to dry by the stove. Stephen’s gaze moved from one thing to another with the mute attention of a man memorising the belongings of the dead.

He had said he would not influence the boy. He kept his word so sternly that he did not go in to draw the blanket higher over Paul’s shoulder, though the night was cold. He feared the tenderness of that common action might betray him.

* * *

Miss Trevor told Paul next day, seated beside him on the flat rock that overlooked the Golden Lady’s door.

She described the room he should have in town—a room papered in blue, with shelves for books and a window looking over gardens. She promised him a pony in summer, skates in winter, journeys by train, and perhaps, when he was older, a voyage across the ocean. He should learn the names of the stars, read all the poems ever written, see great pictures, hear orchestras, and meet boys who would be his friends.

Paul’s eyes grew enormous.

“Would I have a whole shelf of books?”

“Many shelves.”

“And could I write whenever I wanted?”

“You should have a desk of your own.”

“A real desk—with drawers?”

“With drawers, locks, pigeonholes, and every tiresome item required by genius.”

“Could I see a city?”

“You would live in one.”

“And trains?”

“Hundreds.”

“And an elephant?”

“In due season, even an elephant.”

Paul clasped his knees. The prospect rushed upon him in a dazzling procession: bright streets, towers, locomotives breathing smoke, libraries vast as the sea, elephants moving through tropical forests, and himself in the midst of it all, no longer guessing at the world but seeing it. His imagination, enlisted upon Miss Trevor’s side, multiplied every promise until the future shone like Sunset Land with all its doors flung open.

“Would Stephen come?”

Miss Trevor’s voice softened.

“No, dear. Stephen belongs here. But you could visit him.”

Paul’s radiance dimmed.

“Often?”

“Certainly in the summers, and perhaps at Christmas.”

Christmas and summer were immeasurably far apart. He looked toward the cottage, where smoke rose in a thin blue strand. Stephen was mending lobster pots by the wall.

“Does he want me to go?”

“He wants you to choose freely.”

Paul went up the path.

Stephen did not look at him when he came near. The lobster pot between his knees had already been mended, but he continued twisting the twine.

“Miss Trevor says she’d take me to live with her.”

“So she told me.”

“She says I can have lessons and books and travel everywhere.”

“Aye.”

“I might write a real book some day.”

“You might.”

“Do you think I ought to go?”

There it was—the question Stephen had dreaded, laid trustfully in his hands. One word, one tremor, one honest plea would have kept Paul beside him. The man’s heart rose in savage revolt against generosity. Say no. Tell him the town is hard, the lady changeable, the whole glittering future a painted lie. Tell him he is needed. Take the choice away.

Stephen’s face remained expressionless.

“You must settle that yourself, Paul.”

“Would you be lonesome?”

“I’ve been alone before.”

The answer hurt the boy in a place he did not understand. He watched Stephen’s bent head.

“But would you be glad if I had all those things?”

“If they made you glad.”

Paul turned away. He walked down to the shore, where the tide was creeping in under a sky of pale green. Nora waited in her accustomed place, grave and slender, her white cap catching the last light. The Twin Sailors’ crack was already awash.

“I may be going away,” Paul told them.

A little wave sighed along the sand.

“Only to town. There’ll be books there—’normous lots of books—and trains and everything. Miss Trevor says I’m gifted. That means I can do something if I get learned how.”

The sea breathed in and out. Nora said nothing.

“I can come back in summer,” Paul added. “Prob’bly you won’t notice. Rocks don’t count time like people.”

He tried to imagine telling them goodbye, but the word would not come. It was too large and final; it belonged to shipwrecks and death, not to a journey of ten miles.

“I’m not saying goodbye,” he whispered. “I’ll say it tomorrow.”

By tomorrow the glitter had returned. Miss Trevor brought him a small leather travelling case, and Paul, seeing his initials stamped in gold upon the lid, felt that adventure had already begun. He packed his few treasures: a shell with a rosy interior, a gull’s feather, three smooth stones, the volume of mythology, and his foolscap book. Stephen folded his clothes and placed them inside without comment.

When the hired carriage arrived, nearly everyone in Noel’s Cove came to stare. Miss Trevor, handsome and benevolent in travelling grey, directed the driver. Paul climbed in and climbed out twice, first because he had forgotten his cap and then because he had forgotten to look under his pillow.

At last he stood before Stephen.

“Well,” said Stephen.

“Well,” said Paul.

Miss Trevor looked away. She had expected tears and clinging, but neither appeared. Paul’s throat hurt too much for speech; Stephen had gone beyond speech altogether.

“You mind what Miss Trevor tells you,” Stephen said.

“Yes.”

“And keep dry.”

“Yes.”

A silence opened between them, deep as the sea.

Then Stephen held out his hand.

Paul ignored it and flung both arms about his waist. Stephen’s arms closed around him once—hard, almost roughly. In his pocket the broken stem of his pipe pressed into his palm. The next moment he had set the boy away.

“Best get along.”

As the carriage moved, Paul leaned out, waving until the grey cottage, the beach, and Stephen’s upright figure were lost behind the hill. Then he faced forward. The road unrolled before him, white and alluring, and Miss Trevor began telling him about the train they should take next month.

He listened. In the end Paul “liked.”

He liked the carriage, the speed, the farms passing on either side, and the sudden appearance of the town with its clustered roofs and shining windows. He liked Miss Trevor’s house, which seemed to contain more rooms than Noel’s Cove had houses. He liked the blue bedroom, the polished bedstead, the silver-backed brush, the basin painted with birds, and the desk—his desk—with tiny brass handles and a key.

He was charmed by the electric lights, which bloomed at a touch. He ate supper from thin china and was introduced to Mrs. Baines, the housekeeper, who called him “Master Paul” with an expression of respectful doubt.

Afterward Miss Trevor showed him a shelf of books selected for him.

“You may begin any one you choose.”

Paul ran his fingers over the bright spines.

“Are they all mine?”

“All.”

His smile came then, sudden and luminous, as though someone had lighted a lamp within him. Miss Trevor felt richly rewarded. She had been right. Of course she had been right.

“This is only the beginning,” she said. “You shall have music lessons, and on Monday a gentleman will come to discover what you know.”

“He won’t take long,” said Paul.

At nine o’clock she kissed him and left him in the blue room.

The bed was very soft. The curtains were very white. The clock upon the mantel ticked with the precise importance of a creature performing a public duty. Paul lay still, his hands behind his head, and gazed at the ceiling.

For a little while he was perfectly happy.

Then a carriage rattled along the street.

The noise was nothing like the drag of Stephen’s boat over shingle. Another carriage followed, and somewhere a door slammed. The house settled about him with faint taps and creaks; pipes murmured in the walls; footsteps crossed the room overhead. He listened for the sea between these sounds and could not hear it.

At Noel’s Cove the sea was never absent. Sleeping or waking, one felt its great breathing. On quiet nights it whispered along the sand; in storms it thundered against the rocks and shook the cottage windows; at low tide it withdrew with a long, secretive rustling, as though gathering its robes to depart. It was the first voice Paul had known and the last he heard each night.

Here there was only the clock.

Tick—tick—tick.

He sat up. Beyond the window lay roofs, chimneys, and a strip of starless sky coloured yellow by lamps. A wind moved along the street, but it was not the free wind of the headland. It wandered between the houses like a fettered, cringing thing, fumbling at shutters and dying in narrow corners.

Paul thought of Stephen alone at the supper table. There would be one cup, one plate, one chair pushed back. Perhaps Stephen had forgotten to bring in the nets. Perhaps he had remembered and had gone down in the dark. He always whistled when he worked at night—not tunes, exactly, but little broken notes that fitted the wind.

Paul buried his face in the pillow.

He would see Stephen at Christmas. Christmas was only months away. He had books. Tomorrow there would be wonders. He might see a train before the week was out.

But Nora was standing in the tide.

The thought came so vividly that he lifted his head. Nora was there under the stars, weed streaming from her feet; and because he had not said goodbye, she did not know whether he had deserted her or been carried off by enchantment. The Twin Sailors would knock beneath the island and wait for him to answer. The Golden Lady might come in her shell boat and ask where he was, and nobody would be able to tell her.

“They aren’t true,” Paul whispered fiercely. “They aren’t true at all.”

That made no difference.

He began to cry—not neatly and silently, but with his whole small body shaken by grief. He wanted the salt smell of the cottage, the lump in his mattress, the geranium black against the moonlit pane. He wanted Stephen’s boots by the stove and Stephen’s quiet voice saying, “Best turn in.” He wanted the rocks, the wet sand, the path through bayberry bushes, the cry of gulls at dawn. He wanted them now, with an anguish that seemed capable of breaking his heart.

Never—never could books replace them. Never could trains, cities, lessons, elephants, or all the shining world Miss Trevor had promised fill the place of that one grey cove. He had thought home was merely where he happened to live. Now, torn from it, he knew it had lived in him.

The clock struck eleven.

Paul slid out of bed.

His decision was not made so much as discovered. It had been waiting beneath his misery, clear and immovable.

He dressed quickly. The leather case stood by the wardrobe. He opened it, took out the shell, feather, stones, and mythology, and put them into his pockets. The foolscap book he carried to the desk.

For a moment he held it against his chest. It was his dearest possession—the gathered country of his mind, Nora’s history, the Golden Lady’s voyages, the drowned bell, and the boy who sailed in a shadow boat. Miss Trevor had praised it. In some obscure way it represented everything she believed he might become.

Paul laid it on the desk.

He found paper and wrote, slowly and with several blots:

> Dear Miss Trevor,  
> I thank you for the books and the room and all. I am sorry but I have to go home. I b’lieve I am not the kind that can be transplanted. Please keep my book because you liked it. I did not mean to be bad.  
> Paul Hubert.

He hesitated and added:

> None of the stories are true.

Then he crept downstairs with his shoes in his hand.

The front door was bolted above his reach, but a chair stood nearby. He dragged it across the carpet, wincing at every scrape, climbed, drew the bolt, and slipped out. The night received him—not the wide, salt night of Noel’s Cove, but a dim town night smelling of brick, smoke, and sleeping gardens. Yet once he had turned onto the eastern road, the houses began to thin, and the wind lifted its head.

Ten miles lay before him.

At first he walked rapidly, almost merrily. Escape had made him light. The moon rose, a worn silver coin above the fields, and the road shone pale between dark hedges. His pockets bumped with every step; the mythology book was heavy, and before long he hid it beneath a stone, meaning to return for it someday.

After three miles his feet hurt. After five he was cold. Once a wagon overtook him, and he crouched behind a hedge until it passed, fearing Miss Trevor had discovered his flight. Once a dog rushed from a farmyard and barked so fiercely that Paul’s courage deserted him; but he stood still and spoke to it as he spoke to gulls, and presently it sniffed his hand and followed him half a mile.

The night grew kinder as the town fell behind. Stars came out—his own stars, no longer drowned by lamps. The fields opened on either side, silver with dew, and the wind moved over them untrammelled, bringing at last a faint savour of salt.

Paul’s weariness vanished for several minutes.

“The sea!” he cried.

The dog, offended by this sudden enthusiasm, turned homeward.

On and on Paul went. His shoes rubbed blisters, his knees trembled, and more than once he sat on a roadside stone and nearly fell asleep. But whenever he closed his eyes he saw Stephen pushing off alone at dawn, and he rose again.

The east whitened. Black trees separated themselves from the sky; fences emerged; a bird gave one tentative note and then another. Pale green spread along the horizon, deepened to daffodil, and flushed with rose. Morning touched the world delicately, as though waking a beloved child.

At the crest of the last hill Paul saw Noel’s Cove.

The sea lay blue-grey and luminous under dawn. Mist floated in thin scarves above the water; the rocks rose dark and familiar; the cottage chimney sent up its morning smoke. Far below, Stephen was on the beach beside the Mary Ellen, carrying a basket toward the boat.

Paul tried to call, but his throat closed. He began to run.

He ran down the hill, stumbling over stones, scattering sheep, his cap flying off and forgotten. The gladness in him was an upspringing fountain, too strong for his tired body. At the foot of the path he cried:

“Stephen!”

Stephen turned.

For one instant he did not move. His face, grey and sleepless in the dawn, remained blank; then something flashed across it—exultation, gratitude, an almost terrible relief—before the old restraint descended.

Paul rushed against him. Stephen caught him and held him so tightly that the shell in Paul’s pocket cracked.

“I came back,” Paul sobbed. “I couldn’t stay. I tried, but I couldn’t. Are you glad—are you glad?”

Stephen’s chin rested on the boy’s hair. He looked beyond him at the brightening sea, perhaps because he could not safely look anywhere else.

“I reckon I am,” he said.

“Did you know I’d come?”

“No.”

“Did you think I would?”

Stephen’s arms tightened.

“I hoped.”

The word was hardly louder than the tide among the stones, but Paul heard all that had not been spoken—the lonely supper, the wakeful night, the boat made ready because work must continue whether hearts broke or not. He clung to Stephen, laughing and crying together.

“Miss Trevor’ll be angry.”

“Maybe.”

“I left her my book.”

“That so?”

“She liked it. I thought she ought to have something.”

Stephen picked up Paul’s bruised hand and looked at the reddened palm.

“You walked?”

“All the way. It’s only ten miles, but it’s longer in the night.”

“Aye.”

“I don’t want the city. I don’t want a desk—not much. I want to stay here. Could I, Stephen? Always?”

“If you’ve a mind to.”

“And you won’t think I’m wasting my gifts?”

Stephen bent to retrieve the cracked shell that had fallen between them.

“Sea don’t waste what belongs to it,” he said.

The sun lifted then, fiery golden above the rim of the world. Light ran across the water, touched the Mary Ellen’s mast, kindled every wet stone, and entered the cove like a blessing. The waves crisped and silvered on the sand. From the high ledges gulls rose with long, joyous cries.

Paul drew a deep breath. The air filled him with home.

For a few moments he stood beside Stephen, his hand warm in the fisherman’s hard one. Then he remembered.

“Nora!”

He broke away and ran along the beach, limping, laughing, calling explanations before he was halfway there.

Stephen watched him go. Paul scrambled over the familiar rocks toward the tall dark figure in its white cap, where the morning tide curled lovingly about its feet. He had no foolscap book now, no written kingdom to prove what he could create; but the sea was before him, the light above him, and all his people were waiting.

He ran to see Nora.

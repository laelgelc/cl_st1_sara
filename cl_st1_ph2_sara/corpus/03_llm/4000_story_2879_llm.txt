## THE COURT OF NEPTUNE

For eleven days the barkentine *Mohawk*, out of Portland for Buenos Ayres with a cargo of machinery, pine boards, and certain agricultural implements which no man aboard could name with confidence, had lain in the Atlantic Doldrums as helplessly as a painted ship in a shop-window.

Her canvas hung from the yards in folds of discouraged gray; the main boom complained at every lift of the long, oily swell; the blocks tapped and creaked with that maddening irregularity which is more trying to the nerves than any honest gale. The pitch softened in the seams. Brass-work burned the hand. The water alongside shone like blue-black glass, except when some fin broke it or a bubble rose from depths concerning which the youngest hands held opinions as gloomy as they were inaccurate.

Captain Charles Pitkin walked the poop in his slippers and a white drill coat, looking alternately at the sky, the sea, and the barometer, though none of the three had anything new to tell him.

Captain Pitkin was forty-two, broad of shoulder, easy in command, and possessed of that sort of temper which becomes better as circumstances become worse, until some very small domestic inconvenience overturns it altogether. He could carry a barkentine through a north-easter with composure; but a missing collar-button had once caused him to speak sharply to a customs officer, for which Miss Rose Pitkin made him apologize.

Rose was his elder sister by seven years, and had kept house for him, in one sense or another, since he had first gone to sea. She had accompanied him on three voyages, partly because the doctor recommended a change of air and partly because the captain could not be trusted to remember whether he had eaten dinner. She was a spare, upright woman, with steady gray eyes and brown hair only beginning to silver at the temples. Her firmness had been called masculine by people who imagined decision belonged exclusively to men; yet she mended stockings, made excellent coffee, and could tell by instinct when the cabin bread was being stolen by the second mate.

On the morning of the twelfth day a cat’s-paw darkened the water to the eastward.

The captain stopped in his walk.

“Mr. Larkin,” he called, “there’s our wind at last.”

The mate, who had spent the calm looking as if he held Captain Pitkin personally responsible for it, came aft. For half a minute both watched the faint roughness spreading over the sea. Then the royal stirred, the foretopsail gave one reluctant flap, and the green water under the bows began to whisper.

Within an hour the *Mohawk* was leaning gently before the north-east trade. Her wake stretched astern in a bubbling white path; the heat remained, but it was now a clean, moving heat, and every soul aboard recovered some portion of his natural character.

The cook sang over his coppers. The steward ceased predicting hurricanes. The men forward washed clothes, exchanged lies, and discussed the probable date of their arrival. Even the ship’s cat, Alexander, who had spent the calm lying under the windlass with the expression of an unjustly imprisoned emperor, came out and sharpened his claws upon a coil of new manila.

Captain Pitkin, whose spirits rose with the breeze, remembered that the *Mohawk* was approaching the Equator.

“Mr. Larkin,” said he after dinner, “how many of our people have never crossed the Line?”

The mate considered.

“Three certain, sir. Christian, Oscar, and Josef. They shipped at Portland. Fritz claims he crossed once in a Hamburg steamer, but I wouldn’t trust Fritz’s memory on a matter more than two years old.”

“Fritz shall be exempt,” said the captain. “We shall need him.”

“For what, sir?”

“For Neptune.”

The mate’s solemn face relaxed.

Captain Pitkin’s plans, when innocent, were frequently elaborate, and when elaborate were rarely innocent to everybody. He had not held a Crossing-the-Line ceremony for some years, modern crews being irregular in their respect for tradition; but the long calm had left the company hungry for diversion. Christian, Oscar, and Josef were green hands of exceptional freshness, and the captain felt that to carry them across the Equator without introducing them to the sovereign of the seas would be almost a fraud upon that monarch.

The three had come from neighboring villages in Norway, though Josef insisted that his grandmother was Swedish and Christian maintained that one side of his family had once lived in Denmark. They spoke serviceable English when nothing depended upon it, but under excitement their acquaintance with the language dissolved rapidly. Christian, the oldest and boldest, was a fair-haired fellow of twenty-four. Josef was thickset, obedient, and inclined to believe whatever was told him by a person louder than himself. Oscar, the youngest, had pale blue eyes, long arms, and an imagination which supplied terrors faster than experience could correct them.

The captain summoned the privileged members of the crew beneath the break of the poop.

“We cross the Line this afternoon or to-morrow,” said he, “and King Neptune will come aboard to examine the strangers. Fritz, you will represent His Marine Majesty.”

Fritz was the ship’s carpenter, a mountainous German with a brown beard reaching nearly to the middle of his chest. Nature had made him large enough for a philosopher or a conqueror, but had neglected to furnish the inward equipment commonly thought useful to either.

“I got to make somethings?” he asked.

“A crown first. Then put it on.”

Fritz brightened. Carpentry he understood.

“And Mike,” continued the captain, “will be Neptune’s daughter.”

A shout went up, in which Mike himself joined.

Mike O’Rourke was an able seaman of no great size, black-eyed, nimble, and gifted with a tongue which had got him into difficulties from Liverpool to Valparaiso and generally got him out again. He bowed low.

“I always suspected there was royal blood in me, sir.”

“You’ll need a costume.”

“A bit of canvas and some rope-yarn—”

“No, no,” said the captain. “We must do this properly. Neptune may look as if he came aboard through the hawse-pipe, but his daughter must support the dignity of the court.”

He glanced toward the companionway. Miss Pitkin had gone below an hour before and had not reappeared.

This absence was due to a catastrophe which had thrown the domestic regions of the *Mohawk* into mourning. Alexander, the cat, had that morning succeeded in opening the wire door of Rose’s canary cage. When Rose entered her cabin she found the cage overturned, yellow feathers upon the carpet, and Alexander beneath the berth wearing an expression of such elaborate innocence that guilt was established beyond appeal.

The canary had been named Dick. It had travelled farther than many respectable citizens, sung through two gales, and learned to imitate one note of the bosun’s whistle. Rose had been deeply attached to it. She had not wept—she seldom did so when tears were expected—but had removed the murderer from her cabin by the skin of his neck and retired to the after store-room to search for a tin in which to bury what remained of Dick.

Captain Pitkin knew of the calamity and had expressed proper regret. He also knew that Rose’s cabin was at present unoccupied.

“I think,” he said thoughtfully, “I can arrange the costume.”

The mate looked doubtful.

“Miss Pitkin won’t like any liberties taken with her belongings.”

“My dear Larkin, nobody proposes liberties. Merely a loan. She has an old black skirt she never wears, a red dressing jacket, and a green veil in the upper locker. They’ll do magnificently.”

“Did she say so?”

“She would if asked.”

“But are you going to ask her?”

Captain Pitkin regarded the mate with mild reproach. There are questions which, while perfectly straight-forward, contribute nothing to friendship.

“Rose is upset about the bird,” he said. “It would be unkind to trouble her with details.”

Thus do men enter upon danger while persuading themselves that they are sparing a woman’s feelings.

The articles were obtained by the captain in person. The black skirt was not, as he supposed, old; it had been made in Boston four months before. The red jacket, trimmed with black braid, was cherished by Rose because it had belonged to her mother. The veil was green silk and had cost more than any man aboard, except possibly the steward, would have guessed.

Mike surveyed them with admiration.

“Faith, Captain, I’ll be the handsomest fish ever hooked.”

“You are to be a Mermaid,” said Captain Pitkin. “Try to behave like one.”

“As no man has ever seen a Mermaid at close quarters and returned with his reason, I’ve a wide field before me.”

The preparations occupied the remainder of the forenoon. Fritz made a crown of thin wood, with five points, which he gilded with yellow paint intended for the figurehead. A mop-handle became a trident after three wooden prongs had been nailed to its end. An old piano-cover, once used in the cabin and now condemned to the sail-locker, served as a toga; beneath it Neptune wore sea-boots, blue trousers, and a red flannel undershirt, details which became visible whenever the trade wind took liberties with the royal drapery.

Mike’s costume required greater art. The skirt was fastened too high in front and trailed behind. The red dressing jacket strained across his shoulders. Rope-yarns teased into curls formed a wig, and the green veil floated from a crown made of sennit and decorated with shells, brass washers, and two scarlet feathers taken from the captain’s old penwiper. A wooden hand-mirror, cracked from corner to corner, hung at his waist. In one hand he carried a tar brush; in the other, a marline-spike wrapped in silver paper.

The capstan on the main deck was draped with flags and converted into Neptune’s throne. A wash-tub was filled with water. Another tub, larger and less clean, was set beneath the foreyard. A razor four feet long was cut from a pine board, while soap for the candidates was compounded of flour, slush, soot, molasses, and certain secret ingredients contributed by the cook.

“What’s that last stuff?” asked Mike, as the cook poured something from a bottle.

“Medicine,” said the cook.

“For inward or outward use?”

“Either’ll make a man sorry.”

Meanwhile the three green hands had observed these proceedings with different degrees of uneasiness.

Christian pretended indifference.

“It is only foolishness,” he said, coiling down a brace.

Josef looked at the wash-tub.

“Why they put grease in soap?”

“To make it good,” Christian replied.

Oscar had seen the wooden razor.

“That is not for shaving,” he whispered.

“Of course it is,” said Josef, who found reassurance in contradicting somebody more frightened than himself.

“It is for cutting the head off.”

Christian laughed too loudly.

“No heads are cut. On English ships, perhaps one ear. That is all.”

Oscar turned pale.

The mate, passing at that instant, heard the last sentence and might have corrected it. Unfortunately he had a sailor’s belief that apprehension, if properly cultivated, adds flavor to a practical joke.

“You men had better keep out of mischief,” he said sternly. “Neptune’s laws are severe.”

“What laws, sir?” Oscar asked.

“You’ll hear them soon enough.”

He went aft with the grave satisfaction of one who has sown a useful crop.

At two bells in the afternoon watch, a lookout stationed on the foretopmast crosstrees hailed the deck.

“Sail—or something—on the weather bow!”

“What does she look like?” called the captain.

“Looks like a barrel with whiskers, sir!”

“Then it is His Majesty. Stand by to receive him.”

The crew gathered on the main deck, grinning with that obedient solemnity by which sailors improve an absurdity. Christian, Josef, and Oscar had been ordered to stand near the fife-rail. Oscar placed himself behind the other two, an arrangement which comforted him without concealing so much as one elbow.

From forward came the beating of a tin pan, the piping of a whistle, and a deep, uncertain voice:

“Make ways for Neptune, King of the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans, Lord of the Doldrums, Admiral of the Equator, and—and—”

Fritz had forgotten the rest.

“Protector of all them as goes down to the sea in ships,” whispered Mike behind him.

“Protector of all dem vat goes down in ships!” roared Neptune, and appeared from the fo’k’sl.

He was imposing at first sight. At the second, the effect suffered somewhat from the piano-cover becoming entangled about his sea-boots. He recovered himself with a royal scowl and advanced, attended by two minions carrying buckets, a barber with the pine razor, and Mike as the Mermaid Princess.

If Neptune was imposing, his daughter was unforgettable. She came mincing over the deck, the green veil flying horizontally from her head, the black skirt sweeping up rope-ends, and the red jacket brilliant in the sun. She waved the tar brush like a fan, peeped into the cracked mirror, and bestowed smiles upon the crew. Even Mr. Larkin, whose dignity was respected in three ports, turned away to cough.

Neptune mounted the capstan throne. The piano-cover slipped from one shoulder, but Mike replaced it with filial tenderness.

Captain Pitkin removed his cap and bowed.

“Your Majesty does my ship great honor.”

“Captain,” said Neptune, reading from a paper held upside down, “ve have come to inspect dese here alien peoples vot is trying to cross our Line mitout certificates. Produce the landsmen, varlets, lubbers, and unlawful subjects.”

“Your Majesty shall be obeyed.”

Christian, Josef, and Oscar were pushed forward.

Neptune stared at them. They stared back, Christian defiantly, Josef anxiously, and Oscar as a rabbit might regard a committee of foxes.

“Vich is de worst?” Neptune demanded.

“That one, Papa,” said the Mermaid, pointing to Christian. “He has a guilty face.”

Christian was hauled before the throne.

“Name?” thundered Neptune.

“Christian Hansen.”

“Occupation?”

“Sailor.”

At this the court groaned.

“Silence!” cried Mike. “The prisoner is evidently delirious.”

Neptune leaned forward.

“How many times you cross my Equator?”

“Never.”

“Den how you call yourself a sailor?”

“I sail from Portland,” Christian replied cautiously.

“Many things sail from Portland,” said the Mermaid. “Lumber, codfish, and the captain’s bills. They are not sailors.”

Captain Pitkin joined in the laughter until he perceived Rose’s steward near the companionway and abruptly became occupied with the horizon.

Neptune resumed his examination.

“Who dug the Panama Canal?”

Christian hesitated. He knew the canal had not yet been completed, but suspected this might be one of the mysteries learned only by initiated seamen.

“Americans?” he ventured.

“Wrong!” cried Mike. “It was dug by a swordfish with a shovel on his nose.”

The court groaned again.

“Where is the South Pole?” asked Neptune.

“At the south.”

“Evasion! How far under water is the wreck of the Flying Dutchman?”

Christian looked desperately at the captain.

“I do not know.”

“Can you splice the main brace?”

“Yes.”

This was true, but unfortunate.

“Without a needle?”

Christian perceived too late that no answer could assist him.

“Papa,” said the Mermaid, “he is ignorant beyond the common run. I recommend shaving.”

“Shave him!” commanded Neptune.

Christian was seized by four men and seated on an upturned bucket. The cook applied the ceremonial soap with a swab, covering his chin, cheeks, nose, and hair. The mixture smelled of onions, kerosene, and the medicine whose inward or outward virtues had been left undecided.

The barber flourished his wooden razor.

“Keep still,” he advised, “or I may take off more than your whiskers.”

“I have no whiskers,” Christian protested.

“Then we must search for them.”

The razor scraped his face, pushed the soap into his ears, and descended the back of his neck. Mike inspected the result.

“Poor work. I shaved Columbus before he discovered America, and Magellan before he went round the world. Neither complained.”

“They were dead,” said a voice in the crowd.

“Then they knew how to behave.”

Christian’s head was next brushed with tar—not enough to injure him, but enough to give him the appearance of having argued with a chimney. He was tipped backward into the wash-tub, brought out spluttering, and asked whether he now recognized Neptune’s authority.

“No!” he shouted.

This answer, though courageous, was constitutionally unsound. Neptune ordered the extreme penalty.

A bowline was put round Christian’s middle and made fast to a whip from the foreyard-arm. The crew ran away with the fall, and he rose protesting into the brilliant air. At fifteen feet above the deck he ceased protesting and looked down. At twenty feet his courage, which had been doing well on solid planking, departed.

“Enough!” he cried. “I recognize!”

“Too late,” said the Mermaid. “The court has adjourned to the ocean.”

The line was eased. Christian descended outside the rail until his boots touched the sea. Then came a shout, a pull, and a ducking. He vanished in green water, reappeared blowing, and was ducked again. A third immersion washed away the soap, most of the soot, and every remaining doubt concerning Neptune’s practical authority.

When at last he was hauled aboard and dropped in the lee scuppers, he lay there panting, wet as a trawl-net and humble as a converted pirate.

“Next prisoner!” cried Mike.

Josef was dragged forward.

Oscar did not wait to learn whether innocence might be established by evidence. While every eye was on Josef, he slipped behind the foremast, seized the futtock shrouds, and went aloft as if Satan were at his heels—and Satan, in Oscar’s opinion, wore a wooden crown and carried a mop-handle.

The Mermaid saw him first.

“One of the fishes is escaping, Papa!”

The company looked up. Oscar had reached the foretop and was climbing higher, his long arms and legs working with extraordinary speed.

“Come down!” shouted the mate.

“No, sir!”

It was the first direct refusal Oscar had ever made to an officer. Terror is a great republican.

“You’ll come down at once.”

“They kill me!”

“Nobody’s going to kill you.”

“You say Neptune laws severe!”

The mate regretted his earlier agricultural efforts.

Mike put both hands to his mouth.

“Stay where you are, darling! We’ll shave you in the top!”

Oscar went higher.

Captain Pitkin laughed until tears stood in his eyes.

“Leave him, Mr. Larkin. Hunger will fetch him down. Proceed with Josef.”

Josef stood before Neptune with his cap in his hands. His gaze wandered from the tar brush to Christian, who was sitting up in the scuppers and trying to squeeze water from his shirt.

“Name?” demanded Neptune.

“Josef Larsen.”

“Occupation?”

Josef had profited by Christian’s error.

“Not sailor.”

“What are you, then?”

Josef considered. The range of harmless occupations seemed suddenly narrow.

“Cook’s helper?”

The cook uttered an indignant howl.

“He ain’t! I wouldn’t have him in the galley for a gold watch!”

“Spy, perhaps,” said the Mermaid. “Or a pirate.”

“No pirate.”

“Can you prove it?”

Josef shook his head miserably.

“No proof. But no pirate.”

Neptune consulted the upside-down paper.

“How many beans in a five-gallon pot of lobscouse?”

Josef knew lobscouse, but not in terms of beans or arithmetic.

“Many.”

“How many exactly?”

“More than fifty.”

“False!” cried Mike. “There are no beans in lobscouse unless the cook is dishonest.”

The cook looked offended, though he had put beans in the lobscouse that very week.

“What is the Old Man of the Seas?” Neptune continued.

Josef glanced toward Captain Pitkin.

“The captain?”

A roar of laughter swept the deck. Captain Pitkin shook his fist at the prisoner, though he enjoyed the answer more than was proper.

“Contempt of court!” said Mike. “Shave him twice.”

At this point events below deck, which had been proceeding independently and with a far more serious purpose, reached the stage at which they must be related.

Miss Rose Pitkin had found a round cocoa tin for Dick’s remains, but discovered upon opening it that the steward had filled it with nutmeg. She transferred the nutmeg to a jar, washed the tin, lined it with white cloth, and placed therein six yellow feathers and such other melancholy evidence as Alexander had overlooked. During these operations she heard unusual noises overhead—cheering, stamping, and once a splash followed by Christian’s unmistakable cry.

Rose knew of Crossing-the-Line ceremonies. She disapproved of them as disorderly, wasteful of fresh water, and likely to produce torn clothing. Yet she also understood that seamen, like children and certain legislators, must occasionally be allowed noise if one expects quiet afterward. She therefore completed Dick’s modest arrangements before going on deck.

As she passed through the cabin, she noticed that the door of her room stood open.

Rose had closed it. Of that she was certain.

She entered and looked around. One locker had been disturbed. The black skirt was gone. So were the red dressing jacket and the green veil.

Now, had Rose discovered the loss on an ordinary day, she might have begun with questions. But grief for Dick had prepared the powder, the open door supplied the fuse, and from overhead came a voice—high, impudent, and unmistakably male—crying:

“Hold him by the ears, minions! Papa’s subjects must be beautiful!”

Rose’s eyes narrowed.

She mounted the companionway.

The scene which met her gaze would have delighted a painter and horrified a housekeeper. Flags hung from the capstan. Buckets stood everywhere. Christian dripped in the scuppers. Josef sat upon the upturned bucket with half his face coated in black slush, while the barber flourished the pine razor. Neptune, crowned and bearded, ruled from his throne. Above, Oscar clung to the royal stay and prayed in Norwegian.

But Rose saw none of these things first.

She saw her green veil.

It streamed in the trade wind from the head of a rope-haired creature wearing her black skirt and her mother’s red dressing jacket. The creature held a tar brush in one hand and her cracked cabin mirror in the other. It was at that instant bending over Josef with an expression of mermaidly ferocity.

Rose advanced.

The men nearest the companionway became suddenly interested in work. One picked up a rope and coiled it. Another began polishing a belaying-pin with his sleeve. The second mate remembered the horizon.

“Charles,” said Rose.

Captain Pitkin turned.

“Oh, Rose! I thought you were—”

“What is that?”

She pointed at the Mermaid.

Mike straightened. Josef slipped from the bucket and crawled behind the tub.

The captain coughed.

“That is Neptune’s daughter.”

“Neptune’s daughter is wearing my clothes.”

There was a silence so complete that the wash-water could be heard running from Christian into the lee scupper.

Mike attempted a smile.

“Madam,” he said, “appearances—”

“You be quiet.”

Mike obeyed. Quick-wittedness is valuable, but there are occasions when instinct counsels surrender.

Rose looked from him to the men. “Which of you entered my cabin?”

No one answered.

“Mr. Larkin?”

The mate, who would have faced a boarding party with less discomfort, removed his cap.

“I couldn’t say, Miss Pitkin.”

“You could say, but you prefer not to. Very well.”

Her gaze moved to the throne.

“And who is that ridiculous object?”

Fritz rose in alarm. His crown slipped over one eye.

“I am Neptune,” he said, but without the conviction of earlier moments.

“Neptune, indeed!”

Rose stepped forward, seized the mop-handle trident, and struck him smartly across the shin with it. Fritz sat down on the capstan.

“Ow!”

“Take off that piano-cover. It belongs in the cabin.”

“But I am king—”

“You are the carpenter, and the starboard pantry shelf has been loose for a week.”

The authority of mythology collapsed before that of maintenance. Fritz removed his crown.

A murmur, half laughter and half panic, ran through the crew. Rose wheeled upon them.

“Have you all nothing to do? Mr. Larkin, are we sailing this ship or conducting a madhouse?”

“Hands to stations!” bawled the mate with grateful violence. “Clear up this deck! Johnson, take those buckets forward! Barber, put down that fool razor! Look lively!”

Men scattered as if a squall had struck them. The court of Neptune dissolved. Minions became ordinary seamen; courtiers leaped for braces; the royal musician hid his tin pan under the longboat. Christian, not knowing whether he was still a prisoner, crawled toward the fo’k’sl. Josef followed on hands and knees.

Mike alone remained in the center of the deck, encumbered by the skirt.

Rose pointed at him.

“Take off my things.”

“Here, ma’am?”

“Do not be impertinent.”

“I’m not, ma’am. I’m only trying to preserve what small reputation the costume has left me.”

“Come here.”

Mike backed away.

Rose advanced.

He retreated around the capstan. She followed. The captain made one weak effort to interfere.

“Rose, my dear, perhaps if we—”

“Not now, Charles.”

The tone placed him instantly among the spectators.

Mike dodged behind the mainmast. Rose went to one side; he came out the other. She reversed direction. He gathered up the skirt and ran forward, but the trailing hem caught upon a ring-bolt and nearly brought him down. The crew, while laboring with passionate industry, watched from the corners of their eyes.

“Stop!” cried Rose.

Mike glanced over his shoulder. Her face decided him. He sprang into the main shrouds and climbed.

There is something peculiarly undignified in the sight of a Mermaid ascending rigging. Mike’s skirt bunched about his knees; the red jacket caught on a ratline; the green veil streamed below like a distress signal. Nevertheless he reached the main top before Rose came to the shrouds.

She put one foot on the ratlines.

Captain Pitkin hurried forward.

“Rose! You can’t go aloft!”

“Why not?”

“The motion—the height—your dress—”

“My dress,” said Rose, looking upward, “appears already to be there.”

Mike leaned from the top.

“Miss Pitkin, if you’ll promise not to murder me, I’ll come down.”

“I promise nothing.”

“Then I’ll stay where I am.”

“You will not.”

“Respectfully, ma’am, possession of the main top is nine points of the law.”

Rose set her other foot on the shrouds.

“Wait!” Mike cried. “The captain gave them to me!”

Everything stopped again.

Captain Pitkin closed his eyes.

Rose descended to the deck with deliberate care and turned toward her brother.

“Charles?”

The captain looked aft, as if command of the *Mohawk* required his immediate presence on the poop. He even took one step in that direction.

“Charles Pitkin.”

No officer’s rank survives the use of his full name by an elder sister.

He stopped.

“Rose, there is a perfectly simple explanation.”

“There generally is.”

“We needed a costume.”

“So you entered my cabin.”

“I did not exactly enter it in a burglarious spirit.”

“Did you take my skirt?”

“I borrowed it.”

“My red jacket?”

“For a few hours.”

“My green veil?”

“It was indispensable to the general effect.”

Rose regarded him in silence. Captain Pitkin, who had once told a mutinous sailor that one of them would leave the quarter-deck feet foremost, now rubbed the back of his neck and looked approximately twelve years old.

“You went into my room without permission,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You opened my locker.”

“Yes.”

“You gave my clothes to Michael O’Rourke.”

“Technically.”

“Technically?”

“Well—actually.”

From the main top came Mike’s cautious voice:

“If it helps, ma’am, I never asked where they came from.”

“It does not help.”

“No, ma’am.”

Rose held out her hand toward the trident. Fritz, who was still nursing his shin, passed it to her. For one dreadful second Captain Pitkin thought she meant to use it upon him; but she merely leaned the ridiculous emblem against the capstan.

“The black skirt is spoiled,” she said. “The hem is torn, and it has tar on it.”

“I’ll buy another in Buenos Ayres.”

“The veil is stretched.”

“A new veil too.”

“The jacket belonged to Mother.”

Captain Pitkin’s expression changed. His mischief dropped from him, and he looked genuinely ashamed.

“I had forgotten that, Rose. I am sorry. I would not have taken it if I had remembered.”

“You should not have taken it in any event.”

“No. You are right.”

“And you allowed a sailor to wear it while throwing slush and tar about the deck.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

The captain glanced at the remains of his ceremony. The throne was already a capstan again; the tubs were being rolled forward; Neptune, stripped of crown and toga, had become Fritz, and Mike’s bare feet could be seen on the lower edge of the main top. It no longer seemed such a magnificent conception.

“I thought it would be amusing,” he said.

“For whom?”

“For nearly everybody.”

Christian sneezed in the fo’k’sl doorway.

Rose heard him.

“And those men being ducked from the yard-arm—did they find it amusing?”

The captain hesitated.

“They will afterward.”

This, it must be admitted, is the principle on which much traditional amusement has been conducted.

Rose’s anger had not vanished, but its hottest edge was turned by his apology. She had known Charles since he was a red-faced baby with a talent for escaping from cradles. He had borrowed her toys, lost her books, broken her parasol, and once traded her best bonnet-ribbon for a pocketknife. Forty years and the command of a barkentine had altered the scale rather than the nature of his offences.

Still, forgiveness granted too quickly is seldom respected.

“Who keeps your cabin accounts?” she asked.

“You do.”

“Who sees that the steward does not charge twice for stores?”

“You do.”

“Who remembers your clean linen, writes to the owners when you put it off, and finds your spectacles when they are on your head?”

Captain Pitkin touched his forehead involuntarily.

“You do.”

“Who sat up three nights when you had fever off Pernambuco?”

“You did, Rose.”

“And this morning, while I was trying to bury my poor bird, you thought it a suitable time to rummage through my locker.”

The captain winced.

“I behaved badly.”

“You behaved like a boy.”

“Yes.”

“A particularly tiresome boy.”

“Yes.”

Mr. Larkin, directing two men who had been coiling the same rope for five minutes, turned away to conceal a smile.

Rose saw it.

“And the mate knew?”

Captain Pitkin rallied enough to protect a subordinate.

“Mr. Larkin advised against it.”

The mate’s face became a model of virtuous misery.

“I see,” said Rose. “Then there was one sane man aboard, and Charles overruled him.”

The captain sighed.

“Apparently.”

“Very well. You will replace the skirt and veil in Buenos Ayres. You will have the jacket cleaned by someone who understands such work. You will also see that Mr. O’Rourke removes it without tearing the braid.”

“I’ll attend to everything.”

“And there will be no more ducking men from yard-arms.”

The captain glanced toward the fore royal stay, where Oscar remained suspended between fear and hope.

“The ceremony isn’t finished.”

“It is now.”

“But two of them have not been initiated.”

“Then Neptune must endure the disappointment.”

From nearby came a muffled sound which might have been Fritz agreeing.

Captain Pitkin looked at Rose. Rose looked back. The trade wind hummed in the standing rigging, and the *Mohawk* moved steadily southward as if indifferent to the constitutional crisis aboard her.

At last the captain smiled.

“Very well, Rose. Neptune’s court is dissolved.”

“And you are sorry?”

“I am heartily sorry.”

She studied him one moment longer, then relented as completely as she did most things.

“Then I forgive you, Charles.”

He kissed her cheek.

From the main top Mike called, “Does the amnesty extend to the royal family?”

Rose raised her head.

“Come down carefully.”

“Am I to be struck?”

“Not if you obey me.”

Mike descended with great caution. Upon reaching the deck he removed the rope-yarn wig and crown, which reduced his beauty but improved his prospects. Rose led him to the cabin, where the garments were taken off and examined.

The skirt had indeed suffered, though not beyond repair. The veil was wrinkled and smelled faintly of tar. The jacket, by extraordinary good fortune, had escaped everything except sea-air and Mike. Rose folded it over her arm.

“Mr. O’Rourke,” she said, “if you ever enter my cabin without permission, I shall report you to the captain.”

Mike looked toward the chastened commander.

“Couldn’t you punish me yourself, ma’am?”

“I should begin with the captain. He can finish.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“And you looked absurd.”

Mike sighed.

“That wounds me more than discipline.”

Rose almost smiled, but remembered Dick and did not. She went below with her clothes and the cocoa tin.

The deck was restored with the speed peculiar to men whose amusement has been interrupted by authority. The flags were unbent, the tubs emptied, the capstan cleared, and the tar brush returned to legitimate service. Fritz carried the wooden crown to his workshop, where he later used it to patch a box. The pine razor went into the galley fire. The piano-cover was shaken, folded, and placed outside Rose’s door with an unsigned note of apology.

Christian changed into dry clothes. For the rest of the watch he moved carefully, as if uncertain whether the deck might open beneath him. Yet he was proud of having endured the full ceremony and lost no time informing Josef that the third ducking had been “nothing at all.”

“You shouted,” Josef said.

“I shouted to encourage them.”

“You said, ‘Help, I drown.’”

“In Norwegian that means, ‘Continue.’”

Josef, who had escaped with half a shaving, washed his face and gave thanks in two languages. His left eyebrow remained black until the following Thursday, but he regarded this as a small price for deliverance.

Oscar was more difficult.

Though the court had been abolished, he refused to descend from the foremast.

“Neptune is gone,” called the mate.

“He hide.”

“He’s gone back to his shop.”

“Mermaid?”

“Below.”

“Captain promise no shave?”

Captain Pitkin stepped to the rail and looked up.

“I promise.”

Oscar descended one ratline.

“No tar?”

“No tar.”

Another ratline.

“No cut off ear?”

“Who told you anything about cutting off ears?”

Oscar looked toward Christian, who abruptly found work near the windlass.

The captain repeated his assurances. Oscar at last came down, weak in the knees but triumphant in spirit. He passed Rose at the companionway a few minutes later and removed his cap with an earnestness usually reserved for royalty.

“Thank you, Miss,” he said.

“For what?”

“You save my life.”

“Nonsense. Nobody intended to kill you.”

Oscar shook his head. He had seen the wooden razor and knew better.

That afternoon, shortly before six bells, the second mate took an observation and reported that the *Mohawk* had crossed the Equator. Nothing marked the passage except a slight alteration in the figures upon the chart and a cheer from the men when the news went forward. The sea looked the same on the southern side; the trade wind blew as before; no line appeared in the water, though Oscar watched carefully from the forecastle-head.

Captain Pitkin stood beside Rose at the taffrail. She had buried Dick at sunset, committing the cocoa tin to the Atlantic with more ceremony and less noise than had attended Neptune’s court. Alexander the cat was banished from the cabin and spent the evening among the crew, where his crime was considered natural and his exile unjust.

“Rose,” said the captain, “you did rather spoil an old custom.”

“I prevented a riot and saved my jacket.”

“It wasn’t a riot.”

“It would have become one.”

“For all we know, Neptune may take offence.”

“If Neptune wishes to discuss the matter, he may come to me himself. He is not to send Fritz.”

Captain Pitkin laughed.

Below them Christian was describing his initiation for the third time, adding a foot to the height of the yard-arm at every recital. Josef listened with the comfortable superiority of one who had escaped at the last possible instant. Oscar sat near the foremast and regarded Miss Pitkin as a combination of guardian angel, admiral, and armed rescue party.

Thus the three novices represented, as it were, the comparative degrees of thankfulness. Christian was thankful it was over; Josef was more thankful it had been interrupted; Oscar was most thankful that Rose Pitkin existed at all.

As for their standing before the powers of the sea, Christian had been shaved, tarred, washed, hoisted, and ducked, and might therefore call himself a son of Neptune without fear of contradiction. Josef had received only half the mysteries, while Oscar had received none unless climbing for one’s life may be counted as a nautical sacrament. They crossed the Line unlicensed and technically remained lubbers.

But they crossed it dry—or as dry as Christian could manage—and on that particular voyage this was honor enough.

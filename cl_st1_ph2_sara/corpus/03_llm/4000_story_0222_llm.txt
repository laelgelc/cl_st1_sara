### THE RAPID EDUCATION OF ANDY JAFFREY

There are men who collect beetles, men who treasure coins whose chief merit is that no shopkeeper will accept them, and men who, having inherited a competence, expend it in ascertaining whether the Emperor Nero was left-handed; my own weakness—if a civil engineer may confess to one without endangering the public bridges—has always been a taste for odd people. A warped girder alarms me, because its eccentricity is calculable and may prove fatal; but a warped human intelligence, presenting angles unknown to Euclid and deflections not registered by Tredgold, engages my curiosity. I do not believe in apparitions, second sight, prophetic dreams, or any manifestation incapable of being reduced to figures upon ruled paper. Nevertheless, I have sometimes lodged where the stairs creaked without provocation, and have listened with patience to persons who heard voices in the chimney. Thus professional rigidity and private indulgence have lived together in me, like two elderly relations who disapprove of each other but share the same tea.

It was in October of 18—, while superintending the surveys for the Carrick Valley aqueduct, that I encountered Mr. Silas Jaffrey.

My headquarters were at the Black Hart, an inn standing four miles from the nearest village and, as nearly as I could determine, a century and a half behind it. The original house had been built when hospitality implied thick walls, small windows, and the reasonable expectation of siege. Successive landlords had enlarged it by attaching rooms wherever Providence or the ground-plan permitted, so that passages began nowhere, chambers occurred in clusters, and a visitor wishing to descend to the coffee-room was liable to arrive in the hay-loft. The roof had the appearance of having sat down after a long journey. One chimney leaned east, another west, as if the pair had quarrelled; and in a disused wing certain rusty wheels, belts, and wooden drums—the remains of a woollen mill—turned slightly in windy weather, with a melancholy squeal which Mr. Sewell, the landlord, attributed to “dryness in the bearings,” though no oil had corrected it in thirty years.

Behind the inn lay a neglected burial-ground, occupied chiefly by Sewells and Jaffreys, the inscriptions on whose stones recorded a persistent determination to die in February. In front, the coach-road crossed a moor, dipped beneath a plantation of black firs, and disappeared. On damp evenings the empty post-chaise shed resembled an open mouth. The place was picturesque, if one were not obliged to sleep in it.

Mr. Abel Sewell was a square man, constructed, like his inn, for resistance rather than ornament. His red cheeks were divided by a short grey whisker on either side; his eyes were small, active, and distrustful; and when carving he attacked a joint with the concentration of Benvenuto Cellini engraving a pope’s button. He had a special animosity toward vegetables. I have seen him poniard a parsnip merely because it slipped from the fork.

The other permanent boarder was Mr. Jaffrey.

He was then, I judged, seventy years old, though his figure had retained a certain juvenile lightness, as if Time had wrinkled him but forgotten to add the customary ballast. He was tall, narrow-shouldered, and very neat. His black coat, carefully brushed to an ashen gloss, buttoned high upon his breast; his shoes shone; and his white hair stood out over his temples in two luminous bands, giving him, when he sat below a lamp, the effect of a theatre whose footlights had been placed above the stage. His face was finely cut and sensitive. The nose suggested decision; the mouth contradicted it.

At dinner on the first day he spoke little, but glanced at me often with an expression in which curiosity struggled with a ceremonious reserve. Mr. Sewell, meanwhile, questioned me concerning the aqueduct.

“How much water?” he asked.

“Eleven million gallons daily, at the ordinary flow.”

He arrested a potato midway to his plate.

“Eleven million!”

“Approximately.”

“What’ll folks do with it?”

“Drink some of it, I presume.”

“Can’t drink all that.”

“Not at once.”

He considered me for a moment, plainly suspecting official satire, and then resumed his meal.

When I explained that the principal conduit would pass through granite, Mr. Jaffrey brightened.

“Granite!” he exclaimed. “Ah, then you are among the eternal verities, sir. A blessed profession. You put your question to the hill with powder, and the hill answers honestly.”

“Not invariably,” I said. “Hills have their evasions.”

“Like women,” observed Mr. Sewell.

Mr. Jaffrey’s knife fell against his plate.

It was no more than a faint sound, yet I noticed it. His colour changed, and for the remainder of dinner he regarded the fire with an abstraction which Mr. Sewell contemplated sourly. That night, before retiring, I asked the landlord who his other guest was.

“Silas Jaffrey,” he replied.

“So I understood.”

“Born here. Went away clerk to a shipping-house. Came back with money. Not much. Enough.”

“Has he no family?”

Mr. Sewell blew out one of the candles with unnecessary violence.

“No.”

There are monosyllables that close a subject; there are others that open a trapdoor beneath it. This “No” belonged to the latter species. I waited.

“Never married,” he added. “Shilly-shallied.”

“In what respect?”

“In the marryin’ respect. There was Mehetabel Bray.”

“And he loved her?”

“S’pose so. Folks called it that.”

“What happened?”

“What generally happens when a man stands on one foot too long. She died.”

He took up the second candle.

“How long ago?”

“Thirty-eight year come March. If you want the whole history, ask him. He’s got enough of it.”

With this discouraging permission Mr. Sewell left me.

During the next few days I saw little of Mr. Jaffrey. My work kept me on the moor from sunrise until dusk, and he made solitary excursions in the opposite direction, returning with burrs on his trousers and now and then a spray of late heath in his buttonhole. At table he was genial but elusive. He knew books, had travelled, and possessed that miscellaneous old-fashioned information which seems at once extensive and useless: he could tell the age of a stained-glass window, quote Dante concerning a fog, or identify the third wife of an obscure German prince, but he had no opinion on the parliamentary question of the hour and had never heard of the new patent valve that was agitating hydraulic circles.

On the fourth evening rain imprisoned us. Mr. Sewell retired early, after declaring that the weather had “got into his bones,” and Mr. Jaffrey and I remained before the coffee-room fire. Wind moved through the unused wing with the irregular tread of some enormous person seeking a misplaced room. The casements shook; the rusty machinery complained; and twice a loose shutter struck the wall with a noise like a pistol fired in a cupboard.

Mr. Jaffrey sat silent, following the collapse of a coal into flame. Presently he said:

“You have not married, Mr. Alden?”

I admitted the fact.

“But perhaps,” he continued, “there is an understanding?”

“There is not even a misunderstanding.”

He smiled politely, but his eyes remained grave.

“A man should be cautious,” he said. “Yet there is a caution which is only cowardice dressed for church. One discovers the distinction too late.”

This appeared to me an opening deliberately contrived. I mentioned Mehetabel Bray.

He started, though not resentfully.

“Sewell has been talking.”

“A syllable or two.”

“He measures biography by the syllable. Poor Mehetabel! She deserved a more musical historian.”

There followed a silence, after which Mr. Jaffrey began to speak of her. At first his reminiscences were fragmentary: a ribbon she had worn at a harvest supper, the exact turn of her head when listening, her habit of touching flowers with one finger before gathering them. Then the fragments arranged themselves into a portrait. Mehetabel had been neither beautiful nor accomplished according to severe standards, but in his account she acquired a radiance independent of features. She laughed readily, forgave before being asked, and could make an ordinary room seem newly furnished by entering it. Her one defect, he said, was an absurd respect for his judgment.

“She waited for me to speak,” he continued. “I waited until I could offer her a suitable establishment. A suitable establishment! Observe the magnificence of the phrase. I was earning eighty pounds a year and believed happiness demanded mahogany. When I had one hundred, I required one hundred and fifty. At one hundred and fifty I discovered that a wife must have a carriage. Meanwhile Mehetabel wanted only me—which proves that the dear girl’s intelligence, though amiable, was not perspicacious.”

He laughed softly. The laughter failed.

“She died of a fever while I was in Liverpool. I had written her a letter at last. It was in my pocket when I came home.”

He drew his hand across his breast, as if the letter were still there.

A practical man resists sentiment partly because he fears its power and partly because it has no specified limit. I rose, adjusted the curtain, and remarked that the window admitted a draught.

“Yes,” said Mr. Jaffrey. “She always disliked a draught.”

He, too, rose and lowered the shade a few inches.

“That is better. The child is asleep.”

I turned.

“What child?”

He looked toward the vacant sofa beside the window, then at me. For a second his face expressed surprise at my ignorance.

“Our boy,” he said. “Mehetabel’s and mine.”

“A boy!”

“Born at six this morning.”

He made the announcement in a low, triumphant voice. His eyes shone; his cheeks had taken on a delicate colour. Whatever answer common sense demanded, civility forbade. I had not previously assisted at a supposititious birth, the business being not strictly in my line; but one must meet social emergencies with what instruments one has.

“I trust,” I said, “that mother and child are doing well.”

“Admirably! Mehetabel is stronger than she appears. The little fellow weighs nine pounds. Sewell says eight, but Sewell always underestimates whatever he cannot eat.”

“And his name?”

“Andrew. Her father was Andrew. We shall call him Andy.”

He moved toward the sofa, bent with infinite tenderness, and appeared to arrange an invisible covering. The gesture was so natural that I looked involuntarily for the cradle.

“Do not speak loudly,” he whispered. “It took us an hour to settle him.”

I went upstairs considerably disturbed.

In the morning Mr. Jaffrey was absent from breakfast, and I congratulated myself that the incident had been a transient exaltation produced by memory, rain, and the defective ventilation of the coffee-room. Yet when I returned that evening, soaked and weary, he met me in the passage.

“Got a tooth!” he cried.

“Who has?”

“Andy.”

The infant, then, had made extraordinary progress between Tuesday night and Wednesday evening. I followed Mr. Jaffrey into the parlour and found him in excellent spirits. Andy, it appeared, was ten months old, could crawl with alarming celerity, had broken a blue china cup, and manifested a regrettable inclination to eat coal.

“Mehetabel says he gets it from me,” said Mr. Jaffrey. “But I deny that I ever ate coal. As a child I swallowed a brass button, which is quite another thing.”

I ought perhaps to have remonstrated; but with whom? The boundary between humoring a fancy and strengthening it is easily perceived by moralists who are not in the room. Before me stood a lonely old man, transfigured by happiness, asking no service except that I recognize a baby who occupied neither space nor nourishment. Had Andy required a legal guardian or a place in the census, I should have declined. A tooth seemed harmless.

I asked whether he had suffered much.

“Not he! Roared, of course. Has lungs like a boatswain. You should have heard him at three o’clock this morning.”

In fact, I had heard nothing but the machinery in the west wing; nevertheless, I nodded with the air of one whose sleep has been delicately sacrificed to infancy.

Thereafter the domestic history advanced at a rate to bewilder chronology. Each evening on my return from the survey I received a fresh instalment. Andy walked on Thursday, talked on Friday, and on Saturday fell into the horse-trough, being then nearly three years old. Mr. Jaffrey described the rescue with dramatic minuteness.

“I saw his yellow head under the water. There! Then gone. I plunged in—new coat and all—and seized him by the waistband. Up he came, sputtering like a little Triton. Mehetabel was struck all of a heap.”

That last phrase, borrowed from Mr. Sewell, sat oddly upon his cultivated tongue.

“Was he injured?”

“Not in the least. Demanded a biscuit within five minutes.”

Mr. Sewell, who was sharpening a carving-knife at the sideboard, turned and bestowed upon me a glance of profound hostility.

It said, as plainly as physiognomy can speak: You are encouraging him.

I replied by looking at the knife.

At dinner Andy’s immersion dominated the conversation. Mr. Jaffrey feared the boy would develop a cough; I suggested dry stockings; and Sewell dismembered a fowl with vindictive precision. When Mr. Jaffrey left the table to seek a volume in which, he said, there was an excellent remedy for chills, the landlord leaned toward me.

“Better let it be.”

“What?”

“You know.”

“I know very little.”

“You’ll know more’n you want.”

He cut himself a wedge of cheese.

“Is this condition habitual?” I asked.

“Condition’s a long word.”

“It saves several short ones.”

“Then save ’em. I don’t meddle.”

Having delivered this counsel, he would say no more.

By Sunday Andy was five and had begun school. His education caused us considerable anxiety. He showed an aptitude for geography, a distaste for spelling, and a genius for removing objects from the pockets of his companions. Mr. Jaffrey insisted that this last propensity was scientific curiosity.

“The child desires to know how many things a pocket will contain. Archimedes would have understood him.”

“The owners of the pockets may not,” I said.

“Quite. There is the social difficulty.”

On Monday the boy struck a classmate named Pritchard with a slate. The circumstances were obscure, though Mr. Jaffrey maintained that Pritchard had insulted Mehetabel.

“What did he say?”

“Andy refuses to repeat it.”

“That is honourable.”

“Or convenient,” said Mr. Jaffrey, with parental candour. “I fear he is not wholly truthful.”

Here was the first shadow upon our child. Until then Andy had been merely active, and activity in a golden-haired boy may cover a multitude of broken crockery. Falsehood was graver. Mr. Jaffrey paced the room, debating punishment.

“A whipping,” he said at length. “Nothing brutal, you understand. Three strokes.”

“Two might suffice.”

“Three,” he repeated sternly. “A lie is a lie.”

He disappeared upstairs. After an interval he returned, pale and agitated.

“Well?” I asked.

“Mehetabel interfered.”

“Ah.”

“She says he has a delicate nervous organization.”

“Most children develop one when a cane is mentioned.”

Mr. Jaffrey looked at me sharply; then his mouth twitched.

“You have the making of a father, Alden.”

“I had supposed the qualifications different.”

The punishment was commuted to confinement without pudding. Ten minutes later, according to a report brought by Mr. Jaffrey, the prisoner escaped through the pantry window and spent the afternoon in a pear-tree.

It is unnecessary, dear reader, that I should set down every measles, torn jacket, disputed lesson, and nocturnal stomach-ache of Andy Jaffrey’s accelerated enfance. Real children provide enough of these details; imagination need not add to the public burden. Yet certain episodes remain fixed in my memory, because by degrees the boy acquired for me a species of reality, not the reality of flesh—which I continued stoutly to deny him—but that secondary existence enjoyed by Don Quixote, Hamlet, and those distant cousins whose exploits are repeated at family dinners. I knew what Andy would say. I could foresee his mischief. When Mr. Jaffrey entered with a troubled brow, I thought at once: He has been in the orchard again.

My professional reason protested. I answered it that language is merely a convention. If two men speak of a third who does not exist, but concerning whom they agree, the grammatical form of existence is unavoidable. One cannot continually say, “Your hypothetically conceived boy has hypothetically torn his hypothetical trousers.” Conversation would perish under the apparatus.

Nor was Mr. Jaffrey always deluded in the vulgar sense. At moments he seemed aware that Andy was the child of a life which had never happened. Once, when the boy was seven, I found the old man alone in the twilight.

“He has Mehetabel’s eyes,” he said.

He was silent for a while.

“I do not remember her eyes clearly, Alden. Is it not strange? For thirty-eight years I have said they were grey; tonight I think they may have been hazel. If I forget them, what right have I to give them to the boy?”

I could not answer.

Then a log shifted, sparks ascended the chimney, and he exclaimed:

“Listen to that young rascal on the stairs!”

There had been a light running sound overhead. I attributed it to rain, rats, or contraction of timber—the three domestic Fates of an old inn. Mr. Jaffrey hurried to the door and called, with unmistakable pleasure:

“Andy! No sliding down the banister!”

The sound ceased.

I mention this not as supernatural evidence, for it proves nothing except that noises cease. Still, on the following night I heard footsteps pass my chamber, pause, and run away. When I opened the door, the passage was empty; at its farther end a little window showed the moon above the graveyard. I retired, annoyed less by the occurrence than by my own impulse to call after the boy.

The weather appeared to influence Mr. Jaffrey as pressure influences a barometer. In sunlight he gave Andy prizes, prospects, and an honourable career. Under low clouds he discovered constitutional weaknesses, bad companions, and hereditary melancholy. A clear morning destined the child for Parliament; fog reduced him to emigration. When the wind stood west, Andy was to become an engineer like myself.

“He has a turn for mechanism,” Mr. Jaffrey explained. “Yesterday he took Sewell’s clock to pieces.”

“Can he restore it?”

“He believes so.”

“Then he has not yet acquired the first principle of engineering.”

“And what is that?”

“To distrust any structure one has not oneself designed.”

On Tuesday, Andy being nine, he entered the little workroom adjoining the disused mill and set in motion a wooden drum. The resulting uproar brought Sewell, the ostler, two maids, Mr. Jaffrey, and myself to the spot. We found the great wheel revolving slowly, though no one stood near it. A belt struck the wall with a succession of flat reports. Dust filled the air; a family of mice evacuated the premises; and Sewell swore with the comprehensiveness of Richard III on Bosworth Field.

“The wind started it,” I said.

“No wind in here,” he snapped.

“The machinery retains momentum.”

“Thirty year?”

“Rust has eccentric habits.”

Mr. Jaffrey, meanwhile, called into the shadows:

“Come out, Andy. No one is angry.”

“Speak for yourself,” said Sewell.

No child appeared. Presently the wheel stopped. Sewell locked the room and put the key in his pocket.

“That boy,” said Mr. Jaffrey, when we were alone, “will either be a great inventor or be hanged.”

I admitted that many juvenile careers hover between those alternatives.

The next evening Andy was ten. He had developed a passion for reading at the expense of arithmetic and was engaged upon a romance concerning pirates. Mr. Jaffrey deplored the choice.

“Adventure unsettles a boy. He asked Mehetabel whether there were cannibals in the West Indies.”

“What did she tell him?”

“That the supply had diminished.”

“Accurate enough.”

“He proposes to go to sea.”

“He will change his mind.”

“You think so?”

“Before dinner, very likely.”

Mr. Jaffrey seemed relieved. We discussed the proper education of boys, a subject upon which two bachelors may speak with authority because they have no facts to confuse them. I advocated mathematics, cold baths, and moderate pocket-money. He preferred classics, kindness, and a pony. We compromised upon Latin and a donkey.

These absurd consultations drew me nearer to him than I understood at the time. His fantasy was not merely the caprice of an unbalanced mind; it was an entire household rescued from annihilation. Mehetabel moved invisibly through it, forever young, forever patient. Her sewing lay on the table; her voice moderated punishments; her hand closed the window against the rain. Andy was the visible centre of the invisible circle—the proof that hesitation had not conquered love, that the fatal letter had arrived in time, that somewhere the mahogany establishment had been dispensed with and happiness admitted upon its own modest recognizance.

I ceased to study Mr. Jaffrey as a specimen. There is cruelty in analysis when a man’s wound is open.

Our intimacy did not escape Sewell. He watched us with increasing disfavour, serving Mr. Jaffrey’s imaginary domestic announcements as if they were underdone chops for which I was personally responsible. Once, when I asked whether Andy might have an apple, Sewell set down the dish so violently that three apples rolled to the floor.

“There,” he said. “Let him eat them all.”

“Three would be excessive.”

“Won’t hurt him.”

“You appear confident.”

“Know what I know.”

“What do you know?”

He thrust his hands into his pockets.

“Boy’s getting on.”

“Rapidly.”

“Ten, ain’t he?”

“Nearly eleven.”

At this Mr. Sewell’s expression altered. He looked first at Mr. Jaffrey, who was arranging walnuts into little heaps, and then at me. For an instant I thought I detected pity; but the emotion vanished behind his habitual blankness.

“Jest at that point,” he muttered.

“What point?”

“Never mind.”

He left the room.

I should have questioned him further had not the affairs of the aqueduct demanded my attention. A fissure discovered in the granite required a modification of the line, and for two days I remained at the cutting until late. During this interval autumn completed its preparations for violence. Clouds gathered behind the hills; birds flew low; the moor grass flattened in long shivering waves. My instruments registered a remarkable fall of pressure. On the afternoon of the second day the men refused to continue blasting, and I dismissed them.

The storm overtook me halfway to the inn.

Rain did not descend; it travelled horizontally, with the speed and personal animosity of gravel discharged from a shovel. The fir plantation roared. Small branches crossed the road, followed by larger ones; and once, in the white glare of lightning, I saw an uprooted tree leaning upon its neighbours like a murdered giant prevented from falling by the crowd.

The Black Hart showed one red window through the tumult. Its sign had torn loose at one corner and swung above the door with a shriek at every gust. Within, the passages were dark. A maid ran past me carrying candles; somewhere glass broke; the old machinery began its lamentation, wheel answering belt, and pulley groaning to pulley like a congregation of thin family ghosts who kept themselves warm by passing one another through a mangle.

I found Mr. Jaffrey in the coffee-room.

He stood at the window, one hand gripping the curtain. His hair was disordered; his coat hung open; and his face, when he turned, had the grey fixity of stone.

“Where is Andy?” he demanded.

The question struck me with an absurd terror.

“I have only this moment come in.”

“He went to the mill-room.”

“But Sewell locked it.”

“He found the key.”

Lightning filled the room. In the ensuing darkness something struck the outer wall, and the house shook.

Mr. Jaffrey seized my arm.

“The ladder!” he cried. “I told them to take it down. It was left against the loft. He has climbed it.”

Before I could detain him he rushed into the passage. I followed, taking a candle which the draught instantly extinguished. We stumbled through the western wing, guided by flashes from broken panes. The locked mill-room door stood open. Sewell was there with a lantern, shouting to the ostler to secure a shutter.

“What’s happened?” I called.

“Nothin’.”

“Andy’s on the ladder!” gasped Mr. Jaffrey.

Sewell stopped. His face hardened into an expression almost brutal.

“No, he ain’t.”

“He is! I heard him.”

A crash sounded above us—a sliding, splintering fall, ending in one dull blow upon the floor of the loft.

Mr. Jaffrey screamed.

It was not the cry of an old man. It was the naked voice of a father.

He sprang toward the stairs. Sewell caught him, but I pushed past them with the lantern. At the top, beneath a hole in the roof, lay the long ladder, broken across the middle. Rain streamed over the boards. No child was there.

I knew no child could be there.

Nevertheless, I searched.

I looked behind the drums, beneath a heap of sacking, along the narrow gallery, even inside a disused grain chest whose lid resisted my hand. The lantern threw grotesque shadows; the storm thrust wet fingers through the roof; and every object seemed to conceal the small body I dreaded to find.

At last I descended.

Mr. Jaffrey sat on the bottom stair. Sewell stood beside him, lantern lowered. Neither spoke. As I approached, the old man lifted his face.

“He’s gone,” he said.

I knelt, though there was no practical reason to kneel.

“He may not be badly hurt.”

“Broken his neck.”

“You did not see him.”

“I heard Mehetabel.”

The name was scarcely audible.

He pressed both hands to his eyes. His body bent, not theatrically, but with the slow collapse of a structure whose hidden supports have yielded. I put an arm about him. He did not appear conscious of me.

“He was eleven today,” he whispered. “Only eleven.”

Sewell turned away.

We brought Mr. Jaffrey to his room. He refused a physician, water, wine, and all those material remedies which the living offer in the presence of an immaterial grief. He sat beside the empty bed, staring at a small hollow his hands had made in the counterpane. Once he bent over it as if smoothing yellow hair from a forehead.

“Mehetabel,” he murmured, “I ought to have taken away the ladder.”

I remained with him until after midnight. The storm diminished by degrees, grumbling beyond the hills; rain tapped softly at the panes; and the exhausted inn settled into silence. Mr. Jaffrey seemed at last to sleep in his chair.

I went to my room and did not sleep.

Reason resumed its authority and found the establishment in disorder. There had been no Mehetabel since her death, no marriage, no child, no ladder climbed by childish feet. The ladder had fallen because the roof-beam failed under wind pressure—a straightforward mechanical sequence. Yet I mourned. I mourned not merely for the old man but for Andy himself: the impossible infant who ate coal, the schoolboy who assaulted Pritchard with a slate, the adventurer, the mechanic, the prospective engineer. A phantom had broken his neck, and I grieved for the poor little phantom.

At dawn the storm had vanished. The sky was rinsed blue; sunlight lay on the wet moor; and every twig carried a trembling point of fire. I descended late, expecting a sombre breakfast.

Mr. Jaffrey was already at the table.

He wore a sprig of heath in his buttonhole and was spreading marmalade upon toast. His hair had recovered its symmetrical illumination. He greeted me cheerfully.

“A magnificent morning, Alden! The hills look washed and put back.”

I stared at him.

“How are you?”

“Capital. Slept like a bishop.”

He discussed the weather, the fallen tree, and the probable effect of rain upon my excavations. Of Andy he said nothing. Once I intentionally mentioned the broken ladder. He remarked that old timber should never be trusted and requested another cup of coffee.

This serenity was more appalling than his grief. I wondered whether the fantasy had collapsed completely, leaving no recollection, or whether some deeper chamber of his mind had closed over the catastrophe. I dared not force the door.

My work at Carrick was finished that morning. At eleven the hired dogcart arrived to take me to the railway. Mr. Jaffrey accompanied me to the entrance and shook my hand warmly.

“You must come again,” he said. “We have had pleasant talks.”

“Very pleasant.”

“A bachelor’s inn is dull, but one makes out.”

He looked toward the sunny road, smiling with an expression of almost youthful expectancy.

“Good-bye, Alden.”

“Good-bye, Mr. Jaffrey.”

As the ostler strapped my case behind the cart, Sewell emerged from the doorway, wiping his hands on his apron.

“Well,” he said, “you seen it.”

“Seen what?”

“The whole business.”

I glanced toward the house. Mr. Jaffrey had disappeared.

“Sewell,” I said, “that man requires care.”

“Gets it.”

“He has suffered a terrible shock.”

“Has he?”

“You were present.”

Sewell examined the buckle on my case and tightened it one hole.

“Andy is dead,” I said, ashamed of the words even as I uttered them.

“Andy!” He gave a short laugh without amusement. “There ain’t any Andy.”

“I am aware of the biological objection.”

“Then don’t look as if you’d been to his funeral.”

“What happened last night?”

“Same thing.”

“The same as what?”

“Same as always.”

He jerked the strap, satisfied it would hold, and turned to me.

“Silas gets hold of somebody new. Traveller, boarder, painter feller, young parson—anybody that’ll listen. First it’s Mehetabel. Then they’re married. Then comes the boy. Born one night, teeth next, school after that. Grows a year a day, more or less. Always yellow-headed. Always gets into my mill-room. Always eleven.”

I felt a chill unrelated to the morning air.

“And then?”

“Ladder.”

“He dies?”

“Breaks his neck. Every time.”

“How often has this occurred?”

Sewell counted mentally, moving his lips.

“Couldn’t say. Fifteen. Twenty. Started long ago. Sometimes there’s no likely person here, and Silas keeps quiet for months. Then somebody comes along with a soft eye and too much education.”

He looked directly at me.

“And afterward he forgets?”

“Wipes clean.”

“Completely?”

“Till next time.”

The driver gathered the reins. I climbed into the cart, then turned back.

“Why did you not warn me?”

“Did.”

“You said nothing intelligible.”

“Said let it be.”

“That was hardly sufficient.”

“Wouldn’t have made any odds. Folks like you always think you’ll manage him different.”

The horse started. Sewell walked beside the wheel for two or three paces.

“One question,” I said. “Does the boy always have the same name?”

Sewell stopped in the road.

“Always Andy.”

The cart rolled on. At the bend I looked back. The Black Hart stood bright and innocent under the cleansed sky; smoke rose straight from its leaning chimneys; and Mr. Jaffrey was visible at an upper window, gazing after us. He lifted his hand.

For an instant I imagined a smaller hand beside it.

Then the road dipped, and the inn disappeared.

Three years later, business took me through Carrick village. I did not visit the Black Hart, but at the station I encountered a commercial traveller who had spent the previous night there. He complained of the beds, praised the ale, and mentioned an elderly gentleman of remarkable conversational gifts.

“Jaffrey?” I asked.

“That’s the name. Queer old bird, but pleasant. Had a romantic disappointment, it seems. Woman named Mehetabel.”

I said nothing.

The traveller adjusted his samples and smiled.

“Odd thing, though. Just before I left, he told me they’d been married after all. Long ago, of course.”

“Indeed?”

“Yes. And”—the man lowered his voice, delighted with his news—“there’s a child.”

“A child?”

“A boy. Born at six this morning.”

The train entered the station before I could reply. Steam obscured the platform, iron shrieked upon iron, and the traveller vanished into a carriage with his samples. I stood amid the vapour thinking of the old inn, the invisible cradle, and Mr. Jaffrey bending once more to arrange its coverings.

Somewhere beyond the moor, Andy had begun again.

Got a tooth, by now.

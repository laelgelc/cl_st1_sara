## The Milk of Les Rouvres

Long after the lamps of the inn had been extinguished, the big man lay awake, listening to the delicate noises of an ancient house settling itself for the night. Boards whispered beneath the carpets; somewhere in the hollow walls a mouse pursued its furtive domestic business; from the courtyard rose at intervals the soft clink of a loosened shutter-ring. These sounds were ordinary enough, and he was not of a temperament to embroider them with mysteries. Yet behind them, like a darker pattern showing through faded cloth, persisted the memory of the girl at the farmhouse—and of Morton drinking the milk while she watched.

Morton slept in the other bed, or appeared to sleep. Moonlight, admitted through curtains insufficiently drawn, lay across his face and gave it the smooth pallor of wax. His breathing was shallow. Once he moved his lips, and the big man, raising himself upon an elbow, fancied that he heard a word; but it was no more than an exhalation, and presently the room resumed its guarded silence.

“Rubbish!” he murmured, and turned his face to the wall.

He was fifty-seven, broad in shoulder and sound in heart, a man accustomed to cities, committees, timetables and demonstrable causes. Morton, some nine years his senior, possessed the more fluid imagination. He could be delighted by a legend, disquieted by a dream, or held motionless for ten minutes by the colour of evening upon a hillside. Their friendship, already old, had survived precisely because each supplied what the other lacked. The big man steadied Morton; Morton enlarged the world for the big man. That afternoon, however, enlargement had seemed dangerously akin to distortion.

It had been near five o’clock when they entered the remote valley of Les Rouvres.

Their path, descending from a limestone shoulder of the Jura, wound among beech woods whose leaves already showed the first tarnish of autumn. On the heights, the sun still poured a pale gold upon the bare ridges; below, long blue shadows filled the folds of the country, and vineyards climbed the slopes in orderly terraces before surrendering to forest. No smoke marked the valley. No bell sounded from the distant village. Even the wind, lively all day among the upper crags, dropped suddenly, almost as though it, too, had paused to contemplate the quiet beauty of the place.

Morton was tired. He had denied it twice, which made the matter certain.

“We have three miles yet,” said the big man. “And the last mile downhill. Come, old chap.”

“Three miles on your map,” Morton replied. “Maps are drawn by young men at desks.”

He smiled, but his face had a grey look about the mouth. The day had been warmer than expected, and they had eaten little since morning. When, therefore, a roof appeared below them among the trees, the big man felt relief rather than curiosity. It was a steep, dark roof, patched with moss and bowed under age. A rough meadow opened around it, enclosed by broken stone walls, and behind stood a rank of firs so densely grown that they seemed to hold the coming night within their folds.

“A farm,” Morton said. “There will be water, at least.”

The path to it was nearly lost under nettles. They crossed a little bridge above a dry channel and came upon a house more ruinous than it had appeared from the hill. The plaster had fallen in broad scales, exposing black beams beneath; one shutter hung by a single hinge; the trough beside the door was choked with leaves. Yet from the chimney rose one slender thread of smoke, exquisitely blue against the green darkness of the firs.

The big man knocked.

No answer came.

He knocked again, more loudly, and heard movement within—not steps exactly, but a faint dragging sound, followed by silence. Then the door opened.

The girl stood in the aperture.

Her age was difficult to determine. At first glance she seemed little more than twenty; at the next, something in the repose of her features suggested a maturity not of years but of long and passionless observation. She was slender, barefooted, and dressed in a coarse grey gown of a fashion that the big man vaguely judged provincial. Her hair, very black and abundant, was drawn away from her forehead and fell in two heavy braids. Against the dusk of the interior her skin possessed an almost luminous whiteness.

She did not speak.

“Good evening,” said the big man in French. “We are walking to Saint-Aubin. My friend is fatigued. Might we trouble you for water?”

Her eyes moved from him to Morton and remained there. They were dark eyes, but not dull; a peculiar light stirred far within them, as in deep water when the sun catches some hidden movement below.

Morton removed his hat.

“Mademoiselle,” he said with that old-fashioned courtesy which came naturally to him, “you would confer a charity.”

Still without a word, she stepped backward and motioned them inside.

The room was low, clean in patches, and very cold. A fire burned on the wide hearth, yet it seemed to give neither warmth nor cheerful light. Dried herbs hung from the rafters. An oak table occupied the centre; upon it stood a brown earthen pitcher, three bowls, and a knife whose blade had been polished almost white by use. There was no clock. The silence, relieved only by the small consumption of the fire, had an extraordinary density.

The girl indicated a bench. Morton sat heavily.

She went through an inner doorway and returned carrying a stone jug beaded with moisture. From this she poured milk into two bowls. The big man noticed that her hands were very fine, the fingers narrow and colourless, though one might have expected coarser hands in such a place. As she set the first bowl before Morton, one finger touched his wrist.

He started.

“Cold,” he said, with a little laugh. “Your hands are cold, child.”

She smiled then—not broadly, but enough to show teeth remarkably even and white. The smile altered nothing in her eyes.

The big man drank.

The milk was cool, thick and faintly sweet; beneath the sweetness, however, lurked another taste, bitter and earthy, as though the vessel had lately held bruised roots. He swallowed once, felt nausea rise sharply, and spat the second mouthful into the ashes.

“Good Lord!”

The girl turned her gaze upon him.

“This is bad,” he said. “Sour—or tainted.”

Morton had already emptied half his bowl.

“Not at all,” he replied. “It is excellent.”

“You cannot mean that.”

“Excellent,” Morton repeated, though his voice sounded drowsy.

The girl stood opposite him, watching. There was something in the fixity of her attention that offended the big man more than open impertinence would have done. It was not admiration, nor shyness, nor the innocent wonder of a country girl before strangers. It was absorption—complete and hungry—as though Morton’s drinking fulfilled some intention known only to herself.

The big man took the bowl from his companion’s hands.

“That is enough. We must go.”

For an instant the girl’s expression changed. Her lips parted; a small pulse moved in the white hollow of her throat. Then Morton, with unexpected force, reclaimed the bowl and drank what remained.

“Don’t bully me, old man.”

The words were lightly spoken, yet anger glimmered beneath them. The big man stared. In forty years of acquaintance Morton had never addressed him in quite that tone.

They left money on the table. The girl ignored it. She followed them to the door and stood with one hand upon the warped timber while they crossed the meadow. At the broken wall Morton turned. She raised her hand, palm outward, in a gesture that seemed at once farewell and summons.

He waved back.

“Pretty creature,” he said.

“Queer creature.”

“Beautiful.”

“Perhaps. The milk was abominable.”

Morton made no answer. All the way down the valley he lagged behind, turning repeatedly to look back. At first the roof remained visible above the meadow; then the woods intervened, and there was only the steep path beneath their feet and the late sun kindling the limestone ridge. Yet Morton continued to glance over his shoulder.

“What is it?” the big man asked at last.

“She is there.”

“Where?”

“Among the trees.”

The big man looked. Beech trunks, pallid and smooth, stood in the green twilight. Ferns stirred beside the path, though the air was still. Nothing more.

“No one is there.”

“She has followed us from the farm.”

“Nonsense. We should have heard her.”

“Would we?”

The big man laughed, but the laugh fell without resonance. “My dear fellow, you are overtired. That stuff has upset you.”

Morton stopped. His pupils appeared enlarged, and perspiration shone upon his forehead.

“I can smell it still.”

“The milk?”

“No. Her hair.”

This was so unlike him that the big man felt a sudden inward contraction. He took Morton’s arm and urged him onward. Presently the path widened, vineyards returned, and the first roofs of Saint-Aubin appeared below. With ordinary habitations in sight, his concern diminished. Fatigue, hunger, a draught of bad milk—these were causes sufficient to account for much. The mind, once disturbed, finds agents everywhere; and evening among lonely woods is an industrious conspirator.

But at the final bend Morton stopped again.

Behind them, perhaps thirty yards up the path, stood the girl.

No sound had announced her. She was simply there, framed between two white trunks, her grey dress motionless, her bare feet hidden in fern. The light had nearly gone, but her face remained visible. She looked only at Morton.

The big man called out sharply.

At once she moved aside. There was no haste, no startled recoil; she seemed rather to withdraw into the forest by a single gliding motion. A beech trunk passed before her, and she vanished.

They found the inn twenty minutes later.

At supper Morton ate nothing. He drank two glasses of red wine, complained that the room was hot, and asked the host whether a family still lived at the farm above the dry stream.

“What farm, monsieur?”

“The old place in the fir wood,” Morton said. “A mile above the bridge.”

The host considered, then shook his head. “There are shepherds’ huts on the upper slopes, but no farm in that direction.”

“We were inside it this afternoon,” said the big man impatiently.

The host smiled with the politeness reserved for foreigners who have mistaken roads. “Then monsieur means the Vautrin farm, beyond Montfaucon. It is not above the bridge, however.”

Morton’s hand tightened about his glass until the knuckles whitened.

“She is outside,” he whispered.

The big man turned. Beyond the window lay the empty village square, a fountain, and the pale façade of the church. Vines stirred against the glass. Nothing stood beneath the linden trees.

He got Morton upstairs and persuaded him to lie down. Within minutes the older man appeared asleep. Now, two hours later, the big man himself remained wakeful, revisiting every circumstance he had resolved to dismiss: the ruined house with smoke rising from its chimney; the cold room; the bitterness under the milk’s sweetness; the girl’s finger upon Morton’s wrist; above all, that silent figure among the trees.

Night ever italicizes such memories. What daylight states plainly, darkness repeats with emphasis.

He did not know how long he had lain with his face to the wall when a slight current of air touched his neck. He rolled over.

Morton’s bed was empty.

The coverlet had been pushed aside. The door stood open by three inches, showing a black wedge of corridor.

He rose at once, pulled on his trousers and boots, and went out. At the head of the stairs he paused. A sound travelled upward from below—a faint creak, then another. Footsteps, perhaps. Or merely the boards answering some change of temperature.

“Morton?”

No reply.

He descended. The front door was unlatched. When he opened it, cold air entered with the odour of vines and damp stone. The square lay flooded with moonlight. On the pavement immediately outside, bare footprints showed in a patch of mud.

Bare footprints.

He bent over them, feeling absurdly that some commonplace detail must correct the impression. They were small and narrow, the toes distinctly marked. Beside them appeared the blurred tread of a man’s boot.

The tracks crossed the square towards the church.

The big man followed.

A white mist had gathered waist-deep along the valley floor, leaving roofs and trees afloat above it. The limestone heights shone beneath the moon with the pallor of bone. No lamp burned in the houses. Even the fountain seemed hushed, its falling thread of water making less sound than the stealthy movement he now heard ahead.

Step—pause—step.

“Morton!” he called again.

The sound ceased.

He passed through the lych-gate into the churchyard. The church stood on his right, black-windowed and massive, its squat tower dividing the sky. Graves sloped away on the left beneath cypresses. Here the smell reached him.

At first he thought it was rain-damp soil. Then he perceived its rawness—the heavy, mineral odour of earth newly opened.

He advanced between the stones. Several graves had been disturbed. Soil lay scattered across the grass; flowers had been torn up; one slab was shifted sideways, revealing beneath it a narrow mouth of darkness. The big man’s scalp tightened.

Something moved near the cemetery wall.

He saw Morton crouching beside a sunken grave, his nightshirt pale beneath the coat he had thrown over it. His head drooped. Behind him bent a second figure, one white hand spread against his brow, drawing it gently backward.

The girl’s black hair hung forward, concealing both their faces.

For several seconds the big man could not move. His reason, though desperately active, produced no command that the body would obey. He saw the curve of her shoulder, the grey cloth of her gown, Morton’s limp hands resting among clods of earth. He heard a small sound—delicate, intimate, almost like a child drinking.

Then she raised her head.

Moonlight touched her face.

Her lips and chin were stained crimson.

The big man shouted and rushed towards them. The girl released Morton, who swayed but did not fall. She straightened slowly. Her eyes shone with a greenish lustre, and upon her mouth was an expression of such profound contentment that disgust overcame fear.

“You devil!”

He struck at her.

His hand met nothing—or, more precisely, met a cold resistance yielding as mist yields, without impact and without substance. She was suddenly beyond his reach. He could not have said that she leapt or ran. One instant she stood before the grave; the next she had passed between two headstones several yards away, her dress trailing without touching the grass.

He pursued her.

She moved towards the church porch, turned once to look at him, and smiled. Blood gleamed upon her teeth. Then she slipped through the half-open door.

The big man followed so close that the iron latch was still moving when he reached it. Inside, the church was nearly dark. Moonlight poured through the east window in coloured bars, staining pillars and pavement with faded sapphire and ruby. The smell of disturbed earth persisted here, mingled now with dust, extinguished candles, and something sharp—fresh blood.

A narrow crimson trail crossed the stones.

It led up the aisle, turned before the altar, and ended at the door of the sacristy.

He wrenched it open.

Empty!

The room possessed one barred window, a press for vestments, and a wooden chest. There was no further door, no hiding place, no human creature. Yet on the floor before the window lay three drops of blood, bright as garnets.

From outside came a dull fall.

Morton!

The big man ran back. His friend had collapsed beside the opened grave. He knelt, lifted him, called his name, slapped his cheeks. Morton’s eyes opened once. They held neither recognition nor pain, but an astonishment so vast that it seemed to look beyond the whole machinery of life.

His lips moved.

“She was—hungry—”

A tremor passed through him. Then his weight altered subtly in the big man’s arms. What had been a man became a burden.

The shout he gave brought lamps to windows and men running from the nearest houses. The innkeeper arrived in shirt and trousers; after him came the sexton, two labourers and, in due course, the village doctor carrying a black bag. They found the big man seated against a gravestone with Morton across his knees.

The doctor examined the body under a lantern. He was an elderly fellow with white whiskers, composed manners, and the professional aversion to wonder.

“Heart failure,” he said at last. “Sudden, but not surprising at his age, especially after exertion.”

“He was led here,” said the big man.

The doctor glanced at Morton’s bare ankles and insufficient coat. “Walking in sleep is not uncommon.”

“A girl led him.”

No one answered. The sexton crossed himself.

The big man described her—the hair, the grey gown, the bare feet. He told them of the disturbed graves and the blood in the church. The doctor listened gravely, but his expression closed by degrees.

“You have suffered a severe shock, monsieur. Moonlight, anxiety, the sight of your friend—”

“I saw her mouth.”

“Hélas, the imagination at such a moment—”

“Look at his throat.”

Beneath Morton’s right ear, almost concealed by the loose fold of skin, was a tiny wound. No larger than the puncture of a stout needle, it had produced very little outward bleeding. The doctor wiped it, frowned, and brought the lantern nearer.

“A thorn, perhaps.”

“In the churchyard?”

“Or an insect bite sustained earlier.”

The big man looked towards the others. Their faces, yellow in the lantern light, expressed not disbelief but a fear too old for argument. The sexton muttered something in patois.

“What did he say?” demanded the big man.

The innkeeper replied reluctantly. “He says the graves were opened from within.”

By dawn the churchyard had been searched, the church examined, and Morton’s body carried to an upper room of the inn. No girl was found. The bare footprints ended upon the church porch. The trail of blood remained visible along the aisle, but none could discover where it had gone from the locked sacristy. More curious still, the sexton declared that three graves had been disturbed during the previous month, though he had concealed the fact for fear of scandal. In each case the coffin lid had shifted, and the corpse within had been marked at the throat.

These statements were made piecemeal and unwillingly. Daylight had returned with cocks crowing, carts rattling, and wood-smoke lifting above the roofs; under its wholesome influence the villagers grew ashamed of nocturnal confidences. The doctor resumed his formula of heart failure. The innkeeper urged the big man to rest. Only the sexton, a narrow-faced man whose left eye twitched continually, remained near him.

“You went to the house,” he said in French.

“What house?”

“In Les Rouvres. Above the dry stream.”

“You told us there was no farm there,” the big man said to the innkeeper.

The man’s face became sombre. “There is none.”

“We sat inside it.”

The sexton crossed himself again. “Not inside a house, monsieur.”

After breakfast—untouched by all three men—they took him back along the valley. The day was cloudless. Sunlight poured into every hollow, and the vineyards, wet with dew, had an almost festive brilliance. It seemed monstrous that terror should have occurred beneath the same sky only hours before. Yet, as they climbed into the beech woods, the air cooled and ordinary sounds withdrew. The forest accepted them without welcome.

They found the dry channel and the little bridge.

Beyond it, nettles crowded a barely visible track. The big man led them through, recognizing at each turn a stone, a lightning-struck fir, a patch of white rock. Presently the trees opened upon the meadow.

There was no farmhouse.

At its centre lay a blackened foundation, sunk beneath brambles. Two fragments of wall remained, neither higher than a man’s knee. A pear tree, dead and silver with age, grew where the doorway should have been. The hearth was a heap of mossy stones.

The big man stood incapable of speech.

“Here,” he said at last. “The door was here. The table—there. Smoke came from that chimney.”

“There has been no chimney for fifty years,” said the innkeeper.

Among the ruins they found the coin he had left in payment. It lay upon a flat stone, bright and newly minted, beside a brown earthen bowl filled with rainwater. When the sexton saw the bowl, he would not touch it.

The account emerged later that day from the mayor’s records and from the recollections of certain old people who had inherited the story from their parents. Fifty-one years earlier the farm of Les Rouvres had belonged to a widow called Agathe Varnier. She lived there with her daughter, Lucile, a girl said to possess extraordinary beauty and a disposition both silent and solitary. Travelers occasionally sought refreshment at the farm. Two fell ill after drinking milk there; one died upon the road to Saint-Aubin, and another lingered three days, raving of a white face at his window.

Suspicion fastened upon the widow. Poisonous herbs were found drying in her kitchen, though she maintained that they were medicines for cattle. Before any charge could be proved, villagers climbed to the farm one winter night and burned it. Agathe perished in the fire.

“And the daughter?” asked the big man.

No one knew with certainty.

Some said Lucile had burned beside her mother. Others declared that she had been seen running barefoot into the forest before the roof fell. A third account, offered only after much wine by the mayor’s ancient aunt, held that Lucile had already been dead for several months when the farm was destroyed. Her mother had hidden the body and fed it with the blood of animals—until animals no longer sufficed.

“Peasant rubbish,” said the mayor, embarrassed. “Every valley has its monstrous fiction.”

But the old woman leaned towards the big man and fixed him with eyes scarcely dimmed by ninety years.

“She gave him milk?”

“Yes.”

“And she watched while he drank?”

“Yes.”

“Then she had chosen him.”

The big man returned alone to the inn. In Morton’s room the shutters had been closed against the afternoon heat. The doctor stood beside the bed, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and the expression upon his face had changed. His professional composure remained, but it now resembled a door held shut against pressure.

“I have completed the examination,” he said.

“Well?”

“The heart was weak.”

“You said that before.”

“It had ceased in a manner consistent with failure.”

The big man waited.

The doctor looked at the closed shutters. “There is also an abnormal deficiency of blood. Extreme. I have never observed the like.”

“How much?”

The doctor hesitated. “The chambers of the heart are virtually empty. The larger vessels also. Yet there is no wound sufficient to account for the loss.”

“Except the puncture in his throat.”

“Impossible.”

“You have seen it.”

“I have seen a minute perforation. To drain the body through such an aperture would be—beyond all question—”

“Impossible,” the big man repeated.

The doctor sat down heavily. “Yes.”

For some time neither spoke. Outside, the village pursued its modest affairs. A woman called to a child; harness bells jingled in the square; from the church tower came the measured striking of four. Those familiar sounds, instead of comforting the big man, seemed thin and provisional, a screen set before immensities that mankind had agreed not to examine.

At sunset he went to the church.

The blood had been scrubbed from the aisle, but a faint brown mark persisted between the stones. He followed it to the sacristy and discovered, beneath the barred window, a narrow crack where the plaster had fallen. Caught upon its edge was a single black hair, very long and glossy. When he touched it, a chill travelled through his hand to the elbow.

The sexton, watching from the doorway, whispered, “She will go back when she has fed.”

“To the ruins?”

“To wherever the dead wait when they are remembered.”

The big man turned upon him. “Why Morton?”

The sexton spread his hands. “Perhaps because he drank. Perhaps because he looked at her with kindness. There are doors opened by pity as well as by sin.”

That night the big man sat beside his friend’s body until dawn. No footsteps crossed the corridor. No bare print appeared below the window. Once, near midnight, the room filled briefly with the scent Morton had described—not flowers, not any perfume he knew, but damp earth mingled with cold milk. He rose and flung open the shutters.

Across the square, beneath the linden trees, stood a pale figure.

He saw it only for an instant. The church bell struck, a cloud passed from the moon, and the place was empty. Yet from the forested slope above the village came a sound that may have been a night bird—or may have been laughter, very low and very far away.

At dawn he left Saint-Aubin with Morton’s coffin.

For years afterward, when speaking of the affair—which he did seldom and only among friends incapable of easy ridicule—he adhered to the facts. He did not call Lucile Varnier a vampire. Such a word belonged to folklore, to nursery terrors, to benighted regions and credulous ages. He merely described the farmhouse that had not existed for half a century; the milk with its bitter taste; the silent girl whose feet made no sound; the opened graves; the blood upon her mouth; the wound beneath Morton’s ear; and the doctor’s written statement that a dead man’s heart had been found almost wholly drained.

Then he would fall silent.

And if anyone laughed, he did not protest. He would only look at the fire, where the flames consumed the wood yet left darkness gathered close around them, and rub his right hand as though it had touched something bitterly cold.

For reason can deny much, and courage can endure more; but the body preserves its own convictions. Even in warm rooms, even among friendly voices and the secure furniture of ordinary life, that hand remembered the touch of the girl at Les Rouvres—the yielding resistance, the chill without substance—and knew what the mind, to the last, refused to name.

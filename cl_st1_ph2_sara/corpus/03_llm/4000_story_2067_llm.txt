Jefferson Briley’s face had never frightened anybody, unless it might have been a very young child unexpectedly waked from sleep and disposed to take alarm at the first object offered. It was narrow, pale, and mild; his beard grew apologetically upon it in separate brown patches; and his light eyes had a habit of turning aside when another pair met them. Nevertheless, Jefferson possessed what he privately called a warlike soul.

Every Tuesday and Friday he drove the United States mail from Sanscrit Pond to North Kilby, seven miles by the old county road, and no driver between Maine and Mexico took the dangers of his calling more seriously. Under the mail sacks, within reach of his mittened hand, lay a nickel-plated pistol. It was not loaded, since cartridges cost money and were said to spoil by keeping; but Jefferson believed the mere existence of the weapon must exert a discouraging influence upon evil-minded persons. No robber had ever stopped him. This proved, to Jefferson’s satisfaction, that the pistol answered its purpose.

On a gray afternoon in January he drew up before the Sanscrit Pond post office, which occupied one corner of Asa Lunt’s store. The old sorrel mare stopped before he pulled the reins. She knew where the mail went, where passengers commonly entered, and where a mouthful of oats might be obtained by standing still long enough.

Jefferson stamped his feet upon the wagon bottom while Asa brought out two pouches and a bundle of newspapers.

“Road’s master hard,” said Asa. “Wind’s shiftin’ northwest. You’ll git your ears froze.”

“I’ve rode in wuss,” Jefferson answered.

He spoke as if the worse occasions had included prairie fires and hostile tribes, though in fact they had consisted of a wet snowstorm near the Kilby line and one muddy April when a wheel came off beside Deacon Purrington’s sheep pasture.

Asa tied the pouches beneath the seat. “You’ve got a passenger.”

Jefferson looked toward the store door and saw Mrs. Fanny Tobin advancing with a carpetbag in one hand and the corners of a large plaid shawl held together in the other. She was a little woman, though the number and looseness of her garments gave her a broader appearance. Her widow’s black had long since become a patient brown at the seams. She stepped carefully over the icy ridge by the horse trough and greeted Jefferson with a quick, bright look.

“Land, Jeff Briley, be you drivin’ yet?”

“Looks some as if I was,” he said.

“I thought mebbe they’d got a younger man.”

“There ain’t many younger men wants the resk.”

Fanny glanced from the sleepy mare to the empty road, which went white and frozen between walls of snow.

“No,” she said soberly, “I s’pose not.”

She handed him the carpetbag. Jefferson placed it behind the seat with the mail, giving the pouches the safer position. Fanny climbed in and settled herself upon the narrow rear bench.

“You’d better ride front,” he said. “There’s more robe.”

“I do’ know’s I’d ought to crowd ye.”

“You won’t crowd me. I ain’t so fleshy as some.”

“That’s so,” said Fanny, with the air of one accepting a statement supported by long observation. “But I can set here till we git outside the village.”

Jefferson clucked to the mare. She lifted one hind foot, appeared to consider whether the undertaking was reasonable, and at last moved away. The wheels grated over the frozen road. Behind them Asa Lunt went back into the warm store, and the village of Sanscrit Pond—seven houses, a church without a settled minister, the store, and a blacksmith shop whose chimney smoked only when there was work—soon fell out of sight.

The road followed the pond for half a mile. Ice lay dull under the low sky, with brown sedges showing along the coves. Here and there the wind had swept the snow clean from the stone walls, exposing the stones as black and rounded as sheep’s backs. Fanny tucked her feet beneath her skirts.

“Cold behind there?” Jefferson asked.

“I’ve felt warmer.”

“You’ll freeze stiff afore we strike Purrington’s. Come front.”

“Oh, I guess I can stan’ seven mile.”

“There ain’t no sense in standin’ what ye don’t have to. I’ve got two thicknesses o’ buffalo.”

Fanny hesitated just long enough to make the change proper. Then, when the mare slowed at the first hill, she stepped over the low partition and sat beside him. Jefferson pulled the robe across her knees. The buffalo skin had lost hair in several places and showed mended rents, but it retained warmth and an odor of barn loft, harness oil, and old winters.

“There,” he said. “That’s more Christian.”

“Your hands cold?”

“No. I’m used to exposure.”

Fanny looked at his blue knuckles where they showed between mitten and coat sleeve.

“I expect this mail business is wearin’,” she said.

“It ain’t fit fur everybody.”

“Any dangers?”

Jefferson straightened a little. “There’s dangers anywheres, if folks knew enough to see ’em. You take a lonesome road like this. I carry money orders sometimes. Registered letters. Government property. A man don’t know who’s watchin’.”

“Ever been robbed?”

“Not yit.”

“I hadn’t heard you had.”

“Robbers don’t send notice to North Kilby.”

“No,” Fanny agreed. “That would spoil it.”

Jefferson was uncertain whether she had laughed at him, but her face was composed. He shifted the reins to his left hand and let his right rest near the seat, directly above the concealed pistol.

“Out West,” he said, “mail drivers go armed.”

“Do tell.”

“Stage men, too. A feller I read about had seventeen bullet holes through his coat.”

“Was he in it?”

“Course he was.”

“Then I should think he’d ha’ stopped wearin’ it sooner.”

Jefferson gave a reluctant chuckle. “Women don’t understand them things.”

“Mebbe not. Tobin never had but one bullet hole in his clothes, an’ that was when he hung his old vest on the fence fur Silas Cole to shoot at. I mended it afterward. Men are queer about clothes. They’ll ruin a good thing fur sport an’ then expect a woman to put it back same’s ’twas.”

The mention of Tobin settled between them for a few rods. He had been dead six years, after an illness so long that people had first pitied Fanny, then advised her, then almost blamed her because he did not either recover or die. She had nursed him while her two daughters married, one to a farmer in East Thorndike and one to a carpenter at North Kilby. Since his death she had lived first with one daughter and then the other, carrying her carpetbag, her feather bed, her recipes, and a useful but not always welcome willingness to improve each household.

“You goin’ to Almiry’s?” Jefferson asked.

“Fur a spell.”

“Thought you was stayin’ with Jane.”

“I have been. Jane’s got her husband’s aunt now. She come fur two weeks last September.”

“Stayed some.”

“She’s stayin’ yit. There’s folks can’t tell when a visit’s ripened.”

“How’s Jane’s folks?”

“They eat hearty. That’s about all I can say without findin’ fault.”

Jefferson nodded. In that country, eating heartily was both virtue and accusation.

They descended the long pond hill. The mare went no faster, but the wagon made more noise, so that Jefferson held the reins firmly and imagined for a moment that he was controlling four foam-covered horses down a mountain pass. At the foot stood an abandoned schoolhouse. A faded circus bill remained pasted to one wall, displaying half a yellow lion and the legs of a lady on horseback. Jefferson had often looked at it and thought that life elsewhere was crowded with incident.

“Sometimes,” he said, “I’ve had a notion to go West.”

Fanny drew the robe higher. “What stopped ye?”

“Mother, first. Then Father was poorly. Then there was the place to look after. After they died, I’d got the route.”

“So you stayed on account of everything.”

“I could go now.”

“You could.”

“I ain’t tied.”

“No,” said Fanny. “Neither be I.”

This last statement had a brave sound, though both knew that freedom, when it consists of having no house in which one must sleep, may be less comfortable than bondage. Fanny looked across the snowy fields toward a distant farmhouse, where smoke stood blue above the chimney. She had slept in three spare rooms during the past year. Each had contained somebody else’s extra quilts, dried herbs, broken furniture, and opinions concerning how long a mother ought to remain.

“I always calculated to have a little place,” she said. “Tobin talked of buyin’ the Emery house. Had two apple trees, a pump under cover, an’ a bedroom downstairs. But he never got to it.”

“He warn’t forehanded.”

“He had plans enough. If plans was lumber, I’d own a hotel.”

Jefferson considered this. “The Emery place is gone bad now.”

“Roof’s open.”

“My place is tight.”

“I’ve heard ’twas.”

“Roof don’t leak anywheres but over the back chamber, an’ I don’t use that.”

Fanny glanced at him. “Who does fur ye?”

“Do what?”

“Cook an’ such.”

“I board round.”

“I knew you stopped to Mis’ Pike’s.”

“Tuesday through Thursday. Other days I’m to Cousin Marthy’s.”

“How’s Mis’ Pike’s victuals?”

Jefferson’s mouth tightened. “Fillin’.”

“Fillin’ ain’t always nourishin’.”

“She can make beans.”

“Most anybody can make beans if they’ve got beans an’ a pot.”

“Her biscuits are apt to be heavy.”

“Powder?”

“I expect.”

“She don’t stir light. You’ve got to bring biscuit together as if you was persuadin’ it, not punishin’ it.”

Jefferson, who had eaten three of Mrs. Pike’s biscuits that morning and felt each one still preserving its identity, sighed.

“Marthy’s better at pies,” he said.

“Your cousin Marthy puts too much shortenin’ in crust. I et there after the missionary meetin’. It flaked all over my black dress like shavin’s.”

“It tastes good.”

“So’d a lump o’ butter, but you wouldn’t call it pie.”

The old mare stopped where a pair of wheel tracks turned toward the Purrington farm. Jefferson said, “G’long,” without conviction. The mare moved again when ready.

They had gone two miles. The cold increased as the road left the shelter of the pond woods and crossed open upland. Fanny’s shoulder pressed Jefferson’s arm whenever a runner rut swung the wagon sideways. At first she drew away; after the third occasion she allowed the contact to remain. Jefferson was conscious of a warmth not entirely furnished by buffalo.

He had once walked home with Fanny from a singing school. She had been Fanny Dow then, with red cheeks and five young men prepared to carry her singing books. Jefferson had waited until the others were occupied hitching horses and had asked her first. For half a mile he had intended to tell her that her alto voice resembled a thrush’s. Then she complained of cold feet, and all he said was that the thermometer had stood at twelve that morning.

Within a year she married Tobin.

“Remember old singing school?” he asked.

“I guess I do. You used to set back o’ the stove.”

“I sung bass.”

“You tried to.”

“Eph Nason said I had a good low tone.”

“Eph couldn’t hear thunder after the cannon went off at muster.”

Jefferson laughed. “You was a master hand to sing.”

“My voice is all wore out now.”

“Shouldn’t wonder if you could sing yit.”

“I don’t get much chance. Almiry’s oldest girl thumps the organ every Sunday evenin’, an’ the baby hollers. There ain’t room fur another sound.”

“You had plenty o’ beaus them days.”

“Not so many.”

“Five or six, anyway.”

“I never counted.”

“I did,” Jefferson said.

Fanny turned toward him. Her face, sharpened by years of work and watching sickbeds, suddenly showed something of the lively girl who had known exactly how many young men waited outside the vestry door.

“You was one, was ye?”

“I might ha’ been, if I’d had any encouragement.”

“Encouragement! Land, Jeff, a woman couldn’t hitch herself to a man an’ drag him up to propose.”

“No. S’pose not.”

“You never acted as if you cared two pins.”

“I walked home with ye.”

“Once.”

“I was goin’ to ask ye again.”

“What happened?”

“You went an’ married Tobin.”

“You had nigh a year.”

“I was busy.”

“So was Tobin,” said Fanny, “but he found time.”

The mare’s ears moved backward, perhaps in appreciation of the argument. Jefferson’s pride suffered, though not fatally. It was one of his private comforts that Fanny had chosen Tobin because Tobin acted first, not because he was preferred. This theory, maintained for thirty years without evidence, now seemed almost confirmed.

“He didn’t make ye rich,” Jefferson observed.

“He didn’t make me lonesome, neither—not till he got sick.”

There was no suitable reply. Jefferson looked ahead at the road climbing between two bare maples. His house stood empty except when he stopped there between trips. He kept potatoes and apples in the cellar, his old furniture in the rooms, and a clock that ran when he remembered to wind it. On Sundays he sometimes sat in the kitchen wearing his hat because it was wasteful to heat a whole house for one man. He had called this independence. In severe weather it resembled loneliness.

“You must miss havin’ your own kitchen,” he said.

“I miss knowin’ where things be. At Jane’s the pepper’s one place Mondays an’ another by Wednesday. Almiry keeps flour in a covered pail behind the wood box, where any creatur’ with sense would look fur kindlin’. Then if I move it, she says she can’t find nothin’.”

“You’d know where things was to my house.”

“I don’t know’s I would.”

“Things ain’t been moved much since Mother died.”

“That’s fourteen year.”

“Thirteen come March.”

Fanny contemplated thirteen years of unmoved household things. She saw dusty jars, cracked crockery saved because it might be useful, curtains with fly marks, and a pantry containing spices old enough to vote. The prospect did not repel her. Dirt could be washed; disorder could be corrected; and there are women to whom another person’s neglected house appears less a warning than a field of conquest.

“Your front room furniture decent?” she asked.

“Haircloth. Sofa, six chairs, marble-top table.”

“Organ?”

“No.”

“Best not. Organs take room.”

“Got a melodeon in the chamber.”

“Could sell it.”

“I s’pose.”

“How much land?”

“House lot’s four acres. Then the south piece is eleven.”

“Mortgage?”

“No.”

She made a small sound which the wind carried away, but Jefferson knew it was approving. His chest warmed further. He had never before presented his property to Fanny as an argument, yet found the presentation agreeable.

At Purrington’s corner they passed two boys dragging a sled. The boys stared at Fanny sitting close beside Jefferson and shouted a greeting. Jefferson answered with dignity. Before night, Mrs. Purrington would know who had ridden with the mail; by morning, Sanscrit Pond might hear how close they had sat. Reputation traveled the route faster than Jefferson’s mare.

Fanny knew this too. She shifted half an inch away, then the wind entered between them, and she shifted back.

“Folks’ll talk,” she said.

“Let ’em.”

“Easy fur a man to say.”

“Ain’t doin’ no harm.”

“No. But Almiry’ll ask questions till bedtime.”

“You could tell her I kept ye from freezin’.”

“She’d want to know how.”

Jefferson’s ears reddened. “I expect you can handle Almiry.”

“I’ve handled harder.”

They rode another quarter mile in silence. The road entered spruce woods, dark and resinous, with snow mounded upon the low branches. The runners—though the vehicle had wheels, Jefferson always called the wheel ruts runner ruts in winter—struck a stone, and Fanny caught his sleeve.

“Careful!” she cried.

“I got ye.”

“I ain’t fallin’.”

“No, but I got ye.”

His arm remained around her for a moment longer than necessity allowed. Fanny did not object at once; afterward, to restore proper relations, she said his coat collar needed brushing.

“You’d look some better if your hair was cut,” she added.

“I had it cut in October.”

“Which October?”

“Last one.”

“Who done it?”

“Mis’ Pike.”

“That explains why one side’s ahead o’ the other.”

“She done it for nothin’.”

“It looks it.”

A man of Jefferson’s temperament might have withdrawn into injury, but the day had begun to alter him. He had a woman under his robe, her hand still lightly upon his arm, and his property had survived inquiry. His warlike soul, so long occupied with imaginary robbers, suddenly discovered a real campaign.

“You could cut it,” he said.

“I could.”

“Do a good job?”

“I cut Tobin’s twenty-eight year.”

“Always looked neat.”

“He had a head that set level.”

“What’s wrong with mine?”

“Nothin’ that patience won’t help.”

They emerged from the spruces beside a snowed-in pasture. A crow flew heavily across the road. North Kilby was still three miles away, and the afternoon was dimming.

“Be we got four more?” Fanny asked.

“Not quite three.”

“Seems longer in winter.”

“I drive it alone mostly.”

“That must be dull.”

“There’s things to think about.”

“Robbers?”

“Sometimes.”

“What else?”

Jefferson looked at the mare’s moving back. “Livin’ different.”

“How different?”

“Settled, mebbe.”

“You’re settled now. So settled moss’ll grow on ye.”

“I mean havin’ somebody.”

Fanny adjusted the edge of the robe, though it required no adjustment. “You could hire a housekeeper.”

“Hire one’d cost.”

“Marriage costs.”

“Not the same way.”

“No,” she said. “Costs more in some ways, less in others.”

“You think you’d ever marry again?”

“I never laid out to.”

“That ain’t answerin’.”

“It’s answer enough.”

“No, ’tain’t.”

His boldness surprised them both. Fanny studied him. Jefferson’s face remained mild, but a little determined crease had appeared between his brows, giving him, in her opinion, the look of a sheep preparing to dispute a gateway.

“Who’d want me?” she asked.

“I might.”

“Might ain’t much.”

“I would, then.”

“You ain’t asked.”

“I’m comin’ to it.”

“Well, don’t hurry on my account. You’ve had thirty year.”

He drew a breath. The mare stopped at a drift. Jefferson slapped the reins and ordered her forward in a voice intended for a stage team under fire. She leaned into the collar, pulled through, and resumed her usual pace.

“Fanny,” he said, “if you want a home, I’ve got one. If I want somebody, you ain’t tied. We’ve both on us been alone long enough. What d’ye say?”

This proposal lacked moonlight, music, and any reference to undying devotion, but Fanny had received one more ornamental in her youth and knew its market value. Tobin had promised that he would build her a bay-windowed house and take her to Boston. He had fulfilled neither promise, though he had loved her in his way and once walked six miles for medicine during a thaw. Jefferson offered a tight roof, fifteen acres, no mortgage, and the chance to place the pepper where it belonged.

She was not going to accept too quickly.

“You need somebody to cook,” she said.

“I need you.”

“Need’s a poor foundation.”

“Want ye, then.”

“You’ve got Mis’ Pike’s beans.”

“Don’t cast them up.”

“An’ Marthy’s pie.”

“I could leave ’em.”

“Would they be willin’?”

“They ain’t marryin’ me.”

Fanny’s mouth twitched. “You’d expect me to slave.”

“No. Only do what was reasonable.”

“Men’s reason stretches.”

“I’d fetch wood an’ water. You could have hens.”

“How many?”

“As many’s practical.”

“Garden?”

“South side.”

“Front room done over?”

“If it needs it.”

“It needs it.”

“You ain’t seen it.”

“I know haircloth.”

Jefferson considered the expense of wallpaper, paint, hens, and possibly curtains. Then Fanny’s shoulder touched his again, and the empty kitchen appeared before him with a fire, a clean lamp chimney, biscuits brought together by persuasion, and another chair drawn near.

“You could fix it your way,” he said grandly.

“Within means,” Fanny corrected. She distrusted grandeur in a husband. “An’ I’d keep my own money from Tobin.”

“What you got’s yourn.”

“An’ my feather bed.”

“Bring it.”

“Almiry claims she needs it.”

“Let Almiry buy feathers.”

This was the first truly romantic thing Jefferson had said. Fanny’s daughters had divided her possessions in imagination long before her death, and the defense of the feather bed moved her more than praise could have done.

“Well,” she said, “I ain’t sayin’ I will.”

“You ain’t sayin’ you won’t.”

“I’d have to think.”

“How long?”

“I can’t tell.”

“We’re gittin’ near Kilby.”

“The road goes back Friday.”

“I don’t want to wait till Friday.”

She looked away over the field, where the last light lay pale upon the snow. She thought of Almiry’s crowded house, Jane’s husband’s aunt, the carpetbag, and all the spare rooms through which she might continue to travel, useful and displaced. Then she looked at Jefferson: his uneven hair, anxious eyes, thin shoulders, foolish unloaded pistol, and house with the roof tight everywhere but over a chamber nobody used. He was vain, timid, economical, and more tender than he knew. Such material, Fanny believed, could be worked with.

“I s’pose,” she said slowly, “if you’re set on it, I might consider givin’ up my freedom.”

“Mean yes?”

“I mean I might.”

“Fanny.”

“Well—yes, then. But don’t drive into a ditch.”

Jefferson had put an arm around her and nearly dropped the reins. The old mare, finding no direction offered, continued straight. He kissed the cold edge of Fanny’s bonnet and encountered woolen yarn rather than cheek.

“Land!” she said. “Anybody might come along.”

“Nobody has all day.”

“That don’t signify they won’t now.”

Yet she was smiling, and presently moved nearer without blaming the weather. Under the worn robe there came a pleasant glow, made partly by wool, partly by victory, and partly by the human comfort of no longer being obliged to face every coming winter alone.

The lights of North Kilby appeared one by one: the smithy’s red window, the post office lamp, the white gleam from Almiry’s kitchen. Jefferson sat upright as they entered the village. In imagination he had often arrived there after beating off masked men, preserving the registered letters, and receiving the grateful congratulations of the government. This evening no crowd waited. A dog barked, a woman shook a rug from a doorway, and the mare stopped at the post office of her own accord.

“Don’t tell everybody yit,” Fanny whispered.

“I won’t.”

“You’ll tell Asa goin’ back.”

“Only Asa.”

“Asa’ll tell his wife.”

“He tells her everything.”

“Then she’ll tell the Pond.”

Jefferson reflected. “Folks have got to know sometime.”

“Not till I speak to the girls. They’ll act as if I’d lost my senses.”

“Have ye?”

Fanny examined him with affectionate severity.

“Mebbe,” she said. “But I ain’t lost enough not to cut your hair Saturday.”

He helped her down, protecting the mail with one hand and his future wife with the other. Fanny took her carpetbag and went toward Almiry’s, her small figure brisk against the snow. Halfway to the gate she turned.

“Jeff!”

“What?”

“Load that pistol or leave it home. It’s foolish to carry dead weight.”

Then she disappeared into the lamplight.

Jefferson stood beside the wagon, feeling bolder than any rider of a Western stage. For years he had guarded the mail against an attack that never came. At last he had met with an adventure upon the North Kilby road—and the gentle defender, pistol and all, had himself been captured.

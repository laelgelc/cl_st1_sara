By seven in the morning the wall of the American embassy had begun to give back the sun.

It was a high wall, cream-colored, clean in a way that made the road beside it look dirtier. The people in the visa queue stood along that road with their folders pressed to their chests and their shoes whitening from dust. A woman sold sachets of water from a blue basin. A boy carried passport photographs in a transparent envelope and called out that he knew the correct size, the American size, not the nonsense size they would reject inside. Farther down, beneath an almond tree that gave almost no shade, two men in white shirts held a cardboard sign:

AMERICAN VISA MIRACLE MINISTRY  
NO CASE TOO HARD FOR GOD

Ugonna’s mother stood between a young man in a pale green shirt and an elderly woman wearing a black head tie. She held a plastic file that her husband’s friend, Emeka, had arranged for her. Birth certificate. Marriage certificate. Newspaper clippings. Photocopies of threats. The photograph of the sitting room after the men had left. The death certificate was at the back, inside its own clear sleeve.

She had not looked at it since Emeka pushed it into the file.

“Keep everything in order,” he had said. “These people like order.”

The young man behind her tapped the edge of his brown envelope against his thigh. He had a narrow face and a collar so starched it had begun to soften with sweat. Each time the line moved, he cleared his throat before stepping forward, as though someone might be listening for correct behavior.

“Madam, they say today is good,” he said.

She turned a little.

“For visa,” he added. “Somebody told me Wednesday is their best day. They reject less on Wednesday.”

It was Thursday.

Perhaps he had been in the line since Wednesday. Perhaps America observed another calendar. She faced the gate again.

The young man laughed quietly. “All these stories, abi? If they want to give you, they give you. If they don’t want, even if Jesus Christ comes with you as sponsor, they will say insufficient ties.”

The elderly woman in front looked over her shoulder. “Don’t mention Jesus like that.”

“Sorry, Mummy.”

“You people think everything is joke.”

“No, Mummy.”

The line shifted. Shoes scraped. Folders cracked open and shut. A soldier near the gate lifted his rifle strap from where it had sunk into his shoulder. He wore dark glasses, although he stood beneath a canopy. The rifle lay across his chest with the lazy familiarity of something carried every day.

Three men.

No, four. There had been four in the house.

She fixed her eyes on the blue basin of water. The sachets inside it shone like trapped fish. The vendor split one open with her teeth and drank, her throat moving twice. Ordinary things should remain ordinary. Water. Dust. A rifle strap. The sun striking a wall.

The first knock had come after ten at night.

Not a knock. A pounding that shook the framed photograph above the television. Her husband was already gone then, gone for five days, though the newspapers still printed his column and people still telephoned to say he was brave. The government could close a newspaper office, seize copies from vendors, arrest the printer, but words already read could not be arrested. That was what one of his friends said at the dining table two weeks before he escaped.

He had said it while chewing fried plantain.

Words already read cannot be arrested.

She had collected the plates afterward and found a half-moon of plantain beneath his chair.

At the embassy gate a guard called, “Next five, prepare your appointment papers.”

The young man counted people with his finger. “We are among the next ten,” he said. “Madam, your own is immigrant visa?”

“Asylum.”

The word came out lower than she intended.

His face changed. Not pity exactly. Interest first, then the effort to hide interest. “Oh. Political?”

She looked at him.

“Sorry. I’m sorry, madam. I only asked because they say asylum case is different.”

The elderly woman said, “Face your front, young man.”

“Yes, Mummy.”

He did not speak again for several minutes. Around them the queue breathed and complained. A man with a red tie argued that the embassy had reduced the daily quota because Nigerians abused opportunity. A student in jeans said no, it was because of the bombings in America. Someone else said an official inside could be settled if one knew the correct person. The photograph boy moved along the line holding up samples of ears, chins, white backgrounds.

“Correct American visa picture! If your ear no show, they will bounce you!”

A beggar dragged one stiff leg through the dust. He extended a plastic cup and said, “God will open your way.” Nobody wanted to refuse such a blessing outside the embassy. Coins dropped into the cup.

Ugonna had liked coins. He arranged them in a row beneath the center table, smallest to largest, and became angry if she spent one. The ten-naira coin was his favorite because of its pale center. He had called it the egg coin.

Mummy, don’t use my egg money.

Four years old, and already he believed objects could be kept by naming them.

The pounding had woken him. He came into the corridor rubbing his face, wearing the yellow singlet with the small brown bear. She had been standing near the bedroom door with the telephone in her hand. She had tried her husband’s number once, though she knew it would not connect. He had called from Cotonou the night before and warned her not to call back.

“They monitor everything,” he had said.

“Then why are you calling?”

“To hear your voice.”

“Whose voice?”

A silence had followed, filled with the foreign crackle of the line.

“Both of you,” he said.

Both of you.

As though they were one thing.

The line moved again. The old woman took two small steps and stopped. Sweat ran beneath Ugonna’s mother’s blouse, down the center of her back. She had dressed in black because black required no decision. Emeka’s wife had suggested the navy suit.

“You must look serious but also stable,” she had said. “Not as if you are already broken.”

Already broken was perhaps visible through cloth.

Emeka’s wife had brought rice and stew after the burial. She had warmed it, placed a tray near the bed, and said food would give strength. The stew had a bright layer of palm oil on top. Fresh oil, red and shining.

Ugonna’s mother had pushed the tray away so quickly that the glass of water tipped over.

“I’m sorry,” Emeka’s wife had said.

She was always sorry. Everyone was always sorry, which suggested there was a quantity of sorrow that could be divided among visitors in enamel bowls and carried out of the house. Sorry for your loss. Sorry for what happened. Sorry, take heart. Sorry, God knows best.

God knew, and this knowledge was offered as consolation.

At the gate, the soldier struck the metal barrier with his palm. “Close up! Why una leave gap?”

People hurried to obey. The young man’s envelope touched her back. He withdrew it at once.

“Sorry, madam.”

She could smell his aftershave now, sharp and medicinal. He leaned sideways and said, “If they ask you why America, don’t say because there is no work here. Even if that is the truth. Tell them tourism or conference or something.”

“I said asylum.”

“Yes. Sorry. Your own is different.”

Her own.

For many years she had thought her husband’s work was his own. His articles, his midnight meetings, the men who came to speak in low voices on their balcony. His office being searched. His name discussed on the radio. His photographs in foreign papers, always the same photograph in which he looked directly at the camera with a firmness he did not use at home.

He said silence was collaboration.

He said fear was how dictators multiplied themselves.

He said somebody had to speak.

Not that she wanted him silent. Not that she admired General Abacha or the men who carried rifles into newspaper houses. Not that she did not know what happened in detention rooms. Her cousin’s husband had returned from one with a split eardrum and two missing teeth, and for months he flinched when doors closed.

But courage was a spacious public room. People entered it, praised the furniture, praised the man who had built it. Nobody saw the small room behind it where his wife waited beside a telephone, where his child learned not to ask when Daddy was coming.

Her husband had escaped in the back of a produce lorry. Emeka had arranged it after a police clerk sent word that a list had been prepared. Arrest before dawn. Transfer to an undisclosed location. An accident during interrogation.

He came home for eleven minutes to collect a bag.

“You and Ugonna will follow later,” he said.

“Later when?”

“As soon as I am safe.”

She had wanted to laugh. Safety, in his mouth, belonged first to him.

Instead she folded two shirts and put them in his bag. She added his blood-pressure tablets. At the door, he knelt and kissed the sleeping boy’s forehead. Ugonna stirred and said, “Daddy, bring biscuit.”

“I will,” he whispered.

He had not.

The embassy guard examined her appointment paper. He looked at her face, then at the paper, then at her face again.

“Remove all electronics. Phone, charger, anything.”

“I have no phone.”

“No sealed envelope. No liquid.”

She opened her handbag. A handkerchief, keys, a comb, the plastic file. A packet of mints Emeka’s wife had slipped inside.

The guard pressed the sides of the bag and waved her through the first gate.

Inside, the compound smelled different. Cut grass. Wet earth from sprinklers. The lawn was so green that it seemed deliberate, a statement made against the road. Applicants followed a painted line toward a low building with glass doors. The young man came behind her, but the old woman had been held back because something in her bag kept making the detector sound.

“Madam,” the young man whispered, “half battle done.”

She did not answer.

The waiting hall was cold. Air-conditioning touched the sweat on her neck and made her shiver. Rows of plastic chairs faced glass windows. Above the windows, red numbers appeared and disappeared with a soft bell. Families sat with children dressed in church clothes. A baby slept across two chairs, one hand open beside his mother’s file. Television screens displayed instructions about fingerprints and fraud.

DO NOT PRESENT FALSE DOCUMENTS.

The death certificate in her file was not false.

CAUSE OF DEATH: GUNSHOT WOUND.

A sentence so neat it could fit inside a box.

At the registration desk, a Nigerian woman with reddish-gold braids asked for the appointment letter. Her fingernails were painted pale pink.

“Any dependants traveling with you?”

“No.”

“Children?”

The woman’s fingers hovered above the keyboard.

“One,” Ugonna’s mother said.

“Traveling?”

“No.”

The woman waited.

“He is dead.”

The fingers moved again. “I’m sorry. Place four fingers of your left hand here.”

The scanner lit green beneath her skin.

“Now right hand.”

Her fingerprints appeared on the screen in black whorls. Evidence that she existed. Evidence that the fingers were hers.

She was given a number and told to sit.

The young man had already found a chair. He waved to an empty one beside him. She sat three seats away, leaving a space occupied only by cold air. On the wall opposite, a framed photograph showed an American city at night, buildings rising in blue light beside water. Her husband was somewhere in America now. Washington, perhaps, or Maryland. The last time he called, she had not asked. He had given an address to Emeka, who copied it onto the asylum form.

“It is temporary housing,” Emeka said. “Some organization is helping him.”

An organization. People who admired him without having shared a bed with him. People who would invite him to speak beneath bright lights about tyranny, and who would lower their eyes at the correct moment when he mentioned his son.

Would he mention Ugonna?

Of course he would. Ugonna’s death had already appeared in three foreign newspapers. SON OF EXILED NIGERIAN JOURNALIST KILLED IN RAID. The headline made the death sound almost professional, another consequence of a career. Nobody wrote that Ugonna did not like the skin on custard. Nobody wrote that he said lellow instead of yellow until she corrected him and then he cried. Nobody wrote that the week before he died, he had built a crooked tower from red and blue LEGO blocks and called it America.

The raid began with the front door breaking inward.

The first man entered wearing a dark shirt and army trousers. The second carried a rifle. The third had a red cloth tied around his wrist. There was another behind them, but she saw only the metal of his belt buckle as they crowded the sitting room.

“Where is he?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where your husband?”

“He traveled.”

“Travel where?”

“I don’t know.”

The one with the red cloth slapped her. It was almost casual. Her head struck the wall, and the telephone fell from her hand.

“Madam, no make this thing hard. We know he come here.”

“He did not.”

“Call am.”

“I can’t.”

“Call am!”

Ugonna stood in the corridor. He was no longer rubbing his eyes. He watched the men with the grave stillness children sometimes used when adults became unfamiliar.

She said, “Go back to the room.”

The man with the rifle turned.

It was not yet the moment. Memory always wanted to hurry there, but there had been minutes before it. The cushions pulled from the chairs. The drawers emptied. Her husband’s books thrown down. One man kicked open the cabinet and bottles rolled across the floor, clinking. The bottle of palm oil she kept on the lower shelf toppled and broke.

Bright red on the tiles.

That was what she saw first.

Oil spreading around Ugonna’s feet.

A bell sounded in the waiting hall. Number 118 appeared above window six. A family rose together, gathering documents, calling a sleepy child.

Ugonna’s mother pressed her knees close. Her number was 126.

The young man moved into the empty chair between them. “They refused that man,” he murmured.

At window four, a broad-shouldered applicant walked away slowly. His mouth had gone slack.

“How do you know?” she asked.

“He didn’t collect paper. If they approve, they keep passport.”

Perhaps the young man had studied every motion in the hall and turned it into prophecy. Perhaps everyone here did. A lifted eyebrow meant danger. A blue sheet meant success. A passport returned through the slot meant the end of a future one had already furnished.

“What work do you do?” she asked him.

The question surprised them both.

“Computer engineering. Well, I finished last year. I’m going for training in Atlanta.”

“You will come back?”

“Yes, yes.” He spoke quickly, as though the interviewer could hear. “My family is here. My future is here.”

He smiled, embarrassed by the lie or by its necessity.

“My elder brother is there,” he added more softly. “He says if I enter once, I will see how to arrange myself small-small.”

“Then your future is there.”

He looked at her, uncertain whether she was mocking him.

“My future can be in two places,” he said at last.

She turned toward the photograph of the American city.

Her husband had spoken of two places too. The country as it was and the country as it should become. He lived inside the distance between them and called it duty. She had lived there with him without agreeing to the rent.

Before their marriage, he wrote about corrupt council chairmen and missing salaries. Later he wrote about soldiers who became ministers, ministers who became millionaires, men who disappeared after being invited for questioning. His voice grew larger. At parties, strangers clasped his shoulder.

“You are doing what all of us are afraid to do.”

“History will remember you.”

“You give a voice to the voiceless.”

He would lower his head modestly. She knew that gesture. At home he used it when praised for washing one plate.

Once, after a threatening call at two in the morning, she asked him to stop for a month.

“A month will become two,” he said.

“Then let it become two.”

“You don’t understand.”

She had been carrying Ugonna then. The baby moved beneath her ribs.

“No,” she said. “I understand only the life we are actually living.”

He slept in the study that night. The next week his article was published beneath the title THE SILENCE OF GOOD MEN.

Number 124.

The young man stood and smoothed his shirt. He had been given 125.

“Wish me luck, madam.”

“Luck.”

He walked toward window three with his shoulders held stiffly. The official behind the glass was a white woman with auburn hair. She smiled as he approached, and he smiled too broadly in return.

At window seven, a mother lifted a toddler so the official could see his face. The child pressed both hands against the glass.

Ugonna had not cried when the men entered. He cried only after she shouted at him to go back. Her voice frightened him more than the rifles did.

“Mummy.”

“Go!”

The man with the rifle swung around, annoyed by the sound. Another man had opened the balcony doors. A gust entered and moved the curtains.

Then the bottle broke.

Perhaps someone stepped on it. Perhaps the rifle caught the cabinet. She remembered the sharp pop of glass and the red oil spreading. She remembered thinking she would have to clean it before morning because ants would come.

The gun went off.

Not like in films. A hard, flat crack that stopped the room.

Ugonna sat down.

That was what it looked like. Her child simply sat down in the oil, one leg folded under him. Then his yellow singlet changed near the chest. Red moved outward through the cloth.

Fresh palm oil.

“Jesus,” one of the men said.

The rifleman stared at the gun as though it had acted alone.

She crossed the room, but someone seized the back of her blouse. Cloth tightened against her throat.

“Leave the boy! Where is your husband?”

The boy.

Ugonna looked at her. His mouth opened, not crying now. Surprised. He seemed to be asking why she had not yet lifted him.

She struck the hand behind her. She bit skin. A man cursed and shoved her toward the balcony. There were footsteps, bottles rolling, somebody saying they must go, somebody else saying no witness.

No witness.

The open balcony door.

She did not decide to jump. Her body went before thought. One moment the room was behind her; the next, the railing struck her thigh and the darkness tilted. She fell onto the zinc roof of the neighbor’s shed, rolled, and landed among broken buckets.

For a while she could not breathe. Above her, men moved through her home. A curtain lifted from the balcony like a hand.

She did not go back for Ugonna.

This was the sentence beneath every other sentence.

She did not go back.

The bell sounded.

126.

The young man was still at window three. His hands moved as he explained something. The auburn-haired woman had stopped smiling.

Ugonna’s mother rose with the blue file. Her left ankle hurt when she put weight on it. It had hurt since the jump, though the clinic said nothing was broken.

Window five.

The interviewer was a man with pale skin and faded lips. His tie was blue, and a small flag stood beside his computer. He looked at her, then at the screen.

“Good morning.”

“Good morning.”

“Please place your documents in the tray.”

She slid the file beneath the glass. He opened it and checked the first page.

“You are applying to join your husband in the United States on grounds of political persecution?”

“Yes.”

“And your husband is a journalist?”

“Yes.”

He named the newspaper. She confirmed it.

“I understand he left Nigeria following threats from state security personnel.”

“Yes.”

The interviewer read from the screen. His face did not change. Perhaps he had learned not to react to the lives that passed before him. Or perhaps too many lives had passed.

“Can you tell me when you last saw him?”

She gave the date.

“And when did the incident at your residence occur?”

She gave that date too.

“I’m sorry for your loss,” he said. “I’ll need to ask you some difficult questions.”

The formula was gentle. Practiced gentleness, polished by repetition.

He took out the newspaper clippings. Her husband’s face stared upward from the page.

“Were the men who entered your home wearing uniforms?”

“Some parts of uniforms.”

“Could you identify them as military personnel?”

“They had guns.”

“I understand. But were there insignia, identification cards, marked vehicles?”

“It was night.”

“Did they state which agency they represented?”

“They asked for my husband.”

“Yes. Did they specifically say they were acting on behalf of the government?”

She looked at him.

He leaned closer to the glass. “I’m not disputing your account. I need to establish the connection between the attack and your husband’s political activity.”

Connection.

A line drawn between one thing and another. Article, threat, raid, bullet, child. Clean lines in a file.

“They knew his name,” she said.

“Did they mention his articles?”

“They said he had been writing rubbish.”

The interviewer typed.

“Exact words, if you remember.”

She remembered. She remembered the red cloth at the man’s wrist, damp near the knot. She remembered the smell of whisky when he spoke close to her face. She remembered Ugonna’s eyes after the gun went off.

She said, “They said, ‘Where is that newspaper man? Where is the bastard?’”

The typing continued.

“And you believe the shooting of your son was deliberate?”

Her hands went still on the ledge.

The room around her seemed to recede. Numbers sounded. Chairs scraped. At another window, someone laughed with relief.

“No,” she said.

The interviewer paused. “No?”

“The gun went off.”

“Accidentally?”

“Yes.”

He considered this. “But the armed raid itself was connected to your husband.”

“Yes.”

“And after the incident, did you receive further threats?”

Emeka had received a message. Neighbors had seen a dark Peugeot near the house. A man called once and breathed into the telephone. Her husband said it proved they would return.

She said, “I don’t know.”

The interviewer folded his hands. “Mrs.—” He used her husband’s name. “For asylum protection, we must determine whether you have a well-founded fear of persecution. Your husband’s profile is relevant, and the death of your son may be highly significant, but we need as much detail and corroboration as possible.”

He opened the file to the photographs.

She had not known Emeka had included that one.

Ugonna lay on the sitting-room floor beneath a white cloth that covered him only to the shoulders. His face looked smaller than it had in sleep. Beside the cloth, oil and blood had mixed into a dark shine. A red LEGO block rested under the edge of the sofa.

The interviewer glanced at the photograph and then away. “I’m very sorry.”

She reached through the slot and took the file from him.

“I’m not finished reviewing—”

She removed the photograph.

At the burial, the pastor said Ugonna had gone to a better place. The coffin was too small. That was the obscenity of it, not the prayers, not the dust dropping onto wood. The smallness. Two men lowered it easily, their arms hardly straining.

After everyone left, she stayed beside the mound. Ixora flowers grew along the cemetery wall, red clusters above glossy leaves. She picked some and arranged them on the earth. Red, then yellow, then red again, as Ugonna arranged his blocks.

Emeka found her there after sunset.

“You must leave the country,” he said.

She placed another flower.

“They will use you to get to him.”

She almost laughed. They had already used her.

“This is strong evidence,” he said carefully. “Terrible, but strong. They cannot deny you after this.”

Strong evidence.

Her son, promoted from the boy to proof.

Behind the embassy glass, the official was speaking. “I know this is painful. We can take a short break if you need one.”

She put the photograph back into its sleeve.

“Will you want me to describe how he died?”

“We need your testimony, yes.”

“What he was wearing?”

“If you recall.”

“Where the bullet entered?”

“Only what you’re comfortable sharing, but medical documentation—”

“How much blood?”

His faded lips parted. “No. Not necessarily.”

“What he said before?”

The official looked down at the file. “We’re trying to help you establish your claim.”

Claim. Case. Evidence. Dependant. Deceased.

She imagined these words arranged on Ugonna’s grave in place of flowers.

The morning after the burial, her husband called from America. His voice arrived clear, almost without distance.

“My love,” he said.

She held the receiver and listened.

“They told me everything.”

Everything.

“I will bring you here. I swear. I have spoken to lawyers. We will start again.”

Start again was the promise people gave when they had no power to return anything. A new country, a new flat, new furniture. Perhaps another child, though nobody said this aloud. Her husband waiting at an airport with his public sorrow and open arms.

“We will make Ugonna’s death mean something,” he said.

She ended the call.

Now the interviewer pushed the file gently toward her. “Your husband has been granted temporary protection. That supports your application. If you can explain the continuing danger to you personally, it will help.”

Danger to her personally.

She thought of the dark car. The breathing telephone. The men who might return. She thought of America, of snow Ugonna would never touch, of cereal in bright boxes, of her husband writing again at a desk while she stood in a kitchen and listened for footsteps in a corridor.

She thought of the grave beneath Lagos soil.

The new life had been described so often that it had acquired rooms. Emeka’s wife said she would study nursing. Her husband said they would find a good school when they had another child. Friends said America would give her space to heal, as though grief required wider roads. Nobody asked where she wanted to be. They assumed that survival moved westward.

The official waited.

She closed the file.

“I have to go.”

He blinked. “We can pause the interview.”

“No.”

“If you leave before we complete the application, there may be a delay in processing.”

She picked up her handbag.

“Mrs.—please understand, you may not be able to obtain another appointment soon.”

She nodded.

“Are you withdrawing your application?”

The question required a proper answer. Yes or no. Another box.

She turned away.

The young man in the pale green shirt sat near the exit with his passport in his hands. His shoulders had collapsed inward. When he saw her, he tried to smile.

“They said my bank statement is not enough,” he said. “Imagine. My own money is not enough for them.”

She stopped.

“Your interview finished?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“They give you?”

The glass doors opened as somebody entered. Heat pushed into the hall.

She said, “I am going home.”

Outside, the sun had climbed higher. The lawn glittered beneath the sprinklers. At the security post a guard called after her to collect nothing, because she had deposited nothing. She passed through the gate and returned to the dust, the vendors, the beggar’s cup, the miracle ministry.

The queue was longer now.

People watched her face as she came out, trying to read the decision there. One woman whispered to another. A man asked, “Madam, how was it inside?” The photograph boy held up a sample and moved aside for her.

She walked without answering.

At the road, buses growled in the traffic. Conductors leaned from doors and shouted destinations. A taxi slowed, its driver lifting his chin in question. She shook her head.

The cemetery was far. She could take a bus to Ojuelegba, then another. On the way she would buy ixora if the flower woman had any. Red and yellow. Perhaps blue flowers too, though Ugonna had owned only two blue blocks and had always hidden one.

Her ankle hurt. Sweat gathered beneath her black blouse. Behind her, the embassy wall returned the white glare of morning, and beyond it numbers would continue to sound, files would open, lives would be measured and explained.

She kept walking.

Ugonna was waiting nowhere. She knew this. Not beneath the mound, not among the ixora roots, not in a better place arranged by the pastor. Yet the grave was the last place where nobody required him to prove why he had died. There he was not evidence. Not the son of a brave man. Not a headline.

Ugonna.

Her child.

She crossed the road when the traffic broke and walked toward the buses, carrying the closed blue file against her chest.

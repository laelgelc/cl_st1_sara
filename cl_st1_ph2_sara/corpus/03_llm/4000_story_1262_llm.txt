## MR. FARMILOE’S OPPORTUNITY

There are suburbs in the west of London which seem, upon a May morning, to have been washed, painted, polished, and finally set out for the admiration of Providence. Their villas, standing a little back from the road in gardens of laurel and glossy holly, display stucco fronts of cream, pink, or a pale and respectable yellow; their tradesmen’s windows sparkle with salmon, strawberries, patent boots, devotional books, and silver-mounted walking-sticks; their delivery boys whistle as they swing neat baskets over area railings; and on Sundays the bells of two churches and one ambitious chapel mingle above the laburnums with an effect almost cathedral. Nothing appears old except the ivy, nothing poor except the curates, and nothing wicked except, perhaps, the prices.

Elmford Rise was such a suburb. It was pretty, clean, fresh-coloured, and conscious of being all three. Its butcher wore a white apron without a stain, its baker’s carts were varnished like yachts, its dairyman printed the names of prize cows upon his bills, and even the chimney-sweep, when he appeared, did so in a silk hat. The residents—retired merchants, successful solicitors, widows with incomes, and clerks whose solvency had begun to flower in bow-windows—required civility as regularly as milk. They liked a bow with their mutton, a smile with their stationery, a little deferential conversation with their calomel; and a man who failed in these amenities was considered not merely disagreeable, but unsound.

Into this cheerful social mechanism there entered, at the age of thirty-eight, Mr. Septimus Farmiloe, pharmaceutist.

Mr. Farmiloe had been left eight hundred and seventy pounds by an aunt whom he had disliked, though not so steadily as she had disliked him. The legacy arrived at a moment when he was receiving thirty-two shillings a week as an assistant in a chemist’s shop near the Elephant and Castle, sleeping in a room over the laboratory, and submitting daily to the instructions of a proprietor seven years younger than himself. He had long regarded his life as an elaborate injustice devised by persons of inferior education. The aunt’s money seemed, for several weeks, to prove that destiny had recognised its error.

He might have invested it. He might have purchased a modest business in a modest street. He might even have remained an assistant, with money in the bank and resentment in his heart. But caution in Mr. Farmiloe was never strongest when most required. He heard of a pharmacy at Elmford Rise which included a post office, and the combination dazzled him. The price was absurd; this fact, represented by the vendor as evidence of value, impressed him favourably. The postal appointment brought little direct remuneration, but—so said Mr. Cutlow, the outgoing proprietor—every person who came for a postal-order might buy tooth-powder, every lady who posted a letter might remember her neuralgia, every nursemaid who required stamps might be tempted by violet scent.

“Constant traffic,” said Mr. Cutlow, spreading his soft hands over the counter. “That is what you purchase, sir. The public must come.”

The public must come! Farmiloe repeated the phrase during the negotiations and found in it the solidity of a government bond. He paid seven hundred pounds for the business, stock, fittings, goodwill, and appointment; spent sixty more on a new sign; and placed beneath his name, in gilt capitals, the proud legend:

**CHEMIST BY EXAMINATION.**

He was, indeed, qualified. Examinations had never been his difficulty. Bottles did not expect urbanity, scales were indifferent to the expression of his face, and Latin names could be mastered without the necessity of pleasing them. Human beings were less reasonable.

His appearance was against him. He was narrow in the shoulders and broad in the joints, with colourless whiskers, a long upper lip, and pale eyes which seemed to accuse the object upon which they rested. Shyness made him stiff; stiffness made him appear contemptuous; the consciousness of appearing contemptuous rendered him angry; and anger brought into his voice a curious upward squeak, as if indignation were escaping through a small valve. He knew that he lacked ease, but believed ease to be a form of dishonesty. When other men smiled, he suspected them of practising upon him.

His domestic arrangements did not improve the effect. Behind the pharmacy were two rooms and a kitchen. He employed a fourteen-year-old maid named Rose, whose chief qualifications were cheapness and an aunt living nearby; and he engaged, for the shop, a youth named Wilkins, described by the registry office as “accustomed to the trade,” though the trade to which Wilkins had been chiefly accustomed was that of a greengrocer. Thus established, Mr. Farmiloe awaited prosperity.

The public came.

At eight-fifteen on his first morning a cook required a penny stamp. At eight-twenty a gentleman asked whether a letter posted then would reach Manchester by breakfast. At eight-twenty-seven three little girls presented savings-bank books, one of which contained a dead bluebottle. At nine a servant demanded change for a postal-order; at nine-six a lady wished to know the precise rate of book-post to New Zealand; at nine-eleven a builder’s foreman tendered a parcel which was too large; at nine-twelve he declared it was not; at nine-thirteen Farmiloe declared again that it was; and at nine-fourteen the foreman departed, announcing that some people gave themselves airs because they sold pills.

Not one purchased so much as a toothbrush.

By noon Mr. Farmiloe had sold three pennyworth of stamps, issued two postal-orders, weighed seven packages, explained the registration system four times, and refused a damaged half-sovereign. His pharmaceutical receipts were fourpence, contributed by an old gentleman who bought liquorice. At one o’clock, while he was eating bread and cheese behind the screen, the bell rang.

“Post-office?” called a voice.

“Yes,” said Farmiloe, swallowing hastily.

“I want the post-office.”

“This is it.”

“Then why is nobody here?”

Mr. Farmiloe appeared with the cheese still in his hand. The customer was Mrs. Rumbelow, the wife of an accountant and a lady accustomed to discovering inefficiency.

“I have been waiting,” she said.

“You have not been waiting half a minute.”

“That is not the point.”

“Then I don’t know what the point is.”

She purchased two halfpenny stamps with majestic restraint, and by teatime it was known in Beechwood Avenue that the new chemist took his meals in the post office and contradicted ladies.

Nevertheless, for a month he hoped. Hope in business is an elastic article, capable of stretching over figures which would tear common sense. People would become used to him; summer complaints would arrive; the children must have coughs; somebody, surely, would require a prescription. But Elmford Rise possessed two excellent physicians, and both sent their prescriptions elsewhere. Dr. Bunker favoured an old-established house beside the railway station, while Dr. Sloane believed in medicines compounded by his own dispenser. Farmiloe called upon them, wearing a frock-coat which pinched under the arms, and left his card.

Dr. Sloane was not at home. Dr. Bunker received him standing.

“You are the new man at the post office?”

“The new chemist,” said Farmiloe.

“Ah. Yes. Of course.”

“I am qualified by examination.”

“No doubt.”

“My dispensing is accurate.”

“It should be.”

This interview, which might have been endured by a happier man, supplied Mr. Farmiloe with grievance for a fortnight. That pestilent Dr. Bunker! That inflated fellow, with his gold watch-chain and his manner of examining a beetle! Farmiloe returned to his shop convinced that a professional conspiracy had been formed before his arrival.

Meanwhile the bell continued. The post-office, always the post-office.

A nursemaid wanted to telegraph that Master Cyril was sick. A widow wished to insure a parcel containing mourning. A schoolboy asked whether postage stamps could be boiled off envelopes without injury. Ladies arrived at six minutes after closing and tapped upon the glass; gentlemen came at seven minutes before opening and rattled the door. They required information, patience, string, sealing-wax, forms, explanations, forgiveness for illegible handwriting, and occasionally stamps. They rarely required medicine.

The postal authorities, unlike Providence, had forms for every disappointment. Complaints reached them. Mr. Farmiloe had closed four minutes early; Mr. Farmiloe had refused a packet which another office accepted; Mr. Farmiloe had said “Good heavens!” in the presence of a child; Mr. Farmiloe had no change; Mr. Farmiloe had change but gave it sulkily. An inspector called, a smooth-faced man with grey gloves, and observed that courtesy to the public formed part of the obligation attached to the appointment.

“Am I to be insulted in my own shop?” Farmiloe asked.

“You are not to be insulted.”

“I am.”

“I have no evidence of that.”

“I am telling you.”

“I am speaking of evidence.”

The good man raised his voice. The inspector lowered his. This difference gave the victory to the inspector, who departed after remarking that further representations might have serious consequences.

Farmiloe’s difficulties were not imaginary. The direct remuneration from the post office was trifling; the work consumed hours; Wilkins made errors; Rose broke bottles; rent and rates arrived with punctuality; and stock, purchased too generously from Mr. Cutlow, deteriorated upon the shelves. But misfortune, when it entered Mr. Farmiloe’s establishment, was never allowed to remain a single misfortune. He received it, questioned it fiercely, shook it before witnesses, and sent it into the neighbourhood multiplied.

When Mrs. Casterton’s parlour-maid asked for a box of pills on credit, he replied that he was not a charitable institution. When young Mr. Perks complained that a hair-wash had stained his collar, Farmiloe said the collar was already dirty. When the vicar’s wife suggested that the shop might look brighter with flowers, he answered that flowers did not dispense prescriptions. These statements contained elements of truth, which made them, in his opinion, irresistible. Elmford Rise, however, preferred agreeable falsehoods.

Wilkins, who had some instinct for self-preservation, left after three months. Farmiloe accused him of laziness, dishonesty, insolence, and smelling of onions. Wilkins accused Farmiloe of throwing a cork at him. The cork had been thrown, though not, Farmiloe explained, with intent to strike. It was enough. Within a week the butcher’s boy had heard that the chemist threw bottles; within two, that he sampled his own brandy; within three, that he was sometimes “not responsible.”

Rose remained because her aunt insisted. She moved about the back premises with the dumb apprehension of one living near machinery known to explode. Farmiloe saw fear in her face and resented it. He was lonely, but interpreted every sign of human withdrawal as an offence rather than a warning. At night he sat over his accounts, the shop dark around him except for the green glow of the lamp in the window, and added columns which would not improve. He had believed the inheritance would make him independent. Instead it had purchased creditors.

Occasionally he dreamed of shutting the post office. The dream had sunlight in it. He would remove the wire grille, fling away the scales, tear down the notices about foreign parcels, and devote himself to pharmacy. Then he remembered that without the postal appointment the business would be worth almost nothing. The chain by which he was dragged was also the thread from which he hung.

One wet November afternoon, when hope had shrunk to the dimensions of a match-end, fortune entered in the person of a footman from The Cedars, the largest house in Elmford Rise. Sir Joshua Pell, a manufacturer of agricultural machinery and a man whose illnesses were treated with respect, had received a prescription from a celebrated London physician. The footman laid it upon the counter.

“Wanted at six,” he said. “Particular.”

Farmiloe took the paper with a professional composure that concealed ecstasy. Here was the opening. The Pell household employed two footmen, five indoor servants, a coachman, a gardener, and innumerable female relatives, all subject to the ordinary ailments of prosperity. If the medicine pleased them—if the great physician’s prescription were accurately, elegantly, swiftly dispensed—the luckless apothecary might yet become the chemist of The Cedars. Dr. Bunker would hear of it. Elmford Rise would reconsider.

He read the prescription. The handwriting was villainous. Celebrated physicians, having raised obscurity into a distinction, appear to consider legibility beneath their station. Farmiloe deciphered the first ingredient, hesitated over the second, identified the third by its quantity, and set to work. There was no assistant now. Rose was peeling potatoes in the kitchen. Rain rattled against the glass; the green lamp trembled in the draught; upon the post-office counter lay three undelivered parcels, a savings-bank ledger, and a complaint form.

He measured, checked, and measured again. His hands, usually awkward before observers, were steady. The old training returned to him. For ten blessed minutes there was no public, no inspector, no book-post, no postal-order—only weights, liquids, glass, and the quiet exactitude of a craft he understood.

Then the bell clanged.

A woman entered, carrying a large parcel beneath one arm and leading a damp child with the other. She was Mrs. Priddle, a formidable resident of Acacia Grove, and her parcel was wrapped in brown paper, crossed by blue ribbon, and shaped like a badly folded bolster.

“I wish this sent to my sister at Torquay,” she said.

Farmiloe remained behind the screen. “One moment.”

“I am in a hurry.”

“One moment!”

He added the final ingredient to Sir Joshua’s mixture and held the bottle toward the light.

Mrs. Priddle struck the counter with a coin. “Are you attending to the post office or are you not?”

The bottle shook in his fingers.

“I am attending to a prescription.”

“I cannot help that.”

“No,” said Farmiloe, emerging, “you cannot help anything.”

“What did you say?”

“Put the parcel on the scales.”

She did so. It rolled off. The child laughed.

Farmiloe seized it, replaced it, and found it too long for the inland parcel regulations. He explained this, briefly and badly.

“It was accepted last Christmas.”

“Then it ought not to have been.”

“Are you refusing it?”

“I am.”

“You are bound to take it.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I’m not.”

“I shall report you.”

“Report me, then!” cried Farmiloe. “Report me to the Postmaster-General, the Prime Minister, the Archbishop of Canterbury! Take your parcel elsewhere and report the whole transaction. I have work of importance to do.”

Mrs. Priddle drew herself up. “I have never been spoken to in this manner.”

“Then it is time somebody began.”

She gathered the parcel, the child, and her dignity, though the last had suffered compression, and swept out. The bell gave a final violent jangle. Farmiloe stood breathing hard, thrilled for one instant by the dreadful luxury of having said exactly what he felt.

Then he looked at the bottle.

What had he added?

His eye travelled to the row of stoppered vessels. The bottle of tincture he required stood beside another of nearly the same colour. Had he taken the first—or the second? He remembered Mrs. Priddle’s coin striking the counter, the parcel rolling, the child’s laugh. He remembered replacing a stopper. Which stopper?

A coldness passed through him.

He snatched up the prescription and read it again. The never-sufficiently-to-be-condemned handwriting seemed now a pattern of hooks and graves. Suppose the word were not what he had thought? Suppose the quantity—no, impossible! Yet if he had used the stronger tincture, Sir Joshua might even now be drinking—

The footman had taken the medicine five minutes earlier.

Farmiloe rushed to the door. Rain struck his face. The footman was gone. Without hat or overcoat he tore along the pavement, collided with a baker’s boy, slipped at the corner, recovered himself, and sped toward The Cedars, carrying the prescription in one hand and waving the other at every uniformed figure ahead.

He reached the gates as the footman was crossing the drive.

“Stop!” screamed Farmiloe. “Stop that medicine!”

The lodge-keeper came out. A gardener stared from behind a barrow. The footman turned, and Farmiloe, muddy, wet, bareheaded, and gasping like a pursued criminal, wrenched the parcel from him.

“There has been a mistake,” he panted.

“A mistake?”

“My assistant—an incompetent young fool—may have put in the wrong ingredient.”

“You have no assistant,” said the footman.

“He has left. I mean—he returned—temporarily. Don’t stand there! It may be dangerous.”

The word travelled farther than his breath. A kitchen-maid heard it from an open side door; the gardener repeated it at the public-house; the lodge-keeper’s wife conveyed it to Mrs. Priddle’s cook. Farmiloe hurried back to the pharmacy and analysed the mixture.

It was perfectly correct.

He sat down on a packing-case, the rescued bottle between his knees, and began to laugh. The laugh was not cheerful. It rose in short squeaks, ceased, and returned until Rose appeared from the kitchen and retreated again. He had compounded the medicine accurately; but accuracy, being private, could not compete with panic performed before four witnesses.

By evening it was said that Mr. Farmiloe had sent poison to Sir Joshua Pell. By the next morning he had pursued the footman through the streets because he was intoxicated. Before Sunday, the story included a dying baronet, a dismissed assistant, a confession, and two policemen. Sir Joshua, who had never swallowed the medicine and was in no danger except from his ordinary habits, transferred his custom elsewhere. The postal authorities received Mrs. Priddle’s complaint together with three supporting letters. An inspector came again.

“There was no error,” Farmiloe insisted.

“You announced that there was.”

“I feared there might be.”

“You attributed it to an assistant.”

“I was agitated.”

“You have no assistant.”

“That has nothing to do with it.”

The inspector looked at him for a long moment. “It has everything to do with it.”

The appointment was withdrawn soon afterwards. Farmiloe protested, appealed, wrote six letters, and received two acknowledgments. Without the post office his traffic vanished; with its departure his reputation remained. Creditors pressed. The landlord became formal. Mr. Cutlow, consulted about repurchasing the business, regretted that altered circumstances made an offer impossible.

In March the stock and fittings were sold by auction. The gilt words **CHEMIST BY EXAMINATION** fetched nothing and were painted over. Mr. Farmiloe stood across the road while another man took possession—a plump, smiling person who placed flowers in the window and, within a year, secured the postal appointment once more.

Farmiloe went to a large northern town with eighty-three pounds, a box of clothes, and his certificates. He sought employment. Employers found him too old for a junior post, too difficult for authority, too nervous for dispensing, or merely unfortunate in appearance. Money diminished by rent, food, tram fares, postage, and the maintenance of a collar suitable for interviews. He moved from lodgings to a furnished room, and from the furnished room to a common lodging-house where no one required him to be civil because no one expected civility.

During a January frost he waited three hours outside a wholesale druggist’s office, having mistaken the day of an appointment. He returned soaked by sleet, developed inflammation of the lungs, and was taken to an infirmary. There, relieved at last of customers, creditors, inspectors, bells, parcels, prescriptions, and the obligation to make himself agreeable, he became quiet.

He died within the week.

After the infirmary had claimed its charges and the lodging-house keeper had been paid for his box, Mr. Farmiloe’s inheritance, business, opportunity, and independence were represented by a little more than four pounds. It was just enough money left to bury him.

## The True History of Miss Muffet

> Little Miss Muffet  
> Sat on a tuffet,  
> Eating her curds and whey;  
> There came a great spider,  
> And sat down beside her,  
> And frightened Miss Muffet away.

You have heard these lines, of course; but perhaps you have sometimes wondered why Miss Muffet sat upon a tuffet instead of a chair, and why she ate curds and whey instead of cake and jam. There is a reason for everything, even in a nursery rhyme, though grown persons do not always take the trouble to enquire into it.

Miss Muffet lived in a house so large that a stranger might easily lose himself between the front door and breakfast-room. It was built of grey stone, with marble steps, granite pillars, and enough windows to supply a small village. There were servants to open the doors, servants to shut them, servants to brush the carpets, servants to dust the ornaments, and servants to enquire what other servants had done.

Her father was a personage of great importance in the City. He went away every morning in a shining motor-car, and returned in time to dine, unless he dined at his club, which he generally did. Her mother was a beautiful lady who attended concerts, dinners, committees, bazaars, and fashionable lectures. When she remained at home she rested upon a sofa and read novels about neglected children.

Thus Miss Muffet, though she possessed a nursery full of costly toys, scarce knew either of her parents.

I cannot tell you her christened name. It may have been Arabella, or Gwendolen, or Mary Ann. Everyone called her Miss Muffet because Nurse Holloweg said that children of position ought not to be spoken to familiarly.

Nurse Holloweg was tall and stiff, and considered herself a personage of greater importance than Mr. Muffet, because he knew nothing whatever about children, while she knew everything. At least, she said she did.

“You must n’t run, Miss Muffet,” she would say. “You will grow heated.”

“You must n’t climb. You will tear your frock.”

“You must n’t dig. You will soil your gloves.”

“You must n’t shout. Young ladies never shout.”

“You must n’t play with those children at the gate. There is no knowing who their grandfathers were.”

So Miss Muffet walked when she wished to run, whispered when she wished to shout, and stared from the nursery window while other children rolled hoops, made mud pies, chased butterflies, quarrelled, made friends again, and enjoyed themselves in a dozen improper ways.

She had a velvet coat, a fur muff, a gold bracelet, a silver mug, and a rocking-horse which she was forbidden to rock too violently. Yet she was pale, thin, cross, and tired. She did not want her breakfast, she disliked her dinner, and at tea she ate three sugared cakes because Nurse Holloweg said she must eat something.

“Such an ungrateful child!” said Nurse.

“Such a tiresome child!” said Mrs. Muffet.

“Give her a tonic,” said Mr. Muffet, who was signing cheques.

Accordingly, a famous doctor was summoned. He arrived in a carriage, felt Miss Muffet’s pulse, looked at her tongue, tapped her chest, and enquired whether she enjoyed Latin.

“No,” said Miss Muffet.

“Ah!” said the doctor solemnly.

He then asked whether she enjoyed boiled mutton.

“No,” said Miss Muffet.

“Ah!” said the doctor more solemnly still.

At last he put on his spectacles and looked at the child instead of at his watch.

“She requires fresh air, plain food, exercise, cheerful companionship, and freedom from unnecessary restraint.”

“What medicine is that?” asked Mrs. Muffet.

“It is not a medicine,” replied the doctor. “Send her into the country.”

Mrs. Muffet sighed. She had hoped that a bottle might do as well; but a cottage was taken near a country village, and Nurse Holloweg went there with Miss Muffet.

Unfortunately, Nurse took all her rules with her.

The cottage stood among green meadows where lambs skipped, colts galloped, and village children paddled in a brook. Miss Muffet was allowed to observe these things through an open window.

“May I skip with the lambs?” she asked.

“Certainly not.”

“May I run with the colts?”

“You would be kicked.”

“May I paddle with the children?”

“You would catch your death.”

“Then what may I do?”

“You may walk quietly along the gravel path until it is time for your nap.”

This was country life as arranged by Nurse Holloweg.

One warm morning Nurse placed Miss Muffet upon a bench beneath a chestnut tree and told her to remain there while she went indoors to write a letter. The letter was to Mrs. Muffet and described the great benefit Miss Muffet was receiving from perfect quiet.

No sooner had Nurse disappeared than a yellow cart came slowly down the lane. A red-faced farmer sat upon the front board, and behind him were empty baskets, a bundle of straw, and a friendly brown dog.

Miss Muffet had never ridden in a farmer’s cart. Indeed, Nurse Holloweg would have considered the very thought dangerous.

That was why it seemed delightful.

“Where are you going?” she called.

“Home to Hawthorn Farm,” said the farmer.

“Is it far?”

“Three miles, miss.”

“May I come?”

The farmer scratched his head.

“Do your people know?”

“No,” said Miss Muffet candidly. “They never know what I am doing.”

This appeared so melancholy an answer that the farmer lifted her into the cart. She hid herself among the baskets, the dog licked her chin, and away they went, bumping over ruts and under hedges white with blossom.

For the first time in her life Miss Muffet went somewhere without permission. The wind blew her hair loose, the wheels jolted her from side to side, and the dog trod upon her shoes. She had never been so untidy or so happy.

Hawthorn Farm had no granite pillars. It had a thatched roof, a kitchen smelling of bread, a yard full of hens, and a great many flies. The farmer’s wife stood at the dairy door with her sleeves rolled above her elbows.

“Who is this?” she asked.

“A little maid wanting a visit,” said the farmer.

“A visit!” cried his wife. “This is cheese-making day. Visitors must make themselves useful.”

Miss Muffet was delighted. No one had ever expected her to be useful before.

She was given a large apron and permitted to carry a wooden bowl. She watched the warm milk thicken in the vat, and when it had set, the farmer’s wife cut it into shining white curds.

“May I stir it?” asked Miss Muffet.

“You may, if you stir steadily.”

Miss Muffet stirred. At first it was charming. Then her arm began to ache.

Next she helped fill the moulds. The curds slipped between her fingers and spotted her apron.

“This is better than embroidery,” she said.

“Cheese is generally more nourishing,” replied the farmer’s wife.

Then there were pails to carry, cloths to rinse, dishes to scour, and whey to give the pigs. Miss Muffet ran from dairy to yard and from yard to kitchen. Nobody told her not to hurry. Nobody feared that she might grow heated. She grew very heated indeed.

At noon she ate bread and cold bacon at the kitchen table. She was so hungry that she forgot to dislike plain food.

After dinner she fed the hens, gathered eggs, chased a duck from the pantry, and attempted to pump water. By four o’clock her arms ached, her feet dragged, and a scratch had appeared upon one knee.

Farm life, she began to perceive, was not entirely composed of riding upon straw and talking to dogs.

“You look tired,” said the farmer’s wife.

“I think,” said Miss Muffet with dignity, “that I have been useful enough for one day.”

“So do I,” said the good woman. “You have earned your supper, at any rate.”

She filled a blue bowl with fresh curds, poured whey over them, and gave Miss Muffet a wooden spoon.

“Sit outside while I finish the milking.”

Near the dairy stood a little grassy mound, round and green, with violets growing upon one side. Such a mound was called a tuffet in that part of the country, though perhaps it is not called so where you live.

Miss Muffet sat upon it, placed the bowl upon her lap, and began to eat.

The curds were cool and soft, the whey was sweet, and the evening sun shone through the orchard trees. A bee hummed near the clover. A cow rang its bell. Miss Muffet felt tired, hungry, useful, and important.

“This,” she said to herself, “is much better than cake.”

Now, beneath the dairy roof lived a spider. He was an honest spider and had never frightened anyone intentionally. He caught flies, minded his own affairs, and knew nothing of young ladies of position. Seeing the white bowl, he supposed there might be a fly near it, so he let himself down by a silken thread.

Miss Muffet looked up.

There, close beside her, hung the awfulest, biggest, blackest spider that ever appeared to a tired little girl at supper-time.

It had eight legs. Miss Muffet had never had any dealings with a creature possessing more than four.

The spider descended.

Miss Muffet stared.

The spider reached the tuffet and sat down beside her.

Miss Muffet gave one shriek, flung spoon, curds, whey, and bowl into the grass, and fled towards the farmhouse as fast as her aching legs could carry her.

The spider, who had been even more alarmed by Miss Muffet than Miss Muffet was by the spider, fled in the opposite direction. But no rhyme has ever mentioned that.

At that very moment a carriage drove into the yard. Nurse Holloweg sprang out, followed by a constable, a gardener, two footmen, and a village boy who had been promised sixpence if he found the missing child.

Nurse had discovered the empty bench after finishing her letter. Since then she had searched lanes, cottages, ponds, hayfields, and ditches. She had pictured Miss Muffet drowned, stolen, lost, or adopted by gipsies. Worse still, she had pictured herself explaining the matter to Mrs. Muffet.

“Miss Muffet!” she cried.

“Nurse!” cried Miss Muffet.

And Miss Muffet rushed into her arms.

For half a minute Nurse hugged her so tightly that neither of them could speak. Then Nurse remembered her dignity.

“You naughty, disobedient, dreadful child! How could you run away?”

“How could you make me want to?” replied Miss Muffet.

This was not the answer Nurse expected.

“You might have been killed.”

“I might have been happy at home.”

“You have ruined your frock.”

“I helped make cheese.”

“You have scratched your knee.”

“I gathered seven eggs.”

“And what were you doing when we arrived?”

“Running away from a spider.”

Nurse Holloweg looked at the overturned bowl, the grassy tuffet, and the black spider hurrying up the dairy wall.

“You will return with me this instant,” she said.

“Yes,” replied Miss Muffet readily, for the farmer’s wife had just called that there were pails to scour. “But I shall run away again unless I may run when I am at home.”

Nurse gasped.

“And romp,” continued Miss Muffet.

“Miss Muffet!”

“And dig. And paddle. And play with the children at the gate, without asking who their grandfathers were.”

Nurse looked at the constable, the footmen, the gardener, the farmer, the farmer’s wife, and the village boy. They all looked back at Nurse.

At last she said, “Very well. Within reason.”

“Whose reason?” asked Miss Muffet.

Nurse sighed.

“Yours and mine together.”

So Miss Muffet returned to the cottage. From that day she ran in the meadow, climbed low trees, dug deep holes, paddled in the brook, and played with children whose grandfathers remained a mystery. She tore three frocks, lost one shoe, bruised both knees, and became disgracefully well.

Her cheeks grew rosy, her eyes sparkled, and she ate porridge, mutton, bread, apples, and almost anything else that was placed before her. Nurse Holloweg discovered that a tired child sleeps better than an imprisoned one, and therefore found life much easier.

When the famous doctor came again, he felt Miss Muffet’s pulse and looked at her tongue.

“Ah!” he said.

Then Miss Muffet escaped from him to join a game of chase.

“A remarkable cure,” said Mrs. Muffet.

“Entirely due to my advice,” said the doctor, and charged an extra-large fee.

Mr. Muffet signed the cheque without looking at it.

As for the spider, he returned to the tuffet when everyone had gone and found one small curd left in the grass. Whether spiders care for curds and whey I do not pretend to know. But he had certainly earned some supper, for by frightening Miss Muffet away he helped frighten Nurse Holloweg into good sense.

And that is why the rhyme tells us of the tuffet, the curds and whey, the great spider, and the fleeing little maid—but says nothing of the much more important thing Miss Muffet found that day, which was freedom.

The post office at Ulapur occupied one end of a low thatched shed, the other end being used by the indigo factory’s storekeeper for broken tools, empty baskets, and certain earthen jars whose former purpose no one remembered. Behind it lay a green and slimy pond, visited by ducks, mosquitoes, and small boys in equal numbers. On three sides bamboo thickets pressed close, whispering all day even when there was no wind; on the fourth, a narrow path went wandering through the fields until it lost itself among plantain groves and distant huts.

Into this place came a young postmaster from Calcutta.

The factory required a post office, and Government, having satisfied itself upon the matter, sent him to conduct it. But Government did not consider whether a young man accustomed to paved streets, evening lamps, and the voices of educated companions might thrive among frogs, factory clerks, and cultivators whose speech he scarcely understood.

His pay was small. His duties were fewer still. Each morning he sorted a handful of letters, stamped the envelopes, entered them in a large book, and waited for the runner who came barefoot along the muddy road. Thereafter the hours stretched before him like an empty field under the noonday sun.

The men of the factory were busy, and, besides, their manners did not please him. He himself was too shy to seek friendship among the villagers and too proud to confess how much he wanted it. Thus it happened that, though surrounded by human beings, he lived as one cast upon a little island.

His household consisted of two brass plates, an earthen water-pot, a carpet bag, a tin trunk, a narrow wooden bed, and Ratan.

Ratan was an orphan girl of twelve or thirteen who swept the room, fetched water, lighted the cooking fire, and sometimes prepared the evening meal. In return she received food, old clothes when such treasures were available, and no settled wages. She had nowhere else to go. Of her parents she remembered little: a father who returned tired at dusk, a mother whose face had grown dim in her memory, and a younger brother with whom she had once played beside a flooded ditch.

At first the postmaster scarcely noticed her. He called, and she came; he ordered, and she obeyed.

“Ratan!”

From the threshold her voice would answer, “Did you call me, Dada?”

“Light the fire.”

“The sticks are damp.”

“Then dry them.”

“With what shall I dry them, Dada?”

Such exchanges, insignificant as fallen leaves, made up their early acquaintance.

In the evenings, when smoke from the village cooking fires hung beneath the bamboo tops and the cowherds returned singing along the path, the postmaster would sit outside the shed. The tired earth seemed then to draw a long breath. Insects began their monotonous orchestra; somewhere among the dark branches a solitary bird uttered one repeated note, as though it also had been appointed to Ulapur and had applied in vain for a transfer.

At such an hour the young man’s thoughts travelled homeward. He saw his mother sitting by the window, his elder brother bending over a newspaper, his little sister quarrelling about some household trifle. Faces neglected amid the abundance of home became precious in exile. He even attempted verses upon solitude, leaves, clouds, and the melancholy contentment of rural life. Yet had a benevolent giant uprooted the bamboo, paved the pond, and erected a Calcutta street before his door, the poet in him would have perished of gratitude.

One evening he called Ratan, though he required nothing.

She was blowing upon the kitchen fire. “Did you call me, Dada?”

“What are you doing?”

“Cooking your supper.”

“Leave it for a while. Come here.”

She sat upon the ground near the door, careful not to presume beyond the proper distance.

“Do you remember your mother?” he asked.

Ratan considered. “A little. She was often ill.”

“And your father?”

“I remember him better. He used to come home after dark. Once he made me a fishing rod.”

From that fishing rod there emerged, piece by piece, a whole vanished world: her brother’s bare knees, the ditch where they pretended to catch fish, the storm that carried off part of their roof, the copper bangle her mother had promised her but never bought. Some memories were true, some perhaps shaped themselves under the warmth of being heard. God knows by what humble industry lonely hearts furnish their empty rooms.

Then Ratan asked about his home.

The postmaster spoke of his mother, brother, and sister. On later evenings she asked again, until these unseen persons became familiar to her. She called them Mother, Brother, and Little Sister, as if they had already made a place for her beside their Calcutta lamp. Sometimes she formed pictures of them and asked whether Mother wore her hair in a knot, whether Little Sister was taller than herself, whether Brother spoke sternly.

The postmaster, amused, answered without considering how securely each answer was being stored away.

One afternoon, during a lull in the rains, he said, “Ratan, I shall teach you to read.”

Her face grew solemn with delight.

He wrote letters of the alphabet upon a slate. She confused one with another, frowned, repeated them under her breath, and traced them with a finger upon the dusty floor. Soon she was joining letters into words. The dog-eared primer passed between them nightly, and the dim lamp, smoking beneath the thatch, shone upon two heads bent over the same page.

Whenever duties kept him occupied, she waited outside, whispering her lessons so that she might astonish him when he called.

“Ratan!”

She entered at once. “Did you call me, Dada?”

“Bring the book.”

“I have already learnt the next line.”

“Well, let us see.”

Thus the days went by, tied together with slender threads of habit.

Then came the monsoon.

Clouds assembled over Ulapur like armies. Rain fell upon the thatch, fields, pond, and bamboo with a noise that swallowed every lesser sound. The paths dissolved into mud. The pond climbed its banks and spread among the grasses. Frogs cried out from invisible courts, and the river, formerly a modest stream beyond the village, rose brown and impatient, carrying branches, straw, drowned insects, and once the roof of a cowshed.

The postmaster remained shut inside his room. The wet air clung to him; his clothes smelt of smoke and mildew. One afternoon, when the rain had paused and the washed leaves glittered in a pale light, he felt a chill pass through his body.

He called faintly, “Ratan.”

There was no answer. He called again.

She entered with her primer beneath her arm. “I have learnt—”

Seeing his face, she stopped.

“Feel my forehead,” he said.

The little pupil became at once a nurse. She fetched the village doctor, standing beneath his eaves until he agreed to come. She boiled water, measured medicine, prepared thin gruel, and sat beside the bed through the wet nights. When he muttered in fever, she bent close, trying to understand. When he woke, he found her on the floor, her back against the wall and her head fallen upon her knees.

“Ratan,” he whispered once.

“Yes, Dada?”

“Is it morning?”

“Not yet. Go to sleep.”

Her voice had acquired the tenderness of a mother’s. Inscrutable are the ways of a woman’s heart: in the body of an orphan child, deprived of all right to protect or command, there had awakened an ancient patience.

For several days she forgot her lessons. The slate lay beneath the bed while she tended the fire, washed his cup, changed the damp cloth upon his head, and watched his breathing. No service was too small, because each service assured her that she was necessary.

The fever left him at last, but weakness remained. As he sat looking through the doorway at the rain-darkened pond, the village appeared more intolerable than before. Illness had stripped solitude of its poetry. He longed for his mother’s hand, for familiar rooms, for a street where a doctor could be summoned without argument.

He wrote requesting a transfer.

After the letter had gone, he waited with impatience. Ratan resumed her place outside, repeating old lessons, though he seldom called her to read. Her ear remained attentive to every movement within. If he sighed, shifted the tin trunk, or dropped his pen, she looked up.

At length the reply came. His request was denied.

That evening he sat unusually silent. The rain dripped through a new hole in the thatch into an earthen vessel—tap, tap, tap.

“Ratan,” he called.

“Did you call me, Dada?”

“I am going home.”

She looked at him. “When?”

“Tomorrow.”

“When will you come back?”

“I am not coming back.”

The vessel beneath the leak continued its measured tapping. He explained that his transfer had been refused, and therefore he had resigned. He spoke lightly, almost cheerfully, as one speaks after settling a tiresome matter.

Ratan asked no more. She went to prepare supper, but the fire would not catch. Smoke filled her eyes; or perhaps it was not the smoke alone. The bread she made was misshapen, and the postmaster, preoccupied with departure, did not notice.

Late that night she entered and said, “Dada, will you take me with you?”

The notion appeared to him so impossible, so unrelated to the arrangements of real life, that he laughed.

“Take you with me! What an idea!”

He did not explain. All night the careless laughter remained in her ears.

Before sunrise she fetched water from the river. The postmaster preferred river water for bathing, and she wished that on his last morning nothing should be neglected. The earthen vessel was heavy, the bank slippery, and rain began before she returned; yet when he rose, the water stood ready.

After bathing he called her.

“Ratan, you need not worry. I shall tell the new postmaster to look after you as I have done.”

He intended kindness. Indeed, he felt pleased that he had remembered her future. But there are wounds which the hand of consolation only presses deeper.

She flushed and cried, “No! You must not tell him anything. I do not want to stay here.”

He stared at her. Never had she spoken so sharply.

Throughout the morning she moved about the rooms, now folding a cloth, now standing idle, now going to the kitchen and forgetting why. The new incumbent arrived near noon, a stout man carrying an umbrella and complaining of the road. The accounts were explained, the seals handed over, the official books inspected. These grave matters completed, the former postmaster packed his carpet bag. A porter took the tin trunk.

Before leaving, he called Ratan once more.

She came, but remained by the door.

“I have not much,” he said, “but take this. It may help you for some days.”

He drew out the greater part of his remaining salary.

Ratan fell at his feet.

“No, Dada! I beg you, do not give me anything. Do not ask anyone to care for me.”

Then she rose and fled behind the bamboo thicket.

The postmaster stood embarrassed, holding the money. Her refusal seemed unreasonable; yet something in it made him ashamed. He put the coins away, lifted his bag, and, with the porter carrying his trunk and the old blue umbrella, walked toward the river.

A boat waited at the landing. Rain had swollen the current until it rushed level with the reeds, like a stream of tears overflowing the earth. His luggage was placed beneath the curved matting. The boatman pushed off, and Ulapur began to recede—the landing, the sodden fields, the bamboo, the thatched shed wherein so small a portion of his life had passed.

Then a pain touched his heart.

The image of the orphan girl, her face wet with grief, rose before him. For one instant he thought: I shall go back. I shall take her with me.

The wind filled the sail. The current hurried them onward. Already the landing had vanished behind a bend, and on the distant bank appeared the smoking place of the dead.

The traveller looked at the river and consoled himself with reflections. In this world, he thought, meetings and partings are perpetual. Who belongs to whom? Death itself separates us at last. Such philosophy is always at hand when the boat is moving with the current and another person has been left upon the shore.

Alas for our foolish human nature! We surrender slowly to truth. We nourish impossible hope, clasp it to the breast, and pay it with our tears; only after it has consumed our peace do we tear ourselves away—and even then we look back.

Ratan had not torn herself away.

She wandered about the post office, passing the pond, the kitchen, and the doorway where she had so often waited with her book. Perhaps Dada would return. Perhaps the boat had been delayed. Perhaps he had forgotten something—his pen, a letter, the little primer beneath the bed.

Again and again she listened for his call.

But the new postmaster spoke to the porter, rain whispered in the bamboo, and water dropped steadily from the roof into the earthen vessel.

No one called her.

Still she remained nearby, her eyes swollen, her heart refusing the easy wisdom of travellers. For he could turn separation into a thought and carry it lightly away; she, having no philosophy and no home, was obliged to live within it.

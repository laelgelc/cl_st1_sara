## THE THREE LIBERATIONS

The cage containing Romulus, the celebrated anthropoid of Professor Bellamy’s Imperial Circus and Congress of Educated Beasts, was jolting southward through the Santa Clara Valley when a wheel struck a stone, a chain leaped from its staple, and Providence—who sometimes employs very small instruments in the correction of very large injustices—opened an iron door by the breadth of two fingers.

Two fingers were sufficient for Romulus.

The circus wagons had halted beside a dusty road while men disputed over an axle. Horses drooped in the noon glare; a brass cornet, somewhere beneath a canvas, gave one melancholy note whenever its wagon rocked; and the keepers, hot, thirsty, and profane, had gone forward to offer advice which nobody wanted. Romulus watched them through the bars.

He had watched men all his life.

He knew the red-faced man who fed him, the thin man who whipped him, the painted lady who kissed her hand to him before applauding crowds, and the grave gentleman who announced that Romulus possessed “almost human intelligence.” Of humanity itself the ape had formed no flattering opinion. Humanity was a smell of tobacco and grease; a ring of faces; a stick; a chain; laughter when he obeyed and blows when he did not. Yet he had learned one excellent human art—the art of waiting.

He waited now.

The loosened chain swung. His brown fingers entered the narrow interval; his wrist followed, then the long, sinewy arm. He lifted, twisted, pressed, and drew back. The chain slipped again. There was a little metallic complaint, scarcely louder than the cicada’s shrilling in the mustard weeds.

Romulus paused.

No keeper came. The cornet groaned. A mule shook its bells.

Then the door yielded.

He descended so softly that the dust received no more than four dark impressions; crossed the shadow of the wagon; passed beneath the drooping head of a gray horse, which regarded him with one mild and incurious eye; and gained the roadside ditch. A moment afterward the tall mustard closed behind him.

Then the whole world lay before him.

Ah, my friends, we who inherit the horizon and pay no tax for the blue magnificence above us cannot understand the astonishment of a creature to whom heaven had always been divided by iron rods! The valley seemed not merely wide but unencompassed. It spread in wheat, orchard, vineyard, brown road and emerald ditch; beyond it rose the eastern mountains, blue at their foundations and pale with heat toward their summits; and high upon Mount Hamilton the observatory shone like a silver pin fastening earth to sky.

Romulus crouched, bewildered by abundance.

A lizard darted over a stone. He sprang backward, then forward, and with immense caution touched the stone where the lizard had been. A butterfly floated past, absurdly confident in two painted scraps of wing. Romulus followed it through the mustard, first on all fours, then upright, then with arms extended; and because its flight appeared neither laborious nor remarkable, he climbed a small oak, launched himself from a bough, and discovered that liberty does not abolish gravity.

He fell into dry grass, rolled twice, and sat up with an expression of injured dignity.

The butterfly continued toward San José.

Romulus examined his elbows. Finding them serviceable, he climbed again, but this time without attempting the imperious privileges of the air. From the upper branches he could see the circus wagons, little and glaring upon the road. Men ran about them. One waved his arms; another pointed toward the fields; a third beat the mustard with a pole, perhaps believing that the whole green valley could be searched as one searches beneath a bed.

At the sight of them Romulus flattened himself against the bole.

Fear returned—but it was a new fear, for it had distance in it. Before, fear had been locked in the cage with him. Now fear was down there, while he was up here; fear was small, noisy, and deceived; fear could pass along the road and leave him behind.

He grinned.

When the wagons at last creaked onward, scattering curses, dust, and advertisements for the evening performance, Romulus descended into his kingdom.

He ate green almonds and rejected them; tasted a rose and sneezed; drank from an irrigation channel, first slapping the water because it moved, then plunging his face into it because he was thirsty. He invaded a hen-yard and fled from an indignant rooster. He discovered a scarecrow, approached it with profound suspicion, and knocked off its hat; perceiving no retaliation, he removed the coat also and carried it for half a mile before abandoning it as uselessly human.

A farm dog rushed upon him, magnificent with duty. Romulus stood upright.

The dog stopped.

There are moments when inherited courage must negotiate with immediate evidence. The dog barked once, less magnificently; Romulus opened his mouth and advanced; and the defender of the homestead, suddenly remembering urgent business in another county, departed beneath a fence with such alacrity that he left a tuft of yellow hair upon the wire.

Romulus climbed a pear tree and ate until appetite became philosophy. The orchard roof, cool and vermicular with interlacing shadow, enclosed him; bees labored among fallen fruit; dust drifted gold beyond the leaves. No drum commanded him to dance. No trainer required him to wear a military jacket. He might sleep, leap, eat, scratch, or contemplate the universe. Freedom, in its first hour, is chiefly the absence of interruption.

It was then that he heard a laugh.

It was not the cruel, explosive laughter of the circus ring. It rolled over the field in immense, unguarded waves, ceased, and began again. Romulus peered from the pear tree.

Beyond the orchard stood a long, white building with narrow windows, a yard of beaten earth, and a fence higher than a man’s head. Within that fence a young man was pushing a wheelbarrow. He was a giant in a shapeless blue coat: broad of shoulder, heavy of hand, loose of mouth, and slow in every movement except laughter. A dead leaf had fallen upon his nose, and this occurrence delighted him beyond measure.

His name was Moses.

The county had confined him because his mind did not resemble the minds of those who signed papers. He could carry wood, scrub floors, dig trenches, and endure ridicule; he could not reckon money, repeat rules, or explain why an unkind action became respectable when ordered by an official. Therefore society, which forgives wickedness more readily than confusion, had put him behind a fence.

Romulus saw the fence. He saw the blue coat. He saw, too, a keeper crossing the yard with a strap.

The ape’s amusement vanished.

Moses had overturned the wheelbarrow while laughing. The keeper seized his ear, struck him twice, and ordered him to lift it. Moses obeyed. Presently the keeper went away, leaving the young man to stare at the dust with the submissive desolation of one who has never understood his punishments.

Romulus descended.

He approached through the orchard, pausing whenever Moses turned. At last only the fence divided them. Moses saw a brown face between the boards, blinked solemnly, and put one thick finger to his lips. Romulus put a finger to his own lips. Moses smiled. Romulus displayed his teeth. Moses laughed, though softly this time, and offered through the gap a crust of bread.

Romulus accepted it.

Thus was their treaty concluded.

Neither could speak, and hence neither could lie to the other. What need had they of declarations? Each knew the striped garment of servitude, the sudden hand, the regulated meal, the narrow heaven. Romulus touched the board, then the chain at Moses’s waist; Moses touched the board, then Romulus’s scarred shoulder. Some philosophers require volumes to establish brotherhood. These two required a crust and a scar.

The fence was stout. Moses was stouter.

Romulus indicated a loosened board, tugged it, failed, and tugged again. Moses understood. He set both hands upon it. His first effort made the nails cry; his second bowed the plank; his third tore it inward with a shower of splinters. Romulus sprang back in admiration. Moses widened the breach, squeezing through until his blue coat remained hooked behind him like a flag surrendered to captivity.

These two, then, were at large.

I will not tell particularly all that they did during the golden and calamitous afternoon. A farmer found his water-gate lifted and his bean-field converted into a marsh; six schoolboys found their lunch pails empty; a peddler discovered his wagon rearranged in a tree; and a dignified constable, pursuing what he believed to be two vagrants, sat down unexpectedly in a trough. Whether Romulus pulled away the ladder, whether Moses innocently carried it elsewhere, or whether the ladder itself revolted against civic authority, I shall not decide.

They liberated three goats, eleven rabbits, a turkey, and a mechanical reaper.

The reaper could not appreciate liberty. It descended a slope, crossed a cabbage patch, and came to rest in a pond.

Moses laughed until he lay helpless among the poppies. Romulus rolled beside him, beating the earth with his hands. Their mirth alarmed a flock of blackbirds, which ascended in one dark sheet; it disturbed an old mare, which galloped twenty yards and then reconsidered; it even brought a smile to the face of a laborer who had no cause to smile and no permission to stop working.

They climbed trees. Moses climbed badly, but with confidence; Romulus climbed exquisitely and mocked him from above. They ate grapes warm from the vine, staining their mouths purple. They chased their own shadows along a white road. Moses placed wildflowers behind Romulus’s ears, and Romulus crowned Moses with vine leaves. A woman saw them through her kitchen window, dropped a plate, and barred the door; whereupon Moses gravely barred the gate from the outside, an act which pleased him for twenty minutes.

Above the valley the observatory had changed from silver to copper.

All afternoon men searched. The circus keeper visited farms, saloons, sheds and stations, describing Romulus as ferocious because ferocity excused the whip he carried. The asylum attendants searched roads and culverts, describing Moses as dangerous because danger excused the institution that had lost him. Each fugitive was in truth capable of harm, as are horses, engines, magistrates, and frightened men; but neither desired it. They desired only the inexhaustible next thing.

Toward evening the next thing became hunger.

They crossed a road, entered a grove of cypress and pepper trees, and found themselves upon a low rise overlooking a Chinese burial ground. The place was humble, lying outside the more honored cemetery as though prejudice persisted even among the dead. There were mounds of yellow earth, little boards marked with characters, remnants of incense, bowls cracked by weather, and paper offerings trembling in the evening wind. Here men exiled from their ancestral villages attempted, with scant ground and sorrowful ceremony, to open a door toward home.

Romulus and Moses had climbed a great pepper tree when the procession arrived.

First came men bearing lanterns and folded papers; then an old man with a bowed head; then women whose grief moved in low, continuous cries. Last walked a mother beside a little coffin.

She was the mother of Wang Tai, who was seven years old.

What language did that grief speak? Not Chinese, surely, nor English; not any tongue which officials print upon forms or preachers divide into doctrines. Every mother has a mother’s heart. When she stretched both hands toward the little coffin, the gesture was older than kingdoms and clearer than speech. Moses ceased smiling. Romulus drew closer to him. Neither understood death, perhaps; but both understood the outstretched hand that cannot reach what it loves.

The grave had been dug shallowly.

The Christian cemetery beyond the road would not receive the child, and Christian law—so it was explained with gravity—required no more than three feet of earth in this excluded ground. Three feet! Measure, if you please, the depth of civilization; the breadth of mercy; the distance between one mother’s grief and another’s. You may sometimes find them all in a statute, neatly numbered.

The coffin was lowered. Earth struck the lid with a dull, intolerable sound. The mother swayed, but women held her. The men burned papers, set out bowls of rice, roast fowl, cakes, oranges and tea, and placed incense where its blue threads might guide a little spirit. Then the mourners departed slowly, the mother looking backward until the cypress trunks concealed the mound.

Night gathered.

The Mount Hamilton observatory was gold for a moment, blazing above the darkening ridge like a celestial palace; then it became ebony against the last red window of heaven.

Romulus waited until every footfall had died. He descended at once, for grief is solemn but roast fowl is fragrant, and an empty stomach makes an indifferent theologian. Moses followed, sliding the last six feet and landing upon his back.

The offerings provided a banquet. Romulus ate rice with both hands, sampled incense, rejected it vehemently, and devoted himself to the chicken. Moses swallowed cakes whole, peeled an orange with ponderous care, and gave half to his companion. They drank the cold tea; they investigated the papers; they put lantern frames upon their heads. If spirits witnessed this desecration, perhaps they distinguished hunger from malice more readily than men do.

Behind the grave, beneath a mat, Romulus found two spades.

A spade was familiar to Moses. Digging had occupied much of his institutional life, although nothing he planted had belonged to him. He seized one with pleasure and drove it into the fresh mound. Romulus, delighted by the flying soil, copied him with the other.

Why did they dig?

Was it because the earth had recently been moved? Because the concealed spades suggested their purpose? Because food had come from that mound and more food might remain within? Or did some obscure tremor, too faint for wiser senses, rise through the soil to the hands of the ape? I cannot tell. Those who know everything about instinct generally possess very little of it.

They dug.

Moses’s strength sent great shovelfuls aside. Romulus scratched, scooped, tugged at roots, and repeatedly interfered. The moon rose. An owl called from the cypress. Once both fugitives stopped and listened, but the sound they heard—if sound there was—did not return.

At last the spades struck wood.

Romulus leaped into the excavation and beat upon the lid. Moses joined him. Together they clawed earth from the edges, broke the thin fastening with the spade, and lifted.

What they saw within I will not tell particularly.

There was a little face, a silken jacket, black hair arranged for death, and hands folded by those who expected no resistance. Romulus touched the cheek and recoiled. Moses bent near. His great head turned as though listening. Then, with surprising gentleness, he lifted the small body from the coffin.

A breath may be less than a moth’s wing and yet heavier than the world.

Moses laid Wang Tai upon the ground. Romulus pulled at her jacket, patted her face, and, remembering perhaps the rough methods by which circus physicians had restored exhausted animals, rolled her over. Moses imitated him. They shook her, rubbed her hands, pressed her narrow chest, and quarreled silently over the proper manner of doing what neither understood.

The child gave no cry.

They persisted because they were ignorant. A learned man might have pronounced death and departed; innocence has the advantage of not knowing when effort is respectable to abandon.

At length Wang Tai coughed.

Romulus fled up the tree.

Moses fell backward, stared, and began to laugh—not loudly, but in little frightened bursts. The child coughed again, breathed, and opened her eyes upon moonlight, an empty grave, a crowned giant in a torn blue coat, and an ape peering upside down from a branch. It was not a reasonable world to awaken in. Nevertheless, it was the world.

She tried to cry, but only sighed.

Moses wrapped her in the burial cloth. Romulus descended by cautious stages and touched her hair. She was warm now. Whether she smiled at him, or whether moonlight merely altered the stillness of her face, let each reader decide according to the mercy in him.

Exhaustion conquered them. Moses lay beside the mound with Wang Tai against his breast. Romulus curled at his feet, one hand resting on the burial cloth. Above them the pepper leaves whispered; beyond them the valley slept beneath a pale mist; and the three captives, escaped by iron, plank, and spade, entered for a few hours the republic of dreams.

Morning came yellow over Santa Clara.

It gilded the vineyard stakes, kindled the windows of farmhouses, and turned every thread of the observatory dome to silver once more. It also illuminated a spectacle which caused a cemetery caretaker to drop his hoe and run shouting toward the road.

Within half an hour the place was filled with authority.

There came sheriff’s men, asylum attendants, circus keepers, curious farmers, a physician, two reporters, and several citizens who knew precisely what had occurred before they had seen it. They found the grave violated, the coffin open, the offerings consumed, Moses asleep in the soil and Romulus showing his teeth from a low branch.

The circus keeper struck Romulus with a weighted strap. The ape screamed, sprang, and was caught in a net. Four men chained him. The asylum attendants dragged Moses upright and beat him for running away, for tearing the fence, for resisting, and finally for failing to understand why he was beaten.

Thus civilization reclaimed its own.

Then the burial cloth moved.

The physician pushed through the crowd. He knelt, uncovered the child, touched her throat, and shouted for water. Wang Tai opened her eyes. A silence fell so complete that the pepper leaves could be heard brushing one another overhead.

The mother arrived while they were still carrying Wang Tai from the grave.

She came running without hat or dignity, her hair loosened, her arms extended in the ancient gesture; and when the child stirred and knew her, the cry that passed between them seemed to rend the morning from mountain to mountain. Men looked away. Even the circus keeper lowered his strap.

It was concluded afterward, with many signatures and much official surprise, that Wang Tai had fallen into a deathlike trance; that haste, poverty, exclusion, and error had placed her in the coffin alive; that the shallow grave had admitted enough air to sustain the faint spark within her; and that the two criminals, while robbing the dead, had opened the coffin and restored her.

The reporters called it a miracle. The physician called it suspended animation. The sheriff called it unlawful exhumation with mitigating circumstances. The mother called it nothing. She held her little daughter in the yellow sunshine and wept.

Romulus was returned to his iron cage. Moses was returned to the white institution and a stronger fence. Both were punished, for the world readily pardons a good result but cannot tolerate irregular methods. Perhaps Romulus afterward remembered the enlarged heavens whenever the circus lamps flared above the ring. Perhaps Moses, digging obedient trenches, sometimes laughed at a leaf because one golden afternoon remained free within him.

Wang Tai lived.

And if you ask which of the three was truly rescued—the child from earth, the young man from one day’s servitude, or the ape from ignorance of the world—I cannot answer. I know only that the wise had buried, the lawful had excluded, the civilized had chained; while an animal and one whom society called witless broke three enclosures and gave a mother back her child.

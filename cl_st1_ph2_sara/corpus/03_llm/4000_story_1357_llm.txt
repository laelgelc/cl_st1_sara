### The Last Wager at Wingdam

There were four passengers on the Wingdam coach when it left Coloma at noon, although the way-bill, with that contempt for actual humanity which distinguishes official documents, declared there were six. One of the missing travelers was represented by a carpetbag, the other by a hat-box; and as both articles occupied more space and gave less satisfaction than their owners probably would have done, the discrepancy was accepted without complaint.

Of the visible passengers, three were men. The fourth was a woman.

The men had observed this fact before the coach had crossed the first ford. One expressed his knowledge by becoming offensively helpful; another by assuming a chastened silence; the third, Mr. Jack Hamlin, merely drew his hat over his eyes and appeared to sleep.

I fear this conduct did him credit only by comparison. Mr. Hamlin’s indifference to women, like his abstinence from cards, was commonly understood to begin where opportunity ended. He was a professional gambler, known from Sacramento to the southern camps; a young man with a pale, immobile face, long black lashes, a small mustache, and hands which might have belonged to a musician, a surgeon, or a lady of rank, but had in fact acquired their delicacy in manipulating those equally uncertain instruments, cards and men.

Yet on this occasion he seemed genuinely uninterested. The woman’s veil, the dust, and a blue traveling cloak concealed her charms; and Jack, whose imagination was not ordinarily employed where eyesight could serve, kept his lids closed.

At the second relay the driver descended, swore at the horses with Homeric gravity, and announced that the off-leader had “gone lame in his moral principles.” During the consequent confusion, the woman stepped from the coach.

She stood for a moment upon a stone, holding her skirt above the dust. The wind lifted her veil.

Jack Hamlin opened his eyes.

The revelation was brief. It comprised a low white forehead, gray eyes, and a mouth whose softness was contradicted by a slight, almost imperious curve. Yet the gambler’s colorless face changed. A faint red passed over it—not the flush of embarrassment, for Mr. Hamlin was seldom embarrassed, but the involuntary signal of some older and more intimate disturbance.

The woman saw him. Her hand tightened upon the folds of her cloak.

“Jack!”

The name was scarcely audible beneath the stamping of the horses. Jack rose and stepped down.

“You are going to Wingdam?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

She glanced toward the other passengers.

“As you see.”

This answer, though literally exact, appeared to displease him. He helped her into the coach with a ceremonious gravity that converted the mud-splashed vehicle into a chariot and the broken step into an Olympian cloud. Then he mounted beside the driver.

For the remainder of the journey he sat outside, smoking. Once, as the coach descended a long green hollow, he sang. His voice—a high, sweet tenor, wholly unlike his speaking tone—floated backward over the roof:

> “Oh, the road is long when the heart rides light,  
> And short when the heart rides sore—”

The driver looked at him curiously.

“Know the lady inside, Jack?”

Jack removed his cigar.

“No.”

The driver, being accustomed to the frontier meaning of this negative, asked no more.

Wingdam lay under the evening sun like a handful of discarded tools. Its roofs, sheds, flumes, shafts, and chimneys had been flung along a ravine without apparent design, unless Providence had adopted the plan of a drunken surveyor. Above the town rose scarred red hills; below it, the river, diverted, dammed, cursed, and sluiced, crept muddily through a channel of tailings. A line of pines on the farther ridge preserved some dignity, though I wot they had little influence upon the citizens beneath.

The arrival of a respectable-looking woman created a sensation. Men came out of saloons; a faro-dealer abandoned his table; two miners removed their hats with such violence that one dropped his pipe. The coach stopped before the Magnolia Hotel, whose proprietor appeared in a white apron, supported by three bartenders and a Chinaman.

Then a large man pushed through the crowd.

He was broad-shouldered and blond, with the remains of strength in his frame and of good humor in his face. Dissipation had puffed the latter and made the former uncertain. His coat was unbuttoned, his boots muddy, and his hands trembled slightly.

“Maria!” he exclaimed.

The woman stood in the coach door.

“Henry!”

They regarded each other.

What followed has been variously reported. The *Wingdam Clarion* stated, next morning, that “the long-separated pair fell into each other’s arms amid the irrepressible emotion of assembled manhood.” Mr. Barlow, who kept the hardware store opposite, affirmed that Mrs. Brown said, “Is this where you live?” and that Brown answered, “Mostly.” The bartender maintained that neither spoke at all.

I was not present, and therefore may perhaps approach the truth by declining to choose among these accounts.

Jack Hamlin remained on the box until Brown helped his wife down. Then their eyes met. Brown’s face brightened with weak, eager pleasure.

“Jack! By thunder, I’m glad to see ye! Maria—this is Jack Hamlin. You’ve heard me speak of Jack.”

Mrs. Brown bowed.

“I believe I have met Mr. Hamlin before.”

“In Stockton,” said Jack quietly.

A shade passed over her face.

Brown laughed. “Why, that’s so! I’d clean forgot. Well, this is luck all round. Come in, Jack. Take suthin’.”

Jack declined and walked away.

That night he lost eleven hundred dollars at his own table in the Arcade Saloon. As Mr. Hamlin had never before been known to lose heavily when sober, and was certainly sober then, the occurrence was considered significant. Some attributed it to the lady’s gray eyes; others to a cold deck; but the prevailing opinion was that Jack had “let the game slide.” The distinction is important to gamblers, although obscure to moralists.

Brown found him near midnight in a private room behind the bar. The apartment contained a velvet sofa, two gilt chairs, a cracked mirror, and a marble-topped table stained by whiskey glasses. Civilization had evidently reached Wingdam in pieces and had suffered in transportation.

Brown shut the door.

“Jack, old man, I’m mighty glad to see ye.”

“You said that.”

“I know. But I am.”

Jack shuffled a pack of cards and spread them face downward.

“How much?”

Brown started. “Who said I wanted money?”

“Nobody.”

“I only come in friendly.”

Jack turned over the queen of hearts.

Brown watched his fingers.

“The fact is,” he said at last, “things ain’t been just flourishing. I’ve had expenses. Maria’s folks kept her back East all these years, sayin’ I wasn’t fit to provide for her. Maybe I wasn’t. But now she’s come—of her own free will, mind ye—and I’ve got to make a show.”

“How much?” repeated Jack.

“Five hundred would sort of straighten me.”

Jack took out a roll of notes.

“Seven-fifty,” Brown added hastily, “would straighten me permanent.”

Jack counted out a thousand dollars.

Brown’s eyes filled. He seized the gambler’s hand.

“Jack, you’re a gentleman.”

“Don’t tell anybody.”

“I’ll pay ye.”

“No.”

Brown hesitated, then, arriving at a conclusion perhaps not entirely logical, took the money and went away declaring again that he was glad to see him.

Two days later Jack rode out to the North Fork claim, in which Brown possessed an eighth interest and no confidence. The superintendent, who possessed confidence and three-eighths interest, was found to have neglected certain returns, falsified others, and removed gold-bearing gravel by night. Jack’s methods of discovering this were never made public. The superintendent left Wingdam suddenly, with a bruise over one eye and an imperfect recollection of signing a transfer.

Within a week Brown owned half the claim.

Within a month a new lead was struck.

Within three months Henry Brown, who had borrowed ten dollars in the morning and forgotten it by dinner, was spoken of as “one of our substantial citizens.” He moved from a room above the livery stable to a white cottage on the hill. He purchased a piano, six rosewood chairs, and a chandelier designed for gas, although Wingdam was illuminated by oil. The chandelier hung darkly in the parlor, a mute prophecy of civic improvement.

Mrs. Brown became popular.

The town, having at first worshiped her beauty in that mythological fashion natural to communities deprived of women, presently discovered that she could sing, dance, read French, trim bonnets, and pour tea without spilling it into the saucer. These accomplishments established her supremacy. Miners who had faced cave-ins without changing color trembled while handing her a cup; lawyers quoted poetry; the editor of the *Clarion* began an article upon “The Ennobling Influence of Refined Womanhood in Our Midst,” and was afterward found asleep beneath her garden fence.

Mrs. Brown accepted these tributes with grace. If she liked admiration, I cannot think this astonishing. A woman who has passed five years hearing from her relatives that she married beneath her may be pardoned for enjoying a whole town’s assurance that she descended from above.

Brown enjoyed her triumph at first. He stood behind her chair, laughed loudly at her lightest remark, and explained to strangers that she had been “raised elegant.” He bought clothes beyond his taste and liquor beyond his capacity. When she sang, he listened with a moist, proprietary smile; when another man turned the pages, he watched the man.

Jack seldom visited the cottage.

Mrs. Brown sometimes met him elsewhere—at a supper, a picnic, or one of those assemblies which Wingdam called balls because dancing occurred and society because women were present. Jack was always courteous, always remote. He danced with her once if custom demanded it, spoke to her little, and afterward lost or won at the Arcade with his usual pale composure.

Yet people observed that Mrs. Brown’s animation failed in his presence. They observed also that when Jack entered a room she knew it before looking toward the door. Wingdam, which could detect a love affair at a distance of half a mile and overlook a theft committed under its nose, was soon rich in conclusions.

The summer passed.

One Sunday Jack rode alone beyond the diggings. The road climbed from the red wounds of the mining ground into an untouched valley where wild oats ran like pale water under the wind. Children played before a settler’s cabin; a woman shook a cloth at the door; a man in his shirt sleeves lifted a baby to a low branch where a bluebird fluttered. Jack reined in.

The picture detained him, though it contained nothing that could be cashed, staked, or profitably disputed.

He took off his hat. His face, released from the hard reflections of lamps and cards, seemed younger. Presently he began to sing—not for listeners, I think, but because birds and gamblers sometimes express what they cannot defend in argument.

A hawk sailed above the field, predatory, solitary, and respectable. It could not sing, which gave Jack, for the moment, the moral advantage.

On his return the road passed the Brown cottage. Mrs. Brown stood at the gate in a white dress.

“You avoid us,” she said.

“I’ve been busy.”

“At cards?”

“Mostly.”

She looked down the road. “You were not always so cruel.”

“No?”

“In Stockton—”

“That was five years ago.”

“Four.”

“Cards remember better than men.”

“And women?”

Jack leaned over his saddle and lowered his voice.

“Women should forget professionally.”

She lifted her eyes. There were tears in them, though whether of anger, wounded vanity, or tenderness I cannot say.

“I did not come here to be lectured by you.”

“You came to your husband.”

“My husband!” She laughed faintly. “You have made him rich. Did you think money would make him strong?”

Jack straightened.

“Go into the house, Maria.”

Her face whitened at the name.

“Are you afraid?”

“Yes.”

“Of Henry?”

“Of myself.”

For an instant the ironic calm left him. He looked at her as a thirsty man might look at water known to be poisoned and not therefore less bright. Then his hand tightened upon the rein.

“Go in,” he repeated.

She turned without another word.

Jack rode down the hill, through the tailings and the shouting streets, to the Arcade, where he dealt faro until dawn and broke three men with perfect fairness.

After this Mrs. Brown’s social triumph became more pronounced and her domestic contentment less visible. Brown’s pride, having nothing else to do, became suspicion. He resented the men who admired his wife and resented her when they ceased. He drank secretly, which in Wingdam meant that he drank in back rooms rather than at the bar. He began to speak of gratitude, a virtue which weak men usually demand from those they have injured.

He also grew afraid of Jack.

The cause was not any overt act. Jack remained his benefactor, protected his interests, and once paid a debt that Brown had concealed from his wife. But generosity is a humiliating relation when the recipient suspects that the giver possesses everything else he lacks. Brown saw in Jack’s quietness a reproach, in his elegance a taunt, and in his avoidance of Mrs. Brown the most alarming evidence of intimacy.

One October night Brown came to Jack’s room above the Arcade.

The rain had begun—a thin, cold rain that silvered the roofs and reduced Wingdam’s dust to a red adhesive paste. Jack sat by the window playing solitaire. A lamp burned at his elbow. On the bed lay a pair of silver-mounted pistols and a black riding coat.

Brown entered without knocking.

“I’m glad to see ye, Jack,” he said.

Jack placed the red seven upon the black eight.

“Sit down.”

Brown did not. He was wet, unshaven, and breathing heavily.

“I want to ask ye suthin’.”

“Ask.”

“Do you believe a woman can be bad and nobody know it?”

“Everybody usually knows it before she does.”

“This ain’t a joke.”

“No.”

Brown moved nearer. “Maria loves another man.”

Jack’s hand paused above the cards, then descended steadily.

“She told you?”

“No.”

“You saw something?”

“No.”

“Then you’re drunk.”

“I ain’t. I’ve watched her. She’s waitin’—listenin’. She starts when a horse comes up the road. She keeps letters. She cries when she thinks I’m asleep.”

“She may have reasons.”

“Reasons!” Brown struck the table. The cards jumped. “She despises me. She’s right, maybe. I ain’t what I ought to be. I’ve wasted money; I’ve lied to her; I’ve made a fool of myself. But she’s mine.”

Jack looked up.

“No woman is.”

Brown recoiled as if struck.

“You know who it is.”

“I know nothing.”

“It’s one of these smooth, sneakin’ hounds that comes to my house and takes my hand. I’ll kill him.”

Jack gathered the cards, squared their edges, and laid them down.

“That’s sensible.”

Brown stared.

“You mean it?”

“Spot the man, and kill him on sight.”

The short sentence, spoken without heat, had more weight than Brown’s passion. He went pale.

“I thought—you’d maybe tell me to forgive her.”

“I don’t advise beyond a man’s capacity.”

Brown sat down heavily. “Suppose she runs away?”

“Stop her.”

“How?”

“Sell out. Take her away from Wingdam.”

“She won’t go.”

“Make her.”

Brown covered his face. For a little while only the rain spoke against the window.

Then he said, “I’m goin’ away tonight.”

Jack’s eyes sharpened.

“Where?”

“Anywhere. I can’t stand it. Let her have him. I’ll clear out afore morning.”

“You’ll leave your wife to a man you want to kill?”

Brown groaned. “Don’t talk that way, Jack. I’m weak. I know it. I was born weak, I reckon, though I look strong. You’re different. You could sit there if hell opened under the floor and ask what the limit was.”

Jack rose.

“Go back to your room.”

“I ain’t got one at the house. She locked the door. I’ve been sleepin’ over the stable.”

“Then go there. Don’t leave town till I see you.”

Brown caught his sleeve. “You’re my friend, ain’t ye?”

Jack disengaged the hand.

“I’ve been unlucky enough to prove it.”

Brown attempted a laugh, failed, and stumbled out.

Jack remained standing.

The lamp showed his face as pale and fixed as carved marble; but the stillness was no longer repose. He walked to the window, opened it, and looked toward the hill. The rain had ceased. Between ragged clouds the moon cast a merciful light upon Wingdam, disguising its rubbish heaps, smoothing its torn slopes, and turning the puddles to silver. Even the town jail acquired for a moment the dignity of a Roman tomb.

A knock sounded.

The hotel boy entered with a folded paper.

“Fer you, Mr. Hamlin.”

“Who gave it?”

“A lady’s hired gal.”

Jack waited until the door closed. Then he opened the note.

It contained one line:

*Bring the buggy to the old corral at three. I shall be ready. M.*

He read it twice.

At that hour the clock below struck one.

Mr. Hamlin had faced certain death with less concern than he now displayed over twelve words written in a woman’s hand. Yet, because habit survives where courage fails, he returned to the table and took up the cards.

He shuffled them slowly.

“If the queen comes first,” he said aloud, “I go.”

He cut the pack.

The queen of hearts lay exposed.

He smiled, but without mirth.

From a drawer he took two dice and threw them upon the table.

“Double sixes, I go.”

They spun, struck the lamp base, and settled.

Six and six.

Jack leaned back. Fate, which had often cheated him in small matters, had become embarrassingly generous in a great one.

He looked out at the clearing sky. A few stars showed above the ridge.

“One falling star before half past,” he murmured.

None fell.

He waited.

The hand of his watch traveled with intolerable leisure. At twenty-nine minutes past one, a streak of fire crossed the eastern sky and vanished behind the pines.

Jack shut the watch.

For a moment he laughed softly. It was the laughter of a man who finds the universe in conspiracy with his worst desire.

He put on his coat, thrust the note into his pocket, and descended.

The Arcade was nearly empty. A dealer slept in his chair. Two miners argued in whispers over a bottle. Jack passed through the bar and into the stable yard.

“Pete,” he called, “hitch the bay team to the buggy.”

The hostler emerged from the hay, blinking.

“Now?”

“Now.”

Jack crossed the yard to the narrow stair above the stable. At the top he opened a door.

Brown lay dressed upon a cot. A candle burned beside him. He started up.

“Jack!”

“Get up.”

“What’s happened?”

“Get up.”

There was something in Jack’s voice that brought Brown to his feet. Jack closed the door, took out the note, and held it toward the candle.

Brown saw the handwriting.

His jaw dropped.

“Where’d you get that?”

Jack did not answer.

Brown snatched at the paper. Jack’s hand closed.

“Stand aside.”

“Is it him? Is it the man? You know—”

Jack held the note over the flame.

The corner blackened; fire ran along the fold, illuminated the words for an instant, and curled them into a glowing scrap. He carried it to the open window. The ember dropped, wavering through the darkness like a small red star.

Brown watched stupidly.

“The buggy,” he whispered. “I heard you order it.”

“Damn the buggy!”

Jack seized him by the shoulders and shook him once.

“Listen to me. At daylight you go to Carter at the North Fork. Sell him your share.”

“Sell?”

“He’ll pay forty thousand. Take thirty if he bargains. Sell the cottage, the furniture, the horses—everything.”

“What for?”

“Because you’re leaving Wingdam.”

“With her?”

“With your wife.”

“She won’t—”

“She will.”

“How d’you know?”

Jack released him.

“Because you’ll ask her before you accuse her. Because you’ll tell her you’ve been a coward and a fool. Because you’ll give her something she hasn’t had since she came here—a chance to be loyal without an audience.”

Brown stared at him. Some dim comprehension began painfully to shape his face.

“The note,” he said. “Was it—Jack, was it meant for—”

“You never saw a note.”

“But—”

“You never saw it!”

The force of the words silenced him.

Jack turned away and spoke more quietly.

“Take her East. Or south. Buy a ranch where there ain’t a saloon within ten miles. Work with your hands. Let her work too. Don’t make her gratitude pay interest on every dollar you spend. And when you remember Wingdam, remember you were both sick here.”

Brown’s lips trembled.

“You’ve done everything for me.”

“Then do this for yourself.”

“Why?”

Jack looked toward the black square of the window.

“Because this is the last time I ask.”

Below, the buggy wheels grated in the yard.

“Team’s ready, Mr. Hamlin!” called Pete.

Brown drew a long breath.

“Were you goin’ away with her?”

Jack’s face changed—not much, but enough. The mask of the gambler, the cynic, the elegant adventurer, failed him for one naked second; and Brown, weak as he was, understood.

He sank onto the cot.

“My God!”

Jack put on his hat.

“Keep Him out of it. He didn’t deal this hand.”

At the door Brown cried, “Jack!”

The gambler paused.

“I’m glad—I’m glad to see ye.”

It was the old foolish phrase, but broken now by something neither man could ridicule.

Jack nodded and went down.

Pete stood by the buggy.

“Old corral, ain’t it?”

Jack looked at the light vehicle, the impatient bays, and the road climbing silver-gray toward the hill. Three miles beyond, at the abandoned corral, a woman would be waiting with a cloak and a small bag, perhaps listening for wheels, perhaps believing that love had at last become stronger than shame.

He took the reins.

Then he threw them back.

“Put ’em up.”

Pete gaped. “Put ’em up?”

“You heard me.”

Jack crossed to the saddle rack, chose his own bridle, and entered the stall where his black mare turned a fine, reproachful head.

Five minutes later he rode out alone.

At three o’clock a figure stood beside the old corral. At four it was gone. Whether Mrs. Brown wept there, cursed Jack, forgave him, or merely discovered that the night air was cold, I cannot say. She returned before daylight, and no one in Wingdam—save perhaps a silent hired girl—ever knew she had left the house.

At dawn Brown knocked at her door.

What passed between them belongs to that private history which newspapers and neighbors vainly attempt to improve. It is known that within ten days the North Fork interest was sold; that the white cottage, the rosewood chairs, and the useless chandelier followed; and that Mr. and Mrs. Brown departed southward in a two-horse wagon.

The *Clarion* described their removal as “a severe social and financial loss to our flourishing community.” It omitted Mr. Hamlin’s name.

Jack was already far away.

He rode eastward while the night thinned above the ridges. The mining ground fell behind him—the derricks, poisoned streams, gaunt flumes, and raw red earth upon which men had staked health, honor, friendship, and occasionally money. Ahead, the road entered the pine country.

The first sunlight touched the high branches. Frost shone beside the track. From a cabin chimney rose a thread of smoke, straight and peaceful in the windless dawn.

Jack loosened the rein.

Presently he began to sing.

The song drifted across the waking camps, over the tents of laborers and the ashes of dead fires. Men who had forgotten gentler things turned in their blankets; one old miner sat up and listened with wet eyes, remembering a garden, a gate, and a girl whose name he had not spoken in twenty years.

They heard only a romantic vagabond riding through the dawn.

They did not know that chance had given him the winning cards, the winning cast, and the appointed star—and that he had thrown the game away.

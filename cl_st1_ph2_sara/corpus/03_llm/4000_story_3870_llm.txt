The pale man bent over the microscope until the lank ends of his hair almost brushed the brasswork.

“Turn that screw—gently,” said the Bacteriologist. “No, the smaller one. There! You have it now.”

The visitor held his breath. In the illuminated circle beneath his eye lay a faintly stained field, across which were scattered minute curved strokes, delicate as commas in a printer’s smallest type.

“Those?” he whispered.

“Those.”

“And they are alive?”

“Indubitably. Or were, when the preparation was made.”

The pale man drew back and looked about the laboratory. It was a narrow, lofty room, crowded with glass vessels, covered dishes, bottles with Latin labels, racks of tubes plugged with cotton-wool, and instruments whose purpose he could not guess. Afternoon light fell through the high window upon the Bacteriologist’s cuffs and made a little green fire in a flask near his elbow. From somewhere came the soft hiss of a gas flame.

“Alive,” repeated the visitor. “And yet so small.”

“The agents of the greatest events are frequently small,” said the Bacteriologist.

He spoke with the easy gravity of a man accustomed to explaining mysteries to persons of inferior knowledge. His visitor’s morbid attention pleased him. At luncheon his wife had accused him of being silent and absent-minded; here, at least, was an audience that did not object to science.

The pale man looked again into the microscope.

“You are certain,” he said, “that these are the germs of cholera?”

“The preparation is cholera. The living culture is here.”

He took from a rack a little sealed tube. Within it was a colourless, slightly clouded fluid.

The visitor’s fingers closed upon the edge of the table.

“In that?”

“In that.”

“It seems—nothing.”

“So does a match, until it falls in straw.”

The Bacteriologist held the tube against the light. The cloudiness swam faintly as he moved it.

“Consider,” he continued, “what that insignificant turbidity means. A few days of warmth, a proper medium, and each organism becomes a multitude. You have seen yeast ferment; you have seen mould spread upon fruit. These creatures multiply with a speed that makes arithmetic grotesque.”

The visitor’s face grew intent.

“But outside the body?”

“They may live for a time in water. That is the danger.”

“In drinking-water?”

“Precisely.”

There was a pause. In the room beyond, a clock gave a small dry tick.

“Suppose,” said the pale man, “suppose a person emptied such a tube into a reservoir.”

The Bacteriologist smiled. Rhetoric was a professional indulgence with him, though he regarded it as an educational instrument.

“That would depend upon the reservoir, the season, the condition of the water, and a dozen other circumstances. Science is not a conjurer’s box. But suppose the circumstances favourable. Suppose the supply contaminated at its source. Then this little creature might go travelling through London.”

He lifted the tube between finger and thumb.

“Travelling,” he said, “where no police could follow it and no locked door exclude it. Creeping through mains beneath the streets, rising into kitchens, splashing into kettles, standing clear and innocent in bedroom jugs; getting into soup, into milk, upon fruit, into the public fountain where children drink after play. Death at the pump, death in the teacup, death carried in the water-cart; death picking here a labourer, there a clerk, passing one house and striking three in the next; death lying invisible in a glass held to the lips of a bride, death waiting beside the sickbed in the draught meant to bring relief. The hospitals would fill, the carts would go by at night, trade would stop, and fear—fear would move faster than the infection. All from this.”

He stopped.

The pale man’s mouth had opened slightly. His eyes, dull before, were bright.

“Wonderful,” he said.

“Terrible,” corrected the Bacteriologist, though he was not displeased.

“Yes. Terrible. Naturally.”

He followed the tube with his eyes as it was returned to the rack.

A bell rang below.

The Bacteriologist frowned. “Excuse me for one moment. My assistant is out, and my wife—”

He went into the adjoining room. Almost at once there came the muffled sound of a domestic consultation, a woman’s clear voice, and his own impatient reply.

The visitor remained motionless.

Then he glanced at the door.

His hand went to the rack, hesitated above it, withdrew, and returned. The glass was cool against his palm. He slipped the tube into the inner pocket of his coat.

For one second he stood listening to his heart.

Then, with surprising silence, he crossed the room, descended the stairs, and let himself into the street.

A cab was passing.

“Keb, sir?” cried the driver.

The pale man sprang in. “Drive! Anywhere—westward. Fast!”

The whip swished. The horse started, the cab lurched, and the laboratory vanished behind a moving wall of omnibuses.

Upstairs, the Bacteriologist returned, still holding a card.

“My visitor?”

The empty chair answered him.

He looked at the microscope, then at the rack. For several seconds his mind refused the obvious conclusion. He counted the tubes. He counted them again.

“Good heavens!”

He rushed to the window and saw, at the turn of the street, the pale face looking back from a cab.

The Bacteriologist ran from the room.

Minnie, his wife, appeared upon the landing. “George, what is it?”

“My coat!”

“It is in the bedroom.”

“No time!”

He went down the stairs three at a stride, caught his foot in a rug, recovered himself by means of the umbrella stand, and shot through the front door in his shirtsleeves and slippers.

“George!” cried Minnie. “Your hat!”

A second cab stood at the corner. The Bacteriologist flung himself into it.

“Follow that cab!”

“Which keb?”

“The one turning by the red omnibus. Quick, man! It’s life and death!”

The cabman, who had heard this phrase before and had generally found it meant a forgotten train, nevertheless recognised the tone of a generous fare. He cracked his whip magnificently.

Minnie reached the pavement in time to see her husband’s bare head retreating at a violent pace.

She stared after him.

Then she went indoors, collected his coat, hat, and boots, remembered his spectacles, forgot them, returned for them, and came out again. One slipper lay in the road, having deserted the Bacteriologist at the corner.

“This is madness,” she said.

A third cab approached.

“Cabman! Follow my husband.”

The driver looked at the coat and boots in her arms.

“Which is ’im, mum?”

“The gentleman without a hat.”

“Plenty o’ them on a windy day.”

“In that cab—the one chasing the other.”

The cabman stood up to see. “Lor! A race, is it?”

“It is nothing of the kind. Drive at once.”

The third cab joined the pursuit.

The Anarchist crouched in the first vehicle and peered through the little rear window. His persecutor was still there—coatless, hatless, one foot in a slipper and the other apparently in a sock, waving both arms with a complete disregard for dignity.

So they had discovered him! They feared him already.

He pressed a hand against his breast and felt the tube beneath the cloth. It was absurdly small. For years he had spoken in cellars to audiences that dwindled as the evenings passed. He had printed manifestoes that were used by fruiterers for wrapping onions. Men with better coats and louder voices had called him impractical. Even the police, whose oppression he had denounced so frequently, had scarcely troubled to notice him.

But now London pursued him.

He saw himself in tomorrow’s papers. THE RESERVOIR OUTRAGE. A CITY STRICKEN. THE UNKNOWN AVENGER. Perhaps they would never know his name. That was intolerable. No, he must leave a letter, a proclamation, something immense and final. Let them learn that the neglected man had held millions in his hand.

“Faster!” he cried.

“’Orse ain’t a locomotive,” said the cabman.

“I will give you two sovereigns.”

The cabman’s whip described an urgent circle. “Why didn’t yer say so?”

The cab bounded over a crossing. A brewer’s dray loomed ahead; they swept past it with inches to spare. Hooves struck sparks. Wheels hammered upon the stones. A policeman shouted, a costermonger dragged his barrow aside, and two small boys began running after the chase with piercing cries.

Behind, the Bacteriologist stood up and gesticulated.

“Stop him! Stop that cab!”

His driver looked back. “Sit down, sir, unless yer want to arrive by ’ospital.”

“He has stolen—” The Bacteriologist checked himself. To cry cholera in a crowded street would be insanity. “He has stolen a valuable preparation.”

“Medicine, is it?”

“In a sense.”

“Then why don’t yer let ’im take it? Looks as if ’e needs some.”

The second cab swung round a wagon, narrowly missed a lamp-post, and scattered three elderly gentlemen upon the pavement. The spectators, observing a pale fugitive, a wild-haired man in shirtsleeves, and, farther back, a lady brandishing a pair of boots, instantly understood that something of national importance was occurring.

“Go it, no-’at!” shouted a butcher’s boy.

“Lady’s gainin’ on yer!”

“Stop thief!”

“Which one’s the thief?”

“All of ’em, likely!”

The Anarchist heard the shouts and exulted. The city was awakening to his deed. Yet with the exultation came a thin thread of fear.

He took out the tube.

The fluid trembled in the shaking cab. Such fragility! The fate of a capital sealed in glass no thicker than a lady’s ornament. He imagined the reservoir: broad water under evening sky, railings, perhaps a watchman. He would approach carelessly, break the seal, pour in liberation. Then flight—or capture. Capture might be preferable. A trial offered publicity. He would stand erect in the dock and say—

The cab struck a rut.

The tube flew from his fingers. He clutched at it, caught it against the door, and heard a faint crack.

For an instant he did not move.

Then a cold wetness touched his palm.

He opened his hand. A jagged piece of glass lay there, and the colourless fluid ran between his fingers.

The roar of the street became distant.

He was infected.

That was the first thought, stark and animal. The organisms would enter through the small cut near his thumb. They would multiply in the warmth of him. He saw pain, collapse, the blue-grey face of the cholera patient, the body twisted by cramps. He wanted to scream to the driver to stop at a hospital. He wanted disinfectant, doctors, safety.

Then pride came to his assistance.

Was he not an Anarchist? Had he not despised the timid attachment of comfortable men to life? What was death beside the Cause? Besides, the Bacteriologist must not recover what remained. There were still several drops in the broken tube.

He looked back. The pursuing cab was nearer.

A magnificent resolution seized him.

“If I cannot poison the water,” he whispered, “I will become the poison.”

He raised the broken glass to his lips and drank.

It tasted chiefly of salt and dust.

“Vive l’Anarchie!” he shouted.

The cabman glanced down through the trap. “What’s that?”

“Stop!”

“But the two sovs—”

“Stop, fool!”

The cab pulled sharply to the kerb. The Anarchist sprang out before the wheels had ceased turning. He stood in the road, pale but triumphant, one hand bleeding, his coat flung back like a cloak.

The Bacteriologist’s cab drew up.

“You are too late!” cried the Anarchist. “Do you hear? Too late! The pestilence is free.”

The Bacteriologist stared at the broken glass.

“I have drunk it,” continued the Anarchist. “I carry death in my blood. Before night, before tomorrow, a hundred shall receive it from me. You guarded your reservoirs, your laboratories, your secrets—but you did not guard against a man willing to die!”

A crowd gathered with the miraculous speed of London crowds. A fishmonger, two errand boys, a nursemaid, four clerks, and a gentleman with a dog formed the first rank. The dog sniffed the Anarchist’s trousers.

“Stand back!” cried the Anarchist. “I am cholera!”

The first rank became the third.

The Bacteriologist’s expression underwent a curious alteration. Anxiety vanished from it; astonishment remained, and behind the astonishment there appeared the beginning of a smile.

“You swallowed it?” he asked.

“Every drop.”

“My dear sir—”

“Do not approach me!”

The Anarchist turned majestically and walked into the crowd. People separated before him. He brushed deliberately against a stout man, laid a hand upon the shoulder of a clerk, and breathed upon an indignant old lady.

“Murder!” cried the old lady.

“Cholera!” shouted someone.

The crowd broke. The Anarchist, believing terror to be homage, proceeded down the street with solemn steps, touching railings, door-handles, newspapers, and as many fellow citizens as failed to escape him. Presently he entered a public-house, presumably to infect the glasses.

At that moment Minnie’s cab arrived.

She descended carrying the Bacteriologist’s coat and boots. “George! What has happened? Why are you in the street like this? And where is your other slipper?”

“My dear Minnie,” he said, “you will scarcely believe—”

“I shall believe nothing until you put on your coat.”

The first cabman demanded two sovereigns. The second protested that his horse had been ruined for life. The third wished to know whether the race was finished. A policeman began asking questions. Around them, bystanders repeated contradictory accounts of plague, poisoning, lunacy, and an escaped foreign prince.

Minnie pushed the coat into her husband’s arms.

“Was that dreadful man dangerous?”

“He believed himself so.”

“Believed?”

The Bacteriologist sat upon the step of his cab and put on one boot. His smile widened.

“The tube he stole did not contain cholera.”

Minnie closed her eyes briefly. “Then why did you chase him through London without a hat?”

“Because the culture was valuable. Quite unique, in fact. It is a new organism I have been cultivating.”

“What does it do?”

“So far, nothing of medical importance. It produces vivid blue patches in certain animals—blue over the ears and along the extremities. The effect upon man has not been investigated.”

Minnie looked in the direction of the public-house.

“You mean that man may turn blue?”

“Distinctly blue,” said the Bacteriologist. “Possibly in irregular patches. There may also be fever. I cannot say.”

He put on the second boot and considered the matter scientifically. The picture of the pale revolutionary, stalking through London and distributing embraces while slowly acquiring the complexion of an apoplectic peacock, overcame him. He laughed until the cabmen laughed too, though none of them knew why.

Minnie did not laugh.

“You frightened me out of my senses,” she said. “You have stopped traffic, lost a slipper, and gone about in public dressed like a patient escaping from an asylum.”

“My dear, the circumstances—”

“And you told that creature about poisoning reservoirs.”

“Merely as an illustration.”

“An illustration!”

She placed his hat firmly upon his head.

The Bacteriologist rose, subdued but still smiling. The crowd was dispersing, disappointed by the absence of an arrest. From within the public-house came a crash, followed by cries of “Turn ’im out!” and “Don’t let ’im touch the pewter!”

“I ought to observe the development,” he murmured.

“You ought to come home.”

“Yes, Minnie.”

He entered the cab beside her. As they drove away, he looked regretfully toward the vanished Anarchist.

“It is a pity,” he said, “about the culture.”

“The culture!”

“I shall have to prepare another. It may take weeks.”

Minnie adjusted his collar so that it concealed his shirtsleeves as much as possible.

“Then when you do,” she said, “you will keep it locked up.”

The Bacteriologist reflected upon this. It was sound advice, but it lacked imagination.

“Yes, my dear,” he said.

Behind them the traffic closed, the cabmen resumed their ordinary quarrels, and London, threatened for half an hour with destruction and for several days with a remarkably blue Anarchist, went clattering on beneath the evening smoke.

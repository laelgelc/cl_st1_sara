THE X-TRAORDINARY EDITOR OF NOPOLIS

It has been demonstrated by philosophers—and, what is more conclusive, by editors—that the westward movement of civilisation is occasioned less by the attraction of gold, land, liberty, or any other vulgar commodity, than by the inability of certain great men to remain where they are known.

Touch-and-go Bullet-head was such a man.

He came from the East, where his genius had been repeatedly misunderstood by creditors, subscribers, constables, compositors, and three separate committees appointed to inquire into the disappearance of a municipal inkstand. These persecutions established his innocence; for no truly guilty man, as I have elsewhere shown, is ever persecuted by more than one committee at a time.

Mr. Bullet-head was short in stature, vast in opinion, circular in person, and pointed only in name. His forehead retreated from his eyebrows with the decisive rapidity of a defeated army, while his chin advanced to restore the balance. He wore a black coat, a red waistcoat, and an expression of offended wisdom. On being contradicted, his face became purple; on being agreed with, it became suspicious; and on being asked a civil question, it assumed that terrible tranquillity which, in the greater conquerors, commonly precedes the destruction of empires.

Having heard that Nopolis possessed no newspaper, he resolved to supply that deficiency.

Nopolis, I should explain, lay seven hundred miles beyond the nearest place worth mentioning, and consisted of a courthouse without a judge, a jail without a prisoner, a tavern without temperance, and eighty-four inhabitants, including a gentleman who had gone to fetch a doctor in 1839 and was still counted by courtesy. The town had originally been called Alexander-the-Great-o-nopolis; but, as no resident could pronounce the name before supper, and none could remember it afterward, public convenience reduced it to Nopolis.

Here Bullet-head arrived in a yellow coach drawn by four horses and pursued by seven trunks, two presses, fourteen cases of type, a barrel of ink, and a boy named Bob. He descended before the tavern with the majestic caution of a monarch entering a conquered capital.

“Citizens!” said he, addressing the landlord, a mule, and two hens, “the dawn of intellectual illumination is upon you!”

The mule alone replied.

Within twenty-four hours Bullet-head had rented the loft above the cooper’s shop, nailed up a sign bearing the words:

THE NOPOLIS MORNING, EVENING, WEEKLY, SEMI-WEEKLY, INDEPENDENT, UNCOMPROMISING, UNIVERSAL TEA-POT,

and announced that the first number would appear “to-morrow, Providence, the weather, and the junior compositor permitting.”

The junior compositor was Bob.

Bob had lately been employed across the street by John Smith, proprietor, editor, printer, publisher, carrier, and—during periods of financial depression—principal reader of the Nopolis Gazette. It was thus, and only thus, that Bullet-head discovered the fatal fact: Nopolis already had a newspaper.

I pass lightly over the great man’s emotions. There are convulsions of nature which language profanes by describing. When Ætna bursts, when oceans rise, when a cat encounters a closed pantry—silence is proper. Bullet-head stared at the Gazette office; the Gazette office, being made of brick, stared back. He then rushed upstairs, kicked a stool, overturned a type case, dismissed Bob, recalled him, accused him of conspiracy, raised his wages six cents, and composed the following introductory declaration:

“We come among you, fellow-citizens, not as the timid caterer for popular approbation, but as the bold, broad, and boundless organ of moral renovation. We shall expose corruption, oppose oppression, promote improvement, and overthrow whatsoever ought, in our own opinion, to be overthrown.”

The Tea-Pot appeared on Saturday.

On Monday, the Gazette observed:

“A domestic utensil has lately commenced boiling opposite our establishment. Its steam is considerable, its contents uncertain, and its handle apparently cracked. We recommend a low fire.”

Bullet-head read this paragraph at breakfast. He did not finish breakfast. The eggs remained uninjured; the coffee escaped; but the table suffered materially.

Tuesday’s Tea-Pot replied:

“The crawling calumniator of the contemptible concern across the common thoroughfare has obtruded another of his cowardly concoctions upon an outraged community. The fellow Smith—if Smith be indeed the fellow’s name—shall discover that the conductor of this journal is no common cock-o’-the-walk to be crowed over by a dunghill doodle.”

This philippic, though deficient in meaning, was so abundant in dignity that nine additional subscriptions were immediately obtained, five of them on credit.

Smith answered with moderation—which, in editorial warfare, is merely insult wearing gloves.

“Our neighbor’s composition,” said the Gazette, “has a round and rolling richness. He affects the noble vowel O. His thoughts go round because his letters do. We doubt whether the gifted gentleman can write a word without an O, and respectfully invite him to try.”

Now there are injuries which a magnanimous spirit may forgive. A man may be called liar, thief, fool, coward, or candidate for office, and still preserve a Christian serenity; but to criticise his style is to insert a red-hot gimlet into the very centre of his immortality.

Bullet-head came into the printing room with the Gazette crushed in his fist.

“Bob!”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do I employ the vowel O excessively?”

Bob reflected. “Which un be O, sir?”

“This!” cried Bullet-head, drawing an immense circle in the air.

Bob brightened. “Oh!”

“Again!” said Bullet-head. “Even you cannot answer without it. O is the soul of sonority—the orb of eloquence—the round, robust, resonant foundation of all noble composition. Homer! Solon! Plato! Cicero!”

“Cicerx?” suggested Bob.

“Silence! The villain Smith insinuates that I cannot write without O. I will show him that I can!”

This was surely the natural conclusion, though Bullet-head adopted the contrary one.

“No!” he thundered, after reflection. “Perish so vile a thought! To abandon my O’s would be to concede his argument. I shall overwhelm him with them. I shall write an article so round, so rotund, so sonorous, that posterity will mistake it for thunder rolling down a copper conduit. Bring paper!”

He wrote throughout the afternoon. He wrote with both elbows, with his shoulders, almost with his boots. From time to time he sprang up and strode about, exclaiming “O!” whenever inspiration deserted him, which was frequently. At midnight he produced six pages, blotted but triumphant.

“There,” said he. “That is composition.”

Bob glanced at the manuscript.

“There be a powerful lot o’ little round uns.”

“Every one is a cannon-ball aimed at Smith. Set it for the morning edition. Not one word—not one letter—not one point is to be altered.”

“Not one letter?”

“On your existence!”

Here destiny, which frequently conducts important affairs through the agency of boys who require whipping, took command.

Bob had once carried copy for Smith, and retained toward the Gazette that affectionate loyalty which survives employment, especially when the former employer supplies gingerbread. Smith had made no request that the Tea-Pot should be disabled; he had merely remarked, in Bob’s hearing, that Bullet-head’s O’s were more numerous than his ideas, and that the loss of either would improve the paper.

Bob, being literal-minded, determined to improve it.

At two in the morning he returned with a flour sack, opened every type case in the Tea-Pot office, removed each capital O and each small o, and carried the whole circular population across the street. He overlooked none. Roman O, italic O, pica O, brevier O, long-primer O—all departed. The Gazette received them silently, and Bob slept beneath the press with the peace peculiar to innocence and successful burglary.

At six, Bullet-head entered.

“Is the article set?”

“N-no, sir.”

“Why?”

“Please, sir, there beant an o in the shop.”

“What!”

“Not a solitary o, sir. Big uns is gone; little uns is gone; lean uns, fat uns, straight uns, slanting uns—there beant an o.”

Bullet-head examined the cases. The truth was hollow, round, and conspicuous.

“Send across the street and borrow some.”

“Mr. Smith says as how he’s short.”

“Purchase them.”

“He says as how they beant for sale.”

“Cast new ones!”

“No metal.”

“Carve them!”

“No wood.”

“Write them in by hand!”

“No hand, sir—leastways not enough for that paragrab.”

Bullet-head seized his hair, of which there was happily little.

“The paper must appear at eight. The public waits. The nation trembles. Substitute something!”

“What’ll I substertoot?”

“Anything! But alter nothing.”

Bob scratched his head. In the upper case he observed a quantity of X’s—letters of excellent character, crossed in principle, and seldom required in ordinary journalism.

“X is summat like two sticks where O ought to be a hoop,” he reasoned. “But master says anything, and likewise nothin’ altered. So X it be.”

He set the article. Wherever the manuscript demanded an O, Bob inserted an X. The press groaned; the rollers clattered; the sheets emerged; and at eight precisely the intellectual illumination of Nopolis burst upon the world in the following form:

The effect was not merely electrical; it was alphabetical.

The first citizen to read the article was Major Pottle, who understood nothing and therefore perceived treason. He carried it to the postmaster, who understood less and therefore perceived foreign treason. The postmaster showed it to the apothecary, who declared that the symptoms indicated hydrophobia complicated by algebra.

By nine o’clock a crowd had assembled.

“It’s a cipher,” said the schoolmaster.

“It’s a challenge,” said the blacksmith.

“It’s a prescription,” said the doctor.

“It’s agin the government,” said Major Pottle.

“How d’ye make that out?” asked the landlord.

“Plain. All them X’s stands for ex-kings. A paper full of ex-kings is monarchical.”

This reasoning was immediately adopted, not because it was sound, but because it required action. A committee formed itself. Ropes appeared. Tar was discussed, feathers seconded, and a rail moved as an amendment. The citizens rushed across the street, ascended the stairs, and burst into the Tea-Pot office.

They found the press, the ink, the type, the manuscript, Bullet-head’s red waistcoat, and a tumbler containing the remains of a dark beverage.

But Bullet-head was gone.

Whether he escaped through the roof, descended through the floor, folded himself into the press, or was translated bodily to some region where criticism is unknown, I cannot state. As nearly as I recollect, three witnesses saw him running east, four saw him running west, and one respectable lady saw him rise vertically over the courthouse with his coat-tails revolving. Collateral proof supports each account; consequently all are true.

He was never seen in Nopolis again.

The mysterious editorial, however, continued to exercise the public mind. Copies were framed, folded, denounced, interpreted, and sold. The schoolmaster lectured upon it as “An X-ample of X-treme X-pression.” The doctor called Bullet-head an X-traordinary case. The blacksmith said he had been X-pelled. The tavern keeper considered him X-hausted. Major Pottle maintained that the disappearance was an X-it demanded by public opinion.

Smith, with that cold malignity characteristic of successful editors, printed:

“Our late neighbor has made an X-cursion. We trust he will X-perience no difficulty in reaching his X-pected destination. His final article is certainly X-cellent in intention, though X-ceedingly X-centric in execution. We have prepared an X-press edition with X-planatory notes, X-tra copies ten cents.”

The edition sold out before noon.

Bob alone preserved a philosophical reserve. When questioned regarding the missing O’s, he opened his eyes to their widest circumference and declared:

“I knows nothin’ of no O’s. Master was uncommon queer last night—wery uncommon. Wrote circles, talked circles, walked circles, and swore as how he’d knock Mr. Smith into the middle o’ next week. It’s my belief the poor gentleman had took too much of that there ale.”

“What ale?” asked the committee.

Bob pointed solemnly to the abandoned tumbler.

“XXX.”

This explanation satisfied everybody. It accounted at once for the X’s, the editorial, the flight, the treason, and the headache which Major Pottle had acquired while investigating the evidence. The verdict of Nopolis was accordingly entered in the town records:

“Touch-and-go Bullet-head, editor, came to establish public enlightenment; became X-cited through XXX; produced an X-traordinary document; and made his X-it under circumstances X-clusively his own.”

The Gazette absorbed the subscribers of the Tea-Pot, Bob became foreman, and Nopolis enjoyed peace for nearly a fortnight—when Smith published an article asserting that the town council could not spell.

But that catastrophe belongs to another letter.

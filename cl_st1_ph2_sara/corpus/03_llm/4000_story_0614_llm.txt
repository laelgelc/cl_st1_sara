### THE GOING AWAY CLUB

Annie had known William Henry Brachett precisely eleven weeks when she married him, and of these eleven weeks she had spent perhaps thirty hours in his company. This is called a whirlwind courtship. A man and a woman see each other on six Sundays, walk twice in the municipal park, share lemonade and seed-cake at a temperance social, and immediately declare themselves acquainted with the profoundest abysses of one another’s souls. (So they think.)

The affair began with an accident to William Henry’s thumb.

At Pott’s Glaze and Colour Works, where he painted blue Japanese roses on vases—blue Japanese roses on vases from seven in the morning until five at night—he caught his thumb beneath a crate. Annie, who worked in the decorating shop, tied it up with a strip torn from her own handkerchief. Romance is frequently no more than mutual nursing under an assumed name. William Henry, who had a fierce chin and mild grey eyes, regarded the bandage, regarded Annie, and said:

“Ye’ve done that tidy, lass.”

The next Sunday he met her outside chapel. On the fifth Sunday he asked whether she could cook. On the sixth he informed her that he was earning thirty-five shillings a week, with chances. On the ninth he offered marriage. Annie, impressed by the chances even more than by the thirty-five shillings, accepted him.

They rented a house in Birches Street, Hanbridge, at six shillings and sixpence weekly. It was one of thirty houses, each with a red-brick face, a bow window, a brass knocker, two stone steps, and a narrow yard in which a determined woman might hang a washing. Annie made it different from the other twenty-nine by putting cream curtains in the bow window, a pot of artificial geraniums on the sill, and a gilt-edged Bible upon a little table where nobody could read it without first moving a china dog.

She was happy. Need it be said? William Henry neither drank immoderately nor struck her; he brought home his wages unopened; he wiped his boots before entering; he could mend a kettle; and every Saturday night he gave her sixpence for herself. There are queens in Europe who have had less substantial reasons for contentment.

Yet after six months Annie discovered that happiness, when taken regularly, may become monotonous.

Monday brought washing. Tuesday brought ironing. Wednesday brought mending. Thursday brought a careful examination of the remains of Sunday’s joint. Friday brought scrubbing. Saturday brought wages, marketing, and William Henry’s sixpence. Sunday brought chapel, a walk in the municipal park, tea, and chapel again. The park was a wonderful triumph of public spirit over slag, smoke, clay, and the natural desire of children to destroy shrubs. It possessed a bandstand, a pond containing three melancholy ducks, and notices forbidding nearly everything. William Henry admired it. Annie, after the twentieth circuit, knew every pruned bush personally.

No change, no gaiety, no freak! Only Birches Street, with its repeated doors and repeated bow windows; only smoke hanging above the roofs; only the long black factory walls; only blue Japanese roses on vases, and potatoes, and William Henry’s socks.

Nor was William Henry merely economical. He was consecrated to economy.

“Tuppence on greens?” he would demand, examining Annie’s accounts.

“They were fresh.”

“Fresh! They’ll not be fresh when they’re boiled.”

Or he would discover that she had bought coal on a Wednesday instead of a Monday, when the coalman allowed a halfpenny reduction on the hundredweight, and his fierce chin would advance like a weapon.

The chief instrument of Annie’s oppression, however, was the Going Away Club.

Every Friday William Henry placed four shillings in a yellow envelope and carried it to the club secretary at the Crown and Cushion. Four shillings! Two hundred and eight shillings in a year, not counting certain mysterious extras at Christmas and Easter! Annie had never seen the club, never read its rules, and never met a member except William Henry. To her it became a secret monster squatting behind the Crown and Cushion, eating butter, curtains, new hats, tobacco, and Sunday beef.

“What do they do with it?” she asked.

“Keep it.”

“What for?”

“Going away.”

“Where?”

“Away.”

This answer, repeated with variations, did not satisfy her. She suspected that men gathered in a back room, drank ale, smoked pipes, and laughed at wives. William Henry did not even smoke now, except for one pipe on Sunday afternoon.

“I could do with a new mantle,” she said in May.

“Old one’s sound.”

“It’s shiny at th’ elbows.”

“Then don’t walk elbow-first.”

In June she desired muslin blinds for the bedroom, at three shillings and elevenpence the pair. The Going Away Club swallowed them. In July she proposed one evening at the Empire Palace—back seats, ninepence each. The monster swallowed that also.

Thus marriage, which had first appeared to Annie as a decorated arch leading into a broad and brilliant future, narrowed into a passage between account books. She loved William Henry; mind you, she loved him genuinely. But she sometimes looked at his severe profile over the breakfast herrings and wondered whether love ought invariably to smell of herrings and economy.

Then, on the first Saturday in August, William Henry came home at noon, washed his hands twice, changed his collar, and sat down without speaking.

Annie knew something colossal had happened.

He took a folded paper from his pocket and placed it beside the mustard.

“What’s that?” she asked.

“Money.”

She opened it. There were four five-pound notes.

Annie had never before seen twenty pounds assembled in one place. She had known money in shillings, elusive and argumentative; in sixpences, already promised before arrival; in coppers, rubbed warm in the hand at market stalls. But twenty pounds in four grave, pale documents seemed not money, but government.

“Whose is it?”

“Ours.”

“William Henry!”

“Club payout.”

The monster had disgorged treasure.

“We leave Monday,” he said.

“Leave? Leave where?”

“Douglas.”

Annie stared.

“Is that—is that abroad?”

“It’s Isle of Man.”

“But the house?”

“Lock it.”

“And your work?”

“Wakes week.”

“And money for coming back?”

“Tha’ asks too many questions. Pack th’ trunk.”

That weekend was the most agitated of Annie’s existence. She washed garments which were clean, ironed articles that did not require ironing, borrowed a leather strap from Mrs Peake next door, and packed the trunk seven times. She lay awake on Sunday night imagining the sea as an upright wall of green water, Douglas as a collection of palaces, and herself appearing among fashionable strangers in a mantle shiny at the elbows.

At five on Monday morning they locked No. 14 Birches Street. The street was cold, empty, and incredulous. William Henry carried the trunk to the tram stop; Annie carried a basket containing sandwiches, two hard-boiled eggs, and a bottle of cold tea. At Knype Station crowds surged beneath the glass roof. Porters shouted. Doors slammed. Engines screamed like wounded iron dragons. Respectable citizens of the Five Towns, released for one week from pots, kilns, moulds, ledgers, crates, ovens, and blue Japanese roses on vases, rushed towards the coast with a ferocity usually associated with military invasion.

They changed trains, lost a sandwich, recovered the trunk, quarrelled for ninety seconds, and reached Liverpool by ten.

Then Annie saw the ship.

It had marble halls, colossal staircases, mirrors, velvet seats, dining rooms, smoking rooms, bars, bookstalls, trinket cases, brass rails, white ropes, and officers more dazzling than princes. It appeared to Annie impossible that such a civilisation should consent to move. Yet presently the dock slid away, Liverpool unfolded in smoke, the river widened, and the deck trembled beneath her feet.

“Tea?” said William Henry.

“We’ve brought tea.”

“Throw it away.”

She looked at him. His face was calm.

In the restaurant he ordered tea at threepence a cup, bread and butter, ham, and a plate of cakes. The account was two shillings and ninepence.

“Two and nine!” Annie whispered.

“Holiday,” said William Henry.

That word transformed him.

At Douglas he rejected a modest lodging at twenty-eight shillings a week and chose one at thirty-five because the window gave a slanting glimpse of the bay if Annie leaned far enough to the left. He tipped the cabman sixpence. He tipped the landlady’s daughter threepence for carrying up a handbag that he could have lifted with one finger. He ordered meat for supper, though they had eaten on the ship.

Annie watched him with fear, admiration, and the suspicion that the genuine William Henry had been thrown overboard and replaced by a wealthy criminal.

After supper they walked along the promenade. Lamps curved around the bay like a necklace of fire. Music floated from pavilions. Cars rattled, bells rang, hawkers cried, women in bright hats passed laughing, and the dark sea answered the illuminated town with long silver gestures. Hanbridge, at that hour, would have been closing its shops beneath a roof of smoke. Here the world had forgotten to close.

“It’s grand,” Annie breathed.

“Ay,” William Henry replied. “Come on. We’re going in.”

They entered a concert hall at a cost of one shilling and sixpence each. A comedian sang about a mother-in-law, a lady in pink descended from the ceiling upon a crescent moon, and six dancers kicked simultaneously with such precision that Annie nearly wept. Afterwards William Henry bought two shandy-gaffs and a paper bag of shrimps.

They went to bed after midnight.

This was Monday.

On Tuesday they breakfasted upon eggs, bacon, toast, marmalade, and coffee. Annie calculated that the meal must have cost at least one shilling and tenpence for the two of them, even allowing for inclusive terms, and experienced a delicious internal collapse. They rode in a horse tram, climbed a hill, had their photograph taken against a painted waterfall, ate fish dinners with pudding, bought picture postcards, sent seven of them, attended a pier entertainment, drank tea in a glass pavilion, and danced at night beneath electric lights.

William Henry danced badly but commandingly. He held Annie as though guiding a loaded barrow down an incline.

“Enjoying thysen?” he asked.

“Oh, William Henry!”

That was all she could say.

On Wednesday they went round the island. There were green valleys, white farms, cliffs, glens, wheels of water, villages with names that Annie forgot, and sudden seas appearing between hills. William Henry purchased oranges at a station, though oranges were fourpence each. He bought Annie a brooch in the shape of three legs. He bought himself a cap. At luncheon he chose roast beef instead of cold mutton and permitted the waiter to bring a bottle of stout.

The bottle cost tenpence.

Annie no longer protested. She had passed beyond protest into the Bacchic regions of arithmetic, where figures danced and sovereigns dissolved in glory. Each fresh expenditure wounded and exalted her. Sixpence for admission! Ninepence for a programme! One and three for tea! Two shillings for reserved seats! Money flew from William Henry’s pockets with wings, and she, who at home saved string too short to tie anything, assisted at the escape.

On Thursday it rained.

In Hanbridge rain meant wet washing, mud on the linoleum, and William Henry entering the house with the announcement, “It’s raining.” In Douglas rain was an entertainment provided by the management. They visited shops, museums, an aquarium, a waxwork exhibition, and a hall in which a mechanical lady told fortunes for a penny. Annie’s fortune promised “a voyage, wealth, and dark interference.”

“Voyage is right,” she said.

“Tha’s had wealth,” said William Henry.

“And what’s dark interference?”

“Me, likely.”

He bought her a new mantle.

It cost twenty-seven shillings and sixpence.

When she saw herself in the shop mirror, clothed in rich brown cloth with braid at the cuffs and a collar of imitation fur, Annie felt that every woman in Birches Street had been personally defeated. She turned sideways. She turned back. She touched the collar.

“We can’t afford it,” she murmured, in the tone of one hoping to be contradicted.

“Put it on,” ordered William Henry.

She put it on.

That evening they dined at a restaurant where the napkins were folded into bishops’ hats. William Henry had soup, plaice, roast lamb, potatoes, green peas, trifle, cheese, and coffee. Annie omitted the cheese, not from prudence but because nature intervened. The bill, including a tip, was seven shillings and elevenpence.

It was joy, it was life! It was also calamitous finance.

On Friday morning William Henry counted the money. Of the twenty pounds, after lodging had been reserved, there remained five pounds and twopence.

“Five pounds!” Annie exclaimed.

“And twopence.”

“We must stop.”

“Ay.”

They went at once for a carriage drive costing twelve shillings.

That afternoon they bathed from a machine. Annie, wearing a hired costume of astonishing breadth, entered the sea to her knees, screamed, retreated, returned, and ultimately allowed a wave to strike her in the stomach. William Henry swam five strokes, swallowed water, and declared the sea too salty. They celebrated survival with hot pies.

By Saturday only two pounds, three shillings, and eightpence remained. Annie began to feel the old anxiety moving within her, but it was changed. At home anxiety crouched over the future for months; here it pursued them merrily from pleasure to pleasure, counting the diminishing coins like the beats of a gallop.

They took another excursion. They ate ices. William Henry tried a shooting gallery and won a plaster dog with one ear imperfectly formed. Annie bought gifts: a shell box for Mrs Peake, a view of the bay for her mother, a pipe for her father, and two sticks of rock bearing the word DOUGLAS through their innermost souls.

That night they attended a final dance. The orchestra played louder, the lights shone more fiercely, and Annie danced until her new shoes punished her. She saw herself in the long mirrors—brown mantle laid aside, cheeks flushed, eyes bright—and she scarcely recognised the woman from No. 14 Birches Street who argued over tuppence on greens.

“William Henry,” she said as they walked back beside the midnight sea, “I never knew there was all this.”

“All what?”

“This. Everything.”

He considered the bay.

“There isn’t,” he said. “Not mostly.”

On Sunday they behaved respectably. They went to chapel, walked along the shore, and ate a restrained dinner which nevertheless included custard. In the evening William Henry counted eleven shillings and fourpence.

The return fares had been paid in advance.

“We need two shillings for cab and porters,” Annie said, “and there’ll be food.”

“Ay.”

They spent eight shillings and tenpence before leaving.

On Monday morning Douglas glittered at them as though it had never heard of departure. The ship carried them back through a rough sea. Annie was ill, recovered heroically, and demanded tea. At Liverpool they possessed one shilling and ninepence. At Knype, after a porter had been paid and two cups of tea purchased, they possessed sixpence.

Sixpence!

They reached Hanbridge in rain. No cab could be considered. William Henry bore the trunk from the station while Annie carried the plaster dog and the shell box. Their new grandeur moved painfully uphill through familiar smoke.

At Birches Street the repeated houses received them without emotion. The artificial geraniums still occupied the window. Inside, the air was close; dust filmed the furniture; the clock had stopped. Annie set down her parcels and looked at William Henry.

“We’ve sixpence,” she said. “There’s no bread, no milk, no coal chopped, and you won’t be paid till Friday.”

Without replying, William Henry went upstairs. She heard a board creak. He returned with a tobacco tin and opened it.

Inside lay six shillings.

“I put it by afore we left,” he said.

Annie stared at the coins. Here, then, was the whole man: the despot who denied muslin blinds, the prince who bought a twenty-seven-and-sixpenny mantle, the madman who tipped waiters, the statesman who hid six shillings beneath a bedroom board. She flung her arms round his neck.

“William Henry, you are wonderful!”

“Nay,” he said, embarrassed. “I’m hungry.”

On Tuesday the blue Japanese roses began again. Washing began again. Herrings began again. The municipal park, cramped and smoky, received them on Sunday. Birches Street resumed its changeless procession of curtains, knockers, artificial flowers, and respectable Bibles. The prison life had not altered by a brick or a halfpenny.

But Annie had altered.

When William Henry questioned the price of greens, she remembered the lamps around Douglas Bay. When she mended his socks, she remembered the lady descending upon the crescent moon. When smoke pressed against the window, she could still see the dark sea making silver gestures. Monotony was no longer endless; it had a door in it.

Three Fridays later, William Henry placed four shillings in a yellow envelope.

Annie saw him.

“Going Away Club?” she asked.

“Ay.”

She smoothed her brown mantle, calculated fifty-one Fridays, four shillings each, and perhaps an extra payment at Christmas. Then she took threepence from her housekeeping purse and pushed it towards him.

“Put that in too,” she said.

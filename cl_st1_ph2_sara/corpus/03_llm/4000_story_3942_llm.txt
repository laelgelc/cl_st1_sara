I was sitting one April afternoon before the Café de la Paix, watching Paris display that agreeable union of magnificence and decay which is her most permanent fashion. A duchess, brilliant as a jewelled reliquary, had just stepped over a gutter; behind her came a flower-girl whose violets were fresher than the duchess and perhaps less expensive. The boulevards glittered after rain, the chestnut leaves wore their first varnish of green, and every passing carriage seemed to be carrying either a romance or a bill.

I had ordered coffee and the evening papers, but was occupied with neither, when a tall man with a grave English face stopped beside my table.

“Surely you are Murchison?” I said.

He looked at me for a moment and smiled.

“I was Murchison at Oxford. I have since become Lord Murchison, which is less amusing.”

I made him sit down. We had not met for nearly ten years. At college he had been handsome, candid, and incapable of owing sixpence without confessing it to the nearest dean. Age had improved his appearance but not, I suspected, his philosophy. His hair was touched with grey at the temples, and sorrow—or the House of Peers—had given him a certain stiffness.

“You have married?” I asked.

“No.”

“That is remarkable. A man with your income is generally captured before he has learned the value of escape.”

He did not answer. Instead, he drew from his coat a small case of dark green morocco and handed it to me.

Inside was the photograph of a woman. She wore some soft silver stuff, and about her shoulders lay a sable cloak. Her face was pale and delicately oval; her eyes, large and uncertain, appeared to look through the spectator at something which they alone could see. There was no smile on her lips, but the absence of a smile seemed more expressive than gaiety. One loosened strand of hair crossed her temple like a stroke left unfinished by a painter.

“The Gioconda in winter,” I said. “Who was she?”

“Lady Alroy.”

The verb disturbed me.

“She is dead, then?”

“She died last autumn.”

I closed the case.

“You loved her?”

“Passionately.”

“And she?”

“That is what I have never known.”

“My dear Murchison, women are to be adored, not deciphered. The instant a man attempts interpretation, he ceases to be a lover and becomes a magistrate.”

“I wanted only the truth.”

“Truth is an excellent thing in accounts and a dangerous one in affection.”

He gave me the impatient look of a man who suffers and therefore considers wit immoral.

“You may laugh,” he said, “but perhaps you will understand if I tell you.”

He began after a pause.

“I first saw Lady Alroy in Bond Street. It was near five o’clock, in November, and a yellow brougham was drawn up before a bookseller’s. She sat within it, wrapped in furs, with a veil over her face. I could distinguish scarcely a feature, yet I had the extraordinary sensation that I had seen her in a dream. She turned suddenly and looked at me. It was not a glance of recognition, but of alarm—as though I had surprised a thought she believed herself alone in possessing.

“The carriage moved away before I could inquire who she was. For three days I thought of nothing else. I called her ma belle inconnue, which gave my curiosity the dignity of a French emotion.

“A week later I dined at the Brabazons’. She was there. When I entered the drawing-room, she was standing beneath a Venetian mirror, speaking to a Russian attaché. She wore moonstones, and her dress was the colour of a cloud before snow. Mrs Brabazon presented me.

“‘I think I saw you lately in Bond Street,’ I said.

“She grew pale.

“‘I am often in Bond Street.’

“‘You were in a yellow brougham.’

“She touched her fan to her lips. ‘Surely one carriage is very like another.’

“Then she turned to the Russian and spoke of the Opera. Her voice was low and musical, but there was in it that faint tremor one hears in a glass when distant wheels pass along a street.

“After dinner I contrived to sit beside her. She was a widow; her husband had been dead two years. She had a house in Park Lane, no children, and, apparently, no intimate friends. Her conversation was charming, but evasive. She would tell me what flowers she disliked, but not where she had spent the summer; she discussed immortality with freedom, but became reserved if I asked whether she would be at the Duchess of Paisley’s on Thursday.

“Nevertheless, she permitted me to call.

“When I went to Park Lane, I was told she was at home. Yet the servant, before admitting me, disappeared for several minutes, and I heard a door close softly upstairs. Lady Alroy received me in a small room hung with old tapestries. There were white lilacs on the table and a fire behind a silver screen, though the day was warm.

“During our conversation she repeatedly looked toward the door.

“‘Are you expecting someone?’ I asked.

“‘No. Why should you think so?’

“‘You seem afraid of being overheard.’

“She smiled. ‘People who have nothing to conceal are always afraid of listeners. It is only the guilty who feel secure.’

“At that moment a servant entered with letters. She gathered them quickly, and I noticed that one was addressed in a man’s hand to “Mrs Knox.” She slipped it under a cushion.

“I ought to have thought nothing of it. Instead, I thought of it for a fortnight.

“I saw her constantly that winter—at dinners, at the Opera, in crowded drawing-rooms where she seemed lonelier than any person I had ever known. She was beautiful, though not with the beauty sculptors understand. Her charm was psychological, not plastic. One admired in her not the perfection of a line, but the promise of a meaning. She was like a strange crystal in which one expects every moment to see an image form.

“I fell in love with her—in spite of her mystery, I told myself; perhaps, in consequence of it.

“One afternoon I asked her to dine with my aunt the following Tuesday. She hesitated.

“‘Write to me,’ she said, ‘but do not address the letter here.’

“She gave me a name and an address in Bayswater.

“‘Mrs Knox again?’ I asked.

“She became agitated. ‘You must not call me that.’

“‘Then why use the name?’

“‘Because I ask you to.’

“‘That is not an answer.’

“‘It is the only one I can give.’

“I sent the note as she directed, and she came to dinner. She was brilliant that evening, almost happy; but when my aunt mentioned Bayswater, Lady Alroy dropped her spoon, and the little sound it made against the china seemed to terrify her.

“You may imagine what passed in my mind. A hidden marriage, blackmail, some political intrigue—every absurdity became plausible because none could be proved. Jealousy is the imagination of a realist: it invents details in order to punish itself with facts.

“Then came the incident that ended everything.

“I had been at my club and was walking toward Oxford Street when I saw Lady Alroy ahead of me. She wore a thick veil and moved quickly. I was about to overtake her when she turned into a shabby street near Marylebone. I followed at a distance.

“She stopped before a narrow house with dingy curtains. After looking behind her, she took a latch-key from her glove and entered.

“I stood there like a fool. As the door closed, she dropped her handkerchief. I picked it up. Her initials were worked in one corner.

“For an hour I walked through the rain. The street, the key, the false name, her fear—all became parts of a single hateful certainty. That evening I went to her house. She was in the drawing-room, dressed for dinner, with yellow roses at her breast.

“‘Where have you been today?’ I asked.

“She looked surprised. ‘Nowhere. I did not leave the house.’

“I placed the handkerchief on the table.

“Her face became absolutely white.

“‘You followed me?’

“‘I saw you enter a house in a disreputable street.’

“‘There was nothing disreputable in my going there.’

“‘Then why deny it?’

“She rose. ‘Because I did not wish you to know.’

“‘Whom did you meet?’

“‘No one.’

“‘What did you do?’

“‘Nothing.’

“I laughed. Her answer seemed the last refinement of insult.

“‘You expect me to believe that you disguise yourself, use another name, carry a secret key, and visit a hidden lodging merely to do nothing?’

“‘It is true.’

“‘No truth requires so many lies.’

“She put out her hand. ‘Gerald, trust me.’

“‘I cannot love where I cannot trust.’

“‘And I cannot explain what has no explanation.’

“I remember every word. I remember the roses against her dress, the firelight on her rings, the curious calm that returned to her face when she saw that I had condemned her. She looked no longer frightened. She looked remote.

“‘Then we have nothing more to say,’ she murmured.

“I bowed and left her.

“The next morning a letter came from her. I recognized the handwriting and returned it unopened. That was a cruel thing, but anger is merely cruelty conscious of its rights. Two days later I went abroad. I wandered through Egypt and India, trying to escape a woman whom I carried everywhere with me. At Cairo I saw her eyes in the painted faces of dead queens; at Benares I heard her voice beneath the temple bells. I had loved her so deeply, so insanely, that absence did not lessen love; it merely deprived love of occupation.

“After six months I returned to England. On my first evening in London I learned that she was dead. She had caught a chill at the Opera and died within a week.

“I cannot tell you what I felt. Grief has no vocabulary; it borrows cries from animals and silence from stones.

“For months I did nothing. Then, last February, I went to the street where I had followed her. The house had grown even shabbier. A card in the window announced rooms to let.

“An elderly woman admitted me. I told her I wished to inquire about the first floor.

“‘Very comfortable for a gentleman,’ she said. ‘And quite private.’

“‘Was it recently occupied by a lady?’

“She examined me sharply. ‘You mean the pale lady with the veil?’

“I said yes.

“‘A pleasant lady, though fanciful. She rented the sitting-room by the hour, two or three times a week.’

“‘Who visited her?’

“‘Nobody, sir.’

“‘You need not protect her. She is dead.’

“‘Dead?’ The woman seemed genuinely shocked. ‘Poor thing! But there was no one to protect her from. She always came alone.’

“‘What did she do there?’

“‘Sat by the window, mostly. Sometimes she read, sometimes she had tea. Once I heard her walking about and talking, but when I entered she was alone. She paid regularly and asked me never to mention her visits. That is all.’

“I searched the rooms. They were commonplace: a horsehair sofa, two engravings of Italian lakes, a clock under glass, and a smell of extinguished coal. There was no concealed door, no packet of letters, no evidence of any other life. The woman swore that Lady Alroy had never received a visitor.

“So now you know,” Murchison concluded. “What am I to believe? Was she innocent? Did the landlady lie? Was there some secret still?”

He had finished speaking. The lamps were bright along the boulevard, and beyond them the sky had the colour of tarnished silver. Paris had begun her evening transformation, in which respectable people grow adventurous and adventurers become respectable.

I returned the morocco case to him.

“My dear Gerald, Lady Alroy had no secret.”

He stared at me.

“No secret?”

“She loved secrecy itself. She rented a room in order to draw a veil across an empty window. She used a false name because her own name had no romance in it. She wished to feel that she possessed a hidden life, and the hidden life consisted in drinking tea where nobody knew her. Some women create scandals; she created atmosphere.”

“You think it was all pretence?”

“Not pretence. Imagination. Pretence deceives others; imagination begins by deceiving oneself. She was an actress whose audience was her own reflection.”

“A Sphinx, then, with no riddle?”

“A Sphinx without a secret.”

For some moments he was silent. He looked again at the photograph. Under the café lights the pale face seemed almost alive, but the vague eyes preserved their distance.

“Perhaps,” he said at last. “And yet—”

He closed the case and slipped it into his coat.

“And yet?” I asked.

He rose. The crowd received him, and for an instant his grey figure was lost among uniforms, silk hats, painted faces and black umbrellas.

Then he turned back.

“I wonder?” he said.

### Deposition Before the Academy of Practical Sciences

Gentlemen of the Academy,

You have requested an account of my development from an ape into what your secretary, with a tact for which I am grateful, calls “a person of established public standing.” I accept the phrase. It has the advantage of describing my position without examining it.

I must begin, however, by disappointing you. You ask for recollections of my former life: the damp forests of the Gold Coast, the society of my troop, the arrangement of our affections and quarrels, perhaps even some observations concerning the origin of speech. On these matters I can offer almost nothing. The gate through which I came has closed behind me. At first I could still see through its narrowing crack—a branch moving after I had left it, a female’s gray shoulder, the red pulp of a fruit crushed in my hand. Then the crack became a bright line. Now even that line is gone.

It is not modesty that prevents me from answering. Modesty, as I have learned, is useful chiefly when accepting applause. I truly do not know. Human language, which has served me admirably in business, contracts, interviews, railway stations, and after-dinner remarks, will not reach backward into my ape existence. The better I learned to speak, the less clearly I remembered what existed before speech. Education illuminated the room in which I stood, but it burned the corridor behind me.

What follows is therefore assembled partly from memory and partly from the testimony of men who were present, though men who shoot at dusk, drink until dawn, and later sell their reminiscences to newspapers are not ideal archivists. Still, the Academy will understand that history frequently rests upon worse authorities.

I was captured near the coast by an expedition belonging to the firm of Bruckner & Sons of Hamburg, suppliers of animals to zoological gardens, private collections, medical schools, and certain restaurants that preferred not to print their complete bills of fare. I am told that I was a strong young male and unusually quick in the eyes. This last judgment may be true. It was my eyes, in the end, that saved me, though not because they inspired mercy.

The hunters approached our feeding ground at twilight. Two shots struck me. One entered beneath the left shoulder and left by the back. The scar remains, broad and shiny, and during wet weather it pulls as if a hook had been fastened inside me. The second shot lodged in the upper thigh, close enough to a delicate region that the ship’s men later found the matter inexhaustibly funny. The bullet was never removed. It is with me now, gentlemen, a small European contribution introduced before the rest of European culture.

I fell from a low tree. Whether I cried out, attacked, or tried to crawl away, I cannot say. A sailor who claimed to have helped bind me stated that I bit through a leather strap and broke one man’s thumb. Another insisted that I lay peacefully and watched them. Both accounts were published. The peaceful version appeared in a family magazine; the broken thumb was preferred by the illustrated weeklies.

When I regained a durable awareness of my surroundings, I was aboard ship.

My cage was not, properly speaking, a cage. It was a packing frame boarded on three sides and barred on the fourth, placed between the rail and a stack of casks. Its height did not allow me to stand. Its length did not permit me to stretch. If I crouched with my back against the boards, the bars touched my knees; if I turned, my wound scraped the timber. Above me hung a coil of rope, always wet. Below me was a grating through which urine, seawater, tobacco juice, and soup passed according to the hour.

The arrangement was efficient. I could not leap, run, climb, or even scratch myself properly. The men had removed the fleas they could see, but a vigorous colony survived beneath my arms, and I lacked sufficient room to negotiate with them. I sat folded into myself.

Permit me, gentlemen, to emphasize one point. In that box I did not long for freedom.

The word is admired among you. It is shouted in parliaments, printed beneath eagles, and sung by men marching in identical boots. I have watched trapeze artists fly from hand to hand under a painted ceiling while the audience gasped, “How free!” Yet there was a net below, a manager at the curtain, a contract in the office, and supper only after the final bow. I do not criticize this. I merely observe that freedom is often the name given to a well-rehearsed movement inside agreed limits.

No, I did not desire freedom. Freedom would have meant breaking the bars, crossing a deck full of armed men, and entering the sea. Even had I succeeded, the sea would have freed me thoroughly and for a short duration. Or I might have hidden among the rigging until hunger brought me down. Or escaped in port and been shot beside a warehouse. Such possibilities are not freedom. They are conclusions.

What I needed was a way out.

Do not make the common error of giving grandeur to this phrase. A way out need not lead upward. It need not lead home. It need not even lead into open air. It is simply the direction in which destruction is not, for the moment, waiting with its mouth open.

I pressed my back against the boards and looked through the bars. Looking was the one movement allowed me, and therefore I developed it professionally.

The sailors came and went. They inspected me, laughed at me, poked straw through the bars, and called me names that I later learned were also used affectionately among themselves. Their cruelty has been exaggerated. They did not hate me. Hatred requires attention. To them I was cargo that breathed and sometimes amused them.

One man rubbed salt into my shoulder wound, but only to see whether I would shriek. Another gave me a lump of sugar afterward. A third extinguished his cigar on the boards near my hand and apologized when the spark reached my fur, though he apologized to the cigar. They were, on the whole, ordinary decent men. I say this without irony—or with only that quantity of irony necessary for accuracy.

They spat continually.

At first I believed spitting to be a symptom of illness. Soon I saw that it had a social function. A man spat before lifting a rope, after telling a joke, upon losing at cards, and into the sea when no more appropriate surface was available. The act accompanied decision. It was a form of punctuation performed by the mouth.

I began to practice.

My first attempts lacked direction. Saliva ran down my chin and gathered in the fur of my chest. The men applauded. Encouraged, I drew the moisture into my mouth, pushed it forward with my tongue, and one morning projected it through the bars onto the boot of the cook’s assistant.

The success was immediate. The assistant struck the bars with a ladle, but the others laughed until one of them sat down. I had done what they did. More importantly, they had recognized it.

Recognition, gentlemen, is a hinge. Before that moment they looked at me. Afterward, occasionally, they looked for me.

I repeated the trick. I learned to spit to the left, though never elegantly to the right, perhaps because of the shoulder wound. I learned to shake hands through the bars. This custom puzzled me longer. A man extends an empty hand to demonstrate he carries no weapon, then grips firmly enough to show that he could use one. Civilization contains many such compressed negotiations.

Smoking followed. A sailor named Voss—though three later claimed the honor—pushed a lighted pipe between the bars. I bit through the stem. They roared. A second pipe was offered. I held it correctly after watching them, drew smoke into my mouth, swallowed it, and collapsed coughing against the boards.

The pain was severe, but the effect upon the audience was excellent.

Within a week I could keep a pipe between my lips, puff twice, remove it, inspect the bowl, and nod as Voss did. I never liked tobacco. I still do not. I smoke after dinner because refusal makes hosts nervous and because the management of smoke about the face gives one time to prepare an answer.

Alcohol was more difficult.

The bottle came to me on a hot evening. It contained raw spirits. One sailor uncorked it with his teeth, drank, smacked his lips, and handed it through the bars. The smell repelled me. I threw the bottle down. Fortunately it did not break, or my education might have ended there.

They shouted, demonstrated again, and forced the bottle into my hand. I raised it as they did, but at the last moment I could not drink. I poured it into the grating.

This disappointed them. Disappointment in a crowd is dangerous. They shook the cage, jabbed me with a broom handle, and showed their teeth. I understood no words, but the examination conditions were plain.

During the next attempts I swallowed small quantities. My throat burned; my stomach convulsed; I fouled the cage. This restored their good humor. Men forgive failure readily when it ends in filth.

I persisted. Not because I wanted the liquor, nor because I had discovered an admiration for my instructors. I persisted because beyond the bottle stood the door of the box. I could not yet see how the two were connected, but I saw that the men passed freely where I could not, and the bottle belonged to their passage.

One evening, with the entire watch assembled, I uncorked a bottle, raised it, drank without stopping, tossed it away, slapped my belly, and cried out.

The sound I made was not yet a word. The newspapers later represented it as “Good evening,” while a temperance society claimed that my first human utterance was “Never again.” Both are false. It was a rough explosion, half cough, half bark. But it resembled the sailors’ exclamation closely enough.

They became silent.

Then the deck erupted. Caps flew into the air. Voss embraced the cook’s assistant. Someone rang the ship’s bell without authorization. A funnel was brought, and more spirits were poured into me in honor of my achievement. I lost consciousness among cheers.

When I woke, I was still in the cage, but my position within it had changed. Before, I had been an animal attempting tricks. Now I was an animal suspected of progress.

That suspicion opened my way out.

Speech did not arrive by miracle. I did not awaken one morning with grammar in my mouth. I stole sounds individually. The lips of men fascinated me. I watched how they pressed together, how the tongue appeared and withdrew, how the throat tightened. For hours I repeated the same noise until it detached itself from my old cries and stood separately, ugly but useful.

“Water” was among the first. “Voss” came easily, because it required little refinement from a hiss. “Yes” was invaluable. “No” took longer—not as a sound, but as a privilege. In the beginning, every correct answer was yes.

My instructors beat the cage when I failed and rewarded me when I succeeded. There is nothing remarkable in this. Your schools use examinations, salaries, humiliation, medals, and the threat of poverty. The instruments differ; the melody is familiar.

By the time we entered Hamburg, I could drink from a glass, smoke, shake hands, spit with judgment, wear a loose pair of trousers, and say perhaps twenty words. The trousers deserve mention. They were too large and secured with rope, but the sailors’ delight at seeing me dressed surpassed the pleasure they had taken in my wounds. Nakedness, I discovered, is offensive chiefly when it resembles the observer.

At the docks I faced my first important administrative decision.

Representatives of two institutions came aboard. One was from the zoological garden. He measured me through the bars, examined my teeth, and discussed ventilation. The other represented a variety theater. He wore a green coat and carried a cane with a silver knob. He asked me to smoke. I smoked. He asked me to salute. I saluted. He asked whether I wanted employment.

I said, “Yes.”

The zoo offered security. I would have had a larger cage, regular food, painted rocks, perhaps a dead tree, and a brass plate containing my place of origin. Children would have thrown nuts at me. Naturalists would have watched me mate. In time, I might have become the dignified father of a captive family.

The theater offered uncertainty, pain, ridicule, and a possibility.

I chose the theater.

Again, gentlemen: not freedom. A way out.

My first trainer was Herr Eber. He had trained dogs, ponies, two pigs, a goose that played a drum, and a man from Saxony who pretended to be a cannibal. Herr Eber possessed patience of a hard, economical kind. He never struck me from anger. He struck me toward a result.

This distinction mattered. Anger is wasteful. A blow delivered in anger may fall anywhere. Herr Eber’s blows were instructional and therefore landed exactly where the lesson required.

We worked in a locked room from dawn until late evening. I learned to stand upright for longer periods. My hips suffered. My feet bled inside boots. My spine, built for another arrangement, protested each civilized hour. Eber tied a cord beneath my chin so that I would hold my head high. When I dropped onto my knuckles, he touched a hot iron to the floor in front of them. Once my fur caught fire at the wrist. He put it out immediately and praised me for remaining upright.

He was a conscientious man.

Language became the central labor. I learned nouns first: table, chair, glass, door, money. Abstract terms came later and caused more trouble. “Duty” meant doing what was demanded before the stick appeared. “Success” meant the stick did not appear at all. “Dignity” meant enduring the lesson without showing the teeth. “Freedom,” when used by the theater director, meant that my contract had been renewed.

At night Herr Eber slept badly on a sofa in the adjoining room. I heard him groan, mutter, and grind his teeth. Sometimes he woke and stared at me through the glass panel in the door. I believe that my progress terrified him. If I failed, he lost his employment. If I succeeded completely, he lost his distinction from me. Thus we educated one another.

After six months he suffered a collapse. His hands shook so violently that he could no longer light my cigarettes. He was sent to a sanatorium, where, according to a letter I received years afterward, he enjoyed long walks behind iron gates.

Three trainers followed. One improved my pronunciation by placing pebbles under my tongue. One taught me to bow, laugh on cue, and pretend embarrassment when ladies examined me. The last, Madame Sorel, instructed me in conversation. She had once been an actress and had the invaluable ability to repeat the same sentence fifty times while appearing to hate it more on each occasion.

Under her direction I read children’s primers, menus, newspapers, and finally plays. Newspapers were especially useful because they showed me that fluent speech does not require knowledge.

My debut took place in Bremen. I entered wearing evening clothes, stumbled deliberately over a carpet, shook hands with the conductor, drank a glass of brandy, and delivered a brief speech thanking the audience for welcoming “a colleague from the tropics.” The line had been written for me. It received eleven minutes of applause.

The audience did not come to see an ape become a man. They came to see a man fail to conceal an ape. I learned to scratch discreetly, bare my teeth at mention of marriage, leap onto the piano when startled, then apologize in polished phrases. My success depended upon approaching humanity and retreating from it at the proper moments.

Too little humanity, and I belonged in the zoo. Too much, and I became competition.

I mastered the balance.

Years passed. I toured Prague, Vienna, Paris, Rotterdam, and smaller cities whose names appeared only on invoices. I acquired tutors. I studied history, arithmetic, geography, and the social use of ignorance. I learned to distinguish forks, wines, uniforms, and degrees of insult. I learned that one should not smell food before eating it, though one may discuss its aroma for several minutes. I learned to cover my mouth when yawning but not when lying.

At the end of this education, five examining gentlemen certified that I possessed the intellectual attainments of an average European adult. I treasure the certificate. It is framed above my writing desk. The insult, if one was intended, is so evenly distributed that no individual need complain.

My fame grew. Managers competed for my services. I traveled in a private compartment. Hotel servants who would once have driven me from the kitchen now carried hot water to my suite. Journalists asked whether I dreamed in human language. Ladies requested locks of my fur; when supply became inadequate, my dresser provided clippings from a brown carpet. No one noticed.

I accumulated money. Money is the most portable form of enclosure and therefore the most respected. With it I rented spacious rooms, employed a secretary, and purchased the right to be left alone at selected hours.

You may ask whether I was happy.

The question is academically understandable and practically useless. Happiness belonged to neither my plan nor my contract. I sought a way out. I found one. The fact that the passage opened into a stage, then into dining rooms, railway carriages, hotel corridors, and this distinguished hall does not alter its nature.

Still, I do not deny my achievements. That would be ingratitude toward myself. No other animal known to me has traveled so far in so short a time without being carried in a crate. I changed my posture, diet, voice, sleep, desires, gestures, and memory. I suppressed the impulses that could not be employed and developed those that received applause. Men call this character.

Yet the cost cannot be calculated merely by listing scars.

Occasionally, late at night, when I have returned from a performance and dismissed my servants, some ancient movement begins in my shoulder. My hand reaches upward for a branch that is not there. My jaw loosens. A pressure gathers behind my eyes. If I allow it to continue, the room alters: curtains become leaves, gaslight becomes a yellow opening through rain, and the polished furniture seems absurdly low and dead.

I stop it at once.

I recite multiplication tables. I read stock prices aloud. I button and unbutton my waistcoat. Usually this is sufficient. Civilization, once properly installed, is maintained by small repairs.

For companionship I keep, in a room adjoining my own, a young female chimpanzee obtained from a dealer in Antwerp. She is half trained. During the day she amuses herself with ribbons, cups, and a wooden spoon. In the evening, when I return from the theater, I sometimes visit her.

I will not sentimentalize the arrangement. She provides warmth and a certain bodily satisfaction. She has been taught not to bite, except when frightened, and the keeper washes her regularly. From the standpoint of household management, there is little to criticize.

But I cannot remain with her in daylight.

Her eyes prevent it.

When she looks at me, I see no innocence, no noble wilderness, no lost paradise of the kind painters place behind unclothed women and melancholy lions. I see confusion pressed against the inside of a skull. I see the cage before the animal understands the bars. I see an uncompleted passage, begun but not survived.

Sometimes she imitates my bow. Sometimes she lifts a cup as if to drink and spills the contents into her fur. Then she bares her teeth, waiting for punishment or applause; she does not know which. I know. That is the difference between us.

Gentlemen, I have been accused of betraying my nature. The accusation assumes that nature offered me protection. It did not. Nature bled on the deck and crouched in its own waste. Nature beat against boards until its fingers split. If I betrayed anything, I betrayed a sentence already pronounced upon me.

Others call me free. I reject that honor. Freedom is a word men bestow upon visible success in order not to examine the machinery beneath it. I have no wish to insult your ideals. Ideals are excellent decorations for gates. But my concern was never with the view above the gate. My concern was the opening, however narrow, through which I might pass alive.

I passed.

The cage is no longer around me. This is true. Its dimensions, however, have become difficult to determine. At times it seems to include my apartment, my contracts, my cultivated pronunciation, the respectful attention of this Academy. At other times it has shrunk to a pressure in the thigh where the old bullet rests. One should not exaggerate. A cage that cannot be measured is of limited interest to administrators.

You requested a report, not a complaint. I have supplied the principal facts: capture, injury, transport, observation, imitation, instruction, employment, success. The sequence is orderly. It will fit your records.

As for judgment, I leave it to you. You are men of learning and therefore accustomed to placing names upon transformations after they have occurred. Call mine education, adaptation, advancement, or surrender. Each word touches part of the matter and avoids the rest.

I ask only that you do not call it liberation.

I did not find freedom.

I found a way out.

That was enough to keep me alive, and life, gentlemen, is a demanding examiner. It does not require that the answer be noble. It requires only that an answer be given.

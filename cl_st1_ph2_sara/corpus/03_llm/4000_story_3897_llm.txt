Thursdale had always considered candour a primitive virtue—admirable in children, perhaps, and in those large, unmodulated natures whose very defects possess the convenience of scenery. In a man of his experience, however, frankness would have seemed not so much immoral as inartistic: a failure to perceive those intermediate shades in which the difficult business of living acquires something of composition. He had never lied to a woman; his distinction rested on having made truth unnecessary.

There were, of course, women who resented such a method. They demanded statements, dates, definitions; they wished to carry the delicate crockery of emotion away from the picnic and arrange it permanently upon their domestic shelves. With these Thursdale had been brief. But finer material required finer handling, and in Margaret Vervain he had found, some eight years earlier, a nature singularly responsive to the gradations of his art.

She had been a widow then for two years: sufficiently released from grief to recognise solitude, but not yet so accustomed to it as to mistake it for freedom. Her marriage had been one of those unions in which society, having provided the frame, feels absolved from examining the picture. Mr. Vervain had left her a house, an income, and no memories crude enough to be complained of. She emerged from his mild oppression with the dangerous gratitude of a woman who thinks that happiness must consist simply in not being unhappy.

Thursdale’s appearance had consequently possessed the effect of sudden music in a quiet room. He was handsome without offering his looks as evidence, cultivated without the fatigue of displaying cultivation, and attentive with that measured incompleteness which allows a woman to supply the omitted ardour. He had never asked Margaret to love him. The privilege of loving a woman was, in his view, one of the least favours she could grant; but to permit oneself to be loved involved responsibilities which only an expert knew how to evade gracefully.

His success with Margaret had been complete. At first there had been moments—small outbreaks of sincerity, as distressing as rents in fine silk—when she had seemed ready to question him. He had met these crises neither by retreat nor advance, but by a deepened tenderness so subtly proportioned that she mistook reassurance for promise. Gradually she learned his values. She learned not to ask where he had been, not to attach significance to intervals of absence, not to disfigure a reunion by accounting for its cost. She learned that jealousy was ugly, insistence provincial, and suffering, when unavoidable, best worn beneath a surface of perfect ease.

He had been proud of her. There was something almost creative in the transformation: he had taken a warm, impulsive woman and taught her half-tones. Under his influence her feelings acquired chiaroscuro, her speech a beautiful economy, her silences the expressive finish of good acting. She became, in fact, the one person before whom he could be entirely at ease, since he had trained her never to require him to be entirely anything.

It was therefore characteristic that, when he became engaged to Ruth Gaynor, his first serious concern should have been not Margaret’s pain but her performance.

The engagement surprised even him. Ruth was twenty-six, candid, grave, and touched by that moral directness which in a less charming woman he would have called angular. She had not Margaret’s flexible intelligence, nor her instinct for arranging the cushions of conversation around an awkward fact; but she had a clean brightness that made his accustomed penumbra seem, for the moment, stale. With her, speech appeared capable of meaning what it said. He found the discovery exhilarating. It was like stepping from a picture-gallery into morning.

That morning might prove too bright for habitual use; but marriage, Thursdale reflected, was perhaps the art of selecting one’s permanent light. He was forty-three. There came an age when chiaroscuro ceased to flatter and merely obscured. Ruth’s admiration, moreover, had in it something bracingly unconditional. She believed him distinguished, generous, and a little sad; and the exquisite completeness of her error touched him more deeply than a just estimate might have done.

He had told Margaret of the engagement himself. It was part of his code that unpleasant news should never be entrusted to inferior workmanship. She had received it as he knew she would: with a pause so slight that only the artist responsible for it could measure its perfection, and then with an affectionate curiosity about Ruth. No scene, no reproach, no vulgar effort to convert the past into a claim. Margaret had even asked to meet her.

The meeting took place three days later in Margaret’s drawing room, over tea administered with the noiseless certainty of a sacrament. Ruth had been charming; Margaret more charming still. Thursdale, watching them, experienced the serene pride of a manager whose two leading actresses, after rumours of disagreement, sustain the ensemble faultlessly. Margaret had struck precisely the right note—not maternal, which might have seemed an impertinence; not intimate, which would have alarmed; but lightly cordial, with here and there a phrase indicating that she had known Thursdale long enough to recommend him without appearing to guarantee him.

Ruth, on leaving, had kissed her.

That kiss had momentarily disconcerted Thursdale. It was too direct a gesture for the composition, a splash of unmixed colour upon a carefully balanced canvas. Yet Margaret’s acceptance of it had been beautiful. She had laid a hand on the girl’s shoulder and said: “You must come again—alone, if he ever lets you.”

They had all laughed; and Thursdale, as he accompanied Ruth away, had felt that the most delicate difficulty of his engagement was disposed of.

The next afternoon he returned to Margaret’s house to thank her. He would have said that he went to see how she was, but he had eliminated from his vocabulary those dishonest phrases which deceive no one but the speaker. He wanted praise. He wanted her to confirm that Ruth was lovely, that he had chosen wisely, that the transition had been accomplished without damage to the old harmony. Above all, he wished to enjoy with Margaret the retrospective appreciation of her own good taste.

Her drawing room had the composed warmth he associated with her: low lamps, a fire diminished to an architectural glow, flowers whose scent seemed less emitted than remembered. In that room every object knew its place, and human emotion, on entering, instinctively lowered its voice.

Margaret sat near the window with a book closed upon her knee. The declining light made her face unusually pale, though her dress—one of those grey dresses that appeared colourless until one noticed how many colours it subdued—gave her pallor an intentional refinement.

“You came,” she said.

“My dear Margaret! Could you doubt it?”

“I have doubted more probable things.”

He smiled, appreciating the phrase while detecting beneath it a faint roughness of surface. “I came to receive my congratulations on the success of yesterday.”

“Your success?”

“Ours, if you insist. Though I claim only the casting. The performance was yours.”

She looked down at the book. “Was it so good?”

“Perfect. There was one instant—when Ruth kissed you—when I feared an improvisation. But you rescued even that.”

Margaret’s fingers tightened on the cover. “She is very young.”

“Not so young as candid. It gives the effect of youth.”

“And you like candour now?”

“In its place. One does not wish every room hung in half-tones.”

“No. One might forget what daylight is.”

He glanced at her more closely. The phrase, though perfectly delivered, had the disagreeable edge of something not shaped for his amusement.

“You approve of her?” he asked.

Margaret raised her eyes. “Do you need me to?”

“I should like you to.”

“Then I approve.”

“That is not your best manner. Yesterday you were incomparable; today you sound almost sincere.”

A colour rose and vanished in her face. “Ruth came back.”

He had taken the chair opposite her, but at the words he shifted slightly, as if the angle of the light had changed.

“Came back?”

“After you had taken her home. At half past six.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

He waited. Margaret seemed to expect that he would ask the next question, and his dislike of being forced into an obvious part made him delay it.

“My dear Margaret,” he said at last, “how mysterious! Did she forget a glove?”

“No. She remembered a doubt.”

“A doubt about me?”

“About us.”

The monosyllable dropped between them with an appalling absence of reverberation. It was the word they had spent eight years not pronouncing; and its smallness, now that it lay exposed, made all their precautions seem grotesque.

Thursdale rose and went to the chimney-piece. Movement had always helped him to give thought the appearance of leisure.

“What did she ask?”

Margaret turned the book over on her knee. “Whether you were free.”

His hand, resting among the porcelain ornaments, became motionless. “Free?”

“She used the word several times. It seemed important to her.”

“And you told her, naturally, that I was engaged to her.”

“She did not mean legally free.”

“Ruth is too intelligent to suppose that I have a hidden wife.”

“She did not mean that either.”

He gave a slight laugh. “Then the inquiry becomes metaphysical.”

“No. It was terribly simple.”

He looked at Margaret. Something in her quietness warned him that the old mechanism of their intercourse had ceased to work. He was speaking the familiar language, but she no longer translated it into the expected meanings.

“What did you tell her?” he repeated.

“The truth.”

There are truths which, like certain explosives, are harmless until confined; Thursdale felt suddenly that the entire structure of his past had become their container.

“You told her that we had been friends.”

“I told her that I had loved you.”

The room appeared to sharpen around him. The edges of the furniture, the separate petals of the flowers, even the pale seam of ash between the fire’s red fragments, sprang into a cruel visibility.

“Margaret!”

“She asked me.”

“But one does not answer such questions.”

“You have taught me that for eight years. Yesterday I forgot the lesson.”

He stared at her with the offended astonishment of a craftsman who sees his finest work deliberately broken by the object of it.

“What precisely did you say?”

“That I had loved you from the beginning. That I had expected nothing. That you had promised nothing. That there had never been—anything.”

The final word came with difficulty, and his relief at hearing it was so instinctive that he did not at once perceive its danger.

“Then I don’t understand. If you made that clear—”

“She understood perfectly.”

“She understood that there was no claim, no entanglement, no past which could dishonour—”

“Dishonour whom?”

He paused. “You know what I mean.”

“Yes. I know what you have always meant.”

She stood and put the book on the table, but her hand missed the polished surface; the volume fell, opening awkwardly upon the carpet. Neither of them stooped for it.

“Ruth thought,” Margaret continued, “that if I had loved you, you must have loved me. She could imagine betrayal, weakness, even cruelty of the ordinary kind. She came prepared, I think, to forgive a passion.”

“A passion?”

“A past. Something violent, human, perhaps disgraceful. She wanted to know whether it was over.”

“And you assured her there had been nothing.”

“Yes.”

“Then what remains?”

Margaret looked at him with an expression he had never seen in her—not reproach, which he could have answered, nor tenderness, which he could have controlled, but a kind of exhausted wonder.

“I remained.”

For a moment he found no phrase sufficiently prepared. “I’m afraid I don’t follow.”

“That is what she saw. I remained—year after year. You knew what I felt; you took all it gave you—companionship, devotion, understanding, the certainty of finding me whenever the rest of the world bored or wounded you. You accepted everything except the one thing that might have cost you something.”

“This is Ruth’s interpretation?”

“Does it matter whose it is?”

“It matters enormously. I am trying to discover whether you accused me.”

“I defended you.”

The answer restored him slightly. Defence implied attack; attack could be examined, disproved, perhaps converted into misunderstanding.

“Against what?”

“Against the charge of having done nothing.”

He turned from the chimney-piece. “Really, Margaret, that is ingenious to the point of perversity. I might understand being blamed if I had taken advantage of your feeling. But to be condemned because I respected—”

“You respected what?”

“Your position. Your name. Our friendship.”

“My name!” A brief, hard sound escaped her. “Yes, you kept that bright. You polished it while everything else burned.”

“Do you mean that you wished—?”

“No. I don’t know what I wished. You took care that I should cease to know.”

The crudity of the accusation wounded his aesthetic sense before it reached his conscience. He had always believed that their relation’s distinction lay precisely in its freedom from common exactions. Was he now to be punished because he had refused the obvious vulgarity? It was as though Shylock, having declined the flesh, should be haled before the court for leaving the debtor intact.

He drew a chair nearer, choosing a lower one which allowed his voice to take on the gentleness of appeal.

“Margaret, you are excited. Ruth’s visit has upset you. Perhaps she spoke harshly—she has the absolutism of innocence. But you know, whatever she may say, that I never consciously caused you pain.”

“No. You caused it unconsciously, then taught me to conceal it consciously. That was your science.”

He recoiled from the word because it was his own. In some earlier and easier hour he had jestingly described his dealings with women as a science of omissions. Repeated now, the phrase had the ugliness of an instrument found beside a wound.

“What exactly did Ruth say?” he asked.

Margaret bent and picked up the book. She closed it carefully, smoothing a folded page with her palm before replying.

“She asked whether you had ever loved me. I said no.”

“You could not know that.”

“I said I believed not.”

“That is different.”

“Not to her.”

“And then?”

“She asked whether you knew that I loved you. I tried to evade her. She would not let me.”

“How could she possibly judge?”

“She judged from the way I looked at you yesterday.”

His vanity, even under threat, made a last involuntary movement toward gratification; and Margaret, seeing it, turned her face away.

“She said,” she went on, “‘If he knew, why did he come so often? Why did he let you give him your life?’ I told her I had given nothing you asked for. She said that was worse.”

“Worse?”

“Because you had arranged it so that asking was unnecessary.”

He began to pace the narrow space between the hearth and the tea-table. The room seemed encumbered by the very objects which had once supported his ease. A silver box, a bowl of violets, the lamp’s pleated shade—each appeared to preserve some trace of his innumerable visits, like debris left after a long, decorous picnic. How much time had he spent there? How many winters measured by that fire, how many springs condensed into the scent of those flowers? He had thought of the years as separate, light occasions, requiring no reckoning because each had left so little behind. Now their very weightlessness accused him. He had consumed a life in fragments.

“She is young,” he said again, but the observation had lost its earlier charm.

“She is just.”

“Justice without experience can be monstrous.”

“Experience without justice may be worse.”

He stopped beside her. “Does she despise me, then?”

The question was out before he could adjust it. He heard its poverty, saw it reflected in Margaret’s sudden, stricken smile. Even now—when she had laid before him the desolation of eight years—his first instinct had been to inquire after his figure in another woman’s eyes.

“She did when she left,” Margaret said.

“When she left? Did she say what she intended?”

“No.”

“About the engagement?”

“No.”

“She must have said something.”

“She said she had to think. She asked to be alone. She looked—” Margaret’s voice broke, and she turned toward the window.

“How did she look?”

“As I might have looked eight years ago, if you had ever told me the truth.”

He felt now a genuine pain, but one still complicated by his wish to estimate its consequences. Ruth’s clear face rose before him, not radiant as at their last meeting, but fixed in moral revulsion. He imagined her removing the ring. The image had a theatrical explicitness he ordinarily distrusted; yet it struck through him with the force of an event.

“There may be a note waiting at my rooms,” he said.

“Perhaps.”

Her indifference alarmed him more than sympathy would have done. “Margaret, you don’t wish this marriage broken?”

She turned back slowly. “What I wish seems to have had very little practice in existing.”

“That is unfair.”

“To whom?”

“To both of us. You cannot pretend our friendship has meant nothing.”

“Nothing?” She came a step nearer. “It has meant everything. That is the horror of it.”

He had never seen her abandon herself so completely. The training of years had not vanished; it had cracked, and through the break came not the dramatic tumult he might once have expected, but a desolation more difficult to bear because it had forgotten gesture. Her hands hung at her sides; her face, stripped of its habitual attentiveness to his comfort, looked older and strangely uninhabited.

He tried to take one of the hands, but she withdrew it.

“No,” she said. “Not that now.”

“What can I do?”

The question, for once, was sincere; and perhaps its sincerity made its insufficiency clearer.

“For me? Nothing.”

“For Ruth?”

Margaret’s face changed. A tremor passed over it, then steadied into resolution. He saw her gather the torn folds of her composure, though not into their former pattern.

“Yes,” she said. “For Ruth there may be something.”

He waited.

“You can tell her I lied.”

“Lied?”

“About us.”

He stared at her.

“You can say I was afraid—jealous—that I wanted to separate you. You can tell her there was more than I admitted.”

“More?”

“That I was your mistress.”

The blunt word seemed to strike every object in the room. The lamps, the flowers, the closed curtains—all the soft accomplices of implication—shrank before it. Thursdale felt as though they had passed, without movement, from the sheltered stage of manners into some bare place where naked souls confronted one another and no available furniture could be placed between them.

“Margaret, you don’t know what you’re saying.”

“I know exactly.”

“You would have me tell Ruth that we—”

“That you had a past. The kind she can forgive.”

“And ruin you?”

“Who need know? She would never speak.”

“That is not the point.”

“What is the point now?”

He looked at her, and for the first time the full meaning of the offer reached him. She was not merely willing to surrender her reputation; she was offering to falsify the emptiness he had made. She would give him retrospectively the passion he had withheld from her, would stain her life to provide his with a human colour. Even her ruin was to be shaped for his convenience. It was the last perfection of his method—and its final exposure.

“You said she despised me because nothing happened,” he murmured.

“Yes.”

“And you would have her believe—”

“That something did. That you were weak, not cold. That you loved me and betrayed me, perhaps. Women forgive betrayal more easily than waste.”

He walked to the window and drew aside the curtain. Outside, the street lamps burned in a wet dusk; carriages passed, their wheels making a thin sound on the pavement. The ordinary world continued with its vulgar exactitude. Somewhere, perhaps, Ruth was writing. Somewhere a servant had placed a square envelope on his table. He felt suddenly how much of his life had been spent in avoiding just such visible consequences—how carefully he had moved over thin ice, congratulating himself less on reaching the shore than on leaving no mark of the passage.

Margaret spoke behind him.

“If you go to her now, I will write. I will say that I deceived her. I will say whatever is needed.”

He let the curtain fall.

“No.”

“You must.”

“No.”

“It may save you.”

“And condemn her to me?”

Margaret’s lips parted.

He went on, slowly, because the thought was new and therefore difficult to articulate without ornament. “If her judgment is right—if what disgusts her is not an act but a nature—then to deceive her would merely transfer the punishment. She would marry what she believes me to have been, and discover what I am.”

“People change.”

“Do they?” His smile had none of its usual finish. “You are not a reassuring example of my influence.”

She flinched, and he instantly regretted the cruelty; yet regret itself seemed another luxury purchased at her expense.

“I did not mean—”

“I know. You seldom mean. That has been your innocence.”

He accepted the sentence without defence. Something in him still stood apart, observing that acceptance and finding it perhaps too graceful; but beneath the observer there was, at last, a region that hurt without admiring the shape of its pain.

“I will not use your lie,” he said.

She made an impatient gesture. “Why not? You have used everything else.”

The words left them silent.

Then Thursdale moved toward the door. The room appeared suddenly unfamiliar, though he could have named the history of every object in it. There was the small Italian clock he had helped her choose; the screen brought back from Madrid; the chair in which he had once, during an illness, spent an hour speaking softly while she lay on the sofa and mistook his solicitude for love. All these things had seemed to him the properties of a beautiful intimacy. Now they looked like exhibits.

At the threshold he turned.

“Margaret—”

She stood near the table, one hand resting on the fallen book. Her expression warned him against gratitude, apology, or any of the phrases by which he had so often converted consequence into taste.

He said only: “I am sorry.”

“Yes.”

“I shall go home and see if she has written.”

“Yes.”

“If there is no letter, I shall go to her.”

Margaret looked at him with weary comprehension. “You still think there may be no letter.”

He felt the irony, but could not deny the hope. “There may not.”

“No,” she said. “There may not.”

He waited an instant longer, perhaps expecting her to cross the room, perhaps fearing that she would. She did neither. He went out, and the door closed with the discreet precision of all doors in Margaret’s house.

On the stairs he had an odd sensation that the visit was unfinished. He had left her often before—late, hurried, relieved, or pleasantly moved—but always with the consciousness that the room remained available, its warmth banked for his return. Now, though nothing visible had altered, he felt that he had left not a room but a ruin.

Outside, the night had hardened into rain. He walked rather than call a cab, turning up his collar as the drops struck his face with an unwelcome freshness. At his rooms the letter might be waiting. Ruth might release him, condemn him, demand an explanation. Or there might be nothing—nothing but the interval in which judgment, withheld, became more formidable than any sentence.

He discovered that he feared the absence of the letter more than its arrival.

Margaret, meanwhile, remained where he had left her. The fire sank lower, reducing its little architecture of gold to cinders. One of the lamps made a faint sound; somewhere in the house a servant moved, paused, and withdrew, respecting the silence she had been trained to preserve.

For eight years the room had drawn its meaning from expectancy. Every arrangement had anticipated a footstep, a voice, a familiar hand extended toward the cigarette box; even solitude had been merely the tasteful interval between his visits. Now the curtains, the shaded lamps, the flowers breathing out their sweetness into the closed air, no longer composed an intimacy. They disclosed an emptiness.

She looked at the chair he had used and saw, not his absence, but the shape of the life she had made around it. That was what remained: not scandal, not memory, not even the dignity of sacrifice, but a vacancy polished by long attention.

Presently she bent and picked up the book once more. The page was creased beyond smoothing. She held it under the lamp, rubbing the fold with a trembling finger, as though one small damaged thing might still be restored by care.

Then the book slipped from her hands again; and this time she let it lie.

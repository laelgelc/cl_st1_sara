### THE CALASH

The town of B— had been for many years distinguished by nothing whatever, which, to tell the truth, is a distinction not so easily maintained as people imagine. A fire, an embezzlement, an escaped bear, even the arrival of a dentist with a gilt tooth painted over his door, may at any moment deprive a town of its perfect insignificance. B—, however, had successfully resisted all such attacks upon its character.

Its streets were broad because no one had yet found any reason to build houses close together. In wet weather they became long brown rivers in which carts sank to the axle and pigs wandered with grave, administrative faces, as though inspecting the condition of the roads on behalf of the governor. In dry weather the same streets produced a dust so fine that it penetrated boots, cupboards, sealed letters, and, according to the district surveyor, the works of Voltaire, though nobody knew how Voltaire had reached his shelves.

The houses were mostly one-storeyed, with faded green roofs and plaster which had fallen away in patches, giving the walls the spotted appearance of elderly dogs. Here and there stood an ambitious building of two floors, generally unfinished. Its owner would explain that the third floor was to be added next summer, and had been explaining this for eleven summers. A wooden booth in the market sold rope, gingerbread, rusty locks, blue beads, tallow candles, dried fish, women’s stockings, harness buckles, and a portrait of some foreign king whose identity had long been lost. Beside it a woman traded in cucumbers from a barrel and slept with her head upon the weighing scales.

As for society, B— possessed all that was necessary. There was a chief of police, a judge, a procurator, a postmaster, a treasurer, two doctors, and a man whose official position nobody could remember but who wore an official cap and was therefore invited everywhere. The chief of police slept after dinner, before dinner, during visits, and sometimes while standing beside a window. The judge kept racing dogs and a housekeeper whose exact relation to the administration of justice was discussed only when she was absent. The postmaster opened no letters, as he often protested, except those which looked important or unusually thick. In short, everything proceeded with propriety.

The nobility assembled twice a year to eat, quarrel over precedence, and elect men whom everyone declared unfit immediately after electing them. On ordinary days the landowners remained on their estates, where they occupied themselves with threshing, mortgages, lawsuits, French catalogues, and the gradual disappearance of their forests. Their wives came into town to buy ribbon and discover who had become stout. Their daughters played the same three pieces on the pianoforte, each with an expression of remote suffering.

Then a cavalry regiment took up its quarters in B—.

The town awoke.

Windows which had not been opened since the previous governor’s inspection suddenly displayed white curtains, flowerpots, elbows, curls, ribbons, and female faces. The streets rang with hooves, sabres, spurs, orderly shouts, stable oaths, and the metallic clatter which accompanies military men even when they merely cross a room to ask for a glass of water. Tailors lost sleep. Laundresses acquired importance. The confectioner, who until then had sold chiefly stale biscuits and candles mislabelled as sweetmeats, ordered chocolate from Moscow and set out six jars of coloured drops, though the red ones tasted of soap and the green ones of nothing at all.

Officers appeared everywhere: officers with pale foreheads and black moustaches; officers with red foreheads and pale moustaches; officers so narrow that their uniforms seemed to be walking about without occupants; officers whose shoulders entered a doorway several seconds before the rest of them. They smoked in the hotel, smoked at the police chief’s, smoked in the street, and would probably have smoked in church had not one cautious cornet observed that the ladies’ gallery gave an excellent view of them without it.

At assemblies, local young men—clerks, secretaries, and sons of landowners—found themselves reduced to the melancholy office of holding shawls. The officers danced, laughed, bent over chairs, clicked their spurs, asked for refreshments, and spoke of horses with the warmth usually reserved for mothers and mistresses. Every young lady in B— discovered that military life was noble, perilous, and particularly becoming in a dark blue uniform.

The regiment’s brigade general occupied the best house in town, which belonged to a merchant who removed himself, his wife, seven children, a canary, and a sacred lamp into two rooms over his warehouse. The general was a tall, broad gentleman with a face of that deep, polished crimson which may be produced by climate, responsibility, or a daily acquaintance with Madeira. His moustaches projected to either side like two grey clothes brushes prepared to dust the shoulders of anyone approaching too near. He spoke slowly, in short portions, because a pipe generally occupied the intervals.

A lieutenant would say:

“The bay gelding went lame this morning, your excellency.”

“Eh?” the general would ask, removing the pipe.

“The bay gelding, your excellency—”

“Which bay?”

“Lieutenant Kopytin’s, your excellency.”

“Ah.” Puff—puff. “Went lame?”

“Yes, your excellency.”

“Bad.”

This was considered conversation.

Not long after the regiment’s arrival, the general gave a dinner. Whether it was his name day, the anniversary of some victory, the inspection of forage accounts, or merely Thursday, I cannot say with certainty. In provincial life, causes are frequently less important than sauces, and the dinner possessed sauces in abundance.

The table was laid for thirty-two, though forty-six sat down. The discrepancy was overcome by placing the slimmer officers between the stouter officials and by inserting two small tables at the corners, one of which leaned so noticeably that the adjutant supported it with his knee throughout the fish course. There were pies filled with minced game, pies filled with cabbage, pies whose contents remained a secret even after they had been eaten; sterlet with olives; veal under a glossy brown skin; ducks; a ham of such dimensions that it might have served as a footstool; cucumbers floating in cream; mushrooms in butter; preserves in little crystal dishes; mustard, horseradish, capers, lemons, pickled plums, and a salad into which the cook had apparently emptied the entire vegetable garden, including something which looked very much like a leaf from a lilac bush.

Bottles stood in ranks: French bottles with modest labels, Russian bottles with magnificent labels, squat bottles, tall bottles, green bottles, black bottles, and one dusty bottle which the general declared had crossed the Alps, though it was difficult to understand why.

Among the invited landowners was Pythagoras Pythagoravitch Tchertokoutski.

Tchertokoutski had once served in the cavalry and never allowed the fact to become exhausted through lack of repetition. He had resigned under circumstances concerning which several accounts existed. According to him, a dispute over honour had compelled him to sacrifice a brilliant career. According to a former regimental quartermaster, the brilliant career had involved a colonel’s poodle, a borrowed trumpet, and an incident in a public garden. I do not undertake to decide between these accounts. Honour is a delicate thing, and poodles are often malicious witnesses.

He was still addressed as “captain” by people wishing to please him, and as “Pythagoras Pythagoravitch” by those hoping to borrow money. He wore coats of an exaggeratedly military cut, trousers with straps, spurs even when travelling in a covered carriage, and a short black moustache which he brushed upward at the ends until it resembled two thorns. His estate lay twelve versts from town and contained four hundred souls, of whom three hundred and eighty were mortgaged, while the remaining twenty were old women whom no bank had yet discovered a use for.

He enjoyed the reputation of a rich man because he spent money loudly. When he purchased a rug, the whole town heard what it cost; when he sold oats to pay for it, no one was told. He subscribed to a French journal on elegant living, ordered waistcoats from the capital, and owned a silver cigar case which he displayed so often that the hinge had worn loose. His stable contained seven horses: two were lame, one blind in the left eye, one inclined to bite, and three described as “young” by a groom who had known them for fifteen years. The favourite was a long-necked brown mare solemnly named Agrafena Ivanovna.

At dinner Tchertokoutski sat near the general, though not so near as he desired and considerably nearer than the district treasurer desired. He bowed whenever the general looked in his direction and sometimes when the general merely looked at the salt.

The first courses passed under the strict authority of appetite. After the sterlet, conversation began to rise. The officers discussed remounts, the officials discussed prices, and the landowners discussed the officers’ discussion of remounts. A young cornet told a story about a horse which had recognized its former rider after six years. The horse had expressed recognition by biting him in the shoulder.

“Extraordinary intelligence!” cried Tchertokoutski.

“Bit him?” asked the general.

“In the shoulder, your excellency,” repeated the cornet.

“Which shoulder?”

“The right, your excellency.”

“Ah.” Puff—puff. “Remembered him.”

When the champagne appeared, the conversation became nobler. Toasts were drunk to the sovereign, the army, the regiment, the brigade, the town, the ladies, cavalry in general, and horses in particular. The police chief woke during the toast to the ladies, drank to artillery, and went to sleep again with his fork in his hand.

After dinner the company moved to the drawing room, where coffee, liqueurs, pipes, cigars, and cards awaited them. The windows were open. Evening light lay upon the street, and three boys stood outside staring at the officers until chased away, whereupon they returned beneath the window and stared from a lower position.

The general showed several guests a new English saddle. This led naturally to harness, harness to equipages, and equipages to that broad and perilous country in which Tchertokoutski’s imagination travelled without customs barriers.

“Speaking of equipages, your excellency,” he said, leaning forward, “I have lately acquired a calash. A genuine Viennese turn-out.”

The general’s moustaches shifted.

“Viennese?”

“From Vienna itself, your excellency. I confess I had no intention of purchasing another vehicle, possessing already, as your excellency may know, a travelling carriage, a phaeton, two droskies, and a little English gig for mornings. But one sees quality and is powerless. Entirely powerless.”

“How much?”

“Four thousand rubles, your excellency.”

The adjutant raised an eyebrow. A major lowered his cigar.

“Four thousand?” said the general.

“Four thousand, without the harness. And I assure your excellency the maker lost by it. The springs alone! One may drive across ploughed land and believe oneself seated in a drawing room. There is a special compartment for cigars, another for pistols, another for maps, sandwiches, eau de cologne, writing materials, and, if I am not mistaken, two bottles of wine.”

“If you are not mistaken?” asked the major.

“I mean that the compartment holds two bottles; naturally I know whether I keep wine in it. At present, perhaps, there is Burgundy. Or Madeira. My coachman manages these details.”

“What colour?” asked the general.

“Colour? Ah—dark blue. That is, dark blue in shadow. In sunlight it has something of green, or perhaps plum. A remarkable colour. The interior is lined with pale cloth, almost silver. There is a monogram on the door.”

“What monogram?”

Tchertokoutski coughed.

“My own, naturally. P.P.T. The letters are so artfully intertwined that one may at first take them for a princely coronet.”

A lieutenant murmured something to his neighbour.

“Eh?” said the general.

“I only said, your excellency, that it must be a very fine carriage.”

“Very fine,” said the general.

“Your excellency must condescend to inspect it!” cried Tchertokoutski. “Indeed, I will not accept a refusal. Tomorrow! All of you, gentlemen. Come to my estate tomorrow at three. We shall dine in a simple country fashion—no ceremony whatever—and afterward I shall have the calash brought round. Your excellency will judge the springs personally.”

The general looked at the major. The major looked at the colonel. The colonel looked at the decanter.

“Twelve versts?” asked the general.

“Not twelve! Ten and a half by the new road. The road is magnificent. My peasants have repaired it. One flies.”

“And dinner?”

“A mere trifle, your excellency. Whatever the house can provide: sterlet, perhaps; young lamb; a cold pie; asparagus; some snipe if my people have not become complete idiots; dessert, naturally. I have a bottle of Lafitte—genuine Lafitte—which I have reserved for an occasion worthy of it.”

“Good,” said the general. “We shall come.”

Tchertokoutski placed a hand upon his breast and bowed with the expression of a man to whom an empire has just entrusted its frontier.

“Your excellency has honoured my roof.”

It would have been advisable at this point for him to return home.

Indeed, a sensible man would have ordered his carriage, driven directly to his estate, awakened the steward, cook, housekeeper, gardeners, fishermen, poulterers, and any peasant capable of carrying a dish, and passed the night arranging the modest country dinner whose mere outline included half the natural history of the province. Tchertokoutski did rise. He even said that domestic obligations compelled him to depart.

Then somebody proposed one game of whist.

One game is a harmless thing. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and a gentleman may leave upon its conclusion with his character entirely uninjured. Unfortunately, the end of one game is also the beginning of another, a geometrical truth which Pythagoras Pythagoravitch might have appreciated had he not been occupied in losing eighty rubles.

He remained to recover them.

By midnight he had recovered twenty, lost one hundred and ten, won a silver snuffbox, and undertaken to play one final rubber. The general retired. Several officials departed in shared carriages, sitting upon one another’s knees and apologizing at every rut. The police chief was carried home between two constables like a rolled carpet.

Punch appeared.

I cannot say who prepared it, nor by what route one glass arrived at Tchertokoutski’s elbow. Such things occur in rooms where card tables have been opened. A lemon floats past, sugar shines at the bottom, rum gives off a friendly light, and before a man understands the matter he has swallowed hospitality, courage, and forgetfulness in equal portions.

At two in the morning Tchertokoutski was explaining to a captain that Agrafena Ivanovna descended from an Arabian mare captured in Persia.

“At Borodino,” he added.

“Borodino isn’t in Persia,” said the captain.

“I did not say it was. The mare was captured in Persia and afterward present at Borodino.”

“How old is your mare?”

“Blood has no age.”

At three he sang a regimental song, though the regiment concerned denied any knowledge of it. At four he tried to demonstrate a cavalry manoeuvre with two chairs and a footstool. The footstool represented the enemy and was decisively overturned. At half past four he embraced the adjutant, called him “brother,” and confided that the calash had been offered to a grand duke before coming into his possession.

At dawn his servants succeeded in putting him into his carriage. He wore one glove, his cravat was in his pocket, and three thistle heads had attached themselves to his moustache, though how thistles entered the general’s drawing room is another point upon which I possess no reliable information.

He reached home after sunrise and was conveyed to bed. The house remained silent. No command was given. No cook was awakened. No lamb received warning. The bottle of genuine Lafitte, if it existed, slept peacefully wherever it had slept for years.

Tchertokoutski’s wife, Maria Fedotovna, knew nothing of the invitation.

She was a pretty young woman with fair hair, delicate shoulders, and the calm self-possession of a person whose husband has long ago ceased to surprise her in any useful way. She had been educated at a boarding school, spoke enough French to address servants in it, and read novels in which married women suffered beside fountains, though she herself preferred the balcony because it was shaded after eleven.

She rose late that morning. Her maid dressed her hair, then dressed it again because the first arrangement made her look, in Maria Fedotovna’s opinion, too severe. She selected a pale muslin gown, rejected a blue ribbon, recalled the blue ribbon, replaced it with pink, and finally wore both in such a manner that neither admitted defeat. Breakfast consisted of coffee, cream, two little rolls, raspberry preserve, and a discussion of whether the new turkey hen possessed a respectable character.

The day was beautiful. The garden shone with that fresh summer brilliance which makes even a neglected estate appear for an hour to be under competent management. Sunlight rested upon the lime trees; bees entered flowers with businesslike haste; a fountain in the centre of the lawn produced, after prolonged internal effort, a thread of water no thicker than string. Beyond the fence, peasants were carting hay. Somewhere a child cried, a dog barked, and a gardener in a torn shirt slept beneath a currant bush while holding a rake upright like a ceremonial staff.

Maria Fedotovna walked onto the balcony. She shaded her eyes and looked down the road.

At first she saw only dust. Then the dust arranged itself into moving columns. Sunlight flashed on metal. One carriage appeared, then another; behind them rode officers, orderlies, and two men whose exact necessity to the expedition was unclear but whose uniforms gave them confidence.

She called to the maid.

“Dunyasha, who is coming?”

Dunyasha looked.

“Military gentlemen, madam.”

“So many?”

“There is the general’s carriage, madam. I recognize the coachman. He has the red nose.”

Maria Fedotovna was silent. Then she turned sharply.

“Wake the master.”

The maid ran. A footman was sent after the maid, a houseboy after the footman, and an old nurse followed everyone because no crisis in the house had ever been improved by excluding her.

Tchertokoutski lay diagonally across the bed in his dressing gown, breathing with the solemn noise of a sawmill. One foot projected from the blankets. His hair had flattened upon one side and risen upon the other, giving his head the appearance of a field after opposing winds.

“Sir!” cried Dunyasha. “Sir, get up!”

He opened one eye.

“What?”

“Get up!”

“Go away.”

“The general is coming!”

“What general?”

“The general, sir!”

Tchertokoutski closed his eye.

The footman entered.

“Get up, sir! His excellency is at the avenue.”

“Tell him—tell him I congratulate him.”

“Get up!” cried Maria Fedotovna from the doorway. “You invited them to dinner.”

The words reached him slowly. They seemed first to knock upon the outer door of his understanding, then pass through an antechamber, then stumble over several overturned chairs left there by the punch. Suddenly he sat upright.

“Dinner?”

“You invited the general and all the officers.”

“I?”

“You.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

His face changed.

“What time is it?”

“Nearly three.”

“What has been prepared?”

“Prepared? Nothing. I knew nothing.”

“Nothing at all?”

“There is yesterday’s soup.”

“For how many?”

“For us.”

He sprang from the bed, became entangled in the blanket, recovered himself, and ran to the window. Between the lime trees appeared the first carriage, carrying the general and a colonel. Behind it rolled another equipage, then another. Horsemen followed. Dust rose above them like the smoke of an advancing army.

Tchertokoutski turned pale.

“The fish?” he whispered.

“What fish?”

“The sterlet—the snipe—the lamb!”

“There is no sterlet. There are no snipe. The lamb is alive.”

“Kill it!”

“There is no time.”

“Kill everything!”

“To what purpose?”

This question, though reasonable, was cruel.

Tchertokoutski seized his trousers, put both feet into one leg, abandoned them, and began pacing in his dressing gown.

“Say I am ill.”

“They will come in.”

“Say I am dying.”

“The doctor is with them.”

“Say there is scarlet fever.”

“They will ask why I invited them.”

Hooves sounded in the courtyard.

He stopped.

“Say I have gone away.”

Maria Fedotovna stared at him.

“Gone away?”

“Yes. Urgent business. Unexpected. Government matter. Estate matter. Death of an aunt.”

“You have no aunt.”

“Then do not specify the relative. Say I left at dawn. No—before dawn. Say I received a dispatch. A sealed dispatch.”

“And where will you be?”

He looked around the bedroom as if expecting it to provide another province. The wardrobe was too small; beneath the bed, undignified and visible; the attic required crossing the landing. Voices sounded below. A servant ran through the corridor.

“The coach-house,” he said.

Still in his dressing gown, with slippers on his feet and his nightcap crushed in one hand, he hurried down the back stairs. The housekeeper saw him and crossed herself. A kitchen boy carrying onions flattened himself against the wall. Tchertokoutski passed through the kitchen, across the yard, behind a shed, and entered the coach-house.

There stood his equipages.

There was an old travelling carriage with a cracked lamp; a mud-spattered drosky; a little cart used for taking grain to the mill; and, in the centre, the celebrated Viennese calash.

To call it Viennese was harmless enough, since no representative of Vienna was present to object. It was a broad, ordinary, second-hand vehicle, painted a dull green which might under certain conditions—night, fog, blindness—be mistaken for dark blue. One wheel had been repaired with a piece of unpainted wood. The lining was brown and worn smooth at the corners. There were no compartments for pistols, maps, eau de cologne, sandwiches, or Burgundy. A shallow box beneath the seat contained a broken whip, two nails, a crust of bread, and one mitten.

Tchertokoutski lifted the leather apron, climbed inside, folded himself upon the seat, and drew the apron shut.

Meanwhile the visitors had arrived.

The general descended, followed by the colonel, the major, the adjutant, three captains, five lieutenants, four cornets, and several other officers who had joined the excursion because an excellent dinner, like a military campaign, always attracts volunteers. Their uniforms brightened the neglected courtyard. Spurs rang upon the stones. The general looked at the house.

No host appeared.

A footman approached with the expression of a man carrying a dish certain to be thrown at him.

“Pythagoras Pythagoravitch?” asked the adjutant.

“Not at home, your honour.”

The general removed his pipe.

“Not at home?”

“No, your excellency.”

“Where?”

“He has gone away.”

“When?”

“At dawn, your excellency.”

“Where?”

The footman hesitated. Tchertokoutski had provided no geography.

“On business, your excellency.”

“What business?”

“Urgent business.”

The general looked at the colonel. The colonel examined his glove. The major’s moustache twitched.

“Did he leave a message?”

“No, your excellency.”

“Did he forget that we were coming?”

“I cannot know, your excellency.”

The answer was so true that it seemed insolent.

Maria Fedotovna did not appear. She had withdrawn to the rear drawing room and sat there with a novel open before her, though the heroine had been waiting at the same fountain for twenty minutes without being read.

The officers remained in the courtyard. Their irritation was sharpened by hunger. They had breakfasted lightly in expectation of sterlet, lamb, snipe, cold pie, asparagus, dessert, and Lafitte. One cornet had eaten nothing since the previous evening except a pickled mushroom.

“Extraordinary,” said the major.

“Invites the whole brigade and goes away,” said a captain.

“Perhaps there has been an accident,” offered the adjutant.

“Accident?” said the general.

“An urgent summons, your excellency.”

“Without a message?”

“No, your excellency. That is, yes, your excellency. I mean—”

“Eh?”

“Nothing, your excellency.”

Puff—puff.

The general looked once more at the house, whose windows displayed curtains of irreproachable innocence. Then his eye travelled toward the coach-house.

“Well,” he said, “we may at least see the calash.”

This proposal revived the company. If dinner had vanished, the four-thousand-ruble Viennese equipage remained. The officers crossed the yard.

Inside the calash, Tchertokoutski heard them approaching.

A man concealed in a carriage hears with unnatural clarity. Every spur is a bell, every boot a hammer, every casual cough a verdict. He compressed himself beneath the apron, drew his knees to his chin, and attempted not to breathe. One slipper fell from his foot. He caught it before it struck the floor and held it against his chest.

The coach-house doors opened wider.

“There it is,” said the adjutant.

“That?” said the major.

A silence followed.

The general walked around the calash. He pressed one spring with his boot. It gave a melancholy squeak.

“Viennese?” he asked.

“So we were told, your excellency.”

The colonel bent to inspect a wheel.

“Old axle.”

“Repaired,” said a captain.

“Badly repaired,” said the colonel.

One lieutenant examined the paint.

“Dark blue in shadow,” he murmured.

Another scratched it lightly with a fingernail.

“Green in every light.”

They laughed, though with restraint because the general had not yet laughed.

The general opened the door and studied it.

“Where is the monogram?”

The officers searched. On the panel were several scratches, a patch of dried mud, and a faint mark resembling the letter S, probably made by a branch.

“Perhaps on the other side,” said the adjutant.

It was not on the other side.

“What did he say it cost?” asked the general.

“Four thousand rubles, your excellency.”

“Four thousand?”

“Four thousand.”

“I would not give eight hundred,” said the colonel.

“Six,” said the major.

“Five, if the wheels are included,” said a captain.

The general pressed the spring again.

Squeak.

“Ploughed land,” he said.

At that moment an officer noticed the leather apron drawn across the front.

“Let us see the lining.”

Tchertokoutski’s heart struck his ribs so violently that he feared the officers would appraise it separately.

A hand took hold of the apron.

It was lifted.

There, folded into the interior like an article packed by an incompetent servant, sat Pythagoras Pythagoravitch Tchertokoutski in his dressing gown. His hair stood on end; his face was grey; one bare foot was tucked beneath him, and against his breast he held a slipper with the tenderness of a mother holding her infant.

Nobody spoke.

The general’s pipe remained halfway to his mouth. The colonel blinked. The adjutant opened his lips and forgot the word intended for them. In the doorway, the youngest cornet made a small sound and immediately converted it into a cough.

Tchertokoutski tried to smile.

“Your excellency—” he began.

The general looked at him, looked at the slipper, and looked at the interior of the four-thousand-ruble Viennese calash.

“Hullo,” he said. “There you are.”

Then, with perfect calm, he lowered the leather apron, covering Pythagoras Pythagoravitch once more.

He turned and walked out of the coach-house. The colonel followed, then the major, the captains, lieutenants, cornets, adjutant, and all the rest. No one laughed until they had reached the road. The youngest cornet endured as far as the gate; there he bent double over his saddle. A captain struck him on the back. The major’s shoulders began to shake. Even the general’s moustaches moved, though whether from laughter or the jolting of the carriage cannot positively be said.

The equipages rolled away through the dust.

In the coach-house the calash remained silent, ordinary, and worth perhaps six hundred rubles with the wheels included.

Its most remarkable feature was still inside.

# The Flight of Mrs. Tretherick

In the year of grace 1858, when Fiddletown considered itself already old enough to possess traditions, yet was still young enough to invent them overnight, the domestic infelicities of Mr. and Mrs. Tretherick enjoyed a publicity which more fortunate unions have sometimes failed to obtain.

I would not have it inferred that Fiddletown was impertinent. It was merely sympathetic. Having little domestic privacy of its own—its habitations being thin, its windows accessible, and its citizens much abroad after nightfall—it naturally regarded privacy in others as a species of aristocratic affectation. When a pistol was discharged in a bedroom, when a lady’s traveling-trunk was seen upon a veranda, when a gentleman slept in his office three nights consecutively with his boots on, Fiddletown did not pry. It deduced.

The union was not a felicitous one.

Mrs. Tretherick was beautiful. This statement, which has the misfortune of appearing simple, was in Fiddletown complicated by the fact that the town had no standard of feminine beauty which had not been previously corrected by distance, whisky, or memory. Nevertheless, when Mrs. Tretherick passed the Magnolia Saloon, men who had faced hostile rifles with composure became curiously absorbed in the toe of a boot or the adjustment of a neckcloth; while those who had not faced hostile rifles stared frankly, and afterward lied about it.

She was tall, fair, and of that luxuriant figure which, in an age of scant provisions and unremitting labor, possessed almost the dignity of a public benefaction. Her eyes were gray, except when she declared they were blue; her hair was golden, except in shadow, when it was brown; and her mouth, whether curved in disdain, tremulous with emotion, or occupied in the practical destruction of a peach, was held by competent local authority to be perfect. She dressed with a richness which Fiddletown condemned in the abstract and studied minutely in detail.

Nor was she deficient in sentiment. She had wept over poetry which she did not understand, pressed dead flowers in books she had never read, and once sat for an hour beside a brook because its murmur, as she afterward informed Colonel Starbottle, “seemed to be telling her of another life.” The brook had risen from recent rains and was, in fact, carrying away a sluice-box belonging to Mr. Peters; but I know not that this materially impaired the lady’s emotion.

Mr. Tretherick was less ornamental.

He was a thick-shouldered, close-mouthed man, whose face suggested that Nature, having intended firmness, had been betrayed into sulkiness. He owned two claims of doubtful value, three lots of speculative value, and a temper whose value had never been questioned. In his sober intervals he was distrustful; in his cups he was logical; and as his logic invariably established his wife’s guilt before he began his inquiries, its operation was brief.

It was known that he drank. It was hinted that she flirted. It was established, to the satisfaction of both parties, that each had caused the ruin of the other.

The public chronology of their unhappiness began with a shattered lamp, proceeded through an overturned supper-table, and culminated in the discharge of a revolver through the parlor ceiling. Mr. Tretherick explained that the weapon had gone off accidentally while he was pointing it at his wife. Mrs. Tretherick, with that feminine inexactitude which so often destroys an otherwise excellent argument, insisted that the accident had been intentional. Fiddletown divided on the constitutional question involved, the men generally supporting the pistol and the women the ceiling.

There were other circumstances. A scar, pale and thin as a thread, lay beneath Mrs. Tretherick’s left temple. She attributed it to a fall. Her husband attributed it to a wineglass. As both explanations were technically compatible, the town accepted them together.

Colonel Starbottle accepted neither.

The colonel was not, perhaps, a colonel in the severe military sense. His title had been conferred upon him by a constituency whose enthusiasm outran its power, after he had ridden at the head of seventeen citizens during an election disturbance and had, by the simple expedient of losing the road, avoided a collision with the opposing party. But no patent from Washington could have added to the completeness with which he bore the distinction.

He was short, florid, and erect. His blue coat was buttoned so tightly over his swelling breast that pity for the buttons occasionally distracted attention from the wearer. A white hat leaned backward from his forehead; a gold-headed cane swung from his wrist; and his shirt-frill, agitated by every inhalation, appeared to participate in his emotions.

“Madam,” he said to Mrs. Tretherick on one memorable afternoon at the Fiddletown Hotel, “the man who raises his hand against Beauty—against Innocence—against a woman, by heaven!—raises it against Colonel Culpeper Starbottle, sir!”

Mrs. Tretherick, who had selected a position near the window where the declining sun passed favorably through her hair, lowered her eyelids.

“You do not know all,” she murmured.

“Madam, I know enough.”

“No woman should speak against the man whose name she bears.”

“Then, madam, let me speak. Tretherick is a blank scoundrel.”

The colonel did not literally say “blank.” His actual expression, though supported by custom and delivered with feeling, is omitted from this veracious chronicle in deference to those readers whose moral sensibilities have never been tested by residence in a mining settlement.

Mrs. Tretherick sighed. The sigh was admirably graduated. It began in resignation, touched regret, and expired in reproach.

“I have been weak,” she said.

“Never!”

“I have been foolish.”

“Impossible!”

“I have trusted.”

“My God, madam!”

Here the colonel struck his breast with a gloved hand and turned toward the window, ostensibly to master an emotion which had already mastered everybody in the passage, where two chambermaids and the bartender were listening.

That Mrs. Tretherick had trusted Colonel Starbottle more frequently than was consistent with the peace of her household was Fiddletown’s conclusion. That the colonel had justified this trust according to his lights was his own. There had been moonlight walks, whispered consultations, the loan of a volume of verse, and at least one occasion upon which the colonel’s white hat had been found behind the Tretherick woodshed after midnight. The hat itself offered no explanation, and the colonel’s explanation was so comprehensive that it included honor, the Constitution, Southern chivalry, an injured woman, and a lame horse, without noticeably touching the woodshed.

Mr. Tretherick’s criticism had been more direct.

After the episode of the ceiling, Mrs. Tretherick left her husband and took rooms at the hotel. She departed in a black silk dress, without a bonnet, carrying a small reticule and an expression of tragic finality. This last she maintained until she reached the hotel stairs, where, finding that no one was observing her, she paused to recover her breath.

For three days she occupied the best front room, received the commiserations of the feminine population, and refused nourishment with such particularity that the cook prepared six separate dishes. On the fourth day she announced that she would leave Fiddletown forever.

Her trunk, however, remained at the cottage.

This circumstance, trivial to the masculine mind, was insuperable to Mrs. Tretherick. It contained her Paris mantle, two embroidered dresses, her mother’s miniature, a packet of letters, and a pair of satin slippers which could not possibly be of further use to her in a world now emptied of hope, but which she was nevertheless unwilling to abandon to Mr. Tretherick.

Colonel Starbottle undertook the recovery.

“Madam,” he declared, “the baggage of an insulted woman is sacred. Caesar—sir—Caesar crossing the Rubicon never assumed a responsibility more solemn.”

“Caesar had no husband,” said Mrs. Tretherick faintly.

The colonel checked himself. This historical objection was new to him.

“At least,” she continued, turning a ring upon her finger, “not such a husband.”

“Say no more. I shall escort you.”

“Henry is at Angel’s to-day.”

“So much the better for Henry.”

“I wish no encounter.”

“Madam, neither do I. But if that man appears”—the colonel’s hand traveled toward his hip—“if that miscreant obtrudes himself upon our peaceful and lawful errand, if he compels me to defend outraged womanhood—”

“You will not kill him?” she asked quickly.

The colonel’s face became judicial.

“I shall endeavor, madam, to disable him in some locality compatible with ultimate repentance.”

At four o’clock they left the hotel by the side entrance. Mrs. Tretherick wore a dove-colored traveling-dress and a veil whose transparency increased the mystery it pretended to create. The colonel carried his cane and a revolver, the latter concealed so badly that three citizens followed them in the expectation of a tragedy.

They were disappointed. Mr. Tretherick was absent. Ah Fe, the Chinese servant, was also invisible. The cottage stood among the pines, its veranda striped with afternoon shadow, its door unlocked, its silence so entire that the colonel’s gallantry, which had flourished on the road, diminished perceptibly at the threshold.

“Deserted,” breathed Mrs. Tretherick.

“Deserted, madam,” echoed the colonel, although a pan of beans was simmering in the kitchen and a man’s boots stood in the passage.

She entered the parlor. The room had suffered from Mr. Tretherick’s temporary bachelorhood. Ashes lay upon the hearth; newspapers covered the floor; a decanter and one unwashed glass occupied the center-table. Upon the wall hung Mrs. Tretherick’s portrait, painted two years before by an artist who had improved her coloring and misunderstood her hands.

She looked at it long.

“He has left it,” she said.

“Madam, even that man could not destroy Beauty.”

“He has turned it to the wall.”

The colonel examined the picture and discovered that this was true. With an oath he restored it.

“Infamous!”

Mrs. Tretherick pressed a handkerchief to her eyes, but removed it in time to observe the effect in the mirror opposite. Then, gathering her skirts, she went upstairs.

The bedroom door resisted her. Something moved behind it. Mrs. Tretherick drew back, and the colonel, with a promptitude which would have been more impressive had his revolver not caught in the lining of his coat, placed himself before her.

“Who’s there?” he demanded.

There was no answer.

“Come forth, sir!”

A small red head appeared around the edge of the door.

The colonel lowered his weapon. Mrs. Tretherick lowered her veil.

The child was perhaps six years old, though neglect had given her face that shrewd, pinched maturity which occasionally invests childhood with the disagreeable air of a small creditor. Her hair, violently red and apparently self-cut, stood around her head in ragged flames. She wore a faded frock, once costly, torn at the shoulder and shortened by growth; one stocking had descended upon her shoe; the other was held up by a blue ribbon. Under her arm she carried an enormous doll made of flour-sacking, with charcoal eyes, red worsted lips, and arms of unequal length.

“Who are you?” asked Mrs. Tretherick.

The child looked first at the lady, then at the colonel, then at the colonel’s revolver.

“Tarowline,” she said.

“What?”

“Tarowline Tretherick. But Ah Fe calls me Tally.”

Mrs. Tretherick’s face altered.

“Tretherick?”

The child nodded.

“Whose child are you?”

“Papa’s.”

The colonel made a slight movement and looked out of the window.

Mrs. Tretherick grasped the door-frame. She had known of the first wife, dead in the East; she had known vaguely that there had been a child, placed with relatives, lost from discussion, and in the convenient extinction which remoteness permits to disagreeable facts, practically buried. That Mr. Tretherick had sent for the girl, brought her secretly into the house, and established her in Mrs. Tretherick’s abandoned rooms admitted, to the injured lady’s mind, only one interpretation.

He had replaced her.

With a child!

The insult was so grotesque that it became sublime.

“How long have you been here?” she asked.

“Free days.”

“Who brought you?”

“A man on the stage. Papa said I was to live here now.”

“Here? In this room?”

The child nodded again. “Papa said it was a dood room. He said nobody wanted it.”

Mrs. Tretherick’s lips whitened. The colonel coughed.

“And these?” She pointed to a heap of garments upon the bed—her garments, dragged from drawers and wardrobes.

“Tresses,” said Carry, with modest satisfaction. “I tresses myselth.”

She had contrived a turban from a lace scarf, a royal train from a shawl, and a necklace from several strings of pearl buttons. One of Mrs. Tretherick’s embroidered chemises had been folded beneath the doll as a bed.

Mrs. Tretherick crossed the room in two strides and snatched it away.

“You wicked child!”

Carry started.

“You little thief! How dare you touch my things?”

“I didn’t know,” said the child.

“You knew enough to spoil them. Who told you to come here? Who told you to take my place?”

“I ain’t takin’ no place.”

“You are. He brought you here to mock me. He dressed you in my clothes. He taught you to say this.”

Carry’s mouth opened. Her lower lip shook, but no sound came.

Mrs. Tretherick turned with a gesture toward Colonel Starbottle.

“You see! You see his cruelty! Not content to drive me from my home, he fills it with—this!”

The colonel inflated his chest.

“Madam, the conception is worthy of him.”

Carry drew the doll closer.

“Go away,” said Mrs. Tretherick.

The child did not move.

“Do you hear me? Go to your father.”

“He ain’t here.”

“Then go to the Chinaman.”

“Ah Fe’s gone down town.”

“Go anywhere. Only leave this room.”

Carry looked toward the open door, then back at Mrs. Tretherick. There was in her face no rebellion, only a forlorn calculation, as if she were considering which empty part of the house might receive her with the least objection.

“You’re the pretty lady,” she said at last.

Mrs. Tretherick stared.

“Papa’s got your picture.”

The lady’s anger, checked for an instant by the tribute, returned with added shame.

“Go!”

Carry went.

Her dragging stocking made a soft whisper along the passage. The huge doll’s leg struck each baluster as she descended.

Mrs. Tretherick dropped into a chair.

“I am punished,” she said. “I have deserved this.”

“No, madam!”

“I have been vain, heartless, foolish. I thought I could escape my past. I thought—” She looked at the colonel.

The colonel approached with extended arms.

“Angelina!”

It was the first time he had used her Christian name without first ascertaining that the doors were closed. Under other circumstances she might have valued the daring. Now she rose abruptly.

“Not here.”

“Why not here? Why should this mausoleum of tyranny—this domestic Bastille—”

“Please go downstairs.”

“Madam?”

“I must pack. We are to meet at the turn of the road at six. The stage passes at seven. You remember?”

“Remember!” The colonel placed one hand upon his heart and the other upon his watch. “Every pulsation here, madam, marks the approach of that sacred hour.”

“Then leave me.”

The colonel bowed. At the door he paused.

“One word. You will not permit the accidental apparition of that precocious imp—that red-headed instrument of vengeance—to change your resolution?”

Mrs. Tretherick’s eyes flashed.

“Never.”

“Enough.”

He descended, stepped onto the veranda, and marched toward town with the air of a man who had personally arranged the sunset.

Mrs. Tretherick locked the bedroom door. For the next hour she packed with the concentrated energy women sometimes reserve for crises after having consumed the preliminary period in discussion. Dresses vanished into the trunk; ribbons, letters, gloves, jewels, and small memorials of affection were thrust into corners with impartial haste. She paused only before the portrait of her mother and a little cedar box at the back of a drawer.

The box contained a baby’s cap.

It was yellow with age, though scarcely five years old. A narrow blue ribbon ran around it. Mrs. Tretherick held it for one moment; then, hearing a noise in the hall, closed the box violently and pushed it beneath a pile of linen.

Her first child had never breathed. At least, so the doctor said. Mrs. Tretherick, who had gone down into darkness with an agony beyond speech and awakened to an emptiness equally beyond it, had never seen the small still face. Mr. Tretherick had grieved after his fashion, which was brief, angry, and associated with drink. She had grieved after hers, which was longer, concealed, and occasionally converted into verse. Between them the child became first an accusation, then a silence, then a grave over which neither dared meet the other’s eyes.

She shut the trunk.

The sound in the passage was repeated: a low voice, then a shriller one, then a thump. Mrs. Tretherick listened. After a pause she unlocked the door and looked out.

No one was there.

The stairs descended into yellow dusk. A bar of sunlight lay across the passage, filled with slow-turning motes. The house seemed larger in its emptiness, and less like the prison she had named it. From above came the murmur again.

There was an attic at the end of the upper hall, reached by a narrow stair.

Mrs. Tretherick climbed it.

The attic door stood ajar. Through the crack she saw Carry seated on the bare floor in a slanting beam of light. Dust swam around her; her red hair burned at the edges like a ragged aureole. Before her sat the monstrous doll, propped against an overturned box and dressed in Mrs. Tretherick’s lace scarf.

The child had not seen her.

Carry folded her arms and regarded the doll sternly.

“Who is you?” she demanded.

She waited, then answered in a deeper voice:

“Tarowline Tretherick.”

“What?”

“Tarowline. But Ah Fe calls me Tally.”

The imitation was exact enough to reveal that Mrs. Tretherick’s own voice had been sharper than she remembered.

“Whose child is you?”

“Papa’s.”

Carry leaned forward and shook the doll by one deficient shoulder.

“You wicked child! You little tief! Who told you to come here and take my place?”

The doll, whose countenance preserved an enviable serenity, made no defense.

“Go away,” said Carry.

She took up the doll, marched it three paces toward the wall, then stopped. Her face, until now puckered in theatrical severity, suddenly softened.

“Don’t ky,” she whispered.

She brought the doll back, sat down, and gathered it against her narrow breast.

“She didn’t mean it. She’s pretty, and pretty ladies gets mad. Maybe she’s got a little girl somewheres, and maybe she don’t know where. Maybe if you was dood—awful dood—and washed your hands and didn’t tear no tresses, she’d be your mamma.”

Carry looked closely into the charcoal eyes.

“Would you like that?”

The doll appeared to express a guarded hope.

“Then don’t you never take her things. An’ don’t say papa. An’ don’t have red hair.”

She tugged at one of her own locks and frowned.

“It’s ugly. Annie at the ’sylum said so. Mamma ladies don’t like ugly little girls.”

Mrs. Tretherick, standing unseen beyond the door, laid one hand against the wall.

Carry now arranged the lace about the doll’s head and began rocking it. Her voice fell into a tuneless croon, made of fragments collected from servants, stage-drivers, and the institution from which she had lately come.

“Sleep, dolly. Don’t you ky. Mamma’s comin’ bimeby. Sure thing. She’s got tresses, an’ rings, an’ smells dood. She ain’t mad now. She’s comin’—”

Mrs. Tretherick pushed open the door.

Carry ceased singing. The doll slid from her arms.

For a moment neither spoke. The lady had intended—I know not what. Possibly a dignified apology; possibly a question; possibly only the recovery of the lace. What she actually said was:

“Your stocking has come down.”

Carry looked at the stocking.

“Yes’m.”

“Come here.”

The child rose obediently.

Mrs. Tretherick knelt and fastened the stocking with the blue ribbon. Her fingers were not steady. Close at hand the child seemed thinner, smaller, and much dirtier than she had appeared in the bedroom. There was a bruise on one knee, a scratch beneath her chin, and on her wrist the fading mark of a hand.

“Who did that?” asked Mrs. Tretherick.

“Papa.”

The fingers stopped.

“Why?”

“I spilled his glass. He was sick.”

“Sick?”

Carry gave the practical nod of childhood. “Sick like men is when they talks big and falls down.”

Mrs. Tretherick completed the knot.

“Does he often hurt you?”

“He don’t know me much yet.”

This defense, whether learned from Ah Fe or born of the mysterious loyalty of neglected children, affected Mrs. Tretherick more than accusation could have done.

“Where did you live before?”

“In the ’sylum.”

“You said Annie was there.”

“She had black hair. A lady took her. Ladies likes black hair.”

“And before that?”

“With Aunt Ria. Then she died. Then I went there.”

“Do you remember your mother?”

Carry considered.

“I remember a white hand. Maybe it was a curtain. Annie said you can ’member things if you tries hard. I ’member my mamma singin’.”

“What did she sing?”

“I dunno.”

Carry picked up the doll. “This is Mirandy. She ain’t pretty.”

“I can see that.”

“But she’s dood.”

This moral distinction was advanced with such gravity that Mrs. Tretherick smiled. Carry, observing the smile, smiled also—not boldly, but with the cautious delight of one who has accidentally opened a door into warmth.

“You may keep the scarf,” said Mrs. Tretherick.

“For Mirandy?”

“Yes.”

“I won’t tear it.”

“And you may use the room until I go.”

“You goin’?”

“To-night.”

Carry’s smile disappeared.

“Where?”

Mrs. Tretherick stood. “Far away.”

“San Fr’cisco?”

“Perhaps.”

“Is that where all the mamma ladies goes?”

The question was so plainly not designed as reproach that it achieved what malice might have failed to accomplish.

Mrs. Tretherick walked to the little attic window. Through it she could see the road curving among the pines, the far roofs of Fiddletown, and, beyond them, the brown hills fading into violet. Somewhere on that road Colonel Starbottle was undoubtedly waiting, watch in hand, breast expanded, prepared to defend her honor from every danger except uncertainty.

“Do you love your father?” she asked without turning.

Carry reflected again. Her moral judgments, unlike those of Fiddletown, had not been hastened by leisure.

“He’s my papa,” she said.

“That is not what I asked.”

“He said he’d buy me candy. He forgot.”

Mrs. Tretherick turned.

“Come downstairs. You must be hungry.”

In the kitchen they found bread, beans, half a chicken, and a jar of preserves. Mrs. Tretherick, who in the course of her married life had presided over meals without acquiring any precise knowledge of their preparation, cut the bread too thick, burned the chicken while reheating it, and gave Carry so much preserve that Ah Fe, had he been present, might have questioned her maternal science. Carry ate everything with solemn gratitude.

Mrs. Tretherick watched her.

“Does Ah Fe feed you?”

“Yes. He makes rice. An’ he washes me, but I can wash myselth.”

“When?”

“On Sundays. Sometimes.”

“I perceive.”

After supper Mrs. Tretherick heated water. Carry, suspecting some institutional discipline, retreated behind a chair; but the lady coaxed her out, washed her face, untangled her hair, and discovered under its barbarous cutting a head not without beauty. The hair, when brushed, fell in coppery waves to the child’s shoulders.

“There!” said Mrs. Tretherick.

Carry approached the small kitchen mirror.

“Is it black?” she asked hopefully.

“No.”

Carry sighed.

“It is prettier than black.”

“Sure?”

“Quite sure.”

“You ain’t foolin’?”

Mrs. Tretherick turned her sharply and buttoned the torn frock at the throat.

“Ladies do not fool.”

This assertion, uttered in a house where the contrary had been alleged with some frequency, passed unchallenged.

Carry touched the lady’s sleeve.

“Kin I call you somethin’?”

“What?”

The child hesitated, tracing the silk seam with one finger.

“Not the picture lady. Somethin’ little.”

“Call me Angelina.”

“An-ge-lina,” Carry repeated with care. It plainly did not satisfy her.

“Or Mrs. Tretherick.”

The child’s face brightened with recognition, then clouded again.

“That’s papa’s name.”

“Yes.”

“Kin I call you—” She stopped.

“What?”

Carry hid her face in Mrs. Tretherick’s skirts.

“Mamma?”

The lady stiffened.

I fear there are moments when language, having officiously accompanied us through drawing-rooms, courts, quarrels, declarations, editorials, and sermons, deserts us at the threshold of the heart. Mrs. Tretherick, who had never lacked a phrase while phrases could serve her, had none now.

Carry mistook the silence.

“I won’t,” she said from the folds of the dress. “I was only makin’ believe.”

Her small body withdrew.

Mrs. Tretherick caught her.

“Say it again.”

The child looked up.

“Mamma?”

The word was scarcely audible.

Mrs. Tretherick lifted her as if she weighed nothing. Carry’s arms went around her neck with a readiness more terrible than reluctance; the red head rested under her chin; the thin knees pressed against her waist. For one instant Mrs. Tretherick held herself erect, almost ceremoniously, like a painted Madonna conscious of spectators.

Then the pose broke.

Her arms tightened convulsively. She bent over the child, trembling; a sound—not graceful, not poetic, and wholly beyond rehearsal—escaped her. Carry, alarmed, patted her cheek.

“Don’t ky, Mamma.”

Beneath the child’s light pressure, buried deeper than vanity, deeper than grievance, deeper even than memory, the old wound opened. She felt again the darkened room of five years before, the weight suddenly gone from her body, the unbearable emptiness beside her, the doctor’s averted face. She felt the little cedar box beneath the linen upstairs. The grave she had carried secretly within her seemed to stir and fill, not with the lost child—nothing could restore that unknown face—but with warmth, breath, tangled hair, and two arms clinging desperately to life.

“Mamma,” Carry said once more, now with confidence.

Mrs. Tretherick covered the child’s face with kisses.

The clock struck six.

At the turn of the road Colonel Starbottle took out his watch, compared it with the courthouse clock, found the courthouse in error, and forgave it. He had secured two places on the San Francisco stage. His valise, his overcoat, a bouquet, and a case of pistols were concealed behind a rock. The bouquet had suffered from concealment, but the pistols had not.

At a quarter past six he paced.

At half past six he apostrophized the twilight.

At a quarter to seven he detected footsteps and assumed an attitude at once protective and passionate. The footsteps belonged to a mule.

When the stage appeared at seven, Mrs. Tretherick was not upon the road. The colonel delayed it by argument, by personal appeal, and finally by placing one foot upon the wheel. The driver, a man insensitive to romance but jealous of schedule, removed the foot with the butt of his whip.

“Ef your lady’s comin’, she’s comin’,” he observed. “Ef she ain’t, she’s alliz a lady. Git aboard or git out.”

Colonel Starbottle got out.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Tretherick had carried the sleeping Carry upstairs and laid her upon the bed. The child woke immediately and seized her dress.

“I ain’t asleep.”

“No, dear.”

“You won’t go while I’m asleep?”

Mrs. Tretherick looked toward the packed trunk.

“No.”

“Promise?”

“I promise.”

Carry relaxed, but retained a fold of silk in her fist. Presently her breathing deepened. Mrs. Tretherick sat beside her in the gathering darkness, listening to the clock, the wind in the pines, and once, far down the road, the departing stage.

At eight she rose.

Carry’s fingers tightened even in sleep.

Mrs. Tretherick freed her dress gently, went to the trunk, and opened it. She removed one embroidered gown, the Paris mantle, and several objects for which there was no room. Into the vacant space she placed two small frocks from the heap on the child’s chair, a worn pair of shoes, the lace-dressed doll, and a little blue apron she found behind the door.

Then she took out the cedar box.

For a long time she held the baby’s cap. At last she laid it beside Carry’s frocks and closed the trunk.

At nine Ah Fe returned.

He was a small, grave man in a blue blouse, with a face which Fiddletown described as passionless because its citizens were unable to read it. He entered by the kitchen, perceived the washed plates, the disturbed rooms, and Mrs. Tretherick standing at the foot of the stairs.

“You get wagon,” she said.

Ah Fe looked at her.

“Where go?”

“Sacramento road. We must meet the down stage at the crossing.”

“You—one?”

Mrs. Tretherick glanced upstairs.

“No. Two.”

Ah Fe’s eyes moved toward the ceiling. If he was surprised, the surprise respected his own privacy.

“Boss come back maybe one hour.”

“Then hurry.”

He went out. In twenty minutes a light wagon stood behind the house. Mrs. Tretherick had changed into a plain dark dress and bonnet. Carry, awakened and wrapped in a shawl, clutched Mirandy and asked no questions. The trunk was strapped behind.

As Ah Fe lifted the child into the wagon, Carry leaned toward him.

“I got a mamma,” she whispered.

Ah Fe looked at Mrs. Tretherick. She met his gaze defiantly, almost pleadingly.

“Heap good,” he said.

He handed her the reins.

“You won’t tell?” she asked.

“No sabe.”

“You understand me perfectly.”

“No sabe,” repeated Ah Fe.

There are declarations of loyalty less concise and less faithfully observed.

Mrs. Tretherick gathered the reins. Carry sat close against her, the doll between them.

“Mamma?”

“Yes?”

“Where we goin’?”

“To San Francisco.”

“Where all the mamma ladies goes?”

Mrs. Tretherick bent and kissed the red head.

“Where little girls go too.”

The wagon passed beneath the pines and vanished into the dark.

Mr. Tretherick returned forty minutes later in a condition which, while not wholly preventing locomotion, rendered the moral purpose of that faculty uncertain. He called for Ah Fe, kicked over a chair, discovered the open wardrobe, and became sober with remarkable speed.

“Where is she?”

Ah Fe polished a cup.

“Who?”

“The girl! Carry!”

Ah Fe looked around as if the child might be concealed in the cup.

“No sabe.”

Mr. Tretherick seized him by the blouse.

“Where’s my daughter?”

“Lady take.”

“What lady?”

“Missee Tlethlick.”

“Who went with her?”

Ah Fe considered.

“Little girl.”

“Who else?”

“No else.”

“The colonel?”

“No colonel.”

Tretherick released him. He went into the bedroom, saw the emptied drawers, the vacant bed, and the scrap of blue ribbon on the floor. His face, which had borne rage easily, seemed less fitted for bewilderment.

“She stole her,” he said.

Ah Fe continued polishing the cup.

“She stole my child.”

Possibly Mr. Tretherick loved Carry. Possibly he loved his right to possess her. Possibly, in that vacant room, the distinction was less clear to him than it may be to a moral philosopher with adequate lighting. He sat upon the bed and remained there until dawn.

By breakfast-time Fiddletown knew that Mrs. Tretherick had abducted her husband’s child with the assistance of Colonel Starbottle, Ah Fe, the San Francisco stage company, and, according to one authority, a Catholic priest disguised as a commercial traveler.

By noon the colonel had publicly denied the accusation, challenged its author, withdrawn the challenge because the author proved to be a woman, and issued a statement.

The statement appeared next morning in the *Fiddletown Avalanche* beneath the heading:

> **A LADY OUTRAGED!**
>
> **A CHILD TORN FROM A FATHER’S ARMS!**
>
> **WHERE IS THE LAW?**

The article began by declaring that it would make no appeal to passion, and immediately appealed to nothing else. It spoke of “a once happy hearthstone,” although no hearthstone in Fiddletown had enjoyed a less substantiated reputation for happiness. It referred to Mrs. Tretherick as “a dazzling but misguided daughter of Eve,” to Carry as “an infant blossom rudely plucked from the parental stem,” and to Mr. Tretherick as “a stricken sire pacing in desolation the halls of his violated home.”

The rival paper, the *Weekly Clarion*, which supported Colonel Starbottle politically and disliked the *Avalanche* on principle, replied that the “stricken sire” had been found asleep beneath the Magnolia billiard-table the previous evening, and that the parental stem had too often been seen leaning against a bar.

The *Avalanche* demanded evidence.

The *Clarion* offered witnesses.

The *Avalanche* called the witnesses “hirelings of corruption.”

The *Clarion* called the editor of the *Avalanche* a liar.

A duel followed. Both editors fired wide. A butcher standing at a prudent distance was wounded in the thigh and subsequently wrote a letter condemning the irresponsibility of the press.

Public opinion, which had first pitied Tretherick, began to ridicule him. It was said that no man could complain of losing a child whom he had forgotten to mention for two years. It was further observed that Mrs. Tretherick, having taken neither the colonel nor her husband, had shown an unexpected discrimination.

The colonel suffered most.

For three days he strode through the town buttoned to suffocation and prepared to explain. Unfortunately, nobody wished to hear him. The boys greeted him with cries of “Mamma!” A charcoal sketch appeared upon the courthouse wall representing him waiting beside a rock with a bouquet in one hand and a nursing-bottle in the other. Underneath was written:

> THE LAST OF THE STARBOTTLES;  
> OR, LOVE’S LABOR LOST.

The colonel attempted to erase the inscription and fell from the box upon which he stood. The fall was not dangerous, except to dignity.

At the Magnolia he made his defense.

“Gentlemen,” he began, placing one hand inside his coat, “I ask no indulgence. I demand justice. Was I deceived? Possibly. Was I abandoned? The expression is offensive, sir. A Starbottle may be delayed; he is never abandoned. Did the lady choose the claims of helpless infancy over the—over the—”

“Over you?” suggested Mr. Peters.

“Over a future, sir, whose details honor forbids me to disclose. Yes. And do I reproach her? Never! The maternal instinct, gentlemen, is sacred. It is older than law, stronger than passion, and—”

“Stronger’n you, colonel,” said a voice.

The room laughed.

The colonel drew himself up.

“Who said that?”

Nobody had. At least, nobody in Fiddletown could afterward remember doing so.

Tretherick made a formal complaint to the sheriff. The sheriff, who had once attempted to serve papers upon Mrs. Tretherick and retained a vivid recollection of the interview, suggested delay. The stage offices in Sacramento and Stockton were questioned. A woman with a child had been seen; several women with children had been seen; one witness remembered red hair, another black, a third no hair at all. In San Francisco the trail disappeared among hotels, ferries, ships, boardinghouses, and that accommodating fog in which so many Californian identities found temporary relief.

Colonel Starbottle offered to pursue.

Tretherick refused his assistance with language that produced another challenge.

They met at sunrise beyond the slaughter-yard. The colonel’s first shot removed Tretherick’s hat. Tretherick’s shot entered the hindquarters of a passing mule. The mule, adopting the rencounter as its own, pursued both principals into town and brought the proceedings to an end without benefit of seconds.

After this, the matter declined.

Mrs. Tretherick’s portrait disappeared from the cottage wall. Tretherick said he had burned it. Ah Fe said nothing, although ashes of that size were not observed. Carry’s room remained empty. The doll was gone; the blue ribbon lay for several weeks beneath the bed; a single child’s shoe stood in the closet until Tretherick, finding it one night, flung it through the window and afterward went outside to recover it.

He drank more and spoke less.

Fiddletown, having exhausted the moral aspect of the flight, preserved only its humorous details. Mrs. Tretherick became “the fair kidnapper”; Carry became “the red-headed heiress”; Colonel Starbottle became, for a season, “the bereaved.” A ballad was composed to the air of “Lily Dale,” in which rhyme required the fugitives to escape in a boat, though no navigable water lay within forty miles. The final verse, sung with particular fervor at the Magnolia, represented the colonel left upon the shore waving his cane at the setting sun.

Thus does history correct fact.

Six months passed.

Winter descended upon Fiddletown with rain, mud, and a temporary decline in virtue. The pines darkened; the road became a succession of brown pools; the cottage roof leaked over the parlor where the portrait had hung. Tretherick left for Nevada on business, or from necessity, or because the town had become intolerable. Colonel Starbottle transferred his affections, with unimpaired chivalry, to a widow from Sonora who possessed three children and a quartz claim.

Ah Fe remained.

One wet evening he closed the kitchen shutters, drew the bolt, and placed a small lamp upon the table. From inside his blouse he took a packet wrapped in oiled paper.

It bore no postmark. A teamster from Sacramento had handed it to him that afternoon with the remark that a lady had paid him two dollars to deliver it and had declined to give her name. Fiddletown, had it known, would have questioned the teamster for three days and concluded that the lady was a man.

Ah Fe opened the packet.

Inside lay a child’s apron.

It was the little blue apron that Mrs. Tretherick had packed behind the attic door. The torn pocket had been mended with tiny, uneven stitches. One corner bore a brown stain that washing had nearly removed. The apron smelled faintly of soap and, perhaps, of the sea.

There was no letter. There was only a narrow strip of paper on which laborious characters had been made in pencil:

> DEER AH FEE  
> I AM DOOD. MAMMA SAYS I MAY WRIGHT.  
> MIRANDY IS DOOD TOO.  
> I WASHED THIS MYSELTH.  
> TALLY.

Below, in another hand, were three words:

> Safe. Happy. Remembering.

Ah Fe read the paper twice. His expression did not change. Indeed, had any representative citizen of Fiddletown looked through the shutter, he might have found in that smooth yellow face fresh proof that the Chinese possessed neither affection nor domestic sentiment.

Ah Fe folded the message very carefully.

Then he lifted the apron, touched its freshly washed hem to his cheek, and smiled.

Outside, the rain beat upon the deserted house; the pines bowed and straightened in the darkness; and Fiddletown, secure in its facts, knew nothing.

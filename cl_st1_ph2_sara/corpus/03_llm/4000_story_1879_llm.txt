### Mrs. Bowman’s Second Choice

Mr. Nathaniel Clark was losing at draughts, and losing badly. As he had arranged this result himself, the circumstance annoyed him more than it would have done in the ordinary course of play.

For twenty minutes he had endeavoured, by errors of increasing magnitude, to place the victory in Mrs. Bowman’s hands. Mrs. Bowman, however, was thinking of something else, and continually frustrated him by declining to take pieces which no reasonable player could have overlooked. At last Mr. Clark, with a suppressed sigh, pushed one of his men so near the edge of the table that it fell upon the carpet.

“You’ve dropped one,” said Mrs. Bowman.

“So I have,” said Mr. Clark.

He stooped, and in recovering it displaced two more. Mrs. Bowman watched him build the three into a little castle beside the board.

“You’re not playing properly to-night, Nathaniel,” she observed.

“I’ve noticed it,” he said, with feeling.

Mrs. Bowman leaned back in her chair and regarded the fire. She was a handsome woman of forty-seven, with a complexion which had survived sorrow, housekeeping, and the solicitude of intimate friends. Widowhood had become her. It had removed the late Mr. Bowman, who had not.

“Nathaniel,” she said slowly, “I’ve had a letter.”

Mr. Clark, who was short, square, and incapable of appearing romantic except in a high wind, looked at her over the board.

“Who from?”

“Somebody I knew years ago.”

“A man?”

Mrs. Bowman bent toward the fire and adjusted a coal which required no attention.

“Yes.”

Mr. Clark put his hands upon his knees.

“What man?”

“You wouldn’t know him.”

“That don’t stop him having a name.”

“Charles Tucker,” said Mrs. Bowman, almost under her breath.

Mr. Clark picked up a draughtsman and examined it as though the name might be written on the underside.

“Tucker,” he repeated. “What does he want?”

“To see me.”

“What for?”

“How can I tell before he comes?”

“You can tell after he’s gone,” said Mr. Clark. “That’d be better.”

Mrs. Bowman smiled faintly. “He was once very fond of me.”

“Twenty-five years ago?”

“Twenty-six.”

Mr. Clark nodded. “Then he’s had time to get over it.”

“He says he hasn’t.”

Mr. Clark dropped the piece. It joined the castle.

For some moments there was no sound but the clock and a small movement in the chimney which Mrs. Bowman, in another mood, would have attributed to soot.

“He says,” she continued, “that through all his wanderings my memory has been the one bright star that guided him.”

“Guided him where?”

“He doesn’t say.”

“Trimington, apparently.”

Mrs. Bowman’s expression became reproachful. “You needn’t sneer, Nathaniel. Some men have feelings.”

“So I’ve heard.”

“He has been all over the world.”

“That accounts for his taking twenty-six years to get here.”

Mrs. Bowman folded the letter very carefully. Mr. Clark had not seen it, but already disliked the handwriting.

“He is coming to-morrow,” she said.

“Here?”

“To Trimington.”

“That ain’t an answer.”

“He may call.”

Mr. Clark rose and walked to the window. Outside, the lamps shone upon a wet street, and two neighbours passed slowly enough to discover whether the curtains were drawn.

“Amelia,” he said, “our banns are to be put up next Sunday.”

“There’s no hurry.”

Mr. Clark turned.

“No hurry?”

“We’ve waited six months.”

“That’s why there is.”

Mrs. Bowman’s gaze returned to the fire. “It would look unkind if I refused to see an old friend.”

“It’d look sensible.”

“He has remained single for my sake.”

“Did he say so?”

“Yes.”

“Then he’s got a grievance, not a claim.”

Mrs. Bowman’s colour deepened. “I think you are jealous.”

“I know I am.”

This frankness inconvenienced her. She had expected a denial and had prepared several replies to it.

“You’ve no cause,” she said at last.

“Then don’t give me any.”

He took his hat from the stand, placed it on his head, and stood waiting for her to detain him. Mrs. Bowman picked up a draughtsman and set it on top of the castle.

“Good-night, Nathaniel.”

“Good-night.”

He departed with dignity, forgot his umbrella, returned for it without dignity, and departed again.

Charles Tucker arrived in Trimington on the following afternoon.

The station porter, who had seen many travellers and distrusted most of them, watched him descend from the train in a long frock-coat, a broad hat, and a pair of boots more suitable for conquering continents than crossing Station Road. His mustache was large and carefully trained; his hair, though touched with grey, preserved an adventurous wave; and in his buttonhole he wore enough mignonette to suggest either a florist’s funeral or a very determined courtship.

He had one leather bag, two sticks, and an air of having left the remainder of his baggage on the banks of the Orinoco.

At the Crown Hotel he asked, with studied carelessness, whether Mrs. Amelia Bowman still lived in Cedar Terrace.

The proprietor said that she did.

“Alone?” asked Mr. Tucker.

“With a servant.”

“No—relations?”

“No.”

Mr. Tucker’s face softened.

“Ah,” he said.

The proprietor, who knew of Mr. Clark, coughed.

“She’s going to be married.”

Mr. Tucker’s face hardened, softened again, and finally arranged itself into a noble resignation.

“Indeed?”

“To Mr. Nathaniel Clark.”

“What sort of man?”

“Very respectable.”

Mr. Tucker looked relieved. “I asked what sort.”

The proprietor described Mr. Clark as well as a man can be described by one who has borrowed money from him and repaid it.

Half an hour later Mr. Tucker stood in Mrs. Bowman’s drawing room, holding both her hands and looking at her with the solemn astonishment of a man who has discovered spring in November.

“Amelia!”

“Charles!”

“Unchanged!”

Mrs. Bowman withdrew one hand to touch her hair. “Nonsense.”

“The same eyes,” said Mr. Tucker tenderly. “The same smile. The same little way of looking down when emotion conquers speech.”

Mrs. Bowman had been looking down to see whether he had brought mud upon the carpet. She raised her eyes hurriedly.

“You are altered, Charles.”

“I have lived.”

He said it impressively, and turned so that his profile might assist the statement.

“You went abroad soon after—after—”

“After hope died,” said Mr. Tucker.

Mrs. Bowman sighed.

“I heard you had gone to Canada.”

“Canada, the States, Mexico, the Argentine, Africa.” He waved a hand. “Places are nothing. One travels to escape memory and finds that memory has taken the earlier boat.”

“You never married?”

“Could I?”

Mrs. Bowman considered this. “I did.”

“I know.”

There was a pause delicate enough to have satisfied both of them had not Ellen, the servant, entered with tea and stared at Mr. Tucker’s mignonette.

Mr. Tucker drank four cups, ate steadily, and talked of the wilderness. His adventures improved as his hunger diminished. He had crossed mountains without food, deserts without water, and rivers without bridges. He had slept beneath the stars, fled from hostile tribes, and shared his last crust with a dying companion whose name he appeared unable to recollect.

“And through it all,” he concluded, “one face was ever before me.”

Mrs. Bowman’s lips parted.

“A woman’s face,” Mr. Tucker added.

“Oh, Charles!”

“Yours.”

At that moment the door opened and Mr. Clark entered without knocking. He stopped, looked at Mr. Tucker, and then looked at the mignonette.

“Good evening,” he said.

Mrs. Bowman made the introduction. Mr. Tucker rose and extended a hand with the expansive cordiality of a man welcoming an inferior upon neutral ground.

“Delighted, sir. Any friend of Amelia’s—”

“Mrs. Bowman’s,” said Mr. Clark.

Mr. Tucker’s hand remained out for a moment, then went up to stroke his mustache.

“Quite,” he said. “Quite.”

Mr. Clark seated himself near the fire. Mrs. Bowman asked whether he would have tea.

“No, thankee.”

“Mr. Tucker has been telling me some of his adventures.”

“Has he finished?”

Mr. Tucker laughed easily. “I fear one never finishes. Life has treated me roughly, Mr. Clark.”

“You look as if you’d treated it back.”

“I have had to,” said Mr. Tucker, expanding. “Once, in the Rockies, I was attacked by a bear.”

“Black or brown?” asked Mr. Clark.

Mr. Tucker hesitated only long enough to select the more impressive colour. “Brown.”

“Big one?”

“Enormous.”

“What did you do?”

“I had no weapon.”

“That was careless.”

“I fought it with my bare hands.”

Mrs. Bowman shuddered agreeably.

Mr. Clark glanced at Mr. Tucker’s hands. “Who won?”

Mr. Tucker leaned back. “I am here.”

“So’s the bear, perhaps.”

Mrs. Bowman frowned. “Nathaniel!”

“I’m only asking.”

Mr. Tucker smiled with some effort. “A man who has faced death, sir, learns not to boast.”

“He’d better learn sooner,” said Mr. Clark.

The visit ended ten minutes later. Mr. Clark remained, but his victory was without profit. Mrs. Bowman spoke warmly of Mr. Tucker’s courage and coldly of Mr. Clark’s manners. When he reminded her of the banns, she said there was no hurry.

The next day Mr. Tucker called at eleven and stayed to luncheon. On the following day he arrived after luncheon and stayed to tea. By the end of the week he had discovered that the chair near Mrs. Bowman’s worktable suited his back, that Ellen cut sandwiches too thick, and that Mr. Clark generally appeared at half-past seven.

Trimington observed these developments with interest. Mrs. Kersey, at Number Nine, twice met Mr. Tucker emerging from Cedar Terrace and was compelled on both occasions to retrace her steps because she had forgotten to notice the time. The butcher’s wife identified him as a military man; the chemist’s sister said actor; while the proprietor of the Crown, who possessed facts, was disregarded.

Mr. Clark’s position became difficult. If he called, he found Mr. Tucker. If he stayed away, he imagined him. If he spoke sharply, Mrs. Bowman defended her guest; if he spoke civilly, Mr. Tucker treated the civility as surrender.

One evening Mr. Tucker was describing a winter spent among Indians.

“They adopted me,” he said. “The chief called me his white son.”

“Couldn’t have known you long,” said Mr. Clark.

Mr. Tucker ignored him. “His daughter—Moon-in-the-Pines—saved my life.”

Mrs. Bowman looked interested. “Was she beautiful?”

“As dawn upon the prairie.”

“What became of her?”

Mr. Tucker sighed. “She wished me to remain.”

“And you came away?” asked Mrs. Bowman.

“There are some memories a man cannot betray.”

Mr. Clark leaned forward. “Did all these women want to marry you?”

“All?”

“The Indian one, and that Spanish widow yesterday, and the girl in Cape Town.”

Mr. Tucker’s eyes narrowed. “A traveller meets strange destinies.”

“He seems to dodge a good many too.”

“Nathaniel,” said Mrs. Bowman, “you are being rude again.”

“I’m outnumbered again.”

He got up and stood by the mantelpiece. Mr. Tucker, warmed by the room and his own narrative, stretched his legs.

“There were nights,” he continued, addressing Mrs. Bowman, “when I lay starving beneath foreign skies and repeated your name to keep my courage alive.”

“You told us Tuesday it was snow you ate,” said Mr. Clark.

“Snow does not nourish the soul, sir.”

“Nor much else.”

Mrs. Bowman put down her sewing. “I wish you two would not quarrel.”

“I never quarrel,” said Mr. Tucker, mildly.

“You want somebody else for that too, I s’pose,” said Mr. Clark.

He left shortly afterward. Mrs. Bowman watched him go, and for the first time did not remind him of his umbrella. Mr. Tucker moved into the chair he had vacated.

“He loves you,” he said gravely.

Mrs. Bowman’s face softened.

“In his way,” Mr. Tucker added.

“What do you mean?”

“Nothing. A settled, comfortable affection is a valuable thing.”

“He is very steady.”

“I am sure of it.”

“And kind.”

“No doubt.”

Mrs. Bowman’s mouth tightened. “You needn’t speak as though Nathaniel were a sideboard.”

Mr. Tucker looked wounded. “I honour him. He is the sort of man a woman may lean upon without ever feeling giddy.”

The next evening Mrs. Bowman asked Mr. Clark whether it would not be wise to postpone the banns.

“No,” said Mr. Clark.

She had expected arguments and was offended by the absence of them.

“There’s no hurry,” she said.

“There is for me.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m tired of hearing there ain’t.”

Mrs. Bowman smoothed her dress. “Charles has been very lonely.”

“He’s making up for it.”

“He has carried my memory for twenty-six years.”

“He ought to charge storage.”

“You don’t understand sentiment.”

“I understand him.”

“You have never had an adventure in your life.”

“I was engaged to you.”

Mrs. Bowman rose. “If you mean to insult me—”

“I don’t. I mean to marry you.”

“And if I’m not sure?”

Mr. Clark’s face changed slightly. “Not sure of what?”

“Of myself.”

He stared at her. She avoided his eyes and rearranged three ornaments upon the mantelpiece. When she had finished, all three were worse placed.

“Amelia,” he said, “are you telling me you want Tucker?”

“I’m not telling you anything of the kind.”

“You’re doing it without.”

“He has awakened old feelings.”

“Let ’em go back to sleep.”

“I cannot command my heart.”

“No,” said Mr. Clark bitterly. “Anybody with a frock-coat can.”

She turned upon him, but he was already reaching for his hat.

“Where are you going?”

“Home.”

“You always run away when one tries to speak seriously.”

“I’m going before I speak too serious.”

He went out, carrying his umbrella although the night was dry.

During the next fortnight the rivalry became public. Mr. Tucker escorted Mrs. Bowman to church, walked with her in the High Street, and accompanied her upon the tram to West Trimington, where they inspected a furnished villa without any definite object. Mr. Clark continued to call, but he called now with the air of a creditor visiting a house from which the furniture was being removed.

The town sympathised with him. Men who had never invited him to drink did so; women lowered their voices when he passed; and the barber, while shaving him, sighed twice and charged him threepence less.

Mr. Clark endured condolence badly. He also endured Mr. Tucker badly, Mrs. Bowman’s hesitation badly, and solitude worst of all. His face acquired a set expression which some called grief and others indigestion.

At last, on a wet Thursday evening, Mrs. Bowman told him that the wedding must be postponed indefinitely.

Mr. Tucker was present. He had offered to leave, but had done so from a chair into which he had just settled comfortably.

“I think,” said Mrs. Bowman, looking at the carpet, “that it would be wrong to marry while my feelings are unsettled.”

Mr. Clark stood by the table with both hands resting upon it.

“Are they unsettled,” he asked, “or settled the other way?”

“That is cruel.”

“Is it true?”

Mrs. Bowman glanced toward Mr. Tucker. Mr. Tucker lowered his eyes with delicacy, but not before Mr. Clark had seen him.

“I care for Charles,” she said.

Mr. Clark drew a long breath.

“More than me?”

“I don’t know.”

“I do.”

“Nathaniel—”

He raised his hand. His face became pale, and his voice, when it came, had an unearthly depth which surprised even himself.

“Enough. I release you.”

Mrs. Bowman began to cry.

Mr. Tucker rose, stern and protective. “Sir, this lady—”

“You keep out of it.”

“I cannot stand by—”

“Then sit down.”

Mr. Tucker sat down in order, apparently, to rise with greater effect, but Mr. Clark had already taken his hat.

“May you be happy,” he said to Mrs. Bowman.

“Oh, Nathaniel!”

“And may you,” he continued, turning to Mr. Tucker, “get what you deserve.”

He flung his cigar into the fire, where, being unlighted, it remained in excellent condition. Then he walked to the door.

Mrs. Bowman followed him two steps. “Nathaniel, don’t go like this.”

“How would you like me to go?”

“I—I don’t know.”

“Neither do I.”

The front door closed. Mrs. Bowman sank into a chair and covered her face.

Mr. Tucker stood over her in silent misery. After a suitable interval he placed one hand upon her shoulder.

“Better a wound now,” he murmured, “than a lifetime’s regret.”

Mrs. Bowman sobbed.

“A brave man,” said Mr. Tucker, “would rather suffer than stand between two hearts.”

Mrs. Bowman’s sobbing diminished.

“And time,” he continued, “the great physician—”

“He looked dreadful,” she said.

“He will recover.”

“How do you know?”

Mr. Tucker paused. “Men do.”

For the next three days Mr. Clark remained indoors, except when he went out by the back way. Trimington supposed him prostrate. The supposition was assisted by Mr. Tucker, who spoke with generous sadness of a defeated rival, and by Mrs. Bowman, who wore grey and avoided the High Street.

On Monday Mr. Tucker and Mrs. Bowman discussed the future.

“I have a little money,” said Mrs. Bowman.

Mr. Tucker protested with uplifted hand.

“And this house.”

He protested less.

“You have never told me exactly what business you are in.”

“Business?” repeated Mr. Tucker, as though she had introduced an unpleasant smell.

“One must live.”

“I have interests.”

“What interests?”

“Abroad.”

Mrs. Bowman waited.

“They require adjustment,” he added.

“Would we live here?”

Mr. Tucker looked around the room. “At first, perhaps.”

“And afterward?”

“The world is wide.”

“So I’ve heard. I’d like to know which part.”

He crossed to the window. “Amelia, do not bring the language of account books into the hour of dreams.”

“I kept Bowman’s accounts for fourteen years,” she said. “They were more reliable.”

Mr. Tucker smiled tenderly and suggested a walk.

It was during that walk that he first showed uneasiness. A small red-haired woman alighted from a tram, and Mr. Tucker turned so abruptly to admire a shop window that he struck his hat against the glass. Later, when Mrs. Bowman asked how he had obtained her address, he replied that Providence had guided him.

“Providence writes a poor hand,” she said. “The letter looked like a newspaper office.”

Mr. Tucker became interested in a passing dog.

That evening he left his frock-coat in the drawing room while assisting Ellen with a jammed garden door. Mrs. Bowman, moving the coat from one chair to another, heard paper crackle in the breast pocket.

She was not a woman who habitually examined gentlemen’s pockets. Neither was she a woman who regarded a habit as necessary when circumstances were favourable.

The paper was a cutting from a London newspaper, folded twice and worn at the creases. She opened it.

“INFORMATION DESIRED,” she read. “Charles Tucker, formerly of Trimington, believed to have travelled in Canada and South America, is requested to communicate with N. C., Box 47, regarding a matter greatly to his advantage. Expenses paid.”

The room became very still.

Mrs. Bowman read it again. Then she noticed a date, six weeks earlier, and beneath the printed advertisement, in pencil, the address of Mr. Nathaniel Clark.

She folded the cutting, replaced it, and sat down.

Mr. Tucker returned, carrying upon his sleeve the cobwebs of a struggle in which the garden door had taken no part.

“Conquered,” he said cheerfully.

Mrs. Bowman smiled.

He stopped.

It was not a smile he had seen before. It contained no memory, no maidenly confusion, and no admiration for bears. It was calm, pleasant, and entirely awake.

“You are clever, Charles,” she said.

“My dear Amelia—”

“So many adventures.”

He bowed.

“So many narrow escapes.”

His bow diminished.

“And now another.”

Mr. Tucker looked at his coat. Mrs. Bowman looked at it too.

He put it on with care.

“You have been through my pockets,” he said.

“I have been through one. It was enough.”

Mr. Tucker sat down. For perhaps the first time in his adventurous career, he had nothing ready to say.

“Did Nathaniel pay your expenses?” asked Mrs. Bowman.

Mr. Tucker sighed.

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

“And what was the matter greatly to your advantage?”

“You.”

Mrs. Bowman’s eyes hardened.

“That was his phrase,” Mr. Tucker added hastily. “Not mine.”

“Tell me.”

He looked toward the door, measured the distance, and decided that dignity required confession.

“Clark wanted freedom.”

“From me?”

“He said you were attached to him.”

“How conceited.”

“Devoted, was his word.”

“How insulting.”

“He believed you would never release him.”

“How accurate.”

Mr. Tucker shifted in his chair.

“He had become interested elsewhere.”

Mrs. Bowman’s face did not move. “Who?”

“I don’t know.”

“You do.”

“He mentioned no name.”

“Charles.”

Mr. Tucker rubbed his mustache. “A young person connected with the lending library.”

“Miss Vale?”

“I fancy that was it.”

There was another silence.

“And so,” said Mrs. Bowman, “Nathaniel advertised for an old sweetheart, and you came.”

“He wrote that you were comfortably situated.”

“Ah.”

“I came,” said Mr. Tucker with dignity, “because your name stirred memories which I had thought dead.”

“Expenses paid.”

“A man must travel.”

“You told me Providence guided you.”

“Through the medium of the press.”

“And all this—your devotion, your loneliness, the Indian princess?”

“Moon-in-the-Pines was quite real.”

“Was the bear?”

Mr. Tucker drew himself up. “Amelia!”

She rose. “How much did Nathaniel promise you?”

“Nothing improper.”

“How much?”

Mr. Tucker named a sum.

Mrs. Bowman’s anger, which had hitherto been large and general, became exact.

“And if you married me?”

“That possibility was left to—to natural development.”

“You were to take me if you liked the house.”

“Your interpretation is unjust.”

“I shall improve it overnight.”

Mr. Tucker watched her walk to the bell.

“What are you going to do?”

“Sleep.”

“And to-morrow?”

She rang.

“To-morrow,” she said sweetly, “I shall attend to Nathaniel.”

Mr. Tucker spent a disturbed night at the Crown. At breakfast he saw Mr. Clark pass the window on his way to the office, walking with the briskness of one whose broken heart kept excellent hours. Mr. Tucker considered warning him, but friendship between conspirators is most delicate when the conspiracy has succeeded. Besides, there remained the possibility that Mrs. Bowman might forgive romance sooner than calculation.

At nine o’clock she called at Mr. Clark’s house.

He opened the door himself. His face, at sight of her, underwent several rapid changes and settled upon grief.

“Amelia!”

“Nathaniel.”

He stepped back, one hand upon his breast.

“This is unexpected.”

“It needn’t be. You advertised.”

Mr. Clark’s hand fell.

She entered and closed the door behind her.

For a while they looked at one another in the narrow hall. Then Mr. Clark led the way into the parlour, where a vase of flowers stood upon the table. Mrs. Bowman recognised them as the kind sold beside the lending library.

“How are you?” he asked.

“Comfortable.”

“I’m glad.”

“No, you’re not.”

Mr. Clark sat down.

Mrs. Bowman remained standing. “I know everything.”

“Tucker told you?”

“His pocket did.”

“The fool.”

“The other fool is before me.”

Mr. Clark examined the carpet.

“You wished to be free,” she continued.

“I thought it best.”

“You wished to marry Miss Vale.”

Mr. Clark looked up sharply. “Who said marry?”

Mrs. Bowman’s eyebrows rose.

“I mean,” he said, “nothing was settled.”

“Nothing will be.”

He stared.

“You wouldn’t—”

“I would.”

“It’s got nothing to do with her.”

“It has now.”

Mr. Clark rose and began to walk about the room.

“Amelia, let’s be reasonable.”

“I have been reasonable for nearly an hour. I don’t care for it.”

“I never meant to hurt you.”

“You meant to make a fool of me.”

“No. I meant Tucker to.”

“That distinction will comfort you later.”

Mr. Clark stopped. “You can’t force a man to marry.”

“Certainly not. We shall go willingly.”

“We?”

“To put up the banns.”

His face became blank.

Mrs. Bowman moved toward the window and looked across the street. Two lace curtains opposite trembled.

“If we leave at half-past ten,” she said, “we shall meet Mrs. Kersey, the butcher’s wife, and probably Mr. Tucker. I shall take your arm.”

“I won’t go.”

“Then I shall call upon Miss Vale and explain why.”

Mr. Clark sat down again.

“You wouldn’t do such a thing.”

Mrs. Bowman smiled.

He looked at the smile, remembered that he had not seen it until too late, and groaned.

“Do you still want me?” he asked miserably.

“My feelings have changed.”

“Then why—”

“They have changed twice.”

“That ain’t an answer.”

“It is the only one you are getting.”

He buried his face in his hands. Mrs. Bowman waited without impatience. The clock ticked, a cart passed, and somewhere in the kitchen a tap dripped with a regularity which increased Mr. Clark’s distress.

At last he looked up.

“What about Tucker?”

“I have plans for him too.”

Mr. Clark’s gloom lifted slightly.

“What plans?”

“You may come and see.”

At ten-thirty precisely, Mrs. Bowman emerged from Mr. Clark’s house upon his arm. She wore her best hat and carried herself with the composed brightness of a woman whose happiness has returned after creating considerable inconvenience.

Mr. Clark looked as though his had not.

Trimington witnessed the procession. Mrs. Kersey bowed from across the street and nearly walked into a lamppost. The butcher’s wife hurried into her shop to spread the news before the couple had passed the sausages. At the corner, the barber came out without his coat, smiled at Mr. Clark, and received a look which sent him in again.

“You might look pleased,” murmured Mrs. Bowman.

“I might,” said Mr. Clark, “if I had your imagination.”

“Lift your head.”

He lifted it.

“Smile.”

He exposed his teeth.

“Not like that.”

At the Crown Hotel they encountered Mr. Tucker, who was standing in the doorway with his bag at his feet. His mignonette had been replaced by a smaller spray, better suited to uncertainty.

Mrs. Bowman stopped.

“Charles,” she said, “Nathaniel and I are going to have the banns put up.”

Mr. Tucker looked from her serene face to Mr. Clark’s murderous one.

“So I perceive.”

“You will congratulate us.”

Mr. Tucker swallowed. “With all my heart.”

“Not all,” said Mr. Clark.

Mrs. Bowman pressed his arm.

Mr. Tucker recovered himself. “I had intended leaving by the eleven-twenty.”

“How sudden,” said Mrs. Bowman.

“Business abroad.”

“Still requiring adjustment?”

“Urgently.”

She inclined her head. “You must write.”

Mr. Tucker brightened cautiously.

“Though if you forget,” she continued, “I can always advertise.”

Mr. Tucker’s hand went to his pocket.

“I don’t think,” he said, “that will be necessary.”

“Expenses paid,” said Mr. Clark.

Mr. Tucker ignored him. He took Mrs. Bowman’s fingers, bent over them without quite touching them, and murmured a farewell in which lost youth, distant horizons, and undying memory were discreetly represented.

Then he offered his hand to Mr. Clark.

Mr. Clark looked at it.

“Good-bye,” said Mr. Tucker.

“It had better be.”

Their eyes met. Mr. Tucker withdrew his hand and performed, behind a dignified exterior, a hasty sum in comparative values. On one side stood Mrs. Bowman, her house, her money, and her newly discovered disposition. On the other stood the eleven-twenty.

He picked up his bag.

Mrs. Bowman and Mr. Clark continued down the High Street. By eleven o’clock the banns were arranged; by noon Miss Vale had been told, through several trustworthy channels, of Mrs. Bowman’s romantic reconciliation; and by one o’clock Mr. Tucker was travelling south at a speed which twenty-six years of devotion had not enabled him to approach.

That evening Mrs. Bowman set out the draughtsboard.

Mr. Clark regarded it without enthusiasm.

“Your move, Nathaniel,” she said.

He pushed a piece forward.

She captured it at once.

Mr. Clark looked at the board, then at his future wife.

“You never used to see that move,” he said.

Mrs. Bowman built the captured piece into a small castle beside the board.

“I do now,” she replied.

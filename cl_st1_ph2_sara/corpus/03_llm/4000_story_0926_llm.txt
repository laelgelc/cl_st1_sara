In a narrow street where the sun fell chiefly upon ash barrels and broken bottles, a child discovered a dog.

The child was sitting on a curb, scraping designs in the dust with a nail. He had drawn a house with nine chimneys and was adding smoke when the dog paused near him. It was a small dog, reddish under the dirt, with one white foot and a piece of rope trailing from its neck. The rope had evidently survived some former arrangement of society from which the dog had escaped or been dismissed.

The child stared. The dog wagged his tail cautiously.

“Come here,” said the child.

The dog advanced with the solemn uncertainty of one presenting himself before an official whose powers were unknown. He stopped beyond reach and wagged again. His ears went backward in humility, while his eyes expressed a wish to be considered harmless, amusing, and, if possible, useful.

The child snapped his fingers. When the dog came nearer, he struck him across the nose with the nail.

The dog leaped aside and sneezed. He looked at the child in astonishment. Apparently he believed that he had committed some breach of ceremony. After a moment he returned, creeping low, and lay upon his back. His white foot and three brown ones were folded in a peculiar manner above his chest. They formed, no doubt, a small prayer.

The child laughed and struck at them.

The dog scrambled up and fled six yards. There he turned. His tail resumed its humble activity. The child called him, and again he came. Their interview continued in this fashion for some time. The child possessed a nail and superior social rank; the dog possessed only a desire for friendship. The issue was never doubtful.

At length the child rose and went home. The dog followed.

“Go ’way,” said the child, kicking backward.

The dog dodged with strategic ability of a high order. When the child turned, he sat down. When the child walked on, he followed. The rope behind him whispered over the cobbles and occasionally caught about a stone, causing him to tumble. Each mishap seemed to fill him with shame, and he hurried forward contritely, as if apologising for the clumsiness of the world.

At the tenement door the child faced him.

“You can’t come up.”

The dog drooped like a plant that had been held too near a stove. The child entered the dark passage and began the ascent. On the first landing he heard claws scratching below. The dog had ventured into the stairway, but its black height appalled him. He climbed two steps, slipped back one, became entangled in the rope, and uttered a faint cry.

The child descended with impatience, seized the rope, and dragged him upward.

Thus the dog entered his new kingdom.

The family occupied two rooms on the fifth floor. In the larger room were a stove, a table with one sound leg, three chairs, a mattress, a heap of coal, and many articles which had lost their original purposes without acquiring new ones. A saucepan rested under a leak, although it had not rained for four days. The air smelled of cabbage, damp cloth, and old smoke.

The child’s mother stood at the stove. Two older children quarreled beside the mattress. A man slept in a chair with his cap over his eyes.

“What’s that?” the mother demanded.

“My dog,” said the child.

“It ain’t.”

“It is.”

“Put it out.”

The child drew the animal behind him. At once the new proprietor assumed the manner of a baron defending an ancestral estate. “He’s mine. I found him.”

The dog peered from behind the child’s legs. He wagged at the family, submitting his application to each member. The older children laughed. One threw a piece of coal, which struck the wall near his head. He fell instantly upon his back and arranged his paws in prayer.

This performance pleased them. More coal followed.

The child sprang forward, shrieking, and struck one of his brothers with a spoon. A domestic battle began. The mother turned from the stove and administered blows impartially, using a wet cloth. The man woke, cursed all present, and went to sleep again.

During the carnival the dog crept beneath the table. From there he watched his little monarch fighting on his behalf, and an immense devotion took possession of him. It is not to be supposed that he understood the dispute concerning his residence. He understood only that the child stood between him and the coal. This was enough.

Afterward the mother said, “If that beast makes dirt, out he goes.”

The child promised impossible things.

So the dog remained.

He was named Rags, although the name had already belonged to a cat, a doll, and an uncle who was then in jail. The child tied the rope to the table leg at night. Rags slept beneath the chair, curled tightly, with his nose resting on the white foot. If the child stirred on the mattress, the dog’s tail tapped the floor.

In the morning they went into the streets together.

Their expeditions were magnificent. They visited the slaughterhouse fence, the railway arches, a yard where men repaired wagons, and a vacant lot decorated with tin cans. The child commanded armies, drove imaginary horses, arrested criminals, and discovered treasure. Rags served successively as cavalry, prisoner, dragon, and treasure. He accepted all offices with enthusiasm.

Sometimes the child fastened the rope to a broken cart and ordered him to pull. When Rags failed, the child beat him with a stick. The dog would crouch, bewildered by the sudden darkness in his heaven, and then roll upon his back. His paws made their small appeal. The child might continue until some other amusement occurred to him.

A minute later they would be running side by side, Rags capering so joyously that one could suppose the beating had been a misunderstanding for which he had forgiven the universe.

In the rooms above the street, his fortune depended upon the child’s presence. When his patron was away at school, the family treated him as a public convenience. He was kicked from the stove, chased from the mattress, blamed for spilled water, missing food, fleas, and noises made by other dogs in the alley. The older children devised recreations involving string, a washboard, and a cracked saucepan. Rags learned to recognise preparations for these ceremonies and slid beneath the table like an assassin.

His evasions caused merriment. Chairs were moved. Brooms explored the shadows. When captured, he submitted with infinite lowliness.

The mother disliked him because he ate. The father disliked him because he moved. The brothers disliked him because he could be frightened. All agreed that he should be turned out, but no one wished to perform an act which might bring the child screaming into the matter.

For the child’s anger was celebrated. He would rush into a persecution like a knight entering battle, with no concern for size, justice, or household weapons. Once he found a brother dragging Rags by the rope. The child attacked with a bottle and was knocked beneath the stove. From there he continued the war with curses of remarkable maturity.

Another time the mother struck the dog with a broom. The child bit her hand. She beat both child and dog, and afterward they lay behind the mattress together. Rags licked the child’s wet face with delicate care. He seemed to believe that sorrow was a wound which could be cleansed.

Yet on the following day the child struck him because a marble had rolled into a gutter.

Rags did not revise his faith. A god may send thunder as well as sunlight; the duty of a subject is not to inquire. The child was the one creature who sometimes brought food, sometimes offered shelter, and always returned. In the dog’s religion, this amounted to perfection.

The father returned most evenings in a state that altered the government of the rooms. Sober, he was silent and heavy, and the family moved around him as people pass a dangerous machine. Drunk, he became cheerful. His cheerfulness was attended by smashed plates, overturned chairs, songs, accusations, and abrupt experiments with his fists.

At the sound of his boots on the stairs, Rags usually vanished beneath the table. The child, too, grew watchful. Mother lifted breakable things out of reach, though there were fewer each month. The older children found errands in the hall.

On some nights the father ignored the dog. On others he called, “Here, rat,” and held out a hand. Rags, after painful consideration, sometimes approached. He desired the approval of every authority. The father would then seize his ears or blow smoke into his face, laughing as the animal coughed. If Rags fled, a shoe followed him.

The child objected, but his courage had boundaries described by the father’s arm. He could attack his mother, his brothers, doors, furniture, and policemen at a distance. Before his father he became very small.

One hot afternoon the child and Rags went to the river. They returned after sunset, dusty, hungry, and peaceful. Rags had acquired a fish head and carried it as a banner of prosperity. The child had found a brass button. Each considered the day successful.

In the lower hall they heard a crash from above.

The child stopped. Rags stopped because the child had stopped. Another crash followed, then a man’s roaring song and the mother’s thin voice.

The child placed a hand upon the dog’s head.

“Stay,” he whispered.

They climbed.

On the fifth landing the door stood open. A chair lay in the passage. Inside, the father was conducting a festival. The stove door had been torn off. Broken crockery covered the floor. The mattress leaned against the wall, and the mother crouched behind it with blood on her mouth. One brother watched from the doorway of the smaller room. The other had disappeared.

The father stood at the table, waving a blackened coffee-pot. He appeared exceptionally happy. His face shone with sweat, and his eyes had the bright vacancy of windows in an abandoned house.

The child slipped behind the mattress.

Rags did not understand this manoeuvre. He entered with the fish head in his mouth and wagged. Perhaps he imagined that the family’s excitement concerned his return. Perhaps the raised coffee-pot resembled a familiar invitation to sport. At any rate, he approached the father.

“Look at him!” the man cried. “Look at the little king!”

Rags wagged harder.

The coffee-pot descended upon his back.

He dropped the fish head and screamed. Instantly he rolled over, holding his paws in their peculiar manner. He offered the old prayer, proven useless but still the only petition he knew.

The father struck him again.

Rags attempted to crawl beneath the table. The pot rang against his ribs and then against the floor. The man laughed. The older brother laughed faintly from the doorway, because laughter was often the safest opinion.

The child burst from hiding.

“Leave him be!”

He seized his father’s sleeve. The man flung him aside, and he struck the stove. The mother made no movement. Her face had the patient blankness of an object long used.

Rags, hearing the child cry, struggled up. Despite terror, pain, and the manifest hostility of the universe, he tried to reach him. His tail made one uncertain motion. He had apparently concluded that his monarch required comfort.

The father caught him by a hind leg.

For a moment the dog hung upside down. His body twisted, his forepaws paddling at the air. His eyes found the child. They contained no accusation. There was only fright, and beneath the fright the old trust, sublime and foolish.

The window was open to the heat.

The father swung the dog once and threw him through it.

Rags went out among a shower of loose plaster. His rope followed in a graceful curve.

Across the alley a woman watering flowers saw a small brown shape pass her level. She cried out and dropped the cup. Two boys on a fire escape cheered before understanding what they had seen. At a lower window, a man leaned out too late.

The dog struck the sloping roof of a shed. The impact made a dull sound. He rolled, clawed once at the tar, slid over the edge, and fell into the alley among ash cans.

For several seconds the room was quiet.

Then the father looked around for the coffee-pot.

The child lay beside the stove, staring at the window. He rose. No one stopped him. He went into the hall and began to descend.

The stairway that Rags had once climbed with such difficulty now seemed endless. The child held the rail with both hands. At each landing he lowered one foot, then brought the other beside it. He had been struck on the hip and could not walk properly. He descended backward, as very small children descend, facing the stairs.

Above him the father resumed his song.

Doors had opened throughout the building. Neighbours whispered. A woman asked what had happened, and someone said it was only the family on the fifth floor. This explanation satisfied several persons. In the tenement, calamities were common enough to require addresses rather than names.

The child continued downward. On the third floor he began to sob, but made no sound. On the second he sat for a moment, then rose again. The dark stairs smelled of dust, onions, and kerosene.

At last he reached the yard and went through the passage into the alley.

A little crowd had gathered near the ash cans. It moved aside when he came. Rags lay on his side, his rope stretched behind him. One white foot showed against the black ground. His eyes were partly open, and his ears, which had spoken so often of shame, hope, fear, and devotion, were quiet.

The child knelt. He touched the dog’s head and waited for the tail to answer.

Nothing answered.

The crowd soon lost interest. Windows closed. The boys climbed back to the fire escape. Somewhere above, a plate broke and the father shouted triumphantly.

The child sat among the ashes with Rags in his lap. He wiped dust from the white paw and folded it carefully against the other three, as he had seen the dog do in moments of trouble.

Then he remained there, holding his little companion.

## The Great Clock of Interlaken

I arrived at Interlaken by railway, which is the proper
way to arrive anywhere when one has little time and
less luggage, but not the proper way to see a
country. A railway train does not travel through scenery; it
abolishes it. It rushes a valley upon you, gives you
half a waterfall, three cows, the hind quarter of a
ruined castle, and the sudden conviction that you have overlooked
something important; then it plunges into a tunnel and allows
you to meditate in darkness upon the advantages of civilization.

For seeing a country, I prefer a wagon, a mule,
or an infirm man with a wheelbarrow. These methods give
the landscape time to make its statement and furnish the
traveller an opportunity to object. But Switzerland has arranged her
railways with such tact that one forgives them. They climb
where common railways would pray; they turn corners where a
sensible serpent would back away; they hang over blue lakes,
creep under white precipices, and perform feats which would be
condemned as reckless if done by anybody except a government
inspector.

Interlaken lies between two lakes and among mountains of the
first respectability. It is neat, green, prosperous, and apparently washed
every morning before breakfast. The meadows are smooth enough for
billiard tables; the cottages seem to have been painted five
minutes ago; the cattle wear bells and go about advertising
themselves; and the distant heights rise in successive ranks until
the Jungfrau appears above them all, clothed in a spotless
solemnity which makes the hotels look temporary and the tourists
unnecessary.

I had seen mountains before, but they had been ordinary
business mountains—coal mountains, iron mountains, mountains which expected to be
blasted, tunnelled, taxed, and distributed among shareholders. The Jungfrau did
not suggest commerce. She seemed to belong to an elder
jurisdiction. Her upper snows rested in the sky with such
pale serenity that one could easily believe heaven had left
a gate open and that this was the shining threshold.

At sunset the white summit flushed, deepened to rose, burned
crimson, and then slowly withdrew into violet shadow. It was
a silent conflagration, an imperial fire without smoke, engines, insurance,
or a crowd of boys. In America we should have
fenced it, charged admission, and put an advertisement on the
moon to call attention to it. The Swiss merely let
it happen.

Yet the great charm of Switzerland is not wholly in
her mountains. Mountains are impressive, but politically they are unreliable.
They remain calm under any administration. A despot may tax
the valleys, imprison the patriots, suppress the newspapers, and appoint
his wife's relatives to profitable offices; the mountain continues to
wear snow and look noble, thereby giving strangers a false
notion of the country.

Switzerland has earned her scenery by her history. She is
small, but she has not always behaved so. Whenever tyranny
put its hand upon her shoulder, she did not ask
whether the hand was royal, imperial, ancient, or properly gloved.
She struck it away. The lesson is wholesome. A throne
looks large from the floor, but when a people stand
up together the throne often turns out to be a
chair with gilding on it.

One naturally thinks of William Tell. Learned men have doubted
him. Learned men doubt everything except one another's footnotes. They
have examined the apple, the arrow, the boy, the hat
upon the pole, the governor, the lake, and the general
weather conditions, and some conclude that Tell did not exist.
This is unfortunate for Tell, but it does not injure
Switzerland. If he did not exist, he should have; and
a person who ought to have existed is sometimes more
useful than nine persons who did.

The essential part of the story is not the apple.
Apples have been struck before and since, chiefly by worms.
The essential part is that a man refused to bow
to an empty hat merely because authority had ordered him
to. This is among the grand distinctions between a citizen
and a courtier. A courtier bows first and examines the
hat afterward; a citizen examines it, finds nobody inside, and
goes home.

Tell's second arrow has troubled historians. When asked why he
carried it ready, he replied that if the first arrow
had killed his son, the second was intended for the
governor. This answer is not diplomatic, but it is complete.
Modern statesmanship would require a commission, a report, three amendments,
and a regretful declaration that the governor had been misunderstood.
Tell was behind his age in parliamentary procedure and ahead
of it in aim.

But Swiss history is not all arrows, liberty, and respectable
insurrection. It has saints in it, and saints are dangerous
to a narrative, because when one admits a saint the
ordinary rules must leave by the back door. A saint
may cross a river on his cloak, converse with a
bear, borrow a cathedral bell without permission, or raise a
dead man in order to settle a boundary dispute. The
last achievement belongs to St. Fridolin, whose legend I encountered
while studying the country.

Fridolin was an energetic missionary of early times. He travelled
with the Gospel, a staff, and that calm determination which
has made many communities accept improvements they had not requested.
He converted people with success. Some listened willingly; others became
convinced after observing that he intended to remain until they
did. He was, in modern language, a religious organizer, a
field agent, and a walking committee on eternal affairs.

Among his converts was a rich landowner named Ursus. Ursus
possessed a great estate and, what is less common, the
intention of parting with some of it. He presented land
to Fridolin for pious uses. This was generous, though perhaps
unwise, because property given to a saint is apt to
acquire heavenly title while remaining subject to earthly lawsuits.

Ursus had a brother named Landolf, who was not a
saint and had no desire to encourage the profession. When
Ursus died, Landolf looked over the estate, counted the fields,
examined the woods, and discovered that the deceased had been
charitable with property which Landolf felt would have been better
employed in remaining in the family. He therefore seized the
donated lands.

Fridolin objected.

Landolf replied that no gift had been made.

Fridolin said Ursus had made it.

Landolf said Ursus was dead.

This appeared decisive. In those times a dead witness suffered
many professional disadvantages. He could not attend court, recognize a
document, withstand cross-examination, or be fined for contempt. Landolf believed
the case closed and no doubt slept soundly.

Fridolin, however, was not trained in the narrow modern view
that death terminates a man's usefulness. He went to the
grave of Ursus, had it opened, and addressed the former
proprietor.

"Ursus," he said, "thy brother disputes thy gift."

There was no answer at first. This would have satisfied
most lawyers, but Fridolin understood that the dead are deliberate.

"Rise," he said, "and come with me."

Ursus rose.

I do not know what condition he was in. Paintings
represent these occasions with too much courtesy. A man may
remain impressive after a short illness, but several years underground
will generally reduce his social advantages. The account says that
Ursus appeared as a skeleton, and this is reasonable. He
had no hat, no cloak, and very little expression, but
he had the main thing required of a witness—information.

They started for court.

I should like to have seen the journey. Fridolin walked
ahead with the confidence of a man whose evidence cannot
be contradicted without unusual courage. Ursus followed, making that dry,
unofficial music which a skeleton produces when travelling over an
imperfect road. Peasants saw them and reconsidered their plans for
the day. Dogs barked once, reflected, and retired. A tavern
keeper offered refreshment, then perceived that one customer had no
accommodations for it.

When they entered the courtroom, business was proceeding in the
usual dignified manner. The judge was trying to appear awake,
the clerk was trying to appear useful, the lawyers were
trying to appear astonished by arguments they had prepared the
night before, and Landolf was trying to look like an
injured heir. Then the door opened, and Fridolin brought in
the late owner.

The record does not tell us whether the court objected
to the witness's costume. Early courts were less strict in
such matters. Ursus was sworn, though it is difficult to
see what additional penalty perjury could have brought him. A
man already dead has reached a point where legal threats
lose much of their force.

Fridolin asked, "Didst thou give me the disputed land?"

Ursus answered that he had.

Landolf must have passed an uncomfortable moment. It is unpleasant
enough to lose a lawsuit to one's living brother, but
to be followed into court by a deceased relative and
publicly corrected is a humiliation of a special order.

Perhaps Landolf's counsel attempted cross-examination.

"Is it not true," he may have said, "that at
the time of the alleged transfer thou wert elderly, infirm,
and under the spiritual influence of this claimant?"

Ursus might have replied, "I was in better health than
now."

The court would have had to allow the answer.

The difficulty with a skeleton as a witness is not
that he is necessarily untruthful, but that his manner prejudices
the jury. He has an air of final knowledge. He
cannot blush, hesitate, perspire, or look toward his attorney for
assistance. Every statement seems to come from beyond appeal. Moreover,
the opposing party cannot accuse him of seeking personal advantage,
for there is nothing on earth left to offer him.

The land was awarded to Fridolin. Landolf abandoned his claim,
which shows that he possessed judgment after evidence had been
piled high enough. Ursus was then returned to his grave,
his public duties completed.

This legend has been called a miracle. That is not
the remarkable part. The remarkable part is that a land
case was concluded by one appearance of one witness. In
our time Ursus would have been summoned, postponed, examined, re-examined,
sent back for missing papers, recalled by the appellate court,
and kept out of his grave until he had become
impatient with immortality.

After considering Fridolin, I returned to the Jungfrau, which was
visible from my window whenever clouds, weather, or the hotel
curtains did not interfere. I studied her through several afternoons.
A mountain of that magnitude cannot be understood at a
glance. One must watch it as one watches a reserved
person—patiently, and without expecting conversation.

The snow fields looked motionless, but the shadows were continually
at work. They moved with the sun, lengthening, shrinking, joining,
separating, climbing the ridges and pouring into the ravines. They
made the mountain a theatre in which the actors were
darkness and light, the stage was miles broad, and the
performance had been running so long that even geology had
forgotten the opening night.

At four o'clock I noticed a great shadow on the
central snow. It had the appearance of a human profile—forehead,
nose, lips, and chin—enlarged beyond all proper human ambition. The
forehead covered acres. The nose might have sheltered a village.
The chin rested upon a glacier with the stern composure
of a statesman resting it upon his collar.

I went outside to make sure the hotel glass had
not introduced it. The likeness remained.

At first it was the face of an old man,
severe and contemplative. His brow leaned toward the Virgin's white
breast; his lips seemed about to disclose a secret of
the earth; his eye was a dark hollow where storms
might have been stored for centuries. He looked older than
Adam and not so well preserved.

By four-thirty the profile had altered. The nose grew more
Roman, the chin withdrew, and a pointed beard descended upon
the snow. It became the face of an emperor, philosopher,
Hebrew patriarch, or theatrical manager; the mountain did not explain
which. Nature often supplies an excellent likeness and neglects to
label it.

At 4:40 a separate blot appeared below the chin and
gradually took the shape of a shoe. It was a
large shoe. If it belonged to the head, the owner's
proportions were regrettable, but in mountain art one must allow
for perspective. A few minutes later the shoe lengthened into
a tower, complete with a dark roof and one leaning
wall. Then the roof broke away and became a bird,
or possibly an umbrella in a high wind.

At five the old man had vanished, and in his
place lay a younger profile with a small goatee. He
seemed to rest his head against the Jungfrau in an
attitude of immense familiarity.

There was something almost tender in it. The dark suitor
had climbed out of the valley and laid his cheek
upon the Virgin's bosom. He had waited all day behind
the western ridges; then, when the light softened and the
tourists went in to dinner, he came forward. He did
not speak. A lover several miles high has no need
of conversation.

But his courtship was brief. The sun descended, and the
suitor stretched thinner. His forehead became a cape, his nose
a black ravine, his beard a forest, and his mouth
an avalanche track. The Virgin remained cold and shining. By
half past five she had absorbed him almost entirely.

This is the customary result when Shadow pays court to
Snow.

At a quarter before six the remaining darkness formed a
hand with one finger extended—the familiar printer's fist which directs
attention to bargains, warnings, lost dogs, and patented medicines. The
enormous finger pointed toward the summit, as if the mountain
were calling attention to her own advertisement:

**NOTICE. ETERNAL SNOW. BEST QUALITY. NO COMPETITORS.**

At six the hand dissolved. The slopes turned gray, then
pearl, then faintly blue. A last band of fire rested
on the highest ridge and went out. The clock had
struck, though no sound came. The Jungfrau had told the
hour by changing her face.

After that I could read the mountain as one reads
a dial. At four, the ancient man; at four-thirty, the
emperor; at 4:40, the shoe and tower; at five, the
lover with the goatee; near six, the pointing hand; then
night. Hotel clocks varied, railway clocks contradicted them, and my
watch had political opinions of its own, but the mountain
kept the local time majestically.

Still, this natural clock is not convenient for business. It
operates only in clear weather, has no minute hand, and
cannot be carried to appointments. Its machinery consists of the
sun, the earth, several ridges, and distances too large to
measure before dinner. Repairs, when needed, require earthquakes.

While watching those shadows, one's idea of time changes. Four
o'clock becomes not merely an hour but a point in
a performance which has repeated itself across kingdoms, invasions, creeds,
dynasties, and guidebook editions. The same dark forehead may have
appeared before Rome had learned to conquer, before men had
taught bronze to become a sword, before the first ambitious
chief had found a stone and called it a throne.

A thousand centuries ago the shadow came. There were no
hotels to observe it, no professors to explain it incorrectly,
no painters to improve its color, no tourists to say
they had seen finer effects elsewhere. The Jungfrau received the
sunset in privacy.

And a thousand centuries hence—if the mountain remains and the
human race has not organized the sky into building lots—the
face may still return at four, the lover at five,
the pointing hand at six. Our wars, elections, boundary disputes,
coronations, and celebrated scandals will have become less than dust.
Even St. Fridolin's witness may have completed his final rest.

The mountains are useful to pride. A man looks at
one and remembers that his ancestors were late arrivals and
his descendants have uncertain reservations.

This reflection naturally brings me to kings.

It may not seem a natural transition, but few transitions
are natural when properly examined. I was reminded, while speaking
with an acquaintance in Interlaken, of an incident concerning the
King of Greece. The king had travelled incognito through part
of Europe. Kings travel incognito in order to discover what
people think of them, and people, not knowing who they
are, generally tell them about railway connections, hotel charges, and
the weather.

On this occasion the king fell into conversation with a
German-American brewer from Rochester, New York. The brewer was a
large, hearty, practical man who believed every human difficulty could
be corrected by industry, lager, and a sufficiently clear explanation
from himself.

He met the king in a railway compartment. His Majesty
wore plain clothes and had with him only one attendant,
who sat at a distance and tried not to look
employed. The brewer studied the stranger for some time. A
king out of uniform can be misleading. There is nothing
about the ordinary royal chin which proves ownership of a
country.

"European?" asked the brewer.

"Yes."

"Thought so. German?"

"No."

"Austrian?"

"No."

"Italian?"

"No."

The king said he was Greek.

The brewer brightened. "Greek! Well, now, that's interesting. I knew
a Greek in Buffalo. Kept fruit. Fine business if you
understand bananas. You in fruit?"

"No."

"Restaurant?"

"No."

The king explained that he was in government service.

"Government!" said the brewer, with immediate sympathy. "That's bad. What
branch?"

The king hesitated. "I am connected with the general administration."

The brewer nodded gravely. "General work. I see. Everything and
nothing. They use you wherever they're short. Records, inspection, correspondence,
complaints—maybe stand around when some official comes through?"

"Something of that nature."

"How long you been at it?"

"Many years."

"And no better position yet?"

"I hold the highest position available to me."

The brewer looked shocked. "Highest! And still travelling in plain
clothes? What do they pay?"

The king smiled. "The compensation is arranged by the state."

"That's what they always say when it ain't enough. Salary?"

The king mentioned a figure, perhaps hoping the size of
it would end the inquiry.

Instead, the brewer leaned back and whistled. "That sounds good
till you count expenses. You got to entertain, I suppose?"

"Frequently."

"Uniforms?"

"On formal occasions."

"Big house?"

"A rather large one."

"Servants?"

"Several."

"Horses?"

"Yes."

"Family?"

"Yes."

"How many children?"

The king told him.

The brewer struck his knee. "There! I knew it. Big
family, big house, horses, clothes, company all the time—and one
salary. My friend, you ain't highly paid. You're ruined gradual."

The king said his circumstances were considered adequate.

"Adequate! That's a government word. Adequate means you can keep
out of jail if nobody gets sick. Do they furnish
the house?"

"In a sense."

"What sense?"

"It belongs to the nation."

"Then it ain't yours. That's what I'm saying. You work
all your life, bring up your children there, repair the
roof, plant trees, and when they turn you out, what
have you got? Nothing. Not even the front steps. It's
a crime."

"I am not likely to be turned out," said the
king.

The brewer gave him a pitying look. "Everybody says that.
You think the boss can't do without you. Then one
morning there's a new administration, and the man that couldn't
be spared is carrying his own umbrella out the side
door."

"In my country," said the king, "a change can be
more complicated."

"Sure. They make it complicated so you won't understand it.
Committees, proclamations, soldiers maybe. But the result's the same—you're out."

His Majesty tried another direction. "My position is hereditary."

This did not impress the brewer favorably.

"Worse and worse. Your father had it?"

"Yes."

"His father too?"

"In effect."

"And you never got a chance to choose a trade!
That's Europe. They put a boy into the same poor
job his father had and call it tradition. What can
you do?"

The king considered. "I speak several languages."

"Clerk work. Overcrowded."

"I have knowledge of military affairs."

"Any war on?"

"Not at present."

"Then that don't pay."

"I understand constitutional questions."

"Neither does anybody else. Anything with your hands?"

The king admitted that he had no manual trade.

The brewer sighed deeply. "A grown man, a wife, children,
servants eating their heads off, and you can't cooper a
barrel, run an engine, set type, keep books, or brew.
My friend, I don't blame you. I blame the system."

The king's attendant turned toward the window, but his shoulders
became agitated.

The brewer continued: "What time do you start mornings?"

"The demands vary."

"That means all hours. When do you quit?"

"Often late."

"Sunday work?"

"Sometimes."

"No union?"

"No."

"No overtime?"

"No."

"No share in the business?"

"The country is not exactly a business."

"Everything's a business when men work and somebody takes the
money. Who owns it?"

"The people."

"Then get your share from them."

"I receive a civil list."

"A what?"

The king explained it.

The brewer frowned. "Sounds like piecework without the pieces. Listen—you
come to America."

His Majesty appeared surprised. "To America?"

"Rochester. Good town. Solid. Snow in winter, but no earthquakes
worth mentioning. I'll give you a start."

"That is extremely kind."

"Kind nothing. I can use a man who knows languages.
We got Germans, Poles, Italians, Irish, and three men nobody
has identified yet. You start in the shipping room. Not
much money at first—I won't deceive you—but regular. You show
sense, you go up."

The king said he feared he was too old to
begin.

"Too old! Nonsense. You're what—fifty?"

"Nearly."

"Best age, if a man quits fooling. First six months
you learn the business. Then maybe foreman. After that assistant
superintendent. You save. Your boys come in when they're ready.
No inherited jobs—they learn from the bottom. Your girls get
good schooling. Your wife won't need all those servants. One
girl, maybe two when company comes."

"I think the queen—my wife—has become accustomed to her household."

"Queen?"

"It is a family name," said the king hastily.

"Fine name. She'll adapt. Women have more sense than men
about money once they see the figures. Sell the horses.
Get a modest place near the streetcar. Garden in back.
In ten years you own it clear."

"I am afraid there would be diplomatic objections."

"Diplomatic! That's another government word. Who objects?"

"My ministers, for one."

"Your ministers? Church people?"

"No, officials."

"Let 'em object. Are they feeding your children? Are they
paying for that big house? No. They like you where
you are because you do the work. You owe them
nothing."

"My departure might create a crisis."

The brewer laughed. "Every foreman thinks that. The brewery runs
when I'm in Chicago. Greece will run while you're in
Rochester."

"I should hope so," said the king.

"Now you're talking. You write me when you get home.
I'll send the passage money if you need it."

"That will not be necessary."

"Pride! Don't let pride ruin you. I was poor myself.
Came over with twelve dollars and a cheese. First job
I washed bottles. Today I employ eighty-six men and the
cheese is paid for."

The train began slowing. The attendant rose and approached.

"Your Majesty," he said, "we have arrived."

The brewer stared at him, then at the king.

"Majesty?"

The king stood. "I fear I have not been entirely
clear. I am the King of Greece."

For once the brewer had nothing to say. This condition
lasted perhaps four seconds.

Then he seized the king's hand.

"King! Well, that explains the house and horses. But it
don't change the facts. It's hereditary, no security, bad hours,
political interference, and no property of your own. You think
it over. My offer stands."

The king thanked him with genuine warmth.

"And don't wait too long," the brewer called as His
Majesty stepped onto the platform. "A man can't be king
forever. Come to Rochester and I'll make a man of
you."

The train moved on.

I have always admired that brewer. He looked upon royalty
and saw not a throne, a dynasty, an anointed head,
or the purple shadow of history, but an undertrained foreigner
trapped in an insecure position with too many dependents and
no useful trade. This was democratic vision in its purest
form.

And perhaps he was right. Before the Jungfrau, kings and
brewers are of one size. Their titles melt, their salaries
cease, their houses pass to others, and their proudest affairs
become small noises in a valley. The great white mountain
keeps her place. At four the old face comes; at
five the dark lover bends toward the snow; at six
the enormous finger points upward.

Then the light goes out, and all our majesties are
incognito.

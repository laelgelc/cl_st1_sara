Lucy Welch lay diagonally across the black silk sheets as
if she had tried to leave the bed in two
directions at once.

Her nightgown was pale blue, modest except where blood had
soaked it black. The cut in her throat was neat,
deep, and almost professional. There was no weapon in the
bedroom, no overturned lamp, no fingernail scratches on her arms.

Detective Lawrence Peyton stood at the foot of the bed
and looked at her bare feet.

"Toenails painted," he said. "Nightgown buttoned to the chin. Somebody
was expected."

His son Jimmy kept his eyes on the wound. "Or
somebody wasn't feared."

Lawrence's cloud-gray crew cut turned toward him.

"Same thing, maybe. Don't jump."

"I didn't jump."

"You bent your knees."

Jimmy was thirty-two, broad through the shoulders, blond, blue-eyed, and
handsome in the heavy, uncomplicated way department photographers liked. Inside,
he remained a fat little boy in a blond, blue-eyed
hunk disguise. His father could find that boy with one
sentence.

A lab technician dusted the mirrored dresser. The room was
overdecorated in white lacquer, gold trim, and satin bows, like
a bordello designed by a nervous virgin. Perfume bottles stood
in military ranks. A framed wedding photograph had been placed
facedown beside the bed.

Jimmy lifted it with a handkerchief.

Lucy smiled from behind a veil. The groom had narrow
shoulders, thinning dark hair, and the watchful expression of a
man who suspected the photographer of infidelity.

"George Welch," Jimmy said.

"Estranged husband," Lawrence said. "Separated eight months. Lives in Grosse
Pointe Park. Says they were discussing reconciliation."

"Who found her?"

"Neighbor. Came over at eight-thirty. They were supposed to go
to a ceramics class."

The medical examiner, Dr. Eisen, straightened from the body. His
knees cracked.

"Dead between eleven and one. One stroke, left to right.
Killer probably right-handed. No recent intercourse. No major defensive injuries."

"Probably knew him," Jimmy said.

Eisen shrugged. "Or she was asleep."

"She wasn't dressed for sleep," Lawrence said.

Eisen looked at the nightgown. "Detective, some people own more
formal pajamas than you do."

Lawrence wore a brown suit that appeared to have been
upholstered around him in 1968.

A patrolman called from the living room. "Lieutenant? There's a
guy downstairs yelling he oughta be allowed up."

Lawrence sighed. "Husband?"

"No. Eric Dimke."

The name was in the neighbor's first statement. Dimke had
visited Lucy often, loudly, and sometimes with bruised knuckles.

They found him on the sidewalk arguing with two uniforms.
He was six feet four and built like a great
bronzed ape someone had taught to wear a leather vest.
His black hair curled over his collar. A white scar
split one eyebrow, giving him a permanent look of punctuation.

"You cops got no right," Dimke said.

"Wonderful opening," Lawrence said. "Save it for the movie. What
did you have to Lucy Welch?"

Dimke glanced at the bedroom window.

"What do you mean, had?"

"She's dead."

The words hit him visibly. His mouth opened. His face
went slack, and for half a second he looked less
like an ape than a child wearing one.

Then the fists came back.

"George," he said.

"George what?"

"George did it."

"Where were you last night?"

"At the Raven."

"Till when?"

"Closing."

"Witnesses?"

"A hundred."

"Any sober?"

Dimke smiled without humor. "No."

Lawrence stepped close enough to smell his aftershave. "You ever
hit Lucy?"

Dimke's jaw moved.

"That's not a difficult sentence," Lawrence said. "Even for you."

"She'd get wild."

Jimmy watched his father's face go still.

"So you hit her."

"I held her down. Sometimes."

"With your fist?"

"She'd scratch. Throw things."

"That sounds exhausting. Murder quieter?"

Dimke lunged one inch before the uniforms pinned his arms.
It was not much of a lunge, but Lawrence gave
Jimmy a brief wink.

Visible danger. Easy danger. Jimmy felt the wink as if
someone had handed him a medal.

At headquarters, George Welch waited in an interview room with
his hands folded around a paper cup. He sold commercial
insurance and looked like a man who had been born
apologizing for the premium. His suit was charcoal, his tie
gray, his face colorless except for two inflamed patches beside
his nose.

He had cried. Or rubbed his eyes hard enough to
imitate it.

"I left her at ten-fifteen," he said. "Perhaps ten-thirty."

"Why were you there?" Lawrence asked.

"We were talking."

"About reconciliation?"

"Yes."

"Did she want that?"

George stared into the coffee. "She was uncertain."

"Meaning no."

"Meaning uncertain."

Jimmy placed the wedding photograph on the table.

"We found this facedown."

"She did that when she was angry."

"Were you angry?"

"No."

"Was she?"

George's fingers tightened around the cup. "Lucy enjoyed provoking people."

"Dimke?" Lawrence said.

George looked up. "That animal. You know about him?"

"We know he has an eyebrow."

"He frightened her."

"He hit her."

"He did more than that."

"Such as?"

"I don't know. She wouldn't say."

Lawrence leaned back. "You told the uniform you hadn't been
in Lucy's bedroom since the separation."

"That's right."

"You also said you used the upstairs bathroom last night."

George blinked.

"The bathroom's through the bedroom," Jimmy said.

"I meant before last night."

"No, you didn't," Lawrence said.

George put down the cup. "I may have been confused.
My wife is dead."

"Estranged wife."

"My wife."

The words came up like fists, startling in that mild
face.

Jimmy felt something then. Not evidence. A tug in the
machinery.

Lawrence saw him feel it and frowned.

Outside the interview room, he said, "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You've got your conclusion face."

"What does that look like?"

"Constipated."

"You liked Dimke."

"I like steak. Doesn't mean I marry the cow."

Jimmy glanced through the glass. George sat with both hands
flat on the table, studying them as if they had
submitted a report.

"He lied."

"They all lie. Widowers, wives, priests, dogs if they could
talk. Lying isn't a blood test."

A secretary hurried down the corridor carrying a clear evidence
sleeve. "This came to the Free Press this morning."

The envelope had been postmarked downtown before midnight.

Inside was a single sheet of cream paper. The message
had been typed in black capitals:

LUCY WELCH HAS LEARNED THAT LOVE REFUSED IS DEATH ACCEPTED.

YOU HAVE FORGOTTEN ME.

MEPHISTOPHELES

Lawrence read it twice.

The corridor seemed to tip under him.

Seven years vanished.

He was back in a duplex near Wayne State, looking
at Nancy Faber, twenty-six, dead on a yellow kitchen floor.
Her throat had been cut. She had one shoe on.
The other lay beneath the table beside a spilled bowl
of cereal.

Three days later the letter came.

NANCY FABER MOCKED THE DEVIL AND FOUND HIM WITHOUT MERCY.

MEPHISTOPHELES

Newspapers loved the name. They printed it in type large
enough to make a petty man feel biblical.

Then came Carol Givens in an apartment off Woodward, strangled
first, cut afterward. A waitress who had laughed too loudly
and trusted men with motorcycles. Then Jean Palowski in Hamtramck,
found dressed for a date that no one admitted arranging.
Then Donna Reece across the river in Windsor, because the
devil, apparently, had a passport.

Five women in fourteen months.

All had been approached in private. All had been cut
at the throat. All had received attention from men their
friends remembered poorly: a customer, a fellow student, a salesman,
a man at a bus stop. The descriptions contradicted one
another. Dark hair. Light hair. Glasses. No glasses. Tall enough.
Not tall.

Then the killings stopped.

No arrest. No confession. No body pulled from the river
with a knife and stationery in its pockets. Just silence.

Lawrence had spent two years waiting for the next letter.
His wife said he woke at night speaking the victims'
names. Jimmy, then twenty-five and still in uniform, remembered his
father at the kitchen table surrounded by photographs, muttering about
timing, geography, and women who made bad choices.

The last phrase belonged to Lawrence. The women had already
paid for their choices, but he had charged them interest.

Now he stood beneath fluorescent lights with the new letter
trembling almost invisibly in his hand.

Jimmy saw it.

"You okay?"

Lawrence gave him the paper. "Get the old files."

"I've already called records."

"Then call again. They're records. Movement scares them."

By noon the conference room was full of gray boxes
and dead women.

The photographs were worse than Jimmy expected. Old blood developed
brown at the edges. Rooms changed fashions while death stayed
current: shag carpet, beanbag chairs, macramé owls, chrome lamps, a
water bed collapsed under Carol Givens like a punctured animal.

Lawrence moved among them without notes.

"Nancy Faber was first. Worked at a bookstore, then a
medical supply company. Carol Givens waited tables downtown. Jean Palowski
did payroll for an auto-parts shop. Donna Reece taught piano.
Last was Marlene Voss, student at Oakland University."

"You remember all that?"

"I remember what they ate."

Jimmy looked at him.

Lawrence touched Nancy's photograph. "Cereal. Carol had onion rings. Jean
ate canned peaches. Donna had tea. Marlene didn't eat."

His voice remained flat, but his finger rested too long
on the glossy border.

Captain Ortiz came in, read the letter, and swore quietly.

"Press know it's genuine?"

"Enough details match," Lawrence said. "Phrase construction. Paper. Typewriter might
be different, but seven years is a long time to
keep office equipment."

Ortiz looked at Jimmy. "Suspects?"

"George Welch lied about being in the bedroom," Jimmy said.
"Eric Dimke beat her."

"Excellent. A liar and a gorilla. Detroit's narrowed to forty
thousand."

Lawrence almost smiled.

They questioned Dimke for three hours.

He admitted striking Lucy twice. Then four times. Then "not
often enough to call it a thing." His bartender confirmed
he had been at the Raven from nine until two,
except for twenty minutes around midnight.

"Twenty minutes," Jimmy said.

"Bar to Lucy's place is twelve each way," Lawrence said.

"He drives fast."

"He drives a van held together with bumper stickers. It
couldn't murder anybody without roadside assistance."

Dimke had once owned a hunting knife. He had lost
it. He despised George. He knew Lucy's doors and habits.
His alibi was ugly but crowded.

George's alibi was prettier and empty.

He said he left Lucy, drove along Jefferson, stopped for
gas, and went home. No receipt. No clerk remembered him.
His house was expensive and coldly neat, with beige furniture
no human body had dented. The bed was made tightly
enough to bounce a confession.

No knife. No blood. No old letters. No typewriter.

In the garage, Jimmy found a weighted exercise chain in
a box of obsolete equipment.

Lawrence glanced at it. "Millions sold."

"Still."

"Still nothing."

At six, Jimmy went to Dr. Whitney Larsen's office in
the Renaissance Center.

Larsen was a psychiatrist with a thin, vinegary face, red
suspenders, and hair arranged in three ambitious strands across his
skull. His degrees occupied one wall. Another displayed framed posters
from old detective movies. Humphrey Bogart aimed a gun over
the couch.

"Lie down," Larsen said. "Bogart likes a clear shot."

Jimmy sat.

"Serial killer today?"

"How'd you know?"

"It's on television. Also, you have the expression you wear
when murder makes you feel useful."

Jimmy loosened his tie. Beyond the window, Windsor floated in
evening haze.

"My father worked the original case."

"Ah. The dead women have resurrected Dad's unfinished symphony."

"He remembers every detail."

"And you would like to finish it for him."

"I'd like to catch the killer."

"Same sentence, with public funding."

Jimmy looked at the Bogart poster. "You know what bothers
me?"

"Your hourly rate?"

"Women like Lucy."

"Dead ones?"

"Beautiful ones. Women who know it."

Larsen steepled his fingers. "That narrowed your discomfort to most
of recorded art."

"They make me angry."

"Because?"

"They decide who gets close. They can make a man
ridiculous."

"And you never make women ridiculous?"

"I don't kill them."

"A stirring minimum standard."

Jimmy rubbed his palms against his trousers. "Sometimes I imagine
hurting them."

"Women?"

"Women who laugh. Who look through me. Not actually doing
it. Just—images."

Larsen nodded. "Most people's minds are slums. The question is
whether you rent there or buy."

"You always make jokes."

"You always bring me material."

Jimmy told him about George Welch: the mildness, the lie,
the bedroom, the marriage photograph.

Larsen swiveled toward the window.

"What did Lucy wear?"

"A nightgown."

"Sexy?"

"Buttoned up."

"So she expected access without seduction. Husband access. Familiar access."

"Maybe."

"And when did George meet Lucy?"

"Eight years ago. Married seven years ago."

Larsen turned back. "When did your theatrical demon stop?"

"Seven years ago."

Jimmy sat very still.

Larsen raised one finger. "That is not deduction. It is
arithmetic wearing a cape."

"Why start again?"

"Marriage anesthetized him. Or Lucy did. Then she rejected him,
the drug wore off, and Mephistopheles climbed out of the
basement demanding stationery."

"You're saying George killed all of them."

"I'm saying George lied, could enter Lucy's bedroom without alarming
her, appeared at exactly the point your killer vanished, and
has the emotional warmth of a parking meter. You brought
me facts. I shuffled them."

"Dad thinks I jump."

"Your father jumps constantly. He simply calls it veteran instinct
when he lands."

Jimmy smiled despite himself.

"Find out where George was before Lucy," Larsen said. "Men
reinvent themselves, but yearbooks are merciless."

The next morning Jimmy requested records from George's high school,
community college, former employers, and licensing bureau.

Lawrence watched from the adjacent desk.

"What are you doing?"

"Checking chronology."

"You seeing that head doctor again?"

"He's a psychiatrist."

"That's a head doctor with carpeting."

Jimmy told him Larsen's theory.

Lawrence listened without interruption. When Jimmy finished, he said, "Could
be."

Praise entered Jimmy like warm liquor.

Then Lawrence added, "Could also be twenty pounds of horse
manure in a ten-pound theory."

The warmth went out.

"George moved to Detroit nine years ago," Jimmy said. "First
victim worked near his first job. He met Lucy shortly
before the last murder. Then nothing."

"Coincidence."

"Maybe."

"Maybe isn't a warrant."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Jimmy felt himself become twenty-five again, then fifteen, then eight,
a set of collapsing dolls.

The yearbook arrived from a community college outside Toledo. George
Welch appeared in a business-club photograph, twenty-three years old, heavier,
with thick hair and horn-rimmed glasses. The face was softer,
but the eyes were the same: fixed, patient, already offended.

Jimmy copied the photograph.

Nancy Faber's mother lived in Dearborn with plastic runners over
the carpet. She studied the picture through bifocals.

"No."

"Take your time."

"I have. I said no."

At the door she called him back.

"Maybe without the glasses."

Jimmy returned.

She covered the glasses with one finger. "There was a
man at Nancy's funeral. Stood across the street. I thought
he worked with her."

"Did Nancy mention him?"

"She mentioned lots of men. Men always mistook friendliness for
a down payment."

Carol Givens's former roommate recognized nothing. Jean Palowski's sister thought
George looked familiar, then admitted all men in old photographs
looked familiar because they were thin and unhappy.

At the medical supply company where Nancy had worked, Jimmy
found a retired supervisor named Frank DeSoto helping his son
inventory wheelchairs.

DeSoto had a blunt white mustache and the suspicious eyes
of a man who had caught generations stealing tongue depressors.

Jimmy showed him the photograph.

"That son of a bitch," DeSoto said.

"You know him?"

"Called himself George Wells. Temporary shipping clerk. Followed Nancy around.
Left candy on her desk, letters in her coat. She
told him to stop."

"What happened?"

"She laughed at him in the break room. Not cruel,
exactly. Nancy laughed when nervous. He grabbed her arm. I
fired him."

"When?"

DeSoto checked the date beneath the photograph.

"Week before she died."

Jimmy felt the floor open like a thousand-foot cliff, but
this time he did not jump. He stepped carefully.

"Did you tell police?"

"Nobody asked about him. We had fifty temporary workers. I
didn't connect it till later, and by then I couldn't
remember his name."

"Would you make a statement?"

"I just did."

"Another one, with worse coffee."

Jimmy called Lawrence.

There was a pause after he explained.

Then his father said, "Good work."

No qualification.

Jimmy closed his eyes.

"Bring DeSoto in," Lawrence said. "We'll get surveillance on Welch.
Quietly. Don't spook him."

They reached George's office at three. He had left early.

His secretary, a narrow woman with pink eyeglasses, said he
had a dental appointment.

The dentist had not seen George in six months.

At George's house, his car was gone.

Lawrence swore. "Known associates?"

"Dimke," Jimmy said.

"Don't be cute."

Jimmy searched the recent interview notes. Lucy's neighbor had mentioned
a woman twice: Patti Bukowski, a friend from ceramics class.
George had telephoned Lucy while Patti was visiting. There had
been an argument. George accused Patti of turning Lucy against
him.

Patti lived in Madison Heights.

No answer.

Her husband, reached at an auto plant, said they had
been separated for four months. Patti had recently decided to
return to him.

"Recently when?"

"Last night. She called. Said she'd made mistakes."

"Was she seeing anybody?"

A silence.

"Some insurance guy. Older than her. She said he was
gentle."

George sat in his car across from Patti Bukowski's apartment
building.

Rain stippled the windshield. He watched the amber rectangle of
her kitchen window on the second floor.

He had first met Patti through Lucy. Patti had touched
his sleeve when she laughed. She listened. She said women
like Lucy did not appreciate decent men.

Then Lucy was gone, and Patti had become frightened.

That was the pattern. Women begged for decency until it
asked something in return. Then they called it pressure.

A figure crossed Patti's window. She was wearing the cream
robe he liked.

George opened the glove compartment.

The knife lay inside a folded towel. Beside it was
the typed note.

PATTI BUKOWSKI HAS CHOSEN BETRAYAL AND RECEIVED ITS WAGE.

MEPHISTOPHELES

He disliked the sentence. "Wage" sounded biblical but singular. "Wages"
was the phrase. Yet "received its wages" sounded like a
payroll error.

There was never enough time for art.

He put the note in his coat and slipped the
weighted chain into his pocket. The chain was insurance. Knives
could be theatrical. Women were less theatrical after one hard
blow.

Patti had left the outer door unlocked.

She was packing a suitcase when George entered.

Her apartment smelled of coffee and hair spray. A game
show chattered in the living room, contestants applauding a refrigerator
as if civilization had reached its summit.

She turned.

"George."

"You didn't answer my calls."

"I told you. I need space."

"You're going back to him."

Patti tightened the robe at her waist. She was thirty-eight,
soft-faced, carefully blond, with a small mouth made smaller by
fear.

"He's my husband."

"He struck you."

"Once."

"You said once was enough."

"I say things when I'm upset."

George smiled. He had heard that sentence before, in different
rooms, from different mouths.

"I was good to you."

"I know."

"Better than him."

"This isn't a contest."

Everything was a contest. Women invented the rules, changed them
at halftime, then complained when a man carried a weapon
onto the field.

"You used me," he said.

"No."

"You and Lucy. You laughed."

"George, please leave."

She stepped toward the telephone.

He took out the chain.

Patti stopped.

The links were wrapped at one end with black tape.
The weight on the other was a metal cylinder from
exercise equipment. Ridiculous object. Ridiculous until it moved.

"What's that?"

"Something to help you listen."

Her face changed. George saw recognition enter it, not of
him exactly, but of all the warnings she had ignored
because he wore a tie.

She ran for the bedroom.

He caught the back of her robe. The fabric tore.
She screamed and twisted free, knocking over a lamp.

George raised the chain.

The apartment door burst inward.

Jimmy hit him low and hard. They crashed into the
coffee table, scattering magazines and a ceramic ashtray shaped like
Florida.

George swung the chain. The weighted end struck Jimmy's shoulder.
Pain flashed white down his arm.

He grabbed George's wrist.

George was stronger than he looked. Mild men saved their
strength. They kept it in locked rooms with their grievances.

The knife appeared between them.

Jimmy saw blood on the blade.

For one impossible second he wondered whose.

Then Lawrence struck George's forearm with his revolver. The knife
dropped. Jimmy drove George against the wall and heard air
leave him in a wet grunt.

Two uniforms rushed in behind Lawrence.

They cuffed George facedown on the carpet.

Patti stood by the bedroom door clutching the torn robe
closed. She looked at the knife, then at George.

"Is that Lucy's blood?"

George turned his head.

"She made me do it."

Lawrence stared down at him. "They always do."

The words were cold enough to belong to either man.

In George's pocket they found the note. In his trunk
they found gloves, plastic sheeting, rope, a spare shirt, and
a box containing clippings about the original murders.

The knife carried traces of Lucy's blood beneath the fresh
smear from George's palm. The typewriter in his office matched
the new letters. Old letters could not be conclusively matched,
but DeSoto identified him, Lucy's mother remembered the funeral watcher,
and a storage locker produced souvenirs: earrings, photographs, a student
identification card belonging to Marlene Voss.

Mephistopheles had rented locker 214 under his own initials and
paid six months in advance.

The devil, Lawrence said, respected late fees.

George confessed selectively. Nancy had humiliated him. Carol had promised
to meet him and arrived with another man. Jean had
called him pathetic. Donna accepted his gifts but refused him.
Marlene kissed him once and later claimed it meant nothing.

Then came Lucy.

Lucy had needed him. She admired his patience, his courtesy,
his plans. He stopped because he had finally been chosen.

Marriage deteriorated. Lucy grew bored. She mocked his clothes, his
lovemaking, his silences. She took lovers. Dimke had been one,
though Dimke denied it with the wounded dignity of a
man whose standards had been insulted.

When Lucy demanded a divorce, George visited her bedroom one
last time.

She let him in because he was only George.

The city celebrated for forty-eight hours. Reporters surrounded Lawrence on
the courthouse steps. He gave credit to the task force,
the laboratory, "persistent follow-up," and luck.

Jimmy stood slightly behind him, where sons often stood in
family photographs and public victories.

A television reporter asked, "Lieutenant, did you ever believe Mephistopheles
would return?"

Lawrence looked into the camera.

"I believed he was never much of a devil."

That line ran on the evening news.

Two days later, Jimmy returned to Larsen's office with his
arm in a sling.

Larsen pointed at it. "Fashion or gunplay?"

"Weighted chain."

"Of course. Guns have become bourgeois."

Jimmy sat beneath Bogart.

"They charged him with six murders. Windsor may file separately.
Dad's getting a commendation."

"And you?"

"I caught him."

"You sound surprised."

"I followed the pattern. DeSoto identified him. I figured Patti
was next."

"You jumped to a conclusion."

"I checked the ground first."

Larsen smiled. "Your father say good work?"

"Yes."

There it was. The real trophy. Polished daily.

Jimmy described the arrest, the chain, the knife, the note.
He explained how George stopped killing when Lucy accepted him
and began again when she rejected him. Larsen listened with
his eyes half closed, either concentrating or conserving electricity.

"So," Jimmy said, "you were right."

"Please don't spread that around. Expectations ruin a practice."

"You saw the marriage connection."

"I made a good guess."

"You always say that."

"Because genius is mostly good guesses after the embarrassing ones
are buried."

Jimmy looked out at the river. A freighter moved slowly
through the gray, carrying ore or coal or something else
too heavy to admit it needed water.

"George said women made him feel invisible."

Larsen said nothing.

"He said they decided whether he mattered."

Still nothing.

Jimmy shifted in the chair. "I'm not like him."

"I didn't say you were."

"I have thoughts. Everybody has thoughts."

"Yes."

"I don't act on them."

"Yes."

"I save people."

"Sometimes."

Jimmy turned from the window. "What's that supposed to mean?"

Larsen's thin face had lost its usual amusement.

"You were obsessed before the evidence was solid. You needed
Welch to be the killer even when your father told
you not to jump. You needed to understand why he
hated women who refused him. You understood rather quickly."

"That helped catch him."

"It did."

"So what's the problem?"

"No problem. A question."

Jimmy waited.

Outside, the river carried the freighter south. The city reflected
in the office glass, all towers and old smoke, looking
cleaner in reflection than it had any right to.

Larsen leaned forward.

"How much of yourself did you see in George Welch?"

Jimmy opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

For once, no one supplied him with the answer.

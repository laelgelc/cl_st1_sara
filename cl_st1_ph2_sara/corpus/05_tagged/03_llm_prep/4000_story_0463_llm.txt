You may shut the window if the draught troubles you,
sir. It does not trouble me. At eighty-two a body
has more cold within than any March wind can bring
from outside. There—set your chair by the grate, though the
fire's low. I cannot abide a high fire. You will
know why before I have done.

You asked whether I am afraid to die.

I am not afraid of dying. It is what comes
after that has kept me waking these fifty-eight years.

Do not begin with mercy yet. You young clergymen carry
mercy about as a peddler carries ribbons, ready measured for
every woman that whimpers. First hear what I did. Then
tell me, if you dare, that the gate of heaven
stands as wide for me as for a child that
never learned hatred.

My name you know: Isabel Rooke, of Whitecroft. Though there
is no Whitecroft now, save three black stones in a
sheep-field and an elder bush grown up where the dairy
stood. I was born there, and my cousin Lilian came
to us when her mother died. She was six and
I was eight, and from that time till we were
women we ate from one dish, slept in one room
and wore one another's frocks, altered at the waist according
as she was slender and I was not.

I loved her. Remember that.

You look doubtful. That is because your religion makes tidy
parcels of the soul: love in one, murder in another,
tied separate with string. But the heart is no cupboard
with neat shelves. I loved Lilian when I struck the
match. I hated her when I ran into the flames
to save her. Both are true. If you cannot hold
the two truths at once, you cannot understand me, and
may as well go home to your supper.

Our uncle Josiah owned Whitecroft and four hundred acres upon
the Downs. He was a hard man, straight-backed and spare,
with grey whiskers and a voice like a gate slammed
in frost. Yet he fed us, taught us, and meant
to leave the farm between us. His kindness wore hobnailed
boots, but it was kindness all the same.

Whitecroft stood high, without a tree near enough to shelter
it. In winter the wind came over the hill with
nothing to break its teeth. It shook the bedroom latches
and pushed soot down the chimneys and made the rafters
groan above us. Below, in Felscombe, the lamps showed warm
and yellow, and folk there had hedges and neighbours. We
had sky, chalk, sheep and weather.

Lilian loved the place only in summer. I loved it
always. I loved the red tiles, the sour smell of
the cheese-room, the kitchen floor Uncle had us scatter with
sand, the blue china hens on the dresser, even the
pump that froze every January. It was my body outside
my body. When Whitecroft burned, part of me burned because
it had chosen to.

Yes, sir. Chosen.

Lilian was pretty in a pale fashion. Her hair was
brown but shone gold at the edges; her eyes were
the colour of rainwater lying on flint. Men saw softness
in her, though I knew she could be stubborn as
couch-grass. She had little hands, always busy with some foolish
fine work—hemming linen, painting flowers on cards, putting lavender into
muslin bags. She drank from a cup painted with blue
cornflowers and would rinse it herself for fear the maid
should chip it.

I was not pretty. There is no virtue in saying
otherwise. I was broad in the face and heavy at
the shoulder, and my hands belonged to the farm. I
could lift a sack, turn a lamb, mend a hurdle
and walk to Felscombe in rain without complaining. Uncle said
I should have been born his son. It pleased me
then. Afterwards I understood it meant no man would look
at me while Lilian sat by.

Edgar Linley looked at me first.

It was in the great storm of October. You have
heard old men speak of it perhaps—the night three beeches
came down by Harrow Lane and the church weathercock was
found in a pond two miles away. Our maid had
gone to her mother's, and Uncle was at market in
Lewes, unable to return. Lilian and me were alone, with
the shutters barred and every candle trembling though the windows
were shut.

Near ten o'clock there came a pounding at the back
door.

Lilian caught my sleeve. "Do not open it, Isabel."

"It may be someone hurt," I said.

"It may be thieves."

"Then thieves have chosen a poor night for visiting."

I took the stable lantern and went through the wash-house.
When I unbarred the door, the wind tore it from
my hand, and in fell a man on one knee,
wet through, bareheaded and bleeding above the temple. I saw
his face when the lantern swung back. It was thin
and dark, with a mouth that looked proud even in
pain.

"My horse threw me," he said. "I saw your light."

Then he fainted across the threshold.

We dragged him in, Lilian crying out every time the
house shook, and laid him on the settle. I washed
the cut. My fingers were steady. His eyes opened while
I was holding a cloth to his brow, and they
fixed on mine.

"You have saved me," he said.

A common thing to say. He might have said it
to any washerwoman. Yet I carried those four words in
me like a secret jewel.

His name was Edgar Linley. He painted pictures—landscapes chiefly, and
portraits when he required money. He had been travelling to
see the old chalk pits under Cissbury, thinking to paint
them by moonlight, which tells you at once he had
more imagination than caution. His ankle was badly sprained, and
the storm had bruised him cruelly, so when Uncle returned
next morning he agreed Edgar must remain until he could
ride.

That was how the devil entered Whitecroft. Not with horns
and a stink of brimstone, but limping, with a bandage
round his head and gratitude in his eyes.

Do not rebuke me for calling him devil. Edgar was
not wicked. If there was a devil in it, he
found his lodging in me.

For a fortnight Edgar sat by the kitchen hearth with
his ankle on a stool. He drew everything: Uncle asleep
over the newspaper, the dog nosing at a pail, Lilian
bending over her sewing. He drew me once as I
kneaded bread. I did not know till he showed it
me.

"You have made me ugly," I said.

"No," he answered. "I have made you strong."

That was worse, though I smiled. A woman does not
wish the man she loves to praise her as he
would praise a cart-horse.

Yes, I loved him by then. Do not ask when
it began. Love does not knock and wait for permission.
It comes up through the boards like smoke. One day
the room is clear; next day your eyes sting and
you cannot breathe.

I kept it hidden. I thought concealment noble. I carried
his meals, warmed bricks for his bed, found him old
boards to paint upon. He talked to me of London
streets shining after rain, of Italy, which he had never
seen, of colours with names finer than jewels. I listened
and believed the hunger in his voice belonged partly to
me.

Then his ankle mended.

He walked out with Lilian.

At first I told myself he went because she had
leisure and I had work. Then I saw them coming
over the east field at sunset, his head bent toward
hers, her checked shawl drawn over both their shoulders against
the rain. It was a small sight. No lightning split
the sky. No angel spoke. Yet my life divided at
that moment into before and after.

Lilian told me of their engagement three weeks later.

She came to our room while I was brushing my
hair. I can see her behind me in the glass,
twisting the ring he had given her—a little silver thing
with a green stone.

"Isabel," she said, "you will be glad for me."

I knew before she spoke another word.

I laid down the brush. "Why should I be glad?"

She stared. Poor Lilian! She had expected me to kiss
her and ask a hundred questions. She had not guessed.
She never guessed anything that hurt until it had bitten
her.

"Edgar and I are to marry."

"Is that settled?"

"Yes."

"With Uncle's leave?"

"Edgar will ask him tomorrow."

"And if Uncle refuses?"

Her face changed, but only a little. "Why should he?"

I laughed. I am ashamed of the sound even now.
"Because Edgar has no land, no profession Uncle respects, and
three shirts to his name."

"He has genius."

"Genius will not pay the butcher."

"You are cruel."

"I am practical."

Then she came near and put her arms about me.
I stood stiff as a post.

"You do not like him," she whispered.

I could have told her. One sentence, and all might
have altered. Not happily perhaps, but honestly. Instead I said,
"I like him well enough."

Well enough! I had lain awake imagining his hand in
mine. I had kept the rag stained with his blood
from the night he came. It was hidden then in
my Bible, between the Psalms and the Song of Solomon.
A holy bookmark, was it not?

Uncle refused them, as I knew he would. At first
he did so without rage. He told Edgar to make
his way in the world and return in two years
with proof he could support a wife. Edgar, proud fool,
took it as insult. Lilian wept. There were quarrels at
meals and meetings by the lower gate, and letters carried
by a boy from Felscombe who thought no one saw
him.

Then the stories came.

A woman in Brighton said Edgar had promised marriage to
her. Another said he had taken money and left debts
behind. I never knew the whole truth. Perhaps there was
some truth and much malice; perhaps none. Edgar would not
defend himself clearly.

Uncle called him into the parlour on a morning so
cold the frost made white feathers inside the windowpanes. Lilian
and I stood beyond the door. I confess I listened.

"You shall not darken my threshold again," Uncle said. "A
man who ruins a woman and hides behind silence is
not fit company for my girls."

"I have ruined no one."

"Then name the liar."

"I will not drag a woman's name through a village
to clean my own."

"That sounds honourable and proves nothing."

"It must serve."

Uncle struck the table. "If Lilian marries you, she shall
not have one penny from me. Not at my death,
not before it. I will leave her portion to Isabel,
who at least has sense and duty."

There. He had thrown money into the wound, though he
did not know it.

Edgar came out white about the mouth. He saw us,
and his gaze went first to Lilian. Always to Lilian.

"I am forbidden the house," he said.

She reached for him, but Uncle followed and ordered him
away. Edgar took his coat and went into the bitter
morning without breakfast. I watched him cross the yard. Once
he turned. I thought—yes, even then I thought—he might look
at me.

He looked at her.

After that Uncle watched Lilian closely. He locked the stable
at night and kept the postbag key in his waistcoat.
Lilian became quiet. She moved about the house with red
eyelids and a secret in every gesture. I was angry
with Edgar for making her suffer, angry with Uncle for
forbidding him, angry with Lilian for being loved, angry with
God for shaping my face as He had.

And beneath all that anger was hope.

It disgusts you? Good. Let it. I hoped she would
yield. I hoped Edgar would tire of waiting. I hoped
poverty would frighten one of them, or pride divide them,
or the rumours prove true. I smiled at Lilian and
shared her room and heard her crying beneath the blankets,
and hope sat warm in me like an animal by
a stove.

Then came the night.

Uncle had gone to nurse his brother, who was thought
to be dying. The maid was again away—her own child
had fever. Lilian and me were alone at Whitecroft. All
day she seemed strange, too calm after weeks of misery.
She washed her hair, mended the lace on her best
petticoat and asked whether the moon would rise early. I
noticed, but jealousy can make a clever woman stupid.

We supped at seven. She scarcely ate.

"You should sleep," I said.

"Yes," she answered. "I shall go early."

At nine she kissed me.

That was not our habit. As children we had kissed
every night, but not since Edgar came between us. Her
cheek was cold.

"Whatever happens," she said, "remember I have loved you better
than any sister."

"What is to happen?"

"Nothing. I am foolish tonight."

She went upstairs. I heard her door close.

I sat in the kitchen with my knitting. The clock
ticked, the ashes settled, and once a mouse scratched behind
the flour bin. Outside there was no wind. That stillness
was worse than the storm. Whitecroft was never silent, yet
that night it seemed to hold its breath.

I thought of Lilian asleep above me. I thought of
Edgar waiting somewhere, loving her. I thought of Uncle's words:
her portion to Isabel. Money, house, land—all mine if she
defied him. But I did not want the money, not
then. I wanted the world emptied of the one face
Edgar chose before mine.

You say temptation is not sin until we consent. Very
well. I consented.

There was a basket of kindling by the copper in
the wash-house, dry sticks, straw and two broken crates. I
carried it into the narrow passage beneath Lilian's room. I
moved carefully, because even in wickedness I feared waking her
too soon. I laid the straw against the old panelling.
I placed sticks over it as I had built a
hundred kitchen fires.

My hands did not shake.

That has troubled me more than anything. Had I been
wild, had I seen red and known nothing, I might
plead madness. But I arranged the wood neatly. I went
back for a lamp. I lifted the chimney, turned up
the wick, and held a twist of paper to the
flame.

For one instant I saw my own hand bright in
the lamplight, every crease plain, the nails square from work.
I could have stopped.

I lit the straw.

It caught with a whisper.

I stood there while the little yellow tongue ran along
the paper and tucked itself among the shavings. Then another
flame rose, and another. The panelling darkened. A bead of
resin swelled in the heat and burst.

I went outside.

Yes. I left her.

I crossed the yard and stood by the water trough.
The moon had not risen. The house was a black
bulk against the stars, my home, my childhood, the place
where Lilian and me had hidden under tables and stolen
apples and waited for Christmas. At first nothing showed. I
nearly went back to stamp it out.

Then a window reddened.

The fire had climbed inside the wall faster than I
knew. A dull light pulsed behind the lower casement; smoke
pushed from beneath the eaves, brown and thick as wool.
There came a cracking sound, and sparks flew upward.

I stared at Lilian's window.

Still dark.

I told myself smoke would overcome her before pain did.
I told myself she had betrayed Uncle, deceived me, stolen
Edgar. Stolen! As if he had ever been mine.

The glass below burst outward.

That sound broke whatever spell held me. I saw not
a rival then, but Lilian at six years old, asleep
with one thumb curled under her chin. I saw her
falling on the ice and calling my name. I saw
her bringing me broth when I had scarlet fever. All
the years returned at once, and love rose up furious
against the murder I had made.

I screamed her name and ran in.

The passage was already burning. Heat struck me so hard
I turned my face aside. The wall flamed from floor
to ceiling; strips of paper curled black like dead leaves.
I snatched the hearthrug from the kitchen, wrapped it around
my head and shoulders, and went for the stairs.

Smoke rolled down them.

I climbed on my hands and knees, calling, "Lilian! Lilian!"

No answer.

Halfway up, a beam fell behind me and sent fire
across my skirt. I beat it out with my palms.
That is how these fingers became claws, sir. The doctor
used to say I was fortunate to keep them. Fortunate!
Doctors have a merry notion of luck.

At the landing I could not see. I felt along
the wall until I found her door. The handle scorched
my palm. I wrapped my apron round it and turned.

The door would not open.

I threw my weight against it. Once, twice. On the
third blow something gave, and I fell inside.

Her bed curtains were alight.

The room glowed like the mouth of a furnace. The
dressing glass had cracked, and in its pieces the fire
leapt a hundred times. I rushed to the bed, seized
the blankets and dragged them down.

She was not there.

I felt across the mattress, thinking she might have fallen
or crawled beneath it. Nothing. Her travelling dress was gone
from the peg. The little painted box she kept her
letters in was gone too.

Then I understood.

She had fled to Edgar.

I laughed. God forgive me, I laughed in that burning
room. I had set fire to the house to kill
a woman who had already escaped me. All my hatred
had found no victim but myself.

The floor shifted. There was a roar beneath, and the
room tipped as if Whitecroft had become a ship in
heavy water. I tried to reach the door, but the
ceiling came down.

They found me at dawn near the broken outer wall.
How I came there I do not know. Rain had
begun, though not enough to save the house. A shepherd
from East Dean saw the blaze and brought men. They
pulled me from beneath fallen plaster, burned on my face,
arms and side, but living.

Whitecroft was gone.

For three days they thought I would die. On the
fourth, Lilian returned.

She and Edgar had married in Felscombe before riding east.
The boy who kept their secret had seen smoke on
the hill and told them. Lilian came to my bedside
in her travelling clothes, sobbing so that she could not
speak.

Everyone believed the fire had begun in the wash-house by
accident. Perhaps a coal fallen from the copper, they said.
Perhaps a cracked lamp. They believed I had woken, discovered
Lilian missing and gone into the flames searching for her.

They called me brave.

Lilian knelt beside me and kissed my bandaged hand. "You
went back for me."

I could not speak. The burns had swollen my throat.

"You would have died for me," she said.

That was true.

I would have died to save the woman I had
meant to kill.

Edgar stood behind her. His face was full of pity
and reverence. Reverence, sir! He took my unburned hand and
bowed his forehead upon it.

"I misjudged you," he said.

He had never judged me at all. That was the
bitter jest.

Uncle died the next year without forgiving Lilian. He left
everything to me: the insurance money, the lower farm, his
savings, all. Lilian would accept nothing. She said I had
earned it by devotion.

Earned it!

I rebuilt no house at Whitecroft. I could not. I
sold the land, moved here and used the money as
you know. The almshouses, the schoolroom, coal in winter, bread
for widows, boots for children. You were about to mention
those things when you came in. Do not.

Good deeds are not water poured backward through time. Every
loaf I gave away left the straw burning. Every child
I clothed left my hand holding the match.

Lilian and Edgar were happy, I believe. He prospered after
some years. The rumours died because lies often grow weary
before truth does. They had three children. Lilian wrote to
me every month until she died. In every letter there
was love, gratitude, and some mention of "that dreadful night
when you tried to save me."

I kept them all. There, in the walnut box.

No, you may not read them.

Edgar died twelve years ago. Lilian has been gone twenty.
Uncle is dust, Whitecroft is dust, and soon I shall
be dust with them. There is no one living whom
confession can protect or injure—save my own soul, if souls
may yet be injured after so long.

Why did I not confess before? Pride, terror, cowardice. Choose
your word. I liked the way folk looked at me.
Saint Isabel, scarred for love. Even the children were taught
not to stare at my face because I had received
those burns saving another. I hated their praise, and I
fed upon it.

That is the worst corruption: not the first lie, but
learning to live warmly inside it.

You ask if I repent.

What do you think these years have been?

But repentance is not merely pain. A trapped fox suffers
pain and would bite again if freed. Have I changed,
or have I only lacked another Lilian, another Edgar, another
silent house and basket of dry sticks? Age cools the
blood. It does not prove the heart clean.

If God placed that night before me now, would I
act differently?

Yes.

I think yes.

But I am eighty-two. My hand can hardly lift a
teacup. It is easy for ashes to boast they will
not burn.

You say Christ forgave the thief. The thief confessed where
all could hear. I whispered to one tired clergyman in
a small room after everyone else was dead. You say
intention matters, and I turned back to save her. Then
does the later love cancel the earlier hate? Or do
they stand together eternally, as they have stood in me?

Do not answer too quickly.

I see you looking at the grate. The fire has
gone nearly out. Put on no more coal. Let it
die.

Will you give me absolution? Perhaps you must. It belongs
to your office, like the black coat and the little
white bands. You will speak the words, and I shall
kneel if my knees permit, and you will go away
believing some burden has been lifted.

But will you remember me tomorrow?

No, sir—do not protest. You have a christening at noon
and a parish meeting after. There are drains to discuss,
hymns to choose, respectable ladies to soothe. My sin is
a storm to me and an hour's bad weather to
you.

Perhaps that is why I have told you. Your forgetting
makes you safer than love.

Lilian loved me, and I lied to her until she
died. Edgar honoured me, and I let him. The village
blessed me, and I took its blessing. I have been
alone not because no one cared, but because they cared
for a woman who never existed.

Now you know the true one.

She sat beneath her cousin's room.

She laid the straw.

She struck the match.

And when the fire rose, she ran back crying the
name of the person she most desired to destroy.

There, sir. That is all.

Now tell me what mercy can make of such a
creature.

# Only One Life

The summer of 1776 was a dark one for the
American cause.

Only a few weeks had passed since the colonies had
declared themselves free and independent; yet freedom, so bravely proclaimed,
seemed already in danger of being lost. General George Washington's
army had been defeated on Long Island. His soldiers were
weary, poorly armed, and often hungry. Many were ill. Some
had never before heard the roar of cannon in battle,
and the sight of the disciplined British ranks had filled
them with dismay.

Across the waters and upon the neighboring heights lay the
redcoats, confident, well supplied, and strong in numbers. British ships
commanded the rivers. If the enemy moved swiftly, Washington and
his army might be trapped upon Manhattan Island.

Was it strange that Washington's heart was heavy?

He knew that courage alone could not save the patriot
army. He must learn where the British intended to strike,
how their troops were placed, and what roads and landing
places they meant to use. Yet reliable information was almost
impossible to obtain. Friendly reports were confused; rumors contradicted one
another; and the British guarded their plans with care.

One day Washington sent for Colonel Thomas Knowlton, commander of
a band of picked soldiers.

"Colonel," said the General, "it is necessary that we obtain
exact knowledge of the enemy's position. Find me some discreet
and trustworthy officer who will pass within their lines and
bring back such information as may aid us."

Knowlton understood what was being asked. The chosen man must
lay aside his uniform, assume a false name, and enter
the British camp in disguise. If captured, he would not
be treated as a prisoner of war. He would be
hanged as a spy.

Moreover, spying was then considered dishonorable work for a soldier.
An officer might face cannon without flinching, yet shrink from
a task that required concealment and deceit. Knowlton spoke to
several brave men. One after another refused.

"I am ready to die in battle," said one, "but
I cannot consent to go among the enemy in disguise."

Another declared that the service was beneath the dignity of
an officer.

Their feeling may seem strange to us, for every army
must have intelligence if it is to act wisely. But
in those days the spy was regarded with peculiar contempt.
He went without the protection of a uniform. He must
deceive those around him. If discovered, neither rank nor courage
could save him from the gallows.

At last Knowlton called his officers together and told them
plainly of Washington's need. For a moment there was silence.

Then a young captain stepped forward.

"I will undertake it," he said.

The speaker was Nathan Hale.

His companions were astonished. They knew him as a gallant
soldier, a brilliant young scholar, and a stanch patriot. They
loved him for his generous nature and cheerful courage. Some
tried to dissuade him.

"It is too great a risk," urged a friend. "If
you are taken, you will die the death of a
spy."

"I know it," Hale answered quietly.

"But such work cannot bring honor."

"My country requires the service," he replied. "If I can
render it, honor must not be the question."

These were not idle words. Nathan Hale had been trained
from boyhood to place duty before comfort.

He had grown up in a large New England household
where work, study, and religion were parts of everyday life.
He was tall and well formed, with clear blue eyes,
fair hair, and a fresh complexion. A small mole upon
his neck was one of the marks by which those
who knew him could recognize him. He was active in
every manly sport, yet gentle in manner and modest concerning
his own abilities.

At college he had been admired both for scholarship and
character. He could take part in a debate, join heartily
in athletic games, and speak kindly to a younger student
who was lonely or discouraged. After completing his studies, he
became a schoolmaster.

It was a calling suited to him. He loved learning
and wished to make it useful to others. His pupils
found him firm without harshness and merry without foolishness. There
was a brightness in his company that won affection. Many
a boy who disliked his books must have worked more
faithfully because the young master expected him to do his
best.

Then came the quarrel between the colonies and Great Britain.
Taxes were laid without the colonists' consent. British soldiers occupied
American towns. Blood was shed. Throughout New England men asked
whether they should submit or resist.

Hale did not make his decision hastily. His brave and
tender heart must sometimes have been sorely tried. He loved
peace; he had chosen the quiet labor of teaching; and
he knew that war would bring suffering to many innocent
homes. But when the call became clear, he could not
remain at his desk while others defended the liberties of
the country.

He entered the army.

In camp he showed the same qualities that had made
him successful at school. He studied his duties, cared for
his men, endured hardship without complaint, and sought no reward
for himself. When danger came, he was ready. When dull
routine followed, he was still ready. True courage is not
proved only in the excitement of battle. It is proved
also by patience, obedience, and cheerfulness amid weariness and disappointment.

And now, when Washington needed one man for a service
from which all others shrank, Nathan Hale volunteered.

Colonel Knowlton looked earnestly at him.

"Captain Hale," he said, "you understand that there may be
no rescue if you are discovered?"

"I understand."

"You may be condemned without trial."

"I have considered that also."

"Then why undertake it?"

Hale's answer was simple.

"We cannot choose our service merely because it is pleasant.
I believe I owe my country whatever ability I possess."

Think of the poor fellow's resolve! He was young, strong,
educated, and beloved. Life opened brightly before him. Yet he
did not ask whether the task would bring fame. He
asked only whether it was necessary.

Preparations were made in secrecy. Hale removed his military dress
and put on the plain clothing of a schoolmaster. This
disguise was wisely chosen, for he could speak of books
and teaching without fear of betraying himself. He was to
pretend that he had grown discouraged with the struggle for
independence and was seeking employment among those loyal to the
King.

Carrying little that might awaken suspicion, he crossed Long Island
Sound and entered territory held by the British.

His danger began at once.

Redcoats were everywhere. British officers rode along the roads. Loyalists,
or Tories, watched strangers closely and were eager to report
any sign of sympathy with the patriots. A careless question,
an uncertain answer, even the recognition of a familiar face
might send Hale to death.

Yet he moved calmly among them. As a wandering schoolmaster
he attracted little attention. He listened while soldiers talked in
taverns. He observed camps, roads, boats, and batteries. When he
could do so without being seen, he drew the shape
of a fortification or wrote an account of troops moving
from one place to another.

Such notes were dangerous things. If found, they could not
easily be explained. Hale therefore concealed them about his person,
knowing each day that the hidden papers might become witnesses
against him.

For nearly two weeks he continued his work.

We cannot follow him through every hour of that perilous
time. No complete record remains. We do not know in
what houses he slept, what excuses he made, or how
often a suspicious question caused his heart to beat faster.
Perhaps some good woman offered the supposed schoolmaster a meal.
Perhaps British soldiers joked with him beside a fire, never
guessing that his attentive blue eyes were measuring their strength.
Perhaps he lay awake at night thinking of Washington's anxious
army and of the friends he had left behind.

But this much is certain: he obtained information and prepared
to carry it back.

He had passed safely through the most difficult part of
his mission. The precious notes were hidden. A boat was
expected to meet him and take him across the Sound.
Soon, he hoped, he would stand once more before his
commander and place in Washington's hands the knowledge for which
he had risked his life.

How eagerly he must have watched the shore!

While waiting, Hale stopped at a Tory tavern. It was
an unfortunate choice, but he may have had no safer
place to go. The room was likely filled with the
talk of war—of the recent British victory, the movements of
ships, and the expected destruction of the rebel army.

Among those present was someone who recognized him, or at
least suspected him.

Who betrayed Nathan Hale has never been established beyond all
doubt. Some accounts say that the betrayer was a kinsman
loyal to the King. Others leave the person unnamed. History
sometimes preserves the noblest words of a man and loses
the name of the wretch who delivered him to death.

A message was carried to the British.

Hale remained near the appointed place, unaware that enemies were
closing about him. Presently a boat approached the shore.

He believed it was the boat for which he had
been waiting.

Relief must have swept over him. The long strain was
nearly ended! Beyond the water lay the patriot lines, his
comrades, and safety. He came forward to meet the boat.

Then armed men sprang upon him.

It was a British boat.

There was no chance of escape. Hale was seized and
searched. The concealed papers were discovered—notes of troop movements, descriptions
of positions, and drawings of British defenses. No schoolmaster seeking
employment could explain such things.

He was taken before the British commander.

"Who are you?" he was asked.

Hale did not beg for mercy, invent a fresh falsehood,
or accuse another man. Once the mission had failed and
the evidence had been found, concealment could serve no purpose.

"I am an officer in the American army," he said.

His youth and calm bearing may have impressed some of
those who heard him. But the laws of war were
stern. He had been captured within the British lines in
disguise, carrying secret military information. He was a spy.

He was condemned to die.

There was no long trial, no careful hearing, no opportunity
to summon friends. The order was brief.

"To-morrow morning at daybreak."

To-morrow morning at daybreak! Hale had risked death from the
hour he accepted the mission, but a known sentence is
harder to bear than a distant danger. All the hopes
of youth, all thoughts of home and kindred, all unfinished
plans for useful service must end with sunrise.

He was placed under the charge of William Cunningham, the
provost marshal. Cunningham has come down to us as a
brutal and infamous man, harsh toward prisoners and without respect
for courage in an enemy.

Hale asked for a clergyman.

The request was refused.

He asked for a Bible.

That also was refused.

Can we not imagine the lonely young prisoner during those
dark hours? He had been reared in faith. He had
tried to act according to duty. Now, denied even the
comfort of Scripture or a minister's voice, he must prepare
his soul for death alone.

Yet he was not wholly alone. The God whom he
had served in home, school, and camp was not shut
out by prison walls.

Hale was permitted to write letters. One was addressed to
a beloved brother; another may have been intended for an
officer or friend. We do not know their exact words.
They were farewell messages, written by a man who knew
that only a few hours remained.

Perhaps he spoke tenderly of his family. Perhaps he tried
to soften their grief by assuring them that he died
in peace. Perhaps he asked that no one blame him
for undertaking the mission. Whatever he wrote, those letters would
have been precious memorials.

In the morning Cunningham took them and destroyed them.

Why?

The provost marshal is said to have declared that the
rebels should not know they had a man who could
die with such firmness. If this account is true, Cunningham's
cruel act defeated its own purpose. The letters were lost,
but Hale's courage could not be hidden.

The morning of September 22 dawned over the British camp.
The air was clear, and the ordinary life of the
army began. Drums sounded. Soldiers stirred. Orders were given. Men
ate, talked, and prepared for another day.

For Nathan Hale there was to be no other day.

He was led out under guard. He wore no uniform.
He had no sword at his side. There was no
procession of sorrowing comrades and no flag of his country
above him. To the British he was merely a captured
spy being taken to execution.

The gallows was a rope fastened to the branch of
an apple tree.

How cruelly brief!

Some witnesses stood near. They saw a tall young man,
only twenty-one years of age, facing death with a composure
that put his enemies to shame. There was no wild
struggle, no cowardly pleading. He had accepted the service with
open eyes, and he accepted its terrible cost.

He was allowed to speak.

Then Nathan Hale uttered the words that have lived after
the commands of kings and the boasting of conquerors have
been forgotten:

"I only regret that I have but one life to
lose for my country."

A few moments later he was dead.

The British buried him in an unmarked grave. No one
now knows with certainty where his body lies. No loving
hand placed a stone above him. No family member stood
beside the earth into which he was lowered. He vanished,
as his captors may have supposed, into the confusion and
sorrow of war.

But a noble deed does not require a marked grave
in order to live.

The news of Hale's death passed through the American army.
Men who had feared that spying was dishonorable understood that
he had made it honorable by the spirit in which
he performed it. He had deceived the enemy, as his
duty required, but he had not deceived himself. He sought
neither reward nor glory. He gave his life because Washington
needed information and because the cause of liberty was dearer
to him than safety.

The patriot struggle continued through years of hardship. There were
defeats, bitter winters, hunger, mutiny, and grief. At last independence
was won. Nathan Hale did not live to see that
victory. Yet he had helped to make it possible, not
only by the information he tried to obtain, but by
leaving to others an example of steadfast devotion.

In a later year another young officer, the British Major
André, was captured as a spy and executed by the
Americans. André was gifted, charming, and personally brave, and even
his enemies pitied him. But he had entered upon his
dangerous undertaking with hopes of military glory and royal favor.
Advancement and distinction lay before him if he succeeded.

Nathan Hale had no such ambition. He expected neither praise
nor promotion. Indeed, had his mission succeeded perfectly, his part
in it might never have been known. Secrecy was necessary,
and he was willing to serve in secrecy.

There lies the difference between the desire to be celebrated
and the desire to be useful.

Many a patriotic American boy has wished to possess Hale's
courage. Yet courage is not born suddenly beneath an apple
tree. It is formed through years of small duties faithfully
done—through truthfulness, study, kindness, discipline, and obedience to conscience. The
schoolmaster who served his pupils patiently became the captain who
served his soldiers faithfully; and the captain who had always
placed duty first became the martyr who could face death
without regret for himself.

His country had asked for one service.

He gave it all he had.

Superstitious people, said the night-watchman, lowering his voice and looking
hard at the black cat by the warehouse door, is
mostly people wot ought to know better. Sailormen is the
worst, but I've known landsmen as could give 'em points
and beat 'em easy. I knew a carpenter once as
wouldn't drive the last nail into a coffin on a
Friday, not if you was to offer 'im overtime; but
he'd walk under ladders all day without turning a hair.
That shows you wot superstition is. It ain't reason, and
it ain't sense. It's knowing where to draw the line.

The cat got up, stretched itself, and came towards him.

"Git away," said the watchman, without looking at it. "I
don't mind cats, black or any other colour. Only this
one's got no business 'ere."

He shifted his stool a little farther from the warehouse
door and folded his arms.

"I've 'eard," he continued, "that if a black cat crosses
your path it means either good luck or bad luck,
according to whether it's going to the right or the
left. Nobody seems to know which, and by the time
you've found out the cat's gone 'ome. Sailormen don't leave
things undecided like that. They know exactly wot's unlucky. Whistling
at sea is unlucky; sailing on Friday is unlucky; cutting
your nails in a calm is unlucky; and seeing a
red-'aired woman afore breakfast is enough to make some of
'em turn back and spend the day in bed. That's
why red-'aired women mostly get their shopping done in the
afternoon.

"I was at sea myself for over twenty year, and
I never believed in none of it. Never. I've seen
things, mind you, as couldn't be explained by ordinary means,
but that's different. There was a hand come up out
of a bucket aboard the *Mary Ann* one night, and
beckon to the cook. Three men saw it, besides the
cook, and next morning the cook found his best shirt
missing. You may call that superstition if you like; I
call it dishonesty. One of the three men was wearing
the shirt when we reached Gravesend.

"Then there was the mate of the *Sea Pigeon*, who
always said he could hear bells ringing under the water
whenever a ship was going to sink. He heard 'em
regular for fourteen years, and the ship never sunk once.
At last the skipper found out it was two empty
medicine bottles knocking together under the mate's bunk. The mate
said that didn't prove nothing, and p'raps it didn't. It
only proves that some men would sooner believe a ghost
than move their luggage.

"Still, superstition can be useful at times. I knew a
man once as was cured of drinking by a ghost,
and the curious part of it was that the ghost
wasn't dead. Not at the time, anyway. Whether he's dead
now I can't say; he owed me half a crown
when I last saw 'im, and people like that generally
live to a great age.

"His name was Bill Burtenshaw, and a better sailor never
stepped, so long as he was sober. When he wasn't
sober he couldn't step anywhere without assistance. He was a
thick-set man with a red face, a little brown beard,
and a pair of eyes that always looked as if
they'd just heard something behind 'em. He'd been at sea
since he was twelve, and he'd collected superstitions the way
other men collects foreign stamps. He wouldn't sleep with his
boots pointing towards the door; he wouldn't pass a churchyard
without touching iron; and if he heard a dog howl
at night he'd stay awake till morning, unless he happened
to be drunk, in which case you might have fired
the dog out of a cannon beside 'is ear without
disturbing him.

"Bill married a quiet little woman named Martha. She was
the sort o' woman as says very little because she's
busy thinking of things the man ought to 'ave thought
of first. When Bill was courting, he brought 'er silk
handkerchiefs, parrots' feathers, shells, foreign scent, and once a little
stuffed crocodile, which he said was useful as well as
ornamental, though he never said wot for. Arter they was
married he brought 'er nothing but excuses, and most of
them second-hand.

"He earned good money. That was the trouble. If he'd
earned twelve shillings a week, he might 'ave been a
steady, respectable man; but he earned enough to keep a
wife, and so he took to drink. He started on
Saturday night, rested a little on Sunday morning, and went
on again Sunday afternoon to make up for it. Monday
he was sorry, Tuesday he was thirsty, Wednesday he was
cheerful, and by Thursday Mrs. Burtenshaw was wondering which article
of furniture would go next.

"He begun with things he called unnecessary. The parlour clock
went because, he said, there was a clock at the
church. The easy-chair went because sitting down too much was
bad for a woman's figure. Then the china dog off
the mantelpiece went because it was unlucky to keep an
animal as couldn't bark. Arter that the spare blankets went,
a copper kettle, three saucepans, and a pair of curtains.
When Mrs. Burtenshaw asked 'im where the curtains was, he
said they'd been sent to be cleaned, and when she
asked which cleaners he got offended and went out.

"At last there was hardly enough left in the house
to furnish a cupboard. Mrs. Burtenshaw kept the poker in
the bedroom at night, partly for protection and partly because
Bill hadn't thought of selling it. She hid the flat-irons
under a loose board, kept the teaspoons in her pocket,
and wore two petticoats because she didn't trust the chest
of drawers.

"One evening, while Bill was out improving the landlord's business,
there came a knock at the door. Mrs. Burtenshaw opened
it and saw a long, melancholy-looking man with sandy whiskers
and a mouth that turned down at both corners as
if life had disappointed 'im personal.

"'Mrs. Burtenshaw?' he ses.

"'Yes,' she ses.

"'My name's Silas Winch,' he ses. 'Me and Bill was
shipmates aboard the *Golden Fleece*.'

"'Come in,' she ses, 'if you don't want nothing.'

"Silas looked a bit hurt at that, but he came
in and sat down on the only chair. Mrs. Burtenshaw
sat on an empty flour-bin.

"'Bill out?' he ses.

"'Yes,' she ses.

"'At the Sailor's Return?' he ses.

"'No,' she ses. 'At the Jolly Mariner.'

"'Ah,' ses Silas, nodding. 'He always did like a change.'

"He looked round the room, which didn't take long, and
asked whether they'd moved lately.

"'No,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw. 'The furniture has.'

"Then she told 'im about Bill. She told 'im about
the wages and the public-house, the blankets and the saucepans,
and the clock that was no longer required because of
the church. Silas listened with his mouth turned down worse
than ever, and every now and then he shook his
head till his whiskers brushed his coat.

"'Drink,' he ses at last, 'is a terrible thing.'

"'It is when somebody else does it,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw.

"'I never touch it,' ses Silas, 'except when I'm asked.'

"Mrs. Burtenshaw said she'd remember not to ask 'im.

"Silas sat thinking, with his long hands between his knees.
Then all of a sudden he looked up.

"'Is Bill still superstitious?' he ses.

"'Worse,' she ses. 'Last week he gave my washing money
to an old woman because she told 'im his left
eyebrow meant sudden danger.'

"'Did it?' ses Silas.

"'It did when he came 'ome,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw.

"Silas rubbed his hands together very slow.

"'Me and Bill made an agreement once,' he ses. 'It
was aboard the *Golden Fleece*, off the coast of Peru,
or p'raps it was Margate. Wherever it was, the weather
was very bad. We agreed that whichever of us died
first was to come back and tell the other wot
it was like.'

"Mrs. Burtenshaw stared at 'im.

"'Did you put it in writing?' she ses.

"'No,' ses Silas. 'Ghosts don't care for paperwork. But Bill
remembers. He was that frightened arterwards that he made me
promise not to come back during meals.'

"He leaned nearer and lowered his voice.

"'If Bill was to think I was dead,' he ses,
'and if my ghost was to appear to 'im and
warn 'im against drink—'

"Mrs. Burtenshaw sat up.

"'Could you look dead?' she ses.

"Silas considered. 'I've been told I do of a morning,'
he ses.

"They talked it over. At first Silas wanted to appear
in chains, but Mrs. Burtenshaw said chains cost money and
made too much noise. Then he thought of a white
sheet, but she said Bill knew their sheets had all
been pawned. At last Silas said he knew a scene-painter
at a little theatre who had some luminous paint left
over from a demon. He could put it on his
face and hands, wet his hair, and wear an old
sea-cloak.

"'You'll 'ave to groan,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw.

"'I can groan,' ses Silas, drawing a long breath and
giving one.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw jumped.

"'Not like that,' she ses. 'That sounds as if you'd
eaten something. Make it more lonely.'

"He tried again.

"'Too cheerful,' she ses.

"He tried a third time, beginning low and ending with
a sort of wobble.

"'That's better,' she ses. 'It gave me quite a turn
near the end. Do that one when he pulls the
bedclothes over his head.'

"They settled it for the following Saturday. Silas was to
spread a report that he'd been washed overboard from a
barge in the river. Mrs. Burtenshaw was to tell Bill,
and when Bill came 'ome drunk she was to leave
the kitchen window open and the back door unbolted. Silas
was to come upstairs at midnight, wake Bill, reform 'im,
and go.

"'Nothing about money,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw.

"'Certainly not,' ses Silas, looking shocked. 'This is friendship.'

"Saturday came, and the story about Silas being drowned reached
Bill by seven o'clock. It reached 'im in the Jolly
Mariner, where four men told it four different ways. One
said Silas had fallen overboard; one said he'd jumped; one
said a rope had knocked 'im in; and the fourth
said it was a barrel of cement. Bill believed all
of 'em.

"He drank two glasses to Silas's memory and another because
Silas wouldn't have wanted 'im to grieve. Then he remembered
the agreement and turned pale under the red.

"'He can't come yet,' he ses. 'He's only just drowned.'

"'Depends on the tide,' ses one of the men.

"Bill looked over his shoulder, swallowed his drink, and ordered
another to keep the cold out. By closing-time the cold
was out, and so was nearly everything else. Two men
took 'im home and leaned 'im against his own door.
Mrs. Burtenshaw got 'im upstairs by pushing from behind, took
off one boot, gave up the other, and put 'im
to bed crossways.

"Just afore midnight there was a soft tap at the
kitchen window.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw went down, and there was Silas in the
yard. His face shone pale green, his hands shone too,
and his wet hair hung over his forehead. He had
seaweed on one shoulder and a piece of string round
his neck.

"'Wot's the string for?' she whispers.

"'I was going to 'ave an anchor on it,' he
whispers back, 'but it was too heavy.'

"'Take your boots off,' she ses. 'Ghosts don't squeak.'

"He took 'em off and followed her upstairs. At the
bedroom door she reminded 'im of the groan.

"'The lonely one?' he whispers.

"'Yes. And don't overdo it.'

"She went into the little back room and left 'im.

"Silas entered the bedroom. There was a candle burning on
the washstand, and Bill lay on his back, snoring hard
enough to shake the water in the jug. Silas stood
beside the bed, folded his arms, and groaned.

"Bill went on snoring.

"Silas groaned again, louder.

"Bill gave a snort, stopped for a moment, and then
started again with renewed strength.

"Silas leaned over 'im.

"'Bill!' he ses, in a deep sepulchral voice.

"Bill opened his mouth wider, but nothing else happened.

"Silas poked 'im in the shoulder with one luminous finger.
Bill brushed it away and muttered something about five minutes
more.

"Silas poked again.

"'Bill Burtenshaw!' he ses. 'Awake and behold!'

"Bill opened one eye, saw the shining face, and shut
it again.

"'Go away,' he ses.

"Silas wasn't prepared for that.

"'Bill!' he ses. 'It is me—Silas Winch—returned from the bottom
of the sea.'

"Bill pulled the bedclothes over his head.

"'Good-by, Silas,' he ses, muffled.

"'Come out and look at me,' ses Silas.

"'I can see you very well where I am,' ses
Bill.

"Silas gave the lonely groan. Mrs. Burtenshaw, listening next door,
thought it sounded a bit irritated, but Bill began trembling
till the bedstead rattled.

"'Wot do you want?' he ses.

"'I have come with a warning,' ses Silas. 'A terrible
warning from them regions where the waves is always roaring
and there ain't no public-houses.'

"Bill gave a little cry.

"'Don't say that,' he ses.

"'I was washed overboard,' continued Silas, warming to his work.
'Down I went—down and down—past fishes with eyes like lamps,
and a cabin-boy that had been there since eighteen-sixty-four. He
caught hold of my leg, but I kicked 'im off.'

"'Poor little chap,' ses Bill from under the clothes.

"'He was a nuisance,' ses Silas. 'Arter that I came
to a dark gate, and a voice ses: "Silas Winch,
return to earth and warn Bill Burtenshaw."'

"'Why me?' ses Bill.

"'Because of the drink,' ses Silas. 'Every glass you takes
is another link in a chain wot you'll 'ave to
wear for ever and ever, and each link weighs fourteen
pounds.'

"Bill's teeth knocked together.

"'I'll stop,' he ses. 'I swear I'll stop this very
minute.'

"'Not another drop?' ses Silas.

"'Not another drop.'

"'Not even when it's raining?'

"'No.'

"'Nor on your birthday?'

"'No.'

"'Nor when somebody else is paying?'

"There was a pause.

"'No,' ses Bill faintly.

"Silas groaned again, more comfortable now, and turned as if
to go. Then he stopped. It had come into his
mind that a man who was giving up drink would
have a lot of spare money, and Silas always had
ideas quick where spare money was concerned.

"'There is one thing more,' he ses.

"Bill made a miserable noise.

"'I have left behind me,' ses Silas, 'a widow.'

"'You wasn't married,' ses Bill, forgetting his fear for a
moment.

"'We kept it quiet,' ses Silas. 'Very quiet. Her name
is—er—Jane.'

"'Jane wot?'

"'Jane Winch,' ses Silas severely. 'Naturally.'

"'Where does she live?' ses Bill.

"Silas hadn't thought of that.

"'A long way off,' he ses. 'So far that it
would be no use your calling. She is poor, Bill.
Very poor. Sitting at this moment in a room with
no fire, no food, and three little children asking when
father is coming 'ome.'

"'Three?' ses Bill. 'You've been busy.'

"Silas groaned so fierce that Bill disappeared farther under the
bedclothes.

"'You must send her ten shillings a week,' ses Silas.

"Then he thought again.

"'Fifteen,' he ses. 'Ten for food and five for—other things.'

"'That's a lot,' ses Bill.

"Silas bent down till his glowing face was close to
the lump under the blankets.

"'Down below,' he ses, 'there's things fifty million times worse
than anything a man sees after drinking. Things with horns,
Bill. And tails. And eyes all over 'em.'

"'I'll send it,' ses Bill quickly.

"'Every week?'

"'Every week.'

"'By money order, payable to Mrs. Jane Winch?'

"'Yes.'

"'And write "From a friend" on the form.'

"'Yes.'

"'Good-by, Bill.'

"'Good-by, Silas.'

"Silas gave one last groan, which Mrs. Burtenshaw afterwards said
was the best of the evening, and crept downstairs. He
had just got his boots on when she came after
'im.

"'Wot was all that about fifteen shillings?' she ses.

"'A little extra touch,' ses Silas. 'Made it natural.'

"'Natural?' she ses. 'You said nothing about a widow.'

"'A ghost must 'ave responsibilities,' ses Silas. 'Besides, if Bill
sacrifices something, the lesson'll sink in deeper.'

"Mrs. Burtenshaw stared at 'im, but she couldn't speak too
loud for fear of Bill hearing. Silas promised to explain
another time and went off through the yard, glowing gently
in the dark.

"The cure worked. Bill woke next morning with one boot
on and a dreadful memory. He searched the room for
wet footprints, and when he found a smear of luminous
paint on the bedpost he turned so white that Mrs.
Burtenshaw nearly felt sorry for 'im.

"'Silas came last night,' he ses.

"'Nonsense,' she ses.

"'I saw 'im.'

"'You saw the drink,' she ses.

"'He was shining,' ses Bill. 'And wet. He'd been right
down to the bottom. There was a cabin-boy hanging on
to 'is leg.'

"Mrs. Burtenshaw said that ought to teach 'im not to
drink on an empty stomach.

"But Bill was changed. He passed the Jolly Mariner that
night without going in. The landlord came to the door
and asked if he was ill, and Bill crossed the
road. On payday he brought all his wages home, except
fifteen shillings, and Mrs. Burtenshaw's heart gave a jump till
she remembered the widow.

"Next morning Bill bought a money order and sent it
to the address Silas had given 'im. Silas had arranged
for the letters to go to a small shop kept
by a cousin of his, who didn't ask questions because
Silas owed 'im money too.

"Week arter week Bill sent the fifteen shillings. He never
touched drink. His face lost some of its colour, his
hands stopped shaking, and he began getting up in time
for breakfast. He even brought home a small table to
replace one he'd sold, though Mrs. Burtenshaw said it didn't
match anything, and Bill said there wasn't anything left for
it to match.

"You might think she'd have been pleased, and she was—at
first. But fifteen shillings is fifteen shillings, whether it goes
on drink or to a man painted green. Every Friday
Bill counted it out with a solemn face.

"'Poor Jane,' he'd ses.

"'Poor me,' Mrs. Burtenshaw would ses.

"'She's got three children,' ses Bill.

"'So Silas told you,' she ses.

"'And no fire.'

"'She ought to burn one of the children,' ses Mrs.
Burtenshaw. 'It'd save food as well.'

"Bill was shocked. He said sorrow had made her hard.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw went to see Silas. She found 'im in
a new waistcoat, smoking a large cigar.

"'You're looking well for a drowned man,' she ses.

"'A clear conscience,' ses Silas.

"She demanded the fifteen shillings back. Silas said it was
being spent on the widow. When she asked where the
widow was, he tapped his chest and said she was
there, meaning, he explained, that he carried her interests close
to his heart.

"'You cheated me,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw.

"'I cured Bill,' ses Silas. 'Doctors charge money.'

"'Not to the patient's wife they don't.'

"'Somebody has to pay,' ses Silas.

"She threatened to tell Bill the whole story, but Silas
only smiled. He said Bill would either go back to
drink from disappointment or throw them both into the river,
and Mrs. Burtenshaw knew enough of Bill to see that
there was sense in it. She went 'ome thinking.

"For six more weeks the money went. Bill grew stouter
and Silas grew smarter. Mrs. Burtenshaw had two new dresses
in her mind and not a penny to put on
'em. Then one Friday Bill came 'ome upset. The money
order sent the week afore had been returned because of
something wrong with the address.

"'Jane never got it,' he ses, staring at the envelope.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw stared too. Then she saw her chance.

"That night, just after they'd gone to bed, she sat
up sudden.

"'Who's there?' she ses.

"Bill sat up too.

"'Wot is it?' he ses.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw looked towards the foot of the bed and
gave a little scream.

"'Silas!' she ses.

"Bill went under the bedclothes so quick he took most
of them with 'im.

"'Don't look, Bill,' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw. 'He's shining worse than
ever.'

"From beneath the blankets came a sound like a mouse
asking for mercy.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw listened to the empty room.

"'Wot's that, Silas?' she ses. 'The money was lost? Jane
and the children is starving?'

"Bill groaned.

"'No, Silas,' she went on, 'Bill didn't do it on
purpose. Don't point at 'im like that. And put that
chain down—you'll damage the floor.'

"'Ask 'im wot he wants,' whispers Bill.

"'Wot do you want, Silas?' ses Mrs. Burtenshaw. Then she
nodded slowly. 'I see. You don't trust the post no
more. You want the fifteen shillings given to me every
week, and I'm to take it to Jane personal.'

"'That's a long way,' ses Bill, still hidden.

"Mrs. Burtenshaw held up her hand.

"'Hush! He's getting angry. Yes, Silas, I'll take it. Every
Saturday morning. And you'll never trouble Bill again?'

"She paused.

"'He ses he won't,' she told the blankets. 'He's going
now.'

"Then she drew a long, lonely groan. It wasn't quite
so good as Silas's, but Bill was in no condition
to judge. She crept out of bed, opened the door,
shut it softly, and came back.

"'He's gone,' she ses.

"Bill put one eye out.

"'Did he look bad?' he ses.

"'Terrible,' she ses. 'There was seaweed all over 'im, and
the cabin-boy was back on his leg.'

"From that week onward Bill gave the fifteen shillings to
his wife, and she went out every Saturday morning to
deliver it. She always took a large shopping-bag, because appearances
is important, and she stayed away long enough to reach
the imaginary widow and come back.

"Bill never drank again. He remained sober, respectable, and frightened,
which is as good a combination as you'll find in
a husband. Silas kept out of his way, and Mrs.
Burtenshaw never told either of 'em more than was necessary.

"And that," concluded the night-watchman, as the black cat rubbed
itself against his boot and made him start, "shows you
that superstition ain't always a bad thing if it's handled
by somebody with sense. Bill got his health, Silas got
several weeks at fifteen shillings, and Mrs. Burtenshaw got the
rest.

"She spent most of it on clothes, and every time
Bill saw her in a new hat he said it
was wonderful how far a careful woman could make money
go."

# The Awakening of Gladys Glyn

At seventeen Theodora Dace had already learned that genius is
seldom appreciated at the breakfast-table.

This was unfortunate, because the Dace breakfast-table, with its spotted
cloth, three cracked cups, and a silver-plated sugar-bowl whose plating
had retreated from all the more conspicuous places, was the
centre of nearly everything practical in the family. Here Doctor
Dace demanded to know why the coffee was cold; here
Johnny appeared without buttons; here little Emmeline overturned her porridge;
and here Mrs. Dace, from the sofa in the adjoining
room, called faintly to ask whether anyone had remembered her
cod-liver oil.

On the morning when Theodora completed the last chapter of
her novel, these interruptions acquired the character of persecution.

She had risen at five and stolen downstairs, carrying the
lamp under her apron so that its light should not
awaken the children. In the dining-room she spread her manuscript
upon the table and wrote with a speed beyond mere
physical exertion. The heroine, Genevra, had forgiven Lord Alaric; Lord
Alaric, blinded and penitent, had recognised Genevra by the touch
of her hand; and the pale dawn of a nobler
existence shone upon them both.

"Together," wrote Theodora, "they went forth to meet the consecrated
sorrow of the years."

She paused. The sentence trembled before her. It was simple,
inevitable, enormous.

Then she wrote:

THE END

For some moments she could only sit, with her cheek
resting upon one ink-stained hand, while the lamp smoked unnoticed
and morning collected, grey and chill, at the windows. The
sacred structure she had reared during seven months of secrecy
stood complete. She saw it almost visibly: tower upon tower,
white against the sky, with herself—no, with Gladys Glyn—waiting modestly
at the gate while the nations approached bearing laurels.

Gladys Glyn was Theodora's literary name. She had chosen it
after rejecting Eva Meredith as too girlish and Isolde Vane
as perhaps too unmistakably great. Gladys Glyn suggested power restrained
by femininity. It might appear upon the same page with
George Eliot's name and not seem ridiculous—which was more than
could be said for Theodora Dace.

A board creaked overhead.

Theodora started. Breakfast!

She gathered the pages into the old desk and turned
the key. Then she rushed toward the kitchen, where the
range, having received no attention, maintained the appearance of a
monument erected to extinct heat.

"Two o'clock!" she had cried the previous afternoon when she
discovered that dinner had not been put on. Now it
was eight o'clock, which was worse, because her father's first
patient came at half past eight, and physicians—although familiar with
human suffering—are not always patient under cold coffee.

The cook had left three weeks before, declaring that she
would sooner serve in a menagerie. Until Mrs. Dace's health
improved, Theodora was supposed to supervise the household with the
assistance of a girl named Maggie, who had a gift
for breaking crockery and disappearing whenever sought.

At ten minutes past eight Doctor Dace entered.

He was a lean man with iron-grey hair and an
expression which suggested that life had made several inadequate explanations
to him.

"No coffee?" he asked.

"It will be ready immediately, father."

"It has been ready immediately since the reign of Queen
Anne."

Johnny came down in one stocking, his jacket flapping.

"Thea, look!"

It was Johnny's buttons. Again!

"Pin it, dear."

"There aren't any pins."

"There were six in the cushion yesterday."

"Emmie made a necklace."

Emmeline entered at that moment wearing the pins, stuck through
a piece of red flannel, about her throat.

Doctor Dace looked from his children to the smoking lamp,
the uncooked bacon, and the closed desk. "Has literature been
unusually exacting this morning?"

Theodora coloured. Her authorship was still a secret, but her
father had an odious habit of walking directly up to
secrets and tapping them with his finger.

"I have not been reading."

"No? Then perhaps you have been composing an ode to
the kettle."

He went to see his patient without breakfast.

Theodora stood very straight beside the table. Someday, when the
royalties came, there should be a trained nurse for mother,
a competent cook, new suits for Johnny, music lessons for
Emmeline, and a horse for father—a fine dark horse, not
the old yellow buggy-mare whose knees were a public humiliation.
Then he would understand. All these temporary neglects would be
redeemed by one magnificent act of provision.

"Mamma's oil!" cried Emmeline.

Theodora flew.

It was in the afternoon, while Mrs. Dace slept and
Maggie scraped grease from a pan with a knife that
made a noise capable of disturbing the dead, that Theodora
wrapped her novel for the post.

Its title was *When Love Awakes*.

She addressed it to the editor of the *Home Circle*,
Boston.

The choice was not accidental. Kathleen Kyd, the most famous
woman novelist in America, had begun her career in the
*Home Circle*. At nineteen she had sent them *A Woman's
Kingdom*, written, according to an illustrated interview, "in a little
attic chamber amid poverty, sickness, and domestic care." Theodora had
read that sentence until it seemed less a statement concerning
Miss Kyd than a prophecy concerning herself.

There were differences. The Daces had no attic chamber, and
Theodora wrote chiefly at the dining-room table. But poverty, sickness,
and domestic care were abundantly present; indeed, they were often
so present that she could not write at all.

For postage she used the money intended for new braid
on her winter dress. This appeared a sacrifice of the
purest kind, until she remembered that success would soon render
the winter dress unnecessary. Gladys Glyn could not be expected
to receive publishers in brown serge with a shiny elbow.

She carried the package to the post-office beneath her cloak.
When it fell through the brass mouth, she experienced both
bereavement and exaltation. Her child had gone out into the
world.

For six weeks the world made no reply.

During that period Theodora alternated between despair and serene superiority.
On Mondays she knew the manuscript had been rejected and
pictured it arriving, battered and disgraced, while her father asked
who had wasted the postage. By Wednesdays she suspected the
editor had shown it to his entire staff. On Saturdays
she imagined that the delay was caused by negotiations over
terms.

She preferred, she decided, an obscure failure to a vulgar
triumph. If the public could not recognise sincerity, so much
the worse for the public. Yet when the postman approached
she ran to the window with a sincerity that was
wholly vulgar.

The letter came on a windy morning in March.

The envelope bore the words HOME CIRCLE PUBLISHING COMPANY in
the upper corner. Theodora carried it upstairs before opening it,
although Emmeline pursued her, asking whether it contained money. In
the cold bedroom she shut the door, leaned against it,
and tore the envelope.

"Dear Madam," she read.

Dear Madam! Not Miss Dace. They addressed Gladys Glyn.

"We have pleasure in informing you that your novel, *When
Love Awakes*, has been accepted for serial publication in the
*Home Circle*. The opening instalment will appear in our May
number. The unusual emotional interest of the story, together with
its high moral tone—"

The words dissolved. The paper shook in her hands.

Accepted.

The room, with its sloping floor, faded washstand, and Johnny's
snow-boots drying under the bed, suddenly opened upon immortality. She
saw the May number displayed in railway stations; she saw
women weeping over Genevra in parlours from Maine to California;
she saw herself invited to speak before literary clubs, declining
with quiet dignity because true art avoids notoriety.

Then she remembered the cook, the horse, the nurse, the
buttons.

She rushed downstairs.

Mrs. Dace was propped upon the sofa with a shawl
around her shoulders. Doctor Dace stood by the window shaking
medicine into a bottle. Uncle James, who had arrived unexpectedly
from Worcester, was explaining that half the illnesses in Norton
could be prevented by proper traps beneath the kitchen sinks.

"The whole secret," said Uncle James, "is sanitation. People will
paper their parlours in gold and tolerate a grease trap
that would poison a regiment."

"Father!" cried Theodora.

Doctor Dace turned. "Another regiment poisoned?"

"My novel has been accepted!"

There was silence.

"What novel?" asked Mrs. Dace.

Theodora stared at them. It had not occurred to her
that an announcement so tremendous would require explanation.

"My novel—*When Love Awakes*. I wrote it. They are to
publish it in the *Home Circle*."

Uncle James took off his spectacles, polished them, put them
on, and read the letter twice.

"Well!" he said. "Well, upon my word!"

Mrs. Dace began to cry.

Doctor Dace took the letter. His face did not change
as he read it, and Theodora's happiness, rushing toward him,
encountered that immovable expression and hesitated.

"Gladys Glyn?" he said.

"My pen name."

"I see."

She waited.

"I hope Gladys Glyn," he continued, "will now have leisure
to sew on Johnny's buttons."

It was exactly what she might have expected. Father could
never be counted on to behave like a father in
a book.

But Norton behaved beautifully.

By evening Miss Sophy Brill had called to ask whether
Theodora had ever met Mr. Howells, and whether authors received
money by the page or by the emotion. The next
day the *Norton Clarion* printed a paragraph headed LOCAL GIRL
WINS LITERARY HONORS. At church, persons who had never before
noticed Theodora's existence turned in their pews to look at
her. Mr. Fitch, the bookseller, offered to place her photograph
in his window when the serial began.

Mrs. Dace produced the acceptance letter for every caller.

"To think she wrote it under our very roof!" she
said.

There was something charming in the phrase, as if novels
generally required a different kind of roof.

Even Doctor Dace's reserve contributed to the effect. His refusal
to display pride was interpreted as the stern simplicity of
a man accustomed to greatness in his household. Theodora forgave
him. When the first cheque arrived, she would lay it
beside his plate without a word. He would look at
it, then at her. Perhaps his eyes would fill.

In April the world altered. Snow sank into the ground;
water sang in the gutters; and the bare branches along
the Norton road seemed to tremble with green thoughts. Theodora
walked beyond the last houses one afternoon and entered the
little wood above the river.

It was spring—spring! Beneath the wet leaves, pointed shoots thrust
upward with a courage at once humble and divine. Light
moved across the white birches. Far off, a bird called,
paused, and called again, as if asking the awakening earth
a question to which Theodora alone knew the answer.

She was going to be an author.

The phrase expanded until it contained the branches, the bright
river, the whole shining world. Neglect, ridicule, cold breakfasts—what were
they now? Every true worker passed through obscurity. George Eliot
must once have sat among persons who supposed her chiefly
useful for mending stockings. Kathleen Kyd herself had endured an
attic.

At twilight the first star appeared through the branches.

Theodora looked up at it. The star looked back. Between
them there passed—she could not have said what, but something
noble and sufficient.

On the first day of May, Mr. Fitch promised that
the new *Home Circle* would arrive by the noon train.

Theodora spent the morning in a condition of outward composure.
She washed Emmeline's hair, burned the toast, put salt instead
of sugar into Mrs. Dace's gruel, and answered three times
that she was not thinking of anything.

At eleven Miss Sophy Brill came, wearing her best bonnet.

"I thought you might like company when you saw it,"
she explained. "Of course, I shan't ask you to read
aloud if it is too affecting."

By half past eleven the parlour contained Uncle James, two
neighbours, the minister's wife, and Mr. Fitch's youngest clerk, who
had volunteered to bring the magazine personally. Mrs. Dace wore
a lace cap. Johnny had been forbidden to touch anything,
a prohibition which led him to handle each object thoughtfully
in turn.

Doctor Dace had gone into the country to attend a
confinement.

At twelve-fifteen the clerk arrived, breathless and rosy, holding six
copies of the *Home Circle* tied with string.

"There she is!" cried Mrs. Dace.

The cover showed a young woman in a yellow gown
standing upon a balcony while a gentleman in military dress
climbed a ladder toward her.

Theodora untied the string. Her fingers were cold.

There was her title across the upper half of the
contents page:

A NEW SERIAL
*WHEN LOVE AWAKES*

Below it, in smaller type:

By Kathleen Kyd

Theodora read the words once.

Then again.

Kathleen Kyd.

"Why, my dear," said Miss Brill, peering over her shoulder,
"isn't that your title?"

The room became exceedingly clear. She saw the crack in
the blue vase, the mole upon Uncle James's cheek, a
loose thread hanging from the minister's wife's glove.

"Perhaps," Uncle James said, "they have put the wrong name."

"Yes," said Mrs. Dace quickly. "It is certainly a printer's
error."

Theodora turned the pages.

The first chapter began in a castle in Brittany. Her
own story began in a farmhouse in Ohio.

"This isn't mine," she said.

No one replied.

The clerk coughed. Johnny tried to look inside a copy
and was struck on the wrist by Emmeline.

"There must be another serial," Mrs. Dace insisted. "Look farther."

Theodora looked. She examined every page, including the advertisements for
corsets, complexion wafers, and a device which cured deafness by
electricity. There was no Gladys Glyn.

"I shall write to them," Uncle James announced. "Business houses
must learn accuracy. If editors kept their offices as plumbers
keep proper accounts—"

"I am going to Boston," said Theodora.

She heard the voice as though it belonged to another
person.

The following morning she travelled alone, with the acceptance letter
in her pocket and the manuscript in a parcel on
her knees.

The *Home Circle* offices occupied the third floor of a
high building whose corridors smelled of dust, steam heat, and
printer's ink. Behind glass partitions young men bent over heaps
of paper. They seemed to Theodora like zoological specimens preserved
in separate cases—Assistant Editor, Subscription Clerk, Advertising Manager—each protected from
the suffering public by transparent walls.

She gave her name to a boy.

"Miss Glyn?" he said.

"Miss Dace. Gladys Glyn is my pen name."

The boy's face changed almost imperceptibly. He disappeared through a
door.

After a long interval the editor received her.

He was a broad, smooth man with a carnation in
his coat and an expression prepared to deplore anything without
being personally inconvenienced by it. Her acceptance letter lay before
him. Beside it was another sheet.

"My dear Miss Dace," he began, "this is a most
unfortunate accident."

Theodora sat very straight, holding the manuscript.

"You accepted my novel."

He brought his fingertips together.

"On the same day that your manuscript reached us, we
received a new novel from Miss Kathleen Kyd. By an
extraordinary coincidence, the titles were identical."

The room appeared to move backward.

"Identical," repeated Theodora.

"Precisely. *When Love Awakes*. Our reader's reports were attached to
the wrong correspondence. The acceptance intended for Miss Kyd was
mailed to you."

"And mine?"

The editor glanced down.

"Your manuscript was—ah—not retained."

Not retained.

"Then you rejected it?"

"The decision was made by one of our readers. I
have looked into the matter personally, of course. The story
shows feeling—considerable feeling for so young a writer. But it
lacks, perhaps, the firm grasp of construction our readers demand."

Unfortunate accident.

Extraordinary coincidence.

Regrettable oversight.

The phrases floated about her, polished and bodiless. The editor
continued to speak of office systems, junior clerks, sincere apologies,
future efforts. His presence rose before her like water. Each
courteous word pressed upon the exposed nerve of her hope
with the delicacy of a surgeon who is sorry to
cause pain but must finish before luncheon.

"You announced it," she said. "Everyone knows."

"We deeply regret any embarrassment."

"Could you—could you publish it later?"

His eyes changed. That was the answer, though his lips
still arranged themselves kindly.

"I fear we could make no promise."

Theodora stood.

The manuscript slipped. The editor stooped, but she snatched it
up before he could touch it. It seemed no longer
a sacred structure. It was only four hundred and twelve
pages tied with blue ribbon, heavy, foolish, alive—a live thing
that had been hurt.

"We shall, naturally, reimburse your travelling expenses," he said.

"No."

"My dear young lady—"

"No!"

She left him among his smooth apologies and glass partitions.

—At Norton the evening train arrived under a fine, persistent
rain.

Theodora had spent the journey imagining the station. Miss Brill
would be there on some errand; Mr. Fitch's clerk would
be moving freight; the conductor would remember that she had
gone to Boston as an authoress. By tomorrow every parlour
in Norton would possess the truth. The town which had
admired her would laugh. Worse, it would pity her.

At moments she blamed the editor, Kathleen Kyd, the office
boy, the coincidence of titles, and the entire machinery of
popular literature. At other moments, more terrible, she remembered sentences
from her novel. She saw them as the editor's reader
must have seen them—Genevra clasping her "ivory brow," Lord Alaric
crying, "Beloved, the tomb itself is less cold than thy
disdain!" Had she truly written that? Had she expected money
for it?

She wished the train would continue forever through the rain
and darkness.

But it stopped.

There were only two lamps on the platform. The rain
shone in their circles. The station agent rolled a trunk
beneath the eaves, and a solitary man came forward.

Her father.

Theodora remained on the step of the car.

Doctor Dace took the manuscript from her without speaking. He
carried an umbrella, but it was folded beneath his arm.

"You'll get wet," she said.

"So will you."

That was all. He did not ask what had happened.
He had evidently heard, though she could not imagine from
whom. Perhaps Uncle James had telegraphed; Uncle James believed telegraphy
existed chiefly for the correction of business outrages.

They walked away from the station. The buggy stood nearby,
but Doctor Dace led her past it toward the road.

"I thought we might walk," he said. "Unless you are
fagged."

"No."

The rain had softened to mist. In the darkness the
town lost its windows, fences, and inquisitive faces. There was
only the wet road, the sound of their steps, and
her father beside her carrying Gladys Glyn's rejected masterpiece under
his arm.

At last Theodora said, "It was a mistake."

"Yes."

"They meant to accept Kathleen Kyd's novel. It had the
same title."

"So I heard."

"They rejected mine."

The final word broke. She stopped.

"Oh, father, everybody knows! Everybody! They'll think I lied. And
I neglected everything, and mamma told everyone, and the *Clarion*—"

Doctor Dace opened the umbrella, though the rain was nearly
over. Its dark circle shut them away from the road.

"No one who matters will think you lied."

"You will."

"I do not."

"You made fun of me."

"Yes," he said. "I make fun of most things. It
is an inexpensive vice."

She tried to laugh and instead made a small, desolate
sound.

He shifted the manuscript to his other arm. "When I
was twenty-two, I wrote a novel."

Theodora forgot to cry.

"You?"

"A very long novel. Longer than this one, I believe.
The hero was a medical student with black hair and
an intolerable nobility of character. He discovered a cure for
consumption, exposed a dishonest professor, and married a countess."

"Father!"

"The countess had misunderstood him for three hundred pages. She
was not a clever woman."

The darkness concealed his face, but she heard something in
his voice she had never heard before—not amusement exactly, and
not shame, but the tenderness with which one may speak
of a dead absurdity that was once very dear.

"What happened to it?"

"It was rejected by seven publishers. The eighth lost it.
At the time I considered that the deepest insult."

"Did anyone know?"

"My father knew. He advised me to stick to medicine.
I thought him a brute."

Theodora looked down at the shining road.

"Were you—very unhappy?"

"My dear, I believed the world had refused its greatest
physician, novelist, and social reformer in one envelope."

This time she did laugh, though tears ran warm beneath
the rain upon her cheeks.

Doctor Dace continued quietly. "For three days I did not
eat. For six months I despised literature. For ten years
I could not pass a publishing office without wishing it
burned. Then I became occupied with other catastrophes."

"Why did you never tell me?"

"You never needed to know."

"And now?"

"Now you do."

They walked on. Theodora's shoulder touched his sleeve. He did
not draw away.

"Was it very bad?" she asked.

"The novel?"

"Yes."

"Remarkably."

She gave a wounded exclamation.

"But I was not a fool to write it," he
added. "Only young. There is a difference, although it is
seldom visible to the young."

The houses of Norton appeared ahead, blurred and golden in
the mist. Somewhere within them Miss Brill was wondering; Uncle
James was denouncing editorial administration; Mrs. Dace was listening for
wheels. Tomorrow there would be explanations. The paragraph in the
*Clarion* could not be unwritten. Johnny's buttons would still be
loose, the cook still absent, the cod-liver oil still abominable.

Nothing had been redeemed. No royalties would descend in a
shower of gold. Gladys Glyn had not awakened the nation.

Yet the manuscript no longer seemed quite so heavy beneath
her father's arm.

"Do you think I ought to burn it?" she whispered.

"No."

"Send it elsewhere?"

"Not tomorrow."

"What shall I do tomorrow?"

Doctor Dace considered.

"You might mend Johnny's jacket."

Theodora drew away indignantly; then she felt his hand close
over hers.

"And after that," he said, "if you still wish to
write, write something better."

The plain words entered the place where the editor's polished
phrases had wounded her. There was no grandeur in them,
no consecrated sorrow, no star bending from heaven to recognise
genius. Her father, who could never be counted on to
behave like a father in a book, had offered neither
prophecy nor praise.

He had offered companionship.

"Father—O father!"

She turned to him, and for a moment the rejected
novel was crushed between them. Doctor Dace kissed her wet
hair.

"Come," he said. "Your mother is waiting."

Together they went on through the kind darkness toward home.

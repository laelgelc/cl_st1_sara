The lilacs had come early that year.

For three mornings the flower-women had stood at the corners
of the Boulevard des Capucines with their baskets heaped in
mauve and white; and for three mornings Adrienne Farival had
passed them without appearing to see them. She was driven
from rehearsal to the costumier's, from the costumier's to a
luncheon at which she ate nothing, and thence to the
theatre, where twenty persons waited upon her pleasure and declared
themselves ruined whenever she was five minutes late. Paris had
engulfed her.

On the fourth morning a ragged child thrust a branch
almost beneath the horses' noses. The coachman swore, the child
laughed, and the broken blossoms fell against the carriage step.
Their perfume rose.

Adrienne put out both hands.

"Stop, Baptiste! Stop at once!"

The brougham drew up so sharply that Mme. Farival's maid,
seated opposite her, was thrown against a lacquered box containing
a new hat.

"Madame!" cried Sophie. "One would say we were being murdered."

But Adrienne had lowered the glass.

"Give me all of them," she called to the child.

"All, madame?"

"All. The basket too. Here—no, take this."

The child stared at the gold piece in her palm,
then ran alongside the carriage, loading Adrienne's arms with cool
branches, damp leaves, and loose stars of bloom. In an
instant the dove-grey satin of the brougham was strewn with
petals. Sophie rescued the hat-box and began a lament upon
stains, insects, and the singularity of buying a basket when
one possessed three silver flower-stands at home.

Adrienne did not hear her. She had buried her face
in the lilacs.

The boulevard, with its wheels, cries, shining windows, its little
yellow clouds of dust and its procession of impatient people,
dissolved. She saw a long white wall warmed by spring
sunshine; above it, the dark points of cypresses; beyond, a
square bell-tower and a roof upon which pigeons moved with
pink feet. She heard running water, bees among the thyme,
the subdued clatter of plates in a distant kitchen. The
air was clean and spacious. Somewhere a bell sounded the
angelus.

She was eighteen again, and Sister Agathe was saying, "Come
quickly, my child, or the Mother will believe we have
both flown over the wall."

Adrienne lifted her head. Her face, which the newspapers called
mischievous, voluptuous, irresistible, had become grave.

"Sophie, we are going home."

"Home? But madame's rehearsal—"

"Home."

"And M. Valroy is to call at twelve. And the
photographer at one. And there is the fitting for the
second act. And tonight—"

"I shall not play tonight."

Sophie gave a shriek.

She was a small, competent woman of forty-five who had
served Adrienne since the latter's marriage and considered every interruption
of established disorder a personal affront. She knew the names
of all madame's creditors, admirers, physicians, dressmakers, enemies, and dogs;
she knew which jewels were gifts and which were paid
for; she knew at what hour Adrienne must be awakened
after supper and at what hour it was useless to
attempt it. But there was one matter which remained outside
her intelligence, and because it did so she called it
insanity.

"Not play! Madame cannot say such things as if she
were ordering coffee. The theatre will send policemen."

"Let it send the army."

"M. Lemaître will tear out what little hair Heaven has
left him."

"He will look better."

"And M. Valroy?"

Adrienne bent again over the blossoms.

"M. Valroy will console himself."

At the apartment in the Rue de Rivoli, confusion already
reigned. Trunks came from cupboards; gowns were carried from room
to room; a lapdog barked at the basket of lilacs;
two visiting cards arrived on a silver tray; a milliner's
assistant waited in the antechamber with green feathers; and the
cook, informed that madame was departing before luncheon, demanded to
know whether a salmon costing eighteen francs was to be
thrown from the window.

Adrienne moved through it all swiftly, giving contradictory orders with
perfect serenity.

"No jewels. The brown dress. Two chemises, three pairs of
stockings, my black shawl. No perfumes. Take those roses away—they
smell of a dining-room. Sophie, where is my old prayer-book?"

"Madame has not opened it since last spring."

"That is no answer."

"It is in the escritoire, beneath the letters tied with
blue ribbon."

"Burn the letters."

"All of them?"

"All—no, not those from my aunt. Put the rest aside.
Where is the silver lamp?"

"The lamp from St. Eloi?"

"Yes, and the lace for the altar. Pack them separately.
Gently, Sophie! It is point d'Alençon."

Sophie planted herself in the middle of the bedchamber, a
pair of shoes in one hand and a travelling cloak
in the other.

"Where is madame going?"

Adrienne paused, her eyes bright.

"To ma tante."

Now Sophie knew that Mme. Farival possessed no living aunt.
There had once been a stout relation at Rouen who
wore purple mittens and left Adrienne six thousand francs, but
she had been dead for seven years. The answer therefore
confirmed everything.

"It is the insanity," Sophie announced.

"What insanity?"

"The spring insanity. Every year, when the lilacs appear, madame
vanishes. No address. No letters. Nothing. Last year M. Lemaître
sent telegrams to Nice, Brussels, Geneva, and even Vienna. The
year before, M. le Comte thought madame had gone to
Spain with the Russian. The Russian thought madame had gone
with M. le Comte. They nearly fought in the Bois,
and madame was—where? With ma tante!"

Adrienne laughed, caught Sophie by the shoulders, and kissed her
loudly on both cheeks.

"You are an impertinent bonne, and I adore you. I
shall return in a fortnight."

"A fortnight! And if the theatre closes?"

"It will reopen."

"If M. Valroy kills himself?"

"He will miss."

"If madame falls ill?"

"I shall be well."

She was gone before three, wearing the brown wool dress,
a little black bonnet, and gloves so plain that Sophie
regarded them as a disguise. Yet there was no deception
in Adrienne's heart. She left Paris with the relief of
one who has escaped from a room overheated by flowers
and breath. Every turn of the wheels seemed to loosen
some knot within her: the applause, the lamps, the supper
tables, the quarrels conducted beneath silk canopies, the feverish notes
pushed under doors, the diamonds clasped by impatient hands about
her throat. Houses grew fewer; fields opened; poplars passed in
pale green files; and in the ditches the new grass
shone under the afternoon sun.

From the station she hired the same ancient cab she
had hired for five springs. The driver recognized her.

"To the convent, madame?"

"To the convent, Père Louis. But slowly when we reach
the hill."

He touched his hat. Beside Adrienne lay the basket of
lilacs, the silver lamp, the folded altar lace, six wax
candles, two painted porcelain bénitiers, and a parcel of books
for the school. She guarded them with maternal anxiety whenever
the cab lurched.

At the turn of the hill the white wall appeared.

Adrienne leaned forward. There were the cypresses, the pigeons, the
square bell-tower. The pear trees in the convent orchard were
flowering, and behind the wall a magnolia held up its
great waxen cups. Nothing had changed. This immobility, which might
have oppressed another, filled her with tenderness. In Paris a
fashion was old in six weeks, a lover in six
months, a reputation in six hours. Here the cracked saint
still watched above the gate, the bell still gave the
same thin summons, and perhaps Sister Agathe still kept a
lump of sugar in her pocket for the red convent
cat.

Adrienne jumped down before Père Louis could arrange the step.

The gate was opened by a lay sister with broad
red hands.

"Mme. Farival!"

"Yes, Sister Angélique. And I have brought half Paris with
me."

The sister smiled, showing three brave teeth.

"Mother! Mother! It is our lady from Paris!"

Then the commotion began.

Two novices appeared from the laundry passage; the old porteress
came hobbling from her lodge; a pupil peeped round the
cloister column and was immediately recalled. Questions, blessings, exclamations passed
beneath the archway in restrained but delighted murmurs. Adrienne embraced
everybody she was permitted to embrace and kissed Sister Angélique
twice, because the sister's hands were occupied with parcels.

"And Sister Agathe?" she demanded.

"She is in the garden."

Adrienne did not wait.

She crossed the little court, ran under the cloister, and
went through the door near the sacristy. The garden lay
in a green hush. Box borders divided the beds; wallflowers
burned brown and gold beneath the chapel windows; a narrow
stream, brought by a stone channel from the hill, slipped
under three tiny bridges. At the farther end a nun
in a cream-coloured habit stood tying young rose branches against
a trellis.

"Ma tante!"

The nun turned.

She was past sixty, small and a little bent, with
a face whose fine transparent skin seemed polished by years
of quietness. Beneath her white coif, the brows remained dark
and delicate. For one second she looked not old, not
conventual, but simply astonished; then joy moved over her features
like sunlight.

"Adrienne!"

The young woman ran to her, scattering lilacs. Sister Agathe
received her with a cry and folded her close. The
basket came between them; branches were crushed against the nun's
scapular, and purple florets clung to her breast.

"My child—my dear child—gently! You will overthrow me."

"I have wanted you for a year."

"And I have prayed for you every day."

"That is why I have come. You prayed too loudly."

Sister Agathe drew back to look at her. Her hands,
narrow and dry, rested on Adrienne's shoulders.

"You are thinner."

"I am always thinner according to you."

"And pale."

"The same accusation."

"Are you happy?"

Adrienne's laughter came a moment late.

"I am here."

For Sister Agathe this sufficed, or she pretended it did.
She took the basket, protesting that it was too heavy,
while Adrienne insisted upon carrying the pruning knife and ball
of twine. Together they walked toward the house, pausing every
few steps because Adrienne must admire the narcissus, inquire after
the bees, ask whether the old fig tree had survived
the frost, and mourn the death of a white hen
remembered from the previous spring.

The Mother Superior received her in a parlour so white
and polished that the silver crucifix above the table seemed
the only dark object in it. She was a tall
woman with smooth cheeks, an untroubled forehead, and eyes trained
to discover both dust and spiritual weakness. Yet she welcomed
Adrienne kindly.

"We expected you when the lilacs opened."

"Then I am punctual, ma Mère."

"You bring us spring, and, I fear, many unnecessary things."

"Entirely necessary. A lamp for the Sacré-Coeur, lace for the
altar, bénitiers for the little oratory, candles, books, and two
jars of preserves which Sophie says will destroy the Rule."

"The Rule has survived preserves before."

This mild pleasantry delighted Adrienne. She knelt impulsively, took the
Mother's hand, and kissed the ring.

"You will keep me?"

"For the usual fortnight, if your affairs permit."

"My affairs never permit anything. I permit my affairs."

The Mother Superior smiled, though less readily than Sister Agathe.
She asked after Adrienne's health and whether Paris had been
quiet. Adrienne replied that Paris had never been quiet since
the Romans misplaced it. No further questions were put. The
convent knew her as Mme. Farival, widow of a respectable
official, former pupil, benefactress, and orphan of an old provincial
family. That she possessed a cultivated contralto voice was known
because she sang at Benediction. That the cultivation of this
voice had led elsewhere was not discussed. In the still
parlour, beneath the gaze of a plaster St. Joseph, Adrienne's
other existence appeared not sinful, nor innocent, but impossible.

Her room was the one she had occupied as a
schoolgirl: a narrow chamber overlooking the orchard, with a white
bed, a washstand, a chair of black wood, and a
little print of the Virgin pierced at one corner by
a rusted pin. A jug of lilacs stood on the
sill.

Adrienne stopped at the threshold.

"Who put them there?"

"I did," said Sister Agathe.

"You remembered."

"Could I forget?"

Adrienne entered softly. She touched the counterpane, opened the wardrobe,
looked beneath the bed as though expecting to find her
childhood hidden there. On the inner side of the shutter
remained a faint scratched A, made with a compass when
she was fourteen.

"Sister, did you never report me for this?"

"I intended to."

"For twenty years?"

"I have been considering the proper punishment."

Adrienne seated herself on the bed and drew the nun
down beside her. The mattress yielded with a small remembered
sigh.

"Punish me now."

"You must first confess the offence."

"I scratched my initial there after Mother Céleste said that
vanity leaves marks upon everything it touches."

"Then you proved her right."

"I wished to leave one mark that would last."

Sister Agathe glanced at the letter on the shutter and
then at Adrienne's restless, beautiful face.

"It has lasted," she said.

The days assumed the convent's shape. At five the bell
passed through Adrienne's sleep like a silver thread; at six
she heard the subdued movement of habits along the corridor;
at seven came Mass, cold flagstones beneath the knees, wax
and incense in the air, daylight whitening the chapel windows.
Afterward there was coffee in a blue bowl, bread, a
spoonful of plum preserve, and Sister Angélique's anxious hospitality.

Adrienne helped where she could and hindered where she could
not. She shelled peas in the kitchen and ate so
many that the cook threatened to count them. She read
aloud to the older pupils, repaired a torn veil badly,
gathered eggs, polished the new lamp, and spent an entire
morning arranging the altar lace, though the sacristan rearranged it
when she had gone. She walked with Sister Agathe under
the pear trees. She sat by the stream and allowed
the sun to warm her hands.

No one applauded. No one desired anything from her except
that she pass the bread or hold a skein of
wool. The great machinery of charm stood idle.

At first the quiet left small spaces into which Paris
attempted to enter. She would remember that a scene had
been changed, that a note remained unanswered, that Valroy had
looked at her strangely during supper. She imagined Lemaître storming
behind the curtain, Sophie accusing Providence, the newspapers making a
paragraph of her absence. Then a bird would descend to
the gravel, or the chapel bell would sound, and the
anxious thought went from her like smoke.

One afternoon she and Sister Agathe sat beneath the lilac
hedge. The nun was mending an altar cloth; Adrienne lay
on the grass with her cheek against one hand.

"Did you ever wish to leave?" Adrienne asked.

Sister Agathe's needle paused.

"When I was young, perhaps."

"For what?"

"For many things, because I knew nothing of them."

"A husband?"

"Possibly."

"Children?"

"Yes."

Adrienne plucked a blade of grass.

"And now?"

"Now those wishes are like persons seen from a train.
One wonders where they were going, but one does not
follow."

"I follow everybody," said Adrienne. "That is my misfortune."

"My child—"

"Oh, do not look so frightened. I have committed no
murder. I am merely foolish, often late, sometimes vain, frequently
selfish, and always hungry after midnight."

Sister Agathe resumed her stitching, but a little colour had
risen in her cheeks.

"You have a good heart."

"That is what people say when the rest cannot be
praised."

"I praise what I know."

Adrienne turned on her back and looked up through the
leaves.

"You know me better than anyone."

The nun's hands trembled almost imperceptibly. "I knew a child
who hid novels in her arithmetic book, gave her shoes
to a beggar, and accused the bishop's canary of blasphemy."

"It had a dreadful vocabulary."

"I know a woman who remembers old friends and brings
beauty to the chapel."

"You know the part I bring here."

Sister Agathe bent lower over her sewing.

"Are there other parts?"

"Hundreds. Paris requires a new Adrienne for every caller. The
public has one; my manager has another; Sophie keeps three
or four in cupboards; gentlemen invent their own and become
furious when I do not resemble her. But this one—"
She pressed both hands against the cool grass. "This one
belongs here."

Sister Agathe laid the cloth aside and made the sign
of the cross upon Adrienne's forehead.

"Then let her rest."

On Sunday Adrienne sang.

Her voice, which in Paris made men lean forward beneath
the gaslight and women lower their opera-glasses, rose in the
little chapel without ornament. It was not a large voice
there. The white walls chastened it; the grille divided it;
the nuns' thin responses received and absorbed it. She sang
the Ave Maria with her eyes on the small red
lamp before the Sacré-Coeur, and Sister Agathe, hidden in her
stall, wept quietly.

The fortnight passed, as happy fortnights do, with treachery.

On the last evening Adrienne wandered through each familiar place
as if laying claim to it: the kitchen garden, the
schoolroom smelling of ink and soap, the refectory with its
long pale tables, the chapel where dusk had turned the
windows blue. She filled both bénitiers herself and laughed when
Sister Angélique scolded her for spilling water.

At bedtime Sister Agathe came to her room.

"You will return next spring?" she asked.

"When the lilacs call me."

"You might come sooner."

"No. If I came whenever I was unhappy, you would
have me every month; then every week; then you would
tire of me and set me to scrubbing corridors."

"That might improve you."

"I am beyond improvement."

She said it lightly, but when Sister Agathe turned to
go, Adrienne caught her hand.

"Suppose I did not return?"

"I should pray that you were detained by happiness."

"And if I were detained by wickedness?"

"I should pray more."

Adrienne held the hand against her cheek.

"Never believe too much of what you hear about me."

"I hear nothing."

"Then never ask."

Sister Agathe looked down at her. A question moved in
her eyes and was renounced.

"Sleep, my child."

In the morning the whole house seemed to accompany Adrienne
to the gate. Parcels had multiplied: eggs, preserves, a little
medal, a jar of honey, and a bunch of narcissus
which Sister Angélique insisted would survive the journey. Sister Agathe
gave her the lilacs last.

They embraced carefully at first, then desperately. Blossoms were crushed
between them, and the clean wool of the habit took
their perfume.

"A year," Adrienne whispered. "Only a year."

The gate closed after her with a firm domestic sound.

By evening she was in Paris.

Her apartment had the appearance of a place recently searched
by police. Newspapers lay on chairs; flowers crowded the tables;
unopened letters filled a silver bowl and overflowed upon the
carpet. A rose-coloured costume hung across the piano. Cigarette ash
marked the rim of a Sèvres cup. In the salon
M. Lemaître walked up and down, Sophie argued with Baptiste,
the cook announced that she had resigned, and two gentlemen
glared at one another from opposite sides of the fireplace.

When Adrienne entered, carrying narcissus and convent honey, all voices
stopped.

"Yes," she said, "Adrienne is at home."

And then the commotion began.

Lemaître seized his head. Sophie seized the parcels. The younger
gentleman, M. Valroy, seized Adrienne's hands and demanded where she
had been, why she had not written, whether she had
been ill, abducted, imprisoned, or merely cruel. The older gentleman
bowed, said that he had never doubted madame's return, and
knocked over a vase.

Adrienne kissed Sophie, ignored Lemaître, freed one hand from Valroy,
and looked about her with amused weariness.

"Ma foi, what a noise! Has no one in Paris
learned to whisper?"

"Where were you?" cried Valroy.

"With ma tante."

"You have no aunt."

"Then she cannot compromise me."

At the theatre her absence had become betrayal, mystery, advertisement,
catastrophe, and triumph. The first evening of her return, people
stood in the aisles. She appeared beneath a painted arbor
wearing silver gauze, and the house applauded before she had
spoken. The calm of the convent remained in her for
a little while, lending softness to a gesture and an
unfamiliar gravity to a song. Critics praised the new depth
of Mme. Farival's art. Lemaître congratulated himself upon having planned
her retreat.

Then Paris closed over her.

There were rehearsals, suppers, roses, creditors, journeys to the Bois,
quarrels and reconciliations, summer heat, autumn premieres, winter balls. Valroy
was jealous, forgiven, jealous again. Another admirer sent a bracelet.
Sophie declared the bracelet vulgar and locked it away. Adrienne
changed apartments, changed roles, changed horses, changed the colour of
her drawing-room, and did not change her habits. Once, opening
a drawer, she found Sister Agathe's medal among theatre bills
and dried violets. She kissed it and put it aside
where it would be safe.

At the convent, the silver lamp burned.

A year passed.

The next spring was late. Rain washed Paris for weeks,
leaving the pavements black and the chestnut buds tightly furled.
Adrienne grew impatient without knowing why. She slept badly; she
quarrelled with Lemaître over a costume, with Sophie over nothing,
with Valroy because he asked what troubled her. The city
seemed all walls, wheels, wet wool, and stale tobacco.

Then, one evening after rehearsal, she entered her dressing-room and
stopped.

A bouquet of lilacs stood before the mirror.

No card accompanied it. The flowers had been placed among
pots of greasepaint, hairpins, powder, pearls, and the little blue
jar in which she drowned spent matches. Their scent rose
through the heated room.

Adrienne's face changed.

"Sophie!"

The maid appeared with a mouthful of pins.

"Pack the brown dress."

The pins fell into her palm.

"No."

"What?"

"No, madame. I say no. Last year there was scandal.
M. Lemaître lost seven pounds. M. Valroy threatened to shoot
Baptiste because Baptiste knew no more than he did. The
cook joined a spiritualist society to discover whether madame was
alive. This year we remain in Paris."

"We leave tomorrow morning."

"Madame opens on Thursday."

"Then Thursday must wait."

"Thursday has sold every seat."

"It will be crowded while it waits."

Sophie followed her into the bedchamber, protesting at each step.
The annual insanity had returned; the unknown aunt had stretched
out her spectral hand; reason was useless; gratitude was dead.
Adrienne, laughing, opened cupboards herself.

This time the gifts were more splendid. There was a
gilt reliquary from an old church near Chartres, a new
linen frontal embroidered with lilies, and four tall candlesticks chased
with vines. Adrienne had chosen them during the winter without
admitting, even to herself, for whom they were intended. She
added books, chocolate, coffee, and a length of soft grey
wool for Sister Agathe.

Valroy arrived before the trunks were closed.

"You are leaving me again," he said.

He was handsome in the careful, melancholy fashion which had
first amused her. Now his unhappiness only delayed the train.

"For two weeks."

"Where?"

"My dear, you know I never answer that question."

"Is it a man?"

"No."

"A woman?"

"Yes."

He stared.

Adrienne burst out laughing and kissed his forehead.

"She is sixty-two and wears serge."

But he did not laugh. "You keep one chamber of
your life locked against me."

"Yes," she said, suddenly serious. "Because it is the only
chamber you gentlemen have not overturned."

She left him among the open trunks.

The journey seemed brighter than before. The rain had ceased;
every hedge glittered; lambs stood in the fields with absurdly
clean legs. Adrienne bought lilacs at the little station, adding
them to those already in the carriage, until the compartment
seemed to move inside a flowering bush. She counted the
stations, then the milestones from Père Louis's cab.

"To the convent, madame?" he had asked again.

"To the convent. Quickly this time."

At the hill she saw the white wall and clasped
her hands. The cypresses rose above it; pigeons wheeled near
the bell-tower. She imagined Sister Angélique calling through the court.
She imagined Sister Agathe in the garden, turning with that
slow astonishment which joy made young.

Adrienne descended, gathered the lilacs in her arms, and mounted
the shallow step.

She rang.

No one came.

She rang again, smiling at the delay. Perhaps it was
the hour of Office. Perhaps Sister Angélique's brave three teeth
had at last required the dentist. She listened for footsteps.

At length a narrow panel opened.

The porteress's eyes appeared behind the grille.

"Good afternoon, Sister. It is I."

The eyes lowered.

"Wait, madame."

The panel shut.

Adrienne stood amid her parcels. The sun warmed the white
wall. Bees moved in the lilacs. Inside, a bell sounded
once, and the silence following it seemed deliberate.

The small door opened—not the broad gate through which the
cab had passed the year before. Sister Angélique emerged sideways
and immediately closed it behind her. She looked older. Her
red hands were folded beneath her apron.

"Why, Sister, have you forgotten me?"

"No, madame."

"Then embrace me."

The lay sister did not move.

Adrienne's smile faded.

"What is it?"

"I am to give you this."

She produced a sealed letter.

Behind her, bolts had been drawn across the door. Adrienne
heard them. One, then another.

She stared at the envelope. The Mother Superior's name was
written upon it in a regular hand.

"Is ma Mère ill?"

"No, madame."

"Sister Agathe?"

"No."

"Then open the gate."

"I cannot."

Adrienne looked past her at the white door, the cracked
saint, the strip of sky above the wall. Her arms
were full of lilacs.

"Tell them I have come."

"They know."

The words were scarcely audible.

Adrienne broke the seal.

The letter was brief, formal, and exact. The Mother Superior
had learned, through persons whose authority could not be doubted,
circumstances concerning Mme. Farival's public life and private conduct which
rendered her further residence beneath the convent roof impossible. Gratitude
for past generosity did not permit compromise. The house, entrusted
with the formation of young souls, must avoid even the
appearance of sanctioning scandal. Prayers would be offered. Visits must
cease. Gifts recently delivered, or now presented, could not be
retained.

Adrienne read it twice.

The phrases remained beautifully legible. Public life. Private conduct. Appearance
of sanction. Prayers would be offered.

She lifted her head.

"Who told her?"

Sister Angélique's lips moved, but no answer came.

"Was it the curé? A parent? Some excellent lady from
Paris who sits in a box and applauds me, then
washes her gloves after speaking my name?"

"Madame, I do not know."

"I must see the Mother."

"It is forbidden."

"I must see Sister Agathe."

At that name the lay sister winced.

"Forbidden."

Adrienne stepped toward the door. Sister Angélique placed herself before
it.

For one instant the actress returned: the woman whose anger
could stop a rehearsal, whose lifted chin had conquered hostile
audiences, whose tears—when commanded—were visible from the gallery.

"Open."

"I cannot."

"Do you understand me? Open this door."

"The Mother has the key."

"Then fetch her."

"She will not come."

Adrienne's authority fell from her. There remained only a woman
in a brown dress, dusty from travel, holding too many
flowers.

"Tell Agathe I am here," she whispered. "Only that. She
will come."

Sister Angélique's eyes filled.

"She cannot."

"Cannot—or will not?"

No answer.

Père Louis and the cab waited down the road. A
bird hopped along the top of the wall. From beyond
it came the thin splashing of the channel in the
garden, cheerful and unchanged.

The little door opened once more. Two novices set out
the parcels Adrienne had sent ahead: the reliquary, the embroidered
frontal, the candlesticks, the books. They did not look at
her. The door closed. The key turned.

Adrienne put the Mother Superior's letter into her pocket with
great care.

Then she knocked.

At first she used her gloved knuckles. Afterward she struck
with the flat of her hand.

"Agathe!"

No sound answered.

"Ma tante!"

The white wall gave back the cry, diminished.

She laid her forehead against the door. Lilac branches slipped
from her arms and fell upon the step. She did
not notice them.

"I was the same last year," she said. "Do you
hear? I was the same."

The convent remained silent.

At last Adrienne wept—not gracefully, not with the shining tears
the public admired, but with her mouth drawn and her
shoulders shaken, like the abandoned schoolgirl who had once scratched
her initial upon a shutter because she feared that nothing
of her would last. No one came to comfort her.

Père Louis approached slowly, hat in hand.

"Madame?"

She turned away from the door.

"Put the things in the cab."

"All of them?"

"All."

He gathered the rejected gifts. Adrienne bent to lift the
lilacs, but her hand stopped before touching them.

"No," she said. "Leave those."

She entered the cab without looking back.

Inside the convent, in the narrow white room overlooking the
orchard, Sister Agathe lay across the bed which Adrienne had
used. Her face was buried in the counterpane; both hands
pressed against her mouth to imprison the sound of her
sobbing. Upon the inner shutter the scratched A remained, pale
and childish beneath the rusted pin.

On the table lay the grey wool Adrienne had sent
before the Mother's prohibition reached the gate. Sister Agathe had
touched it once. She would not touch it again.

Below, Sister Angélique waited until the cab could no longer
be heard. Then she fetched a broom.

The lilacs lay across the threshold, bruised by Adrienne's knees,
their leaves dulled with dust, their perfume rising in the
afternoon warmth. One cluster had been caught beneath the locked
door.

Sister Angélique swept them away.

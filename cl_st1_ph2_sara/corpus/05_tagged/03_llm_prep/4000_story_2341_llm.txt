THE MORAL is out of fashion, I know. It went
out with antimacassars, improving recitations, and those pictures of little
girls crossing broken bridges in thunderstorms; but I have always
thought it might be brought back in a plain working
coat, without ribbons, and made useful again.

Opposite my balcony stood a row of houses called Mercy
Terrace, though mercy had plainly passed along the other side
of the street. In the hard afternoon light the place
looked like a jawbone dug from a paddock—six yellowish houses,
six black doorways, and windows that stared without seeing. Two
panes were patched with brown paper. One had a flour-sack
curtain printed BLUE ANCHOR BRAND, upside down. Another displayed half
a lace curtain, as though respectability had started there and
lost courage.

People were always moving into Mercy Terrace and always moving
out. They came with bedding tied in rope, kerosene tins,
perambulators containing everything except babies, and vans of the "bottle
ho" variety. A proper furniture van never stopped there. It
would have felt overdressed.

On moving-in day there was confidence. The woman scrubbed the
step; the man carried a chair as if it were
a throne; the children quarrelled over which corner belonged to
whom. By the third week a sheet appeared in the
window. By the fifth the rent collector appeared at the
door. Then, generally at dusk, the bedding went back into
the rope.

I used to watch these proceedings because I am deaf,
and a deaf man becomes a great observer of doors.
He cannot hear a knock, but he notices the instant
tightening of a woman's shoulders when one comes. He cannot
hear the rent collector's boots, but he sees curtains move
in houses where no one is supposed to be home.
Mercy Terrace was full of little betrayals of that kind—candlelight
under a door, a child's hand withdrawn from a window,
smoke rising from a chimney whose tenant had declared the
place empty.

This has nothing whatever to do with my bath.

At least, I thought so at the time.

The Saturday in question was hot enough to soften putty
on the glass. I had been improving the house I
rented, an occupation which landlords admire chiefly when they are
not paying for it. The back room had a rotten
strip of matchboard, the washhouse door hung down like a
tired eyelid, and the fence possessed that free, independent motion
usually associated with seaweed.

My landlady said the repairs were unnecessary.

"Nothing has fallen off yet," she said.

I did not hear her, naturally, but she repeated it
on a slate she kept for conversation with me. She
was a considerate woman, though she wrote in very large
letters, as if deafness were a failure of eyesight.

I replaced the matchboard, rehung the door, and put two
new rails into the fence. Then, finding a tin of
colour that had not entirely turned to pudding, I painted
the washhouse. It was not a beautiful colour. It had
begun life as green, had been mixed at some period
with cream, and now resembled soup remembered from childhood. Still,
it covered.

Labours of love.

There is pure and unadulterated joy in driving a nail
straight, especially after living among people who put a three-inch
nail where a screw ought to go and bend it
over at the back for security. I worked through the
full glare of the day, stopping only to drink water
from the kitchen tap and to admire, from different angles,
the improvements for which I should receive no reduction in
rent.

By six o'clock I was striped with paint, sawdust, sweat,
rust, and something black from beneath the washhouse that I
did not investigate. My shirt could have stood in a
corner by itself. My hands were not hands but exhibits.
A wash at the basin would have been an insult.
I determined upon a bath.

Not merely a bath, you understand. A bath properly conducted.

The bathroom was a lean-to affair of galvanized iron, containing
a tin tub, three draughts, and a spider of unusual
confidence. Cold water came through a pipe. Hot water had
to be heated in the copper and carried in buckets.
This discourages frivolous bathing, but gives serious bathing the dignity
of an engineering project.

I lit the copper fire with shavings from the matchboard.
My landlady objected to this.

"Those pieces might do for kindling," she wrote.

"They are doing for kindling," I replied.

She meant on some later, more deserving occasion. She stored
scraps of wood with religious devotion and would save a
broken clothes-peg from a burning house.

While the copper heated, I swept the bathroom and shook
out the canvas bathing-cover I had designed myself. It fastened
over the tub by cords and trapped the steam, leaving
only the bather's head exposed. A Turkish bath for the
independent working man. I had improved it since its first
trial with a short length of gas-pipe across the head
end, supporting a folded towel so the neck rested comfortably
and the skull did not knock against tin.

I tested the pipe. Firm.

I tightened the cords. Sound.

I hung a bath towel within reach, then moved it
farther from the wall because galvanized iron has a peculiar
gift for making a clean towel dirty. Beside it I
placed a smaller towel for the face, because a man
who wipes his face on the towel used for his
feet may call himself practical, but he is merely impatient.

Next came soap, nailbrush, pumice stone, toothbrush, tooth powder, comb,
and the little knife with which I cleaned paint from
beneath my nails. I set out clean underclothes, shirt, trousers,
socks, and slippers in the adjoining room, arranged in the
order required. There is no pleasure in stepping from a
hot bath and searching, naked and steaming, for the second
sock.

I then considered intellectual refreshment.

A newspaper would collapse in the damp. A book was
better, but not a valuable one. I chose an old
volume of travel in Central Africa, its binding already beyond
injury. There is something cooling in reading about a man
short of water. I placed it on a fruit box
beside the tub, together with tobacco, papers, matches sealed in
a jam tin, and an ashtray made from the lid
of a boot-polish tin.

The coffee required thought. If prepared too soon, it would
cool; too late, and the bath would wait. I ground
the beans—not because I was luxurious, but because someone had
given me beans and I had no wish to waste
them—set the pot on the stove, cut bread, toasted it,
buttered it, and covered it with a plate. I added
two slices of cold mutton and a little pickle. Bathing
produces an appetite, and appetite, like rent, should be anticipated.

By then the copper was boiling. Steam lifted from it
in splendid white rolls.

I carried the first bucket along the passage. Boiling water
has a malicious intelligence. It waits until a man is
passing a polished doorpost, then leaps upon his trousers. I
moved slowly, one hand on the handle and the other
beneath it with a folded rag. Into the tub it
went, striking the tin with a grand, hollow crash which
I felt through the floorboards.

The second bucket followed.

Then the third.

Between journeys I fed the copper, because three buckets were
not enough. The tub was long, and I am not
short. A cramped bath leaves the knees cold, which divides
the body against itself.

My landlady appeared and wrote, "YOU WILL DROWN."

I laughed. She looked hurt, rubbed out DROWN, and wrote,
"YOU WILL SCALD YOURSELF."

I pointed to the cold tap. She departed unconvinced, taking
with her two unburned shavings from beside the copper.

I carried bucket after bucket. The heat in the passage
was terrific; my dirty shirt clung to me; my arms
began to tremble. Yet the anticipation was exquisite. Already I
could imagine the steam closing around me, the canvas holding
the warmth, the coffee at my elbow, Africa open upon
my knees, my repaired fence standing firm in the night.

At last I judged there was sufficient hot water. I
turned on the cold tap for a measured time, testing
the stream with my wrist. A bath should be hot
enough to make entry a negotiation, but not so hot
that a man comes out the colour of corned beef.

I shut the tap.

I carried in the coffee, toast, mutton, and pickle. I
adjusted the lamp. I opened the book at a chapter
called "Disaster at the Salt Marsh." Too ominous, I thought,
and turned instead to "Among Friendly Tribes." I rolled a
cigarette in advance. I checked the clean clothes. I tested
the gas-pipe once more.

Everything was ready.

I undressed in the kitchen because the bathroom floor was
wet—why it was so wet did not occur to me—and
left my filthy clothes in a heap. Then I walked
in, lifted the canvas, and put one foot into the
tub.

My foot struck cold tin.

There was no water.

For several seconds I stood there with one leg in
the bath and one leg out, an attitude neither dignified
nor safe. I looked at the tub. I looked at
the wet floor. I looked at the little dark hole
near my toes.

I'd forgotten to put in the plug.

All that water—the copperfuls, the buckets, the measured cold, the
heat I had carried so carefully through the house—had passed
straight down the pipe and into the yard. Outside, beneath
the bathroom, a flourishing stream ran among the weeds. The
spider alone had benefited.

I could have begun again. There was wood, though my
landlady would have disputed it. There was water. There was
still an hour before bedtime.

But the spirit had gone out of the undertaking. That
is a real thing, the spirit of an undertaking. You
cannot relight it with matchboard.

I stood looking at the soap, the brush, the tooth
powder, the towels, the book, the tobacco protected in its
jam tin, the coffee cooling beside the toast. Each object
had become an accusation. Even the friendly tribes seemed sarcastic.

My landlady came to the doorway. She saw the empty
tub, the water creeping across the floor, and me standing
there in the altogether. Her face expressed such enormous sympathy
that I resented her more than if she had laughed.

She wrote nothing.

I put on my dirty clothes again. That was the
worst of it. Paint, rust, sweat, and the black substance
from under the washhouse—all resumed their former positions. I drank
the coffee standing up, ate half the toast without tasting
it, stumbled to my room, and lay on the bed
fully dressed.

I slept the sleep of utter defeated weariness.

In the morning I went onto the balcony. Mercy Terrace
was pale under the early sun. At Number Four, a
family was moving out. The father carried a rolled mattress;
the mother held a saucepan and a cage without a
bird. Their little boy dragged a wooden horse with only
three wheels. They had arrived a month before with curtains,
a clock, and two geraniums in tins. The geraniums were
dead now. The clock had vanished earlier in the week.

The man paused and looked back at the house with
an expression I understood too well.

People say such families are careless. Sometimes they are. So
am I. They say the poor do not plan. But
I have seen them plan down to the last shilling:
rent here, food there, boots postponed, medicine halved, firewood borrowed,
pride patched with brown paper. Everything considered. Everything arranged.

And then the work stops. Or a child falls ill.
Or a landlord wants the room. Or a man has
weak lungs, a bad leg, failing eyes—or ears that never
heard properly in the first place. One little necessity is
missing, perhaps through neglect, perhaps because life never supplied it.

The water runs away.

That is the moral, if you want one. A man
may prepare his towels, warm his coffee, mend his fence,
choose his book, and labour with intelligence and hope; but
success sometimes depends upon one small plug, and it is
unkind to laugh too loudly at those who discover, tired
and naked, that theirs was never in the tub.

### The Mattress of the *Mary Ann*

Sailormen, take 'em as a class, ain't misers. They may
spend foolish, and they may spend drunk, and some of
'em will spend the same shilling twice if the landlord
ain't looking; but they don't often 'oard. A man as
goes knocking about the world with all 'is property in
a sea-chest soon learns that money was made to move.
Leastways, that was always my belief till I sailed in
the *Mary Ann* with old Thomas Geary.

I'm night-watchman now, as you know, and my sea-boots 'ave
been dry these fifteen years; but I can see Thomas
this minute, sitting on 'is bunk and stitching a patch
on the elbow of a coat that wasn't worth the
thread. He was a little, bent man, with grey whiskers
growing under 'is chin like weed under a pier, and
eyes that looked at every mortal thing as if it
wanted to borrow sixpence.

We was bound from Sydney to London, a barque of
seven 'undred tons, carrying wool, tallow, and one or two
passengers that spent the voyage wishing they 'adn't come. Thomas
was an able seaman by rating, though 'e was sixty-five
if 'e was a day. He'd been going to sea
since 'e was a boy, and according to Bill Hicks
he'd never bought so much as a drink for another
man.

"Six 'undred pound 'e's got," says Bill to me one
evening, while we was putting a bit of spun-yarn round
the handle of a bucket.

"Who says so?" I says.

"Everybody," says Bill.

"That means nobody," I says.

Bill shook 'is head. He was a broad, heavy man,
with a mouth made for eating and hands made for
taking hold of anything that wasn't nailed down.

"I know," he says. "Thomas keeps it in notes, sewed
up in a belt round 'is middle. Six 'undered pound.
And 'e sleeps with one eye open."

"He sleeps with both eyes open when you're near 'im,"
I says.

"That's friendship," says Bill. "He knows I'm interested in 'im."

Thomas was close in everything. He'd smoke the last inch
of another man's pipe if 'e could get it, and
I've known 'im stand ten minutes by the galley waiting
for the cook to turn 'is back, so as to
warm 'is hands without saying thank you. If a man
lost a button, Thomas would look over the side to
make sure it 'ad gone before 'e offered sympathy. Yet
nobody disliked 'im as much as might be expected. At
sea you get used to queer craft, and Thomas was
so regular in 'is meanness that it came to be
looked on like the ship's bell or the second mate's
bad language.

About three weeks out he was took ill.

Walter Jones, who fancied 'imself as good as a doctor
because 'is wife and seven children was always under one,
said it was the liver. The cook said it was
the 'eart. Bill Hicks said it was six 'undred pound
pressing on 'is stomach.

Whatever it was, Thomas got worse. He went off 'is
food, which alarmed nobody, seeing that 'e'd always been careful
with other people's; but when 'e refused a basin of
broth that cost 'im nothing, we knew 'e was bad.

The skipper came down once and asked 'im how 'e
felt.

"Dying," says Thomas.

"Cheer up," says the skipper.

"What for?" says Thomas.

The skipper thought a moment, but not finding an answer,
went away.

For two days Thomas lay quiet in 'is bunk. Every
now and then he'd put 'is hand inside 'is shirt
and feel round 'is waist, and then 'e'd breathe easier.
Bill watched 'im doing it, and became so thoughtful that
'e walked into the mainmast.

On the third night the weather came on dirty. The
wind was aft, but there was a heavy sea, and
the old *Mary Ann* rolled till a man in the
fo'c'sle could put on one sea-boot in 'is own bunk
and the other in somebody else's. I had the first
watch below and was trying to sleep when Jimmy, the
cabin boy, came down the companion-way.

Jimmy was about fifteen, small for 'is age, with a
round face and a pair of sharp brown eyes. He
looked simple when 'e was standing still, but he didn't
often stand still long enough for anybody to make the
mistake.

"Thomas wants me," he says.

"What for?" says Bill, lifting 'is head.

"Because 'e don't want you," says Jimmy.

Bill lay back and said that boys was different when
'e was young. Jimmy passed along to Thomas's bunk and
drew the curtain.

We couldn't 'ear much owing to the wind and the
creaking, but after a bit Thomas's voice rose sharp.

"Promise!" he says.

"All right," says Jimmy.

"Swear it!"

"I swear," says Jimmy.

"Over the side," says Thomas. "Straight over. Don't let nobody
get it. Nobody! D'you understand?"

"Yes," says Jimmy.

Then there was whispering, followed by a rustle like paper.
Bill sat up again. I didn't sit up, because I
was already sitting, but I leaned forward in a friendly
way.

Jimmy came from behind the curtain with one hand inside
'is jacket.

"What've you got there?" says Bill.

"Cold," says Jimmy.

"I 'eard paper," says Bill.

"Then paper's wot I've got," says Jimmy.

He started for the ladder, and Bill put out a
foot.

"Steady, my lad," he says. "You might fall."

Jimmy stepped over it. "Then move your ugly great boot,"
he says.

Bill looked at me.

"Boys," he says, very sad. "No respect for age."

"You ain't old enough," I says.

"No," says Bill, "but I'm ugly enough, according to 'im."

Jimmy went on deck. We waited a minute, and then
Bill jumped out of 'is bunk and followed. I went
too, not from curiosity, but because a growing boy ought
not to be alone on deck in dirty weather with
anything valuable in 'is pocket.

We came up just as Jimmy was standing by the
lee rail. He made a quick movement with 'is arm,
and something small and dark went flying from 'is hand
into the sea.

"There!" he says.

Bill ran to the rail and stared over.

"What was that?" he says.

"Nothing," says Jimmy.

"You don't throw nothing overboard," says Bill.

"I just did," says Jimmy.

"Wot was it?"

"Something old Thomas give me."

Bill's face changed. "Money?"

Jimmy nodded.

"How much?"

"Six 'undred pound."

Bill made a sound like a hatch-cover falling on a
dog.

"You threw six 'undered pound overboard?"

"Thomas told me to."

"Why?"

"So nobody could 'ave it."

Bill gripped the rail. The ship rolled, and for a
moment I thought 'e was going after it. I caught
'im by the belt.

"Where did it go?" he says.

"In the sea," says Jimmy.

"I know it went in the sea. Whereabouts?"

Jimmy pointed astern. "Back there."

Bill gazed into the darkness as if 'e expected the
notes to come swimming after us. Then he turned on
Jimmy.

"You wicked young rascal," he says. "You ought to be
ashamed of yourself."

"I promised," says Jimmy.

"You shouldn't make promises like that."

"Thomas made me."

"Then you should've deceived 'im," says Bill. "It would've been
kinder."

Jimmy gave 'im a long look and went aft to
the cabin. Bill stayed at the rail till a sea
came over and wetted 'im from the knees down. Then
'e followed me below and sat staring at Thomas's curtain.

Thomas died at four bells. He went very quiet, with
one hand round the old belt, which was empty. Walter
Jones listened to 'is chest through a tin pannikin and
said there wasn't much hope. The skipper came down again,
looked at Thomas, and said Walter was right.

We buried 'im next afternoon. The skipper read the service,
the body slid over the side, and Thomas Geary, who'd
spent forty-odd years saving six 'undered pound, went off without
a penny on 'im. Bill watched the splash and sighed.

"Pore old Thomas," he says.

"You're thinking of the money," I says.

"I'm thinking of both," says Bill. "They went the same
way."

For the next two days 'e was very low. He
didn't eat much at breakfast, and at dinner he only
'ad three helpings. Whenever Jimmy passed, Bill watched 'im with
a peculiar look.

On the third evening 'e came to me while I
was at the wheel.

"He never threw it over," he says.

"Who never threw wot over?"

"Jimmy. The money."

"I saw 'im throw something."

"So did I," says Bill. "But wot?"

"Something dark."

"Exactly. Six 'undered pound in Bank of England notes ain't
something dark."

"It might've been wrapped up."

"It might've been a bit of rope yarn," says Bill.
"It might've been an old rag. It might've been the
cook's sock. All we saw was something."

"You said you 'eard paper."

"I did," says Bill. "Good paper. Valuable paper. And that
boy's got it."

I told 'im that if 'e believed that, 'e'd better
tell the skipper.

Bill was shocked.

"Tell the skipper?" he says. "And get the pore boy
into trouble? You've got no 'eart."

"Wot do you mean to do, then?"

"Talk to 'im private," says Bill. "Give 'im good advice.
Save 'im from temptation."

"And take the money?"

"Only to look after it," says Bill. "Till 'e's older."

I said nothing. Six 'undered pound is a large sum,
particularly when you're earning three pound ten a month and
paying for your own tobacco. Bill watched my face.

"Share and share alike," he says.

"Three shares," I says. "You, me, and Jimmy."

"Two," says Bill. "You and me."

"It's Jimmy's money, if 'e's got it."

"That's why 'e mustn't 'ave a share," says Bill. "It
would encourage 'im."

I told 'im I'd think it over, which meant that
I already 'ad.

That night we searched Jimmy's sea-chest. We done it very
careful, and put everything back exactly as we found it,
except a small apple, which Bill ate to prevent it
rolling about. There was no money. We looked under Jimmy's
bunk, inside 'is spare boots, behind the water-breaker, and in
an old concertina belonging to Ned Price. The concertina made
a noise in the middle of the search, and the
cook woke up.

"Who's that?" he says.

"Nobody," says Bill.

The cook sat up. "Somebody tickled my foot."

"Dreaming," says Bill.

"I wasn't dreaming," says the cook. "There's an 'and under
my bunk."

"That's your imagination."

"It's the size of a leg o' mutton," says the
cook.

Bill drew 'is hand out and put it behind 'im.

"You'd better go to sleep," I says. "You're feverish."

The cook said he'd show us feverish if 'e was
tickled again, and kept awake the rest of the night
with a sheath-knife beside 'im. That stopped our searching in
the fo'c'sle for a time.

Next day Bill tried kindness.

"Jimmy," he says, giving the boy half a plug of
tobacco, "you know me."

"Yes," says Jimmy, looking at the tobacco.

"I've always been a father to you."

"My father washes," says Jimmy.

Bill swallowed something.

"I've took an interest in you," he says.

"So does the mate when I'm late with 'is boots."

"What I mean is," says Bill, "you can trust me."

"No, I can't."

Bill glanced round. "Wot did Thomas give you?"

"Advice."

"Wot else?"

"Work."

"Wot else?"

"Six 'undered pound."

"And wot did you do with it?"

"Threw it overboard."

Bill smiled at 'im, but there wasn't any warmth in
it.

"Don't tell lies, my lad."

"I ain't."

"We saw you throw something."

"That was it."

"It wasn't heavy enough."

"Banknotes ain't heavy."

Bill's smile disappeared.

"Suppose I search you?" he says.

"Suppose I call the mate?"

"Suppose I knock your 'ead off first?"

"You'd get blood on your shirt," says Jimmy, "and it
looks bad enough now."

Bill raised 'is hand, but I caught it. Not out
of mercy, exactly. A boy with 'is head knocked off
can't tell you where 'e's hid money.

We let Jimmy go, and for a week we watched
'im. Bill watched 'im by day and I watched 'im
by night, and between us we got so little sleep
that we began suspecting each other. Once I woke and
found Bill looking through my clothes.

"Lose something?" I says.

"My knife," says Bill.

"You're holding it in your mouth."

"So I am," says Bill. "That's where I put it."

After that I kept my sea-chest locked, and Bill said
it was a strange thing when one honest man couldn't
trust another.

Jimmy went about 'is work cheerful as a cricket. He
polished the cabin brass, carried the skipper's meals, and whistled
whenever 'e passed us. That whistle worried Bill more than
anything.

"He's got it," he says. "No boy whistles like that
on twelve shillings a month."

"Perhaps he's 'appy."

"That proves it," says Bill.

We searched the boats, the chain-locker, and a flour barrel
in the galley. The cook caught Bill in the barrel
up to 'is shoulders and hit 'im twice with a
ladle. We looked behind loose planks and inside coils of
rope. Bill even offered to mend Jimmy's jacket, but Jimmy
said 'e'd rather wear the holes.

At last, when we was about five weeks from London,
Bill lost patience. He waited till Jimmy came off duty
one evening, then caught 'im by the collar and hauled
'im into the sail-locker. I followed to see fair play,
by which I mean that Bill shouldn't frighten the boy
into offering less than my proper share.

The lamp swung overhead, making Bill's face come and go
in the shadows. Jimmy leaned against a bundle of canvas
and looked bored.

"Now, my lad," says Bill, "this little game's gone far
enough."

"What game?"

"You know."

"No, I don't."

"The six 'undered pound."

"I threw it overboard."

Bill took hold of 'is ear.

"Say that again," he says.

"I threw it overboard."

Bill pulled.

Jimmy kicked 'im on the shin.

Bill let go and danced round the locker in silence,
being unable to find words strong enough for the occasion.

"Assault," says Jimmy.

"Assault?" says Bill, when 'e could speak. "You near broke
my leg."

"You started it."

Bill shut the door and stood with 'is back to
it.

"We know you kept that money," he says. "We don't
blame you. Any sensible person would. But you're a boy,
and money's a dangerous thing."

"It ain't hurt you," says Jimmy.

"I never 'ad enough to find out. Now, where is
it?"

Jimmy folded 'is arms.

Bill cracked 'is knuckles, one after another. It took some
time because 'e had large hands.

"You wouldn't like us to tell the skipper," I says
gently. "He might say you stole it."

"Thomas give it to me."

"To throw away," says Bill. "If you didn't throw it
away, you disobeyed a dying man. That's serious."

"How serious?"

"Six 'undered pound serious," says Bill.

Jimmy looked from 'im to me.

"If I tell you," he says, "wot do I get?"

"You get our forgiveness," says Bill.

"I don't want it."

"And a share," I says.

Bill scowled at me.

"A small share," he says.

"Three equal shares," says Jimmy.

"You're too young for two 'undred pound."

"I'm old enough for six."

Bill and me drew apart to talk. Bill said fifty
pound was more than enough for a boy. I said
the boy could keep quiet for two 'undred or shout
for six. Bill said one 'undred. Jimmy called out that
'e could hear every word and the price 'ad just
gone up.

In the end we agreed to three equal shares, though
Bill did it with tears in 'is eyes.

"Now," he says, "where is it?"

Jimmy hesitated.

"Safe," he says.

"Where?"

"In the second mate's mattress."

For a moment neither of us spoke.

"The second mate's mattress?" says Bill.

Jimmy nodded.

"How?"

"The day after Thomas died. The second mate was on
deck, and I was cleaning 'is berth. There's a split
at one end. I shoved the packet into the horsehair
and stitched it up."

Bill stared at 'im admiringly.

"That's clever," he says.

"I know."

"It's also wicked," says Bill. "Don't forget that."

"Get it, then," I says.

Jimmy laughed.

"You get it."

That was the difficulty. The second mate, Mr. Small, guarded
'is berth like a dog guards a butcher's shop. He
was a thin, sandy-haired man, very neat in 'is habits,
and proud of 'is bedding. Every morning he aired the
mattress, every week 'e beat it, and any hand that
entered 'is room without leave generally came out quicker than
it went in.

"We'll take it while he's on watch," says Bill.

"How'll we get down the companion-way with a mattress without
anybody seeing?" I says. "You might as well take a
man's false teeth out while 'e's eating."

"We'll think of something," says Bill.

We thought for three days and got nowhere. Then Bill
had an idea. Ideas came to Bill slowly, but when
one arrived it made 'im restless.

"Buy it," he says.

"Buy wot?"

"The mattress."

I looked at 'im.

"You propose going to the second mate and saying, 'Please,
sir, sell me the mattress you're sleeping on because there's
six 'undered pound inside it'?"

"Not in them words," says Bill.

"Wot words, then?"

"Leave that to me."

Next afternoon, when Mr. Small came forward to inspect some
work, Bill met 'im looking solemn.

"Beg pardon, sir," he says, touching 'is cap. "Might I
'ave a word?"

"You're having several," says Mr. Small.

"It's a private matter, sir. Sentimental."

The second mate looked surprised. Nobody had ever connected Bill
with sentiment before.

"Well?"

Bill lowered 'is voice.

"I've took a great liking to that mattress of yours,
sir."

Mr. Small stepped back.

"My mattress?"

"Yes, sir. From the first day I saw it."

"You've never seen it."

"I've seen the corners, sir, when Jimmy was airing it.
A beautiful mattress."

Mr. Small looked at me. I turned away and studied
the sea.

"Wot d'you want with it?" he asks.

Bill sighed.

"My mother 'ad one just like it," he says.

"Your mother?"

"Exactly like it. Same shape. Same colour. Same—same stuffing."

Mr. Small's mouth twitched.

"She died on it," says Bill, with a catch in
'is voice. "Last words she ever said was, 'William, always
think kindly of mattresses.'"

The second mate put a hand over 'is mouth.

"And so," Bill goes on, "I thought if you'd sell
me yours, sir, I could sleep on it every night
and remember—remember things."

"Your mother?"

"And you, sir," says Bill. "Both together."

Mr. Small turned red and coughed.

"I'm using it," he says.

"Not after we reach London, sir. You'll be going 'ome
to your family and feather beds, while a lonely sailorman
goes to 'is lodging with nothing but memories."

"You can have it for two pounds," says Mr. Small.

"Two pound!" says Bill, forgetting 'is mother.

"Take it or leave it."

Bill's face worked. Two pound was nearly three weeks' pay,
and paying money to get at money went against all
'is feelings.

"Would fifteen shillings—"

"Two pounds."

"Suppose one pound five—"

"Good afternoon, Hicks."

"Done!" says Bill. "Two pounds."

Mr. Small nodded and went aft, smiling to 'imself.

Bill watched 'im go.

"He's robbed me," he says.

"You'll soon be rich," I says.

"That don't make it right."

We didn't tell Jimmy about the purchase. It was Bill's
suggestion that we should get the mattress, find the money,
and divide it in two before the boy knew anything.

"He agreed to thirds," I says.

"Under pressure," says Bill. "A bargain made under pressure ain't
legal."

"It was you putting on the pressure."

"That makes it worse," says Bill. "Besides, 'e insulted my
shirt."

I objected for a while, chiefly because I thought Bill
might try to make the two shares into one. At
last 'e swore by everything 'e could remember that I
should 'ave half. We shook hands, then each counted 'is
fingers.

From that day the mattress seemed to grow bigger. Whenever
Mr. Small brought it on deck to air, Bill looked
at it as if it was a roast goose. Jimmy
noticed, of course. There wasn't much Jimmy didn't notice.

"Wot are you staring at?" he asks.

"Nothing," says Bill.

"That ain't nothing."

"It's the second mate's mattress."

"I know."

"I was just thinking it looks comfortable."

Jimmy gave 'im a sideways glance.

"You ain't comfortable to look at," he says, "but I
don't stare."

As we neared England the wind fell light, and the
ship crawled up-Channel with every man talking about 'is pay
and what 'e meant to do ashore. Bill and me
talked in whispers. We decided to wait till the vessel
was secured in the East India Docks. Mr. Small would
hand over the mattress, and we'd carry it below, cut
it open, and pocket the notes while Jimmy was busy
in the cabin.

There was only one question.

"The boy'll ask for 'is share," I says.

"Let 'im ask," says Bill.

"He might go to the police."

"And tell 'em 'e cheated a dead man, hid six
'undered pound in an officer's bed, and then lost it?
No. He'll keep quiet."

Jimmy came up behind us.

"Who'll keep quiet?" he says.

"The cook," says Bill.

"Why?"

"He talks in 'is sleep."

"Wot does he say?"

"Go away," says Bill.

Jimmy grinned. "You bought that mattress, didn't you?"

Bill went still.

"Who told you?"

"Nobody."

"Then you don't know."

"I 'eard the second mate telling the skipper. Two pound
was cheap, he said, for a mattress and a beautiful
memory."

I had to turn aside.

Jimmy held out 'is hand. "My share."

"You get it when we get the money," says Bill.

"Three equal parts."

"That's right."

"And no tricks."

"Would I trick you?" says Bill.

"Yes," says Jimmy.

The docks was in sight by then, all masts, smoke,
roofs, and grey water. Everybody was busy. Orders came quick;
ropes went ashore; men shouted from quay and deck. In
the confusion Jimmy disappeared forward, and Bill smiled.

"Good," he says. "We'll do it now."

The moment we was made fast, Mr. Small came down
carrying 'is mattress rolled under one arm. He handed it
to Bill.

"There you are, Hicks," he says. "Treat it kindly."

Bill paid 'im two sovereigns with a look like a
man burying twins.

"Thank you, sir."

"And think of me every night," says Mr. Small.

"I will, sir," says Bill. "Depend on it."

The second mate gave 'im a kind smile. I didn't
like that smile. It was the sort a man gives
when 'e knows where the joke is and you don't.

We carried the mattress below. It was awkward on the
ladder and twice as heavy as Bill expected.

"Horsehair," I says.

"Banknotes," says Bill, sweating.

We got it into the empty fo'c'sle and barred the
door. Bill pulled out 'is knife.

"Which end?" he says.

"Jimmy said there was stitching."

There was stitching at both ends. We chose one and
cut it open. Horsehair came out in a great black
lump.

"Careful," I says. "Don't cut the notes."

Bill put in 'is arm up to the shoulder. He
felt all round, drew it out, and shook 'is head.

"Other end."

We cut the other end. More horsehair. Soon the deck
looked like a dead pony had exploded. We searched handful
by handful. We felt along every seam and tore open
the centre. There wasn't so much as a tram ticket
inside.

Bill sat down among the stuffing.

"He's moved it," he says.

"Jimmy?"

"Who else?"

"You said 'e couldn't get at the mattress."

"Then the second mate found it."

We looked at each other.

"That smile," I says.

Bill sprang up. "Find Jimmy!"

We rushed on deck. Men was going ashore, officials was
coming aboard, and carts rattled on the quay. We searched
the cabin, galley, fo'c'sle, boats and chain-locker. No Jimmy.

His sea-chest stood unopened beneath 'is bunk. His spare clothes
was there. His boots was there. Even the twelve shillings
wages due to 'im was still in the skipper's account.

The skipper hadn't given 'im leave. The mate hadn't seen
'im go. A dock labourer remembered a small boy slipping
across a barge to the quay just after the first
line was made fast, but by then London had swallowed
'im.

Bill stood at the gangway, breathing hard.

"He's gone," I says.

"With six 'undered pound," says Bill.

"Maybe."

"He never put it in the mattress."

"Maybe not."

"Then why tell us 'e did?"

I looked at the torn mattress, which we'd dragged halfway
up the companion-way in our hurry.

"To keep us busy," I says.

At that moment Mr. Small came along. He looked at
the horsehair sticking from the mattress and then at Bill.

"Not comfortable?" he asks.

Bill opened 'is mouth, but nothing came out.

"You've been altering it," says Mr. Small. "Pity. It was
a very good mattress."

"Did you ever find anything in it, sir?" I asks.

"Find anything?"

"A packet, perhaps."

Mr. Small's eyebrows went up.

"No," he says. "Should I have?"

Bill stared at 'im. The second mate stared back. Then
'e smiled that same kind smile again and walked away.

Whether Jimmy took the money ashore, whether Mr. Small found
it first, or whether old Thomas's six 'undered pound really
did go over the side that dirty night, I can't
tell you. Bill always said Jimmy 'ad it, and for
years afterward 'e studied every well-dressed young man he saw
in London, hoping to recognise the cabin boy.

As for the mattress, Bill kept it. He 'ad to,
after paying two pounds. He stitched it up himself, but
'e never got the horsehair even, and for six months
'e slept with a lump under 'is back as big
as a pig.

"Reminds you of your mother?" I asks him once.

"No," says Bill.

"The second mate, then?"

Bill looked at me very bitter.

"Every night," he says.

And that's the worst of being greedy. You may get
nothing for it, but you generally get something to remember
it by.

When we went down, not long ago, to revisit that
populous quarter of the town in which our childhood had
once been deposited every morning at nine o'clock, and reclaimed—frequently
in a damaged condition—at four, we found that the Railway
had been there before us.

Of the first little School there remained a brick wall,
blackened at the top, three melancholy steps ascending to no
door, and one half of a playground, cut diagonally away
as neatly as a slice of bread. A signal-post had
established itself where the multiplication table used to hang; engines
passed, with shrieks of triumph, through the room in which
we had first made the acquaintance of pothooks; and the
mutilated end of the adjoining house, papered in a pattern
of blue roses, stood exposed to the weather like a
private confidence proclaimed from a platform. The whole fragment resembled
a flat-iron set upright, and seemed equally adapted for domestic
habitation.

Our earliest School had been kept by two elderly sisters,
Miss Purdle and Miss Sophia Purdle, who wore mittens, smelt
of lavender, and believed that all knowledge dangerous to infancy
might be rendered harmless by being repeated in a whisper.
We remember a row of little forms; a globe which
showed the British Empire in pink and everything else in
a discouraging drab; a wooden horse whose occupation was to
stand upon a cupboard; and a boy named Stokes, who
was always going home because his nose bled. We remember
being required to state that Twice Two were Four, and
feeling it an act of personal treachery toward Twice Two,
which might have had private reasons for being Five. We
remember Miss Sophia's brown bonnet hanging behind the door, where,
in moments of abstraction, we addressed it as "Ma'am." Beyond
these particulars, the Establishment is cloudy.

But our second School—Our School, as we called it with
a pride for which there was no discoverable foundation—comes back
in a procession of faces, jackets, ink-stains, grievances, smells, and
noises; and there, if anywhere, we became distinguished. We rose
to be first boy. We were pointed out to strangers
as "a very forward lad," an expression which our Chief
intended to signify proficiency, though we have since suspected it
of other applications.

The Chief was Doctor Blimberton—not a Doctor by any university
known to us, but perhaps by prescription, for he administered
a great deal of physic to the mind through the
medium of the cane. He was a large man, prematurely
purple, with a fringe of black hair retreating round his
head, as if it had received notice to quit. His
waistcoat was always tight, his boots always creaking, his ruler
always swollen at one end by long and honourable service.
He had two principal convictions: first, that boys learned nothing
except under bodily alarm; secondly, that their parents should pay
quarterly in advance.

Learning itself he regarded with some suspicion. He could spell
"philosophy" in two ways, and did; he placed Carthage in
Greece on one memorable occasion; and when asked by a
parent whether we received instruction in the natural sciences, replied
that Nature was attended to on Wednesdays by a competent
person. Yet he possessed, in a high degree, the arts
indispensable to the prosperity of his profession. He could bow
a rich boy into the house, bow a poor boy
out of it, quote the number of pupils on his
books without counting the three who had run away, and
describe a thrashing as "a slight correction rendered necessary by
moral causes."

His room had a green baize door, behind which justice
was performed. We knew the stages of that ceremony: the
Chief's bell; the appearance of Mr. Moffle, the senior usher,
wearing the expression of a man summoned to identify a
body; the culprit's passage through the school; the shutting of
the green door; certain sounds of an administrative nature; and
the reappearance of the offender, endeavouring to sit down upon
principles entirely new to him. Doctor Blimberton never struck in
anger. He struck in appetite, in weariness, in self-importance, and
sometimes, we believe, because the dinner-bell had not yet rung;
but anger would imply an intimacy with the victim of
which he was incapable.

The real labour of the School was done by the
ushers. There were three of them, though one was generally
ill and another generally leaving. Mr. Moffle taught writing, arithmetic,
geography, English grammar, and patience—this last chiefly by example. He
was a thin, fair young man, with a coat too
short at the wrists and an inexhaustible tenderness for penknives,
wounded sparrows, and unhappy boys. He mended our quills, lent
us sixpence which we never repaid, and could explain fractions
so that they appeared almost Christian.

There was also Mr. Crick, the Latin master, who arrived
before breakfast and departed after dark, carrying under one arm
a pile of exercises and under the other an umbrella
without a handle. He had red eyelids, a stoop, and
a perpetual dust upon him, as though the School were
a mill and he the flour it had ground. We
remember him always translating, always coughing, always looking for a
book upon which he was sitting. Poor Mr. Crick! Even
our childish tyranny, which was democratic in selecting its objects,
seldom molested him; for there are faces at which the
most accomplished small boy hesitates to throw a pellet.

The third usher changed so frequently that we have confused
six men into one composite person, who had whiskers on
Monday, none on Tuesday, a limp at Easter, and a
wife in Yorkshire by Midsummer. He taught French for a
fortnight, bookkeeping for three days, and disappeared owing to "family
circumstances," a phrase which, at Our School, covered everything from
matrimony to imprisonment.

The visiting masters were more splendid. Monsieur Paquet came twice
a week to teach French, wearing varnished boots and carrying
a fiddle, though why he carried the fiddle we never
ascertained. He had a little white dog called Henri, from
which circumstance we naturally concluded that the dog was also
French, could converse fluently with its master, and had probably
assisted at the Revolution. Signor Bellicoso taught singing with a
cracked tuning-fork, a broken trombone, and the air of a
man exiled from the Italian Opera by political intrigue. He
made us sing, "See, the conquering hero comes," until every
hero within hearing must have turned back.

Mr. Gudgeon, Professor of Drawing, wore onions in his ears
for deafness, or rheumatism, or inspiration—we forget which—and copied the
same ruined abbey for eleven years. It had two towers,
three crows, a moon, and a fisherman placed inexplicably in
the foreground. Every boy drew it. Boys who could not
draw produced it; boys who would not draw were compelled
to produce it; and one gifted boy, who drew a
horse upon the abbey roof, was beaten for levity.

Our companions were a more various race than any subsequently
assembled in Parliament. There was Lord Augustus Vale, who was
not, strictly speaking, a lord, but whose aunt had married
a person connected with a viscount. This relationship increased daily
in dignity, until the viscount became a duke, the duke
became an uncle, and Augustus was understood to be travelling
incognito for reasons of State. He had twelve waistcoats, received
hampers, and never lent anything. We treated him with constitutional
respect.

There was Raggles, the pirate's son. That his father was
a pirate no official document established; but Raggles had once
been fetched by a dark man with earrings, and possessed
a knife containing two blades, a corkscrew, a file, a
gimlet, a pair of tweezers, and an instrument which nobody
could identify. What honest parent would equip his offspring with
such a knife? Moreover, Raggles had been heard to speak
of "the coast"; and though it later appeared that he
meant the coast of Sussex, the explanation was regarded as
evasive. We fully expected him to be carried off in
a black schooner, after first revealing a map tattooed between
his shoulders. He left because his mother moved to Islington.

Then there was little Pounce, who never spoke of his
family at all. We therefore assigned him the largest fortune
among us. It was whispered that his father had died
in India, leaving him a palace full of jewels, three
elephants, and a curse; that trustees watched him from a
neighbouring chimney; and that, on his twenty-first birthday, he would
either become a nabob or be strangled with a silk
cord. Pounce himself encouraged these reports by looking pale and
saying, "You'll see." We have never seen.

Our School had its own commerce. The recognised currency was
slate-pencil, measured by length, though subject to depreciation when nibbled.
A clean inch would purchase two marbles, half a sheet
of blotting-paper, or the privilege of looking at a forbidden
picture for one minute. Cheese, string, sealing-wax, and brass buttons
formed subsidiary coinage. Insolvency was frequent; credit reckless; and we
have known a capitalist reduced, by three unlucky wagers upon
two beetles, from a whole slate-pencil to a fragment not
worth putting into the mouth.

We kept livestock. Linnets inhabited cages beneath desks; caterpillars escaped
from pill-boxes into grammars; a hedgehog spent one afternoon in
the Chief's greatcoat; and Barlow Minor maintained a family of
trained white mice in his writing-desk. These animals performed military
evolutions over the copybooks, climbed a ladder made of quills,
and rang a bell for cheese. Their education was so
much superior to ours that we resented it. One mouse,
promoted to the rank of General, was lost during prayers
and discovered in Doctor Blimberton's trouser-leg, where its patriotic movements
produced effects at first attributed to religious emotion.

The theatre flourished behind the water-butt. Our scenery consisted of
a blanket, two window-blinds, and the ruined abbey of Mr.
Gudgeon, appropriated without permission. We performed tragedies of our own
composition, in which every character carried a dagger, confessed to
an unsuspected parentage, and died slowly enough to observe the
effect upon the audience. Lord Augustus played kings because he
supplied the crown; Raggles played murderers because of the knife;
and we, being first boy, wrote prologues containing "Albion," "Fame,"
"Virtue," and such rhymes as could be obtained at a
moderate sacrifice of sense.

The Chief prohibited the theatre as immoral after discovering that
he himself had been introduced into a farce under the
name of Doctor Thunderstick. Mr. Moffle, who had painted the
moon, was reprimanded. We buried the crown, burned the prologue,
and continued performances in a coal-cellar, where the atmosphere lent
great realism to scenes of suffocation.

Mr. Moffle's private sorrow was known to us, as all
private sorrows are known to boys, incorrectly but in detail.
He was in love with Miss Celia Brunt, the music
mistress at a young ladies' academy two streets away. We
knew her by a blue ribbon, a brown bonnet, and
the rapidity with which she passed Our School at half-past
three. Mr. Moffle became pink when her name was mentioned,
carried a sealed letter for six weeks, and once entrusted
it to Pounce, who, believing it to contain military information,
opened it over steam.

The letter was chiefly remarkable for its blots and for
the sentence, "My position is at present humble, but my
sentiments are not." Whether Miss Brunt received a second letter
we cannot say. One wet Thursday we saw Mr. Moffle
waiting at the corner with flowers under his coat. Miss
Brunt approached, accompanied by a tall gentleman in a fur
collar; she bowed kindly, the gentleman raised his hat, and
Mr. Moffle went back into the School with the flowers
crushed flat against his breast. He gave them to us,
saying they had been intended for a sick friend. We
divided them unjustly and fought over the best rose.

Soon afterward Miss Brunt married the tall gentleman, who sold
pianos. Mr. Moffle congratulated her in a hand so beautifully
written that Doctor Blimberton displayed the note to a parent
as evidence of the School's penmanship. Poor fellow! He remained
gentle for another term, grew thinner, and departed for an
appointment in Jamaica. For many years we pictured him beneath
a palm-tree, teaching fractions to black children and receiving consolatory
parrots. We hope the climate dealt kindly with him.

Mr. Crick's calamity was less romantic and more public. During
one bitter winter he came to School with a fever,
though he had been ordered to remain in bed. His
wife was ill; there were children; the salary was overdue.
He stood before us translating Caesar in a voice that
seemed to travel from a great distance, until the book
slipped from his hand and he sat down suddenly.

Doctor Blimberton entered at that moment. "What is this, sir?"
said he.

"I am unwell," said Mr. Crick.

"Then be unwell in your own time," said the Chief.
"The parents pay for attention."

We remember the silence afterward. Even Raggles put away his
knife. Mr. Crick attempted to rise, failed, and caught the
desk; whereupon the Chief, perhaps ashamed, though more probably inconvenienced,
ordered Phil to take him downstairs. His salary was docked
for the afternoon.

Phil was the man-of-all-work: porter, furnace-keeper, boot-cleaner, messenger, window-mender, and
gaoler. He had one eye much smaller than the other,
a grey beard growing chiefly sideways, and hands like two
pieces of ship's timber. A hammer projected from him somewhere
at all times. He was gruff, smoky, and believed to
sleep in the coal-hole. We supposed that he had been
wrecked upon the School in early life and had remained
there, like a carpenter abandoned on a desert island, repairing
its doors while awaiting rescue.

He called us "young varmint," confiscated our marbles, and threatened
to nail any boy to the wall who touched his
tools. Yet when scarlet fever entered the house—and it entered
quietly, first in the flushed face of Barlow Minor, then
in two empty places at dinner, then in the hurried
arrival of mothers—the Chief removed himself to lodgings "as a
precaution due to the parents," leaving Phil and an elderly
nurse to manage the infected dormitory.

We were among those who remained. In the red, wandering
nights that followed, when the ceiling swelled and sank like
a sail, it was Phil who lifted us, carried water,
changed the hot pillows, coaxed medicine between clenched teeth, and
sat beside the smallest boys until dawn. His great hands
became gentle. He carved toys from firewood; he told impossible
stories of a sailor who married a mermaid and regretted
it on account of the damp; he allowed Barlow's surviving
white mouse to sleep in his waistcoat; and when little
Pounce cried for the mother whom we had furnished with
elephants and jewels but who was, in truth, a widow
keeping a stationer's shop, Phil turned his face toward the
dark window and said there was no shame in it.

One boy died—not Pounce, but Stennett, a quiet child whom
memory supplies with no history beyond a red comforter and
a habit of warming his hands at the stove. Phil
carried him away before breakfast. The Chief returned three days
later, complained of the expense of fumigation, and warned us
against "morbid feeling." Phil said nothing. But we saw him
burn the red comforter separately, and stand looking into the
furnace long after it was ash.

Such was Our School: a republic of rumours under a
despotism of fees; a menagerie, a theatre, a market, a
prison, and occasionally a place of learning. We cheated and
were cheated; tormented weakness and worshipped absurdity; made heroes out
of boys with hampers and pirates out of boys with
penknives; and received, from tired men paid too little and
commanded too much, fragments of instruction for which others took
the credit. We were not always happy there. We were
often afraid. Yet childhood, with that alchemy which can gild
a cracked inkstand and make an empire of a yard,
has put a light upon it.

We stood beside the Railway cutting and tried to determine
where the green baize door had been. A locomotive rushed
through the supposed position of Doctor Blimberton's room, drawing behind
it bright carriages full of passengers who could know nothing
of slate-pencil finance, the tragedy of Miss Brunt, General Mouse,
Mr. Crick's umbrella, or Phil at the furnace. Perhaps that
was as well. The School had been ugly, ignorant, cruel,
and in no respect deserving of preservation. We would not
rebuild one brick of it.

Nevertheless, when the smoke had cleared, and the last carriage
had dwindled beneath the bridge, we looked for the three
melancholy steps, the blue roses, and the half-playground; and were
sorry to discover that even these poor witnesses had disappeared.

### My Education in the Halls

When I was fourteen my Aunt Julia, who held that
the human character could be improved by almost anything provided
it began at half-past seven and required reserved seats, gave
me seven shillings and sixpence to witness a performance of
*The Merchant of Venice*. The entertainment was to be delivered
by the St. Matthew's Dramatic Association in the Lecture Room
of the Young Men's Institute, and my companion was to
be Charles Skegson, the son of a churchwarden, whose collar
was always clean and whose subsequent distinction at the Bar
surprised nobody who had known him as a boy.

"Remember," said my aunt, placing the money in my hand,
"Shakespeare elevates."

I promised to be elevated as far as the sum
permitted.

Skegson called for me at seven. He wore gloves, carried
a silver-headed cane belonging to his father, and had brushed
his hair into an appearance of commercial integrity. My mother
looked at him with approval. Mothers at that period were
apt to judge a boy by the state of his
boots; and Skegson, whose inner life was one long evasion
of authority, had exceptionally good boots.

We walked in the direction of the Institute until we
reached the corner of Green Street. There Skegson stopped.

"I say," he remarked, "you don't really want to see
that rot, do you?"

I said that I understood Shakespeare to be improving.

"That's just it," answered Skegson. "You can be improved any
time. You don't often get a chance of seeing Lola
Vere, the Bounding Gazelle."

He explained that Miss Vere was appearing that week at
the Albion Music Hall, together with the Brothers Bimbo, Professor
Zambelli's Educated Geese, and Mr. Joss Jessop, Monarch of Mirth.
Shakespeare, he pointed out, had been dead for nearly three
hundred years and could therefore have no reasonable objection to
waiting another evening. Miss Vere, on the other hand, was
advertised for one week only.

I opposed to his argument the circumstance that my aunt's
seven shillings and sixpence had been entrusted to me for
a specific moral purpose.

Skegson replied that the purpose of money was expenditure, and
that moral restrictions placed upon it after it had passed
into another person's pocket possessed no legal force. I do
not reproduce his exact language, but this was the substance
of it. He became a solicitor before he was thirty,
and did well.

There are, according to the books distributed among the young,
two roads through life. One is broad, brightly lighted, and
descends toward destruction; the other is narrow, damp, uphill, and
leads eventually to a city where everybody wears white and
plays the trumpet. The artist generally draws these roads as
dividing sharply beside a signpost, so that no traveller of
ordinary intelligence can mistake one for the other. My own
experience has been less simple. Between the broad road and
the narrow there are little connecting lanes, pleasant footpaths and,
above all, bridges. Skegson was one of the earliest bridges
I crossed.

The Albion stood in a street that had been omitted,
perhaps from delicacy, from my aunt's map of London. Its
entrance blazed with gas. Enormous coloured portraits displayed ladies whose
clothing had evidently shrunk in the wash, comedians whose mouths
were open to an extent incompatible with health, and acrobats
suspended in attitudes which Providence had never intended the human
form to assume. Above all shone the face of Joss
Jessop. He wore a checked suit, carried an umbrella as
large as a tent pole, and smiled upon the world
with six visible teeth.

I hesitated.

Skegson bought the tickets.

Thus do the active determine the moral history of the
reflective.

Inside, the atmosphere was composed in equal portions of smoke,
beer, orange peel and mankind. Waiters hurried among small tables.
Glasses clinked; gentlemen shouted greetings to friends four feet away;
and from the stage came an orchestra playing three tunes
at once, though whether by arrangement or private enterprise I
could not determine. I had never seen so many people
enjoying themselves without permission.

Skegson ordered two whiskies.

I protested that I did not drink whisky.

"Neither do I," he said. "That's why we'd better begin."

The liquor, when it arrived, possessed a taste suggestive of
burning furniture. It descended slowly, making observations on the way,
and settled in my stomach with the evident intention of
remaining there. Skegson drank his at a gulp and looked
thoughtful. I suspect now that he allowed most of it
to pass down the front of his waistcoat; but at
fourteen one credits vice with a courage it seldom possesses.

Next he purchased two cigars from a tray carried by
a girl in feathers. They cost twopence each, which was
not excessive, though the makers had economised upon tobacco. Mine
was a most effective cigar. Before I had smoked half
an inch, it had cured me of desiring another for
several years. Nevertheless, because Skegson held his between his fingers
with the melancholy elegance of a ruined nobleman, I persevered.

The Bounding Gazelle bounded. The Brothers Bimbo struck one another
with boards. The geese declined education and pursued Professor Zambelli
into the wings. Then Joss Jessop appeared and sang a
song concerning his mother's lodger, in which the word "trousers"
occurred eleven times and brought down the house on each
occasion.

I laughed until my cigar fell into the whisky.

At eleven o'clock Skegson observed that we ought to be
going. At a quarter past eleven I observed that the
floor had begun to rise and fall. At half-past I
was sitting on a doorstep explaining to Skegson that the
stars had been arranged without sufficient symmetry. He listened with
the patience of one who has led a companion into
sin and finds the companion tedious there.

We reached my house at twelve.

My mother was waiting in the dining-room. She wore a
grey dressing gown and an expression that reduced Skegson at
once from a man of the world to the churchwarden's
little boy.

"Where have you been?"

"The Shakespeare performance was unusually long," said Skegson.

My mother looked at me.

There are moments when falsehood, though desirable, is physically impossible.
My face was green; my collar smelt like the taproom
of a condemned inn; and one of Professor Zambelli's orange
peels, picked up during a period of enthusiasm, projected from
my pocket.

"We went to the Albion," I said, "and I am
ruined."

Skegson slipped away while attention was concentrated on the principal
criminal. I never blamed him. Retreat is among the first
principles of advocacy.

My mother did not scold. Scolding I had prepared myself
to endure. She merely said, "Oh, my dear!" in a
voice which suggested that the family tomb would have to
be opened before morning. She helped me upstairs, placed a
basin beside the bed, and kissed my forehead. Kindness completed
what whisky had begun. I wept.

During the night I reviewed my career. I saw clearly
that one cigar led to theft, one glass to forgery,
and one music-hall song to the gallows. Whether the forgery
preceded the theft I could not remember, but the gallows
was certain. I pictured my mother, white-haired and starving, asking
passers-by whether they had seen her lost boy; while I,
unshaven and tattooed, played cards in a cellar with men
named Spider.

At breakfast I confessed everything again, adding several offences I
had contemplated but not committed. I surrendered the remaining threepence
of my aunt's money and offered to leave home. My
mother suggested porridge instead.

For the next fortnight I became insufferably virtuous. I rose
early, took cold baths, and read publications designed to preserve
youth from pleasure. These contained illustrations of the most convincing
sort. In the first picture a bright-faced lad accepts a
cigarette from a companion in a cap. In the second
he enters a public-house. In the third he strikes his
employer. In the fourth he is visited in prison by
his aged father, who has somehow acquired white hair between
the first illustration and the last. Beneath were the words,
"Only Six Months Later."

Another series dealt with the theatre. A modest youth was
shown at the door of a music hall. By the
fifth plate he was a bookmaker, though how the management
of a small betting business followed from listening to comic
songs was never made plain. In the final picture his
sister had drowned herself, his mother was blind, and a
dog sat upon the doorstep, all apparently through his having
purchased a seat in the gallery.

These works impressed me deeply. Their theology was straightforward, and
their pace admirable. No modern novelist would dare ruin a
family in six pictures.

Yet certain details troubled me. The narrow road, as represented
in the frontispiece of one tract, terminated in a golden
city labelled REST AND PEACE. From every window leaned an
angel blowing an enormous trumpet. The broad road ended at
an establishment where music and refreshments were provided. I desired
salvation, but I could not help feeling that the arrangements
for quiet were defective.

For three months I avoided tobacco, strong drink, billiards, bright
waistcoats and boys whose fathers were churchwardens. Then summer came.
Reformation, like flannel underclothing, is difficult to maintain in warm
weather. By September I had crossed one or two of
those little bridges previously mentioned, and though I kept away
from the Albion, this was due less to principle than
to the memory of its whisky.

Nearly three years later, having acquired a moustache visible in
favourable light, I decided to become a journalist. No editor
had yet concurred in the decision, but that seemed a
technicality. A journalist, I argued, must know life; life included
music halls; therefore attendance at a music hall was not
dissipation but research. So irresistible was this logic that I
borrowed five shillings from my mother to purchase a dictionary
and spent four of them that evening at the Imperial
Palace of Varieties.

I sat in the promenade with a notebook conspicuously open
upon my knee. Whenever a dancer appeared, I wrote "Social
Tendencies" and watched her carefully.

During the interval I became aware of a gentleman at
the neighbouring table regarding me with severity. He was a
large, respectable man with iron-grey whiskers, a gold watch-chain, and
the manner of one accustomed to ask the blessing at
family dinners. It was my Uncle Theophilus.

Uncle Theophilus had frequently warned me against theatres, racecourses and
loose companions. He held that young men should spend their
evenings at home in the cultivation of serious thought. Finding
him at the Imperial Palace was therefore instructive, though not,
from his point of view, convenient.

He beckoned me over.

"What are you doing here, sir?"

"Studying the people," I replied. "For journalism."

"At your age?"

"One must begin early."

He frowned. "Does your mother know?"

I looked at the tumbler before him, at the cigar
between his fingers, and at the programme upon which he
had placed three approving crosses beside the names of lady
dancers.

"Does Aunt Caroline?" I asked.

There followed a silence such as occurs between two governments
which have discovered each other mobilising.

My uncle was the first to resume diplomatic relations.

"This is very painful," he said.

"Extremely."

"Your presence here can only be explained by youthful folly."

"Certainly. And yours?"

"My position is different."

"I supposed it must be."

"I came on business."

"So did I."

"What business?"

"Journalism."

He examined me for some time. I examined him. Each
perceived in the other a moral disadvantage balanced by a
practical weapon. After negotiations lasting four and a half minutes,
we concluded what may be called the Treaty of the
Imperial. By its first article, neither contracting party was ever
to mention the meeting. By its second, I was to
abandon journalism for the remainder of that evening. By its
third, Uncle Theophilus was to pay for supper.

He performed his obligations honourably and even ordered champagne, saying
it was safer to drink one good wine than several
inferior kinds. This principle, like many uttered by my uncle,
was invented when required and forgotten afterwards.

It was during that same period that I witnessed the
rise of Miss Bella Batts, afterwards famous wherever coster songs
were sung and hats thrown accurately over gas brackets. At
the time she called herself Signorina Ballatino and played the
zithern.

The incident occurred at the Grand Mogul, a hall in
the eastern quarter where the audience had strong opinions and
expressed them before being asked. The Grand Mogul possessed little
gilding and less ventilation. Its chairman, Mr. Puckle, sat at
a raised table near the stage, armed with a hammer,
a water bottle and no authority. He was a mild,
sleepy man who spoke of the artistes as "world-renowned" and
pronounced every foreign name differently from the performer concerned.

The attraction that evening was Joss Jessop, Monarch of Mirth.
Joss had been advertised in letters six feet high. His
portrait hung over the entrance; his songs were cried in
the street; and two hundred dock labourers, market porters, factory
girls and minor tradesmen had come for the specific purpose
of hearing him ask, in music, who had put the
donkey in Uncle's bed.

At nine o'clock the chairman rose.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he began, "I regret to announce that
owing to a sudden indisposition—"

"No Joss?" cried a voice.

Mr. Puckle blinked.

"Mr. Jessop has unfortunately been detained by—"

"Where's Joss?"

The inquiry came from a coalheaver seated immediately below the
chairman. He was a gigantic man with a neck like
a bridge support and hands that appeared to have been
designed for moving furniture without first opening the doors.

Mr. Puckle tapped the table.

"Order, please. Mr. Jessop, owing to a severe attack of
bronical inflammation, is unable to have the honour of appearing
before you this evening."

"Fetch 'im."

"He is confined to his bed."

"Fetch the bed."

The audience applauded this solution. Mr. Puckle waited for calm
and, mistaking a temporary shortage of breath for obedience, continued.

"But the management, sparing no expense, has secured at enormous
cost the services of the celebrated Continental instrumentalist, Signorina Bal—Balatweeno."

"Ballatino," said a voice from the wings.

"Precisely. Signorina Ballertano, whose unrivalled manipulations upon the celebrated zither—"

"Zithern," corrected the voice.

"—upon the celebrated zithering will, I am confident—"

"Where's Joss?" demanded the coalheaver.

Mr. Puckle struck the table with his hammer. The head
flew off and entered a gentleman's beer. This weakened government
prestige.

The curtains parted and Signorina Ballatino came on.

She was small, pale and dressed in white satin ornamented
with silver stars. Her dark hair was arranged beneath a
Spanish comb, though nothing else about her suggested Spain. She
carried a zithern almost as large as herself and smiled
with the desperate sweetness of a person approaching an unfriendly
dog.

The orchestra began.

She seated herself upon a gilt chair and played six
notes.

"Joss!" shouted the coalheaver.

She played three more.

"Blow yer zither!"

"It ain't a zither," called someone from the gallery. "It's
a tea-tray."

"Give us the donkey song!"

The Signorina's fingers trembled. The zithern produced a noise like
rain falling upon a tin roof. Mr. Puckle rose and
appealed to the audience's traditional generosity toward a lady and
an artist, but as no one had ever accused the
Grand Mogul audience of either tradition, the appeal failed.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he cried, "I must insist upon a
decorous reception for this gifted young foreigner."

"Foreign?" said the coalheaver. "She lives in Poplar. I seen
'er buying onions."

There was truth in this. Signorina Ballatino had been born
behind a fried-fish shop in Limehouse, and her travels upon
the Continent had consisted of one wet afternoon at Boulogne.
Her manager had given her Italy because he considered Poplar
insufficiently musical.

She attempted a second selection.

The coalheaver rose. He was not angry; he was merely
determined that the contractual rights of the public should be
observed.

"Look 'ere, miss," he said. "We come for Joss. We
don't want you nor that there furniture. You take it
'ome and practise on the cat."

The house roared. Signorina Ballatino stopped playing.

For a moment she sat motionless, looking down at the
strings. Mr. Puckle fluttered beside her and advised retirement in
the interests of harmony. The coalheaver resumed his seat, satisfied
that constitutional action had prevailed.

Then she stood up.

She placed the zithern carefully upon the chair. She removed
the Spanish comb from her hair and laid it on
the zithern. She took two steps toward the footlights and
looked at the coalheaver.

"'Oo are you," she asked quietly, "when you're washed?"

The audience became attentive.

The coalheaver grinned. "Never mind 'oo I am."

"I don't mind," she said. "Nobody could mind. But I'd
like to know what dustbin lost its lid when you
was born."

The grin faded slightly.

She leaned forward.

Then she began.

I cannot reproduce her speech. Parts of it were unsuitable
for print; other parts were unsuitable for any known system
of morality; and much of it moved too quickly to
be captured by human shorthand. But I can describe its
effect.

It struck the coalheaver first upon his ancestry. It pursued
that ancestry backward through several generations, exposing professions, personal habits
and physical peculiarities which, if true, made his existence a
biological scandal. It then turned upon his appearance. It examined
his boots, his trousers, his ears and the melancholy interval
between his chin and collar. It found each guilty.

It rose, dipped and returned. It cracked like a whip
about his head. It coiled round his knees. It lifted
him, enormous though he was, and shook him before his
neighbours. It accused him of stealing coal from his own
employers, soup from blind pensioners and bones from under starving
dogs. It gave the dates, the streets and, where necessary,
the names of the dogs.

The audience listened in reverent silence.

He attempted to interrupt.

"Now then—"

She swept the words from his mouth and used them
as further evidence against him. She compared his intelligence with
that of a diseased turnip, to the disadvantage of the
turnip. She wondered aloud whether his mother had reared him
or merely failed to prevent him. She called upon heaven
to explain him and, receiving no immediate answer, supplied one
herself.

He rose.

She grew taller.

He pointed.

She described the finger.

He opened his mouth.

She entered it metaphorically, marched through, smashed the furniture, and
came out by way of his left ear.

Every phrase found its mark. Every pause invited laughter and
then cut it short with a fresh assault. The gods
of old, when annoyed, employed thunderbolts; Signorina Ballatino had no
need of machinery. Her voice flashed, struck and returned smoking.
The coalheaver, who could have carried her under one arm,
stood helpless before her, an ox arraigned by a sparrow
with the gift of prophecy.

At length she arrived at his demand for Joss Jessop.
She expressed astonishment that a man of his face should
ask for amusement. She advised him to go home, hang
his head over the mantelpiece and charge the neighbours a
penny to look at it. This, she said, would provide
all the comic entertainment his district could require and allow
Mr. Jessop a deserved holiday.

The house broke.

Men hammered the tables with mugs. Women shrieked and waved
handkerchiefs. The gallery stamped until plaster descended from the ceiling.
Mr. Puckle, perceiving that order had been restored by someone
else, smiled and tapped the handle of his headless hammer.

The coalheaver looked about him for support and found civilisation
united against him. He sat down.

Signorina Ballatino regarded him for three silent seconds.

"Now," she said, "shall I play me little tune?"

"No!" roared the audience.

She nodded.

"Thought not."

She kicked the gilt chair into the wings, tucked up
the satin dress sufficiently to reveal a pair of stout
walking boots, and sang, without orchestra, a song about a
girl who sold winkles near the docks and married a
policeman to obtain inside information.

She had to repeat it four times.

The following week her name appeared outside the Grand Mogul,
not as Signorina Ballatino, Continental Instrumentalist, but as BELLA BATTS,
THE LIMEHOUSE LARK. The zithern vanished. So did the silver
stars and the Spanish comb. She came on in feathers,
apron and enormous boots, abused the conductor, quarrelled with imaginary
neighbours, and sang the language she had spent two years
being taught to conceal.

Within six months she was earning more than Joss Jessop.
Within a year Joss was appearing beneath her name on
the bill. Audiences adored her because she insulted them with
conviction and because, beneath each insult, they recognised the streets,
kitchens, markets and doorsteps they knew. Respectable critics called her
vulgar, then attended twice in order to confirm the opinion.

I saw her many years afterwards at a charity performance.
She arrived in a carriage, wore diamonds, and spoke offstage
with the careful gentleness of a duchess. When introduced to
a bishop, she curtseyed. When the orchestra missed her cue,
however, she turned toward it, and for one glorious second
the old thunder gathered.

The conductor found his place.

Thus was a great career founded, not upon the zithern,
foreign travel or improvement, but upon a coalheaver's mistaken belief
that the smallest person in the room must necessarily be
the easiest to silence.

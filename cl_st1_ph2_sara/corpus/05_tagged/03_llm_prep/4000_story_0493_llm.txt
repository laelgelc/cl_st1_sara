### THE SOCIETY FOR THE REFORMATION OF POKER PLAYERS

I have always regarded poker as a game which no
prudent man would learn and no honest man would need
to know. This opinion is not founded upon experience, for
I never played poker in my life; but a conviction
is not necessarily weakened by the absence of evidence, and
in moral questions it is often strengthened by it. I
knew that poker was played with cards, that money changed
hands, and that the man who smiled least generally gathered
most of it. This seemed sufficient information for a respectable
person.

Nevertheless, I once assisted at a game of poker in
a manner so active that a malicious critic might say
I financed it. I do not accept that description. I
was, in point of fact, engaged in the reformation of
a young man; and if the method adopted was unusual,
the result was satisfactory. The patient was reformed, though perhaps
not by the treatment prescribed.

It occurred during a passage from Liverpool to New York
on the *Columbia*, when that admirable vessel was three days
out and the passengers had arrived at the stage of
acquaintance in which everybody knows everybody else's business, but still
asks permission before borrowing a rug.

A small shipboard company resembles a village, except that in
a village an unpopular person may go away. On a
steamer he cannot. He must continue to appear at breakfast,
lunch, dinner, and on deck, and his habits, clothes, appetite,
politics, and probable income become matters of common property. By
the second morning we knew that Major Price had served
in India, though the Major continued to tell us so;
that Mrs. Wexford's maid was seasick; that Smith represented a
Birmingham house in agricultural machinery; and that young Henry Storm
was going to America to become a farmer.

Storm's name gave a wholly wrong impression of him. There
was nothing tempestuous in his appearance. He was a fair-haired,
mild-eyed young fellow of four-and-twenty, with a fresh complexion and
a manner of apologising when another man trod on his
foot. His father, who had been a country solicitor of
moderate means, was dead, and Henry possessed a few thousand
pounds, a cheerful disposition, and such knowledge of agriculture as
may be obtained by looking over a hedge.

He was full of plans for American farming. He understood
that land could be purchased in the West for a
few shillings an acre, and that wheat, having been planted,
came up in sufficient quantities to make its proprietor independent
in two or three seasons. He intended also to keep
cattle, because cattle, as he explained to me, required practically
no attention beyond being placed on grass.

"You must fence them in," I suggested.

"Oh, certainly," said Henry. "But wire is very cheap in
America."

He had provided for everything. In winter, when the cattle
could not obtain grass, he proposed to give them hay.
When I asked where the hay was to come from,
he said that he should grow it. Thus every difficulty,
on being approached, vanished before him.

Among our passengers was a dark, spare man named Vardon.
I do not know whether that was his real name,
and it did not seem a question one was invited
to ask. He had black hair, a clipped beard, heavy
lids, and a habit of looking at a person without
appearing to raise his eyes. His clothes were quiet and
excellent. He spoke little, drank nothing stronger than coffee, and
spent most of the day in the smoking-room.

Smith told me that Vardon was a professional gambler.

"Does he cheat?" I asked.

"No."

"Then why do you call him that?"

"Because he makes his living at cards."

"But if he plays fairly—"

"He does play fairly," said Smith. "He doesn't cheat."

This seemed almost a recommendation.

"He doesn't cheat," Smith repeated, "because he doesn't need to."

That put the matter in another light.

Poker had begun in the smoking-room on the first evening,
and by the third day Henry Storm had become a
regular member of the party. There were ordinarily four players,
but two dropped out after losing sums which, while small
enough to be described as trifling by the winners, were
sufficient to produce reflection in the losers. Henry remained. Vardon
remained. A red-faced American from Boston sometimes joined them, as
did a Liverpool cotton broker; but the centre of the
game was always the silent man with the clipped beard
and the mild young agriculturist who believed that cattle lived
without attention.

At first we supposed Henry was winning. He came to
meals with a bright face and said the game had
been most amusing. On the fourth morning, however, Smith drew
me aside.

"He has lost eight hundred pounds," he said.

I confess I was shocked.

"You cannot mean eight hundred shillings?"

"I mean pounds."

"In three days?"

"In three nights. He has been playing after we turned
in."

I looked across the deck. Henry was walking beside Mrs.
Wexford, carrying her little dog under one arm and wearing
a fore and aft cap which made him look younger
than ever.

"But why does he continue?"

"He thinks he will recover it."

"That," I said, "is the fatal delusion of the gambler."

Smith agreed. Neither of us had ever gambled, and we
were therefore able to discuss the fatal delusions of gamblers
with authority.

Before luncheon we called together Major Price, the cotton broker—who
had withdrawn from the game—and Dr. Marden, the ship's surgeon.
We formed an informal committee in a sheltered corner abaft
the second funnel. The wind made deliberation difficult, but dignity
was preserved by turning up our coat collars.

I proposed that we constitute ourselves the Society for the
Reformation of Poker Players. Major Price thought the title too
broad, since there was only one poker player whom we
intended to reform; but I pointed out that a society
should have room for expansion. Smith seconded me, and the
motion was carried.

I was elected president, partly because I had suggested the
name and partly because Smith declined the office. The cotton
broker became treasurer, although we had no funds, and Major
Price was appointed honorary secretary, because he possessed a fountain
pen.

Our policy was simple. I was to speak privately to
Storm, explain the dangers of gambling, and induce him to
leave the table. If he had lost all his ready
money, we would lend him enough to meet his immediate
expenses and reach his destination. The cotton broker observed that
Henry had already borrowed from nobody and paid plumply whenever
called upon; but we felt that this merely proved how
urgently our intervention was required.

I found him that afternoon leaning over the rail and
watching the grey Atlantic rise and fall with the solemnity
of a man considering a serious matter.

"My dear Storm," I began, "may I speak to you
as an older man?"

He turned cheerfully.

"Of course."

"We have heard—I should say, it has come to our
knowledge—that you have been playing poker."

"Yes."

"And losing."

His face changed a little.

"Who told you?"

"That is not material. What is material is that poker
is a most dangerous game, particularly when one plays against
a man who makes it his profession."

"Vardon plays straight."

"So I am informed. He does not cheat because he
does not need to."

Henry looked puzzled by this, as I had done, and
I explained it as well as I could.

"You mean he is a better player than I am."

"Precisely."

"I expect he is."

"Then why continue?"

Henry stared at the water.

"I have lost rather more than I intended."

"How much?"

"About twelve hundred."

The figure took my breath. Twelve hundred pounds was not
a sum; it was a condition of life.

"And you propose to win it back?"

"I should like to."

"My dear boy, that is the fatal delusion of the
gambler."

"Yes," he said. "I dare say it is."

There was a pause. I believed I had impressed him,
and I placed a hand upon his shoulder.

"If you are temporarily behind-hand, several of us will gladly
assist you."

"Thank you. That is very decent."

"You will not play again?"

He hesitated.

"Not after to-night."

I removed my hand.

"Why after to-night?"

"Because Vardon sails west from New York, and I go
straight to Chicago. It is my last chance."

"Your last chance to lose the rest?"

"To recover what I have lost."

I repeated my observation concerning the fatal delusion, but it
appeared less effective on the second delivery.

At dinner Henry ate little. Vardon ate with his usual
quiet appetite, peeling an apple so that the skin fell
in one unbroken coil upon his plate. I distrusted him
more than ever. A man who can peel an apple
in that fashion has too much command of his fingers.

At nine o'clock I went to the smoking-room, intending to
make a final appeal. Smith accompanied me as far as
the door, but would not enter.

"You are president," he said. "This is your department."

There were only three persons in the room: Henry, Vardon,
and the Boston gentleman, who sat apart reading an old
newspaper. The ship rolled gently, and the green-shaded lamps swung
over the tables. Vardon was shuffling the cards. Henry had
before him a small heap of sovereigns and several five-pound
notes.

"I hoped you would come," he said when he saw
me.

This was encouraging.

"I have come to take you away."

"Presently. Sit down."

"I do not play."

"You needn't. Just sit by me."

Vardon's half-closed eyes moved in my direction.

"Is the gentleman joining us?" he asked.

"No," I said firmly.

"Then perhaps he will not advise."

"I am incapable of advising anyone at poker."

"That is sometimes an advantage," said Vardon.

I did not like the remark, but took a chair
behind Henry. I thought my presence might exercise a restraining
influence. It did not occur to me that my position
resembled that of a second at a duel who has
come to prevent bloodshed and stays to hold the pistols.

They began.

For the benefit of those who share my ignorance, I
may explain that poker consists largely of putting money into
the middle of a table on the strength of cards
which one endeavours not to show until compelled. There are
pairs, sequences, flushes, full houses, and other arrangements, all possessing
values which every player seems to remember except the spectator
whose money is involved.

At first the sums were moderate. Five pounds passed one
way, ten another. Henry won twice, then lost three times.
His pile diminished. Vardon's face did not change. Henry's did;
he bit his lower lip and handled his cards with
increasing care.

At a quarter before ten he turned slightly towards me.

"Have you twenty pounds?"

I was startled.

"For what purpose?"

"To lend me."

"My dear Storm—"

"Only for a minute."

The money was not, as subsequent events proved, required for
a minute; but I had come prepared to aid him
in case of necessity, and this appeared to be the
necessity. I took four five-pound notes from my pocket and
passed them under the table.

"Thank you," he whispered.

He put them into the game.

Vardon won them.

I rose.

"That concludes the matter," I said.

Henry caught my sleeve.

"Sit down. I have a good hand coming."

"How can you know?"

"I feel it."

"That is the fatal—"

"Yes, yes," he murmured. "I know. Sit down."

The Boston gentleman lowered his newspaper and looked at us
with interest.

Another deal followed. Henry drew two cards. Vardon drew one.
They began to bet.

"Five pounds," said Vardon.

"Ten," said Henry.

"Twenty."

"Forty."

The words were spoken quietly, but to me they seemed
like pistol shots. Henry had no forty pounds. He turned
his head.

"Could you manage fifty?"

"I could, but I shall not."

"Please."

"You have already lost twenty of mine."

"I know. This is different."

Every losing gambler, I reflected, probably says that this is
different. Yet the members of our society had authorised financial
assistance. They had meant assistance after the game, certainly, not
during it; but constitutions framed in haste are liable to
ambiguity.

I gave him fifty pounds.

Vardon placed notes upon the table. Henry did the same.

"Another fifty," said Vardon.

Henry stared at him.

"I raise you a hundred."

I touched Henry's arm.

"You cannot."

"I must."

"You have nothing left."

He leaned close to me.

"How much have you?"

Such a question, put suddenly in public, is embarrassing. I
had intended to purchase certain machinery in New York and
carried a letter of credit, besides nearly three hundred pounds
in notes and sovereigns.

"I decline to state."

"Lend me a hundred."

"No."

"Then I lose what is there."

"That," I said, "would be unfortunate, but conclusive."

"Look at my cards."

"I should prefer not to."

He thrust them towards me. I saw five cards, all
of the same red pattern, running, as I afterwards learned,
from the seven to the knave.

"Well?" he whispered.

"They are very neat."

"It is a straight flush."

"Is that desirable?"

The Boston gentleman made a choking sound behind his newspaper.

"It is almost unbeatable," Henry said.

"Almost?"

"He might have a higher one."

This qualification seemed important.

"Has he?"

"How should I know?"

Vardon sat without movement, one hand resting beside his money.
His eyes were nearly closed. He might have been asleep,
but I had the disagreeable impression that he could see
every note in my pocket.

I lent Henry a hundred pounds.

He pushed it forward.

"Your hundred, and another hundred," he said.

Vardon looked at him for several seconds.

"Another two hundred."

Henry swallowed. Then he turned to me again.

"No," I said before he could speak.

"If I stop now, he takes it all."

"You have told me that."

"I have the winning hand."

"You told me it was almost unbeatable. 'Almost' is a
word that has ruined many men."

"Two hundred," said Vardon quietly.

The ship gave a long roll. Somewhere below us a
door slammed. The Boston gentleman had ceased all pretence of
reading.

I ought to have dragged Henry from the room. I
ought to have summoned Smith, Major Price, the captain, or
some person possessing firmness. Instead I remembered that I was
president of a society whose declared purpose was to save
this young man from ruin, and that if I refused
him two hundred pounds he would lose the money already
advanced. There is no trap so effective as a sum
already spent.

I gave him two hundred.

He counted it upon the table with fingers that trembled.

"And two hundred more," he said.

Vardon's eyelids lifted a fraction.

"Two hundred more?"

"Yes."

I caught Henry by the shoulder.

"You have not got it."

"He thinks I haven't," Henry whispered.

"He is correct."

"Lend it to me."

I had now reached that extraordinary moral condition in which
a man who would refuse a sovereign on principle will
advance two hundred pounds to protect the hundred he was
foolish enough to lend before it.

"This is the last," I said.

"Absolutely."

I produced the money.

Vardon watched Henry push it forward. For the first time
the gambler appeared uncertain. He studied Henry's face, then the
cards lying face downward before him, and finally me. I
endeavoured to present the countenance of a person who understood
everything. I may have succeeded, because I understood nothing.

"Call," said Vardon.

The cards were shown.

Vardon laid down four aces.

Even I recognised them. My heart descended to a region
near my boots.

Then Henry spread his five red cards.

The Boston gentleman sprang up.

"Straight flush!" he cried.

Vardon did not move. Henry gave a laugh which was
almost a gasp and pulled the money towards him with
both arms. There were notes, sovereigns, and counters enough to
stock a modest bank. I was relieved to observe among
them several items which had formerly belonged to me.

"Well," I said, when I could speak, "you have recovered
your losses. We will now withdraw."

Henry counted rapidly.

"I am still short."

"Short!"

"About ninety pounds."

"You have won a fortune."

"I had lost a larger one."

"Then accept the difference as the fee for a lesson."

"One more hand," said Henry.

I objected with all the force at my command. I
invoked prudence, gratitude, the insecurity of human affairs, and the
Society for the Reformation of Poker Players, whose existence I
was obliged to reveal. Henry listened with one eye on
Vardon.

"One hand," he repeated.

Vardon shuffled.

"If the gentleman is afraid to continue," he said, "we
can stop."

That was the longest speech he had made all evening,
and the most effective.

"Deal," said Henry.

I remained because my money was still mixed with his,
and because one does not desert a reforming subject at
the most dangerous stage of treatment.

Five cards were dealt. Henry looked at his and asked
for three. Vardon took two.

The betting began at ten pounds and rose to fifty.
Henry raised another fifty. Vardon answered with a hundred.

I looked over Henry's shoulder. He had a pair of
threes.

Even my limited knowledge told me that this was not
almost unbeatable. It seemed scarcely beatable at all, except by
something worse, and I could not imagine anything worse.

"Stop," I whispered.

He ignored me.

"Another hundred," he said.

Vardon examined him.

"And another."

Henry pushed forward the money.

Vardon raised again.

The pile in the centre grew with appalling speed. Henry's
pair of threes did not improve by being stared at.
I wondered whether he had gone mad through the shock
of good fortune.

Vardon said, "Two hundred."

Henry counted his remaining notes. He was short by fifty
pounds.

His hand crept towards me under the table.

"No," I whispered.

"Fifty."

"You have two threes."

"Hush!"

"They are extremely small threes."

"Fifty, please."

I had not imagined that reform could be so expensive.
Nevertheless, after the previous transactions, fifty pounds appeared almost economical.
I handed it over.

Henry added it to the stake.

"And I raise you two hundred," he said.

This exhausted his funds completely.

Vardon's fingers rested upon the edge of his cards. He
looked at Henry. He looked at me. I attempted again
the expression of profound knowledge, though I fear that what
I produced was indigestion.

For a full minute nobody spoke.

Then Vardon threw down his cards.

"You take it."

Henry gathered in the money.

"What had you?" asked the Boston gentleman.

Vardon turned over three queens.

The room became very still.

Henry showed his pair of threes and burst into laughter.
The Boston gentleman slapped the table. I did not laugh.
I was considering the character of a young man who
had allowed me to risk fifty pounds upon two small
threes without submitting the facts to the president of his
reform society.

Vardon rose slowly. His face had not altered, but a
white line showed about his mouth.

Henry counted the money twice, repaid every penny I had
lent him, and placed the remainder in an envelope.

"I have recovered all I lost," he announced, "and I
am twenty-seven pounds ten shillings to the good."

"Then," I said, "the Society has accomplished its purpose."

"It has," said Henry. "I shall never play poker again."

He said it so simply that I believed him; and,
to his credit, he kept his word, at least during
the remainder of the voyage. Whether he afterwards devoted the
twenty-seven pounds ten shillings to wire fencing, cattle, or further
moral experiments, I cannot say.

As we turned to leave, Vardon spoke.

"You were fortunate," he said to Henry.

Henry smiled.

"I had a good hand."

"Once."

"And the second time?"

Vardon's heavy eyes moved from Henry to me.

"The second time," he said, "you had the combined luck
of two fools—your own and the gentleman's."

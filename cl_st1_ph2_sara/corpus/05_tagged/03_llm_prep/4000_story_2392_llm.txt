## LAURA SILVER BELL

Dardale Moss lies high between the inhabited valleys and that
dark congregation of hills which the shepherds call the Black
Sisters. It is a melancholy waste, more extensive to the
eye than it proves beneath the chain, because its long
swells of peat, its reedy pools, and its low, uncertain
horizons afford no fixed measure of distance. Here a cairn,
gray as November, rises like the ruin of a giant's
house; there a ragged fir leans from the wind, though
no wind blows; and farther on, where the moss sinks
toward Dardale Wood, a black stream creeps under the heather
with so little sound that one may stand upon its
bank and fancy it dead.

The people of that country do not willingly cross the
Moss after sunset. They will tell you that the ground
is treacherous, that the mist rises suddenly, and that an
unwary foot may sink through the green skin of a
bog-hole where neither crook nor spade will find it. These
are sensible reasons. Yet, when pressed, an old man will
speak of lights that move against the wind; an old
woman, of music heard beneath the peat; and children, who
commonly know more than their elders permit, will whisper that
the King of the Moss keeps court under the Black
Cairn.

Whether there is truth in these matters I do not
undertake to prove. I record only what was believed in
Dardale, and what, according to many sober witnesses, befell Mall
Carke and Laura Lew in the year when the red
murrain took the cattle at Hay Fell.

Mall Carke lived alone in a stone cottage at the
northern edge of the Moss. She was called Mother Carke
by those who wanted her assistance and Witch Mall by
those who had already obtained it. She sold simples, foretold
marriages in the soot of a candle, found lost sheep
by turning a sieve upon a pair of shears, and
could, they said, prevent cream from churning merely by tying
three black hairs round a churn-staff. On the other hand,
she had brought more than one child through a difficult
birth; she knew the virtues of feverfew, mugwort, and St.
John's wort; and there were farmers who cursed her at
market but came secretly to her door after dark.

She was a tall, raw-boned beldame, with iron-gray hair and
eyes of that pale green which is sometimes seen in
winter pools. Her dress was always black, not from mourning,
as strangers supposed, but because black cloth could be turned,
patched, and weathered longer than any dyed stuff. A silver
pin fastened her kerchief under the chin. She carried a
hazel staff, and in the hollow brass head of it
were lodged certain grains, filings, and written words which no
one was allowed to examine.

One evening toward the decline of autumn, Mall was returning
from Greystead with sixpence in her pocket, a lump of
bacon in her basket, and rain following close behind her.
The sunset had opened under the storm-cloud like a furnace
door, throwing a blood-red glare athwart the Moss; every pool
burned, every stone cast a long black shadow, and the
Black Cairn seemed to smoke against the western conflagration.

At the place called Hagger's Stile, where a wall, half
sunk in peat, divides the common grazing from the old
lordship, Mall saw a man sitting with his back toward
her.

He was of uncommon height. Even seated, his shoulders rose
above the stile-post; yet his limbs were meagre, his coat
hung in greenish tatters, and the long heels of his
shoes had been worn through. A cap of matted fur
covered his head. Beside him lay a dead hare, though
there was neither dog nor snare in sight.

Mall stopped.

The man did not turn, but said:

"Thou'rt late abroad, Mother Carke."

His voice was soft and deep, like water heard under
earth.

"An' thou'rt ower early for church," answered Mall.

At this he looked round.

Those who afterward heard her description could never agree whether
she had called his face pale or black. She herself
would say only that it was the colour of peat-ash
after rain. The eyes were narrow and amber-bright, the mouth
too large, and the teeth, when he smiled, seemed crowded
together like kernels in an old ear of corn.

"Sit thee down," he said. "I've a word for thy
private ear."

"Keep it," said Mall. "Words are cheap, an' thine'll be
dear enough afore they're done."

He drew from his rags a little box of yellow
metal and opened it. Within lay brown snuff, fine as
dust, and on the lid was engraved a coronet.

"Take a pinch."

"I take nowt frae strange hands."

"Not a pinch? Then a guinea."

From the same impossible folds he produced a gold piece,
bright as though newly struck. It flashed in the red
sunset. Mall's green eyes fixed upon it, for she was
poor and had never possessed a guinea all at once
in her life.

Still she shook her head.

"What would thou buy?"

"Nowt that thou can sell."

The stranger laughed. It was a pleasant laugh; yet the
pools near the stile trembled, although the air was still.

"I would buy a kindness. There's a lass in Dardale,
a bonny thing with lint-white hair. Laura Lew they christen
her—"

"They never did," said Mall sharply.

The man's smile widened.

"That's true. Her mother died before the font could wet
her, and her father followed before the parson was fetched.
So Laura Lew she is by law, and Laura Silver
Bell by love. Bring her to the brook in Dardale
Wood. Bring her at moonrise. Thou shalt have ten gold
guineas and no more hunger while thy roof stands."

Mall gripped her staff.

"Why canna thou fetch her thysel'?"

"She is shy."

"She's none shy."

"She has been warned."

"Then she's warned again. Hear me, Moss-man, or lord, or
devil, whatever mak' thou be. I'll neither bring nor send
her. An' if thou sets one ragged shank across the
chapel road after her, I'll ring such a bell as
thou'll not bide the sound on."

For the first time his face altered. It lengthened, not
as faces lengthen in common anger, but as a shadow
stretches when the sun drops. The dead hare beside him
stirred one hind leg.

"Mind thy trade, old woman."

"I ken my trade. I ken, too, the price o'
fairy gowd. Leaves at dawn, grief at noon, an' a
grave afore night."

"Then keep thy grief."

The sunset closed. In the sudden grayness Mall saw no
man upon the stile, only a blasted thorn with a
strip of sacking caught upon it, fluttering though there was
no wind. The dead hare was gone. At her feet
lay the golden guinea.

Mall stepped over it and went home by the long
road.

The coin was found next morning, they say, turned black
and sunk edgewise in the peat. No grass ever grew
within a hand's breadth of it.

Laura Lew lived at that time with her mother's sister,
Dame Ruth, in a whitewashed cottage under Dardale Hill. She
was seventeen years old, slight but not frail, with blue
eyes, pale hair, and an uncommonly clear complexion. When she
laughed, which was often, her voice had a bright, trembling
sweetness, whence came the name Silver Bell. She could milk,
bake oatcakes, knit stockings, and sing twelve ballads through without
losing a verse; yet she disliked whatever was ordinary, and
would leave a pail half scoured to watch riders pass
upon the high road.

The circumstances of her baptism must be explained. Her mother,
being weak after delivery and the snow lying deep, the
sacrament had been delayed. Before the thaw, the poor woman
died; afterward came illness, debt, removal, and neglect. Dame Ruth
intended every Easter to speak to the parson and forgot
it every Whitsuntide. Thus the girl grew to womanhood outside
that first Christian boundary which, in the opinion of the
country people, neither fairy nor wandering spirit may easily cross.

Mall Carke came to the cottage the morning after her
adventure.

Dame Ruth was hanging linen in a little yard, and
Laura sat on the threshold, polishing her Sunday shoes with
mutton fat. The day was bright, but the Moss beyond
the hill remained under a bar of cloud.

"Laura," said Mall, "has any fremd body spoken wi' thee?"

"Many a one," answered the girl. "Am I to keep
count?"

"A strange man. Tall. Dark-like."

Laura bent lower over the shoe. A colour appeared beneath
her fair skin.

Dame Ruth ceased pinning the linen.

"What's this, Mall?"

"Let the lass answer."

"There was a gentleman by the hazel lane," Laura said
carelessly. "He asked the way to Lairdale House."

"There's no house at Lairdale now," said her aunt. "Not
since old Sir Bertram's roof fell."

"So I told him. But he laughed and said his
own roof was sound enough."

"What manner o' gentleman?" asked Mall.

"A handsome one."

"His coat?"

"Black velvet, with silver at the sleeves."

"His feet?"

Laura looked up, annoyed. "Boots, surely. What should he have?"

"Cloven hoofs, mebbe."

Dame Ruth crossed herself. Laura laughed, but not with her
usual clearness.

Mall came near and laid two hard fingers under the
girl's chin.

"Listen, bairn. Thou'll see him grand, if he wills it.
Thy aunt may see a beggar; thy friend a black
beast; a dog may see nowt but a moving rag.
Yet the eyes are all true save thine. If he
asks thee across running water, bide where thou art. If
he offers a ring, let it fall. If he bids
thee dance, stop thy ears. Above all, take neither meat,
drink, nor gold frae his hand."

Laura pulled away.

"Why should a lord offer me gold?"

"Because thou'rt vain enough to take it."

"I'm not vain."

"Then prove it by forgetting him."

The girl's eyes filled, more with anger than distress.

"He spoke civilly. More civilly than some that come scolding
before breakfast."

Mall regarded her for a long moment. "Did he touch
thee?"

"No."

"Did thou promise owt?"

"No!"

"Did he name thee?"

Laura hesitated.

"What did he call thee?" demanded Mall.

The answer came softly.

"His silver lady."

Dame Ruth dropped the wet cloth she held.

Mall's face became saturnine. "Get the lass christened Sunday."

"The parson's at Hexham till Michaelmas," said Ruth.

"Then take her to another."

"Over Fell Pass? With weather making?"

"Better rain on her head than earth over it."

Laura sprang up, seized her shoes, and went indoors. The
latch fell loudly behind her.

Mall watched the closed door.

"She's half won," she said.

Dame Ruth began to cry.

For some weeks nothing followed. Harvest ended; peat was stacked;
the rowans reddened by the churchyard wall, and rain stitched
the valleys together with silver threads. Laura went about her
tasks, sang less frequently, and denied that she thought of
the stranger. But Bessie Bell, the miller's daughter and Laura's
nearest companion, afterward testified that a change had come upon
her.

She would stand before the mill-pool twisting her hair round
her fingers. She began to despise her homespun gown and
asked what velvet cost by the yard. Once Bessie found
beneath her pillow a small green feather, bright as an
emerald; Laura snatched it away and declared it had fallen
from a jay's wing, though no such bird is known
in those parts.

More troubling still, she was sometimes absent after dusk.

"Where hast been?" Bessie asked one evening when Laura appeared
on the chapel road with wet shoes.

"Nowhere."

"Nowhere's a boggy country, then."

"I went but to the wood."

"What for?"

"To hear the leaves."

"There's leaves at thy own door."

"Not such leaves."

Bessie, who was a plain, affectionate girl and had no
patience with mysteries, took her friend by the elbow.

"Laura, thou's seen him."

"And if I have?"

"Mother Carke says—"

"Mother Carke! A sour old crow in a black shawl.
She'd warn the sun not to rise, lest it set
again."

"She says he's wicked."

"She says every bonny thing is wicked because she's old."

Bessie drew back. In that country age is commonly respected
even when disliked; Laura's words therefore seemed both foolish and
ominous.

"What does he want wi' thee?"

"To marry me."

"Where?"

"At his house beyond Lairdale."

"There is none."

"To thee, maybe."

This answer so disturbed Bessie that she told her mother,
who told Dame Ruth, who locked Laura in the cottage
for three nights. On the fourth morning the girl seemed
penitent. She kissed her aunt, promised obedience, and worked cheerfully
until noon. Thus good sense, which arrives with heavy boots
and a loud voice, supposed it had driven enchantment from
the premises. But enchantment is often most industrious when it
appears to sleep.

On the last fair day of September, Laura and Bessie
went to gather blackberries in Dardale Wood. The wood lies
in a steep hollow under the Moss, and though it
is small, the ancient oaks, thorn thickets, and overhanging rocks
make it seem a wilderness. Through its centre runs Dardale
Beck, shallow in summer but dark at all seasons, over
a bed of red stone. A fallen ash served then
as a bridge, though cattle and children commonly crossed at
the ford.

The afternoon was warm. Amber light entered between the branches;
flies turned in golden columns; the blackberries shone like jet
among leaves already touched with crimson. The girls had filled
one basket and half another when they heard a whistle
from the farther bank.

Bessie looked up first.

A man stood under a yew tree across the beck.
To her eyes he was the same famished giant whom
Mall had encountered: long, crooked, and foully dressed, with bare
shins, a ragged cap, and something dead hanging from his
belt. His hair fell in strings about his face. One
hand rested upon a staff made, as Bessie always maintained,
from a human thigh bone.

She screamed and clutched Laura's gown.

But Laura saw a young lord.

His black velvet doublet was sewn with silver leaves; a
short mantle, green as deep water, hung from one shoulder;
his boots were soft Spanish leather, and a sword with
a moonstone pommel rested at his side. His face was
pale and proud, yet beautiful beyond the measure of mortal
men. About his head the yew branches formed a natural
crown, and every berry upon them glowed like a ruby.

He held out his hand.

"My silver lady."

"Dinna look!" cried Bessie. "Oh, Laura, it's him! It's the
Moss thing!"

Laura smiled as one waking from a tiresome dream into
delight.

"You said you would come."

"And have I not?"

"You stayed away."

"I waited where your friends could not slander me. Cross,
and I will show you such a house as queens
might envy."

Bessie heard only a low muttering, like a bull behind
a wall. Yet Laura answered each indistinct sound.

"No!" Bessie dragged at her. "Mind Mall's word. Running water!
Thou munna cross!"

The beck slipped between them, scarcely three yards wide, reflecting
the sky in dark, broken fragments. Laura set one foot
upon the red stones of the ford.

The giant raised his hand. On his finger was a
ring of pale gold with a little bell of silver
hanging from it. The bell moved, though no sound reached
Bessie. Laura, however, inclined her head as if listening to
distant music.

"See," she whispered. "He's brought my marriage ring."

"It's a rusty link!" Bessie sobbed. "There's a dead beetle
tied to it!"

Laura turned upon her with flushed cheeks and shining eyes.

"Thou'rt jealous."

Then she crossed the beck.

Bessie made one desperate effort and caught her apron. The
string broke. Laura stepped upon the farther bank and took
the stranger's hand.

At once a wind passed through the wood. It tore
no leaf from any tree, but every branch bent toward
the pair. The amber light went out. Bessie, blinded by
flying dust or some darker agency, covered her face for
the space of a breath.

When she looked again, there was no one under the
yew.

She scrambled through the ford, calling Laura's name. Among the
roots she found the broken apron and the basket overturned,
its berries mingled with little clots of black earth. There
were footprints on the bank: Laura's narrow shoe-marks, two or
three; beside them, the impression of a long naked foot.
Both ended at the yew tree.

The hue and cry lasted until winter. Men searched the
wood with lanterns, dragged the mill-pool, opened old mine shafts,
and went in companies over the Moss. Dame Ruth offered
all she possessed. The parson preached against vagrants and Popish
superstitions, though it was remarked that he afterward caused the
church bell to be rung at midnight for nine successive
nights.

Mall Carke joined none of the searchers.

When questioned, she said only, "She crossed," and shut her
door.

Bessie fell into a fever. Upon recovering, she repeated her
account so often and with so little variation that even
those who mocked it were unable to disprove it. She
never entered Dardale Wood again. To her dying day she
affirmed that Laura had smiled when she took the creature's
hand.

A year passed, and a little more.

Dame Ruth's hair whitened. Laura's spinning wheel stood idle under
a cloth. Children ceased singing "Silver Bell" at play, for
their mothers struck them if they did; and though young
men sometimes boasted at the alehouse that they would face
all the fairies in Northumberland for a wager, none went
willingly to the yew tree after vespers.

Mall Carke meanwhile became more solitary. It has been suggested
that remorse drove her deeper into unlawful practices; others say
she wished to discover Laura's condition and dared not seek
it by Christian means, since no church rite had ever
been her familiar instrument. Whatever the cause, she attempted, on
the anniversary of the girl's disappearance, a ceremony strictly condemned
by both clergy and prudent cunning folk.

At midnight she barred her door, covered the window, and
drew a circle upon the floor with chalk, graveyard mould,
and salt. Within it she placed a pewter dish filled
with water taken from three running streams. A rushlight burned
at the north, another at the south, and between them
lay a looking-glass face downward. She put nine pins into
the flame, naming no saint, and recited words which, fortunately,
have not been preserved.

At the ninth word there came a knock.

Mall remained within the circle.

A second knock sounded, louder than the first.

At the third, the latch lifted, although the door was
barred. A gust extinguished both lights, and in the darkness
a woman said:

"Mother Carke, you are required."

"By whom?"

"Lady Lairdale."

"For what service?"

"Her ladyship is in travail."

"Let her send for a lawful midwife."

"She has sent for you."

Mall felt the brass head of her staff grow warm
beneath her hand.

"What's my fee?"

"A purse of guineas."

"I'll take my shilling and my supper. Nowt more."

The unseen woman laughed.

When Mall relit the rushlight, the door stood open. Outside
waited a coach of such magnificence as might have carried
a duchess to court: its panels crimson, its lamps blue-white,
its wheels gilded, and its roof supported by carved figures
with wings. Four black horses pawed the ground, breathing silver
vapour. The driver wore a cocked hat and a many-caped
coat; beside the open door stood a lady in gray
silk, her face hidden by a veil.

No wheel-track marked the wet earth.

Mall put bread, scissors, salt, and a small Bible into
her pocket. She took her staff and entered.

The coach moved before she was seated.

Through the windows she saw Dardale fly past, though no
hoof rang upon the road. The chapel, the mill, and
Dame Ruth's dark cottage went by; then the coach climbed
the Moss, where rain drove level against the glass and
pale fires wandered over the bog. Presently they came to
a gate of wrought iron, twenty feet high, standing between
pillars surmounted by silver wolves.

Beyond rose Lairdale Castle.

Now, readers acquainted with the district will know that no
castle has stood entire at Lairdale within historic memory. A
curtain wall, two broken towers, and a vault choked with
nettles were all that remained even in Mall Carke's youth.
Yet she beheld terraces, windows innumerable, chimneys pouring aromatic smoke
into the night, and a great central keep whose battlements
appeared to touch the clouds. Music sounded from within—not loud,
but intricate and perpetually changing, as though every rain-drop had
become a tiny bell.

The veiled lady led Mall through a hall crowded with
revellers.

Lords and ladies danced under chandeliers of green flame. Their
clothes glittered with gold, pearls, and jewels; their faces were
exquisitely formed; but Mall, who knew where and how to
look, saw peculiarities which splendour could not entirely obscure. One
gentleman's shadow had horns. A lady in yellow satin left
wet webbed prints behind her. Another had beneath her lace
collar the shrivelled head of a hare. At the tables
stood pies, fruits, roasted meats, and crystal wines; yet their
fragrance reminded Mall of damp leaves, carrion, and earth newly
opened by a spade.

A page offered her a goblet.

"Drink, Mother."

"I'm none dry."

"A sweetmeat, then?"

"I've teeth enough left to refuse it."

A child in cloth of silver held up a golden
flower.

"For you."

Mall struck it aside with her staff. It fell upon
the floor as a chicken's foot.

The music stopped.

Every splendid face turned toward her.

Then, from somewhere high in the keep, came a woman's
scream.

The dancers laughed and resumed their measure.

Mall was hurried up a winding stair and into a
chamber hung with blue damask. A fire of cedar logs
burned on the hearth. There were mirrors with frames of
ivory, silver basins, embroidered cushions, and a bed canopied in
white, where a young lady lay with her hair spread
over the pillows.

For one dazzled instant Mall did not know her.

The face was thinner, but still beautiful; the hair remained
pale as flax; and on one wasted hand shone the
ring with its silver bell. Then the sufferer opened her
eyes.

"Mall," she said.

The enchantment broke.

The blue damask became rags of mouldering sacking. The cedar
fire was a heap of smoking peat. The mirrors were
pools of stagnant water in holes of the wall; the
ivory was bone; the silver basins, cracked wooden bowls. The
bed was no bed, but foul straw piled in the
corner of a roofless hovel where rain crawled down stones
green with slime.

And Laura Lew lay there.

Her gown, once perhaps fine, hung in filthy strips. Her
feet were bare and bleeding. A chain, slender as jewellery
in the glamour, passed from her ankle to an iron
staple. The bright hair was matted; the blue eyes had
grown enormous in her starved face.

Beside her stood the lord of Lairdale.

To Mall he no longer wore velvet. He was the
Moss giant in his rags, taller than the low roof
should have permitted, stooping like a withered tree. His amber
eyes shone with malignant content.

"Here is my silver lady," he said.

Mall's hand tightened around the Bible in her pocket.

Laura cried out again. Whatever else might be said of
Mother Carke, she knew her work. She laid aside fear,
boiled water, cut linen from the soundest portion of her
petticoat, and attended the girl through the dark hours.

No detail of that birth need be given. The old
woman herself never spoke freely of it. It is enough
to say that the child, if child it may be
called, did not cry like a human infant. Its first
note was thin, metallic, and merry—the sound of a little
bell shaken under water. It had too many fingers, a
fringe of black hair along its spine, and open eyes
of amber yellow.

The father received it tenderly.

"My heir," he said.

He laid the creature in a golden cradle, which to
Mall's unclouded sight was the skull of a horse.

Laura turned her face to the wall.

Near dawn the giant left them. The moment his footfall
ceased, Laura caught Mall's wrist with surprising strength.

"Listen. He'll pay thee."

"I'll take nowt."

"Take thy due, or he'll claim thou came as guest.
A shilling—thy proper fee—and bread if thou hast worked past
supper. No gold. No jewel. No favor. Not a thread
from this place."

"I ken."

"He'll press thee. He'll show thee riches."

"I ken, bairn."

Laura's fingers shook.

"Mall—can thou take me?"

The old woman looked at the iron chain, then at
the black gap where a door had seemed. Beyond it
moved lights and whispering forms. She drew the scissors from
her pocket and set them to the chain. The blades
snapped.

She touched the chain with the little Bible. A blue
flame ran along the links, and from every side of
the hovel came laughter.

"I cannot," she said.

"Pray, then."

Mall began the Lord's Prayer.

At the first words, stones fell from the wall. At
the second, the peat fire flared green. At the third,
Laura choked, pressing both hands to her throat.

"Stop!" she gasped. "I canna hear it here. He's put
something in me that twists when holy words are spoken."

Mall ceased. For all her hard dealings and doubtful arts,
tears stood in the beldam's eyes.

"Was thou wed?"

Laura looked toward the cradle.

"He said words by the brook. I thought there were
priests and candles. There were only bones stuck in the
mud. Oh, Mall, I saw all fair then. The castle,
the feasts, the dancing! For seven nights—or I thought seven—I
wore a crown. Then I asked to go home."

"And?"

"He showed me what was true."

Outside, the music swelled. It was exquisite and remote, rising
through the ruined place like moonlight through drowned trees.

"How long have I been gone?" Laura whispered.

"A year and a month."

She stared at Mall.

"I have been here a hundred years."

Footsteps approached.

Laura drew the old woman close. Her breath was cold.

"Tell Aunt Ruth I was sorry. Tell Bessie she saw
true. And if children ask, say the handsome folk have
hunger behind their smiles."

The chamber changed as the giant entered. Again there were
blue hangings, mirrors, a cedar fire, and a white bed.
Laura lay robed in silver, pale but radiant; the monstrous
infant slept beneath lace in a golden cradle.

"Mother Carke," said the lord, "you have served us well."

He opened a chest filled with coins. Guineas poured between
his fingers, ringing richly. Each bore a king's head on
one side and a dancing figure on the other.

"Take what your apron will carry."

"My fee is a shilling."

"You refused ten once."

"I refuse them again."

"Then take this ring."

He held out a great emerald surrounded by diamonds.

"A shilling."

"A length of silk?"

"A shilling."

"A secret? I can tell you where Sir Bertram buried
his plate. I can tell you who comes to your
door next winter. I can tell you the day and
manner of your death."

Mall's face twitched, for no temptation is stronger to one
who has spent a lifetime peering through cracks in the
wall of futurity.

But Laura stirred upon the bed and whispered, "Proper fee."

Mall struck her staff once upon the floor.

"One silver shilling, stamped wi' the lawful king. An' bread,
for I've passed supper."

The giant's smile vanished.

A small coin appeared upon the table, beside a crust
of coarse bread.

"Take them."

Mall examined the shilling. It had the right weight, the
right head, and the right worn edge. She bit it,
a custom then common enough, and put it in her
pocket. The bread she divided. Half she ate; half she
laid beside Laura.

The lord's amber eyes narrowed.

"That is not yours to give."

"It was mine when paid."

Laura seized the crust and hid it against her breast.

At once the castle shook.

The chandeliers swung; the mirrors darkened; the blue silk peeled
from the walls in streaming tatters. The noble lord lengthened
into his ragged deformity. His mouth opened too wide, and
through it Mall saw—not throat or tongue—but a red sunset
burning over endless black moss.

"Go, then!" he roared.

A dozen hands seized Mall from behind. They were fine
white hands, clawed hands, paws, wings, and the cold small
fingers of dead children. She was dragged down the stair
while the revellers spun around her, laughing. Their splendid clothes
fell away; one was a fox walking upright, one a
bundle of rushes, one a corpse crowned with rowan berries.
The veiled lady struck Mall across the face. The page
thrust the golden flower into her mouth, where it became
mud.

Then came the open air, storm, a coach door, and
violent motion.

The magnificent horses were now one skeletal mare. The gilded
carriage was a farmer's hurdle mounted upon two crooked wheels.
Its cushions were sodden straw, its lamps lumps of marsh
fire, and its driver a post of rotten wood wrapped
in a coat. It rushed over bog and stone, tipping
almost upright at every hollow.

Mall clung to her staff.

Behind her, from the receding ruin, Laura called once:

"Remember!"

The cry followed over the Moss long after the hovel
had vanished.

At sunrise Dame Ruth opened her cottage door and found
Mall Carke lying across the threshold.

The old woman's clothes were soaked, her face scratched, and
one of her shoes was missing. In her pocket were
a lawful silver shilling and a piece of black bread.
Around her wrist were five bruises shaped like long fingers.

She slept until evening. When she woke, she asked for
the parson.

What passed between them was never disclosed. Within a week
Mall burned her books, broke her divining glass, emptied her
charm-bags into running water, and took the brass head from
her hazel staff. For the rest of her life she
gathered herbs and attended the sick, but she would neither
tell fortunes nor turn a sieve, not even when the
squire's lady offered five pounds to discover a stolen necklace.

The shilling she gave to the church. The parson hesitated
before accepting it, but finally caused it to be melted
and added to the metal of a little bell used
at baptisms.

Dame Ruth did not long survive the news. Bessie Bell
married a farmer beyond the Tyne and had all her
children christened before they were eight days old, regardless of
snow, flood, or illness. She never wore green and would
not allow silver bells among her daughters' toys.

As for Laura, no rescue was attempted. One may judge
this harshly; but those who know the old beliefs understand
that she had crossed running water by her own will,
accepted the fairy ring, eaten at least once of fairy
food, and entered a marriage outside Christian witness. Four boundaries
had closed behind her. No mortal hand could easily reopen
them.

Yet there were later sightings.

A shepherd, sheltering near Lairdale ruins, heard a woman singing
under the ground and recognized an old spinning song of
Dardale. A charcoal-burner saw a pale girl gathering blackberries in
midwinter, an iron chain trailing from her ankle. And once,
many years afterward, a child became lost at dusk near
the wood. She returned before midnight carrying a crust of
black bread and said that a ragged lady with bright
hair had led her to the safe side of the
beck.

"She telt me not to cross," the child reported. "She
said she crossed hersel', and the road back was gone."

Wise folk think that Laura remains in the hollow country,
where one night may be a century and a century
no more than a dance; that she walks when the
red sunset lies upon the Moss, warning those still able
to choose; and that she must remain until the last
trumpet sounds over the Black Sisters and every hidden door
is broken.

Even now the people of Dardale do not step across
the beck after sundown. If music comes from the yew
tree, they stop their ears. If a stranger offers gold,
they look first at his feet. And when the wind
moves through the heather with a small, sweet chiming, like
silver bells very far away, no mother calls the sound
pretty.

She takes her child by the hand and hastens home.

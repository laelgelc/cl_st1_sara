You may ask McQuae to dinner on Tuesday, and he
will come on Wednesday with an appetite, an apology, and
the conviction that he is punctual.

This happened to me at my rooms in Duke Street.
We had finished the fish and were discussing whether my
cook's temper could survive the entrée when McQuae appeared in
the doorway, correctly dressed and entirely at ease.

"Hope I'm not late," he said.

"You are twenty-four hours late."

He looked at the table, then at the five people
seated round it, as though one or other of us
might explain the discrepancy.

"Isn't this Tuesday?"

"No. Wednesday."

"Great Scott!"

He drew out his watch, consulted it earnestly, and put
it back.

"You cannot tell Tuesday from Wednesday by a watch," I
observed.

"No, but it gives one something to begin with."

We made room for him. He ate the remains of
the fish, most of the entrée, and the portion of
cutlet intended for a delayed guest who, unlike McQuae, had
sent a telegram. He was beginning upon a serious conversation
with Lady Bellingham concerning municipal orchestras when he stopped, his
fork suspended.

"By Jove!" he exclaimed.

We had learned to fear this expression. It meant that
some fact, usually one of public importance, had at last
reached him.

"What is it now?"

"I ought to be in Scotland."

"Most of us ought to be somewhere else," said Lady
Bellingham.

"No; I mean I ought to be going. Train leaves
at eleven-ten."

It was then five minutes to eleven.

He had no luggage with him, and was in evening
dress. This did not distress him so much as it
ought to have done. He said he could buy a
toothbrush at York and perhaps borrow a shooting coat from
his host. I was rather glad he had to travel
as he stood. A man's faults should occasionally be brought
home to him, even if they have to accompany him
to Berwick in a white tie.

That was our first experience of McQuae's memory, though not
the last. His mind was not empty; on the contrary,
it was overcrowded. It retained the second verse of songs
no one sang, the names of horses which had lost
the Derby, and the private opinions of dead Cabinet Ministers.
It merely refused to keep anything of immediate use.

The house-boat luncheon was a more ambitious affair.

McQuae had hired a boat above Henley for July, and
invited the Farnboroughs, the Dacres, the little Westons, two unattached
young men, and myself to luncheon on the following Sunday.
As some of the families contained children, governesses, and aunts,
the invitation represented, in all, seventeen persons and a pug
dog.

We arrived by degrees. The river was bright, the awning
was up, and McQuae lay in a deck-chair reading an
illustrated paper.

"Hullo!" he said. "Beanfeast?"

"You might call it so," I replied. "You are giving
it."

He smiled courteously, supposing this to be wit. Then Mrs.
Farnborough kissed him, the children demanded ginger-beer, and the pug
dog went below and discovered the larder.

McQuae sat upright.

"Good God! Is this Sunday?"

"It has been all morning."

"And you've come to lunch?"

"That was the attractive feature of your invitation."

He went below. Presently we heard a cupboard opened, a
plate broken, and a woman's voice speaking with a freedom
which suggested that the cook had discovered the situation. McQuae
returned carrying half a loaf, a pot of anchovy paste,
and three peaches.

"There appears to have been some misunderstanding," he said.

The misunderstanding was that he had ordered nothing. His cook,
who had expected a quiet day, declared that she would
wash her hands of the whole business—figuratively, I trust, for
she was at that moment making an omelette. The luncheon
ultimately consisted of eggs, anchovy sandwiches, peaches cut into sixths,
and some cold mutton borrowed from a neighbouring boat whose
owner was asleep.

The children enjoyed it enormously. Children always do enjoy the
disasters which their elders are paying for. The adults were
less expansive, and Mrs. Dacre asked McQuae whether he made
a practice of inviting people to meals in order to
study their characters under privation.

"No, no," he said. "I remembered you perfectly. It was
the food I forgot."

This distinction did not console her.

Another day he drove Leena Harcourt to Richmond. Leena was
the young woman whom he then recollected being engaged to.
I say "then" because there had been two previous engagements
which escaped him at critical moments; one ended when he
forgot a garden party, the other when he introduced his
fiancée to a roomful of people as Miss—then paused and
asked her, in a whisper audible to everyone, what her
surname was.

On the Richmond expedition Hallyard accompanied them. Hallyard was a
long, solemn man, with knees particularly ill-designed for a dog-cart.
McQuae drove; Leena sat beside him; Hallyard occupied the small
seat behind and disapproved of the arrangement.

At Putney the horse shied from a steam-roller. McQuae pulled
the wrong rein, mounted the edge of the pavement, descended
again, and continued toward Barnes. The cart did not upset,
but Hallyard left it. He described a brief curve in
the air and arrived in a bed of municipal geraniums.

McQuae drove on.

At Richmond he turned to ask Hallyard for a match
and discovered only a rug.

"Where's Hallyard?" he said.

Leena looked behind.

"He was there at Putney."

"Was he?"

"You spoke to him."

"I often speak without receiving an answer. I supposed he
was sulking."

They drove back and found Hallyard in a chemist's shop,
having gravel removed from his person.

"Had an accident?" McQuae asked.

"Yes."

"Cart upset?"

"No," said Hallyard. "Only me."

The shock had shaken his grammar, but not his resentment.
He did not speak to McQuae for a month, which
McQuae failed to notice.

Naturally, when it was announced that McQuae intended to marry
Maud Penrith, we regarded the proposal as visionary. Marriage requires
a man to remember not only that he is married,
but to whom; and there are anniversaries, trains, presents, dinner
engagements, and other details with which the institution has been
needlessly encumbered.

I told him plainly that he could not be depended
upon to remember the day, the church, and the girl
in one morning.

"Maud will remind me," he said cheerfully.

"Maud," I replied, "will remind you once."

Maud was an admirable woman, handsome, decisive, and quite incapable
of accepting absent-mindedness as an explanation for anything. If a
man failed to appear, she assumed not that he had
forgotten but that he had perished; and I fancy she
would have preferred the latter, as being less disrespectful.

Nevertheless, the wedding took place. McQuae arrived at the right
church, on the right day, and, after one uneasy glance
at a bridesmaid, married the right woman. We had under-estimated
him.

For nearly a fortnight all went well.

Then I visited Scarborough.

It was September, and the town was full of persons
who had come for sea air and were keeping carefully
out of it. I had dined at my hotel and
was walking beneath the cliff. Rain had begun; the wind
drove it sideways, and the sea was behaving with that
noisy discourtesy peculiar to northern watering-places after dark.

At the foot of the steps I observed a man
crouched against the wall. He was wet, hatless, and so
dejected that I took him at first for a ruined
financier. When I came nearer he raised his face.

It was McQuae.

"What are you doing here?"

"Waiting for something to occur to me."

"Has it?"

"No."

I took him into a shelter and examined him. His
coat was soaked, one cuff was torn, and there was
sand in his hair.

"Where is Maud?"

"That," he said, "is the difficulty."

"Have you lost her?"

"I shouldn't put it quite so strongly."

"Where are your lodgings?"

"I don't know."

It appeared that they had arrived the previous evening and
taken rooms in a respectable house in a long street.
After breakfast McQuae went out to buy tobacco. He had
left the address on the mantelpiece, his money in another
coat, and his wife upstairs writing letters. He remembered that
the house had a green door and lace curtains.

"Half the town has green doors," I said.

"I know. I have knocked at most of them."

His first three attempts had produced sympathetic landladies. The fourth
had produced a retired colonel, who demanded his name and
regiment. At the ninth house a woman insisted that he
was her nephew from Leeds. At the fifteenth he was
offered a room at thirty-five shillings a week, with use
of the piano. By two o'clock he was hungry. By
four he had become notorious.

"Why didn't you go to the police?"

"I did."

"And?"

"One of them laughed."

"Very natural."

"I struck him."

This complicated the narrative. McQuae, having struck the policeman, had
run down an alley, crossed a yard containing washing, climbed
a wall, and fallen into a water-butt. He escaped arrest
only because the policeman, still laughing, pursued the wrong man.
Since then McQuae had wandered along the shore, unwilling to
risk the streets and unable to remember the name of
either the landlady or the street.

"Surely Maud will have informed the police."

"That is what makes returning awkward."

There was no vanity in the remark. He desired to
find his wife, but his anticipation of the reunion was
not wholly pleasurable.

I took him to my hotel. The manager objected to
his appearance until I guaranteed that he was neither a
vagrant nor a fugitive. Strict accuracy would have made this
difficult, so I guaranteed only the bill. McQuae ate soup,
fish, beef, pudding, cheese, and biscuits. He then became more
hopeful.

"I knew I should find the place eventually."

"You found me."

"It comes to the same thing."

Next morning we consulted a solicitor, Mr. Brogden, whose expression
suggested that Scarborough supplied him with stranger cases than ours,
though not before eleven o'clock. He placed a sheet of
foolscap before McQuae.

"Describe the house."

"Green door."

"Yes?"

"Windows."

"How many?"

"Several."

"Name of street?"

"No."

"Name of landlady?"

"It began with a letter."

Mr. Brogden put down his pen.

"Can you describe your wife?"

"Certainly," said McQuae, with relief. "Tall, dark, grey travelling dress,
and extremely annoyed."

"That last feature may be temporary."

"I doubt it."

Mr. Brogden instituted inquiries among the lodging-house keepers. A messenger
went to the police. Advertisements were drafted but withheld, as
I pointed out that "Gentleman seeks wife, address forgotten" might
bring more wives than McQuae could reasonably explain.

Shortly after luncheon news arrived. A Mrs. Pritchard, of Seventeen
Belgrave Crescent, had reported the disappearance of a newly married
gentleman who had gone out for tobacco and failed to
return. His wife was awaiting information with composure.

"That means Maud," said McQuae gloomily.

We drove there in a cab. At the corner he
asked whether it was too late to send a telegram
saying he had been unexpectedly detained in York.

"Much too late."

"Could I have been unconscious?"

"Not continuously. You ate two breakfasts."

He sighed.

Mrs. Pritchard opened the green door. Behind her, in the
hall, stood Maud. She was pale, erect, and perfectly silent.

McQuae entered after the manner of a hero restored in
the final act, except that heroes are seldom damp, indebted
to a solicitor, and wanted for assaulting a constable.

The door closed.

I saw him at the club a week later.

"Well?" I asked. "What did Maud say?"

He considered.

"Oh, much what I expected."

He never told me what that was; and for once
I believe his memory was acting in his own interest.

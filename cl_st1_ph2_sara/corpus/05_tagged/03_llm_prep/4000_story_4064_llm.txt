In the year 1552, when the broad meadows of the
Volga lay beneath the summer sun like a carpet of
living green, Ivan Vassilievitch, Tzar of all the Russias, marched
against Kazan.

He was then but two-and-twenty years of age. Already men
had begun to call him Ivan the Terrible; yet the
name signified rather the terror of his enemies than that
darker terror which he was afterwards to become among his
own people. He was brave, ardent, devout after the fashion
of his time, eager for glory, and not incapable of
mercy. Those who saw him kneeling before the sacred pictures,
or riding in gilded armour at the head of his
hosts, might well believe that Heaven had raised up a
great prince for Russia.

Kazan, the stronghold of the Tatars, stood proudly above the
Volga. Its walls were of timber and earth, but of
prodigious thickness; its towers were high; its gates were defended
by valiant warriors; and within rose the palace of Khan
Yediguer, the mosques of the Mahometans, and the slender minarets
from which the muezzins called the faithful to prayer. Copper
roofs and gilded crescents flashed in the morning light. Below,
the river bore boats laden with provisions, arms, powder, and
the huge guns in which the Russians placed their chief
hope.

For the conquest of Russia itself had been begun by
mounted bowmen; but Ivan was resolved that Kazan should be
subdued by artillery.

With him came princes and boyards, foreign engineers, Strelitzes armed
with arquebuses, miners skilled in driving passages beneath walls, and
a multitude of soldiers gathered from his wide dominions. Among
the commanders none stood higher in courage or reputation than
Prince Andrej Kourbsky. His temper was proud, his judgment quick,
and his arm renowned in battle. He loved Russia sincerely;
he had served the Tzar faithfully; and Ivan, who could
then still admire merit without fearing it, trusted him as
one of the pillars of his throne.

The Tatars, meanwhile, made ready for a desperate defence. They
knew that if Kazan fell, the independence of their nation
was at an end. Old men who remembered former victories
spoke in the market-place; warriors sharpened their sabres; women carried
stones and water to the walls; and the priests of
their faith promised Paradise to those who should die fighting
the Christians. Whatever may be thought of their cause or
creed, their constancy cannot be denied. They were defending their
homes, their children, and the graves of their fathers; and
courage is courage, whether it beat beneath a Russian coat
of mail or a Tatar robe.

The siege had scarcely begun when tidings reached Ivan that
a great force of Crimean Tatars was advancing to relieve
the city. Had these horsemen broken upon the Russian camp
while the garrison made a sally, the besiegers might have
been crushed between two enemies.

Kourbsky was sent against them.

He marched swiftly, though the rain had softened the roads
and swollen the smaller streams. Near Toula he found the
invading host spread over the plain, their horses restless, their
banners tossing, their archers prepared to pour forth those clouds
of arrows which had so often thrown the Russian ranks
into disorder.

Kourbsky formed his men without delay.

"Let no man think of the distance behind him," he
said. "Kazan is before us, though its walls be many
leagues away; for if we fail here, the siege is
lost. Stand fast, and Russia stands with you."

The Tatars charged with wild cries. Their light cavalry swept
round the Russian flanks; arrows rattled upon shields and breastplates;
wounded horses plunged among the spearmen. Again and again they
came on, swift as a storm driven across the steppe.
But Kourbsky kept his ranks together. When the enemy, wearied
by repeated assaults, gathered for one last effort, he led
his own cavalry against them.

The trumpets sounded. The Russian horsemen bent over their saddles.
Steel met steel; banners went down; the dust rose so
thickly that men fought as in a cloud. Kourbsky's horse
was wounded beneath him, yet he mounted another and pressed
onward. At length the Crimean line broke. The fugitives scattered
over the plain, and the road to Kazan was safe.

The prince returned to the camp covered with dust, his
armour dinted, and his face pale from fatigue. Ivan embraced
him before the boyards.

"Thou hast served me nobly," said the young Tzar.

"I have served Russia," answered Kourbsky; and the words, though
spoken without ill intent, were remembered in after years.

The siege now proceeded with a regularity hitherto little known
in Russian warfare. Trenches crept nearer to the walls. Wooden
towers were raised, from which marksmen fired upon the defenders.
Cannon thundered day and night; the earth shook beneath their
discharges, and smoke rolled heavily over the camp. The Tatars
repaired at night what had been shattered by day. They
made fierce sallies, burned works, slew engineers, and vanished behind
their gates before the Russians could surround them.

There was near the city a lofty wooden tower which
commanded the ground and sorely troubled the besiegers. From it
Tatar archers shot down upon the trenches, while guns placed
beneath its shelter swept the approaches. Ivan's foreign miners declared
that powder must do what courage alone could not. They
drove a gallery beneath the earth, supporting its roof with
beams, and carried into it many barrels of gunpowder.

All was made ready for the great assault.

Before dawn the Russian camp lay strangely silent. The soldiers
stood in their ranks; the archers held their bows; the
gunners waited beside their pieces with lighted matches. Priests passed
along the lines bearing crosses and sacred images. Here and
there men knelt upon the damp ground to confess their
sins, for they knew that many who beheld the sunrise
would never see it set.

Within Kazan also there was prayer. From the minarets sounded
the solemn voices of the muezzins. Upon the walls the
Tatars waited, their arrows set to the string and their
sabres loosened in the sheath. No man spoke loudly. It
seemed as though two nations stood listening for the judgment
of God.

The eastern sky grew pale. Then a narrow rim of
fire appeared beyond the river, and the copper-clad minarets glowed
as though kindled from within.

Suddenly the earth heaved.

A roar greater than that of any cannon burst beneath
the walls. Beams, stones, soil, weapons, and human forms were
hurled into the air; the wooden tower rose, broke asunder,
and fell amid a cloud of dust. Before the echoes
had ceased, the Russian guns opened together.

"God with us!" cried the soldiers.

They rushed towards the breach.

The Tatars met them with a courage worthy of a
happier cause. Stones, boiling water, arrows, and bullets descended upon
the advancing ranks. Men fell into the ditch and were
trampled by those behind them. Ladders broke under their burden.
The first who reached the summit were cut down; yet
others took their places, and still others followed.

Kourbsky fought near the chief breach. He had laid aside
the ornaments of command lest they should mark him for
every bowman, but his soldiers knew him by his voice.

"Forward, children of Russia! The wall is won if ye
believe it won!"

A standard appeared upon the shattered rampart, sank, and rose
again. A Tatar seized its staff; Kourbsky struck him down.
The Russians poured through the opening, and the struggle passed
from the wall into the streets.

Then began a combat more dreadful than the assault. Kazan
was won step by step, house by house, street by
street. From windows and roofs the defenders cast missiles upon
the Russians. Barricades rose at every turning. Flames broke out,
whether kindled by the besiegers or the besieged no man
could tell, and the wind drove smoke and sparks over
the city. The cries of women and children mingled with
the clash of weapons, the reports of arquebuses, and the
deep voices of priests chanting hymns behind the advancing troops.

Near the Khan's palace the last great body of Tatars
gathered round Yediguer. Their garments were torn, their faces blackened,
but their spirit remained unbroken.

"Let us die where our fathers ruled!" they cried.

Many fulfilled their word. But resistance became hopeless. The Russian
banners waved from the towers; the gates had been forced;
and armed men filled every street. Yediguer at last surrendered
himself to Ivan, who received him not as a wild
beast to be slaughtered, but as a fallen prince.

The Tzar forbade the murder of the captive. He checked,
so far as command could check them, the excesses of
victory; and he caused the Khan to be treated with
honour. Yediguer was afterwards baptized into the Christian faith, taking
the name of Simeon, and lived among the Russians who
had overthrown his throne.

Thus fell Kazan. Bells rang throughout Russia; churches were filled
with worshippers; and Ivan returned to Moskwa amid such rejoicings
as few sovereigns had ever received. He had displayed courage
in danger, steadfastness in difficulty, skill in the use of
new arts of war, gratitude towards his servants, and mercy
towards a conquered enemy. Alas! how gladly might the historian
close his book at such a triumph! But the morning
does not reveal the tempests of the afternoon, and the
fairest promise in a prince is vain unless conscience keep
watch over power.

Not long afterwards Ivan was seized by a dangerous illness.
His physicians despaired of him; prayers were offered in every
church; and those who stood round his bed believed that
the Tzar's last hour had come. Ivan required the boyards
to swear allegiance to his infant son, Dmitri. Some obeyed;
others hesitated, fearing that the child could not hold the
throne amid ambitious relatives and contending nobles. Kourbsky's conduct at
this crisis was not forgotten. Whether he refused the oath,
delayed it, or merely associated with those who questioned it,
suspicion entered Ivan's heart.

The Tzar recovered; but he arose from his sick-bed changed.
He had looked upon men while they believed him dying,
and had seen how quickly obedience considers a new master.
A wise prince might have learned humility from that knowledge.
Ivan learned distrust.

For a time the gentle Tzaritza Anastasia still restrained him.
She possessed no army and held no office; yet her
mild voice could accomplish what boyards, bishops, and counsellors attempted
in vain. When anger darkened Ivan's brow, she calmed him;
when informers whispered accusations, she besought him to inquire before
condemning; and when his pride was wounded, she reminded him
that the ruler of men is himself under the rule
of God.

Then Anastasia died.

Ivan persuaded himself that she had been poisoned. Perhaps sorrow
disordered a mind already inclined to violence; perhaps wicked favourites
encouraged his darkest suspicions; perhaps power, long obeyed and seldom
rebuked, had nourished the sleeping lion within him until it
awoke ravenous. Whatever causes may be pleaded, they cannot change
the character of the deeds which followed. Affliction may explain
cruelty; it cannot make cruelty just.

Executions became frequent. Noble families were ruined upon secret accusations.
Men vanished from their houses, and their wives dared not
ask whither they had been carried. To have served the
Tzar greatly was no protection, for extraordinary service itself provoked
fear. Ivan began to see conspirators in brave commanders, poisoners
in physicians, traitors in counsellors, and spies even among priests.

Kourbsky, then engaged in the Livonian war, received warning that
he was marked for death. The message was brought at
night. The prince read it twice and stood long without
speaking.

In his chamber waited Vasili Shibanoff, his stirrup-bearer and serf.
Vasili was neither learned nor illustrious. No chronicler had written
of his birth; no province obeyed him; no troop followed
his banner. Yet there are souls whose nobility needs no
herald, and his was one.

"We must ride," said Kourbsky at last.

"When, my lord?"

"Now."

Two horses were prepared. They passed silently through the sleeping
town and took the road towards Livonia. Behind them lay
Russia; before them lay exile, reproach, and the uncertain protection
of a foreign king. Kourbsky knew that if he remained,
he would probably die without trial. He knew also that
flight would be called treason and that his enemies would
seize it as proof of every accusation.

The riders had not gone far when Kourbsky's horse stumbled
and could proceed no farther. Pursuit was feared.

Without waiting to be commanded, Vasili dismounted.

"Take mine, my lord."

"And thou?"

"I shall follow as I may."

Kourbsky looked at him. In that lonely road, beneath the
cold stars, the difference between prince and serf seemed a
small thing; the one held life at the hands of
the other.

"They may seize thee."

"Then let them."

Kourbsky mounted and rode on. Vasili followed until distance and
darkness hid his master from sight.

The prince escaped into Livonia and entered the service of
Sigismund Augustus, King of Poland. Here his conduct becomes difficult
to defend. That he should flee from an unjust death
may be pardoned; that he should seek shelter from a
foreign sovereign may be excused; but that a soldier should
bear arms against the country whose honours he has received
is a far graver matter. Ivan's wickedness was not Russia's
guilt, and the tyranny of one ruler did not make
the villages, churches, and people of his realm fit objects
for an exile's sword.

Yet Kourbsky believed himself justified. He brooded over his wrongs,
remembered former services, and grew bitter against the master who
had once embraced him after victory. At length he wrote
Ivan a letter, renouncing his allegiance and charging him with
cruelty, ingratitude, and the blood of innocent men.

When it was finished, a messenger had to be found.

Who would carry such a letter to Ivan the Terrible?
To place it in the Tzar's hand was almost to
place one's own head beneath the axe. Kourbsky did not
command any man to undertake the task.

Vasili Shibanoff came forward.

"I will bear it."

"Thou knowest its words?"

"I know enough."

"And what awaits thee?"

"God knows."

Kourbsky was moved. He may have sought to dissuade him;
but Vasili remained firm. Perhaps the servant believed that loyalty
required him to complete his master's act. Perhaps he hoped,
by silence under torment, to protect those who had aided
the escape. He took the letter and returned alone towards
Moskwa.

Ivan was leaving church when the humble messenger approached him.
Around the Tzar stood armed attendants and richly clothed boyards,
each watching the sovereign's face, for in such times a
smile might bring advancement and a frown destruction.

Vasili bowed and held forth the writing.

"From whom?" demanded Ivan.

"From Prince Andrej Kourbsky."

At that hated name the Tzar's countenance changed. He snatched
the letter; and, whether by accident or in sudden fury,
the iron point of his staff came down upon Vasili's
foot and pierced it. Blood flowed into the dust. The
servant neither cried out nor withdrew.

Ivan broke the seal and read.

The letter spared him nothing. It reminded him of Kourbsky's
wounds, victories, and faithful service; accused him of rewarding defenders
with death; and summoned him to answer before the judgment-seat
of God, where no guards, torturers, or flatterers could protect
him.

The boyards stood motionless. Not one dared lift his eyes.

Ivan looked at the bleeding messenger.

"Who helped thy master to flee?"

Vasili was silent.

"Who correspondeth with him? What names are hidden behind these
reproaches?"

"I brought the letter."

"That is not an answer."

"It is all my answer."

Ivan delivered him to the torturers.

From morning until night they questioned him. Pain was added
to pain; promises followed threats; life, freedom, and reward were
offered if he would but name a single accomplice. Vasili
had names enough in his knowledge, perhaps—men who had warned
Kourbsky, men who had opened gates, men who had provided
horses, men whose wives and children would perish if he
spoke. But no name passed his lips.

At intervals an officer returned to Ivan.

"What saith he?"

"Nothing, Gosudar."

"Question him again."

Towards evening the Tzar himself caused Vasili to be brought
before him. The man could scarcely stand. Yet his eyes
remained clear, and no hatred distorted his face.

"Thy master abandoned thee," said Ivan. "He rideth free among
our enemies, while thou sufferest in his stead. Curse him,
tell me the truth, and live."

Vasili answered faintly, but all heard him.

"My master is a good and gallant prince. I pray
God to preserve him."

Even Ivan was amazed.

"Dost thou praise the man who sent thee to death?"

"He trusted me."

Thereupon Vasili sank. His last words were not for himself,
but a prayer for Kourbsky; and thus died a serf
whom princes might have been proud to equal. His loyalty
may have been given to a master whose own duty
had become doubtful, but fidelity in the servant was no
less beautiful because the lord was imperfect. A diamond shines
most wonderfully upon black cloth; and in the darkness of
Ivan's court the steadfastness of Vasili Shibanoff shone with a
light which cruelty itself could not extinguish.

For a moment, it is said, the Tzar admired him.
But admiration which produces no amendment is only another witness
against the guilty soul.

Ivan's tyranny now assumed a more dreadful shape. He divided
the country and formed around himself a chosen body called
the Oprichnina. The Oprichniks dressed in black, rode black horses,
and bore strange emblems—a dog's head, to signify that they
would hunt out treason, and a broom, to show that
they would sweep it away. In truth, they hunted wealth
and swept away the innocent. They plundered houses, tortured citizens,
and filled the roads with terror. To be accused was
almost to be condemned; to be rich was dangerous; to
have an enemy was often fatal.

The Tzar mingled these crimes with extravagant religious observances. He
rose at unseasonable hours for prayer; prostrated himself until his
forehead was bruised; rang bells; chanted with his followers; and
then went forth to give orders for torment and death.
Such devotion was not faith, but fanaticism. The man who
bows before an image while he tramples upon mercy does
not honour God; he mocks Him.

At length Ivan perceived that the Church must have a
chief whom the people respected. His choice fell upon Feeleep,
or Philip, the holy superior of the monastery of Solovki,
far away amid the cold waters of the White Sea.

Feeleep had been born among the boyards, but had renounced
worldly greatness. Under his care the monastery prospered; roads were
made, mills built, the poor relieved, and the brethren ruled
with firmness and tenderness. His white hairs, calm countenance, and
blameless life had made his name reverenced throughout Russia.

Ivan summoned him to Moskwa and offered him the dignity
of Metropolitan.

Feeleep knew the Tzar. He knew also the weight of
that lofty office, a heavy freight for the strongest bark.
He would gladly have returned to his northern solitude, where
bells sounded over the grey waters and men's passions seemed
far away.

"I cannot accept," he said, "unless the Oprichnina be abolished."

Ivan's anger rose. No one was now accustomed to setting
conditions before him. Yet he desired Feeleep's name and authority,
and promises were made. The monk at last consented, not
from ambition, but from the hope that, standing near the
throne, he might shield the oppressed.

For a while his influence prevailed. Condemned men found mercy;
accusations were examined; the Tzar listened when the Metropolitan spoke
to him alone. Feeleep did not humiliate Ivan before others
when private counsel might suffice. He remembered that rebuke is
intended to save the sinner, not to display the courage
of him who gives it.

But the restraint did not endure. The Oprichniks feared the
holy man, for every life he preserved diminished their power,
and every merciful word reproached their deeds. They whispered that
Feeleep protected traitors. Ivan, weary of resistance, returned to bloodshed.
Massacres began anew.

Then Feeleep resolved that silence would itself become a sin.

One day the cathedral of the Assumption was filled with
worshippers. Lamps burned before the sacred pictures; incense floated beneath
the lofty roof; priests in splendid vestments moved about the
altar; and the deep chant of the choir rolled through
the church.

Ivan entered with his Oprichniks. They wore dark garments and
high caps which half disguised them, as though men should
hide their faces while asking the blessing of Heaven upon
wickedness. The crowd made way. No one dared whisper.

Feeleep stood upon the raised place reserved for the Metropolitan.

Ivan approached.

"Holy father, bless us."

Feeleep looked beyond him and made no sign.

The Tzar repeated, more sternly, "Metropolitan, I ask thy blessing."

Still Feeleep was silent.

At last a boyard touched his robe and said, trembling,
"My lord, the Gosudar standeth before thee."

"I know him not," answered Feeleep. "I know not the
Tzar in these strange garments, nor can I recognise the
ruler of Christian Russia among servants of blood."

A shudder passed through the church.

Ivan's eyes flashed. "Dost thou defy me here?"

Feeleep raised his hand, not in blessing, but in warning.

"I beseech thee, O Tzar, fear the judgment of God.
The sacrifice of praise is offered here, but without these
walls innocent blood crieth from the earth. Thou art set
above men to protect them, not to destroy them. All
earthly greatness passeth away. The crown, the sceptre, the guards
around thee—what will these avail when thou standest naked in
soul before the King of kings?"

"Be silent!"

"I have been silent too long. Where are the laws
of Russia? Where is mercy? Even the heathen have justice
among them, but thy people are slain without trial. I
am a stranger and pilgrim upon earth, as thou art;
yet while I bear this pastoral staff I must speak
for the flock."

The congregation expected instant death. Men dared scarcely breathe. Ivan
gripped his own staff until his hand whitened.

"Take heed," he said. "I was too gentle with thee."

Feeleep's countenance did not change.

"Use thy power. I fear God more than I fear
thee."

Ivan left the cathedral, and the black-clad Oprichniks followed him.
The service continued, but all knew that the Metropolitan's doom
was sealed.

Openly to murder one so venerated might have roused the
people; therefore false witnesses were sought. Men from Feeleep's former
monastery were threatened or bribed. Charges of misconduct and sorcery
were invented. A council was assembled, not to discover truth,
but to furnish injustice with the garments of law.

Feeleep answered little. He saw that judgment had been determined
beforehand.

On a day of solemn worship, while he officiated in
the cathedral, armed men entered. Before the altar they read
the sentence which deprived him of his office. They tore
away his pastoral vestments, clothed him in a coarse monk's
robe, and drove him from the church amid insults.

The people wept. Some stretched out their hands towards him,
but the guards forced them back.

Feeleep turned once upon the cathedral steps.

"Pray," he said, "pray for Russia."

He was carried from prison to prison. The cells were
damp and narrow; food was scanty; his gaolers were ordered
to treat him harshly. Yet those who came near him
found no bitterness in his speech. He prayed for his
enemies and still sent entreaties to Ivan, not asking release,
but mercy for the people.

To break him, the Tzar caused the severed head of
one of his relatives to be shown him.

"Behold," said the messenger, "what thy obstinacy hath brought upon
thy house."

Feeleep looked upon the dead face, made the sign of
the cross, and answered, "Blessed are they who suffer innocently.
Tell the Tzar that blood cannot wash away blood."

Ivan might imprison the body, but he could not master
the soul. This was intolerable to him. Tyrants often endure
thieves and flatterers more easily than one upright man, because
virtue possesses an authority which no decree can confer and
no execution wholly destroy.

Some years passed, and Ivan prepared to march against Novgorod,
whose citizens he suspected of disloyalty. Before setting forth upon
that terrible expedition, he desired Feeleep's blessing—or, more truly, he
desired to say that it had been granted.

He sent Maluta Skouratof, one of the fiercest of the
Oprichniks, to the monastery where the deposed Metropolitan was confined.

It was winter. Snow lay deep around the walls, and
the wind moaned through the pines. Within his poor cell
Feeleep knelt before a small sacred picture. A lamp burned
dimly beside him.

The door opened, and Maluta entered.

Feeleep rose.

"I know why thou art come," he said quietly.

Maluta concealed his uneasiness beneath roughness. "The Tzar is departing
on his business. Give him thy blessing."

"Upon what business?"

"That concerneth not thee."

"It concerneth every shepherd of Russia if the flock is
to be slaughtered."

"The Tzar demandeth thy blessing."

"I bless him if he repent, cease from innocent blood,
and rule with mercy."

"Bless him as he is."

"I cannot bless wickedness."

Maluta stepped nearer. "Consider well. One word may preserve thy
life."

"My life hath long been in God's hands."

"Then thy death is upon thine own head."

"No. It will be upon the head of him who
commandeth it, and upon the hand of him who obeyeth."

For an instant the murderer hesitated. Feeleep's calmness seemed to
fill the narrow cell and make the armed man small.
But fear of Ivan was stronger in Maluta than fear
of God. He seized a cushion, or, as others relate,
the old man's own mantle, and pressed it upon his
face.

There was no cry. A brief struggle, a prayer half
heard, and the venerable Metropolitan was dead.

Maluta went forth and declared that Feeleep had been overcome
by the foulness and heat of the cell. The monks
knew the truth, but dared not utter it aloud. They
entered and found the old man lying as peacefully as
if sleep had descended upon him after prayer.

Thus perished Feeleep, who might have lived in honour had
he flattered a wicked sovereign, but who chose disgrace, imprisonment,
and death rather than bless cruelty. His was a loyalty
higher than obedience to any earthly ruler: loyalty to conscience,
to the helpless, and to God.

Did this murder reform Ivan? Alas, no. The blood of
the martyr cried in vain to ears which had resolved
not to hear. Novgorod was visited with devastation. Houses were
plundered, citizens slain, and families driven beneath the winter sky.
Other cities trembled. Boyards bowed lower; favourites watched one another;
and even the Oprichnik who tortured at command knew that
another might torture him upon the morrow.

Yet Ivan was not wholly devoid of great qualities. He
strengthened the state, encouraged trade, corresponded with foreign courts, and
understood much that duller rulers never perceive. This only deepens
the sorrow of his history. A weak man who does
little evil because he can do little aught is less
terrible than a gifted man who turns strength, courage, and
intelligence into instruments of misery.

His long tempestuous day drew towards its dreadful evening.

In a fit of fury he struck his eldest son,
the Tzarevitch Ivan, with the iron-pointed staff he carried. The
blow was mortal. Then the father's rage vanished, and he
beheld, not a disobedient subject, but his own child dying
at his feet.

He threw himself beside him, kissed him, called him by
tender names, and implored physicians and saints to restore the
life his own hand had destroyed. But neither imperial command
nor frantic repentance could recall the departing spirit. The son
forgave him and died.

There are punishments which no executioner can inflict. From that
hour Ivan carried within himself a wound deeper than any
made by sword or spear.

He survived until 1584. Death found him at chess, amid
the splendours of the palace and the fears of a
court which had learned to tremble at his slightest motion.
The hand that had signed so many sentences grew still;
the voice that had condemned nobles, priests, soldiers, and citizens
was silent; and the Tzar passed to that tribunal of
which Kourbsky had warned him and before which Feeleep had
declared that crown and sceptre were of no avail.

This has been a horrible story, and perhaps its scenes
of blood and suffering have been lingered over too long.
Yet they have not been told merely that we may
shudder at Ivan. The crimes of powerful men are common
enough in history; courage like that of Vasili Shibanoff and
Feeleep is rarer and more worthy of remembrance.

Kourbsky was brave in battle, but his fidelity failed amid
persecution and exile. Ivan was brave before Kazan, but became
the slave of suspicion and wrath. Vasili, who possessed neither
rank nor freedom, endured torment rather than betray his master.
Feeleep, stripped of office and guarded by murderers, spoke truth
when a whole court was silent.

The Tzar had armies, cannon, treasure, judges, and executioners. Vasili
had only loyalty. Feeleep had only faith. Yet the terror
of Ivan perished with his power, while the memory of
those whom he could torture and kill remained unconquered.

For victories won by force endure but a season. Walls
are rebuilt, kingdoms change masters, and banners once triumphant become
dust. But the victories of constancy and conscience belong to
no single age. They teach the servant that lowly station
cannot prevent nobleness; the priest that peace purchased by silence
may be guilt; the ruler that authority without mercy is
ruin; and every man that it is better to die
faithful than to live by betrayal.

So, against the blackest night of that unhappy reign, two
lights continue to shine: the poor stirrup-bearer praying for his
absent lord, and the venerable Metropolitan refusing to bless bloodshed.
Ivan conquered Kazan; but Vasili and Feeleep achieved the greater
conquest, for they overcame fear.

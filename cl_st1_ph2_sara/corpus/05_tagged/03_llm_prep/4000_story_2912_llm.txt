# Tommy Puffer's Garden

Everybody in Bell's Corners knew Tommy Puffer, and almost everybody
disapproved of him.

He was a thin, brown, ragged boy of twelve, with
a shock of sunburned hair, a pair of very bright
gray eyes, and no cap worth mentioning. His trousers were
patched in places where respectable trousers were not supposed to
need patching, and his elbows appeared through his sleeves with
a boldness which Miss Octavia Knox considered positively brazen.

Tommy lived with his father in a weather-beaten little house
beyond the mill. Mr. Puffer worked when work was offered,
which was not always; and when there was money he
spent too much of it foolishly, while when there was
none he sat by the stove and complained of his
luck. The house had a broken window stuffed with rags,
three leaning fence palings, and a doorstep which seemed always
to be waiting for somebody to scrub it.

Naturally, the respectable people of Bell's Corners concluded that Tommy
was a bad boy. Respectable people are often very quick
at conclusions. It saves time.

Tommy did steal apples—though never more than he could eat—and
he did whistle in the street on Sundays. He teased
the grocer's cat, tied a tin cup to Deacon Bell's
gate, and once put three green frogs in the schoolmaster's
desk. All this was quite true. But it was also
true that nobody had ever taught Tommy where mischief ought
to stop, or given him many better things with which
to occupy his active hands and quicker mind.

Miss Octavia Knox had an especial dislike for him.

"For the land's sake, don't come leaning over my fence!"
she cried one May afternoon, when Tommy paused beside her
garden. "You'll break the palings, if nothing else."

"I ain't techin' 'em," said Tommy.

"You are looking at my tulips."

Tommy stared at her. Even he could find no ready
answer to that accusation.

"Can't a feller look?" he asked at last.

"Not with such a suspicious expression."

Tommy's expression was not suspicious at all. It was hungry.

Miss Octavia's tulips stood in two splendid rows, crimson and
yellow, their smooth cups full of sunshine. Behind them were
narcissus, white and fragrant, and beyond those a bed of
hyacinths, blue as bits of the April sky. Miss Octavia
had the finest garden in Bell's Corners and knew it.
Her house was square and white, with green shutters, muslin
curtains, shining door-brasses, and a graveled walk upon which no
weed was permitted to show its impertinent head.

Tommy loved that garden.

It was one of the secrets of his life, and
Tommy had several secrets, because nobody had ever taken the
trouble to ask him about them. In the barren patch
behind the Puffer house he had planted a handful of
marigold seeds begged from the minister's wife. Only two had
come up. His father had accidentally trodden on one, and
a stray hen had scratched out the other; but for
several weeks Tommy had visited the bare earth every morning,
seeing there, by that beautiful vision which belongs to the
hopeful, rows and rows of crimson and purple and gold.

He knew every flower in Miss Octavia's garden. He knew
when the first crocus opened and which rosebush had suffered
in the winter. He knew that the larkspur needed thinning
and that one of the pansy beds was too dry.
He had learned these things by watching, for Tommy's eyes,
although publicly devoted to mischief, were privately devoted to beauty.

"You keep out of here," said Miss Octavia severely. "Last
year somebody broke my best lily stalk."

"Warn't me."

"I dare say."

This meant that she did not believe him. Most people
did not.

Tommy thrust his hands into his pockets and slouched away,
whistling loudly to show that he cared nothing for Miss
Octavia, her opinion, or her tulips. But at the corner
he looked back at the glowing garden, and his face
softened.

There was one person in Bell's Corners who never believed
ill of Tommy. That person was Bessie Lane.

Bessie was eleven and lived with her widowed mother in
two neat rooms over Mr. White's harness shop. She had
been lame since babyhood and could walk only a few
painful steps with crutches. Most of her days were spent
in a wide chair by the window, where she sewed
patchwork, read old storybooks, and watched the village pass below.

Mrs. Lane earned a small living by sewing. Their rooms
were poor, but the floor was clean, the muslin curtains
were mended, and a pot of pink geraniums bloomed on
the sill. Bessie herself had a pale little face, serious
blue eyes, and two long braids of fair hair. She
was patient because she had been obliged to learn patience
early; but she was not dull. Her laugh was as
gay as the brook in springtime, and when Tommy Puffer
heard it he generally found occasion to hear it again.

Bessie's friendship was the one little white blossom of pure
affection in the arid desert of Tommy's existence. He would
have fought any boy in Bell's Corners for laughing at
her crutches. He carried her books from the school she
could attend only in good weather, ran errands for Mrs.
Lane, and brought Bessie treasures which had little value to
anyone else—a blue jay's feather, a round red pebble, a
bird's deserted nest, and once a broken china cup with
a painted violet on it.

"You always find the nicest things, Tommy," Bessie said.

"Shucks! They ain't nothin'."

But he went away with his ears glowing.

One June afternoon Tommy appeared below Bessie's window and whistled
their particular signal—two short notes and one long one.

Bessie leaned out.

"Can you come down?" he called. "I got somethin' to
show you."

Mrs. Lane helped Bessie descend the narrow stairs. Tommy took
one crutch and walked slowly on her weaker side, as
attentive as any courtly knight, although his bare toes showed
through his shoes.

"Where are we going?" Bessie asked.

"Jist down to Mercer's. You wait."

Mr. Mercer's shop sold nearly everything: ribbons, buttons, peppermint sticks,
tin pans, shoes, lamp chimneys, slate pencils, and toys at
Christmas. In its window stood a doll such as Bell's
Corners had never before beheld.

She was nearly two feet high, with shining brown curls,
rosy cheeks, blue glass eyes that opened and shut, and
tiny white teeth showing between coral lips. She wore a
blue silk dress trimmed with lace, a blue bonnet tied
with satin ribbons, white stockings, and black slippers with pearl
buttons. Around her neck was a string of imitation pearls,
which seemed real enough to Bessie and Tommy.

"There!" said Tommy triumphantly.

Bessie stopped breathing for a moment.

"Oh!"

That was all she could say at first. Then she
sat upon the wooden box Tommy dragged forward for her
and gazed through the glass.

"Oh, Tommy! Did you ever see anybody so beautiful?"

"Nope."

"She looks like a princess."

"Better'n most of 'em, I bet."

"What do you suppose her name is?"

Tommy had not considered this. "Mebbe Sally."

"Sally!" Bessie was gently scandalized. "She could never be Sally.
She must have a long, beautiful name. Roselle—Roselle Geraldine."

Tommy regarded the doll gravely. "Yep. That's her name, sure
enough."

For half an hour they remained before the window. Bessie
admired the blue silk dress, the pearl buttons, the curls,
the bonnet, the eyelashes, and then began over again. Tommy
watched Bessie more than he watched the doll. Her cheeks
had grown pink; her eyes were radiant. He had never
seen her look so happy.

"How much is she?" he asked Mr. Mercer afterward.

"Five dollars."

Five dollars! Tommy possessed eleven cents, a fishhook, and a
brass button which he had once hoped might be gold.

"Couldn't you let her go cheaper?"

Mr. Mercer laughed. "Not a cent cheaper, Tommy. She came
from Boston."

That evening Tommy counted his eleven cents three times. He
considered possible methods of obtaining four dollars and eighty-nine cents.
He might pick berries, carry parcels, split kindling, and find
old bottles. He might even—here his thoughts grew splendidly reckless—save
every penny for a year.

He began the next morning.

During the following fortnight he earned thirty-seven cents. It was
discouragingly little, but to Tommy it was the substance of
things hoped for. Every day he and Bessie went to
look at Roselle Geraldine. Sometimes, when Bessie was not well
enough to go downstairs, Tommy stood before the shop window
alone and afterward described the doll.

"She looks jist the same," he would report. "Her dress
ain't faded a mite. An' she shut her eyes when
old Mercer tipped her back."

"I wish I could hold her once," Bessie sighed. "Only
once."

Tommy's heart contracted within him. "You will," he said rashly.
"Some day you'll hold her all you want."

Then, one hot afternoon, the doll was gone.

In her place stood a stack of straw hats and
a card announcing SUMMER REDUCTIONS.

Tommy rushed into the shop.

"Where is she?"

"Where's what?"

"You know—the doll!"

"Sold this morning."

"Who bought her?"

Mr. Mercer shook his head. "That's business, Tommy."

Tommy went slowly to Bessie's rooms. He had never before
found the stairs so steep. Bessie saw the news in
his face.

"She's gone?" she whispered.

"Yep."

"Some little girl has her?"

"I s'pose."

Bessie smiled bravely. "Then I hope she loves her very
much."

She did not cry. That made it worse.

Afterward she spoke seldom of Roselle Geraldine, but her mother
noticed that she ate less and lay awake at night.
The summer heat was severe, and Bessie, always frail, grew
thinner. Her cheeks lost their faint color; her blue eyes
looked too large for her face. The doctor said she
must have nourishing food, fresh air, and something to interest
her.

Fresh air and nourishing food cost money. Mrs. Lane did
what she could. Tommy brought wild raspberries and fanned Bessie
during the hottest hours. He told stories, performed tricks with
buttons, and imitated Deacon Bell's solemn cough until Bessie smiled.
Yet even her smile seemed tired.

Tommy blamed himself. He had shown her the doll. Before
that day she had been content with her books, her
patchwork, and the broken china cup. He had opened a
golden door before her and then let it close.

"If I'd only had five dollars," he thought bitterly.

Summer passed, and September brought cool nights. Miss Octavia's garden,
instead of losing its beauty, merely exchanged one kind for
another. The roses gave way to asters, dahlias, salvia, marigolds,
and great feathery chrysanthemums just beginning to show color. Miss
Octavia walked among them every morning with her scissors and
basket, cutting faded blossoms and tying up heavy stalks.

Tommy still looked through the fence, although he took care
not to be seen.

One evening the sky turned strangely clear after rain. The
wind blew from the north, sharp and searching. Tommy, who
had been delivering a bundle for Mrs. Lane, heard men
outside the post office discussing the weather.

"Frost tonight," said Mr. Mercer. "A killing one, likely."

Tommy's thoughts flew to Miss Octavia's garden.

He passed her house on the way home. The dahlias
stood tall and defenseless; the salvia burned red along the
path; the chrysanthemums held hundreds of tightly folded promises. One
white bud had opened that morning. By tomorrow they might
all be blackened.

Miss Octavia was not at home. She had gone that
afternoon to visit a sick cousin in the next town
and did not expect to return until morning. Tommy knew
this because the whole village knew everybody's affairs.

He went home, ate a piece of bread, and sat
by the smoky lamp. Outside, the wind moaned around the
corners. His father had fallen asleep in his chair.

"It ain't none o' my business," Tommy told himself.

He remembered Miss Octavia's sharp voice: "Not with such a
suspicious expression."

"Serve her right," he muttered.

But the dahlias were not to blame for Miss Octavia's
injustice. Neither were the chrysanthemums, with all their unopened gold
hidden from the frost.

Tommy rose.

At the printing office behind the weekly newspaper, old copies
were piled under a shed. Tommy had sometimes been chased
away from that shed. Tonight he waited until the printer
had gone, then gathered a great armful of papers. He
found several burlap sacks near the mill and a length
of string beside the freight depot. These materials were not
exactly his, but his conscience, never overburdened by fine distinctions
of ownership, assured him that flowers in danger justified much.

By nine o'clock he was inside Miss Octavia's garden.

The moon shone coldly. The grass already felt stiff beneath
his knees. Tommy spread newspapers over the low beds and
weighted them with stones. He wrapped the burlap around the
tallest dahlias, tied papers over the salvia, and constructed little
tents above the chrysanthemums. The wind tore away his first
coverings and sent them flapping down the street. Tommy pursued
them, returned, and began again.

His fingers grew numb. A stone crushed one thumb. Thorns
scratched his wrists, and a dahlia branch snapped despite all
his care.

"Drat you," he whispered, then apologized by propping it up
with a stick.

It was long past midnight when the work was done.
The garden looked as if an army of enormous paper
ghosts had encamped there. Tommy stood at the gate, shivering
and exhausted, but content. Beneath those ridiculous coverings slept crimson
and purple and gold.

He limped home and crept into bed without waking his
father.

Miss Octavia returned at eight the next morning. The frost
had indeed been a killing one. Brown vines hung over
neighboring fences, and every uncovered flower bowed a ruined head.

Then Miss Octavia saw her garden.

"For the land's sake!"

Newspapers covered the beds. Sacks surrounded the dahlias. Her clean
stones had been dragged from their borders and laid upon
fluttering pages. For one dreadful instant she thought vandals had
destroyed everything.

She hurried through the gate, snatched up a paper, and
found beneath it a row of perfect asters, fresh and
bright. The salvia was unharmed. The dahlias lifted their proud
heads. Every chrysanthemum bud was safe.

Near the last bed lay a ragged strip of sleeve,
caught upon a rose thorn.

Miss Octavia knew that cloth. She had seen an elbow
sticking through it only yesterday.

Tommy Puffer!

Her first feeling was astonishment. Her second was shame.

Miss Octavia was not a cruel woman. She was merely
a woman who had arranged the people of Bell's Corners
in neat moral rows, much as she arranged her plants,
and had never expected a weed to produce a flower.
Now the boy she distrusted had worked half the night
to save her garden, without witness and without hope of
payment.

She found Tommy behind the mill, warming his sore hands
over a small fire.

"Tommy Puffer," she said.

Tommy sprang up. He supposed the printer had missed his
newspapers, or Miss Octavia had found the broken dahlia.

"I never meant to bust that stalk," he began. "The
wind done it."

"You covered my flowers."

Tommy looked uncomfortable. "They'd have froze."

"Why did you do it?"

He scowled into the fire. "Dunno."

"Surely you did not do all that work for nothing."

"I like flowers," he muttered.

The simple confession moved Miss Octavia more than an eloquent
speech could have done. She saw his scratched wrists and
swollen thumb. She also saw, for the first time, that
his raggedness was not his choice, that defiance might be
armor, and that a neglected boy could possess a heart
more tender than many which beat beneath respectable waistcoats.

"Tommy," she said gently, "I have been unfair to you."

He stared at her. Nobody in Bell's Corners had ever
made such an admission to Tommy.

"I accused you of breaking my lily last year."

"Yep."

"Did you?"

"Nope. Deacon Bell's old rooster got in."

Miss Octavia believed him.

"I want to reward you. Would you like money?"

Tommy thought of food, shoes, and the five-dollar doll which
had already been sold. Then he remembered Bessie's pale face.

"No, ma'am."

"A knife? A new suit? Books?"

He shook his head.

"Well, for the land's sake, what do you want?"

Tommy shuffled one bare heel in the dust. "Bessie Lane's
sick. There was a doll at Mercer's—a blue silk one.
She called it Roselle Geraldine. It got sold, but mebbe
you could get another in the city. I'd like a
doll for Bessie."

Miss Octavia's hand flew to the silver brooch at her
throat.

"A doll in a blue silk dress? Brown curls? Pearl
buttons on the shoes?"

"You seen her?"

"I bought her."

Tommy's face went blank.

Miss Octavia had purchased the doll for a niece in
Boston. But before she could send it, the niece's mother
had written that the child already possessed too many dolls
and should receive books instead. Roselle Geraldine was even then
reposing in Miss Octavia's spare-room cupboard, wrapped in tissue paper.

"Come with me," she said.

Tommy followed her home, scarcely daring to breathe. Miss Octavia
went upstairs and returned carrying a long box. She lifted
the lid.

There lay Roselle Geraldine, more exquisite than ever, her blue
eyes closed as modestly as if she had been asleep
while so many hearts were troubled over her.

Tommy uttered a sound between a laugh and a sob.

"She's the one!"

"You may take her to Bessie."

"All for her? To keep?"

"All for her."

Tommy seized the box, then set it down and rubbed
one sleeve fiercely across his eyes.

"Aw, there's dust in here," he said.

Miss Octavia glanced around her spotless parlor but did not
contradict him.

They went together to the rooms over the harness shop.
Mrs. Lane met them at the door, looking anxious.

"Bessie had a poor night," she whispered.

Tommy entered first, carrying the box behind him. Bessie lay
in her chair by the window, white and listless. Yet
when she saw Tommy she smiled.

"Hello," she said faintly.

"Got somebody to see you."

He brought the box forward. Miss Octavia removed the lid,
and Tommy lifted out the doll.

Bessie stared.

Her lips parted, but no sound came. Then both thin
arms reached out.

"Roselle Geraldine!"

Tommy laid the doll against her breast. Bessie laughed and
cried at once, kissing the brown curls, touching the silk
dress, the pearls, the tiny slippers, and crying, "Oh, Tommy!
Oh, Tommy!" until Mrs. Lane had to wipe her own
eyes with the corner of her apron.

From that hour Bessie began to improve.

It would be foolish to say that a doll cured
her, for nourishing broth, cooler weather, her mother's care, and
the doctor's powders deserved some credit. But happiness is a
wonderful physician. Bessie ate because Roselle Geraldine must be fed;
she sat in the sunshine because the doll required an
airing; and color returned to her cheeks as she sewed
bonnets, capes, nightgowns, and an astonishingly small muff.

Miss Octavia visited often. She brought flowers, cream, and books,
though she pretended some of these were intended for the
doll. She also began to talk with Tommy when she
found him at Bessie's rooms, and the more she talked
with him, the more mistakes she discovered in her old
opinion.

One afternoon she invited him into the garden.

"Not jist to look through the fence?" he asked.

"No. To come in."

Tommy crossed the threshold as solemnly as another boy might
have entered a palace. Miss Octavia showed him how to
take chrysanthemum cuttings and divide iris roots. She found that
he learned at once. His fingers, awkward at a dining
table, were delicate among stems and buds. He understood flowers
as if they spoke to him in a language too
quiet for less attentive people.

Miss Octavia's brother owned a florist's establishment in the city
and had lately written that he needed an errand boy
who could be trained in the greenhouses.

"I know the very boy," she wrote back.

Thus, when winter came, Tommy went to work for Mr.
Ambrose Knox. He swept floors and delivered bouquets at first,
but he was also taught to prepare soil, pot bulbs,
prune roses, and coax violets into bloom. He received wages,
proper clothes, and—what was almost stranger—praise when praise was due.

He remained in Bell's Corners, traveling to the city each
morning, so he saw Bessie every day. In spring he
planted a bed beneath her window: pansies, sweet alyssum, pinks,
and one rosebush which Miss Octavia declared could not possibly
thrive there.

It thrived magnificently.

One evening Tommy found Bessie sitting by the open window
with Roselle Geraldine in her arms. The rosebush below was
covered with tender leaves, each holding a drop of recent
rain.

"She's still the prettiest doll in the world," Tommy said.

"Of course she is."

"You like her a lot, don't you?"

Bessie looked down at the blue silk dress, now slightly
rumpled from faithful loving. Then she looked at Tommy—at his
clean jacket, his sunburned face, and his gray eyes, which
had lost some of their old defiance but none of
their brightness.

"Yes," she said. "I love Roselle Geraldine very much."

Tommy nodded and pretended to examine the rosebush.

"But, Tommy," Bessie added, "I love you more."

He turned scarlet to the roots of his hair.

"Aw, shucks," he said.

Nevertheless, he walked home through the twilight as if all
Miss Octavia's flowers—crimson and purple and gold—had suddenly blossomed within
his heart.

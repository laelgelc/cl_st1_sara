In the autumn of 1887 Morrison and Daly bought the
stumpage on twelve thousand acres of pine lying north of
the Menominee branch, forty-three miles from a railroad and eleven
from the nearest tote-road. The tract was called remote even
by men who measured distance in frozen rivers and provisions
hauled behind bells. It held, according to the cruiser, somewhat
more than thirty million feet of merchantable white and Norway
pine, besides hemlock which nobody then considered worth handling.

They paid four dollars and seventy-five cents a thousand on
the estimate, secured an option on a second winter's cutting,
and agreed to clear the merchantable pine from three townships
without leaving tops high enough to waste a twelve-foot log.
The contract was a good one if the timber ran
as represented, if snow came early, if the ice held
late, if horses lived, if men worked, and if a
dozen other matters remained obedient which commonly do not.

Morrison attended to money. Daly knew lumber.

"We'll need a hard man on the job," said Daly.

They hired James Bourke.

In six counties Bourke was known as Rough Red. He
had got the name partly from his hair, which stood
thick and rust-coloured from his head, partly from the scarlet
sash he wore around his middle, and partly from a
disposition which made the adjective unnecessary. He could lay out
a camp, build a dam, freeze a road, and bring
logs to a rollway against weather and reason. He could
also drink for three days, break a man's jaw, throw
a hot stove outdoors for a delicate jest, and persuade
forty fellows that such acts were the natural recreations of
winter.

His crew came mostly from the river towns. They were
Irishmen by birth or immediate descent, broad-backed, loud-voiced, loyal to
Bourke, and suspicious of any authority not established with a
fist. They gambled, drank, fought, sang, blasphemed, and worked. When
sober they could put more pine on skids than any
crew in the district. When drunk they were a pack
with Rough Red for leader.

Morrison and Daly knew all this.

"That's why we'll send FitzPatrick," said Daly.

FitzPatrick was their scaler.

A scaler is the man who stands between a timber
owner and several comfortable methods of robbery. He measures the
diameter and length of every log, notes rot, sweep, shake,
and other defects, and records what the stick should yield
in boards. His figures determine how much timber has been
cut and, in many jobs, how much the contractor is
paid. The contractor naturally believes the scaler to be an
enemy. The owner believes him honest until the returns fall
short. The sawyer thinks him particular. The swampers think him
useless. No man welcomes him, yet every lumber operation requires
him.

FitzPatrick was tall, lean, and built as though a carpenter
had squared him from dry oak. His shoulders made a
level line. His jaw was horizontal, his nose nearly vertical,
and his grey eyes seemed placed with a rule. He
wore black wool, a knitted cap, German socks, and boots
greased against wet. He neither drank nor fought if fighting
could be avoided. He spoke little. He had no nickname.

"You understand Bourke?" Morrison asked.

"I know him."

"He won't like strict scaling."

"I'm not hired to be liked."

Daly pushed across a map. "We want clean tops, proper
lengths, no merchantable timber left in the slash. Cull what
isn't sound. Take nothing because Bourke says take it."

"I won't."

"And don't start trouble."

"No."

This appeared satisfactory, though each man understood the last answer
differently.

The camp went in during November. It stood on a
bench above Black Otter Creek: two long log buildings roofed
with split cedar, a cook-shanty, a stable for fourteen horses,
a blacksmith shed, and a low office containing Bourke's bunk,
FitzPatrick's bunk, the timekeeper's table, and a sheet-iron stove. Snow
found the place before the moss between the cabin logs
had dried.

The job spread over ridges divided by cedar swamps. Roads
were brushed and graded. Skidways were built at intervals, each
consisting of two peeled hardwood skids laid across the hauling
road. Trees were felled, trimmed, cut into logs, and drawn
on travoys to these skidways. There they accumulated until the
sleighing road had been iced sufficiently for hauling.

Suppose you log a knoll half a mile from the
main road. The biggest pines do not obligingly grow beside
the track. A swamp must be crossed, a side-hill flattened,
turns widened, bridges laid over spring holes. Horses must drag
each stick to the skidway, and later a team must
haul the load to the rollway on the river. Every
foot of needless length is weight. Every bad cut wastes
timber. Every rotten log transported consumes labour that might have
moved a sound one. This explains why Bourke wanted every
doubtful stick counted and why FitzPatrick did not.

The first quarrel concerned quarters.

"Ye'll bunk wid me," Bourke told him. "There's room in
th' office."

"I'll bunk with the men."

"The men don't want ye."

"I'm not particular."

Rough Red stared, his great hands hanging open. Behind him
the crew waited for amusement.

"Ye think ye're too good for me own bunk?"

"No."

"Thin what th' blank do ye mean?"

"The office is for the walking boss. I'll sleep in
camp."

FitzPatrick carried his blankets into the men's shanty and chose
an upper bunk near the door. The crew scowled. That
evening, when he entered after scaling, he said, "Good-night."

Nobody answered.

In the morning he laced his boots, rolled his blankets
out of the way, and said, "Good-mornin'."

Nobody answered that either.

He continued the practice.

At breakfast men passed dishes around him. On the trail
they did not speak unless compelled. At night cards were
slapped on the plank table; songs shook soot from the
roof; tales unsuitable for polite company improved with repetition. FitzPatrick
sharpened his blue crayon, entered figures in his scale book,
and said, "Good-night."

After four days the cook answered him.

The cook was a little, round man called Martin O'Hara.
His face always shone from steam, heat, or anxiety. He
ruled his cook-shanty fiercely but feared violence outside it. He
had fed crews for twenty winters and maintained that the
quickest way to a man's good nature was through flapjacks,
provided another man did not reach it first with whisky.

"Good-night to ye, Misther FitzPatrick," he said.

Rough Red heard. "Are ye courtin' him, Martin?"

"I'm courtin' peace, which is a lean female in this
camp."

The crew laughed, and O'Hara looked relieved.

The second quarrel concerned stumps. FitzPatrick found a cutting gang
leaving them thirty inches high on the uphill side.

"Lower," he said.

The sawyer spat. "They're low enough."

"Contract says eighteen inches where possible."

"Contract can cut 'em itself."

FitzPatrick marked three trees in his book and passed on.

That night Bourke came to him while supper was being
served.

"Ye docked Mulcahy's gang."

"Yes."

"For stumps!"

"For high stumps."

"Th' snow's deep on that side-hill."

"Kick it away."

Bourke leaned across the table. "I'm runnin' this job."

"Yes."

"Thin why in hell are ye tellin' me men how
to saw?"

"I'm telling them what Morrison and Daly will scale."

A silence opened around the table. Bourke's red sash seemed
to flame in the smoky light.

"Ye long black yardstick," he said softly, "some night I'll
break ye over me knee."

FitzPatrick accepted the potatoes from O'Hara. "Pass the pork."

Afterward the stumps were lower.

The third quarrel was over a Norway pine cut twenty
feet long. It measured thirty-one inches at the small end,
was straight, sound, and worth more than an ordinary month's
wages. The sawyer had bungled the butt cut. Instead of
sawing square through, he had undercut one side, split the
holding wood, and torn out a slanting slab nearly three
feet long. The log could have been shortened to sixteen
feet and made sound. As it lay, it would not
give an honest twenty-foot scale.

FitzPatrick struck a blue cross on the end.

"Culled," he said.

The sawyer stared. "That log's sound."

"Not at the butt."

"Take an inch off yer measure."

"Four feet."

"Four feet! There ain't four feet gone."

"There'll be four feet gone when it's trimmed square."

Rough Red arrived with a cant-hook over his shoulder. At
sight of the blue cross his face changed.

"What's this?"

"Cull."

Bourke examined the butt. He kicked snow from it, as
though the defect might be loose. "Scale her eighteen."

"There's no eighteen-foot length on the contract."

"Thin scale her sixteen."

"When she's sawed sixteen."

"Count her now."

"No."

The crew gathered. In the woods a disagreement draws men
as smoke draws eyes. Axes ceased. A horse shook its
harness, sending a brief silver chatter through the stillness.

Bourke laid down the cant-hook.

"FitzPatrick," he said, "that stick goes better'n twelve hundred."

"Nearer thirteen."

"An' ye'd throw her out?"

"Until she's cut right."

"Ye know we're paid by merchantable scale."

"Yes."

"Ye're stealin' thirteen hundred feet from me."

"No."

"Ye're callin' me a liar?"

"No."

"Thin count th' log."

"No."

Rough Red stepped close. He was wider than FitzPatrick by
the breadth of a hand on either side. His fists
swelled at the ends of his arms. FitzPatrick's hands remained
in his coat pockets.

Men expected the blow. It did not come. Bourke understood
that striking the scaler before sober witnesses would reach Morrison
and Daly by the next tote-team. More than that, FitzPatrick's
stillness bothered him. A man who threatens prefers some movement
upon which to fasten anger—an answering oath, a lifted guard,
a backward step. FitzPatrick supplied nothing.

"Move her aside!" Bourke roared.

They rolled the pine clear of the skidway.

Next morning it was back among the sound logs.

FitzPatrick came along with scale stick, book, and blue crayon.
He measured the first tier, called the figures to his
assistant, and reached the Norway.

He renewed the cross.

"Culled."

The following day the log appeared on another skidway a
quarter-mile east. Its blue cross had been chopped off in
a shallow blaze.

FitzPatrick recognised the torn butt.

He marked it again.

"Culled."

After that it travelled. It went to the lower road,
the upper road, Peterson's skidway, and the landing above the
cedar bend. Once they buried the bad end under two
smaller logs. Once they turned it entirely around. Once somebody
scrubbed the crayon with snow and covered the place with
mud.

Each time FitzPatrick found it.

Each time the blue cross appeared.

Each time he said, "Culled."

At first the performance entertained the crew. They made bets.
They invented names for the log—the Wanderer, Bourke's Orphan, the
Blue Ghost. But repeated failure soured the joke. The stick
became a public trial of Rough Red's authority. Every blue
cross accused him before his men.

December hardened. The roads froze under sprinklers. Water hauled in
barrels was spread after dark and became a glassy track
by morning. Loads increased from three thousand feet to five,
then six. Horses leaned low into their collars; runners sang;
teamsters walked beside towering sleds with reins around their shoulders.
At the rollway men used pike poles and cant-hooks to
deck the timber high above the frozen stream.

FitzPatrick worked alone from daylight to dusk. He scaled at
the skidways, inspected tops in the slash, and followed the
hauling to the rollway. Snow gathered on his shoulders. Resin
stiffened his mittens. His blue marks multiplied through the woods,
small assertions of rule in a country without sheriff, judge,
or neighbour.

He continued saying "Good-mornin'" and "Good-night."

O'Hara answered. Then the barn boss did. Later two Canadians
replied in mutters. The Irishmen held out under Bourke's eye.

Christmas approached with a tote-team carrying salt pork, flour, mail,
and two kegs labelled machine oil. No machine in camp
required oil. Bourke received the kegs personally and stored them
beneath his bunk.

On Christmas Eve the last hauling ceased at four. Supper
was turkey, potatoes, dried-apple pie, and plum duff black enough
to shoe a horse. Afterward the machine oil began circulating
in tin cups.

The camp changed character. Men sang, gambled, wrestled, drank, quarrelled,
embraced, fought, and drank again. Somebody put a bearskin on
the barn boss and hunted him with a broom. Somebody
else nailed O'Hara's apron to the door. At midnight a
committee rolled the office stove outside while it was burning,
but discovered that Bourke's bunk had caught fire from the
pipe and rolled it back.

FitzPatrick sat on his bunk entering the week's scale.

"Come now, Pat," cried a teamster, offering whisky. "It's Christmas."

"No, thanks."

"Wan drink won't drown ye."

"No."

"Leave th' corpse be," Bourke said. "He drinks blue chalk."

Toward morning FitzPatrick closed his book and slept, despite the
singing. By breakfast half the crew lay where it had
fallen. The rest continued with disciplined devotion.

Christmas Day was not ordinarily a working day, but timber
contracts respect weather more than saints. A thaw was predicted,
and Bourke wanted the skidways cleared. At ten o'clock he
sent out the soberest teamsters and several men who could
still distinguish a cant-hook from an axe.

The rejected Norway pine went with them.

They loaded it on a sleigh and hauled it to
the rollway. There they placed it in the merchantable deck,
bad butt inward, beneath three fresh sticks. The act required
considerable labour. For men celebrating a holiday, it showed purpose.

O'Hara saw them return.

"They've moved that divil's log," he told FitzPatrick in the
cook-shanty. "An' there's bad talk."

"Where?"

"Th' rollway."

FitzPatrick buttoned his coat.

"Don't go."

"I've scaling to finish."

"Finish it tomorrow."

"The load goes on today's account."

O'Hara seized his sleeve. "Listen to me. Rough Red has
th' liquor in him, an' th' boys are past knowin'
murder from music. They said if ye cull that log
again they'll kill ye."

FitzPatrick looked at the cook's hand until it released him.

"They said kill, not lick. I heard 'em. Bourke said
he'd leave ye where th' foxes could measure ye. Sure,
ye can't scale timber when th' camp's drunk."

"It was cut sober."

"Let it pass, man! It's only wan log."

"It's thirteen hundred feet."

"What's thirteen hundred feet against yer life?"

"Morrison and Daly bought it."

O'Hara's eyes filled with the helpless anger of a humane
man among brutes. "Morrison and Daly ain't here."

"That's why I am."

He took his scale stick and went out.

The afternoon had darkened. Fine snow sifted between the pines,
too dry to cling, whispering over the crust. Smoke from
the camp dropped low beneath the clouds. At the rollway
the woods were empty.

FitzPatrick stopped.

A chain lay beside the road. Cant-hook marks cut the
snow. The sleigh tracks ended at the deck. No voices
came from the men who should have been working there.

The woods were empty. FitzPatrick comprehended.

He climbed the rollway. The logs lay tier above tier,
their round ends pale against the weather. He inspected the
load, saw nothing, then noticed the torn edge hidden between
two butts. He worked down, brushed away snow, and found
the old blue stain.

He drew the crayon from his pocket.

"Culled," he said.

Men rose from behind the deck, from the brush, from
the road embankment. There were twelve or fifteen, some bareheaded,
some carrying handspikes, all drunk enough to borrow courage from
one another. Rough Red came last. His sash showed under
his open coat.

"Ye found her," he said.

"Yes."

"An' what did ye do?"

"Culled her."

Bourke climbed until his face was level with FitzPatrick's boots.
"Rub it out."

"No."

"Count that log."

"No."

The pack muttered. Oaths travelled through it, low and eager.

"Wan last chance," Bourke said. "Will ye scale her?"

"When she's sawed to sixteen."

Rough Red swung the cant-hook.

The iron hook caught FitzPatrick behind the knee and swept
his legs from under him. He fell down the rollway,
struck a projecting knot, and landed in the snow. Before
he could rise, the men were on him.

They kicked him, struck him with mittens hardened by ice,
beat him with short lengths of skid-chain. One held his
shoulders while another pounded his ribs. Bourke dragged him upright
to hit his face, then threw him down for the
rest. Snow filled FitzPatrick's mouth. A boot split his cheek
against a buried stone. He heard the blows before he
felt them, heavy and methodical, like axes in distant timber.

He did not cry out.

That enraged them further.

"Make him talk!"

"Ask him how he scales that!"

"Blue-mark th' blank-blanked thief!"

Someone found the crayon and drew a blue cross on
his coat. Someone else shouted that a culled man belonged
in the slash. Bourke planted a boot on FitzPatrick's chest
and raised the cant-hook by its handle.

Then an owl called from the swamp.

It was a common winter sound, deep and foolish. But
the men were drunk, the light was failing, and murder
had come near enough to show its face. The call
dropped into their fury like a voice from outside the
world they had made.

The pack hesitated.

A second call rolled among the trees.

"Come on," muttered one.

"What's ailin' ye?"

"Come on, I said."

They went suddenly, stumbling toward camp, talking too loudly. Bourke
lingered. He looked down at FitzPatrick and appeared to consider
finishing the work alone. Then the owl called again. Rough
Red spat, turned, and followed his men.

Snow sifted over the blue cross.

O'Hara waited half an hour. Then fear of what he
might find overcame fear of finding it. He put on
a mackinaw, took a lantern, and followed the sleigh road.

He discovered FitzPatrick beside the rollway, half covered.

"Holy Mother!"

FitzPatrick opened one eye.

"Can ye hear me?"

"Yes."

"Are ye dyin'?"

"Not yet."

O'Hara began to cry and curse while examining him. "They've
kilt ye. Th' black villains have kilt ye dead."

"But they didn't."

One arm was useless, three ribs were probably broken, the
left eye had closed, and blood froze from his cheek
to his collar. O'Hara got him onto his feet. FitzPatrick
stood briefly, folded, stood again, and leaned against the little
man.

The return took an hour.

Night settled completely. The lantern made a yellow pit around
them. Beyond it the forest remained black, solemn, and indifferent.
O'Hara talked all the way, partly to encourage FitzPatrick, partly
because silence frightened him.

"I'll get ye to th' cook-shanty. Not th' bunkhouse. They're
sleepin' off their shame, if shame's in 'em. I've hot
water. I've liniment. I've a bottle of brandy for medicine,
an' ye'll drink it if I pour it into yer
ear."

FitzPatrick said nothing.

Near camp O'Hara stopped.

"Look."

A pale thread of smoke lifted above the bunkhouse roof.

"Chimney," FitzPatrick said.

"No. Wind's wrong."

They watched.

Another thread rose through the shingles, then another. A red
light moved behind the small square window.

"The cabin's afire!"

O'Hara dropped the lantern and ran. FitzPatrick took one step
after him and sank to his knee. From there he
saw flame lick along the roof seam.

The bunkhouse held thirty-six men. Most were insensible with whisky.
The doors opened inward. During the festivities somebody had stacked
benches, barrels, and a broken table in the entry. The
windows were small, built to conserve heat, and crusted outside
with frost.

O'Hara raced back. "Come on! Help me wake 'em!"

FitzPatrick remained kneeling.

"They're in there," the cook cried.

"Yes."

"They'll burn!"

"Yes."

The roof reddened. Fire found the dry cedar splints and
ran with a quick, delicate brightness. Smoke pushed from the
eaves.

O'Hara stared at the battered face.

"Ye can't mean it."

FitzPatrick looked toward the cabin in which the pack slept—Bourke,
the sawyer, the teamsters, the men who had kicked his
ribs and marked his coat, the men who had left
him for snow to cover. His straight mouth bent, not
with pain but with bitterness.

"They made their choice," he said.

A window cracked from the heat. The sound was small
and final.

Then, through the wall, somebody coughed.

FitzPatrick got up.

He passed O'Hara, walking at first, then lurching into a
run. At the doorway he threw himself against the heap
of furniture. His injured arm hung useless, so he clawed
with the other, pulling away a bench and a barrel.
O'Hara joined him. Together they cleared a gap.

Smoke rolled out black and hot.

"Wake up!" O'Hara screamed. "Fire! Fire, ye drunken swine!"

No one answered.

FitzPatrick wrapped his coat across his mouth and went inside.

He found the nearest bunk by touch, seized an ankle,
and dragged a man to the floor. The fellow woke
fighting. FitzPatrick struck him once with the sound hand and
shoved him toward the door.

"Fire."

That word entered where reason could not.

Men began shouting. Bodies fell from bunks. Smoke thickened, turned
the room into a box of coughing and trampling. One
man ran to the wrong end and beat at the
wall. Another tried to rescue his boots. A third stood
in his underwear demanding the name of the coward who
had stolen the stove.

FitzPatrick moved among them. He pulled, struck, ordered. His voice,
seldom raised, cut through the confusion.

"Outside."

"Crawl."

"Leave him. I've got him."

"Outside."

The roof over the far bunks caught. Burning splinters fell
upon blankets. O'Hara and the awakened men formed a line
from the creek barrel, but the water froze in sheets
where it missed the fire. Others flung snow with shovels.
The barn boss led out the horses in case the
sparks reached the stable.

Rough Red remained in his bunk.

FitzPatrick found him on his back, overcome by smoke. For
a moment he stood looking down. Here lay the contractor
who had promised the foxes his body, helpless now, red
hair singed, great fists open.

The fire snapped overhead.

FitzPatrick bent, gripped Bourke's shirt with one hand, and hauled.
Pain whitened the darkness. He stopped, breathed once, and hauled
again. Rough Red was two hundred pounds of slack flesh
and wet wool. FitzPatrick dragged him from the bunk to
the floor, from the floor to the aisle, from the
aisle toward the door.

A roof pole fell behind them.

Men outside saw the scaler emerge backward through the smoke,
his torn face blackened, his coat still bearing their blue
cross. Bourke slid after him like a sack.

They took the contractor. FitzPatrick swayed.

"Any more?" asked O'Hara.

FitzPatrick counted faces. Even then he counted.

"Mulcahy."

They found Mulcahy beneath a bunk, carried him out, and
rolled him in the snow. After that the roof settled
inward with a long sigh. Sparks climbed among the pines.
Men shoveled until dawn and saved the cook-shanty, office, and
stable, though the bunkhouse became a rectangle of coals.

At daylight the crew stood in borrowed blankets around the
fire. Sobriety had returned without dignity. Several men bore burns.
All were alive.

Rough Red sat against a stump. He looked from the
ruins to FitzPatrick, who lay on a sleigh while O'Hara
bandaged his ribs.

"Ye came in after me," Bourke said.

FitzPatrick did not answer.

"After what we done."

Silence held the crew more thoroughly than any oath.

Bourke's voice lowered. "Why?"

FitzPatrick opened his uninjured eye. His face was swollen beyond
expression, but his words came measured and distinct.

"Morrison and Daly need this tract logged before the thaw,"
he said. "I couldn't let their crew burn."

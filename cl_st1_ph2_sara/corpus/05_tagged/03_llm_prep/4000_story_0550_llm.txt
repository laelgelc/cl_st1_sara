# Jane Gladys and the Picture Book

Jane Gladys was eight years old and lived in a
tall brick house with green shutters. Her papa had gone
down-town directly after breakfast, as he always did, and her
mamma had gone shopping for a new bonnet. The maid
was in the kitchen polishing the silver spoons and singing
so loudly that she could not have heard a lion
sneeze in the parlor.

Therefore Jane Gladys was alone.

She did not mind this. She sat upon the red
carpet with her dolls arranged before her and was teaching
them how to behave at a tea-party.

"No, Arabella," she said severely, "you must not put your
feet upon the table. If you do it again, you
shall go to bed 'meejitly."

Just then the doorbell rang.

The maid continued to sing in the kitchen, so Jane
Gladys went to the door herself. Upon the step stood
a short, fat man with a bald head, a shabby
coat and a black leather case under his arm. His
necktie was yellow, his shoes were dusty, and he smiled
as if smiling were disagreeable work.

"Is Mr. Gladys at home?" he asked.

"No," replied Jane Gladys. "Papa is down-town."

"Ah!" said the man. "That is fortunate—I mean, that is
unfortunate. And your mamma?"

"She is buying a bonnet."

"Even more fortunate! That is, of course, even more unfortunate."

He opened his leather case and took out a large
book bound in purple cloth. A silver star was stamped
upon the cover, and beneath the star were printed the
words:

**WONDERFUL CREATURES OF EVERY LAND**

"I have brought your papa a present," said the man.

"Why?"

The visitor rubbed his bald head.

"Because yesterday I called at his office to sell him
twelve books concerning the habits of successful men. Your papa
said he was busy and ordered me to leave."

"Papa is very busy," explained Jane Gladys.

"He also told a clerk to put me out."

"Then perhaps you would not go."

"I had not finished my speech," said the man indignantly.
"No gentleman should throw out a book agent before the
end of his speech. So I have come to reward
him."

Jane Gladys looked doubtfully at the purple book.

"It does not sound like a reward."

"You are a thoughtful child," said the man. "What is
your name?"

"Jane Gladys."

Taking a pencil from his vest pocket, he wrote upon
the first page:

**For Jane Gladys, with the respectful compliments of a friend
of her papa.**

"There!" he said. "Now the book belongs to you. Open
it whenever you grow tired of dolls. You will find
the pictures unusually lifelike."

"Thank you," said Jane Gladys, for she had been taught
to be polite even to unpleasant persons.

The short man bowed until his bald head nearly struck
the door.

"Give my kindest regards to your papa," he said, "and
tell him I hope the book will amuse the entire
household."

Then he hurried down the steps, chuckling in a manner
that was not at all respectful.

Jane Gladys carried the book into the parlor. It was
heavy, and something inside it seemed to rustle, although the
windows were closed.

She placed it on the carpet.

"I shall look at only one picture," she told her
dolls. "Then we will continue the tea-party."

The book opened easily. On the first page was a
coloured picture of a clown. He wore a red coat
spotted with blue moons, yellow trousers, green slippers and a
tall white cap. His cheeks were round, his mouth was
wide, and he had been painted entirely from the front.

"That is a very bright clown," said Jane Gladys.

The clown winked.

Jane Gladys leaned nearer. The clown placed both hands against
the edge of the picture, pulled himself out of the
page and tumbled headfirst upon the carpet.

"Dear me!" he cried, sitting up. "What a narrow house!"

"It is not a house," said Jane Gladys. "It is
a book."

"Then I dislike books. Kindly assist me."

She helped him stand. When he turned around, Jane Gladys
gave a little gasp. The clown had a face, chest,
arms and legs, but he had no back at all.
Behind him there was only a flat white surface.

"You are not finished," she said.

"I am perfectly aware of it!" snapped the clown. "The
stupid artist drew my front and neglected the remainder. How
am I to sit comfortably? How am I to lean
against a wall? I cannot even turn my back upon
an enemy."

"You might walk backward."

"And spend my life looking where I have been instead
of where I am going? What a ridiculous suggestion!"

The clown examined the parlor and then bowed to the
dolls.

"Ladies," he said, "I apologize for my incomplete appearance."

Jane Gladys turned another page.

This picture showed a brown monkey sitting in a tree.
He wore a little red waistcoat and held a coconut
in one hand. The instant the page lay flat, the
monkey sprang into the room, landed upon the table and
knocked over the dolls' teapot.

"Mind the china!" cried Jane Gladys.

"Mind the artist!" answered the monkey. "He has given me
two left hands."

He held them up, and this was true. Both thumbs
pointed the same way.

"That must be inconvenient," said Jane Gladys.

"Inconvenient? It is a catastrophe! I cannot button my waistcoat.
I cannot peel my coconut properly. I cannot shake hands
without first explaining which hand I mean."

"You might ask the clown to help you."

"He has no back," said the monkey.

"That is no reason I cannot button waistcoats," declared the
clown. "Come here."

They began pulling at the buttons, each giving directions to
the other and neither accomplishing anything. While they quarrelled, Jane
Gladys turned the next page.

A gray donkey stood in a meadow. His head was
too large, one ear was short, the other ear was
long, and his four legs pointed in four different directions.

"Poor thing," said Jane Gladys.

At once the donkey slid out of the picture and
fell sideways upon the rug.

"I object to being pitied," he announced.

Jane Gladys and the clown tried to set him upright,
but whenever two of his legs touched the floor, the
others waved helplessly in the air.

"The artist did not understand perspective," complained the donkey. "He
drew my front legs as if they were far away
and my hind legs as if they belonged to another
donkey."

"Can you walk?" asked Jane Gladys.

"Certainly. I can walk north, south, east and west at
the same time."

"That must save trouble."

"It causes trouble!" cried the donkey. "Whenever I intend to
go forward, three-quarters of me prefer another direction."

He attempted a step. His front half moved toward the
piano, his hind half moved toward the door, and the
rest of him sat down with a thump.

The monkey laughed so hard that he dropped his coconut
upon the clown's toe.

"Ow!" shouted the clown.

"Pray excuse me," said the monkey. "With two left hands
one cannot be expected to drop things correctly."

Jane Gladys was beginning to think the book agent's gift
more troublesome than amusing. She decided to shut the volume,
but as she reached for the cover, the pages fluttered
by themselves and stopped at a picture of a spotted
leopard.

The leopard's eyes were yellow. His body crouched close to
the ground, his tail curled behind him, and his mouth
was open in a terrible snarl.

"Oh!" said Jane Gladys.

The leopard leaped from the book.

The clown jumped behind the sofa, although he could not
properly turn his back while doing so. The monkey scrambled
up the curtains. The donkey tried to run in four
directions and remained exactly where he was.

The leopard landed before Jane Gladys and roared.

It was not a very successful roar. It sounded like
wind passing through an empty cupboard.

"Are you going to bite me?" Jane Gladys asked.

"I should be delighted," said the leopard gloomily, "but the
artist forgot my teeth."

He opened his mouth wider. There were no teeth inside.

"Then you cannot hurt me."

"I have claws," said the leopard.

He lifted one paw and looked at it. Then he
lifted another.

"No, I have not," he added bitterly. "The careless fellow
painted round yellow toes. How can a leopard maintain his
reputation with round yellow toes?"

"You still look fierce," said Jane Gladys, wishing to comfort
him.

"Looking fierce is useful only while one remains in a
picture. Out here, people ask one to prove it."

The monkey came down from the curtains.

"You might frighten mice," he suggested.

"I am a leopard, not a house cat!"

"You are badly drawn, whatever you are," said the clown.

This remark began a noisy argument. The leopard insisted that
spots were more artistic than blue moons. The clown replied
that a proper coat was superior to any quantity of
fur. The monkey declared that neither of them possessed useful
hands. The donkey, still lying on his side, said that
hands were an unnecessary luxury.

Jane Gladys clapped her own hands.

"Be quiet!" she commanded. "Mamma will return soon, and she
will not like finding wild animals in the parlor."

The creatures became silent.

"You must all go back into the book," she continued.

"Never!" cried the clown. "It is flat in there."

"There are no buttons on the trees," said the monkey.

"The meadow slopes the wrong way," groaned the donkey.

"And everyone expects me to be savage," said the leopard.

"But this is my house," said Jane Gladys.

Before anyone could answer, the pages began to flutter again.

They turned slowly at first, then faster and faster, until
they stopped upon a dark picture of a forest. Between
two black pine trees stood a great grizzly bear.

He was twice as tall as Jane Gladys and four
times as broad. His fur was thick, his claws were
long, and, unlike the leopard, he had a splendid set
of white teeth.

The clown hid behind the curtain. The monkey hid behind
the clown. The leopard crawled under the table, and the
donkey shut his eyes.

The bear stepped out of the book.

The carpet shook beneath him.

"Good afternoon," he said in a deep voice.

"Good afternoon," replied Jane Gladys, because politeness is useful even
when speaking to bears.

The bear sniffed the air.

"I smell a little girl."

"That is probably me."

"Are you Jane Gladys?"

"Yes."

"Then everything is in order."

"What order?"

The bear pointed one long claw at the printed words
beneath his picture.

Jane Gladys read them aloud:

"'The grizzly bear is the most dreadful of all the
creatures in this volume. Upon leaving the forest, he will
at once eat any little girl he finds.'"

"There," said the bear. "You see my duty."

"I do not think it is your duty," answered Jane
Gladys.

"It is printed in black ink."

"Printing a wicked thing does not make it right."

"That may be so outside books," replied the bear, "but
I am a book bear. I must follow what the
author wrote."

He moved one step nearer.

Perhaps you can imagine how Jane Gladys felt. The clown
had no back, the monkey had two left hands, the
donkey could not stand, and the leopard possessed neither teeth
nor claws. Not one of them could protect her.

Still, Jane Gladys did not scream. Screaming might have pleased
the bear, and it certainly would not have improved matters.

Instead she looked at the book.

"Wait," she said.

The bear stopped.

"Why?"

"I wish to read something."

"Be quick. I have been waiting in that forest for
years."

Jane Gladys turned to the first page and pointed to
the words written by the book agent.

"What does this say?"

The clown peeped from behind the curtain.

"'For Jane Gladys,'" he read, "'with the respectful compliments of
a friend of her papa.'"

"So the book was given to me," said Jane Gladys.

"Plainly," agreed the monkey.

"And if the book was given to me, it belongs
to me."

"I see no error in that," said the leopard.

"Everything in the book belongs to me also—the pictures, the
trees, the coconut, the meadow and the creatures."

The bear frowned.

"That is a troublesome argument."

"It is a correct argument," said Jane Gladys. "You came
out of my book. Therefore you are my bear."

The clown stepped boldly into view.

"She owns every coloured inch of you," he declared. "I
am a person of no back, but I understand property."

"You cannot eat your owner," continued Jane Gladys. "An owner
may feed her bear, brush her bear or order her
bear back into his picture. But if a bear eats
his owner, then he has no owner, and a creature
belonging to a book cannot belong to nobody."

The bear scratched his head.

"The author said I must eat little girls."

"He did not say you must eat your owner."

"No," admitted the bear.

"And my name is written before your picture. That rule
came first."

The monkey nodded eagerly.

"Words printed at the front of a book are always
more important. Otherwise they would be printed at the back."

"That is not always true," said the clown, "but it
sounds convincing."

The bear sat down heavily.

"I dislike clever little girls," he grumbled. "Authors ought not
put them into dangerous situations. They spoil everything."

"I am glad to spoil this," said Jane Gladys.

At that moment the front doorbell rang.

"Mamma!" cried Jane Gladys.

At once the parlor was filled with confusion.

"The book!" shouted the clown.

He dived toward it and slipped between the pages like
a coloured card. The monkey followed, carrying his coconut in
both left hands. The donkey rolled across the carpet, struck
the book with his nose and vanished into his crooked
meadow. The leopard sprang after him, pausing only to give
one last toothless snarl.

The bear rose.

"May I eat the maid?" he asked hopefully.

"No."

"The book agent?"

"No."

"Anyone at all?"

"No. Go back to your forest 'meejitly."

The bear sighed, climbed onto the open page and became
flat and still between the pine trees. Jane Gladys slammed
the cover just as the maid hurried from the kitchen
to answer the door.

Mamma entered carrying three parcels and wearing her old bonnet.

"Did you have a pleasant afternoon, dear?" she asked.

"Very interesting," said Jane Gladys.

"And what is that large purple book?"

"A present from a friend of papa."

That evening Papa examined the volume. He looked at the
inscription, frowned, and locked the book in the top drawer
of his desk.

"The man who brought this is no friend of mine,"
he said.

"I thought perhaps he was not," replied Jane Gladys.

She never opened the book again, although she sometimes heard
muffled complaints from inside the drawer. The clown wanted a
back, the monkey wanted a proper right hand, the donkey
wanted four sensible legs, and the leopard wanted teeth. The
bear merely growled.

Thus Jane Gladys learned—and it is well for all of
us to remember—that when danger steps out of a book
or enters by an ordinary door, the best thing to
do is to think quickly and clearly. A small person
with a sound argument may sometimes defeat even a very
large bear.

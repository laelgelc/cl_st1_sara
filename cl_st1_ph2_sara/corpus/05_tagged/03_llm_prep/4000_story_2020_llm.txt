### The Reckoning at Aswarby Hall

Among the papers of the late Dr Henry Elliott, formerly
rector of Wringham in Lincolnshire, there is preserved a packet
endorsed, in a hand much faded by damp, *Concerning my
brother's son Stephen, and the events at Aswarby, March 1823*.
Dr Elliott himself had no part in those events, being
then resident in Malta; and the narrative which he afterwards
compiled rests principally upon the recollections of his nephew, supplemented
by the depositions of two servants, the coroner's minutes, and
certain writings discovered in the library of Aswarby Hall. I
have examined as many of these documents as remain accessible
and have visited the house, though its internal arrangements were
considerably altered in 1871. What follows is, as far as
I can ascertain, an exact account.

Stephen Elliott was twelve years old when the fever of
1822 deprived him, within nine days, of both his parents.
His father, a captain in the 17 th Regiment, had
left little beyond debts and a modest annuity payable to
the boy until his twenty-first year. The nearest relation willing
to assume responsibility for him was Mr Humphrey Abney, a
cousin of Stephen's mother, and the owner of Aswarby Hall.

Mr Abney's offer occasioned some surprise. He had the reputation
of being not merely solitary but inhospitable, and during twenty
years had admitted few guests to his house. Yet his
letter to the family solicitor was decorous, even benevolent. The
child, he wrote, would enjoy country air, sound instruction and
the advantages of a library "not wholly contemptible in those
branches of humane learning which are neglected by our present
seminaries." No objection of substance could be found. Accordingly, on
the 3 rd of September, Stephen was sent northward under
the care of a servant.

Aswarby lies upon the western edge of the Lincolnshire fen
country, where the ground begins almost imperceptibly to rise, and
where, from certain upper windows, a traveller may see the
long horizontal distances of marsh and reclaimed field interrupted only
by church towers, pollarded willows and the white courses of
winter drains. The Hall, built about 1595 and enlarged under
Queen Anne, stood then within a shallow park. Its eastern
front, of honey-coloured stone, looked across a gravel court toward
iron gates; the older western range, irregular and patched with
brick, overlooked a declining lawn, a fishpond and a small
wood of elm and ash.

There was nothing ruinous in its appearance. The roofs were
sound, the windows glazed, and the principal rooms furnished with
a comfort which Mr Abney's neighbours, judging only from his
dress and conversation, would hardly have expected. Nevertheless, the northern
end contained apartments long disused: a schoolroom with a cracked
plaster ceiling, two bedchambers, and a bathing-room fitted, perhaps in
1740, with a sunken lead-lined bath. This part of the
house will require attention later.

Stephen arrived shortly before sunset. He remembered the evening chiefly
for its stillness and for the smoke rising vertically from
cottages beyond the park wall. At the door he was
received by Mrs Bunch, the housekeeper, a broad, cheerful widow
whose cap-ribbons and silver spectacles made an immediate and favourable
impression upon him.

"Well, my lamb," she said, taking both his hands, "you're
colder than a church latch. Come in wi' you. There's
fire and broth, and neither of 'em asks questions."

Mr Abney did.

He entered the hall while Stephen's coat was being removed:
the tall, the thin, the austere—a figure in rusty black,
with a narrow stoop, long colourless hands and eyes which
seemed at first remarkably mild. His age was fifty-five. Although
his published writings had earned him some distinction among students
of late classical religion, his person was known to few;
and the boy, who had imagined an imposing magician surrounded
by globes and skulls, was disappointed to see a nervous
gentleman with ink upon his cuff.

"So this is Stephen. Yes—yes, of course. You have had
a tolerable journey? No mishap? Excellent. And you are twelve
years of age, I think?"

"Twelve last April, sir."

"Last April? The date, if you please."

"The twenty-first."

"The twenty-first," repeated Mr Abney. "Quite so. St George's Eve
approaching—though not, strictly, the feast itself. Your constitution is good?
No faintness, palpitations, or weakness of the chest?"

Mrs Bunch laughed.

"Lord love us, master, let the lad get his supper
afore you make a physician's book of him."

Mr Abney appeared startled, then laughed also, but rather too
loudly. He patted Stephen's head, said that such particulars were
necessary in the proper discharge of guardianship, and retired to
his study.

Life at Aswarby soon assumed an agreeable regularity. Stephen breakfasted
at eight with Mrs Bunch, read Latin and history with
Mr Abney from ten until noon, and spent the afternoon
as he pleased, provided he did not enter the fen
drains or disturb the books arranged upon his guardian's private
shelves. Mr Abney dined alone and rarely appeared after dusk.

The boy found him neither cruel nor consciously unkind. His
instruction was dry but exact, and he sometimes displayed an
awkward desire to please. He gave Stephen a fishing-rod, ordered
a pony for him, and allowed him to examine Roman
coins which no visitor had previously handled. Yet his solicitude
retained a peculiar character. He asked repeatedly whether Stephen slept
well, whether his appetite had increased, whether he felt any
discomfort beneath the left ribs, and whether the date of
his birth had been copied correctly from the parish register.

His scholarship was of a kind then more respected than
it would be now. He had written upon the mysteries
of the Phrygian Attis, the funeral observances of the Samothracians,
and the alleged survival of Chaldean rites in the villages
of southern Italy. His principal work, *De Alimentis Spiritualibus apud
Veteres*, had been privately printed at Leyden in an edition
of fifty copies. It argued, with abundant citation and little
humour, that certain ancient peoples believed the soul to be
concentrated in a material organ and transferable, under fixed celestial
conditions, from one body to another.

I have seen the book. The learning is considerable, though
its conclusions are not.

For the first month Stephen was happy. He explored the
park, climbed the dovecote stairs, fished unsuccessfully in the pond,
and persuaded Mr Parkes, the elderly butler, to show him
the mechanism of the great clock above the stable arch.
It was in conversation with Mrs Bunch, while she mended
a tear in his coat, that he first heard of
the children who had preceded him at Aswarby.

"Was I ever here before?" he asked. "I seem to
remember a little girl by the ash trees."

"You couldn't, Master Stephen. Never set eyes on the place
till Michaelmas."

"She had brown skin and black hair. I thought she
beckoned to me."

Mrs Bunch's needle paused.

"Some village child, belike."

"She had no shoes."

At this Mrs Bunch made an exclamation and looked toward
the door.

"There was one," she said after a moment, "three year
gone. A poor little roving thing—gipsy-born, they said. Master found
her near Gainsborough in hard weather and brought her home.
He was uncommon took up wi' her."

"What was her name?"

"Phoebe Stanley, or so she called herself. Thin as a
lath, but quick and merry when first she came. Then
she got poorly."

"What became of her?"

"Ran away."

"In winter?"

"So we were told."

Mrs Bunch bit off her thread with unnecessary vigour. Presently,
and with more prompting than she perhaps intended to require,
she spoke of another child: an Italian boy named Giovanni
Paoli, who had been found begging outside a church in
London and brought to Aswarby by Mr Abney in the
summer of 1821. He, too, had disappeared.

"Master said the foreign sailors fetched him," Mrs Bunch concluded.
"But no sailor came by front door nor back, and
Parkes keeps his eyes open even when his mouth's shut."

From the adjoining pantry came the butler's voice.

"Ay, and my ears too, mum."

"Hush, you old rook."

"I heard summat that night," Parkes continued, entering with a
tray. "Down below, where the old wine vault runs under
master's study. Scratching first, then a lad a-crying in words
I didn't know. There was a door slammed, though every
door were bolted."

Mrs Bunch crossed herself, perhaps from habit rather than conviction.

"You heard rats."

"Rats don't say *mamma*."

When Stephen repeated this conversation to Mr Abney, his guardian
was not angry. On the contrary, he seemed amused, though
the spoon in his hand struck twice against the side
of his teacup.

"My servants possess vigorous imaginations. The girl returned, no doubt,
to her tribe; the boy to his countrymen. Charity, Stephen,
is frequently rewarded by ingratitude. You will learn that in
time. Still—still, you are not troubled by dreams? No noises
at your door?"

Stephen said that he was not.

"Good. Excellent. If you should be, you will tell me
at once."

Autumn passed into a wet and comparatively mild winter. The
Hall contracted about its occupants; shutters were closed soon after
four, fires burned continuously, and beyond the windows the park
became a blurred composition of rain, bare branches and standing
water. Stephen read more widely than Mr Abney approved. In
a cabinet of the lesser library he discovered a seventeenth-century
history of the estate, which mentioned that the northern apartments
had once served as lodgings for a recusant chaplain. The
bathing-room, according to a pencilled note, had formerly been an
oratory.

On the night of the 19 th of December he
dreamed of that room.

He seemed to stand in the corridor outside it, carrying
a candle which gave no warmth. The door, swollen in
its frame and ordinarily locked, stood open. Within, the leaden
bath was full—not of water, but of grey dust, hair
and fragments of stained linen. A small hand projected over
its edge. As Stephen watched, the hand bent at the
wrist and its long nails scraped slowly upon the metal.

He awoke with the sound still in his ears.

His room was perfectly dark. A branch, moved by the
wind, touched the outer glass at intervals, but beneath that
familiar tapping was another noise, faint and deliberate, proceeding from
the lower panel of his door.

Scrape.

A pause followed.

Scrape—scrape.

He called out. The noise ceased. Almost immediately Mrs Bunch
appeared with a candle and found him sitting upright, pale
but composed. Nothing was in the passage. The door, however,
bore three fresh marks in the varnish, each about four
inches long and each so narrow that, in Mrs Bunch's
opinion, no ordinary knife could have made it.

The following morning similar marks were discovered upon the left
side of Stephen's nightshirt, extending from the shoulder toward the
breast. The cloth was not torn through. Mr Abney examined
it minutely and asked whether the scratches had drawn blood.

"No, sir."

"Not even a puncture?"

"No."

His guardian's relief was unmistakable.

"The cat," he said. "A household animal, however harmless, is
a source of perpetual mischief."

"There isn't no cat in this house," said Mrs Bunch.

Mr Abney folded the garment and placed it in his
pocket.

"I was speaking generally."

That afternoon the bathing-room was opened. Mr Abney himself produced
the key and accompanied Stephen, Mrs Bunch and Parkes along
the northern corridor. The room contained a cracked marble basin,
a chair without a seat, some rolls of discarded wallpaper
and the old bath, covered by warped boards. When these
were lifted, nothing appeared beneath them except dust and a
dead beetle.

"An instructive illustration," Mr Abney observed, "of the value of
dreams as evidence."

Yet Stephen noticed, in the corner where the wall met
the floor, a strip of brown cloth. He stooped to
pick it up, but Mr Abney's shoe came down upon
it.

"Rotten felt," he said. "Do not soil your fingers."

The boy made no objection. He had nevertheless seen that
the cloth was embroidered with three small red beads, of
the kind worn upon the bodices of travelling women.

After Christmas the disturbances ceased for several weeks. Stephen's health
remained good, and his natural liveliness returned. He skated on
the pond, learned enough Italian from a grammar to translate
a nursery prayer written on the flyleaf, and celebrated his
thirteenth birthday in anticipation because Mr Abney declared that he
might be absent in April. The latter circumstance deserves notice:
although the birthday was not yet due, the guardian insisted
upon measuring Stephen's height, weight and pulse on the 21
st of February, exactly two months before the actual anniversary.

The spring equinox fell that year on the 21 st
of March. In the days preceding it, Mr Abney became
increasingly abstracted. He ate little, received several parcels from London—a
bronze knife, chemical vessels and a quantity of aromatic resin—and
spent long hours in his study. At times a sweet,
heavy odour, resembling burnt juniper, penetrated the west passage.

On the 18 th, Parkes reported hearing voices below stairs.

"Two of 'em," he told Mrs Bunch in Stephen's presence.
"A little lass and a lad. Not speaking proper—more like
when birds gets trapped in a chimney. I took lantern
down, and the cellar door were locked on t'other side."

"Then how'd you open it?"

"I didn't. By time master came, it stood wide."

Mr Abney, questioned upon the matter, suggested that sound had
travelled along an unused heating flue. This explanation, though architecturally
possible, did not account for the wet footprints found upon
the cellar floor. One set was narrow and bare; the
other showed the impression of a small broken shoe. Both
began beneath the blank wall at the northern end and
finished before the locked door of the study stair.

On the morning of the 21 st, Stephen found an
envelope beneath his breakfast plate. It contained a note in
Mr Abney's hand:

> My dear Stephen,
> I shall require your assistance tonight in an experiment
of some delicacy but no danger. Come to my study
at eleven precisely. Say nothing to the servants, whose superstitions
might occasion needless inconvenience. I have something of consequence to
communicate respecting your future.
> H. A.

The day was bright, cold and windy. Clouds passed swiftly
over the house, producing abrupt changes of light in the
long rooms, and from the wood came the repeated cracking
of branches. Stephen felt no particular alarm. He supposed that
Mr Abney intended to display some chemical experiment, and the
command to secrecy, far from troubling him, conferred upon the
invitation the dignity of an adventure.

Mrs Bunch was less satisfied. She had not seen the
note, but observed her master at dinner examining the large
Dutch clock in the hall.

"There's weather turning," she said to Stephen. "You bide in
your room after prayers."

"Why?"

"Because I say it, and because this be one o'
them nights when doors opens easier than they shuts."

Parkes, polishing a candlestick nearby, grunted agreement.

"Sun crosses line," he said. "Old folk never liked it."

"You two would make a ghost out of a dishcloth,"
Stephen replied.

"A dishcloth don't leave nail marks."

At half past ten the household retired. Stephen waited in
his bedroom with a candle and a book, hearing the
wind move around the angles of the roof. At ten
minutes before eleven, he put on his dressing-gown and opened
the door.

The corridor was empty, but a pale radiance, distinct from
candlelight and too low to be moonlight, lay upon the
floor. It seemed to issue from the staircase window at
the far end. Stephen walked toward it.

Outside, the clouds had parted. The lawn, gravel path and
first ranks of trees were visible under a colourless moon.
Immediately below the window stood a child.

She was perhaps ten years old and very thin. Her
black hair moved across her face though the wind did
not stir her ragged dress. One transparent hand rested upon
the glass from the outer side. With the other she
pointed—not toward Stephen, but toward a place beneath her left
breast.

There was a dark opening in the dress. Around it
the cloth clung stiffly, and within it nothing could be
seen.

Stephen could neither cry out nor move away. The girl
lifted her face. It was not angry. Its expression was
one of exhausted entreaty, and this, he afterwards said, was
more dreadful than rage would have been. Her lips formed
a word which did not reach him.

Then a second figure emerged from behind the stone buttress:
a boy with an olive complexion, hollow cheeks and one
broken shoe. He held both hands against his chest. As
he drew them apart, Stephen saw the same black wound.

The two children turned their heads simultaneously toward the western
wing.

From somewhere below came the sound of a key being
turned.

Stephen's candle went out. When he looked again, the lawn
was empty; but upon the outer surface of the window
remained the prints of five slender fingers, and from the
study beneath him came a muffled cry.

It was not loud. It began as though Mr Abney
had intended to call a name and found his breath
suddenly obstructed; it ended in a bubbling sigh, followed by
the overthrow of some heavy object.

Stephen ran downstairs.

The study door stood closed. He knocked, received no answer
and tried the handle. The door opened about six inches
and struck against something within. By forcing his shoulder into
the gap, he displaced an overturned chair and entered.

Mr Abney sat—or had been placed—in the high-backed chair before
his writing table. His head had fallen sideways, and his
spectacles hung from one ear. His expression was less one
of pain than of profound astonishment. The front of his
shirt and waistcoat had been torn open. Over the left
side of the chest were wounds which the surgeon afterwards
described as "lacerations caused by several narrow instruments or by
an animal furnished with claws of unusual length."

The heart had been removed.

Upon the table stood a brass lamp, a covered silver
dish and the bronze knife lately arrived from London. The
knife was unused. A circle had been drawn in red
chalk upon the floor; at its centre was a low
stool, and beside it lay a strip of clean linen
cut to a child's size. The window was fastened from
within. No person was found in the room or in
the adjoining closet.

Mrs Bunch and Parkes, summoned by Stephen's cries, removed him
before he could observe more. The village surgeon arrived within
an hour, followed by the rector and, toward morning, a
magistrate. Since the study chimney was too narrow for passage,
the door had been under observation, and every external window
showed an undisturbed film of dust, the inquiry encountered difficulties
from the first.

The official explanation was cautious. Mr Abney, it was suggested,
had suffered a seizure while handling some mechanical apparatus, after
which an unidentified animal—possibly a large dog—had entered and mutilated
the body. No dog was discovered, no apparatus could be
made to produce the injuries, and the missing organ was
never found. The coroner's jury returned a verdict of death
by misadventure "under circumstances not susceptible of complete determination."

Public order was thereby preserved.

The private explanation lay in Mr Abney's writing desk. Stephen,
who remained at Aswarby until his uncle's agents arrived, found
a concealed spring behind a drawer and obtained three notebooks
bound in grey vellum. He did not read them immediately.
Some years later, having reached adulthood and acquired sufficient Latin,
he made a transcript, from which Dr Elliott copied the
relevant passages.

The first notebook contained extracts from Porphyry, Iamblichus, Arnobius and
two authorities unknown to me, one cited as *Melchior of
Pressburg*, the other as *The Syrian Fragment*. Their common subject
was the alleged multiplication of life through the absorption of
what Mr Abney called "the essential seat of an immature
spirit."

A memorandum dated 6 January 1818 reads:

> The vulgar error consists in supposing that blood is
the vehicle. The heart, taken before corruption and consumed under
the appropriate configuration, is indispensable. The subject must be young,
sound, unattached by fixed habitation, and received voluntarily beneath the
operator's roof. Three are required, corresponding to lunar, solar and
equinoctial conditions.

The second notebook recorded the arrival of Phoebe Stanley on
14 November 1819. Mr Abney noted her age, weight, dentition,
habits of sleep and "satisfactory freedom from familial claim." On
20 March 1820 appears the entry:

> First operation completed at 11.35 p.m. Some resistance, unexpectedly
vigorous. The material was secured, though the exterior envelope required
concealment in the northern bath. Subsequent sounds in that quarter
must be attributed to settling timbers, imagination, or an imperfect
discharge of psychic residue.

The phrase "exterior envelope" meant the child's body.

Giovanni Paoli was entered as the second subject. Mr Abney
had enticed him from London with promises of education and
return to Italy. The operation took place on 23 September
1821 in the wine cellar. The notebook complained that the
boy had cried out repeatedly for his mother and that
Parkes had nearly interrupted the proceedings.

The final book concerned Stephen.

His orphaning, far from producing an unexpected act of charity,
had completed a design formed years before. His age, birthday
and health were repeatedly tabulated. The spring equinox of 1823
was selected as the proper hour; the invited residence at
Aswarby fulfilled, in Mr Abney's view, the condition of voluntary
shelter; and the earlier birthday celebration had allowed him to
determine that no defect of constitution would impair the result.

The last entry was unfinished:

> At eleven the third subject will be introduced into
the western circle. Incision to be made beneath the left
papilla, the heart removed while vitality persists, and the prepared
portion immediately—

There the writing broke off. Beneath it, crossing the page
from margin to margin, were five parallel scratches.

It would be agreeable to state that Stephen inherited the
Hall and transformed it into a place of happier memory.
In fact, prolonged litigation consumed much of the estate. The
house passed to a distant branch of the Abney family,
stood empty for eleven years, and was eventually sold. The
northern bathing-room was demolished during the alterations of 1871. Beneath
its floor the workmen discovered the bones of a female
child wrapped in decayed brown cloth embroidered with red glass
beads. In the sealed end of the old wine cellar,
a second skeleton was found, together with a small shoe
of Italian manufacture.

Neither body possessed a heart.

Stephen Elliott entered the Church and died in 1879, respected
by all who knew him. He seldom spoke of Aswarby;
but on every 21 st of March he ordered two
candles to be placed in the western window of his
rectory and left burning until dawn. His daughter believed this
to be a private religious observance. Perhaps it was.

As for Mr Abney, one final circumstance may be recorded.
During my own visit to Aswarby, I was shown the
chair in which he died, then stored in an upper
lumber-room. The upholstery had perished and the wood was worm-eaten,
but upon the inner surfaces of both arms were deep
impressions, as though made by the hands of someone gripping
them at the instant of extreme terror.

They were the marks of small fingers.

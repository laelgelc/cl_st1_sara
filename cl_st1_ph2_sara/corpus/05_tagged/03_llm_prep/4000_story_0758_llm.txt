# The Permanently Tuned Tomb

If you have any property to leave when you die,
put houses in your will, or bonds, or paving stock,
or a red cow with one horn shorter than the
other, but do not put radio in it. Radio is
too lively. A house may burn, bonds may shrink, and
a cow may kick the executor, but radio can alter
its wavelength between breakfast and lunch and make a fool
of a man before supper.

I know. I am the lawyer who drew Remington Solander's
will.

I was interested in radio myself in those days. I
had a six-tube set with three dials, four batteries, a
loop aerial, and a loud speaker that looked like the
morning-glory trumpet Gabriel will use when the last mortgage is
cancelled. It was a good set. I could get Pittsburgh,
Schenectady, Chicago, Havana, and once, during a thunderstorm, something that
sounded like a man drowning in Australia.

My friend Peasley said it was oscillation.

"You're a lunkheaded noodle," I told him. "It was Australia."

"It was your B battery running down," he said.

At that I took him by the collar and led
him toward the back door. I am not a violent
man, but nobody can insult my B battery in my
own office and expect professional courtesy. Peasley grabbed my desk,
upset the ink, and shouted that my hook-up had been
obsolete for six months. For one instant I intended to
hit him over the head with the loud speaker and
bury him at Evergreen Rest Cemetery, where I happened to
be a trustee and could have obtained a lot at
cost. Then Miss Creel, my stenographer, came in and said
Mr. Remington Solander was waiting.

So I let Peasley live.

You have probably heard of Solander. He owned the Solander
Agricultural Implement Works, three flour mills, most of our traction
company, and enough first mortgages to keep half the county
from sleeping comfortably. He was also the most devout man
I ever knew. He was devout before breakfast, devout at
the office, devout while foreclosing mortgages, and especially devout when
somebody else was enjoying himself.

He came into my room in a long black coat.
He had a pale, sheeplike face, a narrow gray beard,
and eyes like two salt mackerel laid on a plate.
When he spoke, the words dripped down his chin as
slowly as cold molasses.

"Mr. Tolliver," he said, "I am informed that you understand
the radio."

I glanced at Peasley.

"I do," I said.

Peasley made a choking sound.

"I understand it thoroughly," I added.

"That," said Solander, "is gratifying. It is most gratifying. Radio,
Mr. Tolliver, is an imperishable voice."

"That is so," I said.

It was not so, of course, as things turned out,
but any lawyer will tell you that a client must
be allowed to state his own facts until the fee
has been arranged.

"The human frame perishes," Solander continued. "The tongue becomes dust.
The lips are sealed. But radio—ah!—radio speaks on through the
ages."

"That is so, too," I said.

Peasley sneezed into his hat.

Solander turned his mackerel eyes upon him.

"Is that person necessary?"

"Not in the slightest," I said, and Peasley went out
muttering about half-baked lunatics. He may have meant Solander. He
may have meant me. Peasley was never precise in his
legal terminology.

Solander then opened a black leather portfolio and laid fourteen
manuscript books on my desk. They were thick books. They
contained sermons he had delivered to the Men's Moral Advancement
League, addresses to the Anti-Dancing Union, reports from missionaries, prayers,
admonitions, hymns, religious poems, and several essays proving that almost
every modern pleasure was the first step toward ruin.

"These," he said, touching the pile reverently, "are my message."

"All fourteen?"

"All fourteen."

I lifted one. It weighed four pounds.

"My purpose," he went on, "is to establish by my
last will and testament a broadcasting station. That station shall
transmit, and shall transmit forever, selections from these fourteen volumes,
together with approved hymns and other uplifting matter."

"That will require an endowment," I said.

"I have provided for an endowment of two hundred and
fifty thousand dollars."

I sat straighter. Religion is a fine thing, but an
endowment makes it legally interesting.

"And now," said Solander, "we approach the crown and summit
of my purpose. I desire that my mortal remains be
placed in a mausoleum at Evergreen Rest Cemetery."

I sat straighter still. As trustee, I was entitled to
be interested.

"In that mausoleum," he said, "there shall be installed a
radio receiving apparatus, with batteries, connections, aerial, amplifying tubes, and
a loud-speaking horn."

"A loud speaker in the tomb?"

"Yes."

"To entertain you?"

He looked hurt.

"To continue my influence. The receiver shall be permanently tuned
to the wavelength of my station. The programs shall issue
from my mausoleum and spread over the surrounding ground. Those
who come to mourn shall hear words of consolation. Those
who pass idly by shall hear words of warning. Even
the indifferent dead—"

He paused.

I waited.

"—will at least be in an atmosphere of righteousness," he
concluded.

This seemed fair enough. The dead at Evergreen Rest had
been getting nothing but lawn mowing and rain for years.

Solander wanted the thing done properly. He was not one
of those careless men who leave a nephew ten thousand
dollars and neglect to say whether the nephew may spend
it. No, sir. He had thought of everything.

The station must always use the same call letters. It
must always transmit on precisely the same wavelength. It must
use only the fourteen volumes, approved hymns, missionary reports, religious
poems, and such additional matter as a board of five
solemn persons might unanimously declare uplifting. No advertising was to
interrupt the programs. No market reports, prize fights, dance music,
jokes, recipes, election returns, or weather forecasts were to be
broadcast.

The receiving set in the mausoleum must never be replaced,
retuned, disconnected, repaired in any manner changing its essential character,
or removed from the marble shelf on which it was
first installed. Fresh A and B batteries must be supplied
perpetually. Burned-out tubes could be replaced only by tubes of
the identical manufacture, design, and capacity. The horn must remain
pointed toward the east gate.

"Why east?" I asked.

"Most persons enter there," he said.

That was true. Solander had considered everything.

His heirs were to receive the income from the rest
of his estate, provided that they kept the mausoleum washed,
the bronze doors polished, the radio operating, the batteries charged,
the aerial erect, the loud speaker unobstructed, and every detail
exactly as specified. Any alteration meant forfeiture. The forfeited property
would go to the trustees of Evergreen Rest, of whom
I was one.

I will say frankly that I liked that clause.

"What if radio changes?" I asked, merely to show foresight.

"Truth does not change," said Solander.

"I mean the apparatus."

"Radio is founded upon eternal scientific principles."

Naturally, I could not argue with eternal scientific principles at
my regular office rate.

It took me three weeks to draw the will. Solander
read it word by word, pausing after every "whereas" as
if it were a prayer. We added six codicils. One
concerned lightning rods. Another required the station announcer to pronounce
Solander with the accent on the first syllable. A third
forbade any female vocalist whose skirts, when she stood, failed
to cover the tops of her shoes.

He signed at last, and I put the will in
my safe.

Eight months later Remington Solander died while dictating an address
entitled "The Folly of Needless Display."

His funeral was the largest we had ever had at
Evergreen Rest. The mausoleum alone cost eighty thousand dollars. It
was solid granite, with bronze doors, stained-glass windows, twelve marble
columns, and a carved angel over the entrance holding one
finger to its lips. That angel looked increasingly inappropriate as
time went on.

The radio set was installed on its marble shelf. The
aerial ran through a bronze tube to a copper wire
hidden in a stone cross. The batteries stood in a
ventilated chamber beneath the floor. The loud speaker, which was
enormous, occupied a niche above Solander's sarcophagus and pointed, as
directed, toward the east gate.

His broadcasting station was built on Solander Hill. It had
a five-hundred-watt transmitter, a studio, an organ, two announcers, a
quartet, and a retired minister who could read a missionary
report in a voice that made cannibalism seem unusually depressing.

At three o'clock on the first Sunday the station began.

"Good afternoon," said the announcer. "This is station WSOL, the
Voice of Solander, transmitting on three hundred and forty-one meters."

Then the quartet sang "When the Roll Is Called Beyond
the Clouds," and the mausoleum spoke.

Yes, sir, it spoke.

The sound came out of that granite tomb as clear
as if Solander himself had climbed onto the roof with
a megaphone. It floated down the drives, across the flower
beds, through the cedars, and over Section F, where the
better class of monuments stood. People stopped. Women cried. Men
removed their hats. One old gentleman, who had come to
visit his third wife, dropped his cane and said he
had never heard anything so improving in his life.

After the hymn, the announcer read from Volume Fourteen, "A
Warning to the Frivolous Young."

You could hear every word as far as the receiving
vault.

The newspapers called it the Talking Tomb.

That name was not dignified, but it sold lots.

Visitors came by the hundreds, then by the thousands. Motorcars
lined the county road. Excursion parties arrived with sandwiches. Ministers
brought Sunday-school classes. Elderly ladies asked to be shown burial
sites within easy hearing distance. One gentleman from Ohio bought
a family lot merely because he said it would be
a comfort to know where he was going to spend
his radio years.

We trustees had to hire three traffic officers and a
man to pick up paper bags. The refreshment privilege outside
the gate brought us eight hundred dollars the first season,
although naturally we condemned eating during the programs.

Demand for lots went right up in the air. Property
in Section C that had lain there for twenty years
at forty dollars a grave sold at one hundred and
twenty-five, cash before interment. Lots nearest the mausoleum commanded a
premium. We called them the Auditory Circle. That was my
phrase, and I am proud of it.

The station broadcast mornings and evenings on weekdays, and continuously
on Sunday except during the batteries' rest period. At ten
came sermons. At eleven came hymns. At noon came missionary
news. At two there were religious poems. At four the
announcer read from the fourteen volumes. At six the quartet
returned, unless the tenor had one of his attacks.

The whole cemetery prospered.

We trustees began to consider expansion. Behind Evergreen Rest lay
the old Kessel farm, one hundred and sixty acres of
stony pasture, thistles, and discouraged apple trees. Kessel wanted seventy-five
thousand dollars for it.

"Outrageous," said Trustee Bell.

"Robbery," said Trustee Krimp.

"Buy it," I said.

We bought it.

We issued improvement bonds, graded drives, laid water pipes, planted
six thousand young cedars, and divided the pasture into Peaceful
Meadow, Celestial Terrace, Angels' Vista, and Radio Hill. Radio Hill
sold first, though it had the poorest drainage. We ordered
three thousand bronze lot markers. We built a new office
with a visitors' room and a window where prospective occupants
could hear WSOL before selecting their location.

Some people accused us of commercializing grief. Such people do
not understand cemetery finance. Perpetual care is expensive, and a
trustee must look ahead. Besides, every grave sold placed another
citizen within reach of Solander's uplifting influence. We were doing
well by doing good, and doing extremely well at it.

For eighteen months nothing went wrong.

Then the government reorganized the wavelengths.

I heard about it from Peasley. He came into my
office waving a newspaper.

"Well, Tolliver," he said, "what will your dead client do
now?"

"If this concerns my B battery—"

"It concerns WSOL. Under the new allocation your station moves
from three hundred and forty-one meters to two hundred and
seventy."

I snatched the paper.

There it was. In order to reduce interference, improve service,
separate high-powered stations, protect remote listeners, and otherwise promote the
public convenience, the commission had reassigned nearly every wavelength in
the country. WSOL was to move on the first of
July.

"It can't," I said.

"It must."

"Solander's will says it must remain at three hundred and
forty-one meters."

"The government says two hundred and seventy."

"Then the government has interfered with a testamentary trust."

"The government often does worse before breakfast," Peasley said.

I called a meeting of the trustees. We summoned the
Solander heirs, the station manager, three engineers, two ministers, and
Judge Wilmot, who understood wills when he was awake.

The station manager said he must obey the government or
close.

The heirs said the station must continue or they would
lose their inheritance.

The engineers said the mausoleum receiver could easily be retuned.

The heirs said retuning was expressly forbidden.

Trustee Bell proposed disconnecting the loud speaker.

The heirs pointed to the clause forbidding disconnection.

Trustee Krimp proposed removing the batteries.

The heirs pointed to perpetual battery maintenance.

One engineer suggested installing a second receiver inside the first
receiver, but Judge Wilmot woke up long enough to say
that this would be an alteration in essential character.

"Can't we shut the tomb doors?" I asked.

"They must remain open during broadcast hours," said the executor.

"Stuff the horn with cotton?"

"Unobstructed," said the executor.

"Move the mausoleum?"

"Permanently situated," said Judge Wilmot, and went back to sleep.

There was only one hopeful circumstance. Perhaps no other station
would receive the abandoned wavelength.

On July first WSOL moved to two hundred and seventy
meters. Its sermons, hymns, missionary reports, religious poems, and the
fourteen volumes continued as before. Every radio set in the
county followed it.

Every radio set except the one in Solander's tomb.

That remained at three hundred and forty-one meters, permanently, unalterably,
eternally.

For two days it gave forth nothing but static. It
crackled and hissed. The angel over the door seemed to
be asking for silence, but there was no silence. Still,
static was not immoral. We hoped the wavelength would remain
vacant.

On the third day station KJOY began broadcasting from the
city.

KJOY called itself "The Pep Station."

At nine o'clock that morning, just as a delegation from
the Ladies' Missionary Circle approached Solander's mausoleum, the loud speaker
cleared its throat with a bang and a male voice
cried:

"Wake up, everybody! Shake a leg! Here come the Hot
Six with 'Kiss Me Again Behind the Kitchen Door'!"

Then the band began.

I have heard jazz before. I have even heard Peasley
tune two stations at once. But I never heard such
jazz as came out of Remington Solander's tomb. There were
trumpets, drums, whistles, cowbells, a banjo, and something that sounded
like a cupboard full of pie tins rolling downstairs. It
came out of the granite at full volume and swept
across Evergreen Rest like a cyclone in a hardware store.

The Ladies' Missionary Circle fled through Peaceful Meadow. One lost
her hat in Angels' Vista. Another crossed two vacant lots
without opening the gates.

The number ended.

A young woman's voice said, "Oh, boys, wasn't that wicked?"

Then a comedian told a joke about a traveling salesman,
a flapper, and a bottle concealed in a hot-water bag.

The Solander heirs went white.

"Turn it off!" they cried.

"We can't," I said.

"Retune it!"

"We can't."

"Do something!"

That was unreasonable. I am a lawyer, not a miracle
worker.

We rushed to court. We asked permission to alter the
receiver. The heirs opposed us because alteration meant forfeiture. The
attorney general opposed us because the government controlled wavelengths. The
station KJOY opposed us because it had obtained the frequency
legally. Judge Wilmot studied the will for three weeks and
decided that Solander had expressed his intentions so plainly that
no court could misunderstand them, however unfortunate those intentions had
become.

The tomb must continue.

And it did.

All day the proper Solander station broadcast uplifting matter on
two hundred and seventy meters, while Solander himself received KJOY
on three hundred and forty-one.

At ten in the morning, when WSOL aired a sermon
on temperance, the tomb aired "Who Hid the Corkscrew, Rosie?"

At noon, while WSOL read a missionary report, the tomb
broadcast baseball scores.

On Sunday afternoon, during Solander's own essay against dancing, KJOY
presented the Metropolitan Charleston Orchestra.

You know the sort of program. A horn would shriek,
a drum would explode, and then somebody would sing:

"Clara wears her galoshes high,
She winks at every passing guy—"

After that came jokes.

The jokes were not the worst jokes ever told, but
they became worse in a cemetery. A remark that might
sound merely foolish in a theater sounded criminal when shouted
across six acres of tombstones.

Our lot sales stopped in one day.

Within a week families began removing bodies. The first was
Mrs. Harkness. She said her husband had disliked jazz while
alive and she would not have him subjected to it
merely because he was helpless. Then the Pritchards removed four
relatives from the Auditory Circle. Reverend Blane transferred his wife
to a cemetery twelve miles away after KJOY broadcast "Oh,
You Naughty Little Widow" during his memorial visit.

We tried reducing the volume, but the will required it
to remain at the level fixed on the day of
installation. We planted trees around the mausoleum. The sound came
through them. We built an ornamental stone screen. Judge Wilmot
ruled that it obstructed the loud speaker. We ran lawn
mowers during the worst programs, but KJOY advertised itself as
the station that could be heard above anything, and it
was.

The removals became a stampede.

Every morning trucks entered Evergreen Rest carrying empty boxes. Every
evening they departed with customers. Monuments came down, headstones were
uprooted, family vaults were opened, and undertakers worked overtime. People
who had not visited their dead for twenty years suddenly
became furious about musical standards.

Our new addition suffered most. Radio Hill was cleared in
nine days. Peaceful Meadow lasted two weeks. Celestial Terrace held
out until KJOY introduced a midnight program called "The Pajama
Frolic." After that even the trustees removed their relatives.

I removed mine for business reasons.

By autumn Evergreen Rest looked like a battlefield. The grass
was trampled, the roads were scarred, and thousands of open
graves and empty foundations covered the ground like shell-holes. The
office was deserted. The bronze markers rusted in a shed.
The Kessel farm, for which we still owed sixty thousand
dollars, was worth less than it had been when it
grew thistles.

Only one body remained.

Remington Solander could not be moved, because his will forbade
it.

His heirs still polish the bronze doors. They wash the
granite. They replace the tubes with tubes of identical manufacture,
design, and capacity. They supply fresh A and B batteries.
They keep the aerial erect and the horn pointed toward
the east gate. If they fail in any particular, the
property comes to us trustees—and there is not enough money
in the world to make me accept it.

KJOY is stronger now. It broadcasts until two in the
morning.

Sometimes I drive past the old cemetery at night. Weeds
cover Angels' Vista. Rabbits live in the visitors' room. Moonlight
shines into the open holes, and from the middle of
all that emptiness comes a trumpet, a laugh, a crash
of drums, and some cheerful idiot shouting:

"Come on, folks! There's life in the old place yet!"

That is why I say Remington Solander was the lucky
man.

He is the only person in Evergreen Rest who cannot
move away.

### The Vacant Chair

On Thanksgiving evening, while the wind went mourning about the
eaves and drove the powdery snow across the frozen highway,
John Inglefield, the blacksmith, sat in his accustomed chimney-corner, surrounded
by those remnants of his household which Death and Sin
had spared him. The ruddy blaze smote full upon his
broad and darkened visage, and showed him like an iron
image, fashioned at his own anvil, yet retaining within its
massive breast a secret heat which no winter could extinguish.
His hair was grizzled; his shoulders, though somewhat bowed by
sorrow, might still have borne a heavier burden than age
commonly lays upon mankind; and his hands, hardened by long
fellowship with hammer and bar, rested idly upon his knees.

Opposite him sat his son Philip, lately returned from missionary
labours among the forest settlements. His countenance possessed that grave
serenity which is born less of an untried heart than
of one accustomed to behold earthly affliction beneath the light
of Providence. Near Philip was Mary, the blacksmith's younger daughter—a
tender rosebud of a maiden, whose soft beauty seemed the
more delicate beside her father's rugged strength. Robert Moore, a
young farmer of the neighbourhood, completed the circle. He had
once been the avowed lover of another daughter of that
house.

There remained one empty chair.

It had belonged to the dead mother, and none had
occupied it since the winter when she went forth from
the warmth of her household into the cold chamber of
the grave. John Inglefield had suffered it to retain its
place by the hearth, as if the visible vacancy might
preserve, more faithfully than any monument, the memory of her
who had made that hearth sacred. Yet to-night the chair
awakened a double grief; for the dead woman and the
absent daughter appeared to mingle in its shadow.

That absent one was Prudence, Mary's twin sister. The two
had sprung like blossoms from one stem; but while Mary
remained sheltered in the garden of home, Prudence had been
torn away by reckless hands, and rumour declared that she
had fallen beneath the feet of the world. Two years
had elapsed since she fled her father's door. During that
interval, no message had come from her which a virtuous
household might receive without shame.

Thus sat they, each striving to give thanks, yet each
conscious of a sorrow that would not be silenced. The
board had been cleared of its humble feast. Apples reddened
on a pewter dish; nuts lay scattered beside it; and
the kettle, singing above the fire, seemed to utter a
cheerful domestic voice in mockery of the gloom. Mary endeavoured
to smile while speaking of the day's small pleasures. Philip
related an incident of his journey. Robert gazed into the
blaze and saw there, perhaps, the countenance which had once
looked upon him with promised love.

Then came a light footstep at the door.

The latch was lifted without ceremony. A gust of wintry
air rushed inward, causing the flames to shrink and the
shadows to leap wildly along the walls; and, in the
doorway, with snow upon her cloak and a hood drawn
closely about her face, stood a woman.

"Father," said she, "have you room for one more at
your Thanksgiving fire?"

John Inglefield arose. His great frame trembled, though no stroke
of his own hammer had ever shaken it. Mary uttered
a cry and clasped her hands. Philip bent forward with
a look in which human affection strove with ministerial solemnity.
Robert sprang from his seat as if the voice had
broken a spell of years.

"Prudence!" they exclaimed.

The wanderer entered and closed the door behind her. For
an instant she stood aloof, while the firelight wavered upon
her form and left her face in obscurity. Then, with
a motion half bold and half despairing, she cast back
the hood.

She was still beautiful. Sin, though it may deface the
soul in an hour, does not always write its whole
history immediately upon the visage. Her cheek was pale from
the cold, but the returning warmth soon kindled there a
colour like that of former days. Her dark hair, once
arranged by Mary's innocent fingers, lay in glossy folds about
her brow. Yet something dwelt in her eyes which had
never entered them beneath that roof—a restless brilliance, now mocking,
now mournful, and terrible when it seemed most joyous.

John Inglefield extended his hand.

"Thou hast come home, Prudence," said he. The sternness of
the father contended with the tenderness of the widower, for
in the guilty daughter he beheld a resemblance to her
buried mother. "If only for this night, thou art welcome."

"Only for this night," repeated Prudence, with a strange smile.
"Remember those words, father."

Mary rushed toward her, eager to entwine their arms as
they had done in childhood; but Prudence drew back.

"Nay, little sister," she said, gently. "Look at me, if
thou wilt, but do not touch me."

Mary's outstretched hands fell slowly to her side.

"Canst thou fear me?" she murmured.

"I fear myself," answered Prudence.

Philip now approached. "Sister," said he, "it is no accident
that hath led thy steps hitherward. Providence has opened the
door which pride and grief had closed. Enter not merely
into this house, but into the repentance and peace that
await thee."

"Good Philip!" she replied. "Thou didst preach even when we
gathered berries in the wood. Perchance I have come to
hear one sermon more."

Robert had meanwhile remained near her, breathing quickly and unable
to command the tumult of his heart. At last he
seized her hand. She snatched it away, as though his
honest touch had burned her.

"Prudence," he cried, "whatever the world may say, I remember
thee as thou wert!"

"Then keep that remembrance," said she. "It is better than
anything thou canst know of me now."

Without further invitation, she crossed the room and seated herself
in the vacant chair.

A silence followed. To every heart it seemed that a
dead woman had surrendered her place to a daughter dead
in another and more dreadful sense. Yet, as Prudence leaned
toward the blaze, extending her pale fingers to its warmth,
the sadness of the image gradually softened. The old chair
appeared to recognise the graceful burden which it had often
supported in her childhood; and the fire, that kindly magician
of winter evenings, spread its warm deception over her countenance.

By degrees the household admitted her into its familiar talk.
She asked concerning neighbours, cows, orchards, weddings, and graves, mingling
laughter with sudden sighs. She remembered the loose stone beside
the well and the swallow's nest beneath the barn roof.
She knew which apple tree gave fruit sweetest after frost,
and where Mary had hidden a broken doll when they
were eight years old. Each recollection was a thread, and
with such threads did affection strive to weave again the
garment of innocence.

John Inglefield watched her, and his iron features relaxed. Once,
when she laughed at some forgotten mischief of her girlhood,
he laughed also—a deep, unwonted sound, at which Mary's eyes
filled with tears. Philip ceased to admonish and listened as
a brother. Robert moved nearer, drawn by a hope against
which reason and experience spoke in vain.

For an hour the past conquered the present.

Prudence herself yielded to the illusion. Her voice lost its
worldly accent; her eyes grew softer; and, sitting where her
mother had sat, she appeared less the sinful wanderer than
the merry daughter who had once made the rafters ring
with song. Mary forgot the forbidden embrace and knelt beside
the chair, her golden head almost resting upon her sister's
knee. The twin blossoms, one preserved and one trampled, seemed
again to bloom upon a single stem.

"Sing the old hymn, Prudence," said Mary.

"No," she answered, and a shadow crossed her face. "Ask
me for anything but that."

The clock in the corner struck eight.

Its first solemn note caused Prudence to start. Before the
last vibration died, she had risen from the chair. The
tender colour vanished from her cheek, and in its stead
appeared a hardened look, as though an unseen master had
summoned his bondslave and she dared not disobey.

"Our hour of worship has come," said Philip. He reached
for the great Bible. "Remain, sister. If prayer be difficult,
let us pray for thee."

Prudence took her cloak from its peg.

"I must go."

Mary clung to the garment. "Go? Into the storm? Thou
hast but just come home!"

"Home!" repeated Prudence. The word burst from her like a
sob, yet she laughed immediately afterward. "Yes, this was home—for
one little hour."

John Inglefield placed himself before the door.

"Daughter," he said, and his voice had in it the
authority that once subdued unruly horses and violent men, "thou
shalt not leave this house to-night."

She looked steadfastly upon him. Then the fire sank, and
its expiring gleam revealed what its brightness had concealed. It
was not age, nor sorrow, nor even shame, that transformed
her countenance; it was the visible sovereignty of Sin. A
fiend seemed to gaze from behind her beauty, claiming the
features as a mask and the soul as its possession.

"Father," she said, "command the dead to arise. They may
hear thee sooner."

He staggered back.

From without came the sound of wheels, muffled by snow,
then the impatient stamping of horses. A carriage halted before
the door. Its lamps cast two yellow beams across the
window and upon the white road, like eyes belonging to
some monstrous creature of the night.

Robert seized Prudence by the arm.

"Let me go with thee," he pleaded. "Or remain, and
I will forget all. Before God, I will make thee
my wife!"

She turned upon him with sudden anguish.

"Forget me, Robert! If there be kindness in thy heart,
forget the girl thou lovedst, and never seek the woman
who stands before thee."

Philip opened the Bible.

"Prudence, while breath remains, repentance is possible."

"Possible?" she whispered.

For one instant the lost one faltered. Her gaze travelled
around the room—from the father whose pride had become grief,
to the brother whose faith still held open the gate
of mercy, to the innocent twin, and lastly to the
chair by the hearth. Her hands rose as if she
would tear the cloak from her shoulders and cast herself
among them.

Then the driver cracked his whip.

The sound was slight, yet it broke every tender bond.
Prudence thrust Mary aside, passed her father, and opened the
door. Snow whirled into the room. She stood upon the
threshold, between the consecrated fire and the outer darkness.

"Look upon me now," she cried, "for ye shall never
look upon me again!"

The door closed. The carriage rolled rapidly away, its lamps
dwindling along the frozen road until the night devoured them.

That same evening, in a neighbouring city, a theatre blazed
with a thousand lights. Music, laughter, painted scenery, and faces
painted more falsely than the scenery dazzled the crowded house.
Among the dissolute performers appeared a woman whose beauty drew
loud applause. She smiled, bowed, and scattered kisses to the
multitude.

It was Prudence Inglefield.

Yet perhaps, amid that profane clamour, her soul still beheld
the quiet hearth, the kneeling sister, and the vacant chair.
Thus does guilty memory revisit innocence, not always to redeem,
but sometimes to sharpen punishment. For Sin may permit its
captives one backward glance toward Paradise, even while it hurries
them onward into darkness; and the saddest homecoming is that
of the soul which can enter the door, can feel
the blessed warmth, and yet hath lost the power to
remain.

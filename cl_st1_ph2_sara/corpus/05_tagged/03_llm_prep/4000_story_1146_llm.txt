Disgrace is a persevering lady. Once she has taken a
man's arm, she will walk with him long after the
law, the minister, and his own mother have declared the
engagement broken. He may change his coat, his address, his
politics, and the part in his hair; but there she
is, rustling beside him, smiling at acquaintances before he can
dodge into an alley.

Ted Terrill found this out when he came home to
Bellwether.

Now, before you picture Ted as a hairy villain with
a scar across one eye and a black cigar pointing
toward further crimes, let us correct you. Authors are fond
of scars. They save explanation. Ted had no scar except
the sort that does not show in a mirror.

Five years earlier he had been the handsomest cashier the
Bellwether National Bank ever placed behind brass bars. He was
twenty-six, fair-haired, quick with figures, quicker with a joke, and
possessed of a smile that caused the young ladies of
our smart set to mislay their deposit slips. His cuffs
were always clean. His neckties suggested courage without actual violence.
When he walked down Main Street, old men said, "There
goes a likely young fellow," and young women said nothing,
which meant considerably more.

Then seven thousand dollars went away from the bank.

Money is skittish. Leave it alone with temptation and it
will climb fences.

Ted had speculated, lost, borrowed from the bank without troubling
the directors, speculated again, and lost with greater finish. At
the trial he did not invent a dead uncle in
Argentina or blame an unnamed stranger with hypnotic eyes. He
pleaded guilty. That was the one dignified thing in an
affair which had very little dignity to spare.

Bellwether attended the trial as if it had been a
church festival. We were shocked, grieved, delighted, and home in
time for supper. The judge gave Ted four years. His
mother died during the second. His friends wrote during the
first three months, sent Christmas cards during the first year,
and thereafter remembered that postage was expensive.

When he came back, on a raw March afternoon, he
had one brown suitcase, twenty-three dollars, and a gray hat
that sat on his yellow hair like a halo assigned
to the wrong department.

He stepped off the train and looked about.

No band played. This was sensible of the band.

The station agent, who had once called him Teddy and
borrowed ten dollars from him, found urgent business among the
trunks. Mrs. Bixby, president of the Thursday Culture Circle, stared
through Ted with such thoroughness that she discovered a freight
car on the farther track. Two boys nudged each other.
A woman drew her little girl closer, though Ted had
never stolen a child in his life.

He picked up his suitcase.

Disgrace picked up nothing. Ladies of her sort travel light.

Ted walked to the Haley House because the Haley House
was near the station, and because a man with twenty-three
dollars cannot afford symbolism in choosing a hotel. Its brick
front had once been red and was now a color
best described as experience. A gilt sign over the door
promised STEAM HEAT, GOOD TABLE, MODERN CONVENIENCES. The steam heat
was temperamental, the table excellent, and the modern conveniences had
been modern when President McKinley was learning to shave.

Jo Haley sat behind the desk, reading the racing page
through spectacles he denied needing. Jo owned the hotel, three
suits, fourteen grudges, and a heart which he kept wrapped
in newspaper so nobody would recognize it.

He looked up.

"Well," he said, "if it ain't the Wandering Boy."

Ted put down his suitcase.

"Hello, Jo."

"You look awful."

"I'm all right."

"That's what dying men say when they don't want the
undertaker to get conceited. Register."

"I'm not staying."

"You're standing in my lobby with luggage. That's close enough
to staying for a warrant."

"I wanted a room until the night train."

"Where you going?"

"West."

Jo leaned back and considered him. "How far west?"

"I don't know."

"Fine country, Don't Know. Good climate. Population mostly fools and
fugitives."

Ted's face tightened. Five years before he would have tossed
back a joke. Now his words came out separately, as
if each had to pass inspection.

"I can get work somewhere people haven't heard of me."

"People hear. It's what ears are for."

"I'll change my name."

"And what'll you do with yourself? Leave that in the
baggage room?"

Ted glanced toward the door. "I didn't come for a
lecture."

"No," said Jo. "Lectures cost more than rooms, and you're
poor. You came to run."

"What else can I do here?"

"Work."

Ted laughed once, without amusement. "Who'd hire me?"

"I would."

That stopped him.

Jo removed his spectacles. "Minnie Wenzel's leaving Saturday to marry
Elmer Trapp. Lord knows why either one of 'em wants
the punishment, but they've applied for it voluntarily. I need
a bookkeeper."

Ted's hand closed over the handle of his suitcase.

"You'd put me on the books?"

"I said bookkeeper. If I wanted somebody to eat 'em,
I'd advertise for a goat."

"You know what they'll say."

Jo waved toward the lobby, toward Main Street, toward all
Bellwether. "They're saying it now. Mrs. Bixby's probably got three
telephone girls holding wires while she tells the Methodist Ladies'
Aid you've returned with a gang of international jewel thieves.
Staying won't start talk. Leaving won't stop it."

"I can't stand it, Jo."

"Then sit down."

"I mean—people looking at me."

"Let 'em look. Most of 'em have nothing better to
do, and the rest ought to be working."

Ted's mouth moved, but no reply came. He looked suddenly
younger and much older—like a boy dressed in his father's
weariness.

Jo lowered his voice.

"You took your lickin'. Maybe not enough for some folks;
they'd enjoy seeing you serve eternity with an extra week
for good measure. But the state says you're through. I'm
willing to take the state's word over Mrs. Bixby's, though
it pains me to oppose a lady."

"I don't know if I can."

"That's why I'm telling you. Men who know they can
do a thing generally don't need advice."

The office door opened, and Birdie Callahan came through carrying
six cups and eight saucers on a tray. Birdie always
carried more saucers than cups. Perhaps she expected reinforcements.

Birdie was head waitress at the Haley House. She had
red hair, iron-gray eyes, square capable hands, and a face
which, considered feature by feature, looked like a huge mistake.
Her nose had enthusiasm but no direction. Her mouth was
too wide. Her freckles had organized beyond all control. Yet
she walked like a panther and could make a dining
room full of commercial travelers obey her by lifting one
eyebrow.

She saw Ted.

The tray tilted. One cup jumped, thought better of it,
and remained.

"Saints above," she said. "It's Mister Terrill."

Ted flushed. "Hello, Birdie."

She set down the tray and wiped her hands on
her apron, though they were clean.

"Well, will ye look at him! Thin as a hotel
blanket and twice as gloomy. Have they no vittles in
that place, or did ye insult the cook?"

Jo said, "He's leaving on the night train."

Birdie turned upon Ted. "Is he, now?"

"I thought I would."

"West," Jo explained. "He's got a position waiting in Don't
Know."

Birdie sniffed. "A fine position. He'll be promoted to Nowhere
in six months."

Ted looked from one to the other. "Jo offered me
the bookkeeping job."

"Did he? There's sense in him yet, though it's hid
like a flea in a carpet."

"But I can't—"

"Can't add?"

"I can add."

"Can't write?"

"Of course I can."

"Then it's the chairs, maybe. Are ye afraid av chairs?"

"Birdie—"

"Or the ink? Black ink is fierce stuff. I seen
a bottle av it chase Minnie Wenzel clear across the
office yesterday."

Jo put a registration card before Ted and pushed a
pen across the desk. "Sign."

There are moments when destiny enters with trumpets. This was
not one of them. A radiator clanked, a fly fell
into the inkwell, and somebody in the dining room shouted
that his gravy had a thumbprint. Still, Ted signed.

Birdie seized his suitcase.

"I'll show him to seventeen."

"You'll do nothing," said Jo. "Get Parker his gravy."

"Let Parker lick the thumbprint. It may be the nearest
he comes to affection."

She carried the suitcase upstairs.

That was how Ted Terrill remained in Bellwether.

The town did not approve. Towns rarely approve of anything
until it succeeds, whereupon they claim to have encouraged it
from the beginning.

For the first week Ted worked in the little glass-walled
office behind the desk. The books of the Haley House
were not dishonest, but they were imaginative. Jo charged his
cigars to Maintenance, flowers for the lobby to Advertising, and
a new dress for his widowed sister to Fire Protection.
Minnie Wenzel had kept everything in a sloping ornamental hand,
with capitals shaped like swans and sevens wearing hats.

Minnie herself remained three days to instruct Ted. She was
a pale, narrow young woman whose upper lip seemed perpetually
offended by her lower one. She wore black sateen sleevelets
at the desk and a yellow plush hat at luncheon.
The hat was famous in Bellwether. Children followed it in
the street, expecting music.

Her marriage to Elmer Trapp, assistant manager of the Elite
Furniture Emporium, was to occur in April. Minnie spoke of
it not as a wedding but as a merger between
two important houses.

"My trousseau," she told Ted, "is entirely imported in effect."

"In effect?"

"The actual importing is immaterial. A thing may be spiritually
Parisian."

She showed Birdie an embroidered luncheon cloth with twelve napkins,
a silk kimono painted with storks, and a corset purchased
from a Chicago catalogue at a price which made Birdie's
own pocketbook yell "Whoa!"

"'Tis grand," Birdie said. "Will Elmer be wearin' the corset,
or is it for company?"

Minnie did not speak to her for two days.

Ted kept his head down and learned the accounts. Guests
passed the glass wall and looked in. Some whispered. Others
ceased whispering and relied on eyebrows. Old acquaintances entered the
lobby, recognized him, and became fascinated by the chandelier.

One evening Philip Dorr, who had once been Ted's closest
friend, came to the desk for a cigar.

Ted rose.

"Hello, Phil."

Phil selected a cigar with religious concentration. "Why—hello, Terrill."

"How are you?"

"Good. Good. Busy, you know."

"Yes."

"Real busy."

Phil paid Jo, though Ted stood nearer the cash drawer.
Then he remembered an appointment and went out without lighting
the cigar.

Ted sat down.

Jo said, "He always was a coward."

"He testified for me."

"He testified because they put him under oath. That's the
advantage of swearing a man in; it sometimes scares the
truth out of him."

Ted bent over the ledger. His ears were red.

Only Birdie behaved as she had before. This was not
quite true, because before the trouble she had called him
"Mister Terrill" and now, when annoyed, called him "ye poor
puddin'-headed corpse." But hers was the only alteration that did
him good.

She brought coffee to the office at ten. She made
him eat. She bullied the chambermaids when they exchanged opinions
outside his door. When a drummer from Peoria asked whether
it was safe to leave his watch in the hotel,
Birdie said:

"Safe enough, sir. No one here would be caught dead
with that watch."

The drummer laughed uncertainly and put it back in his
pocket.

At night Ted sometimes sat alone in the lobby after
the lamps were turned low. He had been a breezy
talker once. Now silence occupied him like a tenant with
a long lease. Birdie, setting tables for breakfast, would glance
through the dining-room doors and see him staring at nothing.

One night she came out with a stack of napkins.

"Ye've got the crease out av your trousers," she said.

Ted looked down.

"That's what happens when a man sits around mournin' over
himself. His soul may recover, but his pants is ruined."

He almost smiled.

Birdie began folding napkins. Her thick fingers made white lilies
with astonishing delicacy.

"You used to like me, didn't you, Birdie?"

She did not look up. "I used to like sassafras
tea. People survive strange tastes."

"I mean before."

"Before what? Before ye learned arithmetic at public expense?"

He winced.

Birdie slapped a napkin flat. "Now don't be after makin'
a weapon out av every word. If I meant to
hurt ye, ye'd not need to wonder."

"My mother used to tell me things would come right."

"Your mother had faith."

"She had too much."

"There's no such ailment."

He rubbed his forehead. "Sometimes I wish—"

"Yes?"

"Nothing."

Birdie saw the child in his face then, the homesick
child hidden behind the prison pallor and the careful, humbled
voice. She wanted to put a hand on his yellow
head. Instead she said:

"Stand up."

"What?"

"Stand up, I tell ye."

He obeyed.

She tugged his coat down, brushed lint from his shoulder,
and inspected his trousers.

"Take these to Dugan's tomorrow. Tell him to press 'em
proper. A man may have a crushed spirit if he
enjoys it, but there's no excuse for baggy knees."

Then she marched into the dining room.

Birdie's heart went with her reluctantly and backward, but hearts
are often poor walkers.

For a month nothing worse happened than Sunday.

Sunday was difficult because Bellwether put on its respectability then.
The church bells rang; the smart set rolled past in
scarlet-upholstered touring cars; ladies with radiant blondeur achieved through private
chemistry bowed to Jo and failed to see Ted. He
went to his mother's grave in the afternoon, taking streets
where fewer people could observe him doing so.

Yet he improved. He answered guests without stammering. He caught
errors in the supply bills. He discovered that the laundry
company had charged the hotel for four hundred napkins it
had never seen, which was clever of both the laundry
company and Ted. Jo raised his wages three dollars.

"You're worth five more," Jo said, "but gratitude's bad for
character."

Ted even laughed.

Then Minnie Wenzel returned.

It was a Tuesday morning, windy and bright. Minnie had
been married eleven days. Matrimony had not softened her. It
had merely given her a new last name, which she
carried like a handbag. She entered in a blue traveling
suit, followed by Elmer, who looked furnished rather than dressed.

Jo glanced at the clock. "Breakfast over."

"We did not come to eat," Minnie said.

Birdie, passing with a coffee pot, murmured, "That's the first
sensible thing marriage has done for her."

Minnie ignored this.

"I must speak with you privately, Mr. Haley."

"Speak publicly. Saves repetitions."

"It concerns the accounts."

Ted looked up from the adding machine.

Minnie's eyes touched him and slid away. "I found certain
memoranda among my papers. They indicated an irregularity which I
felt, as a former employee and a conscientious person, obliged
to investigate."

Jo sighed. "How much?"

"Eight hundred and sixty dollars."

The number entered the lobby and shut all the doors
behind it.

Ted stood.

"What do you mean?"

Minnie opened a leather portfolio. "During my last week here,
several cash receipts were not deposited. I assumed the omission
had been corrected. Yesterday I compared my retained duplicate sheets
with the bank statements."

"You retained duplicate sheets?" Jo asked.

"I am methodical."

"You're a pack rat with sleevelets. Go on."

"These entries show that the money was on hand when
I turned the books over to Mr. Terrill. The later
ledger pages show false deposit totals. The difference is eight
hundred and sixty dollars."

Ted stared at her.

"What's the joke?"

"No joke," said Elmer. He had been waiting to contribute
and looked relieved.

Ted took the sheets Minnie offered. His hand shook. There
were her initials on the closing balance. There was his
own handwriting on later entries. A deposit of six hundred
and forty dollars had become nine hundred and forty. Another
had been changed by five hundred and sixty. The bank
statement showed the smaller sums.

"What's the joke?" he repeated.

Jo came around the desk and pulled the papers from
him.

Minnie said, "I wished to avoid an ugly scene."

"Then why'd you bring Elmer?" Birdie asked. "He improves no
scenery I've noticed."

Elmer reddened. "Madam—"

"I'm no madam. I work for a livin'."

The first whisper started with the bellboy, who carried it
to the kitchen. The second cook gave it to a
chambermaid; the chambermaid dropped it from the second-floor landing to
a traveling salesman; the salesman polished it and presented it
to two hardware merchants. Within fifteen minutes Mrs. Bixby telephoned
to ask whether her silver-backed hairbrush was safe, though she
had not stayed at the Haley House since 1911.

Gossip does not glide. Authors are fond of the word
"glide," but gossip gallops, tumbles downstairs, climbs drainpipes, and arrives
ahead of the facts with its hat on crooked.

Ted did not hear the whispers. He stood inside the
glass office, staring at the ledger.

Jo examined the entries.

"Looks like your writing," he said.

"It is."

"You remember making 'em?"

Ted pressed both hands to his temples. "I—I don't know."

Minnie's offended upper lip relaxed slightly.

Jo looked at him. "Think."

"I'm trying."

"You entered these deposits on March twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth."

"I must have."

"Did you take the money?"

Ted's face went white.

Birdie set down the coffee pot.

"No," she said.

Jo did not turn. "I asked him."

"And I answered ye."

Ted whispered, "Maybe."

Nobody moved.

Jo's voice became very flat. "Maybe what?"

"Maybe I did."

Elmer made a sound of triumph. Minnie lowered her eyes
modestly, as if receiving applause at a piano recital.

Birdie came through the office door.

"Have ye gone daft?"

Ted looked at her. "The figures are mine."

"Figures have no morals. They'll say whatever a dirty hand
teaches 'em."

"I've been afraid—" He swallowed. "Ever since I came back.
Sometimes I lose track. At night. I wake up and
can't remember what I was thinking. Suppose I took it
without knowing? Suppose it's in me, and I—"

He could not finish.

Here was the deepest cruelty of disgrace: it had taught
him to testify against himself. The town's suspicion had moved
inside and taken lodgings. Faced with his own handwriting, he
trusted Minnie Wenzel before he trusted Ted Terrill.

Jo seized his shoulder.

"Look at me. Men don't steal eight hundred dollars in
a trance."

"I stole before."

"And knew it."

"I said I'd pay it back."

"That's what thieves say when they're awake."

Birdie leaned close to Ted. "Where's the money?"

"I don't know."

"Have ye bought anything?"

"No."

"Fine clothes?"

He looked down at his shiny cuffs.

"A motorcar? Diamonds? A tame elephant?"

"Birdie, don't."

"I'll not don't. Eight hundred and sixty dollars is a
large animal. It leaves tracks."

Minnie said coldly, "The absence of visible expenditure proves nothing.
Such persons often conceal money."

"Such persons," Birdie repeated.

"The criminal type."

Birdie turned. Her face, never constructed for angelic expressions, became
downright architectural.

"Minnie Trapp, I helped pack your trunk."

Minnie blinked. "What has that to do with this?"

"I seen the silk nightgowns."

Elmer coughed.

"The luncheon cloth from Chicago," Birdie continued. "The stork kimono,
the hand shoes, the satin slippers with beads, the silver
brush set, the traveling clock, the lavender corset—"

"That is intimate apparel!"

"It was intimate with me when I sat on the
trunk to shut it."

Jo looked from Birdie to Minnie.

Birdie planted her square hands on her hips. "And I
seen the bills."

Minnie's face altered.

Birdie saw it. So did Jo. Ted did not; he
was still looking inward at the cell he had rebuilt
for himself.

"What bills?" Jo asked.

"The ones tucked under her embroidered petticoats. One hundred twelve
dollars for the kimono and nightgowns. Eighty-four for linens. Thirty-six
for the corset, which is robbery itself unless it does
the dishes. Ninety-five for the silver set. Fifty for the
hat with the dead bird, and the bird's the lucky
one. More besides."

"My private purchases are irrelevant," Minnie said.

"Private, is it? Ye waved every stitch under the noses
av the dining room."

"I saved."

"On twenty-two dollars a week?"

"I had investments."

Elmer turned toward her. "You told me your aunt sent
the money."

"My aunt's affairs are not under discussion."

"Your aunt in Scranton?"

"Yes."

Birdie grinned. "The aunt that died when ye were twelve?"

Elmer's furnished appearance began to come apart.

Minnie gripped her portfolio. "This is outrageous."

"B'gorra, it's only startin'."

Birdie snatched the duplicate sheets from Jo and held them
to the window.

"I watched her rule them forms for three years. She
dots her i's like pinholes and curls her sevens like
a pig's tail. Look here. The totals are in her
ink."

Minnie laughed too quickly. "Mr. Terrill could imitate my hand."

"He could also fly over the courthouse if ye put
feathers in his ears, but he didn't."

Jo took the sheet. "She's right. Different ink."

"Old entries fade," Minnie said.

"Not sideways," said Jo.

Birdie reached for the ledger. She riffled the pages, muttering
dates. Then she stopped.

"Here. March twenty-seventh. Minnie stayed late that night."

"I was completing my duties."

"And Ted had gone to Dugan's for his trousers."

Ted raised his head.

Birdie jabbed a finger at the ledger. "Ye left your
pen here, didn't ye, Ted? The green fountain pen your
mother gave ye."

"Yes."

"These false entries are in green ink."

Jo looked at Ted's breast pocket. "Where's the pen?"

"I lost it that week."

Minnie took a step backward.

Birdie advanced one.

"Lost it, was it? Or did Miss Imported-in-Effect borrow it?
She changed the ledger, made duplicate sheets to show the
money had been there, and carried the cash out in
that yellow plush hat. There was room enough for a
brass band."

"You cannot prove—"

"Open your purse," Jo said.

Minnie clutched it.

"I said open it."

Elmer whispered, "Minnie?"

Jo's tone softened, which was dangerous. "We can call Sheriff
Deane. He enjoys opening things."

Minnie looked at Ted. For one instant her thin face
showed naked resentment.

"You had no right to come back," she said.

There it was.

Ted stared.

"You had no right," she repeated, louder now. "Everyone knew
what you were. When Mr. Haley put you in the
office, I knew that if anything was missing—if there were
questions—they would believe—"

"They would believe Ted," Jo finished for her.

"No! They would believe it of him."

The distinction hung in the air like a noose.

Elmer sat down heavily.

Minnie began to cry—not beautifully, not with shining tears and
a trembling lip, but with snorts, blotches, and fury. Real
crying is hard on the complexion and good for the
truth.

She had taken two hundred first, she confessed. Then three.
She expected wedding gifts of money. Elmer's uncle had promised
them five hundred dollars, but the uncle had invested instead
in a patent chicken-waterer and produced neither money nor wet
chickens. The bills came. Minnie took more. When Ted arrived,
frightened and branded, the solution presented itself dressed and ready.

"I meant to replace it," she said.

Ted flinched at the old sentence.

Jo heard it too. "That's generally the first thing stolen
money buys—a good intention."

Minnie had forged the duplicate records, altered the ledger with
Ted's pen, and hidden the original cash sheets. She had
returned only because Jo's monthly audit was approaching and she
wished the discovery to occur under her supervision.

Elmer covered his face.

Birdie looked at him with some pity. "Cheer up. The
corset's paid for."

Jo telephoned the sheriff.

Ted sat at the desk while the lobby filled, emptied,
and filled again. News now galloped in the opposite direction.
The same mouths that had condemned him began revising their
memories.

"I never believed it," said the bellboy, who had believed
it first.

"I told my wife there must be some mistake," said
a hardware merchant who had told his wife Ted would
hang.

Mrs. Bixby telephoned again to say the Thursday Culture Circle
had always favored rehabilitation in principle.

Jo replied that Ted could not come to the telephone
because he was busy being rehabilitated in practice.

By late afternoon Minnie had gone with the sheriff, Elmer
had gone after Minnie, and the yellow plush hat had
gone nowhere, having been left on a lobby chair in
the excitement. Jo put it in the lost-and-found closet, where
it frightened an umbrella.

Ted checked the accounts twice. The missing sum was covered
by Minnie's confession and the goods she agreed to surrender.
Everything balanced.

Figures are cold company, but that evening they were kinder
than people.

At six, Birdie came into the office wearing her coat.
It was a plain brown coat with a collar that
had given up pretending to be fur. Her red hair
was tucked beneath a black hat. She looked tired.

Ted stood.

"Birdie."

"If ye're after supper, there's hash."

"I wanted to thank you."

"Don't. It delays the dishes."

"You saved me."

"I added what Minnie spent. Any woman could."

"No one else did."

"That's because the rest av them were busy enjoyin' your
funeral."

He came around the desk.

"Let me walk you home."

Birdie's hand paused on her coat button.

It was a small request. Main Street to Callahan's boardinghouse
was six blocks. The evening was clear. Lamps were coming
on in shop windows, and the sky above the courthouse
had turned the pale green color that belongs to spring
and old glass bottles.

Ted smiled. Not his former smile—not yet. But a little
of the old radiance had returned, uncertain as a lamp
newly lit.

"I'd like to," he said. "If you would."

Birdie looked at him for a long moment.

She loved him. We may as well say it, since
she would not. She had loved him when he was
the golden cashier with half the town's daughters orbiting him.
She had loved him when the court took him away.
She loved him now, worn, timid, and trying to stand
upright in a place eager to measure his stoop.

Because she loved him, she said, "No."

His smile went out.

"Oh."

"I walk fast."

"I can keep up."

"And I've errands."

"I don't mind."

She took refuge in impatience. "Must a girrul hire a
lawyer to refuse a walk?"

Ted stepped back. "Of course not. I thought—after today—"

"After today ye've work to do."

"The books are balanced."

"Then unbalance them and start over."

He stared at her, wounded, and Birdie nearly surrendered. But
through the window she saw Mrs. Bixby across the street,
pretending to examine spring bonnets while watching the hotel glass.
Beside her stood two young ladies of the smart set.
Ted needed work, respect, and time. Bellwether would forgive him
eventually if he behaved with the tedious perfection it demanded.
But let him walk openly with Birdie Callahan—waitress, immigrant's daughter,
owner of two dresses—and the town would find a fresh
handle for its contempt.

They would say he had sunk. They would say prison
had lowered his tastes. They would turn Birdie's loyalty into
one more stain upon him.

A strong man might have laughed. Ted was not strong
yet.

Birdie knew it. Love sometimes knows when not to collect
its reward.

She buttoned her coat crookedly.

"Listen to me, Ted Terrill. Ye've been lookin' at the
ground so long the worms are gettin' vain. Lift your
head. Go to work tomorrow and the day after. Let
them stare till their eyes ache. Let them whisper till
their tongues wear thin. Ye did wrong once. Ye paid.
Today ye were accused, and ye were innocent—though ye had
less faith in yourself than a blind hen has in
daylight."

He lowered his eyes.

She rapped his chin upward with one hard finger.

"Up, I said."

He obeyed.

"Jo trusts ye. I trust ye. Maybe, one day soon,
ye'll be sensible enough to trust yourself. Until then, don't
go borrowin' trouble from every fool in town. They've none
to spare."

"Birdie—"

"And have Dugan press them trousers again. Vindication is no
excuse for wrinkles."

She turned and marched through the lobby with her panther
stride, past Mrs. Bixby's window, past the yellow hat peering
from the closet, and out into the spring evening.

Ted watched her go.

Then he returned to the office.

He sat at the desk, opened the ledger, and dipped
his pen. Outside, Bellwether talked. Bellwether would always talk. It
praised him now, cautiously, reserving the right to condemn him

But Ted bent over the clean page. After a moment
he straightened his shoulders. After another, he lifted his head.

And six blocks away Birdie Callahan walked home alone, fast
enough to keep her tears from catching her, muttering that
men were fools, towns were worse, and somebody ought to
be shot for inventing embroidered petticoats.

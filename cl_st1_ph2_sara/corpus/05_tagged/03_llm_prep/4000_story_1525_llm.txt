### The Lesser Arts

There is a maxim—usually spoken by persons who have inherited
money—that no labor is burdensome when undertaken for Art. Observe
the capital letter. It is the capital upon which most
young artists are expected to live.

Joe Larrabee believed the maxim. So did Delia Caruthers. They
believed it separately before they met, jointly afterward, and with
diminishing nourishment when the grocer began sending bills ornamented with
red pencil.

Joe came from the Middle West, where he had painted
the Platte River at sunset so successfully that a druggist
displayed the canvas between a pyramid of cough syrup and
an advertisement for liver pills. Local judges predicted a future
for him. A future is the cheapest gift a community
can bestow upon a departing genius.

Delia came from the South, carrying two white blouses, a
chip hat, and the conviction that she would one day
compel concert halls to hush beneath her fingers. At home
she had played the "Moonlight Sonata" until three neighbors developed
insomnia and one declared that Beethoven was a personal enemy.
Nevertheless, a visiting professor said there was fire in her
touch; and so she came to New York to place
the fire under competent European supervision.

They met in a boardinghouse where Art rented the upper
rooms and boiled cabbage occupied the basement.

At dinner Joe spoke of values, masses, atmosphere, and a
picture he intended to paint of a solitary pine against
a winter sky. Delia spoke of phrasing, interpretation, the left
pedal, and her determination to study under Magister Rosenstock, who
charged five dollars an hour for informing pupils that they
had no souls.

Their eyes met across a dish of canned peas.

The peas remained peas, but the occasion became romantic.

Within three months Joe and Delia were married. They took
a small flat on West Twenty-third Street, three flights nearer
heaven than the landlord, and furnished it with two chairs,
a chafing dish, a piano rented by the month, and
Joe's easel. The apartment was so compact that when Delia
played a low note Joe had to move his palette
to make room for it.

But they were happy. Happiness, in a flat, is not
measured by square feet. An unhappy mansion may stretch from
Maine to California and still lack closet space; while love
can make a palace of three rooms, provided nobody tries
to open all the doors at once.

Joe studied with the celebrated Pinkney, whose gray beard had
received more favorable notices than his pictures. Pinkney walked among
the easels, saying "Too brown," "Too cold," and "Where is
your sincerity?" His pupils paid promptly and searched for sincerity
in tubes at the art-supply shops.

Delia went daily to Rosenstock. The Magister had a high
forehead, a low tolerance, and a woollen muffler that seemed
to have been knitted by Wagner. He struck the music
rack with a pencil and cried, "Again! The sorrow is
in the second measure, not in the hallway!"

Delia returned glowing.

"He says my octaves are no longer provincial," she told
Joe.

Joe kissed her.

"Pinkney says my shadows are beginning to think."

Thus encouraged, they spent their little savings upon instruction, rent,
food, brushes, music, carfare, and the thousand humble attendants who
wait upon genius with their hands extended.

Then one Saturday Delia examined the purse.

"Joe," she said, "we have one dollar and seventy-eight cents."

"Good," said Joe. "I always distrust round sums."

"The rent is due Tuesday."

"That is less good. Tuesday has no imagination."

They laughed, because young husbands and wives often laugh at
the wolf while he is still in the next block.
By Monday they could hear his claws on the stairs.

Joe folded his arms before his unfinished winter landscape.

"I shall get work," he announced. "Pinkney can wait. Art
is patient."

"Art may be," said Delia, "but Pinkney's secretary is not.
Besides, you mustn't stop now. You're doing splendidly."

"And you?"

"Oh, I can miss a lesson or two."

"You'll do nothing of the kind."

They argued with great tenderness and no result. Each insisted
that the other possessed genius in wholesale quantities, while personally
admitting to only a small retail stock. At last they
agreed to economize—which is what people call it when there
is nothing left to spend.

The next evening Delia burst into the flat with cold
pink cheeks and triumph under her hat.

"I have a pupil!"

Joe dropped a brush.

"A pupil?"

"A music pupil. Clementina Magister—no relation to Rosenstock, fortunately. Her
father is a widower, enormously rich, with side-whiskers and a
house on Madison Avenue. Clementina is eleven and wears white
dresses with blue sashes. I am to give her three
lessons a week at five dollars each."

"Fifteen dollars!" cried Joe. "Clementina shall have a statue when
I am famous."

"She needs rhythm more than a statue. She counts four-four
time as if each beat owed her money."

Joe's face clouded.

"I don't like your going into strange houses."

"Nonsense. Mr. Magister is kindness itself. He sits in the
library reading financial newspapers and never notices anything below par.
Besides, there is a butler named Ames with the manners
of an archbishop."

It was a well-furnished invention. Delia had hung curtains in
it, placed side-whiskers by the fireplace, and seated Clementina at
a rosewood piano. Love is an excellent decorator.

On Saturday she came home and laid fifteen dollars upon
the blue scalloped dish where they kept their fortune.

"Clementina is improving," she said. "Today she played a scale
without stopping to ask why."

Joe seized her around the waist and revolved her between
the piano and the chafing dish.

"Then I have news too. I sold a sketch."

"You did!"

"To a man from Peoria. He saw it in Blanchard's
window—the little study of the East River with the tugboat.
He paid twelve dollars and wants another."

Delia applauded until the neighbors, who had learned to endure
music but not joy, struck the radiator.

"Who is he?"

"His name is—Bixby. J. Harmon Bixby. He manufactures agricultural machinery
and collects American art. He says European pictures make him
tired because the cows look foreign."

"What will you paint for him?"

"A view of Central Park after rain. He likes wet
trees."

And so Mr. Bixby of Peoria entered their household, shook
hands with Clementina, and took his place among the invisible
benefactors of genius.

During the following weeks prosperity returned in modest envelopes. Delia
brought home her fifteen dollars. Joe produced twelve, sometimes fourteen,
from the discriminating Bixby. The rent was paid; meat appeared
twice a week; and a wedge of cheese took up
permanent residence beneath a glass cover.

Delia left early each morning, though Clementina's lessons were supposedly
in the afternoon. She explained that the child's aunt required
musical selections for charitable receptions and that these must be
copied at the public library.

Joe returned late, smelling faintly of oil.

"Turpentine," he said before Delia asked.

She had not asked.

Delia's hands grew rough. This she attributed to Clementina's stubborn
scales.

"She pounds so dreadfully," Delia said, "that sometimes I have
to guide her wrists."

"Her wrists seem to be hard on your fingers."

"Rich children are exhausting."

Joe's cuffs acquired dark smudges. According to him, Pinkney had
discovered a new black pigment with unusually active habits.

One evening Joe brought home eighteen dollars.

"Bixby bought the rainy park?"

"He did. Said the puddles reminded him of Illinois. He
wants a larger picture now—possibly two cows in a snowstorm."

"That sounds difficult."

"Not at all. The snow saves painting most of the
cows."

Delia laughed, but she looked tired. Joe saw it and
kissed the small line between her brows. Each was guarding
the other from toil by means of toil; and if
the logic appears imperfect, remember that the heart never studied
bookkeeping.

The crisis arrived on a Thursday.

At seven o'clock Joe had supper ready: cheese sandwiches, coffee,
and peas in the blue dish. At half past seven
Delia had not returned. At eight he walked from window
to door until the flat became several miles long.

Then she came.

Her face was pale, and her right hand was wrapped
in white cloth almost to the elbow. The bandage smelled
of oil and medicated cotton.

Joe reached her in one stride.

"What happened?"

Delia tried a smile, but it had insufficient capital behind
it.

"Clementina," she said. "She—she insisted upon having Welsh rabbit after
her lesson. The cook was away, and I tried to
make it. The chafing dish tipped, and the hot cheese—"

"Hot cheese burned through three bandages?"

"It was very determined cheese."

Joe unwrapped his woollen muffler from her shoulders and sat
her beside the table.

"Tell me the truth, Dee."

"I am telling—"

"Your hand smells of machine oil. And the cotton came
from a factory first-aid box."

For a moment she looked at him. Then her brave
little fiction, complete with mansion, butler, side-whiskers, blue sash, and
rosewood piano, collapsed without even saving the curtains.

"There is no Clementina," she whispered.

"I know."

"And no rich Mr. Magister."

"I suspected him. Side-whiskers are often unreliable."

She bowed her head.

"I tried to find pupils, Joe. Truly I did. I
walked all over the city. But nobody wanted lessons from
me. Then I saw a sign at the Eureka Hand
Laundry. They needed pressers. I've been ironing shirts there. It
pays fifteen dollars a week."

Joe held her bandaged hand as gently as though it
were a wounded bird.

"Today," she continued, "a sleeve caught under the press. I
pulled it free, and the hot iron slipped. One of
the men from the engine room ran for oil and
cotton and sent them upstairs. I thought I could still
make you believe—oh, Joe, I wanted you to keep studying!
I didn't mind the work. When one loves one's Art,
no service seems—"

She stopped.

Joe was smiling, but his eyes were wet.

"What?" she demanded. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because," he said, "I sent up that oil and cotton."

Delia stared.

"The foreman called down that a girl had burned her
hand. I carried the first-aid tin to the lift."

"You? At the Eureka?"

"In the engine room. I tend the furnace and help
with the machinery."

"But Pinkney—"

"I left Pinkney three weeks ago."

"And the pictures?"

"They are still facing the wall, where criticism cannot reach
them."

"The man from Peoria?"

"Dee, there may be a man in Peoria named Bixby
who loves wet trees and foreign-looking cows. A large nation
permits almost anything. But I have never met him."

She looked at the eighteen dollars on the dish.

"My wages," said Joe. "With two dollars overtime. The laundry
had a rush order from a hotel."

Delia began to laugh. Joe joined her. They laughed at
Clementina's scales, at Bixby's cows, at the charitable aunt and
Pinkney's pigment, at Welsh rabbit with the temperature of molten
steel. Presently the laughter changed its key, as music sometimes
does, and Delia hid her face against Joe's coat.

"You gave up painting for me," she said.

"You gave up music for me."

"We are two magnificent liars."

"Only in the minor details."

Outside, the city rattled its elevated trains, delivered shirts, bought
pictures, missed notes, burned coal, and pursued Art by every
available street. Inside the tiny flat, Joe cut the cheese
sandwiches while Delia, one-handed, poured the coffee. Their careers had
suffered an intermission; love had taken the program and written
its own selection across it.

You will remember the maxim with which we began: no
service is hard when one loves one's Art.

That is nearly right.

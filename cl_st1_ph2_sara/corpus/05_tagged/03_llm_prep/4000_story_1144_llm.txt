## THE CLEAN SIDE OF MIDNIGHT

There are certain ceremonies which civilized people perform on the
last night of December with a rigidity that would have
astonished the Druids.

At twelve o'clock, for example, a civilized person must put
a paper cap upon his head, although at eleven-fifty he
would have dismissed such a cap as unbecoming. He must
blow a tin horn into the ear of somebody who
has never harmed him. He must drink several liquids which,
separately, would be considered sufficient, but which, taken together, are
called festivity. At twelve-five he embraces his friends. At one-thirty
he quarrels with them. At three he discovers that the
waiter is a thief. At five he gives the thief
ten dollars and asks him confidentially whether life is worth
living.

These rites were being observed with unusual purity in the
Pink Fountain Room of the Hotel Magnifique, New York, on
the first morning of the year.

The Pink Fountain Room contained three fountains, all pink; one
hundred and eighty-four tables, all occupied; nine hundred paper streamers,
all entangled; two orchestras, both playing; and enough electric roses
to illuminate a small but ambitious republic. Satin balloons swayed
against violet draperies. Champagne corks flew among the chandeliers like
frightened quail. Gentlemen whose grandfathers had sold shoes were mistaken
for hereditary princes, while ladies who typed correspondence at thirty-five
dollars a week became, beneath rose-colored lamps, mysterious women with
histories.

It was a wonderful room.

That is, it was wonderful if you remained in it.

Beyond the swinging doors lay the kitchen, where wonder was
manufactured wholesale.

There the air was white with steam and sharp with
roasting meat, browned butter, onions, coffee, singed paper, hot metal,
and the private despair of cooks. Copper kettles shone like
red moons. Carving knives winked. Dish covers clashed. French fried
potatoes traveled in battalions. Men in white caps shouted numbers,
warnings, insults, and occasionally prayers. A lobster Newburg flamed blue
at one range while, six feet away, a pastry cook
placed six pale orchids of frosting upon six chocolate cakes
with the calm of a man decorating tombs.

Between this kingdom of heat and the Pink Fountain Room
sat Gussie Fink.

Gussie was the night kitchen checker. Every platter, plate, pot,
bowl, bottle, and silver dish that went through the service
door passed beneath her eye and under her stamp.

"Two sirloins, medium, table sixty-two."

Thump!

"Breast of guinea hen, table nineteen."

Thump!

"Six oysters, Rockefeller; one without spinach—don't ask me why."

Thump!

"Three double coffees, four mineral waters, one lemon ice, table
one-oh-seven."

Thump!

It was not a romantic occupation, unless one considers accuracy
romantic, as one should. Gussie sat behind a narrow desk
raised on a platform, with the waiters surging below her
and the cooks raging behind her. She wore a white
shirtwaist as stiff and clean as January snow before New
York has had time to improve it. Her dark hair
lay smooth over her ears. A little bunch of artificial
daisies was pinned at her collar, and the daisies, though
purchased for nineteen cents, had more freshness in them than
all the orchids in the Pink Fountain Room.

She was twenty-four, trim, clear-eyed, and tonight thoroughly miserable.

This last fact was not visible except to anyone who
loved her, or disliked her, or worked within ten feet
of her. In a hotel kitchen those categories include practically
everybody.

"Look alive, Gus," said Tony Malatesta, sliding a tray under
her nose. "Maybe you're dreamin' of your beau."

"Maybe I'm dreamin' you paid for them two extra portions
of pâté you slipped on here," said Gussie.

Tony withdrew the tray by one inch. Tony had long
fingers, a small black mustache, and a belief that hotel
property belonged to the man clever enough to carry it
away.

"Extra? Where?"

"Under the napkin."

Tony lifted the napkin. Two silver dishes appeared with the
guilty innocence of children discovered behind a curtain.

"Why, look at that," he said. "How'd they get there?"

"They followed you. You got such a winning way."

"Charge 'em to table forty-four."

"Table forty-four paid and left."

"Then charge 'em to table forty-five."

"Table forty-five is the ladies' cloakroom."

Tony leaned close.

"You act mighty sore tonight, Gussie."

"I'm overcome by your honesty."

"Maybe it ain't me you're sore at."

He turned his narrow head toward the door through which
waiters vanished into the glittering room.

At that moment Henri Finkelstein came through it carrying an
empty champagne cooler in one hand and a tray of
glasses in the other.

Six weeks earlier he had been Heiny.

Heiny had worked at the cold-meat station, where he sliced
tongue, arranged parsley, and possessed a laugh of such size
that it caused the aspic to tremble. He had eaten
his suppers with Gussie on an overturned flour tub. On
quiet nights he had walked her home to East Eleventh
Street, though this took him six blocks out of his
way and made him miss the earlier elevated train. He
had called her Gus, she had called him Heiny, and
on Sundays they sometimes went to Joey's for coffee and
ham on rye.

Then Mr. Brindle, the assistant steward, noticed that Heiny was
tall, quick, clean-shaven, and able to balance four plates on
one arm. The management placed him in a boiled shirt,
buttoned him into a black coat, gave him a napkin,
and sent him among the rich.

The transformation was complete.

Cinderella had required a fairy godmother, six mice, and a
pumpkin. The Magnifique did it with a collar and two
brass buttons.

Heiny became Henri.

He did not announce the change. It appeared on his
time card in his own careful handwriting. Soon afterward he
began saying "Very good, sir" in a voice that suggested
his ancestors had been saying it to dukes since the
Crusades. He acquired the waiter's forward inclination of the body,
a movement combining humility, secrecy, and the expectation of money.
He could remove a fish bone without disturbing a conversation
and present a wine label as though revealing the face
of a royal infant.

He also stopped eating on flour tubs.

"Table fifty-three," said Henri now, setting down the cooler. "One
cold pheasant, two salads, four black coffees."

His manner toward Gussie was correct.

Correctness, between sweethearts, is often a knife with a polished
handle.

Gussie checked the dishes.

"Table fifty-three's had enough to eat for a week."

"They ordered it."

"I didn't say they didn't."

"Then what did you say?"

"What you heard."

Henri looked at her. His eyes were tired, but his
hair remained smooth and his collar remained magnificent.

"Stamp the check, please."

"Certainly, Henri."

She gave the name the little French twist he had
lately put upon it.

Thump!

He picked up his tray.

Tony, who had been lingering with the patience of a
cat at a canary convention, said, "That swell party still
handin' you ten-spots, Hen-ree?"

Henri's face altered. Not much. A waiter at the Magnifique
could receive news of earthquake, murder, or marital disaster without
dropping the potatoes.

"They're generous," he said.

"Generous!" Tony whistled. "I seen the red-headed feller give you
twenty just for bringin' more ice."

"Attend to your own tables."

"And the blond doll," Tony continued, "she give you anything?"

Henri lifted the tray.

"Go fry your ears."

He passed through the swinging doors.

Tony looked at Gussie with tender concern.

"Touchy, ain't he?"

Gussie stamped the next check so hard that a spoon
jumped.

Thump!

Miss Sweeney, who kept the linen count and whose thin
face had been designed by nature for peering around corners,
came gliding up behind her.

"That's her party," she said.

"Whose?"

"The blond one."

"What blond one?"

"Oh, you know. The beauty in gold. Table fifty-three. Hair
down to here, diamonds down to there. You ought to
see the way Henri attends to her."

"I got dishes to see."

"Sure," said Miss Sweeney. "Only I thought you and him
was keeping company."

"So did I."

Miss Sweeney smiled. There are people who collect stamps, and
there are people who collect wounds. Miss Sweeney preferred them
fresh.

"I guess a man changes when he gets promoted. He
sees a different class."

"He still takes off his shoes the same as anybody."

"Maybe. But this dame at fifty-three, believe me! Skin like
peaches. Gold dress cut right down the back. Every time
she crooks one finger, your Henri comes runnin'."

"He ain't my Henri."

"No?"

"No."

This was news to Gussie, though she had said it.

She bent over her sheet. The inked columns blurred for
a second, then steadied. A kitchen checker cannot permit the
collapse of romance to interfere with the movement of grilled
mushrooms. Civilization depends upon these distinctions.

"Four club sandwiches, table eleven," called a waiter.

Thump!

"Two Welsh rabbits."

Thump!

"Caviar and toast."

Thump!

The year grew older.

In the Pink Fountain Room the two orchestras fought for
possession of "Auld Lang Syne," while eight hundred people who
did not know the words sang them with conviction. At
table fifty-three sat the party upon which half the kitchen
had built a mythology.

There were four men and one woman. The men possessed
the broad pink faces of persons who had eaten heavily
all their lives and intended to continue. One wore a
scarlet carnation, one a pearl waistcoat button, one a black
ribbon across his shirt front, and one a paper crown
upon which somebody had written KING OF THE WALRUSES.

The woman sat among them like a lamp in a
butcher's window.

From the service door she appeared beautiful. Her gown was
gold and fastened over one shoulder with a jeweled clasp.
Her hair, the color of pale honey beneath the rose
lights, curled low against her cheeks. Her white arms flashed
whenever she raised her glass, which was often. Men at
other tables turned to look at her. Women looked too,
but with technical objections.

She had arrived at eleven as Miss Celeste Van Arden.

Privately, and entre nous, she was Sally Varden of West
Forty-sixth Street, formerly of Scranton, Pennsylvania, and currently employed in
the second row of the Midnight Frolic. But if a
hotel cannot turn Sally into Celeste on New Year's Eve,
what is the use of fountains?

Henri served her party.

He uncorked their champagne, changed their plates, fetched mint leaves,
found cigarettes, produced a cushion, warmed a glass, chilled another,
and once crossed the entire hotel in search of a
particular brand of mustard which the King of the Walruses
declared necessary to his happiness.

Each favor brought money.

A five folded under a saucer.

A ten pressed into the palm.

Two dollars beneath a plate.

A bill tucked into the breast pocket with a slap.

Henri's pocket became warm, thick, and important. It spoke to
him. Money often speaks, especially when it is newly acquired.
It said that the dining room was life and the
kitchen merely preparation; that men with pearl buttons were superior
beings; that women in gold gowns lived upon perfume and
laughter; that Gussie's white shirtwaist was plain; that ham on
rye was a thing one outgrew.

The money did not mention that the man with the
carnation owned three pawnshops, that the pearl-buttoned gentleman had been
indicted twice, or that the King of the Walruses sold
imitation cough medicine containing enough alcohol to reconcile the consumer
to any cough whatever.

Money has tact.

Back in the kitchen Gussie worked.

One o'clock became two. Two became three. The dining room
sent back its remains: half-eaten cutlets, collapsed soufflés, cigarette ends
floating in coffee cups, napkins stained with rouge, and expensive
grapes squeezed flat for amusement. The dishwashers fed the wreckage
into steaming vats. The busboys cursed. The cooks sweated through
their jackets. A fat chef tasted sauce from a spoon,
shouted that it lacked salt, tasted it again, and shouted
that it contained too much.

Gussie stamped.

Thump!

The sound was steady, official, almost defiant.

At half past three Joey, the oldest busboy, brought her
a cup of coffee.

"Drink," he said. "You look green around the gills."

"I look fine."

"Then drink because I look green around the gills."

The coffee had boiled down until it could have removed
varnish, but it was hot. Gussie held the cup between
her hands.

Through the open bar door came another world of smells:
crushed mint, lemon skin, orange bitters, rye whisky, wet wool,
cloves, tobacco, and the sweet medicinal breath of crème de
menthe. Bartenders moved among ranks of bottles like organists at
a bright glass instrument. Their sleeves were rolled, their faces
solemn. One mixed a drink of seven ingredients for a
gentleman who swallowed it without noticing and asked whether it
was beer.

Miss Sweeney returned.

"He danced with her."

"Who danced with who?"

"Henri. With the gold dame."

"A waiter don't dance."

"He did. Just a couple turns while the orchestra played."

Gussie put down her cup.

"Maybe she was faint."

"She didn't look faint. Had both arms around his neck."

Now, Miss Sweeney had seen Henri guide the woman between
two chairs because she had stumbled, but truth, when it
wears sensible shoes, cannot keep pace with gossip.

Gussie said, "He can dance with the Statue of Liberty
if he wants."

"She's more undressed."

"Go count a sheet."

"I only tell you because I'm your friend."

"Then make me an enemy for five minutes and give
me a rest."

Miss Sweeney went away, wounded but nourished.

Gussie stared at the checking sheet. Two dark spots appeared
beside table seventy-six. She touched one with her finger, which
was foolish, for it spread.

"Steam," she said aloud.

No one had accused her of tears, but Gussie believed
in being prepared.

At four, business slackened. The cooks began extinguishing burners. Pots
were scraped. Roasts were covered and wheeled away. The Pink
Fountain Room still roared, but now there were gaps in
the roar, queer little caverns of silence into which a
single laugh or broken glass fell sharply.

Henri came through with his face flushed.

"Six coffees, table fifty-three."

"You'll float them people away," said Gussie.

"They want coffee."

"Maybe what they want ain't what they need."

"They're customers."

"Then customers ain't always right."

He waited while she checked the cups.

"You been saying things about me?" he asked.

Gussie looked up.

"To who?"

"Tony says you been saying I'm stuck-up."

"Tony says a lot. Mostly when his mouth is open."

"Did you?"

"If your collar pinches, don't blame me."

"My collar's all right."

"It certainly is. You ought to put it in a
frame."

Henri lowered his voice.

"Listen, Gus—"

"Miss Fink."

"All right. Miss Fink. You've been sore ever since I
went on the floor."

"I ain't sore."

"You are."

"I ain't got time."

"You think because I'm trying to get ahead—"

"I think your coffee's getting cold."

His mouth tightened.

"You never understood."

"That's right. I'm dumb. I only check the food."

"Fine."

"Elegant."

He carried the tray away.

Gussie smiled at Tony, who was watching.

"If you got anything to say, swallow it with a
spoon."

Tony swallowed.

By five o'clock the Pink Fountain Room had begun to
reveal the truth.

Electricity is a loyal accomplice at midnight, but dawn is
a detective.

The rose lamps still glowed, yet a thin grayness had
entered above the curtains. Under that honest light the violet
draperies looked dusty, the electric roses looked wired, and the
fountains looked like three painted basins with plumbing trouble. Paper
hats sagged over damp foreheads. Satin slippers pinched. Shirt fronts
bulged. Rouge stood in patches. The air, once rich with
perfume and champagne, had thickened into a warm, stale mixture
of smoke, spilled wine, wilted flowers, and human fatigue.

A man asleep at table fourteen beat time against his
plate with a rye roll.

Two ladies in silver gowns quarreled over a fur wrap
which belonged to neither of them.

A youth with one eyebrow recited a poem to an
ice bucket.

At table fifty-three, Miss Celeste Van Arden had ceased to
be a lamp.

Her hair was coming loose. One jeweled comb hung over
her ear. A dark stain spread across the gold skirt.
Her mascara had traveled in two fine shadows beneath her
eyes, and the lovely white arms were blotched crimson where
the men had gripped them in play.

"More wine!" shouted the King of the Walruses.

Henri looked at the woman. Her chin had sunk to
her breast.

"I think the lady's had enough, sir."

The king stared at him.

"You paid to think?"

"No, sir."

"Then bring wine."

Henri brought a bottle, but he set it behind the
cooler and poured mineral water into the woman's glass.

She drank without noticing.

"You're a nice boy," she said. Her voice was no
longer velvet. It was a small, cracked thing. "What's your
name?"

"Henri."

"No, it isn't."

The words struck him oddly.

She lifted her head and tried to focus on him.

"Nobody's named Henri. Not really."

The man with the carnation laughed.

"She's got you there, waiter."

Henri's ears reddened.

The woman leaned toward him.

"You got a girl?"

"That'll do, Sally," said Pearl Button.

"I asked him if he's got a girl."

Henri looked past her, toward the service door. Through its
round pane he could see one white sleeve moving at
the checker's desk.

"Yes," he said.

The woman smiled, then the smile slid away.

"Don't lose her."

She pushed back her chair.

"I'm going home."

The men objected, not because they cared whether she went
home but because they had not finished being amused by
her. Pearl Button caught her wrist. She laughed too loudly,
pulled free, and stood.

For one second she stood very straight, gold from shoulder
to ankle, diamonds flashing, pale hair bright under the artificial
roses. The whole room seemed to turn toward her. She
was beautiful again—beautiful in the grand, tragic, expensive manner.

Then her knees folded.

She struck the table, swept two glasses and an ashtray
to the carpet, and went down among the streamers.

There was a little silence.

It lasted perhaps three seconds. In a public dining room
this is eternity.

Henri knelt beside her. The four men remained in their
chairs.

"Get her up," said the King of the Walruses.

"She needs a doctor."

"She needs air."

"She'll be all right," said Carnation. "Sally does this."

A woman at the next table stood upon a chair
for a better view.

The headwaiter arrived. He was a short, silver-haired man named
Mr. Delacour, born Dennis Clancy in Jersey City. He surveyed
the fallen woman, the staring guests, and the broken glasses.

"Take her through the service corridor," he said softly. "No
scene."

There had already been a scene, of course, but hotel
management recognizes only scenes seen by the wrong people.

Henri and a busboy lifted the woman. Her head fell
backward. Without animation, the famous golden hair proved coarse at
the roots; without laughter, the painted mouth looked bruised. One
gold slipper had come off. Her stocking was torn at
the heel.

"Where's her wrap?" Henri asked.

The men looked at one another.

"Check room, probably."

"Which one of you is going with her?"

Pearl Button consulted his watch.

"I've got an engagement downtown."

"At five in the morning?"

"Business."

Carnation was already putting on his coat.

"Give her to a cab driver. She knows her address."

The King of the Walruses removed his paper crown and
became suddenly old.

"Here." He put a bill on the table. "Take care
of it."

Of it.

Not her.

Henri looked at the money. An hour earlier a bill
like that had seemed a passport to a higher country.
Now it looked like dirty green paper that had passed
through too many damp hands.

The four men went out by the main entrance, each
discovering elsewhere an urgent duty.

Henri and the busboy carried the woman through the swinging
doors.

The kitchen had quieted. Burners ticked as they cooled. Pans
stood stacked. A tired cook drank milk from a measuring
jug. The workers turned to watch the golden figure pass
among them.

Gussie rose from her desk.

"What happened?"

"Fainted," said the busboy.

"She's drunk," said Tony, with the satisfaction of a diagnostician.

The woman moaned. One arm hung loose, the jeweled bracelet
sliding down toward her fingers. Her face, seen close, was
not beautiful. It was young and ravaged and frightened, which
is another thing entirely.

"Lay her on the bench," said Gussie. "I'll get cold
water."

She hurried around the desk.

Henri shifted his grip.

"No, Gus."

"What?"

"Don't."

"She needs help."

"I know. But don't you touch her."

Gussie stopped.

Her face went white beneath its healthy color.

"Oh," she said. "Too fine for me, is she?"

"No!"

"You afraid I'll smudge the gold?"

"Gussie, listen."

"I heard."

She turned away.

Henri transferred the woman to the bench with the busboy's
help, then caught Gussie by the elbow. It was the
first time he had touched her in weeks.

"I didn't mean she was too fine."

"Take your hand off."

"I meant you're too—"

"Too what?"

He searched for the word. Henri, who could name twelve
French sauces and six German wines, could not find it.
Heiny could.

"Clean," he said.

Gussie stared.

He spoke quickly now, low and plain.

"I don't mean your dress. I mean all of you.
You shouldn't have to handle this. Them fellows got her
like this and walked out. They was laughing at her
an hour ago, shoving money around like they owned the
earth. Now they wouldn't carry her ten feet."

Gussie's expression softened a little, but only a little. She
was not a woman to surrender a good grievance at
the first trumpet.

"She still needs somebody."

"I'll take care of her. Mrs. Bannon from the coatroom's
coming. Mr. Clancy—I mean Delacour—sent for the house doctor."

The fallen woman opened her eyes.

"Don't let them take my purse," she whispered.

Gussie went to her despite Henri and tucked the purse
beneath the limp hand.

"Nobody's taking it," she said. "You rest."

The woman's fingers closed around Gussie's wrist.

"You pretty thing," she murmured.

Then she was unconscious again.

Gussie freed her hand gently.

Henri looked at the two women: one in stained gold,
jewels, paint, and somebody else's idea of splendor; the other
in starched white, with dark hair smooth and daisies at
her throat. He had looked at both before. He had
not seen either.

This frequently happens to the human eye.

The house doctor came, accompanied by Mrs. Bannon, a broad,
capable woman whose bosom could have supported municipal debt. Between
them they took charge of Sally Varden. A blanket was
found. Her gold dress disappeared under gray wool.

"She'll live," said the doctor after listening to her heart.
"Get her upstairs. Quietly."

"Her friends left," said Henri.

"Friends," said Mrs. Bannon.

She put into the one word a complete social philosophy.

When they had carried the woman away, Henri remained beside
the bench. On the floor lay her missing slipper, a
crushed paper rose, and a fifty-dollar bill dropped by one
of the men.

Tony saw the bill.

Henri saw Tony see it.

Their eyes met.

Tony's long fingers stirred at his sides.

Henri picked up the bill and gave it to Mr.
Delacour.

"For the lady's account."

Tony sighed. It had not been a profitable dawn.

Gussie returned to her desk and straightened the stacks of
checks. There was almost nothing left to check, but she
aligned them with great care.

Henri came and stood below the platform.

"Gus."

She continued arranging paper.

"Gussie."

"What?"

"I been a sap."

"That ain't exactly a New Year's revelation."

"I mean it."

"So do I."

He rested both hands on the edge of her desk.

"When they put me in this coat, I figured I
was somebody different. I seen all them people throwing ten-dollar
bills, ordering things they didn't eat, knowing the names of
wines. I thought maybe that was being classy."

"And now?"

"Now I know half of them don't know no more
than me. Less, some ways. A man asked me tonight
if guinea hen was fish."

Gussie's mouth twitched.

"He was probably educated."

"College pin and everything."

"That proves it."

Henri smiled, but the smile faded.

"I got swell-headed. I didn't come around because I thought
you'd make fun of me."

"I would."

"I know."

"You said 'very good, sir' to the pickle man Tuesday."

"I got confused."

"You bowed to Mrs. Schultz's mop."

"I was tired."

"You made Joey call you Henri."

Henri winced.

"That was bad."

"That was terrible."

"I'm sorry."

Gussie looked down at him. Behind the boiled shirt and
black coat, behind the smooth hair and professional bend, Heiny
was coming back—not all at once, but in embarrassed pieces.

"What about the blond doll?" she asked.

"What about her?"

"Miss Sweeney says you danced with her."

"She fell against me."

"Both arms?"

"She was falling hard."

"And she gave you money?"

"She offered. I wouldn't take it."

This was not entirely true. He had accepted money from
the party all night, though not from the woman herself.
He pulled the roll from his pocket and looked at
it with disgust.

"I took plenty from the others."

"You earned your tips."

"Some. Maybe. But I liked them too much."

"There ain't any law against liking money."

"No. Only I liked who it made me think I
was."

Gussie considered this. It was a respectable confession. Not flawless,
but respectable.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Heiny Finkelstein."

"Never heard of him."

"He used to take a girl to Joey's."

"Cheap fellow."

"He bought ham."

"On rye?"

"With mustard."

"Regular spender."

"He used to walk her home."

"Whatever happened to him?"

"He got promoted and lost his mind."

"Sad."

"He thinks maybe it's coming back."

A cook dropped a pot at the far end of
the kitchen.

Clang!

Both of them jumped, and then, without permission, laughed.

It was not a large laugh. It did not shake
aspic. But it crossed six difficult weeks.

Henri reached up and touched one of the daisies at
her collar.

"You kept these."

"They was paid for."

"I gave them to you."

"You contributed a dime."

"I picked them out."

"You picked the cheapest bunch."

"They looked like you."

"Nineteen-cent flowers?"

"Clean," he said. "Simple. Nice."

"Careful. You're ruining it."

He lowered his hand.

"Can I walk you home?"

Gussie glanced toward the clock. It was twenty minutes past
six. Outside, morning had entered the city without music, corks,
or permission.

"I ain't had breakfast."

"Joey's?"

"They may be closed."

"Joey never closes. He says locks make him nervous."

"Coffee?"

"Two cups."

"Ham on rye?"

"With mustard."

She stepped down from the platform.

"All right, Heiny."

At the name, his face changed. Nothing dramatic occurred. No
fountain sprang to life, no orchestra played, and no wealthy
gentleman distributed currency. He merely breathed out, as a man
does when a tight collar has at last been opened.

They collected their coats and went through the dining room.

The Pink Fountain Room was now nearly empty. It resembled
a battlefield fought with pastry. Broken glasses glittered beneath chairs.
Streamers drooped in spilled champagne. Cigarette smoke lay under the
ceiling. A silver shoe stood beside a dish of melted
ice cream. One musician slept over his trombone. Another counted
his pay.

A few surviving celebrants moved toward the doors with the
grave uncertainty of explorers crossing thin ice.

At table fourteen the fleshy gentleman still slept, his fingers
curled around the rye roll.

Gussie looked at the fountains.

"They ain't so pink in daylight."

"They need the lights," said Heiny.

"Lots of things do."

Near the entrance, Tillie Horan was scrubbing the floor.

Tillie worked from midnight until eight and appeared to have
been assembled from materials left after the construction of sturdier
persons. Her back was bent, her gray hair hung in
wet strings, and three of her front teeth had resigned
from service. Her shoes were sodden. Her red hands gripped
the mop handle while dirty water sloshed around her ankles.

All night the celebrated and the beautiful had stepped over
Tillie without seeing her. This was convenient, because Tillie had
seen them.

She had wiped up their wine, retrieved their bracelets, listened
to their quarrels, buttoned the backs of dresses, found missing
husbands, and once held a sobbing bank president's head over
a porcelain basin. She knew society from the floor upward.

As Gussie and Heiny approached, Tillie paused to straighten her
back.

"Happy New Year, kids," she said.

Heiny looked at her.

After the painted woman, the abandoned woman, the men who
left, the paper roses and false names and banknotes lying
on the floor, Tillie's worn face seemed to him restful.
Not pretty. Let us not become hysterical. Tillie was as
pretty as a boot after a week in the rain.
But she was solid, decent, and awake. She was a
gray woolen blanket after a fever dream.

"Happy New Year, Tillie," he said warmly.

He reached into his pocket, peeled off a five-dollar bill,
and held it out.

Tillie stared.

"What's that?"

"For you."

"What'd I do?"

"You worked."

"So did you."

"Take it."

She took the bill, examined both sides, and tucked it
carefully into the top of her stocking.

"Sure you ain't drunk?"

"I'm sobering up."

Tillie laughed, showing the vacancies among her teeth.

"You treat that girl right," she said.

"I'm going to."

Gussie put on her gloves.

"He better."

Tillie dipped her mop into the bucket.

"Men," she observed, "get sense late. Some after midnight. Some
after marriage. Some never. Happy New Year."

They stepped through the revolving door.

The city was pale, cold, and magnificently unconcerned. Snow left
from the previous week had turned gray along the curb.
Milk wagons rattled over the avenue. A street cleaner pushed
soggy horns, programs, and colored paper into a heap. High
above the roofs, the sky had the clear, hard blue
of enamel.

Gussie drew a breath.

The cold air tasted clean.

Heiny took her arm—not in the polished manner of Henri
guiding a customer to table fifty-three, but awkwardly, firmly, as
himself.

"You mad at me?" he asked.

"Some."

"How long?"

"I'll tell you after coffee."

"And the sandwich?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"Whether you call yourself Henri at Joey's."

Heiny glanced back at the Hotel Magnifique. Behind its windows,
electric roses still burned faintly in the daylight, pink and
tired and unnecessary.

"Henri," he said, "is off duty."

They walked east together toward Joey's, where the coffee was
strong, the mustard was yellow, the ham was honest, and
no one had ever thought of installing a fountain.

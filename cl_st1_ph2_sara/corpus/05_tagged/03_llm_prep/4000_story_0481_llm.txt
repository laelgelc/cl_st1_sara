At three o'clock on the afternoon of the championship game,
Mr. Charles Kirk sat alone in the Greek room at
Willard's School and contemplated a letter of resignation.

Outside, beyond the half-open door and the long corridor, the
school was making as much noise as two hundred and
forty boys, assisted by cow-bells, tin horns, and loyal young
throats, could properly make. From the playground came a great
burst of cheering, followed by the sharp, scattered hand-clapping which
meant that Willard's nine had brought off something creditable against
St. Matthew's. The sunlight lay golden across the empty desks.
Through the raised windows entered a mild June wind, fragrant
with clover and warm dust.

Mr. Kirk heard the cheering and frowned.

"They are quite capable of winning without me," he said.

This was true, for he had never played on the
Willard's nine. It was also unreasonable, because no one had
suggested that the success of the nine depended upon him.
But a man who has written his resignation may be
pardoned for regarding all manifestations of happiness as slightly personal
insults.

The letter lay under his hand, enclosed, stamped, and addressed
to Dr. Willard. It was a brief document, but it
represented five months of disappointment, three faculty conferences, two interviews
with the head master, one threatened rebellion, and an almost
continuous ache beneath Mr. Kirk's waistcoat.

To the boys he was "Curly." They had given him
the name during his first week, partly because his brown
hair curled obstinately over his forehead, and partly because Charles
Kirk was too dignified a name for a master who
looked only a year or two older than the sixth
form. He was, in fact, twenty-four, a graduate of a
respectable college, an excellent Greek scholar, and a competent instructor
in German. He possessed a mild disposition, a pleasant voice,
and no more natural inclination toward tyranny than a canary.

Unfortunately, he had begun his career at Willard's by attempting
to become an iron-heeled despot.

The beginning had been simple. Willard's had a tradition concerning
new masters. On the first evening of a new instructor's
residence, certain bold spirits visited his room in disguise, removed
such articles as could be carried conveniently, and left in
exchange a large placard bearing the words: "WELCOME TO THE
FACULTY." Sensible masters permitted the ceremony to run its harmless
course. Very sensible masters provided cake. One historic master had
joined the intruders and helped them lower his own mattress
from a second-story window.

Curly knew nothing of this. Finding at eleven o'clock that
six masked persons were departing with his chairs, his boots,
and a framed photograph of the Parthenon, he pursued them
down the corridor. Being young, active, and thoroughly indignant, he
caught two.

The captured marauders were James Turner, captain of the baseball
nine, and Philip Larkin, president of the sixth form. Curly
reported them. The faculty, startled by such zeal and unable
to retreat without surrendering its dignity, placed both boys on
strict probation for a month and deprived them of several
cherished privileges.

The school decided at once that Curly Kirk was a
tyrant.

The faculty, though it had punished the culprits, decided more
slowly that he lacked judgment. Large bodies move slowly, particularly
when composed of educators; but once they have formed an
opinion, they possess considerable momentum.

Curly might yet have recovered. Instead, wounded by the boys'
resentment and embarrassed by the faculty's coolness, he increased his
severity. Whispering became insubordination. An ink pellet became an assault
upon constituted authority. Four boys who arrived late for German
spent a Saturday afternoon writing irregular verbs; three of them
had been delayed by carrying an injured schoolmate to the
infirmary. The explanation reached Curly only after punishment had been
imposed, and pride prevented him from withdrawing it.

His classroom became orderly, silent, and hateful.

The boys obeyed him, but they obeyed as prisoners obey
a warder. Jokes ceased when he approached. Groups divided to
let him pass. At table, conversation on either side of
him developed an extraordinary interest in salt-cellars and bread. Even
the younger masters, who might have aided him, found his
stiffness uninviting.

By May, Curly's failure was a recognized institutional fact.

Then fate threw him a sop and concealed a hook
in it. Four members of the nine were discovered returning
after hours from the village. Curly, who was master on
duty, intercepted them. They were not vicious boys; they had
gone for strawberries and remained for a moving-picture show. But
rules were rules, and Curly, by this time, had come
to regard mercy as a form of cowardice. He reported
all four.

The faculty placed them on probation. Had Dr. Willard not
intervened, they would have been forbidden to play in the
championship game.

The school never learned that Curly had spoken in their
favor at the second faculty meeting. It knew only that
he had reported them at the first. For three days
his name was uttered with a bitterness usually reserved for
traitors and unsuccessful umpires.

That had completed the matter. He was not fitted to
teach boys. Dr. Willard had not said so, but the
head master's grave kindness had been worse than censure. Curly
would resign before he was dismissed. There were other schools,
other occupations, perhaps a fellowship in Germany. Anything was preferable
to remaining where two hundred boys disliked him and fifteen
colleagues regarded him as an educational accident.

Another cheer rolled in from the field.

Curly drew the letter toward him.

At that instant somebody came running along the corridor. The
steps stopped. There was a knock, and James Turner entered
without waiting for permission.

He wore white baseball trousers, striped blue stockings, and a
jersey with a large W on the breast. His face
was crimson beneath its tan, and perspiration had pasted his
hair to his forehead. The sight of him brought back
unpleasantly the affair of the stolen furniture.

"What is it, Turner?" asked Curly.

The boy hesitated. He did not like Mr. Kirk, and
the fact was evident even through his excitement.

"It's Barlow, sir. He's done."

"Done?"

"Our pitcher, sir. His arm's gone back on him. St.
Matthew's has knocked him out of the box. They've made
four runs this inning, and there's only one out."

"I am sorry," said Curly. "But what can I do?"

Turner looked at him.

"Mr. Harris says you pitched for Ellsworth College."

Curly's fingers closed over the resignation.

Mr. Harris taught mathematics at St. Matthew's and coached its
nine. Eight years earlier—an age, from Turner's point of view—he
had been a substitute catcher at Ellsworth while Curly Kirk,
then a slim sophomore, had pitched the college through a
remarkable season.

"That was some time ago," said Curly.

"He says you were the best college pitcher he ever
caught, sir."

"He is generous."

"We haven't anybody else." Turner swallowed. Asking favors of a
tyrant is one of the severer trials of youth. "Mason
has a bad thumb, and Little can't hold them. It's
the seventh inning, sir. The score is six to five
against us."

Curly glanced toward the window. Beyond the elms he could
see the edge of the field, crowded carriages along the
road, bright parasols, and blue-and-white banners stirring in the sun.
A fresh roar sounded, but this time it came from
the St. Matthew's partisans.

Why should he help them? They had jeered at him,
avoided him, and interpreted his every act as malice. His
resignation was written. In two weeks he would leave Willard's
forever.

Yet Turner stood before him, anxious, exhausted, and proud enough
to suffer humiliation for the sake of the school.

Curly rose.

"I have no uniform."

"We have Johnson's, sir. He went home ill. It will
fit closely enough."

"I have not thrown a ball in three years."

"Mr. Harris says you won't have forgotten."

Mr. Harris, Curly reflected, was taking a great deal upon
himself.

"Very well," he said.

Turner's face altered, though it did not quite become friendly.

"Thank you, sir!"

Curly placed the resignation in the inner pocket of his
coat and followed him.

Five minutes later a strange figure emerged from the gymnasium.
Johnson was broad and short; Curly was narrow and of
medium height. The borrowed trousers ended well above his ankles,
while the jersey hung in folds around his body. A
blue cap, too small for him, perched insecurely upon his
curls. The boys near the players' bench stared. One laughed
and was instantly silenced by Turner.

"This is Mr. Kirk," said the captain. "He's going in
the box."

The announcement traveled through the crowd in ripples.

"Curly?"

"Curly Kirk?"

"Can he pitch?"

"Can he pitch!" said a St. Matthew's graduate who had
heard Harris's story. "You wait."

On the field the situation was sufficiently gloomy. St. Matthew's
had a runner on second and another on third, with
one out. Barlow stood near the box, rubbing his shoulder
and looking sick. The score-board showed:

ST. MATTHEW'S 6
WILLARD'S 5

Curly removed his coat and waistcoat and gave them to
a small boy, who accepted them as if they were
sacred vestments.

"My glove?" he asked.

Turner handed him one.

Curly worked his fingers into it and walked toward the
box.

There was some laughter from the St. Matthew's benches. He
looked too young for a master, too loosely clothed for
a player, and much too solemn for either. Harris came
from the coaching line to meet him.

"I knew they would fetch you," he said.

"You appear to have arranged it."

"Only advertised your buried talents. How is the arm?"

"I have no idea."

"You'll find out. Don't try the fast one at first.
Their man at bat swings late. Keep it outside."

Curly smiled despite himself.

"You used to give too much advice when you were
catching."

"And you never took enough. Good luck, Charles."

The St. Matthew's crowd applauded its coach. Willard's watched in
doubtful silence.

Little, the catcher, approached. He was sixteen and plainly terrified.

"What shall I signal, sir?"

"Nothing complicated. One finger for a straight ball, two for
a curve. If I shake my head, give the sign
again and pray."

Little grinned. It was the first grin any Willard's boy
had bestowed upon Curly in several months.

He threw three practice balls. The first passed above Little's
shoulder and struck the backstop with a bang. The second
bounced before the plate. A groan came from the Willard's
crowd.

The third reached the catcher knee-high and hard.

Curly felt an old, forgotten thrill run from his fingers
to his shoulder. The body remembers what the mind has
laid away. He shifted his foot, bent slightly, and threw
again. The ball broke outward at the plate.

Little's eyes widened.

"All right, sir," he said.

The batter was Henshaw, St. Matthew's strongest hitter. He smiled
when Curly faced him. Curly disliked the smile.

The first pitch was a ball, wide.

The second came straight across. Henshaw swung savagely and missed.

"One strike!"

Curly received the return, rubbed the ball against his trousers,
and looked toward third. The runner danced several feet from
the bag. Curly made no attempt to catch him.

Another pitch. Henshaw fouled it high behind the stand.

"Strike two!"

Now the field had grown quiet. Curly could hear a
horse stamping beside the road and the faint flutter of
a banner over the grandstand.

Little signaled for the curve.

Curly nodded. He wound up deliberately. The ball sped toward
the plate, appeared to hang for the fraction of a
second, and dropped.

Henshaw's bat swept over it.

"Three strikes! Batter out!"

The Willard's cheering broke like a wave.

Two out, but the runners still waited on second and
third. The next batter, a short, quick boy named Page,
crowded the plate. Curly gave him two balls. The third
pitch was a strike. Page struck the fourth sharply toward
the space between first and second.

For a moment it seemed certain to pass. Then Turner,
playing second, leaped sideways, knocked it down, recovered it on
his knees, and threw to first.

"Out!"

The inning was over.

Turner ran toward the bench with the others. As he
passed Curly, he said, "Pretty pitching, sir."

Two ordinary words; but Curly carried them with him as
though they had been a decoration awarded before the whole
school.

Willard's came to bat in the last half of the
seventh. St. Matthew's pitcher, a heavy, cool youth named Felton,
disposed of the first two men. Turner reached first on
an error, stole second, and was left there when Larkin
sent an easy fly to center.

Curly sat at the end of the bench, flexing his
right hand. Boys crowded behind the wire, peering at him
with frank curiosity. The tyrant had disappeared. In his place
sat a ball-player—and boys, who may doubt scholarship, tradition, and
faculty wisdom, accept athletic ability upon visible evidence.

"Did you really pitch for Ellsworth, sir?" Little asked.

"Yes."

"Against Hamilton?"

"Twice."

"Did you beat them?"

"Once. The other game lasted twelve innings."

"What was the score?"

Curly remembered every run, but before he could answer the
side was called out.

In the eighth, St. Matthew's sent its best batters to
the plate. The first man struck at a low curve
and rolled weakly to short. The second waited, forcing Curly
to throw three balls; then he drove a vicious liner
directly back toward the box.

Curly acted before thought. His gloved hand flashed up. The
ball struck the leather with a thud that numbed his
fingers.

"Out!"

He tossed the ball carelessly to Turner, although for several
seconds he could feel nothing from wrist to fingertips.

The third batter fouled twice. Then came a ball. Then
another strike.

"One—two—three!"

St. Matthew's retired in order.

Willard's began to believe.

Belief made a considerable noise. Blue-and-white flags beat the air.
The younger boys stamped upon the wooden stand until it
trembled. From somewhere appeared a chant:

"Cur-ly! Cur-ly! Cur-ly!"

Mr. Kirk heard it as he walked to the bench
and was uncertain whether he liked it. The name had
once been an impertinence. It did not sound like one
now.

In Willard's half of the eighth, Little hit safely. Barlow,
batting in spite of his injured arm, sacrificed him to
second. A grounder advanced him to third. Two out.

Curly came to bat.

He had forgotten that the pitcher must do this also.
Harris called from the coaching line:

"Shorten your grip, Charles!"

The St. Matthew's catcher turned his head in surprise.

Curly selected a bat. His borrowed trousers exposed several inches
of blue stocking; his cap had slipped to one side;
dust streaked his shirt. The spectacle was not imposing. Yet
the laughter had ceased.

Felton's first pitch was fast and inside. Curly moved away.

"Ball one!"

The second cut the plate. He swung late.

"Strike!"

His hands stung. Three years was a long time.

Felton delivered again. Curly met the ball squarely, and the
crack carried to the road. It rose in a clean
line toward left field.

The Willard's crowd stood.

The left fielder ran back, turned, and caught it over
his shoulder.

A groan rolled across the field. Little remained stranded at
third.

Curly laid down the bat.

"Good try, sir," said Turner.

A good try! Curly had spent months demanding results and
had forgotten that boys sometimes honor the attempt. The discovery
troubled him more than criticism would have done.

The ninth began with Willard's still behind, six to five.

Curly knew that he was tiring. His shoulder ached, his
breath came faster, and the skin had rubbed from one
finger. The first St. Matthew's batter perceived it and waited.
Curly missed the plate twice.

Two balls.

A strike.

Another ball.

Little walked out from behind the plate.

"Take your time, sir," he said.

This from a boy who had once received twenty lines
for whispering in Greek! Curly nodded.

The next pitch crossed the corner.

"Strike two!"

The batter fouled one. Curly threw again, and the ball
went wide.

"Ball four!"

The runner trotted to first. St. Matthew's shouted encouragement. The
next man bunted. Curly sprang forward, seized the rolling ball,
and threw to Turner at second. The throw was low,
but Turner scooped it from the dust.

"Out!"

The batter reached first.

St. Matthew's now attempted a steal. Little's throw went high.
Turner leaped, caught it, and swept his glove down upon
the runner's shoulder.

"Out!"

Two gone.

Curly faced the final batter. His arm felt heavy, but
the old cunning had returned. He sent one slow and
high. The boy swung before it arrived. He sent another
hard and close. The bat moved too late.

For the third pitch Curly used the curve.

The batter struck a dribbling grounder toward third. Larkin charged
it, gathered it barehanded, and hurled across the diamond.

"Out!"

Willard's had one final chance.

The school rose as one body. Even Dr. Willard, seated
beneath an elm with members of the faculty, leaned forward
and gripped his cane.

Felton looked as cool as before, but he had pitched
nine hard innings. Turner, first at bat, drove a single
over short. Larkin struck out. Mason lifted a fly to
right.

Two out.

Little came next. With two strikes against him, he poked
a hit into shallow center. Turner raced to third; Little
stopped at first.

Barlow was due to bat, but he could scarcely lift
his arm.

"Mr. Kirk," Turner called, "you're next if Barlow reaches."

Barlow stood at the plate with set teeth. Felton pitched.
Ball. Strike. Foul. Another ball.

Then Barlow chopped a slow roller toward short. The shortstop
ran in, but the ball struck a pebble and bounded
over his glove.

Turner scored. Little reached second. Barlow was safe at first.

Six to six.

The field became a whirl of banners, hats, and dust.
Curly stepped toward the plate.

The resignation letter in his coat pocket was for the
moment entirely forgotten.

Felton stared at him. Curly had hit the last ball
hard, and the pitcher remembered it. He worked carefully.

First pitch—ball.

Second—strike.

Third—ball.

The fourth came over. Curly swung and sent a foul
spinning past first.

Two balls, two strikes.

The runners took their leads. Harris shouted to his fielders.
The Willard's stand had gone breathlessly still.

Felton threw a curve. Curly checked his swing.

"Ball three!"

Full count.

The next pitch came shoulder-high. Curly let it pass.

"Ball four!"

He trotted to first. The bases were full.

The next batter was young Evans, a weak hitter from
the fourth form. He looked at Curly helplessly as he
approached the plate.

"Stand up to it," Curly said. "Make him pitch."

Felton delivered two strikes without difficulty. Evans barely touched the
second, fouling it backward.

Curly watched from first. Felton was taking a long wind-up.
The St. Matthew's catcher returned the ball slowly. The infield,
expecting nothing from Evans, had relaxed by an inch or
two.

Curly moved off the bag.

Felton threw a ball.

Again Curly edged away. On the next pitch Evans struck
out—but the catcher failed to hold the ball. He recovered
it instantly and stepped on the plate, forcing Little from
third.

Three out.

The groan that followed seemed to empty the afternoon of
hope.

The game went into the tenth.

Curly walked slowly to the box. His arm was stiff
now, and every pitch hurt. Yet he had passed beyond
anxiety. The boys behind him were no longer enemies or
judges. They were his fielders. They waited for him; he
depended upon them.

The first batter grounded to Turner.

The second hit safely to right.

The third drove a ball deep toward center. Mason ran
with his back to the diamond, reached upward, and caught
it at full speed. The runner, who had started for
second, scrambled back to first.

Two out.

Curly struck out the last man with three pitched balls.

One—two—three.

As he returned to the bench, the Willard's boys crowded
close.

"Win it, Curly!"

"Hold on, sir!"

"Your turn again, Curly!"

The title of tyrant could not have survived that cheerful
confusion. Mr. Kirk himself had difficulty surviving it with proper
faculty dignity.

Willard's first batter in the tenth went out on a
pop fly. Turner singled again. Larkin sacrificed him to second.
Two out.

Mason struck a hard grounder through the infield. Turner rounded
third, but the left fielder came up with the ball
cleanly. Turner stopped. Mason reached first.

Curly was due to bat.

Felton had little control left, but he retained his courage.
His first pitch nearly struck Curly. His second was low.
The third crossed for a strike.

Then Curly lined a single to right.

Turner scored the winning run—or appeared to. But before the
cheering had fairly begun, the umpire raised both arms.

"Foul ball!"

The ball had passed outside the line by inches.

Turner returned to third. Curly picked up his bat. Felton
smiled wearily.

The next pitch was a strike.

Two strikes, two balls.

Felton tried the outside corner and missed.

"Ball three!"

The runners moved with the pitch. Curly fouled the next
ball into the crowd. Another foul followed. Then another.

Felton wiped his forehead. Curly's arms ached. Around them the
whole bright afternoon seemed suspended: colored stockings against brown earth,
white faces in the stand, dust drifting above the base-lines,
flags hanging motionless in the quiet air.

The pitcher delivered.

Curly swung. The ball bounded high toward third. The third
baseman leaped but could not reach it. The shortstop seized
it and threw to first.

Curly raced.

His arms worked like pistons; his legs drove beneath him
like shafts in a machine. The ball and runner arrived
together.

"Safe!"

Turner had not scored. He had been forced to hold
third.

The bases were full again.

Evans came to bat.

Curly stood on first, breathing hard. He watched Felton. He
watched the catcher. Then an old possibility, absurd and glorious,
entered his mind.

Felton delivered. Evans missed.

While the catcher returned the ball, Curly advanced several steps.
Mason, at second, understood and moved farther off. Turner wandered
down the third-base line.

Felton glanced at him. Turner retreated.

Second pitch. Ball.

Again the runners edged away. The St. Matthew's infield shouted
warnings.

Felton prepared for the third pitch. As his hands rose,
Turner broke for home.

The cry from the crowd was one vast sound.

Felton stopped, wheeled, and threw to the catcher. Turner checked
and raced back toward third. The catcher pursued him.

At that instant Mason dashed from second to third, and
Curly flew from first to second.

The catcher threw to the third baseman. Turner reversed again.
Mason stopped halfway down the line. Confusion possessed the diamond.

The third baseman threw home.

Turner flung himself back toward third. Mason retreated toward second.

Curly did not retreat.

He rounded second at full speed, passed the bewildered Mason,
and tore for third. The catcher, seeing him, shouted. The
ball went from home to third, but Curly slid under
the baseman's glove in a cloud of dust.

"Safe!"

Turner, meanwhile, had regained third only by leaving the base
as Curly arrived. For one astonishing second both stood within
arm's reach while fielders converged upon them.

Then Turner darted toward home again.

The third baseman pursued and threw. The catcher moved up
the line. Turner stopped, trapped beyond escape.

Curly saw the opening.

"Go back!" he shouted.

Turner dropped flat. The catcher lunged to tag him.

Curly sprang over Turner's legs and raced for the plate.

The catcher whirled. Curly was ten yards away. Eight. Six.

The ball came from the third baseman.

Curly ran with his head down, arms pumping, borrowed trousers
flapping ludicrously above his stockings. The catcher caught the throw
and swept his glove forward. Curly launched himself, struck the
dust, and slid.

Glove, ball, runner, and a yellow cloud reached the plate
together.

For a moment no one breathed.

The umpire bent above them. His hands spread wide—

"Safe!"

Willard's had won.

The school descended upon the field. Boys poured from the
stands, over the ropes, and across the diamond. Curly was
pulled upright, struck upon the back, deprived of his cap,
and lifted to a dozen shoulders. Faculty dignity disappeared beneath
dust and cheers. Turner, also dusty and bleeding from one
elbow, seized Curly's hand.

"You did it, sir!"

"We did it," said Curly.

The distinction was important, though scarcely audible.

Later, when the field had emptied and the shadows of
the elms lay long across the base-lines, Curly returned to
the gymnasium. He bathed, dressed, and discovered his coat beneath
a bench.

The resignation crackled in its inner pocket.

He drew it out and stood looking at it.

"Burn it," said a voice.

Harris leaned against the doorway.

"It is addressed to Dr. Willard," Curly replied.

"All the more reason."

"You know nothing about it."

"I know you. I also know schools. They are like
ball clubs: they forgive errors when a man shows he
is playing for the side."

"One baseball game does not make a teacher."

"No. But it may permit a teacher to begin again."

From outside came distant singing. The boys were carrying Turner
and Barlow around the quadrangle. Presently the chant changed, and
Curly heard his own nickname rising above the rest.

Harris held out his hand.

Curly hesitated, then gave him the letter.

There was a little iron stove in the dressing-room. Harris
struck a match, touched it to the corner of the
envelope, and held the paper until the flame caught. Together
they watched the resignation blacken, curl, and fall into ash.

Curly remained at Willard's.

It would be pleasant to say that he never again
made a mistake, but that would carry optimism beyond reason.
He made several. The difference was that he learned to
acknowledge them. His classes remained orderly, but laughter occasionally entered
the Greek room and was not always punished. He discovered
that firmness need not wear a scowl, and the boys
discovered that mildness is not weakness.

For two years thereafter he taught at Willard's with increasing
success. He coached the pitchers, played first base in faculty
games, and provided cake when new masters were welcomed.

The boys still called him Curly.

But now they called him so to his face.

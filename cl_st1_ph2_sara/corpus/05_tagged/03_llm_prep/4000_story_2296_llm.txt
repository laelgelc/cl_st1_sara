### Three Copecks

It was one of those brilliant June afternoons when even
our northern sun seems to have repented of its habitual
coldness and pours over the earth a prodigality of light.
The chestnuts in the public garden were in flower; their
white pyramids, touched here and there with pink, rose among
the broad leaves like gigantic imperial candelabra. Nurses in striped
gowns pushed perambulators along the gravel walks; little girls skipped
over ropes, solemnly counting their jumps; boys in linen blouses
pursued hoops, wooden horses, and one another; elderly gentlemen, their
coats unbuttoned and their faces shining, read newspapers beneath the
lime trees.

Zimina and I had found a bench near the pond.
We had been discussing, I remember, the proposed reforms in
military education, though neither of us possessed information enough to
justify the firmness of our opinions.

Our conversation was interrupted by a peculiar cry—high, broken, and
bird-like.

"Ah! Ah! Oorli! Oorli!"

A bath-chair had stopped at the bend of the path.
In it sat a young man of perhaps twenty-five, though
his soft, hairless face had the shapeless expression of an
infant's. His head hung upon one shoulder; his mouth remained
open; one hand beat continually against the rug over his
knees. Beside him walked a grey-haired lady, very upright and
carefully dressed, who now and then bent down to wipe
the saliva from his chin.

A red india-rubber ball rolled from a child's hands and
stopped beside the chair. The young man uttered a shriek.
He bent forward with such violence that the attendant had
to seize him by the shoulders. Then, clutching the ball,
he pressed it to his breast and laughed.

The child began to cry. The grey-haired lady apologised, recovered
the ball with some difficulty, and returned it. The young
man's laughter changed at once into a howl of rage.

Everyone stared. Two schoolboys imitated the sound and ran away.
A nurse crossed herself.

I watched the chair disappear between the chestnuts.

"There," I said, resuming an argument which we had often
pursued, "you have the strongest objection to all your sentimental
theories. Nature sometimes leaves the human form and takes away
the human being. What is there in such a creature
except appetite, fear, and blind irritation? A dog, at least,
may be trained."

Zimina turned toward me. He was ordinarily a mild man,
inclined to avoid contradiction; but now his narrow, sunburnt face
became grave.

"You are certain," he asked, "that nothing human remains?"

"What could remain? Thought? Conscience? Will?"

"And love?"

I shrugged my shoulders.

He tapped the gravel with his cane.

"When I was young," he said, "I spoke very much
as you do. Indeed, I spoke more cruelly, because young
men commonly mistake cruelty for strength. But a certain person
once answered me. He could not argue; he scarcely knew
four words; nevertheless, he answered so completely that I have
never again dared to judge such people. If you have
patience, I will tell you."

He settled himself in the corner of the bench and
began.

* * *

It happened in the spring of 18—, when I went
to Petersburg to present myself for the examinations of the
Nicholas General Staff Academy. I was then a lieutenant of
infantry, twenty-six years old, thin as a ramrod, vain of
my mathematics, and fully persuaded that the capital had only
to see me in order to recognise my merits.

You smile. Yes, I had even considered what kind of
moustache would best suit a staff officer.

Petersburg received me in its usual fashion. Rain fell for
six days. The pavements shone black; the canals were the
colour of lead; cab wheels sprayed mud upon one's greatcoat;
and the gilded spire above the fortress seemed not to
glitter but to burn coldly through the mist. Yet I
was enchanted. There were marble staircases, bronze eagles, sentries as
motionless as painted soldiers, parquet floors polished like mirrors, and
officers in uniforms of every imaginable splendour—blue, green, scarlet, silver,
gold. I, who had come from a regiment where the
colonel's cracked samovar was our chief ornament, walked about in
a continual fever of admiration and envy.

My allowance was small, but I had calculated everything. Lodging,
fifteen roubles a month; dinner at an eating-house, thirty copecks;
tea and bread, ten; washing, candles, ink, omnibuses, examination fees,
and tobacco—everything stood in neat columns in my notebook. According
to my arithmetic I had enough money for seven weeks.
According to life, as I afterwards discovered, I had enough
for four.

Among my mother's relations there was a second cousin, Alexandra
Ivanovna, who lived on the Petersburg Side. Before I left
home, my mother made me promise to visit her.

"She is poor," she had said, "and has suffered greatly.
Do not expect entertainment. Go because she is alone."

I postponed the duty for a fortnight. At last, one
rainy Sunday, I crossed the river and found the address—a
narrow wooden house behind a cooper's yard. The entrance smelled
of damp boards, cabbage soup, paraffin, and cats. On the
third floor I knocked at a door of cracked brown
paint.

Alexandra Ivanovna herself opened it. She was a small woman
of about fifty, dressed in a black gown rubbed shiny
at the elbows. Her face was pale and timid, but
when I gave my name she clasped both hands.

"Vladimir Petrovich! Our dear Anna Pavlovna's boy! Come in, come
in! Forgive the disorder. If I had known—"

The room was dark, though it was only three o'clock.
A greenish light entered through two bottles of dull glass
in the window; fat hissed in a frying-pan behind a
cotton curtain; stockings hung over the stove. The furniture consisted
of a table, three chairs, an iron bedstead, and a
sofa whose horsehair stuffing protruded like black whiskers.

While Alexandra Ivanovna wiped a chair with her apron, I
heard a movement in the corner.

A large figure rose from the floor.

I confess to you that I recoiled.

It was her son, Stepan. He was nearly my own
age and taller than I, with enormous sloping shoulders, long
arms, and hands red and swollen at the knuckles. His
head was very small above that heavy body. The pale
eyes wandered without fixing themselves upon anything; the lower lip
drooped; close-cropped hair stood upright like wet bristles. He came
toward me with a rocking gait.

"Papan," he said. "Papan—mnya?"

"No, Stepasha," Alexandra Ivanovna said gently. "This is our relative,
Vladimir Petrovich. Say good day."

Stepan stopped so close that I felt his warm breath.
He touched my sword-knot, then the buttons of my tunic,
and suddenly gave a loud cry:

"Oorli!"

"He means the eagles," said his mother. "He likes uniforms."

"Oorli! Oorli!"

He clapped his hands and laughed.

I did not know where to look. Alexandra Ivanovna saw
my discomfort and blushed as though she, poor soul, had
committed some offence.

"He is quiet," she whispered. "Quite harmless. He understands more
than people suppose."

Stepan could say only four or five sounds. "Papan" meant
any man in uniform, because his father, who had died
when the boy was six, had served as a police
clerk and worn brass buttons. "Mnya" meant food, drink, or
anything desired. "Oorli" signified eagles and, by extension, all glittering
objects. The word "teki" had no fixed meaning that I
could discover. He used it while asking, refusing, complaining, or
rejoicing; everything depended upon the gesture with which it was
accompanied.

Alexandra Ivanovna gave me tea in a glass with a
chipped rim and set before me a saucer containing six
lumps of sugar. She spoke of my mother, of our
old house, of people long dead. Meanwhile Stepan sat on
the floor at my feet, touching the toe of my
boot and murmuring "Papan" with complete contentment.

I went away oppressed and even irritated. Such misfortune offended
my youthful belief that life ought to be reasonable.

Nevertheless, I returned. Why? At first because Alexandra Ivanovna's joy
flattered me. Later, I think, because Stepan expected me.

He learned the sound of my step on the stairs.
Before I knocked, the door would fly open and he
would seize my hands, crying:

"Papan! Oorli! Papan!"

His attachment embarrassed me. If I attempted to speak privately
with his mother, he pushed his great head between us.
If I rose to leave, he hid my cap behind
the stove. Once, when another officer accompanied me, Stepan sulked
for an hour and would not look at me. He
was jealous like a child and obstinate like an animal;
yet his affection was so undisguised that one could not
wholly despise it.

At that time I still believed that education was a
universal key. I decided to awaken his intelligence.

I bought an alphabet for five copecks and drew letters
upon squares of cardboard. For three evenings I tried to
teach him A.

"Look, Stepasha. A."

"Oorli," he replied.

"No. A."

"Mnya."

I guided his finger along the letter, made it of
bread, arranged matches in its shape, sang it, shouted it,
whispered it. He ate the bread, scattered the matches, and
kissed my sleeve. After a week he knew no more
of A than before.

Then I attempted numbers. I placed one copper coin on
the table, then two.

"One," I said. "Two."

This interested him greatly, for he had a passion for
money. Alexandra Ivanovna allowed him to keep whatever copper pieces
visitors gave him. His treasure, perhaps forty copecks altogether, lay
in a little tin tobacco-box beneath his mattress. He counted
it after his own fashion, moving each coin from one
palm to the other and grunting solemnly. No one might
touch it. If his mother approached while the box was
open, he covered the coins with his body and howled.

I thought this passion might become the bridge to arithmetic.

"One copeck. Two copecks. How many?"

"Teki," said Stepan, sweeping both coins into his pocket.

After a month I abandoned his education. He had learned
nothing; I had merely increased his collection by eleven copecks.

Meanwhile my own studies went badly.

The Academy, which from a distance had appeared to me
a temple of glory, proved at close quarters to be
an establishment in which exhausted officers waited in corridors, rubbed
chalk from their sleeves, borrowed rulers, disputed over logarithms, and
watched one another with the suspicious politeness of gamblers. I
had been considered learned in my regiment because I could
calculate the contents of a ditch and quote two lines
of Shakespeare in translation. In Petersburg I discovered captains who
solved problems as quickly as I could copy them and
lieutenants who discussed fortification as if Vauban had been their
uncle.

During the written examination in geodesy I made an error
in the first calculation. All the rest followed from it,
exact, elegant, and useless. In military history I confused the
order of two corps. Finally, at topography, my hand began
to tremble. I saw the colonel examiner bend over my
plan; his gold spectacles gleamed; he made a small blue
mark in the margin.

That mark destroyed my future.

My name did not appear on the list.

For two days I walked about pretending indifference. I congratulated
successful acquaintances, criticised the examination system, and declared that regimental
service offered a more honourable career. At night I lay
awake in my lodging and heard the rain strike the
sill.

Then came practical difficulties.

My money was nearly gone. The examination had lasted longer
than expected; my landlord demanded payment; and, to make matters
worse, the military office informed me that my travelling allowance
could not be issued until certain papers arrived from my
regiment. The papers had been sent to the wrong department.
From the wrong department they had gone to a chancery
on Liteiny Prospect; from the chancery they were returned for
a missing signature; the official empowered to sign was ill;
his assistant advised me to come on Thursday; on Thursday
the office was closed for a saint's day.

I went everywhere—headquarters, treasury, postal office, district command, back to
headquarters. Everywhere there were desks, green cloth, sand-shakers, icons blackened
by lamp smoke, and clerks who spoke excellent Russian but
told me nothing.

I wrote to comrades. One had recently married; another had
lost at cards; a third sent a friendly letter explaining
that officers ought not to borrow from officers. I could
not write to my mother. She had sold two silver
spoons to pay for my journey.

I sold my spare boots for three roubles, my field-glasses
for four, and six military textbooks for one rouble twenty
copecks. A bookseller offered me fifteen copecks for a volume
which had cost two roubles.

"Then let it rot," I said with dignity.

Two days later I returned and accepted ten.

Hunger began almost comically. One morning I found that I
possessed twenty-six copecks. I spent five on cigarettes, because a
man may endure hunger but not, as I then believed,
the absence of tobacco. At noon I bought tea and
a roll. By evening I regretted the cigarettes. The next
day I ate only bread. The day after that, nothing.

You have been hungry, perhaps, before dinner; that is not
hunger. True hunger enters into every thought. The bronze horses
on the Anichkov Bridge seem made of chocolate; the smell
from an eating-house follows one for half a verst; even
the paste in a bookbinder's window suggests nourishment. I could
distinguish, from across the street, rye bread at three copecks
a pound, white bread at five, rolls at two each,
and yesterday's cracked rolls sold three for four.

I remember standing before a bakery in Bolshaya Morskaya with
three copecks in my glove. Three copecks! The whole question
of existence lay in them.

If I bought bread, I could eat. If I kept
the money, I might pay for a tram to some
office where, possibly, my papers had arrived. I walked away,
returned, walked away again. At last I entered and, with
an air of exaggerated carelessness, as if purchasing for a
dog, asked for a pound of rye bread.

The shopwoman weighed it.

"Three and a half copecks."

"I said a pound," I replied haughtily.

"That is a pound."

I had only three.

I pretended that the loaf was too stale and went
out. In a lane behind the Kazan Cathedral I laughed
until tears came into my eyes.

That evening I understood that I was in danger—not of
starvation alone, but of something worse. Shame had isolated me.
I could not tell anyone the truth. My uniform was
still brushed, my gloves were clean, my sword hung at
my side; therefore people supposed that I was fed. I
passed restaurants glowing with lamps and heard forks touching plates.
Every stranger appeared to belong to a world from which
I had been expelled.

A black thought began to follow me. The Neva was
near. A bullet was quicker. I still owned a revolver
and two cartridges.

Do not imagine that I formed a tragic resolution. There
was no grandeur in it. I was tired, humiliated, hungry,
and afraid. I thought: "Tonight—or tomorrow morning. But first I
will try Alexandra Ivanovna."

To ask money from that poor woman was disgraceful. I
knew she lived on a widow's pension of eighteen roubles
a month and earned perhaps six more by mending linen.
Yet I went.

The room appeared darker than ever. A paraffin lamp smoked
upon the table. Alexandra Ivanovna was frying potatoes in a
little black pan, and the smell almost made me faint.
Stepan sat beside the stove, arranging his coins in rows.

He saw me, sprang up, and uttered his usual joyful
cry.

"Papan! Papan! Oorli!"

I pushed him aside more roughly than I intended.

"Not now, Stepasha."

He stopped. His mouth remained open, but he made no
sound.

Alexandra Ivanovna looked at me closely.

"You are ill, Vladimir Petrovich."

"No. Merely tired."

"Sit down. The potatoes will be ready."

That was unbearable. If I ate her potatoes, she and
Stepan would have less. I began at once, in a
cold and businesslike voice:

"Alexandra Ivanovna, I have come on an unpleasant matter. My
allowance has been delayed. Could you lend me—temporarily, of course—five
roubles? Even three. I shall repay you as soon as
the papers arrive."

Her face changed. She put down the knife.

"Ah, my dear Vladimir Petrovich…"

"I understand. If it is inconvenient—"

"It is not that I do not wish to. God
knows! But today I paid the landlord, and tomorrow I
must buy paraffin. I have sixty-seven copecks until Saturday."

She opened a purse and showed me, as though I
might doubt her: a silver twenty-copeck piece, some copper, a
button, and a key.

"Of course," I said. "It is nothing. I asked only
because the office is slow. Do not be troubled."

She began apologising. Each word cut me more deeply. I
stood, took my cap, and smiled. I remember even making
a joke about the Empire being governed by papers which
travelled more extensively than its officers.

All the while Stepan watched me.

His coins lay exposed on the floor, but he had
forgotten to cover them. His pale eyes moved from my
face to his mother's purse, then back to my face.
I turned toward the door.

Someone tugged my coat.

It was Stepan.

"Go away," I said.

He held out his closed fist.

"Teki."

"I do not want anything."

"Teki," he repeated.

Then he opened his hand.

There lay his treasure: blackened copper two-copeck pieces, single copecks
worn smooth, and one tiny silver coin. Some were damp
from his palm. Fifty-one copecks in all—the fortune he had
guarded for years, hidden beneath his mattress, counted every evening,
defended even against his mother.

He pressed them toward me.

At first I did not understand. Then I saw his
face. There was effort in it, pain, fear of losing
what he loved, and yet something stronger than fear. He
had no word for sorrow, debt, hunger, friendship, or sacrifice.
He had only "teki."

But he understood.

Educated gentlemen had listened to my explanations and refused me.
Clerks had filled forms. Officers had quoted regulations. This speechless
creature had looked once into my face and understood.

I took one coin—a three-copeck piece.

"Only this, Stepasha," I said. "I borrow it."

He frowned, seized my wrist, and tried to pour the
rest into my hand.

"No, no. Three are enough."

He gave a dissatisfied cry, thrust the coins into my
coat pocket, and fled behind the stove, where he hid
his face against the wall as if ashamed of what
he had done.

I sat down.

Alexandra Ivanovna wept quietly. I wept too—without dignity, without restraint,
like a child. Stepan peered from behind the stove and
began to whimper because we were crying; then he came
to me, laid his heavy head upon my shoulder, and
stroked my sleeve.

Those coins did not rescue me materially. Fifty-one copecks could
not pay my lodging or carry me home. But they
rescued me nevertheless. The black thought vanished. Warmth returned to
the world. I ate potatoes with them; the next morning
a former regimental comrade, whom I had been too proud
to approach, lent me ten roubles; two days later my
papers arrived.

I repaid Stepan with a new tin box and a
whole rouble in copper. But that, you understand, was not
repayment. Such debts cannot be repaid.

* * *

Zimina ceased speaking.

The garden was quieter now. Long shadows lay across the
gravel, and the chestnut blossoms had lost their whiteness in
the declining light. From beyond the pond came the thin
cries of children being summoned home.

"But did Stepan know what he was doing?" I asked,
though less confidently than before.

Zimina looked at me with melancholy kindness.

"What more would you require of knowledge? He perceived suffering
and surrendered what he loved in order to lessen it.
Many clever men perceive suffering, name it, classify it, and
pass on. Stepan could not pronounce the word compassion. He
merely possessed the thing itself."

Along the distant path the bath-chair appeared again. The disabled
young man was calmer now. He held the red ball,
which the child had evidently given him, and turned it
slowly in the sunlight. The grey-haired lady walked beside him
with one hand upon his shoulder.

As they passed beneath the chestnuts, he laughed and uttered
his strange, bird-like cry.

This time I did not turn away.

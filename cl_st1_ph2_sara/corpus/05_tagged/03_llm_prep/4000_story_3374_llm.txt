# The Bump That Brought Him Back

Carl Hemmer had the puck!

Twenty-five hundred throats in the Howard Prep rink flung out
the warning, the challenge, and the unwilling tribute all in
one mighty roar. Scarves waved, sticks rattled against the boards,
and boys and men stood on the benches while the
white-shirted Taber High forward came flashing from behind his own
blue line.

"Stop him, Howard!"

"Smear him!"

"Watch Hemmer!"

Watch him? They could scarcely see him!

Carl was a streak of crimson and black, bent low
over his stick, driving the puck before him with short,
clever taps. One Howard man lunged. Carl swerved, shifting his
weight with the grace of a dancer and the speed
of a frightened deer. A second defenseman stabbed at the
puck. Carl whipped it across his skates, recovered it on
the opposite side, and went between the two guards before
they could close the gap.

A clear lane opened toward the Howard goal.

"Go, Carl!" bawled Lank Broderick from far behind.

Carl went!

He was only a high-school boy, lean and broad-shouldered, with
a shock of dark hair under his helmet and a
face that usually wore a good-humored grin. Yet, with a
hockey stick in his hands and a square of open
ice before him, he became something more—a whirlwind, a dart,
a madman on the loose. Taber followers declared that he
could skate through a stone wall if there were a
goal on the other side. Howard followers, who had seen
him score twice that afternoon, were almost ready to believe
it.

The goal-tender crouched.

Carl feinted left. The goal-tender moved a fraction.

That fraction was enough.

Carl snapped his wrists.

Smack!

The puck shot waist-high toward the far corner. The goalie
flung out a padded arm, but the black disk skimmed
past his glove and slapped the netting.

Goal!

The Taber section exploded. Caps flew into the air. The
substitutes hammered one another on the back. Coach Brewster, who
ordinarily showed about as much emotion as a cigar-store Indian,
jumped clear off the floor.

"Three to one!" shrieked a thin freshman. "Carl's won it!"

Perhaps he had. Less than four minutes remained, and Howard
had been fighting all afternoon to hold the Taber star.
Taber High was undefeated, and most observers credited that proud
record to Carl Hemmer. He passed when a pass was
needed and worked well with his teammates, but he possessed
the rare and dangerous gift of making a game his
own. Give him the puck at center ice and there
was no telling what might happen—except that several opponents would
probably find themselves staring after him.

Howard Prep knew it, too.

As Carl wheeled away from the net, he saw Ed
Prouty, Howard's biggest defenseman, charging down upon him. The puck
had already gone into play at the face-off circle. Carl
was not in the action. There was no reason for
Prouty to touch him.

But Prouty came on, shoulders hunched and face grim.

Carl had time to turn his head.

He had time to glimpse two hundred pounds of angry
hockey player gathering momentum.

He had time to think, "He can't mean to hit
me!"

Then—

Crash!

Prouty's shoulder caught him high. Carl's skates flew from under
him. For one queer instant the glaring lamps swung in
a blazing arc above his eyes; then his head struck
the ice, and the shouts of the crowd rolled away
into a deep, rushing darkness.

He did not move.

The referee's whistle shrilled again and again. Lank Broderick flung
himself at Prouty, and only the combined efforts of two
officials and three Taber players prevented a free-for-all.

"You dirty butcher!" Lank shouted. "The puck wasn't within twenty
feet!"

"Get away from me!" Prout snarled, though his face had
gone pale.

Coach Brewster was already kneeling beside Carl. The rink physician
hurried out with his bag. A silence spread from the
nearest rows until it covered the great building, broken only
by the scrape of a skate and the frightened whispering
of spectators.

The doctor raised one of Carl's eyelids, felt his pulse,
and spoke quickly to the coach.

They carried Carl from the ice.

Taber won the game, three to one. Few persons remembered
the final minutes. The illegal check, the limp figure on
the ice, and the stretcher disappearing through the players' gate
were what remained in their minds.

The newspapers made plenty of it the next morning.

TABER ACE FELLED BY VICIOUS CHECK

HEMMER UNCONSCIOUS AFTER HOWARD GAME

STAR'S INJURY MAY END PERFECT SEASON

The last headline was too gloomy. Carl had a concussion,
but no fracture. For two days he lay in a
shaded hospital room with his head bandaged and an ache
behind his eyes. On the third day he was joking
with Lank. On the fifth he wanted to get up.
On the seventh Doctor Murdock found him sitting on the
edge of the bed, pretending that he had dropped a
magazine there by accident.

"You'll skate when I say you'll skate," the doctor warned.

"How about tomorrow?"

"No."

"Day after?"

"No."

"Next Christmas?"

"If you keep pestering me, perhaps."

Lank, lounging beside the window, grinned.

"Be reasonable, Doc. Carl only landed on his bean."

"Exactly," replied Doctor Murdock. "That is the part I'm trying
to save, though Mr. Broderick seems doubtful about its value."

"Aw, my bean's tough," said Carl. "Prouty probably cracked the
ice."

"The ice did not spend two days unconscious."

Carl's grin faded a little.

The doctor noticed it, but said nothing. He was a
wise man and understood that a boy who had been
struck into blackness might carry away an injury that no
stethoscope could discover.

A week later Carl returned to classes. A week after
that he dressed for light practice. His balance was perfect,
his sight clear, his legs strong. He circled the rink,
took passes, and fired half a dozen wicked shots into
the net.

Coach Brewster breathed more easily.

"There's the old warhorse!" Lank cried. "Siddall had better hire
Prout, a brick wall, and the town police."

Carl laughed. It was good to feel the cold air
against his face, good to hear blades slicing the ice,
good to smell leather, wool, resin, and the faint sharpness
of the frozen surface.

"Come on," he said. "Try to take it from me."

Lank tried. Carl flicked the puck past him, slipped neatly
around his shoulder, and scored.

"Nothing wrong with you," said the coach.

"No, sir," Carl answered.

He believed it then.

Taber's next scheduled opponent was Winston Academy, a team occupying
the bottom of the league. The game was expected to
be little more than a brisk practice for Taber before
its final and decisive contest with Siddall High. Siddall, led
by the famous Whiz Deagen, had lost only one game;
if it defeated Taber by two goals, it could still
claim the district championship under the league's scoring arrangement.

Nobody supposed Winston would cause trouble.

For the first five minutes, Winston did not. Taber scored
almost at once, Lank batting in a rebound after Carl's
shot. Then Carl took the puck at center and swept
toward a pair of defenders.

"Here he comes!" yelled a spectator.

Carl gathered speed.

The two Winston boys spread apart, preparing to close on
him.

He saw their shoulders. He saw their lowered heads. The
glare of the rink lights flashed upon the ice, and
suddenly the lights were those at Howard. The nearest player
became Prouty—huge, merciless, coming at him from the side.

A cold tremor passed through Carl.

Instead of splitting the defense, he jerked away.

The puck slid harmlessly to the boards.

A Winston player captured it and raced in the opposite
direction.

"What happened?" Lank called as he chased after the play.

"Bad bounce," Carl replied.

There had been no bad bounce.

Two minutes later the chance came again. Carl approached one
defender, could have slipped inside him, and turned away. He
tried a long, hopeless shot that struck a skate.

In the second period he had an open path until
a Winston guard crossed before him. Carl slowed. The guard,
surprised by this respect from the great Hemmer, poked the
puck away.

Winston scored on the counterattack.

One to one!

The crowd became uneasy. Coach Brewster paced behind the boards.

"Snap into it, Taber!" he shouted. "You're making them look
like champions!"

Carl tried. His hands were as skillful as ever, his
legs as fast, and his mind saw every opening. But
whenever that opening led between two bodies—whenever a shoulder bore
down, a hip swung toward him, or a stick came
slashing across his path—the same blind alarm seized him.

He would be struck.

His head would hit the ice.

The lights would whirl, and the world would vanish.

He dodged, circled, passed too soon, and surrendered the very
spaces he once had stormed.

Taber finally won, two to one, when a Winston defenseman
accidentally deflected Lank's shot into his own net. There was
little cheering.

In the dressing room the players pulled off their gear
in puzzled silence. Carl sat hunched before his locker, unwinding
tape from the blade of his stick.

Lank dropped onto the bench beside him.

"What's biting you?"

"Nothing."

"You skated like a fellow dodging tax collectors."

"I'm rusty."

"You weren't rusty in practice."

Carl yanked the tape so sharply that it broke.

"Lay off, will you?"

Lank studied him, hurt and astonished. He was a lanky,
freckled boy whose nickname had long ago driven his real
first name from common use. He was not a spectacular
skater, but he could take punishment, dig a puck out
of a scramble, and keep smiling when a game grew
rough. More than that, he was Carl's closest friend.

"All right," Lank said quietly. "I'll lay off."

Coach Brewster came through the doorway carrying the official score
sheet.

"What happened to you, Hemmer?"

"Nothing, Coach."

"Then it had better keep from happening against Siddall."

Carl looked up.

The coach's expression was stern, but anxiety showed beneath it.
Everyone knew what Siddall's defense would do if Carl hesitated.
They would crowd him, drive him against the boards, and
make him hear the echo of Prouty's check in every
rush.

"I'll be ready," Carl said.

But that night, lying awake in his room, he knew
he had made a promise he might not keep.

The town knew something was wrong, too. Public opinion, especially
sporting public opinion, is a sharp-eyed creature. It misses little
and invents much. By Monday the school corridors buzzed with
theories.

Carl's eyesight was damaged.

Carl's heart had weakened.

Carl had quarrelled with Coach Brewster.

Carl was saving himself for a professional contract.

The newspapers were kinder, though not silent.

TABER OFF FORM IN WINSTON VICTORY

HAS HEMMER LOST HIS OLD DASH?

One columnist wrote that the Taber captain "appeared strangely unwilling
to mix with the enemy defense."

Carl folded that paper and pushed it deep into a
wastebasket.

For the following week he practiced furiously. Alone, he flew
down the ice, cut across lines, and drove shots into
the corners. When teammates guarded him lightly, he eluded them.
But the moment Coach Brewster ordered two defensemen to body
him hard, the old terror sprang up.

Once Lank moved across his path. Carl could have met
him shoulder to shoulder and gone through.

He turned aside.

The coach blew his whistle.

"Again!"

Again Carl came. Again he veered.

"What are you afraid of?" Coach Brewster demanded.

"Nothing!"

"Then quit skating as if everybody has a gun!"

The words struck home because they were true. Carl's face
burned. He seized the puck and charged once more, but
when the defense closed, blackness seemed to wait between their
shoulders. He passed.

No soap!

Skill could not command courage. Anger could not force it.
Pride itself was helpless before that swift, instinctive shrinking which
began before thought.

The afternoon of the Siddall game arrived gray and bitter.
Snow hissed against windows, but long before the opening face-off
the Taber rink was packed. Siddall had brought six hundred
followers by special train. They filled one side with blue
pennants, horns, bells, and a brass band that played whenever
anybody tried to talk.

Taber's red-and-black banners covered the opposite stands.

"Undefeated!" proclaimed one enormous sign.

"Keep it that way!" answered another.

The Siddall team took the ice first. They were big,
hard-trained boys, skating in a compact file. At their head
came Whiz Deagen.

Whiz was Carl's opposite in appearance and almost his equal
in hockey. He was stocky, fair-haired, and powerful, with short
legs that drove him forward like pistons. He did not
weave through a defense with Carl's grace; he smashed holes
in it. He had scored more goals than any player
in the district except Carl, and Siddall supporters maintained that
he was the better man because he "never knew when
he was hit."

As he circled past the Taber bench, Whiz glanced at
Carl.

"Heard you lost your appetite for bumps," he remarked.

Carl tightened his gloves.

"You'll find out."

"I aim to."

Whiz skated away, grinning.

The teams lined up.

Lank leaned close to Carl. "Don't mind his chatter."

"I don't."

"Get the first one, old warhorse. We'll stand Siddall on
their heads."

The whistle sounded.

Sticks cracked at the face-off. The puck leaped free, spun
between skates, and was kicked backward to a Siddall guard.
At once the rink became a streaming, smashing spectacle—slashing sticks,
racing forms, skidding slides, bruising falls. Siddall rushed. Taber checked.
The puck ricocheted from the boards and went bounding across
the ice while five boys hurled themselves after it.

Whiz seized it.

He drove straight at the Taber defense, passed at the
last possible instant, took the return, and shot.

Taber's goalie, Pepper Kirk, blocked it with his pad.

The rebound came loose!

Whiz lunged, but Carl swept in from the side, hooked
the puck away, and started up the rink.

The Taber crowd rose.

"Now, Carl!"

He had speed. He had an open center. Only two
Siddall defensemen waited between him and the goal.

They drew together.

Carl came on, stick flickering, shoulders low. He meant to
go between them. He told himself he would go between
them. Then one guard shifted to meet him, and the
gap became a narrowing tunnel bordered by elbows, hips, and
hard, driving bodies.

Carl saw Prouty.

He heard again the crash at Howard.

He cut sharply to the boards.

The Siddall men trapped him there. One pinned him with
a shoulder while the other scooped away the puck.

"Afraid!" roared a Siddall voice.

The blue side took it up.

"Afraid! Afraid! Afraid!"

Carl's jaw set. He raced after the play, but the
chant followed him like a pack of yelping dogs.

For ten minutes the game remained scoreless. Pepper made three
brilliant saves. Siddall's goalie stopped Lank at close range. Carl
shot twice from bad angles rather than force his way
through the defense.

Coach Brewster watched without expression. Inside, the coach was desperate.
He could substitute for tired legs or an injured hand;
he could alter combinations, strengthen the defense, or order a
different attack. But he could not skate onto the ice
and supply the nerve his star had lost.

Late in the first period Whiz and Carl met near
the center line.

Whiz had the puck. Carl swept toward him. They should
have collided shoulder to shoulder, with the stronger and better-balanced
skater winning possession.

At the last instant Carl eased away.

Whiz burst past.

Lank came across, but Whiz spun from him, drove between
the Taber guards, and fired. Pepper dropped to his knees.
The puck struck his glove, bounced upward, and tumbled behind
him into the cage.

Goal!

Siddall's band crashed into a victory march.

One to nothing!

Whiz skated past Carl on his way to the face-off
circle.

"Thanks," he said.

Carl did not answer.

The period ended with Siddall still leading. In the dressing
room Coach Brewster spoke plainly.

"They've had eleven shots. We've had five. They're beating us
to loose pucks, and they're controlling the middle. Carl, you're
letting their defense steer you wherever they please."

"I know."

"Knowing won't tie the score."

The coach outlined a play. Lank was to carry along
the left boards, draw one defenseman, and pass inward. Carl
would take the puck moving at full speed and break
through the remaining gap.

"It'll be tight," Coach Brewster said, looking directly at Carl.
"You'll get bumped. Go anyway."

Carl swallowed.

"Yes, sir."

The second period began with Taber attacking fiercely. Lank won
the puck, battered along the boards, and sent it inward
exactly as ordered.

Carl received it.

The gap was there.

So was Whiz Deagen.

Whiz swung in from the right, timing his check. Carl
saw him and passed backward before the play had formed.
A Siddall stick intercepted.

Groans rolled from the Taber stands.

Again the teams stormed from end to end. Again Carl's
skill carried him near the defense. Again fear turned him
aside. The Siddall guards learned that they need not even
hit him. A lowered shoulder was enough. A threatening movement
drove him toward the boards; a quick closing of ranks
made him throw away the puck.

The clock crept on.

Eight minutes remained in the second period.

Then five.

Then two.

Carl heard every shout. Spectators who had praised him for
three years now called frantic advice.

"Drive in!"

"Don't circle!"

"Take the bump, Hemmer!"

Easy to shout, he thought bitterly. Easy to say it
from a bench!

Yet he knew they were right.

At the break between periods, he remained seated after the
others went into the corridor for water. He stared at
the floor between his skates.

Lank came back and closed the door.

"Well?" he said.

Carl did not look up. "I can't do it."

"Can't do what?"

"Go through them."

"You've gone through better."

"Not now."

Lank's bantering expression vanished.

Carl forced the words out. "Every time they close on
me, I see that Howard check. I know it's foolish.
I know my head's all right. But I feel the
ice hit me before anybody touches me. It's like skating
straight into blackness."

For a moment Lank said nothing. Outside, the crowd stamped
and sang. Siddall's band blared a few triumphant notes.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Lank asked.

"What good would it do?"

"Maybe none. Maybe plenty."

"If I'd told the coach, he might have benched me."

"Maybe he should."

Carl flinched.

Lank gripped his shoulder. "I said maybe. Listen, old warhorse.
We still need your shot. Nobody else can whip it
like you. If you can't crack their bulwark, I'll crack
it for you."

"You?"

"Why not?"

"You'll be killed. Those fellows outweigh you twenty pounds apiece."

"Then there'll be twenty pounds more of them to pick
off the ice."

"I mean it, Lank."

"So do I. On the next rush, trail me. I'll
go into the middle and make them close. When they
hit, I'll kick the puck back. You grab it and
shoot."

Carl shook his head. "No."

"Got a better notion?"

"No, but—"

"Then that's our play."

The whistle summoned them.

As Lank turned to go, Carl caught his sleeve. "Don't
take a crazy chance for me."

Lank grinned again, though his eyes remained serious.

"I'm taking it for Taber."

The final period opened.

Siddall, protecting its lead, drew its forwards back and formed
a human barricade across the ice. The minutes fell away
amid furious checking. Fourteen remained. Twelve. Ten.

Pepper saved Taber when Whiz broke clear. Sliding full length,
the goalie knocked aside a shot that seemed certain to
score.

"Atta boy, Pepper!"

The loose puck sped toward the boards.

Lank got it.

He wheeled and started.

Carl saw him glance back. That glance was the signal.

Lank drove down the left side, his long legs eating
the distance. One Siddall forward tried to hook the puck
away. Lank shouldered past. Another came at him. Lank thrust
the disk forward, skated around the check, and gathered it
again.

The defense waited.

Carl followed six yards behind.

"Now, Lank!" he shouted.

Lank did not pass. Not yet.

He shifted from the boards toward the center. The Siddall
guards moved together. Whiz came racing back to aid them,
realizing the danger. Three blue jerseys converged upon the single
Taber skater.

Lank went straight on.

He was not another Carl. He had no bewildering trick
prepared, no lightning swerve with which to make three men
collide. His plan was brutally simple. He would enter that
moving wall and hold the puck until the last possible
fraction of a second.

"Come on!" he yelled.

The gap closed.

Whiz reached him first. One defender struck from the left,
the other from the right.

Crash!

Lank disappeared in a tangle of bodies. His stick whipped
backward as he fell, and the puck shot out between
skates—backward, cleanly, exactly where Carl was waiting.

Carl caught it.

The collision had swept the center open. He sped through
and faced the Siddall goalie alone.

The keeper charged from the cage.

Carl feinted, dragged the puck aside, and flicked it into
the uncovered corner.

Goal!

The red-and-black stands erupted in one vast bellow.

"One to one!"

"Lank made it!"

"Taber! Taber! Taber!"

Carl turned to celebrate—and saw that Lank had not risen.

Whiz was on his knees. One Siddall guard crawled away.
The other stood unsteadily. Lank lay twisted upon the ice,
his left leg under him and one arm flung across
his face.

The cheering broke off.

Carl raced back.

"Lank!"

The injured boy stirred but did not answer. Blood trickled
from a cut above his eyebrow. When Carl touched his
shoulder, Lank opened his eyes.

"Did you get it?" he muttered.

"We scored. Don't move."

"Knew you would."

Coach Brewster and the doctor hurried onto the ice. They
straightened Lank carefully. When they tried to lift him, he
gasped and clutched his knee.

"Easy!" ordered the doctor. "Probable sprain. Perhaps worse."

Carl knelt beside him, feeling sick.

Lank had gone into the wall that Carl feared. Lank
had offered his own body to open a path for
the star who should have opened paths for others. The
cut, the dazed eyes, the useless leg—these were the price
of Carl's hesitation.

"I'm sorry," Carl whispered.

Lank heard him.

"What for?"

"You did my job."

"Somebody had to."

The attendants raised him onto a stretcher.

Lank caught Carl's glove before they carried him away. His
grip was weak but determined.

"Don't waste it, old warhorse."

Carl stood motionless while the stretcher passed through the gate.

Something changed within him then.

It was not that he forgot the Howard collision. He
remembered it with terrible clearness. He knew ice was hard,
shoulders were heavy, and even the strongest boy could be
knocked senseless. Courage was not ignorance of those things. It
was seeing them, feeling them, fearing them—and going on because
a friend had already paid the price of one's retreat.

Shame struck Carl first, hot and bitter. Gratitude followed. Then
anger—not reckless anger at Siddall, but anger at the terror
that had ridden him, steered him, and made a loyal
teammate do what he would not.

He looked toward the Siddall goal.

The defensemen were taking their positions again.

This time they seemed no larger than ordinary boys.

Whiz approached the face-off circle, rubbing his shoulder after the
collision. Carl skated opposite him.

"That was a tough break for Broderick," Whiz said soberly.
"He's got nerve."

"Yes," Carl replied. "He gave me some."

The puck dropped.

Carl and Whiz struck together.

Smack!

Their shoulders met with a crack that sounded across the
rink. Carl felt the force from his hip to his
neck. For one flashing instant alarm leaped in him.

But he did not turn.

He drove harder.

Whiz slid backward, lost his footing, and dropped to one
knee. Carl kicked the puck free and swept away.

A roar went up—not for a goal, not yet, but
because every Taber follower understood. The old Carl was back!

He tore through center, met the first guard, and swung
inside. The second guard checked him solidly. Carl staggered, kept
his skates, and shoved the puck onward. He recovered it
at the boards, whirled, and shot.

The goalie blocked it.

Carl crashed into him an instant later. Both went down,
but Carl bounced up laughing, wild with the discovery that
impact no longer meant blackness.

"Atta boy!" Coach Brewster thundered. "That's hockey!"

Six minutes remained.

Siddall attacked with desperation, for a tie would leave Taber
undefeated and district champion. Whiz led the rush. He hammered
past one Taber forward, exchanged passes at center, and charged
the right side.

Carl pursued him.

Whiz cut inward and had only Pepper before him. The
goalie crouched, but Whiz held the puck, waiting for Pepper
to commit himself.

Carl came from behind like a demon.

He could not reach the puck with his stick. Only
one play remained—a shoulder check, hard and exact.

For the briefest instant he saw Prouty bearing down on
him.

Then the picture reversed. He was not the helpless player
struck after the action. He was a defender making a
clean check to save his team.

Carl flung himself forward.

Smack!

His shoulder met Whiz's squarely. The two stars spun apart.
Whiz skidded into the boards; Carl slid on one hip
almost to the goal. The puck trickled harmlessly behind the
cage.

Pepper pounced upon it.

The Taber crowd became a roaring maelstrom.

"What a check!"

"He saved it!"

"Carl! Carl! Carl!"

Whiz rose slowly and rubbed his ribs.

"Good one," he called.

Carl nodded. "You'd have scored."

"You're blamed right I would."

There was no bitterness in it. Hard play respects hard
play, and Whiz Deagen was too fine a sportsman to
complain of the medicine he had so often given others.

Four minutes remained.

Both teams were tiring. Jerseys clung damply to backs. Breath
smoked in the cold air. Skates had cut the once-smooth
surface into a gray maze of grooves. Yet the pace
increased. Every loose puck drew three or four diving figures;
every rush ended in a crack of sticks and a
spray of ice.

Three minutes.

Carl carried in, shot, missed by an inch.

Two minutes.

Whiz fired from the blue line. Pepper kicked it out.

Siddall recovered. A shot struck the post with a clang
that froze every Taber heart.

One minute!

The announcer's voice was lost beneath the shouting.

Siddall forced a face-off near Taber's goal. Whiz lined up
for it, chest heaving. Carl crouched across from him. The
referee glanced toward the timer.

The puck fell.

Whiz knocked it backward. A Siddall defenseman shot at once.

Pepper blocked it, but the rebound flew high and landed
among skates. Sticks chopped. Bodies crowded. Nobody could find the
puck.

Carl saw it squirt from the scramble.

He hooked it clear and started up the ice.

Forty seconds!

Whiz turned and chased him. The Siddall defense spread across
center, three men wide. Carl had no teammate ahead. Taber's
other forwards had been trapped deep in their own end.

He was alone.

A week earlier he would have circled. Five minutes earlier
he might have waited for help.

Now he bent lower and gathered speed.

The crowd sensed what he meant to do. The noise
rose until the roof itself seemed to shake.

"Go through!"

"Take them, Carl!"

"Go!"

Thirty seconds!

The first Siddall man came forward. Carl swerved right, dragged
the puck behind his skates, and snapped it left. The
guard crossed his feet and went sliding past.

The second defenseman closed from the boards. Carl met him
shoulder to shoulder.

Crash!

The impact jarred Carl's teeth. His stick nearly left his
hands. But he drove onward, pushing, twisting, fighting for each
inch until the defenseman spun away.

Twenty seconds!

Whiz was beside him now.

The third guard waited ahead, crouched and grim. Whiz crowded
Carl from the right, seeking to pin him. The guard
moved from the left. Once more the old black tunnel
opened between converging bodies.

Carl heard Lank's voice.

Don't waste it, old warhorse.

He charged into the gap.

The guard struck him. Whiz hit an instant later. Three
bodies came together with a sound like timbers breaking.

Carl's knees buckled. One skate flew from the ice. His
shoulder twisted. Yet his stick remained on the puck, shoving
it ahead as he fell.

The guard toppled backward.

Whiz went spinning.

Carl slid through them on his side!

Ten seconds!

The puck rolled several feet ahead. Carl, still sliding, stretched
for it. The Siddall goalie sprang from the cage and
swept his stick outward.

Carl's blade touched the puck first.

He could not rise. He could not aim with his
usual deadly care. He had one hand on the stick
and his body was turning, skidding toward the goal while
the goalie sprawled across his path.

Five seconds!

Carl jabbed.

The puck struck the goalie's pad.

It bounced upward.

For one endless instant the black disk hung in the
air. Carl slid beneath it. The goalie flung back his
glove. Whiz, recovering behind the play, lunged desperately.

The puck dropped beside the post.

Carl swung from the ice.

Tap!

The disk crossed the line.

The timer's gun exploded.

Goal!

For a heartbeat there was silence—the stunned silence of people
unable to believe that the impossible thing before them had
truly happened.

Then the rink went mad.

Students poured over the boards. Caps, pennants, and programs whirled
through the air. The Taber band attempted the school song,
lost it after three bars, and blared whatever notes came
to hand. Coach Brewster was engulfed by substitutes. Pepper Kirk
threw both gloves skyward.

Two to one!

Taber undefeated!

Carl lay inside the Siddall goal with the net wrapped
around one shoulder. The goalie sat beside him, dazed. Whiz
coasted in and halted.

"You got it," Whiz said.

Carl pushed the netting away. "By about half an inch."

"Half an inch counts."

Whiz offered a hand and pulled him upright.

The crowd swarmed them. Teammates pounded Carl until he thought
he might need Doctor Murdock again. A red scarf was
tied around his helmet. Somebody tried to lift him, somebody
else grabbed his stick, and the thin freshman who had
predicted victory at Howard screamed directly into his ear.

Coach Brewster finally fought through.

"Where's Lank?" Carl demanded.

"Dressing room. Sprained knee, bad cut, no fracture. Doctor says
he'll be all right."

Carl escaped the celebration as soon as he could and
hurried from the ice, limping slightly but scarcely aware of
it.

Lank lay on a table with his knee bandaged and
a white strip above one eyebrow. Doctor Murdock was packing
his bag.

"You took your time," Lank said.

Carl stopped beside him. For a moment he could not
speak.

"We won," he managed.

"Heard the riot. Figured either you won or the building
collapsed."

"Last second."

"Your goal?"

Carl nodded.

"Knew you wouldn't waste it."

The doctor glanced from one boy to the other.

"He'll need crutches for a few days," he said, "and
someone must keep that thick head of his quiet tonight.
Fortunately, he has only a mild concussion."

Lank grinned. "See? Only landed on my bean."

Doctor Murdock snorted and departed.

Outside, the crowd began chanting Carl's name. The sound came
through the walls in mighty waves.

Carl sat beside the table.

"They're giving me all the credit," he said.

"Well, you did knock in the winner."

"I wouldn't have gone through if you hadn't—"

"Aw, forget it."

"I can't forget it. You knew what I was afraid
of, and you took the smash for me."

Lank's expression grew serious.

"Maybe. But once I did, you took a few for
yourself. I saw that check on Whiz through the doorway.
Nearly shook the bandage off my bean."

Carl looked down at his scraped gloves.

"When you were lying there, I understood something. I kept
waiting for the fear to disappear before I went through
anybody. It wasn't going to disappear. I had to go
through while it was still there."

"That sounds almost intelligent."

"Don't spread it around."

"Your reputation would be ruined."

They grinned at each other.

Coach Brewster entered with Whiz Deagen behind him. Whiz had
come to inquire after Lank, and he shook the injured
boy's hand.

"That was the gamest rush I ever saw," he said.
"Three of us hit you."

"Only three?" Lank replied. "Felt like your whole school, brass
band included."

Whiz laughed. "Next year we'll try the band."

"No soap. I'll be ready for you."

Whiz turned to Carl. "And I'll be ready for you,
too."

Carl met his eyes without a trace of dread.

"I hope so."

After the Siddall captain left, Coach Brewster folded his arms.

"You boys gave me ten years' worth of gray hair
this afternoon."

"Looks distinguished, Coach," said Lank.

"It'll look more distinguished when I make you skate extra
laps next season."

"On one leg?"

"If necessary."

The coach's sternness softened.

"You played like a team when it counted. Remember that.
Stars may score the goals, but somebody has to win
the puck, clear the lane, make the pass, or take
the check. Nobody wins alone."

Carl looked at Lank.

"No, sir," he said. "Nobody does."

That evening the newspapers rushed out special editions. The headlines
were the largest Taber had seen in years.

HEMMER'S LAST-SECOND GOAL CAPS PERFECT SEASON

TABER WINS THRILLER, 2–1

BRODERICK'S BRAVE RUSH TURNS TIDE

Carl liked the third headline best.

For many seasons afterward, Taber boys argued about the greatest
play in the school's hockey history. Some chose Carl's final
charge through Whiz and the defense. Some remembered his saving
check when Whiz had only the goalie to beat. Others
insisted that the real play was Lank's rush—the long, stubborn
drive into three blue jerseys, followed by that perfect backward
pass.

Carl never argued.

Whenever someone praised his winning goal, he told the story
of the fear that had followed him from Howard, of
the blackness he could not make himself face, and of
the lanky teammate who had battered open a road at
the cost of a sprained knee and a bandaged head.

"They say I came back because I finally got my
nerve," Carl would conclude. "That isn't quite right. Lank got
it back for me."

Then he would smile at his old friend.

"All I needed was a bump—and Lank took it."

# AILEEN MAVOURNEEN

## I

My name is Aileen Mavourneen. It is an Irish name,
and means "the darling that belongs to everybody," or "sweet
sorrow," or perhaps "come into the house with muddy feet";
I have heard all three explanations from persons of education,
and consequently one of them must be right.

My mother was Irish too—not by geography, for she was
born behind a cooper's shop in Connecticut, but by extraction
and the expression of her countenance. She was a beautiful
dog, with a white breast, sorrowful brown eyes, and one
ear which stood up while the other reflected. Mother said
this gave her a distinguished appearance. I do not know
what it distinguished her from, but it was true that
no other dog in our lane had just that arrangement.

She had been educated in a minister's family and was
proud of it. She knew many long words, though their
meanings had been mislaid during a change of residence. This
did not inconvenience her, because she could make new meanings
whenever required.

"Education," she used to say, "is knowing a word that
makes the other person stop barking."

She had many such words: supererogation, synonymous, intellectual, Presbyterian, nautical,
and inflammation. She kept them polished, as you may say,
and brought them out before company. If a strange dog
looked suspiciously at her bone, she would say, "Sir, your
intentions are Presbyterian," and he would generally go away, because
nobody wishes to be called a thing he cannot bite.

Mother's religious advantages were considerable. The minister's family were Baptists
on Sunday morning, Congregationalists at the Wednesday lecture, and very
severe toward Catholics whenever the newspapers required it. Mother listened
from under the dining-room table, where theology is often clearer
than it is in a pew, because there are crumbs
there and no hats to obstruct the view. She learned
that everybody was right and everybody else was in danger,
which is a doctrine possessing much symmetry.

But she was not vain about religion. Education was her
weakness. She enjoyed having people think she knew more than
she did, and perhaps that is an innocent pleasure, since
people often enjoy having themselves think the same thing.

In matters of the heart she never pretended.

Once, when I was very little, a kitchen shelf fell.
A saucer broke, and a shard struck my paw. Mother
ran to me, licked the blood away, and trembled more
than I did. Afterward she said:

"Aileen, pain is an unintellectual commodity. It has no regard
for rank, breed, ribbons, sermons, or market price. Remember that.
When another creature suffers, you must not ask whether it
has deserved assistance. Assistance that has been deserved is merely
wages. The noblest sort is given for nothing."

"What is the noblest sort called?" I asked.

"Heroism," she said.

I had not heard the word.

"What does it mean?"

She considered, for she never answered carelessly if there was
time to invent with dignity.

"Heroism is agriculture," she said at last. "It is planting
yourself where danger is, so that somebody else may grow
safely."

I saw the beauty of this at once. Mother could
make a word bloom like a geranium.

"And does one receive a biscuit afterward?"

"One must not calculate the biscuit. If it comes, accept
it modestly. If it does not, the heroism remains synonymous."

That satisfied me, though synonymous was one of her movable
words.

She taught me other things: never to snap at a
child; never to desert a friend because the friend was
poor or wet; never to laugh at an old dog
who had lost his teeth; and never to chase a
creature merely because it ran. She herself had great courage.
When a cart horse fell in our street and men
shouted and pulled at its mouth, Mother crept under the
shafts and licked its nose until it ceased struggling. A
wheel bruised her side, yet she stayed.

The minister called it instinct.

His wife called it madness.

The horse called it nothing, being occupied; but it looked
at Mother afterward in a way that seemed to me
a complete dictionary.

There was one thing Mother feared: thunder. At the first
crash she would crawl into the coal cupboard and continue
her moral instruction from there. I once asked how bravery
could reside in a coal cupboard.

"My child," she said, "courage does not mean the absence
of fear. It means performing one's supererogation while fear is
present and making a noise."

This was perhaps the truest definition she ever made.

Our circumstances were not affluent, a word Mother used when
there was little to eat. When I was nearly grown,
the cooper sold me. I did not understand selling. A
gentleman gave him money, put a silver chain on my
collar, and led me away. I kept looking back.

Mother stood by the gate.

"Do your duty, darling!" she called. "Plant yourself!"

Then the lane turned, and I could not see her
anymore.

I cried that night. I cried softly, because the gentleman
had spoken kindly and I did not wish him to
think his purchase defective. There is a desolation in being
separated from one's mother which cannot be explained by long
words. If Mother had possessed the longest word in the
world, it would still have been too short.

## II

The gentleman was Mr. Horace Gray, a wealthy man and
a scientist. Wealthy means that there are always carpets where
one expects floors, and scientist means that a person knows
why everything happens except sometimes the things he does himself.

His house stood above the river and had white columns,
many windows, and a garden large enough to lose several
afternoons in. The servants said it contained twelve acres. I
do not know what an acre is, but there were
more smells than I could finish, which is the canine
measurement.

Mrs. Gray came down the steps when we arrived. She
had a gentle face and a voice like warm milk.

"Oh, Horace, she is lovely!"

She knelt, put both arms around my neck, and did
not ask my price, breed, age, qualifications, religion, or opinion
of science. Therefore I loved her immediately.

There were two children: Sadie, who was seven and had
yellow braids, and little Robin, the baby, who had no
useful hair at all. Sadie told me every secret she
possessed before supper. Robin placed three fingers in my mouth
and laughed when I did not bite them. From that
moment I understood that he belonged partly to me.

They gave me the name Aileen Mavourneen because Mrs. Gray
had heard it in a song. I accepted it, though
the coachman called me Leeny, the cook called me Beauty,
Sadie called me Moonbeam, and Robin called me "Ah." A
dog with a loving family must have as many names
as there are hearts to hold her.

Those were happy days. I slept on a cushion in
Mrs. Gray's room, walked with Sadie, supervised Robin's bath, and
helped the gardener by removing things he had just planted.
He did not understand that I was examining his technique.
Gardeners become attached to their mistakes.

The kitchen was governed by Nora, who was also Irish
by geography. She said I had the eyes of a
saint and the appetite of a political convention. I do
not know what convention means, but it apparently consumes sausages.
Nora and I were comrades. She saved me meat ends
and I saved her from receiving the grocer unnoticed.

Mr. Gray liked me too, in his manner. He stroked
my head at breakfast and said my skull was unusually
well formed. He often invited learned gentlemen to dinner. They
discussed intelligence, heredity, reflexes, consciousness, and whether animals could reason.
During these conversations I lay under the table and reasoned
that the gentleman with the green waistcoat was likely to
drop mutton. He did. None of the scientists predicted it.

One evening they debated whether dogs possessed souls.

"What do you think, Gray?" asked Professor Winton.

Mr. Gray tapped his glass. "We must distinguish sentiment from
evidence."

Mrs. Gray looked down at me and scratched behind my
ear.

"If Aileen has none," she said, "I shall be content
with wherever she goes."

That ended the debate for me, though the gentlemen continued
an hour.

In time I had a puppy. Oh, my puppy! He
was small and round, with foolish paws and a patch
over one eye like a pirate's flag. Sadie named him
Patrick, because Nora said every respectable Irish family required one.
Robin called him "Pah," which was near enough for love.

Patrick could not walk at first; he flowed. He rolled
against my side, searched for milk, sneezed, sighed, and slept.
I washed him until each hair knew its duty. When
his eyes opened, he looked at me with grave astonishment,
as if he had discovered the universe and found it
entirely mother-shaped.

I understood my own mother then.

Before Patrick, I had remembered her teachings as one remembers
a noble sermon: with admiration, but not always at a
convenient hour. After Patrick, every word returned warm and living.
The anxiety, the pride, the dread of pain approaching a
body softer than your own—these were not educational words. They
were blood and breath.

Robin loved Patrick. The baby would lie on the nursery
rug while the puppy climbed over him. They pulled each
other's ears without offense. If Robin cried, Patrick sat down
and howled in support; then I would lick them both,
and the nurse would say the room was a madhouse.
A madhouse is evidently a place where affection is noisy.

In the afternoons we went into the garden: Mrs. Gray
with a book, Sadie with her hoop, Robin in his
carriage, Patrick tumbling through the grass, and I moving among
them all to make certain happiness did not escape through
the hedge.

Sometimes I thought of Mother. I hoped she knew I
had found kindness. I hoped she had also found a
carpet, a child, and a cook who believed scraps to
be a religious obligation.

I taught Patrick what she had taught me.

"Never injure the helpless," I told him.

He bit my tail.

"Never take what is not yours."

He took Sadie's ribbon.

"Plant yourself where danger is."

He fell asleep with his nose in a flowerpot.

He was young. Education cannot be hurried; even scientists require
many years before they become confident.

## III

The fire came in winter, on a night of snow
and hard wind.

There had been company at dinner. The house shone with
lamps, voices, silver, and music. Afterward the grown people went
downstairs to hear Sadie play a piece upon the piano.
Robin was asleep in the upper nursery. Patrick was in
the kitchen with Nora, engaged in a bone. I lay
before the drawing-room fire, but something troubled me.

It was a smell.

At first it was small and thin, like a dark
thread drawn under a door. Then it grew.

Smoke.

I rose. Mr. Gray said, "Lie down, Aileen."

I went to the hall. The smell came from above.

I barked.

"Hush," said someone.

I barked again, ran to the stairs, and looked back.

The piano began. Sadie struck brave little notes while the
guests smiled. Nobody followed.

So I ran up alone.

Smoke crawled along the upper hall. At the nursery door
it rolled thickly, and behind it something glowed and snapped.
I had always feared fire. Even a hearth spark made
me retreat. Fire has no scent of mercy; it eats
and rejoices, speaking in a hundred little tongues.

I tried to enter and could not. Heat struck my
face. I backed away, whimpering.

Then Robin cried.

It was not a loud cry. That was worse. A
small, sleepy, puzzled sound—my baby asking why comfort had not
come.

I thought of Mother under the cart, and Mother in
the coal cupboard, saying fear may be present while courage
performs its supererogation. I thought of heroism, which was planting
yourself where danger is.

There was no time to understand the word better.

I went in.

A curtain had caught from the nursery lamp, and fire
was climbing toward the ceiling. Burning cloth fell beside me.
The crib's white hangings smoked. Robin stood holding the rail,
his face red with heat and terror.

I jumped against the crib, but its side was high.
I seized the blanket through the rails and pulled. It
tore. I pulled again until Robin toppled sideways. He screamed.
That sound nearly broke me, but breaking was a luxury.

I caught his nightdress at the shoulder.

The cloth was thin; I feared my teeth would reach
him. I held as gently as I could and dragged
him across the mattress, over the lowered corner where the
burning curtain had fallen, and down. He struck the floor.
I heard him cry again.

The room burst with a bright roar. My fur scorched.
Something hit my back.

I took his dress once more and pulled.

He was heavy—far heavier than a baby ought to be
when a house is burning. I dragged him through the
doorway, coughing and blind, then along the hall. At the
stair landing I could go no farther. My legs folded.

Voices sounded below. Feet ran.

Mr. Gray came first. He saw Robin on the floor,
his nightdress torn and blood at his shoulder where one
tooth had scratched him. He saw me crouched over the
child with my mouth open.

He did not see the fire yet.

"You brute!"

He struck me with his cane.

I tried to rise, perhaps to explain, though explanation is
difficult when one does not possess the human tongue. The
cane fell again. Pain flashed through my ribs. I cried
out.

"You have killed him!"

Another blow struck my head.

I did not understand. Mr. Gray had always been kind.
I thought perhaps the smoke had made him unable to
know me. I crawled toward Robin, for the child was
crying, but Mr. Gray kicked me away. I fell against
the banister. The world became red and black.

Then Mrs. Gray screamed—not at me, but at the fire.
The nurse rushed from the rear stairs, shouting that she
had left the lamp safe, that the curtain must have
blown across it. Nora came running with Patrick behind her.
Men carried Robin down. Others brought water and blankets.

"Look!" Mrs. Gray cried. "Look at the marks on the
floor!"

There were long tracks in the soot where Robin had
been dragged from the crib. A piece of his nightdress
was still caught between my teeth.

The nurse stared, then covered her face.

"She saved him," she said. "Oh, merciful God—she saved him!"

Mr. Gray stood above me. The cane dropped from his
hand.

I wagged my tail.

It hurt to wag it, but I wanted him to
know I understood there had been an error. Humans esteem
intelligence, and nothing makes an intelligent person more unhappy than
discovering that he has employed it backward.

He knelt.

"Aileen," he whispered.

I licked his hand.

Then I knew nothing for a while.

## IV

When I awoke, I was in Mrs. Gray's bedroom upon
the best counterpane. My side was wrapped, one eye would
not open, and each breath felt as if it had
corners. Mrs. Gray sat beside me. Robin was in her
lap, bandaged but alive.

He reached for me.

"Ah," he said.

That was my name from him, and no title given
by kings could equal it.

Mrs. Gray wept into my neck. Sadie wept on my
paws. Nora wept while bringing broth, which is a useful
arrangement because sorrow should never be allowed to interfere with
nourishment. Patrick climbed upon the bed and licked the bandage
over my eye until the doctor removed him.

Mr. Gray came last.

He looked older. He stood at the foot of the
bed and spoke to Mrs. Gray, but his eyes were
on me.

"I cannot forgive myself."

"Then ask her," said Mrs. Gray.

He came near, knelt, and touched my head very softly.

"Aileen, can you forgive me?"

Of course I could. I had forgiven him before I
fainted. Dogs do not preserve injuries as humans preserve medals.
Besides, he had believed Robin in danger. His mistake grew
from love, though it had carried a cane.

I put my paw on his wrist.

He bowed his head.

After that I became celebrated. Reporters came. A photographer made
a picture of me beside Robin, though Patrick moved during
the exposure and appeared as a cloud with a tail.
Newspapers called me "The Canine Heroine," "A Marvel of Animal
Reason," and "Faithful Aileen." One paper said I had leaped
through a wall of flame, carried Robin down two flights
of stairs, awakened the household, and then operated the fire
pump. This was not exact, but Nora said newspapers must
have exercise.

A society gave me a silver medal. It bore words
I could not read and was too large to chew.
At the ceremony a gentleman declared:

"This noble creature demonstrates that instinct, when elevated by devotion,
may imitate the highest operations of reason."

Everyone applauded.

Mr. Gray rose.

"No," he said. "She did not imitate reason. She reasoned.
She smelled danger, sought its source, attempted to summon us,
understood our stupidity, entered the burning room, and chose the
only means of rescue available. We, who pride ourselves upon
superior intelligence, failed at every earlier stage."

The scientists looked grave. Some took notes.

I was proud, not because I understood all of it,
but because Mr. Gray's hand rested on my head. Mother
had said one must not calculate the biscuit; still, when
biscuits arrive with public approval, it is courteous to eat
them.

For months I was treated like a visiting queen. I
had beef when others had chicken, a red cushion, and
permission to enter rooms previously restricted by upholstery. Visitors asked
to see my scars. Mr. Gray would tell the story,
always including his own mistake. This seemed brave to me.
It is one thing to confess that a dog is
intelligent, and another to confess she behaved more intelligently than
oneself.

Yet happiness soon returned to its ordinary dress, which is
better than its ceremonial one. Robin grew. Patrick grew. My
fur covered the burns, except for a pale mark along
my shoulder. We resumed our garden afternoons.

Patrick became a handsome young dog, though his ears disagreed
on policy. He chased butterflies, guarded the family from wheelbarrows,
and brought me objects of no apparent value: a glove,
a root, half a newspaper, one of Mr. Gray's laboratory
brushes. I praised him for each. A mother must encourage
industry before she understands its object.

Mr. Gray's laboratory occupied a stone building beyond the conservatory.
It had tall windows, shining tables, metal instruments, jars, wires,
and many smells—sharp, bitter, dead smells which did not belong
together. I disliked the place. Patrick was curious about it.

Scientists came often. They spoke of nerves and cerebral regions,
of lesions, sensory tracts, motor responses, and proof. Proof is
what scientists call a thing when they have made it
happen to someone else.

One afternoon Professor Winton saw Patrick chasing a ball.

"A quick animal," he said.

"Very," said Mr. Gray. "Remarkably responsive."

Professor Winton bent and felt Patrick's skull.

"An excellent subject."

I heard the words but did not understand them. Subject,
in the house, meant a topic discussed after soup. Patrick
was certainly an excellent topic. I wagged my tail.

Mr. Gray looked at me, then away.

"The family is attached to him," he said.

"That is sentiment."

"Yes."

"And science cannot wait upon sentiment."

"No," said Mr. Gray slowly. "Of course not."

I supposed they were discussing dinner.

## V

In the spring Mrs. Gray took Sadie and Robin to
visit their grandmother. They would be gone ten days. Nora
accompanied them, because the grandmother's cook could not make bread
that rose with Christian conviction.

Before leaving, Mrs. Gray embraced me.

"Take care of Horace," she said.

Sadie made me promise to sleep on her bed. Robin
kissed my nose. Patrick tried to enter the carriage twice
and was returned with laughter.

"Guard the house, Pah!" Robin called.

We watched until the carriage disappeared.

The house became too large. Rooms do this when children
leave them.

Mr. Gray remained, and several scientific gentlemen came to stay.
There was Professor Winton, Dr. Greeley, and a young man
who smelled of tobacco and uneasiness. They spent long hours
in the laboratory.

On the second morning, Mr. Gray called Patrick.

Patrick ran to him joyfully.

I followed.

"No, Aileen," Mr. Gray said. "Stay."

I sat.

Patrick looked back at me from the laboratory door. His
tail waved. Then the door closed.

At first I heard voices. A metal tray clinked. Patrick
gave a small questioning bark.

I went to the door and scratched.

The young man came out. He nearly stepped on me.

"You ought to shut her in the house," he said.

"Why?" asked Mr. Gray from within.

"She knows."

"Nonsense. Animals do not anticipate an operation."

The word operation sounded nautical to me. Mother had said
nautical meant "conducted with ropes and authority." Perhaps they were
teaching Patrick a useful performance.

The young man pushed me away with his foot and
closed the door.

A little later Patrick cried out.

It was one sharp cry. Then another, weaker.

I barked and threw myself against the door. Jenkins, the
butler, came running.

"What are they doing in there?"

The door opened a crack. Professor Winton's face appeared.

"Remove this dog."

Jenkins looked pale. "Sir, is it the pup?"

"This is important work."

Patrick cried again.

I bit the edge of the door and pulled. Someone
struck my muzzle with a rod. Jenkins seized my collar.

"Come away, girl," he whispered. "Oh, come away."

I fought him. I had never fought a servant, but
Patrick needed me. Jenkins dragged me to the pantry and
shut me in. I scratched until my paws bled. I
called and called. No answer came.

Hours passed.

At last Jenkins opened the door. His eyes were red.
I rushed outside.

The laboratory door stood open. The tables had been washed,
but water cannot erase every smell. I found Patrick upon
a folded cloth in the corner.

He was alive.

I licked his face. His eyes were open, but they
did not turn toward me. A bandage circled his head.
He tried to stand and fell.

"My baby," I said in our speech. "I am here."

His tail moved once.

The scientists spoke behind me.

"The result is decisive," said Professor Winton. "Destruction of the
indicated area produces complete cortical blindness."

"Pupillary response remains," said Dr. Greeley.

"Then the distinction is established."

The young man said, "He is in pain."

Mr. Gray answered, "Not consciously, perhaps."

Patrick whimpered.

I lay beside him and warmed him. If he could
not see me, he could smell me. If he could
not stand, I would not stand. His head pressed under
my chin as it had when he was new and
round and the entire universe was mother-shaped.

Mr. Gray approached.

"Aileen," he said.

I looked up.

He did not meet my eyes. He carried a small
glass instrument.

"The animal cannot recover," said Professor Winton. "Finish it."

Mr. Gray knelt and put a hand on Patrick's side.

I thought he meant to help. He had been mistaken
once, during the fire, but he knew better now. He
had praised my reason before important people. He loved Robin.
He had asked my forgiveness.

Surely intelligence, once awakened, does not go back to sleep.

There was a prick in Patrick's leg.

He sighed.

For a little while he breathed very slowly. Then he
became still.

I waited for him to wake.

The gentlemen removed their coats and spoke of the experiment.
Professor Winton congratulated Mr. Gray. Dr. Greeley said the paper
would attract notice abroad. The young man went outside and
was sick beside the hedge.

At evening Jenkins came with a box.

"No," he said when I stood over Patrick. "Please, girl."

He lifted my puppy and laid him inside. Patrick's body
bent strangely, without helping itself. I licked his ear. It
was growing cool.

"He is sleeping," I told Jenkins, though he did not
understand.

Jenkins covered his face.

Then he carried the box to the garden.

## VI

The gardener had gone to his sister's wedding, so Jenkins
dug the hole. He chose a place beneath the pear
tree, where Patrick and Robin had played.

I watched closely.

At first I was alarmed when he placed the box
in the earth. Then I understood.

They were planting Patrick.

It was a scientific matter, perhaps, or agricultural heroism. Everything
in gardens was put underground before it rose. Bulbs went
down and lilies came up. Seeds vanished and returned as
flowers. Once the gardener had buried pieces of potato, and
many potatoes emerged. It was one of nature's most generous
supererogations.

Patrick had grown blind, and Mr. Gray had planted him
so he could grow new eyes.

My confidence returned.

Jenkins filled the hole. I thanked him by licking his
hand. This made him sit suddenly on the grass and
cry.

"Don't, Aileen," he said. "For God's sake, don't thank me."

But why not? He had selected a pleasant place. The
pear tree would shade Patrick when he came up.

I lay on the fresh earth to keep it warm.

Jenkins brought supper. I did not eat. A mother should
be present when her child sprouts; otherwise he may be
frightened by the change.

Night came cold. Jenkins tried to lead me inside.

"Come, girl. He's gone."

Gone? Certainly not. I could smell him below me.

I resisted, and Jenkins fetched a blanket. He spread it
over me and left the plate nearby.

The next morning Mr. Gray came.

His face was gray too, which may be how the
family received its name. He stood by the mound.

"Aileen."

I wagged my tail.

"You must come away."

I looked down at the earth, then up at him,
to explain. He was a scientist. He would know how
long these things required.

He crouched and touched the soil.

"I did not think," he said. "I would not let
myself think."

This was an unintellectual remark for a man of reason.

I licked his hand, because he seemed distressed.

He tried to pull me away. I growled—not fiercely, only
enough to make my duty clear. Patrick might emerge at
any moment.

Mr. Gray left.

For several days they brought food. I ate a little
at first, very quickly, without leaving the mound. Then appetite
became troublesome and I dismissed it. Water was enough. Later,
water was an effort too.

Rain came. It softened the ground, and I became excited.
Rain helps things grow. I watched for the first sign:
perhaps a paw, perhaps one black-tipped ear pushing upward like
a leaf.

Nothing appeared.

I remembered that large plants take longer than small ones.
An oak is not impatient merely because a radish has
advantages.

I spoke to Patrick through the soil.

"Do not be afraid, my darling. Your mother is here.
Push upward when you are ready. The air is mild.
Robin will soon be home."

Sometimes I thought I heard him. A rustle, a faint
stirring beneath the roots. I dug a little, then stopped,
afraid of damaging the new growth. Mother had said heroism
was agriculture, but she had not explained its schedules.

Jenkins sat near me each evening.

"They come home Saturday," he said once. "How shall we
tell them?"

The housemaid, Elsie, began to cry.

"The children will ask for the pup first thing."

"And Mrs. Gray will ask why Aileen's like this."

I raised my head at my name.

Jenkins stroked me. "She saved the baby, and this is
what we've done to her."

They had done nothing to me. I was merely waiting.

Mr. Gray no longer came to the garden. I saw
him sometimes at the laboratory window, standing motionless. The other
scientists departed with papers and handshakes. Professor Winton said the
demonstration had been a complete success.

Success is a strange human word. It may mean applause
in one room and silence under a pear tree.

On Friday I attempted to stand and could not. My
legs had become independent and declined the invitation. This did
not matter. I laid my head upon the mound.

The sun warmed the soil.

I dreamed of Mother. She stood at the old gate
with one ear up and the other reflecting.

"You have planted yourself," she said.

"Is this heroism?"

"It is love," she answered. "Education often confuses the two,
but the heart does not."

I asked when Patrick would grow.

Mother looked at me with her sorrowful brown eyes, and
for once she did not make a definition.

I woke at dusk. Jenkins had placed flowers on the
mound. That was considerate; perhaps they would encourage Patrick by
example.

Far down the road came the sound of carriage wheels.

The servants heard it. Elsie ran from the house. Jenkins
rose and removed his hat.

"They're early," he whispered.

The wheels drew nearer. I heard Sadie laughing. Robin's voice
called something I could not make out. Then, clearer:

"Ah! Pah! Ah!"

My tail moved against the grass.

They were home.

I tried once more to rise, because it was my
duty to meet them at the steps. My body would
not obey. I had waited too long, perhaps, and become
rooted. This seemed suitable. Things planted together ought to remain
near one another.

The carriage stopped.

Doors opened. Feet hurried over gravel.

"Where's Aileen?" Sadie cried. "Where's Patrick?"

No one answered.

I wished to spare them the anxiety. Patrick was safe
beneath me and would surely come up soon—strong, sighted, and
handsome, with his ears perhaps agreeing at last. I would
explain everything if they came close enough: the operation, the
planting, the rain, the patience required by agriculture.

Robin called again.

"Ah!"

I lifted my head a little.

There he was at the end of the path, running
toward me, his arms open. Behind him came Sadie and
Mrs. Gray. They saw the mound. They saw the flowers.
They saw me.

Mrs. Gray stopped.

Jenkins said, "Madam—"

But his voice broke.

Robin reached me first. He knelt and put his warm
hands around my face.

"Ah," he said softly.

I licked one finger.

Then I rested my nose upon Patrick's earth and listened.

The garden was very quiet, but I was not discouraged.
Seeds know their hour. Love waits. And Mother had always
said the noblest work is done without calculating the biscuit.

So I waited.

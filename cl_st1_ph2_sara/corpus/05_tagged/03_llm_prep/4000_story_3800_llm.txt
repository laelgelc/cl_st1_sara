### THE HEARING EAR

The most comfortable room occupied by Company D was situated
twenty-two feet beneath an orchard in which no tree had
borne fruit since 1915. Indeed, most of the trees had
ceased to be trees in any botanical sense, and survived
as splintered posts upon which the German artillery hung, at
intervals, festoons of wire, earth, smoke, and portions of the
French landscape.

The room possessed central heating—a perforated oil drum which smoked
when lighted and smelled when extinguished—ladder-stairs, an upholstered packing-case, and
a salon furnished with four bunks, seven rifles, a broken
mirror, and a table constructed from a château door. The
walls had been decorated by a previous tenant with ladies
clipped from Parisian journals. Damp had softened their complexions and
enlarged their costumes. When wet, the roof dropped mud; when
dry, dust. At all times it dropped insects.

Yet the place was home. Men entered it from the
communication trench as from a storm, shook themselves, cursed the
weather, and asked whether coffee had been made.

On an evening in February, when the front was unusually
quiet and therefore suspected of some enormous indiscretion, three American
soldiers sat about the oil drum debating the future of
civilization.

Phipps-Herrick, who began the debate, was tall, broad, and offensively
healthy. He came from that portion of Philadelphia which is
commonly described by its inhabitants not as a neighbourhood but
as a state of mind. Before the war he had
sold electrical equipment, played a competent game of squash, and
belonged to two clubs in which he knew everyone and
liked almost nobody. His opinions were large, direct, and designed
for immediate delivery.

"After this affair is cleaned up," he said, "the German
language ought to be abolished."

He spoke with the confidence of a man recommending the
removal of a condemned warehouse.

Across the drum sat Rosenlaube, whose name contradicted nearly all
his convictions. His grandfather had emigrated from Baden, his father
manufactured buttons in New York, and Rosenlaube himself had escaped
from both buttons and Baden by attending Harvard and becoming
decorative. He was slender, pale, and possessed a drawl which
made even profanity sound privately educated. Before receiving a commission
and losing it through an unfortunate difference with a major,
he had written dramatic criticism for a weekly paper. The
paper had ceased publication shortly afterward, but Rosenlaube denied responsibility.

"I agree with you," he said. "Eliminate it root and
branch. It is a language constructed for drilling reservists and
frightening horses."

Private Mitchell looked up from a book.

Mitchell was the least impressive of the three until something
required to be done. He had taught modern languages in
a small college in Ohio, where his students called every
course outside their own department a "soft snap," and where
the faculty maintained an Unsocial Socialists' Club whose members met
monthly to condemn organization. Mitchell had studied two years in
Europe, spoke French without insulting France, and read German, Italian,
and a little Russian. He was short, brown, quiet, and
had already brought in a wounded corporal under fire—a circumstance
he had neglected to mention and which the corporal had
been obliged to report.

"What would you substitute?" Mitchell asked.

"For German?" said Phipps-Herrick. "Nothing. Silence would be an improvement."

"Very good," Rosenlaube murmured. "A silent Germany. There is an
idea with genuine charm."

Mitchell closed his book upon one finger. It was a
battered volume of Heine, though he had prudently covered it
with the jacket of a manual on grenade fuses.

"You mean," he said, "that no one should be allowed
to speak German after the war?"

"Exactly."

"Sixty or seventy million people?"

"They can learn English."

"The English may object."

"Then French."

"The French certainly will."

Phipps-Herrick waved away the difficulty. "I don't care what they
learn. Let them invent something. But wipe out German. It
has done enough damage."

"The German Army has done damage," Mitchell said. "The German
Government has done damage. High explosives have done damage. A
language hasn't."

"That," declared Rosenlaube, "is the professor's fallacy. He distinguishes between
the wolf and its howl."

"And you," Mitchell returned, "want to hang the howl."

The argument had started, as such arguments usually did, with
no serious prospect of altering a human opinion. Each man
approached it as a college athlete approaches an annual contest:
the exercise was wholesome, defeat unthinkable, and noise desirable. Outside,
the trenches dripped. Somewhere behind the lines a mule objected
to military life. Farther away a machine gun tried three
notes and stopped.

Phipps-Herrick thrust two large boots toward the stove.

"Language is an instrument," he said. "The Germans have used
theirs to preach conquest, race superiority, obedience, frightfulness—all the poison.
Break the instrument."

"Surgeons use knives," said Mitchell. "So do assassins."

"Cheap debating point."

"Cheap points are sometimes sharp."

Rosenlaube sighed. "My dear Mitchell, you are being sentimental over
grammar. German culture produced the present German. There is your
evidence."

"All of him?"

"Enough of him."

"Did Goethe produce poison gas? Did Beethoven sink hospital ships?"

"Beethoven made a great deal of noise," said Rosenlaube. "I
have never trusted him."

Mitchell smiled. "You used to praise the Ninth Symphony."

"That was before the war."

"The notes haven't changed."

"My ears have."

This pleased Phipps-Herrick, who slapped the table. A candle fell
over, was caught by Mitchell, and resumed its office with
the air of a servant accustomed to family quarrels.

Phipps-Herrick said, "There's practical sense in Rosenlaube's position. Make English
the language of America and keep it English. No German
newspapers, schools, street signs, sermons, clubs, or opera."

"I'll go part of the way," Mitchell answered. "People who
come to America should learn English. Public business should be
conducted in it. Children should not be raised in a
separate linguistic colony. That is civic sense. But burning books,
closing libraries, refusing to teach a useful language—that is civic
lunacy."

"Useful!" Rosenlaube repeated. "There it is again. Useful for what?"

"For chemistry, medicine, music, philosophy, history."

"Also for giving orders to U-boats."

"English can be used for piracy. French can be used
for treason. Latin has justified more tyranny than German ever
dreamed of."

"Latin is dead."

"And therefore respectable?"

Phipps-Herrick leaned forward. "Look here, Professor. We are not discussing
philology in a faculty parlour. We are sitting in mud
because men who speak German put us here."

"The French speak French, and they are sitting in more
mud than we are."

"Because the Germans put them there!"

"Precisely. Men—not words."

A rat appeared behind Rosenlaube's shoulder and regarded the assembly
with cool disapproval. Rosenlaube removed a boot and threw it.
The rat departed; the boot struck the wall; a shower
of earth descended upon Phipps-Herrick's head.

"There," said Mitchell, brushing dust from the table. "An excellent
demonstration. You misused the boot. Shall we abolish footwear?"

"Don't get academic," said Phipps-Herrick.

"I am naturally academic. You might as well ask Barker
Bunn not to be military."

As if summoned by criticism, Sergeant Barker Bunn descended the
ladder.

He came feet first, bringing with him a steel helmet,
a wet mackintosh, and an atmosphere of official calamity. In
civil life Barker Bunn had been assistant manager of a
sporting-goods establishment in Chicago. In military life he had become
a complete institution. He saluted lieutenants in shell holes, shaved
under bombardment, and called every soldier "Mister" when most enraged.

"Who wants to go out on a listening patrol?" he
demanded.

The three disputants stared at him.

Bunn removed his helmet. Rain ran from its rim down
his nose, where it remained, owing perhaps to discipline.

"Intelligence thinks Fritz is mining under the forward sap. There's
been tapping heard near the dead ground beyond Petit Crater.
We want four men to go out, lie doggo, and
listen. On the Q. T., gentlemen. No shooting unless you're
stepped on. If they're working a mine, we want to
know where."

"Tonight?" asked Phipps-Herrick.

"No, Mister Phipps-Herrick. Next Fourth of July, with flags. Of
course tonight."

Phipps-Herrick stood. "I'll go."

Rosenlaube retrieved his boot. "Include me. I should like to
hear German industry at close range before we abolish it."

Mitchell slipped the book into his tunic. "I'll go too."

Bunn looked from one to another. "This ain't a debating
society."

"That," said Mitchell, "is why we volunteered."

At eleven-thirty they reported at the forward bay, where Lieutenant
Sargent gave them instructions. Bunn would lead. They were to
pass through a gap in the American wire, crawl to
Petit Crater, move north along its western lip, and listen
near three shell holes marked upon a map with red
crosses. No Man's Land was scarcely two hundred yards wide
there, but width, like time, assumes new properties when measured
under machine guns.

"Back before three," Sargent said. "If you hear mining, locate
it as nearly as possible. Don't be heroic."

This final direction, frequently issued in war, was seldom useful.
A man cannot determine beforehand whether a given crawl through
darkness will be called heroism, stupidity, or failure. That distinction
is made afterward, often by persons who remained behind.

The rain had stopped. A wind moved low across the
torn fields and carried smells of wet chalk, decay, cordite,
and smoke. The four men blackened their faces, fastened cloth
about anything likely to shine, and climbed out one by
one.

Bunn went first, then Mitchell, Rosenlaube, and Phipps-Herrick. The order
had no social significance; even Philadelphia yields precedence to a
sergeant in No Man's Land.

They crept through the wire.

Behind them the American trench disappeared, not because it was
distant but because the earth had swallowed it. Before them
lay a darkness interrupted by paler darkness—shell holes filled with
water, abandoned sandbags, coils of wire, and the chalky faces
of the dead. A flare rose from the German line.
It hung above the field with intolerable leisure.

"Freeze," breathed Bunn.

They froze.

Under the greenish light each man became suddenly enormous to
himself. Mitchell felt the beat of his heart against the
ground. Rosenlaube's hand rested in mud inches from a dead
man's sleeve. Phipps-Herrick stared at a tuft of grass and
believed it to be moving toward him. The flare sank.
Darkness shut again.

They crawled.

At Petit Crater they stopped and listened. Listening at the
front was not passive. It was a labour of the
entire body. An elbow pressed too sharply into stone became
thunder; a buckle seemed a bell; one's breath was a
locomotive. Beyond these private noises came the public sounds of
war—the thump of distant guns, the whisper of a star
shell, the plop of mud falling from a crater wall,
the occasional nervous crack of a rifle.

Bunn lowered an ear to the ground.

After several minutes he shook his head and moved north.

At the first shell hole they heard nothing. At the
second Mitchell thought he detected a faint, regular tapping, but
it ceased before the others could confirm it. They crawled
farther, edging perilously close to the German wire. Here the
ground was crowded with wreckage left by an attack six
weeks before: rifles, packs, boards, helmets, and men whom no
patrol had been able to bring in.

Rosenlaube touched Mitchell's ankle.

"What?" Mitchell whispered.

Rosenlaube pointed.

Half under a shattered plank lay a black object with
a cord attached. Bunn crawled back. Together they uncovered it.
It was a field-telephone receiver, dropped or abandoned, its wire
running toward the German lines.

Bunn raised his eyebrows.

The receiver was alive. From it came a minute insect
buzzing, then a voice—thin, metallic, unmistakably human.

Bunn held it to his ear. His expression altered from
surprise to irritation. He passed it to Phipps-Herrick, who listened
fiercely, as though intensity might translate.

"German," he breathed.

Rosenlaube took it next.

His grandfather had spoken German, and his father still used
several domestic phrases, chiefly when angered by bills; but Rosenlaube's
knowledge did not extend beyond food, endearments, and certain expressions
unsuitable for a telephone report. He heard rapid words, an
occasional number, and the hard recurrence of military consonants.

"Can't make it out," he whispered.

Mitchell extended his hand.

The receiver was cold and muddy against his ear. At
first the voice seemed blurred by distance and apparatus. Then
his trained attention separated syllables, restored endings, supplied what static
removed. A second man answered. They were not talking to
the patrol; the receiver had apparently been spliced into, or
left connected with, a local circuit.

Mitchell listened.

The mine gallery beneath the forward sap had struck water.
Work on the original direction had been suspended. A new
gallery was being driven southeast, toward the American bombing post
near Petit Crater. Charges would not be ready for two
nights.

Then the subject changed.

At two-forty, twelve men under a Leutnant Kassner would pass
through the wire. They would raid the American advanced trench
after a bombardment from Minenwerfer and machine guns. Their purpose
was prisoners and identification. A separate covering party would occupy
the crater. Mitchell caught the names of two saps, the
signal—three red lights—and the warning that American patrols had recently
been active.

He lowered the receiver.

"Well?" Bunn's lips barely moved.

Mitchell whispered the information. As he spoke, the theoretical discussion
in the dugout acquired a quality almost vulgar in its
irony. German, condemned at eight o'clock as useless, had become
by one o'clock the most valuable possession carried by any
man in Company D. Phipps-Herrick did not comment. Rosenlaube did
not drawl. They looked at Mitchell and listened.

Bunn cut the telephone wire and tucked the receiver under
a dead man's pack.

"We've got to get back," he said.

They turned south.

The enemy flare came without warning. It burst directly overhead,
brilliant and hissing.

"Down!"

They flattened themselves. From the German wire a voice shouted.
Another answered. A rifle cracked; then three rifles; then a
machine gun opened, stitching the darkness with level flashes.

"Cut away!" Bunn shouted.

Stealth ended. They scrambled for Petit Crater.

Bullets struck the ground with vicious, intimate slaps. Phipps-Herrick plunged
into a shell hole, climbed out, and ran bent double.
Mitchell crawled over a ridge of chalk. Rosenlaube, behind him,
gave a startled grunt and toppled sideways.

Mitchell turned.

Rosenlaube lay near the crater lip, one leg twisted beneath
him. Phipps-Herrick came back at once. Between them they dragged
him into the hollow. Bunn dropped beside them as bullets
whipped above the rim.

"Hit?"

"Thigh," Rosenlaube said. His voice was faint but still ceremonious.
"Possibly elsewhere. The German language appears to have called artillery."

Mitchell found the wound. Blood ran heavily from the upper
leg. He tied a field dressing tight and added a
tourniquet.

A shell crashed beyond the crater. Earth fell upon them.

"We can't carry him and make time," Bunn said.

"You're not leaving him," Phipps-Herrick answered.

"No. We hide him. Get the information in. Come back
with a party."

Rosenlaube gripped Phipps-Herrick's sleeve. "That is sensible. I dislike it,
but it is sensible."

There was a recess under the western wall, shielded by
two timbers and the body of a German soldier. They
dragged Rosenlaube into it, gave him a revolver, and covered
his tunic with a strip of muddy sacking.

"I shall be extremely quiet," he said. "It is the
first military accomplishment for which I have shown real genius."

Phipps-Herrick squeezed his shoulder. "We'll be back."

"Bring coffee. And, if convenient, a different war."

They crawled on.

The machine gun shifted its fire. Bunn crossed the open
ground first. Mitchell followed. Halfway to the next depression, he
felt a blow at his knee like the stroke of
an iron bar. His arms gave way. He rolled, tried
to rise, and fell again.

Phipps-Herrick reached him.

"Hit?"

"Knee."

"Can you crawl?"

Mitchell attempted it. The leg would not answer; pain spread
upward in hot, sickening waves.

Bunn crawled back. "We're a hundred yards from our wire."

"Take the information," Mitchell said. "I'll tell you exactly."

He began repeating the details, but a burst of fire
drowned half the words.

Bunn shook his head. "I didn't get those sap names."

"Brunnenweg and Krähen-Sappe."

"What?"

Mitchell repeated them.

Bunn stared at him. "You've got to report it yourself.
I'll cover."

Phipps-Herrick knelt with his back toward Mitchell.

"Get on."

"You can't carry me across that."

"Don't be academic."

It was the phrase Phipps-Herrick used when reason became inconvenient.

Mitchell put his arms about the larger man's shoulders. Phipps-Herrick
rose, staggered, adjusted the weight, and moved forward. He could
not run. He lurched from hollow to hollow while Bunn
crawled beside them and fired twice toward the German flashes.

The field seemed to lengthen. Mud clung to Phipps-Herrick's boots.
His breath came harshly. Mitchell felt every step in his
shattered knee and tried not to cry out. Another flare
rose. Phipps-Herrick stood exposed beneath it, carrying a man upon
his back like some laborious figure from an old legend—except
that the legend wore a dented helmet, swore continuously, and
had lost one puttee.

The machine gun fired.

Bunn's rifle answered. American sentries, hearing the commotion, began shooting
over them. Someone in the trench shouted a challenge.

"Company D!" Bunn yelled. "Patrol coming in! Hold your fire!"

They reached the wire gap.

Hands appeared from darkness. Mitchell was pulled down into the
trench. Phipps-Herrick followed and sat abruptly against the wall, too
exhausted to speak.

Lieutenant Sargent bent over Mitchell.

"Where's Rosenlaube?"

"Petit Crater—west side—wounded," Mitchell said. "Send a party. But listen
first."

He made his report without ornament. The gallery, the new
direction, the hour of the raid, the strength, the covering
party, the signal, the sap names—everything was repeated while a
runner took notes. Only when Sargent assured him that the
message had gone to battalion did Mitchell permit the stretcher-bearers
to touch his knee.

"And the telephone?" Sargent asked.

"Still connected when we found it."

Phipps-Herrick, recovering speech, said, "He understood the whole business."

"I gathered that."

"In German."

"I gathered that too."

Phipps-Herrick looked offended by the lieutenant's composure. A private philosophical
revolution had occurred, and the Army treated it as an
item of intelligence.

The rescue party went out within twenty minutes. Barker Bunn
led it, though his sleeve had been cut by a
bullet and his face was bleeding from chalk splinters. They
found Rosenlaube unconscious but alive. The revolver was still in
his hand; the promised coffee was not, a lapse for
which he later reproached them.

The German raid arrived at two-forty precisely. It found the
advanced trench apparently damaged and nearly deserted. The twelve raiders
entered; the covering party occupied Petit Crater; three red lights
climbed above the German line.

Then the Americans answered.

Artillery dropped behind the enemy's forward positions, cutting communication trenches
and sealing the routes of retirement. Hidden automatic rifles opened
from both flanks. A reserve company, moved into place upon
Mitchell's report, swept around the crater. The projected raid became
first a trap, then a confused local action, and finally
one of those small triumphs which headquarters enlarged upon maps
and men remembered by screams.

By dawn nearly two hundred Germans had surrendered from the
isolated forward works. Three machine guns were taken, together with
papers, prisoners, and confirmation of the mine gallery. American engineers
counter-mined that afternoon. The explosive intended for Company D remained
forever buried beneath the innocent French earth.

Military rewards move both more slowly and more quickly than
military disasters. Within a week Barker Bunn was promoted, Phipps-Herrick
was recommended for a decoration, and Mitchell, who protested that
he had merely listened, was recommended for another. Rosenlaube received
mention for remaining quietly where he had been placed—an achievement
he claimed had required the finest courage of all.

They were reunited in a base hospital three weeks later.

The hospital occupied a former convent whose white walls, clean
sheets, and polished floors seemed to the men more luxurious
than Versailles. Mitchell lay with his knee in an apparatus
of pulleys and weights. Rosenlaube, propped in a wheeled chair,
wore a blue dressing gown and had recovered enough strength
to criticize the soup. Phipps-Herrick arrived on crutches, having discovered
after the patrol that a bullet had grazed his calf.

For several minutes they discussed wounds, nurses, medals, and the
criminal weakness of hospital tobacco. Then Rosenlaube noticed the book
upon Mitchell's blanket.

"Heine?" he asked.

Mitchell displayed the cover.

Rosenlaube regarded it gravely. "In German?"

"In German."

"A language," Phipps-Herrick said, "constructed for drilling reservists and frightening
horses."

"And for explaining raids," Mitchell added.

There was a pause.

Outside, an orderly pushed a cart along the corridor. Bells
sounded from the town; somewhere in the courtyard two convalescents
quarrelled over a newspaper. The war continued, vast and unreasonable,
but in that clean room three men possessed, for the
moment, leisure to revise themselves.

Rosenlaube folded his thin hands across the dressing gown.

"Well," he said, "I withdraw one point."

"Which point?" asked Mitchell.

"That German is useless."

"Only one?"

"One must not surrender an entire position without terms." Rosenlaube
smiled. "I still dislike the language. I reserve the right
to abolish portions of it—especially the verbs. But I concede
that, now and then, it is remarkably handy to know
what the other fellow is saying."

Phipps-Herrick grunted. "You can teach it after the war, Mitchell.
Quietly."

"I intend to."

"To Americans?"

"To anybody willing to learn."

Phipps-Herrick considered this. "Teach them English too."

"Certainly."

"And no German propaganda."

"Certainly."

"And none of that race-superiority rot."

"Certainly."

Phipps-Herrick looked relieved. "Then we understand each other."

Mitchell opened his book.

They did not entirely understand one another; people seldom do,
and friends perhaps less often than they suppose. But Phipps-Herrick
had carried Mitchell through gunfire, Mitchell had bandaged Rosenlaube under
it, Barker Bunn had returned for Rosenlaube, and all four
had listened when listening mattered. This was not a complete
basis for reconstructing civilization, but it was better than many
programs offered by statesmen.

Rosenlaube wheeled himself nearer the bed.

"Read us something," he said.

"In German?"

"If necessary."

Mitchell began, translating as he went. Phipps-Herrick pretended not to
listen. Rosenlaube interrupted twice to dispute the poet and once
to correct the translation incorrectly. Presently the winter sunlight reached
across the convent floor and touched their bandages, their medals,
and the scarred little volume.

Beyond the windows France waited for spring.

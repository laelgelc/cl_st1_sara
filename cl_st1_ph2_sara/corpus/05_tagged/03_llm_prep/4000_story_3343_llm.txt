### The Red Signet

From the terrace of Windycroft the county appeared to have
been arranged for the benefit of persons who disliked reality.
The hills declined in gracious folds; a white road wandered
among beeches without betraying where it went; and, very far
below, a hay wagon moved across a field with the
solemn minuteness of an insect upon a green carpet. Clouds,
leisurely and magnificent, possessed the western sky.

Within the long music room, three tables of bridge occupied
the afternoon. Cards fell with little slaps; bracelets tinkled; cigarette
smoke drifted toward the marble Beethoven, whose expression suggested that
society had disappointed him without surprising him.

"I hear Olive Nettleton is receiving again," said Mrs. Baxter,
making lost the rubber with the serenity of one who
had never yet admitted a defeat.

Lady Wrench looked over her cards. "Receiving whom?"

"Everybody. That is what receiving again means."

"My dear Agatha, one may receive everybody for years without
receiving anybody."

Mrs. Baxter smiled. She was a languid shadow of some
brilliant and devastating yesterday; silver had entered her hair, but
age had not yet ventured upon her eyes. "Olive has
received her husband, which is more adventurous. Husbands, unlike everybody,
stay to breakfast."

At the second table a young man in white flannels
laughed too loudly. His partner frowned at him.

"Do attend, Peter," she said. "Gossip is no excuse for
bad bridge."

"Gossip," said Mrs. Baxter, "is the feminine equivalent of a
smoking-room story. Both depend upon somebody else's catastrophe, and both
are ruined by accuracy."

Beyond the French windows, Caroline Summers stood at the stone
balustrade, apparently intent upon the horizon. Her dress was white,
without ornament except for a narrow silver girdle; the stillness
of her slender figure gave her the air of something
carved from a colder substance than the sunlit stone. Only
the corners of her mouth betrayed fatigue. They had forgotten,
during the last four years, the upward turn which had
once made strangers think her amused.

Osborne Kingsley came out carrying two glasses.

"Mrs. Vane says you are to drink this."

"Mrs. Vane is always ordering people to live. It is
her form of tyranny."

"She says it is lemonade."

"That makes it worse."

He set one glass beside her. He was brown, broad-shouldered,
and insufficiently polished to be entirely at ease among persons
who had reduced sincerity to a social error. He had
been at school with Caroline's brother; had rowed, shot, failed
to appreciate Debussy, and loved Caroline for six years with
the stubbornness of a man who regarded constancy not as
an accomplishment but as a condition.

"You have been out here alone for half an hour,"
he said.

"I have been with the view."

"The view is poor company."

"It asks no questions."

Kingsley rested his hands upon the balustrade. "I shall."

"I know."

Below them, the hay wagon passed beneath a line of
elms and vanished. Caroline watched the empty road it had
left.

Inside, Lady Wrench said, "Four years, wasn't it?"

"Four years and three months," replied Mrs. Baxter. "Olive counted
the months. Women count what men forget."

"Where had Mr. Nettleton been?"

"My dear, if anyone knew that, Olive would be less
interesting."

Their voices, passing through the open windows, seemed at first
merely another summer sound—the click of cards, the hum of
bees against the heliotrope, the thin protests of a badly
handled teacup.

Kingsley looked at Caroline's profile.

"Will you marry me?"

She did not move. "You ask it like a man
calling for the bill."

"I have asked it in every other manner."

"Yes. Once among the daffodils, once at Cowes, and once
beside that appalling fountain in Rome."

"The fountain was your choice."

"One cannot refuse a man permanently beside running water. It
becomes theatrical."

"You refused me."

"I postponed you."

"For four years."

At that, she turned. Her eyes were grey, clear, and
singularly tired.

"Osborne, don't."

"I must. Your mother has asked me to take you
to the Riviera in October. Your brother thinks I ought
to take you to Scotland. Half London thinks I ought
to take you somewhere. I should prefer to take you
home."

"A home is not a geographical convenience."

"No. Nor is a grave."

The whiteness of her face seemed suddenly sharpened by the
light.

"You have no right."

"I know. Rights are what one discusses when there is
no feeling in the matter."

She looked away again. For a moment his hand moved
toward hers, then stopped.

"Caroline, Hugh Arden is gone."

"Gone is not dead."

"He vanished on the morning of your wedding. He left
no letter, no message, no trace. The police searched. Your
brother searched. I searched."

"I did not ask you."

"You did not have to."

"Four years," she said softly. "It is a very little
while when one has not understood a day of it."

"There may be nothing to understand."

"There is always something."

"He may have funked it."

"No."

"He may have been killed."

"Then let me know it."

"He may have chosen to disappear."

She closed her eyes. "Then let me know that."

Kingsley turned abruptly from her and went into the music
room. For a moment Caroline heard a discord struck upon
the piano, followed by the twang of a guitar string.
It was characteristic of Osborne that, denied the privilege of
breaking a man's neck, he should assault two innocent instruments.

Mrs. Baxter glanced up.

"Osborne, if you are going to express yourself, do it
in the shrubbery."

"I beg your pardon."

"Never beg for what everyone is delighted to grant."

He stood by the empty fireplace, beneath a marble bust
of Mozart. The little white face above him wore the
composure of genius and death.

At the card table, Lady Wrench gathered the trick.

"Did Gregory Nettleton simply walk into the house?" she asked.

"Not simply," said Mrs. Baxter. "Nothing so dramatic is ever
simple. He arrived at seven in the morning, without luggage,
wearing a suit which had plainly belonged to a larger
and less prosperous gentleman, and rang the bell of his
own front door."

"And Olive opened it?"

"The housemaid. Providence is seldom so considerate of effect as
a novelist. The maid screamed; the butler dropped a tray;
and Olive came down in a peignoir which, I am
told, was extremely becoming."

"How thoughtful of her."

"Olive has always dressed for calamity. It is the only
occasion on which one can be certain of an audience."

There was laughter. Osborne's face hardened, but he remained where
he was, held by the odd brutality of the tale.

Caroline had come as far as the French windows. The
muslin curtain moved against her shoulder, now concealing, now revealing
her.

"What did he say?" asked Peter.

"He said, 'Good morning, Olive.'"

"That was all?"

"After four years one must begin somewhere."

"But where had he been?"

Mrs. Baxter placed a cigarette in her long holder, though
she did not light it. "He does not know."

"Oh, come!"

"That was Olive's observation, though perhaps less economically expressed."

Lady Wrench laid down her cards. "Amnesia?"

"The doctors call it so. Doctors have Greek names for
all the things which prevent dinner being punctual."

"Does he remember nothing?"

"He remembers leaving his club one foggy November evening. He
remembers walking toward Piccadilly. Then—nothing. The next thing he knows,
he is sitting in a railway carriage outside York, four
years older, with eleven shillings in his pocket and no
notion of his name."

Peter whistled.

"Surely he must have worked, eaten, talked to people?"

"One hopes so. Four years is a long time to
fast."

"And nobody recognized him?"

"He has been in the papers. Apparently not. He may
have been abroad; his hands were browned, and there was
a scar along his temple which he had not possessed
before. He spoke two or three words in Arabic during
a fever at York, though before disappearing he knew no
language beyond club French."

From outside came the sudden call of a bird. Caroline's
fingers closed upon the curtain.

Osborne saw it. "Caroline?"

She made a little gesture commanding silence.

Mrs. Vane, who had until now been occupied with sorting
embroidery silks on a sofa, looked up in mild displeasure.

"This is all very painful. Olive has a child, hasn't
she?"

"A boy," said Lady Wrench. "Born after Gregory disappeared."

"That," observed Mrs. Baxter, "is why the story has been
permitted to end respectably. A wife may forgive a mystery
when it comes home in time to educate its son."

"Does he know the child?"

"No. But the child knows him. Children accept the incredible
because they have not yet acquired the social habit of
doubting it."

Mrs. Vane sighed. "Poor Olive."

"Certainly," said Mrs. Baxter. "Though I am never sure whether
pity is deserved by the woman who waits or the
woman who stops waiting one day too soon."

The words went out through the open windows and struck
Caroline with the precision of a thrown knife.

She stepped into the room.

Every face turned toward her; then, with the instinctive good
breeding of people who have been caught discussing suffering by
its owner, every face assumed an expression of unconnected benevolence.

"What proof had Mr. Nettleton of those years?" she asked.

Her voice was quiet, but Osborne came nearer.

Mrs. Baxter removed the cigarette from its holder. "My dear
Caroline, we had forgotten you were outside."

"No, you had not."

Mrs. Baxter's eyebrows lifted; then something in the girl's face
abolished her amusement.

"Very little proof," she said. "The clothes he wore. The
scar. Some foreign coins. An Egyptian ring."

Caroline's hand went to her breast.

"What ring?"

"A rather barbaric thing, I believe—old gold, with a square
red stone."

"What was on the stone?"

Mrs. Baxter looked at Lady Wrench.

"A letter," said the latter. "Olive showed it to me
yesterday. It was engraved in the intaglio fashion."

"What letter?"

"My dear—"

"What letter?"

"C."

No one moved. From the third bridge table a queen
of hearts slipped from somebody's hand and fell face upward
upon the carpet.

Caroline drew a breath which did not seem to reach
her lungs. "Was the stone carnelian?"

"Yes."

"Dark at the edges, with a flaw like a white
thread across one corner?"

Lady Wrench stared. "I believe so."

"And the gold—there are two little falcons on the shoulders,
very much worn?"

"Yes."

The room, which a moment before had contained eight people,
three card tables, flowers, smoke, sunlight and the accumulated trivialities
of an affluent afternoon, appeared suddenly emptied of everything but
Caroline's white figure.

Osborne said, "What is it?"

She opened the small jeweled purse hanging from her wrist.
Her fingers were cold and clumsy; the clasp escaped her
twice. At last she drew out a ring and laid
it upon the bridge table.

It was old gold, heavy and dull; two worn falcons
guarded a square red stone. Upon the stone, cut deeply
enough to catch the light, was the letter C.

Mrs. Baxter did not touch it.

"Where did you get that?"

"In Cairo, five years ago."

Caroline's voice had acquired the remote distinctness with which people
speak in dreams.

"There were two. Hugh and I found them in a
little shop behind the Ezbekieh Gardens. He said they were
not truly ancient, but I liked the falcons. The stones
were blank. We had them engraved—C for Caroline. It was
a joke, because he said after marriage everything he possessed
would be marked with my initial."

She smiled. It was a dreadful, obedient little smile, produced
in tribute to a vanished happiness.

"I kept one. I gave him the other."

Osborne took a step toward her. "Caroline, are you certain?"

"The flaw was in his stone. Mine has no flaw."
She held up the ring. "They were a pair."

Lady Wrench's hand hovered over the cards and withdrew.

"What was the date?" asked Mrs. Baxter, very gently now.

"The date?"

"When did you meet Mr. Arden?"

Caroline's gaze fixed upon the red stone.

"Four years ago last January, in Capri. He had been
ill. A fisherman's family had taken him in after finding
him unconscious near the cliffs. He said he remembered nothing
before waking in their house."

The silence altered. What had been coincidence became shape; what
had been shape became certainty.

"He called himself Hugh Arden," Caroline continued. "The name was
in a book beside his bed—Thomas Hardy. He laughed when
he told me. He said a man without a past
was entitled to borrow one."

"And you knew nothing of his family?" Mrs. Vane whispered.

"He said there was none. He was alone. We were
all en tour; Aunt Cecilia adored him because he listened
to her account of Rome. My brother made inquiries, but
there was nothing to find. Hugh said he would begin
from that day."

Her eyes went toward the windows, beyond them to the
hills and the remote white road.

"We were to be married in June. On the morning
of the wedding he disappeared."

"And Gregory Nettleton returned—when?" Osborne asked.

"On that same morning," said Mrs. Baxter.

The words were not dramatic. Mrs. Baxter, who could turn
a divorce into a comedy and a funeral into an
epigram, spoke them without ornament.

Caroline's fingers tightened about the ring until the metal marked
her palm.

"He remembered," Peter said under his breath.

"Or forgot again," said Lady Wrench.

"No," Caroline answered.

They looked at her.

"He did not remember me. That is the one merciful
thing. If he had remembered, he would have written. He
would not have left me standing there—in my dress—with everyone
whispering beyond the door."

She swayed slightly. Osborne reached her before anyone else and
took her cold hand between his warm, brown ones.

"Come outside."

She allowed him to lead her through the French windows.
No one followed. Even society knows when curiosity, having obtained
its desire, must put on mourning.

They crossed the terrace and went down three shallow steps
into the formal garden. Box hedges enclosed white roses; a
stone faun grinned from beneath the yews; farther away the
country opened into immense calm distances. The afternoon had begun
to decline, and the light upon Caroline's dress was no
longer brilliant but pearly and cold.

At the end of the path she stopped.

"All those years," she said, "I asked which way he
had gone. North, south, across the sea—Which way? Which way?
And all the while he had gone backward."

Osborne said nothing.

"He had a wife."

"Yes."

"And a child."

"Yes."

"He was not cruel."

"No."

"Nor false."

"No."

She pulled her hand free and looked at the ring.
"I used to wish he were dead. Do you know
that? Not every day—not even often—but sometimes, when people were
kind and I could not bear them, I prayed that
he was dead. Then I might grieve honestly. One cannot
wear mourning for a question."

Osborne's face contracted.

"You wore it."

"Invisibly. That is the most unbecoming kind."

For an instant Mrs. Baxter's cadence seemed to have entered
her voice; then it broke, and she turned away.

"Poor Olive," she said.

"Poor you."

"No. She waited without choosing to wait. I chose it
every morning."

"You loved him."

"I loved the man he was with me. Perhaps Gregory
Nettleton was asleep in him; perhaps Hugh was the dream.
Which of them returned to her?"

"We shall never know."

Caroline looked at the pale hills. "No. But I know
enough."

The wind stirred the white roses, laying their petals briefly
against one another like whispered confidences. From the music room
came no sound. The house behind them, so lately full
of elegant cruelty, seemed to be holding its breath.

Kingsley bent toward her.

"What will you do?"

She slipped the ring from her finger. For a moment
she held both rings—the one she had worn and its
recovered meaning—together in her palm; then she closed them in
the jeweled purse and fastened it.

"I shall send this one to Mrs. Nettleton."

"Why?"

"Because the two belong to the same story, and it
is hers now."

"And you?"

She looked at him fully. Grief remained in her face,
but uncertainty had gone; the frozen fire of waiting had
burned itself out, leaving not happiness, not yet, but the
first warmth of an honest desolation.

"You asked me a question on the terrace."

"I shall not ask it again today."

"No."

"Nor tomorrow."

"No."

"I can wait."

A faint, almost forgotten curve touched the corners of her
mouth.

"Men should never boast of waiting to a woman who
has practised it."

He flushed. "I'm sorry."

"Dear Osborne—you are always sorry when you have been sincere."

He reached for her hand, but did not take it
until she offered it. Her fingers were still cold; he
enclosed them quietly in his.

Beyond the garden, the hay wagon appeared again upon another
road, moving homeward under the lengthening shadows.

"Will you come back to the house?" he asked.

"Presently."

"And afterward?"

She drew a slow breath. Behind her lay four years
of unlocked doors, unanswered letters, imagined graves, and a bridegroom
eternally approaching a church he had never reached. Before her
stood a brown, inarticulate man who had no mystery in
him and offered no romance except faithfulness.

"Afterward," she said, "I will try."

Kingsley's grasp tightened, but he made no answer.

Caroline turned once more toward the horizon. It no longer
seemed to ask her anything. Somewhere beyond those gracious hills,
Gregory Nettleton sat perhaps with his wife and a son
who accepted miracles; Hugh Arden had vanished more completely than
any drowned man, for he had returned to life under
another name.

She bowed her head over Osborne's hands.

"He is dead," she said. "At last—he is dead."

Then, with the grave peace of one who has closed
an empty coffin, she added:

"I have buried my dead."

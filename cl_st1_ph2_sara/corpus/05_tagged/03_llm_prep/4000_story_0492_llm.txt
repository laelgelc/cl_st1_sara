## The Countess's Invitation

When Archbishop Baldwin of Treves had broken the league of
the Moselle nobles, dismantled three towers, hanged two robber barons,
reconciled seven lesser lords to the duties of Christian obedience,
and caused the roads to become so safe that a
merchant might be robbed only by lawful toll, Peace spread
her white wings over the valley and found them somewhat
cramped between the ecclesiastical fortresses.

His Grace was not merely Archbishop of Treves. He was
also Elector of the Empire, lord of broad territories, commander
of men-at-arms, judge, diplomatist, financier, and—when necessity put steel into
theology—a very capable soldier. In those days a mitre did
not prevent a man from wearing a helmet beneath it,
nor was the crozier always distinguishable in its effects from
the mace. Baldwin could celebrate Mass at dawn, dictate the
terms of surrender at noon, and dine cheerfully in the
evening while the castle of an obstinate vassal burned behind
him.

He had recently completed a campaign which later chroniclers, writing
at a safe distance from both the defeated and the
victorious, termed a pacification. The word is a convenient one.
It includes the silence of the graveyard, the quiet of
the dungeon, and the contentment of men who have discovered
that resistance is more costly than submission.

Along the Moselle, where every bend of the river disclosed
a crag fit for a fortress and every fortress contained
a lord jealous of the next, the Archbishop had made
authority visible. His soldiers repaired roads because armies require roads;
merchants then used them and praised the Church for encouraging
commerce. He placed garrisons in castles because rebellion dislikes witnesses;
pilgrims thereafter slept securely under the protection of those garrisons
and gave thanks to Providence. Thus policy, piety, and profit
walked arm in arm, although policy generally chose the road.

It was in the first warmth of summer that Baldwin,
leaving Treves with no more than twelve mounted men, betook
himself toward his palace at Cochem. He rode a black
charger of uncommon size, and wore beneath a plain travelling
cloak a shirt of polished mail. His face was broad
rather than handsome, the chin resolute, the eyes quick and
hard; yet laughter came readily to them when he encountered
courage or folly, which in his experience were often near
relations.

At his right hand rode Sir Gottlieb von Rüdesheim, a
knight who had preserved through twenty years of warfare a
serene appetite and a conviction that most difficulties might be
endured if supper were certain. Behind them came Captain Heinrich,
a lean, vigilant soldier, and ten retainers bearing the Archbishop's
colours.

"You have subdued the valley, my Lord," said Gottlieb, as
their horses followed the river road, "and therefore we travel
with twelve men where last month we required twelve hundred.
That is the pleasant arithmetic of victory."

"The arithmetic is pleasant," replied Baldwin, "but the sum may
be wrong."

Gottlieb glanced at the slopes above them, where vineyards climbed
in narrow ranks between grey rocks.

"I see peasants, vines, and a goat."

"The goat may be in conspiracy."

"If so, your Grace should excommunicate him. He hath already
the beard of a heretic."

Baldwin smiled, but he did not cease to examine the
heights. He had survived too long by mistrusting landscapes which
seemed arranged for a painter. The Moselle glittered below, broad
and placid, curving through hills that rose steeply on either
side. Here the road was pressed between river and precipice;
there it passed under wooded ridges from which a dozen
bowmen might command it. On distant summits stood castles—some whole,
some scarred by siege, some no more than black teeth
against the sky. Each represented not only stone and mortar,
but a family, a claim, a grievance, and a number
of armed men whose loyalty depended upon the direction of
the wind.

One such family had not yet been brought conveniently within
the Archbishop's hand.

The Countess Laurette von Starkenburg, widow of Count Henry, possessed
a castle near the river and lands commanding the approaches
to Trarbach. Her husband had been a restless noble while
living and had become, as dead nobles commonly do, a
much more useful political memory. His adherents spoke of him
as a defender of ancient liberties; Baldwin remembered him as
a gentleman who levied toll without permission and changed allegiance
with the seasons.

The countess had one son, still young, and therefore an
inheritance. Baldwin had determined that she should marry a loyal
knight of his choosing. The knight would gain a wealthy
widow, the widow would gain protection, the boy would gain
a prudent guardian, and the Archbishop would gain Starkenburg. Of
these benefits, the last was the only one that had
required no explanation.

He had sent courteous messages. The countess had answered courteously.
He had suggested three husbands in succession, each brave, obedient,
and insufficiently related to her. She had discovered objections to
all three. One was too old, one too young, and
one possessed a nose which, she said, would alarm her
son. Baldwin had then resolved to remove the difficulty of
choice.

"At Cochem," he said, "we shall prepare a final letter
to the lady of Starkenburg."

"Will it contain a fourth husband?"

"It will contain an order."

"Ah," said Gottlieb. "Orders are husbands without courtship."

Baldwin looked at him.

"Thy wit prospers because I am indulgent."

"My wit prospers because your Grace dislikes riding in silence."

Before the Archbishop could answer, a horn sounded from the
wooded slope.

Captain Heinrich drew his sword. The retainers closed around their
lord, but there was scant space in which to form.
From behind the trees above descended twenty armed men, while
another company appeared round the bend ahead. Their movements were
orderly, their crossbows ready, and their colours those of Starkenburg.

The Archbishop reined in his charger.

"Your arithmetic," he said to Gottlieb, "was indeed wrong."

"Forty against twelve," replied the knight. "Yet I maintain that
supper would improve the calculation."

A horseman advanced from the opposing company. He was old,
square-shouldered, and heavily scarred, with a white beard divided by
the strap of his helmet. Halting at a respectful distance,
he lowered his sword in salute.

"My Lord Archbishop," he said, "the Countess Laurette von Starkenburg
presents her humble duty and begs that your Grace will
honour her poor house by supping there this evening."

Baldwin looked at the crossbowmen above, then at the river
below.

"Inform the countess," he said, "that I am bound for
Cochem and regret that affairs of the Church forbid me
the pleasure."

The old captain saluted again, wheeled his horse, rode ten
paces to confer with his men, and returned.

"My Lord Archbishop, the Countess Laurette presents her humble duty
and begs that your Grace will honour her poor house
by supping there this evening."

"Thy memory is excellent," said Baldwin. "Thy understanding is defective.
I have declined."

Again the captain withdrew. Again he returned.

"My Lord Archbishop, the Countess Laurette presents her humble duty
and begs that your Grace will honour her poor house
by supping there this evening."

The Archbishop's eyes hardened.

"Is this the third invitation?"

"It is, my Lord."

"And if I decline a third time?"

The captain glanced toward the crossbows with an expression almost
apologetic.

"Her Ladyship instructed me to repeat the invitation until your
Grace found leisure to accept."

There are occasions when dignity requires resistance, and others when
dignity is best preserved for future use. Baldwin counted the
men above him, measured the narrowness of the road, observed
that the river offered no retreat, and reached the sensible
conclusion that martyrdom over supper would be an extravagance.

"Tell your mistress," he said, "that her hospitality is irresistible."

The captain bowed profoundly.

"She hoped your Grace would find it so."

The Starkenburg men opened their ranks. Half rode before Baldwin's
party, half behind, while others kept to the heights. No
weapon was taken from the Archbishop or his followers; nothing
so discourteous was necessary. They were treated as honoured guests
whose preference in the matter of departure had merely been
overlooked.

"Forty men," murmured Gottlieb, "to persuade us to dine. The
countess must maintain an excellent table."

"If the table disappoints thee," said Baldwin, "thou mayest complain
to the crossbowmen."

"I have never yet criticised a cook who possessed military
support."

They left the river road at a steep turning and
climbed toward Starkenburg. The castle stood on a rocky height,
its walls rising directly from the crag, its central keep
square and severe. Below it the Moselle bent like a
silver buckle around the hills; beyond, valleys opened toward Trarbach,
where roads and waterways met. Whoever held these heights might
watch trade, troops, and messages pass through the country. A
weak lord in Starkenburg was an inconvenience. A hostile lord
there was a drawn sword over the Archbishop's line of
communication.

Baldwin's expression grew thoughtful as they climbed. The countess, by
bringing him to her castle, had reminded him more eloquently
than any envoy could have done why he desired it.

At the gate a line of servants waited. The drawbridge
stood down, the portcullis raised, and the Countess Laurette herself
came into the outer court to receive him.

She was older than Baldwin had expected, perhaps forty, perhaps
a little more, for anxiety marks some faces and authority
preserves others. Her gown was black, relieved only by a
silver girdle; a white veil framed hair in which grey
had begun to appear. She was not beautiful according to
the songs of minstrels, who demand gold hair, rose cheeks,
and an age rarely compatible with judgment; but her features
were calm, her carriage erect, and beneath the formal softness
of her manner lay a chin as firm as the
stone over which she ruled.

She made a deep reverence.

"My Lord Archbishop, you confer honour upon Starkenburg."

"Madam," replied Baldwin, dismounting, "I have seldom been so earnestly
solicited to confer honour."

"A lonely widow must sometimes be urgent, my Lord, lest
great men forget her."

"A lonely widow with forty armed retainers is not easily
forgotten."

The countess smiled.

"The roads are said to be dangerous."

"They were, until I made them safe."

"Then my men have profited by your Grace's labour."

Their eyes met. Each understood that ceremony had drawn its
sword, and each was too well bred to admit seeing
the blade.

The Archbishop's followers were lodged in chambers overlooking the inner
court. Their horses were taken to the stables and attended
with care. Servants brought wine, water, fresh linen, and every
evidence of honour except liberty. Baldwin noted that guards stood
at each gate, that the battlements were manned, and that
the chamber assigned to him lay in a turret from
which escape would require either wings or an indifference to
broken bones.

A memory, unwelcome but persistent, accompanied him through the passageways.
One of his predecessors in the see of Treves had
once accepted shelter at Starkenburg during a winter journey. He
had dined well, slept badly, and died before dawn. The
physicians called it an apoplexy; his friends called it poison;
the family of Starkenburg called it an act of God.
The difference between theology and politics is sometimes only a
question of who writes the record.

Baldwin turned to Captain Heinrich when the servants withdrew.

"We must send word to Treves."

"The gates are guarded, my Lord."

"Gates are built for guards to watch. Walls are built
for men to neglect."

He crossed to the window. The turret overlooked a narrow
ledge, beneath which the rock fell sharply toward a wooded
ravine. A drain channel ran from the roof along the
outer wall. Baldwin examined it.

"Heinrich, thou hast climbed worse places."

"Usually upward, my Lord, with enemies behind me."

"Tonight thou wilt climb downward, with friends behind thee and
enemies everywhere else. Reach the woods, take a horse from
the first farm, and ride to Treves. Tell Marshal Walram
to bring eight hundred men and engines enough to open
this castle."

"How soon?"

"Yesterday."

"And if I am taken?"

Baldwin's face became still.

"Swallow the message."

"It is not written."

"Then swallow thy tongue. Succeed and live."

Gottlieb, who had been examining a pitcher of wine with
judicious suspicion, said, "Those terms leave little room for misunderstanding."

They waited until darkness. Dinner was to be served late,
according to a message from the countess, who pleaded household
preparations and the suddenness of the Archbishop's visit. Baldwin admired
the phrase. A hostess who had arranged an ambush forty
men strong now professed surprise at the arrival of her
guest.

When the courtyard below had quieted, Heinrich removed his boots,
fastened a rope to the iron hinge of the shutter,
and lowered himself to the ledge. The night was moonless.
His dark clothing merged with the wall; he passed from
sight beneath the window.

For several minutes there was silence.

Then a voice rose from below.

"Have care, Captain. The third stone is loose."

Another voice laughed. A torch flared in the ravine.

"Back!" said Baldwin. "Back!"

Heinrich climbed with desperate speed. An arrow struck sparks from
the wall near his hand. A second cut through the
sleeve at his shoulder. He reached the ledge, seized the
rope, and Gottlieb hauled him through the window as three
crossbow bolts rattled against the shutter.

Almost at once there came a courteous knock.

Baldwin opened the door. The white-bearded captain stood outside, carrying
Heinrich's boots.

"Your Grace's officer appears to have dropped these."

"So I perceive."

"The stones are damp after sunset. Her Ladyship would be
distressed if any guest suffered injury."

"Her solicitude," said Baldwin, "surrounds us on every side."

The captain bowed, placed the boots neatly within the chamber,
and withdrew.

Gottlieb inspected the cut in Heinrich's sleeve.

"They knew which window we should use."

"They knew which room they would give us," replied Baldwin.

He began to pace. In the confined chamber his energy
seemed almost to strike sparks from the walls. He was
like a lion in a travelling cage—no less dangerous for
being enclosed, but compelled to consider the bars before devouring
the keeper.

"How long before Treves wonders?" asked Heinrich.

"Two days, perhaps three. I often change plans without advising
every clerk in my household. At Cochem they may suppose
me delayed; at Treves they may suppose me at Cochem.
By the time suspicion arises, our hostess will have strengthened
every approach."

"Can we take the gate from within?"

"With twelve men? We might take the first court. We
should then die beneath the inner wall, which would make
a stirring song and a poor campaign."

Gottlieb poured wine into a cup and sniffed it.

"Do not drink that," said Baldwin.

"I was merely determining whether death had been mixed with
a good vintage."

"And?"

"If poison be present, it hath a delicate bouquet."

He set the cup aside. Soon afterward servants conducted them
to the great hall.

The Countess Laurette had prepared a banquet suitable for an
imperial envoy. Candles shone upon polished plate; trout from the
Moselle lay among herbs; there were venison, capons, white bread,
preserved fruits, and wines from vineyards which Baldwin himself had
once claimed in a boundary dispute. Musicians played softly in
the gallery. The countess sat at his right, her son—a
grave boy of twelve—at his left. The Archbishop's retainers were
placed among household knights, so that friendship and surveillance occupied
the same benches.

Before seating himself, Baldwin watched the countess raise her cup
and drink. Only then did he taste his own wine.

She observed him without embarrassment.

"Your Grace finds the vintage doubtful?"

"The vintage, no."

"My cellarer will be relieved."

"Thy cellarer is not the person upon whom my doubts
rest."

A faint silence spread near them, though knives and voices
continued at the lower tables.

The countess folded her hands.

"My Lord refers, perhaps, to the unhappy death of Archbishop
Megingod."

"History is useful at supper. It restrains the appetite."

"He died of a seizure."

"So hath many a man whose enemy owned the kitchen."

"Your Grace wrongs my grandfather."

"I have not accused him."

"You have accused the soup."

Baldwin laughed despite himself.

"Madam, if thou hadst wished me dead, thy men might
have killed me on the road."

"That would have been murder."

"And poison would be housekeeping?"

"No poison has entered your cup, my Lord. I require
you living."

"That assurance comforts and alarms me equally."

She inclined her head, conceding the point. Thereafter she spoke
of the vineyards, of the river floods, of disputes among
monasteries, and of the education of her son. She possessed
knowledge beyond the limits usually praised in noblewomen. She knew
what tolls were collected at each crossing, which barons could
provide twenty horse and which only promised them, how much
grain the valley had yielded, and how long an army
might remain in the district before exhausting its forage.

Baldwin questioned her sharply. She answered without haste. Each inquiry
intended to expose ignorance revealed preparation; each polished compliment hid
a probe and met an armoured reply.

The boy listened in silence until the Archbishop asked, "And
what wilt thou be when thou art grown, young sir?"

"My mother says I must first live long enough to
grow."

"A prudent answer."

"She says prudence is what the weak call courage when
they cannot afford a battle."

The countess looked at her son.

"I did not say it in precisely those words."

"No," said the boy, "but I understood you."

Baldwin leaned back, regarding mother and son with renewed attention.

After the meal the countess rose.

"If your Grace is not wearied, I would ask the
favour of a private audience."

"Another favour?"

"A small one."

"I begin to fear thy smaller favours more than the
large."

She led him, accompanied only by Gottlieb and the white-bearded
captain, to a chamber above the hall. There a fire
burned though the night was mild. The shutters stood open
upon the dark valley, but two guards were visible on
the terrace below. A table bore maps, parchments, and an
inkstand.

Baldwin halted beside it.

"Madam, thou hast prepared for business."

"A widow's business is seldom prepared for her by others."

She took the chair opposite him, leaving the higher-backed seat
at the table's head for the Archbishop. It was an
exact observance of rank, and he disliked it more than
open insolence.

"What dost thou desire?"

"Your Grace has spoken often of my second marriage."

"For thy protection."

"I have been protected tonight by forty men."

"Forty men may inconvenience travellers. They cannot resist an army."

"No, my Lord."

"Thy husband's lands require a strong guardian. Thy son requires
guidance. The valley requires peace. Marriage to one of my
knights would secure all three."

"It would secure Starkenburg to your Grace."

"That also."

She did not pretend to misunderstand.

"I do not wish to marry."

"Wishes are luxuries permitted to those whose castles have no
strategic value."

"Then I must make mine valuable enough to purchase the
luxury."

Baldwin's hand rested upon the table.

"Take care, madam."

"I have taken great care. That is why your Grace
is here."

Gottlieb coughed into his fist, concealing what might have been
admiration.

The countess unrolled a map of the Moselle. Her finger
touched a ridge near Trarbach.

"My son has rights over this height. I intend to
build a castle there."

"No."

The answer came like the fall of an axe.

She looked up.

"It commands the road and river," Baldwin continued. "A fortress
on that ridge could threaten my communications between Treves and
the lower valley. I will not permit it."

"That is why I ask permission."

"Permission refused."

"I also ask your Grace to pledge, under seal, that
neither you nor your officers will hinder the work, seize
the materials, detain the builders, or attack the castle once
completed."

Baldwin stared at her. The arguments in his mind stood
like armoured men awaiting command; but before he could advance
one, she placed another parchment upon the table.

"And," she said, "I ask one thousand pieces of gold
from the treasury of Treves to pay for its construction."

For a moment even the fire appeared discreetly to lower
its voice.

Gottlieb looked at the ceiling. The white-bearded captain studied the
floor. Baldwin rose very slowly.

"Thou hast brought me here," he said, "under threat of
arms."

"I invited your Grace to supper."

"Thou hast confined me in thy fortress."

"I have provided the finest chamber in the castle."

"Thy men shot at my captain."

"They missed."

"And now thou demandest permission to build a stronghold against
me, a pledge that I shall not prevent it, and
gold from my treasury to complete the work."

"That is correct, my Lord."

"Madam, common thieves possess the virtue of plain speech. They
say, 'Your money or your life.'"

"A common thief would lack the authority to receive your
signature."

"This is ransom."

"If the word pleases your Grace."

His palm struck the table. The inkstand jumped; the map
rolled in upon itself.

"By the holy robe of Treves, I will pull this
castle stone from stone! I will hang thy captain from
the gate, scatter thy men, confiscate thy lands, and place
thy son in a monastery where he may learn the
obedience his mother lacks."

The countess did not move.

"My Lord," she said, "you must first leave Starkenburg."

He turned toward the white-bearded captain.

"Dost thou think these walls will save thee when my
army comes?"

The old soldier replied reluctantly, "No walls are everlasting, your
Grace."

"Then remember it."

"He remembers," said the countess. "So do I."

Baldwin faced her again.

"Release me now, and I may treat this outrage as
the folly of a frightened woman."

"No."

The single word, quietly spoken, stopped the room more effectively
than a shout.

"Consider thy position."

"I have considered little else for six months."

"My army will seek me."

"Where?"

"At Cochem first. At Treves afterward. Messengers will ride the
roads."

"My riders watch the roads."

"They will be captured."

"Some, perhaps."

"My marshal will bring men enough to encircle this hill."

"If he learns where you are."

"He will learn."

"In three days? Five? Ten?" She leaned slightly forward. "Your
Grace has lately subdued many nobles. Their oaths are new;
their resentment is not. Those who submitted because your army
stood before their gates will reconsider submission when the army
has no commander. Rumour rides faster than a marshal. By
tomorrow it will be whispered that Baldwin of Treves has
disappeared. By the next day, that he is dead. Before
the week is out, it will be said that the
Pope has deposed you, the Emperor has imprisoned you, and
the Devil has carried you bodily from the road."

Gottlieb nodded gravely.

"The last tale will travel farthest."

The Archbishop silenced him with a glance.

The countess continued. "Your army is powerful because you command
it. Marshal Walram is brave, but Lord Gerlach distrusts him;
Gerlach is rich, but the captains despise him; the captains
obey because your punishment is swift and your rewards are
certain. Remove you, my Lord, and your host is an
arch without its keystone. Each stone may be strong; the
arch nevertheless falls."

"Thou flatterest me."

"I calculate you."

"The Emperor will demand my release."

"The Emperor is distant."

"The Pope—"

"Is farther."

"My allies along the river will rise."

"Your allies will first ask whether you are alive. Your
enemies will assure them that you are not. Some will
offer me men to keep you here. Others will offer
gold. A few, I think, will offer poison."

At that word Baldwin's gaze sharpened.

"You said thou didst require me living."

"I do. Your enemies may have different requirements."

"Wouldst thou admit them?"

"No. Yet a castle has servants, and servants have purses.
The longer your Grace remains, the greater the danger—to you,
to me, and to the valley."

He began pacing again, each turn abruptly shortened by the
walls. She had judged the situation with hateful accuracy. His
political dominion, broad as it appeared on parchment, rested upon
motion, messages, command, and the certainty that Baldwin would presently
arrive wherever disobedience arose. Power is often thought to dwell
in crowns, seals, and fortresses; in truth it dwells in
expectation. Men obey the ruler whom they expect to act.
Confine him, conceal him, cast doubt upon his survival, and
authority begins to evaporate like mist from the river.

"How long," he asked, "dost thou propose to keep me?"

"Until we agree."

"And if we never agree?"

"Your chamber is comfortable."

"Winter here is severe."

"We have firewood."

Gottlieb sighed.

"I had hoped to see Cochem before the vintage."

Baldwin ignored him. "Suppose my troops do come. Suppose they
surround Starkenburg before rebellion stirs elsewhere. Thou canst not resist
them."

"No."

"Then thy scheme ends on the scaffold."

"Possibly."

"Yet thou persistest."

"Yes."

"Why?"

For the first time the countess's composure altered, though not
through fear. Her hand closed upon the edge of the
map.

"Because you intend to dispose of me, my son, and
my house as though we were three entries in your
account book. Because every refusal I send is answered by
another command. Because a widow without an army may still
possess a will. And because, if I yield Starkenburg now,
my son will inherit a title, a sword, and nothing
upon which to stand."

Baldwin watched her.

"So thou wouldst build him a castle."

"I would build him a future."

"With my gold."

"With gold which will purchase peace more cheaply than your
army usually does."

A sound escaped Gottlieb, half cough, half laugh.

The Archbishop's anger, though fierce, had never been blind. He
valued courage as a miser values coin, even when it
was found in an enemy's purse. This woman had done
what the river lords, with all their mailed followers and
noisy alliances, had failed to do: she had studied not
merely the strength of his army, but the machinery by
which he directed it. She had chosen the ground, prepared
the ambush, anticipated the messenger, calculated the delay, and placed
her demands where force could not readily answer them. She
had not defeated his soldiers. She had separated Baldwin from
Baldwin's power.

"Leave us," he said.

The white-bearded captain bowed and withdrew. Gottlieb moved toward the
door.

"Not thee," said the Archbishop. "If I murder our hostess,
I shall require a witness that she provoked me."

The countess's mouth curved faintly.

"I am grateful for the protection of Sir Gottlieb."

"He is no protection. He merely dislikes missing the end
of an argument."

They remained in the chamber while the candles shortened.

Baldwin questioned her as he might have questioned the commander
of a besieging force. How many men held Starkenburg? How
long could the stores last? What signal would summon her
allies? Which roads were watched? She answered some questions, declined
others, and lied at least twice—skilfully, though not skilfully enough.
In return she asked what guarantee she possessed that he
would honour an agreement signed under compulsion.

"My word," he said.

"Your word, my Lord, was lately given to preserve the
rights of Lord Ulrich."

"Ulrich rebelled."

"He said he defended his rights."

"Rebellion commonly does."

"And if I release you, will you not call this
rebellion?"

"I may call it hospitality."

"With how large an army?"

He almost smiled.

She required a sealed charter, witnessed by Gottlieb and her
captain, confirming the boy's right to build upon the Trarbach
ridge. The Archbishop was to command his officers and vassals
not to interfere. The thousand gold pieces were to be
delivered in two instalments, the first within twenty days. In
return Baldwin and his followers would depart unharmed at dawn,
and the countess would promise neither to join a league
against Treves nor to admit the Archbishop's enemies into the
new fortress.

"Thou askest trust while practising captivity," he said.

"And your Grace offers protection while arranging my marriage."

"Madam, we understand one another too well."

"That is the beginning of peace."

"It is more often the beginning of war."

They disputed every clause. He reduced the garrison she might
maintain, then increased it when she pointed out that a
castle too weak to defend itself would tempt every neighbouring
lord. He demanded free passage for his messengers. She agreed,
provided they paid the customary toll. He struck out the
toll. She restored it in different language. Gottlieb, called upon
to witness revisions, finally asked whether the castle would be
completed before the document concerning it.

Near midnight servants brought bread, cheese, and fresh wine. Baldwin
made the countess drink first from every vessel, a precaution
she accepted with such patience that he began to feel
unreasonable, which was perhaps her intention.

At length only the payment remained.

"Five hundred gold pieces," he said.

"One thousand."

"Six hundred, and thou mayest retain thy household men under
arms."

"One thousand."

"Seven hundred, with timber rights in the eastern forest."

"One thousand."

"Eight hundred, and remission of tolls upon stone transported by
river."

"One thousand."

"Dost thou know no other number?"

"It is the number required."

"Thy builders are thieves."

"Then they will feel at home spending ecclesiastical gold."

Gottlieb turned away toward the window.

Baldwin looked long at the countess. Fatigue had paled her
face, yet her eyes remained steady. She had wagered her
castle, her lands, her life, and perhaps her son's life
upon the accuracy of her judgment. It is an easy
thing to be fearless when one does not perceive danger;
such courage belongs to fools and saints. She perceived every
danger and had proceeded nevertheless.

At last the Archbishop sat.

"Write one thousand."

Gottlieb turned back.

The countess lowered her gaze, not in triumph but in
acknowledgement. "I thank your Grace."

"Do not thank me. I am buying my own horse,
which thou hast not even stolen."

"The horse has been well fed."

"He will be the only member of my party to
profit by this visit."

"Sir Gottlieb has supped twice."

"That is true," said the knight.

The charter was written. Wax was heated; seals were pressed;
names were added by witnesses whose hands recorded what their
swords had arranged. Baldwin read each line, altered three words,
and signed with a stroke vigorous enough to wound the
parchment.

When all was done, the countess placed the charter before
him.

"My Lord Archbishop, you are free to depart."

"At this hour?"

"If you wish."

He glanced toward the window. The black valley lay below,
roadless in darkness.

"So after forcing me to remain, thou wouldst now force
me to choose to remain."

"I should regret exposing your Grace to dangerous roads."

"The roads," he reminded her, "are safe."

"Not from invitations."

At dawn the gates of Starkenburg opened. Horses stood saddled
in the court, every buckle polished, every cloak folded. The
Archbishop's weapons had never been taken, but their return was
nevertheless made ceremonial by servants who carried them as though
restoring captured standards.

The Countess Laurette came to the gate with her son
and household. Morning light lay upon the battlements; below, the
Moselle shone through veils of mist. The forty retainers who
had issued the invitation stood in two disciplined lines.

Baldwin mounted his black charger. For a moment he looked
toward the ridge near Trarbach, blue in the distance. In
imagination he already saw walls there, towers, a gatehouse, and
his own gold vanishing stone by stone into the sky.

"Madam," he said, "thou hast won."

"I have obtained your permission, my Lord."

"Do not spoil victory with modesty. It is an affectation
in conquerors."

She bowed.

"I trust your Grace bears no lasting anger."

"I bear many lasting angers. They give continuity to government."

Her son regarded him solemnly. Baldwin beckoned the boy nearer.

"Learn from thy mother," he said. "But not too much.
The world could not endure two of her."

The boy glanced at Laurette.

"She says your Grace is the greatest prince upon the
Rhine."

"Doth she?"

"She said that was why twelve men were enough to
capture you."

Gottlieb coughed violently. Even Captain Heinrich stared at his saddle.

Baldwin's face remained stern for one dangerous instant; then he
threw back his head and laughed, and the sound rang
from the walls.

"Madam," he said, "thy son will prosper."

"I pray so."

The Archbishop gathered the reins, but did not yet turn
his horse. His expression altered, amusement yielding to a gallantry
which, though lightly worn, was not wholly jest.

"Countess Laurette, I came intending to provide thee with a
husband."

"So I feared."

"I selected loyal men, brave men, men of estate."

"Your Grace was generous."

"I perceive now that none was fit for thee."

She raised an eyebrow. "Then my captivity has succeeded beyond
expectation."

"Thy captivity?"

"My freedom from marriage."

"Call it what thou wilt. Had the law of Holy
Church not already wedded me elsewhere, I might have offered
thee an Archbishop and permitted thee to refuse him."

The countess looked at the mail beneath his cloak, the
soldiers at his back, and the charter secured within her
keeping.

"My Lord," she said, "had you offered yourself with an
army, I should have required two thousand pieces of gold."

Again Baldwin laughed.

He saluted her, wheeled his charger, and rode down from
Starkenburg with his twelve men. No armed company followed; no
bowman watched from the trees. The road opened before him,
winding between vineyard and river toward Cochem, and with every
mile the invisible machinery of his authority began once more
to move. Messengers galloped, captains received orders, rumours were strangled,
and peace, having briefly folded her wings, resumed her stately
progress along the Moselle.

The Archbishop kept his word. The charter was proclaimed, his
officers were forbidden to hinder the work, and a thousand
gold pieces travelled under guard from the treasury of Treves
to the Countess Laurette. Upon the ridge near Trarbach masons
raised walls, carts dragged timber, and towers climbed year by
year above the river.

Thus the young heir of Starkenburg obtained his fortress, the
countess retained her freedom, and the Archbishop of Treves—who had
conquered many castles at great expense—paid for one which he
had most earnestly desired never to be built.

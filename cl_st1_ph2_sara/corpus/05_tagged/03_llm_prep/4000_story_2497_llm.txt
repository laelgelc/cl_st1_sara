The Francis Spaight was lost through bad seamanship before the
gale had half spent itself.

She sailed from Saint John with a deck-load of spruce,
a crew ill used to obedience, and a captain who
had learned to end disputes by postponing them. For three
days she ran eastward under shortened canvas, wallowing heavily, while
the glass fell and the wind hardened out of the
northwest. The men neglected the lashings on the deck-load, left
halyards snarled where they had fallen, and went growling to
their bunks whenever an order displeased them. At three in
the morning on the fourth day, with sleet whitening the
weather rigging, the mizzentopsail split from leech to bunt.

The captain shouted to take it in. Half the watch
pretended not to hear.

Mahoney and young O'Brien went aft with two others. O'Brien
was fifteen, narrow in the shoulders, red-haired, and quick on
a rope. He could laugh when wet, which the older
men regarded as an impertinence. While he and Mahoney fought
the clewline, the ship yawed under a following sea. The
wheel was in Sullivan's hands. He let the spokes run,
caught them too late, and the Francis Spaight broached to.

The deck-load shifted.

There came first a grinding beneath their feet, then the
reports of parting chains. The bundled timber moved six inches,
stopped, and moved again with the weight of a landslide.
It carried the lee bulwark away and went roaring into
the black water. The ship, relieved on one side and
crushed by the wind on the other, fell upon her
beam ends.

The mate was killed beneath the mainmast when it came
down. Murphy and Shea went through the broken lee-rail and
were seen once in the white smother, their hands lifted
like scraps of weed. No line reached them. The foremast
remained standing, its yards buried, and every sea that struck
the exposed bottom drove the wreck deeper. The loose mainmast,
held by the lee shrouds, beat against the side with
the regular violence of a thunderous sledge-hammer.

Men clung to hatch-coamings, pinrails, and whatever rope had not
gone under. The captain clung with them and gave no
useful order.

"Cut her clear!" Mahoney cried.

No one moved. An axe lay under the weather bulwark,
six feet below and washed at every roll.

O'Brien went for it.

He crept over the sloping planks on hands and knees,
his boots slipping in the sloshing water. Twice a sea
buried him. The second time his fingers loosened, and Mahoney,
leaning down by one leg, caught the back of his
oilskin. The boy found the axe with his other hand
and came up choking.

Together they attacked the shrouds. The hemp was swollen hard
as iron, and each cut cost them breath. A broken
spar swept Behane's cap away and opened his scalp, but
he crawled to safety without helping. Sullivan shouted that the
foremast would tear the bow out if they meddled with
it. The captain ordered them first to cut and then
to stop cutting. They did neither according to him.

When the last weather shroud parted, the foremast plunged, hung
by the rigging, and dragged the bows beneath a green
sea. Mahoney thought the ship was going down. Then O'Brien
hacked through the stay. The mast vanished. Freed of that
weight and of the timber, the Francis Spaight rolled sluggishly
upright.

She rose like a drowned beast unwillingly recalled to life.
Her boats were gone, the galley stove smashed, the cabin
flooded, the rudder damaged, and the deck swept nearly bare.
Yet she floated.

There were thirteen alive.

By daylight the gale had moderated, though a confused sea
continued to board them. The captain took command again when
command required only counting. Besides himself there were Mahoney, Sullivan,
Behane, Gorman the cook, Flynn, Casey, Regan, and five lads:
O'Brien, little Doyle, the brothers Connell, and a cabin boy
called Neddy Walsh. The oldest of the lads was seventeen.
The youngest had not completed fourteen years.

They searched the wreck.

The bread-room was under water. Salt beef had gone through
the side of the galley. Two barrels of flour were
spoiled into paste, and the cabin lockers yielded nothing but
tobacco, a bottle of lamp oil, and a tin of
mustard. In the forepeak they found a small breaker containing
perhaps three gallons of fresh water. It had cracked at
the hoop and must be handled gently.

The captain issued half a pannikin to each man.

"An' what'll we drink to-morrow?" Mahoney asked.

"We'll have rain," said the captain.

There was no rain that day.

The wreck lay low, the main deck awash to the
ankles whenever she rolled. The men crowded upon the quarterdeck,
where a scrap of the after-house gave shelter from the
wind. They had no dry clothes. Their hands whitened and
swelled; their feet became numb, then painful. Before evening O'Brien
had rigged a scrap of canvas between two stanchions. Mahoney
made a sea-anchor from wreckage and warped it forward so
that the Francis Spaight would not lie broadside to the
swell.

The others watched and afterward complained that the canvas shelter
favored one side.

At sunset a sail showed in the south, hull down.
The men shouted until their voices broke, hoisted a shirt
upon a boathook, and burned part of the broken companionway
in an iron bucket. The sail passed eastward and disappeared.

O'Brien maintained that she could not have seen them.

Sullivan struck him across the mouth.

"Yer always knowin'," he said. "Ye know more than the
master, don't ye?"

The boy wiped the blood away. "I knew enough to
cut the mast."

That reply was remembered.

During the second night the breaker leaked. When Mahoney discovered
it, a quart or more had drained into the deck
seams. The captain accused Gorman, because the cook had been
nearest. Gorman denied touching it and began to cry. He
was a loose-jointed, timid man of thirty whose courage failed
whenever another voice rose above his own. He had been
mocked in fine weather and bullied in bad. On the
wreck, where no social habit was left except cruelty, he
became everybody's servant.

They bound the crack with sailcloth and tar, then rationed
the water by spoonfuls. Each man received one in the
morning and one at dusk. The measure was a pewter
tablespoon from the cabin. The captain licked it after serving
the others.

By the end of the second day hunger had become
a continuous pressure beneath the ribs. On the third, they
chewed wet flour scraped from the staves. It tasted of
salt, bilge, and paint, but the men fought over it.
O'Brien found a piece as large as his fist and
shared it with Doyle and Neddy Walsh. Sullivan saw him.

"Keepin' a private store, is ut?"

"I found ut."

"Then ut belongs to all hands."

"There was none when I offered ye the work."

Sullivan took the paste and ate it himself.

The wind shifted into the northeast. Sleet fell, too dry
and driven to be caught. They set out a tureen
cover from the captain's pantry, but gathered only a skin
of grey water mixed with spray. Gorman carried the cover
about anxiously, offering each man a sip. Behane snatched it,
drank twice, and said the second swallow had been an
accident.

On the fifth day they killed a rat.

It came from the flooded forecastle and crouched on a
floating door, too exhausted to run. Casey crushed it with
a marline-spike. Gorman skinned it with the galley knife and
divided it into thirteen portions. The meat was blue, scanty,
and raw. They ate the entrails also. O'Brien gave half
his share to Doyle, who could no longer stand without
holding a rail.

That evening Flynn said the boy was courting favor.

"Favor from Doyle?" Mahoney asked.

"Favor from God, maybe," said Flynn. "He thinks he's better
nor us."

O'Brien laughed, though his lips had split from thirst. "There's
small trouble in bein' better nor you."

After that they hated him openly.

They hated his youth, his sharp tongue, and the fact
that he had saved the wreck when they had lain
bawling upon its side. They hated him because Doyle and
Neddy followed him, because Mahoney defended him, and because his
small acts of usefulness measured their own uselessness. When he
took the lookout, they accused him of sleeping. When he
rested, they accused him of shirking. Sullivan kicked him awake
in the night merely to ask whether he was warm
enough.

On the seventh day a rain squall crossed them. They
spread coats and canvas, set the tureen cover beneath a
drip, and caught perhaps two quarts. Men knelt with mouths
open while icy drops struck their faces. The captain attempted
to reserve half the water for officers. There were no
officers left except himself.

Mahoney stood over him until he divided it.

The water eased their tongues but awakened their stomachs. Hunger
altered its character after that. It ceased to be an
ache and became a thought. They spoke of meals continually:
boiled bacon, potatoes cracked in their skins, cod with butter,
warm bread, onions, puddings. Gorman recited the bill of fare
at a public house in Cork where he had once
worked. Each imaginary dish was discussed, garnished, divided, and eaten
in silence.

Then they began to watch one another's bodies.

Behane had the thickest thighs. Sullivan still carried flesh at
the shoulders. The boys were thin, but their skin was
younger. These observations were not spoken at first. They passed
in glances.

Cold worked upon them without pause. Salt sores opened at
the waist, armpits, and groin. Their feet were puffed and
colourless. When they moved, cloth rasped exposed flesh. Sleep came
in brief collapses and ended with a wave breaking over
the deck or a neighbour's elbow thrust into the ribs.
The wreck smelled of brine, tar, excrement, infected skin, and
unwashed fear.

On the tenth day Neddy Walsh saw gulls. The men
roused themselves, but the birds were bits of foam. On
the eleventh, Casey shouted that land stood to leeward. It
was cloud. On the twelfth, Doyle spoke for an hour
to his mother and then begged O'Brien not to let
the lamp go out, though there was no lamp.

The captain kept a tally with notches cut in a
stanchion. He made the twelfth notch twice. Mahoney corrected him,
and the captain called him mutinous.

No ship appeared.

By the fourteenth day, two men could not rise. The
others moved on their knees. The water was gone. They
sucked splinters, buttons, and pebbles to bring moisture into their
mouths. Gorman cut strips from a leather sea-boot and boiled
them in salt water over a fire made from oakum
and the last dry fragments of the cabin. The broth
increased their thirst. They swallowed it nevertheless and chewed the
leather until their jaws failed.

O'Brien's sores were worse than any man's. His trousers had
stiffened with salt, and when he shifted his position the
cloth tore skin away. He made no complaint before Sullivan.
At night Mahoney heard him whimpering into his sleeve.

"Ye'll see Ballycotton yet," Mahoney told him.

"I'll see somethin'," said the boy.

On the fifteenth morning the sea went down. Pale sunlight
showed through broken cloud and laid a cold brightness over
the water. The horizon was empty in every direction. With
the quiet came a new terror, for there was nothing
to occupy them now but waiting.

The captain called Mahoney aft.

"We cannot go another three days."

"We may not have to."

"We cannot reckon on a sail."

"We can reckon on remainin' men."

The captain rubbed his swollen face. "There are precedents."

"For what?"

"For sacrifice. One to preserve twelve."

Mahoney looked at him. "Then sacrifice yerself. Ye're master."

The captain turned away.

The proposal spread within an hour, though he claimed not
to have spoken of it. Sullivan said there was justice
in lots. Behane said Christians had done it before. Flynn
said the unlucky one would perform a good deed for
all. Each man framed the murder as an institution already
sanctioned elsewhere, upon other seas, by wiser men.

Gorman sat shaking beside the broken skylight.

"I'll have no hand in ut," he said.

"No wan asked ye yet," Sullivan replied.

O'Brien listened. His face, drawn tight over the cheekbones, remained
contemptuous.

"If there's lots," he said, "all draws."

The captain explained that the men were needed to work
the ship if a breeze came.

"What ship?" asked Mahoney. "She's no mast, no rudder worth
the name, an' scarce a side in her."

"The able-bodied men are necessary."

O'Brien looked around at the figures crouched on deck. "Show
me wan."

Sullivan raised a hand, but Mahoney caught his wrist.

The argument continued until dusk. No decision was announced. Yet
that night the five lads were ordered to lie apart
near the lee scuppers. Mahoney brought them back under the
scrap of canvas. Sullivan tried to prevent him, but had
not strength enough.

At dawn on the sixteenth day the captain produced five
lengths of marline.

"Thirteen," Mahoney said.

"Five."

"Thirteen, or none."

Behane said, "The b'ys have less dependin' on them."

"The b'ys have more years before them."

"They're no use to us."

"O'Brien saved every man here."

"That was then," Sullivan muttered.

The captain held up the strings. Four were equal. The
fifth was shorter by a thumb's breadth.

O'Brien saw it. "An' who holds them?"

"The master," said Behane.

"Who cut them?"

"The master."

"Who says the short wan won't be shifted in his
fist?"

Sullivan leaned toward him. "Are ye callin' the captain a
cheat?"

"I am."

The captain's dignity returned now that twelve weaker creatures might
enforce it. "You will draw when ordered."

Mahoney seized the strings and threw them overboard.

For a moment nobody spoke. The marline floated astern, four
pale threads and one short thread, until the swell covered
them.

Then Sullivan struck Mahoney with the pewter spoon. Its edge
opened the skin above his eye. Behane and Flynn pulled
him down. Casey, who had scarcely spoken for two days,
fastened a turn of rope around his wrists.

"Lubberly cowards," Mahoney said. "Every wan iv yez."

They tied him to the stump of the mizzen.

The captain prepared fresh lots. This time he tore strips
from a shirt, rolling them between his palms. He displayed
one short strip before folding all five into a cap.

The boys drew.

Doyle opened his fist first. Long.

Neddy's was long. Both Connells had long pieces.

O'Brien did not open his hand.

"Show ut," said the captain.

The boy looked at Mahoney. Mahoney pulled against the rope
until it cut his wrists.

"Show ut," Sullivan repeated.

O'Brien unfolded his fingers. The cloth strip appeared short.

There was a murmur of relief, indecent in its softness.

"He shifted them," O'Brien said. "I felt his fingers."

"The draw was fair," said Behane.

"Fair," Flynn repeated.

"Fair," said Casey.

The captain nodded. "It is a dreadful necessity."

"Draw again," said Mahoney.

"No need."

"All thirteen this time."

"The matter's settled," Sullivan said. "The lad'll do a good
deed, an' may God receive him."

O'Brien sprang at the captain. He was weak, but terror
lent him a few seconds of strength. His forehead struck
the man's mouth. They fell together. Sullivan crawled across the
deck and kicked the boy in the belly. Behane held
his legs. Flynn gripped his hair.

"Will ye murder me for a trick?" O'Brien screamed. "Ye
starvin' rats! Ye know the lot was changed!"

Doyle began to sob. Neddy hid his face.

The captain spat blood. "Tie him."

They bound O'Brien's ankles and wrists. Afterward no man wished
to proceed.

The boy lay beside the hatch-coaming, breathing rapidly. The delay
restored hope to him. He bargained first. He promised his
boots, his coat, his share of any water. He said
he would take every lookout and never sleep. He would
forgive them. He would tell no one if rescue came.

Then he appealed to each by name.

"Casey, ye've a son me age."

Casey turned his back.

"Behane, I gave ye the dry place when yer head
was split."

Behane studied the sea.

"Captain, I obeyed ye when them others would not."

The captain said, "Prepare yourself."

At last O'Brien cursed them. His voice cracked, but the
words came cleanly, assigning to each man a death more
shameful than the one intended for him. He cursed the
wreck, the calm, the empty sky, and the homes that
might receive these men. He cursed their wives for knowing
them and their children for bearing their names.

Sullivan stuffed oakum into his mouth.

They chose Gorman to kill him because Gorman had the
knife.

The cook recoiled until his back struck the bulwark.

"No. No, I cannot."

"Ye're the cook," said Sullivan.

"I cooked mate. I never killed ut."

"Do yer duty."

"Let the master do ut."

The captain said his position forbade it.

"Then Behane."

Behane's hand was injured.

"Flynn."

Flynn was too weak.

Each man possessed a reason. Only Gorman possessed the knife.

Sullivan dragged him forward. "Do yer duty, now. A good
deed for all hands."

Gorman's teeth chattered. "Sweet Jesus, don't make me."

"Do ut."

They decided first to take blood from the arm, as
though this lesser act might occur without the greater. The
tureen cover was placed beneath O'Brien's elbow. Its faded blue
flowers showed through a crust of salt.

Gorman opened the boy's sleeve with the knife. O'Brien twisted
and moaned behind the oakum. The skin of his arm
was mottled and shrunken. Gorman pressed the point into the
crook of the elbow.

Nothing came.

He made another incision. A dark bead formed, scarcely larger
than a pinhead, and stopped. He rubbed the arm, slapped
it, and cut again. The veins lay flattened beneath the
skin. The body had surrendered almost all its water to
breath, sweat, sores, and the Atlantic air. There was no
stream to catch.

Mahoney stared from the mast stump.

"Can't yez see?" he said. "God Himself has dried him
against yez."

"Be quiet," said the captain.

"Three cuts, an' no blood. Let him be."

For several seconds the men hesitated. The empty tureen cover
stood among them like a verdict.

O'Brien spat out the oakum. "Ye see now. Ye cannot
have me."

He smiled. It was a terrible, hopeful smile.

Then Sullivan said, "The throat."

Gorman dropped the knife.

Sullivan picked it up and put it back into his
hand.

"The throat," he repeated.

The cook began to laugh faintly, not from amusement but
because his breath would not arrange itself otherwise. "I don't
know how."

"Ye know enough."

"I'll spoil ut."

"Do yer duty."

They rolled O'Brien onto his back. The boy screamed then,
all bargaining and defiance gone. His heels hammered the planks.
Behane sat upon his knees. Flynn held one shoulder and
Casey the other. The captain looked away while continuing to
issue directions.

Mahoney tore one wrist free, leaving skin in the rope.
He crawled toward them. Sullivan struck him with a belaying
pin, and he fell beside the hatch.

Gorman knelt. His knife hand moved toward the stretched throat
and withdrew. O'Brien stared directly at him.

"Gorman," he whispered. "Don't."

The cook shut his eyes.

The first stroke was shallow. O'Brien convulsed, and the men
cursed Gorman for clumsiness. Sullivan caught the boy by the
hair and pulled his head back. Gorman sawed once, stopped,
and was threatened. He sawed again.

A thin dark flow ran at last into the tureen
cover.

The boy made a bubbling sound. His bound hands opened
and closed. The men held him until the movements became
small, then until they ceased.

No one spoke.

Mahoney, lying with his cheek against the wet deck, raised
his head. Beyond the captain's shoulder, on the western horizon,
there stood a patch of whiteness too square for cloud.
It broadened while he watched. A black hull lifted beneath
it.

"Ship," he said.

No one heard.

"Ship!"

They turned.

A barque was bearing down under topsails, close enough that
the sun flashed from her wet forefoot as it divided
the sea. Men could be seen at her rail. A
signal flag rose to her mizzen. Presently the report of
a gun crossed the water.

Rescue had been less than an hour away.

The survivors stared first at the barque, then at O'Brien.
The tureen cover held only a shallow pool. Sullivan snatched
it up and flung the blood overboard. Behane dragged a
tarpaulin across the body. The captain wiped the knife on
his trousers and threw it through a scupper.

"Say he died in the night," he whispered.

"Exposure," said Flynn.

"Before dawn," said Casey.

"The draw was fair," Behane muttered.

Mahoney sat up, blood running from his wrist.

The rescuing barque rounded to windward. Her boat was lowered,
the oars flashing as it came. The Francis Spaight's men
tried to cheer, but their voices were thin and broken.

Only Gorman made a strong sound.

He sat beside the blue-flowered tureen cover, looking at the
hump beneath the tarpaulin. A giggle escaped him. He pressed
both hands over his mouth, yet the sound forced itself
between his fingers. It rose as the rescue boat drew
near, rose above the creak of oars and the slap
of water against the wreck, rose until his body shook
and his head rolled backward.

When the boat's bow touched the Francis Spaight, Gorman was
laughing with all his strength.

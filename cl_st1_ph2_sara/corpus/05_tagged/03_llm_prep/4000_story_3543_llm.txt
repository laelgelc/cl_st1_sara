**EDITOR'S NOTE.—The following incident occurred many years ago, when I
still took pleasure in hunting. I have since come to
regard the killing of animals for amusement as wrong; but
I give the account as I remember it, without altering
either my actions or my thoughts at the time.**

It was near the end of December. Snow had fallen
for three days, and on the fourth morning we went
out after a large bear which had been seen in
the forest beyond the river.

There were six of us, besides two village hunters. One
of these was Damian, a young man not more than
twenty-five years old. He was short, broad in the shoulders,
and so light upon his feet that he seemed never
to sink into the snow. He had hunted bears since
boyhood and knew the forest as I knew the rooms
of my house.

We found the bear before noon in a fir thicket.
The hunters placed us along one side, while the beaters
entered from the other, shouting and striking the trees with
sticks.

The bear came out where we had not expected him.
One of our men fired from a distance and hit
him, but not in a vital place. I saw the
animal turn, bite at its side, and disappear among the
firs. There was blood on the snow, but not much.

"We must follow him now," I said.

Damian looked at the track.

"We can follow, sir, but we shall not come up
with him before dark."

"He is wounded."

"That is why he will go far."

The other hunters wished to return. The day was short,
the snow deep, and a wind was rising. But I
did not want to leave a wounded animal in the
forest. Damian agreed to go with me, and the rest
drove home in the sledges, promising to send horses to
a forester's hut where they supposed we would spend the
night.

Damian tightened the straps of his snowshoes. Mine were broad
wooden frames woven with rawhide. When I stepped forward, the
front of one caught under the snow, and I fell
upon my hands.

"You must not lift your feet so much," Damian said.
"Slide them. Like this."

He moved away over the snow as if he were
sitting in a boat that glided by itself. I tried
to do as he did, but every few steps I
caught one snowshoe against the other, trod on the back
of it, or struck a hidden branch. Damian went easily.
I followed with my gun across my shoulder and soon
began to perspire.

The bear's track was plain. At first he had gone
straight through the forest, breaking the crust and leaving dark
drops of blood in the holes. In some places he
had brushed against young firs and shaken the snow from
them. Once we found where he had stopped beside a
fallen tree. The snow there was pressed down and marked
with blood.

"He lay here," I said.

"No," said Damian. "He only sat and listened."

"How do you know?"

"If he had lain down, the mark would be longer.
Look. His hindquarters were here, and his forepaws there. He
heard us and went on."

The track led across a frozen stream, up a slope,
and into older forest. There the snow was softer. It
came above my knees when I stepped off the snowshoes,
and even with them I sank nearly to my ankles.
Small fir trees lay bent beneath it. Their tops caught
my clothes, and when I pushed through, they sprang up
behind me and threw snow down my neck.

After two hours my coat was wet through. The inside
of my mittens was wet, my boots were wet, and
my hair was wet. I was as wet as a
drowned rat, though the frost was severe.

"We must be close to him," I said.

Damian did not answer at once. He knelt and examined
the track where it passed between two birches.

"No, sir. See what he has done."

I saw only a confusion of marks.

"He went forward here," Damian said, "then turned and stepped
backward in his own track."

He pointed to the edge of one hole. The snow
was pressed in the opposite direction.

"He walked backward?"

"For twenty paces. Then he jumped aside."

We searched and found the place where the bear had
leaped from his old track into a clump of firs.
From there he had gone downhill without leaving any blood.

"He knows we are following," Damian said. "He wants us
to pass him."

I looked at the thick firs. They were dark beneath,
and a bear might have been lying within ten paces
without our seeing him.

"Is he there?"

"No. He has gone on."

Again Damian found the track. The bear had made a
wide circle and crossed our own trail. He had then
entered a stretch of young forest where every branch was
loaded with snow.

We followed until the daylight began to fade. The sky
between the trunks changed from grey to yellow and then
to a cold green. I could no longer see the
small drops of blood. Damian still saw them, or saw
things that told him the same story—a broken crust, a
hair on bark, a dark place where a paw had
pressed to the earth.

At last he stopped.

"We must leave the track now."

"Why?"

"If we follow farther, we shall push him out of
the circle. He is tired and wants to settle. We
must go round him and see where he has stayed."

I understood his plan. We were not to follow directly
but to make a wide circuit around the place where
the bear had entered. If we crossed his outgoing track,
we would know he had gone on. If we completed
the circuit without finding it, the bear would be inside.

We turned to the right. Damian marked our course by
the trees and by the slope of the land. I
had lost all sense of direction. The forest appeared the
same on every side—firs, bare birches, fallen trunks, and deep
snow.

It was hard work. We had to pass through thickets
where the snowshoes would not fit between the stems. I
fell over hidden logs. My gun caught in branches. Once
I stepped on a bent fir, and when my weight
freed it, it rose between my legs and threw me
on my back. Damian came to help me, but I
was angry from fatigue and told him to go on.

The circle seemed endless.

"We have passed this place already," I said.

"No, sir."

"That crooked birch—I saw it before."

"There are many crooked birches."

I thought he had lost the way but did not
say so. We went on another half-hour. Then Damian pointed
to a line of holes crossing in front of us.

"There is his track."

"So he has gone."

"This is the old track, where he entered."

He brushed snow from a small fir, and I saw
the place where we ourselves had passed earlier.

"We have made the circle," he said. "There is no
track going out. He is inside."

"Then we have him."

"If no one frightens him in the night."

By then it was nearly dark. The forester's hut was
several miles away, and Damian said we could not reach
it without crossing the bear's circle. We went instead to
a road used for hauling timber and found an empty
sledge track. The horses that were to meet us had
not come.

"We will wait," I said.

We waited until darkness covered the forest. No bells sounded,
and no light appeared.

Damian collected dry branches from beneath the firs. We scraped
away the snow beside a fallen tree and made a
fire. The wood smoked at first, then burned well. We
took off our outer coats and held them near the
flames. Steam rose from them. I had a piece of
bread and some cold meat in my pocket, and Damian
had two boiled potatoes. We divided everything.

After eating, I felt how tired I was. My legs
trembled when I stood. The fire warmed one side of
me while the other froze. Damian cut fir branches and
laid them on the snow.

"Sleep, sir," he said. "I will keep the fire."

"You must sleep too."

"I will wake you later."

I lay down in my coat with my feet toward
the fire. The branches were hard, and at first I
thought I would not sleep. Then the smoke turned above
me, the flames made a low sound, and I knew
nothing more.

When I opened my eyes, I could not understand where
I was. I seemed to be lying in a great
hall. White pillars rose on every side, and above them
was a black vault covered with stars. Everything glittered. For
a moment I remained still and looked in wonder.

Then I saw that the white pillars were birch trunks
coated with hoar-frost, the black vault was the winter sky,
and the stars shone between branches loaded with ice. The
fire had burned low. Damian sat beside it with his
head on his knees.

"Damian," I called.

He raised his head at once.

"It is morning soon," he said.

We put more wood on the fire. My boots had
frozen stiff, and I could hardly force my feet into
them. Our coats were dry on the outside but damp
within. We drank melted snow, ate the last crust of
bread, and set off toward the village.

We reached the road after sunrise and met the sledges
coming for us. By midmorning we had gathered the hunters
and thirty beaters. Damian explained where the bear lay and
placed each man.

The forest inside the circle was about half a mile
across. On one side there was a ravine, on another
a marsh covered with snow, and between them stood a
dense patch of young firs. Damian believed the bear had
settled there.

"You will stand at the lower end," he told me.
"He is most likely to pass near you."

"Why there?"

"The wind comes from that side. He will not want
to run into the men, and he will not climb
the steep bank. He will go along the hollow."

Damian placed another hunter, Sergei, fifty paces to my left.
Then he showed us two narrow openings among the trees.

"Do not shoot into the firs," he said. "Wait until
you see him clearly. And do not leave your places."

He went away to direct the beaters.

I loaded my gun and brushed snow from a fallen
trunk so that I might rest the barrels upon it.
The forest was very still. Hoar-frost fell from the branches
in small showers. Somewhere far behind me a man coughed.

Then the beaters began.

At first I heard only a faint call. Another voice
answered. Soon there were shouts across the whole upper side
of the circle.

"Come out! Come out! Hey, bear!"

Sticks struck tree trunks. The sound moved slowly nearer.

My heart beat strongly. I looked first at one opening,
then at the other. Each dark stump seemed to move.
Once a lump of snow fell from a fir, and
I raised my gun.

A magpie flew over the thicket and settled in a
birch. It cocked its tail and looked downward.

"He is there," I thought.

The magpie flew to another tree and again looked down.
Then it rose suddenly, giving a harsh cry.

At the same moment I heard a branch break.

A black shape moved between the firs. I saw the
bear's head, then lost it. He was coming quietly, not
running. He stepped into the first opening, turned sideways, and
looked toward the beaters.

I fired too quickly.

The smoke hid him. I heard Sergei fire almost at
once. The bear gave a short roar and disappeared among
the young trees.

"Missed," I thought. "We have both missed."

The shouting stopped for a moment and began again, louder
than before.

I reloaded. My fingers were clumsy, though I did not
feel the cold. I expected the bear to pass out
on the far side. Several minutes went by. A beater
shouted that he had seen him. Another cried that the
bear had turned back.

Then there was a roar near me.

The firs shook, and the bear came out not twenty
paces away. Blood covered one shoulder. His mouth was open,
and his ears lay flat against his head. He saw
me and came straight on.

He did not seem to run like an ordinary animal.
He came like a whirlwind, throwing snow from under him.
For an instant I watched without moving because I could
not believe he was coming at me. Then I raised
the gun and fired.

The bear stumbled but did not fall.

I fired the second barrel into his head. His lower
jaw dropped strangely to one side, and blood flew from
his mouth. Still he came.

I turned to run.

I had forgotten the fallen trunk behind me. My heel
struck it, and I fell backward in the snow. The
gun flew from my hands.

I saw the bear above me. He appeared enormous. His
black body shut out the trees. There was a hot,
rotten smell from his mouth. I put up my arms,
but he struck them aside and fell upon me with
his whole weight.

At first there was no pain, only pressure. I could
not breathe. His fur was cold and full of snow,
but beneath it his body was hot.

Then he took my face in his mouth.

I felt the upper teeth press into my forehead and
the lower teeth beneath my cheek. He bit slowly, as
if trying to get a proper hold. The broken jaw
prevented him from closing his mouth, yet the teeth cut
me like knives.

"This is the end," I thought.

I remember seeing a piece of blue sky between the
bear's head and a branch. I remember the snow beside
my ear turning red. I tried to push him away,
but my hands sank into the fur and had no
strength.

Then I heard Damian's voice.

"Leave him! Leave the master, you devil!"

The bear lifted his head but kept one paw on
my chest.

Damian came running from the side. He had no gun.
In his hand was only a thick stick which he
had used to mark the beaters' line.

"Get away! What are you doing? Let him alone!"

He shouted at the bear as if it were a
dog worrying a sheep. He struck the stick against a
tree and advanced.

The bear turned toward him. I saw the blood hanging
from its broken mouth. Damian stopped two or three steps
away and raised the stick.

"Come then!" he shouted. "Come to me!"

For a moment the bear crouched. I expected it to
spring and kill him. But the shouts of the beaters
were coming nearer, and men were crashing through the thicket.
The bear looked toward them, then back at Damian. Suddenly
it left me, bounded across the hollow, and disappeared among
the firs.

Damian knelt beside me.

"Are you alive, sir?"

"Yes," I said, though the word came out badly.

"Can you see?"

I opened my eyes. One was full of blood, but
with the other I saw his face.

"I can see."

He called the others. Sergei was the first to arrive.
When he looked at me, he turned pale and said,
"His face is gone."

This frightened me more than the attack had done.

"Give me snow," I said.

Damian packed clean snow against my forehead and cheek. It
melted at once and ran down my neck. The pain
now came strongly. It burned and throbbed, and I felt
as though half my face were hanging loose.

The men made a sledge from two poles and their
coats, but when I tried to lie on it the
blood ran into my throat. I said I could walk.
With Damian on one side and Sergei on the other,
I went to the road.

We met the horses there. Someone wrapped my head in
a scarf. I sat in the sledge while the driver
urged the horse toward the nearest town.

The road was rough. At every hollow the sledge struck
hard, and pain went through my head. I held the
edges of my face together with both hands. I did
not faint, though I often wished to.

We reached the doctor in the afternoon. He was an
old military surgeon. He removed the scarf, washed away the
blood, and examined the wounds without speaking.

"Well?" I asked.

"You have been fortunate."

I thought he was making a joke.

"My face?"

"The forehead is torn, the cheek is cut through, and
there are wounds below the eye and near the mouth.
But the eye is safe, the nose is not broken,
and the main artery has not been touched."

He gave me spirits to drink and began to sew.

There was no proper means of deadening the pain. Two
men held my shoulders while he passed the needle through
the skin. I counted the stitches at first, but after
twenty I lost count. The doctor worked calmly, pulling together
one part, then another. Once he cut away a small
piece that would not fit.

"You will have scars," he said, "but you will keep
your face."

When he had finished, my head was covered in bandages,
with openings for one eye, my nose, and my mouth.
Damian had waited outside the whole time. I asked that
he be brought in.

"You saved me," I told him.

He looked embarrassed.

"I only shouted."

"You ran at the bear with a stick."

"There was nothing else nearby."

"Were you not afraid?"

"Of course I was afraid. But he was eating you."

He said this so simply that no one answered.

For several days I had fever, and my face swelled
until I could scarcely open my mouth. The doctor changed
the dressings and watched for corruption in the wounds. None
came. In two weeks he removed most of the stitches.
In another week I could eat without difficulty.

When I first looked properly in a mirror, I saw
a red line from my forehead to the corner of
my left eye, two marks across the cheek, and several
smaller scars near the jaw. They were ugly then, but
the doctor said they would fade.

The bear, meanwhile, had not been found. The men followed
his bloody track after taking me away, but darkness came,
and they stopped. Snow fell that night and covered the
signs.

About a month later word reached me that the same
bear had been seen near a charcoal burner's hut. He
had killed a dog and broken open a storehouse where
oats were kept. Damian said there could be no mistake
because the lower jaw hung to one side.

My wounds were not fully healed, but I went with
the hunters. I did not take a place in the
line. I stood with the sledges at the edge of
the forest and listened.

The beat had hardly begun when two shots sounded, one
after the other. Then there was a pause and a
third shot.

After some time a man came running.

"Damian has killed him."

They brought the bear out on a sledge drawn by
two horses. Even dead, he seemed immense. His fur was
black, with brown tips on the shoulders, and his paws
were broader than a man's head. One bullet had passed
through the shoulder. Damian's final shot had entered behind the
ear.

We examined the jaw. My first close shot had shattered
the lower bone and driven several teeth sideways. The second
had passed through the flesh beneath the eye without reaching
the brain. The older wound was in the shoulder. It
was plain why the animal had been unable to bite
with full force. Had the jaw not been broken, the
doctor said, the bear would have crushed my skull.

I had the skin prepared and the whole body mounted.
For many years it stood in my room, upright against
the wall, with its forepaws raised and its black mouth
open. Visitors often admired its size and asked who had
killed it.

I always told them that Damian killed it a month
after it had nearly killed me, and that before killing
it he had saved my life with nothing in his
hands but a stick.

In time the scars faded. One remained near my eye,
and another could be seen when I smiled, but strangers
seldom noticed them. The bear's marks passed away more quickly
than the recollection of its weight upon my chest, the
heat of its breath, and Damian's voice in the snowy
forest:

"Leave him! What are you doing? Let him alone!"

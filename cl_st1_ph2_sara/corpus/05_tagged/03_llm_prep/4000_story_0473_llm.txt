We first saw Patsy Burns one raw March afternoon, when
the wind came charging across Soldiers Field with nothing to
stop it but our sweaters and our profanity. We were
getting the shot-putters into shape for the Dual, and Bull
Fosgill, our scratch man, had just made forty-four feet seven
in practice—a put which pleased him so little that he
stood glaring at the dent in the turf as if
somebody else had made it.

"Rotten," said Bull.

"Within three inches of your best," I reminded him.

"Rotten," he repeated.

Just then a small voice behind us remarked, "Yer foot
slipped."

We turned, and there stood Patsy.

He was perhaps the smallest, dirtiest, most self-possessed human being
who ever criticized a Harvard champion. His trousers had been
manufactured for a larger boy and a happier age; his
coat had no discernible buttons; and through the split of
one shoe appeared a toe, blue with cold. Above all
this ruin rose a thin, sharp face, old with the
sins and sorrows of a hundred centuries, and two gray
eyes in which there was no appeal whatever.

Bull looked him over.

"Who asked you?"

"Nobody," said Patsy. "But it did."

Bull grinned. "Come here, professor."

Patsy approached without embarrassment, inspected the circle, and pointed to
the board.

"Yer heel come up. You was tryin' too hard."

Bull tried again, keeping his heel down, and made forty-five
feet one.

From that moment Patsy belonged to us.

At first he merely watched. Then he fetched the shots,
swept the circle, carried sweaters, and shouted distances before the
tape had been stretched. We never appointed him to anything.
He assumed office, and within a week no throw was
official until Patsy had considered it with his head on
one side and pronounced it "pretty fair" or "punk."

He had a special contempt for Tanner, our second man,
who was six feet three and nervous.

"You ain't got no snap," Patsy told him. "You're all
stretched out like a clothesline."

"What do you know about it, you Mucker?" Tanner demanded.

"I am a Mucker," said Patsy proudly. "That's why I
know."

He had picked up the word somewhere and regarded it
as the title of an ancient and honorable order. We
could never discover precisely where he lived. Sometimes he slept
with an aunt in Cambridgeport, sometimes with a married sister
in Somerville, and sometimes, as he put it, "around." His
father had gone west after an argument with a patrolman;
his mother was dead, though upon that point Patsy's information
was curiously indefinite.

"She went away when I was little," he said.

"Dead?" I asked.

"Guess so."

"Don't you know?"

"Naw. If she was alive she'd come back, wouldn't she?"

He said it not plaintively but as one settles a
proposition in geometry. Then he stole half my sandwich and
told me I ought to eat slower.

Bull was his hero. The rest of us were tolerated
as useful members of the establishment, but Bull was Harvard,
strength, glory, and the whole dazzling future compressed into one
enormous person. Every afternoon Patsy appeared at Bull's elbow.

"Hello, Bull."

"Hello, kid."

"How far to-day?"

"Forty-six, if you keep quiet."

"You won't do it."

"What'll you bet?"

"Nothin'. I ain't a sucker."

When practice ended and the shadows grew long across the
field, Patsy waited while Bull put on his coat.

"Good night, Bull."

"Good night, kid."

The formula never varied. Looking back, I think those little
greetings meant more to Patsy than prayers mean to most
of us.

One day he tried to carry a sixteen-pound shot back
to the circle. He got both hands under it, raised
it as high as his knees, and staggered forward with
a face of terrible solemnity. We watched until he dropped
it and sat down abruptly beside it.

"Heavy?" Tanner asked.

"Naw," said Patsy, rubbing his foot. "Ground's slippery."

Bull lifted the shot in one hand and tossed it
lightly from palm to palm.

"Going to be a shot-putter, Patsy?"

"Sure."

"When?"

"When I get to Harvard."

Nobody laughed. There was something in the way he said
it which made laughter impossible.

"So you're coming to Harvard?" I asked.

"Sure. Bull's here."

"That'll require some schooling."

"I can read."

"How far have you gone?"

"Fourth grade—most of it."

"And how old are you?"

"'Leven."

We found afterward that he was nearly thirteen. Malnutrition had
made a diminutive wisp of him, a starved, slender elf
with the chest of a pigeon and wrists no thicker
than the handle of a racket. Yet in his own
estimation his physical development was merely delayed by the season.

"I'll fill out," he explained. "Bull was little once."

Bull, who at birth had probably weighed fourteen pounds and
asked for a dumb-bell, nodded gravely.

"I was a regular runt."

Patsy examined him with admiration and hope.

"See?" he said to us.

After that his plans unfolded rapidly. He would enter Harvard,
put the shot fifty feet, defeat Yale, and become a
"sportin' editor or somethin'." It was all doped out. The
small matters of food, lodging, preparatory school, tuition, and elementary
arithmetic were details to be attended to by a Providence
which, in Patsy's experience, had never yet attended to anything.

We began to discuss him at dinner. It was Tanner
who first suggested the idea seriously.

"We can send the kid to school."

"With what?" somebody asked.

"There are twelve of us. A dollar apiece a week
wouldn't kill anybody."

"Bull eats more than that in pie."

Bull looked up. "I'll go two."

We mapped out Patsy's future with the sublime confidence of
undergraduates. A decent family could be found to take him.
Dr. Wainwright would examine his lungs. Some tutor would knock
the fourth grade into him and prepare him for a
school where scholarships were obtainable. After that, of course, Harvard.
We saw him already transformed, clean and broad-shouldered, wearing the
crimson, with Bull beside the circle advising him not to
foul.

We meant it, too. There was not a man among
us who would have backed out. We should have carried
the thing through, somehow, if Patsy hadn't gone and spoiled
it all.

We decided not to tell him until after the Dual.
The meet had become too close for comfort. Yale had
the better runners; we counted on the field events, and
particularly upon Bull. He had beaten their best man, Carver,
the year before, but Carver was reported to be putting
forty-six feet consistently. Bull said the reports were newspaper rot.
This was his usual comment upon information that worried him.

The afternoon of the Dual came warm and bright, with
the stands banked in color and the track as clean
as a ruled line. There is no sound quite like
the sound before a great college meet—the band hammering away,
the captains shouting, the crowd rising and settling, and under
it all the nervous talk of men pretending that the
thing is not worth a hang.

Patsy got in through a hole in the fence. We
knew that because he told us.

"Ticket man chased me," he added cheerfully.

"Did he catch you?" Bull asked.

"Do I look caught?"

He wore a crimson rag around his neck and had
washed his face. That washing made him seem thinner and
older. Bull gave him a sweater to hold, and Patsy
hugged it against his narrow body as if it had
been a flag entrusted to him in battle.

By the time the shot put was called, Yale led
on points. We had figured every event, and the figures
had gone wrong in three places. Unless Bull took first
and Tanner at least third, the meet was practically over.

There were seven starters. The preliminaries were slow and ugly.
Tanner fouled his first put, made forty-two feet three on
his second, and fell below that on his third. Carver
opened with forty-five feet eight. Bull answered with forty-five ten
and one-half.

"Yer rushin' again," Patsy told him.

Bull pretended not to hear, but on his next attempt
he held longer in the crouch and drove the shot
forty-six feet two.

Carver made forty-six feet even.

That left Bull leading by two inches as the finals
began.

I was measuring at the sector line. Tanner stood near
the rear of the circle, wrapped in a blanket. Patsy
had somehow worked his way inside the rope and was
crouched beside Bull's sweater, his eyes fixed upon the contestants.

The other Yale man, a big, loose-jointed fellow named Mercer,
improved to forty-four feet. Tanner got forty-three one and moved
into third. We breathed again. Then Carver made forty-six feet
five.

Bull's first final put was a foul by half an
inch.

His second went forty-six feet four and three-quarters.

We watched the officials bend over the tape. A quarter
of an inch! Bull's jaw set hard, and Patsy muttered
something unprintable concerning Yale measurements.

Only the last round remained. Tanner failed to improve. Mercer
fouled. Carver entered the circle, turned smoothly, and put forty-six
feet six.

It was a handsome performance, and even our crowd applauded.

Bull rubbed the shot against his neck.

"Six and a half?" he asked.

"Six," I said.

He nodded. There was no joking in him now.

At that instant Tanner, who had been practicing idly behind
the circle with another shot, attempted a standing put. His
foot slipped on the trampled grass. The shot tore out
of his hand sidewise and low.

The whole thing was beastly sudden and nasty.

Bull had turned to watch Carver's measurement. The shot was
coming at the back of his skull.

Somebody yelled.

Bull began to turn.

Patsy sprang.

He struck Bull behind the knees with both hands. Bull
lurched forward, and the shot missed his head.

It did not miss Patsy.

There was a thick sound—not loud—and the little body went
down as if its strings had been cut.

For a second none of us moved. Then Bull was
on his knees. Tanner stood where he had thrown, greenish-white,
his arms hanging.

"Get the doctor!" somebody shouted.

The doctor came running from the track. He knelt, opened
Patsy's shirt, and put an ear to that miserable narrow
chest. Patsy's face had gone gray, but his eyes were
open.

"Bull?" he whispered.

"I'm here, kid."

"You all right?"

"Yes."

Patsy seemed satisfied. Then the doctor pushed us back.

They carried him away on a stretcher. His head rolled
slightly with every step, and one small dirty hand hung
below the blanket. Bull followed to the gate until the
track captain caught him.

"Fosgill! You've got one put left."

Bull looked at him without understanding.

"The meet," the captain said. "We need it."

"I'm through."

From the stretcher came a faint voice.

"Bull!"

They stopped.

Bull bent over him.

"Put it," Patsy breathed. "Beat 'em."

Then his eyes closed.

Bull came back. I shall never forget his face. He
entered the circle, set the shot against his neck, and
stood quite still. The crowd knew something had happened, though
few understood what, and the field had become strangely quiet.

Bull crouched. He drove across the circle with one savage
rush, checked at the board, and the shot went up
and out against the sun.

It landed beyond the forty-seven-foot mark.

Forty-seven feet three inches, the tape said—a Harvard record in
those days.

We won the shot, and, by those points, we won
the Dual.

But none of us saw the finish. We piled into
two automobiles and raced to the hospital, Tanner sitting forward
with his face in his hands and Bull saying nothing
at all.

The surgeon met us in a white corridor.

"His ribs are crushed," he said. "There is grave internal
injury."

"Will he live?" Bull asked.

The surgeon looked at Bull's track clothes and at the
mud upon the rest of us.

"He may recover consciousness."

That was his answer.

We waited for more than an hour. Somebody telephoned about
expenses; somebody else tried to find Patsy's relatives. The aunt
in Cambridgeport had moved. The married sister was unknown at
the address he had given us. We could discover nobody
who belonged to him.

That hurt us peculiarly. We had been planning to take
charge of his life, and now, when there was almost
none of it left, no one existed who had a
better right than we.

At last the nurse called Bull.

"We're all going," he said.

She objected, but the surgeon let us stand inside the
room, against the wall. Patsy looked absurdly small in the
white bed. Bandages covered his chest. His breathing came in
shallow, careful sips, as though each breath cost more than
he could afford.

Bull sat beside him.

After a time Patsy opened his eyes.

"Hello, Bull," he said.

Bull swallowed. "Hello, kid."

"Did yer put?"

"Yes."

"How far?"

"Forty-seven three."

A little pride brightened his face.

"Knew yer could."

"We won, Patsy."

"The Dual?"

"The Dual."

He considered this, and the shadow of his old impudent
grin appeared.

"I helped some, huh?"

"You won it for us," Bull said.

Patsy's eyes moved toward the line of us by the
wall. "All the fellows here?"

"All of us," I said.

"Gee."

He was silent a while. The nurse touched Bull's shoulder,
but Bull shook his head.

Presently Patsy whispered, "I could 'a' been good, couldn't I?"

There was no room then for truth of the ordinary
sort.

Bull leaned close. "You'd have been the greatest shot-putter Harvard
ever had."

"Better'n you?"

"Better than me."

Patsy smiled. "I'd 'a' showed Tanner some snap."

From the wall came a sound which Tanner turned into
a cough.

Patsy's eyes closed again, and we thought he had gone.
But after several minutes he asked, "You think my mother
knows?"

"Yes," Bull said quickly.

"Where she is?"

"Yes."

"She seen me shove you?"

"I'm sure she did."

"Guess she ain't sore at me, then."

"No, kid."

His hand moved over the sheet. Bull took it. The
great brown hand covered the small one completely.

"Harvard won," Patsy murmured.

"Yes."

"An' I'd 'a' made it—Harvard, I mean?"

"We had it fixed," Bull told him. "School and everything.
You were coming."

Patsy looked pleased, but not surprised. In his mind it
had always been arranged.

"I knew it," he said.

The light was failing beyond the high hospital windows. Somewhere
in the corridor a cart rattled, and a bell rang
twice. We stood there, twelve strong young men who had
been very certain that afternoon of our ability to manage
races, records, money, and the future, and none of us
could do anything but wait.

Patsy's fingers tightened once around Bull's.

"Good night, Bull," he whispered.

Bull bent lower.

"Good night, kid."

Patsy lay quiet. Then, with a little effort, he opened
his eyes once more.

"Naw," he said. "Not good night."

His breath caught. He looked at Bull steadily.

"Good-by—Bull."

Bull pressed the small hand between both of his.

"Good-by, Patsy."

The gray eyes rested on him for another moment. Then
they closed.

Patsy didn't speak again.

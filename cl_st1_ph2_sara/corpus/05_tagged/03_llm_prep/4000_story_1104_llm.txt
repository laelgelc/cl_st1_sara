### A Little Child

When Robert Dixon came home to Danvers after seven years'
absence, the town received him with that mixture of affection
and suspicion which is commonly reserved for those who have
gone away to be improved. The improvement itself was not
denied. It was visible in his black coat, in the
restraint of his gestures, and in the grave, attentive manner
with which he listened even to persons who had nothing
particular to say. But whether it had made him a
better preacher was another matter.

His father, the Reverend Abram Dixon, had never studied theology
except under the birds and the trees and the winds
of heaven. He knew little of books beyond the one
Book, and of that he knew whole provinces by heart.
He had preached for forty-two years in the little wooden
church beyond the honeysuckle road. When the Spirit descended upon
him, as he expressed it, he needed neither manuscript nor
clock. His sermons rose, rushed, thundered, broke into song, and
sometimes lasted until the dinner fires in Danvers had burned
nearly out.

Abram loved his son with a love too proud to
confess its anxieties. He had sent Robert to school by
selling two acres of good bottom land and by wearing
the same Sunday coat until the seams shone like satin.
Yet education, to his mind, was an uncertain servant. It
might sharpen a man's tools, but it might also teach
him to admire the tools and forget the field.

On the evening of Robert's arrival, the family sat in
the best room, whose chief treasures were a chintz-covered sofa
and a glass lamp that smoked whenever the wick was
turned too high. Robert's mother watched him as though he
might disappear between one blink and the next. Abram questioned
him.

"An' so dey gives you a vacation?" he said.

"For three weeks."

Abram shook his head. "Devil don't take no vacation."

"No, sir," Robert answered, smiling. "That is one reason ministers
sometimes need one."

His mother laughed softly. Abram looked from one to the
other, uncertain whether the answer was wisdom or city cleverness.

"I hear," he continued, "dat young preachers plays ball at
dem schools."

"Some do."

"Kickin' an' runnin' in short clothes?"

"Not while preaching."

His mother laughed again, and even Abram's mouth relaxed.

The next Sunday Robert was to preach in his father's
pulpit. For days the event had been spoken of at
wells, kitchen doors, and fence corners. People came from farms
six miles distant. The church windows stood open to the
hum of bees and the sweet, heavy breath of wild
honeysuckle. Women stirred their fans; children, scrubbed into discomfort, sat
between watchful elders; and the deacons filled the amen-corner with
solemn expectation.

Abram gave out the hymn with unusual magnificence. When Robert
rose, the old man settled back, folded his arms, and
prepared to enjoy a vindication of sacrifice. He expected learning,
certainly, but learning set on fire. He wished the congregation
to hear what the colleges had added to the Dixon
thunder.

Robert was nervous. Not because he feared the people, but
because he loved them. He knew their expectation, and knew
also that he could not honestly satisfy it. During his
years in the city he had come to distrust emotion
that left conduct unchanged. He had seen men shout on
Sunday and cheat on Monday, women pray for heathen children
while ignoring hungry children under their own stairs. Yet he
would not wound his father by saying that the old
way was false. Much of it was true, and some
of it was beautiful. He merely believed it incomplete.

His text was, "Be ye doers of the word, and
not hearers only."

He spoke quietly of religion in the home, the shop,
the field, and the street. He said that prayer should
make a man more honest in weighing corn; that conversion
ought to be visible in clean yards, patient tempers, educated
children, and care for the sick. He spoke of Christ
not only in the temple but beside the road, among
the poor, touching those whom others passed at a distance.

The sermon was clear, earnest, and twenty-eight minutes long.

When Robert sat down, there was a silence in which
one could hear a wasp troubling the windowpane. Then a
deacon said "Amen," but in the tone of a man
who has remembered a duty slightly too late.

Abram rose for the closing prayer. His voice, ordinarily rich
with confidence, was constrained. After the congregation had gone, leaving
behind the odour of warm pine boards and crushed flowers,
he remained beside the pulpit.

"You done finished?" he asked.

"Yes, sir."

"Was dat all de sermon?"

"That was all."

Abram rubbed his chin. "I kep' waitin'."

"I know."

"Waitin' for you to rise."

"I was standing, Father."

"I don't mean on yo' feet. I mean rise."

Robert lowered his eyes. "Perhaps I could not rise any
higher without leaving what I had to say."

The old man looked hurt, and Robert regretted the sentence
as soon as it was spoken.

"You talked 'bout brooms an' schoolbooks an' fair weights," Abram
said. "All good things. But folks comes to church to
git religion."

"I thought I was talking about religion."

"Mebbe." Abram turned away. "Mebbe school religion is quieter'n de
kind I knows."

At dinner the matter was not mentioned. Robert's mother passed
the chicken with determined cheerfulness, and Abram praised the biscuits
as if defending the household against an unnamed enemy. But
throughout Danvers the judgment was already forming. The young preacher
had education, people said; no one could deny that. He
arranged his words properly. Still, there had been no mounting,
no trembling, no sudden cry to heaven. Sister Bell declared
that she had not felt "one blessed chill."

Robert returned to the city carrying his disappointment without bitterness.
He had not expected to convert Danvers in a morning;
yet he had hoped that his father would understand him.
Abram, for his part, continued to love Robert and to
doubt him. In letters he asked after his health, his
mother's preserves, and the number of baptisms, but never after
the brooms and schoolbooks.

Nearly a year passed before Abram came to the city.

The journey was undertaken reluctantly. Robert had written that the
church would celebrate its anniversary and begged his father to
visit. Abram replied that he was too old to go
chasing railroad smoke. His wife replied for him that he
would come. Thus, on a damp October afternoon, he descended
from the train into a confusion of porters, bells, wheels,
cries, smoke, and hurrying faces.

Robert met him at the station.

"Dis place," Abram announced, "sounds like Judgment Day wid machinery."

"You'll grow accustomed to it."

"I don't aim to stay long enough."

Robert's rooms adjoined the mission chapel and were plain enough
to reassure even Abram, though the old man examined a
cushioned chair with some distrust.

"You sit on dat every day?"

"When I have time."

"Looks soft for a preacher."

"It is mostly used by people who come to ask
for help."

Abram pressed the cushion with one finger. "Den trouble got
a comfortable seat."

Before supper there came a knock. A small girl stood
outside, bareheaded in the rain. Her dress was too thin,
and one shoe had been tied with string.

"Mister Dixon," she said, "Miss Cora says will you come?
Benny's sick again."

Robert took his hat.

"Father, I must go a few streets over. Supper is
ready. Please don't wait."

Abram looked at the child, whose wet hair clung to
her narrow forehead.

"I'll go 'long," he said.

They followed her through streets unlike any Abram had imagined.
Tall houses leaned toward one another as if burdened with
secrets. Beneath them were cellars in which families lived below
the level of the pavement. Children slept on doorsills or
played beside gutters whose dark water carried refuse toward the
wharves. The air smelled of coal smoke, fish, wet rope,
and too many human beings crowded into too little space.

Benny lay in a room at the top of three
broken flights. His mother washed clothes at night and had
not yet returned. Robert felt the boy's forehead, opened a
window, kindled a better fire, and sent the girl for
a physician with a note.

"You done dis before," Abram observed.

"Often."

"Who pays de doctor?"

"The church, when the church can. I do, when it
cannot."

"And if neither can?"

Robert smiled faintly. "Then I persuade the doctor that he
can."

They returned late. Supper had dried at the edges, but
Abram said no word of complaint. Before he slept, he
heard another knock and Robert's low voice in the passage.

The next morning a policeman came to the mission holding
a boy by the collar. The child was perhaps ten,
though hunger had made him smaller. His face was defiant,
but his eyes followed a loaf lying on Robert's table.

"Caught him stealing from a baker," the officer said. "Loaf
under his coat."

"I didn't steal it," the boy muttered.

"On trial," said the officer. "That's what you are."

"Have you eaten today?" Robert asked.

The boy's mouth tightened.

"Yesterday?"

No answer.

Robert cut the loaf and spread a piece with butter.
The boy looked first at the food, then at the
officer, as though suspecting a legal trap.

"Eat," Robert said.

He ate. Defiance vanished under the first mouthful, leaving only
the terrible haste of hunger. Abram turned toward the window.

The baker arrived shortly afterward, red-faced and indignant. He had
lost goods for weeks, he declared, and somebody must be
made an example. Robert listened without interrupting. Then he paid
for the loaf, though the baker valued it with remarkable
generosity, and asked whether sending the boy to the house
of correction would restore the missing bread.

"It'll teach him."

"What?"

"Not to steal."

"Will it teach him how to earn?"

The baker stared.

Robert proposed that the boy work each morning at the
bakery, receiving breakfast and a small wage, while attending the
mission school in the afternoon. The baker objected, yielded, objected
again, and finally consented, partly because Robert's patience left anger
with nothing solid to strike.

When they were alone, Abram asked the boy his name.

"Joe."

"Where yo' folks?"

"Don't know."

"Where you sleep?"

"Anywhere."

"An' you took dat bread?"

Joe glanced at Robert. "I was hungry."

"That ain't a answer."

"It is part of one," Robert said gently.

Abram looked from the boy to his son. "What you
gwine do wid him?"

"Feed him first. Bathe him, if he permits it. Find
him a bed. Tomorrow we shall begin discovering what else
he needs."

"You trust him?"

"Not with the cashbox."

Joe grinned despite himself.

"But I trust that he can become trustworthy," Robert added.

That evening Abram watched Robert preside over a roomful of
boys gathered from streets, cellars, stables, and wharves. Some were
Black, some white, and several appeared chiefly grey beneath accumulated
city dust. They wriggled, whispered, exchanged buttons, and pursued private
quarrels under the desks. Abram expected Robert to command them
with the voice he had so conspicuously failed to use
in Danvers.

Instead, Robert rang a small bell.

"Gentlemen," he said, "we shall begin when all gentlemen are
ready."

The boys quieted by degrees, amused at being called what
few adults supposed them capable of becoming. Robert taught them
to read from simple stories. He explained fractions by dividing
apples, geography by following the routes of ships they had
seen at the wharf, and morality by matters within their
experience: a fair turn, a kept promise, a smaller boy
defended against a larger one.

There was no shouting. Yet their eyes remained fixed upon
him. When he rebuked, he did not humiliate; when he
praised, the hardest face brightened. A child named Sam slipped
his hand into Robert's as naturally as a country child
might catch at a low branch.

Abram sat near the stove, saying nothing.

At the close Robert read, "Feed my lambs."

"Who are the lambs?" he asked.

"Us," said Joe from the rear.

The answer brought laughter, but Abram did not laugh. He
saw the bare feet beneath the benches, the mended sleeves,
the watchful faces too old in one expression and wholly
childish in the next. He saw his son among them,
not rising above them in a storm of words, but
bending close enough to hear.

On the way upstairs Abram stopped halfway.

"Robert."

"Yes, Father?"

"Dat boy—Joe. If de policeman took him, where he go?"

"To prison first, perhaps. Then to an institution."

"Wid thieves?"

"Yes."

"An' when he come out?"

"He might know more about theft than when he entered."

Abram climbed another step.

"On trial," he said slowly. "Boy was on trial."

"So the officer said."

"Mebbe." Abram rested his hand on the rail. "Mebbe somebody
else was on trial too."

Robert did not answer. He understood that silence is sometimes
the most respectful form of agreement.

The anniversary service was held on Sunday. Robert had asked
Abram to preach weeks before, and the old man had
accepted with the confidence of one who expects to instruct
a younger generation. By Saturday night that confidence had deserted
him.

"I ain't fit," he said.

"You have preached for forty-two years."

"Dat ain't what I mean."

Robert waited.

Abram sat in the cushioned chair he had once distrusted.
"I thought I knowed what you was doin' up here.
Thought you had cooled religion down till it wouldn't warm
nobody. I come to see." He shook his head. "I
didn't understan'."

"You understood many things I am still learning."

"No, son. Don't put a pillow under my pride. Let
it bump de floor."

Robert smiled.

"I didn't understan'," Abram repeated. "When you preached home, I
listened for thunder. Didn't hear de rain."

The church was crowded the next morning. It was not
like the church at Danvers. Its walls stood between a
pawnshop and a warehouse; wagon wheels rattled beyond the windows;
and the congregation included washerwomen, labourers, teachers, sailors, prosperous families,
and children who had come principally because Robert had promised
they might sit together.

Abram entered the pulpit with his great Bible under his
arm. For a moment the old habit returned. His shoulders
lifted; his voice sought its ancient resonance.

"My text," he began, "is from de prophet Isaiah: 'A
little child shall lead them.'"

An answering murmur rose from the older members. Abram looked
down and saw Joe in a clean shirt, Benny pale
but recovering, and Sam leaning against the pew in complete
confidence that Robert would not permit him to be turned
out.

"I been preachin' a long time," Abram said. "Long enough
to git proud of it. I could make folks cry.
Sometimes I could make 'em shout. An' after dey shouted,
I reckoned de Lord had done His work."

His voice softened.

"But de Lord ain't always in de noise. Elijah learned
dat, but Abram Dixon had to come all de way
to dis city to find it out."

There was gentle laughter.

"I had a son went off to school. He come
home talkin' quiet 'bout clean houses, honest scales, books for
children, an' bread for de hungry. I says, 'Where de
religion?' I didn't understan'. No, friends, I didn't understan'."

He told them of the wet child at the door,
the sick boy in the upper room, the loaf beneath
Joe's coat, and the school beside the stove. He did
not make Robert a hero, for he knew his son
would suffer under public praise. He spoke instead of a
gospel that walked up broken stairs, entered police stations, bargained
with bakers, washed small faces, and sat patiently beside ignorance.

"I used to think preachin' was tellin' folks 'bout de
road," he said. "But sometimes preachin' is pickin' up a
child dat fell in it. Sometimes it is bread. Sometimes
it is soap. Sometimes it is standin' 'tween a little
boy an' a prison door."

His words gathered force, but not the old force. There
was warmth without display, emotion disciplined by what he had
seen.

"Dat officer says, 'On trial.' Yes, sir. Joe was on
trial. De baker was on trial. De city was on
trial. De church was on trial. An' a old preacher
from Danvers was on trial too."

Joe stared at his shoes.

"A little child led me. More'n one little child. Dey
led me up dem stairs. Dey led me down by
de wharf. Dey led me into dis church. An' my
son—"

Abram paused. Robert, seated below, lowered his head.

"My son walked wid 'em."

The church was very still.

"I came here thinkin' I might set dis young preacher
right. But I tell you today, de Lord has set
me right. I been converted."

An old woman near the aisle whispered, "Bless God."

"Yes, ma'am," Abram said. "Converted after forty-two years in de
pulpit. Shows you better keep watch. Devil don't take no
vacation, an' neither does pride."

The laughter that followed had tears within it.

Abram closed his Bible.

"Feed de lambs," he said. "Not only wid words. Feed
'em bread. Feed 'em truth. Feed 'em kindness. Feed 'em
a chance. An' when you done dat, don't boast you
brought Christ to 'em. Look close. You may find He
was already there, waitin' for you to come."

There was no shout at the end, no storm of
exhortation. The congregation remained silent for a breath, and then
began to sing.

After the service Abram stood at the door, shaking hands.
Joe approached last.

"Reverend," the boy said, "was you talking about me?"

"Some."

"I ain't a lamb."

"No?"

"I'm too old."

Abram examined him gravely. "How old?"

"Ten. Maybe eleven."

"Ancient," Abram said. "But de Lord can use old folks.
He used me."

Joe considered this and then grinned.

Robert came down the steps beside his father. Outside, the
city continued its unmusical Sabbath: wheels striking stones, vendors calling,
children quarrelling, bells sounding from many steeples. Abram looked upon
it without approval, but no longer without understanding.

"Father," Robert said, "that was a fine sermon."

Abram took his arm.

"Mebbe."

"You rose."

The old man glanced at him sharply, then saw the
smile Robert could not quite suppress.

"No, son," he said. "Dis time I come down."

And together they walked toward the mission, where several children
were already waiting at the door.

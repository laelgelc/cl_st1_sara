### The Last Vigil

All the lodgers had gone away from the city.

They had fled in twos and threes before the furnace-breath
of August: to the sea, to the mountains, to little
villages where trees still cast shadows and night winds moved
among the curtains. Even the mistress of the boarding house
had departed, leaving Mary, the servant, to sleep with a
cousin in the next street and return each morning to
perform such duties as could not be postponed.

Thus the house stood deserted—an upright, narrow sepulchre of brown
stone—while in a room upon the third floor Margaret Vale
watched beside her dying husband.

Beyond the rear windows were brick walls, wooden fences, strips
of scorched earth, and fire escapes hanging black against a
sky from which the daylight seemed to descend not as
radiance but as heat. The yards below contained no living
thing. Even the weeds drooped. From the avenue came the
muffled and intermittent tumult of wheels, bells, and human voices;
yet those sounds appeared infinitely remote, like noises transmitted through
a hot suspended ocean.

Within the room there was no motion save the slow
lifting of a muslin curtain and the scarcely perceptible movement
of the sheet over Edward's breast.

He had been unconscious since dawn.

Margaret sat beside him, holding one of his hands between
her own. It was a hand she knew in every
line and scar; yet now it seemed an object fashioned
in yellow wax, cold at the fingertips and damp in
the palm. She rubbed it gently, foolishly, as though warmth
might be persuaded back into it. But the hand neither
closed nor stirred.

Could this be Edward's hand?

Only yesterday—no, was it yesterday?—that hand had lifted a glass,
turned the pages of a book, rested upon her shoulder.
She could feel again its pressure there: firm, affectionate, possessive.
She remembered how he had caught her fingers across the
breakfast table, because some chance word of hers had pleased
him. The Ego which had looked from his eyes, the
Intelligence which had directed this obedient instrument, the Personality that
had made its least gesture significant—where were they now?

Not gone. They could not be gone. His heart still
beat.

She laid his hand down and bent over him.

The physician had said that he might live an hour;
he might live until midnight. There was nothing more to
be done. Those words had been spoken quietly upon the
landing, and afterward the physician had descended the stairs, carrying
with him the smell of cool linen and the briskness
of the outer world. Margaret had heard the door close
below. Since then the house had seemed to withdraw from
the city and stand alone in some limitless place.

She put her palm upon Edward's breast.

There—it came: the faintest tremor. Then nothing. Then again, far
down beneath flesh and bone, a small, exhausted knock.

He still lived.

She would remain thus until the end. No nurse should
tell her afterward that he had ceased to breathe while
she slept. No stranger's hand should be the last to
touch him. She would feel that final beating beneath her
palm; she would know the exact instant when the invisible
cord was severed and he passed from her.

Passed where?

The question opened in her mind like a black door.

She looked at his face. Illness had sharpened it into
the likeness of white sculpture. The mouth, once quick to
smile, hung slightly open; the temples had fallen in; upon
the forehead lay a moisture which she wiped away with
her handkerchief. How could so much tenderness, wit, impatience, courage—how
could ten years of speech and laughter—be contained in this
disintegrating matter? And if they were not contained there, where
had they withdrawn?

"Edward," she whispered.

The name appeared to travel no farther than his ear.

"Edward, it is Margaret."

No answer.

Suppose he heard and could not respond? Suppose the Soul,
already loosened, remained imprisoned within its putrefying tenement, conscious of
her voice, struggling to turn the eyes, to move one
finger, to cry out: I am here!

She leaned nearer until her lips almost touched his ear.

"If you hear me, press my hand."

She waited.

Nothing.

"If you know me—only once, dear—only once."

The silence received her appeal with an immense indifference.

Then grief, which until that moment had lain upon her
like a weight, moved suddenly within her. She bowed her
head against the bed and wept—not violently, but with long
shuddering breaths which seemed to tear themselves from some hidden
wound. This man had been the witness of her life.
Without him, what proof remained that the years had happened?
Who would remember the blue dress she wore when first
they met, the room in which he had asked her
to marry him, the rain upon the church windows, their
quarrels, their reconciliations, the child that had lived for seven
hours and had possessed, for those seven hours, his eyes?

He was going alone.

That was the intolerable thing—not that he should go, but
that he should go alone. Love had made of two
beings one habitation; yet at the frontier of Death the
union failed. She could accompany him through poverty, disgrace, sickness;
she could follow him across continents and seas; but here,
where the road entered darkness, she must release his hand.

Unless she did not release it.

She raised her head and looked about the room, startled
by the thought, though she could not have said what
it meant.

The daylight had diminished. The brick wall beyond the window,
all afternoon a glaring red, had become purple and obscure.
The curtain lifted once, hung suspended, and fell. In the
corner stood the marble-topped washstand with its pitcher and basin;
beside it, Edward's shoes waited neatly, absurdly, for feet that
would never wear them again.

Would she light the gas?

No. The flame would heat the room still more; besides,
she could see him. While she could distinguish the whiteness
of his face, she need not have light.

She returned her hand to his breast.

Beat—pause—beat.

How slowly the heart laboured! It seemed a weary traveller
striking at a door which no one would open.

The house creaked.

Margaret turned.

It was an ordinary sound, caused by timber shrinking in
the heat; she knew this. Yet the knowledge did not
quiet the swift running of her blood. The empty rooms
above and below appeared suddenly populous—not with men and women,
but with vacancy itself, with Silence made attentive.

She listened.

Nothing followed.

Of course Death would make no sound. Death was not
a person entering by a door. Death was a condition,
an event, the cessation of a bodily function. The physician
had spoken of the heart; he had pointed, with a
clean fingernail, to certain passages in a medical book. There
was no figure, no face, no lifted hand.

And yet—where was Death?

Already in the room?

She examined the shadows. Beneath the table one shadow had
gathered thickly. Another hung behind the wardrobe like a folded
pall. The corners receded as she watched them. She could
not rid herself of the conviction that something had entered
while she wept and was now waiting—patient, cunning, certain of
its prey.

Her palm pressed harder upon Edward's breast.

"You shall not take him," she murmured.

The absurdity of the words brought a tremulous smile to
her lips. Whom did she address? Had terror made a
child of her? Presently she would imagine a skull, a
scythe, a cloak. Death required no theatrical entrance, no music,
no iron step upon the stair.

A scraping noise came from the window.

She sprang up with a cry.

Two green eyes burned beyond the sill. A black shape
crouched there, its head thrust forward; then, with a supple
movement, it entered the room.

Margaret snatched the medicine glass and flung it. The glass
struck the wall and shattered. The cat leaped to the
floor, darted beneath the bed, and fled through the open
door.

For several moments she stood unable to breathe. Then she
laughed—a thin, unfamiliar laugh.

"A cat! Edward, it was only a cat."

She waited, as though he might share the foolishness of
it; as though his mouth might curve and his eyes
open in affectionate reproach.

But Edward did not move.

The darkness thickened rapidly now. His face floated upon it,
featureless save for the hollows of the eyes. Margaret seated
herself and placed her hand once more over his heart.

There.

Still beating.

From somewhere below came a sound.

She held her breath.

Again: a dull impact, followed by a faint metallic rattle.

The front door?

Mary possessed a key, but Mary was not to return
until morning. The mistress was away. Every room was vacant.
No one had business in that house.

Perhaps someone had mistaken the number. Perhaps a thief—

Then the sound came again, from within: a footstep in
the lower hall.

Margaret's whole body grew cold.

One step.

A pause.

Another.

The steps moved slowly, with a deliberation more dreadful than
haste. Whoever had entered did not call, did not stumble
in the unfamiliar darkness, did not question whether the house
were occupied. He knew where he was going.

Death!

The word did not form in her mind; it rose
complete before her, vast and black.

He had come at last—not through walls, not invisibly, but
by the common door through which tradesmen and visitors entered.
He had turned the latch with a material hand. He
was below, pausing, listening, perhaps lifting his eyeless face toward
the room where Edward lay.

The first stair complained beneath his weight.

Margaret could see the staircase: the faded carpet secured by
brass rods; the narrow turn at the landing; the balustrade
polished by innumerable hands. Upon it now rested another hand,
long, colourless, irresistible.

One—two—three.

The footsteps ascended.

She wished to cry from the window, but her voice
was imprisoned in her throat. She wished to lock the
door, but to reach it she must withdraw her hand
from Edward's heart; and if, during that instant, it ceased
to beat? No. She would not leave him.

The steps reached the first landing.

Silence.

Was He waiting? Was He pleased by her terror? Perhaps
Death loitered thus outside every chamber, prolonging the agony because
eternity had taught him patience.

Then the ascent began again.

Slowly—slowly.

Each footfall entered the walls and travelled through the floor
into the iron frame of the bed. The pitcher trembled
against the basin. Or did she tremble? The whole house
had become a monstrous EAR, receiving those measured blows.

Nearer.

Louder.

One—two—three.

Margaret bent over Edward.

"Wake! Wake, dear! He is coming!"

The dying man made no sign.

The footsteps reached the second landing and halted.

A board cracked in the corridor below. Then came the
soft dragging of a shoe, the whisper of cloth against
the wall. Margaret saw Him as distinctly as though the
darkness had opened: tall enough to stoop beneath the ceiling,
with hands hanging to his knees and a face upon
which no human gaze could rest. He had hunted Edward
through fever and delirium; now, sure of him, He approached
without haste.

The stair to the third floor began to sound.

Margaret counted no longer. The beats beneath her palm and
the steps outside had become one rhythm—faint within, thunderous without.

Closer—closer.

The last stair groaned.

He was on the landing.

She heard breathing. A hand touched the wall. The sound
advanced along the passage and stopped before the door.

Margaret flung herself across her husband, covering his breast and
face with her body.

"No!" she screamed. "You shall not have him! You shall
not—"

There came a knock.

The knob turned.

In the doorway stood Mary, carrying a basket. She saw
the overturned chair, the broken glass, the motionless figures on
the bed; and, dropping the basket, she ran forward.

But there was no faint beating now beneath Margaret's hand.

Mary found a dead woman lying across a dead man.

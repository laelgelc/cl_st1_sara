# The Last Room in the World

In 2185, the apartment of the Venn family contained forty-six
people, three folding tables, one toilet, nineteen legal marriages, two
illegal marriages, a canary of uncertain ownership, and a single
room with a door that locked.

The room belonged to Gramps.

Gramps was one hundred and seventy-two years old and looked
a healthy fifty-eight. He had looked a healthy fifty-eight for
nearly a century, thanks to anti-gerasone, the celebrated medicine that
had rescued mankind from old age without rescuing it from
relatives.

The apartment was on the eight hundred and twelfth floor
of a housing block in New York City, Connecticut. Its
windows faced another housing block twelve feet away. Between the
blocks hung laundry, communication cables, and a municipal sign reading:

THANK YOU FOR NOT HAVING CHILDREN
WITHOUT A VALID VACANCY CERTIFICATE

Nobody in the Venn family had possessed a vacancy certificate
since 2129, but children had appeared anyway. So had their
children. And their children's children.

At night the youngest slept in hammocks above the kitchen.
Married couples were assigned drawers, cabinets, or screened sections of
hallway according to seniority. Cousin Pearl and her husband occupied
the bathtub from midnight to six, except on alternate Wednesdays
when the bathtub was required for bathing. Lou Venn and
his wife, Emerald, had a mattress that unrolled beneath the
dining table after the last meal and had to be
rolled up before the first.

The first meal usually ended at eleven at night.

The first began at four in the morning.

Gramps alone had a daybed, a bureau, a private washbasin,
a television, and eighty square feet of floor. He also
had money.

These two facts explained the family.

Every morning Gramps came out of his room carrying his
will in a silver binder. The binder was as thick
as a sandwich and far less nourishing. He would sit
at the breakfast table while six shifts of descendants ate
compressed oat bricks around him, and he would announce the
latest amendments.

"I got something to say," he said on the morning
of October 3.

Forty-five people became quiet. The canary continued to sing, because
the canary had nothing to inherit.

Gramps opened the binder.

"To my great-great-granddaughter Myra," he read, "who yesterday referred to
me as a domestic obstruction, I leave one picture of
myself, suitable for hanging in any room she ever gets,
which will be never."

Myra, aged sixty-one, reddened.

"To Bernard, who used my private washbasin without written permission,
I leave the washcloth he polluted."

"I was choking," Bernard said.

"You can choke in the public sink like everybody else."

The public sink was occupied by Aunt Lollie, who was
thawing six pounds of synthetic liver in it.

Gramps turned a page.

"To Emerald, wife of Louis—"

"Lou," Lou said automatically.

"Don't interrupt your elders, boy."

Lou was one hundred and three.

"To Emerald, wife of Louis, who was decent enough to
bring me my slippers during last Thursday's lunar wrestling finals,
I leave twelve thousand credits, provided her husband shows improved
character before I pass to that great housing development in
the sky."

"Thank you, Gramps," said Emerald.

Lou looked improved.

Gramps closed the binder. "That's all. Could be my last
will. Could be my last breakfast. Could be my last
anything."

"You say that every day," said Myra.

"And one day I'll be right."

This was technically true, though nobody at the table had
seen a natural death. Death was known to them mainly
through history lessons, insurance advertisements, and detective programs. It was
grouped mentally with steam engines, wolves, and people owning farms.

Gramps took his anti-gerasone at breakfast. The medicine came in
green bottles with cheerful labels showing a wrinkled face on
one side and a smooth face on the other. Gramps
measured out two spoonfuls while the family watched.

He made a ceremony of it.

"Still bitter," he said.

"Life's bitter," said Uncle Rollo.

"You trying to be funny?"

"No, sir."

"Good. I cut funny people out."

Gramps swallowed the medicine, shuddered theatrically, and went back to
his room. The lock clicked behind him.

Conversation resumed.

"Domestic obstruction," Myra muttered. "I should've said tyrant."

"You want the picture or not?" said Bernard.

"I don't want anything."

Forty-four faces turned toward her.

"Well," Myra said, "not nothing."

The Venn estate consisted of Gramps's investment account, the apartment
lease, a collection of antique electronics, and mineral rights to
a small asteroid that had not yet been found. Its
cash value was considerable. Its emotional value was the private
room.

The family pretended to care chiefly about Gramps.

Lou pretended harder than most.

He was a narrow, apologetic man who had spent eighty
years trying not to stand where somebody else wished to
stand. His profession was remote elevator supervision. He sat at
a fold-down terminal beside the refrigerator and watched little numbers
rise and fall in towers all over the eastern seaboard.
When an elevator became confused, Lou reassured it.

Emerald repaired flavor circuits in food printers. She was ninety-nine,
pretty, practical, and capable of sleeping through almost anything except
a whispered discussion of square footage.

That afternoon, while Lou was supervising an elevator in Baltimore,
Maryland, District of Columbia, Emerald squeezed behind his chair.

"You know what day it is?" she whispered.

"Tuesday?"

"Our anniversary."

Lou winced. "Which one?"

"Our eightieth."

"I knew that."

"Sure."

"I got you something."

"Where is it?"

Lou patted his pocket.

Emerald reached in and removed a paper ticket. It entitled
two adults to occupy a private restaurant booth for twenty
minutes, beverage not included.

"Oh, Lou."

"Ten-thirty tonight."

"We'll miss dessert shift."

"I traded with Rollo."

She kissed his ear. There was nowhere else accessible.

At ten-fifteen, Lou went to the hall closet to get
his formal jacket. The closet was officially a closet, though
Great-uncle Peder slept upright inside it during daylight hours. Peder
worked nights recording applause for television comedies.

Lou opened the door softly.

Peder was not there.

Cousin Willy was.

Willy, aged seventy-three, stood with Gramps's green medicine bottle in
one hand and a jug of distilled water in the
other. A glass dropper stuck out of his mouth. He
froze.

Lou froze.

From the living room television, twenty million voices shouted, "DON'T
OPEN THAT AIRLOCK!"

"What are you doing?" Lou asked.

Willy removed the dropper from his mouth. "Nothing."

"That's Gramps's anti-gerasone."

"No, it isn't."

The label faced Lou.

RELIABLE ANTI-GERASONE
FAMILY SIZE
NOW WITH LEMON

"Yes, it is."

Willy whispered, "Keep your big bazoo shut."

"You're watering it."

"I am adjusting it."

"You're trying to kill him."

"Don't be melodramatic. It takes years to die. I'm just
giving nature a fair chance."

Lou stared at him.

Willy's face was smooth and pink. He had the guilty
expression of a schoolboy, an effect anti-gerasone had made available
to several generations of criminals.

"You put it back full strength," Lou said.

"Why?"

"Because it's his."

"Everything is his. That's the trouble."

"You can't do this."

"Listen, Lou. He cut me out for chewing. Chewing! I
told him his speakers were turned up too loud, and
now I'm getting a brass button and his opinion of
me in writing."

"That doesn't make this right."

"Right?" Willy pointed toward the crowded apartment. "My wife and
I have spent thirty-nine years sleeping in a linen bin.
We haven't had a private conversation since 2162. Last month
we had an argument, and six cousins took her side
before I knew we were arguing."

"Put the medicine back."

"No."

Lou reached for the bottle.

Willy pulled away.

Lou grabbed his wrist.

Willy jabbed Lou with the dropper.

Lou yelped, Willy stumbled, and the green bottle flew from
his hand.

It made one slow, shining turn in the air.

Both men tried to catch it.

They struck each other's hands.

The bottle hit the closet floor and shattered.

For a moment they stood above the glittering pieces and
the widening medicinal puddle. Lemon-scented immortality soaked into Great-uncle Peder's
best sleeping shoes.

Then a voice behind them said, "Well."

Gramps stood in the doorway wearing his striped nightshirt. Beyond
him, the entire family leaned and craned for a view.

Willy pointed at Lou.

Lou pointed at Willy.

"He did it," both said.

Gramps looked at the broken bottle. Then he looked at
Lou, whose hand still held Willy's wrist.

"My medicine," he said.

"Gramps, I can explain."

"You wanted me dead."

"No!"

"Caught him red-handed," Willy said.

"You were watering it!"

"Liar."

"Murderer," said Gramps.

"Attempted murderer," Emerald corrected gently. "He isn't dead."

Gramps put a hand to his chest. "Give him time."

The family murmured. Some were shocked. Some were pleased to
have a crisis not involving plumbing. A few looked thoughtfully
at Lou's future share of the estate.

"I was trying to stop Willy," Lou said. "He had
the bottle. He was putting water in it."

"Why would I do that?" said Willy.

"To make Gramps age."

"That could take months."

"Quiet!" Gramps thundered.

The canary stopped singing.

Gramps raised one trembling hand. It did not tremble from
age. It trembled from talent.

"One hundred and seventy-two years," he said. "I fed you.
I sheltered you. I permitted you to reproduce until my
home resembled a railway station on Evacuation Day. And this
is my reward."

"You didn't feed all of us," said Myra. "Some of
us have jobs."

"Who said that?"

Nobody answered.

Gramps turned to Lou. "You were always a sneaky boy."

Lou remembered being called sneaky in 2111 for hiding a
sandwich.

"Gramps, please."

"I know what everybody wants. The money. The apartment. My
bed." His voice broke impressively on the last word. "Well,
you'll get it soon enough."

He went into his room and slammed the door.

The lock clicked.

A moment later the television rose to full volume.

"After three hundred and fourteen continuous seasons," announced a solemn
voice, "the secret mother of Captain Galaxy will finally be
revealed."

The family remained in the hall.

Emerald touched Lou's arm. "There goes our restaurant booth."

"You believe me, don't you?"

"Of course."

"Thanks."

"You shouldn't have grabbed for the bottle."

"I was saving his life."

"Nobody saves anybody's life anymore. You save their dosage."

Willy slipped away before Lou could hit him. Lou had
never hit anybody and was not sure there was room
to begin.

That night he and Emerald lay beneath the dining table,
fully dressed. Above them, late supper dishes passed from hand
to hand. Feet moved around their mattress. A spoon fell
onto Lou's chest.

"Do you think he'll cut us out?" he whispered.

"He'll cut you out."

"What about you?"

"He likes me."

"That's nice."

"If I inherit, it's practically the same as you inheriting."

"It isn't cheering me up."

Emerald took his hand. "Maybe he'll calm down."

From behind the locked bedroom door came Gramps's voice.

"Heartless brood! Vultures! Let an old man perish!"

Then came canned applause and the theme song of Captain
Galaxy.

By dawn, Gramps had not emerged.

At six, Aunt Lollie knocked to ask whether he wanted
breakfast. At six-ten, Bernard knocked to apologize again for the
washbasin. At six-twenty, Myra knocked merely to see if the
room was empty.

There was no answer.

At seven, Lou tried the knob.

The door opened.

The family poured into the room so quickly that several
entered without touching the floor. They stopped around the daybed.

It was empty.

The covers had been folded back with funereal neatness. Gramps's
slippers stood side by side. On the pillow lay the
silver will binder and a sheet of paper.

Emerald picked up the paper.

"Read it," said everybody.

She read:

"'To my beloved family. Since my continued existence has become
a hardship and an obstacle, I have gone where I
will trouble you no longer. Do not search for me.
By the time you read this, I shall be beyond
complaint, beyond medicine, and beyond all reruns. Farewell. Gramps.'"

Nobody spoke for six seconds.

Then Lou said, "He killed himself."

"How?" Myra asked.

"Maybe he jumped."

"The windows don't open."

"Maybe he went to the river."

"He hates public transportation."

"Maybe he stopped taking his medicine," said Willy.

They considered this horrible possibility. Without anti-gerasone, Gramps might begin
to age within a month. Within five years, he could
look seventy. There was no telling where it might end.

Emerald opened the will binder.

The first page bore that morning's date.

"Oh," she said.

"What?" Lou asked.

"It's a new one."

That got everybody's attention.

Emerald read the legal introduction, which identified Gramps as being
of sound mind, generous heart, injured spirit, and sole ownership
of all disputed property. Then she reached the important paragraph.

"'I hereby revoke every insult, preference, punishment, promise, exception, conditional
bequest, brass button, polluted washcloth, and moral lesson contained in
all former wills. I leave my entire estate, including leasehold,
accounts, artifacts, asteroid claims, furnishings, and personal effects, in equal
shares to all my living descendants.'"

There was silence again.

It was not the silence of grief. It was arithmetic.

"How many descendants?" Bernard asked.

"Forty-one," said somebody.

"Forty-two."

"Spouses don't count."

"Then Emerald can get out."

"I'm a spouse too, you idiot."

"Adopted descendants count."

"Not after 2174."

"The canary was his."

"Sell it."

Lou sat down on the daybed.

It was the softest thing he had touched in years.

Every face turned toward him.

"Get off that," Myra said.

Lou looked up. "Why?"

"It's estate property."

"So am I—I mean, I'm an heir."

"You chased him away."

"I did not."

"You broke his medicine."

"Willy diluted it."

Willy spread his hands. "Still telling that one?"

Emerald sat beside Lou. The mattress sighed warmly beneath them.

"Oh," she said.

That one syllable did more to inflame the family than
any speech could have done.

Bernard moved forward. "The room should go to the oldest
descendant."

"The apartment is to be divided equally," said Myra.

"You can't divide a room equally."

"You can divide the hours."

"Fine. I want nighttime."

"Everybody wants nighttime."

Aunt Lollie opened the bureau. "Look! Real cotton socks."

Hands reached.

Someone claimed the television as necessary medical equipment. Someone else
claimed the washbasin on grounds of chronic choking. Cousin Pearl
measured the daybed with her forearm. Her husband began unscrewing
the lock from the door.

Lou stood up.

"All of you pipe down! Gramps may still be alive."

"That's easy for you to say," said Bernard. "You've already
had a minute on the bed."

"I didn't ask for it!"

"You sat right down."

"I was upset."

"I'd like to be upset for half an hour," said
Myra.

Willy shoved past Lou and stretched out full length on
the daybed.

"This," he said, "is what Gramps would have wanted."

Lou pulled him up by the collar.

Willy swung.

His fist struck Aunt Lollie, who dropped the cotton socks
and hit Bernard with the will binder. Bernard fell against
the television. Captain Galaxy declared, "Violence is never the answer,"
just before the screen went over.

The room became a machinery of elbows.

Forty-five people could not fight effectively in eighty square feet,
but they could fight thoroughly. The daybed collapsed. The private
washbasin came off the wall. Myra bit someone she believed
to be Willy and discovered later that it was her
third husband. The canary escaped its cage and achieved, for
seventeen seconds, more personal space than any Venn had known
in decades.

Lou tried to protect Emerald.

Emerald tried to protect a bureau drawer she had found
empty and therefore potentially inhabitable.

The apartment's domestic alarm, neglected since 2150 because someone was
always leaning against it, suddenly worked.

Sirens sounded.

The police arrived by exterior lift and found the family
boiling out of Gramps's bedroom into the hall. Two officers
attempted mediation. One lost his helmet. The other was struck
with a ceremonial urn containing the ashes of nobody—the urn
had been purchased in a hopeful period.

More police arrived.

Then more.

The arrest took three hours and improved the apartment considerably.

Lou and Emerald were transported in separate crowd-control vans to
the Municipal Harmony Center. Lou spent the trip imagining Gramps
wandering alone through transit tunnels, weak from missed doses, his
striped nightshirt flapping around his bare knees.

At the Center, a clerk asked Lou his age.

"One hundred and three."

"Occupation?"

"Elevator reassurance."

"Charge?"

"I'm not sure."

The clerk checked a list. "Riot, assault, destruction of property,
interference with police, unlawful competition for residential space, and emotional
disturbance of a bird."

"I was trying to stop it."

"Everybody says that."

Lou was fingerprinted, photographed, disinfected, and led down a quiet
corridor. A guard opened a steel door.

"Temporary accommodation," she said.

Lou stepped inside.

The cell measured six feet by nine. It contained a
bed, a toilet, a shelf, a light, and no relatives.

Lou stared.

"Something wrong?" asked the guard.

"This is all for me?"

"Don't get excited. You're sharing."

His heart sank.

The guard brought Emerald in.

The door closed behind her.

They stood together listening to the silence.

There were distant footsteps. A lock clanged. Somewhere, a prisoner
coughed with admirable restraint.

Emerald touched the bed.

"Lou," she whispered. "It's real."

They sat down.

There was room for both.

They lay back experimentally. The mattress did not need to
be rolled up. Nobody's breakfast was above them. Nobody's shoes
were beside their heads. The toilet had a privacy partition.

Emerald began to laugh.

Lou tried to look serious. "We're in jail."

"I know."

"We could be here a long time."

"Oh, Lou."

She kissed him.

A slot opened in the door and two trays slid
through. Each held soup, bread, vegetables, and a square of
something labeled MEAT—INDIVIDUAL PORTION.

Emerald picked up the bread. "This isn't compressed."

Lou tasted the soup. "It's hot."

They ate without passing anything to anybody.

Later a public defender visited. He was a young man
of eighty-two with a tired, smooth face.

"The situation isn't good," he said. "Property riot, officer injuries,
extensive damage. The district has assigned its best prosecutor."

"How long?" Lou asked.

"Hard to say. Six months, maybe."

Emerald's face fell.

"Only six?" she said.

The defender blinked.

Lou squeezed her hand. "Could there be additional charges?"

"I'm sorry?"

"The bird," Lou said. "It looked very disturbed."

The defender studied them. "You people have been living in
one of the old family units, haven't you?"

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Forty-six."

"I'll see what I can do."

That evening Lou and Emerald watched the cell television. It
had only three channels, but nobody else controlled them. They
chose a documentary about empty deserts.

At ten, lights-out occurred.

They had darkness.

They had quiet.

They had a door.

Lou drew Emerald close. "Happy anniversary."

"This is the best one yet."

Back at the Venn apartment, nothing moved except the canary.

For an hour after the police departed, the apartment remained
silent. The dining room held overturned chairs, abandoned shoes, and
a bowl of synthetic liver that had achieved room temperature.
Captain Galaxy's broken television emitted a faint electrical sigh.

Then the front door opened.

Gramps entered wearing a brown coat over his striped nightshirt.

He carried a small suitcase.

He waited.

No one shouted. No one asked where he had been.
No one accused anyone else of standing in the wrong
place.

Gramps shut the door and locked it.

He walked slowly through the apartment, savoring the wreckage. The
hammocks in the kitchen were empty. The bathtub was vacant.
The hall closet contained only glass fragments, Peder's shoes, and
the clean smell of lemon.

Gramps reached his bedroom.

The daybed had collapsed, but this did not trouble him.
He dragged two mattresses from the dining room and stacked
them. There were dozens to choose from.

He set the private washbasin upright, though it would require
a plumber. He rescued the television and found that only
the outer casing was cracked. After a few hard slaps,
Captain Galaxy returned.

Gramps sat down.

The program was covering the Venn riot.

"—a shocking episode of intergenerational violence," said the announcer, "in
which forty-five residents, ranging in age from nineteen to one
hundred and forty-six, battled over a single bedroom after the
apparent death of their family patriarch."

Gramps grinned.

A picture appeared of Lou being loaded into a police
van.

"Authorities credit Senior Prosecutor Malva Crayne with opposing release on
grounds that the suspects present a continuing threat to one
another, household fixtures, and small domestic animals."

"Good girl, Malva," said Gramps.

Malva Crayne had once received a scholarship from the Gramps
Venn Charitable Fund for Promising Litigators. The fund had supported
exactly one promising litigator and had been created three days
earlier.

Gramps opened his suitcase. Inside were a spare bottle of
anti-gerasone, a portable telephone, sandwiches, and Willy's favorite gold pen.
Gramps had taken the pen while the family slept. It
had served perfectly for the farewell note and the new
will.

The equal-inheritance clause did not take effect until his legally
certified death.

That could be a while.

He called a repair service and ordered a new lock,
a reinforced daybed, and soundproofing for the entire apartment. Then
he called the prison information line.

"All defendants still held?" he asked.

"Yes, sir," said the automated voice. "Estimated preliminary detention: ninety
to one hundred and eighty days."

"Can a citizen contribute to vigorous prosecution?"

"Press one."

Gramps pressed one with Willy's pen.

The television announcer was saying, "And now, a message for
everyone who has ever looked in the mirror and felt
merely young."

Music played. On the screen, a woman with shining hair
ran through a meadow that had almost certainly been reserved
for filming.

"Anti-gerasone conquered age," she said, "but why stop there? From
the laboratories that preserved your tomorrow comes New Juvenex Ultra—the
first treatment designed not simply to halt aging, but to
reverse maturity itself!"

A handsome man appeared beside her. He looked about sixteen
and was probably one hundred and thirty.

"Imagine smoother skin, brighter eyes, stronger bones, faster reflexes, and
the social confidence that comes from looking exactly like everybody
else!"

Gramps leaned forward.

"Juvenex Ultra," the woman continued. "Because fifty is the new
elderly. Ask your longevity consultant whether rejuvenation is right for
you. Family discounts available for households of thirty or more."

"No family discount," said Gramps.

He called the number on the screen and ordered a
year's supply at full price.

Then he stretched out on the stacked mattresses, unwrapped a
sandwich, and turned up Captain Galaxy. The apartment around him
was spacious, peaceful, and entirely his.

On the screen, the Captain approached the airlock where his
secret mother had been waiting for three hundred and fourteen
seasons.

Gramps smiled.

At last, he wanted to know what happened next.

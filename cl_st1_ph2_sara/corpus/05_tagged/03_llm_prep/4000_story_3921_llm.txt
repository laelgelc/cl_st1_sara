## THE WAY TO PAINTED ROCK

### I

Barbara Lowe had been eighteen for three months and beautiful,
by the unanimous decision of Barbara Lowe, for considerably longer.

There were other authorities. Certain young men of her native
city had expressed themselves upon the subject with embarrassing earnestness;
elderly ladies had pinched her cheek and called her a
rosebud; photographers had placed her beneath gauze canopies and discovered
all manner of wistful possibilities in her chin. Even Aunt
Eunice, who disapproved of most modern things, including motorcars, silk
stockings, and Barbara, admitted that the child had "a taking
way."

It was a very taking way indeed.

She had brown hair with an elusive glint of copper,
blue eyes of an innocence they did not invariably deserve,
a small, fine nose, and a mouth which could be
adorable, mutinous, teasing, or tragic, according to the requirements of
the moment. She was petite, dainty, fluttering—a human bit of
chiffon—and she had been trained by affectionate parents and foolish
friends to believe that the world was chiefly a background
prepared for her entrances.

The northern woods did not know this.

They had stood before Barbara was born, before her city
was built, before young men learned to compare eyes with
stars. Straight trunks rose in immense and indifferent ranks; cedar
swamps brooded under still green light; hemlocks whispered over hidden
springs. The wilderness had no admiration to offer a picture-hat,
and could not have told a Paris frock from a
flour sack.

Barbara had been visiting Aunt Eunice and Uncle Nathan for
nine days when she decided to take a walk.

"Not far," she promised at luncheon.

"Keep to the blazed trail," said Uncle Nathan.

"Of course."

"And wear your stout shoes."

Barbara glanced down at the stout shoes in question. They
were broad, brown, sensible, and hideous.

"I shall only walk to the ridge."

"There's rain-water in the low ground," Aunt Eunice warned. "And
don't take that lace parasol."

"My dear aunt, I cannot walk in this sun without
something."

"The trees furnish shade."

But trees did not furnish a pale-green parasol with an
ivory handle, and so the matter was settled.

Half an hour later Barbara descended from her room arrayed
for the wilderness. Her frock was cream-colored, with a mist
of blue chiffon at throat and wrists. Her little coat
fitted exquisitely. Her shoes had high heels and silver buckles;
her stockings were of a delicacy that would have made
a bramble smile. Upon her hair was a broad hat
with three blue roses and a drooping plume.

Aunt Eunice looked at her.

"I thought you meant to walk."

"I do."

"I see."

That was the advantage of Aunt Eunice's conversation: it was
brief enough to be ignored.

Barbara stepped out under a sky of flawless blue. The
path beyond the cabin ran first through white birches, then
climbed among butternuts and popples. Sunlight fell in golden coins;
little winds stirred the fern; far off, a woodpecker knocked
industriously upon a hollow tree.

Barbara was pleased. The wilderness, regarded from a proper trail
and at three o'clock in the afternoon, was charming. She
moved lightly beneath the branches, tipping her parasol against the
sun and imagining herself in Fontainebleau. It was true that
Fontainebleau had never, so far as she knew, contained mosquitoes
of this size, but one could omit them from the
picture.

At the ridge she paused. Below her spread miles of
forest, blue-green and mysterious, with here and there a silver
flash of water. No roofs, no smoke, no railway track
disturbed the splendid loneliness. Barbara's heart rose pleasantly.

What adventures one might have here! Indians, perhaps—not unpleasant Indians,
naturally, but grave and loyal persons willing to rescue one
at great inconvenience to themselves. Or a wounded traveler. Or
a stern, misunderstood man with a secret sorrow, living alone
in a log house and accustomed to command.

She sighed.

Nothing romantic had happened since her arrival. Uncle Nathan talked
of timber; Aunt Eunice made preserves. The neighboring young men
were broad, cheerful creatures who asked whether she liked fishing
and addressed her as "Miss Lowe" with an alarming respectability.
Barbara was not accustomed to respectability in young men. She
preferred despair.

A blue butterfly floated beyond the trail.

Barbara followed it.

It danced over a patch of sunshine, hovered by a
clump of moose-maple, and slipped teasingly between two young basswoods.
Barbara moved after it, lifting her skirts. She had no
intention of leaving the path—none whatever. She merely stepped aside
for an instant, because the butterfly's wings were exactly the
shade of her ribbons.

The butterfly rose. Barbara went on. A branch caught her
sleeve; she freed it. Another swept her hat. She recovered
the hat, said something uncomplimentary about northern vegetation, and turned
back.

There was no path.

An observant person might have noticed the faint blaze on
a maple twenty yards to the right. Barbara was not
observant. She was picturesque, which is often quite a different
accomplishment.

She walked in what she confidently believed to be the
proper direction. After ten minutes she found a fallen hemlock
across her way. She did not remember crossing it before;
therefore, she reasoned, she must have gone around it. She
went around it now.

Beyond it were straight towering trunks, saplings, undergrowth; more straight
towering trunks, more saplings, more undergrowth. The forest, so varied
when viewed from the ridge, had become offensively repetitious.

Barbara called, "Hello!"

Her voice struck the trees, lost courage, and returned thinly.

She called again.

No answer came, unless the harsh cry of a jay
may be considered an answer, and Barbara did not consider
it a civil one.

For perhaps half an hour she maintained both her composure
and her hat. Then the hat was dragged off by
a low branch and one of its blue roses torn
away. She stared at the damage in unbelieving indignation. The
wilderness had touched her hat. It had not merely failed
to admire it; it had attacked it.

"This is absurd," she said aloud.

The forest agreed in whispers.

She hurried. The silver buckles dulled beneath dust; her chiffon
caught and ripped; her parasol became scratched along one side.
Once she stumbled over a hidden root and barely saved
herself by embracing a tamarack. The tamarack offered no apology.

The ground dipped. Underfoot, the moss grew thicker and wet.
Barbara's right shoe sank suddenly to the ankle.

She pulled it out with a cry. Black mud covered
the silver buckle.

Ahead lay a cedar swamp, green, silent, and uninviting. Behind
lay trees that appeared no more familiar than those before
her. To the left the brush was impenetrable; to the
right it was merely vindictive.

Aunt Eunice had said there was rain-water in the low
ground. Barbara began to hate Aunt Eunice for having been
right.

She entered the swamp.

There are ladies who cross swamps with competence, courage, and
stout brown shoes. Barbara possessed none of the three. She
stepped upon roots which rolled, stones which sank, and promising
green surfaces which proved to have no foundations whatever. Her
skirt was splashed to the knee. One heel broke. Her
parasol, employed as a staff, folded backward with a small
despairing snap.

Poor little Barbara!

It was now nearly six o'clock. The golden light had
faded from the upper branches, and the forest floor was
filling with grayness. She had heard that wolves prowled northern
woods. She had also heard of bears, wildcats, escaped criminals,
and men who lived by trapping and had forgotten human
speech. Until this moment they had all seemed attractive accessories
to a wilderness holiday.

A sound came from the brush behind her.

Barbara stopped.

Something moved.

She tried to run, lost her crippled shoe, recovered it,
and fled with the shoe in one hand and the
wrecked parasol in the other. Alder branches whipped her face.
Her breath came in sobs. The woods darkened, closed, multiplied.
Every shadow concealed eyes; every dead limb became a crouching
beast.

At last she reached a little rise where a great
pine had fallen. There her strength deserted her. She dropped
beside the log, wrapped both arms around herself, and began
to cry—not prettily, not with the restrained tears which had
so often softened paternal judgment, but in terrified, exhausted gasps.

The thing behind her emerged from the brush.

It was a porcupine.

Barbara did not see it. Her face was hidden against
the torn chiffon at her knees.

Therefore she did not see the young man who stepped
quietly between the trees, paused, and looked at her.

He was tall and lean, with straight shoulders under a
faded gray flannel shirt. A pack hung from one shoulder;
a rifle rested in the hollow of his arm. His
boots were not boots at all, but soft, worn moccasins,
and they had brought him through the undergrowth with scarcely
a sound. His face was brown from weather, his jaw
clear-cut, his eyes gray and steady.

He looked first at Barbara, then at the broken heel,
the green parasol, the blue rose caught upon a thorn,
and finally at the porcupine, which was departing with dignity.

One corner of his mouth moved.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

Barbara lifted her head.

If the heavens had opened and released a mounted knight
in silver armor, his effect could hardly have been more
profound. But she was Barbara, and a lady does not
fling herself into the arms of the first providential woodsman
who appears. Not immediately.

"No," she said faintly. "I am lost."

"So I gathered."

"I have been wandering for hours."

"How many?"

"I don't know. Hundreds."

He set down his rifle. "Can you stand?"

"Of course I can stand."

She tried and could not.

He caught her beneath the arms before she fell. For
one disconcerting instant she was held firmly against faded flannel
and a leather strap. Then he placed her on the
log as though she had been a child.

Barbara's cheeks burned.

"My ankle," she explained. "It must be broken."

He knelt and examined it with impersonal fingers. "It isn't."

"It feels broken."

"It's tired."

She drew herself up. "Do you know the way to
Mr. Nathan Lowe's cabin?"

"Yes."

"Then please take me there at once."

The young man glanced through the branches at the darkening
sky.

"No."

Barbara stared at him. In her experience, when she said
"please" after giving an order, it constituted irresistible graciousness.

"What did you say?"

"I said no. It will be dark in twenty minutes."

"That makes it all the more necessary."

"It makes it foolish. The low ground is bad, and
you can't walk far."

"You could carry me."

"I could," he agreed, "but I won't carry you through
a cedar swamp in the dark."

She regarded him with outraged blue eyes.

"You are an extremely impertinent person."

"Probably."

"And what do you propose to do?"

"Camp."

"Here?"

"Near here."

Barbara looked about her. The shadows had advanced while they
talked; the spaces between the tree trunks were black, and
the evening wind had begun to move overhead with a
long, oceanic murmur.

"There may be wolves."

"There aren't."

"Bears?"

"Not interested in us."

"Wildcats?"

"Shy."

"Men?" she demanded.

He met her look gravely. "One."

For an instant she was uncertain whether to be alarmed
or amused. Then he picked up her shoe, pack, rifle,
and collapsed parasol.

"Come," he said.

And, to Barbara's own astonishment, she came.

### II

The camp was made in a hollow beneath three great
hemlocks where the ground lay dry and thick with needles.
A little stream ran over stones nearby. The place seemed
to Barbara no different from the hundred other shadowy places
she had passed, but the young man selected it after
one glance, as a hostess might choose the best table
in a dining room.

"Sit there," he directed, indicating a mossy stone.

"I prefer the log."

"The log is damp."

Barbara sat on the stone.

He worked quickly. Dead branches became a snapping fire. Two
forked saplings and a crosspole became the frame of a
shelter. He cut balsam boughs, laid them with neat overlapping
strokes, and spread a blanket from his pack over the
fragrant bed beneath.

Barbara watched with increasing interest.

"Are you a guide?"

"No."

"A hunter?"

"Sometimes."

"A lumberman?"

"Not lately."

"Then what are you?"

"Busy."

"That is not an occupation."

"It has been mine for several years."

He did not even look at her. Barbara, who was
accustomed to seeing attention leap toward her whenever she spoke,
found his composure fascinating and offensive.

He heated water in a blackened tin, then approached her.

"Give me the shoe."

She handed it over judicially.

He examined the broken heel. "This was a poor thing
to wear in the woods."

"It happens to be a very good shoe."

"For a floor."

"I did not expect to walk through a swamp."

"The swamp didn't expect you, either. It seems to have
recovered."

Barbara almost laughed. She decided not to encourage him.

He set both shoes near the fire, stuffed them with
dry cloth from his pack, and gave her a pair
of thick woolen socks.

"What are these for?"

"Your feet."

"I cannot wear them."

"Then you can go barefoot."

Barbara put them on.

They were gray, enormous, and ugly, and they warmed her
delightfully. She stretched her feet toward the fire while he
prepared supper: bacon in a small pan, hard bread toasted
upon a sharpened stick, and coffee strong enough, Aunt Eunice
would have said, to carry an ax.

Barbara ate everything.

When she had finished, she discovered him watching her.

"Well?" she said.

"I was wondering whether city ladies always eat like starving
lumberjacks."

"I had no luncheon."

"You had luncheon at one."

"How do you know?"

"I passed your uncle's place at half past twelve. They
were setting the table."

Barbara looked up quickly. "You know my relatives?"

"I know who they are."

"And you knew where I belonged?"

"The clothes suggested civilization."

She smoothed the torn remains of her skirt. "They are
ruined."

"Yes."

"You needn't sound pleased."

"I'm not pleased. I'm impressed. It takes determination to do
that much damage in so short a time."

The firelight revealed a quiet amusement in his eyes. Barbara
tried to be severe, but the attempt failed at the
corners of her mouth.

"What is your name?" she asked.

"Garrett Stanton."

"Mine is Barbara Lowe."

"I know."

"Everybody seems to know everything except me."

"That may be temporary."

She considered this and then laughed outright. It was perhaps
the first time she had laughed at herself without intending
that someone else should admire the manner in which she
did it.

Stanton's face changed. Not much; merely a lessening of its
guarded stillness. He turned away to add wood to the
fire.

"And where are you going, Mr. Stanton?"

"Northwest."

"That is a direction, not a destination."

"It serves."

"You are very mysterious."

"No. Only tired."

"Of talking?"

"Of being questioned."

Barbara was silent for exactly twenty seconds.

"What is northwest?"

He smiled despite himself. "Painted Rock."

"Why?"

"To reach it."

"Why must you reach it?"

He laid two green branches across the fire and watched
the sparks climb. "Because there is a man there whom
I have tried to find for three years."

"What has he done?"

"Something he must set right."

"Is he a criminal?"

"I didn't say that."

"Is he dangerous?"

"Possibly."

"Are you going to arrest him?"

"No."

"Kill him?"

Stanton turned his head. "Do I look anxious to kill
anyone?"

Barbara inspected him. In firelight he appeared older than she
had first thought, perhaps twenty-seven or twenty-eight. There were fine
lines at the corners of his eyes and a whitened
scar along one wrist. His face held strength without hardness,
but also a patience that seemed purchased rather than natural.

"No," she said more softly. "You look as though you
had been waiting a long time."

He was silent.

Barbara drew the blanket about her shoulders. It had grown
cold; beyond the circle of flame the forest was immense
and secret. Yet she was no longer afraid. Stanton moved
through the darkness, gathering wood, and each time he returned
the wilderness seemed to yield him up unwillingly, as if
he belonged to it and had merely been lent to
the fire.

He arranged a second blaze behind the shelter so that
heat came from both sides.

"Do we need two?" she asked.

"One for comfort, one for insurance."

"Against what?"

"Your imagination."

She made a face at him. "You are not at
all like a knight."

"I'm relieved."

"A proper knight would say that danger surrounded us and
then defend me from it."

"A proper knight would probably have sense enough not to
tell you."

Barbara settled deeper into the blanket. "Did you never read
romances?"

"A few."

"And they did not influence you?"

"They taught me never to trust a man with a
velvet cloak."

"You have no soul."

"That's why I travel light."

It was an absurd conversation to hold in a black
northern forest, with one's hair unpinned, one's gown in rags,
and one's feet enclosed in another person's woolen socks. Barbara
felt suddenly happy.

Dear little Barbara had thought herself happy many times: at
dances when flowers arrived in armfuls; in a new gown
before a crowded mirror; beside young Mr. Philip Crane when
he forgot what he was saying and simply gazed. But
those pleasures had been bright surfaces, pretty reflections in polished
glass. This happiness was warm and quiet and astonishingly deep.
It came from the smell of balsam, the crackle of
fire, and the presence of a man who expected nothing
charming of her.

She looked at Stanton's strong brown hands.

"Have you always lived in the woods?"

"No."

"In a city?"

"For a while."

"Which one?"

"Several."

"Were you rich?"

"No."

"Poor?"

"Usually."

"Happy?"

"Sometimes."

"You answer as though words cost money."

"They cost more than most people think."

Barbara's gaze fell. "Do I talk too much?"

"Yes."

She looked up, wounded.

Stanton's expression softened. "But the woods have been quiet for
a long while. They may forgive you."

She laughed again, then yawned.

"You should sleep," he said.

"In there?"

He had made the shelter into a small green chamber.
Balsam boughs roofed it; the blanket bed waited in shadow,
open toward the fire.

"Where will you sleep?"

"Outside."

"But it is cold."

"I have a coat."

Barbara hesitated. "There is room."

He looked at her swiftly, and for the first time
that evening she became aware not merely that he was
a man, but that he was young. A pause fell
between them.

"I'll stay by the fire," he said.

Her cheeks warmed. "I only meant—"

"I know."

She fled into the shelter, angry with herself and with
him because he had understood her correctly.

For a while she lay awake, watching flame ripple over
the hemlock trunks. Stanton sat with his back against a
tree, rifle across his knees. He seemed carved from the
same brown silence as the woods.

"Mr. Stanton?"

"Yes?"

"Will you wake me if a wolf comes?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because none will."

"But if one does?"

"I'll ask him to wait until morning."

She smiled into the blanket.

Later she woke. The fires had burned low. Moonlight lay
pale among the branches, and the wind was whispering high
overhead. Stanton stood at the edge of the hollow, looking
away into the forest.

His stillness was different now. There was longing in it—stern,
controlled longing—and Barbara knew, with the intuition granted even to
spoiled young women at unexpected hours, that Painted Rock was
more than a place and his journey more than a
journey. Something beyond the trees called him with a voice
older than their acquaintance and stronger than any request she
might make.

She did not like this knowledge.

"Garrett," she said sleepily.

He turned at once.

It was the first time she had used his name.
The fire showed surprise in his face, followed by something
gentler.

"Are you frightened?"

"No. I only wanted to know you were there."

"I'm here."

He rebuilt the fire and tucked the edge of the
blanket around her with careful hands. His fingers touched her
hair, perhaps by accident. They remained still for one heartbeat,
then withdrew.

"Go to sleep, Barbara."

She obeyed.

Stanton sat down again, but he did not look toward
Painted Rock. For a long time he watched the little
blue rose still pinned crookedly upon her hat.

At dawn the forest awoke by degrees. First came a
thrush, sending three liquid notes through the grayness. Then a
squirrel scolded from a butternut; then light gathered in the
east and slid down the hemlock trunks. Mist rose from
the stream.

Barbara opened her eyes to see Stanton cooking breakfast.

She pushed back her disordered hair. "I must look dreadful."

"You look rested."

"That is not the same thing."

"No," he admitted.

He had repaired her shoe by removing the broken heel
and trimming the other to match. The result was inelegant
but serviceable.

"You have made them ugly," she said.

"They were waiting for the opportunity."

She ate bacon and bread, drank coffee without cream, and
discovered that the morning possessed a peculiar freshness never found
at breakfast tables. Stanton packed the blanket and utensils. Then
he scattered the smaller fire, drowned the embers, and covered
the dark earth with wet moss.

"Why do you do that?"

"To leave the place safe."

In a few minutes the camp had nearly vanished. Boughs
sprang back; smoke thinned; the woods leaned inward as though
reclaiming the hollow. Barbara felt a pang of unreasonable sorrow.

"Must we go?"

"Your aunt and uncle will be worried."

That, certainly, was true. Barbara pictured Aunt Eunice's white face
and Uncle Nathan calling through the trees. Shame replaced her
regret.

"Please hurry."

Stanton looked at her, then nodded. "This way."

### III

The journey was easier than Barbara expected.

Stanton found passages where she saw only brush, firm ground
where she saw only moss, and shallow crossings beneath the
deceptive glitter of streams. He lifted branches for her, offered
his hand on steep slopes, and once caught her neatly
around the waist when a stone rolled beneath her shoe.

She remained in his arms one instant longer than necessity
required.

An observant person might have noticed that Stanton's breath changed.
Barbara noticed only that his eyes were very gray at
close range.

"Thank you," she said.

He released her. "Watch the loose stones."

His voice had become unnecessarily curt.

They climbed a low rise. Sunlight shone beyond the trees.

"Are we nearly there?"

"Yes."

"How far?"

He did not answer.

Barbara stepped around a clump of hazel and stopped.

Below them, not two hundred yards away, stood Uncle Nathan's
cabin. Smoke rose from its chimney. The red pump gleamed
in the yard. Aunt Eunice's white sheets stirred upon the
line.

Barbara looked at the cabin.

Then she looked at Stanton.

"How long," she asked with terrible calm, "have we been
this near?"

He rested one hand on the strap of his pack.
"All night."

"All night?"

"The camp was just beyond the ridge. In daylight you
can see the chimney from it."

Barbara's eyes widened. "You knew?"

"Yes."

"You let me sleep in the woods—on balsam—wearing those unspeakable
socks—when I could have been home in ten minutes?"

"Twenty, at your pace."

She took a breath.

"Why?"

There are moments when even a self-possessed man may resemble
a boy caught robbing an orchard. Stanton glanced at the
sunlit roof below.

"It was dark," he said.

"You told me that."

"And you were exhausted."

"You told me that also."

"And—" He paused. "You seemed determined to have an adventure."

Barbara stared.

"So you gave me one?"

"Yes."

"You allowed me to believe there were miles of dangerous
wilderness between me and home?"

"I told you there were no wolves."

"You built two fires!"

"One was for your imagination."

"Mr. Stanton!"

"I'm sorry."

He did not look very sorry. This was perhaps because
his mouth had betrayed him.

Barbara turned away. Her indignation was vast, magnificent, and not
entirely sincere. Beneath it something laughed—a newly discovered Barbara who
wore gray socks and drank bitter coffee and had slept
soundly beneath balsam while a quiet man guarded the fire.

"You made a fool of me," she said.

"No."

"I was already one, perhaps?"

"I didn't say that."

"You thought it."

"I thought you were tired, frightened, and—" His expression changed.
"Very young."

"I am eighteen."

"I know."

She faced him. "And how ancient are you?"

"Twenty-eight."

"Ten years! You speak as though it were a century."

"Sometimes it is."

The amusement left them. The forest stood bright and still
around the little rise; from below came the faint slam
of a cabin door.

Stanton shifted his pack. "I must go."

The words seemed at first perfectly ordinary.

"Yes," Barbara said. "We must."

"I mean northwest."

"Oh."

He lifted his rifle.

Barbara looked at the long lane of trees behind him.
A moment before it had been merely forest. Now it
was the road by which he would disappear.

"But you will come down to the cabin first," she
said. "Aunt Eunice will wish to thank you. Uncle Nathan
too. They will insist that you stay for dinner."

"I can't."

"You can spare an hour."

"No."

"Half an hour, then."

"I've already lost the night."

The brightness of the morning sharpened painfully. Barbara tried to
smile.

"Of course. Painted Rock."

"Yes."

"When must you reach it?"

"By tomorrow afternoon."

"And if you do not?"

"I lose the only chance I'm likely to have."

"To find the man?"

"To finish what I began."

She studied his face. Whatever story lay behind the words
was shut against her, and for the first time her
curiosity did not seem a right. She wanted only to
open some door in that reserve and stand beside him
on the other side.

"When will you return?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"In a week?"

"No."

"A month?"

"Perhaps not."

"You might write."

He was silent.

Barbara's fingers tightened around the ivory handle of the wounded
parasol. "You know where I live in the city. I
told you last night."

"Yes."

"You remember?"

"I remember everything you said."

There was tenderness in the answer, but it did not
comfort her.

"Then you will come to see me."

Stanton looked down toward the cabin. "Barbara—"

"You must. We shall laugh about this. I shall have
a new gown, and you shall wear something civilized, and
I shall show you that I am not always ridiculous."

"I never thought you ridiculous."

"You laughed at my shoes."

"They deserved it."

"Then come and apologize to them."

His smile appeared and vanished. "I can't promise."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know what waits at Painted Rock."

The words hung quietly between them.

Barbara's romantic imagination, which had peopled the woods with villains,
knights, bears, and devoted savages, now deserted her. This was
not an agreeable mystery invented for an afternoon. Stanton might
fail. He might be injured. He might simply go on
beyond Painted Rock, back into whatever grave, lonely life had
shaped his eyes and steadied his hands.

She stepped toward him.

"Do not go."

He became very still.

"You don't know what you are asking."

"I am asking you to stay."

"I can't."

"You stayed last night."

"For you."

"Then stay today."

His hand closed hard upon the rifle strap. "I have
worked three years for this chance. I have crossed half
the country, taken work where I could find it, waited
through two winters for a trail to open. The man
I'm seeking will be at Painted Rock tomorrow and gone
the next day. If I miss him, everything begins again—if
it can begin again."

Barbara heard the words, but youth is not skilled in
surrender. To her, one urgent feeling made all other urgencies
unjust.

"And I mean nothing?"

He looked at her then.

The guarded tenderness of the night was no longer guarded.
It showed in his face with an intensity that frightened
her more than denial would have done.

"You mean too much," he said.

"Not enough to make you stay."

"No."

One short word. One clean stroke.

Barbara's face whitened.

She had never before offered herself, even by implication. Others
had offered; she had accepted flowers, rejected hopes, delayed answers,
summoned and dismissed with the easy cruelty of the beloved
young. She had thought hearts delightful, resilient things, chiefly useful
for proving her power.

Now something within her had been placed, trembling and defenseless,
in a man's hands—and he had refused it for a
journey through the woods.

Pride came to her rescue. Pride, so often a spoiled
child's weapon, became for one moment a woman's armor.

"How very foolish of me," she said.

Stanton took one step forward. "Barbara."

"Please don't." Her voice was light and admirably controlled. "You
are quite right. One must not miss appointments with mysterious
criminals merely because an acquaintance asks one to luncheon."

"It isn't like that."

"No? But I have no claim upon your explanations."

"You have a claim upon more than I can give."

She smiled. It was a little smile, bright and cold
as winter sunshine. "How chivalrous."

"Don't make it harder."

"For you?" Her brows lifted. "My dear Mr. Stanton, I
should not dream of it."

He winced. Barbara saw it and was at once triumphant
and miserable.

Below, a voice called faintly, "Barbara!"

Aunt Eunice.

Stanton turned toward the sound. "They're looking for you."

"Yes. I must go home, and you must go northwest.
How conveniently the world is arranged."

He held out his hand.

Barbara looked at it. Then, because refusal would have betrayed
too much, she placed her fingers in his.

His hand closed around them—not with the brisk clasp of
farewell, but slowly, as if learning their shape. His thumb
touched the small scratch a thorn had left across her
knuckle.

"I am sorry about the camp," he said.

"I forgive you."

"And about the shoes."

"They are beyond forgiveness."

"And for anything else."

She drew a breath. "There is a great deal else."

"I know."

He lifted her hand. For one wild instant she thought
he would draw her toward him, that Painted Rock, three
years, duty, mystery, all the stern architecture of his purpose
would fall away.

Instead he bent and kissed the tips of her fingers.

It was the lightest touch, grave and old-fashioned, yet all
the laughter of the night, all the warmth of the
fires, all the words neither had spoken seemed gathered into
it.

"Good-by, Barbara."

She could not answer.

He released her and turned.

"Garrett."

He stopped but did not come back.

"When you have done it—whatever it is—will you remember that
I asked you?"

"I'll remember."

"And if you can come?"

"I will."

"Is that a promise?"

"No."

Honest to the end. She almost hated him for it.

"Then good-by."

"Good-by."

He walked between the basswoods, gray shirt and brown pack
moving among bars of sunlight. At the edge of the
darker hemlocks he looked back.

Barbara stood straight and small upon the rise, her torn
cream frock bright against the green, the ridiculous parasol held
upright in one hand. Her hair had escaped beneath the
battered picture-hat; her face was pale, proud, and suddenly older.

He raised his hand.

She did not move.

Then the forest took him. A branch stirred; a shadow
shifted; there were straight towering trunks, saplings, undergrowth—and no Garrett
Stanton.

"Good-by," Barbara whispered.

From below came Uncle Nathan's shout, nearer now, and Aunt
Eunice calling her name with tears in it. Home waited:
alarm, embraces, explanations, hot baths, mended clothing, perhaps laughter when
the story had grown old enough to bear it. Beyond
the ridge, two dead fires cooled beneath wet moss, and
the hemlocks leaned over the place where a childish dream
had become, for a few hours, true.

Barbara remained motionless.

The green parasol slipped from her hand, struck the earth,
and lay among the ferns with its ivory handle shining.

She did not stoop to pick it up.

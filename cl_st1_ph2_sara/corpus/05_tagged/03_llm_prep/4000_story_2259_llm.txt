## THE DOOR AT DAWN
*A Play in Two Scenes*

### SCENE I

*The bedroom of CAPTAIN GADSBY'S bungalow in the Punjab. June.
Twenty minutes to four in the morning. The thermometer on
the veranda has forgotten how to fall and stands at
101°. The doors are open, but the night outside is
a black wall. A kerosene lamp burns low upon a
medicine-stained table. Two punkah-coolies sleep against the cord beyond the
doorway, jerking it feebly in their dreams.*

*MINNIE GADSBY, aged twenty, lies under a sheet. Her loosened
hair is dark with fever. Her husband sits beside her,
holding one wrist. The AYAH crouches at the foot of
the bed. SURGEON-MAJOR TREMAIN stands by the table, making calculations
upon the back of an old invitation card.*

TREMAIN. Pulse?

GADSBY. Don't ask me. It goes—and then it doesn't. Here!
Feel.

TREMAIN. I have felt it three hundred times.

GADSBY. Then feel it three hundred and one.

TREMAIN. If bullying could cure fever, the Army List would
be a pharmacopœia. Give me the wrist.

*He takes it. MINNIE moves her head.*

MINNIE. No—no, that is not mine. Minnie has white hands.
Those are dirty.

GADSBY. Minnie, darling.

MINNIE. Who spoke?

GADSBY. Pip spoke.

MINNIE. Pip is riding. He has the red coat on,
and he won't look up at the balcony. Cruel Pip!

GADSBY. I'm here. Look at me, Minnie.

MINNIE. You are not Pip. You have Pip's moustache, but
your face is old—quite old. Ayah, send this man away.

AYAH. Captain Sahib here, Memsahib. Own Captain Sahib.

MINNIE. He must not hear. Pip will be jealous.

GADSBY. Good God!

TREMAIN. Quiet.

MINNIE. Is the church ready? There ought to be flowers.
They promised lilies, but they sent horrid yellow things. Yellow
is unlucky. Mother said— Where is Mother?

GADSBY. At home, darling.

MINNIE. Which home? There are so many. The little house
by the river, and the red house, and this baked
box where everybody dies. Shut the windows. There are faces
outside.

AYAH. No faces, Memsahib.

MINNIE. I can see them. All the station ladies in
their best gowns, waiting to say, "We told you so."
I hate them. Pip, you mustn't ask Major Deane to
dinner. He drops gravy on his waistcoat.

GADSBY. I won't.

MINNIE. Promise properly.

GADSBY. I promise.

MINNIE. Not you! Pip must promise.

*GADSBY turns away. TREMAIN pours medicine into a glass.*

GADSBY. How long?

TREMAIN. I don't know.

GADSBY. You always know.

TREMAIN. Then why retain a doctor? Retain an oracle and
feed him on incense.

GADSBY. Don't play with me, Tremain.

TREMAIN. I'm not playing. She has been running this fever
for eleven days. The heart is exhausted. This may rally
her, or—

GADSBY. Or?

TREMAIN. Or not. Lift her.

*GADSBY raises MINNIE. Her head falls against his shoulder.*

MINNIE. The certificate is wrong.

GADSBY. Drink this, little woman.

MINNIE. They wrote my name too small. Minnie Frances—then a
blot—and then yours all over the paper. You took all
the room, Pip.

GADSBY. I'm sorry. Drink.

MINNIE. Are you sorry? You said I talked through the
band.

GADSBY. I was a brute.

MINNIE. Yes. A large, red brute with spurs. Why do
you wear spurs in the house? You scratch the floor—and
my arms. Don't touch me!

*She strikes at him weakly. Half the medicine spills.*

TREMAIN. Steady, woman! There's precious little left.

MINNIE. I'm not a woman. I'm only twenty. The Colonel's
wife said twenty was a child, and then she asked
me to arrange her horrid picnic. I won't go. It
will rain fire.

TREMAIN. Another spoonful.

MINNIE. Is it poison?

GADSBY. No.

MINNIE. You swear?

GADSBY. By everything I have.

MINNIE. You have me.

GADSBY. Then by you.

*She drinks, shudders, and sinks back.*

TREMAIN. Now we wait.

GADSBY. We have been waiting since yesterday morning.

TREMAIN. Medicine is chiefly waiting conducted by a man with
a diploma.

MINNIE. Don't wait. The train will go. Pip! Pip, my
box isn't packed. The blue frock—the one you like—no, not
that one; it has no sleeves. They'll laugh at me
in Heaven if I've no sleeves.

GADSBY. Minnie—

TREMAIN. Let her talk.

GADSBY. She says things she doesn't mean.

TREMAIN. Fever says what it pleases. You needn't answer it.

MINNIE. He never answers. He reads the Pioneer and says
"Mm." I could be murdered between the advertisements and he
would say "Mm." Pip!

GADSBY. Yes, darling.

MINNIE. You came at last.

*She looks directly at him. For a moment her eyes
are clear.*

GADSBY. Do you know me?

MINNIE. Of course. You look dreadful.

*He laughs once, without sound.*

GADSBY. That settles it. You know me.

MINNIE. Why is Doctor Tremain here?

TREMAIN. Professional curiosity, Mrs. Gadsby.

MINNIE. At this hour?

TREMAIN. Particularly at this hour. Respectable diseases occur after breakfast.

MINNIE. Am I very ill?

*Neither man answers.*

MINNIE. Pip?

GADSBY. You've had a bad turn, but—

MINNIE. Don't lie. Your left eyebrow goes up when you
lie. It went up before you proposed to me.

GADSBY. That was funk.

MINNIE. It is up now.

TREMAIN. Captain, come here.

*GADSBY lays MINNIE down and crosses to the table.*

TREMAIN. The stimulant has cleared her head. It may last
three minutes or thirty. Don't excite her.

GADSBY. What am I to say?

TREMAIN. Whatever she asks for—short of a ball and supper.

GADSBY. Can't you do something?

TREMAIN. I have. If she speaks painfully, remember that she
is very ill.

GADSBY. Damn your professional phrases.

TREMAIN. By all means. But keep your voice down.

MINNIE. Pip, why are you whispering about me?

*GADSBY returns quickly.*

GADSBY. We aren't.

MINNIE. There! The eyebrow. Pip—am I going to die?

GADSBY. No.

MINNIE. Look at me.

*He does.*

MINNIE. Say it again.

GADSBY. You are not going to die.

MINNIE. Your eyebrow— Oh, Pip!

*She catches both his hands.*

MINNIE. I don't want to! I don't want to! Tell
him I mustn't, Pip. You order people. Order him.

GADSBY. Tremain, tell her.

TREMAIN. We shall make the best fight we can, Mrs.
Gadsby.

MINNIE. That means yes.

GADSBY. It means nothing of the sort.

MINNIE. Help me, Pip. Don't sit there. Do something! Call
another doctor. Call all the doctors. Telegraph to Calcutta. The
Viceroy has doctors. He may lend one.

GADSBY. I'll fetch the whole blessed Medical Service.

MINNIE. No! Don't leave me.

GADSBY. I won't.

MINNIE. Not for one minute?

GADSBY. Not for one second.

MINNIE. Hold me harder. I feel as if I were
slipping under the bed.

*He gathers her into his arms.*

MINNIE. I have been wicked to you.

GADSBY. Never.

MINNIE. I broke your pipe.

GADSBY. It was a poisonous pipe.

MINNIE. On purpose.

GADSBY. It improved the house.

MINNIE. And I read your letters.

GADSBY. They were bills.

MINNIE. One was from a woman.

GADSBY. My aunt.

MINNIE. I know. I was angry because she called me
"the young person." I said dreadful things after the Club
dance.

GADSBY. I deserved 'em.

MINNIE. You didn't. I danced four times with Ronnie Carr
because you said my dress was foolish.

GADSBY. Carr dances like a commissariat camel.

MINNIE. He does. Oh, Pip, forgive me!

GADSBY. There's nothing to forgive.

MINNIE. Say the words.

GADSBY. I forgive you—all of it—the pipe, the letters, Carr,
everything. And you forgive me?

MINNIE. For what?

GADSBY. For being twice your age and ten times a
fool. For leaving you alone when I had mess business.
For laughing when you were frightened of the storm. For
that row in Simla. For—

MINNIE. Stop. I forgave you before you did it.

GADSBY. Then we're square.

MINNIE. No—we're married.

*She smiles faintly. TREMAIN turns to the medicine table and
rearranges bottles that do not require rearrangement.*

MINNIE. How long have we had?

GADSBY. Years and years.

MINNIE. Eleven months.

GADSBY. Eleven very long months. You've reorganised my entire existence.

MINNIE. It isn't enough. I was to be old and
ugly, and you were to say I hadn't changed. We
were to go Home together. You promised me roses by
the dining-room windows.

GADSBY. You shall have 'em.

MINNIE. Don't say "shall" as if I were a subaltern.

GADSBY. You will have them. Red roses. Thousands.

MINNIE. And a pony for the children.

*GADSBY lowers his face into her hair.*

MINNIE. Pip, are you crying?

GADSBY. Heat.

MINNIE. Liar. Dear liar.

*A long pause. Outside, a jackal cries. One punkah-coolie wakes,
pulls twice, and sleeps again.*

MINNIE. The church said "till death." Why did it say
that?

GADSBY. It's a form of words.

MINNIE. No. The parson meant it. He shut the book
afterward, as if everything was finished.

GADSBY. Parsons are paid to shut books.

MINNIE. Will it be finished?

GADSBY. No.

MINNIE. Swear.

GADSBY. It won't finish. Not now, not ever.

MINNIE. But if I can't find you?

GADSBY. I'll make enough noise.

MINNIE. You always do. Suppose I forget your face?

GADSBY. You won't.

MINNIE. Suppose you forget mine?

GADSBY. Minnie!

MINNIE. Men forget. They put photographs in drawers and marry
girls with yellow hair.

GADSBY. I shall not marry anyone.

MINNIE. You mustn't say that just because I'm ill.

GADSBY. I say it because it's true.

MINNIE. Not even when the Colonel's wife tells you it
is your duty?

GADSBY. Especially not then.

MINNIE. Promise not to let them throw away my things.
Keep the silver brush, though the handle is loose. Keep
one of my slippers—not the shabby one. And my little
white dress—

GADSBY. Stop it!

MINNIE. Pip—

GADSBY. You are not parceling yourself out like a kit
after an auction. You will use the brush and wear
the slippers and complain that the white dress makes you
look fat.

MINNIE. It does not!

GADSBY. There—you see? You're getting well already.

MINNIE. Kiss me.

*He kisses her.*

MINNIE. Again. Properly.

TREMAIN. No exertion.

MINNIE. Go away, Doctor.

TREMAIN. Delighted, if you will improve sufficiently to make me
useless.

MINNIE. I dislike doctors. They know the end and walk
about in squeaky boots.

TREMAIN. Mine do not squeak.

MINNIE. Your soul squeaks.

TREMAIN. Feverishly observant.

MINNIE. Pip, tell him to go.

GADSBY. Stay where you are, Tremain.

MINNIE. You always side with the men.

GADSBY. Treacherous hound that I am.

MINNIE. You are. Hold me closer.

*Her breath catches.*

GADSBY. Tremain!

TREMAIN. I'm here.

MINNIE. No—don't let him touch me. It is cold now.

GADSBY. The room's like a furnace.

MINNIE. I am cold. Ayah, pull the punkah.

AYAH. Punkah pulling, Memsahib.

MINNIE. Harder. No—stop it. The cloth is pushing me down.

AYAH. Achchha.

*The AYAH rises and knots the punkah cord to the
door-ring. The great fan hangs motionless.*

TREMAIN. Who told you to stop that?

AYAH. Memsahib telling.

TREMAIN. Start it again.

MINNIE. No!

GADSBY. Leave it stopped.

TREMAIN. The temperature in here—

GADSBY. Leave it!

MINNIE. Thank you, Pip. There's a door.

GADSBY. Where?

MINNIE. Behind you. Very dark. Shut it.

GADSBY. There's no door.

MINNIE. Darwaza bund, Ayah. Quick!

AYAH. All doors closed, Memsahib.

MINNIE. Not that one. Pip, don't let them take me
through.

GADSBY. No one shall.

MINNIE. You can't come.

GADSBY. Try stopping me.

MINNIE. They say wives end there.

GADSBY. They lie.

MINNIE. Say my name. I can't hear it.

GADSBY. Minnie.

MINNIE. Again.

GADSBY. Minnie—Minnie—my little Minnie.

MINNIE. Remember.

GADSBY. Always.

MINNIE. Remember the band on the lawn—and the rain at
Simla—and how frightened you were when I said yes.

GADSBY. I remember.

MINNIE. Remember me when I was pretty.

GADSBY. You are pretty.

MINNIE. Remember me when I was cross.

GADSBY. Frequently.

MINNIE. Beast.

GADSBY. Beloved beast.

MINNIE. Pip, I cannot breathe.

GADSBY. Tremain!

*TREMAIN bends over her, listening, counting, waiting. MINNIE's fingers close
once upon GADSBY'S sleeve.*

MINNIE. Not—the end—

*Her hand opens. Her head falls backward.*

GADSBY. Minnie?

*No answer.*

GADSBY. Minnie, that's enough. Wake up.

*TREMAIN places two fingers at her throat.*

GADSBY. Well?

TREMAIN. Wait.

GADSBY. You said we had waited enough.

TREMAIN. Be quiet.

GADSBY. Minnie!

TREMAIN. Gadsby—

GADSBY. Don't say my name like that.

TREMAIN. Put her down.

GADSBY. She hates being alone.

TREMAIN. Put her down, man.

*GADSBY lays her against the pillows and stands with both
hands extended, as if he has forgotten their use. TREMAIN
listens at her chest.*

GADSBY. Is that all?

TREMAIN. I'm sorry.

GADSBY. Sorry?

TREMAIN. I could do no more.

GADSBY. No more. Yes. Quite. Capital phrase.

*He picks up the punkah cord, drops it, lifts a
bottle, reads its label upside down, and puts it in
his pocket.*

TREMAIN. Sit down.

GADSBY. I'm perfectly well. Why shouldn't I be? There'll be
arrangements. Chits. People to notify. Her mother— I must write
to her mother. "Dear Madam, I regret to inform you
that your daughter—" That's wrong. One writes that about troopers.

TREMAIN. Gadsby—

GADSBY. She asked me to shut the door. I couldn't
even do that.

AYAH. Captain Sahib.

GADSBY. Not now.

AYAH. Memsahib body wet.

TREMAIN. What?

AYAH. See. Much pasina. Memsahib sweating.

*TREMAIN turns sharply. He touches MINNIE's forehead, then her throat,
then pulls back the sheet. The AYAH watches him with
severe patience.*

TREMAIN. Lamp here!

GADSBY. You said—

TREMAIN. Bring the damned lamp!

*GADSBY obeys. TREMAIN bends close.*

TREMAIN. There is respiration.

GADSBY. What?

TREMAIN. Very shallow. Stand back.

GADSBY. You said she was dead.

TREMAIN. I said I was sorry. Doctors are often sorry
prematurely. Ayah, dry cloths—quickly!

AYAH. Already bringing.

*She produces them from beneath the bed.*

TREMAIN. Why did you stop the punkah?

AYAH. Memsahib saying stop. Then sweat coming. If punkah pulling,
sweat going away.

TREMAIN. H'm. Accidental wisdom is still wisdom. Rub the hands.
Gadsby, not like you're grooming a horse! Gently.

GADSBY. Is she alive?

TREMAIN. At present.

GADSBY. Will she live?

TREMAIN. The fever may be breaking.

GADSBY. Will she live?

TREMAIN. Her chance was a thread. It is now—perhaps—a cord.

GADSBY. How much of a cord?

TREMAIN. Strong enough to hang a fool who asks questions.
Fetch Muir.

GADSBY. I won't leave her.

TREMAIN. Then fetch someone who can fetch Muir.

*As if summoned by bad timing, THE REVEREND HENRY VALE,
a young chaplain in an improperly buttoned coat, appears at
the veranda door.*

VALE. I beg your pardon. The servant sent word that—that
I might be required.

GADSBY. You're not required.

VALE. I understood the end was near.

GADSBY. The end has been postponed. Run to Doctor Muir.

VALE. Run?

TREMAIN. At once, Padre!

VALE. Yes. Naturally. Providence—

TREMAIN. Providence may wait. Muir lives three bungalows down. Use
your legs.

*VALE bolts into the darkness.*

GADSBY. Tremain, if you're wrong this time—

TREMAIN. I shall emigrate.

GADSBY. She moved!

TREMAIN. She breathed.

GADSBY. Same thing.

TREMAIN. Not medically.

MINNIE. Pip—

*It is scarcely a sound. GADSBY drops beside the bed.*

GADSBY. Here! I'm here.

TREMAIN. Don't make her talk.

GADSBY. I won't. Minnie, don't say a word. Not one
blessed word.

MINNIE. Hot.

TREMAIN. Excellent.

GADSBY. She says she's hot.

TREMAIN. I heard.

GADSBY. Is that excellent?

TREMAIN. At this moment, heat is the most beautiful word
in the English language.

*Beyond the veranda the blackness thins. A single bird makes
an experimental note. GADSBY rises, staggers through the doorway, and
stands in the dust-grey garden. His face is the colour
of good cigar-ash. He takes a curb-chain from his pocket,
though how it came there no one knows, and works
it aimlessly between his fingers.*

VALE'S VOICE. Doctor Muir! Doctor Muir!

GADSBY. Tremain.

TREMAIN. Well?

GADSBY. I c-can't—

TREMAIN. Yes, you can.

GADSBY. I thought she— I had her in my arms
and she—

TREMAIN. Blow your nose.

GADSBY. What?

TREMAIN. Blow your nose. You're making a spectacle of yourself.

*GADSBY blows his nose with military obedience and begins to
sob.*

TREMAIN. That's better. Come inside. Your wife is alive, and
if you wake the station I shall certify you unfit
for duty.

*The AYAH smiles once, privately, as dawn enters the room.*

### SCENE II

*The breakfast-room of the same bungalow, five weeks later. Nine
o'clock. Rain has washed the compound. MINNIE, thin but bright-eyed,
reclines in a cane chair wearing a white wrapper. GADSBY
butters toast with unnecessary concentration. The AYAH stands behind MINNIE.
A khitmatgar waits at the door.*

MINNIE. The tea is weak.

GADSBY. Tremain forbids strong tea.

MINNIE. Tremain forbids existence. Put in another spoonful.

GADSBY. Minnie—

MINNIE. Pip.

GADSBY. Two spoonfuls?

MINNIE. Three.

GADSBY. Reckless woman.

MINNIE. I have been dead. I may take liberties.

GADSBY. You were not dead.

MINNIE. You thought I was.

*GADSBY sets down the spoon.*

MINNIE. I remember.

GADSBY. How much?

MINNIE. The lamp. Your face. The horrid medicine. The dark
door. And you saying it was not the end.

GADSBY. It wasn't.

MINNIE. No. It was a very bad beginning.

*She reaches across the table. He takes her hand.*

MINNIE. We are different now.

GADSBY. You're thinner and more tyrannical.

MINNIE. And you cry more easily.

GADSBY. Heat.

MINNIE. Liar.

*The AYAH discreetly studies the ceiling.*

MINNIE. Ayah, what do you want?

AYAH. Memsahib well. Ayah wanting nothing.

MINNIE. Nonsense. Pip, she is to have fifty rupees.

GADSBY. A hundred.

MINNIE. And the blue shawl.

GADSBY. She'll look magnificent.

AYAH. Memsahib wearing blue shawl.

MINNIE. I have another.

AYAH. Memsahib always saying so, then wanting same shawl.

GADSBY. She knows you.

MINNIE. Very well—a new shawl. And gold bangles.

GADSBY. Both arms?

MINNIE. Both. She stopped the punkah.

AYAH. Memsahib telling stop.

MINNIE. Then she had the intelligence to obey me, which
nobody else in this house possesses.

GADSBY. Darwaza bund, Ayah. The Memsahib grows dangerous.

AYAH. Door closing, Sahib.

*She closes it and leaves them together.*

MINNIE. Pip, the kettle is empty.

GADSBY. I'll ring.

MINNIE. Don't. Stay.

*He remains beside her.*

MINNIE. Do you truly remember everything?

GADSBY. Everything.

MINNIE. Even Ronnie Carr?

GADSBY. Especially that commissariat camel.

MINNIE. Then kiss me before the kettle comes.

GADSBY. Doctor's orders?

MINNIE. Mine.

*He bends over her. Outside, the punkah begins its slow,
measured sweep, and the closed door trembles softly with the
morning air.*

### The Borrower in the Glass

Dutton accepted the invitation because he could discover no graceful
means of refusing it.

He had refused twice already. A third refusal, Mrs. Caldicott
informed him, would be interpreted by Sir James as a
personal criticism of Ashgrove, of the county, and possibly of
England. Dutton, who had no wish to criticise any of
these formidable institutions, packed a small bag and went.

He was a big, shy, awkward man, with the shoulders
of a porter and the apologetic manner of somebody who
has entered a drawing-room carrying coal. In his own studio
he managed well enough. Paint-boxes, canvases, pipes, books and boots
had learned his habits and made allowances for him. At
Ashgrove everything was polished, ancestral, and observant. Chairs stood ready
to detect a false movement; cabinets guarded porcelain with thin-lipped
vigilance; family portraits looked down upon him as if regretting
that no clause in the entail excluded painters.

The people were worse. There were fourteen of them, and
they all appeared to know one another's Christian names, political
opinions, and deficiencies at bridge. They swallowed him.

By the time he was conducted upstairs on Friday afternoon,
Dutton had shaken hands with three ladies whose names he
instantly confused, promised to inspect Sir James's new Dutch garden,
and agreed to play billiards with a young man who
called him "Dut" before they had been acquainted four minutes.

His room was magnificent and sepulchral. It might have swallowed
his London studio, easels and all, without feeling crowded. A
four-poster occupied the centre with an air of contemptuous permanence;
two tall cupboards expressed grave surprise at his arrival; the
fireplace gaped beneath a marble shelf covered with pale china
shepherdesses. Between the embrasured windows stood a dressing-table with a
triptych mirror, its side glasses turned inward so that the
room repeated itself in diminishing corridors—bed behind bed, window beyond
window, Dutton after Dutton retreating deep, deep down into a
silver dusk.

A servant followed with the bag.

He was a small, dark Irishman of perhaps twenty, neat
as a pin and quick without seeming hurried. Against the
enormous furniture he looked almost minute. When Dutton stood beside
him, the pair resembled two ants of very unequal size
consulting in a cave.

"Will I unpack for ye, sorr?"

"No, thank you. I can manage."

The young man's face fell so candidly that Dutton repented.

"Well—yes. If you like."

The bag contained little: a dinner-jacket, shirts, socks, brushes, a
safety razor, a collar-box, three books, two pipes, a tobacco-pouch,
a silver cigar-cutter, nail-scissors, a nickel collar stud, and a
small silver pencil-sharpener in the shape of a cannon. Dutton
had bought the last in Vienna and valued it beyond
reason. Its wheels revolved; its barrel lifted; and a pencil
inserted at the muzzle emerged sharp enough to sign a
treaty.

The servant laid out these possessions with extraordinary care. The
brushes were aligned upon the white cloth; the stud was
placed in a china saucer; the scissors and cigar-cutter lay
side by side; the little cannon stood between two candlesticks,
trained upon Dutton's hair tonic.

"You've made a museum of them," Dutton said.

"They're pretty things, sorr."

"Pretty? That cannon?"

"Bright, anyhow. An' small."

He turned the cannon so that moonlight—there was already a
pale promise of it in the evening sky—touched its barrel.
Then he glanced at the mirrors.

"You'd best put thim away after, sorr."

"Why?"

The boy hesitated. A very slight smile moved at the
corner of his mouth, but his eyes remained solemn.

"They do be losin' little things in this room."

"Who do?"

"The people that sleeps here."

Dutton looked towards the cupboards. They offered no explanation.

"Servants?" he suggested.

"Ah, no, sorr."

"Mice?"

"Not them kind of mice."

The answer had warmth in it, almost affection. Dutton became
aware that the young man wished to tell him something
and feared ridicule. This gave them unexpected kinship.

"What kind, then?"

The servant lowered his voice.

"There's a Borrower."

"A borrower?"

"That's what me grandmother called the like of it. It
takes only what's little, bright an' lovely. Never money, not
as money. A sovereign it might take for the shine
of it, but not for spendin'. Sure, what would it
spend it on?"

Dutton laughed, then checked himself because the boy did not.

"Does it return things?"

"If ye don't make a great sorrow over them. If
ye rage an' hunt, it'll hide them deeper. But if
ye say, 'I've no need of that at all,' back
they come. Most times."

"Most times?"

"It has fancies, sorr."

Dutton considered the silver cannon.

"And where does your Borrower live?"

The boy's glance went again to the dressing-glass.

"Not rightly in the house."

Before Dutton could ask what this meant, a gong sounded
below, solemn enough to announce the end of a dynasty.

"Dinner, sorr. I'm Michael. Ye'll ring if ye want anythin'."

He slipped out. Dutton stared after him, half amused and
half irritated. Country houses bred such inventions. Too many rooms,
too many dead relations, too much time between luncheon and
tea. Even servants were infected.

Yet he put the cannon in a drawer.

At dinner he sat between a deaf dowager and Miss
Caldicott, who questioned him about modern art with the air
of a magistrate investigating an outrage. He upset the salt,
contradicted Sir James by mistake, and called Lady Vane "Mrs.
Vane." Afterward he played billiards abominably. The young man who
called him Dut beat him three games and explained each
defeat.

By midnight Dutton longed for the dressing-room silence.

His collar stud was not in the saucer.

He searched the table, then the floor. He shook his
shirt, examined his cuffs, and went on hands and knees
beneath the bed, where he encountered a landscape of dustless
darkness and one contemptuous slipper. No stud.

Michael's nonsense returned to him.

"If ye rage an' hunt—"

Dutton stood up too quickly and struck his head against
the bed-frame.

"Confound the thing," he said. Then, feeling absurd, he added
aloud, "It's of no importance. I never liked it."

He used another stud and went to bed.

In the morning the missing one stood in the saucer.

It had a quizzical, malicious air of innocence.

Dutton rang.

Michael entered, saw the stud, and looked immediately at Dutton.

"Did you put that there?"

"No, sorr."

"Somebody did."

"Yes, sorr."

"You mean—"

"I mean nothin' at all, sorr."

But he was pleased. He polished the stud upon his
sleeve and set it down tenderly.

Dutton told himself he had overlooked it. The saucer was
patterned with blue flowers; nickel against blue and white might
escape notice at midnight. Perfectly simple.

On Saturday morning the house party drove to a meet.
Dutton, who did not hunt and disliked horses for recognising
this, remained behind under the pretence of sketching the western
terrace. Rain began. The terrace withdrew into a wet grey
sulk, and Dutton returned upstairs.

He sharpened a pencil with the silver cannon and placed
it beside his drawing-book. Then he crossed to the window
to study an ancient cedar, whose branches writhed like black
rivers against the rain.

When he turned back, the cannon had vanished.

Odd.

He had moved only six paces. The door was shut.
The cannon was not beneath the book, behind the candlestick,
nor on the carpet. He explored the drawers, although he
knew he had not opened them. He felt in his
pockets. Nothing.

The room watched.

The bed knew he was foolish; the cupboards knew it;
even the china shepherdesses appeared to exchange pallid little smiles.

"Well," Dutton said, "I've no need of it."

Nothing happened.

"I can sharpen pencils with a knife."

The rain whispered against the windows. Somewhere in the wall
a pipe clicked.

"An inconvenient, ridiculous toy."

Still nothing.

He went downstairs, made a bad sketch of the cedar,
ate luncheon alone, and returned at three. The cannon stood
upon the closed drawing-book, pointing at the door.

Dutton did not ring for Michael. He examined the object
first. It was his; a small scratch crossed the left
wheel where he had once dropped it. The metal felt
oddly cold.

He laughed. The laugh sounded too loud and stopped abruptly.

That afternoon the house filled again with wet boots, loud
voices and excuses concerning foxes. Social life resumed its siege.
Yet beneath Dutton's embarrassed replies and dutiful smiles moved a
private, shining thought. The Borrower. Absurd, naturally. But attractive. A
creature with no interest in banknotes, watches, pearl necklaces or
any of the burdensome valuables over which mankind quarrelled; a
collector merely of little bright and lovely things.

What did it do with them? He imagined a chamber
no larger than a glove-box, furnished with buttons, beads, pen-nibs
and fragments of coloured glass; a throne made from a
match-case; a chandelier of diamond chips too small for jewellers
to notice. The silver cannon might stand before a palace
gate.

At tea Miss Caldicott asked why he was smiling.

"I was thinking of armaments," he said.

She moved away.

Before dressing for dinner he found Michael arranging the brushes.
Dutton shut the door.

"Has anyone else seen it?"

"The Borrower, sorr?"

"Yes."

"Not clear."

"You believe in it?"

Michael considered.

"Believin' is a big word. I know it borrows."

"How long has it been here?"

"Longer than me. Maybe longer than the house. There was
a lady sleepin' here once had a little green stone
took from her ring. She near tore the room apart,
an' never got it back. Another gentleman lost a gold
tooth."

"A gold tooth?"

"He'd left it in a glass."

"That seems neither lovely nor delicate."

"It was bright, sorr."

Dutton conceded the point.

"And the mirror?"

Michael's hands became still.

"They say the glasses in ould houses don't all look
upon this wurrld. Some look the other way."

"Who says?"

"People that know better than ourselves."

This was unsatisfactory, yet delivered with such modest conviction that
Dutton could not dismiss it.

On the table lay the nail-scissors, the cigar-cutter and the
silver cannon. Three temptations.

"Leave them," Dutton said.

Michael looked alarmed.

"I want to see what happens."

"Better not, sorr."

"Why?"

"It might think ye're makin' sport of it."

"I should do nothing so rude."

The boy regarded Dutton's large hands and slow body.

"An' if ye see it, don't grab. Ye might hurt
it."

That notion had not occurred to him. He pictured his
fist descending upon some tender, quick thing, a creature like
a moth or a butterfly with a broken wing.

"I shall be careful."

"Careful men do the worst damage sometimes," Michael murmured.

At dinner Dutton spoke little. This was not unusual, and
nobody noticed. He escaped early, refusing bridge on the ground
of a headache, though his head had seldom felt clearer.

The scissors were gone.

He had left them beside the cannon. Now only the
cigar-cutter remained. He tried the formula.

"They are blunt," he announced. "I intended to replace them."

Then he sat by the fire with a book.

For twenty minutes he read the same page. A coal
settled softly. The clock upon the mantelpiece ticked with judicial
patience. Would they never come back? He would not look.
To look would show concern, and concern was precisely what
the creature desired—or disliked; the rules were not entirely plain.

At last he rose, stretched ostentatiously, and glanced at the
table.

The scissors had returned. They lay open in a perfect
V, their points directed towards the mirror.

Dutton's heart gave a disproportionate leap.

Now curiosity displaced caution. He prepared his experiment with the
earnestness of a scientist and the conscience of a burglar.
Upon the dressing-table he arranged the collar stud, scissors, cigar-cutter
and cannon in a bright row. He added a brass
waistcoat button, a glass marble found in a drawer, and
a strip of silver paper from a packet of cigarettes.
Then he extinguished the lamps, drew the curtains back, and
sat in an armchair near the bed.

Moonlight filled the room.

It did not illuminate so much as transform. The carpet
became frost; the bedposts rose like dark trees; the marble
fireplace was a tomb-mouth under snow. In the triptych glass
the chamber multiplied into pale avenues, each furnished with its
own waiting giant. Dutton scarcely recognised the foremost of these
giants as himself.

He waited.

The house sank by degrees into silence. Doors closed remotely;
water hurried through pipes; a final laugh travelled along a
corridor and was smothered. The clock chimed one. After that,
Ashgrove seemed to draw a long breath and hold it.

Dutton's knee ached. His left foot went to sleep.

It was an absurd position.

At half-past one he nearly abandoned the affair. He had
been tricked by an Irish servant's fairy tale and by
his own carelessness. The stud had never disappeared; the scissors
had caught in his cuff; the cannon—well, the cannon must
have been under the drawing-book. Memory was a clumsy housemaid,
forever putting things in the wrong rooms.

He closed his eyes.

A sound touched the silence.

Not a footstep. Not even the scratch of a mouse.
It was lighter than that: a brushing, a tremor, the
faint musical contact of something hard and tiny against glass.

Dutton opened his eyes.

The bright objects remained in their row.

He watched until his sight blurred. Moonlight lay upon the
mirror, intersected by reflections from the side panels; table within
table, cannon beyond cannon, silver point repeating silver point down
immeasurable corridors.

Again came the sound.

Tink.

The collar stud moved.

Only a fraction, perhaps. It seemed to turn as though
a minute finger had tested it. Dutton held his breath.

Something was present near the glass.

This he thinks he saw: a dimness no taller than
his hand, slender and upright, where no shadow ought to
have been; then a pale curve, delicate as the inside
of a shell. It hovered at the mirror's edge. Closer
it came, and closer, not across the table but from
within that deep reflected room where moonlight had another quality
and distance obeyed other laws.

A hand appeared.

He admits it may have been no hand. Intersecting lights
can devise anatomy; fatigue gives joints to shadows. Yet it
possessed five narrow fingers, each exquisitely small, and they folded
around the collar stud with an Adventurer's caution and a
miser's tenderness.

Dutton remained motionless.

The stud slipped towards the glass and disappeared.

Wonder rose in him, pure and almost painful. There it
was! The impossible, not thundering or terrible, but wee, dainty
and intent upon a trifle worth threepence.

The fingers returned. They passed over the button, rejected the
marble, stroked the scissors, and reached for the cannon.

That was too much.

Not because of its value, Dutton afterward insisted, but because
he wished to prevent the experiment ending. He leaned forward—slowly,
he believed. Yet Dutton's slow movement was another creature's avalanche.
The chair creaked; his great knee struck the table; the
mirrors shivered with moons.

The tiny hand snatched the cannon.

Dutton lunged.

For one instant his palm met something cool and soft,
lighter than a mouse's whiskers. A sensation of startled life
flickered against his skin. Then his fingers struck the central
glass.

The cannon vanished into it.

Or seemed to. There was a silver flash, a ripple
where no glass should ripple, and the reflected chambers swung
together like the blades of scissors. Dutton recoiled, knocking over
the chair.

Darkness settled.

He lit the lamps with shaking hands.

The collar stud lay under the table. The scissors, button,
marble, cigar-cutter and silver paper remained. But the pencil-sharpener was
gone.

He searched until dawn. He moved the dressing-table, emptied every
drawer, turned back the carpet, examined the fireplace and thrust
an arm into spaces where no rational cannon could have
travelled. Once he caught his own anxious face in the
mirror and felt that someone else had dodged away behind
it.

"What a blunderer I am!" he whispered.

At seven Michael entered and stopped.

He took in the displaced furniture, the open cupboards, the
rolled carpet and Dutton upon his knees before the hearth.

"Ye grabbed, sorr."

"I'm afraid I did."

"What's took?"

"The cannon."

Michael crossed himself, though not, Dutton thought, entirely in fear.

"Say ye don't care for it."

Dutton sat back on his heels.

"I don't care for it."

The room maintained a grave reserve.

"I always considered it vulgar."

Nothing.

"I am glad to be rid of the thing."

The mirror returned his lie from three directions.

Michael shook his head.

"Ye care too much now, sorr. It'll know."

They restored the room before breakfast. Dutton made Michael promise
to say nothing—not that the boy needed much persuasion. A
Borrower's confidence, he explained, was not a thing to scatter
among housemaids.

All Sunday Dutton hoped for the cannon's return. He neglected
the Dutch garden, lost at billiards, and answered the deaf
dowager so wildly that she accused him of socialism. Before
leaving on Monday he searched once more. The cannon did
not reappear.

At the station Sir James apologised for the dullness of
the weekend and urged him to come again.

Dutton said he should be delighted.

In town he went directly to a stationer's and bought
a plain steel pencil-sharpener for sixpence. It had no wheels,
no barrel, and no charm whatever.

He kept it in his pocket, away from mirrors.

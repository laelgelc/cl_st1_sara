The first intimation I received that Alfred considered speed a
virtue rather than a circumstance was when we passed through
the front veranda of the Wattle and Whistle Hotel.

The car had been standing at the horse-trough, facing west.
Alfred climbed in, touched two levers, trod upon something, and
the machine sprang forward with the enthusiasm of a dog
released from church. It crossed the road, mounted the veranda,
removed three flowerpots and a chair, descended by the steps,
and entered the main street in the proper direction.

"You might have gone round," I suggested.

"Might," said Alfred. "But it strains the tyres, turnin'."

Alfred was a small, square man with a face which
appeared to have been left out overnight in several climates.
He might have been thirty, or he might have been
the grandfather of the first selector in the district. His
eyes were mild and oddly intelligent; his hands, even when
not occupied, seemed to be adjusting an invisible carburettor.

We passed the post office at a rate which enabled
me to see the postmaster in five successive attitudes of
surprise. A yellow dog ran out barking. There was a
brief interruption beneath the wheels.

"Dog," I said.

"Was," said Alfred.

He sounded the horn courteously to a little girl at
a gate. She waved. Alfred lifted two fingers from the
wheel in a grave salute, then swung aside to avoid
a dray and took forty yards of paling fence instead.

Once established upon the open road, he accelerated. The landscape,
which had previously possessed trees, sheep and occasional habitations, simplified
itself into two brown streaks and a blue one. Alfred
sat upright, steering with the tranquil authority of an emperor
deciding which province to annex.

"Do you always drive like this?" I asked.

"No," he replied. "Sometimes there's traffic."

A horse appeared ahead, harnessed to a sulky. The driver
looked back, saw us, and immediately embraced the horse. We
went by on the wrong side, the correct side being
occupied by a bank, and something long and black fluttered
against my coat.

"What is that?"

"'Orse's tail," said Alfred. "You can keep it."

I placed the trophy on the floor.

"You appear to have had experience."

"I can drive," Alfred admitted. "Learned off of Henery."

"Who was Henery?"

"Chap wot got killed by a helephant."

I turned to look at him. He was watching the
road with complete indifference.

"An elephant?"

"Big grey animal. Trunk one end, tail the other."

"I am acquainted with its general construction. How did a
chauffeur in this district contrive to be killed by one?"

Alfred changed gear. The car uttered a metallic shriek and
leapt at a flock of sheep. Most of them separated.
Two failed to grasp the urgency of the occasion and
passed underneath.

"They're all right," Alfred said. "Sheep is very 'ard to
damage permanent. Henery used to say there wasn't much you
could do to a sheep except lose it."

"And the elephant?"

"I'm comin' to 'im. You got to know about Henery
first, else you won't see why the helephant mattered."

I braced my feet against the dashboard and consented.

"Henery," said Alfred, "was motor mad. Before motors come 'e
drove a steam traction, an' before that 'e used to
sit on the front gate with a billy-can lid an'
pretend it was a steerin'-wheel. 'E couldn't read much except
catalogues, but give 'im a motor stripped to the bones
an' 'e'd put it together so it went faster than
the bloke wot designed it ever intended.

"Now there was an old cove up on Boolabong Station—name
of John Bull. Not his real name, maybe, but everybody
called 'im that because 'e was English, red in the
face, and believed anything printed by the Government. 'E had
money, sheep, stations, an' eyesight that stopped about six feet
in front of him. Beyond that, a bullock team looked
to 'im like a lady with a parasol.

"John Bull wanted a motor. Not a racin' motor, mind.
'E didn't approve of speed. Used to say twenty mile
an hour was temptin' Providence, an' twenty-five was interferin' with
the police. So 'e sends Henery over to England with
a cheque and tells 'im to buy a quiet, comfortable
twenty-horse touring car."

"And Henery obeyed?"

"Henery was very loyal," said Alfred. "But 'e wasn't narrow-minded.

"'E gets to England an' looks at all the respectable
cars. Then 'e hears there's a ninety-horse racin' machine for
sale—long bonnet, low body, cylinders like kerosene drums, an' an
engine that'd pull the roof off Parliament House. It had
won races in France till the driver got religion. Henery
bought it."

"For a shortsighted gentleman who feared twenty-five miles an hour?"

"Just so. But 'e altered it. Took off the racin'
body, put on a sensible body with doors and cushions,
painted it dark green, stuck a brass lamp on each
side, an' made it look as solemn as a hearse.
Then 'e filed the maker's plate, changed the numbers, and
told John Bull it was twenty-horse."

"That seems dishonest."

"No," said Alfred thoughtfully. "The old cove asked for quiet
and comfortable. With Henery drivin', it was quiet because nobody
heard it long enough, an' comfortable because they got there
before they was tired."

A rabbit flashed across the road. Alfred pursued it for
some distance, not from malice, I think, but because it
had taken the lead. When it vanished into scrub, he
returned to the track with a slight expression of disappointment.

"John Bull," he continued, "was pleased with the motor, but
'e had a speed indicator put on. Said 'e liked
to know Henery wasn't exceedin' the legal limit. So Henery
fixed that too."

"In what way?"

"Made it read twelve when they was doin' twenty-four, twenty
when they was doin' forty, an' thirty when they was
doin' anything up to the end of the world. It
wouldn't register more'n thirty. Henery said no gentleman needed to
know bad news."

The road dipped before us, climbed a long ridge and
disappeared. Alfred opened the throttle. We descended into the hollow
and rose on the opposite side with such rapidity that
my internal arrangements seemed to remain temporarily at the bottom.

"Suppose Mr. Bull noticed the speed?"

"Couldn't. Too short-sighted. When they passed another car, 'e'd see
it flash by and say, 'Dear me, Henery, that fellow
is drivin' dangerously toward us.' An' Henery'd say, 'Reckless young
scoundrel, sir.' Then 'e'd give her another notch.

"Every motorin' bloke in the country heard about that dark-green
twenty-horse. Big fellows used to come hundreds of miles to
beat it. They'd wait outside towns, pretend they'd got a
plug dirty, then start when Henery come along. Henery never
passed a challenge. 'E'd sit forward, sort of affectionate, drop
his clutch, an' let the ninety horses out of the
stable.

"John Bull would be in the back readin' the Pastoral
Gazette two inches from his nose. After a bit 'e'd
look up and say, 'We seem to be moving briskly.'

"'Bit of a following wind, sir,' Henery'd tell 'im.

"Once they raced a six-cylinder imported job from Gundaroo to
Black's Creek. The imported car was doin' seventy. Henery went
by so fast its driver thought 'e'd stopped, got out,
and started lookin' for the trouble. Another time a squatter
tried to pass 'em near Murrumbidgee. Henery kept just ahead
for forty mile, then slowed till the squatter come alongside.
Henery asked 'im the time, thanked 'im, and vanished. The
squatter sold his car next day and bought camels."

"But where does the elephant enter this history?"

"Down the mountain," said Alfred. "We ain't got to the
mountain yet."

At that moment we met a teamster occupying the entire
road with twelve bullocks and a wagon-load of wool. Alfred
sounded the horn. The bullocks reflected upon this. He sounded
it again. The teamster removed his pipe and offered us
advice that was physically ambitious.

Alfred drove between the near wheels of the wagon and
the bank. There did not appear to be space, but
the car reduced itself for the occasion. A wool bale
brushed my hat away; one bullock sat down abruptly; and
we emerged carrying a portion of the wagon's brake-chain.

"Useful," Alfred remarked, tossing it behind. "Now, Henery had one
weakness."

"Only one?"

"Only one that affected his drivin'. 'E couldn't bear anything
ahead of 'im. Cart, motor, bicycle, funeral—it was all the
same. If it was in front, 'e had to ketch
it."

"A professional instinct?"

"Pride," said Alfred. "Same thing, mostly.

"One summer John Bull goes up to a property beyond
Mount Terrible. Road over the range was new, narrow, and
mostly hung on with wire. On one side you had
mountain; on the other you had enough empty air to
make a man careful. John Bull told Henery to drive
particular steady.

"'Certainly, sir,' says Henery. 'Dangerous road, sir.'

"Then 'e checks the false indicator to see it can't
get above thirty."

I noticed that our own road was now approaching a
wooded range of sinister elevation.

"Is this Mount Terrible?"

"Nar. This is worse."

Alfred pressed the accelerator.

"Henery and John Bull start up the mountain after dinner.
Halfway they stop at a bush pub for water. Henery
sees marks in the dust—great round holes, wide as wash-tubs,
three sets of 'em, all leadin' up the road.

"'Wot made them tracks?' 'e asks the publican.

"Publican says, 'Biggest turnout you ever seen. Went through an
hour back.'

"'Motor?' says Henery.

"'Sort of,' says the publican.

"Henery wants to know its power. Publican didn't understand engines,
but there was a wood-and-water joey sittin' on a stump,
an' 'e says, 'Three helephant-power.'

"Well, Henery had heard of horse-power, steam-power and manpower, but
not helephant-power. 'E reckoned it must be a new American
system. If one horse could pull what it did, 'e
calculated, one elephant ought to be equal to fifty horses,
easy. Three helephants made a hundred and fifty. There was
a machine ahead of 'im with sixty horse more than
his."

"At last," I said. "The elephant."

"Not yet," said Alfred. "Only the tracks."

We began climbing. The road coiled along the mountainside, narrow
and deeply rutted. Alfred drove as if gravity were a
timid theory recently disproved. Birds erupted from the trees; wallabies
abandoned their domestic concerns; a goanna, overtaken on a straight,
was carried for a quarter-mile upon the bonnet with the
fixed expression of a hood ornament.

"Henery gets excited," Alfred went on. "'E asks how long
since the three-elephant machine passed.

"'Hour, maybe,' says the publican.

"Henery laughs. An hour was nothing. 'E'd allow it ten
minutes to keep things sportin'.

"John Bull comes out wiping his glasses. 'Henery,' 'e says,
'I trust we are not pressed for time.'

"'No, sir,' says Henery. 'We'll take it very easy down
the other side.'

"They climb to the top. From there the road fell
away in twists for twelve mile, with cliffs above and
gorges below, and not a fence because there wasn't anything
a fence could do. Far down the slope Henery sees
dust."

"The elephants?"

"The dust," Alfred corrected. "Could've been anything.

"'E puts the car in top, releases the brake, an'
lets her roll. The ninety-horse knows there's something ahead and
begins pullin' at the road. Forty, fifty, sixty—the indicator says
thirty, calm as a magistrate.

"John Bull peers over the side. 'A magnificent prospect,' he
says.

"'Yes, sir,' says Henery. 'Don't lean out.'

"They take the first corner with two wheels on the
ground and the other two considerin' their position. At the
second, a mail coach appears comin' up. Henery goes outside
it, over the edge, with one wheel runnin' on atmosphere.
The coach driver shuts his eyes and doesn't open 'em
till Christmas.

"Then a mob of cattle fills the road. Henery touches
the horn. Cattle go everywhere—up trees, down gullies, through one
another. John Bull looks up from his paper.

"'Was that thunder?'

"'Heat, sir,' says Henery.

"The dust ahead gets thicker. Henery can see three enormous
shapes movin' through it. They're slow but powerful, swayin' along
and occupyin' the whole track. 'E reckons this new motor
must have cylinders vertical, because the shapes keep goin' up
and down."

I held up a hand. "Surely he could now see
they were elephants."

"Henery never wasted eyesight when 'e could use imagination."

Our car reached the summit. Before us, the road dropped
in white loops through the timber. Alfred switched off the
engine.

"Petrol economy?" I asked.

"Speed economy."

We went down soundlessly, gathering momentum with the grave ease
of a falling safe.

"Now," he said, raising his voice only slightly above the
rush of air, "there was a circus movin' to the
next town. One elephant had sore feet, so they put
it in a big wagon. Another elephant pulled in front
and a third pushed behind with its head against the
tailboard. That was your three-elephant-power. The wheels made the tracks."

"And Henery?"

"Henery sees the rear elephant at last. But all 'e
sees is a grey wall ahead with two ears flappin',
and 'e thinks it's a dust-cover on the back of
the machine. 'E decides to pass.

"There wasn't room on the inside because of the cliff,
nor outside because of the other cliff. So 'e blows
his horn.

"The rear elephant had never heard a motor horn six
inches from its tail. It takes one look round and
pushes harder. Front elephant feels the wagon jump and pulls
harder. The sore-footed elephant inside gets alarmed and sits down.
The whole circus begins doin' near thirty.

"Henery's delighted. Thinks they're racin'.

"He comes up behind, falls back, comes up again. Radiator's
boilin'. John Bull takes off his spectacles, cleans 'em, and
says, 'Henery, are we still within the limit?'

"'Indicator says thirty, sir.'

"'That is rather fast.'

"'Downhill, sir.'

"Then the brakes fade."

Alfred said this with professional sympathy.

"The car leaps forward. Henery changes down, but the lever
comes off in his hand. 'E tries to turn aside,
but there's a mountain on the left and several hundred
thousand feet of vacancy on the right. So the hot
radiator runs square into the rear elephant."

"What happened?"

"Elephant objects."

Our own engine resumed with an explosion as Alfred engaged
a gear. We shot round a bend and disturbed a
party taking tea beside the road. A tablecloth rose into
the air; cups scattered; a stout woman retained the teapot
and poured steadily into space.

Alfred continued.

"The elephant turns round. The wagon keeps goin', so for
a moment the elephant is travelin' backwards, which annoys it
worse. Steam's risin' off its legs, the horn's stuck bellowin',
and Henery's shovin' both feet through the brake-board.

"Then the elephant reaches down with its trunk, lifts Henery
clean out of the driver's seat, waves 'im once to
make sure 'e's the responsible party, and slings 'im over
the edge."

"How far did he fall?"

"Depends where you measure from. They never found enough to
settle it."

"And John Bull?"

"Sat in the back. Car wedged under the elephant, so
'e wasn't hurt. When the dust cleared, 'e climbed out
and asked the circus bloke whether Henery had gone for
assistance.

"Later they explained there'd been an accident. John Bull was
cut up terrible.

"'A more steady and trustworthy chauffeur never lived,' 'e says.
'He never once exceeded thirty miles an hour.'"

There was a silence, filled by the protesting chassis and
the steady impact of insects against my face.

"And the car?"

"John Bull had it repaired. Advertised for a careful driver
for a quiet twenty-horse touring machine. Scotty got the job."

"Did Scotty discover the truth?"

"First time 'e opened the bonnet. Scotty could count cylinders.
He also found Henery's false plate and the fixed indicator."

"And told his employer?"

Alfred looked almost shocked.

"Wot for? Good jobs was scarce. Scotty drives John Bull
still. Keeps the car polished, never lets the indicator above
thirty, an' has the fastest twenty-horse motor in Australia."

"Does he drive as Henery did?"

"Faster in places. But Scotty's cautious."

"In what respect?"

"Won't go within fifty mile of a circus."

At the foot of the mountain we entered a small
township at something less than a hundred miles an hour.
A painted board announced:

GRAND TRAVELLING MENAGERIE
ONE NIGHT ONLY
THREE PERFORMING ELEPHANTS

Alfred read it, braked violently, and turned through the open
doors of a bakery. We passed between two counters, circled
an astonished baker, and came out through a flour shed
into a lane leading back toward the mountain.

"You believe the story, then?" I asked, brushing dough from
my sleeve.

Alfred glanced behind at the circus sign diminishing in the
distance.

"Believe it?" he said. "Scotty's me brother."

Then he accelerated, and we returned over the range by
the cautious route.

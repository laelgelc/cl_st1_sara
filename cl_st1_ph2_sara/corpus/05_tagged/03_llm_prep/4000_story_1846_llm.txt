# The Two Banners of Little Britain

I am an idle man.

This confession, which in a person of consequence might demand
apology, has in me long passed for a description. I
have neither estate to mismanage, profession to adorn, nor family
to govern; and, having discovered early in life that the
world contains a sufficient number of busy persons to perform
its business badly, I have never thought it necessary to
increase their perplexities by offering my assistance. My chief occupation
has therefore been to look from windows, to turn corners
without purpose, to know the histories of houses in which
I have no concern, and to become acquainted with persons
who would have distrusted me had I possessed anything like
a regular employment.

It was in consequence of this harmless nullity that, some
years since, I found myself established in a lodging in
Little Britain, in the very kernel and heart's core of
the city. My apartments were up two pair of stairs
in a house which leaned so affectionately toward the opposite
building that the inhabitants might almost have shaken hands across
the street. The front was embroidered with black beams; the
upper story projected over the lower like the brow of
an ancient counsellor; and beneath the sill of my sitting-room
window was carved a wooden griffin, which had lost one
wing, half its tail, and all pretensions to zoological accuracy,
but still maintained a countenance of undiminished ferocity.

Little Britain was then a world enclosed. Aldersgate Street rolled
on one side of it like a great channel of
trade; Smithfield breathed upon another with the odors of cattle,
straw, drovers, and roasted meat; while the dome of St.
Paul's, seen above roofs and chimneys, appeared to keep a
maternal eye upon the little community, though I doubt whether
the little community returned the compliment. The streets within were
narrow, crooked, and confidential. They seemed formed, not for passage,
but for neighborhood. Every doorway looked into every other doorway;
every window commanded a family history; and a stranger could
not enter the precinct without being observed by three housewives,
a barber's apprentice, two children, and an old gentleman who
had no visible means of knowing anything, yet knew more
than all the rest.

The houses were of several ages, all equally old in
the estimation of their inhabitants. One had a porch supported
by two pillars, which were said to have belonged to
a Roman temple, though they had plainly been turned by
a joiner in Shoreditch. Another contained a fretted ceiling, blackened
by time and smoke, where cherubs, roses, pears, acorns, and
what might have been either a lion or a boiled
codfish struggled together in perpetual ornament. There were windows scratched
with names and dates; cupboards concealing dry sprigs of rosemary;
staircases polished into hollows by generations of cautious feet; and
chimney-pieces bearing landscapes in blue tiles, wherein Dutch shepherdesses, no
larger than teaspoons, contemplated windmills beneath skies of the most
alarming indigo.

Everything had a tradition. A certain iron ring in a
wall had once held the horse of Queen Elizabeth; a
cellar beneath a pastry-cook's had afforded refuge to a cavalier;
a discolored patch on the ceiling of the Blue Boar
was the mark of a bottle flung at a Jacobite;
and a pump at the corner had yielded, on the
night of the great frost, water which froze before it
reached the pail. These facts were not matters of speculation,
but of civic faith. To question them would have been
as indecent as to dispute the calendar in church.

Little Britain was proud of being English. Indeed, I have
sometimes thought that England existed chiefly for the purpose of
supplying Little Britain with a national character. Roast beef, plum-pudding,
church bells, leather breeches, oak furniture, October ale, and the
hatred of Frenchmen were preserved there with a devotion which
left the rest of the kingdom at liberty to neglect
them. The inhabitants spoke of the constitution as if it
were kept in a strongbox behind the cheesemonger's counter; and
every rise in the price of bread, every rumor of
war, every royal marriage, every murder in the newspapers, and
every comet advertised in an almanac was interpreted according to
its probable effect upon the liberties of Britons and the
trade of the neighborhood.

The principal oracle upon these subjects was Mr. Crumb, the
cheesemonger. He was a round, mature man, yellowish in complexion,
with small gray eyes and a smooth bald head, and
sat in the bow-window of his shop so completely surrounded
by cheeses that he seemed the presiding mite of some
vast dairy providence. His political opinions ripened in proportion to
the age of his stock. When seated between a blue-veined
Stilton and an ancient Cheshire, he could settle the affairs
of Europe in less time than it required his boy
to weigh a pound of Gloucester.

Mr. Crumb maintained that the French were naturally incapable of
liberty because they ate frogs, that no nation wearing wooden
shoes could possess sound credit, and that the prosperity of
England depended upon the consumption of cheese by the laboring
classes. He read the newspaper two days after its publication,
in order, as he said, to give events time to
become authentic; and when he had finished it, he folded
it with judicial gravity and observed that ministers might do
as they pleased, but they would find Little Britain a
hard mouthful.

Opposite Mr. Crumb dwelt Mr. Skryme, the apothecary, whose person
was as full of warnings as his shop. His face
consisted chiefly of angles, recesses, and unexpected projections; his nose
leaned toward his chin as if to impart intelligence; and
his eyebrows went up and down with every report of
fever, earthquake, robbery, or foreign invasion. He kept dried alligators,
horned toads, and bundles of herbs in his window, together
with a skeleton whose melancholy grin had frightened several children
into habits of obedience.

Mr. Skryme was the custodian of local superstitions. He knew
the precise houses in which clocks had stopped at the
death of their owners; he could name seven persons whose
funerals had been foretold by coal jumping from the fire;
and he had once heard a death-watch so plainly that
he sent a boy for the undertaker before discovering that
the ticking proceeded from a beetle in a wooden pill-box.
A dog howling at midnight was, in his judgment, a
domestic bulletin from the other world; a knife and fork
crossed upon a plate announced a quarrel; three candles in
a room demanded immediate extinction; and a bride who saw
a black cat before breakfast might as well order mourning.

Yet no one was more cheerful than Mr. Skryme when
his prognostics failed. He had a professional satisfaction in danger,
but no personal ill-will toward mankind; and when an expected
calamity did not occur, he merely postponed it.

The soul of our festivities was Dick Wagstaff, an upholsterer
by occupation and master of ceremonies by natural right. Dick
had bandy legs, a moist blue eye, and a nose
whose color testified to an affectionate understanding with the punch-bowl.
He sang songs of sailors, poachers, loyal lovers, wooden walls,
and brave old England; he could dance a hornpipe within
the compass of a hearth-rug; and he possessed that rare
genius which enables a man to organize the pleasures of
others while enjoying himself more heartily than any of them.

Under his direction we had clubs, suppers, excursions, lotteries, subscriptions,
anniversaries, and commemorations without number. There was the Beef and
Liberty Club, meeting on Tuesdays; the Friendly Brothers of the
Pewter Pot, assembling whenever sufficient thirst could be collected; the
Ancient Order of True Britons, whose antiquity went back nearly
nine years; and the Benevolent Burial Society, which divided twice
over a question concerning the proper width of mourning hatbands.

Our calendar was crowded. On Shrove Tuesday the apprentices played
football with an energy which threatened the windows, the limbs
of spectators, and occasionally the public peace. On May Day
a chimney-sweep, black as destiny and crowned with gilt paper,
danced with a milkmaid whose silver cups rattled like a
traveling kitchen. At midsummer we went to Hampstead with baskets
of cold beef, radishes, pies, gingerbread, and bottles which Mr.
Wagstaff called "the artillery." On the fifth of November we
burned a figure stuffed with shavings, though disputes repeatedly arose
whether it represented Guy Fawkes, the Pope, Napoleon, or the
tax-gatherer. At Christmas every shop hung holly; every door admitted
cousins; every kitchen steamed; and every man who could sing
was compelled to prove it.

No family stood more honorably in these observances than that
of Mr. Lamb, a retired butcher who had made a
handsome fortune in Newgate Market and brought it home to
Little Britain, where he intended, as he expressed it, to
enjoy the remainder of his days in peace and plenty.

Mr. Lamb was a short, stout man, with a fresh
complexion, a square jaw, and gray hair which stood upright
upon his head like the bristles of a well-used clothes-brush.
His hands were broad and ruddy; his laugh came from
a great depth; and he carved a joint with a
tenderness which was almost parental. Though retired from business, he
continued to judge his acquaintances by their weight, substance, and
condition. A thin man was never wholly satisfactory to him.
Of a pale young clerk he would say that there
was "no feeding in him"; while a substantial citizen, with
a red neck and ample waistcoat, commanded his immediate respect.

His wife, Mrs. Lamb, was of a different composition. She
had been handsome, was still imposing, and had never forgiven
Providence for placing her among persons who knew what her
husband had sold. She dressed richly, spoke of "our carriage"
when she meant a hired coach, and possessed an inexhaustible
acquaintance with the names of persons in fashionable life. She
could tell who had been presented at court, who had
married a baronet, who had dined with an alderman, and
who had a cousin residing within three streets of a
duchess. She read the lists of company in the newspapers
as a scholar reads antiquity, and had long maintained that
Mr. Lamb's fortune, properly displayed, might open the best society.

There were three Miss Lambs—Miss Arabella, Miss Clarissa, and Miss
Susan. Arabella was tall and grave, with a long neck
which she adorned by every means likely to make it
appear longer. Clarissa had curls, dimples, and a constant expression
of having heard something too genteel to repeat. Susan, the
youngest, had once climbed trees, played battledore in the street,
and eaten toasted cheese at Mr. Crumb's back parlor; but
she was rapidly acquiring sensibility, a pale complexion, and a
reluctance to be amused.

For many years the Lambs had been pillars of our
society. Their Twelfth Night parties were famous. Mr. Lamb brewed
the punch; Mrs. Lamb dealt cards; Arabella sang "The Lass
of Richmond Hill"; Clarissa played a country dance upon the
pianoforte with two fingers and much determination; and Susan joined
the forfeits without considering them destructive of female dignity. The
house rang with laughter. Mr. Crumb danced with Mrs. Lamb;
Mr. Skryme told ghost stories until the maids refused to
go upstairs alone; and Dick Wagstaff, after balancing a spoon
upon his nose, made a speech upon national happiness.

Then came the Lady Mayoress's ball.

By what combination of municipal accident and maternal diplomacy Miss
Arabella obtained an invitation to that portentous festival, I never
clearly learned. Mrs. Lamb attributed it to family connection; Mr.
Crumb to a mistake in the direction; Dick Wagstaff to
the alderman's appetite for mutton. Whatever the cause, Miss Arabella
went, attended by her mother, in a hired carriage with
two lamps and a coachman whose hat was so broad
and shining that half the neighborhood assembled to admire it.

They departed at nine. At half past nine Mr. Skryme
predicted that the horses would take fright. At ten Mr.
Crumb observed that city entertainments were becoming scandalously late. At
eleven Dick Wagstaff led a party to the corner to
listen for wheels. At two in the morning the carriage
returned, and Arabella descended from it another woman.

She had danced a quadrille.

This simple fact, communicated next day through the butcher's maid,
spread through Little Britain with the violence of a military
invasion. A quadrille was at that time considered among us
a foreign maneuver, compounded of French levity, aristocratic insolence, and
unnecessary walking. That a daughter of honest Lamb should have
advanced, retreated, crossed over, turned corners, and set to a
partner in obedience to unknown figures was felt by many
to be a public apostasy.

Mrs. Lamb, however, regarded the evening as the opening of
a new era. She had sat within three chairs of
a knight's lady; Arabella had danced opposite the nephew of
a sheriff; and Clarissa had heard from Arabella that a
gentleman with a star had looked toward their box, though
whether he looked at Arabella, at the feathers behind her,
or at the wall was a matter beneath investigation.

From that hour the Lamb household underwent a transformation.

The old mahogany chairs were condemned as heavy; the Turkey
carpet as vulgar; the family portraits as frightful; and the
china dogs on the mantelpiece as positively impossible. A drawing-master
appeared, then a dancing-master, then a French teacher with black
whiskers and varnished boots, who addressed Mr. Lamb as "Monsieur
Lomme" and praised the English roast beef while eating three
portions of it. Fashionable novels lay upon the tables, their
leaves uncut but their titles conspicuous. Arabella began a watercolor
of a ruined abbey; Clarissa practiced arpeggios from breakfast until
the neighborhood knew every note individually; and Susan spoke of
having nerves.

Their dress altered with equal rapidity. Bonnets expanded and contracted
according to intelligence from the West End. Sleeves swelled to
such magnitude that two Miss Lambs could scarcely pass through
the same doorway. Feathers nodded, ribbons streamed, waists migrated upward
and downward, and shoes became too delicate for any pavement
within half a mile. Mrs. Lamb imported a French maid
from Camden Town, who pronounced English caps barbarous and reduced
the household servants to rebellion by demanding coffee before ten.

Mr. Lamb alone remained unconverted. He submitted to tight coats,
narrow boots, and a stock which held his head like
a prisoner in the pillory; but his heart was still
with the old company. He would slip across to Mr.
Crumb's of an evening and sit among the cheeses, speaking
in a low voice of former suppers. Sometimes, when he
heard Dick Wagstaff singing at the Blue Boar, his countenance
brightened and his foot moved beneath the table; but a
message from Mrs. Lamb generally recalled him to gentility before
the chorus.

The first open breach occurred at the annual game of
hot cockles, held at the Wagstaffs'. The Lambs sent regrets.
Arabella found the amusement childish; Clarissa thought it rough; Susan
had a headache; and Mrs. Lamb observed that young ladies
who had appeared at the Mansion House could not be
expected to kneel blindfolded upon an upholsterer's carpet and guess
who struck them.

This declaration wounded the neighborhood in its tenderest part. Hot
cockles was not regarded as a game, but as an
institution. Mr. Crumb asserted that it had been played before
Magna Charta. Mr. Skryme recalled an uncle who had refused
to join it and died within the year. Dick Wagstaff
declared that if the daughters of Britain were ashamed of
innocent mirth, the nation might prepare for slavery.

Still, indignation might have subsided had it not been followed
by the Lambs' grand ball.

For three weeks preparations shook the street. Carpenters entered, pastry-cooks
departed, chairs arrived by cart, lamps by basket, flowers by
wagon, and rumor by every door. The drawing-room and dining-room
were thrown together at the expense of a partition which
had stood since the reign of George the Second. A
chalked floor was laid for dancing. A musician with a
fiddle inspected the premises and pronounced them "confined but usable."
There was to be ice, which in Little Britain was
considered a luxury bordering upon necromancy; champagne, known locally chiefly
by pictures in wine merchants' windows; and a supper at
midnight, an hour when respectable digestion had hitherto been asleep.

The question of invitations occupied the whole community. Every morning
produced a new list; every evening destroyed it. The Miss
Lambs were seen carrying gilt-edged cards. The postman became an
officer of destiny. Persons who had never visited the Lambs
began to recollect distant civilities; others who had known them
twenty years examined each delivery with concealed anxiety.

No invitations came.

Mr. Crumb was omitted. Mr. Skryme was omitted. Dick Wagstaff,
who had taught all three girls to dance a country
measure, was omitted. The baker, the stationer, the widow Pike,
the Misses Rumble, the entire Beef and Liberty Club, and
even the curate's aunt were omitted. In their place arrived
a collection of persons from other districts: a coal merchant
from Islington who wore a quizzing-glass; two young men from
a counting-house who were reported to be related to an
admiral; a family from Pentonville possessing a footman; a dancing-master
and his sister; several clerks of fashionable exterior; and a
gentleman described as having "something to do with the court,"
though it afterward appeared that he supplied lamp oil to
an office in Westminster.

The evening of the ball is still remembered among us
as the Night of the Exclusion. The inhabitants sat behind
curtains and watched the carriages. They counted the guests, criticized
the dresses, identified hired servants, and detected false jewels at
distances which would have defeated a jeweler. Mr. Crumb kept
open shop until midnight under pretense of stocktaking. Mr. Skryme
passed repeatedly with medicine for an imaginary patient. Dick Wagstaff
stood at the Blue Boar and furnished reports as from
a field of battle.

At eleven the quadrilles began.

The scraping of fiddles, the thumping of feet, and the
cries of the dancing-master floated through the opened windows. Little
Britain listened in solemn resentment. When a tray of broken
jellies was carried into the yard, Mrs. Pike declared it
a judgment. When one of the hired horses fell down,
Mr. Skryme pointed to the fulfillment of his earliest prediction.
When the coal merchant from Islington came out at three
in the morning without his hat, Mr. Crumb announced that
fashionable society had exposed its true principles.

For several days the neighborhood spoke of nothing else. The
Lambs were denounced as renegades, butterflies, upstarts, and enemies of
the British character. Their French teacher was accused of being
a spy. The quadrille was described as a dance invented
during the Revolution for the purpose of confusing honest people.
Champagne was pronounced sour cider with a string round the
cork. Everyone agreed that the old ways were the best,
that pretension brought its own punishment, and that no respectable
family could desire acquaintance above its station.

Then the imitation began.

Mrs. Pike was the first to observe that there could
be no harm in improving one's manners. The Misses Rumble,
who had ridiculed Arabella's sleeves, appeared with sleeves of twice
the circumference. The stationer placed three fashionable novels in his
window and sold seventeen copies, chiefly to persons who condemned
them. Mr. Crumb's niece learned the beginning of a quadrille
from a magazine. Dick Wagstaff was heard practicing a waltz
step in his workshop, though he maintained that he did
so merely to understand the tactics of the enemy.

Soon pianofortes multiplied. Where once a fiddle had served six
streets, every third house now possessed an instrument, and every
instrument possessed a young lady determined to conquer it. Morning,
noon, and evening the air vibrated with scales, exercises, sentimental
songs, martial duets, and variations upon melodies already sufficiently afflicted.
French words entered ordinary speech. Shops became establishments; gatherings became
soirées; gowns became costumes; and a man who formerly went
upstairs now ascended to the apartments.

It was at this critical period that the Trotters took
the field.

Mrs. Trotter was the widow of a wealthy contractor who
had supplied boots to the army and left behind him
a fortune, three daughters, and a portrait in which he
stood beside a cannon wearing boots of his own manufacture.
She inhabited a large house at the other end of
the street, with brass plates upon the door, stone urns
upon the parapet, and a back garden containing a poplar
tree which she called the avenue.

The widow was a woman of majestic breadth, with a
purple turban, a firm mouth, and a bosom that advanced
into a room several moments before the rest of her.
She had hitherto affected simplicity, partly because Mrs. Lamb affected
splendor; but the ball awakened her military blood. If the
Lambs intended to dominate Little Britain by fashion, the Trotters
would resist by superior fashion. If Arabella knew the nephew
of a sheriff, Miss Amelia Trotter had once been introduced
to the married daughter of a deputy lieutenant. If the
Lambs gave champagne, the Trotters would provide champagne in larger
glasses.

The three Miss Trotters—Amelia, Belinda, and Georgiana—were fine, high-colored young
women, abundantly supplied with shoulders, teeth, and determination. They had
been educated at a boarding school where they acquired the
use of globes, the painting of shells, and a handwriting
sloping so violently to the right that every letter appeared
to be escaping. They entered the contest with spirit. Amelia
cultivated dignity, Belinda romance, and Georgiana music; and all three
agreed to despise the Lambs.

Mrs. Trotter began operations by issuing invitations to an evening
party which included every person excluded by Mrs. Lamb, together
with several persons whom Mrs. Lamb had invited and wished
to retain. The cards were larger, the gilt borders broader,
and the hour later. A manservant in blue livery delivered
them, although it was discovered that he usually worked in
a warehouse and had been hired for the afternoon.

The Trotter entertainment surpassed its rival in noise, illumination, and
provisions. There were twelve additional lamps, two fiddles instead of
one, a harp which occupied nearly as much room as
the refreshments, and a pineapple placed in the center of
the supper table, where it remained untouched as an emblem
of wealth. Mrs. Trotter received her guests beneath the contractor's
portrait. The Misses Trotter danced with vigor. Dick Wagstaff, restored
to honor, called the figures; Mr. Crumb drank to native
merit; and Mr. Skryme, after consuming lobster salad, foretold an
epidemic among the Lamb faction.

The battle was now declared.

Little Britain divided beneath the banners of Lamb and Trotter.
Neutrality became impossible. The baker supplied both houses and was
distrusted by both. The curate attempted conciliation and lost two
dinners. Mr. Crumb adhered to the Trotters, partly from patriotism
and partly because Mrs. Lamb had transferred her custom to
a provision merchant who called himself an importer. Mr. Skryme
inclined first to one camp and then to the other,
according to where illness promised the most intelligence. Dick Wagstaff
became commander of the Trotter festivities, with the title of
Master of Arrangements, which he caused to be printed upon
cards.

The rival families carried competition into every department of life.
If the Lambs appeared in straw bonnets trimmed with roses,
the Trotters produced silk bonnets covered with fruit. If Clarissa
Lamb learned an air from an Italian opera, Georgiana Trotter
learned the overture, with additional thunder. If the Trotters hired
a carriage for Sunday, the Lambs hired one with a
crest upon the panel. The crest was found to belong
to a veterinary surgeon, but this was known only to
the opposition and therefore universally repeated.

Their parties grew more magnificent and less merry. The old
songs disappeared because they were vulgar; forfeits were abandoned because
they disarranged the hair; blindman's buff was pronounced dangerous to
furniture; and supper, once the heart of entertainment, became a
display of structures in sugar which no one dared disturb.
Guests stood in corners, feared to laugh, and spoke of
persons not present. Young men arrived late, looked bored, and
departed early. Young ladies compared dresses with the concentration of
accountants. Mothers conducted alliances behind fans.

Even our public customs suffered. The May Day dance was
discontinued because the Misses Lamb objected to sweeps. The Hampstead
excursion split into two parties, which chose opposite sides of
the same hill and spent the afternoon watching one another
through telescopes. At Christmas the Lamb faction decorated with white
ribbons, whereupon the Trotters adopted blue; and a dispute arose
concerning the loyalty of each color. The Beef and Liberty
Club lost half its members, changed its meeting night, failed
to order supper, and finally expired over a resolution declaring
its undiminished vitality.

Gossip, once general and sociable, became strategic. Every servant was
an emissary; every milliner a courier; every chimney a watchtower.
A cough at the Lambs' was reported to the Trotters
before the patient had finished coughing. A parcel entering the
Trotter house was measured by three observers and its contents
inferred from the string. Engagements were announced, denied, revived, and
buried within a week. Fortunes rose and fell by thousands
at the tea table. Reputations were dismantled over muffins.

In all this agitation I occupied a position of peculiar
danger.

Being the only resident who neither sold, prescribed, upholstered, baked,
preached, nor contracted for anything, I was considered a gentleman.
My idleness, which elsewhere had passed for deficiency, became in
Little Britain a certificate of rank. Both factions believed me
acquainted with high life because I sometimes walked westward and
had once mentioned the opera. Mrs. Lamb asserted that my
family was "extremely good," though she knew nothing of it;
Mrs. Trotter replied that birth was no substitute for independence,
by which she implied that I preferred her house.

I was invited everywhere.

At the Lambs' I drank weak tea from painted cups
and heard that the Trotters were coarse, pushing, overdressed, underbred,
vulgar in prosperity, and descended from a man who measured
soldiers' feet. At the Trotters' I ate strong cake beneath
the contractor's portrait and learned that the Lambs were mean,
affected, ungrateful, ashamed of honest trade, and descended from a
man who measured sheep.

My fault—if an idle man may confess a fault without
appearing industrious—was a desire to be agreeable. When Mrs. Lamb
observed that Amelia Trotter's turban made her look like a
loaded hay cart, I smiled. When Mrs. Trotter declared that
Arabella Lamb's neck resembled a pump handle in pearls, I
did not contradict her. I agreed, in a friendly way,
that the Trotter harp was too large for the room;
and I admitted, with equal friendship, that the Lamb pianoforte
was too loud for the street. I allowed that one
family gave itself airs and the other gave too much
supper. In short, I became a mirror in which each
party admired its own judgment.

For a time this diplomacy prospered. I carried no messages,
yet everyone supposed I did. A silence at the proper
moment was taken for confirmation; a raised eyebrow became evidence;
a cough over my teacup destroyed a reputation. I found
myself quoted upon subjects concerning which I had expressed nothing
more decisive than "Indeed!" and "Can it be so?" Mrs.
Lamb informed me confidentially that I had always seen through
the Trotters. Mrs. Trotter assured me that my opinion of
the Lambs was exactly her own.

The peril of my situation did not become fully apparent
until rumors of peace arose.

Mr. Lamb, weary of tight stocks and solitary dinners, met
Mrs. Trotter one morning at the poulterer's. Both reached for
the same turkey. Mr. Lamb surrendered it with his old-fashioned
bow. Mrs. Trotter, who respected a substantial man, thanked him.
That afternoon Mrs. Lamb sent over a receipt for preserving
apricots, and the next morning Mrs. Trotter returned a jar
of the preserve. Arabella and Amelia exchanged bows at church.
Clarissa praised Georgiana's execution upon the pianoforte. Georgiana replied that
Clarissa's touch was elegant.

These were dreadful prognostics.

I suddenly pictured a reconciliation: the two families united over
tea; confidences exchanged; my judgments compared; my smiles catalogued; my
eyebrows interpreted. I saw Mrs. Lamb repeating what I had
said of the Trotter turban, Mrs. Trotter reproducing my remarks
upon the Lamb carriage, the daughters supplying variations, the servants
adding codicils. I should be exposed not merely as a
double-dealer, but as a man who had failed to invent
sufficiently different slanders for opposing households.

There was no time to lose.

The following morning I informed my landlady that considerations of
health required me to seek a more airy quarter. This
was not wholly false, for the atmosphere of Little Britain
had become charged with disclosures. I packed my books, my
slippers, three cracked tea cups, and the portrait of an
aunt who had never visited me. At dusk a cart
removed my possessions. I departed on foot, avoiding both the
Lamb and Trotter doors, and did not breathe freely until
Aldersgate Street rolled between me and the scene of danger.

I have since wandered through many parts of London in
search of another secluded stronghold of ancient English manners: some
quiet nest where neighbors still quarrel in friendship, where punch
is respected, where a man may sing without musical education,
and where no daughter of a prosperous tradesman has yet
danced opposite the nephew of a sheriff. I have heard
of such places in Clerkenwell, Wapping, and the remoter districts
beyond the Borough; but the metropolis grows like a fungus,
and fashion sends out roots into the narrowest courts.

Sometimes, when I pass near my former lodging, I pause
at the entrance of Little Britain. The old roofs remain;
the griffin still leans from beneath the window; St. Paul's
watches above the chimneys; and Mr. Crumb, or someone very
like him, sits among the cheeses. But the sound of
a pianoforte comes from an upper room, a young lady
steps into a hired carriage, and a card at the
door announces an evening reception.

Then I think of the vanished suppers, the hot cockles,
the May garlands, the songs of Dick Wagstaff, and honest
Lamb smiling over his punch-bowl before gentility put him in
confinement. I sigh, though not too deeply; for nostalgia, like
plum-pudding, is pleasant only when taken in moderation.

As for the reconciliation, I afterward learned that it never
occurred. The apricot preserve fermented, Mrs. Trotter attributed the accident
to design, Mrs. Lamb demanded the return of her jar,
and hostilities recommenced with greater splendor than before.

Thus was I saved; but I have never gone back.

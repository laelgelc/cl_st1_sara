# THE LAST HAND

There were five men in the after-cabin of the *Peregrine*,
and upon the table between them lay a revolver, a
pack of cards, and a little black Testament.

Outside, the tropical night pressed against the schooner like a
hot and monstrous hand. The sea was without a wrinkle.
Not a breath stirred the lamp suspended above the table,
yet its flame seemed to shrink from the faces beneath
it.

Barron, who commanded the expedition by that authority which strong
men exercise before weaker men have consented to it, rested
both fists upon the table.

"Gentlemen," he said, "we have discovered a thief."

No man answered.

Vale, the physician, contemplated his long yellow fingers. Captain Dane
looked straight before him with the petrified expression of a
wooden saint. Pike, whose laughter had enlivened many an evil
hour, did not laugh now.

The fifth man, Rossiter, leaned back in his chair.

He alone smiled.

It was not an agreeable smile. It had in it
defiance, fatigue, and a certain insolent appeal to fortune—as though
he had been arraigned many times before the invisible tribunal
of Chance and had always escaped through some technical informality.

"A thief," he repeated. "The word is picturesque, Barron. But
we were playing cards, not conducting a prayer meeting."

Barron's brows contracted.

"We were playing cards," he returned, "because cards furnish the
only sure test of character."

The proposition was delivered with such implacable solemnity that Pike's
lips twitched; but one glance from Barron froze the unborn
mirth upon them.

"A man may lie in speech," continued the leader. "He
may counterfeit courage in danger, generosity in success, and resignation
in defeat. But place cards in his hands, money before
him, and temptation at his elbow, and before midnight you
shall know the private architecture of his soul."

"An admirable sermon," said Rossiter. "Where is the collection plate?"

"Here."

Barron turned over the revolver.

Beneath it lay the ace of diamonds. A minute crescent
had been clipped from its upper corner.

Rossiter's smile vanished.

Dane drew from his pocket three more cards similarly mutilated
and laid them beside the ace.

"I saw you mark them," he said.

"You saw incorrectly."

"I saw you," repeated Dane.

Vale raised his head.

"And when you left the table," he said, "I examined
the pack. The high diamonds were marked. During the game
you alone profited by that suit."

Rossiter's eyes passed from one face to another. He saw
no pity there, yet pity would have wounded him less
than the grave disgust confronting him.

At last he shrugged.

"Very well. Suppose I marked them?"

Barron straightened himself.

"There is no 'suppose.' You did."

"Then what follows? Shall I repay the stakes? Shall I
apologize? Or will our immaculate brotherhood demand a pint of
blood?"

A calculated silence ensued. It was the silence of judges
who know the sentence before the culprit has been summoned,
but who esteem the ceremony of justice too highly to
shorten it.

Barron touched the Testament.

"At Port Natal," he said, "before we sailed in search
of the San Jacinto gold, we five entered into a
compact. We knew the law could not accompany us where
we were going. We therefore made our own law. Each
man swore that if he deceived, robbed, or betrayed the
others, he would demand—not merely submit to, but demand—to be
set adrift in the dinghy with one oar."

Rossiter laughed softly.

"I remember."

"With one oar," said Pike, looking at him for the
first time, "because one oar gives hope enough to prolong
punishment, but not enough to make escape probable."

"I remember that also."

"You signed," said Barron.

"We all signed."

"And swore."

"We all swore."

Barron's voice dropped.

"Then, Rossiter, what do you demand?"

For an instant the culprit's lower lip trembled. Fear, swift
and shameless, flashed across his eyes. He glanced toward the
companionway, beyond which lay the vast inscrutable darkness; and the
coldness born of that darkness entered his flesh and congealed
the marrow in his bones.

The four men watched him.

He might have pleaded. He might have denounced the compact
as the drunken folly it had been. He might even
have seized the revolver. Every craven avenue stood open.

Then something—pride perhaps, or the last ember of a manhood
squandered in mean victories—stiffened his neck.

He rose.

"I demand," he said, "to be set adrift in the
dinghy with one oar."

No one moved.

"Do you hear me?" he cried, suddenly furious. "I claim
the penalty! It is mine by your law and by
my oath. One oar, gentlemen! Not two. I will have
no cheating."

An hour later the dinghy rocked under the schooner's stern.

They gave him neither food nor water, for the compact
had named none. Dane lowered the solitary oar. Vale, moved
at last by professional humanity, attempted to slip a flask
into the boat, but Rossiter struck it from his hand.

"No," he said. "The game must be played as agreed."

Barron stood above him, black against the stars.

"Rossiter," he called, "I would give much if this had
not happened."

"You should have let me deal again," answered the outcast.

The line was cast off.

For a little while the dinghy remained within the pale
radiance of the schooner's stern lamp. Then the *Peregrine* gathered
way, her canvas filled, and she passed into the night.
The lamp diminished to a spark, trembled upon the rim
of creation, and went out.

Rossiter sat alone with one oar across his knees.

---

On the fourth day the sea had become brass and
the sky a furnace lid.

Rossiter lay in the bottom of the boat, his coat
over his face. The coat gave no shade. Heat pierced
it, entered his skull, and stirred his thoughts into red
confusion. Hunger had ceased to be pain and become an
animal which gnawed his intestines to rags. Thirst was worse.
Thirst had converted his throat into a funnel of hot
brass, his tongue into a blackened rag, and each breath
into a visitation of flame.

He had rowed during the first day.

That was before he understood the sardonic perfection of one
oar. Pull upon the right, and the dinghy swung left;
shift the oar, and she revolved obediently back again. All
his strength produced circles. He traveled, labored, perspired, and arrived
forever at the place from which he had started.

On the second day he cursed Barron.

On the third he cursed God.

On the fourth he had begun to converse with a
shark.

The shark had appeared at sunrise—a grey fin slicing the
molten water twenty yards astern. It did not hurry. It
possessed the terrible leisure of an appetite certain of fulfillment.

Rossiter lifted the coat from his face and peered over
the gunwale.

"There you are," he whispered.

The fin turned.

"Don't pretend you weren't listening. I saw you. Eavesdropping is
dishonorable, my friend. Though perhaps sharks have no honor."

The fin disappeared. A pale shape glided beneath the boat,
and Rossiter recoiled so violently that the dinghy rolled.

"Steady! No liberties! We have not been introduced."

The shark emerged upon the other side. For one hideous
instant its eye showed above the water—small, cold, and judicial.

Rossiter pointed a blistered finger at it.

"You remind me of Barron. Same expression. Same habit of
circling an issue until the victim is sick."

His laugh cracked in his throat and ended in a
groan.

He had once been handsome. Women had told him so,
creditors had admitted it, and mirrors had confirmed the judgment.
Now his cheeks had fallen inward, his lips were split,
and a ragged beard sharpened the desperation of his face.
His eyes alone retained vitality, but it was the unnatural
vitality of lamps burning in a derelict house.

"Do you eat liars?" he asked. "Or only honest men?
Hypo—hypothetical question. There are no honest men."

The shark's fin cruised past again.

"No, no; I amend that. There may be honest men.
I had intended to become one myself. Frequently. Every New
Year's Day, in fact."

He settled against the thwart and stared into the quivering
emptiness.

Memory came to him not in ordered scenes, but in
accusing fragments: his mother waiting beside a window while rain
crawled down the panes; a girl named Marion removing his
ring and placing it gently upon a hotel table; an
old clerk whose savings Rossiter had borrowed upon a promise
as worthless as smoke; Barron's clipped ace of diamonds.

"I meant to repay him," he informed the shark. "That
makes a difference."

The shark showed no sign that it did.

"I meant to marry Marion, too. I meant to stop
drinking. I meant never to play with fools—except that fools
had money and sensible men had revolvers. A fellow must
make distinctions."

His head drooped.

"Meaning to be decent is nearly the same as being
decent, isn't it?"

The sea answered by slapping faintly against the boat.

Rossiter's eyes filled. The tears, being moisture, seemed a prodigality,
and he tried absurdly to catch them upon his tongue.

"Water," he muttered. "Seven oceans of it—and not a drop
fit for a gentleman. That is humor. Do you understand
humor, shark? Of course you smile. You've been smiling since
birth. But a smile without lips is suspicious. Like a
promise without money."

The grey pursuer came nearer.

Rossiter snatched up the oar.

"Back!"

He struck the water. The blade glanced from the shark's
snout, and the sudden exertion hurled him to his knees.
Black wheels revolved before his vision.

The shark retreated three yards.

"Coward!" Rossiter gasped. "Come aboard and fight standing!"

Then he looked down at his own legs and began
to chuckle.

"Standing! Good. Very good. You have no legs, and presently
I may have none. Then we shall be equals. That
is democracy."

For an hour he lay insensible.

When consciousness returned, evening was pouring blood along the western
sky. The shark remained.

Its patience horrified him more than its hunger. Hunger was
an impulse; patience was a character. The brute would wait
through sunset, darkness, and dawn. It would wait until his
hand could no longer lift the oar; until he rolled
over the side by accident, or until madness persuaded him
the sea was cool and merciful.

"You know I'm going to die," said Rossiter.

The fin inclined toward him.

"So do I. That gives us common ground."

He sat up with painful dignity.

"But observe the distinction. You wish me to die like
meat. I wish to die like a man."

The last word stirred something deep within him.

A man.

Had he ever been one? The compact had spoken much
of manhood. Barron had pronounced "honor" as priests pronounce the
name of God. Rossiter had mocked them because mockery was
the shield behind which he hid from every noble demand.

Yet he had accepted the boat.

That had been one honest act.

Was one act enough? Could a single clean coin redeem
a lifetime of counterfeit? No. His fevered intelligence seized upon
that answer with fierce relief. One did not become honorable
merely by suffering the unavoidable consequence of discovery. A trapped
thief was not a martyr. A condemned coward might walk
erect because his knees had forgotten how to bend.

He must choose honor where dishonor remained possible.

The shark's dorsal fin traced a half-circle.

Rossiter watched it, and a strange light entered his eyes.

"You want me," he said. "And I, on the other
hand, am not without interest in you. Shark's flesh is
edible. Tough, perhaps. You'd be tougher than Barron's sermons, but
there is nourishment in you."

He gripped the oar and examined its splintered edge.

"With luck I might kill you. A blow through the
eye. Then I eat you. Without luck, you eat me."

The shark vanished beneath the dinghy. Its back scraped the
keel with a sound like sandpaper drawn across a coffin.

Rossiter shuddered.

"No. Not luck," he said. "Luck can be assisted. I
know that better than most."

He searched his pockets. They contained a dead match, two
sovereigns, a silver pencil, and a visiting card.

He read the card aloud:

"Mr. Stephen Rossiter."

Beneath the name, in smaller type, stood the address of
a London club from which he had been expelled.

"Credentials," he told the shark. "You have none, of course.
But I shall waive the social difficulty."

With the silver pencil he blackened one half of the
card. Then he folded it lengthwise, hiding both surfaces, and
pinched the crease tightly.

"Listen carefully. We will play a final hand. If the
black side lies uppermost when the card opens upon the
water, I win. I shall strike until I kill you—or
until I die attempting it. If the white side shows,
you win, and I come overboard without resistance."

His voice faltered.

The shark appeared ten feet away.

"You object? Naturally. You are accustomed to games in which
every card is marked in your favor."

The monstrous fish turned, exposing for an instant the white
curve of its belly.

"Ah! You prefer white. Very well. White for the shark,
black for the man."

Rossiter examined the fold.

"No thumb upon the corner. No clipped edge. No second
card concealed in the sleeve. Fair—do you hear? Fair!"

His wasted shoulders squared. In that ruined body there arose
at last the austere figure of the man he might
have been.

"The loser pays," he said.

He tossed the card high.

It fluttered against the crimson sky, turning over and over
with maddening deliberation. Rossiter's eyes followed it. The shark's fin
cut straight toward the place where it must fall.

The card touched the sea.

For a second it remained folded like a tiny tent.
Then water loosened the crease. The halves opened.

White showed to heaven.

Rossiter stared.

The shark circled once.

"You win," said the doomed man.

Far across the water came a sound.

He did not heed it.

"You win fairly. That is the unpleasant portion. Had you
cheated, I could have forgiven myself anything."

The sound came again—a deep, prolonged bellow.

Rossiter lifted his head.

Upon the eastern horizon smoke towered above a black hull.
A steamer was bearing down upon him.

---

The officers of the *Caledonia* had sighted the dinghy at
sunset.

Within ten minutes the steamer had altered course. Within twenty
she lay a hundred yards away, her engines reversed, her
decks crowded with passengers.

A boat dropped from the davits.

Rossiter stood unsteadily as it approached. The rescued man should
have shouted, wept, or fallen upon his knees. Instead he
looked from the advancing boat to the white card floating
beside the dinghy.

The shark moved between them.

"Hold on!" cried the officer in the rescue boat. "We
are coming!"

Rossiter made no reply.

Four sailors pulled with desperate force. The officer stood in
the bows.

"Keep your hands inside! There's a shark under you!"

"I know," said Rossiter.

The officer stared at that scorched and spectral figure.

"We have water!"

At the word, every tortured nerve in Rossiter's body screamed.
He saw the breaker beneath the stern seat. He imagined
the tin cup brimming, the water cold against his teeth,
flowing down the funnel of hot brass, restoring life with
each miraculous swallow.

The boat came nearer.

"Water," Rossiter repeated.

"Yes, man! Water, food, and a berth aboard. Hold fast
another minute."

Rossiter bent toward them. His whole body followed the movement.
Succor was twenty yards distant—now fifteen—now ten.

Then the white card struck the side of the dinghy.

He looked down.

The officer extended his arm.

"Jump when we come alongside!"

Rossiter drew himself erect.

"I cannot."

"What?"

"I have lost."

The sailors ceased rowing in sheer astonishment, and the boat
drifted.

"Lost what?"

"A fair wager."

The officer's face hardened.

"He is delirious. Pull!"

The oars bit water.

Rossiter lifted the solitary oar as a weapon.

"Keep away!"

"For God's sake, man!"

"I owe a debt."

"To whom?"

Rossiter glanced at the grey fin.

"To the winner."

The officer saw the card, the shark, and the exaltation
blazing in the castaway's eyes. Understanding came, not as reason,
but as horror.

"Seize him!" he shouted.

The rescue boat struck the dinghy. Two sailors sprang forward.
Rossiter swung the oar, forcing them back. The effort nearly
destroyed him, yet he remained upright, rigid as a soldier
before the final volley.

"You do not understand," he said. "I cheated once. I
shall not cheat again."

"This is madness!"

"Possibly. But it is honest madness."

A sailor caught the gunwale. Another reached for Rossiter's coat.

The outcast stepped backward.

For one instant he stood poised above the sea, his
face transfigured by a terrible and useless triumph. In that
instant he was no longer the card-sharper, the false friend,
the squanderer of promises. He was a man paying freely
what no earthly court could compel him to pay.

"The loser pays," he said.

Then he leaped.

The sea closed over him. There was a violent swirl,
a flash of white, and the water darkened.

The rescuers dragged wildly at the crimson foam, but recovered
nothing except the folded visiting card. Upon its clean side,
blurred by the sea yet still legible, remained the name:

**Mr. Stephen Rossiter.**

The shark had taken the man.

Honor, arriving late, had taken all the rest.

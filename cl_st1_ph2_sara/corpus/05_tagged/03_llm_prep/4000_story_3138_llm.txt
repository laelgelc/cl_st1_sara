### The Lilies at Sainte-Marie

That was the winter when the smallpox crossed the Missouri
on a sledge of buffalo hide, so the old voyageurs
said, and took possession of Sainte-Marie before any man had
sense enough to bar the door.

It came out of the north with the first great
snow. Or it came up from St. Louis in a
bale of blankets. Or it had lain sleeping since autumn
in the painted box of a trader who died without
confessing where he had bought it. Every survivor had his
own account, and each account was delivered with an oath.
But disease, like scandal, requires no honest vehicle. It travels
faster than a horse, enters cabins which wolves cannot enter,
and laughs at locks, prayers, charms, whiskey, and the boastful
prudence of men.

By Christmas half the settlement was marked.

By Candlemas the living had ceased to count the dead.

Sainte-Marie was not much of a town, mind you. It
was a congregation of cabins flung down between the frozen
river and the blue-black bluffs, with a trading post, a
smithy, three warehouses, two drinking rooms, a chapel without a
bell, and more dogs than Christians. In spring it dissolved
into mud; in summer it baked beneath a white sky;
in autumn it smelled of hides, smoke, apples gone rotten
in barrels, and the first sharp promise of snow. Yet
men called it civilization because there was a billiard table
at Fort Bernard, though no two cues were straight, and
because Madame Laroque had once received a fashion journal only
eleven months out of date.

Civilization is a modest plant on the frontier. It will
root in a cracked teacup.

The winter of the pestilence stripped even these refinements away.
Snow closed the trails. The river became a plate of
iron. Smoke hung low over the cabins and crawled along
the ground, as if it too had fallen sick. Within
doors there were foul blankets, bitter medicines, frozen water buckets,
moaning children, and men whose courage had been sufficient for
grizzlies, Blackfoot raiders, broken rafts, and jealous husbands, but who
trembled when a red speck appeared on the wrist.

Father de Smet did not tremble.

He was not the famous Jesuit of that name, though
travelers sometimes confused the two and thereby gave him credit
for journeys he had never made. Our Father de Smet
had made enough of his own. He had crossed the
plains behind oxen; slept under an overturned canoe; eaten mule,
crow, roots, raw flour, and once—under protest—a moccasin boiled with
wild onions. He had been thrown through river ice, carried
a wounded hunter thirty miles, and preached a sermon while
a drunken trapper shot candle stubs from the chapel wall.
He was sixty, broad through the shoulders, short through the
legs, and blessed with a stomach whose temporal demands were
more punctual than any church bell.

He could fast magnificently between meals.

This weakness, if weakness it was, endeared him to Sainte-Marie.
A saint who eats nothing rebukes the common man; a
saint who takes three helpings of stew offers hope.

The priest went from cabin to cabin with his oils,
his crucifix, his little leather case of medicines, and a
flask which contained brandy for the fainting and, on unusually
bitter nights, for the minister of the fainting. He washed
the sick when no relative dared approach them. He chopped
wood. He burned bedding. He prayed over Catholics, Protestants, unbelievers,
and one Delaware gambler who professed faith in the ace
of spades but requested baptism when the cards no longer
answered him.

Where Father de Smet went, death was generally waiting.

Often Ninon was there before him.

Ninon! No surname is needed. There are women whose names
require genealogy, titles, and the endorsement of respectable persons; there
are others who carry a single name as a queen
carries a crown. Ninon belonged to the latter order.

She had come to Sainte-Marie three years before in a
red-lined sleigh belonging to Colonel Morrow, who was married in
Kentucky and forgetful beyond the Mississippi. When the colonel went
south, Ninon remained. She rented a cabin at the edge
of the cottonwoods, hung yellow curtains in its windows, laid
a bearskin before the hearth, and transformed one room into
a little empire of silk cushions, brass lamps, perfume bottles,
painted fans, scarlet ribbons, and mysteries.

Where had these things come from?

From Montreal, she said.

From New Orleans, said others.

From grateful gentlemen, said the malicious.

All three statements may have been true.

Ninon had been born near Tours to a mother who
embroidered altar linen and a father whom she described variously
as a cavalry captain, an actor, a count, and a
man best forgotten. She had lived in Paris, Bordeaux, Quebec,
St. Louis, and certain lesser places whose dullness she never
forgave. She had traveled by packet boat, diligence, steamer, wagon,
horseback, and once for six days in a canoe paddled
by two enamored brothers who quarreled so constantly that she
was obliged to steer.

She was no longer young. This fact was known to
all the women and suspected by none of the men.

Her hair was black; her eyes were gray-green; her hands,
though small, could lift a kettle that made a clerk
grunt. She used rouge with judgment, powder without fear, and
scent in a country where most persons smelled of wood
smoke, bear's grease, damp wool, or honest neglect. Her gowns
had known better floors than those of Sainte-Marie. Her slippers
had crossed oceans. She owned six silver spoons and never
displayed more than four.

Her reputation was doubtful.

Her soups were not.

Ninon could take dried beans, a strip of salt pork,
two onions with frozen hearts, and an elderly prairie hen,
and from these discouraging materials produce a broth which caused
hard men to close their eyes. She made cakes on
a griddle and served them with preserved cherries. She understood
coffee. Consequently her cabin received many visitors after dark, some
of whom may indeed have come for the cakes. Charity
compels us to admit the possibility.

Father de Smet had worried about her from the beginning.

He worried about everybody; it was his profession. But Ninon
presented difficulties not found in ordinary sinners. Ordinary sinners admitted
the charge and disputed only the degree. Ninon disputed the
court.

"My daughter," he had said on the first occasion when
he came upon her arranging white roses—paper ones, but artfully
made—before a gilt mirror, "the life of the soul is
not a masquerade."

"Then Heaven must be very badly dressed," she answered, pinning
a rose above her ear.

"You mistake me."

"Never, mon père. I understand you too quickly. That is
our misfortune."

He returned again. Priests are persistent men; otherwise the world
would have outlasted them centuries ago.

Sometimes she listened with lowered eyes, which encouraged him until
he perceived that she was mending a silk stocking. Sometimes
she argued theology with information gathered, no doubt, from abbés
less durable than Father de Smet. Sometimes she fed him.

This was unfair.

A steaming ragout placed before a hungry missionary will blunt
the finest edge of admonition. He would begin with Saint
Mary Magdalene, proceed through repentance, accept another dumpling, and end
by praising the seasoning.

Yet he did not abandon hope. Beneath Ninon's laughter he
detected sorrow; beneath her artifices, courage; beneath the life she
displayed, another life guarded and silent. If you are a
man like Father de Smet, you may spend twenty years
searching for one clean coin beneath a heap of counterfeit
and rejoice more over finding it than a practical man
would rejoice over the whole treasury.

When the sickness came, the coin showed.

Ninon knew something of nursing. She had learned in Paris
during the cholera, in Quebec during fever season, and in
the less reputable quarters of New Orleans, where illness is
often treated late because pride, poverty, or the police stand
in the doorway. She did not fear ugliness. This is
rarer than not fearing death.

On the third day after Christmas she entered the Bouchard
cabin, where the mother and two children lay sick and
Bouchard himself sat outside in the snow, weeping into his
beard.

"Go in," Father de Smet commanded him.

"I cannot."

"Then fetch wood."

"I cannot."

Ninon stepped from the cabin carrying a basin of black
water.

"You can," she said. "You are merely choosing not to.
There is a difference, monsieur, and God can see it
even through your whiskers."

Bouchard fetched wood.

Thereafter she and the priest worked together, though neither proposed
the arrangement. They met beside beds, over kettles, in sheds
where infected blankets were burned, and on paths drifted waist-deep
with snow. He brought the sacraments; she brought broth. He
spoke of eternity; she changed the linen. He commended souls
to God; she bullied bodies into clinging to life.

They saved some.

Not many.

There was old Leclerc, the trapper, who had once wrestled
a wounded elk and thereafter believed himself proof against all
mortal injury. Ninon shaved his matted beard, spooned water between
his cracked lips, and sang a song about the vineyards
of Anjou. He died before the second verse ended.

There was Mary Cole, fifteen years old, betrothed to a
teamster who had gone east in October. Her mother would
not enter the room. Father de Smet held the girl's
hand while Ninon combed her hair and tied it with
a blue ribbon.

"Will he know me?" Mary whispered.

"Who, child?" asked the priest.

"Tom. My face—"

"He will know you," Ninon said.

Her voice admitted no theology and no contradiction.

There was Antoine Rivet, who had mocked the chapel, cheated
Indians over weights, watered his whiskey, and beaten his patient
wife. When the pustules blinded him he cried that devils
stood around the bed.

"Then they have poor taste," Ninon remarked, wringing out a
cloth. "Be quiet and let Father speak."

Rivet confessed with such energy that he accused himself of
crimes unknown to his wife, the priest, and perhaps even
to Heaven. He recovered. Providence is mysterious.

Night after night the two passed beneath the leaden sky:
the squat priest with his lantern and medicine case; the
tall woman wrapped in Colonel Morrow's fur coat, her black
hair hidden by a scarlet hood. The snow squealed under
their boots. Wolves called from the bluffs. The river cracked
like distant cannon.

Sainte-Marie watched them and revised its judgments.

The respectable women, who had crossed the street rather than
greet Ninon in autumn, accepted her broth in January. The
men who had praised her beauty discovered her endurance. Children
ceased to giggle at her perfume. Even Madame Laroque admitted
that Ninon possessed "a certain heart," though she said it
as if accusing her of theft.

Father de Smet revised nothing aloud. But his admonitions grew
less frequent.

One night, after they had spent six hours with a
family of emigrants stranded in a warehouse, he found Ninon
outside, leaning against the wall. Frost silvered her lashes. Her
face looked gray beneath its rouge.

"You are tired," he said.

"I am bored."

"You are nearly fainting."

"Only with boredom."

He gave her the flask.

She drank, coughed, and handed it back. "Terrible brandy."

"It was donated."

"Then the donor sought absolution cheaply."

He smiled despite himself. "Go home, Ninon."

"There are three sick men at the fort."

"I will see them."

"You cannot lift Baptiste alone."

"I have lifted heavier sinners."

"Yes, but none so fat."

She went with him.

At the fort Baptiste proved delirious and violent. He flung
a stool through a window, cursed his absent mother, and
attempted to bite the priest. Ninon caught his wrists while
Father de Smet forced him back upon the bunk.

"You see?" she panted. "He prefers me."

"His discernment is affected."

They tied Baptiste down with rawhide thongs. Before dawn his
fever broke.

Such nights breed a kind of friendship which daylight conventions
cannot explain. The priest knew what the settlement said of
Ninon, and much of it was true. Ninon knew what
the Church said of her, and some of that was
true also. Yet truth is not always arranged in convenient
ranks. A man may recite every creed and leave his
child without water; a woman may have broken six commandments
and keep the seventh more faithfully than her judges. Heaven
may sort these matters neatly. Sainte-Marie could not.

March arrived without spring.

The snow softened at noon and froze harder at night.
Roofs dripped; icicles lengthened; mud appeared in dark wounds along
the road. Crows returned and hopped among the ashes where
infected bedding had been burned. The settlement, having endured death,
began cautiously to consider life.

Easter would come late that year.

The chapel of Sainte-Marie stood apart from the cabins upon
a little rise. It had been built of cottonwood logs
by voyageurs who promised God a church if He brought
their raft through the rapids. God did so, and they
fulfilled their promise with that economy common to successful petitioners.
The roof leaked. The door sagged. Mice had eaten the
altar cloth, and a winter of neglect had covered the
benches with dust, soot, and the feathers of a hen
that entered in December and became a Protestant.

Father de Smet announced that the chapel must be cleaned
for Holy Week.

No one responded.

He announced it again after Mass.

Madame Laroque had a pain in her back. Bouchard was
repairing harness. The fort men were occupied with an enterprise
involving cards. Others had not yet recovered, or feared infection,
or regarded cleanliness as a foreign custom.

Ninon came forward.

"I will help."

There was a silence. Respectability, though it will accept soup
from any hand during a plague, becomes particular again when
asked to share a broom.

Father de Smet hesitated only a moment. "Thank you, my
daughter."

"Do not thank me yet. I intend to rearrange everything."

On Monday she appeared at the chapel with two brooms,
a pail, soap, rags, a hammer, three children, and Bouchard,
whom she had conscripted by methods never disclosed. Father de
Smet opened the windows. A cold wind rushed through, setting
the candle ends trembling.

They swept out dust enough to bury doctrine. They scrubbed
benches, patched the plaster, straightened the stations of the cross,
and evicted the hen, which protested more intelligently than some
parishioners. Ninon climbed a ladder to wipe soot from the
rafters. Her skirt was pinned above stout boots; her hair
escaped its kerchief; a streak of dirt crossed one cheek.

"Take care," Father de Smet called.

"I have crossed the Atlantic in November."

"The Atlantic did not have a rotten ladder."

"How little you know of ships."

By afternoon she had transformed the sacristy and insulted the
condition of every sacred vessel. The brass candlesticks shone. The
floor smelled of lye. Pale sunlight reached corners which had
lived in sin for years.

Only the statue of the Virgin resisted improvement.

It was a wooden figure brought upriver from St. Louis,
stiff in posture and afflicted with a complexion the color
of cold porridge. Its blue robe had faded to gray,
one hand was chipped, and the gilt heart upon its
breast had turned brown.

Ninon stood before it, hands on hips.

"She looks ill."

"She has endured much," said Father de Smet.

"She has endured bad paint."

The next morning Ninon brought a small box from her
cabin. In it were cakes of pigment, camel-hair brushes, gold
leaf, a pot of white enamel, and other instruments of
civilization. She painted the robe a rich blue, restored the
gold heart, darkened the brows, and placed the faintest rose
upon the Virgin's cheeks.

The faintest, mind you, by Ninon's standards.

When she had finished, Sainte-Marie's Madonna resembled a pious Parisian
beauty of the reign of Louis-Philippe. Her lips curved mysteriously.
Her cheeks glowed. She appeared capable of granting mercy, receiving
homage, and ordering a carriage.

Father de Smet stared.

"Well?" Ninon demanded.

"She is—radiant."

"You mean she is too pretty."

"No."

"You mean it, but you are charitable."

"The Mother of God possessed beauty."

"Certainly. Otherwise painters would have given up long ago."

From the box Ninon lifted a length of lace, yellowed
with age and fine as frost upon a window.

"This was my mother's."

The mockery had gone from her voice.

"She made it?"

"For an altar at Tours. She worked two years. The
curé said it was too delicate for ordinary feast days,
so she kept it until a worthy occasion. No occasion
was ever worthy enough." Ninon ran the lace through her
fingers. "After she died, I took it."

Father de Smet touched the edge. Vines, wheat, and tiny
crosses had been worked into the pattern. "It is beautiful."

"She believed patience was a virtue. Imagine! Two years with
a needle to prove it."

"You kept it all this time."

"I am not patient, but I am careful."

She arranged the lace about the statue's shoulders. The ancient
handiwork softened the newly painted splendor; the Virgin ceased to
be fashionable and became, for a moment, a mother wrapped
in something made by another mother's hands.

Father de Smet looked at Ninon.

She turned away too quickly.

On Wednesday she brought the lilies.

There were two of them, planted together in a blue
porcelain bowl: pale green stalks, narrow leaves, and closed white
buds heavy with promise. No flowers had bloomed in Sainte-Marie
for five months. The sight of living green caused the
children to crowd around as if it were a miracle—which
perhaps it was, miracles being partly a matter of climate.

"Where did you get these?" asked Father de Smet.

"I grew them."

"In winter?"

"In my cabin."

"How?"

"With warmth, water, and conversation. Flowers respond to intelligent company."

The bowl was valuable. Even Father de Smet saw that.
It had little golden birds painted around the rim and
a crack repaired by silver staples.

"For the altar," Ninon said.

"Ninon—"

"No sermon. If you ask who gave me the bowl,
I shall lie. If you ask where I obtained the
bulbs, I shall tell the truth and disappoint you. Let
us preserve mystery."

He accepted them.

The bulbs had come, in fact, from a Dutch gardener
in St. Louis who loved Ninon for five weeks and
forgave her for four. She had carried them north wrapped
in damp moss, not because she intended any holy use,
but because they reminded her of an Easter morning in
Tours: bells, rain-washed stones, her mother's black dress, and white
lilies nodding beside an altar while incense rose into colored
light. A memory may sleep longer than disease. It may
also awaken more gently.

They placed the bowl before the Virgin.

"The buds will open by Sunday," Ninon said.

"You are certain?"

"I have commanded them."

Then she shivered.

It was not an ordinary shiver. It began in her
shoulders and passed through her with a rigid violence that
made the porcelain bowl rattle against the wood.

Father de Smet caught her arm.

"You have a chill."

"The chapel is cold."

"Your hands are burning."

"My hands are always warm."

He looked closely. Beneath the streak of rouge, her skin
had the waxen pallor he had seen too often that
winter.

"Go home."

"There is work."

"Bouchard can finish it."

"Bouchard cannot distinguish a cloth from a fishing net."

"Go home, Ninon."

Perhaps it was the voice he used, or perhaps she
knew already. She pulled free, smiled, and made him a
little curtsy.

"As you command, mon père. But do not forget the
lilies."

"I shall not forget them."

"Promise."

"I promise."

She walked down the chapel path without assistance. She did
not stagger until she reached the cottonwoods, where no one
was supposed to see.

But a child saw. Children always see what adults arrange
not to notice.

By evening the red marks had appeared.

No one went to Ninon's cabin. That is not quite
true. Madame Laroque sent broth, though it was poor broth
and delivered by her husband. Bouchard left wood outside. The
three children stood at the edge of the trees until
darkness, whispering. Courage is unevenly distributed; many who had allowed
Ninon to enter their sickrooms discovered urgent reasons to avoid
hers.

Father de Smet came at sunset.

The yellow curtains were drawn. Inside, the stove burned too
fiercely. Perfume, smoke, vinegar, and sickness mingled in the air.
The brass lamps were lit; their flames shone upon silk
cushions, painted screens, strings of beads, fur robes, bottles, boxes,
and the absurd little luxuries of a woman who had
carried fragments of cities into the wilderness and made them
serve as walls against loneliness.

Ninon lay upon the bed in a white nightdress, her
hair loose across the pillow. She had removed the rouge.

Without it she seemed younger.

Or perhaps merely defenseless.

"You came," she said.

"Of course."

"I hoped you would send Bouchard."

"Why?"

"So that I might die from his broth and spare
the disease the trouble."

"You are not dying tonight."

"No? You have become a physician?"

"I have seen worse cases recover."

"Name three."

He busied himself with the basin.

She watched him. "You cannot."

He could not.

For two days he nursed her. He cooled her skin,
gave her water, darkened the room, changed the linens, prayed
the offices, and refused all assistance except that of a
half-grown Métis girl named Céleste, who had survived the disease
in January and feared it no longer. Ninon's fever rose.
The beautiful face swelled and vanished beneath the eruption. Her
eyes closed. Her hands plucked at the coverlet.

She spoke in French to people not present.

Her mother was there. So was a man named Étienne,
whom she cursed and forgave repeatedly. She called for a
carriage. She sang three lines of a comic opera. Once
she ordered champagne for twelve. Once she begged someone not
to sell the red horse.

Father de Smet heard these fragments and made no attempt
to assemble them into judgment. At the edge of death,
a life empties its pockets: a prayer, a coin, a
theatre ticket, a lie, a child's ribbon, an old injury.
Wise men do not value the soul by this litter.

On Good Friday she woke fully.

The room was dim. Snow had begun again, soft and
wet against the panes. Father de Smet sat beside the
stove, asleep with his chin on his chest and a
rosary wound around one thick hand.

"Mon père."

He woke at once.

"What hour?"

"Near midnight."

"Friday?"

"Yes."

"Then He is dead."

He knew whom she meant. "The Church commemorates His death."

"So precise." Her breath caught. "Priests organize even grief."

He brought water. She drank a little.

"The chapel?"

"Ready."

"The lace?"

"About the Virgin."

"And the lilies?"

"Still closed this morning."

"You did not give them enough water."

"I gave them exactly what you instructed."

"Then they are being stubborn. Like men."

"Ninon, will you make your confession?"

She turned her ruined face toward the wall.

There are moments when doctrine must stand firm; there are
others when it must kneel. Father de Smet waited.

At last she said, "What would satisfy you?"

"Truth."

"All of it? We shall be here until Pentecost."

"What weighs upon you?"

"My blankets are heavy."

"Ninon."

She was silent a long while. "I have been cruel
when kindness was cheaper. I have laughed at good people
because they bored me. I have kept money that was
not mine and given away money that was. I have
loved men I should not, and failed to love those
I should. I have lied to protect myself, and sometimes
for pleasure. I hated my mother for dying. I hated
God for allowing it. Is that enough truth for one
night?"

"It is a beginning."

"Always greedy, you priests."

He bent nearer to hear her.

Her voice became a whisper. Some things she said plainly;
some in fragments; some he asked her to repeat. Once
he closed his eyes. Once she laughed, and the laugh
became a cough. Outside, the snow slipped from the roof
with a soft rush.

When she had finished, he raised his hand.

The absolution was spoken quietly in Latin, in that old
language which had crossed seas and kingdoms to reach this
overheated cabin among the cottonwoods. It passed over the brass
lamps, the scent bottles, the fur coat, the painted fan,
the silver spoons, and the dying woman's disfigured face. Sacred
words are not injured by strange surroundings. Indeed, they were
born for them.

He anointed her.

She followed the prayers as long as she could. Then
her attention wandered.

"Do you think," she murmured, "my mother's lace is too
yellow?"

"No."

"She would say it was."

"It is beautiful."

"She was always washing things. Linen. Floors. My ears. Her
conscience." A pause. "She would not have liked me."

"Perhaps she knew you better than you think."

"No. Mothers know children. They do not always know women."

Her fingers searched the blanket until he took them.

"Will they open?" she asked.

"The lilies?"

"Yes."

"By Easter."

"You must not forget."

"I promised."

"Priests promise many things."

"This promise I shall keep."

She opened her eyes then. Through the swelling, through fever
and pain, something of the old Ninon looked out—amused, skeptical,
almost coquettish.

"Even if they came from a sinner?"

"Especially then."

"That is nearly gallant."

"It is theology."

"Same thing, sometimes."

Shortly after midnight her breathing changed.

Céleste, curled asleep near the hearth, rose and began to
weep. Father de Smet continued the prayers for a departing
soul. There was no final speech, no sudden restoration of
beauty, no revelation fit for a stage. Ninon pressed his
hand once, very hard. Then not at all.

She died while the wet snow turned to rain.

By dawn the river had begun to move beneath the
ice.

They buried her on Holy Saturday because the thaw was
coming and the ground, though stubborn, could at last be
opened. Few attended. Fear still governed many; shame governed others.
Bouchard came, and Madame Laroque, and the three children, and
the fort commander, and Baptiste, thin as a fence rail.
Men who had visited Ninon's yellow-curtained cabin after dark stood
far off among the trees in daylight.

Father de Smet said the words.

The grave received her without commentary.

That night rain washed Sainte-Marie. It beat soot from the
roofs, softened the drifts, filled the wheel ruts, and uncovered
the brown earth in ragged patches. The river groaned, cracked,
and at sunrise broke free, carrying plates of ice southward
in a glittering procession.

Easter morning came clear.

The chapel filled. Those who had neglected it admired its
cleanliness. Those who had doubted Ninon praised the lace. Madame
Laroque looked closely at the Virgin's rosy cheeks and pursed
her lips, but said nothing. Sunlight entered through the small
eastern window and struck the restored gold heart, so that
it shone above the altar like a captured flame.

Father de Smet began Mass.

He was tired. His face had aged during the night,
and his voice, at first, was rough. Yet as the
responses rose—uneven, frontier voices, some devout, some uncertain, some present
merely because winter had ended—his strength returned.

Then the children saw the flowers.

The two buds had opened.

They stood upon their green stalks, white and grave, each
with a heart of pale gold. No snow was whiter,
though snow is cold; no altar linen was purer, though
linen may be washed. Their fragrance passed gently through the
chapel, over mud-caked boots and patched coats, over sinners, cowards,
mourners, cheats, mothers, trappers, and men who had survived what
better men had not.

Father de Smet looked at them and stopped.

Only for a breath.

Then he continued the ancient words of resurrection while the
broken river flashed beyond the door and the two lilies,
commanded by Ninon, flowered in the wilderness.

## The Arithmetic of Gardens

The world was doing wonderfully.

War was obsolete. Hunger was an antique. Cancer could be
seen in museums, preserved in jars for schoolchildren who needed
frightening. Nobody caught colds. Nobody had bad teeth. Nobody got
old unless he wanted to, and almost everybody wanted to.

There were eleven billion, nine hundred million, four hundred and
six thousand, two hundred and twelve people on Earth.

That was enough.

Birth was still permitted, naturally. The family remained a treasured
institution. Parenthood was encouraged in pamphlets printed by the Department
of Continuity.

There was only one rule.

For every person who arrived, one person had to leave.

Edward Wehling sat in the Maternity Exchange of Chicago Central
Hospital and waited for three babies to be born.

He had expected one.

The room was warm and smelled of carnations and disinfectant.
Above the reception desk, a bronze sign said:

**LIFE IS A GIFT. PLEASE KEEP THE RECEIPT.**

Edward had a receipt for one life.

His maternal grandfather had volunteered to die that afternoon. The
old man was one hundred and seventy-two and had apologized
for not doing it sooner.

"Should have made room years ago," he had said. "You
young people need elbow space."

The grandfather's appointment at the Federal Bureau of Termination was
at four. Edward's wife had gone into labor at three-twelve.
At three-forty, a nurse had come out and told him
there were three heartbeats.

"Triplets!" she had cried. "Isn't that marvelous?"

"Marvelous," Edward had said.

"Three healthy little citizens."

"Marvelous."

"Of course, there's the vacancy question."

"Of course."

Now Edward stared at the floor. In his coat pocket
was the gun his grandfather had carried during the Last
Population Riot. The gun was older than most buildings and
less legal than measles.

Across the room, an elderly painter stood before a vast
wall, correcting a mural no one had asked him to
correct.

The mural was called *The Garden of Human Plenty*. It
showed smiling people in white robes planting trees, watering flowers,
and lifting babies toward a yellow sun. Other people, dressed
in purple, cut branches, pulled weeds, and fed dead plants
into clean silver furnaces.

Everybody in the mural looked grateful.

The painter was supposed to be adding a handsome doctor
to the center. Instead, he had painted a gray smudge
over the doctor's face.

A purple-uniformed hostess watched him.

Her name was Leora Voss. She worked at the hospital's
Termination Liaison Desk, helping volunteers arrange convenient, dignified departures. She
wore a little cap shaped like a dove. The dove
had an electronic badge in its beak.

"You've made him dirty," she said.

"Doctors get dirty," said the painter.

"Not in murals."

"That explains murals."

The painter's name was Julian Pike. He was two hundred
and nineteen years old. His hair was the color of
cigarette ash, and his hands shook only when he tried
to paint something cheerful.

Leora pointed at a heap of stained canvas near his
ladder. "What's that supposed to be?"

"My finest work."

"It's a drop cloth."

"Exactly. It has no message, no improvement program, no healthy
citizens. Things fell on it and stayed where they fell."

Leora frowned pleasantly. Hostesses were trained to frown pleasantly because
some visitors confused an uninterrupted smile with insanity.

"You shouldn't make fun of public art," she said.

"I make fun of private art, too. Equality is important."

Edward looked up at them.

Leora noticed him and brightened. "Are you waiting for someone?"

"Three someones."

"Oh!" she said. "Triplets?"

"So they tell me."

"How exciting."

"Perfectly keen."

She clasped her hands. "And have you arranged the necessary
vacancies?"

"One."

Her smile lost half a millimeter. "I see."

"Do you?"

"Well, yes. One permit would authorize one infant. The other
two would be maintained in provisional care for up to
six hours while you secured additional volunteers."

"And after six hours?"

"They would be peacefully withheld."

"Withheld from what?"

"From participation."

The painter climbed down from his ladder. "She means killed."

Leora glanced at him. "That term is emotionally obsolete."

"So are tears," said Julian. "People still leak."

Edward put both hands between his knees. "My grandfather is
leaving at four."

"That's lovely," said Leora. "Please thank him on behalf of
everybody."

"He says you're welcome."

"And perhaps other relatives—"

"No."

"Friends?"

"No."

"Sometimes a civic group will sponsor a vacancy in exceptional
circumstances."

"I called fourteen groups."

"What did they say?"

"They said triplets were poor planning."

Leora nodded sympathetically. "Multiple births can be administratively awkward."

A door opened, and Dr. Benjamin Hitz entered the waiting
room like a parade entering a small town.

He was the hospital's chief obstetrician and Deputy Regional Advocate
for Population Balance. His white coat had gold piping. He
had broad pink cheeks and a voice designed to reach
the back row without amplification.

"Mr. Wehling!" he boomed. "Congratulations, congratulations, and conditional congratulations!"

Edward rose.

Dr. Hitz seized his hand. "Three splendid babies. Two girls
and a boy. Excellent lungs. Excellent reflexes. Your wife is
comfortable and very proud."

"Can I see her?"

"In a moment. There is a small arithmetic matter first."

"One from three," said Edward.

Dr. Hitz laughed as though Edward had told a charming
joke at a banquet. "We prefer to say one now
and two pending."

"Pending death."

"Pending sponsorship."

The painter leaned on his ladder. "You ought to put
wheels on that vocabulary, Doctor. It carries everything away."

Dr. Hitz recognized him. "Mr. Pike! Our mischievous old master.
How is the garden?"

"Infested with symbols."

"Splendid."

Dr. Hitz turned back to Edward. "You are distressed, and
distress is understandable. But let us remember why the system
exists. Two centuries ago, people bred without accounting. They lived
miserably, crowded together, consuming everything in sight."

"Now they live forever," said Edward.

"Precisely! Medicine gave us victory over death."

"And then hired purple ladies to bring death back."

Leora cleared her throat. "We are called transition hostesses."

"Forgive him," Dr. Hitz said. "A father's emotions are powerful.
Parenthood is one of nature's strongest impulses."

"Second strongest," said Julian.

"And what is the strongest?" asked Leora.

"Not dying."

Dr. Hitz regarded the painter with professional patience. "The Balance
Law reconciles both impulses. Citizens may reproduce when vacancies occur.
Citizens may live indefinitely unless they volunteer. Freedom on both
sides."

Edward looked at him. "My children didn't volunteer."

"Your children are not yet registered citizens."

"My wife felt them kicking."

"A beautiful prenatal event."

"They had names."

"Names can be reassigned."

The room became quiet.

Behind the reception desk, a clock shaped like a sunflower
clicked from 3:56 to 3:57.

Dr. Hitz softened his voice. "Mr. Wehling, I have delivered
more than thirty thousand babies. I know what this moment
means. But civilization is a garden. We plant carefully. We
prune responsibly. We cannot allow every seed—"

"Finish that," said Edward.

"Pardon?"

"Say what happens to the extra seeds."

Dr. Hitz glanced at the mural. "Some do not enter
the garden."

The painter snorted. "There's a silver furnace in your garden."

Leora walked to Edward and offered him a lavender brochure.

Its cover showed an old couple strolling into a sunset.
The title was **SO YOU LOVE A BABY**.

"There are emergency options," she said. "The Bureau maintains a
volunteer registry. Most names are reserved months ahead, but someone
may cancel a cancellation."

Edward did not take the brochure.

Leora continued. "We also have the Generous Stranger Appeal. Your
case could be broadcast locally. People respond well to fathers."

"How long?"

"Review takes three days."

"I have six hours."

"Then the rapid review."

"How long is that?"

"Two days."

"Wonderful."

Dr. Hitz put an arm around Edward's shoulders. Edward removed
it.

"You must not think of this as cruelty," the doctor
said. "Cruelty was the old world—babies starving, old people rotting,
millions dying in epidemics. We ended all that."

"You ended dying by rationing birth."

"We ended accidental death. Voluntary departure is civilized."

The painter laughed once.

Dr. Hitz swung toward him. "You object, Mr. Pike, but
you are two hundred and nineteen. You have enjoyed four
ordinary lifetimes because others accepted responsibility."

"Others were gassed so I could criticize your mural longer."

"Nobody is gassed. The Service Division uses individualized methods."

"Perfume, pillows, soft music, and then dead."

Leora smiled. "Many guests choose the Blue Sleep Suite."

"Others choose the Quiet Booth," Dr. Hitz said. "There is
no pain."

"Pain is not the whole complaint," said Julian.

The sunflower clock changed to 3:59.

Edward took a small black communicator from his pocket. It
chimed.

A recorded voice said, "This is a courtesy confirmation from
the Federal Bureau of Termination. Citizen Otto Wehling is now
beginning his farewell interval. His generous vacancy will be certified
at sixteen hundred hours. The nation extends its warmest gratitude."

The clock clicked.

Four o'clock.

A green lamp above the maternity doors lit up. One
vacancy had entered the world.

Leora checked her tablet. "There. Congratulations. One of your children
is legally alive."

"Which one?"

"You choose."

Dr. Hitz smiled encouragingly. "Many parents use birth order."

"They were delivered by surgery."

"Weight, then."

"They weigh almost the same."

"Sex preference?"

Edward stared at him.

The doctor spread his hands. "The law respects family values."

A muffled cry came from beyond the doors. Then another.
Then a third.

The three cries overlapped, rose, and separated again.

Julian looked at the dirty drop cloth.

Leora touched Edward's sleeve. "Would you like a private consultation
room?"

"No."

"We have tea."

"No."

"Perhaps your wife should participate in the selection."

"She thinks all three are coming home."

Dr. Hitz's expression became grave. "That was unwise."

"She was busy."

"Still, candor is the basis of civic adjustment."

"She asked me to solve it."

"And you made every reasonable effort."

"Yes," said Edward. "Every reasonable one."

He reached into his coat.

The painter saw what came out and shut his eyes.

Leora stared at the gun with curiosity before fear arrived.
Guns appeared in historical films, usually in scenes about barbarism.

Dr. Hitz knew it at once.

"Now, Mr. Wehling," he said. His great voice became gentle
and paternal. "Let us not create vacancies impulsively."

Edward pointed the gun at him.

"Put that down," said Dr. Hitz. "You are experiencing temporary
moral confusion."

"I have three children."

"One child," said the doctor carefully, "and two biological surpluses."

Edward fired.

The shot knocked Dr. Hitz backward into *The Garden of
Human Plenty*. He slid down the painted trunk of a
flawless apple tree. A red streak crossed the white-robed gardeners
and reached the silver furnace.

Leora screamed.

Edward turned the gun toward her.

She held up the lavender brochure as though good printing
could stop a bullet.

"I only help people," she said.

"You do it cheerfully."

"We are required to be reassuring."

Edward fired.

Leora fell beside the reception desk. Her dove-shaped cap rolled
across the floor and stopped beneath the bronze sign about
gifts and receipts.

Edward looked at Julian.

The painter opened his eyes.

"Two vacancies," Edward said.

Julian nodded.

Edward placed the gun beneath his chin.

"Don't," said Julian.

"Why not?"

The old painter could think of several answers. None survived
inspection.

Edward pulled the trigger.

That was the third vacancy.

For a little while, the only sounds were the humming
lights, the sunflower clock, and three babies crying beyond the
maternity doors.

Security arrived in twelve seconds.

Hospital accountants arrived in nine minutes.

The babies were certified before sunset.

The boy was named Otto after the grandfather. The girls
were named Hope and Grace because their mother had not
yet been told the details.

The mural was declared structurally undamaged. Julian Pike was asked
to conceal the stains with additional foliage.

He stood alone in the waiting room after the bodies
had been removed.

On the wall, white figures planted. Purple figures cut. The
sun shone over everybody without discrimination, because paint had no
population problem.

Julian dipped his brush in green.

He painted one leaf.

Then he painted a second.

He stopped before the third.

Nature, he thought, had played a joke without a final
line. Every creature carried two absolute commands: make more life,
and do not surrender the life already made. These commands
worked splendidly on an empty planet.

The planet was no longer empty.

Nobody could obey both commands forever, but nobody could stop
wanting to.

Julian picked up Edward's gun. Security had overlooked it beneath
the drop cloth.

It was heavy and ugly and wonderfully direct.

He sat on the painter's platform and put the barrel
in his mouth.

He thought of the purple hostess. He thought of the
doctor's garden. He thought of all the volunteers walking politely
into scented rooms so babies they would never meet could
receive identification numbers.

He tried to pull the trigger.

His finger would not move.

"Coward," he told himself.

His finger remained a loyal servant to the strongest command.

Never die.

Never, never, never.

The gun fell onto the drop cloth, leaving an oily
mark. Julian studied the mark with admiration. It was honest.
It resembled nothing but itself.

He walked to the reception desk and used the communicator.

The directory offered choices.

"For Birth Permits, say 'begin.' For Vacancy Transfers, say 'exchange.'
For Bereavement Assistance, say 'comfort.' For the Federal Bureau of
Termination, say 'good-bye.'"

"Good-bye," said Julian.

"Please repeat."

"Good-bye."

Cheerful music played. A choir sang about spacious streets and
grateful generations.

A woman answered.

"Federal Bureau of Termination, Service Division. This is Miss Amber
Bell. How may I improve the world today?"

"I'd like an appointment."

"How generous! Is this for yourself?"

"Yes."

"Wonderful. Have you selected an experience package? We offer Ocean
Evening, Fireside Farewell, Patriotic Dawn, and Simple Silence."

"Simple Silence."

"A classic choice. Will you be transferring your vacancy to
a particular person?"

"No."

"Then it will enter the National Opportunity Pool. Somewhere, a
family will receive very happy news because of you."

Julian looked at the three small green lights above the
maternity doors.

"When?" he asked.

"We have an opening at ten tomorrow morning."

"Why is it open?"

There was a pause.

"A previous guest postponed."

Julian laughed.

"Is something amusing, sir?"

"Everything."

"I'll reserve ten o'clock. May I have your full name
and age?"

"Julian Pike. Two hundred and nineteen."

"My goodness! What a magnificent life."

"It has been long."

"We recommend arriving twenty minutes early. Identification is required. Clothing
is optional in Simple Silence, though purple robes are available
at no charge."

"All righty," said Julian.

"And, Mr. Pike?"

"Yes?"

"On behalf of the newborn citizens of Earth, thank you
for making room."

The line went dead.

Julian returned to the mural.

He covered the doctor's face with leaves. He covered the
hostess's painted counterpart with vines. Then he painted three tiny
flowers where no flowers had been planned.

When he finished, there was still too much garden and
not enough room.

There was always too much garden.

There was never enough room.

### I

At the season when my unpopularity had attained the noisiest
eminence possible to so humble a man, I withdrew from
Calcutta to my country house.

A pamphlet had discovered that I was an enemy of
the nation; a newspaper, not to be outdone in vigilance,
proved that I was an enemy of mankind; and several
private correspondents, employing a freedom of expression denied to print,
assured me that I was an enemy of God. I
had long suspected that literature was not among the harmless
occupations, but until then I had not known how many
worlds a single pen could imperil.

My friends advised me to reply. My enemies demanded that
I should. Between the two there seemed no refuge except
silence, and silence in Calcutta is expensive. It must be
defended by explanations, fortified by denials, and daily supplied with
fresh arguments. I therefore fled to the village, where my
silence could pass for indolence and offend nobody but the
mosquitoes.

The house stood at some distance from the road, behind
a neglected orchard of mango and guava. Beyond it were
fields, then a belt of palms, and then the river,
which in the rains came near us with the familiarity
of a creditor, but in winter withdrew modestly behind broad
banks of sand. The village itself was neither picturesque enough
for poets nor prosperous enough for officials. It possessed a
little temple with a broken cornice, a market that assembled
twice a week, and a pond whose green water performed
impartially the offices of bath, mirror, shrine, and drinking-place for
cattle.

I arrived in the month when the rains had spent
their passion but not their tears. Mists lay over the
fields at dawn like eyelids swollen after weeping. At noon
the sun descended among the villagers with the mild authority
of an aged grandsire, warming their backs as they husked
rice or repaired nets; toward evening the wet grasses took
fire under his departing glance, and every spider's web held
for an instant a frail rosary of light.

In this retirement I proposed to labour. I had brought
with me manuscripts, proofs, books of reference, and a quantity
of costly paper, all arranged upon my writing table with
an order suggestive of great undertakings. Every morning I sat
before them. Every morning the birds in the bamboo grove,
having no reputation to defend, sang with intolerable ease.

It was during one of these contests between my intellect
and the kingfisher that she first entered.

I heard no footstep. Looking up, I found a woman
standing at the door with a small handful of white
flowers. She was perhaps forty-five, though poverty, exposure, and inward
fire had written different ages upon her face. Her body
was spare; her coarse garment had once been saffron but
had surrendered its colour to sun and rain; her hair,
streaked with grey, was drawn back without ornament. Only her
eyes seemed untouched by weather. They were large, attentive, and
fearless—not the eyes of one who looks at a man,
but of one who looks through a window.

Before I could rise she came forward, laid the flowers
beside my inkstand, and made obeisance, touching her forehead to
the floor.

I confess that adoration, though not wholly unknown to authors,
generally reaches them through reviews written after death. To receive
it alive, in one's dressing gown, is disconcerting.

"Mother," I said, "you have mistaken the room. The shrine
is beyond the tamarind trees."

She raised her head and smiled.

"I have been there."

"Then the god has already had the better flowers."

"He took what was his. These also are his."

Having said this, she bowed once more and departed.

The flowers were woodland jasmine, bruised in her warm hand.
My servant, entering soon afterward with tea, observed them with
suspicion.

"Who brought those?"

"A holy woman."

He sniffed. "She begs in five villages."

"That proves industry."

"She eats in any house."

"That proves catholicity."

"She has no caste left."

"Then she must travel lightly."

He did not approve of my reasoning. Servants tolerate philosophy
in their masters only when it does not disturb the
arrangements of the household. He removed the flowers from beside
my papers and put them into a brass cup at
the far corner of the table, where they stood in
a rigid row like schoolboys punished for some fragrant offence.

The next morning she came again.

This time I was in the veranda taking my breakfast.
The sun had only just cleared the plantain leaves, and
their broad green surfaces shone as though the night had
washed them for worship. My meal was simple—rice, milk, a
banana, and some fried cakes—but simplicity under the guardianship of
a cook acquires several plates and a great deal of
ceremony.

The woman waited at a distance until I had finished.
Then, as the servant came to remove the dishes, she
said, "Give me what remains."

The servant started as though she had asked for his
sacred thread.

"What remains is polluted," he said.

"By whom?"

He glanced at me, unwilling to name the source of
pollution in its own presence.

She understood and laughed. "Then I desire that pollution."

The servant appealed to me. I was tempted to deliver
a discourse upon caste, hospitality, equality, and the ancient symbolism
of food; but she had already taken the leaf-plate in
her hands. Sitting upon the ground by the veranda steps,
she ate what I had left.

There was no humility in the act, still less greed.
She ate slowly, with closed eyes, as one receiving a
sacrament. A few grains of rice clung to the side
of the bowl; she gathered them with her finger. The
piece of banana I had rejected because it was bruised
she placed upon her tongue with grave contentment.

I became ashamed—not because she ate my remnants, but because
I had not known them to be worthy of reverence.

When she had finished, she washed the plate at the
well and brought it back.

"You should not do this," I said.

"Why?"

"It offends people."

"Does it offend you?"

"No."

"Then let the others preserve their offence. It may be
precious to them."

She sat on the lowest step, while I remained above
her in a chair. This arrangement displeased her.

"Come down," she said.

I obeyed, partly because I was amused and partly because
her commands had the innocent authority of sunlight. We sat
side by side upon the step. In the yard two
calves were tugging at the same wisp of hay; a
myna hopped behind them, taking insects from the marks of
their hoofs.

"What do you see when you look at me?" I
asked.

She turned her eyes upon me.

"I do not look at you."

This was a wholesome reply for a man accused by
the newspapers of excessive self-regard.

"At whom, then?"

"At him who accepts my flowers."

"But I have accepted nothing. My servant has imprisoned them
in a brass cup."

"He accepted them before your servant touched them."

I told her, with some unction, that God was present
everywhere, and therefore needed no special vessel. Such truths, being
large and smooth, are easily handled in conversation.

She listened without reverence.

"The sky is everywhere," she said, "but the bird must
alight upon a branch. Water is everywhere beneath the earth,
but the thirsty man digs at one spot. Your God
may be everywhere. Mine is where I find him."

There ended my theology for that morning.

She came often after that, though never at an appointed
hour. Sometimes at dawn I would see her crossing the
field, her garment wet to the knee with dew. Sometimes
at midday she appeared out of the white glare, carrying
a cluster of oleanders or a single red hibiscus. At
times she said nothing. She would sit by the doorway
while I wrote, and the scratching of my pen, the
drone of bees, and the creaking of a distant oil
press joined themselves into a silence deeper than the absence
of sound.

One day she watched me for a long while and
then asked, "What do you write?"

"Words."

"For whom?"

"For people who do not wish to read them."

"Then why do you write?"

"To persuade them that they do."

She considered this and shook her head.

"You hide him under too much paper."

"Whom?"

"The one I come to see."

Thereafter my books incurred her distrust. My clothes had already
offended her. In the village I continued, from force of
city habit, to wear socks in the cooler mornings. She
looked at them with sorrow.

"Even your feet are shut away."

"My feet are not edifying objects."

"That is not for you to decide."

"You would have me go barefoot?"

"How can the dust know you otherwise?"

The argument was irrational, and therefore difficult to answer. The
next morning, as an experiment, I walked without shoes along
the narrow path between the rice fields. The earth was
cool and a little adhesive; blades of grass stroked my
ankles; pebbles, whose existence civilization had concealed from me, made
themselves sharply known. A child driving goats laughed at my
awkwardness. Yet after a while I felt that the road,
which had hitherto been a brown line in the landscape,
had become an intimate fact. Dust entered the creases of
my skin. The earth was no longer scenery; it had
touched me.

When she came that day, she saw my muddy feet
and made obeisance to them.

"Mother," I protested, drawing them back, "if you continue thus,
I shall be compelled to wear boots."

She smiled. "You fear worship more than blame."

There was truth in this. Blame had occupied me greatly;
worship embarrassed me because it ignored the person I had
prepared for public view. My critics struck at an image
made of words, and I could parry with words. But
this woman bowed before something of which I could give
no account. Her devotion passed through me as sunlight through
a cracked wall, making no inquiry into the merits of
the plaster.

My servants began to oppose her visits. They had borne
with her so long as she remained an eccentricity belonging
to their master; but when she crossed the inner courtyard,
spoke to the cook, took water from the household well,
and distributed among the children flowers intended, as they believed,
for better gods, eccentricity became revolution.

One afternoon I heard an altercation.

"You cannot go in," said the gatekeeper.

"Why?"

"Babu is working."

"I have seen him work. He sits very still and
spoils paper."

"He has ordered not to be disturbed."

"Did he order it, or did you?"

The gatekeeper, whose loyalty consisted chiefly in making my existence
inaccessible, raised his voice. I went out and found her
standing beyond the threshold.

"Why did you not send word?" I asked.

"I sent myself."

The gatekeeper looked wounded.

She pointed at him. "This man has built a wall
around you."

"He is paid to do so."

"Then you pay him to keep away what you seek."

"What do I seek?"

She looked toward the fields, where the evening cattle were
returning in a slow procession, bells sounding under their throats.

"That you must know. But you will not find it
behind him."

Nor, she added later, behind my books, my respectable clothes,
my reputation, or my carefully guarded leisure. She attacked all
my fortifications with equal simplicity. It did not occur to
her that worldly distinction might assist spiritual life. Wealth, rank,
learning, and fame seemed to her so many curtains hung
before a lamp; even disgrace, though painful, was useful if
it tore a hole through which light might escape.

At that time news from Calcutta arrived irregularly, but abuse
travels where affection grows weary. A newspaper containing a particularly
energetic assault upon me found its way to the village
tea shop. The schoolmaster read passages aloud; the grocer contributed
explanations; and before evening the inhabitants had learned that the
quiet gentleman in the garden house was secretly responsible for
national weakness, foreign manners, domestic impiety, literary decadence, and perhaps
the failure of that year's jute crop.

When next the Devotee passed through the market, some young
men called after her.

"There goes the saint who worships the condemned babu!"

"Ask your god to answer the newspaper!"

"Will you eat his sins with his rice?"

She stopped. The mockers, encouraged by her silence, came nearer.

"Mother," said one, with pretended courtesy, "does holiness grow from
bad fame?"

She looked at him steadily.

"When the washerman beats the cloth upon the stone, does
the cloth complain that the stone hates it?"

They laughed, but less confidently.

Another said, "Your babu has been stripped of honour."

"Then God has begun to undress him."

This answer reached me before she did, decorated by many
improvements. I asked her whether she had truly said it.

"Yes."

"You believe my humiliation is divine discipline?"

"How else should I bear it?"

"It may merely be human stupidity."

"God uses poor servants."

I laughed. "Then he has an abundant staff in Calcutta."

But she did not laugh.

"When men praise you," she said, "they cover your face
with garlands. When they blame you, they tear the garlands
away. Why are you angry with the hands that let
me see?"

I could not tell her that a garland, though concealing,
is agreeable to wear. That evening I walked alone by
the river. The western sky was empty of splendour; clouds
lay low, and a colourless light endured behind them. On
the sand a lean village dog gnawed a dry bone
with solemn perseverance. A fisherman, knee-deep in water, drew in
his net and found nothing; nevertheless he cast it again.
From the opposite bank came the faint cry of someone
calling cattle home.

These common things appeared strangely complete. The dog needed no
dignity beyond his hunger; the fisherman no applause beyond the
next casting of his net; the voice crossing the river
bore no doctrine, yet it gathered the fading fields into
one human home. I had spent many years separating the
sacred from the ordinary, as a careful householder separates his
best vessels from daily use. The Devotee, with one sweep
of her ragged garment, confused the arrangement. She had found
eternity among leftovers.

On another morning she brought no flowers. Her hands were
empty.

"Has the garden refused you?" I asked.

She stood before me with an expression I had not
seen before.

"The villagers plucked them first."

"Then these hands are an offering sufficient."

At once her face changed. She lifted her hands, looked
at them as though they had just been given to
her, and touched them to her forehead.

"You have taught me today," she said.

I was alarmed. "Do not accuse me of that. I
said the first thing that came."

"Where else does truth come from?"

"Usually from the last thing, after much revision."

She sat down. For a time neither of us spoke.
The mist was lifting from the pond. Under its pale
veil the ducks moved like thoughts not yet formed, and
every now and then one dipped its head, leaving its
tail absurdly upright toward heaven.

At length she said, "You wonder why I wander."

"I have wondered, but I have no right to ask."

"You have the right because I have bowed to you."

"That gives rights to you, not to me."

"No. Worship opens the door both ways."

Her eyes were fixed upon the awakening water. The firmness
that usually held her face seemed to loosen; lines of
fatigue appeared around her mouth.

"I have not always worn this cloth," she said. "I
had a house. I had ornaments. I had a husband
whose heart was gentler than mine. I had a son."

The last word scarcely crossed her lips.

Then she said, "Listen."

### II

"I was married very young, and for several years I
remained younger than my years. My husband loved me with
a patience which I mistook for weakness. He spoke little,
laughed softly, and never forbade me anything. Had he been
harsh I might have feared him; had he been vain
I might have flattered him; but his goodness made no
demand, and therefore I neglected it.

"Our house was prosperous. There were servants, cousins, guests, ceremonies,
songs, journeys to neighbouring houses. I loved company. I loved
the sound of anklets in the courtyard, the preparation of
sweetmeats, the gossip after worship, the festivals when women compared
saris and husbands and pretended that neither comparison mattered. I
was praised for my voice and for the quickness of
my answers. Wherever pleasure called, I went.

"Then my son was born.

"For a little while the world became still. When he
slept beside me, his fist closed upon nothing, I thought
that nothing must be God. His breath was so small
that I watched his breast in terror, and when he
woke and cried I rejoiced at the noise. But even
wonder becomes familiar to the selfish heart. Soon I left
him to the nurse while I returned to visits, songs,
and bathing parties.

"He was not a troublesome child. That was his misfortune.

"He followed me with his eyes. When I went out,
he wished to come; when I returned, he ran to
me. I would take him in my arms, kiss him,
give him some toy, and put him down. I believed
these moments were love. Perhaps they were. But love that
gives only its spare moments is a guest, not a
mother.

"One summer morning several women of our house went to
bathe in the river. The rains had not yet come,
but the current was strong near the landing steps. The
sky was bright; the path burned under our feet; the
water looked dark and cool beneath the banyan shade.

"My son clung to my garment and begged to come.

"'No,' I said. 'Stay with the nurse.'

"The nurse was grinding spices. She called to him from
the courtyard.

"He began to cry. I was impatient. My companions were
laughing ahead of me. I loosened his fingers and went
away.

"At the river we bathed. I was a strong swimmer,
stronger than the other women, and proud of it. I
crossed beyond the safe place while they called after me.
The water held me; the sun glittered upon it; my
body was light. I remember thinking how beautiful the morning
was.

"Then someone screamed.

"At first I thought they called to me because I
had gone too far. I turned and saw the women
upon the steps, all looking upward. A small figure stood
at the top.

"It was my son.

"He had followed us.

"He saw me in the water and called, 'Mother!'

"I called back, 'Stay there!'

"He began to come down.

"The steps were wet.

"He slipped.

"I saw him strike the stone. I saw his little
arms rise. Then the river took him.

"I swam. The bank moved away. The water that had
carried me like a friend became iron. I swam and
could not reach him. Someone caught my hair; someone shouted;
bodies crowded the steps. I went under. I rose. I
called his name.

"They found him below the landing, held among the roots.

"They laid him upon the stone.

"His eyes were open.

"I said, 'He is afraid. Give him to me.'

"No one answered.

"I rubbed his hands. I breathed into his mouth. I
struck his feet. An old woman pressed his belly, and
water came out. I thought life would come after it.
More water came. No life.

"Blow upon blow. I struck him. I called him. Blow
upon blow.

"The women tried to draw me away. I bit one
of them. I took him in my arms and carried
him home.

"My husband was standing in the courtyard.

"He looked first at the child, then at me. He
did not ask what had happened. He understood.

"That silence has never ended."

The Devotee ceased speaking. A koel called once from the
orchard, though the season for its song was past. The
solitary note seemed to lose its way among the leaves.

After a while she continued.

"For many days I did not know who fed me
or where I slept. Sometimes I heard my son crying
from the next room. Sometimes I saw wet footprints crossing
the floor. I would follow them, and they vanished at
the wall. At night I felt a small hand upon
my breast, searching; when I woke, my nails had torn
the skin.

"I accused everyone. The nurse should have watched him. The
women should have stopped him. The steps should not have
been slippery. The river should have returned him. God should
have taken me instead.

"But beneath all accusation one voice remained: I had loosened
his fingers.

"My husband never reproached me. This made my guilt heavier.
If he had struck me, I could have fallen at
his feet. If he had cursed me, I could have
accepted the curse. But he sat near me in the
evenings and spoke of little things—the price of grain, a
sick tenant, a broken door—as though the world, having ended,
must still be gently attended to.

"He had always revered a spiritual teacher who visited our
district. I had laughed at this devotion. The Teacher was
learned, eloquent, and beautiful to look upon. People said that
in his presence doubts fell silent. Before my sorrow I
considered doubt an ornament of intelligence and silence an admission
of defeat.

"My husband asked the Teacher to come.

"He entered the room where I sat beside my child's
empty bed. I did not rise. He did not rebuke
me. He sat on the floor.

"For a long time he said nothing.

"Then he began to chant.

"His voice was deep and quiet. I cannot tell you
the words. They were verses I had heard before without
hearing them. That day each word entered me like rain
entering burnt ground. He chanted of the soul unborn and
undying, of forms that pass and the life that remains,
of the One who receives every child when the mother's
arms fail.

"I wept.

"Until then my grief had been stone. His voice made
it water.

"From that day I worshipped him.

"My husband rejoiced. He believed peace had come to our
house, and for five years perhaps it had. I rose
before dawn, prepared flowers, ground sandal paste, cooked food for
the Teacher when he visited, and listened to his instruction.
I ceased going to idle gatherings. I gave away ornaments.
I learned verses. The rooms that had echoed with laughter
became quiet, but it was not an empty quiet. Devotion
filled them like incense in a shrine.

"My husband and I came nearer without speaking of what
lay between us. We served the same presence. Sometimes, while
the Teacher discoursed, I saw my husband's face turned toward
him with such innocence that my heart bowed to my
husband also. His faith had no hunger in it. He
desired no vision for himself; it was enough that the
lamp burned.

"I believed the Teacher had rescued me from the river
where my son died. The body of my child had
sunk, and my soul had sunk after it; his hand,
I thought, had drawn me upward.

"For five years I believed this.

"One morning in the rainy season I went early to
bathe. The river was full, almost level with the last
step. Clouds covered the sky, and the water was the
colour of smoke. No other women had come. I entered
wearing my bathing cloth, dipped beneath the current, and stood
for a while listening to the rain begin upon the
river.

"When I returned, my garment was wet and clung to
me. My hair hung loose. I carried the brass vessel
at my side.

"The Teacher was standing beneath the peepul tree.

"I had not expected him. I stopped and made obeisance
from a distance.

"He looked at me.

"I knew his look. For five years I had lived
under it as under a sacred flame. That morning the
flame changed.

"He said, 'How beautiful your body is when the wet
cloth reveals it.'

"Only that.

"No hand touched me. No further word was spoken. The
rain fell. A crow shook water from its wings. Somewhere
a cart wheel cried in the mud.

"But the shrine was broken.

"You may say he was a man, and a man's
eye may be surprised by beauty. You may say the
fault was momentary, the words careless, the meaning less than
I imagined. Learned people have many vessels in which to
preserve a cracked truth. I had none.

"I looked at him, and I saw that he knew
what he had said.

"He turned away.

"I went home.

"My husband was seated near the window, reading a scripture.
His finger rested beneath a line. When I entered, he
looked up and smiled. That smile was worse than death.

"How could I tell him? The Teacher was the purest
thing in his life. My husband had placed his heart
at those feet. If I spoke plainly, I would not
merely accuse a man; I would destroy the heaven under
which my husband lived.

"Yet if I remained silent, every act of worship would
become a lie. I would prepare flowers for eyes that
had stripped me. I would listen to words while remembering
the word beneath them. I would see my husband bow
and know what he did not know.

"I changed my wet garment. I sat before him.

"I said, 'The Teacher has commanded me to leave the
world.'

"My husband's finger remained upon the page.

"He asked, 'When?'

"I said, 'This morning.'

"He looked at me a long time. 'What words did
he use?'

"I could not answer.

"He closed the book.

"The rain sounded upon the eaves. It had entered through
a crack, and one drop fell again and again upon
the floor between us.

"He said, 'Must you go?'

"I said, 'I must.'

"He asked, 'Is there no place for truth in this
house?'

"I said, 'There has been too little falsehood here. I
cannot bring more.'

"He bowed his head.

"For three days he said nothing further. He attended to
household matters. He arranged money for me, though he knew
I would not take it. He brought out a pair
of sandals because the roads were flooded. He folded a
cloth and placed it beside them.

"On the third night he came to the room where
I sat.

"He stood at the door. His face had aged.

"'Did he command you,' he asked, 'or did something in
him compel you?'

"I began to weep.

"He understood.

"He did not ask what had happened. Again he understood.

"For the first time since our child died, he spoke
harshly. Not to me. He said the Teacher's name once,
and the name sounded like a door closing.

"Then he sat upon the floor and covered his face.

"I wished to touch him. I did not dare.

"After a long while he said, 'Stay. We will worship
no man. We will live quietly. If God is true,
he will not be lost because one face has lied.'

"These were wise words. They were loving words. But I
could not stay.

"Why? I have asked myself through many nights.

"Perhaps I feared his goodness more than another woman would
fear cruelty. If I remained, he would forgive me for
bringing ruin into his faith. He would make a new
shrine out of our sorrow. He would serve me, and
I would accept his service. Again his gentleness would become
the food of my selfishness.

"I had lost our child because I went toward pleasure.
I had now destroyed his worship because another man looked
upon me with pleasure. My body seemed a road by
which calamity entered the house.

"Before dawn I put on the plain cloth.

"My husband was awake. He placed the sandals before me.

"I said, 'I cannot take them.'

"He said, 'The road has stones.'

"I said, 'Let them know me.'

"He knelt and touched my feet.

"I cried out and drew back. 'Do not!'

"He answered, 'You are going where I cannot follow.'

"I wanted then to remain. I wanted to lay my
head upon his knees and say that no God was
worth this parting. But if I had spoken, he would
have believed me, and I no longer trusted the love
that came through my mouth.

"I left.

"At the end of the lane I turned. He stood
under the doorway, one hand against the post. The morning
had not yet come, but the lamp behind him made
a border of light around his body.

"That is how I remember him: darkness before him, light
behind."

She fell silent.

The sun was now high. The mist had vanished, and
the pond, stripped of mystery, displayed its weeds, broken steps,
floating straw, and one blue lotus opening in the midst
of refuse.

I said, "Have you never returned?"

"No."

"Do you know whether he lives?"

"He lives."

"You have heard?"

"I hear without news."

"And the Teacher?"

She looked at me with surprise, as though I had
inquired after a discarded garment.

"He ceased that morning."

I understood that for her the man might continue to
speak, travel, receive disciples, and be honoured; yet the being
she had worshipped was dead beyond resurrection.

"You have suffered much through worship," I said. "Why, then,
do you still worship men?"

"I do not worship men."

"You bow to me."

"I bow where the door opens."

"And if it closes?"

"I go away."

Her answer frightened me more than flattery could have done.
I had no wish to become another shrine in which
human weakness lay concealed behind incense. I told her so.

"Do not mistake me," I said. "I am made of
ordinary faults. Those who abuse me know some of them;
I know more."

She nodded. "That is why there is air around you."

"Tomorrow there may be no air."

"Then tomorrow I shall not come."

There was neither threat nor complaint in her voice. Her
devotion demanded no permanence from its object. She would not
imprison truth even in gratitude.

She rose and made obeisance.

I tried to prevent her, but she said, "Do not
stand between him and me."

So I remained still.

That day, when my meal was brought, I looked at
the rice, the lentils, the coarse salt, and the slice
of lime as though they had travelled a great distance
to reach me. The grain had stood under rain; hands
had cut it, beaten it, carried it, cooked it. Fire,
water, earth, hunger, labour, and death had conspired to place
one mouthful before my careless appetite. I ate slowly.

Afterward I told the servant not to throw the remnants
away.

"For the holy woman?" he asked.

"For whoever is hungry."

He looked disappointed by the breadth of the instruction, but
obeyed.

Days passed. The Devotee came and went. Sometimes she sang
beneath the neem tree, her voice roughened by roads yet
carrying a sweetness beyond accomplishment. Children gathered. Women listened from
behind doors. Even those who mocked her lowered their speech
when her song crossed the evening.

Then one morning she did not come.

Nor the next.

A week later a cowherd told me that she had
been seen walking northward along the river, carrying nothing but
a water pot. I sent no one after her. To
pursue such a person is to mistake the direction of

Winter entered the village. The mist thickened; the grass silvered
at dawn; my socks returned, though not without inward controversy.
News from Calcutta became less violent. Other offenders arose, as
they mercifully do, and public indignation transferred its inexhaustible virtue
elsewhere.

I resumed my writing. The books still stood around me;
the servants still guarded the gate; my name, neither purified
nor destroyed, continued its uncertain business in the world. Outwardly
nothing had altered.

Yet sometimes, before beginning work, I placed a flower beside
the inkstand—not as an ornament, nor as an offering to
myself, but in remembrance of that homeless worship which had
once passed through my room.

The flower would fade by noon. Its petals would loosen
and fall upon the paper. Formerly I should have brushed
them aside. Now I let them remain.

For I had learned, though imperfectly, that the divine does
not always descend amid thunder, nor wait in the sanctuaries
we have swept and prepared. At times it comes barefoot
by a village road, eats from a rejected plate, quarrels
with a gatekeeper, and rebukes a philosopher for hiding his
feet. At times it shines through grief so severe that
no doctrine can contain it. At times it is betrayed
by the very face through which it first appeared; yet,
betrayed, it does not cease. It seeks another door.

And I, who had retired from the world because men
had spoken falsely of me, found myself instructed by a
woman who had abandoned the world because she could not
endure falsehood in worship.

Toward evening I would walk beside the river. The cattle
came home through the dust; birds settled noisily and then
were still; smoke rose from cooking fires and lay across
the fields in blue bands. The sun, old and kindly,
touched roofs, trees, animals, men, and muddy water without distinction.
For a few moments nothing was common, because nothing was
excluded.

Then darkness gathered all forms into itself.

From some distant path, perhaps only in memory, a woman's
voice would begin a song. I could not distinguish the
words. I did not try.

I listened.

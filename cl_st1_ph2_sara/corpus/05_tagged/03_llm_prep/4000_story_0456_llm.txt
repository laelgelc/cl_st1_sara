### THE DYER'S WIFE, THE HUNCHBACK, AND THE VENOMOUS SWORD

In the good town of Tours, where the Loire goes
sliding between its banks like a fat canon leaving table,
there lived, toward the latter end of King Louis's reign—whether
the Eleventh or the Twelfth matters no more than last
year's snow—a laundress named Perrine Couverte, who washed the linen
of advocates, apothecaries, prebendaries, innkeepers, widows, and other persons whose
sins required much soap.

This Perrine was clean of hand, quick of eye, nimble
of tongue, and made in such a pleasant mould that
when she bent over the washing-stone by the river, every
bachelor on the bridge found urgent business upon the bank.
She had hair the colour of roasted chestnuts; cheeks whereon
April and September had divided the tenancy; teeth ranged like
white nuns at chapter; and a waist so neatly turned
that a man might lose his salvation in considering by
what compass God had drawn it.

Now beauty in a poor woman is a ripe pear
hanging over a public road. All throw stones at it,
and he who gets it thanks his good arm, never
the tree. Perrine, understanding this theology better than many doctors
of Paris, resolved that no varlet should steal for nothing
what a burgess might purchase with a house, a feather-bed,
two vineyards, and a cupboard smelling of nutmeg.

There was at that time in the Rue de la
Scellerie a master silk dyer named Macé Tardiveau, fifty-six years
old, broad in belly, short in breath, red in nose,
and rich in crowns. He coloured silk for ladies of
the court, banners for churches, girdles for brides, and hoods
for magistrates, making scarlet out of cochineal, blue out of
woad, yellow out of weld, and gold out of everybody.
His first wife had gone to heaven, or elsewhere, leaving
him no child, three pewter dishes, and a fear of
draughts. Having seen Perrine wring a sheet with arms as
round and white as peeled willow, he fell instantly into
that grave fever which old men call love and young
women call opportunity.

Perrine kept him simmering for six months, sometimes smiling, sometimes
sighing, always refusing the small coin and seeming not to
perceive the large. At length Master Tardiveau offered marriage. Thereupon
she wept, crossed herself, spoke of the inequality of their
ages, his dignity, her low estate, the malice of neighbours,
and accepted before he could withdraw a syllable.

Thus the laundress entered the dyer's house as mistress thereof,
wearing velvet where she had worn serge, drinking Beaune where
she had drunk river water, and ordering about servants whose
aprons she had formerly washed. She proved a thrifty wife,
a merry bedfellow, a prudent buyer of capons, and so
attentive to her husband's gout that he blessed the day
on which his wisdom had selected her. Husbands commonly name
wisdom that which has tickled their folly.

Among the familiar companions of Master Tardiveau was a mechanician
called Carandas, a man extraordinarily learned in wheels, springs, screws,
balances, locks, pumps, rat-traps, bellows, and all ingenious contrivances except
the governance of himself. Nature, having bestowed upon him the
mind of six craftsmen, had economised upon his body. He
was humped between the shoulders, pinched in the chest, long
of arm, short of leg, yellow of skin, and furnished
with a nose like the handle of a cracked pitcher.
When walking, he went first to one side, then to
the other, as though each of his feet distrusted the
road chosen by its fellow.

But let no one conclude that a crooked back bends
the appetite. On the contrary, Carandas, seeing Perrine enthroned in
the dyer's house, became as amorous as thirty monkeys shut
in a nunnery. He watched her cut bread, pour wine,
shake a cushion, rebuke a maid, or carry a lamp
upstairs; and each little action dropped oil upon the fire
consuming him.

At first he praised her sleeves.

Then her hands.

Then the air surrounding her hands.

Then he declared that Master Tardiveau possessed the finest dye-house
in Touraine, the best cellar between Vouvray and Chinon, and
the most miraculous wife since Adam lost a rib.

Perrine laughed.

That laugh encouraged him, because in affairs of love a
fool takes a shut door for an invitation to enter
through the window.

One evening, finding her alone beside the kitchen fire, Carandas
planted himself before her, twisted his cap, rolled his eyes,
and said:

"Madame Perrine, there is in my breast a machine more
cruel than any I ever constructed."

"Oil it," said she.

"It is fed by your beauty."

"Then starve it."

"It turns night and day."

"Take out the spring."

"You alone can stop it."

"Master Carandas," answered she, "I am my husband's wife."

"That is an accident of law."

"And you are my husband's friend."

"That is an inconvenience of friendship."

"And if I cried out?"

"I should die."

"Then," said she, "we must carefully preserve your life."

Having said this, she allowed him to kiss the tips
of her fingers, which cost her nothing and purchased a
week's amusement. Carandas departed swollen with hope, stepping so high
that his hump seemed to be dragging the rest of
him back to prudence.

He returned the next day, and the next, buzzing about
Perrine like a blue fly round a honey-pot. At last
she said to him in a whisper:

"Come to-night when the cathedral bell strikes eleven. My husband
will be inspecting a vat at Saint-Symphorien. Knock three little
knocks at the garden door. If all be quiet, I
shall let you in."

Carandas nearly unscrewed his head bowing.

Now this happened in January, during one of those Touraine
winters which freeze the mud without cleansing it, sharpen the
wind upon the roofs, and make every chimney smoke like
a theologian contradicted. The moon had tucked herself into a
cloud. The streets were black, the kennels hard, the air
full of a thousand invisible needles. Nevertheless, Carandas came in
a satin doublet, perfumed gloves, and shoes too thin for
a dead saint's patience. Beneath his cloak he carried a
flask of hypocras, sweet cakes, and certain ribbons which he
imagined useful in the campaign of Venus.

At eleven he gave three little knocks.

The door opened the width of Perrine's face.

"Is it you?" she whispered.

"It is my soul, my pearl, my furnace!"

"Hush! My husband has returned unexpectedly. Stand beneath the window.
I will throw down the key when he sleeps."

The door closed.

Carandas stationed himself beneath the window, where a stone gargoyle
dripped upon his neck. Five minutes passed. Ten. Half an
hour. The wind entered his sleeves, explored his breeches, counted
his ribs, and bit each toe separately. Yet he remained,
for desire is a furred cloak to expectation, though a
poor blanket to the body.

Presently the casement opened.

"My angel!" he murmured.

"Catch the key!" replied Perrine.

Instead of a key there descended upon him a great
basin of water in which stockings, napkins, and two of
Master Tardiveau's shirts had been soaking.

Carandas gave a cry fit to curdle milk. Blinded, drenched,
and instantly converted into an icicle with opinions, he sprang
backward, planted one foot upon the frozen edge of the
gutter, whirled thrice like a mill-wheel, and fell through a
rotten cover into the sewer.

Tours had many streets then, and each had a savour
proper to itself. There were streets smelling of leather, streets
smelling of spice, streets smelling of hot bread, wax candles,
wet wool, horse sweat, old parchment, young garlic, fried gudgeons,
incense, onions, and litigation. Ah, the dear old streets of
Tours! Narrow in body, broad in history; crooked as a
usurer's account; overhung by houses leaning toward one another to
exchange scandal; bright with signs of lions, moons, boots, bells,
saints, dragons, and drinking vessels; paved with stones capable of
breaking a bishop's ankle; populated by students, muleteers, monks, serving-wenches,
hawkers, dogs, children, and geese, each crying his own merchandise
or necessity. I love especially the Rue de la Rôtisserie
at dinner-time, when the spits revolved, the fat hissed, and
every poor man's nose ate what his purse could not.
But where am I? In a sewer, with Carandas.

The said Carandas emerged from this municipal broth coated from
ear to heel in substances which modesty declines to catalogue.
Above, Perrine laughed so heartily that the casement shook.

"Have you found the key?" she called.

"I shall remember this!" he answered, his teeth clattering like
castanets in a skeleton's hand.

"Do so, dear friend. Memory warms a man."

For eight days Carandas lay abed with fever, during which
he cursed women, winter, water, sewers, dyers, laundry, marriage, and
architecture. On the ninth day Perrine visited him carrying chicken
broth. She sat by his bed, stroked his hand, blamed
the stupidity of her maid, swore that the basin had
slipped by misfortune, and wept three tears so round and
convincing that Carandas swallowed each with the broth.

"My poor friend," said she, "how could you think me
cruel?"

"I did think it," he replied.

"Then your fever has injured your judgment."

She leaned near enough for him to smell violets. At
once his resentment softened, his prudence melted, and the machine
in his breast resumed its accursed turning.

A month later she promised him a second meeting.

"No doors," said Carandas.

"No windows," said Perrine.

"No water."

"Not a drop."

"No tricks."

"Upon my honour."

A woman's honour in such an oath is like a
tavern spoon: chained to the table, but always missing when
required.

She bade him come at dusk, Master Tardiveau being invited
to supper by the guild of dyers. Carandas arrived dressed
as gaily as a reliquary, and Perrine admitted him into
her chamber, where a good fire burned, curtains were drawn,
and a flask stood ready. He had scarcely embraced her
waist when a violent knocking shook the house.

"My husband!" cried she.

Carandas turned the colour of cheese.

"Hide me!"

"Where?"

"Anywhere!"

The bed was too low, the cupboard locked, and the
chimney hot. Perrine pointed to a great linen chest of
oak, bound with iron, wherein sheets, tablecloths, lavender bags, spare
curtains, and winter blankets were packed as tightly as citizens
at a royal entry.

"In there."

Carandas climbed in. Perrine pressed him down among the linen,
folded his knees against his chin, tucked his hump beneath
a quilt, and shut the lid.

Master Tardiveau entered, complaining that the guild supper had been
postponed because the dean's wife was in labour and the
dean had taken away the key of the hall.

"Why is the fire so large?" he asked.

"I was cold."

"Why are there two cups?"

"One for you, my ram."

"Why does the chest groan?"

"The boards are settling."

From within came a muffled sneeze.

"And that?"

"A mouse."

"A monstrous mouse."

"Linen fattens them."

The dyer, having eaten a plate of sausages and drunk
the flask intended for Carandas, became affectionate. Perrine, who could
turn from danger to dalliance as a cat turns from
a saucepan to cream, sat upon her husband's knee, pulled
his beard, called him her lion, and coaxed him upstairs.

But she did not release Carandas.

She forgot him—that is, she remembered him too well.

Throughout the night the poor mechanician remained folded in four,
with a lavender bag in his mouth, a brass candlestick
against his ear, and his left foot asleep beneath a
bolster. Worse still, the bed stood near the chest, and
through the oak he heard Master Tardiveau demonstrating that years
and girth had not altogether extinguished the embers of conjugal
duty. There were whispers, creakings, sighs, small disputes, reconciliations, and
at one point such laughter from Perrine that Carandas bit
the lavender bag clean through.

Herein may be observed a curious principle: one man's happiness,
heard through a board, makes more noise than a cooper's
shop and gives less satisfaction.

Near dawn Perrine opened the chest.

Carandas rose slowly, like a badly packed resurrection. His joints
cracked; his face was striped by folded napkins; a lace
cap hung from one ear; and his entire person exhaled
lavender, rage, and suffocation.

"Did you sleep well?" she asked.

Carandas looked at her without speaking.

"Come, do not sulk. You are alive."

"I was more comfortably made before I entered."

"My husband might have killed you."

"He shall not have the trouble."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean," said Carandas, "that I am cured."

And in truth his desire had departed. But where desire
goes out by the door, hatred sometimes climbs in through
the chimney. Carandas now hated Perrine with a cardinal hate,
an episcopal hate, a hate of all chapters, councils, and
consistories; a hate distilled from sewer-water, frozen toes, crushed ribs,
lavender, jealousy, and the laughter of a woman. It was
all known hates pounded together in Hell's mortar and boiled
to an elixir in the furnace of remembrance.

Within a fortnight he left Tours, saying he had business
in Lyon. Thence he went to Milan, Venice, Florence, and
certain German cities where men put clocks into towers, birds
into clocks, devils into boxes, and profit into everything. His
engines lifted water, stamped coin, wound silk, turned spits, rang
bells, and opened locks without keys. Princes paid him, merchants
flattered him, and fools purchased his inventions for twice what
wise men would have refused to give. Thus he grew
rich, which is the revenge most eagerly desired by the
poor and least enjoyed by the spiteful.

Twelve years passed.

Master Tardiveau became stouter, slower, and greyer. Perrine became neither
stout nor slow, but ripened magnificently, like a pear kept
in straw. She had borne two children, a boy named
Étienne and a girl named Margot, both handsome, dark-eyed, straight-nosed,
and in no degree resembling the red-faced dyer. This troubled
nobody, because husbands see with the eyes of custom, and
because Tardiveau, being proud of his children, discovered in them
every feature of his family. The boy's black hair came
from a great-uncle. The girl's long fingers came from a
cousin at Loches. Their graceful bodies came, no doubt, from
some ancestor too remote to contradict him.

They came in fact from Brother Anselme, a young priest
attached to Saint-Gatien, who had shoulders like a captain, teeth
like an Italian singer, and a voice capable of making
widows confess sins they had not yet committed. He was
Perrine's spiritual adviser. He directed her conscience so closely that
it seldom went anywhere without him.

Their meetings occurred under colour of charity, confession, feast-day preparations,
and the spiritual education of the children. When Brother Anselme
entered by the front door, he carried a prayer-book; when
he entered by the garden door, he carried no encumbrance
likely to delay devotion. Perrine gave alms to his poor,
wine to his table, linen to his sacristy, and consolations
to his solitude.

Now Carandas returned to Tours in a carriage with six
horses, three servants, velvet garments, gold rings, and a chain
heavy enough to anchor a boat. The citizens who had
once laughed at his hump bowed to his purse, for
wealth straightens every back in the eyes of the world.

Master Tardiveau welcomed him with tears, embraces, and a dinner
consisting of pike, pigeons, a haunch of venison, two capons,
three pies, and sufficient wine to float the abbey of
Marmoutier.

Perrine greeted him sweetly.

"Have you forgiven my youthful follies?" she asked.

"I remember nothing," said Carandas.

He lied with such calmness that she mistrusted him at
once.

During the following weeks he observed the house. No lock
escaped him; no servant's gossip fell unmeasured; no footprint in
the garden went unquestioned. One evening he saw Brother Anselme
leave by the little door, tying his girdle with more
haste than holiness. Another day he noted the resemblance between
the priest and the children. The boy had Anselme's eyes;
the girl had his chin; both made the same impatient
movement with the left hand.

Then Carandas rejoiced.

His old hate, which had slept twelve years like a
scorpion in a bottle, awoke, stretched all its claws, and
requested nourishment.

He went to Master Tardiveau.

"My friend," said he, "I have something bitter to tell
you."

"Then put sugar in it."

"Your wife deceives you."

"With whom? The baker?"

"With Brother Anselme."

The dyer laughed until his belly shook.

"You have brought foreign madness home. Anselme is her confessor."

"Precisely."

"He teaches my children."

"Precisely."

"He calls when I am absent."

"Most precisely."

Tardiveau ceased laughing.

Carandas then gave dates, hours, signs, and circumstances. He spoke
of the garden door, the loosened girdle, the maid bribed
with silver, the children's faces, the sudden abundance of church
errands. Each word entered the dyer's heart like a nail;
and Carandas, excellent mechanician, hammered them to the head.

"I will kill them," said Tardiveau.

"That is a husband's first thought."

"With my cleaver."

"Too vulgar."

"My hunting spear."

"Too long for a chamber."

"My sword."

"Your sword is rusty."

Carandas drew from beneath his cloak a short, narrow blade
in a black sheath. The steel shimmered blue along the
edge.

"This," he said, "was made at Brescia. A scratch from
it kills before a man can finish his Credo. The
blade is dressed with a poison drawn from vipers, toads,
and certain plants which grow only beneath a gallows."

Tardiveau recoiled.

"Is that Christian?"

"My friend, the question is not whether the sword is
Christian, but whether your wife is."

Carandas arranged the trap. Brother Anselme was expected the following
Thursday, when the dyer should supposedly visit his warehouse near
the bridge. Instead, Tardiveau slipped back through the dye-yard, where
crimson cloths hung like the tongues of executed giants. Carandas
conducted him up the private stair. The poisoned sword trembled
in his hand.

From behind the chamber door came laughter.

Tardiveau's face went purple, then white, then a colour known
only to betrayed husbands and badly dyed silk. He kicked
open the door.

Perrine stood beside the bed in a loose gown, her
hair unbound. Brother Anselme, in his shirt, held a cup
of wine. Neither appeared prepared for martyrdom.

"Villains!" roared Tardiveau. "Priest! Wife! I will send you together
to the judgment seat!"

Brother Anselme dropped the cup.

Perrine did not scream. She looked first at the sword,
then at Carandas behind her husband, and understood the whole
machinery of vengeance as readily as if its wheels had
been laid upon the table.

"Macé," she said, "before you strike, look at your children."

"What of them?"

"They are downstairs."

"What of them, adulteress?"

"If you kill this man," said Perrine, pointing to the
priest, "you kill the father of Étienne and Margot."

Silence fell.

It was not an ordinary silence, but a vast, cathedral
silence, with room in it for twelve years of marriage,
two baptisms, a hundred family dinners, a thousand paternal kisses,
and the sudden collapse of a fool's entire ancestry.

Tardiveau stared at the priest.

He saw Étienne's eyes.

He stared at Perrine.

He saw no repentance.

He stared at Carandas.

He saw triumph.

Then his hand opened.

The sword fell point downward upon Carandas's embroidered shoe, passed
through the leather, and pricked his great toe.

"Faugh!" cried Carandas. "Clumsy ox!"

He snatched up the sword, but already the poison had
entered. His toe burned; his leg stiffened; his hump shook;
his teeth locked. He attempted to curse Perrine, Tardiveau, the
priest, the sword-maker, and the science of venom, but accomplished
only a noise like a duck swallowed by a bagpipe.
He spun once, twice, clawed the curtains, overturned a stool,
and dropped dead upon the floor.

Thus did the engine-maker perish by his own engine, the
informer by his own information, and the spiteful man by
the exact point he had sharpened for others.

Master Tardiveau, deprived by astonishment of murder, sat heavily upon
the bed.

Brother Anselme crossed himself.

Perrine tied up her hair.

From below came the voices of the children calling, "Father!
Father!"

At this the dyer began to weep. Whether they called
him or the priest, he could not determine; and since
love sometimes survives truth by refusing to inspect it too
closely, he sent Brother Anselme away by the garden door,
forgave Perrine by degrees, and never again discussed noses, eyes,
chins, or family resemblances.

Carandas was buried with great ceremony, for he was rich,
and wealth purchases bells even for a corpse whom nobody
regrets. Upon his tomb they carved wheels, pulleys, and a
Latin sentence declaring him a man of uncommon genius. They
omitted the sewer, the linen chest, the priest, the poisoned
sword, and the toe, these being matters upon which Latin
is often discreet.

Perrine lived long, governed her household, married both children advantageously,
and gave annually a clean altar cloth to Saint-Gatien. Master
Tardiveau survived until seventy-eight, proud of his son, fond of
his daughter, and careful never to handle pointed instruments.

And Brother Anselme became a canon.

This teaches us not to be spiteful.

## OUR REMARKABLE FRIEND

Our bore is the best-hearted man alive. He would lend
us his umbrella in a thunderstorm, and then accompany us
beneath it to explain where he bought it, what he
paid for it, why the maker failed, how umbrellas were
formerly constructed, and what his brother Theodore—who has lived fourteen
years in the East—thinks of the monsoon. He may extinguish
a dinner-party, disperse a fireside, and render a railway carriage
uninhabitable; but he never intends offence. He puts twenty people
out of humour and preserves his own with the serenity
of a saint.

His face is smooth, mild, and permanently attentive to himself.
His smile is a fixed piece of furniture. His voice
has no more rise or fall than a turnpike road,
and advances at much the same pace. Nothing surprises him,
because everything reminds him of something he has seen before;
and nothing silences him, because silence itself recalls to him
a curious silence he once observed at Naples—or perhaps at
Nismes; he will ascertain which before he releases us.

We know the moment when he is about to begin.
He places one hand inside his waistcoat, inclines his head
with an air of pardoning our ignorance, and says:

"Very singular. That reminds me—"

It reminds him. Nothing ever reminds us.

Our bore has travelled. We do not say that he
has been abroad; many harmless persons have done that and
returned cured. He has travelled in the higher sense. He
has corrected guide-books, instructed couriers, exposed innkeepers, discovered roads, authenticated
ruins, and bestowed Europe upon his friends in portions suited
to their capacity.

Mention Italy, and he looks compassionate.

"Ah, Italy! You know the little church of Santa Maria
della Pace at Borgo San Felice?"

We do not.

"Not know it? My dear sir! Yet perhaps it is
better so. To see it improperly would be worse than
not to see it at all. You enter the town
by the eastern gate—the gate called western by Murray, a
most extraordinary error. You pass a fountain, dry when I
was there, though usually running; take the second turning—no, the
third; the second is opposite the saddler's; then proceed forty-seven
paces, allowing, of course, for the Italian pace, which differs
materially from ours. You perceive a green door."

We perceive nothing but eternity.

"Within that church," he continues, lowering his voice until it
becomes more audible throughout the room, "I discovered a picture."

The picture had hung there three hundred years; the priest
dusted it twice a week; the sacristan's children played beneath
it; and a German professor had published an octavo volume
concerning it. Nevertheless, our bore discovered it. It was a
masterpiece by an unknown hand, though he immediately recognised the
hand as that of an artist whose name he alone
suspected. The colouring combined Titian's splendour with Correggio's tenderness, while
the left knee of an attendant angel suggested a manner
not unlike—though immeasurably superior to—that of Paolo Veronese.

"I said to the sacristan, 'Amico'—friend—'do you know what you
possess?' He shrugged his shoulders. These Italians shrug wonderfully. 'No,'
I said, 'you do not know. Europe does not know.
I know.' He pressed my hand. I shall never forget
the man's expression."

We have often wished to see that expression.

Our bore had intended to purchase the picture for the
nation. Indeed, had the priest accepted the sum offered, had
the bishop consented, had the municipality waived its claim, had
the Austrian authorities not interfered, had our bore possessed the
necessary money, and had the picture been for sale, it
would at this moment adorn our National Gallery. As matters
stand, it remains at Borgo San Felice, where our bore
has generously left it for the education of mankind.

He is equally distinguished in Switzerland. No other traveller has
properly seen Switzerland, because all other travellers have gone where
roads, maps, post-horses, guides, and human prudence permitted them to
go. Our bore rejected these vulgar aids and discovered a
valley.

It happened after breakfast, on the 17 th of August—he
thinks the 17 th, because Callow joined him on the
16 th, and Callow had lost his blue spectacles the
previous evening, unless that occurred at Thun, which would make
it the 18 th. No matter. It was about ten
minutes past nine by his watch, though local time was
seven minutes in advance.

"I said to my guide, 'What lies beyond that ridge?'
He replied, 'Nothing.' 'Nothing,' I repeated; 'then there I go.'
He implored me not to attempt it. I smiled. I
have always held that determination, sir, is the Englishman's true
passport."

After four hours' climbing, three hours' descending, a fall into
a torrent, the loss of one boot, and a quarrel
with the guide, he reached a valley so beautiful that
no language could describe it. He therefore describes it for
forty minutes.

There were pines above, meadows below, snow-peaks in front, a
waterfall behind, cattle on one side, goats on the other,
and a cottage in the centre. A young girl emerged
from the cottage carrying a wooden bowl. Her hair was
braided; her bodice was crimson; her eyes were either blue
or dark hazel—our bore inclines to blue, though atmospheric reflection
may have influenced them. She spoke a dialect wholly unknown
to him, but he understood at once that no Englishman
had ever entered the valley before.

"'Du bist der erste,' she said. That is, 'You are
the first.' I answered, 'Ich bin Engländer'—I am an Englishman.
She clasped her hands."

We should have done the same.

Our bore remained in this unequalled paradise for twenty-three minutes
and spent the next six weeks in a pension at
Geneva, suffering from fever, bruises, rheumatism, and the consequences of
wet boots. The valley has since appeared in several illustrated
annuals, under various names; but none gives its true position,
which he cannot disclose, as he considers himself bound in
honour to the young woman with the bowl.

Our bore is not merely a traveller; he is an
authority upon foreign affairs. This is owing principally to his
brother Theodore, who is "in the East." The East is
a large district, beginning, for conversational purposes, at Vienna and
extending indefinitely beyond Canton. Theodore's precise occupation is uncertain. At
one time we understand him to be connected with the
diplomatic service; at another, with cotton; later, with a highly
confidential mission respecting the navigation of a river whose name
cannot be mentioned. Whatever his station, he knows everything before
it happens and communicates it to our bore after it
has appeared in the newspapers.

If Russia is discussed, our bore says:

"My brother Theodore wrote to me on that subject by
the last overland mail. His words were remarkable: 'Keep your
eye on Russia.'"

If Persia is named:

"Theodore knows the Shah intimately—not socially, you understand, but in
a manner far more useful. He once said to me,
'Depend upon it, Persia is the key.' I will not
repeat the rest."

If somebody ventures upon Turkey:

"You may take it from me that the Sultan's position
is delicate. Theodore could explain why, but there are family
reasons—state reasons, I may almost say—which forbid me to enter
further into it."

He enters further into it for an hour.

We once attempted to confuse him by introducing Peru. He
paused only long enough to pity our simplicity.

"Peru," he said, "cannot be understood without reference to the
East."

Theodore had foreseen Peru.

Our bore has also enjoyed a remarkable proximity to history.
Great events appear to wait until he is near and
then occur, sometimes immediately after he has gone away. He
saw William IV. Not merely in procession, as thousands may
have seen that sovereign, but under circumstances which gave the
incident a private and almost constitutional character.

"I was standing near the corner of St James's Street—on
the left as you descend, not the right, as Jilkins
absurdly insists—when a carriage approached. Something in the coachman's manner
attracted me. I said, 'That is no ordinary carriage.' At
that instant the King turned his head and looked directly
at me. There was recognition in the look—not personal recognition,
of course, but that broad human recognition which passes between
an English monarch and an English gentleman. I raised my
hat. His Majesty moved two fingers."

Whether the King intended to move them, whether the movement
was caused by the jolting of the carriage, and whether
His Majesty saw our bore at all are questions beneath
discussion. Our bore met William IV.

He saw the Houses of Parliament, too, on the very
day before they burned. This circumstance has grown more solemn
with every repetition. At first he merely crossed Westminster Bridge.
Next, he observed smoke about a chimney. Later, he drew
a companion's attention to a peculiar odour. Now, he remembers
laying one hand upon the railings and saying:

"Mark my words, Clatter, there is danger here."

Clatter laughed. The building burned the following evening. Our bore
has never blamed Clatter, but he cannot acquit him.

All his voyages have been unprecedented. If calm, no vessel
ever lay so motionless. If rough, the captain confessed that
in thirty-seven years he had never seen such weather. On
one passage the waves were mountains; on another the fog
was a wall; on a third the heat melted the
pitch in the seams and caused a canary belonging to
a widow from Bath to lose its voice. He alone
retained perfect composure, advised the captain, reassured the ladies, distributed
lemons, and noticed that the second mate had miscalculated their
longitude.

Every speed he has attained is the greatest known, and
every delay the longest recorded. The mail-coach once carried him
from York to London in a time which the guard
declared impossible; yet on another occasion he remained nine hours
between two stations not eleven miles apart. He has crossed
the Channel in fifty-eight minutes and in nineteen hours. He
has been in a train which ran faster than human
nerves could support, and in another which moved so slowly
that he left it, gathered blackberries, and overtook it at
the next cutting.

Our bore has witnessed the finest performance ever given upon
the English stage, though nobody now living remembers the actor.
He has also endured the worst performance, though several persons
present applauded it out of ignorance. He heard Paganini from
a seat immediately behind a pillar and detected subtleties lost
upon the visible part of the audience. He saw a
horse leap a wider ditch than any horse ever leaped
before or since; a balloon rise in a wind pronounced
fatal by scientific men; a prize ox whose dimensions were
suppressed from motives of jealousy; and an eclipse which, though
mentioned in the almanacs, was totally misunderstood until he explained
it to a clergyman standing near.

It is dangerous to speak of weather before our bore.
He remembers the frost of a particular winter when wine
froze in his bedroom, though no other liquid did; the
summer when sparrows fell exhausted at his feet; the shower
in which drops rebounded three feet from the pavement; and
a thunderclap that stopped his watch at fourteen minutes past
four.

"It was not the violence of the sound," he explains.
"It was the electrical character. I mentioned the fact to
Professor Moon, who said, 'My dear fellow, this is important.'"

Professor Moon may never have held a professorship, but he
has served our bore long and faithfully.

Of all subjects, however, health is the most perilous. A
sneeze may cost us the evening. If any rash person
says that he has a headache, our bore looks grave,
asks whether the pain inclines from right to left or
left to right, and begins the history of his own
illness in the spring of 1832.

It began with a sensation—not pain, precisely, but a sensation—beneath
the third button of his waistcoat. He disregarded it. On
Tuesday it returned. Wednesday it disappeared, which alarmed him more
than its return would have done. Thursday he ate whitebait.
Friday he consulted Sir Caleb Purge.

"Sir Caleb heard my account and said, 'Liver.' I said,
'Sir Caleb, I respect your experience, but it is not
liver.' He smiled in the manner of men accustomed to
obedience. 'My dear sir,' he said, 'allow me to judge.'
'Certainly,' I replied, 'when you have heard the facts.'"

The facts occupied two visits. Sir Caleb prescribed mercury, mustard,
abstinence, and a sea voyage. These produced six entirely new
symptoms and strengthened our bore's conviction that the liver had
been slandered.

He next consulted Dr Blister, who pronounced it nerves; Dr
Sponge, who called it suppressed gout; Dr Holloway, who discovered
an affection of the spine; and a celebrated German practitioner
who said "Ach!" four times, wrote a prescription in Latin,
and charged five guineas. One physician forbade coffee; another ordered
it. One removed red meat; another insisted upon raw beef.
He was bled, steamed, packed in wet sheets, immersed in
mineral waters, swung by the feet, and fed for ten
days upon biscuits softened in rainwater.

During this period he kept a diary. He can produce
it.

"April the ninth: slept three hours and seventeen minutes. Pulse
at six, seventy-two; at eight, seventy-four; at ten, apparently sixty-nine,
though the watch may have gained. Tongue coated toward the
centre. Spirits tolerable before breakfast, depressed after marmalade. Curious sensation
in left thumb at eleven twenty-three."

If we exhibit sympathy, he reads onward. If we do
not, he reads onward to convince us.

After two years of medical persecution, he was cured by
an obscure practitioner named Snaffle, who lived over a harness-maker
in a narrow street near Soho Square.

"Snaffle looked at me—simply looked. 'You have been badly treated,'
he said. Those were his first words. 'Doctor,' I replied,
'you understand me.' 'Eat a mutton chop,' said he, 'and
drink two glasses of dry sherry.' I did so. From
that hour I recovered."

Our bore relates this triumph immediately after warning us that
no man should prescribe for another without knowing his constitution.

He has advice for every emergency. If we are choosing
a house, he knows the exact district and the only
honest surveyor. If we have chosen it, he knows why
the soil is damp, the drainage defective, the neighbourhood declining,
and the title insecure. If we employ his surveyor, that
gentleman is unfortunately less competent than when our bore knew
him. If we do not, our misfortunes prove the consequence.

He advises us about marriage after we are engaged, education
after our children are grown, and investments after the company
has failed. His counsel commonly takes the form of having
urged upon some absent person, many years ago, the precise
course which present circumstances now show to have been desirable.

"I said to young Snugglewood, 'Never speculate in Peruvian copper.'
He laughed. You know what happened."

We do not know Snugglewood, young or old, and have
never heard of his copper; but we feel he deserved
it.

Our bore is great in private information. He knows facts
which cannot be repeated and repeats everything except the fact.
He takes us aside in a crowded room and secures
secrecy by speaking more loudly.

"There is a matter in progress," he says, "which may
alter the whole complexion of affairs. Do not ask me
what. I could tell you, but my mouth is closed."

We do not ask him.

"You will observe," he continues, "that I mention no names.
A certain personage—placed high, very high—sent for an individual with
whom I am not wholly unconnected. What passed I must
not reveal. This much only: when Parliament meets, watch the
Treasury bench."

We watch it. Nothing happens.

"That is precisely the point," says our bore. "The thing
has been postponed."

He knows who wrote the anonymous pamphlet, why Lady Dwindle
left town suddenly, what really occurred at the Admiralty, and
which eminent statesman is about to resign. The pamphlet was
written by someone else, Lady Dwindle returns explaining that her
aunt was ill, nothing occurred at the Admiralty, and the
statesman remains in office. Our bore is never discredited. The
pamphlet was designed to mislead; Lady Dwindle's explanation was arranged;
the Admiralty matter has been "hushed up"; and the statesman
stayed only because our bore's intelligence reached the proper quarter.

He is particularly impressive concerning mysteries that everybody else has
forgotten.

"You remember the affair of the green dispatch-box?"

Nobody remembers it.

"Just so. It was intended that nobody should."

Our bore is also great in argument. Not that he
argues to arrive at truth; he arrives with truth in
his pocket and argues to prevent us from escaping. He
begins by misunderstanding our statement, proceeds by correcting words we
did not use, and concludes by demanding that we prove
an opinion we never expressed.

If we say that the morning is pleasant, he answers:

"Pleasant? That depends upon what you mean by pleasant."

We mean pleasant.

"Exactly; but is mere absence of rain pleasantness? Surely not.
You must distinguish between meteorological conditions and personal impressions."

We distinguish by walking away. He accompanies us.

He has never surrendered a point. He merely postpones it
until we are too weak to recognise it. Contradiction feeds
him; agreement stimulates him to explain why we agree; silence
he interprets as profound interest. There is no defence.

Once, at dinner, Mr Pibble observed that French cooks used
garlic too freely. Our bore replied that the assertion required
qualification. Pibble qualified it. Our bore objected to the qualification.
Pibble withdrew the statement. Our bore insisted that a statement
once made could not be withdrawn without examination. At half-past
eleven Pibble confessed that French cooks never used garlic, had
never heard of garlic, and that garlic itself was a
Protestant invention. Our bore shook his hand and congratulated him
upon approaching the subject in a reasonable spirit.

Yet Providence, which tempers even calamities, has endowed our bore
with a remarkable power of recognising his own species. Let
another bore enter the room, and our bore brightens. Across
the heads of ordinary mortals there passes between them a
secret beam. They approach. They fasten. We are free.

We witnessed one such meeting between our bore and Colonel
Clatter, a gentleman celebrated for having spent eighteen months in
Malta and thirty years describing them. The Colonel mentioned the
harbour. Our bore corrected the prevailing account of its depth.
The Colonel appealed to a naval officer long dead. Our
bore cited Theodore. The Colonel produced a memorandum. Our bore
distrusted the handwriting. They began at nine in the evening
and were still disputing at one in the morning whether
a particular warehouse stood six yards east or seven yards
north of a landing-place demolished before either had visited the
island.

Neither listened. Both spoke. It was magnificent.

At breakfast our bore appeared refreshed.

"That Colonel Clatter," he said, "is a most intelligent man."

We have observed that this is his highest praise. He
bestows it upon Professor Moon, who explains eclipses to him;
upon Jilkins, who disputes the route of royal carriages; upon
Callow, who remembers dates incorrectly but at length; and upon
every gentleman who can detain him through two consecutive meals.
Ordinary people he calls agreeable. Silent people he considers sadly
reserved. Those who escape him are superficial.

There are times when we reproach ourselves for our impatience.
Our bore is generous, punctual, loyal, and incapable of a
mean action. He remembers our birthdays, though he also remembers
what the weather was upon each of them. He visits
us when we are ill, though recovery is delayed by
the visit. He would sit beside a dying friend all
night and relate, in perfect detail, the death of another
friend who had suffered from a complaint not altogether dissimilar,
though complicated in that instance by circumstances which it would
be necessary to trace back to the year 1819.

We honour his heart. We avoid his chair.

Perhaps we are unjust. Perhaps the world requires such men,
as forests require insects and libraries catalogues. They preserve forgotten
dates, unimportant roads, superseded remedies, the names of second-rate inns,
and the exact words of conversations that never took place.
They fill those vacancies in social life which might otherwise
be occupied by thought. They make solitude attractive and teach
us the value of a locked door.

Our bore, for his part, remains content. He suspects no
flight, observes no yawn, hears no clock, and perceives no
despair. When the company has dwindled from twelve to two,
he assumes that the others have withdrawn from reverence. When
the remaining listener falls asleep, he lowers his voice out
of consideration and continues. At dawn he departs, convinced that
he has passed a delightful evening among unusually receptive minds.

He has called many men intelligent.

This has sometimes troubled us; for, considering the persons upon
whom he confers the distinction, we should be sorry to
discover that we possessed it.

Happily, though he has known us for years, travelled with
us, advised us, prescribed for us, trusted us with secrets,
corrected our opinions, and narrated to us every remarkable event
of his remarkable existence, our bore has never once called
us intelligent.

We take the omission as a compliment.

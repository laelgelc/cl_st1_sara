### THE BETTER GOVERNMENT

"John Thomas!"

No answer followed this summons, although the voice in which
it was uttered had sufficient sharpness to penetrate every apartment
in the house.

"John Thomas!" repeated Mr. Belknap, more loudly than before.

A chair was heard to move in the adjoining room;
then came the slow, unwilling tread of feet, and, after
a delay that seemed purposely prolonged, a boy of twelve
years entered. His countenance was clouded, his brows slightly knitted,
and his eyes rested upon the floor.

"Did n't you hear me call?" demanded his father.

"Yes, sir."

"Then why did n't you come?"

"I was coming."

"You were coming! A pretty way of coming, truly. When
I call, I expect you to come at once. Do
you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

There was nothing openly disrespectful in the boy's words; yet
his manner was so sullen, and the monosyllables fell so
reluctantly from his lips, that Mr. Belknap felt his impatience
rapidly increasing.

"I want you to go into the shed and bring
me the hammer and a dozen small nails. Go at
once; and do n't be all day about it."

John Thomas turned without speaking.

"Stop!" said his father. "Where is your jacket?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know! Of course you don't. You never know
where anything is. Look for it."

The boy moved toward the hat-rack, and, with an air
of dull indifference, examined the pegs.

"It is n't there," said he.

"Look under your cap."

John lifted the cap; but the jacket was not beneath
it.

"Did you leave it in the kitchen?"

"I don't know."

"Then go and see. Move briskly, John Thomas! It tries
my patience beyond endurance to witness such laziness."

The boy departed, though with no increase of speed. Mr.
Belknap took up the newspaper which had lain across his
knee; but he did not resume its perusal. His eyes
followed his son until the latter disappeared, and a severe
expression remained upon his countenance.

"Of all boys," said he, speaking partly to himself and
partly to his sister Mary, who sat by the window
with her knitting, "John Thomas is the most provoking. There
is no life in him except when he is bent
on mischief. Ask him to do a useful thing, and
he becomes as stupid as a post."

Aunt Mary made no immediate reply. She drew the yarn
gently through her fingers, while a shade of concern passed
over her mild face.

Presently John returned without his jacket.

"Well?" said Mr. Belknap.

"I could n't find it."

"Could n't find it! Did you look?"

"Yes, sir."

"Where?"

"In the kitchen."

"Did you ask Bridget?"

"No, sir."

"Why did n't you?"

John was silent.

"Answer me!"

"I don't know."

"Go and ask her. And straighten out your face! I
will not have you look so sulky whenever you are
told to do anything."

A deeper flush came into the boy's cheeks. He turned
again, and passed from the room. Like a soulless automaton
passed he, moving because an external will compelled him, but
with no cheerful impulse from within. Fear may force the
limbs to obedience; it cannot make the heart go willingly
upon an errand.

After another delay, John reappeared with his jacket hanging over
one arm.

"Where was it?"

"Bridget had put it in the closet."

"And I suppose you passed the closet twenty times. Put
it on. Now go for the hammer and nails; and,
mind, if you are gone more than five minutes, I
shall know the reason why."

John thrust his arms into the sleeves, took his cap,
and went out. The door closed somewhat heavily behind him.

Mr. Belknap's anger rose at the sound.

"There!" he exclaimed. "You heard that, Mary. He slammed the
door in my face."

"I heard the door close," replied Aunt Mary, calmly.

"And do you not call that insolence?"

"I am not prepared to say that he intended it."

"You always excuse him. In your eyes, the boy can
do no wrong."

"No, brother; John Thomas has faults, and some of them
are serious. But I sometimes fear that we mistake the
effect for the cause."

Mr. Belknap looked at her with an impatient contraction of
the brow.

"What do you mean by that?"

Aunt Mary let her knitting fall into her lap. There
was a gentle earnestness in her eyes; goodness looked therefrom
as an angel looks from some clear window of heaven.

"May I speak freely?"

"Certainly. Though I suppose I am to hear that the
whole blame rests upon me."

"Not the whole blame," she answered. "Yet I think you
might do much toward removing it. John is proud and
sensitive. A harsh word wounds him; repeated harshness causes him
to hide the wound under sullenness. You command him as
if obedience were to be beaten out of his nature."

"And should not a child obey his father?"

"Assuredly. But there are two kinds of obedience. One is
yielded by fear, slowly and with a rebellious spirit; the
other springs from affection, and is prompt because the heart
goes with the hand."

"Fine theory!" said Mr. Belknap. "But boys are not governed
by theories."

"They are governed by feelings, brother. You have tried authority,
displeasure, and threats. Have they made John more willing?"

Mr. Belknap did not answer.

"Try kindness," continued Aunt Mary. "Speak to his better nature.
Let him see that you expect good from him, not
evil. There is an old line which I wish you
would remember: 'For love hath readier will than fear.'"

The sentence was uttered without emphasis; but it found a
place in Mr. Belknap's thoughts. He turned toward the window
and remained silent.

Aunt Mary resumed her knitting.

"Children," she said, after a pause, "are like the plants
in your garden. Sunshine calls forth the bud, and gentle
rain nourishes it; but a continual beating storm bows the
tender stem to earth. If we would have good qualities
bloom, we must cultivate them. Neglected flowers perish, while weeds—those
inherent evil tendencies of our nature—need little invitation to flourish."

"You would make me believe," said Mr. Belknap, though less
confidently, "that John's incorrigible temper is of my producing."

"I would have you examine whether your manner does not
increase it. Before a father can govern his child wisely,
he must learn to govern himself."

This touched Mr. Belknap more nearly than all that had
preceded it. He felt the truth, though self-love resisted its
admission. During the last half-hour he had spoken no sentence
to his son that was not edged with censure. He
had looked for reluctance, and had found it; expected forgetfulness,
and almost commanded it by confusing the boy with repeated
accusations. Had he been treated thus by one possessing authority
over him, would his own bosom have remained free from
resentment?

Self-examination is rarely agreeable in the outset; yet, if honestly
pursued, it leads us toward wisdom. Mr. Belknap saw, though
dimly at first, that the child's faults had been magnified
by unwise treatment. He remembered occasions when John had approached
him with an open countenance, only to be repelled by
coldness; occasions when the boy had attempted some little service,
and received, instead of approval, a complaint that it had
not been better performed. Thus had affection, seeking entrance, found
the door shut.

His reflections were interrupted by the return of John Thomas.
The boy entered more slowly than he had gone out.
In one hand he held a paper containing nails; the
other was empty.

"Well, sir," said Mr. Belknap, the old impatience instantly stirring,
"where is the hammer?"

John stopped. A startled look passed over his face; then
his eyes fell.

"I forgot it."

"You forgot it!"

The angry words were already upon the father's tongue. His
hand made a sudden movement, and, for an instant, the
impulse to seize and shake the offender was strong within
him. But Aunt Mary's maxim came back:

"For love hath readier will than fear."

The father must govern himself before he can govern his
son.

By a determined effort, Mr. Belknap restrained his anger. When
he spoke, his voice was calm, though grave.

"I am sorry, John. I wanted the hammer particularly, and
thought you would remember it."

The boy looked up. He had expected reproach, perhaps punishment;
but he saw neither anger nor contempt in his father's
face. Calm disappointment was there, and it smote him more
keenly than violence could have done.

"I am sorry, father," he said; and the words came
from his heart. "I will get it."

Before Mr. Belknap could reply, John was at the door.

"You need not run," said his father, kindly.

But the boy had already bounded away. The heavy, mechanical
tread was gone. Affection had touched the spring of action,
and every limb responded. In less than half the time
formerly consumed in looking for his jacket, he returned with
the hammer.

"Here it is, father."

"Thank you, John," said Mr. Belknap. "That was quickly done."

A glow came into the child's cheeks. His eyes, so
lately dull and evasive, sparkled with pleasure. A single word
of commendation had awakened more life than a score of
threats.

"Is there anything else you want?" he asked.

Mr. Belknap glanced toward Aunt Mary. She did not lift
her eyes from her work; but a quiet smile rested
upon her lips.

"Yes," replied he. "If it is not asking too much,
I should like you to take this note to Mr.
Wilson. You may go when it suits you."

"I can go now," said John, eagerly.

"Very well. Put on your jacket carefully; it is cold
outside."

The boy obeyed. There was no delay, no knitted brow,
no reluctant "Yes, sir." He placed the note securely in
his pocket, adjusted his cap, and stood for a moment
near his father.

"I'll be back soon."

"I know you will," answered Mr. Belknap.

John went out, closing the door gently behind him.

For several moments the father remained silent. His eyes were
fixed upon the place where his son had stood. The
nails and hammer lay before him, but he seemed to
have forgotten the work for which they were required.

"Well, brother?" said Aunt Mary, at length.

"Yes—yes—yes," he answered, slowly. "You were right."

"I am glad that you have proved it so soon."

"So soon!" repeated Mr. Belknap. "Mary, the change seemed like
magic. Yet it was no magic. I had been pressing
down the very qualities I wished to raise. I called
him stubborn, and treated him as though stubbornness were the
only thing in him; while one kind sentence brought out
regret, promptness, and good-will."

"Love is a fire that melts," said Aunt Mary; "fear
is a hammer that strikes and hardens."

Mr. Belknap's countenance became thoughtful.

"How much harm may I already have done? I have
complained that the boy possessed no affection, when I gave
his affection no sunshine. I have demanded confidence, yet met
his little errors with anger. I have desired cheerful obedience,
and taken away every motive that could make obedience cheerful."

"Do not waste the present in unavailing regret," replied Aunt
Mary. "The garden is not ruined because some flowers have
been bent. Lift them gently; give them warmth; guard them
from the storm; and they may yet grow in beauty."

At this moment quick steps sounded upon the walk. John
Thomas had performed his second errand with a willing heart,
and was already returning.

Mr. Belknap listened, and a tenderness to which he had
too long been a stranger moved within his bosom.

"I will try," said he. "I have sought to conquer
my child; henceforth I will seek to win him."

And he did try. Not always successfully, for old habits
are not cast aside in an hour; but when impatience
arose, he remembered that self-government must precede all righteous authority.
He learned to distinguish between firmness and harshness, between correction
and humiliation. In proportion as his manner changed, the better
qualities of John Thomas appeared—like flowers long hidden beneath a
chilling cloud, but opening when the sunshine came.

Thus, in the familiar occurrences of one household, was verified
a truth which pride is slow to learn, but which
experience writes plainly upon every human heart: coercion may command
the body, yet affection alone secures the willing service of
the soul.

"For love hath readier will than fear."

At three minutes past four on a Thursday afternoon Mr
Sandbach and Mr Gale stood beneath the glass roof of
Bursley railway station, each waiting for a train which neither
particularly desired to catch. They were manufacturers of small but
indispensable articles in brass, prosperous in a moderate fashion, unmarried
by conviction, and friends by virtue of a sustained inability
to agree upon anything.

A friendship of twenty years may rest on affection, habit,
mutual esteem, or the fortunate possession of opposite political opinions.
Theirs rested chiefly on wagers. They bet on weather, trains,
municipal elections, the precise age of strangers, and the capacity
of waiters to overlook an empty cup. The stakes were
seldom high; but the moral results were tremendous.

Mr Sandbach, who was broad, deliberate and inclined to regard
his own conclusions as natural laws, had bought the evening
newspaper. Mr Gale, a narrow, active man with a grey
moustache of an argumentative turn, had refused to buy it
on the ground that Sandbach would presently lend it to
him.

"You're mistaken," said Mr Sandbach.

"We shall see," said Mr Gale.

They were still seeing when a local train entered the
station with the noisy solicitude of an excitable mother dragging
an inoffensive child. Porters shouted, doors collided, and three commercial
travellers descended as though the Five Towns had been waiting
all day for them. Amid this earthly disturbance one figure
remained absolutely motionless.

He stood near the bookstall, his right foot planted upon
the platform, his left a little behind it. He was
a thin, shabby man in a greenish coat polished at
the elbows. His cap had lost its original opinion concerning
shape; his boots appeared to have been constructed during different
administrations. Yet his attitude possessed dignity. He did not lean,
shuffle, cough, read the placards or examine the photographs of
seaside resorts. He merely stood.

Mr Gale noticed him first.

"Queer customer," he said.

Mr Sandbach lowered the newspaper.

"What's queer about him?"

"He hasn't moved for five minutes."

"Perhaps he doesn't want to."

"Everybody wants to move sooner or later."

Mr Sandbach regarded his friend with benevolent contempt.

"You couldn't make him move."

"Couldn't I?"

"Not without touching him, speaking to him, or getting anybody
else to interfere."

Mr Gale's nostrils twitched with the scent of battle.

"How far?"

"Ten yards."

"In what time?"

"Before my train goes."

Mr Sandbach's train was announced for four twenty-seven. There remained
eleven minutes.

"What do you bet?"

"Half-a-crown."

"Done."

Now Mr Gale possessed ingenuity, though, like many ingenious men,
he used it chiefly in circumstances where straightforward conduct would
have been cheaper. He walked past the shabby man, took
a half-crown from his pocket, and, with an artfully complete
imitation of carelessness, dropped it within two feet of the
stationary boot.

The coin rang brightly upon the flags.

The shabby man looked down.

Mr Gale continued three paces, turned, and gazed towards the
refreshment-room with the air of one whose thoughts concerned nothing
less material than eternity. Mr Sandbach hid his interest behind
the newspaper.

The shabby man surveyed the half-crown. He then glanced at
Mr Gale, at Mr Sandbach, at the girl in the
bookstall, and finally at his own right boot. But he
did not move.

A minute passed.

The half-crown lay exposed to civilisation, and civilisation ignored it.
A porter almost trod upon it; a boy perceived it,
hesitated beneath the eye of his mother, and was swept
onward into virtue. The shabby man remained fixed.

At length Mr Gale returned, picked up the coin and
put it in his pocket.

"Well?" said Mr Sandbach.

"There are other methods."

"No doubt. Napoleon had other methods after Moscow."

"What's the game?" Gale demanded. "You know something."

Mr Sandbach folded the paper. The moment for superiority had
arrived.

"I observed him before you did. A young woman bought
a magazine at the stall. As she opened her purse,
something fell. That fellow put his foot on it. The
clerk saw him stoop, so he daren't pick it up
while she's there. The clerk hasn't left the stall since."

"What fell?"

"Half-a-sovereign."

"How do you know?"

"I saw the edge."

Mr Gale examined the shabby man anew. Much in human
behaviour which had appeared mysterious became instantly plain. The rigidity
was not obstinacy but policy; the planted boot was a
fortress, and beneath it lay treasure. Mr Gale's mind, trained
by years of calculating costs in sixteenths of a penny,
worked rapidly.

"You admit, then, that he won't move?"

"Not while the clerk's looking."

"And the bet stands?"

"Certainly."

"Half-a-crown that I make him go ten yards, without touching
him, speaking to him, or employing a third person?"

"Precisely."

Mr Gale smiled. It was a smile that had caused
uneasiness among suppliers of sheet brass.

From his waistcoat pocket he took a half-sovereign. He displayed
it upon his palm, taking care that the shabby man
should see both the coin and the absence of any
complicated weapon. Then he pointed to the far end of
the platform.

The shabby man's eyes sharpened.

Mr Gale pointed again: first to the coin, then to
the distant luggage office. He made a small walking movement
with two fingers. No word was spoken. The proposal, though
dumb, had the lucidity of a legal document.

For five seconds the shabby man considered the interference of
fate in his professional affairs. Beneath his boot, according to
Mr Gale's calculation, lay ten shillings obtainable after an uncertain
delay and at some risk. In Mr Gale's hand lay
ten shillings obtainable immediately and without labour. He made the
only decision compatible with sound business principles.

He took the half-sovereign.

Then he walked away.

"One, two, three," counted Mr Gale.

At the tenth yard the shabby man did not stop.
He went through the barrier, descended the station approach and
passed eternally out of the lives of the other two
excellent individuals.

Mr Gale held out his hand.

"Half-a-crown."

Mr Sandbach paid. His expression was grave, for he perceived
that brilliance had occurred in another person.

Gale sprang to the deserted spot. Sandbach followed, less quickly
but with equal spiritual concentration. They stared at the flags.

There was no half-sovereign.

Mr Gale bent down. He rubbed the platform with his
glove. He examined a crack, a cigarette end, and a
spot of ancient grease. Nothing resembling gold revealed itself.

"Where is it?" he said.

"How should I know?"

"You said it was under his foot."

"It was."

"It isn't now."

"Evidently."

Mr Gale rose. "You've done me down."

Mr Sandbach's eyebrows ascended. "I?"

"You knew there was nothing there. You invented the tale
so that I'd give that loafer ten shillings."

"My dear Gale, why should I desire to enrich a
loafer at your expense?"

"Because you're that sort."

This was severe, but friendship has privileges denied to ordinary
malice.

Mr Sandbach tapped the folded newspaper against his palm. "Let
us keep to figures. You offered a wager of half-a-crown.
You won it. Your method happened to cost ten shillings.
Net loss, seven-and-six. That is your affair."

"It is not my affair if the conditions were fraudulent."

"The conditions were yours."

"The information was yours."

"The idiocy was yours."

For a moment the friendship of twenty years hung by
a thread. Mr Gale accused Mr Sandbach of conspiracy, misrepresentation
and conduct unbecoming a gentleman engaged in brass. Mr Sandbach
replied that Gale's vanity had merely received an expensive but
inadequate education. Their voices rose. The bookstall clerk looked across
with the cool interest of one to whom other people's
quarrels furnish refreshment without charge.

At that instant the shabby man reappeared at the barrier.

He did not reappear willingly. A vigilant inspector had discovered
that his platform ticket, besides being yesterday's, belonged to another
station. He was escorted upward, admonished, and turned towards the
booking office. Seeing Mr Gale, he attempted a change of
direction; the inspector corrected it.

As the shabby man hurried past, something glittered near his
right boot. There was a tiny metallic sound, and a
gold coin rolled across the flags.

Mr Sandbach and Mr Gale made a superb and undignified
rush.

Sandbach secured it by the advantage of weight. Gale secured
Sandbach's wrist by the advantage of desperation. The shabby man,
observing that his earlier contract contained no clause requiring him
to remain for an audit, vanished down the stairs. This
time destiny did not return him.

"There!" cried Sandbach. "What did I tell you?"

"It's mine," said Gale.

"Yours?"

"I paid him ten shillings to leave it."

"You paid him to walk ten yards."

"The coin was the understood consideration."

"There was no mention of the coin."

"There was no mention of anything! We didn't speak."

"Exactly. You cannot claim property under a silent agreement which,
on your own showing, omitted the property."

Mr Gale tightened his grip. "Hand it over."

"Remove your hand."

"Hand it over."

They stood wrist to wrist, two respected manufacturers with a
combined annual turnover of some magnitude, struggling beside a bookstall
for possession of a coin neither had owned ten minutes
earlier. Respectability, being largely a matter of witnesses, was fortunate
that most passengers had departed.

"Excuse me," said a voice.

They separated.

A young woman stood before them. She was neat, composed
and sufficiently pretty to make both bachelors assume, in one
miraculous instant, the manners of ambassadors. She held a magazine
and an open purse.

"I believe you have found a half-sovereign."

Mr Sandbach closed his fingers round the coin. "Possibly. Can
you identify it?"

"It is dated 1897, and there is a little scratched
M beside the Queen's neck. My father marked it years
ago. I dropped it while buying this magazine."

Sandbach inspected the coin. The date was 1897; the M,
though minute, was fatal.

"My dear young lady," he said, with a bow that
transformed restitution into philanthropy, "I am delighted to restore it."

"How very kind!"

"Not at all."

"Not in the least," added Gale, who had restored nothing.

"I was afraid I should have to explain the loss
to my husband," she continued. "He is waiting in the
cab outside."

The two gentlemen's sudden ambassadorial radiance diminished, though their courtesy
remained unimpeachable. She thanked them again and departed with the
coin, the magazine and the brief romantic possibilities of the
afternoon.

Mr Sandbach watched her go.

"Well," he said, "that settles ownership."

"It settles nothing," said Gale. "I am out seven-and-six."

"You won half-a-crown from me."

"And paid ten shillings to move the man on the
strength of your information."

"My information was accurate."

"It was incomplete. You never said the coin was stuck
in a crack in his boot."

"How could I know that?"

"You professed to understand the whole affair."

"I understood enough to win the moral issue."

"You lost the bet."

"Through an extravagant technicality."

The four twenty-seven had departed during the crisis, unnoticed by
Mr Sandbach; Mr Gale's train, with impartial malice, had also
gone. They therefore entered the refreshment-room, where Gale ordered tea
and Sandbach ordered nothing, declaring that tea at a railway
station was an offence against both commerce and digestion.

For another forty minutes they reconstructed the transaction. Sandbach maintained
that Gale's loss amounted to seven-and-six, voluntarily incurred. Gale maintained
that Sandbach, having supplied the intelligence upon which the operation
was founded, was liable for half the expenditure, less the
wager, plus the price of two missed trains. Sandbach replied
that no earthly system of accounts could charge a man
for trains he had not intended to catch. Gale then
introduced the half-crown originally dropped as evidence of preliminary expense;
Sandbach pointed out that it had been recovered. Gale charged
him threepence for half the tea.

At last they went out together into the smoky Bursley
evening, each sustained by the certainty that the other had
behaved disgracefully. The shabby man had ten shillings; the young
woman had her property; the railway company had two unused
opportunities of transport; and the friendship remained precisely what it
had always been—injured beyond repair, and perfectly secure.

At the corner Mr Gale said, "You owe me three-and-nine."

Mr Sandbach buttoned his coat.

"That's like you."

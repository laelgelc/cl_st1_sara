# THE COACHMAN OF CANTONMENT ROAD

> When a man cannot salute his conscience, he commonly
tries to outflank it.
> —Barrack-Room Maxim

The regimental ball had shone, spun, and thundered itself into
the small hours. By two o'clock the married ladies had
withdrawn, carrying with them their daughters, their fans, and most
of the proprieties. By three, the band was playing for
its own honour; subalterns danced with one another or with
chairs; and the supper-room looked as though a troop of
cavalry had charged through a confectioner's shop.

Mulvaney found me behind the carriage-shed.

He wore a scarlet mess-jacket of uncertain ownership, a paper
rose above his left ear, and the expression of a
man who has rendered valuable public service without official recognition.

"Come away," said he. "The festivities is degineratin' into noise,
an' I have secured the rations."

On the roof of a hired landau sat Ortheris, embracing
a champagne bottle. Between his boots were a cold fowl,
half a ham, six rolls, two pyramids of jelly in
a silver dish, and a cake bearing the arms of
the regiment in pink sugar.

"Stole it," said Ortheris cheerfully. "'E stole the lot. I
only stole the fizz."

"Borrowed," said Mulvaney, ascending. "Borrowed from the surplush av splendour.
'Tis no theft to remove food from them that cannot
stand upright to ate ut."

We settled upon the roof. Below us the pony slept
between the shafts, occasionally twitching an ear when the band
struck some unusually violent note. Lanterns burned among the trees.
From the ballroom came the crash of brass, the shuffle
of feet, and a young officer's voice demanding whether anyone
had seen his boots.

Mulvaney carved the fowl with a champagne-foil knife.

"Balls," said he, "is inventions av the Evil Wan for
temptin' young officers into debt an' young women into miscalculations."

"You've done both," said Ortheris.

"I have," Mulvaney replied magnificently, "an' therefore I speak wid
authority. But I niver conducted an e-lope-ment."

"You couldn't," said Ortheris. "Nobody'd run away with you unless
the police was behind."

Mulvaney ignored him and gave me the larger wing.

"Wan time," he continued, "I prevented such an operation, an'
by preventin' ut preserved the honour av a colonel, the
innocince av a daughter, the reputashin av the corps, an'—though
this last was entirely accidental—the life av a very bad
captain."

"Was you sober?" Ortheris asked.

"I was a corporal," said Mulvaney. "Which in thim days
meant the same thing at laste four hours out av
the twenty-four. Listen now, an' do not rupture the narrative
wid low observations."

He drank, wiped his mouth on his sleeve, and began.

---

"'Twas whin I was a young man an' beautiful beyond
permission. Me waist was twenty-eight, me chest was forty-two, an'
the girls looked afther me as if I was a
brass band marchin' past alone. I was corporal in B
Company, the smartest, hardest, most misundherstood collection av trained heroes
that iver broke a sergeant-major's heart.

"Our colonel was Colonel Darragh—an ould red bull av a
man, wid eyebrows like horse-brushes an' a voice that shook
dust from the rafters. He cursed us cruel, drilled us
fair, an' knew every man's service, debts, diseases, an' private
habits. If a button was dull in the rear rank,
he'd see ut from the adjutant's office an' roar the
name av the man's grandmother. But he had wan daughter,
Miss Norah, an' before her he was melted butter in
a hot bazaar.

"She was young—no more than nineteen—an' had been educated by
governesses that taught her music, French, flowers, an' nothing useful
concerning men. She had big grey eyes an' a way
av believin' what was said to her. If I'd told
her the quartermaster laid eggs, she'd have asked whether they
was boiled for breakfast.

"Now there was Captain Wrexham, newly come to us from
a depôt where, by all accounts, they had been glad
to lose him. He was long in the neck, narrow
in the shoulders, soft in the voice, an' weak in
the left leg. He could not give a command without
apologisin' to ut. 'Company—ah—perhaps you'd be so good as to
form fours,' he'd say; an' the company, bein' used to
soldiers, did not know whether to obey or lend him
money.

"He owed money everywhere—to the mess, the tailor, the horse-dealer,
the soda-water man, three card tables, two native bankers, an'
a widow in Cawnpore who had trusted him wid a
ring. His pay passed through his hands like rain through
a cane chair. Yet he had gloves for every hour,
scents like a lady's cupboard, an' a little yellow moustache
that he stroked whenever he lied.

"'Sounds like a proper orficer,' said Ortheris.

"'He was a dis-graceful imitation,' said Mulvaney. 'Our officers may
be fools by privilege, but they should not be sneaks
by profession.

"'This Wrexham began makin' love to Miss Norah. Not honest
love, mind ye—not the natural affliction that comes on a
man an' reduces him to poetry, hair-oil, an' walkin' under
windows. He wanted the colonel's money. Colonel Darragh had land
at home, an' Miss Norah was all the children he
possessed. Wrexham calculated her like commissariat stores: wan young female,
sound, affectionate, wid expectations—value sufficient to extinguish liabilities.

"'The colonel hated him by instinct, which is wisdom supplied
before the evidence. He'd watch Wrexham cross the mess veranda
an' growl like a dog at a closed door. But
Miss Norah saw a persecuted lover. Wrexham told her the
colonel despised him because he was sensitive, an' she believed
ut. He told her his debts were the result av
generosity, an' she believed that too. He told her his
limp came from a wound received savin' a brother officer
under fire, whereas the truth was he had fallen off
a hired pony outside a billiard-room.

"'How'd you know all this?' I asked.

"'By attendin' to me duty—which includes knowin' everything not written
in orders. Also, Wrexham's bearer talked in his sleep, an'
I had friends in the cook-house, the post-office, the stables,
an' other centres av military intelligence.

"'At that time the station was afflicted wid amateur theatricals.
Amateur means a thing done without pay that no money
would persuade a sensible man to witness. The officers had
built a stage in the assembly hall, an' there was
to be a grand performance av The Baron's Bride, or
The Bride's Revenge, or some such calamity. Wrexham played a
nobleman in distress, which saved him the trouble av actin'.
Miss Norah played a village girl, though she had never
seen a village except from a railway carriage.

"'I was employed behind the scenes because I had a
hand for carpentherin', a head for emergencies, an' legs worthy
av silk stockings. I shifted castles, forests, cottages, an' wan
cardboard moon. Thus I observed Wrexham whisperin' to Miss Norah
between the acts, an' her tremblin' like a leaf over
a letter.

"'That same night, Corporal Slane—him that was afterward broke for
sellin' the company coal—says to me, "Mick, there's a hired
carriage ordered from Lal Din's stables for Friday night, eleven-thirty,
stage-door, to catch the twelve-fifteen down train."

"'"Who ordered ut?" says I.

"'"Captain Wrexham's servant."

"'"An' who travels?"

"'"Two, wid luggage for wan."

"'Thin I knew. Wrexham meant to carry Miss Norah off
afther the play, marry her beyond the colonel's reach, an'
return wid a wife between himself an' the consequences. 'Twas
neat, dishonourable, an' liable to succeed—which is the most dangerous
combination known to mankind.

"'Why didn't you tell the colonel?' I said.

"'Tell him!' Mulvaney slapped the carriage roof. The pony woke,
considered protest, and slept again. 'Tell Colonel Darragh his daughter
was plannin' to run wid wan av his captains? He'd
have killed Wrexham on parade, locked the girl in the
magazine, disbanded the regiment, an' died av shame before breakfast.
Besides, where was me proof? A carriage ordered, a whisper,
a letter I had not read. Wrexham would deny; Miss
Norah would cry; an' Corporal Mulvaney would be reduced for
malicious invintion against a superior officer. I have been reduced
for many things, but niver for lack av strategy.

"'So I made me plan av campaign.

"'First, the luggage. A woman may elope without wisdom, but
seldom without luggage. On Friday afternoon I discovered two leather
trunks an' a bonnet-box in the back passage av the
colonel's bungalow, labelled with Miss Norah's name. Her maid, who
was in the plot by romance an' five rupees, had
left them ready for removal through the garden when darkness
fell.

"'At six o'clock, while the household was dressing for the
play, I presented meself at the servants' gate in a
commissariat coat, a forage-cap turned backward, an' a beard manufactured
from the colonel's brown door-mat—"

"'You tore up 'is door-mat?' said Ortheris.

"'A small tactical portion only. I informed the gate-keeper that
the luggage was required at the theatre for the last
act. He was doubtful, but I looked more doubtful still,
which overcame him. There is no confidence so convincin' as
the confidence av a man who appears to fear he'll
be beaten if delayed.

"'Two bearers carried out the trunks. I marched them to
an empty store-room behind the hospital, gave them four annas,
an' warned them the contents was stage explosives. They departed
rapidly. I hid the trunks under old tent-canvas, the bonnet-box
behind a barrel, an' returned the commissariat coat to the
man that had not lent ut.

"'"That's theft," says you. No. 'Twas protective relocation.

"'Second, the carriage. Lal Din's driver was a large, black-whiskered
man called Ghulam Rasul, who had the principles av a
jackal an' the arms av a blacksmith. He had been
promised ten rupees to ask no questions, which made him
expensive an' therefore zealous.

"'At ten-thirty I went to the stables. The play had
begun; all the station was at the hall; an' Lal
Din's yard was quiet save for the horses chewin' an'
Ghulam Rasul polishin' his whip.

"'"You drive Captain Sahib?" I asks.

"'"Who asks?"

"'"A friend," says I.

"'"Then the friend may go."

"'He had a bad manner, so I improved ut. I
told him the captain had changed the arrangement an' wanted
the carriage at the north gate. He called me a
liar. I explained the position again. He reached for his
whip.

"'There are conversations that proceed by words, an' conversations that
require punctuation. I punctuated him beneath the ear.

"'He was powerful, but slow. He came at me wid
both arms, breathin' like a beer-engine. I stepped aside, laid
his head against the stable-door for steadiness, an' reasoned wid
him three times in rapid succession. At the third argument
he sat down. At the fourth he lay down. I
had intended no more than moral persuasion, but he possessed
an obstinate skull.

"'"Killed 'im?" said Ortheris.

"'"Not at all. He snored. The man who snores is
alive, though not necessarily useful.

"'I removed his long coat, turban, sash, an' false dignity.
Under a manger I placed him, covered him in horse-blankets,
an' tied his ankles because I respected his future intentions.
Thin I blackened me face wid harness grease, wrapped the
turban round me head, pulled the coat to me heels,
an' regarded meself in a bucket.

"'I was magnificent. Dark, mysterious, horsey, an' full av Eastern
silence.

"'You'd look like a sweep in a tablecloth," said Ortheris.

"'To the vulgar eye, perhaps. But night, excitement, an' a
guilty conscience are the three best tailors for a disguise.

"'I drove out at eleven. The horse was a grey
mare wid hatred in her soul. She objected to me
hands, me voice, the moon, the shafts, an' the general
organisation av creation. We reached the stage-door at eleven-twenty-five after
traversin' half the road sideways.

"'Behind the theatre the play was runnin' to its murderin'
conclusion. I heard Wrexham declarin', in his thin voice, "Though
kingdoms fall, my heart remains!" an' somebody in the audience
coughed for nearly a minute. I sat doubled on the
box, as a hired driver should, an' waited.

"'At eleven-forty Miss Norah came through the stage-door under a
dark cloak. Her face was white as flour. Her maid
followed wid a small bundle, lookin' delighted, for servants love
a catastrophe provided ut occurs in the family that pays
them.

"'"Where are the trunks?" the maid whispered.

"'I grunted an' pointed beneath the seats, where there was
nothing but a rug an' a wrench. Guilty people never
examine explanations carefully.

"'Miss Norah climbed in. "To the station—quickly," says she, in
a little voice.

"'I touched me forehead, cracked the whip, an' drove.

"'Now the station lay east, but the colonel's bungalow lay
west. For the first hundred yards I went east, because
a deception, like drill, should begin correctly. At the church
I turned north; at the post-office I turned west; at
the parade-ground I made a complete circle because the mare
took offence at a paper lantern. From inside came a
cry: "Driver! This is not the station road."

"'I answered in the deepest language available to me, which
was chiefly throat, an' lashed the mare into a gallop.

"'We flew past the mess, round the canteen, through a
patch av dust, an' across the colonel's garden track. Miss
Norah called to stop. The maid screamed that we were
betrayed. I drove faster. The bonnet-box was not there to
fall off, but in spirit I heard ut bumpin' behind
us.

"'At the bungalow I pulled up under the dark veranda.
The front of the house was empty, all the servants
having gone to watch the play from outside the hall.
I jumped down, opened the carriage door, an' stood aside.

"'Miss Norah looked out. She saw her own steps, her
own pillars, her father's cane chair, an' the old dog
asleep by the flower-pots.

"'"What does this mean?" she says.

"'I could not answer without betrayin' me accent, so I
pointed to the house.

"'"Drive on! You have brought me back!"

"'I bowed.

"'She stepped out, furious an' frightened together. "Who ordered you
to do this?"

"'I bowed again, more mysteriously.

"'The maid said, "It is fate, Miss Sahib."

"'Which was useful, because fate cannot be court-martialled.

"'Then Miss Norah began to cry—not loud, but as if
something inside her had broken. I tell you, sorr, that
took the triumph from me. Till then the business had
been manoeuvres—luggage, driver, horse, roads, an' Captain Wrexham waitin' like
a damp ferret on the station platform. But the girl's
tears made ut human.

"'I wanted to say, "Go inside, darlin'; sleep; an' thank
God ye have been brought home before ye learned what
manner av man was waitin'." But I was Ghulam Rasul,
a driver without opinions. So I lifted me hand toward
the door, an' the old dog woke, knew there was
something familiar under the grease, an' wagged his tail at
me.

"'The maid led Miss Norah in by the side entrance.
Before she vanished, she turned an' looked at me hard.
Perhaps she knew. Perhaps she only saw a villainous coachman
wid beautiful eyes. Women notice eyes even in disaster.

"'You ain't got beautiful eyes," said Ortheris. "You've got eyes
like two boiled currants."

"'Champagne has destroyed your perception.

"'I drove back to Lal Din's stables, but me work
was not complete. Captain Wrexham was at the station, an'
soon he would return mad enough to investigate. Therefore the
true driver must be restored to active service.

"'Ghulam Rasul had awakened. He had chewed through no rope,
but he had produced curses sufficient to burn the manger.
I removed the blanket. He tried to bite me.

"'"Peace," says I. "The carriage is returned, the horse is
sound, an' here is the ten rupees."

"'Money calmed him where religion might have failed. I untied
him, replaced his garments after divestin' meself, an' warned him
that Captain Wrexham would shortly arrive in a condition av
spiritual disturbance.

"'"Why?" says he.

"'"Because you drove the lady to the wrong place."

"'"I drove no lady!"

"'"That," says I, "is an admirable defence. Maintain ut under
pressure."

"'I departed by the rear wall.

"'But the trunks?" I asked.

"'Patience. Logistics is the mother av victory an' the aunt
av confusion. I went to the hospital store, recovered the
baggage, an' recruited two sleeping sweepers by the simple process
av wakin' them an' displayin' coin. Before one o'clock the
trunks and bonnet-box were back in the colonel's passage precisely
as found, except that the bonnet-box had acquired a dent
from me shoulder. The gate-keeper, seein' them returned, concluded the
theatre had rejected the explosives.

"'At the station, meanwhile, Wrexham waited beside two tickets, a
rug, an' a bunch av flowers purchased on credit. The
twelve-fifteen came. The twelve-fifteen went. No bride appeared.

"'He remained till half-past twelve, alternately sweatin' an' rehearsin' explanations.
Then he hired a pony-cart and galloped to Lal Din's.

"'I was not present, but I heard the full account
from four independent witnesses and a horse. Wrexham leaped down,
found Ghulam Rasul rubbin' his jaw, an' demanded where he
had taken the lady.

"'Ghulam Rasul replied, reasonably, that he had taken no lady
anywhere.

"'Wrexham struck him with his cane.

"'This was unwise. Ghulam Rasul had already suffered argument from
me, an' his patience was exhausted. He seized Wrexham by
the collar, lifted him till his weak leg waved free,
an' asked why he had been assaulted, robbed av clothes,
deprived av carriage, an' accused concerning ladies.

"'Wrexham heard "clothes" an' understood there had been substitution. But
he could not explain without revealin' the elopement. So he
beat Ghulam Rasul with the cane, an' Ghulam Rasul beat
him with the flat av a shovel. Lal Din's sons
joined in from family affection. The stable-boys contributed advice. In
the end Wrexham escaped with one glove, no hat, a
torn collar, an' a limp increased to the dimensions av
military incapacity.

"'Next morning there was silence.

"'Miss Norah appeared at breakfast pale, but present. The trunks
stood in the passage, which persuaded her that perhaps the
whole adventure had been a nightmare arranged by Providence. The
colonel knew nothing, though he complained that someone had damaged
his door-mat.

"'Wrexham sent in sick.

"'At noon I was carryin' a message past his quarters
when he called me from the veranda.

"'"Corporal Mulvaney."

"'"Sorr!"

"'He was wrapped in a dressing-gown, one eye purple, his
lip cut, an' his left leg bandaged. He looked like
a heron that had been dragged backward through a hedge.

"'"You were behind the scenes last night?"

"'"I was, sorr."

"'"Did you observe Miss Darragh leave?"

"'"No, sorr. But I observed her arrive home this mornin'—or
was ut last night? Faith, sorr, theatricals confuses the hours."

"'He stared. "What do you mean?"

"'"Nothing at all, sorr. Only that plays is dangerous things.
A man may act the villain so natural that Providence
mistakes him for wan."

"'His face changed. "Are you threatening me?"

"'"A corporal cannot threaten a captain, sorr. 'Tis contrary to
regulations. But a driver might lose his road, an' trunks
might walk home, an' a shovel might fall repeatedly upon
a gentleman—none av which is mentioned in Queen's Regulations."

"'He leaned toward me. "You damned insolent Irish brute."

"'"Sorr," says I, "if I was insolent I'd mention the
twelve-fifteen train, two tickets, an' a young lady that had
better remain her father's daughter till she can tell a
man from a bill-collector."

"'He went white beneath the bruises.

"'For wan delicious second I thought he would strike me,
which would have simplified the future. But he knew that
I knew, an' he knew I had said enough without
sayin' anything that could be written down. So he only
whispers, "Get out."

"'I saluted to the mathematical limit an' got.

"'Within a month Wrexham exchanged into another regiment. Within six
months he resigned, pursued by creditors in extended formation. I
heard later he married a widow with money, but she
had three grown sons, all fond av boxin', so justice
may be delayed without bein' absent.

"'Miss Norah never spoke to me concerning the business. Once,
before she went home to England, she passed B Company
on the road. She looked at me, then at the
old scar over me ear where Ghulam Rasul's ring had
cut me, an' she smiled—just a little. That was all.

"'Colonel Darragh gave the company two extra days' pass that
Christmas because, he said, we had improved in discipline. Thus
virtue receives reward by routes unknown to the Army.'

---

Mulvaney finished the champagne and set the bottle upright between
his knees. The ballroom behind us had grown quieter. Dawn
showed like tarnished silver beyond the trees, and the band
was packing its instruments into cases.

"So you saved 'er," said Ortheris, "by stealin' 'er boxes,
knocking a cabman silly, pinchin' 'is clothes, kidnappin' the girl,
lyin' to everybody, an' getting the wrong bloke thrashed."

"I operated wid the materials supplied," said Mulvaney. "Ye cannot
build a cathedral from regulations alone."

"And the driver?"

"Received ten rupees, exercise, an' a story for his descendants."

"And the captain?"

"Received less than he deserved."

"And the girl?"

"Received another chance—which is more than most fools obtain."

Mulvaney folded his arms and looked toward the paling sky
with the noble melancholy of a statue erected by subscription.

"Consider, sorr," he said to me. "I preserved an officer's
family from scandal at personal risk, wid no hope av
medal, mention, pension, or free liquor. An' yet the same
Army that profited by me delicate diplomacy has this week
awarded me ten days' confinement to barracks."

"You was drunk," said Ortheris.

"Temporarily elevated."

"You tried to swim the fort ditch."

"'Twas hot."

"There weren't a foot of water in it."

"I discovered that in the execution."

"You 'ad the sergeant-major's bath-towel round your neck and shouted
you was Admiral of the Red Sea."

Mulvaney drew himself up.

"A detail. A mere excrescence upon an honourable career. Is
there no account kept between a man an' destiny? Does
wan small maritime error oblitherate a lifetime av services?"

"Yes," said Ortheris. "If the guard catches you."

The first trumpet sounded reveille across the cantonment. Mulvaney sighed,
descended from the carriage roof, and tucked the remains of
the regimental cake beneath his jacket.

"Come," he said. "Duty calls, ungrateful as ever. An' if
any man asks where this cake came from, remember—ut was
saved from an e-lope-ment."

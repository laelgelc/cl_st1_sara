### THE MUSICAL PRODIGAL

Being removed, by several green miles and one extremely bad
road, from the agitations of fashion, politics, and the money
market, I am compelled to seek entertainment in occupations which
a gentleman of active ambition would doubtless esteem beneath him.
I know the hour at which the sun strikes the
eastern chimney; I observe whether the brook carries a full
or a diminished treasury; I maintain a familiar acquaintance with
the lilacs, and even condescend to notice when my gard'ner
has tied the raspberry canes improperly. Above all, I listen
to the birds; and if this be a low employment,
I can only plead that it has furnished me with
more agreeable intelligence than I ever obtained from a newspaper.

The first messenger of spring is not always the most
splendid. Indeed, the earliest visitor to my orchard is a
little dusky personage, scarcely larger than a wren, whose name
I have never satisfactorily discovered. Learned authorities may have christened
him; but he declines to present his credentials, and I
am unwilling to disturb so modest a gentleman with questions
of genealogy. He appears while the snow yet lies in
the hollows, and while Winter, though manifestly preparing to depart,
still sits sulking upon the northern hill. From a bare
twig he drops three or four silver notes—hesitates—repeats them—and vanishes
among the thickets.

There is something peculiarly affecting in that small voice. It
is not a song of triumph, but of confidence; not
the trumpet which announces a procession, but the whisper which
assures us that the procession is on its way. I
hear it while the clouds are low, the road is
black with thaw, and the dead grass has the melancholy
aspect of a carpet after a festival; yet those few
notes make a brightness in the air, like sunshine seen
between two curtains. The little songster is to the season
what the first violet is to the meadow,—humble, half concealed,
but carrying in its bosom the whole promise of May.

Soon after him arrive the Phoebe-birds, a sociable and domestic
family, with no false delicacy concerning the proximity of man.
They take possession of the shed, the bridge, or the
cornice of the piazza, according to their taste in architecture;
and having selected a site, they discuss it from sun-rise
to breakfast with an earnestness which would do credit to
a board of public works. "Phoebe! Phoebe!" cries the husband.
"Phoebe!" answers the wife; and whether they are calling each
other, naming their first child, or merely rejoicing in the
sound of their own voices, I cannot determine.

One pair has lodged for several seasons above my kitchen
door, where their nest rests upon a bracket intended, I
believe, for ornamental purposes. The cook objects to the arrangement;
the cat contemplates it with professional interest; I defend it
upon principles of hospitality. Thus the Phoebes remain, bringing mud
upon my paint, straw into my entry, and cheerfulness into
the whole establishment. They dart after insects with admirable dexterity,
wag their tails upon the fence, and conduct their matrimonial
affairs with such frank publicity that every member of the
household becomes, in a manner, their cousin.

Then comes the bluebird, bearing the color of the April
sky upon his back and the warmth of the April
earth upon his breast. If our unknown minstrel is the
violet of the season, the bluebird is its banner. He
appears upon a post beside the lane, looks about him
as if surprised that we have managed to survive his
absence, and warbles a soft congratulation. The maples are reddening;
the willows show their first pale gold; the brook, newly
released from bondage, makes a hundred little speeches over its
stones; and Nature, like a lady awakened from refreshing sleep,
begins to put on her jewels before she has quite
arranged her hair.

How eagerly these signs are welcomed by one who has
endured the long white monotony of winter! I have sometimes
thought of those weather-beaten sailors who, after an immeasurable waste
of ocean, welcomed a floating branch or a bird's feather
as evidence of land. So do I regard every wing
that crosses my field in March. A feather becomes a
prophecy; a note, a proclamation. We stand upon the deck
of the old year, peering toward the green continent of
spring; and the bluebird, flying out of the mist, assures
us that orchards, gardens, and warm evenings are not inventions
of the imagination.

But pleasant as these visitors are, none has so strong
a claim upon my sensibility as the Bobolink. He does
not steal into the season with cautious syllables; he bursts
upon it. When the grass has deepened, when the apple-trees
are full of fragrant snow, when the meadows are enamelled
with buttercups and clover, then this little musical spendthrift mounts
a swaying weed and scatters his fortune upon the air.

What shall I call his performance? It is not a
measured tune, and he has plainly never studied under a
severe master. It bubbles, tumbles, rings, stammers, laughs, and runs
away with itself. One moment he is in the grass;
the next he springs upward, as if the music had
exploded beneath him; then down he comes upon another stem,
pouring forth such a confusion of bells, brooks, laughter, and
broken silver that criticism stands helpless. He wears, moreover, a
striking professional suit,—black, white, and buff disposed with an agreeable
contempt for ordinary arrangement,—and carries himself like a young poet
who has just discovered both genius and love.

A lady once attempted to render his discourse into verse,
and though I cannot answer for the accuracy of the
translation, it conveys something of his spirit:

> "Over the clover, under the cloud,
> Merry and mad and sweet and loud;
> Toss me, meadow, and swing me high,—
> The earth is green and love is nigh!
> Ripple and riddle and ring-a-ling,
> Who can be sober in such a spring?
> Down to the grass, then up to the blue,—
> Heart of my heart, I sing for you!"

There is more philosophy in these lines than appears at
first reading. "Who can be sober in such a spring?"
asks the bird; and I confess that when the west
wind rolls the grass in shining waves, the wild-rose opens
along the wall, and the whole warm world seems breathing
perfume, sobriety itself looks like a species of ingratitude.

In my school-days the Bobolink was at once my delight
and my torment. Our academy stood near a meadow where
he held his morning concerts; and while I sat imprisoned
before a grammar, endeavoring to understand why one dead language
required so many rules for saying so little, the little
varlet outside poured forth whole orations without syntax, dictionary, or
fear of correction. My instructor, a worthy man with a
long forefinger, believed that attention was a moral duty; the
Bobolink believed that joy was one. Between these philosophers my
allegiance was divided, though my heart was never doubtful.

Oh, how I envied him! He had no bell summoning
him to recitation, no ink upon his fingers, no solemn
examination waiting at the end of June. His school-house was
the meadow; his copy-book was the sky; his only task
was to swing upon a stem and tell the world
that he was happy. While I stumbled through conjugations, he
conjugated the verb to love in every tense. When the
master demanded my attention, the rogue flew past the window
in a shower of melody, and I am ashamed to
say that Rome, Greece, and the whole ancient world instantly
disappeared.

Age, however, corrects many youthful conclusions; and I have since
learned that the Bobolink's career is not one of uninterrupted
poetry. Let us follow him beyond the sweetest weather and
the sweetest season. June matures into July; the roses grow
weary; the hay falls beneath the scythe; the days, though
golden, acquire a certain heaviness. What becomes of our inspired
musician?

Does he continue his art, chastened by experience and enriched
by meditation? Does he compose an autumnal ode? Does he
establish a conservatory for young birds of promise? Not he!
His music ceases. The enchanted bells are silent; the aerial
fountains dry up. He exchanges his picturesque attire for a
dull travelling coat, and, having dismissed both poetry and courtship,
devotes himself to dinner.

At first the change is scarcely perceptible. He lingers in
companies among the reeds, discussing, perhaps, the fatigues of the
musical profession. Presently his appetite increases; his manners decline. The
fastidious lover who lately subsisted, as we fondly imagined, upon
dew, fragrance, and affection, discovers an astonishing capacity for seeds.
He eats early; he eats late; he eats between meals.
With each repast his thoughts descend and his corpulency advances.
Yesterday a troubadour, to-day he is a gourmand.

Nor is he satisfied with the plain fare of his
native meadow. No; luxury once admitted is a migratory passion.
He travels southward in search of richer tables, dropping from
marsh to marsh, examining the reeds, tasting the grains, and
recommending the best establishments to his companions. In the rice-fields
he finds his paradise. There, amid bending stalks and warm
vapors, he abandons himself to a gastronomical tour which would
astonish the most experienced diner of the city.

Observe the melancholy contrast. In May he rose toward heaven,
intoxicated with song; in August he sinks among the rice,
stupefied with food. His breast, once the residence of melody,
becomes merely an accommodation for fat. He no longer salutes
the dawn; he inquires what there is for breakfast. He
no longer pursues his beloved through the perfumed grass; he
follows a promising plantation. Even his name is taken from
him. The Bobolink, poet of the North, becomes the rice-bird
of the South,—a commercial article, a morsel, a paragraph in
the bill of fare.

For man, who forgives almost every vice that improves a
dinner, has watched this transformation with interest. The hunter enters
the field; the flock rises heavily; and then—pop! pop! pop!—our
late musician falls from the scene of his indulgence. He
who escaped the school-boy, the hawk, the storm, and the
perils of migration is conquered at last by his own
excellent condition. Had he remained thin, he might have remained
alive; but his reputation has reached the clubs, the hotels,
and the private dining-rooms of persons whose sensibilities are conveniently
suspended at table.

I have seen him there, presented upon a delicate dish,
honored with a sauce, and surrounded by gentlemen who praised
his tenderness without the least recollection of his song. No
garland marked the poet; no inscription recorded the lover of
the meadow. A gourmand lifted him by one small leg,
considered him with judicial gravity, and in two mouthfuls extinguished
all that remained of May.

Such is the history of my favourite,—a comedy in spring,
a satire in summer, and a tragedy at supper. I
do not relate it merely to discourage birds from visiting
rice-fields, for I fear this essay will circulate imperfectly among
them; but its application may possibly reach a few young
gentlemen. Music, poetry, study, affection, and the cultivation of the
mind are not, as some suppose, occupations to be put
away with one's bright plumage. He who deserts the higher
pleasures for perpetual indulgence may travel rapidly, grow sleek, and
acquire a gastronomical celebrity; but he should remember that the
world which applauds his fatness may have designs upon it.

Therefore, little birds and little boys,—and older persons also, if
they will pardon the association,—keep something of your spring song
through the declining year. Eat what is necessary, enjoy what
is innocent, but do not permit appetite to silence melody.
For the road which begins at the meadow, and passes
through luxury, may end beneath a silver cover; and the
hand that lifts that cover is seldom moved by admiration
for the poet.

### The Improper Appointment

There were, at John Hinckman's country place, so many inducements
to tranquillity, recreation, and that agreeable suspension of the ordinary
responsibilities of existence which is supposed to be the peculiar
privilege of a summer guest, that I might have passed
six weeks there in complete contentment had the establishment contained
one attraction less.

The house itself, an ample, many-windowed abode of mellow stone,
stood upon a low elevation from which the eye descended
over lawns whose smoothness was interrupted only by ancient trees,
beds of flowers, and occasional groups of garden chairs. Beyond
these were bosky shades, winding paths, a brook of some
pretension, tennis grounds, a stable containing horses of varied temper
and uniform excellence, and, at the foot of the eastern
meadow, a pond in which one could fish all day
without being troubled by the necessity of catching anything. There
were drives through lanes bordered by wild roses; there were
walks to a ruined mill, which was more interesting than
if it had been in repair; and there were opportunities
for reading beneath trees, for idleness upon verandas, and for
losing at games conducted with balls, mallets, racquets, or cards.

But if every tree had been removed, every flower uprooted,
every horse sold, every fish withdrawn from the pond, and
the house itself reduced to a shed with one chair
in it, I should still have remained, provided the one
chair had been occupied by Madeline.

Madeline was Mr. Hinckman's niece. I call her Madeline because
that was her name, and because no invented appellation could
have expressed to me so much sweetness, dignity, and peril.
She had dark eyes, a clear and thoughtful brow, and
a manner which, though gracious, never allowed an admirer to
suppose that graciousness meant surrender. She listened well, spoke with
discrimination, and possessed that quiet power of compelling a man
to examine every sentence he had uttered, generally at three
o'clock the following morning.

I loved her. This fact, which can now be stated
in three words, occupied at the time an immense and
intricate region of my consciousness. I approached it, withdrew from
it, examined it from various moral elevations, and considered it
in its relation to my income, my future collateral existence,
her probable sentiments, the instability of human happiness, and the
general views of her uncle on the subject. No statesman
preparing a treaty, no commander surveying a fortified position, could
have bestowed more attention upon circumstances which, after all, were
to be decided by the utterance of a very simple
interrogatory.

My chief difficulty was not that I doubted the excellence
of marriage, nor that I doubted Madeline's fitness for it,
nor even—at every moment—that I doubted my own. My difficulty
lay in the ante-interrogatory period of love: that interval in
which a man must discover, by means of glances, pauses,
tones, and other evidences rejected by every respectable court, whether
a woman wishes him to ask a question which she
is determined not to answer until he asks it.

To this natural embarrassment was added the presence of John
Hinckman.

Mr. Hinckman was not an ill-tempered man in the sense
that he was perpetually angry. He was worse: he was
a man liable to become angry upon grounds which no
one else could foresee. His temper resembled one of those
concealed springs in an old path, dry and harmless for
ten months of the year, but capable, after an invisible
disturbance elsewhere, of carrying away a bridge.

He was tall, broad, gray-haired, and energetic. His eyebrows gave
an appearance of severity even to his moments of benevolence,
and when he said, "Certainly," it was often impossible to
determine whether he meant to confer a favour or terminate
an acquaintance. He was warmly attached to Madeline and had,
I believed, no personal objection to me; but I could
never discover whether this latter circumstance arose from approval or
from the fact that he had not yet thought it
worth while to form an objection.

Once, while we were smoking upon the western porch, he
had remarked that young men nowadays married with insufficient reflection.

"Very true," I answered.

On another occasion he had declared that hesitation was the
disease of the age.

"Undoubtedly," I replied.

Having thus committed myself to both deliberation and promptness, I
found no safe course between them.

My visit had nearly reached its fourth week when Mr.
Hinckman was summoned to town on business connected with a
warehouse, a lawsuit, or a person named Burdock. I never
clearly understood the matter, but its results were immediate and
beneficent. He departed after luncheon on Tuesday and did not
expect to return until Friday.

It would be difficult to exaggerate the change produced in
my mental atmosphere by his absence. I did not dislike
him; indeed, I had much respect for him. But there
are persons whose departure from a room seems to increase
the amount of oxygen in it. Before his carriage had
passed the gate I breathed with an amplitude unknown to
me since my arrival.

That evening Madeline and I walked beside the brook. The
hour, the path, and the companionship were all that could
have been desired. The sun had gone down behind the
cedars; a tender light remained in the sky; and the
water, slipping among stones, seemed to murmur with the confidential
insistence of a friend who knows what one ought to
say.

I said nothing.

There were reasons for this. A gardener appeared at one
turn, a cow at another, and a mosquito at a
third. Later, when we sat upon a bench beneath an
elm, Madeline spoke of her uncle's probable return. This chilled
the poetic current of my thought. I postponed my avowal
until the next day, when, I assured myself, daylight would
lend sincerity to my purpose and prevent those excesses of
language into which twilight sometimes betrays a sensitive nature.

That night I occupied the Blue Room, a spacious chamber
in the older portion of the house. It had once
belonged to Mr. Hinckman's grandfather and contained a carved bedstead,
two sombre portraits, a wardrobe of extreme depth, and curtains
which moved when there was no wind. I had been
told that the room was haunted, but as every old
country house possesses at least one haunted apartment, and as
most families are more proud of their spectres than of
their plumbing, I had attached no importance to the information.

I went to bed at eleven, slept badly until twelve,
and did not sleep at all thereafter. The moonlight, passing
through the curtains, drew a long pale rectangle across the
carpet. A branch scraped the pane at intervals. The wardrobe,
though stationary, appeared to increase in size whenever I looked
away from it.

At half past one I heard a sigh.

It was not the sigh of the wind, nor the
creak of furniture, nor any of those domestic noises which,
in daylight, are attributed to the contraction of wood and,
at night, to murder. It was the unmistakable sigh of
a person who has been unjustly treated and wishes someone
to inquire into the matter.

I sat up.

In the chair near the window was a man.

For several seconds I could see only the outline of
his figure. Then the moon emerged from a cloud and
disclosed the face of John Hinckman.

My first conclusion was that he had returned unexpectedly. My
second was that he had entered my room without knocking.
My third was that there must be some explanation which
I was unlikely to enjoy.

"Sir," I said, drawing the bedclothes about me with what
dignity the circumstances permitted, "I was not aware that you
had come back."

"Back!" he exclaimed. "I wish he would stay away forever!"

The voice was not quite Mr. Hinckman's. It possessed the
same depth, but lacked its authority. Moreover, the figure in
the chair was faintly transparent. The moonlight showed through one
shoulder, and a brass handle belonging to the wardrobe could
be dimly perceived in the region of his waistcoat.

"You are not Mr. Hinckman," I said.

"Of course I am not," replied the apparition, irritably. "Do
you suppose I would be sitting quietly in this room
if I were? Do I look as though I owned
the place?"

"You look precisely as though you did."

"That is the misery of my position."

He rose, walked twice across the room, and then seated
himself on the foot of my bed. As the mattress
did not yield beneath him, I was reassured on one
point and alarmed on several others.

"You are a ghost," I said.

"I occupy that position," he answered, "but under protest."

I waited. It seemed more courteous to allow him to
explain than to scream, and, besides, I have never screamed
successfully.

"You have probably heard," he continued, "that Hinckman was once
reported dead?"

I had heard some vague story of the kind. Years
before, while travelling in a remote part of South America,
Mr. Hinckman had disappeared during a flood. His luggage had
been found, a companion had telegraphed that there was no
hope, and memorial observations had been made in several places.
Three weeks later he had returned, sunburned, hungry, and greatly
offended by the quality of the obituaries.

"At the time of that unfortunate error," said the spectre,
"the authorities, acting with an expedition which they never display
when it would be useful, appointed me his ghost."

"Who are the authorities?"

"That cannot be explained to an outsider. Indeed, it has
never been explained to me. One receives notice, instructions, and,
if fortunate, a subject. I received Hinckman."

"But as he was alive—"

"Exactly! As he was alive, I became the ghost of
a man who did not require one. Imagine the irregularity!
Imagine the embarrassment! There I was, duly assigned, equipped with
his countenance, his general outline, and certain privileges of apparition,
and there he was, walking about, eating breakfast, disputing accounts,
and making the house impossible for me."

"You are afraid of him?"

"Afraid!" cried the ghost. "My dear sir, he haunts me!"

This inversion of the natural order struck me as so
reasonable, after his explanation, that I ceased to regard it
as extraordinary.

"When he is in the house," the ghost continued, lowering
his voice, "I scarcely dare emerge from the eastern attic.
Once he saw me in a mirror. He turned about
so quickly that I escaped only by passing through a
bookcase, and there is nothing more unpleasant than passing through
a bookcase when the shelves are closely filled. Another time
he came upon me in the corridor and demanded who
I was. I had no answer prepared. What could I
say? That I was his ghost? He would have denied
it, and very rudely too."

"Could you not haunt elsewhere?"

"A ghost has responsibilities. I am attached to the premises,
the person, and certain associated localities. There are regulations. If
I wander without authority, I may be classified as a
vagrant manifestation. That would ruin every prospect I possess."

He clasped his insubstantial hands.

"For years I have applied for a transfer. I have
represented that my appointment was made under a mistake, that
the supposed deceased is obstinately alive, that my usefulness is
destroyed, and that my nervous condition is becoming serious. They
acknowledge my communications and do nothing. They say there are
few vacancies."

"I should have supposed death created a considerable number."

"Death creates subjects," he answered, "but not always ghostships. Many
persons are ineligible. Some do not believe in ghosts; some
lack suitable houses; some have no unfinished business; and others
are assigned by family interest before the death is officially
recorded. It is a profession crowded with influence."

He leaned towards me.

"You go among people. You read newspapers. Can you tell
me of anyone recently dead who would be likely to
need a ghost?"

I reflected.

"Old Mr. Pemberley died last month."

"Peacefully?"

"In his bed."

"Useless. No mystery, no violence, no concealed documents?"

"None that I know of."

"Then there is no position there. I require a genuine
opening: murder preferred, ancient castle advantageous, family secret desirable but
not indispensable."

I promised to consider the matter. The ghost thanked me
warmly, then asked whether I had any difficulty of my
own in which he might assist me.

The moment appeared favourable. A confidant who could pass through
walls, overhear conversations, and remain unseen by others might, I
thought, prove valuable in affairs of the heart. I therefore
acquainted him, with some delicacy and much circumlocution, with my
attachment to Madeline.

"You wish to marry her," he said.

This crude condensation startled me, but I admitted its accuracy.

"Then ask her."

"I intend to do so."

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"That is what men always say when they mean some
day. Still, I will help you. I have had opportunities
to observe her."

My heart bounded.

"Has she spoken of me?"

"Not to me."

"Of course not. But have you seen anything—noticed anything—which would
justify hope?"

"I have seen her looking at you when you were
not looking at her."

"And how did she look?"

"As people generally look with their eyes. Do not build
too much upon it. But I am favourably impressed. You
need courage. I shall supply it."

I was not certain how courage could be supplied by
a person who hid in an attic from his own
principal, but gratitude prevented the observation.

At last the ghost yawned.

"It is nearly dawn," he said. "I must go. Hinckman
is away, so I may take a little exercise in
the hall before retiring. Remember my vacancy."

He passed through the door, pausing midway to wish me
good night. The sight of half a John Hinckman suspended
in a panel was disturbing; but exhaustion conquered philosophy, and
I slept.

The next morning I found it difficult to decide whether
the interview had been real. A supernatural visitation at half
past one often appears less credible after coffee. Yet while
we sat at breakfast, Madeline remarked that I seemed preoccupied.

"I was visited in the night," I answered before caution
could intervene.

"Visited?"

"By a thought," I added. "A very persistent thought."

"Was it agreeable?"

"The subject was agreeable. The circumstances were peculiar."

She smiled, and I resolved that before luncheon my uncertainty
should end.

We went afterward to the rose garden, where Madeline wished
to gather flowers for the drawing room. I carried the
basket. The morning was bright, the air fragrant, and no
uncle could be seen in any direction. Madeline stood beside
a high hedge, holding a pale rose in her hand,
and I perceived that Providence had arranged the composition with
unusual care.

"Madeline," I began, "there is something which I have long
wished to say to you."

"Then you have been very patient," she replied.

There was encouragement in this, or so I believed.

"It concerns my future," I continued, "and, if I may
be permitted to hope, it may concern yours."

At that instant John Hinckman's face appeared between the branches
of the hedge.

I recoiled so violently that I dropped the basket.

Madeline looked at me in surprise.

"What is it?"

The face resolved itself into that of the ghost, who
emerged through the hedge with an air of satisfaction.

"Excellent!" he said. "Go on. Do not mind me."

"Go away!" I whispered.

Madeline's colour changed slightly.

"I beg your pardon?" she said.

"I was speaking to—to an insect."

"What insect?"

"A very intrusive one."

The ghost bent over the roses.

"I see no insect," he observed. "But your explanation is
good. Proceed."

I attempted to ignore him.

"Madeline," I said, "during the weeks I have spent here,
I have learned that no happiness can be complete unless—"

"Speak more plainly," advised the ghost. "Women dislike abstractions at
such moments."

"Unless it is shared," I continued, "with one whose sympathy,
whose character, whose presence—"

"Too many relatives," said the ghost. "Come to the principal
clause."

"Will you be silent?" I demanded.

Madeline drew herself up.

"I had not spoken," she said.

"No, no! I did not mean you."

"Whom did you mean?"

The ghost shook his head.

"You are mismanaging this deplorably."

"You are the cause of it!" I exclaimed.

Madeline's eyes widened. The rose slipped from her fingers.

"I had no idea," she said coldly, "that my listening
to you was considered an offence."

"It is not! It could never be! I was addressing—"

I stopped. To say that I was addressing the ghost
of her living uncle would not improve the interview.

"Whom?" she asked.

"No one."

"To command no one to be silent seems unnecessary."

"It was an exclamation directed at myself."

"A practice which must make solitude very animated."

She turned towards the house.

In desperation I stepped after her. "Madeline, you misunderstand me
entirely."

"I am glad of that," she answered. "If I understood
you, I might be obliged to resent you."

She left the garden.

The ghost watched her depart and then sighed.

"You should not have dropped the basket," he said.

I faced him with such indignation as a man can
display towards an immaterial being.

"You promised to help me!"

"And I attempted it. You were wandering into a speech
which might have lasted until dinner. I merely urged directness."

"Your interference has ruined me."

"Not at all. A temporary misunderstanding often stimulates interest."

"This is the end of everything."

"My dear fellow, people in love are always reaching the
end of everything, and yet everything commonly continues. Go after
her."

"I cannot."

"Then I will."

"You will do nothing of the kind!"

But he was already moving towards the house.

I sprang before him. "You cannot speak to her. She
cannot hear you."

"That is true," he said, pausing. "It is a defect
in the plan."

"And if she sees you?"

"She will think me her uncle, which may produce complications.
Very well; I shall withdraw. But your manner towards a
person who has offered professional assistance is not encouraging."

He vanished into a clump of lilacs, leaving me alone
with the overturned basket and the reflection that in attempting
to obtain a wife I had offended her, quarrelled with
a ghost, and stamped upon three excellent roses.

During luncheon Madeline spoke to me only when civility made
silence impossible. Her aunt, a gentle lady who regarded all
discomfort as evidence that a window should be closed, noticed
our constraint and asked whether I had a headache. I
said that I had. Madeline remarked that it might be
an insect. Her aunt recommended lavender water.

For the remainder of the afternoon I wandered about the
estate, seeking a method by which the matter could be
explained without disclosing its explanation. Truth is said to be
the shortest road out of difficulty, but those who make
this assertion generally refer to difficulties without ghosts in them.
If I told Madeline what had occurred, she would either
believe me or not. If she did not believe me,
I should appear insulting and deranged; if she did, I
should betray the confidence of a supernatural person whose professional
standing was already insecure.

At dusk I encountered the apparition lounging on the railing
of the eastern porch.

"I have been thinking," he said.

"So have I."

"I hope with better results. Your mistake was in choosing
the rose garden. Hedges give me an irresistible desire to
look through them. Select an open place. I shall remain
absent."

"Can I depend upon that?"

"Certainly. Unless something important occurs."

"Everything you consider important is disastrous to me."

"That is an unjust remark. Have you thought of a
dead person?"

I had, in fact, remembered an item in a newspaper
concerning a Russian nobleman, Prince Karsnov, who had disappeared from
his travelling carriage near the frontier. Blood had been found
upon the cushions, the coachman had fled, and the prince's
relatives were offering rewards while expressing little hope.

The ghost became intensely interested.

"Murder?"

"Probably."

"Noble family?"

"Very old, I believe."

"Castle?"

"Almost certainly."

"Secret?"

"There must be one."

He descended from the railing in excitement.

"This may be precisely what I require! Name, place, date—quickly!"

I supplied all I remembered. He repeated the particulars several
times and declared that he would submit an immediate application.

"Mention," I said, "that the vacancy is uncertain. The body
has not been found."

"So much the better. Uncertainty creates atmosphere. I must go
at once. There are channels for urgent cases."

"Will you be absent long?"

"Until tomorrow, I hope. You may conduct your courtship without
interruption."

He seized my hand; or rather, his hand passed through
mine with a sensation like cold mist.

"If this succeeds, I shall never forget you. Unless forgetting
is required by the transfer."

He disappeared through the wall of the house.

That evening Madeline spent several hours with her aunt. I
saw her only in the drawing room, where the presence
of two other guests, a card table, and a lamp
of remarkable brightness discouraged intimate communication. Once our eyes met.
Hers expressed, I thought, reproach tempered by curiosity. Mine must
have expressed repentance obscured by lampshade.

The ghost did not visit me that night. I slept
little, but this was due not to fear of an
apparition; it was due to fear that no apparition would
come. He alone could corroborate the truth, and he was
the one witness whom I could not produce.

By morning I had formed a plan. I would not
explain the previous day's occurrence; I would apologise for it.
An explanation seeks acquittal, but an apology accepts punishment, and
women are often more merciful when their jurisdiction is conceded.

I found Madeline after breakfast in the library, arranging correspondence
at a writing desk. She looked up when I entered,
and though her manner was reserved, she did not leave.

"Madeline," I said, "I have come to ask your forgiveness."

"For what particular portion of yesterday?"

"For every portion in which I took part."

This brought the faintest change to her expression.

"I behaved unpardonably," I continued. "I spoke in a manner
which you could not understand and then made matters worse
by refusing to explain."

"You seemed to be carrying on a conversation in which
I had been included by mistake."

The accuracy of this description pained me.

"I assure you that nothing I said in impatience was
intended for you."

"Then there was someone else?"

I looked instinctively towards the door, the curtains, and the
bookcases.

"There was a cause," I answered.

"That is not the same thing."

"No. It is much less satisfactory."

She folded a letter slowly.

"Why can you not tell me the truth?"

"Because the truth is incredible."

"Truth is often thought incredible by those who have prepared
something else."

"I have prepared nothing. That is one of my chief
defects."

At this she smiled, though she immediately restrained the expression.

Encouraged, I approached the desk.

"Madeline, believe at least this much: the words which offended
you had no reference to you. If I said, 'Go
away,' I did not wish you to go. If I
said, 'Be silent,' no voice could have been more welcome
to me than yours. And if I appeared distracted, it
was because an interference which you could neither see nor
suspect occurred at the most unfortunate instant possible."

She studied me.

"You speak as though there were an invisible person in
the garden."

I felt the colour leave my face.

"Do I?"

"Yes."

"How singular."

"Very."

For one perilous moment I believed she had guessed everything;
but a rational woman does not readily infer a bureaucratically
displaced ghost from the conduct of a timid admirer.

She rose and went to the window.

"Let us suppose," she said, "that I believe your impatience
was not directed towards me. What were you trying to
say before you became impatient?"

The great opportunity, bruised but living, stood before me again.

"I was trying to speak of my future."

"So I remember."

"And of yours."

"That also remains in my memory."

"Madeline," I said, "there are moments when a man perceives
that all his previous life has been merely a preparation
for one decision."

"Then he should be prepared."

Her quiet tone contained both challenge and kindness. I took
one step towards her.

"I have hesitated, not because I doubted my own feeling,
but because I feared that its disclosure might alter the
happiness I already possess in your friendship. To risk a
certainty for a hope may be called courage by those
who succeed and folly by those who do not."

"Is this a general observation?"

"It is approaching a particular application."

"Then perhaps it should not be delayed."

I drew breath. At that instant a muffled voice came
from the wall behind me.

"Not yet! Wait!"

My whole body stiffened.

Madeline noticed the change.

"What is it?"

"Nothing."

The ghost's head appeared through the paneling above a shelf
of encyclopedias.

"I have news," he whispered.

I shut my eyes.

"Are you unwell?" Madeline asked.

"Perfectly."

The head was followed by shoulders, arms, and the remainder
of the apparition. He stepped out between two volumes of
natural history, looking unusually agitated.

"You must listen," he said. "Hinckman is returning."

I refused to turn towards him.

Madeline waited.

"My application," continued the ghost, "has been considered. There is
a complication concerning the Russian, but I believe it favourable."

"Please," I murmured, "not now."

Madeline's face became pale.

"I see," she said.

"No, you do not!"

The ghost looked from her to me.

"She thinks you are speaking to her again."

"I know she does."

"You know I do what?" asked Madeline.

"This is intolerable," I exclaimed.

Her composure hardened.

"I agree."

She moved towards the door.

I intercepted her. "Madeline, stay. I beg you. I am
caught in circumstances so absurd that I cannot reveal them
and so serious that I cannot ignore them."

"Hinckman will arrive within the hour," said the ghost. "A
telegram came to the station. He has finished with Burdock."

The name, absurdly, gave the announcement an air of official
certainty.

"You have less than an hour," the apparition continued. "Perhaps
less than half an hour. Roads improve one's estimate."

I turned upon him.

"Must you torment me at every decisive moment?"

Madeline recoiled.

"I have done nothing!"

"Not you!" I cried. "Never you!"

"Then whom?"

The ghost waved his arms.

"Do not attempt an explanation. There is no time. Propose
at once. Four words will suffice."

"You promised to remain away."

"I returned only because the circumstances were urgent."

"You have destroyed every hope I had."

"Your hope remains in the room," said the ghost, pointing
to Madeline. "Address it."

Madeline, unfortunately, could see only me pointing nowhere with my
eyes, answering unheard remarks, and exhibiting all the symptoms of
a mind under rapid dissolution.

"I think," she said carefully, "that you should sit down."

"I do not need to sit down."

"Then I do."

She sank into a chair near the window. Her hands
trembled, and at this sight my embarrassment gave way to
misery. Whatever she supposed—that I mocked her, disliked her, or
had gone mad—she was wounded, and I had done it.

The ghost, for once, appeared contrite.

"I may have been overzealous," he said.

"You think so?"

"But the essential matter can still be settled. Speak simply."

Madeline rose again.

"I cannot continue this conversation," she said. "Indeed, I am
not certain that I have been part of it."

"You have been the whole of it," I answered.

She looked at me with such sorrowful doubt that every
elaborate sentence I had prepared deserted me.

"Madeline, I love—"

A distant door slammed.

The ghost uttered a shriek and passed halfway through the
curtain.

"He is here!"

Footsteps sounded in the hall.

The apparition emerged from the curtain in wild agitation.

"I cannot meet him. If the transfer has not taken
effect, I am still attached to him. If it has,
I may be absent from my new post without authority.
Either way, discovery would be ruinous."

The footsteps passed the library door and continued towards the
rear of the house. A servant's voice followed. After a
moment we heard that a messenger, not Mr. Hinckman, had
arrived from the station.

The ghost relaxed.

"False alarm," he said. "Your servants walk like landowners."

Then, from somewhere above us, there came a faint ringing,
though no bell in the room had moved. The ghost
started, listened, and became radiant.

"That is it!"

"What?" I demanded.

"My notice! The transfer!"

He drew from the interior of his coat a pale
document which had not been there before. It seemed made
of smoke compressed into official form. He read rapidly.

"Provisionally confirmed—subject deceased—violent circumstances established—ancestral residence suitable—duties to commence immediately—yes!
Yes! Karsnov was murdered, the body has been found beneath
a bridge, and I am appointed!"

He rushed about the room in an ecstasy, passing through
a table without noticing it.

"At last I am the proper ghost of somebody! No
more hiding in attics, no more listening for Hinckman's step,
no more being haunted by a living man! I have
a castle, a murder, and three generations of suspected relatives.
It is everything I could have desired."

Madeline had withdrawn several paces and was watching me with
tears in her eyes. She could not hear the announcement;
she could only see that, at the climax of our
interview, my attention had again been seized by emptiness.

The ghost approached me.

"My dear friend, I owe this to you. I regret
that my assistance in your affair has not been equally
successful, but courtship is a department of which I have
little experience. Good-bye! I must report before midnight."

He began to fade.

The old likeness to Hinckman softened; the severe face became
transparent, the broad shoulders dissolved into the shadows, and soon
only his delighted expression remained.

My last hope of explanation, companionship, and sanity was vanishing
with him. He was free, while I was left in
the wreckage he had helped create. In a passion of
despair I stretched out my hands towards the fading apparition.

"I would to heaven you were mine!" I cried.

There was silence.

The ghost disappeared.

Then I heard a sob.

Madeline stood by the window, her face turned from me.
The meaning of my exclamation, as it must have reached
her, flashed upon my mind; but before I could correct
it, qualify it, or destroy its only possible usefulness by
explaining it, she turned.

"I am yours," she said.

For once in my life I did not deliberate.

I crossed the space between us and took her hands.
Whether she perceived in my face the declaration which my
lips had failed so repeatedly to accomplish, or whether Providence,
wearied by my circumlocutions, had resolved to accept a sentence

A carriage sounded upon the drive.

"That is Uncle John," she whispered.

The old terror stirred within me, but it found itself
in altered company. A man who has obtained the answer
upon which his future depends can confront many things, including
an irascible uncle.

Mr. Hinckman entered the library five minutes later. He looked
first at Madeline, then at me, then at our joined
hands.

"What does this mean?" he demanded.

It was precisely the question I had feared for weeks.
Yet now it seemed wonderfully simple.

"It means, sir," I said, "that Madeline has consented to
marry me."

He frowned, opened his mouth, and glanced at his niece.

"Is that true?"

"Yes, Uncle John."

He continued to frown, but the concealed spring did not
overflow.

"Well," he said, "it is time. I was beginning to
think the fellow would never ask you."

There are revelations which overturn one's conception of the universe.
This was one of them.

Later, when Madeline and I were alone, she asked me
whether I would now explain my extraordinary conduct in the
rose garden and the library.

I considered telling her everything. But the ghost had departed
to a more legitimate sphere, and I felt that his
professional confidence should be respected. Moreover, there are truths which
strengthen affection and truths which merely encourage questions.

"My dear," I said, "I was struggling with an interruption."

"An invisible one?"

"To you."

"And it has gone?"

"I sincerely believe it has."

She looked at me for a long moment.

"I shall accept that explanation," she said, "because I prefer
it to all the others."

Many months afterward, in a newspaper which had arrived from
Europe, I read an account of strange manifestations in the
ancestral castle of the Karsnov family. Doors opened of themselves;
footsteps crossed locked chambers; a gray, broad-shouldered figure appeared in
corridors; and several suspected relatives had confessed to crimes which
the authorities had not known were committed. The correspondent remarked
that the ghost seemed exceptionally active and perhaps somewhat inexperienced,
but that its position in the household was now unquestioned.

I showed the paragraph to Madeline.

She read it, smiled, and returned the paper.

"An invisible interruption?" she asked.

"An old acquaintance," I replied.

She has never pressed me further. And if, in the
years since our marriage, I have sometimes been slow to
express what I think or feel, she has only to
say, "There is no one else in the room," and
I immediately become direct.

Thus the apparition who had been wrongly appointed to John
Hinckman, while failing in every deliberate attempt to assist my
courtship, accomplished at his departure what neither moonlight, solitude, nor
my own carefully prepared eloquence had been able to achieve.
He obtained his proper ghostship, I obtained Madeline, and Mr.
Hinckman, though still alive and formidable, ceased forever to haunt
either of us.

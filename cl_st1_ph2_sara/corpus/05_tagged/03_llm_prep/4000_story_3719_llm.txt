I am forty-three years old, and I look sixty. Two
years ago I possessed a sound constitution, a clear eye,
a respectable appetite, and a confidence in the future which
was perhaps unbecoming in a bachelor, but was nevertheless sincere.
To-day I am a wreck. The physicians call my disorder
by several long names, according to the school in which
they were educated; but I know that my complaint is
simply the consequence of one night's exposure, assisted by terror,
affection, and an unusually active imagination.

I am going home to Wisconsin to die. Before I
do so, I wish to set down the facts. They
may appear exaggerated, but they are not. Some things are
naturally too improbable to be improved by invention.

My childhood companion, Edwin Marsh, died in the East during
the winter. His parents, who were old and unable to
travel, desired that his remains be brought to their village
near Lake Winnebago. I volunteered for the melancholy office. Edwin
and I had fished, fought, frozen, and been whipped together
as boys; and though the years had scattered us, I
could not permit a stranger to conduct him on his
last journey.

The undertaker enclosed the coffin in a plain pine shipping-case
and delivered it at the railway station. At nearly the
same hour a merchant deposited there a box of rifles,
intended for a sporting-goods house in Illinois. The two boxes
were much alike in dimensions, and each had been hastily
marked. Through an error which was discovered too late, the
merchant received Edwin and went away with him, while I
received the rifles and supposed them to be my poor
departed friend.

I state this explanation now so that the reader may
understand what Thompson and I did not. Without that knowledge,
our conduct might seem unreasonable. With it, I think our
conduct will seem merely disastrous.

The box was placed in the express car. I accompanied
it, having obtained permission to ride there because I could
not bear the thought of Edwin traveling among strangers. While
the station men were shifting freight, somebody set a round
parcel upon the coffin-box—I mean the rifle-case. This parcel contained
Limburger cheese. It was old cheese; not old in the
weak, numerical sense of having existed for a certain period,
but old in character, experience, influence, and settled purpose.

The night was bitter. Snow drove level across the yards,
and the wind behaved as if it had private grievances
against the railway company. The express car, however, was heated
by a large iron stove until it possessed the temperature
of a conservatory. Its doors were shut tightly; one small
window was broken, but Thompson, the expressman, had stuffed a
folded horse-blanket into it.

Thompson was a broad, red-faced man with a yellow mustache
and the composed manner of one who had met most
of the troubles recognized by the Interstate Commerce Commission. When
I explained my mission, he removed his cap.

"Friend of yourn?" he asked.

"My oldest friend."

"Well, sir, I'm sorry. I've handled a many, first an'
last, but I don't never handle 'em careless. A man
that can't show consideration to the departed ain't fit to
be trusted with live packages."

He helped me arrange some mail sacks near the box,
and I sat down. As the train pulled away, Thompson
stood looking thoughtfully at the case.

"Was he took sudden?"

"Very."

"That's generally the way. Here to-day, transferred to-morrow. Life's a
branch line, an' none of us knows where the washout
is."

This observation affected me. I turned my face away, and
for several minutes neither of us spoke.

The car grew warmer. Presently I noticed a faint but
peculiar odor. It was not exactly disagreeable at first; it
was merely earnest. It seemed to be examining the premises.
Thompson sniffed once, then again, and looked toward the box.

"Your friend ben dead long?"

"Three days."

He considered this.

"Three days can make a heap of difference in a
man, specially with steam heat."

The remark shocked me, but I could not deny its
practical force. We smoked cigars. Thompson supplied them, saying tobacco
had defeated many a bad atmosphere in his experience. For
a little while the smoke held its ground. Then the
other odor came through it, quietly but confidently, like an
official with authority to clear a room.

Thompson took his cigar from his mouth.

"He's gettin' gamey," he said.

I protested that the coffin had been properly prepared.

"Oh, I ain't criticizin' the arrangements. They may've started proper.
But thish-yer climate is stimulatin'. Your Colonel's wakin' up to
business."

He had begun by calling Edwin "your friend." The promotion
to Colonel disturbed me. It suggested that the invisible power
inside the box had acquired rank through merit.

We opened the side door six inches. Snow swept in
at once, whitening the floor and putting out the lamp.
The temperature fell with astonishing speed. We shut the door,
relighted the lamp, and huddled by the stove. In two
minutes we were warm again. In three minutes the odor
had advanced from earnestness to enthusiasm.

It became richer, deeper, and more inclusive. It found its
way into one's clothes and hair. It seemed less an
odor than a condition of existence.

Thompson examined the box from several directions.

"I've knowed 'em to be powerful," he said, "but the
Colonel's got reach. He don't wait for you to come
to him; he comes after you."

We shifted the case to the far end of the
car. This required considerable labor, for rifles are heavy, though
we believed the weight represented the mortal remains of Edwin.
Thompson used a crowbar while I pushed. During the operation
the parcel of cheese rolled off, unnoticed, and lodged against
the stove.

The improvement we expected did not occur.

Instead the heat entered into a partnership with the cheese.
The fragrance rose, expanded, and assumed command. It appeared to
possess layers. After one had endured the first layer and
made such inward arrangements as seemed necessary, another arrived, broader
and more deliberate; behind that came a third, with ornaments.

Thompson dropped the crowbar.

"That settles it. Movin' him only riled him. The Gen'rul
don't intend to be dictated to."

He tore the blanket from the broken window, and we
placed our faces near the opening. The wind cut us
like flying glass, but it was clean. We breathed it
gratefully until our ears became numb. Then we returned to
the stove, where the Gen'rul received us.

Thus began our system. We froze at the window until
life was painful, then warmed ourselves until life was impossible.
Between these extremes there was no neutral territory.

At the next station Thompson obtained carbolic acid from a
railway medicine chest. He sprinkled it about with confidence.

"Carbolic's a master hand," he said. "It takes charge of
anything short of politics."

For thirty seconds the acid prevailed. Then the cheese absorbed
it, improved it, and returned it to us altered beyond
recognition. The combined odor had the clean, sharp quality of
a hospital built over a tannery, together with a certain
rural sadness which I cannot describe.

Thompson staggered to the door and put his head outside.

When he returned, powdered with snow, he said, "The Gen'rul's
promoted hisself. He's a Commodore now. He's took the carbolic
fleet an' turned its guns on us."

I did not answer. Speech requires breath, and breath had
become a costly indulgence.

We sat on the platform between the cars. The train
was running rapidly through open country. Snow collected on our
coats; sparks flew over us from the engine; the iron
railings burned our hands with cold. Yet the storm seemed
charitable compared with the interior of the express car.

After perhaps twenty minutes, Thompson's teeth began striking together with
the regularity of telegraph instruments.

"We got to go in," he said.

"I cannot."

"You got to. Freezin' is certain, but the Commodore's only
probable."

This was bad logic, but we were too cold to
expose it. We reentered.

The accumulated atmosphere met us at the door. It had
been strengthening in our absence. I fell backward against a
trunk; Thompson caught me, lost his own balance, and sat
down in a basket of eggs. The disaster to the
eggs was extensive, but their contribution to the air was
insignificant.

For some moments we breathed through handkerchiefs. Then Thompson said:

"There's a principle in warfare which says fight smoke with
smoke, fire with fire, an' a full house with four
of a kind. We ain't tried everything yet."

From among the express parcels he produced feathers, dried apples,
a strip of leather, several old woolen tags, and some
sulphur matches. He placed these in a shallow iron pan
upon the stove. I asked what he meant to do.

"Counteract him."

"With that?"

"Stranger, when a man's drownin', he don't inquire into the
family connections of the rope."

The mixture began to burn.

Its first effect was promising. A black column of smoke
climbed toward the roof and spread beneath it. The feathers
supplied a penetrating domestic bitterness; the leather contributed a solid,
durable body; the woolen tags added mystery; and the sulphur
matches gave the whole composition a theological tendency.

We waited.

From the far end of the car came the influence
of the cheese. It encountered Thompson's preparation, considered it, embraced
it, and passed onward with increased vigor.

Thompson's eyes filled with tears.

"He's used it," he whispered. "The Governor's took our ammunition."

We fled to the platform.

I do not know how long we remained there. Time
had ceased to be measured by minutes and was measured
instead by degrees of suffering. When we could no longer
endure the cold, we went inside; when the Governor approached
the higher limits of his authority, we went out again.
Each return was more dreadful than the last, because the
stove grew hotter and the burning mixture acquired new accomplishments.

Thompson's titles for Edwin continued to rise. He became Senator,
President, Emperor, and finally "the Administration." Thompson declared that no
two private citizens could resist an Administration which had the
weather and the express company on its side.

Between our excursions he moralized.

"It shows you," he said, crouching beside the open door,
"what pride comes to. Man struts around, buyin' clothes an'
votin' an' tellin' his wife how railroads ought to be
run; then some day he's boxed up, an' two other
men spend the night tryin' to keep out of his
way."

I said Edwin had never married.

"That proves it stronger. Even without practice, he can drive
folks outdoors."

At another time Thompson looked solemnly toward the rifle-case and
said:

"I respect him. I don't enjoy him, but I respect
him. There ain't one man in ten thousand could take
carbolic acid, burnt feathers, leather, sulphur, eggs, cigar smoke, an'
a red-hot stove, an' make 'em all smell like they
belonged to him from birth."

This tribute to my friend's supposed powers affected me strangely.
I wished to defend Edwin's memory, yet every breath supplied
testimony for the prosecution. Grief, nausea, cold, and embarrassment struggled
within me, and nausea won.

Near midnight the train stopped at a small station. Thompson
and I descended and ran about the platform, breathing deeply.
A brakeman opened the express car, entered, and came out
so rapidly that his cap remained inside.

"What you got in there?" he demanded.

Thompson pointed toward me.

"Passenger."

The brakeman looked at me with horror and moved away.

We tried to explain, but the train started. Thompson swung
aboard. I followed, and we resumed our position on the
outer platform. Neither of us had the courage to recover
the brakeman's cap.

By two in the morning our resistance was nearly ended.
The cold had stiffened our clothing; the heat had weakened
our hearts; the perfumes had confused our minds. Thompson became
convinced that the box was moving toward us. It was
not; but after he mentioned it, I could see it
moving too.

"He's tired of waitin'," Thompson muttered. "He's comin' to negotiate."

We retreated to the platform and held the door shut
against him.

Presently I remember lying in the snow that had gathered
between the cars. Thompson was beside me, reciting fragments of
Scripture and railway regulations, mixing them without distinction.

"Though I walk through the valley of the shadow," he
said, "no baggage will be delivered without surrender of the
proper check."

After that my recollections are imperfect. There was a lantern
swinging above me, men shouting, a station roof moving in
circles, and Thompson insisting that the Administration be unloaded first
because it had seniority. We had been found unconscious at
a junction, half frozen and severely poisoned by smoke, exhaustion,
and apprehension.

I awoke in a hotel three days later with a
fever. For several weeks my life was uncertain. Thompson recovered
more quickly, owing, the doctor said, to his robust habit
and lack of imagination. I do not believe this was
intended as a compliment to either of us.

The truth reached me gradually.

At the original station the labels had been exchanged. The
case I guarded with such devotion contained twenty-four repeating rifles.
Poor Edwin had traveled comfortably in the merchant's wagon until
the error was discovered. The parcel on our case contained
Limburger cheese consigned to a German grocer. During the night
it had rested close to the stove and achieved that
complete expression of which its species is capable.

Everyone supposed this explanation would restore me.

It did not.

Facts may correct the judgment, but they cannot always reach
the nerves. My reason accepted the rifles; my body continued
to believe in Edwin. If a stove grows too warm,
I become faint. The smell of carbolic acid causes chills.
A cigar in a closed room gives me visions of
snow, and the sight of cheese has more than once
compelled me to leave a table without apology.

My health declined steadily. Specialists prescribed travel, rest, exercise, electricity,
mineral water, sea air, mountain air, and cheerfulness. I tried
them all. Cheerfulness was the most expensive and did the
least good.

Thompson wrote to me once. His letter was kind, though
not consoling. He said he had abandoned the express service
and was running a feed store in Iowa. He added
that he never handled cheese except with the doors open,
and that whenever a customer asked for Limburger he addressed
him as "Governor."

As for me, I have no resentment toward the railway,
the merchant, the cheese, or my poor departed friend. Each
acted according to its nature. Edwin was innocent; the rifles
were silent; the cheese fulfilled its destiny; Thompson displayed courage;
and I displayed, I trust, fidelity.

But fidelity has cost me my life.

The doctors say there is nothing organically wrong with me.
This is their final cruelty. I know better. A man
may be killed by what did not happen as surely
as by what did. For one whole night I believed
I was shut in a heated car with the advancing
corruption of my oldest friend, and belief performed the work
which reality had neglected.

Therefore I am returning to Wisconsin. The journey is being
made in a passenger coach. My window is open, though
it is January, and I have forbidden the porter to
light the stove.

In the baggage car behind me there may be rifles,
coffins, or cheese. I have not inquired.

There are limits to courage.

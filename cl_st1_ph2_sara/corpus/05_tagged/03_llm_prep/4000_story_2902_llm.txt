# The Price of Shelter

If, while travelling in Corsica, you leave the coast near
Porto-Vecchio and turn northwest toward the mountains, you will soon
discover a country in which roads appear less the work
of men than the accidental passages of goats. Granite ridges
rise one behind another; ravines, dry in summer and furious
in winter, divide the slopes; and the low hills are
covered with an almost impenetrable vegetation which the inhabitants call
the *maquis*. Myrtle, arbutus, lentisk, box, wild olive and several
thorny shrubs—whose names, I confess, I never learned—grow there so
closely intertwined that a hare can cross beneath them without
showing its ears, while a man, unless he knows the
paths, advances only at the cost of his coat and
sometimes of his blood.

The formation of the maquis is easily explained. For generations
the peasants have burned patches of woodland in order to
sow grain. When the thin soil has yielded two or
three harvests, they abandon it; roots that escaped the fire
send up new shoots, seeds are carried by birds, and
in a few years the deserted field becomes a fortress.
One may see, beneath the shrubs, blackened stumps and stones
gathered into irregular lines by hands long dead. Thus agriculture
builds a refuge for outlawry.

If you have killed a man—over an inheritance, a girl,
a mule, or some such serious trifle—go into the maquis
before the gendarmes arrive. Take a good gun, powder, balls,
a cloak and a pair of strong shoes. Your cousins
will bring you bread, cheese and chestnuts; the shepherds will
warn you of patrols; and no soldier, burdened with a
cartridge box, a bayonet and the necessity of obeying orders,
will easily find you among those green labyrinths. A bandit
who is prudent, and who does not quarrel with every
passer-by, may live there for years. Sometimes he descends at
night to hear Mass or to embrace his children. Rumor
even tells of men who have attended their own trials,
hidden in the gallery of the courthouse, and returned to
the hills before sentence was pronounced.

The house of Mateo Falcone stood half a league from
the edge of one of these wildernesses. It was a
low building of rough stone, roofed with flat tiles and
surrounded by a yard in which lay a woodpile, a
broken plough, several gourds drying in the sun, and a
haystack large enough to feed two cows through the winter.
Beyond it stretched a few enclosed fields; beyond the fields
began the maquis; and behind that, blue even at noon,
rose the mountains.

I visited that district some years after the event I
am about to relate. An old shepherd pointed out the
ruins of the house with the end of his crook
and assured me that, when he was young, the name
of Mateo Falcone was known from Bonifacio to the forests
above Zonza. It is possible that age enlarged his recollections;
nevertheless, all accounts agree that Mateo was a remarkable shot.

At eighty paces he could break the stem of a
clay pipe. At a hundred and twenty he rarely missed
a mouflon, though the animal might be running among rocks.
He preferred a long gun with a narrow stock, made
on the mainland and fitted with a Spanish barrel. He
loaded slowly, measured his powder with care, and never fired
merely to make a noise. It was said that, in
a dispute, he had once invited an enemy to stand
behind a cork oak. The man accepted, believing himself safe.
Mateo's ball entered the trunk, passed through it, and wounded
him in the shoulder. This exploit restored peace between the
families.

In appearance Mateo was short but powerful. His hair, still
black though he was past fifty, curled close about a
broad forehead. He had an aquiline nose, a beard cut
square, and eyes so bright that, when he looked fixedly
at a man, the latter generally remembered an appointment elsewhere.
He was neither quarrelsome nor vain. He offered hospitality generously,
kept his word, and did not speak of those whom
he had killed. I have been told there were only
two, both before his marriage and both on occasions concerning
which respectable people preferred not to ask questions.

His wife, Giuseppa, belonged to a family of some consequence
in the interior. She had given him three daughters before,
at last, presenting him with a son. The daughters were
already married; the boy, Fortunato, was ten years old and
was expected, in due time, to preserve the name, the
house, the rifle and those obligations toward friends and enemies
which form a Corsican inheritance no less certainly than land.

One autumn morning Mateo and Giuseppa set out early to
inspect a flock grazing in a clearing beyond the maquis.
They expected to return before noon. Mateo carried his gun
and a stiletto; his wife bore a sack containing bread,
onions and a flask of wine. It may seem unjust
that she also carried a heavy basket intended for cheese,
but in that country it was considered unbecoming for a
man to carry any burden except his weapons.

Fortunato remained to guard the house.

The boy wore a brown woollen jacket, patched trousers, rawhide
shoes and a red cap which had once belonged to
one of his brothers-in-law. He was small for his age,
but strongly built, with dark skin, wide black eyes and
teeth of astonishing whiteness. His father had already taught him
to load a pistol, to distinguish the tracks of a
sheep from those of a goat, and to lie without
blinking when a stranger asked an impertinent question. The last
accomplishment should not be mistaken for ordinary dishonesty. In the
maquis, curiosity often wears a uniform.

For about an hour Fortunato lay in the shade of
the house, watching a line of ants drag the remains
of a lizard across the dust. Then he climbed onto
the wall, threw stones at a crow and missed it
twice. This annoyed him. He was searching for a third
stone when a distant report echoed from the hills.

Fortunato stood still.

A second shot followed, then several more in quick succession.
They came from the direction of the ravine. The boy
listened with his head inclined. Presently he heard branches breaking,
stones rolling, and the harsh breathing of a man running
beyond his strength.

A figure burst from the scrub.

He was a young man, though his beard and the
mud upon his face made him appear older. His pointed
cap had fallen back; one sleeve of his jacket was
torn; a cartridge belt crossed his chest. He carried a
gun, but used it as a crutch, for his left
thigh was wounded. Blood had filled his shoe and left
dark drops on the stones behind him.

He saw Fortunato and stopped.

"Are you Mateo Falcone's son?"

"Yes."

"Is your father here?"

"No."

The stranger looked toward the maquis. A faint shout came
from the valley.

"My name is Gianetto Sanpiero," he said. "The yellow-collars are
after me. Hide me."

Fortunato remained seated upon the wall and swung one leg.

"Hide you where?"

"In the house. Under the bed. In the hay. Anywhere!"

"And what will my father say if I hide a
man without his permission?"

Gianetto thrust a hand into his belt. He winced as
his weight shifted onto the wounded leg.

"Your father will say that his son acted as a
Falcone should."

"Perhaps."

"Quickly, child! They are close."

Fortunato looked at the blood on the ground.

"You have left a path."

"Cover it."

"With what?"

"With dust—with branches! Are you a baby?"

Fortunato's eyes narrowed. Gianetto immediately changed his tone.

"You are a clever boy. Everyone says so. Help me,
and when I am safe I shall remember you."

"People remember many things," said Fortunato. "They forget debts."

The outlaw searched his pockets. From one he drew a
five-franc piece, dull with handling but very large in the
boy's eyes. He held it up.

"Will you forget this?"

Fortunato leaped from the wall like a fawn. He caught
the coin, bit it, and slipped it under his jacket.

"Come."

He led Gianetto to the haystack. Near the ground there
was a hollow where the hay had been pulled loose
to feed the cattle. Fortunato enlarged it rapidly with both
hands. The wounded man crawled inside, dragging his gun after
him.

"Not so far," whispered the boy. "You will make the
other side move."

He arranged the hay over the opening, pressed it into
place and scattered loose stalks about the ground. Then, after
considering the matter, he fetched a cat that slept near
the woodpile, tied it by one hind leg to a
short cord, and placed it upon the hay. The offended
animal began to struggle, shaking the surface and leaving confused
marks. Anyone examining the stack would suppose that the disturbance
had been caused by the cat.

Fortunato next swept dust over the drops of blood with
a leafy branch. He threw the branch onto the fire,
waited until the leaves curled black, and returned to the
shade of the wall. By the time twelve soldiers appeared
at the end of the field, he was calmly eating
a piece of bread.

They were voltigeurs: light infantry recruited in the island and
employed principally in chasing smugglers, deserters and bandits. Their brown
uniforms were whitened with dust; their yellow collars were open
at the throat; and perspiration ran from beneath their caps.
At their head came Adjutant Tiodoro Gamba, a distant relative
of the Falcone family—a circumstance which, in Corsica, might mean
either friendship or a particularly well-informed hatred.

Gamba was a large man with heavy black whiskers. He
carried a short carbine and walked with the assurance of
one who expects doors to open before he kicks them.
Seeing the boy, he raised his hand, and the soldiers
halted.

"Good day, little cousin."

"Good day, cousin."

"You have grown."

"So people tell me."

"And you will grow faster if you answer promptly. Have
you seen a man pass this way?"

Fortunato took another bite of bread.

"What man?"

"A man with a pointed cap, a green jacket and
a wound in his thigh."

"A man with a wound?"

"Yes."

"In his thigh?"

Gamba frowned.

"You heard me. His name is Gianetto Sanpiero."

"Gianetto Sanpiero?"

"By Saint Anne, will you repeat everything I say?"

Fortunato smiled.

"If I did not hear it properly, I might answer
wrongly."

The soldiers laughed. Gamba turned upon them, and they became
interested in the condition of their flints.

"Listen, nephew," he said. "Sanpiero came this way. We found
his blood on the rocks. Where did he go?"

"How should I know?"

"You were here."

"I was watching the ants."

"Did the ants see him?"

"They did not tell me."

Gamba seized the boy by the ear. Fortunato's bread fell
into the dust, but he neither cried out nor attempted
to pull away.

"You little fox! I could twist this off."

"My father would know who had done it."

At the name of Mateo, the adjutant released him. His
gesture was almost careless; nevertheless, one of the soldiers smiled.

"Your father is not here," Gamba said.

"He will come back."

"And before he does, I may take you to the
town. There is a stone room there, without windows. Boys
who will not speak are locked inside until they become
talkative."

Fortunato looked past him toward the mountains.

"Will you carry me?"

"You will march."

"My shoes hurt."

"We shall tie you behind a mule."

"Then the mule will carry me."

Again there was laughter, more cautiously concealed.

Gamba bit his moustache. He knew the habits of the
country well enough to understand that threats had failed. A
blow might make the boy obstinate; torture—although certain old sergeants
could have suggested effective methods—was both illegal and inconvenient within
sight of Mateo Falcone's house. He therefore changed his manner.

"Come, Fortunato. We are relatives. I do not wish to
treat you like an enemy. Tell me where Gianetto is,
and I shall bring you something from town."

"What?"

"Whatever you like."

"A gun?"

"You are too young."

"My father gave me a pistol."

"A knife, then."

"I have one."

Gamba searched his pockets. He found a small penknife, two
cartridges, a piece of string and a copper medal of
the Virgin. None of these produced the least alteration in
Fortunato's face. At last he drew out his watch.

It was not an object of great magnificence by continental
standards; in the mountains, however, it might have passed for
a prince's ransom. The case was silver, polished until it
shone like water under the sun. A fine chain ran
from its ring and disappeared into Gamba's waistcoat. The pale-blue
dial was painted with tiny golden flowers. When the adjutant
pressed the spring, the cover opened with a soft click.

Fortunato ceased swinging his leg.

Gamba saw the movement.

"You have never owned a watch, I suppose."

"My uncle Pietrino has one."

"His is old and thick as a cheese."

"It strikes."

"This one does not strike, but it keeps better time.
Listen."

He held it near the boy's ear.

Fortunato heard the quick, delicate ticking. No living thing he
knew had so small and regular a heart. The chain
trembled between Gamba's fingers, sending points of light onto the
wall.

"My cousin in Ajaccio has a watch with three covers,"
Fortunato said.

"Your cousin is a liar. This is a watch from
Marseille. It cost ten crowns."

"Then you will not give it away."

"Not ordinarily."

Gamba closed the case, tossed the watch into the air
and caught it. Fortunato followed its flight with the fixed
attention of a cat watching a young chicken pass within
reach.

"Suppose," continued the adjutant, "I were to fasten this chain
around your neck. On Sundays you would enter the church
square, and every boy would ask you the hour. Even
grown men might ask."

"I do not know how to read a watch."

"I shall teach you. The long hand gives the minutes;
the short hand gives the hours. In one lesson you
will know more than your uncle Pietrino."

Fortunato put both hands under his thighs.

The soldiers watched in silence. One of them, a thin
man with a scar across his mouth, glanced toward the
haystack and then back to the boy. The cat had
stopped struggling and lay panting upon the warm hay.

Gamba let the watch hang by its chain. It swung
before Fortunato's eyes.

"Tell me where Gianetto is, and it is yours."

The boy swallowed.

"Give it to me first."

"No. Show me first."

"You may put it away."

"And you may lie."

"My father says a Falcone's word—"

"Your father's word, perhaps. You are only ten."

Fortunato's cheeks darkened.

"I am nearly eleven."

"Then prove you are old enough to bargain."

The watch swung left, right, left. Sunlight slipped around its
rim. Fortunato thought of the five-franc piece hidden beneath his
jacket. It had seemed enormous a quarter of an hour
before; now it was merely a coin, good for cakes,
powder or a red silk handkerchief. One spent a coin
and it vanished. A watch remained. It spoke without words;
it made men look at its owner; it divided the
day as priests divided prayers.

From inside the hay came no sound.

Gamba began winding the chain around his hand.

"Very well. We shall search elsewhere."

He turned.

Fortunato's chest rose sharply.

"Cousin."

Gamba looked back.

"Is it truly mine?"

"If you show me where he is."

The boy stretched out his right hand, but the adjutant
held the watch just beyond his fingertips.

"Where?"

Fortunato's hand remained suspended. His eyes moved once toward the
maquis, where his father would presently appear; once toward the
road, down which the soldiers might vanish; and finally toward
the shining case.

He pointed with his thumb at the haystack.

Gamba snapped the chain loose from his waistcoat and dropped
the watch into the boy's palm.

"Search it," he ordered.

Four voltigeurs surrounded the stack. Two lowered their guns while
another cut the cat's cord. The animal sprang away. The
scar-mouthed soldier drove his bayonet into the hay.

A muffled curse answered him.

"Come out, Gianetto!" shouted Gamba. "We know you are there."

For a moment nothing moved. Then the hay heaved. A
gun barrel emerged first and fell to the ground. Gianetto
crawled after it, pale beneath the dirt, his hair filled
with yellow stalks. The wound had opened again. He struggled
upright, leaning against the stack, and looked about him.

His eyes found Fortunato.

The boy was fastening the chain around his neck. His
fingers trembled slightly, either from excitement or from shame.

"Son of Mateo Falcone," said Gianetto, "remember that a traitor
enters hell before the man he sells."

Fortunato drew back.

"I hid you. You paid me."

"And he paid you more."

The soldiers bound Gianetto's hands behind him. When they tried
to make him walk, his wounded leg failed, and he
fell on one knee. Gamba ordered a litter made from
branches and military cloaks. While this was being done, Fortunato
ran into the house and brought out a wooden bowl
of water.

Gianetto turned his face away.

"I will not drink from a traitor's house."

"It is my mother's bowl," said the boy.

"Then take it back before you soil it."

Fortunato's lips tightened. He threw the water into the dust.

The soldiers placed their prisoner upon the improvised litter. Gamba
was examining the bandage around Gianetto's thigh when one of
the men, stationed near the field wall, called out:

"Two people coming from the maquis!"

Fortunato looked up. Mateo and Giuseppa had appeared on the
path. Mateo walked first, his gun across his arm; his
wife followed under the basket, which was now filled with
round white cheeses. They were still some distance away, but
they had already seen the soldiers.

Gamba muttered an oath.

He was brave, and he had merely performed his duty;
yet he would have preferred to meet Mateo Falcone with
his men arranged in good order and no silver chain
missing from his waistcoat.

Mateo approached without haste. At thirty paces he halted, lowered
the muzzle of his gun toward the ground and cocked
it. The sound was distinct.

Gamba stepped forward.

"Good day, Mateo."

"Good day, Tiodoro."

"We have made a capture."

"So I see."

Mateo's eyes passed over the soldiers, the litter, the blood
near the hay and his son standing against the wall.
They rested for a moment on the watch.

Gamba cleared his throat.

"This is Gianetto Sanpiero. He wounded one of our men
near the ravine and tried to escape through your field.
Your boy assisted us."

"Fortunato assisted you?"

"He is a clever child."

Mateo did not move. Giuseppa came up behind him, set
down her basket and crossed herself when she saw the
wounded man.

"Gianetto," she exclaimed, "what have you done?"

"He has done nothing here," said Gamba quickly. "We shall
take him to town."

Gianetto raised his head from the litter.

"Mateo Falcone," he said, "I was hidden beneath your hay.
Your son sold me."

Mateo's face showed no change. Only the barrel of his
gun sank a little lower.

"Is this true?" he asked Gamba.

The adjutant attempted a laugh.

"Children like pretty things. I questioned him, and after some
difficulty—well, I gave him my watch."

"The watch on his neck?"

"Yes. It was a reward. You understand; the service—"

Mateo lifted the gun suddenly and brought the butt down
upon the watch. The silver case split against the stones.
The glass flew into the dust; the blue dial cracked
across the centre; the minute hand continued to tremble for
several seconds, absurdly busy amid the fragments.

Fortunato gave a cry and sprang backward.

Gamba stared at what remained of his property.

"Mateo! That watch cost—"

"Take your prisoner away."

The adjutant's whiskers quivered. He seemed about to speak, thought
better of it, and ordered the men to lift the
litter.

As they passed Mateo, Gianetto turned his face toward the
house and spat upon the ground.

"House of traitors," he said.

Mateo grew pale.

One soldier stumbled under the weight of the litter. Gianetto
groaned but did not again look at Fortunato. Soon the
party entered the maquis, and the noise of boots, branches
and weapons faded down the path.

No one spoke.

A fly settled upon one of the cheeses in Giuseppa's
basket. She brushed it away, adjusted her headcloth and looked
anxiously at her husband. Fortunato stood near the broken watch.
He had picked up the chain and wound it around
his fingers.

Mateo laid his gun on the wall.

"Boy," he said, "is Gianetto's story true?"

Fortunato stared at the ground.

"He gave me five francs to hide him."

"And you hid him?"

"Yes."

"Then the soldiers came?"

"Yes."

"And you showed them his hiding place for a watch?"

Fortunato did not answer.

Mateo took one step forward.

"For a watch?"

"Yes."

Giuseppa uttered a low moan.

Fortunato thrust a hand beneath his jacket and brought out
the five-franc piece.

"Here," he said. "I did not keep everything."

He offered the coin to his father, as though the
return of one payment could undo the acceptance of another.

Mateo struck his hand aside. The coin rolled between the
stones and stopped near the spilled water.

"Out of my sight."

The boy ran into the house.

Giuseppa caught her husband's sleeve.

"He is a child."

"He bears my name."

"He was tempted. Even men are tempted."

"Men pay for it."

"He did not know."

"He knew how to bargain."

Mateo picked up his gun and examined the priming. He
poured a little powder into the pan, closed it, and
slung the weapon over his shoulder.

"Call him."

Giuseppa's hands began to shake.

"Mateo, what are you going to do?"

"Call him."

She obeyed. In that house Mateo's quiet voice had always
been more absolute than another man's anger.

Fortunato appeared in the doorway. The red cap was gone;
his black curls clung to his forehead. He had been
crying, but on seeing his father he wiped his face
quickly with his sleeve.

"Come with me," Mateo said.

"Where?"

"To the ravine."

Giuseppa placed herself between them.

"No."

Mateo looked at her.

She fell to her knees.

"He is our only son. I carried him. I prayed
for him before he was born. Mateo, by the Holy
Virgin, forgive him!"

"Has our family ever had a traitor?"

Giuseppa bowed her head.

"Answer."

"No."

"Then do not ask me to keep one."

He took Fortunato by the shoulder and led him toward
the maquis. The boy went quietly at first, perhaps believing
that his father meant only to frighten him. Giuseppa remained
on her knees beside the wall, murmuring prayers so rapidly
that the words ran into one another.

The path descended behind the house, crossed a dry streambed
and entered a narrow ravine between two walls of granite.
Holm oaks grew from the cracks. The earth was soft
beneath them, and dead leaves lay thick in the hollows.
Mateo walked ahead now; Fortunato followed three paces behind.

After several minutes the boy said:

"Father, the soldiers threatened to take me away."

Mateo did not answer.

"They said they would put me in a room without
windows."

Silence.

"I did not tell them at first. I told them
nothing for a long time. Ask the soldiers."

Mateo continued walking.

Fortunato stumbled over a root.

"Father, my shoe is torn."

"Walk."

They reached a place where the ravine divided. One branch
led toward the road; the other ended after a short
distance beneath a granite cliff. Mateo chose the second. There,
hidden from the house and from anyone passing above, he
stopped beside a patch of bare earth.

A carchera—one of those small stone mounds raised over the
dead—stood farther up the slope. It had probably covered a
shepherd or a bandit; in Corsica the two occupations have
often shared the same graves.

Mateo rested his gun against a tree and drew his
stiletto.

Fortunato watched him cut into the earth.

"What are you doing?"

Mateo tested the soil with the blade.

"Father?"

The man straightened.

"Kneel."

Fortunato's eyes opened wide.

"Why?"

"Say your prayers."

The child looked toward the path by which they had
come. Mateo stood between him and escape. For the first
time that day, Fortunato seemed ten years old.

"I have already prayed this morning."

"Pray again."

"You will not kill me."

"Pray."

Fortunato fell to his knees. His hands found each other
but could not remain still.

"Our Father, who art in heaven—"

His voice broke.

"Go on."

"Hallowed be Thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be
done—"

He stopped and looked up.

"Father, forgive me."

"Finish."

The boy began again, skipping words, confusing one petition with
another. Mateo corrected him twice. When the prayer was done,
Fortunato recited the Ave Maria. He had to begin that
also a second time because he forgot the phrase concerning
the hour of death.

At last Mateo said, "Amen."

The boy remained kneeling.

"Father, I will never do it again."

Mateo took up the gun.

"I will hide no one. I will take no money.
I will give the five francs to the church."

Mateo pulled back the hammer.

Fortunato crawled forward and clasped his knees.

"I am your son!"

The father disengaged himself.

"Stand there."

Fortunato rose. His chest heaved beneath the brown jacket. Tears
ran freely now, cutting pale tracks through the dust on
his cheeks.

"Tell Mother I am sorry."

"Tell God."

"Will God forgive me?"

"I shall ask Him."

Mateo stepped back six paces. The place was so narrow
that he could not go farther without climbing the slope.

Fortunato held out both arms.

"Father!"

The shot struck the cliffs and returned from them in
a succession of diminishing thunderclaps. Birds flew from the oaks.
Fortunato fell backward like a stone.

Mateo reloaded his gun. This was habit, not necessity.

He approached the body, placed two fingers against the child's
neck and waited. Then he crossed himself.

"May God forgive him," he said.

Using the stiletto and a flat piece of granite, he
began to dig. The earth was easier to move than
he had expected. After a little while he heard branches
rustling behind him.

Giuseppa stood at the entrance to the hollow. She had
followed the path but arrived too late. One glance at
the brown jacket on the ground told her everything.

She gave no cry. She leaned against the rock, pressed
both hands to her mouth and slowly sank down.

Mateo continued digging.

After a long silence she said:

"Where is he?"

"Here."

"You killed him?"

"Justice was done."

She crawled to the body and gathered it against her
breast. Fortunato's head fell back over her arm; his open
eyes reflected a strip of sky. Giuseppa closed them and
began rocking him as she had when he was an
infant.

Mateo removed his cap.

"I shall have a Mass said for his soul."

Giuseppa did not seem to hear.

The light in the ravine was already changing. Evening arrives
early where mountains stand close together, and it was necessary
to finish before darkness. Mateo deepened the grave, laid branches
across the bottom and took off his cloak to wrap
the boy. When he tried to lift Fortunato from his
mother's arms, she resisted; then, meeting his eyes, she released
the body.

They placed their son in the earth.

Mateo built a low carchera over the grave, fitting the
stones carefully so that foxes could not disturb it. Giuseppa
sat nearby with the broken silver chain twisted around her
fingers. She must have picked it up before leaving the
house.

When the last stone was set, Mateo shouldered his gun.

"We must return," he said.

She looked at him as if she no longer knew
his face.

"The house is empty."

"Not for long. I shall send word to your daughter's
husband, Taddeo Bianchi. He will come to live with us."

Giuseppa stared at the mound.

"He has sons."

"The eldest shall be my heir."

Mateo turned toward the path. After a few steps he
saw that his wife had not moved.

"Come," he said.

She rose and followed him.

Night had entered the maquis. The shrubs closed behind them,
and the path to the house could no longer be
distinguished from the dark earth—except by those who knew it.

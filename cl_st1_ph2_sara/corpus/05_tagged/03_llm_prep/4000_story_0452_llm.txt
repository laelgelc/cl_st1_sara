## THE BREECHES OF POISSY
### A True History Drawn from the Lesser Cartulary of
Saint-Louis, with Certain Necessary Additions Concerning Fleas, Fasting, and the
Sacrament of Matrimony

There are three things, says an ancient proverb of the
Île-de-France, which can never be wholly shut up: smoke in
a chimney, love in a maiden, and news in a
convent. Whether this proverb was first composed by King Philippe
the Long, by a clerk of the Châtelet, or by
some washerwoman whose daughter had been placed among the good
sisters of Poissy, I will not determine; for historians must
know where to stop, particularly when they know nothing. But
it is certain that the abbey of Poissy, though built
of honest stone, enclosed by thick walls, defended by a
great gate, a little gate, a wicket, two portresses, and
a saint of excellent reputation, permitted many rumours to escape
through cracks invisible to the mason.

Certain malicious persons declared that the said rumours escaped by
night in the shape of young gentlemen; others maintained that
the gentlemen entered by day disguised as physicians, confessors, lute-menders,
rat-catchers, dealers in relics, or cousins of the abbess. I
find no proof of these accusations in the greater cartulary,
which mentions only rents, mills, vineyards, lawsuits, and a miraculous
cow. But the lesser cartulary, which was kept behind the
sacristy by an old nun named Sister Gudule, contains, between
an account of twelve hens and a recipe for green
ointment, these words:

> In the time of Madame Alix, much laughter was
heard after compline.

Now laughter after compline, as every theologian knows, is suspicious;
for before compline it may proceed from recreation, but after
compline it must proceed either from the devil or from
a good story, which are often cousins.

Madame Alix, the abbess here mentioned, was a tall, plump,
magnificent lady, sprung from a family so noble that its
ancestors had lost money in every crusade. She loved good
order, provided the order did not inconvenience her; strict silence,
provided others observed it; fasting, when she had already dined;
and poverty, especially in documents intended for the archbishop. Her
table was famous from Mantes to Saint-Denis. At it were
served carp fattened on crumbs, capons stuffed with chestnuts, pies
wherein partridges lay like Christian martyrs under pastry tombstones, cheeses
powerful enough to walk unassisted, and wines of such devotion
that after three cups the drinker embraced his enemy, after
four his neighbour, and after five the table.

Under her government the elder sisters prayed, the middle-aged sisters
counted the linen, and the younger sisters improved the night.

They had established, without bull, charter, seal, or permission of
the chapter, a little assembly called the Confraternity of Saint-Nobody,
because no saint was willing to accept responsibility for it.
The members met in the linen-room, a place admirably chosen,
being warm, remote, and provided with sheets in which any
sudden noise could be buried. Their bell was the scratching
of Sister Jacquette's fingernail upon a cupboard door; their password
was "Have you the key?" to which the answer was,
"The lock awaits it." This phrase, though innocent in locksmiths,
caused much merriment among those virtuous maidens.

The president of the confraternity was Sister Eudoxie, a dark-eyed
Burgundian who could imitate the abbess, the subprioress, the archbishop,
two confessors, a wheezing donkey, and the death-rattle of a
miser forced to pay cash. Sister Perrette supplied food; Sister
Agnès supplied candles stolen one inch at a time from
the chapel; Sister Colette, being the daughter of a vintner,
supplied doctrine in liquid form; and Sister Blanche supplied scandal,
having an aunt at court.

When all were assembled, they spread a cloth upon an
overturned chest and produced their treasures: little sausages, honey-cakes, preserved
cherries, walnuts, a cold pullet, two onions, a jug of
wine, and on feast days a custard whose yellow surface
shone like the conscience of a canon newly absolved. Then,
after a grace of only seven words—lest the food grow
cold—they ate, whispered, laughed, told tales, and examined the defects
of absent sisters with the charity proper to religious life.

They knew which nun snored in plainchant, which one counted
the hours by the visits of the gardener, which kept
sweetmeats in her psalter, which had sent twelve letters to
a cousin who was no cousin, and which wore beneath
her veil a ribbon of scarlet silk "for the honour
of Saint Barbara." They debated whether Saint Joseph, being a
carpenter, had made cupboards with secret drawers; whether angels, having
wings, required undergarments in a north wind; and whether a
chaplain who slept during confession could grant absolution by snoring
three times.

Such were the grave exercises of the Confraternity of Saint-Nobody.

Now it happened that a novice named Margot was admitted
to the abbey. She came from near Pontoise and was
seventeen years of age, according to her mother, nineteen according
to the parish register, and as old as Eve according
to her eyes. She had cheeks like apples washed in
rain, hair the colour of ripe wheat, and a manner
so demure that the mistress of novices called her "my
little dove." This appellation deceived nobody except the mistress of
novices.

For three weeks Margot walked with lowered gaze, folded hands,
small steps, and lips continually moving in prayer. When questioned
she blushed; when reproved she wept; when praised she looked
frightened. She knew neither cards nor dice, had never heard
a tavern song, and supposed, she said, that a man's
doublet was fastened with prayer.

"An angel!" declared Sister Perrette.

"A goose," said Sister Eudoxie.

"A sealed book," said Sister Blanche.

"Books," observed Sister Jacquette, "are sealed only until somebody finds
the clasp."

They resolved to invite Margot to the confraternity, partly from
kindness, partly from curiosity, but principally because nothing renders experienced
persons so industrious as the sight of innocence.

At midnight on the vigil of Saint Barnabas, Margot heard
three scratches at her door.

"Who is there?" she whispered.

"The peace of the Lord."

"What does it want?"

"A little mustard."

This was not the password, but Sister Eudoxie had forgotten
it, having already sampled the wine. Margot opened. She was
wrapped in a white chemise and looked so candid that
the sisters nearly felt ashamed, which is to say they
hesitated for the space of one Credo.

They led her to the linen-room, sat her on the
softest chest, placed a honey-cake in her hand, and informed
her that she was about to receive instruction in certain
lesser observances neglected by the mistress of novices.

"My child," said Sister Eudoxie with pontifical gravity, "you have
entered a holy house, but holiness is beset by enemies."

"The world, the flesh, and the devil," replied Margot.

"And fleas," added Sister Jacquette.

Margot crossed herself.

Fleas were, said Eudoxie, the smallest but most industrious emissaries
of Satan. They respected neither rank nor virtue. They entered
without knocking, dined without grace, and slept in the warmest
bed. A bishop could excommunicate a prince; a pope could
depose an emperor; but neither bishop nor pope had yet
composed a bull which a flea would read.

"For this reason," continued Eudoxie, "every prudent religious should learn
to catch, examine, instruct, convert, and, if conversion fail, execute
the above-mentioned flea."

"Is it difficult?" asked Margot.

"Everything is difficult before practice."

"Where do they hide?"

A rustle of delight passed through the holy troop.

"My dear," said Sister Blanche, "the flea is an animal
of subtle judgment. It despises the hand, which is always
occupied; the foot, which walks; and the face, which everybody
sees. It seeks valleys sheltered from the wind, hills warmed
by the sun, little woods, secret meadows, covered ways, curtained
chambers, gates, doors, wickets, openings, roads, and narrow passages wherein
a pilgrim may repose without paying toll."

Margot lowered her eyes.

Sister Perrette bit her napkin.

"When you perceive," continued Blanche, "a small agitation in some
private province of your person, you must not strike wildly.
No. The flea loves haste because haste opens frontiers. You
must approach with two fingers, which represent Mercy and Justice."

"Which finger is Mercy?" asked Margot.

"The gentler one."

"And Justice?"

"The one that pinches."

At this response Jacquette thrust both fists into her mouth.

"Having caught the infidel," said Eudoxie, "you must first determine
whether it be male or female."

"How?"

"By its behaviour. The male is presumptuous, restless, and returns
after being thrown out. The female drinks deeply and keeps
the best chamber."

"This is natural history," said Sister Colette.

"It is Saint Jerome," said Perrette.

"It is my aunt," said Blanche.

The jug went round.

"The male flea," Eudoxie resumed, "possesses, according to Aristotle, six
legs, two eyes, one stomach, a black robe, a hard
head, an obstinate will, and a little instrument whereby it
torments Christian women."

"Like a tax collector," said Margot.

The sisters looked at one another.

"Precisely," replied Eudoxie. "Now, before killing him, charity requires that
you attempt his conversion. Place him upon a white cloth,
which signifies baptismal purity. Sprinkle him with one drop of
water—no more, lest he drown unshriven—and ask: 'Renouncest thou the
pomps of this world?' If he leaps, he renounces. If
he remains, he meditates. If he bites you, he is
a theologian."

"And if he escapes beneath the cloth?" asked Margot.

"Then," said Blanche, "he has entered religion."

The linen-room shook with laughter. Sister Colette, who had been
drinking during the discourse, gave a squeak and sat suddenly
upon the floor. Margot smiled, but no more than was
proper.

Eudoxie, piqued by this composure, resolved to sharpen the lesson.

"You must also know, child, that fleas vary according to
their lodging. Those taken near the throat are singers; those
near the waist are cooks; those below the girdle are—"

"Porters," said Margot.

"Why porters?"

"Because they keep the lower gate."

There followed a silence of such quality that even the
candle-flames appeared astonished.

Sister Blanche leaned forward.

"Who taught you about the lower gate?"

"Our miller's son."

"The jade!" cried Perrette.

"The miller's son?" said Eudoxie. "Was he learned in fleas?"

"He had studied them for two summers."

"Upon whom?"

"Upon me, principally."

At this confession Sister Jacquette fell backward into a basket
of clean wimples, showing two bare feet and no theology.
Colette spilled the wine. Blanche slapped the table. Perrette cried
that innocence was dead; and Eudoxie, recovering first, demanded all
the facts of the case, including dates, locations, witnesses, preliminaries,
conclusions, and whether the miller's son had employed persuasion, verse,
music, gifts, sighs, threats, ladders, false keys, true keys, or
that oldest of keys which opens when the lock is
willing.

Margot ate her honey-cake.

"He first carried our flour."

"A common beginning."

"Then he brought apples."

"A pastoral advance."

"Then he said my eyes were stars."

"The rogue had read poetry."

"Then he asked for a lock of my hair."

"Customary tribute."

"Then he wished to know whether my heart was free."

"A legal inquiry."

"Then he said he would die."

"They always recover."

"Then he did not die."

"Never."

"Then one evening, behind the mill, he showed me how
fleas are married."

The sisters made a noise like geese when the fox
enters.

"Married!" cried Eudoxie. "Without banns?"

"He said fleas require none."

"Without a priest?"

"He said the moon was sufficient."

"Without a dowry?"

"He gave me a blue ribbon."

Sister Blanche shook her head gravely. "An irregular union, but
valid by local custom."

"And did your mother know?"

"She knew the ribbon was blue."

"But the lesson?"

"She knew nothing of natural history."

The sisters now surrounded Margot as doctors surround a rare
disease. They asked whether the miller's son was blond, brown,
broad, thin, pious, musical, hasty, patient, skilful, clumsy, left-handed, clean
in his apparel, and sound in his doctrine. Margot answered
with such exactitude that there remained no doubt: the sealed
book had been opened, annotated, and perhaps lent out.

Eudoxie kissed her on both cheeks.

"Welcome, my daughter, to the Confraternity of Saint-Nobody."

"Must I take a vow?"

"You must promise never to repeat what is said here."

"To whom?"

"To anyone who does not already know it."

This promise being made, Colette refilled the cups, and Margot,
who was no longer seated on the softest chest by
courtesy but by merit, was asked for a story. She
declined, saying she possessed only experience, not invention.

"Experience," said Blanche, "is invention caught without its breeches."

This mention of breeches should be marked by the attentive
reader, for Providence often plants in the first part of
a history the seed that will bear fruit in the
last. The inattentive reader may mark it also, if he
pleases; no tax is charged.

Since Margot would not tell a story, Sister Perrette related
the authentic history of Sister Petronille, which I cannot omit,
though a severe historian might, because it explains how sanctity
is sometimes manufactured from wind.

Sister Petronille had lived at Poissy forty years earlier. She
was long, dry, yellow, and bent, resembling a candle left
in the sun and afterward regretted. She ate almost nothing,
spoke less, slept upright, and wore next her skin a
garment of horsehair so rough that three sacristans had used
it after her death to polish brass.

People called her an ascetic. This was an error.

Her abstinence arose not from contempt of food but from
fear of consequences. Her stomach was a narrow theologian which
admitted little and disputed everything. A spoonful of peas provoked
a synod; an egg produced schism; cheese caused wars lasting
three days; and beans—God defend us!—awoke in her interior such
trumpets, drums, bombardments, processions, and popular uprisings that the sisters
fled the dormitory.

Being ashamed of these tempests, Petronille declared that she fasted.
The abbess praised her. The convent admired her. Pilgrims began
to visit. A woman from Chartres touched Petronille's sleeve and
afterward declared herself cured of a pain in the knee,
though she had confused knees and touched the sound one.

Petronille was commanded to eat in the refectory as an
example to others. There she would receive upon her plate
a fish, contemplate it sorrowfully, cut it into twelve apostolic
pieces, swallow one, conceal four in her sleeve, give three
to the cat, and leave the remainder "for the poor."
The cat grew fat, the poor remained thin, and Petronille
acquired the odour of a harbour.

One Lent, a zealous prior preached that the truly mortified
should live on beans and water. The abbess, inspired by
economy, ordered beans for the whole house.

Petronille turned pale.

At dinner she counted thirty-seven beans in her bowl, each
one round, smooth, and terrible as a cannonball. She appealed
to the infirmarian.

"Eat," said that officer.

"I shall die."

"Then you will be a martyr."

"I prefer another martyrdom."

"We cannot alter the menu."

Petronille swallowed one bean, then another. At the seventh she
heard distant thunder. At the twelfth her veil trembled. At
the eighteenth the spoons began to rattle. At the twenty-fifth
she rose and attempted to reach the garden, but the
subprioress, believing her overcome by ecstasy, commanded everyone to remain
still.

Then came a sound.

Perrette, in telling it, inflated her cheeks, rolled her eyes,
and made with her lips so prodigious a report that
Margot dropped her cake.

The first sound was followed by a second, longer and
more plaintive, like a cowhorn blown beneath water. The windows
shook. The cat fled. Three novices crossed themselves. An old
sister cried, "The Last Judgment!" and hid a sausage in
her bosom.

Petronille stood rigid, one hand upon the table, the other
raised toward heaven, while her interior monastery rang every bell
at once. The abbess, unwilling to admit a bodily explanation
before the visiting prior, whispered that the sister was receiving
a divine message.

"What does it say?" asked the prior.

Petronille, purple with anguish, replied, "More room."

The prior fell to his knees.

"More room for grace!" he cried.

At that instant the final trumpet sounded, extinguishing two candles
and turning a page of the antiphonary at the far
end of the hall. The visiting prior declared the event
miraculous. The abbess ordered it recorded. For twenty years pilgrims
were shown the bench upon which Petronille had expanded the
boundaries of grace.

"And was she canonised?" asked Margot.

"No," said Perrette. "The cat disclosed the fish."

This conclusion so delighted the confraternity that their laughter passed
through the linen, along the passage, down a stair, and
into the chamber of the subprioress. That lady awoke, listened,
and, being herself a former member of Saint-Nobody, turned her
face to the wall.

Such disorder could not last. Abbesses, like wines, have their
season. Madame Alix died after a supper of lampreys, though
the physician attributed her end to melancholy, which was more
respectable. A new abbess arrived from Fontevraud with a square
chin, cold hands, and statutes. She reformed everything that could
be reformed and locked everything that could not. The linen-room
was searched, the wine confiscated, the secret key broken, and
the Confraternity of Saint-Nobody dissolved by an ordinance containing twenty-three
clauses and no humour.

Sister Eudoxie was sent to oversee chickens. Sister Colette was
placed near the well. Sister Blanche was ordered to copy
sermons against levity. Sister Jacquette, having married neither man nor
doctrine, became fat. Margot took final vows and in time
acquired a reputation for counselling novices with uncommon practical wisdom.

From that date the abbey of Poissy became so regular
that clocks adjusted themselves when carried near it. The sisters
rose, prayed, worked, ate, prayed, worked again, and went to
bed without honey-cakes. They spoke rarely, laughed never in public,
and handled fleas without allegory. The archbishop praised them. The
king praised them. Their neighbours, finding nothing to report, were
furious.

Now we come to the breeches.

The archbishop of Paris at that time was Monseigneur Hugues
de Villiers, a charitable prelate of wide sleeve, generous purse,
short memory, and long sermons. He gave alms to the
poor, counsel to the rich, offices to his nephews, and
old garments to anyone who could use them. His palace
contained seventeen chaplains, nine secretaries, four cooks, six dogs, a
fool, a physician, and a valet named Colin, whose understanding
was like a church door: large, heavy, and opened only
from one side.

Monseigneur possessed a pair of winter breeches made of thick
violet cloth lined with grey fur. They were excellent breeches,
episcopal in breadth, canonical in depth, and so stoutly constructed
that a lesser man might have used them for a
tent. But time, which gnaws palaces, reputations, and waistbands, had
attacked them in several places. One seam had opened near
the southern frontier; two buttons were missing; the lining showed
signs of rebellion; and a rent, modest at first, had
enlarged itself through neglect until no honest tailor could behold
it without making the sign of the cross.

On the same day, the archbishop received a petition concerning
Mademoiselle Héloïse de Poissy, an impoverished gentlewoman who lived with
an old aunt near the Porte Saint-Marcel. She was of
good family, good education, good temper, and almost no money,
those four things being rarely united because the first three
consume the fourth.

Her father had died in war, her mother of grief,
her elder brother of dice, and her younger brother in
pursuit of an heiress who escaped him on horseback. Héloïse
possessed one gown for summer, one cloak for winter, a
silver spoon, a lute without three strings, and a pedigree
long enough to cover all deficiencies except hunger.

Monseigneur had promised to assist her. Remembering, therefore, that charitable
works should not be delayed, he called Colin.

"Take these breeches," he said, "to Mademoiselle de Poissy."

Colin stared.

"Monseigneur?"

"To Mademoiselle de Poissy. Tell her I wish them mended."

Colin stared harder.

"She is to mend Monseigneur's breeches?"

"Certainly. She is poor, not incapable. Give her ten sols
for the work."

Now Colin knew that there existed a Mademoiselle de Poissy,
but he also knew, more clearly, that there existed certain
demoiselles at Poissy, namely the nuns, who were often styled
daughters, ladies, sisters, maidens, brides, or doves according to the
rhetorical needs of the speaker. His brain, confronted by two
Poissys, chose the larger.

He wrapped the breeches in brown cloth, took ten sols,
mounted a mule, and rode to the abbey.

The portress received him through the wicket.

"I bring a parcel from Monseigneur the archbishop."

"For the abbess?"

"For the demoiselles of Poissy."

"We are they."

"He wishes the enclosed article mended."

The portress carried the parcel to the workroom, where twelve
sisters sat sewing altar cloths under the eye of Sister
Sabine, mistress of needlework. The seal was examined, the string
cut, the cloth unfolded, and the episcopal breeches rose from
the parcel like the beast in the Apocalypse.

Nobody spoke.

The younger sisters blushed. The elder sisters frowned. One novice
asked what the object was and received twelve contradictory answers:
a double purse, a saddle-cover, a divided sleeve, a reliquary,
a trap for badgers, and a garment worn by men
against draughts.

Sister Sabine held it at arm's length.

"Did the messenger truly say Monseigneur sent this?"

"He said it was to be mended."

"By us?"

"By the demoiselles of Poissy."

"This is an outrage."

"It is an article of clothing."

"Which is often the same thing."

The abbess was summoned. She arrived with the prioress, the
bursar, and the chaplain, who took one look and immediately
remembered an appointment elsewhere.

The abbess read the archbishop's attached note. It said only:
"For the necessary repairs, with ten sols." The hand was
genuine. The seal was genuine. The rent was undeniable.

"A test," said the prioress.

"Of what?" asked Sister Sabine.

"Obedience."

"Or chastity," murmured an elder nun.

"Or tailoring," said the bursar.

The abbess considered. Since the reformation, the sisters had often
been warned that the enemy would tempt them by strange
devices. No device, she thought, could be stranger than violet
breeches. Moreover, the Rule condemned idleness, praised labour, and nowhere
expressly forbade the repair of an archbishop's lower garments. Canon
law was consulted. It proved silent, having perhaps foreseen that
silence was safest.

"We shall mend them," declared the abbess.

A collective breath escaped.

"But with modesty, discipline, and no unnecessary observations."

Thereupon the breeches were placed upon the largest table. The
nuns approached as surgeons approach a fallen giant. Sister Sabine
examined the waistband. Sister Gudule tested the seams. Sister Margot—our
Margot, now grave, competent, and forty years old—put two fingers
through the principal rent.

"This wound is deep," she said.

"Remove your hand," ordered the abbess.

"I am measuring it."

"Measure with your eyes."

"My eyes do not sew."

Thus began the great repair.

At first the sisters intended only to close the rents,
replace the buttons, and patch the lining. But pious women,
when once persuaded that an absurd task is sacred, perform
it with a magnificence denied to common sense. Sister Sabine
declared the old thread unworthy of episcopal dignity. The bursar
produced silk. Sister Gudule found matching cloth in a chest.
Another sister proposed reinforcing the seat, for prudence supports even
the highest authority. A third suggested embroidery to conceal the
patches.

"What embroidery?" asked the abbess.

"Lilies."

"Too royal."

"Crosses."

"Too sacred for the location."

"Vines."

"Too Bacchic."

"Fleas," said Sister Margot.

The abbess looked at her.

"Why fleas?"

"They signify vigilance."

This explanation, being no worse than many heraldic explanations, was
accepted.

So around the waistband they embroidered a procession of tiny
golden fleas, each one provided with six legs, two eyes,
and, by Sister Margot's private insistence, an expression of theological
obstinacy. Down the right leg ran a vine bearing grapes;
down the left, a line of pomegranates. Over the knees
were stitched two medallions representing Fortitude and Patience, virtues necessary
both to bishops and to breeches. The buttons were replaced
with polished horn. The lining was renewed in scarlet silk.
The rent in the southern frontier was closed by three
rows of stitching so firm that no temptation, however energetic,
could reopen it without tools.

When the work was finished, the garment looked less like
apparel than a banner captured in an Oriental war.

"It is too beautiful to return empty," said Sister Sabine.

This was true. Its two ample divisions lay upon the
table like twin cornucopias awaiting prosperity. The sisters determined to
fill them with gifts, partly from gratitude for the ten
sols and partly because a thing so hollow offended monastic
economy.

Into the right leg they put sugared almonds, quince paste,
preserved plums, gingerbread saints, wafers, walnuts, little cakes stamped with
the Lamb of God, and six marzipan apostles, the other
six having been eaten during manufacture owing to defects in
their halos. Into the left they put ribbons, two pairs
of gloves, a packet of needles, a rosary, three handkerchiefs,
a small bottle of cordial, and a cushion embroidered with
the words LABOUR IS PRAYER.

At the fork, where the two provinces met under one
government, Sister Margot placed a large heart made of rose-coloured
sugar.

"Why there?" asked a novice.

"Because the heart should govern both sides."

The saying was admired and recorded, though its anatomy was
doubtful.

The whole was wrapped in white linen, tied with blue
ribbon, and accompanied by a letter composed by the abbess:

> To Monseigneur, our reverend father in God, the humble
daughters of Poissy return with thanksgiving that which his pastoral
charity entrusted to their hands. They have closed what was
open, strengthened what was weak, adorned what was plain, and
filled what was empty, praying that he may long enjoy
the fruits of their obedience.

There are letters which explain; there are letters which conceal;
and there are letters which, by attempting both, produce calamity.
This was of the third kind.

Meanwhile Monseigneur Hugues, believing his breeches to be in the
hands of Mademoiselle Héloïse, turned his charity toward a more
durable repair. Poverty in a noblewoman, he reflected, is a
rent best mended by marriage, provided the husband be sound
cloth.

After several inquiries he found Messire Raoul de Brévannes, a
gentleman of thirty-five years, owner of a small estate, a
large moustache, two mills, one vineyard, six horses, and no
wife. He had fought bravely in Picardy, danced badly in
Paris, paid his debts eventually, and desired a bride with
more pedigree than appetite. Héloïse desired a husband with more
land than verses. Providence, which sometimes succeeds by lowering expectations,
brought them together.

They met at the archbishop's palace. Raoul bowed, caught his
spur in a stool, and nearly fell. Héloïse pretended not
to see. He loved her immediately for this mercy. She
discovered that he had kind eyes, clean hands, and all
his teeth. Within a fortnight the contract was drawn.

The wedding took place in the chapel of the episcopal
palace on a bright morning in September. Monseigneur himself blessed
the couple. Héloïse wore a gown of blue damask borrowed
from a cousin; Raoul wore crimson velvet purchased for the
occasion and afterward regretted by his steward. The witnesses included
canons, nobles, magistrates, widows, cousins, creditors, and three persons invited
because nobody knew who they were.

After the mass came the feast.

The great hall was hung with tapestries representing the life
of Tobias, though one panel had been reversed and showed
only trees. On the tables stood silver dishes, saltcellars shaped
like castles, pies shaped like churches, roasted peacocks wearing their
own feathers, sturgeons swimming in sauce, hares with rosemary in
their ears, mountains of bread, valleys of cabbage, and fountains
of wine. Musicians played from a gallery; dogs waited beneath
the benches; servants ran; candles smoked; ladies glittered; gentlemen lied.

Monseigneur sat at the high table between the bride and
bridegroom. He had just begun a discourse on the seven
spiritual significations of matrimonial concord when Colin entered carrying the
parcel from Poissy.

The valet had returned late from another errand and knew
nothing of its contents. Seeing the archbishop's seal upon the
reply, he concluded it must be a gift for the
bride.

"From the holy sisters of Poissy," he announced.

"Héloïse!" cried Monseigneur. "Your good namesakes have remembered you."

This was plausible enough. The parcel was placed before the
bride.

The white linen and blue ribbon looked so elegant that
everyone admired them. The abbess's letter was read aloud. Its
phrases—closing what was open, strengthening what was weak, filling what
was empty—were taken for matrimonial counsel and applauded as delicate.

"Excellent women," said a canon.

"Profound women," said a magistrate.

"Experienced women," said Raoul.

The bride untied the ribbon.

Within appeared a mass of violet cloth covered in gold,
grapes, pomegranates, and vigilant fleas. Since the legs were folded
inward and packed with sweets, the object resembled a ceremonial
cushion.

"How magnificent!" cried Héloïse.

"A bridal reliquary," suggested a lady.

"A symbol of abundance," said the canon.

"A saddle ornament," said Colin, hoping to improve matters.

The parcel was set upright in a silver basin at
the centre of the table. Its two filled legs rose
like towers, while the sugared heart at the fork gave
it the appearance of a heraldic monster bearing a pink
shield.

The guests took sweets from it. A child pulled out
gingerbread. A dowager extracted a glove. One canon, reaching deep
into the right leg for sugared almonds, praised the generosity
of cloistered virgins. Another discovered the cordial and drank to
their health. Soon the bridal reliquary was surrounded by eager
hands.

Now every structure, whether kingdom, argument, or breeches, depends upon
balance. As the right leg emptied faster than the left,
the object leaned. A marzipan apostle rolled across the table.
A dog seized him. A page lunged after the dog
and struck the silver basin. The basin tilted. Raoul attempted
to save it by catching the embroidered waistband.

He lifted.

The remaining sweets poured out in a sparkling cascade. Walnuts
bounced among the plates. Quince paste struck a bishop's vicar
on the chin. The rosary fell into the sauce. The
sugar heart landed in the bride's lap.

And above the table, held triumphantly by the bridegroom, unfolded
the archbishop's enormous violet breeches.

There are moments when human understanding advances not by degrees
but by explosion. For one breath nobody comprehended. Then Héloïse
saw the two legs. Raoul saw the reinforced seat. The
ladies saw Fortitude and Patience upon the knees. The canons
saw the golden fleas. Monseigneur saw, above all, the principal
rent, now gloriously healed.

A sound began at the lower tables, where modesty was
weakest. It travelled upward like fire through dry straw. Pages
snorted, soldiers roared, ladies hid behind napkins, magistrates struck the
board, and one elderly dean laughed so violently that his
false tooth entered the sturgeon.

The musicians, supposing some signal had been given, played a
flourish.

Monseigneur turned crimson.

"Colin!"

The valet crawled behind a chair.

"To whom did you take those breeches?"

"To Mademoiselle de Poissy, Monseigneur."

"Which Mademoiselle?"

"All of them."

The laughter doubled. Héloïse bent over the sugar heart. Raoul
stood holding the garment before him like a victorious standard.
The golden fleas winked in the candlelight.

The archbishop attempted dignity.

"This is a misunderstanding."

"A well-lined one," said a captain.

"A charitable work," said the canon who had drunk the
cordial.

"A miracle of restoration," said the magistrate.

Monseigneur glared at them. But wrath cannot stand long in
a hall where one's breeches are embroidered with fleas. His
mouth twitched. He examined the workmanship, the grapes, the pomegranates,
the scarlet lining, and the stern allegories upon the knees.

At last Raoul bowed to his bride and said:

"Madame, the holy sisters have judged marriage better than all
our doctors. They have given us its sweetnesses first, and
beneath them the necessary instrument."

This reply conquered even the archbishop. He laughed until tears
entered his beard. The bride struck Raoul lightly with the
sugar heart; the guests applauded; the musicians played again; and
the breeches were hung behind the married pair as a
canopy, where they remained throughout the feast, admired by everyone
and understood according to each person's innocence.

The nuns of Poissy afterward learned what had happened. The
abbess ordered three days of silence. Sister Sabine said the
stitching had at least been praised. Sister Margot observed that
every garment reaches its proper destination in the end. Colin
was dismissed, rehired, and forbidden thereafter to interpret nouns.

As for Messire Raoul and Héloïse, they lived together in
tolerable concord, had seven children, and kept the embroidered breeches
in a cedar chest. They displayed them only at weddings,
episcopal visits, and occasions when conversation had grown too solemn.

Thus charity mended poverty, error adorned embarrassment, and monastic obedience
supplied matrimony with a standard. If a stricter moral be
required, let some doctor of theology manufacture it; for the
bridegroom's sentence contains all that is necessary, and perhaps more
than should be repeated before compline.

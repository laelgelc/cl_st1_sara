## The Barber and His Victim

It is commonly supposed that a barber cuts hair and
removes whiskers. This is an error which could only have
arisen among persons who shave themselves.

The barber's actual profession is the collection, arrangement, and authoritative
distribution of sporting intelligence. He knows which pitcher has a
weak shoulder, which prizefighter has been seen eating pastry, which
horse dislikes a muddy track, and which football coach intends
to replace his entire team at half-time. The razor, the
shears, the tonics, and the revolving chair are merely the
apparatus by which he secures an audience and prevents it
from escaping before the information has been delivered.

No man would voluntarily sit still for forty minutes while
another man explained the league standings to him. He must
first be tilted backward, wrapped in linen, deprived of the
use of his arms, and covered about the mouth with
warm lather. When these preliminary measures have been taken, resistance
becomes difficult and contradiction almost impossible.

The barber then begins.

"You saw where Benson knocked one over the fence yesterday?"

The customer has not seen it. He has spent the
previous day attending the funeral of an aunt and has
not thought of Benson. Nevertheless, he makes a faint noise
under the soap.

"That's what I say," continues the barber. "Benson's the only
natural hitter they've got."

The noise made by the customer may have meant anything.
It may have meant that he agrees, that the lather
is entering his nostrils, or that he has suddenly remembered
leaving a gas burner alight. The barber accepts it as
agreement. A trained barber will accept any sound as agreement
except a perfectly distinct "No," and even that he regards
as evidence that the customer has not understood the question.

The dissemination of sporting news is a necessary public service.
Without the barber, thousands of respectable citizens would reach their
offices ignorant of the previous evening's contest and would consequently
be unable to speak at luncheon. Commerce would stop. Banks
would close. Men who had been friends for twenty years
would sit opposite each other in restaurants with nothing to
say except that the weather appeared seasonal. Civilization is therefore
dependent upon a small body of men who stand behind
leather chairs with razors in their hands and know the
exact weight of every prominent wrestler.

The information itself need not be accurate. Indeed, accuracy would
limit the barber severely. He is not a historian but
an interpreter. If a horse loses, the barber explains that
it was held back for financial reasons. If it wins,
he has known for a week that it could not
lose. If a boxer is defeated, he was drugged, bribed,
overtrained, undertrained, or worried about his mother. The customer, being
at that moment stretched horizontally with a damp cloth across
his eyes, is in no position to consult the newspapers.

The barber is also a bookmaker in a purely philosophical
sense. He does not necessarily accept money, but he can
tell the customer where money might have been accepted, what
odds ought to have been offered, and how much the
customer would have won if he had possessed the barber's
judgment before the event. This retrospective generosity is one of
the most attractive features of the profession.

As I have said, shaving is incidental. Yet it is
an incidental operation requiring extraordinary preparation. The customer enters the
shop with a face that has served him satisfactorily since
breakfast. Within three minutes the barber has discovered that it
is dry, congested, undernourished, badly ventilated, and in immediate danger
of coming loose.

"You've been using something on this face," says the barber.

"Soap," says the customer.

"That's it."

The barber shakes his head gravely. Ordinary soap, it appears,
has ruined more faces than war. It closes the outlets,
hardens the follicles, attacks the tissues from beneath, and ultimately
produces a condition known to the trade as facial exhaustion.
The fact that the customer's father used soap for eighty-four
years and died with a face of unusual durability is
irrelevant. His father was fortunate. The customer will not be.

The barber presses a thumb against the cheek and watches
the dent disappear.

"Slow reaction," he says.

"What does that mean?"

The barber does not answer at once. He turns to
select a towel, and this silence is worth two bottles
of lotion. When he returns, he says, "Nothing to worry
about yet."

The word "yet" is the principal commercial instrument of the
modern tonsorial establishment. It has sold more preparations than all
the advertisements ever printed. The customer may resist a direct
accusation, but he cannot resist a postponed catastrophe. Tell a
man that he is bald and he will point to
his hair. Tell him that he is not bald yet,
and he will purchase anything in the shop.

The hot towel is now applied. This practice is represented
as softening the beard, though no beard could remain hard
under the conditions imposed. The towel has been taken from
a metal chamber resembling a municipal boiler and is laid
upon the victim at a temperature sufficient to prepare root
vegetables. The barber folds it under the chin, seals it
across the cheeks, and leaves only the nose projecting, so
that the customer resembles a small roast provided with a
breathing tube.

During this period the barber discusses hockey.

At first the customer attempts to follow the argument. Soon,
however, steam enters his ears and the names of the
players seem to come from the bottom of a harbor.
He feels that his face is detaching itself from his
skull. He raises one hand feebly.

"Too hot?" asks the barber.

"Yes."

"That's what opens the pores."

He leaves it on.

When the towel has cooled to the temperature of an
ordinary furnace, it is removed and replaced by another. The
second towel is always hotter than the first because the
customer's skin has lost the power of independent judgment. By
the third application he would accept a paving stone as
refreshing.

The shaving itself is performed in a succession of strokes,
pauses, and startling changes of direction. The barber begins at
the right ear and works toward the mouth, carrying the
skin before the razor in small folds. He then stretches
the customer's nose to the left and approaches from below.
At intervals he grips the head and turns it sharply,
as though attempting to discover whether it has been screwed
on.

A good barber never asks the customer to move. Movement
by the customer would suggest partnership. The trained operator prefers
to seize the jaw and place it where required. If
he desires access to the throat, he pushes the forehead
backward until the victim is looking at the wall behind
him. If he wishes to shave beneath the chin, he
draws the lower lip upward and the ear downward, producing
an expression not commonly seen outside experiments in elastic materials.

All this is done while the barber explains why the
visiting team cannot win on Saturday.

The customer may possess private reasons for supporting the visiting
team, but he keeps them to himself. A man should
not challenge the political or athletic convictions of anyone who
is scraping his throat with steel. Freedom of speech has
practical boundaries, and one of them is located precisely under
the jaw.

The professional preparation of a barber is naturally severe. No
person can be entrusted with a razor, three newspapers, and
the confidence of the public without education. The principal barber
colleges offer a comprehensive course extending over six weeks, with
an additional afternoon for those intending to specialize in mustaches.

A typical curriculum is as follows:

**First Year, Monday Morning**

1. Elementary Shears: Their Opening and Closing.
2. The Human Ear: Location, Uses, and Compensation for Accidental
Loss.
3. Athletics I: Baseball Scores Suitable for Beginners.
4. Practical Towel Heating.
5. Conversation While the Customer Is Unable to Reply.

**Monday Afternoon**

6. Applied Clippers and the Reduction of Prominent Ridges.
7. Commercial Dermatology: Recognizing a Face That Will Buy Something.
8. The Neck: How Far It May Safely Be Turned.
9. Athletics II: Boxing, Racing, and Confident Guesswork.
10. Sweeping Hair Toward Another Barber's Chair.

The advanced student proceeds to Chemical Tonsorial Science, where he
learns that every liquid is a tonic if put into
a narrow bottle, colored amber, and sold with directions to
rub vigorously. He studies the essential compounds: alcohol, perfume, water,
more alcohol, and the mysterious sediment which proves that the
preparation is active. He is taught to name products by
combining a part of the body with a geological or
electrical term. Thus arise Scalp-Ore, Follicular Dynamo, Cranium Mist, Root
Voltage, and the celebrated Capillary Seismic Fluid.

The examination in salesmanship is especially exacting. The student is
placed before a healthy man and required to frighten him
within four minutes.

"You're getting thin here," says the student.

"Where?"

"Just at the crown."

The examiner notes one point.

The student parts the hair with a comb and whistles
softly.

"What?" asks the subject.

"Probably nothing."

Two more points.

"Is it falling out?"

"Not so you'd notice it yourself."

Full marks.

The graduate emerges qualified to detect decay in any head
except his own. It is a curious professional law that
the barber who warns his customers against baldness is frequently
bald. This does not impair his authority. On the contrary,
it gives him the solemn distinction of a man who
has gone down with his theory. He indicates his polished
scalp and explains that, had Root Voltage been invented twenty
years earlier, he would now require assistance in carrying his
hair.

The city shop has brought these arts to their highest
development. Here the customer does not enter a room; he
enters a system. His hat is removed by one attendant,
his coat by another, and his identity by a third,
who assigns him a numbered check. From that moment he
ceases to be a citizen and becomes Chair Six.

Chair Six is propelled, lowered, raised, rotated, washed, dried, perfumed,
and billed. Men appear from hidden doors to perform subsidiary
operations upon him. While the barber treats the upper portion,
a boot specialist kneels below and begins to polish the
shoes. If the shoes are already polished, the specialist removes
the polish and applies a more expensive one.

At the customer's left hand stands the manicure expert.

"Just clean them up?" she asks.

"No, thank you."

"They need it."

"No."

"Only take a minute."

"No."

She takes the hand anyway, regarding the refusal as a
symptom of nervousness. The customer withdraws it and immediately receives
a razor near the ear as punishment for movement.

"Better keep still," says the barber.

At this stage the barber discovers dandruff. Dandruff in a
metropolitan shop is not the simple substance known in the
home. It is a constitutional rebellion involving the nerves, the
circulation, the digestion, and possibly the customer's credit rating.

"Very dry scalp," says the barber.

"It feels all right."

"You can't feel dryness."

This statement closes the scientific issue.

"Better let me give you a Vapor-Gland treatment?"

"No."

"Scalp massage?"

"No."

"Warm oil?"

"No."

"Cold oil?"

"No."

"Alternating oils?"

"No."

The barber looks wounded, as though the customer had refused
medicine for a sick child. He combs the hair silently
for several seconds and then tries another organ.

"Skin's losing tone."

"No."

The customer has become confused and now denies statements instead
of offers.

"See those lines around the eyes?"

"They've always been there."

"That's how they start."

The barber recommends Youth Lacquer, a preparation which does not
remove wrinkles but fills them temporarily, in much the same
manner that plaster repairs a ceiling. Applied in sufficient quantity,
it renders the face smooth, brilliant, and incapable of expression.
The customer who accepts it leaves the shop looking admirably
preserved, like an exhibit that has been coated against weather.

If he continues to surrender, the entire establishment falls upon
him.

One operator floods the scalp with Electrified Birch Essence. Another
rubs the face with Pore Cement. A third inserts something
cooling under the eyes. His shoes are stripped, dyed, waxed,
and burnished; his fingernails are trimmed into ten separate architectural
styles; a tailor emerges to repair a sock that was
sound when the customer entered; and a dental attendant offers
to brighten the visible teeth without disturbing the gums more
than necessary.

The barber himself applies the Hydro-Rotary Rejuvenation. This consists of
striking the head with the finger tips, kneading it with
the knuckles, drawing the scalp toward the eyebrows, and pushing
it back again before it can escape. The theory is
that the blood must be encouraged to circulate. In practice
it is pursued.

The customer is next placed upright and slapped with various
liquids. The barber pours one into his palm, claps his
hands near the victim's ears, and attacks both cheeks simultaneously.
This is called "bracing." The customer, who has already been
boiled, scraped, compressed, and chemically alarmed, is expected to welcome
the slap as a restorative.

A powder is then blown over him. Some enters the
collar, some the lungs, and the rest settles upon the
newly polished shoes. The chair is released with a mechanical
jerk, returning the customer to the vertical world. He rises
unsteadily, fragrant, shining, and ten years younger in the sense
that he has lost an hour and feels like crying.

At the desk he receives the account. A shave costing
a modest sum has developed, through natural processes, into a
major financial operation. The items include chair service, towel service,
antiseptic service, scalp consultation, follicular analysis, lotion, special lotion, removal
of special lotion, and finishing. The customer pays because his
hat is still held behind the counter.

The rural barber follows a simpler method. He has no
attendants, no heated cabinets, and no chemical department. He relies
upon strength.

The country shop commonly contains one chair, two calendars, a
stove, a sleeping dog, and a shelf of bottles which
have not been moved since the construction of the railway.
The barber knows every person in the county, every person's
debts, and every score made within fifty miles. He does
not ask the customer how he wants his hair cut.
Local hair is cut in the local manner.

The customer removes his coat.

"Better take off the collar too," says the barber.

The collar is removed.

"And the shirt. Gets full of hair otherwise."

Presently the customer is stripped to the waist and sitting
beneath a sheet in an atmosphere of public execution. The
barber sharpens the clippers by snapping them repeatedly beside his
ear.

A city haircut proceeds by fractions of an inch. A
country haircut proceeds by territories. The barber places one hand
on the customer's forehead, drives the clippers into the back
of the neck, and advances upward with the determination of
a man clearing neglected land.

The first passage leaves a broad road from the shoulders
to the crown.

The barber steps back to gain distance. Then he charges
again.

The clippers strike low between the shoulder blades, climb the
spine, cross the back of the skull, and emerge at
the forehead carrying a harvest of hair, loose thread, and
whatever else has stood in their way. The customer grips
the arms of the chair. The dog opens one eye.

"Clippers pull?" asks the barber.

"A little."

"Means they're cutting."

There is no mirror in front of the chair, which
is merciful. The customer cannot see the transformation and must
judge it by sound: the rapid chopping near the ears,
the crunch over the crown, and the barber's breathing as
he begins another run.

Between assaults they discuss the county baseball club. The barber
believes the shortstop is useless. The customer happens to be
the shortstop's father, but this relationship offers no protection. Indeed,
it encourages detail.

"He can't move to his left," says the barber, forcing
the customer's head to the right. "And he's afraid of
a fast ball."

"He hit two on Tuesday."

"Wind helped him."

"There wasn't any wind."

"There's always wind up there."

The barber finishes one side, seizes the chair, and spins
the customer toward the window. A farmer passing outside pauses
to watch. Country barbering is partly a public event.

The scissors are then introduced for close work. The barber
holds the customer's ear between two fingers and cuts around
it with the speed of a man trimming paper in
a fire. Occasionally he stops, examines the ear, and appears
relieved to find it still attached.

At last he applies the neck brush. This instrument was
designed to remove loose hair but has gradually hardened into
a weapon. He beats the customer across the neck and
shoulders until every hair is either dislodged or driven permanently
beneath the skin. Bay rum follows, administered by pouring it
directly on the head and rubbing downward. The customer's eyes
close. His wounds become known to him individually.

The barber lifts the sheet with a dramatic snap. A
cloud of hair fills the shop, settles on the stove,
and causes the dog to move to the doorway.

"That'll do you," says the barber.

The customer dresses and looks into the small mirror near
the door. His head appears to have been reduced for
storage. The ears stand out with a new independence, and
the white track at the back of the neck ascends
farther than any road has a right to go.

Yet he pays, tips the barber, and promises to return.

This promise is not weakness. It is social necessity. The
barber already knows the customer's opinions, relatives, occupation, and preferred
team. To begin again with a stranger would require explanations.
Moreover, the stranger might know less about the game.

Thus the institution survives. Men continue to enter barber shops
believing they require haircuts, when what they truly require is
restraint, steam, warning, athletic instruction, and a personal demonstration of
how much pressure the human head can endure.

The city barber reaches this result by organization, chemistry, and
fear. The country barber reaches it by taking a long
run at the chair. Their methods differ, but their purpose
is identical: to leave the customer lighter in hair, heavier
in information, and profoundly grateful to be outdoors.

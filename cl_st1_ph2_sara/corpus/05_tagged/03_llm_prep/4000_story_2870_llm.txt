At our monthly dinner, which brought together five men who
had survived friendship by seeing one another only twelve times
a year, Joseph de Bardon was asked for a story.

He had reached that agreeable age at which a wealthy
bachelor, no longer young enough to be ridiculous through innocence,
is still young enough to be ridiculous through vanity. He
was intelligent without ideas, cultivated without study, and informed upon
everything in the manner of a man who has listened
attentively in drawing rooms. He knew the names of painters,
the prices of horses, the scandals of ministers, the addresses
of good restaurants, and the proper season for leaving Paris.
As a raconteur, however, he was incomparable.

That evening, after the servants had removed the coffee cups
and placed before us a decanter of old brandy, he
stretched his hand toward the little blue flame beneath the
silver warmer and said:

"Messieurs, you have spoken of women who hunt in churches,
women who hunt at watering places, women who hunt in
railway carriages. But do you know the huntresses of the
tombs?"

We laughed.

Bardon did not. He merely turned his petit verre between
his fingers and continued:

"I do not know whether there exists a guild of
them, with rules, districts and professional secrets. I know only
that I met one—or, at least, I believe that I
did. The matter remains uncertain, which makes it more amusing.

"You are aware of my taste for cemeteries. I find
them restful. The living city deafens us with its ambitions,
its wheels, its cries, its bells, its bargains and its
lies; the dead city asks nothing. Its inhabitants have ceased
to borrow money, to give dinners, to write books, to
solicit decorations, to betray their friends and to complain of
the government. They are tranquil citizens.

"I often walk in Montmartre Cemetery. It is not beautiful
in the noble sense. Père-Lachaise has grandeur, avenues, illustrious shades,
something of an imperial necropolis; Montmartre resembles a crowded suburb
whose householders have built according to their means and their
bad taste. There are marble angels with the faces of
shopgirls, bronze geniuses who appear to be blowing theatrical trumpets,
busts of manufacturers in frock coats, and family chapels that
look like small branch banks. The rich dead continue to
display their riches; the poor dead lie packed together as
they lived, wall against wall.

"Yet, in autumn, the place possesses a penetrating sadness. The
yellow leaves gather in corners of the paths; a weakened
sun slides over the stones without warming them; black dresses
pass among the white tombs; and here and there some
solitary cypress lifts its dark finger as though warning mankind
to be silent.

"One afternoon in October I entered there without any purpose
except to escape the boulevard. The sky was pale gray,
the air mild, and Paris had that tired appearance which
comes after several days of rain. I wandered between the
monuments, reading names.

"Reading epitaphs is an excellent lesson in human vanity. Everyone
was a devoted husband, an adored mother, an inconsolable friend,
an honest merchant. Not one bore the inscription: 'Here lies
a tiresome fellow who bored his family for forty years.'
Death, which suppresses the person, improves the character.

"I came presently to the grave of a woman whom
I had once loved. You did not know her. She
was married, charming, imprudent, and capable of lying with such
grace that truth in her mouth would have seemed a
vulgarity. For six months she believed me indispensable; for another
six I believed her unforgettable. Then life, that great remover
of inscriptions, rubbed out our passion. She died several years
afterward.

"I stood before her tomb and tried to summon the
emotion appropriate to the occasion. I remembered her shoulders more
readily than her soul, her laughter more clearly than her
words. Still, I felt a sincere melancholy. We mourn less
for the dead, perhaps, than for the persons we were
when they loved us. Before that slab I saw my
vanished youth buried beside her.

"As I turned away, I noticed a woman kneeling at
a neighboring tomb.

"She was in deep mourning. Her long veil had slipped
from her shoulders and spread across the stone like a
black pool. Beneath the dark edge of her bonnet appeared
fair hair, so pale and luminous that it resembled rays
of dawn imprisoned under night. Her figure was slender, distingué,
and young. She did not pray. She remained bent forward,
her forehead resting upon her gloved hands, in an attitude
of complete desolation.

"I confess that beautiful grief moves me more than ugly
grief. This is unjust, but nature is not a republican.

"I passed slowly. Upon the tomb I read:

"'Captain Henri de Varnet, Twenty-ninth Infantry, killed before Lang-Son, Tonquin.
Pray for him.'

"A sword and a palm branch were carved beneath the
inscription. The widow—she could only be his widow—made a slight
movement, as if a sob had shaken her. I walked
on, ashamed of my curiosity, yet I looked back.

"She had risen. For an instant she stood upright, wavering,
with both hands extended before her. Then she fell.

"I ran to her. Her head had struck the veil
rather than the stone, and when I lifted her I
saw a face of remarkable delicacy, very white, with closed
eyelids and a small sorrowful mouth. There was no one
near us. A gardener was raking leaves at the end
of the avenue, too far away to hear.

"I loosened the ribbons at her throat and called:

"'Madame! Madame!'

"She did not answer. I supported her against my breast
and felt, I admit, that providence sometimes arranges its accidents
with considerable elegance.

"At last she sighed. Her eyes opened—blue eyes, uncertain and
frightened.

"'Where am I?'

"'You fainted, madame.'

"She looked toward the tomb and began to tremble.

"'Ah! Yes. I remember. Forgive me, monsieur. I am ashamed.'

"'There is nothing to forgive. Can you stand?'

"She attempted it, failed, and leaned heavily upon my arm.

"'My maid,' she murmured. 'She should have come. She is
ill. I wished to come alone. I had to come.
It is the anniversary.'

"Then, pointing toward the stone, she added:

"'My husband.'

"I bowed with the gravity demanded by officers killed in
distant colonies.

"'You must not remain here,' I said. 'The air is
cold. Permit me to take you to the gate.'

"'You are very kind, monsieur. I am causing you great
trouble.'

"'On the contrary, madame.'

"She walked with difficulty. At every few steps she stopped,
closed her eyes and breathed deeply. Her hand rested on
my sleeve, light but confiding. Thus we traversed the city
of the dead, looking, no doubt, like two affectionate mourners;
and I was conscious that several old women regarded me
with sympathy, already assigning me some honorable relationship to the
deceased captain.

"Outside the gate stood one of those restaurants where families
celebrate funerals with veal, wine and loud conversation. Death gives
an appetite; tears dry quickly in the odor of sauces.
I proposed that she take tea.

"She resisted.

"'No, monsieur. I have abused your kindness.'

"'You will faint again.'

"'I feel stronger.'

"She nearly fell as she spoke. The argument was decisive.

"We entered a small private room, for the public dining
room was occupied by a funeral party whose chief mourner
was carving a chicken. I ordered tea and rum. The
widow raised her veil. She was even prettier than I
had thought, perhaps twenty-six, with that fragile complexion which appears
made for tears and recovers from them admirably.

"She drank a little tea, then a little rum, and
color returned to her cheeks.

"'I cannot thank you,' she said. 'Without you I might
have remained there until night.'

"'Someone else would have come.'

"'Perhaps. But kindness is rare in Paris.'

"This compliment, though commonplace, pleased me. We exchanged names. She
called herself Madame de Varnet and lived in the Rue
de Douai. Her husband had been dead fourteen months. She
had no children, few relations and scarcely any friends. His
family, military and provincial, had disapproved of the marriage. Her
own parents were dead. She was, she repeated, quite alone.

"Alone! There is no word a pretty woman pronounces with
greater effect before a man who is not.

"She spoke of the captain. He had been brave, severe,
jealous, adored by his soldiers and feared by his servants.
Their married life had lasted two years. Then Tonquin had
taken him, leaving her with a modest income and an
immensity of silence.

"'You cannot imagine,' she said, 'what it is to live
alone, always alone, to return to empty rooms, to hear
no voice, to have no one who notices whether you
are sad or ill.'

"I could imagine it very well, since I had lived
alone for twenty years and found it delightful; but the
solitude of a bachelor is considered independence, while the solitude
of a young widow is a misfortune. I respected the
distinction.

"When she was sufficiently restored, I found a cab. At
her door she held out her hand.

"'Good-bye, monsieur. I shall always remember what you have done.'

"'May I inquire tomorrow after your health?'

"She hesitated with perfect propriety.

"'If you wish.'

"The next day I called. She received me in a
little salon hung with blue silk. I observed at once
that her costume of grief had altered. At the cemetery
she had worn the complete uniform of bereavement: crape, veil,
severe bonnet, gloves without ornament. At home her mourning was
softened; a narrow white collar framed her neck, and a
silver clasp shone at her waist. Sorrow, like diplomacy, has
its ceremonial dress and its private dress.

"She thanked me again. We spoke of music, travel and
the weather. I noticed a portrait of Captain de Varnet
in uniform, a broad-shouldered man with a stiff mustache and
the expression of one prepared to discipline eternity.

"'He had a noble face,' I said.

"She lowered her eyes.

"'Yes.'

"On my third visit she smiled. On my fourth she
laughed. This did not shock me. The dead have no
right to demand that laughter perish with them. On my
fifth visit I kissed her hand, and she withdrew it
so slowly that I understood I might soon kiss something
else.

"Yet she continued to speak of loneliness.

"'You will leave me too,' she said. 'Everyone leaves.'

"'Why should I leave?'

"'Because I am sad. Men fear sadness.'

"'Not when it is beautiful.'

"She looked at me reproachfully, then smiled, then reproached herself
for smiling. It was all extremely delicate.

"We dined together in a private room on the boulevard.
She wore gray, almost black, but a bunch of violets
rested at her breast. During dinner she drank little, spoke
softly, and sometimes allowed a silence to fall between us,
a silence more intimate than conversation. When I took her
home, she did not dismiss the carriage. She asked whether
I would come upstairs for a cup of tea.

"The tea was never made.

"I will not tire you with the history of the
next three weeks. It resembled all brief passions: impatience, pleasure,
tenderness, indiscretion, little lies believed from politeness, and great promises
forgotten from necessity. She loved with a touching mixture of
fear and skill. Sometimes, in the midst of happiness, she
would hide her face and murmur:

"'It is wrong. He is dead, and I am betraying
him.'

"I consoled her by arguments which seemed convincing at midnight
and less so in daylight.

"She never asked me for money. I insist upon that.
Once, however, she mentioned that her landlord was troublesome; another
time, that a jeweler held a bracelet which had belonged
to her mother. These remarks may have been confidences, signals
or mere accidents. I paid nothing. Perhaps that is why
our passion lasted only three weeks; perhaps it would have
lasted only three weeks in any case.

"For I tired of her.

"Do not look virtuous, messieurs. One tires because one tires.
The most exquisite dish, served daily, becomes a diet; the
most charming woman, once she belongs to our habits, is
menaced by the woman who does not. Madame de Varnet's
sadness, which had first seemed like perfume, became atmospheric. Her
'alone, always alone' ended by making me long to be
so.

"I announced a journey to Vienna.

"She turned pale.

"'For long?'

"'A month, perhaps six weeks.'

"'And you will write?'

"'Every day.'

"She knew that this meant never. Women understand departures better
than timetables. She wept, but without violence, and when I
left she said:

"'I knew you would abandon me.'

"The phrase annoyed me because it was true.

"I did not go to Vienna. I went nowhere. I
changed restaurants, avoided the Rue de Douai and allowed thirty
days to pass. Yet I continued to think of her,
not with desire, but with curiosity. There had been in
her conduct something too smooth, too exactly shaded, like the
acting of a great comédienne who appears natural because every
gesture has been rehearsed.

"One afternoon, the weather being clear and melancholy, I returned
to Montmartre Cemetery.

"Perhaps I wished to revisit my former mistress's grave. Perhaps
I wished to see the captain's tomb. Perhaps, being vain,
I hoped to surprise a woman weeping there for two
men.

"The cemetery wore the same colors: gray sky, white stone,
black garments, yellow leaves. I walked toward the avenue where
I had found her. Before I reached it, I saw
a couple approaching.

"The man was about sixty, tall, decorated, with white whiskers
and the grave bearing of a retired ambassador or a
prosperous notary. On his arm leaned a young woman in
deep mourning. Her step was weak; her veil fell over
her shoulders; her fair hair gleamed beneath the black bonnet
like rays of dawn.

"It was she.

"She pressed close to the old gentleman, exactly as she
had pressed against me when I led her from the
tomb. He bent toward her with anxious solicitude. No doubt
he was saying:

"'You must not remain here, madame. The air is cold.'

"As they drew near, her eyes met mine. For a
fraction of a second they expressed surprise; then they became
calm. Almost imperceptibly she raised one finger to her lips.

"Do not recognize me.

"I passed them without a bow.

"At the turning of the path I looked back. The
distinguished old gentleman was supporting her with both hands. She
appeared about to faint.

"I went to Captain de Varnet's grave. Upon the stone
lay fresh flowers.

"Now, what was I to think? Was she truly the
captain's widow, consoling herself methodically? Was she not his widow
at all? Had she chosen that tomb because an officer
killed in Tonquin could not return inconveniently? Did she faint
only before rich men, or before any presentable arm? Was
the old gentleman destined merely to pay the landlord and
redeem the maternal bracelet, or would he also receive tea
that was never made?

"And Captain de Varnet—had he known her? Had anyone known
him? Was he married? Was he perhaps some honest bachelor
whose eternal rest had been transformed into an office for
sentimental introductions?

"I left the cemetery laughing, though the laugh was not
entirely cheerful. After all, she hunted the living among the
dead; but had I done anything very different? She had
offered grief, I had offered consolation; she had used the
captain, I had used her loneliness. Each of us had
brought suitable merchandise to the market.

"Still, I should like to know one thing."

Bardon emptied his glass and glanced around the table.

"Whose widow was she that afternoon?"

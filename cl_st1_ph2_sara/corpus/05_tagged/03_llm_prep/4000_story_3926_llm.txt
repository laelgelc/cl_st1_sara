# The Extreme Realist

I

When Mr. Horace Brown became editor of *The Metropolitan Monthly*,
he found upon his desk seven hundred and forty-three manuscripts,
a blue pencil, three unpaid luncheon bills, and the conviction
that American literature was dying of imagination.

The manuscripts had accumulated under the former editor, who had
possessed a tender heart and weak eyesight. Brown disposed of
both the manuscripts and the former editor's principles within a
week.

"What the public wants," he announced, "is Reality."

He pronounced the word with a capital letter. Editors can
do this orally. It is one of their privileges, and
partly accounts for their salaries.

"No more dreams," said Brown. "No more palaces on coral
islands. No more clever young dukes disguised as gardeners. No
more women whose eyes resemble moonlight upon deep water. Have
you ever seen an eye that resembled moonlight upon deep
water?"

His assistant, Ferris, admitted that he had not.

"Exactly! Eyes are eyes. Water is water. Moonlight is moonlight.
The business of literature is to keep them separate."

Ferris made a note of this. He made notes of
everything Brown said, though he afterward used the backs of
them for calculating postage.

Brown's ideal story must be true to life, short and
crisp, served hot, and devoid of poetic connotation. Especially poetic
connotation. He had once rejected a very good tale because
the author had spoken of a "weary road." Roads, Brown
explained, possessed no nervous system and could not become weary.

He prohibited sunsets unless the author supplied the precise meteorological
conditions. He discouraged love at first sight because, statistically, it
so often led to regret. He objected to villains, holding
that no human being was wholly bad, although he sometimes
forgot this doctrine when discussing rival editors. He demanded that
every story deal with things the author knew personally.

"Write what you have lived," was his motto. "If you
have lived nothing, do not write. Get a position in
wholesale groceries."

His office reflected his creed. There were no flowers in
it, because flowers suggested spring, youth, burial, marriage, and other
poetic connotations. There were two steel filing cabinets, a roll-top
desk, a calendar advertising kidney pills, and an umbrella which
belonged to nobody. On the wall hung a framed notice:

FACTS FIRST.
ADJECTIVES HOT.
SENTENCES SHORT AND CRISP.

What was meant by hot adjectives neither Brown nor anyone
else precisely knew, but the phrase had energy.

For six months Brown searched for an author who would
embody his principles. He found men who wrote from life,
but unfortunately they had led no life worth writing about.
He found men who had led remarkable lives, but they
could not spell. He found one sailor who had been
shipwrecked eleven times and who submitted an article entitled "The
Ocean's Call." Brown rejected it because the ocean did not
call; it merely moved under gravitational and atmospheric influences.

Then Robert Severne appeared.

Severne was twenty-seven, pale, thin, upright, and serious-minded. He had
large gray eyes, a careful necktie, and no sense of
humour. I do not mean that his sense of humour
was undeveloped, misdirected, or asleep. He had none. A joke
entered his mind as a fly enters a bottle: it
buzzed briefly against transparent obstacles and died.

He laid a manuscript upon Brown's desk.

"Is this drawn from personal experience?" asked Brown.

"Entirely," said Severne.

Brown glanced through the story. Then he read it carefully.
Then he shut the office door and read it again.

It concerned a bookkeeper who missed his suburban train, ate
a bad dinner at a railway restaurant, and arrived home
to discover that the kitchen pipe had burst. There was
no murder, no mystery, no hidden will, and no woman
with violet eyes. The pipe was repaired by a plumber
who charged three dollars and seventy-five cents. Severne had paid
precisely that sum.

"It is magnificent," said Brown.

"It is accurate," said Severne.

"Better! The descriptions are photographs."

"I measured the restaurant."

"The dialogue is life itself."

"I took notes."

"The oatmeal—how did you achieve that extraordinary passage about the
oatmeal?"

"I ate it."

Brown rose and clasped his hand.

"My boy," he said, "you are the man American literature
has been waiting for."

American literature, which had not been consulted, continued to wait
with considerable composure.

II

From that day Robert Severne became the prophet of the
New Realism, although he earned only forty dollars a story
and frequently had to borrow carfare. Brown printed his tales
under titles such as "Tuesday," "A Visit to the Dentist,"
and "The Second Floor Back." Critics praised their restraint. One
of them declared that Severne had exposed the colossal tragedy
latent in the ordinary boiled potato. Severne wrote to the
critic asking what tragedy he meant.

He was incapable of describing anything he had not personally
seen, heard, tasted, touched, measured, or suffered. If a character
caught cold, Severne sat in a draft until he sneezed.
Before writing of a clerk dismissed by his employer, he
obtained temporary employment in a haberdashery and contrived to insult
the proprietor. For a story about insomnia, he drank nine
cups of coffee and spent the night counting cracks in
the ceiling.

He once began a tale in which a man was
stung by a bee. Halfway through the opening paragraph, his
conscience stopped him. He had never been stung by a
bee.

For three afternoons he visited parks carrying a pot of
honey. The bees ignored him. On the fourth day a
wasp supplied the required sensation, but Severne discarded the paragraph
because a wasp was not a bee and the pain
might possess an entirely different literary value.

Brown adored him.

"Integrity!" he cried. "That is integrity. The average writer would
change the bee into a wasp. Or, worse, leave it
a bee and trust to imagination. Imagination is the bankruptcy
court of observation."

Severne agreed. He always agreed gravely. Brown might have said
that the Nebular Hypothesis was responsible for weak characterisation, and
Severne would have considered the claim before accepting it in
principle.

Now, there was in Severne's life a young woman named
Lucy Melville.

Why a young woman had entered it is one of
those mysteries with which nature confounds philosophy. Lucy was pretty,
dark-haired, serious-minded, and devoted to romantic fiction. She had no
more sense of humour than Severne, but hers was a
different sort of humourlessness. Severne took facts seriously; Lucy took
emotions seriously. He believed a thing noble if it had
occurred. She believed it noble if it could not possibly
occur.

Her favourite novels contained ruined abbeys, ancestral curses, masked horsemen,
secret marriages, hereditary jewels, and young men who renounced kingdoms
for governesses. The men in them had proud lips and
incurable sorrows. They seldom worked. Their hearts broke with astonishing
regularity, but their health remained excellent through four hundred pages.

Severne loved Lucy because she had soft eyes and listened
when he discussed technique. Lucy loved Severne because his silence
seemed full of suffering. It was actually full of punctuation,
but silence permits generous interpretations.

They became engaged on a Sunday afternoon beneath an elm
tree.

"I have never before asked a woman to marry me,"
said Severne.

Lucy's eyes filled.

"That makes it beautiful."

"It makes it accurate."

"And you will love me always?"

"I can make no statement concerning future emotional conditions without
sufficient data."

She accepted this as a solemn delicacy of feeling and
leaned her head upon his shoulder.

Their engagement proceeded peacefully until Lucy began reading Severne's stories.

At first she tried to admire them. Love can do
much. It can make a girl praise a man's poetry,
endure his friends, and pretend not to notice that he
eats asparagus incorrectly. But love could not make Lucy admire
"The Burst Kitchen Pipe."

"Robert," she said one evening, "does nothing ever happen in
your stories?"

"Everything happens exactly as it happened."

"But nobody sacrifices anything."

"In 'The Insurance Premium,' Wilkins sacrifices thirty-eight dollars and ten
cents."

"For love, I mean."

"His insurance was for his wife's benefit."

Lucy pressed one hand to her forehead.

"You do not understand. I mean a great sacrifice. A
man should cast away ambition, fortune, perhaps honour itself, for
the woman he loves."

"That would be criminally improvident."

"He should cross deserts for her."

"Unless she had chosen to reside in a desert, the
journey would be unnecessary."

"He should face death!"

"I see no practical connection between death and matrimony."

Lucy stared at him. The lamp burned low. Outside, rain
whispered against the windows, though Severne afterward maintained that it
struck them at an average angle of forty degrees and
made a sound more like tapping.

"Robert," she said, "have you ever imagined anything?"

"No."

"Never?"

"Not deliberately."

She rose.

"Then you cannot love."

This was unjust, but admirably romantic.

Severne also rose.

"My pulse increases measurably in your presence."

"Pulse!" cried Lucy. "You speak to me of pulse!"

"It is accepted physiological evidence of emotion."

"A thermometer could love me by your standards."

"A thermometer does not possess a pulse."

That finished it.

Lucy removed her engagement ring. It caught at the knuckle,
which somewhat impaired the majesty of the act, but after
a brief struggle she placed it on the table.

"You must choose," she said. "Your creed—or me."

"My creed is truth."

"And I am romance?"

"You have frequently expressed a preference for it."

"Then write one story for me. One story of imagination.
Invent something splendid, impossible, heroic. Let a man suffer for
love. Let him ride through danger. Let him risk his
life. Publish it under your own name, and I shall
know you have sacrificed your principles for my sake."

Severne became pale. A lesser man might have lied, but
he would first have needed to imagine the lie, and
Severne was helpless.

"You ask me," he said, "to prostitute Art."

Lucy put on her cloak.

"I ask you to prove that you love me."

"There should be another method."

"There is none."

"Could I expose myself to influenza?"

"No."

"Lose money?"

"No."

"Remain awake for forty-eight hours?"

"No!"

She moved toward the door, then paused in the soft
light, her cloak falling about her like the mantle of
outraged Romance.

"When you have written an imaginative story," she said, "come
to me. Until then, everything is over."

The door closed.

Severne stood motionless.

The lamp smoked. The ring lay on the table. Somewhere
a clock struck nine, though possibly it was ten; the
narrator was not present with a notebook. Severne's soul surveyed
the wreckage of its universe.

Then he sat down and recorded the conversation while it
was fresh.

III

Brown received news of the broken engagement with professional enthusiasm.

"Excellent!" he said. "Suffering is material."

Severne looked wounded.

"I have lost the woman I love."

"Precisely. Get it down at once. Short and crisp."

"It is not a subject for fiction."

"My dear boy, everything is a subject for fiction after
it has become sufficiently painful."

Severne walked to the window. Below him the city roared,
surged, thundered, murmured, or merely conducted traffic; choose the verb
according to your school of literature.

"She believes imagination superior to truth," he said.

"Women do."

"All women?"

"Every woman who has rejected a manuscript of mine."

"I must go away."

"Where?"

"Somewhere primitive. Somewhere emotion is stripped of artificial conventions. Somewhere
I may observe fundamental realities."

"Wyoming," said Brown.

"Why Wyoming?"

Brown pointed to a railroad advertisement on the opposite wall.
It displayed a cowboy galloping beneath the words THE REAL
WEST AWAITS YOU.

"There," said Brown, "you have documentary evidence."

Thus it happened that Robert Severne journeyed west with two
trunks, a typewriter, a folding ruler, six notebooks, and a
grief which he believed eternal. Young men commonly mistake the
duration of grief. So do widows, poets, and persons with
toothache.

He found lodging on a cattle ranch belonging to a
broad, weathered man named Ike Mangum. Ike wore leather chaps,
spoke sparingly, and regarded the typewriter as a machine designed
to frighten horses.

"What you figger on doin' with that contraption?" he asked.

"I write stories."

Ike considered.

"True ones?"

"Entirely."

"Then why write 'em?"

The question troubled Severne for several days.

Ranch life supplied abundant material. He learned to saddle a
horse, mend a fence, brand a calf, distinguish between several
kinds of dust, and eat beans without expectation. He recorded
everything. If Ike said, "Mornin'," Severne wrote it down. If
a cow looked at him, he described the expression, then
revised the description because he could not establish what the
cow had intended.

At night he sat in his room over the ranch
kitchen and worked beneath a smoky lamp. The room contained
a bed, a chair, a cracked basin, and one energetic
mosquito. Its window looked toward plains so enormous that even
Severne felt something resembling poetic connotation. He fought it.

His sorrow deepened—or, more accurately, became organised. He pictured Lucy
reading of his lonely exile. He imagined—no, he did not
imagine; he deduced—that future generations would revere him as the
martyr of Realism. Critics would speak of "the Wyoming period,"
during which privation burned away his last concessions to popular
taste. There might be a monument. On one side would
stand Truth, austere and possibly bronze. On the other would
sit Love, veiled and remorseful.

These reflections comforted him, although they were perilously imaginative.

After three months he began a Western story.

It concerned a young ranch hand named John Smith who
discovered irregularities in the ranch accounts, mentioned them to the
foreman, and was discharged. Severne had personally inspected accounts and
been discharged from a haberdashery, so the foundation was firm.
But Brown had lately written that readers desired "movement."

"Give me action," Brown said. "Not imaginary action, naturally. Real
action. Served hot."

Severne therefore arranged for John Smith to be chased by
armed men.

He wrote the opening of the episode.

John mounted his horse and fled. Behind him came four
riders, firing as they rode. Bullets struck the earth near
his horse's hoofs. He experienced considerable fear.

Severne stopped.

"Considerable fear" was vague. Worse, it was dishonest. He had
never been pursued by armed men. He did not know
whether a fugitive heard separate shots or one continuous disturbance;
whether he looked behind him; whether terror dried the throat,
tightened the stomach, weakened the knees, or produced all three
effects in alphabetical order. He had never felt a bullet
pass near his head. He could not continue.

For an ordinary writer, this difficulty would have lasted the
time required to sharpen a pencil. For Severne it became
a moral crisis.

He consulted Ike.

"I require four men to chase me on horseback," he
said.

Ike removed a splinter from his thumb.

"What for?"

"Art."

"Art who?"

"My work. I must ascertain the sensations of pursuit."

Ike thought this over.

"You been drinkin'?"

"No."

"Snake-bit?"

"No."

"Woman trouble?"

Severne stiffened.

"That is irrelevant."

"Then it's a woman."

Eventually Ike consented to help, partly from good nature and
partly because cowboys living fifty miles from a railway welcome
any entertainment not involving a broken leg. Four ranch hands
volunteered: Pete Givens, Shorty Bale, Mexican Sam, who was from
Illinois, and a youth called Deacon because he had once
attended church.

The plan was simple. Severne would ride away with a
five-minute start. The others would pursue him for ten miles,
shout threats, and fire blank cartridges. At Black Tooth Ravine
they would overtake him. Severne would then record his sensations
while hot, short, and crisp.

"Don't shoot too close," Ike warned.

"We ain't shootin' nothin'," said Pete. "Blanks."

"Don't get excited and forget."

"Who, us?"

Ike looked at them. The question answered itself.

The experiment began at two in the afternoon.

Severne wore a dark coat because John Smith wore one
in the manuscript. He carried a notebook in his breast
pocket and a pencil attached to his wrist by string.
Before mounting, he inspected the blank cartridges.

"You understand," he said, "that your expressions must be authentic
but not personally offensive."

"What kin we call you?" asked Shorty.

"Anything a pursuer would naturally call a fugitive."

Shorty brightened.

"No references to Miss Melville," Severne added.

The cowboys exchanged disappointed glances.

Severne rode out across the plain.

Five minutes later the others followed, whooping magnificently and discharging
their revolvers into the air. Their horses sprang forward. Dust
rose. Birds flew from the sagebrush. A distant herd of
cattle reached the conclusion that civilisation had again failed and
moved elsewhere.

At first Severne felt self-conscious. He noted that the shots
were distinct, that his horse's hoofbeats seemed louder than usual,
and that glancing backward at speed endangered balance. He wrote
these facts while riding.

Then a blank charge scorched the brim of his hat.

His observations became less academic.

"Mind the distance!" he shouted.

"Ride, you thievin' skunk!" bawled Pete, entering into his part.

Severne's horse accelerated. The plain rolled beneath him. Wind tore
at his eyes. The pursuing cries came closer, then receded,
then spread strangely to the north.

This last phenomenon required explanation.

A second party of cowboys had appeared beyond a low
ridge. They were seven in number and belonged to the
Bar Circle outfit. That morning someone had stolen a bay
horse from their remuda. Coming upon four armed men pursuing
a pale stranger in a dark coat, they instantly understood
the situation. Cowboys, like editors and lovers, understand situations most
completely when they understand them wrong.

"There he goes!" shouted their leader, Bud Ransome.

"Horse thief!" yelled another.

They joined the chase.

Pete saw them coming.

"Company," he remarked.

"What they want?" asked Mexican Sam.

"Reckon they want in."

"Wave 'em off."

Pete waved. The Bar Circle men interpreted the gesture as
an appeal for assistance and came faster.

Severne glanced back and saw eleven riders instead of four.

His first thought was that Ike had enlarged the experiment
without permission. His second was that this constituted a serious
breach of artistic method. His third was interrupted by the
crack of a rifle and the sharp, vicious sound of
a bullet passing within a foot of his ear.

A blank cartridge does not make that sound.

Severne's scalp tightened. His mouth became dry. His stomach appeared
to remove itself from its customary location. Here, at last,
was exact information, but he felt no satisfaction.

Another shot struck the ground ahead of him and threw
dust into his face.

"Stop shootin'!" cried Pete.

"Spread out!" shouted Bud Ransome.

The two commands reached Severne as one long howl.

His horse, which had entered into the spirit of realism
more completely than its rider, stretched flat and ran. Sagebrush
whipped by. The land fell into a shallow valley and
rose toward a line of broken hills. Severne clung to
the saddle and discovered that a man under fire does
not think noble thoughts. He does not review his childhood.
He does not forgive his enemies. He thinks chiefly of
getting behind something thick.

He also discovered that fear was not "considerable." It was
enormous, cold, intimate, and humiliating. It occupied his boots. It
trembled in his fingers. It made the sky too bright
and every stone offensively distinct.

A bullet struck his canteen.

Water streamed down his leg.

He had time to think, with indignation, that readers would
consider this symbolical.

Then his horse stumbled.

There was a thud, a violent turning of earth and
sky, and the astonishing fact of ground against his cheek.
The horse rolled away, kicked twice, and lay still. Severne
rose on one knee. His pencil string had wrapped itself
around his thumb. His notebook had vanished.

The riders closed in.

"Hands up!" cried Bud.

Severne raised them.

Pete and the other hired pursuers had pulled up fifty
yards away. The Bar Circle men, seeing themselves superior in
number and righteousness, covered everybody with rifles.

"Got him!" said one.

"I surrender," Severne replied.

"Durn decent of you."

"I wish to explain."

"They all do," said Bud.

IV

The Bar Circle party conducted Severne to a patch of
cottonwoods beside a dry creek. Pete and his companions followed
under guard, protesting with such richness of language that Severne,
even in peril, regretted losing his notebook.

Bud Ransome was a tall man with narrow eyes and
a red moustache. He possessed the solemn confidence of one
who has never doubted a mistake.

"Where's the hoss?" he demanded.

"My horse has been shot."

"Not your cayuse. Our bay."

"I know nothing of your bay."

Bud looked at his companions.

"He knows nothin'."

"That's bad," said one.

"Always is," said another.

Pete pushed forward.

"Listen, pards. This here's no hoss thief. He's a writer."

There was a pause.

The Bar Circle men looked at Severne with new suspicion.

"A what?"

"A story writer. He paid us to chase him."

Bud turned slowly toward Severne.

"You paid four men to run you down and shoot
at you?"

"With blank cartridges."

"What for?"

"To acquire authentic experience for a work of fiction."

The silence that followed was profound. A grasshopper made a
brief statement and withdrew it.

Bud removed his hat and scratched his head.

"You mean to say," he began patiently, "that you hired
these yahoos to chase you across Wyoming so's you could
write how it felt?"

"Yes."

"And that dark coat is for the story?"

"Yes."

"And that there pencil tied to your hand?"

"Yes."

"And you ain't stole no bay?"

"No."

Bud replaced his hat.

"Boys," he said, "I've heard some lies."

His companions nodded.

"I've heard whoppers that'd make a Missouri mule climb a
tree to get away from 'em. But this—this has feathers."

"It is the exact truth," said Severne.

"That proves it," said Bud. "No man tells the exact
truth unless he's fixin' to sell somethin'."

Pete attempted intervention.

"He's plumb cracked on realism."

"What's realism?"

"Means he can't write nothin' till it's happened to him."

Bud stared.

"Then let's hang him. He can write about that."

The suggestion was received with approval.

Severne felt the cold fear return, but sharper. He drew
himself up.

"You cannot execute a man upon an unsupported assumption."

"We got support," said Bud. "The bay's gone, you're runnin',
and them four are chasin' you."

"They were employed to chase me."

"That's the unsupported part."

"It is corroborated by all four."

"Four men in on a hoss theft can corroborate a
heap."

Shorty spat.

"You ain't got sense enough to pour sand out of
a boot."

Bud levelled his rifle.

"Maybe not. But I got sense enough to make five
men quiet."

At this point Deacon began laughing. It was not prudent
laughter, but cowboys do not always choose prudence, especially when
it has been a long day.

"Quit that," said Bud.

"I was just thinkin'," Deacon gasped, "how Bob here'll describe
this. 'The condemned man laughed short and crisp.'"

Even Severne might have perceived humour in the situation had
he not been the condemned man.

The Bar Circle riders produced a rope. One end was
thrown across the branch of a cottonwood. The action was
accomplished with an efficiency which did not reassure Severne. Western
men are often casual about hanging because they do not
expect to be the noun in the sentence.

"You are committing murder," he said.

"Mebbe," replied Bud. "But we're doin' it realistic."

They tied Severne's hands.

Here the narrator ought perhaps to describe the dark gorge
in which the cottonwoods stood. It may have been a
narrow canon. Authorities differ as to the geological dignity required
by such places. There were stones, a bank, some trees,
and enough shade to make the proceedings picturesque, which was
the very quality Severne had always distrusted.

The noose was placed around his neck.

Instantly he observed several facts. Rope scratched the skin. A
man awaiting death heard trivial sounds with absurd clarity. One
of the horses was chewing its bit. A Bar Circle
rider had a loose spur. Bud smelled of tobacco. Clouds
moved above the branches with an indifference that would have
seemed poetic had it not been insulting.

Severne thought of Lucy.

This annoyed him. He had expected to think of artistic
principle.

He saw her as she had stood in the lamplight,
asking him to prove love by sacrifice. He saw the
engagement ring on the table. He wondered whether dying for
realism would count. Probably not. Lucy had rigid standards.

Bud took hold of the rope.

"Anything to say?"

"Yes," said Severne. "The sensation produced by a noose is
not primarily one of constriction. It is irritation at the
left side of the neck, accompanied by—"

"Pull," said someone.

There came a thunder of hoofs.

Ike Mangum burst over the bank with six ranch hands
behind him. He had become uneasy when shooting continued too
long and had ridden after the experiment. Unlike Severne, Ike
did not require personal experience to infer trouble.

"Drop that rope!" he shouted.

Rifles swung. Men cursed. Horses wheeled. For ten lively seconds
the air was crowded with commands, explanations, threats, dust, and
hot adjectives.

Ike knew Bud Ransome. More importantly, he knew that the
missing Bar Circle bay had been found an hour earlier,
grazing contentedly in a draw after pushing through a broken
fence.

Bud listened. His red moustache sagged.

"Found, you say?"

"Found."

"Same bay?"

"How many bays you lose this mornin'?"

Bud looked at Severne, who still stood beneath the tree
wearing the noose.

"Well," he said, "that alters the case."

"It does from here," said Severne.

Bud untied him.

"No hard feelin's."

"I possess very hard feelings."

"Natural. But you got to admit your yarn sounded uncommon."

"It was true."

"That's what made it uncommon."

Pete recovered the missing notebook near the dead horse. Severne
found that his notes ended with the sentence: "When genuine
bullets are introduced, terror increases abruptly."

He underlined it twice.

V

For three weeks Severne wrote.

He wrote in the ranch house, on the porch, in
bed, at meals, and once while Ike was explaining that
the roof had caught fire. His Western story expanded beyond
its original dimensions. John Smith was chased, shot at, captured,
accused, threatened, and almost hanged. Every detail was exact. Severne
measured the noose. He interviewed Bud concerning his state of
mind. Bud claimed he had never had one, but supplied
the dialect.

The sentences were short and crisp. The adjectives were served
hot. There was no poetic connotation except where Lucy's face
had appeared to the hero under the cottonwoods; Severne deleted
that passage and restored it four times.

At last the manuscript was complete.

He titled it "The Pursuit."

"It is," he told Ike, "the most realistic story ever
written."

Ike, repairing a cinch, grunted.

"Everything in it happened."

"Too bad."

"Why?"

"Makes it hard to believe."

Severne pitied the limitations of an unliterary mind and sent
the manuscript to Brown.

Then he waited.

He conceived Brown reading it in the office beneath FACTS
FIRST. He saw the editor spring up, overturning the umbrella,
and cry, "At last!" He foresaw a special issue, critical
acclaim, perhaps an editorial manifesto. The story would establish Realism
beyond dispute. Lucy might read it. She would be forced
to recognise the majesty of truth, although he had no
intention of taking her back unless she recanted fully and
perhaps in writing.

He checked the mail twice a day.

Brown's reply arrived in nine days.

It contained the manuscript.

> My dear Severne:
>
> I am deeply disappointed. What has happened to you?
This story is impossible from beginning to end. The pursuit
is theatrical, the accidental arrival of a second posse is
an obvious device, the shooting of the horse is melodrama,
and the attempted hanging exceeds every bound of probability.
>
> Most serious of all, the hero's terror is grossly
exaggerated. No human being notices his stomach changing location or
the sky becoming offensively bright. These are imaginative flourishes, unworthy
of your former method.
>
> I fear Wyoming has corrupted you. Return to observed
life. Give me something quiet, true, short and crisp. A
hotel breakfast, perhaps.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> Horace Brown.

Severne read the letter. Then he read it carefully. Then
he walked out behind the barn and uttered several expressions
which were neither short nor crisp, though they were unquestionably
hot.

He wrote Brown sixteen pages of explanation. He enclosed affidavits
from Ike, Pete, Shorty, Mexican Sam, Deacon, Bud Ransome, and
the owner of the dead horse. Brown returned the documents.

"Proof," he wrote, "does not create plausibility."

This sentence deserves preservation. It contains more editorial wisdom than
Brown intended.

Severne's universe collapsed for the second time that year. First
Love had demanded Imagination. Now Realism demanded restraint from Reality.
He had suffered for Art, and Art had sent the
manuscript back with insufficient postage.

For two days he considered abandoning literature and entering wholesale
groceries.

On the third day, in a gesture of despair, he
mailed "The Pursuit" to *The Hearth and Home Companion*, a
magazine famous for countesses, hidden treasure, noble bandits, and illustrations
of men embracing women on staircases. Its editor, a cheerful
woman named Miss Vale, accepted the story by return mail.

"Your extraordinary romance thrilled us," she wrote. "The invention is
audacious, the danger superbly unreal, and the emotional exaggeration exactly
suited to our readers. We congratulate you upon abandoning the
narrow photographic method of your earlier work."

Severne received three hundred dollars.

This softened the blow without explaining it.

VI

Lucy Melville encountered "The Pursuit" in a railway waiting room.

She was travelling to visit an aunt who possessed bronchitis,
money, and a large house near Albany. The train was
late. Trains are frequently late in romantic circumstances, partly because
Providence understands construction.

Lucy bought *The Hearth and Home Companion* but did not
examine the table of contents. She began reading while seated
beneath a clock that had stopped at twenty minutes past
four.

The story seized her.

Here at last was life as Lucy preferred it: life
purified of probability. A lonely horseman fled across a blazing
plain. Armed riders thundered behind. Bullets sang. A horse fell.
The hero faced unjust death beneath dark trees and, at
the supreme instant, beheld the face of the woman he
loved.

Lucy's eyes filled with tears.

She read faster.

The sentences were short and crisp. The adjectives were served
hot. The details possessed a strange vividness. Dust stung her
own face. The rope seemed to scrape her own neck,
though this may have been the tight collar then fashionable.
Above all, she felt the sacrifice hidden in the work.
Whoever had written it must have cast petty reason aside
and surrendered himself to splendid invention.

She turned the last page and saw the name.

ROBERT SEVERNE.

The waiting room vanished. The clock, the delayed train, the
aunt with bronchitis, and a child eating an orange ceased
to exist. Lucy understood everything.

Robert had done it for her.

He had broken his creed. He had renounced the cold
idol of Fact. He had invented a pursuit, a hail
of bullets, a fallen horse, savage cowboys, and an impossible
rescue. He had even allowed his hero, beneath the noose,
to see the beloved woman's face. No communication could have
been clearer unless accompanied by a diagram.

"What a sacrifice!" she whispered.

The child with the orange stared at her.

Lucy rose. The Albany train arrived. She did not board
it. Bronchitis, money, and a large house might wait; Love
had issued a more dramatic timetable.

By evening she was travelling west.

Now, a less serious-minded girl might have written first. A
girl with humour might have suspected complications. Lucy did neither.
Her conception of reconciliation required distance, surprise, tears, forgiveness, and
preferably a sunset. She intended to stand before Severne and
say, "Robert, I understand." After that, she trusted to his
shattered heart.

Her journey occupied three days. During it she reread "The
Pursuit" twelve times. Each reading enlarged the sacrifice. By Nebraska,
Severne had not merely invented a story; he had crucified
his artistic soul. By Cheyenne, angels were applauding.

She reached Ike Mangum's ranch at six in the evening.

The sunset was available.

Severne sat on the porch repairing his fountain pen. A
bottle of ink stood beside him. His collar was open,
his hair untidy, and his expression that of a man
who has discovered that Truth pays forty dollars while Romance
pays three hundred.

He heard wheels. A ranch wagon stopped at the gate.

Lucy descended.

For once Severne's powers of observation failed him. He rose
so suddenly that he spilled ink upon his trousers.

"Lucy!"

"Robert!"

She came toward him across the yard.

Ike looked from one to the other, entered the house,
and drove all the ranch hands out through the kitchen.
He had no literary theories, but he possessed discretion.

Lucy stopped at the foot of the porch.

"I have read it," she said.

Severne's heart gave a violent beat. He noted this automatically.

"Read what?"

"Your story."

"Oh."

"The story you wrote for me."

Severne opened his mouth.

Lucy continued, and there was no stopping her. Emotion had
accumulated across seventeen hundred miles.

"I understand now. I understand what it cost you. Every
impossible incident, every invented danger, every magnificent exaggeration was a
renunciation. You trampled your principles underfoot for my sake."

Severne's mouth remained open.

"You sent your hero across the plain because I had
asked for a man who would ride through danger. You
put him beneath the rope because I had asked for
a man who would face death. And at that final
instant you made him see the woman he loved."

"That detail—" Severne began.

"I knew! I knew it was meant for me."

"It was based upon—"

"No, do not explain." Lucy mounted the porch. "Explanations belong
to the old Robert, the cold Robert, the man of
measurements and affidavits. The new Robert has imagination."

"There is no new Robert."

"How like you to be modest."

"I assure you, the story is wholly—"

She placed both hands in his.

You must see his difficulty.

On one side stood Truth: austere, naked, shivering slightly, and
furnished with seven affidavits. On the other stood Lucy: warm,
dark-eyed, repentant, and close enough to make physiological evidence unnecessary.
Behind Truth stood Brown, returned manuscripts, bad hotel breakfasts, and
a future in wholesale groceries. Behind Lucy stood forgiveness.

"I was wrong," she said softly. "Love is greater than
a creed."

Severne looked into her eyes.

Somewhere in his mind Art rose, adjusted its robe, and
demanded to be defended.

He ought to have told her everything. He ought to
have described the blank cartridges, the second posse, the live
bullets, the dead horse, the noose, and Bud Ransome's red
moustache. He ought to have produced the affidavits. He ought
to have proclaimed that the impossible story was true, that
strict realism had achieved its purest triumph, and that readers,
editors, and fiancées were united in error.

He drew breath.

Lucy lifted her face.

Severne had personally experienced bullets, terror, unjust accusation, and the
abrasion of rope. He had not, however, experienced this precise
moment.

As an artist, he required data.

He kissed her.

The experiment lasted longer than was strictly necessary.

When they separated, Lucy whispered, "And you will never sacrifice
love to Art again?"

Severne glanced toward the open window of his room. On
the desk lay Brown's rejection, Miss Vale's acceptance, the marked
manuscript, and the affidavits establishing the exact truth.

He considered making one final statement.

Then Lucy kissed him.

The statement was postponed.

And Art—Truthful, uncompromising, short and crisp, with all its adjectives
served hot—Art, oh, where was it?

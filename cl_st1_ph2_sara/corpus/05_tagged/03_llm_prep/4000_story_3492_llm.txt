### The Holidays

On the river-bank lay a great sal log, intended by
its owner for the mast of a boat. To the
ordinary eye it was merely a piece of timber, heavy,
inconvenient, and of no immediate interest; but to Phatik Chakravarti
and his followers it presented the materials of a magnificent
enterprise.

Phatik was fourteen, and therefore old enough to command boys
younger than himself, though not old enough to command himself.
He proposed that they should roll the log away by
main force. The owner, arriving later and discovering its disappearance,
would stand astonished at the villainy of mankind; while the
boys, hidden at a prudent distance, would enjoy the spectacle.
The proposal was carried unanimously.

The gang assembled along one side of the timber. Sleeves
were rolled up; bare feet dug into the earth; orders
flew from Phatik with regal dignity.

"One—two—three—push!"

At that critical juncture Mākhan, Phatik's younger brother, came solemnly
forward and seated himself upon the log.

This was not merely disobedience. It was a public challenge
to established authority.

The boys laughed. Phatik, anxious to preserve his dignity before
his subjects, said, "Get down."

Mākhan did not move. He fixed his eyes upon the
river with the expression of a saint who has renounced
worldly quarrels.

"I shall push you into the mud," Phatik warned.

Mākhan remained immovable.

There are moments when a ruler must either enforce his
decree or cease to rule. Phatik therefore decided that the
log should be rolled with Mākhan upon it. This variation
lent greater splendour to the manœuvre and was greeted with
enthusiasm.

Again the boys took their places.

"One—two—three—push!"

The log turned. Mākhan's philosophy proved unequal to the laws
of motion. He rose upon it for one majestic instant,
waved his arms like a windmill, and fell blind as
Fate and screaming like the Furies.

The gang scattered.

Mākhan picked himself up and flew at Phatik. He scratched
his face, struck his shoulders, and then ran home, weeping
loudly enough to secure justice in advance.

Phatik sat down upon the grass and began biting a
reed. Victory had left a poor taste in his mouth.

Presently a servant came from the house.

"Your mother wants you."

"Go away."

"She says you must come at once."

"Tell her I won't."

The servant, who had no respect for constitutional government, lifted
him by main force and carried him home.

His mother sat in the courtyard with Mākhan beside her.
The younger boy's tears had subsided, but he still breathed
in eloquent sobs.

"So you have beaten your brother again!" she cried.

"I didn't."

"You dare tell me a lie?"

"Ask him."

Mākhan, being thus invited to assist justice, declared that Phatik
had beaten him for no reason whatever.

At this Phatik's sense of injury overcame prudence. "You liar!"
he shouted, and struck him again.

His mother sprang up, seized Phatik by the arm, and
slapped him. "Wretched boy! Will you strike him before my
face?"

Phatik pushed her hand away. The movement was not violent,
but she recoiled as though he had committed an unnatural
crime.

"You would strike your mother too?"

He stared at her, angry, ashamed, and unable to explain
himself. He had not meant to strike her. Yet explanations
are delicate instruments, and a boy of fourteen usually breaks
them before they can be used.

At that moment a middle-aged stranger entered the courtyard. He
had a travelling shawl over one shoulder, and his face
bore traces of a family likeness which Phatik's mother recognised
before memory supplied the name.

"Bishamber!" she exclaimed.

It was her elder brother, returned after many years in
western India. The quarrel was suspended. Questions, blessings, recollections, and
household arrangements took possession of the day. Even Mākhan forgot
his injuries long enough to inspect the visitor's luggage.

During the following days Bishamber observed the family. He saw
that Mākhan was quiet, obedient, and skilful in that useful
art by which a younger child appears innocent when an
elder one is blamed. Phatik, on the other hand, was
noisy, restless, careless, and continually in the way. His widowed
mother loved him; but her affection had become entangled with
anxiety. He seemed to her the source of every broken
jar, every bruised knee, every complaint from the neighbours. She
feared his future, and because she feared it, she was
seldom gentle with his present.

One evening Bishamber said, "Sister, let me take Phatik to
Calcutta. I will educate him with my own boys."

The mother hesitated only long enough to appear maternal. The
proposal promised relief from daily disorder and, perhaps, a respectable
future for her son.

When Phatik was asked, "Would you like to go with
your uncle?" his joy knew no bounds.

"When shall we start, Uncle? Tomorrow? In the morning?"

His mother was wounded by his eagerness. It seemed to
her proof that he cared nothing for home. She did
not know that children often run toward a distant hope
because the love beside them has become uncertain. Phatik himself
did not know it. He imagined Calcutta as a place
of wonders, where his uncle understood him and no one
had already decided that he was wicked.

Before leaving, he bestowed his treasures upon Mākhan: his fishing-rod,
his large kite, and the smooth stones with which he
played at marbles.

"You may have them," he said with elaborate indifference.

Mākhan accepted this inheritance without being softened by it.

The boat departed in the pale morning. Phatik watched the
village recede—the bamboo clumps, the bathing steps, the meadow shining
with dew, and the crooked roof of his mother's house.
For a moment he wished she would call him back.
But she stood upon the bank, shading her eyes, and
the river widened between them.

Calcutta did not receive him with the welcome he had
expected.

His aunt had not been consulted. She already had three
boys, a narrow household, and an exact arrangement of duties.
The arrival of a country lad of fourteen appeared to
her neither a blessing nor a necessity.

"Why have you brought him?" she asked her husband when
Phatik was close enough to hear.

"He will live with us and go to school."

"As if three boys were not sufficient!"

Bishamber laughed and attempted to pass the matter lightly. But
there are remarks which, though spoken only once, remain permanently
in a room. Phatik understood that he was an addition
to the furniture for which no space had been provided.

At fourteen a boy is an awkward article in any
household. He is neither ornamental nor useful. He has outgrown
the charms of childhood without acquiring the confidence of youth.
If he joins the little children, he is called foolish;
if he listens to the elders, he is called impertinent.
His limbs increase beyond his knowledge of them; his voice
betrays him; his appetite is regarded as greed, his silence
as sullenness, and his attempts at affection as clumsiness. Yet
at no other age is kindness more urgently needed. A
word of praise may feed him for a month; a
word of contempt may become the climate of his whole
existence.

Phatik, who had been a leader in the village, now
became a burden in the city. At home he attempted
to please his aunt. If she asked him to fetch
water, he brought too much; if she told him to
dust a room, he raised a storm in it; if
he offered to help in the kitchen, she cried, "That
will do! Go and study. Do not make more work
for me."

Thus dismissed, he withdrew with the humility of a stray
dog driven from a doorstep.

His cousins were not cruel by intention. They merely possessed
the unconscious cruelty of children who know that an adult
shares their contempt. They laughed at his village accent, his
rough clothes, and his ignorance of city games. When visitors
came, they were neatly dressed and presented with pride. Phatik
remained in the background, large-footed and uncomfortable, feeling that even
his hands were an offence.

School was worse.

There he could understand nothing. Letters marched before him like
soldiers belonging to an enemy nation. When the teacher questioned
him, he stood silent.

"Have you no brains?" the man demanded.

Phatik lowered his head.

A cane descended across his back. The other boys laughed,
and among them his cousins laughed also, lest anyone suppose
that they claimed him.

Day after day he endured the same disgrace. In the
village he had known every bend of the river and
every season of the fields. He could swim against a
current, climb the tallest guava tree, judge the wind for
a kite, and lead twenty boys into mischief before breakfast.
None of these accomplishments appeared in his schoolbook. Here he
was simply the dullest boy in the class.

Sometimes, from a window in his uncle's house, he saw
distant roofs imprisoned beneath the smoky sky. A small patch
of cloud passed overhead, and at once his heart travelled
home. He saw the open meadow where cattle returned at
evening; he heard the shouts of boys plunging into the
river; he felt the warm mud between his toes. Above
all he remembered his mother.

Memory altered her. He forgot the slaps, the accusations, the
impatient commands. He remembered the touch of her hand when
he had been ill as a child, the way her
sari rustled at night, and the voice in which she
sometimes called him before trouble had hardened it. His longing
for that voice became like the lowing of a calf
at twilight—instinctive, unanswered, and full of pain.

One evening he asked Bishamber, "Uncle, when can I go
home?"

"When the school holidays come," his uncle replied. "Not before."

"When will that be?"

"In a few months."

A few months! To a boy in misery, time does
not pass by the calendar. It stands like a locked
gate.

Not long afterward Phatik lost his schoolbook. Perhaps he left
it in the classroom; perhaps another boy took it; perhaps,
in the confusion of his unhappy days, it vanished as
all neglected things vanish. Without it he could not prepare
his lessons. The teacher beat him more severely than before.

For two days he concealed the loss. On the third
he went to his aunt.

"Aunt, I have lost my book."

She turned upon him. "Of course you have! Must I
buy you a new book every week? What a clumsy,
country lout you are! Do you think your uncle has
money to throw away?"

Phatik said nothing. His face burned, not from the words
alone, but from the certainty that they were true in
her eyes. He was an expense, an intrusion, a useless
mouth. His own mother had been glad to send him
away; his aunt was weary of keeping him; at school
he was beaten because he could not learn. There seemed
no place where his existence was not an inconvenience.

That night he lay awake with a headache. By morning
his skin was hot and his limbs ached. He understood
that fever had come. His first thought was not fear
but shame: illness would cause his aunt still more trouble.

All day rain gathered over the city. Toward evening it
fell in torrents, drumming upon roofs and flooding the lanes.
When the household was occupied, Phatik slipped out.

He had formed no plan beyond the words: I will
go home.

Home lay far beyond streets he did not know, beyond
the stations, beyond the swollen river. But fever has its
own geography. He believed that if he walked steadily enough,
some road must lead him to his mother.

At supper his absence was discovered.

His aunt exclaimed, "Now what mischief has he done?"

Bishamber searched the neighbouring lanes, then sent word to the
police. The rain continued through the night. Lamps shone dimly
in the water; wheels sank into mud; gutters overflowed. No
trace of the boy was found.

Late the next afternoon a police cart stopped before the
house. Two constables lifted Phatik down. He was drenched from
head to foot, plastered with mud, and trembling violently. His
eyes were red with fever.

Bishamber ran out and took him in his arms.

The aunt stood at the doorway. "The whole house has
been upset for him," she said. "Would it not be
better to send him back to his mother?"

Phatik heard her. He began to sob.

"I was going home," he said. "They caught me and
brought me back."

They carried him upstairs and placed him upon a bed.
A doctor came, looked grave, and gave instructions. The fever
rose through the night.

Phatik tossed beneath the sheet. At times he muttered lessons
he could not remember; at times he flinched and whispered,
"Sir, I did not lose it on purpose." Then his
mind wandered to the river. Boatmen were measuring the depth,
and their long cries floated through his delirium.

"By the mark—three! By the mark—four!"

He seemed to be travelling over an unfathomable sea. No
bank was visible, yet somewhere beyond the darkness stood his
mother.

Near dawn he opened his eyes and asked, "Uncle, have
the holidays come?"

Bishamber turned away to hide his face. "Soon, my boy."

"Then I may go home?"

"Yes. You shall go home."

But promises made beside a sickbed must sometimes seek fulfilment
on roads unknown to those who make them.

Bishamber sent an urgent message to his sister. The day
passed. Phatik's breathing grew harsh. He stared continually toward the
door, as if expecting someone who had been delayed.

At last, near evening, hurried feet sounded upon the stairs.
His mother entered with her hair disordered and her clothes
stained by travel. All the resentment she had once felt,
all the relief with which she had let him go,
vanished before the sight of his wasted face.

She threw herself beside him.

"Phatik! My child! My darling!"

He stirred.

"My darling," she cried again. "My Phatik!"

For so long he had waited to hear that forgotten
tenderness that, when it came, it seemed to reach him
from a great distance. He turned his face toward her
voice.

"Mother," he whispered.

"I am here, my darling. I am here."

His eyes rested upon her, peaceful at last. Then he
said, very softly, "Mother, the holidays have come."

And, having received permission, he went home.

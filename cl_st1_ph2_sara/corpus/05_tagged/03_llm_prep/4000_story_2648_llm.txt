### MALITIA VETUS

It was in the consulship of Marcus Tullius Cicero and
Caius Antonius Hybrida, during that disordered autumn when rumours of
Catiline's designs had begun to move secretly through Italy, that
I, Lucius Cæcilius Rufus, quaestor in the province of Hispania
Citerior, first addressed the Council of Pompelo concerning the people
of the northern mountains. I set down the circumstances with
precision, though I cannot affirm that precision avails aught against
the interpretation subsequently forced upon me.

Pompelo, which Pompeius Magnus had enlarged and honoured after his
Sertorian campaigns, was then scarcely a city in the Italian
meaning, but rather an armed market-town set amongst hostile immensities.
Its walls, recently whitened, enclosed a basilica, prætorium, granaries, baths
of modest extent, and dwellings belonging to Roman merchants, native
magistrates and discharged auxiliaries. Beyond the southern gate stretched vineyards
and stony fields; to northward arose the Pyrenæan foothills, fold
upon fold, until their blue ridges mingled with the sky.
Even at noon those elevations possessed a duskiness which no
sunlight wholly dispersed.

The folk inhabiting their deeper valleys were called Vascones by
our geographers, though the townsmen distinguished amongst them numerous clans
whose appellations I could neither pronounce nor reliably transcribe. Of
certain tribes nothing was known save through traders who descended
twice yearly with salt, black wool, honey, medicinal roots and
small images carved from a greasy green stone. They acknowledged
neither Roman law nor any recognised Iberian divinity. Their priests—if
priests they were—wore masks of horn and hide, and their
rites occurred at the spring and autumn equinoxes, upon nights
which the townspeople, borrowing some obscure eastern term, named Sabbaths.

At each Sabbath persons disappeared.

This fact was not entered in the municipal fasti, and
the ædiles habitually denied it; nevertheless, in the five years
preceding my quaestorship, twenty-seven shepherds, woodcutters, muleteers and children had
vanished from hamlets within a day's journey of Pompelo. No
corpse was recovered. Dogs refused the trails. Once, after the
autumn observance, a sandal belonging to a missing girl was
discovered upon the roof of the temple of Jupiter—though neither
ladder nor adjacent building offered means by which it might
have been placed there.

I had studied forbidden antiquities whilst serving under Lentulus in
Syria, and had heard from an Egyptian grammarian at Antioch
of ceremonies older than the gods of Greece. In Etruria,
moreover, the haruspex Vibenna had shown me a worm-eaten tablet
on which winged beings were represented receiving human offerings beneath
three black stars. I therefore regarded the mountain rumours not
as rustic foolishness, but as distorted survivals of that malitia
vetus—Ancient Evil—which civilised peoples conceal under gentler names.

The immediate occasion of my appeal was a riot in
the western market. Six hill traders had entered Pompelo beneath
the provincial peace, bringing amber-coloured resin and knives of curious
bronze. A butcher accused one of stealing a purse. Whether
the accusation was true I never learned. A crowd gathered—Romans,
Celtiberians, slaves, mule-drivers, veterans and idlers—and before the watch arrived,
four strangers had been beaten to death. A fifth died
in custody. The sixth, though his skull was broken and
one eye destroyed, escaped during the night.

Three evenings later the drums began.

They sounded beyond the vineyards after sunset: slow, hollow pulsations
too deep to proceed, as the legionaries asserted, from any
ordinary instrument. Sometimes they appeared far off amongst the highest
ridges; sometimes beneath the very walls. Yet the sentries saw
no movement. On the seventh night every dog in Pompelo
crept whining into concealment, and the bronze lamps in the
shrine of Vesta extinguished themselves simultaneously.

More alarming was the absence of the customary disappearances. No
shepherd was taken, no lonely household molested. The hill villages
stood empty, their inhabitants having withdrawn into fortified places before
the equinox. Thus I concluded—not without reluctance—that the old folk
no longer sought a few victims. The town itself had
been marked.

I laid these matters before the proconsul, Publius Scribonius Libo,
in the upper chamber of the prætorium. Scribonius was a
grave, bald man of sixty years, fatigued by arthritis and
public service, yet retaining those steady grey eyes which had
faced Mithridatic archers. With him were Aulus Balbutius, legate commanding
the garrison; Decimus Asellius Varro, a young military tribune of
patrician pretensions; the chief ædile, Marcellus Arsa; and two native
councillors who perspired visibly despite the coolness of the room.

Balbutius heard me with compressed lips.

"You propose," said he, "that I disperse the garrison into
unknown hills because peasants beat drums?"

"I propose," I answered, "that the authority of the Roman
People be displayed before those peasants perform what they intend."

"A cohort cannot arrest an echo."

"Nor can an echo place a child's sandal upon a
temple roof."

At this Asellius laughed. He was handsome after the hard
Roman fashion, with a hawk face, polished cuirass and that
superior sneer which youth mistakes for reason. "Our quaestor has
read too many Syrian books. Shall we summon augurs to
prosecute the wind?"

I replied that Roman law imposed protection upon tributary communities;
that murdered traders, however barbarous, had entered beneath our guarantee;
that negligence might transform private vengeance into rebellion; and, finally,
that the autumn Sabbath would occur the following night. My
argument was forensic and, I confess, forceful. I cited the
suppression of the Bacchanalia, the grove of Diana Aricina, certain
proceedings against Phrygian priests, and a senatorial precedent from the
year of Lepidus. In seeking to show that superstition may
become sedition, I neglected the darker possibility—that sedition may be
merely the outward garment of superstition.

Scribonius listened until the drums commenced again, though the sun
had not yet set.

The sound entered the chamber through its western shutters. At
the first beat, one of the native councillors crossed himself
in a local manner; at the second, a cup trembled
upon the table; at the third, the proconsul raised his
head.

"One cohort," he said. "Under Balbutius. We ascend, extinguish their
fires, seize such leaders as may be found, and return
before dawn."

Balbutius protested conscientiously, citing terrain, darkness and deficient maps. Scribonius
overruled him. As quaestor and principal advocate of the measure,
I volunteered to accompany the force. Asellius, unwilling to appear
timid, did likewise. The ædile supplied a guide named Larta,
a Christianised mountaineer who had traded amongst the remote clans
and professed knowledge of their paths.

We departed through the northern gate at the tenth hour
on the day before the equinox: four hundred and eighty
legionaries, twenty archers, eight engineers, officers, servants and a small
cavalry detachment. The sunset lay scarlet and gold upon Pompelo,
touching its walls with so strange a splendour that I
turned repeatedly in the saddle to behold it. Every roof
and tower appeared transfigured, as though the town were not
built of plaster, tile and timber, but suspended within some
luminous medium between the familiar earth and an approaching realm.

No citizen cheered us. The streets had been crowded, yet
silence followed the cohort to the gate.

The first part of our ascent was easy. We traversed
cultivated slopes, passed abandoned huts, and entered a forest of
oak and pine. Here the road became a track bordered
by ravines. Balbutius extended the column and sent scouts ahead,
though none travelled willingly beyond sight of the standards. Larta
rode beside me, muttering prayers in Latin, Greek and his
native tongue. He had been loquacious in the town; amongst
the trees he would answer no question concerning our destination.

Dusk accumulated beneath the branches before it appeared in the
sky. Presently we perceived fires on a distant height—seven red
points arranged, as I thought, in a broken circle. The
drums sounded from that direction, no longer slow, but in
groups of nine beats separated by intervals of absolute stillness.

At the first such interval our horses stopped.

They did not shy or rear. Every animal halted as
if a single command had reached them, and stood trembling,
their nostrils white with foam. Then the proconsul's mare screamed.

I insist upon the word. She screamed, not neighed, but
screamed with the high and desolate voice of a woman
beneath torture. The other horses answered. Several tore free, plunged
amongst the ranks and threw the column into confusion. One
cavalryman was dashed against a tree; another vanished over the
edge of the path, and for a long while we
heard his armour striking rocks below.

Discipline was restored, though imperfectly. We continued on foot, leading
the surviving animals. The night had become unnaturally cold. It
was not the clean cold of altitude, but an intimate
and searching chill which seemed to enter through wool, leather
and flesh, pressing icy fingers along the bones. Breath steamed
before us. Frost whitened leaves that had been green moments
earlier.

Larta stopped beneath a lightning-blasted oak.

"No higher," he whispered.

Balbutius ordered him forward.

"No higher. They have opened the door."

"What door?" I demanded.

The guide looked upward. His remaining expression was not fear
as men commonly know it, but a total collapse of
the mind before something already perceived. He drew the small
utility knife from his belt. Asellius moved to restrain him;
yet with astonishing swiftness Larta thrust the blade beneath his
own jaw and tore it sideways. He fell without a
cry, blood steaming blackly upon the frost.

The drums resumed.

Balbutius wished then to withdraw. Even Asellius had lost his
sneer. But the path behind us was obstructed by maddened
horses, and Scribonius, whose authority had once made hesitation impossible,
stared at the seven fires above.

"They expect retreat," he said. "Forward."

We climbed.

The trees thinned near the summit. The moon, which had
risen pale and nearly full, appeared to tremble in a
vapourless sky. Our torches burned low, though the air moved
scarcely at all. Through gaps in the branches I saw
the altar fires grow larger and redder. Around them moved
tall figures with antlered heads. Whether these were masked men
I could not determine, for their limbs bent with a
looseness repugnant to human anatomy.

Then one of the archers pointed at the stars.

Something enormous passed overhead.

We heard no cry, only a ponderous beating, as of
sails shaken by a storm. A constellation disappeared; then another.
Colossal forms wheeled above the ridge, blotting out patches of
heaven. Their outlines suggested neither bird nor bat, though at
moments I fancied I saw membranous wings and bodies trailing
long, divided appendages. One descended behind the fires. The mountain
shook at its alighting.

The legionaries closed ranks by instinct. Shields rose. Pila angled
towards the darkness. Balbutius shouted the customary orders, and for
one proud instant the cohort became again an instrument of
Rome—measured, armoured, obedient.

A wind came down the path.

It did not blow. It advanced.

Leaves lifted before it without stirring sideways. Torches died in
sequence, beginning at the rear of the column, each extinction
accompanied by a short, choking cry. The thing moved with
sentience and deliberateness, coiling amongst centuries as a serpent coils
amongst reeds. Where it passed, men dropped their shields and
clutched their throats. Others were raised bodily, turned in the
air and borne silently into the forest.

"Form orbem!" Balbutius cried. "Form circle!"

The order travelled only a few paces. Darkness divided rank
from rank, officer from soldier, man from man. The red
fires remained visible above us, and against them appeared shapes
which no nomenclature of natural history could contain: narrow giants
crowned with horns; crouching masses that opened vertically; white, many-jointed
things running upon the undersides of branches. Nameless beasts descended
amongst the standards.

The screaming began.

It spread through the cohort more swiftly than flame through
summer grass. These were veterans of Sertorius, men who had
watched comrades crucified, disembowelled or burned; yet they shrieked like
children in an unlighted tomb. Steel struck unseen bodies and
rang as against immense stones. A standard rose suddenly, carrying
its bearer with it, and vanished upward. Something wet fell
across my face.

Asellius seized my arm. His mouth opened, but no voice
emerged. Behind him a black loop, thick as a ship's
hawser, lowered from the air. It encircled his waist with
the dreadful gentleness of a lover's hand. His polished cuirass
folded inward. I remembered Laocoön; then the young tribune was
gone.

The moon went out.

One by one, the remaining stars were blotted away, until
the heavens possessed neither depth nor mercy. Only the fires
burned, and before them the antlered celebrants bowed—not to us,
but to that which had arrived behind us.

Scribonius stood at my side. His bald head was bare,
his sword broken. The weariness had left his face, and
in its place was recognition.

"Not rebellion," he said.

"No."

"Not gods?"

"No gods known to men."

The cold wind wrapped itself around the cohort. Shields, helmets,
standards, bodies—all were drawn together in its invisible constriction. The
mountain seemed to inhale. Beneath the drumming I heard another
sound, immeasurably deep: the grinding of some vast gate opened
after ages.

Scribonius looked once towards the south, where Pompelo lay beyond
the ridges.

"Malitia vetus," he whispered.

Then the fires rushed towards us.

I awoke in my library at Cambridge, shortly before dawn
on the twenty-third of September, with those words upon my
lips and my hands bleeding where the fingernails had pierced
them. Rain touched the windows. The electric lamp stood lighted
beside my chair, and upon my knees lay a volume
of the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, open at a damaged record
from ancient Pompelo.

I have since examined every accessible source concerning the administration
of Hispania Citerior in Cicero's consulship. No Lucius Cæcilius Rufus
is listed as quaestor there; no proconsul named Scribonius Libo
can be satisfactorily identified; and no chronicle records the loss
of a cohort in the northern mountains. Certain gaps exist,
as they commonly do. A fragmentary inscription mentions repairs to
the Pompelo garrison after an unspecified calamitas autumnalis, but its
date is disputed.

Pompelo itself survived. It became Pompaelo, then Pamplona; Goth, Moor,
Frank and Spaniard passed beneath its walls, and no ancient
people descended to erase it. This endurance has been offered
to me as proof that my dream was no more
than an antiquarian nightmare, compounded of late reading and imperfect
health.

Perhaps so.

Yet I am unable to forget that no villager vanished
during that Terrible Season; nor can I reject the possibility
that the vengeance intended for a town was satisfied by
those who climbed, under Roman standards, to meet it.

Even now, upon equinoctial nights, I sometimes awaken before the
final star is extinguished.

And from very far off—from Spain, from history, or from
some black elevation above both—I hear the drums.

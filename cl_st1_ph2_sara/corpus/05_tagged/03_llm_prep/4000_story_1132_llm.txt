When Miss Emily Grierson died, our town discovered that a
house may keep time differently from the clocks outside it.
For nearly fifty years the courthouse bell had divided our
days, the freight trains had entered Jefferson shrieking through the
bottoms, the cotton gin had raised its silver dust beyond
the creek, and automobiles had multiplied in the square until
hitching posts seemed relics of an older and more ceremonious
species. Yet behind the leaning cedars on Grierson Street, time
had settled instead of passing. It lay upon the roof
and galleries, accumulated in the curtains, and pressed its gray
palm against the windows, so that when Miss Emily's Negro
servant opened the front door on the morning of her
death, it appeared that the whole long-imprisoned past came breathing
out behind him.

The women went to see the inside of the house.
The men went from respect, or from a wish to
examine the final monument of a family whose name had
once commanded credit, deference, and silence. We said we went
to bury Miss Emily, and this was true; but truth
in Jefferson was seldom solitary. It carried rumor beneath its
veil.

Her house had been built when Grierson Street was shaded
by carriages and lined with gardens. It had turrets, balconies
of curving iron, and wooden ornaments under the eaves like
frozen lace. In old photographs it looked almost buoyant, a
pale extravagance above the magnolias. By the time of her
death, garages and gasoline pumps hemmed it in on two
sides, while behind it a brick warehouse presented a wall
as blank as civic progress. The paint had scaled away.
One balcony sagged. A broken weathercock turned only in violent
storms. Yet the house retained a kind of ruined vanity,
like an old woman wearing a stained satin gown because
no one had dared tell her the ball was over.

The servant admitted the first ladies without speaking. He had
grown bent and colorless in Miss Emily's service, though he
had been a young man when he entered it. His
name was Tobe, but for years most of us had
called him merely "the old man," as if employment in
that house had erased even the small distinction of a
name. He took the visitors' cards on a tarnished tray,
carried food from the market, and answered questions with his
eyes lowered. What he knew was never offered, and what
we imagined he knew became, in our telling, larger than
knowledge.

On the morning of the funeral he opened the door,
crossed the hall, and went out through the back. Someone
saw him pass beneath the chinaberry trees without hat or
bundle. He did not look toward the kitchen, the stable,
or the upstairs windows. He walked through the gate and
was not seen in Jefferson again.

That departure caused less surprise than it should have. Miss
Emily's death had released us into the house, and curiosity,
once granted the dignity of mourning, is an energetic thing.

The rooms downstairs smelled of dust, camphor, and shut flowers.
The parlor furniture was heavy and dark, its upholstery faded
to the color of old bruises. A portrait of Miss
Emily's father stood above the fireplace, one hand thrust into
his coat, the other resting upon the back of a
chair that seemed too delicate to support him. The gilt
frame had blackened at the corners. When sunlight entered through
the slats, it did not brighten the room; it merely
gave the dust a place to reveal itself.

We had seen that parlor before, though not for many
years. The last official visitors had been members of the
Board of Aldermen, sent to discuss taxes.

This was perhaps eight years before Miss Emily died, when
a younger generation had taken charge of municipal affairs. They
believed records should mean what they said and streets should
bear numbers, and they possessed that confidence peculiar to men
who suppose disorder begins only where their memory does. The
books showed that the Grierson property owed taxes. The older
men explained that Colonel Sartoris, during his term as mayor,
had excused Miss Emily from payment after her father's death.
No document said so plainly. Colonel Sartoris had preferred arrangements
that depended upon honor, because honor left no paper to
contradict him.

Notices were mailed. They came back unanswered. A formal letter
requested Miss Emily's appearance at the courthouse. She replied on
thick, yellow-edged stationery that she no longer went abroad. At
last the aldermen appointed a committee and called upon her.

She received them in the parlor, dressed in black, a
small gold chain descending from her waist and vanishing beneath
her belt. She had become thick through the body, and
her face, swollen and pale, seemed molded from wax that
had been left too near heat. Her eyes were dark
points in that pallor, moving from one visitor to another
without haste. Behind her, the portrait of her father made
the young officials appear temporary.

They explained the assessment.

"I owe the town nothing," she said.

The chairman cleared his throat. "The records indicate—"

"You may consult Colonel Sartoris."

"Miss Grierson, Colonel Sartoris has been dead almost ten years."

She looked at him as if death were an administrative
mistake which he, by mentioning it, had made vulgar.

"Consult Colonel Sartoris," she repeated.

They tried once more, invoking statutes, dates, and the equal
obligation of citizens. She rang a little bell. Tobe appeared.

"Show these gentlemen out."

That was the whole interview. The taxes remained unpaid, though
not, we think, forgiven. Jefferson simply placed the debt among
those matters it intended to settle later and therefore never
settled at all.

Our town had practice in avoiding direct contests with Miss
Emily. Thirty years earlier, when the smell began, the Board
of Aldermen had behaved with even greater delicacy.

At first it was no more than a complaint from
a neighbor, a nervous woman who came to the mayor's
office after dark. Another complaint followed. Then customers at the
drugstore mentioned that the air near the Grierson place had
become unpleasant. It was summer, and heat enlarged every odor.
The mayor, Judge Stevens, was over eighty and belonged to
the generation for whom a lady's misfortune could not be
named if naming it might embarrass her.

"Tell her to clean the place," a young alderman said.

Judge Stevens stared at him. "Will you accuse a Grierson
woman of stinking?"

No one would.

Four men crossed the lawn after midnight carrying sacks of
lime. They moved along the foundation, around the cellar door,
and beneath the kitchen windows. One of them climbed over
the fence to scatter lime among the weeds near the
smokehouse. As they returned across the grass, they saw a
light in an upstairs window. Miss Emily sat behind it,
motionless, her upper body dark against the lamp.

The men lowered their voices, though the glass was closed
and the room above them silent.

Within two weeks the smell diminished. Soon afterward we ceased
speaking of it openly. We congratulated ourselves on having preserved
Miss Emily's dignity. It did not occur to us that
dignity might be only the clean cloth laid over something
we had no courage to uncover.

The odor had appeared not long after Homer Barron disappeared.
But at that time we connected it with drains, rats,
spoiled meat, or Tobe's carelessness; we connected it with anything
ordinary, because the extraordinary, though eagerly discussed, is rarely believed
until it becomes old enough to be respectable.

Miss Emily had first come down among us after her
father died. Before that she was more emblem than woman.
We remembered seeing her in the doorway or behind the
high wheels of a carriage: a slender figure in white,
with Mr. Grierson close by, upright and broad-shouldered, holding his
horsewhip even when there was no horse to punish. There
had been suitors. We knew this because we saw them
arrive, and later saw them cease arriving. Mr. Grierson found
fault with each. One lacked property, one lacked family, another
possessed money earned too recently, and one was said to
laugh loudly in church. Miss Emily stood in the background
while her father dismissed them, until youth withdrew from her
face as quietly as water sinking into earth.

When he died, she denied it.

The doctor had attended him, and two relatives were notified.
Ladies called with food and condolences. Miss Emily met them
at the door dressed as usual, without mourning, and told
them her father was not dead. For three days she
refused the undertaker. Ministers came. The mayor came. A cousin
threatened legal action, though from a distance. At last, perhaps
because the body itself had begun to speak with an
authority greater than hers, she allowed it to be removed.

We said then that grief had unbalanced her. We said
it kindly, because the fall of a proud family gives
ordinary people an opportunity to practice mercy without sacrifice. Her
father had left her the house and little else. Poverty
made her human to us. She was no longer the
Grierson daughter above the town but a woman alone in
a large building she could not maintain.

For many months she was ill. When next she appeared,
her hair was cut short and framed her face with
an almost childish severity. The town had begun paving the
sidewalks, and the work brought Homer Barron to Jefferson.

He was a foreman for the Northern company: tall, loud,
sunburned, with a broad mouth and a voice that carried
across machinery. He knew every laborer by name, and when
he cursed them he did it cheerfully, as if profanity
were another tool issued by the company. At noon his
laughter could be heard above the picks and stone drills.
Children followed him. Men drank with him. Women who disapproved
of him found reasons to pass the work crew.

Soon he and Miss Emily were seen on Sunday afternoons
in a yellow-wheeled buggy from the livery stable. Homer sat
with his hat tilted back, reins in one hand, cigar
in the other. Miss Emily sat erect beside him, gloved
even in warm weather, her face turned forward.

At first we were pleased. We believed companionship might restore
her. Then we remembered he was a Northerner, a day
laborer's overseer, and no equal of the Grierson name. The
older ladies said her father would have prevented it. Younger
people remarked that her father had prevented enough. On porches
and in stores, we arranged her future according to our
separate needs: some wished to see pride humbled; others wanted
romance; still others hoped for disaster, though they would have
called that hope concern.

Homer himself said he was not a marrying man. This
was repeated throughout Jefferson with additions. He preferred the company
of men, someone said. He liked saloons and card games.
He would leave when the paving contract ended. Therefore, when
Miss Emily rode with him, the ladies lowered their voices
and declared it a shame.

A minister was sent to speak with her. What occurred
in the house he never disclosed, but his face was
gray when he emerged, and he refused to return. His
wife then wrote to Miss Emily's relations in Alabama. Two
female cousins arrived, stiff-backed women in traveling black, carrying trunks
and disapproval.

During their visit Miss Emily entered the drugstore.

The pharmacist came forward. She had grown thinner since her
illness, but carried herself with a hard composure that made
the counter seem an intrusion between equals.

"I require poison," she said.

He asked what kind.

"The most certain kind."

He explained that the law required her to state its
purpose. He spoke of preparations for vermin and of substances
used for weeds. She looked at him without replying.

"Arsenic will do," she said.

He hesitated. "For rats?"

She continued to look at him.

The pharmacist was a man who boasted that no customer
could hurry him, yet presently he went to the rear
room himself. The package was delivered to Miss Emily's house
that evening. On the box his clerk had written, in
a careful hand: FOR VERMIN.

When we heard of the purchase, we said she would
kill herself. This conclusion pleased some of us by its
tragic fitness. The ladies who had condemned the courtship now
said suicide might be preferable to abandonment. We watched the
house. We watched the buggy. We watched Homer.

Then Miss Emily bought a silver toilet set for a
man, with the initials H.B. engraved on each piece. She
ordered a dark suit, a nightshirt, and slippers in Homer's
size. Our judgment changed at once.

"They are married," we said.

"They will be married," others corrected.

The cousins departed. Three days later Homer returned to town.
A neighbor saw Tobe admit him through the kitchen door
at dusk.

That was the last time anyone outside the house saw
Homer Barron.

For a while we believed he had gone ahead to
prepare a home. Then we believed he had abandoned her.
When the paving company finished its contract and departed, his
name faded from conversation. Miss Emily did not appear in
public. The smell began and was dealt with. The upstairs
windows remained shuttered.

Years passed. Her body thickened, and her hair changed gradually
from brown to gray, then to the coarse iron color
it kept until death. For six or seven years she
taught china painting to the daughters and granddaughters of families
who had known her father. Girls entered the house carrying
brushes and undecorated plates and emerged with pale roses, blue
birds, and gold-rimmed cups. In that dim parlor, beneath Mr.
Grierson's portrait, Miss Emily instructed them in stems and petals
while dust sifted through the window light.

Then fashion changed. No one wanted painted china. The last
pupils grew up, married, and sent their children to schools
where they learned practical arts. Miss Emily closed the front
door.

Only Tobe went in and out, his market basket revealing
more of the household than either occupant did. Flour, coffee,
lamp oil, a little meat. In later years, medicines. He
aged as the house aged. We sometimes saw him pausing
at the gate as though listening to a voice from
deep underground.

The town installed free postal delivery. Numbers were fixed to
doors, boxes mounted at gates. Miss Emily refused both number
and box. The postman carried no letters to her except
tax notices, and those returned unopened. Electricity spread through the
streets, but the Grierson house remained dim. Telephone wires crossed
above the garden, yet none entered it. At night its
windows were black among illuminated shops and filling stations, a
square of the old darkness the town had failed to
remove.

Thus she became an inheritance while still alive. Children were
told who she was. Visitors were shown the house. People
born after Homer's disappearance knew the story in fragments, though
each generation altered it. To the oldest she was poor
Emily, victim of her father. To the middle-aged she was
a scandal that had hardened into custom. To the young
she was simply the woman in the dead house who
would not pay taxes.

Once a new deputy delivered a notice by hand. Miss
Emily met him at the door and said, "There is
no debt here."

He began to explain.

"There is no debt here," she repeated, and shut the
door.

We laughed when he told it, but uneasily. She had
outlived every authority that might have compelled her. The colonel
was dead, the old aldermen were dead, the pharmacist was
dead, the minister was dead. Even the cousins in Alabama
had become names in funeral columns. Miss Emily remained, not
untouched by time but incorporated into it, as a tree
may swallow a fence and show only a line of
rust through its bark.

In the final winter Tobe summoned the doctor. No announcement
was made. Curtains stayed closed. For several days women carried
dishes to the back door, though no dishes came back.
Then the bell at the Grierson gate was tied with
black crepe.

Miss Emily died in a downstairs room, in a heavy
walnut bed whose curtains smelled of mildew. She was seventy-four.
Her face had diminished upon the pillow, but the hands
folded over the coverlet were broad and stubborn, the nails
blunt as old ivory.

Her relatives arrived from Alabama. The two cousins were dead,
and younger kin came in their place, people who spoke
of Miss Emily with the confidence of blood and the
ignorance of distance. They opened drawers, consulted lawyers, and objected
to the flowers sent by the town until reminded that
the town had paid for them. We permitted the relatives
their claims because death, like marriage, enlarges the importance of
family.

The funeral was held in the house. Veterans in faded
uniforms sat on the porch, recalling Miss Emily as if
they had danced with her when she was young, though
some were scarcely older than she had been. They spoke
of the past not as a road behind them but
as a wide field where all the dead remained visible,
standing at whatever age memory preferred. Ladies whispered in the
parlor. The men removed their hats beneath Mr. Grierson's portrait.
Flowers crowded the coffin, their sweetness struggling against the house's
colder breath.

We buried her beside her father and beneath the stone
names of Griersons who had once governed county boards, commanded
militia companies, and owned land from the river to the
hills.

Only after she was properly in the ground did anyone
mention the locked room upstairs.

We had known of it for years, of course—or believed
we had. There was a door at the end of
the upper hall which no servant had been seen to
enter. The key had disappeared. Some said it contained Mr.
Grierson's papers. Some said old furniture. One woman remembered a
bridal veil delivered long ago; another denied this. By dusk,
our uncertainty had matured into conviction. The relatives agreed to
open the room.

The lock resisted. A carpenter was summoned. He drove a
chisel between door and frame while we stood behind him
in the hall, the women lifting handkerchiefs to their mouths
against the dust. At the third blow the wood split
inward with a sound like a dry bone breaking.

For a moment nothing could be seen. The shutters were
fastened, and the room held darkness densely, as if it
were not an absence of light but a substance long
stored. When the carpenter forced a shutter apart, a narrow
blade of evening entered. Dust rose from every surface, slow
and thick, turning in the ray.

It was a bedroom prepared for marriage.

Faded rose curtains hung at the windows. A rose-colored shade
covered the lamp. Upon a dressing table stood the silver
set marked H.B., so tarnished that the initials appeared sunk
beneath black water. A man's collar lay beside it. On
a chair rested a suit, folded with careful precision. The
slippers waited beneath the chair.

The bed occupied the center of the room. Its canopy
had once been pink. Time had drained it to the
hue of flesh kept from sunlight.

Homer Barron lay upon the bed.

What remained of him had collapsed within the nightshirt. The
body, held so long in stillness, had taken the attitude
of an interrupted embrace: one arm bent, the head turned
slightly toward the other pillow. The grin of the skull
was exposed beneath remnants of dried skin. Dust clothed him
more faithfully than the blanket.

No one spoke. All the old pieces—arsenic, odor, disappearance, lime—came
together without requiring explanation. We stood before the answer we
had spent decades refusing to ask.

Then one of the women noticed the second pillow.

It bore the hollow impression of a head.

Someone leaned closer and lifted from the indentation a single
long strand of hair, coarse and iron-gray.

We understood then that Miss Emily had not surrendered Homer
to departure, just as she had not surrendered her father
to death, the house to the town, or herself to
time. She had kept what would leave her by making
departure impossible, and afterward had lain beside it through summers
of dust and winters of damp, while Jefferson paved its
streets, numbered its doors, collected its taxes, and congratulated itself
upon becoming modern.

We left the room quietly. Outside, evening had covered the
garages and gin roofs, the courthouse dome, the new electric
signs, and the old Grierson cedars with one impartial darkness.
Behind us the opened window admitted air to the chamber
for the first time in forty years, stirring the ruined
curtains and troubling the dust upon the bridal bed.

By morning the strand of hair was gone. None of
us admitted taking it. Yet for many years afterward, whenever
the Grierson house was mentioned, we described that hair with
perfect certainty—its length, its iron color, the way it had
curved upon the yellow pillow—as though each of us had
held it privately between finger and thumb.

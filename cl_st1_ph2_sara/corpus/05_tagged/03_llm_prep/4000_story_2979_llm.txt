### The Homeward Journey of Gebhard Knopfschrank

No one at the Café Byzantium knew precisely when Gebhard
Knopfschrank had become an artist. There were persons who maintained
that he had studied in Munich, others who assigned him
a formative winter in Paris, and one young woman, who
embroidered Bulgarian proverbs on her gloves, was certain that he
had once worked among the charcoal-burners of Montenegro. Gebhard himself
admitted only to having been born on a farm in
Pomerania, where his father raised pigs, beetroot, and an annual
grievance against the railway authorities.

The Café Byzantium stood in a Soho street which had
changed its name twice without improving its character. It was
a narrow establishment, smelling agreeably of coffee, garlic, damp overcoats,
and unpaid bills. Its proprietor, Mr. Vespucci, was neither Greek
nor Italian, but had found those nationalities more useful than
his native one. He served food of uncertain origin beneath
reproductions of famous pictures, and allowed his customers to remain
for long periods over a single cup, provided they talked
loudly enough to suggest that history was being made.

The Byzantium's clients described themselves as Bohemians. Most of them
possessed private incomes, respectable relations in Surrey, and an invincible
horror of travelling second class. They included poets whose verses
had appeared in magazines conducted by their friends, actresses who
had nearly been engaged by managers who had forgotten them,
painters known chiefly for their scarves, and critics who had
discovered several new movements without incurring the expense of buying
a picture belonging to any of them.

Among these people Gebhard enjoyed an equivocal distinction. He might
be a genius; alternatively, he might be a Pomeranian farm
boy with defective eyesight. The two possibilities were discussed continually
and with equal caution.

His paintings represented London after the disappearance of mankind. Trafalgar
Square appeared as a salt waste across which three giraffes
moved towards Whitehall; vultures nested upon the dome of St.
Paul's; hyenas quarrelled over a bowler hat in Threadneedle Street;
and a melancholy rhinoceros stood knee-deep in the ornamental water
of the Serpentine. Gebhard's largest work, which he called *The
Silence after Empire*, showed Piccadilly Circus buried under reddish sand,
with sand-grouse rising above the statue of Eros and a
family of jackals occupying the entrance to a well-known restaurant.

"It is either prophetic or merely zoological," said Ronald Bevis,
who wrote art criticism for a weekly devoted principally to
motoring.

"The absence of human figures is significant," observed Miss Tansy
Morrow, whose own drawings consisted almost entirely of human figures
without clothes. "One feels that humanity has been judged."

"One also feels," said Claude Harpenden, "that the giraffes have
been drawn from memory."

Claude had never seen a giraffe except in an advertisement
for feeding-bottles, but the objection was considered technically sound.

Gebhard brought a fresh canvas or portfolio to the café
every Thursday evening. Mr. Vespucci permitted him to prop the
larger pictures against the walls, partly because they concealed the
more damaged portions of the wallpaper and partly because an
artist who paid irregularly was still more decorative than a
customer who did so. The Bohemians gathered before each new
production and delivered verdicts which preserved every avenue of retreat.

"I should not care to say that I like it,"
Ronald would remark, "but liking is an essentially suburban emotion."

"It has power," Tansy would concede, "though perhaps not the
kind of power one would wish in a small room."

Claude usually discovered influences. At various times he detected Assyrian,
Flemish, Coptic, Japanese, and Lower Croydon influences in Gebhard's work.
He never detected anything sufficiently persuasive to make him draw
a cheque.

The prices were not excessive. Gebhard asked four pounds for
a study of flamingoes in Leicester Square, thirty shillings for
a sketch of a python descending the steps of the
Royal Exchange, and ten pounds for a vigorous canvas in
which wild asses had established themselves at the Marble Arch.
Nevertheless, no purchase was made. The Byzantium circle belonged to
that advanced school of connoisseurship which regards a rising reputation
as too speculative and an established one as too expensive.

"If Goring-Hall bought one, of course—" Ronald said frequently.

Sir Hector Goring-Hall was a collector whose acquisitions were accepted
as proof of genius, though his taste in neckties was
held to prove nothing except independence.

Gebhard listened to these judgments with a grave, square face
and pale-blue eyes that seemed accustomed to watching weather travel
across flat country.

"You do not buy," he would say.

"My dear Knopfschrank," Ronald explained, "commercial possession would almost vulgarise
my appreciation."

"I reduce price."

"That," said Ronald, "would make the transaction still more commercial."

Gebhard's circumstances meanwhile became perceptibly worse. His thick Pomeranian boots
grew cracked, his coat acquired a greenish polish, and his
Thursday dinners declined through a measured series of economies. At
first he had eaten goulash, black bread, and beer; then
soup, bread, and coffee; then bread and coffee; and finally
coffee, which Mr. Vespucci made strong enough to sustain the
appearance of nourishment while containing none of its more costly
properties.

The group observed his deterioration with sympathetic interest. Poverty, when
endured by somebody else, was recognised at the Byzantium as
a valuable artistic discipline.

"He is getting the authentic look," Tansy whispered. "There was
always something too agricultural about him."

"Suffering may clarify his line," said Claude.

It did not occur to either of them that suffering
might clarify his appetite.

One wet November night Gebhard confessed that he wished to
return home. His father, he said, had written twice, offering
him work on the farm, but the journey to Pomerania
cost more than he possessed. There were also debts: nine
pounds to Mr. Vespucci, three to a colour merchant in
Fitzroy Street, rent for his room, and a sum owed
to a Polish frame-maker whose patience had become geographically inappropriate.

"I go home if I can pay," Gebhard said. "I
make no more pictures. I understand pigs better. A pig,
when he is good pig, no man says perhaps he
is zebra."

This remark was received as a peasant aphorism of some
profundity.

"You must not abandon your vision," Tansy told him.

"Then you buy him?" Gebhard asked, indicating a small drawing
of pelicans roosting on the Admiralty Arch.

Tansy explained that her room already suffered from overcrowding.

During the next fortnight he failed to appear. His absence
produced concern, though not of the expensive variety. Ronald proposed
that they organise a subscription dinner for him, but the
proposal foundered upon the question whether Gebhard should be expected
to pay his own share. Claude suggested an exhibition, provided
someone else could arrange the premises, printing, refreshments, insurance, invitations,
transport, and guarantee against loss.

Then, on a Thursday in December, Gebhard returned.

The transformation was astonishing. He wore a new brown suit,
a collar of aggressive whiteness, and boots which creaked with
prosperity. He entered the café humming loudly, shook Mr. Vespucci
by both hands, and ordered a dinner which began with
oysters and proceeded through turbot, duck, asparagus, cheese, and a
bottle of champagne. The asparagus, being out of season, cost
enough to convince the room that something momentous had happened.

More remarkable still, Gebhard went repeatedly to the mechanical piano
and selected an American patriotic march. The machine performed it
with the emphatic cheerfulness of brass bands and commercial empires.
When it ended, he paid for it to begin again.

The Byzantium drew its conclusions. An American had bought *The
Silence after Empire*. No other explanation satisfactorily accounted for the
champagne, the new boots, and the Stars and Stripes in
musical form. The great canvas had probably been acquired by
one of those transatlantic millionaires who collected European masterpieces between
railway mergers.

"How splendid!" cried Tansy, moving her chair nearer. "I always
felt that America would understand you."

"You said it was too zoological," Gebhard reminded her.

"My dear man, zoology is the coming symbolism."

Ronald calculated rapidly. If the large picture had fetched two
hundred pounds, the sketches would rise at once; if five
hundred, they would become inaccessible; if a thousand, one might
presently be able to sell a flamingo to Sir Hector
Goring-Hall at a handsome profit and with a short explanatory
note.

He picked up the Leicester Square study.

"This has always seemed to me the germ of your
mature manner. You asked four pounds, I think?"

"Five now," said Gebhard.

Ronald paid five.

The sound of money disturbed the remaining caution of the
company. Claude secured the python at two pounds; Tansy bought
three charcoal jackals for thirty-five shillings; a dramatist named Penge
acquired the wild asses, though he lived with an aunt
who objected even to landscape. Within twenty minutes Gebhard had
sold eleven sketches and one unfinished watercolour of ibex on
Waterloo Bridge.

The buyers congratulated one another upon their courage and Gebhard
upon his recognition.

"May we ask," Ronald said at last, with elaborate indifference,
"whether your great work has gone to New York or
Chicago?"

"My great work?"

"*The Silence after Empire*. We assume your American patron has
purchased it."

"No American buy picture," said Gebhard, cutting a pear.

There was a pause in which the mechanical piano commenced
the patriotic march for the seventh time.

"But you have evidently had good fortune," said Tansy.

"Yes. Rich American gentleman."

A collective breath of relief passed round the table.

"He buys no picture," Gebhard continued. "He drives motor-car in
Pomerania, very fast because he must catch train at Stettin.
He comes round bend by our farm and goes into
my father's pigs."

Mr. Vespucci, who had approached with the bill, remained to
hear the rest.

"Into the pigs?" Claude repeated.

"Eleven pigs. Four killed, three not worth keeping, two have
broken legs, and others very nervous. American gentleman has no
time for police or arguing. He pays my father much
money—more than pigs are worth, my father says, though pigs
was very good pigs. My parents send me enough for
debts and ticket home."

The march ended with a metallic flourish.

"I leave Saturday," Gebhard added. "I pay everybody. I work
farm. No more art."

Ronald looked at the flamingoes for which he had just
paid five pounds. Their legs now seemed less prophetic than
badly attached.

"And the large canvas?" he asked.

"Too big to take. Nobody buy. I burn him tomorrow;
he makes good fire, perhaps."

The Byzantium circle regarded Gebhard with an indignation difficult to
formulate. He had told no falsehood, offered no misleading statement,
and employed no stratagem beyond eating asparagus while an American
tune was played. Nevertheless, they felt themselves cheated. Their purchases,
made in the confident expectation that someone richer had judged
first, had been thrown back upon the perilous foundation of
their own taste.

Gebhard paid his bill, tipped Mr. Vespucci sixpence, and departed
humming.

For several weeks afterwards the sketches remained prominently displayed in
their purchasers' rooms, though each owner privately considered exchanging his
example for something less adventurous. Ronald eventually described the flamingoes
as an early experiment by an obscure Continental primitive. Tansy
maintained that Knopfschrank's rejection of art had itself been his
final masterpiece.

At the Café Byzantium, however, it was generally agreed that
Gebhard had possessed a coarse peasant cunning and no genuine
artistic conscience. The possibility that he had possessed talent was
not wholly dismissed, but it ceased to matter when there
was no longer any likelihood of its becoming valuable.

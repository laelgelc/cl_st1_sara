## Kitty's Skipping Shoes

Kitty was a dear little girl in many respects; she
had bright eyes, rosy cheeks, and a laugh that made
the house seem merry. But she possessed one very disagreeable
habit. Whenever she was asked to do anything, she nearly
always answered, "I can't," or, "I won't."

If mamma said, "Kitty, please carry this spool of cotton
to Nursey," Kitty would reply, "I can't; I am busy."

If papa said, "Run and bring me my spectacles, dear,"
she would pout and say, "I won't; they are too
far away."

The spectacles might be only across the room, and her
important business might be dressing a paper doll; but Kitty
thought her own wishes should always come first. This is
a great mistake, as all obliging children know.

One summer morning, a little parcel came home from the
shoemaker's. In it was a pair of red shoes, with
shining black buttons and small heels that made the pleasantest
tap upon the floor.

Kitty clapped her hands.

"Oh, mamma, are they mine?"

"Yes," said mamma. "They are for midsummer day. Try them
on."

Kitty thrust in one foot and then the other. The
shoes fitted exactly. Indeed, they seemed to hug her toes
in a friendly manner; and when Nursey fastened the last
button, Kitty thought she saw both shoes give a sly
little wink.

"What funny shoes!" she cried.

"They are very pretty," said mamma. "Now take this basket
to Cook, if you please."

"I won't go down to the kit—"

The words were hardly out of her mouth before the
red shoes gave one jump, whisked Kitty through the nursery
door, skipped down the passage, and carried her over the
stairs so rapidly that she had no time to count
them. At the kitchen threshold they stopped with a firm
little stamp.

Cook looked up from her pastry.

"Well, miss, you have come quickly."

"I didn't come," said Kitty indignantly. "The shoes brought me."

Cook laughed, supposing this to be one of Kitty's fancies,
and took the basket.

"Since you are here, carry this bunch of parsley to
your mamma."

"I can't. I am too ti—"

Away went the shoes! They sprang upstairs, trotted along the
passage, and landed Kitty beside mamma's chair, with the parsley
held straight before her like a green bouquet.

"Thank you, dear," said mamma.

Kitty stared at her feet. The black buttons certainly winked
again.

"I don't like you," she whispered.

Tap, tap, answered the heels, as if they did not
care in the least.

After breakfast mamma gave Kitty a letter.

"Please post this on your way to school."

"I shall forget it," said Kitty.

"Put it safely in your pocket."

Kitty did so, but when she reached the garden gate
she saw a yellow butterfly and ran after it. The
butterfly fluttered over the lavender, rested upon a rose, and
sailed across the lawn. Kitty followed until she was far
from the road.

"I knew I should forget that tiresome letter," she said.

Instantly the shoes turned about. They rushed through the gate,
pattered down the lane, and carried Kitty straight to the
post office. She tried to catch at a hedge; but
the twigs only scratched her fingers, while the shoes trotted
faster than ever.

"I won't post it!" she cried, pressing both hands over
her pocket.

The right shoe began to climb the post-office step; the
left gave a determined skip; and Kitty's hand, in trying
to keep her balance, flew from her pocket. Out came
the letter. Before she knew what had happened, it was
through the opening.

"There!" said Kitty. "You are the most meddlesome shoes in
the world."

The heels tapped a merry tune.

They then hurried her toward school, for it was nearly
nine o'clock. Kitty wished to stop at the brook and
sail a leaf under the bridge.

"There is plenty of time," she said, though she knew
there was not.

The shoes refused to turn aside. They flew along the
road, skipped over a muddy place at one jump, and
deposited her at the schoolroom door just as the bell
rang.

During the morning Kitty behaved tolerably well, chiefly because she
feared what the shoes might do. Once she whispered to
Lucy Gray when she should have been attending to her
sums; but the shoes rapped sharply beneath the desk, and
the teacher looked in that direction. Kitty immediately bent over
her slate.

At noon, on her way home, she met a small
boy named Robin building a house of sticks beside the
road. He had made four neat walls, a gateway, and
a little garden marked out with pebbles.

Kitty was cross because she had missed two sums.

"What a stupid house!" she said.

"It is not finished," answered Robin. "The roof is to
be made of moss."

"I can kick it down with one foot."

She lifted her right shoe; but instead of striking the
house, it leaped completely over Robin, while the left followed
so briskly that Kitty turned a somersault upon the grass.

Robin laughed, though he politely tried to hide it.

Kitty rose, red and angry.

"I will do it this time!"

Again she raised her foot, and again the shoe sprang
over the little house. This leap was higher than the
first, and Kitty landed in a patch of soft clover
six yards away.

The shoes would not permit mischief, however much their owner
desired it.

Presently a gust of wind scattered Robin's pile of moss.

"Oh!" he cried. "Now I must fetch it all again."

Kitty was about to say, "Fetch it yourself," but she
remembered her two remarkable leaps.

"I will help you," she said, not very graciously.

The minute she consented, the shoes became wonderfully light. They
carried her after the flying moss in three beautiful bounds—one
over the ditch, one across a bed of nettles, and
one to the foot of an elm. Kitty gathered every
bit, brought it to Robin, and helped him lay a
soft green roof upon his house.

"That was splendid!" said Robin. "You jumped like a grasshopper."

Kitty was pleased. She began to suspect that the shoes
were much more agreeable when she was agreeable herself.

At dinner there was chicken pie, followed by a fine
plum cake. Kitty ate all that was proper; but afterward,
finding herself alone in the dining-room, she saw the cake
still standing upon the sideboard.

"I should like just one more slice," she murmured. "Nobody
will know."

She crept toward it. The shoes crept also, so gently
that she believed they meant to oblige her. She stretched
out her hand.

At that instant both shoes sprang backward. Kitty flew across
the room and sat down suddenly upon the sofa.

She tried again. Once more, just as her fingers approached
the sugary top, the shoes skipped away.

"You hateful things!" she exclaimed. "It is my mamma's cake,
and I am sure she would give me some."

Then you should ask her, the tapping heels seemed to
say.

Kitty went to the piazza, where mamma was sewing.

"May I have another piece of cake?"

"No, dear; you have had enough."

"I thought you would say so," Kitty muttered.

"Will you carry these peas to the kitchen?"

Kitty frowned. "I suppose I must."

The shoes walked, but they moved heavily, dragging at every
step.

Mamma watched her and smiled a little.

"Try saying, 'I shall be glad to do it.'"

"I shall be glad to do it," repeated Kitty, though
at first she did not mean it.

Immediately the shoes pranced down the passage. Kitty could scarcely
help laughing; and when Cook thanked her, she felt so
much happier than she had felt while stealing toward the
cake that her good words became almost true.

In the afternoon Nursey asked her to fetch a little
frock from the clothes-line, for dark clouds were gathering.

"I want to finish my doll's bonnet."

A drop of rain struck the window.

"The frock will be wet," said Nursey.

"I can't go this minu—"

Whisk! The shoes carried Kitty into the garden. They raced
between the first large drops, leaped over the currant bushes,
and stopped under the clothes-line. Kitty snatched down the frock;
then the shoes turned and bounded back to the house
just as the shower poured upon the gravel.

"Safe and dry!" said Nursey. "Thank you, my pet."

Kitty looked at the little frock. It belonged to her
baby sister, and she was glad it had not been
spoiled.

When the rain passed, mamma called Kitty into her room.
Upon the bed lay several parcels: some outgrown clothes, two
books, a loaf of bread, a jar of broth, and
a small purse.

"These are for families who need them," said mamma. "Mrs.
Green is ill, the Miller children have few books, and
old Thomas cannot walk to the village. I had intended
to send John, but he is busy with papa's horse.
Will you carry the parcels?"

Kitty looked at the heap. Mrs. Green lived beyond the
meadow; the Millers lived near the hill; and old Thomas
had a cottage at the edge of the wood.

"Must I?" she asked.

"No," said mamma quietly. "This time you may choose. The
parcels can wait until John is free."

Kitty glanced down at the red shoes. They were perfectly
still. Their buttons did not wink; their heels did not
tap. She could refuse, and no one would whisk her
away.

Then she thought of Mrs. Green lying ill, of the
Miller children with nothing new to read, and of old
Thomas waiting alone in his cottage. She also remembered Robin's
mossy house, the dry little frock, and the pleasant lightness
that came whenever she tried to be useful.

"Yes, mamma," she said. "I will take them, and I
will go now."

Before mamma could kiss her, the shoes danced three times
round the room. They did not drag Kitty or hurry
her against her will; they seemed only to share her
gladness.

First she went to Mrs. Green's cottage with the clothes
and broth. The pale mother thanked her, while three hungry
little children gathered round the loaf like young birds in
a nest.

Then Kitty carried the books to the Millers. The children
shouted with delight over the pictures, and one of them
gave her a bunch of pinks from his tiny garden.

Last of all she visited old Thomas, to whom she
gave the purse and a packet of tea.

"Bless your kind heart," said the old man.

Kitty knew her heart had not always been kind; but
she hoped it was growing better. People commonly feel happy
after doing happy-making things for others, and Kitty's face shone
all the way down the lane.

When all the errands were done, she found herself near
the grove which the village children called Fairyland. It was
a green, cosey place, with ferny arches, moss-covered stones, wood
flowers, and a brook that talked all day over its
pebbles.

"I should like to go in," said Kitty, "if there
is time."

The shoes gave an approving tap and carried her under
the hazel boughs.

No sooner had she entered than she heard a squirrel
chattering overhead.

"Here is the red-footed child," he said. "She helped the
small builder whose house had a moss roof."

Kitty stopped in astonishment.

"Can I understand you?"

"To-day you can," replied the squirrel, flourishing his tail. "Midsummer
makes many strange things plain."

A robin hopped upon a twig.

"She did not steal the cake," he sang.

"I tried to," Kitty confessed.

"But you did not take it," said the robin; "and
resisting the second wrong is better than committing it because
one has begun the first."

A procession of ants crossed the path, each carrying a
crumb or a seed.

"We work willingly," said the foremost ant. "That is why
our burdens seem light."

"You are very small to give lessons," Kitty observed.

"Good lessons do not need long legs," answered the ant,
and marched away.

Beside the brook sat a green frog, dressed in a
shining coat.

"Never kick down another person's house," he croaked. "A house
is important to the creature who owns it, whether it
is built of bricks, sticks, or mud."

"I did not kick Robin's down."

"No; your shoes had more sense than your temper."

This was rather a saucy speech, but Kitty could not
deny it.

Butterflies danced above the foxgloves; bees hummed among the clover
bells; a field mouse peeped from beneath a leaf and
told her where his babies slept. Kitty walked carefully, lest
she tread upon any of the innocent and interesting little
creatures whose voices had suddenly become as plain as her
own.

At last the woodland musicians began a midsummer dance. The
thrush sang, the brook tinkled, the woodpecker beat time, and
the grasshoppers played their tiny fiddles.

The red shoes tapped once, twice, three times.

Kitty lifted her arms, and away she went—waltzing over the
moss, turning beneath the ferns, and springing over stones as
lightly as an india-rubber ball. Never had she danced so
gracefully. The squirrels clapped their paws; the birds sang louder;
even the solemn frog cried, "Bravo!"

Then the sun dropped toward the hills, red and round,
like the sleepy eye of a giant preparing to wink.

At the last golden ray, the music softened. The voices
of the creatures became chirps, croaks, rustles, and songs once
more. Kitty's shoes gave a final gentle tap and were
only pretty red shoes again.

She walked home in the dusk, carrying her bunch of
pinks.

The next morning, when mamma asked her to help Nursey,
Kitty nearly said, "I can't." But she looked at the
silent shoes and remembered.

"I will," she said instead.

No magic whisked her away; she went by herself, which
was better.

Kitty did not become perfect in a day, for children's
habits do not vanish as quickly as fairy enchantments. Yet
she grew more obedient, useful, and kind. She learned to
dance beautifully, because cheerful feet soon learn graceful ways; and
she never again wished to injure another child's work.

All her life she loved the birds, beasts, insects, and
quiet creatures of the woods and fields. She could no
longer understand their words, but she had learned to understand
their needs.

And though the red shoes never winked again, Kitty sometimes
fancied, when she ran pleasantly upon an errand, that their
little heels tapped out this tune:

"Willing feet go light and fast;
Kindly deeds bring joy at last."

# The Demon's Eye

You have heard him called Lucky Tom, no doubt. Every
man from Kimberley to Cape Town knew the sobriquet twenty
years ago, and there are fellows on the Rand who
use it still, though they never set eyes upon Tom
Donahue in the flesh. They imagine, I believe, that he
earned it at cards, or by finding a reef where
wiser men had passed it over, or perhaps by marrying
old Van Rensburg's daughter and her thirty thousand pounds. None
of those explanations is correct, sir. Tom never played more
than sixpenny points in his life; he knew no more
of reefs than I do of Greek poetry; and as
for Miss Van Rensburg, she married a missionary and went
to Madagascar.

No, the name came from the affair of the Demon's
Eye.

Fill your glass before I begin. The tale is true,
every word of it, though I grant you it has
the flavour of a fireside invention. You may smile at
the ghostly part if you choose. I smiled myself—at first.
Yet I saw the light with these two eyes, and
I held the thing which caused it in these two
hands; so there is my evidence, and you may cross-examine
me upon it as severely as you please.

Tom and I had been fellow-students in London, reading for
the law in a dingy set of rooms off the
Strand. A dreary trade it appeared to us then—precedent, procedure,
and the opinions of learned judges who had been comfortably
buried for a century. We were young, of course, and
had that admirable confidence which is given to a man
before he has done anything to justify it. We fancied
Africa a country where fortunes lay scattered like pebbles, requiring
only a bold heart and a stout pair of boots
for their collection. So out we came, with eighty pounds
between us, two rifles, a box of legal books, and
a conviction that within five years we should return to
England as men of property.

That was in 'sixty-eight. Seven years afterwards we possessed one
rifle, three mouldy volumes of Chitty, and rather less than
four pounds in silver.

We had tried everything. We had carried goods inland, until
rain spoiled the goods and fever spoiled us. We had
bought oxen which died, and horses which vanished, and a
wagon which broke its axle at the only spot in
the country where no timber could be found for twenty
miles. For six months Tom kept a store, but since
he gave unlimited credit to honest men and was cheated
exclusively by them, the enterprise ended abruptly. I had a
turn at tobacco growing; then locusts arrived and displayed a
stronger interest in the crop than the market had ever
done. We hunted, traded, surveyed, transported, and speculated. Whatever card
we played, Fortune took the trick.

At the period of which I speak we were living
in a mud-walled cabin near the farm of a man
named Madison, in a rough district north of the settled
line. The roof admitted the rain in six places, the
door would not shut, and a scorpion had established a
hereditary claim to the corner where I slept. Our worldly
assets consisted of a lame pony, a pair of revolvers,
certain tools, and a promissory note signed by a gentleman
who had since been killed by a buffalo. Yet, oddly
enough, those are the days I remember with the greatest
pleasure. A full purse cushions a man, but it also
separates him from his companions. Poverty leaves no room for
ceremony. Tom and I quarrelled, laughed, starved, and hoped together,
and no prosperity could afterwards make us better friends.

One evening in the rainy season there came a knock
upon our door.

"Hollo!" cried Tom. "If that is a creditor, Jack, tell
him I died yesterday."

"If it is a creditor," said I, "he has come
farther than any sensible man would travel for so little."

I opened the door and let in Dick Wharton, a
long, raw-boned fellow with a beard the colour of dry
grass. He had been hunting beyond Madison's place and was
then returning to the settlements. Dick was as brave as
any man I ever knew—not a noisy, dare-devil bravery, but
the steady kind which sits quietly in the saddle when
the horse beneath it is trembling. That night, however, his
face was grey under the weathering, and the hand with
which he removed his hat shook perceptibly.

"You look," said Tom, "as if the devil had asked
you for the loan of a sovereign."

"I have seen something worse than the devil," Dick answered.

"Then you have seen our account-book. Sit down, old fellow."

We gave him the stool, while Tom perched upon the
flour chest and I sat on my rolled blanket. The
rain rattled upon the roof, the wind whistled through a
crack in the wall, and the candle between us bent
its flame until it was nearly horizontal. Dick took half
a tumbler of raw spirit, swallowed it without water, and
stared at the door.

"Are you followed?" I asked.

"No."

"Then what is the matter?"

He leaned towards us.

"Ghosts," said he.

Only that one word. Had another man spoken it, Tom
would have laughed; but Dick Wharton was not imaginative, and
he was certainly not a liar.

"Plural?" Tom asked gravely.

"Only one."

"A solitary ghost is easier to manage. Where did you
meet him?"

"In Kloof Valley."

At that I confess I looked sharply at him, for
Kloof Valley had an evil reputation. It was a narrow
ravine, perhaps nine miles beyond Madison's farm, shut in between
walls of black rock and entered by a pass so
cramped that two mounted men could scarcely ride abreast. Water
had once run through it, though the stream had long
since sunk underground, leaving thorn scrub, grey boulders, and a
chain of stagnant pools. The shepherds avoided the place after
sunset. They declared that a fire lived in its farther
cliff—a single red eye which watched the valley and brought
death to the man who saw it.

Such legends grow easily in lonely districts. A beast cries
among the rocks, an echo answers it, and within a
year there is a spirit with a genealogy and settled
habits. Tom and I had heard of the Eye before,
but we had given it no more attention than the
other campfire tales of the border.

Dick knew this.

"I lost the path near sunset," he continued, "and entered
the valley by mistake. The storm came up behind me.
You remember the storm last night?"

"We remember it through six separate holes in the roof,"
said I.

"Well, I could not turn back. The lightning was striking
the ridge, my horse had gone nearly mad, and I
saw what looked like an overhang at the upper end.
I made for it. Before I had gone half-way, a
red light opened in the cliff."

"Opened?" Tom interrupted.

"That is how it seemed. One moment the wall was
black; the next there was a spark upon it, as
red as a coal when the draught catches it. It
grew brighter while I watched. Not large—no larger than a
man's eye at that distance—but steady, Jack, steady and wicked.
The rain passed between us, yet it did not dim.
Then the lightning ceased, and the whole valley went black
except for that one spot."

"Could it have been a fire?" I asked.

"No man could stand where it shone. The cliff rises
sheer for two hundred feet. Besides, a fire flickers and
spreads light upon the stones about it. This thing illuminated
nothing. It was a point—a round, ruddy point—as though the
rock itself looked out at me."

"What did your horse do?"

"Turned before I touched the rein. He carried me back
through the pass at a gallop and nearly smashed my
knee against the wall."

Dick drank again. Tom had ceased smiling. He sat with
his elbows on his knees and his chin in his
hands, watching our visitor with that fixed expression which always
meant some notion was beginning to ferment in his brain.

"How high upon the cliff?" he asked.

"I cannot say."

"Ten feet? Fifty? A hundred?"

"Perhaps twelve or fifteen above the floor of the valley."

"On which side?"

"The northern wall, near the farther end."

"What colour exactly?"

"Blood-red."

"And after the lightning?"

"I told you—the lightning had ceased."

"Yes, yes! But the storm was passing? The clouds breaking?"

"There was a clear strip low in the west."

Tom sprang from the flour chest so violently that the
candle went out.

"By Jove!" he cried in the darkness.

"What is it?" said Dick.

"Jack, where are the matches?"

"You are standing on the box."

He moved, I struck a light, and there was Tom
on his knees before the heap of rubbish which represented
our library. He flung aside Chitty on Contracts, a torn
atlas, and a volume of sermons we had once accepted
in settlement of a debt. At last he dragged out
a bundle of old papers tied with cord.

"I read it," he muttered. "I am certain I read
it. A paper before the Geological Society—or was it a
traveller's pamphlet? Luminous diamonds—phosphorescence—red light after electrical disturbance—"

Dick stared at him.

"What in Heaven's name are you talking about?"

Tom tore through the papers.

"There are diamonds which shine in darkness after exposure to
sunlight, heat, or electrical influence. Not all; only rare specimens.
Some give a pale blue light, some greenish, and one
described by a Dutch mineralogist glowed red after a thunderstorm.
It was set in a ring belonging to some prince
or other. The fellow called it a carbuncle, but proved
it to be diamond. I read the account in London."

I laughed. "And you propose that Dick's ghost is a
diamond stuck in the cliff?"

Tom turned upon me with blazing eyes.

"Why not?"

"Because diamonds do not commonly occur like currants in a
cake."

"Neither do ghosts."

That silenced me for the moment.

Dick leaned back and gave a long whistle. "If it
is a stone," said he, "it must be a large
one to show at that distance."

"Precisely!"

"And valuable?"

"Valuable!" Tom paced the cabin, striking fist into palm. "If
it is what I believe, its scientific interest alone would
make it famous. If it has colour, size, and quality—Jack,
it might be worth anything!"

"Or nothing."

"Of course. Everything is worth nothing until you prove otherwise.
That observation is fit for the law, and the law
is what has kept us poor."

He wheeled round upon Dick.

"Can you lead us to the spot?"

"In daylight."

"No. At night."

Dick shook his head with considerable decision. "I have seen
all I wish to see of Kloof Valley after dark."

Tom argued, coaxed, mocked, and at last offered him one-third
of whatever we found. Dick still refused. He had business
southward, he said, and no hypothetical jewel would induce him
to take his horse again beneath that baleful eye. He
did, however, draw us a plan upon the back of
a summons, marking the pass, the overhang, and the approximate
position of the light. Before dawn he was gone.

All the next day Tom busied himself with preparations. I
cannot say that I believed in his diamond; but when
a man has four pounds, a lame pony, and no
immediate prospect of dinner, even the most fantastic hope assumes
a certain solidity. Besides, Tom's excitement was infectious. He sharpened
two cold chisels, repaired our hammer, plaited a long cord
of hide strips, and constructed what he grandly termed an
optical locator. It consisted of two straight sticks, each split
at the upper end, with a short crosspiece lashed between
them.

"You set the eye, the notches, and the light in
one line," he explained. "Then you leave the frame fixed.
A second line from another position will intersect the cliff
at the object."

"Why not walk directly towards it?"

"Because the light may disappear, because the cliff is irregular,
and because in darkness every bush and stone alters apparent
position. Triangulation, my dear Jack. The principle by which empires
are surveyed."

"With two sticks and a bootlace?"

"The apparatus does not invalidate the principle."

Towards sunset we left the cabin. We carried food, blankets,
tools, a lantern covered by a sack, and both revolvers.
The air had cleared after the storm, but heavy clouds
still lay along the eastern horizon. We rode the pony
by turns until the ground became broken; then we tethered
him near a spring and proceeded on foot.

The entrance to Kloof Valley was gloomy enough by daylight.
Two masses of basalt stood on either side like the
posts of some monstrous gate, and beyond them the ravine
sank into shadow. The last sunset burned above the ridge,
but not a beam descended into those depths. Thorn branches
caught at our sleeves. Once a troop of baboons barked
from the upper rocks, and the sound, repeated from wall
to wall, resembled laughter.

"Cheerful locality," said I.

"Hades must have an entrance somewhere," Tom answered.

We reached the floor of the valley just as the
remaining light went out of the sky. There we waited.

An hour passed.

Nothing appeared.

Tom moved from one stone to another, peering at the
northern cliff. I smoked, though the tobacco was poor and
the silence made every crackle sound like musketry. Another hour
went by. The moon should have risen, but the bank
of clouds hid it. The valley was dark as Erebus;
I could not see Tom when he stood three paces
away.

"This is folly," I said at last.

"Hush!"

"I heard nothing."

"Neither did I."

"That is my argument."

"You were always impatient, Jack."

I reminded him that I had spent seven years waiting
to become rich in his company, which should have established
my patience beyond dispute. He gave no answer.

Then, somewhere high before us, a spark glimmered.

At first I took it for a star seen through
a cleft. It was exceedingly faint, no more than the
red point of a cigar across a dark room. But
stars do not lie below the ridge, nor do they
vanish when one shifts three steps sideways behind a projecting
boulder. I moved back. There it was again.

"Tom," I whispered.

"I see it."

The light strengthened while we watched. It did not cast
a ray, nor show the surface from which it came.
It hung alone in the blackness—a lurid, watchful atom, now
dull crimson, now glowing into angry scarlet. I knew perfectly
well that some natural cause must lie behind it; nevertheless
my skin tightened across my shoulders, and I understood why
Dick's horse had bolted.

"The Eye," said Tom softly.

He sounded not afraid, but reverent.

We uncovered the lantern behind a rock and set up
the first sighting frame. This was more troublesome than Tom's
cabin demonstration had suggested. The soil was hard, the sticks
would not hold, and whenever the lantern was exposed its
glare destroyed our view of the red point. At length
we drove the supports between stones, extinguished the lantern, and
crouched behind the crosspiece.

"A little left," said Tom.

I shifted the upright.

"Too far. Back—half an inch. Hold it! Now raise your
end."

"Which end?"

"Your end, man! There! Do you see through both cuts?"

"I see one cut and part of your ear."

After much whispered abuse, we aligned the two notches with
the light. Tom steadied the frame while I packed stones
around its feet and tied it to a thorn root.
We then stretched our cord along the line of sight
until it reached the base of the cliff. Of course
the cord could not indicate height, but it marked the
vertical plane in which the object lay.

The second observation had to be taken from another point.
We measured fifty paces across the valley, set up a
single forked stick, and sighted again. Tom drove an iron
peg into the ground, then ran a second cord towards
the cliff.

"Where the two planes meet," he said, "there is our
jewel."

"Provided your sticks have not shifted."

"They have not."

"Provided the light itself is stationary."

"It is."

"Provided—"

"Oh, confound your provisos! Come along."

We approached the cliff. The light appeared higher at every
step, until it seemed directly above our heads. The cords
converged at the foot of a sloping bank of rubble
which rose towards a narrow ledge. We could climb no
farther in darkness without breaking our necks, so we marked
the place, withdrew a little distance, and wrapped ourselves in
blankets.

I slept badly. Whenever I opened my eyes the red
spark was there, staring down through the branches. Once it
seemed to wink; once to move along the face of
the rock. I said nothing of this to Tom. He
lay awake beside me, muttering calculations and estimates of value.

Shortly before dawn the light faded. It shrank from scarlet
to dull red, from red to brown, and then was
gone. The cliff remained a blank wall until the east
grew pale and detail returned by slow degrees.

We ate a strip of dried meat and began the
ascent.

The bank was steeper than it had appeared. Loose stones
rolled from under our boots, and the thorn bushes seemed
placed by Providence expressly to catch the cord and pull
us backwards. Twenty feet above the valley floor we found
a ledge three feet wide. Both sight-lines, as nearly as
we could determine, pointed to a shallow cavity directly before
it.

Tom's hands trembled as he examined the rock.

"Quartz," he said. "See the veins? And there—there is a
crystal!"

Within the cavity, half hidden by a crust of earth,
projected a translucent point as large as the end of
my thumb. It had no brilliance in daylight. Indeed, it
looked milky and somewhat greasy; but we were in no
mood for sober mineralogy.

"That is it," Tom breathed.

"Small," said I.

"We see only the tip."

He attacked the surrounding stone with a chisel. The cliff
was extremely hard, and for two hours we hammered in
turns, enlarging the cavity grain by grain. The crystal ran
inward and broadened. Each newly exposed surface appeared clearer than
the last, until even my caution deserted me.

"It is the size of an orange," I said.

"Larger."

"If it is a diamond—"

"It is."

"If it is—what would it be worth?"

"Do not ask."

"But approximately?"

"Jack, if I attempted an estimate I should drop the
hammer."

There was little space to work. A rounded protuberance beside
the cavity obstructed the chisel, and after barking my knuckles
against it for the third time I proposed knocking it
off.

"No," said Tom. "We may crack the specimen. Use it
as a fulcrum."

We slipped the point of our small crowbar beneath the
crystal-bearing matrix and rested the shaft upon the knob. Tom
pressed while I struck with the hammer. The surrounding rock
split suddenly. There was a sharp snap, a shower of
fragments, and both of us nearly went backwards over the
ledge.

"I have it!" I cried.

A whitish lump, larger than a man's fist and crusted
with stone, lay at my feet.

Tom seized it. He scraped one side with the knife,
revealing a cloudy, glass-like interior.

"Wealth!" he shouted. "Jack, you old doubter—wealth!"

He hugged me so violently that I lost my hat
over the edge. We laughed, shook hands, and talked at
once. For ten delirious minutes we disposed of a fortune
which had not yet been valued. Tom would buy land;
I would return to England; Dick should have his third;
our creditors should receive principal and interest, though Tom proposed
making one particularly offensive fellow wait in the passage before
payment.

At last practical considerations prevailed. We wrapped the treasure in
my spare shirt, packed it among grass in the tool
bag, and descended. I remember that the ledge looked disturbed
after our work, but we gave it no attention. There
was scarred stone about the cavity, fragments below, and the
little rounded knob had vanished—broken off, I supposed, when the
crowbar slipped. What did it matter? We had the Demon's
Eye.

Our return journey was a triumphal procession performed by two
exhausted men and one lame pony. Tom sang until his
voice failed. He addressed the wrapped lump as "Your Majesty"
and apologised whenever the pony stumbled. By noon we reached
Madison's farm.

Madison was a square, sunburned man who had spent thirty
years in the country and had acquired the useful habit
of showing astonishment only when there was reason for it.
We found him repairing a gate.

"What have you there?" he asked.

"A mineral specimen," said Tom with magnificent carelessness.

"Looks heavy."

"It is heavy."

"Found it?"

"In a manner of speaking."

Madison rested his hammer on the post. "Gold?"

"Better."

He looked from Tom to me, wiped his hands, and
led us into the house. There, upon the kitchen table,
we unwrapped the stone. Tom waited for an exclamation.

Madison gave none. He turned the lump over, scratched it
with his pocketknife, and touched the blade to his tongue.

"Well?" Tom demanded.

Madison scratched it again.

"Well?"

The farmer looked up.

"Rock-salt," said he.

There was a silence.

"What?" whispered Tom.

"Salt. A fair lump, too, though mixed with dirt."

"That is impossible."

Madison cut off a flake and handed it across. Tom
stared at it as if it were poisoned. I put
the fragment to my tongue.

Salt!

I do not believe any substance upon earth ever tasted
so bitter.

Tom snatched up the lump. He tested one face, then
another. At last he dropped into a chair, and the
expression upon him was so tragic that Madison, who had
begun to grin, became almost apologetic.

"Easy mistake," he said.

"No," Tom replied hollowly. "It is not an easy mistake.
It is the mistake of two congenital idiots."

"Where did you get it?"

"Kloof Valley."

Madison's eyebrows rose. "Not from the Eye?"

"You know of the light?"

"Everyone knows of it. I saw it myself once, years
ago. Kept out of the valley since."

Tom sprang upright.

"You saw it? Red?"

"Red enough."

"And from the cliff?"

"So it seemed."

"Then the light exists! Jack, do you hear? The light
exists independently of Dick's report."

"I saw it last night," said I. "I require no
corroboration."

Tom paced the room. Despair left his face as quickly
as it had entered; thought took its place. He stopped,
turned, and struck the table.

"We removed the wrong crystal."

Madison examined the lump again. "This was in the cliff?"

"Beside the place indicated by our lines."

"Salt sometimes occurs in pockets."

Tom scarcely heard him.

"The light was visible; our triangulation was substantially correct; the
object was mineral, not flame. At dawn we found the
conspicuous crystal and assumed identity without proof. We looked where
we expected to look, not where we had actually seen.
Jack, we must return."

"We have ridden half the day."

"Then we shall ride the other half back."

"The light may not show tonight."

"We no longer need it. The place is marked."

Madison offered us food, which we ate standing, while Tom
questioned him about every luminous stone he had ever encountered.
Within an hour we were again upon the trail, leaving
the lump of salt on Madison's table. He afterwards used
it for curing meat, which was perhaps the only sensible
profit anyone drew from our first expedition.

The pony was too weary to carry us, so we
went on foot. Tom moved ahead at a pace which
I found difficult to maintain. He blamed himself without interruption.

"I saw the account," he said. "I remembered the colour,
the storm, everything—and then I let a lump of salt
deceive me because it was large. Size, Jack! The eternal
temptation of fools."

"It deceived me also."

"You were appointed sceptic. You failed in your office."

"I shall resume it immediately. There was no diamond."

"There was."

"The light may be fungus, an insect, or some trick
of reflection."

"On bare rock?"

"Then a malicious lantern."

"Who would hang it there?"

"The ghost, naturally."

He swore at me and marched faster.

Dusk found us once more at the gate-like rocks. We
lit the lantern, entered the valley, and followed the cords,
which remained where we had left them. One had been
pulled aside by an animal, but the line was easily
restored. We climbed to the ledge.

The cavity was empty.

Tom searched it with fingers, knife, and chisel. He examined
every vein within arm's reach. He descended to check the
sighting sticks, returned, and measured the angles anew.

"This is the place," he said.

"It is."

"The stone should be here."

"It is not."

"It cannot have vanished."

"Perhaps someone followed us."

"No one knew."

"Madison knew."

"Madison could not have passed us."

He stood against the cliff, the lantern at his feet,
his face drawn and shining with sweat. I had seen
Tom disappointed many times, but never broken. On each previous
occasion he had found some new hope before the old
one was cold. Now he looked suddenly aged.

"We were right," he said. "That is the cruelty of
it. We were right—and still we lose."

I put my hand on his shoulder. "Come, old fellow.
We have lost nothing but two days and a shirt."

"Seven years," he answered. "Seven years of playing small cards,
and when a trump comes into my hand I throw
it under the table."

I bent to pick up the lantern. Its light passed
across the scarred cavity, and something about the outline troubled
me. I could not at first say what it was.
Then I remembered my bruised knuckles, the crowbar bending, and
the rounded protuberance against which we had braced it.

"Tom," said I, "where is the knob?"

"What knob?"

"The bit of rock beside the salt. Our fulcrum."

He stared.

"We broke it off," I continued. "When the matrix gave
way, the bar snapped it from the cliff."

Tom snatched up the lantern and lowered it over the
edge.

Below the ledge lay a steep fan of debris ending
among thorn bushes. We scrambled down, heedless of torn clothes
and bleeding hands, and began to search. There were hundreds
of fragments—black basalt, white quartz, grey shale—most freshly dusted from
our hammering. The knob might have rolled ten feet or
fifty.

For half an hour we found nothing.

Then I heard Tom cry out.

Not a shout of triumph; a sharp, strangled sound, as
though someone had struck him. He was kneeling beneath a
thorn, holding a dark, egg-shaped piece of rock. Along one
broken side ran a narrow glassy seam. Embedded at its
centre was a crystal no larger than a walnut.

In the lantern light it looked colourless. Tom covered the
lamp with his hat.

Darkness closed upon us.

For several seconds there was nothing. Then, within his cupped
hands, a point of deep red slowly kindled.

It brightened—ruddy, clear, baleful—and shone between his fingers like a
living coal.

The Demon's Eye!

Neither of us spoke. After all our shouting over the
salt, the true discovery reduced us to silence. Tom uncovered
the lantern and looked at me. Tears had made clean
tracks down his dusty face, though he was smiling.

"Lucky," he said. "That is what I am, Jack. Not
clever. Not deserving. Lucky. I use the wrong stone as
a lever, break off the right one, throw it over
a precipice, and then find it under a bush."

"You remembered the pamphlet."

"Luck."

"You devised the sighting."

"Luck."

"You insisted upon returning."

"Obstinacy."

"And I noticed the knob."

He grasped my hand. "That, my dear fellow, was genius."

We did not attempt to remove the crystal there. We
wrapped the entire fragment in cloth, tied it inside Tom's
coat, and spent the night under the overhang, taking turns
to keep watch. The red glow persisted for nearly an
hour, gradually fading to the faintest spark. At dawn we
left the valley.

The rest can be told briefly, and indeed ought to
be, for no story is improved by accounts of bankers
and lawyers. The stone proved to be a diamond of
extraordinary purity, slightly coloured at its centre, and possessing a
phosphorescent property which made it a curiosity throughout Europe. The
precise geological explanation was debated by men with more learning
than agreement. Some attributed the glow to electrical excitation during
storms; others to sunlight acting upon impurities in the crystal.
Tom listened to them all and maintained that the stone
shone because it had a devil in it.

It was cut into one principal gem and several smaller
ones. The sale made Tom and me wealthy—not princes, you
understand, but wealthy enough that neither of us ever again
had to consider whether a creditor might be discouraged by
an inconvenient death.

We gave Dick Wharton his third, though he protested that
he had done nothing but run away. Tom told him
that running in the correct direction at the correct moment
was the foundation of half the great fortunes in commerce.
Dick used the money to establish a transport concern and
became a substantial man. For all I know, his wagons
are crossing the interior still.

As for Tom, the name Lucky Tom fastened upon him
from the day the newspapers printed the history of the
stone. Every success afterwards was attributed to fortune. If he
chose a sound investment, men said he was lucky; if
he avoided a bad one, luck had warned him; if
rain fell upon his land, Fortune herself had turned the
tap. He laughed at it and encouraged the legend, though
I knew how much reading, observation, and stubborn thought lay
behind that single fortunate accident.

And I? I bought a farm—not far from Madison's old
place—and did tolerably well with cattle. I had knocked about
too long to return happily to England. The broad sky
had entered my blood, I suppose, and a man who
has slept beneath the stars finds ceilings lower than they
used to be.

The Demon's Eye itself belongs now to some collection on
the Continent. I saw it once, years later, in a
glass case beneath three electric lamps. It looked cold, respectable,
and quite incapable of frightening a horse. A card beside
it gave the weight, the composition, the date of discovery,
and a catalogue number. There was not a word about
Kloof Valley, the storm, the two sticks and bootlace, the
lump of salt, or Tom kneeling beneath the thorn with
that red fire between his hands.

Science had explained it, measured it, and locked it away.

But sometimes, sir, when the drink is good, the fire
low, and the night wind moves against the shutters, I
see it as it was before the cutters touched it—a
solitary crimson spark high upon the cliff, watching over those
rough old days when we possessed nothing in the world

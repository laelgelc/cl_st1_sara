## The Red Memorandum Book

Satherwaite opened the lower drawer of his bureau, shut it
with unnecessary violence, crossed the room, rearranged three silver-backed brushes
which were already in an irreproachable line, glanced at the
clock, and then dropped into the great leather chair before
the fire, where he sat with his chin in his
hand and regarded the coals as though they, too, had
failed him at the last moment.

It was five o'clock on Christmas Eve, and he was,
he had decided, the most miserable man in New England.

At four he had received Phil Cushing's telegram:

FATHER DOWN WITH GRIP DOCTOR FORBIDS GUESTS AWFULLY SORRY LETTER
FOLLOWS MERRY CHRISTMAS PHIL

The message lay on the rug where Satherwaite had allowed
it to fall. Its yellow envelope, propped against the brass
fender, wore an air of official finality. The whole beautiful
scheme of the holiday had collapsed in nineteen words, of
which "Merry Christmas" seemed to him the most wantonly offensive.

For ten days he had been looking forward to the
visit. There would have been the journey north in the
smoking compartment with Phil; the great square Cushing house, full
of cousins and holly and old servants; skating, perhaps, on
the river; a dance at the Assembly Rooms; and, above
all, Phil's sister Margaret, whom Satherwaite had met only twice
and had remembered with remarkable persistence. For Margaret's possible approval
he had purchased a dark green waistcoat of a distinction
so discreet that it had cost almost twice as much
as a more assertive garment. It lay now upon the
bed, between two tissue papers, useless and reproachful.

Phil would keep; and so, presumably, would Phil's sister. But
Christmas would not.

Most of the college had gone down on the noon
trains. His own family were in California, where his mother
had declared they were having a "quite absurd Christmas among
roses," and the club dining room was closed until Thursday.
He might have taken a train for New York and
spent the day with an aunt, but the aunt had
three small children and a husband who collected ecclesiastical snuffboxes.
Besides, the useful trains had gone. The last car for
Boston would place him in a hotel at an hour
when hotels became intolerably conscious of being hotels.

He poked the fire. His room was one of the
pleasantest in the Yard: mahogany shelves, deep crimson curtains, two
old sporting prints, cut-glass decanters upon a tray, and a
mantelpiece broad enough to accommodate photographs of the persons whom,
at various times, Satherwaite had thought it proper to admire.
Yet the room had never appeared so large or so
completely untenanted. Even the fire burned with a decorous reserve,
as though engaged for the evening elsewhere.

"What the deuce does a man do on Christmas Eve?"
he demanded aloud.

The room offered no suggestion.

He considered reading. There was a new novel on the
table, but the hero was a young duke whose matrimonial
embarrassments seemed, under present circumstances, trivial. He considered writing letters
and rejected the proposal as morbid. Finally, with the desperate
courage of one who faces an unexampled calamity, he resolved
to put his room in order.

For twenty minutes he laboured. He burned old invitations, placed
seven collars in a drawer, restored a racquet to its
press, and gathered into a pile the notebooks, pamphlets, and
stray papers that had accumulated under the window seat. The
occupation did not consume the evening, as he had rather
hoped it might. It did, however, disclose a black composition
book with "M. F. Doyle, '02" written across the cover.

Satherwaite held it up and frowned.

Then he remembered. Doyle had lent him the notebook after
Bevan's lecture on constitutional history, when Satherwaite had been absent
at the Yale game and had discovered, somewhat late, that
Bevan's lectures might be represented upon the examination. Doyle had
copied them in a small, painstaking hand, with dates underlined
and references entered in the margin. Satherwaite had thanked him,
meant to return the book, and forgotten it.

He knew Doyle slightly. Everybody knew him slightly. Doyle sat
in the first row, wore shiny coats, won scholarships, and
spoke at debates in a dry, deliberate fashion which made
careless men uncomfortable. He belonged to no club of consequence;
indeed, it had never occurred to Satherwaite that Doyle belonged
anywhere. Yet he recalled that the fellow lived in a
boarding house beyond the Square, over a stationer's shop.

The notebook supplied an errand, and an errand, at five-thirty
on Christmas Eve, had the dignity of a destination. Satherwaite
wrapped himself in his ulster, thrust the book into his
pocket, and went out.

The Yard was blue with the early dusk. Snow had
begun falling—not vigorously, but in fine, speculative flakes that hovered
about the lamps and silvered the bare elms. The windows
of the dormitories were chiefly dark. Here and there a
proctor's light shone with official persistence, and from one distant
entry came the melancholy sound of a piano played by
somebody who knew only the first half of "The Palms."

Outside the gates the little shops were closing. Boys hurried
past with parcels; a streetcar, its windows clouded with frost,
clanged toward Boston; and a stout gentleman carrying a turkey
nearly collided with Satherwaite at the crossing. All the world
seemed provided with a household, a parcel, or at least
a turkey. Satherwaite had only Doyle's notebook.

The stationer's shop was dark, but a side door admitted
him to a narrow hallway smelling of boiled cabbage and
damp wool. On the second floor a baby was crying;
on the third, some one laughed, and a chair scraped
over bare boards. Satherwaite found Doyle's name on a visiting
card tacked beside a door at the top.

He knocked.

There was an abrupt silence within—not the silence of an
empty room, but the much more expressive silence of several
persons who have stopped talking at once. Then came a
movement, a whisper, and Doyle opened the door.

For an instant he merely stared.

"Satherwaite!"

"Hallo, Doyle. I found your notebook and thought I'd bring
it round."

"Oh!" Doyle took the book, looked at it, and looked
again at Satherwaite. "That was very good of you. I
shouldn't have wanted you to trouble—especially to-night."

"No trouble at all."

Behind Doyle, Satherwaite saw a small room crowded with men.
One sat on a trunk, one on the arm of
a horsehair sofa, and two were kneeling beside a tiny
evergreen set in a soap box. There were cranberries on
a string, a bowl of popcorn, pipes, newspapers, and six
faces turned toward the doorway with varying expressions of curiosity
and dismay.

Doyle became aware of the pause.

"We're having a little—a sort of Christmas-eve party," he explained.
"Some of the fellows who couldn't get home. It isn't
much of an affair."

"It looks jolly," said Satherwaite.

The remark, though kindly intended, was received with caution. A
tall man in a faded cricket blazer stopped chewing a
cranberry. Somebody behind him hastily removed a paper cap from
his head.

Doyle cleared his throat. "Won't you come in for a
minute?"

The invitation was polite; it was also, Satherwaite perceived, constructed
in the expectation that he would refuse. He might have
done so. He was overdressed for the room, socially unexplained,
and suddenly conscious of his gloves, his stick, and the
faint odour of good cigars that clung to his ulster.
These men knew him as one of the fellows who
dined behind club windows, drove hired tandems, and forgot borrowed
notebooks. His departure would relieve them all.

Then from below there floated upward the sound of the
baby, still crying with patient despair; beyond the hall window
snow pressed softly against the dark; and Satherwaite remembered his
own splendid fire burning alone.

"Thanks," he said. "I'd like to, if you mean it."

Doyle's eyebrows rose. "Of course I mean it. Come in."

Introductions followed. The man in the blazer was Crandall, '01,
who had once made forty-three runs against Princeton and would
probably retain the blazer until it fell from him by
natural dissolution. The broad, bewhiskered senior by the fireplace was
Doak of Minnesota; he had missed his train through work
at the laboratory. Pell was the small, eagle-faced debater; Ashmore,
the long youth on the trunk, came from Oregon and
could not afford the journey; Rugg had intended to go
home to Vermont, but his younger brothers had scarlet fever;
and the round-faced freshman called Beale had discovered that a
money order expected from an uncle did not exist.

"My uncle is a man of imagination," Beale explained. "He
imagines he sent it."

There was a slight laugh, after which constraint returned.

Doyle took Satherwaite's coat and encountered difficulty in finding a
place for it. At last he hung it over the
corner of a bookcase, where its sable collar appeared indecently
sumptuous. Satherwaite was offered the only sound chair. He declined
it and sat on an upturned packing case, thereby producing
a second, more approving silence.

"Smoke?" said Doak.

"Thanks."

Doak extended a leather pouch whose condition suggested service in
several campaigns.

"It's not club tobacco," he said. "It may kill you."

"If it does, don't send word to my family until
after Christmas."

Doak's whiskers moved. "Take my pipe too. The stem pulls
out, but you can hold it in with your thumb."

The pipe was old, black, and formidable. Satherwaite accepted it
without examination, filled it, and applied a match. At the
second inhalation his eyes watered, but he continued heroically.

"Good baccy," he said, when speech became possible.

"Liar," observed Crandall.

It was the first word addressed to him without ceremony,
and Satherwaite felt absurdly pleased.

The room itself, now that he had leisure to observe
it, was shabby to the point of ingenuity. A green
curtain concealed a washstand; the carpet had been cut and
reassembled from at least two former carpets; the mantel clock
lacked a minute hand, and the gas fixture leaned from
the wall as if wearied by education. Yet the fire
snapped generously in a little iron grate. Books stood everywhere
in tight, industrious ranks. Over the windows hung branches of
hemlock, and on the table was a supper of sandwiches,
apples, doughnuts, cheese, and a brown jug whose contents Doyle
guarded with some vigilance from Beale.

The tiny tree occupied the centre. It was scarcely three
feet high and leaned eastward, but the men had adorned
it with cranberries, popcorn, gilt stars cut from tobacco boxes,
and little parcels tied in red string. A white clay
pipe served as an angel.

"That's a good-looking tree," said Satherwaite.

"It has character," Pell replied. "The man who sold it
called it damaged."

"Chop it off!" said Crandall.

This phrase was evidently established among them. Whenever a statement
became too grand, too personal, or too nearly sentimental, somebody
cried, "Chop it off!" and the offender was expected to
subside. Satherwaite stored the custom away for future use.

For a time the conversation moved painfully. They discussed the
weather, the emptiness of the Yard, the probability that Hollis
would be elected captain, and the injustice of Professor Bevan's
marking. Each topic expired after a few exchanges. Satherwaite could
feel the party waiting for him to go, and pride,
loneliness, and a genuine desire not to spoil their evening
combined to make him determined to stay until they wanted
him.

Doyle attempted rescue.

"You were to have gone away, weren't you?"

"Yes. Phil Cushing asked me up to his place."

"And you changed your mind?"

"My mind had very little to do with it. His
father developed the grip at a strategically perfect hour."

"That's hard luck," said Rugg.

"It was worse than that. It was tragedy. You fellows
don't know what preparations had been made." Satherwaite leaned forward,
warming to his theme. "For ten days I've been engaged
in becoming the sort of person a respectable family might
permit in its house. I bought a waistcoat."

"Great Scott!" said Doak.

"A green one—very quiet, very distinguished. The shopman assured me
it expressed character without ostentation. I had my hair cut.
I answered a note from my aunt. I even attended
chapel twice."

"Twice?" Beale breathed.

"On consecutive mornings. And then, at four to-day, I receive
nineteen brutal words from Cushing, including a Merry Christmas which
I regard as actionable."

They laughed—not politely this time, but with the quick recognition
of men who perceive that another is willing to make
game of himself. Satherwaite drew Phil's telegram from his pocket
and read it with the solemnity of a death announcement.

"Father down with grip," Pell repeated. "Do you suspect foul
play?"

"I suspect Margaret."

"Who's Margaret?" asked Crandall.

Satherwaite hesitated just long enough.

"Phil's sister."

A roar greeted this. Doak slapped his knee; Beale fell
against the tree and was dragged away by Doyle; even
the austere Pell grinned.

"A waistcoat!" cried Crandall. "He bought it for a girl!"

"I bought it for myself," said Satherwaite with dignity. "The
girl was merely to be permitted to see it."

"Chop it off!" they shouted together.

After that the party became possible.

The brown jug contained cider, heated with cloves and a
quantity of sugar which Beale had measured under the theory
that no beverage could be injured by sweetness. Doyle filled
mugs, apologized for their differing sizes, and was told by
everybody to chop it off. Sandwiches went round. Satherwaite ate
three and declared the potted meat superior to anything at
his club.

"No club talk," warned Ashmore.

"Quite right. Vulgar subject."

"Especially to those who don't belong," Pell said dryly.

The remark produced a little eddy of discomfort. Doyle glanced
at him; Crandall frowned. Satherwaite, who might yesterday have taken
offence, examined his broken pipe.

"You have the advantage of me there," he said. "Some
of us don't belong nearly so much as we think
we do."

Pell looked up sharply. Then he nodded.

"Fair enough."

The moment passed, and something passed with it. Satherwaite ceased
to be a visitor whom they must entertain. Doak asked
whether he played whist; Crandall demanded his opinion of the
football coaches; Beale borrowed his matches without permission. When the
minute-less clock had, according to Doyle's watch, reached eight, each
man was obliged to tell of the best Christmas he
remembered.

Rugg described a Vermont farmhouse snowed to the second-storey windows,
with twenty cousins sleeping in feather beds and his grandfather
carving a turkey of mythological proportions. Ashmore spoke of an
Oregon logging camp where his father had once brought home
a sack of oranges after three days on horseback. Doak
remembered sleigh bells across a Minnesota lake. Crandall's story concerned
a cricket bat, though nobody understood why a cricket bat
had been required in December. Beale confessed that his best
Christmas had been at twelve, when an elderly neighbour, believing
him to be a poor orphan, gave him two dinners.

"I had parents," Beale explained, "but it seemed discourteous to
mention them."

When Satherwaite's turn came, he thought first of a Christmas
at Newport, with footmen, silver candelabra, and a cotillion at
which every favour had come from Paris. Yet what he
told them was something he had nearly forgotten: an afternoon
when he was seven, walking home through snow with his
father, each carrying one end of a wooden sled. His
father had died two years later.

"No reason it should have been the best," Satherwaite concluded,
looking into the fire. "Nothing happened."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then Crandall said gently, "Chop it off."

They all laughed, Satherwaite loudest.

At nine they lighted the tree. The operation required courage.
The candles had been cut in half and fastened to
the branches with wire; one leaned against a cranberry and
another immediately set fire to a gilt star. Doak extinguished
it between his fingers. The gas was turned low, and
the poor little evergreen stood luminous in the dim room,
its popcorn casting gigantic shadows upon the wall, its clay
angel tipped drunkenly toward the ceiling.

Outside, the snow had thickened. It descended beyond the panes
in a blue-white hush, hiding chimneys, softening roofs, and making
the narrow street seem remote from every troubled and calculating
part of the world. A streetcar passed at the farther
corner, glowing through the storm like a travelling room; then
its bell diminished, and the city was still.

Satherwaite looked from the window to the six faces about
the tree. The candlelight touched Crandall's faded blazer, Doyle's worn
cuffs, the scar on Doak's thumb, and the anxious care
with which Beale shielded one flame from a draught. There
was nothing handsome in the assemblage, nothing correctly appointed; yet
the room possessed what his own had lacked. It was
inhabited—not merely by bodies and voices, but by memories, kindnesses,
the cheerful acceptance of disappointment, and that mysterious enlargement of
space which occurs when no one is guarding his share
of it.

Doyle turned up the gas.

"Presents!" cried Beale.

The parcels were distributed by a lottery of Doyle's invention.
No gift had cost more than twenty-five cents, and most
were designed to insult the recipient. Pell, whose speeches habitually
exceeded his allotted time, received an alarm clock without works.
Crandall was given a pamphlet entitled The Care of Ancient
Fabrics, in reference to his blazer. Doak got a diminutive
comb for his whiskers; Ashmore, who was six feet four,
a child's tin sword; Rugg, a pair of paper cuffs;
Doyle, a rubber stamp bearing the word REJECTED, with which
to mark his poems; and Beale a toy bank containing
a nickel firmly glued to the bottom.

The laughter became violent. The alarm clock was made to
preside over the mantel; Crandall read aloud from The Care
of Ancient Fabrics; Doak combed one whisker into a point
and addressed Satherwaite in the manner of a German professor.

Then Doyle saw him watching and stopped.

"We've made a blunder," he said.

"What's that?"

"We've no present for you."

"My dear fellow, I came without warning."

"That doesn't matter," said Rugg. "Everybody gets a present."

"Certainly," Doak agreed. "It's the Constitution."

"Unwritten," Pell added, "but binding."

They conferred in a corner with much whispering and furtive
inspection of pockets. Satherwaite protested, but was shouted down. Presently
Beale ran into the hall and returned with a parcel
wrapped in a page from the Boston Transcript. It was
tied with the cranberry string that had earlier decorated the
mantel.

Doyle cleared his throat.

"Satherwaite, the committee has learned with pain that you were
decoyed into this gathering under false pretences and have remained
in spite of the food. It therefore begs you to
accept this slight token of—of—"

"Chop it off!" said Satherwaite.

"Of its esteem," Doyle finished obstinately.

Inside the newspaper was a small red memorandum book, such
as the stationer downstairs sold for ten cents. A label
had been pasted across the cover, and upon it each
man had signed his name. Underneath, Beale had written:

FOR ENGAGEMENTS NOT CANCELLED BY GRIP

The thing was absurd, cheap, and hastily contrived. Satherwaite held
it for several seconds without opening it. He possessed a
Russian leather engagement book with gilt edges, sent each year
by his father's bankers, and had never thought twice about
it. This red book, bending already at one corner, seemed
for the moment a possession of incomparable value.

"It's ripping," he said. "Thank you."

His voice betrayed him slightly. Crandall began, "Chop—" but stopped.

Satherwaite slipped the book into his inner pocket. His fingers
touched a narrow parcel there, and he remembered the paper
knife.

He had bought it that afternoon for Phil—a small silver
paper knife with Phil's initials on the handle—and had intended
to take it north. It was not an extravagant gift
by Satherwaite's standards, but among the alarm clock and rubber
stamp it would look barbarously expensive. To present it openly
would be to disturb the delicate equality of their nonsense.
Yet he wished suddenly and urgently to give something.

While Beale was attempting to open the toy bank with
Ashmore's tin sword, Satherwaite moved behind the tree. One little
package remained beneath it: Doyle's rubber stamp had been wrapped
in two parts, and the empty lower paper still lay
there. He took out the paper knife, stripped away the
jeweller's covering, and folded it into the discarded paper. The
initials—P.C.—were an embarrassment. After an instant's thought he drew his
pencil and added, in small letters upon a scrap:

For cutting the pages of books you have time to
read because other men borrow your notes.

He tucked the parcel under a branch.

"Hallo," he said. "There's another one."

Doyle looked surprised. "For whom?"

"It says Doyle."

"But I've had mine."

"Constitution allows two," said Satherwaite. "Unwritten, but binding."

Doyle opened the parcel. The room quieted when the silver
flashed.

"This isn't one of ours," he said.

"Apparently Santa Claus has better taste than the rest of
you."

Doyle read the slip, glanced toward Satherwaite, and understood. For
a moment he seemed about to object. Then, perhaps perceiving
that objection would injure more than acceptance, he ran his
thumb along the blade.

"I've never had one," he said simply. "Thank you—Santa Claus."

"Satherwaite ought to be Santa Claus!" Beale exclaimed.

The suggestion was instantly adopted. A red table cover was
taken from the stand, a cushion was tied about Satherwaite's
middle, and cotton from the soap box was arranged under
his chin. Crandall contributed the cranberry garland as a chain
of office. Thus attired, Satherwaite sat in Doyle's chair and
delivered judgments upon the company.

To Pell he granted concision; to Doak, a pipe whose
stem remained in place; to Ashmore, another inch of height
"to remove all remaining inconvenience from ceilings"; to Rugg, immunity
from scarlet fever; to Crandall, a new blazer, which Crandall
rejected as an insult; to Beale, an uncle of unimpeachable
financial memory; and to Doyle—

Satherwaite paused.

"To Doyle," he said, "the return of all borrowed property
within a reasonable time."

"Hear, hear!" cried Pell.

"And what do you grant yourself?" Doyle asked.

The laughter dwindled. Satherwaite looked around the shabby room, at
the little tree, the steaming mugs, and the men who
three hours earlier had wished him politely out of the
doorway.

"Another invitation," he said.

This time no one cried, "Chop it off."

"You've got it," said Doak.

They sang after that. None had a beautiful voice, and
several had voices of unusual ugliness, but deficiencies of tune
were corrected by vigour. They began with college songs, passed
through "The Spanish Cavalier," "Nellie Was a Lady," and a
ballad concerning a man who had loved six sisters in
Ohio, and arrived at last, by common instinct, at the
carols.

The fire sank lower; the candles guttered; tobacco smoke made
a blue canopy beneath the ceiling. Satherwaite, with the red
table cover about his shoulders and Doak's pipe between his
teeth, sang until his throat hurt. He had attended Christmas
services in churches where paid choirs produced harmonies of angelic
precision, but no music had ever seemed so honest as
these seven untrained voices stumbling together through "God Rest Ye
Merry, Gentlemen."

At a quarter before twelve they carried their mugs to
the window. The storm had ceased. Snow lay smooth along
the roofs and window ledges; stars showed above the chimneys;
and every ordinary object—the ash barrels, telegraph wires, shop awnings,
and rails of the streetcar track—had acquired a temporary purity.
Far off, bells began to sound.

"Not midnight yet," said Doyle, consulting his watch.

"Your clock says it's half past four," Beale observed.

"My clock has always taken a broad view."

They waited, suddenly subdued. Somewhere below, the baby had stopped
crying. From a neighbouring house came a woman's laugh and
the thin notes of a parlour organ. Satherwaite thought of
his mother among California roses; of Phil at the Cushing
place, creeping past his father's sickroom; of Margaret, perhaps fastening
holly to a stair rail; and of his own vacant
chamber, where the green waistcoat lay in tissue paper before
an unwatched fire.

He did not pity himself.

Doyle lifted his hand.

"Now."

"Merry Christmas!" they shouted.

Hands met across the table. Crandall pounded Satherwaite on the
shoulder; Beale embraced Doak and became entangled in the whiskers;
Pell, who disapproved of emotional display, shook everybody's hand twice.
Doyle came last.

"Merry Christmas, Satherwaite."

"Merry Christmas, old man."

There was no more to say, which was fortunate.

It was nearly one when Satherwaite put on his ulster.
The cranberry chain remained around his neck because Beale insisted
it was an honorary decoration. Doak's pipe remained in his
pocket because Doak insisted it should be smoked on the
way home.

"But it's your pipe."

"I have another."

"You said this was the only one seasoned properly."

"I lied. Take it, you beggar."

Doyle walked with him to the lower door. The narrow
hall had grown colder, and the gas jet burned very
low.

"I'm glad you came," Doyle said.

"So am I."

"I thought at first—well, we all thought you'd be bored."

"I was at first," Satherwaite replied gravely.

Doyle stared; then Satherwaite grinned.

"Chop it off," said Doyle, and pushed him into the
snow.

The street was white and empty. Satherwaite had gone six
paces when something struck the back of his hat and
scattered cold powder down his collar. He turned. Six figures
occupied the upper windows, Beale foremost, armed with a washbasin
full of snow gathered from the sill.

"Assassins!" Satherwaite cried.

He made a snowball and threw it. It burst against
the bricks a yard below the window.

"Club training!" Pell called down.

"Rotten form!" shouted Crandall.

A second missile struck Satherwaite's shoulder. He dropped his stick,
made two snowballs with care, and returned the fire. One
entered the open window and produced a yell from Doak;
the other removed the clay pipe from the top of
the tree.

"Run!" Doyle called. "The proctor's coming!"

No proctor was visible, but Satherwaite seized his stick and
ran, laughing, while snowballs broke behind him and the men
at the windows shouted contradictory Christmas greetings. At the corner
he turned, lifted his hat, and bowed with all the
magnificence the cranberry garland allowed.

Then he went on alone.

Yet the street no longer appeared lonely. The snow creaked
companionably beneath his boots; Doak's deplorable pipe bulged in his
pocket; the red memorandum book rested over his heart; and
from the lighted room behind him came a final chorus,
ragged, loud, and affectionate:

"Merry Christmas, Santa Claus!"

Satherwaite waved without looking back. A snowball, thrown with heroic
but inaccurate zeal, sailed over his head and vanished into
the immaculate night.

### The Unsealed Letter

The brougham set Sir Cloud Malpas down at his own
door shortly after three o'clock, an hour at which neither
his household nor, if the truth were known, Sir Cloud
himself expected him.

A committee on municipal lodging had collapsed through the illness
of its chairman; the deputation from Little Marsh had been
postponed; and Sir Cloud, finding himself accidentally in possession of
an afternoon, had returned to Malpas House with the vague
but agreeable intention of surprising his wife.

The surprise, however, appeared to be his.

The hall was sombre after the white glare of Grosvenor
Square. His hat and gloves were accepted by a servant
whose composure seemed almost reproachful.

"Her ladyship at home, Parkes?"

"Her ladyship went out at half-past two, sir."

"Ah! Shopping?"

"I could not say, sir."

Sir Cloud crossed the morning-room and entered Gertrude's little writing-room.
He had scarcely done so twice in as many years.
The room contained several things of whose existence he had
been unaware: a pale Italian cabinet, a screen embroidered with
silver birds, and, upon the escritoire, a gold and onyx
inkstand which he dimly remembered having given her—six—seven—years ago.

Beside the inkstand lay a letter. It was addressed to
him.

He smiled. Gertrude, learning of his unexpected leisure, had perhaps
left some domestic commission. Yet the letter was unsealed; and
at its head, instead of the usual "My dear Cloud,"
stood only his name.

He began to read.

> Cloud,
>
> When you receive this I shall have gone. I
am leaving England to-night, and I am going to Italy.
You needn't send detectives after me, or ask the Foreign
Office to interfere. I am not being abducted, and I
am not mad. I am merely tired—so tired that I
can't stand it any longer.

Gone? To Italy? Sir Cloud turned instinctively towards the door.
The room, the square beyond it, the whole enormous, punctual
machinery of London remained absurdly undisturbed.

He read on.

> I know exactly what you will say. You will
say there has been no scene, no quarrel, no warning.
You will be right. There has been no scene because
whenever I tried to speak to you, you looked at
your watch. There has been no quarrel because one cannot
quarrel with a public institution.

Scene? What scene? She had never attempted—

Had she?

There had been, perhaps, a conversation after the Hertford House
dinner, when she had complained of seeing nothing of him.
But that was in the middle of the housing inquiry.
There had also been something at Easter concerning a week-end
which he had been compelled to spend with the Bishop.
One could hardly describe these as warnings.

> You are admirable, Cloud. Everybody says so. You open
hospitals, preside at meetings, reconcile angry demagogues, subscribe to deserving
funds, and make speeches about the sanctity of the home.
I have sometimes wondered whether the last does not strike
you as funny. It strikes me as funny; but I
have had to get my amusement where I could.

He frowned. The sentence was unjust—deliberately unjust. The sanctity of
the home was not a subject upon which he had
ever spoken lightly. Only last month at Stoke Parva—

But Gertrude's meaning reached him before his defence had formed
itself. He sat down.

> I am not jealous of another woman. That would
be almost comforting. I am jealous of drainage, education, temperance,
your tenants, your committees, and the entire population of three
counties. They have had the best of you. I have
had what was left after dinner, when you were too
tired to speak and too conscientious to go to bed.

Sir Cloud passed a hand over his forehead. It was
true that his obligations had multiplied. A man did not
inherit twelve thousand acres, influence in the county, a seat
upon seven boards and the chairmanship of two national societies
merely to cultivate the society of the wife of his
bosom. Gertrude herself had once admired—

Once. The word intruded disagreeably.

> You will call me selfish. Perhaps I am. You
will point out that thousands depend upon you. Perhaps they
do. But I depended upon you too, and you never
noticed. I can't stand being the one person for whom
Sir Cloud Malpas has no appointment in his book.

He lowered the letter. In the street a seller cried
violets; the sound rose thinly between the houses. He remembered
that Gertrude had worn violets when they first drove together
in Rome. She had been twenty-three, capricious, brilliant, a little
frightened by her happiness. Had he remembered that yesterday? Had
he remembered anything yesterday except the memorandum on rural sanitation?

> Don't imagine there is somebody else. There isn't. I
should almost like there to be, if only to give
you a proper grievance. I am going alone. Florence is
large enough to contain a woman who has ceased to
be useful, and I have friends who will ask no
questions until I am ready to answer them.
>
> Our marriage is over. It has not ended dramatically;
it has simply been neglected to death. You will be
shocked for an hour, wounded for a day, and relieved
before the next committee meets.
>
> Gertrude.

Relieved!

The insult brought him to his feet. Relieved! She could
believe him capable of— Yet the violence of his protest
was checked by a sound in the hall.

A woman's voice. Gertrude's.

He thrust the letter into the inside pocket of his
coat. Why he did so he could not afterwards have
explained. He had the sensation of a statesman surprised with
a secret dispatch: the fact must be concealed until its
consequences were understood.

The door opened.

Gertrude entered quickly, still wearing her travelling coat. Her face,
generally so composed, held an anxiety she failed to disguise;
but at sight of him she stopped.

"Cloud! I thought you were at Whitehall."

"The meeting was postponed."

She glanced at the escritoire.

"I see."

There was a pause. Sir Cloud perceived with extraordinary clearness
that the next ten seconds might determine the remainder of
his life. He must accuse her, demand an explanation, appeal
to her reason. But if he did so she would
say what the letter had already said; and he, with
all his experience of hostile committees, possessed no answer.

He put one hand upon the back of a chair.

"Are you ill?" she asked.

"No. That is—perhaps. I felt suddenly faint."

"Faint?"

Her anxiety changed its object so swiftly that he was
ashamed. She came to him and touched his wrist, then
his forehead. The familiar coolness of her fingers awakened in
him a tenderness more alarming than anger.

"You are overworked," she said. "I have told you so."

"Have you?"

"Often."

Often! There it was again: the whole invisible history of
warnings given and not heard.

He sat down, because to remain standing beneath her concerned
gaze seemed impossible. She knelt beside him—not romantically, but to
loosen the button of his stiff collar—and for a moment
they were husband and wife as they had been before
public dinners, county duties, congratulatory addresses and all the apparatus
of importance had risen between them.

"Gertrude," he said, "suppose we went away?"

"Away?"

"For a holiday. A real holiday. No secretaries. No deputations.
Italy, perhaps."

Her hand became still.

"Italy?"

"Or anywhere you choose. For three months—four. I could resign
the commission temporarily. Bargrave could deal with the tenants. We
have not been alone together since—well, it is an unconscionable
time."

She looked at him with suspicion, hope and something like
fear.

"What has happened?"

"Nothing," he said; and the letter seemed to burn against
his breast. "That is precisely the trouble. Nothing has happened
to us for years."

Her eyes filled. Gertrude, who could command a dinner-table of
twenty and freeze an impertinent duchess at twelve paces, bent
her head over his hand.

"You mean it?"

"My dear, I have rarely meant anything so much."

It was not a complete reconciliation; complete reconciliations belong chiefly
to the theatre. But when she rose, she kissed him.
And he, holding her for an instant, experienced a sort
of hope of a sort of happiness—uncertain, undeserved, but alive.

That evening the Bargraves dined at Malpas House.

Lord Bargrave was Gertrude's cousin, a thick-set man with a
red face, a sound digestion and a reputation for idleness
which concealed considerable practical intelligence. After dinner he and Sir
Cloud withdrew to the smoking-room. Bargrave selected a cigar, rejected
two others, and waited until the servant had gone.

"Well?" he said. "You look as if the Cabinet had
fallen."

Sir Cloud stood before the mantelpiece.

"Gertrude intended to leave me."

Bargrave's match stopped halfway to his cigar.

"Intended?"

"She wrote a letter. I found it this afternoon. She
returned unexpectedly."

"And you had it out?"

"No."

Bargrave lit his cigar and contemplated the smoke.

"Then what the devil did you do?"

"I pretended to feel unwell."

"Excellent."

"It was not excellent. It was contemptible."

"It saved the situation?"

"For the moment."

"Then it was excellent."

Sir Cloud walked to the window. "She says I have
neglected her."

"You have."

The promptitude of the answer offended him.

"My public responsibilities—"

"Damn your public responsibilities."

"Bargrave!"

"My dear Cloud, the poor of England survived before you
were born. With care, they may survive three months without
you."

"This is not a jest."

"No. That is why I am not laughing." Bargrave settled
deeper into his chair. "You fellows who improve mankind are
all alike. You give your minds to the nation, your
stomachs to public dinners and your tempers to your wives.
Then you wonder why domestic gratitude is not unanimous."

Sir Cloud winced.

"She proposed Italy," he said after a moment.

"Did she?"

"In the letter. I proposed it afterwards."

Bargrave's eyebrows rose. "And she thinks you did not read
the letter?"

"I believe so."

"Then let her think it."

Sir Cloud turned. "You advise deliberate deception?"

"I advise marriage. It frequently requires it."

"There can be no permanent understanding founded upon a lie."

"There can be no permanent understanding founded upon telling a
woman you have secretly read her farewell letter and then
allowed her to suppose you hadn't."

"It was addressed to me."

"Unsealed?"

"Yes."

"Left by accident?"

"I presume so."

"Then legally, morally and metaphysically you may be blameless. Domestically
you are in danger of behaving like an ass."

Sir Cloud looked severe, but Bargrave continued.

"She came back. Why?"

"I do not know."

"Good. Don't ask. She returned; you offered what she wanted;
she accepted. There's Providence enough in that for any reasonable
man."

"But honesty—"

"Honesty is a fine wine. Don't pour the whole cellar
over dinner."

The phrase shocked Sir Cloud's principles and relieved his mind.

Bargrave leaned forward. "Take her away. Miss your committees. Let
a few demagogues remain unreconciled. Learn what she eats for
breakfast. And never, while either of you lives, mention that
letter."

Sir Cloud drew it from his pocket.

"This letter?"

"The very corpse. Burn it."

He held it over the fire, hesitated, then replaced it
in his pocket.

"I cannot."

"Why not?"

"It belongs to her."

Bargrave sighed. "Conscience has ruined better men than you, Cloud."

Upstairs, in Gertrude's dressing-room, Lady Bargrave was receiving another confidence.

"I was on my way to Victoria," Gertrude said. "I
had actually reached Sloane Street when I remembered that I
had not sealed it."

Lady Bargrave, who was small, pale and gifted with the
useful appearance of understanding everything, asked, "The letter?"

"Yes. I had left it on my desk for Cloud.
Parkes or the housemaid might have read it. One can
leave one's husband, Evelyn, but one cannot allow the servants
to discuss the reasons below stairs."

"So you came back?"

"I came back to seal it—or destroy it. Then I
found Cloud there."

"Had he read it?"

"No. Thank Heaven."

"How can you be sure?"

"It was exactly where I left it."

Gertrude crossed to the fire. From beneath a glove-box she
took several folded sheets.

"I slipped it away while he was ill."

"Was he ill?"

Gertrude paused.

"He looked ill. And he said he felt faint. Cloud
does not invent such things."

Lady Bargrave made no comment.

"He asked me to go abroad with him," Gertrude continued.
"To Italy, of all places. It was so strange—almost as
though— But no. He could not have read it. If
he had, he would have been magnificent and injured. He
would have forgiven me in paragraphs."

"Will you go?"

"Yes."

"And the separation?"

Gertrude looked at the pages in her hand.

"I meant every word when I wrote them. That is
the dreadful part. But when I saw him sitting there,
exhausted and helpless— Cloud has never been helpless in his
life—I remembered things I had omitted. One always omits something
from a final letter."

"What things?"

"Oh, nothing that could be argued. Rome. The violets he
used to bring me. The absurd room in Amalfi where
the rain came through the ceiling. His face when our
first child died." She folded the letter once more, though
it needed no folding. "Facts, I suppose, but not evidence."

"And you believe it can begin again?"

"No. Things never begin again. They may continue differently."

She placed the first sheet upon the fire. Flame travelled
eagerly across the paper, turning accusation, sarcasm and despair into
a black lace which trembled and fell.

Lady Bargrave watched her.

"You won't tell him?"

"That I meant to leave? Certainly not. Why should I
punish him with a thing I have decided not to
do?"

The last page curled upon itself. For an instant the
signature remained visible; then it too vanished.

Below, Sir Cloud entered the drawing-room with Bargrave behind him.
Gertrude stood near the hearth. Husband and wife looked at
one another across the composed elegance of the room, each
burdened by a secret, each believing the other mercifully ignorant.

"Cloud," she said, "Evelyn tells me the lakes are beautiful
in October."

"Then we shall see them in October."

"And Florence afterwards?"

"If you wish it."

She smiled—not the social smile with which Lady Malpas rewarded
agreeable strangers, but the hesitant smile of a young woman
who had once worn violets in Rome.

Sir Cloud went to her. In his pocket, the letter
remained intact; in the grate upstairs, its ashes were already
cooling. Between those two versions of the truth their future
precariously established itself.

It was not honesty, perhaps, as Sir Cloud would have
defined honesty from a platform. It was not even understanding.
But for the first time in many years, when Gertrude
spoke, he listened; and when he offered her his arm,
she took it.

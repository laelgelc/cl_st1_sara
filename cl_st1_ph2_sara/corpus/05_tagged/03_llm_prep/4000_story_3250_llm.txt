# The Roly-Poly Kitten

Once upon a time there was an old, old house
in a windy lane, with thick stone walls, crooked chimneys,
and so many cupboards that nobody knew where all of
them led.

Mrs. Tabitha Twitchit lived there with her three kittens—Tom, Moppet,
and Mittens.

On ordinary days the kittens ran upstairs and downstairs, and
in and out of the garden, until their pinafores were
torn and their little soft paws were exceedingly dirty.

But this was not an ordinary day.

It was baking day.

Mrs. Tabitha had borrowed yeast from Cousin Ribby, and she
had bought currants, sugar, and a large pat of yellow
butter. She had tied on a clean apron and brushed
the flour from the kitchen table.

"You must be good kittens," she said. "You must keep
out of the kitchen, and you must not climb the
dresser, and you must not touch the dough."

"We will be good," said Moppet.

"Very good," said Mittens.

Tom said nothing. He was looking at the chimney.

Mrs. Tabitha did not trust this silence.

She put Moppet and Mittens into a large cupboard in
the best bedroom. It was a comfortable cupboard, with folded
blankets at the bottom and a little round hole in
the door.

Then she looked for Tom.

Tom was not under the table.

He was not behind the curtains.

He was not inside the flour bin, although there were
paw marks upon the lid.

"Tom!" called Mrs. Tabitha.

There was a faint scrambling in the chimney.

Mrs. Tabitha ran into the parlour. She saw two little
black hind paws disappearing above the fender.

"Come down at once!" she cried.

But Tom went up, and up, and up.

He climbed past the iron hook where a kettle had
once hung. He squeezed through a narrow bend and reached
a shelf of brick inside the chimney. He was pleased
with himself, although his blue jacket was covered with soot.

"Tom Twitchit, I shall fetch you presently!" called his mother
from below. "You will spoil your clothes!"

Tom curled his tail over his paws and pretended not
to hear.

It is a most unwise thing to climb inside an
old chimney. A new chimney generally goes straight upward; but
an old chimney may go sideways, or divide in two,
or end suddenly over a dark hole. It may also
contain soot, jackdaws' nests, loose bricks, and other things which
are better left alone.

Mrs. Tabitha returned to the kitchen, because the dough was
rising over the side of the bowl.

She kneaded it and kneaded it. Then she placed it
near the fire and went upstairs to release Moppet and
Mittens.

The cupboard door was open.

The blankets were tumbled.

The kittens were gone.

"Oh! Oh! Oh!" exclaimed Mrs. Tabitha. "Three kittens missing on
baking day!"

She searched beneath the beds and inside the washstand. She
looked behind the wainscot where a board had come loose.
She opened six cupboards and a trunk full of winter
clothes.

At last Moppet came creeping down the passage. Her whiskers
were white with flour.

"Where have you been?" demanded Mrs. Tabitha.

"A lady rat came through the skirting-board," said Moppet. "She
took a piece of dough. A great piece, Mother! She
rolled it along like a pillow."

"A rat in my best bedroom!" cried Mrs. Tabitha.

Mittens appeared from behind a chest. She was carrying a
currant between her teeth.

"There was a gentleman rat," she said. "A 'normous gentleman
rat. He took the butter, and then he came back
for the rolling-pin."

"The rolling-pin!"

Mrs. Tabitha hurried downstairs.

The bowl beside the fire was half empty. The butter
had vanished. The rolling-pin was gone. A thin white trail
of flour ran across the floor and disappeared beneath the
dresser.

Mrs. Tabitha sat down upon a kitchen chair.

"This house is haunted," she said.

Just then there came a knock at the back door.

It was Cousin Ribby, wearing a shawl and carrying a
small basket.

"I have brought another spoonful of yeast," said Ribby. "Why
are you sitting down, Cousin Tabitha? The oven is nearly
hot."

"Tom is in the chimney. Moppet and Mittens escaped from
the cupboard. A lady rat has stolen my dough, and
an enormous gentleman rat has taken the butter and the
rolling-pin!"

Ribby set down her basket.

"Then we must search," she said.

Ribby was a practical cat. She did not believe the
house was haunted. She believed in rats.

They searched the kitchen first.

They looked beneath the dresser, behind the coal scuttle, and
inside the pantry. They found a piece of string, three
nutshells, a little pile of bones, and one of Mrs.
Tabitha's teaspoons. But they did not find the rolling-pin.

They searched the parlour.

They searched the bedrooms.

They searched the box room, where there were broken chairs,
a birdcage without a door, a cracked warming-pan, bundles of
lavender, and an old rocking-horse with only one ear.

"Tom!" called Mrs. Tabitha into every fireplace.

No Tom answered.

Presently, while they were standing beneath the attic stairs, they
heard a queer noise.

Roly-poly, roly—

Roly, poly, roly—

It came from somewhere inside the wall.

Ribby placed one ear against the plaster.

"That is a rolling-pin," she said.

"It cannot be!" replied Mrs. Tabitha.

Roly-poly, roly—

Bump!

There was a shower of dust from the ceiling.

Moppet and Mittens began to cry.

"The rats are rolling Mother's dough!" said Moppet.

"With Mother's butter!" said Mittens.

"And where is my Tom?" wailed Mrs. Tabitha.

To explain where Tom was, it is necessary to go
back to the time when he had been sitting upon
the brick shelf inside the chimney.

Tom had grown tired of soot.

He decided to climb down by another passage, which appeared
to lead toward the attic. He put one paw into
a crack, then another. A loose brick moved beneath him.

Tom slipped.

He fell through a narrow flue, bounced upon a sloping
board, tore one sleeve upon a nail, and landed in
a heap of soot and cobwebs.

For a moment he could see nothing.

Then he heard somebody say, very politely, "Dear me, Anna
Maria! Something has come down the chimney."

Tom opened his eyes.

He was in a hidden room between the walls.

It had a low roof supported by rafters. The floor
was covered with stolen objects: candle ends, ribbons, spoons, chicken
bones, lace, crusts, buttons, nutshells, and bits of paper. In
one corner stood a doll's tea table. In another was
a little bed made from an old muff.

An enormous grey rat stood before Tom.

He wore a shabby coat with brass buttons. His whiskers
were long, and his expression was exceedingly dignified.

This was Samuel Whiskers.

Behind him appeared his wife, Anna Maria, carrying a piece
of dough in her front paws. She was a thin
brown rat in a striped gown and a white cap.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A kitten," said Samuel Whiskers.

"How inconvenient."

Tom tried to stand up.

Samuel Whiskers placed one foot upon the tail of Tom's
jacket.

"Do not hurry away, young gentleman."

"I must go home," said Tom. "My mother is baking."

"So are we," said Anna Maria.

She looked Tom over from ears to boots.

"He is plump," she observed.

"He is rather sooty," said Samuel.

"Soot may be brushed off."

Tom's fur stood on end.

"I am not anything to bake!"

Samuel Whiskers smiled.

"That," he said, "is a matter of cookery."

The rats took hold of Tom. He scratched and kicked,
but Samuel Whiskers was very strong. Anna Maria fetched a
long piece of string from beneath the doll's table.

They tied Tom's paws.

They tied his elbows.

They tied his tail, because it kept lashing Samuel upon
the nose.

A large cellar spider came down from a beam to
inspect the knots. He walked around Tom twice, critically. Then
he climbed up again without offering assistance.

"String is troublesome," said Anna Maria. "It may not be
digestible."

"We shall remove it before serving," replied Samuel.

"You said that about the parcel paper, and you forgot."

"My dear Anna Maria, this is not the time to
discuss parcel paper."

They carried Tom to another chamber beneath the attic floor.
Here the missing rolling-pin lay beside the pat of butter.
The chamber smelled of flour and mice.

Anna Maria spread the dough flat.

Samuel Whiskers took a knife and cut the buttons from
Tom's jacket.

"Those certainly are indigestible," he said.

"They are my best buttons!" cried Tom.

"Then we shall keep them carefully."

Anna Maria rubbed butter upon Tom's coat.

It was cold, greasy butter, and it stuck the soot
into dreadful little lumps.

"More beneath the chin," said Samuel.

"I know how to butter a pudding," snapped Anna Maria.

Tom wriggled, but the string held fast.

They laid him upon the dough.

Anna Maria pulled one side over him. Samuel pulled the
other. Tom's head remained out at one end, and his
boots stuck out at the other.

"He is too long," said Anna Maria.

"Fold his knees."

"He will not fold."

Samuel Whiskers pressed Tom with the rolling-pin.

Roly-poly, roly—

Tom squealed.

Above him Mrs. Tabitha and Ribby were searching the attic.

Roly, poly, roly—

Tom squealed again.

"Did you hear that?" asked Mrs. Tabitha.

"I heard something," said Ribby. "It came from under the
floor."

They moved a chest and rolled back a dusty carpet.
The boards beneath were broad and black with age. There
was no trapdoor.

Ribby scratched at a crack.

From far below came a muffled voice.

"M-m-mother!"

Mrs. Tabitha gave such a cry that Moppet and Mittens
ran into a basket and hid beneath the washing.

"My kitten is under the floor!"

"We require a tradesman," said Ribby.

John Joiner lived across the lane. He was a large
dog who wore a leather apron and carried tools in
a wooden box. He came at once when Ribby fetched
him.

"There are rats beneath the boards," said Ribby.

"There is a pudding beneath the boards!" cried Mrs. Tabitha.
"And I fear it is Tom!"

John Joiner sniffed the cracks.

"Stand aside, ma'am."

He took out a saw.

The saw went scree—scree—scree through the floorboard.

Then John scratched with his strong dog claws.

Scratch, scratch, scratch!

Beneath the floor, Samuel Whiskers stopped rolling.

"That is a dog," he said.

"It is a carpenter," said Anna Maria.

"It sounds like a dog."

"It scratches like a carpenter."

"No respectable carpenter scratches in that manner."

The saw cut deeper.

Scree—scree—scree!

Dust and splinters fell into the hidden chamber.

Samuel Whiskers dropped the rolling-pin upon Tom's toes.

"We must remove ourselves," he said.

"What about the pudding?"

"Leave it."

"What about the butter?"

"Leave that also."

Anna Maria was horrified.

But John Joiner scratched again, and this time one of
his claws came through the ceiling.

Samuel Whiskers seized a bag of currants. Anna Maria snatched
the best teaspoons. They ran through a jagged opening behind
a rafter and vanished into the chimney passages.

John Joiner lifted the sawn board.

Ribby held a candle over the hole.

There lay a long, grey, exceedingly dirty dumpling.

It had Tom's ears.

"It is my son!" cried Mrs. Tabitha.

John Joiner reached down and lifted Tom out by the
dough around his middle. Tom was greasy, black, floury, tied
with string, and very much ashamed.

Moppet and Mittens peeped from the washing basket.

"Is that Tom?" whispered Moppet.

"He looks like supper," whispered Mittens.

Mrs. Tabitha carried him downstairs.

First she cut the string. Then she pulled away the
dough. Then she put Tom into a hot bath in
the washhouse.

It required three kettles of water, two pieces of soap,
and a nailbrush to remove the soot and butter.

Tom objected loudly to the nailbrush.

"You should have considered that before climbing the chimney," said
Mrs. Tabitha.

Cousin Ribby washed his blue jacket. John Joiner found two
brass buttons among the rubbish below the floor, but the
third was never recovered.

As for the dough, Mrs. Tabitha refused to waste it.

She picked out the string and the worst of the
soot. She kneaded in extra flour, sugar, and a generous
handful of currants.

"The currants will conceal the specks," said Ribby.

The pudding was baked separately in a small brown dish.

Tom would not taste it.

He sat wrapped in a blanket by the fire, staring
at every hole in the skirting-board. Whenever a coal settled
in the grate, he jumped.

The next morning John Joiner returned with boards, nails, and
plaster. He mended the attic floor. He blocked three passages
and repaired the loose wainscot.

But an old house has many ways through it, if
one is small enough and has sharp teeth.

Samuel Whiskers and Anna Maria were not caught.

I know where they went, because I saw them myself
that evening. They crossed the lane behind the stone wall.
Samuel carried the bag of currants over one shoulder, and
Anna Maria carried two teaspoons and the missing brass button.

They travelled to Farmer Potatoes's barn.

It was a warm, disorderly barn, with sacks of meal,
bins of barley, a haymow, and a wooden floor full
of holes. Samuel Whiskers declared it an excellent residence.

Anna Maria declared that there were not enough cupboards.

Nevertheless, they stayed.

They had children, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren—rats, and rats, and
rats! They ate the grain. They nibbled the harness. They
made nests in the hay and tunnels beneath the meal
bins. Farmer Potatoes set traps until he had no traps
left to set.

Moppet and Mittens grew into handsome cats.

They had learned something from the adventure, although not quite
the lesson their mother intended. They became celebrated rat-catchers. Farmer
Potatoes paid them a penny for every rat, and sometimes
gave them a saucer of cream besides.

They worked in the barn, the stable, and the granary.
Mittens watched beside the sacks. Moppet climbed among the rafters.
Neither was afraid of Samuel Whiskers nor of Anna Maria,
though those two old rats remained very cunning.

Tom also grew up.

He grew larger than either of his sisters, and his
whiskers were extremely fine. But he never climbed another chimney.
He never entered a dark cupboard. He disliked rolling-pins, buttered
pastry, string, spiders, scratching noises, and currant pudding.

And although he was a cat, he would never confront
a rat.

Not a large rat.

Not a little rat.

Not even a very young rat with short whiskers.

Indeed, after being rolled in dough beneath the attic floor,
Tom Twitchit would face nothing more alarming than—

A Mouse.

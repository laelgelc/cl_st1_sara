# Nine Hundred Horses

The yellow fog had come down upon London before four
o'clock, and at five it was thick enough to turn
the lamps in Piccadilly into blurred orange moons. Motor horns
cried in it. The wheels of hansoms went by with
a soft grinding sound. Men emerged suddenly from the vapor,
appeared to hesitate at the curb, and vanished again.

Douglas Hargrave, who had arrived from New York three days
before, stood at a window of the Metropolitan Club and
regarded this spectacle with the tolerant interest of an American
who has read about London fogs but has never before
swallowed one.

Behind him Sir Henry Marquis struck the newspaper with the
back of his hand.

"It is mystery in every direction!" he exclaimed.

Hargrave turned from the window.

Sir Henry was seated before a small table in the
visitors' room. He was a baronet, a clubman, and, what
was much more important, the chief of the Criminal Investigation
Department. He was a big, energetic man of fifty, with
a red face, short gray mustache, and heavy shoulders that
strained the seams of his tweed coat. His appearance suggested
an English country gentleman who had come to town to
buy a horse. It did not suggest the director of
a department concerned with murderers, swindlers, counterfeiters, anarchists, international thieves,
and other persons who make travel interesting for the police.

He pointed to a notice in the personal column.

"Read that, Hargrave."

The American took the paper.

The notice was printed among the usual requests for missing
relatives, abandoned wives, discreet loans, lost dogs, and gentlemen desiring
correspondence. It ran:

> TRAINER.—Nine hundred horses leave New York by the usual
road. The stable is prepared. Attend carefully to the second
feeding. Brown groom has instructions. Answer through the old channel.

Hargrave read it twice.

"Well?" demanded Sir Henry.

"It seems clear enough. Somebody is shipping horses."

"Nine hundred?"

"It is a large order."

"From New York to Europe without naming the port?"

"Perhaps the port has been agreed on."

"Then why advertise?"

Hargrave returned the paper.

"I don't know."

"Neither do I," said Sir Henry. "And that is the
devil of it."

A steward entered with whisky and soda. Sir Henry waited
until the man had gone, then pulled his chair nearer
the table.

"This notice appeared yesterday in New York, Paris, Brussels, and
London. To-day it appears again. Word for word. It has
also been telegraphed to Amsterdam. My people picked it up
because the New York police asked us to watch for
any communication containing the words 'nine hundred horses.' They have
reason to believe a shipment left America last Thursday."

"A shipment of horses?"

"My dear fellow, if they were horses, I should not
be sitting here."

Sir Henry filled his glass.

"The message is a cipher," he continued. "That much is
certain. The words are innocent, but the arrangement is not.
'Usual road,' 'second feeding,' 'brown groom,' 'old channel'—all of it
means something. The question is what?"

Hargrave smiled.

"You have cipher men."

"I have cipher men until I need them. Then one
has influenza, one is in Scotland, and the best of
them has gone to Geneva to explain to a committee
why no two governments should use the same diplomatic code.
I have sent for Professor Vassiloff."

"The criminologist?"

"The human dictionary. He speaks seventeen languages, reads thirty, and
swears in all of them. If there is a cipher
in that notice, he will find it."

"And the nine hundred horses?"

Sir Henry frowned at the paper.

"That is what disturbs me. Nine hundred of anything is
important. Nine hundred rifles, nine hundred bombs, nine hundred forged
passports, nine hundred tins of opium—my word, Hargrave, there are
possibilities! But nine hundred horses!"

Hargrave walked back to the window.

He was a tall young man, clean-shaven, quiet in dress
and manner, and perhaps too deliberate in speech to satisfy
an impatient Englishman. He was not, as people commonly supposed
of jewel dealers, a romantic adventurer. The fact is, there
is very little romance in the ordinary business of precious
stones.

A jeweler does not spend his life crawling through ruined
temples after diamonds the size of pigeons' eggs. He sits
at a desk. He examines parcels under a lens. He
weighs stones, estimates color, studies flaws, examines certificates, considers fashion,
and worries about credit. If he is a dealer of
standing, half his time is occupied in refusing bad merchandise
offered by persons who assure him that their grandfathers were
princes.

Hargrave was in London on just such practical business. His
firm in New York dealt in unset stones. He had
come over to attend several auctions, meet a Dutch cutter,
and inspect a private collection in Bond Street. He had
known Sir Henry for two years, having once given evidence
in an affair involving a stolen emerald and an American
insurance company.

"Perhaps," said Hargrave, "the word horses has no significance."

"In a cipher every word may have significance."

"Or none."

"Exactly! Mystery in every direction."

The door opened. A page entered.

"Mr. Hargrave, sir. A lady is asking for you on
the telephone."

"For me?"

"Yes, sir. She said it was urgent."

Hargrave looked at Sir Henry.

"You see," said the Englishman, "you are already becoming a
London celebrity."

Hargrave went to the telephone booth in the corridor.

The voice that addressed him was unmistakably American. It was
also brisk, hard, and impatient.

"Is this Douglas Hargrave?"

"Yes."

"Of Hargrave, Stimson and Bell, New York?"

"Yes."

"This is Mrs. Farmingham. Mrs. Jason Farmingham, of Chicago. You
know the name."

Hargrave did know it. Everybody concerned with American finance knew
the name of Jason Farmingham. He had built railways, bought
mines, organized steel companies, and died leaving his widow a
fortune which newspapers described in figures and relatives described as
insufficient.

"I have heard the name, of course," Hargrave replied.

"I am at the Carlton Imperial. Come over immediately."

There was a pause. Hargrave did not like commands from
strangers.

"May I ask the nature of the business?"

"Jewels."

"I have an appointment at six."

"Cancel it."

"That may not be possible."

"Then come before six. I don't discuss jewels over a
telephone. Ask for Mrs. Farmingham's private sitting room."

The receiver clicked.

Hargrave came out of the booth with a thoughtful expression.

"Business?" Sir Henry inquired.

"Apparently."

"Man or woman?"

"A woman."

"Ah! Then it is not business."

"It sounded very much like business."

Sir Henry laughed.

"Who is she?"

"Mrs. Jason Farmingham, of Chicago."

Sir Henry stopped laughing.

"The railway widow?"

"So she says."

"My word! She is worth millions."

"Perhaps that is why she expects people to cancel appointments."

The page brought Hargrave his coat and hat.

"I shall see Vassiloff at eight," said Sir Henry. "Come
to my office afterward, if you are free. I may
have the destination of our mysterious stable by then."

"If Mrs. Farmingham permits me."

"Be careful. American widows are dangerous."

"So are cipher experts."

Then Hargrave went out.

---

The Carlton Imperial was less than ten minutes away, but
the fog doubled the time. Hargrave walked along the Strand
until he found a cab. The driver protested against the
journey, accepted it, lost his direction twice, and finally set
Hargrave down before the wrong hotel. Twenty minutes later the
American arrived at the Carlton Imperial.

Mrs. Farmingham occupied a suite on the third floor.

A maid opened the door. She was a thin, colorless
woman with lowered eyes and a mouth like a closed
purse.

"Mr. Hargrave?"

"Yes."

"Come in."

She took his coat and showed him into a large
sitting room furnished in the heavy hotel manner. There were
red curtains, red chairs, gilt tables, and an excessive number
of lamps. A fire burned on the hearth. Before it
stood a large woman in black.

She turned sharply.

"Hargrave?"

"Yes."

"I'm Mrs. Farmingham."

She did not offer her hand.

She was perhaps fifty, though her abundant gray hair made
her look older. Her face was broad, pale, and strong.
Heavy diamonds flashed in her ears. A long rope of
pearls hung over her black dress. She carried a gold
lorgnette, and when she moved it the motion was as
of one who cracks a dog whip.

Hargrave had never seen the late Jason Farmingham's widow, but
the woman before him corresponded well enough with photographs he
vaguely remembered: massive, rich, dominant, and entirely accustomed to obedience.

"You are mighty young," she said.

"I am thirty-two."

"That's young."

"It is not usually a disqualification in my business."

"We'll find out."

She pointed with the lorgnette.

"Sit there."

Hargrave sat.

Mrs. Farmingham remained standing.

"I want sapphires," she said. "Not jewelry. Stones. Good stones.
I don't buy junk."

"Neither do I."

"Don't be smart."

"I beg your pardon."

"I have no time for politeness. A man here has
sapphires. He says they are worth twenty-five thousand dollars. He'll
take eighteen. I want to know if they are genuine."

"Why does he not take them to a London dealer?"

"Because I don't want a London dealer."

"That does not answer the question."

For the first time her expression altered. It did not
soften; it merely became more attentive.

"You ask questions."

"It is part of my profession."

"All right. The man is a Romanian officer. Major Mikos.
He has to leave England to-night. Family trouble, political trouble,
military trouble—I don't know and I don't care. He needs
gold. Not a draft. Not a check. Gold."

"Eighteen thousand dollars in gold is a considerable weight."

"I know that."

"And American gold is not commonly used in London transactions."

"I know that too."

Hargrave looked at her.

Mrs. Farmingham cracked the lorgnette against her palm.

"You think there's something queer."

"I think the circumstances are unusual."

"Of course they are unusual. If they were usual I
would buy the stones through Tiffany's."

"Where is Major Mikos?"

"At another hotel. A wretched little place near Trafalgar Square.
He won't come here."

"Why not?"

"Pride. Honor. Foreign notions. He doesn't want to be seen
selling his family property."

"Yet he is willing to sell it to you."

"He was introduced by Baron Lasker in Paris."

Hargrave knew the name of Baron Lasker. He was a
financier of excellent standing.

"Did Baron Lasker recommend the stones?"

"No. He merely said Major Mikos belonged to a good
family. Stop dragging this out. I am going to see
the man now. You are coming."

She crossed to a bell and pressed it.

"I have an appointment at six," Hargrave said.

"You told me."

"It is nearly six now."

"Send word."

"My appointment is with a bank."

"Then they'll wait. Banks always wait when money is coming
and close when money is wanted."

The maid entered carrying Hargrave's coat.

"Car's below," she said.

Mrs. Farmingham put on a sable cloak.

"Come along."

Hargrave rose. The whole matter offended his professional caution, but
it stimulated his professional interest. A parcel of sapphires worth
twenty-five thousand dollars was not offered every afternoon, even in
London. Moreover, if the stones were genuine, there was a
reasonable profit in the transaction. Mrs. Farmingham had not yet
spoken of his commission, but that could be arranged.

He put on his coat.

Mrs. Farmingham nodded as if he had passed a minor
test.

"That's sensible," she said.

Then they went out.

---

The motor waited at the curb, its lamps making two
pale tunnels in the fog.

"Get in," said Mrs. Farmingham.

Hargrave got in. The maid followed, and Mrs. Farmingham placed
herself beside him. She spoke through the tube to the
driver.

"Danton Hotel. And don't crawl."

The motor moved off.

Hargrave asked, "Have you made inquiries concerning Major Mikos?"

"I made inquiries in Paris."

"Of whom?"

"Bankers."

"What did they say?"

"They said he had an account."

"How large?"

"That was none of their business."

"It might be ours."

Mrs. Farmingham made the dog-whip motion with the lorgnette.

"The French are stupid. I wrote my bankers in Paris
to give Major Mikos five thousand dollars if he called
for it. He called. They gave it to him. Then,
when I asked whether he was good for twenty-five thousand,
they said they knew nothing about his credit. Stupid!"

"They obeyed your instructions."

"Exactly. Stupid people always obey instructions and think that finishes
the matter."

Hargrave glanced at her. The remark was characteristic. It was
dominant, aggressive, and unreasonable, but there are wealthy persons who
consider reason a device invented by clerks.

"What became of the five thousand dollars?" he asked.

"He repaid it."

"When?"

"This morning."

"In what form?"

"Notes. English notes. Why?"

"No special reason."

"Then don't ask questions with no special reason."

The car turned, slowed, and nearly struck a cab. Mrs.
Farmingham rapped on the front glass.

"Drive!"

"He cannot see," Hargrave observed.

"He can see enough to stop. He can see enough
to go."

The maid said nothing. She had said nothing since leaving
the hotel. Her hands lay folded in her lap, and
her dull eyes remained fixed on them.

After fifteen minutes the motor entered a narrow street behind
Trafalgar Square and stopped before the Danton Hotel.

The Danton was not wretched, as Mrs. Farmingham had called
it. It was merely small, old, and respectable in a
resigned manner. A brass plate beside the entrance had been
polished until the name was almost rubbed away. The hall
smelled of coal smoke and boiled cabbage.

A clerk looked up.

"Major Mikos?" said Mrs. Farmingham.

"Second floor, madam. Number eighteen."

"No lift?"

"No, madam."

"Of course not."

She went up the stairs, breathing heavily but refusing assistance.
At number eighteen the maid knocked in a peculiar fashion—two
short taps, a pause, and one long tap.

The door opened.

A tall man stood within.

He wore a dark military coat buttoned to the throat.
Two small decorations hung from a ribbon on his breast.
He had a narrow face, black hair brushed straight back,
and a pointed mustache. His skin was pale. His eyes
were melancholy and watchful.

"Madame Farmingham," he said. "You have come."

"I said I would."

He bowed.

"This is Mr. Hargrave, the American jewel dealer," she continued.
"He will examine the stones."

The major's expression stiffened.

"A dealer?"

"An expert."

"You did not tell me."

"I'm telling you now."

For a moment Hargrave thought the man would refuse. Then
Major Mikos stepped back.

"Enter," he said.

The sitting room was poorly heated. A fire had been
laid but not lighted. On a table stood a tray
with a coffee pot, one cup, and an untouched roll.
Two military valises rested against the wall. A long traveling
coat had been thrown over a chair.

Major Mikos closed the door.

"Madame," he said, "this business is painful to me."

"Business usually is. Let's finish it."

He looked at Hargrave.

"These stones belonged to my mother."

"So you said," Mrs. Farmingham answered.

"They were given to her by Queen Elisabeth. They have
remained in my family for thirty-eight years. It is only
because I must depart England to-night that I consent—"

"Yes, yes. Where are they?"

The major's pale face flushed.

Hargrave intervened.

"Mrs. Farmingham means no discourtesy, Major. The hour is late,
and a proper examination will take time."

Mikos bowed to him.

"You speak with consideration, monsieur."

He crossed to one of the valises, knelt, and opened
it with a key carried on a chain around his
neck. From beneath several folded shirts he took a leather
case. He brought it to the table but kept one
hand upon it.

"Before I show them," he said, "there must be an
understanding. If they are purchased, I am paid eighteen thousand
American dollars in gold. Not notes. Not a banker's draft.
Gold."

"Why American gold?" Hargrave asked.

The major hesitated.

"Because I travel where English money may attract attention."

"American gold will attract more."

"It is my requirement."

Mrs. Farmingham struck the table with her lorgnette.

"We know the requirement. Show the stones."

Major Mikos opened the case.

There were seven sapphires.

They lay separately in shallow hollows lined with faded blue
velvet. Five were oval, one was square-cut, and the largest
was a deep cushion shape of remarkable color. Even in
the poor light it gave back a blue so rich
and living that Hargrave's professional indifference disappeared.

He bent over the case.

"Better light," he said.

The major lit two gas brackets and drew the table
near the window, though the fog outside gave little help.
Hargrave removed a small leather wallet from his pocket. From
it he took a lens, forceps, a scale, and several
slips of white card.

Mrs. Farmingham watched impatiently.

"Well?"

"I have not begun."

"They look genuine."

"Glass often does."

"I don't buy glass."

"Then let me make certain."

He began with the smallest stone. He examined the surface,
the facet edges, the color zoning, the inclusions, and the
response under different lights. He weighed each sapphire. He made
notes. He compared the largest stone with the others.

Major Mikos stood motionless beside the table. His ceremonial melancholy
did not alter, but a pulse beat visibly in his
temple.

Mrs. Farmingham moved about the room. She looked at the
clock, at the fog, at the traveling cases, and at
Hargrave. Every few minutes she asked a question.

"How long?"

"Are they right?"

"What is that mark?"

"Why are you weighing it again?"

Hargrave answered until he grew tired of answering. Then he
did not answer.

The stones were genuine.

More than that, they were excellent. Two probably came from
Ceylon, three might be Burmese, and the largest had the
deep, velvety blue which dealers loosely described as royal. There
were flaws. There are flaws in nearly all important stones,
whatever owners may say. But the flaws did not seriously
injure their beauty.

At last Hargrave put down his lens.

"They are sapphires," he said.

Mrs. Farmingham gave a short laugh.

"I knew that."

"Then you did not need me."

"Are they worth twenty-five thousand dollars?"

"Retail, perhaps more."

"What are they worth to buy?"

Hargrave looked at the stones again.

"Eighteen thousand is not unreasonable."

"Not unreasonable? I want a number."

"If they have not been stolen, if the title is
clear, and if there is no undisclosed claim against them,
I would be prepared to pay eighteen thousand for the
parcel."

Major Mikos drew himself up.

"Stolen!"

"It is a professional condition, Major, not an accusation."

"My family—"

"Save it," Mrs. Farmingham said. "He has to say those
things. Hargrave, you buy them."

Hargrave turned.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Buy them on your own hook. I'll take them from
you."

"That was not the arrangement."

"It is now."

"I understood that you wished me to authenticate them."

"I do. You have. Now buy them."

"Why do you not buy them yourself?"

"Because I haven't eighteen thousand dollars in American gold in
my handbag."

"Neither have I."

"You can get it."

"It is after banking hours."

"Your bank will open for you."

"No bank opens merely because I knock at the door."

Mrs. Farmingham smiled for the first time. It was not
a pleasant smile.

"Mine does."

She took a card from her purse and wrote several
words on it.

"Take that to the Anglo-American Bank in Bond Street. Mr.
Latham will give you the money."

"Against what security?"

"Against my name."

"And my receipt?"

"If he wants it."

Major Mikos closed the leather case.

"This is impossible," he said. "I leave from Victoria at
nine. If there is delay—"

"There won't be delay," said Mrs. Farmingham.

"There is also the matter of my train, the crossing,
my papers."

"You'll get your gold."

She faced Hargrave.

"You buy the stones for eighteen thousand. To-morrow morning I
pay you eighteen thousand six hundred."

Hargrave's eyebrows rose.

"A commission of six hundred dollars?"

"For an hour's work."

"For an advance of eighteen thousand dollars and some risk."

"Seven hundred."

"Mrs. Farmingham—"

"Eight hundred. That is my last word."

The transaction was irregular. It was hurried. It depended upon
identities Hargrave had not verified. It involved a foreign officer,
a wealthy widow, a hotel sitting room, and a demand
for gold at an hour when ordinary men used checks.
Everything about it invited caution.

But the sapphires were genuine.

That was the central fact. Hargrave knew sapphires. He had
not trusted the major. He had not trusted Mrs. Farmingham.
He had trusted his lens, his scale, and his professional
experience. If the money were advanced and some difficulty afterward
arose, he would still possess stones worth more than the
amount paid.

As has been said, jewel dealers are not romantic men.
They are practical. Sometimes this makes them safer than other
people. Sometimes it does not.

"I will buy them," Hargrave said, "provided the bank delivers
the gold and Major Mikos gives me a written transfer
of title."

"It shall be done," the major replied.

Mrs. Farmingham cracked the lorgnette against her palm.

"Good. Now move."

---

At the Anglo-American Bank, the porter admitted them through a
side door.

Mr. Latham, the manager, had been detained by a telephone
message. He was a bald, courteous gentleman who displayed no
surprise at Mrs. Farmingham's request. Bank managers dealing with the
very rich train themselves not to display surprise. It is
one of the qualities for which they are paid.

"You require eighteen thousand dollars in American coin?" he said.

"In twenties," Mrs. Farmingham replied.

"We have the amount in reserve, but it will take
a few minutes to count."

"Then start counting."

Mr. Latham looked at Hargrave.

"The money is for Mr. Hargrave?"

"Yes," she said. "Charge it temporarily to my London account."

Hargrave spoke.

"I shall give the bank my receipt and indemnify Mrs.
Farmingham when the stones are transferred to her."

"You'll do nothing of the kind," she said. "I am
buying from you to-morrow."

Mr. Latham adjusted his spectacles.

"Perhaps the simplest course is to debit Mrs. Farmingham's account
now."

"No."

Her answer was immediate.

"No?" said the banker.

"No. Mr. Hargrave is buying the stones. I will buy
from him after I have had them mounted."

"That is not what you told me," Hargrave said.

"It's what I'm telling you now."

The banker looked from one to the other.

Mrs. Farmingham made an impatient gesture.

"Don't complicate a simple thing. Latham, give Mr. Hargrave the
gold against his receipt. To-morrow I give him the same
amount with eight hundred dollars commission. If he fails to
repay you, charge me."

Mr. Latham considered.

"That will be satisfactory."

To Hargrave it was less satisfactory. Still, the stones remained
the security, and their value had not changed.

Clerks brought the money in two stout canvas bags sealed
at the neck. Nine hundred twenty-dollar gold pieces make a
substantial parcel. They weigh more than an impatient buyer imagines.
Hargrave broke the seals and watched while two clerks counted
portions at random and checked the total against the bank's
register.

The coins were genuine. He handled several himself. American double
eagles have a familiar weight, ring, edge, and color. A
dealer who has spent years around gold develops a feeling
for the metal almost without knowing it.

Hargrave signed a receipt.

Mrs. Farmingham looked at the clock.

"Seven-fifteen. We have time."

The gold was placed in a small black portmanteau provided
by the bank. Hargrave kept the key.

They returned to the motor.

At the Danton Hotel, Major Mikos met them in the
hall wearing his traveling coat and gloves. His valises had
been brought downstairs.

"You have it?" he asked.

"We have it," Mrs. Farmingham said.

They went again to number eighteen.

The major locked the door. Hargrave placed the portmanteau on
the table and opened it. Mikos drew a sharp breath
when he saw the gold.

"You will count it," Hargrave said.

"All?"

"All."

The major hesitated.

"There is little time."

"There is enough time to count money."

Mrs. Farmingham laughed.

"You wanted gold. Now count it."

Mikos counted.

He arranged the coins in stacks of twenty, touching each
piece with quick white fingers. Hargrave watched him. The process
required nearly twenty minutes. Outside, the fog pressed against the
window. Somewhere below, a cabman shouted. The sound came faintly,
as if from a great distance.

At length the major completed the last stack.

"Nine hundred coins," he said. "Eighteen thousand dollars."

He placed them in his own leather traveling bag.

Hargrave produced the transfer he had asked a clerk at
the bank to type. It described the seven sapphires by
weight and general appearance and declared Major Stefan Mikos to
be their lawful owner, transferring all title to Douglas Hargrave
for the sum of eighteen thousand dollars.

Mikos read the document.

"My family name is here for any person to see."

"A document without a name is useless."

The major signed.

His hand trembled slightly.

Then he closed the leather jewel case and handed it
to Hargrave.

For one moment he did not release it.

"My mother wore the largest stone," he said. "I remember
it upon her breast when I was a child."

Mrs. Farmingham turned away with a sound of irritation.

Hargrave said quietly, "I shall take proper care of it."

The major released the case.

"It is finished."

He put on his hat, picked up the bag containing
the gold, and called for a porter. In the hall
he bowed formally to Mrs. Farmingham. Then he bowed to
Hargrave.

"Circumstance has made us merchants, monsieur. I hope it does
not make us dishonorable."

He followed the porter down the stairs.

Mrs. Farmingham watched until he disappeared.

"Foreigners," she said. "They can make a funeral out of
a receipt."

She held out her hand.

"Give me the stones."

"Not yet."

"What do you mean?"

"I paid for them."

"And I am buying them."

"To-morrow morning."

"I want them to-night."

Hargrave closed the jewel case and put it inside his
coat.

"You altered the arrangement at the bank. Under the present
arrangement the sapphires remain mine until I am paid."

Mrs. Farmingham's face became hard.

"You don't trust me."

"This has nothing to do with trust."

"Everything has to do with trust."

"In business, very little should."

For several seconds she stared at him. Then, unexpectedly, she
laughed.

"You're all right, Hargrave. Mighty young, but all right. Come
to my hotel."

"Why?"

"I'll pay you."

"The bank is closed."

"I have money at the hotel."

"In American gold?"

"Yes."

Hargrave was surprised.

"Why did you not bring it in the first place?"

"Because it did not arrive until this afternoon. It came
by special messenger from Paris."

"Eighteen thousand dollars?"

"Exactly."

"Then the entire trip to the bank was unnecessary."

"I didn't know the messenger had arrived until my maid
told me in the car."

Hargrave looked at the silent maid.

She nodded once.

Mrs. Farmingham continued, "The money is in a portmanteau at
the Carlton. I'll exchange it for the stones. Your commission
will be paid to-morrow by check."

"Why not pay it to-night?"

"Because my secretary has the checkbook, and she's at the
theater."

"Your bank could add it to the advance."

"My bank has already been bothered enough."

Hargrave almost smiled at this. It was Mrs. Farmingham who
had bothered the bank, but she spoke as if the
institution had brought the whole difficulty upon itself.

"Come along," she said.

They returned to the motor.

---

At the Carlton Imperial the sitting room appeared exactly as
Hargrave had left it, except that a black portmanteau now
stood near the fire.

Mrs. Farmingham removed her sable cloak.

"There's your money."

Hargrave knelt and examined the case.

It was of good leather, new, with brass locks. Mrs.
Farmingham handed him a key.

He opened it.

Inside, neatly packed in green baize trays, were American twenty-dollar
gold pieces.

Hargrave lifted one tray. Beneath it were others.

"You may count them," she said.

"I intend to."

"Of course you do."

Hargrave counted the coins by trays and rows. There were
fifty to a tray, except the last, which held precisely
the balance required. Nine hundred coins.

He selected one and looked at it under the lamp.
It bore the familiar eagle and date. He dropped it
lightly upon another. The ring appeared true, though the thick
carpet and baize dulled the sound.

Mrs. Farmingham walked up and down.

"You watched the bank count its money," she said. "You
watched Mikos count it. Now you count mine. We shall
be here until breakfast."

"Large sums should be counted."

"They should be trusted."

"You have an unusual faith in trust for a woman
who employed an expert to examine sapphires."

She made the dog-whip gesture.

"I told you, I don't buy junk."

Hargrave completed the count.

The weight of the portmanteau was right, so far as
a man lifting it might judge. The coins looked right.
The number was right.

He closed and locked the case.

"Here are your sapphires," he said.

Mrs. Farmingham took the jewel case and immediately opened it.
She examined the seven stones, not with knowledge but with
satisfaction. The largest sapphire caught the red glow of the
fire.

"That will make a necklace," she said.

"It might be better as a pendant."

"I don't like pendants."

"Then a necklace."

"I'll take them to Cartier in Paris."

"When do you leave?"

"To-morrow afternoon."

"My commission—"

"Nine-thirty to-morrow morning. Eight hundred dollars, as agreed."

"Very well."

She closed the case and gave it to the maid.

"Lock that in my bedroom trunk."

The maid left the room.

Mrs. Farmingham extended her hand at last.

"You have done good work."

Hargrave shook it.

"Then I shall expect good payment."

"You'll get it. And, Hargrave—"

"Yes?"

"Don't tell people about the major. Men forced to sell
family jewels deserve silence."

The request was reasonable. It was also the first generous
thing she had said.

"I do not discuss my clients' affairs."

"Good."

Hargrave picked up the portmanteau.

"Send for a porter," Mrs. Farmingham said. "You can't carry
that through the street."

"I shall take a cab."

The maid returned.

"I'll send James with him," Mrs. Farmingham said.

"That is unnecessary."

"Gold ought to be guarded."

"So you do not trust everyone?"

"I trust people when I can watch them."

She rang for a porter.

Five minutes later Hargrave left the Carlton Imperial with the
black portmanteau. The maid watched from the doorway while a
hotel man placed it in a cab.

The fog had grown thicker.

"Metropolitan Club," Hargrave told the driver.

The cab moved into the yellow obscurity.

Hargrave leaned back and considered the evening. The transaction had
been extraordinary, but it had ended safely. Major Mikos had
received eighteen thousand dollars in genuine gold. Mrs. Farmingham had
received seven genuine sapphires. Hargrave had recovered the amount advanced
and would earn eight hundred dollars commission in the morning.

No one had been cheated.

There had been too much hurry, too many commands, too
many changes of arrangement, but wealthy people often conducted simple
business in complicated ways. Major Mikos's demand for gold was
eccentric, but a man fleeing political trouble might have reasons.
Mrs. Farmingham's manners were intolerable, but manners and solvency are
not the same thing.

At the club, two porters carried the portmanteau to Hargrave's
private room.

He locked it in a cupboard.

Then he looked at his watch. It was half past
nine. He had forgotten Sir Henry's invitation to the C.
I. D.

He telephoned.

Sir Henry answered at once.

"Hargrave! Where the devil are you?"

"At the club."

"Stay there. Vassiloff has broken the cipher."

"The nine hundred horses?"

"The destination. I'll come over."

The line went dead.

Hargrave ordered supper.

He had eaten nothing since luncheon, and now that the
excitement of the transaction had passed he was hungry. A
steward brought cold beef, bread, pickles, and a bottle of
ale. Hargrave had scarcely begun when Sir Henry burst into
the room.

He brought the fog with him. It rolled through the
open door about his shoulders before the steward shut it
out.

Sir Henry removed his coat.

"Well!" he cried. "We have it."

"The meaning?"

"Part of it."

He sat before the fire and spread several sheets of
paper across the table.

"Vassiloff is a madman. He read the notice forward, backward,
downward, by alternate words, by letter count, by punctuation, and
in three languages. He said it was German, then Russian,
then an old Italian commercial cipher. He shouted 'Impossible!' six
times. Finally he discovered that the apparently unnecessary words indicate
a transposition."

"And the result?"

"London."

Hargrave poured him ale.

"The horses are coming here?"

"They have already come, if the New York information is
correct."

"How?"

"We do not know."

"By what ship?"

"We do not know."

"To whom?"

"We do not know."

"What sort of horses?"

Sir Henry glared at him.

"We do not know! Mystery in every direction. But London
is the stable. Vassiloff is certain."

He picked up the newspaper.

"Look here. 'Usual road' directs us to take the customary
postal route: New York, Queenstown, Liverpool, London. 'Second feeding' means
the second letters after each word connected with food. 'Brown
groom' gives the key color and occupation—B and G—used as
numerical positions. The resulting groups spell L-O-N-D-O-N. Ingenious, but clumsy."

"Could it not merely mean that an actual consignment is
being landed in London?"

"Nine hundred horses could not enter London without half the
newspapers photographing them."

"They might be arriving separately."

"Why encode the fact?"

"I cannot tell you."

"Precisely."

Sir Henry reached for the ale.

"What have you been doing?"

"Buying sapphires."

Sir Henry lowered the glass.

"My word! While I pursue nine hundred horses through an
international cipher, you buy sapphires in a fog. That is
the difference between official and private enterprise. Tell me."

Hargrave did so.

He began with the telephone call from Mrs. Farmingham, described
the visit to the Carlton, Major Mikos and his family
treasure, the examination of the sapphires, the trip to the
bank, the payment in gold, and the final exchange at
the hotel.

Sir Henry listened without interruption until the end.

Then he said, "You advanced eighteen thousand dollars to a
stranger?"

"I bought sapphires worth more than that."

"From a Romanian major?"

"Apparently."

"Apparently?"

"I did not examine his army record. I examined the
stones."

"And the widow Farmingham repaid you in gold?"

"The same amount."

"Nine hundred twenty-dollar pieces?"

Hargrave nodded.

Sir Henry put down his glass very slowly.

"Nine hundred?"

"Yes."

For a moment neither man spoke.

Then Sir Henry laughed.

The laugh was short and uncertain.

"Well, there is your stable!"

Hargrave smiled.

"It is a coincidence."

"Probably. Still—show me one."

"The coins are locked away."

"Show me one."

Hargrave rose and went to the cupboard. He unlocked it,
drew out the portmanteau, and placed it on the floor
beside the table. He opened the brass locks.

The yellow coins lay in their green trays, orderly and
bright.

Sir Henry leaned forward.

"Nine hundred horses," he murmured.

"You are allowing a word to influence you."

"Of course I am. Words are intended to influence people."

Hargrave took a coin from the top tray and tossed
it across the table.

Sir Henry caught it.

He examined the face, turned it over, and rubbed his
thumb along the milled edge.

"It looks right," Hargrave said.

Sir Henry did not reply.

He weighed the coin in his palm. Then he took
a penknife from his pocket.

"What are you doing?"

"Testing a coincidence."

"You will damage it."

"If it is gold, a small mark on the edge
will not matter."

He pressed the point of the knife into the milling.

A thin yellow shaving curled away.

Beneath it appeared a line of dull gray metal.

Hargrave did not move.

Sir Henry scraped again. More yellow coating came off. The
body of the coin was not gold.

The fog pressed against the windows. A horn cried in
the street below.

Hargrave took the coin from Sir Henry. He held it
close to the lamp. He cut into it with the
knife. The plated surface broke, revealing a heavy base-metal core.

"It is impossible," he said.

Sir Henry seized another coin from the tray.

He scraped it.

Gray metal.

A third coin.

The same.

Hargrave overturned the top tray. Coins rolled upon the carpet
and struck one another with a dull, thick clatter that
no longer resembled the ring of gold.

He picked up one, then another, then another. Every edge
showed the same thin coat, the same false body.

The weight had been contrived carefully. The dies were excellent.
The gold plating was heavy enough to deceive a casual
glance. Packed in baize, the coins had not betrayed themselves
by their sound.

Nine hundred glittering pieces lay in the portmanteau.

Not one was worth twenty dollars.

Hargrave remained on one knee beside the case.

"The sapphires," he said.

"Genuine?"

"Absolutely."

"And the gold paid to Major Mikos?"

"Genuine. It came from the Anglo-American Bank. I handled it.
The bank counted it. He counted it."

Sir Henry's red face had become very still.

"Then your major has eighteen thousand dollars in good American
gold."

"Yes."

"And Mrs. Farmingham has sapphires worth at least as much."

"Yes."

"And you have this."

Hargrave looked at the false coins.

The mechanism of the thing, which had been obscured by
movement, authority, hotels, names, banks, trains, and commands, became suddenly
plain.

The genuine sapphires had been bait.

Major Mikos had offered them at a favorable price because
Hargrave's expert examination must confirm their value. Mrs. Farmingham had
forced Hargrave to become the purchaser so that the genuine
bank gold would pass from him to Mikos. Then she
had exchanged a case of false coins for the genuine
stones. The criminals had separated the two halves of the
transaction, allowing Hargrave's own professional knowledge to guarantee one and
his confidence in appearance to accept the other.

He had not been deceived about the sapphires.

He had been deceived about everything else.

Sir Henry crossed to the telephone and lifted the receiver.

"Get me the Carlton Imperial. Quickly."

Hargrave said, "She will be gone."

"Perhaps."

The hotel answered.

Sir Henry identified himself and asked for Mrs. Jason Farmingham's
suite. He waited. His expression hardened.

"When?"

He listened again.

"Description of the luggage? The motor? Which station?"

Another pause.

"Hold every servant who attended the rooms. Let no one
leave. I shall send officers."

He replaced the receiver.

"She left forty minutes ago," he said. "The maid with
her. They used a private motor. The hotel believed they
were going to Victoria."

"Major Mikos's train left at nine."

"Did it?"

"He said it did."

"He also said he was a Romanian major."

Hargrave rose.

"Mrs. Farmingham may be real."

"The real Mrs. Jason Farmingham is sixty-three, short, almost deaf,
and at this moment, unless my information is poor, taking
the waters at Aix-les-Bains. I saw her two years ago."

Hargrave stared at him.

"You knew?"

"I knew when you described the woman. But rich American
widows are numerous, and I did not suppose—"

He stopped.

"Gray hair," Sir Henry continued. "Large figure. Dominant manner. American
speech. A silent female servant. A face sufficiently altered to
resemble newspaper photographs. My word!"

"Who is she?"

Sir Henry did not answer immediately. He went to the
window and pulled back the curtain. Yellow vapor touched the
glass like smoke.

Then he turned.

"There is a woman wanted in New York, Boston, Paris,
Antwerp, and Buenos Aires. She has passed false bonds, false
notes, and false coin. She is a mistress of wigs,
padding, voice, and social impersonation. She once entered a Boston
bank as the aunt of its president and left with
securities worth eighty thousand dollars. The American police call her
Hustling Anne."

"Hustling Anne," Hargrave repeated.

"She is aggressive, energetic, impatient. She gives orders so rapidly
that people obey before they reflect. She is never still.
She never allows a transaction to keep its original form.
Most important, she makes honest men do the dangerous part
for her."

"And the major?"

"Dago Mulehaus."

The name was ugly, a police nickname from an uglier
age, but Sir Henry spoke it as it stood in
the files.

"He is no more Romanian than I am," the detective
continued. "He was born in Baltimore. He can play six
nationalities, perhaps seven. He has been a count, a priest,
a Brazilian coffee planter, and an attaché from a legation
that did not exist. His specialty is injured dignity. If
anyone asks a proper question, he behaves as though his
ancestors have been insulted."

Hargrave saw again the pale face, the military coat, the
trembling hand upon the jewel case.

"My mother wore the largest stone," he said.

"His mother kept a lodging house in Maryland," Sir Henry
replied.

He lifted one of the plated coins.

"This explains the notice."

Hargrave looked at him.

"The nine hundred horses?"

"In American criminal slang, a counterfeit twenty-dollar gold piece is
called a horse. Or was called one among certain gangs.
I had forgotten it. Our London thieves use other terms.
The New York police knew a shipment of nine hundred
had left America. They did not know the destination. The
cipher told the receivers that London was the stable."

"But why should Hustling Anne advertise the shipment after she
had it?"

"She may not have placed the notice. An accomplice in
New York may have done so. Or the notice may
have told Mulehaus that the false gold had arrived and
was ready. The exact machinery can wait."

Sir Henry's eyes moved over the room, fixing each detail
as if it were a point on a map.

"The portmanteau came from Paris, she said?"

"Yes."

"It probably came from New York by Liverpool. The messenger
may have been waiting in London before you arrived. Her
reference to Paris made you think of bankers and continental
travel. Baron Lasker's name gave the major respectability. The five-thousand-dollar
story was clever."

"The French bankers," Hargrave said slowly.

"What of them?"

"She called them stupid because they obeyed instructions."

Sir Henry smiled without amusement.

"The joke was better than you knew. Obedience was the
foundation of the whole affair. The bankers obeyed. The hotel
obeyed. The driver obeyed. Major Mikos appeared to obey necessity.
You obeyed professional logic. Everyone moved when she cracked the
whip."

Hargrave picked up a false coin.

It was astonishing how real it seemed until one knew
it was false. Then the color appeared crude, the milling
imperfect, the eagle lifeless. Knowledge altered the eye.

"I should have tested the money."

"You tested the bank's money."

"I did not test hers."

"She did not give you time to connect the two.
First the stones. Then the bank. Then the major's train.
Then the hotel. Always hurry. Always another explanation. Each act
reasonable by itself."

"And I was thinking only of the sapphires."

"Naturally. She employed you to think of them."

Sir Henry returned to the telephone.

"Now we begin with Victoria, Charing Cross, the ports, the
motor, and every road out of London. We circulate descriptions,
though they will be nearly useless. The gray-haired widow may
already be a thin young governess. The maid may be
a gentleman with a beard. Mulehaus's mustache is certainly in
a wastebasket."

Hargrave barely heard him.

He stood over the open portmanteau. On the table lay
the coded newspaper notice. In it, nine hundred horses had
crossed an ocean by the usual road and come to
an unnamed stable. At his feet lay nine hundred yellow
coins that had crossed the same ocean and arrived in
London.

Two mysteries, apparently separate, had met in his room.

Sir Henry issued rapid orders over the telephone. Men were
to be sent to the Carlton Imperial, the Danton Hotel,
Victoria Station, Charing Cross, and the Anglo-American Bank. The ports
were to be warned. The motor's number was to be
traced. Major Mikos's signature was to be photographed. Every hotel
servant who had seen Mrs. Farmingham was to be questioned.

Then Sir Henry hung up.

For a moment there was no sound except the low
fire and the muffled movement of London beyond the fog.

Hargrave held the plated twenty-dollar piece under the lamp.

Sir Henry looked at it, looked at the open newspaper,
and suddenly struck the table with his fist.

"My word!" he cried. "One of the nine hundred horses!"

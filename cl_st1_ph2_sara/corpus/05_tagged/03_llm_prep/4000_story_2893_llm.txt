# The Tower of Bannadonna

Long after the principality which reared it had been parceled
among captains, mortgaged to dukes, and at last effaced from
maps by that patient geographer, War, there remained upon the
western plain a circular foundation of black stone, vast as
the stump of some pine of the elder world, whose
roots, striking downward through sepulchres and aqueducts, seemed less buried
in earth than clenched about its heart. From this foundation,
at sunset, a shadow went league-long toward the hills; and
shepherds, beholding that bodiless pillar laid darkly over vineyards and
graves, crossed themselves, though few could tell what substance had
once stood there.

The learned called it the Base of the Great Tower.
The peasants called it Bannadonna's Grave.

Both names were true.

In the days when the little state was rich with
wool, wine, salt, and those subtler revenues wrung from pilgrims
and conquered ports, its Senate, being desirous that all neighboring
powers should hear greatness as well as see it, resolved
upon a belfry which should be at once a monument,
a clock, a fortress, and a voice. Babel had sought
heaven by height; this tower should compel heaven to count
the hours. Noah had built against the flood; this should
build against oblivion. It was decreed that the summit should
bear an enormous state bell, whose sound, rolling from mountain
to sea, should proclaim feasts, victories, treaties, funerals of princes,
and—should rebellion ever lift its head—the gathering of armed men.

For this work there was chosen Bannadonna.

Of his birth the records speak uncertainly. Some averred that
his father had been a founder of cannon in Dalmatia;
others, that he was the natural son of a Tuscan
architect and a woman of the islands, who sang to
the hammering of copper bowls. A monkish chronicle, composed by
one who plainly hated him, calls him "the child of
no lawful forge." Yet all agree that, ere his beard
was grown, he had contrived pumps that drew water uphill,
locks which opened to a whispered note, and a bronze
hawk that, when the sun struck a concealed plate, spread
its wings above a garden gate.

Nature had fitted him outwardly to his fame. He was
tall, but not loosely so; spare, yet charged with an
inward violence, as iron is spare before the furnace whitens
it. His brow overhung his eyes like a dark coping
above twin loopholes. His complexion, naturally swart, seemed at times
illuminated from within, and the workmen, seeing him issue at
midnight from the casting-house, with sparks dying upon his leathern
apron, named his countenance Vulcanic. He heard the name and
did not rebuke it.

Pride in common men seeks witnesses. In Bannadonna it sought
solitude.

He accepted the Senate's commission with that low inclination which,
in some persons, bows the body only that the mind
may remain the more erect. Plans were demanded. He produced
none. Models were requested. He showed a sealed case and
said the model was within it. When an aged magistrate,
whose caution had survived three revolutions, observed that public gold
was commonly expended upon visible designs, Bannadonna answered:

"Your Excellency pays the husbandman ere the wheat appears."

"But the husbandman opens the ground to inspection."

"And thereby teaches birds where to descend."

This reply, repeated through the city, was admired as wit
by those who had no public gold at hazard. The
magistrates, less delighted, would perhaps have withheld the charge; but
the Prince himself, eager that his reign should be marked
by some prodigy not military—for his battles had been small
and his triumphs disputed—commanded that Bannadonna be suffered to proceed
in his own fashion.

Thus began the tower.

Year after year it mounted. Lower stages, rude and Cyclopean,
appeared to have been lifted bodily from foundations laid by
giants; above these arose galleries of a lighter order, ring
clasping ring, until the whole seemed a mountain taught symmetry.
By day, masons crept like pale insects along its face;
by night, pitch-pans burned at uncompleted arches, and the noises
aloft—clink of chisel, groan of windlass, dull descent of rammed
stone—made the citizens think of unseen labor in the firmament.

Higher, higher.

The hawks ceased nesting in its upper courses. Clouds stooped
and divided upon it. Once, during a storm, lightning ran
blue round the scaffolds and entered the copper conduits without
injuring a man. This escape was proclaimed miraculous. Bannadonna called
it calculation.

At length the stonework was crowned, and within the highest
chamber there opened a belfry broad enough for a troop
of horse. Here were to hang lesser bells, tuned by
secret ratios, a metallic aviary whose silver-throated birds should call
and answer through the hours. Above and amid them, in
a central frame ponderous as the ribs of a ship,
was reserved the place of the great state bell.

The casting of that bell became a public solemnity.

Metal had been gathered from every district: cannon captured in
forgotten wars; bronze doors stripped from a pagan tomb; copper
coins bearing the faces of dead tyrants; votive tablets; broken
anchors; a magistrate's cracked seal; even a little brazen image
of Saint Michael, surrendered by a convent after much persuasion
and a promise of repayment in silver. Thus the whole
state, sacred and profane, victorious and conquered, was to be
melted into one voice.

Bannadonna designed the mold and guarded it as an emperor
guards succession. Upon the outer surface of the bell he
had ordered twelve figures, each emblematic of an hour. Dawn
was a child with uplifted lids; noon, a crowned youth
staring into the sun; evening, a matron drawing a veil;
midnight, a blind king with a star upon his breast.
But the first hour was represented by a maiden named
Una, who leaned from a garden wall toward a lover
beneath. Their hands, delicately interclasped, formed a projection from the
bell, and about that clasp ran a scroll bearing words
which Bannadonna would disclose to none.

Whether Una had been drawn from life was disputed. It
was thought that in earlier years Bannadonna had loved a
maiden of that name, who died before their marriage; others
denied the death and whispered desertion. A certain portrait, once
preserved in the north palace, showed a woman with grave
eyes and one hand half extended, as if to restrain
or summon. The likeness between that face and the modeled
Una was remarked upon. Bannadonna, hearing of it, caused the
portrait to disappear.

The night appointed for the casting was windless. Magistrates, priests,
guild-masters, foreign envoys, and privileged citizens assembled in galleries around
the foundry court. Beneath them, the furnace doors glowed like
openings into the kingdom of wrath. The great crucible, bound
with hoops and sweating fire, held the mingled metal in
a state no longer mineral and not yet form—a sun
captive in clay.

Bannadonna moved among the furnacemen without haste. He touched sluices,
examined channels, and listened to the molten mass as a
physician to a diseased breast. Now it muttered; now it
snarled; now, pressed by heat, it gave out a baying
sound, as though hounds chained under the earth had winded
blood.

One workman, a young fellow newly come from the hills,
grew fearful. Perhaps the heat disordered him; perhaps some old
superstition concerning bells returned upon his mind. Whatever the cause,
when Bannadonna gave the signal to draw a retaining pin,
the man recoiled.

"Draw," said Bannadonna.

The fellow looked into the furnace and trembled.

"Master—the metal cries."

"It will sing hereafter. Draw."

The workman put forth his hand, but withdrew it at
a sudden gout of flame. Those nearest laughed; the youth,
shamed and terrified, stood fixed.

Then Bannadonna snatched an iron staff from beside the channel.

"Will you freeze the state for the fear in your
marrow?"

"Mercy, master—I cannot."

The rod rose.

Some declared afterward that Bannadonna intended no mortal blow, but
only such correction as the usages of foundries allowed. Others
maintained that, once resisted before witnesses, he struck with his
whole soul. The staff descended upon the youth's temple. He
fell beside the mold, and in falling clutched at his
assailant's garment. A little clasp of dark wood or horn,
severed from the workman's belt, flew into the running channel;
blood from his shattered brow followed it in bright drops.

At that instant the pin, disturbed by the body, gave
way.

The metal rushed.

It came not as water comes, but thickly, luminously, with
a sovereign heaviness, filling the channel, engulfing clasp and blood,
and plunging into the buried mold. A vapor arose. Men
cried out. The priests lifted their crucifixes. The fallen workman
twitched once, then lay quiet.

Bannadonna looked first at the flowing metal, then at the
dead.

"Carry him aside," he said. "The cast must not be
chilled."

The bell was cast.

Justice, being compelled to take notice of the killing, took
notice ceremoniously. Bannadonna was summoned, examined, and for some days
confined in apartments belonging to the palace. The dead man's
mother came down from the hills and sat at the
gate, holding her son's cap. She received a purse and
absolution was sought for the striker from an indulgent prelate,
who distinguished, with much learning, between malice and the transported
impatience of genius.

The Senate resolved that the homicide had occurred in a
moment of artistic frenzy, deplorable but unpremeditated. The Prince added
that the public undertaking must not suffer through a private
calamity. Bannadonna returned to the tower.

Thus conscience was silenced by theology, law by policy, grief
by gold, and blood by bronze.

When the mold was broken, the bell appeared magnificent. No
bubble marred its visible field; every figure stood clear; the
inscription around the rim was sharp enough to cut wax.
Yet, as the lower flank was cleaned, Bannadonna discovered near
the place where Una's extended arm rose from the metal
a narrow discoloration, like a vein beneath skin.

He dismissed the workmen and sounded the spot with a
small hammer.

The note returned imperfectly.

Again the hammer.

A dulled answer.

He caused lamps to be brought and, by their oblique
light, traced a flaw running inward from the shoulder of
the maiden toward the bell's hidden crown. Whether the foreign
clasp, unconsumed in the molten flood, had divided the metal,
or whether blood, vaporizing there, had opened a treacherous seam,
no founder could later determine. Bannadonna, however, comprehended at once
that the fault might weaken the bell's suspension when its
enormous weight should be shaken by repeated strokes.

For an hour he stood alone before it.

Then he prepared a metallic cement, burnished the surface, deepened
a fold of Una's robe, and concealed the discoloration beneath
ornament. The visible bell was perfect.

The flawed part looked sound.

From that day he permitted no one to strike it.

The raising of the bell occupied forty men, eight windlasses,
and three days. As it ascended through the tower, citizens
crowded every roof. Slowly the bronze dome mounted, turning a
little in the air; and Una, passing gallery after gallery,
seemed to climb heavenward with her hand forever clasped in
that of her lover. At last the bell vanished into
the belfry, and the ropes fell slack.

But the city's wonder was not yet satisfied. Rumor told
of another work—a thing hidden behind curtains in an upper
chamber, where Bannadonna labored alone. Porters had carried thither bars
of tempered steel, coils of wire, boxes of toothed wheels,
sheets of beaten brass, and one long hammer whose head
had been forged from the fragment of an ancient sword.
At night there were heard above the streets slow footsteps
where no watchman had been posted.

Clang.

Pause.

Clang.

Pause.

A mason swore that, looking through a slit in the
shutters, he had seen a gigantic hand rise and fall
in the lamplight. A boy said a brazen face had
turned toward him. The boy was whipped for lying; whereupon
the tale spread the faster.

The magistrates, uneasy that some secret expenditure might lie concealed
beneath mechanical mystery, appointed a deputation to visit the tower.
Bannadonna received them at noon in the lower gallery. He
wore no apron, but a dark robe, and at his
girdle hung a cluster of oddly shaped keys.

"We come," said the eldest magistrate, "less as censors than
admirers. The state, having climbed so high with you, would
not be left upon the stair."

"The stair is narrow," replied Bannadonna. "Admiration may ascend; interference
had best remain below."

"Our commission includes inspection."

"And mine includes completion."

Politeness smiled between them like two men holding one dagger
by opposite ends.

At length Bannadonna conducted them upward.

They passed the chamber of the quarter bells, bright and
delicate in their frames; then the hour bells, ranked in
graduated majesty; then the central belfry, where the great bell
hung silent. Seen near, it oppressed less by size than
by presence. Its lip stood higher than a man's shoulder.
The twelve figures encircled it like captives turned to metal
by some Medusan sentence. Una's face, inclined toward her lover,
bore an expression which one magistrate afterward described as tenderness,
another as warning.

Bannadonna paused before her.

"The first hour," he said.

"And those words beneath the hands?" asked the eldest.

Bannadonna wiped dust from the scroll. The inscription became legible:

AT ONE STROKE IS THE DEAREST CLASP SEVERED.

"A severe motto for lovers," said a younger magistrate.

"All clocks are severe," Bannadonna answered. "They number what they
destroy."

Behind the great bell extended a curved track of iron,
partly hidden by drapery. At one end stood a tall
object veiled in black cloth. Its outline was not that
of a statue. The shoulders seemed too broad; one arm
projected stiffly; beneath the cloth, at intervals, there came a
faint ticking, or so the visitors believed.

"What is there?" asked the eldest.

"The servant of the hours."

"Alive?"

Bannadonna's eyes brightened.

"Your Excellency would scarcely pay a living servant to work
with such punctuality."

He drew away the veil.

The figure beneath was fashioned in human semblance, but enlarged
beyond humanity, as though the artificer had begun with man
and corrected him according to some Titan's canon. It stood
seven feet high. Its limbs were of steel, plated at
the joints with scales of dark brass. The chest, broad
and convex, glittered greenly, like the shell of a monstrous
dragon-beetle. One hand was clenched; the other grasped the long
hammer. Its face had neither beauty nor deformity, but that
more disquieting neutrality which suggests that expression has been withheld,
not omitted. The eyes were crystal disks. Behind them lay
no gaze.

A magistrate stepped backward.

Bannadonna smiled.

"Talus," he said. "Not the bronze guardian of Crete, though
he borrows the name. This one keeps no island. He
keeps time."

He explained that the figure, released by the clockwork at
each hour, would advance along the circular track around the
belfry. At the proper bell it would halt, lift its
hammer, strike the appointed number, then return to its station.
Changing cams governed the distance; a pinned cylinder governed the
count; weights descending through the tower furnished strength; escapements, finer
than a jeweler's work, restrained that strength from violence until
violence was required.

"So," said the younger magistrate, striving to recover his composure,
"the whole performance is but wheels."

"Is the march of an army but feet?"

"You compare mechanism with men."

"I compare obedience with obedience."

The eldest magistrate examined Talus's hammer. "And should a man
stand between this servant and the bell?"

"He would be where no man ought to stand."

"Could the engine perceive him?"

"Could the hour?"

The magistrate did not answer.

Bannadonna replaced the veil. As the cloth settled, a hidden
weight shifted within Talus, and one foot moved half an
inch upon the rail.

All heard it.

The younger man turned pale.

"You said it was not wound."

"I said nothing of the kind."

"Is there any soul above us now?"

"None," said Bannadonna.

The answer was literally true, and for that reason remembered.

During their descent, the magistrates spoke little. At the street
door the eldest looked up at the tower, whose top
burned in afternoon light while the base already stood in
shadow.

"He has made a slave stronger than himself," he said.

"A hammer is stronger than the hand," replied another.

"Yes—but the hammer does not walk."

Preparations were made for a public unveiling upon the first
day of the new year. At noon the great bell
was to sound once; thereafter Talus should proceed from bell
to bell, proclaiming each hour without mortal aid. Foreign envoys
prolonged their residence to witness the marvel. Balconies were hung
with banners. The Church consented to bless the mechanism, though
certain canons objected that a figure without breath ought not
to appear ministering among voices consecrated to heaven.

Bannadonna remained aloft through the preceding week, admitting only two
assistants, and those no farther than the winding-room. He calibrated
the escapement, polished the rails, adjusted the striking arcs, and
tested Talus by fractions of movement. To avoid sounding the
bells before the ceremony, he suspended pads of wool before
them. Thus the automaton advanced in silence, lifted, struck softness,
and returned.

On the final morning a servant carried food to the
belfry. Bannadonna received it at the stair.

"Master," said the man, "shall I remain?"

"For what?"

"The hour approaches."

"That is why you shall go."

"It is near one."

"I hear the clock within me."

The servant hesitated. Behind Bannadonna, the veiled Talus stood at
the far end of the circular path. The great bell
hung in the center, with Una facing the entrance.

"Will you not disengage the weight?"

"Do bakers quench their ovens when bread is near done?
Go."

The servant descended.

What now followed was reconstructed afterward from the condition of
the works, from marks upon the floor, and from the
interrupted labor found beside the bell. Yet uncertainty falls on
the precise order, and it were rash to pretend that
dead mechanism rendered a full confession where the living maker
could render none.

It appears that Bannadonna, having completed all adjustment to Talus,
turned his attention to the ornament of the great bell.
The clasped hands of Una and her lover had troubled
him. Whether the concealed flaw beneath that projection had widened,
or whether mere perfectionism drew him to it, he placed
a ladder against the bell and climbed until his face
was level with the figures.

Upon a small platform he set gravers, files, a burnisher,
and metallic cement. One of Una's fingers, delicately modeled, had
not lain quite close against the lover's hand. Bannadonna resolved
to unite them more perfectly.

Outside, the city waited for two o'clock, at which hour
the public admission was to begin. Within the tower, lesser
wheels moved. A descending weight strained upon its cord. Pins
entered notches. The one-o'clock release, which had been set during
testing and never disengaged, approached its appointed instant.

Bannadonna worked.

The bell's vast flank reflected him obscurely: a little dark
shape leaning against a continent of bronze. He filed the
lover's wrist. He warmed the cement. He pressed Una's hand
inward.

Below, the clock gave a preliminary click.

He did not hear it.

Or, hearing, mistook it for one of the familiar noises
by which mechanism reassures its master. Great artisans, like great
captains, become deaf not to clamor but to warning; for
every danger first addresses them in the accent of obedience.

The pin fell.

A shutter opened in the weight shaft.

Talus stirred.

First the head lifted. Then the right arm tightened upon
the hammer. The left foot advanced along the rail, followed
by the right; not with the wavering imitation of a
man, but with that fearful sufficiency whereby the machine, having
no balance to doubt, seems more assured than life.

Step.

Pause.

Step.

Bannadonna bent closer to Una.

He had placed himself between the automaton's hammer and the
bell.

Talus rounded the curve. Its crystal eyes received the belfry,
the ladder, the creator; receiving all, they recognized naught. The
regulating cylinder turned. The figure halted at the station of
the first hour.

Bannadonna looked up.

What passed in his mind during that final interval no
chronicle records. Perhaps he comprehended merely an error of setting.
Perhaps, seeing his creature confront him with uplifted hammer, he
beheld in one flash the dead workman, the blood entering
bronze, the hidden seam, the magistrates' distrust, and his own
dominion narrowing to the breadth between metal and metal. Some
averred that his hand flew to a lever beyond reach.
Others, from the position of the body, believed he tried
to shield Una's clasp.

The hammer rose.

"Stay!"

No response.

"Talus!"

The hammer descended.

One stroke.

The city heard it.

Not the full voice expected from the great bell, for
Bannadonna's body intercepted part of the blow; yet the sound
which escaped was prodigious, broken, and mournful—a bronze thunder strangled
at birth. Pigeons burst from every roof. Horses fell upon
their knees. In the palace chapel a hanging lamp swung
though there was no wind.

Then silence.

When the servant, followed by magistrates and guards, reached the
belfry, they found the veil fallen, the ladder overturned, and
Talus returned to its station. Upon the floor lay scattered
tools. Against the bell, beneath Una's extended hand, Bannadonna was
prostrate.

His skull had been crushed between hammer and bronze.

The face could scarcely be known; but one hand still
grasped the little burnisher, and the other was closed around
a fragment from the inscription. The stroke had broken away
the words "DEAREST CLASP," leaving only:

AT ONE STROKE IS SEVERED.

Talus stood motionless nearby. On the iron hammer was a
dark stain.

The younger magistrate, who had visited before, covered the crystalline
eyes with the veil.

"So the creature has killed the creator," he whispered.

The eldest corrected him.

"No. The creator placed himself in the command he gave."

Such was the rational judgment. But when they tried to
unwind the mechanism, they discovered that Talus had completed not
only the forward stroke but the return, though one of
the recoil springs was fractured and, according to Bannadonna's own
diagrams, should have stopped it midway. This circumstance was omitted
from the official account.

The Prince ordered the automaton destroyed in secret. Publicly it
was announced that a flaw, discovered after the accident, made
exhibition impossible. At midnight Talus was taken apart only so
far as removal required, wrapped in sailcloth, and lowered from
the tower. Twelve soldiers escorted it to the harbor. There
a galley carried it beyond the headland.

The captain afterward related that, though the sea was calm,
the wrapped figure shifted upon the deck as if resisting.
The crew chained it to a stone and pushed it
overboard. For a moment it stood upright beneath the clear
water, brass chest glimmering, hammer raised by the motion of
descent; then stone, slave, and secret sank together into blue
obscurity.

Some fishermen still avoided that ground. In storms, they said,
a measured knocking came from below.

Bannadonna's funeral was appointed with honors equal to those of
a victorious general. The homicide at the casting was forgotten;
genius had become innocence by dying spectacularly. His coffin, draped
in black velvet sewn with silver wheels, was borne through
streets hung with mourning cloth. Artisans marched carrying reversed tools.
The Senate followed in robes of state. Behind them came
the dead workman's mother, not by invitation, still holding the
old cap. No official noticed her.

The great bell, though not yet declared safe, was ordered
to toll once as the coffin passed beneath the tower.

This command troubled the founders. Bannadonna's assistants knew of no
defect, but they knew his secrecy, which was a defect
diffused through all his works. The eldest magistrate urged that
a lesser bell be used.

"The people expect the great voice," said the Prince.

"The dead cannot be honored by another death."

"Nor rulers by public timidity."

Thus authority prevailed over foreboding, as before policy had prevailed
over justice.

At the appointed moment the coffin reached the square. Above,
men drew the great rope. The bell moved slightly in
its frame. Una and her lover turned toward the east,
then west. The hidden seam, long burdened by the weight
and jarred by the automaton's fatal blow, opened along the
inner crown.

Again the rope was pulled.

The bell swung.

From high in the tower there came, not a toll,
but a rending noise—the groan of a forest splitting under
frost. One iron staple tore free. Another bent. Stone cracked
around the anchoring beam. The keepers fled toward the stair.

The bell lurched sideways.

For an instant it hung by one remaining support, tilted
above the square, its mouth showing black as an eclipse.
Then the concealed flaw severed the crown. The whole bronze
mass dropped through the lower frame, smashing beams, breaking floors,
and driving before it a storm of timber and masonry.

The procession looked up.

Down it came.

Higher dust leapt before lower ruin. The bell struck an
interior gallery, bounded outward through the arched opening, and fell
into the square. Its first impact shattered the stone pavement;
its second broke the bell from lip to shoulder. A
fragment bearing Una's face slid across the ground and stopped
against Bannadonna's coffin.

The bearers had fled. The coffin overturned.

There lay inventor and image amid dust: the dead man
hidden in velvet, the bronze maiden staring upward with her
hand torn away.

No citizen was killed, though many were injured. The old
mother from the hills alone remained standing near the wreck.
She stooped, picked from the broken bell a blackened splinter
of horn—the remnant, perhaps, of her son's clasp—and placed it
inside his cap.

The defect was then exposed to all. Founders examined the
fracture and traced the corrupted seam. Some attributed it to
alien matter in the casting; some to vapor; some to
unequal cooling. The Senate forbade further inquiry. But secrecy, when
broken, flies farther than proclamation. Within a day the city
knew of the blood, the splinter, the false repair, and
the dead workman whose name no public tablet had preserved.

The bell was melted into cannon.

The tower itself was repaired. Scaffolds climbed its wounded side;
new beams were laid; the broken arch was closed with
stone of another color, so that the scar remained visible
in sunshine. Yet no bell of like greatness was attempted.
Lesser chimes occupied the height, and men, not mechanisms, struck
them.

For a season the city prospered. Merchants again boasted of
the tower. Foreigners were shown the repaired gallery and told,
with diminishing accuracy, how Bannadonna had died through an unforeseeable
failure. Children dared one another to climb the foundation after
dark. Time, which eats scandal more quietly than rust eats
iron, had begun its work.

A year to the day after the funeral, before dawn,
the earth moved.

At first there was a murmur under the plain, such
as wagons make upon a distant bridge. Dogs woke and
howled. Water climbed the rims of wells. Then the ground
heaved once, heavily, and every bell in the tower sounded
without hand or hammer.

A second shock came.

The repaired arch opened. Old cracks, hidden beneath plaster, ran
downward like lightning reversed. The tower bent—not visibly at first,
but enough that those who watched from the vineyards saw
the summit pass before a setting star.

A third shock.

The great structure, built to outlast dynasties, lost agreement among
its stones. Gallery separated from gallery. The upper chamber revolved
as it descended. For one impossible moment the tower seemed
not to fall but to kneel, prostrating its pride toward
the east.

Then all came down.

The sound crossed the plain and returned from the hills.
A cloud covered the city. When it cleared, naught stood
above the circular base. The tower of clocks had no
hour. The house of bells had no voice.

Years afterward, divers claimed to have found fragments of Talus
in the sea, encrusted with coral, the joints open like
the limbs of a drowned knight. Others denied it. The
bell's metal, recast into cannon, was said to have burst
in the first battle where it was fired. This also
may be fable. Chroniclers must distinguish what is proven from
what is fitting; yet men, less scrupulous than chronicles, remember
chiefly what is fitting.

Of Bannadonna's genius there can be no question. He raised
stone toward cloud, taught weights to walk, compelled bronze to
number time, and made dead matter counterfeit obedience. But he
mistook obedience for innocence. The thing without a soul executed
the command of the soul that made it; the bell
without guilt bore the flaw guilt had hidden; the tower
without pride inherited the pride of him who raised it.

The slave struck the lord.

The bell crushed the bier.

The blood became the flaw.

The flaw became the fall.

And when the creature had slain its creator, and the
work had betrayed the worker, the earth itself—older than artificer,
tower, bell, prince, and principality—arose beneath them and erased the
boast.

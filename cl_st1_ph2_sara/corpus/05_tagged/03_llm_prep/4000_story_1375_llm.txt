## FOR SIMLA REASONS

There are two reasons why a man leaves Simla in
June. One is that he has been ordered away, and
the other is that he has not. The latter is
generally the more imperative.

When Sparkley, of the Department of Preposterous Accounts, abandoned his
office, his pony, and his engagement to Miss Mary Millikens
in the middle of the hot weather, Society said that
he had embezzled a lakh of rupees, insulted the Viceroy,
embraced Mrs. Awksby in public, or become religious. Society always
allows a man four alternatives, and naturally chooses the most
damaging.

I knew Sparkley. He was incapable of stealing a lakh,
because his Department never possessed more than seven hundred rupees
at one time; he could not have insulted the Viceroy,
because he had never been presented; and if he had
embraced Mrs. Awksby, she would have said so before breakfast.
As for religion, there are limits even to the possibilities
of the Indian Civil Service.

Nevertheless, he vanished. His clerk stated officially that the Sahib
had departed "on urgent private duty connected with jungle statistics,"
which meant nothing; but nothing, when properly docketed, will carry
a Government servant farther than a railway ticket.

Mary Millikens said that she did not care where he
had gone.

Her sister Clara said that she did not know why
he had gone.

Their mother, the Widow Millikens, said that she knew perfectly
well, but could not betray a confidence.

These three statements, taken together, proved that each lady knew
something and that none intended to tell it. Therefore I
went to Bungloore to ask Sparkley.

Bungloore is not Bangalore. It is a settlement beyond the
railway, beyond the cart-road, and very nearly beyond the patience
of God. A river reaches it in the rains, a
dust-storm reaches it in the hot weather, and official correspondence
reaches it whenever the Post Office has nothing better to
do. There are six houses, one broken temple, a police-station
without policemen, and a Club whose rules forbid gambling, loud
language, and native servants after nine. Consequently nobody goes there
before ten.

I travelled three days by train, two by bullock-cart, and
seven hours upon a pony with opinions of its own.
At sunset I found Sparkley seated outside a grass-roofed bungalow,
listening with grave attention to a brass flower-pot.

He had grown thin, and wore a beard which began
uncertainly on one cheek and, losing courage, stopped before reaching
the other.

"Don't interrupt," he said. "It is telling me where the
bearer hides the brandy."

The flower-pot said nothing.

"Indeed?" Sparkley murmured. "Behind the medicine chest? I suspected it."

Then he looked up.

"You!" he cried. "What brings you to Bungloore?"

"Friendship," I said.

"That is impossible. Try again."

"Curiosity."

"Come in."

His drawing-room contained a camp-table, three guns, nine unpaid bills,
a cracked harmonium, two saddles, the flower-pot, and an enormous
padded armchair upholstered in faded green brocade. The chair seemed
unnecessarily respectable for Bungloore, where respectability mildews in a fortnight.

Sparkley gave me whisky and soda. The soda-water bottle sighed
when he uncorked it.

"You heard that?" he asked.

"I heard gas escaping."

"My dear fellow, gas never escapes. It communicates. That bottle
said it had been filled at Umballa by a man
named Chatterjee who had recently quarrelled with his mother-in-law."

"Most soda-water bottles say that."

"You are flippant because you have not suffered."

Here he leaned toward the lamp. "Everything speaks. Chairs remember;
tables complain; doors repeat what was said behind them; inkstands
know all official secrets, though, fortunately, they cannot spell. Even
boots converse, but their talk is low."

"Sparkley," I said, "you are off your head."

"On the contrary, I have come into it. At Simla
I could hear nothing because everybody talked. Here there is
SOLITUDE. Solitude is the mother of revelation, just as necessity
is the mother of invention, except when it is the
aunt. I had a clever aphorism about that once, but
it proved exactly the reverse of what I meant."

"Did the furniture advise you to break off your engagement?"

"I did not break it off."

"Mary says you did."

"Mary is mistaken."

"Clara says she knows nothing."

"Clara is accurate for the first time in her life."

"And Mrs. Millikens knows everything."

"She would."

He poured more whisky, although my glass was full, and
less into his own, although it was empty. This is
the Civilian's instinct of hospitality.

"You remember the amateur theatricals at the Quorum?" he asked.

I remembered them. They had been performed in aid of
the European Orphans' Fund and had produced twelve rupees after
expenses, three engagements, one separation, and a dispute concerning a
pair of blue tights.

"The performance," Sparkley continued, "was *The Rajah's Revenge; or, The
Deputy Commissioner's Daughter*. I played the Rajah. Mary played the
daughter. Clara was a maidservant disguised as a Pathan chief,
and Mrs. Millikens sold programmes in the interval, though she
had no authority to do so."

"Quite."

"After the second act I had determined to propose to
Mary. You know how a man determines these things. He
says, 'Tonight I shall know my fate,' when his fate
has known him for six months and is discussing curtains
with its mother."

"That is true."

"It is not true, but it sounds as though it
ought to be. I had rehearsed the proposal carefully. There
were three portions. First: 'Ever since I saw you, my
life has moved toward you as the river moves toward
the sea.' Second: 'Will you give me the right to
guard and cherish you through every hour allotted us?' Third:
'Say only yes, and make me the proudest and happiest
man in India.' Good, wasn't it?"

"Too good."

"I borrowed the river from a magazine and the rest
from my natural feelings. During the third act there was
a scene in complete darkness. The Rajah, being pursued by
conspirators, enters the Hall of a Thousand Pillars—which was six
chairs and a screen—and discovers the heroine by touch. I
had arranged with Mary that after my speech on vengeance
she should remain by the papier-mâché idol. Then I would
whisper the proposal."

"Very practical."

"So are revolvers. They also go off in the wrong
direction."

Sparkley rose and began pacing the room. The harmonium gave
a small creak, as if it had heard the story
before and distrusted it.

"The lights went out," he said. "I crossed the stage.
Someone shoved me from behind. I fell against a female
figure, caught her hand, and whispered, 'Ever since I saw
you, my life has moved toward you as the river
moves toward the sea.'"

"Mary?"

"I thought so. Before she could answer, Bleareyed—he was playing
a mutinous Sepoy—charged across the stage and drove his musket
into my ribs. I staggered twelve feet to the left,
found another hand, and continued: 'Will you give me the
right to guard and cherish you through every hour allotted
us?'"

"Mary?"

"I still thought so. Then Mulledwiney, who had no part
in the performance but had entered from the bar to
help, struck me between the shoulders with a property palm-tree.
I was projected into the wings, where I encountered a
third woman. I seized her, because by then I was
desperate, and said: 'Say only yes, and make me the
proudest and happiest man in India.'"

"Mary?"

"Her mother."

I reflected. Outside, a jackal laughed in the confidential manner
of one who has seen the official papers.

"And the first?"

"Clara."

"The second?"

"Mary."

"Then you proposed to all three."

"No. That is the vulgar interpretation. In law, in logic,
and under the Regulations governing Covenanted Services, I proposed one-third
of marriage to each. Clara received the scenery, Mary the
duties, and Mrs. Millikens the conclusion. Unfortunately women do not
understand departmental distribution."

"What happened when the lights came up?"

"Clara was blushing. Mary was crying. Mrs. Millikens had her
arms round my neck. The audience applauded, believing it part
of the play. Then Mary boxed Clara's ears; Clara accused
Mary of robbing her of lifelong happiness; and Mrs. Millikens
announced that at her age she had not expected to
love again, but Providence was inscrutable."

"And you?"

"I escaped beneath the stage."

"That was cowardly."

"It was strategy. Cowardice is strategy that has failed."

He told me the rest. For two days the Millikens
household held separate interviews with him. Clara demanded that the
river proceed to the sea. Mary wished to know why
she alone had been offered guarding and cherishing without love.
Mrs. Millikens, who had received the only direct request, insisted
that an engagement existed. Each forbade him to explain the
matter to the others, because a woman will endure public
ruin sooner than private ridicule.

At the same time, Society heard that Sparkley had proposed
to three women during one performance and admired his administrative
capacity. His Department offered him a special appointment dealing with
simultaneous claims. The Chaplain called to discuss a triple ceremony.
Mrs. Awksby sent a note saying that, if his affections
remained divisible, she was at home on Thursday.

"So I came here," Sparkley concluded. "There was nothing else
to do."

"You might have told the truth."

"My dear fellow, this is India. The truth is what
remains after the witnesses have left for England."

I asked whether he intended to return.

"When the affair is forgotten."

"It will never be forgotten."

"Then I shall rise rapidly in the Department. Scandal is
fatal to a junior, useful to a senior, and indispensable
to a Member of Council."

He became silent. From the corner came a dry rustle.

"That is the armchair," he said. "It wishes to speak."

The green chair stood beneath a punkah which had not
moved for years. Its broad arms shone with wear, and
there was a dark stain upon one cushion—port wine, tears,
or furniture polish. In Simla these are chemically indistinguishable.

"Bought it at an auction," said Sparkley. "Used to belong
to Mrs. Awksby."

"Indeed?"

"It remembers everything."

He lowered his voice.

"Everything?"

"Everything. Who sat here, who knelt there, who hid behind
it when Colonel Awksby came home unexpectedly, which Major wrote
the verses beginning 'O moon above Jakko,' and why the
District Judge was discovered at dawn wearing only one spur
and carrying a Japanese umbrella. The chair gives dates."

"Sparkley, you ought not to listen."

"I don't. It talks while I am reading."

"What does it say about Mrs. Awksby?"

The chair creaked with such prolonged satisfaction that I felt
myself intruding upon Society.

Sparkley smiled.

"That is Another Story."

I left Bungloore next morning. At Simla I met Mrs.
Awksby riding down the Mall in a habit fitted so
perfectly that no Christian doctrine could have explained it. She
drew rein and asked whether I had seen "poor dear
Mr. Sparkley."

"I have," I said. "He owns your old green armchair."

For the first time in my knowledge Mrs. Awksby ceased
to smile.

"He says it talks."

Her pony moved sideways.

"He says it remembers."

Mrs. Awksby struck the pony between the ears.

"Mr. Sparkley," she said distinctly, "is a champion liar."

Then she galloped away, which proved either that Sparkley was
mad or that the chair was truthful. I have always
preferred evidence that settles nothing.

## A PRIVATE'S HONOR

The Colonel came to me after mess wearing carpet slippers,
a full-dress tunic, and the expression of a man who
has signed an order without reading it.

"I may be dead tomorrow," he said.

"Then you have been promoted?"

"This is serious."

It concerned Private Mulledwiney, of the Shivering Mugs—a soldier who
had been drunk in Rangoon, chained in Peshawar, lost in
Madras, married in Cawnpore, and officially present at Meerut during
the whole period. He had broken three rifles, two arresting
sergeants, a billiard-table, the regimental goat, and a stained-glass window
commemorating Temperance.

The Colonel loved him.

"He has the true military spirit," said the Colonel. "Buried
very deep, of course."

That afternoon, while Mulledwiney slept outside the canteen, the Colonel
had prodded him playfully with a light Malacca cane.

"Where?" I asked.

"On the person."

"What portion?"

"The portion presented to me."

Mulledwiney awoke, seized the cane, and stated before witnesses that
no officer and gentleman could "lay shtick to the person
av Terence Mulledwiney widout givin' satisfaction, rank or no rank,
Queen or no Queen, an' sorra the divil a button
between thim."

"So," said the Colonel, "I must fight him."

"Court-martial him."

"Impossible. That would injure his self-respect."

"He has none."

"That is why it must be preserved."

The fight was arranged for dawn in a clearing beyond
the native shrine. No gloves were permitted because Mulledwiney considered
them effeminate; no seconds because he had struck both his;
and no doctor because the regimental surgeon had bet heavily
on the private.

"If I fall," said the Colonel, gripping my hand, "tell
my wife I died of measles."

"Why measles?"

"She understands measles. Honor would alarm her."

"And if Mulledwiney falls?"

"He will be charged with malingering."

At dawn the Colonel went into the jungle with Mulledwiney,
Bleareyed, Otherwise, the surgeon, two water-carriers, a bottle of rum,
and the regimental bandmaster, who had misunderstood the nature of
the engagement. I did not accompany them. There are occasions
when friendship demands presence, and others when it demands an
alibi.

At eight, the band played a funeral march.

At nine, a hospital doolie returned carrying something covered by
a blanket. Mulledwiney walked behind it with one eye closed,
the Colonel's carpet slippers in his belt, and an air
of chastened triumph. The Colonel was not visible, but neither
was Bleareyed, and Otherwise had acquired the Colonel's tunic.

That evening the regiment buried somebody with full honors. The
Adjutant read the service, the Colonel's wife received a telegram
concerning measles, and Mulledwiney was awarded three days' confinement for
appearing on parade with blood on Government property.

A week later I saw the Colonel at the Club,
drinking through a straw and unable to sit down.

"You were buried," I said.

"Hush," he whispered. "That was the bandmaster."

"Is he dead?"

"Not exactly. But the Regulations were very clear about the
funeral."

I never learned who had struck whom, why the bandmaster
required burial, or how he afterward became Inspector of Regimental
Music. It was a question of honor, and honor, like
Army accounts, is best left unaudited.

## JUNGLE FOLK

The Watering Place lay beneath seven tamarind trees, a ruined
shrine, and a moon of the sort specially supplied to
writers about the East. It was large, yellow, and excessively
attentive. The jungle breathed all round it, because jungles in
stories are never permitted to sleep naturally.

First came the Moo Kow, moving with the solemnity of
a municipal committee. She drank, considered the water, and said:

"Lo! I am She-Who-Giveth-Milk."

"You give it because they take it," said the Barrack
Cat from a stone.

"Peace, Small Eater of Fish. The White Child lays his
head against my flank and calls me Gentle Mother."

"He also ties a tin to your tail."

"That is affection."

"It is education," said the Cat. "I know the White
Child. He has put me in a pillowcase, dropped me
from a veranda, painted me blue, and written 'VICEROY' upon
my side. Therefore I love him, for I am a
Faithful Beast and this is an Animal Story."

Three Gee Gees came down the path: a Cavalry Horse
with scars, a Lady's Pony with opinions, and a Commissariat
Mule who denied being a horse but attended all horse
meetings in order to correct them.

The Cavalry Horse drank deeply.

"I have borne Man in battle," he said. "I know
the trumpet, the steel, the thunder of guns, and the
pressure of knees."

"You know the pressure of spurs," said the Mule.

"It is the same thing in poetry."

The Lady's Pony tossed her mane.

"My mistress whispers secrets into my ear."

"That is because no human will listen," said the Cat.

"She feeds me sugar."

"And uses a curb-bit."

"It is love."

The Mule folded one ear.

"All you domestic creatures misunderstand Man. He does not love
us. He writes about us. That is much more dangerous."

There was a silence, for even the jungle knows when
criticism has bitten.

The Moo Kow said, "We must speak nobly. Shall we
not say 'ye' and 'thou'?"

"Why?" asked the Cat.

"Because beasts always say 'ye' and 'thou' when men write
down our conversation."

"I say 'you' when I scratch them."

The Cavalry Horse lifted his head toward the moon.

"Hearken, O Brothers—"

"You see?" said the Mule. "He has begun."

"—I perceive the Skool Boaz."

Through the trees came forty-seven British schoolboys, released upon India
for the holidays and marching in irregular order behind a
master who had lost his spectacles, his authority, and one
shoe. They carried catapults, fishing-rods, cricket-bats, dead lizards, stolen mangoes,
and a small native idol which they intended to convert
into a wicket.

Their leader was Stalkington Major, a square child with no
eyelashes. Beside him marched Beetleby, who composed Latin verses against
the Chaplain, and McTurk Minor, whose father owned half a
district and denied the other half existed.

The boys saw the Watering Place.

"Beasts!" cried Stalkington Major.

Immediately they behaved as the sons of empire. One threw
a stone at the Moo Kow. Another attempted to mount
the Cavalry Horse backward. Three pursued the Cat with a
landing-net. Beetleby inserted a lighted squib beneath the Lady's Pony,
explaining that it was an experiment in mobility. McTurk Minor
challenged the Mule to single combat and, receiving no answer,
claimed the victory for England.

The master sat beneath a tree and murmured, "Boys will
be boys," which is the password by which civilization excuses
its young.

The animals withdrew just beyond missile-range.

"Observe their courage," said the Cavalry Horse, while the boys
fastened the native idol to the Moo Kow's tail.

"Observe their resource," said the Mule, while they filled the
master's hat with mud.

"Observe their emotional restraint," said the Cat, escaping from the
pillowcase. "Not one has wept except the smallest, and the
others are beating him for it."

"They will rule provinces," said the Moo Kow proudly.

"Why?"

"Because they have broken everything they do not understand."

The boys now formed a court-martial. A pariah puppy was
accused of treason, convicted of looking foreign, and sentenced to
transportation across the pond upon a plank. The plank sank,
but the puppy swam ashore and was therefore punished for
evading justice.

"Splendid," said the Cavalry Horse. "That one will command troops."

"The boy who tied two frogs together?" asked the Cat.

"No, the one who watched and said it was necessary."

The master found his spectacles in his pocket and counted
the boys. He counted fifty-one, then forty-three, then announced that
all were present according to the best available figures. The
troop departed toward the dak-bungalow, carrying the idol, the master's
shoe, and a notice-board marked GOVERNMENT FOREST: NO TRESPASSING.

The jungle breathed again.

"Are all rulers made so?" asked the Lady's Pony.

"No," said the Mule. "Some are made by examination, which
is crueller."

From far away came a noise resembling a regimental song
falling down a staircase.

The Cat's tail enlarged.

"Men," she said.

"Soldiers," corrected the Cavalry Horse.

"Drunk soldiers," said the Mule. "The distinction is administrative."

Three figures entered the moonlight: Mulledwiney, Bleareyed, and Otherwise of
the Shivering Mugs. Mulledwiney wore the Colonel's carpet slippers, a
cavalry sabre, and the painted head of a temple god
suspended round his neck. Bleareyed carried a grandfather clock without
its pendulum. Otherwise pushed a wheelbarrow containing six bottles, a
brass basin, a live duck, two officers' helmets, a church
hassock, and the left door of the District Magistrate's carriage.

They halted at the water.

"'Tis here," said Mulledwiney, "that we'll encamp, me sons, till
the inimy comes or the liquor goes."

"The inimy went home," said Otherwise.

"Thin we'll wait for the liquor."

Bleareyed set down the clock. It struck thirteen in a
reproachful manner.

"What have ye there?" Mulledwiney asked.

"A clock."

"I can see it's a clock, ye sun-blinded heretic. Who
owns it?"

"The Chaplain."

"Thin it's religious property, an' we're safe."

The animals concealed themselves behind the shrine, for beasts in
fables may understand all human speech except orders addressed to
them.

Mulledwiney drew a bottle from the wheelbarrow and raised it
toward the moon.

"Here's to the wars," he said, "wherein a man learns
Geography by bein' marched to places he'd niver have visited
sober."

"Amen," said Bleareyed.

"Tell us about the Pass," said Otherwise.

"What Pass?"

"The one where you killed the chief."

"I've killed so many chiefs I can't distinguish their faces
from their obituaries."

"The red chief with the silver gun."

"Ah, him!"

Mulledwiney settled upon the stolen carriage door.

"'Twas in the Kreep-mow Pass, beyant the Shivin Mugs Mountains,
where the snow is so cowld it freezes the sound
inside the bugles, an' they must be thawed over a
fire before reveille. We was three hundred men against forty
thousand Pathans, Afghans, Russians, Frenchmen, fakirs, hill-ponies, an' a correspondent
from the *Pioneer*."

"The correspondent was the worst," said Bleareyed.

"By reason av his accurate shootin'. Our orficer—may the angels
give him more sinse than promotion ever did—says, 'Mulledwiney, take
that fort.' I says, 'Which fort, sorr?' for there was
seventeen on the hill. 'All av thim,' says he, an'
goes behind a rock to write the despatch."

"And you took them?" asked Otherwise.

"Wid me left hand. Me right was occupied holdin' the
regimental flag, a wounded drummer-boy, two boxes av ammunition, an'
the orficer's lunch. Up I climbed while the inimy poured
cannon-balls down upon me like hailstones at a weddin'."

"Why a wedding?" whispered the Cat.

"Do not interrupt a military authority," said the Mule.

Mulledwiney continued:

"At the first fort I met a giant seven foot
broad and fourteen foot high. He swung a sword made
from the railway line between Lahore and Delhi."

"That line is still there," said Bleareyed.

"They relaid it afterward. I caught the blade in me
teeth, bit it into three pieces, an' spat the middle
piece through the loophole, where it destroyed the enemy artillery."

"Fine shooting," said Otherwise.

"Instinct. Thin I entered the fort an' found five hundred
men waitin' for me."

"What did you do?"

"I surrounded them."

The Cavalry Horse shivered with admiration.

"That," he said, "is the true epic voice."

"Five hundred against one," Mulledwiney went on, "but they had
no chance, for I was angry an' they was foreign.
I struck the first man so hard that he flew
backward through the second fort, upset the third, an' surrendered
the fourth. The remaining three forts sent flags of truce
askin' me to stop."

"And the chief?"

"He came out ridin' a tiger."

"A tiger?" said Bleareyed.

"A tiger wid brass stirrups and a tail trained to
salute. The chief fired his silver gun. The bullet struck
me on the forehead, flattened itself, an' fell into me
pocket, where I later exchanged it for beer."

"You showed it to me," said Otherwise.

"That was another bullet."

They drank.

The moon lowered herself a little, as moons do when
the tale requires attention. The ruined god on Mulledwiney's chest
appeared to wink, though this may have been bad workmanship.

"What happened after you killed the chief?" asked Bleareyed.

"I did not kill him. He killed himself out av
politeness after I had torn the tiger in halves."

"With your hands?"

"Don't be ridiculous. Wid the tiger."

The Cat covered her eyes.

"Then came the siege," said Mulledwiney. "Seven months we was
shut up widout food, water, ammunition, tobacco, cards, pay, or
permission to complain. We ate our belts the first week,
our boots the second, the mule harness the third—"

At this the Commissariat Mule said, "Libel."

"—and by the fourth," Mulledwiney continued, "there was nothin' left
but the regimental barber, who was too thin to be
useful and had seniority besides."

"What did you eat?" asked Otherwise.

Mulledwiney leaned forward. His audience leaned with him, except the
grandfather clock, which had heard barrack-room tales before and wished
itself elsewhere.

"First," he said, "we ate the inimy."

The Moo Kow ceased chewing.

"Not raw," Mulledwiney explained, offended by their expressions. "We was
soldiers, not savages. We boiled the little ones an' roasted
the tall ones, because tall men is stringy. The cavalry
we stewed wid their boots for flavor, an' the artillery
required two days' soak owing to the powder."

Bleareyed nodded critically.

"Artillery always does."

"We made soup of a general, but the medals spoiled
the broth. A Russian colonel lasted the whole left flank
for breakfast. There was a fat commissary who—"

The Lady's Pony fled into the jungle.

"—fed twelve men, not countin' the drummer-boy, who was delicate.
Then we came upon a regiment wearin' green uniforms—"

The Cavalry Horse bolted.

"—an' we mistook them for vegetables till they began firin'.
After that we mixed them wid the remains av the
tiger and made a curry so strong it blew the
roof off the fort."

The Moo Kow gave one despairing "Moo!" and crashed away
through the tamarinds.

"An' on the last day," Mulledwiney said, warming to his
art, "when there was no inimy left except their commander,
we drew lots for him. I won the head—"

The Cat vanished.

The Mule remained one moment longer.

"I have carried ammunition under fire," he said. "I have
eaten Government fodder and survived. I have listened to officers
explain transport. But there are limits."

He departed at a dignified gallop.

Mulledwiney looked round the empty Watering Place.

"Where's the congregation?"

"Deserted," said Bleareyed.

"Cowards. 'Twas only a story."

"Was it true?" asked Otherwise.

Mulledwiney considered.

"Parts av it."

"Which parts?"

"There was a Pass."

The clock struck fourteen.

The three soldiers finished the bottles. Bleareyed put the duck
into the brass basin and crowned it with an officer's
helmet. Otherwise attempted to wind the stolen god with the
clock-key. Mulledwiney sang of a maiden in Tralee who had
loved a sergeant, a corporal, and the greater part of
a gun-team, but had remained technically faithful because the regiment
moved often.

The moon sank behind the jungle. The ruined shrine grew
grey; the trees flattened themselves into morning; parrots began their
official correspondence; and somewhere beyond the hills a bugle sounded
a note that had been frozen since last winter.

Then the sun came up.

It came up red, round, enormous, and punctual over the
edge of the world, because it always came up that
way there, and no one in the Department had yet
thought to alter the Regulation.

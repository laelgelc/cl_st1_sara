## THE RED-HEADED OUTFIELD

Manager Delaney of the Rochester Stars had won pennants with
lame pitchers, deaf catchers, weak-hitting shortstops, and third-basemen who could
stop anything except a ground ball. He had won games
by luck, by argument, by rain, by riot, and once—so
baseball tradition declared—by persuading an umpire that the setting sun
was a dangerous obstruction deliberately maintained by the home club.

But never, in all his stormy career, had he encountered
anything equal to his red-headed outfield.

There were three of them.

Red Gilbat played left. He was a tremendous hitter, a
magnificent thrower, a brilliant fielder, and as nutty as a
fruit cake.

Reddy Clammer played center. He was fast, graceful, accurate, and
clever; but he would rather make one impossible catch with
his back to the diamond than ten easy catches in
the ordinary manner. He was a grand-stand player. If no
one applauded him, he considered the game a failure.

Reddie Ray played right. He could hit, throw, bunt, slide,
steal, judge a fly, back up an infielder, and remember
the score. More than all, he could run. There had
been swift men in baseball before Ray, but they had
merely traveled from place to place. Ray vanished from one
point and appeared at another.

They were red-headed in three distinct and positive shades. Gilbat's
hair was the color of a burning barn. Clammer's was
glossy copper, carefully parted, brushed, subdued with water, and trained
to appear under his cap in one ornamental curl. Ray's
was dark red, close-cropped, and mostly hidden. Gilbat was proud
of his hair and fought about it. Clammer was vain
of his and exhibited it. Ray never thought of his.

"They're the greatest outfield in the league," Delaney would say,
"and if I live through another week of 'em I'll
be the greatest manager that ever escaped an asylum."

The Stars were fighting Providence for the pennant. On the
morning of the last game of the series the clubs
were tied. Rochester had won eighty-one games and lost forty-nine;
Providence had won eighty-one and lost forty-nine. The season had
narrowed to that afternoon, and that afternoon had narrowed, in
Delaney's mind, to the behavior of three red-headed men.

Gilbat had been quiet for two days. This frightened Delaney.

Clammer had received a bouquet from an admirer. This frightened
Delaney more.

Ray alone appeared natural. He sat in the clubhouse lacing
his shoe, while Delaney walked up and down before the
lockers.

"Listen, you red-headed calamities," said the manager. "This is the
ball game. This is the pennant. No monkey business. Gilbat,
don't eat anything in the field. Clammer, catch the ball
the way it comes. Ray—"

He stopped.

"What about me?" asked Ray.

"Nothin'. You just play ball."

Gilbat raised his head.

"Can I eat on the bench?"

"You can eat the bench," replied Delaney, "if you'll leave
the peanuts out of left field."

"A fellow's got to keep up his strength."

"Your strength!" Delaney roared. "You could lift the grandstand and
yet you need peanuts to hold up your socks. Last
week you missed a fly because you were peelin' an
orange."

"I ketched it on the bounce."

"It cost two runs."

"But I ketched it."

Delaney swung toward Clammer.

"And you—Lord Chesterfield Clammer—if you turn one fly into a
circus, I'll break your looking-glass."

Clammer adjusted his cap before the small mirror over his
locker.

"When a man has style he can't help showin' it."

"You could try."

"The public expects something from me."

"The public expects you to catch the ball."

"The public," said Clammer with dignity, "has imagination."

A veteran pitcher, Pete Haines, pulled a sweater over his
head and spoke from within it.

"The public's got a brick, too. I seen it."

Clammer ignored him. Gilbat reached into his uniform and produced
a paper sack. Delaney snatched it.

"Peanuts!"

"They're mine."

"They belong to the club now."

"You can't confiscate private property."

"I can fine private property twenty-five dollars."

Gilbat considered this. His freckled face assumed a look of
deep legal meditation.

"You couldn't prove they was peanuts."

Delaney thrust the sack toward him.

"What are they, diamonds?"

"Might be."

"Git out!"

They went out through the runway into a blazing Providence
afternoon. Fifteen thousand persons packed the stands, overflowed the aisles,
hung against the fence, and occupied every roof, window, wagon,
tree, and telegraph pole from which a small portion of
the field could be seen. The crowd was hostile, eager,
and well informed. It knew Gilbat. It knew Clammer. It
knew Ray. Most of all it knew red hair.

When the Rochester outfield trotted to its stations a cry
rolled from the bleachers.

"Fire!"

Another voice answered:

"Where?"

"Outfield!"

The bleachers howled. Gilbat turned, bowed solemnly, and removed his
cap. The sun struck his hair. It flamed.

"Call the engines!" yelled a Providence rooter.

Clammer did not bow. He ran lightly toward center, tossing
the ball above his head and catching it behind his
back. The grandstand applauded faintly. Clammer tossed it higher and
caught it behind his neck.

Ray merely sprinted to right, speared a long practice fly,
whirled, and threw to third. The ball traveled low and
straight, rising no more than a man's shoulder, and struck
the third-baseman's glove with a smack. The grandstand gave him
the real applause.

Clammer heard it.

From that instant the day was dangerous.

Providence scored in the first inning. A scratch single, a
sacrifice, and a clean hit to right did it. Ray
charged the ball and threw home so hard that the
runner would have been caught had not the ball struck
the pitcher's shoulder and bounded to the stand. Baseball has
few cruelties more bitter than a perfect throw spoiled by
an innocent man standing where he has a right to
stand.

Rochester tied the score in the second. Gilbat opened with
a drive against the left-field wall and reached second standing
up. Clammer sacrificed him to third, though he paused long
enough after bunting to observe the effect of his start
upon the spectators. Ray sent a long fly to center,
and Gilbat scored.

In the Providence half of the second, Gilbat made his
first contribution to the entertainment.

With one out, the Providence catcher lifted a high fly
toward left. It was not a difficult ball. Gilbat moved
under it, glanced at the sun, placed himself carefully—and then
began to behave in a peculiar fashion. His right hand
was raised to make the catch. His left hand remained
closed.

The ball descended. Gilbat shifted. He tried to catch it
with one hand. It struck his glove, bounded against his
shoulder, dropped to the grass, and rolled away.

The batter reached second.

Delaney came charging from the bench.

"What's the matter with you?"

Gilbat picked up the ball with his right hand and
threw it in. His left fist remained tightly clenched.

"Sun got in my eye."

"The sun's behind you."

"Other eye."

"What've you got in your hand?"

"Nothin'."

"Open it."

"I don't have to."

"Open it!"

Gilbat backed away.

The Providence coachers began shouting. The crowd perceived that something
rare was in progress. A murmur ran through the stands.

"Open your hand!" bellowed Delaney.

Gilbat slowly opened it.

Seven peanuts lay in his palm.

For a moment there was silence. Then Providence rose as
one man and laughed. It was not ordinary laughter. It
came in waves. It struck the field, rolled back from
the grandstand roof, and broke again over the bleachers. Players
sat down. The Providence catcher, standing safe on second, leaned
against the shortstop and wept.

Delaney seemed unable to speak. His lips moved. His hands
opened and closed.

Gilbat regarded the peanuts sorrowfully.

"If I'd dropped 'em," he explained, "somebody might have stepped
on 'em."

Delaney pointed to left field.

"Get back there before I kill you."

The next batter lined sharply to Gilbat. He caught the
ball waist-high, wheeled, and hurled it to second, doubling the
runner by six feet. It was a splendid play—quick, powerful,
and exact.

Gilbat walked toward the bench at the end of the
inning with the calm bearing of a man who had
vindicated a principle.

"See?" he said to Delaney. "Peanuts give me control."

In the third inning Rochester scored twice. Their second-baseman singled,
the pitcher bunted, and the lead-off man drew a pass.
After two were out Gilbat came up.

The Providence pitcher, a thin, sour veteran named Kline, knew
Gilbat's weakness. Nearly every eccentric hitter has one fixed point
of reason, and nearly every pitcher seeks it. Gilbat would
swing at bad balls, high balls, low balls, wide balls,
and balls that were not thrown; but when angered he
became the most dangerous hitter in the league.

Kline put two curves over the outside edge. Gilbat missed
both.

The Providence bench began a steady murmur.

"Peanuts, Red! Want some peanuts?"

Gilbat dug his spikes into the dirt.

Kline wasted a fast ball high. Gilbat checked his swing.

"Two strikes and one ball," called the umpire.

The next pitch came close to Gilbat's head. He flattened
himself backward and glared toward the box.

"Tryin' to bean me?" he demanded.

Kline grinned.

"Thought I saw a peanut."

The bleachers laughed.

Kline made his mistake then. He threw a fast ball
shoulder-high and over the inside corner. Gilbat met it squarely.
The sound was short and vicious—a crack like a board
splitting in cold weather. The ball shot between left and
center, bounded once on the hard turf, and struck the
fence.

Both runners scored. Gilbat reached third before the ball came
in and stood there glaring at Kline. He could have
made home on the throw, but his indignation had become
more important than a run.

"Peanut!" he shouted across the diamond. "How's that for a
peanut?"

Delaney waved frantically.

"Stay there! Stay there, you lunatic!"

Gilbat moved off third, still arguing. The Providence third-baseman tagged
him on the back.

"You're out," said the umpire.

Gilbat stared.

"What for?"

"Tagged off the base."

"I was talkin'."

"You was out."

"Can't a man talk?"

"He can talk from the bench."

Gilbat appealed to Delaney.

"Did you see that? He sneaked up behind me."

Delaney covered his face.

Rochester led, 3 to 1.

The game settled for two innings. Haines, the Rochester pitcher,
worked carefully. He had little speed left, but much wisdom.
A veteran pitcher who has lost his fast ball resembles
a merchant after a fire: if he survives, it is
because he has discovered resources he never knew he possessed.
Haines changed pace, crossed signals, rubbed the ball, delayed his
delivery, quickened it, and looked at runners until they became
ashamed of taking leads.

Clammer, meanwhile, began his exhibition.

He caught a routine fly in the fourth by racing
in too far, whirling, retreating, and taking the ball over
his shoulder. The crowd applauded.

The next ball came directly at him. He could have
stood still. Instead he drifted to the left, sprinted right,
crossed his feet, stumbled artistically, and caught the ball with
one hand while falling.

The applause was louder.

"Look at him!" Delaney growled. "He manufactures trouble."

"Long as he catches 'em," said Ray from the bench.

"That's how it starts. First he catches 'em. Then he
gets applause. Then he tries somethin' that'd strain an acrobat.
Then we send flowers to the hospital."

Clammer returned to the bench and brushed dust from his
trousers.

"Did you hear 'em?" he asked.

"Hear who?" said Haines.

"The grandstand."

"I thought it was thunder."

"They appreciate class."

"They appreciate a man fallin' down," said Haines. "Same reason
they go to a circus."

Clammer smiled indulgently. Sarcasm could not injure him unless the
grandstand heard it.

In the fifth Gilbat disappeared.

He had been sitting at the far end of the
bench, wearing a long linen duster which belonged to a
peanut vendor. How he had obtained it no one knew.
Gilbat was a natural collector of other men's property and
seldom burdened his explanations with details.

Delaney saw the duster.

"Take that thing off."

"I'm cold."

"It's ninety degrees."

"Cold inside."

"Where's the owner?"

"Don't know."

"You stole it."

"Borrowed it."

"Take it off."

Gilbat pulled the duster closer and shivered elaborately.

At that moment a cheer from the crowd announced that
the Rochester batter had struck out. Clammer went to the
plate and flied to right. Ray singled, stole second with
a beautiful hook slide, and remained there while the next
hitter worked the count full.

Gilbat was due after him.

Delaney turned.

Gilbat's seat was empty.

"Where is he?"

No one knew.

Delaney searched beneath the bench, behind the water cooler, and
in the runway. Gilbat had vanished. The Providence crowd learned
of the situation and began calling for him.

"Where's Red?"

"Lost his peanuts!"

"Fire's gone out!"

The Rochester batter drew a base on balls.

"Gilbat!" roared Delaney into the runway. "Gilbat!"

A baby carriage appeared from beneath the grandstand.

It came rolling slowly along the cinder path behind the
Rochester bench. It was a large, old-fashioned carriage, with a
high hood and white wheels. Gilbat pushed it. The long
linen duster flapped around his ankles. His cap was turned
backward. In the carriage sat Reddy Clammer's bouquet, propped upright
and covered with a towel.

Gilbat halted before the bench.

"Fresh air for the baby," he announced.

The Rochester players collapsed. The Providence bench shouted. The grandstand,
seeing Clammer's name printed on a card above the flowers,
understood immediately.

"Clammer's baby!" yelled someone.

"Pretty little thing!"

"Got red hair?"

Clammer sprang from the bench.

"You put them flowers back!"

"Don't wake it," said Gilbat. "Just got it asleep."

The crowd shrieked. Clammer seized the handle of the carriage.
Gilbat resisted. The carriage moved backward and forward between them
while the bouquet nodded under the hood.

The umpire approached home plate and called:

"Batter up!"

Delaney rushed at Gilbat.

"You're up, you idiot!"

Gilbat released the carriage, tightened the duster around him, and
walked toward the plate.

"Take off that coat!" cried Delaney.

"No time."

"You can't bat in it!"

"Watch me."

The umpire objected. Gilbat argued that the duster was part
of his uniform. The umpire said it was not. Gilbat
said it was underwear. The umpire ordered him to remove
it. Gilbat declared that public decency forbade such an act.
The crowd, by this time helpless with joy, demanded that
the game continue.

"Play ball!" shouted the umpire. "And if that coat interferes,
you're out."

Gilbat stepped into the box with the duster hanging nearly
to his shoe tops.

Kline, the Providence pitcher, was laughing. A pitcher should never
laugh at a hitter. It disturbs the breathing, loosens the
knees, and brings the mind away from the business in
hand.

His first pitch was low.

Gilbat swung. The duster wound around his legs and he
sat down heavily.

"Strike one!"

Providence roared.

Delaney gripped the bench.

"Somebody shoot him," he whispered. "Don't aim to kill. Just
enough to stop him."

Gilbat rose, unwound the duster, and resumed his position. Kline
laughed again. The next ball came straight over the plate.

Gilbat hit it.

A home run!

It was not a doubtful drive, not one of those
towering flies which invite three fielders to run, stagger, pray,
and collide at the fence. It was a line shot.
It cleared the right-field wall, struck the roof of a
shed, bounded into the street, and was pursued by twelve
boys and a dog.

Gilbat stood at the plate watching it.

Then he gathered up the skirts of the duster in
both hands and trotted around the bases.

Ray scored ahead of him. The crowd laughed, cheered, hooted,
stamped, and surrendered itself utterly. Even Providence applauded. Baseball crowds
are partisan, but they are also greedy, and when offered
a spectacle beyond expectation they will accept it from an
enemy.

Gilbat rounded third with the duster streaming behind him like
a royal robe. Delaney waited at the plate.

"You hit a home run," the manager said in a
choked voice, "and I hate you for it."

Gilbat crossed the plate and bowed.

"Clothes make the man."

Rochester led, 5 to 1—or would have led, except that
Ray's run was canceled.

The umpire had called him out for leaving second before
the pitch.

Ray protested. Delaney protested. Half the Rochester team protested. Ray
insisted he had touched the base after the pitcher began
his motion; the Providence second-baseman insisted he had not. The
base umpire remained firm. Under the scoring, the runner's out
came before the home run, ending the inning. Gilbat's blow
counted only as far as the rules permitted—which was nowhere.

The scoreboard remained Rochester 3, Providence 1.

That is the way of baseball. A man may perform
a feat fit for a monument, and a small official
with dust on his shoes may wave it into nothing
because another man moved six inches too soon.

Gilbat listened to the explanation.

"You mean I hit that ball for nothin'?"

"That's what I mean," said Delaney.

"Then gimme it back."

"Get to left field."

"I want another swing."

"You're out."

"Ray spoiled my home run."

Ray looked at him quietly.

"I left too soon."

"Why?"

"Thought the ball would be hit."

"It was."

"I know."

Gilbat considered, failed to discover a satisfactory target for his
wrath, and went to left field wearing the duster.

Clammer followed to center, angry about the baby carriage and
the applause Gilbat had received. A vain player does not
easily forgive another man's success, particularly when that success is
ridiculous.

The first Providence batter in the fifth lifted a high
fly to deep center. Clammer ran back, turned sideways, misjudged
it deliberately by three steps, recovered, and made a one-handed
catch against his hip.

The stands applauded.

He threw the ball into the air and caught it
behind his back.

The stands laughed.

Clammer's face darkened. He wanted admiration, not laughter. There is
no food more bitter to vanity than the wrong kind
of applause.

The next batter drove a liner into the gap between
center and right. Ray flashed across, but Clammer called loudly,
"Mine!" and cut in front. He took the ball at
full speed, tumbled twice, rose without stopping, and threw behind
the runner at first.

The runner barely returned safe.

This time the crowd cheered the play.

Clammer smiled. His day had begun.

Providence scored twice in the sixth. A walk, a bunt,
and a double to left caused the damage. Gilbat fielded
the double cleanly but then tried to throw behind his
back. The ball struck his heel and rolled toward center.
Clammer dashed in, picked it up, whirled completely around, and
threw home. The throw was magnificent and useless. Both runners
had scored.

The score was tied, 3 to 3.

Delaney waited for Gilbat at the bench.

"Why did you throw behind your back?"

"Clammer does."

"Clammer's a fool."

"He gets applause."

"You get runs scored against us."

Gilbat pointed toward Clammer, who was acknowledging the cheers.

"He started it."

"You're both startin' somethin' I'm goin' to finish with a
bat."

Gilbat sat down. The duster spread over the bench.

A small Providence boy leaned across the railing.

"Say, Red!"

Gilbat looked up.

"Did your hair come with the coat?"

Gilbat glared.

"Go home."

"Who painted it?"

"Your mother."

The boy squealed with delight.

"Hey, Red! Does it hurt when it burns?"

Gilbat rose.

Delaney caught his arm.

"Sit down."

"He's insultin' me."

"He's ten years old."

"He knows better."

"Sit down!"

Gilbat sat, trembling with wounded dignity. The bleachers had discovered
his weakness. From that moment came cries, chants, songs, and
inquiries concerning red hair.

"Red! Red! Red!"

"Strike a match!"

"Heat wave!"

"Put out the fire!"

At first Gilbat pretended not to hear. Then he replied.
Then he argued. Each reply delighted the bleachers and inspired
ten new insults. The collective mind of a bleacher crowd
is not gentle. It studies a victim, finds the thin
spot in his armor, and drives every word into that
spot until either the victim escapes or the armor breaks.

Ray sat beside Gilbat.

"Don't listen."

"I ain't listenin'."

"Keep your mouth shut."

"My mouth is shut."

"Then how are you talking?"

Gilbat had no answer to this, so he turned toward
the field. For nearly an inning he maintained silence.

Rochester failed to score in the seventh. Providence came to
bat. The first man grounded out. The second lifted a
twisting fly down the left-field line.

Gilbat started badly, recovered beautifully, and made the catch near
the wall. It was a difficult chance, and he handled
it with the easy certainty that made his folly so
maddening. He might have been the best left-fielder in baseball
had the game interested him continuously.

As he turned, someone in the first row yelled:

"Hey, Carrots! Your head's ripe!"

Gilbat stopped.

"Who said that?"

Fifty men claimed the honor.

Gilbat walked toward the fence.

Delaney rose from the bench.

"Come back!"

A tall spectator stood and waved a handkerchief.

"Here's a flag for the fire!"

Gilbat dropped the ball.

He climbed the fence.

It was done with astonishing speed. One moment he stood
on the field; the next he was over the boards
and among the spectators. Men scattered. Women screamed. Hats flew.
The tall spectator tried to retreat but became wedged between
two larger citizens.

Gilbat reached him.

What followed was not a fight in the accepted sense.
It was a disturbance, a migration, and a rearrangement of
the front rows. Gilbat swung once, missed, struck a hat,
was seized by three ushers, shook off two, dragged the
third across a bench, and disappeared under a moving hill
of coats, arms, programs, and Providence policemen.

The bleachers, delighted by their success, howled like wolves.

Delaney ran onto the field and then stopped. There are
crises which render even a manager helpless. He looked toward
the umpire.

The umpire removed his mask.

"Gilbat is out of the game."

"You can't put him out. He ain't in the park."

"Out of the game," repeated the umpire.

"Who'll play left?"

"That's your business."

"Who'll play left?" Delaney demanded of Providence, fate, the league,
and heaven.

The policemen brought Gilbat down the aisle. His duster was
torn, his cap was gone, one eye was swelling, and
he wore an expression of righteous triumph.

"I got him," he announced.

"You got yourself out of the game," said Delaney.

"He called me carrots."

"I'll call you worse than that."

"Fine him!" cried someone from the bleachers.

"Fine him a bushel of peanuts!"

Gilbat lunged toward the fence again. Six teammates caught him
and pushed him into the runway.

Rochester had carried no reserve outfielder. Delaney believed in pitchers,
catchers, and infield substitutes; with Gilbat, Clammer, and Ray, he
had thought an extra outfielder a waste of railroad fare.
Now his choice lay between moving an infielder outward or
asking two men to cover the field.

He sent Murphy, the substitute third-baseman, to left.

Murphy was willing, loyal, and slow. He had never played
the outfield in his life.

"Anything hit near you," said Delaney, "catch it."

"How near?"

"Anywhere in the county."

Providence immediately hit three balls toward him.

The first fell ten feet in front of him while
he waited for it to reach him. The second sailed
over his head while he charged forward. The third he
stopped with his chest. One run scored, and only a
remarkable throw by Ray prevented another.

Providence led, 4 to 3.

Rochester tied the score in the eighth. Their lead-off batter
doubled, went to third on an infield out, and scored
on Haines's sacrifice fly.

Four to four!

The crowd had ceased laughing. The pennant race reasserted itself.
Comic men may interrupt a ball game, but the score
always returns to claim attention.

Clammer ran to center for the eighth inning with a
new determination. Gilbat had captured the day's laughter. Ray had
made the best throw. Clammer intended to make the best
catch.

The first Providence batter sent a low liner to left-center.
Murphy moved uncertainly. Clammer streaked over, dove headlong, and caught
the ball inches above the grass.

It was a genuine great play.

The crowd roared.

Clammer sprang to his feet and bowed.

The second batter flied deep to right-center. Ray had it
judged and was moving under it when Clammer came racing
across.

"Mine!" shouted Clammer.

Ray yielded. Clammer leaped, though no leap was necessary, caught
the ball one-handed, brought it down behind his back, and
tossed it into the infield without looking.

The crowd roared again.

Clammer glowed.

Delaney gripped the edge of the bench.

"He's ripe," he muttered.

"For what?" asked Haines.

"For disaster."

The third batter hit a tremendous drive straight over center.
It was the longest ball of the game. Clammer turned
and ran.

He could run. Vanity does not exclude ability, and Clammer
possessed enough ability for two ordinary men. He raced with
his head back, measuring the flight over his shoulder. The
ball carried toward the deepest corner, where the wooden fence
met the scoreboard.

Ray, crossing from right, saw the danger.

"Fence!" he yelled.

Clammer heard him. He knew the fence was there. He
also knew the crowd was watching.

He increased his speed.

The ball descended. Clammer stretched out one hand.

He caught it.

At the same instant he struck the fence.

The crash sounded throughout the park. The boards shook. Clammer
crumpled at their base, the ball still in his glove.

For a heartbeat no one moved. Then Ray reached him,
Murphy lumbered over, and the players ran from both benches.
The crowd, so noisy a moment before, became absolutely still.

Clammer lay unconscious. His face was white beneath its freckles.
One trouser leg was torn, and his shoulder rested at
an unnatural angle.

The doctor knelt beside him.

"Broken collarbone, maybe. Concussion. Get a stretcher."

Clammer opened his eyes.

"Did I—catch it?"

Ray held up the glove. The ball was inside.

"Yes."

Clammer tried to smile.

"Did they see it?"

"They saw it."

A sound began in the grandstand—not a cheer at first,
but a low, sympathetic murmur. Then the crowd rose and
applauded. Providence men waved their hats. Women stood. Even the
bleachers, cruelest of all baseball creatures, gave Clammer honor.

He heard the applause as they lifted him.

"That," he whispered, "was a play."

Delaney walked beside the stretcher.

"It was a fool play."

Clammer's eyes closed.

"Crowning one," he murmured.

They carried him away.

Delaney returned to the bench, his face gray.

"How many have we got?" he asked.

The scorekeeper counted.

Seven.

Nine men had begun the game. Gilbat had been banished.
Clammer was injured. Rochester possessed no legal substitutes who had
not already been used. Seven men remained.

Seven men—and Providence coming to bat in the ninth.

Delaney looked toward the outfield. Murphy, slow and frightened, stood
in left. Ray waited in right.

"Murphy!" called Delaney. "Come in and play third."

"Who plays left?"

"Nobody."

Murphy trotted in.

Delaney pointed at Ray.

"You've got the outfield."

"All of it?" asked Ray.

"All of it."

A strange cheer came from the stands. The situation appealed
to the crowd's sense of the impossible. One outfielder! One
red-headed young man to cover the vast green territory from
foul line to foul line, from the shallow grass to
the far fences!

Ray pulled his cap lower.

Delaney caught him as he passed the bench.

"Don't kill yourself."

Ray smiled.

"I'll try not to."

"Gilbat lost this game," Delaney said fiercely. "Clammer nearly lost
it. You're due to win it."

Ray made no answer. He ran out.

He took his position in deep center, but position means
little when one man must patrol an entire outfield. Haines
looked back at him. The pitcher's tired face twisted into
a grin.

"I'll make 'em hit grounders," he called.

"Do that," replied Ray.

The first Providence batter, understanding the opportunity, tried to drive
to right. Haines crossed him with a slow curve. The
batter hit a grounder to short and was out.

One down.

The next man lifted a fly toward left.

A groan rose from the Rochester bench. The ball was
not deep, but it was far from Ray's position.

Ray started.

The first steps of a fast runner do not always
reveal his speed. Ray seemed merely to lean in the
proper direction; then he lengthened, opened, and shot across the
grass. His red hair showed beneath the flying cap. His
arms drove. His knees rose. The distance shrank under him.

The ball fell rapidly.

Ray came like stored lightning released. He reached the spot,
thrust out his glove, and caught the ball at his
shoetops.

Two down!

The Providence crowd applauded. A fine play makes its own
citizenship.

Haines faced the next hitter carefully. One strike. Two balls.
A foul. Then a sharp single through the box.

The following batter drove another hit between first and second.
Ray charged, held the runner at third with a bullet
throw, and Providence had men on first and third.

The dangerous Kline, the pitcher, came up. Pitchers are traditionally
weak hitters until a game depends upon getting them out;
then they become inspired.

Haines worked the count to two strikes. Kline fouled off
a curve, missed a slow ball that was called low,
and then drove a hard liner toward the right-field corner.

Ray was shading left.

He turned and went.

The ball struck fair, skipped past the chalk, and rolled
toward the wall. The runner from third scored. The man
from first raced around second.

Ray overtook the ball near the fence, picked it up
without breaking stride, and threw toward third. The throw traveled
more than two hundred feet in the air. Murphy caught
it and swept at the runner.

"Safe!" called the umpire.

Providence led, 5 to 4.

Men stood on first and third. Still two out.

Delaney walked to the box.

"You through?" he asked Haines.

"No."

"You look through."

"I can get one."

"You've been sayin' that since 1908."

"Go away."

Delaney went away. Managers visit pitchers chiefly to hear lies
and decide whether the lies contain courage.

The next batter hit a towering fly to center. Ray
waited under it. The runners moved with the pitch, knowing
they would go with two out.

The wind caught the ball.

It drifted toward left, then farther left. Ray adjusted. For
any normal center-fielder it was an easy catch. For a
man who had just sprinted to the right-field fence, thrown
across the diamond, and returned to center, it was a
demand upon exhausted legs.

Ray ran. The ball carried beyond him. He stretched, touched
it with the end of his glove—and dropped it.

The runner from third scored. The man from first tore
around the bases. Ray recovered the ball and threw home.
The Providence coach waved wildly. The runner rounded third.

The throw came on a line.

The Rochester catcher received it, blocked the plate, and made
the tag.

"Out!"

The inning ended.

Providence had scored three runs and led, 6 to 4.

Ray stood for a moment in left-center, hands on knees.
He had failed to make the catch, but no one
blamed him. A crowd understands effort when effort is too
plain to deny. The Providence stands gave him an ovation
as he came in.

The Rochester players rose to meet him.

"Good work," said Haines.

"We're two behind," Ray replied.

"Good work anyway."

Delaney pulled him aside.

"You're fourth up."

Ray looked toward the scoreboard.

"Get three men on."

"We'll get 'em."

"Then I'll hit."

That was all.

The bottom of the ninth began with Rochester's weakest hitter,
Murphy, at bat. Kline had pitched the entire game for
Providence. He was tired, but the sight of the pennant
strengthened him.

Murphy swung at the first pitch and missed by a
foot.

"Wait him out!" yelled Delaney.

Murphy missed the second by two feet.

"Hit it!" yelled Delaney.

The third pitch came down the middle. Murphy shut his
eyes, swung, and struck the ball. It bounded high over
Kline's head. The shortstop raced in, scooped it, and threw.

Murphy slid headfirst.

"Safe!"

The crowd yelled. It wanted Providence to win, but more
than that it wanted the game to become extraordinary. Crowds
always suspect that fate in baseball is plotting a climax,
and they will assist fate even against their own club.

Haines came to bat.

Delaney caught him.

"Bunt."

"They'll expect it."

"Everybody in the park expects it. Bunt anyway."

Haines bunted the first pitch foul. He bunted the second
straight to Kline, who wheeled and threw to second.

The ball arrived low. The shortstop dropped it.

Both runners were safe.

No one out!

The Rochester bench erupted. Delaney danced along the coaching line,
shouting orders, warnings, prayers, and abuse.

The next batter was the lead-off man, Cully. He tried
to sacrifice. The first attempt went foul. The second popped
behind the plate and was nearly caught. With two strikes
he shortened his swing and slapped a grounder toward third.

The third-baseman stepped on the bag, forcing Murphy, and threw
to first.

Cully beat the throw.

One out. Men on first and second.

Ray waited near the bench, swinging two bats.

The next hitter, O'Brien, was Rochester's best bunter. Delaney signaled
the sacrifice. O'Brien squared. Kline threw high. Ball one.

Again O'Brien squared. This pitch came inside, and the batter
barely pulled away. Ball two.

Providence suspected a trap. The third-baseman crept in. The first-baseman
charged. Kline delivered.

O'Brien pushed the ball softly past the pitcher.

The second-baseman dashed in, barehanded it, and threw toward first.
O'Brien slid. Dust covered the bag.

"Safe!"

Bases full!

One out!

The grandstand became a living wall of sound. Men hammered
seat backs. Boys climbed the fence. Hats flew. The Providence
players gathered around Kline. The pitcher waved them away.

Reddie Ray stood in the on-deck circle.

Delaney seized the next batter, a catcher named Briggs.

"Listen. Don't hit into a double play."

"What do I hit into?"

"I don't care. Get on. Get hit. Foul 'em. Make
him pitch."

Briggs nodded.

Kline's first delivery was a strike. Briggs let it pass.

"Swing!" roared Delaney.

"You said make him pitch."

"I meant hit it when he does!"

Briggs fouled the next ball against the screen. Two strikes.

Providence rose. One more strike might bring the double play
within reach of the pennant.

Kline wasted a curve. Briggs refused it. Another curve bounced.
Ball two.

The runners took their leads.

Kline threw fast and high. Briggs swung beneath it.

"Strike three!"

Two out.

Bases full.

Reddie Ray up.

The sound changed. It did not cease, but tightened, deepened,
gathered. Fifteen thousand voices merged into one sustained cry. Men
who had jeered Rochester all afternoon now called for Ray.
Providence partisans shouted at Kline to strike him out, then
shouted at Ray to hit it. Their loyalty and their
hunger for spectacle fought openly.

Delaney stood beside third, bent at the waist, hands on
knees. He could feel his heart striking his ribs.

"Gilbat nearly lost it," he muttered. "Clammer nearly lost it.
Ray's due. He's due to win it."

Ray walked to the plate.

He carried one bat. He did not scrape the ground,
wave to the grandstand, argue with the catcher, or stare
at the pitcher. He set his feet and waited.

Kline looked at the bases.

Haines at third. Cully at second. O'Brien at first.

Two runs needed to tie. Three to win.

The first pitch was a curve outside.

Ray did not move.

"Ball one!"

Kline rubbed the ball. He tried a fast pitch high.
Ray swung and missed.

"Strike one!"

The Providence bench shouted.

The third pitch broke sharply over the corner.

Ray checked, but too late.

"Strike two!"

One ball, two strikes.

The pennant hung upon a pitch.

Delaney heard Gilbat's voice from somewhere beneath the stand. The
banished outfielder had found a slit in the runway and
was yelling through it.

"Hit it, Ray! Hit it where they ain't!"

Clammer, unconscious or awake in the dressing room, could not
hear. Yet his last question seemed to remain over the
field: Did they see it?

Everyone saw this.

Kline took the sign. The catcher wanted a waste ball.
Kline shook his head. He had challenged hitters for fifteen
years and was too proud to finish the pennant with
a trick.

He wound up.

The runners moved.

The ball came shoulder-high, fast, and over the outside half
of the plate.

Ray stepped.

Ping!

There are hits which announce themselves. Before the ball leaves
the bat, every trained eye knows. This one shot toward
right field, not high enough to be a home run,
not low enough to be caught on a line. The
Providence right-fielder started in, realized his error, turned, and sprinted
back.

The ball passed over his head.

It struck the grass once, bounded high, and raced toward
the corner.

Haines scored.

Cully scored.

The score was tied!

O'Brien tore around third.

The right-fielder reached the ball at the wall and fumbled
it. The crowd broke into chaos.

"Go! Go! Go!"

Ray had rounded first like a rocket. He did not
look toward the ball. He watched the coaches, the bases,
and the runner before him. At second he was already
close behind O'Brien.

O'Brien, no slow runner, heard feet pounding behind him. He
glanced back.

Ray was coming.

The third-base coach whirled both arms.

"Home! Both of you! Home!"

O'Brien rounded third.

Ray cut inside the bag so sharply that his body
leaned toward the ground. He came out of the turn
like a thunderbolt. His cap flew off. His red hair
streamed.

The Providence right-fielder threw to the relay man. The relay
throw went toward home.

O'Brien was halfway down the line.

Ray gained upon him.

The crowd saw it and went mad.

He was overtaking his own runner!

O'Brien ran desperately. He could hear Ray behind him, close
now—the hiss of breath, the hammer of spikes. For an
instant the two Rochester men seemed joined in one streaking
shape, O'Brien in front, Ray driving at his back.

"Move!" Ray shouted.

O'Brien moved. No man had ever tried harder to escape
a teammate.

The throw came home, high and slightly toward first. The
catcher leaped, caught it, and whirled.

O'Brien was ten feet from the plate.

Ray was at his shoulder.

The catcher lunged.

O'Brien slid.

Ray did not.

With one tremendous stride he passed O'Brien on the outside,
leaped over the catcher's sweeping arm, twisted in the air,
and came down beyond the plate. O'Brien's hands struck the
rubber an instant later.

Dust exploded.

Players crashed together.

The catcher rolled over, clutching the ball. The umpire jumped
clear and spread both arms.

"Safe!"

O'Brien safe!

Then he pointed toward Ray.

"Safe!"

For one stunned instant no sound came. The crowd had
seen too much to understand. Ray lay beyond the plate.
O'Brien lay upon it. The catcher sat in the dust.
Three Rochester runners had scored before them, and the batter—the
batter had overtaken the last runner and crossed with the
winning run.

Then came thunder.

Not cheering merely. Thunder! A roar that lifted from the
bleachers, struck the roof, burst over the field, and seemed
to shake the city. Providence spectators flung hats into the
air. Rochester players poured from the bench. Men climbed over
railings. Boys leaped the fence. The police gave up.

Rochester had won, 8 to 6.

An inside-the-park grand slam with two out in the ninth—and
Ray had run so fast that he had passed the
man ahead without passing him illegally, crossing the plate first
by circling outside him in the final desperate instant.

Delaney reached Ray under a mountain of players. He pulled
at arms, legs, shirts, and caps.

"Let him breathe! You'll kill him!"

Ray emerged, dirt-covered and dazed. His shirt was torn. Blood
ran from one elbow. He tried to stand and could
not.

Delaney caught him.

"I told you," he shouted, though his voice broke. "I
told you! Gilbat nearly lost it—Clammer nearly lost it—and you
won it!"

Ray looked toward the scoreboard. The numbers were changing.

Rochester—8.

Providence—6.

"Did the run count?" he asked.

"Count? All four counted!"

"O'Brien?"

"Safe!"

Ray smiled and sat down in the dust.

Gilbat came bursting from the runway with two policemen after
him. No one attempted to stop him now. His duster
hung in ribbons, his eye was purple, and somebody had
given him another sack of peanuts.

"I knew he'd do it!" he cried. "I told him
where to hit!"

"You told him where they ain't," said Haines.

"Well, there wasn't nobody there."

The stretcher bearers appeared at the runway. Clammer, revived and
bandaged, had insisted upon being brought out. They raised the
stretcher so he could see the field.

"What happened?" he whispered.

"Ray hit a home run," said Delaney.

"Over the fence?"

"No."

Clammer frowned weakly.

"Then it wasn't—"

"He ran it out."

"How far?"

"All the way."

Clammer looked at Ray, who stood surrounded by players and
spectators, his bare red head shining in the afternoon sun.

"Did they cheer?"

Delaney glanced at the wild grandstand, the swarming field, the
tossing hats, the roaring bleachers, and the Providence players themselves
applauding their conqueror.

"Some," he said.

Clammer sighed.

"Wish I'd seen it."

Gilbat pushed forward.

"You should've seen my homer in the duster."

"It didn't count," said Delaney.

"It went over the wall."

"It didn't count."

"Ball's still gone."

Delaney stared at him. Then, because the pennant was won,
because Clammer was alive, because Ray had done what no
sane manager could have expected of any human legs, and
because wrath itself has limits, Delaney began to laugh.

The players laughed with him. Clammer laughed until his shoulder
hurt. Even Ray laughed, sitting again upon the ground.

Around them the crowd continued to roar.

Years afterward men argued about that game. They argued whether
Gilbat should have been expelled, whether Clammer could have avoided
the fence, whether Ray's drive should have been scored a
triple and an error, and whether he had crossed the
plate before O'Brien. Men who had sat a quarter of
a mile away swore they had seen every inch. Men
who had not been born described the throw.

That is baseball lore. Records preserve figures, but crowds preserve
wonders.

The official score gave Ray a home run. Delaney kept
the scorecard framed above his desk. Gilbat claimed the torn
duster and wore it at banquets. Clammer, when his shoulder
healed, repeatedly demonstrated his catch, though never again quite so
near a fence. And Providence remembered the red-headed outfield—not with
affection, perhaps, but with the respect due any calamity magnificent
enough to become history.

Yet those who saw the finish remembered more than the
pennant.

It was not so much the game that mattered, nor
even the four runs. It was the sight of a
man running beyond fatigue, beyond probability, almost beyond belief—the red
head bare, the body bent forward, the legs driving like
machinery, the whole figure transformed into a flying streak. It
was O'Brien racing for home with Ray overtaking him; the
ball flashing from relay to plate; the catcher wheeling; the
leap, the dust, the silence, and then the thunder.

Gilbat had given them comedy.

Clammer had given them spectacle.

But Reddie Ray had given them baseball.

I don't know whether children care much about Easter eggs
now. They have so many contrivances for being amused that
a plain egg, even when it is red or blue,
may seem a poor affair. Their rabbits are made of

purple eggs, and mottled eggs, and eggs scratched with initials,

My grandmother had the true art of them. She would

charming uncertainty which belongs to all miracles performed in kitchens.
My aunts would exclaim; we children would dispute possession; my
grandfather would pretend that he had never seen such an
egg since the year the British burned Washington—though what relation
the two events bore I never learned.

Those things stay with one. I have forgotten sermons intended
to regulate my whole life, but I remember the wet

the saucepan, and the look of my grandmother's black silk
apron when she held up the best specimen against it.
Perhaps the things which we call important are merely those
we have agreed to forget, while the trifles are what
the heart keeps for its own use.

The cemetery lay open to the east. I had never

At the borders of the burial-ground bands of musicians had

There was a prayer. I cannot repeat it. There are

Then the horns sounded.

You have heard brass instruments in the street, where they

The sun came at that instant, or memory has made

Edith explained that a dead bird could not hatch from
an Easter egg. This did not help our standing. The
young woman glanced toward the rear of the shop, perhaps
in search of protection, and a stout lady in black
emerged. She was the proprietor, and she possessed the practical
imagination of her sex. After hearing half the story, she
understood the whole of it.

"We can put flowers there," she said. "Forget-me-nots would be
lovely with the ribbon."

Edith embraced this lady morally, if not physically. Tom asked
what would become of the bird.

"We shall use it on another hat."

"That doesn't save it."

"It is too late to save it," I said. "We
can only refuse to profit aesthetically by its death."

"Is that what humanity means?" Edith asked.

"It is sometimes all that humanity manages."

The proprietor laughed and brought out sprays of forget-me-nots, pale
blue with small yellow centers, and a little foliage which
Tom inspected to make sure no animal had suffered. The
bird was detached. Without it the bonnet looked bare, almost
reproachful; then the flowers were bent along the crown, a
cluster set where the bird had perched, and the whole
thing became gentler and, to my eyes, much more like
my wife.

We paid for it. I must say "we," though the
children contributed only two cents and a perforated streetcar token.
The bonnet was packed in a round bandbox, tied with
cord, and committed to Tom, who carried it as if
escorting a national treasure. Edith carried the discarded bird in
a paper bag because she had not the heart to
leave it to decorate another woman.

"What did she do with it?" Acton asked.

"Buried it behind Aunt Julia's tool shed. There was a
service, I believe, conducted by Tom, and the gardener afterward
planted beans over the grave. But that was later.

On our way back, we perfected the presentation. The empty
purple shell was placed in the box with the bonnet,
one half on either side. Edith wished to add a
note from the chick; Tom said chicks could not write.
I reminded him that this one could purchase millinery. He
yielded, and we composed the following:

"Dear Mama: I am what came out of the Easter
egg. Please wear me. Your affectionate Chick."

Tom wrote it in block letters to disguise our hands.
Then he crossed out "affectionate" because he could not fit
the last syllables on the paper, and substituted "loving," which
seemed to me an improvement.

We concealed the box in Aunt Julia's spare room until
afternoon. The children behaved so unnaturally at luncheon that my
wife asked whether they had broken anything. Tom refused a
second helping of pie, which nearly betrayed us; Edith gazed
at her mother with a melting tenderness; and the twins,
who had been given only the general fact that a
surprise existed, whispered "Is it now?" after every course.

At last I announced that the Easter chick had returned
from town.

My wife looked at me. "What have you been doing?"

"Nothing. Nature has been doing it."

We led her into the parlor. The bandbox stood on
the center table surrounded by all five children. Aunt Julia
had been admitted to the secret and was pretending ignorance
so visibly that no jury would have acquitted her. My
wife untied the cord. The children could not endure the
slowness with which grown women preserve ribbon; they twitched, sighed,
and advised her to cut it. She lifted the lid,
moved aside the tissue paper, and saw the bonnet.

For a moment she did not speak. Then she took
it out very carefully. The forget-me-nots trembled along the crown.

"Oh," she said.

Only that. But the children burst into explanation—the egg, the
chick, the trip to town, the dead bird, the humanitarian
question, the flowers, the two cents, the token, the letter,
and the burial still to be performed. Each told the
whole at once, correcting the others in the interests of
truth until truth itself was nearly lost.

My wife put on the bonnet before the parlor mirror.
It suited her. I had known it would; not because
I possessed any taste in bonnets, but because I possessed
some knowledge of her face. The blue-gray softened her brown
hair, and the forget-me-nots gave the thing a spring freshness
which the poor stiff bird had never had.

She turned to us with an expression of such complete
surprise that I congratulated myself upon our secrecy.

"Do you truly like it?" Edith asked.

"I truly do."

"You're not thinking about Tom's overcoat?"

"I am trying not to."

"And you didn't know?"

"How could I know?"

At this the children laughed with the intoxication of successful
conspiracy. They climbed upon her, crushed her skirt, endangered the
bonnet, and kissed her wherever they could reach. Tom stood
back at first, being ten and masculine, but she drew
him to her and kissed him too. Then she kissed
me, which Minver may consider an indecent detail for a
club, but it is essential to the facts.

"No," Minver said, "it is the first fact Rulledge has
fully understood."

Rulledge ignored him. "And she had not guessed?"

Newton smiled.

That evening, after the children had been put to bed
and Aunt Julia had retired, my wife and I sat
in the hotel room among the ruins of travel—open valises,
small shoes, hair ribbons, a wooden horse with one wheel
gone, railway guides, and clothing hung wherever the management had
not intended clothing to hang. The new bonnet rested on
the bureau, safe within its box.

I was still pleased with myself. A husband should distrust
that condition; it commonly precedes instruction.

"I think we carried it off rather well," I said.

"Beautifully."

"They never gave the least hint?"

"Not the least."

"You were entirely surprised?"

"Entirely."

There was something in her voice. I looked at her.
She was sewing a button upon one of the twins'
underwaists and bending her head to hide a smile.

"When did they tell you?"

"Tell me what?"

"The whole plot."

She bit the thread and looked up. "Last night."

"Last night! Before we bought the bonnet?"

"Before breakfast."

"Who told?"

"All of them, except the baby. The twins began it.
Edith supplied the important details, and Tom corrected them."

"But I charged Tom particularly—"

"Yes. He was troubled about that."

"He promised not to say a word."

"He didn't, at first. But when they were saying their
prayers, Edith asked whether keeping a secret from one's mother
was the same as telling a lie. That opened the
ethical question. Tom said it was not a lie if
the secret concerned a present. One of the twins said
it was a lie if Mama asked. Then they all
remembered I had already asked whether they had broken anything,
and they felt the answer might have been misleading."

"They told you during prayers?"

"Between 'Forgive us our trespasses' and 'God bless Papa.'"

"That seems an unfair use of religion."

"I thought it a very natural one."

"Then you knew about the hat, the bird, the flowers—everything?"

"I did not know whether you would really buy it."

"And when I brought you into the parlor—when you opened
the box—when you said, 'How could I know?'"

She laid the little garment aside and came over to
me. "You had all taken so much trouble to deceive
me, dear. It would have been cruel not to be
deceived."

There was the whole marriage in that, if you like:
the children pretending to believe me, I pretending to deceive
them, all of us conspiring to surprise their mother, and
their mother, who knew from the beginning, preserving our illusion
because it was the tenderest gift we had to offer.
Nobody in the affair was exactly cheated, and nobody was
exactly truthful; yet I think there was no falsehood in
it.

She called me a goose again, I remember, and put
her hand on my shoulder. A young man might ask
for a more explicit declaration. An older one knows when
he has received it.

Newton's cigar had gone out. He looked at it with
mild surprise, as if time, and not neglect, had extinguished
it.

The next day we started home. My wife wore the
bonnet. The children insisted upon this, though the weather had
turned windy and she feared for the forget-me-nots. At the
station they surrounded her in a body, guarding the miraculous
chick from trunks, umbrellas, porters, and the general brutality of
travel. The purple shell was packed in cotton and brought
back to Cambridge, where it survived for several months upon
Edith's mantel until the baby ate part of it and
housekeeping disposed of the rest.

I never saw the sunrise service again. I always meant
to. We mean to revisit the great experiences, as if
they were views and would wait unchanged for our convenience;
but the children grew, Aunt Julia died, business intervened, and
Easters came with rain or engagements or ordinary fatigue. The
bonnet wore out. The forget-me-nots were put away in a
drawer, where I found them many years later, faded nearly
white. Tom's overcoat was bought after all. He has sons
older now than he was then, and I have heard
him tell them—without acknowledgment—that green Easter eggs hatch parrots.

So one's inventions pass into tradition.

But when Easter comes, I see two mornings. In one,
the light spreads over the low stones at Bethlehem, and
the horns call above the graves while a multitude sings
of life arising from death. In the other, five children
stand about a broken purple shell and choose, for love
of the game and of one another, to believe that
something beautiful has come out of it.

I do not confuse the two. Yet I cannot wholly
separate them. Faith is not make-believe, and a bonnet is
not resurrection. Still, the heart may learn one language through
many alphabets. That Easter taught me, among greater things, that
imagination can be a form of kindness; that children often
understand our fables better than we do; and that the
person who loves us most may be the one who
consents, with open eyes, to our affectionate deception.

"Well," Newton concluded, "that is the history of the birdless
bonnet. I warned you there was no story."

"There was a story," Rulledge said warmly.

"There were at least three," Wanhope corrected: "the religious experience,
the children's collective fantasy, and the marital collusion. The interesting
point is that every participant maintained two levels of consciousness—"

"The interesting point," Minver interrupted, "is that Newton came to
the club under false pretenses."

Newton looked at him. "How so?"

"He represented himself as a guest. He is plainly an
agent."

"An agent for what?"

"Matrimony. He has spent an hour exhibiting a wife who
allows herself to be surprised, five children who transform boiled
eggs into moral symbols, and a family journey ending in
poetry. It is recruiting propaganda."

Acton said that if marriage included five children, eleven pieces
of luggage, a frantic pullet, and the purchase of millinery,
the propaganda had been unusually candid.

Rulledge remained thoughtful. "Still," he said, "I should have liked
to see her in the bonnet."

Minver rose and touched the bell for fresh coffee.

"That," he told Newton, "is the most alarming effect of
your tale. You have brought Rulledge within sight of marriage."

Newton laughed. "Then perhaps the egg has hatched again."

And because none of us could better that, we let
the matter rest there, while the rain whispered against the
club windows and, beyond the smoke and lamplight, the Easter
city went on displaying its flowers, its feathers, its vanities,
and its perennial hope of beginning the world anew.

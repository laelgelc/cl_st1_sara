From the ridge the colliery appeared less like a collection
of buildings than a black principality, established in defiance of
the green hills surrounding it. The breaker rose above the
roofs, gaunt and ponderous, its long chutes slanting from its
ribs, its windows filmed with coal dust. Smoke streamed from
several stacks and spread beneath the morning sky until the
sun looked through it like a tarnished coin. Below, locomotives
dragged lines of cars across the scarred earth. They shrieked,
collided, retreated, and advanced again, obedient creatures serving an appetite
greater than their own.

We had come as visitors.

That distinction seemed important while we stood upon the ridge
among sumac and late goldenrod, with clean cuffs, polished shoes,
and the privilege of departure. A few hundred yards below
us, the ground had been stripped of grass and trees.
Culm banks lifted their sombre backs beside pools stained orange
by mine water. Houses huddled at the edge of the
waste, small wooden structures whose laundry, children, and broken fences
were powdered alike with black. Even the creek had accepted
the color of the place and crawled under the railway
trestle like a stream of ink.

Then the breaker sounded its whistle.

It was not a call. It was a bellow from
an iron throat, a declaration that the monster was awake
and must be fed.

Our guide, a square man named Harlan, had worked thirty-two
years in and around the mine. Two fingers were absent
from his left hand. He noticed us noticing the mutilation
and held it up with a dry smile.

"Breaker took 'em when I was a lad," he said.
"She was quicker then. Or maybe I was slower."

We descended the road.

As we approached, the building enlarged until it abolished the
landscape. Its beams were black, its sides trembled, and the
whole enormous carcass seemed to shudder with digestion. Coal cars
climbed an inclined plane and vanished through an opening near
the roof. A moment later came crash and thunder, the
rolling concussion of tons released upon iron. Wheels screamed; chains
clanked; screens rattled; chutes boomed; engines coughed; whistles answered whistles.
Every beam had a separate voice, yet together they formed
one implacable roar.

Inside, the dust received us.

It entered the nostrils, settled upon the tongue, and discovered
every fold of clothing. Daylight passed through broken panes but
did not illuminate the interior; it became a brownish substance
suspended among revolving wheels and vibrating screens. At first we
saw machinery only—belts rushing over drums, chains lifting, iron plates
shaking with feverish violence. Then little faces emerged beneath us.

The slate-pickers sat astride narrow boards beside a descending river
of coal.

The floor pitched at nearly forty degrees. Down its shaking
channels poured lumps of anthracite mixed with slate, stone, splintered
timber, and rubbish from the mine. The boys bent over
this flood, seized the refuse with quick hands, and flung
it into chutes at their feet. Some were ten years
old, some perhaps twelve or fourteen. One, we were told,
had begun at nine. Their shirts were open at the
throat despite the cold, and their faces were so uniformly
black that expression survived only in glittering eyes and sudden
white teeth.

They worked from seven in the morning until six at
night, with half an hour at noon. For this they
earned from sixty-five to ninety cents a day.

One may hear such figures in an office and preserve
composure. Written in a ledger they are harmless, almost decorous.
But there, amid the flying dust, each cent seemed measured
against a breath.

The boys' hands moved without pause. They were cracked, scarred,
and swollen at the knuckles. Sharp slate cut them; coal
crushed their nails; the shaking floor transmitted its agitation into
their thin bodies. When a large piece jammed in a
screen, a boy sprang up and kicked it loose with
the swagger of an experienced workman. Another snatched a fragment
from the stream and hurled it at his neighbor. The
victim replied with an oath of astonishing completeness. They laughed,
shoved one another, and returned to their labor before the
overseer could turn his head.

"Mind yer own chute, Jimmy!"

"Aw, shut it. I'll knock yeh down to the culm
pile."

"You couldn't knock over a sick chicken."

Their voices were shrill and nearly devoured by the machinery.
They gambled with marbles at noon, Harlan said. They smoked
behind the boiler house. They fought, boasted, swore, and dreamed
of promotion. They were very ambitious. In time a reliable
picker might become a door boy underground, then a mule
driver, then a laborer beside a miner. At the summit
waited the right to kneel all day under a roof
that might crush him, drilling rock while powder smoke and
coal dust prepared his lungs for burial.

A small boy nearest us coughed into his sleeve. The
cough seized him with such force that his shoulders jerked.
He examined what he had brought up, rubbed the sleeve
against his trousers, and grinned when he perceived our concern.

"Dust tickles," he shouted.

Then the coal came faster, and his hands flew into
it.

We passed through chambers where the breaker cleaned, graded, and
sorted its food. Great teeth reduced boulders; screens divided the
pieces according to size; jets of air drove lighter refuse
aside. Everywhere the building shook with voracious efficiency. It possessed
nostrils that exhaled dust, jaws that cracked stone, bowels that
separated profit from waste, and a hundred mouths through which
the finished coal poured into waiting cars. Men attended it
with oil cans and bars, scrambling along narrow platforms while
belts whipped past their sleeves. One misstep would offer an
arm, a foot, or an entire body to the revolving
appetite.

Outside, the relative quiet stunned us. Yet the roar followed
in our ears like imprisoned thunder.

The shaft house stood beyond the breaker. Before entering it,
we exchanged our coats for old canvas jackets and put
on hard caps carrying tin lamps. Harlan inspected each flame.
He spoke of gas, though this mine used a fan-wheel
to force air through its workings. The fan, forty feet
in diameter, revolved night and day. If it stopped for
long, the mine would begin to fill with invisible death.

"Fire boss goes in first," he said. "Tests the places
before the shift. Mostly it's right enough."

"Mostly?" one of us asked.

He shrugged. "Ain't anything always."

At the shaft's mouth waited the cage, an iron platform
enclosed by bars. It was scarcely larger than a closet
and smelled of wet rope, grease, and old stone. Eight
of us crowded upon it with Harlan. Behind the bars
yawned an opening whose depth could not be measured by
sight. Somewhere below, water dripped with slow, hollow regularity.

The signal bell rang. Machinery answered overhead.

The floor vanished.

It was a plunge, a fall. Our stomachs appeared to
remain above while our bodies were flung downward into a
sable gulf. Timber braces shot past. Levels opened and disappeared
like black mouths. Water struck our faces. The lamp flames
flattened and struggled against the rushing air, frail birds tethered
to our caps. One hand rebelled and clutched the bar;
another found a stranger's shoulder. There was no dignity in
us then. The instincts of the body had overthrown manners.

We descended eleven hundred and forty feet.

The cage slowed so abruptly that our knees bent. A
wall of wet rock emerged from the dark. Beyond it
stretched the gangway, low-roofed and glistening, with narrow rails disappearing
beneath a row of orange lamps. Men stood waiting to
ascend. Their faces were spectres' faces—eyes shining from black hollows,
teeth exposed in luminous smiles. One carried a lunch pail
battered into shapelessness. Another had a bandage around his wrist.

"Fresh tourists?" he called.

"Fresh enough," Harlan replied.

"Send 'em down number nine. Roof's been wantin' company."

The men laughed and entered the cage. Their joke remained
with us after they had risen, a small act of
defiance cast into the darkness.

We followed the gangway. The air was cool and damp,
and the walls sweated against our sleeves. Water ran in
ditches beside the track. Here and there timber sets supported
the roof, but elsewhere bare strata hung above us in
ponderous shelves. Harlan tapped one with his stick.

"Good top," he said.

A hundred yards farther on he pointed to broken stone
packed with props.

"Bad top."

To our untrained sight there was little difference. This was
perhaps the most alarming fact. Death underground possessed no need
of theatrical announcement. A seam could loosen without noise. A
hairline fracture might be the signature of a thousand tons
preparing to descend. The miner learned to read these inscriptions
through touch, sound, and long familiarity; yet nature sometimes wrote
in a hand no man could decipher.

The mine was a city deprived of sky. Its gangways
were streets, its crosscuts alleys, its chambers houses occupied by
toil. Signals traveled along wires. Doors regulated the current of
air. Pumps labored in deep stations. Rails divided and rejoined.
But no map in the mind could retain the labyrinth.
Turn followed turn until direction became superstition. Had Harlan's lamp
disappeared, we should have stood helpless among passages stretching for
more than twenty miles.

At a ventilation door a boy waited upon a bench.
He could not have been more than thirteen. Whenever a
mule team approached, he opened the door, allowed it to
pass, and shut it again so that the air continued
along its appointed course.

"What do you make?" we asked.

"Seventy cents."

"How long are you here?"

"Ten hours, if they're runnin' regular."

"Are you alone?"

He looked down one black corridor and then the other.

"Mostly."

A rat moved near his boot. He tossed it a
crumb from his dinner pail.

"Charlie keeps me," he said.

The door closed behind us, and he was erased.

Farther in, we heard the mules before we saw them:
hoofs striking ties, chains dragging, a driver's cry rebounding from
the stone. Four coal cars appeared, creeping around a curve.
Their lamps made small orange circles in the dark. The
mule seemed at first an enormous rat, black, low, and
gleaming with sweat. It pulled with its head down while
the driver walked beside the cars.

"Gee there, Sal! Git over!"

Sal stopped near us and breathed heavily. Her flanks were
scarred where the harness rubbed. A pale film covered one
eye. She had been underground six years.

"Does she ever go up?" someone asked.

The driver stared as though the question were peculiar.

"What for?"

We touched the animal's warm neck. She had once known
the surface. Perhaps somewhere within that patient skull there remained
a meadow: grass rising cool around the fetlocks, flies humming
in sunlight, wind carrying the fragrance of clover. Perhaps she
remembered rain falling freely rather than dripping from stone, and
trees whose branches moved against blue distances. Or perhaps the
mine had extinguished these images one by one until darkness
was no longer imprisonment but the whole circumference of existence.

There were twenty-three mules below, Harlan told us. Some remained
for eight or ten years. When at last they were
brought up, sunlight frightened them. Open space made them tremble.

Sal leaned into the harness. Chains tightened, wheels complained, and
she disappeared with her black procession.

We entered a chamber where two miners were preparing a
blast. The coal seam lay four feet high, obliging us
to stoop. One man knelt beside a hand drill while
the other swung a sledge. Turn, strike; turn, strike. Steel
rang against stone. Each blow traveled through the cramped air
and into our teeth.

The miners worked by contract and might earn three dollars
on a favorable day, less when the seam was faulty
or cars were delayed. From this came the cost of
powder, oil, tools, and sometimes a helper's wages. Their labor
began before dawn. They drilled holes, charged them, blasted the
coal, broke it into manageable pieces, loaded the cars, and
set props beneath the roof. Payment was made by the
ton; the mountain kept no account of the hours required.

One miner wiped his face, leaving a pale streak near
his temple.

"Stand back when we touch her," he said. "She kicks."

They packed powder into the drilled hole, inserted the squib,
and sent us around a bend. The miner lit it
and ran crouching toward us.

For several seconds nothing happened.

The waiting was more terrible than the explosion. One imagined
the fire traveling inward, a tiny deliberate creature seeking the
buried charge. One listened to the mountain's silence and felt
the vast weight above—rock, earth, houses, railway tracks, perhaps a
church with its bell, all resting upon these fragile chambers.

Then the shot struck.

A red glare sprang from the opening. The mine roared;
air slapped our faces; fragments clattered against the walls. Smoke
rolled around the bend, acrid and blue-gray, swallowing the lamps.
The miners waited for the roof to settle and went
back.

"Good one," said the first.

He sounded pleased, almost affectionate, as if a dangerous animal
had performed a trick.

It is war. Man attacks with drill, powder, pick, pump,
and timber. The earth answers with gas, stone, water, and
the slow black assault upon the lungs. Man gains a
chamber, a pillar, a trainload; the earth exacts fingers, sight,
breath, years. At times she kills one scout. At another
time she buries a regiment. She has strength beyond computation
and patience without limit. She possesses eternity.

Yet to name nature as the sole enemy would be
false. Nature did not set the boys beside the breaker
for sixty-five cents. Nature did not hurry the driver, thin
the props, neglect the fan, or demand another hundred cars
before night. Behind the visible battle stood an invisible commander
whose orders were written in tonnage and profit. The mountain
was implacable, but greed gave method to the sacrifice.

We passed an abandoned chamber where a fall had occurred
the previous winter. Broken timbers projected from the rubble like
snapped bones. Two men had been caught. One died at
once; the other lived behind the fall for thirty-one hours
while rescuers dug toward his voice. They reached him too
late.

Harlan lifted his lamp toward the wreckage.

"His brother still works section four," he said.

There was nothing else to say. Underground, tragedy had no
room for monuments. The rails were diverted, the loose roof
fenced, and work continued around the grave.

The dangers multiplied as Harlan named them. Gas might gather
in a high pocket and ignite at a naked flame.
A premature blast might fill a man with slate. A
runaway car could overtake a driver in the gangway. A
cable could break. A mule could kick. Water imprisoned in
abandoned workings might burst through a wall and flood a
level before the men reached the shaft. Fire could travel
through timber and coal, feeding upon the mine itself. Sometimes
the roof fell with warning—ticks, whispers, little showers of dust.
Sometimes it fell in one instant of ponderous precision.

And always there was the dust.

It floated invisibly after the heavier clouds settled. Men drew
it into their lungs day after day, year after year,
until breath became labor. We met an old miner beside
a pump station. He said he was fifty-one, though his
bent shoulders and gray, dust-stiffened mustache gave him the age
of seventy. After speaking, he paused to breathe. Each inhalation
was a negotiation.

"Worked below since I was twelve," he told us. "Breaker
first. Then doors. Then drivin'. Been cuttin' coal thirty year."

"Will you retire soon?"

He smiled.

"Reckon I will when the mine retires me."

The pump behind him beat steadily, drawing black water from
a sump. Its rods rose and fell like the limbs
of an imprisoned giant. The man coughed, spat into the
ditch, and returned to his wrench.

At our farthest point we extinguished our lamps.

The dark did not merely surround us. It arrived like
matter and pressed against the eyes. A hand moved an
inch before one's face and did not exist. Space disappeared;
the roof might have been one foot above or a
mile away. We heard water, breathing, the small shift of
boots upon coal. Deprived of sight, imagination populated the passage
with falling stone, rising flood, creeping gas. One understood how
a lamp could become more precious than bread, how its
tiny flame might be companion, compass, sun, and salvation.

Harlan relit his lamp. His face emerged piece by piece:
flame, fingers, nose, eyes, teeth. The others followed until we
stood again within our frail constellation.

On the return journey, familiarity did not make the mine
less dreadful. It made the dread more exact. We now
recognized the ventilation doors, the loaded cars, the props bowed
under pressure. We knew that the black walls were not
boundaries but temporary arrangements. Behind them waited old chambers, trapped
water, compressed gas, and acres of excavated darkness.

A mule train passed, and its driver called, "Look lively!"

We flattened ourselves against the dripping wall. Cars rumbled within
inches of our knees, each heaped with coal that shone
under the lamps like broken diamonds. Beautiful, one might have
said, had beauty not seemed an indecency there.

At the shaft, men gathered for the upward trip. They
joked, traded bits of food, and discussed a sick child,
a landlord, a prizefight, and the price of flour. The
infernal disguises fell from them when they spoke. They were
not imps or ghouls or apparitions, though darkness had painted
them so. They were tired men going home. One had
brought a piece of sparkling mineral for his daughter. Another
shared the last coffee in his bottle with a boy.
Their ordinary tenderness condemned the place more powerfully than any
spectacle.

We entered the cage.

The bell rang.

Upward we flew through wet, rushing blackness. Levels flashed past,
each holding its unseen population. The pressure changed; our ears
rebelled; the lamps trembled. Then a gray square appeared overhead.
It expanded rapidly, became silver, became white.

We rose into noon.

Sunlight struck us with almost physical force. We blinked and
covered our eyes. The sky was blue—astonishingly, extravagantly blue. Air
entered our alarmed lungs, cool and sweet despite the breaker's
smoke. A patch of weeds beside the shaft shone with
a green so vivid that we stared at it. Clouds
moved freely. Wind touched our faces. Far hills stood in
golden light like serene monarchs ignorant of the dungeons beneath
their feet.

For several minutes we could not speak.

Then the breaker roared again.

A locomotive pushed loaded cars up the incline. One by
one they climbed, tipped, and emptied themselves into the building's
voracious mouth. The coal we had seen blasted and loaded
in darkness now rushed through the monster's jaws. Within, the
slate-pickers bent over their chutes; below, the door boy sat
with his rat; Sal strained against her harness; miners listened
to the roof and breathed the dust; pumps fought the
accumulating water; the fan-wheel revolved to hold back invisible death.

We walked toward the ridge in the sunlight. Behind us,
empty cars descended and full cars rose. The procession did
not pause. It had begun before our arrival and would
continue after our clean clothes, our astonishment, and our pity
had gone elsewhere.

At the crest we turned.

The colliery lay black upon the wounded valley. Smoke spread
from its nostrils. Its teeth crashed; its iron bowels shook;
its appetite remained inscrutable and immense. Men called the breaker
a machine, as though iron alone had invented its hunger.
But we had seen what fed it, and what it
consumed besides coal.

The whistle bellowed.

Another string of cars crawled from the shaft.

And the monster opened its mouth.

By half past eight the light had already discovered the
one crack between the curtains and laid a pale gold
finger across Reginald Peacock's eyelids.

He turned his face into the pillow.

"Reginald."

No answer.

"Reginald, it's half past eight."

The voice belonged to his wife. It possessed none of
those tender, dissolving qualities which made certain women's voices seem
to come through silver water. It was merely accurate.

He opened one eye. Hilda stood at the foot of
the bed, dressed, buttoned, prepared. How extraordinary that she should
be so completely prepared at such an hour! Her hair
had been subdued, her shoes fastened, her keys hung at
her waist. She looked like someone appointed by the morning
to enforce it.

"I heard you," he said.

"Breakfast is getting cold."

There it was—breakfast! Bacon, toast, the milk jug, the newspaper
folded beside his plate. The whole heavy apparatus of existence
thrust between him and the exquisite fragments of his dream.
He had been standing beneath dark trees while a woman
sang from a marble balcony. Or perhaps he had been
singing. In either case there had been applause.

"You might have let me sleep another ten minutes."

"You have Miss Bell at ten."

"I know perfectly well whom I have at ten."

"And Adrian has missed the eight-fifteen. He says he can't
find his geometry book."

Reginald closed both eyes. Geometry! Already the day was ruined
by angles.

"My dear Hilda, surely at fifteen a boy can be
expected to manage his own books."

"He says you took it last night to prop up
the piano stool."

Reginald sat up. His silver-grey hair, long enough to suggest
temperament but not long enough to alarm the respectable, stood
out over his ears.

"The stool was uneven."

"So is Adrian's education."

She went out before he could answer. That was her
method. She dropped these hard little facts—school, bills, milk, repairs—and
withdrew, leaving them on the carpet like drawing pins.

He rose with tragic slowness and wrapped himself in his
peacock-blue dressing gown. In the mirror above the washstand his
face appeared pale, bruised by sleep, interesting. The suffering artist
at dawn. There was still distinction in the line from
temple to cheek; the eyes, though puffy, retained their peculiar
depth. Women saw that depth at once. Men, naturally, did
not.

Hot water arrived in a can carried by Ellen, the
maid, whose red wrists offended him.

"Not too full," he said. "And Ellen—the towel warmed, please.
Warmed, not roasted."

"Yes, sir."

One had to insist upon beauty even in small things.
Especially in small things. To bathe in tepid water and
rub oneself with a clammy towel was to begin the
day spiritually defeated.

From below came the clatter of dishes and Hilda's voice
telling Adrian not to bolt his tea. Reginald poured water
over his shoulders and hummed a rising phrase. The bathroom,
though narrow and smelling faintly of soap and pipes, gave
back a surprisingly noble resonance. He repeated the phrase, opened
it, adorned it, carried it upward until the final note
shone above the steam like a bright bird.

There! That was what Hilda never understood. Life came to
him as music. Even bathing had its crescendo.

At breakfast Adrian sat hunched over a piece of toast,
his school cap beside him. He had inherited Hilda's brown
hair and Reginald's long hands, though at present he used
them chiefly to conceal his face whenever his father addressed
him in front of other boys.

"My dear fellow," Reginald said, entering, "I believe this belongs
to you."

He held out the geometry book ceremoniously, between two fingers.

Adrian snatched it.

"You might say thank you," said Hilda.

"Oh—thanks."

Reginald smiled with sorrowful tolerance. "Youth accepts everything and acknowledges
nothing."

Adrian glanced at the clock.

"May I go?"

"You may," said Hilda.

But Reginald raised one hand. "A moment. Your tie."

"What about it?"

"It announces despair."

Adrian's ears reddened. Through the window two boys in school
caps had appeared at the gate.

"For heaven's sake, Father."

Reginald leaned across the table and loosened the knot, intending
to retie it with one swift, affectionate movement. Adrian jerked
away.

"It's all right!"

The boys outside were looking in. One grinned.

"To be ashamed of grace," Reginald observed, "is one of
the more barbarous privileges of adolescence."

Adrian fled.

Hilda poured tea.

"You needn't make a ceremony of everything."

"I was trying to help the boy."

"You were making him late."

"I sometimes wonder," said Reginald, spreading marmalade with delicate severity,
"whether anything that cannot be entered in an account book
has reality for you."

"The butcher's account has reality."

Of course. The butcher. At once the morning, which had
almost recovered its radiance, became a ledger ruled in blue.

"He has sent again," she continued. "And the dairy bill
is three weeks overdue."

"My quarterly fees come in next month."

"Two pupils have not paid for the last quarter."

"They will."

"Mrs. Webb said she thought you had forgotten to send
her bill."

Reginald laid down his knife. "I do not pursue people."

"No. I do."

The bacon had congealed. A skin floated on the coffee.
From the street came the shrill call of a newspaper
boy, vulgar and victorious.

Hilda looked tired. For an instant Reginald noticed the little
hollows beside her mouth and remembered, with mild surprise, that
she had once worn white muslin and violets and had
laughed whenever he imitated the conductor at the conservatoire. But
the memory required tenderness, and tenderness before ten in the
morning was impossible.

"You might at least remember," he said, "that my work
demands a certain atmosphere."

"What atmosphere?"

"Peace. Confidence. Freedom from perpetual anxiety."

"I should like some of that atmosphere myself."

He stared at her. So it was open war.

At five minutes to ten he entered the drawing room
transformed. The blue dressing gown, the unpleasant bacon, the butcher
and Adrian's geometry had vanished. He wore a black velvet
coat. A pearl-coloured tie flowed beneath his chin. In a
thin glass on the piano stood three yellow roses, purchased
yesterday and rearranged this morning so that their decline appeared
intentional.

Miss Bell arrived breathless, carrying music and a bunch of
Parma violets.

"For you," she said. "I saw them and thought—oh, I
don't know—I thought they were like your playing."

"My dear child."

Reginald accepted them with both hands. Violets! Hilda would have
put them in a jam jar. He bent over their
dark fragrance.

"You bring spring into a room that has known winter."

Miss Bell blushed so violently that even her ears became
pink. She was a narrow young woman with anxious shoulders
and a voice that trembled whenever it approached the upper
register. But anxiety could be sculpted. Reginald believed in troubled
material.

She sang her first phrase.

"No, no. You are asking permission of the note. Never
ask! Possess it."

She began again.

"Better. But do not merely sing 'I love you.' Become
the love. Let it rise through you—here." He touched his
own breast. "Again."

On the fourth attempt Miss Bell produced a clear, floating
tone. Reginald stepped back as though struck by light.

"Yes!"

She stopped.

"Was that right?"

"Right?" He smiled. "It was inevitable."

Her eyes filled.

"I couldn't do anything before I came to you. My
other teacher said I had no temperament."

"Your other teacher was deaf."

"Oh, Mr. Peacock!"

He turned toward the roses to conceal the exquisite gravity
of his expression. How beautiful it was to awaken a
soul! And how natural that young women should respond to
him. It was not personal vanity—far from it. They felt
in him the thing they lacked: courage, colour, flame.

When Miss Bell had gone, leaving the violets and a
faint powdery perfume, he rang for Ellen.

"Please take these upstairs and ask Mrs. Peacock to put
them in water."

Hilda herself appeared at the door.

"Your eleven o'clock is here."

"Already?"

"It's seven minutes past."

"Time moves differently during creation."

"So does the clock, apparently."

Behind her waited Mrs. Cresswell, large, glossy, draped in fox
fur. Her mouth was painted the colour of sealing wax.

"Dear lady!" cried Reginald, advancing. "Forgive this confusion. The morning
has been conspiring against us."

Mrs. Cresswell extended her hand.

"I've been simply dying for my lesson."

"Then we must restore you to life."

Hilda passed them without expression, carrying the violets toward the
kitchen.

Mrs. Cresswell sang loudly and inaccurately, but with such confidence
that correction had to be introduced as praise.

"Magnificent energy," Reginald told her. "Now we refine it. A
waterfall is impressive, but a fountain has style."

She laughed, delighted. "You wicked man!"

He was not wicked. He was tactful.

After her song she remained beside the piano, turning the
pages without looking at them.

"Are you singing at Lady Wren's tonight?"

"I have been persuaded."

"Everyone is talking about it. She told Julia Sanderson you
were the only person in London who could make that
dreadful Italian song bearable."

Reginald lowered his lashes.

"One serves the music."

"And will you dine with me first? Quite quietly. My
husband is in Manchester."

A tiny silence bloomed between them.

"My dear Mrs. Cresswell—"

"Flora."

"My dear Flora, I should be only too delighted."

She pressed his arm as she left. The pressure lasted
scarcely a second, but its meaning was not measured by
clocks. Reginald stood among the yellow roses, violets and fox-scented
air, conscious that life, when properly approached, yielded abundance.

At luncheon Hilda served cold mutton.

"I shall not be in to dinner," he said.

"No?"

"I am dining with Mrs. Cresswell before Lady Wren's musicale."

"Is Mr. Cresswell going?"

"He is detained in Manchester."

"I see."

The two words were flat, but he heard beneath them
the dull machinery of accusation.

"It is a professional engagement."

"I didn't say it wasn't."

"You need not. Your silence has always been heavily annotated."

Hilda cut a potato.

"Will you want the dress shirt with the pleated front?"

"Yes. And the pearl studs."

"One of them is missing."

"Missing?"

"You dropped it after the Markhams' dinner."

"Then it must be found."

"I've looked."

He gazed at her. Here was catastrophe presented in the
tone one might use to announce rain.

"I cannot wear the black studs with that shirt."

"No."

"The entire line would be destroyed."

"You could wear the plain shirt."

"The plain shirt makes me look like a solicitor."

Hilda pushed the mustard toward him. "Then perhaps Lady Wren
will have to imagine the missing stud."

It was impossible to speak to her. She had no
comprehension of ensemble, impression, the delicate coherence by which an
artist met the world. Yet, as he ate the cold
mutton, she rose, fetched her workbasket and took from it
a small cardboard box.

"I had this matched yesterday," she said.

Inside lay a pearl stud.

Reginald touched it. "My dear Hilda!"

"It cost three shillings."

At once the pearl was turned into money. Always she
dragged beauty back to its invoice.

Nevertheless, when he went upstairs, he carried the box carefully.

The afternoon brought Miss Alicia Vane, who sang like a
small purple flower trying to be brave in wind. She
had recently become engaged and was uncertain whether happiness had
damaged her art.

"I feel so ordinary," she confessed. "Before, I suffered dreadfully,
and suffering made everything vivid."

Reginald sat beside her, leaning close but not too close.

"My child, happiness is not the enemy of art. Unconscious
happiness, perhaps. Complacent happiness. But yours has shadows."

"Has it?"

"All true happiness has."

She looked alarmed and gratified.

"Do you think Charles understands me?"

"Does any man entirely understand any woman?"

"You do."

He sighed. "I listen."

At this her lashes trembled. She sang the song again,
this time with a delicious break in the final phrase.

"Keep that," he whispered. "It is the wound in the
jewel."

When she had gone, he felt almost exhausted by the
number of lives entrusted to him.

His final pupil was sixteen, accompanied by her mother, and
possessed neither voice nor wound. Still, she admired his hair,
and admiration was the first gate of music.

By six he was dressing.

The pearl stud sat perfectly. His tailcoat narrowed his waist;
his white gloves, laid beside the brush, seemed to wait
like two elegant hands willing to applaud him. He dabbed
scent behind his ears—very little, only enough to soften the
severity of evening clothes.

Hilda came in carrying a pair of socks.

"You'll miss the train if you don't leave in ten
minutes."

"A cab has been ordered."

"You said yesterday we couldn't afford cabs."

"That was yesterday."

She put the socks down. He watched her in the
glass.

"Does the tie appear too full?"

"No."

"Too flat?"

"No."

"Then what does it appear?"

"A tie."

He gave a short laugh.

"You might pretend, once in a while, to be interested."

"I found the stud, had another matched, pressed your shirt
and sent Ellen for your gloves."

"That is not what I mean by interest."

"No," said Hilda. "I know."

For one strange second neither moved. In the mirror their
faces hung side by side: his luminous, expectant; hers quiet
and older than he remembered.

Then the cab arrived, rattling gloriously outside.

Mrs. Cresswell's dining room was lit by pink-shaded lamps. Orchids
leaned over a silver bowl; the champagne stood in ice;
the fish arrived beneath a shining cover, breathing steam and
butter. Flora herself wore green silk and a chain of
emeralds that rose and fell upon her bosom when she
laughed.

"To genius," she said, lifting her glass.

"My dear lady, genius must first drink to sympathy."

Their glasses touched.

She told him her husband cared only for machinery. He
told her commerce was the great frost of modern life.
She said she had never truly lived until she began
singing. He replied that some natures waited years for the
hand that opened them.

"You open everyone," she said.

"Not everyone wishes to open."

"I do."

The champagne made her face soft at the edges. It
made the orchids tremble though the table was still. Reginald
felt magnificent and merciful. He could have forgiven Hilda, the
butcher, even the missing original stud.

At Lady Wren's, the rooms glittered with shoulders, diamonds, uniforms
and small hard smiles. A duchess sat beneath a portrait
of another duchess. Two young men leaned against the mantelpiece
with the air of having invented boredom. Somewhere behind a
curtain a violin complained.

Lady Wren advanced upon Reginald in a tower of grey
feathers.

"At last! We have saved the room for you."

He bent over her hand.

"Then I must try to deserve the room."

Introductions opened around him like fans. He caught fragments—"marvellous teacher,"
"such interpretation," "Flora swears by him"—and accepted them with modest
inclinations of the head. How curious that Hilda could live
beside him and remain blind to what strangers perceived in
a moment.

When his turn came, he stood near the piano while
conversation faded by degrees. The accompanist struck the opening chord.

Reginald waited.

Waiting was essential. A singer who began immediately merely obeyed
the piano. Reginald allowed silence to gather, to become conscious
of him. Then he breathed.

The first phrase unfurled darkly. It crossed the room, brushed
the feathers and diamonds, entered the shadows above the chandeliers.
On the high note he felt the champagne, the flowers,
Miss Bell's violets, Alicia's trembling lashes, Flora's emeralds—all of it
lifting beneath him like a warm tide. He was not
a man singing in a crowded room. He was longing
itself given a throat.

The final note diminished until no one could say exactly
when it ended.

Silence.

Then applause broke over him.

Lady Wren clapped above her head. Flora's eyes shone. Even
the two young men at the mantelpiece had straightened.

"Again!" somebody cried.

Triumph upon triumph!

He sang a little French song as an encore, light
as perfume escaping from an opened drawer. There was laughter
in precisely the right place, then more applause. An elderly
countess asked who had taught him his diction. A girl
in silver said she had cried. Lady Wren insisted he
drink another glass.

Afterward the party became a radiant confusion of hands and
faces.

"You must come on Thursday."

"Do teach me that song."

"Where have you been hiding?"

"My dear lady, I shall be enchanted."

"Too kind—too kind."

"Music asks everything of us."

"You felt it? Then I am repaid."

The phrases floated from him smooth and bright, each one
perfect for the person who received it. Women touched his
sleeve. Men nodded with reluctant respect. He moved among them
like a jewelled ship through agreeable water.

It was nearly one when he left.

In the cab, with the cold pane against his temple,
the city seemed emptied for his return. Lamps stretched in
wavering chains; wet roofs shone; a policeman turned slowly at
a corner. His triumph settled inside him, becoming softer, almost
sad.

He thought of Hilda.

She would be asleep, perhaps, or lying awake with one
hand beneath her cheek. He remembered the new pearl stud
in its box, the pressed shirt, the socks. How clumsy
she was with affection! She concealed it beneath errands, mending,
figures. Perhaps he, too, had been unjust. Greatly gifted natures
were often unjust in small matters; it was the tax
of brilliance. But tonight he would rise above that. He
would tell her everything—the waiting silence, the first note, the
applause, the girl in silver who had cried. Not boastfully.
No. Simply, intimately.

He would say: Hilda, there are hundreds who hear me,
but you are the one person I wish could understand.

The thought moved him deeply.

The cab stopped. Reginald paid the driver and, in a
gesture of reckless benevolence, added sixpence.

The house was dark except for the hall lamp. On
the table lay three envelopes, one marked DAIRY ACCOUNT. He
turned it face down.

Upstairs, Hilda's door was ajar. They had slept in separate
rooms for four years, first because of his late hours,
then because of her cough, then because no one suggested
changing back.

He entered quietly.

"Hilda?"

She stirred. "What is it?"

"It's only I."

"I know."

He sat on the edge of the bed. In the
dimness she looked younger, her hair loose, one cheek creased
by the pillow. The sight of her unarranged face gave
him a sudden ache. Once she had waited outside rehearsal
rooms for him in the rain. Once they had shared
oranges in a third-class carriage because there had been no
money for dinner. Once he had sung into her hair.

"Hilda," he said.

"Yes?"

"I wanted to tell you—it was extraordinary tonight."

"I'm glad."

"No, not merely successful. There was a moment after the
song—a silence—you know the silence I mean?"

"I expect everyone was listening."

He almost withdrew. Even now, practicality! Yet she had spoken
sleepily, without malice. He must not be petty. He had
come to make peace.

"I have been thinking," he began. "Perhaps lately we have
allowed—circumstances, the pressure of things—to come between us."

Hilda opened her eyes fully.

"Yes?"

Her attention frightened him. In drawing rooms attention was diffused
among flowers, fans, champagne. Here it was plain and waiting.
He felt suddenly that she expected words he had never
rehearsed.

He took her hand. It was warm and roughened at
one finger where the needle rested.

"My dear," he said, and stopped.

What did husbands say? Not husbands in songs, dying beneath
balconies or sailing at dawn, but a man sitting on
the edge of his wife's bed while the dairy bill
waited downstairs.

He wanted to thank her for the pearl stud. The
phrase appeared absurd. He wanted to say he was lonely.
That was worse. He wanted to ask whether she remembered
the oranges, the rain, his voice in her hair. But
memory, once spoken, might demand an accounting.

Hilda waited.

The silence gathered, but it was not the magnificent silence
before music. It was small, human, unadorned. He could not
command it.

So he bent over her hand with the graceful inclination
that had charmed Lady Wren, Mrs. Cresswell, Miss Bell and
the girl in silver.

"My dear lady," he murmured, "I shall be only too
delighted."

Hilda's fingers became still in his.

"What?"

Reginald lifted his head. For a moment he did not
know what he had meant. The polished words hung between
them, fragrant, useless, like flowers sent to the wrong house.

"Nothing," he said.

He released her hand, crossed the passage to his own
room and closed the door with exquisite care.

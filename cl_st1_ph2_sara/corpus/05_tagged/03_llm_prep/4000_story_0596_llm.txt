At sixteen minutes past six on a black February morning,
Arthur Cotterill awoke with the conviction that his career, his
honour, and his entire future happiness had been irretrievably destroyed.

He sat upright in bed and stared across the room.

The clock on the mantelpiece said twenty minutes to seven.

For several seconds he was incapable of movement. Then a
pair of pincers closed upon his heart, while an anvil,
apparently lowered from the ceiling by a malignant but expert
workman, settled upon his stomach.

"Simeon!"

There was no answer.

"Simeon!"

From the adjoining bedroom came a sound which might have
been either a groan or a protest from some large,
peaceable animal.

Arthur leapt out of bed. The linoleum struck his bare
feet with the coldness of judicial marble. He rushed through
the doorway and shook the mound beneath the blankets.

"Simeon, we've overslept!"

His brother opened one eye.

"What time?"

"Twenty to seven! The porter was to come at half-past
six. We must be at Bursley station by seven-ten. The
train leaves at seven-sixteen. We've missed everything."

Simeon opened the other eye, considered the ceiling, and yawned.

"Light the stove," he said.

"But—"

"Light the stove."

The calmness of this command was almost offensive. It implied
that stoves could still be lighted, water boiled, garments donned,
trains caught, brides married, and civilisation maintained, though the clock
said twenty minutes to seven.

Arthur fled back into the sitting-room of the furnished lodgings
which had been his and Simeon's bachelor home for three
years. Through the green blind the incandescent street lamp showed
as a pallid star. Frost filmed the lower panes. The
gas stove, when approached, behaved as gas stoves habitually behave
at moments of national crisis: it refused to ignite, then
ignited explosively, and finally burned with the innocent steadiness of
a machine that had never given trouble in its life.

Simeon appeared in his shirt and trousers.

Arthur pointed dramatically at the clock.

"Look at it!"

"I have."

"It's now thirteen minutes to seven."

"No," said Simeon. "It's twenty-seven minutes past six."

Arthur blinked.

Simeon took the clock from the mantelpiece, opened its glass
door, and moved the minute hand backward.

"I put it on last night."

"You put it on?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Why?"

"So that you'd get up."

Arthur gazed at him. The pincers relaxed; the anvil was
hoisted slightly.

"You deliberately deceived me?"

"Yes."

"And you let me think—"

"I knew what you'd think. That was the idea. Put
the kettle on."

Arthur obeyed. In that instant Simeon seemed to him not
merely a brother, but a statesman, a strategist, perhaps the
personification of Providence in braces. To advance a clock by
twenty minutes, and thus compel Fate to yield twenty minutes
before breakfast, was a stroke of genius. Arthur's admiration was
profound, though tinged with resentment at having been handled like
an inferior species.

The room filled with purposeful confusion. Collars were searched for,
studs escaped under chairs, boots resisted laces, and the kettle,
after a long period of philosophical silence, boiled with sudden
fury. Simeon moved through all this with infinite calm. He
had packed the great trunk overnight. He had put the
tickets in his inner pocket, the railway labels on the
handle, sandwiches in brown paper, and a small bright object
in a waistcoat pocket which he twice touched to assure
himself of its presence.

Arthur noticed the gesture.

"You have it?"

"Yes."

"Certain?"

Simeon produced a little leather case, opened it for one
second, and shut it.

The gold within flashed.

Arthur sighed.

The wedding ring was safe. Therefore the wedding was, in
some metaphysical sense, half accomplished. A bride might forgive a
late bridegroom; a registrar might stretch a point; railway companies
might be bribed, bullied, or reorganised. But a wedding ring
left in Bursley would have possessed the finality of death.

At six-thirty-five the brothers had breakfasted. At six-forty Arthur was
dressed, except for one cuff. At six-forty-three the cuff was
found inside his travelling overcoat. At six-forty-five everything was ready.

The porter had not arrived.

They waited.

The porter, whose name was Amos Clowes, had been engaged
the previous afternoon. He had received explicit instructions. He was
to present himself with a hand-bar-row at half-past six precisely,
wheel the trunk to Bursley station, and accept ninepence for
the feat. Amos had said, "Right you are," a formula
which Arthur now recognised as having no contractual value whatever.

At six-forty-eight Arthur went to the front door and looked
down the street. It lay empty under the lamps, a
narrow descent of frozen setts between rows of dark houses.
Somewhere, beyond the roofs, a furnace sent a dull red
stain into the sky. Farther off, towards Hanbridge, a whistle
announced that industrial labour, unlike Amos Clowes, had risen at
the proper hour.

"No porter," Arthur said.

Simeon was fastening the trunk straps.

"He'll come."

"At six-fifty?"

"Yes."

"At this rate we shall have exactly sixteen minutes."

"Seventeen."

"We can't get a trunk to the station in seventeen
minutes."

"Amos can."

"But Amos isn't here."

"He may be round the corner."

Arthur went out in slippers, crossed the pavement, and examined
the corner as though Amos might be concealed there by
malice. He was not.

At six-fifty-two the pincers returned and the anvil resumed its
full weight.

"This is the end," Arthur announced.

Simeon put on his coat.

"It isn't."

"We can't leave the trunk. My clothes are in it."

"Most of them."

"My wedding clothes!"

"You're wearing clothes."

"I can't be married in this suit. It has a
shine at the elbows."

"Then we'll take the trunk."

"How?"

"With the barrow."

"What barrow?"

"Clowes's. It's in the yard. He brought it last night."

Arthur stared. Simeon's resourcefulness had reached the god-like. Not only
had he created time by altering the clock; he had
foreseen the treachery of hired labour and caused the instrument
of transport to be deposited in advance.

At six-fifty-four they dragged the trunk into the passage. It
was an enormous article, built in days when luggage-makers believed
that a gentleman should travel with his household gods and
perhaps several minor deities. Simeon took one end, Arthur the
other. They bumped it over the threshold, down two steps,
across the glazed flags of the yard, and on to
the barrow.

"You wheel," said Simeon. "I'll steady."

Arthur looked at the slope of the street, the frost,
the trunk, and the thin front wheel.

"It's dangerous."

"So's getting married. Lift."

They started.

The progress of that barrow through Bursley ought to have
been commemorated in bronze. The road had been polished by
frost to a surface of treacherous brilliance. The trunk swayed.
The wheel squealed. Arthur, gripping the handles, advanced with the
crouching gallantry of a man charging artillery. Simeon walked beside
him, one hand upon the trunk, issuing clipped directions.

"Left."

"I am left."

"Your other left."

"Don't push!"

"I'm not pushing."

"It's running away!"

"Hold it, then."

At the first corner they nearly collided with a lamp-post
belonging to the Corporation. The lamp-post showed no emotion. At
the second they encountered a milk cart, whose horse regarded
them with an experienced contempt. At the bottom of the
hill the wheel struck a rut, turned sideways, and recovered
by a miracle which Arthur attributed entirely to Simeon.

"What time is it?" he panted.

"Seven-one."

"Fifteen minutes!"

"Plenty."

"Plenty? We've half a mile to go."

"Less."

"And the hill."

"Push."

They pushed. Bursley rose before them in terraces of sleeping
houses, chapel walls, pottery yards, cinder-heaps, and blackened chimneys. The
station lay beyond a bridge which the Borough Council had
arranged at the precise gradient most unfavourable to men wheeling
trunks. Daybreak had not yet conquered the sky. The lamps
shone upon the ice; their reflections trembled beneath the barrow
like elongated ghosts.

Arthur's breath tore at his throat. His gloves slipped on
the handles. He thought of Agnes waiting in London, dressed
for the registry office, her mother looking at the clock,
her father making those small noises which signified moral condemnation.
He saw the assembled company gradually understanding that the bridegroom
had not come. He saw himself branded throughout the Five
Towns as a scoundrel. His position in the teapot trade
would become impossible. No customer could order teapots from a
man who had abandoned a lady at the altar. Bankruptcy,
exile, and suicide appeared in their natural sequence; indeed suicide
alone seemed a feasible enterprise, since it required no railway
connection.

"Faster!" he cried.

"I'm going as fast as I can," said Simeon.

At seven minutes past seven they reached the bridge.

The barrow broke.

It did not break nobly. It gave no preliminary crack,
no warning shudder. Its left handle detached itself with quiet
finality, and the whole structure leaned towards the gutter. The
trunk slid. Simeon sprang to stop it. His foot shot
forward on the ice; he twisted, fell, and struck the
pavement with his shoulder.

The trunk came to rest against the kerb.

Arthur remained holding the surviving handle.

"Simeon!"

"I'm here."

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

Simeon attempted to rise, and sat down again.

"Yes," he amended. "Ankle."

Arthur abandoned the barrow and knelt beside him. The tragedy
was complete. It had shape, grandeur, and a disabled relative.
Above them the railway signal showed green with a serenity
more cruel than mockery.

"What shall we do?" Arthur demanded.

Simeon looked at his watch.

"Seven-ten."

"We've lost the train."

"No. Run."

"Leave you?"

"Yes."

"Leave the trunk?"

"Yes."

"But my clothes!"

"Run."

"And the ring?"

Simeon's hand went to his waistcoat. For the first time
his composure altered. Not much; a superficial observer would have
detected nothing. But Arthur, trained by years of brotherhood, saw
the flicker.

Simeon drew out the leather case and thrust it at
him.

"Take this."

Arthur received it as a crusader might receive a relic.

"And the tickets?"

"One's yours." Simeon found the tickets, separated them, and gave
Arthur his portion. "Now go."

"I can't leave you injured in the road."

"You can, and you will."

"But suppose—"

"Arthur!"

There was authority in the name. Arthur rose.

"What will you do?"

"Manage."

"How?"

"I don't know yet. Hurry."

Arthur ran.

He ran over the bridge, down Station Approach, past the
closed bookshop and the weighing machine, through the booking hall
and on to the platform. The station clock said seven-fourteen.
A porter was shutting a carriage door. Steam drifted beneath
the roof. The local train to Knype gave a preliminary
jerk, as though trying to escape before Arthur could reach
it.

"Hold on!" he cried.

The porter did not hold on; but Arthur seized a
brass handle, opened a door that had just been closed,
and tumbled into a compartment as the train moved.

For half a minute he could do nothing but breathe.
Opposite him sat a commercial traveller with a red moustache
and a sample case. In one corner a Methodist-looking gentleman
held a newspaper; he might have been a Levite, though
he subsequently displayed the curiosity of a less sacerdotal class.
Neither spoke. They contemplated Arthur's heaving chest, muddy knees, disordered
collar, and desperate eyes with the tolerant interest commonly given
to the misfortunes of strangers.

The train gained speed.

Arthur was safe.

He had caught the seven-sixteen. At Knype he would join
the eight-five express. He would reach Euston at twelve-forty. The
wedding was fixed for one-thirty. There would be fifty minutes
in London—fifty enormous, luxurious minutes. Even with the journey to
Russell Square and a change into his wedding suit, there
would be time. True, the suit was in the trunk
beside an injured Simeon on Bursley bridge; but this detail,
being for the moment insoluble, could be ignored.

The pincers opened. The anvil vanished.

Then the train stopped outside Knype.

It stopped among sidings, signal-posts, and heaps of frozen slag.
No platform was visible. No human being appeared. The engine
breathed heavily and obstinately remained still.

Arthur looked at his watch: seven-forty-nine.

The connection departed at eight-five.

At seven-fifty-three they had not moved.

At seven-fifty-six the Methodist gentleman lowered his newspaper.

"Signal, likely," he said.

Arthur leaned out of the window. A red light burned
ahead.

At eight o'clock the commercial traveller remarked that they would
have to "shift their-selves a bit."

At eight-two the train moved with exaggerated leisure, paused again,
and then crept into Knype at eight-six.

The London express stood across the island platform.

"London?" Arthur shouted at a porter.

"Front section!"

Arthur ran up the subway stairs. Passengers hurried in several
directions. Doors slammed. A whistle blew. Someone cried, "Birmingham behind!"
and someone else cried, "All seats forward!" Arthur saw a
carriage marked FIRST and rejected it on financial grounds; saw
another crowded to suffocation; saw a third with an open
door and flung himself in.

The express departed.

He had caught it.

Once more he was safe.

The compartment contained an elderly woman, a young man asleep
under a cap, and a thin person carrying a basket
from which came the faint, accusing sound of poultry. Arthur
sat down, clasping the ring case in his pocket. His
heart beat with victorious celerity. The train swept past factories
and frozen fields. Knype receded. Fate had done its utmost
and had failed.

At nine-ten the train stopped at Stafford.

Arthur, whose knowledge of Bradshaw was reverential rather than exact,
felt a slight uneasiness. He had not remembered that the
London section stopped at Stafford. Still, railway companies possessed mysterious
habits. The thin person with the poultry got out. A
stout gentleman entered.

"London?" Arthur asked him.

"London?" said the stout gentleman. "Not this portion."

Arthur's heart became instantly available to the pincers.

"What portion is this?"

"Birmingham."

"But we're attached to the London express."

"Were. Divided at Crewe."

"We haven't been to Crewe."

"Then divided at Stafford."

The stout gentleman said this without concern, as if trains
were divided daily with no loss of blood.

Arthur rushed into the corridor and found a guard.

"Is this going to London?"

"Birmingham."

"I was told the front section."

"This was the rear section."

"I got in where a porter directed me."

"Can't say anything about that."

"I must be in London by half-past twelve."

"You won't."

The guard moved away.

Arthur followed.

"When shall we reach Birmingham?"

"Nine-fifty-eight."

"And the next train to London?"

"Ten-fifteen, if it's right."

"When does that reach Euston?"

"Twelve-forty-seven, booked."

Seventeen minutes lost. It was not ruin. It approached ruin
and shook hands with it, but it was not yet
identical with ruin.

Arthur returned to his seat and began calculations. Euston at
twelve-forty-seven. Cab to Russell Square: ten minutes, perhaps twelve. Dress
in fifteen—Simeon had the trunk. The trunk! Without it he
possessed no frock coat, no waistcoat, no patent shoes, no
decent collar. He would arrive at his own wedding looking
like a man who had escaped from a commercial fire.

At Birmingham he would telegraph. He would say: DELAYED WRONG
PORTION ARRIVE ONE EXPLAIN SIMEON INJURED BRING CLOTHES IMPOSSIBLE BUT
COMING. This lacked grammar, dignity, and hope, but telegrams had
never encouraged any of the three.

Birmingham received the train at ten-one. The platform was long,
populous, and indifferent. Arthur ran to the telegraph office, wrote
three versions of his message, paid for the longest, and
reached the London platform at ten-thirteen.

The ten-fifteen stood ready.

He entered it.

At ten-fifteen it remained ready.

At ten-sixteen it exhibited no intention of ceasing to be
ready.

At ten-eighteen a station inspector walked past with the superb
tranquillity of an official whose own marriage, if any, had
occurred years earlier.

"When does this train start?" Arthur called.

"Presently."

"It's due out at ten-fifteen."

"Fog south of Rugby."

"Fog?"

"Mist, more like."

"Will it be late?"

"May pick up."

At ten-twenty-two the engine whistled. At ten-twenty-four the train departed,
apparently in an ironical mood.

Arthur sat alone in a corner and watched Birmingham's roofs
slide away. He had done all that man could do.
He had risen early, lighted the stove, wheeled the trunk,
survived the collapse of a barrow, caught a local train,
endured railway division, rerouted himself through Birmingham, and telegraphed the
bride. Homeric heroes had done less, and their weddings, where
recorded, had not been constrained by registry-office hours.

The legal limit, as Arthur understood it, was two o'clock.
At two the registrar's power expired. After that hour he
could no more marry a couple than a grocer could
confer knighthood. Arthur had been told this repeatedly. Agnes's father
had emphasised it. Agnes's mother had caused one-thirty to be
written in three letters. Two o'clock stood at the end
of the day like a wall.

The train ran magnificently to Coventry, lost four minutes near
Rugby, recovered three before Northampton, and then entered the mist.

It was not a romantic mist. It had none of
the silver properties admired by poets. It was yellowish, wet,
and dense, and it reduced the landscape to telegraph wires
and occasional hedges. The engine slowed. Signals emerged red from
vacancy. The train paused, advanced, and paused again.

At one-fifteen Arthur consulted his watch.

At one-twenty he consulted it again.

At one-twenty-one he discovered that the hands had hardly moved.
Clocks, he reflected, were malicious objects. When disaster approached they
rushed; when one watched them they refused to stir, preserving
the victim in exquisite uncertainty.

A fellow passenger entered from the corridor, a mild man
with spectacles.

"Any idea when we get in?" Arthur asked.

"Guard says about two."

"About?"

"Possibly a minute or two after."

Arthur turned pale.

"I have to be married before two."

The mild man looked at him with immediate sympathy. He
was perhaps a cousin of a Good Samaritan.

"Dear me."

"If I'm not there, the whole thing is off."

"Couldn't they wait?"

"The law won't."

"Ah," said the mild man, with the respect of an
Englishman for a law he did not understand.

At one-forty the train passed Watford. At one-fifty-two it slowed
outside London. Chimneys, walls, back gardens, and washing emerged from
the vapour. Euston was near. Euston was impossibly far. Arthur
stood in the corridor with his bag in one hand
and the other pressed against the ring.

At two o'clock precisely the train was moving between high
black walls.

The hour had struck.

He had failed.

The pincers met through his heart. The anvil became a
whole ironworks. Agnes would be at the registry office. The
registrar would close his book. Her mother would weep; her
father would become silent, which was worse. The telegraph message
would aggravate rather than excuse. It would be remembered that
Arthur had entered the wrong portion of a train. No
man of moral worth entered wrong portions.

At one minute past two, by the station clock, the
express entered Euston and covered itself with glory.

Arthur got out slowly.

He had no reason to hurry now. Haste belonged to
hopeful men. His wedding day was a ruin, his brother
was lying injured in Bursley, his clothes were in a
gutter, and the ring in his pocket had become a
small gold monument to human vanity.

Then he heard his name.

"Arthur!"

He turned.

Simeon stood ten yards away, leaning on a crutch.

For a second Arthur supposed that calamity had unhinged his
mind. Simeon wore his travelling coat. His left boot was
slit at the ankle. Beside him stood the trunk, labelled,
strapped, and solid.

"You!" Arthur exclaimed.

"Yes. Quick."

"How did you get here?"

"Train."

"What train?"

"The eight-five from Knype."

"But you were on the bridge."

"Milk cart."

"A milk cart?"

"Man came by. I hired it. Loaded the trunk. Got
to Knype at seven-fifty-eight."

"With that ankle?"

"The ankle came too."

"You caught the proper section?"

"Of course."

Arthur resented the "of course."

"But how did you know which was proper?"

"I asked twice."

This seemed unfairly simple.

Simeon turned and limped towards the cab rank with surprising
velocity.

"I've been to the hotel," he said. "Your room's ready.
Clothes laid out. I came back for you."

"You came back?"

"Couldn't trust you to find the hotel."

"Simeon, it's after two."

"I know."

"The wedding's impossible."

"Bosh."

"The registrar can't marry us after two."

"He can."

"He cannot. It's the law."

"Was the law. Changed."

Arthur stopped.

"Changed?"

"Years ago, seemingly."

"But Agnes's father said—"

"Agnes's father thinks Queen Victoria is still taking an interest
in things."

"What time—?"

"Two-fifty."

"The ceremony?"

"Yes."

"You postponed it?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"Went to the registry office. Then to the hotel. Then
telegraphed. Agnes is having lunch."

"Lunch?"

"She was hungry."

The revelation that Agnes could eat while Arthur's honour lay
shattered among the railway sidings produced in him an emotion
too complicated for words.

Simeon opened a cab door.

"Get in."

The cab set off. London passed in damp magnificence: omnibuses,
horses, shiny pavements, shop fronts, umbrellas, policemen, and multitudes of
people who appeared unaware that a wedding had narrowly escaped
extinction. Simeon sat with the crutch between his knees.

Arthur stared at him.

"You carried the trunk?"

"Milkman helped. Porters helped."

"You found a doctor?"

"No time. Chemist strapped it."

"Where did you get the crutch?"

"Bought it."

"You went to the hotel, unpacked my clothes, visited the
registry office, reassured everybody, changed the time, and returned to
Euston?"

"Yes."

"On that ankle?"

Simeon regarded the swollen boot.

"It was the only ankle I had."

The pincers disappeared. The anvil was removed so abruptly that
Arthur felt almost light-headed. He leaned back. For the first
time since six-sixteen he perceived that London possessed buildings, that
the cab possessed upholstery, and that life, in spite of
repeated evidence to the contrary, might continue.

At the hotel Simeon paid the driver, bullied a porter,
directed the trunk upstairs, and supervised Arthur's transformation.

"Shirt," he said.

Arthur put on the shirt.

"Collar."

Arthur attempted the collar.

"Not that way."

"I know how to put on a collar."

"Then do it."

At two-twenty the trousers were on. At two-twenty-four Arthur discovered
a spot upon the waistcoat.

"It's ruined."

"It's dust."

"It may show."

"Brush."

At two-twenty-seven one patent shoe could not be found. Arthur
accused the hotel, the railway company, Amos Clowes, and the
organisation of modern society. Simeon found the shoe under the
bed.

At two-thirty-one Arthur stood dressed: frock coat, pale waistcoat, striped
trousers, patent shoes, pearl pin, gloves. He looked distinguished, pale,
and slightly hunted.

"The ring," said Simeon.

Arthur clutched his pocket.

"Safe."

"Show me."

Arthur produced the case.

"Put it in the other pocket. That one's torn."

The lining had indeed split. The ring might have escaped,
rolled beneath the bed, and brought about a fresh catastrophe
of the first magnitude. Simeon transferred it himself and buttoned
the pocket.

At two-thirty-four they descended. Another cab was waiting.

During the journey Arthur began to rehearse explanations.

"I shall tell Agnes exactly what occurred."

"Don't."

"She ought to know."

"Marry her first."

"Her father will demand an account."

"Let him."

"I must make it clear that the error at Knype
was caused by contradictory instructions."

"You got into the wrong carriage."

"The sections were confusing."

"You got into the wrong carriage."

"And the delay outside Knype—"

"Arthur."

"What?"

"You think too much."

The registry office occupied a respectable building in a street
of grey houses. At two minutes to three the cab
drew up. Simeon descended first, using the crutch. Arthur followed,
suddenly unable to feel his knees.

In an upstairs room Agnes waited in a blue travelling
dress, with her parents, two witnesses, and a registrar who
seemed entirely untroubled by the survival or extinction of ancient
deadlines. Agnes looked at Arthur.

"You've come," she said.

"I have."

"We knew you would."

Arthur glanced at Simeon. Agnes followed the glance and smiled.

"Well," she amended, "Simeon said you would."

Her father inspected the clock. Her mother inspected Arthur's collar.
The registrar opened his book.

The ceremony proceeded with a brevity that startled Arthur. After
the immense machinery of frost, clocks, barrows, milk carts, branch
lines, railway sections, fog, telegrams, cabs, crutches, collars, and legal
misunderstandings, the act itself occupied only a few minutes. Arthur
spoke when told, signed where indicated, and placed the small
bright ring upon Agnes's finger without dropping it.

At eight minutes past three the registrar blotted the page.

"That's done," said Simeon.

Arthur looked at his wife. The word wife had a
terrifying splendour, but it was a different terror from that
inspired by timetables. Agnes's hand lay quietly in his. Outside,
London traffic continued its uproar, indifferent but not hostile.

"You're very pale," she said.

"I have endured a morning," Arthur replied, "which I shall
never be able adequately to describe."

"Simeon described it in six sentences."

"That is Simeon's manner."

Her eyes moved towards the crutch.

"He says he only sprained his ankle."

"He saved the wedding."

Simeon, overhearing, shrugged.

"Milkman did most."

There followed congratulations, handshakes, a mild rebuke from Agnes's father
on the necessity of consulting railway indicators, and a more
serious rebuke from Agnes's mother on travelling upon an empty
breakfast—which Arthur had not done. Simeon accepted thanks with visible
discomfort. He preferred emergencies to gratitude; emergencies could be ordered
about.

Arthur stood near the window with Agnes.

"At two o'clock," he said, "I believed my life was
finished."

"And at three it began?"

He considered this.

"At three-eight, officially."

"You mustn't worry so."

"I do not worry without cause."

Across the room Simeon laughed once.

Arthur ignored him with dignity. He was married. His honour
was intact. His career in the teapot trade might proceed.
The pincers and the anvil had vanished, perhaps for ever—though
Agnes, looking at his expression, doubted this.

Simeon tapped the floor with his crutch.

"Come on," he said. "You'll miss lunch."

And thus, through infinite calm, a milk cart, two telegrams,
one damaged barrow, and the correction of several mistaken assumptions,
there was one bachelor the less in that industrious vale
of tears.

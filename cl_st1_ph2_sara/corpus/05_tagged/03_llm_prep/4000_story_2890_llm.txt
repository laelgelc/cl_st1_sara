When I became, by the decease of my mother's elder
sister, possessor of the old brick house in Cedar Street—not
the Cedar Street which the map now acknowledges, noisy with
drays and reverberant with the brazen theology of commerce, but
that earlier, quieter street, which survives only in the memories
of men whose knees object to stairs—I confess that I
regarded the inheritance with less gratitude than an estate, even
a ruinous estate, commonly excites.

For the house was very old; and age in a
house, unlike age in claret, is not always to be
commended by gentlemen who do not intend to drink the
consequences. Its roof sagged a little toward the east, as
if bowing with excessive urbanity to the sunrise; its chimneys
smoked impartially into the rooms and into the air; its
shutters had lost several slats, and, when the wind was
northward, performed a melancholy percussion throughout the night. The cellars
were divided by brick arches into bins deep enough to
have concealed the private sins of a province. Huge oak
timbers, black as old sermons, traversed the ceilings. The mantels
were carved with garlands, urns, and small mythological ladies whose
noses had been rubbed away by generations of housemaids. There
were wainscots, cupboards within cupboards, bells which rang in rooms
where no wire could be discovered, and one narrow stair
that mounted abruptly, turned upon itself in confusion, and ended
before a blank wall.

Yet I kept the place. Ah, we preserve what inconveniences
us, when it has inconvenienced our fathers! A modern builder
would have split its rooms, levelled its cornices, sold its
mantels, and substituted for its broad, hospitable staircase one of
those contracted wooden ladders by which six families may ascend
to misery at a moderate rent. I did none of
these things. I repaired the roof, appeased the chimneys, strengthened
the timbers, and established myself among the ghosts.

The front parlor remained longest untouched. It was a grand,
dim chamber, having three tall windows, a marble fireplace clouded
by age, and a ceiling ornamented with a plaster wreath
whose fruits, through some error either of art or horticulture,
included apples, grapes, pears, and pineapples upon a single bough.
But the wonder of the apartment was its paper.

You have not seen such paper. No, madam, despite the
costly foliage now climbing your drawing room, you have not.
Upon a ground once cream-colored, but ripened by smoke and
years into the hue of weak tea, strutted peacocks of
imperial magnitude; peacocks with tails of sapphire, emerald, gold, and
a purple known only to bishops and sunset clouds. Between
them grew roses, innumerable roses—red roses, blush roses, white roses
touched at their hearts with carmine—while slender branches twisted through
the whole resplendence in a manner which violated nature but
delighted fashion.

Damp had dealt cruelly with the birds. Here a peacock's
crown had vanished beneath a brown stain; there a magnificent
tail had peeled away in strips, so that the creature
displayed the rear of an undistinguished barnyard fowl. Their eyes
were dim, their feathers mottled, their grandeur ridiculous. But the
roses—how shall I explain it?—the roses endured. Faded, surely; cracked,
certainly; yet still roses, still warm, still tender, still mysteriously
alive upon that perishing wall.

It was this paper which first called Jimmy Rose plainly
back to me.

Not that I had ever wholly forgotten him. Certain men
remain in one's remembrance not because they were wise, useful,
or even consistently good, but because they imparted to ordinary
life an atmosphere, as oranges perfume a room after the
fruit has been taken away. Jimmy had occupied the house
before my aunt; indeed, he had chosen the peacock paper,
and had stood in that very parlor while twenty candles,
multiplied by mirrors, flashed upon his buttons, his wineglasses, his
polished floors, and his smiling, handsome face.

Handsome? He was beautiful. I use the word deliberately, though
he was a man, and though our present sternness permits
beauty to men only when they are dead, foreign, or
painted in armor. Jimmy was slender without frailty, rosy without
coarseness, and graceful without that consciousness of grace which makes
the graceful intolerable. His hair, chestnut and lightly curling, was
worn somewhat longer than the counting houses approved. His eyes
were blue, his teeth excellent, and his hands—those open, prodigal,
incapable hands!—looked created less for possession than for bestowal.

His father, a shrewd merchant in hides, had left him
what was then called a snug lump of coin. I
never learned its amount; fortunes, like scandals, gain magnitude by
circulation. Some declared it seventy thousand dollars, some one hundred
thousand, and old General Van Brunt of the militia, whose
authority upon all subjects was proportional to his ignorance of
each, fixed it at precisely one hundred and thirty-two thousand,
three hundred and six dollars. Enough that Jimmy was rich;
richer in expectation than in arithmetic, richer in friends than
in advisers, and richest of all in the conviction that
money, like spring rain, fell chiefly in order that gentlemen
might prevent it from becoming stagnant.

He entertained.

That small statement conveys no adequate conception of the prodigality.
He entertained on Monday because Sunday had been dull; on
Wednesday because Tuesday had been wet; on Friday because the
week was nearly ended; and, when some pale bookkeeper hinted
that four dinners in seven days might constitute extravagance, Jimmy
entertained on Saturday to disprove the meanness of calculation. His
table shone with plate; his sideboard groaned beneath decanters; his
cook, a tyrant from Martinique, sent forth sauces which reconciled
enemies and pâtés which corrupted patriots. Clerks became poets over
his claret. Judges prolonged dessert. Merchants, who at their own
boards counted almonds, consumed Jimmy's oysters with the spacious appetite
of men performing a public duty.

The ladies came later, in silks that whispered down the
passage, and the roses on the walls appeared to brighten
at their arrival. There was music; there were cards; there
was dancing. Jimmy moved among his guests with that easy
solicitude which seems to give everything while claiming nothing. He
remembered who preferred tea, who detested drafts, whose daughter had
lately recovered from fever, and whose vanity required the first
compliment. No widow sat neglected, no shy girl wanted a
partner, no aged gentleman found his old story anticipated at
the second sentence.

"Mrs. Pelham," Jimmy once said to a formidable matron whose
charms had retreated long before but whose artillery remained, "you
have done the moon an injury by coming out tonight."

She laughed—she who laughed for no one—and wore the compliment,
I believe, for twenty years.

Jimmy's compliments were not lies exactly. They were decorations hung
by kindness upon facts too bare for company. If a
woman possessed one pretty finger, Jimmy praised her hand; if
she had a pleasant voice, he congratulated the nightingales upon
their rival; if age had taken everything but dignity, he
called dignity beauty, and for that instant made it so.
There are worse falsehoods. Indeed, there are truths daily uttered
with such brutality that Heaven, if it keeps accounts morally
rather than grammatically, must enter them upon the debtor's side.

But gold spent itself while compliments did not. Jimmy entered
ventures—what ventures I cannot now say, though one involved timber,
another indigo, and a third some ingenious transmutation of swamp
into city lots. Each prospect was infallible until money touched
it. Then came reverses in trade. A partner failed. A
warehouse burned. One ship went ashore upon Cape Hatteras; another,
freighted richly and insured poorly, disappeared with all hands in
a January gale.

These tidings arrived with vulgar rapidity. Fortune requires years to
build a pedestal and ten minutes to kick it over.

Jimmy was a ruined man.

I remember the announcement. We were standing in the office
of Mr. Abel Thorn, a broker whose black coat always
appeared newer than his principles. Young Suydam came in, shaking
sleet from his hat.

"Rose has burst," he said.

"Clean smash?" asked Thorn.

"Clean as Sunday plate. Ships, notes, endorsements—the whole kettle."

Mr. Thorn whistled, then turned to me with remarkable composure.

"You dine with me tomorrow? Five sharp. We shall have
that haunch from Albany."

Thus Jimmy's prosperity received its epitaph, and the haunch from
Albany, I am ashamed to say, was excellent.

Creditors stripped the house; acquaintances stripped his character. The chairs,
plate, pictures, horses, and wines went first. Then went the
memories of favors. A man who had borrowed five hundred
dollars from Jimmy discovered that Jimmy had always been reckless.
Another, whose appointment to a municipal office Jimmy had quietly
promoted, recollected an early tendency toward dissipation. Ladies who had
accepted his flowers wondered publicly whether his manners had not
been a little too florid. The gluttons moralized; the flatterers
condemned flattery; the parasites, disturbed at finding the tree cut
down, blamed it for not having deeper roots.

God guard us all from the virtue of those who
have finished dining at our expense!

I do not acquit Jimmy. No. He had been vain,
imprudent, incapable of refusing, and almost criminally unwilling to understand
that a dollar may be spent only once, however amiably
it is spent. Generosity without judgment can injure as widely
as selfishness; and Jimmy had encouraged idlers, rewarded importunity, and
mistaken abundance for permanence. But between folly and wickedness there
is a distance which society crosses with astonishing agility when
a fallen man lies on the other side.

The house, entangled in some legal delay, stood empty for
a season. Its windows were shuttered, its knocker tied, its
steps unwashen. I was young then—young enough to confuse curiosity
with concern, as I have since confused concern with curiosity—and,
having heard that Jimmy had been seen entering by the
rear alley, I went one March afternoon to seek him.

Rain had blackened the bricks. A broken gutter poured monotonously
beside the door. I knocked. No answer. I knocked again
and called his name.

From within came a movement—first a scuffle, then silence.

"Jimmy!" I cried. "It is I. Let me in."

A voice answered through the keyhole, so altered, so thin
and furious, that I stepped backward.

"Go away."

"I have come to help you."

"Help!" he repeated, and laughed. It was not Jimmy's laugh;
it was a dry hinge creaking in darkness. "There is
no one here. The house is empty."

"I heard you."

"You heard rats."

"Open, Jimmy."

Something struck the inner panel violently. Whether it was a
cane, a chair, or his fist I do not know.

"I have a pistol," said the voice. "If you enter,
I shall fire."

There are moments when prudence assumes the appearance of cowardice
so perfectly that even after half a century one cannot
distinguish the two. I went away. I told myself that
violence would do no good, that a physician must be
fetched, that the law should intervene, that the unfortunate man's
privacy ought to be respected—in brief, I furnished fear with
a complete wardrobe of benevolent phrases. When I returned next
day with an older friend, the rear door stood open
and Jimmy had gone.

For many years I heard nothing certain. There were reports:
that he had sailed for New Orleans; that he taught
French in a village academy; that he drank himself into
the river; that he had died in a poorhouse under
another name. Time, the true plagiarist of the seasons, repeated
spring after spring, snow after snow; faces altered, streets widened,
old names disappeared from signs and reappeared upon tombstones. I
married, was widowed, gained some money, lost some illusions, and
found gray hairs multiplying where curls had once made an
ambitious display. Twenty-five years went by.

Then, on a bright October morning, I saw Jimmy Rose
in Broadway.

At first I knew only the gesture. A little old
man in a threadbare blue coat had stopped before a
milliner's window, not to examine the bonnets, as I supposed,
but to bow to a lady emerging from the shop.
The lady, richly dressed and hurried, offered him two fingers.

"My dear Mrs. Pelham," said the old man, "autumn has
stolen your colors and put them in the trees."

The voice was feeble, but the cadence—ah, there was Jimmy!

Mrs. Pelham smiled with that mingling of pleasure and embarrassment
which attends the receipt of a compliment from one whom
it is inconvenient to acknowledge. She murmured, "Always gallant, Mr.
Rose," glanced toward her carriage, and escaped.

He turned. The face was lined, the hair white, the
shoulders narrow; but in the cheeks remained two delicate spots
of color, two faithful roses blooming above the frayed collar.
His eyes, too, were clear. Whatever storm had once driven
him into the locked house had passed. Poverty had restored
to him what wealth's ruin had disordered: he was sane.

"Jimmy," I said.

He looked at me for several seconds; then his smile
rose, slowly but wholly, like a lamp turned up in
a neglected room.

"My old friend! You have improved."

"Improved! I am bald."

"A fortunate enlargement of the forehead."

"And wrinkled."

"Time writes only upon distinguished paper."

There he was—reduced to one coat, perhaps one dinner, certainly
one pair of gloves, yet dispensing alms to the rich.

We walked together. I soon learned the arrangement of his
existence, if existence so precarious deserves the substantial name of
arrangement. From a remote relation he received a small annuity—two
hundred dollars a year, I think—paid quarterly and anticipated monthly.
He rented an attic in a house near the river,
where the smell of tar disputed with that of boiled
cabbage. His breakfast was tea and bread. For dinner he
depended, with elaborate casualness, upon old acquaintances.

"On Tuesdays," he explained, "I look in upon the Van
Alstynes. They insist upon carving too much mutton. Thursday belongs
to our good General—no longer general, officially, but military glory,
like a widow's cap, is worn for life. On Sundays
Mrs. Cray serves a chicken of unusual punctuality."

"And Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday?"

"Excellent days for reflection."

He said this cheerfully; but I noticed that his hand
trembled, and that he looked through the window of a
pastry cook with the concentration of a theologian studying eternity.

I took him to luncheon. He protested until the soup
arrived, then ate slowly, ceremoniously, praising every dish and leaving
a portion upon each plate, as though abundance were so
customary to him that appetite might afford discrimination. When the
waiter brought cheese, Jimmy wrapped half of it in his
napkin while speaking to me of opera. He thought I
did not see. I saw, and pretended not to see;
which of us performed the charity I shall not determine.

Thereafter I encountered him often. He became what I once
called, with a levity for which memory rebukes me, a
promenading pauper. He walked the fashionable streets because there he
could meet those who fed him; but he bore his
dependence with so much archaic elegance that invitations seemed favors
granted by him.

"Rose, dine with us tonight," a merchant would say, looking
over Jimmy's shoulder lest a more profitable guest be passing.

"My dear sir," Jimmy would reply, "you tempt me to
neglect another engagement."

The engagement was generally with hunger. After a graceful hesitation
he yielded.

At table he was placed near the foot, among awkward
cousins, deaf aunts, and young gentlemen not yet important. Servants
who once flew at his glance now asked twice whether
he had been served. His old stories were interrupted. His
opinions, formerly repeated as wit, evaporated unheard. Yet if a
neglected girl blushed in silence beside him, Jimmy addressed her
as if she were the evening's sovereign; if an old
woman dropped her handkerchief, he stooped for it, though the
stooping cost him pain; if his host made a poor
joke, Jimmy's laugh arrived before that of the heirs.

Was there vanity in this? Certainly. He still desired to
be Jimmy Rose, still polished the ghost of his social
importance, still preferred a cold dinner among silver candlesticks to
warm stew eaten anonymously. But vanity is sometimes the crutch
by which dignity hobbles past despair. Break it, if you
are strong; I would rather not.

Certain charitable ladies, learning that he lived poorly, formed a
design for his improvement. Charity, especially organized charity, loves improvement
in the recipient more ardently than relief. They sent him
six volumes: two upon temperance, one upon thrift, a memoir
of a missionary, a book of consolations for the aged,
and a treatise proving that poverty, rightly considered, was an
opportunity.

Jimmy returned them.

"My eyesight is weak," he wrote, "and my gratitude, though
strong, is not strong enough for six volumes."

The ladies were offended. One called him ungrateful.

"He might at least have read the book on thrift,"
she said.

"My dear madam," I answered, "a man who eats four
dinners a week has perhaps advanced beyond the elementary chapters."

She did not forgive me; charitable persons forgive the poor
more readily than they forgive criticism of charity.

I visited Jimmy's attic once in winter. Snow pressed its
pale face against the single window. The ceiling descended sharply,
so that a tall man could have stood upright only
in the middle of the room, and Jimmy had never
been more nearly central in his own establishment. There were
a bed, a washstand, two chairs of unequal infirmity, and
a small grate containing more ash than fire. Upon the
wall hung a cracked mirror. On the sill stood a
tumbler holding three paper roses, fashioned, he told me, by
the child of the washerwoman below.

"They do not fade," he said.

"Nor smell."

"No perfection is without reserve."

He made tea over a spirit lamp and produced toast
of a rigidity that challenged the teeth. The cup he
gave me had a handle; his own had none. He
apologized for the tea, the weather, the chair, and the
low ceiling, as though all four had disappointed me after
accepting his hospitality. Never had I felt more plainly that
I was a guest.

I tried, clumsily, to offer him money.

"My annuity is ample," he said.

"It is nothing of the sort."

"Then we must praise it for ambition."

"Jimmy, do not be absurd."

His rosy cheeks deepened. "My friend, I have been absurd
at considerable expense. Permit me to practice the accomplishment cheaply."

I pressed him. He rose, and the old brightness left
his face.

"No."

There was in that little word the locked house, the
blow upon the panel, the threatened pistol, the whole fierce
remnant of a pride stripped naked and shivering. I ceased.
Later I contrived with the landlord that his rent should
be reduced, making up the difference privately; but he discovered
the device within three months and moved away. Ah, poor,
poor Jimmy! He would accept a dinner flavored with neglect,
but not a coin seasoned with pity. Human nature does
not obey the diagrams of moralists.

As the years proceeded, his circuit narrowed. The General died;
Mrs. Pelham became bedridden; merchants removed uptown or retired into
marble houses where new servants denied old names. One family
continued to receive him every Thursday, but at last the
son married a lady who found Jimmy "depressing."

Depressing! He who entered hungry and departed smiling; he who
brought compliments when he could not bring flowers; he who
carried his own sorrow as a gentleman carries an umbrella
in a crowded hall, carefully, lest it drip upon others!

His final illness began in early spring. A chill, taken
during one of his long walks, settled upon the lungs.
When I found him, he lay in another attic, smaller
than the first, beneath a roof upon which rain beat
with intolerable cheerfulness. A physician had come and gone. Medicine
stood uncorked beside the bed; there was no fire.

But Jimmy was not alone.

The daughter of the woman who kept the house—a young
seamstress named Ellen Ward—had undertaken his care. She was perhaps
twenty-two, grave-eyed, plain in dress, and possessed of that quiet
competence which makes much advertised benevolence resemble a brass band
marching behind a candle. She warmed broth, washed his linen,
measured his drops, and sat through the worst nights without
once calling herself charitable.

"Mr. Rose was kind to my little sister," she told
me. "He taught her to make paper flowers."

Jimmy, hearing, opened his eyes.

"Miss Ward exaggerates," he whispered. "I merely instructed nature."

Even then! Death waited on the stairs, and Jimmy complimented
the nurse.

During one lucid afternoon he asked me whether the old
house still stood. I told him it did, and described
the parlor, the mantel, the paper.

"The peacocks?" he asked.

"Badly damaged."

"They were vain fellows."

"The roses remain."

At this he smiled. His cheeks, wasted now, retained the
faintest bloom.

"Roses," he murmured, "have better constitutions."

I wished to say something—something wise, forgiving, final. Age ought
to prepare a man for such occasions; but age, which
furnishes spectacles after the eyes are dim, commonly supplies wisdom
after the need for speech is past. I took his
hand and said only, "Jimmy."

He understood, perhaps. Or perhaps the dying understand nothing and
the living invent comprehension because silence is unbearable.

That night the rain ceased. Toward morning the clouds divided,
and a thin bar of light entered beneath the eaves.
Ellen sat beside him. I had fallen asleep in the
crooked chair, waking when she rose.

Jimmy had ceased breathing.

His expression was composed, almost ceremonious, as if he had
declined one final invitation without giving offense. Ellen bent over
him, smoothed the white hair from his brow, and closed
his eyes.

Jimmy Rose was dead.

We buried him modestly. Few of the old company attended;
most had preceded him, and the rest sent excuses of
considerable social polish. Mrs. Pelham's daughter provided flowers, though whether
from remembrance, duty, or relief I cannot say. Ellen placed
among them three paper roses. They looked poor beside the
lilies and hot-house blossoms. They lasted longer.

After the funeral I went alone to the Cedar Street
house and entered the front parlor. Afternoon had filled it
with a dusty amber light. The carved mantel leaned from
the wall; the plaster fruit hung overhead in its impossible
abundance; beyond the windows the modern city roared and bargained,
built and demolished, praised success and instructed failure. Upon the
wallpaper the great peacocks strutted in ruin, their jeweled tails
scarred by damp, their proud necks broken by cracks, their
crowns dissolving into stain.

I thought of Jimmy in his youth, all blue coat,
bright eyes, open hands, and radiant imprudence; Jimmy at the
head of his table, making gluttons feel witty and plain
women beautiful; Jimmy amid shipwreck and bankruptcy; Jimmy behind the
locked door, half-mad, striking the panel; Jimmy old and hungry,
bowing in Broadway, wrapping cheese in a napkin while discoursing
upon opera; Jimmy in the attic, offering the handled cup
to his guest; Jimmy dying beneath the rain, with a
poor girl's merciful hand upon his brow.

The peacocks were Jimmy's magnificence—splendid, absurd, costly, and perishable. Time
had bedraggled them. Society had laughed at them. Commerce had
torn away their gilded feathers one by one.

But among them the roses remained.

Why should the roses have endured when the prouder colors
failed? I am no philosopher; or, being old, I have
become too much a philosopher to trust philosophy. Yet I
know that something in Jimmy survived his fortune, his sanity's
eclipse, his humiliation, his hunger, even his vanity: some warmth
which poverty could not chill; some courtesy which neglect could
not make contumelious; some generous transmutation by which he turned
the little left him—words, smiles, remembrance—into alms for those richer
and often poorer than himself.

Do I sentimentalize him? Very likely. Tears are the luxury
of age, and we old men, denied many other dissipations,
indulge them without moderation. Jimmy was foolish. Jimmy was proud.
Jimmy squandered, evaded, flattered, and made a comedy of dependence.
But he also preserved, in the attic of a fallen
life, a certain grace; and grace, though it pays no
creditor and buys no coal, is not therefore nothing.

I touched one faded rose upon the wall. A fragment
loosened beneath my finger and fluttered to the floor.

There, in that dim old parlor, I wept—briefly, I hope,
though no witness survives to certify my restraint. Outside, wheels
thundered over the stones, bells rang from counting houses, and
young men hurried after fortunes they believed immortal.

Poor Jimmy! Poor peacock! Dear, indestructible rose!

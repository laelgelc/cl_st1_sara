## HIS EXCELLENCY'S OWN BOSS

The senior Senator was going to die.

This was denied by his physicians, resented by his family,
and editorially condemned by every orthodox newspaper in the State;
nevertheless, it was the one fact upon which all well-informed
persons agreed.

The physicians called his condition "a temporary failure of nervous
energy." His family announced that he had passed a comfortable
night. The newspapers printed an old photograph in which he
appeared as a Roman senator who had accidentally put on
a frock coat, and assured their readers that the veteran
statesman would soon resume his labors at Washington. But Mr.
Tom Styles, president of three railroads, director of two banks,
and philanthropic dabbler in politics, knew that the old gentleman
would never again leave his bed except in a less
comfortable vehicle.

Mr. Styles was not saddened. He had never permitted death—or
birth, marriage, drought, flood, or criminal indictment—to disturb him except
as it affected business considerations.

A Senatorial vacancy was a business consideration.

The logical successor was Governor Marcus Waring. He was logical
because he desired the office, because the organization desired him
to have it, and because Mr. Styles had spent a
large sum of money making him Governor. Nothing could have
been more logical.

There was only one difficulty: the Governor could not appoint
himself.

"That," said Waring, when the two men discussed the matter
in the private room of the Capital Club, "is where
the Constitution is defective."

Styles stirred his coffee.

"The Constitution," he replied, "is never defective. It merely has
to be understood."

Waring did not care for this answer. He had an
uneasy respect for constitutions, not because he regarded them as
sacred, but because they were printed documents which hostile newspapers
might quote.

"It would look bad," he said. "You must admit that."

"My dear Governor, everything looks bad when badly explained."

"The reform papers would say I had resigned for the
purpose of getting the appointment."

"Then the reform papers would be guilty of their usual
malice."

"But that would be the purpose."

Styles looked mildly surprised, as one public benefactor may look
when another insists upon introducing an irrelevant fact.

"You would resign," he said, "because the condition of your
plantation requires your personal attention. Cotton is in a deplorable
state. The boll weevil is threatening the prosperity of the
South. No conscientious landowner could remain indifferent."

"I haven't been to the plantation in five years."

"Precisely. That gives the announcement force."

Governor Waring walked to the window. Below him the lights
of the city shone through a smoky evening, and somewhere
beyond them stood the State-house whose marble corridors had lately
become too narrow for his ambition.

"Suppose Senator Gilman does not die?" he asked.

Styles glanced at his watch.

"He will."

"I mean soon."

"I cannot undertake to arrange every detail."

The Governor turned sharply, but Styles was studying the surface
of his coffee. His face was broad, smooth, and tranquil.
It was the face of a man who had never
raised his voice because so many other men were ready
to raise theirs for him.

"And Berriman?" Waring asked.

"John Berriman becomes Governor upon your resignation."

"Will he make the appointment?"

"He will be told to."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It is the same thing."

Governor Waring resumed his chair. He wished that Styles had
displayed less certainty; yet it was that certainty which had
carried Waring from a county solicitorship to the executive mansion.
One did not quarrel with the engine because it made
a disagreeable noise while pulling one uphill.

John Berriman, the Lieutenant-Governor, had been selected for that office
because nobody objected to him. This is a quality not
always esteemed in private life, but in politics it may
amount to genius. He had offended no faction, advanced no
dangerous opinion, and made no speech which could be remembered
after supper. He possessed a pleasant face, a moderate income,
a reputation for personal decency, and no organization of his
own. Every interest in the State had therefore approved him
as a harmless man.

"Berriman may get notions when he is Governor," Waring said.

"Men do," Styles conceded. "But not Berriman."

Thus the matter was settled.

It was politics—enterprise—life.

The announcement of Governor Waring's retirement was received with proper
astonishment by persons who had known of it for a
week. His Excellency stated that private affairs demanded his immediate
presence upon his ancestral acres. He referred with feeling to
the sacred claims of the soil, the dignity of agriculture,
and the silent sufferings of the cotton planter. He did
not state that the plantation was mortgaged to one of
Mr. Styles's banks, nor that its management had long been
conducted by an overseer whom Waring had met twice.

The organization newspapers praised his sacrifice.

"It is characteristic of Marcus Waring," declared the *Morning Clarion*,
"that he should lay aside the glittering honors of public
station at the call of simple duty."

The reform newspapers were less affected.

"The cotton," observed the *Independent*, "has apparently developed political influence."

Mr. Styles read both comments and approved them. Praise helped
among the faithful, while abuse from the enemy strengthened party
obligation. A machine, like a church, needs a devil.

John Berriman was sworn in on a gray afternoon. He
took the oath with a curious sense of embarrassment, as
though he had been mistaken for somebody of importance and
lacked the courage to explain. Waring shook his hand before
the cameras. Styles stood behind them, benign and almost paternal.

"Governor," he said afterward, "I congratulate you."

The title startled Berriman.

"Much obliged, Mr. Styles."

"You will find that everything works out very smoothly if
you do not let small matters trouble you."

"I hope I can do what's expected."

"I have no doubt of it."

Styles placed a hand upon his shoulder. To an observer
the gesture might have seemed affectionate. Berriman knew that it
was ownership, but ownership so old, so customary, and so
generally accepted that he felt no resentment. The party had
made him Lieutenant-Governor. The party had now, by an unexpected
turn, made him Governor. What could be more natural than
that the party should tell him what to do?

The new Governor moved into the executive office and sat
behind the great mahogany desk. A portrait of an earlier
chief executive hung opposite him—a severe man with white whiskers
who had called out the militia against a railroad during
the disturbances of 1878. Berriman thought the old fellow looked
uncomfortable, and instructed his secretary to move a palm in
front of him.

For nine days Senator Gilman continued to live.

This was regarded in political circles as inconsiderate.

Waring remained upon his plantation long enough to be photographed
beside a cotton stalk. Then he returned to the capital,
explaining that business connected with the marketing of the crop
required consultation with leading financiers. He occupied a suite at
the Capital Hotel and waited.

Styles waited.

The organization waited.

Senator Gilman, having spent forty years obeying the party, now
disobeyed it by breathing.

Bulletins came twice daily. The patient was resting. The patient
was holding his own. The patient had taken nourishment. Each
hopeful phrase caused indignation in places where indignation could not
publicly be expressed.

Waring became nervous.

"What if he rallies?" he demanded.

"Then you will have rendered a noble service to agriculture,"
Styles replied.

"I'll be the laughingstock of the State."

"Only temporarily."

"You're trying to jolly me."

"I never jolly anyone."

This was true. Mr. Styles encouraged, persuaded, instructed, rewarded, and
occasionally destroyed; he did not jolly.

On the tenth night Senator Gilman died.

He died at eleven forty-seven, but the information did not
reach Mr. Styles until eleven fifty-three. By midnight the organization
had prepared a tribute to his incorruptible devotion to public
duty, together with a statement that the appointment of former
Governor Waring would assure the continuance of those policies for
which the deceased statesman had so long and ably contended.

At seven the next morning a messenger delivered a telegram
at the executive mansion.

SENATOR GILMAN DEAD. APPOINT WARING WITHOUT DELAY. IMPORTANT AVOID APPEARANCE
OF UNCERTAINTY.
STYLES.

Governor Berriman read it over his coffee. His wife, who
did not like Mr. Styles, asked what it was.

"Business," he said.

He slipped the paper into his pocket, finished his breakfast,
and went to the State-house.

All day men passed through his office. Some spoke of
the dead Senator with moist eyes and alert expressions. Some
spoke of party harmony. Others spoke of the necessity of
prompt action in order that the State should not lack
its full representation at Washington. Nearly all of them mentioned
Waring's name with the solemnity due to a law of
nature.

Berriman listened and nodded.

He intended to appoint Waring. He had never considered doing
anything else. The arrangement had been explained to him before
the resignation, not exactly as a request but as part
of the day's information, like the hour of a train
or the order of speakers at a banquet. He had
accepted it in the same manner.

Late that afternoon, however, his secretary entered with a card.

"Your brother is outside, Governor."

"My brother?"

"Hiram Berriman, from Clay County."

John rose quickly.

"Well, send him in. Why'd you keep him waiting?"

Hiram came through the door with his hat in his
hand and red mud upon his boots. He was ten
years older than John, broader in the shoulders, bent a
little from work, and dressed in a black suit which
had plainly been bought for funerals and political occasions. His
gray beard was cut close; his hands were large, cracked,
and brown. The executive office, with its velvet curtains, gilded
clock, and official silence, caused him no visible awe.

"Howdy, John," he said.

"Why, Hiram! I didn't know you were in town."

"Wasn't, till the noon train."

They shook hands. John felt suddenly younger. He remembered following
those same muddy boots through plowed fields, and watching those
large hands mend a gate after their father had died.

"Sit down. How's Ellen?"

"Tol'able. Rheumatism's bad. Crops is fair."

Hiram lowered himself into a leather chair and looked about
him.

"Fine place."

"It's comfortable."

"Seems like a powerful lot of room for one man."

"Oh, people are in and out."

"I reckon."

His eyes rested upon the portrait behind the palm.

"Who you hidin' back there?"

"One of the old Governors."

"Done somethin' shameful?"

John laughed and moved the plant aside.

"No. I didn't like him looking at me."

"Mebbe he was tryin' to see what you was up
to."

Hiram said it without humor. John's laugh ended.

"What brought you down?" he asked.

"You."

"Anything wrong?"

"That's what I come to find out."

John leaned back.

"If you mean about the appointment, it's settled."

"Is it?"

"Yes. Waring will be appointed tomorrow."

"You picked him?"

John hesitated at the plainness of the question.

"He is the logical successor."

"Who says so?"

"The party."

"Party got a mouth?"

"You know what I mean. The leaders—the men responsible for
the organization."

"Tom Styles?"

"Among others."

Hiram placed his hat on his knees and began slowly
turning it by the brim.

"This Waring an honest man?"

"Why, Hiram, that isn't a fair question."

"Seems middlin' short."

"Public men are judged by different standards. Waring has enemies.
Any strong man has. Charges are made in the heat
of political controversy which nobody is expected to take literally."

"Is he honest?"

John got up and walked toward the window.

"He is loyal to the party."

"I didn't ask that."

"He has served the State."

"Served who in the State?"

"You can't come in here from a farm and expect
to understand every political question."

"No," Hiram said. "That's why I'm askin'."

The Governor looked at his brother. There was no anger
in Hiram's face, no accusation—only a patient desire for an
answer. It was harder to meet than anger.

"Waring has experience," John said. "He knows Washington. He has
influential connections. In the Senate a man must be practical."

"Practical for who?"

"For the State."

"Will he vote for the railroads or for the folks
that pay freight?"

"That depends upon the measure."

"Will it depend on what Tom Styles tells him?"

John flushed.

"You're repeating reform talk."

"Never read a reform paper in my life. They don't
come our way. I'm askin' you."

"Styles is a large employer. His interests are bound up
with the prosperity of the State."

"His railroad charged me more to send corn eighty miles
last fall than the corn fetched."

"That's a rate question. It has nothing to do with
the appointment."

"Seems like it might have something."

"Hiram, you don't understand."

The farmer nodded slowly.

"Mebbe not. I understand a mule, though. When a man
puts a bridle on him, I know which one's drivin'."

Berriman struck the desk with his palm.

"Nobody is driving me!"

"Then don't holler."

The two brothers stared at one another. In the outer
office a typewriter clicked, stopped, and began again. The gilt
clock ticked with expensive dignity.

Hiram leaned forward.

"You Governor?"

"You know I am."

"You swore an oath?"

"Certainly."

"To Tom Styles?"

"To the Constitution."

"Party named in it?"

"No."

"Railroad?"

"No."

"Waring?"

"Of course not."

"Then what are you fussin' about?"

"It isn't so simple."

"Why ain't it?"

"Because I have obligations. The organization nominated me. Men contributed
money. They worked for me. I owe them something."

"Your salary come from them?"

"No."

"Who pays it?"

"The State."

"Who's the State?"

John made an impatient gesture.

"All the people, naturally."

"The farmers in Clay County part of it?"

"Yes."

"The mill hands over at Granton?"

"Yes."

"Storekeepers? Schoolteachers? Widows payin' tax on little houses?"

"Yes, yes. All of them."

Hiram's voice softened.

"Then ain't you their hired man?"

John turned away again.

The phrase was uncouth. It had none of the breadth
of "party obligation," none of the authority of "business considerations,"
none of the polished inevitability of "logical successor." But it
entered his mind with the sharpness of a nail.

Hiram continued:

"I ain't sayin' who you ought to appoint. Don't know
the men well enough. But you do—or you ought to.
If Waring's the best man for the people, name him.
If he ain't, don't. That's all."

"You think a Governor can ignore his party?"

"I think a man can."

"You have no idea what would happen. They could ruin
me."

"Ruin what?"

"My career."

Hiram looked around the large office.

"Mebbe that's what's hidin' behind the palm."

John wheeled toward him, but the farmer was rising.

"Got to catch the eight-ten," Hiram said. "Ellen'll be frettin'
if I don't."

"You came all this way just to lecture me?"

"No. Came to ask if you was your own boss."

"And are you satisfied?"

Hiram put on his hat.

"Ain't for me to answer."

He started toward the door, then stopped.

"Pa used to say an office didn't make a man
bigger. Just made it easier to see how big he
was."

"That sounds like Pa."

"It ought to. He said it often enough."

For the first time Hiram smiled.

"Come up when you can, John. South fence needs settin'.
Governor might know how to set a post."

Then he was gone.

Berriman remained in the office until after dark. He signed
letters, initialed reports, and read the same paragraph of a
pardon petition six times. Hiram's questions returned with absurd persistence.

Who pays it?

Who's the State?

Are you your own boss?

They were not profound questions. Mr. Styles could have disposed
of them in five minutes. Any trained politician could have
explained that a Governor must consider practical necessities; that parties
are essential instruments of popular government; that campaign contributions express
legitimate confidence in sound administration; that the interests of capital
are inseparable from the interests of labor. There were answers
by the yard, and John Berriman knew most of them.

Yet Hiram had not asked for a speech. He had
asked whether Waring was honest.

At ten o'clock the Governor walked home. The night was
cold, with a dry wind moving down the broad avenue.
Above the State-house the stars looked near and hard. The
dome, which by daylight had always appeared to Berriman somewhat
oversized and inconvenient, rose against the sky with a grave
simplicity.

For the first time he thought of the men who
had built it.

Not the contractors, who had exceeded their estimates, nor the
legislators who had voted the bonds, but the citizens whose
taxes had paid for the stone: farmers driving wagons through
winter mud, mechanics carrying dinner pails, women counting coins at
kitchen tables. They had never entered the executive office. Most
of them would not recognize him on the street. Yet
in some remote and rather tremendous fashion they had placed
him there.

He had always taken the State for granted. Now, for
a moment, he saw it.

The experience was disturbing.

At home he went to bed, but not to sleep.
He considered the probable consequences of disobedience. Styles would break
with him. The organization would denounce him. The newspapers which
had called him a safe and sensible executive would discover
that he was vain, unstable, and ungrateful. His public career
would very likely end with his present term.

Then he considered Waring in the Senate—Waring voting upon railroad
regulation, land grants, tariffs, and banking laws; Waring conferring with
Styles; Waring wearing the dead man's shoes because the fit
had been privately measured before the death.

At two in the morning Berriman rose and found the
telegram in his coat.

APPOINT WARING WITHOUT DELAY.

The sentence had seemed natural at breakfast. Now he observed
something he had previously missed.

It was an order.

He tore it once, then stopped. Tearing a telegram was
easy and dramatic, and he distrusted drama. He placed the
pieces together on the bureau.

"Don't be a fool," he told himself.

His wife stirred.

"What is it, John?"

"Nothing."

"Then come back to bed."

He obeyed. A little before dawn he slept.

Morning came clear and cold. Berriman walked to the State-house
instead of using the official carriage. The air stung his
face and made him feel unexpectedly cheerful. Clerks hurried past
with newspapers under their arms. A street cleaner called a
greeting. Two boys ran beside a delivery wagon, stealing bits
of ice. Shop doors opened; smoke rose; the city resumed
its noisy, ordinary life without consulting Mr. Styles.

At the foot of the State-house steps Berriman paused.

The building no longer seemed merely the place where his
office happened to be. It belonged to the people passing
behind him. He experienced no grand revelation, no assurance that
he had become a hero. He was still John Berriman—cautious,
moderate, and rather afraid. But there are mornings when fear,
though present, ceases to be commanding.

His secretary, George Talcott, met him with a second telegram.

"From Mr. Styles, Governor. Marked urgent."

Berriman opened it.

APPOINTMENT MUST BE ANNOUNCED BY NOON. DELAY CREATING UNFAVORABLE IMPRESSION.
EXPECT YOU TO ACT.
STYLES.

"Mr. Styles telephoned twice," Talcott said. "Former Governor Waring is
at the hotel."

Berriman folded the telegram.

"George, shut the door."

Talcott did so. He was a thin young man with
careful hair and the cautious eyes of one who hopes
eventually to enter politics without being caught at it.

"How long have you lived in this State?" Berriman asked.

"All my life, sir."

"You read the papers?"

"Yes, sir."

"Know public sentiment?"

"As much as anybody can."

"Then who do you think is the best man for
Senator?"

Talcott glanced at the closed door.

"In what sense, Governor?"

"In the sense of being the best man."

"I'm not sure I understand."

"That seems to be the trouble with everybody. Suppose there
were no organization orders, no campaign debts, no Mr. Styles
and no promises. Whom would the people choose?"

Talcott's eyes widened.

"The people?"

"Yes."

"Well—Huntington, I suppose."

"Henry Huntington?"

"Yes, sir."

Huntington led the reform faction. He had opposed Styles's railroad
bills, exposed the purchasing of a legislative committee, and twice
been defeated for Governor after carrying nearly every rural county
in the State. The organization papers called him a fanatic.
Privately, organization men called him dangerous, by which they meant
that he believed the things he said.

"Is he honest?" Berriman asked.

"I've never heard otherwise."

"Capable?"

"Very."

"Popular?"

"He would probably win a direct election by fifty thousand."

"Then why isn't he the logical successor?"

Talcott looked helpless.

"He's not the organization candidate."

"Ah."

The Governor sat down. He moved several papers, examined a
pen, and looked at the portrait opposite him. During the
night the palm had been removed. The old Governor with
the white whiskers met his gaze severely, but Berriman no
longer objected.

"Prepare Huntington's appointment," he said.

Talcott did not move.

"Governor?"

"Prepare the commission appointing Henry Huntington to the United States
Senate."

"Do you mean—now?"

"Before noon. Mr. Styles dislikes delay."

The secretary swallowed.

"Yes, sir."

He reached the door.

"George."

"Yes, Governor?"

"What I asked you—about the people. Were you trying to
jolly me?"

"No, sir."

"Good. Neither was I."

At eleven thirty-seven the commission was signed.

At eleven forty-five Styles arrived.

He entered without sending his card, followed by former Governor
Waring and State Chairman Bell. Styles appeared as composed as
ever; Waring did not. He had the expression of a
man who has ordered a carriage and sees it departing
with someone else.

"What is this nonsense?" Waring demanded.

Berriman looked at Styles.

"Good morning."

Styles closed the door.

"We have heard a report," he said, "which I assume
requires correction."

"If it is that I appointed Huntington, it requires confirmation."

Chairman Bell sat down abruptly.

Waring remained standing.

"You can't do it," he said.

"I have done it."

"You promised me!"

"I don't remember promising."

"You understood."

"Yes," Berriman said. "That was the difficulty."

Styles removed his gloves finger by finger.

"Governor, perhaps you do not appreciate the consequences."

"I'm beginning to."

"This appointment is an act of treachery to the party."

"I thought it was an appointment to the Senate."

"It is political suicide."

"That may be."

"It places the organization in the hands of its enemies."

"No. It places Huntington in the Senate."

Waring struck the back of a chair.

"Why Huntington? What has he offered you?"

"Nothing."

"Support for reelection?"

"No."

"A place on his ticket?"

"No."

"Then what is the bargain?"

"There isn't one."

The three politicians regarded him with varying degrees of disbelief.
Bell was bewildered; Waring was furious; Styles alone seemed almost
interested.

"You have some motive," Styles said.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"I think Huntington is the best man, and I think
the people want him."

There was a silence.

Waring laughed first—a short, contemptuous sound.

"Oh, don't give us that!"

Berriman picked up a document from his desk.

"I have work to do, gentlemen."

Styles looked at him for several seconds. Something had escaped
calculation, and he disliked escaped things. Yet he showed neither
anger nor surprise.

"I fear," he said, "that you will regret this."

"I fear so too."

That answer troubled Styles more than defiance would have done.
He put on his gloves.

"Come," he said to Waring.

They left.

Within an hour the news had crossed the city. By
afternoon it had reached every county seat. The organization declared
that Berriman had betrayed his friends under secret pressure from
Eastern financiers. The reform leaders concluded that he was attempting
to divide their faction. Waring's supporters discovered evidence that Huntington
had promised the Governor federal patronage. Huntington's own friends warned
him to be cautious, since no man made such an
appointment without expecting payment.

A reporter from the *Independent* obtained admission late in the
day. He was young, sharp-faced, and deeply suspicious—qualities which had
already made him valuable to his profession.

"Governor," he said, "the public is demanding the true story
of this appointment."

"The commission is public."

"I mean the story behind it."

"There isn't any."

The reporter smiled patiently.

"Did Huntington agree to support you?"

"No."

"Did the railroad opposition bring pressure?"

"No."

"Was the appointment intended as revenge upon Styles?"

"No."

"Did Waring refuse some demand of yours?"

"No."

The reporter lowered his pencil.

"Then, Governor, why did you do it?"

Berriman looked toward the window. Beyond it the city was
settling into evening. Lamps appeared along the avenue; wagons moved
slowly homeward; the white dome held the last pale light
of the winter sky.

"Because," he said, "I thought the people ought to have
the man they wanted."

The reporter waited.

"Yes—but what was the real reason?"

Berriman turned back to his papers.

"That is the real reason."

The young man studied him, disappointed. He had come seeking
conspiracy and found a sentence too simple for a headline.
There must be something underneath it. There was always something
underneath.

He left to discover what it was.

For many weeks the politicians continued the same search. Mr.
Styles's friends searched angrily; Huntington's friends searched gratefully; neutral observers
searched with professional caution. They found ambitions, bargains, combinations, revenges,
and schemes enough to explain a dozen appointments—but none which
quite explained this one.

No faction believed Berriman. Each knew that public men did
not act merely because they considered an act right. Such
conduct would overturn every accepted rule of practical government.

Only Hiram Berriman, reading the news beside his kitchen stove
in Clay County, appeared unsurprised.

Ellen watched him fold the paper.

"What does it say about John?" she asked.

Hiram reached for his boots.

"Says he found out who was Governor."

Then he went to set the south fence, which, unlike
politics, merely leaned in the direction in which it had
been pushed.

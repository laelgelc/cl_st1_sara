### WHO ARE THE POOR?

After an absence of nearly eight years, I returned to
L——, the pleasant town in which the better part of
my youth had been spent. Few experiences are more affecting
than such a return. Streets which memory hath peopled with
familiar forms seem at first unchanged; but, as we look
more closely, new signs meet the eye, old dwellings have
passed into other hands, and faces once seen at every
corner are nowhere to be found.

On the evening of my arrival, I sat with an
early friend, conversing of those whom we had known together.

"And the Wightmans?" I inquired. "What has become of them?"

My friend shook his head.

"They have gone down sadly in the world. You remember
Wightman's store? He failed about five years ago, and lost
every thing."

"Every thing!"

"Yes. The business was wound up, the handsome house sold,
and the family compelled to seek a livelihood as best
they could. Wightman is now a clerk."

"And his wife and daughters?"

"They are living with him, I believe, in a small
house at the northern extremity of the town. They are
very poor."

"Very poor! Ah me!" I said, with a painful remembrance
of their former condition. I had known Mrs. Wightman when
her home was one of the most attractive in L——;
not splendid, indeed, but governed by taste, hospitality, and a
gentle consideration for all who entered it. Her daughters had
been bright, affectionate girls, educated with care, and accustomed to
every reasonable comfort. The image of these estimable people, struggling
with want, followed me after I had retired, and cast
a shadow over the pleasure of my return.

"Payson, at least, has prospered," resumed my friend. "He is
reputed one of our wealthiest men."

"I am glad to hear it. Payson and Wightman were
intimate in former days."

"They took different roads," was the reply. "Payson understood the
world."

There was something in this last sentence that did not
altogether please me; nevertheless, I accepted the ordinary judgment. One
man had risen, and was rich; the other had fallen,
and was very poor. Could the case be plainer?

On the following morning I called at Payson's counting-room. I
found him at a large desk, surrounded by letters, checks,
bank notices, and account-books. Three clerks were writing rapidly; a
fourth had just entered, and stood awaiting directions.

"Take this note to the bank," said Payson, without observing
me. "If they decline its renewal, see Mr. Martin. Tell
him I must have the accommodation until the fifteenth."

"The payment to Corwin falls due to-day," remarked the clerk.

"I know it. Give him a check dated to-morrow."

"He said that nothing but money would answer."

Payson contracted his brows.

"Then tell him I am out. No—stay! Say that I
shall see him at three o'clock. And, Harris, if Mr.
Bolton calls, I am engaged."

The clerk retired, and my old acquaintance turned toward me.
His care-worn face brightened with genuine pleasure.

"My dear friend!" he exclaimed, grasping my hand. "When did
you arrive? Why did you not come directly to my
house?"

As he spoke, however, his eye wandered toward the pile
of papers before him, and I perceived that half his
thoughts remained entangled among the obligations of the day.

"You are busy," said I. "I will call again."

"Not at all. Merely the usual financiering which every man
of business must undergo. Sit down."

Our conversation was interrupted repeatedly. One person wanted a payment;
another desired security; a third brought intelligence that certain goods
had declined in value. Payson met each demand with readiness
and apparent self-possession, but beneath his prompt manner I detected
an anxiety which he could not wholly conceal. At length
he invited me to dine with him that day, and
I promised to come.

His house stood in the most fashionable quarter of L——.
It was large, newly decorated, and externally imposing. The entrance-hall
was paved in alternate squares of black and white marble;
the parlors displayed rich Brussels carpets, crimson damask, brocatelle chairs,
gilded mirrors, costly lamps, and a profusion of ornaments which
seemed arranged less for enjoyment than exhibition. Every object announced
expense; yet, amid the glossy satin and glittering metal, I
sought in vain for that genuine home feeling which often
warms a room containing scarcely an article of value.

Mrs. Payson received me with elaborate cordiality. She was still
handsome, though there were lines about her mouth which no
art could soften. Her dress was of a costly material;
rings flashed upon her fingers, and a heavy chain encircled
her neck.

"How delighted we are to see you!" she said. "You
find L—— greatly improved, I suppose? We are becoming quite
metropolitan. Society is so different from what it was eight
years ago."

Her eldest daughter entered while she was speaking. The young
lady wore lace and ribbons suited rather to an evening
assembly than a family dinner.

"My dear," said Mrs. Payson, "where is Julia?"

"In her room. She declares she cannot come down. Her
new silk has been spoiled in making."

"Absurd! Tell her Mr. —— is here."

"She says she has nothing fit to wear."

Payson, who had entered behind his daughter, smiled in a
constrained manner.

"Nothing fit to wear!" he repeated. "Her wardrobe would clothe
a small congregation."

"Now, Mr. Payson," said his wife, laughing, "pray do not
begin one of your economical lectures. A young lady must
dress like her associates."

"Her associates seem determined to ruin their fathers."

"How very severe! You forget that your own position requires
something of us."

"I am not likely to forget what my position requires,"
he answered, with an emphasis intelligible to me, though lost
upon his wife.

Dinner was served in a style corresponding with the apartments.
There were several courses, wines of different kinds, and more
food wasted than would have supplied a frugal household for
a day. Yet no cheerful spirit presided over the table.
Mrs. Payson spoke of a new carriage belonging to one
neighbor, the diamonds worn by another, and the expected improvements
in a third person's mansion. Her daughters discussed dresses, parties,
and a projected journey to a fashionable watering-place. Payson ate
little, and answered absently. Once, when his wife said that
their drawing-room furniture must be changed before winter, he laid
down his fork.

"Changed! It was bought only two years ago."

"But the color is no longer fashionable."

"The debt upon it is not yet paid."

A brief silence followed.

"You always say such things before company," returned Mrs. Payson,
with a forced laugh. "Any one would suppose us to
be very poor."

Payson looked across the table at me, and there was
more sadness than anger in his eyes.

After dinner, he drew me into the library.

"You see how it is," he said, closing the door.
"My family see the romance of wealth; I see the
hard reality. Every new indulgence creates a new necessity. What
was luxury last year is comfort now, and will be
an indispensable want to-morrow."

"Then you are not so prosperous as the world supposes?"

He walked twice across the room before replying.

"My business is large."

"That does not answer my question."

"No," he said, lowering his voice. "I am not prosperous—at
least, not securely so. I have property, but it is
burdened. I conduct transactions involving many thousands, yet there are
days when the demand for a few hundreds makes my
blood run cold. Still, what can I do? If I
retrench, people will suspect embarrassment; suspicion will injure my credit;
and the injury to my credit may bring the very
disaster I seek to avoid."

"But your household expenses?"

"I have remonstrated until remonstrance has become useless. My wife
thinks me timid; my daughters believe money may be procured
whenever it is wanted. They compare themselves with those who
spend more, never with those who spend less. If I
gained twenty thousand dollars to-morrow, they would discover before night
that we needed thirty."

I could not but pity him. He had intelligence enough
to perceive the precipice, but lacked courage to turn from
the road that led toward it. Moreover, I suspected that
he himself was not innocent. The fine house, the reputation
of wealth, and the deference paid to supposed prosperity were
pleasant to him. He condemned the harvest while continuing to
sow the seed.

Before I left, I mentioned the Wightmans.

"Poor people!" said Mrs. Payson, who had joined us. "How
dreadfully they must suffer! I met Mrs. Wightman last week.
She had on the same bonnet she wore last season."

"Did she look unhappy?" I asked.

"Really, I did not observe. She smiled, I believe. But
think of living as they do! The girls even work."

Payson turned away.

"Wightman is an honest man," he said. "His failure paid
his creditors more than most failures do. I offered him
a place in my counting-room, but he had already engaged
himself elsewhere."

"They are very poor, are they not?" said I.

"Very poor," replied Mrs. Payson, with ready commiseration.

The words followed me into the street. Very poor! I
had heard them before; yet, after what I had seen,
they possessed a less definite meaning.

That evening I set out for the northern part of
the town. The distance was greater than I had expected,
and the fashionable squares gradually gave place to narrower streets
and humbler dwellings. At last I found the house I
sought—a small, two-story building, painted white, with green blinds. A
narrow garden lay before it. There were no marble steps,
no gilded ornaments, no costly curtains; but the path was
swept, the windows shone, and flowers bloomed in boxes upon
the sill.

A young woman opened the door. For a moment I
did not recognize her; then the face of the child
I had known came smiling through the maturer features.

"Can it be Mr. ——?" she exclaimed. "Mother! Father! Here
is an old friend."

In another moment I was surrounded by the whole family.
Their welcome had in it no formality, no effort. Mrs.
Wightman's dress was plain, but scrupulously neat. Time and trial
had touched her countenance; yet its expression was peaceful, and
her voice retained the gentle music I remembered. Wightman looked
older by more than the eight years of my absence.
His hair was thin, and labor had bent his shoulders
slightly; but his eye was clear, and his hand met
mine with a cordial pressure.

Their sitting-room was small. The carpet had been carefully mended;
the furniture was old, but well preserved; a few books
occupied shelves made by no professional cabinet-maker; and upon the
walls hung several familiar pictures rescued, perhaps, from the wreck
of former fortunes. Near the window sat the younger daughter,
sewing upon a child's dress.

"You find us changed," said Wightman.

"I find you in a different house."

"But not, I trust, different friends," replied his wife.

There was no shame in her manner, and no anxious
attempt to explain their condition. The fact of altered fortune
was accepted as simply as the coming of winter after
summer.

"You must remain to supper," said Mrs. Wightman.

I hesitated, fearing that my presence might occasion expense.

"We shall be disappointed if you refuse," added one of
the daughters. "Father has brought home a remarkable appetite, and
we need assistance in keeping him company."

Her playful tone relieved me. I remained; and the supper
which followed, though composed of bread, preserves, a little cold
meat, and tea, was one of the pleasantest meals at
which I had ever sat. Every thing was good of
its kind; the table was spread with care; and cheerful
affection gave a flavor which no cook can procure and
no money buy.

The conversation turned naturally upon their changed circumstances.

"We had some up-hill work at first," said Wightman. "I
was not prepared for a clerk's duties, and my salary
was small. Pride, too, made the burden heavier than it
need have been."

"But father soon became the most useful man in the
establishment," said his elder daughter.

"Children are partial witnesses," he returned, smiling. "Still, my employer
has increased my salary three times in five years, and
I hope I have earned every increase."

"He has, indeed," said Mrs. Wightman.

"And we have not been idle," added the daughter who
had admitted me. "Mary gives music lessons, and I do
fine sewing. Mother says that six willing hands are a
small capital, but a safe one."

"Your sisters seem happy in their employments," I remarked.

"Why should we not be?" asked Mary. "Work is no
disgrace. I confess that, at first, I disliked meeting those
who had known us in our former home. I fancied
every eye was fixed upon my plain dress, and every
one was saying, 'How they have fallen!' But I found
that honest occupation gives courage. If people despise me because
I am useful, the loss of their esteem does not
make me poorer."

"That lesson was not learned in a day," said her
mother gently.

"No, indeed. I cried over it more than once."

This acknowledgment pleased me better than any boast of untried
fortitude. Their contentment was not the dull submission of persons
without desires; it was a moral attainment, reached through conflict.

After supper Wightman asked me to walk through the little
house. There were four chambers, all plainly furnished, but clean,
airy, and orderly. In one I noticed a desk, music-books,
and a vase containing fresh flowers; in another, half-finished garments
lay folded upon a table. Every part of the dwelling
bore evidence of industry directed by taste.

When we returned below, Wightman placed his hand upon the
mantel and looked around him.

"This is not the house in which you visited us
formerly," he said; "but it is ours."

"Yours?"

"Yes. The last payment was made this morning."

A glow of satisfaction passed over every face.

"We have been six years paying for it," said Mrs.
Wightman. "Sometimes the sum laid aside was very small; but
it carried us nearer the end."

"And now," continued her husband, "no man holds a claim
upon the roof that shelters us. My employer, who is
reputed rich, was searching anxiously yesterday for several thousand dollars
to meet his bank payments. I, a humble clerk in
his service, slept without fear; for I owed no man
any thing which I could not pay."

"Then you do not regard yourselves as very poor?" I
said.

They looked at one another, and smiled.

"We have little money," replied Mrs. Wightman; "but we have
health, employment, affection, and a home. If these do not
make riches, I know not what riches are."

"Still," said Wightman thoughtfully, "I would not speak as though
contentment came without effort. When we lost our property, I
was bitter for a time. I compared my condition with
Payson's, and felt injured because he rose while I fell.
But I found that envy added nothing to my salary,
and took much from my peace. Since then I have
been trying to learn contentment."

"And have you learned it?" I asked.

"Enough to know that it is worth learning. A man
should strive to improve his circumstances; industry is a duty.
But if he refuses to enjoy any blessing until he
has obtained every blessing, he will go sorrowful to the
grave."

Mrs. Wightman laid her hand upon his arm.

"We have had cause for thankfulness even in our reverses,"
she said. "They taught us what we could do for
one another. In the old days, each pleasure seemed to
require money; now many of our best pleasures cost nothing."

"How little did they dream," thought I, "that at this
very hour people seated in splendid rooms were pitying them
as very poor!"

Before I departed, the family sang a hymn. Their voices
were not highly trained, but they blended sweetly; and when
the words expressed confidence in the care of the great
Father, no discord between profession and life disturbed their meaning.
I walked back through the quiet streets with a new
interpretation of poverty in my heart.

Two days afterward I met Payson alone.

"Well," said he, "you have seen the Wightmans?"

"I have."

"And found them very poor, no doubt."

"I found them among the richest people in L——."

He stared at me.

"They have paid for their little house," I continued. "They
owe no man beyond their ability to pay; they earn
what they spend, enjoy what they possess, and love one
another. Their table is plain, but peace sits at it.
Their garments are inexpensive, but they wear no disguises. Their
wishes do not outrun their means by a thousand dollars
every month."

Payson's face grew grave.

"You mean to rebuke me."

"I mean to answer a question which has troubled me
since my return. Poverty is not always the absence of
money. He is poor whose necessities continually exceed his possessions;
who receives blessings without thankfulness, and sacrifices present peace to
the appearance of prosperity. Judged by this rule, my friend,
which household is very poor?"

For some moments he did not speak. Then he said,
in a low voice—

"Mine."

The confession was not made angrily. It came from a
mind upon which conviction had forced itself.

"Yes," he resumed, "we are poor because we are never
satisfied. My wife is poor with rooms full of costly
furniture; my daughters are poor with wardrobes they cannot wear
out; and I am poor with property enough, if unburdened,
to supply every rational want. We have mistaken display for
comfort and credit for wealth."

"It is not too late to change."

He shook his head.

"You do not know how difficult it is to descend
in the eyes of the world. If I sell my
house, curtail my business, and live plainly, every tongue will
announce my failure."

"Better that the world should falsely announce it to-day than
truly announce it to-morrow."

He pressed my hand.

"You are right. I will speak to my family this
very night."

But good resolutions, when opposed by long habit, wounded pride,
and domestic vanity, require a firmer will than Payson possessed.
His wife met his proposal with tears and reproaches. His
daughters declared that removal to a smaller house would banish
them from society. Even he, when the first courage of
conviction had passed, began to hope that one fortunate speculation
might repair all.

A twelvemonth had scarcely gone by when the expected blow
fell. A decline in the value of property, the failure
of two debtors, and the refusal of a bank to
renew a large accommodation brought his affairs to a crisis.
His house, furniture, horses, and carriage were sold; his business
passed into other hands; and those who had courted his
supposed prosperity stood at a prudent distance from his misfortune.

I saw him soon afterward in a contracted dwelling, with
a wife who regarded every retrenchment as degradation, and daughters
who had not learned to work. The outward splendor was
gone, but the inward poverty remained. They had neither the
means which once supported indulgence, nor the gratitude and affection
which might have softened deprivation.

They were then, in the common meaning of the words,
very poor.

But, ah me! they had been very poor long before.

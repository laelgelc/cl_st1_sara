All the years I was making ready for this minute.

Whenever I put on my best dress, whenever I stood
before the glass and fixed on my hat, I used
to think: Maybe to-day I'll meet him. Maybe on Grand
Street, maybe in the subway, maybe in a restaurant where
Sam takes me once in a year. And I would
practise how I would look on him.

I would stand straight. I would smile, but not too
much.

"How do you do, Doctor Levin?" I would say, cold
like an American lady. "This is my husband, Mr. Kaplan.
We got three fine children. My oldest plays already the
piano."

Then I would turn away before he could see anything
in my eyes.

Thousands of times I met him like this in my
head. Thousands of times I showed him how I lived
without him, how he didn't kill me, how from the
poor greenhorn girl in a shirt-waist shop I became Mrs.
Samuel Kaplan, with a fur collar for winter and lace
curtains in the front room.

But when the minute came—Gott im Himmel!—where was my proud
speech? Where was my American smile?

It was at my cousin Bessie's daughter's wedding. The hall
was full from light and noise. The musicians were tuning
themselves in the corner; children ran under the tables; women
shone with beads and satin and rings. Sam was talking
business with a man from Newark. I stood near the
bride's table, holding a plate of cake, when the people
by the door moved apart.

There he was.

David.

Older, yes. Gray by the temples. A little heavier in
the face. But the same eyes—the same deep, thinking eyes
that once looked into my nothingness and made me feel
I was somebody in the world.

On his arm was a woman tall and quiet, with
pearls around her neck. His wife.

The plate gave a shake in my hand. The cake
slid against my glove. The floor swelled up under me
like water.

"Stand!" I told myself. "Sara, stand! Don't you give a
fall here before everybody."

But my knees weren't mine. My hands weren't mine. The
whole wedding hall went far away, and only his face
remained, coming through years and years of darkness.

He had not yet seen me.

I could run. I could hide behind the women in
the cloakroom. But my feet stood nailed.

And in that second I am again the girl with
the red hands from factory needles, waiting by the corner
lamp for a medical student whose one word could lift
me over all the roofs of New York.

I was seventeen when I first met David Levin.

At seventeen I knew nothing except how to hurry.

Hurry in the morning before the foreman locked the factory
door. Hurry with the bundles of shirt-waists. Hurry home with
bread. Hurry up four flights because Mamma needed medicine, Papa
wanted tea, my little brothers were fighting, and the potatoes
burned in the pan.

We lived then in three rooms where even the walls
were tired. The plaster hung loose over Papa's bed. One
chair had a board where the seat should be. In
winter we stuffed rags around the window, but the wind
came in anyhow and walked over us in the night.

Yet Papa called our house a palace because in Russia
he had slept once with six families in a cellar.

"A roof is a roof," he said. "Bread is bread.
And America is America."

America! To Papa it was a prayer. To me it
was a language everybody knew except me.

I could sew eighteen tucks in a minute, but if
an American woman asked me a question, my tongue became
a piece from wood. The girls laughed how I said
"woik" for work and "toity" for thirty. I laughed with
them, but inside I burned.

David came one rainy evening to see my brother Moishe,
who had cut his hand in the machine shop. He
was not yet a doctor, only studying, but in our
house a man with a clean collar and a black
case was already like a prince.

He bent over Moishe's hand while all of us crowded
around.

"Give the patient air," he said.

"Air?" cried Mamma. "In such a cold he wants air?"

David laughed. His laugh had kindness in it. Not the
laugh from people who see your foolishness and step on
it, but a laugh that took you in together with
him.

I was holding the lamp. He looked up.

"Can you bring me clean cloth?"

I understood "cloth," but I was so trembling from his
eyes that I brought a spoon.

"A spoon?" he said.

Blood rushed over my face.

"She don't understand yet good English," Mamma explained. "Only two
years we are in the country."

"I understand plenty!" I cried, and ran for the cloth.

When he went away, he forgot a little notebook on
the table. I found it and ran after him down
the stairs.

"Doctor! Doctor!"

He turned in the hallway.

"I'm not a doctor yet."

"You look already like one."

He smiled. "And you look like you were born here—almost."

"Don't make fun from me."

"I'm not making fun. What is your name?"

"Sara."

"Sarah," he corrected.

"Sara."

"The American way is Sarah."

"Then learn me."

So it began.

He came again to dress Moishe's hand, and after that
he came though the hand was already good. Sometimes he
brought a newspaper and made me read aloud. Sometimes he
walked with me from the factory.

"Not 'dis'," he would say. "This."

"Dis."

"Put your tongue between your teeth."

"In the street I should stick out my tongue?"

"You want to speak correctly, don't you?"

"Correctly, yes. But not like a crazy person."

He laughed until he had tears.

Once he took me to a little restaurant near the
university. I had never in my life eaten where a
waiter stood over me. When I saw three forks, my
heart fell.

"What are they all for?" I whispered. "How many mouths
Americans got?"

"One fork is for the fish."

"Fish needs a special fork? At home, if Papa gets
fish, he blesses God and eats it with bread."

"Watch me," David said.

I watched him. I watched how he unfolded the napkin,
how he held the menu, how he spoke without fear
to the waiter. Everything he did seemed from a higher
world. But when I copied him and the spoon slipped
into my coffee, he did not become ashamed from me.
He took his own spoon and dropped it in too.

"Now it's the fashion," he said.

Ach, what was happiness in those days? Not diamonds. Not
a carriage. It was a spoon falling into coffee and
somebody loving you more for your foolishness.

When I worked at the machine, the belts roared over
my head and the foreman shouted, but I felt no
tiredness. Every white piece of cloth that passed under my
needle had David's face on it. The windows were black
with smoke, yet I saw blue sky. Ten hours I
sat bent like an old woman, and inside me I
was flying.

At six he waited sometimes by the factory door.

The girls nudged me. "Sara, your doctor is here!"

My poor shoes had holes, my coat was thin, my
hair smelled from steam and cloth dust, but when David
took my arm, Fifth Avenue itself could have opened before
me. We walked by the river, where the air had
salt and iron in it. He spoke of bones, nerves,
fevers, and the great things medicine would do.

"You'll have an office?" I asked.

"With a brass plate on the door: David Levin, M.D."

"And I'll polish it every morning."

"You won't need to polish it. We'll have a maid."

"A maid!" I stopped walking. "For me?"

"For Mrs. Doctor Levin."

I could not breathe.

"Say it again."

"What?"

"Mrs. Doctor Levin."

He put his hands on my shoulders.

"When I graduate, Sarah, we'll marry."

The city lifted itself. The bridges, the chimneys, the tenement
roofs—all went under us. There was no poor and rich,
no learning and not learning. There was only his two
hands and my heart beating between them.

I told Mamma that night while she was patching Papa's
undershirt.

"A doctor?" she whispered. "He said marriage?"

"With his own mouth."

She laid the shirt down and kissed me. Then she
began to cry.

"Why are you crying?"

"From too much good, Hertzele. Too much good makes also
tears."

Papa heard and came from the other room.

"What's this secret?"

When we told him, he lifted both hands.

"A learned man in the family! My daughter, a doctor's
wife!"

From that day all the house lived toward David's graduation.
Mamma saved pennies in a sugar bowl for my wedding
linen. Papa spoke of nothing except the fine son-in-law. Moishe
said he would paint the doctor's sign himself.

But sometimes, when David thought I did not watch, a
cloud passed over his face.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Examinations."

"You'll pass."

"It isn't only examinations."

"What then?"

"Nothing. My uncle is coming next month."

This uncle, Mr. Max Levin, had paid for David's studies.
He owned apartment houses and a drug business uptown. David
spoke of him with respect and fear mixed together.

"He wants me to open an office near his neighborhood,"
David said. "He knows people with money."

"So good. You'll have patients."

"He also has ideas about whom I should marry."

The air changed.

"Didn't you tell him from me?"

"I told him there is someone."

"Someone? Why not Sara?"

"I will tell him. Don't begin worrying."

I began worrying.

His grand life and my pinched-in life, his books and
my bundles, his future and my family hanging from every
dollar I made—all these stood suddenly between us like policemen.

"Are you ashamed from me?" I asked.

He caught me against him.

"Never."

"Say you'll marry me."

"I'll marry you."

"Even if your uncle says no?"

"Sara, stop torturing yourself."

"Say it."

"Even if he says no."

I believed him because a woman in love believes not
what is likely, but what keeps her alive.

Then came the day his uncle was to visit our
home.

For a week we prepared as if the President was
coming. Mamma washed the curtains till the cheap cloth tore,
then sewed the tear where she thought nobody could see.
Papa borrowed a tablecloth from Aunt Chave. Moishe painted the
cracked wall, but the new patch showed whiter than the
old. We scrubbed the floor with lye. I covered the
broken chair with a shawl. On the mantel we put
a calendar picture of mountains and the two silver candlesticks
Mamma carried from Russia wrapped in her feather bed.

Mamma baked honey cake though eggs cost high. Papa bought
wine and hid the price from her. My little brothers
had their ears washed till they screamed. I wore a
blue waist I had made from remnants.

Before the knock came, we all looked around the room.

For us it was beautiful.

Mr. Levin entered in a fur-lined coat. He was broad,
smooth-shaved, with a gold watch chain over his stomach. His
eyes went first to the ceiling, then the stove, then
the mended curtain. They touched everything and made it poor.

Papa bowed.

"Welcome to our house. A great honour."

Mr. Levin took two fingers of Papa's hand. Mamma brought
the cake.

"My wife baked special," Papa said. "Taste only."

"I don't eat sweets."

Nobody knew what to do after that.

David stood beside me, pale around the mouth.

"This is Sarah," he said.

Mr. Levin looked at me long enough that I felt
my shoes, my rough fingernails, every wrong word I ever
spoke.

"How long are you in this country?" he asked.

"Three years."

"You work?"

"In shirt-waists."

"And your father?"

Papa answered proudly, "At present I sell notions, but in
Russia—"

"I asked the girl."

My face flamed.

"I give my money home," I said. "But when I'm
married, my brothers will soon work."

Mr. Levin turned to David. "I see."

What did he see? Love? Sacrifice? How Mamma's hands shook
from wanting to please him? No. He saw holes. He
saw mouths eating David's future.

After ten minutes he rose.

"Thank you. David, walk down with me."

David followed him.

I followed too, not from deciding. My feet carried me
into the dark passage. I stopped above the first landing
where they could not see me.

Mr. Levin's voice came up sharp.

"So this is the girl."

"I told you she was poor."

"Poor? She has a regiment on her back. Father, mother,
brothers—soon cousins, uncles, the whole East Side. You'll open an
office or a charity bureau?"

"I love her."

"Love! On what will you pay rent? Love will buy
instruments? Love will furnish a reception room?"

"I can start modestly."

"With whose money?"

Silence.

Mr. Levin spoke softer, and therefore worse.

"I offered you five thousand dollars, the lease on the
Madison Avenue office, introductions to my people. I did not
educate you to throw yourself into a tenement."

"She's intelligent. She can learn."

"Must a doctor begin married to a lesson?"

Each word struck through my skin.

David said, "I promised her."

"You promised before you understood life. Break it."

"I can't."

"Then do without me. Not one dollar. You can hang
your sign in a cellar and treat pushcart peddlers for
twenty-five cents. And when her father cannot sell enough buttons,
you'll feed him too."

Papa moved behind me. I had not known he was
there. In the darkness his breathing sounded old, terribly old.

Below, the outer door opened and shut.

David came up alone. He found us on the stairs.

"Sara—"

I pushed past him into the room.

Everybody looked at us.

"What did he say?" Mamma asked.

"He liked the cake," I said.

Then I laughed. I laughed so hard I had to
hold the table. Mamma stared. Papa lowered his head. David
reached for me, but I pulled away.

That night he swore again.

"I won't give you up."

"You heard him. No money."

"I can borrow."

"You'll marry me?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"After graduation."

"Swear."

"I swear."

Yet from then something began slipping out from my hands.

He came less often. Examinations, he said. Hospital work. Meetings
with doctors. When he sat with me, his body was
there but his mind calculated someplace else. I spoke and
watched him not hear.

One Sunday I saw him looking around our room as
his uncle had looked. Only for a second. But that
second killed sleep in me.

"You hate it here," I said.

"What nonsense."

"You smell the cabbage. You see the cracks."

"Sara, every conversation cannot be a tragedy."

"To you it's a conversation. To me it's my life."

He kissed me, impatient like paying a debt.

Graduation came. I waited for him outside the hall with
flowers bought from my week's wages. There were ladies in
pale dresses and men with silk hats. I stood behind
them, afraid he should be ashamed if they saw me
first.

When he came out, his uncle was on one side
and a young lady in white on the other. David
saw me. His face changed. He came over, but he
did not bring me to them.

"You shouldn't have waited in this crowd," he said.

"I wanted to see you a doctor."

"Thank you for the flowers."

"When will you come?"

"To-night if I can."

He did not come.

The next day he sent no word.

On the third day I went to his lodging. The
landlady said he had moved.

"Where?"

"Uptown. He opened his office."

My heart stopped, but the street went on. Wagons passed.
Boys yelled. A woman bargained over onions. How could the
world move when mine had fallen dead?

I found the address in the directory.

DAVID LEVIN, PHYSICIAN AND SURGEON.

The letters stood straight and black. His dream had come
true. The brass plate, the office, the uptown street.

Only Mrs. Doctor Levin had been rubbed out.

I walked there after dark. The building had marble steps
and electric lamps. Through the front window I saw a
green shade, a mahogany desk, white curtains. His uncle's five
thousand dollars were shining in every corner.

I did not go in.

I stood across the street till the lamp went out.

Then began the night of waiting.

Perhaps he would come home and explain. Perhaps he had
moved first and planned to fetch me after. I ran
back to our rooms.

At ten I sat by the window.

At eleven every footstep was his.

It's twelve o'clock. The elevated trains thunder and fade. Papa
tells me to sleep.

At one I still sit dressed.

At two—Oi weh, two!—the street is empty except a drunken
man singing to himself.

At three the sky turns the colour of ashes.

"Come, my child," Mamma begs. "Lie down."

"He promised."

"A man can be delayed."

"He promised!"

Morning entered the room, gray and shameless. David did not
come.

For six days I went to work. I pushed cloth
under the needle, though I could not feel my fingers.
The girls spoke to me and their mouths moved without
sound. Every evening I hurried home. Maybe a letter. Maybe
he was waiting.

Nothing.

On the seventh day a note came.

He wrote that he had deceived himself. Marriage would ruin
both our lives. Our backgrounds were too different. He would
never forget my goodness. He enclosed money.

Money!

I tore it in pieces so small they floated around
the room like dirty snow.

Then I wrote him. I do not remember all the
words. I wrote from blood.

Come once. Only once. Tell me with your living mouth
that you never loved me. Don't bury me by a
letter. I will not hold you. I ask only to
see your face.

He came the next night.

When the knock sounded, all my strength ran out. I
sat by the table and could not rise.

David entered. His face was wet from rain. He looked
ill, older already.

"Sara."

I had prepared words. Accusations. Questions. Curses. But my tongue
lay dead.

He knelt beside me.

"Forgive me."

I looked on his hands. Those hands had held mine
by the river. Those hands had pointed to words in
books, had promised me a life. Now they trembled on
my skirt.

"I'm a coward," he said. "I thought I could fight
him. I thought love was enough."

Still I could not speak.

"If I marry you without money, I'll begin hating the
burdens. If I marry for advancement, I hate myself. I
don't know what I am anymore."

I wanted to touch his hair. My hand even moved
a little. But between my heart and my fingers was
a grave.

"Say something," he begged. "Call me a murderer. Strike me.
Only don't look like that."

Papa came from the bedroom.

"Get up," he said.

David turned. "Mr. Rabinowitz, let me talk to her."

"You talked enough."

"I came because she asked."

"She asked with the piece of heart you left alive.
Now go before you finish it."

"Papa," I whispered, but no sound came out.

David stood. For one second his eyes held mine. I
saw love there—yes, love, and fear stronger than love. That
was the worst. If he had never loved, maybe I
could have buried him. But he loved and still went.

"Sara," he said.

Papa opened the door.

David went out.

I listened to his steps down the stairs. One flight.
Two. Three. The outer door closed.

Then the earthquake came.

I fell from the chair. Mamma screamed. Papa lifted me,
but I fought him because I had to run after
David. My body knew what my pride did not. It
dragged toward the door.

"Let me go! He's leaving! David!"

They held me.

"David! David! Mein Gott, bring him back!"

But no God brought him.

For weeks I was sick. Not with a sickness a
doctor could name. I breathed, but no air came into
me. I ate, but everything tasted of dust. Sometimes I
woke thinking his hand was under my cheek, and for
one blessed second the world had light. Then the room
showed itself—the cracked wall, Mamma asleep in a chair, dawn
at the window—and I died fresh again.

Afterward I went back to the factory.

What else does a poor girl do with a broken
soul? She must still finish twelve dozen waists before Saturday.

Years passed because years pass even over the dead.

I saw David once in a newspaper. Doctor Levin had
given a lecture. Later I heard he married the daughter
of a manufacturer. I tore the newspaper, then took it
from the stove before the flame caught. I smoothed his
picture and hid it. The next day I burned it.

Sam Kaplan began walking me home.

He was good. That was his whole trouble and his
whole blessing. He did not speak from high places. He
spoke of a grocery store, rent, savings, a flat with
a bathroom. He knew I had loved somebody.

"I don't ask what you can't give," he said. "I
got patience."

"Don't marry me, Sam."

"Why?"

"There is a room in me locked."

"A person don't need every room."

I married him.

I learned to care for him. Maybe love also, but
a quiet love, with slippers by the stove and medicine
when he coughs. We had three children. I washed their
little bodies; I sat through fevers; I saved for music
lessons. When my son called "Mamma!" my heart answered. I
was not stone.

Yet sometimes, while Sam slept beside me, I heard footsteps
that were not in the hall.

Sometimes in a crowd I saw the turn of a
head and ran after a stranger.

Sometimes my daughter mispronounced an English word, and I heard
David laughing: "Put your tongue between your teeth."

Then I would hold the child too tight until she
cried.

And now, at Bessie's wedding, after all the years, he
stands twenty feet from me.

His wife says something. He bends to hear her. There
is gentleness in the bending, and jealousy rises in me
so young and wild that I am ashamed. What right
have I? Sam is across the hall. My children are
almost grown. My hair too has gray.

I tell myself: You don't love him. You love nobody
but a ghost.

Then David turns.

He sees me.

The colour leaves his face.

For a moment the music, the talking, the clatter of
dishes—all breaks apart. We are again on the river street
with the whole sky above us. We are on the
dark stairs listening to his uncle count the cost of
my family. We are in my room where he kneels
and asks forgiveness too late.

He takes one step.

I clutch the table.

His wife follows his eyes and looks at me. She
is not proud-looking. She is only a woman, older and
tired around the mouth. Perhaps she too has waited nights.
Who knows the locked rooms in another person?

David does not come nearer.

Maybe he sees Sam now beside me. Sam has returned
and puts his hand under my elbow.

"You're pale, Sara. Sit down."

"I'm all right."

"You look like you saw a dead man."

"Yes," I say. "A dead man."

But David hears. I know he hears, though the orchestra
starts loud.

He bows his head.

Then his wife leads him toward their table, and he
goes with her.

All my practised speeches remain unsaid. I do not show
him my children's photographs. I do not tell him Sam
owns two stores now. I do not smile like an
American lady. What use are lace curtains, savings in the
bank, a fur collar, when one look can tear open
the poor girl still waiting by the factory door?

The bride begins to dance. Everybody claps. Sam brings me
wine and says it will put colour back in my
face.

I drink. I smile for him because he deserves a
wife who is present.

But inside me something stands by a dark river.

I do not want David. No. Let God hear me—I
do not want him. If he came now and held
out both hands, I would not leave Sam, my children,
my home. The girl who could have gone is buried
under years.

Yet once I breathed air from the high places. Once
the roofs fell below me, and light came even through
factory smoke. A person can live afterward. She can cook,
sew, bear children, laugh at supper, grow respectable and old.

But she cannot forget that air.

She cannot make herself believe the low room is the
whole world.

# THE MURDER BY INSTALLMENTS

Philo Gubb, paper-hanger and graduate of the Rising Sun Correspondence
School of Scientific Detection, was standing before the Medderbrook residence
with a telegram in one hand and a bill for
one dollar and eighty cents in the other.

The telegram was from Miss Syrilla Medderbrook, then visiting her
aunt in Keokuk, and it said:

PLEASE GIVE FATHER ENCLOSED MESSAGE AND ADVANCE WHATEVER HE REQUIRES
STOP YOUR LOVING SYRILLA

There was no enclosed message.

This did not seem peculiar to Philo. Almost everything Miss
Syrilla did seemed romantic to him, and romance, according to
Lesson Four of the Rising Sun course, was "a condition
in which the ordinary deductions are rendered misleading by the
emotions." Philo's emotions had been rendering his ordinary deductions misleading
for three years.

Mr. Medderbrook read the telegram twice.

"She says advance whatever I require," he said.

"So she does," admitted Philo warmly.

"I require five dollars."

Philo's warmth cooled a little. "The telegram cost me the
forty-seven cents already, not including messenger service."

"Then make it four dollars and fifty-three cents."

Philo paid. Mr. Medderbrook put the money in his pocket,
thanked him for delivering the telegram, and shut the door.

Philo went slowly down the path. He had reached the
gate when he remembered the absent enclosed message, but at
that instant he saw Uncle Gabriel Hostetter crossing the street
toward him, and the mystery of love gave way to
the mystery of crime.

Uncle Gabriel was not everybody's uncle, but Riverbank had called
him that so long that even his enemies had acquired
a nephew-like attitude toward him. He was a tall, narrow
man with a pointed white beard, octagonal spectacles, a faded
frock coat, and a silk hat which had outlived two
changes of fashion and one administration. It was said that
when he held a silver dollar too tightly the eagle
upon it opened its beak and cried for mercy. This
was an exaggeration. Uncle Gabriel never held a silver dollar
carelessly enough to make it cry aloud.

He seized Philo's arm.

"Gubb," he whispered, "I'm bein' murdered."

Philo glanced at the old man's erect figure.

"You are bearing up wonderful under the deceased condition," he
said.

"I ain't dead yet. It's a slow murder."

"That alters the legal complexion," said Philo. "Step into my
office."

His office was on the second floor over Kritz's cigar
store and was also his paper-hanging shop. On the door
appeared, in black letters of his own cutting:

PHILO GUBB
PAPER-HANGING IN ALL BRANCHES
DETECTION IN ALL CASES
TERMS REASONABLE WHEN POSSIBLE

Inside were a trestle table, three rolls of wallpaper, a
paste bucket, a chair with one sound leg, and the
framed diploma of the Rising Sun Correspondence School. Philo placed
Uncle Gabriel in the chair, whereupon the old man immediately
rose again.

"Settin' is wasteful when a man can stand for nothin',"
he explained.

Philo took a notebook from beneath a roll of red
flock paper.

"State the corpus of the crime."

"The what?"

"The crime's corpus. The facts, clues, motives, fingerprints, bloodstains, disguised
dukes, secret passages, or other usual circumstances."

"The circumstance is somebody keeps shootin' me."

"With what caliber of firearm?"

"A pea-shooter."

Philo lowered his pencil. His face became as solemn as
if the pea had already passed through a vital organ.

"A regular pea-shooter?"

"Looks regular."

"Shoots peas?"

"What else would it shoot?"

"Beans," said Philo, recalling Lesson Eleven, which warned the student
never to accept appearances. "Or small pills. Or buckshot reduced
by filing. The criminal mind is full of alternatives."

"Peas," said Uncle Gabriel. "Hard peas. Dried."

"Where do they impact upon your person?"

"Anywheres. Back of the neck mostly. Once in the ear.
Twice on the hat."

"And how often?"

"Every day. Sometimes five shots. Sometimes six. Yesterday eight."

Philo bent over the notebook and added the figures. "That
makes approximately an average murder of six peas daily."

"It makes a murder by inches," said Uncle Gabriel. "A
cannonball would kill me all at once, wouldn't it?"

"If aimed accurate."

"A rifle bullet might kill me in ten minutes?"

"Depending on whether the victim coöperated by bleeding."

"A little pistol bullet might take longer?"

"Quite reasonably."

"Well, then, a pea takes longer still. But shootin' is
shootin'. Size don't alter the moral fact of it. A
man killed with a cannonball is murdered quick; a man
killed with peas is murdered slow. I'm bein' murdered so
slow folks don't notice."

Philo considered this. It was not stated in any of
the twelve lessons that murder must be rapid. On the
contrary, Lesson Seven mentioned poison administered drop by drop.

"Peas are vegetable poison," he said at last. "Especially when
swallowed without cooking."

"I ain't swallowin' 'em. I'm bein' hit."

"That may postpone the fatalities, but it don't remove the
intent. Have you suspicioned anyone?"

Uncle Gabriel looked toward the door, the ceiling, and finally
into Philo's paste bucket.

"Farrington Pierce."

"Your son-in-law and cashier of the First Riverbank Bank?"

"The same. He married my daughter Mabel. He wants my
money."

"Most folks do," said Philo.

"But he has reasons. If I die, Mabel inherits my
property. If she inherits, he controls it."

"How much property?"

Uncle Gabriel frowned. "A detective don't need to know that."

"It bears on motive."

"It bears more on taxes. Put down 'ample.'"

Philo wrote "amphill fortune," which seemed to him the technical
spelling.

"Furthermore," Uncle Gabriel continued, "I own enough bank stock to
tell Farrington what he can and can't do. If I'm
gone, he might become president."

"And when do the projectiles assault you?"

"When I'm passin' the bank. Or near it. Or when
Farrington's anywheres around."

"Have you caught him with the weapon?"

"No. A murderer conceals his instruments. Any baby knows that."

Philo accepted the rebuke. It was true. Lesson Two said
substantially the same thing, although it used seven hundred more
words.

"I shall require to shadow him," Philo announced. "I shall
disguise myself, trail him, observe his habitudes, inspect his associates,
and make daily reports. My customary deteckative emolument is five
dollars per day, not including expenses."

Uncle Gabriel placed both hands behind him as if someone
had threatened to put money into them.

"Five dollars! I could get murdered outright for less."

"Scientific detection is skilled employment."

"I don't want skilled employment. I want you."

Philo was hurt, but only briefly, for Uncle Gabriel continued:

"You're a beginner. Beginners ought to pay for practice."

"Pay the client?"

"You paid a correspondence school, didn't you?"

"Yes."

"Well, I'm offerin' you actual crime. Crime is worth more
than correspondence. How much did your last lesson cost?"

"Two dollars and seventy-five cents, counting postage."

"I'll let you detect my murder for two dollars a
day."

"Paid by you?"

"Paid by you."

Philo stared.

Uncle Gabriel raised one finger. "And I'll furnish the victim
free."

This was a consideration. Authentic victims were not easily obtained
in Riverbank, where the population was healthy and the police
duties consisted chiefly of removing bicycles from the sidewalks. Philo
had studied jewel robberies, forged wills, international conspiracies, and murders
in locked rooms, but thus far his practical cases had
involved a lost dog, a missing clothesline, and the question
of who had put stove blacking on the Methodist doorbell.

"One dollar," Philo offered.

"Two."

"One and a quarter."

"Dollar ninety."

"One and a half, with no payment Sundays."

"You may have Sundays for a dollar."

"One sixty on weekdays and eighty cents Sundays."

"Dollar seventy-five straight through."

"Dollar sixty-two and a half, and I provide my own
disguises."

"Done," said Uncle Gabriel, before Philo could recall that half-cents
were no longer convenient money.

He held out his hand. Philo shook it.

"Payment in advance," said Uncle Gabriel.

Philo paid him for one day, receiving no receipt because,
as Gabriel explained, receipts wore out pencils.

The next morning Philo began operations. His first disguise was
that of an unemployed wallpaper hanger. Since he was an
employed wallpaper hanger, he accomplished this by leaving his paste
brush at home. He took a position across from the
First Riverbank Bank and leaned against the front of Kritz's
cigar store.

Farrington Pierce sat behind the bank's brass wicket. He was
a handsome, well-brushed man with a smooth brown mustache and
the steady expression of one who could subtract seven cents
from a dollar while a customer watched him. At ten
o'clock he left the bank and crossed to the post
office. Philo followed at a distance of thirty feet, as
prescribed by Lesson Five.

When Farrington stopped at the curb, Philo stopped. When Farrington
looked back, Philo examined a telegraph pole. When Farrington entered
the post office, Philo entered immediately behind him and pretended
to study a framed notice concerning fraudulent use of the
mails.

Farrington bought two stamps.

Philo bought two stamps.

Farrington turned.

"Morning, Gubb."

"Morning," said Philo, with the cold reserve of an unknown
man in disguise.

"Following me?"

"No," said Philo. "I am merely proceeding coincidental in the
same consecutive directions."

Farrington smiled and returned to the bank.

Philo resumed his station by the cigar store. Uncle Ash
Pegg and Henry Wallace were sitting in tilted chairs nearby,
performing that arduous public service known as watching other men
work. Uncle Ash had been idle since 1886 and disapproved
of Henry because Henry wasted too much time loafing.

"Gabriel hire you to watch Pierce?" Uncle Ash asked.

Philo's hand went instinctively to his false mustache. He had
forgotten to put it on.

"A deteckative does not reveal his client's confidence," he said.

"He ain't got any confidence," said Henry. "He's got stinginess.
Different thing."

Philo moved a little farther along the wall.

"He'll make Gubb pay him," Uncle Ash said.

"He already has," said Henry.

Philo turned sharply. "How did you obtain information of the
transaction?"

"Gabriel told it in the bank," Henry replied. "Said he'd
found the cheapest shadow in Iowa, because the shadow paid
him."

Uncle Ash laughed until his chair came down on all
four legs. This startled him, and he tilted it again.

Philo attempted not to hear. He opened his notebook and
wrote:

10:14 A.M.—Suspect returned from P.O. carrying no visible weapon except
postage stamps. Demeanor smooth. May suspect surveillance, as criminals invariably
do after being told.

Inside the bank Farrington appeared to be attending to the
ledger. At eleven he spoke to a woman at the
wicket. She wore a gray veil and remained only three
minutes.

"There she is," said Henry.

"Who?" asked Uncle Ash.

"Widow Bassett."

"The one Gabriel's after?"

"The same."

Philo's pencil stopped.

Uncle Ash spat thoughtfully into the street. "Seems a roundabout
way to get evidence."

"Gabriel don't care how roundabout it is if somebody else
walks the distance."

"What evidence?" Philo asked, despite himself.

Henry looked innocent. "Why, I wouldn't reveal a client's confidence."

"I ain't asking professional. I'm asking personally."

"That makes it all right," Henry said. "Gabriel thinks Pierce
is sweet on Widow Bassett. Wants him caught at it
so Mabel can divorce him."

Philo emerged from the wall against which he had been
leaning.

"What about the pea-shooting murder?"

Both idlers looked at him.

"What pea-shooting murder?" asked Uncle Ash.

"The one Uncle Gabriel is being killed by, gradual."

Henry slapped his knee. "That old fox! He told Mabel
he'd get proof without it costing a cent. He must've
invented a murder to set you watchin' Farrington."

Philo's ears reddened.

Uncle Ash nodded approvingly. "Good scheme. Gubb thinks he's huntin'
a killer, and meanwhile he reports every female Pierce talks
to."

"Widow Bassett came to cash a check," Henry said. "But
Gabriel'll turn it into a moonlight elopement if he can
save lawyer's fees."

Philo closed the notebook. A Scientific Detective, his course said,
must remain calm when confronted by Treachery, Dynamite, or Foreign
Agents. It did not mention the proper calmness when confronted
by one's own dollar and sixty-two and a half cents.

He crossed to the bank with long, paper-hanger strides. Farrington
Pierce looked up.

"Want to deposit something, Gubb?"

"I wish to deposit a warning," Philo said. "Your father-in-marriage
has retained me to detect you under fraudulent misrepresentations."

Farrington's face hardened. "What does that mean?"

"It means he states you are murdering him with peas,
whereas the actual ulterior intention is divorce evidence concerning Widow
Bassett."

For a moment Farrington said nothing. Then he put down
his pen very carefully.

"He said that?"

"He stated the murder. The cigar-store witnesses conveyed the adultery."

Farrington looked toward the window. Widow Bassett, having completed her
errand, was crossing the street with a parcel of lamp
wicks.

"There is no adultery," he said.

"I have not detected any. I have detected two postage
stamps, but their romantic bearing is limited."

"Gabriel has been hounding Mabel against me for months," Farrington
said. "He thinks because he owns half the bank he
owns everybody in it."

"The amount was described as amphill."

"What?"

"Nothing. A professional memorandum."

Farrington came around the wicket. "Did he really say I
was trying to kill him?"

"With a pea-shooter. Six shots average, eight yesterday. He reasoned
that a small ammunition commits a slower murder than a
large ammunition."

Farrington laughed, but the laugh was not amused. "I'll give
him a pea-shooter."

Philo raised one hand. "Threats made before a licensed deteckative
can be used afterward in the usual manner."

"I'm going to talk to him."

"Talk is not evidence of homicide unless sharpened at one
end," said Philo. "Nevertheless, I advise moderation."

He left the bank and went directly to Uncle Gabriel's
house. The old man was in his yard, counting fallen
apples to see whether the tree had produced more than
he had estimated. Philo opened the gate.

"You deceived me," he said.

"About what?"

"The slow murder was camouflage. Your actual purpose was to
have me watch Farrington with reference to Widow Bassett and
matrimonial divorce."

Uncle Gabriel picked up an apple, examined the bruise, and
put it into the least damaged pile.

"Who told you?"

"Public sources."

"Cigar-store loafers?"

"Sources."

"They talk too much," said Gabriel. "A man with that
much spare time ought to use some of it mindin'
his business."

"The agreement is canceled."

"Can't cancel it. You paid for today."

"I don't want today."

"You bought it."

"Then return the unused portion."

"A day ain't divisible."

"It was divisible when you wanted Sunday at eighty cents."

"That was before contract."

Philo drew himself upright. "I am through shadowing Mr. Pierce.
I shall not report his widows, postages, or other private
movements. Furthermore, I request my dollar sixty-two and one-half cents."

"I ain't got change."

"Give me a dollar sixty-two."

"That cheats you."

"Give me a dollar sixty-three."

"That cheats me."

Uncle Gabriel turned toward the house.

"What about my murder?" he demanded over his shoulder.

"There ain't any murder."

"Ain't there?" Gabriel pointed to his silk hat. A small
round dent showed in its crown. At his feet lay
a dried pea.

"Evidence planted by the client," said Philo. "Lesson Eight warns
particularly against it."

At that instant something clicked from behind the lilac bushes,
and a pea struck Uncle Gabriel smartly on the cheek.

"There!" cried Gabriel. "Did you see that?"

"I heard it."

Another pea sailed out and hit the gatepost. Philo dashed
behind the bushes and found Eddie Boles, aged twelve, crouched
with a tin pea-shooter.

"Who instructed you?" Philo demanded.

"Nobody."

"Who paid you?"

"Nobody."

"Why do you shoot Mr. Hostetter?"

Eddie considered. "Because he hollers."

Uncle Gabriel came around the bush and seized the weapon.

"You young murderer!"

Eddie escaped through a gap in the hedge.

Philo folded his arms. "The criminal is discovered. Motive, hilarity.
Murder, nonexistent."

"He's Farrington's errand boy Saturdays," Gabriel argued.

"He is also twelve."

"A twelve-year-old can be corrupted."

"So can a seventy-year-old," said Philo. "Our commercial relations are
terminated."

He returned to his shop, put on his apron, and
began hanging a pattern of purple roses in Mrs. Clegg's
front parlor. Paper-hanging soothed him because walls, unlike clients, generally
remained where they were put. By noon he had matched
three seams and restored his confidence. By two he had
pasted over a door. By three he had found the
door again.

At half past three, while Philo stood on a plank
smoothing a particularly large rose, Mrs. Clegg's nephew burst into
the room.

"Mr. Gubb! There's been a shooting!"

Philo descended so quickly that one foot entered the paste
pail.

"Who has shot which?"

"Farrington Pierce shot Uncle Gabriel!"

Philo stared at him.

"With what?"

"A little pistol. Tiny thing. Doc Haskins says it ain't
much bigger than a pea-shooter."

Philo removed his foot from the paste and, without taking
time to scrape it, hurried into the street. A crowd
had formed before the bank. Riverbank citizens who had not
entered the bank in six months now found urgent financial
business there.

Uncle Gabriel lay in the directors' room upon the long
table, his frock coat open and his pointed beard aimed
toward the ceiling. Doctor Haskins stood beside him. Farrington Pierce
sat in a chair with his head in his hands.
Marshal Tott had taken possession of a small pearl-handled pistol
and was displaying it with grave importance.

"Dead?" Philo asked.

"Dead," said the doctor.

"Instantaneous?"

"Practically."

Philo shook his head. "That is where the medical profession
fails through insufficient deduction."

Doctor Haskins looked at him. "A bullet went through his
heart."

"The final bullet," Philo said. "But not the beginning bullet."

Marshal Tott frowned. "What are you talking about?"

Philo stepped to the table. It was his moment. Lesson
Twelve, "The Detective Explains," had instructed him to face the
assembled persons, pause until silence was absolute, and reveal the
solution in an orderly manner. Silence was not absolute, because
Uncle Ash Pegg was whispering that Gabriel's shoes looked almost
new, but it was the best silence Riverbank could provide.

"This," Philo began, "is not a sudden murder. It is
a slow murder."

Henry Wallace snorted.

Philo continued. "For days the deceased was shot by peas.
Six per day average, sometimes eight. Each pea was too
small to kill immediate. To-day the assailant increased the caliber.
Therefore death appears sudden only to persons entering the case
late."

"You found Eddie Boles with the pea-shooter," said Uncle Ash.

"He may have been an accomplice unconscious of the main
conspiracies."

"Pierce didn't hire him," said Marshal Tott. "Eddie admits he
did it for fun."

"Murderers frequently use fun to cover motive."

Doctor Haskins tapped the pearl-handled pistol. "Pierce and Gabriel quarreled.
Gabriel accused him of carrying on with Widow Bassett. Pierce
lost his temper, pulled this gun from the drawer, and
fired. That's the whole thing."

"Exactly," Philo said triumphantly. "The whole thing is composed of
parts. The first parts were peas. The last part was
pistol. A cannonball would have done the work in one
payment. A rifle bullet might have done it in ten
minutes. This tiny pistol took several days, assisted by vegetable
ammunition. The deceased was murdered by installments."

"That don't make sense," Henry said.

"It makes consecutive sense."

Farrington raised his head. His face was gray. "I never
shot peas at him."

"You shot the concluding shot," Philo said. "A man who
puts the period to a sentence cannot claim there was
no sentence previous."

Marshal Tott opened his mouth, thought better of it, and
closed it again.

Uncle Ash said, "But this morning you called Gabriel a
liar and quit the case."

Philo's expression remained professionally severe.

"A detective must sometimes quit a case in order to
bring the criminal into overconfidence."

"You went back to paper-hanging."

"A disguise."

"You had paste on both knees."

"A thorough disguise."

Henry Wallace chuckled. "Then you knew Gabriel was really being
murdered?"

Philo looked at Uncle Gabriel, whose dead face seemed, by
reason of the pointed beard and octagonal spectacles, to retain
a certain bargaining sharpness. It occurred to Philo that the
old man had cheated him into a case, cheated him
out of a day's fee, and then, with a thrift
beyond criticism, contrived to be actually murdered before the day
expired.

"I knew," Philo said slowly, "that shootin' is shootin'."

"And peas is peas," Henry added.

"And a fool is a fool," said Uncle Ash.

Philo wished to reply that a Scientific Detective is judged
by results, not by cigar-store opinion. He wished to say
that he alone had been informed in advance of the
murder, that the victim had correctly named the murderer, and
that the weapon had, in the end, proved little more
than a glorified pea-shooter. He wished, above all, to explain
why a detective who had abandoned a false case at
noon should receive credit for a true murder at half
past three.

But Lesson Twelve contained no suitable retort.

So Philo Gubb drew himself up, wiped the wallpaper paste
from his shoe upon the bank's brass threshold, and regarded
the company with the stern, mysterious gaze of a man
whose deductions were too profound to be expressed in ordinary
English.

Then he remembered the dollar and sixty-two and one-half cents.

He looked sadly at Uncle Gabriel.

"Furthermore," he said, "the deceased died owing me a refund."

And Riverbank, which could forgive murder sooner than it could
overlook an unpaid balance, agreed that in this respect, at
least, Philo had solved the case.

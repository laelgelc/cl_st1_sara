On the morning of his last day in England, Reginald
took one look at himself in the glass and decided
that his hair was green.

Not entirely green, of course. Not the green of billiard
cloth or parsley sauce, but greenish where the light from
the wet garden struck it above the temples. It was
absurd. A man could not go and ask a girl
to marry him with green hair. He leaned closer, gripping
the washstand.

There! No—brown again. Rather a decent brown, perhaps, if it
were not so flat. He pushed it up with both
hands and instantly looked like a startled cockatoo. He smoothed
it. Worse. He stepped back.

The new tie was a mistake too. The fellow in
Bond Street had called it distinguished, but its checks—cream, black
and an angry little line of orange—gave Reggie the appearance
of a cat in one of those nursery pictures, a
cat dressed for the Lord Mayor's Show. Anne would see
it at once. She saw everything. She would look at
the tie, then at him, and that helpless silver laughter
would begin.

Dash it all, why should a tie matter?

Because everything mattered to-day.

Here he was! His last day. At six the following
morning he must take the train for Southampton, and after
that came the long voyage, the hot little station, the
dusty road and the fruit farm in Rhodesia, with its
twelve acres of young orange trees, its unreliable pump and
its mortgage. A modest property, people called it. Modest! It
was practically blushing itself out of existence.

He turned sideways before the glass and drew in his
stomach.

Suppose Anne accepted him. Suppose, in spite of the farm,
in spite of his income being what the governor used
to describe as "prospective rather than actual," in spite of
the distance and the heat and the insects, in spite
of his mother, in spite of everything, she said yes.
Then he would take her away. He saw them standing
on the deck at night, Anne wrapped in a white
cloak, the sea immensely black around them. A steward would
come running up to say there was danger—fire, perhaps, or
a man overboard—and Reggie, very quiet, very capable, would put
Anne behind him.

"Stay there," he would say. "Leave this to me."

It was difficult to imagine what came next, but the
effect was top-hole.

He tried the serious expression required by this scene. Unfortunately,
it made his upper lip look as though it had
been caught in a drawer.

"Reggie!"

His mother's voice came from the landing.

He dropped the expression.

"Yes, mater?"

"Are you dressed?"

"Practically."

"Then come to my room before you go down."

That was the first obstacle.

Mrs. Fairburn sat in bed beneath a lilac satin quilt,
with two Pekinese dogs beside her and the morning post
spread about like fallen leaves. Widowhood had left her in
possession of a large house, a small fortune and an
inexhaustible sense of injury. She had been injured by the
climate, by servants, by Reggie's decision to farm abroad, by
the price of asparagus, and most especially by any person
who presumed to want him elsewhere.

The Pekinese dogs stared at him as he entered. Their
round black eyes appeared to say that they knew precisely
where he was going and thought poorly of it.

"My dear boy!" His mother raised both hands. "What have
you done to your hair?"

So it was green.

"Nothing, mater."

"You look feverish. Come here."

"I'm all right."

"Nonsense. Your left ear is crimson."

Reggie put a hand to it. The dogs continued to
judge him.

Mrs. Fairburn had been cutting photographs from an illustrated paper
with a pair of long gardening scissors. Why gardening scissors
should be in bed with her he could not imagine,
but they looked fearfully sharp. Snip! went the blades through
the portrait of a duchess.

"You are not going to the Harcourts', I hope."

"Well—I thought I might stroll over."

"On your last day?"

"I said good-bye to everybody else yesterday."

"Everybody else!" Snip. "I see. Your poor mother is everybody
else."

"No, no, mater. Of course not."

"I had planned that we should spend the afternoon together.
Quite quietly. You might read to me."

The dogs brightened. No doubt they would like that—four hours
of Reggie reading parliamentary speeches while they breathed on his
trousers.

"I shan't be long."

"You always say that."

"I must say good-bye to Anne."

At the name, Mrs. Fairburn looked down and severed a
cabinet minister at the waist.

"I cannot understand what you find so attractive in that
family."

"They've always been very decent to me."

"Decent! People are decent to the postman."

Snip.

For one dreadful moment Reggie saw himself detained until luncheon,
then until tea, then until the cab arrived to take
him to the station in the grey dawn. He would
write to Anne from Southampton. No, from the ship. "Dear
Anne, I had meant to ask you something, but mater
wanted me to read aloud—"

Good heavens!

He bent and kissed his mother on the forehead.

"Sorry, mater. Absolutely must go."

"Reggie!"

He was at the door.

"Reggie, I forbid you to—"

But he had escaped. Down the stairs, across the hall,
past the maid who held out his gloves, and into
the morning. Behind him the bell rang with such violence
that it seemed the whole house had screamed.

The rain had stopped. The road shone, pale and glassy,
beneath a rinsed blue sky. Drops fell from the elms
with little separate taps, and the hedges breathed out the
sweet, dark smell of briar and wet earth. Reggie walked
quickly at first, almost laughing. Free! He had done it.
The house receded behind him; the dogs, the scissors, the
lilac quilt—all of it sank away.

Then the road curved, and beyond the wet fields he
saw the Harcourts' chimneys.

His pace slackened.

He loved Anne. That was the plain fact. He loved
her, he loved her. Not because she was beautiful—though she
was; not because she had money—though it would be idiotic
to pretend that money did not make Rhodesia look less
alarming; not because she listened to him and knew when
he was trying to be amusing. He loved the way
she sat on the edge of a table, swinging one
foot. He loved the little crease beside her mouth when
she was puzzled. He loved even her laughter, though lately
it had begun to make him feel as if he
were running downstairs in the dark and had missed the
last step.

She liked him. Surely she did. She told him things
she told nobody else: that she hated boiled milk; that
her aunt's voice made her dream of teaspoons; that once,
at twelve, she had stolen a peach and buried the
stone beneath her pillow from remorse. She had said he
was her dearest friend.

But a girl could have a dearest friend and marry
somebody else.

The somebody else appeared at once in Reggie's mind: a
tall, bronzed chap with clean-cut features and a quiet manner,
a chap who entered drawing rooms without knocking over footstools.
He had probably been in the cavalry. He would look
at Anne as if he understood women, and his ties
would never resemble cats.

"Rot," Reggie muttered, striking a puddle with his stick.

A shower of diamonds flew into the grass. Beyond the
hedge, hollyhocks glowed pink and wine-red against a cottage wall.
Everything looked absurdly hopeful after the rain. The leaves flashed,
the clouds sailed, a thrush shook itself and began again.
It seemed impossible that on such a morning Anne could
say no.

And yet she might.

At the Harcourts' gate he stopped to adjust the tie
in the tiny oval mirror of his cigarette case. Still
frightful. He loosened it, tightened it, turned the knot slightly
to the left and succeeded only in making the orange
line more prominent.

Let it be prominent. Let it blaze. He was not
proposing with his tie.

The front door stood open. Somewhere in the house a
piano was being played, very softly and badly. A maid
told him Miss Anne was in the garden.

"Shall I announce you, sir?"

"No, thanks. I know the way."

He crossed the cool hall. A blue parasol lay on
the piano, and beside it stood a vase of cream
roses shedding petals on the polished wood. Through the open
drawing-room windows came the sound of snipping.

Snipping!

For an instant he thought his mother had followed him
with the gardening scissors. Then he saw Anne beyond the
terrace, cutting sweet peas.

She wore a pale blue frock and a broad hat
pushed back from her forehead. The wet lawn shone about
her shoes. At the end of the path, two white
doves strutted near a stone basin, their breasts changing from
pearl to pink as they moved.

Anne looked up.

"Reggie!"

There it was—that brightness, that sudden pleased look. His heart
gave one enormous thump.

"I thought you'd come."

"Did you?"

"Of course. You couldn't go without saying good-bye."

"No. Rather not."

He came down the steps. Anne held out her hand,
but it was full of sweet peas; he took two
of her fingers, absurdly careful not to crush them. They
were cool from the stems.

"Your tie!" she said.

He knew it.

"What about it?"

"Nothing." Her lips trembled. "It's splendid."

"Chap in Bond Street said so."

"I'm sure he did."

She turned quickly to the flowers, but a little sound
escaped her. Then another. Her shoulders shook.

"Oh, Reggie, forgive me!"

"It's all right."

"It isn't the tie. It's—oh, I don't know what it
is."

She laughed out, light and clear. The doves lifted their
heads. Reggie smiled because there seemed nothing else to do,
though something inside him became small and hard.

Presently Anne recovered herself and touched his sleeve.

"You don't mind, do you?"

"Good Lord, no. Rather bucked you like it."

"You're so sweet."

Sweet. A man rescuing women from burning ships was never
called sweet.

They walked to a wicker table beneath the cedar. On
it stood a green glass ashtray, a bowl of strawberries
and a jug beaded with water. Anne dropped the sweet
peas beside the bowl and sat down.

"Cigarette?"

"Thanks."

He struck a match. Her hand curved about the flame,
and the pearl in her ring caught it, turning for
a second into a tiny moon. He could smell crushed
grass, tobacco and the warm pollen of the flowers. It
was all so vivid that he felt he would remember
it in Rhodesia when everything around him was red dust
and heat: Anne's bent head, the green ashtray, a strawberry
lying on its side with one white spot where it
had touched the bowl.

"Well," she said, "this time to-morrow you'll be on the
sea."

"Not quite. Train first."

"You know what I mean."

"Yes."

"Are you glad?"

He crushed the match into the ashtray.

"Fearfully."

"You don't sound glad."

"Oh, I am. Farm and all that. Oranges coming on.
Pump fixed, perhaps. Lots to do."

"And no one to make you miserable."

"Who makes me miserable?"

"I do, sometimes."

"You don't."

"I do. I can always tell."

She looked at him steadily. Her eyes were gold-brown, darker
near the centre. Reggie's rehearsed words—he had possessed several excellent
openings on the road—vanished completely.

He had meant to begin calmly. "Anne, there's something I've
been wanting to say." Or perhaps: "We've known each other
a long time." Instead, he heard himself ask:

"Would you hate living in Rhodesia?"

Anne's fingers paused above the strawberries.

"I don't know. I've never thought."

"It's not half bad. Not all snakes and savages, you
know. There's a club. Tennis. People dine out."

"I'm sure they do."

"And the house could be improved. Verandah wanted widening, and
there's a room at the back that could be made
into—well, whatever you liked."

Her face changed. Only a little; but he saw that
she understood.

The garden seemed suddenly enormous. Beyond Anne, every leaf of
the cedar stood separate against the wet blue sky. A
drop slid from the stone basin. One of the doves
pecked twice at the gravel.

Reggie pushed his chair back and stood.

"Anne—I say—"

She looked up at him.

He ought to take her hand. Chaps did. But her
hands lay folded in her lap, so still and complete
that it seemed brutal to disturb them.

"I haven't got much," he said. "You know that. Farm's
sound, though. At least, it will be. And I'm not
much of a chap, either. Not the sort you'd naturally—what
I mean is, you could marry anybody. Some top-hole fellow.
But I—"

He stopped and swallowed.

Anne's lips parted.

"I love you," he said. "That's the whole thing. I
love you most frightfully. Will you marry me?"

Silence.

In that silence the garden opened to the light. The
hollyhocks beyond the wall burned like small flags; the wet
gravel glittered; a pale petal loosened from Anne's flowers and
fell upon the table. Even the doves were still.

Then Anne looked down.

"Oh, Reggie."

The light closed.

He sat again because his knees had become unreliable.

"That means no, I suppose."

"I'm so sorry."

"No need to be. Perfectly all right."

"But it isn't all right."

"Yes, it is. Shouldn't have sprung it on you."

"You didn't spring it. I've known."

"Have you?"

"Not exactly known. Felt."

He stared at the ashtray. His cigarette had gone out.
He picked it up, put it down, and brushed a
thread of tobacco from his cuff.

"I like you more than anyone in the world," Anne
said.

"That's very decent."

"I do. There's nobody I can talk to as I
talk to you. Nobody who understands when I'm hateful. Nobody
who—"

"Then why not?" He looked up. "Sorry. That sounds dashed
rude. But why not?"

Anne turned towards the doves.

Mrs. Dove—the smaller bird, with a faint grey mark upon
her wing—was walking briskly along the border. Mr. Dove followed.
Every few steps he lowered his head and spread his
tail in a white fan.

"Look at them," Anne said.

Reggie looked.

"Rather nice."

"Do you know what they're doing?"

"Walking, aren't they?"

"Watch."

Mrs. Dove hurried on. Mr. Dove overtook her, bowed deeply
and made a bubbling sound in his throat.

"Roo-coo-coo-coo."

Mrs. Dove darted away.

Anne began to laugh.

It was not loud this time. It ran through her
in small helpless ripples while Mr. Dove followed, bowing and
bowing, his pink feet absurdly earnest upon the wet path.

"Roo-coo-coo-coo."

"I don't quite see—" Reggie began.

"That's us."

"Us?"

"Yes. She runs away laughing, and he comes after her
and bows."

"I don't bow."

"Not really. But you know what I mean."

He did not, except that the comparison was somehow intolerable.

"And you're always making me laugh," she continued. "Even now—oh,
don't look like that! I don't mean to. I can't
help it. You're so serious, and then something happens—your tie,
or the way you say a thing—and I laugh. I'd
laugh all my life."

"Cheery sort of marriage."

"No, it wouldn't be fair."

"To whom?"

"To you. To either of us. A wife shouldn't laugh
at her husband."

"Might do worse."

"She ought to look up to him."

"And you don't look up to me."

Anne pulled a petal from a sweet pea.

"You don't want me to pretend."

"No."

"I love being with you. I miss you when you're
away. I can tell you everything. But marriage is different.
A husband should be—"

"The cavalry fellow."

"What cavalry fellow?"

"Nothing. Go on."

"He should be someone one depends upon."

"I can be depended upon."

"I know."

"Then that's something."

"Oh, Reggie, you make it impossible."

She laughed again, but now the sound wounded her too;
he could see it in the way she pressed her
lips together immediately afterwards.

He rose.

"Right. Well. Thanks for letting me down lightly."

"Don't say that."

"Why not? It's what happened."

"You're angry."

"Not a bit."

"You are."

"I'm not. I've no right to be."

He found his hat on the grass and dusted it,
though there was no dust. His hands looked detached from
him, pale and efficient, the hands of a servant packing
somebody else's things.

"Reggie, sit down."

"Better be cutting off."

"Not yet."

"Mater expects me."

This was so monstrous a lie that even in his
misery he almost admired it.

Anne stood too. "You can't go like this."

"Like what?"

"So unhappy."

"I'm not unhappy."

"You look dreadful."

"Thanks."

"I didn't mean—oh, please!"

He waved his hand as if unhappiness were smoke that
could be cleared from between them.

"Forget it. Absolutely forget the whole thing."

"How can I?"

"Easy. Chap proposes, girl refuses. Happens every day."

"But not to us."

"Apparently it does."

He walked towards the terrace.

"Reggie!"

He stopped.

Anne had followed him a few steps. Her face was
pale beneath the hat, and the childish certainty had gone
from it.

"What?"

"You mustn't think I don't care."

"I don't."

"You do. You think I'm cruel."

"Never thought that."

"I couldn't bear you to think it."

"I don't, Anne. Honestly."

"Then smile."

He smiled.

The effort drew his face into such a ghastly shape
that she gave a tiny cry.

"Not like that!"

"Best I can manage at short notice."

She held out both hands. "Come back and sit down.
We'll talk sensibly."

"There's nothing to talk about."

"We can talk about the farm."

"No."

"About your journey, then."

"No."

"About anything."

He shook his head.

"But yesterday we were happy," she said. "We were perfectly
happy."

"Yesterday I thought you might marry me."

"That shouldn't make all the difference."

"It does rather."

"Why?"

Good heavens, how could she ask why? Because yesterday the
world had contained a door and to-day she had shut
it; because the garden, the house, the very road home
had become things he must leave not for months but
for ever; because he could not be her dearest friend
while she waited for the tall chap with the flawless
tie.

He said only, "That's how it is."

Anne's eyes filled.

"You must be just as happy as you were before."

"Can't arrange it."

"You must try."

"Right. I'll try in the cab."

"Reggie!"

Again he started away. Again her voice pulled him back.

"You'll write to me?"

"If you like."

"Not if I like. You must."

"Very well."

"Often?"

"Now and then."

"As often as before."

"No."

The word surprised them both.

Anne stood quite still. A drop from the cedar struck
the table with a soft ping. Behind her, Mr. Dove
resumed his pursuit.

"Roo-coo-coo-coo."

Mrs. Dove ran ahead, quick and light, while he followed
with his breast puffed out, bowing until his beak nearly
touched the path.

Reggie looked at them and then at Anne.

"I'd better go."

This time she did not stop him.

He crossed the lawn. The wet grass brushed his shoes;
the bright garden blurred at the edges, though he kept
his eyes fiercely open. At the terrace he paused, hoping—idiotically,
shamefully—that she would call. There was only the faint snip
of a gardener's shears beyond the wall.

He went through the drawing room. The blue parasol still
lay upon the piano. In the hall, the maid appeared
with his gloves.

"Good morning, sir."

"Morning."

Outside, the road shone exactly as before. That was the
worst of roads and skies: nothing happened to them. A
chap might have his entire future knocked flat, and the
clouds went sailing on, the puddles glittered, the hedges smelt
of briar. In a few minutes he would pass the
hollyhocks. Then the bend. Then his mother's house, where the
bell would be rung and the dogs would stare and
he would say he had had a pleasant visit.

He reached the gate.

"Reggie!"

His hand closed upon the latch.

No. He would not turn. She wanted him smiling, writing,
talking nonsense, trotting after her while she laughed. She wanted
yesterday without to-morrow. It wasn't fair. For once Mr. Dove
would keep walking.

"Reggie, please!"

He shut his eyes.

Then, softer, trembling and amused and unhappy all at once,
her voice came across the wet lawn:

"Come back, Mr. Dove."

He turned.

Anne stood beneath the cedar with one hand at her
throat. She was smiling, but there were tears on her
face. Behind her Mrs. Dove ran in a little circle,
and Mr. Dove followed, bowing and bowing.

Reggie opened the gate, closed it again, and came back
across the grass.

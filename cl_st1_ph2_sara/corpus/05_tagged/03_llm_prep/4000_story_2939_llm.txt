### THE FORTUNE OF THE FLYING DEVIL

There lived in San Francisco, during the latter part of
the year 1887, a certain Rabaya, who called himself variously
a physician of souls, an interpreter of the planets, and
a dealer in those rarer commodities which cannot prudently be
entered at the Custom House. Concerning his nationality I have
never obtained two harmonious accounts. He spoke Arabic with an
Egyptian, Malay with a Javanese, and English with an accent
which, according to his pleasure, might be Portuguese, Armenian, or
merely alcoholic. His establishment occupied two upper rooms in a
blind alley off Dupont Street, and was indicated to the
public by no sign, unless one may regard as such
a green lamp, burning at all hours behind a curtain
of yellow silk.

I knew Rabaya some years afterward. He was then a
prodigiously aged man, with a brown face composed principally of
wrinkles, two watery but observant eyes, and a white beard
which he frequently stroked when preparing a lie of more
than ordinary dimensions. I could hardly have been persuaded to
believe him under oath; yet it is proper to observe
that several circumstances connected with the affair of Captain James
Freeman came to my knowledge through Rabaya, and were subsequently
confirmed from independent sources.

Freeman was master and part owner of the schooner *Blue
Crane*, a vessel of one hundred and eighty tons, built
at Port Townsend and ostensibly engaged in the sandalwood and
copra trade. The word "ostensibly" is necessary. Her voyages were
profitable beyond the deserts of such humble merchandise, and she
carried, as a rule, fewer papers than pistols. Customs officers
had watched her, revenue men had questioned her, and an
insurance company had once declined to continue acquaintance with her;
but no court had convicted Captain Freeman of any particular
crime.

He was a thick, square man of forty-five, with a
brick-colored face, iron-gray whiskers, and pale eyes of that unblinking
sort which seamen occasionally acquire from looking too long at
hot horizons. He feared neither reef, gale, law, nor mutiny.
Contrariwise, he was afraid of thirteen at table, of a
broom laid across a hatchway, of a shark swimming clockwise
about a ship, and of hearing a woman sing before
breakfast. He would sail with a drunken mate, but not
with a red-haired cook. He trusted a rotten cable sooner
than a Friday.

The intended voyage of the *Blue Crane*, which was to
begin on the evening of October 3, caused him unusual
apprehension. Its destination was entered as Honolulu; Rabaya assured me
it was not Honolulu, and named another island which, after
reflection, I shall not name. Whether Freeman expected to carry
rifles thither, to remove treasure from it, or to perform
some enterprise still less consonant with law and morality, is
uncertain. What is known is that three nights before sailing
he went to Rabaya's rooms and purchased an amulet for
the sum of eight hundred dollars in gold.

The charm was a squat image of black-green stone, not
two inches in height. It represented a creature seated upon
its haunches, with folded arms, an enormous head, and a
mouth extending almost to the ears. One eye was a
reddish crystal; the other had been left a mere circular
pit. Across the back were scratched seven characters which no
scholar known to me has satisfactorily translated. A copper ring
passed through the crown of the head, and from this
depended a cord braided of yellow hair.

Rabaya's account of its history occupied nearly two hours and
included an Indian prince, a drowned Dutchman, three monks, a
volcano, and the sack of a city whose existence is
doubted by geographers. The substance of his statement was simpler.
The image, he said, contained Fortune, but Fortune of a
peculiar and discriminating temper. In the hands of a worthy
man it compelled favorable events; in those of a wicked
man it summoned evil, though at first disguising that evil
as success.

"You must be clean in the heart," Rabaya told Freeman.

The captain, who had paid eight hundred dollars and consequently
felt entitled to optimism, replied that his heart was as
clean as another man's.

"It knows," said Rabaya.

"So do I," said Freeman, putting the charm in his
pocket.

This conversation had an auditor.

Among the crew of the *Blue Crane* was a small
Malay sailor entered upon the articles as Ali Pa'ang, though
no one aboard called him anything but the Flying Devil.
He had earned this sobriquet in Batavia, where, during a
disturbance between two ships' companies, he had leaped from the
foreyard of one vessel to the rigging of another, a
distance which sober witnesses estimated at twenty-two feet and drunken
witnesses at half a cable. He was scarcely five feet
three inches in height, lean in body, prodigiously long in
the arms, and possessed of hands so narrow that they
seemed made to enter another man's pocket without disturbing the
cloth. His complexion was the color of old teak; his
cheekbones were high, his teeth small and regular, and his
black eyes exhibited a polished brightness unpleasantly suggestive of wet
stones.

The Flying Devil had followed Freeman from the waterfront. By
climbing a drainpipe and lying flat upon a roof outside
Rabaya's window, he both saw the amulet and heard an
imperfect account of its virtues. That he misunderstood the condition
attached to those virtues is possible. That he considered himself
worthy is less so.

On the afternoon of October 3, while the *Blue Crane*
lay off Broadway Wharf making her final preparations, Freeman discovered
that the image had vanished from a locked drawer in
his cabin. Ali Pa'ang was likewise missing. A boatman afterward
reported landing the Malay at the foot of Green Street
shortly after three o'clock.

It was thus that the Flying Devil began his celebrated
passage across San Francisco.

His first intention appears to have been concealment until he
could obtain transportation across the bay or southward by rail.
Yet the amulet, which he had tied about his neck
beneath his shirt, produced in him—if it produced anything—not caution
but an extraordinary assurance. He walked openly along the waterfront,
although two of Freeman's men were already seeking him, and
displayed money in three drinking shops. At the third, a
saloon near the lower slope of Telegraph Hill, he was
observed by a stranger named Kessler, whose subsequent participation in
the affair was brief but not inconsequential.

Kessler was a German clerk, unemployed, inquisitive, and addicted to
informing the police of matters which did not concern him.
He had seen the Flying Devil exchange Mexican silver for
American currency and concluded, perhaps reasonably, that the coins were
stolen. He followed the Malay up a steep street and
called to him near the parapet above the quarry.

What passed between them is not known. A woman hanging
washing in a yard below heard Kessler say, "You wait.
I know you," and heard the smaller man laugh. She
then saw them stand together at the wall as if
admiring the bay.

The Flying Devil turned. His knife entered beneath Kessler's fifth
rib. He withdrew it at once and stepped aside.

Kessler, who was a tall and loosely articulated person, did
not fall where he had been struck. He staggered backward,
sat upon the parapet, revolved slowly over it, and descended
the hillside through an opening in a fence. At the
bottom stood a cottage belonging to a widow named Clancey.
Kessler passed through Mrs. Clancey's kitchen window, carried away a
shelf of preserves, and came to rest upon her table
at the precise moment she set down the evening stew.
His arrival extinguished the lamp and dislodged the stovepipe. Mrs.
Clancey, believing herself attacked simultaneously by murderers and earthquake, ran
into the street with the saucepan upon her head.

During the resulting confusion the Malay went downhill.

Near the corner of Stockton he encountered a woman carrying
a leather satchel. This was Miss Euphemia Brill, secretary to
a charitable association and a lady of exceptional determination. The
satchel contained fourteen dollars, six pamphlets on temperance, a packet
of peppermint drops, and the association's minutes. Ali seized it.
Miss Brill retained the handle. He twisted her wrist, kicked
her ankle, and dragged her some yards before the stitching
gave way.

Her cries attracted a patrolman, Mr. Daniel Coyle, who was
eating an apple across the street. Coyle threw away the
fruit and pursued. The Malay, burdened with the satchel, ran
toward Washington Square, doubled through an alley, crossed Powell Street,
and entered a stable. Coyle followed so closely that he
seized the tail of the fugitive's shirt as Ali climbed
a stall partition.

The cloth tore. The Malay dropped behind the patrolman.

Coyle turned. Ali slipped the knife between his ribs, bounded
to a ladder, crossed the hayloft, and went through a
small upper window. The wounded officer nevertheless staggered into the
alley and blew his whistle until assistance arrived. He survived,
though one lung remained troublesome to him in damp weather,
and his testimony regarding the yellow-haired cord about the Malay's
neck is among the better evidence that the stolen charm
was still in Ali's possession.

From the stable roof the Flying Devil traveled southward for
nearly a block without touching the ground. Boys in the
street applauded his progress, supposing it an exhibition. One of
them stated that the Malay stopped upon a chimney, drew
out the amulet, kissed it, and showed his teeth at
the gathering crowd. Whether this occurred must remain doubtful, for
boys possess a natural faculty for improving whatever is already
sufficiently remarkable.

At Portsmouth Square Ali descended behind a lodging house, stole
a coat from a line, and emerged with the collar
raised to his ears. The satchel he abandoned after removing
the money and peppermints. Shortly thereafter Freeman's mate, accompanied by
two sailors, reached the square; but by then every idle
man in the vicinity had learned that somebody was being
pursued, and the natural affinity existing between idleness and lying
supplied them with six Malays traveling in nine directions.

The actual fugitive proceeded west. At a horse trough on
California Street he washed Kessler's blood from his hand and
blade. He then entered a shop, purchased bread, and paid
for it with Miss Brill's money. Such calmness may be
taken as evidence either of exceptional nerve or of confidence
in the amulet. The shopkeeper remembered that he repeatedly touched
something beneath his shirt and murmured, "No die. No catch."

The police had by this time connected the murder on
Telegraph Hill, the robbery, and the stabbing of Coyle. Descriptions
were telegraphed to the ferries, the railway stations, and the
military authorities at the Presidio. Mounted officers took the principal
roads. It should have been impossible for one conspicuous foreign
sailor, speaking little English and carrying a bloody knife, to
pass beyond the city. Nevertheless, at six o'clock he was
seen upon the road leading toward the military reservation.

Fog had begun to gather over the western hills and
to pour in long gray folds through the Golden Gate.
The sun, still visible above the ocean, had the appearance
of a copper coin seen at the bottom of dirty
water. Ali ran in the roadside ditch when riders approached
and otherwise maintained a rapid dogtrot. At least two citizens
observed him; neither interfered, for news of the preceding events
had outstripped him.

At the eastern military gate stood Private Lomas, a young
sentry whose zeal considerably exceeded his judgment. The Malay approached
wearing the stolen coat and carrying himself as though employed
upon an errand.

"You stop," said Lomas. "Where are you going?"

"Cook," replied Ali.

"Cook where?"

"Cook for captain."

"What captain?"

Ali pointed vaguely into the reservation and smiled.

Lomas called for the corporal. In doing so he turned
his head.

The Malay sprang. He clapped one hand over the soldier's
mouth, drove the knife twice beneath the tunic, and eased
him to the ground. He then caught the falling rifle
before it struck the road, dragged the body behind the
gatehouse, and went forward at a run.

Lomas lived perhaps three minutes. He did not regain speech.

Beyond the gate, detection became more difficult, for dusk, fog,
and the confusion of retreat movements favored the intruder. A
mounted patrol passed within twenty yards of him without seeing
him. He crossed a parade ground behind a wagon, entered
a storehouse, and there exchanged the long coat for a
fatigue blouse which fitted him badly but supplied a superficial
military appearance. From the same building he took a canteen
and a length of cord.

His progress can be reconstructed from scattered observations. A laundress
saw a "little brown soldier" moving toward the shore. Two
artillerymen met him near a battery and assumed he belonged
to the kitchen detachment. A teamster cursed him for darting
between his mules. None challenged him until he reached a
narrow service path leading toward the water below the fort.

Here a dog discovered what men had overlooked.

The animal was a large brindled mastiff kept by a
civilian watchman. It rushed from beneath a shed and fastened
upon Ali's sleeve. The watchman heard neither bark nor cry
afterward and supposed the dog had returned to its kennel.
In the morning it was found beside the path with
its neck broken and its jaws still entangled in a
strip of blue cloth. Thus the Malay had disposed of
the final guardian between himself and the bay.

At approximately a quarter before seven he reached the shore.
There were several small craft drawn above the tide line,
belonging to fishermen and government workmen. He selected a white
pulling boat, thrust it into the water, and embarked with
one oar. Why he did not take two is unexplained;
possibly the other had been removed, or possibly haste had
now begun to master his confidence.

He pulled awkwardly but powerfully away from the shore.

The alarm had meanwhile reached the fort. Lomas's body had
been found; soldiers were sent toward the batteries and landing
places; the theft of the boat was observed from an
upper parapet. A corporal with six men ran down to
the water and called upon the fugitive to return. Ali
stood in the stern, flourished his knife, and shouted something
which no witness understood.

The soldiers fired.

The first volley threw splinters from the gunwale. The second
struck the stern and caused the boat to yaw sharply.
One witness believed he saw Ali fall, though another maintained
that he merely crouched. The little craft entered the advancing
fog and vanished.

A field piece at the lower battery was trained upon
the last visible position. Its shot struck the water with
considerable violence. For an instant the fog glowed orange; fragments
of white wood appeared upon the waves, and the soldiers
cheered. A boat sent out ten minutes later recovered a
thwart, part of a bow, and the stolen fatigue blouse,
much torn and stained with blood.

No body was discovered.

The official conclusion was that the Flying Devil had been
killed by rifle fire or the shell and had sunk
with the wreckage. This conclusion was natural, economical, and, as
subsequent circumstances suggest, incorrect.

It was about this time that Captain Freeman cast off
and started out to sea.

The *Blue Crane*, delayed by the disappearance of her master's
charm and one member of her crew, got under way
shortly after seven. Freeman had at first refused to sail
without the amulet. His mate, a blunt Nova Scotian named
Pike, reminded him that the tide would not consult sorcery,
that cargo spoiled whether enchanted or not, and that certain
gentlemen waiting at the unnamed destination were not disposed to
forgive delay. Freeman yielded, though with an ill grace, and
stood upon the quarterdeck watching the fog.

The schooner passed Fort Point under reduced canvas. Her bell
sounded at intervals, and answering signals came dimly from shore.
The deck was wet, the rigging dripped, and the lamps
showed in pale circles no larger than plates. Freeman afterward
declared that he felt, as the vessel entered the channel,
an oppression similar to that which precedes thunder.

This assertion may be discounted. Freeman had heard of the
murder, the robbery, and the pursuit before sailing, and had
every reason to believe that his eight-hundred-dollar charm was at
the bottom of the bay. A man deprived of both
property and superstition will commonly discover oppression somewhere in his
anatomy.

At the fort, preparations were being made to fire the
sunset gun. The piece employed was an old smoothbore mounted
en barbette and loaded on ceremonial occasions with powder and
a heavy wad, but no projectile. Sergeant Bell supervised the
operation. He afterward testified that the bore had been sponged,
although this statement has been disputed by Gunner's Mate Haskins,
who said the failing light made inspection perfunctory. A corporal
rammed home the charge. Nothing unusual was observed.

At the proper signal the lanyard was pulled.

The gun discharged with a report distinctly louder than customary.
It leaped backward against its traversing gear, broke one iron
stop, and nearly threw a cannoneer from the platform. A
dark object, or group of objects, passed from the muzzle
into the fog. Sergeant Bell thought it a portion of
the wad. Another man said it resembled "a sack coming
apart."

No inquiry was made until the following morning, when blood
and fragments of cloth were discovered inside the bore.

Meanwhile the *Blue Crane*, having reached a position nearly opposite
the fort, received what Freeman described as a shower.

The first object struck the mainsail with a wet sound.
Then came several others, pattering upon deck, lodging in the
rigging, and splashing into the sea. One fragment broke the
glass of the cabin skylight. Another adhered to the mate's
shoulder. A third struck the binnacle and lay there, smoking
faintly.

The crew believed at first that they had encountered a
flock of birds torn by some atmospheric agency. Examination corrected
this agreeable error. The falling material included portions of human
flesh, splinters of bone, black hair, blue cloth, a piece
of leather sandal, two teeth, and the greater part of
a left hand. All had been scorched. Some bore grains
of unconsumed powder.

The mate ordered the deck washed. Captain Freeman did not
immediately assist.

At the foot of the binnacle, among a quantity of
reddish matter which need not be more particularly described, he
had seen a copper ring. He stooped, drew forth a
cord of singed yellow hair, and attached to it found
Rabaya's image. The stone was warm and blackened; the crystal
eye had cracked across the center. Otherwise the amulet was
intact.

Freeman wrapped it in a handkerchief and put it in
his pocket.

The *Blue Crane* continued through the Gate. She returned four
months later without her captain, he having died at the
unnamed island under circumstances which the mate declined to discuss.
The amulet was not among his effects. Rabaya denied having
seen it again, but I observed, during one of my
visits to his rooms, a squat shape beneath a square
of yellow silk. When I asked what it was, he
put his hand over the cloth and became suddenly deaf.

There remain three circumstances upon which any rational account of
the Flying Devil's fate must turn.

First: the boat was undoubtedly struck and shattered, but the
distance between its last position and the rocks below the
fort was not great. Ali Pa'ang was an exceptional swimmer,
and the fog would have concealed a return to shore.
Blood upon the recovered blouse proves that he was wounded,
not dead.

Second: the sunset cannon recoiled as if it had expelled
a weight far exceeding its blank charge. Blood, cloth, and
skin were found in the bore, though no one could
explain how they came there. The muzzle stood sufficiently low,
while the piece was unattended, to be entered by a
small and desperate man seeking concealment.

Third: human remains fell upon the *Blue Crane* immediately after
the gun was fired, and among those remains was the
amulet stolen by Ali Pa'ang.

I do not assert that the charm guided him through
murder and pursuit, preserved him from bullets, returned him unseen
to the fort, and finally selected, out of all the
hiding places in California, the loaded mouth of a cannon
aimed at the vessel he had deserted. Such an assertion
would exceed the evidence and confer upon Rabaya a credibility
which his general conduct does not merit. It may be
that Ali's confidence, violence, and extraordinary succession of escapes arose
solely from his own nature; that fog, fear, and coincidence
did the rest; and that the image was no more
than an ugly stone passing from one wicked hand to
another.

Yet the worthiness stipulated by Rabaya should perhaps not be
overlooked.

The Flying Devil was never seen again.

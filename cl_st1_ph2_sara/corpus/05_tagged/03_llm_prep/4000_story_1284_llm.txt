At eleven o'clock the bronze clock upon the mantel began
to strike.

Governor John Morrison raised his head at the first slow
note and kept it raised until the last vibration had
died in the high, ceremonial room. Then he looked again
at the desk before him.

It was almost clear.

For three days his secretaries had been emptying drawers, sorting
letters, carrying away the personal records of four years. The
photographs had gone into a black case; the silver inkstand
presented by the County Chairmen's Association had been wrapped in
paper; even the framed portrait of his wife had been
removed, leaving a pale oblong upon the dark wall. There
remained only a blotter, two pens, the seal of the
State, and, at the right-hand corner, a square bundle of
documents tied with red tape.

The convict-labor contracts.

Morrison's eyes rested upon them, then passed unwillingly to the
windows. Snow had fallen during the night. The roofs of
the capital lay white and still beneath a colorless sky,
and beyond them, where the town thinned into fields, the
roads ran like gray threads through the whiteness. Sleigh bells
sounded now and then in the street below. In less
than an hour those streets would be alive with the
inaugural procession.

Frank Leyman would take the oath at noon.

The retiring Governor leaned back in his chair and pressed
his fingers against his eyelids. He had not slept. His
face, when he lowered his hands, was gray with fatigue.
It was not a vicious face, nor even a dishonest
one in the ordinary acceptation of that word. It was
the face of a man who had yielded often, protested
seldom, and at last ceased to distinguish clearly between consent
and obedience.

A light tap came at the door.

"Come."

Harvey Francis entered without waiting for a second permission. He
brought with him the outer air, the odor of a
good cigar, and the easy vitality of a man to
whom every room, public or private, offered an entree. His
fur-lined coat was open over immaculate morning clothes. A white
carnation appeared in his buttonhole.

"Well, John," he said, smiling, "the last lap."

Morrison made a motion toward a chair, but Francis remained
standing. His glance moved over the empty walls and the
cleared desk.

"Housecleaning, eh? You have done a thorough job."

"Nearly."

Francis's glance touched the bundle at the corner. It rested
there for no more than a second.

"Nearly," he repeated. "That is quite right."

There had always been something in his tone which converted
statements into instructions without making them sound imperative. He had
never commanded Morrison. Commands could have been resented. He had
merely made "suggestions," had pointed out necessities, had shown how
one appointment would satisfy organized labour, how another would hold
the western counties, how a third would prevent an unfortunate
quarrel among friends. Always there had been reasons—practical, fluent, unanswerable
reasons. And behind them stood the machine, invisible but exact,
rewarding and punishing through a thousand hands.

Francis walked to the window.

"They are expecting a crowd," he observed. "Leyman has caught
the young fellows' fancy. Clean government, open doors, the public
trust—fine phrases for an inaugural address."

"He believes them," Morrison said.

Francis turned, amused rather than offended.

"Of course he does. That is what makes him effective
on a platform."

"He believed them before there was a platform."

"And he may believe them afterward. Belief is no obstacle
to usefulness when properly managed."

Morrison's hand tightened upon the arm of his chair. Four
years ago Francis had said something much like that about
him. John Morrison was sound, respectable, acceptable to the rural
vote. He had no enemies. He believed in decent government.
His very innocence, delicately praised, had been entered in the
ledger as a political asset.

Francis drew on his gloves.

"You understand about those papers?"

"I understand."

"There was no reason for us to assume the burden.
The present contracts expire in February. The bids were lawfully
advertised, the awards made, every provision regular. But there will
be noise. There is always noise when the words 'convict
labour' are printed in large type. Let Leyman decide whether
he prefers the manufacturers or the labour men."

"He cannot satisfy both."

"Precisely." Francis smiled. "Government consists largely in discovering whom one
can afford not to satisfy."

"And if he refuses to sign?"

"The State loses the bids, the prisons lose revenue, and
we point out his expensive devotion to theory. If he
signs, the unions that elected him learn the market value
of reform. Either course educates him."

Morrison looked down at the papers. Months earlier the prison
board had accepted the bids. Francis's attorneys had shaped the
terms; Francis's legislators had resisted every amendment. The contracts were
legal, perhaps even advantageous according to the narrow calculations in
which men disappeared and only figures remained. Yet the system
had been denounced throughout the campaign. Leyman had promised investigation,
humane regulations, an end to favours concealed in administrative routine.

And now the completed contracts lay waiting for his signature.

"Why were they sent here yesterday?" Morrison asked.

Francis's eyebrows rose slightly.

"They have been moving through the departments."

"They might have been ready a month ago."

"They might have been." Francis adjusted one glove finger by
finger. "But haste in public matters is dangerous."

"You meant them to be left."

"My dear John, I never mean anything so crude. Events
arrange themselves. The wise man merely refrains from disarranging them."

The clock ticked with an exaggerated clearness.

Francis came nearer and laid a hand upon Morrison's shoulder.
The gesture was friendly, almost affectionate; that made it intolerable.

"You have done well," he said. "There will be criticism
for a little while. There always is when an administration
goes out. But your friends do not forget. There is
the railway commission next autumn, and later, perhaps, the Senate.
You need rest now. Leave Leyman his own problems. He
was eager enough for them."

Morrison did not answer.

Francis's hand remained on his shoulder for an instant, then
withdrew.

"I shall see you at the ceremony. Eleven-thirty at the
west entrance. Do not let these solemn last moments tempt
you into a grand-stand play."

He laughed pleasantly, nodded, and went out.

The room seemed larger after him and colder. Morrison listened
to his retreating steps until a door closed somewhere down
the corridor.

A grand-stand play.

He rose and crossed to the mirror between the windows.
The man reflected there looked older than sixty-eight. His white
hair had been carefully brushed for the public ceremony, but
the flesh beneath his eyes sagged, and his mouth showed
the habit of concession. He tried to summon the expression
carried in the official portraits—the composed, paternal dignity of the
Commonwealth's chief magistrate. He could not find it.

What had become of the boy who used to stand
in the little red schoolhouse and recite the names of
the great men of the Republic?

The schoolhouse came back to him with absurd distinctness: the
iron stove glowing in winter, damp mittens steaming from nails,
a patch of snow visible through the low window. He
had been seventeen, tall and awkward, already expected to become
a lawyer because he could speak without notes. Once, after
a lesson upon the Constitution, he had walked home alone
through new snow, imagining public life as a high, severe
calling. Office was not power, he had thought then, but
guardianship. The people placed something sacred in a man's hands.

Sacredness! How easily the old words returned when there was
no longer any use for them.

He had not sold an office for money. He had
never taken a bribe. He had not enriched himself by
a dollar. These negatives had been his defence, repeated privately
whenever some appointment shamed him or some bill reached his
desk bearing Francis's unmistakable mark. Yet what had he done?
Signed where he was directed; withheld where he was advised;
smiled through banquets while other men made the bargains. Because
the stain had not been gold, he had called his
hands clean.

He returned to the desk.

The contracts waited with the patience of official paper. If
he signed them now, the whole affair would be settled
before Leyman entered the building. The newspapers would condemn Morrison.
Organized labour would call the act a final betrayal. Reform
committees would say that the Francis machine had fastened its
policy upon the State at the last possible moment. Francis
himself would be surprised—then perhaps gratified. After all, the contracts
represented the result he wanted; only the political trap would
be gone.

Why not sign?

The thought stirred him so powerfully that he sat erect.
Why not clean the desk in earnest? Why not perform,
in the last hour, one independent act? Let them say
what they pleased. He could issue a statement. He could
explain that the responsibility belonged to his administration, not Leyman's.
He could say that he had refused to pass a
burden to an innocent successor. Perhaps, when the heat had
gone, men would understand.

Perhaps they would say: Morrison was weak, but at the
end he found courage.

His heart beat faster. He saw the headlines; he heard
the grave praise of men who had despised him yesterday.
A dramatic conclusion—an old man rising above his past, accepting
blame, redeeming four wasted years with one stroke of the
pen.

Then he saw the ugliness of it.

He had endured compromise while compromise brought office, dignity, invitations,
the bowed heads of subordinates. Now that the carriage waited
to take him away, he would become heroic. Now, when
courage could cost him no governorship, he would purchase an
honourable memory by signing a bad contract and calling the
signature sacrifice.

Was that repentance? Or vanity in mourning clothes?

Morrison pushed the bundle away.

"No," he said aloud.

The word fell weakly in the empty room.

He could not repair four years in fifty minutes. He
could not go back to the first appointment, when Francis
had placed a list before him and said the party
must be considered. He could not restore the honest commissioner
removed because he had examined accounts too closely. He could
not unsign the franchise bill or recall the pardons granted
at the request of "friends." His chance had not been
hidden from him. It had come again and again, and
each time he had known it—known it, hesitated, yielded, and
afterward invented a practical reason.

Too late.

The clock showed seventeen minutes past eleven.

Morrison began opening drawers already emptied, then closing them. From
the lowest he took a yellow envelope containing a few
personal letters overlooked by the secretary. One was from Leyman,
written three years before, when the younger man had served
in the legislature. It thanked the Governor for supporting a
small appropriation for factory inspection.

"You have done a great deal," Leyman had written, in
the ardent generosity of one who does not suspect how
little has been done, "and I am certain you will
do a great deal more."

Morrison remembered the day the letter arrived. He had almost
laughed at it, then had put it away. Why? Perhaps
because it gave him for a moment the pain of
being mistaken for the man he wished to be.

A sound of voices came from the corridor. The door
opened, and his secretary, Allen, appeared.

"The carriage will be ready at half past eleven, Governor.
The committee is gathering below."

"Yes."

"Is there anything more?"

Morrison looked at the contracts.

"No. Nothing."

Allen glanced about the room with the subdued emotion proper
to a last departure.

"It has been an honour to serve you, sir."

The words were conventional, but the young man's face was
sincere. Morrison could not meet his eyes.

"Thank you, Allen. You have been very faithful."

When the secretary had gone, Morrison folded Leyman's letter and
put it into his breast pocket. Eleven-twenty-two.

Soon Leyman would enter through the west doors, buoyant, confident,
surrounded by men shouting his name. He would take the
oath, come to this office, and find the bundle. Francis
would give him perhaps a day—two at most. Newspapers friendly
to the machine would demand action. Manufacturers would threaten suit.
Labour leaders would call the contracts slavery. Every choice would
break some part of the coalition that had carried reform
to victory.

That was politics, Francis would say. Education.

Morrison imagined Leyman sitting at the desk, the strong young
face troubled for the first time by the knowledge that
legal duty and public faith had been deliberately twisted together.
He imagined the advisers arguing, the delegations waiting, the telephone
ringing. There would be other traps afterward, subtler ones. But
this was to be the first: a greeting from the
old order, placed like filth in a drawer.

He had been thinking of himself.

His shame. His reputation. His lost opportunities. Even his proposed
repentance had turned upon what men might say of John
Morrison.

But the contracts were not about John Morrison—not now.

They were about the man who was coming.

The realization arrived without exaltation. It was plain, almost cold.
He had no right to use the papers to cleanse
his own name. But neither had he the right to
leave them as Francis intended. If the act were dishonourable,
let its dishonour fall where the authority had lain. If
there must be political punishment, why should Leyman bear it
before his hand had touched the government?

Morrison rose and began to walk.

Suppose he signed and said nothing? No statement, no plea,
no noble explanation. The world would assume that he had
obeyed Francis once more. His enemies would call it the
natural completion of a corrupt administration. His friends might privately
praise his loyalty to the machine. Leyman himself might despise
him.

Could he do it then?

The question stopped him beside the window.

Below, a company of militia marched into the square, their
dark ranks cutting across the snow. A few spectators ran
after them. From a distant street came the first uncertain
notes of a band.

Morrison felt something within him resist. To do right and
receive the name of wrong—was that required? Must he surrender
even the comfort of being understood?

Yes. Otherwise the act was still a bargain.

He stood watching the snow until the ranks blurred. There
would be no recovered career, no purified portrait, no late
entrance among the great men whose names he had recited
in the schoolhouse. He was not transformed. He was still
afraid of Francis, still hungry for the approval of men
whose contempt he had earned, still weak enough to wish
that necessity would remove the decision from him.

But Leyman should have a fair morning.

Only that.

The clock showed eleven-thirty-five.

Morrison returned to the desk and cut the red tape.

There were six contracts, each with duplicate sheets, schedules, certifications,
and the opinion of the Attorney-General. He read the first
page slowly. The type wavered. He put on his spectacles
and read again, though he knew the provisions. Five years.
Fixed prices. Exclusive use of specified prison workshops. Penalties for
interference by later regulation.

A shameful arrangement in places; legal in every place.

Could he refuse them instead? He considered it. But refusal
at this hour would be another gesture, legally doubtful and
easily reversed, while leaving Leyman to defend the reversal. The
prison board had acted; the successful bidders had acquired claims.
Only the Governor's execution remained. Francis had preserved that final
ministerial duty because its performance—or nonperformance—could be made poisonous.

Morrison dipped the pen.

His hand hovered over the line.

John Morrison, Governor.

How many times had he written that title? On commissions,
pardons, requisitions, proclamations. At first the words had thrilled him.
Later they became a muscular habit, a curved mark placed
where Allen indicated. Now each letter seemed to ask whether
he possessed the authority he had worn.

He set the pen down without writing.

Eleven-forty.

There was time yet to escape. He could bind the
papers and go below. Francis would be waiting, composed and
cordial. The band would play; the crowd would cheer Leyman;
Morrison would sit upon the platform, an old man released
from burdens. By one o'clock he would be on the
train. By evening the capital would speak of another governor.

Released? No. Carried away unchanged.

He picked up the pen again and signed the first
contract.

The sound was no more than a faint scratching.

He signed the duplicate, blotted both, and reached for the
second. His hand shook so violently that a spot of
ink fell beside his name. He waited, breathing carefully. The
clock ticked. The band outside struck a march and lost
it in the wind.

Second contract. Duplicate.

Third.

At eleven-forty-six Allen knocked and entered.

"Governor, the committee—"

"Not yet."

Allen saw the open papers and stopped.

"Shall I call the Attorney-General?"

"No."

"Mr. Francis is below."

"Let him wait."

The secretary stared. In four years he had never heard
that tone from Morrison. It was not loud; indeed it
was almost exhausted. But it admitted no "suggestion."

"Yes, Governor."

"And bring me the register."

Allen brought the great leather book from the cabinet and
laid it open on the desk. His eyes moved from
the signed sheets to Morrison's face. He asked no question.

When he had gone, Morrison entered the first two contracts
in the register. The columns demanded dates, parties, terms, departmental
references. Bureaucracy, which had so often concealed wrongdoing beneath routine,
now required him to make his decision exact. There must
be no omission through which the machine could return the
burden to Leyman.

Eleven-fifty.

The fourth contract contained eighteen pages. Morrison signed where the
ribbons crossed the final sheet, then pressed the seal into
red wax. The State's arms appeared imperfectly. He melted the
wax and sealed it again.

His fear had not left him. It increased. Francis might
enter at any moment. He might laugh; worse, he might
reason. He would speak of the Senate, of loyalty, of
ingratitude. Morrison knew how readily the old habits might revive
before that assured voice.

He locked the door.

Fifth contract.

From below came a swell of cheering. Leyman had arrived.

Morrison paused, pen in hand. He could see nothing from
his window but the edge of the crowd and flags
moving above it. Yet he pictured the younger man stepping
from the carriage, bareheaded despite the snow, smiling as if
public confidence were not a weapon that could wound its
possessor.

"You will do a great deal more."

"I did not," Morrison whispered. "You must."

He signed the fifth duplicate.

Eleven-fifty-four.

Only the last remained. It was the largest and most
controversial, granting the metalworks company the labour of three hundred
prisoners. Morrison read the title. Three hundred men, reduced to
a clause; a reform governor, reduced to a choice prepared
by enemies; a departing governor, reduced by his own weakness
until only six minutes of authority remained.

Six minutes.

No—five now.

He signed.

A pounding began at the door.

"John!" Francis called. "What is this? They are waiting."

Morrison blotted the signature.

"Open the door."

He did not answer. He sealed the original, then the
duplicate. One impression, two. He compared them, tied each completed
pair, and entered the final numbers in the register.

The pounding ceased.

Francis understood.

There came a pause more menacing than anger. Then his
voice, low and clear:

"You will regret this."

Morrison looked at the clock.

Three minutes to twelve.

Perhaps he would regret it. Fear, habit, loneliness—these would return.
Tomorrow he might awaken and long for the railway commission,
for the Senate, for the approving hand upon his shoulder.
Men did not remake their souls in an hour. But
regret would come tomorrow, and the act was done today.

He gathered the contracts and placed them in the official
drawer. Then he wrote upon a sheet:

Executed and registered this eleventh day of January, at eleven
fifty-seven in the forenoon. Responsibility for these instruments rests with
the outgoing administration.

He hesitated over the final words. They sounded like explanation,
almost defence. He crossed out the sentence about responsibility, leaving
only the record of execution. Beneath it he signed once
more.

No plea.

No claim.

He unlocked the door.

Francis stood outside, his face stripped of humour. Behind him
waited Allen and two members of the inaugural committee. No
one spoke.

Morrison put on his coat.

"The contracts are completed," he said to Allen. "Deliver copies
to the prison board and to Governor Leyman's secretary after
the oath."

Francis's eyes held his.

"You fool."

The words were scarcely audible.

Morrison felt the old instinct to placate him—to explain that
the contracts themselves were safe, that the machine had lost
nothing but a tactical advantage. Instead he buttoned his coat
with slow fingers.

"Perhaps," he said.

The clock began to strike noon.

At the first note, a cannon boomed from the square.
Cheers rose, filling the corridors and shaking the window glass.
The band burst into the State march, brass and drums
confused for a moment by stone walls and winter air.

John Morrison walked toward the stairway.

He had no illusion that the sound was for him.
The crowd had already turned its face toward the new
Governor; his own name belonged to yesterday. Behind him lay
the clean desk, the locked drawer, the wet ink drying
upon six contracts. Before him waited ceremony, judgment, and the
long obscurity of a man who had been powerful without
possessing power.

Yet as he descended, a strange lightness came upon him.

For three minutes, at the end of four years, he
had been Governor.

That was little. It was almost nothing. It could not
restore the years, nor absolve the injuries done through silence.
But the act had been free—free of Francis, free of
reward, free even of the hope of honour. Somewhere above,
upon the desk he had surrendered, lay the first burden
Frank Leyman would not have to bear.

The band played louder.

Morrison lifted his head and went out to meet the
new day.

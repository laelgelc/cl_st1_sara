The family arrives on Saturday in a silver car with
bicycles tied to the roof and bags pressed against every
window. By the time they reach the house, the car
is the color of the road.

I wait beside the gate because Papa is mowing the
lower field. He has gone over the same strip twice.
From a distance he looks like a dark post behind
the machine, straight and narrow, while the cut grass lies
in shining rows around him.

The driver lowers his window. He is the father. Beside
him sits a woman wearing blue sunglasses. Two girls lean
forward between the seats, one fair-haired and one brown-haired, though
they have the same pointed chin.

"Is this Casa Gialla?" the father asks.

The house hasn't been yellow for years, but I tell
him yes.

I open the gate and show them where to park.
I show them the key beneath the flowerpot, though they
have already been sent a message explaining this. I show
them the outdoor tap, the fuse box, the gas stove,
the mosquito screens. I tell them not to leave bread
on the counter because of mice.

The youngest girl asks if there are really mice.

"Sometimes."

"Can we see one?"

The mother laughs. She says they are hoping to see
animals.

I don't tell her about the poison beneath the kitchen
cupboards.

Papa switches off the mower and comes up the hill.
The quiet after the engine is strange. Crickets take its
place. He wipes his face with a cloth and shakes
the father's hand. When Papa says welcome, the word comes
out thick and wet. The man smiles too brightly and
pats his shoulder.

I explain that my father takes care of the property.
If they need anything, we live beyond the olive trees,
in the small house with the red roof.

The mother looks in that direction, though the trees hide
our windows.

"How wonderful," she says. "To live here all year."

Papa has already turned back toward the mower.

Inside, the house is cool and smells of closed rooms.
I opened it yesterday, beat dust from the cushions, swept
dead flies from the stairs and scrubbed yellow marks from
the bath. Now the girls run from room to room
choosing beds. The older one gets the room with the
balcony. The younger one says it isn't fair, then discovers
a lizard on the wall and forgets.

Their parents carry in canvas bags, straw hats, books, bottles
of wine, rubber shoes for the beach, a box of
peaches and a laptop in a black case. The mother
carries the laptop herself. She also has three notebooks held
together with a band.

I know she is a writer before she tells me.

People who come here bring things they don't use. Walking
sticks, watercolors, binoculars, novels about finding happiness. They leave them
in piles all week, as if the objects are resting
too. The mother sets her notebooks on the long table
and places a smooth white stone on top so the
wind can't move the pages.

She asks my name.

"Mina."

She repeats it carefully, the way teachers do on the
first day of school.

The younger girl is called Rosa, though she is not
named after the flower. She tells me this herself. Her
sister is Clara. Their parents are Elena and Paolo, and
I should use their first names because everyone does on
holiday.

I say I will.

I don't.

On Sunday they wake early and eat outside. I know
because I hear their chairs scraping the terrace while I
feed our chickens. I carry six eggs across the field
in a wire basket. Two are still warm and streaked
with dirt. Rosa holds one against her cheek.

"It came out of a chicken?"

Clara tells her not to be stupid.

Their father offers to pay, but Papa has said the
first six are a welcome gift. I tell him this.
He thanks me and says they taste better here, before
he has tasted them.

The mother is writing at the end of the table.
She has coffee beside her and a red scarf tied
around her hair. When Rosa asks what she is writing,
she closes the notebook.

"Nothing yet."

The girls put on swimsuits and dresses. The family drives
to the beach, carrying towels and a striped umbrella. They
invite me, but I tell them I have work.

I have been to the beach twice. Once on a
school trip, when the boys threw seaweed at one another
and someone took my shoes. Once with Mama and Papa,
the first summer after we came here. Papa sat beneath
the umbrella with his shirt buttoned to his neck. He
watched every man who walked past us.

While the guests are away, I water the basil, collect
tomatoes and hang our sheets on the line. Mama works
Sundays at the hotel near the motorway. She leaves before
sunrise and returns smelling of bleach, fried oil and the
perfume from other women's rooms.

At school I say she works in a hotel. I
don't say she cleans it.

The family returns with sand in the car and pink
skin on their shoulders. Rosa has a bucket of shells.
Clara has found green glass worn smooth by the sea.
Their father has bought three kinds of cheese and a
fish wrapped in paper. Their mother says the beach was
almost empty, as if it had been arranged that way
for them.

That evening they cook outside. Smoke crosses the field. I
hear music and bursts of laughter. Papa repairs the broken
wire at the edge of the orchard. The wire marks
where the rental property ends and our part begins, though
both belong to Signor Bassi, who lives in the city
and comes twice a year.

Rosa sees me and waves a wooden spoon.

"We saw a crab!"

I wave back.

Papa pushes the wire deeper into the post.

On Monday I take them to the stream. It is
not part of my work, but the path is easy
to miss. The family follows me through the tall grass.
Their sandals collect seeds. Flies gather around their legs.

The stream is low, with warm pools between gray stones.
Frogs jump when we approach. Rosa screams each time, then
laughs. Clara lifts stones and looks for salamanders. Paolo photographs
a blue dragonfly from many angles.

Elena walks beside me. She asks how old I am.

"Sixteen."

"Do you like living here?"

I tell her it's quiet.

"That must be good for studying."

I say yes.

She asks where I was born. When I tell her,
she says she visited our country when she was a
student. She remembers the markets, the spices, the bright cloth.
She doesn't mention the apartment blocks, the buses or the
clinics where my aunts work.

At the stream, she sits under a willow and takes
out her notebook. The others build a dam from stones.
I watch the water rise behind it, brown with stirred
mud. A trapped beetle circles on the surface.

"What do you write?" I ask.

"Things I notice."

She taps the page with her pen.

"Otherwise they disappear."

On the walk back, Rosa finds a cricket. She cups
it in both hands. I tell her not to squeeze,
but when she opens them, the insect lies crooked in
her palm. She says perhaps it is pretending.

Clara pokes it with a grass stem.

Their mother tells them to leave it in the shade.

That night frogs call from the ditch. Their voices are
ugly when there are many together. The guests sit on
the terrace without turning on the lights. I can see
four dark shapes against the house and the red point
of a mosquito candle.

They think the darkness is peaceful. In winter the same
darkness starts before supper and stays until morning. Rain fills
the ditch. Mud covers the steps. Wind comes under our
door even when Papa stuffs the crack with cloth.

No one rents the house then.

On Tuesday the girls come to see the chickens. Rosa
wants to hold one. I catch the oldest hen and
press its wings to its body. Its feet curl in
the air.

Rosa strokes the red comb with one finger. She says
the hen feels hot.

Clara asks if we kill them when they stop laying.

I say sometimes.

Rosa gives the hen back.

I show them the vegetable patch, the pear trees, the
shed where Papa keeps fuel and tools. I don't show
them the traps. One is behind the sacks, with dried
blood on the metal bar.

They pick blackberries along the wall. The ripe ones collapse
between their fingers. Juice marks their wrists. Clara says she
wants to live here and never return to the city.
Rosa says she would bring her friends and sleep in
the barn.

I tell them the barn roof leaks.

"We could fix it," Clara says.

In the afternoon Elena washes clothes and asks where to
hang them. I lead her to the line behind the
rental house. She pins up swimsuits, small shorts, white shirts,
Paolo's blue underpants. The wind fills everything with bodies.

She says laundry dries beautifully in the country.

At our house it takes two days when the air
is damp. Sometimes insects crawl into the folds. Once I
put on a sweater and a wasp stung my shoulder.

Elena asks if I'll be at the dinner on Thursday.
It is Paolo's birthday. They are inviting friends from the
city and want Papa and Mama to come too.

I say Mama may be working.

"You must all come," she says. "We'll celebrate together."

She hands me a bowl of peaches that are too
soft for their trip to the beach tomorrow. One has
split, and wasps sit inside the wound. I cover the
bowl with a towel and carry it home.

Mama cuts away the brown parts. We eat the rest
after supper. For Papa, I slice the peach thin and
remove the skin.

He eats slowly.

On Wednesday the family takes both bicycles down from the
roof. Paolo checks the tires. Elena writes in the shade
while the girls ride in circles around the house.

When I bring clean towels, I see a page under
the white stone. The writing is small and slanted. I
only read the first line before she returns from the
kitchen.

At the edge of the field, the girl watches us.

I step away.

Elena gives me lemonade. She asks about my school, my
exams, what I want to study. I say maybe languages.
Papa wants me to become a translator, a doctor, a
lawyer, anything with clean hands and doors that lock properly.
His plans change depending on what he hears on the
radio.

"You could be a writer," Elena says.

I laugh because I think she is being polite.

She doesn't laugh. She studies my face, then writes something
after I leave.

That evening Papa and I gather pears for the birthday
dinner. Many are spoiled. The good ones are hard near
the stem. We turn each fruit in our hands, looking
for holes.

Papa says I should take the bus to the library
on Friday. The words are difficult, but I understand him.
I always understand him.

"I have to clean after they leave."

He shakes his head. Study, he says.

He says this often. Study. Go. Don't stay.

I ask where he thinks I should go.

He points beyond the fields.

On Thursday morning, a van delivers the cake. The driver
cannot find the house, so he calls Papa. Papa answers
but cannot make himself understood. I take the phone and
guide the van from the church to the gravel road.

The cake is white, with dark chocolate around the sides
and sugared violets on top. The bakery name is printed
on the box in gold.

The guests arrive at seven. There are two couples and
a man with a beard who brings wine. Mama has
come straight from the hotel. Her hair is wet from
a fast shower. Papa wears a clean shirt, buttoned high.

We sit at two tables pushed together under the fig
tree. There are grilled peppers, bread, olives, chicken, tomatoes with
salt, and pears cooked in wine. The guests talk about
traffic, schools, rents, elections, restaurants. They talk about the silence
here. They say they can hear themselves think.

Papa eats little. Mama puts soft food on his plate
without asking.

When the cake comes, everyone applauds. Paolo blows out the
candles. Rosa gets sugar on her nose. The bearded man
asks where the cake was made.

Elena names the bakery.

"It's just off Piazza Bellori," she says.

Papa puts down his fork.

No one notices at first. They are talking about the
bakery's almond biscuits and whether the old cinema is still
open. Then Mama stops cutting my cake.

Papa lowers his head. His hands rest flat on his
knees. After a moment he stands and walks away from
the table.

Elena asks if he is unwell.

Mama says he needs air.

He goes past the shed, beyond the light from the
terrace. Soon I smell his cigarette.

The bakery had been a shoe shop when Papa worked
in Piazza Bellori. He sold flowers from a cart near
the fountain. Roses, lilies, carnations, bunches of lavender tied with
string. He left home before dawn to buy them wholesale.
In old photographs he is always smiling with his mouth
open.

He knew which flowers people bought when they were guilty
and which they bought when they were in love. He
knew the office workers, the old women from the church,
the restaurant waiters who came for leftover roses after midnight.
He spoke well then. Better than Mama. Better than me.

One evening, three men came across the piazza after the
bars had closed. They asked the price of roses. Then
they asked where he was from. He tried to move
his cart, but one man kicked the wheel.

They pushed him against the fountain. They called him names
he later refused to repeat for us. One held his
arms. Another struck his face with something hard. The third
kicked him after he fell.

No one in the apartments above came down.

The police found him beside the crushed flowers. Most of
his front teeth were broken. His jaw was fractured in
two places. There was damage inside his mouth that the
doctors repaired badly, then repaired again. Even after the swelling
disappeared, words no longer obeyed him. They caught behind his
tongue. Strangers looked away or leaned closer with the patient
expression people use for children.

Papa did not return to the piazza.

For months he stayed in our apartment with the shutters
closed. When footsteps stopped outside our door, he stood without
breathing. When men laughed in the street, he checked the
locks.

Signor Bassi knew someone who knew Mama's cousin. He needed
a caretaker who could mow fields, mend pipes, prune trees,
clean gutters and keep thieves from an empty country house.
Speaking was not important.

So we came here.

People say Papa prefers the quiet. This is partly true.

At the birthday table, the guests continue eating. Their voices
are lower now. Elena says she hopes she hasn't caused
offense. Mama tells her no. She says Papa had work
near that piazza long ago.

I carry him a plate of cake.

He stands by the boundary wire. The cigarette glows near
his hand. I give him the plate and a fork,
though I know he won't eat the sugared violets. They
are hard.

He asks if I am enjoying myself.

The words come slowly, damaged but clear enough.

"Yes."

He points toward the table. Go, he means.

Instead I stand beside him. In the field, moths strike
the lamp over and over. Music starts again on the
terrace. Someone opens another bottle. There is a cheer when
the cork comes free.

The next morning, the family sleeps late. I find plates
outside, glasses with dead insects in the wine, napkins damp
from dew. The cake box is empty except for a
violet and smears of cream.

Elena comes out while I stack dishes. She looks tired.

"Your father," she says. "Was it something about the piazza?"

I tell her he used to sell flowers there.

She waits, but I don't say more.

She picks up the sugared violet from the box and
holds it in her palm. Then she puts it back.

That day they stay near the house. Paolo reads in
a hammock. Clara and Rosa make crowns from clover. Elena
writes for hours. Pages collect beneath the white stone.

In the afternoon, Papa repairs a shutter at the rental
house. Paolo tries to help. They work without much speech,
passing the hammer and nails between them. When it is
finished, Paolo brings two beers.

They drink on the steps.

From our kitchen window, I watch Papa laugh at something.
He covers his mouth with his hand.

Saturday arrives too quickly for them and at the usual
speed for us.

They pack towels, books, shells, dirty shoes, jars of honey
and the green glass Clara found at the beach. Rosa
cries because she doesn't want to leave the chickens. Clara
takes one last photograph of the barn.

Paolo says the week has made them a family again.

Elena hugs Mama. She hugs me too. Her hair smells
of smoke and lemons.

"You're lucky," Rosa says. "You never have to go away."

Papa opens the gate.

The silver car rolls onto the road, bicycles trembling on
its roof. All four wave through the windows. Dust rises
behind them and hangs above the hedge long after the
car is gone.

By noon, the house is silent.

I strip the beds. I open the shutters. I collect
a sock from beneath a chair, two shells from the
bathroom, a broken green pencil, a bottle cap, a paper
crown made by Rosa. There is sand in the sheets
and blackberry juice on a pillowcase. The refrigerator contains half
an onion, butter, a lemon, and cheese wrapped in paper.

Families always leave pieces of themselves. Usually I throw them
away.

This time I put the shells and pencil in my
pocket. I fold the paper crown and carry it home.
I find the white stone on the terrace table and
take that too.

Under the table lies a sheet covered in Elena's slanted
handwriting. Another page is caught behind the leg of a
chair. A third has blown into the rosemary.

I should place them in an envelope and ask Signor
Bassi for her address.

Instead I sit on the step and read.

There is the girl at the gate. There is the
father cutting grass, the mother returning after dark, the eggs
warm from the nest. There are Papa's broken words, Mama's
hands, my careful slices of pear. There is the wire
at the edge of the orchard. There is a sentence
about silence and another about exile. Some things are wrong.
Some are close enough to hurt.

She has been watching us all week.

From beyond the olive trees comes the sound of Papa
starting the mower. The engine catches, fails, then catches again.
Tomorrow another family will arrive. I will show them the
key, the tap, the screens and the poison. I will
tell them where the path begins and where the property
ends.

I fold Elena's pages and place them in my pocket
with the things her daughters left behind. Then I take
up the broom and begin with the sand.

### THE STORY IN THE RUG

From the east chamber of the Bascom house one could
see, on a clear day, the white meeting-house, the Baptist
spire, Deacon Pitkin's red barn, the upper panes of the
academy, and a blue wedge of the river where it
bent behind the willows. On a doubtful day one could
see considerably more; for the chamber was known in Brookvale
as the village watch-tower, and what failed to reach it
by eyesight commonly arrived by way of faith, inference, or
Miss Tempy Cross.

Diadema Bascom herself made little use of the watch-tower for
public purposes. She had no objection to knowing what went
forward, but she held that news, like preserves, should be
kept in proper vessels and not spilled over the pantry
shelves. Her boarder, Priscilla Hollis, schoolmistress of the upper district,
sometimes sat at the east window with her mending and
observed the road below; yet even Priscilla, whose education had
not destroyed her natural curiosity, was less interested in the
village than in the room.

It was a chamber of sober dimensions and violent loyalties.
A blue spread covered the bed; red tassels hung from
the white curtains; a gilt eagle, somewhat moulting about the
wings, spread itself above the looking-glass; and upon the mantel
stood a procession of objects whose patriotic intention was clearer
than their beauty—a crimson scent-bottle, a china dog with blue
ears, two white shells, a red pincushion, three peacock feathers,
and a blue glass slipper containing spills for the lamp.
Diadema had inherited the room in a condition of ordinary
peacefulness and had improved it, as she said, "till there
wa'n't a disloyal corner left."

On the rainy afternoon when the rug began, however, neither
village nor decorations held Priscilla's attention. A hooked-rug frame occupied
the space between the windows, and beside it sat Diadema,
her lap covered with strips of cloth. A basket at
her feet overflowed with faded ribbons, pieces of merino, calico,
broadcloth, silk, flannel, and one or two fragments whose age
and previous use no human eye could have determined without
assistance.

Diadema's hair was gray and drawn so tightly from her
temples that it seemed to enforce moral order upon her
features. Her spectacles rested halfway down her nose. She was
not a large woman, but she produced the impression of
exactness in a considerable area. Dust avoided her; disorder feared
her; and moths, until the preceding week, had been supposed
to understand that the Bascom premises were closed against them.

That supposition had proved tragically mistaken.

"I declare," said Diadema, drawing a strip of green cloth
through the burlap, "if I'd known there was a moth
in this house I'd have set up all night with
a candle an' hunted him as if he was a
burglar. Burglar's more honorable, when you come to think. He
takes your silver, mebbe, but he don't eat your dead
folks."

Priscilla lowered her sewing.

"Was everything injured?"

"Everything I cared most about. That's the moth natur'. Your
good black alpaca he'll leave, 'cause you wear it Sundays
an' air it. Your old brown dress he'll leave, 'cause
you wouldn't grieve if he et it bodily. But give
him a little box tied with blue ribbon, an' he
knows there's heartbreak inside. He'll go through cedar, camphor, newspaper,
an' prayer, I shouldn't wonder."

She hooked another strip into place and examined the loop
critically.

"The clothes were Lovice's," she continued. "Lovice was my twin.
There's folks in Brookvale that never saw her, sence she's
been gone thirty-two years; but they've heard of her, if
only from me. I don't cal'late to let her perish
out complete while I've got hands."

Priscilla looked at the heap of fragments with new attention.
Some were scarcely larger than leaves; others had been cut
into long, even strips. At the bottom of the basket
lay a piece of white shawl, clean and uncut.

"So you decided upon a rug?"

"Well, what else? I couldn't sew the gowns up again;
moths had made sieves of 'em. I couldn't burn 'em.
I stood by the kitchen stove with a whole apronful,
but when I lifted the cover, there was Lovice's wedding
sleeve on top, an' I says, 'No, you don't.' Not
to the sleeve; to myself. Then I thought of a
rug. A rug can take pieces. Folks can, too, if
they have to, but a rug makes the pieces useful."

She pushed her spectacles higher and regarded the design marked
in charcoal upon the burlap. It displayed two roses upon
a single stalk, several buds, a large butterfly of uncertain
anatomy, a border of green leaves, and, around all, an
unfinished outer wreath.

"I drew it myself," said Diadema. "Jot used to say
I couldn't draw a straight line with a ruler, but
there ain't many straight lines in natur', so that never
troubled me. These roses are Lovice an' me. Two flowers,
one stalk. You see?"

"They are very like roses," Priscilla said loyally.

"Well, they will be when I get the red in.
At present they favor cabbages, but a beginning ain't obliged
to explain itself. This green here is the stalk, an'
it come from our winter hoods."

"Our?" Priscilla prompted.

"Lovice's an' mine. Mother always dressed us alike. She said
twins was a dispensation an' ought to look so. If
one of us wore green, the other wore green; if
one had three tucks, the other had three; an' if
one tore her apron, Mother generally boxed both our ears,
'cause she said she couldn't be certain which had begun
it. That was justice according to arithmetic, though not according
to facts."

Diadema's hook moved steadily. Rain threaded the windowpanes, and the
village beyond them withdrew into grayness.

"We was born on a Tuesday," she went on, "just
after the great wind took the top off the Peabody
barn. Aunt Nabby said one child was trouble enough for
any reasonable storm to bring, but Mother never grudged us.
She named Lovice out of a book. I don't know
what book, an' nobody ever found the name in another;
but Father named me Diadema after a hymn he liked,
though why he didn't choose Jerusalem or Coronation I can't
say.

"Lovice was born first by seven minutes, an' she never
let me forget it till we was ten. Then I
pushed her into the millpond, an' after Father pulled her
out she allowed age wa'n't everything.

"She was the pretty one. There's generally a pretty one
in twins, same as there's a good pie an' a
poor pie when you bake two side by side. Lovice
had yellow-brown hair that curled at the ends without rags,
blue eyes, an' a little color that come an' went.
I had hair the shade of wet hay, an' my
eyes couldn't make up their minds between green an' gray.
But I was the forehanded one. At twelve I could
turn a heel, set bread, skim milk, an' tell by
the smell when the damper was shut. Lovice could sing
every verse in the hymn-book an' forget the kettle till
it boiled dry."

"You must have loved each other very much," said Priscilla.

"We did, except when we didn't. There ain't any closeness
on earth that prevents two girls from wantin' the same
blue sash. We fought for it Saturday, wore it turn
about Sunday, an' slept together Sunday night with our arms
round one another. That's twins."

She selected two pieces of faded red wool.

"These are the roses. This dark red was from our
first best dresses. Mother made 'em when we was sixteen,
with basques pointed front an' back, though Deacon Petty said
points was worldly. His wife had five rows of fringe
on her cape, so Mother didn't feel chastened.

"The lighter red come from Lovice's little shoulder shawl. She
wore it the night Reuben Granger walked home with us
from singin'-school. That's where the courtin' truly commenced, though he'd
been lookin' pious in her direction for a month."

"Mr. Granger was a minister, was he not?"

"He become one. At that time he was studyin' with
Parson Cole, an' consider'able uncertain whether he was called to
preach or only disliked farmin'. Folks mixed the two up
oftener then. Reuben wa'n't handsome exactly, but he had a
good forehead, an' when he spoke to you he seemed
to leave all the rest of creation waitin'. Lovice liked
that. Most women do."

The red loops appeared one by one beneath her fingers,
deepening the first rose.

"That night he offered Lovice his arm at the corner.
I knew enough to walk the other side, but Jot
Bascom come jumpin' over the stone wall an' landed in
the road alongside of me. He'd been to the store
for lamp chimneys, an' broke two when he jumped, but
he said the sound expressed his feelin's.

"'Diadema,' says he, 'if Reuben takes Lovice, I'll take you.'

"'You won't do any such thing,' says I. 'I ain't
a parcel to be divided.'

"'I know it,' says he. 'You're the whole freight.'

"That was Jot. He could say what no sensible man
would think of, an' somehow make you pleased he'd said
it."

"Was his name Jonathan?"

"It was intended to be. His mother called him Jot
before his christenin', an' the minister, bein' deaf, thought that
was the name selected. There it stayed. Jot said he'd
rather be one syllable well lived than three syllables wasted.

"He had black eyes an' a way of tossin' his
hair back, though there wa'n't enough of it to interfere
with his sight. He owned half the Bascom farm, an
ax, a red sleigh, an' more plans than any six
men could carry. He planned a cider mill, a poultry
concern, a patent churn, an' a method of heatin' the
meetin'-house from underneath. Nothin' come of any of 'em, but
you never felt poor while he was tellin' you how
rich you'd be."

Priscilla smiled. She had heard in the village that Jot
Bascom had been charming, improvident, generous, and quite incapable of
knowing on Monday what had become of Saturday's wages. Village
opinion, which forgives waste more readily in the dead than
in the living, now regarded these qualities as marks of
genius.

"Did the two courtships continue together?" she asked.

"Near enough. Reuben called regular Wednesday an' Sunday. Jot called
whenever he happened to be passin', which was every night
after he found I'd made doughnuts. Reuben brought Lovice books—sermons,
poems, lives of missionaries. Jot brought me things he picked
up: a bird's nest, a queer stone, two yards of
ribbon he'd bought at auction without askin' the color, an'
once a young raccoon.

"Mother drew the line at the raccoon. It spent one
night in the woodshed, untied six brooms, et the soft
soap, an' went home by way of the chimney. Jot
said he'd thought it might amuse me.

"It did," Diadema added, "after a year or two."

She paused to sort the pieces, rejecting a strip of
purple and choosing a narrow blue one.

"This blue goes round the roses. It was our go-to-meetin'
cloak. One cloak between us, though we was grown girls.
When Lovice wore it, I stayed home; when I wore
it, she stayed home, unless the weather was mild enough
for the plaid shawl. Parson Cole thought we was one
girl with an irregular attendance. He preached at me three
times on constancy before he discovered there was two of
us.

"Religion in our house was plain Congregational, with a Baptist
grandmother, a Methodist aunt, an' one cousin that turned Adventist
every time there was a comet. We had enough doctrines
to keep conversation alive, but Mother said if we did
our work, told the truth, an' didn't slam doors, the
Lord might overlook our differences."

The butterfly at the center of the rug next received
attention. Its upper wings were to be black and gray;
the lower ones blue and brown; its body, according to
Diadema's plan, was represented by a gold braid much tarnished.

"That's our husbands," she said. "The black an' gray are
their neckties; the blue is a piece of Jot's weddin'
vest; the brown is Reuben's coat. The braid come off
a bonnet Lovice an' I shared when we was bridesmaids
for Cousin Elvira. It ain't strictly husband, but the butterfly
looked meagre without a body, an' I hold that art
has rights."

"Why a butterfly?" asked Priscilla.

"Because husbands is hard to symbolize. I thought of two
oxen, but that didn't seem affectionate; then two birds, but
Reuben couldn't sing an' Jot wouldn't stay in a nest.
A butterfly is cheerful, an' men go where they please,
an' there's no use tryin' to make either one perfectly
practical."

She began upon the left wing.

"When Reuben received a call to North Beulah, he went
straight to Father an' asked for Lovice. Father said he
hadn't any objection if Lovice hadn't, an' Lovice hadn't. Jot
heard of it by supper-time an' come over with his
hair damp from pump water.

"'We might as well make one celebration,' says he. 'Diadema,
will you marry me when Lovice marries Reuben?'

"'You haven't asked Father,' says I.

"'I'm askin' you first,' says he. 'Your father can't make
you marry me if you don't want to, an' if
you do want to, I can generally talk round a
man.'

"That was sensible, for Jot. I told him I'd consider.
I'd been considerin' two years, but a woman ought not
to let a man suppose time has no value.

"Father's only objection was Jot's prospects. Jot said his prospects
was magnificent.

"'Where are they?' Father asks.

"'Ahead,' says Jot.

"'How far?'

"'That depends how fast I travel.'

"Father laughed, an' once Father laughed he wa'n't any use
in business. So it was settled."

The double wedding, as Diadema reconstructed it from silk and
homespun, had occupied Brookvale for six months before and six
years after its occurrence. The twins were married in their
parents' parlor under an arch of ground-pine, with white chrysanthemums
at the windows and a bell of cotton batting suspended
from the central chandelier. The bell had leaned toward the
north throughout the ceremony and dropped, immediately after the benediction,
upon Deacon Petty's bald head; an incident interpreted by different
sects as omen, judgment, or defective wiring.

"We wore white," said Diadema, taking reverently from the basket
two pieces of silk, one ivory and the other yellowed
almost to amber. "Not grand silk, but good enough to
stand alone. Mother made both dresses, an' Aunt Nabby trimmed
'em. Mine had eleven little buttons down the back, Lovice's
thirteen, because Aunt Nabby miscounted. Lovice wanted me to have
one of hers, but I said no; if thirteen was
unlucky, she'd be safe married to a minister, an' if
eleven was unlucky, Jot could charm it out of itself.

"These bits go here, under the roses, where the stalk
divides. I thought first I'd make the whole background white,
but a weddin' ain't the whole of life, though girls
think so while it's happenin'."

"Were you married at the same moment?"

"Near's a minister can manage. Reuben stood with Lovice, Jot
with me. Parson Cole began Lovice's name first, then got
the answers crossed an' asked me if I'd take Reuben.
I says, 'No, sir,' so sharp that everybody jumped. Jot
whispered, 'Much obliged.' We commenced again.

"Afterward Reuben kissed Lovice as solemn as a covenant. Jot
kissed me twice an' said one was to grow on.
Mother cried into the cake, Father lost the key to
the carryall, an' Aunt Nabby packed both our nightgowns into
Lovice's trunk. It was a beautiful weddin'."

The rain had strengthened. Water ran from the eaves in
silver cords, and the room darkened about the two women;
but Diadema did not suggest a lamp. She seemed to
see the colors without light, as if each rag gave
off the radiance of its appointed hour.

"Lovice went to North Beulah," she continued, "an' I come
here to the Bascom place. We'd never slept apart more'n
three nights in our lives. The first mornin' after I
was married I woke before daylight an' reached across for
her. There was Jot, sound asleep, with one foot outside
the quilt. I cried a little, very still, so's not
to wake him.

"He woke.

"'Homesick?' says he.

"'No,' says I.

"'That's bad,' says he, 'for I am. I'm homesick for
you, an' you're right here.'

"You couldn't continue miserable with Jot. He'd interrupt."

Their married lives, like their girlhood, ran in likeness and
contrast. Reuben Granger's parish paid him four hundred dollars a
year, twenty cords of wood, and such donations as Providence
prompted the congregation to relinquish. Lovice inhabited a parsonage with
six cold rooms, received sewing-circle committees at inconvenient hours, entertained
visiting missionaries who ate enormously after denying the flesh in
public, and listened with becoming sympathy to spiritual troubles that
frequently originated in somebody else's pew.

Yet she was happy. Her letters, written in a fine,
sloping hand, told Diadema of choir disputes, baptisms, plum preserves,
Sunday-school picnics, and Reuben's sermons. She copied portions of the
sermons, which Diadema generally skipped, though she preserved every page.

"Lovice suited ministerin'," Diadema said. "She could hear the same
complaint from nine women an' make each one feel original.
Reuben worshipped her. I don't use that word careless, specially
about a minister; but he did. He'd come into a
room where she was, an' his face would settle, same
as muddy water when you leave the pail still.

"Jot loved me just as much, but not restfully. He'd
burst in sayin' he'd traded our old mare for a
cow, or loaned the hay money to a man goin'
west, or invited the district-school committee to supper. I never
knew whether to kiss him or take account of stock."

"And which did you do?"

"Both, generally. Marriage has to be managed with both hands."

She chose a strip of brown-and-white calico.

"This was my kitchen dress the year little Jot was
born. I had it let out twice an' took in
once, an' there's labor enough in them seams to build
a theology on. Jot wanted a boy from the start.
He said a girl would be welcome, but a boy
could carry on the Bascom name, which wa'n't in any
danger of dyin', there bein' forty-three Bascoms in the county.

"Lovice come to me a fortnight before my time. Reuben
fetched her in the chaise, wrapped in his buffalo robe,
an' looked so anxious you'd have thought she was the
one expectin'. She took hold of the house the minute
she entered. She cleaned cupboards I'd cleaned already, baked pies
nobody needed, aired the spare-room bed, an' made Jot fetch
water till he said the well was beginnin' to look
discouraged.

"'Lovey,' says I, 'you're doin' too much.'

"'Di,' says she, 'I've been waitin' all my life to
do too much for you.'

"That was her way."

Diadema bent close to the frame. The hook slipped once,
and Priscilla saw her draw the wool through again with
unnecessary care.

"Little Jot was born in a snowstorm. The doctor didn't
get here till after, because his horse cast a shoe
at Pike's turn. Mis' Bascom—Jot's mother—was here, but she lost
her head an' put the kettle on without water. Lovice
never lost hers.

"She held me, talked to me, prayed some, scolded me
some, an' told me lies about its bein' almost over.
There's lies that stand nearer heaven than truth, Priscilla. Women
know 'em.

"Afterward, when the baby cried, Jot cried louder. He went
downstairs an' shook hands with the doctor, the hired man,
an' a peddler that had stopped to ask the road.
Then he come back an' knelt by the bed.

"Lovice laid the baby in my arm. 'There, Di,' she
says. 'Here's somebody belongs to you more'n I do.'

"'Nobody can,' says I.

"She smiled, but she looked queer—pleased an' hurt together. I
didn't understand then. I had my baby."

The small rosebud near Diadema's flower was made from a
scrap of red flannel, the remnant of the child's first
cloak. The woman's fingers lingered over it, smoothing its frayed
edges before she set it into the burlap.

"He's in Ohio now," she said, returning to practical facts
as one closes a door against a draft. "Married, two
girls, does well in hardware. Writes first Sunday of every
month, unless business interferes, an' then his wife writes. He
wanted me to live with 'em, but I told him
my roots didn't travel. Besides, their house has furnace heat,
an' I never trust air I can't see where it
comes from."

Priscilla knew better than to smile. The bright little bud
had altered the rug. What had been decorative now possessed
relation and consequence; it leaned toward the twin roses as
a child leans toward a mother's knee.

Diadema laid down her hook and looked toward the rain-obscured
road.

"Three years after that, Lovice wrote she was expectin'. You
never saw such a letter. She tried to be moderate,
because a minister's wife ain't supposed to set joy above
submission; but every line danced. Reuben added a page at
the end, all about gratitude an' responsibility, but his pen
had dug holes in the paper.

"I went to North Beulah a month before. Jot said
he could manage. I baked him twelve loaves, fourteen pies,
a jar of beans, an' enough doughnuts to support a
prayer-meetin'. I left Mis' Bascom to look in on him.
When I come home, the beans was gone, but so
was the jar; he'd give both to a family movin'
through.

"Lovice met me at the parsonage gate. She was always
slight, an' the baby looked too great a burden for
her; but her face—" Diadema stopped. "Well, some folks have
happiness, an' some shine with it. She shone.

"She'd made everything ready: little shirts fine as cobwebs, bands,
petticoats, a cap with feather-stitchin', an' a cradle Reuben bought
secondhand from the Baptist minister, whose eighth child had outgrown
it. Eight children they had, all boys, an' Mis' Tupper
said Providence had plainly mistaken enthusiasm for ability.

"Lovice had lined the cradle with blue. She wanted a
boy. Not that she'd have slighted a girl, but Reuben
wanted a son, though he preached impartiality.

"At night we lay together as we used to, Reuben
sleepin' in his study. We talked till midnight. She asked
if it hurt much. I told her enough, but not
all. She asked if I'd been afraid.

"'Yes,' says I.

"'Did you think you'd die?'

"'Everybody thinks so once,' says I, 'but most don't.'

"She turned quiet. Then she says, 'If I do, Di,
you'll help Reuben with the baby?'

"I told her not to speak foolishness.

"'Promise,' says she.

"'There won't be need.'

"'Promise.'

"So I promised. You always promise at night what daylight
makes impossible."

The room had become so dim that Priscilla could no
longer distinguish red from brown in the basket. Still Diadema's
hand found the needed fragment: pale blue cashmere, softened by
age.

"This is the other bud," she said. "His cloak was
to be blue. He never wore it, but Lovice had
it ready."

She worked in silence for several minutes.

"The time come sudden. Reuben had gone to preach a
funeral twelve miles off. It was March, roads broke up,
mud to the hubs. I sent the neighbor boy after
the doctor an' got Mis' Tupper from next door.

"Lovice wa'n't frightened at first. She held my hand an'
says, 'Now I know how you felt.'

"'You'll know better by supper,' says I, because I thought
jokin' might hearten her.

"It went hard. Harder than with me. The doctor come
by afternoon, an' Reuben by dusk. He stood outside the
door an' prayed aloud till the doctor told him, not
unkindly, that if he couldn't pray quieter he'd better go
to the barn.

"Near midnight the child was born. A boy. Strong-looking, dark
hair, one little fist shut tight. He cried once, sharp
an' angry, as if the world had commenced by disappointin'
him."

Diadema's voice had lost its wandering cadence. Each sentence stood
alone.

"Lovice heard him.

"'Is he all right?' she asked.

"I said yes.

"'Let me see.'

"I brought him.

"She looked at him a long while, then at Reuben.
'He's yours,' she says.

"Reuben knelt down. She put her hand on his head.

"She didn't speak a word after that."

Priscilla's sewing had fallen unnoticed to the floor. Outside, a
wagon passed through the rain, wheels grinding heavily in the
road; the sound approached, labored beneath the window, and diminished
toward the bridge.

"She lived three days," Diadema continued. "If you call it
livin'. Her eyes opened sometimes, but I don't know as
she saw. Reuben never left the room. He'd read Scripture,
then stop in the middle an' look at her, as
if there must be another verse somewhere that would alter
things.

"The baby failed the second day. Doctor said he'd had
a poor start. I fed him with a spoon, warmed
bricks, wrapped him in wool, did everything. Reuben walked from
the bed to the cradle an' back till I thought
his feet would wear a path in the boards.

"Lovice went first, just before morning. There wa'n't any struggle.
One breath, then none.

"Reuben says, 'She is called home.'

"I says, 'This was her home.'

"I ought not to have said it to a minister,
an' him crazed with grief, but there's times when doctrine
rubs sorrow raw. He didn't rebuke me. He says, 'Yes,
Diadema. This was her home.'

"The baby lived till evening."

Diadema drew the pale blue strip through the foundation. Once,
twice, three times; the little bud took shape beside Lovice's
rose.

"They named him Reuben Lovell Granger. Reuben wanted my name
in it somehow, but I said no. The child belonged
to Lovice.

"They put 'em together. Mis' Tupper thought the baby ought
to have a little coffin of his own, but Reuben
says he'd never been away from his mother while he
lived an' shouldn't be after. So they laid him in
Lovice's arm.

"I fixed her hair. It curled at the ends same
as ever. I put on her weddin' dress, what the
moths found afterward. Reuben brought one white rose from a
greenhouse in Portland. Cost him more'n he could afford, likely.
He laid it between her hands and the baby's blanket.

"Folks said she looked peaceful. Dead folks always do, according
to folks. I thought she looked tired."

The rain softened. Somewhere below, the clock in the sitting
room struck five with an officious clang, each stroke seeming
to insist that ordinary time had rights even in the
presence of remembered death.

Priscilla rose, lighted the lamp, and set it on the
table without speaking. Its circle of yellow fell upon the
rug. The roses glowed; the butterfly's necktie wings looked both
absurd and gallant; the two buds, unequal in size, inclined
toward separate blossoms.

Diadema blinked behind her spectacles.

"There," she said, "I've told it without behavin' foolish. Pass
me them white pieces, will you?"

Priscilla found several strips at the basket's side. They were
of different substances: muslin, flannel, silk, linen, and one soft
piece of wool.

"What will these be?"

"Everlastin's. White flowers all round the outside. Some's from Lovice's
baby things; some's from her weddin' dress; this muslin is
a curtain from the parsonage spare room. I took it
down when Reuben left."

"He did not remain in North Beulah?"

"No. He preached one Sunday after the funeral. Text was,
'We see through a glass, darkly.' Folks said it was
powerful, but he never finished. Stood there in the pulpit,
lookin' at Lovice's empty pew, an' closed the Bible.

"He resigned next week. Went west as a missionary, though
there was heathen enough in North Beulah, accordin' to Mis'
Tupper. He wrote me for years. Never married again. Died
in Kansas of a fever.

"Jot said Reuben had gone west because the sun set
there, an' a man in grief naturally followed it. That
wa'n't geography, but it was Jot's kind of sense."

"You returned here after the funeral?"

"Yes. Jot come for me. I'd been gone near six
weeks. When I got out of the chaise, the kitchen
was full of neighbors scrubbin'. Jot had tried to keep
house, but he said objects multiplied when he wa'n't lookin'.
There was a boot in the pantry, flour in the
woodshed, an' a hen settin' in the warming oven. Mis'
Bascom had done what she could, but Jot tired her.

"He took one look at me an' didn't make a
joke. That frightened me worse than anything.

"That night I woke thinkin' I heard Lovice call. I
went all through the house. Jot followed with a candle,
never said I was foolish. When I come to myself,
I was standin' in the cold kitchen with the back
door open.

"'She ain't here,' I says.

"'No,' says he.

"'She never will be.'

"'Not so's we can see,' says he.

"Then he shut the door an' made a fire, though
it was three in the mornin'. He made coffee strong
enough to bear a spoon, burnt the toast black, an'
sat with me till daylight. Practical? No. Jot wa'n't practical.
But I've known practical men that slept through sorrow as
if it was rain on the roof."

The white flowers grew beneath her hook, crude, many-petaled shapes
set against a dark green field. Their naïveté might have
provoked amusement in another design; here it seemed an honest
language, invented because ordinary decoration had proved insufficient.

"Did you always keep Lovice's things?" asked Priscilla.

"Every scrap. Reuben sent me her trunk before he went
west. I aired it spring an' fall. Folded the dresses
with clean paper, put lavender in the sleeves, camphor in
the corners. For thirty-two years I kep' 'em.

"Then last Thursday I smelled moth.

"I knew. There's odors that tell the whole news at
once.

"I took the trunk upstairs, unlocked it, an' there was
dust where cloth had been. Lovice's green hood, her shoulder
shawl, the parsonage curtain, the baby cloak, her weddin' gown—all
fretted, tunneled, eaten. I sat on the floor.

"I don't often set on floors. It's a drafty habit.

"But I sat there an' held the sleeve. It come
apart in my hands.

"For a minute I hated the whole order of creation.
I says, 'What's the use savin'? What's the use lovin'
anything if time an' worms an' moths can have it?'

"Then I thought, 'They ain't had it yet.'

"So I shook out the pieces, carried 'em down, baked
every one in the oven slow to kill what was
left, brushed 'em, sorted 'em, an' began this."

"You transformed the damage," Priscilla said softly.

"I made a rug," replied Diadema, who distrusted abstractions when
a plain noun would serve. "You can't hook sorrow into
burlap, but you can hook in what sorrow would otherwise
destroy."

She worked faster now. The white everlastings passed around the
lower edge, climbed the right side, crossed the top, and
descended toward the beginning. No two were precisely alike. One
had six petals, another eight; one resembled a star, another
a snowball; yet their very irregularity gave them the look
of flowers gathered rather than manufactured.

"Why everlastings?" Priscilla asked.

"Because they keep. White for the weddin', white for the
buryin', white for—well, whatever comes after, if we're right about
it."

"You doubt it?"

"I don't doubt enough to boast of. I believe in
life everlastin' Sundays easy enough. Weekdays I have questions. When
a baby dies before he's learned his mother's face, an'
a woman dies gettin' him here, you can't say, 'It
was all for the best,' without knowin' more'n has been
revealed to you.

"Mis' Tupper said the sacrifice couldn't be useless because Lovice
was taken to a higher sphere. I told her Lovice
had a husband in this sphere an' a baby that
needed milk. Mis' Tupper never forgave me entirely.

"But I won't believe it was useless, either." Diadema's hook
stopped. "I won't. There must be some place where love
ain't wasted. If there ain't, the universe was put together
careless, an' I can't think that, not after studyin' a
common bean. So I make the flowers white."

A small practical noise followed: the snap of wool between
her fingers. It relieved the pressure in the room more
mercifully than speech.

By six o'clock the rain had ceased. The river showed
again through the willows, pale beneath the evening sky, and
wet roofs took on a subdued brightness. Lamps appeared in
neighboring houses—first at the Pitkins', then at the store, then
in Miss Tempy Cross's front room, where any light at
all was understood as an invitation to information.

Diadema tied off the final strip and leaned back.

"There."

The rug, finished save for trimming and binding, was not
beautiful according to any disciplined theory. Its perspective was innocent,
its colors uncompromising, and its butterfly considerably larger than both
roses together. Yet Priscilla, looking at it, saw neither faults
nor quaintness. She saw two children in green hoods, two
girls disputing a blue sash, two brides under a wavering
cotton bell; she saw Jot leaping a wall to the
destruction of lamp chimneys, Reuben quieting at the sight of
Lovice, a young mother holding her sister through snow and
pain, another young mother listening for the cry of her
only child. She saw a coffin containing a white-gowned woman
and the infant in her arms. Around all shone the
homely white assurance of flowers that would not fade.

Diadema rose stiffly, removed the rug from its frame, and
carried it to the bed. She spread a clean sheet
before laying the rug down, then placed another sheet above
it.

"Shall you put it in the parlor?" Priscilla asked.

"On the floor? Mercy, no. Folks would step on Lovice's
face, an' Deacon Petty grinds gravel in with his heels.
I might hang it on the north wall after I
line the back. No sun there."

"It deserves to be seen."

"Mebbe. But not by everybody that rings the bell. Mis'
Tupper's niece come here once an' set her teacup on
Father's Bible. There's people that can't distinguish sacred things unless
they're labeled."

In the following week the rug was lined with unbleached
cotton, bound with black braid, and suspended upon the north
wall of Diadema's bedroom. No direct sunlight touched it. A
narrow muslin curtain, contrived by Diadema, could be drawn over
it during the day; and when the chimney smoked or
sweeping was in progress, an additional sheet protected it from
danger. Priscilla privately thought these precautions excessive, but she had
lived long enough with Diadema to know that affection, deprived
of its object, may occupy itself with regulations.

Visitors were admitted selectively. Little Jot came from Ohio in
August with his wife and two daughters, and stood before
the rug while his mother explained every fragment. He laughed
at the butterfly, kissed Diadema, and afterward remained alone in
the room for some time. The girls admired the roses
and asked if they might touch the white flowers. Diadema
permitted one finger each, freshly washed.

Miss Tempy Cross heard of the rug before sunset and
reported that it contained the entire Granger genealogy, a portrait
of the deceased minister, and a map of North Beulah.
Diadema made no correction. She had found that falsehoods sometimes
die sooner when denied no nourishment.

Months passed. Winter laid clean snow along the road; spring
dissolved it into the customary mud; summer filled the east
window with maple leaves. The rug hung undisturbed, deepening rather
than fading in the chamber's guarded light. Priscilla often saw
Diadema pause before it, ostensibly to straighten the curtain or
inspect the binding. She never prayed there, never spoke Lovice's
name, never indulged any gesture that could be repeated as
touching by the village. She merely stood a moment and
went about her work.

One November afternoon, while the first storm worried at the
panes, Diadema brought the white shawl from the basket. It
was a fine wool shawl, scarcely worn, with a deep
fringe and a border woven in a pattern of lilies.

"My best," she said. "Little Jot give it to me
Christmas four years ago. Too white for common wear, too
handsome to waste. That's an awkward kind of present."

Priscilla looked up from the school compositions she was correcting.

"You should wear it to meeting."

"An' have the Watson child put a molasses peppermint against
it? No."

She folded the shawl lengthwise, smoothed it, and placed it
in the lower drawer of her bureau. Beside it she
laid a paper upon which directions had been written in
her firm hand.

"What are you doing?"

"Providin'."

"For what?"

"For the rug."

Diadema shut the drawer, then reopened it to make sure
the fringe had not caught.

"There's room round the outside for one more border," she
said. "Not now. Later."

Priscilla's pencil remained suspended above an essay on The Value
of Punctuality.

"I do not understand."

"Yes, you do. You're educated enough to understand what nobody
says."

Diadema crossed to the wall and drew back the muslin
covering. The twin roses, the necktie butterfly, the two buds,
and the white everlastings emerged into the gray afternoon.

"When my time comes," she said, "if this rug outlives
me, I want you should take that shawl an' cut
it in strips. Little Jot's wife won't know how, an'
the girls are bein' taught embroidery, which is good for
makin' holes but not for savin' 'em. You know my
stitch."

"I could not cut your shawl."

"You could after I'm gone. Cloth changes its feel then.
You found that out with your mother's things."

Priscilla did not answer. Her own mother's black silk lay
in a trunk upstairs, preserved for no use she could
name.

"Hook another row of everlastin's," Diadema continued. "All the way
round. Don't make 'em too regular. Natur' don't measure petals,
an' I never did favor stiff work. Use the fringe
in the corners if it don't look foolish. If it
does, use your judgment."

"You speak as though you expected to die soon."

"No. That's why I speak now. Dying folks have enough
to attend to without givin' sewin' directions. I'm sixty-eight, sound
except for rheumatiz, an' likely to trouble you years yet.
But moths taught me not to trust time with loose
ends."

She let the curtain fall over the rug and patted
it once, not tenderly, but to settle it against the
wall.

"Lovice is there," she said, "an' the baby, an' Reuben,
an' Jot, an' little Jot when he was little, an'
me most all the way through. One more white border,
Priscilla, made out o' my shawl—"

She paused, her hand resting on the covered roses.

"Then the story would be finished."

### The Apotheosis of an Onion

The life of Tiberius Braddock—called Tig by all persons who
wished to retain their breath—was preserved upon one memorable occasion
by an onion, a circumstance which will appear less ridiculous
after you have heard the onion's history, and perhaps more
ridiculous after you have heard Tig's.

He began existence in a market basket outside the Church
of Saint Malachy, wrapped in a gray shawl and protesting
against the climate with a voice out of all proportion
to his dimensions. His mother, whoever she may have been,
had entrusted him to Providence; Providence, having many departments and
very few clerks, passed him promptly to Nora Finnegan.

Nora had gone to early Mass to petition the saints
for a child. She had petitioned them for eleven years,
with that persevering confidence which, in less religious enterprises, gets
a person arrested for loitering. When she stumbled over the
basket, and the basket shouted, she looked first at the
church door and then at heaven.

"Well," said Nora, "ye've taken your time about it."

Thus Tiberius Braddock entered her household.

Nora was a washerwoman. Not a poor washerwoman bowed beneath
suds, steam, and the tyranny of linen. Not at all.
She was a washerwoman triumphant. She stood five feet ten
in her stockings, displaced atmosphere like an advancing frigate, and
could wring a sheet so dry that the twelve apostles,
had they been printed upon it, would have cried out
separately. Her arms were red, her heart was warm, and
her laugh—ah, that laugh!—could tear the cave where Echo lies
and bring the embarrassed nymph out by the ear.

She had been married twice. Her first husband had been
carried off by an accident—that is to say, the husband
was carried off; the accident remained and afterward married a
widow in Newark. Her second, a small man with large
theories concerning horse races, vanished one Tuesday with Nora's six
dollars and an umbrella belonging to the priest. Nora regarded
his departure less as bereavement than as surprise.

"But the umbrella," she would say, "was a cruel, cruel
blow."

From these experiences she had retained her name, her good
humor, and a lively distrust of men who knew what
a horse was thinking.

Into her basement rooms she carried Tig, basket, shawl, and
all. She fed him milk from a spoon, baptized him
unofficially with tears, and afterward had the ceremony performed by
a priest who possessed better Latin but no warmer heart.
The name Tiberius came from the priest, who had been
reading Roman history; Braddock came from the label on the
basket. Nora contributed Tig, which was cheaper and fitted him
sooner.

He grew red-haired, freckled, noisy, and lean only in theory,
for Nora believed that the human stomach was a furnace
established by God for the consumption of stew. Her kitchen
had one small window, two chairs, a blackened stove, and
an atmosphere in which hunger lost courage. Potatoes flourished there
after death; cabbage achieved a second and more aromatic existence;
and the onion, humblest of roots, was translated through butter
and gravy into a sphere where emperors might have envied
it.

Tig's favorite dish was one of Nora's own invention: onions
browned in drippings, laid upon bread, drowned—not murdered, observe, but
beatifically drowned—in thick gravy. She called it King's Supper.

"Because," she explained, "if the king had any sense he'd
eat it."

Tig ate it with the solemn concentration of a cardinal
examining a miracle.

Nora sent him to school in collars so fiercely starched
that his neck acquired moral discipline. He learned quickly. At
eight he read the newspaper; at ten he corrected it;
at twelve he announced that he would become a writer.

"A writer of what?" asked Nora, whose acquaintance with literature
consisted chiefly of laundry lists and the inscriptions on monuments.

"Books."

"Good books?"

"The greatest ever written."

Nora considered this modest beginning.

"Then eat another potato," she said. "Greatness is terrible hungry
work."

From that day she accepted literature as a trade, somewhat
cleaner than washing and rather less certain than burglary. She
bought Tig a secondhand volume of Shakespeare, which lacked the
first forty pages and all of *Macbeth*, but contained enough
kings, ghosts, lovers, fools, and unpaid debts to furnish his
imagination. Later came Dickens, Scott, Thackeray, Balzac, and a ragged
Villon whose criminal manners Nora condemned while admiring his appetite.

Tig wrote everywhere: on wrapping paper, margins, shirt-boxes, the backs
of bills. He produced tragedies in which red-haired foundlings inherited
castles, and comedies in which editors were trampled by grateful
subscribers. Nora listened while ironing, laughing at the wrong places
and weeping at those where Tig had intended a delicate
irony.

"You've the gift," she told him. "I don't know what
gift, exactly, but it makes the ironing go faster."

At sixteen he left school and obtained a position on
the *Evening Lantern*. His duties were to carry proofs, sharpen
pencils, fetch sandwiches, and remain invisible whenever the managing editor
had indigestion. Nevertheless he called himself a journalist, wore a
soft black tie, and cultivated despair after the manner of
young poets. Hope, perceiving his red hair and susceptibility, dressed
herself in Paris fashions and visited him nightly. Fame stood
just beyond tomorrow. Fortune was delayed by traffic.

Tig gave Nora his wages. Nora placed part of them
in a blue teapot on the highest shelf, where thrift
sat among cracked china like a severe domestic deity. Someday,
she declared, he should possess a room with sunlight and
a desk not previously used for sorting collars.

For five years they prospered. This means they paid the
rent nearly when due, ate meat on Sundays, and could
hear coal rattling in the bin without mistaking it for
celestial music. Happiness seldom issues printed notices of its presence;
it sits by the stove, mends a sock, and asks
for a second cup of tea.

Then, in a wet March, Nora caught cold.

A cold is a trivial fellow when it visits the
rich; it is given a warm room, doctors, broths, and
soft slippers, and soon departs from sheer boredom. But in
Nora's basement it discovered damp walls, unpaid rest, and a
woman who insisted upon finishing Mrs. Dalrymple's curtains. The cold
grew ambitious. It became pneumonia.

Nora, who had laughed at husbands, debts, broken boilers, and
the opinions of Protestants concerning fasting, could not laugh this
away. She sank, sank, day by day, while Tig sat
beside her and watched the great red hands grow still.

"Don't be frettin'," she whispered once. "There's stew in the
pot."

It was her final domestic order.

That night her breathing ceased. The room, deprived of Nora,
seemed suddenly too large for the world and too small
for grief.

Tig spent the savings in the blue teapot upon a
funeral with six black carriages, although there were not enough
mourners to fill two. Pride is often foolish, and red-haired
pride is practically deranged; but love had put on mourning
and would not be economical. Nora went to her rest
beneath flowers, candles, Latin, and a polished coffin she would
certainly have called an extravagance.

The following week the *Lantern* dismissed Tig. He had missed
four days, mislaid a proof, and told the managing editor
that his soul resembled a damp boot. Journalism will endure
many things, but not accurate description.

So Tig climbed to a garret in Cherry Street, carrying
a trunk of clothes, Nora's frying pan, Shakespeare without *Macbeth*,
and twelve dollars. There he resolved to live by fiction.

The garret possessed a sloping roof, a narrow bed, a
chair with one loyal leg, and a window overlooking chimneys
in all stages of moral collapse. In winter the wind
entered without knocking. Yet to Tig it was a tower
of art. Here Balzac should labor anew, Villon freeze more
respectably, and Fame, having consulted the directory, finally arrive.

A magazine announced a contest: one thousand dollars for the
best short story. Tig wrote for nine nights. His hero
was a lost prince; his heroine sold violets; his villain
owned an apartment house and therefore required little invention. On
the tenth morning he mailed the manuscript with his last
good stamp.

On the eleventh he became ill.

Rheumatic fever took possession of him with the efficiency of
a bailiff. His joints burned, his heart beat wildly, and
the ceiling acquired a habit of circling the bed. A
physician from the charitable dispensary came twice, prescribed warmth, nourishing
food, and rest—three luxuries not stocked in the establishment—and went
away looking professionally sorrowful.

Tig's only attendant was a boy called the Sparrow.

No one knew his true name. He sold newspapers, slept
wherever architecture afforded a ledge, and resembled a sparrow who
had been in a cyclone and had afterward lost an
argument with a cat. He was all elbows, eyes, and
loyalty. Tig had once given him a pair of shoes
and taught him the alphabet; these benefits the Sparrow repaid
at compound interest.

Each morning he climbed the stairs with a handful of
coal, a heel of bread, an apple too bruised for
commerce, or bones from which an optimist might infer soup.

"Where did you get it?" Tig would ask.

"Found it," said the Sparrow.

The city contains many things that can be found, especially
when shopkeepers are not looking.

For two weeks the boy kept fire in the grate
and something resembling life in Tig. Then one morning he
did not come. Nor the next. Nor the next.

The coal disappeared. The bread disappeared. Warmth retreated into memory.
Hunger entered, thin at first, then enormous, then intimate. It
sat on Tig's chest, whispered in his ear, and described
banquets. Despair followed, a black-coated gentleman with excellent arguments.

Snow gathered on the sill. Tig lay beneath two coats
and Nora's gray shawl—the very shawl in which she had
found him—and watched evening fill the room. He had eaten
nothing for three days. His manuscript had surely failed; the
Sparrow was dead; Nora was dead; and he, Tiberius Braddock,
detached citizen and lost pleiad of literature, would shortly become
an inconvenience to the landlord.

He closed his eyes.

Presently he smelled onions.

This was impossible. The house contained many odors, but none
so benevolent. The fragrance deepened: butter, bread, pepper, brown gravy—the
royal procession of King's Supper. Tig opened his eyes.

On the chair beside the bed stood a blue-rimmed dish.

Steam rose from it, curling into a pale halo. Upon
thick bread lay three onions, browned to gold, shining in
gravy. The dish was Nora's dish; he knew the chip
upon its rim. Yet he had left it behind in
the basement.

There came a rustle near the stove, the crisp, important
rustle of starched skirts. Then a low chuckle, warm and
round, traveled through the garret. It touched the ceiling, shook
the window, and vanished as a shell holds and releases
the sea.

Tig did not call out. Some miracles are frightened by
questions.

He raised himself. The spoon beside the dish was warm.
He ate.

O onion, bulb of the common earth, moon buried in
darkness, pearl in a beggar's cupboard! Poets have sung roses,
wine, nightingales, and ladies inconveniently married; but none of these,
at the proper hour, is half so useful as an
onion in gravy. This one underwent apotheosis. It was hearth,
mother, sacrament, and supper. Tig ate slowly at first, then
ravenously, then with tears dropping into the plate and improving
nothing but the sentiment.

When he finished, sleep took him—not the gray stupor of
starvation, but deep, healing sleep. He dreamed that Nora stood
beside a vast celestial wash-tub, arguing with Saint Peter over
the proper starching of angelic robes.

Morning came bright upon the snow.

Tig woke stronger. The dish was gone. On the chair
lay only the spoon, a drop of gravy, and one
translucent ring of onion.

At noon the postman hammered on the door and pushed
a letter beneath it. Tig crawled across the floor. The
envelope bore the magazine's name.

The editors informed Mr. Tiberius Braddock that his story, *The
Prince of Cherry Street*, had won first prize. A check
for one thousand dollars was enclosed.

Tig read the letter once, twice, and a third time,
because Fortune, when she finally arrives, often looks like forgery.
Then he laughed—a thin laugh, but unmistakably descended from Nora's—and
kissed the check.

Two days later, after a doctor had been summoned and
food purchased in quantities bordering upon theology, there came a
scratching at the door.

The Sparrow entered. He was pale, trembling, and thinner than
any boy should be without becoming transparent. Across his narrow
back he carried a sack containing perhaps six lumps of
coal.

Tig stared at him.

"Where have you been?"

"Sick," said the Sparrow. "Under the bakery steps. But I
found coal."

The sack slipped from his shoulder.

Tig caught him before he fell, lifted him onto the
bed, and wrapped Nora's gray shawl around his bones.

"Listen to me," he said. "You shall have soup, shoes,
a doctor, a room, and as many onions as your
constitution can survive. You shall go to school. You shall
sleep warm. You shall never—do you hear?—never be hungry again."

The Sparrow blinked. "Why?"

"Because you brought me coal."

"It weren't much."

"Because Nora brought me supper."

The boy looked toward the empty chair and, being acquainted
with hunger, accepted this explanation more readily than a philosopher
would have done.

Tig gripped his hand.

"By the shade of Nora Finnegan," he said, "while I
have bread, you eat."

And if an onion fragrance passed through the room just
then, though no pan stood upon the stove, and if
somewhere beyond mortal hearing a woman's laughter shook the cave
where Echo lies—well, Providence has been known to wink, and
Nora Finnegan had taught it how.

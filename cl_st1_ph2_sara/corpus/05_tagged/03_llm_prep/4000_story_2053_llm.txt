The Seven Policies

I first met the bald gentleman in a second-class carriage
between Ipswich and Manningtree. I say bald because this was
the first fact one observed concerning him, and, before the
journey ended, it became the most important.

He was a small, patient-looking man in a pepper-and-salt suit.
At every station he purchased a weekly newspaper. By the
time we left Colchester he possessed seven. He did not
read them. He folded each carefully, tore off a coupon,
wrote his name and address upon it, and placed the
paper under the seat.

"Fond of the Press?" I asked.

"No," he replied. "Railway insurance."

He explained that each journal insured its reader against death
or injury by train, provided the coupon had been properly
completed.

"You are insured in seven offices, then?"

"In a manner of speaking."

"What would happen if there were an accident?"

"My sister would receive seven thousand pounds."

"And you?"

"I should probably receive medical attendance."

He said this without enthusiasm, polished his spectacles with a
silk handkerchief, and returned them to his nose. In doing
so he disturbed a parcel upon the rack. From behind
it there slipped a large iron horseshoe, which descended edge
foremost upon the centre of his unprotected skull.

The noise was considerable. The gentleman bent forward, replaced his
spectacles, picked up the horseshoe, and regarded it reproachfully.

"Did it hurt?" I asked.

It was a foolish question. Had a three-pound piece of
iron fallen upon my head, I should have resented inquiry.

"A little," he said. "That is the way with them."

"With horseshoes?"

"With lucky things."

He wrapped the horseshoe in his silk handkerchief and placed
it on the seat beside him.

"I found it outside Ipswich station," he continued. "A porter
told me to pick it up. He said it would
bring me luck. I ought to have known better."

A swelling was already appearing upon his head.

"You do not believe in luck?"

"I believe in it," he said. "I don't like it."

He looked through the window for a few moments, as
though his past might be running beside the train, and
then settled himself in the corner.

"I was born on a Wednesday," he said.

I admitted that this did not seem extraordinary.

"It is supposed to be the fortunate day. Full of
woe is Saturday's child, and so on. My family made
a great deal of Wednesday. Whenever anything was to be
divided, they said, 'Never mind Alfred; he was born on
a Wednesday.' My elder brother had Uncle Matthew's business because
he was delicate. My younger brother had Aunt Rebecca's cottage
because he had no head for business. My cousin Charles
had Grandfather's money because he was born on a wet
Sunday and had always been unlucky. I had nothing because
Providence had already taken care of me."

"And did Providence do so?"

"No. But the family were satisfied."

He touched the swelling experimentally and continued.

"When I was twenty-three, a black cat came to me.
It followed me for three streets and entered my lodging.
The landlady said it was the luckiest thing that could
happen to a young man."

"And was it?"

"It ate a canary."

"Yours?"

"No. That was the difficulty."

The canary, it appeared, belonged to a Miss Amelia Price,
a young woman to whom he was then paying serious
attention. Miss Price had entrusted the bird to him while
she visited an aunt at Clacton. She valued it because
it had once sung during a sermon delivered by her
late father.

"I put it high up," said the bald gentleman, "and
shut the door. But the cat opened doors. It was
an animal of unusual determination."

"How did Miss Price take it?"

"She took the cat to the river in a basket
and me out of her affections. She said a man
who could not protect a canary was not fitted to
protect a wife. It was unjust. I pointed out that
a wife is generally larger and better able to call
for assistance. That made matters worse."

He paused.

"Piebald horses are lucky too," he added.

"So I have heard."

"I once had charge of one."

He had obtained employment with a wholesale stationer near Liverpool
Street. His duties were chiefly clerical, but on Friday afternoons
he carried samples to certain customers in the eastern districts.
For this purpose his employer supplied him with a small
cart and a piebald horse named Emperor.

"Emperor had belonged to a publican," he said. "No one
mentioned it when we bought him. The first Friday he
took me into the yard of the Green Man. I
pulled at the reins, but he backed the cart under
the tap-room window and refused to move."

"What did you do?"

"I went in to ask if anyone understood horses. They
said he would be easier after I had had something.
I took a little whisky. Then the landlord came out
with me and gave Emperor half a biscuit. We got
away in twenty minutes."

On the following Friday Emperor entered the Coach and Bells.
On the Friday after that he stopped at the King's
Head, the Pig and Whistle, and the Royal William.

"He knew every public house from Aldgate to Barking," said
my companion. "At first I resisted. Resistance excited him. He
would stand broadside in the road and hold up an
omnibus. People advised me to humour him. I did. In
two months I had acquired habits that surprised my mother,
and in three months I had lost my situation. My
employer said a commercial traveller ought not to return at
midnight singing to the samples."

"You blamed the horse?"

"Partly. My employer blamed me altogether. That is the difference
between employers and employés."

He rubbed his head again. The swelling had grown to
the dimensions of a hen's egg, though he appeared less
concerned about it than I was.

"But the worst case," he said, "was the goose."

I waited.

"It was given to me."

"By a friend?"

"By my governor. That was what made it so unexpected."

This occurred, he explained, on Christmas Eve, when he was
working for a firm of manufacturers in Clerkenwell. He had
been with them nearly two years and was then engaged
to a young woman named Maria Tibbett, the daughter of
a respectable cabinet-maker.

"At half-past five the governor called me in. I thought
he meant to raise my salary. Instead he said, 'Merry
Christmas, Barlow,' and gave me a goose."

"Was it a good goose?"

"It was enormous. I have never seen a bird so
much larger dead than alive. It was wrapped in brown
paper and tied with cord, but the neck came out
at one end and both feet at the other. The
office boy said it weighed thirty-six pounds. That was absurd,
of course. It could not have been more than twenty-nine."

He had no use for it. He lived in one
room in Camden Town, and his fireplace was suitable for
boiling an egg if the egg were not ambitious. Maria's
family had already purchased a turkey. His mother was spending
Christmas with people who kept geese themselves.

"I said to the governor, 'Thank you, sir.' There was
nothing else to say. One cannot refuse a Christmas goose
without appearing republican. I carried it downstairs."

At the street door he met Peake, the chief clerk,
who congratulated him.

"'That is a bit of luck,' Peake said. 'The governor
must think well of you.'

"I asked Peake if he would buy it.

"'What should I do with a goose?' he said.

"'Eat it.'

"'We're having pork.'

"I offered it for five shillings.

"'Don't like goose,' he said, and went home."

The bald gentleman carried the bird into Farringdon Road. It
grew heavier at every crossing. People smiled at him, and
small boys followed, calling observations concerning its legs.

He entered a poulterer's shop and laid it upon the
counter.

"'How much will you give me for this?' I asked.

"The poulterer looked at the goose and then at me.

"'Where did you get it?'

"'It was presented to me.'

"'Ah,' he said.

"I disliked his 'Ah.' It contained a police court and
two witnesses.

"'It was given me by my employer,' I explained.

"'Then I should keep it, if I were you.'

"'I cannot cook it.'

"'Get married.'

"I told him I was shortly to do so.

"'Then begin practising obedience,' he said, and pushed the goose
back."

The bird left a greasy mark upon the counter. The
poulterer demanded twopence for cleaning it. My companion paid and
departed.

A little farther on he met a man who offered
to purchase the goose for sevenpence halfpenny.

"I was offended," he said. "The string had cost nearly
that. I told him the bird was worth fifteen shillings."

"Was it?"

"I had no idea. But one must uphold the dignity
of a gift."

The man raised his offer to eightpence on condition that
the feet were included. They separated without agreement.

By then it was growing dark and beginning to rain.
The brown paper softened. Whenever my companion shifted the parcel
from one arm to the other, the neck swung out
and struck him on the knee.

He entered the Duke of Cumberland for refreshment and asked
the landlord whether he knew anyone requiring a goose.

"'Depends,' said the landlord. 'Is it alive?'

"'Certainly not.'

"'Then old Jenkins won't want it. He buys live things.'

"A gentleman at the bar said we might raffle it.
There were twelve of us, and we agreed upon sixpence
a chance. I had to take one chance myself, to
inspire confidence."

The names were placed in a pewter pot. The landlord
drew the winner. It was Barlow.

"That seems unusually unfortunate," I said.

"No," replied the bald man. "It was luck. They all
said so."

There was a dispute concerning the sixpences. The other competitors
held that, as owner of the goose, he could not
also claim the pool. The bald gentleman maintained that a
raffle was sacred.

"We discussed it for some time. Finally I accepted ninepence
and a glass of rum. I am not accustomed to
rum, and it made the goose seem less heavy."

He resumed his journey. Outside the public house a cabman
offered him a shilling for the bird, but by then
its market value had risen in his mind.

"I said twelve shillings. He said he would give eighteenpence
if I carried it to Paddington. I told him what
I thought of him. He said something about the goose
which I did not understand, and drove off."

At Holborn he decided to give the bird away. This
proved more difficult than selling it.

He offered it first to an elderly woman with a
basket.

"She asked whether it was wholesome.

"I said, 'Madam, it is a Christmas present.'

"She said that was no answer."

He approached a crossing-sweeper, who refused it because his wife
had gone to prison that morning and had taken the
frying-pan with her. A flower-seller suspected a trick. A boy
accepted the goose, but his mother emerged from a doorway,
struck him on the ear, and compelled him to return
it.

"People have no confidence," said my companion. "That is what
makes generosity so exhausting."

At the corner of Gray's Inn Road he entered another
public house. The rain had penetrated the wrapping, and the
landlord objected to the goose being placed upon a chair.

"'That bird's dripping,' he said.

"'So am I,' I replied.

"'You can stand on the floor.'

"I had a little brandy. I am not used to
brandy, and it affected my judgment."

A cheerful red-faced man suggested that he take the goose
to a charitable institution.

"'There's an orphanage in Bloomsbury,' he said. 'Orphans like goose.'

"I said, 'How many orphans?'

"'About forty.'

"I asked what was the good of one goose among
forty orphans. He said it would teach them resignation."

Another man proposed sending it to the Prime Minister. A
third offered to hold it while Barlow wrote the address.
When Barlow looked round, the man and goose had disappeared.

"I was delighted," he said. "I had never before experienced
the joy of losing property."

He bought the company drinks.

Five minutes later a constable entered, carrying the goose by
the legs. He wished to know whether it belonged to
anyone present.

"The man who had taken it said it was mine.

"'Then why was it in a doorway?' asked the constable.

"I explained that it had been given to me by
my governor.

"'Your governor lives in a doorway?'

"I said he was a manufacturer in Clerkenwell.

"'Then you had better take his goose home,' said the
constable.

"I told him I had been trying to do so
since half-past five.

"'Where do you live?'

"I could not remember for the moment. It is extraordinary
how brandy affects addresses. I knew it was Camden Town,
but the number escaped me."

The constable advised him to walk quietly until the number
returned. The goose was restored to his arms.

By this time its wrapping had split along the back.
Someone in the public house repaired it with newspaper and
red string, so that the parcel now resembled an article
forwarded anonymously to a Cabinet minister.

"I thought of taking it to Maria," he continued. "She
lived near the Caledonian Road. But Maria's father disliked my
calling after ten, and it was then nearly eleven. Besides,
I had promised to abstain from spirits until our marriage."

"Had you taken much?"

"Hardly anything. A whisky, a rum, a brandy, and two
glasses of something hot which they said would keep out
the cold. I was perfectly aware of what I was
doing, except when I moved suddenly."

He walked north. Near King's Cross he entered a third
public house to ask the correct road. The room was
crowded. To protect the goose, he placed it beneath a
bench beside another brown-paper parcel.

"Was that wise?" I asked.

"Nothing was wise by then."

A man in a muffler told him he was going
in precisely the wrong direction. Another said that Camden Town
no longer existed, having been pulled down for railway purposes.
They disputed this warmly. My companion joined the discussion on
the ground that he lived there and ought to know.

"The man asked me to prove it. I offered to
take him home. He said no gentleman would invite a
stranger home at that hour unless he meant robbery. I
called him a liar. After that there was confusion."

The landlord ordered everyone out. Barlow seized the parcel under
the bench and reached the street. It appeared heavier than
before, but he attributed this to fatigue and moisture.

At the next corner he conceived the idea of leaving
it in a cab.

"I hailed one and asked the fare to Camden Town.
The cabman said four shillings.

"I said, 'Take this goose instead.'

"He said he drove men, not livestock.

"I pointed out that it was dead.

"'So'll you be if you don't get out of the
road,' he said."

My companion apostrophised the universe. A second constable advised him
not to do so.

"I explained the whole matter. I told him I had
been burdened with a goose through no fault of my
own; that commerce had rejected it, charity distrusted it, friendship
deserted it, and the licensed trade had taken advantage of
it.

"He said, 'Move along.'

"I asked him whether the law could compel a British
subject to retain a goose.

"He said he would compel me to retain my liberty
for another minute if I moved along at once."

Barlow moved along, but not, apparently, in a straight line.

Presently he reached the Regent's Canal. The rain had stopped.
The water below looked black, quiet and confidential.

"I reasoned with myself," he said. "The bird was dead.
Throwing it into the canal could cause it no pain.
Fish might eat it. If fish did not eat it,
scientific persons might learn something from its decomposition. In any
event, it would cease to be my goose."

He rested the parcel on the parapet.

At that moment the constable appeared again.

"'What are you doing there?'

"'Nothing,' I said.

"'What's in that parcel?'

"'Laundry.'

"He came toward me. I do not know why I
said laundry. It was the first respectable thing that occurred
to me."

Fearing the goose would be restored yet again, Barlow lifted
the parcel and flung it into the canal.

There was a heavy splash.

The constable seized him by the collar.

"'What have you thrown in?'

"'A goose,' I said.

"'You said laundry.'

"'It was a goose when I last looked at it.'"

A whistle brought another policeman. A boatman was roused, hooks
were obtained, and after twenty minutes the parcel was drawn
from the water.

"They opened it beneath a lamp," said my companion. "It
was not the goose."

He looked at me gravely.

"It was a baby."

I confess that I found nothing to say.

"A dead baby," he added, as though accuracy demanded the
qualification.

The swelling upon his head had now become quite remarkable.

"The police did not believe my explanation. They said men
did not usually carry geese wrapped like babies and then
throw them into canals. I said I had not wrapped
it like a baby. I had not even known it
was a baby. They asked where the goose was. That
was just what I wanted to know."

He was conveyed to the station. There, with an inspector,
two constables and a doctor listening, he narrated his evening.

"The inspector said no innocent man could have visited so
many public houses in so short a time. I explained
that I was unaccustomed to drink. He said that made
it worse.

"They asked my address. I remembered the street but gave
Maria's number. They sent for her father."

Mr. Tibbett arrived at two in the morning. Having identified
Barlow, he declared that he had long expected something of
the kind, though he could not say exactly what kind.
Maria came with him and fainted when told of the
parcel.

"I tried to reassure her. I said, 'It is not
my baby, Maria.'

"She replied that this was an unworthy evasion."

He remained in custody throughout Christmas Day and Boxing Day.
Dinner consisted of beef and potatoes.

"Not goose?" I asked.

"No. That would have been insulting."

The mystery was eventually explained. The brown-paper parcel left beneath
the bench at the King's Cross public house had belonged
to a frightened young woman who had concealed the death
of her infant. In the disturbance she had carried away
the goose and abandoned it later in a railway waiting-room.
The red string, the newspaper wrapping and the testimony of
the landlord established the exchange.

"What became of the goose?"

"The stationmaster ate it."

"And you were released?"

"After four days. The magistrate said the serious suspicion was
removed, but fined me ten shillings for being drunk and
disorderly. My governor dismissed me for bringing the firm into
disrepute. Maria married a piano-tuner. Her father said a man
who mistook a baby for a goose was not fitted
to have a family."

The train began to slow.

"So," said the bald gentleman, gathering his seven newspapers, "when
people offer me lucky objects, I prefer them to keep
them."

He attempted to put on his hat. The swelling prevented
it. He turned the hat sideways, tried the back first,
and finally rested it delicately upon the summit, where it
remained for three seconds before rolling beneath the seat.

I recovered it for him.

"You had better carry it," I suggested.

"Yes," he said, taking the hat in one hand and
the horseshoe in the other. "I do not think much
of luck."

The train stopped. He stepped onto the platform, where a
porter, seeing the horseshoe, smiled.

"Found that, sir? Lucky thing, a horseshoe."

The bald gentleman looked at him, looked at the hat
he could not wear, and passed on without trusting himself
to reply.

We were taking thirty-two hundred head of mixed Kansas and
Texas cattle north in the summer of '78, when beef
on the hoof was worth more in Nebraska than it
was below Red River, and a man with a good
rain, a fair cook, and no stampede for a week
considered Providence friendly to the cattle trade. Our herd carried
the Lazy L brand, though there were enough road brands
and blotched irons among them to make a tax collector
suspicious, and they ranged from yearlings no bigger than jack-rabbits
up to six-year-old steers with horns wide enough to hang
a blanket on.

We had crossed the Canadian in good order and were
moseying up through the Territory, keeping the herd long and
loose on fair days and drawing it compact whenever clouds
piled in the west. Old Ike Inks was foreman. Ike
had driven the trail so often that he could tell
the country by the taste of the dust, and he
knew every ford, seep, alkali hole, abandoned camp, and lone
cottonwood between Red River Station and Abilene. He was narrow
in the shoulders, gray in the whiskers, and slow in
his speech. He never used two words where one would
do, and sometimes he would not use the one.

That year Ike hired a boy at the last ranch
before we struck the trail. The boy's name was Lucius
Weller, but somebody called him Lucy before he had been
with us half a day, and it fitted him so
neatly that his lawful name never caught up again. He
was about nineteen, long-legged and mild-eyed, with new boots, a
clean red handkerchief, and questions enough to run a newspaper.

Lucy was not lazy, which saved him from being disliked.
He would ride his circle, hold his side of the
herd, gather strays, and stand his turn at night guard
without shirking. But he wanted a reason for everything. He
asked why the point men rode so far ahead, why
a drag rider tied a handkerchief over his mouth, why
the cook always camped on the leeward side, why some
steers walked with the leaders and others hunted the brush,
and why a horse would cross a river after another
horse but fight the bit if asked to enter alone.

The first week the boys answered him. The second week
they lied to him. By the third week he had
accumulated enough false information to have written a government report.

Stubb was responsible for a good share of it.

Stubb was a short, round man with a face as
good-natured as a pumpkin and a laugh that began in
his boots. He could drink more whiskey than any man
we knew without losing either his seat or his manners,
though after the third glass he was likely to sing,
and after the fifth he would insist that every horse
within reach had once belonged to him. He had cooked,
punched cattle, bought horses, dealt monte, and bossed trail outfits,
according to his own count, and there was no event
of importance west of the Mississippi that he had not
either witnessed or prevented.

He rode our off point and was useful there. Cattle
liked him. Men did too, unless they made the mistake
of believing him.

One warm afternoon we nooned near a pair of low
sand hills where the main trail divided. The older trace
bent west around a stretch of broken ground. A second
trail, narrower but plain as wagon ruts, struck almost due
north, ran several miles apart from the first, and then,
as Ike told us, joined it again beyond the next
creek.

Lucy had noticed the two tracks since morning. He had
also noticed three shallow, weather-beaten graves on the south side
of the sand hills, each marked by a stone at
the head and another at the foot. Curiosity worked on
him until he could no more hold it than a
fresh horse can hold a snort.

While we sat in the scant shade of the bed
wagon eating beans and sour-dough biscuits, he turned to Ike.

"Mr. Inks," he said, for Lucy still called the foreman
mister, "why do them trails split here when they both
go to the same place?"

Ike scraped his plate with a biscuit. "Ask Stubb."

Stubb looked up slowly, his mouth full of beans.

"Did you make that other trail, Stubb?" Lucy asked.

Every man around the wagon became interested in his plate.

Stubb swallowed, wiped his mustache, and looked toward the north
fork with the sorrowful expression of a man who has
been reminded of an old wound.

"I don't commonly refer to that matter," he said.

Ike coughed into his hat.

Lucy glanced from Stubb to the graves. "Was there trouble?"

"Trouble?" Stubb repeated. "No, son. I wouldn't call it trouble.
Trouble is when a man's cinch breaks in a swollen
river, or his cook quits forty miles from flour, or
his boss-man sends him north with three thousand wild steers
and fourteen hands that can't tell a rope from a
bed cord. What happened here was only a little disagreement
concerning the right of way."

The boys settled more comfortably. One-eyed Jim, who had heard
Stubb's tale in other forms, rolled a cigarette and stared
at the horizon. Ike scraped his plate again, though there
was nothing left on it.

Lucy said, "Did that disagreement have anything to do with
them graves?"

Stubb sighed.

"You see," he said, "that's what comes of young fellows
asking historical questions. A man can't answer one without uncovering
circumstances he'd sooner leave planted. But since you've observed the
trail, and likewise the graves, and since ignorance is liable
to get a boy killed quicker than mean whiskey, I'll
tell you enough to clear your mind. I don't want
it repeated as a brag. I never was a bragging
man."

Nobody contradicted him.

"Well," Stubb began, "it was five years ago this very
month, lacking maybe eleven days, when I come through here
with thirty-eight hundred and sixty-one head belonging to old Colonel
Barstow of the Crooked B. There was thirty-eight hundred and
sixty-three when we left Texas, but one steer broke his
neck in a washout and another got bit by a
rattler and swelled up until his hide wouldn't fit him.
I don't count dead cattle. Some trail bosses do, so
their books will look fat, but I always figured an
employer wanted beef delivered instead of arithmetic.

"They were a choice herd—mostly fours, with about six hundred
fives and sixes, long-legged, broad-backed cattle that could walk from
sunrise to moonrise and gain weight doing it. Their horns
averaged five feet from tip to tip. I measured enough
of them to know. The colonel had bought them cheap
after a dry winter, and he says to me, 'Stubb,
take those cattle to Abilene, lose none you can save,
save none that ought to be lost, and don't let
any man crowd my brand off the trail.'

"'Colonel,' I says, 'I'm peaceful by upbringing, but faithful by
disposition.'

"'That's why I hired you,' he says.

"The outfit I picked was small but capable. There was
Big Dick Rawlins on right point, a man so tall
he could look over a standing steer without lifting his
chin. One-eyed Jim rode left point. Not this Jim here.
This Jim has lost the wrong eye. My Jim had
the right one missing, which made him see around a
left-hand bend better than any man alive.

"I had Cherokee Bill and Smoky Davis on the swings,
Little Tom and Buckskin Joe on the flanks, and two
brothers named Hale in the drag. The Hale boys were
twins, except one was four years older than the other,
and neither knew which, their mother having forgot during a
hard winter. My horse wrangler was a Mexican boy called
Angel, who could catch any horse ever foaled, provided it
belonged to somebody else. Our cook was old Andy Scroggins.

"Andy was sixty if he was a day, and he
had made coffee for soldiers, buffalo hunters, freight outfits, surveying
parties, and one traveling preacher who left the church after
eating Andy's biscuits. Andy had only three fingers on his
left hand and claimed he lost the other two fighting
Comanches, but Jim maintained they had gone into the flour
sack and never come out. Andy was touchy about criticism.
We always praised the coffee before asking what was in
it.

"Besides those, I carried a roper named Sam Ketchum. Sam
wasn't much to look at. He was little and dry,
with a neck like a branding iron, and he seldom
spoke because words interfered with his tobacco. But give him
sixty feet of grass rope and a fair horse, and
he could catch a bluebird by either hind leg without
ruffling its feathers. Remember Sam. He comes into this account
where accuracy becomes important.

"We left Texas in April, crossed Red River swimming, and
had a good drive up to the Washita. At Red
River I lost a horse named Pilgrim, not drownded exactly,
but persuaded to remain under water by a saddle that
had no sense. Pilgrim was a bay gelding with one
white stocking and a disposition like a widowed hen. He'd
swim if you pointed him downstream, but he objected to
going across. I told Buckskin Joe not to ride him,
but Joe was young and believed a horse ought to
obey because a man owned spurs.

"Halfway over, Pilgrim swapped ends, got his head under Joe's
knee, and commenced pawing for Texas. Joe left the saddle
and came ashore hanging to a steer's tail. The steer
objected some, but it was too busy swimming to hold
a meeting. Pilgrim went down with my best forty-dollar saddle
and a yellow slicker tied behind the cantle.

"I mention that to show the condition of my mind.
A man who has just lost a forty-dollar saddle is
not hunting additional difficulties.

"North of the Canadian we overhauled another herd carrying a
Turkey Track road brand. There were maybe twenty-seven hundred cattle
in it, common stuff, mixed ages, with sore feet and
bad notions. They were bossed by a fellow named Rance
Spiller. Perhaps you've heard of him."

Lucy shook his head.

"You're fortunate," Stubb said. "Rance was a wide man with
narrow ideas. He had a black beard, pale eyes, and
a voice that made every statement sound like a threat
against the listener's family. He wore two pistols, not because
he could use either, but because one pistol would have
left the other side of him lonely.

"His outfit had started three days ahead of us, but
they traveled like hogs crossing a frozen pond. They would
spread their cattle over two miles of grass, nooning at
every creek, bedding down before sundown, and blocking every crossing
as if they owned water by the barrel.

"We caught them below Salt Fork. I rode ahead and
found Rance sitting on a gray horse while his herd
watered. The leaders were in the river, the tail end
was still half a mile back, and they had cattle
wandering both banks. A proper outfit waters in strings. You
let two or three hundred in, move them out, and
bring the next bunch forward. That keeps the banks from
bogging and the cattle from milling. Rance had dumped his
whole herd into the crossing like a cook emptying dishwater.

"I says, 'Good morning. Are you watering cattle or damming
the river?'

"He looked me over. 'Who wants to know?'

"'A thirsty citizen with thirty-eight hundred and sixty-one dependents.'

"'They can wait.'

"'They have waited since dawn.'

"'Then they're practiced.'

"Now, I could see he was a humorous man, only
his humor had died and he hadn't discovered the corpse.
So I says, 'Mr. Spiller, my herd walks faster than
yours. If you'll give me the crossing, I'll pass you
and be out of your dust by tomorrow.'

"He says, 'Nobody passes me on this trail.'

"'Is that a rule?'

"'It is now.'

"'Did Congress make it?'

"'I did.'

"'Then Congress has improved some in looks and declined badly
in intelligence.'

"He put his hand on his right pistol, but he
did it slow, like a man feeling whether he had
buttoned his pocket. I kept both hands on my saddle
horn. A fellow that reaches first often reaches wrong, and
I never encouraged nervousness in public.

"After a while he says, 'You turn your cattle aside
and wait until I'm gone.'

"'How long will that be?'

"'Till Abilene.'

"'I dislike waiting in Abilene for a man who hasn't
arrived,' I says. 'But I'm not on the prod today.
Water your cattle, and we'll discuss the road tonight.'

"I took my herd upstream and found a crossing of
our own. The banks were steep, so we cut them
down with shovels, and by noon every beast had drunk.
Then we grazed east of Rance's dust and made eight
miles before bedding.

"Next morning we came up behind him again. He was
moving at a rate that would shame a sick turtle.
I sent Jim ahead to ask passage. Rance refused. I
sent Big Dick with my compliments and an offer to
hold my herd while he moved his cattle aside. Rance
told Dick that if another Crooked B man came near
his point, he'd trim his ears.

"Big Dick rode back mighty insulted. His ears were his
best feature.

"'Boss,' he says, 'I'd like permission to shorten that man.'

"'Not yet,' I says. 'Colonel Barstow hired us to deliver
cattle, not make alterations in strangers.'

"So we held back all day. The steers fretted because
they could smell the herd ahead, and cattle don't enjoy
walking in another herd's drag. Dust gets into their eyes
and lungs, and the weaker stock drops back among them.
By sunset our leaders were pushing, our drag was cussing,
and the whole herd had that restless roll which tells
a trail boss he may soon have cattle in every
county.

"We camped three miles behind Rance. I rode forward after
supper, taking only Jim and Big Dick, not because I
expected difficulty but because they expected me to expect it.

"Rance sat by his fire drinking coffee. His men were
scattered around, ten or eleven of them, a mighty rough
collection. One had a green blanket tied around his waist,
one wore a cavalry coat without sleeves, and another had
a scar from ear to chin that looked as if
somebody had tried to open him like an envelope. They
all watched us ride in.

"I got down and said, 'Evening, gentlemen.'

"Rance said, 'You've been told to keep back.'

"'I have. I came to inquire how far back. Texas
is a large state, and I don't want to misunderstand.'

"His men laughed a little, but they looked at him
before they did it.

"Rance says, 'You'll stay behind my cattle.'

"'Your cattle ain't traveling.'

"'They're traveling enough.'

"'Not for my purposes. I've got beeves for market. You
appear to be conducting a funeral, only the corpse keeps
walking.'

"He stood up. 'Are you calling my outfit slow?'

"'No, sir. Slow is a motion, and I haven't got
sufficient proof.'

"That scar-faced man began to rise, but Big Dick looked
down at him. Dick was six feet five and had
arms like wagon tongues.

"'Set comfortable,' Dick told him. 'Standing won't improve you.'

"The man sat back.

"I explained to Rance that trail courtesy allowed a faster
herd to pass when grass and water made room. I
offered to swing west the next morning if he would
hold his cattle compact for two hours. I further offered
him one of Andy's apple pies, which was a serious
sacrifice, since Andy baked only when he had lard and
a forgiving God.

"Rance says, 'There'll be no Crooked B cattle ahead of
me.'

"'Why?'

"'Because I say so.'

"'That's a reason shaped like a fence post,' I told
him, 'but it don't reach deep enough to stand.'

"He stepped close. 'Listen, fat man. You try to pass,
and I'll scatter your herd from here to the Arkansas.'

"I was slimmer then, Lucy. Considerable slimmer. It was the
anxiety of command. Still, I didn't correct him, because a
man who is wrong in every particular can't be repaired
in one evening.

"I says, 'Mr. Spiller, I've asked you friendly, and I've
asked you as one trail boss to another. Tomorrow I'm
going around. If you leave room, nothing happens. If you
don't leave room, something does. You're entitled to choose the
something.'

"Then I turned to his cook. 'How's your coffee?'

"The cook said, 'Poor.'

"'Honest man,' I says. 'Come over to our camp when
you get tired of these people.'

"We mounted and rode away. Nobody shot. I reckon they
were too impressed with our manners.

"The next morning I called the boys in before daylight.
We ate quick, saddled every sound horse, and threw the
herd off the bed ground at first gray. Rance's camp
lay four miles north. The country west of him was
rough with draws and sand, while the east side opened
into rolling grass for maybe ten miles before striking timber.
The regular trail ran north by west. I aimed north
by east.

"'Boys,' I says, 'we're making a road.'

"Big Dick asked, 'How far does it go?'

"'To Abilene if necessary.'

"Old Andy stuck his head out of the wagon. 'You
want breakfast on it?'

"'I expect breakfast every day.'

"'Then don't make it too crooked,' he says.

"We moved at sunup. Jim and Dick took the leaders,
gave them air, and pointed them east of Rance's drag.
I rode forward with Sam Ketchum. The swing men pressed
in just enough to keep our cattle from drifting toward
the Turkey Track herd, while the flankers stretched the rear
into a walking line. Within an hour we were traveling
alongside Rance, half a mile east, the two herds like
two brown rivers running north.

"Rance came boiling across the grass with four men behind
him. His beard looked blacker than ordinary.

"'Turn them cattle!' he shouted.

"'Can't,' I says. 'They've learned geography.'

"'You're off the trail.'

"'I'm on the ground. Ground suits cattle better.'

"'You'll hit broken country.'

"'Then we'll break what's left.'

"He rode close enough that our horses rubbed noses. 'I
warned you.'

"'And I listened. Warning is free, and I copper every
play that costs nothing.'

"'You think you're smart.'

"'No, sir. I think you're slow. Smartness hasn't entered into
it.'

"One of his men, a yellow-haired fellow with a blue
shirt, says, 'We can turn his leaders.'

"Sam Ketchum shifted his tobacco. 'Try,' he says.

"That was all Sam said before noon, but the yellow-haired
man reconsidered.

"Rance looked from Sam to me. 'You keep parallel with
my herd, and I'll crowd you into those gullies ahead.'

"'Crowd away,' I says. 'But be particular which herd you
crowd. Mine has responsible owners.'

"We traveled on. For six miles the grass was good
and the country open. Rance pushed his cattle faster, hoping
to nose ahead and force us west. We matched him.
Then he slowed, hoping we would cross his front and
give him an excuse to mix the herds. We slowed
too. It became a game, and we had the better
cattle.

"A trail herd ain't one body the way a green
man thinks. It has a mind in front, ribs along
the swings, and a contrary tail. The leaders decide direction,
the middle furnishes weight, and the drag complains about everything.
If you control the leaders and keep the sides breathing,
you can snake four thousand cattle through a gap no
wider than a town street. Let another herd brush your
edge, though, and brands mix quicker than cards in a
gambler's hands.

"All morning we kept two hundred yards between outfits. Rance's
riders tried to narrow it. Every time they pushed a
bunch our way, Smoky Davis and Cherokee Bill peeled them
back without fuss. Jim held our lead a steer's length
ahead of theirs. Big Dick sat straight in the saddle,
grinning at Rance until Rance couldn't look at him without
getting angry.

"By noon we had made ten miles and come to
a creek. It wasn't much of a creek, only a
ribbon of brown water with steep banks, but there was
one decent crossing on Rance's side and a boggy slant
on ours.

"Rance raced for the good crossing. I let him have
it.

"'Boss,' Jim says, 'we can beat them there.'

"'Certainly,' I says, 'but then we'd have to fight before
dinner.'

"We dropped the wagon, took spades and axes, and improved
our own crossing. The boys cut the bank, threw brush
into the soft spots, and drove the lead cattle through
in strings. Sam roped two that bogged. We watered the
herd and nooned on the north bank while Rance's cattle
were still milling in his crossing.

"Andy laid out beef, beans, dried peaches, biscuits, and coffee.
Rance's outfit could smell it across the creek. Their cook
had bogged his wagon to the hubs.

"I carried a cup to the bank and raised it
friendly.

"'Come over, Rance,' I called. 'We've got enough coffee for
men on both sides.'

"He told me what I could do with the coffee.

"'It would spill,' I says.

"After dinner we moved first. That put us a mile
ahead by three o'clock, though we were still east of
the regular trail. The country rolled smooth toward these very
sand hills. There wasn't a track under us except buffalo
paths, and every hoof in the Crooked B was writing
a new one. You can see the result yonder. Once
thirty-eight hundred cattle pass over sod, the next herd follows
the scar, and by the third season a road has
been born.

"That should answer your first question, Lucy, but it don't
yet explain the graves.

"Toward evening I noticed Rance had quit trying to pass.
His herd fell back two miles. That worried me more
than his crowding. A loud enemy is advertising; a quiet
one is figuring wholesale.

"I called Sam Ketchum over.

"'What do you make of it?'

"Sam spat. 'Mean.'

"'Can you be more exact?'

"'No.'

"We made camp south of the sand hills. The grass
was belly-deep and cured enough to carry fire, so I
put extra men around the remuda and ordered the night
guards to keep the herd grazing until full dark. A
hungry herd will run farther than a full one, same
as a hungry man will fight longer at supper.

"The cattle settled about nine. We bedded them in a
loose oval, leaders pointing north. First guard went on with
Jim and Little Tom. Second was Smoky and one of
the Hales. Third belonged to Big Dick and Sam. I
always paired a talker with a quiet man. Two talkers
neglect cattle, and two quiet men hear ghosts.

"The moon was a thin shaving, and clouds kept passing
over it. There was heat lightning in the west but
no thunder. I lay near the wagon with my boots
on and my gun under the edge of the blanket.
Trail bosses sleep that way—not because they are brave, but
because removing boots twice in one night destroys authority.

"About eleven Jim rode in.

"'Somebody's moving south of us,' he says.

"'How many?'

"'Couldn't tell. Heard a horse strike a stone.'

"I got up and listened. The herd was quiet. You
could hear steers cropping, a horse shaking its head, and
Andy snoring beneath the wagon like a sawmill working wet
timber.

"I sent Jim back and woke Sam. We walked our
horses south along a shallow draw. Half a mile from
camp we found fresh tracks—three shod horses coming from Rance's
direction. The tracks circled east toward our herd.

"Sam got off and felt one print.

"'Fresh,' he says.

"'I know.'

"'Then why feel it?'

"'To see if you knew.'

"He didn't answer. Sam could be cutting.

"We followed until the tracks divided. Two went east, one
west. That meant the riders aimed to come at both
sides of our cattle. A herd can stand noise from
one direction, because it bunches away from it. Frighten both
wings at once and it piles toward the middle; then
anything breaking loose in front may take the whole outfit.

"I rode back and put every man on horse except
Andy and the wrangler. We didn't wake the cattle or
show lanterns. Big Dick moved north of the leaders, Jim
covered east, Smoky west, and Sam waited with me near
the south edge.

"For nearly an hour nothing happened.

"Then a horse whinnied east of us.

"Something answered from the west—a low, ugly rattle, not loud,
but unnatural. The bedded steers began getting up. Horns clicked.
A ripple moved across the herd.

"'Hide,' Sam says.

"You know the trick?"

Lucy said he did not.

"A dry cowhide tied to an animal's tail will rattle
and flap when it runs. The animal tries to escape
it, but the faster it goes, the worse the hide
follows. Turn such a beast into a sleeping herd on
a dark night, and you can start cattle running before
they know what frightened them. It's a low piece of
work. A man who'll do it would put sand in
a widow's flour.

"We heard hoofbeats east of the herd, then a steer
bawled. A pale shape came tearing out of the dark
with something jumping behind it. Rance's boys had used one
of their own steers, tied the hide, headed him toward
our bed ground, and cut him loose.

"Our cattle surged to their feet.

"I shouted, 'Sam!'

"Sam's rope went out. I didn't see the loop travel.
I only heard it sing. It settled over the steer's
horns at maybe forty yards, but the critter was running
downhill, and Sam's horse got jerked so hard its hind
feet left the ground. Sam dallied once, the rope smoked
against the horn, and the steer swung sideways.

"The hide cracked. The steer fought the rope and came
on toward the herd, dragging Sam's horse.

"Sam couldn't stop him. There was no room for a
second cast.

"So Sam pulled his pistol and shot the steer behind
the eye.

"That beef dropped six lengths from our nearest cattle. The
hide flapped once and lay still.

"But the shot did what the hide had failed to
do. The herd jumped north.

"Big Dick and Jim were ready. They raced the leaders
at an angle, shouting and singing, forcing them into a
turn. Night herding is mostly persuasion until it becomes prayer.
Once cattle start, you don't try to stop them head-on.
You bend the leaders, make them circle, and keep narrowing
the circle until the running wears out.

"Our north edge rolled east. The middle followed. For a
minute it sounded like a hailstorm under the earth. Dust
rose black in the dark, and horns showed whenever the
lightning winked. Jim sang 'The Dying Ranger' in a voice
that would have stampeded church furniture, but the cattle knew
it and bent around him.

"Then firing broke out on the west side.

"Rance's men were shooting into the air to keep the
herd moving. Smoky Davis fired back, not into the air,
because Smoky disliked wasting ammunition on weather.

"I rode toward the flashes. A bullet cut the brim
off my hat. Another struck my saddle horn and flattened
there. My horse, a roan called October, tried to leave
for Texas, but I held him north. We came upon
one of Rance's riders beside the running cattle. He had
a pistol up.

"'Drop it!' I says.

"He fired.

"I fired too. He fell off on the far side,
and his horse went into the herd with empty stirrups.

"I don't know whether I hit the man or scared
his balance loose. In night work a fellow shouldn't claim
details he can't invoice.

"The cattle were circling now, which reduced the danger of
a straight run, but horses and riders had become mixed
around the edge. Somebody shot Big Dick's horse through the
neck. The animal turned a somersault and threw Dick under
two steers. He came out missing one spur and most
of his shirt, caught the reins of the riderless horse
I'd seen, and mounted without asking whose it was.

"On the east side, Little Tom collided with a Turkey
Track man. Both horses went down. Tom broke his wrist,
the other fellow broke his collarbone, and the horses settled
their own account with bites until Jim separated them.

"Then the cloud passed, and we got enough moonlight to
see.

"Rance Spiller was riding straight for me.

"He had one pistol in his hand, and his black
beard was full of dust. I had lost my hat
and one suspender, which annoyed me because I'd dressed neat
for the evening.

"He pulled up ten steps away.

"'You killed my man!' he says.

"'Your man was shooting.'

"'So are you.'

"'That's how I know the signs.'

"Behind him, three of his outfit came up. Behind me
arrived Sam, Smoky, and Big Dick on the borrowed horse.
We sat there while the herd circled north of us,
grumbling and coughing in the dust.

"Rance says, 'This trail belongs to me now.'

"'You're standing on mine.'

"'You ain't got a trail.'

"'Look down.'

"He did. The ground was pounded bare.

"That irritated him.

"'I'm giving you one chance,' he says. 'Turn south tomorrow,
take your cattle back across the Canadian, and stay there
till my herd is through.'

"'You used your one chance when you tied that hide.'

"'Can you prove I did it?'

"Sam pointed toward a rider west of Rance. The fellow
had rawhide thongs hanging from his saddle and a corner
of stiff cowhide caught under his leg.

"'He can,' Sam says.

"The fellow looked down. A guilty man always examines the
evidence when somebody points at it.

"I says, 'Rance, you tried to stampede four thousand cattle
in the dark. You might have killed half my outfit.'

"'Maybe I aimed to.'

"'Then your aim has improved. You've admitted it.'

"He cocked his pistol.

"Now, there are times when a man's words are only
smoke before the shot, and there are other times when
one more word can let the heat out of a
difficulty. I've always preferred the extra word, provided it belongs
to me.

"So I says, 'Before you begin, Rance, I'd like to
explain the arrangement. Big Dick takes the yellow-haired man. Smoky
has the one in the cavalry coat. Sam will attend
to that hide-tying citizen. I'll handle you. If any other
Turkey Track hand joins in, Jim is somewhere behind us
and he has only one eye to divide his attention.
This is favorable to accuracy.'

"Rance looked at his men. They looked at him. The
yellow-haired fellow had gone pale around the mouth.

"'You bluff big,' Rance says.

"'I'm too fat to bluff small.'

"'I can kill you where you sit.'

"'You may. But you'll have to spend the result quick.'

"For a moment nobody moved. The cattle had nearly stopped
running. Their circle tightened to a walk. You could hear
leather creak and one wounded horse blowing through its nose.

"Rance said, very quiet, 'The stuff's off.'

"His pistol came up.

"I fired from the saddle.

"So did everybody else.

"It was a minute's work, if that long. Rance folded
over his horn, tried to straighten, and slid down under
his gray horse. The yellow-haired man shot Big Dick through
the upper arm. Dick shot him out of the saddle.
Smoky and the cavalry-coat fellow fired together; Smoky lost a
piece of his left ear, while the other man lost
further interest in the proceedings.

"The hide-tying citizen got two shots at Sam. One killed
Sam's horse. The other passed through Sam's coat without touching
him, which was narrow work because Sam was still inside
it. As the horse went down, Sam rolled clear, rested
one elbow on the ground, and shot the fellow once.

"Three Turkey Track men came racing out of the dust
then. Jim appeared behind them and fired into the air.

"'The next ones lower!' he shouted.

"They stopped so fast their horses sat down.

"When the smoke blew clear, Rance and two of his
men were taking their last nap. The yellow-haired fellow was
alive but had a ball through his shoulder. Big Dick's
arm was bleeding freely. Smoky's ear looked chewed. Little Tom
had his broken wrist. One Turkey Track man had a
collarbone snapped from the collision, and another carried a bullet
along his ribs. We had four horses down—two dead, one
dying, and one with a leg broken.

"Nobody felt victorious. Victory at midnight is generally too dusty
to admire.

"I told everybody to holster weapons. Rance's second man was
a sandy fellow called Eph Moore. He had stayed with
their herd during the shooting and now rode in carrying
a white flour sack tied to a quirt.

"'Who's boss here?' he asks.

"'I am,' I says.

"He looked at Rance on the ground. 'I reckon you
are.'

"'Only of my own outfit.'

"'That's enough for tonight.'

"Eph was sensible. There are men who need three funerals
before sense comes to them, and men who acquire it
by observation. He asked leave to gather their wounded. I
told him no leave was needed.

"We spent the rest of the night putting things straight.
Jim and the Hale boys settled our herd. Eph's men
quieted theirs a mile south. Andy came with the wagon
and made coffee strong enough to float a horseshoe. He
cut the shirts off the wounded and boiled bandages in
a wash kettle. Andy claimed he had served as surgeon
during the war, though he never specified which side survived
his treatment.

"He probed Big Dick's arm with a spoon handle.

"'Ball went through,' Andy says.

"'I know,' Dick says.

"'Then why'd you make me look?'

"'To see if you knew.'

"Sam said nothing, but I saw him smile. That was
the longest joke Sam ever made, and he didn't use
a word.

"We put the hurt men under blankets near the wagon.
The yellow-haired fellow asked for water. Big Dick carried it
to him with his good arm.

"The fellow says, 'I reckon I owe you one.'

"Dick says, 'We're even. Your bullet went through me and
mine stayed in you. I'm ahead on ammunition, but behind
on lead.'

"They shook hands. That's trail arithmetic.

"At daylight we counted cattle. We had lost none permanent.
Twenty-three were outside the herd, but the boys gathered them
before breakfast. Three had wire cuts from old fence, two
were lame, and the steer Sam shot belonged to the
Turkey Track. Eph said we owed them for him.

"'Deduct him from the horses your outfit killed,' I says.

"Eph considered. 'How do you price a night horse?'

"'Higher after midnight.'

"'That's fair.'

"We counted the Turkey Track herd too. They were short
eleven, all likely strayed in the confusion. I lent Eph
four men to gather them. We were enemies by moonlight
and neighbors by breakfast. The trail does that. A man
can't keep a feud warm while he's borrowing a needle
or asking help with bogged cattle.

"Then came the planting act.

"The ground where the shooting happened was hardpan under four
inches of sod. We tried it with shovels, but every
stroke rang like we were digging a courthouse step. The
three dead men didn't improve while we argued.

"Eph wanted to haul Rance south to the last creek.
His cook objected because the wagon carried flour on one
side and bacon on the other, leaving no respectable place
for a corpse. Andy suggested laying all three across the
tongue, but the mules had already endured the gunfire and
didn't deserve it.

"I pointed toward these sand hills.

"'Ground's softer there.'

"So we wrapped the men in blankets, laid them in
an empty supply wagon, and brought them up here. Our
outfit and theirs dug together. Sand is grateful soil. It
accepts a shovel without debate.

"We made the graves deep on account of wolves, laid
stones at the heads and feet, and Eph said a
few words. He wasn't a preacher, but neither were the
dead particular. Over Rance he said, 'He was a hard
man and went hard.' That covered the facts.

"The yellow-haired fellow, whose name was Pete Lomas, asked us
to put Rance's pistols in with him. Eph said no.

"'Rance got into enough trouble wearing them,' he says. 'I
won't arm him where he's going.'

"So Eph kept the pistols.

"We stood there a minute with hats off. Then Andy
called that the beans were burning, and every living man
remembered his duties.

"Eph came to me after breakfast.

"'What now?' he asks.

"'You take the old trail. I'll take mine.'

"'You still aim to run beside us?'

"'No. Your objections have been reduced.'

"He looked toward the graves. 'Rance had a poor way
of asking.'

"'He had a worse way of hearing.'

"Eph held out his hand. 'No more trouble?'

"'Not unless you've tied another hide.'

"He almost smiled. 'We burned every hide in camp.'

"We shook. His outfit stayed back two days to rest
their wounded and gather cattle. We went north on the
new trace, crossed the next creek where you see those
cottonwoods, and joined the regular trail twelve miles beyond. The
country between was rough but passable. We had to double-team
the wagon through one draw and carry Andy over another
because he refused to walk while there were cattle employed
by the same owner.

"After that the drive went easy. We crossed the Arkansas
on a rise, held one night against a storm, and
reached Abilene with thirty-eight hundred and fifty-four head.

Lucy interrupted. "You said thirty-eight hundred and sixty-one after the
two died."

"I did."

"That's seven short."

Stubb regarded him with approval.

"You have a cattleman's memory. One broke a leg in
a prairie-dog hole, two strayed in a thunderstorm, three were
cut out with Texas fever, and the seventh was eaten."

"By wolves?"

"By Andy. He claimed it had a cough."

Stubb continued before Lucy could ask further.

"Colonel Barstow met us at the shipping pens wearing a
white shirt and a black coat. He counted the cattle,
examined the books, and heard about the trouble from a
letter Eph had sent ahead.

"He says, 'Stubb, did you defend my interests?'

"'Colonel,' I says, 'your interests required considerable defending.'

"'Did you begin the shooting?'

"'No, sir. I only began the accuracy.'

"He walked along the pens looking at those steers. They
had gained near a hundred pounds apiece on the trail,
and their coats shone. Buyers were offering three dollars over
what the colonel expected.

"Finally he says, 'You lost seven cattle.'

"'Yes, sir.'

"'Four horses.'

"'Two belonged to the outfit. One belonged to Rance. One
never belonged to anybody after Big Dick got on him.'

"'You made a new road.'

"'It was going our direction.'

"'And you killed three men.'

"'They were trying to increase the loss column.'

"The colonel stood quiet. Then he says, 'A boss-man ought
to protect his brand, but next year try diplomacy.'

"'I used diplomacy for two days.'

"'Try it farther away.'

"Then he paid every man a month's bonus and gave
Sam Ketchum a silver-mounted saddle for stopping that steer. Sam
sold the silver the same afternoon and kept the saddle,
which proves he knew which part held a man on.

"That new path got used the following season because the
old trail was muddy, and after that outfits took whichever
branch promised grass. That is why the roads divide here
and come together north of the creek."

Stubb leaned back against the wagon wheel. The beans had
long since been eaten. Even the remuda seemed to have
drawn near to listen.

Lucy looked toward the three graves, pale under the afternoon
sun.

"So those are Rance Spiller and the two men?"

"They are," Stubb said solemnly. "Unless somebody has moved them."

Lucy considered the sand hills. "Why didn't you bury them
where they fell?"

Stubb's face softened with patient weariness.

"Son, I told you. The ground there was hard, and
the digging here was easy. A man may die for
pride, but nobody wants to shovel through hardpan on account
of it."

Lucy nodded as if he had been given the missing
figure in a sum.

After a moment he said, "Did you ever see Eph
Moore again?"

Stubb climbed to his feet and dusted the seat of
his trousers.

"I never like to refer to that episode," he said.
"It stirs up too many recollections."

"But you just—"

"Rustle your horse," Ike Inks told Lucy. "We're moving."

That ended the historical inquiry.

We threw the cattle together, pointed the leaders toward the
northern branch, and moved out under a white sky. Lucy
rode drag that afternoon and kept looking toward the graves
until dust hid them. He believed every word Stubb had
told him. We knew he did because, at supper, he
warned the cook never to criticize old Andy Scroggins's biscuits
if Andy happened to visit camp, and he asked One-eyed
Jim whether losing the right eye truly helped a man
see around left-hand bends.

Jim told him it did, but only north of the
Canadian.

Lucy accepted that too.

As for Stubb, he rode off point with his hat
tipped back, humming to himself, and for nearly three days
he refused to discuss the affair unless somebody asked him.

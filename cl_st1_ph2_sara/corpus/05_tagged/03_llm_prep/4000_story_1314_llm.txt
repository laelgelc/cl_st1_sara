### The Road to Otchenchiri

In the autumn of the famine year, when I was
wandering along the Caucasian coast with eight kopecks in my
pocket and a conviction—more durable than my boots—that mankind had
somehow taken the wrong turning, the earth displayed before me
such wealth, such tranquil magnificence, that hunger itself seemed an
absurdity invented by officials.

To my left stretched the sea, dark-blue near the horizon
and green as bottle-glass where it rolled over the stones.
All day it breathed against the shore, now heavily, like
a giant asleep after labour, now with a sly whisper,
drawing pebbles from beneath the foam and rattling them together
as though counting money. On my right the hills mounted
one behind another—emerald at their feet, turquoise above, and farther
away smoky violet; while beyond them, remote and solemn, the
mountains lifted silver foreheads into the clear air. Chestnuts stood
upon the slopes in rusty-golden armour. Beeches burned crimson among
the darker woods. The leaves, loosened by the warm wind,
sailed down in flocks: some twisted like little wounded fishes,
some spread five yellow fingers as if imploring the earth
not to receive them.

And over all this the sun moved slowly, contemplating its
work.

It must be pleasant to be the sun. One sees
everything and is answerable for nothing.

The road ran between the foothills and the shore, now
descending until the spray salted one's lips, now climbing a
stony shoulder from which the coast could be seen curving
away for many versts. Streams came down from the forest,
chattering over white stones. Each had its own voice. One
laughed like a child escaping punishment; another grumbled in an
old man's throat; a third murmured so gently under the
hazel bushes that it seemed a young maiden was telling
the earth about her lover and begged the moss not
to repeat the secret.

I followed, at some distance, a company of migrants from
the government of Orlov. There were eleven of them—seven men,
three women and a boy whose age might have been
twelve or twenty, for famine had dried him into a
little brown root. They had come south in hope of
obtaining work upon a new military road near Otchenchiri. Who
had told them there was work no one knew. Such
information in Russia travels as miraculously as the legends of
saints: it is born nowhere, is believed everywhere, and usually
leads poor people to a closed gate.

Their clothes had the colour of road dust and old
grief. One man wore a soldier's coat without buttons, tied
at the waist by rope; another had wrapped his feet
in pieces of sacking; the tallest carried across his shoulders
a wooden yoke, from one end of which hung a
kettle and from the other a bundle containing, as I
afterwards learned, three onions, half a loaf and an icon
of Saint Nicholas. They walked silently, not because they had
nothing to say, but because speech spends strength, and strength
is dearer than bread when twenty versts lie ahead.

At halting places they spoke of grain.

"Rye was a ruble and seventy."

"Two rubles with us."

"Ours had black worms in it."

"Worms are meat," observed the old soldier.

No one laughed.

The boy searched beside the road for hazelnuts missed by
the local children, and once, finding a rotten pear, divided
it with his mother. They ate even the seeds. Behind
them came a pregnant young woman, a widow named Tatyana.
She was perhaps twenty-six, though suffering had laid another ten
years across her face as a careless traveller throws a
coat upon a chair. Her eyes were grey, large and
suspicious; her cheekbones stood out sharply; beneath her faded brown
kerchief damp strands of fair hair clung to her temples.
She carried a small bundle pressed beneath one arm and
walked with both hands supporting the enormous burden of her
belly.

Her husband, Pavel, had died of fever near Novorossiysk. They
had buried him beside the road under three stones, because
the ground was hard and the company could not remain.
She spoke of this without tears.

"What use crying?" she had said when I asked whether
he had been ill long. "He died, and that was
all."

This answer impressed me. The poor become philosophers through lack
of alternatives, though learned gentlemen, who possess comfortable libraries, write
entire volumes before arriving at the same conclusion.

The migrants mistrusted me at first. I had the appearance
of neither peasant nor gentleman—an appearance which, in those days,
often brought me kicks from both classes. My coat had
belonged to a railway clerk, my trousers to a sailor,
and my cap possibly to a corpse, though the Armenian
who sold it to me denied this with excessive warmth.
I carried a canvas knapsack, a tin cup and a
battered book whose title had vanished beneath grease. Because I
spoke correctly, they supposed me to be a dismissed official;
because I shared tobacco, they decided that I could not
be entirely wicked.

For two days we kept approximately the same pace. Sometimes
I walked among them; sometimes I went ahead, intoxicated by
the air and by those prodigal colours which the south
flung over hill and water; sometimes I lagged behind to
bathe my feet or to stare at the sea. At
night we slept near one another under trees, and the
waves laboured in darkness like unseen mill wheels.

On the third morning Tatyana walked badly. Her lips were
white, and now and then she stopped, leaned against a
rock, and breathed through clenched teeth.

"You're near your time," said the old soldier.

"Leave off," she answered sharply.

"We can wait."

"I said leave off. It is my stomach."

The women glanced at her and then at one another.
A strange modesty governs peasants in these matters. They will
speak without reserve of sickness, death, beatings, vermin and every
humiliation inflicted by hunger, but birth—through which every talkative person
enters the world—is hidden beneath shame as though it were
a theft.

At noon we rested by a spring. Tatyana would not
eat. She drank, vomited behind a chestnut tree, rinsed her
mouth and announced that she felt better. An hour later
she began to lag. One of the women offered to
remain with her, but Tatyana cursed her.

"Go on! Do you want to lose the work because
of me? Go! I'll catch you."

"There may be no work," remarked the old soldier.

"Then you can lose the no-work without me."

This logic was so complete that they obeyed it. The
company moved ahead, looking back uneasily. The road bent around
a wooded promontory, and one after another they disappeared—the kettle
flashing last in the sun.

I had remained behind to mend a strap on my
knapsack. Tatyana stood some twenty paces away, both hands against
the trunk of an oak. Suddenly her knees bent. She
made a low sound, not loud, but so unlike any
ordinary cry that the leaves themselves seemed to hesitate in
the air.

I went towards her.

"Keep off!" she shouted, turning a distorted face upon me.
"Go with the others!"

"Something is wrong."

"Nothing is wrong. Go!"

She stooped, seized a stone, and threw it. It passed
far to my left.

"That was a bad shot," I told her.

"May the devil take you!"

"He has declined me repeatedly."

Another pain caught her. She doubled against the oak and
bit her sleeve. When it released her, she looked at
me with hatred and terror.

"I'll go into the bushes," she said. "You walk on.
Tell Marfa I'm coming."

"You cannot walk."

"I can crawl."

This she attempted. She left the road and pushed between
the hazels, moving with one hand upon the ground and
the other holding her belly. Her bundle slipped from beneath
her arm. I picked it up. She heard me following
and swore with astonishing vigour; even now I recall the
inventiveness of several expressions, though Christian charity and the printer's
compositors forbid me to preserve them.

Beyond the bushes was a little hollow carpeted with dry
leaves. A brook crossed it and went shining towards the
sea. Alder roots rose from the bank like black knuckles.
Tatyana sank among the leaves, tore at the fastening of
her skirt and ordered me once more to depart.

Then she screamed.

Birds burst from the bushes. The brook continued its foolish,
silver conversation.

I confess that I was afraid. A man may meet
policemen, drunken sailors, rabid dogs and even editors with some
preparation of spirit, because their conduct, however disagreeable, has recognizable
rules. But before a woman in labour, Nature herself appears
with sleeves rolled up, bloody-armed and inexorable, and asks: Well,
clever fellow, what do you know now?

I knew very little.

During my student days—which had ended before the university could
derive much glory from them—I had attended two lectures on
anatomy, read part of an obstetrical manual, and once assisted
an old midwife when a laundress gave birth in a
cellar. My chief duty on that occasion had been to
boil water and keep the husband from entering with an
axe. Nevertheless, among the blind the one-eyed man is an
ophthalmologist; and in that deserted hollow, with the migrants already
out of hearing, I represented science.

"Tatyana," I said, kneeling beside her, "listen. I have some
medical knowledge."

"You're lying!"

"Certainly, but not about everything."

"Don't touch me!"

Another contraction seized her. Her body arched and twisted like
a strip of birch bark dropped upon hot coals. She
clutched my wrist so violently that her nails entered the
skin. Sweat ran over her face; her eyes became black
and enormous. When the pain ebbed she released me, ashamed,
and pulled her skirt over her knees.

"Go away," she whispered. "Holy Mother, let him go away."

"There is nowhere for me to go."

"I will die."

"Possibly—but there is no need to begin by being certain
of it."

She struck at me feebly.

I spread my coat beneath her, made a support for
her head from the bundle, and brought water in my
tin cup. She drank two swallows, then pushed it aside.

"Where is your husband?"

"Dead."

"I remember."

"Then why ask?"

To keep her speaking, I suppose. Words are small handholds
driven into the wall of pain.

She began praying—not in the solemn language of church services,
but hurriedly and almost angrily, as one scolds a powerful
relative who has failed to keep a promise.

"Mother of God, you bore a son, you know it!
Help me, then. Saint Anne, holy Saint Anne, don't stand
looking! Pavel, if the dead can hear, speak for me.
Lord, forgive my sins—but quickly, Lord, quickly!"

Then she turned upon me.

"What are you gaping at, long-nosed crow? Do something!"

This request, though discourteously phrased, seemed reasonable.

I examined her as modestly as circumstances permitted, which is
to say modesty vanished altogether beneath necessity. The child was
coming. I remembered clean hands, boiled cloths, warm water, scissors—excellent
things, all absent. I had soap, a pocketknife, two handkerchiefs
of disputable cleanliness, and string from the knapsack. The brook
supplied cold water in scandalous abundance. I washed my hands
and knife with soap, then held the blade in the
flame of a little spirit lamp I carried for making
tea.

Tatyana watched me and groaned.

"You'll cut me."

"Not unless you move very suddenly."

"Murderer!"

"Be still."

"Drunkard!"

This accusation was unjust, since I had not tasted vodka
for four days, but there was no time to defend
my character.

The pains came rapidly. Between them she shivered, though the
air was warm. She begged me to cover her, then
tore the covering away; implored me not to touch her,
then caught both my hands and dragged me near. The
human will, of which philosophers speak with pomp, becomes a
torn rag in the machinery of the body.

A cloud moved over the sun. Its shadow descended the
wooded slope, crossed the road and entered our hollow, cooling
the leaves around us. The sea, visible between two alders,
darkened to iron. For an instant all nature seemed to
hold its breath—not from concern, perhaps, for nature has witnessed
many births and is not sentimental, but because we ourselves
pour emotion into sky, stone and water, filling the indifferent
universe as a poor woman fills a cheap icon-lamp with
her last spoonful of oil.

Then Tatyana cried out, and the head appeared.

"Now," I said, though I had forgotten what should follow
the word.

"Now what?"

"Push."

"I am pushing, fool!"

"Yes. Well. Continue."

She uttered a roar that could have belonged to some
great animal caught beneath a falling tree. Her face became
purple; every tendon in her neck stood out. I supported
the emerging head, terrified by its softness and the appalling
responsibility of my fingers. There was hair—black, wet, plastered close.
Then the shoulders turned, as if the little prisoner knew
more of escape than his gaoler knew of release. One
moment there was unbearable resistance; the next, with a swift
and slippery movement, the child slid into my hands.

He nearly escaped me and fell upon the coat.

For perhaps two seconds he lay there silent, bluish, glistening,
doubled upon himself—a small, ancient-looking creature, more frog than man,
with fists no larger than walnuts. Those two seconds were
longer than the famine year.

Then he opened a mouth of astonishing size and yelled.

The bushes returned the sound. The brook laughed over its
stones. From the sea came the heavy applause of a
wave.

"He lives!" I cried.

Tatyana had fallen back with closed eyes.

"Show me."

I held him where she could see. He waved his
arms and protested against existence with a power quite disproportionate
to his dimensions.

"A boy," I said.

Her eyes opened fully.

"A boy?"

"A man. A very small and slippery man."

She smiled, but at once the smile broke into tears.
Her lips moved.

"Pavel wanted a boy."

There was still the cord. I remembered this with the
panic of an actor who discovers, after the curtain rises,
that he has omitted half his costume. The book I
had once read presented the procedure with admirable calm, as
though newborn children arrived numbered, dry and obedient upon a
professor's table. Reality was otherwise. The infant kicked; the mother
trembled; blood stained my coat; flies, those sanitary inspectors of
the devil, appeared from nowhere.

I tied the cord in two places with clean lengths
of string and cut between the knots. My hand shook
so badly that I had to stop and breathe. The
child screamed at the knife, though it did not touch
him.

"You frighten him," Tatyana murmured.

"He frightens me."

"Give him here."

"Not yet."

"Give him!"

"Woman, allow science one minute."

"Science! Look at your face."

I do not know what expression I wore, but it
caused her to laugh weakly, and the laugh became a
groan. Afterbirth followed. I dealt with it as well as
I could, cleaned her with water and one of my
handkerchiefs, and covered her. She lay pale and exhausted, her
eyelids trembling. I feared bleeding; I feared fever; I feared
everything whose name I knew and several things which had
no names.

The boy, meanwhile, continued roaring.

"He must be washed," I said.

"In warm water."

"There is no warm water."

"Make some."

"With what basin? The kettle is halfway to Otchenchiri."

"The sea," she whispered.

"The sea is cold."

"He'll take cold."

"He has already taken life. Cold will be the smaller
shock."

I wrapped him in my remaining handkerchief and carried him
towards the shore. Behind me Tatyana called:

"Don't drown him!"

"I had not intended it."

"You don't know how!"

"That is precisely why I shall be careful."

The sea lay smooth beneath the returning sun. Farther out,
long strips of light rested upon it as though invisible
carpenters had planed the water and left silver shavings. At
the edge, small waves unfolded over black pebbles, each wave
clear green at the crest, then suddenly white, talkative and
eager.

I crouched, dipped my hand and found the water warmer
than the brook. The boy objected to being unwrapped; he
objected more violently when his feet touched the sea. I
washed him quickly, supporting his head upon my wrist. Blood
and mucus streamed away into that immense blue basin which
has washed conquerors and corpses, ships and cities, and remained
no cleaner or dirtier for any of them.

In the sunlight he changed colour from bluish purple to
an indignant rose. His skin was wrinkled, his legs absurdly
thin, his belly round. He had a flattened nose and
black hair pasted over a pointed skull.

"Beautiful!" I said aloud.

This was not true according to the ordinary conventions of
beauty, but conventions are framed by persons who have forgotten
their own first appearance. To me he seemed magnificent—a rosy
little homunculus, noisy, helpless, wholly new. No priest had yet
frightened him, no official had registered him, no schoolmaster had
lied to him, no employer had deducted money from his
wages. He possessed nothing and owed nothing. His fists were
closed as if he carried two secrets from the darkness
before birth.

What would those hands do? Hold a plough, a rifle,
a pen, a thief's knife, a lover's face? Would he
build houses or burn them? Would he grow kind, being
born amid kindness, or cruel, being received by poverty? The
future slept folded inside him more tightly than his fingers.

He screamed into my beard and thus interrupted philosophy.

I dried him as well as possible upon the lining
of my coat, wrapped him in my shirt—necessitating a certain
rearrangement of my own costume—and returned to the hollow.

Tatyana tried to rise.

"Lie down!"

"My child—"

"Here is your tyrant."

I placed him beside her. She stared, afraid to touch
him. Then one hand crept from beneath the covering and
rested upon his head. The hand was rough, cracked and
stained from road dust; the head beneath it was small
enough to fit in her palm.

"He is black-haired," she whispered.

"Like his father?"

"No. Pavel was fair. Like my brother, perhaps."

The boy opened his mouth, turned his head blindly and
struck his cheek against her breast.

"He wants feeding," I said.

"There'll be nothing."

"We shall inquire."

She flushed and ordered me to turn away. A quarter-hour
earlier I had seen her body in its ultimate rebellion,
bloodied and torn by birth, but now a loosened bodice
string embarrassed her. Such is modesty: useless in catastrophe, it
returns at once to arrange the curtains.

I turned my back and began making a fire. Dry
twigs crackled beneath the spirit flame; soon smoke climbed among
the leaves, blue and thin. Behind me she muttered, struggled,
soothed the child, cursed him tenderly and at last gave
a sigh.

"He's taken it."

"Good."

"There's little."

"He is little."

"He pulls like a calf."

"An industrious citizen."

For some minutes there was silence except for the fire,
the brook and the child's tiny swallowing. I filled my
tin mug from the spring, boiled water, and made tea
directly in it. From my knapsack I produced a heel
of bread, two lumps of sugar, and a handful of
dried plums. This represented nearly all my provisions. I felt
proud of the feast, as a minister feels proud when
distributing grain purchased with the people's taxes.

Tatyana ate the sugar first.

"For strength," she explained defensively.

"The proper order."

She drank tea in small sips while the boy slept
against her. Colour returned faintly to her face. Her eyes
followed each of my movements.

"You truly studied medicine?"

"For a time."

"Why did you stop?"

"The university and I disagreed."

She considered this, then nodded with understanding.

"Drink?"

It was the peasant explanation for every broken male life
and, frequently, the correct one.

"Not entirely."

"Cards?"

"No."

"A woman, then."

"Several causes conspired."

"Drink," she concluded, and looked at me with greater confidence,
for misfortune becomes credible to people only when it assumes
a familiar shape.

I did not undeceive her. A woman who has just
entrusted you with both her life and her child deserves
at least one reassuring falsehood.

After she had eaten, she pointed towards the bloodied mass
wrapped in leaves.

"That must be buried."

"I shall throw it into the sea."

"No!"

Her voice sharpened with fear.

"Not into water. Bury it where no dog can dig.
Deep. And not beneath an alder."

"Why not an alder?"

"It is unlucky."

"To whom?"

"To the child, of course."

The child yawned, indifferent to botany.

I dug with my knife and a flat stone beneath
a young chestnut. The earth was dark, damp and threaded
with roots. Tatyana watched until I had made the hole
sufficiently deep, placed the afterbirth in it, covered it and
rolled a heavy stone on top.

"Make the sign," she said.

"Over the grave?"

"It is not a grave. Only make it."

I crossed myself. She did likewise, slowly, with her free
hand.

The sun had moved westward. Gold light slanted through the
chestnut leaves and laid warm bars across the hollow. A
green lizard emerged upon the stone beneath which I had
buried that first dark companion of the child's life. It
stared at us with bright, ancient eyes and began pulsing
its throat, perhaps pronouncing judgement, perhaps merely digesting a fly.

"We must go," Tatyana said.

"You must rest until morning."

"The others will be far."

"They will wait."

"They won't."

"They are your companions."

"They're hungry."

This answer contained the whole arithmetic of famine. Friendship is
willing; the stomach vetoes.

"You cannot walk."

"I can."

"You have lost blood."

"There is blood in me still."

"The child—"

"I'll carry him."

"You will carry nothing."

At that she became stubborn. She pushed herself upright, swayed
and gripped the alder trunk. Her face turned grey; sweat
appeared again over her lip. Yet she stood.

"Otchenchiri," she said. "They say there is road work. If
I miss it, what shall I feed him? Air?"

"You cannot swing a pick tomorrow."

"I can cook. Carry stones. Wash for them."

"You may die upon the road."

"The Holy Mother will protect me."

It is difficult to argue with the Holy Mother, particularly
when she has not supplied an address for correspondence.

We waited another half-hour while I packed our few belongings.
Tatyana tore a strip from her petticoat and wrapped the
child more securely. She moved stiffly and winced whenever she
bent, but her hands were deft. Already she handled him
with an authority older than either of us.

"What shall you call him?" I asked.

"Pavel."

"After his father?"

"Who else?"

"There are many admirable names."

"His father was not an admirable man," she said. "But
he was his father."

She told me to give her the boy. I refused.
We argued. She cursed me, then suddenly began to cry.

"You'll drop him."

"I carried him to the Black Sea and back."

"You washed him cold."

"He survived."

"You've no wife. How should you know babies?"

"How should you? This is your first."

She stared at me, offended by the justice of this.

"Give me your arm," I said. "I carry Pavel. You
carry yourself, with assistance."

At last she consented.

We climbed from the hollow to the road. The effort
exhausted her; she leaned against me, breathing hard. I placed
my knapsack upon my chest, the infant in the crook
of my left arm, and supported her with my right.
Thus arranged, we resembled a travelling exhibition devoted to the
consequences of bad planning.

The road had changed while we were below. Or perhaps
we had changed, which is the more usual explanation. Every
colour seemed newly washed. The chestnuts blazed overhead like golden
candelabra; red leaves drifted upon the brook and darted through
the shallows like salmon fry. Beyond the trees the sea
shone immense and tender, its blue deepening towards evening. Small
clouds sailed over the mountains, sending violet shadows down the
slopes as messengers announcing night.

Pavel slept. His head, covered in black silk, rested against
my arm. Now and then his lips puckered, and he
made a soft, indignant grunt, perhaps dreaming of the first
outrages committed against him—cold air, sea water and a bearded
stranger.

Tatyana walked with short steps. At first she said nothing.
Her weight dragged upon my shoulder, and I could feel
her trembling. Blood darkened the back of her skirt despite
the folded cloth. Each stone in the road appeared to
strike through her whole body, yet when I suggested rest
she shook her head.

"We must catch them."

"We shall."

"You say that to quiet me."

"Yes."

"How far?"

"Three versts, perhaps."

"You don't know."

"No."

"You're a strange doctor."

"I am stranger than I am doctor."

She almost smiled.

At the crest of a low hill we stopped. Below
us the shore opened into a broad bay. White foam
traced its curve as neatly as lace upon a blue
dress. Far beyond, near a headland, smoke rose from several
huts; and on the road itself, scarcely larger than ants,
moved the migrants. The kettle flashed.

"There!" I cried.

Tatyana peered, shielding her eyes.

"They waited."

"Perhaps they merely walk slowly."

"They waited," she repeated.

Her face softened. She made the sign of the cross
towards the tiny figures, then towards the sea, and then—being
uncertain which quarter of heaven deserved credit—upwards in general.

"Holy Mother, keep them."

We sat upon a warm rock. The boy awakened and
began rooting against my shirt.

"He wants me," she said.

There was triumph in her voice.

I gave him to her. She opened her bodice without
embarrassment now, and he fastened himself to the breast with
fierce concentration. Tatyana watched him, at first anxiously, then with
wonder. The sunset rested upon her face, revealing its exhaustion
without cruelty: the cracked lips, the shadows under her eyes,
the damp hair, the fine dust embedded in every crease.
She was not beautiful. It would insult her to pretend
she was. She was a hungry peasant widow, sore, unwashed,
frightened of tomorrow and too weak to walk another verst.

But then her child's hand opened against her breast.

Five minute fingers spread upon the coarse cloth. She bent
over them as though heaven itself had written there in
letters too small for me to read.

"Look," she whispered.

"I see."

"He knows me."

"He knows warmth."

"No. Me."

"Perhaps."

She lifted her eyes angrily, and I withdrew the objection.
There are truths which science, tactfully, should leave alone.

"He is strong," she said.

"Very."

"Did you hear him shout?"

"All the Caucasus heard."

"He'll grow tall. Pavel was tall before the fever bent
him. He could lift a flour sack with one hand.
This one will be stronger."

"Undoubtedly."

"I'll feed him. Somehow I will. If there's no milk,
I'll work for goat's milk. In winter we'll go back
north. Or perhaps remain here. It's warm here. Do they
let Russians have land?"

"They let people have promises."

"I need only a patch. Cabbage, potatoes, a hen. He
can run by the sea."

Her plans multiplied as she spoke. A quarter-acre became a
cottage; the cottage acquired a stove, a cow, two pear
trees. Pavel learned to walk, to speak, to chop wood.
He attended school, though she distrusted schools. He became a
clerk, then no, clerks steal; a soldier, then no, soldiers
are killed; a road master, perhaps, with boots and a
silver watch. Poverty, which had allowed her no future an
hour earlier, now yielded whole estates to her imagination.

"He must be good," she said suddenly. "Not like men
who drink and beat women."

"Then do not let him."

"Can a mother stop a grown man?"

"Begin while he weighs six pounds."

She looked solemnly at the nursing child.

"Do you hear? You'll not drink."

Pavel sneezed.

"That means yes," she said.

We resumed our journey. The road descended, and walking became
easier. Tatyana no longer leaned upon me continuously; sometimes she
took several steps by herself, one hand resting lightly upon
my sleeve. She insisted on carrying the child, and now
I allowed it, though I kept my arm beneath hers.

Evening spread over the coast with southern swiftness. The mountains
retained the sun after the road had entered shadow; their
snowy summits shone pale rose, then amber, then a cold
supernatural white. Bats flickered among the trees like scraps of
burnt paper. From the forest came the resinous smell of
pine, the damp breath of mushrooms and decaying leaves, and
once the pungent musk of some unseen animal.

The sea darkened but did not become silent. It addressed
the shore endlessly, repeating one grave sentence in a language
older than sorrow. A wave gathered itself, advanced, broke and
withdrew; another followed, saying the same thing with a different
emphasis.

Tatyana listened.

"It is never quiet?"

"Never."

"What does it want?"

"The shore, perhaps."

"It has the shore."

"Possession does not cure desire."

She considered this, then shrugged.

"It only makes noise."

After another hundred paces she said:

"It is beautiful."

"Yes."

"I did not see it before."

"You were busy."

"No. Even before today. I looked, but I did not
see." She glanced down at the child. "Now everything is—how
shall I say it?—as if it has been washed."

This from a woman who could barely read and had
probably never heard of aesthetics. Yet professors have used worse
phrases and required six hundred pages to do so.

We approached the migrants near twilight. They had stopped beside
a roadside shrine—a little whitewashed niche containing a smoke-blackened image
of the Virgin. Marfa, the broadest of the women, came
running towards us. Behind her limped the old soldier and
the famine-shrunken boy.

"Well?" Marfa called.

Tatyana raised the bundle.

The women made a single soft exclamation. Their weary faces
opened, all at once, into smiles. Even the men straightened.
The boy stared with his mouth open.

"A son," said Tatyana.

"When?"

"Today, fool."

"Where?"

"Beside the brook."

"Alone?"

Tatyana glanced at me. For a moment I thought she
would preserve her modesty by denying my assistance.

"The student delivered him," she said.

The old soldier examined me with new respect.

"A doctor?"

"Almost," Tatyana answered. "He left the university because of drink."

The company nodded. At last I had become understandable.

They surrounded the child. Each wanted to see his face,
though there was little to distinguish except nose, mouth and
wrinkles. Marfa declared him the image of his father, whom
she had seen alive only once and then at night.
The old soldier said his chest was broad and therefore
he would become a powerful man. The boy offered a
hazelnut, then, realizing Pavel lacked teeth, ate it himself.

No one reproached Tatyana for delaying them. The tallest man
took my knapsack. Marfa put one arm around the widow.
Another woman carried her bundle. Their poverty, divided among eleven,
had seemed merely multiplied; yet now, divided around one newborn
child, it became for a little while a kind of
wealth.

We camped that night near the shrine. There was no
village, but the spring was clean and the trees sheltered
us from the wind. In the common kettle they boiled
millet as thin as rainwater. Somebody produced an onion; someone
else a pinch of salt. I contributed the dried plums.
It was a miserable supper and tasted excellent.

Tatyana lay near the fire with Pavel against her. The
women attended her, whispering and occasionally scolding. They examined my
knots and pronounced them satisfactory, which pleased me more than
many compliments received later from educated persons. Marfa reheated water
and washed the mother. The old soldier carved a peg
for suspending a blanket as a screen.

After eating, the migrants prayed before the shrine. Their voices
were low and uneven. They asked for bread, work, health,
mercy for Pavel the elder, and protection for Pavel the
younger. They asked without eloquence and without confidence, like petitioners
accustomed to waiting in government corridors. Yet when Tatyana spoke
the words "for my son," her voice rang clearly.

I moved away towards the shore.

Night had gathered the colours into itself, but here and
there it released them in altered form: green sparks of
phosphorescence trembled where the waves broke; the mountains were vast
black silences beneath the stars; the foam shone dimly like
torn linen. Behind me the campfire reddened the trunks and
threw gigantic, wavering shadows of the migrants upon the road.
Those shadows looked stronger than the people who cast them.

I thought of the country from which they had come—the
grey villages, exhausted fields, barns empty as skulls; of officials
writing reports about crop failure in warm rooms; of merchants
storing grain while children searched manure heaps for undigested oats;
of priests preaching patience to stomachs that had practised it
beyond human endurance. Somewhere, I knew, well-fed men were arguing
that famine improved the moral fibre of the peasantry. May
the graveyard worms entrusted with those men's remains discover similar
opportunities for moral improvement.

And yet indignation, though nourishing to the young, does not
feed a child.

Behind me Pavel cried. At once the camp stirred. His
mother murmured; Marfa added advice; one of the men laughed
softly.

That cry crossed the dark road, passed through the trees
and reached the sea. It was a negligible sound beside
the surf, scarcely more than the squeak of a bird.
Nevertheless, I heard in it the insolence of life, which
arrives naked, ignorant and hungry into a world already partitioned
by landlords, customs officers, emperors and gods, and immediately demands
its share.

Yes, it demands warmth.

Yes, it demands milk.

Yes, it clenches empty hands and shouts as if the
earth, with all its mountains, harvests, prisons, palaces and blue
waters, had been prepared solely for its arrival.

And perhaps this impudence is our salvation.

In the morning the road would continue. There might be
work in Otchenchiri; there might be none. Tatyana might recover,
or fever might seize her. The child might become the
strong man she imagined, or vanish before winter beneath one
of those small nameless crosses that stand beside Russian roads.
Hope does not abolish probability. The Virgin herself, if she
watched, offered no contract.

But that night the mother slept with one hand over
her son. The migrants lay around them. Above, the stars
burned in the clear southern sky, and the mountains kept
their silver silence. The fire sank, sending up red sparks
which whirled briefly among the branches like souls too merry
to ascend in a straight line.

At dawn we set out together.

Tatyana was pale, but she walked. Marfa supported her on
one side and I on the other. The old soldier
carried Pavel for the first verst, holding him as solemnly
as a military banner; then the boy carried him for
twenty paces and nearly tripped; after that I took him.
Tatyana watched every transfer with the vigilance of an empress
guarding the heir to a troubled throne.

The sun rose behind the hills. Light poured down the
slopes, touched tree after tree, entered the ravines, and ran
across the sea in a broad golden road. The chestnut
leaves shook themselves awake. Streams hurried beside us. Far ahead,
around the promontory, lay Otchenchiri and whatever answer it intended
to give.

Tatyana gazed at the mountains, then at the bright water,
then at her son sleeping in my arms.

"Look at the world, Pavel," she whispered, though his eyes
were closed. "Look what a world!"

And the sea came beside us, mile after mile, murmuring
to the stones—murmuring of grief, perhaps, and endurance; of all
that is born, all that is broken, all that returns;
murmuring without promise and without despair, while we, hungry and
ridiculous, wounded and hopeful, walked onward beneath the sun.

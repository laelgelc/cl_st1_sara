## THE LAST TWO MILES

I had not slept for three nights.

There are men who speak of insomnia as though it
were a tiresome servant who has forgotten to draw the
curtains. They have never known the thing. True insomnia is
not an absence of sleep; it is an active, malignant
wakefulness. The mind becomes a lighted house in which every
door stands open and every trivial recollection walks noisily from
room to room.

At two in the morning I abandoned my bed and
went down to the private salon overlooking the harbour. The
hotel lay silent. Beyond the curtains Monaco glittered upon the
black Mediterranean, while the lamps along the quays made trembling
ladders of gold in the water.

Barclay sat before the dying fire.

He was a huge American, broad in the shoulders and
loose in the waist, with a large pock-marked face which
at first sight suggested brutality and at the second a
certain rough generosity. He had been gambling since dinner. A
sheaf of counters lay beside his elbow, and from their
melancholy scarcity I judged that the tables had not treated
him kindly.

He looked up as I entered.

"You too, Sir Henry?"

"I cannot sleep."

"Neither can I. But I've got forty thousand francs' worth
of reasons."

He pushed a chair toward me with his foot, struck
a match on the sole of his shoe and lighted
a cigarette.

"Sit down. There's brandy under that silver arrangement. I don't
know what they call it in French, but it pours."

I poured for us both. For some minutes we listened
to the faint clink of chains and the wash of
water against the harbour wall.

"You gamble too heavily," I said.

Barclay glanced at the counters.

"Not heavily. Badly. There's a difference. If a man loses
a million on a sound proposition, that's business. If he
loses five dollars on a fool proposition, that's gambling. To-night
I've been a gambler."

"You'll recover it."

"Maybe. Money's the one animal that'll generally come back if
you know where it feeds. Reputation's different. So's health. So's
time."

He said the last word without his usual flippancy. Then
he rose, crossed to the fire and stirred the embers
with the toe of his patent-leather shoe.

"Did you ever know Charlie Tavor?" he asked.

I knew the name, as every reader of geographical journals
knew it. Tavor had crossed portions of Central Asia which
had previously been represented on maps by little more than
conjectural shading. He had disappeared for three years in Mongolia,
emerged at Kashgar with a beard to his waist and
a collection of inscribed tablets, and subsequently made some remarkable
journey through southern Arabia. But I had never met him.

"I saw him once at the Royal Geographical Society," I
said. "A thin, fair man with stooping shoulders."

"That's Charlie. Thin as a surveyor's rod and twice as
straight."

"Is he still alive?"

"No."

Barclay returned to his chair. He held his hands toward
the fire, though the room was warm.

"He died in a house in Hampshire, Sir Henry, with
three servants downstairs, a nurse outside the door and six
peacocks making an infernal racket under the window. He had
a cedar lawn, a yew walk, a sunken garden, two
stone urns from Italy, and a butler who looked like
an archbishop. Charlie considered that a successful finish."

"I had imagined he died abroad."

"He did most of his living abroad. That's why he
wanted to do his dying in England."

Barclay paused. Cigarette smoke rose in a straight blue column
toward the painted ceiling.

"There are two sorts of explorers," he continued. "One sort
goes out with a government grant, a cinematograph operator, twelve
cases of patent food and a contract for six lectures
at Carnegie Hall. The other sort goes because there's a
blank place on the map and the blank place worries
him. Charlie was the second sort. He had more brains
for country than any man I ever met and less
brains for money than a child with a birthday sovereign.

"He could look at a dry valley and tell you
where water had run two thousand years before. He could
sit down with a broken inscription, three native legends and
an old Venetian trading account and resurrect a caravan road
that had been dead since Marco Polo was in knee-breeches.
But when he came home with the road, or the
mine, or the inscription—why, some fellow in a striped office
would take it away from him for the price of
lunch."

"You mean he was cheated?"

"I mean Charlie could have patented gravity and died owing
rent."

Barclay leaned back and closed his eyes.

"The worst of them was Nute Hardman. You know Hardman?"

"By reputation."

"That's the safest way."

Hardman was a financier whose name had been connected with
oil, shipping, copper, newspapers and politics. None of these connections
had improved the thing connected. Yet he had been decorated
by two foreign governments, possessed honorary degrees from three universities
and was said to be constructing a private museum in
New York.

"He's got the acquisitive instinct raised to a religion," Barclay
said. "A shark eats because it's hungry. Hardman ate because
something else had not yet been eaten. He wanted money,
but he wanted credit more. Not financial credit—he had plenty
of that. I mean glory. Scientific standing. A portrait in
oils with a globe under one hand and an extinct
bird under the other.

"Charlie met him after the first Mongolian trip. That trip
was a sweet proposition. Charlie had followed an ancient route
across the southern edge of the Shamo, where the old
Chinese traffic bent westward to avoid a chain of salt
marshes. He found the ruins of three stations, a nest
of records and, most important, indications of copper in a
range the Russians had guessed at but never examined.

"He needed money to go back.

"Hardman gave him eight thousand dollars."

"That was generous."

"Wait. For eight thousand dollars Hardman got every commercial right
Charlie might discover, every specimen except duplicates, the motion-picture rights,
the book rights in America and an option on Charlie's
boots if they developed mineral value. Charlie signed. He thought
the phrase 'all fruits of the expedition' referred to dried
apricots.

"He went back, located the copper and nearly died doing
it. Hardman formed the Trans-Asiatic Development Company, capitalized the thing
at twelve million, sold nine million to the public, and
presented Charlie with a silver cigarette-box engraved: 'To the Intrepid
Pioneer.'"

"Did Tavor protest?"

"Charlie? He wrote a letter thanking him for the cigarette-box."

Barclay's large face twisted.

"That was Charlie's trouble. He thought a contract represented the
intention of two honest men. Hardman knew it represented the
appetite of the cleverer one.

"Then came Arabia. Charlie had a theory about the incense
roads. Most fellows traced them north from Hadhramaut in the
conventional line, but Charlie had picked up references suggesting that
one branch swung deep into the Empty Quarter—El-Khali—and touched an
oasis which had disappeared from recorded geography.

"People laughed. They always laughed before Charlie left and printed
monographs after he returned.

"He was gone twenty-eight months. For eleven of them no
message came out. A Frenchman said he was dead. A
Turkish merchant said he had been taken as a slave.
A professor in Berlin proved conclusively that the oasis had
never existed. Then one morning Charlie walked into Aden wearing
a robe, one sandal and a British military helmet he
had got from nobody knew where.

"He had found the road. He had found the buried
walls of two depots and a stone covered with Sabaean
characters. He had also found enough geological evidence to make
a certain stretch of desert interesting to oil men.

"Hardman got that too."

"How?"

"The old agreement had a tail. Lawyers manufacture tails for
that purpose. It covered discoveries arising directly or indirectly from
information obtained during the Mongolian expedition. Hardman's lawyers argued that
Charlie had first discussed Arabian trade routes with a Chinese
scholar in Kashgar, and therefore Arabia had arisen indirectly out
of Mongolia.

"It was dirty. It was ridiculous. It was also legal
enough to keep Charlie in court until the Day of
Judgment. He surrendered for five thousand dollars and another testimonial."

"I wonder that his friends permitted it."

"His friends were explorers. They had two hundred dollars among
them and three hundred thousand miles of advice."

Barclay stood and drew the curtains farther apart. The night
had begun to pale behind the roofs. On the harbour,
a small launch moved without visible sound.

"After that," he said, "Charlie vanished.

"Not vanished in the newspaper sense. No reporters came round
asking what hat he had worn. He simply dropped out.
One man heard of him at Muscat. Another had seen
him near Urga. There was a rumour from the Persian
Gulf and a yarn from a Russian surveyor who claimed
to have passed him in a district where no Englishman
had any reasonable business. Four years went by. Then five.

"I thought he was dead.

"Six years after Arabia I was in New York, living
at the Waldorf and trying to promote a railroad that
had everything except rails, roadbed and public confidence. One wet
November afternoon a clerk brought up a card.

"CHARLES TAVOR.

"That was all. Charlie never put letters after his name.
He had enough of them before it.

"I told the clerk to send him in.

"He came through the door slowly, and for perhaps three
seconds I did not recognize him. His hair was white
at the temples. His clothes hung from him as though
they belonged to a larger, absent man. His face had
a smooth, grey look—not pale, exactly, but like plaster that
has not dried.

"'Charlie!' I said. 'Where in thunder have you been?'

"'Several places,' he said.

"That was like him. You could ask Charlie where he
had spent six years and get an answer suitable for
a weekend.

"He sat down. I ordered food. He wouldn't touch it.
I offered whisky. He shook his head. Then I noticed
his hands. They were perfectly steady until he reached for
a glass of water. Halfway to it, the fingers jumped
as if an electric current had struck them.

"'What's the matter?' I asked.

"'I've damaged myself, Barclay.'

"'Fever?'

"'No. A drug.'

"That was the last thing I expected. Charlie had never
smoked anything stronger than three cigarettes after dinner.

"He explained. Somewhere beyond the caravan routes—he wouldn't tell me
where—he had lived with a tribe who chewed a bitter
resin. It kept a man awake. More than awake. It
abolished fatigue. A man could walk all night and all
day, go without food, endure heat that would drop a
camel, and feel himself as fresh as when he started.

"'Useful stuff,' I said.

"'Abominably useful,' said Charlie.

"He had used it first to cross a desert belt
after losing his animals. Then he used it because work
pressed. Then because without it ordinary endurance seemed intolerably slow.
For three years he had driven himself as a mechanic
drives an engine with the safety valve tied down.

"'And now?' I asked.

"'Now the engine has objected.'

"He had suffered a seizure in Alexandria. Not a fit
exactly. Something involving the heart and nervous system. A doctor
there had examined him; two specialists in Paris had confirmed
the opinion. The resin caused a progressive alteration—some poisoning of
the nerve centres. The first seizure was a warning. The
second would kill him.

"'When?' I said.

"'Six months after the first.'

"Charlie said it quietly.

"I laughed at him. What else could I do?

"'Doctors say anything,' I told him. 'It keeps the fees
moving.'

"'These men know.'

"'No man can name a date like that.'

"'They can in this case. The stages are regular. There
may be a variation of a day or two, but
not more.'

"He took a folded paper from his pocket. It was
full of figures, pulse records, chemical terms and French obiter
dicta. I couldn't make head or tail of it, but
it looked expensive.

"'How long have you got?' I asked.

"'Five months and twenty-three days.'

"That, Sir Henry, altered the atmosphere."

Barclay reached for the brandy and poured half a glass.

"I asked him what he intended to do.

"'Live,' he said.

"'You've been doing that.'

"'No, Barclay. I have been travelling.'

"There was a difference to Charlie.

"He told me something I had never known. All his
life, from the time he was a boy, he had
possessed one ridiculous, fixed ambition. Not to find a city,
cross a desert, climb a mountain or be President of
the Royal Geographical Society. He wanted to live as an
English country gentleman.

"Not permanently. He had never expected permanence. But for one
year—later he reduced it to six months—he wanted an old
house with lawns running down from the terrace, clipped yews,
formal beds and a lake with a stone bridge. He
wanted a library where the books had been collected by
dead people. He wanted to dress for dinner and have
a man behind his chair. He wanted to complain about
the gardener, receive dull neighbours and ring a bell without
knowing who answered it.

"'You've slept in palaces,' I said.

"'As a guest. Usually on the floor.'

"'You've had servants.'

"'Camel men. They leave when shot at.'

"'Why this particular lunacy?'

"He smiled. 'My father was a schoolmaster. Every summer we
walked past a place called Orleton Hall. I used to
look through the gates. I suppose I have never stopped
looking.'

"'How much money have you?'

"He produced a little memorandum book. Charlie had three hundred
and eighty pounds. That included money due for an article
on prehistoric wells.

"'And what will your country-house performance cost?'

"'I have made inquiries. There is a furnished place in
Hampshire which can be had until March. With servants, horses
and other charges, I calculate two thousand pounds. To do
it properly, perhaps twenty-five hundred.'

"Ten thousand dollars," Barclay said. "That was the figure."

"You gave it to him?" I asked.

"I didn't have it."

"You could have raised it."

"My dear Sir Henry, at that particular moment my railroad
had eaten my cash, pledged my securities and was nibbling
at my furniture. I could have raised ten thousand on
a pistol, but the transaction would have distressed the cashier.

"I told Charlie I'd get it somehow.

"He said, 'I hoped you might.'

"Then he put a packet on the table.

"It was a map."

Barclay smiled faintly at the fire.

"Not one of your romantic maps, browned with age and
marked by a skull. It was straight technical work: latitudes,
longitudes, bearings, soundings, distances, coastal observations. Charlie had made it,
so it was accurate. If Charlie marked a stone three
feet east of another stone, that stone would be there
waiting.

"'What's this?' I asked.

"'A way to the money.'

"'What money?'

"'Gold. About three hundred thousand dollars.'

"That interested me.

"'Yours?'

"'No.'

"'Who owns it?'

"'That is a legal question.'

"'Where is it?'

"'The map shows you.'

"I opened the sheets. Charlie stopped me before I had
examined them properly.

"'I don't want you to go, Barclay.'

"'Why not?'

"'Because it would cost too much. And I require the
money now.'

"'Then sell it.'

"'To whom?'

"Straightaway I thought of Hardman.

"I didn't tell Charlie. If I had, he would have
refused. He bore no malice, but he disliked causing inconvenience.
Being robbed by Hardman was, in Charlie's mind, no reason
for troubling the man.

"So I asked questions.

"Was the gold there?

"Yes.

"Was it worth three hundred thousand dollars?

"At the current valuation, rather more.

"Was the map accurate?

"Absolutely.

"How far was the place from civilization?

"That depended on one's definition. It could be reached within
several days of a coastal town.

"What kind of country?

"A wide, level plain.

"Desert?

"'It has many of the characteristics of a desert,' Charlie
said.

"Was there water?

"Too much, he said, and then changed the subject.

"I ought to have noticed that. But I was busy
thinking commercially.

"Was the plain sand?

"No. Its surface was pale, almost white in places—chalky to
the eye, though not composed solely of chalk.

"Could vehicles cross it?

"Special vehicles.

"Were there natives?

"None resident.

"Political complications?

"Probably not. The ownership of the gold might be disputed
if recovered, but the discoverer's position would be extremely strong.

"And the difficulties?

"Charlie considered.

"'The approach is simple until the last two miles.'

"'And then?'

"'Then it becomes exceptionally difficult.'

"I asked what equipment would be required. He said that
depended upon the method adopted, but a properly financed expedition,
directed by competent engineers, could reach the site.

"Now, Sir Henry, put yourself in my chair. Here was
Charlie Tavor, the one man on earth who didn't fake
geographical dope. Here was a hand-drawn map. Here was three
hundred thousand dollars in gold on a chalk-coloured plain within
several days of a coastal town. And here was Nute
Hardman, who had made millions stealing Charlie's work.

"I could feel the devil move up beside me and
whisper, 'Barclay, this is your deal.'"

"You intended to deceive him?"

"I intended to sell him the map."

"Knowing the expedition would be exceptionally difficult?"

"Every expedition is exceptionally difficult when Hardman pays for it."

Barclay grinned.

"Besides, I hadn't yet understood Charlie's proposition. I only knew
there was a hook in it. Charlie never lied. He
couldn't. If he told you the moon was made of
Stilton, you could order biscuits. But during those six missing
years he had apparently learned that a man may tell
the truth in such a way that another fellow's greed
does the lying for him.

"I sent him to his hotel and told him to
leave the map with me. Then I telephoned Hardman.

"He wouldn't speak to me.

"I called again and said the message concerned an unpublished
discovery by Charles Tavor.

"That hooked him.

"At four o'clock next afternoon I was in Hardman's office."

Barclay crushed his cigarette into the tray.

"You've seen reptile houses, Sir Henry? Hardman's office had that
temperature. Warm, polished and without air. He sat behind a
desk large enough for a treaty conference. He was a
small man with a dome of yellowish forehead and little
eyes which never looked directly at anything until he meant
to bite it.

"'Where is Tavor?' he said.

"'Sick.'

"'What has he found?'

"'Gold.'

"'How much?'

"'Three hundred thousand dollars.'

"'Where?'

"'That costs ten thousand.'

"He stared at me.

"'You're selling information?'

"'An accurate map.'

"'Why doesn't Tavor come himself?'

"'He doesn't care to.'

"Hardman understood that. He had the grace to look at
his blotting-paper.

"'How do I know the map is genuine?'

"'Because Tavor made it.'

"'That is not a financial guarantee.'

"'I'll give you one.'

"That surprised him. It also surprised me, because until the
words came out I hadn't decided what guarantee I could
give.

"'What kind?'

"'Twenty thousand dollars in United States government bonds, lodged with
your bank. If any material statement I make concerning the
place proves false, the bonds are yours.'

"Hardman leaned back.

"'You have twenty thousand in government bonds?'

"'I can get them.'

"That was not strictly cheerful, but it was true.

"'State the facts,' he said.

"I took out a sheet of paper. Charlie and I
had spent most of the previous night composing it. Not
lying—trimming. There's a difference. A lie manufactures a false fact.
Good trimming merely allows the customer to manufacture it himself.

"I read:

"'First: Mr. Charles Tavor has identified and charted gold to
the present market value of not less than three hundred
thousand dollars.

"'Second: the charted position lies upon a broad and substantially
level plain, whose surface is pale and of a chalk-like
appearance over large areas.

"'Third: the plain is uninhabited and the gold is not
now in the physical possession of any private individual.

"'Fourth: the position may be approached within a period of
several days from a recognized coastal town, ordinary weather and
modern transport being assumed.

"'Fifth: no unusual physical difficulty need be encountered upon the
journey except in respect of the final two miles.

"'Sixth: the map and coordinates are correct.'

"Hardman held out his hand.

"'Let me see that.'

"He read it twice.

"'What country?'

"'Not included.'

"'Asia?'

"'Not included.'

"'Africa?'

"'Not included.'

"'You expect me to pay ten thousand dollars without knowing
the continent?'

"'I expect you to pay ten thousand for information worth
three hundred thousand.'

"He got up and began walking. I could see the
picture forming behind those little eyes: Hardman leading a major
expedition into an unknown waste; Hardman discovering an ancient treasure;
Hardman giving interviews beneath a large map; Hardman presenting a
gold relic to a museum founded by Hardman.

"Money alone would not have landed him. Glory did it.

"'The gold is ancient?' he asked.

"'I haven't said so.'

"'Associated with ruins?'

"'I haven't said so.'

"'A caravan?'

"'That's your word.'

"'Tavor's recent work has been in Arabia.'

"'That's public knowledge.'

"'Or Mongolia.'

"'Also public.'

"He looked annoyed. Then his face softened.

"'Barclay, we need not conduct this transaction like horse traders.
Tavor and I have had business relations. I have always
admired him.'

"'He keeps the cigarette-box polished.'

"Hardman's eyes narrowed.

"'If he needs money, perhaps I could assist him personally.'

"'Ten thousand buys the map.'

"'Five.'

"'Ten.'

"'Seven, and no guarantee required.'

"'Ten, with the guarantee.'

"He sat down again.

"'Why are you so confident?'

"'Because every word on that paper is straight stuff.'

"'Why does Tavor not recover the gold himself?'

"'He cannot.'

"'Lack of funds?'

"'Among other things.'

"'How much would recovery cost?'

"'I don't know.'

"'One hundred thousand?'

"'Possibly.'

"'Two hundred?'

"'Possibly.'

"'More than the value?'

"'A badly run expedition can spend anything.'

"That was another point I had worked out with Charlie.
Hardman could not say we promised a profit. We promised
gold.

"He tapped the paper.

"'What is the nature of the final difficulty?'

"'That's shown by the map.'

"'Mountains?'

"'The paper says only that the plain is level.'

"'A gorge?'

"'Not included.'

"'Hostile tribes?'

"'The plain is uninhabited.'

"He questioned me for an hour. I gave him nothing
beyond the written statements. The less I gave him, the
more he supplied. He had decided on the southern Gobi
before we finished. I could almost see his camels.

"At last he said: 'I want independent verification by a
geographer.'

"'No.'

"'Then by an engineer under a pledge of secrecy.'

"'No.'

"'I will not buy blind.'

"'Then don't.'

"I folded the paper and put it in my pocket.
That was the most expensive gesture I ever made, because
I didn't own the bonds I had offered and had
no idea how to get them. But the devil was
still working our side.

"As I reached the door, Hardman said, 'Wait.'

"I waited.

"'The map is accurate in every particular?'

"'Yes.'

"'The position can be found from it?'

"'Yes.'

"'And the gold is there?'

"'Yes.'

"'Tavor himself guarantees these points?'

"'Tavor guarantees them to me. I guarantee them to you.'

"'Ten thousand dollars?'

"'Cash.'

"'Against twenty thousand in government bonds?'

"'Lodged before you pay.'

"'If any one of those six assertions is false, the
bonds become mine?'

"'Yes.'

"'If they are all true, I have no claim, irrespective
of recovery costs?'

"'Correct.'

"He pressed a button.

"'My lawyers will draft it.'

"'My lawyers will read it.'

"'You have lawyers?'

"'For this deal I'll rent some.'

"That was how we shook hands."

"But the bonds?" I asked.

Barclay sighed.

"The bonds nearly killed me. The railroad couldn't help. My
bank considered me a picturesque risk. I went to three
men who owed me favours, and each remembered he owed
somebody else money. Finally I put up every unencumbered thing
I possessed and borrowed the balance from a bookmaker at
terms which would have embarrassed a pirate.

"The bonds went into escrow.

"Hardman's lawyers spent two days trying to improve the English
language in his favour. My fellow—a little Hebrew attorney named
Schlesinger, with more brains than the Supreme Court—kept bringing them
back to the six assertions. At last all parties signed.

"Hardman handed me ten thousand dollars.

"I handed him Charlie's sealed packet.

"He opened it before I left.

"For ten seconds he stared at the first sheet. Then
he looked up.

"'This is a marine chart.'

"'Is it?'

"'The coastal town is St. John's, Newfoundland.'

"'So the map says.'

"He opened the second sheet. There were latitude and longitude,
a track across the North Atlantic and a carefully marked
position.

"His face altered.

"'What is this?'

"'Your property.'

"'The plain—'

"'Is shown.'

"'These are soundings!'

"'Then I guess they're soundings.'

"He ran to a cabinet, pulled out an atlas and
began turning pages. I didn't wait. The sale was complete,
and there are moments when a wise man gets on
an elevator before the shooting starts."

Barclay laughed softly.

"Charlie was in my room when I returned. He stood
at the window with his hat in his hands.

"'Did he buy it?' he asked.

"'He bought it.'

"I laid the money on the table.

"He looked at the bills, and the plaster colour in
his face seemed to warm.

"'That will be enough,' he said.

"'It had better be. I've got my entire financial anatomy
hanging on your map.'

"He touched the packet of money but did not count
it.

"'Barclay, I hope you have not lied.'

"'Not once.'

"'Or implied anything dishonourable?'

"'Charlie, implication is the buyer's department.'

"He did not like that, but he wanted Orleton—or the
substitute we had found in Hampshire—more than he disliked Hardman's
education.

"He left for England the following week."

"What did Hardman do?" I asked.

"What could he do? At first he thought the map
was a joke. Then he sent for oceanographers, salvage men
and marine engineers—privately, you understand. That secrecy mattered.

"One engineer told him the proposition was beyond existing equipment.
Another said it might be possible with a specially designed
apparatus. A third offered to conduct preliminary experiments for fifty
thousand dollars and expenses. Hardman spent money.

"He always spent when prestige was ahead of him.

"For a while there were rumours of a scientific expedition
to study deep-water pressures in the North Atlantic. Hardman endowed
a chair of submarine engineering. He bought patents. He had
a steel chamber built that crushed like a biscuit during
its first test. Then the war in Europe changed prices
and priorities, experts became unavailable, ships were requisitioned and the
whole enterprise stalled.

"Meanwhile Charlie rented his house."

Barclay's voice changed. The hard, comic edge went out of
it.

"It was called Merecombe. Not a great place, but old
enough. Red brick, grey stone about the windows, and chimneys
which leaned at different angles as though discussing the weather.
The gardens had been laid out under Queen Anne. There
was a long canal with lilies, a cedar lawn and
a yew walk ending in two stone urns.

"Charlie took it for six months.

"He hired the proper people. A butler named Baines, who
had served dukes and disapproved of explorers. A French cook.
Two footmen. A housekeeper, a gardener, a groom and a
boy whose sole apparent duty was to be shouted for.

"He bought evening clothes. He joined the county hunt, though
the doctor forbade him to ride. He gave a dinner
for twelve neighbours and listened gravely while they talked of
drains, pheasants and a disputed right of way. He had
cards engraved. He ordered wine and learned to send back
the wrong bottle with an air of inherited irritation.

"I visited him in January.

"It was the coldest day I remember. Frost lay white
over the lawns and the clipped yews were black against
it. A footman opened the door, another took my coat,
and Baines announced me as though my arrival affected European
diplomacy.

"Charlie came down the staircase.

"He was thinner. His face had that dreadful smoothness, but
he wore a velvet smoking-jacket and carried himself like the
owner of the county.

"'Barclay,' he said, 'you are late for tea.'

"'The train was late.'

"'One does not blame trains before servants.'

"That was Charlie's idea of the governing class.

"We drank tea in the library. The fire was enormous.
There were old books, old portraits and an elderly spaniel
belonging to the landlord. Charlie put his hand on the
dog's head and looked about the room.

"'It is exactly right,' he said.

"'Worth ten thousand?'

"'Worth the journeys.'

"He had become absurdly happy. Every small detail delighted him—the
morning letters on a silver tray, the gardener asking permission
to cut a branch, the sound of a gong before
dinner. He had spent half his life where a man
ate from a tin beside a dead camel, yet he
could take pleasure in folded napkins without pretending they were
unimportant.

"That evening we dressed. Baines decanted a claret which cost
more than Charlie's first expedition. After dinner we sat in
the long room overlooking the frozen canal.

"Charlie asked about Hardman.

"'He's bucking the Atlantic.'

"'Will he succeed?'

"'Not with the machinery he has.'

"'Somebody will one day.'

"'Maybe.'

"'Then the gold will be recovered.'

"'Maybe.'

"He was silent.

"'You regret it?' I asked.

"'No. But the gold belongs to others.'

"'So did the copper you found. Hardman didn't lose sleep.'

"'That is not an answer.'

"'It's the only one you're getting.'

"He smiled. 'You have been a very good friend, Barclay.'

"I said something foolish about business. He let it pass.

"At eleven Baines entered and announced that it was time
for Mr. Tavor's medicine. Charlie looked at me.

"'You see? Even death is more civil when announced by
a butler.'

"He died three weeks later."

"The doctors' calculation was right?"

"To the day."

Barclay bent over the fire and stirred the grey ash.

"He knew in the morning. There was numbness in one
hand and a particular flickering of the sight. He ordered
breakfast in the library, dictated two letters and sent for
the gardener.

"The gardener came in carrying his cap. Charlie told him
the west border had been too red the previous summer
and ought to have more white flowers.

"'But you won't be here to see it, sir,' the
gardener said. The man knew.

"'No,' Charlie replied. 'That is no reason for getting it
wrong.'

"After luncheon he had them move his bed beside the
window. From there he could see the cedar, the canal
and the yew walk. Baines opened the curtains. Charlie died
at sunset while the footmen were lighting the lamps downstairs.

"He had five pounds left after the bills."

For a time neither of us spoke. The fire settled
with a whisper. Outside, the eastern sky had become the
colour of pearl.

"And Hardman?" I asked at last. "Did he claim the
bonds?"

"He tried."

"On what ground?"

"Every ground except a good one.

"First he alleged that the gold was not upon a
plain. Schlesinger produced hydrographic surveys showing that the site lay
on an extensive abyssal plain with a gradient flatter than
plenty of country Hardman had mined.

"Then Hardman said the surface was not chalk. We had
never said it was chalk. We said chalk-like. The bottom
deposits included pale calcareous ooze composed largely of the remains
of microscopic organisms. Chalk-like was almost too modest.

"He argued the place could not be approached in several
days from St. John's. A chartered steamer had reached the
coordinates well within the period.

"He argued that the plain was not uninhabited. No human
beings resided there. Even Hardman's lawyers couldn't manufacture a tenant
two miles under the Atlantic.

"He said there were unusual difficulties before the final two
miles. Weather, navigation and sea travel, we answered, were ordinary
difficulties and expressly qualified by the phrase concerning modern transport.

"Finally he attacked the value and existence of the gold.

"That was the dangerous point. But Charlie had documentation—cargo lists,
insurance schedules, sworn records and testimony concerning the shipment. The
value exceeded three hundred thousand dollars at the date of
contract. The gold had gone down in the ship and
had never come up.

"The position was accurately charted.

"Every word was true."

"And your bonds?"

"Came back with interest."

I looked at him across the cold room.

"Surely Hardman sued?"

"He began proceedings. Then Schlesinger asked whether he desired the
full map, the contract, the engineering reports and the object
of his secret expedition read in open court.

"That stopped him.

"Remember, Hardman had been advertising himself as a patron of
serious science. He could not afford to have the newspapers
print that he had paid ten thousand dollars for what
he imagined was a lost Asiatic caravan, then spent another
hundred thousand trying to reach a treasure whose location every
schoolboy could name.

"He withdrew. Publicly the submarine work remained a contribution to
oceanography. Privately he called me names for twenty minutes over
the telephone.

"Some were inaccurate. Most were repetitive."

I turned the matter over in my mind. One point
still eluded me.

"You said the final two miles presented the only exceptional
difficulty."

"That's right."

"But if the plain was the ocean floor—"

Barclay looked at me.

The expression on his broad, scarred face had become almost
innocent.

"Where exactly was the gold?"

He rose and drew the curtains wide.

Dawn had come to Monaco. The harbour shone steel-blue below
us; roofs, terraces and pale hotel walls climbed into the
morning. Far out upon the Mediterranean, a liner moved slowly
across the brightening horizon, trailing a long thread of smoke.

Barclay watched it for a moment.

"Charlie's level plain," he said, "was the bed of the
North Atlantic. The chalk was deep-sea ooze. The coastal town
was St. John's. And those last two difficult miles—"

He pointed downward.

"—were vertical."

I understood then.

"The Titanic?"

"The Titanic," said Barclay. "The gold was in her submerged
hold."

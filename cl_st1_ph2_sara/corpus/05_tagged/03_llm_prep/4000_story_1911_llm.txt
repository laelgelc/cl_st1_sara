I see a young chap and a gal leaning over
the railings there, and I don't disturb 'em. If they
was to topple into the river through leaning too far,
I might tap 'em on the shoulder; but so long
as they keep their feet and don't obstruct the pavement,
it ain't no business o' mine. Young people must learn,
same as old ones did afore 'em.

Some men regret getting married, and some regret not getting
married, and the sensible ones regret both. I knew a
ship's carpenter once who kept out of it till he
was sixty-five, and then married a widow with four grown-up
sons. He spent the first part of his life wishing
'e had a family, and the last part wishing somebody
else 'ad 'em. There's no pleasing everybody.

Talking o' marriage reminds me of Sam Small and the
time 'is sister's boy, Joe, got engaged to Miss Gill.
Sam's sister lived at Poplar, and Joe was her only
son. He was twenty-one, with a clean collar every Sunday
and more confidence than a man of forty, and when
he told 'is mother that he was going to marry,
she spoke to Sam about it.

"You're 'is uncle," she ses. "You ought to advise 'im."

"I've never been married," ses Sam.

"That's why," she ses. "A man that's been married might
be prejudiced."

Sam scratched 'is chin and looked thoughtful. He was staying
ashore with Ginger Dick and Peter Russet at the time,
the three of 'em having been paid off from the
brig *Martha Ann* with more money in their pockets than
was good for 'em.

"I'll talk to 'im," he ses. "I'll put 'im right."

"You won't make 'im angry?" ses his sister.

"Me?" ses Sam. "I shall just reason with 'im quiet
and manly."

He went off that evening to the lodging-house where Ginger
and Peter was sitting with their boots on the mantelpiece,
smoking and wondering what to spend their money on next.
Ginger had already bought a concertina he couldn't play, and
Peter had got a new overcoat with a velvet collar
that made 'im look like an undertaker on 'oliday.

"I want your 'elp," ses Sam.

Peter took his boots down at once.

"Not if it means lending," he ses.

"It ain't," ses Sam. "It's my sister's boy, Joe. He's
going to get married."

Ginger shook his head and clicked 'is tongue.

"At twenty-one," ses Sam.

Ginger clicked it agin, louder.

"Pore young feller," ses Peter. "Can't nothing be done?"

"That's wot I want you two for," ses Sam. "You
know women."

Ginger sat up and pushed his cap back. If there
was one thing Ginger liked, it was being told 'e
knew women. He had been walking out with three at
different times, and two of 'em had married other men
while he was at sea. The third had moved and
forgot to leave 'er address.

"I know 'em root and branch," he ses.

"So do I," ses Peter. "I can tell wot a
woman's thinking by the way she looks at another woman's
bonnet."

"You come round to my sister's with me to-morrow night,"
ses Sam. "We'll talk to Joe. Three experienced men ought
to be able to save one boy."

"Easy," ses Ginger.

They went round next evening, and Joe, who was sitting
in the parlour with a photograph in front of 'im,
welcomed 'em very hearty. He was a good-tempered young chap,
and instead of getting annoyed when Sam mentioned the engagement,
he smiled all over 'is face.

"So you're thinking of getting married?" ses Sam, severe-like.

"I'm not thinking," ses Joe. "It's settled."

"There's time to unsettle it," ses Peter.

Joe shook his head.

"Twenty-one," ses Ginger. "Why, at twenty-one a man don't know
whether 'e wants tea or coffee till 'e's swallowed it."

"I know wot I want," ses Joe.

"Course you think you do," ses Sam. "That's where the
danger is. Wot do you know about gals?"

Joe thought a moment.

"Not much," he ses, "except Miss Gill."

Ginger gave a groan.

"They're artful," he ses. "You bear that in mind. Artful
ain't the word for it, but it'll 'ave to do
till somebody makes a stronger one."

"And extravagant," ses Peter. "A gal'll look at a sixpenny
ribbon for ten minutes, and then spend half a crown
because she's tired of standing."

"And changeable," ses Sam. "They change their minds oftener than
a ship changes 'er course in a fog."

Joe listened very respectful.

"I dare say you're right," he ses.

The three of 'em looked at each other, rather taken
aback.

"Dare say?" ses Ginger. "We know."

"I'm sure you do," ses Joe. "You've had experience."

Peter coughed and pulled down his waistcoat.

"More than enough," he ses.

"I knew a gal once," ses Ginger, "as said she'd
wait for me for ever. I was away eleven months,
and when I come back she'd been married six and
was talking about naming the baby after 'er husband."

"Shocking," ses Joe.

"And I knew one," ses Peter, "as told me she
didn't care for money. She proved it by spending mine."

"Terrible," ses Joe.

Sam leaned forward and tapped 'im on the knee.

"That's why we're warning you," he ses. "You take three
men of the world like us. We ain't got nothing
to gain by it. We don't want to see you
throw yourself away."

"I'm much obliged," ses Joe. "And everything you say is
true—about most women."

"Most?" ses Ginger.

"All except Miss Gill," ses Joe, smiling agin. "She's different."

Ginger's face fell.

"They always are," he ses.

"I mean it," ses Joe. "She's sensible, steady, affectionate, economical,
and as innocent as a child."

Peter looked at Sam, and Sam looked at Ginger.

"How old?" ses Peter.

"Twenty," ses Joe.

"Twenty!" ses Ginger. "And innocent! Why, a gal of twenty
knows things a man of fifty wouldn't like to 'ear."

"Miss Gill don't," ses Joe. "She's never had another sweetheart."

"How d'you know?" ses Sam.

"She told me."

Ginger got up and walked twice round the table. Then
he stopped in front of Joe.

"P'r'aps she told the others the same," he ses.

"There wasn't no others," ses Joe. "You'll understand when you
meet 'er."

"We don't want to meet 'er," ses Sam.

"I do," ses Ginger, quick.

Sam turned and stared at 'im.

"Only to judge," ses Ginger. "For Joe's sake."

Peter said he wouldn't mind judging too, and Joe, who
seemed pleased at their interest, arranged for 'em to meet
Miss Gill on the following Saturday evening.

She came to tea at Sam's sister's, and when she
entered the room all three men stopped talking. She was
a small, neat-looking gal, with bright eyes and a quiet
way of smiling as if she knew something pleasant and
was considering whether to tell it. She shook hands with
Sam and Peter, but when Ginger held out 'is hand
she gave the least little start and looked at 'im
twice.

Ginger noticed it. So did Peter. Sam was looking at
the cake.

Miss Gill sat beside Joe and listened while the three
men told stories about foreign parts. Ginger spoke most. He
had never been farther east than Rotterdam, but before tea
was over he had given Miss Gill a very good
idea of Bombay, Yokohama, and the habits of South Sea
Islanders. Peter tried to correct 'im once or twice, but
Ginger talked louder.

"You must have had a very adventurous life, Mr. Dick,"
ses Miss Gill.

"I 'ave," ses Ginger, looking at 'er. "But lonely."

"Lonely?" she ses, soft-like.

"Terrible," ses Ginger.

Joe passed 'im the bread and butter and told 'im
to keep 'is strength up.

When Miss Gill went home, Joe walked with 'er. The
moment the door shut behind 'em, Ginger turned on Sam.

"You're right," he ses. "This marriage must be stopped."

"Why?" ses Peter.

"Because that gal ain't suited to Joe," ses Ginger. "A
nice gal, mind you, but not suited."

"She seemed very fond of 'im," ses Sam.

"That's only 'er innocence," ses Ginger. "She don't know 'er
own mind yet."

"And you do, I s'pose?" ses Peter.

"I know human nature," ses Ginger. "Did you see the
way she looked at me?"

"She was wondering whether Bombay was in Japan," ses Peter.

Ginger took no notice.

"I could take that gal away from Joe in a
week," he ses. "Not that I want to. I wouldn't
hurt the boy's feelings for anything. But it might be
a kindness to show 'im 'ow mistaken he is."

Sam rubbed his nose.

"I don't like it," he ses.

"It's for 'is good," ses Ginger. "If she can be
took away by me, she ain't fit to marry 'im."

"And if she can't?" ses Peter.

Ginger smiled.

"She can," he ses.

By Monday evening he had arranged to meet Miss Gill
the following Wednesday. He did it very artful, telling Joe
that he wanted to talk to 'er about a little
surprise for 'im. Joe thanked 'im, and Miss Gill, after
looking doubtful for some time, consented to go with Ginger
to the Zoological Gardens.

Ginger bought a new necktie for the occasion and had
'is boots cleaned twice. On Wednesday morning he went out
before breakfast, and Peter, who was watching from the bedroom
window, said he walked past the house three times afore
he remembered which way the omnibus went.

He met Miss Gill at two o'clock. She was dressed
very quiet, but Ginger said afterward that everybody in the
Gardens looked at 'er. The truth was they looked mostly
at Ginger, because 'e had put his new necktie on
with the wide end behind.

They started with the lions, and Miss Gill shivered and
moved closer to 'im when one of 'em roared.

"Don't be frightened," ses Ginger. "He can't get out."

"I know," she ses. "But I'm so nervous."

"If he did get out," ses Ginger, swelling up, "I'd
know wot to do."

"Would you?" she ses, looking at 'im.

"I'd stand between you and 'im," ses Ginger.

Miss Gill looked grateful, and Ginger, backing away careless-like to
show how little he thought of lions, trod on a
child's hoop and sat down. The child screamed, its mother
screamed louder, and Ginger gave 'em twopence to go away.

After that they saw the monkeys, and Miss Gill said
one of 'em reminded 'er of somebody. Ginger asked who,
and she laughed and said she wouldn't tell 'im for
worlds. He laughed too, though he didn't see much in
it, and bought 'er a bag of nuts.

At the elephant-house she said she had always wanted to
ride on an elephant, and Ginger paid for both of
'em. He didn't want to ride 'imself, but he wasn't
going to let 'er go alone. The elephant got up
at both ends separately, and Ginger clutched the seat with
one hand and Miss Gill with the other.

"Ooh!" ses Miss Gill.

"It's all right," ses Ginger, with 'is eyes shut.

"You're not afraid?" she ses.

"Me?" ses Ginger. "I was just thinking."

The elephant took six steps and stopped. Ginger opened one
eye.

"It's very high," ses Miss Gill.

"Nothing when you're used to it," ses Ginger.

Then the elephant moved agin, and Ginger's hat dropped off.
A boy picked it up and wore it round the
enclosure till Ginger offered threepence. By the time they got
down, Ginger said elephants was stupid beasts and ought not
to be encouraged.

Miss Gill was full of apologies for suggesting it.

"I've been such a trouble to you," she ses.

"Trouble?" ses Ginger. "I'd ride an elephant to the North
Pole for you."

She turned 'er head away, but not afore he saw
'er smile.

They had tea and buns, and then Ginger took 'er
on a roundabout outside the Gardens. Miss Gill chose a
horse, and Ginger, not to be outdone, chose one alongside.
The machine started, and every time he tried to speak
to 'er his horse went up while hers went down.
He called "Miss Gill!" fourteen times, and once caught hold
of an elderly lady's bonnet by mistake. When it stopped
he got off the wrong side and walked round twice
looking for Miss Gill, who was standing behind 'im.

After that they tried a boat-swing. Ginger didn't like it
from the first, but Miss Gill said it was so
romantic, and he sat opposite 'er and pulled the rope.
In less than a minute he wished 'e hadn't. Miss
Gill laughed, with 'er hair blowing about 'er face, while
Ginger grew pale and swallowed hard.

"Higher!" she ses.

Ginger pulled once more and then shut 'is eyes.

When the swing stopped he remained seated.

"Aren't you coming?" ses Miss Gill.

"I'm giving the next party a start," ses Ginger.

The man in charge helped 'im out, and Miss Gill
took 'is arm. She was very kind about it, and
said that some of the bravest men couldn't bear swinging.
Ginger said he could bear it well enough, but he
had eaten something at tea which disagreed with 'im.

They walked along quiet for a bit, and then Miss
Gill sighed.

Ginger heard it and waited.

She sighed agin.

"Anything wrong?" he ses.

"No," she ses, quick. "Nothing."

Ginger waited till she had done it a third time.

"You're unhappy," he ses. "I can tell."

"It's only a little trouble," she ses.

"Tell me," ses Ginger.

"I couldn't."

"Why not?"

"It wouldn't be right."

Ginger stopped walking.

"Anything that troubles you concerns me," he ses.

Miss Gill looked at 'im with tears in 'er eyes.

"You're very good," she ses. "Much too good."

Ginger felt as if he had grown six inches. He
led 'er to a quiet seat, and after saying she
couldn't tell 'im five or six times she told 'im.
An aunt she lived with had trusted 'er to pay
a bill of three pounds, and on the way she
had lost the money. The bill had to be paid
next morning, and her aunt was strict, and if she
found out there was no knowing wot might happen.

"Is that all?" ses Ginger.

"All?" she ses. "Three pounds!"

"Three pounds ain't much," ses Ginger, though it was more
than he had meant to spend in a week.

"It's a fortune to me," she ses. "I've tried everywhere."

"Let me lend it to you," ses Ginger.

Miss Gill drew back.

"Oh, no!"

"Why not?"

"I couldn't take money from you."

"I should be offended if you didn't," ses Ginger.

"It's impossible."

Ginger put 'is hand in his pocket and took out
his purse. Miss Gill turned 'er head away.

"I can't," she ses.

"You must," ses Ginger.

He counted three sovereigns into 'er hand, and she looked
at 'em as if they was red-hot.

"I'll pay you back," she ses. "Every penny."

"There's no hurry," ses Ginger.

"I don't know how to thank you."

Ginger looked up and down the path. There was nobody
close except an old gentleman asleep behind a newspaper.

"You could if you tried," he ses.

Miss Gill lowered 'er eyes.

"Could I?" she ses.

Ginger put his arm round 'er waist and kissed 'er.

The next moment his cap was in the dust and
'is left ear felt as if somebody had shut a
door on it. Miss Gill stood up, trembling all over.

"How dare you!" she ses.

Ginger opened 'is mouth, but nothing came out.

"You wicked, deceitful man!" she ses. "I trusted you."

"But—but—" ses Ginger.

"Keeper!" ses Miss Gill.

The old gentleman woke up behind 'is newspaper and called
"Keeper!" too, without knowing why. Two other people joined in,
and afore Ginger could explain, a man in uniform had
'im by the arm.

"He insulted me," ses Miss Gill, bursting into tears.

"I didn't," ses Ginger. "I only—"

"Out you go," ses the keeper.

Ginger went. He tried to walk dignified, but the keeper's
hand between 'is shoulders interfered with it. At the gate
he looked back and saw Miss Gill dabbing 'er eyes
with one hand and holding tight to the three pounds
with the other.

He got home at ten o'clock. Sam and Peter was
waiting up for 'im.

"Well?" ses Sam.

Ginger hung up 'is cap.

"Pleasant day?" ses Peter.

"Fair," ses Ginger.

"How did you get on?" ses Sam.

"Same as anybody else," ses Ginger.

"Did she flirt with you?" ses Peter.

Ginger turned round very fierce.

"Flirt? Miss Gill? Certainly not. She's one of the most
modest gals I've ever met."

Peter stared at 'im.

"I thought you was going to take 'er away from
Joe," he ses.

"I've changed my mind," ses Ginger. "She's devoted to 'im."

"Did she tell you so?" ses Sam.

"She didn't need to," ses Ginger.

Peter looked at 'is ear.

"You've got a mark there," he ses.

"Mosquito," ses Ginger.

"In London?" ses Peter.

"It must have escaped from the insect-house," ses Ginger. "And
if you two have finished asking silly questions, I'm going
to bed."

He went upstairs, and Peter watched 'im.

"There's something wrong," he ses.

Sam nodded.

"He's lost 'is three pounds," he ses.

"How d'you know?"

"He counted 'is money afore he went out," ses Sam,
"and he's been feeling 'is empty pocket ever since he
come in."

Next day Peter tried to get the truth out of
'im, but Ginger wouldn't say nothing except that Miss Gill
was a credit to her sex. The more he praised
'er, the more suspicious Peter became.

"You made a mess of it," he ses at last.

"I didn't try," ses Ginger.

"You did."

"I didn't."

"Then you won't mind me trying," ses Peter.

Ginger looked at 'im.

"You?"

"Yes, me," ses Peter. "I bet you a sovereign I
take 'er out and she likes it."

"You couldn't get 'er to come," ses Ginger.

"I bet I can."

Ginger thought it over. He wanted to warn Peter, but
he couldn't do that without telling the truth, and the
thought of Peter coming home with a red ear comforted
'im.

"Done," he ses.

Peter arranged it through Joe, same as Ginger had done.
He said he wanted Miss Gill's advice about a birthday
present for Sam's sister, and Joe, who seemed anxious to
help everybody, fixed it up for Saturday.

Peter chose the Crystal Palace. He wore 'is velvet-collared coat
and carried a walking-stick which he had bought that morning.
Ginger advised 'im not to swing it where there was
elephants about, but Peter merely smiled.

Miss Gill met 'im looking prettier than ever. She was
a little shy at first, but Peter told 'er that
he wasn't like Ginger.

"In wot way?" she ses.

"All ways," ses Peter.

"He's very kind," she ses.

"Kind enough," ses Peter. "But not clever."

"He seems clever."

"That's because he talks fast," ses Peter. "A clever man
don't 'ave to hurry."

Miss Gill said she had never thought of that, and
looked at Peter so admiring that he began to walk
slower.

They went through the Palace and saw nearly everything. Peter
explained the statues and pictures to 'er, making it up
as he went along, and Miss Gill listened without smiling.
At a model of an ancient temple he told 'er
it had been discovered by Captain Cook in Egypt, and
she said it was wonderful how much sailors knew.

At the rifle gallery Peter said shooting was 'is special
gift. He fired six shots and missed the target five
times. The sixth hit a little tin bird belonging to
the next gallery, and the proprietor made 'im pay fourpence
damages.

"The sights are wrong," ses Peter.

"Of course," ses Miss Gill.

He tried agin and knocked a clay pipe out of
the attendant's mouth. After that they went somewhere quieter.

At dinner Peter ordered more than he could eat and
pressed delicacies on Miss Gill till she said she couldn't
swallow another mouthful. Then she ate two ices and a
bag of chocolates while they listened to the band.

"You've made me very happy," she ses.

Peter twirled 'is moustache.

"I generally do," he ses.

"I mean," she ses, "I was miserable when I came."

"Miserable?" ses Peter.

"I oughtn't to say anything."

Peter put on a sympathetic look.

"You can trust me," he ses. "I'm different from most
men."

"I know that," she ses.

Peter moved closer.

"It's only a little money trouble," she ses. "But I'd
rather not speak of it."

Peter's face changed for half a second, and then he
smiled.

"How much?" he ses.

"Three pounds," she ses, in a whisper.

"Three pounds?" ses Peter.

Miss Gill nodded, and a tear rolled down 'er cheek.

Peter stared at the band. He had heard something very
like it afore, though Ginger had been careful not to
tell 'im. He began to wonder whether Ginger had lent
'er three pounds too, and then he remembered the bet
and the way Ginger would laugh if he came home
without getting farther than this.

"I've got three pounds," he ses.

Miss Gill shook 'er head.

"I couldn't borrow from you."

"Why not?"

"It might spoil our friendship."

"Nothing could do that," ses Peter.

"It wouldn't be right."

Peter took out three sovereigns. He held 'em for a
moment, as though hoping one of 'em might turn out
to be a button, and then placed 'em in 'er
hand.

"There," he ses.

Miss Gill looked at 'im with wet eyes.

"You've saved me," she ses.

Peter looked round. There was a lot of people about,
so he suggested a stroll outside. Miss Gill went with
'im, and presently they came to a quiet path with
bushes on both sides.

"I don't know how to thank you," she ses.

Peter thought that sounded familiar too, though he couldn't remember
where he had heard it.

"There are ways," he ses.

Miss Gill looked down.

Peter put his walking-stick under one arm, took 'er hand,
and bent towards 'er.

He came home at eleven with no walking-stick and a
scratch down one cheek. Ginger and Sam was playing cards.

"Well?" ses Ginger.

Peter took off 'is overcoat.

"Splendid," he ses.

"Enjoy yourself?" ses Sam.

"Never had a better day," ses Peter.

Ginger looked at the scratch.

"Mosquito?" he ses.

"No," ses Peter. "Tiger."

"At the Crystal Palace?"

"Model tiger," ses Peter. "Very lifelike."

"Did she flirt with you?" ses Ginger.

Peter laughed scornful.

"A lady like that?" he ses. "You ought to be
ashamed."

"Did you kiss 'er?" ses Ginger.

Peter's face got red.

"A gentleman doesn't talk about such things," he ses.

"You didn't," ses Ginger.

Peter pulled a sovereign out of 'is pocket and tossed
it on the table.

"There's the bet," he ses.

Ginger picked it up.

"So you failed," he ses.

"I only said I'd take 'er out and she'd like
it," ses Peter. "She said she'd never been so happy."

"That don't mean nothing," ses Ginger.

"It means more than anything she said to you," ses
Peter.

They began arguing, and in two minutes each was calling
the other a liar. Sam separated 'em and asked Peter
how much money he had left.

"That's my business," ses Peter.

"You lent 'er three pounds," ses Ginger, sudden.

Peter stared.

"'Ow did you know?" he ses.

Ginger jumped up.

"She got three off you too?"

Peter looked at Ginger, and Ginger looked at Peter. Then
they both looked away.

"She had a little difficulty," ses Peter.

"Lost some money?" ses Ginger.

"Yes."

"Belonging to an aunt?"

Peter nodded.

"Bill due next morning?" ses Ginger.

Peter nodded agin.

There was a long silence.

"Did she slap you?" ses Ginger.

Peter drew himself up.

"Certainly not," he ses.

Ginger waited.

"Well—not exactly," ses Peter. "She caught me with 'er hand
accidental."

"Three times?" ses Ginger.

"Twice," ses Peter. "The third was with my own walking-stick."

Sam dropped into a chair and laughed till the tears
ran down 'is face. Ginger told 'im to shut up,
and Peter said he'd knock 'is head off if he
didn't. Sam tried to stop, but every time he looked
at their faces he started agin.

"You pair of experienced men," he ses. "You judges of
women!"

"She's a swindler," ses Ginger.

"A dangerous criminal," ses Peter. "We ought to tell Joe."

"And tell 'im wot?" ses Sam. "That each of you
tried to take 'is gal away, lent 'er three pounds,
and got slapped for your trouble?"

"It was for 'is good," ses Ginger.

"Course," ses Sam. "And the kissing?"

"That was evidence," ses Peter.

They was still discussing it next evening when Joe came
in. He looked happier than ever, and he had a
small parcel under 'is arm.

"I've come to show you something," he ses.

Ginger and Peter stopped talking.

"Miss Gill bought me a present," ses Joe.

He opened the parcel and took out a silver watch
with a handsome chain. Then he held up 'is other
hand, and there was a gold ring on 'is little
finger.

"She gave me the ring too," he ses. "Ain't it
a beauty?"

Sam took the watch and examined it.

"Must have cost a bit," he ses.

"Six pounds the lot," ses Joe, proud-like. "Three for the
watch and chain, and three for the ring. She's been
saving up for months. That's wot I call affection."

Nobody spoke.

Joe looked at Ginger.

"She says she enjoyed the Zoological Gardens," he ses. "She
told me you was very generous."

Ginger made a noise in 'is throat.

"And she liked the Crystal Palace too," Joe went on.
"She says Peter is a real gentleman."

Peter buttoned his coat up to the chin.

"She's a wonderful gal," ses Joe. "She never spends money
on herself. You don't meet many like 'er."

"No," ses Sam. "You don't."

Joe held out the watch.

"Listen to it," he ses. "Goes beautiful."

They listened. It ticked away cheerful as anything.

"Six pounds," ses Ginger, very slow.

"Six pounds," ses Joe. "And she made me promise never
to ask where she got it."

Ginger turned and looked at Peter.

Peter looked at Ginger.

Then Ginger ses, "She slapped me."

Peter rubbed the scratch on 'is cheek.

"She slapped me too," he ses.

And Joe, thinking they was talking about somebody else, held
the watch to 'is ear and said it was a
funny thing how unlucky some men was with women.

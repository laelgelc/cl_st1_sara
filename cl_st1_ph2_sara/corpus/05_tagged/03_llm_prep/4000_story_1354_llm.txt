# The Social Advancement of Bones

It has been stated, chiefly by persons from Sandy Bar,
that Bones belonged to Rattlers Ridge. This is an error
which I have always felt bound to correct, although I
admit that in later years—after his connection with Judge Preston
had given him a certain standing—we encouraged the belief. But
at the period when Bones first appeared among us no
citizen of the Ridge would have admitted ownership in him
under oath, nor, I think, without it.

He certainly WAS yellow.

I do not mean that agreeable golden hue which is
observed in expensive spaniels, nor the pale cream affected by
dogs reclining on drawing-room rugs, but a harsh, uncompromising, mining-camp
yellow, suggestive of clay, mustard, old boots, and the inside
of a badly washed sluice-box. His tail had been shortened,
either by accident, economy, or an imperfect conception of art;
his ears disagreed on all public questions; his legs were
too long for a terrier and too short for anything
else; and his general form seemed to have been adopted
after consultation with several breeds, none of which had been
permitted a deciding vote.

Still, he had points. His body was thin, supple, and
astonishingly quick; his nose was black; his eyes were brown
and bright, although he commonly viewed mankind sideways, as if
anticipating a stone. This attitude was not without justification. Rattlers
Ridge possessed a direct method of correcting dogs, children, Chinamen,
and disputed statements; and while we afterward called Bones's caution
"sagacity," it began, I fear, in experience.

No one fed him regularly, though every one accused his
neighbor of doing so. No one allowed him to sleep
in his cabin, though he was expelled every morning from
some cabin with language indicating previous occupation. When asked whose
dog he was, the citizen interrogated generally pointed to another
citizen and said:

"His."

The other citizen replied:

"Mine? I'd sooner own a spotted mule with religious opinions."

Thus Bones enjoyed the largest number of masters and the
fewest privileges of any animal in the county.

His intelligence, however, was admitted. Indeed, it was the only
thing about him that we admitted without qualifying his blood,
color, habits, or moral responsibility. Tennessee Joe declared that Bones
could read the election notices pasted on the wall of
the Magnolia Saloon, because he invariably barked at the name
of the opposing candidate. It was afterward shown that the
opposing candidate's poster covered a rat-hole; but as Joe observed
that no politician could reasonably ask for a better explanation
of public hostility, the evidence was accepted.

French Pete asserted that Bones understood poker. According to Pete,
the animal had once stood behind Colonel Starbottle during a
game, and by moving his right ear, left ear, or
abbreviated tail had indicated respectively a pair, three of a
kind, and a full hand. This might have established his
reputation permanently had not the Colonel lost seventy dollars during
the experiment. Pete then explained that Bones had been signaling
HIS cards to the other players, which proved the dog's
comprehension, if not his loyalty.

His qualifications as a hunting dog were less certain. He
once pursued a black bear for two miles through the
chaparral, with a persistence that excited our highest admiration, until
it appeared that he was pursuing it toward the camp.
The bear arrived first, entered the Magnolia, overturned a faro
table, removed the greater portion of Kentucky Jake's trousers, and
retired by way of the roof. Bones came in three
minutes later and received the public congratulations under the stove.

"That dog," said Tennessee Joe, binding Jake with a bar
towel, "knowed we wanted sport."

"Then why didn't he stay WITH the bear?" asked Jake.

This question, being personal, was suppressed.

On another occasion Bones retrieved a mallard from the river
and laid it proudly at Boston's feet. As Boston had
not fired his gun, and as the bird proved to
be alive, tame, and the property of a widow two
miles downstream, there was some criticism. But we held that
the essential idea of retrieving had been correctly grasped, and
that the merely technical circumstance of the duck not having
been shot showed initiative rather than ignorance.

He was also called a water spaniel after crossing the
mill ditch during a flood, although it should be added
that he crossed it in a packing case. When the
case was recovered, Bones had eaten part of the label
and was sleeping on the remainder. The term "spaniel" was
thereafter retained by his friends, while his enemies substituted "freight."

Yet these distinctions did not improve his social condition. He
stole bacon, chased mules, upset pans of "slumgullion," slept on
clean shirts, and attended fights without taking either side until
the loser was down. He had a habit of following
strangers out of camp, which we considered hospitality, and returning
with portions of their provisions, which they did not. He
lied, too—not in the coarse human fashion, but with his
entire person. He would approach a cabin limping painfully on
three legs, accept food with a look of Christian resignation,
and depart upon four at a speed that made pursuit
ridiculous. We regarded this as hypocrisy until it was discovered
that he used a different disabled leg at each cabin.
Then it became versatility.

His worst weakness was his affection for drunkards.

A sober man might call him, whistle to him, offer
meat, or discuss his pedigree with tenderness; Bones would listen
with politeness and withdraw. But let a citizen come uncertainly
down the trail, holding conversation with the moon, placing one
foot before him as a matter of experiment, and occasionally
embracing a pine tree, and Bones would join him at
once. He walked beside such a man with sympathy; he
waited during his rests; he accompanied his songs in a
low howl; and if the sufferer fell, Bones lay down
beside him as one who had at last reached the
object of the excursion.

This attachment culminated in his friendship with Uncle Billy Rankin.

Uncle Billy was not, strictly speaking, the uncle of anybody
at Rattlers Ridge, although he frequently claimed relationship with men
who had money. He had been a miner, a teamster,
a justice of the peace, and, by his own account,
a lieutenant-governor; but at the time of which I speak
his active profession consisted in getting drunk with a regularity
that inspired confidence. He began each morning with the declaration
that he had sworn off, and ended each evening by
attempting to climb the flagpole in front of the Magnolia.

Bones adopted him.

At first the dog merely followed Uncle Billy. Then he
began to imitate him. When Billy zigzagged, Bones zigzagged; when
Billy sat suddenly in the road and reproached society, Bones
sat beside him and reproached it in a higher key.
If Billy slept in the ditch, Bones occupied the ditch.
Presently, and this fact can be supported by seven men
whose recollections differed only regarding the day, Bones acquired an
intoxicated gait even when Uncle Billy was absent. He lurched
against posts, crossed his legs, stared solemnly at the earth,
and once attempted to drink from an empty whisky bottle.

The camp was scandalized. Not by Uncle Billy's condition, which
had become a recognized feature of the landscape, but by
the dog's imitation of it.

"It's degradin'," said Boston.

"To Billy?" I asked.

"To the Ridge," returned Boston severely. "Strangers might think we
learned him."

A committee waited upon Uncle Billy and represented that his
influence upon canine morals was injurious. Billy wept, embraced Bones,
called him his only true friend, and offered to fight
the committee separately or collectively. The committee therefore waited until
he slept, carried him ten miles down the stage road,
and paid a teamster to take him to Red Dog.
Bones was shut in the assay office during this operation.

For three days afterward the animal refused food, declined society,
and sat upon the road looking south. On the fourth
he disappeared. A week later he returned alone, thin, muddy,
and with a strip of blue cloth between his teeth
which was identified as part of Uncle Billy's coat. Whether
Bones had found his companion, deserted him, buried him, or
merely assisted at another of his difficulties was never proved.
But from that hour the dog abandoned drunken imitation. We
spoke of this as his reformation, though I suspect it
was despair.

About a month later Pinkey Preston came to Rattlers Ridge.

She was the daughter of Judge Preston, of Hawthorne, and
visited us in company with Mrs. Dodd, whose husband owned
the Excelsior claim and a white shirt which he wore
on Sundays. The whole settlement turned out for the stage.
Men who had not shaved since the spring freshet shaved
one cheek and, being discouraged by the result, left the
other in its natural state. Red Mike appeared in "store
clothes" so new and severe that he moved only from
the knees. The Magnolia displayed a green bough over the
door, three flags of uncertain nationality, and a sign reading
WELCOME TO RATLERS RIDGE, the missing T being explained as
a local abbreviation.

The stage came up the grade at noon, green-bodied, red-wheeled,
and bright with brass, trailing a yellow cloud of dust.
On the box sat Yuba Bill, vast and impassive; within
were Mrs. Dodd, two commercial travelers, a nervous clergyman, and
Pinkey.

She was perhaps seventeen, though the camp immediately agreed that
she was "about twenty," because we felt more competent to
converse with that age. She had blue eyes, fair hair
escaping beneath a gray traveling hat, and a pink muslin
dress which seemed, among our red shirts and clay-colored boots,
to belong to a superior climate. When she stepped from
the coach, every hat came off—except Kentucky Jake's, which had
been knocked over his eyes in the effort.

Bones, who had been sleeping under the stage platform, was
also disturbed. He emerged in bad humor, shook the dust
from his yellow sides directly upon Red Mike's store clothes,
and looked for the offender. Seeing Pinkey, he stopped.

She saw him too.

"Oh, what a dear dog!" she said.

No statement ever astonished Rattlers Ridge more. We looked at
Bones; we looked at Pinkey; then we looked at one
another, as men do when the natural laws have been
suspended in their presence.

Bones approached her cautiously, sideways, expecting, perhaps, the usual foot.
Pinkey bent down, put both hands about his disreputable head,
and looked into his eyes.

"Good dog!" she said.

She emphasized the adjective—not loudly, but with a conviction that
made it final. GOOD dog.

Bones did not move. His uncertain ears lifted; his brown
eyes widened; the habitual suspicion went out of them for
an instant. Then he laid his nose against her pink
sleeve and trembled.

We were embarrassed. Red Mike coughed. Tennessee Joe observed that
there was "pizin" oak near the platform, though there was
not an oak within six miles. For my own part,
I had seen Bones beaten, kicked, chased, praised, cursed, and
accused of intelligence; but I had never seen anybody tell
him, as if it were a fact and not a
disputed local theory, that he was good.

Pinkey went on to the Dodds' cabin. Bones went with
her.

He remained outside during dinner, rejecting a bone from the
cook and accepting a biscuit from Pinkey. He accompanied her
that afternoon to the hydraulic claim, kept between her dress
and the muddy bank, and growled at every miner who
approached too suddenly. When she returned to the cabin, he
slept across the door. Next morning he was still there,
washed by the rain into a brighter and even more
offensive yellow.

At the end of Pinkey's visit she took the stage
for Hawthorne. Bones followed it.

We called him back. Tennessee Joe whistled; Boston offered bacon;
Red Mike ran behind the coach shouting that Pinkey had
only been "jokin'." Bones never turned. He trotted after the
red wheels until both dog and stage vanished around the
bend.

"He'll be back," said Jake. "He ain't no town dog."

Bones returned three days later, footsore and hungry. He ate,
slept eighteen hours, and departed again.

This occurred four times. The distance to Hawthorne was twenty-seven
miles, over a road involving two grades, a river crossing,
and the tollgate keeper, who disliked dogs. Nevertheless Bones learned
it. He would appear at the Preston house, remain near
Pinkey until removed, and return to Rattlers Ridge only when
Judge Preston's gardener drove him away. Once the gardener shut
him in a stable; Bones left through the roof. Once
he was sent back in the stage, tied to the
rear boot; he ate the strap and arrived at Hawthorne
before the coach. Yuba Bill, whose admiration was not lightly
given, declared that the dog "had more schedule in him
than the company."

Our opinion of Bones now changed. We pointed him out
to travelers. We denied that he had ever stolen poultry
except for scientific purposes. His color became "old gold"; his
mixed breed was described as "a cross between a Californian
lion dog and one of them Scotch kinds"; and his
devotion to Pinkey was cited as proof that Rattlers Ridge
produced loyalty in its native animals. Men who had thrown
stones at him began saying, "There goes OUR dog."

Bones accepted this altered reputation with reserve. Fame did not
keep him at the Ridge. Whenever Pinkey's visit ended in
rejection, he rested, restored himself at our expense, and started
again.

The decisive event took place in church.

The church at Hawthorne was a white wooden structure possessing
a bell, a steeple, and more paint than any building
in our county not occupied by gamblers. Pinkey attended with
Judge Preston each Sunday. On a certain morning Bones, having
escaped from the gardener, followed them and entered during the
second hymn.

I was present, with Boston and Red Mike, our attendance
being connected less with doctrine than with the report that
Pinkey might sing a solo. Bones came quietly up the
aisle, his nails clicking on the bare boards. The congregation
turned; the choir faltered; but Pinkey smiled and moved her
skirts. Bones lay beneath her pew with his nose upon
her shoe.

The Reverend Mr. Dawes, after a pause, continued. He was
a tall, energetic man with black whiskers and a manner
of preaching which suggested that salvation lay at the top
of a hill and was being reached under fire. Bones
listened. At first he appeared impressed. When Mr. Dawes raised
his voice, Bones raised his head; when the reverend gentleman
struck the pulpit, Bones started; when he pointed toward the
sinners, Bones looked in that direction with concern.

Then Mr. Dawes lifted the large pulpit Bible.

Now Bones had known books only as objects occasionally thrown
at him by men interrupted in their reading. He saw
a black, rectangular missile raised above the minister's shoulder; he
observed the minister's flashing eye and forward motion; and he
reached a conclusion which, though incorrect ecclesiastically, was justified by
his education.

With one bark he sprang upon the pulpit.

Mr. Dawes retreated. Bones seized the Bible by its binding
and leaped down. The congregation rose as one body. For
a moment the church contained waving bonnets, falling hymnals, shouting
men, and one yellow dog dragging the Scriptures beneath the
front pew. Deacon Shadwell attempted rescue and was deprived of
his coat-tail. The sexton advanced with a broom; Bones, recognizing
a familiar instrument of persecution, carried the Bible into the
choir. The soprano climbed the organ stool, the alto entered
the vestry through a window, and Red Mike, forgetting his
surroundings, offered three dollars on the dog.

Then Pinkey spoke.

"Bones!"

He stopped.

"Bring it here, good dog."

Bones came out slowly, the Bible in his mouth, and
laid it at her feet. He sat down beside it,
looking from Pinkey to the minister, prepared, I think, to
protect either from the other.

There was silence. Judge Preston's face was grave, but his
eyes were not. Mr. Dawes recovered the Bible, examined its
damp binding, and said with commendable presence of mind:

"My friends, even this interruption may teach us that judgment
follows the light afforded."

Tennessee Joe afterward maintained that this was an admission in
Bones's favor.

After the service Judge Preston approached us outside. He was
a large, smooth-faced man whose habit of asking questions had
caused many citizens to confess things not previously known to
themselves.

"I understand," he said, "that this dog belongs to Rattlers
Ridge."

We glanced at one another. Here was a serious demand
upon our public character.

"Collectively, Judge," said Boston.

"His title's kind o' vested in the camp," added Red
Mike.

"Though I reckon," said Jake, "his affections is freehold."

The Judge nodded as if these legal conditions were familiar.

"My daughter is attached to him. Would the Ridge object
if he remained with us?"

Object! We felt pride, grief, and the importance of negotiation.
A conference was held under the pepper tree. It was
agreed that Bones might be transferred provided he was not
chained, misrepresented as born in Hawthorne, or prevented from visiting
his native camp. The last phrase passed unanimously, though Bones
had been born, so far as we knew, behind a
Chinese laundry at Poverty Flat.

Judge Preston accepted the conditions.

Thus began Bones's ultimate and somewhat monotonous reformation.

He slept upon a rug. He ate regularly from a
tin marked B—an initial which gave him his first private
property. He was washed, brushed, licensed, and introduced to visitors
without apology. Pinkey tied a blue ribbon around his neck;
the Judge permitted him in the study; even the gardener,
finding resistance useless, addressed him without agricultural tools.

Respectability improved his coat, his weight, and his manners. It
did not alter his color. He ceased stealing because food
was given him before the theft became necessary; he ceased
limping because no appeal was required; and he no longer
looked sideways at Pinkey, although he preserved that glance for
the minister. On Sundays he remained outside the church, where
his doctrinal difficulties could offend no one.

We visited him when business took us to Hawthorne. At
first he greeted us moderately. Later he merely wagged his
tail. Finally, upon an occasion when Boston, Red Mike, and
I called at the Preston house, Bones came down the
steps, looked at us with polished indifference, and returned to
Pinkey's side.

Boston was wounded.

"He's ashamed of the Ridge," he said.

"Naturally," said Red Mike. "He's movin' in judicial circles."

I confess that we resented it. Yet that evening, at
the Magnolia, Boston informed two strangers that "our dog Bones"
lived with Judge Preston, had a private dish, and could
enter any room in the house. He omitted the circumstance
that Bones had ignored him. In this omission we all
concurred.

Nearly a year passed.

Then a letter came from Judge Preston. It was addressed
to the citizens of Rattlers Ridge and posted on the
door of the Magnolia. Pinkey, the Judge wrote, had been
ill with a fever. During her sickness Bones had refused
to leave the passage outside her room. He could not
be coaxed away except for food, and even then returned
before eating. On the fifth night Pinkey's fever broke. In
the morning they found Bones lying against her bedroom door.

He was dead.

There was no wound, no poison, no visible disease. The
Judge said the dog's heart had failed. Tennessee Joe maintained
that this was medically inaccurate, since Bones's heart had succeeded
where better ones had not; but he made the correction
privately.

For some minutes after the letter was read nobody spoke.
Then Boston said:

"We ought to fetch him home."

But Pinkey had asked that he be buried beneath the
acacia outside her window. Against that request no man at
Rattlers Ridge was prepared to argue.

We took up a collection for a gravestone. Men who
had never given Bones food gave money; men who had
kicked him subscribed twice; Uncle Billy, hearing of it at
Red Dog, sent a silver button and a message so
stained and misspelled that only its affection was clear. Judge
Preston declined the money at first, but Pinkey persuaded him
to accept enough for a small stone.

The question of an inscription occupied three nights.

"Faithful unto death" was proposed, but rejected as too scriptural
after the church affair. "Property of Rattlers Ridge" was considered
accurate in sentiment but defective in fact. Tennessee Joe suggested
a complete list of Bones's exploits; the stonecutter estimated that
this would require a monument thirty-eight feet high, assuming the
bear story was reduced.

At last Red Mike rose.

"Put down," he said, "'GOOD Dog!' That's wot she called
him. That's wot made him one."

There was no debate.

And so beneath the acacia at Hawthorne stands a little
gray stone, plain except for two words and an exclamation
point which the camp insisted upon at additional expense:

**GOOD DOG!**

Whether Bones deserved that title before Pinkey gave it to
him is a question upon which Rattlers Ridge could once
have supplied abundant testimony in both directions. But I have
sometimes thought that goodness, like ownership, is less a matter
of original breed than of somebody's courage in making the
first claim.

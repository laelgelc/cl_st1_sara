### THE ELEVATOR'S APOLOGY

The Governor was commonly supposed to be the first person
in the State-house. This was an error natural to strangers,
school children, and members of the Legislature.

The real character of the State-house was Freckles McGrath.

His name on the pay-roll was William McGrath, but William
belonged to official documents and to his mother when she
was displeased. The State-house knew him as Freckles. He was
fifteen, small for his age, red as to hair, innumerable
as to freckles, and possessed of two grey eyes which
seemed no larger than pinheads until there was something worth
seeing. Then they became microscopes.

Freckles ran the eastern elevator. He ran it up, down,
slowly for the Governor, swiftly for reporters, and with a
jolt of delicate significance for legislators who forgot to say
"please." He knew who came early, who stayed late, who
entered by the front door for appearance and departed by
the basement to escape inquiry. He knew which senators smoked
the Governor's cigars and which carried away two for future
reflection. He knew also that when eminent gentlemen spoke of
"the people," they did not always mean anybody Freckles had
met.

Thus, though he had no vote, no office, no committee
assignment, and no right to enter the Senate chamber except
with a message, he possessed advantages denied to many statesmen.
He had ears, mobility, and no reputation to protect.

The winter session had been a reform session. It was
called so in the newspapers, and the Governor called it
so in speeches. Certain senators called it something else in
private, while several gentlemen who occupied rooms at the Grand
Hotel called it an outrage upon established business.

At the centre of the disturbance was the Kelley Bill.

Freckles did not entirely understand the Kelley Bill. He had
read half of it in the evening paper, but after
encountering the words *reciprocal demurrage* he had gone out to
help a newsboy fight. He understood, however, that the bill
proposed to make the railroads do something they did not
wish to do, and that farmers and small shippers wanted
them to do it. This was enough for Freckles. His
father had once waited six weeks for a freight car
and had used language concerning railroads which remained one of
the boy's richest inheritances.

The Governor wanted the bill. The Governor was Freckles' friend.

Their friendship was not publicly acknowledged, being founded on small
services. Freckles had once held the elevator while the Governor
finished a story. The Governor had once sent him a
turkey at Christmas. Freckles had carried notes which were not
to pass through the outer office, and the Governor had
refrained from noticing when Freckles smuggled three boys from his
neighbourhood to the top of the dome. Between great men
and elevator boys, confidence is often best maintained without minutes.

Against the Kelley Bill stood Henry Ludlow, attorney, counsellor, citizen
of consequence, and representative—though never by such a crude title—of
three great railroad systems.

Mr. Ludlow was an eminent lobbyist. His coats fitted him
with moral severity. His shoes shone even in muddy weather.
His iron-grey moustache was clipped so precisely that it appeared
to have been approved by a committee. He never hurried,
because important men are supposed to have time waiting for
them; but everybody else hurried when he lifted one finger.

Freckles disliked him.

This dislike had begun when Ludlow entered the elevator on
the first day of the session, looked over Freckles' head,
and said, "Three," as if speaking to a mechanical attachment.
It increased when he called the Kelley Bill "the hysterical
demand of the uninformed," for Freckles suspected that he himself
belonged to the uninformed. It became permanent on the morning
Ludlow pushed aside an old farmer who was trying to
ask a senator a question.

"Some men," Freckles afterward remarked to the building engineer, "is
born with a silver spoon in their mouths, an' some
with a door-knob, so's they can shut everybody out."

The decisive vote was to come on Thursday.

For three days the State-house had throbbed with prophecy. The
bill would pass by six votes; it would fail by
two; it had been killed in committee; it had never
been stronger; Senator Stacy had pledged himself for it; Senator
Stacy had pledged himself against it; Senator Stacy had pledged
himself to remain unpledged until he received further information.

Senator Stacy was a tall, pale gentleman from a doubtful
district. He suffered from principles whenever public opinion grew severe,
but he recovered under private treatment. On Thursday morning he
was believed to possess the vote which would determine the
fate of the Kelley Bill.

At eleven o'clock Freckles carried two representatives from the second
floor to the fourth. They continued a conversation begun before
entering and concluded after leaving, observing him no more than
they observed the brass rail.

"Stacy's coming over," said one.

"If Ludlow doesn't get him."

"Ludlow will get him."

"Not if the vote's called before noon. Stacy told Kelley
he'd support the bill unless he heard from the railroad
people first."

"Where is Stacy?"

"In the Senate cloak-room."

"And Ludlow?"

"Due here any minute."

The doors clanged shut.

Freckles sat on his stool.

Below him lay the basement. Above him rose committee rooms,
offices, legislative chambers, unused corridors, store-rooms, an attic, and the
narrow iron stairs leading toward the tower. Between Henry Ludlow
and Senator Stacy there remained one elevator boy.

Freckles rubbed a freckle beside his nose.

He was not a statesman, and he knew it. He
had never addressed a convention, unless one counted the meeting
of elevator boys held behind the boiler-room. He could not
have explained the constitutional questions involved in the Kelley Bill.
Yet his mind, unburdened by these deficiencies, moved directly to
the essential fact.

If Ludlow reached Stacy, the bill might fail.

If Ludlow did not reach Stacy, the bill might pass.

The elevator belonged, for practical purposes, to Freckles McGrath.

At eleven twenty-three Henry Ludlow entered the east corridor. He
came with the rapid dignity of a man pretending not
to hurry. Under one arm he carried a leather case;
in one hand he held his gloves. His sneering eyes
were fixed upon the floor indicator as though warning it
against delay.

"Senate," he said, stepping into the car.

"Yes, sir," said Freckles.

The doors closed. The car started, rose five feet, trembled,
and stopped.

Ludlow looked up.

"What's the matter?"

Freckles moved the lever backward. The car descended one foot,
jerked twice, and remained suspended between the first and second
floors.

"She's stuck," he said.

"Then unstick her."

Freckles tried the lever again. The elevator groaned like a
citizen facing taxation.

"She's been acting queer all morning," he observed.

"I have no interest in her disposition. Take me to
the Senate."

"Yes, sir. I'm trying, sir."

He pulled the lever sharply. The car shot upward.

It passed the second floor. It passed the Senate floor.

"Stop!" cried Ludlow.

"I'm trying!"

The car climbed past the Governor's floor, past the law
library, past floors used for store-rooms, past floors used for
nothing at all. The cables hummed, the shaft roared, and
Freckles clung to the controller with an expression of gallant
despair.

"Stop this car!"

"She's running away!"

"Turn the lever!"

"I've turned it!"

"Use the brake!"

"It ain't listening to the brake!"

Henry Ludlow's immaculate dignity loosened at the collar. He seized
the brass rail as the elevator rushed up—up—up, bearing the
eminent lobbyist farther from Senator Stacy, farther from reciprocal demurrage,
farther from all those centres where his advice was valued.

"What's above us?" he demanded.

"The attic."

"And above that?"

"The roof."

"And above that?"

Freckles considered.

"Nothing we can stop at."

Ludlow's face changed.

The car was not actually moving at a dangerous speed.
Freckles knew every bolt and complaint of the old machine
and could have stopped it within three inches. But noise,
as all experienced politicians understand, is frequently more influential than
fact. The boy shook the lever, kicked the floor, and
looked overhead with such terror that Ludlow looked overhead too.

"We're going through!" shouted Freckles.

The top of the shaft approached. At the last possible
moment the car slowed with a violent shudder and stopped
three feet below the attic landing.

For several seconds neither spoke.

Freckles breathed as though each gasp had to be approved
separately.

"Well?" Ludlow snapped.

"She's wedged."

"Get me out."

"I can't move her."

"Open that door."

The landing door was above their heads. Freckles climbed upon
his stool, forced the car gate aside, and pushed at
the outer door. It opened. A dark slit appeared, through
which came dust and the smell of abandoned records.

"If you climb on the rail," said Freckles, "you can
pull yourself up."

"Climb?"

"There might be another run. She could drop next time."

Ludlow glanced downward. Between the floor of the car and
the attic landing yawned the black shaft.

"If I fall—"

"I'll catch you," said Freckles.

Mr. Ludlow examined the boy's size and found no encouragement
in it.

The elevator gave a small, artful lurch.

And Henry Ludlow climbed.

He mounted the brass rail, thrust his leather case ahead,
caught the edge of the landing, and dragged himself through
the opening with a loss of dignity impossible to calculate
in money. His knees struck the floor. Dust appeared upon
his trousers. One polished shoe came off, but Freckles passed
it up with respectful haste.

"Stay there!" said the boy. "I'll go down an' get
the engineer."

"How will you go down if the car is stuck?"

At that instant the car descended eighteen inches.

Freckles gave a cry.

"She's loose!"

"Wait!"

But the opening had begun to sink below the landing.

"Bring help!" shouted Ludlow.

"I'll bring everybody!" shouted Freckles.

He pulled shut the landing door from below. Its spring
lock clicked.

Then he sent the elevator down—down—down, past floors used for
nothing, past store-rooms, past offices, until the Senate floor came
level with the gate.

A messenger stood there.

"They're calling the roll!" he cried.

Freckles leaned out.

"What about Stacy?"

"He voted aye!"

The boy's small grey eyes blazed. He forgot his car,
his wages, the eminent captive in the attic. He forgot
that reform is properly achieved by petitions, debate, caucus, compromise,
and the pressure of enlightened opinion. He remembered only that
he, William "Freckles" McGrath, aged fifteen, had personally lifted the
railroad lobby above the field of action.

"How's it stand?"

"Bill passes by one!"

Freckles shut the gate and sent the car to the
basement, not because anybody wanted to go there, but because
emotion demanded movement.

For ten glorious minutes he was the youngest champion of
reform in the State. He saw his name in histories.
He imagined a bronze tablet in the elevator: *From this
spot William McGrath struck a decisive blow for the people.*
He considered how future Governors would point him out to
visiting dignitaries.

Then he remembered Henry Ludlow.

By that time Mr. Ludlow had discovered that the attic
door was locked.

He had also discovered three broken chairs, fourteen bundles of
legislative reports, a portrait of a former Governor with a
hole through its canvas, and a family of mice unmoved
by railroad influence. He had shouted down the stairway. The
stairway door was locked too. He had pounded until dust
descended upon his hair and moustache.

At twelve twenty Freckles returned.

He brought the car quietly to the attic level and
opened the door six inches.

A face appeared.

"You!" said Ludlow.

"Yes, sir. I come back."

"You infernal young scoundrel! Get me out of here, and
I'll have you discharged before you reach the first floor.
I'll have you arrested. I'll see that you never get
work in this city again!"

Freckles regarded him sorrowfully.

"You ain't calm yet."

"Open that door!"

"It wouldn't be safe to take down a nervous passenger."

"McGrath!"

"I'll give you another hour," said Freckles. "Maybe you'll feel
better."

He shut the door and descended.

At one twenty-five he returned. Ludlow was seated upon a
box containing the journals of the Senate for 1889. Dust
softened his clothes; rage had exhausted itself into a deadly
quiet.

"Are you calm now?" asked Freckles.

"Open the door."

"You won't interfere with me running the car?"

"No."

"You won't excite her?"

"No."

"You won't shout?"

Ludlow's eyes promised a future of elaborate revenge, but his
voice was controlled.

"I shall not shout."

Freckles opened the door.

They descended in silence. At the second floor Ludlow stepped
out, paused, and turned.

"You will hear from me."

"Yes, sir," said Freckles. "An' if you're taking the elevator
again, just ring."

The complaint reached the Governor at two o'clock. Ludlow delivered
it in person.

The Governor was a large man with a calm brow
and a mouth which had been trained by public life
to reveal nothing prematurely. As Ludlow described the runaway ascent,
the perilous escape, the locked attic, and the second abandonment,
the Governor repeatedly raised one hand to his lips. This
appeared to be concern.

It was not concern.

Inside his official person the Governor was one great chuckle.

Nevertheless, executive dignity demanded an investigation. The Governor sent for
the superintendent of the building, the chief engineer, and William
McGrath.

Freckles entered looking small, clean, and anxious. He had washed
his hands and arranged his stubby red hair with water.
Water had lost the struggle.

"William," said the Governor gravely, "Mr. Ludlow makes a serious
complaint."

"I'm sorry to hear he ain't feeling well, Governor."

"I am feeling perfectly well!" said Ludlow.

The Governor's hand approached his mouth, reconsidered, and rested upon
the desk.

"Mr. Ludlow states that you carried him above the Senate
floor, induced him to leave the elevator at the attic,
locked him there during the vote on the Kelley Bill,
and afterward refused to release him."

Freckles looked at Ludlow with widened eyes.

"He says I done all that?"

"You know you did!"

"Mr. Ludlow," said the Governor, "permit the boy to answer."

Freckles drew a troubled breath.

"The elevator run away," he said.

"The engineer reports nothing wrong with it," snapped Ludlow.

"Maybe she got over it."

"There was nothing to get over!"

Freckles turned to the engineer.

"Didn't she squeak Tuesday?"

The engineer scratched his chin.

"She squeaks most Tuesdays."

"There!" said Freckles.

The Governor bent over a paper.

"Continue, William."

"Well, sir, Mr. Ludlow got in an' says 'Senate,' an'
I starts her. She stops. Then she starts again awful
sudden. I tries the lever, an' she keeps going. I
tries the brake, an' she keeps going. I speaks to
her—"

"You speak to the elevator?" asked the Governor.

"Sometimes she minds better if I do."

The Governor's shoulders moved once.

"So," continued Freckles, "when we gets near the top, I'm
afraid she'll bump the roof. Mr. Ludlow's afraid too."

"I was not afraid."

"No, sir," said Freckles gently. "Maybe it was me seeing
double."

Ludlow took one step forward.

The Governor lifted his hand. "Go on."

"I gets the attic door open. I tells him he
can climb out if he wants. I don't order him.
I ain't got authority to order eminent gentlemen. He climbs.
Then the elevator starts down."

"And you closed the attic door," said Ludlow.

"From habit," said Freckles.

"Habit!"

"Yes, sir. We got rules about leaving shaft doors open.
Somebody might fall in. I was protecting the public."

The superintendent coughed violently.

The Governor waited until quiet returned.

"Why did you not immediately summon the engineer?"

"I was going to, sir. But when I got to
the Senate floor they says the roll was being called,
an' there was senators wanting rides an' messengers wanting rides,
an' representatives wanting rides. If I'd left the car, official
business might have been obstructed."

"You had already obstructed official business!" cried Ludlow.

Freckles looked pained.

"I ain't responsible for where official business goes when it
gets out of my car."

The Governor placed both hands flat upon the desk.

"After the vote, why did you not release Mr. Ludlow?"

"I did go back."

"You opened the door and went away again!"

Freckles hesitated. It was the hesitation of an artist approaching
his most difficult passage.

"When I got there, sir, Mr. Ludlow was excited. He
made threats. He said he'd have me fired and arrested,
an' that I'd never work again. I figured a gentleman
in that condition shouldn't ride in machinery. He might grab
the lever."

"I never touched the lever!"

"You said you would," answered Freckles.

"I said I would wring your neck!"

"That's right," Freckles admitted. "Maybe I misunderstood."

The superintendent looked out of the window. The engineer studied
the ceiling. The Governor raised his hand to his mouth
and kept it there.

Ludlow's face darkened to a colour rarely achieved outside of
sunsets.

"This is a conspiracy," he said. "The boy knew the
Kelley Bill was being voted upon. He knew I intended
to see Senator Stacy. He deliberately imprisoned me so I
could not do so."

The room sobered a little.

The Governor turned to Freckles.

"Did you know Mr. Ludlow wished to speak with Senator
Stacy?"

"I heard something about it."

"Did you desire the Kelley Bill to pass?"

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

Freckles considered reciprocal demurrage, railroad rates, constitutional police powers, and
the proper relationship between corporations and the State. He abandoned
them.

"My father waited six weeks for a freight car," he
said.

The Governor looked at him for a moment. Then he
looked at Ludlow.

"That establishes motive," said Ludlow triumphantly.

"It establishes a grievance," replied the Governor. "Motive requires proof
of an act."

"What more proof do you need?"

The Governor assumed the expression with which chief executives approach
matters too delightful for hasty disposition.

"The machinery must be carefully investigated. Statements must be taken.
Rules governing shaft doors, nervous passengers, attic exits, and runaway
elevators must be examined. Until that inquiry is complete, final
disciplinary action would be improper."

"I demand his dismissal now."

"I cannot dismiss a State employee merely because an elevator
misbehaves."

"The elevator did not misbehave!"

Freckles' face showed hurt surprise.

"Don't blame her too hard, Mr. Ludlow. She's old."

The Governor's hand flew to his mouth.

Ludlow seized his hat.

"If this is your administration's idea of justice—"

"My administration's idea of justice," said the Governor, with recovered
solemnity, "includes full inquiry, due procedure, and an opportunity for
even the humblest employee to be heard."

Ludlow stared at him. He stared at the superintendent. He
stared last at Freckles, who lowered his eyes with a
modesty almost saintly.

"This is not ended," said Ludlow.

"No, sir," replied the Governor. "The engineer's report will be
filed Monday."

Ludlow walked to the door. There he turned.

"At least," he said, "you will require the boy to
apologize."

The Governor reflected.

"That seems reasonable. William, Mr. Ludlow has suffered grave inconvenience
and considerable distress. Whatever the cause, the elevator was involved.
You will apologize."

Freckles looked at the Governor. For one instant the grey
pinhead eyes met the calm executive eyes, and a complete
treaty passed between them without writing.

Then Freckles faced the eminent lobbyist.

"Mr. Ludlow," he said, "on behalf of the elevator, I
apologize."

The superintendent coughed again. The engineer made a sound like
steam escaping from a pipe. The Governor bent over his
papers, his hand covering the lower half of his face.

Ludlow departed by the stairs.

The investigation lasted a week. It discovered that the elevator
was old, that it sometimes squeaked on Tuesdays, that shaft
doors ought invariably to be closed, and that no rule
specifically required operators to carry passengers while being threatened with
strangulation. The matter was postponed for further consideration and was
never afterward able to catch up with the Governor.

The Kelley Bill became law.

On the following Monday Freckles appeared in the State-house wearing
a gold signet ring. It was much too fine for
an elevator boy and slightly too large for his finger.
He displayed it with studied carelessness, especially when senators were
present.

"Where'd you get that?" asked the engineer.

"Friend of mine sent it."

"What friend?"

"Friend up home."

"What's the mark on it?"

Freckles polished the ring upon his sleeve. Engraved in the
stone was a small device resembling a sheaf of wheat
crossed by a railway track.

"Family arms," he said.

"Your family never had arms."

"Sure they did. Two apiece."

He rang the bell, closed the gate, and sent the
elevator upward.

It rose past the basement offices, past the halls of
representatives, past the Senate chamber where the Kelley Bill had
passed, and stopped at the Governor's floor. The Governor entered
alone.

His eyes fell upon the ring.

"That is handsome, William."

"Yes, sir. Come from a friend."

"Up home, I suppose?"

"Yes, sir."

The Governor looked straight ahead. Freckles looked straight ahead. The
elevator ascended with perfect innocence.

At the next floor the Governor stepped out. Before the
doors closed, he turned.

"How is the machinery behaving?"

Freckles rested one hand upon the lever and the other,
with its shining ring, upon his knee.

"Fine, Governor," he said. "But she's got strong political opinions."

The doors clanged, and the real character of the State-house
went down to the first floor for another senator.

The fire had fallen low in the grate of the
long room overlooking the square, and the rain, driven eastward
over London, brushed the high windows with the faint sound
of a hand upon silk.

Sir Henry Marquis sat beneath the green-shaded lamp at the
library table. Before him lay a little book bound in
calf, the leather bleached at the corners and the brass
clasp stained with verdigris. Its leaves were of that old
foolscap which seems to retain, long after ink and hand
are dust, the odor of cedar chests and vanished houses.

He had found the volume that morning in a locked
case of provincial records brought from Virginia before the American
war. The catalogue described it only as:

MEMORANDA OF ELIZABETH PENDLETON,
BEING CERTAIN RECOLLECTIONS OF HER FATHER,
A JUSTICE OF THE PEACE.

Sir Henry turned the fragile leaf with a reverence that
I had seen him accord to no illuminated missal.

"You will observe," he said, "that a lie may employ
a sacrament, a statute or a ruined church; but if
it employs a material thing, it must leave its thumb
upon it."

Then he began to read.

---

My father, Godfrey Pendleton, was in his fiftieth year when
the affair of the house at Saint Cuthbert's occurred. He
was then a justice of the peace in the western
counties of Virginia, and held also, by the habit of
the people rather than by any express commission, a sort
of moral magistracy over that remote and scattered commonwealth.

He was a very big man, as I remember him:
not merely tall, but built with the gravity and permanence
of a pier supporting a bridge. His shoulders were broad,
his hands immense, and his chin so strong and square
that men who came to dispute with him commonly altered
their intention after looking upon it. He wore his hair
tied behind with black ribbon, and his coat, although plain,
was always of good cloth and severely brushed. He did
not employ loudness. His authority seemed to reside in the
pauses between his words.

My father had been bred to the law at Williamsburg,
but he regarded the law not as a contrivance for
the advantage of clever persons, nor as a game in
which the more adroit advocate might prevail. He considered it
an imperfect transcript, made by fallible clerks, of an older
and more terrible ordinance.

"Elizabeth," he once said to me, when I complained that
a statute was unjust, "the statute is not the law.
A king's printer can make a statute. The law existed
before the king."

It was in April of the year 1769 that Abel
Zindorf sent for him.

The messenger came shortly after sunrise, riding a raw-boned gray
horse covered with foam. He was one of Zindorf's hired
men, a silent fellow with a scar across his mouth.
He delivered no letter, but said his master required a
deed of conveyance to be drawn immediately, and that the
consideration would be ten thousand dollars in Spanish eagles.

My father was then in the orchard directing two servants
who were setting young apple trees. He heard the man
without interruption.

"What property is to be conveyed?" he asked.

The messenger looked toward the house before he answered.

"A woman, sir."

My father thrust the spade upright into the brown earth.

"What woman?"

"I do not know her name."

"To whom is she to be conveyed?"

"To Mr. Lucian Morrow."

At that name my father's face changed very little; but
the servants ceased their work.

Lucian Morrow possessed one of the greatest estates on the
lower river. His grandfather had sat in the Governor's Council,
and his father had entertained two royal governors beneath the
painted ceiling at Morrovale. Lucian himself was a young man
of exceptional beauty, exquisite manners and no discernible occupation beyond
the corruption of whatever came within reach of his leisure.

My father went into the house, put on his coat,
took his riding whip and ordered his black horse.

I was nineteen years of age and perhaps too curious.
I followed him to the mounting block.

"Is it a criminal business?" I asked.

"All business is innocent until one discovers the men in
it," he replied.

"Then why do you take no clerk?"

"Because Zindorf has asked for paper, and I think he
requires a witness."

He mounted and rode away under the flowering branches.

The morning was luminous. Rain had fallen in the night,
and the fields shone beneath the sun as though every
furrow had received a benediction. Beyond the orchard the road
climbed in long red curves toward the Blue Ridge, passing
between plantations, patches of forest, and little farms where the
smoke rose blue and perpendicular from stone chimneys. The country
had that washed and innocent aspect which sometimes precedes the
discovery of a crime.

The house of Abel Zindorf stood twelve miles away, where
the old north road crossed a branch of the Shenandoah.

It had once been a church.

Before the present settlements were established, certain German monks—or men
whom the people called monks—had built there a small stone
chapel dedicated to Saint Cuthbert. A fire destroyed the wooden
roof, and the congregation vanished or moved westward. Zindorf, many
years afterward, purchased the ruin, divided the nave with floors,
closed the chancel arch with oak boards, and converted the
place into a dwelling.

Yet the building refused wholly to submit. Two narrow gothic
windows remained in the eastern wall; a mutilated stone cross
stood above the entrance; and behind the house, half hidden
by cedar and sumac, the bell frame rose between two
blackened uprights. The bell itself had been recast after the
fire, and hung there mute except for burials, alarms and
the gathering of the neighborhood in time of danger.

Zindorf had once been a cleric.

He came into Virginia in a coarse robe, with a
rope about his body and a silver crucifix against his
breast. He spoke Latin, French and Spanish, knew some medicine,
and could preach with a power that caused rough men
to kneel in the road. But presently he ceased to
preach. Then he ceased to pray. Finally he entered commerce.

At first he traded in skins and horses. Thereafter he
dealt in indentures. In the end, he became a buyer
and seller of enslaved persons, conducting the traffic with such
appetite that the warehouses at Alexandria knew his mark.

Still, his former appearance clung to him like the shadow
of a discarded office. He wore coarse black garments, not
from humility but economy. His head was bald at the
crown, as if it retained the tonsure. He walked with
little noiseless steps, his hands folded before him, and his
trousers were patched at the knees because, as he said,
cloth cost money and patches cost nothing.

Thus avarice counterfeited sanctity.

When my father reached Saint Cuthbert's, he found two horses
before the door: Zindorf's heavy sorrel and a glossy bay
bearing the silver-mounted saddle of Lucian Morrow.

The old church-house was close and foul within. Tobacco smoke
drifted under the dark rafters. A decanter stood among papers
on the long table, and the air held the mingled
odors of rum, sealing wax, damp stone and the perfume
then fashionable at the court of France.

Morrow stood before one of the gothic windows, striking the
ash from a slender cigar.

He wore a dove-colored coat embroidered at the cuff, a
waistcoat of figured silk, and shoes with red heels. His
dark hair was dressed and powdered, his throat enclosed in
lace. One jeweled hand rested on the hilt of a
little court sword. He seemed not a man placed in
an abandoned chapel among the Virginia mountains, but an elegant
figure escaped from a painted screen at Versailles.

Zindorf sat at the table.

"Pendleton," he said, "you are punctual."

"My messenger suggested haste."

"Haste," replied Morrow, smiling, "is the one vulgarity that necessity
excuses."

My father removed his gloves.

"You require a conveyance?"

Zindorf pushed forward a sheet of foolscap.

"A deed of sale without flaw, defect, ambiguity, reversion, reservation
or possibility of challenge in a Court of Equity."

"For what property?"

"For a female slave."

"Her name?"

"Isabel."

"Her age?"

"Eighteen years."

"Her place of birth?"

Zindorf's fingers moved uneasily among the papers.

"Near Havana."

"Her present owner?"

"I am."

"By what title?"

Zindorf sat back.

"My title is complete."

"Then it can endure recital."

Lucian Morrow laughed softly.

"Take care, Zindorf. Pendleton will presently inquire whether Adam filed
a patent to Eden."

My father did not look at him.

"By what title?" he repeated.

Zindorf opened a narrow iron box and took from it
several documents tied with a faded cord.

"You knew my former partner, Diego Ordez?"

"I knew him."

"Before he disappeared, he executed an assignment to me of
all his movable property, obligations, credits and enslaved persons. The
assignment includes this girl."

My father examined the paper.

The signature was Spanish; the witnesses were men employed by
Zindorf; the date was three years old. A broad clause
undertook to assign every slave owned by Ordez, whether named
in the schedule or omitted by error.

"Where is Ordez?" my father asked.

"No man knows."

"Dead?"

"Perhaps."

"Living?"

"Perhaps."

"Then why does he not confirm the transaction?"

Zindorf showed his yellow teeth.

"A conveyance does not become invalid merely because the grantor
neglects to remain accessible."

My father turned the leaf.

"Isabel does not appear in the schedule."

"She falls under the general clause."

"If she belonged to Ordez."

"She did."

"Prove it."

Zindorf poured rum into a glass but did not drink
it.

"Ordez lived for many years with a female slave named
Suzanne, an octoroon. The girl is Suzanne's daughter. By the
law of this colony, the status of a child follows
the condition of the mother. Suzanne was enslaved; hence Isabel
was enslaved. Ordez owned Suzanne; hence he owned Isabel. He
assigned both to me; hence Isabel is mine."

The argument was arranged like a row of locked doors.
Every conclusion seemed to follow the one before it. My
father looked again at the paper.

"And Suzanne?"

"Dead."

"Where?"

"At sea."

"Who certifies the birth?"

"Ordez always acknowledged the girl as his child."

"That is not the question."

Zindorf spread his palms.

"My servants saw her in Suzanne's care. The whole household
knew the relation."

"Relation!" said Morrow. "My dear Pendleton, must we turn a
pleasant contract into the Council of Trent?"

He crossed to the table and laid his white fingers
on the iron box.

"The fact is simplicity itself. Zindorf possesses a beautiful young
slave. I desire her. He requires ten thousand dollars. I
possess ten thousand dollars. We have called upon the most
incorruptible justice in Virginia to provide the tedious blessing of
the law."

"A blessing?" said my father.

"A sacrament, if you prefer."

My father looked at him.

"Do not employ that word in this room."

It was spoken quietly; nevertheless, Morrow took his hand from
the box.

Zindorf drew another paper from beneath the assignment.

"There is no occasion for a sermon. Draw the deed.
State the descent through Suzanne, incorporate the assignment by reference,
and bind me to defend the title against every person
whatsoever."

"No."

The word fell with the weight of an iron bar.

Zindorf's little eyes contracted.

"You deny the law?"

"I deny your haste."

"What more do you require?"

"I will see the person whom you propose to sell."

Morrow turned away with an impatient gesture, but Zindorf rose.
Although he was not a short man, he seemed diminished
when my father stood before him. His shoulders had the
habitual stoop of one who bends not in worship but
over accounts.

"The girl has been told nothing of the legal discussion,"
he said.

"What has she been told?"

"That Mr. Morrow will take her to his estate."

"In what character?"

Zindorf hesitated.

Morrow answered.

"She believes I shall marry her."

For a moment there was no sound except a faint
stirring of the new leaves against the eastern glass.

My father looked from one man to the other.

"Who told her this?"

"It was necessary to avoid distress," said Zindorf.

"Who told her?"

"I did."

"And you, Morrow?"

The young man lifted his shoulders.

"I have not contradicted an agreeable expectation."

My father walked to the window and thrust it open.
The spring air came through the pointed arch, fresh with
wet earth, growing grass and apple blossom. It moved the
papers on the table and drove before it the stale
exhalations of liquor, smoke and perfume.

Then, from the distance, a bell sounded.

One stroke only.

It came thinly across the fields, and whether it was
the bell of some plantation, a smith's hammer, or the
old bell behind the house disturbed by the wind, my
father could not then determine.

Zindorf went quickly to the window.

"What is it?" Morrow asked.

"Nothing."

My father turned.

"Bring the girl."

Zindorf did not move.

"Bring her," my father said again.

Zindorf crossed the room and pulled a woven cord hanging
near the door. A bell tinkled somewhere above. Presently steps
descended the narrow stair built against the old chancel wall.

The girl entered.

My father had seen beautiful women at Williamsburg and Annapolis,
and once, when he was young, at the court in
London. But, as he afterward told my mother, the young
woman who came into that desecrated church possessed a beauty
that made all ornament seem an intrusion.

She was tall and very slender. Her hair, black with
a blue luster in it, was drawn back from a
clear forehead and fastened by a comb of tortoise shell.
Her skin had the warm whiteness of old ivory. She
carried a little cluster of apple blossoms, and she wore
a dress of white muslin with a veil folded over
one arm.

She looked like a bride.

There was no fear in her face. There was expectation,
innocence and a grave happiness. She first looked toward Morrow,
who bowed with elaborate grace, and then toward my father.

Zindorf spoke in Spanish.

"This is Justice Pendleton, who has come to arrange the
legal matters."

She answered in English, though with a delicate foreign accent.

"My father has written to him?"

"No," said Zindorf. "Your father is still absent."

The girl's expression clouded.

"But he knows?"

"Of course."

My father came forward.

"What is your name, child?"

"Isabel Ordez, sir."

"Not Isabel Suzanne?"

She seemed surprised.

"No, sir."

"Who was your mother?"

"My mother died when I was born."

"What was her name?"

"Doña Maria Isabella de Villacampa."

Zindorf made a sudden movement.

"She has been told that fable from infancy."

My father silenced him with a glance.

"Where were you born?" he continued.

"At the house of my grandfather, Don Esteban de Villacampa,
near Havana."

"Do you remember it?"

"No, sir. I was brought here when I was very
small."

"And Suzanne?"

"Suzanne was my nurse. She was kind to me."

"Was she your mother?"

The girl drew herself up, not with anger but with
the instinctive dignity of her blood.

"No, sir."

My father took the apple blossoms from her hand and
placed them in a cup upon the table.

"What do you understand is to occur today?"

A faint color entered her face.

"Mr. Morrow is to take me to Morrovale. Mr. Zindorf
says that my father has arranged everything and that we
shall be married when Father returns."

Morrow examined the jeweled ring upon his finger.

"Has Mr. Morrow said so?"

The girl looked toward him.

"He has been very courteous."

"That was not my question."

Morrow's face hardened.

"Pendleton, this catechism exceeds your office."

"My office," said my father, "has not yet begun."

He turned again to Isabel.

"You will not go to Morrovale."

The color left her face.

"Sir?"

"You will go to Havana, to the house of Don
Esteban de Villacampa. If Mr. Morrow desires your hand, he
may cross the sea, enter by your grandfather's door, give
his name to the servants, and make his proposal in
the presence of your family."

Morrow laughed, but there was no amusement in the sound.

"You dispose of distances and grandfathers with a majestic ease."

"There will be no sale," said my father.

The young woman looked at Zindorf.

"Sale?"

No one answered her.

She looked at Morrow. He turned aside.

Then she looked at my father, and the truth, not
in all its degradation but in its broad and dreadful
shape, began to enter her mind.

"Sir," she whispered, "what sale?"

My father took off his coat and placed it about
her shoulders, covering the white dress and the bridal veil.

"None," he said.

The second stroke of the bell came over the fields.

This time it was unmistakable. It was the bell behind
Saint Cuthbert's.

Zindorf hurried to the western window. Along the distant road,
beyond the line of wet sycamores, several mounted men had
appeared.

He wheeled upon my father.

"What have you done?"

"I have asked the county to attend me."

"By what authority?"

"By the authority under which men come when a bell
rings for the dead."

Zindorf's face had become the color of ashes.

Morrow went to the door, but my father placed his
great hand against it.

"Remain."

"Do you arrest me?"

"Not yet."

"Then stand aside."

"No."

For the first time Lucian Morrow's elegance failed him. His
lips drew back; the courtly mask became for an instant
the naked face of appetite balked in possession.

"You forget my name."

"I remember it perfectly."

"You forget my family."

"I remember that also."

"My father sat in the Council."

"Your father is dead."

"My house—"

"Will not stand between you and the law."

Zindorf returned to the table.

"This is folly. The girl is property. Your sentiment does
not alter her condition. Her mother was Suzanne. The assignment
is valid. You may dislike the transaction, but you cannot
destroy title by pronouncement."

My father nodded.

"That is true."

The admission restored something of Zindorf's courage.

"You concede it?"

"I concede that indignation is not evidence."

"Then draw the deed."

My father reached inside his waistcoat and brought out a
packet wrapped in oilskin.

"No," he said. "I shall read the evidence."

He untied the packet.

Within it were three papers: a certificate bearing ecclesiastical seals;
a letter in Spanish; and a narrow instrument crowded with
signatures and ribbons.

At the sight of them Zindorf gripped the edge of
the table.

Morrow saw the movement.

"You know these documents?"

"I do not."

"You knew them before Pendleton unfolded them."

"I knew nothing."

My father addressed Isabel.

"This is a certificate from the parish of San Cristóbal
in Havana. It records the marriage of Diego Ordez to
Doña Maria Isabella de Villacampa, daughter of Don Esteban de
Villacampa."

The girl did not speak.

"This second paper records your baptism. It names you as
the legitimate child of that marriage."

He lifted the third document.

"This is an authenticated copy of the manumission of Suzanne,
executed four years before your birth."

Zindorf struck the table.

"Copies! Foreign papers! Seals purchased in a Spanish port! Will
a Virginia court receive this rubbish?"

"It will receive the originals when Don Esteban brings them."

"Don Esteban is in Cuba."

"He is now in Norfolk."

Zindorf stared at him.

"And tomorrow," my father continued, "he will be here."

Outside, the bell sounded a third time.

The mounted men had reached the lower road. Others were
approaching on foot from the farms to the north. They
moved without haste, but from every road and path the
county was converging upon Saint Cuthbert's.

The old chapel, so long abandoned to avarice, had begun
again to gather a congregation.

Isabel put one hand against the table.

"My grandfather is in Virginia?"

"Yes."

"And my father?"

My father was silent.

She read the answer in that silence.

"Where is he?"

"My child—"

"Is he dead?"

"Yes."

The veil slipped from her arm and fell to the
floor.

Zindorf spoke quickly.

"This is an outrage. Ordez vanished three years ago. No
man knows that he is dead."

"I know," said my father.

"How?"

"I found his body yesterday."

The room seemed to contract about the words.

Even Morrow was still.

My father folded the marriage certificate and returned it to
the oilskin.

"Three years ago Diego Ordez came to me. He was
in fear of his life. He had entered partnership with
Zindorf in the purchase of land and in certain commercial
ventures. He discovered that Zindorf had altered accounts, forged obligations
and used the assignment now on this table to claim
property never intended to pass under it."

"It is a lie!" Zindorf cried.

My father went on.

"Ordez intended to sail for Havana with his daughter. Before
he could do so, he disappeared. Zindorf announced that he
had gone west to escape his creditors. There was some
evidence for this. His horse was found beyond the mountains.
Letters in his name were sent from the Ohio settlements.
And the assignment appeared to place his affairs in Zindorf's
hands."

"Then why did you not inquire?" Morrow asked.

"I did."

"For three years?"

"For three years."

Zindorf's voice was thin.

"You found nothing."

"I found the horse had been led westward by one
of your drivers. I found the letters had been copied
from words Ordez wrote in an old account book. But
I did not find Ordez."

"Until yesterday?" said Morrow.

"Until yesterday."

The bell rang again.

This time the sound rolled close above the roof, shaking
the air in the old stone room. Isabel started, but
my father remained immovable.

He said:

"Last winter a flood carried away part of the bank
below the abandoned meetinghouse at Black Run. There had been
a chapel there before Saint Cuthbert's was built. Nothing remained
but three wall stones, an altar step and a wild
pear tree. Yesterday, Ephraim Cole was clearing the place for
pasture when the earth gave way beneath the roots. He
found a human arm."

Zindorf's hands were flat upon the table.

"The body had been thrust into a shallow trench beside
the old altar. The skull was broken. There were fragments
of a Spanish clasp, three silver buttons, and on one
finger a ring engraved with the arms of Ordez."

Isabel made no sound. My father's coat enclosed her like
a dark mantle. She seemed very young beneath it.

"My father," she said at last, "was he alone?"

"No," my father answered. "I was with him when they
raised the body. He was not alone."

She understood the mercy intended in that reply. She bowed
her head.

Morrow crossed slowly to the open window.

"You have proved murder," he said. "You have not proved
the murderer."

"That is so."

"And these papers?"

"They were sewn inside the lining of Ordez's coat. The
oilskin had preserved them."

Zindorf laughed abruptly.

"Then your mystery is ended. Some robber killed him and
overlooked the documents."

"No," said my father. "The robber searched him."

"How can you know?"

"Because the coat had been opened. The pockets were turned
out. The money belt was cut away. The boots were
pulled from the feet. The murderer knelt in the damp
clay beside the body and searched every visible part of
the clothing. But he did not know that the lining
concealed a second seam."

"A highwayman."

"Perhaps."

"One of Ordez's servants."

"Perhaps."

"A wandering savage."

"Perhaps."

The repeated word disturbed Zindorf more than accusation.

He licked his lips.

"Then you have nothing."

"I have the ground."

"The ground?"

"The trench was covered quickly. The clay beneath the overhanging
altar stone remained sealed from rain and sun. When the
bank fell away, the old surface appeared as it was
on the night of the murder."

Morrow turned from the window.

"Footprints?"

"No."

"Finger marks?"

"No."

"Then what?"

My father did not answer.

Outside, voices sounded. The first riders were entering the yard.
Bridles jingled; a dog barked; men called to one another
beneath the ruined bell frame.

Zindorf moved toward the iron box.

My father's hand closed over his wrist.

"Leave it."

"There are private accounts."

"They will presently be public."

Zindorf tried to pull away, but my father held him
as one might hold a child.

"You have no warrant!"

"The sheriff carries it."

"You cannot charge me upon a body found after three
years!"

"I can charge you upon the body. Whether you hang
will depend upon the sign."

Zindorf ceased struggling.

"What sign?"

My father released him.

"There are signs that men choose and signs that are
imposed upon them. Cain received a sign. The children of
Israel marked their doors. Kings wear a crown; judges bear
a commission; the priest goes before the altar in vestments.
But evil also loves a sign. It steals sacred garments,
sacred words, sacred buildings. It makes theft resemble commerce and
appetite resemble marriage. It puts on humility as a coat."

Morrow's eyes went to Zindorf's coarse black clothing.

The ex-cleric drew back.

"You preach now, Pendleton?"

"No. I identify."

The door shook beneath a heavy blow.

"Justice Pendleton!" a voice called. "It is Sheriff Brooke."

My father did not open it.

He looked at Morrow.

"You desired a sacrament. Observe what becomes of a sacrament
in the hands of men such as you. A bride's
dress is made the wrapping of a sale. A father's
arrangement is made the name of a deception. A deed
is summoned not to defend liberty, but to place an
iron title upon a free woman."

Morrow's face was pale, but he retained some portion of
his insolence.

"You cannot make me a murderer because Zindorf proposed an
accommodation."

"No. You are not the murderer."

The young man breathed.

"You are merely the purchaser."

The words struck with a colder force than accusation. Morrow
looked toward Isabel, but she did not lift her head.

My father turned to Zindorf.

"You entered the Church and abandoned it. Yet you kept
the walk, the tonsure, the folded hands and the coarse
garment. Men called you monk long after you had ceased
to believe in God."

"I owe no answer for my clothing."

"Your clothing will answer for you."

Zindorf's eyes dropped involuntarily to his body.

My father saw it.

"So!" he said. "You know the sign."

"I know nothing."

"You are lost, Zindorf."

The sheriff struck the door again.

"You are lost!"

Zindorf sprang suddenly toward the eastern window.

Morrow stepped aside. The movement might have saved him from
implication, or it might have been merely the instinct of
a graceful man unwilling to be touched by another's ruin.

Zindorf reached the sill, but my father caught the back
of his coat. The coarse cloth tore from the collar
to the shoulder. Zindorf twisted, fell against the table and
dropped upon both knees.

There he remained.

The position was grotesquely like prayer.

Everyone looked down.

Across the knees of his trousers were two great patches
of darker cloth. They were not alike. The left was
an oblong with one corner doubled beneath; the right had
been cut in a half-circle to avoid a burn in
the original fabric. The stitching was heavy and irregular. In
one patch a cross-shaped darn had been made with pale
thread.

Zindorf tried to rise, but my father's hand pressed him
down.

"The ground at Black Run was damp," my father said.
"The murderer knelt to search the corpse."

No one moved.

"In the clay beside Ordez's ribs were two impressions. Not
the impressions of knees merely, but of patched knees—the very
patches, the very particular patches: on the left, an oblong
with the folded corner; on the right, a half-circle; and
in the clay, clear as a seal upon wax, the
crossing of the coarse stitches."

Zindorf's mouth opened.

"The clothes are old. Another man could have worn them."

"You were seen in them the week before Ordez vanished."

"Then the marks prove only that I found him!"

"You told the county you believed him alive."

"I found him dead and feared accusation."

"You robbed him?"

"No!"

"You cut away the money belt?"

"No!"

"You removed the boots?"

"No!"

"You sent his horse beyond the mountains?"

"No!"

"You forged his letters?"

"No!"

Each denial was weaker than the one before it, and
my father's questions came like blows upon a gate.

"You took the assignment?"

"It was mine!"

"You sought the papers?"

"I had a right—"

"To the marriage certificate?"

Zindorf stopped.

The room became utterly silent.

My father bent over him.

"How did you know which papers were hidden?"

"I did not say—"

"You sought them. You searched the coat, the boots, the
belt. You knew Ordez carried proof that his child was
legitimate and free. You killed him to obtain it. But
the papers remained inside the lining. For three years you
possessed the girl under a false claim, and now, fearing
the return of her grandfather, you determined to sell her
beyond immediate recovery."

Zindorf lifted his face. Whatever monk-like quality had once clung
to it was gone. Avarice and terror had stripped away
the counterfeit. He appeared neither priest nor merchant, but an
old predatory creature caught over the thing it had buried.

"The assignment was mine," he repeated.

My father's voice rose then, and those outside the door
afterward said it seemed to come through the stones of
Saint Cuthbert's.

"The assignment conveyed slaves. She was not a slave. It
conveyed chattels. She was not a chattel. It conveyed what
Ordez owned. A father does not own his legitimate child!"

He pointed to Isabel.

"She stands before you under the law of Spain, under
the law of Virginia, under the law that kings acknowledge
and wicked men deny. She is the daughter of Diego
Ordez and Doña Maria Isabella de Villacampa. She is free."

He opened the door.

Sheriff Brooke entered with six men behind him. Beyond them
the yard was full of citizens: farmers in hunting shirts,
planters in riding coats, smiths, millers and laborers, some mounted
and some on foot. The bell rope swung above their
heads in the April light.

"Take Abel Zindorf," my father said.

The sheriff advanced.

Zindorf did not resist. He remained on his knees, staring
at the patches that had betrayed him.

Then, as Brooke bent to raise him, Zindorf began to
laugh.

It was not the laughter of amusement or defiance. It
came in small dry bursts, like sticks breaking in a
fire.

"The knees!" he said. "You would hang a man by
his knees!"

"No," replied my father. "By what he did upon them."

They took him out.

The people in the yard drew aside. No insult was
spoken, and no hand struck him. This silence, my father
said, was more dreadful than violence, for the county had
already withdrawn from Zindorf the fellowship of living men.

Lucian Morrow remained by the window.

"Am I detained?" he asked.

My father regarded him.

"The criminal law has no charge against you."

Morrow adjusted his torn lace cuff.

"Then I shall leave."

"You may."

He crossed the room with his accustomed grace, but at
the threshold Isabel spoke.

"Mr. Morrow."

He stopped.

She had removed my father's coat. She stood again in
the white dress, but it no longer made her appear
a bride. It was now like the garment of one
awakened from a ceremony before the false vows had been
spoken.

"Did you know?" she asked.

Morrow did not pretend to misunderstand.

"I knew what Zindorf told me."

"That I was enslaved?"

"Yes."

"And you permitted me to believe that you would marry
me?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Perhaps he
sought some elegant phrase, some ambiguity upon which honor might
appear to balance. But the presence of my father, the
sheriff and the open daylight made elegance useless.

"Yes," he said.

Isabel inclined her head.

"Then I am glad you need not go to Havana."

Morrow's face flushed. He bowed to her, to my father
and to no one else. Then he descended the stone
steps, mounted his bay horse and rode out through the
gathered people.

No man barred his path.

Yet he departed less freely than Isabel remained.

My father sent two matrons from the neighborhood to attend
her. Before noon she left Saint Cuthbert's in the sheriff's
carriage and came to our house, where my mother received
her. Don Esteban arrived the following evening, an old Spanish
gentleman with white hair, a black cane and a retinue
so formal that our servants watched them in wonder.

When he saw Isabel, he knelt.

This action startled those present, because Don Esteban was proud,
and because men of his rank were accustomed to receive
homage rather than offer it. But he did not kneel
to abase himself. He knelt to gather his daughter's child
into his arms.

Zindorf was tried at the autumn session.

The papers found in the coat were accepted after testimony
from the Spanish consul and from Don Esteban. The patched
garments were produced. My father also brought to court two
shallow molds of plaster taken from the clay at Black
Run. When the patches on Zindorf's trousers were pressed into
soft earth beside those molds, line answered line, stitch answered
stitch, and even the doubled corner on the left knee
appeared.

There were other proofs. A servant confessed to leading Ordez's
horse westward. A clerk identified Zindorf's hand beneath the forged
letters. Ordez's silver buckles were found concealed behind a stone
in the old church-house. Each fact joined the next until
the chain could neither be broken nor mistaken.

Yet the people remembered chiefly the patches.

Zindorf was convicted of murder and hanged in November.

On the morning before his execution he asked for a
priest.

Some believed fear had restored his faith. My father did
not permit men to mock the request.

"If he counterfeited prayer," he said, "that is no reason
to deny him the true thing."

Lucian Morrow went abroad. Reports placed him in Paris, Naples
and Madrid. He returned years afterward, ill and greatly altered,
and died at Morrovale without wife or child.

Isabel sailed for Havana with her grandfather. She wrote often
to my mother and sent me, on my marriage, a
little silver cross that had belonged to Doña Maria. I
have it still.

The house at Saint Cuthbert's was never again inhabited. After
Zindorf's death, the county removed the dividing floors and the
boards across the chancel. No congregation formally claimed the ruin,
but travelers sometimes entered to rest, and circuit preachers occasionally
held service there.

The bell remained.

My father said that this was proper.

"It had rung once for a dead church," he told
me. "Then it rang for a dead man. In the
end it summoned the living."

I asked him whether he believed Providence had preserved the
marks in the clay.

He sat for some time before answering. We were on
the western porch, and the sun lay upon the fields.
His great hands rested on the head of his cane.

"Elizabeth," he said, "Providence does not commonly write its judgments
in letters of fire. Men would dispute the spelling. It
writes them in the ordinary substance of the world—in clay,
in cloth, in a seam overlooked, in a bell rope
pulled by a human hand. The proud expect thunder. They
are therefore taken by a thread."

"But why," I asked, "should the sign have been upon
his knees?"

My father looked toward the mountains.

"Because that was the last sacred form he had left,
and he had corrupted it. He dressed greed in poverty.
He made a church into a market. He made marriage
into purchase. He bent his knees, not before God, but
beside the dead whom he had robbed."

He rose then, and his shadow crossed the porch.

"The thing a man counterfeits," he said, "will sometimes become
the witness against him."

Sir Henry Marquis closed the diary.

The fire settled with a little rush of sparks. Beyond
the tall library windows, the London rain continued to fall;
but in the silence of the room I seemed to
hear another sound, thin with distance and age—the iron voice
of a bell above the green roads of Virginia.

Sir Henry touched the faded calfskin cover.

"There," he said, "was a murderer betrayed not by the
footprint of his flight, nor by the hand that struck,
but by the posture of prayer."

And after a moment he added:

"The patches on the knees were the sign of death."

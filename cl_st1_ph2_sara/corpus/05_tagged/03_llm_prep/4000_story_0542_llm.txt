## Gilligren and the King's Pie

You have heard, no doubt, the old song about sixpence
and a pocketful of rye, and four-and-twenty blackbirds baked in
a pie. But perhaps you have never been told how
the sixpence bought the rye, how the blackbirds came to
be in the pie, or why they began to sing
when the pie was opened. If so, draw your chair
nearer, and I will tell you the whole story.

Many years ago, when roads were dusty, coaches were slow,
and kings wore crowns more often than they do nowadays,
there lived a boy named Gilligren.

Gilligren was an orphan. His father and mother had died
when he was very young, and he had been sent
to live with his aunt and uncle, who owned a
small house beside the turnpike road. They were not poor,
though they liked people to believe they were, and they
were neither gentle nor generous.

Gilligren worked for them from early morning until night. He
fetched water, chopped sticks, weeded the garden, swept the floor,
fed the hens, and carried baskets to market. His aunt
gave him the smallest piece of bread and his uncle
found fault with the largest piece of work.

"If you were not such a useless boy," his aunt
would say, "you would be grateful for all we do."

Gilligren was grateful for the roof above him, but he
could not be grateful for blows, harsh words, and an
empty stomach. Still, he was patient and cheerful, and whenever
he had a moment to himself, he would stand at
the garden gate and watch the coaches rolling towards London.

Now it happened that a new king was to be
crowned, and all along the turnpike road people spoke of
nothing else. Merchants, musicians, soldiers, cooks, tailors, and country families
passed the house daily, travelling to see the coronation.

"London must be a marvellous place," thought Gilligren. "A clever
boy might make his fortune there."

One evening, after his uncle had beaten him for dropping
a cracked earthenware bowl which was scarcely worth a penny,
Gilligren made up his mind.

"I shall go to London," he said. "I have two
strong legs, a willing pair of hands, and more courage
than my aunt and uncle suppose. Surely those are enough
to begin with."

He possessed only one treasure: a silver sixpence which had
belonged to his mother. He had kept it hidden beneath
a loose stone near the henhouse, and before sunrise he
took it out, wrapped it in a scrap of cloth,
and put it in his pocket.

Then he left the house without waking anyone.

The morning was fair, the sunshine lay bright upon the
fields, and larks sang above the dark green hedges. Gilligren
walked briskly, pleased by every mile that stood between him
and his relations. He had never travelled so far before,
and even the road signs, carts, toll gates, and distant
church towers seemed to him part of a grand adventure.

By noon, however, his feet were sore and his stomach
was empty. He had brought no food, for there had
been none he could take honestly, and the smell of
bread from a passing market wagon made him painfully hungry.

Presently he saw a farmer sitting beside the road upon
an overturned bucket. At the farmer's feet lay a small
sack.

"Good day, lad," said the farmer. "Where are you going?"

"To London," replied Gilligren, "to make my fortune."

"With what money?"

"With sixpence," said Gilligren proudly.

The farmer was a wicked fellow, and when he heard
this, his eyes narrowed greedily.

"Sixpence is just the sum you need," he said. "By
good luck, I have here a sack filled with rye.
Take it to London, sell it at the coronation fair,
and you may double your money."

Gilligren examined the sack. It was tied tightly, and though
it seemed light, he knew little about the weight of
grain.

"Is the sack truly filled with rye?" he enquired.

"As full as honesty itself," answered the farmer, which was
not full at all in his case. "But I must
have the sixpence at once."

Gilligren hesitated. His mother's coin was dear to him, yet
a fortune cannot be made unless something is ventured.

"I will buy it," he said.

The farmer snatched the sixpence, pushed the sack into his
hands, and hurried away.

Gilligren carried the sack along the road until he reached
a green meadow. There he untied it, intending to eat
a few grains to quiet his hunger.

To his dismay, the sack contained no more than a
handful of rye. The rest was air.

"The rogue has cheated me!" cried Gilligren.

For a moment he felt so miserable that he could
have wept. His sixpence was gone, London was still far
away, and his splendid sack of grain would scarcely have
fed one hen.

But Gilligren was not the sort of boy who remained
miserable for long.

"The farmer has my sixpence," he said, "but I still
have a handful of rye and a good sack. I
must discover what can be done with them."

He tried chewing the rye, but it was dry and
hard. So he scattered it upon the grass, thinking that
at least the field mice or birds might enjoy what
he could not. Then he crawled beneath the hedge, placed
the empty sack under his head, and fell asleep.

The next morning he awoke to a tremendous chirping, fluttering,
pecking, and quarrelling.

He raised his head cautiously. A flock of blackbirds had
discovered the rye and were breakfasting upon it. Some hopped
through the grass, some flapped their wings, and some scolded
their neighbours in loud, bright voices.

Gilligren counted them.

"One, two, three—why, there are four and twenty!"

Then he looked at the sack and conceived what he
thought to be a very good idea.

He rose slowly, holding the sack open between his hands.
The birds were so busy disputing over the last grains
that they did not notice him until he sprang forward
and dropped the sack over the whole noisy company.

There followed such a commotion as you may imagine. The
sack leapt and rolled upon the grass while four-and-twenty blackbirds
flapped inside it.

"Be still, my friends," said Gilligren. "I mean you no
harm."

He tied the sack loosely, leaving room for air, and
slung it over his shoulder. It was now much heavier
than before, and every few steps a black beak poked
through the cloth, but Gilligren continued towards London.

By evening he reached the outskirts of the city. The
road was crowded with people arriving for the coronation. Flags
hung from windows, bells rang from steeples, and vendors shouted
offers of cakes, ribbons, medals, roasted chestnuts, and coloured prints
of the new king.

Gilligren tried to sell his blackbirds.

"Four-and-twenty fine blackbirds!" he cried. "Who will buy them?"

But one person said they were too noisy, another said
they were too thin, and a third offered him a
penny for the sack and nothing for the birds. A
pie-maker wished to buy them cheaply, but when Gilligren discovered
that the man meant to wring their necks, he refused.

"They have done me no injury," he said. "I will
not sell them to be killed."

Night came, and Gilligren slept beneath a market stall with
the sack beside him. In the morning the birds sang
so loudly that a watchman chased him away, and he
wandered towards the royal palace.

There he found the greatest bustle of all. Servants hurried
through gates, guards shouted orders, carts delivered barrels and baskets,
and cooks ran to and fro carrying fish, fruit, meat,
cream, and spices.

Gilligren approached a stout man in a white apron who
stood wringing his hands near the kitchen door.

"Please, sir," said Gilligren, "would the royal household care to
buy four-and-twenty blackbirds?"

The man stared at him.

"Blackbirds?"

"Four and twenty," replied Gilligren. "I counted them myself."

"Bless my ladle!" exclaimed the man. "I am the King's
chief cook, and His Majesty has this very morning commanded
that a blackbird pie be served at dinner. It is
a jest he has taken into his royal head, but
not one blackbird can be found in all the market.
If I fail, I may lose my place—and perhaps my
head with it. Bring them in!"

Gilligren followed the cook into the vast kitchen. Fires roared,
copper pans shone, and boys turned spits beneath roasting joints.
In the middle stood a pie dish broad enough to
hold a washbasin.

"Give me the birds," said the cook, reaching for the
sack. "We shall pluck them at once."

"No," replied Gilligren.

The cook blinked.

"What did you say, sirrah?"

"I said no. You may have the blackbirds, but you
must not kill them."

"Not kill them? How am I to bake a blackbird
pie without killing blackbirds?"

"That is your business," said Gilligren, "though I think I
know a way."

The cook groaned. "Do not play games with me, boy.
The King dines in three hours."

Gilligren lowered his voice.

"Bake an empty pie crust with a hollow beneath it.
When the crust is cool, make a little door in
the side, put the live birds inside, and close the
opening with pastry. Then carry the pie to the King."

The cook stared first at Gilligren, then at the jumping
sack, and then at the great dish.

"When the pie is opened," continued Gilligren, "the blackbirds will
fly out. They will be glad to escape, and perhaps
they will sing. It will be a far finer entertainment
than a heap of cooked birds."

The cook scratched his chin.

"It might delight His Majesty," he murmured.

"And it will save the birds."

"It might also enrage His Majesty and ruin me forever."

"Then say the idea was mine," replied Gilligren calmly.

The cook looked at him with sudden interest. "What payment
do you expect?"

"I want no gold for the birds," said Gilligren. "But
if the King is pleased, you must tell him the
truth and ask him to give me a place in
the royal household."

"You bargain boldly for a ragged boy."

"I began my journey with sixpence," replied Gilligren. "I mean
to make my fortune from it."

The cook laughed until his round body shook.

"Very well! If your plan succeeds, I shall speak for
you. If it fails, we may both be looking for
new heads."

The empty crust was baked golden brown and allowed to
cool. Gilligren himself placed the four-and-twenty blackbirds beneath it, speaking
gently so they would not struggle. The little door was
sealed, and the pie was decorated with pastry leaves and
sprigs of myrtle.

That evening it was carried into the banqueting hall.

The room blazed with candles. Lords and ladies sat in
jewelled garments, ambassadors wore silks and chains of gold, and
at the head of the long table sat the new
king beneath a crimson canopy.

Gilligren watched through a crack in the musicians' gallery.

The King regarded the enormous pie with pleasure.

"A blackbird pie!" he said. "Excellent! Let it be opened."

A servant lifted the upper crust.

For one breathless instant nothing happened.

Then a blackbird shot out and flew over the King's
crown.

A second perched upon a golden cup. Six more circled
the chandeliers, and all the rest burst from the pie
in a fluttering cloud. Four-and-twenty blackbirds swooped above the astonished
company, scattering pastry leaves and making the ladies duck their
feathered heads.

And then, as Gilligren had hoped, the birds began to
sing.

They whistled and warbled from the curtains, the windows, the
chair backs, and the royal canopy. One bold blackbird perched
upon the edge of the pie and sang directly to
the King.

For a moment the whole court was silent.

Then the King laughed.

He laughed so heartily that the Queen laughed too, and
soon every lord, lady, ambassador, servant, and musician in the
hall was laughing and applauding.

"This is the merriest dish ever set before a king!"
declared His Majesty. "Who invented it?"

The chief cook came forward, pale but honest.

"Your Majesty, the notion belongs to a boy named Gilligren,
who arrived at the kitchen door with the birds in
a sack."

"Bring him to me."

Gilligren was led into the hall in his dusty clothes.
He bowed as well as he knew how.

The King enquired where he had obtained four-and-twenty blackbirds.

Gilligren told him everything: how he had left his cruel
relations, how the wicked farmer had taken his sixpence, how
the sack had contained only a handful of rye, how
he had scattered the grain, and how the birds had
come at dawn.

"And why did you not allow the cook to kill
them?" asked the King.

"Because they had only eaten the rye I gave them,"
replied Gilligren. "It seemed unjust to take their lives for
accepting my breakfast."

"A humane answer," said the King. "And what reward did
you ask?"

"A place in Your Majesty's household, Sire."

The King smiled.

"You have courage, wit, honesty, and mercy. Those are four
things more valuable than a purse of gold. From this
day, you shall be one of my personal pages."

So Gilligren was dressed in royal livery and given a
chamber of his own, with a bed far softer than
the ground beneath a hedge. He served the King faithfully,
learned to read and write, travelled on royal business, and
became known for finding hope in difficulties and usefulness in
things others cast aside.

Years afterwards, the King made him a trusted gentleman of
the court, and Gilligren gained both honour and wealth. He
never became proud, nor did he forget hunger, cruelty, or
the fluttering sack that had carried his fortune to London.
He was kind to servants, generous to orphans, and especially
friendly to birds.

As for the dishonest farmer, he spent Gilligren's sixpence the
very same day and had nothing lasting from it. But
Gilligren, who had exchanged the coin for a handful of
rye, and the rye for four-and-twenty blackbirds, and the blackbirds
for a place beside the King, proved that courage and
cleverness may turn even a poor bargain into a rich
one.

And that is the whole story of the sixpence, the
rye, the blackbirds, and the pie.

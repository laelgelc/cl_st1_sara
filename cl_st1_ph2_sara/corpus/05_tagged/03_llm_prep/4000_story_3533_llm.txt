Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov was a young merchant in the town
of Vladimir. He owned two shops and a house, and
he was known as a cheerful man who liked songs,
laughter, and a glass of vodka. Before his marriage he
had sometimes drunk too much and become noisy, but after
marrying he gave up such habits, except on rare occasions.

One summer he prepared to go to the fair at
Nizhny Novgorod. His carts were loaded with goods, the horses
stood ready in the yard, and the drivers waited for
daylight.

As Aksionov took leave of his family, his wife said:

"Ivan Dmitrich, do not go today. I had a bad
dream about you."

He laughed and asked, "Are you afraid I shall go
to the fair and enjoy myself too much?"

"I do not know what I fear," she said. "I
dreamed you came home and took off your cap, and
your hair was all grey."

Aksionov had thick, curly hair, dark as a raven's wing.
He took off his cap and bent his head toward
her.

"Look well," he said. "There is not one grey hair.
Your dream means I shall return with great profit."

His wife did not smile.

"Stay one more day," she said.

But Aksionov kissed her and the children, climbed into his
cart, and set out.

He travelled all day and stopped toward evening at an
inn. There he met another merchant whom he knew. They
drank tea together beside the samovar, spoke about the fair
and the prices of goods, and then went to sleep
in neighbouring rooms.

Aksionov was not a heavy sleeper. He liked to travel
in the cool of the morning, and before dawn he
rose, paid the innkeeper, and ordered his driver to harness
the horses. The other merchant was still asleep, so Aksionov
did not disturb him.

After travelling about twenty-five miles, he stopped at a roadside
tavern to rest the horses. He sat on the porch,
ordered tea, and took out his guitar. The morning was
bright, and he began to sing quietly.

Then a troika drove up. An official in uniform got
out with two soldiers. He came to Aksionov and asked
who he was, where he had spent the night, and
when he had left the inn.

Aksionov answered readily. He even invited the official to drink
tea with him.

But the man went on asking questions.

"Were you alone at the inn?"

"No. Another merchant stayed there."

"Did you see him this morning?"

"No. I left before dawn."

"Why did you leave so early?"

"I always travel while the air is cool."

Aksionov tried to smile, but the official watched him closely.

Then he said, "I am the police officer of the
district. The merchant who slept near you was found this
morning with his throat cut. We must search your belongings."

Aksionov stared at him.

"Murdered?" he said. "But who killed him?"

"That is what we shall discover."

The soldiers opened Aksionov's bags and threw out his shirts,
boots, papers, and goods. At last the officer pulled a
knife from one of the bags. The blade was stained
with blood.

"Whose knife is this?" he asked.

Aksionov looked at it. His face became pale, and his
hands shook.

"It is not mine," he said.

The officer held the knife before him.

"The room was locked from within. No one but you
could have entered. You left before dawn, and this bloody
knife was hidden in your luggage. You also have money.
Tell me the truth. How did you kill him, and
how much did you steal?"

"I did not kill him," said Aksionov. "The money is
my own—eight thousand rubles. I have never seen that knife."

Yet his voice broke, and he trembled so badly that
he seemed guilty.

The officer ordered the soldiers to bind him. As they
tied his hands and put him in the troika, Aksionov
crossed himself and wept. His money and goods were taken
from him, and he was carried to the nearest town
and put in prison.

People were questioned in Vladimir. They said that Aksionov had
once been fond of drinking, but that he was a
good man and honest in business. None of this helped
him. He was charged with murdering the merchant and stealing
twenty thousand rubles.

His wife did not know what to believe. Her children
were still small, and the youngest was at her breast.
She took them with her and travelled to the town
where her husband was imprisoned.

At first the guards would not let her see him.
She begged them until at last she was taken into
the prison yard.

When she saw her husband dressed in prison clothes and
chained among thieves, she fell to the ground. For some
time she could neither speak nor move.

Aksionov raised her and held the children. They cried and
clung to him. He told her all that had happened
and asked her to send petitions to the Czar.

"I have already sent one," she said. "It was refused."

Aksionov lowered his head.

"Then God alone can help us."

His wife looked at him for a long time. Then
she said in a low voice:

"Vanya, tell me the truth. Was it not you who
did it?"

Aksionov drew back as if she had struck him.

"You too believe it!" he cried.

He covered his face with his hands and wept. A
soldier came to say that the visit was over. His
wife and children were led away, and Aksionov never saw
them again.

After that he ceased to hope in human justice. He
thought, "My own wife doubts me. What use is there
in begging men? God knows the truth. I must trust
in Him."

He was tried and condemned. He was flogged until the
wounds on his back bled, and when they healed, he
was sent in chains to Siberia.

Twenty-six years passed.

During those years Aksionov grew old. His dark curls became
white, as they had been in his wife's dream. His
beard grew long and thin. His back was bent, he
walked slowly, and he seldom laughed or spoke of his
former life.

In prison he learned to make boots and earned a
little money. With it he bought a book of sacred
lives. He read whenever there was enough light in the
prison, and on Sundays he read aloud in the chapel
and sang in the choir.

The prison authorities respected him because he was obedient and
peaceful. The prisoners called him "Gran'dad" and "the holy man."
When they quarrelled, they came to him to settle the
matter. When they wished to send a request to the
governor, they asked him to speak for them.

Aksionov accepted their respect without pride. He prayed often, worked,
and waited for death. He did not know whether his
wife was living, whether his children remembered him, or whether
his shops and house still belonged to the family. At
first such thoughts had tormented him. Later he said to
himself, "All worldly things pass. God alone remains."

One day a group of new prisoners arrived from Vladimir
province. In the evening the older prisoners gathered around them,
asking where they came from and why they had been
condemned.

Among the newcomers was a tall, strong man of about
sixty. His beard was cut short, and although his hair
was grey, he carried himself boldly. He laughed easily and
spoke as if prison were only another inn along the
road.

"My name is Makar Semyonich," he said. "I come from
Vladimir. They accuse me of stealing a horse, though I
only borrowed it. The driver was my friend, but the
police did not care about that. I ought to have
been sent here long ago for other matters, but they
never caught me. Now they send me for a thing
I did not do. So it is with justice."

When Aksionov heard the name of his town, he raised
his eyes.

"Do you know the merchants Aksionov in Vladimir?" he asked.

"Know them? Of course. They are rich merchants, though their
father is in Siberia. A sinner like the rest of
us, I suppose. But you, Gran'dad, why are you here?"

Aksionov did not wish to speak of it.

"For my sins," he said. "I have spent twenty-six years
in chains."

The others told Makar how Aksionov had been accused of
murdering a merchant and how a bloody knife had been
found in his bag.

Makar slapped his knee.

"Well, well!" he exclaimed. "How strange! And how old you
have grown, Gran'dad!"

Aksionov looked at him sharply.

"Why do you find it strange? Have you heard of
the affair?"

"Who has not heard of it?" Makar replied. "That was
long ago. People forget such things."

"But how could the knife have been put into my
bag?" Aksionov asked.

Makar laughed.

"The man who put it there must have been the
murderer. Unless the bag was under your head. Then he
could not have reached it without waking you."

At these words Aksionov's heart began to beat violently. No
one had told Makar where the bag had lain.

He thought, "This is the man."

That night he could not sleep. All the grief of
twenty-six years returned to him. He saw his wife as
she had been on the morning of his departure. He
saw her face and heard her warning. He saw his
children, one in a little coat and another at her
breast. He saw himself young and strong, sitting on the
tavern porch with his guitar. Then he remembered the flogging,
the chains, the long road to Siberia, the winter cold,
the prison walls, and his white hair.

Rage rose in him.

"All this was done by that man," he thought. "Because
of him my life was destroyed. If I could kill
him, I would gladly die for it."

For many days he avoided Makar. He prayed, but his
prayers gave him no peace. Sometimes he wished to accuse
Makar before the governor. Sometimes he wished to seize him
by the throat. At other times he thought, "What good
would revenge do? My youth will not return. My wife
may be dead. My children may not know me. Nothing
can restore twenty-six years."

Yet the hatred remained.

Two weeks later Aksionov was walking through the prison at
night when he saw loose earth falling from beneath one
of the sleeping platforms. He stopped. Makar Semyonich crawled out
from under the platform and stared at him.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Makar seized Aksionov's wrist.

"Ivan Dmitrich, say nothing," he whispered. "I am digging a
tunnel. Each day I carry the earth out in my
boots and scatter it in the yard. Keep quiet and
you may escape with me. If you betray me, they
will flog me—but before they do, I shall kill you."

Aksionov looked at him, and all his anger returned.

"I have no wish to escape," he said. "You killed
me twenty-six years ago. As for betraying you, I shall
do what God tells me."

The next day, while the prisoners were being marched across
the yard, a soldier noticed earth spilling from one man's
boot. A search was made, and the tunnel was discovered.

The governor came and questioned the prisoners.

"Who dug this tunnel?"

No one answered. They knew that Makar would be severely
punished, and even those who disliked him would not betray
another prisoner.

The governor questioned them one by one, but all denied
knowing anything. At last he turned to Aksionov.

"You are an honest old man," he said. "You speak
the truth. Tell me before God who dug the tunnel."

Makar stood nearby. He did not look at Aksionov.

Aksionov's whole body trembled. He thought, "Now God has given
him into my hands. I need only speak, and he
will be flogged. Perhaps he will die under the lash.
Why should I spare him? Did he spare me?"

He opened his mouth, but no words came.

The governor waited.

"Well, Ivan Dmitrich, who was it?"

Aksionov looked at Makar. For twenty-six years he had dreamed
that the truth might be known. Now he could punish
the man who had taken everything from him. But another
thought came: "If I betray him, shall my heart be
clean? His pain will not remove mine. God sees both
of us."

He lowered his eyes.

"I cannot say, your honour," he replied. "God does not
permit me to tell. Do with me as you please.
I am in your hands."

The governor repeated the question, threatened him, and urged him
to speak. Aksionov said nothing more. The matter was left
undecided.

That night Aksionov lay on his bed and heard someone
approach. Makar sat down beside him.

"What do you want?" Aksionov asked. "Go away, or I
shall call the guard."

Makar leaned close.

"Ivan Dmitrich, forgive me."

"For what?"

Makar was silent. Then he slipped from the bed and
knelt on the floor.

"It was I who killed the merchant," he said. "I
put the knife in your bag. I meant to kill
you too, but I heard a sound outside and had
to flee."

Aksionov could not answer. He had waited twenty-six years to
hear those words, yet now they brought him no joy.

Makar bowed until his forehead touched the floor.

"Forgive me, Ivan Dmitrich. Forgive me for God's sake. I
shall confess. They will send word that you are innocent,
and you will go home."

Aksionov looked at the kneeling man.

"It is easy for you to say that," he replied.
"But I have suffered twenty-six years. Where can I go
now? My wife may be dead. My children have forgotten
me."

Makar began to weep.

"When they flogged me, I did not suffer as I
suffer now. You saw me digging, and you could have
betrayed me. But you had mercy on me. I am
worse than a beast. Forgive me. Forgive a cursed man."

He struck his head against the floor and sobbed.

Aksionov remembered his wife's doubt, the bloody knife, the sentence,
and the road into exile. He remembered every winter and
every night behind the prison walls. He wanted to say,
"You cannot repay me." Yet when he saw Makar weeping,
his hatred weakened.

For many years Aksionov himself had begged God for mercy.
Now another man begged mercy from him.

At last Aksionov also began to weep.

"God will forgive you," he said. "Perhaps I am a
hundred times worse than you."

When he spoke these words, a great burden left his
heart. The longing for home, which had lived secretly within
him, disappeared. He no longer wished for revenge, and he
no longer feared death. For the first time in twenty-six
years he felt free.

Makar rose, but he could not stop crying.

After that night Makar was changed. He no longer boasted,
laughed loudly, or quarrelled. Whenever he looked at Aksionov, he
lowered his head. Aksionov spoke kindly to him, but he
did not ask when he would confess. He left the
matter to God.

Several days passed. Then Makar went to the governor and
admitted everything. He told how he had murdered the merchant,
hidden the bloody knife in Aksionov's luggage, and escaped before
dawn. He said that Ivan Dmitrich had been innocent.

The officials questioned him many times. They examined old papers
and sent reports from one office to another. At last
the truth was accepted, and an order was prepared for
Aksionov's release.

When the prisoners heard of it, they rejoiced.

"You will go home, Gran'dad," they said. "You will see
Vladimir again. You will walk without chains."

Aksionov only smiled.

"My home is where God wills," he said.

He did not accuse Makar, nor did he ask what
punishment would be given him. He had ceased to count
the years taken from him. In his heart there were
no prison walls, no chains, and no bloody knife. There
was only peace.

The order for his release travelled slowly across Russia. It
passed through offices and was carried over long roads. Before
it reached the prison, Aksionov fell ill.

He lay quietly on his bed while the prisoners gathered
around him. Makar stood at his feet and wept.

"Forgive me once more, Ivan Dmitrich," he said.

Aksionov looked at him.

"I have forgiven you," he whispered. "May God forgive us
both."

That night he died.

The next day the governor received the order stating that
Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov had been wrongly condemned and was to
be set free.

But the prison could no longer hold him, and no
order of the Czar was needed to release him.

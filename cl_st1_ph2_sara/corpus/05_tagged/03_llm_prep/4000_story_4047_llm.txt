# The Golden Deed of Vercingetorix

There came a time when the free peoples of Gaul,
divided as they were by ancient jealousies, perceived that the
Roman foot of iron was being planted upon their native
soil, and that, unless they could forget their quarrels, their
forests, rivers, hill-forts, and sacred places must alike pass beneath
the dominion of a foreign master. For seven years Julius
Caesar had carried war from the Rhine to the ocean.
Tribe after tribe had been defeated; chiefs had submitted, hostages
had been demanded, tribute imposed, and Roman roads and camps
had begun to mark the land as the first meshes
of a vast net. The conqueror called these things peace.
To the conquered they seemed but servitude wearing the grave
countenance of law.

Gaul was not then one nation, as France is now.
The country was inhabited by many tribes—the Aedui, Arverni, Bituriges,
Sequani, Senones, and others—each proud of its own name, its
own warriors, and its own traditions. In battle the Gauls
were terrible: tall men, impetuous in onset, splendidly armed, and
careless of death. But they lacked that discipline by which
Rome made one will govern ten thousand swords. The Roman
legion moved like a great machine; the Gallic host rushed
like a mountain torrent, mighty at first, but often broken
by the obstacles which steadiness could overcome.

It was among the Arverni, whose home lay in the
wild and beautiful region now called Auvergne, that the deliverer
arose. His name was Vercingetorix, a name which is believed
to signify the great king, or chief, of warriors. He
was young, of noble birth, commanding in stature and countenance,
eloquent in council, and of that ardent nature which draws
other men after it. His father had once sought supreme
authority and had paid for the ambition with his life.
The son desired power also; but, if we may judge
him by his deeds, he desired it not for idle
pomp, but that he might unite his country against the
stranger.

When he first called the Arverni to revolt, the cautious
elders drove him from their chief town. He did not
therefore abandon his purpose. In the open country he gathered
the poor, the daring, and the patriotic about him; and
so swiftly did his following increase that he returned in
strength, expelled those who had opposed him, and was proclaimed
commander. Messengers passed secretly from tribe to tribe. Everywhere men
who had mourned some kinsman slain, some hostage carried away,
some field trampled by Roman soldiers, listened eagerly. The revolt
spread like flame through dry heath.

Yet Vercingetorix understood that courage alone would not overthrow Caesar.
He imposed discipline upon his followers, punished disobedience with severity,
demanded hostages from doubtful allies, and summoned contingents of horse
and foot according to the strength of each tribe. More
painful still, he required the Gauls to destroy that which
was dearest to them. The Romans lived upon the country
through which they marched; therefore the country must become a
desert before them.

Villages were burned, granaries emptied, cattle driven away, and lonely
farmsteads given to the flames. The winter sky was reddened
by the sacrifice. Men stood silent while the roofs beneath
which they had been born fell inward amid showers of
sparks; women turned back to look upon homes which they
knew they should never see again. It was a piteous
necessity, but it was the price demanded by freedom.

One city only was spared. Avaricum, the chief town of
the Bituriges, stood strongly placed amid marshes and waters, approachable
by one narrow way. Its inhabitants knelt before Vercingetorix and
implored him not to destroy the fairest city of Gaul.
They promised that its walls could defy every Roman assault.
The young chief yielded reluctantly, though his better judgment warned
him against mercy in war.

Caesar came. Hunger had already begun to trouble his army,
for the scorched fields supplied neither corn nor forage; but
the Roman general was not easily turned aside. He set
his soldiers to work against Avaricum. They raised a vast
terrace, pushed covered galleries toward the walls, and brought forward
towers from which archers and engines could sweep the battlements.
The Gauls answered mine with mine, fire with fire, and
engine with engine. By day and night they labored, and
even Caesar praised their ingenuity and courage.

Rain fell; the marshes steamed; hunger sharpened the faces of
the legionaries; yet the Roman work advanced with deadly slowness.
At length, beneath the shelter of a storm, the soldiers
gained the wall. Avaricum was taken.

Then followed one of those massacres which stain the brightest
pages of Roman glory. Neither grey hairs nor helpless childhood
availed. Of the multitude within the city, scarcely any escaped
to bear the tidings to Vercingetorix. The fair town which
had trusted to its walls became a mournful heap, and
the chief who had wished to burn it stood justified
by the ruin which his compassion had permitted.

He did not reproach the survivors. Calling the Gallic leaders
together, he reminded them that one disaster did not decide
the fate of a nation. The Romans, he said, had
prevailed not by superior bravery, but by skill in siege
works; the tribes must therefore remain united, enlarge their armies,
and learn obedience. Such calmness after calamity is rarer than
courage in the hour of victory. Men who might have
blamed him instead loved him the more, and new forces
came to his standard.

The struggle now turned toward Gergovia, the stronghold of the
Arverni, set high upon a mountain above the plain. Here
every path and slope was familiar to Vercingetorix, while Caesar
had to bring his legions upward through difficult ground. Intrigue
labored for the Roman as well as the sword; for
Caesar sought to divide the Gallic tribes and win away
the Aedui, who had long been allies of Rome. But
at Gergovia Roman fortune failed.

The legions advanced too far in an assault upon the
heights. Whether the signal to retire was unheard or disregarded,
the soldiers pressed onward until the Gauls, descending upon them
with fierce cries, smote them from above and flank. Standards
were lost; centurions fell; the ordered line wavered and broke.
Caesar, who knew that a retreat must sometimes preserve what
rash valor would destroy, drew off his army. The victory
was not complete, but it rang through Gaul. Rome had
been compelled to turn away from an Arvernian fortress, and
the name of Vercingetorix rose higher than before.

Had all the tribes remained steadfast, the invader might perhaps
have been driven beyond the Alps. But union newly made
is easily strained. Caesar, watchful, rapid, and inexhaustible, repaired his
losses, obtained German horsemen from beyond the Rhine, and once
more sought his enemy. In a battle of cavalry the
Gallic riders were defeated, and Vercingetorix withdrew to Alesia.

Alesia stood upon a lofty hill between two rivers. The
slopes were steep; above them rose the fortified town; around
lay an open plain and, beyond it, a ring of
hills. Within the walls and camp were gathered nearly eighty
thousand fighting men, besides inhabitants and attendants. It was a
mighty force, but a multitude shut within one enclosure must
eat; and when the roads are closed, numbers become not
strength but hunger.

Caesar resolved not to storm Alesia, but to imprison it.

We would wish here to dwell upon the extraordinary works
by which the Roman soldiers performed this purpose; for they
show how discipline and patient labor may accomplish what mere
daring cannot. Around the hill they dug a line of
trenches extending for many miles. One ditch, broad and deep,
was filled where possible with water from the rivers. Behind
it rose ramparts and breastworks, strengthened by timber and crowned
with towers at regular distances. In front were concealed pits,
sharpened stakes, iron hooks, and caltrops, so disposed that an
attacking host might be disordered before it reached the wall.

But Caesar had not only the garrison to fear. Gallic
horsemen had escaped before the enclosure was complete, bearing orders
that every tribe should raise a relief army. If that
army came, the Romans would themselves be between two enemies.
Therefore a second line of fortifications was built outside the
first, facing away from Alesia. The besiegers were thus enclosed
within a double ring: the inner works held Vercingetorix in;
the outer works kept his countrymen out. Redoubts, towers, ditches,
palisades, traps, camps, and roads covered the valleys. The hill
was caught as a fly is caught within an enormous
web, and at the centre of that web the brave
defenders waited.

Food soon grew scarce. The inhabitants of the neighboring country
had crowded into Alesia for safety, bringing wives and children,
but little provision. Vercingetorix ordered the grain to be measured
and distributed. He forbade waste and held out the promise
of relief. Day followed day. The heaps in the storehouses
sank; horses were slaughtered; the weaker looked with hollow eyes
upon the portions given to the strong.

At last the chiefs determined upon a cruel measure. Those
who could not bear arms were sent out of the
town, in the hope that Caesar would allow them to
pass through his lines. Old men, women, and children descended
the hill and approached the Roman works, stretching forth their
hands and praying to be received as slaves, if only
they might be fed. Caesar would not admit them. Vercingetorix
could not take them back without hastening the ruin of
his army. Between the ramparts and the walls they remained,
rejected by both, victims not of their own fault, but
of that hard ambition which had brought the war upon
their homes.

Meanwhile the summons had gone throughout Gaul, and a vast
relief army assembled. Its numbers were perhaps magnified by fear
and hope, as numbers often are in ancient histories; yet
it was certainly great enough to threaten Caesar with destruction.
The warriors came in tribal companies, with painted shields, long
swords, banners, horns, and chiefs glittering in gold. They covered
the distant heights, and when those upon the walls of
Alesia beheld them, a shout went up which rolled across
the valleys.

Help had come.

The first attack was made upon the open plain. Gallic
cavalry advanced against the outer Roman line, while Vercingetorix led
his men from the town against the inner works. Thus
every legionary had enemies before and behind. For hours the
horsemen fought; dust hid the field; missiles darkened the air.
At last Caesar's German cavalry charged with such force that
the relief troops were driven back. The defenders of Alesia,
seeing the failure, retired sorrowfully up the hill.

A night attack followed. In darkness the outer Gauls crept
toward the trenches, carrying hurdles to fill the ditches and
hooks to tear down the ramparts. Suddenly their horns sounded,
and at the same moment Vercingetorix attacked from within. Torches
moved like fiery serpents along the walls. Stones, javelins, and
leaden bullets poured from the Roman towers. Men who avoided
the open pits trod upon buried spikes; those who reached
the ditch found the bank above them crowded with defenders.
The conflict continued until dawn began to whiten the sky,
when the Gauls again withdrew.

Still they would not yield. A weak place had been
discovered where a hill prevented Caesar's outer line from being
carried over the highest ground. Against this point a chosen
body of warriors was sent secretly, while the main host
advanced in full view. Within Alesia, Vercingetorix watched from the
height. He could see the distant movement, the Roman cohorts
hurrying from redoubt to redoubt, the standards bending through the
press. Then he led forth all who had strength to
fight.

Never was the double enclosure more fiercely assailed. The relief
army filled trenches with earth and brushwood, tore at the
palisades, and climbed over the bodies of the fallen. The
defenders from Alesia brought forward beams, ladders, and hooks. At
one place the Roman line began to give way. Caesar
sent reserve after reserve; but the attack continued, and for
a moment it seemed that the web must be rent.

The general himself now appeared, recognizable by the scarlet cloak
which he wore in battle. He was no longer young,
and his thin, marked, eagle face showed the toil of
many campaigns; his temples were narrow, his head already bald,
but the searching eye missed nothing. Ambition was in that
countenance, and genius also. He rode to the threatened point,
addressed his soldiers, and flung his last reserves into the
struggle. Then Roman cavalry, sent round by a concealed way,
fell upon the rear of the Gallic host.

The effect was terrible. Attacked in front and behind, the
relieving warriors lost their order. Some fought where they stood;
others fled down the slopes. The Romans pursued. From the
walls the garrison saw the standards of its countrymen disappear,
and heard the victorious cries of the enemy.

Hope was gone.

That night there was council in Alesia. No provision remained
by which so great a multitude could long be sustained.
The relief army had been scattered; the Roman lines were
unbroken. Vercingetorix did not conceal the truth. He told the
chiefs that he had undertaken the war not for his
own advantage, but for the common liberty. If his death
could appease Rome, he was ready to die. They might
slay him and send his head to Caesar, or deliver
him living. His one prayer was that the people should
be spared.

There are victories gained by the sword, and there are
victories of the soul. The first may give a man
provinces, treasure, titles, and a triumph; the second asks him
to surrender all these things, and life itself, for those
whom he loves. Vercingetorix could have sought escape while horsemen
still remained; he could have left the weaker to endure
the conqueror's anger. He did not do so.

On the following day the gates of Alesia were opened.
The Gallic soldiers came forth and laid their weapons in
a great heap—swords, spears, shields, helmets, bows, and standards which
had been borne proudly through the war. The clang of
each arm upon the mournful pile declared that another free
warrior had yielded.

Then Vercingetorix appeared.

Tradition has pictured him mounted upon his finest horse, arrayed
in his richest armor. He rode before Caesar's seat, circled
it, dismounted, cast down his arms, and stood silent at
the victor's feet. Whether every circumstance occurred precisely thus we
cannot know; but the truth beneath the picture remains. The
chief gave himself up. The master of Gaul sat before
him; the defender of Gaul stood unarmed.

Caesar accepted the sacrifice, but showed no generosity worthy of
it. The captives were divided among the Roman soldiers or
reserved for other purposes; the tribes submitted; and Vercingetorix was
carried away in chains. He never again beheld the jagged
peaks and wild valleys of Auvergne, nor the hill of
Gergovia where Rome had once recoiled before him.

For six years he remained a prisoner. During those years
Caesar completed his conquests, crossed the Rubicon, defeated his Roman
rivals, and became the foremost man in the world. Kings
obeyed him; senates trembled; cities contended for the honor of
his favor. Yet in some dark place the captive Arvernian
waited, preserved not from mercy, but because Roman glory had
use for him.

The day came at last when Caesar had leisure to
celebrate his triumphs. Rome put on festival attire. Flowers and
colored cloths adorned the streets; incense rose from altars; the
temples were opened; balconies and roofs were crowded. Magistrates in
their robes led the procession. Trumpeters sounded; wagons bore gold,
silver, curious arms, and images of captured towns. Painted tablets
displayed rivers crossed, mountains climbed, armies overthrown, and nations subjected.
White oxen, their horns gilded and their bodies wreathed for
sacrifice, moved slowly toward the Capitoline Hill. Captive princes and
chiefs walked in chains. Behind them came Caesar in a
chariot of ivory and gold, crowned with bay, clothed in
triumphal purple, while soldiers followed singing songs of victory.

The multitude shouted. They saw the treasure, the standards, the
pictures of battle, and the conqueror whose fortune seemed greater
than that of mortal man.

But where was the vanquished?

He too was there. Vercingetorix, once surrounded by Gallic warriors
and obeyed from the mountains to the sea, walked chained
in the procession of his enemy. The Roman crowd gazed
upon him as part of the spectacle. They beheld the
man who had burned cities to save his country, endured
defeat without despair, and surrendered himself for his followers. To
them he was the final ornament of Caesar's glory.

When the triumphal chariot turned upward toward the Capitol, Vercingetorix
was led aside to the prison beneath the hill. There,
while music sounded above and Rome feasted, he was put
to death. Thus ended a gallant life.

Caesar lived yet a little while to possess the world
and discover how insecure is power founded upon fear. In
the very Senate-house of Rome he fell beneath the daggers
of men who would not endure his greatness. His victories
remain among the marvels of military history, and none may
deny his genius. But genius without mercy may command admiration
while failing to deserve love.

Vercingetorix lost his army, his freedom, and his life. Yet
he gave himself for his people; and this was his
Golden Deed. Caesar gained Gaul and a triumph. Vercingetorix gained
no province, wore no laurel, and heard no applauding multitude.
Which, then, was truly great—the conqueror who sacrificed a nation
to his glory, or the conquered chief who sacrificed himself
for his nation?

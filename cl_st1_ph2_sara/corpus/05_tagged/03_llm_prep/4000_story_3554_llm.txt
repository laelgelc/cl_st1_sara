## The Root, the Bread, and the Road

### I

A certain proprietor, before departing into a distant country, entrusted
to his tenants a broad meadow, from which they were
to obtain hay for their cattle. The meadow was good,
the soil deep and moist, and the grass, when properly
tended, grew higher than a man's knee. But among the
grass there appeared a coarse, bitter weed, which the cattle
would not eat, and which, if suffered to ripen, scattered
innumerable seeds.

The proprietor assembled the tenants and said to them:

"You must not mow this weed with the grass, for
its stalk is jointed, and every severed part which remains
upon the earth puts forth roots. Neither must you wait
until it has seeded. Go through the meadow in spring,
when the ground is soft, seize each plant low down,
and pull it up by the roots. It is troublesome
at first, but thereafter your labor will diminish."

The tenants heard him and promised obedience.

During the first spring they did as he had commanded.
They went forth slowly, stooping, and drew out the weed
with its long pale roots. The work seemed humiliating to
some of them. They said that a man looked foolish
crawling over a field and wrestling with single stalks. Nevertheless,
the meadow was cleansed.

But in the second year the proprietor was still absent,
and a new steward arose among the tenants, a loud
man who delighted in dispatch and visible activity. Looking upon
those who pulled the weed, he laughed.

"What an idle manner of working!" said he. "While you
creep on your knees, I can sweep down a hundred
plants with one stroke of the scythe."

He sharpened his blade, called the young men, and mowed
the weeds. The stalks fell in rows, and because the
field appeared smooth after them, all praised the steward's wisdom.

"See," they said, "how swiftly evil is destroyed! The master
spoke in former times, when instruments were imperfect and men
had little knowledge. We have advanced beyond him."

When autumn came, the severed joints had rooted, and in
spring there were ten plants where there had formerly been
one. The steward then declared that the multiplication proved the
necessity of mowing more vigorously. More scythes were bought; days
of mowing were appointed; songs were composed concerning the valor
of those who cut the weed; and the finest scythe,
decorated with ribbons, was hung in the common hall.

Year succeeded year. The hay diminished, the weed increased, and
the tenants, instead of doubting the practice which produced the
calamity, ascribed the calamity to insufficient zeal in the practice.
He who cut down five hundred stalks was honored; he
who broke a scythe in the labor was called a
martyr; and children were taught that the security of the
meadow depended upon the sacred art of mowing.

At length an old book was found in a chest,
and in it were written the proprietor's original instructions. A
quiet man read them and said:

"Brothers, we are doing the very thing which our master
forbade. He did not tell us to leave the weed,
nor did he tell us merely to strike its visible
stem. He told us to pull it up by the
roots."

At once there arose a tumult.

"What!" cried the steward. "Would you have the weed flourish?"

"No," answered the man. "I would destroy it so completely
that it should not rise again."

"But you condemn the scythe."

"I condemn its use upon this weed, because it multiplies
that which you desire to destroy."

"Then you are the defender of weeds!"

"I say that the weed must be uprooted."

"He says we must cast aside our weapons," shouted one.

"He mocks those who have fallen in the meadow," shouted
another.

"He would surrender the grass to the enemy," cried a
third.

And so often was it repeated that the man desired
the triumph of the weed, that those who had not
heard him believed it, and even some who had heard
him began to doubt whether his plain words might not
conceal a secret treachery.

They brought him before the council.

"Do you admit," they asked, "that the weed is harmful?"

"I do."

"Do you admit that it must be resisted?"

"Certainly."

"Yet you oppose mowing?"

"I oppose the method which cuts the stem and strengthens
the root. Not to cease resisting the weed, but to
resist it wisely; not to spare it, but to destroy
it utterly; not to mow the weed, but to pull
it up by the roots—this is what I say."

The council conferred, and afterward announced that the man held
dangerous opinions concerning the toleration of weeds. He was forbidden
to speak in the meadow. Yet the mowing continued, the
weeds spread, the cattle starved, and whenever the tenants saw
the desolation, they exclaimed:

"Behold what would happen if men such as he prevailed!"

So it has been with that teaching which was proffered
to men, not for the increase of evil, but for
its destruction.

Christ said: "Resist not evil with evil." He said: "Love
your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to
them that hate you." He did not say, "Approve falsehood,
submit your conscience to wicked men, abandon the weak, or
call evil good." No such thing was said. He taught
us to resist the root of evil—which is hatred, fear,
covetousness, pride, and the desire of one man to dominate
another—by truth, patience, courage, love, and goodness.

But men, having for centuries resisted violence by violence, punishment
by punishment, and murder by murder, have made of this
mowing a sacred custom. They see that every war prepares
another war; that every execution brutalizes those who perform and
behold it; that every humiliation sows a desire for revenge;
and yet they say that these consequences prove only that
more soldiers, prisons, gallows, and engines of destruction are requisite.

If one reminds them of the master's command, they do
not examine whether the command is true. They do not
ask whether kindness may disarm what cruelty inflames, whether reason
may persuade where threats only harden, whether courageous non-participation in
evil may be stronger than obedient violence. They hasten instead
to alter his words.

"He tells us not to resist evil," they say.

No. I say that evil must be resisted, but not
nourished.

"He would allow the robber to rob, the murderer to
murder, the tyrant to oppress."

No. I say that the robber, murderer, and tyrant are
upheld by the same fear, anger, falsehood, and obedience which
violent resistance increases.

"He would have the innocent stand helpless."

No. I would have them cease pusillanimously lending their hands
to the institutions which prepare murder, and begin courageously speaking
truth, sheltering the persecuted, refusing unjust commands, sharing bread with
the hungry, and exposing the lie by which violence calls
itself order.

But this is not heard, because men prefer an accusation
which is easy to answer to an argument which requires
them to change their lives. It is easier to cry,
"He loves weeds!" than to kneel upon the earth and
undertake the patient labor of pulling out the roots.

And the strangest thing is that those who defend violence
claim to be the practical men. Their practice has filled
the earth with armaments, prisons, executions, rebellions, and perpetual terror;
nevertheless, they call practical the means which has never attained
its end, and impractical the means which they have never
sincerely attempted.

The weed rises thicker each spring, and they sharpen the
scythes.

### II

In a large town there were merchants who supplied the
people with flour, milk, oil, honey, and other provisions. At
first they sold what they promised. The flour was ground
from grain, the milk came from cows, the oil from
olives, and the honey from bees. Their profits were moderate,
and the townsmen were nourished.

Then one merchant discovered that powdered stone could be mixed
with flour, and that few purchasers perceived it. Another learned
that water, chalk, and a little fat might be passed
off as milk. A third colored a cheap and deleterious
liquid and sold it for oil. Others prepared a shining
syrup, perfumed it with flowers, and called it honey.

Because counterfeit goods cost little to produce and were advertised
loudly, the honest merchants could not compete with the dishonest
ones. Some abandoned their trade; some imitated the fraud; and
some protested, but were told that commerce must advance according
to the demands of the age.

In time, the people became accustomed to the adulterated provisions.
They no longer knew the taste of pure milk or
wholesome bread. The children grew pale, the old men suffered
pains, and physicians wrote learned treatises upon the mysterious decline
of public health. Yet the merchants prospered, built magnificent shops,
founded academies of commerce, and distributed medals among themselves for
improvements in the preparation and sale of nourishment.

Now in that town lived a housekeeper who had served
in many homes and knew how every wholesome article ought
to look, smell, and taste. She bought flour, rubbed it
between her fingers, and found grit. She boiled the milk,
and chalk remained at the bottom. She set the oil
in the cold, tested the honey, and proved that nearly
all was false.

She went into the market and said:

"This flour is not breadstuff, but stone mingled with breadstuff.
This milk is not milk, but whitened water. This oil
will not strengthen the body, and this honey never came
from a hive. Take them away and give us wholesome
food."

The merchants smiled among themselves, but before the people they
assumed solemn countenances.

"You have heard her," they said. "She wishes to destroy
the food supply."

"I wish to destroy no food," answered the housekeeper. "I
demand food in place of these counterfeits."

"She rejects our flour, milk, oil, and honey."

"I reject the stone, chalk, poison, and syrup which you
sell under those names."

"Are we, then, to close the shops? Shall bakers no
longer bake? Shall children be deprived of milk? Shall the
lamps go dark for lack of oil?"

"Let the shops remain open, but let them sell what
their signs announce. Let the bakers bake bread of grain;
let children receive milk; let oil be oil and honey
honey."

The merchants perceived that if the people understood this distinction,
their gain would cease, and therefore they repeated more loudly:

"She is the enemy of trade! She hates bread! She
would abolish milk and overthrow the noble industry of honey!"

They hired singers to compose verses about the blessings conferred
by the market. They invited professors from the academy, who
proved by elaborate calculations that a town without merchants must
inevitably starve. They led the housekeeper past cases filled with
loaves, bottles, jars, and shining packages, and asked with triumphant
gestures:

"Can you behold all this abundance and still maintain that
we supply no nourishment?"

She replied:

"I behold the packages and the signs; I question what
is within them."

But this answer displeased everyone, because the displays were beautiful,
the academy respected, the songs agreeable, and the truth inconvenient.
The crowd seized the housekeeper, tore her shawl, and drove
her from the market. Then the townsmen returned to their
purchases, and when their children sickened they blamed the weather,
the water, foreign peoples, and the inscrutable laws of nature,
but never the substances which they daily consumed.

The same thing occurs when I speak of that which
is called art and science.

I have nowhere said that men ought not to sing,
paint, write, discover, calculate, investigate, or communicate to one another
what they know and feel. To abolish art and science
would be as absurd as to abolish bread and milk.
Art is among the means whereby one man shares with
another his deepest feeling; science, in its plain and genuine
meaning, is the knowledge necessary for life. The question is
not whether these are needed, but whether that which is
sold under their honored names is indeed what it professes
to be.

If a book excites lust, vanity, contempt, and despair; if
a spectacle presents cruelty as amusement; if music, inaccessible to
all but a small idle class, serves only to produce
refined sensations in those whose labor is performed by others—am
I bound to call this art merely because critics have
labeled it so? If learned men devote their lives to
cataloguing what neither diminishes suffering nor increases brotherhood, while neglecting
the questions of bread, labor, war, poverty, and the meaning
of life—am I forbidden to ask whether their science is
nourishment or only whitened water?

Immediately the tradesmen of intellect reply:

"He wishes to burn the galleries."

I wish pictures to transmit sincere human feeling rather than
flatter wealth and appetite.

"He would silence music."

I wish music to unite men, not to become an
expensive narcotic for the satiated.

"He despises knowledge."

I despise no knowledge; I ask only what is known,
by whom it is known, to what end it is
known, and at whose expense the knower has leisure to
know it.

"He would return mankind to ignorance."

No, I would deliver it from the ignorance which memorizes
innumerable facts and does not know how a man ought
to live with his neighbor.

But no one, no one, not a single man among
the recognized dealers wishes to answer the actual question. They
prefer to prove that civilization cannot exist without art and
science, which I have never denied, just as the merchants
proved that a town cannot exist without food. They point
to libraries, laboratories, theaters, universities, exhibitions, journals, and societies, and
they ask whether such abundance can be counterfeit.

I answer again: the size of the shop does not
prove the purity of the bread.

There is genuine art whenever a sincere human feeling is
transmitted so clearly that it becomes common to strangers: the
grief of a mother, the joy of labor, the terror
of guilt, the peace of forgiveness, the recognition that every
man is one's brother. There is genuine science whenever knowledge
is sought and imparted according to the most urgent needs
of mankind: how to feed without enslaving, heal without exploiting,
labor without degradation, live without hatred, and die without falsehood.

Yet because these measures would dethrone much that now possesses
fame, payment, and institutional prestige, the measures themselves are declared
barbarous. The counterfeiters place pure bread and powdered stone beneath
one general name, and then accuse whoever rejects the stone
of rejecting bread.

The crowd, which has been trained to esteem what is
costly, difficult, obscure, and praised by authorities, joins in the
cry. It attacks the housekeeper and afterwards wonders why it
remains unfed.

There are splendid banquets everywhere, and everywhere men hunger.

### III

A company of travelers set out to cross a wide
country and reach a city in the east. At first
they followed a marked road. They had a chart, a
compass, and certain signs concerning the stars. But after many
days a mist covered the land, the road vanished beneath
reeds, and the travelers entered a bog.

They continued for some time, believing that the ground would
soon become firm. Instead, the water deepened, the carts sank
to their axles, and the travelers found themselves among dark
pools and crooked islands of moss. Their provisions diminished. Quarrels
arose concerning who had led them astray.

One group said:

"The only safety lies in constancy. We have been moving
in this direction; therefore we must continue in it. To
turn aside would confess that our previous labor was wasted."

They yoked more horses to the carts and pushed forward.
When the wheels sank, they called for stronger wheels; when
the horses foundered, they demanded more horses; and when men
perished in the black water, they erected signs honoring those
who had died in the advance.

A second group mocked the first.

"Constancy is stupidity," they said. "Salvation lies in change."

They rushed north until they met a pool, then west
until they met reeds, then south until the ground trembled.
Every hour they chose a new leader, and every leader
promised that his direction alone was progressive. They ran so
swiftly and altered their course so often that they mistook
movement for approach.

Among them was one man who climbed a dead tree
and tried to see beyond the mist. He examined the
compass, compared the chart with the streams, observed where the
moss grew upon the trunks, and waited at night for
a glimpse of the stars.

Then he called to both groups:

"Friends, we are lost. Let us stop. Let us unload
the carts which hinder us, determine where we are, and
choose our course by signs which do not depend upon
our wishes. If we continue merely because we have begun,
or run in every direction merely to avoid standing still,
we shall perish."

The first group replied:

"He is a coward. He wishes to halt the advance."

The second cried:

"He is idle. While we seek a hundred roads, he
sits and looks at the sky."

"I do not ask you to remain forever," said the
man. "I ask you to pause in order that your
movement may have a direction."

"Why pause?" shouted the leaders. "Why consider? Push forward!"

"You are moving deeper into the bog."

"He insults our martyrs!"

"You are circling back to the place from which you
started."

"He condemns freedom and experiment!"

"I condemn neither courage nor movement. I say only that
courage without knowledge leads a man more rapidly to destruction,
and movement without a chosen end is not progress."

But the travelers had become divided into parties, and each
party valued the triumph of its direction more than arrival
at the city. They tore the chart, broke the compass,
and accused the solitary man of discouraging the company. Some
forced him to drag a cart eastward; others pulled him
westward; and all agreed upon one thing only—that he who
advised them to discover their position was the enemy of
the journey.

Meanwhile the mist thickened, and from the distance came the
cries of those already sinking.

Is not our condition the same?

In one country laborers cease work and masters threaten hunger;
in another the masters dismiss thousands and the laborers prepare
violence. Governments multiply armies in order to preserve peace, and
because each government multiplies armies, all live in expectation of
war. Machines save labor, yet those whose labor is saved
are deprived of bread. Harvests are abundant, but men starve
because abundance cannot be profitably sold. Cities blaze with light,
while families sleep beneath bridges. Learned assemblies discuss the welfare
of mankind, and at their doors stand men whom no
one has leisure to hear.

One party says: "Continue in the ancient direction. Strengthen authority,
protect property, increase discipline, arm more thoroughly, punish more severely.
We have always proceeded thus; therefore salvation lies ahead."

Another says: "Change rulers, change institutions, change the distribution of
offices, multiply decrees, seize the machines, transfer the wealth, hurry
here, hurry there; movement itself is deliverance."

And when anyone says, "Before increasing the speed, let us
ask whither we are going; before rearranging the burdens, let
us ask why one man burdens another; before organizing new
violence, let us ask whether violence can establish brotherhood," both
parties denounce him.

The defenders of the old order call him a destroyer.
The makers of the new order call him a reactionary.
The first say that he undermines obedience; the second that
he impedes action. Neither asks whether what he says is
true.

I have been told: "The workers suffer; therefore we must
fight the owners."

And I ask: "By what means?"

"By compulsion."

"And when the workers possess the power of compulsion, will
compulsion cease to be evil?"

I have been told: "Nations threaten us; therefore we must
prepare war."

"And why do the nations threaten one another?"

"Because each fears the other's preparation."

"Then will greater preparation remove the fear?"

At this the practical men grow impatient. They say that
the questions are abstract, that circumstances are urgent, that there
is no time to consider first principles. Yet for centuries
there has been time to conscript, tax, imprison, manufacture weapons,
suppress revolt, organize revolt, destroy cities, rebuild them, and prepare
to destroy them again; there has been time for everything
except to pause and consider whether the road itself is
false.

What, then, are the reliable signs?

They are not hidden. They do not belong to one
party, nation, profession, or century. They are those truths which
the conscience recognizes before interest teaches it to evade them:
that every man values his life as I value mine;
that I must not demand from another what I would
refuse to do myself; that no title can make murder
good; that the misery of my brother cannot be a
proper foundation for my comfort; that truth requires no prison
to defend it; that love unites, while hatred, even when
clothed in patriotic, revolutionary, judicial, or scientific language, divides and
destroys.

These truths are called too simple. But the compass is
simple. The north star is simple. Bread is simple. A
root is simple. Men reject them, not because they are
obscure, but because their acceptance would require the unloading of
the carts—the renunciation of privilege, vengeance, national pride, profitable falsehood,
and the delicious conviction that all evil is committed by
one's opponents.

It is said that we cannot pause, because children are
hungry, armies are gathering, workers are embittered, and governments are
afraid.

For that very reason we must pause—not cease feeding, helping,
speaking, or laboring, but cease that blind activity which increases
hunger, armies, bitterness, and fear. To pause does not mean
to fold the hands. It means to withdraw the hands
from what conscience condemns. It means that the soldier refuses
to kill, the judge refuses an unjust sentence, the merchant
refuses deceit, the scholar refuses to call uselessness wisdom, the
artist refuses to adorn corruption, the worker refuses hatred, and
the wealthy man refuses to live upon another's want.

This labor is more difficult than joining a procession, obeying
a commander, shouting a slogan, or casting a vote for
a newly proffered guide. It begins where no multitude applauds—in
the solitary conscience. Therefore men stigmatize it as idleness. They
suppose that action consists only in moving bodies, issuing orders,
transferring property, and spilling blood. But the mightiest action is
often the refusal to perform what all men praise and
what one knows to be wrong.

The travelers continue shouting in the bog. One company strains
eastward, another dashes westward, and each points to the agitation
of the other as proof that greater agitation is requisite.
The man in the tree still calls:

"Pause and consider. Determine where you are. Look to the
signs."

"He is a fool!" answer those who sink.

"Push forward!" cry those who lead them.

And this is the calamity of men: not that truth
has never been given them, nor that they are incapable
of understanding it, but that they have made customs of
their errors, trades of their counterfeits, and honors of their
wandering; and therefore they persecute whoever distinguishes the root from
the stem, nourishment from its label, and the true road
from mere movement.

Yet the root remains beneath the scythe, the hunger beneath
the banquet, and the bog beneath the hurrying feet.

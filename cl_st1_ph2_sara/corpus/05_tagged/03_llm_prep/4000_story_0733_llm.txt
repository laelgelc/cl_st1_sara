# Old Ironsides

In the year 1833 an unpleasant rumor traveled through the
seaports of our country. The old frigate Constitution, it was
said, had grown useless. Her timbers were decayed, her fittings
out of date, and the government intended either to sell
her or to break her up.

Break up the Constitution!

Men who had watched her victories could scarcely believe it.
Yet many noble ships had already come to such an
end. Their masts were taken out, their cannon removed, and
their stout planks sold for firewood. Was this to be
the fate of the vessel that had humbled pirates, escaped
a British squadron, and carried the American flag through some
of the fiercest battles in our early history?

A young poet named Oliver Wendell Holmes read the report.
His indignation rose to a white heat. He seized his
pen and wrote the ringing verses beginning:

"Ay, tear her tattered ensign down!
Long has it waved on high."

The poem flew from newspaper to newspaper. It was copied
throughout the land. Schoolboys declaimed it; old sailors repeated it
with tears in their eyes; merchants, mechanics, farmers, and statesmen
joined in protest. If the gallant ship must perish, cried
the poet, let her not be destroyed by workmen's axes.
Better that she should go down beneath her flag, amid
the thunder of her guns!

Public feeling swept everything before it. Congress heard the demand,
and the Constitution was preserved and rebuilt.

But why did the people care so much for an
aging wooden frigate? Why should a poem about her stir
the whole country?

To answer that question we must go back to the
troubled years following the American Revolution.

Our young republic had won its independence, but it had
not yet won respect upon the sea. American merchant vessels
went bravely out to every quarter of the world, carrying
grain, timber, fish, tobacco, and manufactured goods. They brought home
sugar, coffee, spices, silks, and many other treasures of foreign
commerce. But they sailed with little protection.

Foreign cruisers stopped them. Their cargoes were seized. Their sailors
were sometimes carried off and forced into alien service. Along
the North African coast, the Barbary powers sent out swift
pirate ships whose crews captured merchantmen and held Christian sailors
for ransom. The governments of Europe often paid tribute to
these sea robbers. America, poor and weak, was expected to
do the same.

There were many citizens who cried, "We need no navy!
A navy costs too much."

But events supplied the answer. Without warships, our flag could
not protect our trade. Without trade, the new nation could
not prosper. So in 1794 Congress authorized the building of
six frigates. They were to be no ordinary vessels. They
must be swift enough to escape from ships stronger than
themselves and powerful enough to defeat ships of their own
class.

One of them was the Constitution.

She was built at Boston under the direction of skilled
shipwrights, from plans prepared by Joshua Humphreys. The finest materials
were chosen. Her principal timbers were of live oak from
the southern forests, a wood so dense and tough that
cannon shot had difficulty piercing it. Great care went into
every knee, beam, plank, and fastening. Paul Revere furnished copper
bolts and spikes, and later rolled copper for the sheathing
of her hull.

When launched in 1797, the Constitution was a magnificent sight.
She measured more than two hundred feet from the end
of her bowsprit to the stern. Her tall masts carried
clouds of canvas. Her black sides were pierced for heavy
guns, and a white stripe marked her gun deck. She
was rated as a forty-four-gun frigate, though at various times
she carried more than that number.

She looked strong because she was strong. Yet she was
also graceful and fast. There was life in her long
hull. Once the wind filled her sails, she seemed eager
to run.

Her first duties were against French armed vessels in the
West Indies, during the undeclared naval conflict with France. But
her first great service came in the Mediterranean.

The Barbary rulers had gone too far. The bashaw of
Tripoli declared war upon the United States, and his corsairs
hunted American ships. At first our operations against them brought
mixed fortune. The frigate Philadelphia ran aground and was captured.
Her officers and men were imprisoned, while the enemy prepared
to use the ship against us.

Then Lieutenant Stephen Decatur entered Tripoli harbor by night in
a small vessel, boarded the Philadelphia under the guns of
the forts, and burned her. It was a deed that
made all Europe marvel.

The Constitution, meanwhile, became the flagship of Commodore Edward Preble,
a stern, energetic officer who trained his young captains in
a hard school. He demanded obedience, courage, and exact seamanship.
The officers who served under him became known as "Preble's
boys," though there was little that was boyish in the
manner in which they fought.

In the summer of 1804 the Constitution led the American
squadron against Tripoli. The harbor was guarded by forts, gunboats,
and batteries. Ahead lay shoals and dangerous water. But Preble
would not be frightened away.

The signal flags climbed the flagship's rigging.

Everybody hurries to his place. Powder boys run between magazine
and gun deck. Sailors strip to the waist beside their
cannon. Officers study the shore through their glasses. Then the
order comes.

"Stand in!"

The little American gunboats pull toward the enemy. Their crews
board the Tripolitan craft and fight hand to hand with
pike, pistol, cutlass, and clubbed musket. Behind them moves the
Constitution, majestic amid the smoke.

The shore batteries open. Shot plows the water about her.
A ball tears through a sail; another cuts a rope;
splinters leap from the bulwarks. Still the stanch ship goes
on. She turns her broadside toward the town and pours
in her fire. The gun deck flashes from end to
end. The roar rolls against the African hills.

Again and again the Americans attacked. Tripoli was not conquered
in a day, but the courage of our officers and
sailor boys taught the Barbary powers that the United States
could no longer be insulted without danger. In time peace
was secured, and the Constitution returned home with honor.

Greater trials were yet before her.

For years Great Britain and France had been locked in
a tremendous struggle. Each tried to prevent neutral nations from
trading with the other. American ships suffered from both, but
British impressment aroused particular anger. British officers stopped our vessels
and carried away seamen, claiming that they were deserters from
His Majesty's service. Many were Americans.

At last, in June 1812, the United States declared war
upon Great Britain.

It seemed a daring act. Great Britain possessed the most
powerful navy in the world—hundreds of warships manned by officers
and crews who had fought through years of conflict. The
American navy had only a few frigates and smaller vessels.

What could our little fleet do?

It could fight.

In July the Constitution sailed from Chesapeake Bay under Captain
Isaac Hull. Hull was broad, good-humored, and much loved by
his men, but no one should mistake his pleasant manner
for weakness. He was a superb seaman and a determined
fighter.

Off the coast of New Jersey his lookouts sighted sails.
At first Hull hoped they belonged to an American squadron.
Morning revealed the truth. They were British warships—five of them—and
they were hunting the Constitution.

The wind died almost entirely.

This was grave danger. If the British frigates came alongside
together, the Constitution must be captured or destroyed. Hull could
not fight a whole squadron. He must get away.

But how?

The sea lay smooth beneath the summer heat. The sails
hung useless. Now and then a faint breath stirred them,
but not enough to carry the ship beyond her enemies.
The British put out boats to tow their leading frigate.
Slowly she crept nearer.

Hull ordered out his own boats. The men bent to
their oars, towing the heavy Constitution. Yet the enemy continued
to gain.

Then an officer suggested kedging.

A kedge was a small anchor. It could be carried
ahead in a boat and dropped. The crew aboard ship
then hauled upon the line, drawing the vessel toward the
anchor. While one anchor was being pulled in, another could
be carried forward.

At once the work began. Sailors rowed ahead under the
burning sun, dropped the kedge, and hurried back. Men at
the capstan tramped round and round, hauling the great frigate
through the still water. Then the process was repeated.

The British understood the trick and began kedging too.

All day long and through the night the strange race
continued. No broadsides thundered. There was only the creak of
capstan bars, the splash of oars, the calls of officers,
and the heavy breathing of exhausted men. The Constitution's boats
pulled. Her crew hauled. Every foot mattered.

Water casks were emptied overboard. Spare anchors and other weights
were shifted. Sails were dampened so that they might catch
the least air. Hull himself watched every cat's-paw that darkened
the surface of the sea.

A puff comes.

"Trim sail!"

The canvas shakes, fills, and draws. The Constitution moves ahead.
The breath dies, and once more the boats and kedges
are employed.

For nearly three days the chase went on. At last
a favorable squall approached. Hull prepared with care, furling some
sails so that the British might not guess what he
intended. When the squall struck, the hidden canvas burst out.
Rain swept over the ship. The Constitution rushed ahead beneath
the dark cloud.

When the weather cleared, she had gained precious distance. Soon
the British abandoned the chase.

Our frigate had escaped through seamanship, discipline, and tireless labor.
Yet Isaac Hull had not taken her to sea merely
to run away.

On August 19, south of the Gulf of St. Lawrence,
a sail was sighted. It proved to be the British
frigate Guerrière, Captain James Dacres commanding. The Guerrière had long
been known as a powerful cruiser. She was looking for
an American opponent. Now she had found one.

The ships cleared for action. Sand was scattered upon the
decks to keep the men from slipping. Buckets of water
stood ready against fire. Surgeons arranged their instruments below. The
guns were loaded, tackles cast loose, and matches lighted.

The Guerrière opened at long range. Her shot fell short
or passed through the Constitution's rigging. Hull held his fire.
He meant to come close, where every American ball would
tell.

The sailors waited beside their guns.

"Shall we fire, sir?" an officer asked.

"Not yet."

The enemy's cannon spoke again. One of her balls struck
the Constitution's side and bounded away. A sailor, seeing it
fall into the sea, shouted:

"Huzza! Her sides are made of iron!"

The men took up the cry. Old Ironsides! The name
was born in battle.

Hull came nearer. Nearer still. The British could see the
faces at our gun ports.

"Now, boys," cried Hull, "pour it into her!"

The Constitution's broadside crashed out.

The effect was terrible. Shot smashed through the Guerrière's hull,
tore her rigging, and struck down men at the guns.
Smoke covered both ships. Flames leaped through it. Marines fired
from the tops. Officers shouted commands that could scarcely be
heard above the thunder.

The Guerrière answered gallantly. Cannonballs came aboard the Constitution, cutting
ropes and showering splinters. But her massive live-oak sides endured
the punishment.

Hull paced the quarter-deck, excited by the fight. In a
moment of great exertion, he bent suddenly—and split his tight
breeches. A sailor who saw the mishap could not resist
a grin, even under fire. Hull sent below for another
pair and fought on with no loss of dignity.

The two ships drew together. The Guerrière's bowsprit became entangled
with the Constitution's mizzen rigging. Boarders were called. Men rushed
forward with cutlasses. But the sea rolled the ships apart
before either crew could cross.

Then the Guerrière's mizzenmast went over the side. Soon her
foremast and mainmast followed. She lay helpless, a dismasted wreck
rolling in the waves.

The Constitution drew off to repair her rigging. Then she
returned.

The British flag came down.

Captain Dacres was brought aboard to surrender his sword. Hull
looked at the wounded ship and the gallant officer before
him.

"No, sir," he said. "I will not take a sword
from one who knows so well how to use it."

The victory astonished the world. The battle had lasted only
a short time, but it shattered the belief that British
frigates could not be beaten. When the Constitution returned to
Boston, boats crowded the harbor, cannon boomed, bells rang, and
cheering citizens welcomed Hull and his crew.

Old Ironsides had won her immortal name.

Her next captain was William Bainbridge, a tall, grave officer
who had known both hardship and humiliation. Years before, he
had been captain of the captured Philadelphia and had suffered
imprisonment in Tripoli. Now fortune gave him an opportunity to
win fresh honor.

Late in 1812 the Constitution cruised off the coast of
Brazil. On December 29 she met the British frigate Java.

The Java was swift and well handled. She carried experienced
officers and a large crew, including soldiers and passengers bound
for the East Indies. Her captain, Henry Lambert, promptly accepted
battle.

The ships maneuvered for position. The Java tried to cross
the Constitution's stern, from which she could pour a raking
fire down the whole length of the deck. Bainbridge prevented
it. The frigates circled and fired, their white sails standing
above banks of powder smoke.

Early in the action Bainbridge was struck in the hip
by a musket ball. He remained on deck. A little
later a splinter or shot wounded him again. Blood ran
into his shoe, but he would not leave his post.

"Keep her close," he ordered.

The Java came up fiercely. Her bowsprit struck the Constitution's
rigging, and for a moment the ships were locked. British
boarders gathered, but American guns swept the enemy's deck. The
vessels separated.

Old Ironsides had lost her wheel. Bainbridge did not permit
this to throw his ship into confusion. Men went below
and steered by tackles attached to the rudder, passing the
word from the quarter-deck.

The Constitution's gunnery was deliberate and destructive. One by one
the Java's spars crashed down. Her captain was mortally wounded.
Her decks were torn, her crew terribly punished, and at
last she struck her colors.

Captain Lambert's sword came aboard with the surrender. When Bainbridge
learned that the British commander was dying, he returned it
as a mark of respect. Courage honored courage.

The Java could not be saved. Her shattered hull was
burned, and the Constitution sailed for home. Again the nation
rejoiced. Two powerful British frigates had met Old Ironsides, and
both had fallen.

Yet the old ship's fighting story was not finished.

In 1815, near the close of the war, she sailed
under Captain Charles Stewart, a cool and skillful commander. Off
the Madeira Islands, Stewart sighted two British vessels, the Cyane
and the Levant. Together they carried more guns and men
than either one alone, and they hoped by cooperation to
overpower the American frigate.

Night came on, and moonlight shone fitfully through clouds. Stewart
understood that he must prevent his enemies from using their
combined strength. His orders were quick and exact.

The Constitution passed between the two vessels and fired from
both sides—one battery against the Cyane, the other against the
Levant. Smoke shut the ships from one another. Stewart changed
position within the cloud, appearing where the British least expected
him.

The Cyane was badly cut up and struck her colors.
Stewart sent a prize crew aboard, then turned after the
Levant. That vessel had withdrawn but now came back, hoping
to assist her consort. Instead she found herself alone against
Old Ironsides.

There was another sharp action. The Constitution gained the better
position and poured in a destructive fire. The Levant also
surrendered.

Two enemies had been defeated in one battle! It was
a triumph not merely of heavy guns and stanch timbers,
but of superb seamanship. Stewart had handled his frigate like
a swordsman using a trusted blade.

The prizes were taken toward the Cape Verde Islands. At
Porto Praya a powerful British squadron suddenly appeared. Stewart cut
his cables and escaped with the Constitution and the Cyane,
though the Levant was recaptured. Once more the old frigate
had slipped from a superior force.

This was her final battle. Her record in the War
of 1812 was extraordinary. She had escaped when escape was
the only wise course, fought when battle offered, and conquered
every enemy that met her ship to ship. Her victories
gave courage to a nation facing one of the greatest
maritime powers on earth. They showed that American ships, American
officers, and American sailor boys deserved the respect of the
world.

Afterward the Constitution continued to carry our flag. She cruised
in the Mediterranean, the Atlantic, and the Pacific. She bore
ministers abroad, protected commerce, and served wherever the country sent
her. But steam was changing naval warfare. Iron took the
place of oak; engines replaced spreading canvas; shell guns made
the old wooden walls dangerously vulnerable.

She could no longer remain a first-class fighting ship.

For a time she became a schoolship, and young midshipmen
learned their profession upon the decks where Hull, Bainbridge, and
Stewart had stood. Later she served as a receiving ship,
housing recruits before they were assigned elsewhere. Her tall masts
were shortened or removed, shelters were built upon her deck,
and there were sad years when little of her former
beauty could be seen.

Again and again decay threatened her. Again and again the
people remembered.

Money was raised. Congress acted. Naval officers, schoolchildren, patriotic societies,
and private citizens insisted that Old Ironsides must not die.
She underwent repeated restorations. Rotten timbers were removed, but sound
pieces were preserved wherever possible. Masts rose once more above
her hull. Rigging spread in a noble web. Gun ports
opened along the familiar black-and-white sides.

She was not kept because she remained useful in war.
A modern shell could have destroyed her. She was kept
because usefulness is not the only measure of value.

Walk upon her deck and think of the men who
served there. Think of Preble watching the batteries of Tripoli
through the smoke. Think of Hull scanning a breathless sea
while British ships crept nearer. Hear the capstan creak as
exhausted sailors kedge the frigate to safety. Hear the cry
when a cannonball bounds from the oak:

"Her sides are made of iron!"

See Bainbridge, twice wounded, refusing to leave the quarter-deck. See
Stewart turning between two enemies beneath the moon. Remember also
the nameless seamen—the gun captains, topmen, powder boys, carpenters, surgeons,
marines, and cooks—whose discipline made every victory possible.

The Constitution belonged to all of them. In time she
came to belong to all of us.

She survived the axes that threatened her in 1833. She
survived changing fashions, neglect, decay, and the passing of the
navy for which she had been built. The nation that
launched her was small, uncertain, and often scorned. The nation
that preserved her had grown powerful. Yet the lesson of
the old frigate remained the same.

Strength without skill is not enough. Courage without discipline is
not enough. A ship, like a country, is saved by
foresight, labor, loyalty, and the willingness of ordinary people to
stand firm in an hour of danger.

Today Old Ironsides rests in honored preservation, still a commissioned
ship of the United States Navy. Her ensign rises over
decks worn smooth by generations. Visitors tread softly beside her
cannon and gaze upward at her towering masts. The harbor
around her has changed; the great wooden fleets have vanished;
steel warships cross oceans at speeds her builders could never
have imagined.

Still she endures.

Her live-oak walls preserve more than bolts and planks. They
preserve the memory of a young republic defending its rights,
of commanders who joined daring with humanity, and of sailor
boys who fought their guns amid smoke, splinters, and falling
spars. They preserve the thunder off Tripoli, the long silent
contest of the kedges, and the broadsides that brought down
the Guerrière's masts.

Oliver Wendell Holmes asked that her tattered ensign not be
torn down. The people answered him. They saved the stanch
old ship, and in saving her they kept a precious
memorial of our country's courage, honor, and birth as a
naval power.

That is why we remember the Constitution.

That is why we call her Old Ironsides.

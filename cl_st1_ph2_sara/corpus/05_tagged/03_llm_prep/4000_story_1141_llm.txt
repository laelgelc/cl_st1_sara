### The Girl Beyond the Plaster

If this were a New York story, the girl would
live in a studio overlooking Washington Square, and the young
man would be an assistant dramatic critic with a black
necktie and an income which nobody, least of all his
landlord, could understand.

But this is a Chicago story.

Therefore the girl lived in a boarding house on North
Clark Street, in a room overlooking the roof of a
laundry, and the young man sold plumbing fixtures. There was
nothing symbolic about the plumbing fixtures. Editors are earnestly requested
not to discover that the porcelain bathtub represented his soul.

The girl's name was Gertrude Schrader, though nobody had called
her Gertrude since the Beloit High School commencement exercises of
1908. She was Gertie, and she worked in the shirtwaist
department of Henshaw's Great Central Store, where the women clerks
wore black and the floorwalkers wore expressions indicating that they
had just swallowed a memorandum from the management.

Gertie was a looker.

That statement is made promptly because in stories about shopgirls
the question always arises, and there is no use making
the reader wait through two pages of weather. She had
brown hair, gray eyes, a straight little nose, and the
kind of color which beauty advertisements attribute to country air
and which city girls attempt to obtain from a box
costing sixty cents.

She also had good teeth.

Good teeth were important at Henshaw's, because a clerk was
expected to smile when a customer asked to see the
same shirtwaist in eight sizes, four colors, and a style
which had not been manufactured since Tuesday. Gertie could smile
until the muscles at the corners of her mouth felt
as if they had been starched.

"Personality sells goods," said Mr. Biddle, the buyer.

Mr. Biddle had no personality whatever, which perhaps explained his
faith in it.

Every morning Gertie rose at seven in a room that
had been carved from a larger room by means of
a partition no thicker than a respectable cracker. She heated
curling irons over a gas jet, brushed her hair, polished
her fingernails, rubbed cold cream from the end of her
nose, washed the cold cream from the washcloth, pressed her
collar, mended anything that had given way during the previous
day's struggle, and inspected her shoes for signs of moral
collapse.

Then she went downstairs and ate oatmeal in the dining
room beneath curtains that had once been white and now
matched the smoke of the Illinois Central.

The landlady, Mrs. Coggins, believed in nourishing food. Her boarders
believed she believed in it because she had never furnished
any.

On Monday there was creamed cabbage. On Tuesday the cabbage
appeared without its cream, like a lady who has taken
off her evening wrap. Wednesday brought hash, Thursday brought something
Mrs. Coggins called a fricassee, and Friday produced a bread
pudding of such architectural firmness that an ambitious boarder might
have rented rooms in it.

Gertie paid seven dollars a week for room and board.
She earned nine.

Here is the arithmetic of independence: seven from nine leaves
two, unless there are car fares, stockings, laundry, toothpaste, hairpins,
church envelopes, presents for the folks at Christmas, and the
occasional piece of Irish crochet which a girl buys because
all the other girls have one. In that case seven
from nine leaves a headache.

Gertie had come to Chicago two years earlier because Beloit
was "dead."

That was the word she had used.

"Dead, dead, dead," she told her mother. "Everybody knows when
you buy a new hat, and before you've worn it
twice they know what it cost."

In Chicago nobody knew what her hats cost. Nobody knew
she had hats.

At first the city thrilled her. Elevated trains roared like
iron dragons past lighted windows. Great department stores blazed with
silks, mirrors, perfumes, gloves, brass railings, and women who seemed
to have been born wearing fur. There were theatres, cafés,
lake steamers, illuminated signs, and enough men on State Street
to furnish husbands for every girl in Wisconsin.

That last was an error in municipal statistics.

The men were there, certainly. They were in doorways, streetcars,
restaurants, offices, and on corners. Some tipped their hats. Some
tipped nothing but their intentions. A girl alone learned to
distinguish, by the pressure of an elbow in a crowded
car, six varieties of masculine civilization.

Gertie learned to walk fast.

She also learned that city friendliness frequently came with supper
attached and a bill afterward. She learned that a young
woman might know twenty-seven clerks, sleep within six inches of
an unseen neighbor, eat among fourteen boarders, and still spend
Sunday afternoon so lonely that the ticking of her alarm
clock sounded like company.

By the second winter the dragons of the elevated had
become trains, the theatres had become prices in a newspaper,
and the lighted signs had become advertisements for things she
could not afford.

Still, she kept herself up.

Every beauty column agreed that a woman must keep herself
up. A beauty editor with a photograph showing shoulders like
a swan advised girls to brush their hair one hundred
strokes nightly. Another prescribed lemon for the hands, ice for
the eyes, walking for the figure, sleep for the complexion,
and cheerful thoughts for everything else.

Gertie attempted the full program.

She walked because car fares were five cents. She slept
when Mrs. Coggins's plumbing permitted it. Her thoughts, while not
always cheerful, were clean.

And she brushed her hair.

Up went the brush, down through the thick brown rope
of it. Up—down. Up—down. Fifty-six, fifty-seven, fifty-eight.

On the particular night with which this story finally becomes
concerned, Gertie had spent nine hours selling white waists to
women who wanted colors and colored waists to women who
wanted white. At five minutes before closing, a large woman
had returned a waist because her husband said it made
her look stout.

No retail clerk has ever devised an answer to that.

Gertie climbed three flights to her room, removed her shoes,
washed her face, put on a cotton kimono, and began
the hundred strokes.

Up—down.

Her arms ached.

Up—down.

The girl in the advertisement had hair that floated around
her like a golden mist. Gertie's hair caught in the
brush and yanked her scalp.

Up—down.

At seventy-three she stopped.

She looked into the narrow mirror over the bureau. A
streak of cold cream remained on one cheek. Beneath her
eyes were two faint shadows. Her mouth drooped from the
day's smiling. On the bureau stood a photograph of a
white house with a porch, a pear tree, and three
people squinting into Wisconsin sunshine.

Her father held his hat in both hands. Her mother
had one arm around the dog. Behind them was the
garden fence.

Gertie picked up the photograph.

That was foolish.

A homesick person should no more handle family photographs after
eleven at night than a hungry person should read a
restaurant bill of fare. Yet mankind persists in both practices,
and magazine writers profit thereby.

She thought of her room at home, with the blue
wallpaper. She thought of the kitchen stove ticking as it
cooled, her mother's geraniums, her father shaking ashes before breakfast,
and Mrs. Hummel next door calling across the fence to
ask who had died whenever the telephone rang.

She thought even of Elmer Voss.

Then she began to cry.

At first she cried carefully, because cold cream on a
kimono leaves a grease spot. But presently discipline failed. Tears
made small white rivers down her cheeks, met the cream
at her chin, and dropped upon the collar in a
mixture not recommended by beauty experts.

She sat on the edge of the bed and tried
not to make a sound.

The wall heard her.

So did the man beyond it.

There came three soft taps.

Gertie stopped crying.

Three more taps.

"Say," said a voice through the partition, "is somebody murdering
you, or is this private?"

Gertie stared at the wall.

The voice belonged to the room next door. She knew
it as one knows a clock or a streetcar bell.
It whistled while shaving, sang one line from a musical
comedy over and over, coughed at seven-fifteen, and occasionally said
fierce things to a collar button.

"I beg your pardon?" she said.

"I asked if you were being murdered. Because if you
are, I'll put on my pants and interfere. If you
aren't, I'd rather not. They're hanging behind the door."

Gertie gave a wet, unwilling laugh.

"There's nobody here."

"I know that," said the voice. "I've lived beside you
four months."

"Well!"

"I don't mean anything fresh. These walls tell all. I
know when you oversleep, when you drop a shoe, and
when you get a letter from home, because you hum
afterward."

Gertie looked indignantly at the partition, which remained structurally indifferent.

"You've got your nerve."

"Yes, ma'am. It's my largest asset. You've been crying eight
minutes. Cut out the weeps and come sit on the
front steps. I've got two ham sandwiches and half a
pie."

"What kind of pie?"

"Apple."

This question settled several moral difficulties. A strange man might
be dangerous, but a strange man with half an apple
pie was at least open to investigation.

"I'm not dressed."

"Neither am I, officially. Put on something respectable. I'll wait
in the hall."

"You will not!"

"Then I'll go downstairs first. Count forty."

She counted sixty-five.

When she opened her door, the hall was empty. Gertie
wore her dark skirt, the kimono under her jacket, and
her hair in one heavy braid. She had washed away
the cold cream, though her eyelids remained pink.

The front stoop was narrow and smelled faintly of wet
brick, coal smoke, and somebody's late onions. Across the street,
three windows burned in a factory building. A streetcar clanged
at the corner, passed, and left the night humming behind
it.

The man sat on the bottom step with a paper
parcel beside him.

He stood.

So this was the Kid Next Door.

He was perhaps twenty-seven, thin without being weak, with sandy
hair and an eager, crooked smile. He wore trousers, shoes
without socks, a shirt with the collar removed, and a
coat buttoned to conceal the absence of certain intermediate formalities.

"I'm dressed enough for sandwiches," he explained.

"So I see."

"My name's Augustus Pohl. Gus unless I owe you money."

"Gertie Schrader."

They shook hands solemnly.

"I thought you were taller," said Gus.

"How could you think anything about me?"

"Your shoes sound tall."

"That's the silliest thing I ever heard."

"It's midnight. A man's best thinking is over by ten."

He spread the parcel on the step. There were two
sandwiches wrapped in paper, the promised pie, and one spoon.

"What's the spoon for?" Gertie asked.

"In case the pie turns hostile."

They ate.

No moon shone over the city. No soft breeze stirred
scented boughs. There were no scented boughs within five blocks,
unless one counted the three discouraged shrubs in Mrs. Coggins's
front yard, and nobody could count them without being accused
of charity. The sky had the brownish glow of a
city refusing to go to sleep. Somewhere a freight engine
coughed like a giant with bronchitis.

"This is quiet," Gus said.

"Quiet!" Gertie waved her sandwich toward the elevated.

"I mean nobody's talking to us."

Gertie liked him for that.

She liked him again when he gave her the larger
piece of pie without making the sacrifice look noble. A
man who gives a woman the larger piece and does
not mention it has been overlooked by the writers of
etiquette books.

"What were you crying about?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"That's a big subject. Women cry about nothing and men
stay out late about nothing. Keeps the world moving."

"I was tired."

"Store?"

"How did you know?"

"You leave at eight-twenty in black, come back at six-forty
in a grouch, and Saturday night you count change."

"Henshaw's."

"Which department?"

"Shirtwaists."

He nodded gravely. "Plumbing fixtures."

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to. I travel for the Great Lakes
Sanitary Supply Company, except that 'travel' means I take a
streetcar to Evanston once a month. My specialty is bathtubs.
White, roll-rimmed, porcelain-enamelled. Any time you want a bathtub, wholesale,
I'm your man."

"I'll remember. I've been thinking of putting one beside my
bureau."

"Don't. Crowds a room."

Gertie laughed again. It had been a long while since
laughter arrived without her first summoning it for a customer.

"Where are you from?" she asked.

"Waupun."

"Wisconsin?"

"Is there another? Population full at nine o'clock and asleep
at nine-five. I came here five years ago to make
my fortune."

"Have you made it?"

"I expect it Tuesday."

"That's how I came. To make mine."

"Where from?"

"Beloit."

He looked at her with sudden interest. "Sure enough? Then
you know what a real winter is."

"And a real summer, and gardens, and people who speak
to you without trying to sell you something."

"Or marry you," Gus said.

"Sometimes they try that, too."

The joke fell between them and lay there quietly.

Gus picked crumbs from the paper.

He told her about Waupun: his father's hardware store, the
creek where he had caught bullheads, the red barn behind
the Lutheran church, and the room he had shared with
two brothers until he grew so eager for privacy that
Chicago seemed like heaven.

"What did you find?" Gertie asked.

"A hall bedroom with company breathing on both sides."

"I'm sorry if I breathe too loud."

"Yours is all right. The gink on the other side
snores like a detective story. First there's the mystery, then
the chase, then an explosion."

He told her how he had arrived with forty-three dollars
and a brown suit his mother considered stylish. He had
expected to become sales manager before the suit wore shiny.
Now he earned twenty-one dollars a week, sent three home,
paid eight for board, and spent the rest proving to
Chicago that he was not poor.

"The city's like a club," he said. "You keep paying
dues, but nobody introduces you."

Gertie looked up.

"That's it exactly."

"Sure it is."

"It makes you lonesome in layers," she said. "Plain lonesome
underneath, Sunday lonesome over that, and on top a hard
city kind with steel buttons. Three-ply and rainproof."

"Say, that's good."

"It isn't good when you have it."

"No."

For a moment neither joked.

The street had emptied. Far down the block a policeman's
heels struck the pavement with the patient rhythm of public
order. A light went out in Mrs. Coggins's parlor, though
how any light had remained there after midnight was a
question for theology.

Gertie told Gus about the store.

She told him how Miss Delaney had been dismissed for
sitting down when there were no customers; how the girls
were expected to wear fresh collars even when they were
saving for overshoes; how Mr. Biddle could detect a run
in a stocking at forty feet; and how women who
spent forty dollars on clothes complained if a clerk's hands
looked rough.

"They tell us to look as if we don't need
the job," she said, "or we lose the job."

"That's business."

"That's crazy."

"Same thing, mostly."

Gertie liked him for that, too.

She told him about Beloit. She described the white house
in the photograph, the porch screened in summer, the lilac
bushes, the patch where her mother grew beans, and the
hammock her father fastened every May between the maple trees.

"And everybody knows everybody," she said.

"You hated that before."

"How do you know?"

"Because I did."

She admitted it. In Beloit, everybody had known when she
walked twice with Elmer Voss. Everybody had known when she
stopped walking with him. By the next Thursday, three women
had asked her mother whether Gertie was too proud for
a steady young man.

"Was he?" Gus asked.

"Was he what?"

"Steady."

"Like a table."

"Handsome?"

"No."

"Rich?"

"He'll have the feed business when his uncle retires."

"Does he talk?"

"Certainly."

"What about?"

"Orders. Weather. The choir picnic."

Gus whistled under his breath. "A regular Arabian Nights."

"I thought he was dull."

"You thought right."

She turned on him. "He's dependable."

"Oh, that's different. A dependable bore is one of the
foundations of civilization."

"He never got fresh. Never once."

"Maybe he was saving it."

"He asked me to marry him."

Gus stopped smiling.

"What did you say?"

"I said I was going to Chicago."

"And now?"

She folded the sandwich paper into a small square, then
folded it again.

"Now I'm going home."

Gus leaned back against the railing. "For a visit?"

"For good. I gave notice today."

The city continued its various noises, but the stoop had
become very still.

"When?"

"Saturday."

"That's why you were crying."

"Partly. I'm glad to go. Only—well, you can be glad
and sorry together. Women can. It saves time."

Gus rubbed his hands over his knees.

Then, because he was young, lonely, tired, decent, foolish, and
sitting at one in the morning beside a pretty girl
who understood the exact price of city ambition, he did
what popular fiction had been urging young men to do
for years.

He proposed.

Not elegantly. Elegance requires preparation and usually a better collar.

"Listen, Gertie," he said. "Don't go."

She looked at him.

"I mean it. We're both working. We get along. We
could take a little flat—three rooms, maybe, with a gas
range. Your money and mine together would beat this place.
I could get breakfast. I make dandy coffee. We'd have
a table of our own and curtains that weren't smoked
black and nobody serving cabbage in disguise."

"Gus—"

"There's a flat over on Sheffield I saw last week.
Thirty-two dollars. Murphy bed in the front room, but who
cares? There's a pantry. A real pantry."

He took her hand.

Her fingers were cold. His were warm and rather bony.

"We're both lonely," he hurried on. "Maybe that isn't what
fellows in novels say, but it's honest. I like you.
I liked you before I saw you. I liked the
way you laughed through the wall, and how you always
turn off the gas twice to make sure. You've got
sense. You're pretty. We'd have fun. We could save. Maybe
I'd get the Wisconsin territory and you could quit the
store after a while. Say yes."

A magazine heroine would have lowered her lashes here. Gertie's
lashes were perfectly good lashes, but she used her eyes
instead.

"You don't know me."

"I know enough."

"You know how my shoes sound."

"That's a start."

"And we've been talking one hour."

"It's the best hour I've had in Chicago."

That nearly did it.

For one swift instant Gertie saw the three-room flat. She
saw checked curtains, a blue coffee pot, Gus coming home
with his sample case, herself waiting in a clean apron.
She saw two chairs drawn together beneath a lamp. The
picture was warm, compact, and dangerously easy.

Then she saw herself at Henshaw's the next morning, smiling
with aching cheeks. She saw bills on the checked table.
She saw two homesick people trying to build a home
merely because they had met beside a thin wall and
shared a pie.

She withdrew her hand gently.

"You're a good fellow, Gus."

"That sounds fatal."

"It is."

"You're going back to the feed merchant."

"Yes."

"You love him?"

She considered this with the seriousness it deserved.

"I trust him. I know his mother, and he knows
mine. I know how he acts when he's angry and
how he acts when he has a cold. I know
he'll come home when he says he will. There's a
house next to his uncle's with room for a garden."

"A garden."

"Yes."

"And a porch, I suppose."

"Yes."

"Lilacs?"

"If I plant them."

Gus stood up.

The proposal vanished from his face, leaving behind a young
salesman in an uncollared shirt at an unfortunate hour.

"Well," he said, "that's settled."

"Don't be sore."

"I'm not sore."

"You are, too."

"I've got a big day tomorrow, that's all. Inventory sheets.
New line of low-tank closets. The romantic life."

"Gus—"

"No harm done. You're right. Fellow has to be crazy
to propose to a girl before breakfast."

He gathered the paper, missed a crumb, picked it up,
and put everything neatly into the parcel.

Gertie rose.

"I'm glad you knocked," she said.

"Sure."

"I mean it."

"Sure."

At the top of the stairs they paused before their
two doors, six feet apart in the hall and six
inches apart inside their rooms.

"Good night, Gus."

"Good night, Gertie."

She entered her room and closed the door. A moment
later his door shut.

The wall stood between them again, cheap, thin, and efficient.

Gertie sat on the bed, holding the family photograph. She
did not cry. Beyond the partition she heard Gus remove
his shoes, drop one, mutter at it, and wind his
alarm clock.

Then his voice came through the plaster, brisk and businesslike.

"Got to sleep. We're running a sale on bathtubs."

Gertie smiled in the dark.

After a little while the building settled, the pipes knocked,
a train went by, and the two small rooms became
quiet—as quiet, that is, as loneliness ever permits in a
city.

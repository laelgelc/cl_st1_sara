### White When He's Wanted

Buckalong was a station of such dimensions that a man
might ride out on Monday with a clean shirt and
return on Saturday without having reached the fence he was
sent to mend. There were two hundred thousand acres in
the head-station paddock, according to Sandy M'Gregor, and three hundred
thousand according to the stockmen, who had ridden over it
and therefore knew less. The cattle were numbered in tens
of thousands, the sheep in mobs, and the employees chiefly
by the gaps they left.

In those days Buckalong was worked by Sandy, six stockmen,
three boundary riders, a cook who was always leaving, and
ourselves—four jackaroos who got nothing a year and earned it.

Sandy M'Gregor managed the place with economy, suspicion, and a
voice which could turn a quiet horse in the drafting-yard.
He had a red beard, iron spectacles, and a dislike
of drovers, swagmen, horse-dealers, travellers, and anybody else who arrived
at meal-times. Horse-dealers he regarded as a special punishment sent
into the back-block to test the honesty of managers.

"A mon that sells a horrse," he used to say,
"kens something aboot the beast that the buyer doesna. If
he didna, he'd keep it. There's the philosophy o' the
horrse trade, and ye may put it in your pipes."

We had no pipes, being unpaid, but accepted the philosophy.

One November afternoon a drover came up the track leading
a pony and carrying his swag across the saddle. He
was a tall man reduced by illness to the thickness
of a stockwhip. A scar ran from his right temple
into the beard, and his skin had the yellow-grey colouring
of a man who had camped too long with fever.
The pony looked little better. He was a flea-bitten grey,
though so stained by mud, sweat, and neglect that his
colour was a matter for argument. His ribs stood out,
his hips were sharp, and he carried his head as
if the weight of his own ears wearied him.

Sandy watched the pair approach.

"Here comes bankruptcy riding starvation," he said. "And he'll be
wanting to sell me the bankruptcy."

The drover got down slowly. He had the true bushman's
objection to using three words where one would leave a
reasonable doubt.

"Manager?" he asked.

"Aye."

"Want a horse?"

"No."

The drover appeared to consider this complete conversation. He took
off the saddle, set his swag on the verandah, and
waited.

Sandy glared at him.

"Did ye no hear me? I said no. I've horrses
standing idle now that are eating their heads off, and
better heads than yon brute's. What's wrong wi' him?"

"Nothing."

"Then why sell him?"

"Need the money."

"That's wrong wi' you, no the horrse."

The drover leaned against a post. We saw then that
he was shivering, though the heat had laid the fowls
under the pepper-trees with their wings spread.

"Where from?" Sandy asked.

"Gulf."

"Fever?"

"Yes."

At that one word Sandy's voice altered. Years before, he
had had Gulf fever himself and had been carried for
sixty miles in a dray, cursing every rut when strong
enough and the driver when not. He came down the
verandah steps and looked closely at the man.

"Have ye eaten?"

"Yesterday."

Sandy turned on us as though we had deliberately starved
the visitor.

"Ye four ornamental objects, standin' there like galahs on a
rail! Tell the cook to put meat on. And tea.
Strong tea. No the dishwater he sends me."

The drover laid a hand on the pony's neck.

"Fifteen pounds," he said.

Sandy walked round the animal. He lifted each foot, examined
the mouth, ran a hand down the legs, and stood
off to inspect the general ruin.

"Fifteen! I could buy a better horrse frae a hawker,
and a set o' saucepans wi' him. He's narrow in
the chest, slack behind, rough in the coat, and old
enough to remember the flood."

"Eight."

Sandy frowned.

"If ye're going home, ye'll no get there on eight
pounds."

The drover said nothing.

"Where's home?"

"Wagga way."

"And the fare?"

"About twelve."

Sandy looked again at the fever in the man's face,
but pretended to be looking at the horse.

"I'll give ye fifteen," he said angrily. "Not for the
horrse, mind. The horrse is worth thirty shillings, and dear
at that. I'm buying him because the station can stand
the loss better than you can."

The drover counted the notes twice, not from distrust but
because his fingers trembled. Then he put them inside his
shirt. We brought him beef and bread, and he ate
a little, though Sandy bullied him over every mouthful.

"You'll camp in the spare room," said Sandy. "And if
ye die in it I'll charge your estate for the
sheets."

Next morning the mailman took him toward the railway. Before
he climbed to the box-seat, the drover went to the
grey, who was tied near the stable. For a while
he rubbed the pony between the eyes. The horse, who
had shown no affection for anyone, pushed his muzzle against
the man's chest.

"He's white when he's wanted," the drover said to Sandy.

"What's that?"

"White when he's wanted."

"Aye, and black when he's no, I suppose."

The drover mounted beside the mailman. As the coach moved
off, he looked back.

"White when he's wanted," he called again.

That was his farewell.

We put the grey in the horse-paddock and called him
Whitey for want of imagination. For six weeks he did
nothing except recover his appetite. He ate with steady application
and escaped work with genius. If the night-horses were brought
in, Whitey was behind a log. If the stockyard gate
was left open, he was through it. When caught, he
allowed the saddle to be put on with the resignation
of a martyr and moved away at a pace that
gave a man time to reconsider his destination.

He would not trot unless the road sloped downhill, and
even then he preferred to stumble. A stockman named Billy
Crow rode him after cattle and came home at dark,
leading Whitey and using language that caused the cook to
cross himself, though he was a Presbyterian.

"As useful as side-pockets to a pig," Billy reported.

Sandy defended the horse because he had bought him.

"There's nothing wrong wi' the beast that feed and work
will no cure."

"He's had the feed," said Billy.

"Then give him the work."

We gave him work. He received it without enthusiasm and
returned as little as possible. He became a camp-horse, carrying
packs, rations, hobbles, and once a sick calf. If a
mob walked, he walked. If it trotted, he walked more
urgently. If it galloped, he waited for it to come
back.

But he never tired. After forty miles he looked as
disreputable as he had at the first mile and no
worse. He never got a sore back, never pulled a
shoe, never refused water, and could live on country that
would disappoint a goat. These virtues, being unobtrusive, did not
raise him in our estimation. A station horse is judged
by what he does in excitement, not by his capacity
for enduring boredom.

The chance came in autumn.

A mob of brumbies had appeared in the ranges beyond
Cattle Creek—twelve mares, some young stuff, and a black stallion
with a tail like a flag. They had broken into
the horse-paddock twice, taken three station mares with them, and
treated our fences as if wire had been invented for
their amusement. Sandy decided they must be run in.

Every man at Buckalong wanted the black stallion. Billy said
he would make a racer. Thompson the head stockman said
he would make a stock-horse. We said he would probably
break Billy's neck, which was another recommendation.

The brumbies knew the ranges better than we did and
had become as cunning as policemen. For three mornings they
let us sight them and then vanished through gullies where
no sensible horse would put a foot. We wore down
our best mounts, tore our clothes in hop-scrub, and lost
one pack-bag, two bridles, and most of our dignity.

On the fourth day we had only tired horses left.

"Take the grey," said Sandy to young Alec, the lightest
of us.

Alec protested.

"Take him," Sandy repeated. "If he canna catch the brumbies,
he can carry ye home after the others are done."

We found the mob at sunrise on a long open
ridge. They stood with their heads up, the black stallion
between us and the mares. Then he wheeled, and the
whole mob streamed over the shoulder of the hill.

Our horses jumped after them.

For the first mile Whitey occupied his usual place at
the rear, travelling with his nose out and his ears
hanging sideways. Alec kicked, whipped, and addressed him personally. Whitey
ignored all three. The brumbies crossed a stony flat, took
a low rise, and headed for broken country. Billy and
Thompson were nearest; the rest of us followed in a
scattered line.

Then the black stallion gave a shrill call.

Whitey's ears came up.

It was the first sign we had seen that he
possessed any.

He lifted his head, shortened himself, and went. He did
not begin with a rush or waste time fighting the
bit. He merely lengthened, gathered speed, and passed us. He
passed the tired horses first, then the fresh ones; passed
Thompson on the bay and Billy on the chestnut as
though they were tied to trees. Alec, who had been
flogging him a moment before, now sat quiet and looked
frightened.

How he did open our eyes!

The brumbies dropped into a gully choked with saplings. Whitey
went down the bank like a kangaroo, changed feet among
the stones, and climbed the far side without losing a
stride. Across a flat he stretched till his belly seemed
close to the ground; through scrub he bent and twisted
as though he had chosen the track the day before.
The black stallion doubled back. Whitey doubled inside him. The
mares broke toward a stringybark ridge. Whitey swept round their
shoulder and turned them.

For two hours he worked that mob from hill to
hill, from range to range, over ironstone shelves, through gullies
and timber, across flats where the grass hid holes deep
enough to bury a dog. The other horses were blown
and dropping away. Whitey was wet to the ears, but
his stride stayed level. He seemed to know what the
brumbies meant before they knew it themselves. When they tried
the scrub, he was at their heads; when they split,
he drove the mares back; when the stallion challenged, Whitey
ran beside him and smothered him for pace.

At last Alec brought the mob toward a wing of
the boundary fence, where we had rigged a rough yard.
We came up by twos and threes, riding horses whose
tongues hung out. Whitey turned the brumbies at the opening,
held them there, and followed them into the yard.

Then he stopped.

His head dropped, his ears fell sideways, and he became
once more the oldest, ugliest, laziest pony on Buckalong.

Alec slid off and sat on the ground.

Billy stared at Whitey.

"Well," he said, "the fever man knew."

Sandy arrived an hour later, having wisely taken no part
in the chase. He inspected the captured brumbies, listened to
six accounts at once, and then walked over to Whitey.

"I told ye," he said. "There was never anything wrong
wi' the horrse."

This was not what he had told us, but argument
with a manager is merely insubordination with extra words.

After that, Whitey's reputation travelled faster than he did. Men
came from stations forty and fifty miles away to see
him. They looked at his coarse head, drooping quarters, and
scarred knees, and suspected us of inventing the story. Then
there would be a muster, a wild bullock, or a
horse that refused to be headed, and Whitey would wake.
He could turn a beast on ground where another horse
could scarcely stand. He could carry a man from dawn
to dark and finish with enough left to chase the
camp-horses. He knew when to save himself and when saving
was no longer the business.

Offers came. Sandy refused a fiver, ten pounds, twenty, and
at last fifty.

"Fifty pounds for a horrse I bought for fifteen," he
said. "What kind o' fool would sell at a profit
and spend the rest o' his life regretting it?"

We called the pony White-When-Wanted now, which was shortened to
Wanted in busy moments. Sandy pretended the name was foolish,
but used it himself.

Nearly two years after the drover's visit, Wanted disappeared.

There was no broken fence, no open gate, no track
that we could follow beyond the hard road. We searched
the paddocks, creeks, ranges, and abandoned huts. Boundary riders were
questioned. Police were informed. Sandy offered twenty pounds reward and
accused every horse-trader within a hundred miles.

Nothing came of it.

For a while we expected Wanted to walk in at
breakfast, looking sleepy and pleased with himself. Then weeks became
months. Other horses did his work badly. The black brumby
was broken, threw Billy twice, and was sold for thirty
shillings. Buckalong continued, as stations do, with one useful creature
missing and twenty useless ones eating.

About a year later a traveller from far beyond the
western runs stayed the night. Over tea he spoke of
a grey pony he had seen at a back-block camp
near the border of settled country—a rough-looking flea-bitten moke that
had run down a mob of scrubbers after three days'
work.

"Rider had a scar down his face," the traveller said.
"Thin chap. Said he bought the pony from Buckalong."

We looked at Sandy.

"Bought him?" said Billy. "From who?"

"Didn't say."

Sandy took off his spectacles and polished them for a
long time.

The police could do nothing. The place named by the
traveller lay beyond regular patrols, beyond telegraph, and nearly beyond
maps. A man might be found there if he wished
to be found, which the drover evidently did not.

Billy called it theft. Thompson called it gratitude badly expressed.
We waited for Sandy.

At last he put his spectacles on.

"The mon lied," he said. "He didna buy the horrse
from Buckalong."

That was all.

But we understood then, better than we had at the
time, why the drover had touched the pony's neck before
leaving, and why he had twice repeated the only praise
that mattered. Fever had forced him to sell his old
companion; health had brought him back to reclaim him. Whether
the fifteen notes had bought the horse, or merely held
him in trust, was a question for lawyers, and there
were none within several hundred miles.

We never saw the drover again, nor White-When-Wanted. Yet now
and then a story reached us from the outer country:
of a scarred man on a shabby grey who could
yard clean-skins no other pair could turn; of a little
horse that travelled three days without failing; of a drover
who refused fifty pounds and said only that the pony
was not for sale.

Perhaps some of the stories were lies. Most good bush
stories improve on the road.

But we believed them.

## I

A week before Christmas Gerasim returned to Moscow.

He was a tall, broad-shouldered peasant of twenty-five, with strong
hands and a quiet, good-natured face. During the summer he
had worked at a brickyard outside the city; in autumn
he had helped a merchant carry goods from the river.
When that work ended, he went home to his village.
But there was little for him there. His father was
dead, his mother lived with his married brother, and the
strip of land belonging to the family scarcely supplied them
with bread.

"You had better go back to Moscow," his brother told
him. "A strong fellow like you will not remain without
work."

Gerasim thought the same.

He put on his sheepskin coat, tied his spare shirt
in a bundle, received his mother's blessing, and betook himself
to the city. He had eleven rubles in his pocket
and expected to find a position within a few days.

But Moscow had no need of him.

Every morning, while it was still dark, he went out
to look for work. He visited warehouses, shops, inns, stables,
and building yards. Wherever a strong back might be required,
there he presented himself.

"Have you a place for a man of all work?"

"No."

"Perhaps you will need somebody after Christmas?"

"We have men enough."

At one establishment the steward looked him over and asked
whether he could read.

"A little, sir."

"Can you keep accounts?"

"No, sir."

"Then what use are you?"

At another place they promised to take him, but when
he returned the following day, the position had been given
to a porter's nephew. A butcher offered him work for
his food alone. Gerasim agreed even to that, but the
butcher changed his mind.

So the days passed.

At first Gerasim ate meat pies and drank tea in
the eating houses. Then he began to count every kopek.
He moved from a clean lodging to a cellar where
six labourers slept together. At last he bought only black
bread and kvas, and on some days he ate nothing
until evening.

Not a stone did he leave unturned. Yet everywhere it
was the same: no work, no place, no need of
him.

It disheartened him.

## II

Among the men from Gerasim's village who lived in Moscow
was a coachman named Yegor. He served the merchant Sharov,
a wealthy dealer in leather, and had been with him
for seven years.

Yegor was a stout, red-faced man, always well dressed. His
boots were polished, his beard combed, and on holidays he
wore a blue coat with silver buttons. He was proud
of his position and often said that the whole household
rested upon him.

"The master will not stir without me," he would boast.
"If Yegor says the road is bad, the master stays
at home. If Yegor says a man is dishonest, that
man is sent away. I know how to serve—not like
some who merely eat their master's bread."

Gerasim did not much like to ask him for assistance.
Still, hunger is a bad counsellor to pride. One evening,
after another fruitless day, he went to Sharov's house and
waited near the stable.

Yegor came out carrying a lantern.

"Who is there?"

"It is I, Yegor Danilych—Gerasim."

"Ah, my boy! What wind blew you here?"

Gerasim explained his circumstances. He spoke simply, but when he
mentioned that his last three kopeks had gone for bread
that morning, his voice trembled.

Yegor clicked his tongue.

"That is bad. Very bad. Why did you not come
to me sooner?"

"I did not wish to trouble you."

"Trouble! You village fellows are all alike. You have no
sense. Is not Yegor Danilych held in high favour? Could
I not say a word?"

"Say one, for God's sake."

Yegor thought awhile, stroking his beard.

"There is no empty place," he said. "Still—wait. We have
an old servant, Polikarpych. He looks after the yard, carries
wood, goes on errands, cleans boots—in short, a man of
all work. But he has grown feeble. He is more
than sixty, and his legs scarcely carry him. The master
keeps him from habit."

Gerasim listened eagerly.

"Perhaps another servant might be needed?"

"Two servants for one man's work? No. But if the
old fellow were sent away—"

Yegor stopped.

Gerasim felt uneasy, though he did not understand why.

"I shall speak to the master," Yegor continued. "Come tomorrow
afternoon. And mind you, when he asks questions, do not
wag your tongue. Bow, answer shortly, and leave the rest
to me."

Gerasim thanked him again and again.

That night he went to sleep with hope in his
heart. He saw before him a warm corner, regular meals,
wages, and perhaps a new coat in spring.

## III

The next morning Yegor found his master in the counting
room.

Sharov was a heavy, gray-haired man who disliked making changes
in his household. He paid his servants little, but seldom
dismissed them, and this was considered goodness among the merchants.

"What is it?" he asked, seeing Yegor linger at the
door.

"I wished to speak about Polikarpych, sir."

"What about him?"

"It is not for me to judge," said Yegor, lowering
his eyes. "You are the master. Only I fear there
may be trouble."

"What trouble?"

"The old man is past his work. Yesterday he forgot
to bolt the side gate. Last week he carried a
parcel to the wrong street. Then there is the wood.
We burn and burn, and still the rooms are cold.
Where does the wood go? I say nothing, sir, but
people talk."

"Do you think he steals it?"

"God forbid that I should accuse anyone! He may burn
it in his own room. His wife is always ill
and wants warmth. But your property is your property."

Sharov frowned.

"He has served me eighteen years."

"That is true. And in former days he was useful.
But does a man serve with his former days? You
need young legs, strong arms, sharp eyes. Otherwise, one morning
you will wake and find the yard empty."

Sharov did not answer.

Yegor then spoke of Gerasim. He praised his strength, honesty,
sobriety, and humble character. He said that he knew all
Gerasim's family and would be answerable for him.

"Let me see the fellow," Sharov said at last.

Gerasim was brought in that afternoon. He stood near the
door, holding his cap in both hands.

"You are looking for a place?" asked Sharov.

"Yes, sir."

"What work can you do?"

"Whatever you order, sir."

"Can you tend a horse?"

"Yes, sir."

"Carry sacks?"

"Yes, sir."

"Have you served in Moscow before?"

Gerasim told him where he had worked.

Sharov looked at his shoulders and hands.

"He is strong enough," he said to Yegor.

"A treasure, sir," replied the coachman. "And he asks no
great wages."

Sharov sighed.

"Well, come after the holiday. Polikarpych must be told."

At these words Gerasim's heart leaped. He bowed so low
that his bundle nearly fell from under his arm.

"Thank you, sir! May God reward you!"

"Serve honestly," said Sharov.

Outside, Yegor slapped him on the shoulder.

"There, my boy! What did I tell you? When Yegor
Danilych takes a matter in hand, it is as good
as done."

## IV

Gerasim walked into the yard as if he had suddenly
grown wings. The frost no longer troubled him. The smoke
above the roofs looked cheerful, the bells of the passing
sledges sounded like holiday music, and even the pale winter
sky seemed bright.

In three days he would enter service.

He calculated what he might do with his wages. First
he would repay the men who had lent him money.
Then he would send something to his mother. If he
saved carefully, he could buy boots before Lent.

Yegor invited him into the servants' kitchen and gave him
cabbage soup and buckwheat porridge. Gerasim had not eaten so
well for many weeks.

"Eat, eat," said Yegor. "You must put flesh on your
bones. A servant in a rich house must look respectable."

After the meal Yegor went to harness the horses. Gerasim
remained alone. Presently he remembered that he had left his
cap on a bench in the passage and went to
fetch it.

As he approached the passage, he heard voices behind a
thin partition. One belonged to an old woman; the other
was low and hoarse.

"What shall we do now?" the woman was saying. "Tell
me, what shall we do?"

"Do not cry," answered the man. "Perhaps the master will
change his mind."

"He will not. Yegor has settled it. He has brought
his own friend."

Gerasim stopped.

The woman began to sob.

"We work and work all our lives," she said, "and
this is our reward. Eighteen years in this house! You
went out in rain and frost. When the master was
ruined by that contractor, you stayed without wages. When his
children were sick, who sat up at night? And now—away
with you, old dog! A young one has come."

"Hush, Matryona."

"How can I hush? We are ruined, ruined us they
have! Where shall we go? Your brother is dead. My
daughter has five children of her own. Will they take
us at the almshouse? They say there is no room."

"We shall find something."

"What will you find with those legs? Yesterday you could
scarcely carry the coal scuttle."

There was silence. Then Polikarpych spoke again.

"Yegor has long wanted me gone. I saw it. He
told the master I stole wood."

"You? Steal wood!"

"I took three sticks when you had fever. I told
him so."

"And for three sticks they throw us into the street?"

"No. The sticks are only an excuse. His village friend
needs my place."

The words stabbed Gerasim like a dagger.

He stood with his hand upon the wall. Only a
few moments before he had been thinking of boots, wages,
and warm meals. Now he saw an old man with
weak legs and a sick wife driven out into the
frost so that he, Gerasim, might enter.

The woman continued weeping.

"May God forgive that young fellow. Perhaps he does not
know."

"He knows now," thought Gerasim.

He took his cap quietly and went back into the
kitchen.

## V

Yegor returned in good spirits.

"Well, have you eaten your fill?"

"Yes."

"Then come. I will show you where you will sleep.
Polikarpych's room is small, but warm. When the old people
clear out their rubbish—"

"I cannot take the place," said Gerasim.

Yegor stared at him.

"What?"

"I cannot take Polikarpych's place."

"Why not?"

Gerasim shifted his cap from one hand to the other.

"He is old."

"Did you discover that by yourself?"

"And his wife is sick. They have nowhere to go."

Yegor's face darkened.

"So you have been listening at doors."

"I did not mean to listen. I went for my
cap."

"Your cap! A clever excuse. What is their business to
you?"

Gerasim was silent.

Yegor came closer.

"Listen, my lad. Yesterday you were hungry. You begged me
to help you. I spoke to the master and pledged
my word. Now you wish to make a fool of
me."

"Forgive me, Yegor Danilych."

"Forgive! Will forgiveness restore my credit with the master? Do
you imagine places fall from heaven? One man goes, another
comes. That is the way of the world."

"I know."

"You know nothing. Polikarpych is useless. The master pays him
out of charity."

"He has served eighteen years."

"What of it? Must the master feed him until Judgment
Day? Perhaps you will feed him?"

Gerasim's face reddened.

"I cannot feed him."

"Then do not meddle. Think of yourself. You are not
a gentleman. You have no land, no trade, no money.
In a week you will be begging again."

Gerasim knew that every word was true. He saw the
cold streets, the indifferent faces, the long walks from yard
to yard. He remembered the gnawing pain of hunger. For
a moment his resolution wavered.

Yegor saw this and softened his tone.

"Be sensible. The old man will manage somehow. His daughter
will take him, or the parish will help. You cannot
carry every poor man on your shoulders."

"No," Gerasim said slowly. "But I cannot push this one
off with my own hands."

## VI

At that moment Sharov entered the kitchen.

"What is this noise?"

Yegor bowed.

"It is Gerasim, sir. He has taken offence at something
and refuses the position."

Sharov looked surprised.

"Did you find better work?"

"No, sir."

"Then what is the matter?"

Gerasim wished the earth would swallow him. It was not
easy for a hungry man to refuse a wealthy master.
He bent his head.

"I heard Polikarpych and his wife, sir."

Sharov's brows drew together.

"You were spying?"

"No, sir. It happened by chance. They said they had
nowhere to go. I did not know my coming would
send them away."

Sharov glanced at Yegor.

"The old man is unfit," Yegor said quickly. "I told
you, sir. Gerasim is foolish. Give him an order and
he will obey."

Sharov turned again to Gerasim.

"Well? I cannot keep two men for one duty."

"I understand, sir."

"Then you accept?"

Gerasim drew a deep breath.

"No, sir."

"And what will you do?"

"I shall look for work."

"You may not find it."

"That may be."

Yegor threw up his hands.

"There! A blockhead! A complete blockhead!"

Sharov was silent for a little while. His face showed
annoyance, but also shame. He had known that Polikarpych was
old; until that moment he had not permitted himself to
think what dismissal meant.

"You may go," he said at last.

Gerasim bowed.

As he reached the door, Sharov called after him.

"Wait. Here is something for your trouble."

He took money from his pocket.

Gerasim looked at the coins, then shook his head.

"I did no work, sir."

And before either man could answer, he went out.

## VII

In the yard he met Polikarpych.

The old servant was thin and stooped. A broom was
in his hand, though the snow he had gathered lay
in an uneven heap. He looked anxiously at Gerasim.

"You are the new man?" he asked.

"No, grandfather."

"No?"

"I am going away."

Polikarpych's lips moved, but for a moment he said nothing.

"Has the master changed his mind?"

"Perhaps," answered Gerasim. "Ask him."

The old man understood. Tears came into his faded eyes.

"What is your name, my boy?"

"Gerasim."

"May God remember you, Gerasim."

Gerasim became embarrassed.

"There is nothing to remember. I did not do anything."

He hurried through the gate before the old man could
answer.

Yegor ran out upon the steps.

"Go, then!" he shouted. "But do not come crawling to
me when your belly is empty. I shall not lift
a finger. Do you hear?"

"I hear," Gerasim replied.

"And you will be sorry!"

"Perhaps."

He went into the street.

## VIII

Evening had fallen. Snow was beginning to descend, fine and
dry, upon the roofs and pavements. People hurried past with
parcels for Christmas; sledges flew by, and from the shops
came the smell of bread and smoked fish.

Gerasim had no position, little money, and no certainty of
tomorrow's meal. The cellar where he slept was far away,
and the wind blew through his worn coat. Nothing in
his circumstances had improved. Indeed, he had never been further
from security.

Yet as he walked, he felt happy and lighthearted.

He had lost a warm room, wages, and bread, but
the burden that had made him sick at heart was
gone. Somewhere behind him an old man still held his
broom, and an old woman had perhaps ceased weeping.

"Work will be found," Gerasim said to himself. "I have
two sound hands."

He tightened the cord around his bundle and walked on
through the snow.

# Rome Saved by Iron

Long before Rome had become mistress of the world, when
her eagles had not yet been borne beyond the narrow
seas, and when the proud names of Greece, Carthage, and
Macedonia still sounded mightier than her own, she stood upon
the hills beside the Tiber, a strong but unfinished city,
already conscious of greatness, yet still encompassed by enemies. Around
her stretched the Campagna, now green beneath the rains, now
brown and dusty beneath the summer sun; beyond rose hills
of amethyst and blue, upon whose slopes dwelt tribes that
watched with jealous eyes the growth of their formidable neighbor.
Through the midst of that plain flowed Father Tiber, yellow
with the earth which he carried down from the interior,
winding past marsh, meadow, and cultivated field, until he reached
the sea.

The city itself was less magnificent than that imperial Rome
of which every schoolboy has heard. There were as yet
no marble palaces, no vast amphitheatres, no triumphal arches commemorating
victories over half the nations of mankind. But the Forum
was already the heart of the Roman State. Thither came
the citizens to hear the magistrates, to vote upon public
questions, to witness trials, to buy and sell, and to
learn the news of war. Above it rose the Capitol,
steep, rocky, and fortified, crowned by the temple of Jupiter,
whom the Romans, being heathens, revered as the special guardian
of their city. Near at hand sat the Senate, that
grave council of elders which directed affairs with a constancy
and wisdom rarely found in popular governments.

The Romans had expelled their kings and established a republic.
Yet by a republic they did not mean precisely what
we understand by that word. Their citizens were divided into
orders; the patricians claimed descent from the ancient fathers of
the State, while the plebeians, though free, had long been
excluded from many dignities. The chief magistrates were attended by
lictors, who carried bundles of rods as emblems of authority.
Tribunes defended the commons; consuls commanded the armies; and in
seasons of extreme peril a Dictator might be appointed, to
whom, for a limited time, the whole power of the
State was entrusted. The Romans loved law, office, ceremony, and
prescribed forms. Even their ambition submitted itself, at least in
outward show, to rules inherited from their ancestors.

Their army was likewise the army of a citizen people.
The husbandman left his plough, took shield and spear, marched
beneath the standards, and, when the campaign was ended, returned
to sow or reap. The legion was already gaining that
stern discipline which afterward made it terrible from Britain to
the Euphrates; but Rome had not yet learned all the
lessons of war, nor had she acquired the calm confidence
of long dominion. She could be rash; she could be
unjust; and, as often happens with nations no less than
with men, her very courage sometimes tempted her into sin.

Northward of the Roman territory lay the lands of the
Etruscans, among whose cities was Clusium. Farther still, beyond the
familiar fields of Italy, had come wandering bands of Gauls—huge
men, as the Romans thought them, with fair or fiery
hair, brawny limbs, broad shields, and long swords. They were
hardy from mountain journeyings, impatient of restraint, fierce in onset,
and terrible in the noise with which they entered battle.
Their customs appeared wild to the orderly Romans, and their
speech sounded harsh and barbarous; yet they were not without
leaders of ability, nor were they incapable of understanding honor.
Their chief at this time was Brennus, a man bold
in enterprise, keen in judgment, and haughty in success.

The Gauls descended upon the country about Clusium and demanded
land. The frightened Clusians, unable to resist them alone, sent
to Rome for succor. Rome did not at first send
an army, but dispatched three ambassadors of the distinguished Fabian
family, charging them to learn the cause of the quarrel
and, if possible, to persuade the strangers to withdraw.

An ambassador is protected because he bears words, not weapons.
Even among nations which agree in little else, the person
of a herald has commonly been regarded as sacred; for
if he who carries a message may fight, slay, or
be slain at pleasure, there is an end to conference,
and war becomes the only interpreter between peoples. The Fabii,
therefore, ought to have spoken calmly, delivered the judgment of
Rome, and returned.

But they were young, proud, and foolish. When Brennus refused
to abandon his enterprise, and when the Gauls and Clusians
came to battle, the Roman envoys forgot their sacred character.
They armed themselves, joined the Clusians, and fought against the
very men to whom they had come as messengers. One
of them encountered a Gallic chief and slew him with
his own hand.

The deed was brave if performed by a soldier; performed
by an ambassador, it was shameful. Thus often does the
moral quality of an action depend not upon the strength
required to accomplish it, but upon the duty which it
violates. The Fabii may have returned exulting in their courage,
yet they carried home a guilt heavier than the armor
they had borne.

Brennus did not instantly avenge the wrong. He sent envoys
to Rome and demanded that the covenant-breakers should be delivered
up for punishment. His demand was stern, but it was
just. The Senate itself perceived the justice of it; nevertheless,
the Fabii were powerful, and their father possessed influence among
the people. Family pride pleaded where public honor ought alone
to have spoken. The guilty men were not surrendered. Nay,
as if Rome meant to crown misconduct with dignity, the
people shortly afterward appointed them military tribunes—officers entrusted with high
command.

By that election the offense of three rash men became
the offense of the nation. The citizen who protects a
wrong because his kinsman has committed it takes part in
the wrong; and the State which honors those whom it
ought to punish teaches foreign peoples that its treaties are
but snares and its promises empty breath. Rome, who boasted
that her greatness rested upon reverence for law, had herself
despised the law of nations.

When the Gallic ambassadors returned and related what had been
done, Brennus raised his standard. He turned from Clusium toward
Rome.

The news flew before him. At first the Romans could
scarcely believe that the barbarians would pass hostile cities and
rich fields in order to strike directly at the State
which had insulted them. Yet on they came, rapid and
terrible, declaring to those through whose territories they marched that
they sought no quarrel with them.

"Our road is to Rome," they said.

There was no time for careful preparation. The Roman army
was hastily assembled and led out to meet the invaders
near the little River Allia, which flowed into the Tiber
some miles from the city. Men had been summoned, but
courage had not been organized; officers were present, but authority
was uncertain; and the guilty tribunes who should have inspired
confidence could not forget that the enemy had come because
of their own misconduct. A sinful heart commonly makes a
failing hand. He who knows that justice stands upon the
opposing side may shout loudly, but beneath the shout there
is weakness.

The Gauls appeared upon the field in formidable array, their
tall bodies and great weapons magnified by Roman apprehension. Brennus
observed that the Roman line had been extended too far
and that its center was weak. He attacked with vigor.
The wild cries of his warriors rolled over the plain;
swords flashed; shields clashed; dust arose; and almost before battle
had fairly begun, order vanished from the Roman ranks.

The army was routed at the Allia.

Some soldiers fled toward Veii; others cast away their arms
and sought the Tiber. Many perished in the river, burdened
by armor or struck down along the banks. Few hastened
back to Rome, for terror had scattered each man toward
whatever refuge seemed nearest. No strong force remained between Brennus
and the city.

At Rome the day passed in dreadful uncertainty. There was
no swift messenger bearing an orderly report, no returning consul
able to say that the army, though defeated, still held
together. At last fugitives arrived singly and in little bands.
Their faces told the calamity before their lips could frame
it. The army was gone. The road lay open. The
Gauls were coming.

Then arose such confusion as a city may know but
once in its lifetime. Mothers gathered their children; householders snatched
what treasure could be carried; priests removed sacred objects; men
shouted contradictory commands; carts blocked the ways; and crowds pressed
toward the bridges and gates. Most of the inhabitants fled
to neighboring towns. Some escaped across the Tiber; some sought
refuge wherever old friendship or kinship promised shelter. The soldiers
who returned, together with such citizens as could bear arms,
withdrew to the Capitol and fortified the approaches to that
steep and sacred height. They had not strength to defend
the whole circuit of Rome; they would preserve, if they
could, the citadel, the temples, and the name of the
State.

But there remained in the city eighty aged senators.

These venerable men had administered laws, advised armies, and watched
the republic grow through years of toil and danger. Some
could no longer wield a sword; none could endure the
privations of a siege without becoming a burden to the
younger defenders. They believed also that Rome's calamity was a
punishment for public guilt, and that the gods—whom they knew
only through the dim light permitted to the heathen world—might
be appeased by a solemn offering. Therefore they resolved not
to flee. They would devote themselves for their country.

We who have been taught a purer faith know that
no blood of senator or soldier can cleanse a nation
from sin. Yet there was in their act a shadow,
or presage, of that great truth that the innocent may
willingly suffer for the guilty, and that love counts not
life dear when others may be saved. Their belief was
imperfect; their courage was noble.

They arrayed themselves in white robes, bordered according to their
rank; some bore ivory staffs, and some wore the insignia
of magistracy. Then they took their seats in the Forum
and in the porches of their houses, waiting for death.

The Gauls delayed, suspecting perhaps that the open gates concealed
some Roman stratagem. On the following day they advanced cautiously.
They found no guard upon the walls, no soldiers in
the streets, no multitude in the market-place. The city seemed
enchanted into silence. Doors stood open; the wind moved dust
along the ways; above, upon the Capitol, armed figures watched
from the ramparts; below, Rome sat desolate.

At length the invaders entered the Forum and beheld the
senators.

A wondrous and awful scene it must have been. There
sat the old men, motionless in their robes, their white
hair falling about grave faces, their ivory staffs in their
hands. Neither fear nor supplication disturbed their composure. To the
rude warriors they seemed not men, but statues of gods
or spirits of the city. The Gauls gathered around them
in astonishment, whispering and gazing, scarcely daring to touch beings
so majestic and still.

But curiosity overcame reverence. One barbarian laid a hand upon
the beard of a senator. The insult broke the solemn
charm. The old Roman struck him with his staff. Instantly
the Gallic sword descended. Then the slaughter began. One after
another, the fathers of Rome were killed where they sat,
offering neither flight nor entreaty, until the white robes were
stained and the Forum had received its sacrifice.

The Gauls plundered the houses, carried off what was valuable,
and gave the buildings to the flames. Fire passed from
roof to roof; beams crashed; smoke folded the hills; temples,
dwellings, and public halls were consumed together. From the Capitol
the defenders beheld the ruin beneath them. There lay the
Forum blackened, the streets choked, the homes of their childhood
blazing in the night. Rome, the proud mother of warriors,
seemed dying at their feet.

Yet the Capitol remained.

Brennus attempted to take it, but its steep approaches and
resolute garrison defeated open assault. He therefore surrounded the height
and settled down to a siege. If the defenders could
not be overcome by the sword, hunger might conquer them.
The Gauls occupied the ruined city; the Romans watched above;
and day followed day in misery.

Food became scarce within the citadel. Every measure of grain
was precious; every animal that could furnish nourishment was considered.
Nevertheless, the geese sacred to Juno were preserved. The Romans
believed that to kill creatures dedicated to the goddess would
be impious, and though their religion was darkened by error,
their sincerity deserves respect. It is easy to keep a
ceremony when the table is full; reverence proves itself when
obedience costs something.

Meanwhile Brennus sent bands through the surrounding country in search
of food. One such company approached Ardea, where Marcus Furius
Camillus lived in exile.

Camillus was the greatest Roman captain of his time—stern, proud,
and brilliant in war. Political quarrels and popular resentment had
driven him from the city he had often served. He
had departed wounded in spirit, yet he had not ceased
to be a Roman. When he learned that a Gallic
force lay carelessly encamped near Ardea, he warned the inhabitants
and offered to lead them. Under cover of darkness he
attacked the raiders, slew many, and scattered the rest.

The report of this victory spread among the fugitives. Romans
who had escaped from the Allia gathered at Veii; others
came from farms, villages, and friendly towns. Shame began to
overcome fear. They remembered their discipline, sought weapons, and longed
for a leader. One name passed from mouth to mouth—Camillus.

Messengers begged him to assume command. But Camillus refused.

He had been wronged by his country, and he might
have made that wrong an excuse for indifference; instead, he
chose a nobler reason for delay. He would not command
without lawful authority. The Senate still existed upon the Capitol;
the Roman State, though narrowed to one beleaguered hill, had
not ceased to be. If he drew the sword merely
because soldiers acclaimed him, he might save Rome from the
Gauls and yet injure her laws. He declared that he
would accept power only when regularly appointed Dictator.

But how could authority come from the Capitol? Between Camillus
and the Senate lay the Gallic army, the ruined city,
and the guarded river. Who durst pass through them? Who
could climb the steep where Brennus watched for every movement?

A young Roman named Pontius Cominius offered himself.

When night had fallen, he approached the Tiber, laid aside
what might impede him, and entered the dark stream. Silently
he swam beneath the shadow of the bank. The current
bore against him; the cold water weighed upon his limbs;
above him, perhaps, glimmered the watch-fires of the enemy. One
splash too loud, one call from a sentinel, one arrow
seen only at the instant it struck—and his mission would
have ended. Yet he gained the farther bank.

He passed among the ruins and reached the base of
the Capitol. The accustomed paths were guarded, but Pontius selected
a precipitous place which the Gauls deemed impossible. He began
to climb.

Upward he went, grasping roots and projections of rock, pressing
his body against the cliff, testing each support before he
trusted it. Beneath him yawned darkness; above him rose the
unseen wall. Loose earth slipped under his feet. Once dislodged,
a stone might betray him; once exhausted, he could expect
no rescue. Still he climbed—slowly, silently, steadfastly—until Roman hands reached
down and drew him over the summit.

The defenders crowded about him. He told them that the
fugitives were rallying, that Camillus had defeated the raiders at
Ardea, and that he would lead the Romans only by
lawful appointment. The Senate assembled amid the privations of the
siege. Even there, with famine within and barbarians without, the
forms of the republic were observed. A decree was passed
naming Camillus Dictator, and Pontius, bearing this lawful commission, descended
by the same perilous route, crossed the Tiber again, and
carried the authority of the State to the exile.

Thus did one courageous swimmer reunite the scattered body of
Rome with its governing mind. There are deeds which make
more noise and accomplish less.

Yet the passage of Pontius had left marks upon the
cliff. Shrubs had been broken; soil had been disturbed; the
surface showed where a human foot had pressed. The next
day a Gaul noticed the traces. They led upward. The
impossible path was possible.

Brennus resolved upon a night attack. His strongest warriors were
chosen and commanded to climb without armor that might clash
against the stones. When darkness covered the Capitol, they crept
to the foot of the cliff and began their ascent.
One followed another, placing hands and feet where Pontius had
placed his own. No trumpet sounded; no battle cry was
uttered. The Roman sentinels heard nothing. Even the watch-dogs gave
no alarm.

Higher and higher came the Gauls.

Upon the summit, near the temple of Juno, were the
sacred geese. Hunger had made them wakeful, or Providence employed
their natural vigilance for an end of which neither priest
nor warrior had dreamed. They heard the faint scraping upon
the rock. They stirred, stretched their necks, beat their wings,
and broke into loud, angry cries.

Marcus Manlius, a soldier of courage and strength, awoke. He
seized his weapons and rushed toward the sound. At the
edge of the height he beheld a Gallic warrior rising
above the parapet. There was no time to summon a
council, no time even to array the garrison. Manlius struck
the foremost assailant and hurled him backward. The falling man
crashed against those beneath; others lost their hold; shouts burst
from the darkness.

"Awake! The Gauls are upon us!"

The Romans sprang from sleep, snatched shields and swords, and
ran to the threatened point. The narrowness which had aided
the silent climbers now prevented them from using their numbers.
Manlius stood foremost, thrusting down one attacker after another, while
his companions rolled stones and drove spears upon the crowded
ascent. Some Gauls fell from the cliff; some were crushed;
the survivors abandoned the attempt.

Morning revealed the danger from which Rome had escaped. The
sentinels who had slept were disgraced; Manlius was honored before
the army. Each defender, though food was scanty, contributed a
portion of his own allowance as a reward to the
man who had preserved them all. Nor were the geese
forgotten. Creatures incapable of moral purpose had nevertheless given the
warning which men had failed to give; and Roman gratitude,
mingling piety with remembrance, afterward held them in peculiar honor.

Still the siege continued. Courage could repel an assault, but
it could not create bread. The Romans grew thin and
weak; disease appeared among the Gauls, who lived amid ashes,
corpses, and the corruption of a ruined city. Their great
bodies, accustomed to open country and abundant food, suffered beneath
the unhealthy air of the Campagna. Brennus wished to depart,
yet he would not appear to retreat. The Romans wished
to endure, yet famine was waxing sharper every day.

At last negotiations began. It was agreed that the Gauls
should withdraw upon receiving a large ransom in gold. To
surrender treasure for liberty was bitter; but the defenders could
not know how near Camillus might be, and hunger speaks
with a voice which even brave men must heed. Gold
was brought forth—ornaments, vessels, and whatever precious metal had survived
the sack. Scales were set up amid the ruins, and
the ransom was weighed.

Then the Romans perceived that the Gallic weights were false.

They protested. Brennus, instead of correcting the fraud, laughed at
their helplessness. Drawing his heavy sword, he cast it also
into the scale, increasing the burden of gold required. When
the Romans cried out against this insolence, he answered with
the words that have passed down through the centuries:

"Vae victis!—Woe to the conquered!"

The saying expressed the doctrine of mere force. According to
Brennus, the defeated possess no rights; justice belongs to the
stronger arm; covenant, measure, and mercy may all be thrown
into the scale against those who cannot resist. It is
the creed of every tyrant, whether he wears a crown,
commands an army, or merely oppresses the weak within his
reach.

But Brennus had spoken too soon.

A stir arose beyond the ruined Forum. Armed men appeared
among the blackened streets; Roman standards advanced through smoke and
dust; horsemen in mail came swiftly on. At their head
rode Camillus, Dictator by decree of the Senate and commander
of the reunited Roman forces.

He entered the place where the ransom was being weighed
and demanded the meaning of the scene. The agreement, he
declared, had been made without his authority; now that a
Dictator had been appointed, no lesser officer could bind the
State. He ordered the gold to be removed.

Brennus angrily appealed to the compact. Camillus looked upon the
false weights and the sword lying in the scale.

"Rome," he said, "is saved with iron, not with gold."

The brief truce ended. Swords, not coins, were now to
determine the possession of the city.

The first struggle among the ruins was fierce and confused.
The Gauls, encumbered by plunder and surprised by the sudden
appearance of a disciplined enemy, could not form their line
with advantage. The Romans, animated by shame, vengeance, and the
sight of their desecrated homes, attacked with a resolution very
different from the panic of the Allia. Camillus directed them
calmly. He had studied the Gallic manner of fighting; he
knew the fury of their onset and the disorder which
followed if that onset were withstood.

Brennus was driven from Rome.

But Camillus did not permit him to depart unpursued. A
victory which merely removed the enemy for a day would
not restore the security or honor of the State. The
Roman army followed the retreating Gauls, overtook them, and forced
them again to battle. This time there were no false
weights, no helpless negotiators, no starving garrison. Legion stood against
war-band; discipline against violence; a lawful commander against a chief
made insolent by success. The Gallic army was broken and
destroyed.

Rome had been delivered.

Yet what a Rome awaited her returning children! The streets
were heaped with rubbish; walls stood roofless; familiar houses had
vanished; the Forum was scarred by fire; and around the
Capitol stretched a blackened desolation. Many citizens, beholding the ruin,
wished to abandon the place and settle elsewhere. Why rebuild
what flame had consumed? Why not choose another site, less
burdened with grief?

Camillus opposed them. Rome was not merely timber, tile, and
stone. She was the seat of their laws, the dwelling
of their fathers, the guardian of their worship, the common
mother whose wounds imposed duty rather than released them from
it. To forsake her because she was ruined would be
to imitate the coward who deserts a friend in adversity.
The citizens yielded, and the work of rebuilding began.

For this deliverance Camillus was called the second founder of
Rome. The first founder had given the city walls; Camillus
restored her life. Marcus Manlius was remembered as the defender
of the Capitol; Pontius Cominius as the brave messenger who
carried lawful authority through river, enemy, and darkness; the aged
senators as men who chose a solemn death rather than
burden the last defenders of the State; and even the
geese of Juno received honor because their cries had awakened
vigilance at the instant when a nation's fate hung upon
a ledge of rock.

As for the Fabii, their rashness had written its lesson
in ashes. Three ambassadors forgot their duty, a powerful house
shielded them, and a people preferred favor to justice; from
that small beginning came defeat, slaughter, fire, famine, and humiliation.
Rome's strength did not save her while she defended wrongdoing.
Her restoration began when courage once more joined itself to
lawful authority—when Pontius sought a decree, when Camillus refused unlawful
command, when Manlius kept faith with his post, and when
scattered citizens remembered that they belonged to something greater than
themselves.

The full truth of this famous narrative has been greatly
doubted. The early Romans preserved their history through traditions repeated
by families, priests, soldiers, and public speakers; records may have
perished in the very flames which consumed the city; patriotic
pride may afterward have concealed defeats or adorned victories. Some
have questioned whether Camillus arrived at the precise moment when
the gold was being weighed, and whether Brennus's army suffered
the complete destruction later chroniclers described. Legend loves a dramatic
entrance, a memorable saying, and an exact recompense.

Yet the uncertainty of details need not deprive the tale
of all value. That the Gauls defeated the Romans, entered
and burned their city, and failed to master the Capitol
may well preserve the solid outline of fact. Around that
outline the Roman imagination placed those forms which expressed its
highest ideals: the senator who can die, the youth who
can dare, the soldier who can wake, and the commander
who can obey before he commands.

If it be history, it records one of the sternest
trials of ancient Rome. If in part it be romance,
it is romance fashioned by a people who believed that
courage without justice is ruinous, that authority without law is
dangerous, and that no treasure can redeem the honor which
only duty restores. The gold upon Brennus's scale has long
since vanished; the sword of Camillus is dust; but the
opposing sentences remain. On one side stands the brutal cry,
"Woe to the conquered!" On the other, the answer of
a reviving nation—that freedom and honor are guarded, not with
gold, but with iron.

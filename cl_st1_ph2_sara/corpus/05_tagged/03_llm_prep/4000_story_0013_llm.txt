### The Christmas Circle

Walnut Grove lay in the Cherokee Strip where the Cimarron,
after wandering through sand hills and red cut-banks, bent northward
against a low line of cottonwoods. The grove was neither
walnuts exclusively nor much of a grove by Eastern measurement,
but it furnished shade, firewood, and a name, which are
the three things necessary to establish a place on the
cattle frontier. West of it spread the salt plain, white
in dry weather and treacherous after rain; eastward rolled the
winter-brown grass toward Coldwater Pool; and along the river stood
dunes, plum thickets, blackjack, and cane sufficient to hide every
gray wolf from Eagle Chief to the Black Bear country.

A mile below the grove the Cimarron formed a horseshoe.
The stream entered from the northwest, curved nearly upon itself,
and escaped southeast, leaving within the bend a pocket of
sand and brush about three-quarters of a mile broad at
its widest part. On the landward side rose broken dunes,
and behind those dunes extended open prairie. There was no
better natural trap in the Strip, provided men could persuade
the wolves to enter it and afterward refrain from shooting
one another.

The first requirement seemed possible. The second was regarded as
speculative.

For three winters the wolves had multiplied along the river.
They killed calves when calves were to be had, yearlings
when hunger sharpened them, and, on occasion, full-grown cows weakened
by storm or bogged in a crossing. A wolf did
not consume enough beef to ruin a ranch, but he
possessed an aggravating habit of taking his portion without consulting
the owner. Worse, where one wolf pulled down a calf,
five companions frequently came to supper. The carcass was then
charged, in the cowman's arithmetic, to every member of the
pack.

By December the range men had tallied losses sufficient to
produce indignation, and indignation, when distributed among a dozen cow-camps,
becomes organization. Bill Miller proposed a circle hunt for Christmas
morning.

Miller had ridden the country until its draws and crossings
were as familiar to him as the rooms of a
small house. He was not the oldest man present, nor
the loudest, nor the quickest with a rope, but he
possessed the uncommon faculty of remembering what he had set
out to do after somebody fired a gun. For that
reason he was elected captain.

Six hundred horses were gathered at camps scattered from Walnut
Grove to Coldwater. Not six hundred men, for each sensible
rider brought a change of mount, and several insensible riders
brought three. There were cowboys from the LX, J+H, Eagle
Chief, Black Bear, and smaller outfits whose brands were better
known than their owners. Farmers came in wagons. Hunters arrived
with rifles of every bore and ancestry. Two merchants closed
their stores, a doctor brought a shotgun, and one schoolmaster
appeared upon a mule, explaining that Xenophon had recommended mounted
encirclement.

"No Greek is riding my left flank," said Miller. "But
you can take the mule and keep ten yards from
the man ahead."

The plan, delivered on Christmas Eve beside a fire at
Walnut Grove, had the severity of a military operation. Two
hundred beaters were to sweep the upper river bottoms. One
hundred and fifty riders would guard the south flank; an
equal number the north. The remainder, divided into squads of
ten under local captains, would close the rear. No man
was to pass inside the line. No man was to
fire until ordered. Deer, turkey, antelope, coyotes, and all lesser
temptations were to be ignored. When the circle contracted upon
the horseshoe, designated riflemen would dismount and kill the wolves
from beyond shotgun range.

"There will be loaded firearms in every direction," Miller concluded.
"Any man shooting wild is as liable to hit a
friend as a wolf."

"That friend ought to keep out of the way," observed
Reese.

Reese was a lean, red-mustached rider from the J+H, with
a capacity for remaining cheerful under reprimand. He wore a
revolver, carried a Winchester, and had tied a shotgun across
his saddle. The collection gave his horse the appearance of
transporting a small arsenal.

"You," said Miller, "will carry one gun."

"Which one?"

"The unloaded one."

This was received as excellent wit, though Miller had not
intended it so.

Christmas dawned hard and brilliant. Frost silvered the bunch grass;
ice filmed the quiet edges of the river; smoke from
a dozen campfires stood upright in the windless air. The
sun came red over the prairie, touching saddle horns, gun
barrels, tin cups, and the vapor blowing from horses' nostrils.
Men ate bacon, biscuits, and beans, drank coffee black enough
to stain a branding iron, and spoke of peace on
earth while tightening cinches for the extermination of wolves.

At seven o'clock Miller sent the divisions out.

The north flank crossed at a shallow ford and moved
upstream under the command of Asa Cave of the LX.
The south flank, led by Tom Sevenoaks, filed beyond the
dunes. Beaters spread through the river timber in a skirmish-line,
some mounted, some on foot, with instructions to strike brush,
shout, and keep abreast. The rear cordon remained open until
both wings had advanced six miles; it would then swing
inward and drive everything toward the bend.

For the first hour the movement proceeded like clockwork, if
one allows that frontier clockwork occasionally bucks, swears, and stops
to roll a cigarette. Riders held intervals of twenty to
thirty yards. Squad captains passed signals by hat and arm.
The beaters crashed through cane and plum brush, announcing themselves
with cow-camp yells calculated to alarm any beast that had
not already died of natural causes.

Miller rode a rangy bay along the inside of the
south line, watching the hills. He had scarcely begun to
believe the operation might succeed when three shots cracked from
the rear.

He wheeled and galloped back.

Reese emerged from a thicket carrying a wild turkey by
the legs. The bird was a gobbler of respectable dimensions,
and Reese's expression suggested that he had personally invented Christmas
dinner.

"What did I tell you?" Miller demanded.

"You told us not to shoot wolves until ordered."

"I told you not to shoot anything."

"I didn't shoot anything. I shot at this turkey, and
then I shot him."

"You abandoned your station."

"Only seventy yards."

"That is enough to open the line."

Reese lifted the gobbler appraisingly. "He'll weigh eighteen pounds dressed."

Miller looked at the bird, at Reese, and at the
gap in the cordon.

"Get back in line."

"Should I leave him?"

"No."

"I knew you had judgment."

Thus early did the good resolutions of the expedition become
paving material.

At half past eight a whitetail buck broke from the
north timber. He passed between two beaters, bounded up a
dune, and stopped broadside to inspect the mounted line. Fifteen
cowboys simultaneously remembered that Miller's prohibition was advisory. Rifles rose.

Cave rode in front of them.

"Anybody shoots that deer," he called, "will explain the hole
in my coat."

The buck departed untouched, partly through discipline and partly because
no one wished to buy Cave a coat.

Ten minutes later three does crossed the south flank, where
no officer happened to be directly in the way. The
fusillade resembled a skirmish-line in action. Sand jumped ahead of
the deer, behind them, and on both sides. One animal
stumbled, recovered, and vanished over the ridge. Sevenoaks examined the
empty shells scattered in the grass.

"Forty-one shots," he said. "That doe has had a narrow
escape from the general direction of danger."

The noise started an antelope from a hollow. It ran
toward the rear cordon, discovered horsemen there, and turned along
the line at a speed that stirred every sporting instinct
in five counties. Men roweled their horses and streamed after
it.

For two miles the antelope led twenty riders across frozen
prairie. It ran easily, its white rump flashing above the
grass, while the horses stretched and labored. The line behind
them opened as neatly as a gate.

Miller came over a rise and met the racers returning.
Sevenoaks had roped the antelope, which proved to be a
long-legged yearling scarcely larger than a sheep. It trotted behind
him with a loop around its neck and an expression
of injured surprise.

"What is that?" Miller asked.

"Future property," said Sevenoaks. "Too small to eat, so I
earmarked him."

The antelope's right ear had indeed acquired a notch.

"Turn it loose and close your line."

Sevenoaks removed the rope. The yearling stood for a moment,
shook itself, and walked away without haste, having learned that
dignity is useless but may be preserved.

News of these diversions traveled through the hunt faster than
orders. Men laughed and repeated the particulars until Reese's gobbler
weighed twenty-six pounds and the antelope had been branded, dehorned,
and entered in the LX books. Yet beneath the holiday
disorder the great circle continued to contract. Flankers appeared on
distant ridges as black points against the pale grass. Beaters
moved downstream. Whatever lived in the bottoms was being pushed
toward the horseshoe.

The first wolf showed at nine-thirty.

A rider from Black Bear saw it climb a sandy
cut-bank, stand against the sky, and look backward. It was
a large gray bitch, long-legged and winter-coated. She regarded the
line ahead, the shouting men behind, and the open prairie
to the north. Then she dropped from sight.

The report passed to Miller.

"How many?"

"One."

"Where there's one looking, there are six waiting," he said.

He ordered the north flank strengthened and sent word that
no firing would be tolerated. The word arrived at nearly
every squad, though its effect diminished in proportion to the
distance traveled.

More wolves began to show. Two trotted from the cane,
tested the south line, and turned back. Five appeared along
a dune crest and disappeared like smoke. A coyote darted
between horses, provoking excited yells but, by a miracle, no
gunfire. The beaters found fresh beds in the grass and
the torn remains of a yearling steer.

The country itself now aided the hunt. The river on
one side and the mounted cordon on the other offered
the wolves fewer choices with every quarter mile. They yielded
ground reluctantly, slipping through brush, doubling behind dunes, or lying
flat until a beater nearly stepped upon them. Several rushed
the line and were turned by swinging ropes and pistol
shots fired into the ground.

One beaters' squad entered a jungle of plum and cane
so dense that a man could not see his horse's
head. Asa Cave, riding in from the flank, pushed into
it after a wolf. Branches whipped his face; thorns dragged
at his coat; his horse plunged over driftwood and into
a wash. There came a crash, a roar, and language
of such heat that the men outside waited respectfully.

Cave finally emerged without his hat and with half a
plum bush attached to his stirrup.

"What was it like in there?" somebody asked.

"If there is a worse place," he replied, "the preacher
has understated hell."

Near the rear, Juan Mesa encountered a buck.

Juan was a roper from the lower Cimarron, compact in
the saddle and proud of a rawhide reata he had
braided himself. The buck sprang from cover fifteen feet ahead.
Juan's rope was off the saddle faster than reflection. His
loop settled over the antlers.

The buck went east. Juan's pony went west.

For one stretched second all three were connected by rawhide
and bad judgment. Then the rope snapped tight. The pony
sat upon its haunches; Juan passed over the saddle horn;
and the buck, astonished by resistance, wheeled downhill. Juan struck
the ground but retained the rope, a feat admired by
every witness except the pony.

"Take your turn!" Juan shouted as he was dragged through
the grass.

Two men caught the rope, and three more piled on.
The buck bounded, swerved, and pulled the entire party through
a patch of soapweed. At last the loop slipped from
one antler, sailed free, and wrapped itself around Juan's boots.

The animal escaped. Juan sat up, removed grass from his
hair, and examined his rope.

"He is not so much deer," he said, "but he
has plenty locomotive."

By ten o'clock the heads of both flanks had reached
the lower bend. The open circle had become a narrowing
crescent. Miller rode from squad to squad, dressing the line,
closing gaps, and placing the better horsemen where the dunes
concealed movement. The river within the horseshoe was forty to
seventy yards across, shallow at the upper ford but deep
beneath the outer cut-bank. Ice clung to the margins; the
middle current ran the color of coffee with cream.

Wolves could swim it. Miller knew they would rather not.
A wet wolf in December is an animal deprived of
half his confidence and all his comfort.

The mounted cordon came within half a mile of the
pocket. The beaters climbed the last ridge. Then the wolves
began collecting upon the sandbar.

They appeared singly at first, drifting from separate covers, then
in twos and threes. Gray forms crossed the open sand
and vanished among willows. Men counted twelve, then nineteen, then
lost count. A big dog wolf stood on a dune
within the bend, his chest dark, his ruff nearly black,
his tail hanging straight. He looked over the approaching riders
without panic, as if selecting the weakest point.

"That one has wintered on beef," said Miller.

The dog turned and entered the brush.

Miller halted the line at two hundred yards. He chose
eight riflemen reputed to hit what they aimed at and
placed them on a low ridge. The remainder were ordered
to hold position. No mounted man was to enter the
pocket until the rifles had done their work.

The riflemen dismounted, rested barrels across saddles, and waited.

A wolf crossed a strip of open sand at perhaps
a hundred and sixty yards.

Eight rifles fired in ragged succession.

The wolf stopped, looked about as though annoyed by the
noise, and trotted into cover.

"Range too long," said one marksman.

"You shot under him," said another.

"I saw hair fly."

"You saw sand."

A second wolf offered a standing target. Five men fired.
It disappeared.

"Got him."

The animal emerged fifty yards farther down, running strongly.

The practical failure of select marksmanship now awakened confidence in
every spectator. Advice traveled along the line. Men explained windage,
elevation, bullet drop, and the personal defects of the shooters.
One cowboy proposed that all fire together. Another suggested artillery.
Reese, who had tied his gobbler behind the cantle, declared
that turkey loads would do the business if a man
got close enough.

"That," Miller said, "is exactly what I am preventing."

Several wolves made for the river. They entered at a
shallow angle, heads low, gray backs showing above the current.
Riflemen shifted aim. Bullets struck water in white splashes. One
wolf rolled, regained its stroke, and reached the far bank.
Another spun in the current and drifted against a sand
tongue. Two gained the opposite side and climbed slowly through
the mud, where riders stationed beyond the bend killed them.

The gunfire stirred the pocket. Willows shook. Wolves ran from
cover to cover. One rushed toward the mounted line and
turned beneath a storm of shots. A second passed through
a gap before the riders understood what they had seen.
It vanished northward with three men pursuing until Miller's shouts
brought them back.

Then the great dog wolf came out.

He had been struck high in the shoulder. Blood darkened
one side, but the wound had neither broken him nor
persuaded him to surrender. He crossed the sand at a
swinging lope, climbed the dune, and came straight for the
south line.

Horses smelled him and crowded backward. Men lowered rifles, uncertain
whether to shoot past one another. The wolf chose the
instant of hesitation and charged.

He passed between two ponies like a gray cannonball. One
horse wheeled, unseating its rider. Another kicked and missed. Reese
fired from the saddle, removing a portion of Sevenoaks's hat
brim.

"Save your ammunition!" Sevenoaks yelled. "You've killed my shade."

The wolf reached open prairie.

Juan Mesa was already after him.

His bay pony ran low and fast. Juan shook out
the reata, measured the wolf's stride, and threw. The loop
fell over the animal's head and one foreleg. Juan dallied
around the horn.

The wolf struck the rope's end, somersaulted, and came up
fighting. His jaws snapped upon the rawhide. Juan's pony braced,
eyes rolling. Two riders closed as wheelers on the rope,
each adding a line. The dog wolf lunged, twisted, and
nearly reached Juan's horse.

"Keep him away!" somebody shouted.

"I have noticed that," Juan answered.

A rider fired point-blank and missed. Another struck the wolf
with a quirt. At last Sevenoaks rode in, leaned from
the saddle, and finished the animal with a pistol shot
behind the ear.

The great body lay still among the trampled grass. Measured
from nose to tail, it exceeded six feet; measured in
the reports told that evening, it approached nine.

Miller looked at the cut ropes, the frightened ponies, and
the men congratulating one another.

"Back to the line," he said. "We have not begun
yet."

The selected riflemen continued for another quarter hour and accomplished
little beyond making the wolves restless. The animals would not
remain exposed. Smoke drifted low over the sandbar. Every patch
of brush seemed alive with movement, but a gray hide
seen for half a second among gray willows did not
furnish a reasonable target at two hundred yards.

Miller therefore changed the plan. Thirty mounted men armed with
shotguns would advance at a walk, intervals of ten yards,
and drive the wolves from cover. The outside cordon would
remain fixed. Shotgun men were to fire only ahead and
downward. Rifles were to stay quiet.

The order was repeated three times.

The riders entered the horseshoe.

Their horses disliked the business from the beginning. Wolf scent
lay heavy in the pocket, mingled with powder smoke and
blood. The ponies advanced with rigid necks, snorting at every
clump of grass. Men held shotguns across the pommel and
tried to preserve the prescribed intervals.

At eighty yards the line still looked military.

At sixty it looked doubtful.

At forty a wounded wolf erupted beneath a horse.

The animal had been lying in a wash, its hindquarters
disabled. It rose with a snarl and fastened upon the
horse's nose. The horse screamed, went upright, and struck with
both forefeet. Its rider fired downward. The blast cut brush,
fur, and possibly leather, but did not release the wolf.

A second rider rushed in. His horse collided with the
first. The wounded wolf let go, seized a fetlock, and
was kicked rolling.

Someone shouted, "They're breaking!"

Whether he meant wolves, horses, or men was never determined.

The outside line surged forward.

The dogs of war were loosed.

Hell popped.

Every wolf in the pocket seemed to rise at once.
Gray bodies burst from willow clumps, gullies, drift piles, and
grass beds. Shotguns belched. Rifles cracked from behind despite orders.
Revolvers joined without invitation. Smoke boiled across the sandbar.

Horses wheeled, plunged, and collided. Ropes sailed above hats. Men
shouted warnings that no one heard and advice that no
one followed. A wolf ran beneath a pony's belly; the
pony sat down upon its rider. Another wolf leaped a
fallen log and received three shotgun charges, two of which
struck the log. A cowboy roped his partner's front legs
and brought horse and man to earth in one magnificent
wreck.

Reese pursued a wolf through the brush with the gobbler
still bouncing behind his saddle. A willow branch caught the
turkey's neck and pulled it loose. For a moment the
dead bird flew backward through the smoke, causing one excited
farmer to fire both barrels at it.

Sevenoaks saw what happened.

"You've killed Reese's Christmas!"

"I thought it was a wolf."

"Then I hope you never cook for me."

On the river edge five wolves plunged into the current
together. Bullets churned the water around them. Two turned downstream.
One sank. The others gained a mud shelf beneath the
opposite bank, where they struggled upward while men galloped along
the rim firing down. Seven were eventually killed across the
river, though at the time they were counted variously as
eleven, sixteen, and "near a wagonload."

Within the pocket the confusion deepened. The smoke hid friend
and quarry alike. Men dismounted to club wounded wolves and
were nearly ridden over by those still mounted. A gray
bitch with a broken foreleg faced three horses, snapping with
such savage resolution that none would approach. She charged the
nearest, received a load of buckshot, and still crawled forward
until a roper pinned her with his loop.

Another wolf escaped by the simplest method. It lay flat
while the entire line passed, rose behind them, and walked
out through the abandoned rear.

Miller saw it go and could do nothing. He had
lost his hat, his bay was white with lather, and
every command disappeared beneath the gunfire. He rode into the
thickest of it, striking up barrels with his quirt.

"Hold your fire! Hold it!"

A rifle exploded beside him.

He seized the shooter by the coat and pulled him
half out of the saddle.

"If you fire again," Miller said, "I will feed you
to what's left."

The man believed him.

Gradually the violence spent itself. Wolves that could flee had
fled; those that could fight had fought; those remaining were
dead or hidden. Ammunition ran low. Smoke lifted in torn
curtains. Riders drew apart and began recognizing one another.

The sandbar looked as though a small cavalry action had
passed over it. Brush was flattened, the ground stamped and
scored, and empty shells glittered among gray fur. Two horses
bled from bites. One had been peppered lightly with birdshot,
and its owner was circulating through the crowd with personal
inquiries. Three men nursed sprains or bruises. Cave had lost
a second hat. The schoolmaster's mule stood in the river,
refusing civilization.

No man had been shot seriously, which most considered proof
of good management.

Miller restored the cordon and sent careful parties through the
brush. They found wounded wolves and dispatched them. They recovered
saddles, ropes, hats, one boot, two revolvers, a pair of
spectacles, and Reese's gobbler, whose appearance had suffered but whose
substance remained.

At last the dead were dragged into the open.

Twenty-six wolves lay upon the sandbar. Seven more had been
killed beyond the river, making thirty-three. At least five had
escaped, and estimates rose whenever the teller wished to enlarge
the achievement. The great dog wolf was placed at the
head of the row. Around him lay old bitches, young
males, and half-grown wolves of the season.

For a while the men stood quietly.

In death the animals appeared smaller, except the leader, who
looked as formidable as he had alive. Their winter coats
stirred under the cold breeze. Whatever account the cattlemen held
against them had been settled without appeal.

Then somebody began disputing ownership of a hide, and ordinary
life resumed.

The sun was dropping when the outfits separated. Riders turned
toward Walnut Grove, Coldwater, Black Bear, and distant camps along
the Strip. The great circle dissolved into squads, the squads
into pairs, and the pairs into solitary figures moving across
the winter grass. Behind them the Cimarron ran between its
icy margins, carrying powder-stained foam downstream.

Miller found Reese tying the battered turkey behind his saddle.

"You left your post, fired without orders, shot Sevenoaks's hat,
and helped stampede the line," Miller said.

Reese considered the indictment.

"I also retrieved supper."

"A farmer shot that bird after you did."

"He merely tenderized it."

Sevenoaks rode up wearing what remained of his hat. "Who
owns the gobbler?"

"I killed him," Reese said.

"The farmer killed him again."

"And I rescued him twice. Possession favors me."

Juan Mesa joined them, coiling a reata shortened by several
yards. "The big wolf owns part of my rope. I
claim part of the turkey."

"You can have the neck," said Reese.

"The neck was shot away," Sevenoaks observed.

Reese looked from one face to another, then toward the
west where the first evening star shone above the prairie.

"All right," he said. "Come to the J+H camp. We'll
divide the bird according to injury, bravery, and appetite. I've
got eggs, whiskey, and a little milk. We'll make eggnog."

"Whose eggs?" Miller asked.

Reese mounted.

"That," he said, "is a question better postponed until after
drinking."

They rode toward camp, laughing, while Christmas night settled over
the Cherokee Strip and the wolves that had escaped called
faintly from far down the Cimarron.

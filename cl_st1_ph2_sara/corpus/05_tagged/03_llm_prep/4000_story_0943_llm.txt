At the seaside resort of Bluehaven, where the Atlantic came
ashore with the noise of a vast congregation saying amen,
Mr. Amos Stimson owned the Imperial Galloping Pavilion.

The title was painted in gold above a red-and-white striped
canopy, and under it thirty-six wooden horses, four chariots, two
dragons, and an animal which had begun life as a
tiger but had been repaired into a species unknown to
science, revolved daily in a campaign without victory. From ten
in the morning until midnight they charged, fled, pursued, and
returned, their glass eyes burning with the fixed enthusiasm of
soldiers who had forgotten the cause.

Children mounted them with shrieks. Mothers stood beside the silver-painted
railing and issued warnings which vanished under the brass thunder
of the mechanical organ. Bells clanged. Gears complained beneath the
floor. The proprietor stalked around the platform with the majesty
of a field marshal inspecting an empire.

Mr. Stimson was a little man. This circumstance had never
reached his mind in the form of a fact. He
possessed heavy black whiskers, a stomach of official proportions, and
a conviction that his will had the hardness and usefulness
of a granite seawall. When he spoke, he thrust his
chin forward as if launching a weapon. When others spoke,
he narrowed his eyes, thereby announcing that deception had met
its master.

His daughter Lizzie sat in the cashier's enclosure.

This was a small octagonal structure of varnished wood and
silvered wire, perched near the entrance to the platform. A
sign above its opening said CASHIER in black letters. The
sign had a sternness not shared by Lizzie. She was
nineteen, brown-haired, round-cheeked, and usually dressed in red, because her
father held that a bright dress attracted customers and gave
an appearance of prosperity.

Across the whirling cavalry, Frank Heron tended the ring apparatus.

Frank stood upon a narrow elevation beside the outer course.
In one hand he held a long iron arm from
which brass rings were dispensed to the riders; with the
other he occasionally caught a child who had mistaken ambition
for balance. He was tall, fair, and solemn in those
moments when he knew Lizzie was looking at him. At
other times he was merely a young man earning nine
dollars a week.

Between the cashier's cage and the ring stand there flowed
continually a river of painted horses, children, ribbons, hats, elbows,
and parental alarm. Yet Frank's glance crossed it. Lizzie's glance
returned.

They had developed a system.

It was not a good system. No diplomat would have
trusted it with the fate of a fishing boat. A
lifted eyebrow might mean "I love you," "Father is watching,"
or "The boy on the gray horse has dropped his
ticket." A turn of Lizzie's head could indicate jealousy, modesty,
annoyance, or a desire to see who was buying popcorn.
Nevertheless, the two young people believed that their eyes conducted
a correspondence of immense precision.

At eleven each morning Frank's first look said: "I have
thought of you through a night as long as exile."

Lizzie's reply said: "If that is true, why did you
speak to the girl in blue yesterday?"

Frank's second message protested that the girl in blue had
asked whether the tiger was safe.

Lizzie then lowered her lashes, which meant that no tiger,
safe or unsafe, justified the warmth Frank had displayed.

By noon they were estranged.

At two a child fell from the white charger, and
while Frank restored him to his sobbing mother, Lizzie saw
courage, tenderness, and the profile of a hero. At three
she smiled. At four Frank, intoxicated by pardon, nearly presented
the brass prize ring to a stout butcher from Trenton
who had made no attempt to seize it. At five
they were pledged again in the sacred privacy of their
imaginations.

Mr. Stimson observed these events.

For three days he said nothing. Silence, in his opinion,
was the velvet scabbard of authority. He marched behind the
cashier's enclosure, paused near the gate, examined bolts, tickets, reins,
hinges, and the morals of his employees. His eyes traveled
from Lizzie to Frank, from Frank to Lizzie.

He became convinced that he had discovered a conspiracy so
subtle that only his extraordinary penetration could have uncovered it.

On the fourth afternoon the pavilion was crowded. Sunlight burned
on the sea beyond the boardwalk. Red, green, and amber
lanterns hung unlit beneath the canopy, pale as fruit in
the glare. The organ bellowed a march. The horses went
by in ranks, rising and falling with the grave idiocy
of cabinet ministers.

Stimson suddenly entered the cashier's enclosure.

Lizzie looked up. "What is it, Pa?"

"What is it?" he repeated.

He closed the door behind him. The space had been
built for one moderate person, and Stimson's indignation made three.

"You know mighty well what it is."

Lizzie rearranged a pile of tickets. "I don't."

"Don't you?"

"No."

He bent toward her. His whiskers advanced like infantry through
a narrow pass.

"This is too much of a good thing."

"What is?"

"This makin' eyes."

Lizzie's face turned crimson. The dress and the face now
proclaimed a festival.

"I ain't makin' eyes."

"Yes, you are. At that young fellow."

"I have to look over there sometimes."

"You have to look at your business. That's what you
have to look at."

"He works over there."

"He won't work over there long if he don't tend
to what he's paid for."

Lizzie's fingers moved among the tickets. Her heart, assailed at
once by shame, anger, fear, and loyalty, could find no
suitable banner and therefore fought under all four.

"I never said anything to him."

"You don't need to. You're talkin' enough with your face
to run up a telephone bill."

"Pa!"

"This is too much of a good thing. First thing
you know, he'll get fired."

Having discharged this cannonade, Stimson emerged from the cage. He
felt both righteous and wounded. A father, he reflected, might
labor for years, feed a child, clothe her, educate her,
buy her a red dress, and place her in charge
of cash receipts; then some nine-dollar-a-week ring attendant had only
to tilt his head and all paternal sacrifice became rubbish.

He crossed the platform.

Frank saw him coming and assumed an expression of mechanical
devotion. He adjusted the ring arm. He examined its socket.
He watched the riders with the vigilance of a lighthouse.

Stimson halted below him.

"Heron."

"Yes, sir."

"You're very busy, ain't you?"

"Yes, sir."

"Well, keep so."

Frank waited.

"I don't hire a man to stand around makin' faces
at the cashier."

"I wasn't—"

"Don't tell me. I got eyes."

Frank's own eyes, being the organs accused, looked guilty.

"The business here is rings," said Stimson. "Brass rings. Not
weddin' rings. You understand?"

A freckled boy swept past and snatched nothing, although Frank
had held the prize within six inches of his hand.

"First thing you know, you'll get fired," Stimson continued. "This
is too much of a good thing."

He marched away. The warning seemed to hang in the
air like smoke after a battlefield discharge.

For the next half hour Frank looked nowhere except at
the ring machine. Lizzie looked nowhere except at the ticket
drawer. Both suffered nobly.

The world, meanwhile, continued its vulgar business. Children laughed. A
woman lost her parasol. The popcorn man shouted of hot
buttered abundance. Gulls drifted above the beach, white and aloof.
The sea struck the sand in great green folds and
withdrew without displaying the slightest concern for prohibited affection.

Frank's misery hardened into resentment. If Lizzie had denied everything
to her father, perhaps she was ashamed of him. Perhaps
the looks had meant nothing. Perhaps she laughed about him
with Jennie Wade, who assisted at a photographic booth two
doors down.

Lizzie, glancing once through the mesh, found Frank staring ferociously
at a wooden dragon. She decided that he had been
frightened by her father. A man whose love could be
dispersed by five feet four inches of whiskered opposition was
no man for her.

They remained separated by thirty feet, forty wooden animals, and
an abyss.

That evening the pavilion closed at ten. The last riders
descended, dizzy and triumphant. The lanterns under the canopy glowed
violet, scarlet, green, and yellow. Workers swept paper tickets into
little drifts. Beyond the boardwalk the beach stretched silver under
a rising moon.

Lizzie came out wearing a dark cape over her red
dress. Jennie Wade waited by the railing.

Jennie had a long, thoughtful face and a heart devoted
to romances in which she herself had no part. She
was twenty-two and had attended so many emotional emergencies that
she possessed the air of a nurse in a hospital
for disappointed princesses.

"You ready?" she asked.

"Yes."

They descended to the sand.

Stimson had gone to argue with an electrician about three
bulbs which, in his judgment, represented theft, sabotage, and the
collapse of modern workmanship. Lizzie knew he would be occupied.
She also knew Frank often walked home along the beach.
These facts, she told herself, had no connection. A maiden
may walk beneath the moon without being accused of strategy.

They had gone two hundred yards when a figure appeared
near the water.

Jennie saw him first. "There's Frank."

"I can see."

"Want to go back?"

"No."

"Want to keep on?"

"I don't care."

This answer furnished Jennie with complete instructions.

Frank approached, kicking at the hard sand. The moon put
a pale edge on his shoulders. He had planned, if
he met Lizzie, to pass her with the frozen courtesy
of a betrayed duke. But Jennie's presence confused the program,
and when he came abreast of them he said, "Evenin',"
in the voice of a grocer.

"Evening," said Jennie.

Lizzie said nothing.

They walked together. Jennie discussed the weather with a desperation
worthy of a cabinet crisis. She mentioned the day's heat,
the probable heat of tomorrow, the possibility of rain, the
opinion of a fisherman regarding wind, and the unreliability of
fishermen.

Frank walked beside her. Lizzie walked on the other side.
Thus Jennie, who had been born to stand between lovers
until the proper instant, occupied the center of a triangle
charged with silent lightning.

At length she stopped.

"My shoe's got sand in it."

She sat on an overturned boat and began to unlace
the shoe with elaborate difficulty.

Lizzie waited.

"You two go ahead," said Jennie. "I'll catch up."

"There's plenty of time," Lizzie replied.

"No, there ain't. This lace is knotted."

The lace was not knotted. Jennie had tied and untied
shoes for eighteen years and could have freed it during
an earthquake. But she lowered her head and entered into
a tremendous struggle.

Frank and Lizzie moved on.

For a little distance neither spoke. The surf came running
obliquely up the beach, flashed around their feet, and slid
back whispering. Far out, a sail was a gray scratch
upon purple water. Along the boardwalk the resort shone in
bands of electricity, its towers and signs floating against the
indigo sky like a kingdom assembled by jewelers and lunatics.

"I s'pose your father told you not to speak to
me," Frank said.

"He told me to mind my business."

"Well, I s'pose I'm not your business."

"You've been acting as if I wasn't."

"I've been acting?"

"You never looked at me once after he talked to
you."

"He said he'd fire me."

"Oh."

The small word crossed the space between them and removed
several fortifications.

Frank continued, "And you looked like you didn't care."

"I had Pa in the cage tellin' me I was
making eyes at you. What did you expect me to
do—wave a flag?"

"I thought maybe you said I'd been bothering you."

"I never."

"He came at me like somebody'd been killed."

"He always does. Yesterday a man gave him a bad
quarter and Pa acted like the government had fallen."

Frank laughed. Lizzie tried not to, failed, and looked down.

Peace came toward them shyly. Peace is suspicious when young
lovers have recently been inventing injuries, for it knows they
may destroy it merely to test its sincerity.

"So you ain't ashamed of me?" Frank asked.

"I never said I was."

"You seemed mad."

"You seemed scared."

"I wasn't scared."

"You looked scared."

"I was thinking."

This claim to intellectual occupation impressed neither of them, but
Lizzie allowed it to pass.

They walked nearer the water. Their hands swung close, separated,
and came close again. Each collision of their fingers was
treated as an accident by two persons who had never
worked harder to achieve a result.

Frank said, "I'd marry you tomorrow if I had a
better job."

Lizzie's breath caught. She gazed out at the moonlit ridges
of foam as if the sea had made the proposal
and required a careful answer.

"I don't care about a better job."

"Your father does."

"I'm not marrying Pa."

This was so reasonable that Frank felt his courage expand.

"I got forty-three dollars saved."

"I've got twenty-eight."

"That makes seventy-one."

They contemplated this fortune. To the experienced it might have
seemed insufficient to support a household through a severe weekend.
To them it was capital, estate, fortress, and fertile province.

"I know a man in Seabright who'd give me work
at the boatyard," Frank said. "Twelve dollars, maybe fourteen."

"Would we go there?"

"If you wanted."

"I'd go anywhere."

The phrase, having been spoken, frightened them by its grandeur.
Their hands met and remained together.

Behind them Jennie sat upon the boat with her shoe
off, staring reverently at their dwindling figures. She was cold,
and a crab had taken an interest in her stockinged
foot, but she would not disturb the sacred interview. She
considered herself the custodian of a tragedy, although at present
everybody concerned appeared intolerably happy.

Frank and Lizzie stopped where the lights of Bluehaven had
grown small. Above them, stars pierced the dark with yellow
points. The sea heaved under an emotion of its own,
ancient and inarticulate. The lovers mistook some part of it
for sympathy. Humanity has always expected the universe to serve
on its private committees.

Frank kissed her.

It was brief, awkward, and decisive.

For the walk back they spoke with the confidence of
monarchs who had just divided the future. They would save
money. They would leave Bluehaven. They would have rooms near
the boatyard, with curtains Lizzie would make. Frank would rise
rapidly. There was no precise explanation of the rising, but
its certainty was beyond arithmetic.

Jennie put on her shoe when she heard them coming.

"Get the knot out?" Lizzie asked.

"Finally," said Jennie, with the exhausted dignity of one who
had strangled a serpent.

For the next week Stimson believed his campaign successful. Lizzie
kept her gaze lowered. Frank attended to the brass rings
with a ferocity which endangered riders' fingers. Their outward discipline
pleased the proprietor. He congratulated himself upon having crushed folly
at its source.

"In this world," he told the popcorn seller, "you got
to have a will."

The popcorn seller, who was deaf in one ear and
bewildered in the other, nodded.

"A will," Stimson repeated, striking the counter. "Otherwise people run
right over you."

The young people were not running over him. They were
arranging to run away from him.

Jennie carried messages. A stable boy named Pete, whose principles
were adjustable for twenty-five cents, promised a fast sorrel mare
and a buggy on Thursday afternoon. Frank wrote to the
boatyard in Seabright. Lizzie packed a small valise and concealed
it beneath old canvas in the photographic booth.

Their plan was simple, and consequently seemed divine.

On Thursday Stimson went across town to purchase a replacement
belt for the organ. He departed at three, after warning
every employee twice and Frank three times. The afternoon was
hot. Heat trembled over the boardwalk. Bathers moved on the
sand like bright insects pinned to a vast yellow cloth.

At twenty minutes past three Lizzie hung the CASHIER sign
crooked.

At twenty-two minutes past three Frank abandoned the ring platform
while the carousel was in motion. A small girl on
a black horse reached vainly for the brass ring and
later remembered this disappointment longer than anyone would have thought
possible.

At twenty-five minutes past three Lizzie and Frank met behind
the photographic booth.

Jennie handed over the valise.

"Be happy," she whispered, already near tears.

"We will," Lizzie said.

"Write to me."

"I will."

Frank shook Jennie's hand as if she were an ambassador
who had secured peace between nations. Then he and Lizzie
hurried through an alley, climbed into the waiting buggy, and
drove south.

The sorrel mare leaped forward. The wheels sang on the
hard road. Lizzie looked back once at the garnet canopy
of the Imperial Galloping Pavilion, at its flags, its lanterns,
its painted army circling beneath the roof. Then buildings shut
it from view.

She took Frank's arm.

They were very happy.

At four ten Stimson returned with the belt.

He noticed first that the cashier's enclosure was empty. He
noticed next that Frank's platform was empty. His mind refused
the relation between these facts. It examined them separately, like
a magistrate unwilling to recognize twins.

"Where's Lizzie?" he demanded.

No one knew.

"Where's Heron?"

The carousel operator pointed uncertainly toward the ring stand, as
if Frank might be hidden inside it.

Jennie emerged from the photographic booth. She tried to assume
the innocent face of a person who has never heard
of horses, buggies, marriage, or the south road.

Stimson saw her.

"Where are they?"

"Who?"

"Don't you 'who' me!"

Jennie retreated.

"Where's my daughter?"

"I—I think—"

"Think where?"

"They went for a ride."

The words fell. For a moment Stimson became still. His
granite will encountered the spectacle of events already accomplished.

"A ride," he repeated.

"Yes, sir."

"With him?"

Jennie nodded.

"Which way?"

She did not answer.

Stimson's roar struck the pavilion. Children turned in their saddles.
Mothers clutched them. The organ continued a cheerful waltz, being
a mechanism and therefore indifferent to paternal ruin.

"Which way?"

"South road."

Stimson ran.

He ran past the popcorn stand, past the shell shop,
past a row of astonished benches. His legs moved with
a speed which surprised his stomach. At the cab rank
he seized the door of a hired carriage.

"You! Driver! Follow 'em!"

The hackman, Mr. Beale, sat asleep beneath a straw hat.
He opened one eye.

"Follow who?"

"Buggy—sorrel horse—young fellow—girl in red! They've gone south. Quick!"

Beale looked at Stimson, at the road, and at his
own horse, an elderly bay named Moses whose highest ambition
was to remain perpendicular.

"How long ago?"

"Maybe an hour. Maybe less. I'll pay you. Git!"

The promise of money entered Beale like military music. He
whipped off his hat, gathered the reins, and cried, "Go
on, Moses!"

Moses began.

The carriage rolled away with a rattle suggesting that every
bolt had been privately opposed to the expedition. Stimson climbed
in while it moved. He stood behind the driver, one
hand gripping the seat, the other pointing south.

"Faster!"

"We ain't flyin' yet," said Beale.

"Whip him!"

"He's goin'."

"He's crawlin'!"

Moses clacked along the road, offended but not transformed. His
hooves struck a dignified four-beat argument against haste. The wheels
hummed; the body wheezed; loose glass in the lamps chattered
with nervous excitement.

The south road ran near the sea. On the left,
dunes lifted their pale backs under coarse grass. On the
right, summer cottages, hotels, and empty lots streamed past. Stimson
scanned every reach of road.

"There!" he shouted.

It was a vegetable wagon.

They overtook it. A farmer raised one finger in greeting.
Stimson glared at him as if vegetables were aiding the
escape.

"Faster!"

Beale slapped the reins. The pursuit had awakened in him
an unsuspected soul. He no longer cared greatly about the
fare. He wanted to catch the buggy. He became a
hunter, a charioteer, an agent of outraged fatherhood.

"Go on, Moses! Show 'em!"

Moses showed them that age has standards.

Several miles ahead, the sorrel mare ran easily. Frank held
the reins. The buggy skimmed between sunlight and shadow, its
wheels throwing little clouds from the road. Lizzie's red sleeve
lay against his dark coat.

"Do you think he knows?" she asked.

"By now."

"Will he follow?"

"He might."

She looked back. The road was empty except for heat.

"I'm afraid."

Frank straightened. "He can't stop us."

This was not legally clear, financially supported, or physically guaranteed,
but it sounded excellent at speed.

The sorrel stretched into a faster trot. Before them the
road ran bright and narrow toward Seabright, toward the boatyard,
the rented rooms, the homemade curtains, the fourteen dollars a
week, and all the magnificent uncertainties which young hope calls
arrangements.

Far behind, Stimson saw a black mark upon the road.

"That's them!"

Beale stood in the carriage. "Might be."

"It's them! Drive!"

The old carriage lunged and swayed. Moses broke briefly into
a gallop, shocked by his own recklessness. The cottages fled
backward. Dust climbed around the wheels. Stimson's whiskers blew apart
in the wind.

The dot ahead became a buggy.

"I'll get 'em," he muttered. "By Jiminy, I'll teach him.
I'll—"

He had no settled conclusion. He imagined seizing Frank by
the collar, overturning the buggy, commanding Lizzie home, dismissing the
young man publicly, perhaps striking him, perhaps invoking law. Each
vision ended with Stimson restored upon his platform, master of
the pavilion, father of his daughter, proprietor of events.

"Hey!" he shouted.

The wind took the call and broke it.

Lizzie turned. She saw the pursuing carriage.

"There he is."

Frank looked back. For an instant his face whitened. Then
the road, the mare, and Lizzie beside him combined to
make a courage that was half love and half terror.

He flicked the reins.

The sorrel bounded forward.

The buggy's wheels began a high, thin song. Lizzie clung
to Frank's arm. Her hat blew loose; she caught it
and laughed. The laugh surprised her. It contained fear, freedom,
and the wild delight of seeing the circular world fall
behind.

"Stop!" Stimson cried.

The distance widened.

"Whip him!" he commanded.

Beale whipped Moses. The old horse galloped with laborious gallantry.
His ribs worked, foam flecked the bit, and each stride
appeared to require consultation with the past. The carriage groaned
like a house dragged from its foundations.

For half a mile the chase held. The young mare
flew; the old horse pursued. The two vehicles rushed beneath
a sky turning slowly from blue to gold. Through gaps
in the dunes the sea flashed steel, then purple. Gulls
hung in the air. Evening spread its calm over the
coast with criminal inattention to Stimson's emergency.

The buggy ahead grew smaller.

Stimson ceased shouting.

He saw Lizzie's red dress, no more than a spark
now. He saw Frank turn once, then face forward. He
saw the young horse's smooth, tireless motion, the light wheels,
the road opening before them.

Something entered his fury and made it heavy.

It occurred to him that he was not merely chasing
a disobedient employee and an ungrateful girl. He was chasing
youth in a hired carriage drawn by Moses.

The perception was dim, unwilling, and profound. Ahead went speed,
love, folly, fresh muscle, confident poverty, and a future which
had not yet revealed its teeth. Behind came possession, experience,
authority, unpaid bills, weak knees, and an old horse doing
his honest best. Stimson felt suddenly that the road itself
favored the fugitives. It poured toward them and away from
him. Every turn delivered them into new country while returning
him to what he already knew.

He remembered Lizzie as a child asleep against his shoulder
after the pavilion closed. He remembered buying her first shoes,
scolding her for going near the surf, carrying her through
a fever. Those memories had seemed to him receipts of
ownership. Now they became merely things that had happened.

"Go on," Beale pleaded to Moses, but without conviction.

The old horse stumbled, recovered, and slowed.

The buggy passed over a rise. For one second Lizzie's
red sleeve burned against the evening. Then it vanished.

Stimson remained standing. Dust drifted around the carriage. The sea
boomed beyond the dunes.

Beale sat down.

"Can't catch that mare," he said gently.

Stimson did not answer.

Moses dropped to a walk, his head low. The defeated
vehicle creaked onward a little way, as if ashamed to
stop before witnesses. There were no witnesses except the gulls,
and they had seen empires fail without comment.

At last Stimson said, "Turn around."

Beale obeyed.

The return journey was slow. Twilight gathered in the hollows.
One by one, lights appeared in cottage windows. Far north,
Bluehaven began to glow—red signs, green globes, golden towers, violet
chains of lamps. The resort looked from a distance like
a miraculous city, though Stimson knew where the paint peeled,
where the wires failed, and where boys crawled under fences.

When they reached the pavilion, the carousel was still revolving.

Jennie stood near the cashier's enclosure. The CASHIER sign remained
crooked. Frank's platform was vacant, and the brass rings continued
to drop into their box without romance or supervision. The
wooden horses rose and fell, rose and fell, forever advancing
and forever returning to the same boards.

Stimson climbed stiffly from the carriage.

"Well?" Jennie asked.

He looked at her with the remnants of imperial severity.
Then he looked beyond her at the dark road to
the south.

"I done what I could," he said.

Jennie lowered her eyes.

Stimson straightened the CASHIER sign. He examined the empty cage,
the money drawer, the ticket rolls. His hands trembled once,
and he put them behind his back.

"This is too much of a good thing," he announced,
but the phrase had lost its artillery.

He walked onto the platform and ordered a boy to
attend to the rings. Then he inspected the organ belt,
rebuked the operator, and complained that the lanterns were wasting
electricity.

These duties comforted him. They belonged to a universe in
which commands still had consequences.

Later, standing beneath the garnet canopy, he watched the painted
horses charge past in their endless campaign. Their riders shouted.
The organ roared. Beyond the pavilion, under the yellow stars,
the sea moved toward the shore and away again.

Stimson reflected that Lizzie was stubborn, that Frank was impudent,
that young people never listened, and that no reasonable father
could be expected to overtake a sorrel mare with a
broken-down hack horse.

At any rate, he decided, he was not responsible.

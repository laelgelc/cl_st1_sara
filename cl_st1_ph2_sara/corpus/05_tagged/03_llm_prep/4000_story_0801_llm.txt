# The Last Flight of the Stars

"I have committed crimes," Flambeau once said to me, "which
were as carefully composed as sonnets, and others which were
merely thrown together like salads. I have crossed a frontier
disguised as a bishop, and crossed another disguised as a
box of biscuits. I once removed a gold cup from
a museum without entering the museum; on another occasion I
entered seven times and removed nothing but the curator's confidence.
Yet the most perfect thing I ever did was also
the last. It was perfect because everybody saw it, everybody
laughed at it, and nobody believed it had happened. Crime,
my friend, is like the theatre: the cheapest seat often
has the best view, but the man in it is
generally looking at his programme."

He added, after a pause unusual in one whose pauses
were generally only opportunities for increasing the audience:

"There was a little priest there who did not look
at the programme."

The house in which this singular confession had its origin
stood near Putney, on the respectable side of a respectable
road, behind a respectable wall over which certain laurels leaned
with the heavy discretion of green eavesdroppers. It had everything
proper to such a house: a brass plate that shone
without vulgarity, a bell that rang without enthusiasm, a gravel
path that rebuked hurried footsteps, and windows whose curtains appeared
to have been trained never to betray a family secret.
Even the smoke went up straight, as if it had
been educated at an expensive school.

It was Boxing Day, and the evening had fallen with
that theatrical magnificence sometimes granted to the suburbs, perhaps in
apology for the rest of the year. Behind the black
chimneys the sunset was a torn banner of crimson and
orange; above it lay a band of poisonous violet; and
higher still the first stars glittered in a greenish sky
like pinholes in a painted scene. A little snow had
fallen at noon and melted everywhere except in the shadow
of walls and bushes. There it remained in white strips,
so that the garden looked as if a gigantic child
had tried to outline it in chalk and then grown
tired.

Into this scene came Father Brown, a small Roman Catholic
priest with a round face, a round hat, a round
umbrella, and an expression that seemed at once to apologize
for existence and to understand it. He was accompanied by
Colonel Adams, whose house it was, a stiff and cheerful
elderly soldier with white moustaches and a manner so abrupt
that his hospitality sounded like a series of commands.

"Come in," said the Colonel. "Family party. Nothing ceremonial. Fischer
here. Girl here. Young fellow here. Canadian relation here. Christmas
nonsense. You'll endure it."

"I have endured Christmas before," said Father Brown mildly. "It
generally survives me."

Colonel Adams made a noise like a gun being cleared
and led him into the drawing-room.

The room was long, warm, and crowded with the amiable
debris of the season. Holly bristled above gilt picture frames;
paper chains drooped across the ceiling; crimson candles stood in
silver branches; and a large Christmas tree occupied one corner
with the air of a green barbarian who had been
captured, decorated, and forced to attend a civilized dinner. Its
glass ornaments flashed whenever the fire leapt, and its wax
angels looked down with that fixed astonishment common to angels
manufactured in Germany.

Near the hearth stood Sir Leopold Fischer, the financier, under
whose touch mines, railways, shipping companies, and governments had been
known to change owners. He was a large man in
a smoking-cap shaped like a Turkish fez, and his grey
beard came to a point so emphatic that it seemed
to be making an argument before he opened his mouth.
He had the smooth hands of one who handles no
tool except the world. In one of them he held
a small black case.

Opposite him stood Ruby Adams, the Colonel's daughter and Fischer's
godchild, a tall, dark-haired young woman whose name would have
seemed too decorative for her had not her face possessed
a warm and serious brightness. Beside her, and a little
too near her for indifference, stood John Crook.

Crook was a lean young man with untidy brown hair,
humorous eyes, and a red tie so red that it
seemed less an article of clothing than an outbreak of
politics. His coat had been brushed but not subdued. There
was something birdlike in the quick turns of his head,
though the bird suggested was one that might steal the
labels from cages rather than the food.

At the far end of the room a broad-shouldered man
with a yellow moustache was trying to balance a paper
crown upon a marble bust. This was James Blount, Mrs.
Adams's brother from Canada, who had arrived in England some
weeks after that lady's death and had been welcomed by
the family with the mournful warmth due to a relative
returning too late. He spoke loudly, laughed loudly, moved largely,
and appeared to regard furniture as an obstacle placed in
houses for the encouragement of athletics.

"Here's the padre!" cried Blount, abandoning the bust and striding
forward. "Now we've got religion as well as finance and
revolution. All we want is the law, and we can
have civilization arrested."

Father Brown looked up at him. Blount was dressed in
a loose brown coat that made him resemble a parcel
of unusual value, badly wrapped. His blue eyes were bright
with boyish energy.

"The law may come later," said the priest.

"Not if it knows we're acting," said Crook. "The law
has always been nervous about competition."

Sir Leopold turned from the fire with a controlled smile.

"My dear John," he said, "your wit is much like
your Socialism. It depends on property supplied by other people."

"And your property," replied Crook, "is much like your wit.
It improves when distributed."

Ruby laughed, though with the faint uneasiness of one who
has heard the same battle fought with different weapons. The
Colonel merely said, "No economics before dinner," in the tone
of a man forbidding artillery practice in the passage.

Sir Leopold opened the black case.

The room changed.

It did not change greatly; the fire still burned, the
paper chains still sagged, and Blount still held the paper
crown. But three white sparks lay suddenly upon blue velvet,
and all the lesser lights in the room seemed to
become servants around them.

They were the diamonds known as the Flying Stars.

Their history had been printed often, generally incorrectly. One had
been cut from a stone found in the mud of
a South African river; one had once belonged to an
Indian prince who lost three kingdoms and retained only his
cook; the third had passed through the hands of a
Russian grand duchess, a Parisian actress, and a Chicago pork
merchant. Sir Leopold had acquired them all by methods which
were entirely legal and therefore far too complicated to explain.
He had named them the Flying Stars because they had
crossed continents, courts, revolutions, and auction rooms with the restless
brilliance of comets.

"And now," he said, looking at Ruby, "they have finished
flying."

He lifted the necklace into the light. The stones were
set simply, with a severity that made their splendour seem
almost insolent.

"For me?" asked Ruby.

"For you," said Fischer. "A Boxing Day gift. I had
intended to wait until your marriage, but as that event
appears uncertain—"

"It is only uncertain to people who think everything can
be quoted on the Stock Exchange," said Crook.

"It is uncertain," replied Fischer, "because I cannot approve of
a young man who announces that property is robbery and
then proposes to marry property."

"I propose to marry Ruby," said Crook. "If you have
incorporated her, I shall inquire into the company."

"You object to wealth."

"I object to wealth when it objects to everyone else."

"You have called me a parasite."

"No," said Crook. "A parasite lives on one creature. You
are more cosmopolitan."

The Colonel's moustaches twitched. Ruby coloured, partly with annoyance and
partly because she was trying not to laugh. Sir Leopold
shut the jewel case with a snap like a small
pistol.

"The difference between us," he said, "is that I know
what money means."

"The difference," said Crook, "is that I know what it
does not mean."

"And what does it not mean?"

"Merit, taste, wisdom, courage, happiness, immortality, or permission to insult
me."

For an instant the financier's face hardened. Then Blount clapped
him heartily on the shoulder.

"Come, Uncle Moneybags," he cried, "don't quarrel with the Red
Indian. It's Christmas. We'll settle the ownership of the world
after pudding. Meanwhile we're going to act."

"I never act," said Sir Leopold.

"My dear sir, you do nothing else," said Crook.

But Blount had already begun unfolding his scheme. He had
discovered, he declared, in an upstairs cupboard a number of
old theatrical costumes, the survivals of some charitable entertainment given
by the late Mrs. Adams. With the aid of curtains,
screens, cardboard, and courage, they would perform a Christmas pantomime.
The plan had the peculiar advantage of being wholly unprepared.

"That," observed Father Brown, "is often the advantage of plans."

Blount announced that he himself would play Harlequin, having discovered
a costume covered with coloured lozenges and imitation gems. Ruby
should be Columbine. Crook, because of his tie and opinions,
must be the Demon of Discontent. The Colonel would play
the Baron, which required no acting. Sir Leopold would be
the Rich Uncle, which required less. Father Brown might be
the Fairy.

"No," said the priest. "I have never been light enough."

"You could be the comic policeman," suggested Crook.

"I have sometimes been comic," said Father Brown, "but never
officially."

At that moment a servant entered with a note for
Blount. He tore it open and roared with delight.

"Better than a policeman! Florian is coming!"

Even Sir Leopold looked interested. Florian was a French actor
whose art had made him fashionable in London drawing-rooms, though
no two Englishmen agreed whether he was a genius or
merely a Frenchman. Blount explained that he had met him
in Montreal—at a supper, on a train, or possibly during
a fire; his anecdotes had a geographical freedom—and had written
begging him to join the amusement if he happened to
be near London.

"He says he may look in late," said Blount. "And
he'll come disguised. That's the fellow! Never enters a room
as himself if he can enter as Napoleon, a nun,
or a Newfoundland dog. We shall have to guess him."

"A French actor disguised as something else," said Crook. "That
should be almost restful."

The party broke up amid noise and laughter to prepare
the theatre. Sir Leopold placed the jewel case in the
inner pocket of his coat.

Father Brown noticed this, not because he was watching the
diamonds, but because he appeared to watch nothing. His eyes
strayed over the Christmas tree, the fire irons, the French
windows, the coloured costume Blount had draped over his arm,
and the paper crown still perched sideways on the marble
head. They rested for a moment on James Blount's face.

The Canadian winked at him.

"You're wondering whether I can act, Father."

"I was wondering whether anyone can stop."

Blount laughed and vanished upstairs.

Within an hour the drawing-room had lost the last remains
of reason. Two folding screens became a palace; a red
curtain became a sunset, a cave, and an outbreak of
fire according to where it was held; three dining chairs,
placed in a row, represented a railway train; and the
Christmas tree, moved to the centre, stood for a forest.
The actors had made themselves monstrous with the perfect innocence
of children.

Colonel Adams wore a military cloak, a cardboard coronet, and
an expression of deep disgrace. Ruby, in white and silver,
had become a Columbine less frivolous than the part but
more beautiful than the convention required. Crook appeared in black
with horns, a scarlet sash, and his own red tie,
which outshone all the official infernal apparatus. Sir Leopold had
declined costume but consented to wear a long robe trimmed
with false ermine. This made him look, as Crook remarked,
exactly like Finance receiving an honorary degree.

James Blount was magnificent.

His Harlequin suit fitted tightly upon a figure unexpectedly active
for so large a man. It was patterned in green,
purple, orange, and blue diamonds, each bordered with silver braid.
Across the breast, belt, cuffs, and cap were sewn dozens
of imitation jewels—glass rubies, paste emeralds, crystal studs, and white
stones that flashed like frost. His face was masked. In
his hand he carried the traditional wooden wand or bat,
painted black and gold.

"There," he shouted, springing upon a chair with the agility
of a grasshopper escaping from a bear. "I'm a rainbow
with a grievance."

"You are more like a jeweller's shop during an earthquake,"
said Crook.

The performance began badly and improved steadily by becoming worse.

There was no plot, unless Blount's intention to create one
may be counted as such. The Baron imprisoned Columbine. The
Demon of Discontent nationalized the prison. The Rich Uncle purchased
it back and raised the rent. Harlequin appeared through a
screen, struck everybody with the wooden bat, and transformed a
footstool into a motorcar by calling it one. Father Brown
sat among the audience beside two elderly ladies from the
next house and watched with solemn attention.

Sir Leopold, entering at the wrong moment, made a speech
about the beneficent responsibilities of capital.

"The rich man," he proclaimed, "is the custodian of civilization."

"Then civilization ought to ask for the key," cried Crook
from behind the Christmas tree.

Blount skipped round Fischer, patting his pockets and pulling out
handkerchiefs, letters, a cigar case, and a silver pencil. The
audience applauded. Sir Leopold protested with ponderous good humour.

"Now, now! Respect private property."

"Harlequin knows no property!" cried Blount. "Everything belongs to the
stage!"

From somewhere inside his costume he produced a long paper
tail and a box of pins.

"The old gentleman has evolved!" shouted Crook. "Capital has at
last developed a conscience, but unfortunately at the wrong end."

While Sir Leopold stood indignantly explaining that the joke was
childish, Blount danced behind him, fumbling at the tails of
his coat. He seemed unable to fix the paper appendage.
He tried one pin, then another; embraced Fischer round the
waist in a grotesque struggle; spun him half round; slapped
both pockets flat; and finally sent him forward with the
tail attached.

The spectators laughed till the paper chains trembled.

Nobody watched Blount's hands because everybody watched Fischer's dignity.

That was the first thing that happened in full view
of everyone.

The second began with the bell.

It rang sharply through the laughter—a real sound, hard and
metallic, cutting across the theatrical noise like a knife through
painted canvas.

The servant had been sent to watch the pantomime with
the rest, and Colonel Adams was about to remove his
crown when the front door opened. A tall man appeared
in the doorway of the drawing-room.

He wore a policeman's uniform.

For perhaps two seconds the spectacle was alarming. The officer
was broad and dark, with a black moustache and a
helmet shining under the hall lamp. His eyes travelled over
the ridiculous scene—the crowned colonel, the horned Socialist, the silver
girl, the millionaire with a paper tail—and fixed themselves upon
Harlequin.

Then Blount flung up both arms.

"Florian!" he shouted. "Splendid!"

The room relaxed into delighted recognition. Of course this was
the celebrated actor. The uniform was too exact not to
be a costume; the gravity was too complete not to
be acting. Florian's genius, everybody remembered at once, consisted precisely
in looking more like a policeman than any policeman would
dare to look.

The newcomer opened his mouth.

"James—"

Harlequin leapt.

It was a beautiful leap, swift, enormous, and absurd. The
coloured giant struck the policeman's wrist with his wand, hooked
one foot behind the man's ankle, and sent him staggering
into the room. The officer recovered and seized him. For
an instant they struggled with a violence so convincing that
the elderly ladies screamed and then applauded themselves for screaming.

"Bravo, Florian!" cried Ruby.

"Arrest the rainbow!" shouted Crook.

The policeman gasped something about a warrant. Blount twisted free,
caught him by the belt, and with a wrestler's turn
flung him across one of the dining chairs. The chair
collapsed. The audience roared. Colonel Adams, recognizing a good throw,
struck his knee with approval.

The officer rose again, furious and perhaps frightened. He drew
a whistle, but Harlequin slapped it from his hand and
forced a small silver flask between his teeth.

"Refreshment for the force!" cried Blount.

The man choked, fought, and swallowed. A moment later his
knees buckled. Harlequin caught him, rolled him dexterously in the
red curtain, tied it with a gold cord, and left
him upon the carpet like a large and rather official
Christmas parcel.

There was prolonged applause.

Blount bowed till the bells on his cap jingled.

"The conquering clown!" cried Crook.

The unconscious policeman moved faintly inside the curtain.

"Good heavens," said one of the elderly ladies, "how wonderfully
he keeps it up."

"That is art," said the other.

The third event occurred almost quietly.

Sir Leopold, who had borne the paper tail longer than
his patience permitted, tore it off and threw it into
the fire. Then, perhaps reminded by Blount's pocket-searching, he put
his hand inside his coat. He put it into the
other pocket. He searched the outer pockets. His face, which
had been red with comic resentment, became pale with genuine
fear.

"The case," he said.

Nobody heard him.

"The jewel case!"

The pantomime stopped. Even Harlequin ceased jingling.

Sir Leopold pulled off the robe, shook it, searched it
again, and stared around the room as if he had
suddenly discovered that the human race was composed entirely of
thieves.

"The Flying Stars are gone."

There are moments when a sentence changes not merely the
mood of a company but the meaning of everything that
has preceded it. The coloured costumes became disguises; the improvised
stage became a trap; every joke became a possible signal;
every burst of laughter became cover for a hand moving
secretly. The paper palace looked suddenly sinister because it was
false, and the glass jewels on the Christmas tree glittered
with a guilty imitation of the real.

Colonel Adams closed the doors.

"Nobody leaves," he said.

Crook laughed once, without amusement.

"At last," he said, "the Baron remembers his part."

Sir Leopold turned upon him.

"You find this entertaining?"

"I find it inevitable. The moment property vanishes, the man
who has criticized property is searched for the crumbs."

"You publicly denounce wealth. You threaten owners. You have every
reason—"

"To steal diamonds?" Crook interrupted. "My dear sir, if I
wanted to discredit Socialism, I should write a book. It
would be safer and more profitable."

"You were beside me."

"So was half the cast."

"You need money."

"Everybody needs money. That is one of the arguments against
it."

Ruby stepped between them, her face white and angry.

"Sir Leopold, you cannot mean—"

"I mean only that the doors must remain closed until
the police arrive."

From the red roll on the floor came a muffled
groan.

All eyes turned toward it.

Blount smacked his forehead.

"Florian! We've forgotten Florian."

He hurried forward, but Father Brown was there before him.
The little priest knelt, loosened the gold cord, and drew
back the curtain from the stranger's face. The man was
deeply unconscious. There was a bruise above his eyebrow and
a bitter medicinal smell upon his breath.

"Magnificent fellow," said Blount, with uneasy admiration. "Won't come out
of character."

Father Brown picked up the fallen whistle and looked at
it.

Then he looked at the uniform: the mud dried along
the boots, the worn leather at the belt, the numbered
collar, the small notebook in the breast pocket.

"This costume," he said, "has been acted in a good
deal."

Colonel Adams bent over him.

"You don't mean—"

"I think," said Father Brown, "that this gentleman really is
a policeman."

For the first time since his entrance, James Blount was
silent.

The discovery produced a sensation stranger than terror. The spectators
looked ashamed, though they did not yet know of what.
They had seen a man assaulted, drugged, and bound; they
had encouraged each movement; and the difference between comedy and
crime had consisted only in a name shouted by another
man.

Crook stared at the unconscious constable.

"So that was not Florian."

"No," said Father Brown. "Florian was an explanation."

"But why did the policeman come?"

"That," said Sir Leopold sharply, "can be discovered when he
wakes. Meanwhile my diamonds—"

"Are the more important because they cannot feel pain," said
Crook.

Sir Leopold swung round.

"And again you defend the criminal."

"No. I attack the arithmetic."

Colonel Adams examined the constable's notebook. Several pages were filled
with addresses and brief official entries. On the last written
page appeared the name "James Blount," followed by the Putney
address.

Every face turned toward Harlequin.

But Harlequin laughed.

It was an excellent laugh—large, surprised, offended, and almost convincing.

"Well, that's rich! Police coming for me? Must be some
Canadian business. Customs, perhaps. Once brought a moose head through
Quebec without declaring the antlers. Or there's another James Blount.
Whole colonies of us over there."

"Do you know the man?" asked the Colonel.

"Never saw him before."

"You attacked him rather promptly," said Father Brown.

"He came dressed for the joke!"

"He came dressed for his profession."

"But I thought he was Florian."

"Why?"

"Because Florian wrote he might come disguised."

Father Brown held out his hand for the note. Blount
felt in one pocket, then another, slapped his coloured sides,
and finally produced a crumpled sheet. The priest read it.

It contained only a brief message: Florian regretted that he
was detained and could not attend.

"That is very curious," Father Brown said.

"What is?"

"You read this aloud as if it said he was
coming."

Blount stared at him, and then burst into another laugh.

"Did I? By thunder, I read it too fast. French
handwriting. Never could make it out."

"It is written in English," said Father Brown.

Sir Leopold's suspicion, once set loose, ran as greedily as
a dog.

"Search him," he said.

Blount's blue eyes hardened behind the mask.

Colonel Adams stepped forward, but Father Brown raised a hand.

"No. Not yet."

"Why not?" demanded Fischer.

"Because if Mr. Blount had wished to steal diamonds, he
would hardly have dressed himself as a jewellery counter."

Crook gave the priest a quick glance.

Sir Leopold snorted.

"You appear anxious to protect everybody."

"No," said Father Brown. "Only to suspect them properly."

He went to the window. Outside, the last colour had
left the western sky, and the garden lay in silver
and black. The moon had risen over the wall. Bare
branches crossed it like cracks in a white plate, while
the laurels massed below them in dark green waves.

"Colonel," he said, without turning, "when did Mr. Blount arrive
from Canada?"

"Early December."

"And Mrs. Adams died when?"

"Last March."

"So she never saw her brother after his return?"

"Obviously not."

"Who in this house had seen James Blount before?"

The Colonel hesitated.

"My wife had not met him for twenty-five years. Ruby
was a child when he emigrated. I had never met
him at all."

"And Sir Leopold?"

"Never," said Fischer.

"Mr. Crook?"

"I have neglected my Canadian relations," said Crook.

Father Brown turned.

James Blount was no longer in the room.

The French windows stood open. A square of moonlit lawn
shone beyond them. One curtain stirred inward in the cold
air like a pale hand beckoning.

Colonel Adams swore briefly and ran forward. Crook followed. Sir
Leopold shouted for the doors to be guarded, forgetting that
the necessary man lay drugged on his carpet. Ruby stood
beside the Christmas tree, silver-clad and motionless, while the imitation
ornaments flashed around her like frozen tears.

But Father Brown had already stepped through the window.

The garden was not large, but moonlight made it mysterious
by removing its measurements. A path curved between yew bushes
and vanished under an arch of bare roses. In summer
those bushes had probably been domestic and dusty; now they
were black towers. The dead rose heads, grey with frost,
hung like the ghosts of small fires. Beyond the wall
the lamps of London made a dull orange haze, but
within the garden all was sharp silver, dark green, and
depthless black.

Father Brown moved without haste. This was partly because he
was not built for speed and partly because he knew
that very large men, when fleeing in harlequin clothes, have
limited opportunities for invisibility.

A coloured shape crossed the path beyond the rose arch.
It moved with astonishing silence. For one instant the glass
stones on the costume caught the moon and vanished.

"Mr. Blount," called Father Brown.

There was no answer.

"Flambeau," he called.

The coloured figure stopped.

The name seemed to alter the garden. Harlequin was gone.
The genial Canadian uncle was gone. In their place stood
one of the most famous criminals in Europe, a man
gigantic in body and imagination, whose robberies had been told
in clubs, courts, prisons, police offices, and wine shops from
Brussels to Barcelona. Flambeau had been many men because he
had never learned to be one. He had climbed like
a cat, fought like a gorilla, lied like a poet,
and escaped like an idea.

He turned slowly.

Even masked, he seemed to smile.

"My little priest," he said in a voice no longer
colonial, "you have spoiled the best exit of my life."

"You spoiled it yourself," said Father Brown, coming nearer. "You
made it too good."

"That is a criticism I have suffered before."

"You could have left quietly. You had a respectable name,
a place in the family, and plenty of time. Instead
you had to make the police applaud their own defeat,
though there happened to be no police present to applaud
it."

"There was one."

"He was unable to applaud."

"The audience did it for him."

Behind them came faint shouts from the house. Lantern light
bobbed near the windows. Flambeau glanced toward the wall.

"You cannot stop me," he said.

"No," replied Father Brown. "I am not physically arranged for
stopping you."

"And you have no weapon."

"I have one or two words. They are sometimes blunt
enough."

Flambeau laughed softly.

"You know the trick, then?"

"I think so. The strange part is that the robbery
was easier than the comedy."

"It was all comedy."

"That is what made it nearly tragic."

The priest stood beneath the arch of dead roses, his
round face colourless in the moonlight. Beyond him the harlequin
costume glittered with many little false fires.

"You took the diamonds," Father Brown said, "when you fastened
the paper tail to Sir Leopold's coat. Everybody saw you
search his pockets because everybody believed you were pretending to
search them. You embraced him, spun him, slapped his coat,
and worked at the pin. Your fingers went into the
inner pocket, and the case went into your hand."

Flambeau bowed.

"A clumsy description of a graceful movement, but substantially just."

"You had to dispose of the stones at once. A
jewel case makes an ugly bulge in a tight costume,
so you emptied it. Then you set the diamonds among
the false stones sewn over your breast and belt. The
safest place for three famous jewels was in the middle
of fifty worthless ones. If you hide a white sheep
among black sheep, it is noticed; if you hide a
pearl in a coal scuttle, it may be found; but
if you hide a real lie among a thousand jokes,
men will laugh past it."

"You have an instinct for my profession," said Flambeau.

"I have an instinct for sin. It is a more
crowded profession."

The giant made an impatient movement.

"And the policeman?"

"You knew he was coming. Perhaps you had been followed.
Perhaps you intercepted a message. You had already invited Florian,
or invented the invitation; when the actor declined, you read
the refusal as an acceptance. Thus you prepared everybody to
see a disguised performer. When the real officer arrived in
his real uniform, the truth looked exactly like a joke."

Flambeau spread his hands.

"The highest art is not to imitate reality but to
persuade reality to imitate art."

"And then you attacked him before he could speak your
name."

"He did speak it."

"He began to. But James Blount was the name he
had come to arrest. The family heard only what they
expected to hear."

"I admit," said Flambeau, "that the chair broke more loudly
than I intended."

"You struck him, threw him, drugged him, and tied him
up. Every act was criminal; every witness saw it; every
witness approved it. You had put a label marked 'pantomime'
on violence, and respectable people accepted the label as evidence
against their eyes."

"That was the pretty point."

"It was the ugly point."

For the first time Flambeau's huge frame became still.

Father Brown continued in the same mild voice.

"You were nearly safe after that. The policeman could not
expose you. Sir Leopold's diamonds were believed to have vanished
mysteriously. Suspicion would go where social suspicion always goes—not toward
the cheerful relative who organized the entertainment, but toward the
angry poor man who had denounced wealth."

"Crook is not poor."

"He is poor enough to be suspected by a millionaire
and rich enough to wish to marry his goddaughter."

"Crook advertised his hatred of property."

"Yes," said Father Brown. "That is why he did not
steal the diamonds."

Flambeau tilted his masked head.

"That sounds like one of your little paradoxes."

"It is only common sense wearing its hat backwards. A
man planning a theft does not generally ring a bell
before it. Mr. Crook may be vain of being a
Socialist, but he is not so vain as to make
himself the first suspect in a robbery. His opinions are
noisy because they are honest. Secret crimes grow best under
respectable silence."

"You guessed me because he talked too much?"

"I began by doubting him because he talked too much.
Then I doubted you because no one knew enough. A
family relation is usually the person most easily identified. You
had chosen a family in which the one woman who
could identify James Blount was dead. You arrived after her
death, wearing old memories as another man wears old clothes.
The Colonel had never seen his brother-in-law. Ruby remembered only
a huge laughing uncle from childhood; any huge laughing man
could fill that shadow. Grief prepared the house for you.
Good manners kept it from asking questions."

Flambeau's hand moved toward one of the white stones on
his belt.

"So you have it all. Why talk? Call your friends
and take the Stars."

"I cannot take them."

"Then I shall keep them."

"You can."

The answer appeared to disturb him more than a threat.

From the lawn came Colonel Adams's voice, calling the priest's
name. Father Brown did not respond. Flambeau looked again toward
the wall, judging its height.

"You have perhaps thirty seconds," said the priest. "You could
be over that wall in ten. You could cross the
river, change clothes, reach a station, and be another man
by morning. I know that. You know that. The only
question is what sort of man."

Flambeau's laughter was low and dangerous.

"A wealthy one."

"No. A poorer one."

"With three diamonds worth a king's ransom?"

"A king's ransom is what a king pays to be
free. You would be paying it to remain a slave."

The giant's shoulders shifted uneasily. The moon glittered on his
patchwork suit. He looked like some ancient carnival spirit halted
at the edge of a graveyard.

Father Brown spoke more quickly now, but still without loudness.

"You have thought yourself an outlaw, Flambeau. There is something
almost innocent in the young outlaw. He sleeps under hedges,
laughs at locks, risks his neck, and gives policemen stories
to tell their children. He may be wicked, but he
is not yet small. He imagines himself free because he
has broken rules.

"But crime does not remain romantic. Nothing evil remains romantic.
It grows efficient. It begins with a leap over a
wall and ends with a little key turning in a
little room. It begins with masks and moonlight and ends
with account books, informers, stale rooms, suspicious eyes, and men
paid to do what you once dared to do yourself.
You will stop climbing because your knees hurt. You will
stop laughing because laughter betrays the teeth. You will send
boys into danger while you sit behind a curtain. At
last you will not steal for adventure, or beauty, or
even appetite. You will steal because you are afraid not
to steal."

Flambeau said nothing.

"You call yourself free," continued Father Brown, "but already you
are obeying your crime. Tonight you had to hurt a
man who was only doing his duty. You drugged him
before people who mistook cruelty for comedy. Perhaps you did
not intend to kill him. Next time you will intend
less and do more. The road bends that way.

"And you have done something meaner than striking a policeman.
You have used another man's honesty as your disguise. Crook's
foolish red tie, his foolish speeches, his quarrel with a
rich man—all these were convenient pieces of scenery to you.
You knew suspicion would fall on him. You knew Sir
Leopold would think: 'He attacks wealth, therefore he steals jewels.'
You let an honourable man's convictions become evidence for a
crime he did not commit."

Flambeau shifted again.

"The young man can defend himself."

"Not against everything. A man can defend his name in
a court; he cannot defend it so easily in the
heart of the woman he loves. A doubt need only
be very small to poison happiness. Ruby may speak bravely
for him tonight and wonder tomorrow. Sir Leopold may never
prove anything and never cease hinting it. You have stolen
more than diamonds. You have stolen trust and hidden it
where no search warrant can find it."

The shouts were closer now. A lantern glowed behind the
yew hedge.

Flambeau pulled off his mask.

His face was broad, powerful, and unexpectedly weary. It was
the face of a man who had spent years treating
the world as a stage and had suddenly noticed the
empty seats.

"You preach well," he said.

"I preach badly. That is why I use short words."

"You believe I shall become what you describe?"

"I believe every road goes somewhere. You cannot walk south
forever and call it north because you started singing."

Flambeau looked down at the gems on his costume. The
paste stones burned in blue, green, and scarlet; among them
three cold white fires held the moon as if it
were trapped inside ice.

"I made them fly," he murmured.

"No," said Father Brown. "They made you run."

The giant gave a harsh little laugh.

"That is unfair."

"Morality often feels unfair to a man who has been
cheating."

"What do you want?"

"The diamonds."

"And then?"

"Then I shall tell them the thief escaped."

"You would let me go?"

"I could hardly prevent it."

"That is not an answer."

Father Brown's face grew grave.

"I do not want to catch you. I want you
to stop."

For a moment neither moved. The garden seemed to wait
with them. The moonlight lay upon the dead roses; the
bare trees raised their branching hands; and the distant city
murmured beyond the wall like an ocean of human mistakes.

Then Flambeau began plucking stones from his costume.

The first diamond came from the silver border across his
chest. The second had been fastened among glass crystals at
his belt. The third he detached from the cap, where
it had shone above his forehead like a frozen thought.

He held them in his enormous palm.

"How absurdly small they are," he said.

"Most things are, when one has sinned for them."

Flambeau placed the three stones in Father Brown's hand. Against
the priest's short fingers they looked alive, restless, and extraordinarily
pure.

The lantern light brightened beyond the hedge.

"Goodbye, Father."

"Not goodbye," said Father Brown.

Flambeau smiled.

"You expect to see me in prison?"

"I expect to see you somewhere more difficult."

"And where is that?"

"Among honest men."

Flambeau stared at him; then the old flamboyant humour returned,
but changed and softened, like a tune remembered from childhood.

"That will require my finest disguise."

"It may require your face."

With one swift movement the giant sprang upon a stone
bench, caught the top of the wall, and swung himself
upward. For an instant he stood balanced against the moon—a
gigantic Harlequin in a blaze of false jewels, poised between
the respectable garden and the unknown dark. Then he dropped
beyond the wall and disappeared.

It was the last crime of Flambeau.

Colonel Adams broke through the rose arch with Crook at
his heels. Behind them came Sir Leopold, puffing in his
false ermine robe, and two servants carrying lanterns. They found
Father Brown alone beneath the dead roses, examining three diamonds
in his hand.

Sir Leopold uttered a cry so sharp that it might
have cut glass.

"The Stars!"

He seized them, held them to the lantern, counted them
twice, and pressed them into his palm.

"Where is the thief?"

"Gone," said Father Brown.

"Who was he?"

"Not Mr. Crook."

Crook's thin face relaxed, but he said nothing. Sir Leopold
stared at the priest.

"You allowed him to escape?"

"I was not consulted."

"Which way?"

Father Brown pointed toward the wall.

Colonel Adams sent the servants running toward the gate and
hurried after them. Crook remained.

"Was it Blount?" he asked quietly.

"It was a man who had been Blount for some
weeks," said the priest. "He may be somebody else tomorrow."

Crook looked toward the wall.

"And the diamonds?"

"They were hidden in the obvious place."

"Which is never obvious until it is empty."

Father Brown smiled.

They returned to the drawing-room. The unconscious policeman had begun
to stir and was being attended by Ruby, who knelt
beside him with water and bandages. When Crook entered, she
rose at once and crossed the room. She did not
ask him whether he had taken the diamonds. She only
put both hands into his.

Sir Leopold observed this with some displeasure, but his recovered
jewels had restored enough of his benevolence to make displeasure
almost majestic.

"My dear Ruby," he said, "the danger is over."

"For whom?" asked Crook.

"For everyone."

"The policeman may have another opinion."

The constable groaned and opened one eye.

"Got him?" he muttered.

"No," said Father Brown.

The officer looked at the little priest, then at the
wrecked chair, the red curtain, the crowned Colonel returning empty-handed,
and the assembled company still in pantomime costume.

"You lot," he said faintly, "stood there laughing."

Nobody answered.

It is hard to apologize for a mistake when the
mistake consists of believing one is amused.

Sir Leopold coughed and slipped the Flying Stars back into
their case.

"I shall place these in the bank," he declared.

Ruby released one of Crook's hands.

"They were a gift to me."

"Certainly, certainly. But after this experience—"

"If they are mine, I may choose where they go."

"And where do you propose to put them?"

Ruby looked at the pale constable, the broken chair, the
paper palace, and the false jewels glittering on the costume
Flambeau had discarded near the window.

"I think," she said, "they had better be sold."

Sir Leopold nearly dropped the case.

"Sold!"

"For the hospital."

Crook's eyes brightened.

"Ah," he said. "The first successful flight of the Flying
Stars."

"My dear child," protested Fischer, "their historic associations—their unique value—the
prestige—"

"Will produce a better price," said Ruby.

"This is Crook's influence."

"No," said Crook. "I should have proposed nationalizing them and
returning the receipt."

Ruby laughed; and because laughter had been made ugly that
night, this honest little laugh seemed to cleanse the room.

Sir Leopold turned for support to Father Brown.

"You, Father, at least understand that valuable objects cannot be
treated as if ownership were a joke."

"Ownership is not a joke," said Father Brown. "That is
why gifts should belong to the people who receive them."

The financier frowned, but the diamonds were safe, the thief
was gone, and his goddaughter's rebellion might still be attributed
to the excitement of the evening. He therefore settled back
into his habitual view of the universe, in which his
judgments were pillars and contrary facts were only seasonal decorations.

The real police arrived soon afterward. They searched the garden,
questioned the household, examined the wall, and discovered several things
Father Brown already knew and several things that did not
matter. The revived constable gave a confused but forceful account
of his mission. A warrant had indeed been issued for
a man believed to be the notorious Flambeau, who had
entered England under the name of James Blount. Information had
led him to the house; he had expected resistance, though
not applause.

The false uncle's rooms yielded a Canadian passport of admirable
workmanship, two wigs, a coil of silk rope, several foreign
banknotes, and a book of devotional poetry. The last item
puzzled the police more than the rest.

Colonel Adams apologized to the constable with military completeness. The
elderly ladies apologized with tears. Crook apologized by helping to
carry him to a sofa. Sir Leopold offered him money,
which the man refused. Ruby gave him tea, which he
accepted.

Near midnight the company began to disperse. The cardboard crown
lay crushed under a chair. The paper chains hung crookedly.
The Christmas tree had been returned to its corner, but
it no longer looked like a captured barbarian; it looked
like a witness resolved to say nothing.

At the front door Sir Leopold shook Father Brown's hand.

"You have been most helpful," he said. "Most helpful indeed,
though I confess I cannot follow all your conclusions. You
clergy live so much outside practical affairs. You understand sin
in the abstract, no doubt, but the modern criminal world
requires knowledge of men, money, travel, politics, disguise, psychology—things one
can hardly learn in a confessional."

Father Brown buttoned his shabby coat.

"No," he said. "Hardly."

Sir Leopold nodded, pleased to find agreement.

"A clever fellow, this Flambeau. Very nearly deceived us all."

"Very nearly," said Father Brown.

"And yet, after all, a fellow like that must be
fundamentally ignorant of respectable society."

"Yes," said the priest. "That was why he chose it."

Sir Leopold did not hear the answer, being occupied with
locking the jewel case into an inner pocket and then
checking the pocket twice.

Father Brown stepped out into the cold. Behind him the
rich man closed the door of the respectable house, under
the respectable laurels, on the respectable side of the wall.

On the other side of that wall, somewhere beneath the
winter moon, a great man in a harlequin suit was
walking away from crime; and the little priest, whom Sir
Leopold considered ignorant of the world, knew that this was
the most difficult escape Flambeau had ever attempted.

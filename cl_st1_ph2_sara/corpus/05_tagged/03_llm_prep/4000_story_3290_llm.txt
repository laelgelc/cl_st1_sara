The kitchen at Lower Torworthy was built, like the house
itself, of granite gathered from the skirts of the moor—great
stubborn stones whose asperities the whitewash had softened but never
wholly concealed. The floor was lime-ash, cracked into continents and
islands by the tread of six generations; there was a
deep ingle with a peat fire biddicking red under a
black pot; and from the rafters hung sides of bacon,
ropes of onions, bundles of thyme, and one venerable rush
basket which nobody used and nobody had ever thought to
remove. In the low western window, where the last rain
still pearled the little panes, stood three geraniums in chipped
blue bowls, so violently scarlet that they seemed to have
captured all the colour denied to Dartmoor in November.

It was there, while the rain drew grey threads across
the yard and the kettle muttered on the crook, that
Farmer Endacott's sister told me the story.

She had been paring apples, turning each fruit steadily against
the knife, and the peel fell in long red spirals
into her lap. When she came to the curious part
she stopped her hand and looked toward the narrow stair
that went up beside the bread oven.

"They hid him above there," she said.

"In the apple-loft?"

"Garret, 'twas then. Apples one side, fleeces t'other, and a
plank laid over two meal-chests for a bed. My grandmother
slept below with a carving-knife under her pillow, though what
good she supposed that would do against the King's soldiers
I can't tell you. Grandfather had the gun, but he
said if he loaded it he might be fool enough
to fire it, and if he didn't load it no
one could hang him for murder. He was a reasoning
man, Grandfather, except when he was joking."

She put another apple in her hand.

"You'll have heard of the American prisoners at Princetown?"

I had, but she told me nevertheless, as storytellers properly
do.

There were thousands of them on the moor during the
wars—sailors, privateersmen and boys caught in vessels not much larger
than fishing boats—shut behind high walls in a place where
winter began in September and sometimes had not ended by
June. The prison stood bare against the sky, with the
tors watching it. Snow drove under doors, rain entered by
the slates, and the mist could swallow wall, sentry and
road together. Some prisoners carved bone toys, straw boxes and
little portraits, selling them through such persons as were allowed
to trade at the gate. Others plotted escape, though the
country round was almost another prison: open ground, few trees,
houses visible from distant heights, and every road known to
every shepherd.

"My grandmother's name was Grace Endacott," the farmer's sister continued,
"and she had no more fear in her than a
warming-pan. Not that she went looking for danger. 'Twas worse
than that. Danger would come by, and she'd take pity
on it."

Grace had driven to Princetown on a Thursday in early
spring to deliver eggs and two cheeses to the wife
of an assistant warder. She went in the little moor
cart behind a rough-coated pony named Emperor, who possessed neither
the stature nor the manners his name suggested. The warder's
wife had also promised her an empty rabbit hutch, made
by one of the prisoners and paid for with a
pound of butter. Grace intended to breed silver-grey rabbits in
it, though her husband Abel had said rabbits ate more
than they were worth and there were enough of them
loose on Dartmoor without housing them like gentlefolk.

The hutch was waiting near the traders' entrance, a stout
oblong thing of wooden slats, with a boarded sleeping compartment
at one end and wire at the other. It was
larger than Grace expected.

"Two rabbits?" she said. "Why, they could hold chapel in
there."

The warder's wife laughed and went back indoors for sacking
to cover it against the rain. Grace remained by the
cart, steadying Emperor, who had found a tuft of sour
grass and was pulling at it with determination.

Then someone whispered, "Ma'am."

She looked round.

There was nobody in the yard except a lame sweeper
crossing near the wall, two soldiers by the inner gate,
and a young man standing where the shadow of a
shed met the shadow of the wall. He wore prison
clothes of a dun colour, with a cap pulled down
over his brow. His face was thin, and though he
had not moved, his breast rose and fell as if
he had been running.

"Ma'am," he whispered again. "For God's sake."

Grace ought to have called the sentry. She said so
afterward. A prisoner loose inside the outer yard might have
attacked her, stolen the cart or brought ruin on the
household. She knew all this in the space between one
beat of the heart and the next.

Then she saw how young he was.

"What have you done?" she asked.

"Got this far."

"That's plain."

"They're changing guard. I slipped under a wagon. They'll miss
me directly."

He glanced at the soldiers. One had turned his head.
The young man flattened himself against the shed.

"I won't hurt you," he said. "Only hide me one
minute. One minute, ma'am."

Now the hutch stood with its boarded end open, and
the warder's wife was still indoors looking for sacking. Grace
did not make a plan. She lifted the small door.

"In," she said.

He stared at her.

"Mun get in, then! You've no time to grow smaller."

He doubled himself with a sailor's agility and crept into
the hutch. Grace shut the door, threw an old horse
rug over the boarded end and climbed into the cart
just as the warder's wife returned with two pieces of
sacking.

"There," said that good woman. "Cover the wire, Mrs Endacott,
else the wet'll warp it."

Together they spread the sacking. The soldiers opened the outer
gate. One came to help lift the hutch more securely
across the back of the cart, and Grace afterward declared
that the American's head must have been within six inches
of the soldier's hand.

"Heavy build," the soldier remarked.

"Prison work," said the warder's wife. "They make things to
last."

Emperor set off. At the gate a sentry thrust his
bayonet into a bundle of straw beneath the seat, examined
the empty egg baskets, and scratched the pony between the
ears.

"Taking home a menagerie, missus?"

"Only a hutch."

"Where's the rabbit?"

"Not catched him yet."

The sentry grinned and waved her through.

Grace drove at a sober pace until a turn of
the road hid the walls. Then, from under the horse
rug, came a muffled voice.

"Ma'am, if you don't let me breathe soon, there won't
be any call to hunt me."

She stopped beside a bank of yellow grass. The young
man pushed up the rug, white-faced and streaming with sweat.
He unfolded himself by degrees, like a carpenter's rule.

"What be your name?" Grace asked.

"Elias Ward. Baltimore."

"That your name or where you come from?"

"Both together, near enough."

"How old?"

"Twenty-two."

"You look seventeen."

"I was seventeen when I entered that prison. At least,
it feels so."

"You've been there five years?"

"Fifteen months."

He laughed, but not as a man laughs at anything
amusing. Then he looked back toward Princetown, and the laugh
died.

Grace told him he might jump down at the next
hollow and take his chance. She told him her husband
would never consent to hiding a prisoner; that there were
children at home; that harbouring him might cost them the
farm and perhaps transportation besides. Elias listened with his hands
clasped on the hutch.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

"And if I carry you farther, you'll do exactly as
I bid?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"You'll not show your nose from under that rug?"

"No, ma'am."

"You'll lie still, though your bones crack in two?"

"I'll lie still if they crack in forty."

So she carried him home.

Abel Endacott was in the linhay mending a gate hinge
when the cart came in. He noticed first that his
wife drove too fast, next that the hutch was bigger
than any sensible rabbit required, and last that a hand
appeared for one instant beneath the horse rug and withdrew.

He laid down his hammer.

"Grace," he said, "what hast brought?"

"Rabbit hutch."

"I can see what thee hast brought outside."

She glanced toward the lane.

"Come shut the yard gate."

He shut it, not hurriedly, for Abel never did anything
hurriedly when haste might be observed. Then Grace lifted the
rug, and Elias looked out.

For perhaps ten seconds Abel said nothing.

At last he asked, "Do rabbits in America commonly wear
boots?"

"Abel—"

"I only want to know what sort of feeding this
breed'll take."

The young man crawled painfully from the hutch and stood
before him.

"Sir," he began, "your wife saved my life."

"Ah. And now she's having a try at ruining mine."

"Abel!"

"I don't blame you, lad. I blame her, and I'll
attend to that private." He considered Elias from head to
foot. "Can you climb?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then climb. Grace, fetch bread and bacon. Not the good
ham. A fugitive can't taste the difference."

But Grace brought the good ham nevertheless.

The garret stair rose from the kitchen and turned sharply
under the eaves. At the top was a door so
low that Abel had to bow his head. Beyond it
lay wool, apple trays, sacks of oats, two broken chairs,
a spinning wheel without a spindle and a small gable
window overlooking the yard. They made Elias a bed behind
the fleeces. Grace carried up a basin of hot water,
old clothes belonging to Abel's younger brother, and enough food
for three men.

"You bide close," Abel said. "There's windows on the tors
better than eyes. If a search party comes, get under
the wool and don't sneeze."

Elias promised.

That evening news came that an American had escaped. A
soldier visited the neighbouring farms, and two mounted men went
along the Princetown road while the light was still up.
Abel stood by his gate discussing sheep with them. Grace
churned butter in the dairy, though there was nothing in
the churn but cold water. Elias lay under the wool
in the garret and heard the horses pass.

At supper the Endacotts spoke in whispers.

"We must get him Plymouth way," said Grace. "Once among
ships—"

"Every road'll be watched."

"Then across the moor."

"He doesn't know the moor."

"Thou could guide him."

"And leave thee to tell soldiers I've gone visiting after
midnight? No. Let the first search spend itself. Two nights,
perhaps three. Then we'll find him clothes and send word
by Cousin Tom."

From above came the faint creak of a board.

"He'll go mad in that garret," Grace said.

"He's been fifteen months in prison. He can last two
days under a roof that doesn't leak."

That, you may suppose, settled the matter. But next morning
Abel found that Elias's confinement in the house brought a
difficulty he had not anticipated: he himself could not bear
to remain indoors. If he sat by the window, Grace
told him he made the place look watched. If he
walked in the yard, she said he was drawing attention.
In the end he took a hoe and went to
a patch of last year's potato ground beside the road,
where there was nothing whatever to hoe.

"A new kind of agriculture," his sister said when she
told it. "Hoeing stubble and staring at soldiers."

The road from Tavistock to Princetown ran within sight of
the field. All morning people passed—farmers, prison carts, two red-coated
patrols, a constable on a bay mare, and several men
who had attached themselves to the hunt in hope of
reward. Abel leaned on his hoe and counted them. He
also kept an eye on the old scarecrow in the
adjoining field, for beyond it a footpath crossed toward the
farm, and he feared searchers might come that way.

Near noon Nathan Nathaniel appeared.

Nathan was a traveling dealer in clothes, hats, boots, buttons,
ribbons, pins and whatever else might be bought cheaply in
one village and sold less cheaply in another. He came
over the moor twice a year with a stout pony
carrying two leather bags and a wooden case strapped across
its back. He had traded at Lower Torworthy often enough
to know Abel and Grace by name, though his affection
for any household rose and fell according to whether the
inhabitants were buying or selling.

He was a small, quick man in a long brown
coat, with bright eyes and whiskers neatly clipped. Three hats
were tied to the top of his pack, and another
sat squarely on his head.

"Good day, Mr Endacott," he called.

Abel nodded.

"Any old garments?" Nathan asked. "Any boots, waistcoats, coats gone
shiny at the elbows? I offer honest prices."

"Nothing today."

"Mrs Endacott told me last autumn there was a blue
coat."

"Mrs Endacott tells many things in autumn."

Nathan dismounted and adjusted the pony's girth. Then he nodded
toward the road.

"They are hunting your escaped American."

"So I hear."

"Dangerous fellow, perhaps."

"Most likely."

"They say he has no hat and poor English."

Now Abel had been anxious all morning, and anxiety in
some men produces prayer, in some silence, and in Abel
a regrettable desire to make mischief. He pushed his own
hat back, widened his eyes, and said in the broadest
American voice he could contrive:

"Say, stranger, which road goes to Plymouth City?"

Nathan froze.

Abel, encouraged by this success, looked over his shoulder and
lowered his voice.

"Don't tell the British, mister. I calculate I'll give 'em
the slip."

Nathan's mouth opened. His eyes went to Abel's muddy high-lows,
his rough coat, the hoe in his hand and the
road beyond. Evidently he decided that the supposed prisoner had
stolen a farmer's clothes and was pretending to work.

"Indeed," Nathan said carefully. "Plymouth, you say?"

"Where the ships lie."

"You have escaped from Princetown?"

Abel winked.

That wink cost him an afternoon, five guineas, and much
wounded dignity.

Nathan backed toward his pony.

"I will not betray you," he said. "Stay here. I
know a path."

"Obliged."

"Do not move."

"No, sir."

Nathan mounted and rode away at a trot. Abel watched
him go, laughed to himself and resumed his observation of
the road.

Not twenty minutes later six soldiers came round the bend,
Nathan riding among them and pointing. Their sergeant shouted. Abel
looked behind him, thinking for one foolish instant that the
true American must have left the house. Then the soldiers
spread out and ran into the field.

"Halt!"

"I be halted."

"Drop that weapon!"

Abel examined the hoe.

"This?"

"Drop it!"

He let it fall.

Nathan remained by the road, breathing hard but triumphant.

"That is the man," he called. "He confessed to me.
He asked the way to Plymouth."

The sergeant seized Abel's arm.

"Name?"

"Abel Endacott."

"That won't serve."

"Served me forty-seven year."

"Where did you get those clothes?"

"My wife bought the cloth and made 'em. If you
want the pattern, ask her civil."

The younger soldiers began to grin, but the sergeant had
been promised an escaped American and was unwilling to surrender
him.

"Speak again."

"What for?"

"Speak!"

Abel understood. His sense of humour, being offended by arrest,
deserted him completely.

"I've naught to say."

Nathan cried, "He spoke like an American only now!"

"Then let him speak like one," said the sergeant.

"I won't."

This refusal settled the question against him. A guilty man,
they reasoned, would naturally refuse to provide the very proof
that must convict him. They tied Abel's wrists and marched
him toward Princetown.

Grace saw them from the dairy window.

She told afterward that her knees failed beneath her. She
thought Abel had confessed; that soldiers were even then turning
toward the house; that Elias would be found in the
garret and all of them taken. She ran to the
kitchen, where the eldest child was feeding peat to the
fire.

"Upstairs," she said. "Tell him soldiers have thy father. He
mun stay hid whatever happens."

Elias came halfway down before the message was finished.

"I'll give myself up."

"You'll do no such thing."

"They've taken him for helping me."

"We don't know why they've taken him."

"I can't let him pay for me."

Grace stood at the foot of the stair and looked
up.

"If Abel Endacott be in trouble for helping thee, walking
into the road won't save him. It'll only prove the
trouble. Back up."

Elias gripped the rail.

"Ma'am—"

"Back up, or I'll take the broom to thee."

He went.

Meanwhile Abel was marched through Princetown with a soldier at
either side and Nathan following. Villagers came to their doors.
A dog barked. Two women stared from the baker's. Abel,
who had never in his life been taken anywhere against
his will, became more offended at every step.

At the prison gate the sentry looked at him, looked
again and hid a smile behind his hand.

"What's this?"

"Escaped American," said the sergeant.

"That so?"

"Taken near Lower Torworthy."

The sentry knew Abel well, having bought cider from him
the previous Michaelmas, but military amusement is not always military
mercy.

"Dangerous-looking rascal," he said. "Best take him to Captain Sharpland."

Captain Sharpland, the governor, sat in a stone office writing
beneath a window that gave little light and less warmth.
He was a lean, exact gentleman who wore an eyeglass
on a black ribbon. At the entrance of the party
he set the glass into his eye and regarded Abel.

"Endacott," he said, "why are you under arrest?"

"That's what I've walked three mile to learn."

The sergeant cleared his throat.

"This man was denounced by Mr Nathaniel, sir, as the
prisoner missing from the fourth block."

Captain Sharpland looked at Nathan. Then at Abel. Then again
at Nathan.

"You believe this is an American sailor?"

"He admitted it," Nathan said. "He asked me the road
to Plymouth in an American accent. He said he had
escaped. I acted from public duty."

"Did you indeed?"

"I brought the soldiers immediately."

Captain Sharpland removed his eyeglass.

"Endacott has supplied this prison with turnips for seven years."

"Eight," Abel said.

"He was born at Lower Torworthy."

"In the room over the dairy."

"His father held the farm before him."

"And's buried at Walkhampton, beside Mother."

"Yes, Endacott, that will do." The governor turned to Nathan.
"Did you examine the man before denouncing him?"

"He confessed."

"What precisely did he say?"

Abel repeated the exchange, including his wink, though with increasing
reluctance. Captain Sharpland listened without changing expression. The sergeant looked
at the ceiling. One soldier coughed into his sleeve.

When Abel finished, the governor said, "A childish joke."

"Ess," Abel admitted.

"And a credulous response."

Nathan coloured. "I did what any loyal subject would do."

"A loyal subject might also identify a neighbour of eight
years."

"I am not a neighbour. I travel."

"So I perceive."

Nathan protested that the reward notice promised payment for information
leading to capture. Captain Sharpland replied that it did not
promise payment for abducting respectable farmers. Abel then said he
had lost half a day's work, though there had been
no work in the field to lose, and that he
had been humiliated before the whole village.

"You began the deception," said the governor.

"A man can't be arrested every time he talks foolish,
else you'd need a prison big as Dartmoor."

That answer nearly betrayed Captain Sharpland into a smile.

In the end he ordered Abel released, reprimanded the sergeant
for insufficient inquiry, reprimanded Abel for provoking public officers in
a time of alarm, and required Nathan to pay five
guineas compensation.

"Five!" Nathan cried.

"You may prefer the magistrates."

Nathan did not prefer them.

He counted the money onto the governor's desk as if
each guinea were a piece of his own flesh. Abel
put the coins into his pocket, but his victory gave
him less satisfaction than it ought. Captain Sharpland had treated
him fairly, and Abel knew all the while that the
true escaped man lay under wool in his garret.

At the outer gate the sergeant overtook Nathan.

The soldiers had been running half the morning, had crossed
two wet fields, and had been laughed at by their
comrades. Nathan's complaint that he too had suffered did not
improve their temper. What occurred on the road beyond the
last houses was afterward described by Abel as "a pleasant
little revenge," though Nathan would not have chosen those words.
The soldiers chased him round his pony, knocked off his
hat, emptied one bag into a ditch and applied the
flat of a belt to the rear of his brown
coat. He escaped only by scrambling into the saddle and
galloping into the mist with ribbons, stockings and two pairs
of secondhand breeches streaming behind him.

Abel came home near dimmety.

Grace met him at the gate.

"What's happened?"

"I've earned five guineas."

"Where's soldiers?"

"Gone."

"Do they know?"

"Know what?"

"Abel!"

He looked up at the gable window, where a face
vanished behind the glass.

"They don't know," he said. "But if ever I play
American again, thee may strike me dumb."

Over supper he told the tale. Elias laughed so hard
that he had to put both hands over his mouth.

"I don't see much to laugh at," Abel said.

"You asked him the way to Plymouth?"

"For a joke."

"And he had you arrested?"

"Ess."

"And the governor paid you?"

"Nathan paid."

"Sir," said Elias, "that's the finest thing I've heard in
fifteen months."

Abel tried to sulk, but five guineas lay on the
table beside him and his own account improved in the
telling. Before bed they were all laughing, even Grace, though
she told him he deserved every mile of the march.

Only Elias became quiet.

The fire sank. Rain touched the window, ceased, and touched
it again. He sat near the ingle in Abel's old
shirt, looking at the red hollows in the peat.

"Fifteen months," he said at last. "Fifteen months without an
open door."

Grace watched him.

They had allowed him down from the garret after dark.
The shutters were closed, the children asleep and the dog
shut in the dairy lest it bark. Yet the kitchen,
warm and secure to the Endacotts, seemed another box to
him.

"I'd like to step outside," he said.

"No," Abel answered.

"Only the yard."

"There may be men on the road."

"At this hour?"

"At every hour till they catch thee or stop looking."

Elias nodded. For a little while he said nothing.

Then he asked, "Could I stand in the doorway?"

Grace looked at Abel.

"Doorway's neither in nor out," Abel said, "which makes it
twice as foolish."

But he saw the young man's face and relented.

They unbarred the back door. Cold air entered, smelling of
wet stone, peat smoke and the black earth of the
garden. Above the roof the cloud had broken, and three
or four stars shone with extraordinary sharpness.

Elias stood upon the threshold.

He did not move for so long that Abel told
him quietly to come in. Instead he took one step
into the yard, then another. He lifted his head. The
wind passed over his face and stirred the hair Grace
had cut unevenly round his ears.

"Liberty," he whispered.

The word was not spoken to them. It was spoken
to the darkness, the stars, the wet yard, perhaps to
God.

"Liberty," he said again.

He walked to the well.

"Far enough," Abel called.

Elias turned and raised a hand. Then he walked toward
the linhay, touched its stone corner, and came back. Grace
thought the matter ended. Abel barred the door after him,
and they settled him in the garret.

Sometime after midnight Elias rose.

Whether he meant only to look again at the stars,
or to walk once round the house, no one ever
knew. He lifted the latch of the back door without
waking the dog and crossed the yard. The moon came
out. The open moor beyond the fields lay silver and
black, without wall or sentry. He climbed the stile.

"Liberty!" he said.

He ran.

He ran past the potato patch, over the brook, and
up the slope where the wind bent the grass. He
ran until Lower Torworthy was a dim pale block behind
him. Then he walked, then ran again, intoxicated by distance.
Every step was one more step not ordered by a
guard. Every breath belonged to him. Fifteen months of walls
fell away in a single hour.

But the moor gives no liberty without asking whether a
man knows how to use it.

Cloud covered the moon. Mist gathered in the hollows. Elias
lost the track, found a wall, followed it in the
wrong direction and came at last, near dawn, to a
field he did not recognise. His prison clothes were hidden
beneath Abel's coat, but the roads would soon be watched,
and he had neither food nor hat.

Then he saw a figure standing by the hedge.

It was a tall, ragged figure in a torn smock,
with arms stretched outward and an old beaver hat upon
its head.

Elias dropped behind a bank before he understood it was
the Endacotts' scarecrow. In his wandering he had circled back
almost to the farm.

He crept into the field. The scarecrow was built on
a stout crosspiece, with a sack stuffed for its body
and brushwood for its arms. The dawn was strengthening. There
was no time to reach the house without being seen
from the road.

So Elias stood behind the scarecrow, pressed close against its
stake, and drew the torn smock round them both.

At Lower Torworthy, Grace woke and knew before she reached
the garret that he was gone. The back door stood
open. Abel found footprints by the stile, but the ground
beyond was too hard to hold them.

"He'll be caught," Grace said.

"Likely."

"We must find him."

"And lead every soldier to him? No."

"He may be lying hurt."

"He may be halfway Plymouth."

"He didn't say goodbye."

Abel looked toward the pale fields. He was angry, frightened
and, beneath both, wounded.

"We gave him shelter," he said. "Food. Clothes. Risked all
of us."

"He wasn't himself."

"He was himself enough to lift a latch."

They waited through the morning. Search parties passed twice. One
soldier came to the door and asked whether they had
seen a stranger. Abel replied that he had seen too
many strangers already and described his arrest until the soldier
retreated.

Near noon Nathan Nathaniel limped into the yard.

His brown coat was gone. So were his hats, pack,
pony and one boot. His shirt was torn, his face
swollen, and a bruise purpled one eye. He looked as
if the moor had chewed him and found him tough.

Grace, who had no cause to love him, nevertheless brought
him inside.

"What happened?"

"Robbery," Nathan said.

"Who robbed thee?"

"A villain."

"Where?"

"On the moor."

Abel came from the dairy and stared.

"Soldiers do all that?"

Nathan looked away.

"The soldiers behaved disgracefully, but that was yesterday."

"What happened today?"

Nathan asked for brandy.

They had none, so Grace gave him hot cider. He
drank it, groaned and would say only that he had
been attacked near a field by a dangerous man. When
Abel asked what dangerous man, Nathan shut his mouth. When
asked where his pony was, he said stolen. His bags?
Stolen. His hats? Stolen. His excellent brown traveling coat? Also
stolen.

"And my best beaver," he added bitterly. "Nearly new when
I bought it."

Abel's eyes went to the field beyond the window.

"The scarecrow," he said.

"What of it?" Grace asked.

"Naught."

But the old beaver hat had been on the scarecrow
the previous evening.

Abel went out when Nathan had gone to bed in
the settle. He crossed the potato patch and reached the
field. The scarecrow remained upright, though its smock hung loose
and one sleeve had been torn. The beaver hat was
gone. In the soft earth behind the stake were the
prints of two men, confused together, and near the hedge
he found one bright brass button from Nathan's coat.

That was all.

Elias did not return. Nathan recovered his pony two days
later at a toll gate toward Tavistock, but the bags
and clothes were never found. He refused to explain how
he had lost them, and if Abel guessed, he kept
the guess to himself. Grace was certain the American had
taken Nathan's coat and gone toward Plymouth, but certainty is
a poor blanket in the months of waiting that followed.

Winter passed. Spring greened the slopes. No word came of
an American recaptured in the district, which gave them hope,
though Abel said a man could drown, starve or be
buried nameless without troubling the Princetown records.

Nine months later a letter arrived.

It bore stamps and markings from America and had traveled
by way of Liverpool. The writing was cramped, as if
paper remained precious to the writer. Grace carried it to
the window, but her hands shook, and Abel read it
aloud.

The letter began:

> Dear Mrs Endacott,
>
> You will think me the worst and most ungrateful
young fellow alive for leaving your house without farewell. I
have thought so myself every day since. I ask you
to forgive me, and Mr Endacott too, if he can,
though I do not deserve it.

Here Abel stopped and cleared his throat.

"Go on," Grace said.

He went on.

Elias explained that he had intended only to walk to
the edge of the field. The open night had entered
his head like strong liquor. He had run without plan
until the mist turned him round, and by dawn he
found himself hiding behind the Endacotts' scarecrow.

Then came the part Nathan had refused to tell.

Nathan, after escaping the soldiers, had lost the road in
the mist and wandered all night. At sunrise he saw
the scarecrow. Being a dealer in clothes and still smarting
from the loss of his own hat, he noticed that
the old beaver upon its head, though weathered, might be
brushed, blocked and sold for a few shillings. He crossed
the field, stood on tiptoe and reached for it.

From immediately behind the scarecrow a voice said:

"I calculate that hat is occupied."

Nathan dropped his hand but not his ambition. He peered
round the smock and saw Elias.

The fugitive had hoped to frighten him away. Instead Nathan
recognised the prison clothes beneath Abel's coat and cried out
that here was the true American and the reward would
yet be his.

"I only hit him once," Elias wrote, "but perhaps after
what the soldiers did to him, once was more than
he could comfortably bear."

Nathan fell unconscious.

Elias took the brown coat, the best beaver hat, two
other hats, a bag of clothes and such money as
he believed sufficient for the journey. He left Nathan his
purse, his pony and Abel's coat folded beside him. Dressed
as a traveling dealer, with one hat on his head
and two tied to the bag, he walked south. People
saw what they expected to see: not an escaped prisoner,
but a young tradesman in secondhand clothes.

A carrier gave him a ride. A widow near Yelverton
sold him breakfast. By night he reached Plymouth, where an
American-born sailor hid him aboard a coastal vessel. From there
he passed to France, then sailed home after the peace.

The letter continued:

> I have married the sister of the friend who
helped me from Plymouth. Her name is Anne, and she
knows that I owe my life first to the kindness
of a lady who put me into a rabbit hutch,
and next to an old scarecrow wearing a hat too
good for its station.
>
> I am employed in a shipyard and am happy.
There are mornings when I wake believing I hear the
prison bell, but then Anne opens the shutter and I
see the harbour. I cannot explain what the open door
of your house meant to me. I behaved badly by
going, but I had been shut up so long that
liberty was not a thought. It was a fever.
>
> Please tell Mr Endacott that I am ashamed to
have taken his coat. Tell him also that the dealer's
coat fitted me better.

At that Abel laughed, though he pretended to cough afterward.

Folded in the letter was a small black silhouette mounted
on card. It showed a young man in profile, with
a straight nose, short hair tied at the nape and
a proud lift of the head. Elias had cut it
while in prison from black paper purchased with money earned
carving bone counters. He had carried it hidden in the
lining of his shoe through the yard, the rabbit hutch,
the garret, the night on the moor and the voyage
home.

> I meant it for my mother, he wrote, but
she died while I was confined. I send it to
you because you looked at me once and saw somebody's
son.

The farmer's sister had finished paring her apples. Rain still
moved over the yard, and above us the garret door
remained closed beneath the low, smoke-darkened ceiling.

"Grandmother kept the silhouette all her days," she said. "Mother
had it after, and now I have it."

She rose, went to the dresser and opened a narrow
drawer. From beneath old receipts and a packet of lavender
she took a little frame.

The paper had browned. The black profile was faded at
its edges, but the head still lifted toward some invisible
distance.

Behind it, written in Grace Endacott's firm hand, were four
words:

**Elias Ward—set at liberty.**

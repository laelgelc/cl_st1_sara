### THE CREDIT OF REGGIE BURKE

*He drank, he diced, he played at love,*
*He mocked the Powers below, above;*
*Yet, when one poor account fell through,*
*He forged the books—and made them true.*

There were two Burkes in Jumna Cantonment, and everybody knew
both. There was Reggie Burke of the Club verandah, who
played polo beyond his means, sang songs after midnight, and
maintained that a regulation was merely a timid man's substitute
for judgment. Then there was Mr. Reginald Burke, Manager of
the Northern and Provincial Bank, who knew the private value
of every trader within fifty miles, could detect a false
endorsement while talking about the weather, and kept his branch
so straight that even the Calcutta auditors found nothing to
improve except the shape of his paper-clips.

The Directors knew only Mr. Reginald Burke. Jumna preferred Reggie.

He was thirty-four, square-built, brown, cheerful, and without visible reverence.
He had debts, dogs, seven polo-sticks, and a reputation which
would have ruined a less competent man. His white raiment
was seldom immaculate after tiffin, his cheroots were infamous, and
his opinions upon Official Morality were not fit for the
chaplain's wife. Nevertheless, if a trooper's widow required a remittance,
or a junior civilian had overdrawn his account in the
belief that Providence paid racing debts, Reggie somehow arranged matters
before the scandal became public.

This is not virtue according to the printed rules. You
must see for yourself.

Silas Riley was the accountant of the branch. He was
long, gawky, rawboned, yellow about the temples, and constructed generally
as though Nature had begun with the elbows and forgotten
to fill in the intervals. He came from Yorkshire, had
educated himself by candle-light, and never forgave the world for
not having watched him do it. His handwriting was copper-plate,
his balance sheets were faultless, and his conception of banking
embraced the whole duty of man.

He considered Reggie unsafe.

"The institution, Mr. Burke," he would say, leaning over the
green baize table, "is not maintained by popularity."

"No," said Reggie. "It is maintained by people putting money
into it. Try to be civil to them."

"Civility is no substitute for principle."

"Nor is principle a substitute for deposits."

That was their daily prayer.

Riley despised polo, racing, whisky, soldiers, women who laughed loudly,
men who laughed at all, and every form of raiment
not suited to a funeral. He had also an especial
contempt for India, though India had supplied him with three
times the salary he could have earned at Home. The
Indian clerks feared his thin voice and the tap of
his pencil. The cashier, Mukherji, who had served the Bank
before Riley left Yorkshire, bore correction with a dignity Riley
mistook for submission.

"A pack of children," Riley said. "If I were removed,
the branch would fall to pieces in a month."

"Make it six weeks," said Reggie. "I should like time
to send for help."

They did not hit it off.

In April, Riley began coughing into a blue cotton handkerchief.
By May he had developed a fever and an irritability
beyond even his normal allowance. He came to office because
he believed absence to be a vice practised by the
underbred, but at eleven o'clock he would sit staring at
the ledgers while the figures swam before him.

Reggie watched for three days, then sent for Doctor Lenant.

"I have no need of medical interference," Riley protested. "It
is the heat."

"It is always the heat," said Reggie. "The heat has
killed half the people in India and written condolence to
the others."

Lenant examined him in the private room. When he came
out, he did not make the usual joke about accountants
having no lungs. The doctor did not laugh.

"How long?" Reggie asked.

"Perhaps four months. Perhaps six weeks. Send him Home if
he can travel."

"He cannot?"

"He might reach Bombay. He would not reach England. Has
he family?"

"None that he admits to."

"Then keep him quiet, feed him, and prevent him from
worrying. Above all, don't give him a shock. The frail
beast lives by pride."

Reggie looked through the glass partition. Riley was correcting Mukherji's
addition, which happened to be right.

"That ought to keep him alive for ever," he said.

Three mornings later came a registered envelope from Calcutta marked
PERSONAL AND CONFIDENTIAL. Reggie opened it in his bungalow while
eating an egg and swearing at the punkah-coolie.

The Directors had heard, through the inspecting officer, that Mr.
Riley's health rendered the continued discharge of his duties uncertain.
They regretted, but could not permit sentiment to interfere with
efficiency. Under Clause Nine of his agreement, his services would
terminate on the last day of June. Mr. A. H.
Pritchard was being sent from Allahabad to assume charge of
the accountant's department.

There was a further paragraph intimating that no pension could
be considered.

That was the Directors' notion of mercy. Directors, like gods
and artillery, operate at a distance.

Reggie read the letter twice, folded it, and put it
into his pocket. Then he went to the Bank.

Riley was sitting in Reggie's chair.

"I have been examining the advances," he said. "Your loan
to Dhanraj is irregular."

"It is secured on grain."

"On anticipated grain."

"The rains will come."

"Banking cannot be conducted upon meteorology."

"Quite right. Give me my chair."

Riley rose with dignity, coughed, and was obliged to catch
the table.

"Any mail from Calcutta?" he asked when he had breath.

"Nothing for you."

Reggie lied easily; but this lie had weight, and he
felt it settle upon him.

Pritchard arrived on the fifteenth—a pink, anxious young man with
new sun-helmets and a letter authorising him to take over
at once. Reggie met him at the station.

"You're not taking over," he said.

Pritchard blinked. "But the Directors—"

"Are in Calcutta. Riley is here. He is dying, and
he doesn't know he has been dismissed."

"What am I to do?"

"You are an old friend visiting me for the hot
weather. You wish to learn local conditions. You will touch
no ledger unless Riley asks you, and if you mention
Clause Nine I shall slay you."

"I don't think the Directors would approve."

"They seldom approve till the half-yearly profits appear."

Thus Pritchard became a guest. Riley disliked him immediately.

"Another of your Club acquaintances?" he asked.

"A serious student of exchange," said Reggie.

"He appears very young."

"So does the Bank, from the outside."

At the end of June, Riley's salary stopped. Reggie discovered
this through Natural Curiosity before Riley could discover it through
his passbook. He paid the amount—eight hundred rupees—from his own
account and persuaded Mukherji to enter it as a Head
Office credit.

Mukherji looked at him once.

"Mr. Riley will not inquire from Calcutta, sir?"

"He is not to write on business. Doctor's orders."

"And if Head Office inquires?"

"Then I am a thief, Mukherji."

The cashier adjusted his spectacles. "No, sir. A thief usually
takes out."

That was all he said.

Reggie now intercepted Riley's mail. There was little of it:
bills, a missionary circular, one letter from an aunt in
Leeds who believed him still to be twenty-seven, and two
communications from Calcutta acknowledging matters Riley had no longer any
right to handle. Reggie locked these away and substituted a
typewritten note bearing the Bank's heading.

The Directors, it said, had observed with satisfaction Mr. Riley's
devotion under difficult climatic conditions. They relied upon his judgment
and trusted he would spare his health in the interests
of the institution.

Riley read the paper in bed. His room was darkened
against the white fury outside, and bottles stood among the
accountancy manuals on the table.

"'Relied upon my judgment,'" he repeated. "They have been slow
to acknowledge it."

"Head Office moves slowly," said Reggie. "It is full of
heavy furniture."

"I have sometimes thought," Riley continued, "that your reports failed
to represent my position adequately."

"Very likely."

Reggie was humble.

"You are not a bad fellow, Burke, but you lack
the moral seriousness necessary in finance. Ability without restraint is
a danger. I have tried to teach you that."

"So you have."

"You take correction badly."

"I'll practise."

Riley lectured him for twenty minutes and slept contentedly afterward.
Reggie went to the Club, drank one whisky-and-soda in silence,
tore up the Directors' dismissal, and returned before midnight because
Riley's fever commonly rose at one.

By July, Reggie had abandoned polo. By August, he had
abandoned the Club. He worked at the Bank from nine
till five, doing Riley's labour and his own; he spent
the evenings beside the long chair in the bungalow, listening
to complaints of the servants, the climate, the doctor, Pritchard,
and the degeneration of modern banking. At night he slept
in the next room with both doors open.

Pritchard, who was not a fool, learned the branch from
Mukherji.

"You look rotten," he told Reggie.

"I am developing moral seriousness."

"You can't go on paying Riley's salary."

"I can till October if I sell the pony."

"Thunder was your best pony."

"Riley is my worst accountant. Therefore the sacrifice is Biblical."

He sold Thunder to a cavalry captain and entered another
eight hundred rupees as a credit from Calcutta.

The rains broke late. Steam rose from the compounds, frogs
shouted in every ditch, and Riley dwindled among pillows. His
face sharpened; his hands became transparent; but his temper retained
astonishing vitality. He rebuked Reggie for sitting with his coat
unbuttoned and demanded that the Bank's last balance sheet be
brought to him.

"You have placed too much confidence in Dhanraj," he whispered.

"Dhanraj has repaid."

"That does not justify the original risk."

"No successful loan ever does."

"I cannot argue when you are flippant."

"You mustn't argue at all."

"It is my duty. If I were to leave you—"

Here he stopped, frightened by his own phrase.

Reggie's face changed at once.

"You won't leave yet," he said. "The Bank needs you."

Riley searched his eyes. "Has Lenant spoken to you?"

"Lenant speaks continuously. Doctors are paid by the word."

"What does he say of me?"

"That you require rest and mental stimulus."

The last two words were true. Lenant had used them
that morning.

"He is sinking because he has nothing to fight for,"
the doctor had said. "Give him an interest. Good news,
responsibility—anything that persuades him next month matters."

"How much good news?"

"As much as you can find."

Reggie found none. Therefore he manufactured it.

That night, after Riley slept, he sat in the Bank
with samples of official correspondence before him. Pritchard held the
lamp; Mukherji brought the old letter-book; and between them they
composed a masterpiece.

The Directors, deeply impressed by Mr. Riley's services and exceptional
devotion, had resolved to raise his salary by three hundred
rupees monthly from the first of September. They further contemplated,
upon his restoration to health, entrusting him with an enlarged
sphere of supervision. His criticisms of branch procedure had received
their MOST CAREFUL consideration.

"Too much?" Pritchard asked.

"Dying men are entitled to capitals," said Reggie.

"What enlarged sphere?" said Mukherji.

"Heaven, perhaps. Give me the sealing-wax."

Forgery is difficult only when committed for profit. Reggie had
no qualms, and his hand was excellent. He copied the
secretary's signature from the dismissal order, enclosed the letter, stamped
it at the bazaar post-office, and caused it to arrive
by the morning dak.

Riley read it once. Then again. Colour came into his
cheeks—a poor, hectic colour, but life travels under queer flags.

"Three hundred," he said. "They have recognised the principle at
last."

"They generally do when you explain it long enough."

"An enlarged sphere! That must mean provincial supervision. I have
plans, Burke—plans I have never communicated because I doubted whether
they would be understood."

"You shall tell me."

For the next week Riley improved. He ate half a
chicken, demanded files, and dictated a memorandum on the moral
hazards of unsecured advances. It ran to thirty-seven pages. Reggie
typed every page himself because the clacking nuisance of the
office machine offended the sick man when operated by anyone
else.

You will admit that this was patience.

September came in brass and glare. The rain-water vanished; the
earth cracked; Riley's improvement ended as suddenly as it had
begun. He no longer asked for ledgers. Sometimes he spoke
broad Yorkshire, a thing he had trained himself not to
do in prosperity.

"My father kept accounts wi' chalk," he said once. "He
said I'd come to nowt."

"He was wrong."

"Aye. Three hundred a month, and t' provincial work after."

"Exactly."

"I could have done summat—something considerable—for the Bank."

"You did."

"No. Not yet. I have been hampered." His eyes moved
toward Reggie with a shadow of the old accusation. "You
never properly supported me."

"I know."

"But you will learn. There is sound material in you,
Burke, if you abandon frivolity."

"I'll abandon the worst of it."

"Polo?"

"Polo first."

"Liquor?"

"In due course."

Riley sighed. "You evade moral obligations."

That evening Reggie drafted one more letter. It conveyed the
personal congratulations of the Chairman, expressed confidence in Riley's future,
and mentioned that portions of his memorandum might become standard
instructions throughout the Bank. It was the most splendid lie
of the series.

He meant to post it before dinner, but Dhanraj came
in concerning a grain consignment, the treasury figures were late,
and Pritchard made an error in exchange which required immediate
correction. Reggie carried the envelope in his pocket back to
the bungalow.

Lenant was on the verandah.

"He has understood," the doctor said. "Don't contradict him now."

Riley lay very still, looking toward the open window. The
sunset had made the cantonment dust red, and far off
a bugle was sounding from the lines.

"Burke?"

"Yes, old man."

"It's death."

Reggie sat down beside him.

"No promotion, then," Riley said after a while. His voice
was faint, but the bitterness remained. "All plans wasted. Man
comes to nowt."

"The Directors know what you were worth."

"They raised me."

"Yes."

"Three hundred."

"Yes."

"And supervision?"

"As soon as you are fit."

Riley almost smiled. "They were slow. But I compelled recognition."

"You did."

His fingers moved over the sheet as if casting up
a column. "Tell them—I could have done great things."

"I'll tell them."

"Tell them you must be watched."

"I'll put it in the report."

That contented him. Near midnight he said something about an
error of nine annas, drew one careful breath, and died.

Reggie remained beside the bed until Lenant came. Then he
walked out to the verandah and found the forged letter
in his pocket, sealed, stamped, and useless.

The Directors afterward approved Pritchard's appointment, questioned two irregular salary
entries, and accepted Reggie's explanation that they concerned exceptional expenses
during illness. They did not inquire closely. The half-yearly profits
were excellent.

Reggie bought another pony, returned to the Club, and recovered
his reputation for every vice except cruelty. He never spoke
of Riley unless compelled.

But once, when Pritchard called the whole business magnificent, Reggie
turned upon him.

"Magnificent be damned," he said. "If I'd posted that last
letter in time, it might have kept the poor devil
alive another day."

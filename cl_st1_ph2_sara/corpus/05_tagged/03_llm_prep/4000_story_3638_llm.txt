**THE PRICE OF SIMPLICITY**

**VIENNA, May 17.—**The morning papers report that each of the
American gentlemen lately employed upon the Peace Commission in Paris
received one hundred thousand dollars for six weeks' work. The
papers do not say whether this sum included stationery, shoe-leather,
and the expense of keeping peace after it had been
made; but I take it that these were extras, and
properly so.

I trust the report is true. I trust it with
that unselfish and almost religious fervor which a man feels
when the prosperity in question belongs entirely to somebody else.
I do not know any member of the Commission, and
therefore cannot be suspected of gratitude; nor am I a
member of it, and therefore cannot be charged with defending
my own wages. I stand on the high, dry, and
commanding ground of the disinterested taxpayer.

If the report is true, a precedent has been born.
It is a healthy child, it weighs one hundred thousand
dollars, and its lungs are good. We should feed it,
clothe it, educate it, and guard it from the ordinary
diseases of governmental infancy—investigation, retrenchment, and the Congressional committee. A
precedent of this kind might grow up and do the
country real service. It might even become attached to the
diplomatic branch of the Republic, which has long been in
need of some respectable relative with money.

The Commissioners' pay amounts, roughly, to sixteen thousand six hundred
and sixty-six dollars a week. There will be persons at
home who will call this extravagance. There are always persons
at home ready to save the nation at the expense
of a man who is three thousand miles away and
unable to strike them. These economists would have paid the
Commissioners seven dollars a week and required them to furnish
their own maps. They would have lodged them over a
bakery, fed them on condensed patriotism, and instructed them to
settle the affairs of the earth before Saturday night so
that the room might be let to a traveling dentist.

That is not economy. It is merely cheapness with a
clean collar on.

The United States is a rich nation. It has mines,
railroads, factories, wheat fields, oil wells, skyscrapers, millionaires, trusts, private
yachts, public debts, and more food at breakfast than many
European principalities possess at harvest. When such a nation employs
a man to represent it abroad, it ought to pay
him enough to look as though he had met the
nation somewhere. At present we give him a salary suitable
to the overseer of a rural graveyard, then send him
among kings and tell him to uphold the majesty of
seventy million people without wrinkling his trousers.

We are very particular about that majesty. We insist that
it shall be upheld morning, noon, and night. But we
have never decided who is to pay the laundry bill.

Our diplomat is expected to maintain a residence worthy of
the Republic; to give dinners worthy of the Republic; to
subscribe to charities worthy of the Republic; to attend ceremonies,
receptions, weddings, funerals, baptisms, military reviews, royal anniversaries, national catastrophes,
and international refreshments worthy of the Republic. If his salary
does not cover these things, that is his affair. The
Republic furnishes the dignity; he furnishes everything else.

This system has one merit: it prevents poor men from
becoming ambassadors unless they are unusually foolish. It selects the
rich with almost scientific exactness. We may appoint genius, experience,
tact, learning, courage, or familiarity with foreign affairs, provided those
qualities are accompanied by a private fortune. But when they
are found in a man who has only his salary,
we cannot use them. He might know Europe, understand commerce,
speak six languages, charm a cabinet, and disentangle a treaty
in the dark; but if he cannot give twelve courses
and champagne to a duke, he is constitutionally unfit.

The British arrange these matters differently. The British are supposed
to be weighed down by monarchy, aristocracy, precedent, ermine, crown
jewels, and hereditary furniture; yet they have somehow retained enough
mental freedom to pay their representatives. They give an ambassador
a large salary, furnish him a residence, contribute to his
official entertainments, and allow him to represent Great Britain without
first proving that his grandfather sold steel rails.

We give our ambassador perhaps seventeen thousand five hundred dollars,
according to the post, and then ask him to compete
with a British representative who has salary, house, servants, carriages,
allowances, and a government that knows a dinner costs money
even when eaten in the national interest. Our man begins
the race fifty thousand dollars behind, carrying his own dining
table.

The result is picturesque but not triumphant. The British embassy
shines with light; ours conserves lamp oil. The British ambassador
receives a minister at eleven; ours asks him to call
after twelve, when the hired girl has finished dusting the
front parlor. The British give a banquet; we give an
explanation. They serve turbot, venison, peaches in February, wines older
than several republics, and coffee capable of concluding an alliance.
We reply with cold chicken, three olives, a cake bought
by the yard, and a speech on Republican Simplicity.

Republican Simplicity is a noble phrase. It has served us
long and faithfully, especially when we wished to avoid paying
a bill. It came down to us from our fathers,
together with liberty, equality, the town pump, and certain pieces
of furniture nobody has dared throw away. It was appropriate
to a young country whose statesmen rode horseback, whose presidents
knew the butcher personally, and whose entire foreign policy could
be carried in a carpetbag. But a custom may be
venerable and still have rheumatism.

Benjamin Franklin went to France in plain dress and conquered
a court. This has misled us for more than a
century. Franklin could have gone in a flour sack and
conquered it. He had genius, wit, fame, wisdom, and a
head which looked official without assistance. The fur cap became
him. But we have taken that cap, enlarged it into
a national doctrine, and forced it upon generations of diplomats
whose heads were designed for ordinary weather.

Because Franklin wore no court uniform, our civilian representatives must
appear at European state functions in black evening clothes. This
is called democratic. It is also conspicuous, inappropriate, and discourteous.

Imagine a great ceremonial at this imperial court. There are
generals blazing in scarlet and gold; admirals afloat in embroidery;
chamberlains paneled with silver; ministers wearing stars, ribbons, swords, feathers,
crosses, cords, leaves, chains, medals, and enough metal to stock
a hardware shop. The walls glow, the mirrors multiply the
splendor, the chandeliers hang overhead like captured constellations. Into this
river of color steps the representative of the United States
in a swallow-tail coat, white tie, low shoes, and the
solemn expression of a man who has come to measure
the corpse.

He is visible at once. Republican Simplicity sees to that.

He resembles an undertaker who has mislaid the funeral; a
head waiter seeking employment; a Presbyterian elder dropped by accident
into the Book of Revelation. Every eye turns toward him.
People ask who he is. They are told that he
embodies the power, wealth, dignity, enterprise, and sovereign majesty of
the Great Republic beyond the sea. They look at his
coat again and conclude that the Great Republic has been
unexpectedly bereaved.

It is said that a uniform is undemocratic. This is
interesting, because we put uniforms upon soldiers, sailors, policemen, postmen,
firemen, railway conductors, messenger boys, and the young person who
carries the keys at a hotel. Apparently liberty can survive
blue trousers upon a letter carrier but would perish under
gold braid upon an ambassador.

I offer a suggestion gratis. The government is accustomed to
accepting gratis suggestions, though it frequently spends several millions discovering
why it should not have done so.

Give every ambassador and minister, during his term of service,
temporary rank in the Army or Navy. Make him a
colonel, general, commodore, or admiral according to the temperature and
importance of his post. Let him wear the uniform at
official ceremonies. He need not command troops. Few generals do
so at a court reception. He need not sail a
ship. Many admirals spend years resisting that temptation. His rank
would merely permit him to appear among the official plumage
of Europe without looking like a black beetle in a
tulip bed.

If he is transferred or recalled, the rank vanishes. He
lays aside the sword and resumes citizenship, rheumatism, and the
privilege of criticizing his successor. Nothing could be simpler. We
should have dignity abroad and democracy at home; and if
a diplomat became too fond of his feathers, we could
console ourselves with the knowledge that the nation has survived
more dangerous decorations.

But clothes are only the visible part of the difficulty.
The salary is the bone.

People who have never lived abroad imagine that an ambassador's
business is done in an office. They suppose foreign ministers
come in, state their proposals, receive an American answer, and
go away convinced. In that case a desk, two chairs,
a flag, and a lunch basket would be enough. Unfortunately
mankind has not been arranged on this economical plan.

A large part of diplomacy is social. Men who distrust
one another at noon may understand one another after dinner.
A minister who will not yield a comma across a
green table may surrender a semicolon over coffee. Acquaintance softens
official stiffness; hospitality creates opportunities; confidence grows in drawing rooms,
on terraces, at country houses, and in those apparently idle
conversations where no one says anything important and everyone remembers
it.

The commercial traveler knows this. He entertains a customer because
dinner is cheaper than failure. He does not invite the
customer to a garret, set out sardines on a newspaper,
and explain that his firm believes in Business Simplicity. He
pays for the dinner, puts it in the expense account,
and sells the goods.

An ambassador is, among other things, the chief commercial traveler
of his country. He has a larger sample case and
less liberty to mention it, but the principle is the
same. He opens doors for merchants; obtains information; brings manufacturers
and officials together; explains new enterprises; removes suspicions; promotes concessions;
protects contracts; discovers opportunities; and prepares the social soil in
which trade may grow. Yet we refuse him the ordinary
tools of salesmanship. We send him out to capture markets
with a folded flag and a ham sandwich.

Then we wonder why another nation gets the contract.

Suppose an American company seeks a railway concession in some
European or Oriental state. The British, French, and German representatives
are active. They know the ministers, the financiers, the engineers,
the cousins of the ministers, the bankers of the cousins,
and the elderly countess who settles matters after the cabinet
has talked itself tired. They can bring these persons together
under pleasant conditions. Their governments provide the means.

Our representative also knows them, perhaps. He would like to
entertain them. He considers his salary, his rent, his daughters'
dresses, his official subscriptions, his cook's ultimatum, and the price
of fish. Then he invites two of the least important
to tea.

The concession goes elsewhere, and the American public saves eleven
dollars on diplomacy.

There is no economy in ham and lemonade when the
competitor is serving commerce with silver covers on it.

The American ambassador's residence presents another triumph of thrift. In
several capitals our government furnishes no proper embassy. The representative
must rent one. If the house is large enough for
official receptions, the rent bites a hole through his salary
and then asks for dessert. If it is small enough
for his salary, he must receive the diplomatic corps in
relays.

A rich ambassador can correct the government's omission. He rents
a palace, employs servants, gives dinners, supports ceremonies, and quietly
pays thousands of dollars for the privilege of serving his
country. We call this patriotism. It may be patriotism, but
it is also an involuntary tax levied upon one citizen
because he accepted an office.

A poor ambassador cannot correct it. He rents what he
can. Perhaps the entrance is narrow, the staircase dark, and
the reception room heated by a stove. He hangs the
American flag over the stove, because the stove is the
most imposing object in the apartment. On state occasions the
eagle smells faintly of coal. Guests deposit their cloaks in
the bedroom. The ambassador's wife hides the family washing behind
a screen and receives a princess with the calmness of
a martyr. The prince sits on the piano stool. The
military attaché supports himself against the mantel, which is painted
on the wall. The Republic is present, but crowded.

This is Republican Simplicity.

We must understand what a representative represents. He is not
merely Mr. Jones of Ohio, with a wife, two daughters,
a private opinion about oysters, and a weakness for whist.
Officially he is the United States. When he enters a
room, the flag enters. When he is received with honor,
the country is honored. When he appears shabby, embarrassed, or
unable to perform the ordinary courtesies of his station, foreigners
do not say, "Mr. Jones is underpaid." They say, "America
does things badly."

This judgment may be unjust, but international society, like every
other society, has never agreed to be just before dinner.

We cannot reform all European customs by attending them in
inadequate clothes. We cannot abolish ceremony by being the least
impressive object in it. We cannot teach foreign governments simplicity
while our tourists are filling the best hotels, buying pictures
by the acre, hiring private railway cars, scattering tips, renting
castles, and asking whether the cathedral can be illuminated after
supper.

Europe knows we are rich. There is no concealment possible.
The noise of our money can be heard through stone
walls.

At home we build public offices of marble, railway stations
fit for emperors, hotels with fountains in the dining room,
theaters covered with gold, shops brighter than palaces, private houses
containing ballrooms, conservatories, organs, galleries, swimming tanks, and occasionally a
family. We send a millionaire's dog across the continent in
a private compartment and then congratulate ourselves because our ambassador
travels second class when nobody is watching.

This is not simplicity. It is extravagance wearing a false
mustache.

The Republic is no longer a barefoot boy carrying apples
to market. It has grown. It has put up its
hair, lengthened its skirts, learned foreign languages, acquired territories, entered
international society, and begun receiving invitations engraved on stiff paper.
It cannot now sit on the kitchen step and plead
youth whenever the bill arrives. A grown nation is a
thundering expense.

We have interests everywhere. Our ships enter every sea; our
machinery goes into every factory; our wheat feeds cities whose
names our schoolbooks omit; our bankers lend money to governments;
our missionaries, engineers, actors, inventors, speculators, teachers, tourists, and patent-medicine
labels precede the flag into the most distant places. We
demand influence proportioned to our power and respect proportioned to
our influence. But we propose to get it at wholesale
rates, with a discount for cash.

The thing cannot be done.

Let Congress establish salaries fit for the posts. Let the
government own proper embassy buildings in the chief capitals. Let
it pay the necessary expenses of official hospitality. Let accounts
be audited, if that produces happiness; let every oyster be
numbered and every bottle cross-examined; but let the representative have
the means to represent.

If an ambassador receives seventy-five thousand dollars and spends fifty
thousand in the public service, the nation has not lost
seventy-five thousand. It has purchased access, information, influence, dignity, commercial
opportunity, and the ability to give dinner without pawning the
eagle. These are not sentimental luxuries. They are part of
the machinery of power.

The farmer oils a reaper. The manufacturer repairs an engine.
The merchant advertises. The railway maintains stations. None of them
calls it extravagance. But the nation buys an ambassador, declines
to oil him, and expresses surprise when he squeaks in
public.

Our objection comes partly from an ancient fear that proper
compensation will create an aristocracy. We imagine that if a
diplomat wears a uniform or lives in a suitable house,
he will return home with monarchical designs upon the county
courthouse. This anxiety underrates the county courthouse. A citizen who
has endured three years of European ceremony will generally be
glad to get home, take off his decorations, put his
feet on the stove, and discuss the tariff with a
man who calls him Bill.

No republic was ever overthrown by a dinner allowance.

Republics are endangered by folly, corruption, ignorance, injustice, vanity, cowardice,
and the habit of mistaking phrases for principles. Republican Simplicity
was once a principle. It is now often a phrase
used to save small sums in conspicuous places while losing
large sums where nobody can photograph them.

We must separate simplicity from meanness. Simplicity is Washington refusing
a crown. Meanness is Washington refusing to pay the cook.
Simplicity is plain speech, honest conduct, modest authority, and public
service without ostentation. It does not require the national representative
to go cold, hungry, badly housed, badly dressed, and socially
disabled merely that a congressman may point to him from
a comfortable hotel and praise the virtues of the fathers.

The fathers themselves were practical men. If they returned now
and saw the United States as it is—its cities roaring,
its wires circling the globe, its locomotives crossing deserts, its
factories darkening noon, its fortunes larger than old kingdoms—they would
not ask us to pretend we still keep the national
treasury in a teapot.

They would enlarge the diplomatic appropriations.

At least Franklin would. He had too much common sense
to insist that every successor win France with the same
hat.

And so I return to the Peace Commissioners and their
one hundred thousand dollars apiece. I welcome the precedent. I
salute it. I urge it to resist assassins. Let it
spread from commission to embassy, from embassy to legation, from
legation to consulate. Let our servants abroad be paid according
to the work expected of them and the nation behind
them. Let America cease appearing at the banquet of nations
with diamonds in her pockets and a paper collar around
her neck.

I make these observations wholly in the public interest. They
have no personal application. I am not an applicant for
diplomatic employment; I have never desired an embassy; I would
not accept one except under circumstances so peculiar, honorable, and
financially adequate that they are not worth mentioning. My concern
is for the flag, for commerce, for national dignity, and
for those unfortunate gentlemen who must support all three while
calculating whether they can afford another chicken.

I have spoken plainly because patriotism that never criticizes is
only applause with a brass band behind it. We are
a great people, but greatness does not become smaller when
it pays its bills. We are democratic, but democracy need
not wear an undertaker's coat to prove it. We are
practical, but a policy that saves a dinner and loses
a railway is practicality standing on its head.

Pay the men. House them. Dress them. Give them the
tools of their trade. Then, if they fail, recall them
with dignity and appoint better men. But do not send
them unarmed into a contest of influence and blame them
because they return carrying nothing but the flag.

**POSTSCRIPT, May 18.—**I have just learned from an authoritative source—an
under-secretary who heard it from a chamberlain whose wife was
told by an American lady at luncheon—that the Vienna appointment
has been settled.

I am not to have it.

This information is of no consequence. None whatever. I mention
it merely to correct a rumor which I had not
heard until I corrected it. There has been some foolish
talk, apparently, that my interest in diplomatic salaries might possess
a personal color. The suggestion is grotesque. I never wanted
Vienna. I have always regarded Vienna as too musical. Its
architecture is excessive, its cakes dangerous, and its official dinners
occur at an hour injurious to literary men.

Besides, the climate does not agree with me. I have
enjoyed perfect health here, but that proves nothing.

The gentleman selected is doubtless worthy. I do not know
him, which enables me to say this cordially. He may
possess experience, tact, languages, judgment, and a private income large
enough to preserve them. I wish him every success. If
his salary compels him to choose between national hospitality and
boots, I trust he will choose the hospitality and remain
seated.

As for myself, I repeat that I would refuse an
ambassadorship. Positively. Unless the salary were seventy-five thousand dollars, the
residence furnished, entertainment provided for, uniform authorized, carriage included, and
return passage guaranteed. Even then I should hesitate—briefly, but with
dignity.

My argument remains untouched. America must abandon this threadbare masquerade
called Republican Simplicity. It is no more convincing than a
billionaire in a paper collar, a king in a breechclout,
a locomotive in baby shoes, or an archangel wearing a
tin halo to avoid the appearance of pride.

We are wealthy, loud, luxurious, energetic, extravagant, generous, wasteful, hospitable,
ambitious, overdressed, overfed, overbuilt, and inordinately fond of seeing our
name in large letters. These are our national facts. Let
us confess them like men.

And let us pay the ambassador.

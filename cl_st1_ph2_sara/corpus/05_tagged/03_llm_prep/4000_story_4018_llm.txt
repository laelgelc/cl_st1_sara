### The Last Song of Cumhal

It was in the black end of November, when the
reeds of Drumcliff were bowed under rain and the sea
beyond Sligo had the colour of an old sword, that
Cumhal the Gleeman came limping to the Abbey of the
Little Bell. He had thin yellow hair that made a
lonely island in the midst of his baldness, a face
pale and puckered from hunger, a parti-coloured doublet whose reds
and greens had been chastened by many weathers, and shoes
so long and pointed that a prudent man might have
used them for extinguishing candles. At his girdle hung a
great brown wallet, patched with hare-skin and calf-skin, and bulging
with pipes, balls, apples, crusts, ribbons, bones, and all the
poor magnificence of his trade.

The night wind blew behind him out of Tireragh, and
before him the abbey windows burned yellow among the yew
trees; and he praised Saint Brigid, Saint Columcille, and every
hospitable power, Christian or otherwise, because his feet were blistered
and the rain had entered at his collar and run
coldly down his back.

Yet as he crossed the little bridge he saw three
ravens sitting upon the parapet.

"Black counsellors," he said, "may you find a sheep in
the bog and leave me my two eyes till morning."

The ravens cocked their heads, as though they knew some
jest not yet told, and the river beneath them hurried
brown and swollen toward the ungovernable sea.

Cumhal knocked at the gate with his hazel staff. After
a long while a lay brother opened a narrow wicket
and thrust out a red nose and one watery eye.

"Who disturbs holy men at their devotions?"

"One whom holy men are commanded to shelter," said Cumhal.
"I am Cumhal, son of Maelduin, son of that Tadhg
who played the bronze harp before the King of Connacht
and made a barren queen laugh till she bore twins.
I can sing the sorrows of White-breasted Deirdre, the voyages
of Bran, the wooing of Etain, the cattle raid of
Cooley, and the battle in which Cuchulain the Swift, Wild
Horse, stood alone against an army. I can balance nine
knives, imitate twelve birds, and tell a tale so merry
that a corpse will sit up to hear the end."

"Then tell it to the corpses," said the brother, "for
the living are abed."

"Give me a dry corner, a little fire, a mouthful
of bread, and water not yet walked through."

The brother admitted him grudgingly and led him, not to
the guest-house, where strangers of consequence were received, but to
a stone shed beside the swine-yard. There he gave Cumhal
six sods of turf that had lain all day beneath
a broken gutter, and a loaf mottled with green mould,
and a wooden bowl of water from which the hens
had drunk and in which one hen had plainly washed
both feet. Thereafter he flung down a blanket so alive
with fleas that it appeared to shiver though there was
no wind.

"May peace be upon your sleep," said the brother.

"And may justice awaken yours," said Cumhal.

He blew upon the turf until his cheeks ached, and
struck sparks until his fingers bled, but the sods only
hissed and sent forth a smoke bitterer than the breath
of Balor. He scraped the mould from the loaf and
discovered that the mould had entered farther than his knife.
He raised the bowl, saw a feather, two straws and
a drowned beetle revolving therein, and set it down again.
Then he wrapped himself in the blanket; but the fleas
rose against him in such multitude that he leaped up
crying that the hosts of Queen Maeve were less numerous
and showed more mercy.

Now hunger, cold and insult are three hounds that run
swiftly together, and they hunted Cumhal's patience out of the
shed. He went into the courtyard beneath the abbot's window,
lifted his staff like the rod of a prophet, and
cried in a voice that carried over roof, orchard and
cloister:

"Woe to the Abbey of the Little Bell,
Where wet turf smokes and foul hens dwell;
Where bread wears green for a festal gown,
And fleas bear off the stranger's crown.
May its ale turn sour, its candles spit,
May mice in the abbot's missal sit;
May girls at spinning and thieves at night
Sing how the brothers deny men right!"

Windows opened. Tonsured heads appeared like pale stones set in
darkness. Cumhal, seeing that he had an audience, drew a
longer breath.

"May the abbot's geese forget to lay! May his bees
fly over Ben Bulben and give their honey to the
sidhe! May his great toe grow through his shoe, his
ink freeze in midsummer, and his sermons make even the
demons sleep! May this verse be learned by children, carried
by tinkers, whistled by shepherds, shouted at fairs, and scratched
with rusty nails upon every privy door from Ballysadare to
Donegal!"

Thereupon the abbot sprang out of bed. He was a
heavy man with a small tonsure, and his nightcap stood
crookedly upon his head, giving him the appearance of a
white mountain upon which an absurd flag had been planted.
He called the cellarer, the porter and six friars, and
demanded the cause of the uproar.

The lay brother repeated the curse word for word, though
he declared that such wickedness wounded his ears. The abbot
listened first with anger, then with fear; for he understood
that a sermon may be forgotten before the congregation reaches
home, whereas a rhyme runs barefoot through the world and
enters every house without knocking.

"If the spinning girls learn it," he said, "they will
sing it over their wheels."

"And if the children learn it," said the cellarer, "they
will cry it after us on the road."

"And robbers may sing it," said the porter, "while dividing
our stolen cattle."

The abbot shuddered.

"Seize the man. A curse well measured is more dangerous
than an army badly led."

The friars rushed into the courtyard. Cumhal struck one upon
the shin, bit another's thumb and pulled a third by
the cord of his habit, but hunger had weakened him
and the fleas had deprived him of sleep. They carried
him to the river and ducked him three times in
the icy water, asking after every immersion whether he withdrew
his verses.

"I withdraw nothing," he gasped. "But I add that your
river is cleaner than your drinking bowl."

They ducked him a fourth time for insolence and brought
him, streaming and blue-lipped, before the abbot.

"Why," asked the abbot, "do you disturb the order established
by God?"

"Because your order gave me wet fire, rotten bread, filthy
water and a blanket occupied by a nation of blood-drinkers."

"Poverty is holy."

"Then keep more of it for yourself."

"Murmuring is rebellion, rebellion is pride, pride is the door
of pagan error, and pagan error leads souls to destruction.
You sing of Druids, enchantresses, warriors, merrows and women whose
beauty troubles the blood. You turn men's minds from obedience."

"I turn their minds from dullness," said Cumhal.

The abbot crossed himself.

"You are a remnant of the old darkness, as stubborn
as a tree stump in a ploughed field. If we
leave you alive, your satire will pass from mouth to
mouth; and in a hundred years men will remember the
mould upon one loaf and forget a thousand masses. Therefore
charity requires that we silence you."

"What shape will your charity take?"

"The shape of a cross."

The next morning was clear and cruel. Frost silvered the
cabbage leaves, and Ben Bulben stood beyond the plain like
a gigantic table prepared for invisible kings. The friars led
Cumhal to the carpenter's yard and bade him make the
instrument of his death. They gave him two rough beams,
three nails, a mallet and a blunt adze. He worked
slowly, partly because his hands were swollen from the river
and partly because no man willingly finishes such labour. The
younger friars watched him, whispering among themselves.

"Make the arms wider," said the cellarer.

"I have embraced too much already," answered Cumhal.

"Plane that knot."

"Leave it. It has harmed no one, which is more
than can be said of this abbey."

When the cross was finished they set it upon his
shoulder and marched him through the gate. The abbot did
not accompany them, having accounts to examine and a disputation
concerning the proper number of candles at the feast of
a minor saint. Twelve friars went instead, with the lay
brother carrying the nails in a leather bag.

After half a mile Cumhal stumbled and let the cross
fall.

"He delays us," said the cellarer.

"My shoulder is not made of oak," said Cumhal. "Let
me warm my limbs, and I shall walk the better."

Without waiting for permission, he took three red apples from
his wallet and began to juggle them. The apples rose
into the cold sunlight and crossed and recrossed like little
planets governed by a pleasant law. Then he added a
white stone, a knife and the lay brother's leather bag,
snatched so lightly that the brother did not perceive its
absence until the nails were circling above his nose.

The young friars laughed. Cumhal balanced the knife upon his
chin, caught the apples behind his back, and made the
bag vanish into his sleeve. He appeared to swallow the
stone, drew it from the cellarer's ear, and finally sent
all six objects upward together, where for a moment they
seemed to hang motionless beneath a flight of grass-barnacles travelling
westward.

Cumhal watched the birds, and his face changed.

"They go where there are no walls," he said, "over
bays no bell has commanded and islands where the seals
lift human eyes from the foam. Happy are they, for
the wind is their abbot and the sea sets them
no penance."

The cellarer struck the apples from his hand.

"This is vanity," he said.

"It made you laugh."

"That is how we know its danger."

They restored the cross to his shoulder and went onward.
Another half-mile along the road Cumhal stopped beside a thorn
tree where frost sparkled among the red berries.

"I cannot breathe," he said. "Let me tell one small
tale, no longer than a wren's tail, and the telling
will shorten the road."

Before the cellarer could forbid him, Cumhal began.

"There was once a bishop who preached that every beast
had been appointed to its proper labour. The horse must
carry, the cow give milk, the dog guard, the goose
hiss, and the ass endure. Now an ass heard this
sermon through the chapel door and entered, saying, 'My lord
bishop, what labour has God appointed to you?' The bishop
replied, 'To govern fools.' 'Then,' said the ass, 'I beg
your blessing, for we are brothers in the same trade.'"

The youngest friar laughed so suddenly that he dropped his
rosary. Another hid his mouth in his sleeve. Even the
lay brother's red nose quivered.

Cumhal told of a miser who tried to bribe Saint
Peter with the same penny he had withheld from a
beggar; of a hen that confessed to stealing barley and
was absolved because she supplied the abbey kitchen; and of
a demon who fled from a theologian, crying that hell
itself had never prepared him for so long an argument.

The friars laughed until tears shone upon their cheeks, but
when the laughter ceased they looked at one another in
shame.

"You have made mockery of sacred persons," said the cellarer.

"I made mockery of fools," said Cumhal. "If sacred persons
have entered the tale, it is by their own resemblance."

They beat him with cords, and the lay brother kicked
him where his doublet was most patched. Then they lifted
the cross again.

The road climbed among fields divided by black walls, and
noon passed into a dim, windless afternoon. At the third
mile they heard the sea muttering beyond the dunes. Cumhal
had grown so weak that his knees trembled, yet once
more he lowered the cross.

"Grant me a song," he said, "and thereafter I shall
trouble you no more."

"No song of harlots," said the cellarer.

"I will sing of a queen."

"That is often the same thing in heathen poetry."

Nevertheless Cumhal sang. His voice was cracked by cold and
thirst, but the first notes went out softly, as light
moves before dawn across a flooded lake. He sang of
Nessa of the Golden Eyelids, who loved Diarmuid of the
Ash Spear; and how he went across the sea with
thirty ships to win a kingdom for her; and how
false tidings came that he was slain; and how she
lay down beneath an apple tree and commanded her maidens
to bury her where the first blossom touched the grass.
He sang how Diarmuid returned on the seventh day, carrying
a crown and a cage of eastern birds, and found
her grave white with fallen petals. Then he opened the
cage, and the birds would not depart, but sang above
the grave until their hearts broke one after another.

The young friars bent their heads. One remembered a girl
who had gathered rushes beside the Moy before he entered
religion. Another remembered his mother combing her hair by the
fire. Another saw in his mind a road running west
under the evening star and wondered whether it remained open.
Their buried lives stirred within them like salmon under winter
ice.

When the last note faded, the cellarer's face was grey.

"You have awakened unlawful thoughts."

"I awakened what was already alive," said Cumhal.

"That is worse."

They struck him again, and those who had wept struck
hardest. Blood ran from his lip and made a bright
thread down his pale chin.

At length they reached a bare hill above the strand,
where two uprights already stood, black against the sea. Around
them waited beggars from the roads: blind men, limping women,
children with old faces, a leper wrapped in sacking, and
a grey-haired fellow whose hands shook continually though the air
was still. They had come because executions sometimes left behind
shoes, cords, scraps of cloth or food not eaten by
the condemned.

Cumhal laid down the cross.

"Before you nail me," he said, "let me eat what
remains in my wallet."

The friars agreed, for the rule of the abbey commended
moderation in all things. Cumhal sat upon a stone and
emptied the wallet between his feet. There were two oatcakes,
an onion, a rind of cheese, three hazelnuts, half a
salted fish, a twist of honeycomb blackened with lint, coloured
ribbons, six wooden balls, a cracked flute and a little
bronze mirror.

The beggars stared at the food.

"Behold my treasure," said Cumhal. "Kings have less, because they
cannot carry their kingdoms in a wallet."

"You are rich enough to jest," said the grey-haired beggar.

"No," replied Cumhal, "I am the poorest man beneath the
turning sky. For poverty is not to go without shoes
when the feet are content with dust, nor without a
roof when the stars make a bright house. Poverty is
to bear within oneself a city of high towers and
shining bridges, and to wake each morning among pig-sties. It
is to carry in the heart a woman more beautiful
than the first day of summer, with hair like dark
rivers under the moon, and never to find her face
among living women. It is to hear music fit for
Aengus the Subtle-hearted and bring forth only a cracked tune
that men reward with mouldy bread. My outward life has
always limped behind my inward life, as a lame dog
follows a chariot. Therefore I am poorer than you all."

The blind man laughed bitterly.

"Fine words do not fill the belly."

"That is true," said Cumhal, and divided an oatcake, the
cheese and the salted fish among them. He kept only
the onion, one hazelnut and a morsel of honeycomb.

At once the beggars fell upon the gift. The lame
woman struck the leper with her crutch; the grey-haired man
thrust a child into the mud; the blind man crawled
among their legs, snapping like a dog; and they cursed
one another by saints, demons, graves and diseases. While they
fought, the friars stretched Cumhal upon the cross.

The first nail went through his right hand. He cried
out to Lugh of the Long Arm, and the friars
crossed themselves. The second went through his left, and he
called upon Christ, who also had known nails. The cellarer
hesitated at that, but only for a moment. They drove
the last nail through both feet, raised the cross with
ropes, and dropped it into its hole. The shock tore
a great groan from Cumhal, and the sea answered from
below.

The friars recited a prayer, gathered their tools and departed
before darkness, lest the tide cover the safe path across
the sands.

For a while the beggars remained, searching the ground for
crumbs. Cumhal watched the line of brown habits diminish along
the road.

"Friends," he called, "stay near me tonight. Drive away the
birds and beasts. You are cast from warm halls, as
I am; surely the rejected should keep faith with the
rejected."

The grey-haired beggar found one of the wooden balls and
put it into his pocket.

"Did you not call yourself poorer than all of us?"

"I did."

"Then be richer in courage."

The others laughed.

"Outcasts," cried Cumhal, "will you abandon an outcast?"

At that word their faces hardened, for no man loves
the name that tells the truth about him. They picked
up stones and pelted him. One cut his brow, another
bruised his ribs, and a third struck his mouth.

"Keep your proud names for yourself," they shouted, and went
away disputing over the cracked flute and the bronze mirror.

Evening entered the world. The abbey bell sounded faintly inland,
and each stroke seemed to build another wall between Cumhal
and every living hearth. Far overhead the grass-barnacles passed onward,
their cries falling through the dusk like fragments of a
language spoken before men learned obedience.

Then the three ravens from the bridge came beating over
the dunes. They settled upon the crossbeam, glossy and patient,
and one leaned down to examine his eye.

"Black counsellor," whispered Cumhal, "I asked you for a sheep."

The raven struck his cheek with its beak.

After nightfall wolves came from the scrub beyond the river,
lean from winter and silent as drifting smoke. They circled
the cross, then rose upon their hind legs and tore
at his nailed feet. Cumhal cried to the abbey, to
the beggars, to the wandering birds, to the heroes under
the hills, and to the bright inward city he had
never entered. No answer came but the grinding sea.

At last he lifted his head, though the effort was
well-nigh beyond him, and spoke into the immense darkness.

"I made songs for those within walls, and they feared
me. I gave bread to those outside walls, and they
hated me. Where, then, shall the wandering heart find its
own people?"

The wind came over the salley gardens and moved through
his torn doublet as through a tattered banner. It carried
his question above the dunes, beyond the yew trees and
the sleeping abbey, and outward across the waters where no
bell governed the waves; but whether any heard it—saint, merrow,
drowned king, or lonely bird—the old tale does not say.

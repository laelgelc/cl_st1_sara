In the First Beginning, when the world had been put
together in a hurry and the paint was scarcely dry
upon it, all the High Veldt was one enormous sandy-yellowish-brownish
colour from sunrise to supper-time. There were sandy-yellow rocks, and
sandy-brown grass, and yellow-brown dust that got into the noses
of the animals and made them sneeze—Hurrashoo!—exactly when they wished
to be dignified.

Upon this High Veldt lived a Leopard; but he had
no spots.

Remember that particularly, Best Beloved. He was a plain, pale,
biscuit-coloured Leopard, as blank as a breakfast bun and almost
as conceited. With him lived an Ethiopian hunter, and he
too was pale-brownish-goldish, because that was the best colour for
the High Veldt in those early unfinished days.

Together they hunted the Giraffe and the Zebra and the
Kudu and the Hartebeest and the Bushbuck and every other
long-legged, quick-eared, dinner-avoiding animal they could discover.

The Leopard would flatten himself beside a sandy rock, and
the Ethiopian would lie among the sandy grass, and the
animals would look straight at them and say, "Nothing here
but ordinary geography."

Then—Hi! Hi!—up would leap the Leopard, and—Ho! Ho!—up would spring
the Ethiopian, and somebody would very nearly become supper.

This happened at breakfast-time, dinner-time, tea-time, and the uncomfortable little
time just before bed when one feels hollow under the
waistcoat. It was altogether too frequent for the animals.

So the Giraffe called a Council beneath a dusty acacia.

"We are being eaten by the landscape," he said.

"Not by the landscape," said the Zebra, who liked accuracy.
"By two persons dressed to resemble it."

"That is worse," said the Kudu.

"Much worse," said the Hartebeest.

"'Sclusively worse," said the Bushbuck, who had heard a long
word once and was determined not to waste it.

They considered the matter forward, backward, sideways, and upside down.
At last the Giraffe, who could see farther because of
his neck, said, "Beyond the High Veldt there is a
forest full of shadows. Let us go there."

So they went—Giraffe and Zebra, Kudu and Hartebeest, Bushbuck and
all the rest—softly, secretly, hoof after hoof, until the sandy
High Veldt was left with nothing upon it except the
Leopard, the Ethiopian, the dust, and several extremely surprised grasshoppers.

The forest was not one colour. Oh no!

It was stripy and streaky and flecky and freckly. Sunlight
came down between the leaves in golden ladders, shadows lay
across the ground like spilled ink, and every branch made
a different pattern. There were black bars, green pools, brown
blotches, silver dots, purple dimnesses, and bright little coin-shaped flashes
that danced whenever the wind wiggled the leaves.

Say "wind wiggled the leaves" quickly, Best Beloved, and you
will hear the forest rustling.

At first the Giraffe was too yellow for the forest,
and the Zebra was too pale, and the others showed
up like cakes on a black tablecloth. But they stayed
very still where the shadows touched them.

Day after day the leaf-light fell in broken golden patches
upon the Giraffe until the patches stuck. Night after night
the narrow shadows lay across the Zebra until the shadows
stuck too. The Kudu became duskier, the Bushbuck became dappled,
and the Hartebeest acquired several useful shades of uncertain brown.

They did indeed!

After a while the Giraffe looked like sunlight broken by
branches, the Zebra looked like moonlight behind reeds, and the
Bushbuck looked like six shadows arguing in a shrubbery. Unless
they moved, you could not tell where animal ended and
forest began.

Meanwhile, on the High Veldt, the Leopard and the Ethiopian
waited for breakfast.

Breakfast did not arrive.

They waited for dinner.

Dinner failed to keep its appointment.

They waited for tea, but tea was only a dry
grass-stalk and a beetle who had important business elsewhere.

The Leopard grew lean from his whiskers to his tail-tip.
The Ethiopian tightened his belt until it nearly went around
him twice.

"I can smell Giraffe," said the Leopard. "I can smell
Zebra. I can smell Kudu and Bushbuck and a small
but nourishing something with four legs. But I can't see
'em."

"I can hear Giraffe," said the Ethiopian. "I can hear
Zebra. I can hear Kudu and Bushbuck and something licking
its nose behind a tree. But I can't locate the
present habitat of the aboriginal Fauna."

"Fiddle!" said the Leopard. "Where have they gone?"

So they went to consult Baviaan, the Baboon, who was
the Wisest Animal in All the Neighbouring Districts and knew
it. He sat on a red stone, holding his chin
in one hand and a wild fig in the other,
looking twice as wise as was strictly necessary.

"O Baviaan," said the Ethiopian, "the aboriginal Fauna has absconded."

"Gone away," translated the Leopard.

"I know," said Baviaan.

"Our internal arrangements are empty," said the Ethiopian.

"Hungry," translated the Leopard.

"I know," said Baviaan.

"Then tell us where our dinners have gone," said both
hunters.

Baviaan ate the fig very slowly, including the part with
an ant on it. Then he said, "The game has
changed, because the game has gone into a changeable place."

"That sounds like wisdom," said the Leopard, "but it smells
like nonsense."

"Go after it," said Baviaan. "And when you find it,
change as much as you can. Particularly you, Leopard."

"Change what?" asked the Ethiopian.

"Your appearances."

"How?"

"Go into other spots," said Baviaan.

"Other places?" asked the Leopard.

"Other spots," said Baviaan.

"Spots where?"

"Exactly," said Baviaan, and he would say no more, because
he had another fig and meant to be mysterious over
it.

The Leopard and the Ethiopian followed the tracks of the
animals away from the High Veldt. They crossed the dry
river-bed, climbed the red ridge, passed three thorn trees and
a rock shaped like a pudding, and came at last
to the forest.

The Ethiopian entered first. The Leopard followed with his stomach
close to the ground.

"Ummm," said the Leopard. "It is dark."

"It is diversified by an excessive multiplicity of optical interruptions,"
said the Ethiopian.

"Patchy," said the Leopard.

"Precisely."

They crept beneath the stripy, speckly, flickery leaves. Presently the
Ethiopian whispered, "I can smell Zebra."

"I can hear Zebra," whispered the Leopard.

"But I can't see Zebra."

They stared into a tangle of black stems and white
sun-bars.

Then one of the white bars twitched.

"Zebra!" cried the Leopard.

But the Zebra slipped between the shadows, and the hunters
collided nose-first with a tree.

A little later the Leopard said, "I can smell Giraffe."

"I can hear Giraffe," said the Ethiopian.

"But I can't see Giraffe."

They looked into a golden-brown confusion of leaves, branches, light-patches,
bark-patches, and gaps.

Then several of the patches blinked.

"Giraffe!" cried the Ethiopian.

But the Giraffe folded his long legs, unfolded them, and
vanished into the blotchy brightness, while the hunters tumbled together
into a thorn-bush.

All day it was the same. They could smell dinner
and hear dinner and almost touch dinner, but they could
not see dinner until dinner moved; and by then dinner
was generally departing at a speed unsuitable for carving.

At sunset they sat beside a pool and examined one
another.

The Leopard shone pale-gold against the dark earth like a
pat of butter on a boot.

The Ethiopian showed among the shadows like a brass button
in a coal-bin.

"No wonder," said the Ethiopian. "We are conspicuous."

"Painfully," said the Leopard, removing a thorn from his tail.

"This environment demands chromatic readjustment."

"Speak short words."

"We must change."

"Baviaan said that," replied the Leopard. "He also said spots."

The Ethiopian looked at the pool, which was dark under
the trees. He looked at the damp black mud along
its edge. He looked at the forest, which was deepest
brown wherever the light did not reach.

Then he laughed.

"I shall change first," he said.

He took the dark forest colour—not mud, Best Beloved, though
it looked something like it, but the deep shade that
sleeps beneath leaves—and rubbed it over his arms, his legs,
his shoulders, his back, and his face. Wherever he touched
himself, his skin became rich dark-brown, deeper than polished wood
and warmer than midnight earth.

When he had finished, he stood beneath the trees, and
the shadows welcomed him so completely that only his eyes
and teeth showed when he smiled.

"Where have you gone?" asked the Leopard.

"I am here."

"Come out."

"I am already out."

The Leopard walked around him three times, bumping into him
twice.

"That is extremely clever," he said at last. "But what
about me? Shall I become dark all over?"

The Ethiopian considered. "No. You sleep on branches and creep
through patches of sun as well as shade. One colour
would be insufficiently complicated."

"Then complicate me," said the Leopard. "But do it handsomely."

The Ethiopian spread his fingers and looked at them. They
were black from the forest colour—five black fingertips on each
hand.

"Baviaan said you must go into other spots," he murmured.

"I have gone into another spot," said the Leopard. "This
forest is another spot."

"No," said the Ethiopian. "I believe he intended a more
literal application."

Before the Leopard could ask what "literal application" meant, the
Ethiopian pressed his five fingertips upon the Leopard's shoulder.

Pat!

They left five black marks close together, like a tiny
dark flower.

"Hi!" cried the Leopard. "What are you doing?"

"Giving you spots."

"You have made fingerprints!"

"Several fingerprints arranged with artistic purpose become a spot."

He pressed again.

Pat! Pat!

On the Leopard's back appeared another cluster and another—four marks
around a fifth, or five around an empty middle, never
perfectly round and never exactly alike.

"Not too many," said the Leopard anxiously.

Pat-pat! Patter-pat!

"Not too few," he added.

The Ethiopian printed spots upon the Leopard's shoulders, ribs, flanks,
legs, neck, and tail. He made them close together where
the forest was darkest and wider apart where the golden
light fell. He left the Leopard's throat pale and put
especially splendid clusters along his sides, because the Leopard insisted
upon seeing his best side reflected in the pool.

When it was done, the Leopard stood beneath a branch.

At once his pale coat became little pieces of sunlight,
and his black spots became little pieces of shadow. His
outline broke into sprottles and dottles and dusky rosettes. You
could see light, and you could see shade, but you
could not see Leopard—not unless he winked.

The Zebra came cautiously to drink.

"I can smell Leopard," he said. "I can hear Leopard
breathing. But I can't see—"

"Ho!" said the Leopard softly.

The Zebra went away in a tremendous hurry, and the
Leopard laughed until every spot shook.

After that, the Ethiopian and the Leopard could hunt in
the forest without looking like misplaced furniture. They did not
catch everything, because the Giraffe had his blotches and the
Zebra had his stripes and the Bushbuck was still six
shadows arguing in a shrubbery. But they caught enough for
breakfast, dinner, tea, and sometimes a small midnight snack.

The Ethiopian remained dark because it suited the forest light
and because he was content with it. The Leopard kept
his clustered fingertip spots because they were useful, becoming, and
admired by everyone except the animals he hunted.

And that is how the Leopard got his spots.

If anyone tells you a leopard cannot change them, you
may answer that this is quite true now. But once—only
once, in the First Beginning—he changed them very thoroughly indeed.

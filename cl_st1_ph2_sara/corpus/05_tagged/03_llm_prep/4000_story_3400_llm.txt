## The Seventeenth of August

In the latter years of his ministry, when the Reverend
Murdoch Soulis had grown grey in Balweary and the children
of those he had baptized were themselves bringing infants to
the font, there remained about him a terror which neither
time nor prayer had wholly subdued.

He was a tall, spare man, still upright despite his
age, with a face that seemed less worn by years
than scorched by some inward fire. His eyes, ordinarily lowered
and mild, would at moments lift suddenly to an empty
doorway or a darkened pane, and then the nearest observer
might see in them the fixed attention of one who
watches an old enemy. He lived alone, though the parish
would gladly have supplied him with a housekeeper; and after
sunset no persuasion could induce him to cross the threshold
of the manse. If duty called him late to a
deathbed, he remained in that house until daylight. He would
not travel the moor in darkness, not even with company.

Once in every year, on the seventeenth day of August,
he preached concerning the adversary of mankind.

It was no discourse of metaphor, nor any courteous theology
concerning temptation and the frailties of the flesh. He spoke
of the Devil as of a person known to him:
subtle, embodied, watchful, and near. He named him the Accuser,
the Father of Lies, the Tenant of Empty Flesh; and
his voice, beginning low, would rise until the rafters shook
and the people sat rigid in their pews. At the
last he always stopped abruptly, with both hands gripping the
pulpit, and stared down the aisle as though he saw
beyond the church door into a region no other eye
could enter.

Then he would say:

"Resist him, though he come wearing the face of one
ye have loved. Resist him in the Name."

Afterward he returned to the manse and barred himself in
until morning.

Balweary was not a place likely to lessen such impressions.
The village lay in a hollow country between the Black
Hill and the Dule Water, with Hanging Shaw darkening the
east and a spread of broken moor to the west.
The kirk and manse stood apart from the cottages, upon
a low rise reached by a stony causeway. Behind the
manse was an old burial-ground, long disused, where the grass
grew rank among leaning stones; beyond that, the ground fell
into a marshy hollow called the Devil's Hag. The names
were older than any written charter, and the people, who
knew the histories belonging to them, did not care to
explain them after nightfall.

What occurred at Balweary in the first summer of Mr
Soulis's ministry was spoken of for two generations. By then
fact, recollection, and warning had been woven together, as they
will be in country places. Yet those who doubted might
have found it hard to account for the minister's altered
countenance, or for the scorched stone that long remained beside
the manse path, or for the strange sermon of every
seventeenth of August.

Old Andrew Brodie, who was a herd-laddie in those days
and lived to be ninety, told the matter thus.

---

"Aye, ye've heard tell o' Mr Soulis as he was
after it," Andrew would begin, setting down his glass and
looking toward the fire. "But ye didna ken him afore.
That's the differ. Ye saw an auld man wi' the
fear o' God in him, an' maybe another fear besides;
but I mind the young callant that cam' riding into
Balweary wi' his books tied ahint the saddle an' his
head fu' o' learning.

"He wasna abune five-and-twenty, Mr Soulis, an' as braw a
scholar as ever wagged a gown in Embro'. Latin? He
had it. Greek? He could ha'e scolded a Greek dominie
in his ain tongue. He had logic, philosophy, physic, an'
a hantle o' new notions about the natural causes o'
things folk had aye kent to be unco. But for
a' his learning, he hadna yet learned that the world's
wider than a library, nor that the deil can quote
a clever man's reasoning back at him.

"The first clash cam' afore he'd been three weeks in
the parish.

"There was an auld wife named Janet M'Clour, that bided
in a clay biggin by the Dule. She had been
bonnie once, folk said, but gin I mind her she
was lean as a winter kail-stalk, wi' red-rimmed een and
a tongue that could strip the bark frae a tree.
She gathered simples on the hill, helped at births, laid
out the deid, an' kenned when a coo was bewitched
or only eaten ower much wet clover. So, by course,
half the parish went to her for aid, and the
same half cried witch when the aid went wrang.

"Weel, there came a Sabbath when wee Davie Sanderson took
a fit in the kirk-yard. The bairn fell among the
graves, girned till his mouth was white, and kicked as
though six men were hauling at him. His mother screamed
that Janet had overlooked him, for the auld wife had
cursed her hens the day afore. That was enough. The
guidwives raised a skirl, the men gathered, and in less
time than ye could say the Lord's Prayer they had
Janet at the Dule Water, meaning to duck her.

"I was there—no' helping, mind ye, but a laddie follows
a crowd the way a craw follows the plough. They
had a rope about her waist, and she was spitting
and swearing fit to set the water boiling.

"Then down the brae came Mr Soulis.

"'What wickedness is this?' says he.

"'Justice!' cried Elspeth Sanderson. 'The wife has witched my bairn.'

"'Your bairn suffers from an illness,' says the minister, 'and
you from ignorance.'

"That didna please them. Ignorance is a complaint few folk
will own, though they'll confess to rheumatism by the hour.

"Tammas Rusk held up the rope. 'She'll sink if she's
honest and soom if she's a witch.'

"'And if she sinks?'

"'Then she's honest.'

"'And drowned,' says Mr Soulis.

"He took the rope from Tammas's hand. There wasna much
strength in the minister then, but there was authority, an'
that will whiles lift what muscle canna stir. He rebuked
them from Scripture, named murder by its right name, and
threatened the magistrate besides. Syne the men began looking at
their boots, and the women minded their dinners, and the
crowd melted from about the burn.

"Janet stood dripping on the bank, her grey hair clapped
to her skull and a look on her face that
would sour milk.

"'You are safe now,' says Mr Soulis.

"'Safe?' says she. 'An' wha made me unsafe?'

"'These foolish accusations.'

"She looked after the folk, then back at him. 'Ye're
a braw young cock to craw sae loud. See that
the nicht doesna wring your neck.'

"'You should be grateful,' says he.

"'Grateful!' she cried, and laughed till the sound went through
me like a saw. 'Ye've ta'en me frae the water.
Maybe the water wasna the worst.'

"Now, that was Janet's way, and there were some said
afterward she had given him fair warning. Mr Soulis heard
only an embittered old woman. When he learned that her
landlord had turned her out and no soul in Balweary
would shelter her, what must he do but take her
to the manse as housekeeper?

"Ye may smile, but there was courage in it, and
charity too. Pride as well, maybe. The young are fond
o' making sermons wi' their deeds, and Mr Soulis meant
the parish to learn that superstition would get no footing
under his roof.

"Janet went up the causeway after him, dripping all the
road.

"That very nicht the change came upon her.

"Nane could say what happened between sunset and dawn. The
minister slept soundly, for he'd ridden far that day, and
the manse walls were thick. But about four in the
morning Kirstie Low came past wi' milk from the farm,
and she saw Janet standing at the kitchen door.

"Kirstie dropped the pitcher.

"It was Janet's gown, Janet's face, Janet's grey hair; but
her head lay over upon her left shoulder as though
the neck had been wrung and badly set. One eye
looked downward and the other straight ahead. Her mouth was
drawn at the corner, and when she tried to speak,
the words came thick and broken.

"'G-guid mornin', K-kirstie.'

"Kirstie ran screaming to the village.

"By breakfast-time, every soul in Balweary had heard that Janet
M'Clour was thrawn. Some said the mob had hurt her.
Some said a palsy had struck in the night. But
them that kenned best said the true Janet had died
before dawn, and something else had lifted her body and
put it on like a wet sark.

"Mr Soulis would hear none o't.

"He examined the woman as far as decency allowed, asked
her questions, and concluded that fright, cold, and rough usage
had brought on a seizure.

"'Can ye say the Lord's name?' asked old Isobel Pringle,
who had come bold as brass to the kitchen.

"Mr Soulis turned on her. 'Leave my house.'

"'Let her say it.'

"Janet's jaw laboured. Her teeth clicked. 'The—the G-guid—'

"'His Name,' says Isobel.

"Janet's crooked eye rolled toward her, and there came a
grin on the slack side of her mouth.

"'Gae,' she muttered. 'Gae doun.'

"'Say God,' Isobel demanded.

"Janet made a sound like water bubbling in a drain.

"Mr Soulis put the old woman out himself.

"From that day, Janet kept the manse. She cooked, though
Mr Soulis ate little of what she set before him;
she swept, though the dust seemed ever to gather behind
her; and she washed the minister's black clothes at the
burn, beating them upon the stones while the bairns watched
from the far bank. She could say 'kirk' and 'Bible'
and 'heaven,' if she tried. But God's Name never crossed
her lips. If challenged, she would grin that sideways grin,
and her teeth would click thegither.

"She grew stranger in her gait as the weeks passed.
Ye'd see her coming up from the Dule at gloamin',
her basket on one arm, her head lying on her
shoulder, and every step set down slow and exact—as if
walking was a thing she had learned by watching others.
Dogs wouldna pass her on the road. Horses sweated when
she came near. Once the minister's own mare broke its
halter and leapt the dyke rather than stand while Janet
entered the stable.

"Still Mr Soulis held to his learning.

"'Animals are sensitive to deformity,' says he.

"'Aye,' says Rob Muir, 'an' maybe to something else.'

"'There is no something else.'

"'Then ye'll sleep easy,' says Rob.

"But whether he slept easy or no, the minister's face
began to alter. He was quieter in company, and twice
he asked the beadle if any stranger had been seen
about the parish. He preached against credulity; yet he started
when the kirk door creaked, and when Janet sat below
the pulpit, twisted and still, his eye would find her
as a needle finds north.

"Then came the heat.

"I've lived through sixty summers since, but never one like
that. June dried the burns to threads. July burned the
heather grey. The cattle stood knee-deep in the Dule, too
weary to crop the bank, and the birds went silent
at noon. There was no wind, not on the hill,
not among the ash leaves, not even in Hanging Shaw.
Day after day the sky hung white over Balweary, and
night after night the dark came warm and breathless, wi'
a red smother along the north.

"The folk slept wi' their doors open, though they barred
them fast enough when they heard steps outside.

"For there were steps, or so it was said. A
tramp along the moor where no road ran. A foot
on the causeway after midnight. A stir in the old
graveyard and the crows rising before dawn.

"On the second Tuesday of August, Mr Soulis went to
visit a sick woman beyond Black Hill. He returned late,
taking the path by the Devil's Hag, though the folk
had warned him against it. The sun was low, red
as a furnace mouth, and the old burial-ground lay directly
between him and the manse.

"As he came among the stones, he saw a man.

"Mind me: when we spoke of the black man, we
meant no traveller from foreign lands. This thing had no
nation that God created. It was black the way a
hole is black, or a burned tree at midnight. Clothes,
face, hands—Mr Soulis could make out no difference among them,
save for two pale sparks where the eyes should be.

"The stranger was crouched beside an open grave.

"'Who are you?' cried Mr Soulis.

"The black man rose. He was tall—ower tall—and thin, and
for one instant he stood with his head bent, listening.
Then he turned and ran.

"Mr Soulis was young, and there was anger in him
as well as fear. He thought of thieves, resurrection-men, or
some rogue seeking to feed the parish tales. So he
ran after.

"Away went the black man among the graves, never stumbling.
Over the broken wall he went, across the dried marsh,
and up toward the manse. Mr Soulis followed, losing ground
on the rise but keeping the figure in sight. The
stranger reached the garden dyke. He didna climb it. He
seemed to pass through the shadow of an elder bush
and was gone.

"The minister came up panting.

"There was no gate there, no gap, no place a
child could hide. The garden lay bare in the evening
light. Behind him was the empty causeway; before him, the
manse.

"Then the kitchen door opened.

"Janet stood there.

"Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Her hands were
wet, and something dark dripped from her fingers to the
threshold.

"'Wha was yon?' Mr Soulis demanded.

"Janet watched him with the eye that faced forward. 'Wha—wha
was what?'

"'The man who came this way.'

"'Nae man.'

"'I saw him.'

"'Ye saw—' Her teeth clicked, once, twice. 'Ye saw naething.'

"At that the minister felt what he later called the
cauld grue: a coldness beginning under the heart and spreading
through the body, though the air was hot enough to
melt tallow. He looked at Janet's wet hands. He looked
beyond her into the black kitchen. And for the first
time he asked himself what manner of illness could twist
a neck without killing, still a tongue before one sacred
word, and teach a human face to smile like a
trap.

"He said nothing more. But that evening he went through
his books, not the medical volumes this time, but the
older works he had mocked at college: accounts of possession,
apparitions, revenants, and the powers of evil. He read until
the candle guttered. Whiles he shook his head; whiles he
glanced at the ceiling, for Janet's room was above.

"Next day he went to the burial-ground.

"The grave beside which he had seen the stranger was
old and sunken. No earth had been disturbed. Yet on
the flat stone at its head were three marks, long
and narrow, as if made by fingers pressed into soft
clay. The stone itself was hard granite.

"Crows circled overhead, though there was no wind to hold
them up.

"From then till the seventeenth, Mr Soulis was seen little
in the village. He kept to the manse, reading and
praying. Janet moved about him as before, setting his meals
down, carrying water, trailing her slow shadow from room to
room. He tried her twice with the Name. Once she
pretended not to hear. Once she turned upon him so
quickly that her neck gave a dry crack, and the
sound silenced him.

"On the evening of the seventeenth, the heat was worst
of all.

"There was a haze over the Dule, and clouds stood
piled behind Black Hill, but no thunder came. The air
in the manse was close as a sealed tomb. Mr
Soulis ate no supper. Janet sat in the kitchen, her
hands folded and her head upon her shoulder, humming through
her crooked mouth.

"Near ten, she rose.

"'I'm awa' to my bed,' she said.

"It was the clearest sentence she had spoken since the
change.

"Mr Soulis looked up.

"Janet grinned.

"'A guid nicht to ye, meenister.'

"She went out. Step by step she crossed the passage.
Step by step she climbed the stair. At the top
she paused, and Mr Soulis, sitting below with the Bible
open before him, heard three small sounds:

"Click. Click. Click.

"Her teeth.

"Then the chamber door shut.

"The minister remained in the kitchen. He tried to read
the Psalms but found himself following every noise of the
house: the settling of wood, the faint scrape of a
branch against stone, the ticking of the clock. Eleven passed.
Then midnight. Still there was no wind.

"A little after one, he heard a foot stamp overhead.

"Then another.

"Then came a dragging, a thud against the wall, and
a low muttering in two voices.

"Two, Mr Soulis swore thereafter, though there was but one
creature in the room: one voice thick and broken like
Janet's, the other deep and rapid, speaking no tongue he
knew.

"He took the candle and went to the stair.

"The muttering ceased.

"'Janet?' he called.

"No answer.

"He climbed. The flame bent though the air was still.
At the chamber door he listened. Something inside gave one
last violent struggle; the bedstead struck the wall; then all
was quiet.

"Mr Soulis lifted the latch.

"The room was empty at first sight. The bedclothes were
tossed, the casement open, and the curtain hanging without a
stir. Then his candle rose, and he saw her.

"Janet hung from a nail behind the door.

"It was a small nail, scarcely fit to hold a
coat, and from it ran a single length of grey
worsted no thicker than knitting thread. Yet there she swung,
her bare feet a hand's breadth from the floor, her
body turning little by little. Her neck was no longer
bent sideways. It was stretched straight and long, and the
face beneath it had gone dark.

"Mr Soulis stood frozen.

"Then one of the dead eyes opened.

"Only a fraction. Only enough to show a thread of
white.

"He backed out, pulled the door shut, and turned the
key. He took the key with him. Downstairs he went—not
running, for his knees would scarce obey him—and into the
kitchen, where he laid the Bible on the table and
tried to pray.

"He couldna.

"The words were there, but his mouth was dry, and
his thoughts flew apart like frightened birds. He told himself
Janet had taken her life. He told himself the eye
had not opened. He told himself the thread might be
stronger than it looked and the nail set deeper than
appeared.

"Then the wind began.

"It came first within the house, not without. A breath
moved down the chimney and scattered cold ashes across the
hearth. The kitchen door lifted on its hinges. Papers rustled
on the table. Yet through the window he could see
the elder bush standing still beneath the heavy sky.

"Above him there came a faint creak.

"The nail, he thought.

"Creak.

"The thread.

"Creak.

"Then a soft sound, like bare feet touching the floor.

"Mr Soulis rose.

"There came a scratch at the chamber door. A pause.
Another scratch, lower down, as though fingers were feeling for
the latch.

"He had locked it. The key lay on the table
before him.

"The latch lifted.

"The chamber door opened.

"Ye maun understand, he heard each thing. No haste, no
confusion. First the latch, then the hinge, then a pause
on the landing. After that came the steps.

"One.

"A drag.

"Two.

"A drag.

"Three.

"Each stair cried under the weight. Mr Soulis watched the
kitchen doorway, and the cauld grue took him so that
his teeth knocked in his head.

"Halfway down, the thing stopped.

"A thread gave a little twang in the dark.

"Then the steps came on.

"Four.

"Five.

"Six.

"The minister seized the Bible, but his hand shook and
the book fell open upon the floor. He looked toward
the outer door. It stood ajar. Whether he chose to
flee or whether the Lord drew him forth, he never
could say; but he crossed the threshold backward, keeping his
face toward the passage, and came into the yard.

"The sky was black now, cloud upon cloud, but still
no rain had fallen. A pale, sickly light lay over
the causeway. The old graves beyond the dyke seemed to
lean nearer.

"Within the manse, the dragging reached the bottom stair.

"A hand appeared around the passage wall.

"It was grey and swollen, the fingers crooked, and about
one wrist trailed the broken worsted thread.

"Then came the arm, the shoulder, and Janet's face.

"Deid she was. There was no deceiving it now. Her
neck was lengthened by the rope, her tongue dark between
her teeth, her eyes glazed and starting. Yet she walked.
Step by step, dragging one foot, she came through the
kitchen and turned toward the open door.

"Behind her, high upon the passage wall, her shadow rose
in another shape: tall, narrow, and black, with a head
that brushed the ceiling.

"Mr Soulis saw it, and knowledge entered him whole.

"This was no sick wife. This was no palsy, no
village ignorance, no harmless matter for learned argument. Janet M'Clour
was dead—maybe since the night of the Dule, maybe only
since the hanging—and a tenant not born of woman had
lodged in her flesh.

"The corpse reached the threshold.

"I've heard folk say courage means feeling no fear. That's
blethers. A man is courageous when the fear has eaten
the marrow of him and still he stands. Mr Soulis
was near senseless with terror, but somewhere beneath his learning
and pride there remained the faith he had preached before
he understood its cost.

"He lifted both hands.

"'In the Name of God,' he said.

"The corpse stopped.

"The shadow behind it spread along the wall.

"Janet's jaws opened. From them came the deep voice he
had heard overhead.

"'He is not here.'

"'He is everywhere,' said Mr Soulis.

"The thing took one step.

"'Ye saved this flesh,' it said. 'Ye opened the door.'

"'I saved a woman from murder.'

"'An' gave me a house.'

"Another step. The smell of wet earth came with it,
though the ground was dry.

"Mr Soulis's voice broke, but he raised it again.

"'Whatever ye are, ye have no claim here.'

"The corpse smiled, and the stretched neck bent gradually sideways
until the head rested once more upon the shoulder.

"'Nae claim?' it said. 'She cursed Him. She called me.
They drove her to the water, and ye—ye proud bairn
wi' your books—ye took my hand and led me hame.'

"At that the minister nearly yielded, for the words struck
where his guilt lay hidden. But from the open Bible
at his feet a phrase returned to him—not as print
upon a page, but as a command remembered from before
memory.

"Resist.

"He stepped toward the corpse.

"'Janet M'Clour, if aught of you remains, the Lord have
mercy upon your soul. But you that use her—hear me.'

"The thing recoiled.

"Mr Soulis pointed past it, toward the burial-ground and the
dark hollow of the Hag.

"'Go back to the grave, if the grave will hold
ye. Go down to hell, if hell will take ye.
By the Blood, by the Word, by the Name ye
cannot speak, I command ye—depart!'

"Janet's body sprang at him.

"At that same instant the whole world turned white.

"There was no space between flash and stroke. Lightning came
down upon the yard with a noise that split the
night. Mr Soulis was hurled against the dyke. The manse
windows burst outward. Every bell in the kirk began ringing
without a hand.

"And where the corpse had stood there rose a pillar
of blue flame.

"It burned for no more than a breath. Then the
rain fell—great warm drops at first, then sheets of water
driven by a wind that tore limbs from the ashes
and flattened the heather on the hill. Thunder went rolling
over Balweary, stroke upon stroke, and the Dule rose black
between its banks.

"The folk found Mr Soulis at dawn, crouched beside the
causeway with the Bible clasped to his breast. He could
not speak. His hair, that had been brown the day
before, was marked with a broad streak of white.

"Of Janet there remained only ashes, grease upon the stones,
two iron buckles from her shoes, and the little grey
thread.

"That same morning Rob Muir and three others saw the
black man crossing the crest of Black Hill. The storm
was still breaking, and he went against the wind with
such speed that no living man could have kept his
feet. When he reached the standing stones, he turned once
toward Balweary.

"Then the rain hid him.

"No trace was found beyond the hill."

---

For many weeks Murdoch Soulis lay between fever and insensibility.
When speech returned, he spoke first in Latin, then in
fragments of Scripture, and only after some months could he
give any connected account of the night. Even then he
would not name all that he had seen. The chamber
in which Janet died was stripped and sealed; later, when
the manse roof required rebuilding, Soulis ordered that entire portion
of the house pulled down.

He resumed his ministry, but the young scholar who had
ridden into Balweary did not resume it with him. His
contempt for the old beliefs was gone, though he never
encouraged cruelty against any suspected witch, seer, or unfortunate outcast.
Indeed, he preached more fiercely than before against the violence
of crowds. Superstition, he said, might be a lie; but
pride was another lie, and sometimes the Devil entered by
the door a man had opened merely to display his
wisdom.

He never married. He kept no servant overnight. Those who
visited the manse found iron lamps burning in every passage
and a Bible laid open beside each door. Yet children
loved him in daylight, and the sick called for him
willingly, for no minister in the district was more gentle
with mortal fear.

Only after dark did his face assume that listening look.

And once each year, upon the seventeenth of August, the
people of Balweary came from the Dule Water, Hanging Shaw,
the Black Hill, and the farms beyond the Devil's Hag.
They filled the kirk before sunset and waited in silence
while the aged minister climbed the pulpit stair.

Outside, perhaps, the evening would be fair. The moor might
lie purple and quiet; the ash leaves might scarcely stir.
But before Soulis had spoken ten minutes, the congregation would
imagine the oppression of that ancient heat, the open grave,
the slow foot upon the stair, and the dead hand
feeling along the wall.

Then the minister's eyes would fix upon the dark beneath
the gallery, and his voice would deepen.

"The powers of evil," he would say, "are not made
harmless because ye give them softer names. Nor is the
Lord absent because your courage fails. I have stood where
reason left me. I have seen the Tenant in the
empty house."

At these words the oldest parishioners bowed their heads, remembering
what their fathers had told them; and the youngest, though
they understood nothing, ceased to move.

Soulis would lean forward, his white hands trembling on the
pulpit.

"If it come with threats, resist it. If it come
with promises, resist it. If it come wearing pity, resist
it. And if it come wearing a kent face and
speaking with a kent tongue—look not to the face, but
call upon the Name."

Then, from somewhere in the settling kirk, there would sometimes
come a small dry sound, no more than the click
of shrinking timber.

At that sound the Reverend Murdoch Soulis always stopped.

And every soul in Balweary stopped with him.

## The Child of Lisnavoura

Between the brown and melancholy chain of the Ballyhoura hills
and that wide tract of bog which, in autumn weather,
lies level and dim as a lake of tarnished metal,
there runs a road little travelled except by turf-carts, cattle-drovers,
and the country people going eastward to Mass. It is
not, properly speaking, an ancient road; yet it crosses older
ways, whose stones are buried under rushes, and passes more
than one grass-grown foundation which the peasantry attribute, according to
their humour, either to the Danes, the monks, or the
Good People.

To the north may be seen the broken crown of
Kilfinnan Castle; westward, beyond the ash woods, the mountains rise
in long blue folds; and immediately south of the road
stands Lisnavoura.

Lisnavoura is not a high hill. A stranger might pass
it without remarking anything more extraordinary than a circular bank
near its summit, a few thorn-trees bent all one way
by the wind, and certain grey rocks protruding from the
heath like the ruined battlements of a fortress. But the
inhabitants of that district held it in no light estimation.
They would not willingly cross its western slope after sunset,
nor cut a branch from its thorns, nor remove even
a loose stone from the ring upon its top. Cattle,
they said, had been found there in a sweat, though
the night was cold; lights had gone round the old
enclosure against the course of the sun; and music, ineffably
sweet and small, had been heard beneath the ground when
no piper, fiddler, or wandering musician was within three miles
of the place.

It is necessary to observe that the name is commonly
interpreted in the neighbourhood as signifying the Fort of the
Yew, although there has been no yew upon it within
the memory of man. Some antiquaries, of whom I have
spoken elsewhere, maintained that the circular work was military. Old
Maurice O'Daly, the schoolmaster, declared it pagan and sacrificial. The
poorer people, who troubled themselves little with the disputes of
learned men, called it simply the Fairy Rath; and their
explanation, if less elaborate, was at least universally understood.

At the foot of this hill, where the bog-road turns
beside a stunted rowan-tree and then descends toward the river,
stood the cabin of Mary Ryan, a widow with four
children. It was a low dwelling, built of stone and
yellow clay, with two rooms, a roof of thatch, and
a small window looking westward. Behind it were a potato
garden, a turf-stack, and a shed for the goat. Before
the door a narrow strip of beaten earth sloped to
the road, and beyond that road the purple heath ran
upward toward Lisnavoura.

Mary's husband, Michael Ryan, had been killed three years before
by the fall of a bank in a quarry. He
had left her no property beyond the cabin, the goat,
a chest, several iron tools, and a debt at the
meal-shop in Kilfinnan. She supported herself and her children by
digging potatoes, cutting turf, spinning when flax could be had,
and assisting at harvest in the farms below the mountain.
Poverty, however, had not made her negligent. The cabin was
swept, the hearth-stone whitened, and the children, though patched in
every garment, kept as clean as the weather and their
occupations permitted.

The eldest, Nell, was nearly thirteen, grave and dark-haired, with
much of her mother's caution. After her came Con, a
sturdy boy of nine; little Rose, who had completed her
seventh year; and Billy, five years old, light-limbed, blue-eyed, and
possessed of such an abundance of curling golden hair that
strangers often stopped to look at him. This admiration gave
Mary no pleasure.

"Don't be praising him," she would say, drawing the child
close and making the sign of the cross upon his
forehead. "Beauty's no luck to a poor mother. God stand
between him and them that might covet him!"

By "them" she meant the inhabitants of Lisnavoura.

Her precautions were numerous. A horseshoe was nailed above the
door; a rowan cross, bound with red thread, hung behind
the cradle which Billy had long outgrown; house-leek grew upon
the roof; and a little bottle of holy water, renewed
every Easter, stood upon the dresser beside a blackened image
of the Blessed Virgin. No live coal was lent from
the hearth on May Eve. No water used in washing
the children's feet was thrown out after nightfall without the
warning cry, "Take care of the water!" And when the
wind turned suddenly round the cabin, lifting dust in a
narrow pillar, Mary blessed herself and compelled the children to
do likewise.

Such observances may appear contradictory to those who imagine that
an orthodox devotion must exclude every remnant of elder superstition.
In that part of Munster no contradiction was perceived. The
priest, the blessed candle, the mountain ash, and the iron
tongs were all regarded as forces enlisted upon the same
side against powers whose existence was attested by generations of
calamity.

Upon the evening when the misfortune occurred, Mary had gone
with a neighbour to bring home turf from a distant
clamp. The day was in the latter end of October—clear,
dry, and touched with that melancholy brightness which belongs only
to the decline of the year. The bog pools reflected
a pale sky; the fern had turned the colour of
rust; the ash leaves, thinned by the first frosts, dropped
one by one though scarcely a breath stirred them; and
over the western hills the sun hung red and enlarged,
as if reluctant to enter the bank of vapour awaiting
it.

Before leaving, Mary charged Nell to keep the younger children
near the house and bar the door before the shadow
of Lisnavoura crossed the road.

"Mind me now," she said, settling the rope of the
creel across her forehead. "The nights are drawing in, and
there's no knowing what does be travelling when decent Christians
should be within."

"Sure I'll mind them, mammy," answered Nell. "I have the
stirabout to make, and they'll not go beyond the rowan."

"You hear that, Con?"

"I do, mammy."

"And you, Rose?"

"Yes, mammy."

Billy, who sat on the threshold arranging acorns in a
broken saucer, looked up and smiled.

"Come in when Nell bids you, a chuisle," said Mary,
stooping to kiss him. "Don't be stopping out to see
the sun go down."

She departed along the bog-road, and Nell watched until the
turf-creels of the two women had dwindled to dark specks.
She then set about her household duties. The three younger
children played first beside the turf-stack, building a castle with
sods, and afterward crossed the road to gather blackberries from
a bramble which had somehow escaped the earlier searchers. Nell
called them back twice. They answered readily, and their voices,
mingling with the thin evening song of a robin, seemed
so near that she ceased to be anxious.

The stirabout, however, was slow in thickening, for the turf
upon the fire was damp. Nell knelt to blow the
embers, rose to fetch salt, and afterward went into the
inner room to bring out four wooden noggins. It could
not, she always said, have been more than ten minutes
from the last time she heard the children until she
came to the door.

Twilight was deepening.

The red light had faded from the bog, and the
shadow of Lisnavoura lay across the road. No child was
before the cabin.

"Con!" she cried. "Rose! Billy! Come in this minute!"

There was no answer.

She ran to the turf-stack, then round the goat-shed, then
across the road. The bramble branches were still trembling, but
she could see no one upon the heath. Fear, sudden
and hereditary, rose in her throat. She remembered her mother's
tales of voices that answered from the ground and of
children found sleeping inside rings of scorched grass.

"Con! Rose! Billy!"

Far down the road came the patter of feet. Two
figures emerged from the dusk, running wildly and clinging together.
They were Con and Rose. Rose's apron was torn, Con
had lost one shoe, and both were so white and
terrified that Nell's first question died unspoken.

"Where's Billy?"

Rose screamed.

Con caught Nell's arm. "Shut the doore! Shut it, for
the love of God!"

"Where is he? Where's Billy?"

"They took him," said Con, gasping. "The grand people—they took
him away to the hill."

At that instant Mary Ryan appeared upon the road, bent
beneath her load of turf. Hearing the cries, she let
the creel fall and came running. She looked once at
the children, once toward the darkening hill, and knew that
the fear of all her poor precautions had at last
entered her house.

What the children related, first by fragments and afterward more
connectedly, I had better set down in the order in
which the thing occurred.

They had strayed farther than they intended, following the blackberry
hedge until they came to a green lane branching from
the public road. At the entrance of this lane stood
an ash-tree under which they had often played; but that
evening Billy stopped and listened.

"D'ye hear the bells?" he asked.

Con heard nothing. Rose fancied there was music, very far
off and high in the air. Presently all three perceived
a sound like the ringing of many silver thimbles, accompanied
by a low rushing, though the leaves about them were
still.

Then from behind the turn of the lane came two
horsemen.

They were no taller than boys of seven, yet their
faces were old, sharp, and brown, with pointed chins, glittering
eyes, and teeth which showed when they laughed. Their coats,
of scarlet cloth, were sewn with gold; each wore enormous
riding-boots, a three-cornered hat, and a short sword; and each
sat upon a great white horse whose shoulder rose above
the children's heads. The animals were of an unnatural beauty,
with red nostrils, milk-coloured eyes, and manes streaming like smoke,
although they moved at the solemn pace of a funeral
procession.

One rider leaned from his saddle and snapped his fingers
close to Rose's face.

"Out o' the road, ditch-rat," he said, grinning.

The other drew his sword half out, causing Con to
retreat. The blade shone green, as moonlight shines in deep
water.

Behind them came six more white horses, harnessed in silver
and drawing a carriage such as had not been seen
in that country since the old lords left their houses
to ruin. It was high, narrow, and old-fashioned, painted a
lustrous blue and enriched upon every panel with golden birds,
stars, crowns, and flowers. Small lamps burned at its four
corners, though evening was not yet wholly gone; long silken
tassels swung from the roof; the wheels, scarlet at the
spokes and gilt at the rims, turned without sound over
stone or rut; and upon the hammercloth behind sat two
dwarf servants in green livery, their little withered faces maliciously
attentive.

There were two ladies within.

The elder, who occupied the farther corner, was wrapped in
black. A veil descended from a pointed cap and concealed
her features to the mouth; but the visible mouth was
thin and grey, and her long hands, folded upon her
lap, were covered with dark rings. She neither moved nor
spoke. The nearer lady leaned from the open window.

The children could afterward describe every jewel she wore, yet
none could agree upon the colour of her dress. To
Con it seemed crimson, to Rose green as young leaves,
and Nell declared that Billy, at one of his later
appearances, had whispered—or seemed about to whisper—that it was white.
Her hair was bright brown, braided with pearls; her arms
were bare to the elbow and covered with bracelets; and
her face had such sweetness that even Rose, while recounting
the horror, began to cry because she could not forget
its beauty.

"What pretty little craythurs!" said the lady, in a voice
clear and delicate as a bell heard across frost. "And
whose children may ye be?"

Con remembered his mother's warnings and did not answer.

The dwarf upon the leading horse made a hideous grimace.
"No manners," he said. "Bog-bred and bare-legged."

The lady reproved him with a smile and turned again
to the children.

"Will none of ye speak to me?"

"We're Mary Ryan's," said Rose.

"And which is the youngest?"

Billy stepped forward before Con could restrain him.

"I am, ma'am."

The lady's smile brightened. "And what is your name, golden
head?"

"Billy Ryan."

"Come nearer, Billy Ryan. I have something for you."

She held out a small red apple. Its skin was
so polished that it caught the last light and seemed
luminous from within. Billy approached. Con seized the back of
his coat, but one of the mounted dwarfs struck Con's
fingers with his riding-switch. The pain was so sharp that
he released his hold.

The lady touched Billy under the chin.

"A handsome child," she said.

At this the veiled woman in the carriage turned her
head. Though her eyes could not be seen, the children
felt her gaze pass over Billy slowly, as a cold
shadow passes across grass.

"Too pale," she murmured.

"He will redden with us," replied the beautiful lady.

Then she took Billy by both hands. He rose from
the ground as lightly as a feather lifted by an
upward draught, passed through the carriage window without striking the
frame, and settled upon her lap. He did not cry
out. He looked surprised, but happy, and suffered her to
place the apple between his hands.

Con ran at the carriage. The dwarf servants hissed and
showed their narrow teeth. One kicked at him with a
buckled shoe, while another uncoiled a whip whose lash moved
of itself upon the road like a black worm.

"Give him back!" Con shouted. "That's our Billy!"

The lady laughed—not loudly, nor, as Rose insisted, unkindly.

"Why, certainly. He shall go home. Follow us a little
way, and I will set him down where the road
is smooth."

The carriage moved on.

Con and Rose followed, calling to Billy. The white horses
did not appear to quicken their pace, yet the children
were obliged to run. Billy looked from the window, smiling,
with the apple pressed against his breast. Whenever Con drew
near the rear wheel, one of the dwarfs threatened him;
and whenever Rose began to wail, the beautiful lady leaned
out and spoke soothingly.

"Do not cry, little sister. See what I have for
you."

She scattered three apples upon the road. They rolled ahead
of the children, bright and red in the grey twilight.
Rose, thinking perhaps that the carriage would stop while she
gathered them, stooped and caught one. The moment she touched
it, another appeared farther on.

Again and again the lady threw the fruit, sometimes red,
sometimes yellow, sometimes of a deep purple colour such as
no natural apple bears. The children pursued them, and by
this innocent machination were drawn onward without considering the distance.
They passed the rowan-tree, the abandoned limekiln, and the stream
where the road forks. Before them Lisnavoura grew black against
a strip of dying gold.

At the crossroads Con at last overtook the carriage and
caught the brass handle behind. It was cold enough to
burn his palm.

"Billy!" he cried.

Billy turned. For the first time his smile faded. He
put out his hands and seemed about to spring down.

The veiled woman bent forward and enclosed him in her
long arms.

At once a whirlwind arose in the centre of the
road. Dust, dead leaves, straws, and little stones spun upward
in a roaring column. The horses screamed; the lamps of
the carriage shone blue through the murk; and the dwarfs'
laughter came from every side—above, below, behind the children, and
apparently from within the ground itself.

Con was flung into the ditch. Rose fell upon her
knees and covered her face.

The wind ceased.

The road was empty. The last daylight had gone, and
from the thorn-ring upon Lisnavoura there came, or they imagined
there came, the faint chiming of bells.

Mary did not wait for the conclusion. With Nell carrying
the holy-water bottle and Con holding a lighted wisp of
straw, she ran to the crossroads. Neighbours joined them: old
men with lanterns, women with shawls thrown over their heads,
boys carrying pitchforks and spades. They searched every ditch and
drain. They called Billy's name along the road, through the
heather, and beneath the thorn-trees of the rath. Mary would
have entered the ring itself, but the others restrained her,
not from cowardice, as some afterward said, but because a
person who invades such a place in anger may be
lost along with the one sought.

"Billy, astora!" she cried until her voice broke. "Billy, mavourneen,
answer your poor mammy!"

No answer came.

At midnight Father Maher arrived from Kilfinnan. He brought a
stole, a crucifix, and holy water, and went farther upon
the hill than any of the others dared to go.
What prayers he recited there were not repeated; but near
dawn he returned, pale and exhausted, and said only that
the child was not to be found by ordinary searching.

For nine days Mary kept the door unbarred and food
upon the table. For nine nights a candle burned in
the western window. Fairy doctors were consulted from three counties.
One prescribed foxglove gathered with the left hand; another directed
that Billy's clothes should be burned at the crossroads; a
third listened at the hearth with his ear to the
floor and declared that the child was living beneath running
water. Their ceremonies, though expensive to a woman who could
ill afford them, produced nothing.

Winter entered the valley. Rain loosened the thatch, snow covered
the rath, and the bog became impassable. Still Mary looked
up whenever the latch stirred.

Toward the end of January, Rose awoke after midnight and
saw Billy standing outside the window.

The moon was bright, and she knew him at once.
He wore the same little grey coat in which he
had disappeared; his golden hair was neatly curled; his cheeks
were rosy; and he smiled as though he had returned
from a pleasant visit. He raised one finger and beckoned.

Rose sprang from the bed, calling his name. Before her
feet touched the floor, he was gone.

Tracks were found beneath the window next morning—small footprints upon
the frozen earth. They led toward Lisnavoura and ended where
the road began.

Thereafter he appeared at uncertain intervals. Nell saw him at
the cabin door in March, holding a bunch of primroses.
Con saw him beside the stream on a summer evening.
In each instance Billy smiled and beckoned, but did not
speak; and if the witness advanced, called aloud, or made
the sign of the cross, he vanished—not gradually, like a
figure retiring into mist, but instantaneously, as the reflection of
a face leaves dark water when the light is removed.

Mary never saw him.

This was the sharpest point of her affliction. Strangers might
have supposed that the children imagined these visitations out of
grief; but no argument could console the mother, who believed
that Billy was permitted to approach the house, though some
fairy prohibition kept him from her sight.

"Why wouldn't he show himself to me?" she would say.
"Didn't I carry him? Didn't I rear him? Ah, God
help me, maybe he comes when I'm sleeping, and stands
over me, the poor innocent, and I not knowing!"

As the months passed, the appearances became rarer. The grey
coat looked worn. The primroses were replaced by withered leaves.
Once Con saw marks like bruises around Billy's wrists; and
once Rose heard, after he vanished, a sound beneath the
road resembling a child sobbing at a great distance.

Nearly a year and a half had elapsed when the
last occurrence took place.

It was early spring, before dawn, and the weather bitterly
cold. The family slept in the inner room, the fire
in the kitchen having sunk to a red spark among
the ashes. Nell, who had become a light sleeper, awoke
to a faint scraping. At first she thought the goat
had entered; then she saw the red gleam of the
embers brighten, as if someone knelt before them.

She rose and looked through the open doorway.

A child sat upon the hearth.

He was barefoot and dressed in rags. His hair, once
golden, hung in damp, colourless locks; his face was narrow
and white; and his hands, stretched above the dying fire,
were so thin that the light shone between the bones
of the fingers. Upon the settle beside him lay a
crust of bread which he had taken from the table,
and he ate it ravenously, though without making the least
sound.

Nell knew him.

"Billy," she whispered.

He looked round.

There was no smile now. His eyes were large with
hunger and fear. For one suspended moment brother and sister
gazed at one another across the poor room—the dresser, the
iron pot, the bottle of holy water, the rowan cross,
all those familiar and insufficient safeguards.

Then Nell cried, "Mammy! Mammy, he's here!"

Billy leaped up. She reached him before he gained the
door and caught a fold of his ragged coat. The
cloth came away in her hand, cold and wet as
something taken from a grave. He passed over the threshold.

Mary ran after him barefoot, followed by all the children.
The sky was beginning to whiten above the eastern hills.
They saw Billy upon the road, moving with extraordinary swiftness,
though his feet appeared scarcely to touch the ground.

"Stop, astora!" Mary cried. "Only stop till I bless you!"

He paused at the rowan-tree.

For the first and last time since his abduction, mother
and child looked upon one another. Billy lifted both arms
toward her. Whether he spoke, none could say. Mary believed
she heard the word "Mammy," but the others heard only
the wind.

A grey light flowed over Lisnavoura. Billy became indistinct in
it, wavered like a figure seen through rain, and disappeared.

They found no footprints, though the roadside mud was soft.
The fragment of cloth remained in Nell's hand until sunrise,
when it crumbled into a reddish dust and was blown
about the floor.

Billy Ryan was never seen again.

Father Maher afterward performed prayers at the cabin and upon
the road; another priest, younger and less tolerant of the
old beliefs, urged Mary to regard the visions as delusions
born of sorrow. She submitted to his instruction, as a
devout woman should, but in her heart nothing was altered.
She continued for many years to place a portion of
bread beside the fire on the anniversary of Billy's return,
and she never allowed the door to be wholly fastened
before dawn in spring.

The children grew. Nell married a farmer near Mitchelstown; Con
went to sea; Rose entered service in Cork. Mary remained
in the cabin until age bent her almost double and
whitened her hair. Those who visited her found her gentle,
rational, and little inclined to recount the marvel; but if
a child with fair curls passed the door at twilight,
she would rise suddenly and follow it with her eyes
until it was lost from view.

She died without certainty and was buried beside her husband.
There was no smaller grave between them.

The cabin has since fallen. Nettles grow within its walls,
and the rowan-tree was split by lightning many winters ago.
Yet the road remains, the crossroads remain, and above them
Lisnavoura keeps its ancient circle of thorns and stones. Travellers
who know nothing of Mary Ryan may admire, on a
clear autumn evening, the purple heath, the westward mountains, and
the last red brightness resting upon the bog; but the
older people hurry their steps when the hill's shadow reaches
across the road.

For they remember that beauty came from Lisnavoura in a
gilded carriage, with lamps and music and apples shining in
the dusk; and that a poor woman's child went away
smiling among its splendours, to return only once, barefoot, starving,
and afraid.

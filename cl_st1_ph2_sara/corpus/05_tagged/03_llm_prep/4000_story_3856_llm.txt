"I have collected," said the Ethnologist, closing his notebook with
a slap, "seventeen versions of the same preposterous story."

"Only seventeen?" said Lieutenant Harcourt. "They are losing their industry."

"In one version you have black wings. In another they
are the wings of an eagle. You are impervious to
bullets, naturally; you can cross a river in three strides;
and you leap over elephants."

"There were no elephants within two hundred miles."

"That," said the Ethnologist severely, "is the sole point in
the legend which troubles you."

Harcourt stretched his boots towards the little wood fire. Outside
the mess tent the rain ran steadily from the eaves,
and somewhere in the dark a mule shook its harness.

"I couldn't leap over an elephant," he said. "Not without
annoying it."

"You have ruined the folklore of an entire valley. I
came here to obtain their creation myths, their tribal heroes,
their ancient accounts of the mountain spirits. Instead, every greybeard
and goat-boy tells me about Harcourt Sahib, who fell out
of the moon and slew men by alighting on them."

"One man."

"I beg your pardon?"

"I only slew one by alighting on him."

The Ethnologist stared across the lamp.

"Then there is a foundation for this nonsense?"

"Oh, certainly. Most sound nonsense has one. Whisky?"

"No. Explain yourself."

"That is a mistake," said Harcourt, pouring his own. "However,
if you insist—"

He settled deeper into the camp chair.

"It was during the Darash expedition. You remember the business?"

"I remember that the official report described the district as
pacified."

"Quite. Whenever a district is officially pacified, it is prudent
to take ammunition.

"We had been sent up the eastern branch of the
valley to look at a village called Palti. The headman
had sent in protestations of devotion, three sheep, and a
cousin as hostage. The sheep were excellent. The cousin escaped.
We ought to have drawn a conclusion from that, but
Colonel Braddock said the people were friendly, and sent me
with nine men to receive their submission.

"I had six Sepoys, two Sappers, and Hooker, who was
a lance-corporal then and considered all mountains a personal insult.
We also had a mule carrying the tent, rations, and
reserve cartridges. Not much of an army, but enough for
receiving affection.

"Palti lay in a cup among the hills, with stone
houses stacked one above another and apricot trees below. Nothing
stirred when we came in. No children, no dogs, no
old women looking through cracks. I disliked that. A village
without dogs is like a mess dinner without complaints—unnatural.

"Hooker said, 'They're waitin' to cheer us, sir.'

"'Then they are remarkably considerate of our nerves,' I said.

"We reached the square. The headman's house stood at the
upper end, and I had just dismounted when somebody fired
from a roof. The shot went through my sleeve and
hit the mule's pack-frame. Then every wall in Palti began
coughing smoke.

"Our friendly village had arranged itself very neatly round us.

"We got behind a stone irrigation bank. The men behaved
well. They fired at loopholes, while the Sappers knocked a
gap in the wall behind us. There was no point
in staying to discuss the headman's sincerity, so we went
through the gap and down among the orchards.

"That was the beginning of the retreat.

"At first it was almost respectable. We kept together, fired
by sections, and persuaded the more adventurous fellows to remain
at a distance. Their matchlocks made a tremendous noise and
generally missed. Unfortunately they had some Martini rifles as well,
and those did not miss so reliably.

"We had about six miles to go before joining the
main track. The direct path followed the stream, but the
villagers were running along both ridges to get ahead of
us. You could see their white clothes flickering among the
rocks. They knew every goat-track, of course. We knew the
map, which was less useful.

"After an hour, Narayan Singh was hit in the thigh.
He said it was nothing, as men do when they
have been shot. We put him on the mule and
went on.

"The valley narrowed presently to a gorge—a wicked little place,
with the river squeezed between cliffs and the road no
wider than a billiard table. Hooker saw men crossing the
ridge in front.

"'They'll stop the bottle, sir,' he said.

"He meant they would hold the gorge while the people
behind drove us into it. A fair summary.

"On our right was a hillside steep enough to make
a fly thoughtful. Across it ran what looked like a
path, climbing diagonally through scrub. I could not see where
it went, but it went somewhere, which was more than
could be said for the gorge. So up we started.

"The mule objected. Hooker addressed it in language which would
have moved a less principled animal, and the Sappers hauled
at the bridle while the rest fired downhill.

"The supposed path was not a path at all. It
was a seam in the rock, perhaps used by sheep
with religious convictions. It grew steeper, turned round a shoulder,
and narrowed to a shelf. Still, by then the tribesmen
were below us, and bullets were knocking chips from the
stones, so we continued.

"A bullet on a slope has a nasty, leisurely sound.
It strikes above you, whines away, strikes again, and comes
twanging down as though searching the whole hillside for your
particular spine. Stones followed it. The enemy had discovered that
rocks were cheaper than cartridges.

"We climbed for perhaps half a mile. Then the shelf
widened abruptly, and we found ourselves on a ledge about
thirty yards long and ten deep. Above us was a
cliff; below, another cliff; at the far end, the mountain
had broken off sheer.

"And there we were."

"Trapped?" said the Ethnologist.

"Completely. Xenophon had ten thousand men and reached the sea.
I had nine and reached a blank wall."

"Could you not go back?"

"The shelf behind us narrowed to a corner commanded from
below. The first man to show himself there would have
been potted. The place was an admirable fortress, if one
happened to be provisioned. We were not.

"The mule had the food, but during the scramble its
load had shifted, and one pannier containing most of the
rations had gone down the slope. Worse, both water-skins had
been pierced. There was perhaps a pint left between them.

"We distributed that. I gave Narayan Singh a little more
because of his wound. No one objected. The men lay
behind the rocks and watched the corner. The enemy tried
it twice, lost three men, and decided to wait.

"That was sensible of them.

"The river ran below us. We could see it in
places through the cleft—a bright strip no wider than a
knife-blade. It made a small chuckling sound against the stones.
Giggle, giggle, all day. I have disliked cheerful rivers ever
since.

"The sun got round to us at noon. The rock
held the heat like an oven brick. By evening our
tongues had begun to swell.

"Hooker crawled over to me.

"'They'll rush us after dark, sir.'

"'I hope so.'

"He looked shocked.

"'Then we can shoot them,' I explained. 'At present they
are doing nothing useful.'

"But they did not rush us. They sang instead. One
fellow had a drum. It was not good music, but
it lasted.

"In the night, Karim, one of the Sepoys, thought he
heard movement near the corner. He went forward with two
others. There was a shot and a shout. The two
came back. Karim did not.

"Whether he slipped, was hit, or went down among them
with his bayonet, I never knew. We could not reach
the place without losing another man.

"The next morning we had no water.

"You may talk learnedly about hunger, but thirst is a
simpler scholar. It wipes everything else off the slate. At
first one calculates distances and chances. Then one thinks of
wells, taps, rain-barrels, green coconuts, bottles sweating in ice. By
midday even those pictures vanish, and there is only the
throat.

"One of the Sappers put a pebble in his mouth.
Soon they all did it. Hooker sucked a rifle cartridge
until the brass shone.

"Narayan Singh grew feverish. His bandage was black with blood,
and flies settled on it. He kept asking leave to
go down to the river. I told him he would
be shot before he reached it.

"'Then I shall not be thirsty, sahib,' he said.

"An hour later he tried to stand. The mule shifted
beside him, Narayan caught at the saddle, and both went
against the outer edge. Hooker seized the bridle. The girth
snapped. Narayan fell without a cry.

"He struck once on the cliff and disappeared.

"The mule remained, trembling and staring down. We pulled it
back.

"By then it was obvious relief might not arrive in
time. Braddock knew our route, but not where we had
gone off it. He would send men when we failed
to return; they would search Palti, find the gorge, fight
their way through, and perhaps discover our ledge after we
were all neatly dried upon it.

"We shot the mule that afternoon.

"There was a little blood, and we caught what we
could in a mess tin. I will not describe the
taste. The meat was warm and tough, and most of
us could not swallow it. We cut strips nevertheless.

"Then, while Hooker and the Sappers were skinning the carcass,
I noticed the tent roll.

"It was stout drill canvas, about twelve feet square when
spread. There were guy-ropes, ridge ropes, and mule lashings. I
had once watched a balloon section demonstrate parachutes at Rawalpindi.
Their apparatus had been silk, properly corded, tested, and accompanied
by several officers who understood it. Mine had mildew at
one corner and a bullet-hole in the middle.

"Still, the principle seemed plain. A large surface resists the
air. A man beneath it descends more slowly than a
man without it. How much more slowly was the doubtful
point."

The Ethnologist leaned forward. "You made a parachute?"

"I made an object with aspirations in that direction.

"We spread the tent behind the rocks. I cut it
roughly circular, reinforced the edge with strips from the mule
harness, and fixed ropes at intervals. The men watched without
comment. That was the worst of it. Had they laughed,
I might have abandoned the scheme as ridiculous.

"Hooker held up the result.

"'Looks like my aunt's washing, sir.'

"'Did your aunt's washing ever save a platoon?'

"'No, sir. Took a constable's helmet off once.'

"We tied the ropes to a loop round my waist
and shoulders. I intended to jump after dark, reach the
river if I survived, and get to camp. The current
ran towards our lines. Even if I broke a leg,
I might float.

"'Beggin' your pardon, sir,' said Hooker, 'why you?'

"'Because it was my excellent idea.'

"'That ain't a reason.'

"It was reason enough. Besides, if I ordered one of
the men to try it and he went down like
a dropped spoon, discipline would suffer.

"At dusk we tested the thing by throwing a rock
tied beneath it. The canvas opened, turned sideways, collapsed, reopened,
and deposited the rock on a lower shelf. The descent
was not encouraging.

"We made the ropes more equal.

"Night came clear. There was a half-moon, enough to show
the river and far too much to show the drop.
The enemy's fires burned below the corner. From somewhere near
the water came voices.

"I stood at the back of the ledge with the
folded canvas in my arms. The plan was to run,
throw it outward, and leap after it.

"I ran.

"At the edge I stopped.

"There is no ornamental way of saying it. I funked.
My knees became independent. The cliff seemed to rise in
front of me, though cliffs ordinarily remain fixed. I sat
down rather suddenly.

"No one spoke. That made it intolerable.

"Hooker began examining his rifle with immense delicacy. The Sepoys
looked at the stars. They were determined not to witness
an officer's embarrassment.

"I said, 'The ropes were crossed.'

"They were not.

"I arranged them for several minutes, drank an imaginary drink,
and tried again.

"This time pride carried me farther than courage. I remember
the ledge coming at me, the black emptiness beyond, and
Hooker shouting, 'Keep 'er open, sir!'

"Then there was nothing under my boots.

"For the first second I fell inside the canvas. It
wrapped itself round my head and I thought, Well, that
settles the technical question. Then the air caught it with
a crack like a sail taking wind. The ropes snatched
my arms upward, nearly cutting them off, and I swung
beneath the thing.

"I was still falling, but not in the decisive fashion.

"The cliff went sliding past. I could see cracks and
tufts of grass with extraordinary clearness. Once I swung inward
and struck it with my knee. Then the air pushed
me away, turning me slowly.

"Above, the tent looked black against the moon. Wings, I
suppose, if one had imagination and had not seen a
tent before.

"The river grew broader. Its sound rose from a chuckle
to a roar. I began to believe the machine would
work, which was premature, because I had made no arrangement
for steering or landing.

"There was a gravel spit beside the water. On it
burned a small fire. Three men were crouched there over
something.

"As I descended, I saw what it was.

"They had found Narayan Singh's body and were cutting off
his head.

"One of them looked up. I shall remember his face:
mouth open, knife in hand, firelight on one cheek. He
shouted. The others stared. From their position a white-faced man
with a black shape spread above him had detached himself
from the sky and was coming directly at them.

"They might reasonably have been surprised.

"Two ran. The third chose the wrong direction.

"I landed upon him.

"The parachute collapsed over us, and for a moment I
was inside canvas, stones, arms, and someone's knife. When I
crawled free, the man was dead. I had broken his
neck or his back. My own left ankle was sprained
and my knee bleeding, but otherwise I was annoyingly alive.

"The other two had stopped thirty yards away. They looked
at me. I stood up with the canvas hanging from
my shoulders.

"I wish I could claim I said something majestic. In
fact I yelled at them to clear off.

"They did. One dropped his matchlock.

"I went to Narayan. There was nothing to be done.
So I took the matchlock, limped to the river, put
my face into it, and drank.

"That nearly finished me. A thirsty man ought to sip.
I drank like a horse, was sick, drank again, and
lay in the shallows listening to the water go gluck
round my ears.

"After that, matters were comparatively dull. I followed the river
until dawn, hid when I heard voices, and reached one
of our picquets shortly before noon. The sentry first challenged
me and then asked why I was wearing a tent.

"I told Braddock where the ledge was. He sent two
companies and a mountain gun. They cleared the gorge that
afternoon and brought the men down by ropes from above.

"Hooker and four others were alive."

The Ethnologist's expression changed. "Four?"

"Two more Sepoys had jumped during the night. One perhaps
thought I had reached the river safely; the other was
delirious. They found both bodies below."

For a moment the rain outside seemed louder.

"And the people at the river," said the Ethnologist at
last, "carried the account back to the valley."

"I imagine so. A man drops from the moon on
black wings, kills your companion by touching him, rises from
the dead man wearing the wings, and commands you to
depart. One can forgive a little embroidery."

"The invulnerability?"

"They had been firing at me all day."

"The elephants?"

"Pure art."

"And you never attempted to correct them?"

"My dear fellow, would you believe the parachute?"

The Ethnologist considered this, then opened his notebook.

Harcourt reached for the decanter.

"What are you writing?"

"The authentic version."

"Then put in that the descent was perfectly controlled."

"It was not."

"No," said Harcourt, splashing soda into his glass. "But neither,
apparently, is folklore."

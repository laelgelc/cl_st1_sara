### The House with the Green Shutters

"There are more matters in the world than your little
philosophy accounts for," said Rita, laughing, while she fastened one
glove and watched Camillo in the mirror.

She had misquoted the prince of Denmark, but with such
pretty conviction that Shakespeare, had he been present, might have
forgiven her. Camillo did not. He disliked superstition in other
people, where it seemed a defect of intelligence; in himself,
as we shall see, it took another name.

"What philosophy?" he asked. "I possess none."

"That is precisely why yours is little."

"And what new matter has entered the world?"

"A woman who reads the future."

Camillo, who had been walking from the window to the
table, stopped.

"A fortune-teller?"

"Yes."

"You went to one?"

"I did."

"Rita!"

She turned from the mirror. At thirty, she still retained
the animated freshness which, in some women, is less a
property of the skin than of the spirit. Her eyes
were dark and ardent; her mouth, a little too wide
for classic beauty, had the advantage of appearing always on
the point of asking a piquant question. A few strands
of hair had escaped from beneath her hat. She looked
young, guilty, amused, and tender.

"You may say 'Rita' as many times as you please,"
she replied. "I went because I was anxious. You had
been so cold, so absent. You came less often, answered
my letters in three words, and when you saw me
you looked at the doors. What was I to think?"

"That prudence had at last visited me."

"Prudence! She might have sent in her card."

He smiled in spite of himself. She stepped closer and
laid one hand upon his arm.

"I asked whether you still loved me," she said. "The
woman turned over the cards, looked at them, looked at
me, and said yes. She said you loved me deeply,
though you were frightened. She also said there was no
danger."

"No danger?"

"None. Our secret is safe."

Camillo laughed. This time he laughed more loudly than the
pleasantry required. A man sometimes makes a noise in order
not to hear an inner voice.

"And you paid money for that?"

"Five milreis."

"Five milreis! I could have told you the same for
nothing."

"No, you could not. You have told me the contrary
in twenty different ways."

"I have told you to be careful."

"She told me not to fear."

"Then she is plainly the wiser adviser. Where does this
sibyl live?"

"In Rua da Misericórdia, above an old furniture shop. The
house has green shutters. Do not laugh, Camillo. I went
veiled. No one saw me."

"My dear Rita, if no one saw you, all Rio
will know by tomorrow."

She struck his sleeve with her fan, but he caught
her hand and kissed it. This concluded the philosophical debate.
Affection commonly has the last word where logic has failed;
and if the reader objects that a kiss proves nothing,
I answer that lovers do not require proof, only conclusions.

Let us proceed to explain how the wife of Villela
came to be kissing the hand of Villela's childhood friend
in a rented room near the Largo da Carioca.

Camillo and Villela had been boys together. They studied beneath
the same drowsy masters, stole fruit from the same gardens,
fought the same larger pupils, and shared those ambitions which
at fifteen cost nothing and promise everything. Villela was then
the graver of the two. He possessed an instinct for
order, a firm chin, and the patience to finish whatever
he began. Camillo was quick, graceful, easily tired, and capable
of confusing good impulses with good conduct.

Their roads separated when Villela went to the provinces to
study law. Camillo remained in Rio, first with his mother,
then in a minor government office obtained through the exertions
of an uncle. The office did not burden him. He
copied memoranda, bowed to superiors, discussed the ministry with subordinates,
and departed before sunset whenever illness, weather, family duty, or
simple inclination supplied a pretext.

His mother, a gentle and credulous widow, believed in saints,
dreams, omens, lucky days, unlucky numbers, and the prophetic dispositions
of coffee grounds. Camillo, at twenty, mocked all these things.
But I express myself ill. He did not reject them
through reason; he merely disliked being governed by them. He
had no system of unbelief, no philosophy armed with argument,
no intellectual rampart against mystery. His skepticism was a coat
worn in fair weather.

When his mother died, he grieved sincerely and lived badly.
She had managed his money, his linen, his conscience, and
much of his daily courage. Deprived of her, he drifted
into a melancholy indolence that seemed poetic from a distance
and inconvenient close at hand.

Villela returned at that time, married and established in Rio.
His legal practice was prospering. He had grown broad in
the shoulders and somewhat severe in expression, though his old
affection for Camillo remained intact. He sought him out, reproached
his solitude, made him dine at the house, found a
better physician for a persistent fever, and even disentangled one
of his debts. Friendship, when practical, may perform offices that
love only promises.

Rita was Villela's wife.

She came from a respectable family of modest fortune and
had been married three years. She was nine years younger
than her husband, fond of music, visitors, bright fabrics, and
all those small agitations which make a day feel inhabited.
Villela loved her with the concentrated gravity he brought to
law and friendship. He did not speak much of his
feelings; he arranged them. There were flowers in the drawing
room because she liked flowers, a carriage on Sundays because
she liked the Botafogo road, a piano because she had
once said life without music was a corridor without windows.

Rita welcomed Camillo first as her husband's friend, then as
a sorrowing son, finally as a man.

The stages were not announced. Propinquity is an architect that
builds without plans. Camillo dined with them twice a week,
then three times; he accompanied Rita to shops when Villela
was occupied; he turned the music for her at the
piano; she asked about his mother; he spoke of childhood;
she pitied him. Pity is a modest door through which
many grander passions enter without knocking.

There were also differences of temperament to assist them. Villela's
gravity, admirable in court, sometimes sat heavily at table. Rita's
questions fluttered against it and fell. Camillo answered them. He
praised her new bonnet, noticed when she changed a ribbon,
brought verses, repeated gossip, laughed before the end of her
stories. A woman does not always fall in love with
the man who understands her; sometimes it is enough that
he appears to listen.

Camillo resisted. We owe him this justice, though not too
much of it.

He remembered Villela's kindness. He saw the baseness of betrayal.
He avoided being alone with Rita, then regretted the occasions
he had escaped; he resolved not to write, and spent
half an hour choosing a phrase for a note; he
accused her of imprudence, which was his method of sharing
the blame. Honor struggled in him, but it had the
disadvantage of being abstract, while Rita had burning eyes, warm
hands, and a voice that altered when she spoke his
name.

One wet afternoon, in Villela's library, she found him examining
a print he had already examined for ten minutes.

"Why do you avoid me?" she asked.

"I do not."

"You came yesterday and scarcely spoke."

"Villela was here."

"He is generally here. It is his house."

Camillo attempted a smile.

"You are angry with me," she continued.

"With you? Never."

"Then with yourself."

That observation, which might have come from a moralist, came
instead from a woman standing very near him. Rita touched
his hand. Camillo withdrew it; she turned pale; he took
hers; and thus honor, having made a formal protest, left
the room.

The affair unfolded with the customary mixture of ecstasy and
administration. There were secret notes, accidental meetings, borrowed rooms, false
errands, sudden silences, and jealousies born from nothing. The smallest
obstacles inflamed them. Villela's trust, instead of restraining Camillo, gave
a dreadful sweetness to danger. Rita was bolder, because love
occupied her whole imagination; Camillo was more fearful, because his
imagination admitted consequences.

For nearly a year, however, no consequence appeared.

Then Camillo received an anonymous letter.

The handwriting slanted laboriously to the left, as if the
writer had attempted disguise and achieved only ugliness. The message
was brief: everyone knew why he frequented Villela's house; a
man who repaid friendship with dishonor should beware.

He read it once with astonishment, twice with fear, and
a third time in search of some phrase that might
prove it a jest. There was none. He burned it,
though he first compared the writing with three or four
hands he knew. Such is man: he destroys the evidence
and preserves the suspicion.

He told Rita. She grew pale but declared the writer
a coward and the danger imaginary.

"Someone wishes to frighten us."

"He has succeeded."

"Then do not reward him."

A week later came another letter, more explicit than the
first. After that, Camillo reduced his visits to Villela's house.
He invented work at the office, a sick acquaintance in
Andaraí, evening headaches, and an interest in public lectures. Villela
complained good-naturedly.

"You are becoming important," he said. "Soon I shall require
an appointment to see you."

Camillo replied that the ministry was in confusion. Governments, like
illnesses, are useful to persons requiring excuses.

Rita suffered from his absence. She wrote too often, then
reproached him for not writing; she arranged encounters that terrified
him; she imagined Villela examining her face, questioning the servants,
opening drawers. Camillo urged calm in letters composed with trembling
fingers.

It was during this interval that she visited the fortune-teller
in the house with green shutters.

Her report amused him, reassured him a little, and irritated
him much. He called the woman a fraud, the cards
painted pasteboard, and prophecy a tax upon fear. Rita listened,
smiling with the serenity of one who has already received
the desired answer.

"Laugh," she said. "You will remember her yet."

He denied it and kissed her. Then they parted, she
to return home before Villela, he to spend an hour
walking under the arcades and looking behind him.

Time passed. Nothing happened.

The anonymous letters ceased. Villela remained cordial, Rita affectionate, the
city indifferent. Camillo's fear lost its sharpness, though not its
habit. He continued to avoid the house and saw Rita
elsewhere. Danger, if prolonged without fulfillment, becomes almost a form
of security. One begins by saying, "It may happen today,"
and ends by saying, "Since it has not happened, it
will not."

On a Friday morning in November, Camillo reached his office
later than usual and found a note awaiting him.

The envelope bore Villela's hand.

He broke the seal. The message contained one line:

"Come at once to my house. I must speak to
you without delay."

Camillo read it standing. The room, which a moment before
had been full of scratching pens, moving chairs, and the
nasal complaint of a clerk near the window, appeared suddenly
silent. In truth, the scratching and complaining continued; it was
Camillo who had withdrawn from them.

Come at once. Without delay.

He read the line again. There was no "dear friend,"
no explanation, no signature beyond the initial V. Yet men
in haste omit courtesies. Villela might be ill. Rita might
be ill. A legal difficulty, perhaps; a private request; some
matter concerning Camillo's late mother; a debt; a duel; a
recommendation to the ministry.

Or the letters.

He folded the note. Unfolded it. Examined the paper as
though the fibers might contain a second message.

"Bad news?" asked the clerk at the neighboring desk.

"No."

"You look ill."

"A headache."

The clerk accepted this, having headaches of his own. Camillo
took his hat and left.

Outside, the sun was brilliant. The city carried on with
that peculiar insolence which ordinary life displays beside a private
catastrophe. Shopkeepers stood at their doors; a fruit seller cried
oranges; two young officers laughed beneath a blue awning; carts
groaned over the stones. Camillo found these things sinister. The
laughter was directed at him, the cries warned him, the
sky itself had the hard and polished appearance of a
blade.

He hailed a tilbury and gave Villela's address.

As the horse moved off, reason returned—or what he called
reason. If Villela knew, would he summon him so plainly?
Would he write? Would he not seek him at the
office, confront Rita first, consult a relative? Perhaps Rita had
confessed. No, impossible. Unless she had been surprised. A letter
found, a servant questioned, a veil recognized. He saw a
dozen scenes, each more vivid than the last.

Villela standing beside Rita's chair.

"Whose handwriting is this?"

"I do not know."

"Do not lie."

Then tears, denial, the locked door, the note dispatched.

Camillo pressed his fingers to his forehead.

The tilbury turned into Rua do Ouvidor and was forced
to slow among carriages. Why had Villela written "at once"?
Because the matter admitted no delay. But illness also admits
none. Rita might have fainted. She had lately complained of
palpitations. Perhaps she was dying and wished to see him—no,
that thought was madness. Villela would not summon him to
a sentimental farewell.

Unless Villela knew.

The horse stopped.

Ahead, a cart loaded with building stones had lost a
wheel. Traffic compressed behind it: carriages, riders, porters, curses. The
driver rose and shouted, but shouting is a weak instrument
against a broken axle.

Camillo leaned out. To the left stood an old house
above a shop crowded with worm-eaten chairs, cracked mirrors, and
tables whose legs protruded into the doorway. The upper shutters
were green.

He recognized it at once.

Rita's fortune-teller.

Coincidence is the secular name we give to fate when
we do not wish to bow. Camillo stared at the
shutters. They were closed, and the blind façade resembled the
countenance of one who knows a secret and will not
speak. He remembered Rita's laughter: You will remember her yet.

He sat back.

Absurd. The woman was a cheat. The cards were pasteboard.
Five milreis for a handful of generalities! He would remain
in the tilbury. The obstruction would clear. He would go
directly to Villela's house like a man.

The driver cursed again. Someone behind them answered with greater
talent. The line did not move.

Camillo looked once more at the green shutters.

Suppose he entered merely from curiosity? There was no harm
in hearing what she said. He need not believe. A
physician may examine a charlatan without accepting the remedy. Besides,
the visit would occupy the delay; he would lose no
time, since the street was blocked. This final argument pleased
him because it transformed weakness into economy.

He stepped down.

"Wait for me," he told the driver.

The doorway beside the furniture shop opened upon a narrow
staircase. It was dark, steep, and worn in the middle
by feet poorer or more hopeful than his. The air
smelled of dust, fried oil, and some sweet, stale perfume.
Halfway up, Camillo nearly turned back. Then a woman's voice
called from above:

"Come in."

He had not knocked.

This circumstance impressed him, though the woman had probably heard
the door and his steps. Natural explanations are plentiful after
fear has chosen the supernatural.

The room was small. A faded curtain divided it from
another. There were two chairs, a round table, a shelf
with a plaster saint, and a window so thick with
grime that daylight entered already old. The fortune-teller sat opposite
the door.

She was about forty, thin, dark, and wrapped in a
yellow shawl despite the heat. Her eyes were sharp and
lively; her nails, though long, were not clean. A red
stone hung at her throat. Poverty surrounded her, but did
not diminish her authority in Camillo's eyes. On the contrary,
the dirty room, the dimness, the shabby curtain—all seemed to
remove her from ordinary judgment. A prophetess in a well-swept
parlor might have looked merely respectable.

"Sit down," she said.

Camillo obeyed.

"You are troubled."

He started. Any cheerful man who climbed that staircase would
have been remarkable, but Camillo did not consider this.

"Very troubled," he admitted.

She took up a pack of cards and shuffled them
slowly, watching his face rather than her hands.

"It concerns another person," she said.

"Yes."

"A woman."

Camillo's heart struck hard.

"And a man," she added.

There are few human troubles to which these two guesses
cannot be applied; but truth, arriving where desire awaits it,
is received like a sovereign.

The woman laid out the cards. A king, a queen,
a black knave, seven of hearts. She frowned, moved one,
covered another, and murmured words Camillo could not hear.

"You are afraid," she said at last, "that the man
knows everything."

Camillo leaned forward.

"Does he?"

She did not answer at once. Silence is the fortune-teller's
capital; words are merely the interest. She studied the cards,
touched the red stone at her throat, and closed her
eyes.

"No."

Camillo exhaled.

"He suspects nothing?"

"Nothing. The lady is unhappy because of your doubt. You
have withdrawn from her. She thinks your love is dying."

"It is not."

"I know."

She looked again at the cards.

"There is no danger to either of you," she continued.
"The man trusts you. His mind is occupied with another
matter—money or business, perhaps. You will see. You may go
without fear."

"Then the message—"

"Is not what you imagine."

"What is it?"

The fortune-teller's eyes sharpened. Here the oracle encountered the limits
of information. She turned a card.

"A concern of his own. He needs you."

Camillo felt an immense tenderness for this woman. Her dirty
nails became sibylline fingers; the greasy curtain, a veil between
worlds. She had said what he longed to hear, but
this did not make the words less true to him;
rather, it seemed proof that truth and longing had at
last agreed.

"You are certain?"

She smiled.

"The cards do not lie."

Cards possess this advantage over men: when events contradict them,
they do not blush.

Camillo rose. He placed a ten-milreis note on the table,
twice Rita's payment and far more than he had intended.
The fortune-teller's face brightened with an expression so earthly that,
had he been calm, it might have restored his skepticism.
But he saw only benevolence.

"Go," she said. "Go in peace. The lady loves you.
The man knows nothing. There are more things between heaven
and earth than people suppose."

The quotation, changed again, completed his conversion.

Descending the staircase, Camillo felt ten years younger. The passage
no longer smelled foul; it was mysterious. The darkness no
longer threatened; it protected. He emerged into the street just
as the broken cart was dragged aside.

"You took your time," said the driver.

"Go on."

The tilbury advanced.

Now the city had altered. The sun was no longer
a blade but a blessing. The officers beneath the awning
laughed because youth was merry; the fruit seller's cry had
a musical cadence; the white clouds sailed in a sky
of astonishing purity. Fear had painted the world black, and
reassurance washed the brush. Neither world was the world; both
were Camillo.

He smiled at his recent terrors. Villela knew nothing. Of
course he knew nothing. Had he not always trusted him?
The message concerned business. Perhaps Villela wished to borrow money—though
Camillo had little. Perhaps some question of the estate, some
document, some witness. He would enter naturally, complain of the
traffic, embrace his friend if the occasion invited it, and
later send Rita a note: "Your prophetess has made a
fool of me and a believer besides."

He imagined her delight. She would clap her hands and
say, "I told you." He would forbid her to return
to the woman; they would laugh; he would kiss the
piquantly inquiring mouth. Their future opened before him, wide and
sunlit. Caution would still be necessary, certainly, but not cowardice.
They might continue for years. Villela might travel on business.
Camillo might receive a better appointment. Rita had once spoken
of a small house beyond the city, with a garden
descending toward the water.

At the end of a cross street he glimpsed the
bay, blue and immense. Sails shone upon it. The hills
rested in a transparent distance; bells sounded somewhere above the
roofs. Life seemed inexhaustible.

There are moments when happiness, not grief, most resembles madness.
A man delivered from an imagined death spends immortality in
advance.

The tilbury left the crowded center and moved through quieter
streets. Camillo noticed familiar houses, iron balconies, curtains stirring behind
open windows. He passed a church where his mother had
once taken him to fulfill a vow after a childhood
fever. He remembered her faith without mockery. Perhaps she had
understood more than he. Perhaps the universe contained invisible correspondences,
warnings, mercies; perhaps love itself was one of them. The
parasitic vegetation of childhood superstition, cut down but never uprooted,
pushed green shoots through his mind.

He repeated the fortune-teller's assurances.

The man knows nothing.

There is no danger.

Go in peace.

He gave each phrase the weight of revelation. Had she
not divined the woman, the man, the fear, the withdrawal?
It did not occur to him that he had supplied
these facts with his face, his pauses, and his eager
questions. Vanity persuades us that our secrets are impenetrable until
a stranger guesses them; then we call the stranger inspired.

The tilbury stopped before Villela's house.

The shutters were closed.

Camillo's heart contracted, but only for an instant. The heat
explained it. He paid the driver, adjusted his coat, and
glanced at his watch. His visit to the fortune-teller had
delayed him nearly half an hour. He regretted this now,
not from fear, but from politeness.

The front door stood ajar.

He entered and found the hallway empty. Usually a servant
appeared at once. Today there was no one. The air
inside was cool and smelled faintly of wax and flowers.

"Villela?" he called.

No answer.

He removed his hat and went toward the drawing room.
His step sounded unnaturally loud on the floor. At the
far end of the passage, a door opened.

Villela appeared.

He wore no coat. His face was colorless, his hair
disordered. In his right hand he held a revolver pointed
toward the floor.

Camillo stopped.

For one second neither man spoke. The fortune-teller's words still
moved in Camillo's mind, but they had lost their meaning,
like a prayer in an unknown language.

Villela said, "Come."

His voice was low.

Camillo followed him into the drawing room.

Rita lay beside the sofa. Her dress was crushed beneath
her; one arm was bent under her body, the other
extended across the carpet. Blood had dried at her temple.
Her burning eyes were open and saw nought.

Camillo uttered a broken cry and stepped back.

Villela seized him by the collar.

Camillo raised both hands.

Villela fired twice.

Camillo fell dead beside Rita.

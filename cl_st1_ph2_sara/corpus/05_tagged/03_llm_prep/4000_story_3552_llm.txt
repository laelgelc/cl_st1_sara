## The Christmas Dream

For three weeks the young Tsar had done nothing but
work.

He had risen before daylight, while the lamps still burned
in the palace corridors and the frost drew white branches
upon the windows; he had received ministers before breakfast, generals
after breakfast, governors at noon, foreign envoys in the evening,
and secret reports when all sensible men were already asleep.
Papers followed him from room to room. They lay beside
his plate, appeared beneath his napkin, waited in red and
green portfolios upon his writing table, and were carried after
him by silent officials whenever he attempted to walk.

There were reports concerning the collection of arrears from peasants
in six provinces; a proposal to increase the duty on
imported cloth; a petition from merchants requesting protection against foreign
competition; another petition from landowners requesting cheaper railway rates for
grain; a request from the Ministry of Finance to enlarge
the sale of spirits in districts where revenue had declined;
and a recommendation from the Ministry of the Interior that
certain newspapers be warned, three suspended, and one suppressed altogether.

There were sentences for confirmation. Two soldiers had struck superior
officers. One had been drunk, the other had refused to
fire upon a crowd. There were promotions to sanction, dismissals
to approve, decorations to distribute, and names of political offenders
to be transferred from one place of confinement to another.
A governor asked permission to declare martial law in two
districts. A bishop complained that peasants had gathered without a
priest to read the Gospel. The Holy Council requested authority
to remove children from parents who persisted in an unlawful
confession. The Customs Department demanded stronger measures against smuggling. The
police sought increased funds for surveillance. The army asked for
more recruits. The navy asked for new ships. Every ministry
declared that its request was urgent, indispensable, and directed solely
toward the welfare of the State.

The young Tsar read, listened, asked questions, and signed.

Sometimes he disliked what he signed. Sometimes a phrase—"exemplary punishment,"
"administrative necessity," "without public trial," "losses unavoidable"—made him pause. Then
the minister who stood before him, an elderly man with
silver hair, ribbons, and a look of weary devotion, explained
that the wording was technical; that His Majesty's tender heart
naturally recoiled from severity; that government, however, was not possible
without severity; and that a ruler must consider not one
individual, whose fate might be regrettable, but the peace and
prosperity of millions.

The young Tsar believed this. How could he not believe
men who had served his father, and in some cases
his grandfather, who knew the provinces, the laws, the finances,
the army and police, while he, though placed above them
all, knew scarcely anything except what they told him?

And yet he wished to do good. This wish was
sincere. He desired that the peasants should be prosperous, the
workers contented, the soldiers brave, the judges honest, the officials
incorruptible, the priests devout, and all the peoples beneath his
sceptre grateful for the happiness of belonging to so great
an empire. When he signed an order increasing taxation, he
did so because the railways and schools required money. When
he approved the sale of spirits, he did so because
the Treasury required revenue. When he confirmed punishment, he did
so because order had to be maintained. When he strengthened
censorship, he did so because false doctrines confused simple minds.
Every isolated act was explained to him as a means
toward good; and because he desired the good, he accepted
the means.

By Christmas Eve his head ached continually. He felt as
a thirsty traveller must feel who sees water before him
but is not permitted to drink, for sleep was always
near and never obtainable. Even at meals he caught himself
reading the expressions of those around him as if they
were official documents. His wife spoke to him twice before
he heard her.

"You are not listening."

"I am, my dear."

"What did I say?"

He smiled, confessed, and took her hand. She was young
like himself, but calmer, and there was in her clear
gaze something he both loved and feared: she had not
been brought up amid men who considered obedience the first
of virtues, and she sometimes asked questions to which ministers
gave no satisfactory answer.

"You must rest tonight," she said. "It is Christmas."

"Yes, tonight I shall rest."

"You have said that every night."

"But tonight there are no audiences after supper."

"There are always papers."

"I will not look at them."

When he entered his bedroom, however, he found upon a
small table beside the fire three portfolios marked Urgent. He
opened the first. It concerned the execution of a man
convicted by a military court. The sentence awaited confirmation. The
second contained proposals for preventing unlawful emigration across the western
frontier. The third concerned disturbances among factory labourers who had
demanded higher wages and a shorter working day.

He read the military sentence first. The condemned man had
belonged to a secret society, had distributed proclamations, had possessed
a revolver, and had fired at a police officer during
his arrest. The officer had not been wounded. The court
recommended death because an example was necessary.

The young Tsar took up his pen, put it down,
and read the minister's note. Clemency, the note said, would
be interpreted as weakness at a time when revolutionary agitation
was increasing. He wrote his name beneath the sentence.

In the second case he approved increased patrols along the
frontier. In the third he sanctioned the governor's measures, including
the arrest of the organisers and the occupation of the
factory district by troops.

Then, exhausted not merely by work but by the effort
of persuading himself that what he had done was right,
he went to bed.

His wife was already asleep. A shaded lamp cast a
gentle light over her face and over one bare arm
lying upon the white coverlet. Beyond the windows the city
was silent beneath snow. Church bells sounded far off, announcing
the feast of peace and goodwill.

The young Tsar thought of the Child in the manger,
of angels, shepherds, and the star. He remembered Christmas in
his own childhood: wax candles reflected in golden icons, the
scent of fir branches, his mother's soft hand upon his
hair. He crossed himself.

"Lord, help me to govern these people for their good,"
he whispered.

He closed his eyes and fell asleep in that unknowing
manner in which all of us surrender ourselves nightly, never
certain that the world we leave will be restored to
us.

He did not know how long he slept. Suddenly he
was conscious that someone stood beside him.

The room looked unchanged. The lamp still burned, his wife
still slept, and the frost still covered the glass; but
near the foot of the bed stood a man whose
face he could not see. The man wore neither uniform
nor court dress. His presence caused the Tsar no fear,
yet he understood at once that he must follow him.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"One who will show you the work you have completed
today."

"I have completed a great deal."

"Yes."

"And all of it, I trust, for my people's welfare."

"Come and see."

The walls of the room seemed to dissolve. The warm
air, scented with wax and linen, was replaced by a
damp and suffocating stench. The Tsar found himself in a
low corridor lit by a smoking lamp. Water trickled down
stone. Somewhere a man coughed without ceasing. Somewhere else someone
cried out in sleep and was answered by obscene laughter.

"Where are we?"

"In one of your prisons."

"My prisons?"

"All prisons of the State are called yours."

A guard unlocked an iron door. The air that came
from the cell was so foul that the Tsar recoiled.
On plank beds, on the floor, and under the beds
lay men in grey clothing. There were too many of
them for the space. Their faces were yellow or ash-coloured;
some were blotched with sores; one old man's beard crawled
with vermin. A bucket overflowing in the corner gave off
the stench which filled the room.

Near the door a young prisoner paced three steps forward
and three steps back, turning sharply each time, like a
tiger in a narrow cage. His lips moved. He had
once been handsome, but sleeplessness had sharpened his features and
fixed his eyes in a feverish stare.

"What has he done?" asked the Tsar.

"He was found with forbidden books."

"And has he been tried?"

"He has been waiting for trial eleven months."

"Why so long?"

"The investigating magistrate was transferred. Then the papers were sent
to the wrong office. Then additional testimony was required."

"That is an abuse. It must be corrected."

The guide said nothing.

On the lowest plank lay a boy who seemed scarcely
sixteen. His bare feet were purple with cold. He stared
at the Tsar without blinking.

"And this one?"

"He carried a message for his elder brother."

"Did he know what it contained?"

"No."

"Then why is he here?"

"Because the message contained a proclamation."

The Tsar turned toward the guard.

"Release him."

The guard neither moved nor seemed to hear.

"Release him, I order you!"

"He cannot hear you," said the guide. "Here your will
is present only in regulations, seals, signatures, and punishments."

The young Tsar felt indignation. "I never ordered innocent boys
to be imprisoned in filth."

"You ordered the system by which they are imprisoned. You
appointed those who administer it. You confirmed the laws which
permit it. You accept reports saying that the prisons are
satisfactory."

"But I cannot inspect every prison."

"No. Yet you call them yours."

The lamp went out. The Tsar felt himself falling into
darkness.

When he recovered consciousness, a wind full of snow struck
his face. He stood beneath a black sky beside a
frozen river. A customs station, half buried in drifts, showed
one yellow window. Mounted guards waited among the trees. Their
horses breathed white clouds into the darkness.

"What is this?"

"The frontier."

A shot sounded. Then another.

Men ran over the ice. One fell and slid forward
upon his breast. A dark stain spread beneath him. The
others disappeared among reeds, abandoning bundles of cloth.

A guard approached the fallen man, turned him with his
boot, and bent down.

"Dead," he said.

From the distant bank came a woman's scream.

The young Tsar hurried toward the body. It was a
peasant, perhaps thirty years old, with coarse mittens and a
ragged sheepskin coat. Beneath the coat, tied around his waist,
were packets of foreign cloth and tea.

"He was shot for tea?" asked the Tsar.

"For evading the duty."

"But the value of these goods cannot equal a man's
life."

"The regulations authorise the guards to fire upon smugglers who
fail to halt."

"Who made such regulations?"

"Your Customs Department, with your sanction."

The woman came stumbling across the ice, her skirt dragging
in the snow. She threw herself beside the dead man.

"Stepan! Stepan!" she cried, though his open eyes showed that
he could not answer. "What am I to tell the
children?"

A young guard stood apart. His rifle trembled in his
hands. He looked at the corpse, then at his commanding
officer, and tried to speak.

"He ran," said the officer. "You acted according to orders."

The guard's lips moved, but no sound came. He turned
away and vomited upon the snow.

"They say smuggling injures honest trade," said the Tsar weakly.
"The law must be enforced."

The guide pointed first to the dead peasant, then to
the kneeling woman, then to the soldier whose shoulders shook
as if he were weeping.

"It has been enforced."

Again there was darkness.

Now they stood in a village street. It was evening.
Mud covered the road, though frost whitened the roofs. From
one low building came singing, shouting, and the crash of
broken glass. Above the door shone the imperial eagle and
a sign announcing the authorised sale of spirits.

Inside, peasants crowded around a counter. Their faces were swollen
and bloodshot. A broad man with a red beard demanded
another bottle while his wife, holding a child, pulled at
his sleeve.

"Come home, Fedya. There is no flour."

"Let go."

"The children have eaten nothing since yesterday."

"Let go, I say!"

She clung to him. He struck her across the face.
The child began to scream.

The publican, a smooth-haired man in a clean apron, pushed
a bottle over the counter.

"No disorder," he said. "Drink if you pay, fight outside."

The wife seized the bottle, but the man tore it
from her, knocking the child to the floor.

The Tsar entered between them.

"Stop selling to him."

The publican looked through him.

"He cannot hear me either?" asked the Tsar.

"No."

"Why does the State permit such places?"

"It does more than permit them. It licenses them, protects
them, and depends upon their revenue."

The publican tapped the imperial eagle above his counter.

"I sell by the Tsar's authority," he said to the
woman. "If you object, complain to him."

The woman wiped blood from her mouth. "The Tsar is
far away. God will judge."

The young Tsar remembered the financial report he had approved
that morning. Revenue from spirits had declined in three districts.
The minister proposed opening additional shops and rewarding officials who
increased sales.

"But the Treasury needs money," he said. "Without revenue there
can be no roads, no schools, no army."

"And so the people are made drunken to pay for
institutions intended for their welfare."

"I do not make them drink."

"No. You merely arrange that temptation shall stand in every
village, bearing your emblem, and that those who profit from
weakness shall be servants of the State."

The drunken peasant dragged his wife by the hair into
the road. Men laughed. The child remained on the floor,
silent now, watching without blinking.

The Tsar covered his face.

"Take me away."

He awoke, as he believed, in another place.

This time he sat in a courtroom. Above the judges
hung his portrait. Three judges in embroidered collars whispered together.
A prosecutor sorted papers with white, well-kept hands. Behind a
railing stood a thin woman in a black shawl. Her
knees shook so violently that she supported herself against the
wood.

She was accused of stealing money from the house where
she worked as a servant. The amount was small, but
the prosecutor spoke for a long time concerning the sacredness
of property and the danger of moral corruption among the
lower classes.

"Did she steal it?" asked the Tsar.

"No."

"How do you know?"

"The master's son took the money. His father paid the
investigating officer to omit certain testimony."

"Then she will be acquitted."

The guide looked at him.

A witness was called: a cook with frightened eyes. She
glanced toward the master, who sat among the spectators.

"Did you see the accused near the desk?"

"Yes."

"Did she behave suspiciously?"

"She was crying."

"Why?"

"She had been dismissed."

The woman behind the railing tried to speak.

"I cried because his son—because the young master—"

The presiding judge struck the table.

"You will answer only when questioned."

The prosecutor explained that poverty did not excuse crime. Indeed,
he said, poverty often produced envy, and envy led to
theft. The judges withdrew, returned, and sentenced the woman to
exile.

She listened, failed to understand, and attempted to sit down
where there was no chair. A guard caught her beneath
the arms.

"I have two little girls," she said. "Where will they
go?"

The court moved on to the next case.

"This cannot be justice," said the Tsar.

"It is justice administered by men who seek promotion, fear
influence, accept bribes, and obey forms."

"But judges are independent."

"Some are independent of mercy, some of truth, and almost
none of those above them."

"I will punish these judges."

"Another judge will sentence them, another official investigate them, another
minister report to you that corruption has been eradicated. Meanwhile
the machine will continue, for its first purpose is not
justice but obedience."

The Tsar was about to reply when the courtroom widened,
blurred, and became many rooms at once.

Then other scenes passed before him.

He saw tax collectors entering a peasant hut in which
there was nothing to seize except a samovar, a winter
coat, and the cow whose milk kept three children alive.
The father pleaded that the harvest had failed. The collector
replied that arrears could not be cancelled without authority. The
samovar, coat, and cow were taken. In his report the
collector wrote: "Collection successfully completed."

He saw labourers building a railway across a marsh. They
stood knee-deep in black water, ate rotten cabbage, and slept
in barracks where fever passed from man to man. The
contractor supplied inferior food and charged the State for good
food. The inspector received an envelope and certified that conditions
complied with regulations. When two workers died, their wages were
withheld to cover the cost of burial.

He saw a governor's reception, brilliant with uniforms and jewels,
while outside the palace petitioners waited in snow. A widow
had walked two hundred miles to complain that her land
had been taken by a nobleman. An aide told her
the governor was occupied. Inside, the governor was explaining to
his guests that the peasants loved the Tsar and had
no serious grievances.

He saw a police office where a merchant placed money
beneath a ledger and received permission to open a factory
in a crowded district. Months later the factory burned because
the doors opened inward and had been locked during work.
Women threw themselves from upper windows. The report blamed panic
among the employees.

He saw a church where soldiers entered during worship and
dragged old men from their knees because they crossed themselves
with two fingers instead of three. Sacred books were confiscated.
A baby was taken from its mother so that it
might be baptised according to the approved rite. The priest
said that error must not be permitted to endanger souls.

"But they believe in Christ," said the Tsar.

"They believe that they must obey God rather than your
council."

"Surely belief cannot threaten the State."

"Then why do you punish it?"

He saw students beaten by mounted police for gathering in
a square. One had come merely to look. His skull
was split by a sabre. An officer later explained that
firmness had prevented wider disorder.

He saw a newspaper office closed at midnight. Type was
broken, papers sealed, and the editor arrested because he had
published an account of famine not authorised by the censor.

He saw villages where men, women, and children mixed bark
with flour. Government warehouses nearby contained grain reserved for military
needs. An official wrote that reports of famine were exaggerated
by enemies of the State.

He saw a regiment on parade beneath the summer sun.
A soldier fainted. A sergeant kicked him until he stood,
then struck him because his hands trembled. The colonel, observing
from horseback, praised the regiment's discipline.

He saw recruits stripped naked while clerks mocked their bodies;
old mothers clung to sons chosen for service; married men
were sent thousands of miles away; and youths who had
never harmed anyone were taught that, upon command, they must
plunge steel into the bodies of other youths who had
never harmed them.

"An army is necessary," said the Tsar. "Without it foreign
powers would attack us."

"Each ruler says the same. Each increases his army because
the others increase theirs, and all prepare the slaughter which
none claims to desire."

"But soldiers defend their country."

"Sometimes. And sometimes they fire upon workers asking for bread."

The young Tsar saw such a firing. A crowd approached
a government building carrying icons and his portrait. They sang
a hymn and called him their father. Infantry blocked the
street. An officer read a proclamation which no one could
hear. Stones were thrown from somewhere behind the crowd. The
order was given.

Rifles cracked.

A girl in a blue scarf spun around and fell.
An old man holding the Tsar's portrait looked astonished, as
if betrayed by the painted face. A mother knelt over
her son while soldiers advanced with bayonets.

"I did not command this!" cried the Tsar.

"Your governor did."

"I would have forbidden it."

"Your laws gave him authority."

"He should have waited."

"Your ministers praise promptness."

"I knew nothing of it."

"You will receive a report saying the troops displayed restraint."

Again darkness covered him, but now he prayed not to
see more. Yet the guide's hand—or what seemed like a
hand—touched his shoulder.

"You wished to see the work done in your name."

"I have seen enough."

"You have seen only what one night can contain."

They stood in a military prison. A soldier was tied
to a bench. His back was naked. Two men held
him while another raised a rod.

"What is his offence?" asked the Tsar.

"He struck a corporal."

"Why?"

"The corporal struck him first."

The rod fell. The soldier's body tightened, but he did
not cry out.

"Why was the corporal not punished?"

"A superior may strike an inferior to maintain discipline. An
inferior who returns the blow attacks the principle of authority."

The rod fell again. Blood appeared in thin lines.

The Tsar turned away, but another scene waited behind him.
In a fortress cell a man sat alone. His beard
had grown to his chest. He spoke continually to someone
who was not there. Each morning a guard looked through
a hole in the door and recorded that the prisoner
was alive. He had spent nine years without trial because
his release was considered dangerous and his trial inconvenient.

In another cell a woman pressed her ear against the
wall to hear a fellow prisoner tapping. She had been
arrested for hiding her brother. Her child had died while
she was in confinement. The letter announcing the death had
been withheld because officials feared it might agitate her.

Then came carts moving eastward beneath rain. Men and women
in chains walked beside them. Some were thieves, some murderers,
some rebels, some merely companions of rebels. Among them was
a schoolteacher who had taught peasants to read from an
unapproved book; a doctor who had treated a fugitive; a
young woman who had printed leaflets; and an old sectarian
who refused to swear an oath. Their feet bled through
torn boots. Villagers crossed themselves as the convoy passed.

"Criminals," said one villager.

"Unfortunate ones," said another.

"They must have done something."

"Yes," answered the guide. "People say this because otherwise they
would have to ask why they themselves are free."

The road became white with snow. A prisoner fell. The
convoy officer ordered him lifted. When it was found that
he could not walk, he was placed in a cart
beside a corpse.

"Where are they going?"

"To settlements, mines, prisons, and places from which many will
not return."

"Who decided their fate?"

"Courts, governors, police chiefs, ministers—and you."

The Tsar groaned.

"No, not I. I do not know their names."

"Did you know the name of the man whose death
sentence you signed tonight?"

The Tsar remembered the first portfolio, the neat summary, and
his signature beneath the words "Let the sentence be carried
out."

At once he stood in a small stone room. Dawn
had not yet come. Several officials waited. One smoked a
cigarette and complained of the cold. A priest arranged his
stole. The condemned man was brought in with his hands
bound.

He was younger than the Tsar had imagined. His face
was colourless, but he walked steadily. When the priest held
out a cross, he kissed it, though with an expression
that showed he thought less of the priest than of
the figure nailed upon the wood.

"Have you any final request?" asked an official.

"Send my letter to my mother."

"It will be examined."

"And tell her I was not afraid."

Outside stood a scaffold.

The Tsar seized the guide.

"Stop this. I revoke the sentence."

"You are dreaming."

"Then let me wake!"

"You signed while awake."

The condemned man looked at the pale sky. For one
moment terror broke through his composure; his mouth opened, and
he drew a breath like a child about to cry.
The executioner covered his face.

"Whatever he has done," said the Tsar, "we cannot do
this."

"Why not?"

"Because to take a human life is terrible."

"Is it less terrible when done by many men, each
performing one small duty?"

The priest murmured prayers. The clerk read the sentence. The
executioner adjusted the rope. The official watched his clock. Each
had only a limited task, and therefore each believed himself
innocent.

The body dropped.

A soldier guarding the scaffold made a choking sound. He
had never seen a man die. That evening he would
drink until he could no longer see the covered face;
in later years he would strike his wife and children;
and everyone would say that he was naturally brutal.

The young Tsar fell to his knees.

"O God, what have I done?"

The guide's voice came from everywhere and nowhere.

"You wished to do good."

"I did. I do."

"And you believed that because your intention was good, deeds
done through your authority could not be evil."

"What can I do? I am one man. The empire
is immense. Abuses cannot be avoided."

"Then abandon the claim that all authority is yours."

"But if I surrender authority, disorder will follow. Ambitious men
will struggle for power. Provinces will rebel. Blood will be
shed."

"Blood is shed now."

"My duty is to preserve the State entrusted to me."

"Who entrusted it?"

"God."

"Did God command you to imprison the innocent, sell drink
to the poor, flog soldiers, persecute worshippers, silence truth, seize
the peasant's cow, exile the courageous, and hang those who
resist you?"

"I did not command these things as such."

"You commanded officials to preserve an order founded upon compulsion.
They used the means compulsion always uses."

"What, then, is required of me?"

"First, do not ask what is required of a Tsar.
Ask what is required of a man."

The execution yard vanished.

The young Tsar opened his eyes and found himself in
his bedroom. The lamp still burned. His wife slept beside
him. His heart beat violently, and his nightshirt was wet
with sweat.

"A dream," he said. "Thank God, only a dream."

But the relief lasted only a moment, for the portfolios
remained on the table. He saw the red one that
contained the death sentence.

He rose, wrapped himself in a dressing gown, and rang.

A servant appeared.

"Send at once for Prince Sergei Ivanovich."

"At this hour, Your Majesty?"

"At once."

The old courtier arrived less than half an hour later,
fully dressed, grave and composed, as though he had expected
the summons. He had served the young Tsar's father and
had been among the first to kiss the son's hand
after the accession. He loved the dynasty with a love
in which devotion to the throne could no longer be
separated from devotion to his own place near it.

The Queen had awakened and sat near the fire. The
Tsar paced before them, relating what he had seen: the
prison, the frontier, the tavern, the courtroom, the hungry villages,
the persecuted believers, the soldiers, the exiles, and the execution.

He spoke rapidly, sometimes losing the thread, sometimes repeating, "In
my name—do you understand?—in my name."

The old courtier listened with respectful concern. When the Tsar
had finished, he sighed.

"Your Majesty's dream does honour to your heart."

"It was more than a dream."

"Certainly it reflects real anxieties. No government is perfect. Abuses
occur and must be corrected."

"Corrected? A man is to be hanged because I signed
this."

"A convicted assassin."

"He killed no one."

"He fired upon an officer of the law."

"And therefore we kill him?"

"The court weighed the circumstances."

"I confirmed the sentence without knowing him, without hearing him,
without even seeing his face."

"Majesty, that is precisely why courts exist. A sovereign cannot
personally examine every case."

"Yet it is called my judgment."

"The forms of sovereignty must be preserved."

The Tsar stopped pacing.

"Forms? A rope is not a form to the man
whose neck it breaks."

Prince Sergei Ivanovich lowered his eyes, permitting the outburst to
pass.

"Your Majesty is exhausted. Weeks of labour, combined with the
solemn associations of Christmas, have produced a vision of unusual
force. But one must distinguish emotion from policy. The prison
system may require reform; customs regulations may be moderated; corruption
must certainly be punished. None of this alters the foundation.
Russia needs a strong, undivided authority."

"Why?"

"Because the people are not prepared for anything else. They
are good, faithful, religious—but childlike. Give them liberty before they
understand its duties, and demagogues will deceive them. The empire
will break apart. Property will be attacked, the Church insulted,
officers murdered, and Your Majesty's own family endangered."

"So I must retain unlimited power for the people's good?"

"It is your sacred burden."

"And if evil is done by that power?"

"Then remove the evil servants, Sire, but preserve the power.
Your father understood this. Authority itself is innocent; only its
misuse is blameworthy."

The Queen, who had remained silent, now spoke.

"But if one man cannot know how his authority is
used, how can he prevent its misuse?"

The old courtier turned toward her with ceremonious courtesy.

"By choosing faithful advisers."

"He chooses them from among those who tell him that
all is well."

"Not all, Madam."

"Most. And those who do not soon cease to be
advisers."

The courtier smiled sadly, as one smiles at intelligent but
impractical youth.

"Representative institutions are fashionable in the West. They produce speeches,
factions, ministerial crises, and newspapers which insult everything sacred."

"They also allow those who pay taxes to say how
taxes are spent," said the Queen, "those whose sons become
soldiers to say when war is made, and those who
suffer under laws to take part in making them."

"The common people cannot govern."

"Perhaps not. But neither can one man govern millions merely
by signing what others place before him."

The Tsar looked from the old courtier to his wife.

"What do you advise?"

She met his gaze.

"Call representatives from the towns, the provinces, the peasants, the
workers. Give them authority, not merely permission to speak. Let
laws be made openly. Let courts be independent of ministers.
Let no one be imprisoned without trial. Let the government
answer to the people whose lives it directs."

Prince Sergei Ivanovich grew pale.

"Such a step once taken cannot be recalled."

"Perhaps it should not be recalled."

"It would be the end of autocracy."

"Perhaps autocracy should end before it destroys those who maintain
it and those compelled to endure it."

The old courtier stood.

"I beg Your Majesty to consider not the passing terror
of a dream but the judgment of history. Your ancestors
gathered this empire through sacrifice. You received power intact. You
have no right to diminish the inheritance of your son."

The Tsar looked toward the bed where, beyond a half-open
door, the cradle of his infant heir stood in the
adjoining nursery.

No right to diminish his son's inheritance. The words had
the weight of tradition, family, duty, and fear. Yet beneath
them he heard another question: What was this inheritance? Palaces,
armies, ceremonies, obedience—and prisons, gallows, taxes, hunger, and blood?

Then the voice of the guide spoke again, though neither
the Queen nor the courtier appeared to hear it.

"You are still asking what preserves your power."

The Tsar pressed his hands to his head.

"What else can I ask?"

"What preserves your soul."

"If I divide authority, will evil cease?"

"No."

"If I establish assemblies, will officials cease taking bribes, judges
cease lying, men cease tormenting one another?"

"No."

"Then what certainty have I?"

"You seek certainty where only duty is given."

"What is my duty?"

"You know it."

"I do not."

"You know that you must not kill."

"Yes."

"Then do not sign death sentences."

"You know that men must not be imprisoned without judgment."

"Yes."

"Then release those held without trial."

"You know that faith cannot be forced."

"Yes."

"Then cease persecution."

"You know that drunkenness destroys body and soul."

"Yes."

"Then do not make the State its merchant."

"You know that truth must not be silenced."

"Yes."

"Then abolish the power that silences it."

"You know that no man should possess another man's conscience,
labour, body, or life."

The Tsar whispered, "But if I act alone, they will
say I betray my office."

"Fulfil the office of a human being."

"They may remove me."

"Then be removed."

"They may kill me."

"All men die."

"My wife, my child—what of them?"

"Love them. But do not purchase their safety with the
suffering of other wives and children."

The Tsar looked at the Queen. Her face was full
of concern. The old courtier watched him with controlled alarm.

"Did you hear?" the Tsar asked.

"Hear what?" said the Queen.

"The voice."

Neither had heard.

Prince Sergei Ivanovich bowed.

"Your Majesty needs sleep. At daylight the sentence can be
reviewed, if that will ease your mind. We may also
appoint a commission on prison conditions. Such measures will reassure
the public without weakening authority."

A commission. Reports, inspections, recommendations, new regulations, new portfolios. The
same words returning, while somewhere a prisoner paced three steps
forward and three back; somewhere a woman begged for the
cow that fed her children; somewhere a soldier raised a
rod; somewhere an official wrote "successfully completed."

The Tsar went to the table and took up the
red portfolio. He opened it, found the sentence, and tore
the sheet across. Then he tore it again.

The old courtier moved involuntarily forward.

"Majesty!"

"The execution is cancelled."

"That may still be done by a formal act of
clemency. But the document—"

"Send a telegram at once."

"Certainly. Yet I implore Your Majesty not to confuse one
act of mercy with a change in the principles of
government."

The Tsar opened the second portfolio, concerning the frontier patrols.
He saw again the peasant sliding over the ice, the
woman in the snow, the young guard unable to speak.

He opened the third and saw the signatures authorising troops
against workers.

How easily paper could be torn. How difficult to tear
the net of habits, fears, offices, interests, honours, and lies
which covered the empire and reached into this very room.

"What can I do?" he said once more.

The Queen approached and placed her hand upon his arm.

"Begin with what you know is right. Then give others
the power to prevent what you cannot see."

The courtier shook his head.

"Begin by mastering yourself, Sire. A monarch's personal feelings, however
noble, must yield to the needs of the State."

And the inward voice said:

"Begin by remembering that the State is not a god,
and that no throne stands higher than the law written
in the human heart."

The bells rang again. Their sound swelled through the dark
city, over palace roofs and frozen canals, over barracks, hospitals,
taverns, police offices and prisons; over warm houses where children
woke to candles and gifts, and over huts where there
was no bread; over the scaffold where men waited for
dawn, and over the churches in which priests proclaimed peace
on earth.

The young Tsar listened.

He thought he was fully awake now. He believed that
the torn sentence in his hand was real, that his
wife's hand upon his arm was real, that the old
courtier's anxious face was real, and that the choice before
him could no longer be postponed.

Then he opened his eyes.

He was lying in bed.

His wife slept beside him, one bare arm upon the
white coverlet. The shaded lamp burned quietly. The three portfolios
remained unopened upon the table.

For a long time he did not move.

Had he dreamed only the prisons, blood, exile, and execution?
Or had he also dreamed the awakening, the torn paper,
the courtier's warning, his wife's counsel, and the voice that
demanded of him not the duty of a ruler but
the duty of a man?

Outside, Christmas bells continued to ring.

The young Tsar rose upon one elbow and looked at
the portfolios. He could summon the old courtier. He could
consult his wife. He could sign, delay, pardon, appoint commissions,
dismiss ministers, call representatives of the people, surrender part of
his authority, or attempt to preserve it all. He could
persuade himself that abuses were unavoidable, that the people were
unprepared, that history required firmness, and that God had placed
him where he was. Or he could refuse to command
what no man, whether crowned or obscure, may do without
sin.

He reached toward the bell.

Whether he rang for the ministers, for his wife, for
the order of pardon, or for those who would help
him renounce the terrible solitude of unlimited power, no one
in that room yet knew.

Perhaps he himself did not know.

What he chose, and whether he preserved his throne at
the cost of his conscience, divided his power in the
hope of justice, or obeyed the law which speaks more
quietly than ministers and more sternly than armies, would be
told not on that Christmas morning, but by the lives
of the people during the fifty years that followed.

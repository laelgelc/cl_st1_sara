## THE RUBE BREAKS INTO FAST COMPANY

I have seen pitchers come and pitchers go in twenty-five
years of baseball. I have watched lean boys with whips
for arms burn out before the Fourth of July, and
old fellows with no more speed than a grocer's clerk
fool the best batters in the land by putting the
ball where bats could not meet it square. I have
seen courage without skill, skill without judgment, and both of
them without the iron nerve that holds a man together
when three runners are dancing off the sacks and twenty
thousand throats are roaring for his ruin.

But I never saw the equal of Whittaker Hurtle's first
game in fast company.

We called him the Rube. That name stuck to him
because he came out of the hills wearing trousers too
short, boots too heavy, and a straw hat that appeared
to have been used in several seasons of harvesting. He
was six feet four if he was an inch, broad
through the shoulders, loose in the joints, and so long
in the arms that when he stood easy his hands
seemed to hang somewhere near his knees. Off the field
he moved as if each foot had to wait for
separate instructions. On the field—well, that was another matter.

Chicago bought him from my club late in August.

"You're selling a pennant, Mac," Nan told me when the
papers printed the news.

Nan was the Rube's wife, a slim, bright-eyed girl who
knew a sacrifice from a squeeze play and could tell
by the sound of a hit whether it was fair
or foul. She had followed our club through rain, dust,
railroad wrecks, double-headers, and six-game losing streaks. There were men
on my pay roll who did not know half as
much baseball.

"I'm selling a pitcher," I said.

"You're selling the best pitcher alive."

"He hasn't faced big-league batting."

"He's faced bats."

"That's different."

Nan snapped her fingers.

"A bat's a bat, Mac. And Whit's Whit."

That was her argument, and you could not drive a
wedge into it.

Chicago was out of the race, but Philadelphia needed only
one victory to land the flag. The schedule had tightened
until every game had become a public trial. The Quakers
had won two straight championships and were the hardest, cleverest,
most confident set of ball players then drawing salary. They
could hit speed, wait out curves, steal signs, upset young
pitchers, and manufacture a run out of a base on
balls, a scratch single, and a bad breath from the
umpire. Their captain, Cogswell, was an old catcher who knew
every trick invented since the first ball was pitched underhand.
Their glory was Lane, the center fielder—the finest right-handed batter
in the league and, in my opinion, the most dangerous
hitter of his time.

Chicago chose the pennant game for the Rube's debut.

When I heard that, I swore.

Nan clapped her hands.

"They want to see what he's made of," she said.

"They want to feed him to wolves."

"He'll choke the wolves."

So we went to Philadelphia—Nan and I, old Billy Spear,
who had caught the Rube in our league, and two
club directors who had discovered an intense friendship for Whittaker
after Chicago paid the purchase price. We got seats behind
the Chicago bench, not twenty feet from the runway.

The grounds filled early. Philadelphia had come to clinch. Men
poured through the gates carrying horns, pennants, newspapers, cushions, and
the settled conviction that the afternoon belonged to them. The
grand stand darkened row by row. The bleachers waved and
glittered. Vendors bawled; bands blared; boys scrambled along the railings.
High over center field hung a blue autumn sky, clear
and cold enough to put life into a pitcher's arm.

Everywhere there was one cry:

"One more! One more game!"

The Quakers came out to practice, and the stands welcomed
them with a roar that rolled from pavilion to pavilion.
They pranced and danced and bounced over the turf, snapping
the ball round the infield, racing under flies, driving sharp
liners against the fences. They had the loose, laughing ease
of champions. Each man knew his business; each expected his
neighbor to know his. That certainty is worth runs before
a game begins.

Lane trotted past our seats. He was not a large
man, but he had thick wrists, quick shoulders, and the
easy balance of a cat. The crowd called his name,
and he lifted his cap without looking up.

"There," I said to Nan, "is the man your Whit
must beat."

She studied Lane soberly.

"He stands too close to the plate."

Billy Spear chuckled.

"So he does, ma'am," he said. "But nobody's persuaded him
to move yet."

Then Chicago came on, greeted by scattering applause and plenty
of good-natured abuse. Last out of the runway lumbered the
Rube.

For a moment the park did not understand what it
saw.

His new uniform pinched him under the arms and stopped
several inches short of his shoe tops. The club had
given him a cap too small, so he carried it
in one hand. His hair stood up in a yellow
shock. He wore one red stocking and one dark one,
though how that calamity had escaped the clubhouse attendants I
never learned. In his glove hand he bore a horseshoe
charm Nan had tied there.

A laugh began behind the Philadelphia bench. It traveled along
the boxes, climbed the grand stand, and scattered through the
bleachers.

"Hey, farmer!"

"Where's your plow?"

"Did they leave the barn door open?"

A Philadelphia substitute flapped his arms and crowed like a
rooster.

The Rube looked toward him with mild interest.

"Gol darn," he said, loud enough for us to hear.
"They got a chicken loose."

Nan went white with anger. I caught her wrist before
she could answer the whole Quaker bench.

"Save it," I told her.

"I hope he kills them."

"Not all of them. We need witnesses."

Chicago's catcher that day was Mike Kelly—not the famous Kelly
of earlier years, but a stocky Irishman with a hard
hand and a cool head. He led the Rube to
one side and began warming him up.

The first throw sailed six feet over Kelly and struck
the wire before the grand stand.

The crowd shouted with delight.

The second bounced ten feet in front of the plate.

Philadelphia's players pounded the bench.

The Rube grinned. He had done that to Billy Spear
the first day I ever saw him. It was his
way of loosening his back, though there was no reason
the Quakers should know it. His third pitch went straight
to Kelly's mitt with such speed that the smack cracked
through the park like a board splitting.

The laughter dropped.

The fourth ball broke down and away.

The fifth darted in at Kelly's fists.

The sixth was a slow curve that appeared to stop
halfway and then tumble over the plate.

Kelly took off his mask and stared at the Rube.

"That all you got?" he asked.

"Nope," said Whittaker.

"What else?"

"Some."

Kelly came over to the bench, where Chicago's manager, Brady,
sat rolling a plug of tobacco in one cheek.

"He's got enough," Kelly said.

Brady glanced at me. I nodded. He did not need
my approval, but he wanted it just then.

The umpire called the clubs in. Philadelphia took the field.
Chicago went out in order, three up and three down,
while the crowd prepared to celebrate.

Then the Rube walked to the box.

He did not run. He did not look into the
stands. He dug a shallow place for his right foot,
rubbed the new ball between his palms, and gazed toward
Kelly as if nothing existed beyond the catcher's glove.

Philadelphia's first batter was Downey, a little left-handed fellow with
the patience of a tax collector. He had drawn more
passes than any man in the league. Against a youngster
he would not offer until forced.

"Make him pitch!" Cogswell called from the bench.

"Work him out, Danny!"

The Rube's first major-league pitch was a fast ball at
the knees, cutting over the outside corner.

"Strike!"

A hum ran through the crowd.

Downey smiled and stepped out. The smile meant nothing. Old
batters smile at young pitchers the way card sharps smile
at boys with money.

The second pitch was wide.

The third looked wide. Downey let it go.

"Strike two!"

That was the Rube's fadeaway—not a true screwball, but a
hard pitch starting at a left-handed batter and sliding across
the outer edge at the last instant. Downey's smile vanished.

Kelly signaled for a curve. The Rube wound up slowly.
His big shoulders swayed back; his left knee rose; his
long body coiled until it seemed too great a contrivance
ever to come forward in order. Then he uncoiled. One
stride! A sweep of the arm! The ball floated shoulder-high,
dipped, and cut across the plate.

Downey swung after it had passed.

"Strike three!"

There was a quick burst of applause—not friendly applause yet,
but the tribute a crowd gives a trick neatly done.

Morse came next. He was a chopping hitter, fast enough
to beat a ball into the ground and reach first
before an infielder had set his feet. He crowded the
plate and called cheerfully:

"Put one over, Hayseed!"

The Rube put one over.

Morse slashed. The ball shot directly back. Whittaker stuck out
his bare hand, caught it belt-high, and stood looking at
it.

Two out.

That catch disturbed me more than it pleased me. A
line drive can break a pitcher's fingers before he knows
it has been hit. But the Rube merely tossed the
ball to first and rubbed his palm on his trousers.

Then Lane came up.

The ball park changed. It did not become quiet; it
became concentrated. The hum tightened. The bench stirred. Men leaned
forward. Lane scraped one foot into the earth and fixed
his eyes upon the Rube.

"Here's a batter," Billy Spear whispered.

"I know it."

"You shaking?"

"No."

"You're biting your cigar in half."

Lane always tested a strange pitcher's speed first. Kelly knew
it. I knew it. Whittaker did not know Lane, but
he knew hitters. His opening pitch was a quick curve
on the inside.

Lane did not move.

"Ball one."

The next was a fast ball shoulder-high. Lane swung under
it, and the force of his cut spun him halfway
round.

A roar went up—not for the Rube, but from astonishment.

Lane reset his feet. His face had changed. He had
expected speed. He had not expected that much.

The Rube threw another fast one, lower and farther out.
Lane fouled it straight back. The ball struck the roof
with a report like a pistol.

One and two.

Kelly crouched and gave the sign for the drop. Whittaker
shook his head. Kelly repeated it. Again the Rube shook
him off. Then Kelly spread his fingers for the wide
curve.

The pitch started at Lane's hip. For the smallest fraction
of a second Lane stiffened, thinking it would strike him.
Then the ball swept outward, crossed the plate, and settled
in Kelly's mitt.

Lane's bat never left his shoulder.

"Strike three!"

This time there was no laughter at all.

The Rube walked to the bench while ten thousand men
discussed him at once.

Nan was gripping the railing.

"He had Lane backing away," she said.

"One inch," I answered.

"He'll get more."

Chicago threatened in the second but did not score. A
single, a sacrifice, and a long fly brought a runner
to third. Philadelphia's pitcher, Stetson, struck out the next man
with a jump ball above the hands. The Quakers came
in talking louder than before. Their humor had become ridicule
sharpened by purpose.

"Now get after him!"

"Don't let the busher settle!"

Cogswell led off. He was slow afoot and no longer
dangerous on a high fast ball, but he could make
a young pitcher throw fifteen times and remember each pitch
for nine innings.

He looked at two balls.

"Make him come in," shouted the bench.

The Rube came in with a straight fast one. Cogswell
fouled it. Another fast pitch cut the corner for strike
two. Cogswell stepped away, adjusted his belt, rubbed his hands
with dust, spoke to the umpire, and examined the left-field
flag. Every movement said: I have all afternoon, youngster; let
us see whether you have.

Whittaker waited without impatience.

At last Cogswell entered the box.

The Rube threw his slowest curve.

Cogswell swung too soon and rolled an easy grounder to
short.

"Good head!" Billy exclaimed. "He changed pace on the old
fox."

The next man, Harkness, hit a towering foul near our
seats. Three Chicago players chased it. The catcher called, the
first baseman kept coming, and the ball dropped between them.

The Quaker bench erupted.

"Got 'em rattled!"

"Shake him up!"

But the Rube had returned calmly to the box. He
struck Harkness out with three pitched balls—the last one so
fast Kelly's mitt was driven back against his chest.

Pepper, Philadelphia's burly first baseman, then drove a hard smash
toward right. Chicago's second baseman knocked it down, recovered on
one knee, and threw too late.

That was Philadelphia's first hit.

The crowd awoke in earnest. Pepper clapped his hands at
first. The coach began a stream of talk. Pepper took
a long lead, though no one in the league had
ever accused him of stealing anything but signs.

The Rube paid no attention.

"Watch him!" Kelly warned.

Whittaker looked over, lifted his foot lazily, and shot the
ball to first. Pepper dived back in a cloud of
dust.

"Whoa, there!" yelled somebody. "The farmer's roping cattle!"

Pepper rose red-faced. The next pitch was inside to Sykes,
a dangerous pull hitter. Sykes turned and drove it foul
past third. The crowd groaned.

Whittaker tried a drop. Sykes laid off.

He tried the fadeaway. Sykes fouled it.

Then he threw a fast ball exactly at the letters.
Sykes swung late.

Three out.

Through the third and fourth innings the game remained scoreless,
and the history of the Rube's reception could be read
in Philadelphia's faces: humor; then ridicule, curiosity, suspicion, doubt. After
that came anger.

The champions did not like what was happening.

They had expected a holiday, the flag, speeches, flowers, and
supper at the hotel. Instead a giant busher in mismatched
stockings was making their finest hitters lunge, hesitate, and swear.
They tried crowding the plate. He came inside. They moved
back. He worked the outer corner. They waited for curves,
and he shot speed past them. They set themselves for
speed, and the stitch-ball floated up with the red seams
turning lazily, only to duck below their bats.

Best of all was his control. Not control in the
mere sense of putting the ball over. Any batting-practice pitcher
can do that. The Rube put it over portions of
the plate no larger than a handkerchief. Low to a
man who lifted, high to a man who chopped, close
to the wrists, away from the reach, changing not only
direction but pace and expectation. He pitched to bats as
a locksmith works at tumblers—feeling, listening, turning delicately until the
mechanism yielded.

In the fourth, Lane came up for the second time.

He received no taunts now.

Whittaker started him with a curve low. Lane let it
pass.

"Ball."

A fast ball followed on the outside. Lane drove it
foul into the upper stand, and the crowd gave a
delighted yell at the sound of his bat.

The Rube threw inside.

Lane turned on it savagely. The ball streaked toward third,
fair by inches. Chicago's third baseman, Dugan, dove full length,
knocked it down, sprang up, and threw across the diamond.

Lane was out by half a step.

He ran through the bag, stopped, and looked toward the
Rube. Whittaker looked back. Neither spoke, but from that moment
Lane ceased to regard him as an accident.

Philadelphia got a man to second in the fifth. A
scratch hit bounded over the pitcher's head, and Cogswell laid
down a sacrifice as fine as lacework. With one out
and a runner in scoring position, the crowd began stamping.

"Now! Now! Here's the run!"

Downey came up. The Rube fed him two low balls
and a strike. Downey shortened his grip, intending to punch
one through the infield. Whittaker gave him a fast ball
that rose just enough to meet the upper half of
the bat. The result was a soft fly to second.

Morse followed. He bunted unexpectedly.

The ball rolled dead along the third-base line. Dugan charged,
but the Rube crossed in front of him, scooped the
ball with his bare hand, and, without straightening, whipped it
underhand to first.

Out!

The grand stand applauded. Philadelphia applause! It began sparsely, almost
unwillingly, and then gathered force. Men admire a ball player
even while he ruins their hopes. That is one glory
of the brotherhood of fans: its loyalties are fierce, but
knowledge compels respect.

As Whittaker came in, Brady met him at the bench.

"Feeling all right?"

"Yep."

"Arm good?"

"Yep."

"Getting tired?"

"Nope."

That was the full report.

Nan leaned over the rail.

"Whit!"

He looked up, and his face softened.

"Howdy, Nan."

"You're splendid!"

"Air's nice and cool," he said, as if she had
complimented the weather.

Then Chicago scored.

It happened in the sixth with two out and nobody
on, which is the way lone runs often come—quietly, after
hope has relaxed. Chicago's left fielder, Red McGraw, chopped a
bounder toward short. The ball struck a stone or a
buried clod and jumped over the shortstop's shoulder. It was
scored a hit, though Philadelphia disputed that judgment in three
languages.

McGraw stole second on the first pitch to Dugan. Cogswell's
throw was high, and McGraw slid under the tag.

Now the Chicago bench was alive.

"Bring him home, Dug!"

Stetson worked carefully. Dugan fouled two curves, missed a fast
ball that was called wide, and then took another just
off the plate. Two and two.

Stetson tried to waste one outside.

Dugan reached for it and slapped a thin liner over
first. Pepper leaped. The ball tipped his glove, dropped fair,
and spun toward the pavilion.

McGraw came round third like a red-headed thunderbolt.

The throw from right was hard and true. Cogswell blocked
the plate. McGraw slid with spikes flashing, crashed into him,
and disappeared in dust.

The umpire spread his arms.

"Safe!"

One to nothing!

The Chicago bench emptied halfway onto the grass. Our little
party shouted itself hoarse. Nan seized Billy Spear by both
shoulders and shook him until his teeth clicked.

"I told you!" she cried.

"We ain't won!" Billy gasped.

"We've scored. Whit'll do the rest."

That was easy for Nan to say. I had managed
too many ball games to believe one run could cross
six outs, much less twelve, without encountering calamity.

Philadelphia came to bat in the sixth with Lane due
second. Morse flied out. Then Lane walked toward the plate
amid a tremendous outburst. The champions had been challenged; their
pennant was being delayed by one thin run; and all
their confidence now centered in the best bat they owned.

Lane dug in closer than before.

Whittaker's first pitch drove him back.

The crowd hissed.

Lane picked up the ball where it had struck the
catcher's glove and tossed it toward the box.

"Trying to scare me, Hurtle?" he called.

The Rube caught it.

"Nope. Jest pitching inside."

The next ball was a curve for a strike. Lane
lashed a foul down the right-field line. One and two.

Kelly signaled for the drop. This time the Rube agreed.

The pitch came in belt-high and sank. Lane checked his
swing. The umpire called it a ball.

Two and two.

Kelly wanted the curve away. Whittaker shook him off. Kelly
called for speed. Another shake.

I sat upright.

"What's he after?" Billy muttered.

Then Kelly gave a sign I recognized. My heart jumped.

The Rube had a rising fast ball. That is what
we called it, though no pitched ball truly rises after
leaving the hand. His jumped because of the tremendous backspin
and because its final line remained higher than a batter's
judgment predicted. Hitters saw it at the belt, swung at
the belt, and found it above the bat. But it
took too much out of his arm, and I had
forbidden him to use it except when nothing else would
do.

"No," I whispered. "Not yet."

Whittaker began his motion—then stopped and stepped off the slab.

Lane smiled.

Kelly went to the mound. They spoke briefly. Kelly returned.
The Rube pitched an ordinary fast ball outside.

Lane drove it on a line to center.

For one sick second I believed it would split the
fielders. Chicago's center fielder raced to his left, stretched at
full speed, and caught the ball waist-high.

Lane rounded first before he realized it had been held.
He turned back slowly.

The Rube had saved the jump ball.

The seventh passed in smoke and noise. Philadelphia's men had
abandoned mockery. They fought. They dragged their feet in the
box, complained of shadows, demanded examination of the ball, and
sent runners along the foul line to study Kelly's signs.
Cogswell shouted instructions. Coaches barked from first and third. The
crowd beat upon the boards.

Whittaker met every device with the same long face and
quiet eyes.

Pepper singled with one out. Sykes forced him at second.
The speedy Harkness ran for Sykes and took such a
lead that half the park expected him to steal.

The Rube held the ball.

Harkness danced.

Whittaker still held it.

The crowd laughed nervously. Harkness pranced farther. The Rube suddenly
wheeled. The throw flashed to first. Harkness threw himself back
and barely escaped.

On the next pitch he ran.

Kelly's throw cut straight through. The shortstop dropped his knee
before the bag. Harkness slid into leather.

"Out!"

That ended the seventh.

When the Rube walked to the bench, Cogswell came past
him carrying his mask.

"Pretty work, boy," the old captain said.

Whittaker nodded.

Praise wrung from the bosom of a great foe is
worth a column of newspaper print. Nan heard it, and
the fierce line around her mouth became a smile.

In the eighth Chicago went out quickly. Philadelphia had the
top of its order, and the park rose to meet
the crisis.

Downey hit a hard grounder to short and was retired
by a step.

Morse lifted a foul behind first. Three men converged. The
ball twisted in the wind. Chicago's first baseman reached backward
and caught it with one hand.

Two out.

Lane came up for the fourth time.

The crowd stood. Hats waved. A chant rolled from the
bleachers:

"Lane! Lane! Lane!"

I had heard mobs call for champions before, but that
cry had weight. It pressed down upon the field. Lane's
face was pale and composed. His bat moved in a
small, steady circle.

The Rube threw a strike at the knees.

Lane took it.

The second pitch missed outside.

The third was a slow curve. Lane waited and drove
it foul.

One and two.

Then came a battle such as hides inside a score
card's smallest mark. Lane fouled a fast ball into the
boxes. He tipped a drop behind the plate. He spoiled
an inside curve, then another fast one. The Rube tried
the fadeaway; Lane reached and nicked it. Six foul balls!
Seven! Each pitch had enough upon it to retire another
man, but Lane endured.

The crowd roared after every contact. Kelly walked to the
mound.

"What can't he hit?" he asked.

Whittaker rubbed the ball.

"A good one."

Kelly laughed despite himself and returned.

The next pitch was a sharp-breaking curve low and away.
Lane lunged. The ball struck the end of his bat
and bounded slowly toward second. Lane flew up the line.
The second baseman charged, seized it, and threw on the
run.

Lane hit the bag as the ball met the glove.

"Out!"

The inning was over.

Lane stopped and stared at the umpire. He did not
argue. He looked toward the box instead, where the Rube
was already walking off.

Eight innings! Philadelphia scoreless!

By now the crowd belonged partly to Whittaker. It still
wanted the pennant; it still loved the Quakers; but it
knew it was watching the arrival of a pitcher. Men
who had laughed at his stockings now shouted his name.
Boys leaned over the rails. Newspaper reporters sent messengers racing
from the press box.

Chicago failed to add a run in the ninth. Stetson
struck out two men and retired the third on a
pop fly. He had pitched a grand game himself, allowing
only the scratch and the Texas leaguer that made Chicago's
run. Under ordinary conditions the day would have been his.
But destiny, like a pennant crowd, watches only one mound
at a time.

The Rube came out for the last inning.

I studied his walk. It was no slower. His shoulders
did not sag. Yet eight hard innings against a championship
club leave marks where spectators cannot see them—in the fingers,
in the small of the back, behind the eyes.

"Three more," Nan said.

No one answered her.

Philadelphia's fourth hitter, Cogswell, led off. All afternoon he had
studied Whittaker, and now he went to bat with the
collected knowledge of thirty years. Kelly signaled for a fast
ball outside.

The Rube pitched.

Cogswell tapped a routine grounder to short.

I breathed again.

The shortstop came in too quickly. The ball struck the
heel of his glove and rolled away.

Cogswell reached first.

An error!

The park exploded. A white wave of faces rose in
every tier. Cogswell clapped from the bag and shouted toward
his bench:

"Here we go!"

Pepper was next. He could break up a game with
one swing. Whittaker worked him low, but the first pitch
was called a ball. Pepper fouled the second. Then he
took a curve outside.

Two and one.

"Make him pitch!" screamed Philadelphia.

The Rube came in with speed. Pepper drove it sharply
between first and second. The second baseman reached but could
not hold it. Cogswell stopped at second.

Two on. Nobody out.

I felt Nan's fingers close around my sleeve.

Sykes stepped up, a dangerous bunter and a fair hitter.
Everyone knew he would sacrifice. Dugan crept in from third.
The first baseman charged with the pitch.

Sykes pulled the bat back and swung.

The ball rose in a short fly toward left. McGraw
raced in, dove, and trapped it.

No catch!

The bases were loaded.

Nobody out.

The roar that struck then seemed to lift the grand
stand from its supports. Men danced in the aisles. Horns
shrieked. The Philadelphia bench surged against the railing. A single
would win the game and the pennant. A long fly
would tie it. Chicago's one run, defended through eight innings
of magnificent pitching, had shrunk to a thread.

Brady left the bench.

The crowd saw him and screamed for another pitcher.

"Take out the busher!"

"His arm's gone!"

Brady crossed to the mound with Kelly. The infield gathered
around them. Whittaker stood holding the ball.

I could not hear the conversation, but I knew its
substance. Should the infield come in? Should they play for
one at the plate or two around second? Was the
Rube tired? Did he want relief?

Brady looked toward the bullpen.

Whittaker caught his sleeve.

Even at that distance I could read the Rube's lips.

"I got 'em here. Let me finish."

Brady stared at him, slapped him once upon the arm,
and walked back.

The infield came halfway in.

Harkness batted for the next man. He was fast and
clever, just the fellow to slap a ball through drawn-in
fielders. The Rube's first pitch was low.

Ball one.

The second cut the corner.

Strike one.

Harkness shortened his grip. Whittaker threw high and tight. Harkness
backed away.

Two and one.

I thought the Rube was losing his control. Kelly called
time and rubbed dirt upon the ball, giving him a
breath. Then Kelly crouched again.

Whittaker delivered a drop.

Harkness swung over it.

Two and two.

The stands were a storm. The runners edged off. Cogswell
at third shouted something at the Rube. Pepper bounded from
second. Sykes crept from first. Every motion was designed to
divide the pitcher's mind.

Whittaker looked once at each runner.

Then he threw to Harkness—a fast ball low and outside.

Harkness chopped it to Dugan at third.

Cogswell broke for home.

Dugan seized the ball, stepped on the bag, and fired
to Kelly. Cogswell slid. Kelly planted one foot, took the
throw, and swept the ball down.

"Out!"

A force at third and a force at home—a double
play!

For three seconds no one understood. Then the Chicago bench
leaped, while Philadelphia groaned like a great animal struck in
the chest.

Two out.

Runners at first and second.

I began to believe again.

But Downey came up, and Downey, for all his patience,
could punch a single with the best of them. The
Rube's first pitch missed by inches.

The second was a strike.

Downey fouled the third.

One and two.

Whittaker tried to finish him with a curve. Downey let
it go.

Two and two.

Another curve missed low.

Three and two.

Both runners would go with the pitch. A base on
balls would fill the sacks, but a strike would end
it.

Kelly called for speed.

The Rube threw. Downey checked his swing, and the ball
passed barely outside.

"Ball four!"

The bases were loaded again.

Two out.

And Lane was coming to bat.

Lane up! Three men on bases! Two out! One run
between Chicago and disaster, between Philadelphia and the flag!

There are moments in baseball when a whole season seems
to draw itself into one narrow passage sixty feet six
inches long. The shouts of thousands become a single voice.
Every error, every sacrifice, every weary railroad journey, every wet
morning of practice and blazing afternoon of play stands behind
one pitcher and one batter.

Lane walked to the plate.

The sound of his name shook the park.

He had been retired four times. That made him more
dangerous, not less. A great hitter does not carry failure
as a burden; he stores it as information. Lane had
seen the curve, drop, fadeaway, slow ball, and speed. He
had fouled off everything the Rube possessed. He knew how
the fast ball cut, where the curve began, how far
the drop would sink. He had measured Whittaker through eight
innings.

And Whittaker had measured him.

Lane tightened his belt and spoke to Kelly.

"He's got one left," he said.

Kelly did not answer.

The Rube stood on the slab with the ball hidden
at his hip. His face was blank, but his chest
rose deeply once. Only once.

Nan leaned so far forward that I put an arm
around her.

"Whit," she whispered.

The first pitch was a curve outside.

Lane watched it.

"Ball one!"

Philadelphia roared.

The second pitch was a fast ball at the knees.
Lane swung and fouled it backward.

"One and one!"

The third was the fadeaway. Lane let it slide past.

"Ball two!"

The crowd howled at the umpire; Chicago howled back. Kelly
held the ball and waited for the noise to fall,
but it would not fall. It rolled and struck and
returned from every wall.

Lane stepped out. He rubbed dust upon his hands. Cogswell,
coaching at third after being forced, shouted:

"Make him come to you!"

The runners danced from their bases.

The Rube looked at Lane. Then, very slowly, he looked
toward our seats.

Perhaps he saw Nan. I believe he did.

He turned back.

Kelly gave a sign for the drop. Whittaker shook his
head.

Kelly signaled the curve.

Another shake.

Fast ball?

The Rube nodded.

But I knew, and Kelly knew, that it was not
the common fast ball.

It was the jump ball—the rising one—the pitch Whittaker had
saved when Lane faced him in the sixth; the pitch
that wrung strength from shoulder and wrist; the pitch no
champion in that park had yet seen.

"He's going to use it," Billy whispered.

Nan did not ask what.

Whittaker set himself.

Lane crouched slightly, waiting for speed. The runners went with
the motion. The Rube swayed back until his great body
seemed to lean against the noise. His knee lifted. His
shoulders coiled. Then that enormous stride shot toward the plate,
his long arm whipped over, and all his weight, all
his nerve, all the hard roads that had brought him
from the hills into fast company uncoiled behind the ball.

It came like a white streak out of a gun.

Lane swung.

His bat passed under it.

"Strike two!"

A thunderclap broke from the crowd. Lane stared at Kelly's
glove. For the first time that afternoon he looked amazed.

The Rube had shown him the pitch.

Now came the greater question: would he dare throw it
again?

Kelly tossed back the ball. Whittaker caught it. Lane stepped
away and breathed through his mouth. Cogswell shouted from third.
Brady screamed from the bench. The umpire brushed the plate
though no dust lay upon it.

Everything waited.

Kelly signaled for a curve outside.

The Rube shook his head.

Kelly called for the drop.

No.

The ordinary fast ball.

No.

Then Kelly hesitated and gave the sign.

Again!

My heart stopped. A rising fast ball requires the finest
release a pitcher has. Throw it high and the batter
lets it pass. Throw it low and it does not
jump enough. Lose the spin and it becomes a straight
ball in the strongest part of the swing.

But Whittaker wanted it.

He began his motion.

Lane did not move until the ball left the hand.
Then every muscle in him sprang. His front foot planted;
his hips turned; his shoulders followed; the bat cut level
through the heart of the plate with the full, deadly
sweep that had won a hundred games.

The ball leaped above it.

Kelly's mitt cracked.

Lane spun round.

The umpire flung up his hand.

"Strike three!"

For one breath there was silence—a vast, unbelievable silence in
which the ball rested in Kelly's glove and Lane stood
with the bat across his body.

Then the world came apart.

The Chicago players poured from the bench. The Philadelphia crowd
roared despite the lost chance to clinch. Men hurled hats,
cushions, and newspapers into the air. The bleachers stamped until
boards trembled. Boys crawled under ropes and raced toward the
box. The whole field filled with running figures.

Kelly reached the Rube first and struck him upon the
back. Dugan arrived next, then McGraw, then the rest, shouting,
pounding, dragging at him. Whittaker stood among them as bewildered
as if he had merely found a stray cow in
the road.

Lane remained near the plate for a moment. He looked
after the ball, then down at his bat. At last
he walked through the crowd to the mound.

The Chicago players quieted.

Lane held out his hand.

"Boy," he said, "that last one was a major-league pitch."

Whittaker took his hand.

"Had to be," he replied. "You're a major-league hitter."

Cogswell came up behind Lane, still carrying the dust of
his slide upon his uniform.

"I've caught pitchers for thirty years," he said, "and I'd
rather catch you than bat against you."

That was the verdict. Not the umpire's raised hand, not
the score of one to nothing, not even the shouting
multitude. When Lane and Cogswell offered their hands, Whittaker Hurtle
ceased to be a busher in borrowed clothes. The champions
had admitted him to their company.

The crowd closed around the players. Police tried to make
a path and failed. Somewhere a band began playing the
wrong tune. Hats flew. A reporter climbed onto a bench
and shouted questions nobody answered.

Nan broke from my arm.

She went over the railing before I could stop her,
landed among a squad of startled substitutes, and forced her
way onto the field. Billy and I followed as best
we could.

The Rube saw her coming.

He lifted her clean above the mob, horseshoe charm, red
stocking, foolish cap, and all. Nan laughed and cried together.
She held his face between her hands while photographers pushed
close and the stands thundered his name.

"What'd I tell you, Mac?" she called over his shoulder.
"What'd I tell everybody?"

I reached them at last.

"You told us plenty," I said.

She pointed at Whittaker as if presenting evidence to the
whole baseball world.

"He's broke into fast company!"

The Rube set her down carefully.

"Reckon so," he said.

Then he looked toward Lane, toward Cogswell, toward the scoreboard
with its one white Chicago run and its row of
Philadelphia ciphers. A slow grin moved across his plain, sun-browned
face.

That afternoon the champions did not land the flag. They
landed it later, as great clubs will. But the pennant
was no longer the only story in Philadelphia. An unknown
pitcher had walked into a hostile park, endured laughter, tricks,
errors, hits, the loaded bases, and the finest batter of
his day. He had answered reputation with control, cunning with
patience, noise with composure, and crisis with the one pitch
he had held in reserve.

By nightfall the newspapers had discovered that his stockings did
not match.

By morning every fan in the country knew the name
Whittaker Hurtle.

And whenever men ask me what makes a major leaguer,
I do not begin with speed, though the Rube had
speed enough to scorch a catcher's glove. I do not
begin with curves, control, strength, or size. I tell them
to picture the ninth inning in Philadelphia: one run, two
out, three men on, Lane at bat, and the pennant
crowd roaring.

Then I tell them how the Rube looked in for
the sign—and asked for the jump ball twice.

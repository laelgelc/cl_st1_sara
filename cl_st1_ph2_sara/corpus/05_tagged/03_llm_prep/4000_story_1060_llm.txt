"Halloa! Below there! Look out!"

The cry came to me from no visible mouth.

I had walked, toward the close of a pale October
afternoon, beyond the last cottages of a manufacturing town, and
had followed a neglected path which rose among furze and
chalk until the country fell away on either side. The
railway, though near, was concealed from me. I heard it
at intervals—a distant iron muttering, succeeded by a prolonged concussion
among the hills—but I saw neither engine nor carriage. Presently,
reaching the edge of a steep excavation, I looked down
into a cutting so deep and narrow that it seemed
less a work constructed for the passage of men than
a wound driven through the earth.

Far below, beside a small cabin, stood a man with
a furled flag in his hand.

"Halloa!" I called. "Below there!"

Instead of looking immediately upward, as any person might naturally
have done, he turned himself about and fixed his attention
upon the mouth of a tunnel at some distance from
him. Near that black entrance burned a red Danger-light, dimly
perceptible in the declining day. He watched it with such
stillness that I supposed some train to be approaching. But
there was no movement on the Line, and after a
pause he raised his face to mine.

His attitude, his solitude, and the extraordinary depth at which
he stood produced an impression I cannot perfectly account for.
There was room enough in the cutting for two rails,
a narrow path, the signal-box, and little more. Its walls,
almost perpendicular, were stained with damp and tufted in places
with a coarse black vegetation. Above them lay a strip
of sky already losing its colour. The man appeared removed
not by distance only, but by some separation of condition,
as though I regarded him from the ordinary world while
he waited in another.

"Is there a way down?" I asked.

He continued to examine me before answering. Then, pointing with
his flag toward a place considerably to my left, he
said, in a low voice which the hollow made strangely
distinct:

"You will find a path there, sir."

I walked along the brink until I discovered a rough
zigzag descending the face of the cutting. It was steeper
than I had anticipated, slippery with clay, and so enclosed
by jutting stone that, after the first turn, the upper
country disappeared. The air became colder at every step. Water
oozed from the rock; the smell was earthy, metallic, and
close. The telegraph wires, drawn through the hollow, gave out
a faint lamenting vibration whenever the wind crossed them. I
stopped once, imagining I heard a voice among them, but
the sound prolonged itself without articulation and died away.

When I reached the rails, the man was waiting. He
was dark-complexioned, with a heavy beard, grave eyes, and an
expression in which vigilance seemed to have become habitual. His
dress was plain and appropriate to his employment; his manner,
though reserved, was civil.

"You called to me," I said.

He looked toward the tunnel.

"I heard you call," he replied.

"Was there anything remarkable in that?"

He turned his eyes slowly upon me.

"What words did you use, sir?"

"Why, the ordinary words. 'Halloa! Below there!' And afterward, 'Look
out!' because I could not tell whether you had heard
me."

He made no answer.

I observed that his hand, which held the flag, tightened
upon it. Thinking I had perhaps committed some offence against
railway regulation, I added that I was merely a traveler,
passing the place out of curiosity, and that I hoped
I had caused no inconvenience.

"No inconvenience, sir."

"Nor alarm?"

He hesitated. "You came from above?"

"Certainly."

"And you had not been at the tunnel?"

"I have never been in this cutting before."

He looked once more toward the red light. The dusk
had increased rapidly, and the lamp now shone with a
more formidable distinctness, its small circle of colour fixed against
the great black arch.

I asked whether I might enter his cabin. He assented,
though without the ready cordiality of a man pleased to
have his solitude interrupted. The room was little more than
a stone recess warmed by a fire. It contained a
desk, a stool, a clock, a telegraphic instrument, and several
official books ruled in columns. Everything was scrupulously ordered. There
were no ornaments, unless a printed timetable and a framed
list of regulations could be so described. A bell connected
with the telegraph stood above the instrument; beside it were
the handles by which certain signals on the Line were
controlled.

He placed a chair for me, and resumed his station
near the apparatus.

"You have a lonely post," I remarked.

"It is quiet, sir."

"And damp."

"The damp is worst at night."

"You pass many hours here?"

He named them. His duty, as I understood it, required
exact attention through long periods, broken by trains, messages, and
the operation of the signals. At particular times he emerged
to display a lamp or flag; at others he reported
by telegraph, recorded a passage, or answered an inquiry from
a neighboring station. While explaining these matters, he spoke with
intelligence and precision. I had at first taken him for
an uneducated man, but soon detected in his language traces
of reading and reflection unusual in one of his position.

He had, he told me, once attended public lectures and
had begun the study of natural philosophy. In youth he
had possessed opportunities that he had not used well. He
referred to his errors without complaint and to his present
circumstances without resentment. He had taught himself decimals and some
elementary algebra, and he understood such principles of electricity as
were then commonly applied to the telegraph. His pronunciation was
careful, though his voice remained low, as if it had
been subdued by the walls around him.

Our conversation was interrupted by a tremor in the rails.
It began so delicately that I felt it through my
boots before I heard anything. The telegraph wires stirred; the
darkness in the tunnel appeared to become active; then a
remote spark enlarged within it. The signalman went out with
his lamp.

The approaching engine filled the cutting by degrees—not merely with
sound, but with pressure, hot breath, and a violence that
seemed to drive the air before it. The red light
was obscured. A succession of bright windows rushed past, each
showing for an instant a face, a hand, a lamp,
or an empty compartment; and the train, which had appeared
capable of occupying the whole world, contracted immediately into two
red points and was gone. Smoke rolled along the wet
walls. A loose chain struck somewhere beneath a carriage with
a hard, repeated clang, and the echo continued after the
cause was beyond hearing.

The signalman returned, made an entry in his book, and
answered the bell.

I noticed that he did not merely attend to the
bell. He started before it moved.

At least, such was my impression.

"Did that ring?" I asked.

He looked at it. "The signal rang, sir."

"I thought you turned before it sounded."

He passed his hand across his forehead. "I know its
call."

There was no sufficient reason to question the explanation, yet
I felt that he had not given it freely. He
had several times glanced at the red light during our
conversation; not the quick professional glance of a man verifying
an instrument, but a fixed and apprehensive look. His reserve
communicated itself to me. The cabin, warm as it was,
seemed oppressively close, while outside the red lamp burned steadily
upon the dripping wall and the tunnel received every sound
without returning any intelligible answer.

When I rose to go, he accompanied me to the
foot of the path.

"I shall pass this way again tomorrow," I said. "May
I come down?"

"If you wish, sir."

"At what hour?"

"After ten, I shall be less occupied."

"Very well. I will call from above."

He seized my sleeve—not violently, but with unexpected haste.

"I beg your pardon. Would you come down without calling?"

"Certainly. But why?"

He released me and seemed embarrassed by his own action.

"The words you used—"

"'Halloa! Below there!'"

He recoiled so distinctly that I stopped.

"Those words are not pleasant to me, sir."

"Have they some association?"

"Yes."

"Will you tell me what it is?"

"Tomorrow night, perhaps."

I promised not to call. Before ascending, I turned and
asked one further question.

"When you first saw me above, did you mistake me
for another person?"

He was standing between me and the red light, so
that his face was partly lost in shadow.

"I did not know," he said, "whether I had seen
you before."

"Where?"

He lifted one arm and pointed toward the tunnel.

"By that light."

I ascended slowly. At the second turn I looked back.
He had not moved. Higher still, I could see only
the lamp, the little illuminated window of the cabin, and
the pale oval of his face raised after me. When
at last I emerged upon the open ground, the ordinary
wind, the distant town lights, and even the muddy road
appeared reassuring. Yet the words with which he had first
alarmed me returned in my thoughts, and I was conscious
of an unwillingness to look down into the cutting again.

Throughout the next day I endeavored to judge the circumstance
reasonably. The man's employment involved monotony, isolation, and an excessive
demand upon the senses. He lived among signals; he listened
for bells and watched lights; a slight derangement of attention
might readily connect an accidental phrase with some former agitation.
The reverberations of the cutting were peculiar, and the wires
gave out tones in which an expectant mind might detect
almost any sound. I had no inclination to encourage superstition,
either in him or in myself.

Nevertheless, I kept my appointment.

The night was cold and without moonlight. I had provided
myself with a lantern, but once within the descending path
its beam served chiefly to reveal the wet rock close
before me and to deepen the obscurity beyond. The wind
passed overhead; little reached the bottom. At intervals the telegraph
wires sang one thin note, which appeared to travel beside
me.

I found the signalman standing at his door.

"I am glad you did not call," he said.

"I remembered."

He conducted me inside, secured the door against the draught,
and offered me the chair. His face looked worn. For
some minutes he attended to his duty in silence. The
clock ticked loudly; the fire settled; moisture dropped somewhere beyond
the wall with a slow, regular sound.

At length he said, "You asked me whether I had
seen you before."

"I did."

"I had not seen you. But I had seen something
like your figure, and I had heard your words."

"At the red light?"

"At the red light."

"When?"

He bent toward me, lowering his voice though we were
alone.

"One night, nearly a year ago, I was sitting where
you sit now. The bell did not ring, but I
heard a cry outside—so loud and sudden that I sprang
to my feet. 'Halloa! Below there! Look out!' I opened
the door. There was a Figure beside the Danger-light."

"A man?"

"It had the appearance of a man."

"What was it doing?"

He rose and placed himself near the wall to demonstrate.
Turning toward the imagined tunnel, he raised one arm across
his face, covering his eyes from brow to cheek, while
with the other he waved vehemently from side to side.

"Like this, sir. And calling: 'Look out! Look out! For
God's sake, clear the way!'"

His imitation was so earnest, and the contrast between its
urgency and his habitual self-command so painful, that I felt
the skin contract upon my arms.

"What did you do?"

"I took my lamp and ran toward it."

"Did it remain?"

"It continued waving. I came within a few yards. I
called, 'What is wrong? Where is the danger?' It made
no answer except the same warning. I advanced. Then it
vanished."

"Into the tunnel?"

"I cannot say. It was there—and was not there."

"You searched?"

"I ran into the tunnel for five hundred yards. I
held my lamp to the walls. I examined the refuges.
I found no one. I came back and telegraphed in
both directions: 'Alarm given. Is anything wrong?'"

"And the replies?"

"Both answered, 'All well.'"

He resumed his seat.

I suggested, with as much delicacy as possible, that the
figure might have been a person who entered the tunnel
and concealed himself; or that the light, fogged by moisture,
might have cast an enlarged reflection of his own form
upon the vapour. A sound from above, repeated by the
rock, could have seemed to issue from below.

He listened without impatience.

"I considered those explanations, sir."

"And rejected them?"

He looked at the bell. "Six hours afterward there was
an accident on the Line."

He spoke without emphasis. That restraint made his statement more
disturbing.

"What accident?"

"A train came out of the tunnel at speed. Something
had failed beyond the farther entrance. The carriages left the
rails here in the cutting. Some struck the wall; some
were driven one into another. The dead and injured were
brought through the place where the Figure had stood."

He pointed through the cabin wall toward the red lamp.

There was a pause, during which the regular dropping outside
seemed louder.

I asked, "Were many killed?"

He named a number. I do not record it, because
the precise amount has no bearing upon what followed, and
because I afterward verified enough of his account to know
that the disaster itself had occurred. He described no wounds
and offered no theatrical particulars. He had assisted in carrying
the sufferers, had sent and received messages, and had performed
his duties amid confusion. His distress arose not from the
recollection of the spectacle only, but from the warning that
had preceded it.

"You naturally connected the two events," I said.

"Could I do otherwise?"

"You could not easily do otherwise. But two events may
be connected by our thoughts when they have no relation
outside them. A vivid impression, followed by a calamity, fixes
itself in the memory. Had nothing happened, the impression might
have faded."

He regarded me steadily.

"I have told myself so."

The bell made a small movement and rang. He turned
instantly, read the instrument, and answered. It was an ordinary
communication. Yet after he had finished, he continued to watch
the bell.

"Was that the sound?" I asked.

"No."

"The sound you heard on the night of the apparition?"

"No, sir. That cry came from outside."

"But you have heard something from the bell too?"

He did not reply at once.

"I will tell you in order," he said.

A train was due, and until it had passed he
would add nothing. He went out, changed the signal, and
stood with his lamp. I remained at the doorway. The
tunnel, viewed from that spot, had an effect unlike any
darkness I had known. It was not merely the absence
of light; it seemed a positive depth into which the
red lamp warned all things from entering, while at the
same time it existed solely to regulate their entrance. The
rails shone faintly and converged beneath the arch. From within
came a pulse, then a roar. The engine emerged with
sparks above it and steam torn backward along its sides.
For one instant the driver and the signalman exchanged gestures;
then window followed window, each illumined chamber complete and gone
before the eye could comprehend it.

As the final carriage passed, I saw a woman sitting
alone. Her face was turned toward the glass, but whether
she looked outward or at her own reflection I could
not determine.

The signalman returned.

"The second time," he began, "was seven months after the
accident. It was morning. The sun had risen above the
cutting, though it never reaches this place until later. I
was at the door and saw the Figure again beside
the red light."

"Did it call?"

"No. It stood with both hands before its face, bowed,
in this manner."

He covered his features and bent his head. The gesture
was that of one overcome by grief.

"You went toward it?"

"I did. It disappeared before I had gone many steps.
I telegraphed again. Nothing was wrong."

"And afterward?"

"That same day a train came through. As it passed
me, I saw confusion at one of the windows. A
gentleman waved to me to stop it. I signaled, and
the driver brought the train to a stand beyond the
tunnel. In one compartment a young lady had died."

"From an accident?"

"No. She had been speaking a few minutes before; then
she fell back. They said it was her heart."

"Was she known to you?"

"Never seen by me."

"And they brought her—"

"Out here. They laid her on the ground between the
rails and the wall, close to the Danger-light."

His voice failed upon the last words. He turned away,
opened the stove, and adjusted a piece of coal which
required no adjustment.

I confess that my reason, though not convinced, had become
less easy in its authority. The first appearance, as described,
might have arisen from nervous excitation; the accident following it
might be coincidence. The second might have been a recurrence
produced by memory, while the death of a passenger—sudden, but
not impossible—supplied a second coincidence. Nothing in his account established
that the Figure had any external existence. Yet the correspondence
of gesture and event, and above all the calm reluctance
with which he stated it, affected me more than I
desired him to perceive.

"You have seen it again," I said.

He closed the stove and faced me.

"For a week."

"Every night?"

"Sometimes by day. Sometimes in the dark. Not always for
long. It comes and goes."

"Beside the same light?"

"Always."

"And what does it do?"

"The first gesture. It hides its face with one arm
and waves the other. It cries, 'Halloa! Below there! Look
out!' Then: 'For God's sake, clear the way!'"

"Have you approached it?"

"I have. It is gone before I reach it."

"Have you heard it tonight?"

"Twice."

I had heard nothing.

"Since I entered?"

"Yes."

"The bell?"

He nodded toward it.

I looked at the bell. It hung motionless.

"You are certain the instrument was not actuated by one
of the stations?"

"The Spectre's ring is not like the signal's, sir. It
is a little vibration, followed by a dull stroke. I
know every lawful sound that instrument makes."

While he spoke, his eyes shifted past me. He started,
turned to the door, and stared through the window toward
the red light.

"There!" he whispered.

I looked. There was the wet wall, the black tunnel,
and the lamp staining the mist immediately around it. I
saw no figure.

"It is waving."

"I see nothing."

"Now it cries."

I heard the wire moan overhead and, far off, the
low laboring of an engine.

"No," he said, answering something beyond my hearing. "I cannot
clear the way. Where? What danger? Tell me what is
to be done!"

He opened the door. The cold entered sharply.

I caught his arm.

"There is no one there."

He submitted to my grasp, but his eyes remained fixed
on the light.

"It has gone," he said.

I closed the door and persuaded him to sit. He
trembled, not violently, but through the whole frame, like a
mechanism under continuous strain.

"Listen to me," I said. "Whatever the cause of these
impressions, you must not allow them to master you. Your
duty requires composure."

"That is what tortures me."

"How?"

"If I were a man with no charge, I could
endure it. But lives pass here. Every train trusts the
signals. If the warning means that some danger is near,
what ought I to do?"

"You have found no defect?"

"None."

"You have examined the Line?"

"Again and again."

"The instruments?"

"All correct."

"Have any messages indicated trouble?"

"No."

"Then you have done what your office permits."

"But the warning continues."

"Without information, it is not a warning upon which you
can act."

"That is the cruelty of it!" he exclaimed. "It calls
me because warning is my business. It stands by the
Danger-light; it rings the bell; it commands me to clear
the way. Yet it does not say where the danger
lies, or when it will come, or whom it threatens.
If I telegraph, what can I send? 'There is danger
somewhere'? They will demand particulars. I have none. They will
think me mad, remove me from my post, perhaps laugh—and
still the danger may come."

His words were logical within the boundary of his belief.
That fact increased my concern.

"Could you not report some suspicion regarding the tunnel?"

"I should be required to state its nature."

"An obstruction?"

"There is none."

"A weakness in the wall?"

"The inspectors would discover no weakness."

"Then you must treat the appearance as a disorder of
the senses."

"And if another train is wrecked?"

He leaned toward me.

"Suppose I say nothing, and tomorrow we carry the dead
past this door again. Suppose I recognize among them the
meaning of the gesture only when it is too late.
Would you tell me then that I had done my
duty?"

There was no useful answer except the one I had
already given: that responsibility cannot extend to knowledge denied us.
But I saw that such a proposition, though sound in
abstraction, offered him no relief.

I questioned him concerning his health. He slept badly. His
appetite was reduced. He had headaches, and at times a
pressure behind the eyes. He drank little and denied the
use of narcotics. No insanity, he said, had occurred in
his family. I inquired whether he had ever suffered fever
in that damp place, whether the telegraph's electrical discharges might
produce unusual sensations, whether prolonged attention to the red lamp
caused after-images. He answered with patience. Once, when I proposed
that the Figure might be no more than the lingering
image of his own signal gesture projected upon darkness, he
said:

"Then why does it use words I had not spoken?"

"Perhaps you had heard them elsewhere."

"All men on the railway have heard such words."

"Then memory supplied them."

"And supplied the accidents also?"

"No. Chance supplied the accidents; memory made a pattern."

He considered this, and for a moment I hoped the
explanation had touched him.

"But it is here again," he said. "Before the third
event."

The clock indicated that my visit had extended far into
the night. I was reluctant to leave him, yet my
remaining could not be indefinite. I asked whether another employee
would relieve him.

"At dawn."

"Can you continue safely until then?"

He looked offended, though only for an instant.

"My work has never been neglected."

"I did not suggest neglect."

"You feared it."

"I fear that you are ill."

He lowered his head. "Perhaps I am."

This admission gave me an opportunity. I said that diseases
of the nerves, like diseases of any other part, might
produce real suffering without moral weakness. A competent physician could
advise him. I knew a medical gentleman in the town,
sober in judgment and discreet. If the signalman permitted, I
would bring him down on the following evening, not in
any official capacity but as an acquaintance.

He did not consent; neither did he refuse.

"We will speak of it tomorrow," he said.

"Before I go, tell me one thing. When I called
yesterday, did my gesture resemble the Figure's?"

"You had one hand near your face because of the
sun."

I recollected shading my eyes as I looked down.

"And the words were the same?"

"The first words."

This explained, naturally enough, his alarm on seeing me. Yet
it also sent a chill through me when I considered
how casually I had repeated the very phrase which oppressed
him.

At the foot of the path he asked me once
more not to call from above.

"I will not."

"Nor wave?"

"Nor wave."

He held up his lamp while I began the ascent.
Its light followed me over the first turns; then a
projection of rock intervened. Midway, I heard a cry from
below.

"Halloa! Below there!"

I stopped so suddenly that gravel slid beneath my feet.

The cry was repeated, fainter.

"Look out!"

I descended two steps before recognizing the sound as the
signalman's own voice. Then came the thunder of a train,
and I understood that he was calling in the performance
of his duty to someone upon the Line. Nevertheless, I
waited until the train had passed. When silence returned, I
continued upward with a quickness I should formerly have considered
undignified.

Sleep came imperfectly. I saw the Danger-light at intervals, sometimes
as a railway signal, sometimes as a red eye fixed
at the end of an immeasurable vault. I heard the
signalman ask where the danger lay, and each time some
answer seemed about to be given, the noise of a
train destroyed it. In the morning, ordinary objects restored my
judgment. The white cloth upon the breakfast table, the clatter
of cups, vehicles in the street, and voices discussing business
all belonged to a world in which apparitions were unnecessary.

I called upon the physician I had mentioned. He was
absent upon a distant visit and would not return until
late. I therefore determined to see the signalman alone that
evening, obtain his definite permission, and arrange for the doctor
to accompany me on the next day. I did not
intend to inform railway authorities unless his condition appeared immediately
dangerous. He was conscientious, and an abrupt accusation of unfitness
might ruin him. Yet if his senses failed during the
passage of a train, silence on my part would be
equally indefensible.

This question occupied me throughout the afternoon: when does concern
become interference, and at what point does hesitation become culpable?
I had known the man for only two days. I
possessed no evidence that his work had suffered. Indeed, every
practical observation indicated the contrary. His records were exact, his
responses prompt, and his knowledge complete. The disorder—if disorder it
was—existed beside his duty without entering it. But how long
could such a division be maintained?

The weather cleared before sunset. A low band of gold
rested beneath heavy clouds, and the higher ground commanded a
broad view of fields, factory chimneys, and distant roofs. Nothing
in that open prospect prepared one for the imprisoned place
below. I reached the cutting earlier than arranged and approached
its edge without calling.

At first I thought the Spectre stood near the tunnel.

A man was there, his back toward me, one arm
raised across his eyes and the other moving repeatedly. My
heart struck hard enough to cause actual pain. Then I
observed two other figures beside the cabin, and a fourth
upon the Line. The waving man wore the coarse clothes
of an engine-driver. He lowered his arm and spoke to
the others.

Relief, though immediate, was incomplete. There were too many men
in the cutting; one of them appeared to be an
official; and all were gathered about something on the ground.

I descended.

Before reaching the bottom I saw that the cabin door
stood open. A little group had formed near it. The
telegraphic bell sounded, but no one answered until the official
stepped inside. Beside the rails lay a body covered from
the breast upward.

"What has happened?" I asked the nearest man.

He glanced at me, then at the covered form.

"Signalman killed this morning, sir."

For some moments I believed he referred to another signalman.
The mind, when confronted with a statement it refuses, can
make even plain language obscure.

"The man who belongs to this box?"

"Yes, sir."

"The dark man—with the beard?"

"That's him."

I went nearer. His face had been covered, but his
hands were visible. One was closed as though it still
held a flag.

"How?"

"Run down by an engine."

"Here?"

"Just before the tunnel."

The driver, who had been making the gesture I observed
from above, approached us. He was a broad, weathered man,
greatly distressed, and repeated more than once that no blame
could attach to him. No one, indeed, appeared to accuse
him.

"I saw him on the Line," he said. "I'd come
round the bend and had the tunnel ahead. The light
was clear. He ought to have been off the rails.
I whistled."

"Did he hear?"

"He didn't move."

"Was his back toward you?"

"Not exactly. He was looking toward the red lamp, as
if watching somebody there."

The official said, "Tell the gentleman what you told us."

The driver removed his cap.

"I shut off and braked, but there was no stopping
in the distance. I shouted. I shouted as loud as
I could."

"What words?" I asked.

He seemed surprised by the question.

"The natural words, sir. 'Halloa! Below there! Look out!'"

The walls, the rails, the tunnel, and the cold air
appeared for an instant to withdraw from me, leaving only
that phrase.

The driver continued:

"He turned his head, but not enough. I called, 'For
God's sake, clear the way!' He stood as though he
couldn't understand where the voice came from."

"And your arm?" I asked.

"My arm?"

"What did you do while shouting?"

He demonstrated. With one arm he covered his face, to
shield his eyes from the steam and the horror before
him; with the other he waved violently toward the side
of the Line.

"I did this, sir. I kept on doing it. But
he never cleared."

The gesture was exact.

I cannot say whether I uttered any exclamation. I remember
looking toward the Danger-light. It burned in the dimness of
the cutting, though the evening had not fully fallen. There
was no Figure beside it.

The official invited me into the cabin when he learned
that I had known the dead man, however briefly. His
final entries lay open upon the desk. They were written
in a firm hand and contained nothing unusual. The instruments
were in order. The signal had been properly set. Whatever
agitation had afflicted him, he had given no technical warning,
committed no recorded error, and left no statement.

"Was he killed immediately?" I asked.

"Immediately."

"Had he shown any illness?"

"None that was reported. A remarkably dependable man."

The driver stood at the door, still holding his cap.

"I never knew him fail," he said. "If a man
was safe anywhere, you'd think he was safe with him
at the signals."

The body was afterward removed along the same narrow space
by which, according to his account, the sufferers from the
first disaster and the dead young woman had been carried.
I remained until this was done. No necessity compelled me
to stay, but departure seemed at that moment an evasion.
When the men lifted him, the telegraph bell gave a
faint vibration. Every person in the cabin heard it and
turned. The official examined the instrument.

"Only the wire," he said. "No call."

The night wind had begun to move above us. It
entered the cutting by imperceptible degrees and passed along the
wires in a low, continuous complaint.

I made my ascent for the last time after the
lamps were lit. At one turn I paused and looked
down. The driver stood near the tunnel, repeating to another
man the manner in which he had waved. From that
height, with his features indistinct and the red light beside
him, he resembled what the signalman had described. Then he

I have related these circumstances in the sequence in which
they occurred, omitting no material fact and adding, to the
best of my knowledge, no colour not present in my
recollection. It may be said—and I have often said it
to myself—that the signalman's mind, strained by solitude and responsibility,
formed a warning out of familiar railway cries and gestures;
that two accidents, once associated with his illusion, gave it
authority; and that the driver's conduct at the fatal moment
followed from the common necessities of danger. Any engine-driver, seeing
a man upon the Line, might have shouted substantially the
same words. Any man confronted with unavoidable destruction might have
screened his eyes and waved.

This is reasonable.

Yet there remains a circumstance which reason states more easily
than imagination accepts: the warning that had twice appeared to
concern the lives of others, and that had returned a
third time without naming its object, reproduced in advance not
merely the fact of danger, but the very words and
action by which the signalman was summoned to escape his
own death.

I do not assert what stood beside the red light,
nor whether anything stood there except in the unhappy man's
perception. I record only the coincidence.

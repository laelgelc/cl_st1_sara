### THE SECRET DRAWER

The asylum stood beyond the last houses of the town,
upon a chalky hill where no tree seemed able to
flourish. Its long gray façade, pierced by innumerable identical windows,
had something more sorrowful than a prison: a prison suggests
guilt, escape, perhaps pardon; but this place announced only the
patient extinction of human reason.

Doctor Valmore, who had been my schoolfellow and was now
physician-in-chief there, conducted me through the wards.

"You wished," he said, "to see madness. That is like
wishing to see the wind. One sees only what it
overturns."

At that moment a frightful cry issued from a cell
at the end of the corridor. It began as a
groan, swelled into a howl, then broke into words, indistinct
but tender, as though the sufferer implored a mistress while
being strangled.

The doctor opened a small shutter in the door.

"Look."

A man was crouching upon the floor. He appeared forty
years old, but suffering had given him the decrepitude of
eighty. His body, scarcely covered by a coarse shirt, was
lean beyond belief. The knees were knots, the shoulders angles,
the hands long, yellow and agitated like starved spiders. Yet
the face retained traces of distinction. The brow was high;
the nose delicate; a short red beard covered the hollow
cheeks. His eyes alone were terrible—wide, glittering, fixed upon something
invisible above us.

Suddenly he sprang upright and extended his arms.

"Come!" he cried. "Come down! They have hidden you, but
I feel you moving through the walls. Your hair is
under their hands. It suffers. Ah, murderers!"

Then, with a movement of extraordinary violence, he hurled himself
against the stones, kissed them, scratched them, and began murmuring
words so intimate that I drew away in shame.

Valmore closed the shutter.

"An interesting case," he said. "No hereditary lesion that we
can discover. No drunkenness. No fever. An idea entered him,
established itself, and consumed everything. He no longer eats unless
forced. He sleeps only after seizures. Desire, grief, jealousy, terror—all
his passions are directed toward an object which is not
a person."

"What object?"

The doctor regarded me for a moment, smiling with that
cold indulgence physicians assume before the healthy.

"Read this."

He led me to his office and gave me a
thick notebook bound in green morocco. Its pages were crowded
with a firm, elegant handwriting that became larger and more
irregular toward the end.

"It was found in his house," he said. "I have
removed nothing. Ring when you have finished."

Then he left me.

I opened the notebook.

---

I have always been content.

This declaration may appear singular from a man whom others
considered solitary. I was not solitary; I possessed the company
of things. Human beings fatigue us by their demands, their
unexpected moods, their perpetual insistence upon themselves. Things are discreet.
They wait. They receive our affection without devouring it, and
they preserve, in their silent substance, more memories than men.

My fortune allowed me to live without profession. I traveled
when I desired; I remained at home when the world
seemed ugly. I had no wife, because marriage alarmed me
by its daily familiarity. I had mistresses, but I avoided
loving them. Present women disappointed me. Their conversation, fashions, perfumes,
gestures—all belonged too completely to the hour in which they
lived.

The dead women were otherwise.

Do not mistake me. I did not then desire the
dead. I loved what time had made of them: dreams,
portraits, names half effaced from letters. A woman who has
vanished for two centuries possesses all virtues, because none can
contradict us. She is silent, mysterious, infinitely accommodating to imagination.

I collected objects that had belonged to such women.

I had rings worn on fingers long since dissolved; fans
whose painted silks had stirred the air before forgotten faces;
little boxes that had held powder, patches, billets of love.
I owned a Venetian mirror, clouded with age, in which
perhaps some doge's mistress had watched her beauty perish day
by day. Often I stood before it and asked: What
did you reflect? What eyes leaned toward your glass? What
mouth smiled there? Where are those eyes, that mouth, that
warm and perfumed flesh?

Where are yesterday's roses? Where are last year's snows?

Clocks troubled me most. Their little mechanisms had continued to
beat while hearts stopped around them. Tick—tick—tick! A lady fastened
a jewel at her throat; a clock watched. She waited
for a lover; it watched. She grew old; it watched.
They carried her to the churchyard, and still the delicate
machine counted the hours, as if time were not the
murderer but merely the witness.

Thus my collections gave me melancholy, but a soft melancholy,
almost a pleasure. I believed myself protected from passions. I
was wrong. No fortress resists an immaterial enemy. An idea
is invisible, impalpable, intangible, without weight or substance; yet it
enters the brain, multiplies there, drinks the blood, eats the
flesh and finally inhabits the entire man.

One afternoon, while passing through the Rue des Orfèvres, I
saw an Italian cabinet in the window of an antique
dealer.

It was tall and narrow, made of blackened walnut, with
panels carved in wreaths, fruits and small grimacing faces. The
dealer believed it Florentine and dated it from the latter
half of the seventeenth century. I knew at once that
I must possess it. Why? There are moments when objects
summon us. One might say that they have waited beneath
dust and darkness for the particular hand that will awaken
them.

I bought it and had it carried home.

For eight days I examined it with a lover's curiosity.
The workmanship was admirable. Each drawer slid with a grave,
smooth whisper. The interior smelled of dry wood, old wax
and that indefinable odor which belongs to closed rooms, extinguished
houses and graves opened after centuries.

I imagined its former owners.

Had it stood in the chamber of a noblewoman? Had
she hidden letters there? Perhaps a jealous husband had searched
these compartments while she trembled. Perhaps a dagger, still wet,
had once been placed beneath those innocent linens. Every old
piece of furniture contains a drama; we merely lack the
key.

On the ninth evening I found the key.

While pressing one of the carved masks, I heard a
faint click. A narrow panel sprang forward, revealing a concealed
drawer no deeper than my hand. It resisted at first,
then yielded suddenly.

Something unrolled and fell over my wrist.

I recoiled.

It was hair—a great coil of woman's hair, bound at
one end by a tarnished thread.

It streamed from the drawer in a golden-red cascade. Not
blond, not auburn, but of that rare color seen when
evening sunlight passes through autumn leaves. It was astonishingly long,
soft, heavy and brilliant. Though imprisoned perhaps for two hundred
years, it had preserved its splendor. Hair survives us. The
eyes sink, lips blacken, flesh melts from the bones; but
hair remains, incorruptible, insolent, the last living part of the
dead.

For several minutes I did not touch it again.

Whose was it? Why had it been hidden? Had it
been cut in mourning, in love, in punishment? Was it
the relic of a girl dead before marriage? Had a
lover severed it from her corpse? Or had some crime
been committed beside that cabinet—a pale throat bent backward, a
knife, a carpet drinking blood—after which the murderer concealed the
only thing he could not destroy?

I laughed at these imaginings, replaced the tress, and shut
the drawer.

That night I dreamed of a woman whose face I
could not see. She stood before me with her back
turned, combing hair that descended to the floor. I called
to her. She did not move. I approached and touched
her shoulder. Then she turned—

I awoke.

The dream had vanished, but the sensation remained in my
fingers.

The next morning I opened the drawer. The hair lay
there, luminous and tranquil. I lifted it carefully. It was
colder than I expected, yet after a moment it warmed
in my palm. I let it run between my fingers.
It slipped, twisted, clung; it seemed at once fluid and
alive.

I put it back.

All day I thought of it.

In the evening I took it out again. I spread
it upon the table beneath the lamp. The light entered
it, divided into a thousand copper flames, and escaped trembling.
I searched for traces of its owner: a ribbon, a
perfume, anything. There was only a faint odor—perhaps of ancient
dust, perhaps of violets, perhaps imagined.

I touched it, caressed it, smoothed it.

A strange tenderness came over me.

During the following days the tenderness increased. I hurried home
to find the secret drawer. Before receiving visitors, I made
certain it was closed; after they departed, I released the
hidden spring and greeted the hair. Yes, greeted it! I
smiled when it fell into my hands.

Soon I no longer returned it immediately. I kept it
for hours upon my knees. I divided it into strands,
recombined them, braided and unbraided them. I pressed it to
my cheek. I passed it across my eyelids and lips.

Once, without reflection, I kissed it.

The kiss frightened me. I rose abruptly, ashamed, and walked
about the room. But shame is weak against desire. I
came back. The tress shone where I had abandoned it,
curved upon the velvet like a sleeping animal.

I kissed it again.

From that evening I loved it.

I loved it. I can give no other explanation. One
does not explain love by weighing the beloved, measuring her,
submitting her to reason. This hair was dead matter, certainly;
but what is the body of a living woman except
matter animated by an unknown flame? I supplied the flame.
My thought entered the relic. I gave it memory, voice,
soul.

Her soul.

For now she existed.

She was tall, because the hair was long. She was
pale, because women of that vanished age were pale. Her
eyes were dark green, her mouth grave and passionate. I
did not invent these things; I discovered them. They came
one after another, with the authority of recollection. I remembered
a woman I had never seen.

Was she gentle? Yes, but only with me. Had she
loved before? I suffered at the thought. I became jealous
of men dead for centuries, of hands reduced to dust,
of kisses whose mouths no longer existed. Sometimes I questioned
her.

"Who hid you there? Whom did you love?"

She answered by her silence; and her silence contained everything
I desired.

I ceased going into society. Living women appeared coarse, unfinished,
loud. They had red hands, imperfect teeth, dresses that rustled
disagreeably. How could they rival my unknown one, purified of
every fault by death and perfected by my longing?

At night I placed the tress beneath my pillow.

At first I merely wished to feel it near me.
Then I drew it out and laid it across the
sheet. It spread beside my head like a ray of
sunset. I took it in both hands; I wound it
about my wrists, my throat, my breast. It covered me
with its softness. I trembled. I kissed it, called it
by names—Aurelia, Lucrezia, Beatrice—none was hers, yet each brought her
nearer.

I slept badly. I waited.

For what?

For her.

Do the dead come back? We say no because we
fear yes. What is death? A door closed to our
senses. But love has senses of its own. If a
desire becomes sufficiently violent, does it not knock upon that
door? If the dead are nothing, why do they weigh
so heavily upon us? Why do rooms retain them, garments
imitate their forms, perfumes restore their presence in an instant?

I waited.

One rainy night in November, while the clocks were striking
two, I felt the hair move.

I was not asleep. I swear it. It stirred upon
my arm with a slow, deliberate caress.

I held my breath.

The mattress yielded beside me, as though beneath the weight
of a body. A warmth entered the bed. The tress
slipped from my fingers, spread over the pillow, and I
perceived—first confusedly, then with certainty—the form of a woman lying
against me.

I could not see her face. The room remained dark.
But her forehead touched my mouth; her arms enclosed me;
her breath passed over my cheek with a perfume of
violets and earth after rain.

She had returned.

Ah! What words could describe that first embrace? Terror froze
my heart while joy set it on fire. I held
her, lost her, found her again. She was solid, warm,
tender; yet sometimes my hands passed through emptiness and encountered
only the cold, abundant hair. I implored her to speak.
She answered with kisses.

At dawn she vanished.

The tress lay twisted around my body.

Thereafter she came every night.

I lived only for darkness. Day was an exile filled
with insipid obligations; night restored my beloved. I purchased jewels
for her, perfumes, antique laces. I placed them beside the
bed, and in the morning they were disturbed. Once I
found a long golden-red hair caught in the clasp of
a necklace.

Was that not proof?

But love hidden becomes torment. I wished to show her
the world and to show the world my happiness. Why
should I conceal her as a crime? Other men walked
beneath the trees with women they loved. I too would
walk with mine.

I succeeded on the following day.

I placed her hair inside my coat, close against my
breast. Immediately I felt her arm within mine. She accompanied
me through the streets.

People stared, because I spoke to her and made room
for her beside me. Their astonishment amused us. At the
opera I bought two seats. She sat at my right.
I leaned toward her, breathed her perfume, touched beneath my
cloak the warm and silken proof of her presence.

Soon I took her everywhere: to gardens, museums, restaurants. I
ordered food for her. I introduced her to acquaintances.

"This is the woman I love."

Some laughed. Others withdrew.

They were jealous; or blind.

One evening two men followed me home. The next morning
my servants entered with a physician. They spoke softly, treacherously.
They asked where she was.

"Asleep," I said.

They seized me.

I fought. I called to her. Someone tore open my
coat and dragged the hair from my breast. It flew
through the room like a golden bird. I saw it
in their brutal hands, streaming, struggling, alive!

They have taken her.

They have imprisoned me.

But she is near. At night I hear her sliding
under the doors, brushing along the walls, whispering above the
ceiling. I call, and she tries to reach me. They
torture her because she loves me. They cut her, perhaps.
They burn her.

Give her back!

Give her—

---

The final line tore across the page and ended in
a blot.

At that instant the same howl I had heard in
the corridor shook the office door. It rose and fell
through the building, full of rage, supplication and obscene tenderness.
I closed the notebook.

Doctor Valmore entered.

"Well?"

I could not immediately answer.

"Did the hair exist?" I asked at last.

Without speaking, he unlocked a cabinet. He took out a
small box and set it before me.

My heart began to beat absurdly.

He lifted the lid, thrust in his hand, and drew
forth a long coil of golden-red hair.

Then, with an almost cruel smile, he tossed it toward
me.

It opened in the air, glittering under the lamp like
a fiery wing, and fell across my hands.

I shuddered.

It was extraordinarily soft. Cold at first, it warmed beneath
my fingers. A faint perfume reached me—dust, violets, perhaps nothing.
I felt an immense disgust, the disgust of corruption, coffins
and kisses given to the dead; and beneath it, quick
and shameful, something resembling desire.

I let the tress fall.

Valmore replaced it in the box.

"You see," he said, while the madman cried out once
more in the depths of the asylum, "the mind of
man is capable of anything."

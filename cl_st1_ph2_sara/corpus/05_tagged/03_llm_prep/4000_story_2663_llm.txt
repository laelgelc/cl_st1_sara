### A Quiet Place

When Mrs Parker came in, the literary gentleman was standing
in the passage with one hand in his dressing-gown pocket
and the other holding a letter.

"Oh, there you are," he said. "I was beginning to
wonder. The place is rather beyond me this morning."

It was beyond anybody. A fish bag lay on the
floor beneath the hatstand, leaking a thin brown line over
the boards. Three cups, none of them empty, stood among
books and cigarette ends on the sitting-room table. In the
grate a saucepan had been left with porridge dried round
its rim like plaster.

The gentleman looked closely at Mrs Parker.

"And the funeral?" he asked. "Did it all go off
satisfactorily?"

She put down her old black bag.

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir."

"Good," he said, with relief. "I'm glad it was a
success."

He meant to be kind. Having said the proper thing,
he went into his study and shut the door.

Mrs Parker took off her coat. Her boots hurt worse
than usual. It was the standing yesterday, and the slow
walking behind the little box, and then all them stairs
home. The leather had rubbed the backs of her heels
raw. She unlaced the boots and set them beside the
kitchen fender. Her stockings showed two grey holes at the
toes.

For a minute she sat with her hands on her
knees.

Lennie had hated holes in stockings.

"Gran's got windows in her toes," he used to say,
pulling at them. "Gi' us a look through, Gran."

"Go on with you, you monkey."

"Can see right into next Sunday."

She could hear his laugh, the quick little gasp that
followed it, and then the cough. That cough had come
out of him as if someone much bigger had been
shut up inside his chest, knocking to be let out.

Mrs Parker stood.

There was work to do.

She filled the kettle, picked the fish bag up by
one corner, wiped the boards, gathered the cups, carried the
saucepan to the sink. The water came out rusty at
first. She waited, holding the cloth under the tap, watching
the brown turn clear.

"Mrs Parker!"

"Yes, sir?"

The gentleman called through the closed door.

"Would you mind being particularly careful with the cocoa? I
noticed last week that it seemed to be going down
rather rapidly."

She looked at the cocoa tin. There was scarcely enough
in it to cover the bottom.

"Yes, sir."

"I don't suggest anything, of course. Only one does have
to keep an eye on these little matters."

"Yes, sir."

He returned to his writing, satisfied that he had shown
firmness without sacrificing delicacy.

Mrs Parker scrubbed the saucepan. Round and round went the
wire, catching in the black. Cocoa! She had never taken
so much as a teaspoonful. Not her. Though there had
been evenings when the children had had no milk and
she had carried home scraps of bread under her apron,
not knowing whether scraps counted. But cocoa—no.

She rinsed the cups, cleaned the knives, shook the crumbs
from the cloth, swept under the table, wiped the shelves.
In the bedroom she drew back the curtains and opened
the window. Cold air pushed in, lifting the ends of
the blanket and blowing her apron out behind her.

The bed was all anyhow, sheets twisted, pillows on the
floor. A silk sock hung from the lamp. There were
books under the bed, boots beside the washstand, and on
the chair a plate with a yellow curl of cheese
upon it.

She made the bed tightly. Corners first, then the sides,
then the blanket smooth with the flat of her hand.

They had taught her that at her first place. She
was sixteen then, though small for it, and the cook
said she had the brains of a button. Up before
light, black the grates, carry the coal, scour the steps,
warm the plates, answer the bell. If the bell rang
twice, run. If it rang once, run quicker, because perhaps
you had missed the first.

The mistress kept white gloves for testing shelves. She would
pass one finger over the wood and hold it up
without speaking.

"You've left dust."

"No, ma'am."

"Are you contradicting me?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then you agree you've left dust."

After that came the house with six children, then the
hotel kitchen where the steam soaked her hair all day,
then marriage. Mr Parker had been a baker's man. He
smelled of flour and horses, and on Saturdays he brought
home a bun that had got crushed and could not
be sold.

"A treat for the missus."

He was not much of a talker. He took off
his boots by the door, ate what there was, and
slept with one arm across his eyes. But he never
struck her, not once, and when the first baby came
he stood outside the room whispering, "Is she all right?"
till the woman sent him away.

The first baby died before he had a tooth.

The second lived to be six.

The third and fourth went in the same winter. Measles,
they said, though there was cold in the room and
no money for coal. The fifth was born blue. The
sixth, little Annie, was taken with fever. The seventh lasted
three weeks.

Seven.

People had always said, "You've had a hard life, Mrs
Parker."

She had answered, "You don't want to take no notice
of that."

What was the good? Things happened. You folded the little
clothes, emptied the basin, went out for bread. If the
rent was due Friday, grief didn't make it Saturday. If
a child was sick, the others still wanted washing. There
was never time to sit down and begin.

The three who grew up were Jim, Alice and Maud.

Jim went to sea and sent two postcards, both showing
foreign harbours. Then nothing. Alice married a man with a
red neck who disliked relations. She lived only across the
river, but it might have been India. Maud came home
with Lennie, left him in Mrs Parker's bed and said
she would fetch his things. She fetched herself instead, one
evening two years later, smelling of scent and rain.

"You're better with Gran, my duck," she told him.

Then she was off again.

So Lennie belonged to Mrs Parker. Not legally, perhaps. But
in all the ways that mattered: his socks behind the
stove, his mug with the cracked blue soldier, the warm
dent of his head on the pillow. He followed her
from room to room, dragging a wooden horse.

"Where you going, Gran?"

"To the moon."

"Can I come?"

"No room."

"I'll sit on yer lap."

When she went cleaning he watched from the window until
she turned the corner. Coming home, she saw his pale
face squeezed between the curtains. Even in summer he was
pale. His arms were thin, but his head was round
and covered with soft fair hair.

"My little king," she called him.

"I ain't a king."

"What are you, then?"

"Gran's boy."

In the gentleman's bedroom, Mrs Parker lifted the silk sock
from the lamp and laid it across the chair. Her
hands had begun to shake. She pressed them into the
blanket.

Lennie's illness had started with a whistle in his breathing.
The doctor called it bronchitis and wrote something on a
paper. The medicine cost one and nine. It smelled sweet,
and Lennie turned his face away.

"Just a little taste for Gran."

"No."

"One spoon."

"Burns."

She held his nose and tipped it in, then kissed
the wet corner of his mouth. At night his chest
bubbled and knocked like potatoes jumping under a saucepan lid.
She sat with him upright against her, wrapped in the
old shawl.

"Gran?"

"Yes, my lovey?"

"Open the window."

"It's too cold."

"Want the sky."

She pulled the curtain back so he could see a
square of darkness above the roofs. There was one star.
He watched it until his eyes closed.

For six nights she did not undress. She boiled water,
warmed bricks, rubbed his chest, changed the damp shirt under
his arms. The neighbours came in and said, "Poor mite,"
and "He's always been delicate," and "You ought to get
some sleep." As if sleep were in a cupboard and
she had only forgotten to take it out.

On the last morning his breathing stopped making a noise.

That was the worst. The silence.

Mrs Parker bent over him and said, "Lennie?"

His mouth was open a little.

"Lennie, Gran's arsking you."

She shook him carefully, because he was ill.

Then the woman from downstairs came and took Mrs Parker
by the shoulders. Someone closed the window. Someone covered the
cracked blue mug with a saucer, though there was nothing
in it.

Mrs Parker did not cry.

At the funeral she had stood while the minister spoke.
The wind went under her coat. Maud arrived late in
a hat with a green feather and cried into a
clean handkerchief. Alice sent a wreath with FAMILY printed on
a card. No one knew where Jim was. Men lowered
the little box, ropes rubbing softly against wood, and Mrs
Parker thought: He doesn't like the dark.

Still she did not cry.

The gentleman's sitting room was no cleaner for being thought
about. She went back to it. She dusted the mantelpiece,
straightened the papers, emptied the ashtrays. Under a newspaper she
found half a crown.

She held it on her palm.

Half a crown would pay for bread, tea, a bit
of dripping, and perhaps sixpence toward what she owed the
undertaker. She could put it in her pocket and say
she had never seen it. A coin under a newspaper
was as good as lost in this place.

She set it in the centre of the table.

"Mrs Parker," said the gentleman, coming in, "have you seen
a half-crown anywhere?"

"There, sir."

"Ah." He picked it up. "I had an idea I
left it about."

He smiled at her, pleased with his memory and with
the world's general honesty.

"You're not looking quite yourself," he added.

"No, sir."

"You should take things easily for a day or two."

"Yes, sir."

"But if you could finish the study before you go.
There's rather a lot of dust behind the books."

She cleaned behind the books.

At twelve she put on her boots. The swollen heels
would hardly go in. She pushed, tied the laces, and
stood slowly. In the kitchen she folded her apron and
placed it in the black bag.

The gentleman came out with money between two fingers.

"Your usual morning," he said. "And do try not to
brood. It never helps."

He gave her the coins.

"Thank you, sir."

Outside, the cold caught her face. She walked without deciding
where. Home was to the left, past the baker's and
the pawnbroker's, but she went right.

She could not go home yet. At home there was
Lennie's bed, Lennie's mug, Lennie's horse lying on its side.
The downstairs woman would hear her. The walls were thin.
If she began, Mrs Sims would come knocking.

"You all right up there?"

Then there would be tea, questions, a hand on her
arm. No, she couldn't cry there.

She passed shops where women bent over trays of meat
and vegetables. A boy cycled by with loaves in a
basket. Two men in long coats crossed the road, their
thin legs opening and shutting like scissors. Women stepped around
puddles with quick careful feet, like cats testing a wet
yard.

Everybody was going somewhere.

Mrs Parker's bag knocked against her knee. Her boots pinched.
She thought she might sit in the square, but the
benches were full: nurses with perambulators, old men reading papers,
girls eating buns from paper bags. A policeman stood at
the gate.

Suppose she sat down and cried? They would look. One
of the nurses would ask if she was ill. The
policeman might tell her to move along. Crying in a
square was like being drunk; it made you public property.

She walked on.

There was the church. Churches were meant for sorrow, weren't
they? But the doors were locked except on Sundays. She
tried one, then another. A notice said the roof fund
urgently required assistance.

She went down a narrow street of warehouses. Surely there
would be a corner here, an empty yard, a stairway.
But every doorway had a man in it, smoking or
lifting boxes. Every window had a face behind it. A
cart clattered past, splashing her skirt.

All her life she had kept herself to herself.

When the babies died, she had kept herself to herself.
When Mr Parker came home coughing flour and blood and
had to give up the bakery, she washed offices at
dawn and cared for him by night. When he died,
she laid out his suit, polished his boots, and went
the next morning to clean a doctor's house because it
was Tuesday.

When Jim stopped writing, she said the sea was a
busy place. When Alice turned away from her in the
market, she pretended not to notice. When Maud left Lennie,
she thanked God she had one little warm body to
care for.

A hard life?

Yes, well. Plenty had harder.

But Lennie—Lennie had been the soft place. The one warm
place. And now something inside her had risen so high
that it pressed against her throat and eyes. She must
let it out. She must put down her bag, bend
her head, and cry until there was nothing left in
her, not one breath, not one memory.

But where?

She turned into another street. The sky hung low over
the chimneys, grey-brown like an old cloth stained with tea.
A spot of rain struck her cheek. Then another dotted
the sleeve of her coat.

There must be some little place. A shed. An empty
room. A passage nobody used. Even a cellar would do.

She saw a railway arch and hurried toward it, but
two boys were playing there, kicking a tin. Beyond the
arch, a woman sold flowers under an awning. Farther on
stood a public house with bright windows and men at
the door.

Not there.

The rain thickened. It tapped on roofs, darkened the pavement,
ran into the gutter carrying matches, cabbage leaves and a
strip of red paper. Mrs Parker's coat grew heavy. Water
slid beneath her collar.

She stopped under the bare branches of a tree.

No shelter. No quiet. Houses stood shoulder to shoulder, each
window belonging to someone. Behind every wall, ears. On every
pavement, eyes. Even the rain had the whole sky to
fall through, while she had not the width of a
chair where she might bury her face.

Her mouth trembled. She gripped the handle of the black
bag with both hands.

"Lennie," she whispered.

A woman passing turned to look at her.

Mrs Parker lowered her head and moved on.

There was no private corner in all that city for
a woman like her.

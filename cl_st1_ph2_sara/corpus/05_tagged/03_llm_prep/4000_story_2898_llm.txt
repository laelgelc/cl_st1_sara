## The Great Morass-Delivering Engine

I was seventeen years old when my uncle Phineas took
me and old Yorpy ten miles up the Mantucket River
to Quash Island, there to witness the triumph—or burial—of a
machine upon which he had spent ten years, eleven months,
and, as he afterward computed, seven days of his mortal
pilgrimage.

My uncle was then sixty-eight. He was spare, tall, and
somewhat bent, not so much by age as by perpetual
stooping over wheels, valves, pipes, and philosophical difficulties. His hair
stood outward in a silver mist, as if every thought
that entered his brain immediately electrified it. He had been,
by turns, surveyor, schoolmaster, clock-mender, lecturer upon pneumatics, and unsuccessful
cultivator of a melon alleged by him to possess the
nutritious properties of beef. But the Great Hydraulic-Hydrostatic Morass-Delivering Engine
was the child of his age, the Moses of his
decline, and the destined rescuer of all drowned lands from
Maine to the Pontine marshes.

The apparatus itself lay in a vast dry-goods box, secured
by nails enough for a fortress and covered by sailcloth,
tarpaulin, and an old carpet upon which the Judgment of
Solomon had once been depicted in red worsted. Yorpy and
I had carried this mysterious ark from the shed to
the boat before sunrise.

"Carefully!" cried my uncle, though neither of us had yet
touched it. "Remember that within those vulgar boards sleeps an
industrial revolution."

"Sleep mighty heavy," muttered Yorpy. "Revolution ought to carry itself."

"Silence!" said my uncle. "Posterity may be listening."

Yorpy looked across the vacant fields.

"Posterity ain't up yet."

This answer offended my uncle, and he threatened to leave
Yorpy behind; but since Yorpy alone could lift one end
of the box without rupturing either himself or the box,
the sentence was not executed.

We embarked in my uncle's long, leaky skiff. The engine
occupied the middle; Yorpy pulled the forward oars; I pulled
the after pair; and my uncle, enthroned upon a keg
of nails at the stern, steered us northward with the
air of Caesar conducting an expedition against Britain.

It was a misty April morning. The river went between
low, black banks tufted with reeds; beyond them spread wide
marshes, pale with standing water and pierced here and there
by the skeleton of a drowned tree. To any common
eye the scene appeared dreary. To my uncle it was
a continent of submerged money.

"Behold," quoth he, pointing to the sedges, "the unappropriated wealth
of the republic! Millions of acres asleep beneath a few
inches of superfluous fluid! Millions of bushels of corn unborn!
Cities unbuilt! Churches unsteepled! Taxes uncollected! And why?"

"Because it's wet?" I suggested.

"Because," he replied severely, "mankind has awaited me."

The current ran briskly against us, and the box, being
wider than was convenient, compelled us to row with short,
crabbed strokes. Whenever the boat inclined a hair's breadth, my
uncle sprang upright.

"Tip! You are tipping!"

"We must tip somewhat," said I. "A boat that never
tips is generally ashore."

"A leetle to port, then. No—not so much! Carefully! Yorpy,
do you desire to drown the accumulated meditations of ten
years?"

"No," said Yorpy, "but I wouldn't mind drowning that box."

My uncle declared he would put him ashore. Yorpy observed
that the nearest shore was a bog, and requested to
be put upon a dry portion of it. My uncle,
unable to discover one, contented himself with glaring.

Presently a black speck appeared upon a distant hill.

"Crouch!" whispered my uncle.

We continued rowing.

"Crouch, I say! There is a spy."

The black speck was a cow.

"Undoubtedly hired," said my uncle, when I represented this circumstance.
"The agents of the Dutch drainage companies employ disguises which
would astonish you. Yesterday a man loitered near my gate
pretending to sell grindstones."

"He sells grindstones every spring," said I.

"That proves the completeness of his preparations."

We crouched, therefore, while Yorpy rowed; and the boat, deprived
of my exertions, swung broadside to the stream and nearly
lodged upon a stump. My uncle leaped from the nail
keg.

"Tip! Tip! A leetle more! Now back! Carefully!"

After much agitation we escaped both the stump and the
cow, and by ten o'clock reached Quash Island, a deep-wooded
patch of high ground encircled by shallow water and evil-smelling
mud. No house stood upon it. No road approached it.
Its only inhabitants were turtles, mosquitoes, and a family of
herons, whose secrecy my uncle considered satisfactory.

We landed at a small cove. The box was dragged
through bushes to a level place where the island descended
into the marsh. My uncle paced out distances, consulted a
brass compass, held a wet finger to the wind, and
finally selected the identical spot first proposed by Yorpy.

"Here," said he, "the experiment shall be made. If successful—and
who, acquainted with the principles involved, can entertain a rational
doubt?—this hour will divide history. There was an age before
steam; there shall be an age after Phineas."

"Will there be dinner in it?" asked Yorpy.

My uncle again commanded silence.

We pried up the lid. At first I could distinguish
nothing but a prodigious entanglement of copper tubes, leather hoses,
iron rods, glass globes, paddles, screws, springs, chains, bellows, and
wheels within wheels. The whole resembled a huge nest of
anacondas and adders, surprised in the act of swallowing a
brass chandelier. Near the centre stood a square wooden chamber
painted green, upon which were written in gilt letters the
words:

**PHINEAS B. DORR'S UNIVERSAL HYDRAULIC-HYDROSTATIC
MORASS-DELIVERING, FEVER-ABATING, AGRICULTURE-EXTENDING APPARATUS.
PATENT CONTEMPLATED.**

"Uncle," said I, "what does it do?"

"What does it do!" he cried. "Young man, it takes
hold of stagnant nature and bids her circulate."

"But how?"

"By the combined action of suction, pressure, gravitation, atmospheric persuasion,
and a principle which I shall not disclose until my
rights are secured."

"Does the water go into it?"

"It goes where I direct."

"And where is that?"

"Elsewhere."

I confessed that this was desirable, and thereby restored his
good humor.

For two hours we labored. We planted stakes, joined pipes,
fastened hoses, unfolded a canvas reservoir, and carried one long
copper snout into the marsh. Another pipe, ending in what
looked like a watering-pot, was turned toward the river. My
uncle consulted a manuscript covered with diagrams and spots of
candle grease. Every joint required adjustment; every adjustment required silence;
and every silence was broken by my uncle's cry of
"Carefully!"

At noon the machine stood complete.

It was not beautiful. It leaned. One wheel was larger
than its fellow, the bellows hung like the lungs of
a slain giant, and the principal pipe turned three unnecessary
corners before entering the water. Yet its very complication imposed
upon me. I began to believe that so many parts
could not possibly agree to accomplish nothing.

My uncle removed his hat.

"Before invoking the forces," he said, "we will acknowledge the
Source of force."

His voice, hitherto inflated like the bellows, became low and
tremulous. He prayed that his long labor might be made
useful; that pestilential swamps might become wholesome farms; that riches,
if granted, might not corrupt him; and that fame, if
attached to his name, might redound beyond it. Then, after
a pause, he added, "Yet not my will."

Those words affected me strangely. For the first time I
understood that beneath all his pomp, suspicion, and ridiculous titles
lay a high-hearted, persevering endeavor. Ten years of lonely hope
stood beside that shabby contrivance. I ceased smiling.

My uncle replaced his hat.

"To your stations! Yorpy, at the crank. Nephew, watch the
indicator. When the needle reaches the scarlet mark, raise your
hand. Not before. Not after. Precision is the soul of
science."

Yorpy seized the crank.

"Turn!"

The machine groaned.

"Faster!"

It clattered.

"Steady!"

It shook from its iron feet to the watering-pot extremity.
The bellows opened and shut; the glass globes clouded; chains
danced; wheels spun; somewhere within the green chamber a hammer
beat furiously against wood.

"Needle?" cried my uncle.

"It does not move."

"Impossible. Tap the dial."

I tapped it.

"Again!"

I tapped harder. The glass cracked.

"You have destroyed the indicator!"

"The needle moved," I said. "It fell off."

"Never mind it! Yorpy, faster!"

Yorpy turned until the sweat shone upon him. The machine
squealed, coughed, rattled, and emitted from a hidden aperture one
small gray feather, although no feather had knowingly entered into
its construction.

We watched the marsh.

Nothing happened.

My uncle ran to the copper snout and thrust his
hand into the water. He ran back, opened a valve,
closed another, tightened a screw, loosened the same screw, and
commanded Yorpy to reverse the crank.

The machine's groaning altered pitch.

Still nothing happened.

"A leetle more!" cried my uncle. "There! Hold! Forward again!
Carefully!"

For twenty minutes we obeyed. A frog seated three yards
from the suction pipe remained wholly unconcerned. A floating leaf,
which ought at least to have trembled before so considerable
an application of philosophy, drifted past it with sphinx-like blankness.

"There is an obstruction," my uncle said.

He removed the snout. Mud had clogged it. He cleaned
it with his handkerchief, replaced it, and ordered another trial.

This time the watering-pot end dripped once.

My uncle saw it.

"Aha!"

We crowded around. A second drop gathered, swelled, and fell.

"Behold," said he, "the commencement of drainage!"

We waited.

There was no third drop.

Yorpy sat down.

"Get up!" cried my uncle. "Rome was not drained in
a day."

"Rome wasn't drained with this," said Yorpy.

My uncle's face darkened. He rushed upon the engine, cranked
it himself, blew into a pipe, sucked at another, burned
his lips upon a brass fitting, and accused the atmosphere
of insufficient pressure. The machine gave a convulsive leap. One
hose sprang loose and struck him across the hat. A
glass globe burst. The green chamber emitted a sound like
a sheep sneezing in a barrel.

Then all was still.

The marsh glittered peacefully in the sun.

My uncle stood with both hands hanging at his sides.
His face, usually red and resolute, seemed to wither before
us into the likeness of a mildewed grape. He looked
at the reeds, the pipes, the cracked indicator, and the
indifferent frog.

Ten years had asked nature a question. Nature had not
troubled to answer.

Suddenly he kicked the green chamber.

"Fraud!"

He kicked it again.

"Idiot! Impostor! Thief of years!"

With a wrench he tore away the watering-pot. He stamped
upon the glass globes. He seized the copper tubes and
dragged them forth in a glittering coil, as Samson might
have disemboweled some mechanical lion. Yorpy caught his arm, but
my uncle shook him off with unexpected strength.

"Let me alone! I made it—I may unmake it! Down
with the Great Hydraulic Humbug! Down with Phineas, Emperor of
Mud!"

He battered the wheels with a stake until spokes flew.
He ripped the canvas reservoir. Springs sprang into the bushes;
screws vanished into the grass; the bellows, released from restraint,
gave one melancholy sigh and collapsed.

At last he stood among the wreckage, breathing hard.

I had laughed at him a hundred times; now I
wept. Yorpy turned away and pretended to examine the river.

My uncle sat upon the broken box.

"No dinner," he said faintly. "We go home."

We began collecting such pieces as might be useful or
dangerous if left behind. Presently I saw my uncle lift
his head. His eye rested upon a small brass cylinder,
almost uninjured, attached to half a valve and a length
of hose. Hope, which had been cut down in him
like a stubborn stump, put forth one final green sprout.

"Wait," he whispered.

He snatched up the cylinder.

"The secondary evacuator! The defect may have lain in the
distributive chamber. This part is independent. Boy, bring that bucket.
Yorpy, set the little frame upright."

Neither of us opposed him.

Within ten minutes he had constructed from the ruins a
lesser apparatus: a pump-like device with the brass cylinder for
its heart and the bucket for its universe. He filled
the bucket from the marsh, inserted the hose, and looked
at us with terrible eagerness.

"If this acts," he said, "the principle survives."

He worked the handle.

A bubble rose in the bucket.

He worked again. A second bubble appeared, whether from the
machine or from air already trapped in the hose I
cannot say. Then the handle came off in his hand.

The water remained.

My uncle swayed. Yorpy caught him before he fell.

For a long time the three of us made no
sound. The herons returned to their trees. Evening had begun
to gather in the eastern woods, and the river, reddened
by the lowering sun, hurried past the island as though
ashamed to have delayed.

We put my uncle in the boat. Yorpy packed around
him the carpet, the tools, and those remnants of the
apparatus that might serve ordinary life: boards, wire, bolts, two
sound wheels, and the wounded bellows. Then we pushed into
the current.

How different was our return! In the morning we had
fought every mile, striking upward against the stream as ambition
strikes against the universal current that bears men toward obscurity.
Now the river received us without effort. The island withdrew;
the marshes glided past; our oars rested across the gunwales.

My uncle sat silent beneath the Judgment of Solomon.

After perhaps an hour he said, "Boy."

"Yes, uncle."

"You may laugh."

"I do not wish to."

"You wished to this morning."

"This morning I knew less."

He looked at me, and something gentle entered his ruined
countenance.

"So did I."

We drifted on.

At length he said, "Never invent anything."

"I promise."

"No—that is foolish advice. The plow was invented, and the
fiddle, and hot cakes. Invent if you must. But invent
happiness first. If a man cannot contrive that from the
materials at hand, no wheel, valve, kingdom, or patent will
contrive it for him."

Yorpy, who sat forward, cleared his throat.

"We could make a chicken-house out of the box," he
said. "Them wheels might do for a handcart."

"And the bellows?" asked my uncle.

"Smith over at Miller's Crossing needs bellows."

"The copper?"

"Patch the roof."

My uncle considered these humiliating destinies. Then he smiled.

"Excellent. My universal machine shall achieve particular usefulness."

He laid a hand on Yorpy's shoulder.

"You turned faithfully."

"I turned because you told me."

"You remained when I abused you."

"I remained because somebody had to fetch you home."

"That," said my uncle, "is perhaps the best definition of
loyalty I have heard."

He apologized to Yorpy—not in general terms, nor with any
saving reservation concerning atmospheric pressure, but plainly and by name.
Yorpy stared ahead, greatly embarrassed, and said the matter was
nothing. Yet when darkness fell, I saw him draw the
carpet more closely around the old man's knees.

Near our landing the moon rose over the marsh. My
uncle watched its image tremble in the black water.

"I asked for fame," he said, "and received exposure. I
asked for wealth, and have saved the price of a
chicken-house. I asked to master the swamp, and the swamp
has drained me."

He laughed softly.

"Praise be to God for the failure."

In the years that followed, those words became his household
refrain. The box did become a chicken-house; the wheels served
a cart; the copper kept rain from the pantry; and
the bellows awakened many a respectable forge fire. My uncle
invented no more engines. He repaired clocks for widows who
could not pay, taught Yorpy's grandson his letters, and listened
with patience to opinions which formerly would have been driven
from his door by classical allusion and personal insult.

He lived eight peaceful years after the expedition to Quash
Island. If disappointed ambition visited him, he gave it no
chair. Sometimes, when summer light lay upon the Mantucket and
the distant marshes shone like sheets of beaten brass, he
would raise one finger and say, "There lies my fortune."

Then, seeing alarm or pity in our faces, he would
add, "Safely buried."

On the last evening of his life I sat beside
him. His hair still rose in its old silver mist,
though the head beneath it was quiet. He asked whether
I had invented anything.

"Nothing of consequence," I told him.

"Happiness?"

"A little."

"Improve it," he whispered. "The patent is free."

Before dawn he died.

I was young when I went upriver with an uncle
whom I loved and sometimes mocked; I returned with a
teacher. His machine failed to move one cupful of marsh
water, yet it accomplished what ten prosperous inventions might not
have done: it drained pride from a human heart and
left the good earth visible beneath.

Others learn humility by long injury, loss, and age. Example
did for me the work of experience. And whenever some
cherished contrivance of my own—some plan, hope, argument, or grand
apparatus of the soul—clatters magnificently and produces nothing, I remember
the quiet boat, the moon upon the current, and my
uncle's chastened voice:

"Praise be to God for the failure!"

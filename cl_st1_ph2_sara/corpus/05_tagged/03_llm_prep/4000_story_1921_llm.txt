## A Guest at Wragg's

Mr. Wragg's cottage stood halfway up the steep road from
Bellwick Harbour, where it could obtain smoke from the chimneys
above, tar from the boats below, and every piece of
news before it had travelled fifty yards in either direction.
It was a narrow house, with a brass knocker too
large for the door and two front windows which Mr.
Wragg cleaned every Saturday and looked through during the remainder
of the week.

He was looking through one of them when George Gale
came up from the harbour carrying one end of a
sea-chest. Ned Harris carried the other, and Sam Brown walked
behind offering advice.

"Lift your end, George," said Harris.

"I am lifting it."

"Then lift mine."

Mr. Wragg opened the window.

"Take that thing off my pavement," he said.

George stopped. He was a broad young fisherman with cheerful
blue eyes and the settled belief that age had been
created to furnish youth with amusement.

"What thing?" he asked.

"That great ugly box."

Harris lowered his end thoughtfully. George, taken by surprise, retained
his; and the chest swung round and struck Mr. Wragg's
wall with a report which brought two women to neighboring
windows.

"Mind my 'ouse!" shouted Mr. Wragg.

"Your house moved," said George. "I saw it."

"It moved when you hit it."

"Then it ought to be more careful."

Mr. Wragg, who had spent sixty years sharpening a natural
gift for unpleasant reply, looked from George to the chest.

"Whose is it?" he demanded.

"Sam's uncle's."

"Then put Sam's uncle inside and throw 'em both in
the harbour."

Brown laughed so heartily that he walked backward into the
chest. George and Harris, inspired by his example, lifted it
suddenly, and one corner caught Mr. Wragg, who had emerged
to defend his property, with sufficient firmness to seat him
on his own doorstep.

The young men apologized at once. Harris took off his
cap, Brown brushed Mr. Wragg's sleeve, and George inquired whether
he had hurt the step.

"You wait," said Mr. Wragg, rising. "You wait, George Gale.
I'll get even with you one o' these days."

George looked respectfully up and down the road.

"Where shall I wait?" he asked.

Mr. Wragg went indoors and closed the door with such
vigor that the brass knocker struck twice. The young men
continued uphill, and by evening the story had reached the
King's Arms in three forms, all of them unfavorable to
Mr. Wragg's dignity.

For a week he saw no opportunity of repaying George.
At the end of that time a cab stopped outside
his cottage, and the driver lowered a brown trunk which,
by a regrettable coincidence, struck the same doorstep.

A young woman descended after it.

She was Mr. Wragg's niece, Miss Lucy Miller, who had
come from London to keep house for him. Before she
had been in Bellwick two hours the harbor knew her
name, the color of her eyes, the probable value of
her hat, and the fact that she had refused three
offers of assistance between the station and the cottage. Before
she had been there two days, the young men of
Bellwick had developed an interest in Mr. Wragg which he
found both sudden and suspicious.

George Gale developed the strongest interest of all.

He first saw Miss Miller on the following morning, when
she stood in the doorway shaking a tablecloth. The wind
carried one corner over her head and displayed a small,
laughing face beneath it. George, who had weathered gales in
the North Sea without losing either his balance or his
appetite, walked into a fish basket.

Miss Miller withdrew indoors. Mr. Wragg appeared in her place.

"You done that basket any good?" he asked.

"I was looking at the weather," said George.

"Ah. Weather generally comes from the sky, but perhaps it's
altered."

George kicked the basket aside and went down to his
boat. At dinner he ate only two herrings instead of
four, a circumstance which caused his mother to feel his
forehead and his father to remark that no good came
of sitting up late at public houses.

During the next few days he passed Mr. Wragg's cottage
so often that even the road seemed surprised. Sometimes Miss
Miller was at the window; sometimes Mr. Wragg was. The
difference became plain before George reached the gate. Miss Miller
smiled. Mr. Wragg picked up things.

One evening George paused to admire a geranium in the
front garden.

"Pretty flower," he said, as Mr. Wragg came out.

"Which one?"

"That red one."

"It's a brick."

"I meant the one behind it."

"That's a broken pot."

George considered the garden more carefully.

"You don't want any help with it, do you?" he
asked.

"Yes," said Mr. Wragg. "I want somebody to stand outside
and keep fools away."

George leaned on the gate.

"Your niece settling down all right?"

"She was till now."

"London must be very different from Bellwick."

"It is. There's more room there for you to keep
away from 'er."

George straightened himself.

"I'm only being neighborly."

"Then go and be neighborly to somebody else."

He slammed the gate. The latch caught George's fingers, and
Miss Miller, appearing at the window, laughed. George walked down
the road with a face of great composure until he
reached the corner.

That night at the King's Arms he placed the matter
before Harris and Brown. He did not say that he
wanted to court Miss Miller. He said merely that a
friendly call was one of the ordinary duties of civilized
life, and that Mr. Wragg, by preventing it, was placing
himself outside society.

"Knock at the door," said Brown.

"He'd shut it."

"Put your foot in."

"He'd break it."

Harris, who had been listening with his head on one
side, looked toward the yard, where his bicycle leaned against
the wall.

"Suppose you was took ill outside his 'ouse?" he said.

"Wragg would send for the undertaker."

"Suppose you was hurt?"

"He'd finish it."

"Not with Miss Miller there," said Harris. "Women won't let
injured people lie in the road. It's against their nature."

George looked doubtful.

"What sort of injury?"

"Bicycle accident," said Harris. "I lend you mine. You ride
down the hill, fall off at Wragg's gate, and me
and Sam carry you in."

"I can't ride."

"So much the better. It'll look natural."

Brown put down his mug. "There's a doctor coming to
see old Mrs. Pike at seven to-morrow. We fetch him.
He says George mustn't be moved. George stays in the
'ouse, Miss Miller nurses him, and old Wragg boils."

George's eyes brightened.

"How long do I stay?"

"That," said Harris, "depends on how badly you fall."

They arranged the details with great care. George was to
coast down the hill shortly before seven, apply the brake
opposite Mr. Wragg's gate, and lie still. Harris and Brown
were to be close behind. They spent half an hour
deciding which leg should be injured, and another quarter deciding
that the head offered greater freedom.

"You can't forget and walk on your head," Brown explained.

The following evening was clear and calm. At six fifty-five
Mr. Wragg sat in his parlor reading the newspaper, while
Miss Miller sewed by the window. The road outside was
unusually well attended. Harris examined a wall fifty yards uphill;
Brown waited below, studying the harbor; and five boys lingered
opposite with the air of persons expecting history.

At six fifty-eight George appeared at the top of the
road on Harris's bicycle.

He had intended to coast slowly. The road intended otherwise.
He came down with his knees wide apart, his elbows
trembling, and his eyes fixed upon Mr. Wragg's gate. Harris
shouted to him to use the brake. George replied that
he was using everything he could find.

The bicycle gathered speed. Mr. Wragg, attracted by the shouting,
reached the gate just as George reached it from the
opposite direction. George turned the handle sharply. The bicycle struck
the curb, rose with unexpected intelligence, and deposited him among
the geraniums.

There was a short silence.

"Beautiful," said Brown, hurrying up. "Couldn't have done it better."

George, whose shoulder had encountered the broken pot, opened one
eye.

"Get me inside," he whispered.

Harris knelt beside him.

"Don't speak, old man," he said loudly. "Save your strength."

Mr. Wragg pushed through the gate. "Save his strength for
what?"

"He's badly hurt," said Brown.

"He's ruined my garden."

Miss Miller came running out. She gave one glance at
George's pale and solemn face, and another at the twisted
bicycle.

"Oh, uncle, we must take him in."

"No," said Mr. Wragg.

"He may be dying."

"Then he can die where he fell. He chose the
place."

George closed both eyes and gave a faint groan which
surprised even himself.

Miss Miller bent over him. "Can you hear me, Mr.
Gale?"

He opened his eyes just far enough to see her.

"Mother," he murmured.

"His mother's six foot tall and weighs fourteen stone," said
Mr. Wragg. "He knows better."

"Uncle!"

Harris and Brown lifted George. Their method of carrying an
injured man was to seize whichever part came nearest. George's
head struck the gatepost, and his left boot caught Mr.
Wragg in the stomach.

"He's delirious," said Harris, when Mr. Wragg complained.

"I'll make 'im worse."

They bore George into the cottage. Mr. Wragg attempted to
direct them toward the kitchen settle, but Miss Miller, observing
that it was narrow, led the way upstairs to the
best bedroom. In another minute George was placed in Mr.
Wragg's own bed, his boots resting affectionately upon the white
counterpane.

"My bed!" gasped Mr. Wragg.

"It's the nearest," said Miss Miller.

"It's the furthest. The settle's downstairs."

"Don't excite him," said Brown.

"Excite 'im?"

"He may have internal injuries."

"I'll give 'im some external ones."

The arrival of Dr. Price interrupted this promise. Harris fetched
him from Mrs. Pike's, and the doctor entered with the
brisk resignation of a man accustomed to finding accidents wherever
he was least wanted.

He examined George's head, felt his pulse, moved his arms,
and asked him several questions. George answered feebly, selecting at
random from ailments he had heard discussed by elderly relatives.

"Pain here?" asked the doctor, pressing his ribs.

"Terrible."

The doctor pressed on the other side.

"Here?"

"Worse."

"Any dizziness?"

"Everything goes round."

Mr. Wragg, standing at the foot of the bed, looked
at the ceiling as though hoping it might fall.

The doctor straightened.

"No bones broken," he said. "Severe shock, possibly concussion. He
must remain perfectly quiet."

"Where?" asked Mr. Wragg.

"Here."

"Here?"

"He must not be moved to-night. Keep the room quiet
and cool. Nothing to eat."

George's expression changed slightly.

"Nothing?" said Miss Miller.

"Water only. If he becomes restless or talks wildly, don't
be alarmed. It may be delirium."

Mr. Wragg's face became thoughtful.

"When can 'e be moved?" he asked.

"I'll call in the morning."

The doctor left. Harris and Brown followed after shaking George's
hand with funereal tenderness.

"Good-bye, old chap," said Brown.

"Bear up," said Harris.

George watched them go with a growing suspicion that they
were enjoying themselves. Miss Miller arranged the pillows beneath his
head. Her fingers were cool, her voice gentle, and George
decided that the evening had been well spent.

Mr. Wragg stood beside the bed.

"You comfortable?" he asked.

"Not very," said George, remembering his condition.

"I'll see what I can do."

He drew the blanket up to George's chin, tucked it
firmly beneath the mattress, and went out. Miss Miller followed
to fetch water.

Mr. Wragg returned alone carrying the bicycle lamp.

"So you fell off," he said.

George closed his eyes.

"Can't talk."

"Doctor said you would."

"Head's bad."

"Use another one."

George breathed heavily.

Mr. Wragg sat on a chair and regarded him with
patient malice.

"Funny thing," he said. "Harris's bicycle having a lamp when
Harris told Bill Thompson at six o'clock that 'e'd lent
it to you."

George's breathing became less regular.

"Funny thing, too, Brown telling everybody at the King's Arms
there'd be an accident outside my place at seven."

George opened his eyes.

"Did he?"

"He did. Delirious, ain't you?"

"Very."

"Good. I like a cheerful invalid."

Miss Miller came in with a jug and glass. Mr.
Wragg immediately assumed concern.

"Poor young feller," he said. "Don't know where 'e is."

George looked at Miss Miller.

"Where am I?" he asked weakly.

Mr. Wragg answered before she could.

"Buckingham Pallis."

Miss Miller bit her lip.

"Am I—am I the King?" George inquired.

"No," said Mr. Wragg. "You're the Queen's aunt. Drink your
water."

George drank. Miss Miller told him to sleep and left
the room. Mr. Wragg remained until his niece's steps had
died away; then he rose, took George's clothes from the
chair, and rolled them into a bundle.

"What are you doing?" demanded George.

"Taking care of your things. Delirious people eats their clothes
sometimes."

"Put 'em back."

Mr. Wragg smiled.

"You're in my bed, young George, and doctor says you
can't move. If you get up, you confess there's nothing
wrong with you. If you stay, you stay as I
choose. Pleasant dreams."

He went out with the clothes and locked the door.

George sat up. His head was sore from the genuine
portion of the accident, his shoulder ached, and the room
contained nothing edible. He examined the bedside table and found
a Bible, a button, and Mr. Wragg's false teeth in
a tumbler.

At nine o'clock Miss Miller brought more water.

"Could I have a little bread?" George asked.

"The doctor said nothing but water."

"Doctors say things in a general way."

"Uncle says we must obey him exactly."

"Your uncle," said George, "is enjoying this."

Miss Miller's eyes widened.

"Enjoying your dangerous illness?"

"He knows."

"Knows what?"

George looked at her closely. Her face was innocent, though
one corner of her mouth appeared to require discipline.

"That the accident wasn't altogether an accident."

She set down the jug.

"Oh! Then you came off a-purpose?"

"The wheel turned."

"Wheels often do."

"I meant to fall," admitted George, "but not quite so
thoroughly."

"And all to get into uncle's house?"

George became modest.

"I wanted to see somebody."

"Uncle?"

"No."

"Me?"

George looked at the blanket.

Miss Miller blushed very prettily. "I think it was a
foolish, deceitful trick."

"So do I now."

"And you deserve to be punished."

"I'm being punished."

"You have a comfortable bed."

"I've had six glasses of water."

"Water is good for you."

"Not in those quantities. I can hear the tide turning
inside me."

She laughed, took the candle, and wished him good night.
George called after her, but the key turned in the
lock.

At half past nine Mr. Wragg's voice sounded below.

"He's asleep now, Mrs. Green. Poor chap. Doctor says 'e's
wandering in 'is mind."

Mrs. Green's voice answered from the passage.

"Fancy! And such a fine young man."

"Ah, there's often very little inside the biggest parcels."

A second neighbor arrived, then a third. Mr. Wragg gave
each a report in the doorway, raising his voice so
that no symptom should be lost upstairs.

"Thinks 'e's at Buckingham Pallis," he said. "Calls me the
Dook of Wellington. Tried to eat the soap. Doctor says
it's a most peculiar case."

George climbed out of bed and crossed the room. The
door was locked. He tried the window and found that
it opened above the front gate, where seven neighbors were
collected.

A small boy saw him.

"There 'e is!" he shouted.

Every face turned upward. George retired so quickly that he
struck his shin against a chair.

"Get back to bed!" called Mr. Wragg. "You ain't got
no wings."

Laughter rose from the road. George returned to bed and
pulled the blanket over his head.

In the morning Dr. Price called early. Mr. Wragg admitted
him with grave satisfaction and accompanied him upstairs.

The doctor found George's pulse excellent and his temper less
so.

"How do you feel?"

"Hungry."

"A good sign. Any dizziness?"

"Only when people talk to me."

The doctor smiled. "You may get up this afternoon if
you feel steady. Light food first. An egg, perhaps, or
a little broth."

"He shall have water," said Mr. Wragg. "It agreed with
'im beautiful."

"Something light," repeated the doctor. "And no excitement."

When he had gone, George held out his hand.

"My clothes."

"What clothes?" asked Mr. Wragg.

"The clothes I came in."

"You came in by fairy magic. Fairies don't wear clothes."

"I'll get out of this bed."

"Do. There's twelve people opposite waiting to see you."

George crossed to the window. The cluster outside included Harris,
Brown, Mrs. Green, two fishermen, five children, and the landlord
of the King's Arms. Harris waved.

"How's Buckingham Pallis?" he called.

George closed the window.

At breakfast Mr. Wragg sent up another jug of water.
At dinner, he carried a chair into the passage and
discussed his meal with Miss Miller immediately outside the locked
door.

"That chicken smells good," he said loudly.

"It's only cold beef," said Miss Miller.

"Same thing to a man with imagination. Pass the pickles."

There was a pause.

"Port wine would go well with this," he continued.

"We haven't any port."

"We might pretend. George is doing well on pretending."

A knock sounded from inside the bedroom.

"What is it?" called Miss Miller.

"Food."

"Coax 'im," said Mr. Wragg.

"What?"

"Coax 'im to eat his water."

"I don't want water!" shouted George.

"There," said Mr. Wragg. "Delirious again. Says 'e don't want
water. Fish refusing water is serious."

"I'm not a fish!"

"Doesn't know his own trade."

By mid-afternoon George's romance had assumed the character of a
military siege. He had eaten nothing since the previous day,
unless one counted half a peppermint found in the pocket
of Mr. Wragg's dressing gown. He had considered escaping in
the gown, but the crowd outside had increased after news
spread that he believed himself to be a member of
the Royal Family.

Harris and Brown called at three. Mr. Wragg admitted them.

"Can we see him?" asked Harris.

"If you don't mind being recognized as angels."

They entered the room with solemn faces. Brown placed two
fingers on George's wrist, and Harris stared closely into his
eyes.

"Poor fellow," said Brown.

"Get my clothes," said George.

"Listen to him," said Harris. "Always loved clothes."

"You pair of swine planned this."

"Memory's coming back."

"You go downstairs and make Wragg give me my things."

Brown shook his head. "We dursen't interfere with the doctor."

"The doctor says I can get up."

"Not without clothes," said Harris reasonably.

George reached for him. Harris avoided the hand and moved
to the foot of the bed.

"We'll tell everybody you're improving."

"I'll tell everybody you helped."

"Delirium," said Brown.

"Two black devils," said George.

Harris opened the door. "He's worse, Mr. Wragg!"

George flung the water glass. Harris ducked; it struck Brown
on the ear. His companions, observing that excitement might retard
his recovery, held him down and thumped his pillow until
he became comparatively quiet. They then left, promising to call
again.

That evening the door handle came off in George's hand.
He had twisted it in the hope of forcing the
lock; the square iron bar slid free, leaving him with
a brass knob and no immediate use for it. He
threw it on the bed and addressed Mr. Wragg through
the hole.

"You'll have to let me out now."

"Why?"

"Handle's broken."

"Then you can't get out."

"What if there's a fire?"

"I'll bring you some water. You've plenty of practice."

Miss Miller came up after dark. She waited until her
uncle went down the road to the King's Arms, then
unlocked the door.

George stood before her wrapped in the bed quilt, Mr.
Wragg's dressing gown being insufficient in several important directions.

"I brought you some beef," she said.

He seized the plate and ate with a concentration which
allowed no room for courtship. Miss Miller watched him cut
slices of cold beef with the edge of a spoon.

"You might chew it," she suggested.

"I can chew later."

She had also brought bread, cheese, and a cup of
tea. George disposed of them and sat back with a
sigh.

"I was slowly fading," he said.

"You seemed very strong when you threw the glass at
Mr. Brown."

"That was the last flare of life."

She regarded him with amusement.

"You'd better go before uncle comes back."

"In this?"

"Your clothes are in the kitchen cupboard. I'll fetch them."

She returned with the bundle and turned her back while
he dressed. Mr. Wragg had knotted every garment separately and
then tied the knots together. George worked at them in
silence.

"There are people outside," Miss Miller said.

"How many?"

"Only nine."

"I can't go through the front."

"You could use the back."

"The back way comes out beside the King's Arms yard."

"That is convenient. Your friends are there."

George paused with one trouser leg in his hand.

"I shall leave Bellwick," he said bitterly.

"Because of a little joke?"

"Little? By to-morrow every soul in the place will call
me Queen Victoria."

"They might forget."

"They won't. Fishermen remember a joke longer than they remember
a debt."

Miss Miller sat on the window ledge.

"It does look rather foolish," she admitted.

George stared at her.

"Thank you."

"Coming into a man's house by pretending to be hurt
merely because you wanted to see a young woman."

"I know what I did."

"And being put to bed and fed on water."

"I know that too."

"And losing your clothes."

"Have you anything fresh?"

"No. I was only wondering whether there might be some
way of making it look less foolish."

George stopped working at the knots.

"What way?"

Miss Miller looked down. "If—if people thought you had some
good reason for wanting to see the young woman."

"They know I wanted to see you."

"Yes, but if—if it was understood that you were serious."

"I am serious."

"If—if you weren't just making yourself ridiculous for amusement."

"I wasn't amused."

"If—if there were some proper explanation afterward."

George gazed at her bent head. A blush had risen
to her cheeks. His own face changed slowly from despair
to astonished hope.

"You mean," he said, "if you and me—"

"If what?"

"If we were—"

"If we were what?"

"You know."

"I don't."

George swallowed. He had faced winter seas with more confidence
than he now faced Miss Miller's eyelashes.

"If I asked you to marry me," he said.

She looked up quickly.

"Mr. Gale!"

"You said—"

"I said nothing of the kind."

"You said if—if—"

"If what?"

"If people thought I had a reason."

"So you ask every girl you make a fool of
yourself over to marry you?"

"No. I never made a fool of myself over anybody
else."

"That is not very flattering."

"I mean—Lucy, you know what I mean. I've been trying
to speak to you ever since you came. I've walked
past this house till my boots are half worn out.
I've been thrown off a bicycle, starved, locked up, and
called the Queen's aunt. If that doesn't show I'm serious,
nothing will."

Miss Miller's expression softened.

"Do you truly want to marry me?"

"Yes."

"Even after uncle's treatment?"

"I'm not marrying him."

"He may come to live with us."

George hesitated for one fatal instant.

Miss Miller drew herself up. "I see."

"No, no. Let him come. We'll have a room ready."

"With a lock outside?"

"With two, if he likes. Lucy, will you?"

She looked toward the door. Steps sounded below, followed by
the scrape of Mr. Wragg's key in the front lock.

"Quick," whispered George. "Say yes."

"After knowing you one week?"

"It's been a long week."

"After such deceitful behavior?"

"I'll never have another bicycle accident."

"And while you're in uncle's bedroom, dressed in clothes stolen
from his cupboard?"

"They're my clothes!"

"You are getting excited," she said anxiously. "The doctor warned
us."

George stared at her.

"What?"

"You must lie down."

"Lie down?"

"Perhaps you're delirious again. You imagined I wanted you to
propose."

"You made me!"

"Made you?"

"You went on saying 'if—if—' till there was nothing else
a man could do."

Miss Miller moved toward the door.

"I'm afraid you are very ill."

George caught her hand.

"You little rogue," he said. "You knew."

"Knew what?"

"That I'd ask."

"Let go. Uncle's coming."

"Not till you answer."

"I have answered. I think you're mad."

Mr. Wragg's footsteps sounded on the stairs.

George lowered his voice. "Will you marry me?"

"No."

"Will you?"

"No."

"Lucy!"

Miss Miller tried to free her hand, laughing in spite
of herself. George drew her closer; she pushed him away;
and at that moment Mr. Wragg entered carrying a paper
parcel and an expression of suspicion.

He stopped.

George stood in the middle of the room with one
trouser leg properly arranged and the other tucked into his
boot. The quilt lay on the floor. Miss Miller was
flushed and laughing, and both her hands were held in
George's.

Mr. Wragg looked at the empty plate, the open door,
and the clothes.

"What's all this?" he demanded.

"He's delirious," said Miss Miller, snatching her hands away.

"Delirious?"

"He thinks I'm going to marry him."

Mr. Wragg turned slowly to George.

"Marry 'er?"

"She put it into my head," said George.

"I did not!"

"You said there was a way to stop people laughing."

"I said there might be."

"And then you said 'if' about twenty times."

"I was trying to be kind."

"Kind!" exclaimed George. "Coaxing a starving man into proposing and
then calling him mad!"

"Coax?" said Miss Miller. "Coax?"

"Yes, coax."

"I never coaxed you."

"You coaxed every word out of me."

Mr. Wragg looked from one to the other.

"Stop," he said. "I want to understand. You"—he pointed at
George—"pretended to break your neck to get into my 'ouse."

"Yes."

"You let yourself be put in my bed."

"Yes."

"You lived on water, lost your clothes, and had half
Bellwick laughing at you."

"Yes."

"And now you want to marry the girl that helped
do it?"

George looked at Miss Miller. She was trying to appear
offended, but the corners of her mouth betrayed her.

"Yes," he said.

Mr. Wragg turned to his niece.

"And you unlocked the door, fed 'im, gave 'im back
'is clothes, and then talked to 'im till 'e proposed?"

"I did not talk him into anything."

"You said 'if,'" George reminded her.

Miss Miller stamped her foot.

Mr. Wragg backed toward the door. His usual readiness of
speech had deserted him. He regarded George's disordered clothes, his
niece's blushes, the broken handle, the empty beef plate, and
the bicycle lamp still standing on the chest of drawers.

Then he shook his head.

"Doctor was wrong," he said. "It ain't only him. You're
both mad."

He went out and locked the door behind him.

For a moment the young people stared at it.

Then George turned.

"Well?" he said.

Miss Miller sat on the edge of the bed.

"Well what?"

"There's no handle, and he's got the key. We may
be here some time."

"That is your fault."

"Most things seem to be."

She smiled.

Outside, Mr. Wragg's voice was already audible at the front
gate, informing Mrs. Green that the patient had developed a
second and even more alarming delusion. Inside, George sat beside
Miss Miller and resumed the discussion from the point at
which it had been interrupted.

By the time Mr. Wragg returned with Harris and Brown
as witnesses, George had received an answer. Miss Miller, however,
denied this so sweetly, and George contradicted her so warmly,
that Mr. Wragg's original medical opinion remained unaltered.

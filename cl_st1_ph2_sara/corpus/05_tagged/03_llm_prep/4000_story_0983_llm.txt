# The One Woman

The day had died reluctantly.

For an hour after sunset a copper stain lingered above
the flat roofs of Bhanipur, as though the west, having
endured the furnace of the afternoon, still smouldered behind the
world. Then it faded. Darkness came down swiftly, wrapping cantonment,
native city, and dusty plain in one vast pall; but
the darkness brought no coolness. The heat remained—a crushing, sentient
weight beneath which men breathed slowly and dogs lay with
tongues extended in the shadows.

In the bungalow beyond the old parade-ground a lamp burned.

It stood upon a table littered with drawings, springs, screws,
and cartridges. Its yellow radiance fell strongly upon the figure
of a man seated beside it, touched the whitewashed wall
behind him, and spent itself before reaching the open door.
Beyond that oblong of light the compound was a gulf.
At intervals the dry leaves of a plantain stirred without
wind, making a faint sound like stealthy feet.

John Conyers waited.

He was a lean man, grey at the temples though
he could not have been more than forty, with a
face that appeared to have been hewn out of some
dark and stubborn material. Poverty had left its signature upon
him, but not its stoop. His coat was old and
his shirt frayed at the wrist; yet his immobility possessed
a certain stern distinction. He sat like a man who
had learned that movement avails little against fate.

On the table before him stood a glass half filled
with whisky and soda. He had not touched it for
twenty minutes.

Presently he turned his head.

There had been a sound upon the road.

He listened. Nothing followed. The silence flowed back, immense and
oppressive, broken only by the soft swish of the punkah
in the adjoining room, where an old servant pulled the
rope with mechanical patience.

Conyers rose and crossed to a writing-table near the wall.
He unlocked its upper drawer.

Within lay a heavy military revolver.

He looked at it without touching it. The lamplight slid
along the barrel and rested upon the brass of the
cartridges. After a moment he closed the drawer, but did
not lock it.

"It's loaded," he said aloud.

His voice sounded curious in the empty room—dry, detached, almost
amused. He returned to his chair.

Against the opposite wall hung a cheap reproduction from Doré:
the fallen Archangel descending through chaos, magnificent even in ruin,
his wings beaten back, his face turned from the lost
splendours above. Damp had curled the edges of the paper,
and one corner fluttered whenever the punkah drew a draught
through the room. To-night those ghostly fingers seemed to pluck
persistently at the defeated figure.

Conyers watched it for a while.

"Melodrama," he said.

But he did not take it down.

Once more a sound came from the road. This time
there could be no mistake. Hoofs struck the hard earth
far away, hesitated where the track divided, then advanced at
a canter.

Nearer came the hoofs and nearer yet.

The man at the table did not move. Only his
hand closed slowly upon the arm of the chair.

The horse passed the tamarind trees, thundered across the culvert,
and swung in at the gate. A voice shouted; gravel
scattered; there followed the impatient stamping of an animal pulled
up sharply. Then spurred boots rang upon the veranda.

A broad-shouldered young man filled the doorway.

"Hullo, Conyers!"

He entered like a gust of cooler air, though he
brought none with him. Hugh Palliser was six-and-twenty, sunburnt, fair-haired,
and superbly alive. His white uniform clung damply to his
powerful body; his eyes shone; every line of him proclaimed
health, confidence, and the careless favour of the gods. He
looked, in that poor room, like a young Hercules who
had strayed by accident into a hermit's cave.

Conyers rose.

"Well?"

For an instant Palliser stared at him; then, with a
great laugh, he sprang forward and seized both his shoulders.

"It's through!"

Conyers remained rigid.

"What is through?"

"The gun, man—the gun! Approved, accepted, recommended forthwith for field
trials under the most flattering minute ever written by a
Government department. By Jove, don't look at me as if
I had announced a funeral! You've done it, old chap.
You've won!"

The silence after that was so complete that the creak
of the punkah rope became suddenly distinct.

Conyers's face altered very little. Yet something behind its impassivity
seemed to break and blaze up, as fire may run
for one swift second through a cavern.

"Officially?" he asked.

"I saw the paper. I bullied Henderson into letting me
read it, though he swore three times that I had
no business to know. The tests were conclusive. Less recoil,
greater accuracy, no fouling after six hundred rounds—and the mechanism
so simple that even a commissariat colonel might understand it.
There's to be a contract. There'll be royalties. There'll be
fame. There'll be more money than you know how to
spend."

Conyers sat down.

Palliser's enthusiasm halted. He bent over him.

"Are you ill?"

"No."

"Then say something."

Conyers lifted the neglected glass, found it empty of soda,
and set it down again.

"I have been waiting seven years," he said. "One loses
the trick of receiving good news."

"Rot! You'll recover it. Give me a drink."

Palliser flung his helmet upon a heap of blueprints and
strode to the sideboard as though the bungalow already belonged
to him. He discovered the whisky, condemned the tepid soda,
and mixed two formidable drinks.

"To the Conyers quick-firing gun," he cried, raising his glass,
"and to its confoundedly obstinate inventor—millionaire, knight, and terror of
every War Office fool from here to Whitehall!"

Conyers drank.

The spirit steadied him, or seemed to. His thin lips
relaxed in the shadow of a smile.

"A knight," he repeated. "That would be entertaining."

"You may sneer, but it's coming. Henderson says the patent
alone is worth a fortune. You'll go home, of course."

"Home?"

"To England."

Conyers looked toward the open door. The night outside was
absolute.

"I have no home in England."

"You'll acquire one. A place in Surrey, perhaps, with decent
shooting and a cellar that doesn't serve whisky at blood
temperature. You'll have servants who don't vanish for three days
because an aunt's parrot has died. And you'll marry."

"No."

Palliser laughed and dropped into the other chair.

"That was quick."

"It required no consideration."

"My dear fellow, money changes everything."

"Not everything."

"Nearly everything worth changing."

The young man stretched his long legs and gazed with
cheerful pity about the room—the cracked plaster, the shelves of
technical books, the battered despatch-box, the coarse curtains moving faintly
in the hot draught. He had known Conyers for three
years. Their friendship, inexplicable to others, had begun when the
older man repaired a sporting rifle which every armourer in
the province had declared useless. Palliser had thereafter attached himself
to him with the generous tenacity of a dog.

He knew Conyers to be poor, proud, and unaccountably solitary.
He did not know why.

No one did.

"Anyhow," Palliser said, "you can't bury yourself here after this.
Why, you've been living like a fakir. I believe that
pistol of yours has eaten more regularly than you have."

Conyers's glance shifted to the writing-table.

"What pistol?"

"The one in the drawer."

"How do you know it is there?"

"I saw it when I came in last week. You
locked the drawer in a deuced suspicious fashion."

"Did I?"

Palliser rose, crossed the room, and pulled it open. The
revolver lay beneath his hand.

"You oughtn't to leave this thing knocking about," he said.
"The servants might get hold of it."

"They will not."

He lifted it, broke it open, and whistled.

"Loaded, too."

"Yes."

"What for?"

Conyers looked up at him.

"Emergencies."

Something in the answer—or in the face from which it
came—checked Palliser's smile. For one uneasy second youth and happiness
drew back before an experience which they could not comprehend.

Then Palliser snapped the weapon shut.

"Well, the emergency's over. You've won."

He replaced it and closed the drawer. Conyers rose, crossed
deliberately, turned the key, and put it in his pocket.

"Yes," he said. "I have won."

Thunder muttered very far away.

The sound rolled along the horizon like a cart crossing
a bridge, died, and returned more deeply. The hot season
had held the land in torment for weeks; all day
clouds had towered beyond the river, vast and purple, but
had refused to break. Now a breath entered through the
doorway and touched the Doré print. The paper rustled.

Palliser poured himself another drink.

"You know," he said, "I had a second reason for
coming."

"More official favours?"

"No. Something better. At least, better for me." He laughed,
but an unfamiliar embarrassment coloured his sunburnt face. "I'm engaged."

Conyers, who had been turning the key between his fingers,
let it fall.

It struck the floor with a small, clear sound.

Palliser stooped and recovered it.

"So even you can be surprised! Here you are, old
chap."

Conyers took the key.

"Who is she?"

"The one woman."

"Every man calls her that—for a month."

"I shall call her that when I'm eighty."

"You will be fortunate if you remember her name at
fifty."

"You cynical old devil!" Palliser's eyes flashed, yet affection remained
beneath his indignation. "You don't know her."

"That is probable."

"She is not a girl."

"Ah."

"Don't say 'ah' like that. She's thirty-seven."

"And you are twenty-six."

"Exactly. I can count."

"You asked her?"

"Last night."

"And she accepted you?"

"This morning."

There was no mockery in Conyers's face now. He had
withdrawn a little beyond the lamp's direct circle, and the
shadows gathered under his cheekbones, making his eyes appear cavernous.

"What is her name?"

"Damaris Leigh."

The wind stirred again. From the wall came that soft,
papery whisper, like corn bending beneath an invisible hand.

Conyers turned away.

Palliser, watching only his own splendid vision, noticed nothing.

"You may have heard of her," he continued. "She has
been in India less than a year—staying with the Brabazons
at Government House. Her uncle is Sir Everard Leigh. She's
not beautiful in the ordinary, doll-like way. She's too pale,
and there are silver threads in her hair. But when
she looks at you—by Jove, Conyers!—it's as if a door
opened in a dark room."

Conyers stood beside the print.

"And she looked at you?"

"At last."

"At last?"

"Well, it wasn't easy. She had made up her mind
never to marry."

"That is a decision often made in hope of being
persuaded out of it."

"Not in her case. She meant it."

"Why?"

Palliser hesitated, then sat forward, clasping his hands between his
knees.

"I oughtn't to tell you. Still, you're not a gossip,
and I want you to understand. There was another man."

Conyers's hand rose to the wall. His fingers touched the
lower corner of the print.

"Living?"

"I suppose so."

"Then perhaps she is still bound to him."

"She waited fifteen years."

The room seemed to contract.

Outside, the thunder spoke once more, nearer now. A low
wind ran across the compound. Dust came whispering along the
veranda, and the lamp flame leaned as though straining to
overhear.

"Fifteen years," Conyers said.

"Yes. Think of it! She was twenty-one when it happened.
He was a young fellow without money, but clever, proud,
all that sort of thing. They were secretly engaged."

"Secretly?"

"Her people objected. They knew something of the family, I
fancy. Then the man's father was arrested for forgery."

Conyers's fingers tightened upon the paper.

Palliser went on, with the careless vehemence of one to
whom another man's tragedy was merely the black background of
his own happiness.

"It was a bad business. The old fellow had speculated,
lost everything, and forged trustees' signatures to cover the losses.
He went to prison. The son disappeared before the trial
ended."

"Disappeared?"

"Utterly. Left no address, wrote no letter—nothing. Some said he
went to South America; some that he enlisted under another
name. He may have died. He may be alive."

"And the girl?"

"Waited."

"For a man who sent no word?"

"For fifteen years," Hugh repeated. "That is the sort of
woman she is."

Conyers's hand dropped. A ragged piece of the print had
torn away between his fingers.

"She was loyal," he said.

"More than loyal. Superhuman. Her family begged, argued, threatened. Other
men wanted her. She refused them all. She believed he
would return and explain."

"Perhaps he could not."

"Any man can write."

"Can he?"

The words were spoken so quietly that Palliser glanced up.
Conyers remained with his back turned, studying the torn fragment
in his hand.

"You defend him?"

"I know nothing of him."

"Then I'll tell you what I think. He was a
coward."

Conyers did not move.

"A coward," Palliser said again. "His father's crime was not
his. If he loved her, why abandon her? Pride? Shame?
Those are fine names for selfishness. He left her to
bear the scandal and uncertainty while he escaped. If I
had been in his place—"

"You were not."

"No. Thank God."

"What would you have done?"

"Gone to her. Told her everything. Worked, starved, fought—anything. But
I would not have condemned her to fifteen years of
waiting."

The older man turned.

For one instant there was flame in his eyes: fierce,
tortured, almost murderous. Hugh sprang to his feet, startled by
the sudden revelation of power in that wasted face.

Then it vanished.

Conyers smiled.

"You are twenty-six," he said.

"What has that to do with it?"

"At twenty-six, all roads remain open."

"And at forty?"

"One discovers that some roads end against a wall."

Palliser stared at him, then flung out his arms impatiently.

"Oh, I know you've had a rotten time. But don't
make a religion of defeat. Look at to-night! Seven years
you've fought for your gun, and now the wall is
down."

"Is it?"

"Of course it is."

Conyers looked toward the locked drawer.

The first drops of rain struck the veranda, large and
widely spaced. Each fell with the sound of a pebble.
The smell of wet dust rose rich and sudden from
the earth.

"Did she cease to love him?" he asked.

Palliser's face softened.

"She told me she had ceased to wait."

"That is not the same thing."

"No." Hugh paused. Honesty was natural to him, even when
it wounded. "No, perhaps it isn't. She said that the
girl who loved him had lived in another world, and
that both were dead now—the girl and the world. She
said she had spent half her life faithful to a
ghost."

"And you are content with what remains?"

The question was cruel. Palliser flushed.

"I am content with her."

"Even if the ghost returned?"

"He won't."

"If he did?"

The young man wheeled toward him.

"Why do you keep asking that?"

"Curiosity."

"It sounds like something else."

"What should it sound like?"

For a moment they faced one another across the lamplit
table—the splendid young animal, instinctively alert to danger, and the
lean, shadowed man whose face had again become inscrutable.

Rain struck the roof in a sudden volley and ceased.

Palliser laughed, but without mirth.

"If the fellow returned now, I'd tell him he was
fifteen years too late."

"And if he refused to accept that?"

"I'd make him."

"You are very sure of your strength."

"Yes."

"So was Goliath."

"That's unlike you, Conyers. I never knew you to quote
Scripture."

"The climate produces strange disorders."

Palliser moved to the sideboard. He poured soda into a
glass, but his hand was no longer quite steady. His
imagination, generous yet imperious, had conjured up the vanished lover
as an adversary standing between himself and Damaris. The vision
enraged him.

"I tell you this," he said. "If he came back
to torment her—if he tried to claim her after throwing
away fifteen years—I'd kill him."

Conyers regarded him.

"Would you?"

"Yes."

"Even if she loved him?"

The glass slipped from Palliser's fingers and broke upon the
floor.

At that instant the storm broke.

Lightning filled the open doorway with a sheet of violet
fire. Thunder crashed directly overhead, not like an inanimate concussion,
but like the cry of some chained being in anguish.
The walls shook; the lamp leapt; darkness and flame seemed
to grapple in the room.

Conyers spoke.

The thunder drowned his words.

Palliser bent closer. "What did you say?"

The older man's lips remained parted. For one immeasurable moment
it appeared that he would repeat the sentence. His right
hand had closed around the back of the chair; the
knuckles shone white. Behind him the fallen Satan writhed upon
the wall in the jumping light.

Then Conyers released the chair.

"I said you would be a lucky beggar."

Hugh drew a great breath. The storm's assault lessened as
swiftly as it had come, retreating across the city with
rolling reverberations.

"I am," he said. "The luckiest alive."

"Yes."

"And you're not to think I begrudge her the memory.
I don't. If he was worthy of her once, then—well,
I'm sorry for him. But he must remain dead."

"Dead men are usually obliging."

"Conyers, you have a ghoulish wit."

"It passes for humour in poor districts."

Palliser laughed, relieved. He kicked the fragments of glass aside
and came forward, holding out his hand.

"You will meet her?"

"If she wishes it."

"She will. I've spoken of you often."

"That was indiscreet."

"I told her you were the cleverest man in India
and the most infernally gloomy."

"Did that recommend me?"

"It interested her. She said lonely men generally had reasons."

Conyers looked down at the extended hand before taking it.

"Did she?"

"Yes. And when I tell her the gun is accepted,
she'll rejoice as much as I do. We want you
with us, old chap. Whatever has happened—whatever you've hidden in
this beastly room—come out of it. Begin again."

"Begin again," Conyers repeated.

"It can be done."

"By the young."

"By anyone with courage."

There was no reproach in Hugh's voice; only warmth, faith,
and the unconscious arrogance of happiness. Conyers gripped his hand.

"You are a good fellow, Hugh."

"Then promise to dine with us on Friday."

"I will see."

"That means no."

"It means I will see."

"You're impossible." Palliser seized his helmet. "I must get back
before the nullah floods. Damaris will be waiting for my
message."

At the door he turned, broad and radiant against the
rain.

"To-morrow," he said. "I'll come to drag you into civilization."

"To-morrow."

"And lock that pistol away."

"It is locked."

"Good. I don't trust gloomy inventors on nights of triumph."

"Nor should you."

Palliser grinned.

"The one woman," he said, lifting an imaginary glass.

Conyers raised his own.

"To the one woman."

The hoofbeats departed through the rain.

For a while they sounded loudly upon the road; then
more faintly, then faintlier still. At last they were lost
beneath the steady drumming on the leaves.

Conyers remained standing with the glass uplifted.

Slowly, very slowly, his arm descended.

The drink was untouched.

* * *

The old servant had ceased pulling the punkah. Somewhere at
the rear of the bungalow a door closed, and silence
resumed possession of the house.

Conyers crossed to the lamp and lowered the wick.

The room altered. Shadows advanced from the corners and drew
about him like dark waters. The open doorway was no
longer a gulf; rain had made it a shifting silver
curtain through which the compound trees appeared and vanished like
drowned things.

He went to the writing-table.

The key turned easily. The drawer opened.

For several seconds he stood looking down at the revolver.
Then he took it out and carried it to the
chair beside the lamp.

There had once been a young man named John Conyers
who possessed a father of distinction, a clean name, and
the love of Damaris Leigh. That young man had believed
pride to be honour. When disgrace came, swift and filthy,
he had refused to drag her into it. He had
written a letter and destroyed it; written another and destroyed
that also. Before dawn he had gone away.

At first he had meant to return when he had
made a new name.

Months became years. Misfortune followed him with the patience of
a creditor. By the time his invention promised redemption, hope
had worn thin; and still he had postponed the message
which shame made harder with every passing season.

She had waited.

He knew that now.

Fifteen years.

Conyers placed the revolver on the table. Beside it lay
the torn fragment of the Doré print—a portion of a
black wing against a field of fire. He picked it
up, crushed it in his fist, and let it fall.

One shot would be simple. Hugh would grieve, perhaps; Damaris
would hear of the lonely inventor who had died upon
the night of his success. She need never know. The
dead kept secrets better than the living.

His hand moved toward the weapon.

The rain whispered upon the earth. It had the sound
of wind passing through ripe corn, a sound from another
country and another life. He saw a garden in England,
white roses beside an old brick wall, and a girl
standing beneath a cedar. Her hair had been dark then.
Her eyes had held all the future.

Now there were silver threads in her hair.

Now she had ceased to wait.

His fingers touched the cold metal.

Then Hugh's voice returned to him, eager and unguarded: *We
want you with us. Begin again.*

Conyers bent his head.

To die to-night would not be silence. It would be
an interruption, a question, perhaps a revelation. Hugh had seen
the pistol. Hugh would remember every word. Damaris, with that
grave intuition of hers, might discover the truth, and happiness
once more would be poisoned by the ghost she had
at last laid down.

For her sake, then, he must live.

That was harder.

He lifted the revolver, returned it to the drawer, and
closed it. The key grated once in the lock.

The struggle was over.

Conyers went to the open doorway. The storm was passing
eastward, and beyond the ragged clouds a few pale stars
had appeared. Water ran from the roof in shining chains.
The parched earth drank without gratitude; the darkness remained.

He stood there a long while, looking toward the road
by which his friend had gone.

"It doesn't matter," he said.

But the night knew that it did.

Behind him the lamp flickered, rallied, and burned steadily. Before
him stretched the wet road, empty beneath the stars. He
would not follow it. He would make the gun, take
the money, perhaps even return to England when all danger
of meeting her was past. He would endure success as
he had endured failure. He would carry his name in
silence until silence claimed him altogether.

The paper upon the wall stirred once more.

Conyers turned, tore the fallen angel from its nail, and
thrust it into the dying fire. For a moment the
dark figure blazed splendidly; then wings, pride, ruin, and remembered
heaven folded inward and became ash.

He watched until the final spark was gone.

Outside, thunder murmured far away, no longer in anger but
like a voice withdrawing beyond immense doors. The rain softened.
Somewhere in the washed darkness a night-bird called, and after
a pause another answered.

Conyers extinguished the lamp.

He did not open the drawer again.

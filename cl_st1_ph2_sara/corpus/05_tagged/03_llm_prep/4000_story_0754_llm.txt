# Mose and the Ghosts That Weren't

Now, Mose was afraid of ghosts.

Yes, sir, he was powerfully afraid of them, no mistake.
He was afraid of tall ghosts and short ghosts, fat
ghosts and thin ghosts, pale ghosts that stood still and
gray ghosts that floated sideways. He was afraid of ghosts
with chains, ghosts without chains, ghosts that moaned, ghosts that
laughed, and especially ghosts that did not make any sound
at all, because a silent ghost might be standing directly
behind a boy and never give him the common courtesy
of warning him.

Mose was not ashamed of this fear. He had cogitated
upon the subject and reached a scientific conclusion.

"If ghosts leave me alone," he said, "I'll leave ghosts
alone. That is fair business."

And it would have been fair business, too, if grown
folks did not always take a boy's private arrangements and
stir them around with a spoon.

It was Halloween night at the house of Uncle Silas
Diggs. Uncle Silas had invited Sally Ann Piper, who lived
up the hill, and Zack Badget, who knew every superstition
ever invented and three that had not been invented yet.
Miss Flora Bell, the schoolteacher who boarded with Uncle Silas,
was there as well. The kitchen smelled of apples, cinnamon,
molasses, and hot cider. There were paper bats on the
walls and corn shucks over the doorway. Everybody was ready
for the party.

Everybody, that is, except the pumpkin.

Uncle Silas had forgotten the pumpkin in his wagon shed
at the far end of the lower field.

"Mose," he said, "run fetch that pumpkin."

Mose stopped with a molasses cookie halfway to his mouth.

"Which pumpkin?"

"The big one in the shed."

"Which shed?"

"My wagon shed."

"Which way?"

"Across the lower field."

Mose considered this information with the solemnity of a judge
considering a horse thief.

"The lower field past the old burying ground?"

"That's the one."

"And past Miller's Cemetery?"

"Only along the fence."

"And past the place where old Widow Peavey said she
saw a headless man sitting on a stone?"

Uncle Silas folded his arms.

"Mose, there are no ghosts."

"I know that," said Mose quickly. "Everybody knows that. But
does the headless man know it?"

Zack Badget laughed. Zack was brave whenever there were walls
around him, lamps above him, and several other people close
enough to catch him if his bravery failed.

"You scared?" Zack asked.

"No," said Mose. "I'm only prudent. Prudence and fear are
second cousins, but they don't live in the same locality."

Miss Flora Bell smiled. "There is nothing out there but
moonlight."

Mose looked through the window. The moon had not yet
risen, and the darkness outside pressed against the glass like
black wool.

"That moonlight is taking its time arriving," he said.

Uncle Silas gave him a lantern. "Go on, now. We
can't have a proper Halloween party without a pumpkin."

This was true. A party without a pumpkin is merely
a meeting, and nobody had dressed Miss Flora's broom in
a bonnet and hung six paper skeletons from the ceiling
merely to attend a meeting.

So Mose took the lantern in one hand and a
sack in the other. He buttoned his coat clear to
his chin, though the air was mild. Then he stepped
onto the porch.

The wind moved among the bare branches.

Hoooo-oo-oo-oo.

Mose stopped.

"That's wind," he told himself.

From the cedar tree came the cry of an owl.

Who-whoo-oo-oo?

"That's an owl."

Far off, beside the creek, a mourning dove called.

You-you-oo-oo.

"That's a dove," said Mose. "And it needn't concern itself
with who I am."

Then Uncle Silas opened the door behind him and said,
"Go on!"

Mose went.

He went down the path, through the gate, and into
the lower field. The lantern made a small yellow circle,
but all around that circle the night leaned close and
inspected him. The grass whispered at his shoes. Cornstalks rattled
along the fence.

Every ten steps Mose looked over his shoulder.

Every twelve steps he wished he had looked at ten.

But there was nothing behind him.

Nothing visible, anyhow.

Presently he reached the old burying ground where six crooked
stones stood under a wild plum tree. Folks had been
buried there so long that nobody remembered who they were,
which was just as well, Mose thought, because remembering them
might encourage them.

He walked faster.

Hoooo-oo-oo-oo, said the wind.

Who-whoo-oo-oo? asked the owl.

You-you-oo-oo, mourned the dove.

"Not me," Mose whispered. "Somebody else."

Just then his foot struck something round.

It rolled out of the grass.

Mose held the lantern lower.

There, beside his shoe, was a head.

It was not an especially lively-looking head. Its eyes were
shut, its mouth was crooked, and one ear had a
sprig of grass caught behind it. Still, a head lying
by itself on a path possesses a certain unmistakable significance.

Mose's eyes opened as wide as two butter-pats.

Then the head opened its eyes.

"Kindly don't kick me," it said.

Mose jumped backward so hard that the lantern flame bowed.

"I beg your pardon," he gasped. "I didn't know you
were occupied."

"Quite all right," said the head. "Accidents occur."

A long white hand reached out from behind a gravestone
and felt around in the grass. It patted a stone,
patted a root, patted Mose's shoe, and finally found the
head by the ear.

Then a tall body rose from behind the stone. It
wore a ragged coat with silver buttons, but there was
nothing above its collar.

The body lifted the head and tucked it under one
arm.

Mose's eyes grew from butter-pats to saucers.

"Evening," said the head from under the arm.

"Evening," said Mose, because politeness is useful when facing persons
who have misplaced their anatomy.

The headless figure stepped closer. Its free hand touched Mose's
hair.

That hand felt like a wet rag laid in a
cellar in January.

Mose shivered from his heels to his eyebrows.

"You appear nervous," said the head.

"A little," Mose admitted.

"No reason for that."

"No?"

"Certainly not. There are no ghosts."

Mose stared.

The body placed the head on its neck, but backward.
The head said, "Oops," and the body turned it around.

"There are no ghosts?" Mose repeated.

"None whatever."

"But—if you'll pardon the personal observation—what are you?"

"Me?" The figure seemed offended. "I am not a ghost."

"What are you, then?"

The figure thought about it. "I am a gentleman taking
the evening air."

"With your head off?"

"It was warm."

That explanation possessed weak places, but Mose did not wish
to poke at them.

The ghost—that is, the gentleman taking the evening air—pointed toward
the path.

"You'd better hurry. This is no place for a boy
after dark."

"Because of ghosts?"

"There are no ghosts," the gentleman said firmly. "Good night."

Then he took off his head again, tucked it beneath
his arm, stepped behind the stone, and disappeared.

Mose stood still for a minute. He touched the place
where the cold hand had rested. His hair felt peculiar,
but he had no mirror, and sometimes hair naturally feels
peculiar after it has been touched by something that does
not exist.

"Well," Mose said, "that settles one thing."

He was not sure which thing it settled, but a
boy feels better after saying such words.

He continued along the path.

The old burying ground ended, and the fence of Miller's
Cemetery began. Miller's Cemetery was larger and more important. It
had an iron gate, stone angels, marble lambs, and enough
deceased citizens to form three committees and a brass band.

Mose kept to the outside of the fence.

Hoooo-oo-oo-oo, breathed the wind.

Who-whoo-oo-oo? called the owl.

You-you-oo-oo, cried the dove.

"Still not me," Mose answered.

He had nearly passed the cemetery when he heard footsteps
on the other side of the fence.

Crunch.

Crunch.

Crunch.

Mose walked faster.

The footsteps went faster.

Crunch-crunch.

Mose slowed down.

The footsteps slowed down.

Crunch.

Mose stopped.

The footsteps stopped.

He raised the lantern.

On the other side of the fence stood three pale
figures in long coats. One was so tall that his
chin nearly touched the branch above him. One was so
short that his coat dragged on the ground. The third
had no face at all, only a smooth white surface
beneath his hat.

Mose's eyes widened from saucers to soup bowls.

"Evening," said the tall one.

"Evening," said the short one.

The faceless one nodded.

"Evening," Mose managed.

The tall figure pointed down. "You have my hand."

Mose looked at his own hands. One held the lantern.
The other held the sack.

"I don't believe I do."

"Under your foot."

Mose lifted his left shoe.

A pale hand lay in the dust. Its fingers wriggled
irritably.

"I beg your pardon," said Mose. "It resembles a root
in this light."

"No injury," said the tall figure.

His arm stretched through the fence, longer and longer, until
his sleeve reached the ground. The hand seized his wrist
and attached itself with a damp little pop.

Then it reached up and stroked Mose's hair.

The fingers felt like five cold toadstools.

Mose's knees performed a small private dance.

"You are trembling," said the short figure.

"Just keeping my circulation circulating."

"Are you afraid of ghosts?"

"No," said Mose. Then honesty tugged his sleeve. "Maybe one
or two."

"Absurd," said the tall figure.

"Preposterous," said the short figure.

The faceless one shook his head.

"There are no ghosts," all three declared.

Mose considered them carefully.

"You gentlemen appear a little ghostlike."

The tall one drew himself up. "That is an optical
confusion."

The short one nodded. "Moonlight has no discrimination."

Mose glanced at the sky. The moon still had not
risen.

"What about him?" he asked, pointing toward the faceless figure.
"He hasn't got a face."

The faceless figure reached into his pocket, took out a
face, and put it on. Unfortunately it was upside down.

"Carelessness," said the tall one. "Anybody may forget a face."

The third figure removed the face, rotated it, and replaced
it correctly. It was a stern face with long whiskers.

"There," said the short one. "You see? Entirely natural."

The tall figure leaned across the fence. His face came
close to Mose's, though his feet remained six yards away.

"Ghosts are a superstition," he whispered.

His breath smelled of wet leaves and old stone.

Then all three figures turned transparent, drifted backward through the
graves, and vanished into a marble monument.

Mose watched them go.

"Well," he said, "that settles three more."

He touched his hair again. It felt colder now. Some
of it seemed stiff.

He continued along the fence.

The farther he walked, the darker the darkness became. That
may sound impossible, but the night had entered into serious
negotiations with itself and agreed to become twice as black
for Mose's special benefit.

The wind sighed.

Hoooo-oo-oo-oo.

The owl called.

Who-whoo-oo-oo?

The dove mourned.

You-you-oo-oo.

Then something behind the cemetery wall whispered:

Mooooose.

Mose stopped.

"That wasn't wind."

Mooooose.

"That wasn't an owl."

Mooooose.

"That dove has learned my name."

He raised the lantern. Over the stone wall appeared a
row of pale faces. There were ten at first. Then
twenty. Then forty. More rose until the entire wall was
decorated with noses, teeth, empty eyes, and fluttering hair.

A ghost in a tall hat floated through the gate.
Behind him came another carrying a rusty trumpet. Behind that
one came six ghosts in uniforms, twelve ghosts wrapped in
sheets, nineteen ghosts with ropes around their necks, and one
little ghost who appeared to be looking for his spectacles.

They surrounded Mose in a wide circle.

His eyes grew from soup bowls to dinner plates.

The tall-hatted ghost raised a transparent hand.

"Meeting will come to order."

The other specters stopped moaning.

The ghost with the trumpet blew a long note.

Blaaaaa-a-a-a-at!

His cheeks inflated, but the note came from the trumpet's
wrong end.

The tall-hatted ghost frowned. "Secretary, read the purpose of this
extraordinary confabulation."

A thin ghost unrolled a parchment.

"Whereas a boy has entered this locality," he read, "and
whereas said boy displays evidence of alarming agitation, and whereas
agitation may cause unfounded speculation regarding supernatural manifestations—"

"Speak plain," called a ghost in a sheet.

The secretary sighed. "The boy thinks we're ghosts."

A murmur passed through the assembly.

"Outrageous!"

"Slander!"

"Scandalous!"

"No such things!"

The little ghost found his spectacles inside his own chest,
put them on, and stared at Mose.

"Do you think we're ghosts?" he asked.

Mose looked around at the transparent assembly.

"Not if it gives offense."

The tall-hatted ghost stepped forward. One of his legs remained
where it was, stretching behind him like a ribbon. He
noticed, went back, gathered the leg, and fastened it properly.

"Boy," he said, "you must understand that there are no
ghosts."

"That's what the others said."

"What others?"

"The gentleman with his head off, and the three gentlemen
who walked through the monument."

The assembly groaned.

"Those fellows again," muttered the secretary.

The tall-hatted ghost drew himself up. "They were entirely correct.
There are no ghosts."

"Then who are all of you?"

The tall-hatted ghost looked over the crowd.

"We are members of the Miller's Cemetery Nocturnal Improvement Association."

"What do you improve?"

"Mostly the nocturnal portion."

Mose nodded slowly. "That sounds reasonable, except for all of
you being dead."

The crowd murmured again.

"Technicality!"

"Personal remark!"

"Strike it from the record!"

The secretary scratched something from the parchment.

A large ghost with an iron chain stepped up behind
Mose. "Perhaps the boy requires reassurance."

"That would be welcome," Mose said.

The chained ghost rested both hands on Mose's head.

Those hands felt like two lizards dipped in ice water.

Mose's teeth knocked together.

"Are you reassured?" asked the ghost.

"Not in the direction you intended."

The tall-hatted ghost called for a vote.

"All in favor of the proposition that ghosts do not
exist, raise one hand."

Every ghost raised one hand. Several loose hands rose from
the ground without owners. One ghost raised three.

"Opposed?"

A hand started to rise from an open grave, but
another hand pulled it down.

"Proposition carried unanimously," announced the secretary.

"There," said the chairman. "It is official."

"That helps," said Mose.

"We will also pass a resolution that you are not
frightened."

All the ghosts raised their hands again.

"Unanimously resolved," said the secretary.

Mose's knees still knocked, but he felt bound to respect
parliamentary procedure.

"Thank you kindly."

The chairman struck the ground with a bone gavel.

The cemetery gates flew open. Every tombstone tilted. The whole
association whirled upward like dead leaves in a chimney draft,
crying:

"No ghosts! No ghosts! No ghosts!"

Then they vanished.

Mose was alone.

The lantern flame straightened itself and looked relieved.

Mose touched his head. His hair now felt as stiff
as broom straw. He pulled one strand down where he
could see it.

It was white.

"Maybe moonlight did that," he said.

The moon, still hiding below the horizon, offered no comment.

Mose had come too far to go back without the
pumpkin. Returning empty-handed meant traveling the whole road for nothing,
and there is no fear so strong that it cannot
be argued with by the prospect of having to repeat
itself.

So Mose walked on.

Beyond Miller's Cemetery lay a narrow bridge over the creek.
Beyond the bridge lay the wagon shed. The pumpkin rested
inside, large, orange, and innocent, though pumpkins are generally innocent
only until somebody cuts a face into them.

Mose reached the bridge.

The water below made whispering sounds.

"Hush," Mose told it.

Halfway across, the bridge bent downward.

Mose stopped.

Something enormous rose from the creek.

First came a crown made of bones. Then came a
forehead broad as a barn door. Then two eyes opened,
green and glowing. A nose like a church bell appeared,
followed by a mouth wide enough to swallow a wagon
without chewing.

The thing rose higher and higher until it towered above
the trees.

It wore a royal robe woven from fog, dead vines,
and scraps of moonless night. Around its neck hung seven
skulls, each one chattering from the cold.

The giant specter placed one finger on the bridge.

That finger was longer than Mose was tall.

Mose's eyes expanded from dinner plates to the bottom of
a wash-tub.

"Who crosses my dominions?" thundered the apparition.

The bridge shook.

The trees bowed.

The water ran backward.

"I'm Mose," said Mose in a voice about the size
of a mosquito. "I'm fetching a pumpkin."

"I am the King of All Ghosts!"

Mose swallowed.

This was unfortunate. When one has already met the citizens,
the association, and the officers, there is small comfort in
meeting the government.

The king leaned lower.

His breath came over Mose like wind from a frozen
cellar.

"Are you afraid?"

Mose remembered the vote.

"No, Your Majesty. It has been unanimously resolved that I'm
not."

"Who passed such a resolution?"

"The Miller's Cemetery Nocturnal Improvement Association."

"Ah," said the king. "A worthy organization. Their minutes are
always accurate."

Mose gathered a little courage from this administrative connection.

"I'd like to ask a question, if it's legal."

"Proceed."

"Are you truly the King of All Ghosts?"

The giant's crown flared with green fire.

"I am king of every wandering shade, every rattling phantom,
every midnight specter, every pale shape that peeps through a
window, every moan beneath a stair, every cold breath in
an empty room, every invisible footstep, and every hand that
reaches from beneath a bed."

Mose wished he had not heard the final portion.

"How many subjects do you have?"

The king began counting on his fingers.

"Eleven million, nine hundred thousand, forty-two, and a few left
over."

"That is considerable."

"It is."

"Then ghosts do exist?"

The king recoiled so sharply that his crown knocked three
stars crooked.

"Certainly not!"

Mose blinked.

"But you just said—"

"I said I am King of All Ghosts. I did
not say ghosts exist."

"Can a king have subjects that don't exist?"

"Most kings prefer that kind," said the ghost king. "They
pay fewer complaints."

Mose tried to cogitate. The thoughts in his head collided
like chickens in a basket.

"If there are no ghosts," he said, "how can you
be king of them?"

"That is a constitutional matter."

"Oh."

"Further, it has been established by custom, resolution, rumor, and
personal testimony that there are no ghosts."

"Personal testimony from whom?"

"From ghosts."

Mose nodded. "That is what they all tell me."

"Then why do you doubt it?"

"Because they're the ones telling me."

The king stared down at him.

For a moment the night became very still.

Then the giant ghost began to shrink.

He shrank from the size of a church to the
size of a house, from a house to a horse,
from a horse to a man, and from a man
to a small figure no taller than Mose's lantern. He
stepped onto the bridge rail.

"You are applying too much logic," he said. "Logic is
dangerous when carried past its recommended distance."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The little king reached out and touched Mose's hair.

His hand felt like a cold frog wrapped in a
wet handkerchief.

Every last dark strand on Mose's head turned white.

"There," said the king. "Now you look wiser."

"I don't feel wiser."

"That frequently accompanies the appearance."

Then the king snapped his fingers.

From the creek, the cemetery, the woods, the fields, and
the sky came ghosts.

There were headless ghosts, faceless ghosts, armless ghosts, legless ghosts,
and ghosts with extra heads that argued among themselves. There
were soldier ghosts, sailor ghosts, farmer ghosts, baby ghosts, dog
ghosts, horse ghosts, and one indignant chicken ghost. There were
ghosts wearing crowns, helmets, nightcaps, bonnets, veils, pots, and nothing
at all.

They filled the bridge.

They covered the banks.

They hung from the branches like pale fruit.

They stretched across the field as far as Mose could
see—about eleven million, nine hundred thousand, forty-two, and several that
had plainly just arrived.

The king raised his hand.

"What do we declare?"

The millions answered:

"THERE ARE NO GHOSTS!"

The sound knocked leaves from the trees.

It lifted Mose's cap.

It blew out the lantern.

Then every specter vanished at once.

The bridge was empty.

The creek flowed forward again. The stars settled back into
their proper localities. The owl, which had observed the proceedings
from a hollow tree, gave one uncertain call.

Who?

"I don't know," Mose replied. "And I'm done asking."

He hurried across the bridge, ran to the wagon shed,
and found the pumpkin.

It was enormous. Uncle Silas had neglected to mention that
the pumpkin weighed about as much as a sleepy calf.
Mose struggled it into the sack, dragged the sack outside,
and started home.

Now, a road that is terrifying when you walk away
from home becomes twice as long when you drag a
pumpkin over it. Every root caught the sack. Every stone
rolled under the pumpkin. Every ten yards Mose stopped to
breathe, and every time he stopped, the darkness seemed to
stop with him.

But he had information now.

When the wind said, Hoooo-oo-oo-oo, Mose answered, "There are no
ghosts."

When the owl asked, Who-whoo-oo-oo? Mose answered, "Nobody ghostly."

When the dove cried, You-you-oo-oo, Mose said, "Not according to
the official vote."

At the cemetery gate, he heard whispering.

"No ghosts," he called.

A hundred pale faces appeared over the wall.

"Correct," they whispered, and sank again.

At the old burying ground, the headless gentleman stepped onto
the path. He was carrying his head by the hair.

"Need assistance?" asked the head.

"I can manage."

The gentleman helped anyway. He picked up the sack with
one hand and carried it as lightly as an apple.

At the far end of the burying ground, he set
it down.

"Much obliged," said Mose.

"Think nothing of it."

The cold hand patted Mose's shoulder.

"You are not afraid now?"

"No, sir. You convinced me."

"Excellent."

The gentleman tucked his head beneath his arm and walked
directly into a tree.

There came a hollow bump.

"I meant to do that," said the head.

"Of course," said Mose.

He dragged the pumpkin onward.

When he reached Uncle Silas's gate, he saw yellow lamplight
in the windows. The party had already begun. Mose could
hear laughter, chairs scraping, and Zack Badget speaking in the
low mysterious voice he used whenever he intended to frighten
people smaller than himself.

Mose left the pumpkin on the porch and entered quietly.

Nobody noticed him.

The guests sat in a circle around the stove. All
the lamps had been turned low. Zack stood in the
middle, telling a ghost story.

"And then," Zack whispered, "the dead man's hand came crawling
up the bedpost."

Sally Ann covered her face.

Miss Flora Bell leaned forward.

Uncle Silas pretended not to be interested, but his chair
had moved closer to the stove.

"The hand crawled higher," Zack continued. "It reached the pillow.
It felt around in the dark. Then it grabbed—"

Mose put one white-haired head around the doorway.

"Evening."

Sally Ann screamed.

Miss Flora Bell screamed.

Zack Badget screamed loudest of all and jumped behind Uncle
Silas.

Uncle Silas rose so fast that his chair tipped over.

"Mose!" he shouted. "What happened to your hair?"

Mose came into the room.

"What's wrong with it?"

"It's white!"

Mose pulled down a strand and examined it in the
lamplight.

"So it is."

"How did that happen?"

"Ghosts touched it."

The room became silent.

Even Zack stopped screaming, though he retained the position for
convenience.

Uncle Silas cleared his throat. "I thought you understood there
were no ghosts."

"Yes, sir," said Mose. "I understand it better now."

Miss Flora Bell stared at him. "You met ghosts?"

"Several."

"How many?"

"First one, then three, then an association, then the king,
then about eleven million, nine hundred thousand, forty-two, and a
few left over."

Sally Ann gripped the arms of her chair.

"And you aren't frightened?"

"No, ma'am."

"Why not?"

"Because every one of them told me there are no
ghosts."

Zack emerged a little from behind Uncle Silas. "But if
ghosts told you—"

"Yes?"

"Then there must be ghosts."

Mose shook his head. "That isn't logical. If anybody ought
to know whether ghosts exist, ghosts ought to know. And
they were unanimous."

Miss Flora Bell opened her mouth, closed it, and cogitated.

Uncle Silas rubbed his chin.

Sally Ann said, "That does sound reasonable."

"Certainly," said Mose. "They held a vote."

"A proper vote?" asked Uncle Silas.

"Hands up and everything."

"Well," said Uncle Silas, "that settles it."

Zack looked doubtful. "All eleven million?"

"The king spoke for them."

Zack nodded reluctantly. A king's testimony has influence, even when
the king and all his subjects do not exist.

They brought in the pumpkin. Uncle Silas carved it, Miss
Flora put a candle inside, and soon its triangular eyes
glowed from the window. The lamps were raised. Apples bobbed
in a tub. Cider steamed in mugs. Everybody laughed about
how frightened they had been.

Mose laughed, too.

He laughed when Zack imitated Sally Ann's scream.

He laughed when Sally Ann imitated Zack hiding behind Uncle
Silas.

He even laughed when Miss Flora tied a ribbon around
one lock of his white hair and declared him the
wisest-looking boy in the county.

But every now and then Mose glanced toward the dark
window.

Once he saw a pale face pressed against the glass.

It winked.

Mose looked away.

There was no sense paying attention to something that did
not exist.

The party continued until the clock struck eleven. Then Sally
Ann went home with her brothers, Zack went home carrying
a lantern in each hand, and Miss Flora retired to
her room. Uncle Silas banked the fire.

"Mose," he said, "time for bed."

Mose stood beside the stove.

"Yes, sir."

"Your room is ready."

"Yes, sir."

"Go on, then."

Mose did not move.

Uncle Silas raised an eyebrow. "You aren't afraid, are you?"

"Certainly not."

"You just proved there are no ghosts."

"That is correct."

"Then what's the trouble?"

Mose glanced toward the stairs. They climbed into darkness. At
the top was a narrow hallway. At the end of
that hallway was his bedroom, and beneath his bedroom was
the space under his bed—the very sort of locality the
king had mentioned.

"I'm not afraid of ghosts," Mose explained.

"Good."

"I'm not afraid of any ghost that is."

"Good."

"And I know for a fact that no ghosts are."

"Exactly."

Mose moved a little closer to Uncle Silas.

"But," he added, "I'm still uneasy about all those ghosts
that aren't."

From somewhere upstairs came a soft step.

Creak.

Then another.

Creak.

Then a whisper slid down the chimney:

There are no ghosts.

Mose took hold of Uncle Silas's sleeve.

"Seeing as ghosts don't exist," he said, "you won't mind
coming upstairs with me."

Uncle Silas looked toward the dark stairway.

"No," he said quickly. "Not at all."

So Uncle Silas carried the lamp, and Mose carried Uncle
Silas's sleeve, and the two of them went upstairs together,
each one protecting the other from all the ghosts that
weren't.

Perfectly logical.

Just like sensible folks.

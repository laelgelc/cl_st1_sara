## WILL IT PAY?

"Will it pay?"

Mr. Jones elevated his eye-brows, drew back from his desk,
and regarded his neighbour with an expression in which surprise
and amusement were blended.

"I cannot answer that question," replied Mr. Smith. "I have
not considered the matter in dollars and cents."

"Then you have not considered it at all," said Mr.
Jones, with a short laugh. "Every prudent man asks, before
he gives his time to any undertaking—'Will it pay?'"

"It may pay in a manner not entered in our
ledgers."

"Ah! sentiment!" returned Mr. Jones. "A very pretty commodity, no
doubt; but one which bears no quotation in the market."

The two men were seated in the counting-room of Mr.
Jones, a merchant whose reputation for sharpness was almost equal
to his reputation for wealth. Bills, invoices, and letters lay
upon the broad desk before him; and near his right
hand stood a little book in which he had just
made certain figures that seemed to afford him unusual satisfaction.
Mr. Smith, who was likewise engaged in mercantile pursuits, had
called to speak of a matter which did not immediately
concern either man's business.

"You remember Mr. Lloyd?" he asked.

"Lloyd? Lloyd?" Mr. Jones tapped his forehead. "Yes—the commission merchant
who failed five or six years ago."

"He afterward recovered himself, in part."

"Never wholly," said Jones. "A man who once fails carries
the scar through life. He died last week, I believe."

"He did."

"And left very little?"

"His widow fears so. Mrs. Lloyd is almost wholly ignorant
of business. Her husband's affairs are unsettled; she has three
daughters dependent upon her; and, unless some experienced person advises
her, she may sacrifice the small property that remains."

"Well?"

"I came to ask you to accompany me to her
house. Your experience might be of great service."

Mr. Jones leaned back and thrust his thumbs into the
arm-holes of his waistcoat.

"What commission am I to receive?"

"Commission!"

"Yes. You say that my experience is wanted. Experience is
capital, Mr. Smith, and capital should earn interest."

"This is a case of distress."

"There are a great many cases of distress."

"She is the widow of an old acquaintance."

"I knew Lloyd in business; I did not marry his
family."

"She needs only an hour or two of intelligent advice."

"And an hour or two may cost me fifty dollars.
No, no, Smith. I am too poor to give away
my time."

Mr. Jones was worth, at the lowest estimate, sixty thousand
dollars.

Mr. Smith looked at him steadily.

"You are resolved, then, not to go?"

"Unless it can be shown that it will pay."

"It will pay," said Mr. Smith, rising.

"In what?"

"In the pleasing consciousness of having served one who is
unable to serve herself."

Mr. Jones sneered.

"Can that consciousness be discounted at the bank?"

"No."

"Will it meet a note?"

"No."

"Will it buy a bale of cotton?"

"No."

"Then I do not want it."

"You may want it one day more than you now
imagine."

"Good morning, Mr. Smith. When you have any thing profitable
to propose, I shall be happy to listen."

Mr. Smith bowed and withdrew.

"An excellent man," muttered Jones, as the door closed, "but
wholly unfit for business. He will never be rich. He
allows humanity, as he calls it, to interfere with every
advantageous operation."

The word humanity fell from his lips as though it
had been the name of some mischievous speculation by which
weak men were continually ruined.

Mr. Smith passed from the counting-room into the busy street.
It was nearly noon, and more than one important affair
demanded his attention; but the pale face of Mrs. Lloyd,
as he had seen it on the previous evening, arose
before him. He remembered also the three young girls sitting
silently in their darkened parlour, frightened by the vague prospect
of poverty, yet striving to conceal their fears from their
mother.

"Business may wait for an hour," said he. "Sorrow seldom
can."

He went immediately to the widow's dwelling.

Mrs. Lloyd received him with visible emotion. Her cheek was
pale from grief and watching; and when she attempted to
speak of her affairs, her voice trembled.

"I fear I am giving you a great deal of
trouble, Mr. Smith."

"Do not think of that," he replied. "Your husband and
I were friends in earlier years. If I can aid
you, I shall consider it a privilege."

She placed before him a small packet of papers. There
were a few notes, several unpaid accounts of doubtful value,
and certificates for fifty shares in the Western and Northern
Railroad Company.

"My husband always believed these shares secure," said Mrs. Lloyd.
"They were intended, I think, as a provision for us.
Mr. Perkins, the broker, has offered to sell them if
I desire it; but he says the market is unsettled,
and I do not know what to do."

Mr. Smith examined the certificates.

"What price does Mr. Perkins think they will bring?"

"Ninety-two."

"And have you any other ready money?"

"Only enough to meet our expenses for two months."

"Then, in my judgment, you should sell."

"But the stock once sold, the money may soon be
spent."

"It must not be spent. It must be converted into
some form that will afford both shelter and employment."

Mrs. Lloyd looked at him anxiously.

"I have thought," she said, "that perhaps we might keep
a small shop. Before my marriage I assisted an aunt
in the dry-goods business. My eldest daughter is skilful with
her needle; Ellen has taste in selecting colours; and even
little Mary could make herself useful. But a store requires
capital, and we must also have a home."

"Perhaps the same building can provide both."

Hope stirred faintly beneath the widow's sorrow.

"Do you think it possible?"

"I will ascertain. In the mean time, give me authority
to confer with Mr. Perkins and dispose of the shares
if the present price can be obtained."

"I place the matter wholly in your hands."

This expression of confidence touched Mr. Smith more deeply than
any promise of compensation could have done. He left the
house with a warmer heart than he had carried into
it. Yet, according to Mr. Jones, his visit had not
paid!

On returning toward his counting-room, Mr. Smith called upon Perkins,
whom he found preparing to go out.

"You are fortunate," said the broker, after hearing his errand.
"I have a purchaser for fifty shares of Western and
Northern at ninety-two. He expects a rise within ten days,
and is eager to close."

"Is the stock weak?"

"There are rumours; nothing certain. The last dividend was not
earned, I suspect, and some heavy obligations fall due next
week. Still, men who ought to know are buying."

"I prefer certainty for Mrs. Lloyd," answered Mr. Smith. "Sell
the shares."

The necessary authority was given; and before two o'clock the
transaction was completed.

Who was the purchaser?

Mr. Jones.

Scarcely half an hour after Mr. Smith had left him,
Perkins entered his counting-room.

"I have something that will pay," said the broker.

The little eyes of Jones brightened.

"What is it?"

"Western and Northern Railroad stock. Fifty shares at ninety-two."

"Too high."

"It sold at ninety-four yesterday."

"It may sell at eighty to-morrow."

"Not likely. There is a report that the company will
consolidate with the Central line. If true, the shares will
go to one hundred and ten."

"If true!" Mr. Jones shook his head. "Reports are cheap."

"So are the shares," said Perkins, moving toward the door.
"But I can place them elsewhere."

"Stop! Whose stock is it?"

Perkins hesitated.

"I am not at liberty to say."

"Then some frightened holder is sacrificing."

"Possibly."

"When must the money be paid?"

"To-day."

Mr. Jones rubbed his hands. The thought of a profit
of eighteen dollars upon each share took possession of his
imagination. He asked several rapid questions, affected indifference, complained of
the risk, and finally offered ninety-one.

"Not a cent below ninety-two," replied Perkins.

"Then let it go."

"Very well."

The broker reached the door.

"Perkins!"

"Well?"

"I suppose the transfer can be made at once?"

"In ten minutes."

"I will take it."

Mr. Jones signed the necessary paper and paid four thousand
six hundred dollars. As he laid down the check, he
experienced that peculiar exhilaration which comes to a mercenary mind
when gain appears certain and another person's necessity is believed
to have furnished the opportunity.

"Who was the seller?" he asked again.

Perkins smiled.

"Mrs. Lloyd."

"What! Lloyd's widow?"

"The same."

A pleased expression passed over the merchant's face.

"Then Smith's charitable expedition has borne fruit, after all; though
not for Smith. Ha! ha! He gives his time, and
I take the profit."

For what a small gain will some men sacrifice delicacy,
sympathy, and honour! The mere belief that a widow had
parted ignorantly with valuable property increased, rather than diminished, the
satisfaction of Mr. Jones. He did not ask whether she
had been wisely advised. It was enough that he expected
to gain.

Nor was this the only promising operation of the day.

Late in the afternoon, a merchant named Armor entered the
counting-room. His dress was neat but worn; his face bore
the marks of anxiety; and the hand in which he
held his memorandum trembled slightly.

"Mr. Jones," he said, "I have come to speak about
my note for fifteen hundred dollars, due to-morrow."

"It will be paid, of course."

"I cannot meet the whole of it."

Mr. Jones's countenance hardened.

"Then why did you give it?"

"When I gave the note, I expected remittances that have
not arrived. I can pay five hundred dollars to-morrow and
the remainder within thirty days."

"I do not extend notes."

"My circumstances are peculiar."

"So are those of every man who cannot pay."

"If you press me, I shall be compelled to sacrifice
goods at forced sale."

"That is not my concern."

Armor remained silent for a moment. Necessity is a stern
master, and sometimes bends an honest man almost to the
earth.

"I have twelve cases of fine French cloths," he said
at length. "They are worth at the lowest market rate
twenty-four hundred dollars. If you will take them for the
note and allow me three hundred dollars in cash—"

"Impossible."

"The goods are in perfect order."

"I do not want them."

"You deal in the same article."

"I have a full supply."

This was not true. Mr. Jones had that morning written
to his agent requesting the purchase of similar goods.

Armor arose.

"I must try elsewhere."

"Stay," said Jones. "I might take the cloths, merely to
oblige you; but there is danger of a decline. I
could not allow more than the amount of the note."

"Twelve cases for fifteen hundred dollars!"

"You proposed them for eighteen hundred."

"Because I must have three hundred dollars to meet the
wages of my workmen and certain smaller debts."

"I cannot help that. The goods may remain on my
hands for months. Cash is cash."

Armor's lips grew pale.

"Allow me two hundred dollars."

"No."

"One hundred, then."

"Not a dollar. The note and protest expenses—that is all."

"You would gain nearly nine hundred dollars by my distress."

"Gain!" exclaimed Mr. Jones. "You forget interest, storage, risk, and
experience. Business is business."

Armor turned toward the window. There was a hard struggle
in his mind. A protest might destroy his remaining credit;
acceptance of the offer would strip him of nearly all
his available merchandise. At length he said—

"I have no alternative. Take the goods."

"Very well. Have them sent round before five o'clock."

Mr. Jones drew up the agreement with great precision. When
Armor had signed it and gone, the merchant leaned back
in his chair.

"That will pay," said he.

Will it?

At the tea-table that evening, Mr. Jones was in unusually
high spirits.

"Smith," he said, for the two families lived in adjoining
houses and the neighbours had met at their doors, "how
did your charitable consultation prosper?"

"Very well."

"Any commission?"

"None."

"Any profit?"

"Much."

"In money?"

"No."

"Then it exists only in your imagination. I, meanwhile, have
made two excellent operations. You remember the Western and Northern
stock?"

"I do."

"I bought fifty shares at ninety-two."

Mr. Smith looked at him with surprise.

"From Perkins?"

"Yes. Your widow's stock, my benevolent friend! While you were
giving advice, I secured the bargain."

"I am glad Mrs. Lloyd has sold."

"Glad! You may change your opinion when the shares reach
one hundred and ten."

"They may not reach it."

"I have information."

"Of what kind?"

"The best kind—the kind that promises profit."

Mr. Smith smiled.

"I sincerely hope your information is trustworthy."

This calm answer irritated Jones, who had expected either envy
or mortification.

"You will never prosper as you ought," he said. "You
allow feeling to stand between you and fortune."

"And you," returned Mr. Smith, "may sometimes find that feeling
would have stood between you and misfortune."

The next morning a rumour passed through commercial circles that
the Western and Northern Railroad Company had failed to provide
for the payment of its maturing bonds. Before noon the
rumour was confirmed. The company had suspended. Its road was
burdened with debt, its receipts had been overstated, and its
expected consolidation with the Central line had never advanced beyond
an interested director's conversation.

The shares fell to seventy.

Mr. Jones hurried to Perkins.

"Sell my stock!"

"At what price?"

"Any thing above sixty-five."

"There are no buyers."

"No buyers!"

"Not at present."

"Yesterday you told me men who ought to know were
buying."

"They were."

"And what are they doing to-day?"

"Trying to sell."

By three o'clock the stock stood at forty-eight. On the
following morning it was offered at thirty-five, and within a
week it became nearly worthless.

Mr. Jones had lost more than four thousand dollars.

He did not rub his hands now. His eye-brows were
drawn together; his answers were sharp; and every clerk in
his establishment discovered, before the day ended, that the Western
and Northern Railroad had suspended. One young man was reproved
for using too much sand upon a letter; another for
neglecting to close a door; and a third, who ventured
to ask for an afternoon to attend his sick mother,
was told that business could not be interrupted by every
domestic inconvenience.

Selfishness never suffers alone. Like a poisonous vapour, it spreads
from the heart in which it is generated and clouds
the atmosphere around it.

When Mr. Smith heard of the failure, his first thought
was of Mrs. Lloyd.

"Thank Heaven, her shares were sold!" he exclaimed.

He went at once to tell her that the money
was safe. Tears filled her eyes.

"But for you," she said, "our little property would have
been swept away."

"Your decision was prompt," replied Mr. Smith. "I deserve very
little credit."

"We know to whom the credit belongs," said the eldest
daughter, with a grateful smile.

The sum obtained from the shares, after deducting charges, amounted
to a little more than four thousand five hundred dollars.
Mr. Smith had already heard of a modest brick house
in a respectable street. Its lower front had formerly been
occupied by a milliner; behind the shop were two comfortable
rooms; and the chambers above would afford sufficient accommodation for
the family. The owner, wishing to remove westward, offered the
property for four thousand dollars.

Mr. Smith examined the title, discovered a small defect, required
it to be corrected, and then negotiated the purchase for
thirty-eight hundred dollars. He also obtained six months' credit for
part of the price, leaving Mrs. Lloyd enough ready money
to purchase a careful assortment of dry goods.

During these arrangements, many hours were taken from his own
affairs. Yet they were not lost hours. He carried to
his home each evening the pleasing consciousness of having done
good; and this consciousness softened his voice, brightened his countenance,
and made his wife and children happier.

"How much trouble you take for Mrs. Lloyd," said his
wife one evening.

"She has no one else to take it."

"Then you must continue," was the warm reply. "I will
call upon her to-morrow. Perhaps I can aid the girls
in arranging their new home."

Thus does benevolence propagate itself. A generous act is rarely
solitary. It awakens sympathy as a lamp communicates its flame,
losing none of its own brightness while increasing the light
around it.

Mrs. Smith called upon the widow. Her daughters accompanied her
on a second visit. Curtains were altered, shelves were covered,
old furniture was repaired, and the little shop began to
assume a cheerful appearance. One friend recommended a seamstress; another
promised custom; a third sent a parcel of ribbons to
be sold on commission.

At length the store was opened. Above the door appeared,
in modest letters—

**MRS. LLOYD & DAUGHTERS
DRY GOODS AND FANCY ARTICLES**

There was no brilliant display, no costly stock, no attempt
to rival wealthy establishments. But the goods had been selected
with judgment; the prices were fair; and the widow's gentle
manners won confidence. Ellen's taste made the little window attractive,
while Mary's bright face became a pleasant object to all
who entered.

The first day's sales were small.

"We must not be discouraged," said Mrs. Lloyd.

On the second day they were larger. Before the end
of the month, the business promised to support the family
and pay the deferred portion of the purchase money.

"Does it pay?" Mrs. Smith asked her husband, smiling, after
they had spent a cheerful evening with the Lloyds.

"Yes," he replied. "Better than I expected."

Meanwhile, Mr. Jones had sought consolation for his loss in
the twelve cases of cloth obtained from Armor.

"There, at least," he said, "I am secure."

But even this satisfaction was not without alloy. The circumstances
of the transaction became known. Armor had spoken of it
to no one in anger; yet his necessities compelled him
to explain to two creditors why he had parted with
so large an amount of property without obtaining money. The
story passed from one counting-room to another.

"Jones drove a hard bargain," said one merchant.

"A shameful bargain," said another.

"He had the right to demand payment."

"A legal right, perhaps."

"And what more does business require?"

"Character requires something more."

This distinction Mr. Jones had never learned.

A few weeks later, Captain Jackson arrived from the Mediterranean
with a valuable cargo of oils, fruits, silks, and other
merchandise. He was part owner of the vessel and had
authority to select a house to receive the goods on
consignment. The commissions would amount to several thousand dollars.

Mr. Jones had long dealt with Jackson and confidently expected
the business. He met the captain upon the wharf with
unusual cordiality.

"Glad to see you! Fine passage?"

"Fair enough," replied Jackson.

"Cargo in good condition?"

"Excellent."

"When shall we begin entering the invoices?"

The captain looked at him.

"We shall not trouble you with them this voyage."

"Not trouble me! What do you mean?"

"I have placed the consignment elsewhere."

"Elsewhere! With whom?"

"Armor."

"Armor!" Mr. Jones could scarcely believe his ears. "The man
is embarrassed."

"He has passed through embarrassment without dishonour."

"He cannot command the capital."

"He has friends who can."

"And you take your business from an old correspondent to
give it to him?"

"I do."

"May I ask why?"

Captain Jackson's weather-browned face assumed a grave expression.

"You may. When my vessel was detained here two years
ago, Armor rendered me a service for which he would
accept no compensation. Since then I have watched his course.
He has been unfortunate, but upright. I also heard how
you used his necessity to obtain twenty-four hundred dollars' worth
of goods for fifteen hundred."

"It was a lawful transaction."

"Very likely."

"He came to me of his own accord."

"Necessity drove him."

"I took a risk."

"You took an advantage."

"Captain Jackson, do you presume to instruct me in business?"

"No. I merely choose the man with whom I transact
my own."

"You will regret it."

"I think not. Goods may be intrusted to a poor
man whose honour is rich. They are not always safe
with a rich man whose humanity is poor."

The captain turned away.

Mr. Jones stood upon the wharf, his face burning with
anger. The lost commission would have more than repaired his
railroad loss. He had sacrificed it for the apparent gain
of nine hundred dollars.

For what had his bargain paid?

He returned to his counting-room and shut the door with
violence. Armor's subsequent success added bitterness to his mortification. The
consignment restored that merchant's credit; his knowledge and industry enabled
him to dispose of the cargo advantageously; and within a
year he stood again upon firm ground. Men trusted him
because adversity had proved his integrity. Mr. Jones, who had
imagined that money alone constituted commercial strength, was compelled to
see that character is often the most productive capital a
man can possess.

But the lesson did not improve him. Misfortune humbles only
those who are willing to examine themselves. Mr. Jones blamed
Perkins, the railroad directors, Captain Jackson, Armor, the market, and
the times. He blamed every one but Jones.

At home his temper became a daily affliction. His wife
learned to observe his face before speaking. His children ceased
their play when they heard his step in the passage.
If the dinner were late, he complained; if it were
early, he was not ready. A small account from the
dress-maker provoked a lecture upon extravagance; a request for a
book was answered by a statement of his recent losses.

One evening his little daughter approached him with a flower
she had cultivated in a broken cup.

"See, father! It opened to-day."

"Take it away," he said. "Do not trouble me with
such nonsense."

The child's lip quivered. She carried the flower to her
mother, who kissed her in silence.

At that very hour, Mr. Smith's parlour presented a different
scene. Mrs. Lloyd and her daughters were there. The first
quarter's business had proved better than expected, and the widow
had brought a small sum toward repaying an advance made
by Mr. Smith for the fitting up of the store.

"You must keep it," he said.

"But the money is yours."

"You will need it to increase your stock."

"We can spare it."

"Then spend a part in making the chambers more comfortable.
Winter is near."

Mrs. Lloyd's eyes became humid.

"How shall we ever repay you?"

"You have repaid me already."

The youngest girl looked up.

"How, Mr. Smith?"

"By giving us three new friends—and their mother."

There was a warm smile upon every face. The children
gathered around Mary; Mrs. Smith drew the widow nearer to
the fire; and the conversation flowed with that easy confidence
which springs from mutual kindness. No costly entertainment graced the
table, but contentment sat at the board. No gain was
entered in Mr. Smith's ledger, yet he was richer in
affection, gratitude, and inward peace.

Through the wall that divided the houses, a faint sound
of laughter reached Mr. Jones. He sat gloomy and alone,
though his family was around him. His wife attempted a
remark, but received no answer. His son bent over a
book; the little girl guarded her flower from her father's
displeased eye.

"What are those people making such a noise about?" he
muttered.

"They have company," said Mrs. Jones.

"Smith is always wasting his time upon company."

"They seem happy."

"Happy!" He pushed back his chair. "What does happiness amount
to? Will it pay?"

No one answered.

There are questions which answer themselves.

Let us now strike the balance.

Mr. Jones refused two hours to a widow because charity
would not pay. He employed those hours in purchasing doubtful
stock and taking advantage of a distressed merchant. The stock
became worthless; the cruel bargain injured his reputation; and a
profitable consignment passed from his hands. He had gained cloths
and lost confidence. He had saved time and thrown away
peace. He had preserved his money from the claims of
humanity, only to surrender it to speculation and distrust.

Mr. Smith gave freely of his time and experience. He
saved a widow's little property, placed a family in the
way of honest independence, strengthened the bond between his own
household and worthy friends, and carried into his home the
sunshine of a benevolent spirit. His wife loved him more
for his goodness; his children learned generosity from his example;
and those whom he aided blessed his name.

Which transaction paid?

There are men like Mr. Jones in every community. Their
first inquiry is not, "Is this right?" or, "Will this
relieve suffering?" but, "Will it pay?" They weigh friendship against
interest, duty against convenience, and human tears against gold. If
no money can be seen at the end of an
action, they pronounce it useless. Yet selfishness is a monster
that consumes the very treasure it promises to guard. It
casts its gigantic shadow over the household, chills affection, narrows
sympathy, and often destroys even the worldly prosperity for which
honour and humanity were sacrificed.

Reader, you also keep an account, whether it is written
or not. Every day brings its gains and losses. A
kind word may yield no interest at the bank, but
it may return to you after many years in love.
An hour given to the troubled may seem taken from
business, yet it may purchase a memory more precious than
gold. A hard bargain may add dollars to your purse
while subtracting confidence from your name.

When, therefore, some claim of humanity is presented, do not
ask only, with Mr. Jones, "Will it pay?" Ask rather
what coin you are willing to receive. If you seek
merely money, kindness will often appear unprofitable. But if you
value friendship, peace of conscience, household affection, virtuous integrity of
character, and the gratitude of hearts made happier by your
hand, then do good whenever opportunity invites you.

It will pay.

This is not a baseball story.

I make that statement at the start because there are
people—chiefly women who have never seen a ninth inning with
two out and the tying run dancing off third—who flee
at the first mention of a batting average. They will
sit up until two in the morning to learn whether
a duke marries a governess, but ask them to discover
whether a left-handed hitter can solve a southpaw's curve, and
they remember an engagement elsewhere.

So this is not a baseball story. It is the
story of a young woman's heart, which is a much
more complicated article and has caused more errors than any
ball ever knocked between short and third.

Baseball merely happens to be mixed up in it from
beginning to end.

Our town had a professional club in those days, if
you are liberal with the word professional. The owners were
professional; that is to say, they expected to make money.
The players were professional in June and July, but in
winter they became plumbers, brakemen, cigar clerks, piano movers, insurance
agents, and, in one particularly melancholy case, a teacher of
penmanship.

The park stood beyond the car barns, between the glue
factory and a strip of river that the Chamber of
Commerce called scenic. On Saturday afternoons it was our Ascot,
our Grand Prix, our Roman circus and our Congress of
Nations. Everybody went. Judge Belden went and yelled "Robber!" at
the umpire, after which on Monday he fined a Lithuanian
laborer three dollars for contempt of court. Mrs. Van Slyke
went in lavender silk and pretended not to understand the
score. The mayor went because the club president supplied his
cigars. Even Reverend Peek attended occasionally, though he called it
"studying the recreational temptations of the young."

There were temptations.

Our shortstop, Pug Mullen, had arms like an orangutan and
a disposition that improved only when he was asleep. Chick
Haney, the second baseman, could catch a ball in any
position except when it was hit directly at him. Otto
Plimke, who played first, weighed two hundred and fifty pounds
before breakfast and was considered fast because he once stole
a base while the opposing catcher had fainted.

And then there was Rudie Schlachweiler.

You may pause over the name. We did. The girls,
after two weeks, shortened it to Rudie, and the sporting
editor printed it as Schlockwiler, Schlaweiler, Schlachwiler, and once, during
a labor dispute at the newspaper office, as Rudolph Smith.

But no printer could injure Rudie's appearance.

He was tall, yellow-haired, blue-eyed, broad in the shoulder and
narrow where the modern young man is supposed to be
narrow. In uniform he resembled a blonde god engaged temporarily
by the management to pitch Tuesdays and Fridays. His cap
sat on the back of his head. His sleeves ended
above two capable forearms. He had a slow, melancholy smile
which hinted at buried sorrows, although his deepest known grief
was that he could not get a ten-dollar advance from
the club treasurer.

He could pitch, too.

Not always, of course. If Rudie had always pitched as
well as he looked, Boston would have had him before
breakfast. But on the afternoons when his curve behaved and
the umpire was not, according to Rudie, "blind in both
lamps," he made visiting batters wave at the atmosphere until
they grew homesick.

Now for the story.

Ivy Keller came home from school in June.

By school I mean Miss Dorrington's Select Academy for Young
Ladies, a place where the daughters of prosperous grocers and
unsuccessful bankers acquired French accents, silver photograph frames, and a
permanent inability to eat corn from the cob in public.
Ivy had been away two years. When she returned she
brought three trunks, five hats, a framed picture of Saint
Cecilia, a mandolin she could not play, and a manner
suggesting that our town was a railway accident from which
she hoped soon to recover.

Before school Ivy had been a brown, active child who
climbed fences and traded buttons with boys. After school she
was pale and deliberate. She said "Really?" with her eyebrows.
She read books whose heroines died from misunderstandings in Italian
gardens. She wore a white shirtwaist with a black ribbon
at the throat and stood at windows as though expecting
Jean Valjean to come up the gravel walk.

Her father, Henry Keller, watched these improvements with alarm.

Henry owned the Keller Hardware and Implement Company, was vice-president
of the Citizens' Bank, treasurer of the Volunteer Firemen's Picnic
Fund, chairman of the Street-Oiling Committee, and an alternate delegate
to nearly everything. He was a round, energetic man who
believed there was no human difficulty that could not be
settled by a committee of three and a written agreement.

Mrs. Keller believed in rest cures, imported face powder, and
letting Henry settle things.

On Ivy's third evening home, her father said, "Well, girlie,
how does it seem to be back?"

Ivy looked toward the sunset, which was doing its best
over the glue factory.

"So still," she sighed. "So narrow. One misses stimulation."

"Band concert Thursday," said Henry. "And they're putting a new
roof on the Methodist church."

Ivy gave him the patient smile of Madame de Staël
listening to a child describe a mud pie.

Two days later Undine Harcourt came to see her.

Undine had not been to a select academy, but nature
had selected her with unusual care. She was red-haired, cheerful,
and constructed along lines that made young men lose the
thread of whatever they were saying. She took one glance
at Saint Cecilia, another at Ivy, and said:

"Come on. We're going to the game."

"The game?"

"The ball game."

"I don't care for violent sports."

"You never saw one."

"I have read of them."

"That ain't the same. I've read of Paris, but nobody's
asked me to leave the Louvre yet. Put on a
hat."

Thus, at three-fifteen on a hot Saturday afternoon, Ivy Keller
entered our ball park.

She went languidly. She sat languidly. She opened her parasol
languidly. During the first half of the first inning she
asked whether the men in gray were convicts and why
the crowd disliked the umpire before he had done anything.

Then Rudie Schlachweiler walked out of the dugout.

He wore our white uniform, which was not precisely white,
our groundskeeper being an old enemy of soap. The sun
struck his hair. He picked up the ball, rubbed it
between his palms, and looked toward the plate with that
remote, tragic gaze which in fact meant he had forgotten
the catcher's signal.

"Who is that?" Ivy asked.

"Rudie Schlachweiler."

"What position does he play?"

"He pitches."

"What does that mean?"

Undine explained.

Rudie wound up. His body swung in a smooth, powerful
arc; his arm came over; the ball snapped into the
catcher's mitt.

"Strike!" cried the umpire.

Ivy's parasol closed.

"Was that good?"

"Pretty good."

"Does he always do it like that? Why does the
batter stand so far back? What is a curve? Why
are they shouting 'Give him the dark one'? Is the
catcher signaling? What happens if he hits it? Who is
the man near first base? Why has Rudie turned around?
What's a balk? Was that a strike? It looked like
a strike. That was certainly a strike!"

"Ball two," said the umpire.

"Robber!" cried Ivy Keller.

Her education was complete.

By the fourth inning she knew that Pug Mullen was
playing too deep, that Chick Haney ought to be benched,
and that the visiting pitcher had nothing but a fast
ball and an unpleasant face. In the sixth, when Rudie
struck out three men, she stood on her seat and
applauded with both hands over her head. In the eighth
she had an argument with a butcher from Ward Three
about the infield-fly rule. In the ninth, with two runners
on, Rudie caused the opposing cleanup man to lift a
weak fly to center, and Ivy made a sound not
taught at Miss Dorrington's.

Our club won, four to two.

As Rudie walked off the field, dusty, tired, and Olympian,
he glanced toward the grandstand. Now Rudie glanced toward the
grandstand after every game. It was a habit, like breathing.
But this time his eye encountered Ivy's, and Ivy turned
pink from her collar to the roots of her cultivated
hair.

"I want to meet him," she said.

Undine smiled.

"I thought maybe you would."

There are introductions which require diplomacy, written references, and the
intervention of an elderly aunt. There are others which require
only a red-haired girl who knows the catcher.

That evening Ivy met Rudie at Lempke's ice-cream garden.

Out of uniform, Rudie was still handsome, although less like
Adonis and more like a young man who had paid
six dollars for a blue serge suit. He bowed from
the waist, knocked over a spoon, and said he had
noticed Ivy in the stand.

"Your control was splendid in the sixth," she replied.

Rudie blinked.

"Yessir—yes, ma'am. I had the old hook breaking."

"You wasted two pitches on Daley in the seventh."

"He crowds the plate."

"But he couldn't have hit your fast ball."

Rudie looked at her with deepening admiration. Most girls told
him his eyes were blue. Here was one who understood
that Daley could not hit a fast ball above his
hands.

Within a week Rudie called at the Keller house.

Henry received him in the parlor under Saint Cecilia and
asked what line of business he followed.

"I'm with the club," Rudie said.

"Yes, yes. Summer work. What's your regular line?"

"I pitch."

"Certainly. But what do you do?"

"That's what I do."

Henry rubbed his chin. "All year?"

"No, sir. Season closes in September."

"And then?"

"I generally pick up something."

"What?"

"Whatever's open."

This answer did not satisfy a man who carried accident
insurance on his wheelbarrow.

Nevertheless, Rudie returned Thursday. On Sunday he took Ivy walking.
On Tuesday he appeared with chocolates. On Friday he brought
a photograph of himself in uniform, signed across the front
in a large hand:

To Ivy, the Best Little Rooter in the League, from
Her Friend Rudie.

Ivy placed it beside Saint Cecilia.

Mrs. Keller objected first.

"He has no prospects," she told Ivy.

"He has a wonderful drop."

"That is not what I mean."

"He'll go up."

"Up where?"

"To a major-league club, naturally."

Mrs. Keller had visions of Rudie being promoted to the
roof of something and retired with a headache.

Henry objected next. His objections were organized, numbered, and delivered
after dinner.

"In the first place," he said, "the young fellow has
no permanent position. In the second place, he has no
capital. In the third place, his name would cost a
fortune on engraved stationery. In the fourth place—"

"Papa, I love him."

That scattered the agenda.

The truth, if we must drag truth into a love
story, was that Ivy loved him from the pitcher's box
outward. She loved his windup, his sweeping curve, the little
cloud of dust his spikes raised when he pivoted, the
careless way he accepted applause, and the sporting page's occasional
reference to "Schlachweiler, our sterling slab artist." Whether she loved
Rudie when he was chewing a toothpick in an ordinary
chair was another matter, but no one thought to ask
it.

Not even Rudie.

He was genuinely in love with Ivy. Her white dresses,
her grave eyes, her ability to pronounce schedule with a
faint trace of Miss Dorrington's eastern accent—all these got his
goat completely.

He tried to speak of romance.

One evening on the Keller porch he said, "Ivy, do
you ever think about—well, about us?"

"Yes," she said eagerly. "I think if you'd use your
change of pace oftener against left-hand batters, you'd save yourself
a lot of trouble."

"I mean after the season."

"So do I. Scouts will be watching you in August."

"Suppose I wasn't pitching?"

"But you will be."

"Suppose I got hurt."

Ivy's face became tragic. "Your arm?"

"No, I ain't hurt. I only said suppose."

"You mustn't even speak of it."

Rudie, touched by what he believed was concern for his
person, took her hand. Ivy allowed it, though at that
moment she was wondering whether the manager would let Rudie
start both games of Monday's double-header.

His visits multiplied.

On Monday Henry coughed in the library. On Wednesday he
walked through the parlor twice looking for a newspaper he
held under his arm. On Friday he sat down between
Ivy and Rudie and discussed retail prices in barbed wire
until Rudie left. On Sunday Rudie came at two and
remained until ten-fifteen, surviving lemonade, supper, a thunderstorm, Mrs. Keller's
neuralgia, and Henry's detailed account of the new municipal sewer.

At ten-sixteen Henry held a family conference.

"He's got to quit coming here," he said.

"I shall see him if I choose," Ivy replied.

"You're nineteen."

"Twenty in January."

"Then in January you can make twenty-year-old mistakes."

"Henry," murmured Mrs. Keller.

"I mean it. That young man earns—what does he earn?"

Ivy lifted her chin. "One hundred and seventy-five dollars a
month."

"For four months."

"Five, if they make the postseason series."

Henry made a noise like a man discovering rust in
a shipment of tools.

"There! You see? A hundred and seventy-five when it doesn't
rain, when his arm doesn't go bad, when the club
doesn't fold, and when somebody doesn't knock him out of
the box in the second inning. Marriage is a double-header
every day, and there ain't any rain checks."

"You don't understand him."

"I understand installment payments."

"You judge everything by money."

"Not everything. I judge ball players partly by earned-run average."

Ivy swept from the room.

The following week Rudie proposed.

He did it by the river, beneath a willow tree,
with a freight train switching behind the flour mill. The
surroundings were not Italian, but Rudie supplied emotion enough for
Lake Como.

"Ivy," he said, "you're the only girl I ever seen
that was different."

This was inaccurate, but sincere.

She looked at him. He was wearing his road uniform
because the club train left in an hour. The gray
cloth fitted his shoulders; his cap was tucked into his
pocket; the clean afternoon light touched his hair. He looked
like Romance pitching the deciding game of the World's Series.

"Yes," Ivy whispered.

"Will you marry me?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Whenever you say."

Rudie kissed her, and the freight engine contributed a long,
scandalized whistle.

Henry's response, upon receiving the news, lacked poetry.

"No."

"If you refuse," Ivy said, "I shall leave home."

Mrs. Keller became Little Eva without the cough. She lay
on a sofa and spoke of mothers' graves, broken hearts,
and the dangers of night trains.

Henry said nothing for several minutes.

That worried Ivy. She was accustomed to his words and
knew how to dodge them. Silence indicated machinery at work.

Finally he said, "You're determined?"

"I am."

"You believe you'll feel the same in six months?"

"Always."

"And this has nothing to do with his uniform and
the cheering and his picture in the paper?"

Ivy froze him with a Dorrington look.

"How can you be so vulgar?"

"Practice," said Henry. "Now listen. I won't lock you up,
and I won't have your mother dying all over the
furniture. I propose an agreement."

"This is not a business matter."

"Everything's a business matter if it keeps people from acting
like lunatics. The season ends October first. You wait until
then. After the season you don't see him, write him,
telephone him, send him picture postal cards, or get messages
through Undine for one full month."

Ivy started to protest.

Henry raised one hand. It was the hand with which
he had silenced the Street-Oiling Committee.

"On November first I'll take you to see him wherever
he is. If you still want him, and he still
wants you, I withdraw my objections. I'll give you a
wedding, furniture, and enough money to start. Is that fair?"

Ivy considered. A month was eternity, but victory stood beyond
it in orange blossoms.

"Will you give your word?"

"I'll put it in writing."

"Then I agree."

Rudie disliked the arrangement, but accepted it. The club was
in fourth place, he had lost three games in succession,
and Henry's promise of furniture possessed a solid sound.

The remainder of the season was an opera in nine
innings.

When Rudie won, Ivy saw destiny unfolding. When he lost,
she blamed the catcher, the sun, the wind, the umpire,
the infield, the manager, and once the shape of the
pitcher's box. She kept his clippings in a blue album.
She learned his record, his strikeouts, his walks, and his
batting average, which last item was not worth learning.

On the final day Rudie pitched a shutout.

The crowd poured onto the field. Boys followed him toward
the clubhouse. Men pounded his back. Ivy, watching from the
stand, saw him carried through a golden haze of dust
and applause like a conquering knight, if a conquering knight
may be imagined in striped stockings with a rip at
one knee.

That picture remained with her through October.

She did not see him. To her credit, she obeyed
the agreement. She wrote no letters. When Undine attempted to
mention that Rudie had taken winter work in another town,
Ivy stopped her.

"I do not want to know. Papa's test is absurd,
but I shall meet it honorably."

She spent the month choosing, in a detached and maidenly
way, the sort of apartment she and Rudie might occupy.
There would be pennants in his study, photographs of great
players, and perhaps a baby named Matthewson if it was
a boy. She pictured herself traveling with him when he
entered the major leagues, sitting in boxes in New York,
Philadelphia, and Chicago, calm amid fashionable throngs while thousands murmured,
"That is Schlachweiler's wife."

At night she dreamed of white diamonds under the sun
and Rudie winding up against a sky full of flags.

November first arrived gray and wet.

Henry brought the automobile around at nine. It was a
red touring car with brass lamps and the temperament of
Pug Mullen. Mrs. Keller packed sandwiches as if they were
crossing the Andes. Ivy wore a dark blue traveling coat
and a hat wide enough to influence traffic.

"Where are we going?" she asked.

"Bellford."

"Is he there?"

"Yup."

"What is he doing?"

"You'll see."

Bellford was forty miles away. The roads were deep, the
wind was sharp, and Henry discussed carburetors. Ivy scarcely heard
him. In her mind Rudie was perhaps coaching at an
athletic club, perhaps consulting with a major-league scout, perhaps keeping
fit in a gymnasium where other heroes recognized him as
their equal.

At noon they entered Bellford's business district.

Henry stopped before the Regal Shoe Emporium.

It was a narrow shop with two gilt boots hanging
over the door and a window full of patent-leather shoes
arranged around a plaster swan. A sign announced:

FALL FOOTWEAR EVENT
COURTESY—COMFORT—CORRECT FITTING

"Why are we stopping here?" Ivy asked.

"Thought I'd look at some shoes," Henry said.

"Now?"

"Good a time as any."

He went in. Ivy followed, first with impatience, then with
suspicion.

The shop smelled of leather, floor wax, and feet nobly
endured for commercial purposes. Three clerks moved among the chairs.
One was bald, one had a mustache, and one stood
at the rear in a black alpaca coat.

Then the third clerk turned.

It was Rudie.

For one second nobody moved.

He had no cap tilted above his yellow hair. His
hair was parted carefully and plastered down. There was no
white uniform, no gray road suit, no stitched town name
across his chest. The black shop coat hung from his
shoulders and buttoned high at the throat. A tape measure
drooped from his pocket. In one hand he held a
shoehorn.

The blonde god of summer rubbed his hands together.

"How-do?" he said.

Ivy stared.

Rudie's face flamed with joy. "Ivy! Gee, I been counting
the days!"

The bald clerk looked up. The mustached clerk smiled as
clerks do when romance enters during a slow hour.

Rudie hurried forward.

"Well!" said Henry. "Schlachweiler! Didn't know you were here."

Henry was a liar, but a civic one.

"Yessir, Mr. Keller. Started two weeks ago. Mr. Brodsky says
I got a natural way with the trade." Rudie turned
to Ivy. "You look swell. I mean—you look just the
same."

He reached for her hand. She gave it to him
automatically.

His fingers were dusty with shoe powder.

There are moments when love triumphs over poverty, social difference,
bad tailoring, parental opposition, and a spot of grease on
the vest. Poets have made a living out of such
moments.

This was not one of them.

Ivy looked at the shoehorn. She looked at the tape
measure. She looked at Rudie's deferential shop smile—a smile meant
to assure the public that no bunion was too grotesque
for respectful attention.

Behind him waited a stout woman with one stockinged foot
planted on a velvet rest.

"Rudolph," called the bald clerk, "finish with the lady's fitting."

"Just a minute, Mr. Brodsky."

"No hurry," said the stout woman, though her tone promised
that there soon would be.

Rudie laughed nervously. "I'm working here till spring. Pretty good
job. Nine a week and commission. Sold four pairs yesterday."

Ivy's eyes widened by the smallest degree.

"Nine?"

"Going to be twelve when I learn the stock. Say,
you folks sit down. I'll be through in a jiffy."

He returned to the stout woman.

Ivy watched Rudie Schlachweiler, conqueror of the Mill City Champions,
kneel on the carpet.

He lifted the woman's foot. He pressed the toe of
the shoe. He asked whether it pinched across the instep.
He removed it, selected another box, and said, "This model
runs a shade broader, madam."

The transformation was complete.

A uniform, you understand, is not clothing. It is scenery.
Put a man in white flannel before five thousand shouting
citizens and he may become Hector, Galahad, and the favored
son of destiny. Put the same man in an alpaca
coat before one impatient woman with a difficult instep, and
he becomes a fellow named Rudolph who knows the stock.

Rudie glanced up and smiled.

It was the same melancholy smile that had caused three
girls in Section B to save his scorecards.

Ivy shuddered.

Henry examined a display of work boots with the intense
innocence of a burglar reading Scripture.

Rudie finished the fitting, wrapped the shoes, wrote the sales
slip, and came hurrying back.

"Now," he said, "we can talk. I get an hour
for dinner. There's a dandy little chop place down the
street. Ivy, I got a whole lot to tell you.
About us, I mean."

"Us?" Ivy repeated.

"Sure. Your pa said after the month—"

"Oh." She looked toward the window. "There has been a
misunderstanding."

Rudie stopped rubbing his hands.

"What misunderstanding?"

"I did not know we were coming here. Papa wished
to buy shoes. We are merely passing through."

Henry dropped a boot.

"But, Ivy—"

"I am afraid we must leave at once."

Rudie looked at Henry, then at Ivy. His face was
no longer a pitcher's face or a clerk's face, but
simply that of a young man who had waited a
month and did not understand why waiting had failed.

"I thought you came to see me."

Ivy adjusted one glove.

"How could you think that?"

"Because—well, because you said—"

The bald clerk called, "Rudolph! Customer!"

Rudie did not move.

Ivy walked toward the door. Henry followed, pausing long enough
to say, "Good day, son," in a voice stripped of
all victory.

The bell over the shop door jingled behind them.

They rode the first ten miles in silence.

At last Henry said, "Want a sandwich?"

"No."

"Cold?"

"No."

"Car running pretty good."

Ivy turned on him. "Papa, I think it was contemptible
of you."

Henry kept his eyes on the road.

"Maybe."

"You planned it."

"Yup."

"You wanted to humiliate him."

"No."

"You did!"

"I wanted you to see him."

"I had seen him hundreds of times."

"No," Henry said. "You'd seen a pitcher."

Ivy's chin trembled. For a moment he thought she might
cry. Then she looked away and did not speak again
until they reached home.

She removed Rudie's photograph from beside Saint Cecilia that night.
She did not destroy it. That would have been romantic.
She put it in the blue album, shut the album,
and placed it at the bottom of a trunk beneath
her school emblems and the mandolin.

Rudie wrote twice. Ivy returned the first letter unopened and
answered the second with four courteous lines that would have
chilled a furnace. After that he wrote no more.

Winter passed.

Henry never mentioned Bellford. He had won, but he took
no pleasure in the score. Mrs. Keller announced that Ivy
had recovered from her "nervous infatuation," and Ivy began attending
lectures, charity teas, and meetings of the Browning Circle. If
baseball was mentioned, she became interested in something across the
room.

Then spring came, as it generally does even after a
fourth-place season.

One May evening Henry sat on the porch reading the
sporting page. Ivy was sewing something pale and useless beside
him.

"Well," he said casually, "see where Rudie's been traded to
Grand Rapids."

Ivy's needle did not pause.

"Indeed?"

"Yup. Pitched yesterday. Beat Lansing two to one. Struck out
eleven."

There was a small silence.

"Always said the boy had stuff," Henry continued. "When his
control's right, he's about as good a pitcher as you'll
find in this league."

Ivy bit off her thread.

"Piffle," she said. "He never had a thing but a
roundhouse curve. He can't hold runners, he fields his position
like a cigar-store Indian, and any club that gives up
two hits in an inning will knock him out of
the box. He's a hopeless dub."

Henry lowered the newspaper and looked at her.

Ivy resumed her sewing.

And that, as our best talent would put it, was
the end of her first great love.

Also, in a manner of speaking, the end of the
baseball story which this was not.

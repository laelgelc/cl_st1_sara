## The Olive's Errand

The olive fell at dinner on the fourth evening of
his holiday.

It fell, moreover, from the fork of a young woman
seated three tables away; and since the dining-room floor was
of polished parquet, inclined perhaps by age, earthquakes, or the
management's economical disregard of levels, the olive did not remain
where Providence—or clumsiness—had dropped it. It rolled.

He watched it pass beneath a waiter's shoe, avoid the
chair of a German widow, hesitate beside a potted palm,
and emerge again into the open with a moist black
gleam. Then, gathering courage from the slope, it crossed the
intervening space in a deliberate curve and came to rest
against his left shoe.

It was a small thing, this journey of an olive.
Yet he had been alone for three weeks, overworked for
eleven months, and professionally occupied for fifteen years with substances
that behaved according to law. He therefore regarded the olive
with an attention out of all proportion to its size.

The girl looked across at him.

She was English; that he had already decided. The pale
linen frock, the untroubled shoulders, the sun-browned arms and the
air of having been born accustomed to foreign waiters all
proclaimed it. She sat with an older couple whose conversation
appeared to concern railway strikes, the ruinous rate of exchange,
and a leather box bought that afternoon from a persuasive
shopkeeper. Until now he had noticed chiefly her stillness. She
listened without listening, and her eyes wandered often toward the
open windows, beyond which the evening sea lay purple and
metallic beneath the hills.

Now she caught his glance, saw the olive by his
shoe, and smiled.

There was no apology in the smile. There was delight—frank,
sudden, intimate delight—as if the olive had performed a trick
they alone understood.

He stooped and picked it up.

The proper thing would have been to summon the waiter.
A more adventurous man might have carried it to her
plate with some neat remark. He was neither proper nor
adventurous at that instant. He merely held the olive between
finger and thumb and looked at it.

It was shiny, damp, and slightly misshapen. One end had
been pierced by her fork. A ridiculous object; juicy, black,
lob-sided, and fragrant with oil.

"Thank you," she said across the room.

The words were scarcely audible, but he heard them.

He bowed. Then, Heaven alone knows why, he put the
olive into his waistcoat pocket.

The girl's smile widened before she turned back to the
older couple.

He finished his dinner without tasting it.

The hotel stood above a small town on the Italian
Riviera, a white and ochre building with striped awnings, inadequate
plumbing, and a terrace from which the management claimed that
Corsica could be seen on clear mornings. No one had
yet seen it. The guests were chiefly English people engaged
in recovering from England. They rose dutifully, consulted guidebooks, complained
of the coffee, walked to prescribed viewpoints, and returned with
mimosa they had bought from children on the road.

He had joined them because his doctor had used the
expression "complete change." The doctor, who had never entered a
laboratory after leaving medical school, blamed overwork. He himself blamed
the directors, two incompetent assistants, the failure of a catalytic
process, and a peculiar weariness which had begun in the
nerves behind his eyes and spread until even pleasure seemed
an appointment he had forgotten to cancel.

In London he occupied two rooms near the laboratory. He
ate at a club where nobody knew him well enough
to interrupt his newspaper. His relations were affectionate and distant.
Women had occurred in his life as possible arrangements which,
on examination, proved chemically unstable.

He was forty-one and lonely, though he preferred the word
independent.

On the Riviera he had attempted rest with professional thoroughness.
He bathed before breakfast, walked after luncheon, slept from three
until four, and read in the evening. His reading included
an expensive guidebook, a detective novel left in the lounge,
and a volume of Freud purchased at the station because
its paper cover promised an explanation of dreams.

Freud had explained much. Freud had explained too much. By
the fourth chapter his hidden nature appeared to consist entirely
of childhood, embarrassment, and objects with an unsuspected anatomical importance.
He persevered because he was a chemist and respected theories
even when they were unpleasant.

But that evening he did not read Freud.

He went to his room, placed the olive on the
marble washstand, and examined it.

There it lay under the electric lamp, succulent and absurd.

He told himself that the girl was pretty. He told
himself that any woman who looked at a solitary man
in that way might set his imagination moving. He told
himself that the olive was no more than the accidental
projectile of careless fingers.

Then he touched it.

It seemed still warm.

He withdrew his hand and laughed aloud. The sound startled
him. He had not laughed alone in his room for
years.

From his window he could see the folds of the
hills descending toward the sea, each slope covered with olive
trees. By daylight their leaves had a dry, silvery look,
but under the moon they gathered into a soft dark
mass that moved continually, though the air at the window
was still. Somewhere below, sailors were singing in the harbour.
Their voices rose in a slow phrase, stopped, and began
again.

The girl would be in the lounge, perhaps. He might
go down. The older couple would introduce them. Or there
were civilised devices: a dropped handkerchief, an inquiry about excursions,
a mutual assault upon the hotel coffee.

He did none of these things.

Instead he undressed, put the olive on the table beside
his bed, and opened Freud.

At half-past eleven he read that dreams represented the disguised
fulfilment of suppressed wishes. At twelve he wondered whether a
wish could be suppressed if one had never admitted possessing
it. At a quarter past twelve he turned out the
lamp.

The olive shone faintly in the moonlight.

He slept.

Some time before dawn he heard three soft taps upon
the window.

He woke at once. The room was cold and grey.
The shutters stood open, framing a sky in which the
stars had lost their sharpness.

Three taps came again.

The girl was on the balcony.

This was impossible, since his balcony was three floors above
the terrace and separated from the neighbouring balconies by stone
partitions. Yet there she stood outside the glass door, her
face close to it, one finger lifted to command silence.

He sat up.

She wore a loose white dress, or perhaps it was
only a wrap. Her hair hung about her shoulders. She
was barefoot.

"Come," she whispered.

He opened the door.

"How did you get here?"

"Too late for questions."

"You'll fall."

"I never fall."

"But—"

"Hush! Bring it."

"Bring what?"

Her eyes moved toward the table.

The olive lay there, moist and dark as when it
had crossed the dining-room floor.

He took it.

"That's right," she said. "You understood part of the message,
then."

Before he could answer, she stepped lightly onto the balcony
rail and vanished.

He sprang forward. There was no cry, no white body
dropping through the air. Instead he saw her below on
the terrace, looking up and beckoning.

The intervening descent remained unexplained. He found himself in trousers
and shirt, with shoes but no socks, hurrying down the
hotel stairs. The corridor lamps had been extinguished. From behind
doors came snores, coughs, the mutter of sleep, and once
the clear voice of an elderly woman saying, "Not that
train, Henry," to someone long dead or merely in another
bed.

The front door was locked. The girl stood beside it.

"Slow," she murmured. "You're still thinking."

She touched the lock. It opened.

Outside, the night received them.

The road was white beneath the paling stars. On one
side the land dropped toward the sea; on the other,
terraces climbed into the olive groves. The air smelled of
earth, salt, crushed thyme, and some flower too sweet to
be named. No wind stirred, yet the olive leaves whispered
everywhere.

"Where are we going?" he asked.

"To meet the morning."

"We can do that on the terrace."

"Not this morning."

She slipped her hand into his. Her fingers were cool
and strong.

They left the road by a narrow path between stone
walls. Almost at once the hotel disappeared, although he knew
it stood not two hundred yards behind them. The grove
closed round them—twisted trunks, pale leaves, pockets of darkness, sudden
spills of starlight. He stumbled over roots; she moved without
effort.

"You've no shoes," he said.

"Neither had you once."

"Once when?"

"Before you became so careful."

"I don't remember."

"You remember chemicals."

"Naturally."

"And timetables."

"When necessary."

"And all the things that stop you remembering anything else."

She laughed and ran.

He ran after her.

At first he was conscious of his years, his lungs,
the absurdity of chasing an unknown woman through an Italian
plantation before breakfast. Then the path sloped downward, the grass
brushed his ankles, her white shape flashed between the trees,
and something inside him broke—not painfully, but like a shell
under pressure.

He caught her beside a ruined wall.

She turned, and he took her in his arms.

There was no introduction. There was not even surprise. Her
mouth met his with a fierceness that drove questions from
him. The wall, the trees, the fading stars swept outward.
He felt her laughter against his lips; he felt the
quick warm life of her, the loose hair on his
face, her hands at the back of his neck. They
sank into fragrant grass, and the earth seemed to rise
about them, receiving their weight.

Afterward they lay without speech.

His head rested upon her arm. Above them the olive
branches crossed and recrossed, silver on black. The sea breathed
far below. He could hear her heart, or perhaps it
was his own.

"This is madness," he said at last.

"Of course."

"I don't even know your name."

"You couldn't use it."

"Why not?"

"Not yet."

He lifted himself to look at her. There was light
enough now to see the curve of her cheek and
the brightness of her eyes. She was smiling, but behind
the smile lay something remote, watchful, almost sorrowful.

"Who are you?"

She touched his ears, first one, then the other, as
if testing their shape.

"Not bad."

"What isn't?"

"These. And your eyes are beginning."

"My eyes are beginning what?"

"To see sideways."

He laughed, though uneasiness moved in him.

"You speak as if I were turning into an animal."

"No. Animals don't need to turn."

She sprang up.

From the grove above came the thin sound of a
pipe.

It was no melody he knew. Three notes climbed, doubled
back, and ran lightly downward. The trees seemed to listen.
A second pipe answered from farther off; then a drum
sounded once, softly, like a palm striking hollow wood.

The girl's face changed.

"They're coming!"

"Who?"

"You'll see. If you can."

She pulled him to his feet.

Between the trees a figure passed.

He glimpsed a brown shoulder, tangled hair, the quick backward
turn of a laughing face. Another followed, then two together.
A girl in a green garment ran through the leaves;
behind her bounded a squat, bearded creature with something unmistakably
wrong about its legs.

The chemist stared.

"Goats," he said weakly.

"Not altogether."

More shapes poured into the grove. Young men crowned with
vine leaves; women with bare arms and streaming hair; children—or
things like children—darting in and out among the trunks. Some
carried torches whose pale flames made no smoke. Some shook
little bells. Some were naked and shaggy, with pointed ears;
others shone with a beauty so swift that his eyes
could not hold it.

They greeted the girl with cries.

She answered in a language he did not know and
yet almost understood. Hands touched him as the procession streamed
around them. A wreath was thrust onto his head. Someone
pressed a wooden cup to his mouth. The wine tasted
of resin, honey, sunlight, and danger.

"Come!" cried the girl.

They joined the rush.

They ran downhill through the groves, though presently the path
turned upward. They crossed a stream without wetting their feet,
climbed walls without checking, and passed beneath branches that lifted
out of their way. The pipes multiplied. Drums thudded. Laughter
leapt from terrace to terrace, and the olive trees answered
with a dry ecstatic clapping of leaves.

His body grew light. His laboratory, his club, his physician,
his decent suit folded over a hotel chair—these belonged to
a grey person who had been impersonating him. He flung
out his arms and shouted. No one minded. A creature
with horns seized his hand, spun him round, and kissed
him noisily on both cheeks. A nymph with poppies in
her hair called him brother.

Brother! The word entered him like wine.

For years he had believed himself self-contained. Now he saw
that he had been sealed. The seal was broken. The
world poured in.

The procession swept along a ridge. To the left the
sea spread vast and dark purple; to the right mountains
floated in rose-coloured mist. Ahead, on a ruined hill, stood
the broken village.

He knew it from the guidebook. An earthquake had emptied
it decades earlier. The inhabitants had built a safer town
below and left the old houses to lizards, weeds, tourists,
and memory. During the previous afternoon he had considered visiting
it, but the hotel porter had advised against the road
because of a motor-bus dispute.

Now the place waited under the paling sky.

Roofless houses leaned together. Streets ended in fallen masonry. Figs
grew from cracked walls. At the summit rose the church,
its façade split, its bell tower wounded, its doors gone.

The revelers entered the village more quietly.

Laughter ceased. The pipes softened. They moved through narrow lanes
in a long, swaying line, and the chemist became aware
that dawn was close. It did not merely approach; it
gathered. It stood beyond the eastern hills like a veiled
presence.

The girl held his hand tightly.

"Now you mustn't be afraid," she said.

"Of what?"

"You'll know."

"I'm not afraid."

"You will be."

They entered the church.

The roof had fallen above the nave, leaving a jagged
frame of stone around the sky. Plaster saints stared from
the walls with blind, rain-washed eyes. The altar was split.
Grass grew between the flags, and a young olive tree
had rooted where the sanctuary rail had been.

The company filled the place without sound. Nymphs, fauns, horned
men, bright girls, grave old creatures wrapped in skins—they crowded
among the broken pillars and knelt.

He knelt too.

"Whom are we waiting for?" he whispered.

The girl's face was pale with joy.

"The One who comes back."

A wind moved through the church.

It began no higher than the grass. He saw each
blade bend, though the air against his cheek remained still.
Then the young olive tree trembled. Its leaves flashed silver,
dark, silver, dark. The motion spread outward into the village
and down the hills until grove after grove shivered beneath
the coming light.

The pipes gave a single piercing cry.

From the mountains came an answer.

It was laughter, perhaps. Or thunder. Or the sound of
enormous hooves striking rock far underground.

The earth moved.

Dust sifted from the broken arches. A stone fell somewhere
in the village and shattered. The kneeling creatures bowed until
their foreheads touched the ground.

The chemist tried to bow with them, but terror lifted
his head.

Something stood where the ruined altar had been.

At first he saw only size: a vast brown shape
against the dawn. Then shaggy thighs, a broad chest, the
curve of horns, hands both human and not human. The
face could not be looked at steadily. It was old
as sunlight on stone, young as the first animal leaping;
brutal, merry, tender, and appalling. Leaves tangled in the beard.
The eyes held summer noon and winter starvation, mating beasts,
laughing children, blood in the dust, sap rising, roots splitting
tombs.

The god breathed.

Every creature in the church cried out.

The cry was worship and panic together. It burst from
the chemist's own throat. Love shook him—love so enormous that
it became fear, fear so fierce it broke into joy.

The god lifted a pipe to his lips.

The tune was very small.

Three notes climbed, doubled back, and fell.

Yet at their sound the floor heaved. The shattered tower
rang though no bell hung in it. Across the valley
rocks rolled from the hills. The dawn tore open, pouring
gold and crimson through the roofless church.

The god laughed.

The worshipers fled.

They scattered in wild delight, shrieking through doorways, bounding over
walls, tumbling down the streets. The chemist was dragged with
them. Behind him masonry crashed. Before him the girl ran,
her white dress streaming, her hand locked about his wrist.

"Faster!" she cried. "He's awake!"

They raced from the village into the groves. The earth
pulsed beneath their feet. Trees danced—there was no other word
for it—their twisted trunks swayed, their leaves tossed, and from
each tree seemed to spring a pale, laughing spirit that
ran beside them for a moment before sinking back into
bark.

The company vanished by twos and threes. A horned head
disappeared behind a wall. A green-clad girl dissolved into mimosa.
The drums receded into the ground.

Soon only the two of them were running.

The sun had not yet risen, but the whole sky
burned.

He stumbled. She caught him and pressed him against an
olive trunk.

"It's nearly over," she said.

"No!"

"It must be."

"Come with me."

"I am with you."

"Back to the hotel. Stay. Tell me your name."

She looked at him with that sudden remoteness.

"You'll hear my name after breakfast."

"Then this is a dream."

"If that helps."

"Did you dream it too?"

She smiled.

"You ask so many straight questions. Straight questions are fences."

The sound of the pipe came again, very far away.

Her face sharpened with urgency. She put both hands upon
his shoulders.

"Listen. You must remember me."

"How could I forget?"

"People forget everything at breakfast."

"I won't."

"You'll try to explain. That's nearly the same thing."

He reached for her, but she evaded him and stood
a pace away, bright and elusive among the silver leaves.

"Do you remember the olive?"

He thrust a hand into his pocket. It was there.

"The message," she said.

"What message?"

"That you were ready."

"For you?"

"For play."

"Is that all?"

"No." Her expression softened. "You were half-and-half already."

"Half what?"

She came close, rose on her bare toes, and kissed
him.

"You'll be altogether soon."

The sun broke over the hill.

Light struck his eyes. The grove blazed white and gold.
Her hands slipped from his; the leaves rushed upward like
a flock of birds; the earth dropped—

He awoke in his hotel bed.

It was seven o'clock.

A motor-lorry rattled below the window. Someone in the next
room was shaving and singing "On the Road to Mandalay"
in a damp, courageous baritone. The room smelled of soap
and dust.

He lay motionless.

His clothes were folded on the chair. His shoes stood
beneath it, with socks tucked neatly inside. The glass door
to the balcony was closed.

On the table lay Freud, face downward.

Beside it was the olive.

He picked it up.

It was wrinkled now, and a little dry, but the
fork mark remained. A smear of green stained one side.
He examined his hands and found no dust on them.
His feet bore no scratches. There was, however, a crushed
olive leaf in his hair.

This proved nothing. Olive leaves were everywhere. One had blown
into his room; he had acquired it during yesterday's walk;
the chambermaid had introduced it. Science offered alternatives generously.

He dressed with care.

At breakfast the dining-room was full of sunlight and conventional
noises: cups, newspapers, complaints about eggs. The German widow dissected
a grapefruit. The older English couple occupied their usual table.

The girl was not with them.

His disappointment was so violent that he nearly laughed.

Then she entered.

By daylight she looked younger, more English, and less possible.
She wore a blue dress and carried a letter. Her
hair was neatly arranged. Shoes—entirely ordinary white shoes—covered her feet.

She greeted the older couple and sat down.

A minute later the gentleman rose and approached him.

"My wife tells me we've been rather remiss," he said.
"Fellow-countrymen in the same hotel and all that. I'm Harcourt.
My wife is over there, and our niece, Miss Sylvia
Vale. May we join you for coffee?"

The chemist stood too quickly and knocked his spoon onto
the floor.

Miss Vale looked up.

Their eyes met.

No recognition appeared in her face. There was courtesy, curiosity,
and then—very faintly—that same spontaneous delight.

Introductions were made. Mrs. Harcourt discussed the railway strike. Mr.
Harcourt explained the leather box, which had proved to be
cardboard under the leather. Miss Vale buttered a roll.

"You have been walking already?" she asked the chemist.

"No."

"How lazy of you."

"It is only eight."

"The best things happen before eight."

He stared at her.

She raised her brows. "Don't you agree?"

"I'm not certain what has happened."

"That sounds serious."

Mrs. Harcourt intervened with an account of the church at
the ruined village. Their guidebook declared the excursion indispensable; the
hotel porter declared it impossible; the conflict had occupied the
family since luncheon yesterday.

"Have you been there?" the chemist asked Miss Vale.

"Perhaps."

"My dear," said her aunt, "you have not."

"Then I haven't."

"Did you hear anything in the night?"

Miss Vale lifted her coffee.

"What sort of thing?"

"Music. A pipe."

"No."

"An earthquake?"

Mr. Harcourt laughed. "If there had been one, the management
would have added it to the bill."

The chemist felt himself colouring. Miss Vale watched him over
the rim of her cup.

After breakfast they met on the terrace by accident. The
accident occurred again before luncheon, on the path above the
sea; once more at tea; and a fourth time beneath
the mimosa after dinner. By then even Mrs. Harcourt, whose
mind moved cautiously, had recognised design in the universe.

They walked together the next morning.

Miss Vale talked easily of London, books, Italian children, and
the mysterious incapacity of English tourists to sit under a
tree without consulting a map. Of dreams she said nothing.
When he referred, indirectly and then directly, to their supposed
meeting, she denied it with cheerful firmness.

"I was asleep."

"You're certain?"

"I usually know where I spend the night."

"You said the best things happened before eight."

"They often do. Sunrise, for example. Rolls when they're fresh.
Other people's indiscretions."

"And you called questions fences."

"Did I?"

"In the grove."

She stopped and looked at him.

For a moment her face became remote. The sun flashed
along the sea below them; insects hummed in the grass.

Then she touched his left ear.

"Not bad," she said softly.

His heart lurched.

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing. It is rather pointed, that's all."

She walked on.

Within four days they were in love, though he, being
a chemist, delayed naming the condition until its symptoms were
beyond dispute. He forgot his walking schedule. Freud remained unopened.
He missed luncheon twice and did not care. The laboratory
wrote to him about the catalytic process; he put the
letter in a drawer.

Miss Vale was tender, teasing, swift in mood, and occasionally
absent while standing beside him. She liked sunlight, storms, rough
wine, neglected paths, and music heard at a distance. She
disliked clocks. She could sit for an hour without moving,
then spring up because some leaf had turned in the
wind.

On the seventh evening he asked her to marry him.

They were in the olive grove above the hotel. The
sunset had faded, leaving the trunks black and the leaves
pale. She listened without surprise.

"You're sure?" she asked.

"I have been sure for five thousand years."

He had not intended to say that. The words arrived
of themselves.

Her eyes shone.

"Ah," she whispered. "You do remember."

"Then it was real?"

"I didn't say so."

"You were there."

"I said I was asleep."

"You knew about my ears."

"Anyone can see your ears."

"And the olive?"

At this she laughed, and the whole grove seemed to
stir with her.

He drew the olive from his pocket. It had become
hard and shrivelled, a poor relic after a week in
linen and wool, but he had carried it everywhere.

"May I keep it?"

She took it from him and turned it between her
fingers.

"Moist once," she said. "Shiny. Rather lop-sided."

"The message."

"Yes."

"What did you mean by that?"

She considered him, and for an instant he saw behind
the English girl another shape—barefoot, leaf-crowned, bright with dawn.

"I saw the way you picked it up," she said.
"Most men would have called the waiter. Some would have
sent it back with a joke. You looked at it
as if it had travelled to you on purpose."

"Had it?"

"Of course."

"From you?"

"Through me."

"And you knew I was—what was the phrase?"

"Half-and-half."

He waited, but she offered no explanation.

"Half civilised and half lunatic?"

"Something like that."

"Half chemist and half fool?"

"Nearer."

"Half man and half—"

She put a finger to his lips.

From deep among the trees came three thin notes of
a pipe.

He turned. The grove appeared empty. Far below, the hotel
windows shone in orderly rows; a gong announced dinner; someone
called for a waiter.

When he looked back, she had placed the olive in
his palm and closed his fingers around it.

"Keep it," she said. "You won't need messages much longer."

"Why not?"

She rose on tiptoe and tweaked both his ears.

"Because, my dear," she murmured, "you'll soon be altogether."

Then she kissed him, while the olive trees whispered overhead
and the earth, very gently, moved.

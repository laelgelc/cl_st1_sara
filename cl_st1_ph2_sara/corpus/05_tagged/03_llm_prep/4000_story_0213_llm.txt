# Lily's Journey Through the Lands of Food

## I. The Red Umbrella

Lily was a pale, fretful little girl who loved sugar
better than sunshine, and would rather have eaten six peppermints
than one slice of honest brown bread.

This was unfortunate, because she had a kind mamma, a
warm home, and every reason to be as rosy and
agreeable as a June morning. Yet Lily pushed away her
porridge, complained that milk was dull, declared potatoes tiresome, and
looked mournfully at her bread unless it wore a thick
overcoat of jam.

"I wish," she often said, "that everything in the world
were made of candy."

"Even your shoes?" asked mamma.

"Yes."

"They would stick to the carpet."

"Then the carpet might be sugar."

"And the chairs?"

"Chocolate."

"You would sit through them on a hot day."

"Then I should eat them," replied Lily triumphantly.

Mamma laughed, but she sighed also; for the child grew
thinner, and her temper, which should have been sweetened by
all the sweet things she ate, became sourer every day.

One windy morning mamma sent Lily to school carrying a
large red umbrella.

"Hold it tightly," she said. "The wind is strong, and
this umbrella has a will of its own."

Lily promised; but promises made in a warm hall are
sometimes difficult to keep at a blustering street corner. No
sooner had she gone beyond the gate than the wind
rushed at her, seized the umbrella, turned it inside out,
righted it again, and filled it like the sail of
a ship.

"Stop!" cried Lily, though whether she spoke to the wind
or the umbrella she did not know.

The umbrella did not stop. It tugged her along the
pavement, over a puddle, through a hedge, and across a
field. Lily clung with both hands. Her schoolbooks flew from
beneath her arm; her hat sailed away like a black
bird; and presently her feet left the ground.

Up she went!

She rose above the chimney-pots, where the smoke made her
sneeze; above the church steeple, whose weathercock looked astonished to
see her; above the clouds, which felt like wet feathers
brushing her cheeks. The red umbrella carried her so swiftly
that the houses dwindled into toy boxes, the roads into
gray threads, and the people into moving crumbs.

At first Lily was frightened. Then she remembered that frightened
children cannot scream forever without growing tired, and she began
to look about.

Below her spread a country brighter than any rainbow. Its
hills shone pink, yellow, violet, and green; its rivers were
brown and golden; its trees sparkled as though hung with
jewels. A delicious perfume floated upward—vanilla, cinnamon, wintergreen, warm caramel,
and something else that smelled exactly like the inside of
a confectioner's shop on Christmas Eve.

"Oh!" cried Lily. "It is Candy-land. I knew there must
be one!"

At that moment the wind dropped. The umbrella folded itself
with a snap, and Lily tumbled down upon a soft
hill of pink sugar.

She sank to her knees, tasted the hill, and found
it strawberry.

Her wish had come true.

## II. A Very Sweet Country

Lily's first act was to eat the ground on which
she stood.

It seemed a sensible act at the time; but had
she been obliged to eat every road before travelling over
it, she would never have gone far. Fortunately, the pink
hill sloped into a broad avenue paved with squares of
jujube—red, amber, purple, and emerald—so she skipped along, biting a
piece from the road whenever she pleased.

On either side grew hoarhound hedges, with candied cherries shining
upon them like transparent lamps. The trees had striped peppermint
trunks and branches of twisted barley sugar. From these hung
gum-drops, sugared almonds, lemon kisses, and little silver-coated seeds that
tinkled when the breeze stirred them.

A brook of chocolate ran beside the road. Its banks
were powdered cocoanut, its pebbles were burnt almonds, and small
boats of citron peel floated under bridges of crisp taffy.

"This," said Lily, "is the most sensible country I ever
saw."

Before long she met a company of people no taller
than her hand. They were dressed in colored papers with
fringed edges, and each wore a silver cap.

"Welcome!" they cried. "Welcome to Saccharissa!"

"Is that the name of the town?" asked Lily.

"It is the name of everything worth naming," replied a
pink Bonbon. "Our town, our queen, our sweetest flower, and
our favorite song are all called Saccharissa. It saves trouble."

The Bonbons took Lily by the fingers and led her
toward the city. Its walls were built of nougat, with
towers of rock candy that flashed in the sun. The
gates were two enormous sticks of cinnamon, and over them
hung a sign in chocolate letters:

**EAT, DRINK, AND BE MERRY,
FOR TO-MORROW THERE WILL BE MORE.**

Within the walls were marshmallow cottages with roofs of caraway
comfits; chocolate palaces whose windows were sheets of clear barley
sugar; and shops overflowing with molasses candy, peanut brittle, Turkish
paste, cream walnuts, sugared violets, licorice pipes, and every dear
delight we all love so well when we do not
love it too much.

Even the street lamps were tall candles, which burned with
blue flames and dripped vanilla cream.

"What do you do all day?" Lily asked.

"We amuse ourselves," said the pink Bonbon.

"Do you go to school?"

"Certainly not! Knowledge is bitter."

"Do you work?"

"Work makes one tough."

"What do you eat?"

"One another, occasionally," answered the Bonbon pleasantly. "But only at
parties."

Lily thought these people most agreeable. They crowned her with
a wreath of gum-drops and carried her to the palace,
where Queen Saccharissa sat upon a throne of spun sugar.
The queen was made of white fondant, with rose-colored cheeks,
citron hair, and a gown sparkling with silver beads. Beside
her stood the Candy King, a splendid person in a
chocolate coat, a lemon-wafer waistcoat, and a crown of hard
golden caramel.

"So this is a real child," said the queen. "How
large she is!"

"And how hungry!" said the king, noticing that Lily had
bitten the corner from a footstool.

A banquet was ordered at once. There were lakes of
custard with islands of meringue; castles of ice cream defended
by soldiers of lady-fingers; pies filled with syrup and stars;
mountains of macaroons; and a roast peacock made entirely of
blue and green sugar, though it was called roast only
because the cook thought the word fashionable.

Lily ate until she could eat no more. Then, seeing
a plate of sugared strawberries, she discovered that "no more"
is a phrase with a very elastic meaning.

After dinner there were games. The Bonbons rolled down a
powdered-sugar hill; the Chocolate Drops played at melting in the
sun and being carried home in spoons; and the Peppermint
Twins turned cartwheels until their red stripes ran into their
white ones.

Lily played harder than anyone. When she broke a little
Candy Boy's arm, he merely licked the pieces and stuck
them together.

"You see," said he, "we never remain injured long, unless
the weather is damp."

At sunset a molasses river carried them through a garden
where candied flowers opened and shut to the music of
licorice flutes. Lily lay in a boat made of almond
paste and thought she would never wish to go home.

That night she slept in a bed of marshmallow, beneath
blankets of thin chocolate. The pillow was flavored with violet,
and whenever she turned her head she took a bite.

In the morning she ate the rest of it.

## III. When Sweetness Sours

For several days Lily was happy in the way one
is happy at a fair: laughing loudly, running everywhere, and
never sitting still long enough to discover how tired one
is.

She ate the doorknobs, the window curtains, three toy horses,
part of a fountain, and nearly all the steps leading
to the palace. She drank from the chocolate brook until
it ran low, and snapped branches from the peppermint trees
without asking whether the trees objected.

At first the sugar people applauded.

"What an appetite!" they said. "How natural! How complimentary!"

But Lily's cheeks lost their color. Her tongue felt thick,
her head ached, and her fingers were always sticky. She
woke cross, remained cross all day, and went to bed
crosser still. Nothing tasted as delightful as it had on
the first morning, so she tried to recover her pleasure
by eating twice as much.

That is a common mistake, not only in Candy-land.

One afternoon the Bonbons invited her to a picnic beside
the Molasses Sea. They spread a cloth of rice paper
and arranged baskets of cream dates, chocolate mice, almond frogs,
sugared plums, and tiny cakes iced in nine colors.

"I do not like these cakes," said Lily after eating
seven.

"They are our best cakes," protested a little green Bonbon.

"They are too small."

"They were made for us."

"I want that basket."

"It belongs to Princess Praline."

"I want it all the same."

Lily seized the basket. Princess Praline held fast, and in
the struggle her slender sugar arms broke off.

"You wicked giant!" cried the Bonbons.

"I am not wicked," said Lily. "I am hungry."

"You are always hungry."

This was true, and therefore especially disagreeable to hear. Lily
kicked the rice-paper cloth into the sea, scattering the feast.
Then she stamped upon a village of cream candies, crushed
a chapel made of white sugar, and knocked over the
Peppermint Twins, who shattered into striped fragments.

Everyone screamed. The Candy Guard hurried from the city armed
with brittle swords, but Lily ate the captain's sword and
laughed.

"You shall be brought before the king!" he declared.

"I am bigger than the king."

"Size is not goodness."

"It is in Candy-land," said Lily, though no one had
ever told her so.

The guard escorted her to the palace, where the Candy
King sat in judgment. He looked rather less splendid than
before, for Lily had nibbled one of his coat-tails during
a dance, and the hot weather had bent his caramel
crown.

"Lily," he began solemnly, "you have eaten public property, damaged
loyal subjects, drained half a river, and bitten the nose
from my Lord Chamberlain."

"He had a very good nose," said Lily.

"That does not excuse you."

"It explains me."

"Besides," continued the king, "you have become sour."

"I cannot be sour. I eat nothing but sweets."

"Too much sweetness often ends in sourness," replied the king
wisely. "You must leave our city until you learn moderation."

"I will not!"

Lily snatched him from his throne. Perhaps she intended only
to frighten him; perhaps the golden crown looked particularly tempting;
perhaps a fretful child seldom knows what she intends. At
any rate, she opened her mouth and bit off the
king's head.

There was a dreadful silence.

The head was caramel outside and chocolate cream within.

Lily had scarcely swallowed it when the whole court began
to ring tiny bells and cry, "Murder! Crumbs! Disaster!"

Queen Saccharissa fainted into a silver sugar bowl. The guard
charged. The courtiers threw burnt almonds, which struck Lily sharply;
and from every street came battalions of Candy Canes, Marshmallow
Men, Chocolate Soldiers, and furious little Nonpareils.

For the first time Lily was afraid of the country
she had wished for.

She ran through the palace, breaking a door of butterscotch;
tore down the jujube avenue; leaped over the chocolate brook;
and fled beyond the hoarhound hedges. Behind her came the
enraged sugar people, crying for justice and carrying the headless
king in a paper box.

Lily ran until the bright city disappeared. The sweet-smelling air
grew hot and dry. The colored hills flattened into a
white, glaring waste.

She had reached the Sugar Desert.

## IV. Across the Desert

The Sugar Desert was not nearly so pleasant as powdered
sugar on a cake.

The fine grains blew into Lily's eyes, filled her shoes,
and stuck to her hair. The sun beat down upon
the white plain until it glittered cruelly. Here and there
rose black licorice rocks, too bitter even for Lily, and
thorny bushes of crystallized ginger pricked her hands.

She had no water. The thought of chocolate made her
ill. A bag of candy hearts, which she had snatched
while fleeing, melted in her pocket and stained her frock
with pink messages of **LOVE ME**, **BE MINE**, and **SWEETEST
ONE**—sentiments that did not match her present appearance.

By afternoon she was crying.

"I want mamma," she said. "I want my bed. I
want some cold water. I should even like bread and
milk."

This was a remarkable confession.

She wandered on until she saw a brown speck in
the distance. It grew into a house with sloping walls,
a frosted roof, and windows made of clear sugar. Lily
groaned.

"Another sweet house! I shall never eat again."

The door flew open, and out sprang a brisk little
gingerbread man. He had currant eyes, a citron-buttoned coat, and
boots that clicked crisply when he walked. One corner of
his hat was chipped, but he wore it jauntily.

"Never eat again?" he cried. "Nonsense! Everyone must eat; the
question is what, and how much, and whether it helps
him do his work. Come in before you are baked
brown by the sun."

He took Lily's hand and led her inside.

The house smelled of flour, spice, and warm ovens. Rows
of cakes stood upon shelves: sponge cakes as light as
cushions, pound cakes as solid as bricks, seed cakes freckled
with caraway, dark fruit cakes full of raisins, and delicate
little tea cakes wearing white icing caps.

"My name is Ginger Snap," said the gingerbread man. "I
am a baker's assistant, third class, hoping to rise."

"Rise where?" asked Lily faintly.

"Why, into Bread-land, of course. But first you need treatment."

He gave her cool water, then a bowl of plain
gruel with a pinch of salt. Lily made a face.

"No sugar?"

"Not a crystal."

"No cinnamon?"

"You have had adventures enough with cinnamon."

She was too hungry to argue. The gruel tasted dull
after Candy-land, yet it rested comfortably in her stomach. When
she had finished, Ginger Snap put her to bed between
clean sheets that were real cloth and could not be
eaten.

A round doctor arrived presently. He was a ginger cake
of great age, soft in the middle but firm in
opinion, and carried a thermometer made from a slender stick
of macaroni.

"Dr. Ginger," said Ginger Snap, "here is a child with
acute saccharine disagreeableness."

"Tongue out," ordered the doctor.

Lily obeyed.

"Dear me! Frosted. Pulse?"

He held her wrist.

"Sticky and irregular. Temper?"

"Very bad," admitted Lily.

"Appetite?"

"Enormous."

"Usefulness?"

Lily stared.

"None yet," said Ginger Snap.

Dr. Ginger nodded gravely. "A common case. The patient must
take plain food, useful exercise, and two lessons daily in
considering other people. If she complains, double the exercise."

He left three little brown pills, which proved to be
toasted bread crumbs, and went away.

Lily slept all night and half the next day. When
she awoke, Ginger Snap was kneading dough at a long
table.

"What country is this?" she asked.

"Cake-land."

"It is not as pretty as Candy-land."

"We have no time to be pretty all day," said
Ginger Snap. "We are busy becoming something better."

## V. The Workers of Cake-land

When Lily was strong enough, Ginger Snap showed her the
town.

Cake-land lay in a warm valley surrounded by Oven Hills,
from whose chimneys rose fragrant smoke. Its streets were dusted
with flour; its houses were built of firm gingerbread, with
real wooden doors to prevent hungry travellers from making free
with the walls; and everywhere people hurried about carrying mixing
bowls, rolling-pins, egg baskets, spice boxes, and pans.

There were lofty Pound Cakes with square shoulders; fashionable Lady
Cakes in many layers, who feared the least jolt; sober
Seed Cakes who taught small Cooky children; light-headed Sponge Cakes
who absorbed every opinion they heard; and Brownies, plain in
dress but excellent at work.

At the town square a band played upon egg-beaters, graters,
and pan lids. A procession was passing before the Chief
Baker.

"What is happening?" asked Lily.

"Examination day," said Ginger Snap. "Those who have worked well
may earn yeast."

"Why do they want yeast?"

"Because without yeast they cannot truly rise. Cakes may be
charming, rich, and ornamental, but Bread-land is the place of
usefulness. There one studies grain, nourishes people, and becomes part
of the world's good work."

Lily did not understand this, but the procession interested her.

First came a proud Wedding Cake, four stories high, decorated
with sugar roses.

"What have you done?" asked the Chief Baker.

"I have stood upon a silver table and been admired
by two hundred guests."

"Did you nourish them?"

"My lower corner did. The rest was saved in boxes."

"Too ornamental," said the Chief Baker. "Return next year."

The Wedding Cake moved away in offended magnificence.

Next came a small, brown Cooky with a cracked edge.

"I carried lunch to a hungry schoolboy," she said. "He
divided me with his sister."

"Good," said the Chief Baker. "You have begun to understand."

Then Ginger Snap stepped forward.

"What have you done?" asked the Chief Baker.

"I have worked at the ovens, taught six young cakes
not to scorch, repaired the bakery chimney, crossed the Sugar
Desert to guide lost travellers, and lately cared for a
sick and exceedingly cross child."

Lily blushed.

"Excellent," said the Chief Baker. "Continue faithfully, and your yeast
shall soon be granted."

All Cake-land cheered.

From that day Lily worked beside Ginger Snap. At first
she disliked everything. Flour settled on her nose; dough stuck
to her fingers; the oven was hot; the bowls were
heavy; and the cakes would not arrange themselves merely because
she told them to.

"Why must I knead this?" she demanded.

"To make it smooth."

"My arms ache."

"They are growing useful."

"I would rather eat it."

"Raw dough is a poor dinner and a worse companion."

Ginger Snap taught her to measure carefully, for a spoonful
too much or too little may change the fate of
a cake. He showed her how saleratus must be used
with judgment, how eggs should be beaten until foamy, how
butter and sugar become light under patient hands, and how
an oven must be warm enough to encourage but not
hot enough to destroy.

"Baking is like bringing up children," said old Dr. Ginger,
who sometimes looked in. "Too much indulgence leaves them heavy;
too much heat makes them hard; neglect ruins them entirely."

Lily learned to sweep the floury floor, polish pans, wash
bowls before the batter dried, and carry cakes without pinching
off their corners. This last lesson was difficult.

She attended school with the young Cookies, where Mistress Caraway
taught Kitchen Arithmetic.

"If twelve buns are made," said Mistress Caraway, "and a
hungry giant eats seven, how many remain?"

"Five," answered the class.

"None," said Lily. "The giant would eat those too."

"You are improving in figures," replied Mistress Caraway, "but not
in restraint."

There were also lessons in Spice Geography, Oven Management, and
the History of the Great Pudding War, in which the
Plum Cakes defeated the Tapiocas by remaining firm under boiling
conditions.

Weeks passed. Lily's face became less pale, and she no
longer awoke cross every morning. She discovered that food tasted
better after work, sleep came more sweetly to tired limbs,
and the praise earned by a well-baked pan of buns
lasted longer than the pleasure of gobbling six of them.

One evening a golden bell rang from the Chief Bakery.

"Ginger Snap!" cried a messenger. "Your yeast is granted."

All the town gathered as the Chief Baker placed a
tiny living bit of yeast upon Ginger Snap's breast.

"May you rise in body and purpose," he said.

Ginger Snap was put into a warm bed of dough
and carried into the Great Oven. Lily waited anxiously. There
came a rich, wholesome fragrance; the oven door opened; and
out stepped a new person—rounder, softer, and stronger than before,
with a golden-brown coat and a kindly split at the
crown.

"I am Muffin now!" he cried.

Lily hugged him, though carefully, for he was still hot.

"To-morrow I go to Bread-land," he said.

"Take me with you."

"You are not bread."

"No, but I can learn."

Muffin considered. "Then come. Anyone may enter Bread-land who truly
wishes to become useful."

## VI. Bread-land

They left Cake-land at sunrise, following a road sprinkled not
with sugar but with clean yellow meal. The Oven Hills
sank behind them, and the air grew fresh. They crossed
a plain where windmills turned their white arms; passed fields
of wheat waving like golden water; and came at last
to a broad gate built of dark rye loaves.

Over it were carved the words:

**BY LABOR WE RISE;
BY SERVICE WE ARE SHARED.**

Beyond lay the loveliest country Lily had ever seen, though
at first she wondered why. It had no rock-candy towers,
no rivers of chocolate, and no palaces iced in nine
colors. There were green farms, red barns, white mills, orchards,
gardens, clear streams, and comfortable brown houses from whose chimneys
came the warm smell of baking bread.

The roads were firm beneath her feet. Cows grazed in
clover fields. Children carried books to school, farmers sang as
they worked, and every window seemed to say, "Come in;
there is enough, and you are welcome."

A sturdy boy approached, rolling a hoop.

"I am Johnny Cake," he said. "Are you the traveller
from Candy-land?"

"How did you know?"

"You have pink sugar in your pocket and a bite
missing from your umbrella handle."

Lily looked down. In her hungriest moment she had indeed
bitten the handle, which was painted wood and had proved
disappointing.

Johnny Cake was a cheerful fellow with a cornmeal complexion
and a broad straw hat. Beside him walked Sally Lunn,
a plump, golden girl carrying a basket of rolls.

"You must be tired," said Sally. "Come to our house
for supper."

Their home stood near a wheat field. The table was
spread with brown bread, yellow butter, milk, baked apples, and
fresh lettuce from the garden. Lily waited for the sweets,
but none appeared.

"Is that all?" she asked before she could stop herself.

Sally looked surprised. "All? We have wheat from the south
field, butter from Clover, milk from our own cow, apples
we gathered, and lettuce Johnny nearly lost to the rabbits.
It seems a great deal to us."

Lily tasted the bread. It was warm and fragrant, with
a crisp crust and a tender crumb. Butter melted into
it. After the plain labor of the day and the
fresh country air, it tasted better than the king's whole
caramel crown.

"I beg your pardon," she said. "It is a feast."

Muffin smiled.

The next morning Lily entered the Bread-land school. Its head
was Professor of Grainology, a tall, thin gentleman made of
a French roll, with spectacles perched upon his floury nose.

"We do not merely bake here," he told her. "We
study why we bake. Wheat is not born a loaf.
The earth receives a seed; rain softens it; sun draws
it upward; farmers tend it; reapers gather it; threshers free
the grain; millstones grind it; bakers mix, knead, wait, and
heat. Many hands and much patience make one loaf."

Lily examined a grain of wheat lying in her palm.

"It is very small."

"So is every beginning."

The school taught Grainology, Yeast Philosophy, Domestic Economy, Mill Mechanics,
and the Ethics of Sharing. Lily learned the difference between
wheat, rye, barley, oats, corn—which the old books sometimes called
Indian meal—and buckwheat, which was not wheat at all and
seemed pleased with the deception.

In the laboratory she watched yeast wake in warm water.

"It is alive!" she cried.

"In its fashion," said the professor. "It enters dull dough
and gives it spirit. But warmth must attend it, and
time must be allowed. You cannot command true rising with
a shout."

Lily tried shouting nevertheless. The dough paid no attention.

She learned to knead with steady palms, folding and pressing
until the rough mass became smooth and springy. She learned
that water too hot kills yeast, water too cold leaves
it sleeping, and impatience produces a flat loaf. She learned
to cover the bowl, set it in a gentle place,
and wait.

Waiting had always seemed to Lily a waste of time.
In Bread-land she discovered that unseen work may be going
on beneath a quiet cloth.

## VII. What Bread Is For

Muffin lived at the House of Useful Loaves, where bread
people prepared for their final service. Some hoped to feed
scholars, some laborers, some travellers, some sick children, and some
poor families whose cupboards were bare.

One evening Lily found Muffin looking across the fields.

"Are you unhappy?" she asked.

"No."

"You look solemn."

"I am thinking of my purpose."

"I thought your purpose was to live in Bread-land."

"That is only preparation. Bread does not fulfil itself by
sitting proudly upon a shelf."

"Then what does it do?"

"It nourishes."

"But if someone eats you—"

"I shall become strength in that person. Perhaps I shall
help a farmer lift his plough, a mother tend her
baby, a student learn a difficult lesson, or a brave
person perform some needed deed. That is better than growing
stale while admiring my own crust."

Lily remembered the Candy King, eaten from greed and anger.
The thought of Muffin being eaten from love and usefulness
felt entirely different, though she could not at once explain
why.

"Are you afraid?"

"A little," he admitted. "Courage is not being made without
fear. Even dough trembles when first put into the oven."

At school the next day Professor of Grainology spoke of
substance.

"Candy pleases the tongue," he said, "and a little pleasure
is good. Cake celebrates holidays and makes tea tables cheerful.
We must not scold them merely for being what they
are. But bread has substance. It stays, strengthens, and supports.
A wise life may contain sweetness and celebration, yet it
must be built upon nourishment and work."

Lily raised her hand. "Can a person who has behaved
very badly ever gain substance?"

"Certainly. Character, like dough, is worked into shape."

"What if she bit off a king's head?"

The professor polished his spectacles. "That is an uncommon offense,
but repentance has dealt with worse ingredients."

Bread-land had no prisons. Those who behaved poorly were assigned
useful work, which was considered both punishment and cure. Lily
volunteered at the village kitchen, where Sally Lunn showed her
how to prepare meals for the old, the sick, and
families with new babies.

At first Lily wished to make elaborate dishes. Sally shook
her head.

"Mrs. Rye is ill. She needs broth and toast, not
a castle of frosting."

"But a castle is more impressive."

"Meals are meant to help the eater, not glorify the
cook."

This lesson struck Lily as very deep.

She carried soup to an old Corn Dodger who lived
alone near the mill. He thanked her so warmly that
she felt a glow no sugar feast had given her.
She made soft rolls for a family of small Biscuits,
and when the youngest put both arms around one roll
and sighed with happiness, Lily did not wish to take
even a nibble.

She worked in the fields, too. She scattered seed, pulled
weeds, tied sheaves, and learned how heavy grain becomes when
one must lift it rather than merely eat it. Her
hands, once soft and sticky, grew brown and capable. Her
arms strengthened. Her eyes brightened. She laughed more often and
complained less, though she never ceased complaining entirely, for perfection
would have made her tiresome in another way.

On holidays Bread-land served cakes, and sometimes even candy. The
first time Lily saw a dish of peppermint drops, she
drew back.

"You may have one," said Muffin.

"I might become dreadful again."

"One sweet does not make a tyrant, any more than
one sensible supper makes a saint. Choose, enjoy, and stop."

Lily took one peppermint and let it melt slowly upon
her tongue. It was delightful. Because she did not seize
the whole dish, she remembered its freshness far longer than
she had remembered any of the mountains she devoured in
Candy-land.

Moderation, she discovered, did not destroy pleasure; it rescued pleasure
from greed.

## VIII. Lily's Loaf

At last Professor of Grainology announced that Lily might attempt
the Master Loaf.

"If it is well made," he said, "you shall be
counted a true Friend of Bread-land."

"What if it fails?"

"You shall learn why and try again."

This answer was less exciting than a promise of instant
success, but much more useful.

Lily chose the finest flour, yet not the whitest; for
she had learned that grain loses some of its goodness
when stripped merely to look fashionable. She measured warm water,
crumbled yeast, added salt, and mixed the ingredients in a
great earthen bowl.

The dough was sticky.

"You may add flour," said Sally, "but gently. Too much
will make it stiff."

Lily dusted, folded, pressed, and turned. The dough clung, resisted,
and finally yielded. Beneath her hands it became smooth as
a child's cheek.

She set it to rise.

For an hour nothing seemed to happen. Lily walked around
the bowl, peeped beneath the cloth, and wished to poke
it.

"Leave it," advised Johnny Cake.

"What if it is asleep?"

"Then poking will make it cross."

When she looked again, the dough had doubled.

"It rose!" she cried.

"As good things often do when given warmth and time,"
said Muffin.

She shaped the loaf, let it rise once more, and
slid it into the oven. Then came the hardest waiting
of all. A rich fragrance filled the bakery. At last
the loaf emerged, tall and golden, with a crisp brown
crust.

The Professor tapped its bottom.

It sounded hollow.

He cut one slice. The crumb was even, soft, and
creamy; the crust crackled; the flavor was nutty and clean.

"A perfect loaf," he declared.

Everyone cheered. Johnny Cake tossed his hat so high that
it landed in a flour bin. Sally kissed Lily. Muffin
bowed as if she were a queen, though Lily felt
happier than she had in Saccharissa's palace.

"What reward do you choose?" asked the professor.

Lily looked at the loaf. Suddenly she thought of home:
the little breakfast room, mamma's blue cup, the rejected porridge,
the patient hands cutting bread she had so often refused.

"I wish to take it to mamma."

"Then you have understood the lesson," said the professor.

Muffin stepped forward. "And I shall go with you."

Lily stared. "How?"

"I have waited for my service. Let me become part
of your loaf."

"No! I do not want you eaten."

"You were willing to eat a candy king because you
wanted pleasure. Can you not accept a friend's gift when
he wishes to give strength?"

"But then you will be gone."

"Not gone. Changed. Grain disappears into flour, flour into dough,
dough into bread, bread into the life of those who
eat it. Change is not always loss."

Lily began to cry.

"You are my best friend."

"And because I am your friend, I would rather help
you and your mamma than sit here becoming dry and
crumbly. Gingerbread breaks; cakes grow stale; bread is meant to
be shared. Do not deny me my highest purpose."

The people of Bread-land gathered silently. Muffin climbed upon the
table and laid one warm hand against the loaf. At
once a golden light passed over him. He grew softer,
brighter, and fragrant; then, like a little cloud of flour
drawn by a breeze, he melted gently into the bread.

The loaf shone for a moment and became still.

Lily laid both arms around it.

"Thank you," she whispered.

From somewhere within the warm crust came a faint, cheerful
voice:

"Carry me level. You always tilt the basket."

Lily laughed through her tears.

## IX. Home on the Wind

The red umbrella had waited all this time beside the
schoolhouse door. It looked dusty, and its wooden handle still
bore the mark of Lily's teeth.

"How shall I return?" she asked.

"In the manner by which you came," said Professor of
Grainology. "Wishes brought you away; a wiser wish may take
you home."

Lily opened the umbrella. Sally placed the loaf in a
basket and tied it securely to Lily's arm. Johnny Cake
gave her a packet of seeds. The professor presented her
with a small book entitled *Plain Directions for Excellent Bread
and an Excellent Temper*.

"Remember," said Sally, "warmth, patience, and proper measure."

"In baking?"

"In nearly everything."

The people of Bread-land embraced her. Lily held the umbrella
and made her wish.

"I wish to go home—not because I am tired of
learning, but because I want to use what I have
learned."

A fresh wind swept across the wheat fields. The umbrella
filled and lifted her gently. Bread-land sank below: first the
houses, then the windmills, then the golden fields, until all
became a warm patch upon the earth.

She flew over Cake-land and saw Ginger Snap's old bakery
smoking busily among the Oven Hills. Far beyond gleamed the
Sugar Desert. Candy-land sparkled in the distance, as beautiful and
dangerous as frost in sunlight.

For a moment Lily wondered what had become of the
headless king.

As if the wish were a window, she saw Queen
Saccharissa supervising the royal confectioner. The Candy King had received
a new head, handsomer than the first, though made of
lemon candy, which gave his judgments a slightly tart character.
The Peppermint Twins had been stuck together again, one with
his stripes running the wrong way. Princess Praline wore silver
sleeves over her repaired arms. The broken chapel had been
rebuilt with a notice over the door:

**GIANTS MUST NOT NIBBLE DURING SERVICE.**

"I am glad," said Lily. "And I am sorry."

The vision faded.

Clouds closed around her. She smelled rain, chimney smoke, and
the familiar damp wool of her school cloak. The umbrella
descended with a bump.

Lily found herself standing outside her own garden gate.

The sky was still gray. The church clock had moved
scarcely ten minutes since she left. Her lost schoolbooks lay
in the hedge, and her hat hung from a branch.
Yet the basket on her arm was warm, the book
was in her pocket, and wheat seeds rested in her
hand.

Mamma ran from the house.

"Lily! Where have you been? I saw the wind carry
you across the field."

"To Candy-land, Cake-land, and Bread-land," said Lily.

Mamma looked at the red umbrella.

"I feared it might take you farther than school."

"I brought you a loaf."

They went inside. Lily set the table without being asked,
fetched the bread knife, and cut two slices. The loaf
was tender and fragrant. Mamma spread a little butter upon
it.

"This is the best bread I have ever tasted," she
said.

"It contains my friend Muffin."

Mamma paused, but wise mothers know when not to ask
too many questions.

"Then we must be grateful to him."

They ate together. Warmth seemed to pass from the bread
into Lily's body—not merely the warmth of an oven, but
the strength of fields, mills, patient hands, and a brave
little gingerbread heart. Mamma's tired face brightened. Lily felt as
though Muffin had become part of every good intention she
possessed.

For supper she ate bread and milk and did not
request jam.

This alarmed mamma more than the umbrella voyage.

"Are you ill?" she asked.

"No," replied Lily. "I believe I am getting well."

## X. The Good of the Grain

Lily's improvement did not occur in one magical instant. No
sensible loaf, however excellent, can do all our work for
us.

The next morning she wanted sugar on her porridge and
felt cross when mamma said one spoonful was enough. She
nearly complained, remembered the Candy King, and shut her mouth
so suddenly that the spoon clicked against her teeth.

She began to help in the kitchen. Her first home
loaf was heavy enough to stop a door, and the
second rose on one side only, like a hill in
a windy country. The third burned. The fourth was forgotten
near an open window and became so cold that the
yeast never stirred.

"Perhaps Bread-land did not truly teach me," she said.

"Perhaps it taught you to try again," replied mamma.

The fifth loaf was good.

Thereafter Lily practised faithfully. She learned the character of their
home oven, which heated fiercely at the back and sulked
in front. She kept flour dry, yeast fresh, pans clean,
and cloths sweet. She wasted nothing. Stale slices became toast;
crumbs thickened puddings; crusts were dried for soup; and extra
loaves went to neighbors who needed them.

She planted the Bread-land seeds in a sunny corner of
the garden. They grew into tall wheat, brighter than ordinary
grain. When the wind moved through it, Lily sometimes heard
a crisp little voice say, "Carry the basket level!"

Years passed, as years do even in stories where umbrellas
fly and candy kings require new heads. Lily grew from
a thin, peevish child into a strong young woman. Her
cheeks became rosy, her step firm, and her hands clever.
She could make white bread, brown bread, rye loaves, corn
bread, rolls, muffins, Sally Lunn, Johnny cake, seed cake, and
ginger cookies so crisp that she always thought lovingly of
Ginger Snap.

She did not banish sweets. On birthdays she made splendid
cakes; at Christmas she filled jars with peppermint drops; and
sometimes she cooked molasses candy for children, who pulled it
until it shone gold.

But when any child cried, "I wish everything were made
of candy!" Lily smiled.

"Even your bed?"

"Yes!"

"It would melt in summer."

"Then I should eat it."

"So I once believed," Lily would say. Then she told
of Saccharissa, the Bonbons, the chocolate brook, the Sugar Desert,
and the unfortunate Candy King. She told of industrious Cake-land,
where every good worker hoped to rise; and of Bread-land,
with its fields, schools, mills, and hospitable tables.

The children laughed at the king's lemon head and the
sign forbidding giants to nibble during service. Yet they also
listened when Lily explained that pleasure is sweetest when not
snatched, that work gives appetite its proper sauce, and that
ordinary bread may hold the labor of a hundred hands.

In time Lily became known as the best housekeeper and
bread maker in the county. Her home was not grand,
but it was bright, orderly, and welcoming. No hungry person
left her door unfed. No sick neighbor lacked broth or
toast. No tired traveller was refused a place at the
table. She taught young people to measure truly, knead patiently,
and remember whom the loaf was meant to serve.

"Bread should be good," she would say, "but the baker
should be better."

Mamma grew old in comfort, cherished by the daughter who
had once pushed away every wholesome dish. On quiet evenings
they sat together near the window, and Lily read aloud
while dough rose by the hearth. The red umbrella hung
in the hall. It never carried anyone away again, though
on windy days it trembled upon its peg as if
remembering.

Once, many years later, Lily opened it in the garden.

A breeze lifted its crimson edges. For one heartbeat she
smelled peppermint forests, warm gingerbread, and wheat beneath the sun.
Then something small and golden dropped into her apron.

It was a single perfect grain.

Lily planted it among her flowers. From it sprang a
sheaf of wheat, and from that wheat she made one
little loaf each year. She shared it with mamma while
mamma lived; afterward she shared it with the loneliest person
she knew.

For Lily had learned that food reaches its noblest end
not when it is admired, hoarded, or devoured in greed,
but when it becomes comfort, health, courage, and strength for
useful deeds.

As for Candy-land, it still sparkles beyond the Sugar Desert,
and perhaps it is well that it should. We all
need a peppermint now and then, a sugared almond at
a party, or a slice of frosted cake when candles
are lit and friends gather round. Cake-land still keeps its
ovens glowing, too, and the cheerful workers continue to hope
and labor and rise.

But beyond them both lies Bread-land, peaceful and golden, where
the windmills turn, the schools stand open, and warm loaves
cool upon broad windowsills. There Muffin's spirit may still be
heard in every generous kitchen, reminding careless bakers to carry
their baskets level, to give dough time, and to remember
that the plainest task becomes noble when it is done
well for someone else.

And if Lily sometimes looked toward the clouds when the
wind blew strongly, she did not wish to escape her
ordinary life. She had discovered the secret that many travellers
search for in stranger countries than Candy-land:

An ordinary life, filled with useful work and kindly sharing,
is itself a very great adventure.

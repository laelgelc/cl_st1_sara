## A Report Concerning the Wall and the Imperial Distance

I served for eleven years as a subordinate supervisor in
the construction of the Northern Wall, and before that, from
my twentieth year until my appointment, I was trained in
masonry, calculation, elementary surveying, the reading of official tables, and
the management of labor groups not exceeding five hundred men.
This is not to say that I understood the work.
I understood how a foundation must be tested, how the
lower courses should be bound, what allowance must be made
for frost, how many baskets of lime may be entrusted
to a porter, and at what point exhaustion ceases to
be temporary and begins to corrupt the spirit of a
whole company. These things I understood, or believed I did.
The Wall itself I did not understand.

Indeed, no one whom I met understood it, although there
were many who spoke as if understanding were a reward
of rank. The ordinary mason referred questions to the assistant
foreman, the assistant foreman to the foreman, the foreman to
the district supervisor, and the district supervisor, if pressed, pointed
toward the sealed packets sent from the provincial office. The
provincial office cited instructions of the Central Commission. As to
the Central Commission, whether it consisted of nine persons or
ninety, whether it sat in the capital or traveled continually
among hidden administrative residences, whether its members were old men
preserving decisions made centuries ago or young officials inventing decisions
each morning—nothing reliable was known.

We called it simply the leadership.

One must not conclude from this that disorder prevailed. On
the contrary, the daily work was regulated with such exactness
that disorder seemed almost a theoretical impossibility. At the third
drum we rose. At the fourth, the measuring ropes were
stretched. Stone was delivered according to numbered tablets; food according
to lists copied in duplicate; punishments according to a scale
distinguishing negligence, quarrelsomeness, theft, sacrilege, and resistance to technical correction.
Even the pauses were supervised. A man might rest only
where a small white flag had been planted, and the
flag itself was moved every seventh day by an official
who had no other duty. The higher purpose escaped us,
but every fragment of the purpose was sharply illuminated.

It is precisely the fragments that concern me.

The Wall was not built as a line advancing steadily
from one sea, mountain, or border to another. It was
built in sections. A company completed a stretch of perhaps
five hundred paces; another company, many days' journey away, completed
another. Between them lay gaps—not accidental cracks or places where
the earth itself provided defense, but broad open intervals through
which a horseman, an army, or the whole northern emptiness
might have passed without touching stone.

Later, some gaps were closed. Others remained open for years.
Still others, according to reports, remain open today, though these
reports may be old, and the places mentioned may by
now have vanished beneath later work. No complete map was
shown to us. We received local plans only: a curve
around a hill, a tower at a ridge, stairs rising
at a prescribed angle, drainage channels, storehouses, signal platforms. Beyond
the margins of the plan there was white paper.

A child looking at such a method would have asked:
Why not begin at one end and continue to the
other?

The question is reasonable. It is therefore difficult.

The official explanation, so far as any explanation was official,
concerned the morale of the skilled workers. In my view
this explanation is not false. It is only insufficient, and
because it is insufficient it may conceal something greater than
falsehood.

A trained mason is not like a man hired for
a harvest. His work demands more than strength. He must
believe in exactness when no one else can see the
difference; he must set a stone for descendants whose names
he will never know; he must preserve a straight course
through rain, mockery, sickness, and the complaints of those who
carry rather than build. Such discipline can be maintained for
a season, even for several years, but not indefinitely if
the work appears endless.

Suppose, then, that a mason had been ordered to begin
in the far west and continue eastward until the Wall
was complete. He would have labored ten years, twenty, perhaps
forty. The Wall would still have stretched before him farther
than rumor. Behind him, the completed work would have disappeared
over mountains and across provinces. He could not inspect it.
He could not embrace it in thought. A man may
give his life for an immense work, but he cannot
live daily inside immensity. The mind requires edges.

After some years he would have begun to ask whether
the line was truly continuous behind him. He would hear
that a flood had damaged a district, that rebels had
pulled down a tower, that inferior mortar had been used
by men long dead. He would suspect that decay followed
at the same pace as construction, so that the Wall
advanced without growing. His skill would become bitterness. He would
place a stone carelessly, then another. He would teach carelessness
to his apprentices. Nothing would have been achieved.

I need to say it again: nothing would have been
achieved.

The method of sections prevented this. A company was assigned
a measurable task. Five hundred paces, perhaps one thousand. A
foundation from the black pine ridge to the river bend.
Three towers and the stairs between them. The men saw
the beginning, and after two, three, or five years, they
saw the end. The final stone was set; banners were
raised; the district inspector arrived in ceremonial robes; oxen were
slaughtered; the names of the supervisors were entered in a
register. The company marched home.

What a return that was!

I remember my first return, though the memory has perhaps
borrowed colors from later occasions. We came down from the
northern hills in patched coats, carrying blunt tools that we
had been ordered to retain as badges of service. Children
ran beside us. At the provincial boundary the roads had
been swept. Priests rang bronze bowls from the temple steps.
Old men who had never left their county questioned us
about snowstorms, barbarian arrows, stone-cutting, and the appearance of the
imperial surveyors. Women touched the dust on our sleeves and
then touched their foreheads. We had not defeated an enemy.
Most of us had not seen one. Yet we were
received as men returning from the outer rim of the
human world.

At home, the local headman read aloud a proclamation praising
our section as indispensable to the defense of the entire
realm. None of us could say where our section stood
in relation to the whole. That did not matter. For
several weeks the Wall passed through our village as distinctly
as the canal. It was discussed in kitchens, schools, workshops,
and ancestral halls. Little models of towers were made from
clay. Songs were composed, all very poor. A widow gave
us the use of her orchard for a feast because
her three sons, who had died years earlier of fever,
would, she said, have wanted to join us.

Then the celebration ended. Daily life returned. Yet the men
were changed. They had looked upon their work as a
completed thing and upon themselves as participants in something larger.
After one year, sometimes after only six months, orders came
assigning them to a new section, usually in another province.
They left willingly. A man who might have deserted from
an endless line hurried toward a fresh fragment.

Thus the broken method made continuation possible. That is the
explanation.

But is it the whole explanation?

If the purpose was to prevent northern invasion, the gaps
were indefensible. Our teachers answered that no section remained isolated
forever. The neighboring sections would approach it; gaps would be
closed; local walls would become regional walls and regional walls
the Wall. Yet even as boys we knew that distances
were too great and records too uncertain for complete confidence.
We had seen maps in which the north was represented
by three brushstrokes and a row of animals. We had
heard of construction companies that marched for months toward their
appointed district and never found it. One party returned claiming
that the district had already been completed by another party;
the provincial office accused both of error and sent them
back by different roads. Neither was heard from again.

Perhaps they became shepherds. Perhaps they built their section in
the wrong valley. Perhaps the valley was correct and the
empire had moved around them.

Moreover, while some sections were being joined, others fell into
disrepair. A Wall of such extent does not wait in
stillness for completion. Rain enters it, roots disturb it, villagers
remove stones for ovens and graves, commanders alter stairways, local
governors add gates, then seal them, then add new gates
beside the sealed ones. To speak of the Wall as
though it were a single object is already an administrative
convenience. In the field, it is age, weather, intention, neglect,
and renewed intention laid course upon course.

At the academy where I studied, one instructor, Master Ren,
devoted seven lectures to the claim that the Wall had
been complete from the beginning—not materially, of course, but in
the design of the leadership. He drew a circle on
a board.

"The circle exists," he said, "even when the hand has
drawn only scattered arcs."

A student asked whether the spaces between the arcs also
belonged to the circle.

Master Ren praised him for the question and expelled him
for insolence.

This did not seem contradictory to us at the time.
The question had indeed been excellent, and excellence can be
dangerous.

Our education had prepared us for the building long before
the first general orders were proclaimed. Village schools taught children
to stack pebbles in interlocking rows. Provincial examinations included problems
in load, incline, distance, and the distribution of provisions. Ancient
histories were revised to emphasize frontier works. Even moral instruction
took an architectural form. Obedience was called the foundation, family
duty the inner wall, reverence the gate, study the watchtower.
A child who gave an evasive answer was told that
he had left a breach through which the northern wind
might enter.

How did this preparation begin before the Wall itself had
supposedly been conceived? Some scholars argue that scattered fortifications had
always existed, and education merely reflected a permanent defensive need.
Others maintain that the leadership planned the Wall generations before
announcing it. Still others say that the leadership has no
relation to time as we understand it, so that preparation
and command, command and execution, execution and purpose need not
occur in our order.

This last view is most often held by officials who
have never carried stone.

I do not dismiss it for that reason.

There was, in the academy library, a damaged treatise concerning
the Tower of Tongues, an ancient structure raised by the
earliest nations when humanity still believed heaven to be a
neighboring province. According to the treatise, the tower failed not
because it was too ambitious, but because its foundation was
too concentrated. All peoples pressed toward a single center; disputes
over access became disputes over speech, ritual, measurement, and authority.
The higher the tower rose, the more violently its base
divided.

The unknown author proposed a correction. Before any tower could
safely reach heaven, the peoples of the earth must first
be joined by an encircling wall of such breadth that
every nation would labor upon it and every road would
lead into its plan. The wall would not support the
tower physically. It would support it politically, perhaps spiritually. Once
the circumference had been established, the center could rise without
conflict.

The treatise broke off there.

For years I considered it a fable written by a
disappointed architect. Later, while watching section after section completed in
places that had no apparent strategic value, I began to
wonder whether our Wall was the foundation of that impossible
tower—not in stone, but in obedience. Perhaps defense against the
north was only the visible justification. Perhaps the leadership cared
less for the line than for the movement of workers,
officials, proclamations, tax grain, songs, maps, and stories that the
work set in motion.

A continuous method would have confined construction to one advancing
region. The method of sections scattered it everywhere. Men from
the southern marshes met men of the western deserts. Mountain
dialects were heard beside coastal speech. Provincial engineers argued over
measures and were compelled to accept a standard rod. Villages
thousands of miles from the frontier contributed rope, iron, paper,
oxen, and sons. Every completed fragment became a reason for
another district to imagine the whole.

Unity! Unity!

The word was carved above supply depots and printed at
the head of wage lists. We mocked it when rations
were late. Yet when we returned home and found the
children building miniature towers, we felt it in our blood.

If this was the deeper purpose, the gaps were not
failures. They were necessary. A completed Wall would have ended
the undertaking, and with it the great circulation of the
empire. An incomplete Wall required endless participation. The stone sections
stood still, but the country moved between them.

This is a troubling conclusion, because it attributes to the
leadership both vast wisdom and a willingness to deceive. Yet
perhaps deception is too harsh a word. A physician tells
a child that bitter medicine is sweet. A general conceals
the destination of a march. A mason covers a foundation
that no future inhabitant will see. Truth itself is distributed
according to rank, capacity, and need.

But who distributed it? Who knew the whole?

Whenever our discussions reached this point, someone would ask where
the leadership was located. No answer could be given. The
capital, certainly; but the capital contains thousands of offices, and
every office points inward toward another. A ministry receives a
sealed instruction from a council; the council cites an archive;
the archive preserves a copy authenticated by a chamberlain; the
chamberlain serves the emperor; the emperor, according to court teaching,
expresses the will of Heaven. Yet Heaven issues no receipts.

Perhaps the leadership is the passage of the instruction itself.
Perhaps no one decides, and the decision forms while descending
through the ranks, like sediment forming an island in a
river. A suggestion leaves a district office, is copied in
the province, summarized at the ministry, interpreted by a commission,
returned as an imperial initiative, and then obeyed by the
district that first proposed it. If so, the empire commands
itself from a center produced by its own obedience.

I once wrote this thought in the margin of a
technical handbook. The next morning I scraped it away. Even
now I do not know whether I feared punishment or
agreement.

One must also ask whom the Wall was intended to
repel.

The answer given everywhere is: the northern nomads.

In my childhood I knew them from illustrated sheets distributed
in school. They had open mouths, long teeth, bristling hair,
thin legs, and arms capable of drawing bows larger than
themselves. They roasted infants. They drank mare's blood. They slept
while riding and rode while sleeping. Their chiefs wore chains
made from the finger bones of magistrates. When the teacher
unrolled these images, the youngest children cried, though none of
us lived within a thousand miles of the north.

Our village lay among rice terraces where the air was
so moist that paper swelled in locked chests. Between us
and the frontier were rivers, cities, mountain systems, and provinces
whose names we could not list. No nomad had ever
entered our district. No grandparent recalled such a visit. Even
if the entire Wall vanished overnight, an invading horseman would
need years to reach us, and during those years he
would encounter armies, floods, hunger, unfamiliar roads, and the ordinary
resistance of distance.

Still, after lessons, we sometimes crouched behind the irrigation bank
and looked north. The direction was uncertain; three boys disagreed
about it, and one pointed south for an entire season.
Nevertheless we watched the horizon, expecting dark riders. The fear
was real because the distance was real. What is near
may be examined and perhaps dismissed. What is far away
enters the imagination without resistance.

Years later I saw northern prisoners at a military post.
They were smaller than the school pictures and looked exhausted.
One had a cough. Another carved a bird from a
piece of bone and traded it to a cook for
an onion. I felt cheated by their ordinariness, then ashamed
of the feeling. They looked at our unfinished Wall without
curiosity.

Through an interpreter I asked one whether his people planned
to cross it.

He asked, "Where does it end?"

I could not answer.

He laughed, though not disrespectfully, and said, "Then perhaps we
are already inside."

The interpreter insisted that this was a northern joke with
no political meaning. It has remained with me longer than
many official statements.

If the nomads were not the true reason, or not
the only reason, then perhaps the Wall faced inward as
much as outward. It taught us to distinguish the human
world from the wilderness, the ordered field from the open
steppe, the registered household from the wandering tent. Yet because
it was incomplete, the distinction could not be maintained in
stone. It had to be maintained in thought. Each citizen
carried a piece of the Wall within him, and where
no citizen stood, there was a gap.

This may explain why the farther a village was from
the Wall, the more fervently it revered the emperor who
ordered it.

Our southern district knew the emperor intimately and not at
all.

His portrait hung in the magistrate's hall, though the portrait
had been painted during the reign of his grandfather and
altered by adding a narrower beard. His birthday was celebrated
on the seventeenth day of the Ninth Month, unless flood
conditions required postponement, in which case the postponed festival often
merged with the harvest rites. Prayers were offered for his
health. Tax remissions were announced in his name, and new
taxes also. Every marriage contract began with the year of
his reign, but many villagers used the reign title of
a previous dynasty because the local scribe had inherited old
forms.

Was the emperor alive? Of course.

Which emperor? That was less certain.

When I was eight, a government courier arrived carrying news
of victory in a western war. The headman assembled us
beneath the camphor tree and read the bulletin. There were
drums, incense, and two jars of wine. My great-uncle listened
carefully and then said that he remembered the beginning of
that war when he was a boy. The courier corrected
him: this was a new war, fought under the present
Son of Heaven.

My great-uncle bowed and apologized. Later he told us that
couriers were required to call every war new, just as
fish sellers wet yesterday's catch.

Neither statement could be verified.

Several years afterward, refugees passed through our district and reported
that the dynasty had fallen. They said the capital gates
had burned, the emperor had fled east, and a rebel
commander had established a new calendar. The magistrate ordered the
refugees flogged for spreading absurdities. The next spring he replaced
the inscriptions on his office with the name of a
new dynasty and issued a proclamation declaring that the transition
had occurred peacefully under celestial guidance. The refugees had been
wrong in every punishable detail and right in the only
detail that mattered.

But did it matter to us?

Taxes were collected on the same days. The canal gate
opened according to the same water level. The magistrate retained
his chair. We burned the old calendar and received a
new one in which several month names differed. The emperor
had changed, perhaps, yet imperial authority remained as cloud remains
when its shapes alter.

Our loyalty was not weakened by ignorance. It may have
depended upon it.

A known ruler can be blamed. One may criticize the
shape of his nose, the number of his concubines, the
promotion of his cousins, the cost of his palace roof.
But our emperor was not a man available to judgment.
He was the direction from which seals arrived. He was
the still point assumed by ceremonies. He was the remote
cause named after floods and victories alike. To doubt him
would have been like doubting the north.

This does not mean we obeyed every order. We often
did not know an order had been issued until it
was obsolete.

Once, a decree reached us forbidding the sacrifice of white
goats in public squares, the practice having been judged wasteful
and disorderly. No one in our district sacrificed white goats.
We had black goats, and these were sacrificed behind temples,
not in public squares. The magistrate nevertheless commanded strict observance.
Notices were posted. Inspectors were appointed. For six months, no
white goat was publicly sacrificed. The success of the decree
was entered in provincial records.

Another order reduced the salt levy for three years because
of an earthquake in the eastern provinces. It arrived eleven
years after the earthquake. The magistrate reduced the levy for
three days, arguing that delay had compressed the period of
mercy. The people praised the emperor.

There is no irony in this praise, or not only
irony. The decree had crossed enormous distances. It had survived
rain, bandits, copied errors, the death of officials, and the
replacement of archives. Its mercy had become almost weightless, yet
its arrival proved that a thread, however fine, connected our
market square to the throne.

The imperial administration resembles a river so broad that people
on one bank may live and die without seeing the
other. Orders enter it near the source, but they do
not simply flow downstream. They circle in eddies, divide into
channels, disappear underground, flood villages for which they were not
intended, and leave dry the fields named in their instructions.
Still, without the river there would be no valley.

There is an old legend told by copyists and night
watchmen. Scholars reject it, which is perhaps why it has
been preserved so faithfully.

According to the legend, an emperor lay dying in the
innermost chamber of the palace. Which emperor is not stated.
Perhaps he was the first, perhaps the last, perhaps the
emperor reigning now. Around him stood physicians, astrologers, generals, ministers,
princes, and servants. Beyond them waited further ranks of officials,
each ranked according to robe, badge, and distance from the
imperial bed.

At the final moment the emperor remembered one subject in
the farthest southern province—a humble person unknown to court, unknown
even to the provincial census, but known to the emperor
with absolute clarity. To this subject he wished to send
a message.

The message may have been a command. It may have
been forgiveness. It may have contained the explanation of the
Wall.

The emperor whispered it into the ear of a messenger
chosen for strength, speed, and incorruptibility. To prevent error, he
made the messenger repeat it. Then, while those nearby lowered
themselves to the floor, the emperor confirmed the words with
a nod and died.

The messenger rose.

Imagine that you are the distant subject. You sit in
your house beyond the rice fields, beyond the southern hills,
beyond provincial roads that disappear each rainy season. You do
not know that you have been remembered. Nevertheless, the message
is yours.

The messenger pushes through the physicians. They yield slowly, burdened
by medicine chests and ceremonial sleeves. He passes the astrologers,
who cannot move until they have completed their observations. He
forces his way between kneeling ministers. The princes protest that
no one may step over the train of a mourning
robe. The messenger displays the imperial token. At once all
bow, but in bowing they fill the floor more completely.

He advances.

Chamber after chamber lies before him. In each, news of
the emperor's death has already arrived by some quicker route,
though the death occurred behind him. Curtains are lowered. Gongs
sound. Officials assemble in prescribed formations. The messenger runs, but
everyone else is kneeling, and a kneeling multitude is more
difficult to cross than a standing one.

He reaches the first courtyard.

How vast it is! Regiments wait there for orders. Tribute
elephants occupy the eastern side. Petitioners who entered the palace
as young men have grown old beneath the arcades. Kitchens
send carts across his path. Funeral carpenters carry planks toward
the inner halls. The messenger raises the token, and the
crowd cries, "A message from the Son of Heaven!"

The cry spreads faster than he can travel. People beyond
the courtyard hear it and rush inward to see him.
His authority creates the obstacle it is meant to overcome.

Still he advances.

At the first gate, guards inspect the token. They recognize
it, but because it is genuine they must summon a
superior officer. The superior recognizes it and summons the Keeper
of Tokens. The Keeper compares it with impressions stored in
nine locked cabinets. The cabinets require nine keys held by
nine officials, two of whom are absent for the funeral.
The messenger waits, trembling with urgency. At last the token
is certified.

Beyond the gate stands another gate.

Beyond that gate, stairs.

Beyond the stairs, halls containing the records of reigns forgotten
in the outer provinces. Scribes sit in rows copying announcements
that will be superseded before the ink dries. Servants carry
empty boxes inward and full boxes outward, though no one
remembers what the boxes contain. The messenger threads among them,
striking his breast and calling that the emperor's final word
must pass.

They honor him. They obstruct him with honor.

Years pass, though he does not age; or perhaps he
ages and is replaced by another messenger who receives the
words through a chain of sworn transmission. The legend is
unclear. Dynasties may change around him. Walls inside the palace
are demolished and rebuilt. He emerges from one courtyard only
to discover that ceremonies have enclosed it within a newer
palace.

Still he advances.

Suppose that at last he reaches the outer gate of
the original palace. Has he reached the city? No. Before
him lies the administrative city, with ministries, barracks, warehouses, temples,
examination halls, prisons, gardens, and residences of officials whose households
are themselves like small provinces. Every street is crowded. Every
crossing requires a permit. The news of his approach causes
balconies to overflow with spectators. The emperor's message, still unheard,
becomes a festival.

He advances by inches.

Beyond the administrative city lies the imperial capital. Its avenues
extend farther than a rider can travel in a day.
Whole populations have been born in its western wards who
have never heard of its eastern walls. Markets open before
dawn and close after the messenger has passed, but when
he returns the next morning he finds that the crowd
has carried him backward during the night.

Yet he persists. He guards the message inside himself as
a lamp cupped against infinite wind.

And beyond the capital lie the provinces.

And beyond those provinces, mountains.

And beyond the mountains, rivers without bridges, roads erased by
armies, districts renamed by governors, ferries operating according to calendars
no longer used.

And beyond all that, you.

You sit at a window in the south. Evening gathers
over the fields. Perhaps you have heard that an emperor
died long ago, though you do not know which one.
Perhaps the present emperor is said to be immortal. Perhaps
your children have gone to work on the Wall. You
watch the road, not because you expect a messenger but
because roads create expectation.

The message will never reach you.

Still, when darkness enters the room, you imagine its words.

This legend was told to me by an archive clerk
who claimed that his grandfather had met the messenger in
the fourth palace courtyard. When I asked how the grandfather
knew the contents were intended for a southern subject, the
clerk replied that all imperial messages are intended for a
southern subject. He was drunk, but drunkenness does not always
weaken an argument.

The legend clarifies our relation to the emperor better than
official doctrine. We receive no personal message, yet we live
as if one were on its way. If an order
arrives, however irrelevant, we treat it as evidence of that
deeper communication. If nothing arrives, the distance explains the silence.
Thus imperial authority cannot disappoint us, because its failure to
appear proves its magnitude.

In the capital, I am told, the emperor may be
visible. Citizens may glimpse his carriage, know the names of
ministers, discuss court factions, and calculate which prince will inherit
the throne. If this is true, their relation to power
is entirely different from ours and perhaps less pure. They
see the machinery and therefore mistake it for the source.
We see only effects and therefore preserve the source from
examination.

There are villages farther south than mine where even the
concept of dynasty is weak. An inspector once reported that
the inhabitants of a mountain district worshiped seven emperors simultaneously.
One had reigned eight hundred years earlier, two had never
existed, one was a local bandit, and three were different
names for the same ruler. The villagers maintained shrines to
all seven and paid taxes correctly. The inspector ordered them
to remove six shrines. They removed the shrine of the
reigning emperor because its timber was newest and easiest to
dismantle.

Were they disloyal?

The inspector thought so. The provincial governor did not. He
wrote that loyalty concerns the root, not the branches of
nomenclature, and that a population willing to revere seven emperors
possesses an excess rather than a deficiency of imperial feeling.
The shrine was rebuilt. For safety, the villagers added an
eighth.

News of rebellions reaches such places as legend. A tax
collector is attacked in the north; twenty years later children
in the south perform a puppet play in which a
dragon steals a grain measure from the moon. A prince
is executed; generations afterward he becomes a river god. A
dynasty falls; its banners continue to be used at weddings
because their color is considered fortunate. History does not travel
across the empire as a straight road. It blows like
dust, gathers in corners, is swept away, rises again.

The educated complain that the people confuse past and present.
Yet the empire itself encourages confusion. Every new ruler claims
ancient authority. Every reform is announced as a restoration. Every
unprecedented tax is justified by a precedent. The present clothes
itself in the past so thoroughly that the villagers cannot
be blamed for greeting one as the other.

During my service I once lodged in a village whose
people believed the reigning emperor to be the same emperor
who had ordered the first canals dug. They knew that
this emperor had died many times. Death, they explained, was
among his ceremonies. When a court bulletin announced the birth
of an imperial son, the villagers nodded and said that
the emperor had wisely become his own child.

I attempted to correct them. For two evenings I drew
genealogies on a wooden table: reign succeeding reign, dynasty overthrowing
dynasty, sons, nephews, usurpers, restorers. The villagers listened politely. On
the third evening the eldest among them asked, "If these
are all different emperors, why do their orders carry the
same weight?"

I answered that legitimacy passed through Heaven.

"Then Heaven is the emperor," she said.

"No," I replied, "the emperor is the Son of Heaven."

"Which son?"

I returned to my room and erased the table.

It would be easy to call such people ignorant. They
are ignorant. But their ignorance may correspond more closely to
imperial reality than our scholarship does. We distinguish rulers because
documents require distinctions. The people experience only the continuity of
remoteness. For them, emperor succeeds emperor as wave succeeds wave,
while the sea remains unnamed and indivisible.

Here the Wall and the throne approach each other.

The Wall is made of separated sections imagined as a
continuous whole. Imperial authority is made of separated decrees, festivals,
taxes, portraits, rumors, and memories imagined as a living ruler.
In both cases, the gaps are obvious and yet do
not destroy belief. Perhaps they produce it. A complete Wall
could be inspected and found wanting. A present emperor could
be judged and found human. But incompleteness invites completion by
the mind, and the mind builds more swiftly than any
labor company.

Does this mean the leadership deliberately arranged the imperial distance
just as it arranged the sections of the Wall?

I cannot say.

It is possible that no arrangement was necessary. The empire
is simply too large. Mountains delay couriers; officials protect themselves;
language changes; roads fail; rulers die. Human intentions, entering such
immensity, become whirling dust. The leadership may issue lucid commands
that reach us as nonsense, just as we may send
accurate reports that arrive at court as myth. No conspiracy
is required. Extent itself governs.

Yet extent is not merely an obstacle. It is the
substance of imperial majesty. If every subject could walk to
the palace in an afternoon, would the emperor remain the
emperor? If every stone of the Wall could be seen
from one hill, would it remain the Wall? Distance defeats
administration, but nourishes reverence. Fragmentation weakens defense, but circulates unity.
Ignorance prevents coordination, but protects loyalty.

Once again we encounter the same structure.

Nothing would have been achieved by continuity alone. Nothing would
have been achieved by knowledge alone.

This conclusion is dangerous, not because it is certainly true,
but because it can justify everything. An unfinished wall becomes
proof of farsighted planning. A delayed decree becomes evidence of
imperial magnitude. Administrative failure becomes spiritual success. If a village
starves because grain orders went astray, may one praise the
distance that unites the realm? If a garrison falls through
an unclosed gap, may one say that the gap served
a deeper construction in the national soul?

No conscientious supervisor can accept this. Stone either bears weight
or it does not. A ration arrives or it does
not. Men freeze while philosophers explain the climate.

And yet a conscientious historian cannot dismiss what he sees.
The workers did return renewed. The southern villages did speak
of the Wall. The empire did circulate through us during
construction like blood through an immense body, reaching limbs that
had scarcely known themselves to belong to one another. The
method was impractical, wasteful, sometimes cruel. It was also effective
in a domain not named in the orders.

Perhaps the leadership understood this.

Perhaps it merely benefited from it.

Perhaps "the leadership" is the name we give to consequences
after they have become too large to attribute to anyone.

In my old age, former workers sometimes visit me. We
unfold local plans and try to place our sections in
relation to one another. A man from the western salt
country built seven towers beside red cliffs. Another supervised stairs
through a forest of dwarf pines. I built along a
ridge where two streams descend in opposite directions. We compare
dates, measurements, official seals. Sometimes we persuade ourselves that our
sections must meet. Then a discrepancy appears: a river flowing
east on one plan and west on another, a distance
of sixty miles compressed into six, two towers occupying the
same hill in different provinces.

We laugh, but quietly.

One of us always asks whether the Wall is finished.

No one knows.

Travelers say yes. Soldiers say no. Government notices celebrate completion
and request labor for continuation. Maps show a continuous line,
though mapmakers admit that several northern districts have never been
surveyed. In some temples the Wall is represented as a
dragon encircling the whole earth and biting its own tail.
If that image is correct, then there is no outside,
and the nomads, the capital, the southern villages, the emperor,
and the Wall itself are enclosed together.

Perhaps that was the purpose.

But purpose is the tower we continually attempt to raise
upon foundations we cannot inspect. Each explanation adds a level.
Defense rests upon labor; labor upon morale; morale upon fragmentation;
fragmentation upon unity; unity upon distance; distance upon authority; authority
upon belief; belief upon ignorance. At what point does the
structure cease to rise and begin to fall?

I have spent too many years asking.

There are evenings when I sit at my window, as
the unknown subject sits in the legend. The road below
my house is seldom used now. Grass has grown between
its stones. Sometimes a provincial courier passes, bent over a
tired horse, carrying documents addressed to officials already dead. Sometimes
children play at building the Wall. They make two short
rows of stones and leave a broad gap between them.
When I ask why they do not close it, they
answer that another company will come.

No company comes.

The light fades. The two sections remain, each complete in
itself, each awaiting the other, and the gap between them
fills with shadow. Far to the north, the true Wall
may be standing, collapsing, advancing, or already forgotten. Far to
the center, the emperor may be awake beneath golden roofs,
or dying, or long dead, while a messenger struggles through
the first of innumerable rooms.

I honor the Wall. I honor the emperor. These statements
are sincere.

But sincerity does not close a gap.

There is a thought, suggested by all I have written,
that the remoteness of imperial power—its inability to make itself
present, its failure to deliver the message, its dependence upon
obsolete portraits, delayed decrees, confused dynasties, and ceremonies without certain
objects—is not merely a weakness tolerated by the empire, but
the force by which the empire remains one. It may
be that we are united less by what reaches us
than by what all of us wait for; less by
the commands we obey than by the message each believes
to be delayed on his behalf.

If I pursued this thought, I would have to ask
whether the message exists.

I would have to ask whether there is a messenger.

I would have to ask whether, at the center of
the endless courtyards, anyone ever spoke.

I will not ask.

Certain investigations resemble rivers confined by wise embankments. So long
as they follow their course, they irrigate knowledge. If they
break through, they do not become greater rivers. They become
floods.

Here, therefore, my report ends.

### The Last Chapter

I met Miss Sylvia at the Sea View Hotel, although
nobody who had once known that amiable establishment would have
thought of calling it by so commonplace a name. To
us it was simply the Sea View, as if there
were no other sea and no other view in the
world.

It stood on a low red cliff above a harbour
where white sails went in and out like leisurely thoughts.
The hotel itself was old, broad-verandaed, and rather complacent. Its
carpets had faded to colours not found in any honest
rainbow, and its bells rang only when they felt disposed
to do so; but the cooking was excellent, the sunsets
were beyond criticism, and everybody came back year after year.
This last circumstance proved either the charm of the Sea
View or the incurable folly of humanity. I incline to
the former explanation because I, too, came back.

Miss Sylvia occupied the western corner of the veranda every
fine afternoon. She was sixty then, though no one ever
thought of her as old. Age had merely laid a
dainty silver powder on her abundant hair and written a
few delicate lines about her eyes. She wore black silk,
a little cap of creamy lace, and a heliotrope shawl
which was forever slipping from one shoulder. The slipping was
not wholly accidental. If Miss Sylvia permitted it to remain
down, she desired solitude; if she drew it up and
patted the chair beside her, you might approach.

There she knitted endlessly—white and fluffy, or pink and fluffy,
or pale blue and fluffy. The young women of her
acquaintance were continually being married, and Miss Sylvia provided their
infants with jackets, caps, boots, blankets, and mysterious woollen contrivances
whose uses were mercifully hidden from a bachelor scribbler. She
said that knitting kept her from meddling in other people's
affairs. As she always knew everything that happened in the
hotel, I doubted its complete success in that respect.

Young people adored her. They brought her their engagements, quarrels,
poems, and unsuitable plans. She listened without superiority, which is
perhaps the rarest virtue possessed by sixty. She never laughed
at a confidence, although she laughed often enough at the
person who made it, and the distinction was understood.

As for me, I was twenty-seven, moderately vain, immoderately hopeful,
and had just sold my sixth story to a magazine
whose editor had rejected my previous seventeen. This success gave
me an agreeable consciousness of literary importance which Miss Sylvia
took pains to correct.

"Another masterpiece?" she would ask when she saw manuscript paper
under my arm.

"Possibly the masterpiece," I replied.

"My dear boy, you said that of the last one."

"The last one was the masterpiece of my youth. This
is the masterpiece of my maturity."

"Then your old age will overtake you before dinner."

It was impossible to be offended with Miss Sylvia. Her
smile had something of a girl's mischief in it, and
when she laughed her face became wondrously young. I soon
formed the habit of taking my stories to her before
I sent them away. She had no theories concerning art
and despised the expression "human document," but she knew when
a character had behaved like a cad, which is more
than can be said for certain distinguished critics.

One August evening I read her a story called "The
Courage of Silence." I have since changed the title, and
would willingly change several other things about it, but at
the time I considered it subtle.

The hero, Gerald, loved a maiden named Elinor. Gerald learned
that he had inherited an incurable malady and could not
live a year. Being a young man of lofty ideals
and deplorable common sense, he went away without telling Elinor
that he loved her. He wished to spare her the
grief of losing him. Elinor believed herself indifferent until he
had gone; then, of course, she discovered that she loved
him. Gerald died nobly in a foreign land, and Elinor
married a deserving man with no imagination whatever. My story
ended with her watching the sunset and remembering Gerald.

I finished the last page in a glow of satisfaction.
The actual sunset assisted me. Beyond the harbour the clouds
were pale gold and rose, heaped one above another like
Miss Sylvia's fluffy knitting. A path of crimson light ran
across the water, and the little waves broke on the
shore with a sound as soft as whispered regret.

Miss Sylvia did not speak. Her needles lay idle in
her lap.

"Well?" I prompted at last.

"You must change it."

The glow of satisfaction went out.

"My editor may think otherwise."

"Your editor is probably a man."

"He is. But I have never regarded that as a
fatal defect."

"Gerald must tell Elinor."

"That would spoil the story. The whole point is his
renunciation."

"It is not renunciation. It is cruelty."

"My dear Miss Sylvia, he wants to spare her."

"Spare her!" She leaned toward me, and her eyes were
suddenly bright and troubled. "He leaves her to wonder all
her life. He lets her think that perhaps he did
not care—that perhaps she had imagined every look, every word,
every little happiness. No, no. He must tell her. Even
if he is dying, especially if he is dying, he
must tell her the truth."

"But suppose she loved him? Would not the truth make
his death harder for her?"

"Not so hard as doubt."

Her voice had changed. It was no longer the serene
voice with which she discussed babies' jackets and foolish engagements.
Beneath it sounded the tremulous cry of some younger woman,
long silent but not dead.

I laid my manuscript down.

"Forgive me," I said. "I did not know."

Miss Sylvia looked out over the harbour. The heliotrope shawl
slipped from her shoulder and remained there. I should have
gone away, according to the law of the shawl; but
she put one hand on my sleeve.

"Stay. I want to tell you something. I have never
told anyone, but your story brought it back. Not that
it has ever been far away."

For a little while she was silent. A gull wheeled
above the cliff, white against the fading rose. From the
parlour came the sound of someone industriously murdering a nocturne.
Miss Sylvia folded her thin hands over the pale green
wool in her lap.

"I was twenty when I met Alan," she said.

The name meant nothing to me then.

"He was twenty-three, tall and dark, with grave grey eyes.
He was not strong. People called him delicate, as if
illness were a refinement. We were together all that summer
at my aunt's house near the shore. It was a
beautiful summer—too beautiful, perhaps. There were long blue days when
we sailed, and evenings when we walked through the dunes
after sunset. He read poetry to me, very badly. I
did not care how he read it."

She smiled, but her smile quivered.

"Did you know that you loved him?" I asked.

"Not at first. I was a vain little thing and
had several beaux. Alan never flattered me. Sometimes I thought
he hardly noticed me; then I would look up and
find his eyes upon me with an expression I could
not understand. He remembered everything I said, even foolish things.
Once I admired a spray of wild roses on the
cliff. The next morning it was beside my plate. There
was no note. Alan was like that—never a word when
a word was most needed.

"At the end of August he went away. Abruptly. He
told my aunt that business called him home. To me
he said only, 'Good-bye, Sylvia. I hope you will be
very happy.' I was too proud to ask when he
would return. I laughed and told him that happiness had
always been a habit of mine."

"And he never wrote?"

"Once—a brief letter at Christmas. He asked after everyone. Everyone,
you understand. He did not ask after me in particular.
In the spring we heard that he had gone to
California for his health. Six months later he died there."

She paused. The sunset had faded, leaving the harbour pearl-grey
and infinitely lonely.

"I knew then that I loved him," she went on.
"Perhaps I had known before, but pride is a skilful
liar. When word came of his death, it seemed to
me that all warmth had gone out of the world.
Yet grief was not the worst of it. If I
had known that he loved me, I could have borne
grief. I should have had something beautiful and certain to
keep. But I did not know. I have never known."

"He must have cared," I said.

"Must he? I have told myself so a thousand times.
Then I have told myself that I was a romantic
girl who mistook kindness for love. Alan was kind to
everyone. There were days when certainty seemed near enough to
touch, and nights when I thought myself an old fool
for remembering. Forty years is a long time to ask
one question."

She turned to me, and her face in the dusk
was not old. It was the face of the girl
who had laughed when her heart begged her to speak.

"That is why you must change your story. Do not
let Elinor wonder. Let Gerald play the man and tell
her. Silence may look noble to the person who keeps
it, but it can be very cruel to the person
who must interpret it."

I promised. Authors make promises more readily than revisions, but
I kept this one. Gerald confessed his love; Elinor gave
him six months of radiant happiness; and he died with
her hand in his. The editor said the altered ending
was sentimental and accepted it at once. I have never
been able to determine whether this proves the editor right
or merely human.

Miss Sylvia said little when I gave her the magazine.

"It is better," she whispered. "It is kinder."

Summer ended. The young people scattered to cities, colleges, offices,
and marriages. Miss Sylvia returned to the quiet inland town
where she lived with a widowed cousin. I went to
Montreal and became involved in the feverish business of making
literature pay rent. We exchanged letters—hers charming and punctual, mine
affectionate and disgracefully irregular. She knitted me a grey muffler
which I wore even in mild weather because it possessed
more friendship than beauty.

That winter my father's elder brother died. Uncle Robert had
been a collector of family relics and a destroyer of
family patience. His house contained five generations of cracked china,
unpaid bills, obsolete medicines, daguerreotypes of severe babies, and chairs
which nobody dared sit upon. Since my father was abroad,
the pleasant task of sorting these treasures fell to me.

I postponed it until April.

Spring had come reluctantly that year. Snow still lingered under
the cedars, but the fields were breathing moisture, and the
willows along the lane wore a pale green mist. Robins
hopped over the wet lawn with an air of having
personally arranged the season. The sky changed from silver to
blue and back again within an hour. It was a
fickle, tender, secretive spring, full of small sounds and unaccountable
hopes.

For three days I worked through Uncle Robert's belongings. On
the fourth I climbed to the attic and found a
cedar trunk beneath the eaves. It bore the initials A.B.

Those initials stirred a vague memory. My grandmother had had
a younger brother, Alan Blair, who died before I was
born. His portrait hung in our old family home, but
as a child I had been more interested in the
brass frame than in the melancholy young gentleman within it.
I knew only that he had gone west for his
health and died unmarried.

The lock yielded after an argument. Inside were a few
books, a packet of letters from his mother, a faded
blue necktie, two photographs, and a narrow journal bound in
brown leather.

The first photograph was of Alan. I held it near
the attic window.

There are moments when coincidence ceases to be amusing. The
young man had grave grey eyes, dark hair brushed back
from a broad forehead, and a sensitive, unsmiling mouth. On
the back, in my grandmother's writing, were the words: "Alan,
aged twenty-three, taken before his last summer at the shore."

His last summer at the shore.

My hands had begun to tremble before I looked at
the second photograph. It showed a girl in a white
dress, standing beside a low stone wall. She wore a
broad hat and held a cluster of wild roses. Time
had changed her, of course, but I knew the curve
of the cheek, the proud little tilt of the head,
the eyes which even in a faded picture seemed about
to laugh.

Beneath it Alan had written one word.

"Sylvia."

I sat down on a trunk, indifferent to dust, dignity,
and the probable presence of spiders. Outside, rain began softly
upon the roof. I opened the journal.

The early pages dealt with college, books, and family matters.
Then came the summer by the sea. Alan did not
write every day, and he was no great stylist—a humiliating
discovery for his literary nephew—but the plainness of his words
gave them a force no ornament could have improved.

"June 17. Sylvia came today. I had forgotten that anyone
could bring so much light into a room. She laughed
at my solemnity and made me sail with her. I
was tired afterward, but I would go again if it
killed me."

A later entry said:

"July 2. She wore blue and had wild roses at
her belt. I nearly told her. What right have I?
The doctor may be wrong. I cling to that, though
in my heart I know he is not."

Then:

"July 29. We walked beyond the dunes. Sylvia spoke of
the future as young people do, scattering years before them
as if years were autumn leaves and equally plentiful. She
asked what I meant to do. I said I might
go to California. She looked startled and then laughed. If
she cared, she would not laugh. It is better so."

I stopped reading and said something uncomplimentary about masculine intelligence.
The attic gave no answer.

The entries grew shorter.

"August 10. A bad night. There was blood again. The
doctor says I must leave before autumn. He gives me
less than a year."

"August 13. I love Sylvia. There is no use writing
around the truth here, though I must keep it from
her. I love her beyond wisdom, beyond hope, beyond life.
If I tell her and she loves me, I bind
grief upon her. If she does not, I burden her
with pity. Silence is the only courage left to me."

I thought of Miss Sylvia's voice: Not courage. Cruelty.

"August 24. She admired the roses on the cliff yesterday.
I left them by her plate this morning. She knew
they were from me. I saw it in her eyes,
but neither of us spoke. God help me, I wanted
her to understand, and yet I pray she did not."

"August 31. I said good-bye. She was gay and cold
and beautiful. She told me happiness was her habit. I
believe her. I must believe her."

The next entry had been written months later in a
shaking hand.

"December 21. Wrote to her aunt. I dared not write
to Sylvia alone. Asked after everyone because her name by
itself would betray me. I have carried her picture through
every mile of this journey. The doctors promise nothing now."

The final pages were dated in California.

"February 8. The hills are green here, and there are
flowers even in winter. I wonder whether the snow lies
deep at home. Sylvia loved summer. I hope she is
dancing tonight."

"March 14. I dreamed that we walked beside the sea.
She asked me why I went away. I could not
answer."

"April 3. Mother writes that Sylvia is well and much
admired. She will marry, of course. Some strong, fortunate man
will give her all I could not. I am trying
to be glad."

"May 1. I have been wrong, perhaps. Pain makes a
coward of certainty. If she cared, I have left her
nothing—not even truth. But it is too late now, and
I cannot summon her across a continent to watch me
die."

The last entry filled only half a page.

"May 19. Very weak. Sunset on the mountains, gold above
and violet below. It reminds me of the shawl Sylvia
wore that last cool evening. I have loved her every
day. If there is any mercy beyond this life, let
her somehow know."

The words blurred. I am not ashamed to say that
I cried, though I should prefer it not to be
reported to certain critics who have accused my work of
excessive tenderness.

For forty years Miss Sylvia had asked her question. The
answer had waited in a cedar trunk under a dusty
roof.

I left for her town the next morning.

She lived in a small cream-coloured house behind a row
of budding lilacs. I found her in the parlour beside
a window, knitting something pale pink and fluffy. She wore
her black silk and lace cap, and the heliotrope shawl
lay about her shoulders. For a moment the years between
the hotel veranda and that spring morning vanished.

"My dear boy!" she exclaimed. "You should have warned me.
I would have made a cake."

"I came on important business."

"Then I am glad there is no cake. Important business
and cake seldom improve one another."

She smiled, but when she saw my face she laid
down her needles.

"What is it?"

I put the photograph of Alan on her lap.

The colour left her cheeks. She did not touch the
picture at first. Then one finger moved over the faded
outline of his face with infinite gentleness.

"Where did you get this?"

"He was my great-uncle. Alan Blair."

She looked from the photograph to me, and I knew
suddenly why, on our first summer together, she had sometimes
watched me with a puzzled tenderness. I had his eyebrows,
perhaps, or a turn of the head inherited through the
careless generosity of blood.

"Your uncle," she whispered. "I never knew."

"I found his journal."

Her hands closed upon the edge of the shawl.

"Does it—does it speak of me?"

"On every page that matters."

I read the journal to her. Not all of it;
there were passages belonging only to Alan and the silence
in which he had written them. But I read of
the wild roses, the walks beyond the dunes, the farewell,
the journey west, and the love he had carried to
his death.

Miss Sylvia did not weep while I read. She sat
very still, with the old photograph against her heart. Only
once she interrupted me.

"Read that again."

It was the sentence: "I have loved her every day."

I read it again.

When I finished, the room was full of spring sunshine.
A spray of lilac tapped softly at the pane, and
somewhere down the street a child was calling to another
child. The ordinary world went on, serenely unconscious that an
old grief had just received its answer.

Miss Sylvia lifted her face. It was wet now, but
radiant—so radiant that she seemed not young again, but something
better than young: a woman whose faith had crossed forty
years of darkness and found dawn waiting.

"He loved me," she said.

"Yes."

"He truly loved me."

"All his life."

She laughed then, a small tremulous laugh through tears.

"Oh, foolish Alan! Dear, foolish Alan! He thought he was
sparing me. And I thought—sometimes I thought I had invented
it all. But he loved me. Nothing can hurt me
now. Not the years, not his going, not even his
death. It is over."

She pressed the journal to her breast.

"No," she corrected softly. "Not over. Completed."

I had intended to conduct myself with dignity. But dignity,
like art, occasionally becomes an impertinence. I bent over Miss
Sylvia and put my arms around her.

"I think," I said, "that Uncle Alan would wish me
to do this."

Then I kissed her smooth, faded cheek—once for myself, because
I loved her, and once for the young man who
had loved her in silence beside the summer sea.

Her heliotrope shawl slipped to the floor, and neither of
us remembered to pick it up.

### Easter Passage

At forty, Colonel Nikolai Andreyevich Voznitsin began to grow old.

This did not mean that he stooped, or coughed in
the mornings, or counted the steps to the third floor;
on the contrary, he was broad in the shoulders, his
moustache was still almost entirely black, and at reviews he
could sit a horse for six hours without afterwards acknowledging
so much as a stiffness in the knee. But there
are men whose old age begins in the bones, and
others in the soul; and in Voznitsin it had begun
where no doctor could find it. Pleasures had become suspiciously
alike. Wine warmed him without making him merry; women pleased
and wearied him in the same hour; music, which at
twenty had shaken his heart as a storm shakes a
young birch, now produced reflections concerning the orchestra, the heat
of the room and the absurd neck of the gentleman
in front.

He noticed, too, that he had begun to speak of
death, not as a religious mystery or a poetical misfortune,
but as a personal inconvenience. When some old general died,
Nikolai Andreyevich no longer said: "He had a fine career,"
but calculated the dead man's age; when a comrade of
his own years was carried off by fever, he remembered
for several days a certain heaviness under his right rib.
Most alarming of all, he had ceased to be surprised.
Rain, love affairs, dishonesty, courage, the birth of children, the
blossoming of orchards—everything seemed to him to have happened already,
and more than once.

This condition, by no means rare and by no means
original, he nevertheless regarded as a private catastrophe.

On his way south in the spring, being free for
several days, he stopped in Moscow and resolved to revisit
the places of his childhood. He expected no miracle, of
course; officers of forty are ashamed of miracles. Yet secretly,
beneath his scepticism, there stirred a hope that some courtyard,
some bell, some remembered smell of apples or varnished school
desks would open in him a concealed door, and that
through it would run the boy he had once been—thin-legged,
credulous, easily wounded, always hungry, and capable of happiness for
no better reason than that the sun was shining on
a red wall.

He first went to the house where his aunt had
lived. The old green mansion had disappeared. In its place
stood a yellow building with plate-glass windows, and behind those
windows were ladies' hats displayed upon stands shaped like decapitated
heads. The courtyard, where he and the Yurlof children had
once dug a fortress in the snow and defended it
against three cook's boys, was roofed over with iron and
smelled of paraffin, damp cloth and cabbage soup. A porter
in an apron told him there had never been a
garden there.

"There was a lilac bush by the gate," Voznitsin said.

"Possibly before the fire," replied the porter, and looked at
his medals.

At the gymnasium he found the staircase narrower than he
remembered, the assembly hall smaller, and the bronze bust of
the emperor newly polished and stupidly cheerful. A class was
reciting geography behind a closed door. The thin, uncertain voices
named rivers and provinces; chalk squeaked; somebody was ordered to
stand in the corner. Nikolai Andreyevich waited for his heart
to contract. It did not. He smelled dust, ink and
wet wool, and thought only that boys had a disagreeable
odour when kept together in heated rooms.

In the Church of Saint Nicholas, where his mother had
taken him on feast days, there were new icons, new
gilding and an old beggar who seized his sleeve with
professional tenderness. The candles made the same little blue hearts
at their bases; incense drifted beneath the cupola; a deacon's
voice rolled downward like warm, dark honey. Still nothing. Voznitsin
crossed himself, gave the beggar a silver coin, and went
out almost resentfully.

Toward evening he wandered to the boulevard. The snow had
gone from the open places, but grey ridges remained under
the benches; water ran busily along the gutters, carrying straws,
matchsticks and the sodden husks of sunflower seeds. The first
poplar buds filled the air with their penetrating bitterness. That
smell had once meant freedom to him—the removal of double
windows, muddy boots, Easter, excursions beyond the town—and he breathed
it in deeply, even greedily. But the memory did not
come. There was only a smell, agreeable and sad, and
beyond it an emptiness which no effort could fill.

"So that is all," he thought. "The places remain, or
they do not remain; but in either case they are
silent."

He dined alone, drank more wine than he wanted, and
afterward stood before the hotel mirror, examining the two silver
hairs which had lately appeared above his left temple. Under
the electric light his face seemed not old but completed,
as though no essential alteration could now occur in it
except decay.

"Old age," he said aloud.

The words sounded theatrical, and he smiled; yet during the
night he woke twice with the same words in his
mind.

From Moscow he travelled to Odessa, intending to take the
steamer to Sevastopol and then continue to his regiment in
Crimea. It was Easter Eve. Odessa lay beneath a pale,
windy sky; the sea was the colour of a sword
blade, and the steep streets leading down toward the harbour
glittered after rain. Cabs rattled over the stones, Greek merchants
shouted, bells rang from churches hidden among warehouses, and everywhere
women carried covered baskets from which came the warm smells
of saffron bread, smoked fish and vanilla.

The steamer, the *Saint Olga*, was small, freshly painted and
almost empty. Most sensible travellers, the clerk informed him, preferred
to spend Easter on land rather than among ropes, seasick
children and indifferent cooks. Voznitsin found this circumstance pleasant. The
wet deck shone; brass fittings glimmered in the milky light;
from below came the odours of hot metal, varnish, oranges,
tar and something anciently marine—seaweed, rope and fish scales—which no
cleanliness could conquer.

He placed his valise in the cabin and returned to
the deck as the last cargo was loaded. A cage
containing two turkeys stood beside the hatch. The birds, naked-headed
and mournful, thrust their necks forward with the solemnity of
antediluvian monsters. Near them a sailor slept upon a coil
of rope, one cheek flattened against his sleeve.

Only three first-class passengers were visible: a stout commercial traveller
who immediately disappeared into the dining saloon, a young woman
in a blue cloak, and an older lady seated beneath
the awning. The older lady was perhaps thirty-eight, perhaps forty-five—an
age which Voznitsin, where women were concerned, ordinarily judged with
cruel accuracy. Her figure had thickened; her hair, under a
travelling hat, was threaded with grey; the skin around her
eyes was delicately folded. She wore gloves of good quality,
though one had been mended near the thumb, and held
on her knees a small leather case.

There was nothing remarkable in her appearance. Nevertheless, as Voznitsin
passed, she raised her eyes, and something in the movement—quick,
expectant, almost mischievous—made him look again.

She, too, looked at him.

They bowed with the involuntary politeness of travellers who expect
to share several meals and perhaps a storm. He walked
to the stern. The gangway was drawn up; the water
widened between the steamer and the quay; handkerchiefs fluttered, though
hardly anyone on shore had come to bid farewell to
those aboard. Odessa began to move slowly away, its houses
rising in pale terraces, its cupolas and chimneys suspended in
a rose-grey mist.

When Voznitsin turned, the lady was still watching him.

"Forgive me," she said. "Are you not Nikolai Andreyevich Voznitsin?"

Her voice struck him more forcibly than her eyes had
done. It was deeper than the voice he remembered, and
careful, as women's voices become after years of ordering servants
and calming children; but beneath it sounded a lighter note,
teasing and swift.

"Yes," he replied. "And you—"

She smiled, and in the smile her ageing face was
for one instant displaced by another: a narrow brown face
sprinkled with freckles, with red lips and eyes that never
asked permission before laughing.

"Lenotchka Yurlof!"

"Elena Sergeyevna Lvova now."

"Lenotchka!"

He seized both her hands. The leather case fell from
her knees; neither noticed it. Voznitsin looked at her forehead,
her cheeks, the grey hair beside her ear, and then
again at her eyes. Recognition did not occur all at
once; it advanced in waves, the woman alternating with the
girl, so that he saw now Madame Lvova, respectably dressed
and a little tired, now Lenotchka running through a garden
with one stocking falling down.

"Kolya," she said softly. "How extraordinary!"

No one had called him Kolya for many years. The
name seemed to touch him physically, like a hand laid
on his bare head.

They sat side by side. For a while they could
only laugh and ask questions without waiting for answers.

"Your mother?"

"Dead, twelve years ago."

"And Sergei?"

"Poor Seryozha died in Turkestan. Fever. You remember how he
was always catching beetles?"

"And Katya?"

"A doctor, imagine! In Tver. She terrifies the whole province
and cures half of it."

"You are married?"

"Of course. To Dmitri Pavlovich Lvov. He serves in the
Ministry of Finance. A very good man—you need not look
so solemn. And I have a daughter."

"A daughter!"

"Seventeen. She is below, quarrelling with a trunk."

"And you?" she asked. "Wife? Children?"

He shook his head.

"Neither?"

"Neither."

Elena Sergeyevna looked at him with that directness which in
childhood had disconcerted him. Then she sighed.

"You always took too long to decide, Kolya."

He laughed, but the words wounded him a little, perhaps
because they were true. The harbour receded; the shore became
purple; gulls flew after the ship, hanging almost motionless in
the wind.

They spoke of houses, servants, cousins, teachers, dogs. Each name
rose for a moment like a face at a window
and vanished. Old Matvei, the coachman who drank furniture polish;
Mademoiselle Blanche, who claimed to be French but came from
Riga; the red setter Trezor, who had stolen an Easter
cake and been sick beneath the grand piano. With every
recollection Elena's gestures grew younger. She touched his sleeve when
she laughed; she narrowed her eyes before repeating some absurd
phrase; once, forgetting the years, she cried, "Nasty, horrid boy!"
exactly as she had cried it in the Yurlofs' garden.

And then Moscow, which had shown him nothing, opened within
him.

The Yurlofs' house stood again at the end of a
narrow lane, behind a wooden fence painted green. Its veranda
smelled of sun-warmed boards and cherry jam; in the pantry
lay rows of apples upon newspaper, and in the damp
corner of the garden black-currant shoots gave off their sharp,
almost medicinal fragrance when broken. He had spent there all
his holidays until his sixteenth year, because his mother and
Elena's mother were cousins, or had been educated together, or
were bound by one of those relationships which children accept
without understanding.

At first Lenotchka had been only one of the children.
They rolled down slopes, climbed trees, persecuted the turkeys, stole
cucumbers and carried on together like young puppies, without modesty,
calculation or tenderness. He had pulled her braid; she had
bitten his wrist. Once they had spent an entire afternoon
constructing a funeral for a dead frog, and Lenotchka, acting
as priest, had excommunicated him for laughing.

Then, during one winter away at school, something happened.

He returned the following summer and found that Lenotchka had
become a young woman.

The transformation seemed to him indecently sudden. She was fifteen.
Her dresses reached her ankles; her dark braid had been
wound upon her head; beneath the thin muslin at her
breast were two mysterious curves which he tried not to
see and saw continually. She no longer ran when called,
but approached slowly, with a gravity contradicted by laughter trembling
at the corners of her mouth. She knew that he
looked at her. That knowledge had awakened in her an
innocent cruelty, a feminine coquetry still small and playful, like
a kitten sharpening its claws upon a velvet chair.

At dinner she asked him to pass the salt, although
it stood beside her hand. When he obeyed, their fingers
touched.

"Thank you, Nikolai Andreyevich," she said ceremoniously.

He knocked over a glass.

She danced that autumn with a cavalry cornet named Bestuzhev,
who had yellow moustaches and jingling spurs. Voznitsin, still in
his school uniform, stood behind a palm and experienced hatred
in its purest form. Bestuzhev's hand lay on Lenotchka's waist;
her head bent toward his epaulette; the room turned with
them, full of lamps, perfume and the dry whispering of
silk.

Later she found Nikolai on the dark veranda.

"Why are you sulking?"

"I am not sulking."

"You look as if someone had stolen your last kopeck."

"Go and dance with your officer."

"Ah! So that is it."

She leaned near him. He smelled violets in her hair
and felt that if she remained another second he would
say something monstrous. She waited, enjoying his suffering without comprehending
its depth; then she laughed and ran inside.

From that evening he loved her.

He loved her morning and night, at lessons and at
prayer, in dreams and during the most commonplace occupations. The
very letters of her name acquired splendour. He wrote them
in margins and erased them. He composed speeches while walking
to school, but in her presence could say only, "Is
Sergei at home?" He imagined rescuing her from fire, wolves,
drowning, bandits and dishonour; in these fantasies he was shot
through the chest but remained standing long enough to place
her safely in her mother's arms.

The Easter of the following year came early. Snow still
lay in the garden hollows, blackened by twigs and soot,
while the paths were wet and shining. During Holy Week
the house was full of activity. Copper bowls rang in
the kitchen; almonds were chopped; eggs were dyed purple, yellow
and sapphire blue; the cook, crimson from the oven, moved
among cakes as among sacred monuments. On Thursday the children—though
Lenotchka now objected to being counted among them—coloured eggs with
onion skins and scraps of silk. Her sleeves were rolled
above the elbow. A blue stain marked the inside of
her wrist.

Nikolai watched that wrist until she caught him.

"What are you staring at?"

"Nothing."

"Nasty, horrid boy."

She flicked water at him.

On Easter Eve they went to the midnight service. The
air was cold and fragrant with thawing earth. The whole
household walked except Elena's mother, who went in the carriage
with the old aunt. Lanterns moved along the lane; from
distant quarters of Moscow came bells answering one another, now
near and brazen, now soft as if ringing beneath water.

Inside the church, heat, wax and incense enveloped them. The
crowd pressed close. Faces shone in candlelight; soldiers, merchants, old
women, servants and children waited with the same grave expectancy.
Nikolai stood behind Lenotchka. A loose strand of hair curled
against her neck. Whenever the crowd moved, her shoulder touched
his chest, and each touch ran through him with such
violence that he scarcely heard the prayers.

Then the doors opened, voices rose, and joy, immense and
public, broke over the people.

"Christ is risen!"

"He is risen indeed!"

They kissed—men with men, women with women, relatives, acquaintances, even
strangers. Bells poured their sound into the night. In the
churchyard flames trembled in the wind, and the snow beyond
the fence shone bluish under the stars.

Returning home, the young people ran ahead of the elders.
Sergei and Katya began a contest, tapping coloured eggs together
to see whose shell was strongest. Someone threw a snowball.
Lenotchka slipped through the gate and went toward the garden,
saying she had lost a glove.

Nikolai followed.

He knew very well that she had both gloves, one
in each hand.

The garden was neither winter nor spring. Snow remained beneath
the fir trees, but the earth beside the path was
bare and breathing; water dripped; last year's leaves, black and
glossy, gave out a bitter smell. Above them the branches
were still naked, and through their net could be seen
a sky flaming faintly in the east.

Lenotchka stood near the currant bushes.

"Why did you follow me?" she asked.

"You lost a glove."

She lifted both hands.

"So I did not."

He stopped several paces away. His heart struck so heavily
that it seemed to shake his collar. All the speeches
he had composed deserted him.

"Lenotchka…"

"What?"

"Lenotchka…"

"Well?"

"I love you."

She lowered her hands. For the first time since he
had known her, she seemed unable to answer. He took
one step, then another.

"I love you," he repeated. "I love you. I have
loved you all year. When I am away, I think
only of you. When I am here, I cannot speak.
I would die for you. I know you think I
am a schoolboy, but I—Lenotchka, I love you…"

It was absurd, solemn, excessive; it was also entirely true.
Tears stood in his eyes. She looked frightened, and then
moved, and then—he never knew whether from tenderness, curiosity or
the contagion of that holy night—she held out her hands.

He seized them and kissed them, first the gloves and
then, because one glove had loosened, the warm skin at
her wrist. She whispered his name. He drew her toward
him.

Her body, so familiar in games and so wholly unknown
now, yielded for an instant. He felt her waist beneath
his arm, the soft pressure of her breast, the coolness
of her cheek and the warmth of her mouth. The
kiss was clumsy. Their teeth touched; his cap fell off;
somewhere close by a branch released a load of melted
snow with a wet thump. Yet no later embrace, however
skilful or desired, ever possessed for him that terror, that
trembling gratitude, that sense that all creation—the bells, the stars,
the wet earth and the rising dawn—had entered his blood.

Then she pushed him away.

"Oh, Kolya…"

"Lenotchka!"

"You must not."

"But you—"

"I know. I do not know. Let me go."

She was flushed; her lips were damp; her eyes, enormous
in the uncertain light, looked almost angry. He tried again
to take her hand.

"Nasty, horrid boy," she whispered, but she was near tears.

From the house Sergei called them. Lenotchka stooped, picked up
his cap and thrust it against his chest.

"Not a word to anyone."

She ran away.

At breakfast she would not look at him. The long
table glittered with silver, coloured eggs and sugar; everyone ate
cold meats after the fast and spoke too loudly from
fatigue. Lenotchka sat between the old aunt and Cornet Bestuzhev.
Nikolai could scarcely swallow. Yet beneath the table, for one
second, the toe of her slipper touched his boot—or perhaps
he imagined it.

During the weeks that followed she was kind to him
when others were present, but avoided being alone with him.
If he entered the garden, she went to the house.
If he found her in the library, she called Katya.
Once he cornered her on the veranda and begged only
to know whether she loved him.

"Do not ask me such things," she said.

"Then you do not?"

"I like you, Kolya. I have always liked you."

"Like!"

"You are behaving foolishly."

"What was that night, then?"

She reddened and became severe.

"It was Easter. Everyone kisses at Easter."

The explanation was cruel, ingenious and not wholly false. He
suffered as only the young suffer—that is, without perspective and
with a secret enjoyment of the wound. By autumn he
was sent away. Letters were attempted, torn up, sent, unanswered,
answered in three lines. Years began their ordinary work. His
mother died; the Yurlofs moved; he entered the army; other
women came, and departures, promotions, cards, gun smoke, railway stations,
debts, campaigns and rented rooms. Yet the damp garden remained
in him, untouched. Lenotchka at fifteen remained beneath the bare
trees, holding out her stained wrist.

"And I truly said it was because everyone kisses at
Easter?" Elena Sergeyevna asked.

The steamer had entered open water. Evening lay over the
sea in bands of pearl and violet, and the first
star showed above the darkening shore. Voznitsin had spoken for
a long time, sometimes aloud, sometimes only in fragments which
she completed.

"You said exactly that."

"How heartless girls are! Though perhaps I was frightened."

"You were wise."

"No. Only frightened. And vain. I knew you loved me;
it was agreeable to know it. One is not proud
of such things afterward."

He looked at her. For a moment the two faces
again occupied the same space: the girl, flushed and evasive,
and the woman, candid through weariness.

"Did you forget it?" he asked.

She smoothed the mended place on her glove.

"No."

The ship's bell sounded. From the saloon came the clatter
of plates and the commercial traveller's voice demanding horseradish. A
sailor passed carrying a lantern, and the ordinary world, with
its meals, draughts and schedules, closed gently around them.

A light step sounded behind Voznitsin.

"Mama, they say supper is ready. Oh—I beg your pardon."

He turned.

The young woman in the blue cloak stood there. She
had taken off her hat, and the wind lifted dark
curls from her forehead. She was taller than her mother
had been, and paler, but the eyes were the same—not
identical, for nature does not copy like a clerk, yet
filled with the same bright impatience; and when she smiled
uncertainly, he saw the little movement at the corner of
the mouth which had preceded Lenotchka's laughter forty years before.

"This is my daughter," Elena said. "Elena Dmitrievna. We call
her Lenotchka."

The girl held out her hand.

"I have heard of you, Nikolai Andreyevich."

"Nothing good, I hope."

"Mama says you pulled her braid and ruined a funeral."

"A frog's funeral," Elena explained.

"And he has just accused me of being heartless."

"I was sixteen."

"I was fifteen."

The younger Lenotchka laughed. The sound was clear and careless;
it seemed to fly out across the water. She bent
to fasten her cloak, and from her hair came a
faint scent—not violets, but fresh soap and the green sharpness
of crushed currant leaves. Perhaps the scent was truly there;
perhaps memory supplied it. Voznitsin did not inquire. For the
first time in many months, he felt no need to
distrust happiness merely because it had arrived without proof.

The girl ran below to reserve their places. Elena rose
more slowly.

"She is the age I was then," she said.

"Yes."

"Sometimes she looks at me as if I had been
born old. She cannot imagine that I once ran, lied,
flirted, kissed boys in gardens."

"One boy."

"Perhaps one."

They walked toward the rail. Night had not yet fully
descended. Behind the steamer the wake stretched pale and luminous,
continually destroyed and continually renewed. Far away, the shore burned
with scattered lights; above it the sunset smouldered like the
last embers beneath a church censer.

Voznitsin thought of Elena's mother, of beetle-catching Sergei dead in
Turkestan, of his own mother under the Moscow earth, of
the green house replaced by a shop, of the schoolboys
reciting rivers in rooms where no one remembered him. All
that had seemed in Moscow to prove only extinction. But
here was Elena's hand on the rail, older and recognisable;
here was her daughter carrying in her face a gesture
which had crossed years and death without knowing that it
travelled.

The past, he understood, does not remain waiting for us
in houses. Timber rots, gardens are cut down, churches are
regilded, and a porter denies the lilac bush. Nor does
youth sleep whole inside the heart, ready to rise at
the command of a smell or a street. It changes
its dwelling. A word passes into another mouth, courage into
another action, the inclination of a head into another body;
what one person has loved alters what he touches, and
that altered thing proceeds beyond him. Nothing returns exactly. Nothing
is entirely lost.

This thought, too, was perhaps not original. But truth does
not become less consoling because many have needed it.

"I believed I had grown old," he said.

Elena glanced at his hair.

"You have."

"So have you."

"Thank you, Kolya."

"No, listen. I thought old age meant that everything had
ended behind us, and we went on merely because the
body had not received the order to stop. But that
is foolish. Sergei is dead, your mother is dead, mine
is dead; one day we shall be dead. Yet something
continues—our features, our foolish phrases, the harm we have done,
the kindness too, if there was any; even a kiss
under wet trees may continue, not as a kiss, perhaps,
but as tenderness somewhere else. Life keeps rising from the
dead. Not we ourselves—not in the childish way we desire—but
life."

Elena's eyes filled with tears. She smiled and touched his
sleeve.

"You always did make speeches too long."

"And you always ran away before the end."

"Nasty, horrid boy."

Below, the first bells of Easter began to ring from
the little chapel in the stern; at once, from the
distant shore, other bells answered, faint but innumerable. The commercial
traveller emerged with a red face, crossed himself and kissed
the steward. Sailors called to one another. The two imprisoned
turkeys awakened and protested with prehistoric indignation.

The younger Lenotchka appeared at the companionway, holding three coloured
eggs.

"Christ is risen!" she cried.

"He is risen indeed," Voznitsin answered.

She gave him a sapphire-blue egg. Their fingers touched, and
the touch was only the touch of a young hand
offering a painted shell to an old family friend; yet
he received it with reverence.

At Sevastopol, where they were to separate, dawn was whitening
the harbour. Elena's husband's servant waited on the quay, and
porters shouted around the luggage. The daughter ran ahead. Elena
and Voznitsin stood for one last moment beside the gangway.

"Will we meet again?" she asked.

"Certainly," he said, though both heard the uncertainty.

She offered her cheek. He kissed it, feeling the cool
skin, the fine lines near her eye, and beneath them—not
hidden, not restored, but mysteriously continued—the warmth of Lenotchka.

"Goodbye, Kolya."

"Goodbye, Lenotchka."

They smiled through tears, a little ashamed and therefore all
the more tenderly. Then she went down to the quay,
where her daughter turned and lifted both arms toward her.

Voznitsin remained at the rail. The harbour smelled of pitch,
salt water, oranges and spring rain. Above the roofs the
east flamed gold. Bells rang over the waking city, and
the sea, touched by the new light, broke into a
thousand restless fires.

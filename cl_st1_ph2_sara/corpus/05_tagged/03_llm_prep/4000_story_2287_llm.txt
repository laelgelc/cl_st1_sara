### Many Years

Father Olympus was so large that, when he passed beneath
the low gate of the bishop's courtyard, strangers involuntarily bent
their own heads. His shoulders appeared to have been designed
for carrying bells, his chest resembled the polished casing of
a municipal fire engine, and above the broad purple face
rose a mane of iron-grey hair which no comb had
ever completely subdued.

Children followed him in the streets. Shopkeepers came to their
doors. Ladies peered through carriage windows. Everybody knew him, though
few knew anything about him except that on feast days
he could shake the crystal pendants of the cathedral chandeliers
with a single low note.

"Listen! Olympus is giving us the bottom doh!" people would
whisper with delighted alarm.

His voice was his glory, his bread, his private temptation,
and almost his theology.

Singers, like actors and unusually handsome men, are seldom permitted
to forget the instruments with which nature has distinguished them.
They may speak modestly of grace, labour, discipline, and divine
favour, but at heart they remember the evening when a
governor applauded, the rehearsal at which a rival turned pale,
or the exact note that made three ladies faint. Father
Olympus was not vain in an ordinary way. He did
not praise himself. He merely pitied those who could not
hear what he heard inside his own ribs.

In the morning, while still in his shirt, he stood
before the cracked mirror and tested the cathedral acoustics upon
the bedroom furniture.

"Doh—oh—oh…"

The wardrobe gave no answer.

"Re—eh—eh…"

A blue glass on the washstand trembled agreeably.

Then his wife, Pelageya Andreevna, would appear behind him, small,
thin, yellowish, with shoulders sharp as folded scissors.

"Are you beginning again, you buffalo? The neighbours are asleep."

"I was only seeing whether the throat—"

"I know what you were seeing. Yesterday you drank cold
kvass. The day before yesterday you stood in a draught.
Tonight you snored like a sawmill. One day you'll lose
your voice, and then what shall we eat? Your beard?"

Father Olympus, who could fill the cathedral with terror at
forty paces, lowered his eyes before this little woman.

"Yes, Pasha."

"Gargle."

"Yes, Pasha."

"And no vodka."

He sighed.

"Not even one little glass for warmth?"

"For warmth! Your liver is warm enough to roast a
goose."

Thus were the domestic lives of celebrated men regulated in
those days.

For several weeks, however, Father Olympus had been less occupied
with his throat than usual. In the evenings he had
been reading a novel by Count Leo Tolstoy. A schoolmaster
from the district gymnasium, a nervous, snuffling man whose spectacles
were always greasy, had lent him the book after a
christening.

"You should read it, Father," the schoolmaster had said. "Not
for doctrine, naturally. Purely as literature."

Father Olympus had frowned at the word "literature." He distrusted
words that seemed to require spectacles. Yet the volume was
thick, handsomely bound, and had the sweet dusty smell of
old paper. He took it home.

At first he read with suspicion, moving his lips and
occasionally glancing at the title page, as if the count
might creep out from behind a paragraph and deny the
Holy Trinity. Then something unexpected happened. He forgot to be
cautious.

There were no celestial ladders, no brass-tongued prophets, no disputes
concerning the procession of the Holy Ghost. There were rooms,
roads, dogs, frightened children, peasants coming home at dusk, a
woman bending over a cradle, a condemned man looking through
a barred window at the morning sky. A tired horse
stood in the rain and shook water from its mane.
Two moths flew into a candle flame, and someone gently
caught them in a handkerchief and released them into the
darkness.

That passage disturbed Father Olympus most.

He read it twice, then closed the book and stared
at the lamp.

The moths, foolish little things, had struggled toward the flame
because they mistook destruction for light. The hand that saved
them received no reward. No one even saw it. Yet
the saving of those two specks of life seemed to
Father Olympus more mysterious than certain miracles which, though attested
by respectable authorities, had never touched him.

"Pasha," he called.

"What now?"

"If a moth flies into the lamp, don't kill it."

Pelageya Andreevna appeared in the doorway.

"Have you gone soft in the head?"

"No. Only it wants to live."

"It wants to burn, that is what it wants. And
you want to sit till two in the morning reading
forbidden rubbish and ruin your eyes. A fine deacon you'll
be when you're blind."

She snatched the lamp chimney, pinched a moth between finger
and thumb, and threw it into the stove.

Father Olympus groaned as if she had pinched him.

After that he read secretly.

The following Sunday was the first Sunday of the Great
Fast, when the cathedral commemorated the triumph of Orthodoxy and
condemned those who, through pride, perversity, curiosity, German philosophy, Latin
corruption, Armenian stubbornness, Jewish deceit, Mohammedan darkness, materialist arrogance, or
simple failure to understand the instructions of their ecclesiastical superiors,
had departed from correct belief.

The service was popular.

People who neglected matins, slept through sermons, and could not
have distinguished Arius from a railway inspector came eagerly to
hear the anathemas. There is a grandeur in public damnation
that attracts even the lukewarm. Moreover, Father Olympus sang.

By nine o'clock the cathedral was crowded. Fur collars steamed.
Boots creaked. Old women pressed their foreheads against pillars. Civil
servants with pale, fasting faces stood near the governor's pew,
arranging their expressions in accordance with rank. Wax ran down
the tall candles in yellow tears.

Behind the screen, the choir leader, Mr. Piskunov, struck a
tuning fork against his thumbnail.

"Doh," he whispered.

Father Olympus answered softly:

"Doh…"

The tuning fork seemed ashamed of itself.

"Not so low, Father. His Eminence asked for restraint."

"Restraint?"

"Yes. Last year you came in under the basses and
frightened Archpriest Vassily."

"He should not frighten so easily."

"He has a heart complaint."

"He has a stomach complaint."

"Please, Father. Begin on re."

Olympus looked at the choir leader with sleepy contempt. Piskunov
was a narrow, damp person who used cocaine for his
nose and believed all sacred music could be improved by
increasing the number of rehearsals. At every service he fluttered
his hands as though trying to dry ten handkerchiefs at
once.

"Re," said Olympus.

"Higher."

"Re!"

"Not louder. Higher."

"May the devil take your higher," the deacon thought, but
aloud he said meekly, "Very well."

Then the bells ceased.

The procession entered in gold and white. First came the
candle-bearers, solemn because their sleeves were too long; then subdeacons,
priests, archpriests, and finally the Archbishop, a tall old man
with a delicate nose and the expression of one perpetually
correcting a punctuation mark. His Eminence was learned. He knew
how many angels had appeared to Saint So-and-so in the
year 714 from the Creation, which council had condemned the
use of three fingers by persons of suspicious nationality, and
whether a certain fourth-century bishop had said "essence" when he
ought to have said "substance." He was therefore considered a
great shepherd of souls.

The chanting began.

At first Father Olympus performed magnificently and without thought. His
body knew the service better than his mind. He breathed,
expanded, and released sound. The notes rolled under the arches,
climbed the pillars, entered the dome, and descended upon the
congregation in invisible waves. Heads bowed. Children stared. The great
voice seemed too enormous to belong to one man; it
belonged to the stone, the smoke, the gold, and the
ancient darkness behind the icons.

The Creed was proclaimed.

Then came the affirmations, the memorials, the long declarations that
the Church preserved truth pure and inviolate, as received from
apostles, fathers, councils, martyrs, ascetics, wonder-workers, patriarchs, teachers, and pious
sovereigns.

"Eternal memory!" sang Father Olympus.

The choir answered.

"Eternal memory!"

Piskunov's hands trembled with happiness.

Then the deacon advanced to the ambo holding a lighted
candle. Silence gathered around him. Even the babies seemed to
understand that the agreeable part was beginning.

He unrolled the paper.

"To those who deny the existence of God and affirm
that this world is self-existing, and that all things in
it occur by chance and not by the providence of
God—"

He drew breath.

"Anathema!"

The word came out black and vast. It struck the
far wall and returned.

"Anathema!" replied the choir.

"To those who say that God is not spirit but
flesh; that He is not righteous, merciful, wise, all-knowing, and
beyond all change—"

"Anathema!"

"To those who dare to declare that the Son of
God is not consubstantial with the Father, or that the
Holy Ghost does not proceed according to the doctrine faithfully
received—"

"Anathema! Anathema!"

The names followed: Arius, Macedonius, Nestorius, Eutyches, Dioscorus; ancient men
who had been dust for fifteen centuries but whose errors
remained, in ecclesiastical memory, distressingly fresh. Father Olympus cursed them
sonorously. He cursed iconoclasts, schismatics, pretenders, rebels, false prophets, sorcerers,
blasphemers, rationalists, and those who explained miracles by natural causes.
He cursed with such professional force that the candles guttered.

Yet between two clauses he suddenly saw the tired horse
from the novel.

It stood beneath cold rain beside a broken fence. Water
streamed from its flanks. A man who had beaten it
all day now removed his own coat and laid it
over the animal's back.

Father Olympus blinked.

"To those who revile the sacred traditions and ordinances established
by the holy fathers—"

"Anathema!"

The women had begun to weep.

"To those who subject divine revelation to the proud judgment
of human reason—"

"Anathema!"

A condemned prisoner looked through bars at a strip of
dawn. In the prison yard, snow shone blue. He knew
he would die before noon, yet he smiled because a
sparrow hopped near the wall and was free.

Father Olympus's voice faltered.

Piskunov raised both hands sharply: stronger, stronger!

"You wave, you dried herring," thought Olympus. "You try saying
it."

He continued.

The ritual was old, and old rituals possess momentum. No
single man carries them; they carry the man. Phrase called
to phrase, curse to response, as one iron link draws
another through the wheel. Father Olympus read of fire unquenchable,
the worm that dieth not, outer darkness, grinding teeth, separation
from the faithful, expulsion from the communion of saints, judgment
without appeal, and punishment everlasting.

He had often read these things. Until that morning they
had meant little more to him than musical instructions. "Outer
darkness" required a grave tone; "fire unquenchable" permitted splendour; "grinding
of teeth" called for a pause. The words were steps
by which his voice ascended.

Now each word had a face.

The face of a child asleep beside its mother. The
face of a peasant laughing over black bread. The face
of a soldier who had spared an enemy. The patient
muzzle of the horse. The two moths fluttering inside the
handkerchief, waiting to be released.

An altar boy approached and handed a folded note to
the archpriest. The archpriest passed it to the Archbishop. His
Eminence read it, lifted one eyebrow, made a correction with
a pencil, and beckoned to the deacon.

Olympus bent down.

"The new formula," whispered the Archbishop. "It is to be
pronounced today."

"What formula, Your Eminence?"

"The condemnation of Count Tolstoy."

Father Olympus stared.

The Archbishop's nose sharpened.

"Read."

Olympus took the paper.

It was written in a clerk's precise hand. A word
had been inserted above the line; another had been crossed
out. Apparently even damnation demanded good editing.

"To the former servant of God, Count Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy,"
he read silently, "who, seduced by pride of mind, has
audaciously risen against the Lord and His Christ, rejected the
mysteries, mocked the Holy Church, corrupted the simple, and by
writings harmful and blasphemous separated himself from communion with the
faithful—"

Below stood the prescribed word.

ANATHEMA.

Father Olympus felt cold beneath his vestments.

He looked toward the altar. Gold flashed. Incense rose. The
Archbishop waited. Piskunov held his hands poised. Thousands of faces
had turned toward the ambo.

"Read," repeated His Eminence.

The deacon swallowed.

A singer may face death more calmly than a doubtful
entrance. Death comes once and does not require one to
land exactly upon F-sharp. Father Olympus had sung before governors,
princes, famous tenors, and once before an imperial cousin. Never
had his mouth been so dry.

"To the former servant of God…"

His voice was barely audible.

Piskunov leaned forward in horror.

Father Olympus began again.

"To the former servant of God, Count Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy,
who, seduced by pride of mind…"

The handkerchief opened.

Two moths rose into the night.

"…has audaciously risen…"

A woman bent over a cradle. Outside, the wind troubled
the shutters, but within the room there was warmth, milk,
sleep, and the small perfect fist of a child resting
against its mother's breast.

"…rejected the mysteries…"

The condemned prisoner smiled at the sparrow.

"…mocked the Holy Church…"

The beaten horse stood beneath the man's coat.

Father Olympus stopped.

The silence was immediate and enormous.

Piskunov gave the note with the tuning fork. Doh—oh—oh…

It sounded ridiculously tiny.

"Continue," said the Archbishop.

Olympus examined the candle in his left hand. According to
the ritual, at the word of condemnation he must lower
it, turn the flame toward the floor, and extinguish it.
He imagined that small flame reversed above Tolstoy's name. He
imagined a hand—not God's hand, not Christ's, but his own
thick, hairy hand—pushing a living thing toward darkness.

"Why?" he thought.

It was not a learned question. He knew nothing of
exegesis, criticism, ecclesiastical politics, or the precise errors of the
count. He could not have disputed with a seminarian. But
he knew that when he had read those pages, something
hard in him had softened; he had pitied a horse,
a prisoner, a woman, two moths. If a book made
a man less cruel, how could cruelty be the answer
to it?

The Archbishop rose.

"Deacon!"

Father Olympus turned his great head.

"Your Eminence," he said, and his voice, though quiet, travelled
to the last door, "is Christ malice?"

A rustle passed through the cathedral.

"What are you saying?"

"I ask: is Christ malice?"

"Complete the appointed formula."

"But he taught mercy."

"The Church has judged."

"Then let the Church judge me also."

Somebody gasped. An old woman crossed herself rapidly. The governor
looked at his aide, and the aide looked at a
colonel, since in official life responsibility travels sideways before it
travels upward.

Piskunov crept nearer. His plan, formed in panic and not
entirely practical, was to strike Father Olympus behind the knee
with the tuning fork. Perhaps the deacon would cough, the
choir could enter, the disturbance would be covered, and ecclesiastical
order restored in B-flat major.

Olympus saw him.

"Stand back."

Piskunov stood back.

The deacon looked again at the paper. Then he tore
it once, slowly, down the middle.

The sound was very small.

It was heard everywhere.

"No," said Father Olympus.

He lifted the candle high.

His chest expanded. The arteries swelled in his neck. For
one instant he appeared not comic, not vain, not frightened
of draughts and governors and Pelageya Andreevna, but ancient and
formidable, like a figure hewn for the façade of some
cathedral no emperor had yet built.

Then he sang.

"To the servant of God, Leo—"

Piskunov squeaked.

"—Mnogaya leta!"

Many years.

The phrase burst from him like light through a broken
door. It climbed beyond the choir stalls, beyond the painted
saints and blackened rafters. The chandeliers rang. Dust fell from
a cornice. The whole cathedral seemed to inhale.

"Many years!" he sang again.

The choir did not answer.

He gave them the note.

It was a command now, not a request.

One young tenor, white with terror, responded instinctively. A bass
joined him. Then two boys, then half the choir, dragged
by habit, music, or courage—they could not afterward say which.

"Mnogaya leta! Many years!"

The Archbishop struck the arm of his chair.

"Stop!"

But an archbishop's speaking voice is a modest instrument, and
Father Olympus had already become a silver trumpet at the
lips of an archangel.

"Many years to him who awakened pity! Many years to
him who spoke for the beaten and the condemned! Many
years!"

The congregation broke apart inwardly before it moved outwardly. Some
fled. Some knelt. Some laughed from nervousness. Women wept now
without knowing whether they wept for Tolstoy, for Olympus, for
themselves, or simply because the voice had entered their bodies
and shaken what was hidden there.

The deacon descended from the ambo.

Two archpriests attempted to stop him. He moved between them
without violence, and they spun away from his shoulders like
doors pushed by a heavy trunk. In the vestry he
removed his stole and outer vestment, folded them carefully, and
laid the torn formula on top.

"You cannot leave," said Archpriest Vassily, clutching his stomach.

"I have left."

"You will be suspended."

"Yes."

"Defrocked!"

"Yes."

"Arrested, perhaps."

Father Olympus put on his coat.

"Yes."

Outside, the March air struck his damp forehead. Snow was
melting along the gutters. The cathedral bells had not begun;
no one had ordered them, and bells, unlike consciences, rarely
act without instruction.

Pelageya Andreevna awaited him at home.

News, especially scandalous news involving persons of size, travels faster
than carriages. She stood in the middle of the room
with her bonnet still tied, shaking from head to foot.

"You beast," she said.

Father Olympus closed the door.

"You ruined us! You mad ox! You blaspheming trumpet! Twenty-seven
years I guarded that throat. I boiled milk. I bought
lozenges. I stopped you drinking cold beer. I endured your
rehearsals, your basses, your stinking gargles, your friends who eat
herring at midnight. And now—now—you throw away your salary, our
rooms, our firewood, the bishop's allowance, everything, for a count
who does not know you exist!"

"He need not know."

"What shall we live on?"

"I can work."

"Work! What can you do?"

He considered.

"I am strong."

"You are fat."

"I can carry sacks."

"You'll rupture yourself."

"I can load timber."

"You'll crush somebody."

"I can sweep streets."

"With that beard? Dogs will nest in it!"

He removed his hat and placed it on the table.

"I will do what is given."

She stared at him. Usually at this point he would
have sighed, begged, promised caution, or offered some foolish compromise
involving vodka. But the man before her did none of
these things.

"You have abandoned God," she said.

"No."

"The Church."

"No."

"The Creed?"

"I believe it."

"The Apostles?"

"I believe them."

"Then what have you abandoned?"

Father Olympus looked at his wife with such steadiness that
her next insult remained unfinished.

"Malice," he said.

She opened her mouth.

He drew himself up.

"Well?!"

The single word filled the room more completely than any
chant. The cups shivered on the shelf. Pelageya Andreevna sat
down.

For the first time in their marriage, she was silent.

Father Olympus put on his hat again. He looked once
around the room—the mirror before which he had practised, the
blue glass that trembled at his doh, the stove where
the moth had been thrown—and then he went out.

He walked slowly through the thawing streets, above the crowd,
immense and solemn. People stopped to look at him, as
they had always looked: at the mane, the shoulders, the
extraordinary chest that seemed to contain an organ pipe.

But now he did not notice them.

He had lost his place, his salary, his vestments, his
public glory, and perhaps by evening he would lose his
freedom. Yet his step was calm. The magnificent voice within
him had ceased to be an ornament. For the first
time it belonged to him.

And as he walked beneath the pale Lenten sky, Father
Olympus seemed no longer a cathedral celebrity, nor a frightened
husband, nor even a deacon, but a man carved out
of living stone, carrying in one uplifted hand an invisible
candle whose flame no command could make him turn toward
the earth.

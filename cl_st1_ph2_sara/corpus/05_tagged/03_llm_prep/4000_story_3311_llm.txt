## Maine's March Weather

"It is snowing!" cried Rhode Island, pressing her small nose
against the school-room window until Massachusetts requested her not to
flatten it.

"I am not flattening the window," said Rhode Island.

"No; but you are flattening your nose, which is a
personal possession and should be treated with respect."

"What nonsense!" exclaimed New York.

"Slang," said Massachusetts, without looking up from her algebra. "One
cent for the missionary fund."

New York tossed her head and dropped a penny into
the little blue box on Miss Wayland's desk. New York
had handsome dark eyes and was fond of tossing her
head, particularly when the light fell favorably upon her hair.

The snow, meanwhile, came faster. It was a March snow,
hurled out of a gray sky by a wind which
appeared to have spent the winter gathering strength for this
particular afternoon. It swept across the playground, powdered the bare
elms, and rattled the windows as if a regiment of
icy fingers were drumming for admission.

Virginia shivered gracefully.

"How can you all stand it?" she asked. "The very
sight makes me feel frozen to the marrow. At home
the violets would be considering whether to bloom."

"At home," said Maine, "we should call this a flurry."

Every girl turned toward her.

Maine stood by the stove, not because she needed its
warmth, but because she had been oiling the straps of
her snow-shoes and Miss Wayland would not allow oil upon
the desks. She was tall for fifteen, straight and springy,
with gray eyes, wind-colored cheeks, and a long braid which
never remained smooth for more than ten minutes. Her blue
woollen dress was shorter than Virginia considered elegant, and her
thick boots were built for service rather than admiration.

"A flurry!" repeated Virginia. "My dear Maine, the world has
disappeared."

"Only the unnecessary parts of it," replied Maine cheerfully. "The
road is there, and the wall, and Mr. Withers's barn.
You simply cannot see them."

"That is remarkably comforting," said Massachusetts.

Massachusetts was broad-browed, composed, and so reliable that Miss Wayland
sometimes found herself asking her opinion before remembering that she
was only sixteen. She kept the missionary accounts, ruled the
literary society, and had lately imposed a fine of one
cent for every expression which she classified as slang, vulgar
colloquialism, or needless personality.

Maine lifted one of the great oval snow-shoes.

"If it keeps on, I shall have splendid walking to-morrow."

"You would go out in that?" demanded New York.

"Of course."

"Simply to show that you can!"

"Personal remark," said Massachusetts. "One cent."

This time the penny entered the box with considerable violence.

Miss Wayland came hurrying in from the hall, carrying a
lamp though it was not yet four o'clock. She was
a gentle, conscientious woman, whose imagination multiplied every danger entrusted
to her care. Twenty-four girls, twenty-four sets of parents, and
twenty-four opportunities for measles, broken bones, homesickness, or misplaced overshoes
weighed upon her heart from September until June.

"Girls," she said, "the wind is rising. I have sent
Martha to fasten the shutters upstairs. No one is to
go outside—not even upon the steps. Maine, my dear, put
those dreadful contrivances away."

"They are not dreadful, Miss Wayland. They are beauties."

"One cent," murmured Massachusetts.

"For what?"

"Slang."

Maine laughed and paid.

By five o'clock it was plain that even Maine's "flurry"
had become a storm. The last day scholars had been
detained, for no responsible person could send a child down
the village road through that white uproar. Supper was eaten
by lamplight, while the wind rushed around the school like
a wild creature searching for a weak door. Sometimes it
roared in the chimney; sometimes it screamed under the eaves;
sometimes it flung a solid burden of snow against the
windows, making the younger girls start and catch one another's
hands.

Virginia declined baked apples on the ground that she was
too anxious to digest.

"You have eaten two biscuits," observed Massachusetts.

"Biscuits are sustaining. Apples require peace of mind."

Maine ate everything that came within reach and listened to
the gale with shining eyes.

"I wish you would not look so pleased," New York
said. "It seems almost heartless."

"I am not pleased that you are frightened. I am
pleased with the storm."

"It sounds as if a thousand wolves were outdoors," whispered
Delaware.

"Wolves do not commonly travel in thousands," said Massachusetts. "Nor
do they play the chimney like an organ."

At that instant came a lull. The great voice sank;
the windows ceased their clatter; and through the sudden stillness
another sound crept—thin, wavering, and pitiful.

Every spoon stopped.

"What was that?" asked Miss Wayland.

No one answered. They listened.

The wind sprang up again, sweeping around the corner with
a shriek. Beneath it—or within it—came the sound once more.

"Somebody is crying," Maine said.

"It may be a shutter," suggested Virginia quickly.

"Shutters do not call 'Mother.'"

Miss Wayland turned pale. "Surely no child can be outside!"

Maine was already at the window. She cupped her hands
about her eyes, but the glass showed only streaming whiteness.

Again the cry came, farther away or more nearly smothered.

"Oh, do something!" cried little Rhode Island.

Miss Wayland went to the door, opened it an inch,
and was driven backward by a gust that scattered snow
across the hall carpet. The lamp flame leaped blue and
nearly vanished.

"We cannot go out," she said. "It would be madness.
Martha, ring the hand-bell from the upper window. Perhaps whoever
it is will hear."

Martha rang until her arm ached. The cheerful dinner-bell gave
out a small, foolish clang against the vast noise of
the storm.

Maine had disappeared.

"Maine!" called Miss Wayland.

She returned wearing a scarlet cap, a short fur jacket,
and mittens. A coil of stout garden twine hung over
one arm.

"My snow-shoes are in the cloak-room," she said. "Tie one
end of this to the inside knob. I will follow
the wall toward the sound."

"No!" Miss Wayland caught her sleeve. "I cannot permit it."

"If it is a child, he will not last long."

"But you may be lost."

"Not with the line."

"The line may break."

"Then I shall come back without it."

Miss Wayland's eyes filled. "You speak as if it were
crossing a room."

Maine's face softened. "I know snow, Miss Wayland. Truly I
do. Father took me out in worse weather last winter
when the sheep drifted under. I can keep my head,
and I can carry Benny Withers if it is Benny.
He is not heavy."

"Why should it be Benny?" asked Massachusetts.

"He goes to his grandmother's on Thursdays. Mr. Withers's house
lies beyond the lower wall, and that cry came from
the wall."

This practical reasoning had more effect than any heroics could
have done. Miss Wayland still hesitated, but the faint cry
sounded yet again.

"Go," she whispered. "And Heaven guard you!"

The girls crowded into the hall. Massachusetts tied the twine
twice around the heavy door-knob and tested the knot. Maine
fastened the other end about her waist, stepped into her
snow-shoes, and pulled the cap low over her ears.

"Keep the line as free as you can," she directed.
"Do not jerk unless I jerk three times. Then pull."

"You must not be gone ten minutes," said Miss Wayland.

"I may need fifteen."

"Twelve."

Maine smiled. "We will not quarrel over minutes."

The door opened, and winter sprang bodily into the house.
It seized skirts, extinguished one lamp, and sent Virginia flying
backward with a cry. Maine bent her head and passed
out. For one second her scarlet cap showed amid the
swirling snow; then the door closed, and she was gone.

Outside, there was no earth and no sky. There was
only snow—snow above, below, before, behind; snow rushing sideways as
if the whole frozen world had broken loose and taken
flight.

The first blast caught Maine broadside and shook her like
a sapling. She crouched, set the teeth of her snow-shoes
firmly, and waited. When it had passed she went forward,
paying out the twine behind her.

The school vanished after six steps.

Maine could see scarcely farther than her mitten. The snow
struck her cheeks like sand and forced itself beneath her
scarf. She kept one hand upon the line and stretched
the other ahead. Somewhere to the left should be the
old stone wall; but distances had become deceitful, and the
gale pushed her continually downhill.

She stopped and listened.

Nothing but the monstrous shouting of the wind.

"Benny!" she called.

The storm snatched the name from her lips.

She moved on. Once a snow-shoe caught beneath a buried
branch, and she fell upon her knees. The twine tightened
at her waist. Back in the school, she knew, twenty-four
girls were staring at that thin cord as if it
were alive. She rose quickly lest they should feel the
pull and be frightened.

"Benny!"

This time an answer came—a weak little wail to her
right.

Maine turned toward it. The wind met her full in
the face. It clutched at her jacket, pressed icy hands
over her mouth, and drove her backward step by step.
She lowered her head and pushed on.

Ten yards seemed a mile. She struck the wall so
suddenly that her knee bruised against a stone. Kneeling, she
searched along its drifted base. Her mittens swept loose snow;
found rock; found a broken basket; found something soft.

A small blue sleeve appeared.

"Benny!"

She dug fiercely. The drift had curled over him as
a wave curls above a sinking boat. First came one
shoulder, then a woollen cap, then a white and terrified
face.

"Maine?" he whimpered.

"Yes, it is Maine. You are all right now."

"I ain't. I'm froze."

"Not yet. Put your arms around my neck."

"My legs won't go."

"They need not. Mine will."

She freed him to the knees and pulled him out.
He was six years old, but six-year-old boys in wet
clothes are astonishingly heavy. Maine turned her back, dragged his
arms across her shoulders, and rose with a mighty effort.

At that moment she blessed her little brother Jamie, who
had demanded pig-a-back rides over every hill and wood-road at
home. How often she had called him a lazy porpoise!
She would never do so again—unless he richly deserved it.

"School," Benny murmured against her cap.

"No. Home is nearer."

He began to cry. "Ma won't know where I be."

"She will in five minutes."

Maine tried to follow the wall. The Withers cottage stood
beyond its lower end, not two hundred yards away. But
the twine pulled her back toward the school. She unwound
it from her waist.

For a moment she hesitated. The line meant safety; Benny
meant speed.

She tied it to a projecting stone.

Inside the school, the girls watched the cord.

"It has stopped," whispered Delaware.

"She has found something," said Massachusetts.

"How can you know?" Virginia asked.

"I cannot. But believing it is more useful than imagining
disasters."

The line moved again, then sagged.

Massachusetts took it gently in both hands. There was no
answering pressure.

Miss Wayland's face went white. "Pull it."

Massachusetts pulled. The twine came easily—too easily—slithering across the threshold
in yard after yard of wet coils. At the end
was a ragged break where the cord had rubbed against
stone.

Virginia covered her face.

"She is lost!"

"No," said Massachusetts, though her lips were colorless. "She has
merely lost the string."

"That is precisely what I meant!"

"One cent for needless contradiction," Massachusetts replied automatically.

No one smiled.

Maine, meanwhile, had reached the wall's end. With Benny sagging
upon her shoulders, she turned toward the spot where the
cottage ought to be. There was no light. Either the
windows were drifted over or she had mistaken the direction.

She took twenty steps and struck a fence.

The Withers fence! She followed it hand over hand, while
the rails rose and vanished beneath the drifts. Her breath
came painfully now. Benny no longer spoke. Every few moments
she twisted her head to feel his breath against her
cheek.

"Stay awake," she ordered.

"I be awake."

"Tell me what your mother made for dinner."

"Doughnuts."

"How many did you eat?"

"Three."

"Shameful greed!"

"I saved one."

"For whom?"

"For me—to-morrow."

"That is worse still."

A faint chuckle stirred beside her ear. Good! A boy
who could laugh was not yet beaten.

Her outstretched hand struck wood. She felt along it, found
a latch, and pushed. The gate was blocked by snow,
so she climbed over, an awkward business with snow-shoes and
a boy upon her back. Halfway across she toppled, and
they rolled together into the yard.

Benny squealed.

"Excellent!" said Maine, spitting snow. "You have discovered your voice."

The cottage door flew open. A woman holding a lantern
peered into the storm.

"Who's there?"

"Mrs. Withers! I have Benny."

The lantern dropped into the snow, but its flame miraculously
remained burning. Mrs. Withers plunged forward, caught her child, and
cried over him all at once.

"My boy! My poor lamb! I've been nigh crazy! His
pa went to look along the upper road, and I
can't get nowhere through these drifts. Benny, speak to me!"

"Doughnuts," said Benny sleepily.

"He is cold," Maine said. "Get off his wet things,
wrap him in blankets, and rub him—gently, not hard. Is
the kitchen warm?"

"Warm as toast. Come ye in, dearie. Come this minute
and rest ye."

"I cannot. They will be worrying at school."

"You can't go back alone!"

"I can if you will lend me a lantern."

"The wind'll blow it dead."

"Then I shall carry it while it lasts."

Mrs. Withers clutched her arm. "You brought my boy through
that wicked storm. I won't have ye lost for him."

Maine looked at Benny, already bundled in his mother's shawl.
She longed suddenly and terribly for the bright kitchen, the
stove, and perhaps the saved doughnut. Her arms trembled; her
knee throbbed; melted snow trickled down her neck.

But in her mind she saw Miss Wayland's anxious eyes
and Massachusetts holding the broken cord.

"I must go," she said.

Mrs. Withers thrust a lantern upon her, tied an extra
scarf over her cap, and pointed toward the fence. "Keep
the wind at your left shoulder. The school bell-tower's straight
beyond the orchard."

Maine climbed the fence again. The lantern lasted thirty paces.
Then Boreas, having tolerated it long enough, thrust one furious
breath through the tin vents and put it out.

She went on in darkness.

The wind had shifted—or she had. There was no way
to tell. The drifts rose like hills, and twice she
slid into hollows from which she escaped only by crawling.
Her snow-shoes, so splendid upon an open road, became clumsy
among the orchard trees. A branch struck her forehead. She
sat down abruptly.

For one dangerous minute it seemed quite sensible to stay
there.

Then she remembered Benny's blue sleeve under the snow.

"No, you don't," she told the storm, and got up.

She walked until she could no longer feel her feet.
Once she shouted and thought she heard a bell, but
the wind might have been mocking her. She turned toward
the sound. A dark shape loomed ahead. Her heart leaped—then
sank. It was only an elm.

Yet the school playground had elms.

She staggered forward, struck a low iron rail, and knew
it for the border around Miss Wayland's frozen flower-bed. Ten
steps more brought her against the porch.

Inside, every girl was in the hall. Miss Wayland had
sent Martha for ropes and blankets, and Massachusetts was organizing
a search party composed of herself, New York, and the
reluctant Virginia. No one had been permitted to open the
door until the ropes were fastened.

A heavy thump sounded against it.

They stared.

The thump came again.

Massachusetts flung the door wide. A white, shapeless figure tumbled
across the threshold and lay still.

"Maine!" shrieked twenty-four voices.

They pulled her in, shut out the gale, and surrounded
her so completely that Miss Wayland had to order everyone
back before the heroine could breathe.

Maine raised her head. Her eyelashes were crusted with snow,
her scarf trailed behind her, and one snow-shoe was missing.

"Benny—is home," she gasped. "Warm kitchen. Mother. Doughnuts."

Miss Wayland knelt and gathered the snowy creature into her
arms.

"Oh, my dear, brave, blessed child!"

"Personal—remarks," murmured Maine. "Two, I think. Two cents—for the missionary
box."

Then she fainted in the most satisfactory manner, and even
Massachusetts forgot to record the fine.

By morning the storm had spent its rage. The sun
rose laughing over a transformed world. Roofs wore dazzling caps;
fences had become white ridges; the road was buried beneath
rolling billows; and the school stood in a sparkling solitude
beneath a sky as deep and blue as midsummer.

Maine slept until eight, ate breakfast at nine, and at
half past nine announced that she was perfectly well.

"You are to remain upon the sofa," Miss Wayland commanded.

"We have only half a loaf of bread," said Maine.

"We shall eat crackers."

"There are eleven crackers."

"We shall divide them."

"Among twenty-six persons? Massachusetts will have to use algebra."

Massachusetts looked into the pantry. "The situation is serious."

Maine's second snow-shoe had been recovered near the porch, and
by ten she had fastened both to her feet. Miss
Wayland protested, Virginia predicted pneumonia, and New York declared that
public opinion would condemn the expedition.

"Public opinion may have my share of the crackers," said
Maine.

She harnessed herself to the hand-sled and skimmed away across
the drifts toward the village. The morning was so beautiful
that yesterday's terror seemed almost a dream. Snow curled over
walls in shining crests; bare twigs glittered with diamonds; and
chimney smoke rose straight into the quiet air.

At the grocer's, Maine found Mr. Pell standing helplessly behind
a barricade of flour barrels.

"Miss Wayland's young ladies will starve," she announced, "unless you
fill this sled."

"Road ain't broke."

"I did not bring a road. I brought snow-shoes."

Mr. Pell supplied bread, bacon, oatmeal, milk, potatoes, and a
bag of apples. When Maine returned, hauling the laden sled
like a cheerful young reindeer, the whole school received her
from the windows with such cheers that she bowed and
nearly upset the milk.

At noon another visitor arrived. Doctor Hale came plunging through
the gate, sinking to his waist every third step. He
had been summoned to the Carter farm, two miles beyond
the village, where a child lay dangerously ill.

"My horse cannot pass the drifts," he told Miss Wayland.
"Pell said Maine had snow-shoes. I came to borrow them."

Maine brought them at once.

"You may have them on one condition," she said.

The doctor frowned impatiently. "Anything reasonable."

"Ten cents for the missionary fund."

"Maine!" exclaimed New York. "You might ask ten dollars. A
physician's visit is worth that."

"Personal reference to the doctor's purse," said Massachusetts. "One cent."

Doctor Hale laughed for the first time that day. "I
accept both assessments. Help me put on the machines."

Maine showed him how to fasten the straps and warned
him not to cross the tails. He stumbled twice in
the playground, but on the third attempt found his balance
and went striding over the white fields with his medicine
case strapped to his back.

He reached the Carter farm in time.

Two days later, when the roads had been opened and
ordinary life had begun again, Doctor Hale returned the snow-shoes.
The entire school was assembled for morning exercises.

"The Carter child will recover," he said. "Without your loan,
Maine, I should have arrived too late."

Maine looked uncomfortable. Praise always embarrassed her more than wind
or snow.

"You paid the ten cents," she said.

"I have reconsidered the price." He laid an envelope upon
Miss Wayland's desk. "New York possessed the better commercial judgment."

Inside were ten crisp one-dollar notes.

Massachusetts rose with shining eyes and placed the gift in
the blue missionary box, which had never before contained so
magnificent a sum.

"Doctor Hale," she said solemnly, "you have overpaid."

"Then credit the balance to courage, common sense, and the
proper use of snow-shoes."

The girls applauded. Virginia waved her handkerchief; Rhode Island climbed
upon a bench; New York declared it perfectly splendid and
paid one cent for the phrase before anyone could ask
her.

As for Maine, she stood by the window, looking out
upon the glittering drifts with a smile.

"After all," she said, "it was a very good flurry."

Massachusetts lifted her pencil.

"Slang?"

"No," she decided. "Regional accuracy."

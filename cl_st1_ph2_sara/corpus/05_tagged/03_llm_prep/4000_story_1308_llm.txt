# THE LAW WITHIN

## I

The raft floated down the Volga.

All night the river had lain under a ragged gray
covering of clouds, and now, toward morning, the clouds had
descended so low that they seemed to rest upon the
black crests of the wooded banks and to drag their
wet bellies across the water. The river was scarcely visible.
It appeared and vanished between strips of fog—here dull as
lead, there polished like old steel, and farther away swallowed
altogether by the humid darkness. The piled timber of the
raft groaned, scraped, and trembled; the water, divided by its
broad snout, went away on either side with a wistful
wailing swish, as though complaining that this rude, clumsy thing
had broken the hidden meditative silence of the night.

At the stern stood three men.

Silan, the owner of the timber and master of the
raft, held the steering sweep beneath his arm. He was
a broad, tall peasant of five-and-fifty, bluff, healthy, red-bearded, with
a chest like the front of a blacksmith's shop and
hands so large that the wooden handle looked slender in
them. His shirt was open at the throat; wet red
hair covered the powerful neck and breast, and whenever he
shouted an order the sound rolled over the water with
the abundance of a strength which must spend itself in
noise, labour, anger, or love.

Beside him worked Sergei, the hired man—a long-legged fellow of
thirty, lean in the hips, broad in the shoulders, with
a pointed beard and quick, mocking eyes. His bare feet
gripped the damp logs as if they had claws. He
was never silent for long. Silence oppressed him. In it
he became conscious of the fog, the fathomless water, the
dark banks, his own poverty, and the unpleasant fact that
there were no people near to laugh at his jokes.
Therefore he talked, whistled, cursed the river, hailed invisible fishermen,
and teased whoever stood nearest.

The third was Mitia, Silan's son.

He was fair, weak, melancholy-looking, and scarcely seemed to belong
either to the strong raft or to the mighty river
beneath it. Though nearly twenty-seven, he had the narrow shoulders
and smooth, tired face of a sickly lad. A pale
beard, fine as dried grass, grew upon his cheeks; his
blue eyes were large and uncertain; his thin fingers, wound
about the sweep, reddened under the strain. From time to
time he coughed, bending his head, and after each cough
he looked ashamed, as though even his illness were an
offence to the healthy men beside him.

"Pull, you sleepy saints!" Silan roared. "Pull to port! Do
you want the bank in our teeth?"

The three leaned against the steering sweep. The massive blade
groaned in the current. Loose ropes creaked; water gurgled musically
between the timbers.

"Harder!" shouted Silan. "Mitia! Have you fallen asleep standing?"

"I am pulling," answered Mitia.

"Pull with your bones, then, if you've no flesh!"

Sergei laughed.

"That's right, master! Warm him up. A fellow such as
he needs fire under his heels. He lives as if
he were asking pardon of the earth for walking on
it."

Mitia said nothing. They brought the raft round, avoiding a
low, mist-covered spit of sand, and the river once more
seized them and drew them onward. Silan planted the sweep,
glanced ahead, and then strode over the logs toward the
forward hut.

His heavy steps went thud-thud, thud-thud, diminishing in the fog.

Sergei looked after him and winked at Mitia.

"There goes a man!" he said. "Eh? A real man,
your father. He need only stamp and the ground remembers
it. Whereas you—Lord save us!—you could fall out of heaven
and not bend a blade of grass."

Mitia kept his eyes upon the water.

"Look ahead," he said quietly.

"I am looking. There's fog ahead, fog behind, fog inside
your skull. Nothing else. Ah, Mitia, Mitia! How did the
Almighty contrive it? Such an oak of a father, and
from his acorn—this little mushroom!"

"Do your work, Sergei."

"I am doing it. My tongue does not hinder my
hands. A working man has two hands and one tongue,
and all three must earn their bread. Besides, if I
keep quiet, I begin to think. Thinking's a bad maggot—it
gnaws holes in a poor man. Better talk."

Mitia's thin shoulders rose and fell.

"Then talk of something else."

"Why? Is this not interesting? It is a wonder fit
for a fair booth. 'Come and see, honest Christians! The
husband steers at the stern while his wife lies warm
at the bow with his father!' Ha!"

Mitia turned his face toward Sergei. Even in the uncertain
grayness it was possible to see the blood rise under
his pale skin.

"Don't."

"One kopeck admission! Children half-price! The father's young, the son's
old; the husband fasts, the father feasts—"

"Enough."

Sergei grinned, pleased by the success of his words. Until
that instant he had only desired amusement. He did not
hate Mitia. Indeed, he bore him the obscure resentment which
a vigorous but dependent man often bears toward one weaker
than himself who possesses property, a name, and the right
to call the master father. Yet there was also curiosity
in his mockery, and even envy. The scandal had warmth
in it. It glowed before his imagination like a house
fire in winter, and he wished continually to come nearer,
to thrust in his hands, to see who would be
burned.

"Why enough? It's true."

"Truth can be sinful in the mouth."

"Oh-ho! Hear the preacher! Then a lie may be holy,
perhaps?"

"Yes. Sometimes silence is holier than truth."

"Silence! There's your king of trumps. Whenever life catches you
by the collar—silence! When your wife calls you—silence! When your
father takes her—silence! If a wolf bit off your leg,
you'd say, 'Brother wolf, let us not discuss it.'"

Mitia closed his eyes for a moment.

Far in the mist a steamer gave a deep hoarse
cry. It sounded not like a machine but like some
enormous blind beast wandering between the banks and calling for
its mate.

"Ah-oy!" Silan shouted from the front.

"Ah-oy!" answered Sergei with delight.

The steamer's paddle-wheels struck the water: thud-thud, thud-thud, nearer and
louder. A yellow light came swimming through the fog, grew
into two fiery eyes, and passed across the river. Then
the dark bulk of the vessel emerged, streaming moisture. Men's
voices rang from its deck; a bell clanged; sparks flew
from the funnel and died over the black water. For
a minute the river awakened into movement and sound. The
raft rocked on the waves; ropes snapped taut; the hut
creaked; water splashed over Mitia's feet.

Sergei waved his cap.

"Ahoy, you devils! Take us with you!"

Someone on the steamer whistled shrilly. Laughter answered from the
darkness, and then the vessel passed. Its red stern lamp
diminished, trembling like a drop of blood, and the fog
closed over it. The waves continued to run, lifting the
raft and letting it fall with long, sleepy sighs.

When silence returned, it seemed heavier than before.

Sergei spat into the water.

"There! People are travelling, eating, drinking, kissing the girls in
the cabins. And we creep on logs through soup thick
enough for a spoon. Talk, Mitia."

"I have nothing to say."

"You have a wife."

Mitia's hands tightened on the sweep.

"Do not speak of her."

"Why not? She's handsome. Dark eyes, red mouth, hips like
a mare—"

"Be quiet!"

The cry was sharp but weak. Sergei looked at him
and chuckled.

"Ah! So there is blood in you. I thought it
was holy water. If you cannot bear another man to
praise her, why did you send her from your bed?"

"I did not send her."

"No? She ran of herself?"

"I never called her."

"Same thing. Worse, perhaps. Tell me, Mitia—between ourselves, like Christian
men—what happened? Your father married you, the priest sang, the
people drank, she took off her boots and sat on
your bed. Then what? Did you read her a psalm?"

Mitia moved away, but the sweep held them together.

"You know nothing."

"Then teach me. I am ignorant. Was she not willing?"

"She was willing."

"And you?"

"I could not."

Sergei whistled.

"Could not! There's a word! A young wife, warm from
the wedding, and he could not. Were you ill?"

"No."

"Frightened?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Mitia looked ahead. The fog had formed a wall across
the river, smooth, dense, and gray-black. The raft seemed to
be travelling toward that wall and could never reach it.
Behind them, their wake disappeared almost at once; thus they
were cut off from what had been and barred from
what might be, imprisoned upon a few wet logs between
vapor and water.

"I did not love her," Mitia said.

Sergei waited; then laughed so loudly that the fog returned
a faint broken echo.

"Did not love her! Oh, I'm blest! Must a cock
love every grain he pecks? She was your wife."

"That is why."

"What is why?"

"Because she was my wife I could not deceive her.
If I had taken her without love, I should have
used her body and lied to her soul. The priest's
words could not make that clean."

Sergei scratched his beard.

"Wait. You were married before God."

"Before men."

"Before God too. There were icons."

"Icons do not answer for us."

"The priest blessed you."

"The priest is not my conscience."

"Well, then!" Sergei's voice grew triumphant, as if he had
caught a thief. "You set yourself above the priest, above
the Church, above your father—above God, perhaps?"

"No. Under God."

Mitia spoke softly, but something stubborn entered his voice. It
was the stubbornness of a thin root which, unable to
overthrow a rock, nevertheless grows crookedly round it and lives.

"There is a law written inside us," he continued. "Not
on paper, not in books, not in the mouth of
a priest who has eaten too much, but in the
soul. A man may obey every custom and yet be
filthy before God. He may break a custom and be
pure."

Sergei stared at him.

"What law says a man must leave his wife untouched?"

"The law that forbids a lie."

"A lie! You'd not have been lying. You'd have been
lying beside her."

He laughed at his own joke, slapped his thigh, and
nearly slipped on the wet timber.

Mitia bent his head.

"You make dirt of everything."

"Because everything comes out of dirt. We're born in blood,
eat from the earth, sweat in work, rot underground. What
else are we? Angels? Have you wings beneath your shirt?"

"We are souls."

"We are stomachs first. Ask a hungry man."

"No. A beast is stomach first."

"And a man without stomach is dead. Listen to me,
Mitia. God gave a man teeth and claws—"

"We have no claws."

"Then he gave us fists! Look at your father's. A
man must bite life before life bites him. He must
take a wife, give her children, earn money, fight when
struck, shout when wronged. He must stand. You don't stand.
You hang from the world like an empty sleeve."

Mitia's face contracted. He breathed heavily through his mouth.

Sergei, who felt himself becoming eloquent, leaned closer.

"And now what? You would not be a husband, so
another man became one. A woman is not an icon
to wait in a corner while you pray. She has
blood. She has arms that want holding, a breast that
wants a child. Marka was married, but she lived like
a widow. Your father—well, your father is no widow."

"Stop."

"He looked at her. She looked at him. Fire knows
dry straw. Who is to blame?"

"I am," Mitia whispered.

Sergei had expected denial, and this answer momentarily checked him.

"There! You know it."

"I am to blame for consenting to the marriage. I
told father I did not wish it. He shouted. He
said she was strong, good, from decent people; he said
a wife would cure me of thinking. I gave way.
That was my sin."

"And afterward?"

"I asked her forgiveness."

"What did she say?"

Mitia's lips moved before any sound came.

"She wept."

"And then?"

"She asked if she were ugly. I said no. She
asked if there were another woman. I said no. She
asked if I hated her. I said no. She said,
'Then why?' I tried to explain."

Sergei shook his head with grim amusement.

"You explained the soul to a bride in bed."

"I told her that my heart was not joined to
hers, and that the body must not go where the
heart cannot follow."

"Oho!"

"She did not understand."

"Naturally."

"She thought I was laughing at her. The next day
she would not look at me. After a week she
called me a dead man. Then father began to quarrel
with me. He said I had disgraced him. He struck
me."

"Didn't you strike back?"

"He is my father."

"There again! Always a fine reason for doing nothing."

The clouds shifted slowly. Through a rent in them the
moon showed itself, swollen and yellow, looking down upon the
river with a sick, unfriendly face. Its light spread in
a narrow road over the water, and in that road
the waves moved like scales upon an enormous serpent. For
a few moments Mitia's face became clear—his hollow cheeks, moist
eyes, and the bluish shadow beneath them. Sergei saw that
he looked older than his father.

"And Marka?" he asked, more quietly.

"She became hard. Shame made her hard. When people began
to whisper, she laughed so that they should think she
did not care. Father defended her. He scolded me before
her. They stood together, and I—"

"You what?"

"I went away."

"To where?"

"The shed. The fields. The church porch. Anywhere."

"And left them together?"

"I did not know then."

Sergei gave a contemptuous snort.

"You did not know because you would not see. A
blind man at least feels with a stick. You shut
your eyes and called it holiness."

Mitia flinched as if the words had struck him.

From the forward part of the raft came Silan's voice,
speaking indistinctly, then a woman's low laugh. The sound was
scarcely louder than the gurgling water, yet both men heard
it. Sergei's quick eyes turned toward Mitia.

Mitia stood rigid.

Marka laughed again.

It was a warm, full laugh, not merry so much
as contented, and amid the chill vapor it seemed improper
and splendid, like a red flower opening in a graveyard.

Sergei rubbed his nose.

"She does not sound unhappy now."

Mitia breathed out slowly.

"No."

"Your father has cured her."

Mitia's lower lip trembled.

"Sergei, in God's name—"

"What? I only say—"

"In God's name, leave me."

Sergei was uneasy for an instant, but he could not
abandon the jest. To withdraw would have seemed like defeat,
and he had the cruel instinct, common in strong animals
and heedless men, to press upon whatever recoils.

"Come, don't sour yourself! There is an easy cure. Give
her up openly. Let your father marry her."

"She is my wife."

"Now she is your wife! A moment ago the priest
and paper meant nothing."

"A bond can be sinful and still bind."

"That's twisted rope indeed. Then take her back."

"No."

"Beat your father."

"No."

"Go to the magistrate."

"No."

"Run away."

"Yes."

Sergei blinked.

"Where?"

"To the Caucasus."

"The Caucasus! What for?"

"There are brethren there."

"What brethren?"

"Christians."

"We are Christians here."

Mitia looked at him, and in his eyes there appeared
a strange, distant brightness.

"No. Here men cross themselves, then rend one another's flesh.
They bow to painted boards, then sell the poor for
profit. They call killing lawful if a uniform orders it,
marriage holy if a drunken priest blesses it, and greed
respectable if the thief has land. Those are not Christians."

Sergei glanced toward the front of the raft.

"Speak lower. Your father will hear."

"What if he does?"

"He'll pitch your Christianity into the river."

"He has beaten me before."

"And these brethren—what are they? Sectarians?"

"They follow Christ's law. They will not bear arms. They
will not swear oaths. They own all things in common.
They feed the hungry and call no man master."

"Then how do they live?"

"By work."

Sergei laughed again, but less heartily.

"Every fool says that before dinner. After dinner he finds
a master. And they'll take you?"

"If God leads me."

"Can you plough?"

"I can learn."

"Fell timber?"

"I can learn."

"Carry a sack?"

"I will carry what strength permits."

"Strength!" Sergei seized Mitia's wrist and held it up. "Look
at that! A goose's neck. The Caucasus has mountains, not
feather beds. You'll cough once climbing a hill and roll
down dead."

"Then I shall die among brethren."

"Why not die at home? It's cheaper."

"Because here there is no air."

Sergei looked around at the immense river, the moving fog,
the unseen spaces beyond the banks.

"No air! There's enough to drown in."

"Not for the soul."

Again Marka's voice sounded ahead, but the words were lost.
Silan answered, and his deep laugh rolled along the raft.
Mitia shuddered.

Sergei saw it.

"Jealous after all," he said.

Mitia shook his head.

"Don't lie. Your belly twists when she laughs with him."

"It is shame."

"Jealousy."

"Shame!"

"Call a dog a lamb and it still barks. You
wanted her only when another took her."

"No!"

Mitia pulled at his wrist, but Sergei held it. The
hired man was smiling, and in the yellow moonlight his
teeth showed strong and white beneath his pointed beard.

"Confess it. You dream of her now. At night you
hear them through the wall. You lie there under your
blanket, holy as a corpse, and listen—"

Mitia tore himself free.

"Be silent!"

His cry flew across the water. Silan's voice stopped at
the front. For several moments there was only the river.

Then Silan called:

"What are you barking about?"

"Nothing!" Sergei answered. "Mitia's preaching to the fog!"

"Watch the channel!"

"We're watching!"

Sergei turned, still grinning, but Mitia's expression silenced him. The
weak man seemed suddenly to have been drawn upward by
an inward flame. His pale eyes shone; his hands shook;
every tendon stood out in his neck.

"You think because I am weak you may tear me,"
he said. "You all think so. Father thinks so. Marka
thinks so. A wolf sees a lame creature and bites
because it limps. You laugh and bite. You are strong,
therefore everything is permitted you—is that your law?"

"Come now—"

"You speak of teeth, fists, blood! Beasts! All of you
beasts, rending one another's flesh and proud because your jaws
are red! One takes money, another takes a woman, another
takes a life. And afterward you cross yourselves and say:
'It is custom. It is nature. It is lawful.' Whose
law? The wolf's law!"

Sergei's smile vanished.

"You'd best not call the master a wolf."

"He is a wolf. My father is a wolf. He
brought Marka into our house for me when I begged
him not to. He fastened us together like two dogs
and beat me because we would not mate. Then he
desired her himself. Now he walks before me, eats before
me, lies with my wife, and shouts that I am
weak. Yes, I am weak! Does that make his sin
clean? Does strength turn filth into gold?"

"Keep your voice down."

"And you—you follow him because you admire his shoulders. You
laugh because his wickedness is lively and my suffering is
dull. If I struck you, you would understand. If I
bled, you would understand. But the soul bleeds without colour,
and so you put your finger into the wound for
sport."

Sergei stepped back.

Mitia's anger left him as quickly as it had come.
His body bent; he grasped the sweep with both hands,
not to guide it but to keep himself upright. A
dry cough shook him. Then another. He tried to speak,
coughed again, and suddenly began to sob.

There was nothing restrained or dignified in the sound. It
burst from him in broken, childish gasps. His narrow shoulders
jerked; his mouth opened helplessly; tears ran down his cheeks
and hung upon the pale hairs of his beard. He
hid his face against his sleeve.

Sergei stared in astonishment.

He had seen men cry when drunk, when flogged, when
a horse died, or when a mother's coffin was lowered;
such tears were comprehensible and even respectable. But this naked
collapse frightened him. It was as though, in tapping upon
a thin wall for amusement, he had broken through and
discovered behind it not a room but a black, bottomless
pit.

"Oh, come," he muttered. "That's enough. I was joking."

Mitia wept harder.

"Don't," said Sergei. "What are you doing? The master will
hear."

"Leave me," Mitia gasped.

"I didn't mean—well, I meant it, but not like that.
A man talks. My tongue's loose."

"Leave me!"

"I can't leave the sweep."

"Then be silent. For God's sake, be silent."

Sergei shifted from one foot to the other.

"You mustn't take words so deep. Words are wind."

"They are nails."

"A man cannot live if every fool's word enters his
heart."

"I know."

"Wipe your face. Come! Marka might come back."

At her name Mitia uttered a strangled sound.

"Don't say it."

Sergei lowered his voice.

"All right."

"Do not speak to me again tonight."

"All right."

"Because I cannot bear it."

Sergei looked uneasily at the water slipping past. The moon
had gone behind cloud once more, and the river was
black, soft, and apparently without surface or bottom. Small whirlpools
opened beside the raft, turned with oily deliberation, and disappeared.

Mitia too was looking at them.

"Do not torment me," he whispered. "Sometimes, when you laugh,
and he shouts, and she—when there is nowhere to go—I
think the water is quiet underneath."

Sergei's scalp prickled.

"What nonsense."

"It would close over me. No priest, no law, no
father."

"Stop that."

"I know it is sin."

"Then don't think it."

"Can a man command thoughts as he commands oxen?"

"You said the law was inside."

"The law is there. Strength is not."

Sergei gripped his shoulder.

"Listen! You're not to jump. Do you hear?"

Mitia raised his wet face.

"You hurt me."

Sergei released him at once.

"I only— You gave me a fright. A fellow says
such things in darkness, on a river—what am I to
think?"

"Think nothing. Be silent."

They stood together at the stern, and for a long
while neither spoke.

The raft floated onward.

The fog thinned in places. A dark bank appeared on
the right, high and wooded, with birches standing ghostly among
the pines. Somewhere beyond it a dog barked, faint and
solitary. An answering dog called farther away; then another, until
the sounds passed from village to village like watchmen handing
along a warning. The river murmured. The logs scraped. A
loose chain knocked softly against a ring—clink, clink, clink—with the
patience of a clock measuring time for men who could
neither hasten nor turn back.

Sergei watched Mitia from the corner of his eye.

The pale man had ceased sobbing, but tears still came
silently. He steered correctly, almost mechanically. That frightened Sergei more
than the weeping. He wished to say something kind, yet
kindness was a language his tongue had scarcely learned. Every
phrase that came to him sounded like a jest or
command.

At last he muttered:

"When we reach Samara, I'll buy you tea."

Mitia did not answer.

"Hot tea," Sergei added. "With sugar."

Still no answer.

Sergei sighed. He looked ahead toward the low shelter where
Silan and Marka were, and for the first time he
did not envy the master. The raft seemed no longer
a merry scandal but a trap made of timber, fog,
flesh, and sin, bound with ropes too thick for any
knife.

"Look ahead," Mitia said suddenly.

Sergei started.

A black snag thrust from the water. Together they heaved
on the sweep. The raft turned slowly, its flank missing
the snag by a few feet. The current caught the
timbers again and bore them into darkness.

## II

At the front of the raft, under an awning of
sailcloth stretched beside the hut, Silan and Marka sat close
together.

A lantern hung from a hook above them, but its
flame had burned low and red. It threw an uncertain
light upon coils of rope, an iron kettle, sacks of
flour, and Silan's heavy boots. Beyond the awning, fog streamed
past in gray veils. The wet air smelled of pine
resin, river mud, smoke, and the warm human bodies crowded
beneath the canvas.

Marka was twenty-three, dark, handsome, full-bodied. Her black hair had
come loose from beneath her kerchief and lay in a
thick braid over one shoulder. She wore a red blouse,
and although its colour had dulled in the lantern light,
it seemed to hold warmth of its own. Her eyes,
usually bold before other people, softened when she looked at
Silan. Yet about her mouth there remained something wounded and
defiant—the fixed trace left by months of humiliation, whispers, and
resisted tears.

Silan had one arm around her. His broad palm covered
her side almost from breast to waist.

"What did he shout?" she asked.

"Who?"

"Mitia."

"Sergei is needling him."

"You should stop it."

"Why? Let him learn to answer."

"He cannot."

"Then let him learn."

Marka looked toward the stern, but the piled timber and
fog concealed the men.

"He sounded strange."

"He always sounds strange. Like a door squeaking in an
empty house."

"You are cruel."

Silan's beard moved with his smile.

"And you love me."

She lowered her eyes.

"That is why I know."

He drew her nearer. Against his strength she became pliant;
her shoulder fitted beneath his chin, and when he bent
to kiss her, she received the kiss first gravely, almost
sadly, then with a warmth that made him utter a
low pleased sound.

"There," he whispered. "Is that cruelty?"

"Yes."

"Then I'll be cruel again."

She pushed at him, smiling now.

"Enough. They may come."

"Let them."

"Sergei has eyes like a thief."

"He has nothing to steal here."

Silan said this with such simple possession that Marka's smile
faded. She touched his beard and drew her fingers through
it.

"Am I yours?" she asked.

"Whose else?"

"His, before the law."

"Paper law."

"And before the Church."

"Church law."

"And before God?"

At that Silan's face grew serious. He was not irreligious.
On saints' days he attended service, gave candles, fed beggars
when they approached him respectfully, and feared thunder, bad dreams,
and death. But his religion, like his labour, belonged to
the earth. God for him was a great master who
loved courage, understood a man's necessities, punished weakness, and could
be appeased by repentance when the work of living was
done.

"Before God," he said slowly, "we are sinners."

Marka shivered.

"You say it calmly."

"Should I lie?"

"No."

"I know sin when I see it. You were married
to my son. I take you. That is sin."

"Then give me up."

His arm tightened until she caught her breath.

"Say that again."

She smiled faintly.

"I knew you would do that."

"Give you up! For what? So that Mitia may look
through you as through window glass? So that you may
freeze in his room while he warms his soul? No."

"Then you choose sin."

"I choose you."

Beyond the awning the water struck a projecting log: slap-slap,
slap-slap. Somewhere behind them Mitia coughed. Marka's face stiffened.

Silan felt it.

"You pity him?"

"I don't know."

"You pity yourself."

"Yes."

"That is more honest."

She rested her head upon his chest. Through his shirt
she could hear his heart, steady and strong. That sound
had first conquered her. After the silent misery of Mitia's
room, after his evasive eyes and gentle explanations, Silan's loud
breathing, laughter, anger, and powerful heartbeat had seemed to her
not merely masculine but merciful. He desired her; and for
a woman who had begun to believe herself ugly, accursed,
or somehow less than a woman, desire had been a
hand pulling her from deep water.

"You ought not to have married us," she said.

"I know."

"You forced him."

"I thought marriage would straighten him. A bent sapling must
be tied to a stake."

"I was the stake?"

"You were a fine girl. Strong. Sensible. Your father owed
me money; I knew your people; you worked well. What
more was needed?"

"Love."

Silan grunted.

"You talk like Mitia."

"No. He used that word to keep me away. I
use it because I came to you."

He considered this, then nodded.

"You are right."

"Did you never think of me for yourself?"

"At that time my old woman was alive."

"She was dying."

"She was alive."

"And if she had died before the wedding?"

"I would have married you."

Marka raised herself to see his face.

"Truly?"

"Why should I lie?"

"You never said."

"What use now?"

The question opened before them a glimpse of another life,
so near in imagination and so completely lost that both
were silent. Had Silan's wife died half a year earlier;
had he noticed Marka not as a useful bride for
his son but as a woman fitted to himself; had
Mitia resisted more firmly; had Marka refused—then the same passion
which now crawled furtively beneath canvas and darkness might have
stood crowned before the altar, blessed by priest and parents,
welcomed with bread, salt, songs, and drunken cries. Their children
might have run openly through Silan's yard. Neighbours who now
spat after Marka would have praised her strong arms and
obedience.

The difference between honour and disgrace had been no wider
than a few months, a mistaken arrangement, a dead woman's
last breaths.

Silan frowned.

"I was a fool."

"You are never a fool."

"I was then. I saw Mitia growing thinner, always with
books and prayers, afraid of girls, afraid of vodka, afraid
even of slaughtering a chicken. I thought: marry him to
Marka. She has life enough for two. She'll shake him
awake."

"I tried."

"I know."

"He looked at me as if I were tempting him
to murder."

"He is rotten with thoughts."

"He was kind."

"Kindness without strength is sour milk. It feeds no one."

"He never struck me."

"That is not much praise."

"He did not curse."

"Nor bless you as a husband."

Marka's mouth twisted.

"No."

Silan lifted her chin.

"Do not remember it."

"How can I not? The first night—"

"Do not."

"I had been frightened all day. The women laughed and
whispered. Your wife was already ill, but she helped unpin
my veil. She said, 'Bear him sons, Marka. This house
needs noise.' Then they left me. Mitia came in. He
sat on the chest and would not look at me."

Silan's jaw hardened.

"He asked whether I was tired. I said no. He
asked if I wanted water. I said no. Then he
began—oh, those words! Soul, truth, conscience! At first I thought
he was shy. I went to him. He stood up
as if a snake had touched him."

"He'll answer for that."

"He said I was beautiful."

"What use was that?"

"He said it was not my fault. That was worse.
Not my fault! As if I had a disease."

Silan stroked her hair, but his eyes were fixed upon
the darkness.

"I should have thrashed him until he had sense."

"You did."

"Not enough."

"No beating could make him love me."

"Love! Again love."

"Yes, love. You say it justifies us. Why should it
not have mattered to him?"

Silan's broad face became impatient. He had accepted the contradiction
in himself, but disliked hearing it named.

"A husband's duty is one thing. What is between us
is another."

"Why?"

"Because it is."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer life gives. One horse pulls,
another lies down in the harness. Do you ask why?
Beat the lazy one or cut it loose."

"Mitia is not a horse."

"No. A horse is useful."

She pulled away.

"Do not speak so."

Silan looked at her with narrowed eyes.

"You defend him often tonight."

"I defend myself. If he is nothing, then what am
I? I was married to nothing, rejected by nothing, ruined
because nothing would touch me."

"You are mine. That is what you are."

Again the possessive force of his voice soothed and frightened
her. She leaned back into his arms.

From the stern came Mitia's cry—"Be silent!"—thin but piercing. Marka
started. Silan raised his head, and the muscles in his
arm swelled under her hand.

"What are they doing?"

"Sergei is a fool."

"Go and see."

"No. If Mitia cannot manage Sergei's tongue, he can swallow
it."

They listened. The words did not carry clearly, only the
tones: Mitia's high, broken vehemence, Sergei's mutter, and then Silan's
own shout asking what was the matter. After Sergei replied,
quiet returned.

Marka crossed herself.

"He will do something."

"Who?"

"Mitia."

"He never does anything."

"He may throw himself into the river."

Silan was silent.

The possibility entered the shelter and sat with them. Neither
looked at the other. The lantern flame bent and straightened,
bent and straightened, making the shadows of the ropes crawl
upon the canvas like black serpents.

At length Marka whispered:

"If he died, what would happen?"

Silan's hand ceased moving upon her shoulder.

"What happens when any man dies? They wash him, bury
him, divide what he leaves."

"You know what I mean."

"Yes."

"Could we marry?"

"After a proper time."

"How long?"

"A year, perhaps."

"A year!"

"Would you have people say we killed him for the
wedding feast?"

"They say worse now."

"Let them talk. Talk does not break bones."

"It breaks other things."

Silan sighed. For all his contempt of opinion, he understood
its practical force. A man might despise the village and
still need labourers from it, buy fodder from it, borrow
horses, find godparents, witnesses, customers, and sons-in-law. Even strength lived
among people; only the dead were wholly independent.

"We could go away," he said.

"Where?"

"Downriver. Beyond Saratov, perhaps. Or east. Sell the house, sell
the remaining timber, buy land where no one knows us."

"Begin again?"

"Why not?"

"You are fifty-five."

"And stronger than Sergei."

She smiled and put her hand upon his chest.

"That is true."

"I'll get you sons yet."

Her smile deepened, but sadness remained behind it.

"And then?"

"We work. We raise them. We give to the poor.
Build a chapel, maybe. A man can atone."

"For this?"

"For any sin, if his heart does not lie."

Marka gazed toward the black river.

"If Mitia died by his own hand, could we atone?"

Silan's brows drew together.

"He must not do that."

"Why?"

"Suicides are buried outside consecrated ground."

"You care for that?"

"He is my son."

The words were blunt, almost angry. Marka lowered her head.

"Yes."

"I don't wish him damned."

"But you wish—"

She stopped.

Silan's eyes fixed upon her.

"What?"

"You wish he were gone."

He pushed her away enough to examine her face.

"Do you?"

She did not answer.

"Speak."

The domineering note in his voice compelled her as always.

"Yes."

The word was scarcely audible.

"Gone where?"

"Dead."

The lantern hissed. A drop of condensed moisture fell upon
the flame, and for a moment darkness filled the shelter.
Marka gave a little cry and clutched Silan. The flame
recovered, weak and blue at its base.

"You pray for that?" he asked.

She covered her face.

"Sometimes."

"Marka."

"I am wicked. I know."

"Do you pray?"

"At night. I say: 'Lord, forgive me. Take him gently.
He is ill, he suffers, he wants to leave this
world. Take him, and let us live.' Then I am
afraid and pray for his health. But afterward I hear
him cough, and I think—soon. Perhaps soon."

Silan's face had grown grave. Yet beneath his gravity something
responded to her confession—not horror alone, but hope. He saw,
against his will, the future she had named: Mitia lying
peaceful beneath a cross; a year passing; a wedding in
a distant village; Marka beside him without shame; children; fields;
broad daylight instead of canvas and fog. He hated himself
a little for seeing it so clearly, and because he
was accustomed to mastering whatever opposed him, he turned the
hatred upon his son.

"He is half dead already," he muttered. "Never had health.
His mother spoiled him, kept him by the stove, filled
him with saints' lives. At ten he spoke of monasteries.
At fifteen he refused meat on fast days and fainted
in the field. What use is such a man?"

"He feels everything."

"That is his disease."

"Perhaps we are the disease."

Silan made an impatient gesture.

"Must three people die because one cannot live? You were
withering. I was burning. He stood between us like a
fence and called the fence holy."

"He did not put himself there. You did."

"I know!" Silan struck his fist against a timber. The
lantern jumped. "How many times must I say it? I
made a mistake. A man turns into the wrong channel;
does he smash the boat out of reverence for the
mistake? No. He finds deep water."

"And Mitia?"

"He must find his own."

"He says he will go to the Caucasus."

Silan stared.

"He told you?"

"Long ago."

"To those mad brethren?"

"Yes."

"They'll put ideas in him against the Tsar, against priests,
against property. He'll be arrested."

"He wants to go."

"He will not."

"Why keep him?"

"Because I will not have a son of mine wandering
with sectarians."

"You despise him, yet you hold him."

"He bears my name."

"And I bear his."

Silan's eyes flashed.

"Not in my heart."

She looked at him steadily.

"That does not change the papers."

He swore under his breath and reached for her, but
she rose and stepped beyond the shelter.

The fog was beginning to lift. The eastern sky remained
cloudy, yet its darkness had thinned from black to pearl-gray,
and a pale radiance lay upon the river. Marka stood
barefoot on the wet logs, holding the awning rope. The
current lifted strands of her hair and pressed her blouse
against her body. She looked young, strong, and sorrowful.

Silan followed.

"Come back. It's cold."

"No."

"You'll be wet through."

"I want air."

"There is air under the awning."

"Not enough."

The phrase, echoing Mitia without her knowledge, irritated him.

He took off his coat and put it around her
shoulders.

At the stern the figures of Mitia and Sergei had
become visible through the dissolving vapor. They stood close to
the long sweep. Sergei's head was bent, and the usual
angular liveliness had left his body. Mitia looked small beside
him, almost transparent in the growing light.

"What if he goes?" Marka asked.

"He won't."

"What if he does?"

"I'll fetch him back."

"Why?"

Silan was silent.

"Let him go," she urged. "Give him money. Let him
find his brethren. Then we could go away too."

"Still married."

"Far away, no one would know."

"God would know."

She looked at him in surprise.

"You say love justifies us."

"I say it makes the sin worth bearing. I don't
say there is no sin."

"What is the difference?"

"The difference is a man must know the weight he
carries."

Marka wrapped the coat more tightly around herself.

"And if the weight crushes someone else?"

Silan followed her gaze to the stern.

"He should grow stronger."

"Can grass grow strong under a cartwheel?"

"He is not grass."

"To you he is."

The clouds overhead began to break. Their lower edges kindled
faintly, first ash-white, then yellow. The immense gray wall which
had all night barred the river opened into drifting gates.
Through them appeared spaces of tender green sky, pure and
remote. The wooded bank emerged from mist; every birch trunk
shone silver, every pine branch carried trembling drops. A field
beyond the trees glimmered emerald, and over it the vapor
lay in long rosy bands.

Day broke gradually.

Nature, indifferent during the dark hours or seeming to brood
upon them, now thrilled, quickened, and smiled. The river changed
beneath the growing light. Its leaden surface became blue, then
purple, then gold where the east touched it; every small
wave raised a bright edge and hurried away rejoicing. Dew
upon the ropes flashed like diamonds. Birds called from the
bank, tentative at first, then in a multitude of clear
voices. A fish leaped and fell with a silver splash.
Smoke rose straight from an unseen cottage, and the smell
of damp earth came over the water.

Silan breathed deeply. Dawn restored his faith in strength. The
clearing sky, the broad river, the raft laden with his
timber, the woman warm beneath his coat—all seemed to confirm
him. Life moved forward. It did not apologize; it did
not ask permission of the weak or halt before old
mistakes. It burst through cloud, bred in mud, sang in
throats, swelled in muscle and breast. To live was to
desire, labour, possess, suffer, and go onward.

He put his arm around Marka.

"Look," he said. "Would you throw that away?"

She leaned against him.

"No."

"Nor I."

"Even for salvation?"

"We'll earn salvation afterward."

"How?"

"By living honestly when we can. We'll help people. Feed
beggars. Raise children well. No cheating. No cruelty."

She glanced toward Mitia.

"No cruelty?"

Silan's face hardened, then softened again.

"I'll speak to Sergei. He shall leave the boy alone."

"And you?"

"What of me?"

"Will you leave him alone?"

"He is my son."

"That is not an answer."

"It is all I have."

They stood watching the sunrise. Silan bent and kissed the
side of her neck. Marka closed her eyes. The light
spread over them boldly now, revealing what darkness had concealed:
his gray-threaded beard against her black hair, his thick arm
about her waist, her full lips parted in response. Their
tenderness was sinful, selfish, and alive; and because it was
alive, it possessed a beauty which made judgment difficult.

At the stern, Sergei saw them.

He glanced quickly at Mitia, hoping the pale man was
looking elsewhere. But Mitia too had seen.

For a moment his face showed nothing. Then he lowered
his eyes to the water.

Sergei cleared his throat.

"Sun's coming."

"Yes."

"It'll be warm."

"Yes."

"We'll make the landing by noon if the current holds."

Mitia did not answer.

Sergei moved nearer the edge, placing himself awkwardly between Mitia
and the lovers, though there was no way his lean
body could hide them across the length of the raft.

"Tea soon," he said. "I'll boil it strong."

Mitia's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, not quite pain.

"You need not be kind from fear."

"I'm not afraid."

"You are."

"Of you? I could tuck two of you under my
arm."

"Not of me. Of what is in me."

Sergei looked down into the brightening river.

"Perhaps."

The admission cost him effort.

Mitia turned his pale face toward the opening sky. In
the dawn his tears had dried to shining tracks. He
appeared exhausted, but his eyes were calm.

"Do you believe God can lead a man out?" he
asked.

"Out of what?"

"Sin. A house. Himself."

Sergei scratched his beard.

"I believe a man needs legs."

"And if his legs are weak?"

"Then someone may carry him a little."

Mitia studied him with surprise.

Sergei became embarrassed.

"Not me, mind! I've work enough. I mean—someone. Those brethren
of yours, perhaps."

"My father will not let me go."

"You are twenty-seven."

"He will beat me and lock away the money."

"Earn your own."

"With what strength?"

"There are ways. Keep accounts for a merchant. Read letters.
You read well, don't you?"

"Yes."

"Then use what God gave, even if it's only eyes
and a gloomy face. A widow might pay you to
read holy books."

Mitia looked again toward the bow. Silan and Marka still
stood together, luminous in the sunrise.

"He will always stand in the way."

Sergei followed his gaze.

"No man stands forever."

Mitia shivered.

"Do not say that."

"I meant—move round him. Like the snag. You don't break
the snag or ask it to be kind. You put
the sweep over and find water."

For the first time that night Mitia smiled. It was
a small, sorrowful smile, but it changed his whole face
and made Sergei aware of the youth buried beneath illness.

"You have preached to me now," Mitia said.

"Don't insult me."

They were silent again, though the silence had altered. It
no longer suffocated Sergei. Daylight filled it with birds, running
water, and the soft hiss of mist lifting from the
wet timber.

At the front, Silan released Marka and strode toward the
stern. The raft trembled under his steps. He came immense
and red in the morning light, his beard like fire,
his eyes clear from wakefulness and desire.

"Sergei!" he shouted. "Get the kettle on. We'll take tea."

"Aye."

"And mind the bend. There are shoals beyond that point."

"I know."

Silan stopped before Mitia.

The two looked at one another: the father overflowing with
life, the son worn thin by the effort of defending
an invisible law. Between them lay Marka's body, the authority
of the Church, the mockery of men, blood kinship, property,
fear, pity, and hatred—ropes knotted so tightly that no hand
could discover which end to pull.

"What was your yelling about?" Silan asked.

"Nothing," said Mitia.

"He was taken with a cough," Sergei interposed.

Silan glanced at him, sensing the lie.

"A cough has words now?"

"This one does."

"Keep your jokes for work."

Sergei lowered his eyes.

Silan turned back to his son.

"You're white as flour."

"I am tired."

"Sleep after tea."

"I will."

"And pull when I say pull. We're nearing a bad
channel."

"Yes, father."

The obedience pleased Silan and angered him at once. He
wanted resistance so that he might conquer it, yet Mitia's
submissiveness denied him the dignity of battle.

He seized the steering sweep.

"Look ahead!" he commanded.

All three looked.

Ahead, the Volga widened magnificently beneath the risen sun. Islands
floated in blue haze; sandbanks shone like gold; distant villages
lifted white bell towers above green fields. The clouds, defeated,
withdrew in purple masses toward the west. The river seemed
to promise freedom because no wall was visible upon it,
though every current had its channel and every raft was
bound to move where the water carried it.

Marka remained at the bow, Silan's coat around her shoulders.
She crossed herself before the bright east.

Her lips moved.

Perhaps she prayed for forgiveness. Perhaps she prayed for Mitia's
health. Perhaps, beneath both prayers, there remained the dark petition
she had confessed: that the frail obstruction might soon be
taken gently away, leaving the strong to live.

Mitia watched her, then looked beyond her toward the far
south, where the river vanished in light and where, immeasurably
farther, rose the mountains and the imagined brotherhood of pure
men. He saw no path from the raft to that
distant life. Yet he held the vision stubbornly within him,
a small flame guarded by thin hands against wind.

Silan planted his feet and leaned upon the sweep.

"Port!" he roared.

Sergei and Mitia threw their weight beside his. The great
blade resisted, bent, and slowly yielded. Water boiled around it
in green and golden whorls.

"Harder!"

They pulled.

The raft groaned like a living creature under torment. Logs
strained against ropes; chains clinked; the river slapped and gurgled
along its sides. Marka clung to a post at the
bow, her red blouse bright beneath the dark coat. Sergei
bared his teeth with effort. Mitia coughed, stumbled, recovered, and
pulled again.

Together, though each desired a different shore, they turned the
raft into the channel.

Then the current took them.

Behind, the fog closed over the night they had passed
through; ahead, the sun blazed upon fathomless water. Silan shouted,
Marka prayed, Sergei busied his tongue no more, and Mitia,
pale beneath the immense morning sky, held fast to the
sweep while the Volga bore them all relentlessly onward.

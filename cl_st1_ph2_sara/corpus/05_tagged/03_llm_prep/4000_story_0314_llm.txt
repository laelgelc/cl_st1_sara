### The Multiplication Table

"A hundred dollars!"

"Precisely."

"And all your own?"

"Every cent of it."

"Saved from your salary?"

"From what else should it be saved?"

"But you receive only six hundred dollars a year."

"Neither more nor less."

"And you intend to spend this money upon a journey?"

"I do. I shall see Niagara, pass a fortnight among
the White Mountains, and return with something still in my
pocket-book."

Hoffman pushed back his chair and regarded his companion with
open incredulity.

"Impossible!"

"Yet true."

"Then you have some private income."

"Not a shilling."

"Your uncle assists you."

"My uncle has six children of his own."

"Perhaps the firm allows you extra compensation."

"Not one six-pence. You and I entered the counting-room upon
the same day, perform nearly the same duties, and receive
the same salary."

"Then," said Hoffman, lowering his voice, "you must deny yourself
everything."

"Not everything."

"You never enjoy life."

"I think I enjoy it very well."

"You never smoke?"

"No."

"Never play billiards?"

"Rarely, and then only when another pays for the table."

"You do not go to the theatre?"

"Sometimes."

"Nor take drives, nor make excursions, nor indulge in a
supper?"

"When I can afford them."

Hoffman laughed uneasily.

"That means never."

"It means precisely what I said."

The two young men sat alone in the counting-room after
the other clerks had departed. The shutters were closed, but
through an upper pane came a narrow beam of the
declining sun, laying a bar of gold across the tall
desks and dusty ledgers. Hamilton had finished his work and
was tying a packet of papers. Hoffman, whose columns had
been completed half an hour before, still lingered.

They were both twenty-two years of age. Both had respectable
connexions, good health, fair prospects, and salaries of six hundred
dollars. But here the resemblance ended. Hamilton understood the use
of money; Hoffman practised the abuse of it. The one
had prepared for a pleasant vacation by a year of
steady foresight. The other, though he had never been beyond
the neighboring towns, owed nearly two hundred dollars and was
daily disturbed by demands which he could neither meet nor
silence.

"Hamilton," said he, after a pause, "will you lend me
twenty dollars?"

"No."

The answer was quiet, but decisive.

Hoffman colored.

"I did not expect such a refusal from a friend."

"I refuse because I am your friend."

"That is a convenient form of friendship."

"Perhaps. But tell me why you need twenty dollars."

"I have a small bill which has become troublesome."

"What bill?"

"A tailor's bill."

"Is that all?"

"There may be one or two others."

"How much do you owe?"

"I do not exactly know."

"Then twenty dollars will not relieve you."

"It will quiet the tailor."

"For a week; perhaps for a month. After that he
will return, while the money I lent you will have
gone to increase the number of your creditors."

"You speak as though I were a bankrupt."

"You spend more than you receive. What is that but
travelling the road to bankruptcy?"

"Preposterous! My salary is not large, but it ought to
support me."

"It ought."

"And yet I cannot save a dollar. Indeed, I do
not see how any young man in the city can
live respectably upon six hundred dollars."

"I live upon less."

"But you are peculiar."

"Arithmetic is not peculiar. Sit down, Hoffman."

Hamilton untied the packet, drew a clean sheet of paper
toward him, and dipped his pen.

"What are you going to do?"

"To discover where your money goes."

"I know where it goes. It goes for necessaries."

"We shall see. What do you pay for board?"

"Six dollars a week."

"That is three hundred and twelve dollars a year."

The sum was entered.

"My room, fuel, and lights are not included," added Hoffman.

"What do they cost?"

"About fifty dollars."

Hamilton wrote the second amount.

"Clothing?"

"A man must dress properly in our situation."

"Certainly. What does proper dress cost you?"

"I cannot say."

"What did you pay during the last twelve months?"

Hoffman hesitated.

"My new coat was thirty dollars; trousers and waistcoats perhaps
twenty-five; boots nearly twenty; hats, gloves, cravats, and such matters
may have been another twenty."

"Ninety-five dollars."

"It appears so."

"Washing and mending?"

"Two shillings here, three there. Say thirty dollars."

"Postage, church contributions, stationery, and other true necessaries?"

"Twenty dollars will cover them."

Hamilton added the figures.

"Your necessary expenditure, according to your own statement, is five
hundred and seven dollars. That leaves ninety-three dollars."

"Exactly!" exclaimed Hoffman. "And who can save a hundred dollars
out of ninety-three?"

"No one. But we have not yet examined your pleasures."

"My pleasures cost very little."

"A leak is a leak, though each drop be no
larger than a tear. Do you smoke?"

"Only moderately."

"How many cigars?"

"Two or three a day."

"Say three. What do they cost?"

"Three cents apiece."

"Nine cents a day; sixty-three cents a week; thirty-two dollars
and eighty-five cents a year."

Hoffman leaned forward.

"There is some mistake."

"Multiply for yourself."

"True as I live!" he muttered, after taking the pencil.
"Thirty-two dollars in smoke!"

"Thirty-two dollars and eighty-five cents, puffed away, leaving neither comfort,
strength, nor improvement behind it."

"One must have some little indulgence."

"Assuredly, if he can pay for it. But a little
indulgence which helps to make a debt of two hundred
dollars is not little in its consequences. Do you play
billiards?"

"Occasionally."

"How often?"

"Once or twice a week."

"Say twice. What do you pay?"

"I seldom lose more than a shilling on the game.
With my share of the table, perhaps twenty-five cents."

"Twice a week makes twenty-six dollars a year."

"Impossible!"

"Figures are inexorable."

Hamilton placed the sum beneath the first.

"Anything more connected with billiards?"

"Nothing worth naming."

"Name it."

"We sometimes take ale and oysters afterward."

"How often?"

"Not always."

"Once a week?"

"Twice, perhaps."

"What do they cost?"

"From half a dollar to a dollar."

"We will take the smaller average—seventy-five cents."

"You call that the smaller?"

"It lies midway between the sums you named. Twice a
week makes seventy-eight dollars a year."

Hoffman started to his feet.

"Seventy-eight dollars for ale and oysters! I will not believe
it."

"Then disprove it."

He examined the calculation, sat down again, and dropped his
eyes.

"I had no conception of it."

"No. Appetite commonly collects its tax in small coins, that
the payer may not perceive how heavy it is. What
next?"

"There is no next."

"Do you go to the theatre?"

"Every young man goes sometimes."

"How often?"

"Twice a month, perhaps."

"What does the evening cost?"

"The ticket is fifty cents. Refreshments and small matters make
it about a dollar."

"Twenty-four dollars a year."

"I suppose so."

"The opera and concerts?"

"Those are not regular."

"How many during the last season?"

"Six."

"What did they cost?"

"Two dollars each, including the carriage on rainy nights."

"Twelve dollars."

Hamilton continued writing. Hoffman watched the descending column as a
culprit might watch the preparation of evidence against him.

"You bought a gold chain in February," said Hamilton.

"A man should have a respectable watch-chain."

"Your old ribbon kept the watch safely."

"It did not look well."

"What did the chain cost?"

"Eighteen dollars."

"And the seal?"

"Two."

"Twenty dollars for jewelry. Did you pay for it?"

"Not all."

"Then it is not merely an ornament upon your waistcoat;
it is a weight upon your integrity. Anything more?"

"I cannot think of anything."

"How do you come to the office in bad weather?"

"In the omnibus."

"How often?"

"Perhaps four times a week during winter, and occasionally at
other seasons."

"What does transportation cost in the year?"

"I never counted it."

Hamilton questioned him closely, reducing wet mornings, late risings, and
evening rides to their money value. The total was twenty-six
dollars.

"Twenty-six!" cried Hoffman. "Why, each ride is scarcely felt."

"So is each drop from a leaking flagon. But while
you observe the drop, I observe the empty vessel."

Hoffman moved impatiently.

"You make life a ledger."

"Life keeps a ledger, whether we do or not. We
may neglect the entries, but we cannot escape the balance."

"What else have you discovered?"

"I have often seen you bring confections into the counting-room."

"A few nuts or sweetmeats."

"What do they cost weekly?"

"Not more than twenty-five cents."

"Thirteen dollars a year."

Hamilton entered the amount.

"And now the drives."

"What drives?"

"The horse and light wagon you hire on pleasant Sundays."

"Surely you do not condemn a breath of country air!"

"By no means. I condemn nothing until I know whether
it is bought with your money or your creditor's. How
many such drives?"

"Perhaps twelve in the year."

"At what cost?"

"Two dollars each, when shared with a friend."

"Twenty-four dollars."

Hoffman sighed.

"Anything more?" asked Hamilton.

"I begin to dread those words."

"Truth is sometimes unwelcome before it becomes useful. You went
upon several excursions last summer."

"Only six."

"To the islands, the fishing grounds, and neighboring towns?"

"Yes."

"What did each cost?"

"Five or six dollars."

"We will say six. Thirty-six dollars."

"That seems monstrous."

"The excursion was not monstrous. The folly consisted in taking
it when you had no money to spare."

Hamilton drew a line beneath the figures and began to
add. The scratching of his pen was the only sound
in the dim counting-room. Hoffman rested both elbows upon the
desk and pressed his hands against his temples.

"Well?" he asked at last.

"Your indulgences amount to two hundred and ninety-one dollars and
eighty-five cents."

"No!"

"Add them yourself."

Hoffman did so slowly. Twice he stopped, examined an item,
and resumed. At the end he stared at the total.

"Nearly three hundred dollars!"

"Yes."

"But I never possessed three hundred dollars at one time."

"You did not need to possess it at one time
in order to waste it. The spendthrift seldom throws his
purse into the river; he empties it by pennies."

"And with my necessary expenses—"

"Your total expenditure is seven hundred and ninety-eight dollars and
eighty-five cents."

"Against an income of six hundred!"

"Precisely."

"Then my debt—"

"Is no mystery. You have been spending nearly two hundred
dollars a year more than you earn."

Hoffman rose again, walked to the window, and looked through
the dusty pane into the street. The revelation had touched
him more deeply than any rebuke could have done. He
had considered himself unfortunate, underpaid, and pursued by unreasonable tradesmen.
He now saw that no secret calamity had overtaken him.
His embarrassment was of his own making. It had been
manufactured cigar by cigar, game by game, supper by supper,
and ride by ride.

After some minutes he turned.

"Hamilton, I am ashamed."

"That is better than being angry."

"I suspected you of meanness."

"Because I saved what you wasted?"

"Yes. When you declined a supper, I called you narrow.
When you walked instead of riding, I thought you wished
to display your economy. When you wore the same coat
another season, I imagined that you had no taste."

"And now?"

"Now I see that you were purchasing freedom while I
was buying thraldom."

Hamilton's grave expression softened.

"You see only the beginning. Debt brings more than a
want of money. It brings concealments, excuses, broken promises, fear
of meeting an honest tradesman, humiliation before those whose respect
we desire, and a perpetual disquietude which poisons even the
pleasures for which the debt was incurred."

"That is true," said Hoffman. "I have crossed the street
to avoid my tailor. I have denied myself to the
man who sold me the chain. Yesterday I told the
bootmaker that my salary had been delayed, though it had
been paid that morning."

"There is the moral cost. Your cigars did not consume
money alone. Your billiards did not waste time alone. Every
indulgence which you could not honestly afford weakened your integrity
of purpose. Appetite became master, and reason its servant."

"But must a man renounce all amusement?"

"No. Thrift is not misery, nor is self-denial the destruction
of enjoyment. I have books, friends, walks, music, and innocent
recreation. I sometimes attend the theatre; but I first ask,
'Can I pay for it without injustice to another duty?'
If the answer is no, I remain at home and
am not degraded by the refusal."

"How did you save your hundred dollars?"

"By spending less than I received."

"That sounds too simple."

"All great rules are simple. I found a respectable boarding-house
at five dollars a week. I kept an account of
every expense. On receiving my monthly salary, I set apart
the sum intended for saving before I touched a dollar
for pleasure. I bought no article merely because others possessed
it. I distinguished between what was useful and what was
only desired."

"And you never found the restraint irksome?"

"At first, often. But self-command grows by exercise. The man
who denies himself a cigar to-day finds it easier to
deny himself a foolish supper to-morrow. Presently he is no
longer the trembling subject of every appetite, but its governor.
He becomes self-poised, self-controlling, self-possessing."

Hoffman looked again at the paper.

"If I abandoned cigars, billiards, suppers, jewelry, and unnecessary rides,
I might not only pay my debts but save."

"Certainly."

"In two years I might be free."

"In less, if you are earnest."

"And afterward I, too, might travel."

"With money honestly your own, and therefore with tranquillity of
mind. I shall enjoy Niagara not merely because the cataract
is grand, but because no creditor has been deprived that
I may look upon it. Pleasure purchased by injustice carries
bitterness in its cup."

Hoffman extended his hand.

"You have refused me twenty dollars, Hamilton, but you have
given me something worth more."

"Use it, then."

"I will. To-night I shall write down every debt. To-morrow
I shall speak plainly to each creditor and promise only
what I can perform. The chain shall be returned or
sold. The cigars are ended. Billiards, ale, oysters, needless drives—all
must wait until they can be honestly paid for."

"Make no violent promises merely because you are moved at
this moment."

"This is not a momentary movement. I see that the
question concerns character. If I cannot govern six hundred dollars,
I should abuse six thousand. If I cannot resist a
small appetite, I shall never be fit for a large
trust."

"Spoken wisely."

"And when temptation comes?"

"Open this account. A single coin may look insignificant; fifty-two
weeks will show its offspring. Remember that poor Richard's time,
poor Richard's pennies, and poor Richard's habits all multiply while
careless men sleep."

Hoffman folded the paper and placed it in his pocket-book.

"I shall keep it."

"Not as an object of shame, I hope."

"As a warning. It has shown me that disorder does
not descend upon a man like a storm. He builds
it by degrees. So, too, order must be built—one refusal,
one payment, one honest entry at a time."

Hamilton took his hat.

"Then my work is finished."

"Not quite," said Hoffman, smiling faintly. "When you return from
Niagara, you must examine my accounts."

"If you keep them faithfully, you will need no examiner."

"I shall keep them. You have taught me anew the
multiplication table."

They left the counting-room together. Hamilton walked with the quiet
step of one whose means, wishes, and duties were at
peace. Hoffman walked thoughtfully, feeling the weight of his debts,
but feeling also the first strength of a resolution honestly
formed. The road before him was not one of splendid
sacrifice. It consisted of humble acts—of refusing, recording, paying, and
waiting. Yet by such acts men acquire independence; for wealth
begins not in abundance, but in order, and true enjoyment
belongs less to him who gratifies every desire than to
him who has learned wisely, prudently, justly, and cheerfully to
govern himself.

The grey prairie came down from the north in long,
dusty billows, broke against a ragged barricade of cottonwoods, and
then went crawling toward the ford as though thirsty enough
to drink the river dry. Beyond it, remote and blue,
the mountains appeared to have been put there by a
careless stage carpenter who had forgotten to bring them forward.

Above the ford squatted Bailey's Roadhouse.

It was a broad, low structure of sun-blackened timber and
adobe, with a porch sagging over its entrance and two
dead chimneys sticking out of its roof like the ears
of an attentive jackass. From the road it watched three
trails meet, quarrel briefly, and separate. One went west to
the Bar X range, one east toward town, and the
third vanished among southern ridges where honest men were not
encouraged to inquire after it.

Bailey himself harmonized with his establishment. He was long in
the body, short in the neck, pale about the eyes,
and possessed of hands that were always wiping themselves upon
his apron without becoming cleaner. He asked every traveler where
he had come from, where he was going, how much
money he carried, and whether anybody expected him. These questions
he described as sociability.

At four o'clock on a blistered September afternoon, Bailey stood
in his doorway and saw a moving object approach along
the western trail. First it resembled a whisky barrel traveling
by its own volition. Then it became a cannon ball
mounted on two bowed legs. Finally, at the hitching rail,
it dismounted from a large sorrel horse and disclosed itself
as Shorty McCabe of the Bar X.

Shorty was four feet and a fraction in height, though
he had been known to dispute the fraction with firearms.
His shoulders were as broad as Bailey's doorway; his hands
hung almost to his knees; his head was large, round,
and thatched with rust-colored hair. Viewed from behind, he suggested
a stocky catapult in chaps. Viewed from the front, he
suggested that the catapult had been loaded.

He threw his reins over the rail and stamped into
the barroom.

Three men sat at a table near the window. They
were freighters, and had been drinking long enough to regard
the universe as an entertainment arranged at somebody else's expense.
The youngest looked up, stared at Shorty, and laughed.

It was not an especially malicious laugh. It was merely
unfortunate.

Shorty stopped.

"Did somethin' humorous happen to your insides?" he asked.

The freighter's companions became intensely occupied with their cards.

"Nothin'," said the young man. "I was jest thinkin'."

"Don't," advised Shorty. "You ain't built for it."

The freighter laughed again, partly from embarrassment and partly because
he had not yet appreciated the geographical danger of his
position.

Shorty crossed the room with astonishing speed. He did not
run in the ordinary sense; he bounced. One moment he
was at the door, shaped like a large red bug;
the next he had seized the freighter by belt and
collar, lifted him from his chair, and inserted him beneath
the table. He then kicked the table over, sat upon
it, and addressed the muffled victim through the boards.

"I'm violently addicted to peace an' quiet," he said in
his cavernous bass. "But if you keep gigglin', I'll take
you apart till you look like cut feed."

"Lemme out!"

"Will you maintain a respectful melancholy?"

"Yes!"

Shorty rose. The freighter emerged, rumpled and cautious. His companions
examined their cards with the solemnity of judges considering a
hanging.

Bailey leaned upon the bar.

"You got a mighty touchy disposition," he remarked.

Shorty's eyes turned toward him.

"Bailey," he said, "I come twenty-seven miles for a drink,
not for a description of my disposition. Put the bottle
where it can do some good."

Bailey set out a glass, but kept one hand on
the bottle.

"You stayin'?"

"Reckon not."

"Headed back to the Bar X?"

"Mebbe."

"Tonight?"

Shorty's face hardened.

"What's the unnecessary heat in them questions?"

"No heat," Bailey said quickly. "Road's bad after dark, that's
all."

"The road an' me has an understandin'. I don't trip
it, an' it don't laugh at me."

He filled his glass. Before he could drink, hoofbeats sounded
outside—two horses, traveling easily. Bailey turned toward the window with
such abrupt attention that Shorty noticed it. A young man
rode into the yard, followed by a woman on a
neat bay mare.

The man was tall, sun-browned, and carried himself with the
unworried straightness of one who had recently been entrusted with
authority and had not yet discovered its bookkeeping. A silver
star shone on his vest. He dismounted, lifted the woman
from her saddle, and kissed her hand with a gallantry
so unashamed that the freighters grinned.

Shorty looked at the star and scowled.

"Law," he muttered. "An' wearin' it outside where it can
catch cold."

The young man entered with the woman beside him.

"Evening," he said. "Ross Turney. This is Mrs. Turney. We'd
like supper, beds, and enough grain to persuade two horses
they haven't been cheated."

Bailey's pale eyes flickered at the badge.

"Turney," he repeated. "New sheriff over in Mason County?"

"The electorate committed that error Tuesday."

"Thought you wasn't married."

"I repaired that Thursday."

The bride smiled. She was not fragile, though Shorty instantly
imagined her so. Dust lay on her riding skirt, and
the afternoon wind had loosened strands of dark hair beneath
her hat. Her face had the clear, rested kindness which
belongs to people who have not yet learned that courtesy
may be mistaken for surrender.

Ross glanced at Shorty.

"You must be Mr. McCabe."

Shorty set down his untouched whisky.

"I might be. Who's wantin' to know?"

"Bar X foreman told me about you."

Shorty swelled ominously.

"What'd he say?"

"That if I ever needed six feet of fighting man
and didn't care how it was folded, I ought to
find Shorty McCabe."

One of the freighters choked upon a laugh. Shorty whirled,
but Ross's own expression held no ridicule—only cheerful admiration.

The bride looked at Shorty gravely.

"I'm pleased to meet you, Mr. McCabe."

She offered her hand.

Shorty stared at it.

Many persons had pointed at him. Some had prodded him
while he slept. Children had followed him in settlements; men
had measured him against bar fixtures and proposed wagers concerning
his weight. But no woman such as this had ever
held out her hand as if there were nothing invidious,
comic, or alarming in the transaction.

He took it with exquisite caution. His hand engulfed hers.

"Ma'am," he said.

The single word emerged in a rumble fit to loosen
plaster, yet Shorty himself went crimson from collar to hair.

"You ride all the way from Mason?" he asked after
a painful interval.

"Since morning."

"Road's rough."

"So I discovered."

"I could—if your saddle—there's a spring loose, likely. I seen—when
you come in—I know saddles."

Ross grinned. "He knows saddles, Clara. That's luck."

Shorty darted him a murderous glance, mistaking the grin for
amusement. Then Clara said, "Would you look at it for
me, Mr. McCabe? I should be grateful."

Shorty marched from the room as though commissioned by the
President to save the nation.

In the yard he inspected Clara's saddle, found nothing wrong,
and spent twenty minutes repairing it. When he returned, supper
was ready. Bailey showed the Turneys to a table and,
with an economy of hospitality, informed Shorty that there was
no place for him.

Shorty examined the nearly empty room.

"I perceive the congestion," he said.

Clara moved her chair.

"Please sit with us."

Shorty sat.

This required negotiation between his body, the chair, and the
table, none of which had been designed with the others
in mind. His knees rose on either side of his
plate; his shoulders obscured portions of the horizon; and whenever
he reached for bread, the lamp flame retreated. Yet Clara
spoke to him about the Bar X, the mountains, and
the country west of the ford without once appearing to
notice these phenomena.

Ross talked freely. He was going to inspect the southern
settlements, he said. Rumor placed the Tremper gang in the
hills. Their leader, Clay Tremper, had robbed two stages and
killed a deputy.

Bailey dropped a spoon.

"Thought Tremper was in Mexico," he said.

"So does everybody who'd rather not meet him," Ross replied.

Bailey picked up the spoon and went into the kitchen.

Shorty watched him leave. "You huntin' Tremper alone?"

"I've got warrants and optimism."

"Optimism's hard to reload."

"You interested?"

"No. I'm a cow hand. Peaceful profession."

Ross looked toward the freighter still brushing dust from his
clothes.

"So I heard."

Shorty's eyes narrowed, but Clara asked whether he had lived
long at the Bar X, and his bellicosity evaporated. He
told her of winter storms, trail herds, and the time
a Bar X bull had chased a visiting senator into
a water tank. Under the beatifying influence of her attention,
Shorty grew almost graceful. He forgot his hands. He forgot
his boots did not touch the floor. Once he even
laughed.

Then he saw the freighters watching him.

Their faces were not cruel, but they wore the smothered
mirth of men observing a bear trying to thread a
needle. Shorty's joy collapsed. He pushed back his chair.

"I'll be ridin'."

"It's dark," Clara said.

"Moon's adequate."

"You could stay here."

"No, ma'am. If I don't git back, they'll think I
been drinkin'."

Ross glanced at the full glass still standing on the
bar. "That would be unjust."

Shorty put on his hat. At the door Clara thanked
him for the saddle. Ross shook his hand and said,
"Come see us when we're settled."

They meant it. That was the trouble.

Outside, Shorty saddled the sorrel, mounted, rode half a mile
toward the Bar X, then stopped beneath the cottonwoods. The
moon had not yet risen. The prairie lay flat and
horrent beneath the stars. Behind him, Bailey's windows made four
yellow holes in the darkness.

Shorty sat with bowed head.

He was acquainted with loneliness as one becomes acquainted with
an old ranch dog: by tripping over it repeatedly and
pretending not to care. He had met kindness, too, but
usually the rough, male variety which slapped his back, praised
his fists, and invited him to perform feats of strength.
Clara Turney's kindness was different. It had not forgiven his
appearance, because it had not accused him.

He imagined her in Mason County, walking beside Ross, entering
shops, planting flowers by a white house. There was no
place in that picture for an object resembling a whisky
barrel in boots.

"That's sensible," he told the horse. "Now move."

The horse did not.

Shorty turned it around.

"I forgot to water you."

At the roadhouse he led the sorrel to the trough,
though the animal drank with the reluctance of one being
made an accessory to dishonesty. Shorty slipped in through the
kitchen. The building had grown quiet. Bailey was nowhere visible.
A lamp burned low behind the bar.

Shorty contemplated the bottle.

Whisky, in his experience, did not alter the world's opinion,
but it provided a temporary indifference to the verdict. He
poured a glass, drank it, and felt no benefit. He
drank another. Presently a new man arose within him: taller,
handsomer, and magnificently contemptuous of mankind.

"You're a liar," Shorty told this new man. "But you're
agreeable."

He drank to him.

Footsteps sounded on the porch.

Shorty looked around. He did not wish Bailey to catch
him drinking, since Bailey might tell the Bar X, and
then both Shorty and the new man would be dismissed.
He dropped behind the bar.

The front door opened. Bailey entered carrying an unlit lantern.
He went to the window and raised it an inch.

"Now," he whispered.

Far off, a night bird called twice. Bailey answered by
passing his hand three times across the lamp flame.

Shorty's intoxicated brain pursued the significance of this act and
caught it by the tail.

Hoofbeats came softly from the south.

He crouched lower. Through a crack between barrels he saw
the door open again. Five men entered. They wore dusters,
neckerchiefs, and the hard, depleted faces of wolves that had
learned to count money.

The first man was Clay Tremper. Shorty knew him from
a poster: thin, black-haired, with a white scar crossing his
chin. The poster offered fifteen hundred dollars for him alive,
one thousand dead, and thereby revealed an official but puzzling
preference.

"Where is he?" Tremper asked.

"Upstairs," Bailey said. "Him and the woman. Guns on a
chair in their room, likely."

"Likely?"

"I couldn't search 'em at supper."

Tremper struck Bailey with the back of his hand.

"Don't sell me guesses."

Bailey's face went pale, but his eyes filled with a
furtive venom. "I got him here, didn't I?"

"Rufe, cover the stairs. Hank, back door. We take Turney
alive till he tells us where them warrants are."

"What about the woman?" asked one outlaw.

Tremper shrugged. "Keep her quiet."

Behind the bar, Shorty's fingers closed around his revolver.

Then they loosened.

Ross Turney was a sheriff. He had chosen the occupation
and must accept its inconveniences. Moreover, he had laughed—not cruelly,
perhaps, but he had laughed. Tall men laughed easily. They
could afford generosity; the world was built to their measurements.

Shorty reached for the bottle.

Upstairs a board creaked.

Ross's voice called, "Bailey?"

Tremper's men flattened against the walls.

Ross came down in shirtsleeves, his badge removed, one suspender
hanging. He held a lamp in one hand and no
weapon in the other. Halfway down, he saw the rifles.

"Evening, Clay," he said.

"Hands up."

Ross raised them contemporaneously with his eyebrows. "This seems inhospitable."

Rufe climbed behind him, pressed a pistol into his back,
and marched him down. Bailey snatched the lamp away.

"Where's your gun?" Tremper demanded.

"Somewhere you aren't."

Tremper hit him in the stomach. Ross folded, recovered, and
smiled thinly.

"You'll have to improve that if you mean to run
for office."

They struck him again and bound his wrists. Shorty remained
motionless. A rancid little argument conducted itself inside his skull.
One voice said there were five outlaws and Bailey. Another
observed that he was drunk. A third, newly constructed of
whisky and injured dignity, maintained that Ross was excessively tall
and could spare some suffering.

Then a door slammed overhead.

There was a scuffle, a man's curse, and Clara screamed.

Shorty stood up.

Unfortunately, his legs had not been consulted. He rose like
a pigeon flushed from a barrel, collided with the shelves,
and sent three glasses leaping to the floor.

Every face turned.

Shorty emerged from behind the bar with a revolver in
each hand and Bailey's towel draped over one shoulder.

"Gentlemen," he boomed, "you got one opportunity to become a
peaceful congregation. Face that wall."

For one second the gang stared.

Then they laughed.

Shorty blinked.

The laughter was not aimed at him. It was the
incredulous response of armed men surprised by an apparition four
feet high, half drunk, and decorated with bar linen. But
lifelong habit supplied what sobriety might have withheld.

His right-hand gun exploded.

Clay Tremper spun sideways. His pistol fell, and blood appeared
along his forearm.

"Next feller," Shorty said, "loses a more personal portion."

The laughter stopped.

Rufe's rifle barrel tilted.

Shorty shot the lamp out of his hand.

"Wall," he ordered. "Noses friendly against it."

Ross moved abruptly, knocking his shoulder into Hank. In the
confusion, Tremper dove for his fallen gun. Shorty fired into
the floor between his fingers.

"You're disappointin' me, Clay."

Tremper froze.

Bailey began edging toward a shotgun beneath the bar. Shorty,
whose vision was now divided into several overlapping editions of
the room, saw at least three Baileys reaching for it.
He selected the middle one and threw his left-hand revolver.
It struck Bailey above the ear with a sound like
an ax hitting a melon. Bailey sat down.

"Sheriff," Shorty said, "I got a modicum of control here,
but it ain't durable. Where's your artillery?"

"Upstairs."

At that moment Clara appeared at the top of the
stairs.

Her hair was loose. She held Ross's revolver in both
hands, and behind her sprawled an outlaw whom she had
apparently discouraged with a water pitcher. She was unharmed.

Shorty stared at her.

The muzzles of his remaining gun wandered toward the ceiling.

Tremper saw it and sprang.

Ross kicked Tremper's knee. Shorty, recovering, fired. The bullet shaved
the outlaw's shoulder and buried itself in the wall. Tremper
fell across a table, bringing it down upon himself with
comprehensive violence.

"Clara!" Ross shouted.

She tossed him the revolver.

Ross caught it, wheeled, and covered the room.

"Everybody still," he said.

His voice had changed. The easy humor was gone, leaving
something level and metallic.

Rufe released his rifle. Hank followed. The outlaw at the
top of the stairs groaned and decided not to participate.

Shorty leaned both hands upon the bar. The floor rolled
gently under him like a prairie viewed from a ship.
Clara hurried down, stepped past Tremper, and reached Ross. She
tried to untie his wrists, but her fingers shook.

"Knife in my boot," Ross said.

Shorty drew it and cut him loose. Ross took command
with brisk efficiency. He disarmed the gang, tied them with
curtain cords and rawhide, and arranged them facing the wall.
Bailey, having revived, joined them with a lump above his
eye and a grievance against architecture.

When it was finished, Ross turned to Clara.

"Did they hurt you?"

"No. The man upstairs grabbed my arm. I hit him
with the pitcher."

Ross embraced her. The spectacle caused Shorty to inspect his
boots. He felt himself receding, becoming once more an oddly
shaped object in a room designed for other people.

Clara disengaged and came to him.

"Mr. McCabe."

Shorty kept his eyes down. "Ma'am."

"You saved us."

"No, ma'am. Sheriff done considerable. You fetched the gun. I
mostly damaged the furniture."

She placed her hand on his sleeve. "You saved us."

A tremor passed through Shorty, more destabilizing than whisky.

Ross gripped his shoulder.

"You little red thunderbolt," he said. "I thought you'd gone
back to the Bar X."

"I did. Forgot to water my horse."

Ross looked toward the trough, then at the full water
bucket beside the door. "Naturally."

Shorty frowned. "You laughin'?"

"Not unless you give permission."

The answer disarmed him more completely than the Trempers had
been disarmed. He studied Ross's face, found only friendship there,
and gave a reluctant grunt.

"One small laugh," he conceded. "Don't spread it around."

Ross laughed once.

Shorty laughed too.

The remainder of the night was occupied with the tedious
aftermath of heroism. Wounds had to be bandaged, horses gathered,
knots examined, and Bailey's hidden cellar searched. It contained stolen
mail sacks, three cases of rifles, a judge's watch, and
enough illicit whisky to float the roadhouse downriver. Shorty helped
with the search until he found the whisky, after which
Ross assigned him to guard the prisoners from a chair.

Shorty sat with a revolver across his knees.

Clay Tremper glared over his shoulder. "You fool. You know
who I am?"

"Yep."

"You think these ropes'll hold me?"

"Nope."

Tremper smiled.

"That's why," Shorty continued, "if you wiggle, I'm goin' to
shoot you in a region that'll reduce your enthusiasm for
posterity."

Tremper ceased wiggling.

Near dawn Ross found a reward circular among Bailey's papers.
He smoothed it on the bar.

"Clay Tremper, fifteen hundred. Rufe Mason, eight hundred. Hank Doolan,
six. The other two, five apiece. Bailey should bring something
for conspiracy and bad coffee. That's close to four thousand
dollars, Shorty."

Shorty's eyelids lifted with difficulty.

"Seems excessive. They wasn't much trouble."

"It's yours."

"Mine?"

"You captured them."

"I was drunk."

"The circular makes no stipulation regarding temperance."

"I ain't takin' all that."

"You earned it."

Shorty looked across the room. Clara had fallen asleep in
a chair, wrapped in Ross's coat. Pale morning light touched
her face. She seemed even younger asleep, and the tenderness
which seized Shorty was so large and painful that he
attempted to disguise it by scratching his ribs.

"You two was headed on a trip after this sheriff
business?" he asked.

Ross nodded. "We meant to go to San Francisco. Election
cost more than expected. We postponed it."

"Then she gets half."

Ross's expression softened. "Shorty—"

"She fetched your gun."

"That leaves two thousand for you."

Shorty scowled. "I can cipher."

"You could buy land."

"I got land. Bar X got ninety thousand acres, an'
I ride all of it without payin' taxes."

"A house, then."

"What'd I do with a house?"

"Live in it."

"By myself?" Shorty snorted. "That'd be like lockin' a bullfrog
in a cathedral."

Clara opened her eyes.

"What are you discussing?"

"Your honeymoon," Shorty said, immediately losing fluency. "Reward—half—San Francisco—sheriff's careless
with money."

She understood. "I can't take it."

"You can."

"I won't."

Shorty rose, which gave his declaration such altitude as was
available to him.

"Ma'am, I'm responsible for them prisoners, an' I say half
the reward belongs to you. If you refuse, I'll turn
'em loose an' we'll all have to do this again."

Ross considered the five bound outlaws. "He may mean it."

Clara came to Shorty and kissed his cheek.

The formidable dwarf stood petrified. His eyes closed. His countenance
became beatified, foolish, and defenseless. When he opened them, the
freighters—who had crept downstairs during the disturbance and hidden beneath
their beds—were watching from the doorway.

One of them smiled.

Shorty pointed at him.

"Careful."

The smile vanished.

Sunrise found Ross organizing a mounted procession toward Mason County.
The outlaws were tied to their saddles. Bailey rode among
them, complaining that his own rope was too tight. Ross
replied that justice was frequently uncomfortable around the waist.

Shorty saddled his sorrel.

"You aren't coming with us?" Clara asked.

"Got to git back. Foreman's likely suffered enough peace by
now."

"You will visit?"

Shorty looked at Ross.

"Invitation stand?"

"It stands if we have to widen the door."

Shorty contemplated whether this constituted ridicule. Ross's solemn face gave
nothing away.

"I'll bring an ax," Shorty said.

Clara smiled. "Goodbye, Mr. McCabe."

"Shorty," he muttered.

"What?"

"Friends call me Shorty."

"Goodbye, Shorty."

He mounted and rode west beneath the cottonwoods. The morning
spread gold across the prairie; the mountains came forward from
their painted remoteness; dust lifted behind his horse in a
long, luminous plume.

Near noon he reached the Bar X.

The foreman came out of the bunkhouse, surveyed Shorty's red
eyes, torn shirt, powder-blackened hands, and the bruise on his
forehead where the whisky shelf had struck him.

"Been drinkin'?" he asked.

"Not to any practical extent."

"Where was you all night?"

Shorty dismounted.

"Helpin' the new sheriff collect the Tremper gang."

The foreman stared, then laughed. Men emerged from the bunkhouse.
Shorty's hand drifted toward his belt, stopped, and fell away.

"Laugh," he said magnanimously. "I'm broadenin' my nature."

"You caught the Trempers?"

"Me, Turney, an' his bride."

"All five?"

"An' Bailey."

The foreman whistled. "How'd you happen to take their side?"

Shorty considered the question. Behind him the trail ran back
through the vast grey morning to the roadhouse, to Ross's
handshake, and to a woman who had looked upon him
without surprise.

"Well," he said, "I took a likin' to them outlaws."

"The Trempers?"

"Sure. Outside of Mrs. Turney, they was the only folks
in the place that didn't laugh at me."

Then he bounced toward the bunkhouse like a cannon ball
going home.

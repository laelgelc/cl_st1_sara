In the winter when the snow lay upon Tullagh from
the feast of Saint Brigid until the first crying of
the curlew, there was such cold in the valley that
the little river ceased beneath its stones, and the oak
trees split in the night, and wolves came out of
the northern wood to sniff about the wicker cells of
the brethren. The monks wore two habits beneath their white
cloaks, and slept with their sandals against their breasts, and
even Brother Bald Fox, who was old and lean and
proud of enduring every hardship, confessed that the frost had
bitten three of his toes.

Yet within the refectory there were warmth and good fellowship;
for a fire of ash logs burned upon the hearth,
and one brother mended a net, and one carved a
handle for a spade, and one ground colours for an
illuminated book, and Brother Bittern told how the crystalline spheres
were turned by angelic orders, while the youngest acolytes stared
upward through the smoke hole, where now and again a
star appeared.

Among these young ones sat Olioll, a fair-haired child with
grey eyes, whom all loved and none could teach.

If Brother Dove said, "Olioll, how many persons are there
in the Holy Trinity?" the boy heard the mouse scratching
beneath the rushes.

If Brother Bittern said, "Olioll, decline unto me the word
for heaven," the boy watched a spark ascend into the
darkness.

And if the abbot himself asked, "Olioll, what is nothing?"
the child answered, after long thought, "It is that which
God had before He made the world."

At this the brethren smiled, but the abbot did not
smile.

Now, while they sat thus, a knocking came upon the
outer gate, although the wind blew fiercely and the snow
had covered every path. Brother Gatekeeper crossed himself, for no
Christian man willingly travelled on such a night, and Brother
Bald Fox whispered that perhaps the wolves had learned to
knock. But the abbot lifted his hand and said:

"Open. For whether it be man or beast, it seeks
succour."

When the gate was opened, there stood a beggar, crooked
with weariness and clothed in rags. Snow lay upon his
shoulders, blood stained the wrappings around his feet, and ice
hung from his beard. He carried neither staff nor wallet,
and his head was uncovered to the storm. Behind him,
at the edge of the torchlight, stood three wolves; yet
they did not spring, nor bare their teeth, but watched
him as tame hounds watch their master.

"Peace be upon this house," said the beggar.

"And upon thy soul peace," said the abbot. "Enter and
be warmed."

The stranger came in, and when the gate closed the
wolves turned silently toward the wood. The monks gave him
bread and broth, and Brother Bittern brought a cup of
ale, but the beggar drank only water. They gave him
a place near the fire, but he chose the stones
beside the door. They asked his name, but he bowed
his head.

"I had a name," he said, "but it grew too
heavy, and I laid it down."

Brother Bald Fox muttered that a nameless guest might be
a thief or an excommunicated king, but the abbot rebuked
him.

"Christ also comes without a name," he said, "and often
in torn garments."

In the morning the beggar begged leave to remain until
the roads were opened, and the abbot consented, saying that
every man at Tullagh must take his share of labour
and prayer. Now there was in the millhouse a great
quern which two strong brothers could scarcely turn, for its
axle was warped and its upper stone rough. To this
task they appointed the beggar, thinking it would warm his
blood and earn his bread.

He set his shoulder to the beam and turned it
from prime until noon, and from noon until vespers; and
all the while he sang softly, not songs of battle
or of earthly love, but broken verses of the Psalms,
mingled with words no brother knew. The stone groaned, the
grain cracked, the flour rose white upon his rags, and
small birds came through the roof and perched upon the
beam above him. When Brother Dove entered to find fault
with the fineness of the meal, a robin sat upon
the beggar's hand and took crumbs from his palm.

"Thou hast bewitched it," said Brother Dove.

"Nay," said the beggar. "It is only less afraid than
we are."

Olioll often came to the millhouse, for he loved the
circling stone, the yellow grain, the creaking beam, and the
narrow shaft of sunlight wherein the flour dust moved like
a host of golden spirits. While the beggar worked, the
child sat upon a sack and asked questions.

"What are the stars?" he asked.

"Windows," said the beggar.

"Who looks through them?"

"Those who have forgotten themselves."

"Where is God?"

"Everywhere."

"Is He in the fire?"

"He is the love within the fire."

"Is He in the snow?"

"He is the purity beneath its cold."

"Is He in nothing?"

"Most surely, little brother, for where nothing remains, God has
room to be all."

Olioll pondered these replies; and when the bell rang, the
beggar brushed flour from the boy's hair and sent him
back to his book.

Thereafter a marvel began. Olioll, who had never remembered two
letters together, read the Gospel appointed for the day without
stumbling. He explained why the seraphim are said to burn
and the cherubim to know; he recited the succession of
kings, the computation of Easter, and the names of martyrs
whose bones lay in jewelled reliquaries beyond the sea. He
who had stained every parchment now copied a page so
fair that the red capitals seemed berries upon snow.

The brethren rejoiced, but Brother Dove was troubled, for he
had taught the boy seven years and believed that knowledge
should enter painfully, like a nail driven into oak.

"This is no natural learning," he said. "Either the child
deceived us before, or someone deceives us now."

He questioned Olioll, but the boy only answered, "I sleep,
and when I wake the words are there."

Then Brother Dove feared witchcraft, and for three days he
watched. Upon the fourth evening, after compline, Olioll slipped from
the chapel and went toward the millhouse. Snow had begun
to fall, and the moon was thin as a shaving
from the host. Brother Dove drew his hood about his
face and followed.

At dawn he came before the abbot, pale, trembling, and
wet to the knees. The brethren gathered round him—one bearing
a lamp, one a censer, one the abbot's crozier, and
one the Book of the Gospels—and Brother Dove spoke.

"O blessed abbot, I have sinned through suspicion, yet through
suspicion I have seen a thing more glorious than all
the painted books of Tullagh.

"I followed the child to the millhouse, and there the
beggar waited beside a little fire. Olioll laid his head
upon the old man's knees and said, 'I am weary
of knowing.' And the beggar answered, 'Then know nothing for
a while.'

"The child asked, 'Will the learning leave me?'

"And the beggar said, 'All things leave us except love.'

"Then he told Olioll of the birds that fly beyond
sight, and of the seeds that sleep under snow, and
of the blessed dead who rest nearer to us than
breath. And the child slept.

"When he was sleeping, the beggar wept over him. His
tears fell upon the boy's brow, and he lifted his
hands, saying, 'O Thou Who dwellest beyond the stars, and
within the smallest grain, behold this empty vessel. Pour into
him not knowledge that brings honour, nor wit that conquers
men, but such wisdom as shall make him gentle. Let
him know Thy works, yet remain innocent of pride. Let
him bear light, yet believe himself darkness.'

"As he prayed, a brightness appeared around the child. It
was not the redness of fire, nor the whiteness of
moon, but a living radiance, soft and clear, and in
its heart burned a colour like a ruby held before
the sun. The roof seemed to vanish, and I saw
stars above us though clouds covered the sky. Then there
came the scent of roses, although all the earth lay
frozen.

"I was afraid and fled into the wood. But the
wolves found me. One stood before me, one behind me,
and one at my side, and I thought my punishment
had come. Yet they harmed me not. They led me
back to the path and left me at the gate."

When Brother Dove had finished, there was silence, save for
the bell rope tapping against the chapel wall.

The abbot covered his face. Long ago, when he was
a novice, he had heard of Aengus of the Fires,
who healed the sick and spoke with beasts, and whose
prayers were said to shine visibly in darkness. Kings had
sought his blessing, bishops had offered him high office, and
poets had carried his fame throughout the island. But pride
had entered him like a hairline crack in a chalice;
and when he perceived it, he cast away his crozier,
his books, his cloak, and even his name, and vanished
among the roads.

"At last," said the abbot, "he has become what he
desired."

"What is that?" asked Olioll, who had entered unheard.

"Nothing," answered the abbot, "and therefore full of God."

Then the abbot put on his white mantle, and Brother
Bittern brought the silver cross, and Brother Bald Fox, forgetting
his bitten toes, ran to awaken every sleeper. The monks
formed a procession: first the youngest with candles, then the
readers with their books, then the workers with spade, axe,
hammer and sickle, then the priests with censers, and last
the abbot bearing no crozier, for he would carry no
sign of authority before so humble a man.

They crossed the snow while morning opened pale above Tullagh.
No bell was rung, yet birds came from the hedges;
and wolves appeared between the trees, walking without fear; and
the frozen river made a low music beneath its stones.

At the millhouse they heard the quern turning.

They did not enter. The abbot knelt in the snow,
and all the brethren knelt behind him—Brother Dove who knew
many things, Olioll who had learned to know nothing, Brother
Bald Fox, Brother Bittern, the acolytes, the scribes, the cooks
and the gatekeeper. They waited to honour Aengus and to
ask him by what road a man might flee himself
and come near to God.

But within, the beggar only pushed the beam, and the
stone went round, and the flour fell softly, and he
sang for joy of the common grain.

The rain had been falling since noon, not violently, but
with that patient, incurable persistence which persuades one that the
sky has forgotten how to do anything else. It hung
in the air like a grey net; it polished the
stones, soaked the timber of the empty quays, and drew
from the black river a smell of mud, rotten rope,
fish-scales, and cold iron. The river itself hurried past the
town with a guilty, frightened look, as though fleeing before
winter, which was already sharpening its white teeth somewhere beyond
the northern fields.

I was then eighteen years old—a splendid age, as you
see!—and possessed precisely three things: a wet coat, an empty
stomach, and an immense confidence in my future usefulness to
mankind.

The last of these cost nothing and was consequently the
easiest to preserve. I had come to the town that
morning hoping to find work at the docks. By evening
I had learned that hope, like every other luxury, is
more comfortably enjoyed by those who have eaten. There was
no work. A foreman had looked at my narrow shoulders
and laughed; another advised me to grow stronger and return
in spring; a third, more humane, merely turned away. I
knew not a soul there, and had not a farthing.
The eating-houses shone warmly through their clouded windows, displaying the
backs of men who bent over bowls of soup, but
the sight of those backs only increased the emptiness within
me.

In those days I had read many books and digested
none of them. I knew that the social system demanded
re-organisation; I knew that property was theft, labour sacred, the
people heroic, and the intelligent man responsible for the happiness
of the universe. All this wisdom moved about magnificently in
my head while my belly contracted like a fist. It
is strange how rapidly philosophy loses its ceremonial robes when
confronted by a crust of bread.

Toward evening I wandered into the wharf district.

The place was deserted. Warehouses stood along the water with
blind windows and vast locked doors. Stalls from the morning
market crouched under their drenched awnings. Battered trees shivered behind
a fence, their few leaves fluttering like scraps of yellow
paper on which summer had written its last bankrupt promises.
The wind came from the river, rushed between the buildings,
whistled beneath the eaves, struck me in the chest, and
went on howling, perhaps enraged because it found so little
warmth to steal.

Everything around me seemed exhausted.

The sky wept its undryable tears. The river slapped the
pilings with a dull, angry sound. Loose boards knocked together;
chains clinked; somewhere a sheet of tin groaned and rattled.
And beneath all these noises the earth appeared to breathe
heavily—an endless, labouring sigh, such as comes from a sick
creature too weary even to complain.

I walked on because to stop was to freeze. My
boots, split at the sides, admitted the water freely, with
a democratic hospitality they had never shown my feet. Each
step made a small squelching sound. I remember thinking that
if I died there, I should at least provide the
town with an instructive example of the neglect of young
social forces. This thought consoled me for perhaps half a
minute.

Then, beside a row of market stalls, I heard a
scratching noise.

At first I supposed it to be a dog. I
stopped. The scratching ceased. I went nearer, and it began
again: scrape, scrape, scrape—hurried, cautious, and obstinate.

"Who's there?" I called.

Something moved beneath one of the stalls. A head appeared
between two supporting beams, then a shoulder. A young woman
crawled backward from the mud, rose quickly, and stared at
me.

She was perhaps a year or two older than I,
though misery often alters the arithmetic of faces. A ragged
shawl covered her hair. Her dress was torn at one
sleeve, and beneath her left eye there spread a bruise,
dark at the centre and yellow at the edges. Her
upper lip was split. Rain ran over her cheeks as
if the night, not content with its own sorrow, had
borrowed her face to weep upon.

"What are you creeping about for?" she asked.

Her voice was hoarse, but not frightened.

"I might ask you the same."

"You might. And then?"

"Then you might answer."

She looked me over. I cannot imagine that the inspection
reassured her. Thin, blue with cold, and attempting dignity in
a coat whose buttons had deserted one by one, I
was scarcely the physiognomy of authority.

"Looking for something to eat," she said. "What else would
anyone look for under a cabbage stall?"

That simple question abolished our mutual suspicion. Hunger is a
password readily understood by those admitted to its secret fraternity.

"Did you find anything?"

"Rotten leaves. A turnip end. Here."

She held out a pale fragment coated with dirt, considered
it, and threw it away.

"There's bread in that stall," she added, nodding toward a
wooden kiosk secured by a padlock. "I saw the baker
put two loaves under the counter this morning. Maybe he
took both. Maybe not."

The proprietor of the bread had doubtless a family, a
conscience, and a legally established interest in the inviolability of
his stall. I considered these facts. Then my stomach uttered
a long, humiliating complaint.

The woman grinned.

"That's settled it."

We examined the kiosk. The door was stout, the lock
stronger than either of us, but one of the lower
boards had warped away from the frame. She knelt at
once and pushed her fingers into the gap.

"Help, then! Don't stand there blessing it."

We pulled. The board creaked but held. We found a
length of iron near the gutter, thrust it into the
opening, and leaned together. The wood protested shrilly. Each time
the wind dropped, we stopped, listening; each time it rose,
we strained again, using its howl to conceal our assault
upon proprietorship.

"Harder!" she whispered.

"I am pushing."

"You push like a clerk."

"I have never been a clerk."

"You've missed your calling."

At last the nails tore loose with a noise like
pistol shots. We froze. Nothing stirred in the warehouses; no
watchman came running; only the river answered by striking the
stones below the quay. The woman dropped flat, wriggled through
the opening, and disappeared.

There was silence.

"Well?" I whispered.

"Wait."

More silence; then a rustling, a thump, and her hand
emerged holding a round loaf of black bread.

"There! A fortune!"

She crawled out, hugging it to her breast. We did
not waste time in ceremony. Under the narrow awning, with
rain dripping from its edge onto our shoulders, she broke
the loaf in half. The interior was stale and sour-smelling;
never since have I eaten anything so excellent. We tore
at it with our teeth, swallowing too quickly, hardly breathing.
For several minutes we were not a man and a
woman, not an aspiring benefactor of humanity and a bruised
thief, but two famished animals crouching in the dark over
food.

Therein lay more honest fellowship than in many banquets I
have since attended.

When the loaf was gone, the cold returned to our
notice with renewed malice. The woman rubbed her bare hands.

"We'll croak if we stay here."

"Do you know somewhere to sleep?"

"If I knew, would I be digging after rotten turnips?"

She glanced toward the river. On the strip of shore
below the warehouses lay several small boats dragged up for
winter. One had been overturned, its keel pointing upward like
the spine of a dead beast.

"Under there," she said. "It'll keep off some rain."

We climbed down the slippery embankment, stumbling over stones and
coils of discarded rope. The river roared close beside us,
black water lifting pale edges and flinging them at the
shore. Together we raised one side of the boat just
enough to crawl beneath it.

It was not a dwelling that would have attracted a
respectable tenant. The sand was wet. The interior smelt of
tar, weeds, and old fish. Through cracks in the planking
came threads of rain; whenever the wind struck broadside, the
whole shell trembled, and a loose splinter tapped above our
heads with maddening regularity. Yet compared with the open quay
it seemed almost intimate.

We lay side by side in the narrow darkness.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Natasha."

"Mine is—"

"Never mind. I'll forget it."

This was not flattering, but it proved prophetic.

For a while we listened to the storm. The wind
ran along the boat, seeking entrance; the rain pattered on
its bottom, sometimes lightly, sometimes with a sudden drumming fury;
waves hissed up the sand and withdrew, dragging pebbles after
them. Natasha coughed.

"Who struck you?" I asked.

"Who do you think? Pashka."

"Your husband?"

She laughed shortly.

"Husband! No. He plays the concertina at the Red Lantern.
Thinks himself an artist because he knows six tunes and
wears his hair over one eye."

"Why did he beat you?"

"Because he wanted to."

This answer, delivered without anger, disturbed me more than a
cry would have done.

After a moment she continued:

"He came to me yesterday half seas over. I'd put
by some money—three roubles and a bit. He knew it.
First he kissed me, then asked for it. I said
no. He said he needed new boots. I said his
old ones were good enough for kicking women. Then he
began."

She touched her swollen lip with two fingers.

"He tore my dress, smashed the little mirror, pulled my
hair out in handfuls. Look."

She lifted the shawl. Even in the darkness I could
distinguish a bare, reddened patch above her temple.

"And the money?"

"Took it. Naturally. Then today I found him with another
girl, a red-haired cow from the singing room. He told
me to clear off. She laughed. So I hit her
with a bottle."

"Did you hurt her?"

"Not enough."

Again that tired, almost businesslike tone! Violence belonged to her
days as rain belonged to that autumn night: it fell,
it soaked, it spoiled, and one spoke of it only
because speaking helped to pass the time.

"Why not go home?" I asked foolishly.

"My home is the house behind the Red Lantern."

I understood then.

"You mean—"

"I mean what I mean. The madam won't let me
in like this. Says bruised goods frighten customers, and this
dress isn't fit for the kitchen rag. Besides, I owe
her. Pashka took what I was to pay."

She shifted, trying to place her torn sleeve beneath her
body.

"So here's Natasha, grand lady of the riverbank, supping on
stolen bread and lodging under a boat. Fine life, eh?"

I murmured something about injustice. At eighteen I had a
great many words for injustice, all of them clean, polished,
and utterly useless.

Natasha spat toward a crack in the boards.

"Men!" she said. "A cursed breed. Always talking, promising, grabbing.
If you've money, they love you till it's gone. If
you haven't, they beat you for wasting their time. Pashka
used to say he'd take me away. We'd open a
tavern in the country, he said. I'd keep the room
bright, and he'd play for the guests. Fool that I
was, I saw it all—the red curtains, the brass lamp,
even a geranium in the window. A geranium! Just imagine!"

She laughed again, but her laugh broke in the middle.

"I bought him shirts. I went hungry for that pig.
Once, when he had fever, I sat three nights by
him. And today he says to the red-haired one, 'That
Natasha? She's old rubbish.' Old! I'm twenty."

She became silent. Outside, the wind beat the boat with
both hands.

"What a cursed thing life is," she said at last.
"Better to slip into the water. Two steps and done.
You go under, swallow a mouthful, kick a bit—then peace."

"You mustn't talk so."

"Why not?"

"Because life may change."

"There speaks a scholar."

"I'm not a scholar."

"You talk like one. 'Life may change.' Of course it
may. Rain may turn to vodka, and Pashka may wake
with a saint's halo."

I felt wounded. Her mockery seemed directed not merely at
my phrase but at all the noble thoughts with which
I had decorated my poverty. I wished to explain to
her that mankind was advancing, that social relations were not
eternal, that education and collective action would one day abolish
the conditions which had produced her misery. I had no
doubt she would be interested. But my teeth began to
chatter so violently that the lecture was postponed.

Natasha heard them.

"You're freezing."

"No."

"You rattle like loose crockery."

"I'm all right."

"Don't be an idiot. Come closer."

Before I could object, she moved against me and drew
part of her shawl over my shoulder. The shawl was
wet and thin; nevertheless, where her body touched mine there
spread a faint warmth.

"You're made of sticks," she muttered. "Have you eaten at
all today?"

"Some bread."

"With me, yes. Before that?"

"Yesterday."

"Lord!"

There was no admiration in this exclamation. She put one
arm around my neck and pressed my head against her
breast, as a mother might quiet a sick child.

"Lie still. We'll warm each other."

And I, who had entered the town prepared to become
a social force, lay trembling in the arms of a
prostitute whom I had met digging in mud.

Mark the arrangement well. I had theories; she had bruises.
I possessed ideals; she possessed not even an intact dress.
I belonged, by education and expectation, to that portion of
society which discusses the fallen, classifies them, regrets them in
articles, and sometimes, after dinner, proposes their redemption. Yet beneath
the boat it was I who was helpless, and she
who gave shelter. Society had taken from her money, safety,
health, reputation, and hope; still, from some hidden treasury which
our moral accountants had overlooked, she produced kindness.

The cold did not depart, but it retreated a little.
Her breath touched my forehead. She rubbed my back with
her palm.

"There, there," she said. "It'll be morning soon."

No woman had spoken to me so tenderly since childhood.
I had been alone for many months, nourishing myself on
pride because other nourishment was scarce. I had imagined loneliness
a stern school, useful to the development of character. But
at those simple words—"There, there"—something in me gave way.

I began to weep.

At first I turned my face aside, ashamed. Natasha noticed.

"What's wrong? Does something hurt?"

"No."

"Then why—?"

"I don't know."

"Ah, cry if you want. It warms the eyes."

She drew me closer. Her fingers, cold and rough, stroked
my hair.

"Poor lad," she whispered. "Poor wandering lad. No mother here,
no bed, no work. Never mind. Tomorrow we'll ask at
the flour warehouse. The carters sometimes need hands. I know
a cook there; she's ugly, but not bad. She'll give
us tea. You'll see."

I wept harder.

Perhaps my tears were caused by hunger and exhaustion; philosophers
may accept that explanation if it comforts them. But I
believe they washed out something worse than fatigue. They carried
away a little of my vanity, a little of the
dirt left by contempt, a little of that evil stupidity
which permits us to love "humanity" while shrinking from an
actual human being whose clothes offend us.

Natasha kissed my forehead.

"Hush now. You'll make yourself ill."

"And you?" I said. "What will you do?"

"Me? I'll manage."

"Come with me tomorrow. We'll both find work."

She laughed softly.

"What work will they give this face?"

"The bruise will fade."

"Others come."

"Leave Pashka."

"I've left him twenty times."

"Then leave him twenty-one."

"That may be the lucky number."

She said it to please me. I knew this even
then, though I pretended otherwise. We made plans beneath the
rattling boat: honest work, a cheap room, regular meals. I
would carry sacks; she would wash dishes or sew; we
should save money. All poverty can be defeated in conversation,
especially at night, especially by the young. Yet Natasha listened
gravely, asked what wages I expected, warned me against a
certain foreman who cheated strangers, and promised to show me
the warehouse.

Then she began to tell me of her childhood in
a village far away. There had been birch trees, a
pond, and a small blue cup which belonged to her
mother. Such objects survive strangely in ruined lives. Houses vanish,
faces decay, years become a dirty confusion, but a blue
cup remains bright in memory, standing on a shelf in
sunlight.

"My mother drank tea from it on Sundays," Natasha said.
"Only Sundays. I broke it when I was nine. Thought
she'd kill me, but she just sat down and cried.
Funny, isn't it?"

"No."

"She died that winter. Afterward my father married again. Then
I came to town. The usual."

The usual! Within that phrase she buried beatings, hunger, flight,
seduction, sale—perhaps worse. Misery, when repeated among thousands, receives the
convenient name of "usual," and decent people are thereby spared
the trouble of astonishment.

Her voice grew drowsy. From time to time she muttered
against men in general and Pashka in particular; then, without
perceiving the contradiction, she tucked the shawl more carefully around
my neck.

The wind… how it cried that night! It cried in
the cracks, in the ropes, in the hollow warehouses. The
rain… how it pursued us, tapping above our heads like
the fingers of an importunate creditor. The river… how darkly
it hurried past, carrying leaves, refuse, reflections, and perhaps the
dreams of those who stood upon its banks considering two
steps and peace.

Yet beneath the boat a little fire lived—not a fire
of wood, for we had none; not of hope, which
was feeble; but the warmth of one battered human creature
refusing to let another freeze.

Toward dawn I slept.

When Natasha shook me, the storm had weakened. A colourless
light entered through the cracks. We crawled out stiffly and
stood upon the shore.

The world looked washed but not cleansed. Fog hung over
the river. The warehouses emerged from it like enormous, misshapen
animals. Along the eastern sky lay a narrow strip of
exhausted yellow, and in that poor light Natasha's face appeared
more bruised than before. Her hair was clotted with sand;
her lips were blue; one cheek bore the imprint of
a seam in her shawl.

She smiled.

"Well, alive?"

"Thanks to you."

"Don't start making speeches."

We climbed to the quay. The town was waking. A
cart creaked over the stones; smoke began to rise from
chimneys; a baker's boy passed carrying warm rolls in a
covered basket, and the smell followed him with intolerable richness.
Natasha called out to a woman sweeping before a tavern.
They spoke together. The woman looked at me, shrugged, and
brought us two mugs of hot tea and a heel
of bread.

Natasha pushed the larger piece toward me.

"You take it."

"We'll divide it."

"You need strength for the warehouse."

"So do you."

"For what? Smashing bottles?"

We divided it.

At the crossing near the market, she stopped.

"The flour warehouse is straight on, then left by the
church. Ask for Stepan with the broken nose. Tell him
Katka the cook sent you."

"Aren't you coming?"

"Later. I must fix myself first."

"Where?"

She looked away.

"Somewhere."

I begged her to meet me at noon. She agreed.
We chose a bench beside the churchyard. Then she held
out her hand.

"You're a brick," she said. "A skinny one, but still."

It should have been I who praised her, thanked her,
blessed her! Instead I muttered some awkward nonsense and pressed
her hand. She turned, crossed the wet street, and disappeared
behind a line of carts.

I found the warehouse. I found Stepan with the broken
nose. By one of those rare accidents which encourage young
men to credit Providence with sound administrative habits, I obtained
work that very day.

At noon I went to the bench.

Natasha did not come.

I returned in the evening, and the next morning, and
on many Sundays afterward. I asked at the Red Lantern,
where they cursed me and shut the door. I searched
taverns, market lanes, lodging-houses, police yards, and the river embankment.
For six months I looked whenever work and hunger permitted.
Some said she had gone away with a musician; some
that she had been arrested; one woman thought she had
entered a hospital under another name. No one knew. In
such quarters people disappear quietly. The river, the prison, the
road, disease—each has a wide mouth and keeps no register
that friendship may consult.

I never saw Natasha again.

Years have passed. I have known persons of excellent reputation,
persons who spoke eloquently of brotherhood while ringing for a
servant to remove a beggar from the steps. I have
sat in warm rooms where men re-organised the social system
between supper and cards. I too have spoken, written, judged,
and more than once mistaken the noise of my own
opinions for the voice of truth.

But when I am tempted to measure a human being
by rank, cleanliness, grammar, or the approval of society, I
remember an overturned boat, the bitter river, and a bruised
woman wrapping a wet shawl around my shoulders.

What became of her? Perhaps she returned to Pashka; perhaps
he killed her; perhaps she escaped and lived long enough
to possess another blue cup. I know not. May she
rest in peace, whether she is dead or still wandering.

She had been robbed of almost everything, yet gave what
no prosperous moralist had offered me that night: bread, warmth,
tenderness, and hope, asking no payment and preserving no account.

And from that little fire, kindled beneath rotten planks on
a barren shore, I have warmed my conscience all my
life.

# The Day the World Went Round

The promise was made on a Tuesday, between the second
cup of tea and a discussion concerning Aunt Eliza's sleeves;
and because it was made in that careless, floating manner
in which grown-up people scatter kingdoms, voyages, and felicities about
the nursery floor, no one but Harold and myself perceived
that the axis of the earth had shifted.

"If it's fine on Saturday," said my mother, "we might
take the children to the circus."

My aunt, who was pinning a piece of lace against
her shoulder and regarding its effect with a judicial eye,
said that it would be rather amusing.

"Then we're going?" demanded Harold.

"We'll see," said my mother.

Now, to Them, "we'll see" meant nothing more binding than
a sigh, a cough, or the removal of a teaspoon
from a saucer. To us it was a sealed covenant,
witnessed by heaven, registered among the stars, and enforceable, if
necessary, by siege operations. The qualifying phrase about the weather
did not disturb us. Fine weather had been ordered. Saturday
had been commanded to arrive in blue and gold; it
would scarcely dare present itself otherwise.

From that moment the ordinary furniture of existence put on
a new complexion. The dining-room chairs became spirited bays, requiring
a firm hand at the bridle. The passage was a
ring, the umbrella-stand a cage of Nubian lions, and the
hearthrug—upon which Harold performed several daring somersaults, splitting his lip
against the fender—was the tan-bark arena where Fame waited with
a wreath in one hand and sticking-plaster in the other.

We had never seen a circus. This circumstance, far from
restricting our knowledge, enlarged it immeasurably. A person who has
seen one circus knows, after all, only that circus; whereas
Harold and I possessed every circus that had ever glittered
from Rome to Samarkand. Ours included white horses with scarlet
nostrils, elephants carrying jewelled towers, clowns of inexhaustible humour, ladies
who sprang through paper moons, tigers who ate unpopular persons,
and a master of ceremonies in boots so polished that
the sun rose twice in them. At night, when the
household had retired to the inferior business of sleep, we
held whispered consultations across the darkness.

"There'll be lions," Harold said.

"Of course."

"And a man'll put his head in one."

"He may," I replied, unwilling to guarantee a detail which
had not appeared in the promise.

"And if the lion bites it off?"

"Then another man comes in."

"What for?"

"To be the lion-tamer."

This satisfied him. Childhood requires logic, but accepts it in
convenient shapes.

I myself cared less for the lions than for the
ladies. Some rumour, distilled perhaps from an old poster or
the testimony of a stable-boy, had informed me that circus
ladies were beautiful. Beauty, at that period, was not an
attribute but a vocation. A beautiful person necessarily wore gauze,
carried a silver wand, and was waiting for me. I
foresaw our meeting with exactness. She would enter upon a
milk-white horse; our eyes would encounter; she would immediately perceive
in the small boy of eight, seated beside his younger
brother and smelling faintly of soap, a spirit formed for
perilous devotion. Thereafter our course was simple. I should rescue
her from a fire, a tyrant, or an elephant; she
would offer me half her kingdom; and Harold, being younger,
would have to content himself with the clown.

The days before Saturday moved with a deliberate cruelty peculiar
to calendars. Wednesday crawled. Thursday sat down in the road
and refused to stir. Friday extended itself like an old
sermon. Yet each brought us nearer, and expectation, though it
charges ruinous interest, remains the only bank in which children
are always willing to deposit their entire fortune.

We prepared as men prepare for battle. Harold practised roaring,
in order that he might reply suitably to the lions.
I trained myself to mount the banisters at speed and
descend without visible fear. We examined our best clothes, debated
whether circus horses respected velvet collars, and concealed two biscuits
in a wooden box against the possibility that we might
be carried away by gipsies. The box also contained three
marbles, a bent knife, a piece of sealing-wax, and the
skeleton of a mouse. Thus provisioned, we were ready for
continents.

Saturday came.

It was fine.

The morning wore that peculiarly festive expression which nature assumes
when she knows that human happiness is in contemplation. The
lawn shone; the elms stood freshly dressed; little clouds sailed
at a discreet distance from the sun; and every bird
in the shrubbery appeared to be repeating, with variations, the
words, "Circus! Circus!" Even the gravel crunched expectantly under our
shoes.

We breakfasted in a condition of heroic restraint. Harold swallowed
his porridge in four gulps and asked whether lions had
porridge. I ate nothing, being already sustained by love and
horsemanship. After breakfast we stationed ourselves near the stairs to
watch for preparations.

None occurred.

Instead of the travelling dresses which justice demanded, my mother
and aunt appeared in pale gowns unsuitable for tan-bark, lions,
or any adventure involving speed. There were flowers at their
waists. Parasols emerged. A carriage was ordered for three o'clock,
but the instructions included the name of Mrs. Fotheringay, whose
house lay in precisely the opposite direction from the circus.

Harold, less experienced than I in the darker practices of
Them, asked directly when we were going.

"Going where, dear?"

"To the circus."

"The circus?" My aunt looked as though he had proposed
a visit to the drains. "Oh, not today. We promised
Mrs. Fotheringay we would go to her garden party."

"But you promised us."

"Did we? I think we only said we might."

"It's fine," said Harold.

"Yes, beautifully fine. That's why the garden party will be
so pleasant."

There are moments when language, that old and generally serviceable
bridge between souls, breaks down under the weight laid upon
it. I knew then that the catastrophe was complete. The
very fineness of the day, which had been our certificate
and ally, was to be used in evidence against us.
The sun had betrayed its clients.

Harold did not accept the verdict. He threw himself immediately
into public life.

"You said circus! You said circus! You did! You did!"

"Don't shout."

"I'll shout if I like. You promised! We're goin' to
the circus! We're goin' to the circus!"

He marched up and down the hall, beating time upon
the walls, and converted his grievance into a chant of
considerable carrying power. At first there was argument; then persuasion;
then the darker threat that he would make himself ill;
finally the announcement that, if abandoned, he intended to yell
and yell until he burst. He approached this undertaking with
the conscientious energy of one who had found his profession.

My mother said he was naughty. My aunt said he
was over-excited. The nurse was summoned, and Harold, still proclaiming
the circus to the indifferent centuries, was borne upstairs under
protest.

I made no sound. Some misfortunes are too august for
noise.

Slipping out through the side door, I went down to
the paddock and sat behind the old water-butt, where no
one could behold the ruins of my faith. Before that
morning the world had been round, bright, and revolving; roads
curved toward scarlet tents, seas bore ships to enchanted ports,
and the horizon itself resembled the brim of some colossal
circus ring. Now everything was flat. The paddock was flat;
the sky was a painted ceiling; the road went nowhere;
adulthood waited at the end of it wearing pale gloves
and discussing refreshments.

I decided to go to sea.

This was not petulance, but policy. The sea, unlike Them,
kept its promises. If it advertised storms, pirates, sharks, and
shipwreck, it supplied them without substituting a garden party. I
would walk to the coast, enter as cabin-boy aboard a
black schooner, and rise by moral worth and swordsmanship to
be captain before I was twelve. Years later I should
return, bronzed, silent, and accompanied by a faithful Malay. They
would be having tea. My aunt would drop her cup.

"Can it be—?"

"It can," I should reply.

Harold, who had escaped from custody by methods never disclosed,
discovered me before I had reached the coast. He came
round the water-butt carrying an orange.

His face was blotched from battle, but his spirit remained
erect.

"I've got this," he said.

The orange appeared genuine. It possessed weight, colour, and a
small brown navel. Yet when he bit it, a stream
of water flew from a minute hole and struck me
accurately in the eye.

Harold laughed.

There are hours when a jest refreshes the heart; there
are others when it tramples upon sacred ashes. I seized
the orange, filled it at the butt, and squirted Harold
down the neck. He rushed at me. We rolled beneath
the gooseberry bushes, delivering blows of more indignation than science.
Harold demanded blood; I demanded justice; the bushes supplied scratches
impartially. But blood, that coveted seal upon all honourable combat,
declined to appear.

At length the orange was crushed between us. Its skin
split; its borrowed waters returned to the earth; and its
body, flattened and fragrant, lay upon the grass like a
little golden fraud whose schemes had overtaken it.

We sat up.

"You've torn my collar," I said.

"You've broke my orange."

"It wasn't a proper orange."

"It was mine."

This was unanswerable. I offered him one of the biscuits
from our expeditionary box. It had acquired a taste of
sealing-wax, but grief is not fastidious. He ate half and
gave me the rest. Peace, like war, was concluded without
adult assistance.

The carriage came at three. From behind the laurels we
watched Them depart beneath parasols, composed, fragrant, and unconscious that
they had left two lives in fragments. My mother waved.
Harold did not wave back. I did, but only because
a returning sea-captain should preserve manners.

Thereafter we tried to occupy ourselves. We hunted the cat
until she took refuge on the greenhouse roof. We dug
a pit across the kitchen-garden path, intending it for hostile
cavalry, but the gardener detected it before history could justify
us. We climbed the wall, descended into the lane, ambuscaded
a milk-cart, stalked an old gentleman whom Harold suspected of
being a spy, and attempted to smoke dried bean-stalks in
the tool-shed. Nothing answered. Each amusement, promising for a minute,
collapsed inward like a damp paper bag. Over everything hung
the unseen circus: horses were galloping somewhere, cymbals clashed somewhere,
and somewhere a beautiful lady was entering the ring and
looking in vain for me.

By five o'clock even Harold's resources were exhausted. We sat
on the gate, dusty, bruised, and full of that ancient
weariness which descends upon children when the world has proved
smaller than advertised.

It was then that the funny man came along the
road.

He was not funny in the accepted sense. He never
pulled faces, imitated animals, or asked us whether we had
swallowed sixpence. Indeed, his face was grave, his clothes dark,
and his walk deliberate. But he spoke to children as
if they were representatives of an allied power, and he
spoke to adults as though he were privately amused by
their arrangements. For these reasons we had long ago classified
him as funny.

He carried a parcel under one arm and a list
in his hand. Stopping at the gate, he looked first
at Harold, then at me, and finally at the immense
vacancy surrounding us.

"You appear prosperous," he said.

"We aren't," said Harold.

"No? I had mistaken despair for repose."

"We were going to the circus," I explained.

"And have returned already? The pace of modern entertainment is
alarming."

"We didn't go," Harold cried. "They said we would, and
then they went to a garden party."

The funny man considered this. He did not say that
we must not mind, that there would be other circuses,
or that garden parties were pleasant too. He did not
pat our heads. Above all, he did not explain Them.
His silence admitted the full dimensions of the crime.

"I see," he said at last. "You are suffering from
satiety and world-weariness."

"Yes," I replied, though uncertain which of us had which.

He examined his list. "I was to buy bootlaces, call
upon the chemist, and return a book concerning the reign
of Queen Anne. These obligations seem remarkably dull."

"They are," said Harold.

"So I begin to perceive."

He folded the list, put it in his pocket, and
looked toward the town, where, beyond three miles of dust
and hedges, the striped tents waited under the evening sun.

"Would either of you care to inspect a circus?"

For one second we did not understand him. Happiness, returning
too suddenly, often wears the mask of nonsense.

"A real circus?" Harold whispered.

"I am acquainted with no other useful kind."

"Now?"

"Unless you have an engagement."

We came off the gate like two shells discharged from
one gun.

There were difficulties, of course. Grown-up life consists largely in
manufacturing difficulties and afterward taking credit for overcoming them. We
required cleaner faces, intact collars, permission from someone, and conveyance.
The funny man disposed of these obstacles with an ease
approaching sorcery. The cook, who held no brief for garden
parties, scrubbed us and supplied bread and jam. A note
was left upon the hall table. The gardener harnessed the
old grey mare to the dogcart. Within twenty minutes we
were seated beside our deliverer, while the mare—who had previously
seemed composed chiefly of ribs, patience, and philosophical objections to
haste—sprang forward as if fire had entered her hooves.

The road unrolled. Hedges fled backward. Dust arose in golden
clouds, and the telegraph poles, those stiff officials of the
highway, bowed past us one by one. Harold shouted whenever
we overtook anything. I sat speechless, guarding my joy lest
the least careless movement spill it.

Far ahead, above the town, a flag lifted and sank.

"There!" cried Harold.

It was no larger than a red spark, yet it
drew the whole landscape after it. The road curved toward
it; the trees leaned toward it; even the sunset poured
its copper river in that direction. We crossed the bridge
at a gallop, rattled through streets loud with Saturday, and
presently entered a region where civilization had loosened its collar.

Vans stood in a field. Horses stamped and jingled. Boys
ran beside us offering programmes, oranges, and mysterious opportunities. A
painted board displayed a lady floating horizontally above a stallion,
a clown divided into several impossible attitudes, and a lion
whose appetite appeared political. From the great tent came music,
muffled but urgent, and beneath the music sounded the deep
collective murmur of a crowd awaiting marvels.

Then the smell reached me: trampled grass, warm canvas, horse,
sawdust, lamp-smoke, orange-peel, and something indefinable—wild, tawny, foreign—which I immediately
attributed to the lions. No perfume since has possessed that
authority. It opened the gates of the fabulous.

The funny man bought tickets. He performed this act without
bargaining, hesitation, or reference to our moral conduct. We passed
beneath the striped awning.

Inside, the world had become circular.

Rows of faces climbed upward into shadow; lamps burned along
ropes and poles; brass instruments flashed; vendors cried their treasures;
and in the centre, sacred and enormous, lay the ring.
It was smaller than the empires I had imagined, yet
it contained them all. Harold clutched my sleeve. The band
drew breath.

With a crack of the whip, existence began again.

Horses swept in—black, white, chestnut, piebald—flowing round the ring in
a storm of manes, bells, coloured reins, and lifted knees.
Men in scarlet coats stood upon them; ladies passed from
saddle to saddle; banners streamed; the ringmaster's boots shot back
the lamps in two blue-white flames. Everything moved round and
round, yet seemed perpetually to arrive. The globe, restored to
its proper shape, spun beneath us.

Then Coralie appeared.

I learned her name from the ringmaster, who proclaimed it
as trumpets might proclaim the entrance of spring. She came
through the curtain upon a white horse, and the lamps,
recognising their sovereign, gathered upon her. She wore rose-coloured gauze,
silver stars, and a small bright crown; her hair floated
behind her; one foot rested lightly upon the broad saddle,
while the other appeared to have no earthly business at
all.

I loved her.

The fact was instantaneous and complete. It required no conversation,
no introduction, no inquiry into character. Coralie stood upon the
cantering horse; therefore Coralie was perfection. She passed us once,
twice, thrice, smiling over the audience with an impartial radiance
which I nevertheless received as private correspondence.

On the fourth circuit she leaped through a hoop.

My heart leaped with her and failed to return.

I began planning. The life of a circus equestrienne was
no doubt beset by dangers—jealous princes, broken bridges, runaway horses,
and managers with black moustaches. In each crisis I should
appear. Though presently only eight, I expected growth to occur
rapidly under the influence of devotion. At sixteen I would
be tall, armed, and gloomy. Coralie, recognising me as the
boy whose gaze had sustained her during that difficult hoop,
would place her hand in mine.

Harold pulled my sleeve.

"She's mine," he said.

I turned upon him. "She can't be."

"Why?"

"Because I'm older."

This fundamental law, which had settled the ownership of chairs,
apples, and military commands, could scarcely fail in romance. Harold
frowned but did not contest it. He watched Coralie with
a younger brother's hopeless admiration.

When she vanished, taking beauty with her, the ring seemed
briefly bereaved. But scarcely had I settled into the noble
ache of fidelity when a man in clothes of stupendous
misfortune ran out and fell over his own feet.

He rose with dignity, tripped over a bucket, sat in
it, blamed the ringmaster, and was struck by a broom.

This was the clown.

His face was white, his mouth a red catastrophe, and
his eyebrows occupied unequal levels. His coat had been designed
for a giant, his trousers for another and more expansive
giant, while his hat seemed the property of a very
small bishop. Yet from this panoply of ruin he exercised
absolute power. Men who would have ignored us, horses, weather,
and possibly the police, surrendered to him at once. Each
solemn attempt he made ended in fresh disaster; each disaster
increased his dignity; and every injury done him returned upon
his persecutors enlarged and decorated.

When the ringmaster commanded him to leave, he produced a
chair.

When the chair was removed, he produced a pig.

When the pig was captured, he discovered a trumpet in
the ringmaster's coat and blew a note so appalling that
one of the horses appeared to laugh.

The tent shook.

Here was a vocation! Coralie required years of balance, gauze,
and horse-training; the clown conquered by falling down. I had
been falling down with considerable natural ability since infancy and
had received neither applause nor salary. The injustice of my
circumstances struck me forcibly.

Romantic love yielded to ambition. I would become a clown.

My programme began next morning. I should refuse breakfast in
ordinary clothes and demand trousers of a breadth worthy of
my profession. Harold would strike me with a broom. I
would emerge from the coal-cellar carrying the cat, three plates,
and a lighted squib. By noon my genius must be
evident. Thereafter the circus would send a deputation. I saw
myself travelling through Europe, Asia, and the Americas, beloved by
emperors, pursued by policemen, and everywhere falling from chairs before
distinguished audiences.

The funny man glanced down at me.

"A serious career attracts you?" he asked.

"Yes," I said.

"I thought as much."

He offered no discouragement. That was his genius.

The clown departed at last in a small cart drawn
by a large dog, bowing to us with the melancholy
of an exiled monarch. I watched him go with professional
scrutiny, already his rival.

There followed acrobats, dogs, a lady who hung by her
teeth, two Japanese gentlemen who treated gravity as a provincial
regulation, and a family of elephants. Harold devoted himself to
the elephants. He laughed when the smallest stole the ringmaster's
handkerchief, and afterward declared that he would keep one in
the shed. The funny man agreed that the shed might
require enlargement.

Then the lamps were lowered.

A hush moved over the crowd—not silence exactly, but the
drawing inward of a thousand breaths. The band began a
tune unlike its earlier marches: slow at first, then mounting,
fevered, full of deserts and pursuit. A single blue light
opened upon the entrance.

Zephyrine rode out.

If Coralie had been dawn, Zephyrine was midnight with a
sword in its hand. Her horse was black; she wore
black and gold; a scarlet scarf streamed from her shoulder
like a wound in the darkness. She rode without saddle,
standing erect while the stallion raced. The ringmaster's whip cracked.
The music surged. Zephyrine leaned, wheeled, vanished against the horse's
side, and rose again with both arms flung wide.

The audience cried out.

So did something within me.

Coralie had been beautiful; Zephyrine was destiny.

I did not reason about the transfer of allegiance. Children,
like nations, conduct their revolutions first and write the justifications
afterward. Coralie belonged to the mild province of hoops, roses,
and afternoon sunshine. Zephyrine came from a sterner geography. She
had crossed deserts. She had escaped from pashas. She slept
with a dagger beneath her pillow and spoke, when compelled,
in a low voice about vengeance.

On the second circuit she appeared to look directly at
me.

This glance was not the smiling benevolence Coralie had distributed
among the public. It was a summons. Zephyrine knew that
beyond the respectable boy in the velvet collar existed a
horseman of the wastes, temporarily delayed by education and bedtime.
She recognised me.

At once my clownish career became impossible. One may fall
into buckets for art; one cannot do so before Zephyrine.

I would instead become an explorer. I should meet her
in the Sahara, where she had been captured by a
robber chief. There would be a moon, camels, and a
fortress of red stone. Entering disguised as an astrologer, I
would overthrow the guards, release Zephyrine, and ride beside her
through a sandstorm. Harold might come too, provided he rode
behind and did not talk excessively.

Round and round she flew. Her scarf burned in the
air; her hair loosened; the black stallion seemed not to
touch the earth but to hammer sparks from some invisible
road above it. Faster went the music, faster the horse,
faster the whole tent, until faces, lamps, poles, brass, shadow,
and scarlet flame whirled together. Then, at the very height
of speed, Zephyrine sprang through a paper circle and vanished.

The paper fragments drifted down.

I sat stunned amid thunderous applause. Beauty had entered, shattered
the moon, and gone away.

More things happened afterward. There was a procession; flags passed;
the clown returned with his hat on fire; all the
horses came round once more; the band played until the
roof trembled. I saw everything, yet as one sees weather
through a dream. Zephyrine had taken possession of the central
country of my mind. Coralie lingered at its border, injured
but still lovely. The clown occupied a province of his
own and continued to fall down there.

When the performance ended, we went out beneath the evening
sky. For a moment the open world appeared oddly square
and motionless. People moved toward gates; lanterns swung; horses called
from behind the vans. Somewhere a lion gave a low,
coughing roar, and Harold stopped dead.

"That was him," he whispered.

It was enough. One lion, at least, had kept the
appointment.

The funny man led us back to the dogcart. He
did not ask whether we had enjoyed ourselves. Such a
question would have been like asking a survivor whether the
sea was damp. He tucked the rug round our knees,
climbed up, and turned the mare homeward.

Night had come. The town lights slid behind us, and
the road ran pale beneath the moon. Harold talked for
the first mile without punctuation.

"The elephant took it and the clown fell and did
you see the dog and I'm going to have an
elephant and it can sleep by the greenhouse and when
the lion roared—"

Then his voice weakened. Presently his head struck my shoulder,
rebounded, and settled there. He slept.

I remained awake, though not in the dogcart. I rode
elsewhere. The hedges became dark crowds; the moon was a
circus lamp; the rhythmic beat of the mare's hooves carried
the band's tune onward through the night. Sawdust, orange-peel, horse,
lamp-smoke—the sacred perfume clung to my clothes. Whenever I closed
my eyes, scarlet flashed.

We reached home late. The garden party had also ended,
leaving behind several adult headaches and a quantity of crushed
muslin. My mother came into the hall, surprised, relieved, and
disposed to ask questions. The funny man handed her the
note she had not yet observed and said simply that
we had been in urgent need of fresh air.

Harold woke enough to declare, "There was a lion," and
had to be carried upstairs.

No one reproached us. Perhaps the funny man's gravity discouraged
inquiry; perhaps guilt, that slow and reluctant domestic animal, had
at last entered the house. In any case, They understood—dimly,
belatedly—that the promise had somehow been fulfilled without them.

In bed, I lay with my eyes open. The room
was dark, but the circus continued.

Horses circled the bedposts. Cymbals shivered in the washstand. The
clown stepped from the wardrobe, sat in the water-jug, and
saluted me. Coralie floated past in rose-coloured gauze, beautiful and
wounded, asking by her silence how I could have forgotten
her so soon.

I had no answer.

Then came the desert. The ceiling lifted into a sky
crowded with enormous stars; the blankets rose and fell as
dunes; beyond them waited the black stallion. Zephyrine held the
bridle. Her scarlet scarf streamed upon the night wind.

I mounted behind her—or perhaps before her; dreams are generous
concerning horsemanship—and together we rode southward, where cities of brass
slept beneath the sand and robber princes guarded towers of
ivory. Harold followed upon an elephant, shouting advice. The funny
man, dressed inexplicably as a caliph, stood at the gate
and bowed us through.

Far behind, on the last white edge of the world,
Coralie watched from her patient horse.

She looked reproachful.

I promised to return.

And being still a child, and not yet one of
Them, I meant it.

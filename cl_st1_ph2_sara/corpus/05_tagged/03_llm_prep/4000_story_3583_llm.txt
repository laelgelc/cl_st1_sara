The rain had begun before dusk and continued with such
patient malice that by ten o'clock the inn yard had
disappeared beneath a sheet of black, trembling water. My carriage
stood under a lean-to; one wheel was cracked, the coachman
was drunk, and I myself had been seized by chills
after crossing the flooded meadows. There was nothing to do
but send for the district doctor.

He came near midnight.

Trifon Ivanich was a short, broad-shouldered man of perhaps forty-five,
with reddish whiskers, a nose permanently inflamed by snuff, and
small grey eyes which appeared timid until he smiled. His
coat was wet through at the shoulders. He carried no
umbrella, only a battered leather case, and on entering he
first apologised for the mud, then for the lateness, and
finally for his name.

"My father called me Trifon," he said, pulling off his
boots by the stove. "What can one do? A doctor
named Trifon! It inspires confidence in peasants, perhaps, but not
in cultivated people."

He examined me carefully, tapped my chest, felt my pulse,
and assured me that I had nothing worse than a
fever brought on by damp and fatigue. He prescribed hot
tea, rum, and rest. When I asked what I owed
him, he named so modest a sum that I doubled
it. He hesitated, coughed, and took the money with an
embarrassed bow.

"You are generous," he said. "A doctor in our district
must live on generosity, you know. Fees—well, fees are like
swallows: one sees them in summer."

Since neither of us could sleep—the storm rattled the shutters,
and the inn servant had brought a samovar fit to
boil a regiment's linen—I invited him to stay. Rum loosened
his reserve. We spoke first of the roads, then of
the ignorance of country landowners, then of medicine, which Trifon
Ivanich declared to be "a science in the capitals and
a guessing game among us." At length, while crumbling bread
between his thick fingers, he fell silent.

"You asked," he said, "whether a doctor becomes accustomed to
death. Accustomed? Yes and no. One grows accustomed to certificates,
funerals, mothers screaming, priests arriving too late, and all that.
But sometimes—how should I say?—one death remains. Not because it
was remarkable medically. Quite the opposite. Because one was a
fool."

He glanced toward the inn servant asleep by the stove,
lowered his voice, and began.

"I was not yet thirty then. I had been in
the district scarcely two years and was thin—would you believe
it?—thin as a nail. I wore spectacles, though I did
not need them, because without spectacles no one trusted me.
My practice brought me bread and butter, hardly more. The
peasants paid in eggs; the merchants summoned me only after
trying herbs, prayers, cupping, and a veterinary surgeon; the landowners
sent for doctors from the town. So when, one winter
evening, a servant arrived with a note from a lady
named Anna Semyonovna Lugina, asking me to come at once
to her daughter, I was delighted. Yes, delighted! There is
honesty for you. A young woman lay dangerously ill, and
I thought first: a decent house, educated people, perhaps five
rubles.

"The servant had two shaggy horses in a sledge. Snow
was falling, but he swore the road was passable. Passable!
We had gone seven versts when one runner split; then
we reached the broken dyke, where the water had risen
under the ice, and the horses sank to their bellies.
I got out, naturally, and lost one boot in the
mud. It was frost above and water below—devilish country! We
dragged the sledge by hand, crossed a field where there
was no road at all, and arrived after sunrise.

"The house surprised me. From the note I had imagined
columns, carpets, liveried servants. Instead I found a low thatched
house with green shutters, one chimney smoking, and hens under
the porch. Everything was clean, but poor—poor in that quiet
way which educated people conceal by polishing old furniture and
placing books over cracks in the wall.

"Anna Semyonovna met me in a faded black dress. She
had kind, frightened eyes and kept saying, 'Doctor, save her.
You must save her.' Behind her stood two younger daughters,
both pale, and an old aunt holding a holy image.
They looked at me as though I had brought life
in my case along with the mustard-plasters.

"The patient was Aleksandra Andreyevna. Twenty years old.

"You know, I have seen beautiful women. A doctor sees
them without ribbons, without smiles, sometimes without hair; beauty soon
becomes anatomy to us. But she—no, wait, I am describing
badly. She was not beautiful in the regular fashion. Her
face was too narrow, her mouth perhaps a little large.
Yet there was something—intelligence, ardour, I don't know. Her black
hair had been cut because of the fever and lay
unevenly about her temples. Her eyes were large and dark,
and when she looked at you it seemed she had
understood something before you had spoken.

"She had been ill six days. High fever, headache, weakness,
then delirium. Their local feldsher had bled her. Of course!
Whenever he did not understand an illness, he removed blood
until the illness or the patient gave way. I examined
her and believed it was an ordinary fever, severe but
not hopeless. I gave instructions, ordered cold compresses, changed the
medicine, forbade them to heat the room like a bakery,
and told the mother that in a week we should
see improvement.

"'Then she will live?' Anna Semyonovna asked.

"What could I answer? 'Certainly,' I said.

"That word certainly! We doctors scatter it as priests scatter
holy water.

"I intended to leave that afternoon, but the snow became
a blizzard. The servant said the dyke was impossible. They
gave me the aunt's room and fed me cabbage soup.
I stayed the night.

"The next morning Aleksandra Andreyevna was worse.

"I changed the treatment. That is what one does when
one is young: if the illness pays no attention to
medicine, one punishes the medicine. I sat by her bed
counting the pulse, listened to her breathing, questioned her. She
answered sensibly and even smiled.

"'Are you the district doctor?'

"'Yes.'

"'You look younger than I expected.'

"'That is my misfortune.'

"'And are you clever?'

"Imagine asking a doctor that! I said, 'Not especially.'

"'Then you are honest,' she whispered. 'That may be better.'

"She closed her eyes. After a moment she asked, 'Will
I die?'

"'What nonsense!'

"'You said that too quickly.'

"I began talking of symptoms, crises, constitutions—words, words. She listened
and thanked me. Her politeness made me uneasy.

"By evening the storm had stopped, but I did not
go. I told myself the roads were bad, that the
case interested me, that they had no other doctor. All
true. Still, I could have gone.

"Days passed. In the morning she seemed better; by night
the fever returned. I slept in my clothes on a
sofa outside her room. Whenever the floor creaked I started
up. I wrote to a colleague in the town, but
the messenger returned because the bridge was down. I tried
quinine, then abandoned it; applied mustard-plasters; argued with the aunt,
who wanted to place hot bricks at the patient's feet;
drank endless tea with the mother. And always they trusted
me.

"That was the worst of it. If they had reproached
me, I should have defended myself and remained a doctor.
But Anna Semyonovna would take my hand and say, 'God
sent you to us.' The sisters walked softly when I
slept. They washed my shirts because I had brought only
one change of linen. Think of it! I was killing
their darling through ignorance—yes, perhaps not killing her, but failing
to save her—and they washed my shirts.

"Do not misunderstand me. I had studied. I knew my
work as well as another. But when you are alone
in a distant house and the illness goes its own
way, your science shrinks to the size of the spoon
with which you give the medicine. You listen to the
chest again; you count again; you say, 'Let us wait.'
Wait for what? For recovery or death. The body knows;
the doctor waits.

"Aleksandra Andreyevna had been educated at an institute. She spoke
French, read German, played the piano, though the piano had
been sold. Their father, an army officer, had died leaving
debts. She knew they were poor. She knew also—this came
out little by little—that in that district there was no
future for her. Two neighbouring landowners had proposed: one was
sixty, the other drank. She refused both. She said she
would teach, but her mother wept whenever she mentioned leaving.

"During the first week we spoke only of her illness.
During the second—well, she began asking about me.

"'Have you always wished to be a doctor?'

"'No. As a child I wished to keep pigeons.'

"'Why did you not?'

"'Pigeons do not pay fees.'

"She laughed, and the laugh brought on coughing. I cursed
myself, but she waved me away.

"'Are you married?'

"'No.'

"'Have you been in love?'

"I said I had no time. That was foolish; everyone
has time for unhappiness.

"She looked at me closely. 'No one has loved you?'

"'My mother, I hope.'

"'That is different.'

"Then her mother entered, and we were silent.

"Why did I remain? The roads were open by then.
A peasant came twice to fetch me for a sick
child, and I sent medicine with instructions. I told myself
Aleksandra's condition was too grave to leave. Perhaps it was.
Perhaps I also wanted to hear her ask whether anyone
had loved me.

"You see what sort of man I was. I had
never been admired. At the university I was poor, badly
dressed, red-haired, and called Trifon. Women danced with officers, students
with fine voices, men who knew how to enter a
drawing room. I entered rooms as if apologising for using
the door. And here was an intelligent, beautiful woman watching
for my step.

"One night the fever rose terribly. She did not recognise
her mother. She spoke of a garden, though there was
no garden; begged someone not to shut a gate; recited
verses; then suddenly seized my wrist and said clearly:

"'Do not leave me.'

"'I am here.'

"'Not as a doctor.'

"I thought she was delirious.

"'Rest,' I said.

"'You always say that. Rest, drink, turn over, breathe. You
hide behind your profession.'

"Her hand was burning. I tried to free mine, but
she held it with surprising strength.

"'Why are you frightened?'

"'I am not frightened.'

"'You are. You are more frightened than I am.'

"She was right. My heart sank into my boots.

"'Aleksandra Andreyevna, you must not excite yourself.'

"'Must not! Must not! If I am dying, why must
I obey you?'

"'You are not dying.'

"She smiled in a way I shall never forget—tenderly, almost
pityingly.

"'Poor Trifon Ivanich. You cannot lie at all.'

"She turned her face to the wall. I sat there
until dawn, unable to move.

"The following day she was quieter. She apologised for having
spoken wildly. I assured her she had said nothing. She
watched me.

"'Nothing?'

"'Nothing of consequence.'

"'Then I shall say it when I am in my
right mind.'

"I interrupted her. I began describing the weather. Imagine! Frost
on the windows, snow on the roof—anything. She let me
speak, but her eyes did not leave my face.

"That evening Anna Semyonovna asked me into the parlour. There
was an account book open on the table and three
silver spoons beside it. She said, blushing, that she feared
they could not pay me properly at present, but she
would sell the spoons or send money later.

"I was offended. Not at her—at myself, because I remembered
my first thought about the five rubles.

"'Madam,' I said, 'if you mention payment again, I shall
leave.'

"She kissed my hand. By Jove, she kissed it! I
ran out like a schoolboy and stood in the passage
trying not to cry.

"When I returned to the sickroom, Aleksandra asked what had
happened.

"'Nothing.'

"'My mother spoke of money.'

"'How do you know?'

"'Because she has been counting spoons all day.'

"'There will be no fee.'

"'You are kind.'

"'No, no. It is merely—professional courtesy.'

"'You are very kind,' she repeated.

"And at that moment I understood I loved her.

"Or believed I did. No, I loved her. Why make
myself smaller now? I loved her walk, though I had
never seen her walk; her voice when it was strong
and when it sank to a whisper; the books beside
her bed; the little frown with which she swallowed medicine;
even the illness—I hated it, yet it enclosed us together.
Outside that room were snow, debts, patients, my shabby lodgings.
Inside were her eyes and the sound of her breathing.

"But along with love came shame. She was ill, dependent,
frightened. I was the doctor; every word of mine carried
authority. If she reached for my hand, was it me
she wanted or life? If she smiled, was it love
or gratitude? I understood all this and still sat closer.

"Three days later she improved so suddenly that the whole
house rejoiced. Her temperature fell. She asked for broth. The
younger sisters laughed in the kitchen, and Anna Semyonovna ordered
the samovar polished. I dared to think she would recover.

"I went outside for the first time in many days.
The sky was blue, and icicles shone from the thatch.
I walked to the gate imagining absurd things: I would
ask for her hand after she recovered; we would live
in town; she would read while I visited patients; perhaps
I would become a proper physician and not a mud-covered
district beast. I even calculated my income. Love, you know,
does not prevent arithmetic.

"When I returned, she was worse.

"The fever came back with greater force. Her pulse raced,
then weakened. She could not swallow. By midnight I knew—yes,
knew—that there was little hope.

"I sat beside her. The lamp smoked. Her mother had
fallen asleep in a chair, and the aunt muttered prayers
behind the screen. Aleksandra opened her eyes.

"'Trifon Ivanich.'

"'Yes.'

"'Send them away.'

"'You need quiet.'

"'No. I need you.'

"I woke her mother and said the patient must sleep.
They went reluctantly. When the door closed, Aleksandra held out
her hand.

"'Now tell me the truth.'

"'About what?'

"'Cruel man! About death.'

"I could not answer.

"'I shall die soon?'

"'There is danger, but—'

"'Soon?'

"'Perhaps not.'

"'Tonight?'

"'No, no!'

"She breathed with difficulty, considering.

"'Then there is time.'

"'Time for what?'

"'To live.'

"She pulled me toward her. I bent down because her
voice had become almost inaudible.

"'I have not lived,' she said. 'I have read books,
refused fools, mended dresses, comforted Mama. Is that life? No
one has loved me. No one I loved has ever
known. And now I shall go into the earth as
if I had never—never—'

"'Do not speak.'

"'I love you.'

"I stood up. My chair fell behind me.

"'You are feverish.'

"'I am perfectly conscious.'

"'You must rest.'

"'Do you love me?'

"I said nothing.

"'Do you?'

"What was I to do? Lie? She was dying. Speak
truth? Perhaps that was a kind of theft. I walked
to the window, though it was dark outside. My whole
body trembled.

"'Look at me,' she said.

"I turned.

"'Do you love me?'

"'Yes.'

"She gave a little sigh—not joy exactly, but relief.

"'Come here.'

"I knelt beside the bed. She placed both hands on
my head, awkwardly, as if blessing a child.

"'Then I have lived,' she whispered.

"I kissed her hand. Then her forehead. She drew my
face closer and kissed me on the lips.

"You may despise me. I despised myself and was happy—happy!
That is the horror. The mother prayed in the next
room; death stood at the foot of the bed; and
I was happy.

"After a while she said, 'We are pledged now.'

"'Yes.'

"'If I recover, you will marry me?'

"'I will.'

"'You do not mind that I am poor?'

"'Hush.'

"'And Mama will live with us. My sisters too, until
they marry. We shall have a house with large windows.
You will not go out at night in winter unless
it is necessary. I shall keep your accounts, because you
would never ask people to pay.'

"She smiled, and I promised everything. Large windows, her mother,
her sisters, even that I would cease taking snuff. What
are promises beside a deathbed? Yet I meant them. Every
one.

"From that night she called me her betrothed.

"At first only when we were alone. She would wake
and whisper, 'Are you there, my dear?' If I touched
her pulse, she turned her hand and pressed my fingers.
Once, in delirium, she asked what dress she should wear
for the wedding.

"Then she ceased concealing it.

"Her mother entered while I was giving medicine, and Aleksandra
said, quite clearly, 'Mama, Trifon Ivanich and I are to
be married.'

"Anna Semyonovna stopped at the door.

"I nearly dropped the glass.

"'Sasha, darling, you must not talk.'

"'It is true. Ask him.'

"The poor woman looked at me. Suspicion, hope, fear—everything passed
over her face. I should have explained. I should have
said the patient was delirious. Instead I bowed.

"'If Aleksandra Andreyevna recovers,' I said, 'I shall consider myself
the happiest of men.'

"Anna Semyonovna began to weep. She kissed her daughter, then
me. Aleksandra watched us triumphantly.

"Later, when we were alone, I reproached her.

"'Why did you tell her?'

"'Are you ashamed?'

"'No, but excitement is dangerous.'

"'Always the doctor!'

"'And you are ill.'

"'Then indulge me.'

"She turned away, wounded. I begged forgiveness. In those days
she could command anything from me with a movement of
her eyelids.

"Yet I knew—do you understand?—I knew her love was bound
to the illness. I had come from the world outside;
I offered hope, rescue, movement. I was the last man
she saw before death closed the door. Had the priest
been younger, perhaps she would have loved the priest. Cruel
thought! Cruel, but it came. Then I would tell myself
no, she had understood me, chosen me, seen through my
ugliness and ridiculous name. Then again: fever, fear, gratitude. I
went round and round until I no longer knew whether
I was noble, contemptible, beloved, or merely useful.

"Her strength declined day after day. Worse and worse. She
suffered less toward the end, which frightened me more than
pain would have done. Her face became transparent; her hands
were light as paper. She spoke rarely, but her mind
remained clear.

"On the last evening she asked everyone to leave us.

"Snow was falling again. I remember the flakes tapping the
window, though snow cannot tap—perhaps it was a branch. The
lamp stood before the holy image. She motioned for me
to sit near.

"'I shall die tonight,' she said.

"'No.'

"'Do not waste our time.'

"I lowered my head.

"'You are sorry you promised to marry me?'

"'Never.'

"'You will marry someone else.'

"'No.'

"She smiled faintly. 'You must. You are not made to
be alone.'

"'Sasha—'

"It was the first time I called her that. Her
eyes filled with tears.

"'Say it again.'

"'Sasha.'

"'My dear, forgive me.'

"'For what?'

"'I forced you to love me.'

"'You did not.'

"'I used death. It made me bold. If I had
been well, I might have passed you in a room
and only thought: there is the district doctor. But here—'
She looked around at the narrow bed, the bottles, the
shaded lamp. 'Here you became the whole world.'

"I tried to protest, but she put a finger to
my lips.

"'Perhaps it was not true love.'

"'It was true.'

"'Perhaps. I don't understand. I don't understand anything. But remember
it as true.'

"'I shall.'

"'And forgive me.'

"'There is nothing to forgive.'

"She pulled a thin gold ring from her finger. It
had belonged to her father and was too large for
her.

"'Take it.'

"I refused. She became agitated, so I took it.

"'Keep it always.'

"'Always.'

"'Even when you marry.'

"I promised.

"She closed her eyes. After a few minutes she whispered,
'Do not let Mama think I was afraid.'

"Then she asked me to call them.

"Her mother came, the sisters, the aunt, and the priest
who had been waiting in the parlour. I stood at
the foot of the bed while they prayed. Once she
looked at me and smiled, barely. Before dawn she died.

"Well. That is all."

Trifon Ivanich stopped. He reached for the snuffbox, found it
empty, and scraped its corners with one finger. The storm
outside had weakened; water dripped steadily from the eaves.

"And the ring?" I asked.

He frowned as if he had not heard.

"The ring? Ah. I kept it for a long time.
Then—well, things happen. I was in debt. No, no, I
did not sell it. What do you take me for?
My wife found it."

"You married, then?"

"Of course. Five years later. A merchant's daughter. Her father
owned two flour mills and gave forty thousand rubles. A
practical arrangement. She is healthy, very capable, and has a
temper like boiling tar. We have three children."

"And the ring?"

"My wife asked whose it was. I told her it
had belonged to a patient. She threw it into the
stove."

He said this lightly, but his fingers continued searching the
empty snuffbox.

"Perhaps it was better so," he added. "One cannot keep
every relic. The drawers would become crowded."

For a while neither of us spoke. Then the doctor
yawned loudly, rubbed his eyes, and glanced at the clock.

"By Jove, nearly four! You ought to sleep. Excitement is
bad for fever."

He rose, walked twice around the room, and suddenly noticed
a greasy pack of cards on the shelf.

"Do you play preference?"

"A little."

"One small game, then. It settles the nerves."

We played until the rain stopped. Trifon Ivanich recovered his
spirits, disputed every trick, accused me of concealing an ace,
and laughed when the inn servant fell off his bench.
At dawn he counted his winnings with great care.

"Two rubles and fifty kopecks," he said, pleased. "That is
more than my fee."

He buttoned his damp coat, prescribed tea once more, and
went out into the pale, muddy morning. Through the window
I saw him cross the yard, stepping around the puddles
with surprising lightness, the coins jingling in his pocket.

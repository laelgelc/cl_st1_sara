### THE PEACH-TREES OF TCHING-TOU

> "The peach-tree opens its crimson eyes above the forgotten
tomb;
> The spring wind asks the dead their names, but
none make answer."

Thus singeth the old poet Lou-Tchang; and the verse, though
commonly repeated by young maidens at the Festival of Lanterns,
hath a significance which few suspect. For what are blossoms
but beautiful promises made by the earth to those whom
the earth must presently receive again? And what is love
but a spring wind passing over graves?

The tale I am about to relate may be found,
though confusedly written, in the seventh book of the *Records
of the Western Province*. It hath also been told by
wandering reciters in the tea-houses of Ching-Kiang; yet they, desiring
rather to astonish the ignorant than to instruct the wise,
have changed many things, and have represented as a demon
one whose only crime was that she remembered too long.
I shall set the matter down as it was reported
by the honorable persons concerned therein, leaving the reader to
determine whether the dead may love, and whether the living,
having once been beloved by the dead, can ever again
belong wholly to life.

In the latter years of the Ming dynasty there dwelt
in the province of Sse-tchouan a young scholar named Ming-Y.
His family was neither rich nor illustrious; but for three
generations its men had cultivated letters, and the fragrance of
such cultivation, though it bring no silver into the money-chest,
clings to a house as the fragrance of sandalwood clings
to an empty casket.

Ming-Y's father, Ming-Lien, had once hoped to obtain rank through
the examinations. Twice he failed because his essays were too
subtle for the examiners, and once because a malicious clerk
spoiled his name upon the register. Thereafter he accepted poverty
with dignity, teaching the children of merchants, copying legal petitions,
and spending his leisure among histories which the worms had
already begun to read before him.

The son possessed all the talents which had profited the
father so little. Before his fifteenth year he could recite
the Five Classics; at eighteen he composed verses which were
repeated by the scholars of his district; and at twenty-one
his calligraphy was compared to young bamboo bending under rain.
He was gentle of speech, pale of countenance, and somewhat
taller than other youths. His eyes, always seeming to contemplate
something remote, caused old women to say that he had
been born beneath a wandering star.

Yet Ming-Lien often admonished him.

"My son," he would say, "thy nature is like pure
silk: it receiveth every color. Guard it, therefore, from stains.
Wine, gaming, and the painted faces of women have destroyed
men wiser than thou. A sword may be avoided because
it glittereth; beauty woundeth before it is perceived."

To this Ming-Y bowed, replying:

"Your unworthy son will preserve the instruction within his heart."

But instructions preserved only within the heart are sometimes like
decrees locked in a magistrate's box: they remain inviolate because
no one obeyeth them.

Not far from Tching-tou stood the country residence of the
Imperial Commissioner Tchang, a nobleman related through his mother to
a princely house. The Commissioner, having heard of Ming-Y's learning,
engaged him as tutor to his two sons and to
a nephew whose intelligence was believed to be concealed beneath
unusual thickness of skull. The residence lay among wooded hills,
at a distance of some leagues from the dwelling of
Ming-Lien; and because the road was difficult during the rains,
the young tutor commonly lodged in an eastern pavilion of
his patron's estate.

The Commissioner treated him with distinction. A servant carried his
books; he ate at the table of the household scholars;
and on moonlit evenings he was occasionally summoned to discuss
the ancient histories. Thus passed more than a year.

When the second month came round, and the Feast of
Pure Brightness drew near, Ming-Y received permission to visit his
parents. The morning of his departure was of incomparable loveliness.
Rain had fallen during the night; every stone shone; every
leaf seemed newly lacquered; and over the valleys floated thin
vapors of pearl and violet, through which the roofs of
distant villages appeared and vanished like islands in an enchanted
sea.

Ming-Y rode a small gray mule, attended by a servant.
The road wound beneath cedars, crossed a stream by a
bridge of red sandstone, and entered a region of peach-orchards
just awakening into bloom. Here the air was pink with
innumerable petals. Blossoms opened their eyes upon every branch; the
wet grass sparkled; yellow butterflies, deceived by the warmth, fluttered
unsteadily from shadow to shadow.

As the travelers approached a narrow path leading into the
woods, Ming-Y heard laughter—not the loud laughter of peasant girls
gathering herbs, but a sound low and silver-sweet, like the
trembling of jade pendants beneath a silken robe. He turned
his head.

Among the peach-trees stood a young woman.

She wore a robe of pale green figured with cloud-forms,
over which fell a gauze mantle of the faintest purple.
Her hair, blacker than the wet bark of the trees,
was fastened with a single pin of yellow jade; one
hand rested upon a flowering bough, while in the other
she held a little basket filled with fallen petals. Two
maidservants attended her, but in that first moment Ming-Y perceived
them scarcely more than one perceiveth the dim stars beside
the moon.

The woman also looked at him. Her face was oval
and delicately pale; her brows were long as the wings
of the kingfisher; and her eyes, though modestly lowered almost
at once, had in them a depth which troubled the
young scholar's breath. A blossom, shaken by the wind, fell
upon her hair. She smiled, but whether at the flower
or at Ming-Y he could not tell.

Then the path curved. Branches intervened. The women disappeared.

Ming-Y checked his mule so suddenly that the servant nearly
struck against him.

"Didst thou observe those persons?" he asked.

The servant looked toward the orchard.

"I saw only peach-trees, young master."

"Thou art blind."

"Possibly," answered the fellow; "yet a blind servant may still
perceive that the road is muddy and that the honored
mule desireth to proceed."

Ming-Y rode onward, but the image of the woman accompanied
him. During the festival he burned incense at the ancestral
graves, received his mother's embraces, listened to his father's discourses,
and answered the salutations of neighbors. Nevertheless, beneath all familiar
sounds he seemed continually to hear that soft laughter among
the peach-trees.

His mother, observing his abstraction, supposed him weary. His father
supposed him troubled about the examinations. Neither knew that a
single fallen blossom may outweigh all the maxims of the
sages.

After seven days Ming-Y returned toward the Commissioner's residence. He
had calculated the hour of his departure so that he
might reach the peach-orchards before sunset; and when the gray
mule entered that region, his heart beat so violently that
he became ashamed before his own servant.

The day was warmer than before. The blossoms had multiplied
until the branches seemed burdened with rosy snow. Ming-Y searched
among them, but beheld no woman, no maidservants, no little
basket of petals. He dismounted upon the pretext of tightening
the saddle-girth and walked some distance along the narrow path.
Nothing appeared except trees, flowers, and drifting light.

He was about to return when a perfume reached him—not
wholly the perfume of peach-blossoms, but mingled with aloes, musk,
and some ancient incense rarely burned in modern houses. At
the same moment he heard, very faintly, the sound of
a lute.

"Proceed without me," he told the servant. "A friend dwelleth
near at hand. I shall follow before dark."

The servant, who knew of no dwelling in that solitary
place, opened his mouth to speak; but Ming-Y's manner forbade
inquiry. Leading the mule, the fellow departed reluctantly.

Ming-Y advanced among the trees.

The path, at first scarcely wider than a ribbon, descended
through mossy rocks and entered a grove of dark pines.
The music grew clearer. It was not a melody then
fashionable, but an air of the Thang period, grave and
wandering, such as women had sung when the Emperor Ming-Houang
dreamed of his lost favorite. With the lisping of the
leaves, with the quivering of many-fingered boughs, with the fluttering
of lights and shadows, the music mingled so perfectly that
Ming-Y could not determine whether it came from strings or
from the forest itself.

Presently the pines divided, and he beheld a mansion.

It stood within a garden enclosed by a low vermilion
wall. The roofs were of azure tile; little bronze bells
hung beneath their upturned corners; carved galleries crossed a pond
in which white lotus-buds slept upon black water. Beyond the
gate rose flowering magnolias, dwarf plum-trees, and a willow whose
golden branches trailed like the sleeves of a dancer. The
whole place shone with that peculiar radiance seen in dreams—a
light without visible source, soft as powdered pearl.

While Ming-Y wondered how so noble a residence could have
remained unknown to him, the gate opened. An elderly waiting-woman
appeared and bowed.

"My mistress," she said, "hath observed the honorable scholar standing
without. She fears that the dew may injure him and
humbly entreats him to enter."

Ming-Y, startled by this courtesy, replied that he was an
insignificant tutor who had wandered from the public road and
was unworthy to disturb the household. But the waiting-woman bowed
again.

"My mistress saith that beneath peach-blossoms there are no strangers."

At these words Ming-Y entered.

He was conducted across the garden to a hall screened
with embroidered silk. There, beside a table of fragrant cedar,
sat the woman whom he had seen among the peach-trees.

She rose. Her robe that evening was the color of
moonlight upon water, and at her girdle hung a tiny
lion carved from green jade.

"I feared," she said, "that the young scholar who looked
backward upon the road would never look forward into my
poor garden."

Her voice was even lovelier than he remembered. Ming-Y made
a profound reverence.

"The wild goose," he answered, "having once glimpsed the southern
clouds, cannot forget the direction of its flight. Yet I
did not know that one so exalted dwelt within these
woods."

She smiled.

"My name is Sië. My late husband was distantly related
to your excellent patron, the Commissioner Tchang. Since his death
I have lived here with an aged kinswoman and a
few servants. We receive no visitors; therefore the neighboring people
have perhaps forgotten us."

Although Ming-Y had never heard the Commissioner mention such relatives,
he considered it discourteous to express surprise. Sië invited him
to sit. Tea was served in cups thin as eggshells;
preserved fruits appeared upon dishes of blue porcelain; and the
old waiting-woman placed near them a bronze censer from which
smoke ascended in a thread so slender that it seemed
to write invisible characters upon the air.

They spoke first of ordinary matters. Soon, however, Sië referred
to a commentary upon the *Book of Changes*, and Ming-Y
discovered that she had read not only the familiar version
but a rare recension believed lost. When he quoted a
line from Li-Taï-Pe, she completed the poem and corrected an
error made by modern copyists. She discussed painting, astronomy, court
music, the formation of seals, and the proper proportions of
regulated verse. Her learning had none of the heaviness of
pedantry; it flashed and vanished like gold fish beneath moving
water.

"How," cried Ming-Y, forgetting ceremony, "can a lady secluded in
these mountains know matters upon which gray-haired scholars dispute in
vain?"

Sië looked toward the darkening garden.

"The dead dynasties," she said, "are less silent to women
than to men. Men question history concerning rank, revenues, battles,
and laws. Women ask who loved, who waited, who was
forgotten. History answereth the latter questions more readily."

Ming-Y was troubled by the sadness of her tone. Yet
before he could reply, she took from a shelf a
roll of pale silk inscribed with poems. The characters were
fine, spirited, and of an antique elegance impossible to imitate.

"Whose writing is this?" he asked.

"A foolish woman's."

"Then that foolish woman must be numbered among the masters
of calligraphy."

"She was praised in her day," answered Sië, "but praise
is a flower which withereth even before the hand hath
tired of holding it."

Night had fallen. The bronze bells beneath the roofs whispered
in a wind that Ming-Y could not feel. Sië ordered
wine to be brought; it was purple, aromatic, and strong,
and was poured from a silver ewer shaped like a
phoenix. Then she played upon the lute the melody he
had heard in the woods.

The hours passed unperceived. At last Ming-Y remembered his duty
and rose in alarm.

"My honored hostess will pardon me. The Commissioner's gates must
long since have been closed."

"There is a little gate beside the eastern pavilion," said
Sië. "It is never locked."

Ming-Y wondered how she knew this, but her eyes met
his, and the question dissolved from his mind.

"May I come again?" he murmured.

"If the road remembereth your footsteps."

The old waiting-woman led him to the outer gate. When
he looked backward, Sië stood beneath a lantern of scarlet
silk. For one instant the lantern-light made her robe transparent
as mist; then the gate closed, and he was alone
among the pines.

He reached the Commissioner's residence after midnight. As Sië had
said, the little gate beside the eastern pavilion stood open.

On the following evening he returned to the woodland mansion;
and on the evening after that he returned again.

Thus began an enchantment which endured from spring until the
decline of summer.

By day Ming-Y instructed the Commissioner's children. He explained the
duties of princes, the virtue of sincerity, the necessity of
filial obedience; but when the sun sank behind the hills,
he laid aside the books of wisdom and hastened to
Sië. Sometimes she awaited him in the hall beside the
perfumed censer; sometimes upon the moon-viewing terrace; sometimes in a
painted boat no larger than a couch, drifting among lotus-leaves
upon the pond.

They drank wine warmed with spices. They played chess with
pieces of crystal and ebony. They composed verses upon rain,
separation, wild geese, old mirrors, and flowers seen by candlelight.
Sië sang songs of the Thang musicians—songs so ancient that
even the names of their melodies had perished from books.
Her voice seemed now like a brook gliding over white
pebbles, now like silk torn softly in a distant chamber,
now like a bird calling beneath the snow.

Often she spoke of the former capital as though she
had herself walked in its streets. She described the lanterns
of the imperial avenues, the painted cavalry of forgotten generals,
the pleasure barges upon vanished canals. She told of a
governor who was also a poet, proud and magnificent, whose
banners had once crimsoned the gates of Tching-tou.

"Didst thou read these matters in a chronicle?" Ming-Y once
asked.

"I read them," she replied, "where all things are written."

"And where is that book?"

"Under the earth."

Seeing him startled, she laughed and touched his sleeve.

"My scholar is too grave. Didst thou never hear that
books buried in tombs preserve characters which daylight would destroy?"

Another time Ming-Y observed that certain candles in her chamber
gave light without appearing to diminish.

"They are costly candles," said Sië.

"Of what wax are they made?"

"Of the wax of remembrance."

"And where may that be purchased?"

"Only from those who have waited a hundred years."

She spoke lightly, yet such sayings left a chill behind
them, as perfume remaineth after the passing of a funeral
procession. Ming-Y chose not to understand. Youth is ingenious in
defending its happiness against truth.

When the nights grew warm, Sië abandoned her outer chambers,
and they sat together beneath the willow. Fireflies burned among
the reeds; frogs sounded their little drums; the stars watched
from the purple sky. The lovers exchanged poems, or were
silent, which is sometimes the more complete exchange. Gradually the
ceremonies between them diminished. Her sleeve rested against his sleeve;
her hair touched his cheek; their voices blended over the
same song. At last, upon a night when the moon
was hidden and the whole garden breathed of lotus and
wet earth, Sië laid her head against his breast.

"My life," said Ming-Y, "until I saw thee, was a
lamp unlighted."

"And mine," she whispered, "was a lamp left burning in
an empty house."

Thereafter they were lovers.

Concerning the delights of those secret nights it is unnecessary
to speak too plainly. The discreet reader knoweth that happiness,
like certain music, is injured by explanation. It is enough
to say that time loosened its girdle about them; that
hours became moments, and moments worlds; that Ming-Y forgot ambition,
duty, the examinations, and even the existence of morning. When
Sië smiled, the room grew luminous; when she sighed, shadows
gathered beneath the eaves. Her beauty appeared less that of
a mortal woman than of some perfect portrait into which
longing had breathed a soul.

Yet pleasure, even when supernatural, cannot altogether escape the calendar.

The peach-blossoms fell. The lotus opened and faded. Cicadas began
their shrill lament in the branches. Sië became at times
strangely mournful. She would cease in the midst of a
song and listen, though no sound was audible to Ming-Y.
Once he awoke near dawn and found her standing beside
the window, clothed in white, gazing toward the western hills.

"What dost thou fear?" he asked.

"The cock's cry."

"That sound merely announceth morning."

"To some," she answered, "morning is only morning."

She would say no more.

Meanwhile Ming-Y's nightly absence had not passed wholly unobserved. At
first he told the Commissioner's steward that he went to
study at a neighboring monastery. Later, fearing inquiry, he said
his father had fallen ill and required him after sunset.
The Commissioner, who held filial duty above convenience, praised him
and reduced his daytime labors.

But one afternoon Ming-Lien himself came to the residence, carrying
a newly copied volume as a gift for his son's
patron.

The Commissioner received him courteously and said:

"Your son's devotion doth honor your family. I trust that
your illness is now abated."

"My illness?" repeated Ming-Lien.

"Indeed. Since he attendeth you almost every night, I feared
your condition must be grave."

The father's face altered; nevertheless he answered with composure:

"My humble body hath known no sickness this year, and
my son hath not slept beneath my roof since the
Festival of Pure Brightness."

The Commissioner was a man accustomed to detect falsehood among
officials. He immediately summoned Ming-Y; but the young tutor had
gone to instruct the youngest child in archery. During his
absence the two elders questioned the servants. None knew where
he spent his nights. The keeper of the eastern pavilion
confessed that the scholar often returned shortly before dawn, pale,
perfumed, and fatigued.

"A woman," said Ming-Lien, groaning. "Did I not warn him?"

"Possibly," said the Commissioner; "yet there is no reputable house
in the eastern woods, and the villages lie in another
direction."

That evening they ordered a trusted servant named Ho to
follow Ming-Y secretly.

The moon was a thin hook of silver. When Ming-Y
left by the little gate, Ho slipped after him, keeping
among the shadows. He saw the scholar cross the lower
field, enter the peach-orchards, and proceed toward the pines. The
path was narrow but plain. Ho followed at a distance
of fifty paces.

Suddenly Ming-Y vanished.

There had been no turning, no wall, no hollow in
the ground. One moment his pale robe glimmered between two
trees; the next there was only darkness. Ho hurried forward.
He found a thicket so dense that no man could
have passed through it without tearing his garments. Beyond stood
ancient pines rooted among broken stones. The earth smelled of
moss and rain. There was no lamp, no house, no
human voice.

Then, from somewhere beneath his feet, Ho thought he heard
the faint vibration of a lute-string.

He fled.

At the mansion that same night, Sië had prepared a
greater feast than usual. Twelve lamps illuminated the hall. Gold
cups stood upon the table; rare fruits were heaped in
bowls; the purple wine had been scented with chrysanthemum. Yet
no servants moved behind the screens, and the house seemed
unnaturally still.

Sië wore a robe of crimson silk embroidered with falling
leaves. In her hair were ornaments of kingfisher feather and
pearls, such as might have adorned a princess of an
earlier age. Her face was exquisitely beautiful, but paler than
Ming-Y had ever seen it.

"My beloved," she said, "we must part."

Ming-Y laughed, believing this another of her melancholy fancies.

"Then we shall part at dawn and meet tomorrow."

"We must part forever."

The cup shook in his hand.

"Why dost thou speak thus? Have I offended thee?"

"No."

"Have thy kinsfolk returned? Hath the Commissioner discovered us? Tell
me the obstacle, and I will overcome it. If thou
fearest scandal, I will ask my father's consent. Though thou
art a widow and richer than I, he cannot deny—"

Sië raised one hand.

"Ask nothing. Promise nothing. There is a wall thy courage
cannot climb, and a river no boat can cross."

"Death alone could divide us."

At that word all the lamps trembled.

Sië bent her head.

"Even death," she said, "doth not always divide soon enough."

Ming-Y stared at her; but love, which had blinded him
to a hundred warnings, would not suffer him to comprehend
the last. He fell at her feet and implored her.
He accused fortune, her family, himself. He swore to abandon
rank, parents, and name. Sië listened with tears upon her
cheeks.

"Speak not against thy parents," she said. "The living owe
allegiance to the living. Thou hast given me more than
thou canst understand: one spring after many winters; one voice
in a house of silence; one heart that did not
ask whether I was worthy of remembrance. Should I repay
thee by stealing thy whole life?"

"My life is already thine."

"No. Only this portion of it."

She lifted him and poured wine into two cups.

"Tonight we shall not dispute with heaven. We shall drink
as formerly. Thou shalt play chess badly, and I shall
pretend not to observe. We shall sing the song of
the River Lo. Thou shalt correct my poem, though thy
correction be inferior. And when the sky grows pale, thou
shalt depart without looking backward."

So they passed their final night.

They played chess, but neither saw the board. They sang,
but their voices failed. They drank, yet the wine tasted
of tears. Near midnight Sië brought forth a small box
containing the poems they had composed together. These she gave
to Ming-Y, together with a brush-case of reddish agate carved
in cloud patterns.

"This belonged," she said, "to one who loved poetry more
faithfully than he loved women. Keep it, if thou wilt."

She then unclasped from her girdle the little lion of
green jade.

"And keep this also. The lion guardeth what cannot guard
itself."

"What shall I give thee?" Ming-Y asked.

"Thou hast given me remembrance."

"But I desire some pledge that thou wilt return."

Sië looked at him long.

"If peach-blossoms open above my dwelling next spring, think not
that I have returned. Flowers remember nothing."

Before dawn the old waiting-woman appeared. She carried no lamp,
yet her face was visible in the grayness like a
face reflected in old metal.

"It is time," she said.

Sië accompanied Ming-Y to the outer gate. The garden had
changed. The lotus-pond was black; mist covered the galleries; the
azure roofs looked dim and remote, as though already seen
across many years.

"Do not look backward," Sië whispered.

He crossed the threshold. The gate closed.

For several paces Ming-Y obeyed. Then anguish overcame him, and
he turned.

There was no gate.

The pines stood before him, immense and silent. Between their
trunks drifted a pale vapor. He called Sië's name. The
woods answered with the long murmur of leaves, but no
woman replied.

Half senseless, he wandered until sunrise, when servants sent by
the Commissioner found him lying beside the path, clasping the
agate brush-case in both hands.

He was carried to the residence and confronted by his
father and patron. Ming-Lien's anger, sharpened by fear, was terrible.

"Unfilial son!" he cried. "Thou hast lied to thy teacher,
dishonored thy household, and consorted with some abandoned woman. Speak
her name!"

Ming-Y, exhausted by grief, made no attempt at further concealment.
He related everything: the encounter among the peach-trees, the woodland
mansion, the widow's claim of kinship, the months of secret
love, and the final disappearance.

The Commissioner listened without interruption. When the tale was finished,
he examined the poems, the jade lion, and the brush-case.

"These objects are not modern," he said at last.

He summoned an antiquary then lodging in the western wing.
The old man inspected the agate carving through a crystal
lens.

"This pattern," he declared, "was used in the middle Thang.
No craftsman hath cut cloud-lines in this manner for six
hundred years."

The jade lion bore beneath one paw two minute characters,
almost effaced. The antiquary read them aloud:

"*Thao's treasure.*"

Then the Commissioner unrolled the poems. His expression changed from
suspicion to amazement.

"This hand," he said, "imitateth no ancient manner—it is ancient.
Observe the hooked ending of the *heart* character, the narrow
form of *moon*, the unbroken grass-stroke. And these verses! No
woman now living employeth such rhymes."

Ming-Lien, though still angry, began to tremble.

They determined to visit the supposed mansion immediately. Guided by
Ming-Y, they entered the peach-orchards and followed the path. At
the place where he remembered the vermilion gate, they found
only pines and tangled undergrowth. Ming-Y pressed forward, tearing his
robe upon thorns. Here, he insisted, had stood the wall;
there the lotus-pond; beyond, the moon-viewing terrace.

Ho and the other servants cleared away creepers. Beneath them
appeared fragments of blue tile, blackened by enormous age. Further
search revealed the foundation stones of a small building, half
sunk in the earth. At the center of the ruins
rose a mound covered with green moss, fern, and wild
peach-roots.

It was a tomb.

The inscription upon the stone had been almost destroyed by
rain. The Commissioner knelt and rubbed the surface with his
sleeve. After long scrutiny he distinguished several characters:

> "Sië-Thao… accomplished in music and verse… beloved of the
Governor Kao… departed beneath the autumn moon…"

Ming-Y uttered a cry and fell against the stone.

The Commissioner remained silent for some time. Then, being learned
in provincial history, he understood.

"I have read of this woman," he said. "She was
called Sië-Thao, though certain books name her Thao-Sië. She lived
when Kao-pien governed Tching-tou in the declining years of the
Thang. Celebrated alike for beauty, music, calligraphy, and verse, she
entertained scholars in a pavilion near these woods. Kao-pien loved
her and gave her a jade lion; another patron, a
poet of the court, presented her with an agate brush-case.
After the governor was summoned away, she waited for his
return. He never came. She died young, and her companions
buried her here."

He turned to Ming-Y.

"Her evasions contained the truth. She said her house was
related to mine because Kao-pien once held the office I
now hold. She read history beneath the earth because she
herself belonged to history. Her candles were remembrance; her songs
were those of her own age; and the gate through
which thou enteredst was not a gate opened by human
hands."

Ming-Lien covered his face.

"Will the spirit destroy my son?"

"I think not," replied the Commissioner. "Had she desired his
death, she would not have dismissed him. Yet affection between
the living and the dead is a bridge built over
mist. Whoever lingereth upon it must fall."

They raised Ming-Y and carried him home.

For many weeks he lay ill. Sometimes he called for
Sië; sometimes he recited verses in a voice so faint
that his mother could not distinguish the words. Peach perfume
filled his chamber though no blossoms stood nearby. Once, just
before dawn, the strings of a lute hanging upon the
wall sounded of themselves. Ming-Lien burned charms, summoned priests, and
caused passages of the Sacred Books to be recited; but
the Commissioner advised him not to accuse the dead woman
harshly.

"She returned what she had borrowed," he said. "Let gratitude
close the door which terror cannot."

When Ming-Y recovered, his father sent him to relatives in
a distant province. There the young scholar resumed his studies.
Grief did not leave him, but it entered more deeply
into his nature, as rain sinketh into earth and afterward
nourisheth trees unseen. He passed the examinations with distinction, obtained
office, and became known for judgments at once merciful and
just. In obedience to his parents, he married the daughter
of a worthy magistrate. She was virtuous, intelligent, and kind;
he treated her with unfailing honor, and their household was
peaceful.

Two sons and a daughter were born to them. The
elder son became a provincial judge; the younger wrote a
commentary upon the Odes; the daughter's paintings of bamboo were
praised even in the capital. Thus Ming-Y gained the blessings
promised by propriety: rank, descendants, reputation, and a tablet among
the honored members of his clan.

Yet he never spoke of Sië.

When musicians performed old airs of the Thang, he would
sometimes leave the room. When peach-trees blossomed, he avoided looking
toward them at twilight. If questioned concerning certain poems written
in a woman's antique hand, he answered only that they
had come from a ruined library near Tching-tou.

Upon his writing table there always stood two objects: a
little lion of green jade and a brush-case carved from
reddish agate. Servants were forbidden to move them. His children
supposed them heirlooms; his wife, wiser perhaps than they, asked
no question.

Ming-Y lived to a great age. On the evening before
his death he requested that the window be opened, although
it was still early spring and the air was cold.
Those beside him heard a faint sound resembling the music
of a lute very far away. The old man smiled
and turned his face toward the west.

Afterward, when his papers were examined, there was found among
them a single strip of silk upon which he had
written no official judgment, no moral maxim, no farewell to
his sons, but only these two lines:

> "I looked backward once, and the jeweled gate had
vanished;
> I have looked backward all my life, and still
she standeth there."

Whether the jade lion and the agate brush-case were buried
with him is not certainly known. One account declareth that
his daughter placed them beside his hands within the coffin.
Another affirmeth that they were returned to the moss-covered tomb
in the woods. But the peasants near Tching-tou say that
on certain nights of the second month a mansion with
azure roofs may yet be seen among the pines; that
lamps burn behind screens of pale silk; that a woman
in an ancient robe gathers fallen blossoms into a little
basket; and that if a young scholar should hear her
laughter, he must neither follow nor look backward.

Still, who, having once beheld her, would possess the strength
to obey?

The peach-trees bloom above Sië-Thao's tomb. The spring wind asks
the dead their names; and though none make answer, the
forest remembereth.

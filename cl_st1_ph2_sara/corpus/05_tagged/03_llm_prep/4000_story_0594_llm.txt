### THE LAST LETTER

The morning came delicately upon London. A thin April rain
had ceased before dawn, leaving the young leaves varnished, the
railings black and shining, and every shallow hollow in the
road filled with a pale reflection of the sky. Within
the park the birds conducted their small, important business among
the branches; without, beyond the locked gates, the first omnibuses
rolled eastward, and the milk-carts rattled cheerfully over the stones.

Nature, having washed the world, appeared satisfied with it.

Along the pavement beside the iron railing lay those whom
the night had rejected from doorways, arches, staircases, and sheds.
They rose by degrees as the police moved them on:
old men with the humility of beaten dogs; women whose
bonnets had collapsed into grotesque memorials of respectability; boys with
pinched noses and quick, dishonest eyes; and one or two
creatures so wrapped in rags that sex and age had
alike disappeared. The fresh morning showed them without mercy. Darkness,
which lends even misery a kind of privacy, had withdrawn.

Upon an iron seat near the gate sat two men.
One was perhaps forty-five, though dirt, drink, and exposure had
written sixty upon his face. He was known as Darkey—not
from his complexion, which was grey, but from an old
habit of blacking his eyebrows when he sold ballads outside
music-halls. The other was a young sailor.

Willie's clothes still preserved some ghost of better days. His
pea-jacket, though stained at the cuffs and green at the
seams, was of good cloth; his boots had once been
made for him; and beneath his ragged blue neckerchief there
showed the collar of a linen shirt. But his face
destroyed whatever argument his clothes might have advanced. It was
terribly white. His eyes, large and feverishly bright, had the
fixed attention of a man listening to footsteps that no
one else could hear.

Darkey rubbed his hands beneath his armpits.

"Cold enough to freeze the tail off a brass monkey,"
he said. "An' me innercently expectin' spring. Got anythink left?"

"Only expectations," said Willie.

"Can't eat 'em."

"No. That is their chief defect."

Darkey looked at him with suspicion. Willie's manner of speaking
sometimes offended him as a personal boast.

"Yer was talkin' about that Jap place," he said. "Where
they cooks rats."

"Nagasaki."

"That's it. Nagger-sarky. Was there grub there?"

"Plenty, if you had money."

"Then it weren't no better'n London."

Willie smiled, and the smile altered suddenly into a cough.
He bent forward, pressing one hand to his side. The
attack lasted so long that Darkey ceased rubbing his arms
and watched him with an uneasy resentment, as though illness
were an unfair method of attracting attention.

When Willie recovered, he remained bowed, his damp hair hanging
over his forehead.

"We had a Dutchman aboard," he said presently, "who swore
he could eat anything that had possessed a mother. In
Nagasaki he bought a little pie from a woman in
the street. He ate half before he found a claw
in it."

"Rat?"

"Monkey."

Darkey considered this.

"Waste not, want not," he said. "Did 'e finish it?"

"He did. Then he went back and asked for another."

Darkey laughed loudly enough to disturb a woman sleeping beneath
newspapers on the next bench. She raised a swollen face,
cursed them both, and settled again.

"That's a good 'un," he said. "I'll remember that. A
monkey pie! Lord, what a world! I could do one
now."

"You would require the monkey."

"I'd do without."

Willie leaned his head against the wet iron. He had
talked through much of the night—not from sociability, for Darkey's
society was a burden, but because silence had become populous.
In silence came the creaking of cordage, the slap of
black water against a ship's side, the shriek of a
Lascar whose arm had been caught in a winch, and,
more troublesome than any of these, the faint noise of
a gate swinging before a house in Staffordshire. Talk kept
these things at a distance. So he had told of
typhoons, knives, drunken captains, yellow ports, and one man washed
from a yard in the China Sea; had made them
humorous when he could, brutal when he could not, and
had accepted Darkey's interruptions concerning food with the patience of
exhaustion.

"Ever been wrecked?" Darkey asked.

"Several times."

"Proper wrecked?"

"Once. The other occasions were moral."

"Ah," said Darkey, without understanding. "I been that meself."

A constable approached, broad and deliberate, revolving the doors of
the morning by the mere pressure of his boots.

"Come along, now. Up with you."

Darkey rose immediately. Authority had been the one consistent fact
of his life.

"Right, guv'nor. We was only restin' our feet."

Willie made an effort to stand. His knees straightened, his
hand slipped along the back of the seat, and for
an instant he appeared absurdly surprised. Then he fell sideways,
striking his temple against the iron arm.

The constable knelt beside him.

"What's his name?"

"Willie."

"Willie what?"

Darkey scratched his jaw.

"Willie. Sailor chap. Been tellin' me about monkey pies."

The constable glanced up.

"Friend of yours?"

"Not what yer might call a bosom pal."

"No," said the constable. "I shouldn't think anybody was."

Yet it was the constable who sent for the ambulance,
and Darkey who slipped away while it was coming, being
oppressed by a belief that hospitals asked questions concerning one's
means of support.

---

The ward was long, clean, and full of subdued noises:
the whisper of slippers, the click of a spoon against
a medicine-glass, a cough suppressed under blankets, the murmur of
men whose dreams were less orderly than the beds in
which they lay. Sunlight entered through high windows and made
bright rectangles upon the polished floor. There was warmth, too—an
immense, soft, institutional warmth which seemed to Willie the most
luxurious thing he had ever known.

They cut away part of his shirt. A nurse washed
his face and found him younger beneath the dirt. The
doctor, after listening to his chest, drew the nurse aside.

"How long?" she asked.

"Possibly till morning. Not much beyond."

Willie heard him. Doctors habitually speak in low tones within
three feet of a bed, as though death were deaf
as well as solemn.

Till morning! It was curious that the phrase should mean
so little. He had been till morning under hatches, till
morning in police cells, till morning in rooms where cards
and brandy had made time ridiculous. This was only another
waiting. Yet the doctor's calm voice had placed a wall
at the end of it.

He closed his eyes.

The nurse had used some sharp-smelling preparation upon his forehead.
Acetic acid, perhaps. The smell carried him, without transition, into
the pantry at home, where his mother had once made
preserves. A blue bowl stood upon the table; sunlight warmed
the red tiles; his little sister Ellen, then no higher
than the table edge, had dipped a finger into the
sugar and looked at him in terror and delight. He
had promised not to tell. He had been sixteen and
magnificent in his own estimation, already impatient of the quiet
house, the prayers, the measured affection, the narrow green lanes
that led nowhere worth going.

His mother had stood at the window cutting labels.

He saw her hands.

During the years since, he had remembered many women's faces
and very few hands. Now the hands were clearer than
anything: capable hands, a little reddened, with a plain ring
upon one finger. They had mended his shirts, packed his
sea-chest, held his head when he was ill, and, on
the last morning, remained at her sides because he had
refused to take them.

He had been right, of course—or had seemed right, which
at twenty is the same thing. His father's reproaches were
intolerable; his mother's tears were a form of tyranny; his
brothers were dull; the child Ellen a nuisance. He would
sail, make money, return in splendour, and compel them to
confess that he had understood life better than they.

It is a peculiar satisfaction to recognise oneself as the
architect of one's own misfortune. Fate may be cursed, society
arraigned, Providence examined with bitterness; but when a man perceives
that every stone of his prison was selected and laid
by his own hand, he enjoys at least the dignity
of authorship.

Willie had enjoyed it often. He had deserted one ship
for higher wages and lost both wages and character; struck
an officer who had insulted him; gambled away the money
meant for home; laughed at the friend who offered him
work ashore; and, when fever weakened him, treated gin as
medicine until medicine became necessity. There had always been an
explanation. Explanations are the small change with which failure pays
its debts.

But now—his mother.

He wanted neither pardon in heaven nor justice upon earth.
He wanted his mother, or his brothers, or his little
sister; one of them standing beside the white bed and
saying his name as it had been said before he
spoiled it. He wanted the impossible domestic sound of a
kettle on the hob. He wanted the gate in Staffordshire
to click, and a lamp to burn behind the parlour
blind. He wanted to go home.

A nurse approached with milk.

"Can you take this?"

"I've taken worse."

She raised him gently. His head rested for an instant
against her sleeve, and the impersonal kindness of the movement
nearly undid him.

"Would you like anyone sent for?" she asked.

He stared at the glass.

"There may be someone."

"Your wife?"

"No. I spared a wife."

"A mother?"

He nodded.

"Where does she live?"

"Staffordshire."

"Where in Staffordshire?"

The precise question embarrassed him. He knew, surely. A village
near Lichfield; a brick house beyond the mill; his father's
name on a brass plate beside the door. But years
had blurred the geography. They might have moved. His father
might be dead. Ellen might be married. His mother—

"Let me write," he said.

The nurse brought paper, an envelope, and a pencil. She
placed a board across his knees, arranged the pillows, and
left him with the conscientious privacy accorded to the dying.

He wrote slowly.

"My dear Mother,

"I do not know whether this will come to you,
or whether you will wish to read it when it
does. I have been a bad son, and I know
there is no use in saying now what I ought
to have said long ago. I thought I had the
world before me, but it seems I only had it
behind me, for I never could catch up with it."

He paused. That sounded like one of his clever sayings.
He had hidden behind clever sayings all his life. He
crossed out part of the sentence; the effort exhausted him.

"I am in a hospital in London, and they have
been very good to me. Do not think I am
afraid. I should like you to know I remembered everything,
especially the last day, and I am sorry for the
sorrow I caused you. Give my love to Tom and
Henry, if they are at home, and to little Ellen,
though she cannot be little now. Tell her I remember
the sugar in the pantry and never told."

The pencil slipped.

He saw the label in his mother's hand. Raspberry. The
word was written in her neat, upright script. Outside, someone
called to a dog; his father turned a newspaper; Ellen
laughed with sugar on her finger.

Willie tried to continue.

"If you could forgive—"

The line descended across the paper and ended in a
grey mark.

The nurse found him breathing, but unconscious. The letter lay
beneath his hand. There was no address upon the envelope.

He died shortly after midnight, without recovering speech.

In the morning his few possessions were examined: a broken
clasp-knife, two foreign coins, a brass button, a pawn-ticket no
longer valid, and the letter. No surname could be established.
The constable remembered the older man from the park, and
by a combination of official patience and the gossip of
lodging-houses, Darkey was found that afternoon carrying a sandwich board
advertising a miraculous cure for baldness.

"You knew the deceased," said the porter.

Darkey looked over his shoulder at the painted head of
a gentleman whose hair had been restored in luxuriant waves.

"Who?"

"The young sailor. Willie."

"Oh, 'im. Gone, 'as 'e?"

The porter gave him the letter.

"There's no address. He mentions his mother in Staffordshire. You
said he'd spoken of his home."

Darkey turned the envelope over.

"Staffordshire—that's right. Near Litchfield way. Told me once. I can
find out. Trust me."

There are men who become trustworthy for the duration of
a promise. At that moment Darkey sincerely intended to find
Willie's mother. He thrust the letter inside his coat, assumed
an expression appropriate to bereavement, and told the porter that
he and the dead man had sailed together for years.

"Poor lad," he added. "Died in me arms, near enough."

The porter, who knew otherwise, let this pass.

Darkey resumed his board and walked away with considerable dignity.
At the corner he received his day's pay. The possession
of money revived in him an old pain which could
be treated only at a public house.

The bar was bright with mirrors, brass taps, coloured bottles,
and the comfortable steam of wet coats. Darkey ordered gin,
then bread and cheese, then more gin because the cheese
was salty. Three men near the fire were discussing voyages,
though none had travelled farther than Gravesend.

"Talkin' o' foreign parts?" Darkey said, joining them. "I was
in Nagger-sarky once."

"Where's that?"

"Japan. We got wrecked there. Starvin', we was. Come across
a pie-shop—monkey pies. Little claws stickin' out the crust."

He embellished the story with confidence. By the second glass,
he had eaten the pie himself; by the third, the
monkey had been alive; by the fourth, Willie had disappeared
from the adventure altogether.

The men laughed. Darkey, pleased, ordered tobacco. He filled his
pipe, searched his pockets, and found no matches.

"Got a light?" he asked.

"Not me."

Darkey felt in his coat and drew out the letter.
For a second he examined it, puzzled. Then recollection stirred
and was drowned almost at once beneath gin, warmth, applause,
and the immediate importance of tobacco.

"Old bill," he said.

He tore the letter across. One half he twisted into
a spill and held to the gas flame. Willie's apology,
his mother's remembered hands, Tom and Henry and little Ellen,
burned with a clear edge of blue. Darkey drew the
flame into his pipe, puffing until the tobacco caught. The
first half died between his fingers. He used the second
to make the ember glow more evenly.

A man beside him leaned forward, pipe ready.

"After you, mate."

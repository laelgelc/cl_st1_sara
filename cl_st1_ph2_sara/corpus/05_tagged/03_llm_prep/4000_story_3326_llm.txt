### THE HIDDEN JEWEL

"Anna May is dying."

Mrs. Bell spoke these words with a grave tenderness; but
her friend, Mrs. Ellis, who stood before the mirror arranging
a cluster of pale roses in her new bonnet, only
lifted her eyebrows and answered—

"Anna May!—who is Anna May?"

"The young seamstress who worked for you last winter. Surely
you remember her?"

"Oh! the pale girl who altered my blue silk? Yes,
I recollect her now. But dying, did you say? How
very shocking! Does this rose sit too high, my dear?"

Mrs. Bell looked sorrowfully at the fair, prosperous woman before
her. Everything in that elegant apartment spoke of ease. Rich
curtains shut out the chill of the declining day; a
bright fire shone upon gilded frames and polished tables; soft
carpets gave back no echo to the foot. Yet amid
all these things, gathered for comfort and pleasure, the thought
of death entered like an unwelcome stranger.

"The rose is well enough," said Mrs. Bell. "But Anna
has asked to see you."

"To see me!" Mrs. Ellis turned quickly from the glass.
"What can she want?"

"Perhaps nothing that money can supply. She spoke your name
this morning and said she wished to take your hand
once more."

"But I cannot go to a deathbed. Indeed, I cannot!
Such scenes overcome me. I should be ill for a
week."

"And shall the fear of one hour's sadness prevent you
from granting the last request of a dying girl?"

Mrs. Ellis drew off one glove, then put it on
again.

"I am sure I have always paid her fairly."

"No one says otherwise."

"And when she was ill before, I sent her broth."

"Yes."

"Then what more can be expected of me? Besides, I
know nothing of sickness. It is dreadful to see people
die."

"Dreadful to whom?" asked Mrs. Bell quietly.

"To everyone, surely."

"Not to Anna."

Mrs. Ellis looked at her friend with surprise.

"Not dreadful to her? Poor creature! She must be wandering."

"She is weak, but her mind is clear. I have
never heard wiser words from human lips."

This awakened something better than curiosity in Mrs. Ellis, though
curiosity mingled with it. She glanced once more at the
mirror, sighed, and removed her bonnet.

"If you will remain with me, I will go. But
you must not leave me alone in the room—not for
a moment."

Mrs. Bell promised; and, in a short time, the two
ladies left the warm and brilliant house.

The streets through which they passed grew narrower and darker.
Fine dwellings gave place to little shops, and these to
low houses whose windows showed few signs of comfort. At
last Mrs. Bell stopped before a plain brick building and
led her companion up a steep staircase.

"How can anyone breathe here?" whispered Mrs. Ellis.

"Anna has breathed here many a weary month," replied Mrs.
Bell; "and has labored here, too, that others might be
clothed in beauty."

They entered a small chamber. It was very poor, but
perfectly clean. A narrow bed stood near the window; beside
it were a table, a worn Bible, a cup, and
a single white flower in a cracked glass. The last
rays of evening had found their way between the roofs
opposite and rested softly upon the pillow.

There lay Anna May.

Mrs. Ellis would scarcely have known the seamstress whose quiet
figure had once bent over silks and laces in her
dressing-room. The young face was pale and wasted; the hands
folded upon the coverlet were almost transparent; and every breath
appeared too slight a thread to bind the departing spirit
to its earthly dwelling. Yet there was no terror in
her countenance. Her drooping lashes lifted as the visitors approached,
and a smile, faint but peaceful, passed over her features.

"You have come," she said. "I am glad."

Mrs. Ellis took the thin hand, but could not speak.
She had expected groans, wild looks, and the gloomy confusion
of mortal agony. Instead, the room seemed full of an
extraordinary repose.

"I feared you would not remember me," Anna continued.

"I remember you now," said Mrs. Ellis, blushing beneath the
dying girl's gentle gaze. "I am sorry you are so
ill. Is there anything you would like? Anything I can
send?"

Anna looked around the humble room.

"I shall soon need nothing that can be sent here."

Mrs. Ellis trembled.

"Do not speak so! The physician may yet restore you."

Anna shook her head.

"The physician has been kind, but he cannot turn back
the messenger."

"What messenger?"

"The angel who is coming to lead me home."

Mrs. Ellis drew nearer to Mrs. Bell, as though the
dark angel were already visible.

"How can you speak of death in that manner?" she
asked. "Are you not afraid?"

"Why should I fear to go to my Father?"

"But death! Think what it is—the end of everything we
know."

"The end?" Anna's eyes brightened. "It is the end of
pain and weariness, perhaps; but not of life. This dim
evening is only the border of my morning."

Mrs. Ellis was silent. The words were strange to her.
She had called the world bright because it gave her
admiration and abundance; yet she feared every shadow which suggested
that these possessions must be left behind. Anna, who had
little to lose and no earthly splendor to remember, spoke
as one already rich in an inheritance which neither sickness
nor poverty could take away.

"But how can you be certain?" Mrs. Ellis said at
length. "No one has returned to tell us."

"One hath ascended," answered Anna softly, "and because he lives,
we shall live also. He has promised that where he
is, his servants shall be. Is not his word enough?"

"It ought to be," murmured Mrs. Bell.

Anna turned her luminous eyes toward her.

"It is enough. When I was well, I did not
always feel its strength. Sometimes the days were long, and
the work was heavy, and I wondered why my path
had been made so narrow. But now I see that
even the narrow path led home."

Mrs. Ellis remembered, with a pang, the many hours during
which this poor girl had sat unnoticed in her house.
While Anna's tired fingers fashioned garments for festivals she could
never attend, Mrs. Ellis had fretted over colors, ribbons, and
trifles too small to retain a name. Had she ever
asked whether the worker was cold, or weary, or sad?
She could not remember.

"The preparation, Anna!" she exclaimed suddenly. "How does one prepare
for such a change? I go to church. I give
something to the poor. Yet if I lay where you
are lying, I should be terrified."

Anna's face became very earnest.

"To prepare for heaven, we must begin to love what
is heavenly."

"And what is heavenly?"

"God is love."

The little chamber was still. The simple sentence seemed to
remain in the air after the feeble voice had ceased.

"God is love," Anna repeated. "And if we would dwell
with him, we must desire to become like him—not in
power, for we are weak; not in wisdom, for we
know almost nothing; but in love. We must forget ourselves
enough to seek another's good."

"But no one can be perfect," said Mrs. Ellis.

"No. Yet we may turn our faces toward the light.
A child cannot measure his father's steps, but he can
follow them."

"And is kindness all that is required?"

"True kindness is not a little thing," replied Anna. "It
is not the coin given because we wish to be
rid of a beggar, nor the pleasant word spoken because
we desire praise. It is patience when we are provoked;
forgiveness when we are wounded; faithfulness when no eye commends
us; pity for those who cannot repay. It is to
carry another's burden, though our own hands ache. Such love
comes from God and leads back to God."

Mrs. Ellis bowed her head.

How poor appeared her costly charities beside this rule! She
had given what she did not miss, smiled when smiling
was easy, and called herself benevolent because gratitude had followed
her gifts. But had she loved? Had she ever sought
another's good at the price of her own comfort?

"Can one begin so late?" she asked.

"So long as we can hear the call, we may
answer it."

Anna's voice had sunk almost to a whisper. Mrs. Bell
raised the cup to her lips, but she could not
drink. For several minutes she lay with closed eyes.

Outside, the common noises of the street went on. A
cart rolled over the stones; a child called from a
doorway; hurried feet passed beneath the window. How strange that
the world should make its accustomed sound while one immortal
soul stood upon the shore of eternity! Yet so it
is continually. We buy and sell, adorn our bodies, enlarge
our houses, and contend for a little honor, while beside
us some traveler silently enters the everlasting country.

Presently Anna opened her eyes.

"It grows light," she said.

Mrs. Ellis glanced toward the window. The last ray had
faded, and the room was filling with dusk.

"The sun has gone down," she replied.

"Not there," said Anna, with a smile. "Here."

She laid one hand upon her breast.

Mrs. Bell knelt beside the bed.

"Is there anything you wish us to do?"

"Only love one another. Tell those who are burdened that
the Father does not forget them. Tell those who are
happy not to close their doors against sorrow. We are
all going to one home, if we will walk in
his way."

Her breathing grew fainter. Mrs. Ellis, forgetting her dread, sank
upon her knees and clasped the wasted hand between her
own.

"Anna," she cried, "forgive me! You sat beneath my roof,
and I scarcely saw you. I thought you only a
poor sewing-girl."

"I was poor," Anna answered, without reproach; "but I have
never been alone."

Then her gaze passed beyond the faces bending over her.
A wonderful tranquillity clothed her countenance. Was it only the
calm which follows suffering, or did some brightness from the
opening gates fall upon that lowly pillow? Who shall say?
There are moments when faith beholds what mortal eyes cannot
discern, and the departing seem to hear music for which
we still wait.

"Home," Anna whispered. "My Father—"

A soft breath followed. Then no breath came.

How hushed the room! Mrs. Ellis waited in trembling expectation,
unable to believe that death could enter so gently. But
the messenger had come and gone, leaving no terror behind.
The snowy cheek, the folded hands, the faint smile—all wore
the peace of childlike sleep. The poor chamber had become
sacred, and the humble bed seemed richer than a throne.

Mrs. Ellis wept.

"I have feared death all my life," she said, "and
she has taught me how to die."

"She has taught you how to live," replied Mrs. Bell.

"Yes—how blind I have been! I measured people by their
dress, their speech, their place in society. I passed this
precious soul as though she were nothing."

"The richest jewel is sometimes hidden in the plainest casket."

Mrs. Ellis bent and kissed Anna's cold forehead.

"I cannot undo my neglect," she said; "but, with God's
help, I will not remain what I have been. I
will seek those whom ease makes us forget. I will
try to love—not for praise, but because God is love."

Mrs. Bell placed her hand upon her friend's shoulder.

"Then Anna has not spoken in vain. The peace you
have witnessed is not reserved for the poor, nor purchased
by suffering. It belongs to every heart that trusts the
Father and learns his likeness through self-forgetting love."

They left the chamber beneath the quiet stars. Mrs. Ellis
returned to her beautiful home; but its mirrors, flowers, and
costly ornaments no longer appeared the chief realities of life.
A pale young seamstress, whom the world had scarcely noticed,
had shown her treasures that do not fade and a
morning that no evening can darken.

Reader, must we wait beside a deathbed to learn the
worth of love? The messenger draws near to palace and
garret alike. Blessed are they who, before he comes, have
learned to see God's children in the lowly, to seek
another's good, and to walk toward the Father's house with
a faithful and loving heart.

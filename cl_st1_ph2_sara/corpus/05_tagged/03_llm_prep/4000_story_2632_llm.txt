# The House Beyond Coldwater Hill

There are men who traverse deserts, descend into sepulchres, or
penetrate the rank luxuriance of equatorial forests in search of
those sensations which civilised life has rendered rare; yet I
have long maintained that the most profound horror is not
necessarily entombed beneath Oriental sands, nor hidden among the shattered
fanes of extinguished empires. The traveller who seeks only remote
continents and fabulous ruins mistakes distance for strangeness. Antiquity, when
sufficiently isolated, may endure anywhere; and there are corners of
our own sober New England where the past has not
receded, but merely crouched out of sight.

Such regions are seldom marked upon ordinary maps. They lie
beyond the prosperous towns and cultivated roads, among valleys where
the hills gather closely about black streams, and where ancient
farmhouses, leaning beneath gigantic elms, appear to brood upon memories
older than the Republic. In these places the stern religion
of the first settlers did not always subdue the inherited
savagery of exiles, soldiers, persecutors, and outcasts. It sometimes merely
drove that savagery inward, where solitude, intermarriage, ignorance, and the
habitual contemplation of sin nourished it into shapes more tenacious
than any open barbarism.

A traveller may perceive the change by gradual signs. The
roads narrow and become grass-grown; stone walls sink under moss;
the houses turn their blind gables from the highway; and
the inhabitants, if any appear, gaze from doorways with an
immobility suggesting that speech itself has become an obsolete custom.
One sees no painted barns, no children at play, no
fresh curtains behind the small greenish panes. The chimneys lean,
yet continue to smoke. Wells gape beside twisted apple trees.
Vines crawl over doors as though nature wished to seal
what human prudence had neglected to close.

It was genealogical research, rather than any appetite for the
horrible, which brought me into such a district in November
of 1896. I had undertaken, for a family of respectable
standing in Providence, to investigate a doubtful connection with certain
early settlers of the Miskatonic Valley, and had spent several
profitless days examining church registers, probate records, and crumbling inscriptions
in burial grounds where the oldest stones had been effaced
by two centuries of rain. Because the railway served only
the principal villages, I travelled by bicycle, carrying my notes
and a few necessities in a leather case strapped behind
the saddle.

The morning had been cold but clear when I left
Aylesbury, though an oppressive band of cloud rested upon the
western hills. My intended route followed the river for some
miles, then crossed a wooded elevation known locally as Coldwater
Hill before descending toward Arkham. The map in my possession—an
inaccurate county survey of 1878—showed a secondary road which promised
to shorten the journey; but after an hour I found
myself among a labyrinth of lanes whose ruts, stones, and
recurrent forks had scarcely been improved since the colonial period.

The country grew increasingly deserted. Low hills rose on either
side, mantled with oaks whose last brown leaves clung like
withered hands. Here and there a field appeared, but the
stone fences enclosing it had fallen into ruin, and the
soil bore only burdock, mullein, and tall dead grasses bending
beneath the wind. Once I saw a distant roof among
the trees and approached in hope of obtaining directions, but
found merely the shell of a dwelling, its door absent
and its windows staring through the opposite wall. A fox
slipped from the cellar-hole as I passed.

Shortly after three o'clock the sky underwent a change so
abrupt that I recognised the folly of continuing without shelter.
The western darkness, hitherto confined behind the hills, surged upward
in immense bluish masses, extinguishing the weak November sun. Wind
rushed along the road, turning the dead leaves into shrill,
circular flights and bowing the roadside alders until their pale
undersides flashed like disturbed water. Then came rain—not the gradual
rain of autumn, but great cold drops which struck the
earth separately for half a minute before the whole firmament
seemed to descend.

Within moments I was soaked, blinded, and scarcely able to
keep the bicycle upright. Thunder rolled with a copiousness extraordinary
for the season, and the intervals between its reverberations shortened
ominously. I had begun to consider taking refuge beneath a
ledge, despite the danger of falling branches, when a flash
revealed, perhaps fifty yards from the road, the angular outline
of a house.

It stood upon a slight elevation beyond a field of
flattened grass. A narrow track led toward it between remnants
of a wall, and I turned thither without hesitation. Only
when I had crossed half the distance did something in
the aspect of the place awaken that unreasoning aversion which
sometimes precedes conscious judgment.

The house was large, ancient, and repellent. Its central portion,
built in the steep-roofed fashion of the seventeenth century, had
been enlarged by a low addition whose timbers sagged toward
the earth. Shingles, black with rain and age, curled from
the roof like diseased scales. The chimney was of massive,
irregular stone; one of the upper windows had been boarded,
while the others, bleared by dirt and tiny diamond panes,
blinked faintly whenever the lightning whitened the sky. No paint
remained upon the warped clapboards. Moss furred the foundation, and
vines, long dead yet tenacious, clutched the walls in a
network suggestive of veins upon a corpse.

Nevertheless, the house was inhabited. Smoke issued from the chimney,
though the violence of the wind flattened it against the
roof; and near the door I observed a newly split
heap of firewood, together with an axe whose bright edge
contrasted strangely with everything about it. That evidence should have
reassured me. Instead, the clean blade and massive billets produced
an indefinable uneasiness, as though the strength required for such
labour were inconsistent with the apparent decrepitude of the dwelling.

I dismounted beneath a dripping elm and approached the door.
My knocking brought no answer. After a decent interval I
knocked again, louder, and called out that I sought shelter
from the storm. Still no one replied. A particularly violent
detonation, followed by the cracking fall of some tree in
the woods, overcame my reluctance; and finding the latch unsecured,
I entered.

The odour within was the first thing to offend me.
It was not merely the smell of damp wood, smoke,
mould, and unwashed clothing, though all were abundantly present. Beneath
these hung something heavier—faint, stale, and sweetish—which I then attributed
to decaying provisions or a dead rat under the floor.

I found myself in a broad kitchen, lit imperfectly by
the storm-darkened windows and a low fire on an enormous
hearth. The room displayed an astonishing preservation of antiquated forms.
A settle with a high carved back stood near the
chimney; pewter vessels, blackened by neglect, occupied a shelf; and
over the mantel hung a long fowling-piece whose stock and
lock seemed of pre-Revolutionary manufacture. An eight-day clock, lacking both
hands, filled one corner. The table was a heavy oaken
structure scarred by innumerable cuts, while the chairs, of rude
turned wood, had bottoms woven from split ash.

Nothing suggested modern life. There was no newspaper, lamp, iron
stove, machine-made utensil, or framed photograph. Even the candle-holder on
the table was of hammered brass, green in its recesses.
For an antiquarian, the collection should have been fascinating; yet
the absence of order or care robbed it of all
charm. Dust clung to the walls in greasy films, cobwebs
sagged from the beams, and a hideous accumulation of refuse
had gathered in the corners. The room was not a
museum but a survival—a fragment of an earlier century continuing
without consciousness of the years beyond its windows.

Having called again without answer, I concluded that the occupant
was in some remote portion of the house. Good breeding
forbade further intrusion, but the tempest equally forbade retreat. I
placed my bicycle against the wall and approached the fire,
whose warmth was grateful despite the hateful atmosphere.

Several books lay upon a small table near the settle.
Their calf bindings were cracked, worm-eaten, and stained by moisture.
I recognised a colonial psalter, a damaged collection of sermons
by Increase Mather, a Latin Bible printed at Amsterdam, and
a medical compendium whose title-page had been torn away. Beneath
these was a larger volume bound in dark leather, its
boards swollen and its brass clasps missing.

When I lifted it, the book opened of itself near
the middle.

The title, which I subsequently copied from memory, identified it
as an English account of voyages and observations made upon
the western coast of Africa, printed in London in 1683.
The text, set in dense black type, was embellished with
copper engravings of unusual vigour. Several represented animals, fortified trading
stations, native ceremonies, and monstrous fishes reported by credulous mariners.
The leaves were brittle, greasy at the edges, and marked
in places by crude annotations.

One plate, occupying an entire page, immediately arrested my attention.
It depicted a roofed market or butcher's shop in the
Congo, where human bodies, divided into joints, hung from hooks
or lay arranged upon benches. Dark-skinned figures stood about with
knives and baskets, while one grotesquely smiling merchant displayed a
severed arm to a customer. The draughtsman had furnished the
scene with a minuteness at once absurd and abhorrent: the
cords around the ankles, the texture of the butchered limbs,
the complacent expressions of the buyers, even a child reaching
toward some loathsome scrap upon the ground.

The picture possessed no artistic merit sufficient to explain the
condition of the page. Unlike the neighbouring leaves, it was
darkened and softened by continual handling. The book, moreover, seemed
trained to open there; for when I closed it and
examined the binding, it slipped again from my fingers and
exposed the same odious engraving.

At that moment a board creaked behind me.

I turned and saw the inhabitant standing in the doorway
which led to the rear of the house.

He was an old man—indeed, by every visible indication, an
incredibly old man—yet his great height and breadth of shoulder
suggested no ordinary feebleness. His beard, yellowish-white and matted with
dirt, descended almost to his waist. Coarse grey hair straggled
from beneath a shapeless cap, and his face was seamed
into such innumerable wrinkles that the small eyes glittered from
cavernous folds. His clothes were ragged and archaic: a collarless
shirt, stained breeches, heavy stockings, and buckled shoes which might
have survived from the preceding century.

My first sensation was disgust; my second, embarrassment that he
might have observed it. The old man's expression was not
hostile. On the contrary, he smiled with obsequious hospitality, revealing
several large and discoloured teeth.

"Come in out o' the rain, hev ye?" he said.
"Wal, ye done right. Ain't fit fer Christian nor beast
out thar. Set close by the fire, young sir. Set
jest as close as ye're minded."

I apologised for entering uninvited and explained the circumstances of
my journey. He listened with surprising alertness, nodding at the
mention of family records.

"Genealogies, eh? Old names an' old blood. They's plenty o'
both in these hills, though folks down Arkham way don't
recollect half they ought. Names dies outside, but they keeps
here. Keeps a powerful long spell."

His dialect was more archaic than any I had heard
among the oldest rural inhabitants, retaining pronunciations and expressions which
students of philology commonly associate with the early colonial settlements.
Because scholarly curiosity temporarily mastered uneasiness, I encouraged him to
speak, asking how long his family had occupied the house.

"Long enough," he replied. "My gran'ther's gran'ther raised part on't,
or so they used to say. Other part's older. Timbers
don't rot much ef ye know how to keep 'em
dry."

The assertion was peculiar, since the entire structure reeked of
dampness. Before I could frame another question, he saw the
open book and uttered a thin chuckle.

"Lookin' at the picters, was ye? That's a mighty curious
book. Mighty curious."

He approached the table. Though his gait had at first
appeared shuffling, I noticed that each step was firm and
that the floor complained beneath his weight. His hands were
enormous, with thick nails and coarse black hair upon the
backs. When he bent over the volume, the intolerable odour
increased.

"Got that off a sea-captain," he continued. "Captain Obed Harlow,
out o' Kingsport. He fetched queer truck from Afriky an'
the Indies. Died afore your granddad was born, I calc'late."

The date printed in the book and the age implied
by this statement perplexed me. I supposed he repeated a
family tradition, confusing himself with an ancestor.

"It is an uncommon work," I said. "I have seen
a copy listed in the catalogue of the Widener collection,
but never one in private hands."

"Ain't the writin' that counts," he said. "Never took much
to readin'. But picters tells things plain."

He turned the volume toward himself, though it already lay
open at the cannibal market. His dirty forefinger moved slowly
over the engraved lines, pausing upon the suspended bodies and
butcher's blocks. The tenderness of the gesture was more offensive
than violence would have been.

"See here," he whispered. "Ain't that suthin'? Folks says them
Afriky people is heathen an' don't know no better. Mebbe
so. But they knows what's good to look at."

I attempted a laugh, intending to dismiss the subject as
rustic morbidity. The sound came unnaturally from my throat and
was swallowed by thunder.

"The engraving is certainly calculated to excite horror," I answered.

"Horror?" He looked at me with mild surprise. "Wal, mebbe
at fust. I recollect fust time I seen it, I
felt kind o' queer. Sickish, almost. Used to shut the
book an' put it off. Then I'd fetch it back.
Couldn't help it. Wanted to see them cuts agin—the meat
hung neat, an' them fellers standin' round so common-like."

He chuckled and rubbed one thumb against the forefinger, as
if testing the edge of an invisible blade.

Outside, the storm increased. Wind battered the walls; rain rattled
upon the panes; and from somewhere above came a dull
sound which I took for a loosened shutter. I began
to consider whether the weather might have moderated enough for
departure, but a glance through the window showed only a
moving grey obscurity split by lightning.

The old man drew a chair near mine.

"Picters puts notions in a body's head," he said. "Preachers
knows that. That's why they favors picters o' martyrs bein'
burnt an' saints with arrows stuck through 'em. Makes a
notion live. This here made a notion live in me."

His weak voice had acquired a tremulous eagerness. I answered
nothing.

"We kep' sheep then," he continued. "Plenty sheep on the
south pasture. I'd done slaughterin' afore, same as any farmer,
but never thought on it special. After I studied this
here picter, it got different. I begun to like the
work. The warm feel of it, ye know. The way
a critter kicks, then lies still. An' the smell when
the knife goes proper."

The civilised mind possesses curious defences. Even then I tried
to interpret his speech as the disgusting but harmless confession
of a senile farmer. Rural life familiarises men with slaughter;
solitude exaggerates their eccentricities; age loosens the restraints of conversation.
I reminded myself of these facts while every instinct demanded
that I rise and flee.

He bent closer.

"Beef's good," he said, "an' mutton's better. Hogs is near
enough to folks in some ways. But none on 'em
is jest the same."

A concussion shook the chimney, sending sparks into the room.
In the brief brightness I saw his eyes fixed not
upon the engraving, but upon my face.

"Surely," I said, with an effort at severity, "you speak
only from some fanciful theory."

"Theory?" He tasted the unfamiliar word. "Mebbe. A man wonders,
that's all. If them black fellers liked it, an' Scripture
says all flesh is flesh—wal, a man wonders."

"You would do better not to discuss such matters."

My rebuke, though feeble, appeared to amuse him. He sank
back and wagged his beard.

"Town folks is squeamish. Had a peddler come through once—must
be twenty year, mebbe more. Young feller, pert an' smooth.
He talked same way. Said some notions hadn't ought to
be spoke. Never come by this road agin."

There was nothing explicit in the remark, yet its implication
entered my mind with icy precision. I remembered rumours encountered
during my researches: a tax assessor who vanished in the
northern valley; a travelling schoolmaster last seen near Coldwater Hill;
a hired man whose departure had been assumed because no
relatives could be found. Such disappearances were not extraordinary in
isolated districts, and I had never connected them. Now each
returned with a hideous new significance.

The old man rambled onward.

"Another was a surveyor. Had chains an' brass tools. Stout
sort, but no grit. Then a minister come huntin' old
graves. Thin as a rail, that one. Wouldn't amount to
much, ye'd say, but looks fools a body sometimes."

I stood abruptly.

"I believe the rain is lessening."

It was not. The windows streamed as if submerged, and
the darkness beyond them had become nearly absolute. Nevertheless, I
reached for my coat.

The old man rose also, and the ease of the
movement destroyed my last hope that he was physically helpless.
Towering over me, he placed one huge hand upon the
table—not touching me, not barring my path, but making plain
how easily he might do either.

"Don't hurry," he said. "Road's bad. River'll be up. Set
a spell more."

His tone remained deferential, yet his gaze had become intimate
and appraising. I saw that the apparent frailty of his
voice belonged not to weakness, but to age alone. Beneath
the rags was a body preserved in monstrous vigour.

"How old are you?" I asked suddenly.

The question escaped before prudence could suppress it.

He smiled.

"Old? Ain't kept count. Years don't trouble a man ef
he feeds right."

The thunder ceased for several seconds, leaving a silence more
oppressive than its noise. In that interval I heard, distinctly,
a faint dragging sound from the room above.

The old man heard it too. His eyes flickered toward
the ceiling, then returned to me.

"Rats," he said.

No rat could have produced that slow, weighted movement.

He lowered himself again and turned the leaves of the
book, only to let them fall back upon the cherished
engraving.

"Fust time's hardest," he murmured. "Same with all things. A
body's raised to think one way, an' he's afeard o'
what he wants. But once he's had a little—jest a
little—he sees nature ain't beholden to parsons."

His finger tapped the butcher's block in the picture.

"An' it does suthin' to a man. Makes him hearty.
Keeps the cold out o' his bones. Folks grows old
because common victuals ain't strong enough. But some victuals has
life in 'em yet."

The meaning was now unmistakable. My composure, maintained through habit
and pride, began to fail. I looked toward the door
and calculated the distance. The old man watched me with
the absorbed expression of one who contemplates food not yet
served.

Then something struck the open page.

It made no sound that I could hear. I saw
only a small dark spot upon the engraving, directly over
the pictured butcher's shop.

For an instant I supposed that rain had penetrated the
roof.

But rain is not red.

A second drop fell, shining crimson in the firelight. It
spread slowly through the ancient paper, mingling with the printed
shadows of the mutilated bodies. The old man ceased breathing.
His face tilted upward, and upon it appeared an ecstasy
so naked, so ravenous, that all pretence of humanity vanished.

I followed his gaze.

On the low plaster ceiling above the table, a brownish
stain was widening around a crack. As I watched, its
centre brightened to wet scarlet. Something lay in the chamber
overhead—something recent, heavy, and still bleeding.

The old man's lips moved.

"More the same," he whispered.

I remember rising; I remember the chair overturning; I remember
his enormous hand lifting from the table. Whether he reached
for me or merely for the book I shall never
know.

The universe became white.

There was no interval between light and sound. An immeasurable
brilliance filled the room, exposing every beam, wrinkle, stain, and
filament of cobweb with the terrible clarity of final judgment.
The chimney burst inward. The floor rose beneath me, the
walls opened, and a roar beyond thunder flung consciousness into
vacancy.

I awoke beneath cold rain at dawn.

Men from a distant farm, having seen smoke after the
storm, found me half buried among splintered timbers at the
edge of a blackened foundation. The house had been struck
directly by lightning and almost wholly consumed. Of its inhabitant
nothing identifiable remained. The searchers discovered fragments of old furniture,
fused metal, bones of animals, and certain other calcined remnants
concerning which no public statement was made.

The great book had vanished. Whether it burned, or whether
those charred leaves which drifted across the wet field belonged
to it, I cannot say.

My injuries confined me for many weeks, and fever mercifully
obscured portions of the event. I resumed my profession, though
never again in the remoter Miskatonic country, and I have
endeavoured to regard the experience as an encounter with madness
and crime rather than with any violation of nature. Yet
the dates remain. The old captain from whom the book
was obtained had died more than a century before my
visit; the house, according to records later consulted, had been
believed unoccupied since 1811; and among the effects of a
missing surveyor there existed a memorandum describing an aged recluse
on Coldwater Hill in terms identical to mine—written sixty-three years
earlier.

These facts I cannot reconcile.

I know only that, when autumn rain beats upon my
windows and thunder moves over the hills, I sometimes see
again that room narrowing about one ancient book, one hateful
image, and one descending drop. Then I bless the lightning
which destroyed the house and spared my life.

Whether it spared my reason, I am less certain.

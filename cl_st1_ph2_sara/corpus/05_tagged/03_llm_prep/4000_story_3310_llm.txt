# The Child at Golden Farm

## I

It was half-past six on a windy March morning when
somebody knocked at the front door of Golden Farm.

Mother Golden, who was kneading bread in the kitchen, lifted
her floury hands and listened.

"There it is again," she said. "Ruthie, run and see
who's come so early."

Ruth, being twelve and therefore much too dignified to run
when walking would do, went through the dining-room, past the
cabinet organ and the tall clock, and opened the door.

Then she screamed.

"A baby!"

The words flew back through the house like a bird.

"A baby?" cried Sarah from the stairs.

"A baby!" shouted Adam, tumbling out of the bedroom with
one stocking on.

"A baby?" repeated Joseph, following him.

"A baby!" called little Jonathan, who understood nothing except that
something remarkable had happened.

Father Golden laid down the leather Bible from which he
had been reading before breakfast.

"Now, saying a baby and supposing a baby are two
different matters," he observed. "What is it, Ruth?"

"It is a baby!" Ruth answered. "A real one! In
a basket!"

Mother Golden came hurrying, wiping her hands upon her apron.
On the broad doorstep stood a market basket, lined with
a faded blue shawl. In it lay the plumpest, rosiest,
most astonishing little creature that had ever appeared at Golden
Farm without an invitation. A woollen cap had slipped sidewise
from his head, showing yellow flossy curls. His round fists
were tucked under his chin, his lips were parted, and
he slept through all the commotion as peacefully as if
doorsteps had been made expressly for babies.

"My heart!" said Mother Golden.

She stooped and gathered him up, shawl and all. The
child opened two dark-blue eyes, looked straight into her face,
and smiled.

"My heart!" she said again, but more softly.

"There's a paper pinned here," Ruth announced.

Father Golden took it and carried it to the window.
The writing was crooked and blotched, as though tears or
rain had fallen upon it.

He read aloud:

> To the woman with the kind eyes. I seen
you once with your children, and I know you will
not be hard on mine. His name don't matter, for
he has nobody left to give him one. He is
six months old and good. Keep him if you can,
for Christ's sake. If you can't, send him where you
think best. I shall never trouble you.

For a little while no one spoke.

The fire snapped in the kitchen stove. The clock ticked.
Outside, the bare branches of the lilac bush scraped softly
against the house.

Then Mother Golden carried the baby into the sitting-room and
sat down in Father Golden's great leather chair. The morning
sun came through the geraniums in the window and fell
over them both—the motherly face beneath its smooth brown hair,
the faded blue shawl, the yellow curls, and the rosy,
dimpled creature on her knee.

"Whoever wrote that," she said, "knew where she was sending
him."

Father Golden looked troubled.

"We do not know that it was his mother."

"No."

"We do not know who his people are."

"No."

"We do not know whether he is well."

"He looks most scandalously well."

At that moment the baby seized Mother Golden's apron-string and
put it into his mouth.

"He is swallering your apron," said Jonathan, much concerned.

"That proves he's hungry," Sarah declared.

"It proves he has no judgment," said Joseph. "Apron ain't
nourishing."

"He hasn't any teeth," Adam remarked. "Maybe he can't talk
till they come."

"Uncle Lem hasn't but one tooth," Joseph said, "and he
talks like a buzz-saw."

"Children!" Father Golden said.

They became quiet, though Adam and Joseph continued to make
faces at each other behind his back.

Mother Golden loosened the baby's shawl. His clothing was clean
but worn, and a little red flannel bag hung from
a string about his neck. It contained nothing but a
brass button and a curl of dark hair tied with
white thread.

"Somebody loved him," Mother Golden said.

"That is plain," Father replied.

"And somebody gave him up."

"That also is plain."

"And here he is."

Father Golden folded the note very carefully.

"Saying he is here and supposing he is to remain
here are two different matters, Mary."

Mother Golden looked at him. She had not been called
Mary in that tone since Jonathan had brought a lame
crow into the best bedroom.

"I know we have five children," she said.

"Five active children."

"I know the farm did poorly last year."

"Very poorly."

"I know the south barn needs shingling, and Adam must
have boots, and Ruth's outgrown her winter coat."

"All true."

"But I know something else, Samuel Golden. This child was
laid at our door because somebody believed there was mercy
inside it. I should be ashamed to send him away
before he has had his breakfast."

"No one proposes to starve him," Father said mildly.

"Then, Ruthie, warm some milk. Sarah, fetch Jonathan's old nursing-bottle
from the top pantry shelf. Adam, put another stick in
the stove. Joseph, don't twitch the shawl off his feet.
Jonathan, you may let him hold your finger."

The room sprang into action.

Father Golden stood in the midst of it, holding the
paper and looking as a man may look when events
have taken the reins out of his hands and are
driving the wagon themselves.

"You are arranging for breakfast," he said.

"I am."

"Only breakfast?"

"We will not decide about dinner till he has finished
it."

The baby did finish it. He finished a second bottle
also, pausing only to stare solemnly at Adam's hair, which
stood up in five directions. Then he gave a little
sigh and went to sleep against Mother Golden's breast.

"What'll we call him?" Sarah whispered.

"We shall not name him yet," Father said. "Naming and
keeping commonly go together."

"But we can't say 'it,'" Ruth protested.

"We could say Moses," suggested Joseph. "He was found in
a basket."

"He was found in the bulrushes," Ruth corrected.

"This one might have been, if so't had been warm
enough."

"He looks like a Peter," Adam said.

"He does not," said Sarah. "Peter is a thin name."

"Call him Daniel."

"No, David."

"Samuel," Jonathan said loyally.

"There is one Samuel in this house," Father observed, "and
at times that is sufficient."

Mother Golden looked down at the sleeping child.

"There was a youngest son in the Bible," she said,
"and his father loved him dearly. His name was Benjamin."

"Benny!" cried Jonathan.

The baby woke, sneezed, and smiled.

"There!" Jonathan shouted. "He knows it!"

"Six-month-old babies do not know names," Ruth said.

"This one does. He's uncommon."

"Benjamin is a good name," Father admitted. "But remember, children,
he may not be here long."

That afternoon Father drove to town to speak with the
constable, the minister, and the county officer. No one had
heard of a missing child. No stranger had been seen
except a woman in a dark cloak who had taken
the dawn train from the little station three miles away.
The stationmaster had not noticed her face. She had sat
in the farthest corner, with her hands clenched together, and
had carried no bundle.

For three weeks inquiries were made. A notice appeared in
the weekly paper. Father watched the post. Mother started whenever
wheels stopped at the gate.

No one came.

Meanwhile Benjamin took possession of Golden Farm.

He took possession first of Mother Golden's heart, though there
had never been much doubt concerning that. He slept in
the old cradle by her bed, wore Jonathan's outgrown dresses,
and drank warm milk in the kitchen while five children
disputed the right to hold the bottle.

He took possession of Father Golden by catching one of
Father's broad fingers and refusing to release it.

"See!" Sarah cried. "He's chosen you."

"He has a strong grip," Father said, but he sat
still for twenty minutes rather than disturb him.

He took possession of Adam by laughing whenever Adam whistled.
He took possession of Joseph by pulling his hair. Ruth
sang him hymns, and Sarah sewed crooked little flowers on
his bibs. Jonathan, who had hitherto been the youngest and
was uncertain whether to feel promoted or injured, finally made
up his mind when Benjamin rolled off a folded quilt
and bumped his nose.

"Don't cry, Benny," Jonathan said, climbing beside him. "I'm your
brother, and I'll bump mine too."

He did so, and they cried together until Mother Golden
comforted both.

At the end of three weeks Father called a family
council after supper. Benjamin sat on Mother Golden's knee, wearing
a white nightgown. The lamp shone on the leather Bible,
the red geraniums, the antimacassars—called tidies by everybody in the
house—and the seven grave faces gathered round.

"Children," Father began, "no person has claimed this baby."

"He's ours, then," Adam said.

Father held up his hand.

"No law has made him ours. The county will receive
him and place him in a proper institution. He will
have food, clothing, and schooling."

"He won't have Mother," Sarah said.

"Nor Father," added Jonathan.

"Nor us," Joseph put in.

"An institution is not necessarily cruel," Father continued. "There may
be kind persons there. But your mother and I have
considered the matter. Saying we pity a child and supposing
we undertake to rear him are different things. He will
cost money. He may bring anxiety. His birth and people
are unknown. Some day he may be difficult."

"He ain't difficult now," Jonathan said.

Benjamin promptly caught hold of the tablecloth and pulled Father's
cup into his lap.

"My heart!" Mother Golden cried.

"There," Father said, standing while Ruth wiped coffee from his
trousers. "The first evidence has arrived."

"He didn't know," Adam defended.

"Maybe he thought you needed washing," Joseph suggested.

When order was restored, Father sat again.

"If Benjamin remains," he said, "he must not be treated
as a visitor, nor as a charity child, nor as
one who eats our bread by favour. He must be
taught, corrected, nursed when sick, and loved as faithfully as
any child here. Your mother believes we can do this."

"We can," said Ruth.

"We will!" cried the others.

Father turned to Mother Golden.

"And you, Mary?"

She kissed Benjamin's yellow head.

"I think," she said, "that sometimes the Lord sends a
blessing dressed up like extra trouble, just to see whether
folks will know it."

Father's eyes rested on her face. Then he opened the
leather Bible and placed the abandoned note between its pages.

"Very well," he said. "His name is Benjamin Golden."

"Amen," said Mother Golden.

"Amen!" shouted the children.

Benjamin slapped both hands upon the table and crowed louder
than any of them.

## II

Ten years passed, which is a short time by the
clock in the hall and a long time in the
life of a family.

The south barn received its shingles. Adam's boots were replaced
many times, and at twenty-two he became taller than Father.
Ruth taught the village school. Joseph worked the upper fields
and believed himself a better farmer than Adam, while Adam
believed the opposite. Sarah, now seventeen, kept house with a
neatness that made untidy persons uncomfortable. Jonathan, fifteen and long-legged,
was preparing to enter the academy in town.

Mother Golden lay in the churchyard beneath the maple tree.

Her leather work-basket still stood beside the great chair, with
a needle rusting in a half-finished hem. Her geraniums still
reddened the windows, because Ruth tended them faithfully. Over the
cabinet organ hung her portrait, and sometimes, when the evening
light touched it, her eyes appeared almost living—steady, gentle, and
amused, as though nothing done by her children could entirely
surprise her.

Benjamin was ten.

His curls had darkened, his knees were generally bruised, one
pocket always sagged with treasures, and peace fled before him
like a hen before a dog.

"Benny!" Sarah called one April morning. "Have you seen the
kitchen clock?"

"No'm," came an innocent voice from the woodshed.

"It was on the shelf last night."

"Maybe it walked off. Clocks have hands."

"They haven't legs."

"This one might have uncommon works."

Sarah entered the woodshed and discovered Benny sitting on the
chopping-block. Behind him the clock was fastened to a toy
wagon, while two white mice from Joseph's grain bin were
harnessed to it with blue thread.

"Benjamin Golden!"

"I'm testing time," Benny explained.

"You're testing me! Why have you tied mice to the
clock?"

"I wanted to see if time flies."

Sarah shut her eyes.

"Take it apart. This instant."

"Yes'm. Only the big mouse has got kind of attached
to the minute hand."

Before breakfast the clock had been rescued, though it never
struck seven correctly afterward.

At the table Joseph reached for the sugar bowl and
drew back a wet hand.

"Benny!"

"What?"

"There's molasses on the handle."

"Is there?"

"You know there is."

"I suspected it."

Adam opened his napkin and a small green frog sprang
into his lap.

"Benjamin!"

"Don't hurt him!" Benny cried. "That's General Washington."

"Why is General Washington in my napkin?"

"He was cold."

Ruth lifted the lid from the teapot and found it
full of buttons.

"Benny," she said, with sorrow more impressive than anger, "where
is the tea?"

"In the blue jar."

"The blue jar holds salt."

"Not this morning."

Jonathan, meanwhile, had taken a hearty swallow from his glass
and was staring at it.

"What is this?"

"Water," Benny said.

"It tastes like peppermint."

"That's improved water."

Father Golden ate his oatmeal slowly.

It was the first of April, but the older children
had been too busy to remember. Benny had remembered enough
for the whole county.

When breakfast ended, Father went to the barn. Benny escaped
by the back door, accompanied by the frog. The five
older children remained at the table.

"I cannot endure much more," Sarah said.

"You say that every week," Joseph replied.

"And every week there is more."

"He put an alarm clock under my bed Sunday night,"
Ruth said. "It rang at two in the morning."

"He told me Father wanted the west pasture harrowed," Adam
said. "I got halfway through before Father asked what possessed
me."

"He filled my boots with oats," Joseph added.

"He said your feet looked hungry," Jonathan remarked.

Joseph glared at him.

"Yesterday," Sarah continued, "he tied Mother's best tidy to a
stick and sailed it from the hayloft."

"He said it was a flag," Ruth murmured.

"It landed on the pig."

"King Alfred looked very patriotic," Jonathan said.

"You encourage him."

"I do not. Not often."

"Last week he whitewashed the red rooster," said Adam.

"Only one side," Joseph corrected. "The other side stayed natural."

"He said the rooster wanted to be a surprise whichever
way he turned," Ruth said, and despite herself she laughed.

Sarah's mouth twitched, but she pressed it firm.

"It is no laughing matter. He is ten. When Adam
was ten, he did chores."

"When Adam was ten," Joseph said, "he put Aunt Matilda's
false teeth in the dog's mouth."

"That was one time."

"The dog smiled at the minister."

"I never meddled with clocks."

"You took Father's watch apart."

"To learn machinery."

"And Benny tied the clock to mice to learn science."

"That is different."

The discussion grew so warm that no one noticed Father
Golden return. He stood in the doorway, his grey head
bent a little, his weathered hand resting on the latch.

"Goodness knows we love him," Ruth was saying, "but he
wears a person out."

"He isn't like the rest of us," Sarah answered.

Father entered.

The children became silent.

He crossed the room, drew out his leather chair, and
sat down beneath Mother Golden's portrait.

"You have said one true thing," he observed. "Benjamin is
not like the rest of you."

"I didn't mean—" Sarah began.

"I know what you meant."

"He's a good boy mostly," Jonathan said. "Only there's so
much of him."

"Benny has twice the life of ordinary folks," Joseph agreed.
"And most of it gets loose."

Father folded his hands.

"There is a matter I have neglected too long. Perhaps
this is the day to settle it."

Something in his voice made them look at him. The
lingering laughter died from every face.

"When Benjamin came here," Father said, "your mother and I
promised to raise him as our own. Your mother kept
that promise while she lived. I have tried to keep
it since."

"You have, Father," Ruth said.

"But you were children then. You shouted 'Amen' because your
mother did, and because a baby is a pleasant thing
to own until it cries at night. You are grown
now. The farm and what savings I possess will one
day belong to you."

Adam stirred uneasily.

"We don't need to speak of that."

"We do. I have made a will. Under it my
property is divided into six equal portions."

"Six?" Sarah whispered.

"One for each of you, Benjamin included."

No one spoke.

The ticking of the injured kitchen clock sounded unusually loud.

Father went on in the measured manner which always meant
he had considered his words beforehand.

"Benjamin is not bound to you by blood. Neither are
you bound to him, except through promises made when you
were too young to understand them. Saying he is your
brother and supposing you will share a lifetime's duty with
him are different matters."

Ruth glanced up at Mother's portrait.

"I do not want any of you," Father continued, "to
feel wronged. Nor will I keep Benjamin in a house
where he is merely tolerated for your mother's sake. Therefore
there are two ways, and only two."

"Father!" Jonathan cried.

"Let me finish. Benjamin may go. There is an excellent
orphan home in the city. I have written to its
superintendent before now and know its conditions. The boy can
be educated there. I shall provide for him, but he
will cease to be one of the heirs of Golden
Farm."

Sarah had grown white.

"Or," Father said, "Benjamin may stay. If he stays, he
stays not as a foundling, not as a burden, not
as a boy who owes gratitude for every mouthful. He
stays as my son and your brother. He receives an
equal sixth of all I leave. You bear with his
faults as he must bear with yours. You stand by
him in honour or disgrace, fortune or poverty. You must
not remind him, in anger or jest, that he was
laid upon our doorstep."

"We never have!" Adam protested.

"No. Your mother taught you better. But now you must
choose for yourselves, with your eyes open."

"Does Benny know?" Ruth asked.

"He does not."

"Would you truly send him away?"

"If five of my children cannot accept the sixth, it
would be kinder to send him than keep him half-loved."

"Oh, Father!" Sarah covered her face.

"No decision made by a chorus," he said. "Each shall
answer alone."

He turned to Adam.

Adam's broad hands closed upon his knees. For a moment
he looked across the fields, where Benjamin's small figure could
be seen racing along the stone wall with a paper
parcel under his arm.

"I've been thinking," Adam said slowly, "how he let the
calf out last month and it ate every early cabbage
I had set. I said then I'd give ten dollars
to be rid of him."

Father waited.

"But when he had pneumonia two winters ago, I sat
up three nights listening to him breathe. I'd have given
the farm to hear him holler. I don't care about
the sixth share. He's my brother, and I want him
to stay."

"Speak to him, then," Father said, "as though he stood
here."

Adam swallowed.

"Benny, you little nuisance, stay home. Stay always."

Father turned to Ruth.

Ruth's eyes were full of tears.

"Mother put him in my arms the first night," she
said. "I was afraid I should drop him. He caught
my braid and wouldn't let go. He has been pulling
at me ever since—my hair, my patience, and my heart.
Benny, dear, I ask you to stay. Whatever comes, I
will be your sister."

Joseph rubbed his nose with his sleeve.

"It is your turn," Father said.

"I was calculating," Joseph began, "that a sixth of the
farm is considerable."

"So it is."

"And Benny'll likely lose his share trading for a three-legged
horse or inventing a churn that runs backward."

"Likely," Adam said.

"But he gave me his two dollars last year when
my hunting dog was hurt. It was all the money
he had, and he'd been saving for skates. He said
legs mattered more than runners. Benny can have his sixth.
If he wastes it, I'll scold him. If he needs
more, I'll help him earn it. Stay, Benny. I ain't
much at saying, but stay."

Sarah was crying openly now.

"I said he wasn't like us," she whispered.

"He is not," Father answered. "That does not settle whether
he belongs."

"No. He is untidy, and noisy, and he never shuts
a door. He puts angleworms in my flowerpots because he
says the plants need company. But when Mother died, he
was only seven. He crept into my bed every night
for a month and held my hand because he thought
I was lonely. I told myself it was I who
comforted him. It wasn't. Benny, I am ashamed. This is
your home. Stay, little brother, and I'll never again count
what you cost."

Jonathan had risen from his chair.

"You needn't ask me," he said fiercely. "He's nearer my
age. We sleep in the same room. He borrows my
shirts and loses my pencils and kicks me in his
dreams. If he goes, I go."

"You will answer properly," Father said, though his voice had
softened.

Jonathan looked at the empty doorway as if Benny truly
stood there.

"You bumped your nose because I bumped mine, long ago,"
he said. "I don't remember it, but Mother told me.
I reckon we've been bumping along together ever since. You're
my brother, Ben. You always were. Stay."

Father leaned back. Above him Mother Golden's painted eyes seemed
very kind.

"You have all spoken," he said. "Remember the words."

"We will," Ruth answered.

"Amen," said Sarah.

"Amen," echoed the others.

At that instant the door flew open and Benjamin burst
into the room. His cap hung by its string, his
cheeks were scarlet, and a magnificent red-and-yellow kite was tucked
beneath one arm.

"I might as well confess before anybody asks!" he cried.
"It was me put the buttons in the teapot, and
the tea in the salt jar, and the salt in
the blue pitcher. The frog was Adam's surprise, only General
Washington escaped before the proper time. Joseph, there ain't molasses
on your Sunday gloves; I only said there was. April
fool! And I'm sorry about the clock, Sarah, but time
did fly, only the mice went under the stove and
six-fifteen came off."

The family stared at him.

Benny stopped.

"What's everybody been crying for?"

"No matter," Sarah said, and pulled him to her.

"Ow! You're hugging most awful tight."

"Bear it," Joseph advised.

Benny looked from one solemn, smiling face to another.

"Has somebody died?"

"No," Father said. "Quite the contrary."

"Well, then!" Benny wriggled free and held up the kite.
"I made her myself. She's got Mother's old blue ribbon
for a tail—not the good ribbon, Ruth; the faded piece
from the sewing basket—and she'll fly higher than the church
steeple if there's enough string. Who'll come to the hill
with me?"

"I will," said Adam.

"I will," said Ruth.

"I will," said Joseph.

"I will," said Sarah.

"I will," shouted Jonathan.

Benny blinked.

"All of you?"

"All of us," Father answered.

So they went out together, the six children of Golden
Farm, though five were grown or nearly so. Benny ran
ahead with the kite lifting and tugging above him; Jonathan
carried the string; Sarah held the tail clear of the
briars; Joseph shouted contrary directions; Ruth laughed when the wind
loosened her hair; and Adam, longest-legged of all, pretended he
could not catch the smallest.

Father Golden stood at the gate and watched them climb
the April hill.

The kite dipped once, rose again, and sailed into the
clear light, its blue ribbon streaming bravely behind it.

"My heart, Samuel!" Mother Golden would have said.

And Father, looking from the bright sky to the six
figures running beneath it, could almost hear her.

# The Three Homely Oracles

There are certain dreams which, though they lead a man
through deserts, over mountains, and among the multitudinous habitations of
mankind, have nevertheless been awaiting him all the while beneath
the eaves of his birthplace. He goes eastward and westward
to meet them; he questions gray astrologers, listens at ruined
temples, and watches the midnight stars from the decks of
ships; whereas the answer, had he possessed the humble wisdom
to receive it, was spoken daily by his mother's voice,
written upon his native acres, and treasured in the faithful
bosom of one whom he left behind. Such a dream—and
such an awakening—were allotted to Ralph Cranfield.

Ten years before the period of our narrative, Ralph had
departed from the little village of Brambleford, in the northern
portion of New England. He was then a youth of
two-and-twenty, tall, dark, and stately, with an eye which seemed
to discern some object beyond the common horizon. The villagers
thought well of him, though they shook their heads at
his silence, his solitary walks, and the strange books that
kept a light burning in his chamber long after every
other casement had grown black. His widowed mother, who understood
him less and loved him more, feared only that he
would forget his woollen stockings when the weather turned cold.
Faith Egerton, his playmate from childhood, said nothing concerning his
peculiarities; but her quiet eyes followed him whenever he passed.

It had happened—or Ralph believed that it had happened—that, on
the eve of his departure, he slept beneath an ancient
oak at the boundary of his mother's field. Whether he
truly slept, or whether twilight, ambition, and the murmuring leaves
composed a waking vision, we say not. Yet three figures
appeared to him: venerable men, with beards as white as
frost upon dead grass, and robes that seemed woven of
evening mist.

The first held forth a jewel shaped like a human
heart.

"Search through the world," said he, "until this emblem shall
appear upon the breast of a maiden. She alone shall
possess the answering heart; and when thou shalt ask, 'Wilt
thou be the companion of my destiny?' she will reply,
'Through shadow and sunshine, to the end.'"

The second sage stretched out a hand, thin and brown
as a withered branch. Its forefinger pointed downward.

"Where the hand of Fate shall indicate," he said, "dig;
for there lies thy treasure. Above the appointed spot shalt
thou behold the word *Effode*—Dig!"

Then the third and most majestic of the shadowy counsellors
lifted a wand—or perhaps it was merely a bough agitated
by the night wind—and spoke thus:

"When three venerable messengers approach thee, and the foremost smites
the earth with his rod, know that they summon thee
to authority. Accept the charge. Thy empire shall be wider
than thou deemest, and futurity shall acknowledge thy power."

At sunrise Ralph awoke, if indeed he had slept. In
the dewy grass beside him glittered a small piece of
quartz, fancifully resembling a heart; while one crooked root of
the oak protruded from the soil like an admonitory finger.
These circumstances, which a cooler fancy might have interpreted without
troubling destiny, confirmed the young man in his belief. The
universe had spoken to him. Henceforth his life must be
a quest after a threefold fate—love, wealth, and dominion.

Before he went away, he visited the Egerton cottage. Faith
was standing in the doorway, with the morning behind her.
She was neat, comely, and quiet; no radiant princess of
faëry, but a brown-haired New England maiden, whose hands knew
the mysteries of bread, butter, linen, and healing herbs. Ralph
gave her the heart-shaped quartz, enclosed in a narrow rim
of silver.

"Wear it sometimes," said he, "for remembrance of our childhood."

Faith fastened it at her bosom.

"I shall wear it till you come back," she answered.

Had Ralph then possessed the interpretation which ten wandering years
were slowly to teach him, the first portion of his
destiny might have been fulfilled before sunset. But youth commonly
desires that happiness shall arrive with trumpets, whereas she often
comes barefoot and waits unheard at the door.

Pass we over the years of his pilgrimage, though each
had its marvel and each its disappointment. He sought the
destined maiden wherever beauty was most renowned. In a southern
island he saw a princess whose necklace contained a ruby
carved into the similitude of a heart; but the heart
was pierced through with a golden arrow, and its wearer
laughed when Ralph spoke of destiny. Among the mountains he
met a dark-eyed shepherdess with a garnet on her breast.
She would willingly have accompanied him, but when he pronounced
the ordained question, she answered in a language that he
did not comprehend and married a goatherd the following week.
In an imperial city he followed for months a veiled
lady whose jewel flashed crimson whenever her carriage passed. At
last the veil was lifted, and the lady proved to
be old enough to have dandled Ralph's mother in infancy.

Nor was the treasure more easily found. He saw hands
of marble pointing from tombs, hands painted upon ancient walls,
and hands of iron swinging above the shops of money-lenders.
Once, in a ruined abbey, a ray of moonlight fell
from the uplifted finger of a saint directly upon a
flagstone. Ralph labored there until morning, and discovered nothing except
the bones of a monk, who had perhaps been equally
disappointed in his expectations. Again, in a distant port, a
tavern sign represented a gigantic hand, beneath which some mischievous
scholar had scrawled the Latin word *Effode*. Ralph dug at
midnight behind the building, only to break a water conduit
and be chased through three streets by the landlord.

As for dominion, thrice had three old men approached him;
and thrice had hope, like a war trumpet, sounded in
his breast. The first deputation consisted of beggars asking alms.
The second were merchants who mistook him for another person.
The third invited him to preside over a fraternity whose
authority extended scarcely beyond the back room of an inn.
Ralph refused that little empire with disdain, though it may
have contained as much human nature as a kingdom.

He wandered among snows where his breath froze upon his
beard, and beneath suns that browned his cheek almost to
the hue of the desert tribes. He heard lions roar
beyond the red light of an encampment; he beheld temples
vast enough to house the whole population of Brambleford; he
sailed on seas whose waves seemed to lift his vessel
against the stars. Yet in all places his destiny receded
before him. The heart-shaped gem proved false; the pointing hand
indicated emptiness; the three sages dwindled into impostors, mendicants, or
men of business. At first he had accused accident; afterward,
mankind; and finally himself. Still the prophecy clung to him,
as a fragrance may linger in a garment long after
the flower is dust.

At length, wearied less in limb than in spirit, Ralph
turned homeward.

It was an autumn evening when he came again within
sight of Brambleford. The sun hung low over fields of
stubble, and the woods, touched with scarlet and gold, looked
richer than the gardens of foreign princes. A mist had
begun to collect beside the river. Through it appeared the
meetinghouse steeple, the mill roof, and a cluster of moss-grown
dwellings, so little changed that Ralph might have imagined his
ten years no more than one troubled night.

There stood old Deacon Plumtree's barn, leaning somewhat farther toward
the earth, yet not fallen. There was the tavern, with
its sign newly painted but its bench occupied by the
sons of those who had formerly sat there. Cows moved
soberly along the roadside, each apparently mindful of an ancestral
right to delay travellers. The mill wheel repeated the same
deep syllable which Ralph had heard in boyhood. Even the
air had a familiar savor—wood smoke, fallen apples, damp leaves,
and that indescribable breath of cultivated soil which no wilderness
can imitate.

"The world is wide," thought Ralph, "yet this handful of
houses hath somehow survived without it."

He did not smile. There was more melancholy than mirth
in the reflection; for the village had remained, while the
hopeful youth who went forth from it had vanished utterly.

The youth returns not.

In his stead came a man with foreign dust upon
his cloak, streaks of gray at his temples, and the
habit of gazing past whatever was near. A few villagers
recognized him; more stared without recognition. Children, to whom he
was merely a dark stranger, ceased their play as he
approached. Ralph felt, with a sudden chill, that ten years
may be a small portion of eternity, but are a
considerable fragment of mortal life.

His mother's cottage stood beyond the village, at the foot
of a low hill. When he saw its roof among
the apple trees, his composure deserted him. The gate hung
by the same obstinate hinge; the path was bordered by
the same box shrubs; and beside the millstone doorstep lay
a broom, as though his mother had but that moment
gone indoors. He lifted the latch.

The old woman sat knitting by the hearth. Her hair,
once iron-gray, was now white, and the hands that held
the needles trembled. Yet she knew his footfall before she
raised her eyes.

"Ralph!"

That cry accomplished what storms, dangers, and disappointments had failed
to do. The wanderer bowed his face upon her shoulder
and wept.

"My son," she murmured, patting his cheek as if he
were still a boy, "you are thin, and your coat
is damp. Sit nearer the fire. I knew you would
come when the Lord had finished showing you whatever it
was you went to see."

Here was a theology which Ralph's sages might have envied,
though they would doubtless have expressed it in more obscure
terms.

For several days he remained beneath his mother's roof. She
asked few questions respecting his adventures, being chiefly concerned whether
he had eaten regularly, whether the eastern fevers had injured
his constitution, and whether his boots admitted water. Ralph, on
the other hand, walked about the homestead in a reverie.
Every object recalled some forgotten portion of himself. The well
curb bore the mark of his boyish hatchet; his initials
were cut upon the barn door; and in the loft
he found a wooden sword with which he had conquered
kingdoms before destiny had required him to travel farther than
the hayfield.

One afternoon, while his mother was engaged in gathering late
herbs, Ralph wandered to the boundary of the farm. There
stood the ancient oak beneath which the vision had visited
him. Lightning had shorn away half its crown, and decay
had entered the trunk; yet one vast limb still stretched
over the wall.

Upon the lower side of that limb a singular excrescence
had grown. It resembled a human hand—the wrist emerging from
the bark, the knuckles bent, and one wooden finger pointing
steadfastly toward the ground.

Ralph stopped.

The resemblance was so exact, the gesture so imperative, that
for an instant the ten intervening years were annihilated. Again
he saw the brown hand of the second sage; again
he heard the command to seek his treasure where Fate
should indicate. Beneath the wooden finger the soil was black
and deep. On the trunk, immediately above, some village idler
had carved a word. Moss filled the ancient letters, but
Ralph, brushing it aside, read:

EFFODE.

His heart beat violently. Could the appointed hour have come?
Had Providence withheld the treasure until his return? Perhaps some
forgotten settler, scholar enough to know the Latin command, had
concealed his gold beneath the oak. Perhaps the hand itself
had grown to mark the spot. Ralph hurried to the
shed, found a spade, and returned.

He had driven the blade but once into the earth
when his mother's voice sounded behind him.

"Why, Ralph, what are you doing?"

"I am digging," he answered, almost sternly, as though no
further explanation could be needed.

"So I see. But why?"

"There may be treasure here."

His mother looked at the oak, the carved word, and
the hand-shaped growth. Then she looked at Ralph. A smile,
tender but a little sorrowful, passed over her face.

"Your father made those letters," said she. "He had lately
learned the word from Parson Willoughby and was proud of
remembering it. The queer branch put him in mind of
a hand, so he carved *Dig* above it."

"And did he dig?"

"Ay, for thirty years."

Ralph glanced at the shallow hole.

"Then he found nothing."

"Nothing?" repeated his mother. "This field paid for the cottage
and kept us fed. It sent you to school, bought
your books, and gave you the money with which you
went wandering. There is not a better piece of earth
in Brambleford, if a man will but put his strength
into it."

She stooped, crumbled a clod between her fingers, and added,
"Your father used to say the treasure in land lies
too deep for a lazy man, but near enough to
the top for an industrious one."

Then she went back toward the cottage, leaving Ralph beside
the prophetic tree.

Long did he remain there, leaning upon the spade. The
declining sun cast the oak's shadow across the field, and
within that enormous shadow Ralph's own figure seemed diminished to
its proper stature. Was this, then, the buried wealth promised
him—no cavern of jewels, no iron chest of coin, but
dark earth, patient seed, recurring harvest, and bread honestly earned?
His imagination rebelled against so plain a conclusion. Yet he
could not wholly reject it. The hand pointed; the word
commanded; and under his feet lay the acres which his
father had enriched and his own neglect had almost surrendered
to weeds.

The next morning Ralph dug again, but not for gold.
Before breakfast he had turned a long furrow along the
boundary.

The second recognition—for we follow the order in which truth
chose to instruct him—occurred three days later.

Ralph was repairing a broken wall near the road when
he beheld three elderly men approaching in solemn procession. Foremost
walked Squire Hezekiah Hawkwood, chief selectman of Brambleford, arrayed in
a three-cornered hat which had outlived several changes of fashion.
He carried a silver-headed cane and flourished it with such
authority that the roadside asters appeared almost guilty of insubordination.
Behind him came Deacon Plumtree, tall and melancholy, and Captain
Josiah Brindle, short, round, and red of face. The three
halted before Ralph.

Squire Hawkwood struck the earth with his cane.

The sound passed through Ralph like an electric influence. Three
venerable messengers! The foremost with his wand! At last, perhaps,
the summons to power!

"Master Cranfield," began the squire, expanding his chest, "we approach
you in our united, official, and representative capacity."

Ralph bowed gravely.

"The community," continued Hawkwood, "having received intelligence of your extensive
peregrinations, your acquaintance with foreign nations, climates, potentates, antiquities, customs,
languages, sciences, and other branches of useful learning, hath judged
that your extraordinary attainments should no longer remain unavailable to
the public welfare."

Captain Brindle nodded so vigorously that his hat slipped over
one eyebrow.

"The post," said Deacon Plumtree, "is one of weighty responsibility."

"Of extended empire," added the squire, smiting the road again.

Ralph's breath came quickly. Could it be that some political
convulsion had elevated Brambleford into importance? Were these men commissioned
by a hidden party? Absurd as the conjecture appears beneath
the daylight of our cooler reason, destiny had so long
been the atmosphere of Ralph's mind that any event, once
touched by prophecy, became vast and luminous.

"What authority," he asked, "would you commit to me?"

Squire Hawkwood placed both hands upon the head of his
cane.

"The winter school."

Ralph stared.

"The schoolmastership," explained Captain Brindle. "Thirty-seven scholars, unless the Phelps
twins come back from their aunt's, in which case thirty-nine."

"The former incumbent," said the deacon, "was struck by an
inkstand."

"Only on the shoulder," Captain Brindle hastened to add. "But
it discouraged him."

For one perilous moment, laughter and anger struggled within Ralph.
Was this the empire promised by a Heaven-instructed messenger? Was
he, who had conversed with ministers, crossed seas, and stood
among the ruins of conquerors, to rule over thirty-seven village
children, armed with slates, primers, and inkstands?

Squire Hawkwood perceived his hesitation and assumed a still more
imperial dignity.

"Consider, sir, that the schoolmaster exercises dominion over futurity. We
selectmen govern the town for a year at a time;
but he may govern unborn generations through the minds he
forms. The compensation is modest, owing to the public treasury
being at present afflicted; yet the authority, as I have
remarked, is extensive."

Here, out of the squire's pompous phrase, came a truth
greater than the speaker. Ralph looked beyond the three officials
toward the schoolhouse, whose low chimney rose among the pines.
He remembered kingdoms broken into dust, monuments whose inscriptions no
living man could read, and the proud faces of rulers
already forgotten. Then he imagined a child taught justice, courage,
and knowledge; that child becoming a parent; the lesson passing
onward, invisible but alive.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I accept the charge."

Squire Hawkwood struck the earth a third time, partly in
ratification and partly because a pebble had disturbed his footing.

Thus was Ralph Cranfield summoned to his empire.

Yet one sign remained; and because it concerned the heart,
it was the hardest for pride to recognize.

He had heard that Faith Egerton still lived in the
village, unmarried, in the same low-gabled cottage where he had
bidden her farewell. He had not visited her. Sometimes, while
following a foreign beauty through a palace garden, he had
remembered Faith's brown hair and tranquil eyes; but the remembrance
seemed too homely to be prophecy. Now shame withheld him.
What right had he, after pursuing a thousand phantoms, to
return and ask whether one faithful human being had waited?

On the evening after accepting the school, Ralph walked toward
the Egerton cottage. The sun had descended behind the hills,
leaving a tender brightness in the west. Smoke rose straight
from the chimneys; a boy drove cattle homeward; and from
an open window came the measured click of a loom.
No enchanted music accompanied him, unless the crickets in the
grass may be accounted a faëry orchestra.

Faith stood beneath an apple tree, gathering the last fruit
into a basket.

Ten years had changed her, though not as they had
changed Ralph. Her girlhood had ripened rather than vanished. She
was still neat, comely, and quiet; the same, yet more
fully herself. When she saw him, color rose in her
face, but she neither cried out nor turned away.

"Faith," said Ralph.

"You have come home," she answered.

The words were simple. Nevertheless, there are welcomes which open
wider kingdoms than gates of brass.

They walked beneath the trees. Ralph spoke of his travels—not
boastfully now, but as a man confesses a long error.
He told her of the jewel sought on princely and
peasant bosoms, the treasure pursued beneath tombs and ruins, and
the power expected from mysterious ambassadors. Faith listened without mockery.
Twilight deepened around them until her face became pale and
indistinct, like one of the gracious forms of his youthful
vision.

Then, as she bent to lift the basket, the last
ray of sunset touched something at her breast. A small
stone flashed within a silver rim.

It was the heart-shaped quartz.

Ralph seized her hand.

"You have kept it!"

"You asked me to wear it till you came back."

"And have you worn it all these years?"

"All these years," said Faith.

The world, which had been so vast to Ralph, suddenly
contracted to the space beneath that apple tree; yet within
the narrow circle were love, home, labor, memory, and peace—more
than all his travels had contained. He saw that the
jewel's worth lay not in its substance, but in the
constancy that had made common quartz more precious than ruby.
He saw, too, that the ideal maiden whom he had
pursued through many lands was but Faith Egerton magnified by
distance, disguised by vanity, and stripped of those familiar virtues
which alone could have made her dear.

Still the prophecy demanded its words.

"Faith," he said, and his voice trembled, "wilt thou be
the companion of my destiny?"

She looked at him long and tenderly. Perhaps she remembered
the proud youth who had gone away; perhaps she perceived
the weariness of the man who had returned. Her hand
remained in his.

"Through shadow and sunshine," she replied, "to the end."

Then Ralph bowed his head.

"The dream is fulfilled," he said. "Yet how poor was
I in wisdom! I crossed the world for what waited
beside my mother's hearth. Faith, you have interpreted my wild
and weary dream."

They were married before the first snow. Ralph cultivated the
homestead, and the earth yielded him no miraculous coffers, but
gave grain, fruit, cattle, comfort, and that independence which is
wealth without fever. He ruled the winter school, not always
peacefully—for the Phelps twins returned, and one of them possessed
a genius for rebellion—but with growing love for his little
dominion. Some of his scholars became farmers, some sailors, one
a physician, and one, it was whispered, a poet. In
each went forth a portion of the schoolmaster's influence, so
that his empire, though unrecorded upon maps, extended beyond his
knowledge.

As for Faith, the heart-shaped brooch remained her favorite ornament,
even when its silver grew dim. Squire Hawkwood continued to
govern Brambleford with his cane; Ralph's mother bustled happily between
the young household and her own; and the ancient oak,
bearing its wooden hand and mossy command, stood until a
winter tempest laid it low. Ralph fashioned part of its
timber into a desk for the schoolhouse.

Would all who cherish such wild wishes interpret their omens
as wisely! The hand that points to fortune may direct
us toward neglected duty; the summons to dominion may call
us only to the guidance of a child; and the
jewel of our destiny may shine, unregarded, upon a faithful
breast close by. We need not cast imagination away, for
without it the common earth would be poor indeed. But
let imagination learn humility. The wild and wonderful are not
always banished when a dream is brought home; sometimes they
are merely translated into labor, usefulness, and love.

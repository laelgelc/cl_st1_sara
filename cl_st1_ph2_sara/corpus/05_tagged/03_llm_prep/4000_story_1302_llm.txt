My acquaintance, Sergei Petrovitch, told me this story one winter
evening in Moscow. We had been speaking of loneliness, and,
as generally happens, speaking very wisely of a misfortune from
which neither of us was at that moment suffering.

"You gentlemen who write books," he said, "make loneliness too
dignified. You give it a pale face, a quiet room,
and rain at the window. But sometimes loneliness has red
hands, a torn skirt, a voice like a market bell,
and smells of onions."

Then he told me the following.

When I was a student, I lived in a narrow
lodging-house not far from the Sukharev Tower. My room was
opposite that of a Polish woman named Teresa. She was
tall—no, monstrous!—a mastodon in petticoats, with shoulders fit for carrying
a grand piano and a face that seemed to have
been carved out with a blunt hatchet. Her hair was
black, her eyebrows blacker, and her skin had the smoky
hue of a dove that has spent the winter in
a chimney.

Her reputation was questionable. I say "questionable" because respectable persons
prefer respectable words, even when their meaning is filthy. Men
visited her at night. She quarrelled on the staircase. Once
she threw a boot at the landlord and split a
lamp. She drank, sang mournful Polish songs, and laughed in
a way that made the window-glass complain.

I despised her.

This confession does me no honour; but at twenty we
carry our virtues like officers' epaulettes, and expect the whole
world to salute them. If Teresa met me in the
passage and said, "Good morning, Mr. Student," I bowed as
coldly as a magistrate sentencing a thief. If her skirt
brushed my coat, I wiped the spot afterward. I had
read books about humanity and therefore knew exactly which human
beings were unworthy of it.

One morning there came a knock at my door.

"Who's there?"

"It is me, Mr. Student."

I knew the voice and frowned.

"What d' ye want?"

Teresa entered sideways, though the doorway was broad enough for
an ordinary Christian. Her hair was untidy, and beneath one
eye lay a bluish mark. She folded her enormous hands
upon her breast and smiled timidly.

"Sir, I ask a little service."

"No money."

"I do not ask money."

"What then?"

She shifted from one foot to the other. The floor
creaked.

"You write letters, Mr. Student?"

"Occasionally. That is one of the uses of literacy."

"Ah! You are angry already. Do not be angry. I
only ask—write for me one letter."

"To whom?"

"To Boles."

"Who is Boles?"

"My sweetheart," she said, and blushed.

Phew! The mountain had a sweetheart. I imagined some Polish
Hercules, broad as a wardrobe, waiting in a forest with
an axe over his shoulder.

"Where does this Boles live?"

"In Warsaw. No—in another place. But the letter goes to
Warsaw."

"What am I to write?"

At once her coarse face grew soft and foolish.

"Write: 'My beloved Boles, my soul, my golden falcon—'"

"Not a dove?"

She did not understand my mockery.

"No, sir. He is a falcon. And write: 'Why have
you forgotten your Teresa? Your poor, sorrowing little dove waits
and weeps. She has no joy without you. Come soon,
or write, for your words are bread to her heart.'"

I looked at the creature. A sorrowing little dove! She
could have strangled a watchman with one hand.

"Well, well! Anything more?"

"Write that I am faithful. Write that Moscow is cold.
Write—May the Mother of God protect thee, my Boles. Write
that Teresa kisses him a thousand times."

I wrote the letter, scarcely concealing my amusement. She listened
with parted lips while I read it aloud; then she
took the paper carefully, as though it were an icon.

"You have written it beautifully, Mr. Student. God repay you."

"See that Boles does."

She went away happy. I laughed, lit a cigarette, and
forgot the matter.

A fortnight later she knocked again.

"Well?"

"Mr. Student, I have another service."

"Another letter to your falcon?"

"No. From him."

"From whom?"

"From Boles."

She produced a clean sheet of paper and laid it
on my table.

"He has sent you a blank letter?"

She twisted the corner of her apron.

"No, sir. You must write what he says."

I stared at her.

"Am I also Boles now?"

"You write like him."

"How do you know how he writes?"

Teresa's eyes moved toward the window.

"He writes lovingly."

"Then bring me his letter."

"It is lost."

"Conveniently?"

"Yes—no—I mean, unfortunately."

I pushed back my chair.

"Deuce take you! What foolish game is this? First I
write from you to Boles, and now I must write
from Boles to you. Do you take me for an
idiot?"

"No, Mr. Student."

"For a professional deceiver, then? Is this letter to be
shown to someone? The police? A landlord? One of your
drunken friends?"

"No, no!"

Her fear inflamed my righteousness. There is no courage cheaper
than that which attacks the already ashamed.

"Perhaps there is no Boles at all," I said. "Perhaps
there is no sweetheart. Perhaps the whole thing is one
of your dirty tricks."

She went very still.

"Well? Speak!"

"There's no Boles," she whispered.

"What?"

"There is no Boles."

"And the letter?"

"There is no letter."

"And Teresa—the Teresa to whom he writes?"

She gave a strange smile.

"No Teresa either. Not that Teresa. Not the one he
loves."

For a moment I thought she was drunk.

"What nonsense! You are Teresa."

"I am this Teresa." She struck her breast with her
fist. "But his Teresa—his little dove—she is not. And he
is not. There's no Boles. He doesn't exist."

She sat down without asking leave. Her great body collapsed
upon the chair, and she covered her face.

"I made him," she said through her fingers. "Is that
a great sin, sir?"

I said nothing.

"You see, Mr. Student, there is nobody. Nobody at all.
My mother is dead. My sister—who knows where? Men come,
men go. They say, 'Teresa, bring vodka; Teresa, don't shout;
Teresa, get out.' Nobody says, 'Teresa, I wait for you.'
Nobody asks if I am cold. Nobody writes."

She raised her face. Tears moved slowly down its rough
planes.

"So I thought: let there be Boles. Somewhere there must
be one man who remembers me. I ask you to
write my letter, then I keep it a few days.
I think he reads it. Perhaps he kisses it. Then
you write his answer, and I hear what he says
to me. Only a few words: 'My Teresa, be patient.
My dear heart, I have not forgotten thee.' What harm
is there? The paper lies on my table, and I
am not alone."

She began to sob—not delicately, but with deep roaring breaths
that shook her shoulders.

"I know I am ugly. I know what people call
me. But when the letter is read, I close my
eyes. Then I am not this. I am his sorrowing
little dove. Laugh, Mr. Student. You laughed before."

But I could not laugh.

Before me sat Teresa, whom I had regarded as a
lump of corrupt flesh, and within that grotesque prison a
human soul had been starving so fiercely that it had
created another soul to feed it. Boles had never lived;
yet he had shown more tenderness than all the living
men around her. And I, a student, an educated fellow,
a lover of noble ideas, had tried to rob her
even of that phantom.

I felt myself a blockhead—worse, an impeccably virtuous blockhead.

"Teresa," I said, "what would Boles write?"

She lowered her hands.

"Will you do it?"

"Yes."

"Truly?"

"Truly. Sit there. Only don't shake the table."

Then I wrote:

"My beloved Teresa, my faithful little dove, do not think
I have forgotten thee. Every day I remember thy kind
heart, and every night I pray that sorrow may pass
from thee. Though the road is long, my thoughts travel
it. Be strong. Boles loves thee."

"Read it again," she said.

I read it.

"Again, please, sir."

I read it a second time. Teresa listened with her
eyes shut. Tears ran down her cheeks, but she smiled.

"Boles speaks beautifully," she said.

"An educated rascal," I replied, for I was near tears
myself and ashamed of them.

From thenceforth I became secretary to two persons, neither of
whom could write and one of whom did not exist.
Twice a week Teresa came to me. On Tuesdays we
wrote to Boles; on Fridays Boles replied. Their correspondence grew.
He worried about her cough, advised her not to drink
too much, remembered feast days, described imaginary streets, imaginary work,
imaginary hopes. Teresa sent him kisses, scoldings, prayers, and news
of the weather.

Sometimes she objected to my composition.

"No, Mr. Student, Boles would not say 'esteem.' That is
a cold word."

"What would he say?"

"He would say, 'I hold thee against my heart.'"

So I altered it. She knew Boles better than I
did.

In payment she mended my shirts, darned my socks, sewed
buttons upon my coat, and once scrubbed my room while
I was at lectures. We never spoke openly of our
bargain. I gave her words, and she answered with those
red, capable hands. Between us stood the invisible Boles, solemnly
performing the duties which society had declined.

This continued for three months.

Then one evening her door was open and her room
empty. The landlord told me she had been arrested after
a tavern quarrel. There had been a broken bottle, a
wounded man, policemen, much shouting. Whether she was sent to
prison or expelled from Moscow, I never learned.

On my table lay a shirt she had mended. The
needle was still caught in the cuff, and beside it
was the last letter from Boles, waiting to be read.

Sergei Petrovitch stopped. He shook the ash from his cigarette
and watched it fall.

"That," he said, "was the first time I understood loneliness."

We are wonderfully proud of our judgment. We divide mankind
into the respectable and the fallen, as if we ourselves
stood upright by some strength of our own. We point
out the path of righteousness to those whom hunger, brutality,
and abandonment have already pushed into the mud; and if
they cannot climb out, we congratulate ourselves upon having clean
boots.

But what do we give them? A sermon? A cold
glance? A name spoken with disgust?

Teresa asked only for two letters a week.

We, who possess families, friends, books, memories, and a hundred
doors at which we may knock, call such inventions madness.
Yet which of us could live if no voice, real
or imagined, ever said, "I remember thee"? We wrap ourselves
in the rags of our virtues and mistake them for
royal garments. Then some poor woman, blackened by life and
ridiculous in her longing, teaches us charity.

And still we dare to call her fallen—as though we
had not helped to push her down.

### The Island of Departing Shadows

*« La solitude est la patrie des âmes qui écoutent.
»*—Solitude is the native country of listening souls. Thus writes,
if my memory be not treacherous, some forgotten French moralist;
yet the sentence, felicitous as it appears, contains but half
a truth. For is it the soul that listens in
solitude—or is solitude itself the listener? Music demands companionship, perhaps,
because its delight is multiplied by sympathy; the cadence that
moves one bosom acquires, from a second bosom similarly moved,
a certain sensible confirmation. But natural Beauty—Beauty unarranged, unmeasured, and
unviolated by man—shrinks, I have always believed, from human witnesses
in the plural. A landscape beheld by two is already
divided. Conversation profanes it; admiration uttered becomes applause; and applause,
whether before a singer or a sunset, implies the stage.

Therefore I have loved to wander alone—not from misanthropy, as
superficial persons have alleged, but from reverence. When I stand
solitary beneath ancient trees, I feel no solitude. The innumerable
leaves possess cognizance; the mosses dream; the waters, in their
voiceless dialect, converse with the shores. Shall we deny consciousness
to a forest because its thoughts require centuries? Shall we
call a mountain dead because its pulse is slower than
our own? The minuteness of man's temporal measure is the
source of his arrogance. To an ephemeron, an oak is
eternal; to a star, the oak and the ephemeron perish
in one instant. Life may be nothing more than relation—and,
if so, what atom is lifeless beneath the regard of
God?

Such speculations—fantastic, doubtless, to the arithmetical understanding—were habitual with me
during a season of solitary travel through a region whose
name I shall not disclose. I had gone thither with
neither guide nor destination, following those obscure roads which appear
less to conduct the traveler than to escape from him.
It was late in autumn, although the weather retained the
voluptuous languor of summer; and on the evening of which
I speak, I had wandered far beyond every habitation, into
a valley surrounded by low, solemn mountains.

The sun had declined until his rays, entering almost horizontally
between the summits, seemed tangible bars of crimson gold. A
river, broad but noiseless, wound through the valley. Its surface
was so smooth that the clouds beneath appeared more substantial
than those above; and I entertained, for one bewildered moment,
the fancy that Heaven lay drowned at my feet, while
the actual sky had become merely its pale and imperfect
remembrance.

Following the bank, I arrived at a recess formed by
two gigantic sycamores. Their roots, black and contorted, clasped the
earth like the fingers of buried Titans. Between them was
a couch of moss, upon which I threw myself; for
fatigue, mingled with that spiritual drowsiness which exquisite scenery sometimes
induces, had rendered motion intolerable. From this natural alcove I
looked directly upon a small island, nearly circular, lying in
the middle of the stream.

Never had I seen so much of Paradise confined within
so little space.

The western half of the island burned beneath the sunset.
Slender poplars lifted their leaves into the radiance, and every
leaf became a separate flame. Roses, late violets, yellow asphodel,
and flowers unknown to botany crowded the margin in a
luminous confusion. Their colors were repeated in the water—not mirrored
merely, but deepened—as though the river preserved beneath its bosom
a richer garden than that permitted to mortal sight. Birds
moved among the branches, yet sang no note. Golden insects
floated over the blossoms. Even the stones seemed warm with
inward felicity; and the little waves, approaching that enchanted shore,
broke into smiles.

But the eastern portion exhibited a character wholly opposed, though
scarcely less beautiful. There grew the cypress, the yew, and
trees whose names I could not determine—thin, melancholy forms, clothed
in leaves of so deep a green that they appeared
black. No flower relieved the soil. Long grasses drooped like
hair upon a sepulchre. The shadow of the mountain had
already covered this half of the island, and the adjacent
water, receiving no reflection from the sky, resembled ebony newly
polished. It was impossible to regard that region without thinking
of graves; yet there was nothing terrible in it. Its
sorrow was too august for fear. It possessed the peacefulness,
not of sleep, but of that dissolution after which sleep
is no longer necessary.

Thus the island seemed divided between two sovereign Powers—Joy and
Grief, Youth and Memory, the Bridal and the Tomb. Nevertheless,
the division was not absolute. The roots of the radiant
trees penetrated the sombre earth; the dark boughs extended, here
and there, into the gold; and along the uncertain frontier
light and shadow embraced with the intimacy of sisters. I
thought then that all beauty must contain the prophecy of
its extinction. Without Death, could Beauty move us? Were the
rose immortal, should we stoop to inhale it?

While these reflections occupied me, the sun sank lower, and
a vapor arose from the river—not broadly, but in delicate
filaments which ascended, wavered, and vanished. My eyelids were half
closed. I had reached that ambiguous condition wherein Fancy, released
from the tyranny of judgment, accepts the visible world as
raw material and refashions it according to her secret laws.

A fragment of bark drifted from behind the island.

At first I regarded it merely as bark. Then it
assumed the outline of a shallop; then the shallop became
a canoe—minute, fragile, and curiously fashioned, as though carved from
one enormous pearl. Within it sat a female figure, scarcely
larger than a child, yet possessing none of childhood's disproportions.
Her wings—if wings indeed they were—lay folded behind her in
translucent curves. Her hair floated loose, and caught the sunset
so completely that her head appeared surrounded by a soft
conflagration.

She held no visible paddle. Nevertheless, the canoe moved, urged
by the mere phantom of an oar, and proceeded slowly
along the western shore.

Her countenance was turned toward the flowers. She smiled; and,
although no sound traversed the water, I imagined that she
sang. The poplars inclined as she passed. The insects gathered
above her like sparks from an unseen censer. She seemed
not a creature inhabiting the landscape, but the Spirit by
whose gladness its blossoms had been created.

Presently the canoe reached the eastern boundary.

There the Fay—for by this name my revery knew her—ceased
to smile. She looked back once upon the golden trees;
then, without resistance, entered the shadow.

A singular thing occurred. Her own shadow, which had followed
the canoe distinctly through the illuminated water, detached itself at
the instant of transition. For a moment it trembled beside
her; then the black river drew it downward, and it
disappeared, as ink disappears in ink. The Fay continued beneath
the cypresses, pale but visible. Their branches bent over her;
the dark water closed behind her without a ripple; and
I thought her figure wore an expression of patient, immeasurable
sorrow.

She passed around the eastern extremity and vanished from my
sight.

A minute elapsed—perhaps an hour; for time, in such conditions,
has no arithmetic. Then the pearl-like canoe reappeared at the
western edge. Again the flowers brightened. Again the leaves became
flame. Yet the Fay's smile was less joyous than before.
She looked upon the roses with the tenderness of one
revisiting a home already lost, and her hand—exquisitely white—rested upon
her breast.

Once more she completed the radiant semicircle; once more she
entered the gloom; once more a shadow separated from her
and was absorbed by the ebony water.

"Each circuit," I murmured, "is a year."

The fancy, having pronounced its law, compelled all things to
obey it.

Again and again the canoe returned. Again and again the
western shore received the voyager into its declining festival; again
and again the eastern wood accepted her as a mourner.
At every revolution some change became apparent. Her hair lost
a portion of its fire. Her wings grew less transparent,
or more—so faint were they that at times I saw
the cypress trunks through them. Her posture, at first erect,
became weary. She no longer extended her hand toward the
flowers. She no longer seemed to sing.

And each time she crossed from light into darkness, another
shadow fell from her.

How many shadows can one being possess? As many, perhaps,
as there are years in life—each year a subtler body,
each experience a duplicate of the soul, cast off and
abandoned in the stream of oblivion. We say that the
past is gone; but may it not remain somewhere, accumulated
in invisible depths? Are there not rivers whose darkness is
composed entirely of the shadows of departed hours?

The sun now touched the mountains. The golden district narrowed.
Crimson withdrew from the flowers, lingering longest upon a single
tall lily at the island's point. The Fay emerged yet
again, but so indistinctly that I distinguished her from the
vapor only by the sorrowful inclination of her head. The
canoe itself seemed no longer pearl, but the hollow whiteness
of a shell found upon a grave.

Slowly she moved through the last illumination.

No bird stirred. The insects had vanished. The roses folded
their petals, not with the gentle motion of flowers yielding
to night, but with the solemnity of hands closed in
prayer. On the opposite bank, the shadows of the sycamores
separated themselves from the trunks that gave them birth and
advanced across the river. All Nature appeared to hold her
breath, awaiting some inevitable word.

At the boundary of the cypresses, the Fay paused.

She turned toward the sun, of which only a narrow
and intolerable splendor remained above the mountain. Her face was
calm. Sorrow had passed beyond grief into that divine tranquillity
which painters vainly seek upon the brows of martyrs. For
one instant she became perfectly distinct—more distinct than the island,
the river, or my own hands before me. I saw
her eyes; they were dark and filled, not with tears,
but with the memory of tears.

Then the sun sank to his slumbers.

The Fay entered the eastern gloom.

Her final shadow did not fall away. Rather, the darkness
rose to meet her—rose like a silent wave, encompassing canoe,
wings, and wan countenance. I watched for her reappearance beyond
the cypresses. The river moved onward. The vapor thickened. One
star opened above the valley, regarding its image below with
cold and sleepless wonder.

But the canoe did not return.

Long afterward I remained beneath the sycamores, uncertain whether I
had witnessed a dissolution or merely dreamed an emblem. Had
a Fay died within that island's shade? Had the landscape,
conscious of my meditation, shaped from mist and sunset a
hieroglyph of fallen mortality? Or had my own soul projected
upon the water some portion of itself—some delicate hope condemned
to circle between delight and grief until darkness claimed it?

I cannot answer. At midnight I departed, and I have
never found the valley again. Yet often, when a year
concludes, I remember the pearl canoe and the shadows surrendered
one by one. Then I ask whether we, too, travel
always about a little island—through light that diminishes, through gloom
that deepens—returning after every sorrow less substantial than before.

And sometimes, in the final silence before sleep, I seem
to hear a noiseless oar upon black water.

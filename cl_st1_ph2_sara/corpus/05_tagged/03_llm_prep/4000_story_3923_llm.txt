# Passing the Love of Woman

I make no apology for this story being a trifle
blood-and-thundery. Blood was common enough in Arizona in those days,
and thunder, though less dependable, made up for its infrequency
by being generally accompanied by enough rain to drown a
government mule. Besides, if a man objects to shootings, horseflesh,
dry arroyas and moral sentiments scratched upon clay, he may
stop here and read the market reports.

I knew Tom Ransome. That is my warrant for telling
it.

He was an ordinary cow-puncher—which means, according to those who
have never known one, that he could sit a horse,
swear at cattle and entertain no feeling higher than hunger.
He was long in the leg, narrow in the hip,
slow in speech and had a face browned by weather
until his eyes looked surprisingly pale in it. He had
once loved a girl in Kansas. She married a man
who owned a hardware store, three Sunday suits and, I
believe, a bath with nickel taps. Tom thereafter affected contempt
for settled occupations, furniture and women with light-colored hair.

Yet one phrase troubled him.

He had heard it from a preacher in Abilene, on
one of those unfortunate Sundays when rain closes the saloons
and drives homeless cowboys into church.

"Greater love hath no man," the preacher had said, "than
that he lay down his life for his friend. It
is a love passing the love of woman."

The preacher may have joined two texts in an unauthorized
fashion. Preachers, like cowboys, sometimes improve a thing in the
telling. Tom remembered only the last six words.

"Passing the love of woman," he would mutter, cutting the
phrase upon a bit of pine with his pocketknife. "Passing
the love of woman."

He did not believe it. Or, rather, he was afraid
he did.

His partner, Alfred Dacey, usually told him to shut up.

Alfred was short, square and entirely without poetry. He had
a mustache of so sandy a color that it became
invisible at ten paces, and legs which, though excellent for
gripping a saddle, had been distributed with economy. He spoke
little because he had learned early that silence causes other
men to credit you with ideas you have never had.
He and Tom had ridden together through three round-ups, two
stampedes, a winter night-herd in New Mexico and one disastrous
attempt at operating a livery stable. Each knew the other's
faults with the accuracy of a wife and tolerated them
with considerably less discussion.

In the year of which I speak they were working
for the San Jacinto outfit, south of Tucson. A dispute
had arisen over wages with a band of Mexican vaqueros
who had been hired for the spring drive. The dispute
was not, at first, serious. Nobody had been killed; consequently
it was described as a misunderstanding. The owner paid the
men off and asked Tom and Alfred to escort them
across forty miles of desert to a trail leading south,
partly as guides and partly to prevent their returning to
argue.

There were eleven vaqueros. They were angry, armed and badly
paid, which is a combination no sensible man escorts anywhere.
Their leader, Esteban Vela, was a lean fellow with silver
on his bridle and manners polished enough to show that
he was dangerous. His younger brother José had no manners
and made up the deficiency with a knife.

Black Sam went along as cook.

Sam's proper name was Samuel Pickett, but nobody on the
range used proper names except sheriffs and widows. He was
a broad, elderly man whose great gift was the production
of edible biscuits under conditions that would have discouraged chemistry
itself. He drove the chuck wagon, addressed the mules as
"ladies," and regarded every delay in supper as a personal
attack upon civilization.

On the second evening clouds climbed over the western hills
and deposited seven minutes of rain precisely where Sam had
stacked his firewood.

This was serious.

Sam crouched over a reluctant flame, his elbows on his
knees and his face thrust forward in an attitude of
profound grief. Smoke rolled into his eyes. The bacon remained
cold. The coffee pot, three skillets, a Dutch oven, a
flour sack, a meat fork, two tin cups and a
collection of spoons lay around him as though the wagon
had burst.

The vaqueros sat apart beneath their blankets, smoking and talking
in low voices. Tom watched them. Alfred watched Tom.

"Supper'll be late," said Sam.

"No!" Alfred exclaimed, with unusual animation.

Sam looked wounded.

"Wet wood," he explained. "Even the Almighty took six days,
an' He had dry kindlin'."

José called something from the darkness. Several men laughed.

Sam did not understand the words, but he understood laughter
directed toward a cook whose fire would not burn. He
straightened.

"Tell that young rooster," he said to Tom, "that if
he wants his beans before sunup, he can come over
here an' persuade this timber."

"Let it go, Sam."

"I been lettin' it go. It's the fire won't."

José called again. This time Sam caught his own name
and an imitation of his anxious blowing at the coals.
He picked up the meat fork and pointed it.

"Laugh all you got to," he said. "But when you
git hungry, don't come—"

José rose with the quickness of a cat. He crossed
the strip of firelight in three steps and struck once.

Sam made no dramatic sound. The fork fell. He sat
down heavily among his pans, one hand pressed to his
side, and looked with astonishment at the fire which had
given him so much trouble. Then he rolled forward, scattering
spoons into the ashes.

Tom's six-shooter cleared leather.

José turned, knife red in his hand.

Tom fired once.

The young vaquero went backward into the dark.

For perhaps two seconds nobody moved. The little flame found
a shaving, crept along it, and began at last to
burn properly.

Then Esteban reached for his rifle.

"Alfred," said Tom.

They moved together. Alfred fired into the ground before Esteban's
boots, Tom kicked the nearest saddle toward the embers, and
both men sprang for their horses. There was no opportunity
for explanations, and explanations would in any case have had
a poor chance against ten bereaved and hostile vaqueros.

They went out of camp at a run.

Shots followed them. One clipped the brim from Alfred's hat.
Another struck the wagon with a sound like an axe
blow. The mules broke their tether and went ghosting across
the flats, harness chains flashing in the last of the
light.

Tom and Alfred rode north.

Now it is necessary to explain the country, though I
know there are persons who prefer their geography in small
doses and their gunfights uninterrupted.

Long before men invented wages, revolvers or disputes concerning either,
a river had run across that region. It must have
been a magnificent river, broad enough to carry whole forests
and patient enough to spend a thousand centuries eating its
way through clay and stone. Then the earth lifted somewhere,
or settled somewhere else; the water turned aside, the forests
vanished, and the river left behind a dry bed winding
beneath bluffs the color of old blood.

Wind and sudden rain continued the labor. They cut side
gullies, scooped caves, raised shelves and towers, and made several
places where a man could enter with a horse but
could not leave except as a spirit. One narrow ravine,
however, passed clean through the western bluff. It was scarcely
twenty feet wide at the mouth and narrower within, but
beyond it lay open desert and, fifteen miles farther, the
Bar-M ranch.

Tom knew that cut. So did Esteban.

The sunset flattened itself in crimson strips beneath the clouds.
Ahead, the bluff ran black against it, without break except
for the little notch of the ravine. Behind came the
hard drumming of pursuit.

"They're gainin'," Alfred said.

"We got the better horses."

"They got more of 'em."

This was Alfred's manner of pointing out that Esteban's men
could ride without regard for preserving their mounts, since they
had spare animals, whereas Tom and Alfred each possessed only
one horse and a strong desire to keep it under
him.

They bent low. Their slickers flapped. Alfred lost his hat
altogether and did not go back for it.

At the ravine mouth a rifle cracked.

Tom's horse stumbled, recovered, and plunged between the clay walls.
Alfred followed. Bullets struck the bluff and threw out white
puffs. The sound of every shot doubled in the narrow
place until it seemed that the dead river itself had
taken up firearms.

Tom's bay made thirty yards more. Then its hindquarters sagged.

"Hit," Tom said.

They were forced to stop where the ravine narrowed beside
a shelf of crumbling clay. The horse's right hind leg
was shattered above the hock. It tried to rise, sweating
and trembling.

Alfred dismounted, placed his revolver behind its ear and fired.

The animal dropped.

They pulled the body crosswise as far as they could.
Tom's saddle came off. Alfred snatched the rifle from its
scabbard, and both men crouched behind the dead horse as
the first vaqueros entered the cut.

There is little to say about the next minute, because
action of that sort is plain.

A man appeared beside the wall. Tom fired. The man
fell.

Two more rushed in. Alfred shot one through the shoulder.
The other dropped behind a boulder.

Esteban shouted from outside. Rifles answered from the heights, but
the angle was bad and the twilight poorer.

Tom fired at a hat. The hat vanished.

Then the attack stopped.

Smoke drifted between the walls. The dead horse jerked once
beneath their arms and was still. Somewhere down the ravine
an injured man groaned steadily, with the regularity of a
saw.

Alfred opened his rifle's loading gate.

"Nine," he said.

"Cartridges?"

"Men."

"Started with ten."

"José makes eleven."

"Oh."

They counted ammunition. Between them they had forty-three rifle cartridges
and seventeen for the six-shooters. That sounds plentiful to a
person sitting safely at a table. It sounds less plentiful
when nine men are waiting around a corner to learn
how fast you can spend it.

Night came.

The clay walls turned gray under a rising moon. Esteban's
men fired occasionally to discourage movement. Tom and Alfred replied
only when a target showed. Once a vaquero tried to
crawl near beneath a blanket dusted to match the ground.
Alfred let him come within twenty yards before shooting.

"Eight," he said.

Tom peered over the horse.

"Maybe."

"Seven, then, if you're particular."

"I ain't."

An hour passed. Their water bottle had been lost with
Tom's saddle gear. Alfred had one swallow in a canteen.
They divided it without argument and each accused the other
of taking too little.

At midnight they heard hoofbeats moving away.

"Goin' for more men," Alfred said.

"And rifles."

"And somebody who knows the trail atop this bluff."

The walls rose perhaps sixty feet. They leaned outward on
the eastern side and were nearly sheer on the west.
From above, a man could shoot directly behind the barricade.
Tom studied the pale clay, the dark ledges, the narrow
seams made by water.

"Dawn?" he asked.

"Likely."

"They'll be over us before that."

"Likely."

Tom nodded. Alfred examined the cliff.

There was one possible climb. A crack ran upward behind
them, widening in places to afford a grip, narrowing elsewhere
until a climber would have to trust his fingers and
the toes of his boots. No large man could pass
the first shoulder. Alfred, being small and built with none
of the ornamental excess that tailors value, might.

If he reached the top, he could cross the broken
country westward, descend beyond the bluff and run to the
Bar-M. Fifteen miles. He would have to leave his boots,
gun belt, chaparajos and possibly most of his skin upon
the way.

The other man would remain behind.

Neither mentioned this immediately. They discussed the route, the time,
the ammunition, the moon and the probability that the vaqueros
would rush as soon as they heard scraping on the
wall. Their voices were quiet. It was merely a difficult
division of labor.

At last Alfred said, "You climb."

"Can't fit."

"You could."

"I got shoulders."

"So have I."

"Not noticeable."

Alfred looked at him.

"Yo' laigs is longer," he said. "You can make the
ranch faster."

"Your carcass is narrower. You can make the top."

"Tom—"

"Cut for it."

Tom produced a greasy deck of cards from his coat
pocket. He carried them always, not because he gambled well
but because he disliked being without a method for losing
money. The cards were bent and stained from many camps.

"High stays," he said.

"High goes."

"Man drawin' high wins his choice. Winner stays."

Alfred considered. A fair chance was a thing he understood.
He nodded.

Tom shuffled behind the dead horse.

It should be said that Tom had performed card tricks
in Dodge City during a period of unemployment. He could
bring an ace to the top, deal himself a full
house or cause the jack of diamonds to appear wherever
it would do the most harm. Alfred knew none of
this, having always supposed Tom too honest to cheat a
friend and too clumsy to cheat anyone else.

Tom spread the cards face down across the ground.

Alfred drew the nine of clubs.

Tom reached for a card, hesitated, then turned up the
jack of diamonds.

"Jack takes it," he said.

Alfred stared at the two cards. The moon made the
diamond look black.

"Again."

"One cut."

"We didn't say."

"We always play one cut."

Alfred's jaw tightened. "You're a fool."

"Frequently."

"You can run farther."

"You can climb."

"I could stay."

"Lost the draw."

No heroics, you see. No embracing, no speeches about mothers
or flags. They argued rules.

A shot struck the horse and sent a warm, unpleasant
tremor through their shelter.

Tom handed over his pocketknife, half the cartridges and his
socks.

"What for?"

"Your feet."

"I got socks."

"Then you got four. Millionaires run better."

Alfred removed his boots and chaparajos. Without them he looked
smaller than ever, a compact and somewhat embarrassed figure in
long underwear, torn trousers and a shirt dark with sweat.
A cowboy on horseback may suggest the centaur of classical
mythology; set the same cowboy running barefoot through cactus country
and classical mythology has less to say.

Tom boosted him to the first ledge.

Alfred climbed.

Clay crumbled beneath his toes. He wedged a shoulder into
the crack, reached for a knob and pulled himself upward.
Tom began firing down the ravine—slowly at first, then rapidly,
shifting position and voice so that the defenders might seem
more numerous.

"Come on!" he shouted. "We got daylight to burn!"

The vaqueros answered. Bullets chopped clay from the walls.

Alfred gained ten feet. Then twenty.

At the narrow shoulder he stuck. His shirt caught. He
tore it free and left a strip hanging like a
flag.

Below him Tom fired again.

A vaquero rushed the barricade. Tom shot him. Another dragged
the body aside and fired from one knee. His bullet
cut Tom's sleeve. Tom dropped behind the horse, reloaded, rose
and drove the attackers back.

Alfred reached the lip. For one terrible instant he hung
with his elbows over empty space and his feet clawing
loose clay. Then he rolled onto the plateau.

He looked down.

Tom glanced up and grinned.

"Mosey," he said.

Alfred moseyed.

He crossed the high ground beneath the moon, bent almost
double where the ridges exposed him. Once a bullet from
below hummed past, though whether it had been fired at
him or Tom he could not tell. At the western
edge he slid through gravel into a wash and began
to run.

Fifteen miles is not far on a map. It is
far in stocking feet.

Alfred ran over stone that cut through his socks, through
sand that dragged at his ankles, through thorn and greasewood
and dry channels whose banks had to be climbed on
hands and knees. His cartridge belt slapped his hip until
he threw it away. The rifle became too heavy, then
impossibly heavy, but he kept it because arriving unarmed at
a ranch in Arizona was liable to create delay.

Behind him the bluff flickered with gunfire.

Tom held the ravine.

He fired when they charged and waited when they did
not. He shifted cartridges from one pocket to another. He
listened to men whispering beyond the bend and understood enough
Spanish to know that Esteban expected help before dawn.

Between attacks, Tom found Alfred's nine of clubs and his
own jack of diamonds upon the ground. He picked up
the jack, looked at it and laughed once.

Then, because waiting is harder than shooting, he used his
knife upon a slab of clay broken from the wall.

He had always carved when troubled. Horses' heads on cottonwood,
initials on bunkhouse doors, the face of the Kansas girl
upon a bit of pine—though he afterward maintained it was
meant for a coyote. Now the old preacher's words came
back.

Passing the love of woman.

He had spent years arguing with them. A woman's love,
as he knew it, might be exchanged for a hardware
store and nickel taps. Friendship asked fewer questions. It rode
night-herd when you were sick. It gave you the dry
blanket. It shot your ruined horse. It called you a
fool and obeyed a rigged card draw because rules were
rules.

Tom scratched until the knife point broke.

Far away, Alfred fell.

He lay with his face against cold sand and heard
his heart striking the earth. His feet were bleeding. One
knee would no longer straighten properly. The Bar-M windmill showed
against the paling sky, impossibly thin and distant.

He rose and ran again.

At the ranch the dogs began barking before any man
saw him. A hand named Lew Parsons came out with
a shotgun and found a hatless little scarecrow staggering through
the gate in four socks, one of which had worked
halfway up his calf.

"Ravine," Alfred said.

Then he fell under the water trough.

They poured coffee into him, wrapped his feet, saddled eight
men and wasted twelve minutes finding out what he meant.
Alfred cursed them for every one of those minutes. He
demanded a horse, but when they lifted him upright his
legs folded. They put him in a buckboard and drove
behind the riders.

The sun rose.

They reached the bluff three hours later.

The vaqueros were gone.

So were Tom's revolver, boots, hat, belt buckle and the
few dollars he had carried. The dead horse had been
hacked aside. Cartridge shells littered the ravine. Blood marked the
clay at three separate places, and the tracks showed that
the final rush had cost Esteban more men.

Tom lay near the foot of Alfred's climb.

He had been shot several times. What had been done
afterward need not be told. Men who are ashamed of
the price they paid for victory sometimes take revenge upon
the dead. It does not reduce the price.

Alfred climbed from the buckboard without help. He went to
Tom, knelt and remained there so long that the rescue
party moved away.

They found the jack of diamonds in Tom's coat lining.

That was how Alfred knew.

Near Tom's hand lay the clay slab. The letters were
rough, some scarcely finished, and dark where dust had settled
into the cuts. Alfred washed it with water from a
canteen.

It read:

**THERE IS A LOVE GREATER THAN THE LOVE OF A
WOMAN. A MAN MAY GIVE HIS LIFE FOR HIS FRIEND.**

Beneath that Tom had begun another line:

**PASSING THE LOVE—**

The knife had broken there, or the last attack had
come.

Alfred carried the slab back to the Bar-M. He never
spoke much of the ravine, and if anyone called Tom
a hero he became disagreeable.

"Tom cheated," he would say.

That was all.

Years later, when Alfred had a ranch of his own,
the clay slab stood on the mantel beneath a glass
case. Visitors mistook it for an Indian relic, a mineral
specimen or an unusually ugly decoration. Alfred corrected them only
if they persisted.

As for the jack of diamonds, he kept it in
his wallet until the red faded from it and the
pasteboard wore thin as cloth.

Now there are people who maintain that a common cow-puncher,
being rough in speech, awkward on foot and unacquainted with
the finer uses of table silver, is incapable of the
higher feelings. Such people may be right. People frequently are,
especially at a safe distance.

I can only say that I knew Tom Ransome.

And Tom, who doubted that there was any love passing
the love of woman, remained behind to find out.

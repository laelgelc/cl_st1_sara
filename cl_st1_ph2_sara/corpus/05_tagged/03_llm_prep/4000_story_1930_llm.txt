The Japanese Sword

The clock upon the mantelpiece had just struck nine when
James Fletcher laid down the blue paper and looked across
the library at his partner.

"There is no mistake," he said.

Trayton Burleigh, who had been standing with his back to
the low fire, shifted his pipe from one hand to
the other. The movement was deliberate, but his face had
lost its colour.

"You have said that before."

"And I shall say it again if necessary. The deficiency
is three thousand eight hundred pounds."

"You speak as though I had taken it in bank-notes
and put it in my pocket."

"I speak according to the books."

"The books!" said Burleigh, with a short laugh. "You know
as well as I do that half our transactions cannot
be judged by a clerk's columns. Money has been moved,
invested, advanced—"

"Without my knowledge."

"For the good of the firm."

"For your own speculations."

The room was a heavy, comfortable one, furnished rather as
a place of business than of repose. A great writing-table
occupied the middle; two leather chairs stood before the fire;
and against the dark paper of the walls hung several
weapons, masks, and pieces of lacquer-work brought home by Fletcher's
elder brother from the East. A green-shaded gas-jet burned over
the desk. Beyond its circle of light the corners of
the room were dim.

Burleigh looked towards the door.

In the dining-room across the passage the remains of dinner
still lay upon the table. Their housekeeper had left at
seven, as she did every evening, and the maid slept
at her mother's house owing to illness there. The two
partners were alone in the large, untidy bachelor residence, with
three empty floors above them and a narrow garden behind.

Fletcher folded the paper and placed it in his pocket.

"I will make good the loss."

Burleigh stared at him.

"You?"

"Yes."

"With what?"

"I can raise it on my property."

"You would do that?"

"The name of Fletcher and Burleigh shall not appear in
the newspapers."

A little warmth returned to Burleigh's face.

"I knew you would see reason. We have quarrelled over
words, James. Let us forget them. In six months I
may repay the whole amount."

"You misunderstand me," said Fletcher. "I shall preserve the firm,
not you."

Burleigh's smile remained, but it had become fixed.

"What do you mean?"

"You must go."

"Go?"

"From the office and from this house. To-night, if possible;
to-morrow morning at the latest."

For several seconds nothing could be heard but the ticking
of the clock and the faint hiss of the gas.
Fletcher had not raised his voice. He sat squarely at
the table, a broad, plain-featured man of forty-five, with iron-grey
hair and the patient heaviness of one not readily provoked
and never turned from a decision once made.

Burleigh thrust his pipe into his coat-pocket.

"You propose to buy me out?"

"No."

"My share in the business is worth money."

"Your share has been swallowed by your defalcations."

"Defalcations!" repeated Burleigh. "You choose your expressions well."

"They are not for publication."

"You will conceal the matter, then?"

"I shall pay the money and give no reason. Beyond
that, I promise nothing."

"What does that mean?"

"If I am asked a direct question, I shall answer
it."

Burleigh took a step towards the table.

"So, after ruining me, you reserve the right to send
me to penal servitude."

"You have ruined yourself."

"And you call this friendship?"

"I call it more than you deserve."

Fletcher opened a drawer and took out a small packet
of notes.

"There are two hundred pounds. Take them and begin elsewhere.
If you live honestly, no one need know what has
happened."

Burleigh did not touch the packet.

"Two hundred pounds," he said. "After twelve years."

"After three thousand eight hundred pounds."

"You sanctimonious fool."

Fletcher's eyes hardened, though his voice remained quiet.

"You will leave my house."

"Your house?"

"It is mine."

"We have lived here together for eight years."

"The lease and furniture are mine. That was arranged when
we came."

"Eight years," muttered Burleigh. "Eight years at the same table,
under the same roof, and you turn me into the
street with two hundred pounds."

"You may take your clothes and personal belongings."

"How generous!"

"I do not wish to quarrel further."

"No," said Burleigh. "You have robbed a man of his
livelihood and honour, and now you do not wish to
quarrel."

Fletcher rose.

He was the taller of the two, and his standing
seemed to Burleigh a final assumption of authority. The green
light fell upon his hands, broad and steady upon the
papers. There was neither anger nor triumph in his expression.
That stolidity enraged Burleigh more than abuse could have done.

"You will go upstairs and pack," said Fletcher. "I shall
remain here."

"And if I refuse?"

"I shall send for the police."

The words were no louder than the others.

Burleigh saw, beyond Fletcher's shoulder, a short Japanese sword hanging
between two carved masks. Its black sheath was ornamented with
dull gold flowers. He had taken it down a hundred
times to show visitors the quaint workmanship of the guard.
Neither man had supposed the blade sharp.

He moved towards the wall.

Fletcher watched him without suspicion.

"Take the notes," he said. "You will need them."

Burleigh's hand closed upon the sheath. The sword came out
with a soft metallic whisper.

Even then Fletcher did not appear to understand.

"What are you doing?"

"You shall not send me to prison."

"I have not said I would."

"You mean to do it."

"Put that thing down."

Burleigh remembered afterwards the extraordinary clearness with which he saw
the room—the blue paper on the desk, the ash from
his pipe upon the carpet, the edge of the green
shade, and one bright point of light upon the blade.
Fletcher came round the table. He held out his hand.

"Give it to me."

Burleigh struck at him.

There was a movement, a confused sound of feet against
the rug, and something with the sword that seemed to
jar Burleigh's whole arm. Fletcher turned half round as if
somebody had called from behind him. Then his knees bent.
He caught at the tablecloth, dragged it several inches, and
fell between the table and the wall.

Burleigh remained with the weapon extended.

The clock ticked.

Fletcher did not move.

Burleigh let the sword fall upon the carpet. His mouth
was dry, and a sharp pain ran from his right
wrist to his elbow. Fletcher had gripped him there during
the struggle; four red marks showed above the cuff.

He listened for breathing.

There was none that he could hear.

"James," he said.

The name sounded absurdly familiar in the altered room.

He went nearer, stopped, and went nearer again. Fletcher lay
partly upon his side, his face turned into shadow. One
hand was beneath him; the other, palm upward, showed beyond
the displaced tablecloth.

Burleigh touched the hand. It was warm.

Of course it was warm. A dead man did not
become cold in a minute.

Dead? Was he dead?

Burleigh bent until he fancied he detected a slight movement
of the coat. Then the gas hissed, and the moving
shadow proved to be that of the tablecloth stirring in
the draught.

He straightened himself and wiped his forehead.

He had not intended it. That was important. Fletcher had
threatened him. Fletcher had advanced. There had been a struggle.
Yet who would believe him? The books would be examined;
the shortage discovered; the notes found upon the table. Motive,
weapon, opportunity—all lay ready as neatly as a solicitor's brief.

He picked up the notes and put them in his
breast-pocket.

There were eight hours before daylight. Eight hours in which
to act.

He crossed to the window and looked through the blind.
The street was quiet. A wet mist hung about the
lamps, and the opposite houses showed only one or two
lighted upper windows. A constable passed the corner at a
slow pace and disappeared.

Eight hours.

Burleigh turned down the gas until the library was almost
dark, then went into the passage and shut the door.
The dining-room looked offensively commonplace. Two used plates, a cut
loaf, a bottle of claret, and Fletcher's folded napkin remained
upon the white cloth. The clock there was three minutes
slower than the library clock. Its hands pointed to six
minutes past nine.

His first thought was the railway. The eleven-fifteen from Victoria
would carry him to the coast, and by morning he
might be across the Channel. But they would telegraph. At
the ports his description would be waiting before he arrived.

Suppose he did not go abroad? Suppose he changed his
appearance and remained in London? He could shave his moustache,
buy rough clothes, and take a lodging in the eastern
districts. Two hundred pounds would last a long time if
handled carefully.

But his own clothes upstairs, his papers, his letters—everything would
proclaim hurried flight.

Suppose he removed the body?

The thought came coldly, like a business proposal. There was
a disused well at the bottom of the garden, covered
by two rotten planks. Yet the garden walls were overlooked
by six houses, and Fletcher was a heavy man. Burleigh
saw himself dragging that weight down the passage, across the
kitchen, over the wet gravel. No.

He poured out whiskey, swallowed it, and stood with both
hands upon the sideboard. The pain in his wrist had
become a steady throbbing. When he pulled down the cuff,
the marks still showed.

He must wash, pack, destroy papers, choose a disguise. Action
was safety.

He went upstairs.

The gas on the first landing had not been lighted.
He struck a match, found the tap, and turned it.
A weak flame revealed the stairs ascending into darkness. His
bedroom was at the front; Fletcher's was behind it. The
doors stood opposite one another, and both were open.

Burleigh entered his room and drew out a travelling-bag. He
put in shirts, collars, a razor, and a second suit,
then took them out again. A man carrying luggage was
remembered. Better to travel with nothing and purchase what he
needed.

He opened the little cash-box in his wardrobe. Twenty-three pounds
ten. With Fletcher's notes, enough.

A floorboard creaked overhead.

Burleigh's hands stopped.

There were two attics above. One held boxes; the other
had not been entered for months.

He listened.

Nothing.

The house was old. Boards creaked when the temperature changed.
Pipes knocked. Woodwork contracted after the fires went low.

He resumed counting the sovereigns.

Another sound came, not overhead but from below—a light scrape,
followed by a pause.

He went to the bedroom door.

"Who is there?"

No answer.

Was Fletcher alive?

The question struck him with such force that he gripped
the doorpost. Suppose the blow had merely stunned him. Suppose
at that moment Fletcher was crawling across the library, reaching
for the bell, leaving some terrible trail behind him.

Burleigh took the candle and descended.

The lower hall was empty. The front door appeared closed.
A thread of light showed beneath the dining-room door, but
the library was black.

He stood outside it, listening.

There! A dull knock from within.

"James?"

Silence.

He put his hand on the knob but did not
turn it. If Fletcher lived, what then? Another blow? Could
he enter and finish what he had begun?

A faint rustling sounded behind him.

Burleigh wheeled round. The passage to the kitchen was dark,
yet at its farther end he saw—or fancied he saw—a
deeper shadow move across the wall.

He set down the candle. In the dining-room sideboard was
a revolver, kept since a series of burglaries in the
neighbourhood. He found it beneath a pile of napkins, broke
it open, and saw that it was loaded.

He returned to the hall.

"Come out," he called. "I am armed."

No answer.

He examined the front door. The chain hung loose. The
upper bolt, which Fletcher had drawn after dinner, was now
back.

Burleigh stared at it.

Someone had entered the house.

Not a ghost, not Fletcher returned from the dead, but
a man. The kitchen window perhaps; or the front door
with a key. A burglar. He felt fear first, then
a swift, almost joyous relief. A burglar in the house
while Fletcher lay murdered!

Suppose the man were found in the library. Suppose he
had the sword in his hand. Suppose—

Burleigh extinguished the hall candle and withdrew into the dining-room,
leaving its door open by an inch. From there he
could see a part of the passage and the dark
rectangle of the library entrance.

For several minutes nothing happened.

The clocks ticked in disagreement.

Then a shoe grated softly upon the tiles near the
kitchen. A man advanced, paused beneath the stairs, and listened.
Burleigh could not see his face. The intruder carried no
lamp, but moved with the cautious certainty of one accustomed
to strange houses.

He tried the dining-room door.

Burleigh held it from within.

The man waited, then moved away. A moment later came
the slight click of the library latch.

Burleigh's heart beat violently.

The door opened. The intruder went in.

There followed a silence, a whispered oath, and the noise
of something being overturned.

Burleigh slipped into the passage, pulled the library door shut,
turned the key, and drew it out.

At once the handle moved.

"'Ere!" said a muffled voice. "What's this?"

Burleigh stepped back, raising the revolver.

The handle rattled harder.

"Open the door!"

"Remain where you are," Burleigh called. "The police are coming."

"The police? I 'ain't done nothing!"

"You have murdered a man."

There was a sudden stillness within.

Then the voice came again, changed and frightened.

"Murdered? There's a bloke 'ere, but 'e was down when
I come. You open this door."

"You killed him."

"I never touched him! Open it, guv'nor. 'E may not
be dead."

That suggestion shook Burleigh. He heard movement near the body
and imagined the intruder bending over Fletcher, discovering breath, perhaps
reviving him.

"Keep away from him!"

"Then fetch a doctor!"

Burleigh could not allow the fellow to remain quietly there.
He must be captured in confusion, with the weapon beside
him. The police must arrive believing him dangerous.

Burleigh pointed the revolver towards the upper panel and fired.

The report filled the passage. Flame leapt from the muzzle;
plaster fell from the opposite wall; and from within came
a cry and the crash of furniture.

Burleigh ran to the front door, flung it open, and
shouted into the street.

"Police! Murder! Police!"

The constable he had seen earlier was less than fifty
yards away. Another officer stood at the corner. Both came
running, their capes flapping in the mist.

"What is it, sir?" demanded the first.

"A burglar. He has killed my partner. He is locked
in the library."

A fist struck the door from within.

"I 'ain't killed nobody! Let me out!"

"You hear him," said Burleigh. "He is armed. He fired
at me."

The constable glanced at the revolver in Burleigh's hand.

"That yours, sir?"

"Yes—yes, but he had it. I wrested it from him."

The second officer blew his whistle. Windows opened along the
street.

A police sergeant arrived within two minutes, a compact, red-faced
man with rain upon his shoulders. He looked from Burleigh
to the damaged panel and then at the key in
Burleigh's hand.

"Who's inside?"

"The murderer."

"And the deceased?"

"My partner, James Fletcher."

The sergeant took the key.

"Stand back. You two, one on either side."

He unlocked the door and opened it a few inches.

"Come out with your hands up."

The door was pulled from within, and a thin man
in a ragged jacket sprang into the passage. One constable
seized his collar; the other caught his arm. He struggled
with surprising violence.

"Leave go! I never done it!"

"Steady," said the sergeant.

"There's a dead man in there, but I 'ad no
hand in it. The door was open when I come.
Ask him! He locked me in and fired through it."

Burleigh gave a gesture of disgust.

"The man is lying. I heard him in the library
and came down. He rushed at me with the pistol.
After I got it from him I locked the door
and called for help."

"Where's the dead man?" asked the sergeant.

"Behind the table."

The sergeant entered. One constable dragged the prisoner after him;
the other remained beside Burleigh.

The library, when the gas was raised, showed the disorder
plainly. A chair lay upon its side. The bullet had
passed through the door and buried itself in a bookcase.
The tablecloth hung awry, concealing the space beyond.

The sergeant looked at the prisoner.

"You say he was lying there when you entered?"

"So 'elp me, he was. I come for the silver.
I don't deny that. But I never killed no man."

"And how did you get in?"

"Kitchen window."

"What did you touch?"

"Nothing—only the gent. I felt to see if 'e was
alive."

Burleigh laughed harshly.

"A likely tale."

The sergeant did not answer. He went round the table
and drew back the cloth.

Fletcher lay as Burleigh had left him, except that his
face was now turned upward. The Japanese sword rested near
his hand. The prisoner stared at it.

"I never seen that before."

"You took it from the wall," said Burleigh. "He must
have surprised you."

"I tell you I didn't!"

"Quiet," said the sergeant.

He knelt beside Fletcher and put two fingers beneath the
jaw. After a moment he lowered his ear towards the
man's mouth.

"Well?" asked Burleigh.

The sergeant looked up.

"Fetch Dr. Price," he said to the constable. "Run."

Burleigh felt the room contract about him.

"A doctor? Surely he is—"

"May be. May not."

The constable hurried out.

The prisoner twisted in the other officer's grasp.

"I told you! I said 'e mightn't be dead."

Burleigh leaned against the desk. He had a sensation that
something cold was moving beneath his clothes. Fletcher alive. Fletcher
able to speak. Yet the sergeant might be mistaken. Men
had strange movements after death. A last breath meant nothing.

The sergeant rose and approached Burleigh.

"You've had a shock, sir."

"A considerable one."

"You live here?"

"Yes. Mr. Fletcher and I are partners."

"No one else in the house?"

"No."

"Better sit down."

"I prefer to stand."

"As you please. Give me that pistol."

Burleigh handed it over.

The sergeant opened the chamber, glanced at it, and slipped
the weapon into his pocket.

"Your wrist hurt?"

Burleigh instinctively covered it with his left hand.

"Nothing serious. The burglar seized me."

"Let me see."

"It is merely bruised."

"Let me see," repeated the sergeant.

Burleigh pulled back the cuff. The four marks had darkened,
and below them ran a narrow cut where the sword-guard
had caught the skin.

The sergeant examined it without comment.

"That's him," said the prisoner. "He done it. Look at
'is face."

Burleigh rounded upon him.

"You miserable scoundrel!"

"None of that," said the sergeant.

"Do you permit a housebreaker to accuse me?"

"I permit everybody to keep quiet."

There was a ring at the front bell. Dr. Price
entered carrying a black bag, his coat buttoned crookedly over
his nightshirt. He was a small elderly man and wasted
no time upon questions. After one look at Fletcher, he
ordered hot water, towels, and brandy.

The constable went to the kitchen.

Burleigh remained by the desk while the doctor worked behind
the hanging cloth. He heard the snip of scissors, the
clink of glass, brief instructions, and once a low sound
which might have come from Fletcher.

Was it fancy?

The burglar had ceased struggling. He watched Burleigh now with
a vindictive curiosity, as though some instinct told him that
their positions were changing.

The sergeant moved about the room. He looked at the
empty hooks upon the wall, the sheath below them, the
sword, the dragged tablecloth, and the overturned chair. He looked
also at the blue papers on the desk and at
the packet-band from the notes.

"Sergeant," said Burleigh, "I should like some whiskey."

"Certainly, sir."

The sergeant poured it himself.

Burleigh took the glass with his left hand.

"Right hand troublesome?"

"A little."

"Drink it."

Burleigh drank. The spirit struck his empty stomach and made
him cough.

The doctor rose from behind the table.

"He is alive," he said. "Just."

The words were quietly spoken, but to Burleigh they had
the force of the pistol-shot.

"Will he recover?" asked the sergeant.

"I cannot say. The wound is grave. We must get
him to hospital."

"Can he speak?"

"Not properly."

"I need only a name."

The doctor hesitated.

"A few words, perhaps. It may cost him."

"It may save an innocent man."

The burglar drew a great breath.

Burleigh put down the glass.

"This is absurd. The fellow was found beside him. What
further evidence can be wanted?"

The sergeant turned.

"Plenty, generally."

"I insist that you arrest that man."

"He is already detained."

"Then take him away."

"In good time."

The sergeant stepped towards Burleigh.

"Put out your hands."

Burleigh stared at him.

"What?"

"Your hands. In front of you."

"You cannot mean—"

"Do as you're told."

The constable released one hand from the burglar and produced
a pair of handcuffs. Burleigh backed against the table.

"This is outrageous. I summoned you. I captured the murderer."

"Put out your hands."

"You have been deceived by that creature's lies."

The sergeant caught Burleigh's left wrist. Burleigh tried to wrench
himself free, but the constable seized the other arm. Pain
shot through the bruised wrist. The steel closed with two
small clicks.

"Take them off," said Burleigh.

No one answered.

"Take them off! You have no right."

"I've every right," said the sergeant. "You told three tales
about that pistol in less than five minutes. First the
man inside fired it. Then you took it from him.
But the shot went from this side of the door,
and the powder is on your cuff. Your wrist wasn't
marked by that fellow, either. He has dirt on his
hands, and there's none in those bruises."

Burleigh's eyes went to Fletcher.

"The burglar used the sword."

"He says he never saw it."

"He lies."

"Perhaps. Mr. Fletcher may settle that."

The doctor had administered something from a glass tube. Fletcher's
eyelids moved. His lips parted, but no sound came.

The sergeant knelt beside him and spoke slowly.

"Mr. Fletcher, I am Sergeant Wade. Do you understand me?"

A pause. Then the faintest movement of the head.

"You have been injured. Who did it?"

Fletcher's eyes wandered towards the gas, the ceiling, and the
dark figures gathered near the table. At last they found
Burleigh.

Burleigh stood rigid.

Fletcher's mouth moved.

The sergeant bent lower.

"Who struck you?"

"Trayton."

It was scarcely more than breath.

"Trayton Burleigh?"

"Yes."

"Did the other man attack you?"

Fletcher's gaze shifted towards the prisoner. For an instant there
was plain bewilderment in it.

"No."

The doctor raised a warning hand.

"One question more," said the sergeant. "Why did Burleigh attack
you?"

Fletcher's face tightened. The answer came brokenly, with long spaces
between the words.

"Money. Books. He—took it."

His eyes closed.

"That is enough," said the doctor sharply.

The sergeant took out his notebook and wrote at the
edge of the desk. The scratch of his pencil seemed
louder than any sound in the room.

Burleigh watched him.

Only ten minutes earlier the house had appeared to belong
to him: its doors, shadows, weapons, even the chance entrance
of a thief had seemed materials placed at his disposal.
He had arranged them with ingenuity. He had locked the
stranger beside the body. He had fired the shot. He
had called the police. Every action meant to secure his
escape had drawn another bar across it.

The burglar rubbed his released wrist.

"I told you," he said. "I 'ain't no murderer."

"No," said Sergeant Wade. "Only a housebreaker. You'll come along
as well."

The man's satisfaction faded.

A stretcher was brought from the ambulance. While the doctor
and constables lifted Fletcher, the displaced tablecloth fell entirely from
the desk. Beneath it lay Burleigh's pipe, crushed in the
struggle, and a button torn from his coat.

The sergeant picked up the button, compared it with the
empty place below Burleigh's lapel, and put it in an
envelope.

Burleigh gave a weak laugh.

"You have Fletcher's word. Why trouble with that?"

"Words sometimes fail," said the sergeant. "Buttons don't often."

They carried Fletcher through the hall. His face was grey,
and one arm hung until the doctor placed it upon
the blanket. For the first time Burleigh felt something that
was almost pity, though it was chiefly fear wearing another
shape.

"If he dies," he said, "what then?"

Sergeant Wade looked at him.

"You know what then."

The dining-room clock struck ten. From the library, three minutes
later, the other clock answered.

Burleigh shivered.

"May I take a coat?"

The sergeant selected one from the hall stand and put
it over his shoulders. The gesture had an appearance of
consideration.

"Take them off," Burleigh muttered, lifting the handcuffs. "At least
until we reach the station."

"No."

"I shall not run."

"No."

"I give you my word."

The sergeant opened the front door.

Outside, the mist had thickened. A small crowd stood beneath
the lamps, whispering and craning towards the house. The ambulance
moved off first with the doctor beside Fletcher. A constable
followed with the burglar, who protested loudly that housebreaking was
one thing and murder quite another.

Burleigh stopped upon the threshold.

Behind him the gas burned over the disordered table. The
Japanese sword lay where it had fallen; the whiskey glass
stood half empty; the two hundred pounds pressed against his
breast. The house was still warm, still familiar, still untidy
with the ordinary remains of dinner. Yet it no longer
offered concealment. Every solid object in it had taken evidence
and was prepared to speak.

"Come along," said Sergeant Wade.

Burleigh descended the steps.

At the gate he looked once towards the upper windows.
Eight hours until daylight, he had thought. Eight hours in
which to begin again.

He had not been given one.

### THE DARK CHAMBER OF THE HÔTEL BARBETTE

Among the many adventures of Louis, Duke of Orleans, brother
to King Charles the Sixth of France, there is one
which certain discreet chroniclers have passed over as a cat
passes a hot porringer—looking another way, yet smelling the meat
all the while. Monstrelet says nothing of it; Juvenal des
Ursins says less; the anonymous clerk of Saint-Denis, who knew
the weight of a crown and the price of parchment,
gives but three crooked lines, since scraped away by some
pious hand. Nevertheless, the tale was preserved in the house
of Hocquetonville, and afterward repeated at Lille by old Madame
de Croy, who had it from her grandmother's confessor, whose
uncle had served the Duke of Burgundy in the year
of the great frost. This is sufficient authority for a
scandal; indeed, twice as much as scandals commonly possess.

Louis of Orleans was then the brightest, boldest, best-hosed, worst-governed
prince in Christendom. He hunted stag in the forest, boar
in the marsh, heron by the river, wenches in the
town, ladies at court, widows after vespers, and reputations wherever
honest husbands had stored them. He loved hawks with wide
wings, horses with narrow flanks, wines without water, suppers without
reckoning, and women without scruples—though, to do him justice, he
preferred them with scruples, for resistance whetted him as vinegar
sharpens an onion.

He had pages to carry his gloves, equerries to hold
his stirrup, musicians to tune his sleep, astrologers to excuse
his follies, physicians to repair them, priests to absolve them,
and flatterers to persuade him they were virtues. Gold ran
through his fingers like rain through a gargoyle's mouth. His
hôtel was a fair of silks, spices, lapdogs, lutes, peacock
pies, silver cups, perfumed baths, velvet cushions, Italian sweetmeats, Spanish
leather, Flemish tapestry, German steel, and those painted hussies who
came from nowhere, belonged to nobody, and knew everybody.

Now there are men who, having eaten enough, cease eating;
others, having drunk enough, fall under the board and thereby
offend no more until morning. But lust is a pantry
without a door, a barrel without a bottom, a creditor
who doubles the account whenever he is paid. The duke,
having tasted every easy pleasure, began to hunger after the
difficult; and as a child who has a basket of
apples cries for the one upon the church wall, so
his Grace coveted most fiercely those ladies who belonged by
honour, affection, law, habit, children, sacrament, and common sense to
other men.

At that time there served him a knight called Raoul
d'Hocquetonville, descended, according to the family roll, from Hugues the
Iron-Bearded, who accompanied Count Robert upon some crusade which the
count himself never made. Raoul was not rich, but of
good lineage; not subtle, but of good faith; not eloquent,
but of good courage. He rode straight, struck hard, paid
his debts when able, and when unable confessed them without
inventing a Jew, a flood, a fire, or a sick
aunt in Picardy. Such men are rarer in courts than
white ravens, feathered oysters, or lawyers who shorten a lawsuit.

Raoul had married Bertha de Montfaucon, called by those who
saw her Bertha the Fair, though beauty was the least
valuable of her possessions. Her hair was of that brown
which takes fire in sunlight; her brow smooth and broad;
her eyes grey, clear, and steady; her mouth neither too
grave for kindness nor too merry for prudence. She was
not one of your sugar saints, pale from vanity and
devout from indigestion, but a woman of warm blood, sound
understanding, and firm affection. She bore Raoul two children, tended
his old mother, ordered his household, remembered his tenants' names,
and loved her husband after seven years of marriage—which last
accomplishment should have placed her among the miracles of Saint
Denis.

The duke first saw her at a Christmas entertainment given
by the queen. Bertha wore blue damask bordered with miniver,
no jewels except a little cross, and no paint except
the colour that rose when she laughed. She spoke little,
danced once with her husband, and left before the masks
became insolent. From that evening Louis carried her image as
a fever carries heat.

He questioned Raoul concerning her, praising first her gown, then
her manners, then her wit, and last her beauty, as
one who walks round a fortress seeking the weakest gate.

"My lord," said Raoul, smiling, "you honour my poor house.
Bertha is better than I deserve."

"Then lend me thy fortune, Hocquetonville."

"All that I have is yours, saving my wife and
my salvation."

The duke laughed.

"Keep the latter; it is a lean possession. As for
the former, guard it well."

Raoul, hearing only jest where malice had already laid its
eggs, laughed likewise. Thus do honest men often ring the
bell for their own calamity.

The duke began with courtesies: flowers from his gardens, fruits
from the south, a little book of prayers illuminated in
gold, a cage of linnets, a mirror from Venice, and
a length of cloth so delicate that, as his messenger
declared, it might be drawn through a wedding ring. Bertha
returned the jewels, gave the birds to a convent, sent
the fruit to the sick, covered the mirror, kept the
prayer book because sacred words are not dishonoured by profane
intentions, and had the cloth cut into altar veils.

This amused the duke prodigiously.

"She makes ladders of my arrows," said he. "By Saint
Cupid, I shall climb them!"

Then came letters, those paper ferrets which princes thrust into
every domestic hole. In one he called her the rose
of Valois, the moon of Paris, the pearl in Raoul's
wooden cup, the white hind of his forest, the key
of his paradise, and the sole physician capable of curing
the wound she had made. Bertha burned it. In another
he compared himself to Leander, Paris, Lancelot, Tristan, Pyramus, and
King Solomon, thereby confessing more sins than examples. That she
burned also.

At length he met her in a gallery of the
queen's apartments, where she waited upon a kinswoman. The gallery
looked upon the royal garden, and the snow outside made
the winter sun so bright that every flaw in a
man's face—and sometimes in his soul—seemed plainly visible.

"Madame d'Hocquetonville," said the duke, barring her passage with the
grace of a dancing master and the authority of a
gaoler, "you treat my devotion cruelly."

"My lord, I treat it as it deserves."

"You have not read my letters."

"I have read enough to know they should be burned."

"Fire is the proper altar for love."

"It is also the proper end of corruption."

"Ha! A thrust! Raoul has married a swordsman in petticoats."

"Raoul has married a wife, my lord. Remember that word."

The duke leaned nearer. He was handsome when he wished
to be, and terrible when handsome, for beauty in a
powerful wicked man is a gilded handle upon a poisoned
blade.

"Words," said he, "are little fences built by frightened gardeners.
Love leaps them. Consider, sweet lady, how short is youth.
Spring hastens, roses fall, snow invades the hair, teeth desert
the mouth, and the eyes which now command kingdoms must
one day bargain with spectacles. Why hoard treasure for the
worms? Let us drink while the cup is warm. I
offer you secrecy, splendour, tenderness, palaces—"

"You offer me shame."

"A name given by old women to pleasures they can
no longer enjoy."

"I have children."

"They shall be enriched."

"I have a husband."

"He shall be advanced."

"I have honour."

"I shall worship it."

"You have already insulted it."

Then Louis changed his instrument, as a musician who, failing
with the lute, takes up the trumpet.

"You speak boldly to your prince."

"I speak faithfully to my husband."

"Your husband is my servant."

"And therefore doubly under your protection."

This answer struck him, but did not cure him. Vice
seldom lacks ears; it lacks obedience to what enters them.

"Madame," said he, lowering his voice, "there are doors at
court which open to a whisper and close upon a
cry."

"And there are windows, my lord, through which an honest
woman may escape a dishonourable prince."

She left him standing beneath the painted histories of Lucretia
and Susanna, which, had he looked upward, might have preached
what no confessor had yet persuaded him to hear.

For some weeks he ceased his pursuit, and Bertha hoped
the storm had passed. Alas! A prince's silence is often
not peace but carpentry: he is building the trap.

In February, when the queen lay at the Hôtel Barbette,
Bertha was summoned to attend her. The message bore the
queen's seal, or an imitation so good that the devil
himself might have kissed it. She came at dusk, passed
through the outer court, ascended the stair, and was led
by a chamberwoman whom she did not know into a
room hung with green tapestry.

"The queen will presently receive you," said the woman, and
withdrew.

Bertha waited. Presently the duke entered.

She rose so quickly that her chair fell.

"My lord!"

"My lady."

"This is infamous."

"This is private."

He turned the key.

The chamber was richly furnished: a fire of applewood, candles
in silver branches, wine warmed with cinnamon, preserved quinces, a
low couch spread with cloth of gold, and upon the
floor fresh rushes scented with lavender. What splendour! What comfort!
What excellent preparations for wickedness! Hell itself would be tolerable
if the upholsterers of Orleans had charge of it.

The duke spoke softly, then warmly, then magnificently. He promised
estates, titles, jewels, protection, pleasures fine enough to make angels
forsake their clouds. He knelt; he jested; he sighed; he
swore. He painted delight as a garden where every tree
bore sugar, every fountain ran wine, every bird sang madrigals,
and no serpent had ever studied theology. Bertha stood against
the table, white but composed.

"Never, while I live."

"Then do not live too stubbornly."

At that she drew from beneath her girdle a little
Italian stiletto, thin as an icicle and bright as an
evil thought. She set the point against her breast.

"Come one pace nearer," said she, "and my blood shall
cry between you and God."

Louis stopped.

He knew courage when he saw it. He had seen
men charge pikes and horses leap flames, but there was
in Bertha's stillness something stronger than fury. For a moment
shame touched him; unhappily it touched only as a swallow
touches water, and flew away.

"Put up your bodkin," said he. "I am not a
village ravisher."

"No. A village ravisher may be hanged."

"You shall go free."

"Open the door."

He did so, bowing with mock humility. She passed him
without a word.

Had the matter ended there, Louis might have been called
a libertine checked by virtue, which is a black title
written upon grey paper. But wounded vanity is lust wearing
armour. He could forgive chastity more readily than contempt, refusal
more readily than defeat, and the stiletto had pierced him
though it never left Bertha's hand.

He waited, smiled upon Raoul, promoted him in trifles, praised
his horsemanship, sent toys to his children, and devised a
revenge which only a courtier could have polished and only
a devil could have invented.

Three months later Raoul was summoned to supper at a
house belonging to the duke near the Porte Barbette. With
him came Guillaume de Saveuse, Hutin de Vermeilles, young Colard
de Mailly, and two other gentlemen whose names charity has
blotted from my copy. The room was low, warm, and
painted with vines. Upon the table stood capons stuffed with
chestnuts, eels in pepper, lampreys in red wine, larks baked
by the dozen, a boar's head with gilded tusks, cheeses
strong enough to confess their own sins, wafers, marchpane, pears,
nuts, and bottles from Beaune, Auxerre, Cyprus, and places which
geographers have since hidden out of respectability.

The duke resolved that no man should leave sober enough
to distinguish his conscience from his appetite.

They drank to the king, the queen, Burgundy, Orleans, France,
the ladies, absent friends, present fools, future victories, past debts,
Saint Martin, Saint Venus, and the blessed inventor of corks.
They sang. They diced. They told stories concerning abbesses, millers'
daughters, merchants' wives, shepherdesses, bath women, actresses, pilgrims, laundresses, and
certain noble ladies whose names were concealed so carefully that
every hearer knew them.

Raoul drank less than the others.

"What, Hocquetonville!" cried Louis. "Art thou become a monk?"

"I have a wife who prefers me Christian."

"A wife! There is the cork in every good bottle."

"Mine keeps the wine from souring."

The company groaned at this domestic sentiment as wolves might
groan at a sermon upon mutton.

The duke filled Raoul's cup himself.

"Drink. Thou art among friends."

That phrase has ruined more men than open enmity.

Now, behind the tapestry at the eastern end of the
supper room there was a narrow door. Beyond it lay
a dark bedchamber, and in that chamber Bertha had been
confined since afternoon. She had been seized while returning from
the convent of the Celestines, hooded, carried by closed litter,
and brought through the garden passage. The duke himself had
visited her only long enough to explain enough of his
purpose to make terror complete.

"You shall not be touched by a stranger," said he.
"Indeed, I provide you a gallant whom you have often
favoured."

"You are mad."

"No, madame. I am just. You chose a husband above
a prince; this night shall prove the excellence of your
choice."

When she understood, her knees failed her.

"You cannot!"

"A duke can do many things which a wise man
would avoid."

"I will cry out."

"And proclaim before six drunken gentlemen that Raoul has entered
a strange woman's bed? Proclaim yourself the woman? Which tale
will Paris believe—the prince's or yours? Honour is fine glass,
madame; once cracked, witnesses mend it badly."

"I will tell Raoul."

"Tell him, and he kills me or I kill him.
Be silent, and he wakes ashamed of a common infidelity,
never knowing whom he wronged. Choose: his life, or your
relief."

Then he left her in darkness.

She tried the door, the shutters, the panel behind the
bed. All were fast. She searched for a weapon, but
even the pins had been taken from her hair. She
prayed—first calmly, then desperately, then with that wordless inward cry
which perhaps reaches heaven more quickly than Latin. Through the
wall came laughter, cups striking wood, boots scraping rushes, and
the voice of her husband.

Imagine her torment if you can; if you can imagine
it easily, examine your heart.

At supper the duke began to describe a woman hidden
nearby: not noble, he said, but more accomplished than ten
countesses; discreet as a tomb, soft as ermine, lively as
a flame, and skilled in every article of love's guild,
from the first glance to the final benediction.

Saveuse demanded her price. Mailly demanded her age. Vermeilles demanded
whether she had a sister.

"She asks no money," said Louis. "She has seen one
among us and desires him."

At this every man straightened, sucked in his belly, adjusted
his beard, and believed himself chosen. Such is masculine modesty.

"Who?" they cried.

The duke looked round, prolonged the silence, and laid his
hand upon Raoul's shoulder.

"Our married saint."

The room burst into laughter.

Raoul shook his head.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I love my wife."

"Thou needst not marry this one."

"I have no hunger."

"Then eat for courtesy."

"I am no capon to be stuffed after supper."

"Art afraid?"

This word, which should frighten only cowards, is commonly the
leash by which fools are led. The others took it
up.

"Raoul is afraid!"

"Bertha keeps his courage in her workbasket!"

"He must ask leave of his nurse!"

Raoul flushed. He had drunk enough to feel insult more
sharply than duty, yet not enough to lose all shame.

"My lord, command me against Burgundians, Englishmen, bandits, or boars.
In this, excuse me."

Louis smiled and poured another cup.

"I command nothing. I offer paradise. Refuse, and we shall
know that marriage has made a hermit of the best
lance in Valois."

They teased, praised, challenged, embraced, and drowned him by drops.
Wine spoke where friendship should have warned. At last Raoul
rose unsteadily.

"One moment only," said he, "to silence you muckworms."

"Ha! Ha! Ha!" cried the duke. "The saint performs a
miracle!"

Behind the wall Bertha heard the chair fall backward.

The hidden door opened. Raoul entered the unlighted chamber, and
Louis locked it after him.

What passed there shall not be ornamented. There are jests
which become crimes when pursued beyond the table, and curiosities
which dishonour him who satisfies them. Raoul, clouded by wine,
believing himself admitted to a courtesan's chamber, did not know
the silent woman who trembled beneath his touch. Bertha dared
not speak; for the duke's threat stood before her like
an executioner, and her husband's life seemed to hang upon
her voice. She endured that which, between loving spouses, should
have been tenderness, but which deceit transformed into humiliation and
horror.

When Raoul had gone, confused, half-ashamed, and boasting falsely to
hide his shame, the duke entered carrying a candle.

Bertha sat upon the edge of the bed. Her loosened
hair covered her face. She appeared neither furious nor weeping,
and this frightened Louis more than the stiletto had done.

"Well, madame," said he, though his wit sounded thin, "you
see I kept my promise. No stranger."

She raised her head.

"You have made my husband betray me without knowledge, and
me betray him through fear. You have put poison into
what was pure. Listen now, Louis of Orleans. This chamber
will follow you. It will walk behind your horse, sit
beneath your council table, lie down beside your pleasures, and
stand at last between you and mercy. You have not
conquered me. You have destroyed something which you cannot restore,
and the price will be paid by you, by him,
and by me."

The duke tried to laugh.

"Prophecies are women's arrows; they fly far and strike nothing."

"This one will strike."

He let her leave through the garden gate before dawn.

Raoul returned home late the next day, sick in body
and uneasy in spirit. Bertha received him with her accustomed
gentleness, but when he kissed her brow she shuddered. He
believed his drunken infidelity had somehow been discovered. He confessed
that he had been led into folly, though he spared
the details, and begged forgiveness.

She looked at him for a long while.

"You were deceived," she said.

"That does not make me innocent."

"No. But it makes another more guilty."

She forgave him; yet from that day she changed. Not
in affection—for she loved him more sorrowfully than before—but as
a flower changes when deprived of air and eaten secretly
by a worm. She grew pale. Music troubled her. Laughter
heard through a wall made her tremble. She avoided dark
rooms and could not endure the smell of cinnamon wine.

Raoul, seeing this, questioned her. For months she denied illness
of the heart; but truth, like a spring beneath stones,
finds at last some crevice. When the duke sent again
for Raoul to attend him, Bertha clung to her husband
and cried:

"Serve Burgundy, serve the king, serve the poorest knight in
France, but serve that man no longer!"

Then all came out—not neatly, not in the order chroniclers
prefer, but with tears, broken phrases, pauses, and the dreadful
repetition of details which the injured soul cannot cease touching.
Raoul listened standing. Once he gripped the table so strongly
that the oak split at the edge. Once he asked,
"Was it I?" and when she answered yes, he made
a sound which she remembered until death.

He did not reproach her.

"Thou didst save my life," he said.

"I lost mine."

"No. He took it; and I will collect the debt."

Shortly afterward Raoul quitted Orleans and entered the service of
John, Duke of Burgundy. Some say he revealed everything to
his new master; others that Burgundy knew only that Raoul
bore Louis a mortal hatred and considered that recommendation sufficient.
Princes are not overcurious concerning the griefs which furnish them
useful swords.

On the twenty-third day of November in the year fourteen
hundred and seven, Louis of Orleans supped with Queen Isabeau
and afterward rode from the Hôtel Barbette. The night was
cold; torches smoked in the mist; the horses' shoes rang
upon the street. Near the old gate, armed men rushed
from concealment. "Death! Death!" they cried, in the Burgundian fashion.

Louis raised his arm.

"I am the Duke of Orleans!"

"That is why," answered a voice.

Raoul d'Hocquetonville stepped from the shadow with a knotted club
of applewood, bound at the head with iron. He might
have used a sword, which is the weapon of gentlemen,
but chose the club, saying afterward that villainy should be
beaten like a rabid dog. He struck once upon the
duke's shoulder, once upon his temple, and a third time
after Louis had fallen.

Whether Bertha's prophecy stood visibly at the corner, I cannot
say. But the duke died in the mud, his rings
bright, his cloak rich, his blood black beneath the torches;
and all the palaces, poets, perfumers, pages, flatterers, astrologers, hounds,
hawks, lutes, velvets, and sugared quinces in France could not
purchase him one further breath.

Raoul escaped to Burgundy's protection. He lived, but not happily.
Within the year Bertha died at Hocquetonville, being not yet
thirty years of age. The physician wrote "decline of the
lungs"; the priest wrote "God's will"; her husband said nothing.
She was buried beneath a plain stone bearing these words:

**HERE RESTS BERTHA, TRUE WIFE OF RAOUL,
WHOM FALSEHOOD WOUNDED AND HEAVEN RECEIVED.**

Raoul caused no name to be added, for he said
hers was enough. Each year, upon the day of her
death, he laid upon the tomb a branch of white
hawthorn. He never married again. In battle he was fierce,
at feasts silent, and whenever men laughed too loudly over
wine he would rise and leave them.

The Duke of Burgundy, passing the tomb some years later,
read the inscription and remained thoughtful.

"Two honourable people," said he, "were broken to feed one
prince's appetite."

"My lord," replied his chaplain, "and the prince was broken
afterward."

"That is justice."

"Late justice."

"All justice is late," said Burgundy, "or men would call
it prevention."

Thus ended the affair of the dark chamber, which began
with a glance at Christmas and concluded with a body
in the Rue Vieille-du-Temple, a widow's grave, and a husband
who outlived his happiness. Let young lords who imagine power
to be permission remember Louis; let companions who pour wine
upon another man's conscience remember Raoul; and let those who
call steadfast women cold remember Bertha, who defended her honour
first with reason, next with steel, and last with silence—though
silence proved the cruellest weapon turned against herself.

As for the old saying that there is a woman
at the bottom of everything, it was doubtless invented by
a man standing at the top and pushing her down.
In this business there was indeed a woman at the
bottom: buried there. But above her stood appetite, mockery, wine,
cowardly laughter, and the abused privilege of a prince.

Folly prepares the banquet, wickedness locks the door, and vengeance,
though she arrives after the candles have guttered, never forgets
the house.

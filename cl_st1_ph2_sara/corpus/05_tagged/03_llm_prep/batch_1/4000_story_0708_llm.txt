### The Road Over Litacy

Martin first perceived that something was wrong at the sign-post.

Until then the afternoon had proceeded with an almost professional
accuracy. He had left Westerbridge at twelve minutes past two,
had eaten bread and cheese beside the abandoned quarry at
three, and at twenty minutes to five had reached the
stone bridge marked upon his map. The weather, too, had
observed its engagements. A pale October sun accompanied him over
the lower slopes; clouds gathered towards evening; and the wind,
rising precisely when the guide-book had predicted, travelled across the
heather with a sound like distant water.

Martin was an accountant, and though he escaped figures during
his annual walking-tour, he did not escape the habit of
exactness. A wrong total offended him morally. An omitted milestone,
an inaccurate map, a careless direction—these were minor sins against
the order of existence.

The sign-post therefore disturbed him.

It stood where two white roads divided upon the empty
moor. One arm indicated:—

WESTERBRIDGE 7
PENRYDD 4

The other bore the words:—

LITACY HILL 3

Yet Litacy Hill, according to the map, lay behind him
and somewhat to the north. The road before him ought
to lead to Penrydd by the old mine, while the
left-hand branch, which was not shown at all, curved away
over a desolate rise.

He removed his knapsack and unfolded the map against the
post.

There could be no mistake. He had crossed the bridge,
passed the three solitary pines and entered the road between
the sheepfold and the marsh. All were printed clearly. Nevertheless,
the country refused to agree. There was the marsh, certainly,
shining beneath the twilight; but the sheepfold had vanished, and
the pines stood half a mile farther west, black and
inexplicable against the sky.

"A misprinted map," he said aloud.

The words sounded absurdly inadequate in that enormous silence.

He examined the sign-post. Its letters were weathered, its bolts
rusted. It had not been newly erected or turned by
mischievous hands. Moss grew along the northward side with convincing
propriety.

Now the old short-cut snare is as ancient as human
impatience. A strange road promises economy, concealing beneath that promise
every variety of discomfort and disaster. Martin knew this. He
knew also that darkness would fall within an hour, and
that the familiar way, if familiar it could still be
called, offered certainty.

Yet an impulse—sudden, unreasonable, almost authoritative—directed him towards Litacy Hill.

It was not desire. It possessed none of desire's warmth.
It resembled rather an instruction entering the mind after the
fashion of a wireless message caught by the wrong receiver.

"Go over Litacy," said something within him.

He frowned, refolded the map and took the left-hand road.

For the first mile he experienced merely annoyance. The road
rose steadily; loose stones made walking difficult; his knapsack dragged
upon his shoulders. Heather extended on either side in sombre
acres, and here and there water gleamed in black trenches
cut through the peat. Behind him the west retained a
strip of diluted gold. Ahead, the road curved like a
white river running into space.

Presently he noticed that his thoughts had altered.

This was difficult to define. He was still Martin, forty-three
years of age, unmarried, senior clerk to Benson, Wade and
Company; yet certain impressions moved beneath his ordinary consciousness like
fish beneath ice. He thought of money—not the modest sum
in his pocket, but money folded flat and worn against
the skin. He felt, without reason, the pressure of a
broad belt beneath his waistcoat.

He stopped.

His hand went instinctively to his body. There was no
belt.

At the same instant he remembered a German phrase.

The circumstance was peculiarly disquieting, for Martin knew no German
beyond two or three expressions acquired in school and long
forgotten. Nevertheless the phrase entered his mind complete, accompanied by
a second phrase and an obscure conviction that they referred
to him.

He looked behind.

The road was empty.

"This is fatigue," he decided. "Fatigue, combined with insufficient food."

The explanation had the form of reason, but not its
substance. He ate a biscuit and continued.

Five minutes later he saw the tramps.

They sat in a hollow beside the road, sheltered from
the wind by a bank of turf. One was short
and heavy, with a red handkerchief knotted about his throat;
the other, very tall, wore an army coat several sizes
too large for him. A small fire smouldered between them.
Their boots were broken, their faces dirty, and a bottle
passed from hand to hand.

Martin's first feeling was relief. Human beings, however uninviting, restored
proportion to the landscape. As he approached, the tall man
stood and touched his cap.

"Could you tell me, sir, vot is de time?"

The accent was unmistakable.

Martin took out his watch. "Six minutes past six."

"T'ank you; much opliged."

The short man laughed. He said something in German, quickly
and under his breath.

And Martin understood him.

Not every word; understanding came by another route, an intuition
more immediate than language. The short man had said that
the road was empty. The tall man replied that darkness
would be sufficient in ten minutes.

Martin walked on without increasing his pace. His heart beat
violently, but his mind, by contrast, became cold and busy.
Germans, then. Tramps perhaps, though their hands were too clean
where the dirt had rubbed away. Spies? The newspapers had
lately discovered a Teutonic agent behind every reservoir and pigeon-house.
But what concern could such men have with an accountant
on holiday?

He glanced back.

They had disappeared.

At once the alien pressure within his mind strengthened. Pictures—not
memories, not reasoned apprehensions, but certainties—rose before him. The short
man without his beard. The tall man in a dark
suit, wearing a pearl tie-pin. A railway compartment. Gold-rimmed spectacles.
A leather belt containing bank-notes.

"They are disguised," Martin whispered.

There were two tramps.

They were disguised.

They were following him.

The conclusions fell one after another, each final, each unexplained.
His knees weakened. Sweat gathered coldly beneath his cap, while
his face burned in the wind. He began to hurry.

The moor changed with the failing light. Hollows deepened; stones
took the posture of crouching men. On his right a
green field shone for a moment between dark ridges, like
a surviving thought of daylight, then vanished as the road
descended.

He heard footsteps.

He turned. Nobody was visible, but the footsteps continued—one light
and rapid, the other dragging slightly. He walked faster. So
did they.

There was no house, no tree, no wall; only heather,
sky and the interminable road. To leave it would be
useless. The bog waited on either side with smooth, deceitful
surfaces.

The footsteps drew nearer.

Then the tall man was beside him.

It happened at once and in a heap. A question,
a hand upon his arm, the reek of spirits, the
short man rushing from behind. Martin struck out with his
walking-stick. It was torn away. A blow reached his temple;
another crushed the breath from him. He fell upon one
knee, rose, and fell again.

The tall man held him from behind. The short man
searched his pockets, cursing. Martin shouted, but the cry travelled
no distance. The wind took it.

Then the searching hands paused at his waist.

"Here!"

That word he understood ordinarily.

Martin knew what they had found—the belt that was not
there, the money he did not possess. The contradiction split
his thoughts. He struggled with a strength born less of
courage than bewilderment. Why should they find upon him what
was not upon him? Why should he die for another
man's money?

The short man produced something that shone.

There was a swift movement. Fire entered Martin's throat. He
tried to breathe and swallowed instead. A thick, sweet thing
filled his mouth. The stars swung down; the white road
turned slowly over; the heather rose and covered the world.

Then everything ceased.

* * *

Martin stood at the sign-post.

His map lay open in his hands. The sun had
not yet disappeared, and the wind moved across the marsh
exactly as before. From far away came the complaining cry
of a sheep.

For several moments he did not move.

Then he touched his throat.

It was uninjured. His collar was dry. There was no
bruise upon his temple, no mud on his knees. His
watch showed eighteen minutes to six—the hour at which he
had first reached the cross-roads.

The road to Litacy Hill stretched before him, untouched by
any foot that he could see.

Martin folded the map with great care. His fingers trembled
so violently that he folded it along the wrong crease,
corrected it, and immediately folded it wrongly again.

Hallucination was a word; fatigue was a word; psychic impression
was another. None restored the machinery of his just and
accurate soul. He had not merely imagined an attack. He
had felt the men's hands, smelt their breath, understood their
speech and died beneath the short man's knife. Yet it
had not happened. Or had not happened to him.

That last qualification arrived with dreadful distinctness.

"It was not meant for me," he said.

Someone else had transmitted it. Somewhere a mind had thrown
out its terror before the event, and Martin, passing an
accidental point of contact, had received it as a telephone
receives a voice intended for another number. He had walked,
for twenty intolerable minutes, in another person's future—another's map of
life.

The idea was preposterous.

He accepted it instantly.

Martin took the road to Penrydd and almost ran.

Darkness overtook him among the lower fields, but the first
lamps of the village appeared soon afterwards. Their yellow squares,
the smell of coal-smoke, a dog barking behind a gate—these
commonplace things moved him nearly to tears.

At the Lamb and Crown he ordered a room and
a hot supper. The parlour was crowded. Farmers discussed the
price of feed; a commercial traveller arranged dominoes upon a
table; two well-dressed men drank whisky near the fire.

Martin recognised them.

One was short and clean-shaven. The other, tall and broad-shouldered,
wore a dark suit and a pearl tie-pin.

They glanced at him without interest. Their speech, when they
addressed the servant, was faultlessly English. A moment later the
short man murmured something to his companion, and beneath the
murmur Martin caught the hard syllables of German.

His glass rattled against his teeth.

What ought he to do? Inform the landlord that two
respectable guests were criminals because he had seen them, in
clothing they did not at present wear, murder a person
who perhaps did not exist? Go to the police with
an accusation founded upon his own death, from which he
had returned without a scratch?

He possessed no facts. In accountancy an unsupported entry was
inadmissible; in justice, surely, the principle must be stricter still.
Suspicion was not evidence. Clairvoyance—if that tremendous word must be
employed—conferred no locus standi.

Moreover, the experience had not been meant for him.

He repeated this until the words took on the comfort
of a rule. He ate little, went upstairs early, and
pushed a chair beneath the door-handle.

In the morning the two men had gone.

Sunlight and coffee restored Martin's judgment without restoring his peace.
He decided to abandon the walking-tour and take the eleven-ten
train home. The decision was sensible. Sensible decisions had seldom
before required courage.

He was fastening his knapsack in the courtyard when an
elderly gentleman approached him. He had a mild, precise face,
a grey moustache and gold-rimmed spectacles. His clothes were good
but unfashionable; in one hand he carried a guide-book, in
the other a walking-stick with a silver band.

"Excuse me," he said. "Can you direct me to Litacy
Hill?"

Martin stared.

A pressure tightened about his waist—a remembered belt, full of
remembered money.

"Litacy Hill?" he repeated.

"Yes. They tell me there is an excellent prospect from
the summit, and I can reach Darrington by evening."

"You must not go."

The violence of Martin's answer surprised them both.

The stranger smiled courteously. "Is the road impassable?"

"No. That is—not exactly. It is lonely. There are bad
characters about."

"Indeed? I have walked alone in many countries."

Martin lowered his voice. "Two men—Germans—may be waiting on the
road."

The smile faded. "You have seen them?"

There was the question. Simple, reasonable, fatal.

Had he seen them? He had, and he had not.
He had seen them as tramps in an event which
never occurred, and as gentlemen in an inn where they
committed no offence.

"I cannot explain," Martin said.

The stranger's politeness became guarded. "Then I think I shall
risk it."

"I will come with you."

Even as he spoke, Martin knew that this offer was
the utmost boundary of his courage. To accompany the man
was legitimate; to forbid him was not. One might give
companionship upon a lonely road without invoking the supernatural or
accusing strangers of murder.

But the elderly gentleman shook his head.

"You are very kind. Quite unnecessary, I assure you. I
have business at Darrington and prefer my own pace."

He moved towards the yard gate.

At that moment a tramp entered from the street.

He was tall and wore a torn army coat. His
face was dirty, his boots broken. He looked first at
Martin, then at the elderly tourist.

"Could you tell me, sir, vot is de time?"

The courtyard contracted about Martin. He heard the wind of
the moor, though no wind blew there. His throat burned
with an injury it had never received.

The tourist consulted his watch.

"Ten minutes past nine."

"T'ank you; much opliged."

The tramp touched his cap and withdrew.

These were not coincidences. They were repetitions. Signals. Warnings!

Martin caught the tourist's sleeve. "For God's sake, do not
take that road."

The man disengaged himself, not angrily but with the firmness
one employs towards the unbalanced.

"My dear sir, you are overwrought."

"Yes. Perhaps. But listen—have you money upon you? A large
sum? In a belt beneath your clothes?"

The stranger's face changed.

That change ruined everything. Alarm became suspicion, and suspicion hardened
into fear—not fear of the road, but of Martin.

"I do not know who you are," he said, "or
how you obtained that information. If you molest me again,
I shall call the police."

He walked away.

Martin remained beside the gate. He could still follow. He
could tell the constable, invent a tangible accusation, raise an
alarm, seize the man by force. But by what right?
Knowledge acquired by some psychic trespass remained morally dubious. Suppose
the warning itself caused the event? Suppose his interference drove
the stranger towards an alternative danger? The mysterious message had
been "interpolated" in Martin's consciousness, but no command accompanied it.
Perhaps he had been shown, not to prevent, but merely
because minds sometimes overlap as roads cross upon a map.

No right, he thought. No evidence. No right.

The phrases balanced each other with the neatness of a
fraudulent account.

At eleven-ten Martin took the train.

Two days later, in his London lodging, he opened the
evening newspaper. The paragraph appeared beneath a heading concerning county
affairs.

An elderly visitor had been found dead beside the road
over Litacy Hill. His throat had been cut. A considerable
sum, carried in a belt beneath his clothing, was missing.
Police wished to question two foreign tramps, believed to be
Germans, who had been observed near Penrydd on the previous
evening.

Martin read the report twice.

Then he laid the paper upon the table, aligning its
edge unconsciously with the edge of the blotting-pad.

For many years afterwards he maintained that he had possessed
no right to interfere. No legal right, certainly; no rational
right; no right that he could have explained to police,
magistrate, or murdered man. He repeated this with increasing precision
whenever conscience reopened the account.

But always, beneath the careful totals of his argument, there
remained an unexplained discrepancy.

And sometimes, waking in darkness, he felt again the pressure
of another man's belt against his body, heard footsteps advancing
upon a white road, and knew that a warning, however
strangely addressed, becomes the property of him who receives it.

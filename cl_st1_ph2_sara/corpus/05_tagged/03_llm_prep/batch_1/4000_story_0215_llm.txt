### At Daybreak

Among the many faces which looked up at me from
the narrow cots of the army hospital, there is one
that has never faded, though years have softened harsher memories,
and time, that merciful nurse, has laid cool fingers upon
wounds of the spirit which once seemed past all healing.
I have forgotten names that were shouted through the wards
in pain or delirium; forgotten voices that blessed me, and
others that cursed with equal fervor the hand which hurt
them in order to save; forgotten even certain death-scenes which,
at the time, appeared graven upon my heart as with
a sword. But John Mercer remains,—not as I first saw
him, bloodless beneath the surgeon's hands, nor as he lay
in the solemn quiet after all was over, but as
he looked in the interval between battle and immortality: patient,
comely, and serene, with the grave courtesy of one who
had obeyed every earthly command, and waited only for the
last word of his Divine Commander.

He came to us after one of those battles whose
glory was printed in great letters at home, while its
misery arrived in dumb procession at our door. All day
the ambulances had jolted in; all night stretchers passed along
the corridors; and before morning our clean wards had become
a place of confusion, anguish, and heroic endurance, such as
no pen can justly picture. Mud stained the floors; bloody
coats lay in heaps; the air was heavy with ether,
fever, wet wool, and that indescribable odor which belongs to
crowded suffering. Men called for water, mothers, wives, or death;
attendants hurried to and fro; surgeons, pale with fatigue, worked
as if their own bodies were machines incapable of weariness;
and we nurses learned anew the hard lesson that pity
must have hands as well as tears.

John was carried in at dusk. He had been shot
through the right lung, and had lost so much blood
that his face possessed the clear pallor of marble. Yet
there was nothing wasted or delicate in him. He was
a large man, broad through the shoulders, straight in limb,
and fashioned with that grave beauty which strength gives when
it has long served useful labor. His hands told his
history before his tongue did: they were darkened by sun
and smoke, scarred across the palms, and powerful even in
helplessness. I afterward learned that he had been a blacksmith
in a little Virginia town, and could swing the great
hammer from dawn until evening; but the same hands had
mended his sister's toys, shod a neighbor's poor horse without
payment, and planted roses about the doorstep for his mother.

When they lifted him to the bed, his eyes opened
and rested upon us without alarm.

"Easy, boys," he said; "don't strain yourselves. I'm a heavy
load."

One of the bearers laughed, though his lips trembled.

"You carried Billy Crane near half a mile yesterday, John."

"That was different. Billy couldn't walk."

He fainted before the sentence was finished; but the quiet
self-forgetfulness of it remained with me, and should have taught
me sooner what manner of man had been committed to
our care.

For two days he spoke little, asked for nothing, and
bore every necessary torment in silence. Because the ward was
full of importunate suffering,—poor shattered creatures who begged, groaned, or
caught at my dress whenever I passed,—I gave them much
of my time, believing that John, who never called, had
no need of me beyond medicine, food, and clean bandages.
He thanked me for each service with a respectful "ma'am,"
turned his face to the wall, and seemed content to
be left alone. His dignity intimidated me. There are persons
whose sorrow invites consolation, as an open door invites a
guest; his was like a shut chamber before which one
pauses, unwilling to intrude.

One afternoon, however, I returned unexpectedly to fetch a cup
I had forgotten. The ward was unusually still. A pale
strip of winter sunshine lay across the floor, and beyond
the high windows the bare branches moved against a cold
blue sky. John believed himself unobserved. He had turned his
head toward a neighboring cot, where a boy scarcely seventeen
slept with his cheek against his mother's hand. The woman,
newly arrived from the country, sat bending over him, smoothing
his hair and whispering some little household name which made
him smile even in fever.

I saw John watching them.

No envy disfigured his face; there was only a hunger
so patient and so profound that I felt myself rebuked.
Presently he raised one rough hand, as if it moved
of its own accord, and passed it once over his
cropped hair. Then, hearing my step, he let it fall
and resumed the composed expression I had mistaken for independence.

That simple gesture pierced me more sharply than reproach. I
had attended his body and neglected the lonely heart within
it. Because he was strong, I had denied him tenderness;
because he would not beg, I had supposed he did
not desire. We are all unjust in this way, giving
freely to clamorous grief and passing by the silent sufferer,
who may need us most. Pride is sometimes only pain
standing upright.

I went to him, carrying no cup now, but a
basin of warm water, a clean towel, and the small
brush I kept for such offices.

"John," I said, with more humility than he could have
guessed, "your hair is untidy. Will you let me set
it right?"

A faint color came into his face.

"If you've got time, ma'am."

"I have time."

I bathed his forehead, brushed back the thick brown hair,
straightened the coarse hospital shirt, and arranged the blanket beneath
his wounded shoulder. These were trifles; but human love, when
it can do no great thing, puts its whole soul
into trifles, and so makes life beautiful and outlives death.
John lay perfectly still. Once he shut his eyes, and
I thought the movement of the brush had hurt him;
but when I asked, he answered quickly,—

"No, ma'am. It feels like home."

From that hour he was my special charge, though no
agreement passed between us. I brought him flowers when any
could be found,—a few snowdrops from the chaplain's garden, a
sprig of cedar, once a brave little cluster of violets
that had traveled in a parcel from the North. He
liked them with a shy fondness, touching them to his
lips before I set them in a bottle beside the
bed. I read to him in the evenings, not long
sermons, for he preferred the histories of David and Jonathan,
and the Psalms which speak of mountains, storms, and the
shadow of wings. Sometimes he talked of Virginia: of the
forge beside a sycamore tree; of the sparks flying upward
on summer nights; of his mother sitting near the door
with her sewing; of Ben, the next brother, steady but
too ready to doubt himself; of Ruth and little May;
and of Thomas, who could mimic every bird in the
woods.

He never praised himself, and I learned his virtues as
one discovers a spring beneath leaves,—by following the signs of
refreshment it has spread around. His comrades told me that
he had been the strongest man in his company, yet
never used his strength to shame a weaker one; that
he had shared his blanket with a feverish boy through
a night of freezing rain; that he had gone back
under fire for Billy Crane, and received his mortal wound
while bringing water to men who could not reach the
stream. When I repeated some part of this, he frowned
slightly.

"They'd have done the same, ma'am."

"Not all."

"Well, most," he said, and no argument could move him
from that generous falsehood.

The surgeon, Dr. Hale, had watched him with increasing gravity.
One evening, after dressing the wound, he beckoned me into
the passage. The lamp there burned dimly, and through an
open door came the sharp cry of a man under
examination.

"He cannot last," the doctor said. "The lung is badly
torn, and inflammation has begun. A few days, perhaps less."

I had known it; yet hope, that obstinate little rebel,
had kept its flag flying in some hidden corner of
my mind. At his words it fell.

"Does he know?"

"He suspects. He asked me this morning, and I put
him off. He trusts you. Tell him when you think
best."

"Why must I?"

The question was childish, almost angry; but I had seen
death enough to hate its errands, though not enough to
refuse them.

"Because," said Dr. Hale gently, "he has a family, and
possibly things to say. I would tell him myself, but
he speaks more freely to you."

Then he went back to his work, leaving me in
the passage with the sentence laid upon me like a
command.

That night I could not speak. John noticed my constraint,
but spared me by pretending sleep. The next morning he
was weaker. Four small marks, made by the surgeon's fingers
while examining his chest, stood white against the bruised skin;
his breath came with a soft inward catch; and from
time to time a red stain touched the handkerchief at
his lips. Yet his eyes were clear.

After the ward had been put in order, he said,—

"Ma'am, I wish you'd sit down a minute, if you
ain't too busy."

I sat beside him and took up the flowers, foolishly
rearranging them to avoid his look.

"The doctor didn't say much yesterday."

"No."

"I guess that means there ain't much good to say."

My hands became still.

"John—"

"You needn't make it easy. I'd rather know."

There are truths which become cruel when adorned. I put
the flowers down and laid my hand upon his.

"The wound cannot heal. Dr. Hale thinks you have only
a little while."

He drew one long, difficult breath, and looked toward the
window. Outside, snow had begun to fall, wandering slowly through
the gray air.

"A little while," he repeated.

I waited for fear, protest, or that stunned disbelief with
which the young often hear the first announcement of death.
None came. His fingers closed about mine,—not in terror, but
as if to thank me for honesty.

"Well, ma'am, I'm obliged to you. I've been thinking so."

"Are you afraid?"

He considered the question as seriously as if I had
asked his judgment on a piece of work at the
forge.

"No. I don't reckon I am. I'd like to live.
There's a difference."

That answer, so simple and so wise, has remained with
me through all the years. Courage is not the absence
of love for life; it is the acceptance of a
greater claim.

He was silent awhile, then said,—

"Mother'll take it hard."

The first shadow crossed his face.

"Your mother is not alone?"

"There's Ben. He's twenty. A good boy,—better read than me.
But I've been the head since father died. The forge
is mine, and there's payments on the place. Ruth's fifteen,
May's eleven, and Thomas is only eight. I kept thinking
I'd get home and straighten things."

His voice failed, and for the first time I saw
anguish conquer his bodily composure. It was not death that
shook him, but unfinished duty.

"Do you regret enlisting?" I asked softly.

"No, ma'am."

The answer came at once.

"Even now?"

He turned his grave eyes upon me.

"I didn't go because I wanted glory. There ain't much
glory in wet shoes, bad bacon, and seeing good fellows
hurt. I went because it seemed like the job had
come to my door. If every man stayed home on
account of his own folks, whose folks would be safe?
Mother cried, but she understood. I'm sorry to leave 'em
burdened. I ain't sorry I went."

Poor rhetoric beside the polished declarations of statesmen, perhaps; yet
to me it was nobler than any trumpet-speech. His goodness
had not been taught to phrase itself; it had bloomed
into character, as naturally as a sound tree bears fruit.

"Could you write for me?" he asked after a time.
"To Ben?"

I brought paper, pen, and a book to rest upon
my knee. John watched the white page with solemn attention.

"Start, 'Dear Ben.' No,—'My dear brother Ben.'"

I wrote it.

"Tell him I've been wounded, and the doctor don't think
I'll get over it. Say I'm easy."

At this I glanced up, for every breath cost him
pain.

"Write it, please, ma'am. Mother'll want to know."

So I wrote the merciful untruth.

"Tell him the money in the town bank is for
Mother, all of it. Mr. Willis owes twelve dollars for
the gray mare's shoeing and wagon work, but Ben needn't
press him till harvest. The note on the forge is
in the blue box. Tell Ruth she must keep on
with school if she can, because she learns quick. Tell
Thomas the little hammer's his, but not to use it
till Ben shows him. And tell May—"

He paused, smiling faintly.

"Tell May I didn't lose the red thread she tied
around my Testament. It's here."

He touched the pocket of his coat, which hung at
the foot of the bed, stained and cut where the
surgeons had opened it.

"Now tell Ben he's the head. He must be patient
with Mother, because grief makes folks say things they don't
mean. He mustn't sell the south field. Father set store
by it. If trouble comes, he's to ask Uncle Caleb,
but not unless he has to."

I wrote rapidly, blinded more than once.

"Put in that I trust him."

"Only that?"

"That's a big thing, ma'am."

Indeed it was. I set the sentence down alone.

He dictated messages to each of them, plain and tender;
then, after long consideration, added,—

"Say I died believing I done right, and bearing no
grudge against any man. Say I'd like an answer, if
it can get here. Maybe there'll be time."

We both knew how little time remained, but I sealed
the letter and sent it with urgent entreaties by the
first post.

During the next day the tide of life ebbed visibly.
His breathing grew harsher, fever burned in his cheeks, and
a gray veil seemed at moments to pass across his
face; yet he never complained. When pain wrung sweat from
him, he would say only, "It's a rough spell," as
though speaking of weather that must presently clear. I cooled
his head, gave him bits of ice, lifted him when
the wounded lung oppressed him, and performed those small offices
through which affection, helpless before death, still maintains its sacred
protest.

His comrades understood that he was going. Men able to
walk found reasons to pass his cot; those confined to
bed watched him across the ward. Their admiration had no
ceremony in it. One left half an orange beside him;
another offered a treasured newspaper; a third, whose arm had
been taken off, awkwardly tucked the blanket about John's feet
with his remaining hand. Such gifts were poor in value
and royal in love.

Late in the afternoon Ned Price was brought from another
building. He had been John's closest comrade,—a slight, dark-eyed Maryland
boy, with one leg bound from ankle to thigh. Two
attendants carried him in a chair and placed him near
the bed.

For a moment neither spoke. They looked at one another
with that full knowledge which sometimes passes between friends and
makes words seem almost an indignity. I thought of David
and Jonathan; yet these two humble soldiers needed no ancient
names to ennoble their affection.

At last Ned leaned forward.

"John."

"Ned."

"They tell me you're moving on."

"I guess I am."

"I wish I was going with you."

"No," said John, with sudden firmness. "You get well. Your
wife's waiting."

Ned's mouth trembled.

"You saved me at the creek."

"I helped carry you. That's all."

"You went back."

"You'd have gone back for me."

There was a pause. Ned reached out, and John lifted
his hand with painful effort. Their fingers met and held.

"Good-by, old fellow," Ned whispered.

"Good-by."

"I won't forget."

"I know."

That was all. No eloquence could have deepened it. Ned
bent his head over their joined hands; John looked at
him with a tenderness almost brotherly, and for one instant
the strong, reserved man's face became childlike. Then the attendants
carried Ned away. As he passed through the door, he
turned and lifted his hand. John answered the gesture; afterward
his arm fell heavily upon the blanket.

Night came. Lamps were lowered, footsteps hushed, and the ward
settled into that troubled quiet which is not rest, but
only weariness ceasing to struggle aloud. Snow had stopped; the
clouds thinned; and a cold moon shone upon rows of
beds, making the white bandages strangely luminous. I sat beside
John, counting the intervals between his breaths.

Once he asked me to open the window. I raised
it a little, though the air was bitter. The wind
entered, clean and sharp, bringing a faint smell of wood
smoke from the city.

"That's good," he murmured. "Like outdoors."

Later he became restless. His hand moved over the coverlet
as if seeking some familiar tool.

"The fire's low, Ben," he said. "Put another bar in.
Thomas, stand back from the sparks."

I leaned close.

"John, you are in the hospital. I am here."

His eyes found me and cleared.

"Yes, ma'am. I know."

Near midnight the pain grew terrible. The doctor gave what
relief he could, but death sometimes approaches by a road
so steep that every step shakes the failing body. John's
chest labored; the muscles stood out upon his neck; blood
stained his lips. Still no cry escaped him. I begged
him not to resist for our sake.

"I ain't," he whispered. "Only—trying—to get through."

It seemed to me then that he was like a
soldier climbing under fire toward a final height,—wounded, spent, yet
obedient to some command inaudible to us. Around him the
ward had awakened. Men watched in silence; one prayed beneath
his breath; another turned his face to the wall and
wept. Many there were maimed, fevered, or condemned to lifelong
weakness; yet each appeared to forget his own misery in
reverence for that greater patience.

At two o'clock he asked if the letter had come.

"Not yet."

"It don't seem possible it could. But I thought maybe."

"You may still have it in the morning."

"Yes, ma'am."

He knew I spoke from love, not hope, and spared
me the shame of correction.

Toward four the struggle lessened. This frightened me more than
the pain, for the quiet was not recovery, but exhaustion.
His hand had grown cold, though the fever still flushed
his face. I wrapped it between mine and rubbed the
broad fingers, observing, with a sorrow absurdly fixed upon trifles,
the black traces of iron still buried in the lines
of his skin. Those hands had worked faithfully, defended the
weak, supported a household, and carried a wounded friend; now
they could not lift a cup.

"Are you comfortable?" I asked.

"Very."

"Do you want anything?"

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Just stay, if you can."

"I will."

A little later he said, "Mother used to hold my
hand when I was sick. I was a big boy,
but she always did."

I could not answer. I only clasped his hand more
closely.

He smiled.

"That's right."

So the stately soldier, who had endured battle without faltering
and death without complaint, went back at the last to
the earliest comfort he had known. There was no weakness
in it. The bravest heart is not made less manly
by its longing for home; rather, affection is the secret
source from which true courage springs.

The eastern windows slowly changed. Black glass became gray; the
gray grew pearl-colored; and presently a narrow red streak lay
along the horizon, bright as a banner seen far off.
Dawn entered the ward so gently that for a moment
no one perceived it. Faces emerged from shadow, pale and
watchful; brass buttons glimmered; the flowers by John's bed showed
their faded purple.

He opened his eyes.

"Morning?" he asked.

"Yes. The sun is coming."

"I'd like—to see."

With the orderly's help I raised him. His head rested
against my shoulder, and we turned him toward the window.
The first beam touched the roofs beyond us, then climbed
the wall and fell across his face. It transfigured him.
The marks of suffering remained, but something serene had already
mastered them; the mortal wreck was still before us, yet
the immortal voyager seemed looking beyond it toward a shore
we could not see.

"Pretty," he breathed.

After a moment he said, "Tell Mother—I remembered."

"I will."

"And Ben—trust him."

"Yes."

His fingers tightened once around mine.

"Thank you, ma'am. You've been—good to me."

"No, John," I whispered. "You have been good to us."

Perhaps he heard; perhaps the voices of earth had already
grown distant. His gaze remained fixed upon the widening day.
One breath came, then another, faint as the turn of
an ebbing tide. There was a pause. His hand relaxed,
but I continued to hold it, unwilling to believe that
a presence so strong could depart so quietly.

The red light broadened.

John was dead.

For several minutes no one moved. Then a soldier in
the nearest cot raised himself and saluted. Another did the
same. The gesture passed silently down the ward,—poor arms, bandaged
hands, trembling fingers lifted in farewell. No drum sounded, no
flag covered him, no officer pronounced his praise; yet I
have never witnessed honors more august. A king might have
envied that homage, because it was paid not to rank,
but to character.

We laid him straight, closed his eyes, and smoothed the
thick hair he had once been too shy to ask
me to brush. With the pallor of death upon him,
he resembled one of those old knights carved upon a
tomb,—hands composed, features calm, warfare ended. I placed the violets
on his breast. Their color looked painfully alive against the
whiteness of his shirt.

An hour later the mail arrived.

I knew the letter before it was given to me.
The address was written in an uncertain hand, and beneath
his name someone had added, "Army Hospital—please hurry." The postmark
was from his town in Virginia.

For one rebellious instant I hated the innocent paper. It
had crossed rivers, camps, and ruined roads; it had been
carried faithfully by strangers; it had hurried as well as
lifeless things may hurry,—and yet it was late. Only an
hour late. What is an hour to the healthy, who
squander so many? To him it had been the measure
between longing and fulfillment.

I opened it, for I could not bear that the
words should remain unread. Ben wrote that their mother sent
her blessing; that the forge would be kept; that the
south field should never be sold; that Ruth would stay
in school, Thomas should have the little hammer, and May
still tied red thread around everything she wished not to
lose. They had heard he was wounded, but believed he
would recover. "Come home as soon as you can," the
letter ended. "We are all waiting. Mother says there is
no place by the fire that looks right without you."

I read it beside his bed. Then I folded it
and placed it in his cold hand.

When they came to take him away, I removed the
plain ring he wore,—his father's, as I had learned,—and cut
one lock of brown hair from his temple. These, with
the letter, the Testament and its faded red thread, I
sent to his mother. I wrote what comfort truth allowed:
that he had not been alone; that he had spoken
of them all; that he had met death without fear;
and that at the last his hand had been held
as she herself would have held it. I did not
tell her how often I had failed before I learned
to understand his silence. That confession belonged to my own
heart, where it has remained a lasting admonition.

Long afterward I visited other wards, heard other cannons, and
watched the war consume youth as a furnace consumes flowers
cast into its flame. Men more famous than John Mercer
survived in memory; monuments rose; speeches were made; histories gathered
up the names of generals and the movements of armies.
No marble, so far as I know, bears the name
of the Virginia blacksmith who carried a comrade from the
creek and died thinking of a mortgage, a schoolbook, a
child's hammer, and his mother's hand.

Yet I cannot call him forgotten.

Whenever strength is gentle, whenever duty is accepted without ambition,
whenever suffering refuses to burden those already burdened, something of
his spirit lives. The world may preserve worn-out and worthless
bodies while casting its noblest youth into untimely graves; but
death, though it destroys the instrument, cannot annul the music
that has once awakened courage or compassion in another soul.
John's life was obscure, his speech unpolished, his grave perhaps
unmarked; nevertheless, he taught me what no book had taught,—that
the heroic may wear a workman's scars, that dignity may
lie silent upon a hospital cot, and that the simplest
human touch, bestowed before it is too late, may become
a sacrament.

Even now, when dawn reddens a winter sky, I see
again that long ward, the faded violets, the watchful soldiers,
and the noble face turned toward morning. I feel the
broad hand growing cold within my own; I hear the
broken voice saying, "Just stay, if you can."

And I am grateful that I stayed.

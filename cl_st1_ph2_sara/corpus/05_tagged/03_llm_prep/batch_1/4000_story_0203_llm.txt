### Marjorie's Three Gifts

On the morning of Marjorie Bell's twelfth birthday, the rain
came through the roof and dropped precisely into the blue
saucer on the table.

"Plink!" said the first drop.

"Plink—plink!" answered two others, as if they were little wet
messengers knocking to be admitted.

Marjorie looked up from the worn story-book which lay open
before her, and laughed.

"You may come in if you like," she said, "but
you mustn't wet the fairy tale."

Then she moved the saucer two inches to the left,
where it caught the drops with great cleverness, and went
on reading.

It was a proper fairy tale, with a woodcutter's daughter,
a crooked Brownie, a silver charm, and a prince in
a green cloak. The girl had been very poor, but
because she had shared her last crust with the Brownie,
he gave her a lucky penny that never failed while
she worked honestly. Afterward a fairy gave her a charm
which enabled her to see sunshine even on cloudy days;
and, last of all, a prince carried her to a
kingdom where every one loved her, from the old king
upon his throne to the smallest yellow duck in the
palace pond.

"Dear me!" sighed Marjorie, shutting the book. "Why did all
the lovely things happen long ago?"

The kettle began to sing on the little stove; the
clock ticked; the rain whispered against the window; and the
gray cat, Pudding, washed his whiskers as if the question
did not concern him.

Marjorie rested her chin in both hands. She was a
small, brown-haired lass, with large eyes which could look sober
or merry quicker than a sunbeam can slip behind a
cloud. Her gingham frock was faded at the shoulders, and
the toes of her shoes had been polished until they
almost forgot that they were scuffed. But her white apron
was as clean as Granny's soap and Marjorie's own busy
fingers could make it.

"Brownies and fairies never come here," she continued. "And princes
always choose girls with golden hair."

"Do they, deary?" asked Granny Bell from the doorway.

Marjorie jumped up. "I thought you were asleep!"

"I was resting my eyes. Ears don't rest so easily."
Granny smiled and came in, leaning upon her hickory stick.
She was thin and bent, but her eyes had a
pleasant twinkle, like two stars seen through a frosty window.
"What is this about princes?"

"It's only the story. The poor girl gets three gifts
and goes to a beautiful kingdom. I was wishing such
things happened now."

Granny lifted the kettle. "Perhaps they do."

"Here?"

"Why not?"

Marjorie looked around the tiny kitchen. One chair had a
lame leg; a pan stood beneath the worst leak; the
wallpaper showed pale patches where roses had once bloomed; and
a green pea, escaped from yesterday's dinner, had rolled beneath
the dresser and was being watched by Pudding with the
solemn attention of a tiger.

"It doesn't look much like a kingdom," Marjorie said.

"Kingdoms," observed Granny, "aren't always known by their wallpaper."

She set two cups upon the table. There was no
frosted cake, for flour and sugar cost money, but Granny
had made a round biscuit and put twelve currants upon
it, one for every year. A purple violet stood in
the middle.

"To my little maid," she said.

Marjorie hugged her so heartily that the hickory stick fell
down and Pudding fled beneath the stove, convinced that an
earthquake had begun.

"It is the sweetest cake in the world!" cried Marjorie.

She divided it carefully, giving Granny the larger half, though
Granny as carefully exchanged the pieces while Marjorie was looking
at the violet.

After breakfast Marjorie washed the cups, swept the floor, shook
Granny's pillow, fed the hens, and carried a pan of
meal to Speckle, their red cow. The rain had stopped,
and the world came sparkling out of its bath. Every
leaf wore a diamond; the puddles held pieces of blue
sky; and a robin on the fence gave three cheerful
notes, perhaps because he had no roof to mend.

Beside the woodshed stood Marjorie's birthday treasure, a little tin
savings bank shaped like a cottage. Billy Hart had made
it from an old tea-can, cutting a slit in the
roof and painting crooked windows on the sides.

"It looks richer than it is," Marjorie told the robin.

The robin cocked his head, which was impudent of him,
as he possessed no bank at all.

Marjorie had great plans for that cottage. She intended to
gather berries for Mrs. Lane, weed gardens, carry parcels, and
sell bunches of wild flowers to summer visitors. With her
earnings she would buy school-books and stout shoes. After that
she would save toward shingles for the roof, a shawl
for Granny, and a red ribbon for herself. The red
ribbon came last, but it shone very brightly in her
thoughts.

She had not yet put a single coin into the
bank.

"I shall begin to-day," she said firmly.

Taking a tin cup, she started toward the spring for
water. The lane was soft and brown, with silver pools
lying in its wheel-ruts. Daisies nodded along the wall, buttercups
held up golden mirrors, and the wet grasses caught at
Marjorie's skirt as if begging her to stay and play.

Near the old stone bridge she heard a man calling.

"Steady, Button! Stand still, you foolish four-legged fiddle!"

An old man sat beside a small gray pony. One
of the man's boots was strapped about the ankle, and
a crutch lay in the grass. The pony, who appeared
to have strong opinions, backed away whenever his master tried
to reach the stream.

"Can I help?" asked Marjorie.

"You can, if you are not afraid of dragons."

Marjorie examined the pony. "He doesn't look like a dragon."

"That is his cunning. He disguises himself."

The old man's round spectacles flashed, and his white eyebrows
danced above them. He looked so like the crooked Brownie
in the story-book that Marjorie nearly dropped her cup.

She took Button's bridle.

"Come, sir," she said. "You mustn't be troublesome."

Button considered this advice, snorted upon her apron, and allowed
himself to be led to the water.

The old man explained that a stone had worked into
the pony's shoe, and, because of his lame leg, he
could not remove it. Marjorie knelt in the damp grass,
soothed Button with one hand, and worked the pebble free
with the other. Then she filled her cup again and
again, letting the pony drink while the old man rested
beneath a willow.

"That is enough," he said at last. "You have watered
my beast, muddied your knees, and missed half an hour
of your holiday."

"Oh, it is no matter."

"Is it a holiday?"

"My birthday. I'm twelve."

"Twelve!" cried the stranger. "A grave age. At twelve I
had already decided to become a pirate, a peddler, and
President. None of the plans succeeded."

Marjorie laughed.

"What do you mean to become?"

"Useful," she answered after a moment.

"Upon my word, that is the largest profession of all.
How will you begin?"

She told him about the tea-can bank, the berries, gardens,
books, shoes, shingles, shawl, and, rather shyly, the red ribbon.

The old man listened as soberly as a judge, though
his spectacles kept dancing.

"You expect the bank to fill itself, perhaps?"

"No, sir! I mean to earn every bit."

"Then here is the first bit."

From an old leather purse he took a small gold
coin and laid it in her palm. It shone there
like a captured sunbeam.

Marjorie stared. "I can't take it!"

"You can, because you earned it. Button and I might
have remained here until winter. Then he would have become
an icicle, and I should have had to sell him
as a chandelier."

"But it is too much."

"Not if it teaches you to begin. Money honestly earned
is a good servant, little lass, though a poor master.
Put it in your bank, and let it remind you
that willing hands generally find work."

He climbed into his cart with difficulty. Button, now relieved
of his stone and apparently of his temper, trotted away.

"Good-by!" called the old man. "Remember your profession!"

"I will!" cried Marjorie.

She ran home, clasping the coin so tightly that its
edge marked her hand. Into the slit of the tin
cottage it went with a brave little clink.

"My first gift," she whispered. "A lucky gold piece from
a Brownie!"

Then, because Marjorie was sensible even when she was imagining,
she added, "It will only be lucky if I work."

Before dinner she gathered a basket of early strawberries from
the sunny bank behind the cow pasture. Granny said Mrs.
Lane would give six cents for them, and six cents
meant that the golden guest in the bank need not
live alone.

The afternoon grew warm and blue. Clouds sailed overhead like
white swans; bees hummed among the clover; and Speckle stood
in the shade switching her tail with the air of
a lady who disapproved of flies but did not care
to argue with them.

Marjorie was crossing the meadow when the wind came racing
over the hill. It tossed her hair, turned the maple
leaves silver-side out, and then, seeing a broad straw hat
upon a stranger's head, snatched it off and went rollicking
away with it.

"Oh!" cried the stranger.

The hat rolled over the grass, leaped a tuft of
daisies, danced around a thistle, and sailed toward the brook.
Marjorie set down her basket and flew after it. The
daisies seemed to stretch their white fingers to catch the
runaway; a grasshopper sprang aside in astonishment; and a frog,
seated on the bank, watched with round golden eyes.

Just before the hat reached the water, Marjorie caught it
by one blue ribbon.

The frog plunged into the brook, perhaps from disappointment.

"Thank you, child!" said the owner, coming up breathless.

She was a tall lady dressed in white, with a
pale green umbrella. A little wooden box hung from her
shoulder, and she carried a folding stool. Her soft gray
hair had been loosened by the wind, and her face
was brown from the sun.

"You have saved my best hat from drowning."

"I'm glad," said Marjorie, smoothing the ribbon.

"And at the cost of your berries!"

The basket had overturned. Red strawberries lay scattered in the
grass, and one bold robin had already seized the largest.

Marjorie's face fell. Six cents were rapidly becoming five, and
the robin looked prepared to reduce them to four.

The lady helped gather the fruit. "I am afraid some
are crushed."

"They'll do for jam," Marjorie said, though she had wanted
very much to put those six cents into the bank.
"The hat might have been spoiled entirely."

"That is a cheerful arithmetic. You subtract your loss and
count my gain."

Marjorie glanced at the wooden box. "Are you an artist?"

"Yes. I came to paint the meadow."

The lady opened the box, which contained cakes of color,
brushes, and small white boards. Marjorie watched, enchanted, as the
green field, purple hill, silver brook, and red cow appeared
beneath the quick brush. Even Speckle's tail became beautiful in
paint, though its actual employment was still only the discouragement
of flies.

"I wish I could make lovely things," Marjorie said.

"You may learn."

"We haven't money for paints."

"One can make loveliness without them."

"How?"

"By finding it first. That is the important lesson."

The artist looked toward the Bell cottage on the hill.
Its shingles were dark and uneven; one shutter hung crookedly;
bean vines climbed about the door; and the tin pan
beneath the roof leak could be seen through the open
window.

"Is that your home?"

"Yes," said Marjorie, wishing for once that it were larger.

"It is charming."

Marjorie supposed the lady was being kind.

"It's very shabby."

"Then we must look again."

The artist set a fresh board before her. She painted
the cottage beneath the apple tree, with sunshine lying warm
upon the weathered wall. The crooked shutter became a patch
of blue shadow; the bean vines made an emerald arch;
the old brass knob shone like a tiny star. Through
the doorway one could see Granny's white cap, and in
the yard the tin pan, turned upside down to dry,
gleamed like a silver shield.

Marjorie leaned closer.

"Why, it looks like our house—and yet it looks happy."

"It is happy, isn't it?"

"Oh, yes!"

"Then I have painted what is true. Shabbiness is true,
too, but it is not the whole truth. There are
nearly always two sides to common things, as there are
two sides to a leaf. Seek the sunny one whenever
you honestly can."

The lady wrote beneath the picture:

**TO MARJORIE—THE SUNNY SIDE OF HOME.**

Then she gave it to her.

Marjorie's eyes widened. "For me?"

"For the rescuer of my hat."

The wind fluttered the blue ribbons again, but the artist
held them firmly.

"Are you a fairy?" Marjorie asked before she could stop
herself.

The lady laughed, and even Speckle turned to look.

"Not before luncheon. Afterward I cannot say."

Marjorie carried the painting home as carefully as if it
had been a window into fairyland. Granny hung it above
the table, covering a spot where the paper roses had
faded almost entirely away.

"Now," said Granny, "we have a picture gallery."

Marjorie looked from the painted cottage to the true one.
She saw the worn floor, but she saw also the
square of sunlight upon it. She saw the chipped cups,
but noticed how their blue rims matched Granny's eyes. She
saw the old stove, black and squat, but smelled the
soup bubbling upon it.

"My second gift," she thought. "A charm for seeing happiness."

At four o'clock a tremendous clatter arose in the lane.

There were shouts, jingling bells, squeaking wheels, and a shrill
music which might have been a trumpet, though it proved
to be Billy Hart blowing through a rolled piece of
birch bark.

A wagon appeared, drawn by two patient farm horses decorated
with ferns. It was filled with children. Red-cheeked Nell Lane
sat beside solemn Peter Cobb; the three Mason twins waved
paper flags; and tiny Ruthie Green wore a necklace of
daisies which reached nearly to her knees.

Billy stood in front, holding a maple branch like a
sceptre. Upon his head was a crown of braided clover.

"Hail!" he cried. "Is the Princess Marjorie Bell within?"

Marjorie ran to the door.

"I'm not a princess."

"Then we'll make you queen. It's quicker. Come on, Marjy!
This is a s'prise party."

"But Granny—"

"Granny planned it," said Granny, appearing behind her with a
smile.

The children seized Marjorie—not roughly, though with a good deal
of royal enthusiasm—and carried her to the wagon. Nell set
a crown of daisies on her hair. Peter presented her
with a sceptre made from an old broom handle wrapped
in gilt paper, and the Mason twins cried, "Long live
Queen Marjorie!" at different moments, because they had not practiced
sufficiently.

The wagon rolled toward Pine Island, a green hill encircled
by the winding brook. Billy blew his birch trumpet; the
children sang; dogs barked from doorways; and a flock of
geese followed for half a mile under the impression that
the procession required a white-uniformed guard.

At Pine Island they spread blankets beneath the trees. There
were egg sandwiches, ginger cookies, apples, pink lemonade, and a
birthday cake made by four mothers together. It leaned a
little to one side, but twelve white candles stood proudly
upon it.

"For me?" Marjorie whispered.

"For you, goose," said Nell, kissing her.

They played hide-and-seek among the pines, sailed bark boats, and
held a royal tournament with beanbags. Billy, as Prince of
the Birch Trumpet, challenged Peter, Lord of the Lemonade, and
was defeated when he tripped over his own sceptre. Ruthie
found a beetle and appointed it Minister of the Treasury.
The frog from the brook, or one of his relations,
croaked during the speeches.

Before the cake was cut, Billy commanded silence.

"Queen Marjorie has to make a royal wish."

Marjorie looked around the circle of dear, sun-browned faces. She
had often supposed that children liked Nell best because Nell
had curls, or Billy best because he could whistle, or
the Mason twins best because there were three of them.
She had never imagined that so many friends would spend
a whole afternoon making her happy.

"I don't need to wish," she said softly.

"Everybody needs a wish," insisted Peter.

"Then I wish Granny were here."

At that moment there was a rustle among the ferns,
and Granny appeared upon the old gray mare, led by
Mrs. Lane.

The cheer that followed frightened three crows from the pines
and caused the candles to tremble.

Granny sat in the place of honor while Marjorie cut
the cake. The largest slice was offered to Her Majesty,
but Marjorie divided it among Ruthie and the Mason twins,
who had dropped theirs frosting-side downward. Billy noticed this, although
he pretended to be occupied in driving away a mosquito.

The sun sank slowly, laying golden roads across the grass.
Fireflies lit their tiny lanterns. The brook turned rose-colored, then
purple, and evening folded the meadow in cool blue wings.

At last the wagon carried them home. The children sang
until their voices grew sleepy. Billy's clover crown slipped over
one eye; Ruthie lay with her head in Marjorie's lap;
and Peter guarded the empty lemonade jug as if it
contained the royal jewels.

At the cottage gate they all kissed Marjorie good-night, except
Billy, who shook hands in a very dignified manner and
then thrust a bunch of red clover at her.

"Princes generally bring roses," he muttered, "but the cow ate
ours."

"It is prettier than roses," Marjorie assured him.

When the wagon had vanished, she and Granny went inside.
The rain clouds were gone, and moonlight entered through the
window, turning the humble room to silver. Above the table
hung the artist's picture. In the tin cottage-bank rested the
gold coin. Upon Marjorie's hair clung one small daisy from
her crown.

Granny tucked her into bed beneath the patchwork quilt.

"Did fairy tales happen to-day?" she asked.

Marjorie considered.

"A Brownie gave me a lucky coin," she said. "Only
it means I must work. A fairy gave me a
charm, but it means looking for the sunny side. And
a prince took me to a kingdom where everybody loved
me."

"Indeed!"

"Billy wasn't much like a prince when he fell over
the broomstick," Marjorie admitted. "But perhaps princes do fall sometimes."

"As every one knows, deary, the best of them do."

Marjorie looked around the little room—the cracked pitcher filled with
clover, Granny's spectacles upon the table, Pudding curled like a
gray muff at the foot of the bed, and the
moon laying a bright hand upon the worn floor.

"I'm glad the prince brought me back," she said. "I
wouldn't want any kingdom without you."

Granny bent and kissed her.

So Marjorie's three gifts did not vanish at midnight, as
enchanted things so often inconveniently do. The gold coin remained
to remind her of **Industry**; the sunny picture taught her
**Cheerfulness**; and the memory of the flower-crowned wagon, the crooked
cake, the joined hands, and Granny's kiss spoke always of
**Love**.

These are common gifts, perhaps. They may be found in
cottages as often as castles, and no fairy wand is
required to bestow them. Yet they are among the strongest
charms in the world.

And while Marjorie slept, the moonlight kissed the little maid's
forehead, the brook sang beyond the hill, and one last
drop from the leaky roof fell into the blue saucer
with a soft, contented "plink!"

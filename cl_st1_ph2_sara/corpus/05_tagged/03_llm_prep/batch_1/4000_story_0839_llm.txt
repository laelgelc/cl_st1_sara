They called her La Folle because no other name seemed
to explain a woman who had lived twenty years within
sight of a road she would not travel and within
sound of neighbors she would not visit.

Her cabin stood beyond an abandoned field at the edge
of Bellissime plantation. Behind it rose a ragged wall of
pines; before it the bayou curved like a dark crescent
through reeds and willows. On the farther bank began the
broad life of the plantation—white cotton fields, clustered cabins, barns,
gardens, and, in the distance, the owner's great house with
its square galleries and gray roof.

La Folle saw these things every day. She saw people
crossing the footbridge above the lower bend, wagons creeping along
the lane, children chasing one another through the stubble. Yet
to her they belonged to a country as remote as
one beyond the sea.

She planted corn and potatoes in the rich ground around
her cabin. She kept hens, a cow, and a narrow
bed of marigolds. She was tall and powerful, with long
arms hardened by labor and a face whose grave lines
softened readily for children. People came to her when they
wished. She mended nets, brewed tisanes, dressed wounds, and could
lift a sack of meal that troubled many a man.
But she never went to them.

The boundary of her world was not marked by fence
or ditch. It existed only in her mind, though it
held more strongly than iron. It began at the bayou.

When La Folle was a little girl, fighting had broken
out near Bellissime. She had been alone in the old
kitchen when a man staggered through the door, his shirt
torn open, his body black with powder and crimson with
blood. He fell across the floor without speaking. Behind him
came the sound of horses, shots, and men shouting among
the trees.

What happened afterward had never arranged itself clearly in her
memory. There remained only the red upon the boards, the
powder-black face, and the bayou gleaming beyond the yard while
death seemed to move everywhere on its opposite shore. From
that day she could not cross the water. Persuasion failed;
mockery failed; even force failed. Once, when several women attempted
to lead her over the bridge, she fought them with
such terror that they released her.

In time everyone accepted the matter. La Folle lived yonder.
She was strange, but harmless. She had all she needed,
they said, and the world could come to her if
it chose.

Of all who came, none was so welcome as Cheri,
the plantation owner's youngest son. His proper name was Henri,
but La Folle had called him Cheri from the hour
his nurse first carried him to her cabin, a round
baby in a white gown.

As he grew, he made her clearing his kingdom. He
hunted imaginary wolves among the pines, fished for minnows, robbed
her fig tree, and slept at noon upon the bearskin
before her hearth. La Folle kept croquignoles for him in
a blue jar and saved the ripest melons beneath her
bed. She told him stories in which saints, rabbits, kings,
and plantation dogs moved together without embarrassment.

"Tell 'bout de little prince," he would command.

"Again, mon bébé? Dat prince done been los' in de
woods six times dis week."

"He can get lost once more."

And once more he was lost, and once more La
Folle carried him safely home in the story, though she
herself had never carried anything beyond the bayou.

Cheri understood her fear without respecting it. Children accept certain
mysteries only to play around them.

"Why you don't come see me, La Folle?" he asked
one afternoon.

She glanced toward the water, where sunlight quivered among the
reeds.

"Some day, maybe."

"You always say that."

"Den some day I goin' mean it."

He laughed and flung his arms around her neck. She
laughed also, but her eyes did not leave the bayou.

It was Saturday afternoon in early autumn when Cheri came
hunting near her cabin. The cotton stood high and white
across the farther fields, and the heat lay over the
ground like a thin sheet of brass. Cheri wore blue
cottonade trousers and carried a small rifle which his father
had permitted him to use under strict conditions. Those conditions
had not included hunting alone, but Cheri possessed the easy
confidence of a boy much beloved.

La Folle sat beneath her arbor shelling peas. She heard
him moving among the trees and now and then the
crack of a twig beneath his feet.

"Don' go too far, Cheri!" she called.

"I won't."

"An' mind dat gun!"

"I know."

She smiled, shaking her head. The peas fell with soft
taps into the pan between her knees. A hen scratched
under the steps. From beyond the bayou came the slow
singing of women returning from the cotton rows.

Then the rifle exploded.

The sound was close and wrong. It was followed by
a cry.

La Folle overturned the pan as she rose. Green peas
scattered over the bare earth, but she did not see
them. She ran into the pines, breaking through briars, calling
the boy's name.

She found him beside a fallen log. The rifle lay
several feet away. Cheri sat propped upon one hand, staring
at his other leg. The blue cottonade was darkening above
the knee.

"La Folle," he said, in a voice small with astonishment,
"I shot myself."

For one instant she stood rigid. The blood on the
cloth was blood from long ago; the pines were crowded
with armed men; a powder-black figure fell again across a
kitchen floor.

Then Cheri whimpered.

The old vision vanished.

"Mon bébé! Mon pauvre bébé!" She dropped beside him and
tore the trouser leg from knee to hip. The wound
bled freely, but the bone appeared sound. She twisted a
strip of her petticoat above it, tightening the cloth with
strong, steady hands.

"It hurts," Cheri said.

"I know, my heart. I know. But you look at
La Folle. Ain't nothin' goin' take you while La Folle
got arms to hold you."

She lifted him. He was eleven years old and heavy,
but she gathered him against her breast as if he
were still the child in the white gown. His head
fell upon her shoulder.

At the edge of the clearing she began to shout.

"Au secours! Somebody come! Cheri hurt! Monsieur! Mis' Armand! Somebody
come yonda!"

Her voice traveled across the bayou and broke against the
distant fields.

No answer came.

She called again, louder. A flock of blackbirds rose from
the reeds. The women's singing had ceased. Far away a
wagon rattled over hard earth, then faded.

Cheri stirred in her arms.

"I'm sleepy, La Folle."

"No, no, bébé. Don' sleep. Talk to me."

"I'm tired."

"Help!" she screamed. "Bon Dieu, send somebody! Come take dis
chile!"

The plantation lay before her in the brilliant afternoon, near
enough for her to distinguish the white gate of the
house. Yet between herself and help moved the narrow brown
water.

She carried Cheri to the bank.

The bayou was shallow there. She knew it. She had
watched cattle wade across in summer, the water scarcely reaching
their bellies. But knowledge had no power over the terror
that seized her. Her heart beat like a muffled hammer.
The opposite shore receded, widened, became filled with gun smoke
and galloping shadows.

She shouted until her throat burned.

At last figures appeared in the cotton field. One person
stopped, then another. They had heard her. She saw men
running toward the water and women following. Someone waved.

"Bring a boat!" La Folle cried. "Cheri shot! Hurry, hurry!"

But the nearest boat was tied at the landing beyond
the bend. The footbridge was farther still. La Folle understood
that they could not reach her quickly.

Cheri's face had grown pale. His lashes lay dark against
his cheeks.

She looked down at him. Then she looked at the
bayou.

Those upon the opposite bank saw her move back several
paces. They thought she meant to wait. Instead, she bent
over the boy, settled his weight more firmly in her
arms, and ran.

Her feet struck the water.

A great cry went up from the farther shore.

The cold seized her ankles, then her knees. Mud drew
at her shoes. She closed her eyes and went forward,
carrying Cheri high against her breast. The bayou roared in
her ears though its surface scarcely rippled. Every step seemed
to descend into an immeasurable depth.

"Bon Dieu—Bon Dieu—Bon Dieu," she repeated.

A root caught her skirt. She stumbled, recovered, and pressed
on. Cheri moaned once. That sound pulled her more surely
than any hand.

When the water began to fall below her knees, she
opened her eyes.

Men were wading toward her. Beyond them stood a crowd,
silent now, their faces fixed upon her as though she
had risen from the bayou itself. She would let no
one take Cheri until she felt dry earth beneath her
feet.

Then she surrendered him to his father.

"De gun," she tried to say. "He—he ain't mean—"

Monsieur Armand gathered the boy and turned toward the house,
calling for the doctor. Cheri's mother came running, white-faced, her
skirts lifted from the dust.

La Folle watched them carry him away.

The fields swung slowly around her. The white cotton reddened,
darkened, and disappeared.

She fell upon the grass.

They bore her to a nearby cabin, where the women
loosened her dress and bathed her face. Some said she
was dying. Others whispered that the crossing had broken what
little reason remained to her. An old woman burned herbs
and murmured prayers. But La Folle suffered no fever, and
before midnight her breathing was deep and peaceful.

Death had passed her by.

The first touch of the cool gray morning awakened her.
For a while she lay still, listening to unfamiliar sounds:
another woman's clock ticking, a baby breathing, roosters answering one
another across the quarters.

Then memory returned.

"Cheri?"

A woman sleeping beside the hearth lifted her head.

"Doctor say de chile goin' be fine. Ball pass clean
through. He sleepin' at de big house."

La Folle closed her eyes. A smile passed over her
face.

She rose before anyone could prevent her, wrapped a shawl
around her shoulders, and stepped outdoors. Mist rested upon the
fields. The cotton gleamed like frosted silver, and dew jeweled
the weeds beside the path.

People watched from doorways as she walked toward the bayou.

No one spoke.

At the bank La Folle paused. The water that had
thundered in her mind for twenty years slipped quietly between
grasses. A pale strip of sky rested upon it. Tiny
insects touched the surface and vanished. She placed one foot
in the shallows, then the other.

There was no smoke. No blood. No armed shadow waited
beyond the reeds.

She crossed slowly, feeling the cool current curl around her
ankles. In the middle she stopped to look upstream, where
the bayou bent like a silver bow. A kingfisher flashed
blue among the willows.

On the farther shore she climbed the bank and stood
amazed.

This was the world she had watched all her life,
yet it was not the world she had imagined. The
turf yielded softly under her feet. Morning glories opened along
a fence. She smelled damp earth, crushed mint, and roses
from the distant garden. Birds called from the pecan trees
with such variety that she wondered how she had never
truly heard them.

La Folle turned toward the great house.

She did not hurry. She passed the quarters, where men
and women stared after her. She passed the barns and
the lane bordered by chinaberry trees. At every step the
country widened. Beyond the cotton fields the river shone through
the mist, curving like a silver ribbon beneath the brightening
sky.

When she reached the house, a servant sweeping the gallery
dropped her broom.

"La Folle! How you come here?"

"Walk," she answered simply. "How my Cheri?"

The boy's mother appeared at the open door. Her tired
face softened when she saw the woman who had carried
her child home.

"He is safe," she said. "The doctor says he will
suffer more from being kept in bed than from the
wound."

La Folle laughed, a low laugh of relief.

"Dat sound like him."

"You may see him later. He is sleeping now."

"I wait."

She seated herself upon the veranda step. No one asked
her to leave. Presently a cup of coffee was brought,
and she held it between her hands while warmth rose
into her face.

The sun came slowly above the fields. Gray became pearl,
pearl became gold. Light touched the cotton, the roofs, the
garden beds, and the far river. The whole plantation awakened
beneath it: doors opened, bells sounded, horses stamped in their
stalls, and somewhere within the house a child called faintly
for water.

La Folle lifted her head at the voice.

Then, reassured by the quiet movements indoors, she settled again.
Before her lay roads she had never walked, gardens she
had never entered, and the river whose brightness she had
known only from afar. Nothing in that wide country demanded
that she discover it all at once.

For the present, it was enough to sit near Cheri
and watch the morning spread over her newly opened world.

And she watched it with wonder and deep content.

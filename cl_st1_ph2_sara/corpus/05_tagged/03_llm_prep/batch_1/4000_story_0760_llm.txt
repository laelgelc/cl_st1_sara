## Not Going to Heaven Yet

By nine o'clock on Tuesday morning, the justice court of
Bellweather, Iowa, had acquired one broken chair, two muddy dogs,
seven spectators who had no business there, and a case
that nobody understood.

By ten, the dogs understood it least, the spectators understood
it best, and Judge Murphy had ceased to believe that
understanding was a necessary part of the law.

The trouble stood before him in four separate shapes.

First came Sally Bell, wife of Noah Bell, carrying under
one arm a bed slat split down the middle. She
was a tall, handsome woman with a yellow scarf tied
around her head and the carriage of an injured queen
entering a rebellious province. Her left sleeve had been torn
at the shoulder, soot crossed her forehead like a military
decoration, and every line of her figure announced that somebody
was shortly to be corrected.

Second came Silas Green, tenant of Noah Bell, wearing a
coat that appeared to have been designed for a larger
and more prosperous man. Silas had the long, descending face
of one whose hopes had not merely fallen but had
gone through the floor and lodged in the cellar. He
stood with his knees turned slightly inward, his chin down,
and his eyes fixed upon the bed slat. Milton supplied
devils with more grandeur, but none with a more complete
expectation of punishment.

Third came Constable Flaherty, who held Silas by the elbow,
Sally by the eye, and the entire legal situation by
a thread. His brass buttons were polished, his mustache was
damp, and his official dignity had already suffered scratches.

Last came Noah Bell.

Noah was Sally's husband, Silas's landlord, and a man of
uncertain antiquity. He might have been sixty if time had
treated him badly, eighty if it had treated him well,
or one hundred and seventeen if, as he often claimed,
he remembered a winter before snow was invented. His white
beard stood out from his chin in three thoughtful points.
He leaned on a cane, held one hand behind his
ear, and smiled upon the court with the serene good
humor of a guest who had arrived for the wrong
wedding.

"Call the case," said Judge Murphy.

The clerk rose.

"Sally Bell against Silas Green, complaint of unlawful imprisonment, assault,
attempted drowning, malicious extinguishment of fires, destruction of household property,
disturbance of the peace, and—"

"Attempted drowning?" said the judge.

"Yes, Your Honor."

"In a house?"

"So alleged, Your Honor."

Judge Murphy looked at the ceiling.

"I knew the day would come," he said, "when somebody
would drown on dry land in this court. Proceed."

Constable Flaherty pushed Silas half a step forward. Silas immediately
took three steps backward.

"Stand up, man," said the judge.

"I am standing, sir."

"Then do it with more confidence."

Silas straightened. His knees remained unconvinced.

Sally raised the bed slat.

"And make him stand where I can reach him."

"You'll reach nobody," said Flaherty, catching her wrist. "The court
has rules."

"I had rules too," Sally said. "One of them was
that nobody nails me inside a shack for three days."

The spectators stirred pleasantly. A woman in the rear whispered,
"I told you there was nailing," and a man beside
her answered that he had heard it was chaining.

Judge Murphy struck his desk with a paperweight.

"Silence! Mrs. Bell, put down that piece of lumber."

"It is evidence."

"It looks like a weapon."

"It became evidence after it finished being a weapon."

"Put it down."

Sally placed the slat on the clerk's table with such
precision that the ink bottle jumped.

Noah cupped his hand behind his ear.

"What's she putting down?"

"Evidence," shouted Flaherty.

"Whose residence?"

"Evidence!"

"I've got my own residence."

Judge Murphy shut his eyes briefly, as a man may
close a door against weather.

"Mr. Bell," he said, "you are not yet testifying."

Noah smiled.

"Much obliged."

"Sit down."

"What?"

"SIT DOWN."

"I am down."

He was not, but after Sally seized the tail of
his coat and pulled, the chair beneath him rose to
meet the requirements of the court.

Judge Murphy pointed at Silas.

"You are accused of abducting Mrs. Bell, imprisoning her in
a shack, and pouring water down the chimney whenever she
attempted to make a fire. How do you plead?"

"I didn't do it."

"That is not one of the customary forms."

"I didn't do it, not customary nor otherwise."

"Not guilty, then?"

"Not guilty, sir. Not present, either."

"You are present now."

"That is my misfortune."

Sally made a movement toward the slat.

Flaherty put his baton across it.

"Mrs. Bell," said the judge, "you will give your account
in order, calmly, and without striking the defendant."

"I can do two of those things."

"Do all three."

"I will do my best with the first two."

She stepped forward, planted both hands on the rail, and
looked directly at Judge Murphy.

"Last Saturday morning," she began, "I went over to the
shack Silas rents from my husband."

"Why?"

"To clean it."

Silas lifted his head.

"I never asked her."

"No," said Sally. "Dirt seldom sends invitations."

There was a sound from the spectators which might have
been a cough if six people had not made it
together.

"Silence," said the judge. "Mrs. Bell, describe this shack."

Flaherty cleared his throat.

"If the court pleases, I can locate it."

"Can you do so briefly?"

"I can try."

The judge made a note of this ambition.

Flaherty pointed through the east wall as though the shack
might be visible through plaster.

"It is on the vacant lot behind Weller's feed store."

"Very well."

"Not the first vacant lot, Your Honor. The second."

"There are two?"

"There are three if you count the one with the
house on it."

"Then it is not vacant."

"The house is vacant."

"The lot is occupied by the vacant house."

"Yes, Your Honor, but the shack is not on that
one. It is on the vacant lot beyond the vacant
house, just this side of the other vacant lot."

Judge Murphy stared at him.

"Which other vacant lot?"

"The one that is properly vacant."

"And the shack?"

"Occupies it."

"Then that lot is not properly vacant."

"It is called vacant."

"By whom?"

"Everybody."

"Why?"

Flaherty considered.

"Because nobody lives there except Silas."

Silas raised one finger.

"Not lately."

"Enough!" Judge Murphy said. "The court will take judicial notice
that the shack exists somewhere. Mrs. Bell, continue."

Sally drew breath.

"I went there Saturday morning with a scrub pail, a
mop, soap, two cloths, and the expectation that by noon
I might discover the floor."

"It has a floor," Silas said.

"I found part of one."

"I know where all of it is."

"Then why didn't you introduce the pieces?"

"Silence from the defendant," said Judge Murphy.

Sally nodded and resumed.

"The door was not locked. I went inside. Silas was
not there."

"Where was he?" the judge asked.

"If I had known that then, I would have gone
there and cleaned him instead."

Silas's face descended another inch.

"The room," Sally said, "contained one bed, if charity permits
that word; one table supported by faith and a brick;
three shoes, none of them acquainted with the others; a
stove with a cracked lid; four religious handbills; and enough
dust to bury a mule standing up."

"Those handbills were important," Silas murmured.

"They were cleaner than the table."

"What did they say?" asked the judge.

Sally glanced at Silas.

"They said the world was ending Saturday night, and a
prophet named Amos T. Rapture was holding a gathering at
Mount Promise for the faithful who expected to rise bodily
into heaven."

The clerk stopped writing.

"Bodily?" said Judge Murphy.

"Yes, sir. Shoes and all."

Flaherty looked down at Silas's three unrelated shoes.

"Was there a charge?" the judge asked.

"For heaven?"

"For the gathering."

"Twenty-five cents for standing room, fifty cents if you wanted
a chair, and one dollar for a white robe."

Silas spoke with sudden dignity.

"The robe was optional."

"Silence."

"It was too short anyhow," Sally added. "I found it
under the bed."

At this, Silas's misery reached a depth unvisited by poets.

Judge Murphy rubbed his forehead.

"You entered the shack. Then what?"

"I opened the shutters, shook out the bedding, swept the
floor, and put water on the stove to heat. While
I was scrubbing behind the bed, I heard hammering."

"What sort of hammering?"

"The sort nails make when a fool is driving them."

"Where?"

"At the door first. Then the window."

"What did you do?"

"I called out."

"What did you say?"

Sally turned her eyes on Silas.

"I said, 'Who is out there?' Nobody answered. I tried
the door. It would not open. I went to the
window. The shutters had been nailed shut from outside. Then
I heard a voice."

"What voice?"

"A man's voice."

"Whose?"

"His."

She pointed at Silas with an arm so direct and
commanding that Silas leaned sideways to avoid being pierced by
it.

"What did the voice say?" asked Judge Murphy.

"It said, 'You ain't going to heaven yet.'"

Noah, seated behind her, chuckled.

Sally wheeled.

"What is funny?"

Noah raised his hand to his ear.

"Did somebody say money?"

Judge Murphy struck the desk.

"Mr. Bell, remain silent."

"I am paying attention."

"No, you are not."

"That too."

Sally looked at Noah for a long second, then faced
the court again.

"I shouted that I was not trying to go to
heaven. I said I was Sally Bell, lawful wife of
Noah Bell, and if the door did not open, somebody
would be sorry before sundown."

"Did the voice reply?"

"Yes. It said, 'You can change your voice, but you
ain't going to heaven yet.'"

The spectators shifted delightedly.

Silas shook his head.

"That wasn't me."

Sally seized the bed slat.

Flaherty seized Sally.

The clerk seized the ink bottle.

Judge Murphy seized what remained of his temper.

"Put it down!"

"I only picked it up to identify it."

"You may identify it from a distance."

"I know it intimately."

"Put it down."

She did.

"Mrs. Bell, why did you suppose the defendant had confined
you?"

"At first I supposed he had gone mad. After an
hour, I supposed he had always been mad and I
had been slow to notice. After three days, I did
not trouble myself with fine distinctions."

"Three days?" said the judge.

"Three days and two nights."

"And nobody found you?"

"Nobody looked."

Noah brightened.

"I looked for her."

"When?" Sally demanded.

"Sunday."

"Where?"

"At home."

Judge Murphy pointed at Noah.

"Do not interrupt."

"I found she wasn't there."

"We had established that."

"What?"

"Nothing," said the judge. "Mrs. Bell, continue."

Sally's testimony proceeded, not like a stream—which may be diverted—but
like spring floodwater carrying fences, chickens, and municipal property before
it.

She explained that after her first threats had failed, she
tried persuasion.

"I said, 'Silas, open this door and I may leave
enough of you to recognize.'"

"You call that persuasion?" asked Judge Murphy.

"For me, yes."

"What happened?"

"Nothing. I shouted until my throat hurt. Then I decided
to make smoke. I thought someone would see it."

"So you lit the stove?"

"I put in kindling, paper, and one of those heaven
handbills."

Silas looked wounded.

"That was Brother Rapture's personal message."

"It burned personally."

"What happened then?" asked the judge quickly.

"The moment the smoke began to rise, water came down
the chimney."

"How much water?"

"A pailful."

"Are you certain?"

"I was under it."

The spectators considered this satisfactory expertise.

Sally touched the soot on her forehead.

"The stove spat ashes. The room filled with steam. The
fire went out, the floor became mud, and my best
yellow scarf turned gray on one end."

She lifted the scarf as Exhibit B.

"Then I heard the voice again."

Judge Murphy sighed.

"What did it say?"

"'You ain't going to heaven yet.'"

Noah slapped his knee.

Sally turned so sharply that Flaherty stepped between wife and
husband without waiting for a request.

"What are you laughing at?" she asked.

"I like that part," Noah said.

"You had better stop liking it."

"What?"

"You heard me."

"No, I didn't."

Judge Murphy leaned across the desk.

"Mr. Bell, if you make another sound, the court will
have you removed."

Noah looked at Flaherty.

"What does he want proved?"

"Removed!"

"I haven't moved."

Flaherty bent close to his ear.

"Keep quiet."

Noah nodded and announced, "I can do that," at a
volume sufficient to notify the county.

The judge waited. Silence settled at last, except for one
muddy dog scratching itself by the stove.

"Proceed," he said.

Sally proceeded.

She told how she tried the door with the poker;
how the poker bent; how she attempted to pry up
a floorboard and discovered that Silas's floor, though reluctant to
show itself during cleaning, had become remarkably firm in opposition
to escape.

"I called through the chimney," she said. "I called until
my voice sounded like a saw going through a knot.
I told him I had no wish to ascend anywhere.
I told him I would sign a statement. I told
him I would sit on the ground and hold to
the stove. I told him heaven itself could wait."

Silas lifted both palms.

"I was six miles away."

"You were near enough to fetch water."

"I wasn't."

"The voice was yours."

"My voice doesn't sound like that."

Sally gave a perfect imitation: "'You ain't going to heaven
yet.'"

Silas appeared insulted.

"I sound better than that."

"You will sound worse when I finish."

"Mrs. Bell!" cried the judge.

She folded her hands.

"Calmly, sir."

Judge Murphy looked at Flaherty.

"Did you see the shack afterward?"

"I did, Your Honor."

"Was it nailed shut?"

"Completely. Door fastened with six tenpenny nails. Shutters with four
each. Back vent blocked by a board. I had to
use a crowbar."

"Did you find water?"

"A great quantity. Stove wet, floor wet, bed wet, Mrs.
Bell wet, and a small frog in Silas's left shoe."

Silas looked alarmed.

"The brown shoe?"

"The black."

"That's all right, then."

"Did you find any means by which the defendant could
pour water down the chimney?"

"A ladder was lying outside."

"There!" Sally cried.

Silas spread his arms helplessly.

"A ladder is not a man."

"No, but you are the next worst thing."

Judge Murphy held up his hand.

"Mrs. Bell, tell us what occurred on the second day."

"The second day I was hungry."

"Had you no food?"

"I had half a loaf, two onions, and something in
a tin that may once have been fish. I left
the tin alone on religious grounds."

Silas muttered, "It was nearly new."

"You told me last month it came with the shack."

"The shack isn't that old."

Judge Murphy pointed his paperweight at them.

"One voice at a time. Mrs. Bell?"

"Sunday morning, I tried another fire."

"Why?"

"I was cold, and my clothes were damp. Also I
thought the person outside might have gone."

"Had he?"

"No. The instant the flame caught—down came the water."

"Another pail?"

"Two."

"How do you know?"

"The first pail hit the stove. The second hit me."

A murmur of sympathy passed through the room.

"And the voice?"

Sally's expression became terrible.

"The same words."

Judge Murphy bowed to the inevitable.

"'You ain't going to heaven yet'?"

"Exactly."

Noah leaned toward the spectator beside him.

"That fellow had good sense."

Sally's head turned slowly.

The spectator moved three seats away from Noah.

Judge Murphy heard only the movement.

"What is happening back there?"

"Nothing, Your Honor," said Flaherty. "Nothing yet."

"That word 'yet' has become offensive to me. Continue."

Sally explained that she spent Sunday dismantling the bed. The
iron frame resisted her, but its wooden crosspieces did not.
She loosened one bed slat and used it first as
a lever, then as a hammer, and finally as an
object upon which to make promises concerning Silas.

At noon, she heard footsteps.

"I called out. The footsteps stopped. I said, 'Whoever you
are, open this shack.'"

"Did the person answer?"

"Yes. 'You ain't going to heaven yet.'"

"Of course," the judge said.

"This time I knew."

"Knew what?"

"That it was Silas. He has a dragging step."

Silas looked down at his shoes.

"One heel is low."

"And one conscience lower," said Sally.

"Did you try to identify yourself again?" asked the judge.

"I told him my name, my mother's name, the date
I married Noah, the color of the dress I wore,
the name of the minister, and the amount Noah still
owes the minister."

Noah sat upright.

"That was a gift."

"It was a fee."

"What?"

"You heard that."

"I heard 'free.'"

"It was not free."

Judge Murphy slapped both palms on his desk.

"Mr. Bell!"

Noah beamed.

"Yes, sir?"

"Be quiet!"

"What?"

Flaherty bent down and spoke directly into his ear.

"The judge says to hush."

"Why didn't he say so?"

"I did," said Judge Murphy, though not loudly enough to
begin another campaign.

Sally resumed.

"Sunday afternoon, I heard singing in the road."

"Singing?"

"A hymn. I shouted, but they sang louder. Then I
pounded on the wall with the slat."

"Did they hear?"

"One voice said, 'The spirits are active around here.' Then
they walked faster."

A woman among the spectators crossed herself; another asked which
hymn.

"Sunday night," Sally continued, "it rained."

Judge Murphy looked puzzled.

"If there was rain, why would the defendant need to
pour water down the chimney?"

"I did not say he needed to. I said he
did."

"How could you distinguish rain from water poured by hand?"

"Rain does not climb a ladder, mutter about rent, splash
all at once, and say, 'You ain't going to heaven
yet.'"

The courtroom became very still.

Judge Murphy leaned forward.

"What did you say?"

"I said rain does not—"

"No. About rent."

Sally frowned.

"I heard muttering."

"What words?"

"I could not make them all out. Something about payment.
Thirty dollars, perhaps."

Silas's eyes flickered toward Noah.

Noah, unaware of being observed, was counting on his fingers.

Judge Murphy noticed.

"Does the defendant owe thirty dollars?"

Before Silas could answer, Noah rose.

"Thirty dollars exactly!"

The declaration rang in the room like a bell.

Sally stared at him.

Silas stared at him.

Flaherty stared at him.

Judge Murphy stared at him longest.

"You know of this debt?" the judge asked.

Noah cupped his ear.

"Who's dead?"

"Debt! Thirty dollars!"

"Yes, he owes it."

"Sit down."

"He owes three months."

"Sit down!"

"Ten dollars each."

Sally caught Noah's coat and pulled him into the chair
again.

"Stay there."

"What?"

"Stay."

"Today?"

She gripped the bed slat.

Noah interpreted her expression correctly if not her word and
settled back.

Judge Murphy made a slow note.

"Mrs. Bell, what happened Monday?"

"By Monday," Sally said, "I had finished being patient."

Nobody in the court had previously suspected patience of any
participation in the affair, but they allowed the claim to
pass.

"I tried a third fire, not because I expected heat,
but because I wanted to know whether he was still
there."

"And was he?"

"The water came down before I struck the second match."

Silas shook his head mournfully.

"Then the voice said it again. I answered, 'Very well.
If I am not going to heaven, one of us
is going somewhere else.'"

Judge Murphy pointed warningly.

"Do not specify."

"I did not specify then, either. I wished to preserve
uncertainty."

"Afterward?"

"I knocked out the bottom hinge with the slat. The
top hinge held. I kicked the door until part of
the frame split. Then I got the slat between the
door and the jamb and pulled."

Flaherty nodded.

"The frame was rotten."

Silas looked almost proud.

"It is an old structure."

"The court has gathered that," said Judge Murphy.

"The door opened six inches," Sally continued. "I put my
shoulder through, then my head, then the rest of me.
I tore my sleeve. I fell in the mud. I
got up. I looked for Silas."

"And found him?"

"Not then. But I found him an hour later coming
down Market Street."

Here Silas shuddered. The moment lived yet in his nerves.

"He saw me," Sally said.

"What did he do?"

"He ran."

"I saw the slat," Silas explained.

"An innocent man does not run."

"An innocent man with good eyesight does."

Sally took one step toward him. He took two backward,
preserving the ratio established on Market Street.

"What followed?" asked Judge Murphy.

"I followed."

"How far?"

"From Weller's feed store to the courthouse square, around the
fountain twice, through Haber's laundry, out the back, across the
alley, over a chicken coop, and into the arms of
Constable Flaherty."

Flaherty polished a button thoughtfully.

"Not exactly into my arms. More across my path at
speed."

"Who?" asked the judge.

"All of them."

"Explain."

"I was standing by the fountain when Silas came around
it. Mrs. Bell came behind him with the slat. He
went through the laundry. She went through the laundry. I
went around the laundry because I know Mrs. Haber. When
I reached the alley, Silas had upset a basket, Mrs.
Bell had upset Silas, and a chicken had upset everybody."

"Did Mrs. Bell strike the defendant?"

"Repeatedly."

"I struck him twice," Sally said.

"Three times," said Silas.

"The chicken was the third."

"The chicken didn't have a bed slat."

"It may have wished for one."

Flaherty raised his hand.

"If the court pleases, I stopped the fourth blow."

"There!" Silas said. "There was a fourth."

"It never arrived."

"I suffered anticipation."

Judge Murphy looked at Sally.

"You assaulted him in the public street."

"I corrected him in the public street. The assault began
at the shack."

"You allege."

"I demonstrate."

Her hand moved toward the slat. Flaherty's baton came down
on it with a click.

"Leave the exhibit," he said.

Judge Murphy leaned back. His chair complained, but no more
bitterly than he did.

"Mrs. Bell, is that your full account?"

"No."

The judge paled.

"But it is enough for now."

He recovered slightly.

"Mr. Green, step forward."

Silas approached the rail in the manner of a man
invited to inspect his own grave.

"Tell the court where you were from Saturday morning until
Monday afternoon."

"At Mount Promise."

"For the ascension gathering?"

"Yes, sir."

"What time did you go?"

"Friday night."

"Why so early?"

Silas looked surprised by the question.

"If a person expects to rise into eternal glory, he
doesn't want to miss the wagon."

"Did others attend?"

"Forty-three at first. Thirty-nine after Brother Rapture announced there would
be no refunds."

"And you remained there until Monday?"

"Yes, sir."

"But the promised ascension was Saturday night."

"That was the first appointment."

"The first?"

"Brother Rapture recalculated."

Judge Murphy put down his pen.

"How many times?"

"Saturday midnight, Sunday dawn, Sunday noon, Sunday sunset, and early
Monday, though by then the white robes were showing dirt."

"What reason did he give for the delay?"

"The atmosphere was unfavorable."

"To heaven?"

"To rising."

Flaherty coughed into his hand.

"On Sunday noon," Silas continued, "Brother Rapture said doubt among
the company had increased our spiritual weight."

"Did he identify the doubters?"

"He charged us ten cents each to have doubt removed."

"Did that work?"

"We remained heavy."

The spectators were openly pleased. Even Sally allowed herself a
grim smile.

"Can anyone confirm your presence?" asked the judge.

"Brother Rapture can."

"Where is he?"

Silas looked at the floor.

"He departed."

"For heaven?"

"For Nebraska."

"Anyone else?"

"There were forty people."

"Names."

Silas began counting.

"Sister May Cooper, Mr. Ledbetter, the Watkins twins, Deacon Blue,
Mrs. Blue, their nephew, a boy called—"

"Enough. Constable, have you made inquiries?"

Flaherty nodded.

"I spoke with Mrs. Cooper. She confirms he was there
from Friday night until Monday morning. Mr. Ledbetter confirms it.
So do the Blues, though they disagree about whether they
rose four inches Sunday afternoon."

"Then the defendant has an alibi."

Sally struck the rail with her palm.

"He has a voice."

"He also has witnesses."

"He nailed me in."

"If he was six miles away, he did not."

"He poured water down the chimney."

"From six miles away?"

"He is shiftless enough to avoid work, but he is
not too shiftless to torment me."

Silas's face took on a faint, injured animation.

"I was not tormenting anybody. I was preparing to go
to heaven."

Noah stood up again.

"Not before he pays thirty dollars!"

Every head turned.

Noah smiled around at the room, pleased at last to
have found a subject of common interest.

Judge Murphy beckoned.

"Mr. Bell, come forward."

"What?"

"Come forward."

"I can hear you fine from here."

Flaherty placed a hand behind Noah's elbow and guided him
to the rail.

"Raise your right hand," said the clerk.

Noah raised his cane.

"Your hand."

"This is my right one."

"The other right one."

Noah transferred the cane, raised his hand, and peered at
the clerk.

"Do you swear to tell the truth, the whole truth,
and nothing but the truth?"

"I never took it."

"Do you swear?"

"Occasionally, when the mule deserves it."

The clerk looked at the judge.

Judge Murphy looked at the ceiling again, perhaps for legal
precedent, perhaps for escape.

"Ask him if he promises to tell the truth," he
said.

Flaherty bent close.

"Will you tell the truth?"

"Certainly. Why didn't he ask?"

"He did."

"No need to get excited."

"Noah," said Judge Murphy, loudly and distinctly, "where were you
Saturday morning?"

"Married."

"Where!"

"Where what?"

"Where were you?"

"Home mostly."

"Did you go near the shack?"

"What attack?"

"The shack your tenant rents."

"Ten dollars."

"What?"

"A month."

Judge Murphy turned to the clerk.

"Put down that the witness was home."

The clerk hesitated.

"Mostly?"

"Put down nothing. Save the ink."

Sally folded her arms and watched Noah with narrowing eyes.

Judge Murphy tried again.

"Did you know your wife had gone to clean the
shack?"

Noah heard "wife," "clean," and "shack," which was enough to
arrange the wrong answer.

"She said it needed cleaning. I said it needed paying."

"Did you see her go inside?"

"Who?"

"Your wife."

"My wife is Sally."

"I know."

"She's right there."

"I know!"

Noah looked at Sally.

"He says he knows you."

Sally did not answer. Her attention had become concentrated and
dangerous.

Flaherty stepped in.

"Noah, on Saturday, did you see anyone go into Silas's
shack?"

Noah considered.

"Might have."

"Who?"

"Silas."

Silas jerked upright.

"I wasn't there!"

Noah glanced at him.

"When did you come in?"

"I've been here all morning."

"I mean Saturday," shouted Flaherty.

"I was at Mount Promise," Silas shouted back.

"What for?"

"To go to heaven!"

Noah banged his cane on the floor.

"Not with thirty dollars due!"

The court erupted. Spectators talked, the clerk dropped his pen,
Sally reached for the slat, Silas retreated into Flaherty, and
both dogs began barking at the general theology.

Judge Murphy hammered the desk.

"Order! Order! Silence!"

Noah cupped both hands around his ears.

"What did he order?"

"Nobody has ordered anything," said Flaherty.

"I'll have coffee if somebody's going."

Judge Murphy rose.

"Nobody is going anywhere! Mr. Bell, answer this question: Why
did you think Silas was in the shack?"

Noah looked uncertainly at Flaherty.

"He wants to know why you thought Silas was inside."

"I saw him go in."

Silas shook his head violently.

"You could not."

"Saw the yellow cloth on his head."

Sally's hand rose slowly to her yellow scarf.

Noah continued.

"Saw him carrying a pail and mop, sneaking along toward
the shack."

"I was not sneaking," Sally said.

Noah did not hear.

"I knew what he was planning."

Judge Murphy leaned forward.

"What was he planning?"

"To go to heaven."

Silas stared.

"With a mop?" asked the judge.

"He took stranger things into that shack."

"That was me," Sally said.

Nobody attended to her yet. They were too near the
edge of revelation and, like all crowds at an edge,
afraid to step back.

Judge Murphy spoke carefully.

"You saw a person in a yellow head cloth carrying
a pail and mop into the shack, and you believed
that person was Silas?"

"Yes."

"Did you see the person's face?"

"No need. Silas wears cloth on his head when it's
cold."

"It was June," said Silas.

"Cold comes to the unprepared."

Sally removed her scarf.

"Noah."

He turned.

She held it up.

His smile weakened.

Judge Murphy pressed on.

"What did you do after you saw this person enter?"

Noah became cautious.

"Nothing unlawful."

"Let the court decide that."

"Can the court collect rent?"

"No."

"Then the court has limits."

"What did you do?"

Noah looked at Silas with the injured gravity of a
creditor discussing moral philosophy.

"I went home and got my hammer."

A great quiet settled over the room.

Sally picked up the bed slat.

Flaherty put both hands over it.

Judge Murphy's voice dropped.

"And then?"

"I nailed the door shut."

The clerk's pen raced.

"You nailed the shutters too?"

"Yes."

"And the back vent?"

"Couldn't be too careful."

Silas's mouth opened, but his habitual hopelessness had no shape
large enough for speech.

Sally's voice was very soft.

"You nailed me inside that shack?"

Noah heard her this time. Some sentences require no sound.

"I thought you were Silas."

"You thought I was Silas?"

"From the back."

Sally looked at Silas, then at herself, then back at
Noah. The comparison seemed not to improve his prospects.

Judge Murphy held up both hands.

"Wait. Why did you nail Silas—or the person you believed
was Silas—inside the shack?"

"He was going to heaven."

"So you confined him?"

"Only until he paid."

"Paid what?"

"Thirty dollars."

Noah's voice filled with righteous injury.

"Three months he owes me. Three months! Ten dollars a
month. Friday evening he tells everybody he's going up Saturday
night. Up! Just like that. No notice to his landlord.
No forwarding address. No arrangements. A man can't carry thirty
dollars into the clouds and leave me holding an empty
shack."

Silas found his voice.

"I intended to settle after the ascension."

"How?" Judge Murphy asked.

Silas considered.

"I had not completed the details."

Noah struck the rail with his cane.

"That's what I said. So when I saw him go
in there with that yellow cloth, I knew he was
packing for glory. I fetched the hammer and nailed him
in."

Sally took one step.

Flaherty tightened his grip on the slat.

"Why," Judge Murphy asked, "did you say, 'You ain't going
to heaven yet'?"

Noah looked almost offended by the simplicity of the question.

"Because he wasn't."

"You said it repeatedly?"

"He kept arguing."

"I kept telling you I was your wife!" Sally cried.

Noah turned to her.

"Silas was changing his voice."

"Into mine?"

"He's a secretive man."

Silas clutched the rail.

"I have never sounded like Sally."

"You will before I'm done," Sally said.

Judge Murphy raised his hand.

"And the water? Did you pour water down the chimney?"

Noah brightened, as if the court had finally reached the
portion of his conduct most deserving admiration.

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

"To stop him."

"From doing what?"

"Going to heaven by fire."

The judge stared.

Noah explained patiently.

"First way was straight through the roof. I nailed the
shutters and door, but roofs are uncertain. Then smoke came
out of the chimney. I knew he was trying another
method."

"Another method of ascension?"

"Fire. Elijah went up with fire."

"In a chariot," said the clerk.

"I didn't see any chariot," Noah replied, "but I wasn't
taking chances."

"So you poured water down the chimney."

"Good clean water."

"How many times?"

Noah counted on his fingers.

"Saturday once. Sunday twice. Monday once before breakfast and once
after."

"Five times!" Sally exclaimed.

"The last pail missed the stove," Noah said apologetically.

"It hit me."

"I thought Silas was making his voice higher to get
sympathy."

The spectators could bear no more. Laughter burst out in
the rear, swept across the benches, struck the clerk, and
nearly carried Flaherty with it. Even the muddy dogs appeared
to grin.

Judge Murphy hammered until the paperweight split.

"Silence! This is a court!"

The laughter diminished in stages, with occasional aftershocks from those
who dared to look at Noah.

Sally did not laugh.

She stood like a monument erected to the consequences of
mistaken identity.

Judge Murphy addressed Noah.

"On Sunday, did you not wonder where your wife was?"

"I looked for her."

"You said you looked at home."

"Yes."

"When she was not there, what did you think?"

"I thought she had gone to her sister's."

"Her sister lives forty miles away."

"They are close."

"Did you ask anyone?"

"I asked the person in the shack."

Sally's eyebrows rose.

"You asked me?"

"I called, 'Sally gone to her sister's?'"

"What did I answer?"

"You said, 'I am Sally, you deaf old fool!'"

"And what did you conclude?"

"That Silas was getting disrespectful."

The laughter began again, but one glance from Sally crushed
it where the judge's hammer had failed.

Flaherty released one hand from the bed slat and wiped
his eyes.

Judge Murphy sank into his chair.

"Let the court understand. You believed your tenant intended to
ascend to heaven without paying thirty dollars in back rent.
You mistook your wife for him, nailed her inside his
shack, and poured water down the chimney to prevent a
supernatural escape."

"Yes, sir."

"And every time she protested, you believed the tenant was
disguising his voice."

"Yes, sir."

"And it did not occur to you to open the
door and look?"

Noah considered this new and rather reckless theory.

"He might have run."

"Toward heaven?"

"Or Nebraska. Same result to a landlord."

Silas drew himself up.

"I object to that comparison."

Judge Murphy ignored him.

"Why did you leave the ladder outside?"

"So I could reach the chimney."

"Why did you leave the hammer?"

"I didn't want the weight."

"Why did you return five times?"

"To make sure he hadn't gone."

"Did you ever hear him—I mean her—say anything about the
rent?"

"All the time."

Sally's face darkened with remembrance.

"I said, 'Wait until I get out; you'll pay for
this.'"

Noah nodded eagerly.

"There! He knew he owed me."

Judge Murphy let his head fall into his hands.

For several seconds the court waited respectfully, believing perhaps that
he was praying. If so, it was the first religious
exercise of the week with any prospect of a practical
result.

At last he looked up.

"Mr. Green, you did not imprison Mrs. Bell."

"No, sir."

"You did not pour water down the chimney."

"No, sir."

"You merely went to Mount Promise to ascend to heaven."

"Yes, sir."

"And failed."

Silas's face returned to its natural depth.

"Temporarily."

"You owe Mr. Bell thirty dollars?"

"That is a separate case."

"It had better remain separate forever."

Sally stepped forward.

"Then charge Noah."

Noah smiled at hearing his name.

Judge Murphy looked from her soot-streaked forehead to the slat,
from the slat to Noah's untroubled beard, and from Noah
to the cracked paperweight.

Technically, the court had before it a confession to false
imprisonment, property damage, reckless endangerment, and perhaps an entirely new
offense involving theological interference with a tenant. Practically, Judge Murphy
had spent two hours shouting at a man who interpreted
legal questions as invitations to discuss rent. Justice, which is
blind, had met a defendant who was deaf, and the
judge saw no profit in arranging a longer acquaintance.

"This affair," he said carefully, "appears to arise from a
domestic misunderstanding."

Sally stared at him.

"A what?"

"A domestic misunderstanding."

"I was nailed inside a shack for three days."

"By your husband, who believed you were his tenant."

"He poured five pails of water on me."

"He believed he was preventing an ascension."

"He left me with half a loaf and two onions."

"The court does not approve."

"You sound calm."

"The court is required to sound calm."

"The court was not under the chimney."

Judge Murphy drew himself up.

"Mrs. Bell, Silas Green is plainly innocent of the charge.
The complaint against him is dismissed."

Silas exhaled so completely that his coat collapsed around him.

Sally pointed to Noah.

"And him?"

"The court recommends that you and your husband settle this
matter privately."

Flaherty looked at the bed slat.

"Is that wise, Your Honor?"

Judge Murphy looked at the bed slat too.

"The court recommends moderation."

Sally rolled up her torn sleeve.

Noah leaned toward Silas.

"What did he say?"

Silas, whose gloom had been replaced by the first pale
sunrise of self-preservation, answered loudly:

"He says you're going home with Sally."

Noah smiled.

"That's fine."

Sally picked up the bed slat.

"No," she said. "He ain't gone home yet."

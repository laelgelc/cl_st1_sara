### ZUT

There are quarrels which begin with a kingdom, a woman,
or a point of theology; and there are others, no
less implacable, which begin with a saucer of milk.

The feud between Madame Alexandrine Caille, patronne of the *Épicerie
Parisienne* at No. 117 bis, avenue de la Grande Armée,
and Madame Espérance Sergeot, cashier and co-proprietress of the *Salon
Moderne* adjoining, had several causes, all of them sufficient, and
one cause which was the veritable root of all evil.

That cause was Zut.

Zut was a white angora cat of prodigious dimensions, sapphire
eyes, and a tail resembling one of those feather dusters
wherewith the attendants of the Louvre pursue, without hope, the
dust of dynasties. She belonged—if so sovereign a creature could
belong to anybody—to Alexandrine. She ate Alexandrine's sardines, slept on
Alexandrine's sacks of lentils, and accepted Alexandrine's worship with the
calm of an idol accustomed to sacrifices. Yet she preferred
the Sergeots.

And above all, now!

But we advance too rapidly.

The *Salon Moderne* was new. That, in itself, was an
offence. It had arisen in six weeks from the melancholy
remains of a dealer in artificial flowers, and when its
blue shutters descended for the last time, revealing brass letters,
frosted globes, and a façade of cream-coloured paint picked out
with gilt, the entire quarter perceived that Hippolyte Sergeot intended
to succeed.

He did succeed.

He had three chairs upholstered in red morocco; three mirrors
framed in imitation bamboo; three nickel basins, the which shone
with such purity that a customer hesitated before soiling them
with his beard. There were shelves of brilliantine, bay rum,
violet water, lilac water, quinine lotion, pomades, powders, soaps in
almond, rose, heliotrope, and "English Military" perfumes; there were ivory-handled
brushes, badger shaving-brushes, little silver sprays, hot towels in a
white enamel heater, sponges disposed in glass bowls, and combs
as regimentally aligned as troops on the morning of the
Fourteenth of July.

A revolving pole striped blue, white, and red stood at
the entrance. Over the door appeared, in gold:

**HIPPOLYTE SERGEOT**
**COIFFEUR POUR DAMES ET MESSIEURS**
**FRICTIONS—MASSAGE—MANUCURE**

The manicure did not yet exist; but Hippolyte, who understood
commerce, knew that in business the desirable must first be
announced as possible, afterwards regarded as probable, and finally accepted
as permanent.

He himself wore a white jacket of spotless severity. His
moustache was black, pointed, and perfumed. His hair lay upon
his head in polished waves, so exact that a surveyor
might have established property lines upon them. He had served
his apprenticeship near the boulevard des Italiens, where he had
shaved a deputy, trimmed an actor, and once applied a
tonic to a Russian prince—at least, to a gentleman whom
everybody called "Prince," which in Paris amounts to the same
thing until the bill is presented.

Espérance sat behind a glass cash desk, with a nickel
bell, an account book, and a bowl of sugared almonds.
She was small, smooth, and placid; her pale hair was
dressed neatly; her grey eyes saw everything. She spoke little,
smiled often, and never forgot the price of anything. Hippolyte
supplied animation, fragrance, and gesture; Espérance supplied arithmetic. Their marriage
was therefore complete.

Each morning, before the entrances of the Métropolitain had finished
disgorging their clerks, shopgirls, soldiers, messengers, and respectable persons who
appeared ashamed of having travelled underground, Hippolyte washed the pavement.
Espérance polished the window. By eight o'clock the *Salon Moderne*
glittered. By nine there was a customer. By ten there
were two waiting, reading *Le Petit Parisien*, *L'Intransigeant*, or a
sporting journal three days old. At noon the shop smelled
of warm towels, soap, tobacco, violet, and prosperity.

Next door, the *Épicerie Parisienne* smelled otherwise.

Alexandrine sold potatoes, onions, haricots, dried cod, cheeses, biscuits in
glass jars, sardines in tins, coffee, chocolate, rice, candles, soap,
vinegar, cooking oil, and eggs which possessed various degrees of
historical interest. Her shop had once been prosperous. This was
when Alexandrine herself weighed fifty kilos, wore a red ribbon
at her throat, and could lift a sack of flour
while laughing at the admiration of deliverymen.

Time had increased her from fifty kilos to ninety, while
diminishing nearly everything else. The gilt letters over her door
had flaked; the green paint had become the colour of
boiled spinach; the flypapers hung above the cheeses with a
terrible fertility. Dust silvered the bottles of cassis. A tower
of preserved peas leaned towards a pyramid of soap. The
onions sprouted in their baskets, and the biscuits, which had
begun life crisp, had acquired the meditative softness of old
age.

Commerce does not pardon melancholy. Alexandrine, faced with accumulating debts
and decreasing earnings, attempted to repair the one by economising
on the other. She mixed old coffee with new, damp
sugar with dry, and a modest quantity of chicory with
coffee which had already endured more chicory than was just.
Eggs, when accused by odour, were turned so that the
clean side faced the purchaser. Butter was treated with water;
milk with the same. These were not frauds, Alexandrine explained
to herself, but adaptations to circumstances. Misfortune has always had
an admirable vocabulary.

Then arrived the Sergeots, with their nickel, their mirrors, their
red morocco, and their shameless custom.

"There they are again!" Alexandrine would mutter from behind her
counter, observing two young clerks enter the salon. "One would
say men have nothing to do but cut hairs. Three
chairs! For what? A man has but one head."

Zut, enthroned on a sack of rice, yawned.

"And she, with her cash desk! A glass box like
at the Opéra. Does she sell diamonds? No. She takes
sous for shaving chins."

Zut closed her sapphire eyes.

The cat had been presented to Alexandrine by a wine
merchant of Neuilly in settlement of an account which he
had no intention of paying by more conventional methods. At
that time Zut was a small white ball with pink
ears and an appetite. Alexandrine loved her instantly, because childless
women sometimes bestow upon animals that portion of tyranny which
mothers call devotion.

She named the kitten Zut after the first exclamation it
had caused her to utter by falling into a tub
of cream. The word, Alexandrine said, meant everything and nothing.
It expressed annoyance, tenderness, surprise, resignation, and the failure of
human speech before the essential absurdity of events. Philosophers have
required larger terms and explained less.

In five years Zut became enormous. She passed her days
among the groceries, white as innocence, perfumed with cod. Customers
admired her; children attempted to touch her tail and were
corrected; deliverymen addressed her respectfully. Alexandrine fed her liver on
Sundays and the oil from sardine tins on Thursdays. She
had made for her a cushion from an old shawl,
set beside the stove in winter and carried to the
coolest corner in summer.

Zut accepted all this as her due and loved nobody.

There is a serpent in every Eden; there is also,
in every commercial street, a person who carries news from
one shop to another. In the avenue de la Grande
Armée the office was filled by Madame Louvet, a widow
who bought half a litre of milk each morning and
extracted, gratis, the moral condition of the quarter.

"The Sergeots do well," she observed one Tuesday.

Alexandrine struck the counter with a cheese knife.

"Do well! One sees it. They have not been here
three months and already they put another lamp outside."

"A very pretty lamp."

"Pretty? It dazzles drivers. There will be an accident."

"They say Monsieur Sergeot has taken an assistant."

"He needs assistance to shave badly?"

"A young man from Levallois. And Madame Sergeot talks of
engaging a manicure."

Alexandrine laughed until the jars trembled.

"A manicure! Here! Name of a name! The ladies of
the avenue perhaps have no scissors?"

Madame Louvet lowered her voice, the which was unnecessary, for
nobody else was in the shop.

"And Agent Flique goes there every other day."

Alexandrine stopped laughing.

"Abel?"

"Abel Flique. Yes. He is there this moment. They say
Monsieur Sergeot trims his moustache without charge."

"Nothing is without charge," said Alexandrine darkly.

This maxim was true, and wounded her because she had
not invented it sooner.

Abel Flique had once desired to marry Alexandrine. That was
fifteen years before, when he was a porter at Les
Halles, broad-shouldered, red-haired, and poor; while she, slender and lively,
possessed six thousand francs, the prospect of the grocery, and
a laugh which could be heard from the place de
l'Étoile.

Alexandrine had refused him.

"You are a good boy, Abel," she had said, "but
you have no position."

Now he had one. He was Agent Flique, municipal policeman,
wearer of a blue uniform, a leather belt, a sword,
a cape for rain, and an expression of patient authority.
He had filled out considerably. His hair had retreated, his
face had ripened to a handsome crimson, and his moustache
projected over his mouth like two auburn awnings. He knew
every concierge from the Porte Maillot to the avenue Carnot,
every cabman who drove too rapidly, every café that remained
open three minutes after the legal hour, and every cook
who might be persuaded to provide a glass of wine.

He was unmarried, which Alexandrine considered evidence that he had
never recovered from her refusal.

It was not. He had forgotten it for twelve years,
except when reminded by Alexandrine.

Flique's first visit to the *Salon Moderne* had been professional.
Hippolyte's revolving pole projected seven centimetres farther over the pavement
than regulations permitted, or appeared to do so to a
rival barber who had informed the police. Flique measured it,
accepted Hippolyte's explanation, drank a glass of white wine in
the back room, and emerged with his moustache refreshed by
brilliantine.

Thereafter he returned.

Hippolyte admired authority. Flique admired being admired. Espérance observed that
a policeman in the chair reassured respectable customers and discouraged
troublesome ones. Thus friendship arose from mutual esteem, free trimming,
white wine, and the natural tendency of institutions to establish
themselves where they are comfortably received.

When Alexandrine saw Abel seated before the great mirror, a
hot towel beneath his chin and Hippolyte attending him like
a surgeon to the Republic, she felt the wound of
fifteen years ago reopen—with the important difference that she had
inflicted the original wound herself.

"He goes there to mock me," she said.

Zut washed a paw.

"He wishes me to see what I refused. A policeman!
He thinks I regret him."

Zut continued washing.

"And that little dried pea behind the cash desk smiles
at him. Let her smile. I could tell her things."

There were no things to tell, but their absence did
not diminish Alexandrine's readiness.

The crisis would perhaps have remained economic and sentimental—two departments
in which humanity has developed safeguards—had Zut not crossed the
threshold.

It occurred on a wet November afternoon. The grocery was
chill, the pavement grey, and Alexandrine, after disputing with a
supplier over seventeen francs, had retired to the back room
to calculate how the supplier might be made to wait
without ceasing to supply. Zut descended from her rice sack,
passed between two crates of turnips, and approached the door.

Across the wall came the soft humming of Hippolyte's clippers,
masculine laughter, and a smell of warm soap.

Zut stepped outside.

She paused beneath the awning, one paw lifted from the
damp pavement. Espérance, seated at her desk, perceived her.

"What a beauty!" she exclaimed.

Zut looked in.

"Come then, my angel. Come, *ma petite*."

Zut weighed nearly nine kilos and was nobody's little one;
nevertheless she entered.

Espérance rang the nickel bell from pure excitement. Hippolyte turned,
razor in hand.

"Tiens! The grocer's cat."

"The poor treasure is wet."

"Do not touch her. Alexandrine will say we have stolen
the animal."

"Stolen! She came."

Espérance lifted Zut, with effort, onto a chair. She dried
her with a towel warmed in the enamel cabinet, combed
the long white fur, and placed before her a saucer
containing milk.

Zut drank.

This was ordinary milk, unwatered, which must be understood if
one is to grasp the permanence of subsequent events.

When Alexandrine discovered the absence, she called from the door.

"Zut! Zut! Where is that creature? Zut!"

Espérance appeared in the adjoining doorway, smiling.

"She is here, Madame Caille."

"Here?"

"She came to visit us."

"A cat does not visit."

"Then she came to take milk."

Alexandrine entered. She beheld Zut upon the red morocco chair,
dry, combed, and occupied with the final white crescent in
the saucer. A warm towel lay around her shoulders like
the stole of a duchess.

"What do you do to my cat?"

"I dry her."

"With a customer's towel?"

"A clean towel."

"You feed her?"

"She was hungry."

"She is never hungry."

At this Zut placed one paw in the saucer and
licked it, thereby undermining the statement.

Espérance smiled more broadly.

"You see."

"I see that she has no manners."

Alexandrine snatched up Zut, who became suddenly boneless, as cats
do when opposed to transportation. The nine kilos extended into
a yard and a half of passive resistance.

"Come, my cabbage. Come to thy mother."

Zut stared over Alexandrine's shoulder at the saucer.

"The next time," said Alexandrine, "do not feed what is
not yours."

"The next time," replied Espérance, "do not let what is
yours go hungry."

It was not yet a declaration of war; but diplomatic
relations became uncertain.

The next day Zut returned.

On that occasion Espérance gave her a little minced liver.
On Friday there was cream. On Saturday, the skin of
a sole. By Monday Zut had established a routine. She
left the grocery at ten, entered the salon by eleven,
slept beneath the radiator until one, received a delicacy, inspected
the customers, and returned next door at four, in time
to demand her dinner from Alexandrine with the reproachful cries
of an animal neglected since birth.

At first Alexandrine shut the door. Zut waited until a
customer entered and escaped between the ankles. Alexandrine tied a
ribbon to her collar and fastened the ribbon to a
basket of potatoes. Zut gnawed it through. Alexandrine confined her
in the back room. Zut climbed to a small window,
pushed aside a cardboard advertisement for chicory, and descended by
way of the rubbish bin.

All creatures desire liberty; cats desire it particularly when liver
is at the other end.

Espérance had always wanted a white angora. Her mother had
possessed one in Saint-Mandé, a beast named Blanchette which died,
after a life of cushions and chicken, at the age
of eighteen. Hippolyte knew this history. He had heard it
during their courtship, their honeymoon, and on each occasion when
a cat appeared in an illustrated journal.

"We cannot have one," he would say. "There are the
hairs. The customers."

"Zut leaves hairs."

"Zut is not ours."

"Precisely."

Here Espérance sighed.

Hippolyte, who disliked sighs because they implied an unpaid domestic
account, bought her a canary. The canary died within three
weeks, partly from draughts and partly, one suspects, from the
concentrated attention of Zut.

Then came the attempted reconciliation.

It was engineered by Flique, who, like many policemen, believed
peace to consist in assembling enemies where he could watch
them. On a quiet Wednesday he persuaded Alexandrine to enter
the salon after closing.

"Come," he said. "You are neighbours. It is ridiculous."

"I have nothing against them."

"You call Sergeot an ape with a razor."

"I said he had the arms of an ape. That
is anatomy."

"And you say his wife steals your cat."

"The cat is stolen."

"At present she sleeps on your flour."

"She returns because she has honour."

Flique sighed.

Within the salon, Hippolyte stood by his chair with the
solemnity of an ambassador. Espérance had placed four glasses and
a bottle of vermouth on a small table. Zut lay
beneath the radiator.

"Madame Caille," said Hippolyte, "among neighbours there should be neighbourliness."

"There should also be limits."

"Certainly. A limit on each side."

"And each should keep what belongs to her."

Espérance poured the vermouth.

"Drink, Madame. We wish only that all be agreeable."

"I am always agreeable."

This was exaggeration.

"To demonstrate our goodwill," continued Hippolyte, "I offer you, for
one month, all coiffure at half price."

Alexandrine touched her hair, which she arranged herself in a
dense structure secured by six pins and a comb.

"My coiffure displeases you?"

"Not at all!"

"You think I need a barber?"

"A lady's arrangement—Espérance, explain!"

"My husband means a wave, perhaps," said Espérance. "Something more
modern."

"More modern than what?"

"Than—than the present fashion."

"My hair is in the present."

"Assuredly," said Hippolyte. "It is on your head."

Flique coughed into his glass. Espérance's lips tightened.

Alexandrine looked from one to the other.

"You laugh at me."

"No one laughs," said Flique.

"She laughs inside. I see it in her nose."

"My nose!" cried Espérance.

"Yes, your little pointed nose."

"It is not pointed."

"It points where it should not."

"Madame," said Hippolyte, "you insult my wife's nose."

"I speak of what I see."

"Then look in our mirrors," returned Espérance, "and speak of
what you see there."

There was a silence. The Republic itself, represented by Flique,
looked into its glass.

Alexandrine became purple.

"Mal élevée!"

Espérance rose.

"Gueuse!"

Hippolyte placed himself between them as Alexandrine seized an ivory
hairbrush and Espérance reached for the vermouth bottle. Flique caught
Alexandrine's wrist. Zut opened one eye, judged the matter unworthy,
and closed it.

"Enough!" cried the policeman.

"I came in friendship!"

"With insults in your mouth!"

"Thieves of cats!"

"Seller of rotten eggs!"

This last cry altered everything.

Alexandrine ceased struggling.

"What eggs?"

"The eggs you sell."

"They are fresh."

"Fresh from the Flood!" said Espérance.

"Take care."

"It is you who must take care," said Flique quietly.

His ruddy face had acquired the official expression which he
employed with cabmen and political demonstrators.

"I know complaints have been made, Alexandrine. Madame Louvet was
ill. The concierge at 119 returned a Camembert which moved
of itself. There is the milk also."

"What has the milk?"

"Too much water."

"Do I know what cows do?"

"Cows do not use the tap."

Hippolyte laughed. Alexandrine raised her fist.

"Do not harass these people," Flique continued. "Do not make
scenes, do not enter their shop shouting, and do not
accuse them of theft when the animal goes by herself.
Otherwise an inspector may visit your *épicerie*. An inspector has
no sentiment. He opens tins. He tests butter. He breaks
eggs."

Alexandrine's fist descended slowly.

She looked at Abel as she had looked fifteen years
before, but without the red ribbon or the laugh.

"You threaten me."

"I advise you."

"For them?"

"For tranquillity."

"And the free moustache."

Flique stroked that ornament.

"It is well trimmed," he admitted.

Alexandrine departed without farewell, carrying Zut under one arm. At
the threshold she turned.

"My cat shall not come here again."

Espérance poured herself another vermouth.

"Ask her."

From that day the feud assumed the dignified silence of
international hostility. Alexandrine no longer called the Sergeots thieves aloud.
She referred to them as "persons." Espérance no longer mentioned
rotten eggs, except when purchasing eggs elsewhere and explaining loudly
why. Hippolyte washed an extra half-metre of pavement each morning,
including a portion before Alexandrine's shop; she immediately threw onion
skins upon it.

Flique continued his visits.

And Zut continued hers.

The cat's sojourns grew longer. What had begun as an
hour became an afternoon; the afternoon extended into evening. Zut
discovered a shelf in the back room, between clean towels
and cartons of soap, where she could sleep undisturbed. Espérance
placed a cushion there. Then a bowl. Then a little
basket.

"This is abduction," Alexandrine told Madame Louvet.

"It seems rather attachment."

"To liver!"

"Attachment often begins with less."

Alexandrine attempted bribery. She purchased half a kilo of excellent
liver, though she owed twenty-three francs to the butcher, and
placed it before Zut.

Zut ate it, washed herself, and went next door.

She attempted tenderness.

"Stay with thy Alexandrine, my jewel. See, I have warmed
the cushion."

Zut sat upon the cushion until it cooled, then went
next door.

She attempted force, shutting the front and back doors, the
window, and even the coal hatch. Zut vanished through a
hole behind a cupboard which Alexandrine had not known existed,
although mice had known it for years.

Each escape was followed by a visit to the salon,
and each visit by Espérance's calm caresses. Alexandrine watched through
her window. She saw Zut exhibited to customers, admired, combed,
and occasionally tied with a blue ribbon.

"They make an advertisement of her," she said. "They profit
by my property."

This, in part, was true. Zut became one of the
attractions of the *Salon Moderne*. Children pressed their faces to
the glass. Ladies entering for a shampoo asked after "the
beautiful white cat." Gentlemen waiting to be shaved allowed her
upon their knees and afterwards bore away portions of her
upon their trousers. Hippolyte, who had objected to hairs, discovered
that hairs attached to prosperity could be brushed away.

He even commissioned a sign:

**ZUT, MASCOTTE DU SALON**

Espérance prevented him from displaying it.

"That would be too much," she said.

Espérance was reasonable, especially when triumph could be achieved without
vulgarity.

Winter passed. The lamps shone early; rain streaked the avenue;
buses rumbled towards the Arc de Triomphe; the Métropolitain breathed
warm crowds onto cold pavements. Alexandrine's business declined. The Sergeots
installed an electric hair-dryer. Hippolyte engaged the promised assistant, a
narrow youth named Émile who smelled of cloves and said
"Perfectly, monsieur" even when no question had been asked.

Then, in March, Alexandrine began repeating a mysterious phrase.

"Above all, now!"

She said it to Madame Louvet.

"She goes there every day. Above all, now!"

She said it to Flique, when she met him near
the café du Canon.

"You permit them to keep her for hours. Above all,
now!"

She said it through the wall, although no one on
the other side replied.

"Feed her, then! Stuff her! Above all, now!"

The significance of "now" was not at first apparent. Human
beings often invest time with a drama which time itself
does not acknowledge. Espérance supposed Alexandrine referred to the approach
of spring. Hippolyte thought she had raised her prices. Flique
thought nothing, having just received a plate of sardines.

Zut herself grew more majestic. Her sides rounded. Her appetite,
always respectable, became diplomatic in its breadth: fish in the
salon, liver in the grocery, milk from Madame Louvet, and
a fragment of sausage extracted from Émile by methods known
only to herself.

"She becomes fat," said Hippolyte.

"It is the winter coat," said Espérance.

"She has two winter coats."

"Do not criticise her."

One evening Zut did not return to the grocery.

At eight, Alexandrine called. At nine, she rattled a sardine
tin in the doorway. At half past nine she put
on her shawl and hammered upon the closed shutters of
the salon.

Hippolyte opened a slit.

"What is it?"

"My cat."

"She sleeps."

"I demand her."

"It is late."

"Late! Am I to make an appointment to recover my
property?"

Espérance appeared behind him in a dressing gown.

"Zut does not wish to move."

"I shall move her."

"You shall not enter our home at this hour."

"The shop is not your home."

"The back room is."

"My cat is in it."

"Your cat chose it."

Alexandrine pushed the shutter. Hippolyte held it. For a moment
they remained opposed, she upon the pavement, he within, two
champions of property and domestic peace.

"Give her!"

"No."

"Thief!"

"Bad egg!"

A window opened above.

"Silence!" shouted a tenant. "There are honest people asleep."

Alexandrine stepped back.

"Keep her tonight. But if harm comes to her—above all,
now!—I shall hold you responsible."

She marched away.

Hippolyte closed the shutter.

"What does she mean, now?" he asked.

Espérance had already returned to the back room. Zut lay
in the towel basket, breathing heavily. She did not rise
when Espérance touched her. Instead she made a deep, uncertain
sound and kneaded the towels with her paws.

"Hippolyte," said Espérance.

"What?"

"Come."

He came, still fastening his dressing gown.

"What has she?"

Espérance looked at him with wide eyes.

"I think she will have little ones."

Thus was revealed the mystery of "and above all, now!"

The Sergeots' establishment, created for the removal, arrangement, and perfuming
of hair, became during the night a maternity hospital. Espérance
lined a wooden soap crate with towels. Hippolyte boiled water
because he believed hot water indispensable to all crises. He
fetched clean cloths, a lamp, scissors, cotton wool, and a
bottle of cognac; the last proved useful chiefly to himself.

At eleven came the first kitten, striped grey.

At midnight came the second, black with white paws.

At one came two together, one yellow, one tortoiseshell.

Zut conducted these operations with less noise than Alexandrine ordinarily
made over a disputed sou. She licked each infant, arranged
it, and resumed her labours, indifferent to Hippolyte's pacing and
Espérance's whispered encouragements.

At twenty minutes past two, the fifth kitten appeared.

It was white.

Pure white—from its blind head to its minute tail, it
possessed not a spot, not a shadow, not one compromising
hair.

Espérance clasped her hands.

"Hippolyte!"

"I see."

"Like Blanchette!"

"So it appears."

"The same little pink nose!"

"All kittens have noses."

"But this one—Mon Dieu, this one!"

The infant squeaked. Zut drew it towards her with a
paw.

Espérance began to cry.

There are tears before which a husband is powerless, and
others before which he becomes heroic. Hippolyte looked at his
wife, at the white kitten, at Zut, and at the
wall separating them from Alexandrine.

"You shall have it," he declared.

"Hippolyte, we cannot."

"You shall have it."

"She will make a scandal."

"Let her."

"She will call Flique."

"We shall call him first."

He was magnificent, was Hippolyte!

At seven in the morning Agent Flique entered by the
back door. His helmet was damp with mist, his face
freshly crimson, his moustache untrimmed.

He examined the mother, the five kittens, the soap crate,
and Espérance's expression.

"What is wanted of me?" he asked.

"The law," said Hippolyte.

Flique looked uneasy. He preferred the law in matters involving
traffic, closing hours, and persons plainly drunk. The law regarding
cats in confinement was less familiar.

"Whose are the kittens?" Espérance asked.

"The cat's."

"And after?"

"Do I know?"

"They were born in our establishment," said Hippolyte. "They have
used our towels, our heat, our attendance."

"And cognac," added Espérance.

"The cognac was for me."

Flique scratched one cheek.

"In cases of found property—"

"They were not found."

"In cases of animals straying—"

"She came voluntarily."

"In cases of birth upon premises—"

"Yes?" breathed Espérance.

Flique frowned with the concentration of a magistrate. He had
once heard of a dispute concerning a hen which laid
eggs in a neighbour's yard. The eggs, it had been
decided at the café, belonged to the owner of the
yard, though the hen did not. Whether café jurisprudence applied
to cats remained uncertain; yet friendship, breakfast, and the future
trimming of his moustache all urged in one direction.

"The young belong," he said slowly, "to the place where
they were born."

"Entirely?" asked Hippolyte.

"In principle."

Espérance bent and kissed the white kitten between its folded
ears.

"And the mother?"

"The mother belongs to Alexandrine."

"And the four others?"

Flique regarded the assortment.

"For peace," he said, "return them."

"Why four?"

"Because five would be war."

At nine o'clock Alexandrine opened her shop after a sleepless
night. She had arranged a speech, a complaint, and, if
necessary, a fainting fit. Before she could employ any of
them, Émile entered carrying a covered basket.

"For Madame," he said.

"What is it?"

"Perfectly, madame."

He placed the basket on the counter and fled.

Within lay Zut and four kittens.

Zut looked tired but serene. The striped, black, yellow, and
tortoiseshell infants pressed against her belly. Alexandrine counted them once,
twice, and a third time.

Then she saw the letter pinned to the cover.

It was written in Espérance's neat commercial hand.

> Madame,
>
> We have the honour to return to you your
cat Zut, who last night vouchsafed to give birth in
our establishment to five kittens. In accordance with the opinion
of Agent Flique, the offspring born upon our premises belongs
legally to the which premises. Nevertheless, desirous of maintaining neighbourly
relations and unwilling to take advantage, we return four kittens
together with their mother.
>
> We retain only the white one, in compensation for
towels, heating, nourishment, attendance, loss of sleep, and cognac.
>
> Receive, Madame, the assurance of our distinguished consideration.
>
> **ESPÉRANCE SERGEOT**
> *Salon Moderne*

Alexandrine gave a cry which caused Madame Louvet to drop
her milk bottle upon the pavement.

"She has taken the white one!"

Madame Louvet entered.

"What white one?"

"My kitten! The only white! She returns these—these chimney sweeps,
these patchworks, these mustard pots—and keeps the angora!"

"The yellow is pretty."

"I do not want yellow!"

Zut began to wash herself.

Alexandrine seized the egg basket from the counter.

"I shall go there. I shall break every mirror. I
shall throw—"

She looked at the eggs in her hand.

At that instant Flique appeared in the doorway.

He saluted.

"Good morning, Alexandrine."

"You! This is your law?"

"It is an interpretation."

"She stole my kitten."

"The kitten was born there."

"Of my cat!"

"On their towels."

"I shall complain."

"You may."

"I shall go to the commissaire."

"You may."

"I shall tell him everything."

"And he may send an inspector," said Flique.

His gaze descended to the egg basket.

One egg displayed a hairline crack; another had the greenish
pallor of approaching confession.

Alexandrine's hand trembled.

Flique leaned closer and spoke with genial gravity.

"Consider. You have received the mother and four sound kittens,
all without expense. The Sergeots retain one superior kitten. An
inspector, on the other hand, might discover several inferior eggs."

Madame Louvet nodded, for logic is irresistible when applied to
somebody else.

Across the wall came the cheerful ring of the salon
bell. Through the glass, Alexandrine could see Espérance seated behind
her cash desk, the tiny white kitten wrapped in blue
ribbon upon her lap. Hippolyte stood behind Flique's accustomed chair,
stropping a razor. The nickel shone, the mirrors multiplied the
morning, and bottles of lilac and violet water caught the
sun. A customer entered; another waited. Prosperity, that indecent spectacle,
continued.

Zut, meanwhile, climbed from the basket, stepped over her four
children, and rubbed herself against Alexandrine's skirt—not from affection, perhaps,
but because breakfast had not yet been served.

Alexandrine looked down at her idol, her deserter, her ruin,
and the innocent author of all these calamities. Rage swelled
within her; protest rose to her lips; eloquence, law, maternal
feeling, commercial honour, and fifteen years of wounded pride struggled
for expression.

What emerged was:

"Oh, Zut!"

And that, as Alexandrine had always maintained, meant everything and
nothing.

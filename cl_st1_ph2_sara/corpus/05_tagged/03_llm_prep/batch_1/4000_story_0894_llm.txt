### TIDBURY EPPS TAKES FIRE

Specifications of Tidbury Epps, clerk:

Age: thirty-two.

Height: sufficient.

Weight: mostly conscience.

Hair: brown, retiring at the temples before its owner had
learned to advance anywhere else.

Eyes: gray and apologetic.

Salary: low.

Stool: high.

Prospects: filed under Miscellaneous and forgotten.

For ten years Tidbury had sat upon that high stool
in the offices of Spingle & Blatter, Wholesale Hatters, adding
little figures in big black books. During that decade presidents
had been elected, wars threatened, skirts shortened, and the price
of straw braid advanced three-eighths of a cent. Tidbury alone
remained stationary.

He wore a pepper-and-salt suit, plain shirts, dark ties, and
the expression of a man who had just discovered that
he was occupying somebody else's air.

Mr. Spingle trusted him.

Mr. Blatter trusted him.

The cashier trusted him.

The office boy trusted him with lunch money.

But trust, in modern commerce, is a medal frequently pinned
upon the man who is not to be promoted.

When the assistant sales managership became vacant, Mr. Spingle and
Mr. Blatter held a council of war in the private
office. Mr. Spingle, Napoleon of Hatdom, spread his silk-shirted bulk
over a mahogany chair. The shirt looked like a tropical
sunset suffering from delirium.

"Epps?" suggested Blatter.

"Epps!" Mr. Spingle's face, which covered considerable territory, registered pain.
"Good man. Fine man. Honest. Accurate. But can he show
a buyer Broadway?"

"He knows the accounts."

"Can't take an account to supper. What does he know
about shows, cafés, racing, dancing?"

"Nothing."

"There you are. Our business friends are our personal friends.
That's the motto of this house."

It was printed in gold letters in the reception room,
where it saved the firm thousands of dollars annually in
genuine friendship.

Mr. Spingle ticked off Tidbury's deficiencies on fingers equipped with
diamonds.

"No pep. No flash. No nerve. No conversational horsepower. Put
him beside an important buyer and he'd fade like a
gray sock in a fog."

So the post went to Granville Hydeman, who had been
with the firm eleven months, knew six head waiters by
their first names, and could pronounce hors d'oeuvres without injuring
himself.

Hydeman was a polished article. His hair shone. His shoes
shone. His nails shone. A diamond on his right hand
sent messages to distant planets. He wore suits of such
architectural perfection that one expected elevators in them.

Tidbury congratulated him.

That was the trouble with Tidbury. If a man stepped
on his foot, Tidbury apologized for having put it there.

The explanation was Aunt Elvira.

Aunt Elvira had brought him up in Maine, where she
conducted morality as other women conduct a laundry—boiling everything. Pleasure,
according to Aunt Elvira, was the devil's bait. Laughter led
to frivolity, frivolity to dancing, dancing to perdition, and perdition,
she implied, was very badly heated.

When Tidbury, aged seven, whistled on Sunday, she applied a
switch. When he ate preserves without permission, she used a
slipper. When he was detected smiling during family prayers, she
produced an instrument resembling a private cat-o'-nine-tails.

Thus joy and punishment became associated in his mind. Modern
science would have called it a Puritanical complex. Aunt Elvira
called it bringing up a boy right.

She eventually died from an excess of virtue, leaving Tidbury
three hundred dollars and a permanent suspicion that happiness was
illegal.

Then Martha Ritter came into the office.

Martha was twenty-five, from Ohio, and tired of being told
that New York was wicked by people who had never
been there. She had round brown eyes, a tilted head,
and an appetite for life which ten months in a
Brooklyn boarding house had not satisfied. She wanted music, lights,
restaurants with lamps on the tables, and at least one
evening requiring a dress that could not sensibly be worn
to work.

Tidbury fell in love with her on Tuesday at eleven-fifteen.

Love did not enter gradually. It struck him amidships.

He showed her home. He called on her. He brought
salted peanuts. They attended a band concert in Central Park,
visited the Aquarium, and walked twice across Brooklyn Bridge. Their
courtship was inexpensive, wholesome, and as exciting as a municipal
report.

Martha tried to encourage him.

"Don't you ever do anything suddenly, Tidbury?"

"I sneeze suddenly sometimes."

"I mean something reckless."

He considered.

"Once I got off at the wrong subway station."

Martha sighed.

She liked his gentleness, his clean cuffs, his dependable appearance.
But a woman may admire a clock without wishing to
marry it.

Then Hydeman began to hover.

He brought Martha violets. He knew where to dance. He
could tell, without consulting a newspaper, what was playing at
the Winter Garden. When he talked, waiters appeared in the
story carrying silver dishes.

One noon Tidbury looked through the office window and saw
Martha enter a taxicab with Hydeman.

Jealousy, hitherto absent from Tidbury's emotional equipment, arrived complete with
claws, horns, steam whistle, and demolition permit. Little figures danced
in the big black books. The eight became a snake.
The three resembled Hydeman's mustache.

At four o'clock Hydeman sauntered over.

"Working hard, Epps?"

"Yes."

"Fine thing, work." Hydeman lighted a cigarette with a gesture
used by dukes in motion pictures. "Keeps a fellow out
of mischief."

Tidbury's pen creaked.

Hydeman drew two tickets from his pocket and snapped them
beneath Tidbury's nose.

"Take a look. Midnight Revels. Best seats. If you knew
my plans for tonight, old man, you'd envy me."

Tidbury stared at the tickets.

"Are you taking Miss Ritter?"

Hydeman smiled a glossy smile.

"I never publish a lady's name in advance."

Something broke inside Tidbury. Possibly it was Aunt Elvira's last
switch.

He descended from the high stool.

Hydeman was astonished. So was the stool.

"If you are taking Martha," said Tidbury, "you will treat
her respectfully."

"My dear fellow!"

"And if you don't, I shall—"

Tidbury paused. Ten years of submission, twenty-five years of Aunt
Elvira, and thirty-two years of apologizing crowded at the exit.

"I shall knock your brilliant head off."

Hydeman laughed.

Unfortunately for him, he laughed once too often.

Tidbury's fist traveled only eighteen inches, but it carried the
accumulated momentum of a blameless lifetime. It struck Hydeman on
the chin. Hydeman sat down abruptly in the wastebasket, wearing
it like the latest model from Paris.

Silence took possession of Spingle & Blatter.

The office boy dropped a rubber band.

Mr. Spingle rushed out of his room, followed by Mr.
Blatter and an elderly visitor with white side whiskers.

"What's this? What's this?" demanded Spingle.

Hydeman struggled among discarded invoices.

"The man's gone crazy!"

Tidbury looked at his fist. It appeared to belong to
someone else, but he admired the owner.

"I hit him."

"You did?" said Spingle.

"Yes."

"Epps?"

"Yes."

"Well, I'll be— Why?"

"He implied he was taking Miss Ritter to the Midnight
Revels."

Martha, who had entered during this confession, pushed through the
stenographers.

"He is not taking me anywhere."

Hydeman ceased brushing paper clips from his trousers.

Martha's eyes flashed.

"He asked me. I refused. Then he said perhaps I
preferred evenings at the Aquarium with a human adding machine."

Tidbury turned scarlet.

"I suppose I am dull."

"You are," said Martha. "But that does not give Mr.
Hydeman the right to advertise me like a fall line
of felt hats."

The elderly visitor with side whiskers stepped forward.

"Did I hear that young man say Midnight Revels?"

Mr. Spingle became solicitous.

"Mr. Pottle, this little incident—"

Mr. Pottle was Ephraim Pottle of Pottle Brothers, Cincinnati, the
largest hat buyer west of Pittsburgh and east of nearly
everything else. He had come to New York to place
an order capable of affecting the straw-hat market, three factories,
and Mr. Spingle's blood pressure.

Hydeman had been assigned to entertain him.

Mr. Pottle picked up the tickets.

"This is where you intended taking me?"

Hydeman's luster dimmed.

"Just a lively show, sir. Business pleasure."

"Humph! I don't hold with midnight. Nothing profitable happens after
ten-thirty."

Mr. Pottle peered at Tidbury.

"Young man, where do you spend your evenings?"

Tidbury swallowed.

"Sometimes at band concerts. Sometimes I walk across Brooklyn Bridge."

"Any drinking?"

"Cocoa."

"Cards?"

"Solitaire, but not for money."

"Dancing?"

"No, sir."

Mr. Pottle beamed like a rural sunrise.

"Spingle, this is my kind of man. I came here
to buy hats, not to inspect actresses. Let him show
me your figures."

Mr. Spingle's commercial soul changed direction without losing speed.

"Epps is our figures," he declared warmly. "Best man in
the house. Sound. Reliable. Full of—of latent fire."

Tidbury escorted Mr. Pottle to the ledgers. For two hours
he explained costs, discounts, freight allowances, and delivery dates. Numbers,
which had turned traitor under jealousy, now rallied around him.
He knew every account, every shipment, every eighth of a
cent.

Mr. Pottle placed the largest order Spingle & Blatter had
received in seven years.

By six o'clock Hydeman had been relieved of entertaining important
buyers, Mr. Pottle had invited Tidbury to Cincinnati, and Mr.
Spingle had discovered that moral character was the newest thing
in salesmanship.

"Epps," he said, laying a jeweled hand on Tidbury's shoulder,
"you're assistant sales manager."

Tidbury blinked.

"Me?"

"You. Salary doubled. Expense account. Our business friends are our
personal friends."

"Yes, sir," said Tidbury, who had begun to suspect the
motto.

Outside, Martha waited by the elevators.

"I'm sorry I hit him," Tidbury said.

"I'm not."

"It was wicked."

"It was accurate."

The elevator arrived, but Martha did not enter it.

"What are your plans for tonight?" she asked.

Tidbury's first impulse was to say cocoa. Aunt Elvira stirred
in her grave, tightening spectral fingers around a spectral switch.

Then Tidbury looked at Martha. He looked at the bright
avenue below. He looked at Hydeman's two abandoned tickets, still
in his hand.

Love laughs at complexes, and occasionally gets excellent seats.

"We," said Tidbury, "are going to the Midnight Revels."

Martha smiled.

"Tidbury!"

"Afterward," he continued recklessly, "we shall have supper."

"Where?"

"I don't know."

That was the finest answer he had ever made.

They entered the elevator together. Tidbury stood erect in his
pepper-and-salt suit, a promoted man, a violent man, a man
with two tickets and no definite plan.

For the first time in ten years, the high stool
remained empty after six o'clock.

And Aunt Elvira, wherever excessive virtue had carried her, could
do nothing about it.

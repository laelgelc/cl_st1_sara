From the west window of my house I can see,
on clear days, the smoke of Chicago lying against the
sky. The city itself is hidden. Between it and me
are miles of flat land, brown fields, strips of road,
telegraph poles, and little towns gathered about railway stations. At
night a red glow sometimes rises beyond the fields. It
trembles in the sky and one thinks of furnaces, street
lamps, bedrooms, hospitals, saloons, and people walking alone among crowds.

I had gone to the country because I believed quietness
would restore me. For a time I worked in the
morning and walked after noon. I watched freight trains go
eastward and listened to the wheels after the trains had
disappeared. In October the leaves along the road became yellow.
Rain came and beat them down. It rained all one
night, and in the morning the road was covered with
leaves pressed into the mud.

An old man lived in the village near my house.
His name was Aaron Pike. No one knew much about
him, although everyone had known him for years. He rented
a room over a harness shop and did small jobs
when he could get them. He sharpened tools, carried coal,
and sometimes swept the railway platform. The villagers said he
was crazy.

He was a little man with a large head. White
hairs grew on his chin in separate tufts, and his
coat hung almost to his knees. He had a brown
dog no larger than a rabbit. The dog followed close
to his heels and stopped whenever he stopped. When Aaron
sat on a bench outside the post office, the dog
sat between his shoes.

The old man read newspapers. He could buy none, but
gathered them from wastebaskets or took them after travelers left
them on trains. Every person whose name appeared often in
print became, in some way, a member of his family.
A governor in Wisconsin was his cousin. An actress who
had quarreled with her husband was a niece. Once he
told me that an admiral commanding ships in the Pacific
was his father.

"My father went away when I was a boy," he
said. "He did not like farming. He wanted water. You
understand how that can be."

When I reminded him that the admiral was younger than
he, Aaron looked hurt.

"There are different ways of being a father," he said.

The villagers laughed at him, and he knew they laughed.
Their laughter made him stubborn. He would sit before the
grocery store with the dog held against his breast and
invent long histories. He told how he had played in
an orchard with a famous senator, how they had stolen
pears and had been whipped together. He told how a
woman whose portrait appeared in the Sunday paper had once
sung to him as he lay sick.

At first I avoided him. Later I began to listen.
It seemed to me that he did not lie as
other men lie. The names in the papers opened doors
for him. He entered and found rooms filled with brothers,
sisters, fathers, and children. The fact that these people had
never seen him mattered little. He had seen their printed
faces. He had read of their marriages, illnesses, journeys, and
crimes. They had come near enough to be loved.

In that autumn the newspapers were filled with the trial
of a man named Elias Kern. He had been foreman
in a bicycle factory on the north side of Chicago.
He was thirty-eight years old and had worked in the
same factory for fourteen years. One August night he killed
his wife.

There was nothing remarkable about him before that night. This
was repeated in the newspapers. He was regular at work.
He drank little. He had three children and gave his
wages to his wife. Neighbors had heard no quarrels. The
policemen, the lawyers, and the reporters all wanted something remarkable,
but no one could find it.

Each morning another picture of Kern appeared. His face was
broad and pale. His hair had begun to go back
from his forehead. In one photograph he sat beside his
lawyer with his hands on his knees. The hands were
thick and without shape. Even in the blurred photograph they
appeared stained.

I began to imagine those hands among the shining frames
in the factory. They lifted bars of steel and fitted
bearings. They held wrenches and wiped grease on a cloth.
Under the nails black lines remained after washing. Perhaps Kern
scrubbed them at night in the kitchen sink. Perhaps he
looked at them and thought they belonged to another man.

His wife, Lena, was thirty-four and pregnant. She had once
been a laughing girl. By the time of her death
she had become broad through the waist and slow in
her movements. Her mother lived with them. The old woman
was kind and worked all day. She cooked, washed clothing,
and cared for the children. There was no cruelty in
the apartment, unless the mere closeness of five people in
four rooms may become a cruelty.

Across the factory office sat a young woman named Celia
Ward. She kept accounts and answered the telephone. She came
from a town in Iowa and lived with two other
girls in a furnished room. The newspapers printed her picture
because Kern had spoken of her after his arrest.

She was not extraordinarily beautiful. Her nose was short, and
her eyes, in the photograph, looked almost sleepy. She wore
her hair high and had a narrow neck. Yet Kern
had watched her for more than a year.

From his bench he could see her beyond a glass
partition. Men passed between them carrying wheels and frames. Belts
moved overhead. Metal struck metal. Now and then she raised
her head. The electric light shone on her cheek.

He did not want to touch her. At least, that
is what he said. He had made her into something
that could not be touched. She was a star seen
through factory smoke. Her lips were two low hills at
evening, and beyond them lay a country into which his
stained hands could not enter.

Celia knew he watched her. Women know such things. She
may have liked the gentleness that came into his eyes.
No man had ever looked at her in quite that
way. The young men she met wanted to take her
dancing, wanted to boast, wanted to put their arms about
her. Kern wanted nothing, or seemed to want nothing. His
silence became a gift she could accept without payment.

Sometimes they left the factory together and walked toward the
streetcar.

"It's hot," he would say.

"Yes, it's hot," she would answer.

On another day clouds gathered.

"It may rain."

"Yes. It may rain."

That was nearly all. Within him there were speeches, prayers,
and journeys. He wanted to tell her that he had
seen her standing alone in a field while darkness came
over the world. He wanted to say that she was
the white sail of a boat going out upon a
black lake. Instead he spoke of heat and rain.

At home Lena took off her shoes because her feet
were swollen. The youngest child cried. The mother-in-law moved from
stove to table and back again. Kern sat near a
window from which a narrow piece of the lake could
be seen between buildings.

Lena spoke to him of rent, coal, schoolbooks, and the
child to come. She spoke because these things were real
and because she trusted him.

He answered, but his mind went toward the factory office.
It may be that he hated himself for going there.
It may be that he hated the patient woman sitting
near him because she made escape impossible. There are feelings
that grow by being hidden. They grow without names. A
man wakes in the night and finds them standing beside
his bed.

The trial revealed little. Witnesses told of ordinary days. Celia
Ward cried on the stand. She said Kern had never
touched her and had never spoken against his wife.

"He only looked at me," she said.

The prosecutor asked how he looked.

"I cannot say."

"Was it the look of a lover?"

"I cannot say."

"Were you afraid of him?"

"No."

"Did you encourage his attentions?"

"There were no attentions."

The lawyer grew impatient, but the girl could tell no
more. How could she explain that the foreman's devotion had
pleased her because it asked nothing? Perhaps she had been
lonely too. Perhaps, in the furnished room at night, she
remembered his shapeless hands resting quietly upon the workbench and
was comforted.

On the night of the murder Kern took his wife
to a picture show. The children stayed with their grandmother.
It had been a hot day, and after dark the
air remained heavy.

They sat side by side while pale figures ran across
the screen. There was a story of a young woman
pursued by thieves and rescued beside a river. People applauded
when the hero came. Lena laughed once and laid her
hand upon her husband's arm.

Afterward they walked home. She tired easily, and he slowed
his steps. They bought peaches from a stand. One newspaper
said she ate a peach as they walked, but another
denied this. The small fact became important for a day
and was then forgotten.

At the apartment building the hallway light had gone out.

"There is no light," Lena said.

"No. There is no light," he answered.

They climbed one flight of stairs. The mother-in-law had left
the apartment door unlocked. Somewhere above them a baby cried.
A man coughed behind a door.

Kern entered the kitchen and took a knife from a
drawer. His wife had gone a few steps along the
hall toward the bedroom. He followed.

There was no quarrel. There was no light.

He stabbed her once below the shoulder. She turned and
spoke his name. He stabbed her again. She fell against
the wall and then to the floor.

For a little time he stood looking down. Doors opened
on the floor above. The crying baby became quiet. Nothing
happened. That detail, told by Kern himself, remained in my
mind.

"Nothing happened," he said. "I stood there, and nothing happened."

Then he ran into the street. He threw the knife
through a fence into a vacant lot and walked rapidly
for several blocks. At a drugstore he telephoned the police.
He said a stranger had entered the apartment and attacked
his wife. He described a tall man in a gray
hat.

Before dawn the knife was found. At noon Kern confessed.

"Why did you do it?" they asked.

"There was no light," he said.

"What has that to do with it?"

"There was no light in the hall."

"Did you wish to marry Celia Ward?"

"No."

"Did your wife mistreat you?"

"No."

"Were you angry?"

"No."

"Then why did you kill her?"

He looked at his hands.

"I don't know."

For weeks the newspapers tried to complete the unfinished sentence.
Doctors gave names to his condition. The prosecutor spoke of
concealed lust. The defense spoke of temporary madness. Reporters interviewed
neighbors and cousins. The dead woman's mother said Kern had
always been a good husband.

"He brought his money home," she said. "He fixed things.
He was good to the children."

She wept, not only for her daughter but for him.
That confused the reporters. Goodness should have stood on one
side of the courtroom and evil on the other. Instead
the old woman's compassion crossed the space and sat beside
the murderer.

Aaron Pike read every account. For several days he said
nothing to me of Kern, and I was glad. Then
one afternoon in November I met him on a road
beyond the village.

Fog had come up from the low fields. Trees appeared
and vanished. The road was soft, and water stood in
the wagon tracks. I heard the little dog's feet before
I saw the old man.

Aaron came out of the fog with his coat buttoned
to the throat. His head seemed too large for his
body. The dog was in his arms.

"I have been looking for you," he said.

I told him I was going home.

"I'll walk," he said. "You know about my brother."

I knew at once whom he meant, but pretended not
to understand.

"My brother in Chicago. Elias. Elias Kern."

"He is not your brother."

Aaron stopped. Fog moved between us. Behind him a willow
tree bent over the ditch, and for a moment the
old man and the tree became one twisted thing.

"You must not say that," he whispered. "We are brothers."

Then he began to tell me of their childhood. They
had been born, he said, in a farmhouse near a
river in Ohio. Their father made barrels. Their mother had
red hair and sang while kneading bread. Elias was the
stronger boy, but Aaron could run faster. Together they slept
in an attic room under a sloping roof.

"It rained one night," he said. "It rained and the
river rose. Elias held my hand. I was afraid. He
was not afraid."

None of it had happened. Aaron had been born in
Vermont. Kern's parents were German immigrants who had lived in
Milwaukee. These facts did not trouble him.

"We went away together," he continued. "There was a bridge.
We stood on the bridge and looked down. He wanted
to go with the water. He always wanted to go
somewhere."

The little dog moved uneasily in his arms.

"You have read too many newspapers," I said.

"No. They don't know him."

"And you do?"

"I know. We are brothers."

His voice had become stern. He stepped close and caught
my sleeve.

"He did not want the girl," he said. "That is
what they say, but it is wrong. He wanted—"

The old man's mouth opened and closed. He could not
find the word. His face wrinkled until it looked like
the bark of an ancient tree. He turned his eyes
toward the hidden city.

"He wanted the far place," he said at last. "The
place beyond. Do you understand?"

I did understand, or believed I did. In my mind
the factory appeared. Steel frames hung from hooks and passed
slowly beneath white lights. Kern stood among them. Through glass
he saw the young woman bending over a ledger. She
was not a woman to him. She was an opening
in a wall. Beyond her were roads, lakes, clean rooms,
and evenings in which no one asked him for money
or coal or speech. It was a foolish dream and
a cruel dream, but it was his.

At home waited the pregnant wife, the children, the good
old mother, and all the warm, tired bodies of life.
They loved him and therefore held him. Their need pressed
against him. He could not tell them that he wished
to become light enough to pass through a wall.

Perhaps the wife's hand on his arm in the picture
show had frightened him. Perhaps her trust was heavier than
hatred. In the dark hall he destroyed the person nearest
to him because nearness itself had become unbearable.

None of this excused him. The dead woman lay on
the floor. Her unborn child died with her. Yet as
Aaron spoke, I felt the murderer's dumb longing move through
the fog and enter us. I thought of the red
glow beyond the plains, of thousands of windows and men
sitting behind them. Each man desired some star. Each man
had his hands upon some living thing.

"We are brothers," Aaron said again.

He had begun to rock the dog against his breast.
The animal's head protruded beneath his chin. Its eyes were
bright and frightened.

"We are all brothers," he said. "Elias and me. You
too. You don't think so, but you are."

His arms tightened. The dog gave a little sound.

"Aaron, put it down."

"He was alone," the old man said. "You don't know.
A man gets a wife and children and is still
alone. He works beside men and is alone. He speaks
and the words fall down between them."

The dog struggled. Aaron pressed it harder. His fingers disappeared
into its brown hair.

"We slept together under the roof," he said. "It rained.
He held my hand."

I seized the dog. For an instant the old man
resisted. His strength surprised me. His face lifted above the
animal, and in the fog he looked like a hanged
body twisting at the end of a rope.

"Let go," I shouted.

He released it suddenly. I stumbled backward, holding the dog.
It trembled and tried to bite me. When I set
it on the road, it ran several yards, stopped, and
looked back.

Aaron began to cry. There was no violence in his
crying. Tears slid quietly through the white hairs on his
cheeks.

"I wouldn't hurt him," he said.

The dog came back. It approached upon its belly and
then stood beside the old man's shoe. Aaron did not
pick it up.

We walked toward the village. Fog gathered on our clothing.
At the first houses he left me without speaking. The
dog followed him.

The trial ended two days later. Kern was found guilty
and sentenced to die. His statement before sentence was brief.
He said he was sorry for the children. He did
not mention Celia Ward. He did not explain the darkness
in the hallway.

The papers soon found other matters. A train struck a
wagon. An alderman was accused of taking money. A singer
came to Chicago and was photographed stepping from a railway
car. Elias Kern's face moved from the first page to
the third and then disappeared.

Aaron continued to collect papers. For a week I did
not see him. I asked at the harness shop and
was told he still slept in his room.

One evening, while I sat at the west window, he
passed my house.

The day had been rainy. Water dripped from the bare
branches, and the light was almost gone. Aaron walked slowly
along the road. His arms hung empty at his sides.

I went to the door.

"Where is the dog?" I called.

He did not turn. Perhaps he did not hear. He
walked on toward the fields, where fog was beginning to
rise.

The next morning I asked in the village. No one
had seen the dog. A boy said he had heard
something crying behind the harness shop during the night, but
boys say many things. Aaron would answer no questions.

"I never had a dog," he said.

When I reminded him, he looked at me with mild
surprise.

"My brother had one when we were boys," he said.
"It drowned in the river."

After that he avoided my road.

Winter came. Kern was executed before spring. I knew the
morning it happened, although I had stopped reading the accounts.
The sky above Chicago was colorless, and a freight train
stood for a long time on the tracks. Steam drifted
over the fields. I thought of a man looking down
at his shapeless hands while other men fastened straps.

I thought also of Aaron's dog. I did not know
that he had killed it. Perhaps it ran away. Perhaps
someone took it in. Yet when I remembered his arms
tightening in the fog, I believed the small creature had
died because it was near him and because he loved
it.

That is a harsh thing to believe. It may be
untrue. Still, there are affections that do not know how
to open their hands. They draw the beloved nearer and
nearer. They whisper of brotherhood. They promise shelter from the
rain. Then the breath is crushed out.

Tonight I sit again at the west window. Chicago is
hidden beyond the plains. There is no red glow. Rain
falls steadily, and the glass is cold beneath my hand.

It rains on the factory roofs and on the vacant
lot where the knife was found. It rains on the
apartment hallway, on the courthouse steps, and on the railway
that enters the city. It rains on the old man's
coat as he wanders beyond the village, calling strangers his
brothers.

Along the road the last autumn leaves cling to the
trees. They are yellow and thin. All day they have
trembled in the wind as though they might break free
and fly over the fields.

But they do not fly.

The rain beats them brutally down.

# The Parliament of the Bells

Christmas Eve had laid a white hand upon the city.

Snow rested on the roofs, softened the sharp shoulders of
warehouses, crowned the market-stalls, and made even the chimneys—those black
preachers of toil and smoke—look innocent beneath their silver caps.
The streets, noisy enough by day with wheels, cries, bargains,
quarrels, and the hurried tread of men pursuing money as
though it were flying for its life, had fallen strangely
still. Here and there a lamp burned at a corner;
here and there a watchman passed, leaving a crooked line
of footprints behind him; but the great city slept, or
seemed to sleep, under the moon.

Yet it did not wholly sleep. Sorrow kept vigil in
many chambers; pleasure prolonged its feast in others; avarice counted
its gains behind locked doors; charity went quietly up dark
stairs with bread beneath her cloak; and prayer, poor and
rich, ignorant and learned, rose from a thousand hearts toward
the same stars.

Above all these, in the ancient steeple of Saint Athanasius,
six bell-spirits assembled to hold their yearly parliament.

They came at the stroke of midnight.

First appeared a broad-shouldered spirit in a brown merchant's coat,
with a steel pen behind one ear and a key-ring
at his belt. His cap was fashioned like a little
counting-house, and the buttons upon his waistcoat bore stamped upon
them the words Weight, Measure, Promise, and Trust. He was
the spirit of the great Exchange Bell, whose voice went
daily over banks, offices, wharves, and warehouses.

The second entered softly, though jewels sparkled on his sleeves.
He wore velvet, satin, and fine lace, but over all
was flung a plain gray mantle. In one hand he
carried a golden cup, and in the other a loaf
of bread. He belonged to the bell of Saint Aurelia's,
which looked down upon palaces, theaters, broad avenues, and the
warm mansions of the fortunate.

The third was thin and pale. His garment resembled a
laborer's frock, patched at the elbows and worn at the
knees; and around his neck hung a small wooden shuttle.
There was frost upon his hair. He was the spirit
of the bell called Little Mercy, in the old chapel
among the narrow courts where poverty lodged so closely that
one family could hear another sigh through the wall.

Then, with a gust that made the rafters creak, came
a red-cheeked fellow in a tarpaulin hat, sea-boots, and a
pea-jacket crusted with imaginary salt. A coil of rope served
him for a belt, and a tiny silver anchor swung
at his breast. He was the spirit of the Mariners'
Bell by the eastern docks.

The fifth wore green beneath his ecclesiastical black, and his
round, merry face shone under a cap ornamented with a
little cross. Rosary beads hung from one pocket, and from
the other peeped the stem of a clay pipe, which,
out of respect for the occasion, he did not light.
He was the spirit of Saint Bridget's Bell, guardian of
an immigrant quarter where many tongues mingled, but grief and
gratitude needed no interpreter.

Last came a tall, vigorous personage in a blue cloak
lined with sunrise-colored silk. His helmet bore no plume, but
a small lamp; books were buckled at one side of
him, ballots at the other, and upon his breast he
wore a shield inscribed with the word Forward. He was
the New Century Bell, cast within living memory, yet inhabited,
as bells sometimes are, by a spirit older than empires
and younger than hope.

They seated themselves upon the beams in a circle. Beneath
them hung the mighty bells, dark and silent, their bronze
sides faintly brightened by moonbeams that entered through the arches.
Far below, the city stretched like a vast book closed
for the night; but every spirit had read his appointed
pages.

The eldest among them, the Exchange Spirit, rose.

"Brothers," said he, in a voice that sounded like coin
honestly tested upon stone, "another year has gone to its
account. The columns are ruled; the sums are carried; the
balance must be struck. I would gladly tell you that
all is fair beneath my watch—that every measure is full,
every signature sound, every bargain honorable, and every fortune clean.
But if I said so, my tongue would crack my
bell.

"I have seen men enter their counting-rooms poor in purse
but rich in reputation, and leave them rich in purse
but beggared in soul. I have seen them call gambling
enterprise, falsehood diplomacy, oppression economy, and theft a sharp transaction.
They build castles out of paper, sell rooms in them
to widows, and, when the rain comes through the roof,
declare that Providence altered the weather.

"One merchant adulterates flour, and then kneels on Sunday to
pray for daily bread. Another shortens the wage of fifty
workmen by a penny each, and gives fifty pennies to
a public charity in order that his name may be
printed in large letters. A third ruins his partner, cheats
his customer, bribes an officer, and cries out against the
dishonesty of servants! Bless us, what delicate consciences some men
possess! They are troubled by a missing spoon and sleep
soundly beside a stolen estate.

"I rang above them. At morning I cried, 'Be just!'
At noon I cried, 'Remember the poor!' At evening I
cried, 'What shall it profit a man?' But they heard
only the clink of gain. Gold has a small voice,
brothers, yet when held close to the ear it can
drown a cathedral."

The brown-coated spirit paused. His face softened.

"But my account has another side. God forbid that we
should denounce a city because some of its citizens mistake
cunning for wisdom! I know merchants whose word is stronger
than sealed parchment; employers who regard laborers not as hands,
but as men with wives, children, infirmities, hopes, and immortal
souls. I know one gray-haired importer who, when a fire
destroyed his warehouse, paid his clerks before he paid himself.
'They have only wages,' said he; 'I have courage and
friends.'

"I know a grocer who keeps no book against the
poor after winter begins. He pretends to be stern, shakes
his head, makes a terrible scratching with his pen, and
writes down debts in ink that charity washes away. I
know a banker who refused a glittering fraud when acceptance
would have doubled his fortune. 'My children,' said he, 'must
inherit my name before they inherit my money.' There was
wealth, gentlemen! There was capital bearing interest in heaven!

"Therefore I condemn not trade, which binds shore to shore
and household to household. Commerce is a noble ship when
Honor stands at the helm, Justice studies the chart, and
Charity keeps watch for wrecks. But when Greed seizes the
wheel, Speculation cuts the compass-card, and Dishonesty drills holes in
the hull—then look out for breakers! My report is entered.
Let the coming year demand honest weights, living wages, truthful
books, and clean hands."

He sat down, and from somewhere among the beams came
a grave metallic murmur, as though the great Exchange Bell
approved.

The spirit in velvet rose next. The jewels on his
sleeves flashed, but he drew the gray mantle more closely
around them.

"My district," he began, "is warm tonight. Fires burn behind
curtains thick enough to clothe a family; tables bend under
food sufficient for a street; music floats from chambers in
which flowers, summoned from summer by gold, bloom in defiance
of December. Carriages pass from door to door. Diamonds answer
chandeliers, and Beauty, dressed by Weariness in some attic room,
enters late and complains that life is dull.

"I have no quarrel with beauty. Let color brighten our
dark days; let music lift the heart; let the feast
be spread where gratitude says grace. But extravagance without gratitude
is a lamp burning in an empty temple. I have
seen women wear upon one evening what would educate twenty
children for a year, and men lose at cards what
would keep an honest household from despair. I have seen
a family purchase an Italian picture of the Holy Mother
and turn away a living mother who begged for coal.

"They speak much of style in my quarter. This house
lacks style; that coat is out of style; this person
has style; that prayer, perhaps, is not offered in the
latest manner. Yet kindness never goes out of fashion, and
humility is becoming to every figure.

"Do you know what I hear when midnight ends a
splendid entertainment? I hear tired horses stamping in the snow;
servants coughing in cold halls; musicians packing their instruments with
hungry fingers; dressmakers climbing homeward to rooms where no fire
waits. Pleasure dances upstairs, but the bill is sometimes paid
in tears below.

"And yet"—here he raised the loaf in his left hand—"wealth
is not sin. It is power, and power may become
angel or devil according to the hand that commands it.
I have seen noble use made of fortune. A woman
whose name fills no newspaper has opened clean lodging-houses for
working girls. A young heir, tempted by idleness, built a
school where boys learn both a trade and the dignity
of labor. An old lady, thought proud because she speaks
little, goes every week to a hospital and reads to
those whose eyes are dark. She reads badly, brothers—very badly!—but
the sick love every blunder.

"I have watched a mansion where rich and poor enter
by the same door, not on one ceremonial day, but
throughout the year. Its master says, 'I am only steward
here.' His children know the names of the servants' children.
When death came to the coachman's house, they mourned as
neighbors, not as employers. The gold in that house shines,
because affection polishes it.

"Let no man be ashamed because Providence has entrusted him
with abundance; let him tremble lest he mistake the trust
for ownership. The coat in the closet, the bread in
the pantry, the unused room, the idle book, the wasted
hour—all cry out for service. Luxury builds high walls; charity
opens gates. May the new year teach those beneath my
bell that no drawing-room is elegant where sympathy is a
stranger, and no jewel becomes its wearer so well as
a generous deed."

When he resumed his seat, the moonlight touched his golden
cup, but rested longer upon the bread.

Then the pale spirit with the shuttle stood up.

At once the others uncovered their heads.

"My brothers," he said, "you have spoken of sins that
glitter. Mine lie in darkness. The poor are not ornaments
in the city's pageant. They hide themselves. Hunger is ashamed;
cold creeps close to the wall; and despair, fearing rebuke,
often makes no noise until it dies.

"I saw a girl this year."

His voice faltered, and far below a clock struck one.

"She was seventeen. Her name was Ruth, though scarcely anyone
used it, for to the warehouse she was Number Eighty-Seven,
and to the woman who rented her room she was
'the attic.' She sewed fine linen for persons who would
never know that her fingers had touched it. The garments
passed under bright lamps and over warm shoulders; she sat
beneath a roof where the snow sifted through a crack
and melted on her work.

"I saw her in spring, when her cheeks still carried
a little color. She had two doves in a wooden
cage. They were poor, foolish, affectionate creatures, and she loved
them because they welcomed her home. She fed them before
she fed herself.

"I saw her in summer. Work was plentiful, and wages
were small. She bent from dawn until the lamp failed.
Stitch, stitch, stitch—the needle went in; the breath came out.
For twelve shirts she received what a fashionable gentleman might
give to have one glove cleaned. 'Faster,' said the agent.
'There are many girls waiting.' There are always many waiting
where necessity bargains with greed.

"I rang at noon. My whole bronze heart cried, 'Look
down! Look down!' Ladies passed beneath me carrying parcels of
lace. Gentlemen discussed prosperity. A committee met to consider the
moral improvement of the poor, and adjourned before visiting them
because the weather threatened rain.

"In autumn Ruth coughed. The doves turned their little heads
whenever she did so. Her cheeks faded; her eyes grew
large and bright; the blue veins showed at her temples.
She pawned her Sunday dress, then her blanket, then the
shawl her mother had left her. Still she sewed.

"I rang at six: 'Rest, child!'

"The warehouse message came: 'More work.'

"I rang at nine: 'Help her!'

"The city answered with theater music, carriage wheels, and laughter.

"I rang at twelve: 'She is dying!'

"No one came."

The spirit bowed his head. The wind moved through the
open arches with a sound like restrained weeping.

"On the twenty-third of December," he continued, "she carried home
a bundle through the snow. Her shoes were broken. Her
feet left faint red marks where the pavement cut them,
but the falling flakes covered the marks, as the world
covers many evidences against itself. She climbed five flights, resting
at each landing. In her room the water had frozen
in the cup.

"She gave the doves their last seed.

"'You must eat,' she told them. 'Tomorrow is Christmas.'

"Then she worked. The lamp sank lower; the wick charred;
the flame leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like a
spirit unwilling to depart. At ten she dropped the needle.
At eleven she tried to rise and could not. At
midnight she heard my bell.

"I called as I had never called before. 'Mercy! Mercy!
Here is one for whom Christ was born! Here is
your sister, city! Here is your child!'

"But stone does not hear, and flesh may make itself
stonier than stone.

"She drew the cage toward her and opened it. The
doves fluttered out, frightened by the cold, then settled on
the bed. One nestled against her hand.

"'You can fly,' she whispered. 'Find a warm home.'

"At two, the lamp went out.

"At three, snow entered through the crack and lay upon
the sill.

"At four, Ruth dreamed—or perhaps she woke indeed. Her face
changed. She seemed to see a door where none had
been, and beyond it a room not built by human
hands. There was bread upon the table, fire upon the
hearth, and someone who knew her name.

"'Mother?' she said.

"Then, more softly, 'Lord?'

"At five she was dead."

No spirit moved.

"The doves remained beside her until morning. The warehouse sent
for the unfinished linen and complained of delay. The landlord
asked who would pay the rent. A parish officer wrote
a line in a book. Yet I tell you, brothers,
the heavens did not record her as Number Eighty-Seven. The
Shepherd who marks the fall of a sparrow knew every
stitch she had made, every crust she had surrendered, every
tear she had hidden. She had no home on earth;
therefore the everlasting doors opened wide.

"But let no one soothe his conscience too quickly with
heaven! It is a cruel piety that permits a sister
to perish and then sings loudly over her grave. The
cross is not an ornament for steeples only; it is
a burden to be carried into courts, factories, attics, and
alleys. If our Christmas faith cannot warm a room, feed
a body, shorten a day's labor, or save a child,
what is it but music without meaning?

"I have finished."

He sat down, covering his face. For a while there
was no sound but the wind and the slow beating
of snow against the louvers.

Then the Mariners' Spirit blew his nose with such force
into a blue handkerchief that the smallest bell trembled.

"Beg pardon, shipmates," he said. "There's weather in my eyes."

The others smiled through their sadness, and he planted his
sea-boots wide upon the beam.

"My watch runs from the fish market to the outer
harbor, taking in sailors, stevedores, pilots, oyster-men, rope-makers, ship-chandlers, and
a population of small boys who appear to be born
with tar on their breeches. It's a rough coast, morally
speaking; shoals of rum, rocks of violence, fog-banks of ignorance,
and now and then a pirate sailing under the flag
of respectability. But there are brave hearts there—aye, hearts big
enough to tow a disabled neighbor through a gale.

"Our preacher, Parson Flint, is the liveliest craft afloat. He
has shoulders like a capstan, eyebrows like storm-signals, and a
voice that could reef the topgallants on Judgment Day. He
preaches no perfumed sermons. 'Up and at 'em!' says he.
'If you've wronged a man, steer back! If drink has
boarded you, throw it over! If your wife is carrying
all the canvas while you lie in the bunk, turn
out and take your watch!'

"The sailors understand him. Last March a mate who had
not entered a church for twenty years came in during
a storm. Flint was preaching about the lost sheep.

"'Call it a lost codfish, parson,' says the mate afterward,
'and I'll follow your argument better.'

"'Call it what you please,' says Flint, 'so long as
you understand that Heaven is fishing for you.'

"And it caught him too! He signed the pledge, found
honest work, and now spends Saturday nights repairing toys for
dock children. You may call that conversion, reform, regeneration, or
hocus-pocus; I call it getting a leak stopped before the
vessel sinks.

"As for sailors, landsmen judge them by their oaths and
trousers. True, Jack may swear when a polite villain would
smile; but I have seen Jack share his last biscuit,
pull an enemy from drowning, nurse a fevered comrade, and
send half his pay to an old mother who believes
him an admiral. When fever struck the emigrant ship Aurora,
who carried the sick ashore? Sailors. When the river flooded
the southern ward, who rowed through second-story windows? Sailors. When
a child fell between the piers in November, who went
in after her? A fellow named Tom Pike, who cannot
spell philanthropy and would suspect it of being some foreign
disease.

"Tom saved the child. The grateful father said, 'My brave
man, how can I reward you?'

"'Keep the little craft better moored next time,' says Tom,
shivering like a loose spar.

"The father pressed money upon him.

"'No, thank'ee,' says Tom. 'But if you happen to have
a dry pair of breeches about your person, I'm open
to negotiation.'"

The belfry rang with laughter. Even Little Mercy lifted his
head.

"So," continued the sailor spirit, grinning, "let us not sink
under too much solemn ballast. The world has grief enough,
but courage is afloat, kindness has not lost its compass,
and humor is the pump that keeps many a laboring
soul above water. My wish for the new year is
a clear light in every harbor, a strong hand for
every wreck, and a sober captain in command of every
human ship."

"Faith, and amen to that," cried the spirit of Saint
Bridget's, springing up. "Though I'd add a warm kitchen after
the voyage, for a man can't live on compass points."

His voice carried the music of Ireland, but behind it
sounded Poland, Italy, Germany, and other far countries whose people
dwelt beneath his bell.

"My district," he said, "is a patchwork quilt, and every
patch remembers another bed. We have old men who speak
of green hills, mothers who dream of vineyards, fathers who
still hear the village clock across an ocean, and children
who answer in the new tongue when their parents call
in the old. They came with trunks tied by rope,
saints' pictures wrapped in aprons, seeds in little bags, and
hope in quantities too large for the customs officers to
measure.

"They are grateful, these people—not always quietly, to be sure.
They thank God with candles, flowers, fiddles, tears, and such
singing as would make a stone saint step down for
a dance. At Saint Bridget's on feast days, the church
is full before dawn. The laborer kneels beside the shopkeeper;
the washerwoman beside the magistrate's wife; the newly arrived boy
beside the gray citizen whose grandchildren have forgotten the ship
that brought him.

"And no velvet rope divides their prayers.

"That is as it should be. Death has no reserved
pews; neither should mercy.

"I will not pretend all is perfect. We have tempers
hot enough to bake bread, old-country quarrels imported duty-free, and
mothers who think every son called to the priesthood when
he merely dislikes work. We have sinners in abundance—sure, where
there are people, sinners will never be scarce. But the
church door stands open. The tired woman may enter with
her market basket. The laborer need not change his coat.
The child is welcomed even if the baby cries in
the middle of the sermon—and sometimes the baby makes the
sounder argument.

"I say to my Protestant brothers, with affection and no
brickbat concealed under my cassock: make your religion warmer! A
man frozen by the world needs more than a correct
opinion; he needs a hearth. Give him music he can
sing, a hand he can grasp, a place where his
poverty is not inspected like a suspicious parcel. Do not
preach of the Father and then behave like distant cousins.

"And to my own people I say: candles are beautiful,
but let every candle light a deed. It is a
poor devotion that kisses the image of a saint and
neglects the sick neighbor upstairs. Say the beads, yes; but
count also the hungry. Adorn the altar, yes; but remember
the living temples in rags.

"This year I watched immigrants build one another's homes after
a fire. The Germans brought tools, the Irish brought labor,
the Italians brought food, the Poles brought timber, and the
children brought confusion. A Jewish physician came without being asked
and tended the burned. No one stopped to compare creeds
while the roofs were open to the sky. That, my
friends, was a council worth attending.

"If we can work together in fire and flood, why
not in fair weather? The Almighty understands every language and
is not frightened by an accent. Let each worship according
to conscience; let none suppose he has purchased the whole
of heaven and may charge admission to the rest. The
road may pass by chapel, meetinghouse, cathedral, or quiet room;
but if it does not pass by love, it leads
nowhere."

The spirit's little cross shone in the moonlight. He made
a cheerful bow and sat down.

Last rose the New Century Bell.

During the reports he had listened like a commander receiving
news from scattered fronts. Now he placed one hand upon
the hilt of a sword that was, on closer inspection,
only a ruler engraved with the words Reason and Mercy.

"Comrades," he began, "the campaign is unfinished, but the army
advances.

"I have watched city councils where private interest fought public
duty and too often won; yet I have also seen
citizens drag hidden corruption into daylight. I have heard politicians
promise reform with one hand while accepting payment with the
other; yet young voters are asking inconvenient questions, and inconvenient
questions are the artillery of democracy.

"I have seen prisons where punishment hardens the fallen; but
I have seen new workers enter with books, trades, patience,
and the belief that a man is more than his
worst act. I have seen children labor when they should
learn, teachers underpaid, classrooms overcrowded, and education treated as though
memory were a barrel to be stuffed with dates. But
I have seen schools where hand, head, and heart are
trained together; where a child studies the stars without losing
sight of his neighbor; where girls receive the same key
of knowledge once reserved for boys.

"Our literature has its camp followers—sensational tales that paint vice
in scarlet merely to sell tickets to the spectacle, shallow
verses that rhyme moon with June and call the business
inspiration, histories that flatter nations and forget humanity. Yet strong
books are being written: books that enter tenements, factories, farms,
prisons, and the secret chambers of conscience. The printing press,
like a cannon, may destroy; like a bell, it may
awaken.

"I have watched women organizing for education, property rights, fair
wages, and a voice in laws they must obey. Some
laugh at them. Laughter is an old weapon, but it
has never permanently defeated justice. I have watched laborers form
associations, sometimes wisely, sometimes angrily, learning through error that strength
without fairness becomes tyranny, whether it wears a silk hat
or a workman's cap.

"I have seen hatred parade under banners of race, nation,
class, and creed. Each prejudice declares itself ancient, as if
age could make a wrong venerable. But I have also
seen barriers fall. Children study together who once would have
been strangers. Scholars compare faiths without drawing swords. Ministers exchange
pulpits. Citizens born on opposite shores stand beneath one flag—not
a flag of conquest, but of common obligation.

"Do not misunderstand me. Progress is not a carriage in
which mankind may sleep while history drives. It is a
steep road climbed by bleeding feet. Every reform has cowards
behind it, profiteers beside it, and fanatics rushing ahead of
it. Justice must be brave without becoming cruel, patient without
becoming idle, and strong without worshiping strength.

"The younger generation is accused of presumption. Good! Every generation
should presume that evils tolerated by its fathers can be
overthrown by its children. Let youth question us. Let it
expose our comfortable compromises. Let it laugh at our pomp,
reject our hypocrisies, and demand proof where we offer phrases.
But let youth remember that enthusiasm without discipline is a
trumpet without an army. Study, organize, labor, vote, teach, build—then
up and at 'em!

"I believe in the dawn because I have watched the
darkness thinning. I believe in tolerance, not the lazy tolerance
that says all truth is equal and all conduct indifferent,
but the generous tolerance that holds conviction firmly without strangling
a neighbor. I believe in peace, not because nations have
grown gentle, but because war has grown so terrible that
conscience must either master it or perish beneath it. I
believe in reform, because mercy and reason, though often defeated
in a skirmish, enlist again.

"And I believe in these bells. We cannot descend to
carry bread, sign laws, open schools, or bind wounds. Yet
we can call. We can warn. We can remind the
merchant of honesty, the rich of stewardship, the city of
its poor, the sailor of his harbor, the worshiper of
fellowship, and the reformer of hope. A voice faithfully used
is also a deed."

As he finished, a faint grayness appeared along the east.

The moon grew pale. Stars withdrew one by one, like
candles extinguished after prayer. Beneath the snow-covered roofs a wagon
rattled; a baker opened his door; somewhere a child awoke
and whispered, "Christmas!"

The six spirits stood.

"Brothers," said the Exchange Spirit, "shall we enter the new
year with the old warning?"

"With a stronger one," replied Little Mercy.

"And a merrier one when occasion serves," added the Mariner.

"With doors open wide," said Saint Bridget's.

"With bread as well as gold," said Saint Aurelia's spirit.

"With ranks unbroken," cried the New Century Bell.

Then each spirit laid a hand upon the bronze of
his bell. Their forms brightened, becoming transparent in the approaching
dawn; brown coat, velvet mantle, patched frock, sea-jacket, green-bordered vestment,
and blue cloak all shone for one moment like colors
in a cathedral window.

The first bell spoke for honesty.

The second rang for charity.

The third cried for the forgotten.

The fourth pealed for courage.

The fifth chimed for fellowship.

The sixth thundered for reform and peace.

Their separate tones met above the waking city. They did
not contend; they completed one another. Deep and high, grave
and joyous, old and new, they rolled across mansions and
attics, markets and docks, schools and prisons, churches and council
halls. The honest heard encouragement; the selfish, warning; the sorrowful,
consolation; and children, not knowing why, ran laughing to the
windows.

The spirits rose with the sound.

Over the city they passed, no longer six figures but
six streams of music, flowing together into one mighty hymn.
Snowflakes trembled in the air; smoke lifted straight from the
chimneys; the eastern clouds opened like golden gates.

And whether men truly heard the words, or whether the
words were spoken only in that language which conscience understands,
the message descended upon every roof and entered every heart
that was willing to receive it:

"Peace on earth—not peace purchased by silence in the presence
of wrong, but peace founded upon justice.

"Goodwill toward humanity—not goodwill in song alone, but in wages,
bread, shelter, welcome, truth, patience, courage, and love.

"Peace in the counting-house and goodwill in the market.

"Peace in the mansion and goodwill in the attic.

"Peace in the harbor and goodwill among strangers.

"Peace in the church and goodwill among creeds.

"Peace in the nation and goodwill throughout the world."

The sun rose.

The spirits vanished into their bells, there to wait, watch,
remember, and speak throughout another year.

But their music remained.

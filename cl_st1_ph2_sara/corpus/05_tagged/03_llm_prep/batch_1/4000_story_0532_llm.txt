## Humpty Dumpty's Great Adventure

In the darkest corner of the red barn, beneath a
fragrant heap of hay, the Speckled Hen had made her
nest. It was a secret, comfortable place, where the sun
entered through a knot-hole in the boards and laid one
round gold penny upon the straw.

Here rested eleven white eggs and one brown one, all
so smooth and respectable that the Speckled Hen was exceedingly
proud of them.

"My children," she would say, turning them with her bill,
"remain quietly where you are. If you are patient, you
may some day be chickens."

The eggs thought this a splendid prospect, though none of
them knew precisely what a chicken was.

The youngest egg was called Humpty Dumpty. He was larger
round the waist than his brothers and sisters, and possessed
a lively mind.

"I do not see why we must wait," said he.
"There is a world beyond the nest, and I intend
to observe it."

"You will crack your shell," said his eldest sister.

"Or be eaten," added another.

"Bad eggs are eaten," replied Humpty with dignity. "I am
perfectly fresh."

Now the nest was crowded, and the eggs were forever
rolling against one another and complaining. One morning, while the
Speckled Hen was in the barnyard looking for a worm,
the eldest eggs quarrelled as to which of them had
been laid first. They pushed and bumped until Humpty, who
was resting at the edge, received such a kick that
he flew out of the nest.

Down he fell upon a heap of soft chaff.

"Oh!" cried all the eggs.

Humpty lay still for a moment. Then he rocked himself
upright and examined his shell.

"Not even a crack," said he. "You see, I was
plainly intended for adventure."

His brothers and sisters begged him to return, but the
nest was above his head, and no egg can climb
a ladder unless some very unusual arrangements have been made.

Humpty therefore set off across the barn. He walked by
tipping forward, catching himself, and then tipping forward again. It
was not the speediest manner of travelling, but it answered
very well.

The barn appeared enormous. Great beams crossed the roof like
the timbers of a king's ship. Dust danced in the
sunshine, swallows darted through the open door, and the cows
in their stalls looked to Humpty like melancholy mountains.

"Good morning!" he called to one.

The cow lowered her broad head.

"Are you fit to eat?" she enquired.

"I am not travelling for that purpose," replied Humpty, and
went quickly on.

Behind a basket he discovered a smaller egg, pale brown
and prettily speckled.

"Who are you?" asked Humpty.

"I am Coutchie-Coulou," said the stranger. "My mother is the
little Bantam Hen. She hid her nest here because she
fears the cook."

"A sensible fear," said Humpty. "I am Humpty Dumpty, an
explorer. Would you care to behold the world?"

Coutchie-Coulou trembled slightly.

"Is it safe?"

"Certainly not," said Humpty. "If it were safe, it would
hardly be worth beholding."

Coutchie-Coulou considered this. He had always done what he was
told, and had consequently seen nothing except the basket, three
cobwebs, and the legs of a wheelbarrow. So he took
Humpty's hand, which an egg may do in a fairy
tale without inconvenience, and together they went toward the open
door.

They passed the kitchen, from which came a terrible clattering
of pans.

"What are they doing?" whispered Coutchie-Coulou.

"Making breakfast, perhaps."

"Of what?"

"It is wiser not to enquire."

They hurried past and reached the barnyard. The sun shone
upon the pump, the ducks sailed in a green pond,
and beyond the fence were waving fields of grain. A
silvery brook twisted through the meadows, and far away, upon
a hill, stood a palace with golden turrets and a
hundred windows flashing like diamonds.

Humpty uttered a cry of delight.

"There," said he, "is where we must go."

"Why?" asked Coutchie-Coulou.

"Because it is far away and magnificent."

They slipped beneath the gate and followed the dusty road.
Every pebble was an obstacle, every rut a valley, and
once a black beetle passed them with such a fierce
expression that Coutchie-Coulou wished to return.

But Humpty encouraged him.

"We are eggs of character," said he. "We may become
chickens, omelets, or angel's-food; but until then we must conduct
ourselves bravely."

At noon they rested beneath a buttercup. Coutchie-Coulou said that
the palace seemed no nearer, but Humpty declared this was
an illusion caused by the roundness of the earth, concerning
which he knew nothing whatever.

Presently a market-cart came rattling along, and the eggs hid
in the grass until it had passed. Then they crossed
the road and entered the royal garden through a small
gap beneath the hedge.

It was the loveliest place they had ever seen. There
were roses red as rubies, lilies white as moonlight, fountains
that scattered pearls into marble basins, and peacocks trailing their
gorgeous tails along the walks. In the middle stood the
palace, with ivory steps, painted balconies, and silken banners waving
from its towers.

"I am glad we came," said Coutchie-Coulou.

"So am I," replied Humpty. "You see how profitable it
is to follow my judgement."

They wandered beside a bed of tulips and then came
to a wide gravel road. Just as they began to
cross it, a trumpet sounded from beyond the trees.

"What is that?" cried Coutchie-Coulou.

"Possibly a welcome," said Humpty.

But it was the King's cavalry exercising in the garden.
Around the corner swept twelve riders in scarlet coats, mounted
upon tall black horses. The hoofs beat the ground like
thunder.

"Run!" cried Humpty.

The two eggs rolled as fast as they could. Humpty
reached the grass and turned to help his companion, but
timid little Coutchie-Coulou had become confused. He rolled first one
way and then another.

A horse's iron hoof descended.

There was a sharp crack, and Coutchie-Coulou's delicate shell burst
into a hundred fragments. His white life spread upon the
gravel, and his golden heart lay shining in the sun.

The horseman did not see what he had done.

Humpty stared in horror. He called his friend's name twice,
but Coutchie-Coulou could not answer. He would never become a
Bantam chicken, nor behold the world, nor even be made
into an omelet. This was very sad, as was but
natural.

"You were an excellent egg," said Humpty solemnly, "though not
sufficiently decided in a crisis."

Then he heard footsteps and feared that he too would
be crushed.

A young Princess came along the path. She wore a
blue gown embroidered with silver stars, and her dark hair
was bound with a little crown. She saw the broken
egg and the round white traveller beside it.

"Poor creature," she said. "Was he your friend?"

"My dearest and only companion," replied Humpty. "We came from
the barn to admire your palace, and one of your
father's horses destroyed him."

"The King's horses are careless," said the Princess. "They once
trod upon my favourite doll, though she did not spread
nearly so far."

She lifted Humpty gently and placed him in a basket
lined with her handkerchief.

"You shall come with me. I will show you everything,
and no horse shall tread upon you."

Humpty thanked her, for he had good manners when kindness
made them convenient.

The Princess carried him through the palace. He saw gorgeous
rooms with ceilings painted like the sky, a dining-hall where
a hundred golden plates stood ready, and a throne carved
from ivory and covered with crimson velvet. Courtiers in satin
bowed to the Princess, though they stared when Humpty bowed
also.

"Is that egg to be cooked?" asked the royal cook.

"Certainly not," said the Princess.

"Then I am relieved," said Humpty. "I feared that your
hospitality might be excessive."

The Princess laughed and took him to her own chamber.
From its balcony he could see forests, villages, shining rivers,
and mountains blue with distance.

"You have a very large world," said Humpty.

"It is my father's kingdom."

"Indeed? Then I hope he takes proper care of it.
Parts of it are dangerous."

The Princess sighed. She was kind and beautiful, but she
was not entirely happy. The King wished her to marry,
and many princes, dukes, knights, and wealthy lords had come
to seek her hand. She cared for none of them
except Master Gracington, a young gentleman of modest fortune and
excellent heart. The King considered him pleasant but not clever
enough for a royal son-in-law.

Humpty listened to this account.

"Keen wits are often hidden beneath plain shells," he observed.

"That may be so," replied the Princess, "but my father
judges men as he pleases."

In the afternoon she carried Humpty back into the garden.
The King and his riders had gone hunting and were
expected to return at sunset. Humpty begged to see them.

"I should like to observe the horses from a safe
position," said he.

The Princess placed him atop the broad garden wall. It
was covered with warm moss, and from it he could
see the road winding through the trees.

"Sit still," she warned. "The ground is hard."

"I have travelled from a barn," said Humpty. "You need
not instruct me concerning danger."

When she had gone, Humpty looked down upon the flowers,
and then up at the swallows. He felt himself a
person of great importance. Presently the trumpets sounded, and the
King's company appeared beneath the golden evening sky.

First came the hounds, then the huntsmen, then the riders
with feathers nodding from their caps. In their midst rode
the King upon a white horse, and beside him rode
Master Gracington.

Humpty leaned forward to see them better.

The riders passed below. Humpty leaned farther.

"I can see everything!" he cried.

At that instant a loose piece of moss slipped beneath
him. He rocked once, twice, and made a desperate effort
to recover his balance. But an egg is round, and
pride supplies no feet.

Humpty Dumpty fell from the wall.

He struck the gravel with a dreadful crash. His shell
flew apart, his white robe was scattered, and his golden
centre broke upon a stone. He lay crushed and mangled
beyond all mending.

The King drew rein.

"What has happened?" he demanded.

The Princess ran from the palace.

"It is Humpty!" she cried. "Pray save him!"

All the King's horses crowded around, but their hoofs were
not suited to delicate work. All the King's men knelt
upon the road and attempted to gather the pieces. The
royal physician brought bandages, glue, wax, and a silver spoon,
but he shook his head.

"Your Majesty," said he, "I could mend a soldier, a
courtier, or even a cracked china shepherdess. But I cannot
put this egg together again."

So Humpty's remains were collected in a small golden bowl,
and the Princess wept three tears over them. She might
have wept more, but a royal banquet was to begin
at seven.

That evening the great hall blazed with candles. The Princess's
suitors sat at a long table, and the King, who
enjoyed contests almost as much as hunting, rose from his
throne.

"My lords," said he, "I have decided how my daughter's
husband shall be chosen. Let each man offer me a
riddle. If I solve it, he shall depart. But he
who asks what I cannot answer shall have the Princess's
hand."

The suitors whispered eagerly, for the King was shrewd and
had solved every riddle in the kingdom.

One asked, "What has a face but cannot smile?"

"A clock," said the King.

Another asked, "What runs but has no legs?"

"A river," said his Majesty.

A third asked, "What has a crown but is not
a king?"

"A rooster," said the King. "And a noisy fellow he
is."

One by one the suitors failed.

Meanwhile the Princess had drawn Master Gracington behind a velvet
curtain.

"Listen," she whispered. "I will give you a riddle, but
you must remember every word."

"It is not honourable to receive help," he said.

"It is not honourable to marry a Princess who dislikes
you," she replied. "The others have no objection to that."

Then she told him what to ask.

At last Master Gracington stood before the throne.

"Your Majesty," said he, "pray answer me this:

"White was my coat, and gold was my heart.
No foot had I, yet I travelled afar.
A horse slew my friend, but kindness saved me.
I sat above kings, yet fell below men.
All your horses and all your men
Could never make me whole again.
What was I?"

The King frowned. He guessed a cloud, a fallen star,
a snow-covered castle, and a pudding with raisins. Each answer
was wrong.

At length he struck the arm of his throne.

"I cannot solve it. Speak, Master Gracington. What was the
thing?"

"Humpty Dumpty, your Majesty," said Gracington. "And Humpty Dumpty was
an egg."

The Princess smiled, and the King, who always kept his
promises when the whole court was watching, declared Gracington the
winner.

They were married in the spring, beneath flowering apple trees,
and proved very happy. The Princess caused a small white
monument to be raised beside the garden wall. Upon it
were carved an egg, a crown, and these words:

**HE WENT FORTH TO SEE THE WORLD,
AND HIS LAST FALL BROUGHT GOOD FORTUNE.**

Thus Humpty Dumpty, though too adventurous for his shell and
too proud to heed a warning, repaid the kindness of
the Princess. And if he never became a chicken, an
omelet, or angel's-food, he became something almost as lasting—a riddle
which children have remembered ever since.

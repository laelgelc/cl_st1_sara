"I am in love with my wife," Martin Vale said.

No one had asked him.

We were sitting in the back room of my house
after dinner, and the others had gone into the garden
because the night was warm. Through the open window we
could hear a little laughter and the clink of cups
being carried outside. Martin had refused coffee. He sat with
his hands between his knees, looking at the carpet.

"I am entirely in love with her," he said. "I
have been married twelve years, and I am happier now
than I was at the beginning. That is the truth.
You must understand that first."

I said I understood.

"No," he said. "You can't yet. I have not told
you anything."

He got up and closed the window. Then he came
back to his chair.

"This happened the week before I married Helen. I have
never told anyone. I don't know why I'm telling you.
Perhaps because you don't know her very well, and perhaps
because you will not make a theory out of it.
People are always making theories out of things that were
muddled from the start."

He looked at his hands and began.

That week, he said, everything had happened to him at
once.

On Monday morning a letter arrived from the Ministry offering
him a post he had wanted for three years. It
was not a magnificent post, though at the time it
seemed magnificent. It meant a private office, a secretary two
mornings a week, and a salary large enough to make
him feel that marrying Helen had become not merely possible
but proper.

At noon the same day a newspaper printed two of
his poems. By the afternoon a critic had called them
the most promising work produced by a young writer that
year. Martin had read the sentence six times. He knew
it was foolish, but he cut it out and put
it into his pocket.

On Tuesday there were telegrams, letters, telephone calls, lunches, handshakes,
invitations. Men who had previously forgotten his name now remembered
poems he had written at nineteen. Women who had once
looked past him held his hand and said that they
had always known. One elderly novelist called him "our young
voice," which Martin said was a ridiculous expression and had
thrilled him utterly.

"And then there was the wedding," he told me. "The
wedding itself was news among our friends. Helen was beautiful
and well connected, and I was supposed to be clever,
and people liked the arrangement. They liked us as a
pair. They admired themselves for liking us."

By Wednesday he had stopped sleeping.

He lay awake in his small apartment while the moonlight
moved over the wall, and he imagined the Ministry waiting
for him, the newspapers waiting for him, Helen waiting for
him, and all the people who would see his name
in print. He felt there were eyes everywhere. Not ten
pairs or a hundred, but a hundred thousand pairs of
eyes, all looking upward at him.

"Upward is important," he said. "I was above them. Do
I make myself clear? I was not really above anyone,
but that week I had been placed on a perch.
Every compliment pushed me higher. Every letter put another inch
beneath my feet."

He laughed without amusement.

"I began thinking of my marriage in the same way.
That is the ugliest part, perhaps uglier than what came
after. I thought I would be good to Helen. I
would guide her. I would protect her. I would be
patient with her small fears. Imagine that. Helen had more
courage in one finger than I had in my whole
body, but I thought I was bringing her the gift
of myself."

On the ground floor of Martin's building there was a
tobacconist's shop. Its owner was a heavy, cheerful man called
Foster, who stood behind the counter in shirtsleeves and knew
every customer's preferred cigarettes. Martin disliked him for no reason.
Or rather, he said, he disliked Foster because Foster seemed
quite satisfied with being Foster.

The tobacconist's wife sometimes helped in the shop.

Her name was Clara. Martin knew this only because her
husband called to her through the curtain at the back.
She was about thirty, perhaps a little younger. She was
small and quick. She wore plain dresses, usually dark ones,
and one blue dress with a tear near the pocket
that had been repaired with thread of the wrong color.
Her hair was brown. Her eyes were bright grey.

"She was not beautiful," Martin said. "I must be exact.
Helen was beautiful. Anyone could see that. Clara's face was
narrow and a little tired, and there were lines at
the corners of her mouth. She had ordinary hands. Her
fingernails were sometimes stained with tobacco. If I tell you
I was drawn to her beauty, I shall be making
the whole thing grander than it was."

He had seen Clara hundreds of times. She had sold
him matches, stamps, newspapers, cigarettes. Until that week she had
scarcely existed for him.

On Wednesday evening he went downstairs because he wanted to
walk. He had tried lying in bed, but he could
feel praise buzzing in him. That was how he put
it. Praise buzzed in his head, in his chest, even
in his fingertips. He wanted the cold air to quiet
it.

Clara was alone in the shop.

Martin asked for cigarettes. She placed a packet on the
counter. When he gave her the coins, two pennies slipped
from her fingers and rattled on the glass. She looked
at him.

That was all.

"No, it was not all," he said. "That is what
happened, but it was not all that happened. Her hands
trembled. I saw them tremble, and I knew it was
because of me."

He paused.

"Am I telling the truth? Perhaps her hands always trembled.
Perhaps she was tired. Perhaps she had heard about my
appointment and was nervous because she thought I had become
important. But I knew. At least I believed I knew,
and belief was enough."

He picked up the cigarettes but did not leave.

Clara bent to retrieve one of the pennies that had
fallen to the floor. When she rose, he was still
standing there. They looked at each other. He said good
night. She said good night. He walked for nearly two
hours.

The streets were pale with moonlight. He went past Helen's
house once, then crossed the road and went back past
it again. He thought of calling her, but it was
late. He imagined her asleep. In his mind she was
remote and light, like a bird resting high in a
tree. He loved her. He said he loved her even
then, and I believed him, though belief hardly settles such
matters.

"But I wanted Clara," he said. "There is no better
sentence. I wanted her."

He had not thought he was capable of wanting anyone
with such simplicity. His desire for Helen had always been
mixed with admiration, tenderness, plans for the future, and the
pleasure of being admired by her in return. With Clara
there was no future and no admiration. There was only
nearness. Her grey eyes, her stained nails, the quick movement
of her small body behind the counter.

He went home and did not sleep.

On Thursday morning the telephone rang seven times before ten
o'clock. The Ministry wanted to know when he could begin.
A journalist wanted a photograph. His aunt wanted to discuss
flowers. A poet he scarcely knew wanted to introduce him
at a public dinner. Martin answered each call with a
bright voice.

Between calls he thought of Clara.

He tried to work, but he wrote her name on
a sheet of paper without meaning to. He tore it
up. He went to the window and saw Foster unloading
cartons from a van. A few minutes later Clara came
outside and lifted one of the cartons. Foster laughed and
took it from her. She pushed his arm. They both
laughed.

Martin was furious.

"Not hurt," he said. "Furious. I had no right even
to be hurt, but I was furious. I saw her
laughing with her husband and felt she had betrayed me.
That will show you what praise had done. By Thursday
morning I believed the world owed me even another man's
wife."

He went downstairs at eleven.

Foster was in the back room. Clara stood behind the
counter wearing the blue dress with the badly mended tear.
Martin bought a newspaper he did not want. She gave
him his change. Their fingers touched.

He asked her to come to his apartment that evening.

He said the words very quietly. He did not explain
why she should come. He did not need to. Clara
looked at him for a long moment and then moved
her head once.

Not a nod exactly, he said. More a stillness followed
by consent.

"What time?" he whispered.

She did not answer. Foster called from the back, asking
where the invoice book was. Clara turned away. Martin went
upstairs with the newspaper under his arm.

At first he felt strong. He had done a decisive
thing. The whole week had been decisive: the appointment, the
poems, the marriage. He began walking through the rooms, arranging
them. He removed a stack of papers from the sofa.
He changed his shirt. He put on a tie and
took it off. He opened a bottle of wine and
then poured it away because wine made the meeting seem
planned.

At one o'clock a letter came from Helen.

It had been sent by hand. Her younger brother brought
it and ran down the stairs before Martin could speak
to him. The envelope had Martin's name on it in
Helen's large writing.

He did not open it immediately. He placed it on
the table and walked around it. Then he laughed at
himself, tore open the envelope, and read.

Helen wrote that she had woken before dawn and become
frightened. Not of marrying him, she said, but of all
the noise around them. Everyone was speaking of his work,
his future, his gifts, and she had begun to wonder
whether there would be room in the marriage for her
ordinary fears and foolish jokes.

Then she wrote:

I do not want to be your pupil, Martin, or
the first citizen of your kingdom. I want you. I
want the man who loses his gloves and forgets to
eat and becomes cross when his collar is tight. Tomorrow
let us stand together without all these people between us.
We will be two human beings. That is enough for
me.

Martin read the letter three times.

"I cried," he said. "I sat there and cried in
the middle of the day. She had seen exactly what
had happened to me. She had seen the perch, though
she did not call it that. And instead of scolding
me, she asked me to come down."

He decided Clara must not come.

For half an hour he was entirely firm. He would
go downstairs and tell her. He would say he had
made a disgraceful mistake. He would ask her forgiveness, though
he understood even then that asking forgiveness can be another
way of making oneself important.

He reached the landing and heard voices in the shop.
Foster was talking to a customer. Martin went back upstairs.

He picked up the telephone. He knew the shop's number.
He lifted the receiver and put it down. He lifted
it again. He imagined Foster answering. He imagined saying, "May
I speak to your wife?" He put the receiver down
once more.

Then he decided simply not to open the door.

"That sounds weak because it was weak. I had become
very good at decisions that no one could test."

The afternoon passed.

Relatives came with parcels. A clerk from the Ministry delivered
documents. Two young writers appeared with a bottle and asked
him to sign a copy of the newspaper. He accepted
their admiration and hated them for bringing it. He wanted
everyone to leave, and when they left he was afraid
of being alone.

At six he ate bread without tasting it.

At seven the shop closed.

At half past seven he heard Foster's voice in the
street. He looked through the curtain and saw the tobacconist
walking away with his hat pushed back. Foster often spent
Thursday evening playing cards at a public house.

Martin stood in darkness by the window. Across the street
the lamps came on one after another. He could see
the moon over the roofs. His hands began to tremble.

Eight o'clock passed, then half past eight.

He felt relief. Clara was not coming. He read Helen's
letter again and pressed it to his lips. He told
himself the danger had passed. He told himself he had
resisted, though in fact nothing had yet required resistance.

At nine there was a knock.

It was so soft he thought at first it came
from the wall. Then it came again.

He did not move.

A third knock followed, no louder than the others.

"When I remember that moment," he said, "I always see
the door and the strip of yellow light beneath it.
I see my own hand reaching toward the latch. I
still think there was time. Even while my fingers touched
the metal, there was time."

He opened the door.

Clara stood on the landing. She wore a dark coat
over the torn blue dress. She had no hat. Her
hair was damp from mist, and her breathing was quick,
as if she had climbed the stairs too fast.

Neither of them spoke.

He stepped aside. She entered and closed the door herself.

That movement, he told me, was what ended his hesitation.
Not because it tempted him but because it took the
matter from him. All day he had imagined himself as
the one who had summoned, chosen, risked. But Clara closed
the door. Clara turned the key. Clara crossed the room.

He said, "You came."

She looked at him with those bright grey eyes.

"I want you," he said.

She put her arms around him.

Martin stopped speaking. From the garden came a burst of
laughter, muffled now by the closed window. He sat still
for so long that I thought he might say no
more.

"At first," he continued, "I was ashamed of how happy
I was. No, not afterward. At first. While I held
her. Happiness is the word, though it seems a cruel
word. Everything became quiet. The telephone, the poems, the Ministry,
the wedding, all those eyes—gone. I was not above anyone.
I was not even particularly myself."

He would not describe the hour. He said there was
darkness, the faint smell of tobacco in Clara's hair, the
small hard strength of her arms, and once the sound
of a cart passing in the street below. He remembered
moonlight lying across the floor. He remembered her cheap dress
folded over a chair. He remembered that they did not
speak.

"It was not love," he said. "I know how that
sounds, and perhaps it is cowardly to insist on it,
but it was not love. I did not want to
know what frightened her or what she had wanted as
a child. I did not want to share mornings with
her. I did not want Foster to disappear so that
I might take his place. I wanted that hour."

When it was over, Clara dressed quickly. Martin tried to
help her with one button and she moved his hand
away. Her face was soft and quiet. There was no
guilt in it that he could see, and no triumph
either.

At the door he asked if she would come again.

"She looked at me then," he said, "and I knew
at once what a stupid thing I had said."

She shook her head.

He almost asked whether she loved her husband. He almost
asked whether she had thought of Martin before that week,
whether her hands had truly trembled because of him, whether
she would remember him. But she opened the door and
went downstairs.

He listened for the shop door. A minute later it
closed.

For some time he remained in the middle of the
room. Then he washed, changed his shirt, put Helen's letter
in his pocket, and walked to her house.

It was nearly eleven. The family were still awake because
of the wedding. Lamps burned in every downstairs window. Someone
saw him at the gate, and the door opened before
he reached it.

Helen came running into the hall.

She wore an old green dress and no shoes. Her
hair was loose. She did not look like the distant
bird of his thoughts. She looked young and tired and
entirely real.

Martin said he must speak to her alone.

She took him into a little sitting room and shut
the door. He had no plan to confess. He wanted
to, or part of him wanted to, but another part
knew that confession might only place the burden of his
act upon her. He stood there unable to speak.

Helen touched his face.

"You look different," she said.

He asked, "Worse?"

"No. You look as if you've come back."

Then she laughed and threw her arms around him.

"I told her I loved her," Martin said to me.
"That was true. It was more true than it had
been in the morning, and yet I had betrayed her
between morning and night. Can you make those things fit?
I cannot. I have tried for twelve years."

They sat together on the sofa. Helen told him about
the confusion in the house, the missing flowers, her mother's
tears, her father's insistence that the weather would fail. Martin
listened. For the first time all week, he said, he
listened without waiting to speak.

After a while Helen took his hands.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we will be two human beings."

"Yes," he told her.

And he understood then that until that evening he had
planned to marry her as a generous superior. He would
descend from his success to bless her with it. Now
there was no descent. He had been brought down already.

"Clara brought me down," he said. "That sounds as if
I am excusing myself. Perhaps I am. Do I make
myself clear? I do not mean she taught me a
noble lesson on purpose. She did not come to educate
me. She wanted something, and she had the courage to
take it, and afterward she went home."

He leaned toward me.

"But when she closed that door, all the air went
out of my greatness. I became a body. A frightened,
hungry, ordinary body. And when I went to Helen, I
no longer thought I was giving her a poet or
an official or any of that nonsense. I was giving
her one human being, damaged and weak and very much
alive."

They married the next morning.

The day was bright. The flowers arrived. The weather did
not fail. The church was crowded with relatives, colleagues, writers,
neighbors, and people Martin hardly knew. The newspaper critic attended
and squeezed his arm afterward. The Permanent Secretary sent a
silver clock. Everyone admired Helen.

Foster came to the church with his wife.

Martin saw them at the back just before the service
began. Foster wore a suit that pulled across his stomach.
Clara wore a grey hat and the dark coat. She
did not look toward Martin.

Or perhaps she did. He had never been certain.

When he and Helen came down the aisle, he kept
his eyes on his wife. Outside, people shouted and threw
rice. Helen laughed, seized his face between her hands, and
kissed him in front of them all.

That afternoon they left the city.

When they returned two weeks later, the tobacconist's shop was
closed. A notice in the window said the business had
been sold. Foster and Clara had moved north to be
near his brother. Martin never saw either of them again.

"For years," he said, "I told myself she had vanished
from my mind."

Their marriage was happy. He repeated this several times, not
with pride now but with care. They had quarrels. Helen
once left him for three days after he mocked something
she believed in. He lost his government post and later
found a better one. His poetry became less fashionable. They
had a child who died, and another who lived. They
endured illness, money troubles, bad summers, good winters, and the
slow accumulation of shared jokes.

He loved Helen in grief and in boredom. He loved
her when she was unjust. He loved her most, he
thought, when she had no need of his love.

"And Clara was gone," he said. "Entirely gone."

Then he shook his head.

"No. That is not the truth."

Sometimes, walking home in the evening, he would smell tobacco
on a woman passing him and his knees would weaken.
Sometimes moonlight would fall across a floor in a particular
way, and he would remember the cheap blue dress over
the chair. Once, in the Ministry, a clerk dropped some
coins. The pennies rattled on the desk, and Martin had
to sit down.

The memory did not come as a thought. It came
through his body.

He said it was like being a seed under dark
soil when warm rain began. For an instant he was
no longer a husband, a father, a civil servant, or
a man with a name. He was something blind and
living, pushing downward and upward at once. At other times
he felt like a tree in spring, full of sap
and without conscience.

"It is an earthy feeling," he said. "That is the
only phrase I have. Earthy and very strong. It does
not ask my permission."

Each time he was certain Clara had returned forever. Each
time he was frightened that the life he had built
was only a thin covering over that one hour.

Then Helen would speak from another room, or touch his
shoulder in bed, or complain that he had left his
shoes in the passage, and the memory would withdraw.

Not be defeated, he said. Not be forgiven. It simply
withdrew.

He looked toward the closed window. In the garden his
wife called his name.

"There," he said softly. "You hear her."

He stood, but did not yet go.

"What I am trying to say is that I chose
Helen. I chose her the next morning and every morning
afterward. But choice does not erase everything that was not
chosen. Perhaps people pretend it does because they are afraid."

He opened the window. Warm air entered the room, carrying
the smell of grass and coffee.

"I am in love with my wife," he said for
the last time.

Then he went out to her.

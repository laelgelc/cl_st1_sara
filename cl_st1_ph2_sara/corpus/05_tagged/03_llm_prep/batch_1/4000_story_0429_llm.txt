### The Laundress of Portillon and the Judge's Bodkin

At Portillon, which is not so far from Tours that
a good horse may not carry scandal thither before supper,
there lived, in the reign of a king whose name
I shall spare, because kings have long memories even when
dead, a young laundress called Marion. Portillon, you must understand,
stands where the Loire takes pleasure in reflecting vineyards, steeples,
washerwomen, and other upright things that appear crooked in the
water; and the people there are industrious, inquisitive, and so
careful of their neighbours' honour that they keep none of
their own unattended.

Marion washed linen for gentlemen, priests, advocates, and certain ladies
whose shifts came to her with more secrets than stains.
She was quick of hand, modest of speech, brown of
eye, and so well made that the wet sheets upon
the bank seemed to embrace her shadow. Yet she was
accounted virtuous, which is a dangerous reputation for a poor
girl, since the rich regard virtue as a locked coffer
and cannot rest until they have tried every key upon
it.

Among those who heard of her was Monsieur du Fou,
chamberlain to the king, a lord well perfumed, well feathered,
and ill governed. He lodged near Portillon while the court
hunted in that country; and, having seen Marion carry home
a basket of linen, he asked whose daughter she was.

"A laundress, Monseigneur," said his man.

"Then she understands stains."

"And the removing of them."

"Send for her."

Thus are great affairs begun: Troy by an apple, Rome
by twins, and a poor wench's mischief by a dirty
ruff.

Marion came to his lodging the next morning, supposing he
had shirts to wash. Monsieur du Fou showed her twelve,
though he had soiled only three and borrowed the others
from his servants to increase the dignity of the commission.

"You shall wash for me alone," said he, "and I
will pay double."

"A crown washes no whiter than a penny," answered Marion,
"but it is heavier in the purse."

He laughed, for gentlemen commonly admire wit in women until
it is turned against them. He gave her the linen,
pressed money into her hand, praised her eyes, and touched
her chin. She stepped back.

"My lord's shirts may require handling," she said, "but his
laundress does not."

He laughed again, though less heartily.

For several days he employed gifts, promises, sighs, verses composed
by another man, and even the declaration that he should
die unless she pitied him. Marion replied that noblemen died
more comfortably than laundresses and that his chaplain would know
the proper office. At last du Fou, finding courtesy too
slow for his appetite, sent away his people when she
brought the linen, barred the chamber, and took by force
what she had denied him by word.

I shall not deck this villainy with ribbons, as certain
tellers do, calling violence love and terror coyness. Marion resisted,
cried out, struck him, and left blood upon his cheek,
but he was stronger, and the walls belonged to him.
When it was done, he offered her ten crowns, as
though injustice, like a tavern reckoning, might be discharged upon
the table.

She flung the coins in his face.

"You hired my labour," said she, "not my honour."

"Take care," he answered. "No one will believe you."

"Then I shall make belief dearer than disbelief."

She went straight from his lodging, disordered as she was,
to the house of the royal judge then sitting at
Tours. This judge, whose name also deserves concealment, though for
another reason, was learned in Roman law, canon law, feudal
law, municipal custom, and every law except that which says
a strong man ought not injure a weak woman. He
received Marion in a chamber lined with books, each of
which had more mercy between its covers than he had
beneath his cap.

She told her complaint plainly and demanded one thousand crowns:
not because honour may truly be bought, but because a
poor person must name a sum before justice will hear
her voice.

"One thousand!" cried the judge. "Why, wench, a princess might
be ruined for less."

"A princess has guards," said Marion. "I had only fingernails."

The judge folded his hands over his belly.

"You say you resisted?"

"With all my strength."

"And he nevertheless accomplished the act?"

"He did."

"Then either your strength was small or your resistance imperfect."

"My strength was less than his, but my refusal was
whole."

Here the judge smiled as those do who have mistaken
cruelty for philosophy.

"My child, the thing is naturally impossible. If a woman
is truly unwilling, no man can prevail, provided she moves
and opposes him continually. The law cannot condemn upon a
tale contrary to reason."

"Show me this reason," said Marion.

"Willingly."

Upon his table stood a cushion, and in the cushion
was a long bodkin used for fastening papers. The judge
took it in one hand and a length of thread
in the other.

"Come here. You shall hold the thread, and I the
bodkin. Attempt to thread it while I prevent you."

Marion took the thread.

The judge moved the bodkin to the right. She followed;
he jerked it left. She lifted her hand; he lowered
his. She advanced; he drew back. For half a minute
the silver eye danced before the thread, and the thread
pursued it as a young clerk pursues preferment, always near,
never admitted.

"You see?" said the judge.

"I see Monseigneur's hand is lively."

"Try again."

She tried. He twitched, dodged, wheeled, and flourished the bodkin
until it seemed a fencer defending its virginity. Marion missed
once, twice, ten times.

"There," said he. "So long as I resist, you cannot
enter the thread. Do you thoroughly understand?"

"Not yet," said Marion. "The bodkin is willing to move,
but perhaps the judge is willing to tire. Let us
continue."

He agreed, pleased with his demonstration.

Marion pursued the eye again, and he escaped. She praised
the quickness of his fingers. He moved faster. She sighed
that no young courtier could have so nimble a wrist.
He began to make circles in the air. She lunged
high, low, sideways, and once so near his nose that
he drew back swearing.

"Gently, little fool!"

"A thousand pardons. Justice is difficult to thread."

The judge resumed. Marion now altered her method. Instead of
chasing steadily, she waited, then darted without warning. She brushed
his sleeve, upset an inkpot, and tangled the thread about
his thumb. While he freed it she came close to
success, but he snatched away the bodkin.

"Still no marriage," said he, holding it up.

"Many courtships last longer."

By this time his wrist ached. Marion asked him whether
learned men possessed greater endurance than laundresses. He declared that
learning strengthened every faculty. She therefore continued, and whenever he
slowed she reproached him with surrender.

"Move, Monseigneur! Resist! Surely the bodkin does not consent?"

"I resist perfectly."

"Then keep it from me."

He did so. Yet no man can wag his hand
forever, not even under authority of the king. Sweat gathered
upon the judge's brow. His sleeve slipped down. Marion made
him reach over the table, then beneath it, then around
the great volume of decrees, which proved more useful in
the sport than it had in the lawsuit. She feigned
to abandon the attempt, and when he relaxed, sprang again.
He saved the bodkin, but struck his knuckles against the
chair.

"A plague on your impatience!"

"Nay, sir, blame my persistence."

Again they began. She teased, hurried, paused, and renewed her
assault; she drew his attention to the spilled ink, questioned
him about a noise in the passage, asked whether a
flea sat upon his nose, and, when he angrily answered
no, came within a hair of passing the thread.

At length his arm grew heavy. He transferred the bodkin
to the other hand.

"Ah," said Marion, "may one change hands in law?"

"The principle remains."

"So does mine."

She recommenced with such determination that the judge backed about
the room, still brandishing the needle. His cap fell off.
His chain of office caught upon a chair. The clerk
outside, hearing the stamping, supposed justice was being administered and
wisely did not enter.

The judge now panted. His resistance became slower, then uncertain.
Marion crowded him against the table. He tried to push
her hand aside; she returned. He raised the bodkin; she
caught his elbow. He freed it; she seized his sleeve.
He protested that this was unfair.

"Does force make the proof unfair?" she asked.

"Release me."

"If the bodkin truly refuses, it can prevent the thread."

He struggled, but she had youth, anger, and both hands,
while he had only dignity, which is a poor weapon
at close quarters. She gripped his wrist, waited until his
fingers slackened, and thrust the thread through the eye.

"There!" she cried.

The judge stared at the threaded bodkin.

"You held my arm."

"Yes."

"You wearied me."

"Yes."

"You distracted me, pressed upon me, and took advantage when
my strength failed."

"Yes."

"That proves nothing."

"It proves," said Marion, "that what cannot be done against
fresh and equal resistance may be done by persistence, strength,
surprise, and the tiring of another. You are a man,
armed with law, in your own chamber, and I have
nevertheless made your bodkin receive my thread. I was a
girl, locked in du Fou's chamber, and he was stronger
than I. Now tell me which of us had the
easier task."

The judge looked first at Marion, then at the bodkin,
and lastly at the door, as though hoping jurisprudence might
enter and rescue him. None came.

"Take the thread out," he muttered.

"Not until you take my complaint in."

He did so.

Now the judge, though vain, was not wholly corrupt, and
perhaps he feared that Marion would repeat the lesson publicly,
for the image of a royal magistrate chased around his
chamber by a laundress is not one that improves with
telling. He therefore spoke discreetly to a councillor, who spoke
to the chancellor, who spoke to the king; and by
the time the tale reached His Majesty, Marion had threaded
not one bodkin but seven, tied the judge to his
chair, and embroidered upon his gown the full confession of
Monsieur du Fou. Such is the laundry of rumour: it
receives a plain sheet and returns a tapestry.

The king summoned du Fou.

"Did you force the girl?"

"She exaggerates, Sire."

"All women exaggerate, as everyone knows; nevertheless, some men give
them a large beginning. You shall pay her one hundred
crowns."

"One hundred!"

"You may appeal, and pay two."

Du Fou paid one.

Marion, however, had demanded a thousand, and when she received
the hundred she said, "This repairs the king's justice, perhaps,
but not my loss."

The saying circulated at court. Nine young lords, each believing
himself handsomer than the others and more persuasive than du
Fou, sent messages offering one hundred crowns apiece if Marion
would grant willingly what had been taken by violence.

You will condemn her now, perhaps; but condemn softly, for
a hungry woman hears morality preached chiefly by those who
have dined. Marion considered that men had already priced her
reputation without her consent, and she resolved that, if they
would make an account book of her, she would at
least hold the pen.

She accepted the nine offers, one after another, choosing the
hours, the place, and the terms, and within a month
possessed her thousand crowns. Then she shut the book.

A tenth lord came with money.

"The sum is complete," she said.

"I will pay double."

"My virtue was taken once, my price earned nine times,
and my folly is now retired."

"Are you become honest?"

"No, Monseigneur. Merely solvent."

After this the court multiplied nine into ninety, and ninety
into a hundred, for noblemen who had never spoken to
Marion boasted of favours she had never granted, while respectable
ladies repeated the boast in order to deplore it. Thus
a woman may commit ten sins and be charged with
a thousand, whereas a man commits a thousand and is
praised for remembering ten.

The king himself grew curious and had Marion brought before
him. He found her merry, well dressed, and no longer
smelling of soap except in the matter of her answers,
which cleansed pretence to the bone.

"What would you take from a king?" he asked.

"Good government, Sire."

"I mean for yourself."

"Then the same, if Your Majesty can spare it."

This reply pleased him dangerously. But the king's mistress, a
lady devout in all matters concerning the sins of other
women, perceived his inclination. She sent Marion five hundred crowns
upon condition that she depart at once for Orléans.

"Must I stay there?" Marion asked.

"Stay anywhere except here."

"Madame," said Marion, weighing the purse, "jealousy pays better than
love and twice as promptly."

She went to Orléans.

There, some people say, she lived soberly; others say that
sobriety visited her only on saints' days and never remained
overnight. I report both, for a chronicler ought not prefer
truth so openly that gossip takes offence. It is certain
that after some years Marion repented, whether of her sins,
her fatigue, or the declining generosity of gentlemen, and performed
public penance. She gave money to the church, fed prisoners,
and endowed a bed in a hospital for lepers, saying
that those whom the world would not touch had the
greatest need of clean linen.

This was well done, though certain matrons objected that charity
purchased with scandal remained scandalous. The priest answered that alms
did not inquire from what purse they came, any more
than soap asked who had stained the shirt.

At last Marion married Taschereau, a substantial citizen, patient in
commerce and less patient at home. She brought him money,
wit, and a name polished by so much public handling
that nothing further could tarnish it. He loved her, quarrelled
with her, forgave her, watched her, and was deceived whenever
he watched too closely; for Marion, though penitent, did not
become foolish, and though married, did not cease to attract
adventures.

Whether all the amorous escapades attributed to her were true,
God knows, and God has kept silence, which is more
than Portillon ever did. She was remembered there as laundress,
complainant, courtesan, penitent, benefactress, wife, and little hussy; but above
all as the woman who made a judge understand with
his own bodkin what his law refused to comprehend.

And if you ask the moral, take this one, though
it cuts two ways and therefore is fit for courts:
strength may stain honour, patience may wash reputation, money may
bleach almost anything, and with strength and patience justice itself
can be violated.

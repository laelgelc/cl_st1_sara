The night before the fighting, Edmond Pillier sat upon an
overturned powder keg and polished his tin cup with a
corner of his sleeve. The labor did not improve it.
Grease, smoke and old coffee had become part of the
metal, as dust and wear had become part of the
men.

Around him the little camp lay uneasy beneath the pines.
Some soldiers slept wrapped in blankets worn thin as cobwebs.
Others played cards upon a drumhead, disputing in low voices
over stakes of tobacco. A pot simmered over a sickly
fire, giving out the odor of burnt meal. No one
spoke of the battle expected at dawn. Yet each man
listened whenever a horse stamped on the distant road.

Edmond was young enough to feel ashamed of fear. He
thought of home instead.

He saw the white gallery of his father's house, the
magnolias standing glossy and solemn before it, and Octavie descending
the steps in her pale dress. She had given him
the locket beneath the old fig tree on the morning
of his departure. There was no miniature within it, only
a lock of her dark hair tied by a blue
thread. On the back she had caused two words to
be engraved: Je vous attends—I await you.

He wore it now against his breast, suspended by a
narrow chain.

A messmate, passing behind him, stopped to warm his hands
at the fire. He was called Silas, though Edmond knew
no more of him than that. The man was lean
and habitually silent, with pale lashes and a way of
looking at whatever another possessed. He had joined their company
somewhere in Mississippi and had never received a letter.

"Got a charm there, Pillier?" asked one of the card
players.

Edmond's shirt had fallen open at the throat. He pushed
the locket within it.

"Better'n a charm," he answered.

"Gal's picture?"

"None o' your affair," another laughed. "Let him kiss it,
an' maybe the Yankees'll turn their bullets."

Edmond smiled, but presently he moved apart and lay beneath
a tree. The earth was cold through his blanket. Above
the black branches the stars appeared near enough to touch,
and one shone with such tenderness that he persuaded himself
Octavie must be looking at it also.

In his imagination she was seated at her window. The
lamp stood behind her, turning the loose strands of her
hair to gold. She was not embroidering, though the frame
rested upon her knees. She was thinking of him. He
could almost feel the quiet strength of her waiting.

He drew the locket out, held it in his closed
hand, and whispered her name.

Then he slept.

The fire sank. The card game ended. Men rolled themselves
in blankets or sat with their backs against trees, their
heads fallen upon their chests. From the neighboring fields came
the thin cry of an animal, answered once and then
no more.

The night grew profound and treacherous.

Before dawn an order passed from mouth to mouth. The
men rose stumbling, cursing the darkness, gathering rifles, cartridge boxes,
blankets and kettles. Somewhere a mule broke loose and ran
among them. A sergeant struck at it with the flat
of his sword. Officers shouted contradictory commands.

Edmond awoke with the taste of earth in his mouth.
He seized his rifle and coat. His blanket was gone;
so was his cup. He thrust his hand against his
breast, but someone pushed him forward.

"Git along, Pillier! You can hunt your pretties in kingdom
come."

The column moved before the sun had reddened the edge
of the woods.

A black bird, flying above the fields later that morning,
saw lines of men advance and disappear. To the bird
they seemed engaged in some immense and foolish sport. They
ran from fences to ditches, knelt, rose, waved their arms
and vanished behind white clouds of their own making. The
ground cracked beneath them. Trees shed leaves without wind.

The bird circled higher when the cannon spoke.

It could not know that one man called upon his
mother, or that another carried in his pocket a child's
sock, or that Edmond Pillier, running with the others through
smoke, put his hand suddenly to his throat and found
the chain gone.

There was no time to search. The company had received
an order to advance, then another to fall back. Men
who had shouted together became separated. A flag appeared through
smoke and was swallowed again. Edmond saw his captain turn
slowly and sink upon one knee as if to pray.
Then something struck Edmond above the temple.

He fell beside a split rail.

When he returned to consciousness, the field was quieter. A
wagon rumbled somewhere beyond the trees. Blood had dried across
his face, sealing one eye. He heard voices and, fearing
they belonged to the enemy, dragged himself into a ditch
choked with weeds.

There he remained until evening, when rain began.

The black bird came down at last. It walked among
torn knapsacks and broken muskets, tilting its head with grave
curiosity. The game had ended. Many of the players had
stayed behind.

Among them lay the silent soldier called Silas. His coat
was open, and against his begrimed shirt shone a small
gold locket.

Father Renaud found him on the second day.

The priest had followed an ambulance party to the field,
carrying water and a little case of oils. His cassock
was spattered to the knees. He passed from one fallen
man to another, bending where a breath or movement gave
hope, murmuring absolution where none remained.

When he saw the locket, he knelt.

He had known Edmond since baptism. He recognized the name
engraved inside the case, beneath the lock of dark hair:
Octavie Laroque. The dead man's features were altered by mud,
blood and the violence that had ended him. The uniform
bore no mark of rank, and no papers could be
found. Father Renaud did not doubt. Love supplied what certainty
lacked.

He removed the locket, blessed the body and wept.

Three weeks afterward, he carried it to Octavie.

She received him in her aunt's parlor, where the shutters
were half closed against the afternoon heat. Before he spoke
she saw the small object lying upon his palm. The
room, the priest, the strip of sunlight on the carpet—all
withdrew from her.

"This was found upon him?" she asked.

"My child—"

"Upon his body?"

Father Renaud bowed his head.

She took the locket and pressed it first to her
lips, then to her heart. She did not cry out.
Her aunt, watching from the doorway, thought for an instant
that the girl had not understood. Then Octavie sank into
a chair and drew her hand across her eyes with
a gesture so old and tired that the woman began
to sob.

The priest told what he knew. In Octavie's mind his
broken sentences arranged themselves into a cruelly distinct vision. She
saw Edmond abandoned beneath a lowering sky. She saw the
open coat, the stained shirt, the locket bright upon his
breast. She saw Father Renaud kneel where she could not
kneel, speak the prayers she had not spoken, and leave
Edmond in strange earth among strangers.

From that day she wore black.

Autumn passed into winter. Rain beat the last leaves from
the trees, and the garden paths became dark channels of
mud. Octavie went to Mass, visited the poor with her
aunt, and answered gently when spoken to. People praised her
resignation.

They did not know how little resignation resembled peace.

At night she opened the locket and laid the lock
of hair across her finger. Sometimes she remembered Edmond as
a boy, angry because she had outrun him to the
bayou. Sometimes she heard his voice beneath the fig tree,
saying that absence was only a road with an end.
Then the battlefield returned, blotting out every other image.

She ceased to ask for news of the war. There
was no news the war could give her.

When spring came, it came without permission.

Jasmine loosened its fragrance over garden walls. The orange trees
flowered. Small birds quarreled beneath the eaves and bathed in
rainwater collected among the stones. Every growing thing seemed possessed
of a secret joy.

Octavie resented it.

Why should the mornings shine so tenderly if Edmond could
not see them? Why should the earth put forth flowers
over graves? The season entered her room like an unwelcome
guest, touching the black dress on her chair and the
severe veil upon its peg.

One April afternoon Judge Pillier called upon her.

Edmond's father had aged during the winter. His white hair
appeared whiter, his shoulders less commanding; yet he retained the
ceremonious courtesy that made even grief observe good manners.

"My dear Octavie," he said, "I have come to request
the favor of your company."

She looked at him with concern. "You are not ill,
monsieur?"

"No. No, I assure you. But I am selfishly lonely,
and the day is fine. Will you drive with me
to Belle-Rivière?"

His plantation lay several miles beyond the town. Octavie had
not visited it since Edmond's departure. The thought of entering
those rooms, of seeing his books and the chair in
which he had sat, filled her with dread. But the
old man's hand trembled upon his cane.

"I will go," she said.

She put on her veil.

The road ran between fields newly green. Freed from winter,
the countryside seemed to breathe. Children waved from a cabin
door; cattle stood knee-deep in shining water. Judge Pillier spoke
little. Once Octavie caught him looking at her with an
expression she could not understand—pity, certainly, but behind it some
concealed agitation.

At Belle-Rivière the lilacs were in bloom.

Their perfume came down the steps in an embrace of
welcome. Octavie faltered. Every familiar object wounded her: the brass
knocker Edmond had polished as a child, the cracked urn
he had once hidden behind, the great oak beneath which
he had saddled his horse.

"I cannot," she whispered.

Judge Pillier placed her hand upon his arm.

"One moment more, my child."

He led her through the hall. The house was strangely
still. At the far end a door stood open upon
the garden, where afternoon light trembled among the leaves.

A man rose from a chair near the window.

He was thin. A scar crossed his temple, and one
arm hung in a sling. But when he spoke her
name the dead yielded it back in his own voice.

"Octavie."

She stared at him. Her fingers sought the wall. The
veil slipped from her head and fell unnoticed.

Judge Pillier turned away.

Edmond came toward her uncertainly, as if she, not he,
were the apparition. Then she touched his face. She touched
the scar and his hair and the rough cloth of
his sleeve. Her dead Edmond; her living Edmond. She began
to laugh, and the laughter broke into sobs.

He held her with his one sound arm.

Outside, the birds filled the trees with clamor. The angelus
bell sounded faintly from town, each note crossing the fragrant
distance like a benediction.

Later, when amazement had become questions, Edmond told them of
the blow, the ditch, the enemy patrol that found him,
the prison hospital and the fever which had kept him
nameless for weeks. A letter sent north had never arrived.
Only after an exchange had he reached Louisiana.

"But the locket," Octavie said. She drew it from beneath
her black bodice. "Father Renaud found it upon you."

Edmond looked at the gold case in bewilderment.

"I lost it before I fell. In the hurry, when
we were driven back. I thought the chain had broken."

Judge Pillier took the locket and examined the clasp.

"It was cut," he said.

For a little while none of them spoke.

Then Edmond remembered the cold camp, his open shirt, the
laughter near the fire, and the pale eyes of the
messmate who had never received a letter.

"There was a fellow named Silas," he said slowly. "He
slept beside me that night."

The silent man had worn it into battle.

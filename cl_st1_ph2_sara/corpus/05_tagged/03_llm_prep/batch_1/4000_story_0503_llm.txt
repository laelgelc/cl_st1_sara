# A Descent from the Stelvio

Herr Lorenzo Lenz was not a German, though he had
acquired a German name, a German waistcoat, and a German
conviction that every traveller entering his hotel had been created
by Providence for the payment of extras. He was, in
fact, an Italian of the lake country, and proprietor of
the Hotel Paradiso, which stood above Como amid roses, chestnuts,
marble terraces, blue water, purple hills, and such other natural
attractions as cost the proprietor nothing.

The hotel itself was a long white building with green
shutters, a leaking fountain, and a view which Herr Lenz
valued at six francs a day without breakfast. From the
upper balcony the tourist might behold Bellagio shining in the
golden evening light, the mountains rising fairy-like through a haze,
and three boats belonging to the proprietor, for the use
of which separate charges were made.

Lenz had begun life with two mules and a persuasive
smile. The smile had disappeared early, having proved less useful
than the mules; but by the time he was fifty
he owned the Hotel Paradiso, twelve carriages, an orange grove
which produced excellent bills and indifferent oranges, and a daughter
named Tina.

Tina was eighteen, dark-eyed, slender, quick-witted, and so naturally flirtatious
that she could not ask a one-armed beggar the way
to the post-office without causing him to hope for domestic
happiness. This was not wickedness. It was temperament, climate, and
practice. If her eyes promised more than her judgment intended
to deliver, the fault lay partly with the eyes, which
were unusually large and had never studied commercial morality.

Of all the young men who came within their influence,
the most seriously affected was Pietro Baldi, one of Lenz's
drivers.

Pietro was handsome in the violent Italian fashion. His hair
curled, his teeth flashed, his shirt lay open at the
throat, and when he sat upon the box of a
carriage with the reins in his brown hands he appeared
capable of driving the chariot of Apollo, provided Apollo paid
in advance. Unfortunately, Pietro had no property, no prospects, and
no conversation except upon horses. He could describe the temper,
ancestry, ailments and secret vices of every animal between Milan
and the Splügen; but when separated from the stable he
was like an actor deprived of his part.

Tina, who knew very little of men and a great
deal of being admired, mistook Pietro's jealousy for depth, his
silence for mystery, and his black eyes for a settled
income.

They met in the lemon garden.

"Your father would sell you to a rich pig," Pietro
told her one evening.

Tina plucked a blossom and looked offended, though not on
her father's behalf.

"No one shall sell me."

"I would kill him first."

"My father?"

"The pig."

"Oh," said Tina, relieved. "Would you really?"

"With this hand."

Pietro raised a hand admirably shaped for crime, portraiture, or
the management of four horses. Tina shivered pleasantly. At eighteen,
a threatened murder may appear a touching proof of devotion;
later in life one generally prefers flowers.

Their meetings might have continued until Pietro had exhausted every
horse in Lombardy, which would have required several months, had
not Lenz discovered them. He came upon the pair behind
a stone Cupid whose nose had been removed by frost,
and his paternal indignation was so immediate that he did
not pause to calculate whether Cupid's mutilation could be charged
to the guests.

"Tina!"

She sprang up.

"Papa!"

"Pietro!"

The driver remained seated. It was his misfortune to believe
that a tragic attitude compensated for an empty pocket.

"Signor Lenz," he said, "I love your daughter."

Lenz's mouth closed like a cash-box.

"You love my daughter on thirty francs a month?"

"Love is not measured in francs."

"Everything is measured in francs," replied Lenz, who spoke from
experience. "You leave my service tonight."

Pietro rose slowly.

"I shall marry Tina."

"You shall marry the road to Lecco. Your wages will
be paid, less the three francs for the lamp you
broke on Tuesday."

"It was the bay mare."

"Then collect the three francs from the bay mare."

Pietro's eyes became dangerous. He looked at Tina, at Lenz,
and finally at the stone Cupid, who preserved the calm
of one accustomed to domestic scenes.

"If she is not mine," he said, "she shall belong
to no man."

This declaration, which in an opera would have brought down
the curtain amid applause, merely caused Lenz to summon two
porters.

Pietro departed before midnight. He did not take the road
to Lecco. He remained in the neighbourhood, sleeping in haylofts,
eating at humble inns where they knew how to respect
passion when accompanied by cash, and meeting Tina whenever she
could escape from the hotel.

For a fortnight the romance flourished. Pietro spoke of flight,
blood, vengeance and horses. He compared Tina's hair to the
mane of a black Arab stallion, her eyes to those
of a frightened filly, and her light step to that
of a chestnut mare who had once thrown a Russian
prince into a ditch near Menaggio.

With deep regret it must be here admitted that Tina
began to find this monotonous.

A lover may possess the profile of a Roman emperor
and the heart of a volcano; but if, after supper,
he can discuss nothing except fetlocks, harness and the proper
treatment of spavin, a thoughtful woman will eventually remember that
there are other men in the world. Tina did not
cease to like Pietro. She merely ceased to await his
arrival with breathless impatience, and on two occasions forgot to
come at all.

Then Walter Standish reached the Hotel Paradiso.

Standish was an English artist of thirty-one, moderately celebrated, moderately
wealthy, and immoderately certain that his watercolours had revealed Italy
to the Italians. He had painted Venice in rain, Florence
at sunrise, Rome after lunch, and a great many peasants
who had been compelled to remain picturesque for sixpence an
hour. His work was admired by ladies with drawing-rooms and
purchased by gentlemen with daughters.

He arrived with two trunks, three easels, a portfolio, a
folding stool, and a conviction that the room reserved for
him must be unsuitable.

"The southern balcony has the finer prospect," said Lenz.

"Then why have you given me the northern?"

"The northern room is quieter."

"I prefer the view."

"The southern room costs five francs more."

"I prefer quiet," said Standish.

Thus mutual respect was established.

On the morning after his arrival he carried his paint-box
to the terrace and beheld Tina leaning over the marble
balustrade, feeding crumbs to a bird which had no intention
of paying for them. The lake behind her was a
sheet of blue silk; the mountains dissolved into pearl-coloured distance;
a rose had fallen against her black hair. Standish forgot
the landscape, which was the highest compliment he had ever
paid a human being.

"Please do not move," he said.

Tina turned.

"What?"

"Non muovete—no, that is plural or imperative or possibly insulting.
I wish to paint you."

"I do not speak English."

Standish placed his hand on his heart, pointed to Tina,
then to the sketchbook, and smiled. He had often found
this method successful in foreign countries, particularly when the foreigner
happened to be young and pretty.

Tina understood at once. She also understood that his coat
had been made in London, that his boots were expensive,
and that her father had bowed to him twice before
breakfast.

"You make picture?"

"Yes. Picture. Bella—beautiful."

"Beautiful is bella?"

"It is now," said Standish.

She laughed and resumed her place at the balustrade.

The first sketch took forty minutes. The second occupied the
whole afternoon. By the end of the week Standish had
painted Tina feeding birds, Tina carrying roses, Tina beneath an
archway, Tina beside the fountain, and Tina looking across the
lake with an expression of yearning which was directed in
fact toward the kitchen, luncheon being late.

In return, he taught her English. She taught him Italian.
His progress was artistic rather than grammatical, while hers was
alarmingly rapid.

"I love the lake," he said one evening.

"I love the lake," she repeated.

"I love the mountains."

"I love the mountains."

"I love Italy."

"I love England," she replied, watching him.

This was not the lesson as arranged, but Standish found
no fault with it.

Lenz observed the courtship with the vigilant benevolence of a
banker examining a security. He learned that Standish had inherited
a comfortable income from an uncle in Yorkshire, and from
that hour referred to him as "the distinguished English master."
The cost of Standish's room was not increased, because Lenz
felt that there are moments when avarice should give place
to diplomacy; the price of his wine, however, advanced almost
imperceptibly.

Tina's lessons with Pietro now became extremely inconvenient. She avoided
the lemon garden, sent excuses through a kitchen-maid, and once
pleaded a headache while sitting for Standish in a white
dress on the upper terrace.

At last Pietro stopped her on the path behind the
chapel.

"You do not come."

"I have duties."

"You sit for the Englishman."

"He pays my father."

"He looks at you."

"He is an artist. Artists look. It is their business."

"He loves you."

Tina examined a lace handkerchief. "Englishmen do not love so
quickly."

"Then why did he carry your shawl yesterday?"

"It was heavy."

"It was lace!"

"Englishmen are strong."

Pietro seized her wrist.

"You are mine."

"Do not hurt me."

"I have left my work, my home, everything, for you."

"You were dismissed."

The distinction was accurate but unkind. Pietro's face whitened.

"You laugh at me."

"No, Pietro. But you are always angry, and you speak
only of death and horses. Signor Standish speaks of London,
Paris, pictures, theatres—"

"I will cut his heart out."

"There! You begin again."

She freed herself and hurried away.

Pietro watched her until she vanished among the chestnuts. Then
he drew from beneath his coat a narrow stiletto, regarded
its polished blade, and replaced it. He was not a
subtle man. In his philosophy a horse was controlled by
reins, a rival by steel, and a woman by threats.
All three principles were shortly to prove defective.

Standish meanwhile had fallen genuinely in love. We are obliged
to say genuinely because the fact sounds unlikely when connected
with watercolours, foreign travel, and a hotelkeeper's daughter. Yet he
loved Tina's laughter, her swift intelligence, her graceful movements, and
even those little silences with which she concealed whatever might
be troublesome. He imagined taking her to England and installing
her in a small Surrey house which, under the modifying
influence of love, became an ancestral palace with terraces, cedars
and no mortgage.

One sunset, while Como lay in purple shadow and the
peaks beyond Bellagio shone like the gates of a celestial
city, Standish proposed marriage beside the leaking fountain.

"Tina, I have painted you eleven times."

"Yes."

"I have learned nearly two hundred Italian words."

"Some of them correctly."

"I have remained at this hotel six weeks."

"That has been expensive."

"It has been worth it. I love you."

Tina lowered her eyes. She had expected the statement, but
expectation does not spoil a proposal if the proposer is
acceptable.

"You love me very much?"

"So much that I am prepared to speak to your
father."

This was an English declaration, and therefore more serious than
suicide.

Tina held out both hands.

"I love you also, Walter."

Standish kissed her. The fountain dripped, the evening bells rang
across the water, and Lenz, observing the scene through the
dining-room window, ordered the best champagne to be put upon
Standish's bill.

Tina said nothing about Pietro. She intended to do so
at some suitable moment, but suitable moments are timid creatures
and rarely appear when a confession is waiting. Besides, the
affair was over; Pietro had been foolish; Walter might misunderstand;
her father would shout; and history, when buried, is generally
considered improved.

Three nights later Standish was returning from a sketching expedition.
He had remained above the village until darkness because the
final light on the mountains was exceptionally fine and because
artists, though otherwise mortal, believe supper can be delayed for
atmosphere.

The path entered a grove of olives below the hotel.
Standish heard a step behind him and turned.

A man sprang from the shadow.

The stiletto flashed once. Standish, who was carrying a folded
easel beneath his arm, thrust it forward by instinct. The
blade struck the wooden crosspiece, passed through the canvas strap,
and stopped within two inches of his waistcoat.

"Confound you!" cried Standish.

This was not eloquent, but Englishmen in emergencies commonly sacrifice
poetry to precision.

He swung the easel. Its brass-bound leg caught Pietro across
the temple, and Pietro fell like a sack of flour,
though considerably better shaped. Standish stumbled backward against the wall.
The point of the knife had grazed his side, and
blood was beginning to stain his shirt.

Guests came running from the hotel: Lenz in slippers, Tina
in a pale wrapper, two German professors, a French widow,
three servants, and an American tourist who had been hoping
for an incident since Naples.

"My son!" shouted Lenz, already anticipating legal complications. "What has
happened?"

"This fellow tried to stick me."

A lantern was held over Pietro's face.

Tina saw him and gave a little cry. For one
dangerous second her hand went to her heart; then her
intelligence, which in moments of crisis moved faster than most
people's honesty, took command.

"I do not know him," she said.

Pietro's eyelids stirred.

"You have never seen him?" asked Standish.

"Never. Perhaps he mistook you for someone else."

"That must be it," cried Lenz. "A mistake! An unfortunate
mountain mistake."

The distinction pleased him because deliberate murders injure a hotel's
reputation, while mistakes may happen anywhere.

Pietro opened his eyes and fixed them on Tina.

"Tina—"

She bent swiftly over Standish.

"He is fainting! Walter, do not move. Papa, send for
the doctor."

The servants lifted Standish. In the confusion Pietro struggled upright,
struck away the American's restraining hand, leaped over the low
wall, and disappeared among the olives.

"After him!" cried Standish.

"You are wounded," said Tina, clinging to him. "You may
die."

The possibility diverted his attention. By the time the porters
had found courage and lanterns, Pietro was beyond pursuit.

Standish's wound was painful but not dangerous. Nevertheless he remained
in bed for twelve days, partly by the physician's direction
and partly because Tina nursed him with such tenderness that
recovery appeared a form of ingratitude. She cooled his forehead,
read English novels aloud with unexpected interpretations, and forbade discussion
of the attack.

"But the fellow knew your name," Standish said.

"He heard Papa call me."

"He spoke first."

"You had lost blood."

"Not enough to make me deaf."

"Walter, you excite yourself."

She placed two fingers on his lips, and the investigation
ended where many masculine investigations have ended before it.

Pietro fled northward, crossing ridges, sleeping in shepherds' huts, and
descending at last into the Engadine. His love, having been
refused, became a vendetta; and a vendetta is merely love
stripped of good manners. News travelled after him. In a
wine-shop near Pontresina he learned from a driver that Tina
Lenz was to marry the English painter and that the
bridal pair intended to cross the Stelvio on their journey
toward Austria.

Pietro said nothing. He bought wine, left it untasted, and
went out to look at the mountains.

The wedding took place at the little church above the
lake. Tina wore white; Standish wore a coat which made
him uncomfortable; Lenz wept loudly and charged the wedding guests
for luncheon. The French widow declared that Tina resembled a
princess, the German professors disputed the architecture of the church,
and the American photographed everybody while the priest was still
speaking.

Standish's carriage stood before the hotel the next morning, piled
with valises, paint-boxes, hat-cases, rugs and a square leather trunk
which Tina insisted could not be left behind because it
contained things she might need. What these things were no
husband has ever discovered, but civilisation rests upon its luggage.

Their route was to lead by the Engadine and the
Stelvio into the Tyrol. Lenz embraced Tina, embraced Standish, and
examined the straps.

"The road is excellent," he said. "Except in places."

"What places?" asked Standish.

"The dangerous ones. But Giuseppe is my best driver."

Giuseppe, a grey-moustached man of stable habits, touched his hat
from the box.

"The horses are safe?" Standish asked.

"Safe as the Bank of England," said Lenz, who had
never visited that institution but respected its reputation.

They drove away beneath showers of flowers and account-books. Tina
leaned from the carriage, laughing and crying, until the Hotel
Paradiso vanished behind the chestnuts.

For three days all went well. The weather was brilliant;
the roads climbed through pine forests and descended beside torrents;
the peaks rose white and solemn against a sky of
incredible blue. Standish sketched whenever the horses rested, and Tina
discovered that marriage, though less dramatic than secret meetings in
a lemon grove, provided better meals.

At St. Moritz Giuseppe received news that his sister was
ill at Sondrio. At least, a boy brought him a
message to that effect. Giuseppe was devoted to his sister,
though she had in fact been dead for seven years,
a detail of which the boy had not been informed.

A substitute driver presented himself—broad hat, heavy cloak, face darkened
by dust, a scarf drawn high around the mouth. He
carried a letter apparently signed by Lenz's agent.

"My name is Paolo," he said. "I know the Stelvio."

Standish glanced at the letter.

"You are recommended."

"I have driven the pass twenty times."

This was approximately true. Pietro had driven it twenty-three times.

Tina looked at him from the hotel door. The scarf,
the shadow of the hat and the altered voice deceived
her; moreover, happiness is a poor detective. They set out
at dawn.

The road mounted steadily. Forests dropped below them. The air
sharpened; grass gave place to rock; waterfalls leaped in silver
threads down black precipices. Above towered the Ortler, white and
enormous, while glaciers hung between the peaks like frozen seas
arrested in the act of falling.

Standish was delighted.

"Magnificent!" he cried. "Look at that light on the snow."

"I am looking at the road," said Tina. "It is
narrower."

"It is perfectly safe."

There are few phrases more frequently punished by Providence.

At the summit they stopped at the inn near the
crest of the Stelvio, where travellers may contemplate eternity while
paying two francs for coffee. The wind blew bitterly. Standish
buttoned his coat; Tina wrapped herself in furs; Pietro remained
apart beside the horses.

The descent began toward Franzenshöhe.

At first the carriage moved cautiously through the upper turns.
The road coiled down the barren mountainside in sharp zigzags,
each bend exposing another abyss, another wall of rock, another
glimpse of the valley lying thousands of feet below. The
horses' hoofs rang on the hard surface. The wheels grated
near the edge.

Then Tina saw the driver's left hand.

On the third finger was a silver ring fashioned like
a coiled serpent. She had given it to Pietro at
a village fair.

Her breath stopped.

"Pietro," she whispered.

The driver turned.

The scarf had slipped. His eyes met hers, and in
them she read love, hatred, sleepless nights, injured vanity, and
a complete absence of concern for the return of the
carriage.

Standish heard the name.

"What did you say?"

Pietro laughed. He stood on the box and lashed the
horses.

They plunged forward.

The carriage swung round the next bend with one wheel
almost clear of the road. Tina was thrown against Standish.
Boxes shifted on the roof; a hat-case flew into the
abyss and diminished with great dignity until it struck a
rock and ceased to be useful.

"Stop!" shouted Standish.

Pietro lashed again.

The horses broke into a gallop.

Down they went, the carriage bounding, swaying and roaring between
cliff and precipice. Sparks flew from the wheels. A leather
trunk tore loose, sailed over the parapet and burst upon
the rocks below in a cloud of garments.

"My blue dress!" cried Tina.

Even at such moments the feminine mind preserves its balance.

Standish reached for the door.

"What are you doing?" she screamed.

"Getting at the driver."

"You will be killed!"

"That appears to be his idea."

He forced the door open, but the carriage lurched outward.
Beneath him the mountain fell away into blue emptiness. Ahead,
Pietro bent over the reins with the rigidity of a
man driving not toward a destination but toward a conclusion.

"Pietro!" Tina called. "Stop! I command you!"

"You chose him," he shouted back. "You shall go with
him—and I with you!"

"This is absurd," said Standish.

It must be confessed that the remark was inadequate, but
he had no time to improve it.

The road doubled upon itself around a projecting buttress of
rock. Below the bend, perhaps eighty feet down the slope,
the lower turn ran in the opposite direction. Between the
two lay loose scree, dwarf scrub and a path used
by guides as a short cut.

Standish saw it.

He seized Tina's shoulders.

"Stay low. Hold the side."

"What will you do?"

"Miss the next few corners."

Before she could stop him he jumped.

He struck the road, rolled, lost his hat, and slid
toward the edge. His hand closed upon a wooden post.
The carriage vanished round the bend above, leaving dust, thunder
and Tina's cry.

Standish dragged himself upright. His knees were torn, his coat
split, and one sleeve was decorated with mud. He had
no time to lament any of these disasters, except perhaps
the coat, which had cost nine guineas.

He scrambled down the slope.

Stones broke beneath his boots and sprang into space. Twice
he fell; once he collided with a stunted pine; once
he crossed a patch of old snow upon which dignity
became impossible. Below, the carriage appeared on the lower road,
racing toward the point where his path met it.

He ran, slid, bounded and finally descended in a shower
of gravel.

The horses were almost upon him.

Tina saw him and screamed.

Standish leaped for the carriage. His hands caught the rail
beside the forward door, but his feet swung outward over
the precipice. The wheel struck a stone. He was flung
against the body, lost one hand, recovered it, and dragged
himself onto the narrow footboard.

Pietro looked down.

"Signor Inglese!"

"Yes," panted Standish. "Again."

Pietro dropped the reins into his left hand and drew
the stiletto with his right. The horses, receiving no guidance
except fear, increased their speed.

The blade came down. Standish twisted aside; it struck the
carriage roof. He caught Pietro's wrist.

For several seconds they struggled while the vehicle rushed uncontrolled
toward another turn. Pietro kicked backward. Standish clung to the
rail, hauled himself higher, and struck him under the jaw.

Pietro reeled but did not fall.

"You cannot have her!" he cried.

"I have married her," said Standish. "Do try to keep
up."

Pietro raised the knife once more. Standish, whose patience was
now exhausted, drove his boot squarely into the driver's chest.

Pietro flew from the box, struck the road, rolled twice,
and came to rest against a bank of snow. The
stiletto spun away over the edge.

Standish climbed onto the vacant seat.

The reins trailed between the horses. He leaned down, caught
one, missed the other, and nearly followed Pietro. Tina crawled
through the front window and seized the loose strap.

"Here!"

He grasped it. Together they pulled.

The horses did not stop. They had reached that state
in which horses, men and nations mistake momentum for policy.
Standish wound the reins around his hands and braced both
feet against the iron board.

"Whoa!"

The leaders swerved. One wheel struck the low stone parapet,
climbed it, and hung for an appalling instant over space.
Tina shut her eyes.

Standish hauled left with all his strength. The wheel dropped
back onto the road. The carriage swung broadside, scraped along
the rock wall, and lost another box. Then the exhausted
horses slowed from a gallop to a canter, from a
canter to a trot, and finally stopped in a cloud
of white dust.

For a few seconds nobody spoke.

Far below, cowbells sounded from the valley with pastoral indifference.
Above, the glaciers glittered. A torn silk stocking drifted from
a bush beside the road, having survived Tina's trunk with
greater courage than might have been expected of it.

Standish climbed down. His hands were bleeding, his face scratched,
and his trousers ruined.

"Tina?"

She descended into his arms.

"Walter!"

"You are not hurt?"

"No."

"Thank Heaven."

He held her tightly. Then his expression changed.

"That scoundrel—who is he? You called him Pietro."

Tina had known that this question must come, and, like
a good general, had prepared her defence during the last
two minutes of mortal danger.

"A driver who once worked for Papa."

"The man who stabbed me?"

"Perhaps."

"Why should he wish to kill us?"

"He is mad."

"But why did he say you chose me?"

"Madmen say everything. It is what makes them so difficult."

Standish looked back up the road.

"I am going to fetch him."

Tina caught his sleeve.

"No!"

"He may be injured."

"He tried to murder us."

"That is why I should like a word with him."

"Walter, please. The horses may bolt again. I am terrified.
Do not leave me."

This was tactically excellent. An Englishman may abandon vengeance, justice
and even a satisfactory explanation if informed that a lady
is terrified.

Standish hesitated.

"He deserves to be handed to the police."

"He may already be dead."

"All the more reason—"

Tina swayed and put one hand to her forehead.

"I feel faint."

He caught her.

"My darling!"

Her faint was not complete, since complete unconsciousness at the
edge of a precipice would have been inconvenient; but it
served. Standish lifted her into the carriage, fetched water from
a roadside stream, and forgot Pietro for the immediate present.

Pietro was not dead. He recovered after half an hour
with a bruised body, a damaged dignity and no horse.
Hearing distant voices on the road, he limped across the
slope toward a shepherd's track and disappeared among the rocks.
Whether he later emigrated to America, joined a circus in
Vienna, or became the prosperous proprietor of a stable in
Turin cannot be stated with certainty. A narrative of fact
must draw the line somewhere, though it seldom does so
before exhausting the reader.

Standish repaired the harness with a luggage strap, quieted the
horses, and resumed the descent at a pace which would
have bored a funeral procession. Tina sat close beside him
and held his arm.

At Franzenshöhe they related the adventure to the innkeeper. Standish
described a mad substitute driver; Tina confirmed every detail except
those requiring explanation; and the innkeeper regretted the occurrence profoundly
until he discovered that the rescued couple required rooms, bandages,
supper, new clothing and a man to recover their luggage.
His grief then assumed a more commercial and therefore more
manageable form.

Two days later they continued into the Tyrol. Standish never
learned that Pietro had been Tina's lover, or that the
stiletto in the olive grove had been guided by jealousy
rather than mistaken identity. He believed his wife's account because
he loved her, because she told it prettily, and because
the alternative demanded more intellectual labour.

Their marriage proved happy. Standish painted Tina in London, in
Surrey, in Venice, beside lakes, beneath cedars, and eventually surrounded
by children who inherited her eyes and his confidence. Lenz
visited them once, admired the house, criticised the servants, and
attempted to calculate its weekly value as a hotel.

Tina became an affectionate wife, an accomplished hostess, and a
woman of singular discretion. She never again encouraged a volatile
carriage driver; she never contradicted her husband when he told
the story of the Stelvio; and when he declared that
perfect happiness between man and wife depends upon perfect candour,
she kissed him with such sweetness that he remained convinced
of it to the end of his days.

## The Miller's New Song

Once upon a time, beside a broad and shining river,
there stood a little mill with white walls, a mossy
roof, and a great brown wheel which turned from morning
until night.

"Cree-e-eekety-cruck-crick!" said the wheel.

And while the wheel spoke in its fashion, the miller
sang in his:

> "I care for none, and none for me;
> As free as wind my heart shall be.
> The river runs, the millstones spin—
> I ask no love to enter in."

The miller's name was Matthew. He was not an old
man, nor a sour man, nor a bad man. His
eyes were blue, his shoulders broad, and his laugh as
hearty as the clatter of a wooden pail upon the
stones. He gave fair weight, ground honest meal, and never
turned a hungry traveller from his door.

Yet he would make no friend.

Farmer Dobson often invited him to supper, but Matthew always
answered, "My mill and I are company enough."

The shepherd asked him to join the village feast, but
Matthew shook his floury head.

"Dancing is for folk who need one another," quoth he.
"I need nobody."

Now this was not quite true, though Matthew believed it.
He wished to be loved as all good men do,
but he feared that if he loved others, they might
one day leave him. So he locked his heart before
anyone could enter it, and then complained that the room
was empty.

"Nobody cares for Matthew," he would say.

"Nobody can," Farmer Dobson once replied, "for you shut the
door in everybody's face."

But Matthew only laughed and sang more loudly:

> "I care for none, and none for me!"

One autumn evening, when the sky was grey and the
reeds bent low before the wind, Matthew went outside to
fasten the mill shutters. The river had risen after three
days of rain, and broken branches hurried past upon the
brown water.

"Wild weather!" cried Matthew. "A good night for every sensible
creature to be indoors."

Just then he saw something turning in an eddy below
the willow trees. At first he thought it was a
log. Then the thing struck a stone, swung around, and
came floating toward the mill.

It was a box.

Matthew ran along the bank. He caught the box with
a flour-sack hook and dragged it from the water. It
was made of dark wood, and a little brass star
was fixed upon its lid. From within came a soft,
strange sound.

"Mew—mew—mew!"

Matthew stepped back.

"Surely," said he, "no kitten would voyage in such a
grand boat."

He lifted the lid.

And behold! Wrapped in a woollen shawl lay a baby
girl, rosy with cold and waving two tiny fists at
the storm as though she meant to scold it.

For a moment Matthew stared. The baby stared back. Then
she caught one of his fingers and held it with
her little dimpled hand.

The miller's heart, which had resisted farmers, shepherds, feasts, and
friendly neighbours, yielded at once to that small grasp.

"You poor little waif," he whispered. "Who sent you down
the river?"

The baby sneezed.

"Well," said Matthew, "that is no answer."

He carried the box indoors, warmed a blanket by the
fire, and fed the child milk from the corner of
a clean linen cloth. He did everything awkwardly, but everything
tenderly. When she cried, he walked round and round the
kitchen, while the great wheel outside murmured more softly:

"Cree-eek—cruck—crick."

At last the child slept against his shoulder, and Matthew
dared not move for fear of waking her. He sat
there until the candle burned low, gazing at her small
face.

In the morning he went to the village and asked
whether any child had been lost. No one had heard
of one. Messengers went upriver and downriver, but they returned
without news. There was no name in the box and
no token except the little brass star.

"What will you do with her?" asked Farmer Dobson.

"What should I do?" answered Matthew. "She has come to
me, and she shall stay with me."

The farmer smiled. "Then you care for somebody at last."

"Nonsense," said Matthew, though his ears turned red. "A baby
must be looked after. That is all."

He named her Nathalie, because he had once heard that
name in a song and thought it beautiful.

From that day onward the mill became a different place.
A cradle stood beside the sacks of meal. Little garments
fluttered upon a line where onions had formerly hung, and
Matthew learned to warm milk, tie ribbons, and sing lullabies.
His lullabies had neither proper tune nor sensible words, but
Nathalie liked them. She would lie in his arms making
soft little noises, and sometimes she seized his brown whiskers
and pulled them.

"Mercy!" Matthew would cry. "Is this how you repay your
father?"

Yet he laughed, and Nathalie laughed too.

He called himself her father without noticing when he had
first begun to do so.

Year after year passed pleasantly. Nathalie learned to sit upon
a flour bin, then to crawl across the kitchen, then
to walk by holding to Matthew's thumb. Soon she followed
him everywhere. She scattered grain for the hens, carried tiny
measures of corn, and waved to customers from the mill
door.

But habit is a stubborn guest, and Matthew still sang
his old song while he worked:

> "I care for none, and none for me;
> As free as wind my heart shall be."

When Nathalie was very small, the words meant nothing to
her. But one spring morning, when she was seven years
old, she sat plaiting daisies while Matthew poured grain into
the hopper.

The wheel cried, "Cree-e-eekety-cruck-crick!"

Matthew sang, "I care for none, and none for me!"

Nathalie's hands became still.

"Father," she said, "is that true?"

"Is what true, little bird?"

"That nobody cares for you."

Matthew looked down and saw tears upon her cheeks.

"Why, what troubles you?"

"I care for you," she said. "I love you more
than the river and the daisies and warm bread together.
And you care for me, do you not?"

Do you think the miller was angry? Not at all.
He was ashamed, for children sometimes show us truths which
pride has hidden.

Matthew lifted Nathalie into his arms.

"Yes," he said. "I care for you beyond anything in
the world."

"Then your song tells a falsehood."

"So it does."

He wiped away her tears and, after thinking deeply, began
another song:

> "One heart loves me, and I love one;
> So bright my days, so warm my sun.
> Turn, merry wheel, and sing with me—
> No richer man could ever be!"

Nathalie clapped her hands, and thereafter they sang it together.

The years passed, as years will, though we often wish
them to hurry when we are sad and to linger
when we are happy. The little girl grew into a
maiden. Her silken ringlets shone like dark honey, her eyes
were clear and kind, and her voice was sweeter than
the blackbird's in the hawthorn.

The country folk loved her, for beauty pleases the eye,
but goodness makes a home in the heart.

Across the fields stood a great house belonging to a
young Squire named Edmund. He was neither proud nor idle,
as some Squires in tales are apt to be. He
knew the names of his tenants, rode gently through planted
fields, and once spent a rainy afternoon helping Matthew repair
the mill race.

When Edmund first saw Nathalie, she was carrying a basket
of bread to a sick shepherd. When Nathalie first noticed
Edmund, he was standing in mud up to his knees,
rescuing a lamb from a ditch. Thus each saw the
other in an act of kindness, which is a better
beginning for love than fine clothes or clever compliments.

After that, Edmund often found reason to pass the mill.

Sometimes he brought a book for Nathalie. Sometimes she showed
him the shady lanes beside the river. They walked beneath
the willows and talked of simple matters, yet simple words
may carry deep affection when two hearts understand them.

The neighbours observed everything, of course, for country folk seldom
miss a courtship.

"The Squire pays court to your Nathalie," Farmer Dobson told
Matthew.

"He comes to discuss the river," said the miller.

"Then the river has learned to blush," answered Dobson, "for
Nathalie does so whenever his name is spoken."

Matthew began to watch them anxiously. He liked Edmund, but
fear crept back into his heart. If Nathalie married, who
would sit with him beside the fire? Who would sing
above the millstones? The rooms would be empty, and the
old loneliness would return worse than before.

At last Edmund came to him.

"Master Matthew," he said, "I love Nathalie, and she loves
me. With your consent, I wish to make her my
wife."

"No," said Matthew at once.

Edmund turned pale, but bowed courteously. "Have I offended you?"

"No."

"Do you think me unworthy?"

"No."

"Then why may I not marry her?"

"Because she is all I have," Matthew replied.

That evening Nathalie found her father sitting beside the silent
wheel.

"Do you love Edmund?" he asked.

"With all my heart," she answered softly.

"And would refusing him make you unhappy?"

"Yes, Father. But I will not leave you against your
will."

Then Matthew understood that she was ready to sacrifice her
happiness for his, and he knew that if he accepted
that sacrifice, his love would become selfishness.

The next morning he called Edmund back.

"Take her," said Matthew, with a heavy heart, "but cherish
her. She came to me upon the river with nothing
but a shawl and a brass star, and she is
more precious than any treasure in your great house."

"I shall cherish her all my life," said Edmund.

So Nathalie and the Squire were married. The village bells
rang, tables were spread beneath the orchard trees, and Matthew
danced until his boots were dusty. He laughed loudly so
that no one would guess how he dreaded the coming
night.

At sunset the bride and bridegroom rode away to the
great house. Matthew returned alone.

The kitchen seemed enormous. Nathalie's chair stood empty, and her
blue cup remained upon the shelf. The miller lit one
candle, though formerly he had always lit two.

Next morning he set the wheel turning.

"Cree-e-eekety-cruck-crick!" it cried.

And Matthew, feeling abandoned, began his ancient song:

> "I care for none, and none for me;
> As free as wind my heart shall be."

But before he reached the final line, a clear voice
behind him said, "Father, that song is false again."

He turned.

There stood Nathalie in a bright cloak, holding a basket
upon her arm.

"You came back!"

"Certainly," she said, kissing his floury cheek. "Did you suppose
marriage would make me forget you?"

"But you have Edmund now."

"Yes, and I love him. But love is not a
loaf which grows smaller when it is shared. My love
for Edmund does not take away my love for you."

"Will you truly visit me?"

"Every day that I can. And on days when I
cannot, you shall visit us."

She unpacked the basket, and they ate breakfast together as
they had done for years.

Nathalie kept her promise. Sometimes she came alone, sometimes with
Edmund, and in time she brought children who climbed upon
the flour bins and pulled Matthew's grey whiskers just as
she had once pulled the brown ones.

And the miller learned his last and happiest song:

> "The heart that gives has more to spend;
> A child, a neighbour, love, a friend.
> Turn, merry wheel, beside the river—
> Love shared is love that lasts forever."

So the wheel turned, and the river ran, and Matthew
lived contentedly for many years.

And if ever you feel that nobody cares for you,
remember the miller. Open the door of your heart before
you complain that no guest enters, for love does not
leave us poorer when it is given away. Like meal
from honest grain, it feeds more people than one pair
of hands can count.

When Mrs. Lavinia Pottle went after a thousand dollars, she
wore her best hat. It had a purple wing on
one side and an optimistic buckle on the other, and
was generally reserved for funerals, weddings, and assaults upon capital.

Felix Winterbottom received her in the office behind his hardware
store. He was a lean, gray man with eyebrows like
two strips of winter felt. Granville said he had made
his money by squeezing nickels until the buffaloes bellowed.

"Mr. Winterbottom," began Mrs. Pottle, expanding beneath the purple wing,
"I have come in the name of helpless childhood."

"Whose?"

"All childhood. The future citizens of Granville."

"Got enough citizens now."

"It is the day nursery," she said. "You know the
Browning-Tagore Club has undertaken—"

"I know."

"We need one thousand dollars."

"So do I."

She smiled patiently. "You misunderstand. We are asking you to
give it."

"No."

Mrs. Pottle had met refusals before. She possessed a tear
of unusual civic efficiency. It was not a loose, watery
tear, but a compact one with a visible nucleus, and
she now advanced it to the corner of her right
eye.

"The babies," she murmured.

"No."

"The tired mothers!"

"No."

"The higher community spirit!"

"No."

The tear retired for repairs.

"But, Mr. Winterbottom, Mrs. Thaddeus Gulick is honorary president of
the undertaking. Mrs. Mortimer Gulick is chairman of distinguished patronesses,
and Miss Imogene Gulick—"

"That settles it," said Winterbottom.

"Settles what?"

"Not a red cent."

Mrs. Pottle stiffened. "The Gulicks are among Granville's oldest and
most prominent families."

"They've been telling me so forty years."

"Their ancestor founded this town."

"He got here early. That isn't the same thing."

"Mr. Winterbottom!"

"My father came late," said Felix. "He came late because
old Abner Gulick sold him a farm that was three
feet under the river every April. The Gulicks called it
bottom land. Pa called it something else."

"One cannot hold hereditary resentment against helpless infants."

"I don't. I hold my money."

Mrs. Pottle rose, majestic but crimson.

"Granville," she said, "will learn that public spirit does not
depend upon one man's checkbook."

"Hope it learns quick."

"I shall raise the thousand dollars myself."

"Good."

"And when we succeed, Mr. Winterbottom, I trust you will
remember this moment."

"I always remember money I don't spend."

She swept out. The purple wing struck the doorframe, but
she preserved the motion as though civic dignity habitually molted
on thresholds.

At home, Ambrose Pottle was in the Morris chair with
his shoes off and the evening paper spread over his
stummick. Ambrose sold insurance, owned two blue suits, and had
learned during eleven years of marriage that silence was not
a defense but merely an interval during which Lavinia loaded.

"We are going to present a historical pageant," she announced.

Ambrose lowered the paper an inch.

"Who is?"

"Granville."

"Tell Granville I'm busy."

"A magnificent outdoor drama depicting two hundred years of progress.
All the clubs, churches, schools, lodges, patriotic orders, and civic
bodies will unite. We shall rent the opera house if
the weather is threatening. The proceeds will endow the day
nursery."

Ambrose lowered the paper another inch.

"How much will this magnificent cheap way of raising a
thousand dollars cost?"

"Practically nothing."

"Then it'll cost two thousand."

"Ambrose!"

"Costumes?"

"Cheese-cloth."

"Scenery?"

"Voluntary."

"Music?"

"Local talent."

"Horse?"

"What horse?"

"There's always a horse in these uplift circuses."

"It is not a circus. It is The Spirit of
Granville: A Pageant of Two Centuries."

"Then there'll be two horses."

Mrs. Pottle removed her gloves one finger at a time.
"I have been counting on you for treasurer."

"Count again."

"And for the central symbolic figure."

Ambrose sat up. "What figure?"

"The Spirit of History."

"No."

"You would wear a flowing robe."

"No."

"And carry the Lamp of Truth."

"Not in a nightshirt, I won't."

"It will be classical."

"My knees aren't classical."

At forty-eight, Ambrose's knees were round, modest, and inclined to
blush like spring rosebuds when exposed. He guarded them as
another man might guard a family disgrace.

"Very well," said Mrs. Pottle. "I will ask Mr. Crump."

"Ask him."

"He has beautiful legs."

"Then history can walk around on Crump."

Mrs. Pottle looked toward the portrait of her first husband,
Wilbur, who had been removed from terrestrial argument by pneumonia
and thereafter won every domestic debate.

"Wilbur," she sighed, "never refused to help me in cultural
work."

"Wilbur died before he got the chance."

"He understood my ideals."

"He understood enough to get pneumonia."

The tear returned, larger and carrying reinforcements.

Ambrose folded his paper.

"Oh, put me down," he said. "Treasurer and Spirit of
Whatever. But if this thing costs more than it takes
in, I'm going to rear up."

"You are a dear."

"I'm a goat."

"You may wear trousers under the robe."

"Then I'm a goat in trousers."

Within three days Mrs. Pottle had organized the Executive Pageant
Committee, the Advisory Pageant Council, the Historical Accuracy Board, the
Committee on Costumes, the Committee on Tickets, the Committee on
Group Movement, and a special Subcommittee on Feathers. By the
fourth day every woman in Granville had a title except
Mrs. Runkle, who had been absent with neuralgia and consequently
became chairman of Publicity.

The first general meeting was held in the Browning-Tagore Club
rooms, above Doolittle's Undertaking Establishment, where Culture enjoyed a reduced
rent because nobody wanted to climb the stairs.

Mrs. Pottle stood at the front beside a blackboard.

"Our pageant," she declared, "will arise in seven splendid episodes.
First, Granville Before the White Man. Second, The Coming of
the Pioneer. Third, The Struggle with the Savage. Fourth, The
Birth of Commerce. Fifth, Granville in the Nation's Wars. Sixth,
Education, Art, and Woman's Widening Sphere. Seventh, The Triumphant Present."

Mrs. Thaddeus Gulick raised a lorgnette.

"Where does Joshua Gulick enter?"

"In the second episode."

"He should enter in the first."

"He was not here before the white man."

"He might be foreshadowed."

Mrs. Mortimer Gulick nodded. "A Voice could speak from behind
the hill."

Miss Imogene Gulick, who wrote verse in a green fountain
pen, fluttered several sheets.

"I have already composed the passage:

"Ere the pale-face smote the wildwood,
Ere the cabin pierced the gloom,
Destiny beheld a Gulick
Advancing through the womb—"

Mrs. Pottle blinked.

"Perhaps 'mist,'" she suggested.

"'Womb' rhymes with 'gloom.'"

"The rhyme may not be worth it," said Ambrose from
the rear, where he had been adding columns.

Three Gulick heads turned. Collectively they looked like a tribunal
of well-fed eagles.

Mrs. Thaddeus said, "Joshua Gulick founded Granville in 1724."

"According to the courthouse records," said Ambrose, "a trapper named
Eli Spavins built a shack here in 1719."

"A shack is not a town."

"Joshua built a tavern."

"That," said Mrs. Thaddeus, "was the social nucleus."

"The records say it was closed twice."

Mrs. Pottle struck the blackboard gently with a pointer.

"Facts," she said, "must never be permitted to obscure the
larger truth."

"That's what I'm afraid of," said Ambrose.

The Gulicks continued. Joshua must enter on a gallant stallion.
His wife, Prudence, must distribute corn to starving settlers. Their
son Ebenezer must repel an Indian attack. Captain Lemuel Gulick
must lead Granville's Revolutionary contingent. Judge Peleg Gulick must welcome
Lafayette, whether Lafayette had visited Granville or not. General Horace
Gulick must appear in the Civil War tableau, and Miss
Imogene herself would represent the Spirit of Culture in the
concluding apotheosis.

"On what?" asked Ambrose.

"On a pedestal," said Imogene.

"I meant what did General Horace do?"

"He offered his services."

"Were they accepted?"

"That is immaterial."

By adjournment, the seven episodes had become eleven, the cast
had reached two hundred and eighteen, and Granville's history had
been rearranged until it resembled a long family driveway leading
to the Gulick front porch.

The difficulties began next morning.

Mrs. Crump refused to let her twins represent onions in
the Agricultural Dance.

"They were violets at the church cantata," she said, "and
they ain't going backward."

"Onions are fundamental," Mrs. Pottle explained.

"Not to my children."

Mrs. Doolittle demanded that her daughter be transferred from Second
Factory Girl to Colonial Belle because the Doolittles had a
silver teapot.

The hook-and-ladder company volunteered to portray Washington's army on condition
that it might bring the truck. The Sons of Veterans
refused to march behind them. The high-school principal wanted thirty-seven
pupils introduced as The Awakening Mind, while the superintendent insisted
that no mind in Granville had awakened without authority from
his office.

There was trouble over the Indians.

"We must have twelve," said Mrs. Pottle.

"You can get six men from the gas works," said
Ambrose. "Paint 'em."

"They have mustaches."

"Indians had hair."

"Not there."

At last Mr. Runkle, chairman of Properties and Real Estate,
hired a traveling medicine-show troupe stranded in Plummer's Junction. They
advertised themselves as Chief Roaring Elk and His Five Full-Blooded
Sons of the Forest, though two spoke with an Italian
accent and the youngest answered naturally to Clarence.

"They demand a chicken supper," Runkle reported.

"They shall have sandwiches," said Mrs. Pottle.

"They say chicken."

"Sandwiches are more historical."

"They say if there ain't chicken they go on the
warpath."

"Book the chicken," said Ambrose. "I'm treasurer of an orphan
asylum for healthy actors."

Then came the horse.

Mrs. Thaddeus Gulick desired a milk-white charger for Mr. Thaddeus
Gulick, who was to impersonate Joshua. Granville possessed only one
white horse, an elderly animal named Pearl, belonging to Swoboda's
Dairy.

"She is perfectly safe," said Mrs. Pottle.

"She stops at every house where she's ever delivered milk,"
said Ambrose.

"She will be inspired by the occasion."

"She's been inspired by oats for twenty-three years."

Pearl was engaged for five dollars and all the hay
she could remember.

Meanwhile, Imogene's pageant text expanded like yeast. Messengers brought new
scenes, revisions, chants, apostrophes, invocations, and one allegorical passage in
which Granville addressed the Erie Canal, although the Erie Canal,
maintaining geographical reserve, had never come within eighty miles of
Granville.

Ambrose, as Spirit of History, was given seventy-four lines.

"I won't learn 'em."

"You must."

"I can say, 'Years passed.' That covers history."

"You have to bind the episodes together."

"Use string."

The costume committee draped him in gray cheese-cloth, silver stars,
sandals, and a crown resembling a dismantled colander. Beneath the
robe he wore his oldest trousers, rolled above the ankles.
His knees emerged, pink and startled.

Mrs. Pottle clasped her hands. "You look noble."

"I look ventilated."

For six weeks Granville rehearsed. It rehearsed in parlors, barns,
schoolrooms, vacant lots, and the opera house. Babies were abandoned
in committee meetings so their mothers could rehearse for the
nursery intended to care for babies. Husbands ate canned salmon.
Children went to bed painted blue. The town smelled of
glue, greasepaint, damp cheese-cloth, and chicken-supper negotiations.

Ambrose spent his evenings at the courthouse checking dates for
the printed program. He came home one night with dust
on his sleeves and a dangerous serenity in his eye.

"Lavinia," he said, "do you know who really fought off
the Indians in 1741?"

"Ebenezer Gulick."

"No. Ebenezer hid in a cider barrel. A woman named
Goody Spavins fired from the blockhouse roof."

"That cannot be used."

"Why?"

"The part has been assigned."

He drew out another paper.

"And Captain Lemuel Gulick didn't lead a company in the
Revolution. He sold them spoiled pork."

"Ambrose!"

"At patriotic prices."

"Those records may be malicious."

"They're court records. He was fined twelve pounds."

Mrs. Pottle began arranging cushions with feverish culture.

"And Judge Peleg didn't welcome Lafayette," Ambrose continued. "He welcomed
a French umbrella drummer named Lafitte and made a speech
before anybody corrected him."

"History contains many conflicting interpretations."

"Here's another. General Horace Gulick wasn't a general. He was
elected secretary of the Granville Gooseberry Association."

"He was called General as a mark of affection."

"So's General Delivery."

She faced him. "The pageant is not a criminal trial.
It is an artistic presentation of civic continuity."

"It's a Gulick minstrel show."

"They have influence."

"They've had all the influence, all the best parts, all
the complimentary tickets, and the only horse."

"Their cooperation is indispensable."

"So's their chicken supper, I suppose."

"Ambrose, promise me you will read your lines exactly as
written."

He stared at the Lamp of Truth, which lay on
the table awaiting a new battery.

"I'll read 'em," he said. "Unless I rear up."

"You must not rear up."

"Then keep 'em off my knees."

On the night of the pageant the Granville Opera House
was packed from orchestra pit to ventilator. People came from
fourteen miles away. Felix Winterbottom occupied an aisle seat in
the sixth row, having bought his ticket at the last
possible minute and at the child's price because Mrs. Runkle
had printed no age restriction.

The orchestra began "Poet and Peasant." The violins and trombones
became separated in the first passage; the cornet passed both,
and finished half a page ahead, flushed but victorious.

The curtain rose upon Granville Before the White Man.

Six Indians crouched around an electric campfire. Chief Roaring Elk
wore a war bonnet, football stockings, and tan shoes. Clarence,
forgetting the primeval solemnity of the scene, waved to a
girl in the balcony.

Mrs. Pottle, directing from the wings, hissed, "Commune with Nature!"

Clarence picked up a papier-mâché fish and communed with that.

Ambrose entered as the Spirit of History, holding his Lamp.
One sandal caught in the robe. He traveled eight feet
with the slow grandeur of a man falling downstairs in
a dream, recovered himself beside a pasteboard oak, and raised
his arm.

"Before the white man came," he proclaimed, "Granville was inhabited
chiefly by Indians, mosquitoes, and—"

Mrs. Pottle hissed furiously.

"—and silence," he concluded.

The Indians began their dance. The pianist played "Hearts and
Flowers" by mistake, giving the massacre a tender, retrospective quality.

Episode Two disclosed a painted forest and Pearl, the milk-white
charger. Mr. Thaddeus Gulick entered as Joshua in high boots,
plume, cloak, sword, and an expression of hereditary mortgage.

Pearl walked to the footlights, turned left, and stopped before
Dr. Beemer's box, where she had delivered milk for eleven
years.

"Onward!" whispered Mr. Gulick, kicking.

Pearl waited for the empty bottles.

"Onward, gallant steed!" cried Imogene from the wings.

Pearl sighed with her entire framework and backed into the
Spirit of History.

Ambrose's Lamp went out.

The audience applauded.

Prudence Gulick entered carrying a basket of rubber corn. Mrs.
Mortimer Gulick, who played Prudence, forgot her speech and distributed
the corn in silence, giving four ears to the minister
and striking Felix Winterbottom on the shoulder with a fifth.

Then came the Struggle with the Savage.

Chief Roaring Elk's sons rushed upon the settlement. Mr. Arthur
Gulick, portraying Ebenezer, was to fire a blank pistol and
cry, "Thus Granville answers tyranny!"

The pistol failed.

"Thus Granville answers tyranny!" he cried, pulling twice more.

The pistol discharged into Pearl's immediate neighborhood.

Pearl, who had tolerated civic uplift but drew the line
at artillery, surged across the stage, carrying Joshua through the
painted forest, over a bench, and into Education, Art, and
Woman's Widening Sphere, which was waiting in the next set.

The Awakening Mind screamed. Three Colonial Belles climbed a factory.
Spirit of Culture fell from her pedestal into a tub
representing Inland Commerce.

Chief Roaring Elk removed his bonnet.

"This wasn't in the contract," he said distinctly.

From the wings came Mrs. Pottle's voice: "Continue! Continue!"

They continued.

The Revolution occurred before the founding of the school system,
the Civil War entered during Lafayette's visit, and sixteen onions,
led by the Crump twins dressed defiantly as violets, crossed
the stage during the signing of Granville's charter.

Through it all Ambrose appeared between episodes, gray robe torn,
crown sideways, knees blazing, and Lamp of Truth intermittently flickering.
He spoke his lines with mounting compression.

"Years passed."

"Commerce grew."

"Granville prospered."

"The Gulicks remained."

At last came the Grand Historical Tableau.

The entire Gulick family assembled on a golden platform beneath
a banner reading TWO CENTURIES OF SERVICE. Joshua held the
town charter. Prudence held the corn. Ebenezer held the smoking
pistol at arm's length. Captain Lemuel wore a Revolutionary uniform.
Judge Peleg extended a hand toward an absent Lafayette. General
Horace brandished a Civil War sword, and Imogene, wringing wet
from Inland Commerce, stood as the Spirit of Culture with
pond lilies attached to her bosom.

Ambrose entered for the final narration.

Mrs. Pottle, from the wings, clasped the script to her
heart.

Ambrose looked at the Gulicks. He looked at his extinguished
Lamp. He looked down at his knees.

Then Captain Lemuel whispered, "Stand farther back, Pottle. You're spoiling
the family grouping."

Something in Ambrose quietly unbuttoned.

He stepped forward.

"Friends," he said, "the printed program tells you that Joshua
Gulick founded Granville."

Mrs. Pottle made a sound like escaping steam.

"The courthouse says Eli Spavins got here five years before
him. Joshua did start the first tavern. He also watered
the rum."

A ripple passed through the house.

Mr. Thaddeus Gulick turned purple above his white collar.

"Prudence Gulick," Ambrose continued, "is represented as feeding the settlers.
According to Goody Spavins's diary, Prudence locked up the meal
barrel and charged three cents a scoop."

The ripple became surf.

"Ebenezer Gulick did not repel the Indians. Ebenezer spent the
engagement in a cider barrel, and when hauled out was
mistaken for a pickled cucumber."

The audience broke.

Chief Roaring Elk sat down on the stage and laughed
into his war bonnet.

"Captain Lemuel sold spoiled pork to Washington's soldiers. Judge Peleg
welcomed the wrong Frenchman. And General Horace—"

"Stop him!" cried Mrs. Thaddeus.

"—was general secretary of the Granville Gooseberry Association and never
got nearer the Civil War than a lithograph of Grant."

Felix Winterbottom rose in the sixth row. His lean body
shook. His gray face cracked open in a grin so
astonishing that three hardware clerks failed to recognize him.

"Go on, Pottle!" he shouted.

Ambrose raised the dead Lamp of Truth.

"And thus," he said, "through war and peace, flood and
fire, fact and family tradition, Granville has advanced—dragging the Gulicks
whenever possible and being dragged by them whenever necessary."

The applause hit the stage like weather.

It lifted onions, Indians, factory girls, ancestral eagles, and the
Spirit of Culture into one magnificent democratic wreck. Pearl, hearing
the noise and perhaps believing bottles had at last been
collected, trotted through the golden platform. Two Centuries of Service
folded in the middle.

The curtain came down on the Gulicks.

Backstage Mrs. Pottle confronted Ambrose. Her cheeks smoldered, her bosom
heaved, and the tear with the nucleus stood armed and
dangerous.

"You ruined it!"

"I improved the history."

"You humiliated the leading family in Granville!"

"They'd been leading too long."

"The nursery is lost!"

At that moment Felix Winterbottom pushed through the crowd. He
still wore the expression of a man who had unexpectedly
found interest accumulating on an ancient grudge.

"Mrs. Pottle," he said, "what did this circus clear?"

Ambrose produced a crumpled envelope.

"After the hall, costumes, printing, horse, pistol damages, chicken supper,
and two dollars for getting Miss Gulick out of the
tub—eighty-six dollars and forty cents."

Mrs. Pottle closed her eyes.

Winterbottom drew out his checkbook.

"I'll give the thousand."

Her eyes opened.

"For the nursery," he added. "On one condition."

"No Gulick shall be honorary anything?" asked Ambrose.

"No. Put up a brass plate saying: 'This nursery is
dedicated to the helpless children of Granville, who are not
responsible for their ancestors.'"

He wrote the check.

Mrs. Pottle took it with trembling fingers. Civic triumph struggled
with marital murder and, after a spirited engagement, won.

"My dear Mr. Winterbottom," she breathed, "this noble generosity reveals
the true spirit of history."

"No," said Ambrose. "That's me. And I'm going home to
put on pants."

Behind them the orchestra attempted the national anthem. The cornet
took an early lead and was never overtaken.

## The Manton House

There are three kinds of haunted houses. In the first,
no one has seen a ghost, which is proof that
it appears only to persons of uncommon discretion. In the
second, everyone has seen one, which shows how generally observation
may be improved by rumor. In the third, the apparition
has been attested by a journalist, and disbelief is therefore
indecent. The Manton house belonged to all three classes.

It stood a mile west of Grayford, on a neglected
demesne where the weeds had attained something of the dignity
of timber and the timber showed a concerted inclination to
withdraw. Several of the trees leaned sharply away from the
house, exposing their roots like feet arrested in flight. The
windows were boarded; shingles had slipped from the roof; a
chimney leaned against the sky with the air of a
drunkard maintaining, upon a point of honor, that he was
erect. No road led to the place, though a pair
of pale wheel-ruts, visible in dry weather, indicated that vehicles
had once gone there and regretted it.

The house had been unoccupied for ten years, ever since
Mr. Silas Manton had found it expedient to cut the
throats of his wife and two young children and afterward
remove himself to some region beyond the immediate jurisdiction of
the sheriff. The bodies had been discovered in an upper
room. Mrs. Manton lay beside the bed, the children upon
it, and the family dog under it. The dog had
not been injured, but had died within the week—an excess
of sensibility which no person acquainted with dogs will regard
as impossible.

There had followed searches, proclamations, rewards, arrests of innocent men
and editorials upon the decline of domestic virtue. Of Manton
himself no ocular evidence was obtained. With that enterprise which
distinguishes communities from individuals, Grayford therefore ceased looking for him
and began looking for his ghost.

At eleven o'clock on a night in October, four men
approached the house across the field. One carried a lantern;
another, a long candle and a bottle; the remaining two
had each a knife beneath his coat. The moon had
not risen, and the lantern made a small yellow protest
against an immensity of blackness.

The first man was James Rosser, a young lawyer of
no great practice but considerable confidence. His apparel was good,
his manner easy, his courage—as courage is commonly computed by
those who possess knives—above suspicion. Behind him walked his friend,
Henry King, who had brought the lantern and would have
preferred to bring it home unused.

The other pair were Drayton, a deputy clerk of the
county court, and a stranger calling himself Robert Grossmith.

Grossmith was a powerful man past fifty. His hair was
gray at the temples, his face broad and unnaturally pale.
Deep wrinkles crossed his forehead without imparting wisdom to it.
His eyes were small, sunken and restless; his mouth seemed
fashioned less for speech than for the retention of some
bitter substance; and his lower jaw had the width which
physiognomists associate with determination when they approve its owner, and
brutality when they do not. Nature, in writing a man's
character upon his face, is often an indifferent penman, but
in this instance she had made several heavy strokes.

At the sagging gate Rosser paused.

"This is the place, Mr. Grossmith."

"I am not blind, Mr. Rosser."

"That circumstance," replied Rosser, "will shortly be corrected."

King laughed. Drayton did not. Grossmith looked from one to
the other.

"You have a pleasant wit," he said.

"It has survived less serious dangers than your disapproval."

"Gentlemen," said Drayton, "we are not here for a colloquy."

A second is commonly selected for his judgment and sobriety,
virtues which he proves by assisting another man to be
killed according to rule. Drayton had accepted the office reluctantly,
having met Grossmith only that evening; but he was devoted
to punctilio, and punctilio has led better men than he
into worse houses.

They passed through the gate. The path had been obliterated
by grass and thorn, and the lantern-light moved among the
weeds like a low wandering fire. At the porch King
raised one foot through a gap where a step had
been. The door, although swollen by rain and clasped with
rust, yielded after a shoulder had been applied to it.
It opened inward with a long complaint.

The air within was cold and stale. Cobwebs hung from
the ceiling in gray festoons, rotting lace upon the funeral
furniture of the place. The walls retained patches of paper
where faded flowers climbed toward a border they never reached.
A broken chair lay upon its back. Rat-droppings, plaster and
dead insects diversified the floor.

King lifted the lantern.

"No spectators," he said.

"Unless the local account is accurate," Drayton replied.

Grossmith turned sharply. "What account?"

"Only the usual nonsense," said Rosser. "A woman in white;
two children crying; lights at windows which have no glass.
Every abandoned house acquires tenants of that sort. They pay
no rent and are difficult to evict."

They crossed the hall and entered what had once been
a drawing-room. Its two windows were boarded from the outside.
The door was stout and had an old brass key
in the lock. A mantelpiece, blackened by smoke, projected from
one wall; otherwise the room was vacant.

King set the lantern upon the floor. Drayton lighted the
candle and fixed it in the neck of the bottle.
Its flame disclosed four faces of such irregular modeling that
each man might reasonably have distrusted the identity of the
others. Shadows enlarged Grossmith's jaw and hollowed his eyes. Rosser
appeared cheerful, but his cheerfulness had the fixed quality of
an expression painted upon wood.

"The arrangement," said King, "is as agreed. The principals will
stand back to back in the center. Each will take
three steps when the light is extinguished. They may then
turn and act as judgment directs. The seconds will retire,
lock the door and remain in the hall until all
sound has ceased."

"I object to the locking," said Drayton.

"It is essential," Grossmith said.

His voice had a dull vibration, like something spoken in
a vault.

Rosser smiled. "Mr. Grossmith fears that I may leave."

"I fear nothing from you."

"That is almost discourteous."

King produced two knives and examined them with professional gravity.
They were hunting-knives, equal in length and equally well adapted
to the modification of a gentleman's interior arrangements. He handed
one to Grossmith and one to Rosser.

"The weapons are identical."

"No weapons are identical," said Drayton, who had once read
an expert's testimony. "One may have a defect."

"So may one of the gentlemen," King replied, "but we
cannot replace him."

The duel proposed was not lawful, but it was honorable;
these conditions frequently coexist. Its peculiar method had been used,
according to Grossmith, on the Mississippi, where civilization had spread
a thin varnish of chivalry over the more serviceable customs
of the savage. In darkness each man would be deprived
of every advantage except hearing, agility, ferocity and luck.

King placed the two principals in the middle of the
room. Their shoulders touched.

"Ready?"

"Ready," said Rosser.

Grossmith said nothing, but his knife shone beside his thigh.

King lifted the bottle and candle. Drayton took the lantern
and stood at the door. For a moment nobody moved.

It was then that something passed along the corridor outside.

The sound was soft and irregular—three or four quick contacts
upon the bare boards, a pause, then others. It might
have been a rat; but rats, like poets, are seldom
so considerate as to adjust their performances to the apprehensions
of an audience.

Drayton looked into the hall.

"What is it?" asked Grossmith.

"Nothing."

"There was a sound."

"An old house settling," Rosser said. "It has had ten
years in which to make up its mind."

Grossmith's pale face had become paler. "Let us proceed."

King stepped backward through the doorway.

"Gentlemen, upon the count of three. One—two—three."

He blew out the candle.

Drayton shuttered the lantern.

Darkness fell—not the mitigated darkness of night, which has air
and distance in it, but an absolute and palpable negation.
The room vanished; the men vanished; even the sense of
direction seemed withdrawn. Someone's breathing was audible. A board creaked.

The seconds closed the door. The key turned.

King opened the lantern in the corridor, revealing Drayton's strained
face. At that same instant the door of the room
opened without noise, though the key remained in King's hand,
and Rosser slipped out. He carried no knife.

For one second the three stood looking at one another.

Then from within the room Grossmith spoke.

"Rosser?"

The voice was low and uncertain.

Rosser put a finger to his lips.

The three men crossed the corridor, passed into the hall
and gained the porch. As they sprang down among the
weeds, a sound came from the house—not a cry, exactly,
but a sudden harsh expulsion of breath, followed by a
blow which shook one of the boarded windows.

They ran.

A gentleman running at night across rough country retains little
of the dignity which distinguishes him in a drawing-room. King
lost his hat. Drayton encountered a bramble and left with
it a portion of his coat. Rosser fell once, rose
without comment and continued. Not until the lights of Grayford
appeared did any of them speak.

"He recognized nothing?" Drayton asked.

"Nothing," said Rosser.

"He may break out."

"The boards are nailed from outside, and the door is
locked."

King held up the key.

Drayton stopped. "Then how did Rosser leave the room?"

No one answered him.

It is now necessary to explain why three respectable persons
had enclosed a fourth in a haunted house, an undertaking
which may appear irregular even to readers favorably disposed toward
respectable persons.

Earlier that evening Rosser, King and Drayton had sat in
the rear room of the Eagle Tavern, where they drank
whiskey and discussed the Manton murders. They did this because
the courthouse had adjourned, the weather was wet, and Mrs.
Manton's right foot had once lacked the middle toe. The
connection between these facts is clearer than many connections upon
which history depends.

"My father made her shoes," King had said. "A child
could know the impression. The second and fourth toes leaned
together over the gap."

"Then she had four toes," said Rosser.

"Five, originally."

"Four at the time relevant to your anecdote. Precision is
the soul of law."

"Precision is the refuge of a man with nothing to
say," Drayton observed.

The stranger at the adjoining table turned his head. Until
then he had appeared occupied with his drink. His coat
was wet, his boots muddy and his hat drawn low.

"You are speaking of Mrs. Manton?" he asked.

Rosser inspected him. "We were, sir, though without any intention
of excluding you from the lady's society."

The stranger rose.

"You speak lightly of the dead."

"It is a privilege they seldom contest."

"You are a coward."

The word produced silence. A tavern is a temple in
which men worship reputation, commonly sacrificing reason upon its altar.

Rosser stood. "You will withdraw that."

"My name is Robert Grossmith. I withdraw nothing."

King, whose attention had sharpened at the name of Manton,
studied the stranger's face. He had been a boy when
the murders occurred, but he remembered a broad jaw, a
pale complexion and small eyes under a heavy brow. Time
alters a face as weather alters stone: it softens some
lines and deepens others, but does not always improve the
monument.

Drayton took Rosser's arm. "Not here."

Grossmith gave a short laugh. "Your friend is prudent."

"He is a deputy clerk," Rosser replied. "Prudence is among
the appurtenances of the office."

There followed the inevitable formalities. Grossmith demanded satisfaction. Pistols were
suggested, then rejected on account of the weather, the sheriff
and Grossmith's declared aversion to them. Knives in darkness were
proposed by Grossmith himself, with the Manton house named as
neutral ground.

During a consultation in the passage King said quietly to
Rosser:

"That man is Silas Manton."

Rosser's smile disappeared.

"Are you certain?"

"No. Certainty is available chiefly to fools and coroners. But
I would wager my hat."

As King later lost the article, the magnitude of this
confidence cannot now be determined.

They devised their plan at once. Drayton, informed of the
stranger's probable identity, objected in the name of law, religion
and personal safety; being outvoted in all three jurisdictions, he
consented. They would leave Manton locked in darkness until morning,
when the sheriff could be summoned. It was a cruel
stratagem, but they excused it as a measure for securing
a murderer. Men readily confer a public character upon private
malice when they wish to enjoy both.

One additional circumstance encouraged them. For years the Grayford *Herald*
had printed accounts of a white female figure seen at
the Manton windows, sometimes accompanied by two smaller figures. The
reports came from travelers, drunkards, children and one minister—classes of
witnesses whose credibility overlaps in curious ways. Rosser regarded the
stories as nonsense. King hoped Manton did not.

At the edge of town, Drayton again asked how Rosser
had emerged through a locked door.

"The key had not turned fully," Rosser said. "The bolt
caught but did not enter the socket. I pulled the
door open, and King locked it afterward."

This explanation was accepted because it was possible, and a
possible explanation is the natural enemy of an unpleasant inquiry.

At nine the next morning Sheriff Abel Norton received a
visit from King and Rosser. Drayton was not present, having
discovered an urgent obligation to remain in bed. Rosser's account
omitted the projected duel and represented the matter as an
ingenious citizen's arrest. Sheriff Norton, who had held office long
enough to understand that truth is generally improved for official
use, listened without interruption.

"You locked a suspected murderer in the Manton house?"

"Yes."

"All night?"

"Yes."

"Without food?"

"There was whiskey."

"That is food in this county."

The sheriff sent for Stephen Vale, Mrs. Manton's brother, who
had known Silas intimately and hated him with an endurance
that did credit to his memory. Vale arrived carrying a
revolver and a face from which sleep had been forcibly
excluded.

"If it is Manton," he said, "I shall know him."

"If it is Manton," said the sheriff, "you shall refrain
from knowing him with that weapon."

The four men walked to the house beneath a hard,
bright sky. Noon would have been kinder to the place
than dawn, yet even the full day diminished its terrors.
Its weeds were only weeds; the withdrawing trees were revealed
as victims of prevailing winds; the black windows became boards
imperfectly fitted. Sunlight is a competent advocate for reason, though
it occasionally proves its case by suppressing evidence.

At the porch they found no footprints except their own
from the previous night. The front door stood as they
had left it. Dust filmed the hall. King called aloud.

"Grossmith!"

No reply came.

"Manton!" shouted Vale.

Something in the house seemed to receive the name and
keep it.

They crossed to the drawing-room. The key remained in the
lock on the outside. King touched it, then drew back
as if it were hot.

"I locked that door."

"We have your affidavit in advance," said Norton. "Open it."

The key turned with difficulty. The door moved inward three
inches and stopped.

"Something behind it," Rosser said.

The sheriff put his shoulder against the panel. There was
a scraping sound from within, heavy and brief. The opening
widened.

The body of Robert Grossmith—or Silas Manton—sat in the corner
behind the door.

He had drawn his knees up and pressed his back
against the meeting of the walls. One hand clutched the
knife; the other was raised before his face, the palm
outward. His head was thrown slightly back. His eyes were
open.

No man there doubted that he was dead, yet none
approached at once.

There was no blood. His clothing was neither cut nor
disordered. The knife had not been used. The room contained
no weapon but his own and no person living or
dead besides himself. The boarded windows were secure. The fireplace
had been closed for years by a fall of masonry.

Sheriff Norton knelt and touched the dead man's throat, then
tried an arm. It resisted him with the rigidity of
iron.

"He has been dead several hours."

"From what?" King asked.

The sheriff looked at the face.

The dead man's mouth was open, its corners drawn downward.
The lips had receded from the teeth. In the eyes
remained an expression not of pain, nor even of surprise,
but of recognition perfected by terror. Whatever he had seen,
he had known it.

"From fright, perhaps," Norton said.

Vale stepped nearer and looked long at the corpse.

"It is Manton."

His voice had no triumph in it.

Rosser went to one of the windows and examined the
boards, perhaps because the dead face was disagreeable to contemplate.

"He heard us leave," he said. "He understood the trick.
Alone, in darkness, in this house—his imagination did the rest."

"That will be the sensible explanation," said Norton.

A sheriff's preference for sensible explanations is professional, since the
insensible kind cannot be arrested.

King had picked up the lantern left in the corridor
the night before. He opened it and found a little
oil remaining. Then, though daylight entered at the door and
through seams in the boards, he lighted it.

"What is that for?" Rosser asked.

King did not answer. He lowered the lantern close to
the floor.

The boards were covered with dust, except where the movements
of the previous night had disturbed it. The tracks of
four boots crossed and recrossed near the center. Manton's own
prints led toward the corner. About them ran confused marks
made when he had retreated.

But between the center of the room and the corpse
were other impressions.

Sheriff Norton saw them first. He bent lower.

"Do not step here."

There were three sets of bare footprints in the dust.

They began near the middle of the room—began without approach,
as though three invisible persons had first acquired weight at
that spot. From there they proceeded directly toward the corner
where Manton sat. None turned aside. None returned.

Two sets were those of children.

The impressions were small and distinct: heel, arch and tiny
toes, repeated with a dreadful deliberation. They ended before the
dead man's knees. The third track was made by an
adult woman. It passed between the others and stopped where
the raised hand of the corpse seemed still to forbid
its nearer advance.

No corresponding prints led away.

For some moments the four men remained silent. Outside, a
bird called from one of the leaning trees, and the
sound was so ordinary that it seemed an indecency.

Rosser was the first to speak.

"They were made earlier. Ten years ago, perhaps."

Norton looked at him. "Dust does not preserve a print
beneath later dust."

"Then someone entered after we left."

"Through what?"

Rosser indicated the door, then remembered the key. He looked
at the boarded windows, the blocked fireplace and the unbroken
walls. His face changed.

Vale knelt beside the woman's track. He did not touch
it. He merely stared.

King raised the lantern, though there was no need of
additional light. The flame trembled in its glass enclosure, projecting
the four men's shadows against the walls. By that uncertain
illumination the bare impressions seemed deeper than they had in
day, as if the feet that made them had pressed
upward from beneath the floor.

The sheriff cleared his throat.

"You knew your sister's feet?" he asked Vale.

Vale nodded.

It is difficult to say why this question was necessary.
The dead woman's identity had already entered the minds of
all present, yet the human intellect, when confronted by the
impossible, demands a small familiar detail upon which to hang
its surrender.

Vale pointed to one impression. The woman's right foot had
left the print of the great toe, then the second;
after that there was a narrow vacancy in the dust,
and beyond it the marks of the fourth and fifth
toes leaning together.

"My sister Gertrude," he said, "had no middle toe."

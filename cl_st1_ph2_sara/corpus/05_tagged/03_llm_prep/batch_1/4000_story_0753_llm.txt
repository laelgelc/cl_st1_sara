## PHILO GUBB AND THE NEGATIVE BRIDE

On the morning following the day on which Jonas Medderbrook
swindled Philo Gubb, Mr. Gubb arose from his bed, if
not refreshed by sleep, at least considerably wrinkled by the
lack of it. He dressed himself with the careful melancholy
of a man who has lost money and a sweetheart
in the same transaction, and then sat down upon the
edge of his washstand to consider whether breakfast was worth
eating.

It was.

Mr. Gubb was too practical a man to permit even
a broken heart to interfere permanently with fried potatoes. He
descended to the dining-room of the Riverbank House, ate the
potatoes, four buckwheat cakes, three eggs and a section of
sausage, and then returned to his room to resume his
despair.

In appearance Mr. Gubb was long and thin. His legs
began in the usual place, but seemed reluctant to end,
and when he stood beside a picket fence the resemblance
between himself and the upright portions of that structure was
remarkable. His neck was of such length and looseness that
his head appeared to have been placed upon it as
an afterthought. When he walked rapidly, his knees rose high
and his elbows moved outward, giving him somewhat the look
of a flamingo hurrying to catch a train.

At that time Mr. Gubb followed the trade of paper-hanger,
but he was also, by correspondence, a deteckative.

The Rising Sun Correspondence School of Detective Work had informed
him, upon receipt of eleven dollars and seventy-five cents, that
no man with ordinary brains, good eyesight and the ability
to read English need remain outside the detective profession. Mr.
Gubb possessed all three qualifications in forms satisfactory to himself.
He had already received Lessons One to Twelve, together with
a magnifying glass, a cardboard badge and a printed certificate
stating that he had displayed commendable zeal in the mastery
of Crime Detection, Shadowing, Disguises, Bloodstain Analysis and the Proper
Manner of Entering a Room.

He had not yet received Lesson Thirteen, entitled "How to
Detect Guilt by the Way a Suspect Eats Soup," because
he still owed the school one dollar and twenty-five cents.
This temporary deficiency did not prevent him from practicing such
portions of the science as were already paid for.

Unfortunately, no amount of professional education could make Syrilla Bleeze
weigh anything.

At least that was what Syrilla had told him.

Syrilla was, or had been, the lady of Mr. Gubb's
heart. She had also been the Fat Woman in the
Grand Imperial United Congress of Wonders, Wild Beasts and Moral
Exhibitions. She was a woman of generous outlines and a
disposition which, before Jonas Medderbrook arrived, had seemed equally generous.

Mr. Gubb had admired her from the first moment he
saw her seated upon a velvet-covered platform beneath a sign
reading:

SYRILLA
THE HUMAN EARTHQUAKE
SEVEN HUNDRED AND ELEVEN POUNDS
SHE EATS WHAT YOU EAT—ONLY MORE OF IT

His love had not been chilled by the admission price,
nor by the fact that Syrilla's professional weight varied according
to the size of the town. In small villages she
was advertised at six hundred and eighty pounds. In county-seat
towns she was seven hundred and eleven. In cities where
competition was severe she reached eight hundred and four. To
Mr. Gubb these figures were not deception. They were poetry.

Jonas Medderbrook, however, had entered the affair with a weighing
scale and a scheme.

He sold Mr. Gubb an interest in a machine which,
he declared, could weigh anything from a feather to a
freight locomotive and tell not only its weight but its
moral character. Mr. Gubb paid him one hundred dollars, representing
years of careful paper-hanging, and allowed him to place the
machine in Syrilla's room.

The scale had a large brass dial, two handles, a
green platform and an arrangement underneath which Jonas advised nobody
to examine, because examination injured the scientific currents. When Syrilla
stepped upon the platform, the needle swung backward, passed zero
and came to rest at the mark below it.

"There!" Jonas cried. "Five pounds less than nothing!"

Syrilla stared at the dial.

"Philo," she said solemnly, "I cannot marry you."

Mr. Gubb's jaw descended.

"Why not, Syrilla?"

"Because," she said, "a woman that weighs five pounds less
than nothing does not legally exist. You cannot marry what
ain't there."

"But I can see you," Mr. Gubb protested.

"That's optical deception," Jonas said. "A scientific scale is more
reliable than an eye. Eyes is influenced by affection."

Mr. Gubb looked from Syrilla to the scale and back
again. There appeared to be more Syrilla than could conveniently
be explained away by optical deception, but she shook her
head sadly.

"I suspected for some time I was getting thinner," she
said. "My blue dress hung loose in the left shoulder.
Now the truth is known. I am less than zero."

"You might eat up to existence again," Mr. Gubb suggested
hopefully.

"It is too late," said Jonas. "She has passed the
vanishing point."

That afternoon Jonas Medderbrook departed with Mr. Gubb's hundred dollars,
the scale and Syrilla. Syrilla left a note saying she
was going away to be nothing in private.

Thus, when Mr. Gubb had finished breakfast and returned to
his room, he took Syrilla's photograph from the mantel and
addressed it with the quiet anguish of a man betrayed
by mathematics.

"Syrilla," he said, "had you weighed as much as one
pound over nothing, my love would have supported you. But
you are absent from the world, and Jonas Medderbrook has
took the money I would of used to support you
with."

The photograph did not answer. It showed Syrilla as she
had looked during the circus season at Peoria, wearing a
spangled dress, a crown and an expression of large contentment.

Mr. Gubb sighed, put on his derby hat and went
forth to detect crime.

There was, at that moment, very little crime in Riverbank
that required detection. The most important unsolved case was the
disappearance of a red-and-white calf belonging to Mr. Hezekiah Wiggins,
who lived three miles outside town. Mr. Wiggins had offered
five dollars for the recovery of the calf and ten
dollars for the conviction of the thief, although he said
privately that conviction alone would satisfy him if he could
be permitted five minutes with the convict and a fence
rail.

Mr. Gubb had accepted the case. He had examined the
pasture with a magnifying glass, a reading glass, an opera
glass, the lens from a broken lantern and the bottom
of a medicine bottle. He found hoofprints, but as the
pasture contained eleven cows, two horses, the missing calf and
Mr. Wiggins, who wore boots, the prints did not immediately
clarify matters.

According to Lesson Seven, every criminal left a clue.

According to Mr. Gubb's experience, every farm left so many
clues that the criminal could hardly find room to leave
one of his own.

He had been working upon the case for four days
without progress when Chi Foxy arrived in Riverbank.

Chi Foxy was a tramp by occupation, inclination and long
practice. His true name, if he had one, had worn
out before his shoes did. He called himself Chi because
he had once slept in Chicago and Foxy because, as
he explained, "A guy that ain't foxy don't stay outside
jail long enough to get hungry."

On the evening of his arrival he was exceptionally hungry.
He had eaten nothing since noon, when a farmer's wife
had given him a turnip and advised him to seek
steady work. He had eaten the turnip and rejected the
advice.

The weather had turned cold. As Chi followed the road
west of Riverbank, his coat performed chiefly the duty of
showing where his shirt was torn. A thin rain had
begun, and the damp wind searched him with an intimacy
he resented.

He came at last to a square white house standing
back from the road. Through the kitchen window shone a
yellow light of the exact color that makes a hungry
man think of butter.

Chi approached quietly and looked in.

A lanky man with a coarse brown beard sat at
the table. Opposite him was a narrow woman whose hair
was tied in a hard knot. Between them stood a
platter of fried veal chops, browned at the edges and
exhaling an odor of such virtue that Chi Foxy closed
his eyes.

When he opened them, the lanky man was lifting a
chop. He bit it. The woman lifted another. She bit
it. They chewed.

Chi swallowed.

On the table were also mashed potatoes, gravy, bread, apple
preserves and a pie with a triangular section missing. Chi
watched until the platter contained only bones.

Then the man gathered the bones into a chipped blue
breakfast plate.

"You better get rid of them, Lem," the woman said.

"I'm a-going to," the man answered.

"Bury 'em deep."

"I know how to bury evidence."

Chi's interest increased. A tramp may be hungry, but he
is not necessarily deaf to the dramatic value of evidence.

The woman looked toward the window, and Chi lowered himself
behind a rain barrel.

"Suppose somebody finds 'em?" she asked.

"They won't."

"Suppose that deteckative feller comes?"

"Him?" The man laughed. "That long drink of wallpaper paste
couldn't find a ham in a smokehouse."

"Don't be too sure. He was around again this afternoon,
measuring tracks."

"What with?"

"A pickle bottle."

The man rose, took the plate of bones and wrapped
them in a piece of newspaper. He then carried them
out of the rear door. Chi, moving with the silent
caution natural to men who frequently leave places before being
invited to do so, crawled after him.

Behind the house lay a garden, and beyond the garden
stood a small barn. The lanky man went to the
fence near the barn, selected a spot where the soil
was sandy and began digging with a spade.

He dug a hole two feet deep, dropped in the
bundle of bones, filled the hole, stamped the soil and
scattered crushed straw over it.

Then he went back to the house.

Chi Foxy remained crouched beside a currant bush.

"A murder," he whispered.

He considered this conclusion. The bones had appeared small, but
Chi's knowledge of anatomy was limited to knowing which portions
of his own anatomy hurt when he was hungry. He
had heard of murders. He had also heard of rewards.

He crept to the burial spot and inspected it. The
rain had softened the sandy soil, and the crushed straw
was darker than the surrounding straw. It was, to his
mind, as plain a grave as any man could wish
to discover, provided he did not have to dig it
up himself.

He returned to the road and walked toward Riverbank.

As he walked, he considered the problem of food. He
might report the murder to the police, but policemen had
an irritating habit of remembering him. He might report it
to the sheriff, but the sheriff might ask why Chi
had been looking into the window. He might confront the
lanky man, but the lanky man had eaten all the
veal.

Then Chi remembered the woman's mention of a deteckative feller—a
long drink of wallpaper paste who measured tracks with pickle
bottles.

A private detective, Chi reflected, would probably be eager for
a murder. He would also have an office, and any
office might contain crackers.

Mr. Gubb's office was easily found. It occupied the rear
half of his paper-hanging establishment, and its window bore the
legend:

PHILO GUBB
PAPER-HANGING IN ALL ITS BRANCHES
CRIMES DETECTED IN ANY BRANCH ALSO
STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL
TERMS REASONABLE

When Chi Foxy entered, Mr. Gubb sat at his desk
studying Lesson Nine, "The Use and Abuse of Disguise." Beside
him stood a paste bucket. On the wall hung samples
of wallpaper, handcuffs and Syrilla's photograph.

Mr. Gubb looked up.

Chi removed a soft hat which had not originally been
soft.

"Are you the celebrated deteckative?" he asked.

"I am a deteckative," said Mr. Gubb modestly. "Whether celebrated
or not depends which circles you travel into."

"Name of Gubb?"

"Philo Gubb."

Chi advanced and leaned both hands upon the desk.

"Then arrest me," he said.

Mr. Gubb stared.

"For what crime?"

"Murder."

Mr. Gubb pushed back his chair.

"Did you do one?"

"I done the complete act," said Chi. "Murdered a party,
buried the spirit dee corpse and come here to surrender.
Slip the bracelets on me, pard, and lead me to
the nearest jail where there's hot soup."

Mr. Gubb did not reach for the handcuffs. Lesson Four
warned against accepting voluntary confessions until the motive had been
established, and also warned the student to maintain a mask
of professional impenetrability.

He therefore narrowed his eyes until Chi Foxy became a
blur.

"You claim you murdered a man?"

"Who said man?" Chi demanded. "I said party. Might have
been a man. Might have been a lady. In the
excitement of doing a high-class murder you don't stop to
ask social questions."

"What was the name of the deceased corpse?"

"That," said Chi, "is a professional secret."

"You can tell me. I'm professional, too."

Chi considered. Hunger urged him toward a rapid arrest, but
Mr. Gubb's caution demanded embellishment.

"All right," he said. "I murdered the Duke of Spaghetti."

Mr. Gubb opened Lesson Four and glanced at the section
headed "Foreign Criminals."

"An Italian?" he asked.

"English," said Chi. "One of the oldest dukes in England.
His family come over with William the Conductor."

"Conqueror," Mr. Gubb corrected.

"That's the one. Conducted the whole invasion. The duke and
me was raised together."

Mr. Gubb looked at Chi's trousers, which were tied at
one knee with string.

"You was raised with a duke?"

"Certainly. I was a famous deteckative in them days."

This interested Mr. Gubb more than the murder.

"What school did you graduate out of?"

"The London School of Personal Experience," said Chi. "I was
head pupil and assistant janitor. Shermlock Hollums used to come
to me for advice."

Mr. Gubb sat upright.

"You knew Sherlock Holmes?"

"Knew him?" cried Chi. "Why, bo, me and Shermlock was
like twin brothers, except different mothers and fathers. Many's the
case he couldn't solve until I happened around. 'Chi,' he'd
say, 'this mystery has got me laid flat.' 'Sherm,' I'd
say, 'get up off the floor and use your intelligence.'
Then I'd solve it for him."

Mr. Gubb's expression became respectful in spite of himself. The
Rising Sun lessons referred often to Sherlock Holmes, though they
spelled the name correctly.

"What cases did you assist him into?"

"All of 'em," said Chi. "The Speckled Band, the Baskerville
Dog, the Red-Headed Leaguer and the Mystery of the Four
Orange Apple Seeds. He never let my name appear because
I was operating in disguise."

Mr. Gubb leaned forward.

"You are disguised now?"

"You bet I am."

"As what?"

"A tramp."

Mr. Gubb inspected him. Chi's beard was uneven, his hair
was muddy, his coat was torn, his shoes were split
and the fragrance of freight cars clung to him with
fidelity.

"It is a remarkably complete disguise," Mr. Gubb admitted.

"Complete?" Chi said. "It's perfection. When I disguise myself as
a tramp, I do everything a tramp does. I sleep
in haystacks, walk on railroad tracks, dodge work, get chased
by dogs and go without food. I ain't had a
bite since noon."

"That," said Mr. Gubb, "shows how deep you penetrate into
the character."

"Deep is right. My stomach is touching my spine."

"You undoubtedly studied Lesson Nine."

"I wrote it," Chi said. "Your correspondence school bought it
off me after Shermlock refused to pay royalties."

Mr. Gubb picked up his lesson and regarded it with
fresh reverence.

Chi pointed to the wall.

"Who's the broad?" he asked.

Mr. Gubb turned toward Syrilla's photograph.

"That," he said sadly, "was Syrilla Bleeze, the lady I
was expecting to marry before science proved her nonexistence."

Chi approached the photograph.

"Looks plenty existent."

"The scale showed five pounds less than nothing."

"Bad business," Chi said. "Them scales don't lie, except when
somebody fixes 'em."

Mr. Gubb's head turned sharply.

"Fixes them?"

"Sure. A crooked guy can make a scale say anything.
I once had a scale said I weighed fourteen tons
and was worth thirty cents a pound."

Mr. Gubb paled, but his faith in Syrilla resisted suspicion.

"Jonas Medderbrook said the currents could not be wrong."

"Jonas Medderbrook?" Chi repeated.

"You know him?"

"I know every international crook," said Chi, recovering instantly. "But
we better stick to my murder. Arrest me before I
weaken and become innocent."

Mr. Gubb folded his hands.

"You have not supplied the motive."

"Motive?"

"Lesson Four says every murder must contain means, motive and
opportunity. You have supplied the murder, which may be the
means, and the buried corpse, which may be the opportunity,
but the motive remains absent."

Chi sat down heavily.

"Hunger," he said.

"You murdered the duke because you was hungry?"

"No. I'm confessing because I'm hungry."

"That is not a motive for the murder."

"It's the best motive I got."

Mr. Gubb shook his head. "Scientifically, I cannot arrest you
until you tell why you killed him to death."

Chi looked around the office. There were no crackers. On
a shelf rested a jar, but it contained wallpaper paste.

"Jealousy," he said.

"Over a woman?"

"Certainly. Lady Clara Van Astorbilk. Beautiful creature. Hair like gold,
teeth like pearls, eyes like—like eyes. Me and the duke
both loved her wildly."

"Did she love you?"

"Madly."

"Did she love him?"

"Also madly. She was broad-minded."

Mr. Gubb drew a sheet of paper toward him and
began taking notes.

"Where did this happen?"

"England."

"The body is buried in England?"

"No. The murder happened here."

"But you said you and the duke were raised together."

"In England. Then he followed me to America, pursuing Lady
Clara, who had come over to learn typewriting."

Mr. Gubb wrote this down.

"When was the murder committed?"

"Last night."

"Where?"

"Near a white house west of town."

"What weapon?"

Chi hesitated. He had seen no weapon. He remembered the
spade.

"A shovel."

"You struck him?"

"Repeatedly, until dead."

"Did the deceased cry out?"

"Not after he was dead."

"That is suspicious," Mr. Gubb said.

"Why?"

"A man murdered with a shovel would naturally cry out
before death."

"This duke was English," Chi explained. "Very reserved. Never raised
his voice."

Mr. Gubb accepted this as a point in favor of
the story.

"What did you do with the corpse?"

"Chopped it into bones."

"With the shovel?"

"With scientific processes. Then I wrapped the bones in newspaper,
put 'em on a chipped blue breakfast plate, carried 'em
near the barn and buried 'em in sandy soil under
crushed straw."

"You buried the plate also?"

Chi recollected that the lanky man had carried the empty
plate back inside.

"No. The plate was a clue, so I made it
vanish."

"How?"

"Perfect disguise."

"You disguised the plate?"

"As a breakfast plate. Then I returned it to the
kitchen. Nobody would suspect a breakfast plate on a kitchen
table of being a murder clue. That's high-class detection."

Mr. Gubb was impressed. It accorded perfectly with Lesson Nine,
which said the finest disguise was the disguise that caused
the disguised object to appear to be exactly what it
seemed.

"Why did you bury the corpse near the house?"

"So nobody would find it."

"Would it not have been safer farther away?"

"That's what an amateur would think," Chi said scornfully. "A
professional hides a thing where it looks foolish to hide
it. Shermlock learned that from me. Once we had to
hide the Crown Jools. Where did I put 'em?"

"I could not say."

"Right in the crown. Nobody thought to look there."

Mr. Gubb nodded slowly.

Chi pressed his advantage.

"Another time the Queen of Spain lost a pet giraffe.
Whole police force searched the country. Shermlock says, 'Chi, where
can a giraffe be concealed?' I says, 'Sherm, disguise it
as a giraffe and put it in the zoo.' We
done it. Stood there six months. Nobody suspected."

"How did you know it was the lost giraffe if
it was disguised as one?"

"Private mark."

"What mark?"

"Long neck."

Mr. Gubb wrote "long neck" upon his paper.

"Now," Chi continued, "if you'll arrest me and put me
in jail, we can discuss the finer points over supper."

Mr. Gubb's expression hardened.

"No."

"No?"

"As a deteckative, I must proceed by the numbered instructions.
First I must verify the corpus delicti."

"What's that?"

"The murdered dead body."

"You doubt my word?"

"I doubt everybody's word. That is Lesson Two."

Chi placed a hand dramatically upon his chest.

"After all I done for Shermlock Hollums!"

"You might be an impostor."

"Of course I'm an impostor. I'm disguised."

Mr. Gubb frowned. This was reasonable.

"You claim to be a famous deteckative disguised as a
tramp, and you claim to be the murderer I am
expected to capture."

"You got it."

"Then you are both the criminal and the person detecting
him?"

"Exactly. It saves time."

"And you came to me because—"

"Because," Chi said, with tears of hunger in his eyes,
"one great mind naturally seeks another great mind when there's
a jail supper connected."

Mr. Gubb rose.

"I will accompany you to the remains."

"After which I get arrested?"

"If the remains are human."

"And if they ain't?"

"They must be," Mr. Gubb said. "Lesson Four states that
a confession should agree with the facts, and you have
confessed too much for the facts to disagree entirely."

Chi had his doubts, but he led the way.

The rain had stopped, and the night was cold and
clear. Mr. Gubb carried a lantern, his handcuffs, a folding
ruler, the magnifying glass, the reading glass, the opera glass,
the lantern lens, the medicine-bottle bottom, a notebook, a pencil
and Lesson Four. Chi carried his hunger.

On the road westward, Mr. Gubb questioned him further.

"What became of Lady Clara Van Astorbilk?"

"She died of grief."

"When?"

"Tomorrow," said Chi absently.

Mr. Gubb stopped.

"How can she die tomorrow?"

"Telegram," Chi answered. "She notified me in advance."

Mr. Gubb resumed walking, but his suspicion increased by a
small amount.

"What did the duke look like?"

"Tall," Chi said.

"How tall?"

"Sometimes tall, sometimes short."

"Why did his height alter?"

"Disguise."

"Was he disguised when you killed him?"

"Certainly."

"As what?"

Chi thought of the meal seen through the window.

"As a calf."

Mr. Gubb stopped again.

"A calf?"

"A complete calf disguise. Hair, hide, feet and everything. Shermlock
himself would have took him for veal."

"How could a duke fit into a calf disguise?"

"He was a small duke. England is crowded, so their
nobility don't grow large."

This time Mr. Gubb remained silent for nearly half a
mile. There were elements in Chi's confession that did not
match any lesson he had studied, but he remembered that
genius often appears insane to ordinary minds. As he had
been trained by mail to avoid ordinary-mindedness, he chose to
keep his doubts professional and concealed.

When they reached the white house, its kitchen was dark.

Chi led Mr. Gubb around the garden, beyond the currant
bush and toward the barn. He found the fence and
pointed.

"There lies the Duke of Spaghetti."

Mr. Gubb held up the lantern. Several feet of ground
were covered with crushed straw.

"Which exact place?"

Chi looked at the earth. During his absence the wind
had shifted the straw, the rain had smoothed the soil
and the lanky man had walked across the area twice.
The burial spot no longer appeared so definite.

"There," Chi said, pointing.

Mr. Gubb unfolded his ruler and measured the place. Then
he got down upon his knees and applied the magnifying
glass.

"No loosened soil," he said.

Chi pointed a yard to the left.

"I mean there."

Mr. Gubb used the reading glass.

"No."

"Try there."

Mr. Gubb used the opera glass from a distance of
eight inches.

"No."

"That other spot."

Mr. Gubb removed the lens from the lantern, darkening the
scene, and peered through it unsuccessfully.

"You have extinguished our light," Chi said.

"That proves the clue has vanished," Mr. Gubb answered.

"Maybe the duke moved."

"Dead men do not move."

"English dukes is very active."

Mr. Gubb relit the lantern and examined the ground through
the bottom of the medicine bottle. Everything appeared greenish and
distorted, which he considered promising but indefinite.

Chi's stomach made a hollow sound.

"What was that?" Mr. Gubb demanded.

"Death rattle," Chi said. "The corpse is under here."

He dropped to his knees and began pawing straw aside.
Presently his fingers found a patch of softer soil.

"Here!"

Mr. Gubb examined it with every instrument in sequence. Having
completed the scientific portion of the investigation, he borrowed a
shovel from beside the barn and began to dig.

At the first thrust of the spade, a light appeared
in the kitchen.

At the second, the rear door opened.

At the third, the lanky man came out carrying a
shotgun.

"What are you doing there?" he demanded.

Mr. Gubb straightened. With the lantern on the ground, his
tall form rose out of the dark like a pair
of dividers wearing a derby.

"Official deteckative business," he said.

The man's face went pale beneath his coarse beard.

"What business?"

"Murder," Chi said cheerfully.

The man lowered the shotgun.

"Murder?"

"We have reason to believe," Mr. Gubb announced, "that the
remains of a deceased party, possibly noble and foreign, have
been secretly concealed into this spot."

The narrow woman appeared behind her husband. She clasped both
hands before her.

"I told you!" she cried. "I told you that deteckative
would find out!"

"Shut up, Marthy," said the man.

Mr. Gubb's eyes narrowed. A detective could not fail to
recognize guilty behavior when it announced itself in plain language.

"Stand aside," he ordered. "Any attempt to interfere will constitute
resisting the capture of the criminal classes."

The lanky man did not interfere. He and the woman
watched while Mr. Gubb dug.

The spade struck newspaper.

Chi Foxy's hopes rose. The newspaper appeared. Beneath it lay
bones—clean bones, small bones, unmistakable bones.

Mr. Gubb lifted one and held it to the lantern.

"A portion of the corpse," he said solemnly.

The woman moaned.

The lanky man dropped the shotgun.

"All right!" he cried. "You got me! I done it!"

Mr. Gubb turned upon him.

"You admit murder?"

"I admit everything. No use denying it with them bones
dug up."

"What was the victim's name?"

"Name?" The man stared.

"The Duke of Spaghetti," Chi prompted.

"I never heard of him."

Mr. Gubb looked from the bone to the man.

"Who did you kill to death?"

"I didn't kill no man," said the lanky one. "That's
the Wiggins calf."

Silence followed. It was the rich, complete silence that sometimes
surrounds a great scientific discovery and sometimes surrounds a mistake
too large to approach immediately.

Mr. Gubb examined the bone.

"A calf?"

"Yes," the man said miserably. "I stole it out of
Wiggins's pasture four nights ago. Times has been hard, and
Marthy wanted veal. We butchered it in the shed, salted
most of the meat and had the chops tonight. I
buried the bones so you wouldn't find 'em."

Marthy began to cry.

"I told Lem it was wrong," she said, "but veal
fries so nice."

Mr. Gubb drew himself to his full height.

He had not found a murdered duke. He had, however,
found the missing Wiggins calf, the thief, the thief's confession,
part of the stolen property and the remains of the
evening meal. Lesson Eight stated that the successful detective never
permitted minor discrepancies to obscure the main triumph.

"You will consider yourself captured up," he told Lem.

Lem submitted.

Mr. Gubb turned to secure Chi Foxy, intending to ask
him several questions concerning the Duke of Spaghetti, Lady Clara,
Sherlock Holmes and the astonishing completeness of calf disguise.

Chi was gone.

During the confession he had slipped around the side of
the house, entered the kitchen and filled his pockets with
bread, cold potatoes, three veal chops, half the pie, two
biscuits, an onion, a jar of apple preserves, six slices
of ham, a wedge of cheese and four sandwiches Marthy
had prepared for Lem's next day's dinner.

The sandwiches were one ham, one veal, one fried egg
and one consisting chiefly of mustard between bread. Chi ate
the egg sandwich before leaving the yard, placed the ham
sandwich inside his shirt, put the veal sandwich in his
coat pocket and carried the mustard sandwich in his hat.

He departed by way of the railroad tracks, reflecting that
the detective profession was difficult but nourishing.

Mr. Gubb took Lem to Riverbank, and the next morning
Mr. Wiggins identified the hide and paid the promised fifteen
dollars. The constable congratulated Mr. Gubb. The Riverbank Eagle printed
a paragraph beneath the heading:

LOCAL DETECTIVE UNEARTHS BONES
CONFESSION FOLLOWS SCIENTIFIC WORK

Mr. Gubb said little regarding the exact processes by which
he had solved the crime. When asked how he knew
where to dig, he closed one eye and replied:

"A deteckative has methods which the general public would not
understand without taking lessons."

For two weeks afterward, he carried himself with the inscrutable
dignity of a man who has dug up precisely what
he intended to find. He paid the Rising Sun Correspondence
School the one dollar and twenty-five cents he owed and
received Lesson Thirteen. He also ordered the Advanced Course in
Murder, Blackmail and Mysterious Footprints.

But professional success did not heal his heart.

At night, when his wallpaper work was finished and Riverbank's
criminal element had gone home to supper, Mr. Gubb sat
beneath Syrilla's photograph and mourned.

"Five pounds less than nothing," he would whisper. "How could
affection grasp onto a vacancy?"

He sometimes imagined that Syrilla had eaten steadily after leaving
him and had climbed by slow ounces toward existence. Perhaps,
he thought, she was now only three pounds less than
nothing. Perhaps Jonas Medderbrook was feeding her buttered toast. Perhaps
one day she would weigh zero, then half a pound,
then a whole pound, and might write to him.

No letter came.

Winter passed. Spring turned the roads to mud. Mr. Gubb
solved the mystery of Mrs. Pitkin's missing washtub by discovering
it behind her shed, where she had placed it. He
followed a suspicious stranger for seven hours and learned that
the stranger was following him under the belief that Mr.
Gubb was suspicious. He detected a burglar in the First
Methodist Church, who proved to be the sexton locking a
window. None of these successes brought him comfort.

Then, one afternoon, Chi Foxy returned.

He entered the office with the air of a man
calling upon an old professional associate. His coat was more
torn than before, his hat had acquired an opening in
the crown and his face expressed the lean confidence of
an appetite already planning its next meal.

Mr. Gubb looked up from Lesson Sixteen, "How to Tell
a Poisoned Cigar from One That Is Merely Poor."

"You!" he exclaimed.

"Me," said Chi. "The greatest disguised deteckative and duke murderer
in two hemispheres. How's crime?"

"You deceived me."

"Professional test," Chi said. "Wanted to see if your education
was solid. You passed. Found a criminal, didn't you?"

"You said them was human bones."

"I said duke bones. A duke disguised as a calf
naturally leaves calf bones. That's the completeness of the disguise."

"You stole food out of Lem's kitchen."

"I removed evidence. Sandwich evidence, pie evidence and a jar
of preserve clues."

Mr. Gubb arose.

"You are under suspicion."

"Fine," Chi said. "Arrest me right away. I ain't ate
since yesterday morning."

Mr. Gubb hesitated. He remembered the former trick.

"No. Your hunger is part of the disguise."

"It's a wonderful disguise," Chi admitted weakly. "Gets more complete
every minute."

"You cannot deceive me twice by the same identical method
again. Lesson Fifteen says never repeat an error unless circumstances
have altered sufficiently to make it a new error."

Chi glanced around.

"Got any crackers?"

"No."

"Cheese?"

"No."

"Cold meat?"

"No."

"Wallpaper paste?"

"Yes."

Chi considered it, but decided that even genius should recognize
limits.

He moved toward Syrilla's photograph and studied it again.

"Say," he remarked, "I seen her lately."

Mr. Gubb's long body became rigid.

"Whom?"

"This lady. The absent one. The dame that weighs backward."

"You could not see Syrilla," Mr. Gubb said. "She is
five pounds less than nothing."

"She looked like plenty more than that to me."

Mr. Gubb came around the desk.

"Where did you see her?"

"At a circus."

"What circus?"

"The Mammoth Combined Colossal Aggregation of Educated Animals, Human Curiosities,
Moral Wonders and Twenty-Seven Kinds of Peanuts. Out near Dayton."

Mr. Gubb placed both hands on Chi's shoulders.

"When?"

"Week ago. Maybe eight days. I don't keep a calendar
except meal times."

"You are sure it was Syrilla?"

"Sure? Bo, you don't mistake a lady that size. She
was sitting on a gold platform under a sign saying
'Syrilla, the Eight-Hundred-and-Ninety-Pound Princess of Plenty.' Folks paid a dime
to look at her and another nickel to watch her
eat a watermelon."

Mr. Gubb's face slowly lost its color.

"Eight hundred and ninety pounds?"

"Advertising pounds," Chi said. "But she was no minus quantity."

"That scale—"

"Fixed, like I told you. Saw the guy that fixed
it, too."

"Jonas Medderbrook?"

"That's him. Slick party. He was managing the wedding."

"What wedding?"

Chi, who had hoped to trade his information for food,
recognized that he had reached the valuable portion. He seated
himself and crossed his legs.

"I could remember better over a meal."

Mr. Gubb opened a drawer, took out thirty cents and
placed it on the desk.

"Tell me."

Chi pocketed the money.

"Samwiches would help."

"What wedding?"

"Her wedding," said Chi. "Syrilla's."

Mr. Gubb gripped the desk.

"To whom?"

"The Living Skeleton."

The room seemed to lengthen around Philo Gubb, although it
was already a narrow room and needed no assistance.

Chi continued, pleased by the attention.

"Skinniest gent I ever saw. Name of Professor Oswald Thinn.
Professional weight forty-one pounds with shoes, watch and a roll
of pennies in each pocket. They was billing it as
the Marriage of the Extremes. Biggest bride and smallest bridegroom
ever joined under one tent. Jonas had the whole scheme
arranged."

Mr. Gubb tried to speak, but no words came.

"Course," Chi said, "there was business reasons. Public loves a
wedding, especially when there's a brass band, two extremes and
a lemonade privilege. They had posters all over the county.
'Will Love Bridge Eight Hundred and Forty-Nine Pounds?' That was
what the posters said."

"Syrilla agreed?" Mr. Gubb asked at last.

"She rehearsed twice. Jonas stood by the ring and told
her when to cry. Skeleton practiced putting on the ring
with both hands, account of his fingers being weak. Pretty
ceremony."

Mr. Gubb sank into his chair.

"But she told me she did not exist."

"Maybe she didn't exist for you," Chi said. "She existed
considerable for the circus."

"And Jonas took my hundred dollars."

"Probably needed it for posters."

The logic was brutal because it was simple. Mr. Gubb
looked at Syrilla's photograph. The crown, the spangles and the
contented expression remained unchanged, but he now perceived behind them
the cold machinery of deceit.

The false scale had not deprived Syrilla of weight. It
had deprived him of Syrilla.

Jonas Medderbrook had not proved her nonexistent. He had merely
arranged matters so that she could vanish from Riverbank and
reappear in Dayton at an increased professional poundage.

Mr. Gubb's correspondence lessons had warned him against altered footprints,
false whiskers, substituted jewels, forged signatures, counterfeit blood, reversible coats,
trapdoors and hollow canes. Not one had warned him that
a seven-hundred-pound woman could be hidden by announcing that she
weighed less than nothing.

"Did she marry him?" Mr. Gubb whispered.

"Not while I was there. Wedding was scheduled Saturday at
two, directly after the trained seals and before the balloon
ascension. Jonas said he expected a packed tent."

Mr. Gubb covered his eyes.

Chi waited respectfully for several seconds.

"About them samwiches—"

Mr. Gubb took his hands from his face.

"Go," he said.

Chi looked at him and, for once, saw that invention
would add nothing useful.

"Sorry, bo," he said. "Women is uncertain. That's why Shermlock
never married, except three times in disguise."

Mr. Gubb did not answer.

Chi went out, bought six sandwiches with the thirty cents
and continued east.

For some time Mr. Gubb sat alone. Evening darkened the
front window. Across the street a boy rolled a hoop
and laughed when it struck a hitching post. From the
hotel dining-room came the smell of beef stew. A train
whistled beyond the river.

Mr. Gubb removed Syrilla's photograph from the wall.

He considered tearing it, but could not. He considered turning
it face downward, but that seemed unkind to the woman
who had never existed. At last he replaced it in
the drawer beneath his lessons, between "The Detection of Anonymous
Letters" and "What to Do When Locked in a Cellar."

He then took out the cardboard badge of the Rising
Sun Correspondence School and pinned it more firmly to his
vest.

Professionally, he had prospered. He had captured Lem, recovered the
stolen calf, collected fifteen dollars, achieved mention in the newspaper
and completed Lesson Thirteen. He had solved a crime while
hunting a murder that had never occurred, guided by a
murderer who had murdered nobody, to bones concealed by a
thief who confessed before being accused of theft.

Such a triumph might have made a less serious man
laugh.

But Mr. Gubb thought of Syrilla, alive, enormous and preparing
to marry the Living Skeleton beneath Jonas Medderbrook's profitable canvas.

He never laughed again.

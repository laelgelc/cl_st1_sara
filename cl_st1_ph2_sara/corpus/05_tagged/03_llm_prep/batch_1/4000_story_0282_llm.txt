Dr. Mercer was a psychoanalyst and had been one for
twenty years. When I knew him he was a thin
man with heavy eyelids and a neck too small for
his collar. He had a habit of putting two fingers
between the cloth and his throat, as though the collar
choked him. The habit became more noticeable when he was
tired.

We walked one evening through a park at the edge
of the city. It was early spring. The grass was
gray and wet, and here and there small leaves had
begun to show on the bushes. Beyond the trees there
were factories, and beyond the factories a strip of lake
shone like metal.

The doctor had spent the day listening to people. A
woman believed her husband wanted to poison her. A clerk
could not enter an elevator without crying out. There had
also been a boy who washed his hands fifty or
sixty times a day.

"I listen and listen," the doctor said. "I go down
into them. That is what they pay me to do.
They ask me to go into the dark places where
they themselves are afraid to go."

He took hold of my arm. His fingers trembled.

"Do you know what happens? I bring things out with
me. I tell myself I am trained, that I am
only observing, but I bring things out. Their fears cling
to me. Their dead fathers and mothers follow me home."

"You are tired," I said.

He stopped and stared. A sneer came upon his face.

"Yes, you are clever. You put things aptly. The doctor
is tired. Give him a holiday. Send him to the
sea."

He turned away, walked several steps, and came back.

"It is not weariness. Or it is weariness, but not
of the body. Think of a young tree in a
forest. Old vines have wrapped themselves about it. The vines
were there before the tree. They climb it and feed
upon it. Presently the tree cannot breathe.

"That is what we are. Ideas planted by dead people
grow in us. Beliefs, shame, duty, the fear of touch,
the fear of loneliness. They twist around our lives. Then
people come to me and say, 'Cut me free.' What
am I to do? I have vines of my own."

His voice had become shrill. The cords stood out in
his throat.

"I want to escape," he said. "I want to be
clean. I want to begin without all the crawling crawling
things men have put into one another."

I tried to quiet him. It was my weakness then,
as it has often been, to find a sentence that
would put a wall around another person's distress.

"All suffering comes from the desire to be loved," I
said. "Perhaps if you keep that in mind—"

Again he sneered.

"You understand, then?"

"No."

"But you have said it. All suffering comes from the
desire to be loved. Very fine. Very complete."

He walked away into the dusk. Under the trees his
figure became small. Then, like a man who had forgotten
something, he returned.

"There is something in life that will not be named,"
he said more quietly. "You approach it with a fine
phrase and it slips away. Remember that."

I did remember. Years afterward, when the painter LeRoy told
me of the woman from Iowa, the doctor's fingers seemed
again to tug at his collar.

LeRoy was not a successful painter. He lived in rooms,
borrowed money, and talked of going to France. He was
long and narrow, with red hair beginning to thin over
his temples. One leg was shorter than the other, and
when he hurried he moved like a wounded bird trying
not to show its hurt.

At the time of the Iowa woman he lived in
a boardinghouse on the north side of Chicago. The house
stood in a street of narrow buildings with stone steps
and dirty windows. There were lace curtains in the parlor,
artificial flowers on the dining table, and a smell of
boiled cabbage that entered everything.

The woman's name was Clara Voss. She was nearly thirty
and taught piano to children. She had come from a
town surrounded by cornfields, a place where her father owned
a store and spoke little. Her mother had died when
Clara was twelve. There were four daughters in the house
and no sons. Men entered only as customers or as
callers for the older sisters, and then they sat stiffly
in the parlor while the clock ticked.

From girlhood Clara had been told that men wanted one
thing and that a decent woman must not permit it.
At the same time she was told that without a
husband a woman's life was empty. The two ideas grew
together in her. She desired what she had been taught
to fear, and feared what she had been taught she
must desire.

This is easily said. It is even neat. I have
made two sentences, and Clara's youth appears explained.

LeRoy would have objected.

"You weren't there," he told me. "You didn't see her
hands."

Her hands were what he first remembered. They were large,
pale hands with blunt fingers. When she played the piano
they were strong and sure, but when a man spoke
to her they flew to her throat or twisted together
in her lap.

She came to Chicago because she had imagined that life
there would open. There would be concerts, streets full of
people, lights, young men who read books. Love would come,
but it would come in some form different from the
awkward courtships she had seen at home. It would be
noble and safe. It would understand her before touching her.

There were five men in the boardinghouse. They were ordinary
young men—a salesman, a bookkeeper, two students, and LeRoy. At
first they paid little attention to Clara. She ate quietly,
wore dark dresses, and went out each morning carrying music
books.

Then she began to talk.

She asked the bookkeeper whether he ever felt lonely in
the city. She stood beside the salesman on the front
steps and asked him to walk with her. She entered
LeRoy's room one afternoon to look at his pictures and
remained for an hour. Her face became bright when the
men gathered about her at dinner. She laughed too loudly.
Once, when a student told a foolish story, she put
her hand on his sleeve and left it there.

The men thought her eager for love.

That was their phrase. They whispered it in the hall.
The salesman offered to take her to the theater. On
the way home he tried to kiss her in the
shadow beside the house.

Clara struck him across the mouth.

The next evening she sat near him and asked whether
he was angry. She spoke softly and looked into his
face. When he touched her hand she jumped up, upsetting
a lamp.

With the bookkeeper it was the same. She asked him
to walk beside the lake and spoke of marriage, children,
and the sadness of returning at night to an empty
room. He put his arm about her. She screamed and
ran from him.

The young men began to hate her. Nothing angers some
men more than a woman who awakens desire and then
is afraid of the desire she has awakened. They said
she was playing with them. They called her cold, sly,
hungry, unnatural. Each word relieved them of the need to
wonder about her.

LeRoy also wondered very little at first.

"She needs a man," he thought. "Once she has been
taken and knows there is nothing terrible in it, she
will become simple."

He told me this without defending himself.

"I was a fool," he said. "I thought the body
was a locked door. Open it and the prisoner walks
out. That was my wisdom."

Clara was not beautiful in the usual way. She had
a narrow face and light eyebrows. Her shoulders stooped, and
when she crossed a room she took short, careful steps.
Yet at certain moments her face changed. Hope came into
it suddenly and made her young. A man seeing that
hope felt chosen. He thought she was offering him entrance
into a secret life.

Then he moved toward her, and the hope became terror.

The landlady, Mrs. Dorgan, was a broad woman whose husband
had died and left her the house. She believed in
decency because decency kept a boardinghouse quiet. She had watched
Clara on the steps with the salesman. She had heard
the lamp fall. More than that, she had heard the
talk of the men.

One night she went to Clara's room.

"You'll have to leave Saturday," she said.

Clara stood beside the bed in her nightdress. Her hair
hung in two long braids.

"What have I done?"

"I don't want trouble."

"What trouble?"

"You know."

Clara did know—or she thought she knew. The old words
returned. Indecent. Forward. A woman who asks for what she
must refuse. She began to tremble.

"I have nowhere to go," she said.

"There are other houses."

"They will be the same."

Mrs. Dorgan shrugged. She had bills to pay and reputations
to consider. She left the room.

Clara dressed hurriedly. It was after eleven. The men had
gone upstairs, and the house had become quiet. She stood
in the hallway for a long time. Then she went
to LeRoy's door.

He was awake, sitting before an unfinished painting. He had
painted a field under rain, and in the field a
small dark figure bent against the wind.

Clara came in and closed the door. She wore a
gray dress without a belt. Her hair was still in
braids.

"What is it?" he asked.

She looked at the bed and then at him.

"Take me," she said.

He did not understand.

"She is putting me out. They all know what I
am. You know too. Take me now."

She began unfastening the buttons at her throat. Her fingers
worked desperately but could not manage them.

LeRoy rose.

"Clara, stop."

"Why? You wanted me. All of you wanted me. I
am tired of being afraid."

She came toward him, and he saw that her face
was not the face of a woman surrendering to passion.
It was the face of someone preparing to be hurt.
She believed she could end fear by commanding the feared
thing to happen.

He caught her wrists.

"Don't," he said.

At that moment Mrs. Dorgan opened the door. She had
heard Clara in the hallway and followed.

The landlady looked at Clara's loosened dress, at the bed,
at LeRoy's hands upon her wrists. Her mouth hardened.

"So," she said.

LeRoy did not think. The words came out of him
because he wanted to strike down the judgment in the
woman's face.

"We are engaged," he said. "Clara and I are to
be married."

Mrs. Dorgan stared.

"Since when?"

"Since tonight. She came to tell me she would marry
me."

Clara made a small sound.

"I will take care of her," LeRoy said. "You needn't
concern yourself."

The landlady went away. Perhaps she believed him. Perhaps she
did not. The announcement gave her what she needed—a respectable
name for what she had seen.

Clara sank to the floor.

LeRoy knelt beside her. She was not crying. She stared
at the painted field and at the dark figure bent
in the rain.

"You lied," she said.

"Yes."

"You do not want to marry me."

"No."

"You do not even want me."

He could not answer that simply. He had desired her.
The desire remained in him, confused now with pity and
fear.

He found her a room the next day in another
house. For several weeks he visited her. They walked in
the park or sat beside the lake. Sometimes she talked
rapidly of her childhood. Sometimes she said nothing. If he
came too near she moved away, but if he missed
an evening she sent a note asking whether he had
grown tired of her.

He tried to explain her to herself.

"You have been taught to be ashamed of your body,"
he said. "That is the trouble."

She looked at him coldly.

"Is that all?"

"No. Not all."

"You think if I slept with you I would be
cured."

"I did think that."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know."

She laughed. There was no pleasure in the laugh.

"You are very smart, LeRoy. You say things so well."

One evening they stood on a pier. The lake was
dark, and the lights along the shore shook in the
water. Clara took his hand.

"I want someone to love me for a long time
before he wants anything," she said. "Long enough for me
to believe him. Do you understand?"

"I think so."

"No, you don't. Neither do I."

She released his hand.

What she required was not a dramatic rescue. She did
not need to be seized, awakened, conquered, or instructed. She
needed love repeated patiently through days and years, love that
could endure her advance and her retreat, her hunger and
her fear. She needed someone who would not punish her
for being unable to give what she had been taught
to withhold.

Perhaps no man could have given it. Perhaps she could
not have received it. That is the part hidden from
us.

LeRoy did not become her lover. He was poor, restless,
vain about his work, and afraid of promises. His false
engagement had been an impulse, not a revelation of heroic
character. There were days when he wished Clara would surrender
to him and end the confusion. There were other days
when he wished she would go away.

At last she did go.

Her father became ill, or she said he had. She
packed her music books and returned to Iowa. At the
station she kissed LeRoy on the cheek. Because the kiss
was freely given, it seemed to him more intimate than
anything that had happened between them.

"Will you come to see me?" she asked.

He said he would. He never went.

For a year they wrote to each other. Then her
letters stopped. LeRoy heard that she taught again in the
town where she had been born. He did not know
whether she married. He did not try to find out.

When he finished the story, we were walking on a
road outside the city. Autumn had come. Dry leaves ran
before us in the wind and gathered in hollows.

"You see what happened," I said. "She changed you."

LeRoy's uneven step quickened.

"Changed me! That is your phrase."

"You began by thinking she needed sex. You learned she
needed love."

He turned upon me. The cords in his neck tightened.

"Love! You say the word and settle everything. What kind
of love? Given by whom? For how long? Must the
giver ask nothing? Can anyone ask nothing? You put a
fine clean label on a dirty bottle."

He walked away down the road. His thin body leaned
into the wind, and because of his limp he seemed
to struggle against something wrapped about his legs.

I waited. Presently he returned.

"After Clara I began to see people differently," he said.
"That is what frightened me. I looked at the salesman
and saw a boy begging to be admired. I looked
at Mrs. Dorgan and saw a widow holding her house
together with rules. I looked at myself and saw a
man who wanted another person's wound to be simple so
he could cure it with his body."

He gripped my shoulders.

"We are all twisted. Do you hear? Not evil—twisted. Old
hands have been at us. Dead voices speak in our
mouths. A mother is afraid and puts fear into her
daughter. A father is ashamed and calls the shame duty.
One lonely person reaches for another, and because both have
been taught wrongly, the reaching becomes a blow."

His voice rose until it was almost a cry.

"You cannot touch anyone without touching all the dead who
planted things in him. Their seeds grow. Their vines tighten.
You think you are embracing one living body, but a
whole graveyard lies between your arms."

"You are tired," I said.

The words escaped me before I could stop them.

LeRoy's face sharpened into the doctor's sneer.

"Yes. Very apt. I am tired. A rest will cure
me. A journey. Sunshine. New scenery."

He let go of me and walked into a field.
The dry weeds reached his knees. Against the pale sky
his narrow body looked weightless.

Then he came back.

"I want to get free of them," he said. "Clara,
Mrs. Dorgan, my father, all the people I have painted
and judged. I want to strip off every idea they
have hung on me. I want to be clean."

The wind lifted the leaves and sent them spinning across
the road. One leaf rose higher than the others. It
went over the ditch, over the weeds, and for a
moment sailed freely toward the dark line of hills.

LeRoy watched it.

"I would like to be that," he said. "Dead, perhaps,
but clean. Blown over the hills. Blown out over the
water. Then dropped somewhere in spring, where nothing old had
begun to grow."

I thought of Dr. Mercer tugging at his collar under
the trees. I thought of Clara's pale hands flying to
her throat. I wanted again to explain them to one
another. I wanted to say that all three suffered from
the same need, that each desired love and feared the
cost of it.

The sentence formed itself in me. It was clear, almost
beautiful.

I did not speak it.

LeRoy had turned away. His limp carried him slowly down
the road, and the leaves went before him, creeping, crawling,
rising at last in the wind.

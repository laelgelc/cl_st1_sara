### The Silver Chain

There was nothing in Antoine Bocaze to arrest the attention
of a young woman who had been taught from childhood
that the world was made to arrest hers.

He was tall, broad through the shoulders, and loose in
the joints; a fisherman of twenty-seven who could manage a
lugger in weather that sent older men ashore to count
their beads, yet who, upon entering a room, was likely
to strike his knee against a chair, overturn a glass,
and stand helplessly before the mischief he had made. The
people of Barataria called him Tonie, as his mother had
done when he was a round-eyed child following her among
the orange trees. They esteemed him because he knew the
passes, the tides, and the temper of the Gulf; they
laughed at him because he seldom knew what to do
with his hands when a woman addressed him.

His face was dark with sun and weather, and appeared
heavier than it was because he carried his head bent,
as if continually watching for some net or rope beneath
his feet. He was not stupid. Père Dufour, the curé,
had once said that Tonie possessed the kind of intelligence
which lives in the fingers and the blood, making no
noise in the head. He could smell a squall beyond
the horizon; he could mend a sail in darkness; he
could tell, from the shiver of a line, whether a
redfish or a drum had taken the hook. But speech
embarrassed him, books wearied him, and the delicate play by
which men and women disclose and conceal themselves was to
him more obscure than any midnight channel through the marsh.

He first saw Claire Duvigné without seeing her.

It was at the little church near Caminada, on a
Sunday in June. The morning was breathless. The palmettos hung
motionless, and the gulls stood with lowered wings along the
hot sand. Within the church, women stirred their fans; children
slept against starched sleeves; and the candles upon the altar
seemed to soften in the heat. Tonie had come late,
with salt still dried white upon his boots, and taken
his place near the door.

At the elevation of the Host, when the congregation had
sunk to its knees, there sounded from the organ loft
a phrase so clear and tender that Tonie raised his
head.

He had heard the instrument before beneath the uncertain hands
of Madame Valtour, who played loudly whenever she lost her
place. This was another thing. The music moved through the
small church like cool water. It mounted among the plaster
saints, trembled above the bowed heads, and seemed for one
instant to open the roof upon a region of blue
light in which sorrow had become sweet and desire was
not yet sin.

Tonie forgot to kneel.

When at last he did so, it was because old
Ma'me Loulou Jolivet, behind him, pulled sharply at his coat.
But his eyes remained fixed upon the loft. He could
see only a white sleeve, the edge of a pale
cheek, and a small hand moving over the keys.

After Mass the Duvigné party came out beneath the tamarinds.
They had arrived from New Orleans to pass the summer
at Grand Isle, with trunks enough, Ma'me Loulou declared, to
clothe all Lafourche Parish. Madame Duvigné was large and handsome,
with a complexion protected through many summers by veils and
parasols. Her niece carried a pug dog. Her son Raoul
smoked cigarettes and looked tired of everything. Around Claire were
already gathered two young men from the city, a sugar
planter's son from St. James, and Captain Lucien Maurin, whose
white uniform had the happy effect of making all other
masculine clothing appear accidental.

Claire was nineteen. Her dress was of some thin gray
stuff scattered with white flowers, and a blue ribbon encircled
her waist. Tonie, who understood neither fabrics nor fashions, saw
only that she resembled the sky before sunrise, when light
is present but has not declared itself. Her hair was
brown, with warm glints in it; her mouth was quick
to smile; and her eyes, when she turned them upon
a person, had the flattering habit of appearing for that
moment wholly occupied with him.

"Who played?" Tonie asked Ma'me Loulou.

She looked at him with surprise. He did not commonly
ask questions after Mass.

"Why, Mamzelle Claire. They say she studied with a professor
in the city. You did not hear the mistakes?"

"No."

Ma'me Loulou laughed. "Then you heard better than Madame Duvigné,
who counted every one."

Claire passed near them. Her skirt brushed the dried grass.
She was listening to something Captain Maurin said, yet she
glanced aside and saw Tonie. It was no more than
a glance—casual, bright, already departing. Tonie took off his hat.
In doing so, he let it fall. One of the
city young men smiled. Claire did not; or, if she
did, it was kindly and not at him.

That kindness completed what the music had begun.

From the hour in which Tonie heard Claire Duvigné play
the organ, the ordinary arrangement of his life became disordered.
He continued to fish, because habit and necessity are strong
even in men who believe themselves delivered over to passion;
but he fished badly. He forgot where he had set
his lines. He let a basket of shrimp spoil in
the sun. Once he sailed halfway toward Leeville before remembering
that his mother expected him to carry flour home from
the store.

"Tonie, you got fever?" Ma'me Antoine asked.

She was a spare, brown woman, energetic despite her sixty
years, with eyes that had sharpened through widowhood, storms, debt,
and the rearing of one silent son. She kept a
small house where oleanders crowded the gallery and hens scratched
beneath the bread oven. Tonie lived with her when he
was not sleeping aboard his lugger.

"No, maman."

"You act like fever."

"I'm not sick."

She pressed a hand against his forehead. He endured it
impatiently.

"You don't eat. You don't hear. Yesterday Baptiste Curole fell
drunk in the bayou and near drowned. I told you
three times."

"Did he?"

"He did. And old Zélie dead since Monday."

"Who?"

Ma'me Antoine crossed herself and stared. Tonie had known Zélie
all his life.

The truth was that he saw and heard only Claire.
When he pulled his nets before dawn, she moved with
the first whiteness over the water. When the sun rose,
turning each wet cord to fire, he remembered the blue
ribbon at her waist. The cries of terns became fragments
of the music from the organ. Even the little silver
fish leaping in his boat seemed to him bright and
remote things from the element she inhabited.

He began going to Grand Isle upon excuses so transparent
that no one troubled to expose them. He carried oysters
to the hotel when the hotel had ordered none. He
offered fish at half the price asked by other men.
He brought letters, repaired an oar, delivered a parcel which
might easily have waited for the packet. If questioned, he
looked away and said there was business.

At the hotel the verandas were noisy from morning until
after midnight. Ladies in loose white gowns took coffee while
their children chased one another between the columns. Men discussed
politics, races, sugar, and the chances of a storm. In
the evenings lamps shone through the trees, and music from
the ballroom mingled with the long breathing of the Gulf.

Tonie stayed at the margin of these pleasures, patient as
a heron.

He watched Claire play croquet upon the grass, striking the
ball with more vigor than skill and crying out against
every misfortune. He watched her dance with Raoul, with Captain
Maurin, with young Dalcour of St. James, and with a
dozen others whose names he took pains to learn in
order that he might hate each separately. He saw her
swim in a dark costume, her hair tied beneath a
scarlet kerchief, while the young men splashed and shouted around
her. He saw her ride along the beach at sunset.
He saw her sit beneath a striped awning, embroidering a
flower she never finished.

At times she spoke to him.

"Is the sea rough today, Tonie?"

"No, Mamzelle."

"Will it rain?"

"Not before night."

"Those are beautiful pompano. Did you catch them?"

"Yes, Mamzelle."

He would then go away burdened with happiness and shame:
happiness that she had spoken his name, shame that he
had answered only as any fisherman might. Alone, he devised
phrases of great simplicity and force. He would tell her
that the Gulf was never rough when she looked upon
it; that rain must wait upon her pleasure; that the
fish had come willingly into his net because they were
destined for her table. But when next she addressed him,
his tongue thickened, and he said, "Yes, Mamzelle," or "No,
Mamzelle," and escaped.

People noticed his devotion. It would have been difficult not
to notice a man who remained for three hours beside
a palm trunk merely to watch a tennis game. The
hotel servants laughed; the boatmen teased him; the older women,
who considered the passions of others both comic and nourishing,
discussed him over their café au lait.

"That poor Tonie," said Madame Lebrun. "He looks at her
as if she were the Blessed Virgin."

"The Blessed Virgin did not bathe with six young men,"
observed Ma'me Loulou.

"She is innocent."

"All pretty girls are innocent when there are enough witnesses."

Claire herself knew that Tonie watched her. A woman is
seldom ignorant of adoration when it is offered so continuously,
though she may mistake its quantity, its quality, or the
price which the worshipper secretly expects to receive. His attention
pleased her as the admiration of an unusual but inferior
creature might please: a great dog, perhaps, faithful and shy,
which would permit no other hand to touch it.

She smiled more gently upon him than upon the others.
The gentleness cost her nothing. She had not imagined that
to Tonie it was food, sleep, religion, and promise.

Their boat trip occurred in August.

Madame Duvigné wished for crabs from a bank beyond the
western point. Raoul had agreed to accompany Claire, but at
the last moment a card game detained him. Captain Maurin
had gone shooting. The other young men were sleeping after
a dance. Tonie, who had brought his lugger near the
hotel wharf without any purpose he could have admitted, heard
Claire complain.

"I shall go with Tonie," she said. "He is safer
than all of you."

The words nearly deprived him of the ability to make
the boat safe.

He spread a clean sailcloth for her to sit upon
and helped her down. Her gloved hand rested for an
instant in his palm. It was light, warm, and alive.
He released it quickly, fearing that she would perceive the
violent beating which had begun in his chest and seemed
to shake his whole body.

They sailed out under a pale sky. The Gulf was
smooth, swelling slowly beneath them, and the island diminished until
its hotels and oaks lay in a thin line between
water and heaven. Claire removed her hat and held it
upon her knees. The wind loosened strands of hair at
her temples.

For some time she talked. She spoke of the dance,
of Raoul's laziness, of a woman at the hotel who
wore mourning but flirted outrageously, of her desire to return
to New Orleans and her regret that summer was ending.
Tonie listened without replying. Every word entered him less by
its meaning than by the fact of her voice.

"You are very quiet," she said.

He looked at the sail.

"Are you always so quiet?"

"I don't know, Mamzelle."

"You must know."

"I don't talk much."

"So I have observed."

She smiled, but he did not. The boat moved farther
from shore. There were no other sails near them, only
a brown pelican descending heavily toward the water and, far
off, a thread of smoke where a steamer crossed the
horizon.

Claire became conscious of their solitude.

It did not frighten her. She trusted Tonie, as she
had said, and there was in his awkward deference nothing
which society had taught her to fear. Yet, as she
studied his bent face and the large hand resting upon
the tiller, she began to perceive something that had escaped
her amid the games and laughter of the hotel. His
silence was not emptiness. It was constraint. Beneath it pressed
a feeling so immense that speech could not contain it.

"Tonie," she said more softly, "why do you come so
often to the island?"

He raised his eyes.

There are confessions made without eloquence, without movement, almost without
change of countenance, which are nevertheless complete. Claire saw in
that one look the summer as he had lived it:
the journeys invented for her sake, the nights beneath the
hotel lamps, the jealousy, the humility, the hope that fed
upon crumbs. She saw that he loved her. She did
not see that, for him, nothing else remained in the
world.

Her first sensation was triumph; her second, pity; and the
two mingled agreeably. To inspire love had not yet become
commonplace to her. The city young men complimented, pursued, sulked,
and returned; but their desires were divided among ambition, amusement,
vanity, and other women. Tonie's gaze suggested something entire. It
made her feel beautiful in a new and solemn fashion.

"You foolish Tonie," she murmured.

He turned white beneath his brown skin.

Claire looked down at her hands. Around one wrist she
wore a slender silver chain, from which hung a little
medal of Saint Cecilia. It had been bought in Chartres
Street and was worth perhaps five dollars. She unfastened it.

"Give me your hand."

He obeyed.

She laid the chain across his palm. The medal flashed
once in the sunlight.

"You shall keep it," she said. "Because you love music
so much."

He closed his fingers, but he continued looking at her.

It was a pretty action. Claire knew it was pretty.
She had read of ladies giving tokens to knights, of
queens rewarding devotion, of maidens binding ribbons upon the arms
of men who went to war. She imitated the sentiment
without intending the consequence, as young persons often imitate grief,
sacrifice, and passion before life has taught them the weight
of such things.

Tonie put the chain inside his shirt.

"Thank you, Mamzelle."

His voice was almost inaudible.

They reached the crab bank. He did not permit her
to help. He filled the hamper quickly, and they started
back. The wind had freshened; little waves struck the bow
and scattered drops upon Claire's dress. She laughed when one
touched her face. Tonie watched a bead of water slide
from her cheek to her throat.

Then there arose in him a desire so sudden and
terrible that he gripped the tiller until the wood hurt
his hand.

He wished to take her in his arms.

He wished neither to persuade nor to plead. He wished
to seize her, to feel her resist, to press his
mouth upon hers before she could call his name. Beyond
that image lay another, darker and strangely peaceful: the overturned
boat, the cold green water closing above them, her struggling
body held against his until struggle ceased. No Captain Maurin,
no Raoul, no city with its houses and balls and
marriages could follow them there. The Gulf, which had fed
him from childhood, would receive them and hide them together.

Claire, seeing only that his face had altered, asked, "Is
anything wrong?"

"No."

"Are we in danger?"

"No, Mamzelle."

At that moment the Angelus bell sounded faintly from the
island.

The distance reduced it to three strokes of silver in
the wind. Tonie heard it. He saw, with the mechanical
obedience of childhood, his mother's head bending, the fishermen removing
their caps, women pausing in doorways while the prayer passed
over marsh and bay. He crossed himself.

The desire did not vanish; but action withdrew from it.
He kept his eyes upon the island and brought Claire
safely to the wharf.

That night, lying upon the deck of his lugger, he
reproached himself.

He did not call the imagined act murder. Men give
gentler names to crimes which promise the satisfaction of an
absolute passion. He told himself that he had failed in
courage. For one instant she had belonged to no one's
protection but his; the sea had been empty; eternity had
opened. He might have embraced her and gone down with
her, her silver chain between them like a sacrament.

If they were ever alone again, he resolved, he would
not be weak.

They were never alone again.

Whether Claire had been vaguely troubled by the boat ride,
or whether chance merely resumed its ordinary command of events,
Tonie could not determine. She was kind to him afterward,
but always from the center of a group. If she
walked upon the beach, two companions walked with her. If
she went sailing, Raoul or Captain Maurin went also. She
played the organ once more, and Tonie stood at the
church door, trembling beneath the music. But she did not
again ask him to take her beyond the point.

September brought a storm that tore away two galleries and
drove a schooner into the marsh. After it, the visitors
departed. The Duvignés returned to New Orleans. Claire wore a
dark traveling dress. She held out her hand to Tonie
before boarding the packet.

"Good-bye. Do not forget me."

He looked at her in astonishment. Forgetting her seemed to
him less possible than forgetting the sun while it burned
above his head.

"No, Mamzelle."

"You must come to the city."

"Yes, Mamzelle."

The packet moved from the landing. Handkerchiefs fluttered. Claire stood
beside her mother, then beside Raoul, then vanished behind a
pile of trunks. Tonie watched until smoke and distance dissolved
the vessel.

The island afterward appeared to him like a house from
which the dead had been carried. He wandered beneath the
oaks, past the croquet ground and the ballroom with its
closed shutters. Here she had laughed; there she had fastened
her shoe; upon that step she had once rested her
hand. The geography of the place had become a map
of gestures invisible to everyone but him.

He returned to Barataria and grew ill.

There was no fever at first. He ate little, slept
badly, and worked without attention. Then chills came upon him.
His strength, which had seemed a permanent property like the
width of his shoulders, deserted him. He sat for hours
before the house, staring beyond the oleanders toward a strip
of marshy sky.

Ma'me Antoine brewed orange-leaf tea, summoned a traiteur from down
the bayou, and at last brought Doctor Guidry, who listened
to Tonie's chest and asked questions.

"What pains you?"

"Nothing."

"You cough?"

"Sometimes."

"You got trouble in the stomach?"

"No."

The doctor looked at Ma'me Antoine and shrugged. Physicians dislike
maladies which refuse the dignity of a location.

During the winter Tonie went twice to New Orleans with
fish. The city oppressed him. Streets confined the air; wheels
clattered upon stones; bells rang without telling him wind or
tide. Yet Claire was there. This knowledge transformed each crowd
into an expectation.

He walked past the Duvigné house on Esplanade Avenue. It
stood behind an iron fence, with green shutters and a
garden where camellias bloomed. Once he saw Madame Duvigné enter
a carriage. Once he saw Raoul smoking upon the banquette.
He never saw Claire.

He imagined her behind every shutter. He imagined her at
the opera, at Mass in the cathedral, descending a staircase
beneath chandeliers, bending while some polished young man whispered over
her shoulder. Most of all he imagined her married.

That vision destroyed him. The husband had no fixed face.
Sometimes he was Captain Maurin, sometimes Dalcour, sometimes a stranger
endowed with all the advantages Tonie lacked: money, education, smooth
hands, easy words, a house in which Claire might play
her music. Tonie saw children about her knees. He saw
her smile upon them with an intimacy no silver chain
could purchase. He saw years accumulating between himself and the
summer at Grand Isle until she would scarcely remember the
fisherman who had taken her for crabs.

He began to think of death, not as he had
thought of it in the boat—a bridal descent, fierce and
triumphant—but as a door through which one exhausted man might
quietly pass. There were nights when the bayou looked black
and near. There were mornings when his gun seemed to
wait in the corner with a certain practical friendliness.

Only his mother prevented him. She moved about the house,
scolding the hens, kneading bread, counting the few coins in
a blue cup. He understood that his death would leave
her alone. The understanding was not always sufficient, but it
delayed him.

In February he carried a load of oysters to the
French Market. Rain had fallen all night, and the city
morning was raw. Gutters overflowed. Mules steamed beneath their harness.
Women in shawls hurried among baskets of vegetables, fish, game,
and early strawberries.

Tonie had finished his business and was drinking coffee at
a stall when he heard two men speaking behind him.

"The Duvigné girl," one said. "You knew her?"

"Claire? Certainly. What of her?"

"Buried yesterday."

Tonie set down his cup.

The saucer broke. Neither man noticed.

They continued. She had taken cold after leaving a concert.
There had been congestion of the lungs. At first no
one considered it dangerous. Then fever, delirium, the priest, the
final sacraments. Three days. Such a young girl. Such a
blow to the family.

Tonie turned.

"Mamzelle Claire Duvigné?" he asked.

The men looked at his fisherman's clothes.

"Yes."

"Dead?"

"Why, yes, my friend."

He left the market. The rain had stopped, and a
watery light lay over the roofs. He walked without choosing
a direction until he reached the river. There he stood
among bales and carts, watching the broad current roll past.

Claire was dead.

The sentence entered him cleanly. There was no room to
doubt, hope, bargain, or invent. She would not marry Captain
Maurin. She would not marry Dalcour. She would not bear
children whose faces resembled another man's. No hand would possess
the hand that had given him the chain. No one
would awaken beside her, hear her careless morning speech, watch
her hair turn gray.

A deep breath lifted Tonie's chest.

He was glad.

The gladness horrified him only for an instant. Then it
spread through his weakened body like warmth. He looked at
the river and saw it truly for the first time
in months—the tawny water, the gulls wheeling above the wharves,
the smoke lifting from steamers. Hunger seized him. He returned
to the market and ate bread, sausage, and another bowl
of coffee.

Within a week his cough diminished. His sleep returned. By
March his arms had recovered their strength, and he hauled
nets that had burdened him in January. Ma'me Antoine watched
the change with gratitude and suspicion.

She had heard of Claire's death from Ma'me Loulou. She
waited for Tonie to speak. He did not.

One evening she cooked a court-bouillon of redfish, rich with
tomato, onion, and pepper. Tonie ate twice, wiped the plate
with bread, and asked if there was more. His mother
placed the pot before him and sat down opposite.

"You know Mamzelle Duvigné dead?"

"Yes."

"When you know?"

"In the city."

She studied him.

"You don't grieve?"

"I grieved before."

"Before she dead?"

"Yes."

Ma'me Antoine folded her hands upon the table. "Loulou says
you loved that girl."

Tonie drew the silver chain from beneath his shirt. He
had worn it there through fever, rain, sleep, and labor.
The medal of Saint Cecilia was darkened by salt and
the heat of his body.

His mother's eyes widened.

"She give you that?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"She knew."

Ma'me Antoine crossed herself. "And now she dead, you get
well."

"Yes."

"You glad?"

"Yes, maman."

She rose so abruptly that the chair scraped the floor.

"Antoine!"

He looked at her calmly. It was the calmness that
alarmed her. She had expected tears, denial, perhaps anger; not
this restored color, this steady appetite, this rude and peaceful
strength.

"You must not say such a thing. It is a
mortal sin to be glad for the death of that
poor child."

"I did not kill her."

"But you glad!"

Tonie considered. Speech remained difficult for him, yet the truth
within him had become simple.

"When she was living," he said, "I could not rest.
Every day I thought, maybe she marry. Maybe this man,
maybe another. A rich man. A gentleman. Somebody could talk
to her and dance with her. Somebody she could love."

His mother said nothing.

"I saw the house," he continued. "I saw the children.
I saw her forget me. It was like a hook
here." He pressed his fist against his breast. "All the
time. Pulling."

"Tonie—"

"Now nobody can have her."

Ma'me Antoine recoiled, but he went on with the grave
patience of a man explaining weather to one who has
never sailed.

"She is with God. There are no rich people with
God. No fishermen, either. No Grand Isle hotel, no fine
house in the city. Souls don't have money. They don't
wear good coats. Père Dufour says all stand the same."

"The saints stand above us."

"She was not a saint," Tonie said, and then, fearing
disrespect, added, "Not yet."

Outside, dusk had settled upon the bayou. Frogs called from
the reeds. A pirogue passed, its paddle dipping with soft
regularity. From a distant house came the crying of a
child and a woman's voice singing it quiet.

Tonie looked toward the window.

"Maybe there she can see," he said.

"See what?"

"That I loved her best."

His mother sank slowly into the chair.

"She knew you loved her."

"No. She knew a little. She thought it was a
little thing."

He touched the chain.

"I could not tell her. If I talked, it came
out wrong. But when I die, I won't need words.
She will know all of it. And there won't be
any man to say she belongs to him."

Ma'me Antoine's lips moved in a prayer. Whether she prayed
for Claire's soul or her son's, she could not have
said.

"Tonie, the dead belong to God."

"Yes," he answered. "That is better."

There was no reasoning beyond this. His consolation had taken
the shape of faith, and faith, when built by desire,
may support structures which neither charity nor doctrine would willingly
inspect. He had surrendered Claire's body because death had placed
it beyond rivalry; he claimed her soul because heaven, as
he conceived it, abolished the distinctions which had excluded him.
His hope was humble in language and boundless in possession.

Ma'me Antoine began to weep.

Tonie looked at her with surprise. Her tears he understood
better than Claire's smiles. He rose and came around the
table.

"Why you cry, maman?"

"You frighten me."

"I'm well now."

"That is what frightens me."

He stood awkwardly beside her. Then he placed one large
hand upon her hair. The gesture recalled his childhood, when
she had bent over him during storms and told him
that thunder was only the wagons of heaven crossing the
clouds.

"I won't leave you," he said.

She caught his wrist. "Promise?"

"I promise."

"You won't go in the bayou? You won't use the
gun?"

He understood that she had known more than he supposed.

"No. I got no reason now."

She wept harder at the reassurance, but Tonie, believing the
matter settled, bent and kissed her cheek.

The next morning he rose before daylight. The air was
cool and smelled of damp earth, ashes, and the faint
salt carried inland from the Gulf. He fed the hens,
split cypress kindling, and crouched before the outdoor oven. Flame
took slowly, then ran blue and gold along the wood.

Ma'me Antoine watched from the gallery, wrapped in a shawl.

Beyond the house, the eastern sky opened in long bands
of pearl. Birds stirred in the orange trees. Somewhere across
the marsh a church bell began to sound the Angelus,
each note traveling alone through the mist.

Tonie uncovered his head.

Beneath his shirt, the silver medal rested against his heart.
He saw Claire as he had first imagined her: not
among dancers or admirers, not in a city house beside
an unknown husband, but above him in a region of
music and clear light, waiting without impatience because eternity permitted
no lateness. There she would at last look steadily upon
him. There she would understand. And there, where wealth, speech,
beauty, and birth had fallen away, she could not prefer
another merely because another had known how to ask.

The bell ceased.

Tonie put on his hat and turned back to the
oven. He arranged the burning wood with care, tested the
heat upon his palm, and called to his mother that
it would soon be ready for the bread.

Then, hearing her cough in the morning chill, he went
into the house to bring her shawl more closely about
her shoulders.

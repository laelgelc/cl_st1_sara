### THE DRESS QUESTION

Helen Maynard sat reading near an open window, with one
hand resting upon the volume and the other lying, almost
motionless, among the folds of her gray silk dress. The
declining sun touched her placid forehead, and gave warmth to
a face whose chief beauty was not in color or
regularity, but in its expression of thoughtful kindness. Nothing in
her apparel sought attention. A narrow collar of fine lace
and a small brooch were its only ornaments; yet no
one of cultivated perception could have looked upon her without
receiving an impression of refinement.

The door opened quickly, and her niece entered.

"Well, Aunt Helen!" exclaimed Alice, advancing with a light step
and turning herself round before the window. "Am I not
splendid?"

Splendid she certainly was, if brightness and abundance constitute splendor.
Her white dress was crossed and recrossed with rose-colored ribbons;
pearls encircled her throat, bracelets glittered upon both arms, and
a spray of artificial flowers, mingled with silver leaves, rested
among the curls at one side of her head. A
jewelled clasp confined a scarf upon her shoulder, while another
cluster of flowers ornamented her bosom. She had the appearance
of a beautiful girl whom some mischievous fairy had condemned
to carry upon her person the contents of a milliner's
window.

Helen closed her book, but did not immediately speak.

"You are looking at me as if I were a
picture," said Alice, smiling, though a little uneasily. "I hope
you approve the frame."

"I was looking at you, my dear," replied Helen, "and
wishing that I could see rather more of Alice and
rather less of her decorations."

The smile faded.

"Then you do not like my dress?"

"I like the material. It is delicate and suitable. But
you have burdened it with ornaments until the eye finds
no repose."

"Mrs. Linton said the ribbons were charming."

"They are charming in themselves."

"And Clara chose the flowers."

"They also are pretty."

"And the pearls were mamma's."

"I value them for that reason."

"Then what can be wrong?" asked Alice, with a slight
elevation of the brows. "Everything is pretty, and yet the
whole displeases you. That seems scarcely reasonable."

"Many agreeable sounds, uttered at the same instant, do not
necessarily make music," said Helen. "Harmony depends less upon the
separate sweetness of each tone than upon the just relation
of all. Your ribbons, flowers, pearls, bracelets, scarf and clasp
are each emulous of ornament; not one consents to be
subordinate, and poor Alice is lost in the contention."

The young girl's face crimsoned.

"I did not expect ridicule from you, Aunt Helen."

"Nor have I intended it. You asked for my judgment,
and affection would be but a selfish sentiment if it
feared to speak sincerely."

"I asked whether I looked splendid."

"And I answer that you do. But splendor is not
always beauty, nor effect always taste."

Alice walked to the mirror and surveyed herself. Until that
moment she had been entirely satisfied. The reflection had seemed
brilliant, picturesque, even triumphant; for she had dressed with the
secret hope that, among the ladies at Mrs. Hartley's party,
no one would be more observed. Now, as she looked
again, the flowers appeared larger, the ribbons broader, and the
bracelets more numerous than they had appeared ten minutes before.

"I cannot go looking like a nun," she said, impatiently.

"Certainly not. A young girl should be dressed like a
young girl. Simplicity need not be severity; and modesty, my
dear Alice, is very far from gloom."

"But all the ladies ornament themselves."

"All women adorn themselves in some degree; yet there are
two classes among those who enter social assemblages—the women who
consult taste in dress, and the women who study effect.
The first desire that their apparel shall be appropriate to
themselves; the second desire chiefly that it shall compel observation.
One is willing to be remembered after she has been
known; the other would be noticed before she has spoken."

"And you think I belong to the second class?"

"I think your present dress expresses the wish of that
class."

Alice looked down. A sigh, half anger and half mortification,
escaped her.

"You are very severe."

"No, my dear; I am very much your friend. A
stranger might praise you, because praise costs nothing and wins
a smile. I would rather risk your displeasure than permit
you to expose yourself to the quiet criticism of sensible
persons."

"I do not care for their criticism."

"Do you not?"

There was something in Helen's voice which brought a deeper
color to Alice's cheek. She did care. More than this,
there was one sensible person whose criticism had occupied her
thoughts throughout the day.

Helen resumed, gently—

"Mr. Benton, I have heard you say, admires intelligence and
good taste."

"I did not mention Mr. Benton."

"No; but your face did."

"Aunt Helen!"

"My dear child, you need not be ashamed. He is
a young man of estimable character. But if you would
gain the regard of a man who values moral and
mental attractions, do not appeal to him through a multitude
of ribbons. A butterfly may draw his eye for a
moment; it is not therefore chosen for a companion."

Alice turned again to the glass.

"Perhaps the flowers are too much," she said.

"The flowers, the scarf, and two or three other things."

"Two or three! You would strip me of everything."

"I would strip you only of that which hides you."

After a brief contest, in which vanity lost ground by
slow degrees, Alice went to her chamber. She removed the
flowers first, then the ribbons, then, with a sigh more
profound than either sacrifice deserved, the glittering bracelets. At Helen's
suggestion she laid aside the elaborate dress altogether and put
on another of soft pearl-colored silk. Its graceful folds revealed
the beauty of her figure without attracting attention to it;
fine lace softened the neck and sleeves; and a single
diamond, suspended by a slender chain, gave light without display.
Her hair, relieved of silver leaves and artificial roses, was
arranged with studied plainness.

When she returned, Helen's face brightened.

"You are beautiful now, Alice."

"I am very insignificant," replied the young lady.

"You are reposeful. There is a difference."

"I shall look like a gray cloud among beds of
tulips."

"Then those who have wearied their eyes upon the tulips
may be thankful for the cloud."

Alice could not help smiling; but her heart was not
wholly reconciled. She went to the party obedient to her
aunt's judgment rather than convinced by it, and all the
way she imagined the brilliant figures that would surround her,
and the comparative plainness in which she must enter among
them.

Mrs. Hartley's rooms were crowded. Light flashed from chandeliers upon
bare shoulders, jeweled heads and silken dresses of every hue.
Alice had scarcely crossed the threshold before she saw a
lady in crimson velvet, richly apparelled, standing near the piano.
Diamonds sparkled in her dark hair; a golden vine wound
about one arm; and her dress, ornamented with black lace
and small pendent jewels, gave to her appearance something at
once striking and theatrical.

Beside her stood Mr. Benton.

He was listening with attention, and the lady was smiling
up into his face. Alice's heart sank. At that moment
he turned his eyes toward her, but, whether from the
distance or from the subdued character of her dress, gave
no sign of recognition.

"So Aunt Helen was wrong," thought Alice. "He does not
even know me."

She passed into the next room, where she was soon
joined by Clara Linton.

"My dear Alice!" cried Clara. "What have you done with
your beautiful dress?"

"I changed it."

"And the rose ribbons?"

"Abandoned."

"The silver leaves?"

"Condemned."

"And all your bracelets? Alice, you have been robbed!"

"Not entirely," said Alice, trying to laugh.

Clara drew back and examined her.

"Well, it is very elegant, certainly; but I should never
have known that diamond was there if I had not
looked for it."

"Perhaps that is no great misfortune."

Clara had already turned to watch the crimson velvet. "There
is Miss Harwood. Is she not magnificent? Every gentleman has
looked at her."

Alice looked also. Mr. Benton still remained near Miss Harwood,
and a foolish regret began to trouble her. What if
men, whatever they said of modesty and intelligence, were governed
after all by color, jewels and gay plumage? What if
Aunt Helen's excellent principles belonged to quiet parlors, but had
little authority beneath chandeliers?

For several minutes she was silent. Then a feeling better
than wounded vanity arose within her.

"If Mr. Benton can value me only when I am
covered with flowers," she thought, "his preference is not worth
gaining. I will not be a signboard hung with bright
objects to call the eyes of passers-by. If I am
noticed to-night, it shall be as myself; and if I
am not, I shall at least preserve my self-respect."

This resolution restored the natural expression of her countenance. She
ceased to watch Miss Harwood and entered into conversation with
an elderly gentleman who stood near her. He had been
speaking of a recent book of travels, which Alice had
read; and her intelligent observations soon drew others into the
circle. She possessed a quick mind, a lively imagination, and,
when not occupied with the impression she was making, a
peculiarly engaging manner. Thought gave animation to her eyes; benevolence
softened her smile; and the tasteful quietness of her dress
permitted these better attractions to be seen.

Men and women see down into our states of feeling
with wonderful quick intuitions. Self-consciousness repels even when accompanied by
beauty; but a sincere interest in others creates around an
ordinary face a charm which ornament cannot supply. Alice had
not been speaking ten minutes before three or four persons
were listening with pleasure.

"I had no idea Miss Maynard conversed so well," said
a lady behind her.

"Because you had never before heard her converse," replied another.
"She generally wears something so conspicuous that one remembers the
dress and forgets the woman."

"How admirably she is apparelled this evening!"

"So simple, and yet so complete. There is not one
ornament too much."

Alice heard the last words, and the warm blood rose
to her cheeks. They were pleasant, but their chief value
lay in the confirmation they afforded to Helen's judgment.

A few moments afterward Mr. Benton approached.

"I owe you an apology, Miss Maynard," he said. "When
you entered, I looked toward you without recognizing you. You
appeared so unlike—and, permit me to add, so much more
like yourself than usual."

Alice smiled. "The distinction is delicate, Mr. Benton."

"I fear it is awkwardly expressed. I mean that I
have sometimes seen your natural character obscured by—"

"By ribbons and flowers?"

"Since you have named the offenders, I need not. This
evening there is nothing to interrupt the expression of your
face."

"Aunt Helen would consider that a triumph."

"Then I am indebted to your aunt?"

"For nearly everything I am not wearing."

"I shall regard her as a public benefactor."

The sincerity of his tone left no doubt of his
meaning. From that time he remained frequently at Alice's side.
He asked her to dance; afterward he brought her an
ice; later they stood near a window and spoke of
books, music, and the charities in which Mrs. Hartley was
interested. Alice discovered that she had never before conversed with
him so freely. On other occasions she had been watching
the effect of her dress, wondering whether he saw a
new bracelet or approved a bright color. Now, relieved from
the labor of exhibiting herself, she listened, answered, and forgot
to be charming; for which reason she charmed him more
than ever.

Miss Harwood passed them once, her crimson train sweeping broadly
behind her. She was still splendid; but the freshness of
the first impression had departed. One looked again at the
velvet, the diamonds, and the golden vine, and found nothing
more. A richly decorated apartment may excite admiration upon entrance;
yet, if there be no warmth upon the hearth and
no books upon the table, we do not desire to
remain in it.

Before the evening closed, Mrs. Hartley said to Alice—

"My dear, you have been one of the most admired
young women in the room."

Alice glanced instinctively toward Miss Harwood.

"I do not mean one of the most stared at,"
continued Mrs. Hartley, with a smile. "That is a very
different distinction."

When Alice returned home, she found Helen awaiting her. The
gray silk had been exchanged for a dressing-gown, but the
same peaceful expression rested upon her face.

"Well?" she said.

Alice knelt beside her chair.

"You were right, Aunt Helen."

Helen laid a hand upon her hair. "Touching the dress?"

"Touching everything. At first I was miserable. Mr. Benton did
not recognize me, and I saw him beside the most
magnificently dressed woman in the room."

"And then?"

"Then I resolved that if he preferred magnificence, he might
have it. After that I forgot myself, and somehow everybody
became agreeable."

"That is often the way, my dear. We receive most
freely when we cease demanding."

"Several persons praised my dress. Mrs. Hartley said I was
admired. And Mr. Benton—" Alice paused.

"Yes?"

"He said I looked more like myself than usual."

"A very discriminating compliment."

"He remained with me nearly the whole evening."

Helen smiled, but did not triumph. True affection is never
emulous of victory; it seeks correction, not humiliation.

"You have learned," she said, "that simplicity does not conceal
beauty. It removes the obstructions through which beauty is imperfectly
seen. Dress should be the quiet servant of a woman,
not the noisy herald that walks before her."

"I shall never wear an ornament again."

"That would be merely the opposite error. Good taste does
not consist in rejecting beautiful things, but in using them
with fitness and moderation. Pearls and flowers have their proper
places. Vanity begins when we ask them to express what
character alone can reveal."

Alice took the single diamond from her neck and held
it thoughtfully in her hand.

"We wear jewels outside," she said, "because people cannot see
those within."

"The finer jewels," replied Helen, "are not displayed at once.
They are shut within the casket of the mind, and
only time, trial and familiar intercourse disclose their value. But,
believe me, Alice, they are worth the seeking."

The next evening, a little after seven o'clock, the servant
entered the parlor with a card. Alice, who was sewing
near her aunt, received it; and the color mounted instantly
to her forehead.

Helen did not ask the visitor's name. She had read
it already in Alice's face.

"Mr. Benton is below," said Alice, endeavoring to speak with
indifference.

"So I inferred."

"What shall I wear?" The question escaped before she reflected;
and then, meeting Helen's amused glance, she laughed. "No, I
will not change anything."

She wore a plain blue dress, with a narrow white
collar and no ornament whatever. Yet, as she went down
to meet him, happiness brightened her eyes and hope gave
lightness to her step. There are decorations which no jeweller
can furnish and no fashion prescribe.

Helen resumed her book, though for some time she did
not turn a page. When, an hour later, she heard
Alice's cheerful voice in the hall and Mr. Benton asking
permission to call again, she smiled quietly to herself.

That second visit removed all doubt from the dress question.

## THE LAW IN PROUDFOOT'S CAVE

A dead chestnut bough snapped somewhere below the laurel.

Dan Kerry stopped so abruptly that the muzzle of his
revolver struck a rhododendron stem and sent a little shower
of rain-water down his sleeve. For ten seconds he stood
there, one boot braced upon the wet shoulder of the
mountain, listening with every faculty he possessed; which was no
inconsiderable number, though patience was not generally included among them.

Nothing followed the sound.

Above him rose the black ribs of the Nantahala range,
swathed in blue vapour and autumn forest. Below, through rents
in the foliage, he caught uncertain flashes of a creek,
twisting white over its stones; farther still, range beyond range
sank into the smoky afternoon until earth and cloud were
one. A trumpet-creeper, flaming upon a dead pine, tossed scarlet
torches in the wind. The scene was majestic, solitary, almost
indecently peaceful; but Kerry, whose artistic temperament had survived police
headquarters, bad coffee, and ten days of mountain travel, regarded
it at present chiefly as an excellent place in which
to be shot from behind.

"Big game, is it?" he whispered. "Dan, me lad, ye've
hunted rabbit with less noise and more courage."

He wiped his palm upon his trousers, changed the revolver
to his left hand, and peered into the laurel.

The chestnut bough had not broken of its own volition.
Its pale, splintered end showed newly against the mould; and
in a patch of red clay beyond it appeared the
front half of a moccasined footprint, broad and deep. Kerry's
heart made one sonorous movement against his ribs.

Andy Proudfoot.

For eight months the printed handbills had decorated sheriffs' offices
from Philadelphia to Asheville: ANDREW PROUDFOOT—MURDER—FIVE HUNDRED DOLLARS REWARD. They
described a man six feet four in height, heavily built,
grey-eyed, with brown curling hair, and "dangerous when approached." The
last phrase had given Kerry a cold pleasure when he
first read it. Five hundred dollars was not ordinarily paid
for approaching harmless persons.

Five hundred dollars! With that sum he could take Katy
Scanlan out of the sour little house on Shunk Street
where her father, inflamed by whiskey and injured dignity, made
every evening a judgment day. He could marry her, buy
two railway tickets, and go West—to Colorado, perhaps, where a
man's past did not sit forever on his doorstep, and
a policeman might become something broader than the arm of
a municipal ordinance.

For Katy's sake he had obtained leave, followed rumours through
Tennessee and Carolina, slept in cabins whose roofs admitted the
stars, eaten corn bread that resisted both tooth and prayer,
and finally learned from an old bear-hunter that smoke had
been seen lifting from a cliff above Little Snowbird. Now,
somewhere within pistol range, was the reward in buckskin breeches.

Another sound came—not below this time, but behind him: a
measured tread, the brushing of leaves, then a low, involuntary
groan.

Kerry wheeled.

The man standing between two chestnuts was even larger than
the handbills had promised. He wore moccasins, brown trousers, a
faded blue shirt open at the throat, and a deerskin
coat flung across one shoulder. A rifle rested in the
hollow of his left arm. His uncovered head, magnificent in
proportion and carriage, bore a tempestuous crest of brown curls;
beneath it the grave face was pale from pain. His
right hand was wrapped in a bloody rag.

Neither man spoke.

The fugitive's eyes travelled quietly from Kerry's boots to his
face, lingering for one instant upon the revolver. Then, with
no more haste than if he had encountered a neighbour
at a mill, he shifted the rifle away from readiness.

"Evenin', stranger," he said.

The voice was deep and far-carrying, with the resonance of
a church bell heard across winter fields.

"Good evenin'," answered Kerry.

"Ye huntin'?"

"I am."

"B'ar?"

Kerry swallowed. "Somethin' bigger."

A flicker, scarcely mirth and scarcely suspicion, touched the mountain
man's eyes.

"B'ar's about the biggest thing hyar," he observed. "Come dark
ye'll find the mounting toler'ble lonesome. My place hain't fur.
Ye're welcome to fire an' vittles."

Hospitality was the first weapon with which Andy Proudfoot disarmed
his pursuer. The second was the bloody hand.

"What happened ye there?" Kerry demanded.

"Rifle busted wrong. Breech-pin tore me."

He lifted the injured member a little. Blood had soaked
through the rag and dripped from one finger.

"Saints above! Let me see it."

Proudfoot's brows rose. "Reckon ye're a doctor?"

"No; I'm worse—I'm a Philadelphy policeman."

The words sprang out by professional habit. Kerry bit his
tongue and added hastily: "Or I was, till I come
off on this fool hunt. Give me the hand, feller.
I know enough surgery to stop a man leakin' away."

If the name of policeman moved Proudfoot, he showed it
only by a slow inclination of the head.

"Cave's up thar," he said.

He led the way around a shoulder of rock which
seemed, until one passed within ten feet of it, to
be solidly clothed in laurel. Behind a curtain of leaves
opened a dark cleft, narrow at the mouth but broadening
within into a dry chamber. The smoke of a small
fire escaped through some invisible chimney. Along one wall lay
fir boughs and two bear-skins; against another stood sacks of
meal, a Dutch oven, a coffee-pot, powder, traps, dried venison,
and such other stores as can transform a hole in
the earth into the rude domestic kingdom of a solitary
man.

Kerry entered with his revolver still in hand.

Proudfoot saw it, and smiled—not derisively, but with a patience
that made the little weapon seem theatrical.

"Lay hit down," he said. "Ye'll need both hands."

The injury was ugly. A sliver of metal had torn
the palm below the thumb and travelled nearly to the
wrist. Kerry heated water, washed the wound, picked out black
grains and shreds of cloth, and bound it with strips
cut from a clean shirt which Proudfoot produced without complaint.
Once, when the probing was severe, the giant's nostrils widened
and a dampness came upon his forehead; but he neither
groaned nor withdrew the hand.

"Ye've the constitution of a courthouse," Kerry said. "And about
as much sense. That'll fester if ye use it."

"Hain't nobody else to use hit."

"There is now. Sit quiet."

He said it with the authority of a mother, a
surgeon, and a patrolman combined. Proudfoot obeyed.

Presently venison was frying in bear grease, coffee bubbled, and
corn pones baked beneath the iron lid. Kerry, who had
eaten nothing since dawn but two apples of such an
astringent nature that they seemed to draw his stomach into
a knot, watched every operation with shining eyes.

"Ye live well for a hermit."

"A man can live toler'ble if he don't crave what
the mounting hain't got."

"There's a philosophy that'd empty half the shops in Chestnut
Street. Though I notice ye have coffee."

"Hit come from Murphy."

"And sugar?"

"A mite."

"Tobacco?"

"Some."

"Then ye do crave what the mountain hasn't got."

Proudfoot looked at him gravely. "A man's philosophy hain't obliged
to rob him complete."

Kerry laughed, and the cave altered with the sound. Until
then it had seemed a den, a place whose shadows
concealed rifles and guilt; now the fire shone companionably upon
the coffee-pot, the folded blankets, the smoke-blackened roof. Proudfoot gave
him the larger pone and the tender portion of venison.
He ate his own supper left-handed, awkwardly but without remark.

Kerry's handcuffs pressed against his waist beneath his coat.

He had only to wait until the wounded man slept.
Proudfoot's rifle stood six feet away. The cave had but
one entrance. A turn of steel about those great wrists,
a leveled revolver, and the five hundred dollars was earned.

Yet while this calculation moved with official precision through one
division of his mind, another watched Proudfoot's injured hand, the
pale fatigue of his face, and the courteous care with
which he pushed the sugar nearer his guest.

"Got folks?" Proudfoot asked.

"One."

"Wife?"

"Not yet."

Proudfoot's eyes fell to the fire.

Kerry was quick to detect a shadow, especially when silence
obliged him to look at it.

"What about yourself?"

"Nary."

The answer was quiet and final.

That night Kerry lay upon a bear-skin with his revolver
under his cheek and the handcuffs hidden beneath a loose
stone at his elbow. For an hour he listened to
Proudfoot's breathing. At length the giant stirred.

"Stranger," he said into the darkness.

"Well?"

"Ef ye aim to shoot me, don't do hit while
I'm asleep. I don't hold with wakin' up dead."

Kerry sat bolt upright. "Who said I aimed—"

Proudfoot laughed softly.

"Go to sleep. Ye talk even when ye hain't speakin'."

Kerry did not sleep for some time.

In the morning Proudfoot's hand was hot and swollen. By
noon fever had drawn a red streak toward the forearm;
and Dan Kerry, who had intended to march his prisoner
into Robbinsville before another sunset, spent the day boiling poultices,
muttering over the incompetence of mountain firearms, and threatening his
patient with instant burial if he presumed to die.

The fever lasted three days.

During those days Proudfoot's strength ebbed astonishingly from him. He
lay upon the fir bed, his great frame extended beneath
a patchwork quilt, while Kerry cooked, carried water, changed bandages,
and listened to broken words which came from depths where
the conscious man had locked them. Once Proudfoot cried, "Euola!"
and lifted both arms as though a woman stood beyond
the fire. Once he cursed a man named Dickert. More
often he begged someone to wait at the old chestnut
ford.

Kerry answered him as one answers a sick child.

"I'm here, Andy. Be easy now. Nobody's goin' without ye."

The first use of the name surprised him; the tenderness
with which it came surprised him more.

On the fourth morning the fever broke. Proudfoot woke sane
and weak, and regarded Kerry from wells of exhausted grey.

"Ye stayed."

"Where would I go? I've not had breakfast yet."

"Reckon I've put ye to trouble."

"Trouble! Ye've had me runnin' like a hotel for lunatics.
There's no trouble in that."

Proudfoot's gaze moved over the cave: the washed cloths drying
by the fire, the pan of broth, the rifle which
Kerry had cleaned, and Kerry himself, unshaven, red-eyed, and wearing
an apron contrived from a meal sack.

"I hain't had much kindness," he said. "Not lately."

"Then ye're out of practice recognizin' ordinary Christian conduct. Drink
this."

Proudfoot drank.

Kerry carried the cup away because his eyes had unaccountably
filled.

The golden September days passed, one flowing into another through
blue mornings and purple dusks. Proudfoot recovered slowly. At first
he could walk only to the cave mouth, where he
sat with his bandaged hand in his lap and watched
hawks wheel over the valley. Later the two men went
together along the ridges. Kerry carried the rifle and missed
squirrels with an extravagance which wounded both his pride and
the ammunition supply.

"Ye pull afore ye sight," Proudfoot told him.

"I do not."

"Ye shut both eyes."

"I close one."

"Both."

"How could ye see me? Ye were watchin' the squirrel."

"I watched hit after ye fired. Hit looked surprised."

Proudfoot took the rifle in his left hand and brought
down the squirrel cleanly from the crown of a hickory.

Kerry glared at him. "Show-off."

A rare smile broadened the mountain man's face, and for
a moment he was younger by ten years.

They gathered wild grapes and chinquapins; they set a snare
for turkey and found it robbed by a fox; they
climbed one windy afternoon to a bald from which the
mountains rolled away in sunlit immensities, blue as smoke and
solemn as the sea. Proudfoot knew every spring, game trail,
bee-tree, and sheltered cove. He knew where ginseng grew under
the poplars and where a wildcat denned among the rocks;
he could read a bear's passage from a turned stone,
and predict rain from the manner in which mist lay
along the Little Snowbird.

In return Kerry told him of Philadelphia: of horse-cars, brick
pavements, market women, policemen's parades, river fog, election nights, oyster
cellars, and the astounding number of human beings who could
inhabit one street while remaining strangers.

"Reckon folks up thar is powerful lonesome," Proudfoot said.

"Lonesome? With three families listenin' through every wall?"

"More folks thar is, less a man gets seed."

Kerry pondered this and concluded it was probably true.

He had always been susceptible to beauty—in music, women, horses,
heroic statuary, and, though he would not have catalogued it
so, men. Proudfoot's physical grandeur delighted him. As health returned,
the fugitive seemed to fill the cave with a virile
and harmonious bulk; his head, with its Jove-like curls, rose
superbly above his shoulders, and his measured motions possessed the
unconscious dignity of a great animal that has never been
taught embarrassment. Yet it was not strength alone that conquered
Kerry. There was gentleness in the giant, a strange, almost
feminine consideration hidden beneath mountain reserve. Proudfoot divided the last
spoonful of sugar evenly. He stepped aside rather than crush
a spider's web. When Kerry blistered his heel, Andy silently
cut a soft piece from his own deerskin coat to
pad the boot.

Every kindness added weight to the handcuffs under the stone.

At night, after Proudfoot slept, Kerry sometimes drew them out.
The steel links glittered in the firelight. They were merely
two bracelets, a chain, a key—five dollars' worth of official
hardware; yet they had become heavier upon his soul than
the mountain itself.

He would remember Katy.

He saw her little face, too pale from anxious evenings;
saw the mark upon her arm where Scanlan had seized
her; heard her whisper, "Take me away, Dan—anywhere at all."
Five hundred dollars would do it. Five hundred dollars lay
asleep ten feet away.

Then Proudfoot would turn upon the bed, his wounded hand
resting open like a child's, and Kerry would thrust the
irons beneath the stone with a sickness of self-contempt.

On the twelfth evening rain enclosed the cave. It swept
the laurel in grey sheets, drummed upon the mountain, and
sent threads of water sparkling across the entrance. Within, the
fire burned bright. Proudfoot roasted a wild turkey; Kerry compounded
biscuits of a density which he defended as "Northern style."

They had eaten and smoked in silence when Proudfoot said:

"Dan, what were ye huntin' the day ye come?"

Kerry's pipe stopped midway to his mouth.

"A bear."

"Ye said bigger."

"A very big bear."

"Walked on two legs, mebbe."

The rain seemed suddenly louder.

Proudfoot turned his face from the fire. There was no
threat in it, only a grave expectancy.

"Why'd ye come hyar, Dan?"

Kerry sprang up.

"I'll tell ye why! Because I'm the dirtiest Judas that
ever drew city pay. I come to take ye, Andy
Proudfoot. There! Ye might as well know it. I'm a
police officer from Philadelphy, and there's five hundred dollars offered
for ye, and I followed ye across three states like
ye were a wolf with a price on the hide."

Proudfoot did not move.

Kerry's voice gathered speed as his shame gathered force.

"I had irons under the stone the whole time—yes, handcuffs!
I meant to slap 'em on ye while ye slept,
wounded as ye were. I'd have marched ye down to
the sheriff and collected the money and called meself clever.
But ye had to feed me. Ye had to give
me your bed, and get near dyin' under me hands,
and turn out to be the finest, gentlest great feller
God ever wasted in a cave! Oh, it's a grand
joke on Officer Kerry!"

He kicked the loose stone aside, snatched up the handcuffs,
and flung them upon the ground between them.

Proudfoot looked at the irons perfunctorily.

"I knowed," he said.

Kerry's mouth remained open. "Ye—what?"

"Knowed ye was police."

"From when?"

"First evenin'."

"How?"

"City boots. Pistol carried wrong fer a hunter. Ye watched
my rifle, then my hands, then the cave mouth. An'
when ye slept, ye kept feelin' under that rock."

Kerry sat down heavily.

"All these days ye knew?"

"All these days."

"And ye let me stay?"

"Ye fixed my hand."

"I might've chained ye!"

"Reckon ye could've tried."

The quiet reply restored to Kerry an understanding which fever
and friendship had obscured: wounded or sound, Andy Proudfoot had
never truly been in his power.

Proudfoot leaned forward, his elbows upon his knees.

"Five hundred's a heap to a workin' man. What did
ye need hit fer?"

Kerry's anger dissolved. He stared at the fire.

"There's a gurrl."

"Thought so."

"Her name's Katy Scanlan. She's got hair black as a
crow's wing, and eyes—well, there's no use describin' eyes to
a man who thinks wildcats handsome. Her mother's dead. Her
father drinks. He beats her when the humour takes him,
and me when I object, though not with the same
success. Katy and me aim to marry, but my pay
barely keeps one person poor. I thought the reward would
carry us West. She wants sunlight and clean air and
a door her father can't come through."

Proudfoot listened without interruption.

"I kept thinkin'," Kerry continued, "that if ye'd killed a
man, ye'd have to answer for it anyhow. The law
wanted ye; I needed the money; Katy needed me. It
fitted neat as a store box—until I met the man
I was packin' into it."

"Ye love her?"

"More than me life."

Proudfoot's bandaged hand closed slowly.

"Then take me."

Kerry blinked.

"Take me down. Git the money."

For an instant only the rain spoke.

Then Kerry rose with such violence that the coffee-pot overturned.

"Take ye!"

"I'm tired, Dan."

"Ye think I'd sell ye now?"

"Ye come fer that."

"I came before I knew ye!"

"Law hain't changed."

"To perdition with the law!"

Proudfoot's eyes narrowed slightly, not in anger but in pain.

"Don't make noise over hit. I been runnin' nigh a
year. Winter in one cave, summer in another; never hearin'
a woman sing, never sleepin' two nights without wonderin' who's
on the trail. I'm tired. Ye've been good to me.
Let hit buy somethin'."

Kerry stood trembling, his freckled face crimson.

"Andy, if ye speak of buyin' me wife with your
neck, I'll strike ye—big as ye are, I'll climb a
stump and strike ye! Do ye hear? I'd rather carry
Katy out of that house barefoot and starve beside her
than put them irons on you. I'm ashamed they ever
touched me hand."

He seized the handcuffs and hurled them through the cave
entrance. They flashed once in the rain and vanished among
the laurels.

Proudfoot looked after them.

"Sheriff's property, mebbe."

"I'll pay the city."

"With what?"

"That is a low and unbrotherly question."

A sound came from Proudfoot's chest, half laugh and half
sob. He lowered his head. His massive shoulders heaved once.

Kerry went to him immediately.

"Ah, Andy—don't. Or do, then. I've been wantin' to meself
for a fortnight."

Proudfoot lifted his face. Tears stood openly in his eyes.

"Never had a brother," he said.

"Well, ye have now," Kerry answered; and, with no regard
for dignity, official discipline, or the difference in their sizes,
he threw both arms about the outlaw's neck.

They remained thus while the fire hissed and the rain
swept past the cave mouth. It was Kerry who finally
drew away, blowing his nose with sonorous violence.

"Now," he said, "tell me about the man ye killed."

Proudfoot's face changed. The softness withdrew, leaving an expression carved
from old grief.

"Name was Dickert. Abel Dickert. Folks called him Abe, though
some knowed him as Dick. He was agent fer the
Blue Crown Company when they come buyin' land along Tusquitee."

"Mining?"

"Copper, they said. Euola's daddy owned eighty acres by Hanging
Dog—timber, spring, cabin, all. When he died, land come to
Euola. Her an' me had been married three weeks."

At the name, his voice deepened.

"She was a mounting-gal?"

"Born under Haw Knob. Little—no higher'n my shoulder. Black hair.
Proud. She could shoot a hawk on the wing an'
read any book the preacher had."

Kerry smiled faintly. "A dangerous combination."

"Dickert offered twelve hundred fer the land. We aimed to
take hit an' go to Arkansas, where my mother's people
lived. Papers was signed. Then he give us two hundred
an' said the rest would come when the company measured.
I waited six weeks. Rode to Murphy time an' again.
Last time I found him at the hotel."

The fire sank. Proudfoot did not replenish it.

"He said thar weren't no more money. Claimed the paper
called two hundred full payment. Euola had trusted what he
read out. She couldn't make out law words. I told
him he lied."

"Did he?"

"Shore."

"What then?"

"He laughed. Said Euola knowed him afore she knowed me.
Said she'd been his woman down in Atlanta, an' I
ought to count the two hundred generous."

Proudfoot's good hand gripped his knee. The injured one lay
rigid.

"I knowed hit was a lie. Euola looked at me—jest
looked. Dickert smiled. I struck him."

"With what?"

"My fist."

"Once?"

"Once. He fell agin the iron stove. Didn't move after."

Kerry imagined the blow delivered by that tremendous arm, the
backward fall, the edge of iron. Murder in the handbill;
rage, fraud, insult, and one uncontrolled instant in the cave.

"Was he dead?"

"Men said so. I didn't wait. His desk stood open.
I took the thousand dollars he'd held from us—nary cent
more. Rode home. Euola begged me to hide. She said
she'd settle things, then foller me to the chestnut ford
above Valley River."

"And did she?"

"No."

The two letters seemed to extinguish the warmth in Proudfoot's
voice.

"I waited three nights. Posse come through. I run. Sent
word by a boy from Andrews—no answer. Sent ag'in come
spring. Nothin'. Heard she'd left the cabin. Reckon she believed
what Dickert said, or hated me fer killin'."

"Would she believe a lie about herself?"

"She might hate me fer hearin' hit."

"A woman doesn't generally blame a man for knockin' down
the feller who slanders her."

"I killed him."

"Accidentally."

"Dead's dead."

Proudfoot crossed to the rear of the cave. Removing a
flat stone, he dug in the earth and brought out
a buckskin pouch. From it he poured gold pieces and
folded bank-notes upon a blanket.

"I used some. Seven hundred an' forty left. Half's Euola's.
Other half's mine. Take mine, Dan. Git yer Katy out.
Ef ye ever find Euola, give her what's hern."

Kerry scarcely heard the offer.

A memory had risen—an office in Asheville, a hot afternoon,
a local detective chewing a match while he displayed reports
concerning a woman called Mrs. Ellis. Dark hair. Small, young,
remarkably close-mouthed. She worked at a boarding-house, paid men to
guide her toward the Nantahala country, and twice had been
turned back by deputies who suspected her of carrying messages
to Proudfoot. Police believed her to be the fugitive's wife,
though she denied it. Three weeks earlier she had disappeared
from Asheville; then returned ragged, exhausted, and without money.

Kerry stared at Andy.

"What does Euola look like exactly?"

Proudfoot answered, puzzled. "Black hair to her waist. Grey eyes,
darker'n mine. Little scar under the chin where a calf
kicked her. Wears a silver piece at her throat—Mexican money,
with a hole bored through."

Kerry slapped both palms upon his knees.

"Mrs. Ellis!"

"Who?"

"She's in Asheville—or was when I came through. A little
woman with a scar, and a silver coin at her
neck. The police watched her because she kept tryin' to
get into these mountains. Once she hired a guide, and
he robbed her; once she walked as far as Topton
before a deputy found her. Andy, ye magnificent sorrowful fool,
she's been huntin' ye!"

Proudfoot did not stir. His eyes alone changed, widening until
their misery and dawning hope became almost unbearable to witness.

"Don't," he said.

"It's true."

"Don't tell me lessen ye know."

"I know what they said. Her right name was believed
to be Proudfoot. She'd ask every traveller about smoke, camps,
lone hunters. She's not abandoned ye; she's been batterin' her
poor heart against half the sheriff's offices in Carolina!"

Proudfoot rose.

For a moment he stood in the firelight with both
arms hanging at his sides, a figure of such sanguine
expectation and terror that Kerry dared not speak. Then the
giant swayed. Kerry caught him about the waist and was
nearly carried to the floor.

"Euola," Proudfoot whispered. "She come."

"She tried."

"I thought—God forgive me—I thought she didn't want me."

"She wants ye enough to walk through a county full
of warrants."

Proudfoot covered his face. His weeping made no sound; tears
escaped between his fingers and fell upon the bandage Kerry
had tied. Kerry wept noisily in sympathy, pacing, laughing, wiping
his eyes, and returning at intervals to pound Proudfoot upon
the back.

At last Andy sat down.

"What am I gwine do?"

"Do?" Kerry cried. "Why, we fetch her!"

"Deputies watchin'."

"Then we outwit them. I'm a policeman."

"Ye don't appear powerful loyal to the trade."

"I understand its weaknesses."

He began to pace more rapidly, his natural element being
a plan not yet troubled by details.

"Listen now. I go to Asheville alone. Nobody knows I
found ye. I tell 'em the trail's cold—no, better, I
tell 'em ye crossed into Tennessee. I locate Euola. I
bring her out by the south road, disguised maybe—"

"She won't wear britches."

"Who said britches? There's dignity even in crime. She can
be somebody's widow. Mine, perhaps."

Proudfoot stared.

"No, that has complications. Your sister! We hire a wagon
to Murphy, then take the rail west."

"They'll watch stations."

"Then we travel in pieces. Ye cross into Georgia through
Hiwassee. Euola and I meet ye. We go by wagon
to Marietta, rail from there. And Katy—"

His pace stopped.

Katy still waited in Philadelphia. The reward was gone. His
leave would expire. He had seven dollars and some coins.
For one sober instant the architecture of his hope collapsed.

Proudfoot pushed the buckskin pouch toward him.

"Take hit."

"No."

"Hit's mine."

"Stolen."

"Mine by right."

"The law may debate that."

"Ye jest sent the law to perdition."

"I reserve the privilege of callin' it back when convenient."

Proudfoot's mouth twitched.

Kerry picked up one gold piece, considered it, and put
it down.

"All right. Not as payment for deliverin' ye. Not as
reward. As a loan between brothers. I take enough to
reach Philadelphy, fetch Katy, and get us West. When I'm
rich I repay ye with scandalous interest."

"Take half."

"A hundred."

"Three hundred."

"One-fifty."

"Two-fifty."

"Two hundred, and ye're robbin' me."

"Done."

They clasped hands, Proudfoot offering the left because the right
was still bandaged.

Then, until long after midnight, they constructed their future.

It grew magnificently beneath Kerry's speech. In Colorado they would
buy land under a sky so wide that no warrant
could cross it without being seen three days away. Proudfoot
would raise cattle; Kerry would keep accounts, this being assigned
to him on the dubious ground that city men understood
figures. Euola would plant an orchard. Katy would have roses
beside the door and never again hear her father's step
upon the stair. There would be two houses at first,
then perhaps one larger house between them, though Kerry admitted
that wives possessed mysterious opinions concerning domestic arrangements.

"We'll have horses," he said. "Not these triangular mountain beasts,
but Western horses—long-legged, quick as light."

"An' water?"

"A river through the middle."

"Timber?"

"Enough to build a cathedral."

"Game?"

"Deer'll eat out of your hand."

"Hain't sportin' to shoot 'em thataway."

"Then we'll train 'em to run first."

Proudfoot smiled into the fire, and in that smile the
cave relinquished its claim upon him.

Toward dawn the rain ceased. A wind travelled over the
ridge, shaking drops from the laurels. The two men went
outside.

The world had been washed. Every leaf glittered; wet boulders
shone like iron; the far ranges, released from mist, rose
clear and blue beneath a paling sky. In the east
a narrow line of gold opened above the mountains.

Proudfoot stood bareheaded, breathing deeply. His face was altered. The
old grief remained, but it no longer possessed the whole
territory; hope had entered like sunlight into a shuttered room.

Kerry went down among the laurels and searched until he
found the handcuffs. They lay in a runnel, half covered
with red mud.

"Thought ye was done with them," Proudfoot said.

"They belong to the city. I'm a dishonest officer, not
a thief."

He washed them in the creek and tucked them into
his pack. The gesture no longer burdened him. They were
dead metal now.

By noon his provisions were wrapped and his route determined.
Proudfoot gave him a map scratched upon deerskin, a packet
for Euola, and the Mexican silver piece's mate—a coin cut
in two long ago, one half worn by the wife,
the other kept by the husband. Kerry placed it safely
inside his shirt.

At the cave mouth they faced one another.

"Three weeks," Kerry said. "Wait at the chestnut ford on
the twentieth day after tomorrow. If I'm late, wait two
more."

"I waited fer her afore."

"This time she'll come."

"Dan."

"Well?"

"Ef ye git took helpin' us—"

"I'll arrest meself and claim whatever reward is offered."

"I mean hit."

"So do I. Andy, I've spent years learnin' that men
are either guilty or innocent according to a line in
a book. Then I met ye and discovered the book
has narrow pages. I'm not sayin' ye did right. Ye're
not sayin' it. But I know what ye are, and
I'll trust that before I trust a handbill."

Proudfoot extended his healed hand. Kerry took it carefully.

"I'd give myself up yet," Andy said, "ef hit'd save
ye."

"And I'd break ye out, so spare us the exercise."

The mountain man's eyes glowed. "Brother."

"Brother," Kerry answered.

He turned quickly, because farewells caused him to behave without
restraint, and plunged down the trail.

After fifty yards he looked back.

Proudfoot still stood before the dark mouth of the cave,
broad against the morning. Behind him were the fire, the
lonely bed, and the months of hiding; before him, ridge
after ridge shone westward beneath the sun. He lifted his
bandaged hand.

Kerry waved his hat.

Then he descended through the scarlet creeper and golden poplars,
slipping on wet stones, frightening grouse from the fern, and
singing at a volume which warned every creature within a
mile that the law was abroad in the mountains—and in
a jubilant, highly irregular mood.

In his pocket lay two hundred dollars of disputed ownership.
Against his heart lay half a silver coin. Ahead waited
Euola, stubbornly faithful; farther still waited Katy, imprisoned but not
for long. Behind waited Andy Proudfoot, outlaw, host, wounded giant,
and brother.

The road to the West was confused, perilous, and almost
certainly illegal.

To Dan Kerry it had never looked so straight.

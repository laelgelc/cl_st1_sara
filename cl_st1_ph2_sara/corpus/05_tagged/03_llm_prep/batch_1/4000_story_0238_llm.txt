There was once a merchant so wealthy that he could
have paved the whole market-place with silver coins and still
had enough left to pave the little lane behind it;
but he did not do so, for he understood the
value of money, and that is a rarer understanding than
many suppose.

His son understood nothing of the kind.

When the merchant died, the son inherited warehouses, ships, houses,
and more gold than he could count without growing tired.
He gave splendid dinners to people whose names he scarcely
knew; he made kites out of banknotes and watched them
sail away over the chimneys; and once, merely to hear
the splash, he threw a handful of gold pieces into
the sea.

"Money is made to go round," he said.

And round it went, to be sure, but it never
came round again.

Before long the ships belonged to other merchants, the warehouses
were empty, and the great house had so many creditors
at the door that there was hardly room for the
owner to get out. At last he had nothing left
but a flowered dressing gown, a pair of embroidered slippers,
and four copper coins. His friends disappeared with his fortune.
One alone remembered him and sent him an old wooden
trunk with a note:

"Pack up and be off!"

This advice was excellent, only the young man had nothing
to pack. He therefore climbed into the trunk himself.

The moment he pressed down the lock, the trunk gave
a creak, sprang through the open window, and flew into
the air.

"Dear me!" cried the merchant's son, clutching the sides.

Over roofs and steeples it carried him, higher than the
larks and faster than the clouds. He passed forests, mountains,
and a strip of shining sea; then, toward evening, the
trunk descended in a strange country and set him down
among trees outside a great city.

He hid the trunk under dry leaves and walked toward
the city in his dressing gown and slippers. The people
there wore long robes and bright turbans, so they supposed
he was dressed in some distant fashion and paid him
little heed.

"What city is this?" he asked a seller of sweet
cakes.

"A city of Turkey," replied the man. "And that castle
yonder belongs to the king."

One tower of the castle rose so high that its
windows seemed to look straight into heaven.

"Who lives up there?" asked the young man.

"The princess," said the cake seller. "A wise woman foretold
that she would be made unhappy by a lover, and
therefore the king and queen have shut her in the
tower. No man can reach her."

"No man on foot, perhaps," thought the merchant's son.

That night he returned to the forest, climbed into the
trunk, pressed the lock, and flew up to the princess's
window. It stood open because the air was warm. He
sailed straight into her chamber and landed on a carpet
beside her bed.

The princess awoke and gave a little scream.

"Do not be alarmed," he said, stepping out in his
flowered gown. "I am a Turkish angel."

This appeared quite possible to her, for he had entered
through a window higher than any ladder could reach, and
his dressing gown was covered with golden flowers.

He sat beside her and spoke so prettily that she
forgot to be frightened. He told her that her eyes
were two dark lakes in which silver-tailed mermaids studied the
secrets of the heart, and that her forehead was a
snowy mountain containing halls of crystal, where the thoughts of
wise queens danced by moonlight.

The princess had never heard anything of the sort.

"Are all angels so eloquent?" she asked.

"Only the truthful ones," he replied.

Then he told her stories—one about the moon losing a
slipper, another about a rose that wished to become minister
of state—and she laughed until the tears stood in her
eyes.

"You must marry me," she said at last.

"With the greatest pleasure," said he, for she was beautiful
and would inherit half the kingdom.

"But you must come again on Saturday," she continued. "My
father and mother will take tea with me. They will
be proud to meet an angel, though my mother is
difficult to please. You must tell us a story that
is amusing and instructive."

"I have a whole cupboard full of such stories," he
said, though in truth he had not even a cupboard.

He kissed her hand, climbed into the trunk, and flew
away.

Until Saturday he wandered through the streets considering what tale
he should tell. It had to make the king laugh,
the queen think, and the princess love him more than
before. That is a great deal to demand of one
story, though not so much as some people demand.

On Saturday evening he flew again to the tower. The
king, the queen, and the princess were waiting.

The king received him very graciously and called him "thou,"
because kings may do so without first asking permission. The
queen held out her hand and said:

"Let us have the instructive part first."

"But do not forget the amusing part," whispered the princess.

The merchant's son bowed and began:

"There was once a kitchen where all the household objects
agreed that they were much more important than the people
who owned them.

"'Without us,' said the iron pot, 'the family would eat
nothing warm. Therefore I am the foundation of civilization.'

"'You are black inside and out,' said the porcelain plates.
'We are painted with blue flowers and stand upon the
dining table. Good society never sees you.'

"'Good society eats what I cook,' answered the pot, 'and
leaves grease upon you.'

"This was so rude that the plates rattled indignantly.

"On the shelf stood a tea-urn, polished until she could
see her own face in her side, which was what
she liked best to see.

"'Birth and breeding are everything,' she said. 'I enter the
drawing room every afternoon, while certain persons remain among onions
and ashes.'

"By certain persons she meant the pot. The pot knew
it but pretended not to hear.

"Near the door stood the broom, thin, severe, and very
proud of her long handle.

"'Talk, talk, talk!' she cried. 'What this household needs is
reform. There is dust under the cupboard, crumbs beneath the
table, and scandal in every corner. Give me authority, and
I shall sweep away abuses.'

"'You will sweep away anything that cannot get out of
your path,' said the fire-tongs. 'That is not the same
as reform.'

"The market basket stood folded against the wall. He had
been out in the world and considered himself a statesman.

"'Order is necessary,' he declared. 'Eggs at the bottom, bread
at the top, and parsley in its proper place. Society
collapses when a cabbage is permitted to crush a peach.'

"'Very profound!' whispered the tea-urn.

"The kettle began to sing, but everyone begged her to
stop.

"'My music is natural,' she said.

"'So is thunder,' replied the pot, 'but we do not
keep it on the stove.'

"A quill pen, which had rolled in from the master's
room, now spoke from the windowsill.

"'You are all common creatures,' he said. 'I make poetry,
accounts, laws, and love letters.'

"'Does the hand make nothing?' asked the broom.

"'The hand is merely my servant,' said the pen.

"At that moment a bundle of matches, tied neatly together,
called from beside the stove:

"'It surprises us to hear such boasting from persons of
uncertain descent.'

"Every object turned to look at them.

"'And who are you?' asked the iron pot.

"'We are descended from one of the loftiest families in
the north,' said the matches. 'Our ancestor was a magnificent
pine tree. His crown touched the clouds, birds sang among
his branches, and every morning he drank diamonds.'

"'Dewdrops,' murmured the kettle.

"'Diamonds,' repeated the matches. 'The sun himself visited our family
daily and clothed us in gold. The wind told us
news from foreign lands, and the stars shone especially for
us.'

"'How did you come to live in a kitchen?' asked
the basket.

"'A temporary misfortune,' said the matches. 'Our noble ancestor was
cut down by coarse men, and we were separated into
small but distinguished persons. Even now we carry celestial fire
in our heads.'

"'You have red caps,' said the broom.

"'Crowns!' cried the matches. 'They are crowns.'

"The tea-urn was greatly impressed.

"'I suspected at once that you were of high descent,'
she said. 'There is something wonderfully dry and refined in
your appearance.'

"'We have never been used,' said the matches. 'That proves
our superiority.'

"'It proves only that nobody has needed you yet,' said
the pot.

"Now the whole kitchen began arguing. The plates demanded a
concert, the broom demanded an election, the basket demanded a
committee, and the pen proposed to write an account of
the proceedings before they had occurred.

"'Let everyone display his talent!' cried the tea-urn. 'Then we
shall know who deserves the highest place.'

"The kettle sang until her lid danced. The plates clinked
in time. The broom swept three times across the floor
and raised so much dust that everyone coughed. The basket
gave a speech about cabbages and national prosperity. The pot
told a useful fact, but no one listened, because useful
facts seldom arrive dressed for company.

"At last came the turn of the matches.

"'Prepare yourselves,' they said. 'You are about to behold noble
brilliance.'

"The kitchen maid entered just then. She took one match
and struck it against the wall.

"Flame sprang up, yellow and blue, and every object stared.

"'See!' cried the match. 'I wear a crown of fire!
I outshine the kettle, the plates, the moon, and all
the stars of my ancestral forest!'

"For one glorious moment it illuminated the whole kitchen.

"Then it burned its fingers, fell into the ashes, and
went out.

"The kitchen was silent.

"'A short performance,' said the pot.

"'But very fashionable,' said the tea-urn.

"And the broom swept the little black remnant away."

The king laughed until his crown slipped over one ear.
The princess clapped her hands, and even the queen smiled,
though queens are careful with smiles because people attach political
meaning to them.

"That was amusing," said the king.

"And instructive," said the queen. "The pot spoke with deep
learning."

The marriage was approved, and the wedding was fixed for
the following Monday. All the city heard that the princess
was to marry a Turkish angel who could fly and
tell stories.

On Sunday evening the merchant's son decided to give the
people a sign of his heavenly nature. He bought rockets,
wheels, crackers, stars, and fiery serpents with his last four
copper coins and packed them into the trunk. Then he
flew over the city and set them off in the
sky.

What a sight it was! Red fountains streamed above the
roofs; golden stars burst among the clouds; blue flames twisted
like dragons, and thunder rolled from one end of the
city to the other.

The people fell on their knees.

"I saw the angel himself!" cried a baker. "His wings
reached from one street to the next."

"He had eyes like suns!" declared a tailor.

"He had seven crowns," said the cake seller, who had
seen nothing but disliked being outdone.

The merchant's son heard their cries from above and was
exceedingly pleased. After landing in the forest, he left the
trunk under a tree and hurried into the city to
hear more. This was foolish; yet when people are praising
us, we naturally wish to stand as near as possible.

All night he listened while the angel grew taller, brighter,
and more magnificent with every telling.

But a spark had remained among the fireworks. It smouldered
in the trunk, caught the lining, and then the dry
wood. By morning nothing was left but black hinges and
a little heap of ash.

When the merchant's son returned, he stared at the ashes
and wept. He could not reach the tower; he could
not keep the wedding; and he had not even decent
clothes in which to explain himself.

The princess waited all Monday in her bridal dress. At
sunset she was still looking from the high window, and
perhaps she is looking still, for princesses in towers have
great patience when a prophecy requires it.

As for the merchant's son, he wandered from country to
country in his dressing gown and embroidered slippers, earning his
supper by telling stories. He told thousands, and some were
witty, some wise, and some very nearly true; but he
never again told one so entertaining as the tale of
the matches.

For he, too, had blazed splendidly, dazzled a whole city,
and burned up the very thing on which his happiness
depended.

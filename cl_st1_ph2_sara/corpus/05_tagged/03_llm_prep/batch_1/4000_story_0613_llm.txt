## The First Patient

At half-past eleven on a Friday night Mr Horace Cowlishaw
arrived in Hanbridge with two portmanteaux, a case of dental
instruments, seven pounds fourteen shillings in ready money, and the
conviction that he had committed a grave error.

The conviction was not new. It had occurred to him
first when the train, having left the pastoral discretion of
the south, entered a region where the sky glowed without
sunset and where enormous chimneys appeared to be holding some
sombre ecclesiastical conference over the roofs. It had strengthened when
a porter at Hanbridge Station lifted the case of instruments,
shook it experimentally, and asked whether there was "owt alive
in it." And it became established doctrine when the cab
turned into Crown Square and was nearly annihilated by an
electric tramcar belonging to the Five Towns Electric Traction Company,
Limited.

Mr Cowlishaw had been reared in a cathedral town in
which even noise seemed to have received episcopal permission before
occurring. He was twenty-seven, fair, narrow-shouldered, and unmarried. His moustache,
after four years of conscientious encouragement, still lacked decision. He
had passed every necessary examination, read two unnecessary professional journals,
and could discourse with precision upon alveolar abscesses; but no
examiner had demanded that he should remain calm while a
tramcar of forty-eight seats advanced upon him with a blue
spark overhead and an iron scream beneath.

"Pretty near," he said to the cabman, recovering his breath.

"What were?"

"The tram."

The cabman looked round.

"Oh, her! Her's all right."

This was Mr Cowlishaw's first lesson in the philosophy of
the Five Towns. An object might weigh twelve tons, emit
lightning, and approach at sixteen miles an hour; but if
it belonged to a prosperous local company it was all
right.

He had bought, for a sum which looked modest in
an advertisement and formidable on a cheque, the practice of
Mr Shrapnell, dental surgeon, who had retired to Rhyl after
thirty-one years of conflict with the molars of Hanbridge. The
practice occupied the first floor above a boot shop in
Duck Bank. Mr Shrapnell had guaranteed nothing except the furniture,
the lease, and the goodwill; and even the goodwill, when
examined legally, proved to consist chiefly in permission to put
the words "Established 1873" beneath Mr Cowlishaw's own name.

As the rooms were not yet fit for habitation, he
had engaged a bedroom at the Grand Imperial Hotel. The
Grand Imperial was the leading hotel of Hanbridge. This fact
was proved beyond controversy by the circumstance that its proprietress,
Mrs Clowes, was also mayoress. In certain lesser towns a
person becomes important because he owns the principal hotel; in
Hanbridge the hotel became principal because its owner was important.
The distinction, though subtle, is essential.

Mrs Clowes's late husband had been mayor. Upon his death
the Council, unwilling that so much civic prestige should perish
at once, elected her eldest son, Councillor Ephraim Clowes, to
the office; and Mrs Clowes, as his mother and the
only woman whom he obeyed immediately, became mayoress. Her second
son managed a tile works. Her daughter had married a
solicitor. Her brother sat on the Watch Committee. A nephew
played half-back for Bursley. A cousin supplied meat to two
workhouses and an asylum. One may say, therefore, that she
did not merely live in Hanbridge: she extended through it.

But Mr Cowlishaw knew none of this as yet. He
knew only that the Grand Imperial possessed a revolving door,
red plush, electric bells, and a hall porter whose uniform
seemed designed for the suppression of mutiny in India. These
things soothed him. The commercial room smelt of cigar smoke,
gravy, and enterprise. The barmaid smiled. The boots addressed him
as "sir." He ate cold beef, drank a glass of
milk, and began to think that perhaps Hanbridge had been
exaggerated by persons of nervous temperament.

At twelve-fifteen he went to bed.

His room, numbered 36, was on the second floor and
overlooked Crown Square. It contained a brass bedstead, a mahogany
wardrobe, three antimacassars, a lithograph of Llandudno Pier, and an
electric push marked "PRESS ONCE." Mr Cowlishaw pressed it once
in order to obtain hot water. Nothing occurred. After seven
minutes he pressed it again. A chambermaid appeared instantly and
regarded him as one who had broken a treaty.

"I only wanted hot water," he explained.

"You rung twice."

"I'm afraid the first ring—"

"It rings downstairs both times."

"I beg your pardon."

She brought the water. He washed, arranged his collar-studs in
a row, wound his watch, and lay down. The mattress
received him with hospitality. He had been travelling and negotiating
all day. His brain had borne mortgages, inventories, testimonials, estimates
for repainting, and the question whether artificial teeth should be
advertised as "lifelike" or "natural in appearance." He closed his
eyes.

Then the last tram from Knype came into Crown Square.

It did not come secretly. It announced its approach by
a metallic ululation, crossed points with the report of small
artillery, and halted under the bedroom window in a shower
of blue fire. The entire Grand Imperial trembled. The wardrobe
muttered. The water in the ewer registered the seismic phenomenon
in concentric circles.

Mr Cowlishaw sat up.

Passengers descended. Other passengers, though apparently at a distance of
several streets, called advice to them. The conductor rang twice;
the driver rang once; a late passenger struck the side
of the car with an umbrella; and the tram, offended
by delay, departed towards Bursley, dragging its thunder after it.

Mr Cowlishaw lay down again.

At twelve-forty three commercial travellers entered the hotel. Their friendship
had reached a stage at which no man could utter
a sentence without all the others laughing. They laughed in
the hall. They laughed on the stairs. They laughed outside
Room 34. One of them dropped a boot, and this
surpassed everything.

At one o'clock they separated.

Then silence.

Mr Cowlishaw had almost crossed that mysterious frontier beyond which
hotel noises cease to matter when a woman shrieked in
the square. He sprang up. The shriek was repeated, followed
by a man's resonant declaration that he would "tell her
mother." Presently it appeared that the woman was not being
murdered; she was merely unwilling to leave an actor who
had been performing at the Theatre Royal. Several other actors
arrived. The farewell, which civilization might have compressed into a
handshake, occupied nineteen minutes and included a song.

At one-thirty came another tram.

"Impossible!" murmured Mr Cowlishaw.

It was possible. The Five Towns Electric Traction Company, Limited,
had arranged its time-table without consulting the new dentist.

After the tram there occurred an interval during which Mr
Cowlishaw heard a remote, deep, measured breathing. It came from
beyond the houses, from some infernal creature too large to
be seen. He listened, entranced by apprehension. Every few seconds
the breath brightened the sky.

At breakfast he learned that this was Number Four blast
furnace at Cauldon Bar.

To the citizens it was no more alarming than a
grandfather asleep in the next room. To Mr Cowlishaw it
seemed that the earth itself suffered from bronchitis.

At two-ten a horse refused to move a cart. The
carter first reasoned with it, then denounced its ancestry, and
finally revealed, in a voice of astonishing carrying power, his
private opinion of horses as a species. The horse yielded
at two-twenty-eight.

At three a guest returned without his key.

At three-twelve the night porter found the key.

At three-twenty a baby began.

Mr Cowlishaw, whose bachelorhood had protected him from the methods
of babies, waited for the parents to stop it. The
parents, more experienced, waited for it to stop itself. It
did so at four, having established moral supremacy over that
wing of the hotel.

The dentist slept.

At four-fifteen a train passed beneath the town, although Mr
Cowlishaw could have sworn that the railway lay half a
mile away. It passed slowly, wagon after wagon, with the
solemnity of an iron funeral. Then another blast furnace took
breath. Then carts began to arrive at the market. Their
wheels were apparently octagonal.

At five the steam muezzins of the manufactories called the
faithful to labour. One whistled thinly and with malice; another
roared like a sea-beast; a third began before the second
had ended, either through rivalry or imperfect chronometry. Doors opened.
Clogs struck pavements. Men coughed. Women shouted from bedroom windows
instructions touching tea-cans, mufflers, wages, and the purchasing of haddock.
Hanbridge arose, not refreshed—for Hanbridge had no need of refreshment—but
ready.

At five-twenty Mr Cowlishaw slept again.

At five-thirty the alarm clock which he had carefully set
for half-past seven exploded on the table beside him.

He had set the small hand wrongly.

There are moments when character is determined. Napoleon, faced by
Europe, acted. Socrates, faced by hemlock, conversed. Mr Cowlishaw, faced
by the alarm clock, put it beneath his pillow and
wept.

Nevertheless, at eight-forty-five he descended to breakfast shaved, brushed, and
professionally attired. Only his eyes betrayed that during the night
he had personally supervised the traffic, amusements, industries, domestic disagreements,
and infant population of a great manufacturing centre.

The coffee restored him somewhat. The ham restored him further.
And when the head waiter said, "Morning, doctor," he experienced
the first exquisite taste of status.

Strictly, a dentist is not necessarily a doctor. But in
the Five Towns the title was granted to anyone who
possessed an instrument case, a black coat, and the power
to inflict suffering in a scientific manner.

At nine-thirty Mr Cowlishaw walked to Duck Bank and ascended
the stairs to his practice.

On the door, freshly painted, were the words:

**HORACE COWLISHAW, L.D.S.**
**DENTAL SURGEON**
**LATE ASSISTANT TO J. P. MORTIMER, ESQ.**
**ESTABLISHED 1873**

The conjunction of youth and antiquity pleased him. He unlocked
the door.

The waiting-room had six chairs, five magazines, an aspidistra, and
a framed coloured diagram demonstrating the ruin consequent upon neglect
of the teeth. The surgery beyond glittered with nickel, porcelain,
glass, and hope. The chair stood in the centre like
an improved engine for medieval justice. On one side waited
the drill; on the other, ranged with military exactitude, were
forceps, elevators, probes, mirrors, pluggers, and other devices whose beauty
is most fully appreciated by the person not sitting in
the chair.

Miss Selina Bostock, whom Mr Shrapnell had left behind with
the furniture, arrived at ten. She was nineteen and had
been described in the inventory as "competent attendant." She wore
a white apron and knew every family within a radius
of three miles.

"No appointments?" asked Mr Cowlishaw lightly.

"Not for to-day."

"Ah! Naturally people will not yet be aware—"

"Oh, they're aware," said Miss Bostock.

She indicated the morning paper. On the front page, below
news of the football match and above an advertisement for
liver pills, stood the announcement of his succession. Mr Cowlishaw
had paid twelve shillings for it.

"Possibly the match—" he suggested.

"It'll be the match."

Hanbridge United was playing Axe in the fourth round of
the English Cup. The matter was not sport. It was
destiny. Shops closed by consent; works stopped by diplomacy; men
who had complained of mortal illness at seven-thirty were seen
entering the football ground at two. A funeral had been
advanced by an hour. It was said that the chief
constable, while officially neutral, wore the club colours beneath his
waistcoat.

Mr Cowlishaw sat in his private room and read the
*Dental Practitioner*. At eleven he heard footsteps on the stairs.
He straightened, smoothed his hair, and adopted the expression of
benign omniscience proper to a professional man.

The footsteps passed to the accountant on the second floor.

At eleven-forty there were other footsteps.

A boy entered to ask whether the boot shop had
any football rattles.

At twelve-fifteen Mr Cowlishaw lunched upon a sandwich. At one
he walked twice across the waiting-room. At one-thirty he examined
the street. Humanity was flowing in one direction, towards the
ground. At two the flow ceased. Duck Bank became the
avenue of a deserted capital.

Professional men often remark that they prefer not to be
overwhelmed with patients at the beginning of a practice. This
is true. They prefer to be moderately overwhelmed.

At three-twenty a distant sound rolled over the town.

"A goal," said Miss Bostock.

"How can you tell?"

"That was us."

At three-fifty came another roar, confused and grievous.

"That was them," she said.

Mr Cowlishaw marvelled. She interpreted twenty thousand throats as a
mother interprets the cry of her child.

At four-forty the streets awoke. Men appeared in groups, arguing
with an intensity that might have altered the result had
it been employed sooner. Boys ran shouting. Newspaper placards sprang
mysteriously into existence.

**HANBRIDGE'S GREAT VICTORY**
**AXE BEATEN—THREE TO TWO**
**RANNOCH DISABLED**

At five minutes past five the door opened, and a
broad young man entered with blood on his chin.

Miss Bostock gasped.

"Mr Rannoch!"

It was indeed Andrew Rannoch, the celebrated centre-forward, whose weekly
achievements were discussed in every public-house and whose right foot
had been compared in print to a piece of field
artillery. He wore a thick overcoat over his football clothes.
Mud decorated him from hair to boot. His face had
the plain, durable construction proper to a man who earned
his living among knees, elbows, and the changing tempers of
twenty-two thousand spectators.

There was, however, a gap in his smile.

"Dentist in?" he demanded.

"I am Mr Cowlishaw," said Mr Cowlishaw.

Rannoch looked at him with momentary disappointment, as though he
had expected age, whiskers, or at least greater width. Then
he opened his mouth.

Three upper teeth were missing.

"Dear me!" said Mr Cowlishaw, professional sympathy contending with professional
excitement.

"Goalkeeper," said Rannoch. "Came out feet first. Referee never saw
it."

He placed a folded handkerchief on the cabinet. Within it
lay three teeth, each with a fragment of root attached.
They had made their final appearance before a larger audience
than Mr Cowlishaw was likely ever to address.

"You cannot replace these immediately," said the dentist, after examination.
"The gum must heal. There is considerable laceration."

"How long?"

"A temporary plate might be prepared in—well, perhaps a week.
A permanent appliance later."

"What'll it cost?"

Mr Cowlishaw named a sum.

Rannoch whistled through the new architectural opening in his mouth.

"I lose three teeth winning match for town, and then
I pay that?"

"The circumstances do not affect the laboratory charges."

"They ought."

Mr Cowlishaw murmured that he could perhaps make some allowance.
He had no intention of losing the first famous patient
who had crossed his threshold.

Rannoch glanced at the three teeth, then at the blank
window of the waiting-room, then at Mr Cowlishaw. His mind,
trained to seize openings in a defence, perceived an opening
here.

"I'll tell ye," he said. "You do 'em for nowt,
and keep these."

"Keep the teeth?"

"Put 'em in window. 'Rannoch's Teeth. Knocked Out in Great
Cup-Tie.' People'd come miles."

Mr Cowlishaw was silent.

The vision presented itself instantly. He saw the teeth mounted
on blue velvet beneath glass. He saw crowds blocking Duck
Bank. He saw paragraphs in the *Evening Sentinel*: "Interesting Relics—Local
Dentist's Enterprise." He saw mothers dragging reluctant children to the
window. He saw his waiting-room full, his appointment book black,
his financial anxieties dissolved in a tide of incisors and
sovereigns.

Rannoch observed the vision passing across his face.

"Big card," he continued. "'Preserved by Mr Cowlishaw, Dental Surgeon.'
Could have my photograph. I'll sign it."

There was genius in the scheme. In London it might
have been vulgar. In Hanbridge it would have been history.

Mr Cowlishaw picked up one of the teeth. It was
a stout, serviceable specimen, worn at the edge and indisputably
heroic. He thought of his rent. He thought of Mr
Shrapnell's goodwill. He thought of the empty chairs.

Then, most unfortunately, he thought of the British Dental Association.

Professional etiquette rose before him in a frock coat. It
carried the regulations in one hand and social extinction in
the other.

"I'm afraid," he said, "that would be impossible."

"Why?"

"It would amount to advertisement of an objectionable character."

Rannoch stared.

"You've advertised in paper."

"That is different. A formal announcement is permissible."

"And teeth aren't?"

"Not in that manner."

"They're my teeth."

"Undoubtedly."

"I give 'em ye."

"The ownership is not the point."

"What is?"

Mr Cowlishaw attempted to explain. He spoke of dignity, accepted
usage, the traditions of the profession, and the distinction between
legitimate publicity and solicitation. The more lucidly he spoke, the
less Rannoch understood; and the less Rannoch understood, the more
clearly he perceived that free teeth were receding.

"So ye won't do it?"

"I regret that I cannot."

Rannoch folded the handkerchief around the relics.

"Old Shrapnell would."

Mr Cowlishaw suffered this blow without movement.

"He'd have had 'em out there afore six," Rannoch added,
indicating the window.

"Mr Shrapnell must have been guided by his own conscience."

"He was guided by business."

The centre-forward rose.

"You'll find someone else," said Mr Cowlishaw, with stiff courtesy.

"Aye," said Rannoch. "And so will you—if you wait long
enough."

He departed.

Miss Bostock entered after an interval.

"Well?" she asked.

"He proposed a most unprofessional arrangement."

"What arrangement?"

Mr Cowlishaw told her.

Miss Bostock's face expressed awe at the magnitude of the
lost opportunity.

"His actual teeth?"

"Yes."

"In the window?"

"Yes."

"Everybody'd have come."

"That is precisely why it was impossible," said Mr Cowlishaw.

He retired into his room and shut the door with
quiet majesty. For ten minutes he despised Hanbridge. For the
next ten he despised professional etiquette. Then he began to
despise himself.

By six o'clock darkness had covered Duck Bank. The boot
shop below lighted its lamps. Mr Cowlishaw could hear the
proprietor telling every customer that Rannoch had been in the
dentist's and had come out still gapped. Reputation, in the
Five Towns, travelled rapidly; adverse reputation travelled by electric traction.

"Shall I go?" asked Miss Bostock.

"Yes. There will be no one now."

At that instant the outer bell rang.

Miss Bostock opened the door and immediately altered in bearing.
Her shoulders drew back; her expression became alert, respectful, almost
municipal.

Mrs Clowes entered.

She was a large woman in black silk, with a
fur mantle, a bonnet of civic amplitude, and the deliberate
step of one accustomed to passing through committees. Her face
was broad and commanding. She carried a handbag, an umbrella,
and the atmosphere of the Grand Imperial Hotel.

"This the new dentist?" she asked.

"Yes, madam," said Mr Cowlishaw.

"I'm Mrs Clowes."

The name conveyed little to him, but Miss Bostock's eyes
conveyed much.

"I am very pleased to make your acquaintance."

"You can be pleased after," said Mrs Clowes. "It's this
tooth."

She sat in the chair without invitation and removed her
bonnet. Such was her method. She did not ask whether
a railway train was going to Manchester; she entered it,
and Manchester made the best arrangements it could.

"Which tooth troubles you?"

"This side. Back."

Mr Cowlishaw requested her to open her mouth.

It was an august mouth. Mr Cowlishaw had inspected hundreds,
but never one that gave so powerful an impression of
being connected with public affairs. The tongue lay within like
a creature accustomed to speaking at banquets. The tooth indicated
was a lower molar of exceptional dimensions. Around it the
gum was inflamed, but the crown remained sound enough to
merit preservation.

"There is a cavity," he said, probing gently. "It is
deep, but I think the tooth can be saved."

"I don't want it saved."

"With treatment—"

"I want it out."

Mr Cowlishaw smiled professionally. "Extraction should be the last resort."

"It is the last resort. I've had brandy, oil of
cloves, hot salt, cold salt, a mustard plaster, two aspirins,
and language. Out it comes."

"I would strongly advise dressing the cavity to-night. When the
inflammation has subsided—"

"Out."

There are monosyllables against which science beats its wings in
vain.

Mr Cowlishaw looked at Miss Bostock. Her expression said that
Mrs Clowes had ordered the new market clock, dismissed a
chef during a mayoral luncheon, and once compelled the gas
company to alter a bill of fourpence. He perceived that
he was opposed not to a patient but to an
institution.

"Very well," he said. "I shall administer a local anaesthetic."

"No."

"No?"

"No injections."

"It will prevent pain."

"I've had five sons," said Mrs Clowes. "Don't talk to
me about pain."

The exact relevance of sons to molars may be questioned;
but as Mrs Clowes stated it, the argument appeared conclusive.

"A little gas, then?"

"I've no time to be put to sleep. There's a
dinner at the hotel. Forty-three covers, besides the officers of
the club."

"The extraction may be difficult."

"That's what you're here for."

Again Mr Cowlishaw looked at Miss Bostock. Again Miss Bostock's
countenance informed him that retreat was impossible. More than that,
it informed him that this formidable lady held in her
handbag the future of the practice.

If Mrs Clowes left satisfied, the town would know it
before breakfast. If she left dissatisfied, the town would know
it before dinner. The opinions of a mayoress who owned
a hotel were repeated by commercial travellers, aldermen, actors, auctioneers,
football directors, wedding parties, and persons awaiting the last tram.
She was a newspaper, a club, and an advertising agency
in black silk.

Mr Cowlishaw's fatigue descended upon him suddenly. Behind Mrs Clowes's
monumental tooth he seemed to see the trams, the actors,
the blast furnaces, the carts, the baby, the steam whistles,
the defeated ethics of Rannoch, and the alarm clock beneath
his pillow. His hands were cold.

Nevertheless, he prepared.

He washed them. He selected a pair of lower-molar forceps.
He rejected them and chose another pair. He adjusted the
lamp. He moved the chair by two inches. He asked
Miss Bostock for a napkin which already lay beside him.
These activities, known to nervous practitioners as precautions, occupied three
minutes.

Mrs Clowes watched.

"Young, aren't you?" she said.

"I am fully qualified."

"I asked if you were young."

"I am twenty-seven."

"My Ephraim was on the Council at twenty-six."

Mr Cowlishaw could think of no suitable reply. He placed
the napkin.

"Open, please."

Mrs Clowes opened.

The tooth stood at the rear of the lower jaw
like a fortress erected by a forgotten race. Its crown
was broad; its roots, as Mr Cowlishaw afterwards estimated, extended
at least seven-eighths of an inch into the earth's crust.
He applied the forceps.

"Now," he said, "you will feel pressure."

Mrs Clowes made a sound which might mean anything.

He gripped, tested, and began the first movement.

The tooth did not move.

He increased the pressure. The tooth remained loyal to Mrs
Clowes.

Every manual of dental surgery insists upon firmness combined with
delicacy. It does not explain how firmness and delicacy are
to be combined while an influential mayoress watches one with
both eyes.

Mr Cowlishaw altered his stance.

"Is it coming?" asked Mrs Clowes, round the forceps.

"Please do not speak."

"Mm?"

"Do not speak."

He tried again. A tremor passed up his arm. The
chair creaked. Mrs Clowes's umbrella fell to the floor with
a crash. Miss Bostock picked it up.

The tooth remained.

Mr Cowlishaw withdrew the forceps.

"Done?" asked Mrs Clowes.

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"The root formation appears unusual."

"Same roots as anybody else."

One may admire local democracy while doubting its dental accuracy.

Mr Cowlishaw selected another instrument and loosened the gum. Mrs
Clowes neither flinched nor closed her eyes. She observed him
continuously, as if judging a public competition. A small bead
of perspiration appeared on his forehead. Then another. He was
conscious that his weak moustache had become moist.

He reapplied the forceps.

This time the tooth yielded a fraction.

Encouraged, he rocked it outward, then inward, employing the measured
force prescribed by authority. The measured force was insufficient. He
employed a little more. The chair groaned. Mrs Clowes gripped
both arms. Miss Bostock held her breath.

The extraction now assumed the character of a campaign. Mr
Cowlishaw had committed his troops and could not withdraw without
disaster. The enemy's outer works had been shaken, but the
citadel resisted. He changed the line of attack. He leaned.
He straightened. He summoned from his exhausted frame energies which
at breakfast he could not have believed it contained.

Mrs Clowes uttered a deep noise.

"Nearly," he gasped.

It was not nearly.

The tooth, having survived mustard, cloves, brandy, five sons, municipal
banquets, and the administration of the Grand Imperial, appeared determined
to survive Mr Cowlishaw also.

He paused.

"What are you stopping for?" demanded Mrs Clowes.

"To avoid fracturing the root."

"Don't avoid. Get on."

He got on.

The charge of the Light Brigade has been praised because
men advanced though confronted by cannon. Mr Cowlishaw advanced though
confronted by a lower right first molar and the certainty
that, if he broke it, Mrs Clowes would discuss him
in the hotel bar. Courage assumes forms adapted to civilization.

He gripped once more. His knees were unsteady. The room
seemed excessively warm. The nickel fittings shone with supernatural brilliance.
From the street below came the cries of boys celebrating
Hanbridge's victory.

"Rannoch! Rannoch! Three-two! Three-two!"

Mr Cowlishaw shut his mind against football, etiquette, publicity, and
sleep. There remained in the universe only the forceps, the
tooth, and his will.

He twisted.

Something gave.

For one magnificent instant nothing happened; then the monumental molar
emerged entire, two roots glistening beneath it like the legs
of a prehistoric animal.

"Got it!" cried Miss Bostock.

Mr Cowlishaw held the forceps aloft. His face expressed triumph,
astonishment, and appeal. Mrs Clowes sat upright, one hand to
her mouth.

The room moved gently sideways.

Mr Cowlishaw attempted to tell Miss Bostock that the socket
should be examined. What he actually said was, "The—ah—the—"

Then he fainted.

He did so with completeness. His knees folded; his shoulder
struck the cabinet; and he descended upon the floor, still
holding the forceps and still gripping Mrs Clowes's tooth. A
tray of instruments rang out like the orchestra at the
conclusion of an opera.

Mrs Clowes rose.

"Well!" she said.

Miss Bostock rushed to the fallen dentist. "He's dead!"

"Nonsense. Men don't die like that."

"He's as white as paper!"

"So's paper, and there's plenty of that about."

Mrs Clowes, who was bleeding slightly but had lost none
of her administrative capacity, poured water from the tumbler into
the basin and instructed Miss Bostock to fetch brandy. Then,
deciding that delay was undesirable, she emptied the remainder of
the tumbler over Mr Cowlishaw's face.

He opened his eyes.

The first object he saw was Mrs Clowes bending above
him with a handkerchief to her mouth. The second was
Miss Bostock carrying a bottle. The third was his own
right hand, still raised a little from the floor and
still armed with the forceps.

Memory returned.

He sat up.

"I beg your pardon," he whispered.

"Don't mention it," said Mrs Clowes.

"I am exceedingly sorry. A temporary cerebral anaemia—fatigue—nothing whatever connected
with the extraction—"

"Drink this."

She held out the brandy.

"But you are the patient."

"I'm all right."

Mr Cowlishaw drank. He rose with assistance and leaned against
the chair, overwhelmed by shame. The first famous man in
the town had left him because he was too ethical;
the first famous woman had seen him collapse on the
carpet. Mr Shrapnell's goodwill was ashes. His career had lasted
one day, and most of that day had contained no
patients.

Mrs Clowes examined the extracted tooth.

"That it?"

"Yes."

"Big one."

"It was unusually firmly rooted."

"I should think it was."

"I trust," he said miserably, "that you have suffered no
inconvenience through my—my indisposition."

Mrs Clowes looked at him.

Her expression had altered. The official severity was gone. In
its place appeared a warmth almost maternal, though no mother
had ever looked prouder of a tooth.

"You took it hard," she said.

"I fear I was overtired."

"No, no. It was me."

Mr Cowlishaw blinked.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Some women wouldn't have affected you. I did."

"The extraction was certainly—"

"I've always had that effect." She turned to Miss Bostock.
"Did you see him when it came out?"

"Yes, Mrs Clowes."

"White as that wall."

"Yes, Mrs Clowes."

Mrs Clowes tied her bonnet strings with satisfaction. She had
endured the operation without anaesthetic, uttered scarcely a sound, and
overcome the operator merely by the magnitude of the encounter.
The interpretation pleased her profoundly. The fainting was no longer
a professional calamity; it was a compliment.

"I'll send people to you," she said.

Mr Cowlishaw stared.

"You're gentle. Old Shrapnell used to wrench as if he
was pulling nails out of packing-cases. And you feel things.
I like a man that feels."

Mr Cowlishaw attempted to speak.

"Come to the hotel on Sunday," she continued. "One o'clock.
You'll dine with Ephraim and me. He wants looking at.
Says there's nothing wrong, so there'll be plenty. And my
daughter's eldest has two front teeth crossing each other. You
can put them straight."

"It would be an honour."

"No honour. Business."

She put the tooth into her handbag.

"Would you care to leave it?" asked Mr Cowlishaw faintly,
before he could prevent himself.

"What for?"

He saw, in one blinding flash, a glass case in
the window: **THE MAYORESS'S MOLAR—EXTRACTED WITHOUT ANAESTHETIC.** Crowds filled Duck
Bank. Professional etiquette again raised its warning hand.

"For disposal," he said.

"I'll keep it. Ephraim won't believe the size else."

She paid the fee in full, added that the charge
was reasonable, and departed with the stateliness of a vessel
leaving dock. Miss Bostock followed her to the door.

Already, on the stairs, Mrs Clowes encountered the boot-shop proprietor.

"New dentist any good?" he asked.

"Good?" replied the mayoress. "I nearly killed him."

By seven o'clock the story had reached the Grand Imperial.
By nine it had entered the commercial room in four
versions. In one, Mr Cowlishaw had fainted before touching the
tooth; in another, Mrs Clowes had extracted it herself; in
a third, the tooth weighed three ounces; and in a
fourth, which eventually became the popular account, the young dentist
had worked for forty minutes, refused assistance, drawn a molar
with roots like a bulldog's, and then collapsed from sheer
professional devotion.

The account lost nothing through Mrs Clowes's correction. She corrected
only those details which diminished her.

On Sunday Mr Cowlishaw dined at the Grand Imperial. On
Monday Councillor Ephraim Clowes called. On Tuesday Mrs Clowes's daughter
brought three children and a sister-in-law. On Wednesday two aldermen,
a theatre manager, and the wife of a pottery manufacturer
requested appointments. By Friday Miss Bostock had begun a second
page in the book.

Rannoch, meanwhile, went to a dentist in Bursley, who supplied
the temporary plate at full price and kept no teeth.
The footballer scored twice the following Saturday, though one newspaper
complained that his smile lacked its former menace.

As for Mr Cowlishaw, he never again mistook five-thirty for
half-past seven, and within six months he could sleep through
the trams, the actors, the carts, the furnaces, the steam
muezzins, and even the octagonal wheels of the market traffic.
Hanbridge had accepted him. This is not the same thing
as saying that he had become accustomed to Hanbridge; but
it is more important.

He had refused the greatest advertisement offered to any dentist
in the Five Towns, and had thereby secured nothing. He
had then fainted before the most influential woman in the
borough, and had thereby secured everything.

Such are the rewards of professional virtue.

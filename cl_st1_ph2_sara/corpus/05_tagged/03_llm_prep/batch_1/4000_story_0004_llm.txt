"Foolish Virgin," said Jehan Tugluk Khan, stroking the silver river
of his beard while the brazier sighed blue smoke between
them, "you ask whether love grows weary when it has
eaten from one dish too long, slept beneath one roof
too safely, and heard the same dear footstep until prophecy
becomes habit. You ask whether freedom preserves love, or whether
bondage proves it. These are not questions for merchants, accountants,
or parrots. Therefore open the seven doors of your hearing."

The Foolish Virgin folded her green shawl about her knees
and laughed.

"I have only two ears, Khan."

"Wisdom," said Jehan Tugluk Khan, "must build the other five."

Then he struck the floor with his ebony staff.

"LET ALL THE WISE CHILDREN LISTEN TO MY JATAKA!"

This is the tale.

In the year when the white peacocks nested on the
ruined walls of Gandhara, and a comet like a flaming
sword hung above the Vindhya hills, there ruled in the
city of Avanti a king named Vikramavati. He was young.
This fact explains much, excuses little, and has overturned more
kingdoms than drought.

His eyes were black almonds floating in milk; his limbs
were straight as sandalwood spears; his voice could persuade a
falcon from the clouds and a miser from one small
coin. He was generous because his treasury was full, fearless
because death had not yet contradicted him, and romantic because
no woman had refused him. When he rode through the
eastern gate, girls cast champak blossoms beneath the bronze hoofs
of his stallion; when he sat in judgment, poets compared
him to Indra, priests compared him to Manu, and courtiers—who
possessed more comparisons than convictions—compared him to everyone who had
ever worn gold.

His prime minister was Deo Singh, a square, elderly man
whose nose curved like the beak of a thoughtful kite.
He knew the price of grain in eleven provinces, the
ambitions of forty-seven cousins, the secret debts of six priests,
and the precise moment at which a king's noble emotion
becomes a public inconvenience.

"A kingdom," Deo Singh often said, "is a horse with
too many riders and only one stomach."

The young king did not always listen. Wise counsel is
a cold dish, and youth prefers sweetmeats blazing with saffron.

Now south of Avanti lay the Kingdom of the Three
Rivers, a hot green land of black soil, red temples,
pearl fisheries, banana groves, cinnamon gardens, and hills on whose
slopes the clouds came down like celestial elephants to bathe.
Its king defied Vikramavati, not because he was strong enough
to win, but because pride is a drummer who makes
even the lame march to battle.

The armies met beside the River Kistna.

For seven days the sky wore iron. War elephants screamed
beneath embroidered towers; horses broke like waves against forests of
spears; arrows darkened noon; drums bellowed; conches cried to gods
who, being accustomed to human noise, gave no visible answer.
Crimson ran into green water. Golden banners fell into brown
mud. The southern king was defeated, his palace opened, his
treasury measured, his idols inventoried, and his surviving household divided
among the victors according to the ancient mathematics by which
misery is given orderly names.

Among the captives stood the defeated king's daughter, Vasantasena.

She had been found beneath a fallen pavilion after the
battle, surrounded by dead guards and fragments of a moon-white
chariot. Three soldiers had reached for her. Before their hands
touched her, Madusadan, captain of horse, came riding through the
dust.

Madusadan was older than the king by twenty years and
younger than the mountains by a lifetime. His shoulders were
broad as the lintel of a temple gate; his hands,
scarred and bronze, rested quietly upon his sword; gray had
entered his hair at the temples like winter entering a
forest without conquering it. He spoke seldom, and because he
spoke seldom, his words did not wander about naked like
beggars. Men trusted him in battle. Horses trusted him always.

"Stand away," he told the soldiers.

"She is spoil," said one.

"She is under my protection," said Madusadan.

"By whose order?"

"Mine."

The soldier laughed, for there are men who confuse cruelty
with courage until courage unsheathes itself.

Madusadan did not unsheathe his sword. He merely looked at
him. Slowly, slowly, the soldier's laughter became a cough, the
cough became silence, and the silence became obedience.

Madusadan dismounted. He removed his bloodstained cloak and placed it
around Vasantasena's shoulders without staring at the beauty it concealed.
He gave her water without touching her mouth. When her
knees failed, he lifted her, but held her as a
priest holds an offering entrusted to him—firmly, reverently, without theft.

"Princess," he said, "your father's kingdom is lost."

"My father?"

"Dead."

"My brothers?"

"Dead."

"My freedom?"

He looked toward the smoke-covered south.

"That answer does not belong to me."

It was then, says the tale, that the first seed
fell. Not when he lifted her. Not when he defended
her. Not when his arm enclosed her trembling body. It
fell when he refused to pretend that the key to
her freedom hung at his belt.

For desire may be awakened by beauty, but love often
wakes when it is understood.

Vasantasena was brought to Avanti in a caravan of conquered
magnificence: elephants draped in purple cloth, wagons of carved ivory,
cages of blue parrots, jars of musk, bales of silk,
bronze gods, emerald-handled daggers, and maidens whose anklets had been
removed so that their grief should make no music. She
entered the Hall of a Hundred Pillars wearing Madusadan's plain
cloak over a torn garment of white cotton.

Vikramavati saw her.

The sun, which had been singing upon the gilded roof,
stopped to listen.

Her hair descended in black coils, serpents tamed by moonlight.
Her forehead shone pale and serene above brows curved like
the bows of twin archers. Her eyes were deep wells
in which midnight had dropped two stars. Her mouth possessed
the rose-red gravity of fruit not yet tasted; her throat
was a slender marble column; her waist had the proud
inward curve of a lioness; her feet, dust-stained from captivity,
were nevertheless delicate enough to shame the lotus.

She did not lower her gaze.

The king forgot the victory proclamation prepared by three poets
and copied by nine scribes. He forgot the ambassadors, the
drums, the tribute, and even the jeweled sword he was
supposed to receive. Love—or what youth often calls love before
wisdom has inspected it—fell on him like summer lightning upon
a dry forest.

"Who is she?" he whispered.

"The daughter of your late enemy," said Deo Singh.

"A princess?"

"A captive."

"Then she is free."

Thus generosity leaped swiftly from his tongue, and all the
courtiers praised it as though they had suggested it.

Vikramavati descended from the throne. Before the assembled court he
broke the copper tablet on which Vasantasena's servitude had been
recorded.

"You are no slave," he said. "No hand in my
kingdom shall command you. Choose a palace, servants, gardens, horses—whatever
may soften sorrow."

"My sorrow is not a cushion, Majesty," she replied. "It
cannot be softened by embroidery."

The court inhaled as one creature.

The king smiled, delighted by resistance because he had never
yet learned its endurance.

"Then let embroidery attend you while you despise it."

He gave her the House of Pomegranates beside the royal
gardens. He gave her twenty maidservants, four musicians, two physicians,
seven white mares, a chariot plated in silver, and a
fountain whose water passed through a golden flute and fell
singing into a basin of green stone. He gave her
Kashmir shawls light enough to be drawn through a ring;
veils from Bukhara; crimson brocade from Cathay; sandalwood boxes from
Malabar; mirrors backed with beaten silver; lamps of rock crystal;
carpets on which hunters pursued gazelles forever without catching them.

He gave her diamonds cold as imprisoned dawn, rubies dark
as sacrificial blood, sapphires in which tiny skies appeared to
dream, cat's-eyes, moonstones, amber full of ancient sunlight, pearls pink
as the fingernails of Lakshmi, emeralds green as monsoon rice,
coral branches, topazes, opals, turquoise, lapis lazuli, and one black
pearl said to have formed from a tear shed by
the ocean when the moon abandoned it.

He has given, whispered the court.

He has given palaces.

He has given horses.

He has given gems.

He has given freedom.

Surely, said those who weigh the heart in merchant's scales,
she must give love.

But love is the one coin that becomes false when
paid as debt.

Vikramavati visited the House of Pomegranates each evening. He came
perfumed with rose and vetiver, clothed in silk the color
of ripe mangoes, accompanied by flutes, trays of sweetmeats, tame
gazelles, dancing children, Persian singers, and once a dwarf carrying
a mechanical nightingale that sang when hot water was poured
into its golden stomach.

Vasantasena received him courteously.

He recited verses comparing her arms to moonbeams, her breasts
to golden jars, her silence to a shrine, and his
passion to an ocean.

At last she said, "Majesty, an ocean asks the river
to lose its name."

"I ask only that you love me."

"You ask what cannot be commanded."

"Have I commanded? I have freed you."

"You speak of freedom every time you wish to collect
its price."

The king grew pale.

"I would give my life for you."

"That is still your life."

"My throne!"

"Your throne."

"My kingdom!"

"Your kingdom."

"What then must I give?"

Vasantasena looked at him with neither cruelty nor fear.

"Attention."

"I attend you every hour!"

"No, Majesty. You attend your passion. You hear your longing,
your sacrifice, your sleeplessness, your generosity. You kneel before the
image of yourself in love and call the image by
my name."

This was a hard saying. Hard sayings enter the proud
heart as arrows enter a shield: they may remain there
without reaching the flesh.

Vikramavati departed in anger, returned in hope, departed in despair,
and returned with greater gifts. He ordered a garden planted
overnight with a thousand rosebushes. He released prisoners on her
birthday. He abolished a tax on widows because she frowned
at it. These acts were good, and yet goodness performed
as courtship carries a hidden petition.

Meanwhile Madusadan avoided the House of Pomegranates.

He drilled cavalry at dawn, inspected frontier posts, judged remounts,
mended saddles with his own hands, and found reasons to
be wherever Vasantasena was not. He had desired her from
the moment she stood amid the ruins with smoke behind
her and defiance before her; but his desire wore armor.

She saw him sometimes across the royal enclosure—a bronze figure
beside a white horse, patient beneath the shrill commands of
young officers. He never approached. If their eyes met, he
bowed and turned away.

Thus absence began its work.

The king laid carpets toward her; Madusadan left a road
untrodden. The king covered her in jewels; Madusadan had covered
her in a bloody cloak. The king spoke for hours
of love; Madusadan had asked nothing. Restraint became a hand
touching her from a distance. Silence became a deep drum
beneath her ribs.

One afternoon Vasantasena sent a servant to request that the
captain inspect her mares.

Madusadan came with two grooms.

She dismissed the grooms.

He did not look pleased.

"The gray mare is lame," she said.

"She is not."

"You have not examined her."

"I watched her cross the courtyard."

"Then perhaps another horse is lame."

"Perhaps."

"Will you look?"

"At the horses?"

"At me."

Madusadan's breath changed, but his hands remained still.

"That would be unwise."

"So I have observed. You are a warehouse of wisdom."

"Princess—"

"I am not a princess."

"You are to me."

"Then obey me."

"No."

That word opened more doors in her than the king's
thousand gifts.

"Why do you avoid me?"

"Because I am loyal to Vikramavati."

"Loyalty forbids conversation?"

"Not conversation."

"What does it forbid?"

His eyes met hers. They were not the eyes of
a boy begging his own dream to be reflected. They
saw her grief, her loneliness, her pride, her body, and
the danger of all four.

"It forbids this," he said.

Nothing had happened, yet everything had been named.

Vasantasena stepped closer. The courtyard smelled of dust, horse sweat,
jasmine, and approaching rain.

"You carried me from death."

"I carried you from a field."

"You protected me."

"I performed a soldier's duty."

"You desired me."

Madusadan closed his eyes.

"Princess, mercy."

"Is truth cruelty?"

"Sometimes truth is fire. One may warm one's hands by
it, cook by it, worship it—and still refuse to burn
down the house."

She touched the scar across his right hand.

He trembled once. It was the smallest movement she had
ever seen and the most powerful. Vikramavati had placed a
kingdom at her feet without awakening her; this man's single
tremor filled her with summer.

"I have waited," she whispered.

"You must not."

"I waited while the moon whitened my chamber. I waited
while the fountain played its foolish golden song. I waited,
waited, waited, and every night your refusal sat beside me
more intimately than another man's devotion."

He drew away.

She let him go.

Patience is not coldness. Patience is flame taught to remain
upon the altar.

Days passed. Monsoon clouds gathered like blue-black armies. The mango
leaves turned their pale undersides to the wind. Vasantasena and
Madusadan met in gardens, stables, temple corridors, always briefly, always
guardedly. They spoke first of horses, then of war, then
of her father, whom he had seen die bravely. He
did not flatter the dead. She loved him for that.
He told her when she was unjust. She loved him
painfully for that. He listened when sorrow returned without warning.
She loved him most for that.

Love can begin in the eyes.

Love can descend to the lips.

Love can thunder in the blood.

But unless it learns the road to the listening heart,
it remains a traveler and never becomes a citizen.

One night the rains broke over Avanti. Water rushed from
gargoyles, temple bells shuddered in the wind, and lightning opened
white doors in the sky. Vasantasena found Madusadan beneath the
pavilion of blue tiles, where he had taken shelter after
inspecting the flooded stables.

No servants were near.

"You must leave," he said.

"I have left," she replied. "I left fear. I left
gratitude. I left the prison in which the king's kindness
placed me. Where else should I go?"

"To safety."

"You are my safety."

"That is why I am dangerous."

She stood before him, rain bright upon her hair. "I
do not ask you to rescue me. I ask you
to know me."

"I know you."

"Then know this."

She put his scarred hand against her heart.

The disciplined captain of horse, who had held a broken
line while elephants charged, who had watched friends die without
abandoning command, who had carried thirst through desert marches and
silence through nights of temptation, surrendered.

He kissed her.

The storm entered the pavilion. Thunder became a drum of
marriage; the rain flung silver garlands across the garden; jasmine
flowers shook loose and whitened the floor. His arms enclosed
her, not as when he had carried her from battle,
but as earth encloses seed—with strength, darkness, promise. Her mouth
opened beneath his; her fingers entered the gray at his
temples; and the long patience between them broke like a
river gate.

Love's play is worship when each kneels before the freedom
of the other.

But kings have doors everywhere.

Vikramavati, coming unannounced with a necklace of yellow diamonds, saw
them.

For one instant he stood motionless. The necklace slipped from
his hand. The gems scattered across the wet stones like
fragments of a murdered sun.

Then youth, grief, majesty, humiliation, jealousy, and the memory of
every gift he had given rose together within him, a
many-headed demon.

"Seize them!"

Guards poured into the pavilion.

Madusadan reached for his sword, then stopped. To resist was
to stain Vasantasena with rebellion. He allowed iron hands to
bind him.

Vasantasena did not kneel.

In the Hall of a Hundred Pillars, Vikramavati sat upon
the lion throne while rain lashed the bronze shutters. His
face was gray clay; his eyes were coals buried in
ash.

"You," he said to Madusadan, "ate my salt."

"I did."

"You commanded my horse."

"I did."

"You betrayed me."

"I loved her."

"That is your confession?"

"That is the truth."

The king turned to Vasantasena.

"And you—you whom I freed from slavery, clothed in silk,
loaded with wealth, sheltered from every insult—"

"You sheltered me from every insult but ownership."

"I never owned you!"

"You believed each kindness purchased a room within me."

"I offered devotion."

"You offered hunger and called it a feast."

Vikramavati struck the arm of his throne.

"What has he given that I did not?"

Vasantasena answered softly, and softness cut deeper than a shout.

"Space in which I could desire him."

The king rose.

"Then take eternity! At dawn you shall be chained to
separate pillars in the Place of Vultures. Water shall stand
one step beyond your reach. You shall watch each other
die slowly, and when death comes, your bodies shall hang
until the winds have divided what treason joined."

The courtiers bowed their heads. Even the rain seemed ashamed.

Madusadan looked at Vasantasena.

"Do not fear."

"I do not."

"The body is one province only."

"I will meet you beyond its border."

"If we are reborn as trees?"

"Our roots will find each other."

"As birds?"

"We will share one wind."

"As rivers?"

"We will mingle before the sea."

"As dust?"

"The same footstep will lift us."

Vikramavati cried, "Silence!"

But death enlarges love when fear has no entrance. A
king may command tongues; he cannot command the meaning carried
by silence.

Then Deo Singh bowed.

"Majesty, may your servant speak?"

"No."

"Excellent. I speak best without permission."

The king glared, but Deo Singh continued.

"Your proposed punishment is majestic, terrible, poetic, and useless."

"Useless?"

"Entirely. You would give them distance, thirst, approaching death, stars
overhead, vultures as witnesses, and eternity as a subject of
conversation. These are costly spices in the kitchen of passion.
By sunset they would become a legend. By next year
shepherd boys would sing of them. By the tenth year
priests would sell little clay images of their embrace."

A courtier coughed to hide a laugh.

"What punishment do you propose?" asked the king.

"The most ancient and dreadful."

"Name it."

"Constant companionship."

The court blinked.

Deo Singh folded his hands over his round stomach.

"Chain them together. Not at a distance—close. Lock them for
three days in the Pavilion of Pleasure. Give them every
comfort: food, wine, music, cushions, perfumes, hot water, cold water,
sweetmeats, fruit, books, dice, chess, and one another. No servants
within. No escape. No mystery. No noble danger. Let each
breath be heard, each appetite observed, each silence misinterpreted. Let
one wish to sleep when the other wishes to speak.
Let them discover how often the beloved scratches, yawns, contradicts,
spills syrup, or tells the same story twice."

The king stared.

Deo Singh warmed to his doctrine.

"Poets praise moonlight because they do not pay its oil
bill. Lovers adore closeness because doors remain available. Remove the
door! Passion is a tiger in the jungle; confinement makes
it a rug. Within one day they will argue. Within
two they will blame. Within three each will beg to
be chained to a crocodile instead."

"Is this written in scripture?" asked Vikramavati.

"Not yet, Majesty. But scripture is frequently experience copied by
someone with better handwriting."

Despite himself, the king smiled.

"Do it."

So the lovers were led to the Pavilion of Pleasure,
an octagonal chamber built by Vikramavati's grandfather for a queen
he had loved every third Thursday. Its walls were painted
with gardens where eternally young couples pursued one another among
eternally flowering trees. The floor was covered with carpets from
Samarkand, thick as moss. There were low couches heaped with
rose, purple, and gold cushions; silver lamps perfumed with aloes;
a marble bath; a fountain; trays of pistachios, dates, figs,
peaches, pomegranates, sugared violets, rice cakes, spiced lamb, pickled mangoes,
and honey.

In the center waited a chain.

It was not a dungeon chain. Deo Singh possessed theatrical
judgment. The links were polished steel; each was slender enough
to appear delicate, strong enough to restrain an elephant's intention.
One end was fastened around Madusadan's left wrist, the other
around Vasantasena's right. Between them hung six feet of iron.

"Three days," said Deo Singh. "At sunset on the third,
the king shall hear what remains of your love."

"Only three?" asked Vasantasena.

The minister raised one eyebrow.

"Confidence," he said, "is passion's favorite disguise."

The doors closed.

At first the chain was an enemy. When Vasantasena crossed
toward the fruit, Madusadan was pulled from the couch. When
he reached for water, she dropped a book. When one
turned, the other must turn; when one rose, the other
rose; when one paused, iron made impatience audible.

They laughed.

"That will pass," said a guard outside.

Inside, Madusadan examined the chain.

"Six feet," he said. "Deo Singh is generous."

"Too generous."

She wound one link around her wrist and shortened the
distance.

Passion was the first link.

They ate pomegranate seeds from the same bowl. Juice stained
Vasantasena's lips crimson, and Madusadan kissed it away. The painted
couples upon the walls seemed poor ghosts beside them. The
fountain sang; the lamps breathed fragrance; the chain moved over
silk with the whisper of a snake blessed by gods.

Yet passion is a splendid guest and an incompetent householder.
Before midnight, inconvenience arrived.

Madusadan wanted the lamp dimmed. Vasantasena wanted to read.

"You said the flame hurt your eyes," she remarked.

"I was struck above the brow at Ujjain."

"You never told me."

"You never asked about Ujjain."

"I have asked about every scar."

"You asked in circumstances unfavorable to military history."

She laughed so violently the chain shook.

Later she slept diagonally, taking more than half the couch.
Madusadan tried the carpet. The chain pulled her arm.

"Where are you going?"

"To invade the floor."

"You cannot sleep there."

"I have slept in saddles."

"Then imagine the couch is a broad, perfumed saddle."

Patience was the second link.

Toward morning she woke from a dream of her father's
death. She sat upright, gasping, and dragged Madusadan from sleep.
He asked no foolish question. He did not tell her
it was only a dream, for sorrow does not become
unreal merely because it visits by night. He held her
until the dead withdrew.

Understanding was the third link.

At dawn they bathed awkwardly, laughing at the iron between
them. They fed one another because each had only one
hand entirely free. Vasantasena combed gray dust from Madusadan's hair;
he untangled hers with a warrior's concentration and a child's
uncertainty.

Service was the fourth link.

By noon they argued.

She spoke of fleeing north if released. He said the
passes would soon close with snow. She accused him of
thinking first as a captain and only second as a
lover. He replied that a lover who ignores winter becomes
a frozen fool. She said she had lived beneath commands
long enough. He said weather was not monarchy.

Her anger rose scarlet.

His became bronze and silent.

The chain between them seemed heavier. They sat apart at
its full length while the fountain's golden note became intolerable.

Outside, Deo Singh heard no voices and nodded sagely.

"Behold," he whispered to the king, who had come secretly
to listen. "The tiger approaches rughood."

But inside, Vasantasena looked at the iron joining them.

"This chain," she said, "does not yield because I am
angry."

"No."

"Nor because you are obstinate."

"Practical."

"Obstinate."

"Very well."

"If we pull against it, it wounds us both."

Madusadan looked at the red mark upon her wrist.

"I am sorry."

"So am I."

Forgiveness was the fifth link.

They discussed the northern road again. This time he listened
to what flight meant to her: not merely escape, but
the recovery of a life in which no king named
her fate. She listened to what winter meant to him:
not an argument to defeat her, but avalanches, starving horses,
and her body blue beneath snow.

Tolerance was the sixth link.

They chose to travel west.

Friendship was the seventh.

On the second evening monotony entered, dressed as a courtier
and carrying no announcement. They had eaten, talked, slept, bathed,
remembered, planned, kissed, argued, and played chess. Vasantasena defeated Madusadan
twice.

"You moved the elephant illegally," he said.

"The elephant is conquered. Its laws are mine."

"You would make a dangerous king."

"I would abolish chess."

"That is why princesses must be restrained."

She threw a fig at him. He caught it in
his mouth.

Humor was the eighth link.

Then they grew quiet. No declaration could be made that
had not already been made. No touch was new merely
because it was desired. The chamber, so sumptuous at first,
shrank around them. The painted lovers grinned idiotically from the
walls. The perfumes cloyed. Honey became glue upon the tongue.
Even the fountain seemed to repeat one silver word until
that word lost meaning.

Vasantasena said, "Perhaps Deo Singh is right."

Madusadan did not answer too quickly.

"Perhaps."

"Are you weary of me?"

"I am weary."

The truth chilled her.

"Of me?"

"Of confinement. Of sweet food. Of that fountain. Of being
unable to scratch my left shoulder without negotiating a treaty."

She smiled faintly.

"But not of you?"

Madusadan lifted the chain.

"You are not this."

"What am I?"

"Someone I must continue to discover."

"You know me."

"I know what you have revealed. I know what I
have been capable of seeing. Tomorrow you may grieve differently.
Next year you may become braver, harsher, kinder, more foolish.
I may disappoint you in ways I cannot yet invent.
If I decide that I know you completely, I will
cease to look. Then I shall love a memory while
a living woman stands neglected before me."

Tenderness was the ninth link.

She placed her forehead against his.

"And if I change beyond your liking?"

"I must tell you."

"And if I fail you?"

"I must not pretend you have not."

"And if love becomes difficult?"

"It is already difficult."

"Then what saves it?"

"Nothing once and forever."

Fulfillment was the tenth link—not the lazy fullness of one
who has eaten, but the deep sufficiency of two who
can hunger without believing themselves betrayed.

On the third day they ceased fighting the chain. They
moved with it. When Vasantasena rose, Madusadan rose before the
iron tightened. When he reached for the water jar, she
turned her wrist. Their gestures became a dance; the chain,
once bondage, became measure and rhythm. It warned them when
one moved too swiftly, called each back when either forgot
the other, and shone between them like a narrow road
laid across dangerous country.

At sunset Vikramavati and Deo Singh approached the pavilion in
soft shoes. The minister carried a satisfied expression prepared for
public use.

"They will now despise each other," he whispered. "We shall
hear accusation, pleading, perhaps litigation."

The king placed his ear against the carved door.

Inside, Vasantasena was speaking.

"When this chain is removed, I shall miss it."

"Then I will have a goldsmith make another," said Madusadan.

"No. Gold is too proud. Silver tarnishes. Iron remembers the
earth."

"What shall each link be called?"

"The first, passion."

"The second, patience."

"The third, understanding."

"Friendship."

"Tolerance."

"Tenderness."

"Forgiveness."

"Service."

"Humor."

"And the last?"

They were silent for a moment.

"Vigilance," said Madusadan.

"Why vigilance?"

"Because love is not preserved by being chained safely in
a chamber. Nor by oaths, nor marriage fires, nor shared
children, nor the habit of one bed. Security may become
sleep, and sleep may become neglect. We must never say,
'She is mine, therefore I need not win her today,'
or, 'He loves me, therefore I may cease to know
him.'"

Vasantasena answered, "Then our chain must always be loose enough
for freedom and strong enough for remembrance."

"Yes."

"And if fear enters?"

"We speak."

"If anger enters?"

"We listen."

"If weariness enters?"

"We rest."

"If vanity enters?"

"We laugh."

"If death enters?"

"We go through it together."

Outside the door, Deo Singh's satisfied expression collapsed like an
umbrella in monsoon wind.

The king listened. Each word entered the empty chambers of
his own devotion, where gifts lay piled and no beloved
dwelt. He understood at last—not fully, for understanding is not
a mango that ripens in one afternoon, but enough.

He ordered the doors opened.

Vasantasena and Madusadan stood facing him, the polished chain between
their wrists.

Vikramavati looked older than he had three days before.

"I meant this chain as punishment," he said.

"Majesty," replied Vasantasena, "men often name things before they know
what those things are."

He turned to Madusadan.

"You betrayed my trust."

"I did."

"You love her."

"I do."

"And she loves you."

"She must answer."

Vasantasena smiled.

"I do."

There it was: the answer no treasure had bought, no
throne compelled, no threat extinguished.

Vikramavati drew his sword.

The guards stiffened. Madusadan moved before Vasantasena, and because the
chain joined them, she moved with him.

But the king struck only the central link. Once, twice,
three times the sword rang. The iron broke.

"You are free," he said.

Vasantasena rubbed her wrist.

"We were free before."

"Yes," said the king. "That was what I failed to
understand."

He restored Madusadan's rank, then relieved him of it at
the captain's request. He gave them two horses, provisions, plain
clothing, and safe conduct to the western frontier. No jewels.
No palace. No fountain with a golden flute. At last
the king had learned to give what was needed rather
than what displayed the giver.

Deo Singh watched them depart beneath a rose-red dawn.

"My theory," he complained, "was flawless."

"Except in this case," said Vikramavati.

"A case is merely an exception wearing evidence."

The king laughed.

And because laughter is sometimes the first door through which
wisdom enters, he became in later years a ruler less
dazzling and more just.

As for Madusadan and Vasantasena, songs disagree. One says they
crossed the desert and bred horses near the Persian sea.
Another says they returned south and raised a palace upon
the ruins of her father's citadel. A third says they
became wandering physicians, healing soldiers of wounds no king considered
glorious.

But all the songs agree that they carried one broken
link of iron wrapped in red silk.

Whenever love grew careless, they unwrapped it.

Whenever gratitude pretended to be desire, they looked at it.

Whenever passion demanded ownership, they touched its cold curve.

Whenever familiarity said, "I know all," they remembered the locked
pavilion and began again.

For passion alone is flame, and flame consumes.

Patience alone is stone, and stone does not embrace.

Friendship without desire is a lamp at noon.

Desire without friendship is lightning in dry grass.

Tolerance without truth becomes cowardice.

Truth without tenderness becomes a knife.

Forgiveness without memory invites the wound again.

Memory without forgiveness poisons the cup.

Service without humor becomes slavery.

Humor without service becomes mockery.

And love without vigilance lies down upon the royal cushions,
closes its jeweled eyes, and dreams that because it was
alive yesterday it cannot die tomorrow.

Jehan Tugluk Khan ceased speaking. The brazier had fallen to
red embers. Beyond the lattice, the moon hung over the
sleeping roofs like a silver begging bowl.

The Foolish Virgin sat very still.

"At first," she said, "the chain made them prisoners."

"Yes."

"Then it made them mindful."

"Yes."

"And when it was broken, they kept its meaning."

Jehan Tugluk Khan nodded.

"Now you have built a third ear."

"Only a third?"

"Wisdom is economical with praise."

She considered the tale and then asked, "Did they never
grow tired of one another?"

"Certainly."

"Did they never quarrel again?"

"Frequently."

"Did they remain in love?"

"That," said the Khan, "is not the absence of weariness
or quarrel. It is the art of returning without pretending
one never departed."

He lifted his ebony staff and struck the floor once
more.

"This is the tale. The chain that compels is bondage;
the chain that reminds is devotion. Love must possess passion,
patience, fulfillment, friendship, tolerance, understanding, tenderness, forgiveness, service, and humor—but
above all it must keep watch at its own gate.

"For love demands eternal vigilance.

"LET ALL THE WISE CHILDREN REMEMBER MY JATAKA!"

## The Last Hand of the Poker Steer

On a warm night in April, when the Nueces ran
shallow and secretive between its banks, and the young mesquite
wore a mist of green against the moonlight, a brindled
cow withdrew from the grazing herd and made her way
into a bend of the river. The moon, narrow and
yellow as a hunter's horn, stood over the chaparral; frogs
called from a muddy lagoon; and now and then a
night bird swept low above the water, leaving silence folded
behind its wings.

The cow knew nothing of moons or omens. She knew
only that her hour had come, and that the open
prairie, though broad enough for ten thousand cattle, was no
place for the business before her. In a hollow screened
by willow and cane, she brought forth a bull calf.

He lay at first like a wet, limp rag, his
legs tangled beneath him and his black nose pressed into
the sand. His dam turned at once, lowing in that
deep, private voice which cattle reserve for their young, and
began licking him with a tongue rough enough to stir
the blood under his hide. Presently he sneezed, lifted his
head, and regarded the night with large eyes full of
astonishment.

It was a considerable world for one so recently introduced
to it. There were scents of wet earth, milk, crushed
weeds, cowhide, and distant water; there were the tremblings of
insects in the grass and the soft slap of the
river against its bank. His mother's breathing seemed to him
the central law of creation. When she moved, he struggled
to follow. He tried one leg, then another, rose halfway,
and collapsed. He rested, gathered courage, and tried again. Before
the moon had lowered to the western brush, he stood
beneath her flank, uncertain as a man on a rolling
deck, and found the warm reward prepared for him.

At daylight the cow led him from the willow brake.
He was red along the sides, dark about the muzzle
and legs, and carried down the whole ridge of his
back a clean white stripe, as if some careless painter
had drawn a chalk line from his withers to the
root of his tail. This distinction, which no doubt meant
nothing to him at the time, was to identify him
through brands, winters, poker games, disputed ownerships, and nearly three
thousand miles of country.

The herd received him without ceremony. Cows sniffed him, yearlings
stared, and a bull regarded him for an instant with
the indifference of established power. Near the center of the
herd stood another new calf, a little speckled heifer whose
coat was white, red, and brown in patches. She trembled
like a fawn when he approached, but curiosity overcame her,
and by noon the two were lying together in the
shade of a live oak while their mothers grazed nearby.

Thus began the first and happiest partnership in the life
of the subject of this sketch.

All that spring he learned the pleasant necessities of being
a calf. He learned that grass was good to nose
before it was good to eat, that a horse was
an object of suspicion, that a man on foot was
worse, and that a man mounted and swinging a rope
was an outrage against nature. He learned to cross the
river by keeping close to his mother's shoulder, to leap
at the sudden whir of quail, and to run with
his tail in the air for no reason beyond the
glorious discovery that he possessed legs.

The speckled heifer shared these investigations. They raced through patches
of bluebonnet, butted foreheads in imitation of their elders, and
lay chewing imaginary cuds while cicadas shrilled in the thorn
trees. At evening, when the heat went out of the
ground and long shadows reached eastward, they frisked about their
dams with the solemn foolishness peculiar to the very young.

His mother was patient, protective, and in his estimation perfect.
She washed the dust from his coat, called him from
dangerous bogs, and placed herself between him and every disturbance.
Once a coyote crept too near the bedded herd. The
cow charged with lowered head, and the wolf vanished into
the brush as though jerked away by a string. From
this the calf concluded that his dam controlled coyotes, weather,
darkness, and the movements of all respectable cattle.

Summer hardened him. The river shrank into green pools joined
by threads of warm water, dust whitened the trails, and
the sun lay upon the range with the weight of
a branding iron. He became leaner, quicker, and less dependent
upon milk. His white dorsal stripe stood out against his
darkening body, and the vaqueros began to notice him.

"There goes the line-back," said one.

"He'll make a steer worth watching," answered another.

The calf, hearing only voices and the creak of saddle
leather, ran to his mother.

Autumn brought northers that bent the grass flat and drove
dust into every hollow. Then came winter, uncommon in its
bitterness for that region. Rain turned to sleet; the river
flats glazed at night; and cattle drifted south before a
wind that seemed to enter through hide and bone. The
line-back stood on the leeward side of his mother, shivering
from muzzle to tail. His playmate pressed close to her
own dam, and for several days the two calves had
neither inclination nor strength for sport.

Some cattle died standing in the brush, held upright by
thorns after life had left them. Others sank in mud
about the water holes. The line-back survived because his dam
knew the country and kept moving him from shelter to
shelter, and because youth, having no memories of better seasons,
accepts misery as merely another arrangement of the world.

When spring returned, it came suddenly. Wildflowers covered the low
country, water shone in every draw, and the poor survivors
cast off winter hair in ragged handfuls. With grass rising
about his knees, the line-back forgot cold as completely as
a prosperous man forgets hunger.

Then occurred the first momentous day in his life.

The rancheros gathered the cattle for the spring round-up, driving
scattered bunches toward the branding-pens. For three days the air
was full of dust, bawling, hoofbeats, and the yells of
riders cutting out cows and calves. The line-back stayed near
his dam, but all the wisdom she had shown against
flood, coyote, and norther was useless before mounted men.

A rope sailed through the air and dropped over his
head. He sprang backward. It tightened. The world became choking
dust and violent astonishment. A second loop caught his hind
legs, and two horses drew apart. He struck the ground
with a force that knocked breath and dignity from him
together.

Hands seized him. Someone knelt upon his neck. Another pulled
his legs straight. He saw the fire, smelled scorched hair
from calves ahead of him, and heard their cries; but
he could not understand that these preparations concerned him until
the hot iron touched his side.

Pain broke over him in a white flash. He bellowed,
struggled, and felt the iron lifted and applied again, shaping
upon his hide the ranch mark—a bar, a circle, and
another bar. Before he had gathered himself, the knife did
its work, and a deeper sickness followed the burning. He
was released at last and stumbled blindly through the gate.

His mother came to him. She licked his face and
called, but there are injuries against which even maternal government
has no authority. He stood with legs wide apart, trembling,
while flies gathered at the wounds and smoke from the
branding fire drifted above the pens.

He wanted to die.

The speckled heifer had suffered only the brand. She approached
him timidly, sniffed his scorched side, and remained near while
evening settled over the camp. By morning he could walk,
though stiffly; within a week he could graze; and before
summer he had learned, as animals and men must learn,
that pain may be remembered long after it ceases to
command attention.

He was now a steer, and therefore assigned by civilization
a peaceful and profitable future.

Two years passed over the Nueces range. The line-back grew
tall, broad-horned, and muscular. His white stripe became more remarkable
as his coat darkened, and he carried himself with the
importance of one aware of admiration. The speckled heifer matured
beside him, trim in flank and gentle of eye. Their
calfhood companionship altered into something more ceremonious. He followed her
to water, kept other yearlings from her chosen shade, and
regarded himself, so far as a steer may entertain such
illusions, as her natural protector and lord.

But springtime came, and with it a red, heavy-shouldered steer
from an outlying pasture. He was older, wider between the
eyes, and had one horn chipped from some former dispute.
The speckled heifer looked at him with interest.

The line-back objected.

They met one morning on a bare patch near the
river. The contest began with sidelong approaches, pawing, and threatening
tosses of the head; then their horns locked, and the
prairie narrowed to dust, breath, and straining muscle. For a
moment the line-back held. Pride ran through him like fire.
Then the red steer shifted, twisted, and drove.

The line-back went backward. His hind feet furrowed the earth.
He recovered, charged, and received the point of a horn
in his shoulder. Blood ran down his leg. The red
steer came on with the calm brutality of superior strength
and sent him rolling into a clump of prickly pear.

When the defeated champion rose, one eye was swollen, his
shoulder burned, and pieces of cactus clung to his hide.
The speckled heifer stood beside the victor.

No narrator should pretend to determine the exact borders of
an animal's grief, yet humiliation is not limited to men.
The line-back remained apart from the herd all day. He
would not graze near his former companion, and when she
approached the water at sunset, he turned away.

His dam offered no comfort. She had borne another calf,
a small yellow creature with absurd ears, and all her
attention belonged to the newcomer. When the line-back came near,
hoping perhaps for some renewal of the old law, she
lowered her head and drove him off. He stopped several
yards away and stared at her. She licked the yellow
calf and forgot him.

Within one week he had lost mother, sweetheart, and rank.
Home, which until then had seemed the fixed center of
existence, became merely the place where his defeats were known.

He resolved to leave it.

Opportunity came in early summer, when a trail herd passed
fifteen miles east of the ranch. The line-back first heard
it at dusk: a far, continuous lowing, mingled with the
faint calls of men. By morning the air carried the
smell of thousands of strange cattle and the dust of
their passage. He stood on a rise, head high, listening.

The herd moved north in a column that seemed without
end. Point riders guided the leaders; swing riders held the
flanks; drag men followed in a cloud of dust thick
enough to tan a hide. Wagons creaked behind, and loose
horses traveled under the care of a wrangler. It was
less a collection of cattle than a moving province, with
its own laws, servants, guards, and destination.

That afternoon the line-back deserted his home range. Keeping to
brush and low ground, he traveled toward the trail. Near
sundown he came upon the rear guard of the herd
and followed at a distance. A drag rider saw him.

"Stray coming in," the cowboy called.

"What brand?"

"Bar-circle-bar, looks like. South Texas steer."

"Let him come. We ain't riding back to Nueces County
to return one critter."

The line-back entered the dust, was jostled by tired strangers,
and found himself absorbed into the commercial movement of the
nation.

At first trail life delighted him. Each day opened new
country; each evening brought strange water and untrampled grass. He
no longer saw the red rival or the speckled heifer,
and no cow drove him from her calf. He had
traded humiliation for anonymity, which is often the first shape
freedom takes.

Yet freedom in a trail herd was bounded by horsemen.
If he drifted too far, an outrider turned him back.
If he lagged, the drag men urged him forward through
the dust. He crossed rivers because the leaders crossed, bedded
because the herd bedded, and rose at the cook's morning
racket as surely as the men. His act of self-determination
had placed him inside a machinery greater than any he
had known.

Still, he traveled willingly.

The country unfolded northward: black-soil prairies, belts of timber, steep-banked
creeks, open plains where the horizon appeared as round and
final as the edge of the sea. At river crossings
the cattle crowded down muddy banks and plunged into brown
water, swimming with noses high while mounted men shouted from
either side. The line-back learned to angle upstream and let
the current carry him toward the landing. Once a weaker
steer rolled beneath him, and for several seconds the water
was full of horns and desperate legs; but he reached
the opposite bank, shook himself, and began grazing before his
hide was dry.

At night the herd was held in a great compact
circle. Cowboys rode around it, singing not for art but
because a human voice soothed nervous cattle. The line-back heard
old ballads, trail songs, fragments of hymns, and verses improvised
concerning the cook's beans. Above him the stars appeared immeasurably
bright. The horsemen passed like sentries around the encampment of
some horned king's army, and the wind carried their thin
music over miles of grass.

One stormy night lightning struck near the leaders. The herd
rose as one body and ran.

The line-back ran with them. There was no choice, only
pressure, thunder, and fear. Horns struck his ribs; mud flew
into his eyes; the ground shook under ten thousand hooves.
Cowboys raced along the flanks, shouting and firing pistols, trying
to swing the leaders into a circle. For miles the
cattle poured across the prairie like floodwater. At last the
front bent, then curved, and the whole mass began milling
until exhaustion accomplished what horsemanship had begun.

Morning showed dead cattle, injured horses, and one cowboy with
a broken arm. The line-back bore a long scrape down
his side but remained sound. He had survived the Nueces
winter, the branding-pen, his rival's horn, and now the blind
violence of the herd. Confidence returned to him in a
sterner form.

Near the northern border of Texas, three cowboys sat down
to poker after supper. Their blankets were spread beside the
wagon, and a lantern, hung from the wheel, threw wavering
light over the cards. Money was scarce. One man played
a pair of silver spurs; another staked a month's claim
on a spotted saddle horse. The third, being nearly empty
of possessions and rich in invention, nodded toward the bedded
cattle.

"I'll put up that line-backed steer against ten dollars."

"That your steer?" asked the trail boss.

"He followed my side of the herd, didn't he?"

"He wears a bar-circle-bar."

"So he does, and I admire him for it. Ten
dollars says he's mine till someone proves otherwise."

Frontier title, like frontier theology, was often strongest where least
examined. The bet was accepted. The cowboy lost the hand,
and the line-back changed owners without waking.

The winner took pride in his acquisition for two days.
On the third he wagered the steer against a fancy
bridle and won. A week later he lost him to
the cook on three queens. The cook, who had no
horse suitable for tending his property, sold a half-interest to
the horse wrangler for four dollars and a plug of
tobacco. That joint ownership survived until both men entered the
same game and each put up his share. A quiet
point rider took the pot with a straight.

"There," said the cook, "goes the best-traveled chip in Texas."

From that evening the line-back was called the poker steer.

No animal was ever less consulted in his transfers. Men
pointed him out, assessed his weight, praised his horns, disputed
his brand, and risked him on the turn of a
card. He grazed. One night he belonged to a man
from San Patricio; by breakfast he was the property of
a rider from the Brazos; before noon the owner traded
his saddle and "all appurtenances, including one poker steer" for
a gray horse. The horse went lame, the saddle split,
but the steer continued north, increasing in value with every
mile of grass.

In Indian Territory the herd encountered an inspector charged with
examining Texas brands and identifying stolen stock. He was a
narrow man with a notebook, a quick eye, and an
exalted belief in documentation. The cattle were halted and worked
past him in dusty files.

When the line-back came through, the inspector raised his hand.

"Cut that steer out."

A rider moved in, separated him from the herd, and
held him near the wagon.

"Bar-circle-bar," said the inspector. "Registered on the Nueces to Don
Esteban Vela."

"He's mine," said a lank cowboy named Sorrels, who had
won the steer the night before with two small pair.

"Your name Vela?"

"No."

"You carry a bill of sale?"

"No, but I carry the six bits I paid to
sit in the game."

The inspector closed his notebook. "Stray property. I'll hold him
for the owner."

Sorrels looked at the steer, then at the inspector. "That
animal has changed hands more often than a campaign promise."

"Maybe so. His first brand hasn't."

The law had spoken. The trail herd moved on, leaving
the poker steer in a small holding pasture beside the
inspector's camp.

For the first time since joining the drive, he heard
the herd departing without him. Its lowing faded northward. He
walked the fence, tested the wire with his horns, and
called once. No answer came. By noon he stood under
a cottonwood, chewing without contentment.

Sorrels had ridden away with the others, but he had
not surrendered in spirit. That night, under a moon no
broader than the one which had watched the steer's birth,
he returned alone. He found the inspector asleep in a
tent and the line-back standing near the fence.

"Well, old gambler," he whispered, "seems the government drew a
high card on us."

He cut the wire.

The steer hesitated only a moment, then stepped through. Sorrels
drove him in a wide circle around the camp and
followed the trail herd until dawn. When the inspector discovered
the escape, he found hoofprints, cut wire, and no proof
acceptable in court. He suspected Sorrels, of course, but suspicion
without witnesses is merely conversation.

The cowboy did not immediately return the steer to the
herd. Instead he kept him among the remuda for half
a day, then eased him into the cattle while crossing
a creek, where tracks and numbers became confused. The poker
steer was again a traveler.

"You stole him," said the cook that evening.

"I rescued my lawful property."

"You never lawfully owned a hair on him."

"Then I rescued somebody else's lawful property. That's neighborly."

But the old brand had become dangerous. At a trading
camp farther north, Sorrels consulted a man who knew something
of irons and more of avoiding questions. They heated a
curved running iron and added spokes to the circle between
the bars, working patiently while the steer was thrown and
tied. When he rose, aching and enraged, the bar-circle-bar had
become a rough pilot wheel.

The alteration was clever enough at a distance and doubtful
at arm's length, which described many arrangements on the cattle
trail. The poker steer, who had once been marked to
establish ownership, was marked again to obscure it. His hide
had become a ledger in which men corrected the entries
with fire.

By autumn the herd reached a northern rail town. Some
cattle were shipped east, some sold locally, and others turned
onto winter range. The poker steer, then too rangy to
command his best price, went with a smaller bunch to
a ranch on the Republican River.

Sorrels lost him the last night before the crew separated.
He had a full house and wagered the steer against
forty dollars. Another man laid down four of a kind.

"That pilot-wheel beef is yours," Sorrels said. "Treat him kindly.
He's sensitive."

The new owner, Mack Haney, was a square-built cowman with
a black beard and a talent for winning at cards
without seeming pleased. He looked over his prize the next
morning.

"He'll be heavy by another summer," he said. "If he
doesn't walk off to Mexico."

Haney wintered the steer with three hundred others in a
sheltered valley. The season was cruel. Snow crusted the grass,
rivers sealed over, and the cattle worked all day to
uncover a few mouthfuls of dry forage. They drifted before
blizzards until riders turned them, their backs white, their heads
hanging. The poker steer remembered, perhaps in the obscure chambers
of animal memory, the winter of his calfhood and his
mother's warm flank. There was no mother now. There were
only other steers, equally cold, and men chopping holes in
river ice.

He survived.

The next spring found him gaunt but taller, his horns
wider, his body prepared to take flesh. Green grass repaired
him with miraculous speed. By midsummer his ribs disappeared beneath
smooth hide, his shoulders thickened, and cattle buyers began to
study him with the respectful greed accorded to profitable beauty.

"He was a stray when I got him," Haney told
one buyer, which was true in the spacious Western sense.

"What's that brand?"

"Pilot wheel."

"Looks worked over."

"All brands look worked over if you stare long enough."

Haney did not sell. Instead he lost the steer in
a poker game during a Fourth of July celebration. The
winner traded him for two yearling heifers. Their owner lost
him that autumn to a freighter, who sold him to
a rancher on the Platte. The rancher retained him for
a year, and during that interval the poker steer knew
fat grass, clear water, and little interference beyond the seasonal
round-ups.

He became a magnificent beef. His line-backed coloring remained vivid;
his horns swept outward and upward with impressive symmetry; and
he carried enough flesh to excite calculation in every experienced
eye. Men no longer valued him at ten dollars, a
bridle, or a plug of tobacco. Yet increased value brought
him no security. It merely raised the stakes.

He changed hands in bunkhouses, saloons, tents, and beside campfires.
Once he was lost on the final card of a
game played during a three-day rain. Once he was divided
into imaginary eighths among four partners, then reunited when one
man won all outstanding shares. Once a drunken rider staked
him without owning him, won the pot, and thereby escaped
detection. Another time two men argued so violently over title
that they agreed to settle the matter by a fresh
deal, and a bystander won the steer from both.

"Some cattle have pedigrees," Haney said when the story reached
him. "That one has a card record."

Years and miles accumulated. The white line down the steer's
back became known from the Platte to the Powder River.
Men who had never owned him claimed to have lost
him; men who had lost him claimed afterward that they
had given him away. His biography enlarged with each telling.
It was said that he could pick the winner in
a poker game by standing behind the lucky player, though
no one explained how a steer might enter a bunkhouse
to perform this service.

Through all these transactions he remained indifferent to cards but
attentive to gates. The impulse that had carried him from
the Nueces had not entirely died. Given a broken fence
or careless guard, he drifted. Yet every open country now
led into another pasture, another herd, another tally book. The
farther he traveled, the more thoroughly men's arrangements surrounded him.

Late in his fifth year, a cattle contractor assembled a
herd for delivery at the Fort Peck Indian Reservation in
Montana. The government had undertaken to furnish beef under treaty
obligations, and the contractor, who measured justice by the hundredweight
and profit by the difference between promise and delivery, purchased
cattle wherever they could be had cheaply.

The poker steer entered the contract herd through Mack Haney,
who had won him back in Cheyenne after an absence
of three years.

"I knew he'd come around," Haney said. "A good poker
chip returns to the table."

He admired the steer and might have kept him, but
admiration is seldom entered on the credit side of a
cattleman's account. The Fort Peck buyer offered a sound price.
Haney accepted, then won back half the sale money from
the buyer that night. By dawn the contractor had recovered
his losses and acquired the steer a second time in
the same game.

"You own him twice," Haney observed.

"Then he ought to weigh double."

The northward journey began.

This was not the youthful adventure of the first trail
herd. The poker steer was mature, deliberate, and heavy. He
wasted no strength in unnecessary movement. When pressed, he moved;
when released, he grazed. He knew the signals of riders,
the danger of soft riverbanks, the relief of bedding with
his nose away from the wind. Younger cattle followed his
calm example, and on difficult crossings he often entered water
among the first.

The land grew broader and lonelier. They crossed plains where
grass rolled unbroken beneath the wind and buttes rose like
ruined fortresses. Buffalo bones whitened old trails. At distant agencies,
forts, and river landings, representatives of the nation kept books
concerning beef, flour, blankets, boundaries, and human hunger. Contracts passed
through many hands; cattle lost weight on the road; figures
gained it in ledgers.

The herd traveled like a tribute collected for an empire,
though the empire was careless of both those who received
and those who furnished it. Cowboys, those vikings of the
Plain, rode beside the beeves in patched trousers, slept in
wet blankets, cursed the cook, and performed daily wonders for
monthly wages. The cattle advanced under the ancient compulsions of
thirst, grass, fear, and pressure. Above them all moved commerce,
invisible but more commanding than any point rider.

Autumn was beginning when they reached the Missouri country and
turned toward Fort Peck. Cottonwoods yellowed along the river. Frost
silvered the low grass at dawn. The poker steer had
traveled nearly three thousand miles, counting his wanderings, since he
first stood beneath his mother beside the Nueces.

The contract called for beef delivered alive, and alive the
cattle arrived. Thereafter came the killing.

At intervals, animals were selected and turned loose beyond the
agency, where mounted hunters pursued them. The practice preserved the
appearance of a hunt while assuring the result expected of
a slaughter. Riders carried rifles; the cattle carried only the
strength purchased from years of grass.

Mack Haney happened to be at Fort Peck when the
poker steer's turn came. He had driven a small remuda
north and remained to trade horses. Seeing the line-backed animal
in the beef enclosure, he recognized him at once.

"Well," he said softly, "they dealt you a long hand."

The steer stood among a dozen chosen cattle. His pilot-wheel
brand was scarred and thick, the original bar-circle-bar hidden beneath
later fire. He was heavier than Haney remembered and carried
his broad horns with grave authority.

A gate opened. The cattle were driven onto the plain.

For a moment they grazed, confused by the absence of
a containing herd. Then riders appeared behind them, shouting and
urging their ponies into a run. A rifle cracked. One
steer stumbled, recovered, and went on with blood spraying from
his nostrils.

The line-back lifted his head.

The first shot intended for him struck high in the
rump. He leaped, kicked, and broke away from the others.
Another bullet cut across his ribs, burning a groove through
hide and flesh. He ran toward broken ground near the
river, his stride long and astonishingly swift for so heavy
an animal.

Three riders followed.

They expected him to tire. Beef cattle were not buffalo,
and this one had already been hit. Yet the poker
steer had spent his life walking away from one ownership
into another, crossing rivers and territories because something ahead seemed
preferable to what closed behind. He ran now with that
whole history concentrated into muscle.

A rider came alongside and fired. The bullet entered behind
the shoulder but ranged too high. The steer faltered, then
swerved so sharply that the pony shied. Horse and rider
lost ground.

The plain opened before him, tawny under the pale sky.
Perhaps he smelled water. Perhaps pain alone directed him. His
breath came harshly; blood darkened both sides; and foam hung
from his mouth. Still he ran.

The hunters spread out. One sought to head him off
while the others pressed from behind. Shots followed, some missing,
some striking. Each impact jarred him, but his body, built
through years for the very meat they meant to take,
possessed a terrible reserve.

Haney watched from a low ridge with several cowboys.

"That steer's too mean to die on schedule," one said.

"He ain't mean," Haney replied. "He's particular."

Below them, the foremost hunter turned his pony across the
steer's course and raised his rifle. He was too close.
Whether blinded by blood, maddened by pain, or guided by
the old pride once wounded beside the Nueces, the line-back
suddenly stopped fleeing.

He turned.

The pony saw him before the rider did. It tried
to wheel, slipped on loose earth, and came up scrambling.
The steer charged with his head low. The rider fired
downward. The bullet struck somewhere in the massive neck but
did not check him.

One horn entered the pony behind the foreleg.

The force lifted horse and rider sideways. The pony screamed,
a sound sharper than any cattle cry, and fell with
its belly torn open. The rider's leg was pinned beneath
the saddle. His rifle flew several yards away.

The poker steer backed, shook blood and entrails from his
horn, and came again.

The trapped man clawed at the ground, trying to drag
himself free. The pony kicked weakly. For an instant the
steer stood over them, swaying, his hide torn by bullets,
his white back-line streaked red. His eyes fixed upon the
moving figure beneath the horse.

The other hunters shouted. One fired and missed for fear
of striking the man. The second galloped close, placed his
rifle almost against the steer's side, and shot.

The line-back lurched but did not fall. He swung at
the new horse, forcing it away, then turned once more
toward the trapped rider. His knees buckled. He recovered. He
took two slow steps, horns lowered.

Another shot entered behind the ear.

The great head dropped. His forelegs folded, and he went
down so heavily that dust rose about him. Even then
he tried to rise. One hind leg drove against the
ground; his shoulder lifted; the wide horns plowed a furrow.
But the machinery of his body, which had survived winter,
iron, knife, flood, stampede, hunger, and the commerce of half
a continent, had reached its final limit.

He fell back.

The line-backed calf born beneath the hunter's-horn moon on the
Nueces died under a gray Montana sky, beside a disemboweled
pony and within a few yards of the man he
had nearly killed.

For a while no one spoke. The trapped rider was
freed, shaken and bruised but alive. Someone ended the pony's
suffering. The hunters stood looking at the steer with that
solemn respect which courage commands even from those who have
destroyed it.

Haney walked down from the ridge. He placed one boot
against the steer's horn and examined the battered pilot-wheel brand.

"Is that the poker steer?" asked a cowboy.

"That's him."

"How many times did you own him?"

"Couldn't say. Nobody could."

The cowboy looked at the dead steer, at the torn
pony, and at the bloody track running back across the
plain.

"He near took the pot with him."

Haney nodded. "Played his last trump, anyhow."

Soon the carcass was dressed and issued, for contracts do
not pause over biography. The hide, bearing its confused testimony
of brands, was thrown aside; the horns were claimed by
a hunter; and the meat entered the ordinary channels appointed
for it. No monument marked the place. By winter, snow
covered the blood, wolves scattered what remained, and the prairie
resumed its old indifference.

Yet among the trail men the story traveled southward. It
was told in bunkhouses and around night fires, gaining here
a mile, there a bullet, and everywhere the dignity that
men grant to an animal only after they have finished
profiting by him. Some said the steer had killed two
ponies. Others declared that he had recognized an old owner
among the hunters and deliberately spared him. A few maintained
that the pilot-wheel brand was natural, though this theory required
more imagination than honesty.

The plain facts were enough.

He had been born beside the Nueces and had followed
his mother into the morning. He had played with a
speckled heifer, suffered the iron, endured winter, and mistaken affection
for a permanent law. He had been beaten, abandoned, and
driven by humiliation into the road. He had joined a
trail herd by his own choice and thereafter been claimed
by nearly everyone except himself. Men had gambled him under
lanterns, altered his identity with fire, rescued him from law
in order to steal him more conveniently, and carried him
north as beef, property, chip, profit, and promise.

Chance dealt the cards, but he walked the miles.

His freedom had never been complete. Even his boldest choice
led into another herd; every open gate closed somewhere farther
on; every escape ended beneath a fresh eye of ownership.
Yet within those limits he possessed an obstinate will, and
at the end, when all roads narrowed to rifle smoke
and blood, he turned upon his pursuers.

It was not victory. The contract was fulfilled, the meat
delivered, and the account settled. Civilization kept the stakes.

But for one fierce moment on the northern plain, the
poker steer played a card no man had dealt him,
and those who saw it never forgot that last defiance
against a destiny which, from the branding-pen onward, had always
been beyond his control.

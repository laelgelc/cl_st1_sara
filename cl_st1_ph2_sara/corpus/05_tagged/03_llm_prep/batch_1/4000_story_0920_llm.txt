## Abner's First Call

The first pair of long trousers came to Abner Talcott
on a Saturday afternoon, and by Sunday morning they had
altered not merely the visible length of his legs, but
his understanding of society, his obligations toward the female sex,
and, in some degree, the destiny of the human race.

They were gray trousers, cut with an amplitude which suggested
that the tailor had faith in Abner's future. At present
they hung somewhat mournfully from his hips and broke over
his shoes in folds; but Abner saw in the mirror
no such defects. He saw a young gentleman standing upon
the solemn threshold of manhood. Behind him lay marbles, bare
knees, fishing worms, pantry raids, and the undignified habit of
sliding down banisters. Before him opened a shining avenue of
calls, compliments, business engagements, perhaps duels.

His mother, who had brought the trousers home wrapped in
brown paper, viewed their spiritual effect with less enthusiasm.

"You needn't stand there looking like the governor," she said.
"Go fetch me some kindling."

Abner turned slowly from the glass.

"In these?"

"In those or without them."

A cruel world has ever begun by assigning kindling to
its statesmen.

He obeyed, but he carried the basket with a remote
air, as if he had consented to the task only
because great men must sometimes humor domestic institutions. Upon returning,
he brushed two chips from his knee, resumed his station
before the parlor mirror, and began considering whom he ought
first to honor with a formal call.

The name of Champe Claiborne occurred to him almost immediately.

Champe was thirteen, red-haired, freckled across the nose, and given
to speaking the truth at moments when truth was least
becoming. She could climb the mulberry tree better than Abner,
had once beaten him in a foot race, and had
thrown a green apple at him during Sunday-school picnic exercises.
None of these circumstances disqualified her. On the contrary, they
invested the project with danger; and danger, contemplated in long
trousers before a mirror, is exceedingly attractive.

Moreover, Champe's father owned the big white house beyond the
locust grove, with six porch columns, a dinner bell, an
arbor, and a gravel carriage sweep. The Claibornes received callers.
Abner had seen buggies draw up there on Sunday evenings,
and young men with canes disappear through the front door.
It followed that Champe, though regrettably familiar with his childhood
crimes, was a lady upon whom one might call.

The difficulty was that Abner did not know how.

Providence—or some agency less benevolent—had prepared for this. In the
glass-fronted bookcase stood a volume entitled *The Young Gentleman's Social
Guide and Manual of Polite Deportment*. It had belonged to
Cousin Garland during his courtship, though Cousin Garland, after six
years of polished deportment, had married a widow who laughed
loudly and kept a tobacco shop. Abner took the book
down and retired with it to the woodhouse.

There, seated upon an overturned nail keg, he entered civilization.

The book was severe but encouraging. It told him how
to bow, how to enter a room, how to leave
one, how to accept a cup of tea, how to
decline a cup of tea, how to conduct himself at
weddings, funerals, boating parties, musicales, and fires. It warned him
against discussing politics with ladies, placing his hat upon a
piano, coughing into another person's face, monopolizing conversation, or appearing
either too eager or too indifferent.

The section entitled "The Formal Call" shone like a lamp.

"A call," the book announced, "should ordinarily occupy from fifteen
to twenty minutes. The caller, having presented his card, enters
with quiet self-possession. He does not perch upon the edge
of his chair, nor does he betray embarrassment by twisting
his hat. Let a rich stream of conversation flow, avoiding
alike the stagnant pools of silence and the noisy cataracts
of frivolity."

Abner read this twice.

The rich stream troubled him. He had conversed with Champe
many times, but chiefly in such expressions as "Give me
that," "You're scared," "I am not," and "If you tell,
I'll kill you." Whether these constituted a stream, and whether
its mineral content was socially desirable, he could not decide.

Another paragraph counseled the inexperienced caller to secure, when possible,
the companionship of a friend of composure and tact.

Abner shut the book. Ross Dillard was plainly selected by
fate.

Ross lived three houses away, behind a picket fence from
which every third picket was absent. He was discovered in
the stable loft, lying on his stomach and reading a
paper about pirates. His red hair stood in a crest
where he had pushed it back with dirty fingers, and
his freckles advertised a rich soil.

"Ross," said Abner, "how would you like to go into
society?"

Ross held his place in the paper with one thumb.

"What society?"

"Society."

"What does it do?"

"It don't do anything. You go into it."

Ross considered this uncertain pleasure.

"Do they have anything to eat?"

"That isn't the point. I'm going to make a formal
call on Champe Claiborne this afternoon, and I want you
to go along."

Ross rolled over and stared.

"You're going to what?"

"Call. Pay a call. A gentleman's call."

"You owe her something?"

"No, fool. It's social."

Ross looked at the long trousers. Enlightenment dawned, though without
admiration.

"Oh," he said. "It's them."

Abner mounted the loft ladder another rung.

"The book says a gentleman should take along a friend
of composure and tact."

"What's tact?"

"Knowing what to say."

"I don't know what to say to Champe except she's
got my pocketknife."

"You won't have to talk much. I'll lead the conversation."

"Then what do you need me for?"

This was a question not adequately treated in the manual.

"For support," said Abner at last.

"I ain't goin'."

Abner opened the book and read aloud the passage concerning
the social advantages accruing to the polished young gentleman. He
added certain original observations. He pictured the Claiborne parlor glowing
with hospitality, iced lemonade passing in tall glasses, cakes upon
silver plates, Champe subdued by admiration, and the entire household
impressed by their manly bearing. He hinted that girls generally
preferred boys who had served their novitiate in polite society.
He suggested that Ross's present refusal arose from fear.

This last argument, though primitive, had survived all changes of
fashion.

"I ain't scared," Ross said.

"Then come."

"I don't want to."

"Because you're scared."

"I'm not."

"Then come."

Ross sighed. Oh, youth—youth—youth, with its rash promises extracted in
haylofts and redeemed in collars!

"All right," he said. "But if she laughs, I'm leaving."

The expedition was fixed for five o'clock. The book recommended
late afternoon, but warned against the dinner hour. Neither boy
knew the Claiborne dinner hour. Abner maintained that rich people
never had regular meals, but ate whenever servants carried food
past them.

There remained clothing, conversation, and cards.

Ross possessed no long trousers except a black pair inherited
from his brother, who had died of pneumonia at twenty-one
and had been five inches taller. Mrs. Dillard, after hearing
that Ross was invited to the Claibornes', turned up the
legs, tightened the waistband by violent means, and furnished him
with a white collar of such moral rigidity that it
seemed designed to prevent not only slouching but sin.

"You mind your manners," she said. "Don't you run through
their flower beds, and don't ask for anything."

"We're making a call," Ross explained.

"That's nice. Be home before dark."

The ease with which she received this announcement offended him.
He had anticipated questions, astonishment, perhaps a maternal tear at
his first social enterprise. Instead, she tied his necktie, wiped
one of his ears with a damp corner of her
apron, and sent him away smelling faintly of soap and
resentment.

At Abner's house the preparations were military. Abner polished his
shoes until their cracks shone. He wetted his hair and
combed it into dark, parallel ridges. He selected a tie
of deep purple, rejected it as too ardent, chose a
black one as too funereal, and returned to the purple
with the judgment that ardor, properly controlled, was suitable in
a caller.

Then came the cards.

A gentleman presented his card. The manual was immovable on
this point. Abner had no engraved cards, but civilization could
not be permitted to fail for want of a printing
press. With stiff white paper, a ruler, and his father's
pen, he manufactured two:

**MR. ABNER TALCOTT**

**MR. ROSS DILLARD**

Ross's name ran uphill, and the final D in Dillard
resembled a fishhook; nevertheless, when the cards lay side by
side on the table, they had a cold metropolitan beauty.

"What do we do with 'em?" Ross asked.

"Hand them to the servant."

"What if there isn't one?"

"There's always one at the Claibornes'."

"What does the servant do?"

"Takes them to the lady."

"What if Champe's on the porch and sees us?"

Abner frowned and consulted the manual. The manual, composed for
homes with vestibules, footmen, and dependable architecture, had not foreseen
Champe sitting on the porch steps shelling peas.

"We ask for her," he said.

"What if she says, 'Here I am'?"

"We don't pay attention. We send in the cards."

"What if she comes and gets them herself?"

"She won't."

"What if she does?"

"Ross, if you keep trying to ruin everything, you can
stay home."

Ross rubbed one polished shoe against his trouser leg.

"I wish I could."

They rehearsed the call in the parlor. A straight-backed chair
represented Champe. Abner entered, bowed, and said, "Good afternoon, Miss
Claiborne. I trust I find you well."

Ross, acting as Champe, replied, "You know I'm well. You
saw me at church."

"That is not what she'll say. She'll say, 'Quite well,
thank you. And you?'"

"What do you say then?"

"'Never better, I assure you.'"

"What if you ain't?"

"You say it anyway."

"What if you've got the stomachache?"

"A gentleman doesn't have the stomachache during a call."

Ross received this evidence of social superiority in silence.

They proceeded to conversation. Weather was safe, provided it did
not become technical. Travel was excellent, though neither had traveled
farther than the county seat. Books were permissible. Personal compliments
must be delicate.

"If she says, 'That's a pretty tie,'" Abner instructed, "I
say, 'You flatter me.'"

"What if she says it's ugly?"

"She won't."

"What if she does?"

"Then I say—" Abner searched the book. "I say, 'You
are pleased to be severe.'"

Ross began to grin.

"And if she asks your opinion," Abner continued, "you must
not answer too abruptly. If she says, 'Which flower do
you admire?' you say, 'Name yours.'"

"What if I like sunflowers and she likes roses?"

"You say, 'You flatter us.'"

"That don't mean anything."

"It's etiquette."

They practiced the rich stream. It flowed badly, spread into
marshes, vanished underground, and twice became entangled in Ross's pocketknife
grievance. Yet by half past four Abner felt that enough
phrases had been stored to carry them through twenty minutes,
if no one introduced a wholly unreasonable subject.

At a quarter to five the two young gentlemen set
forth.

The white road to the Claiborne place had never appeared
so public. Ordinarily they ran along it, kicked dust, chased
grasshoppers, and shouted at passing wagons. Today every gate seemed
occupied by an eye. Mrs. Braddock, watering geraniums, called, "My,
my! Where are you boys bound, dressed fit to bury
somebody?"

Neither answered. They bowed slightly and moved on.

Ross carried his hat in both hands. Abner had ordered
this, though they were still outdoors. His collar had already
begun to wilt. The cards lay in Abner's breast pocket,
where he touched them every fifteen steps to make sure
that society had not escaped.

At the locust grove they stopped.

Beyond the trees stood the Claiborne house, broad, cool, and
unconcerned. Four people occupied the front porch. Champe was in
the swing. Her mother, whom everybody called Aunt Missouri whether
related or not, sat sewing near the door. Mr. Claiborne
lay back with a newspaper over his face. Champe's younger
sister, Babe, was arranging pebbles on the steps.

The callers retreated behind a locust tree.

"They're all out there," Ross whispered.

"Of course they are. People receive in the parlor."

"They ain't in the parlor."

"We ring, and the servant shows us in."

"What if Aunt Missouri says hello?"

"We bow."

"What if Champe says, 'What are you doing with them
clothes on?'"

"We maintain our self-possession."

Abner consulted the book once more. The sentence "Enter without
awkward hesitation" had acquired the aspect of an accusation.

They advanced from the grove.

Champe saw them first. She stopped the swing with one
foot and stared. Babe looked up, then ran into the
house crying, "Mama, look at Abner's legs!"

Aunt Missouri lifted her eyes from the sewing.

"Well, bless my soul," she said. "If it isn't Ab
and Ross. Come on up, boys."

This was not the expected form.

Abner had prepared for a servant, a card tray, an
interval of dignified waiting, and then Champe's entrance. He had
not prepared for Aunt Missouri's cheerful voice traveling across the
yard. She stood between him and formal society like a
lion in the way—a kind lion, a hospitable lion, a
lion who might offer preserves, but a lion nevertheless.

They reached the porch and removed hats already removed.

"Good evening," Abner said faintly.

"Good evening," Ross echoed.

Mr. Claiborne lowered his newspaper.

"Hello, young gentlemen. Running for office?"

Champe made a sound suspiciously like a choke. She had
noticed the purple tie.

Abner's right hand crept toward his breast pocket. This was
the psychological moment. He should produce the cards, request that
they be presented, and ask if Miss Claiborne was at
home. Unfortunately Miss Claiborne was six feet away, staring directly
at him. To ask whether she was at home seemed
idiotic; to hand her mother a card seemed worse; to
hand the card to Champe herself seemed, under Ross's questioning,
to have been outlawed.

His hand came away empty.

"We—we were passing," he said.

Ross turned upon him a look of injured astonishment. They
had walked half a mile specifically not to be passing.

"Well, I'm glad you passed," Aunt Missouri said. "Sit down."

There were chairs. There was the porch rail. There were
steps. There was the swing beside Champe. Each place contained
a separate form of peril.

The boys selected two chairs near the edge of the
porch and sat with their hats clutched to their stomachs.
They did not lean back. They did not cross their
legs. They did not permit any part of themselves to
suggest repose. They perched, in direct violation of the manual,
like two young saplings weakened at the root.

A pregnant silence followed.

"Hot today," said Mr. Claiborne.

Abner brightened. Weather!

"Unseasonably so," he replied.

"Is it?" asked Champe. "It's July."

Ross examined his hat.

Aunt Missouri's needle moved steadily. "How's your mother, Ab?"

"Quite well, thank you. And you?"

"I'm tolerable."

"Never better, I assure you," said Abner.

Aunt Missouri paused. Mr. Claiborne lowered the paper again. Champe's
eyes widened.

Abner felt a scorching realization travel from his collar to
his shoes. He had given the response belonging to himself
as though it belonged to Aunt Missouri, or perhaps had
assured the family that Aunt Missouri was never better without
medical authority.

Ross tried to rescue him.

"We've been reading books," he announced.

Abner kicked him.

"What kind?" Champe asked.

"Pirates," said Ross, abandoning the endangered subject of etiquette.

Babe came out carrying a rag doll. She stood before
Abner and inspected the new trousers minutely.

"Are you going to a funeral?"

"No," he said.

"Why are your pants so big?"

"They are not big."

"They wrinkle up at the bottom."

"Run along, Babe," said Aunt Missouri, smiling into her sewing.

Babe did not run along. She touched the crease at
Abner's knee with one finger.

"Can you climb a tree in them?"

"A gentleman does not climb trees upon a formal—"

He stopped.

"Upon a what?" Champe asked.

"Nothing."

Ross's fingers closed violently around his hat brim. The card
in his pocket seemed to burn like concealed treason.

The rich stream of conversation now encountered a dam. Abner
searched his memory. Travel. Books. Flowers. He looked toward the
garden.

"You have many handsome flowers," he said to Champe.

"We always have."

"Which do you admire?"

"Those red ones."

"Name yours," said Abner.

"I just did."

"You flatter us."

There are silences which are empty, and silences crowded with
unseen witnesses. This one contained the entire population of the
county.

Mr. Claiborne's newspaper began to shake. Aunt Missouri bent low
over her sewing. Champe stared at Abner for one second,
two seconds, three; then she sprang from the swing and
went into the house with a violent cough.

Ross whispered, "Let's cut and run."

"A call is fifteen to twenty minutes," Abner whispered back.

"We've been here a year."

It had been eleven minutes.

At thirteen minutes a servant named Martha opened the screen
door and announced that supper was ready. Aunt Missouri gathered
her work.

"Come wash up, boys."

The callers did not move.

This was an inferential invitation to supper, an event explicitly
forbidden. The manual declared: "A caller must not prolong his
stay merely because refreshments appear probable." But refreshments had not
appeared probable; they had sounded a dinner bell.

"We—we can't stay," Abner said.

"Nonsense," Aunt Missouri replied. "Your mothers won't expect you before
dark."

Ross looked at Abner. Abner looked at Ross. Neither had
the strength to explain that their mothers expected them before
dark precisely because no one had imagined a formal call
would swallow supper.

Mr. Claiborne folded his newspaper. "Come along, statesmen."

They followed.

In the washroom, Ross seized Abner by the sleeve.

"Give her the card now."

"To whom?"

"Anybody. Slip it under a plate."

"That would be vulgar."

"Then tell Champe."

"Not at supper."

"We'll be here forever."

"After supper we rise and thunder out our farewells."

They did not rise. They did not thunder out anything.

Supper extended before them in courses not because the Claibornes
practiced European dining, but because Martha repeatedly remembered something left
in the kitchen. There were fried chicken, biscuits, corn, tomatoes,
beans, preserves, and a peach cobbler whose arrival destroyed Ross's
remaining loyalty to the fifteen-minute rule.

Etiquette made every mouthful dangerous. Abner declined butter, then recalled
that abrupt refusal was discourteous and accepted it. He tried
to converse without food in his mouth, which left him
no time to eat; then, fearing that his abstinence might
seem critical of the table, he ate too rapidly and
choked on corn.

Champe sat opposite him.

"Do gentlemen always turn purple when they swallow?" she asked.

"You are pleased to be severe," he gasped.

Mr. Claiborne coughed into his napkin.

Ross, whose collar prevented him from looking down without bending
from the waist, lost a bean. It rolled across the
tablecloth and stopped beside Aunt Missouri's plate. Babe pursued its
course with loud commentary.

"Ross threw a bean at Mama."

"I did not."

"I saw it roll."

"It fell."

"Beans don't fall sideways."

"Babe," Aunt Missouri said, "eat your supper and quit prosecuting
the company."

Company! The word encouraged Abner. They were recognized as callers.
Perhaps no declaration was needed. Perhaps the cards could be
left discreetly upon departure, fulfilling the spirit of social law.

Under cover of the cobbler he removed the cards and
held them on his knee.

Ross saw them.

"Now," he mouthed.

Abner shook his head.

"Now."

"To who?"

Ross indicated Champe with his eyebrows so violently that Babe
noticed.

"What's wrong with your face?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You keep twitching at Champe."

Ross dropped his spoon.

Champe leaned across the table. "Is there something you want
to tell me?"

The moment arrived, splendid and terrible. Abner could have placed
the card beside her plate. He could have said, "We
have called." He could have risen, bowed, and brought the
enterprise to a victorious conclusion.

Instead he put both cards in his mouth.

It was not intentional. Aunt Missouri turned toward him just
as his hand rose; panic completed the gesture. For one
dreadful instant he sat with the corner of his own
social identity protruding between his lips. Then he coughed, covered
his mouth, and transferred the damp cards into his pocket.

"What are you eating?" asked Babe.

"Nothing."

"I saw paper."

"You did not."

"Was it a note?"

"No!"

Champe's smile grew thoughtful.

After supper the family repaired to the parlor. The boys
believed departure possible, but hope, like a young chicken, is
most vulnerable just outside the coop. Before Abner could announce
anything, Mr. Claiborne set out a checkerboard and challenged Ross.

Ross dared not refuse. A gentleman, said the manual, should
adapt himself gracefully to innocent amusement. He adapted for forty-five
minutes while Abner sat beside Champe on the sofa, separated
from her by his hat.

"Why are you holding that?" she asked.

"A gentleman should never place his hat on the piano."

"You're not near the piano."

"No."

"You could put it on the floor."

"It might get stepped on."

"Put it on the table."

"That would be improper."

"Then wear it."

Abner suspected mockery, but could not prove it.

Aunt Missouri asked Champe to play. Champe went to the
piano and performed "The Blue Bells of Scotland" with energetic
inaccuracies. The manual advised the young gentleman, following music, to
express restrained appreciation.

"You flatter us," Abner said when she finished.

This time there could be no mistake. Mr. Claiborne got
up and went out on the porch. Through the open
window came a series of explosive coughs.

Ross, defeated at checkers because his attention had been fixed
upon escape, joined Abner near the door.

"Let's go," he whispered.

"Wait till there's a break."

There was never a break. Babe recited a poem. Aunt
Missouri told a story about a minister and a mule.
Mr. Claiborne returned and proposed that Ross show Champe the
card trick he had performed at the church social. Ross
denied knowledge of all cards, natural or supernatural. Lemonade appeared,
followed by cake, proving that Abner's original prophecy had been
accurate but tragically late.

Darkness pressed against the windows.

"Your mothers will be wondering," Aunt Missouri said at last.

The boys rose so suddenly that their knees struck the
table.

"Yes, ma'am," they said together.

Aunt Missouri glanced toward the road. "It's fixing to storm.
Missouri clouds, too. You boys had better wait till it
passes."

A low roll of thunder confirmed her hospitality.

"We can make it," Ross said.

Lightning whitened the room. Rain struck the porch roof with
the instantaneous violence of thrown gravel.

"No, you can't," Mr. Claiborne said. "Sit down."

They sat.

The storm settled over the house with professional thoroughness. Water
poured from the eaves. The road vanished. The clock struck
nine, then ten. Abner's collar had ceased to be a
collar and become a damp recollection. Ross's turned-up trousers sagged
around his ankles. Their hats, crushed by hours of moral
pressure, no longer possessed form.

At ten-thirty Aunt Missouri said the sentence that broke them.

"Martha, make up the spare room for these boys."

Abner stared.

Ross stared.

Babe clapped.

"They're going to sleep here!"

"No," said Abner.

Everyone looked at him.

He had only to speak. He had only to say,
"Madam, we came to pay a formal call upon your
daughter, and having exceeded the prescribed twenty minutes by approximately
five hours, we must withdraw regardless of atmospheric conditions." It
was a long sentence, but desperation might have carried it.

Instead he said, "I mean—Mama doesn't know."

"I'll send word if the rain lets up," Aunt Missouri
replied. "She'll know you're safe."

"We could sleep in the barn," Ross suggested wildly.

"The spare room's got two beds."

"We're used to barns."

"Ross," said Mr. Claiborne, "you're not a horse."

The family laughed, not cruelly, but with the terrible ease
of persons who are at home. The boys were conducted
upstairs.

The spare room contained two narrow beds beneath a framed
text reading, *Be Ye Also Ready*. Champe placed a lamp
on the table and wished them good night with exaggerated
courtesy.

"I trust I find you well," she said.

Abner whitened.

"Never better, I assure you," Ross replied, and shut the
door in her face.

They listened to her laughter retreat down the hall.

Then Ross turned.

"We've got to get out."

"It's raining."

"Through the window."

"We're on the second floor."

"Down the vine."

"That's ivy. It won't hold."

"Then tie the sheets together."

"That's stealing."

"We won't take 'em with us!"

Abner sat on one bed, his long trousers ballooning around
him.

"We'll leave at daylight."

"We never gave her the cards."

Abner brought them out. They had merged into one soft,
purple-edged mass from the lemonade spilled at supper.

Ross contemplated the ruin.

"All this," he said, "and we ain't called yet."

"We have called."

"We said we were passing."

"We sat in the parlor."

"We ate supper."

"Callers sometimes—"

"Callers don't get put to bed."

That was unanswerable.

They undressed to their shirts and drawers, placing the ceremonial
clothes over chair backs. Without long trousers Abner's manhood suffered
a rapid contraction. He became again a boy with thin
shins and bruised knees, lying awake in a strange room
while rain whispered in the gutters.

From below came muffled voices, then laughter.

"They know," Ross said.

"They do not."

"Champe knows."

"She suspects."

"They're laughing at us."

"They're probably laughing at Mr. Claiborne's story."

The laughter rose again, and in it could plainly be
distinguished Champe's piercing note.

Abner pulled the sheet over his head.

Morning arrived offensively clear.

Sunlight entered the spare room as if no social catastrophe
had occurred. A rooster crowed in the yard. Somewhere below,
dishes clattered and bacon hissed. The boys dressed in silence.
Their collars were beyond restoration. Abner's purple tie had acquired
a list to port. Ross's trousers, having lost one pin
in the night, were longer on the left side.

"Soon as we get downstairs," Ross said, "we tell her."

"Before breakfast?"

"Before anything."

"Perhaps we should wait until we're received."

"We've been received till I'm sick of it."

They descended.

The entire household sat in the dining room: Aunt Missouri,
Mr. Claiborne, Champe, Babe, Grandmother Claiborne, two visiting cousins who
had not been present the evening before, Martha at the
sideboard, and the gardener visible through the open window. The
audience had increased by unexplained but malign natural processes.

Every face turned.

"Well!" Aunt Missouri cried. "Here come our callers."

The word struck both boys like a broom handle.

Champe bent over her plate. Her shoulders shook.

Mr. Claiborne stood with solemn ceremony. "Good morning, Mr. Talcott.
Good morning, Mr. Dillard. I trust you rested after the
fatigues of your visit."

"You flatter us," Champe murmured.

The room collapsed into laughter.

Babe trembled like chocolate pudding. Grandmother Claiborne wiped her eyes.
Even Martha turned toward the sideboard, though her back betrayed
her. The boys remained in the doorway, hats in hand,
their faces passing successively through red, scarlet, and a shade
for which nature has provided no flower.

They knew. Everybody knew.

During the night, their mystery had been examined, compared with
evidence, and solved. Perhaps Champe had seen the cards. Perhaps
Aunt Missouri had heard Abner's unfinished "formal." Perhaps no evidence
was necessary beyond two collars, two hats, and six hours
of unexplained agony.

Aunt Missouri recovered first.

"Come eat, boys. Formal callers need breakfast."

"We were just leaving," Abner said.

"Not before you tell Champe what you came for," said
Mr. Claiborne.

A path opened before them. It led to Champe, who
sat with her chin in her hands and malice sparkling
in her eyes.

Abner glanced at Ross. Ross glanced at Abner.

"Fire away," Ross whispered.

"You do it."

"It was your call."

"You came too."

"As support."

"Then support."

Aunt Missouri poured coffee with maddening patience.

"Well?"

Abner's hand entered his pocket and drew forth the pulpy
remains of the cards. He attempted to separate them. Ross
Dillard's final D tore away and adhered to Abner Talcott's
name.

"We—we—" Abner began.

All conversation ceased. Even the bacon seemed to stop sizzling.

"We came—"

A horse galloped into the yard.

Never did rescue approach with a more heroic sound. Never
was rescue more deceptively mounted.

A boy from the Talcott place leaped from a mule
at the back gate and ran to the porch. It
was Abner's younger brother, Sam, barefoot, breathless, and delighted with
his commission.

"Ab!" he shouted through the screen. "Ma says you're to
come home this minute!"

Abner's relief lasted one second.

Sam opened the door and delivered the remainder.

"She says if you're not home before I get back,
she's coming after you with the buggy whip. She says
you took Pa's good pen, left the woodhouse lamp burning,
and went off without filling the kindling box. She says
long pants don't make a man out of a natural-born
fool."

Silence followed—not a pregnant silence, nor an expectant silence, nor
any silence known to manuals of deportment. It was the
vast, crystalline stillness which precedes universal destruction.

Then Babe squealed.

Champe threw back her head and laughed.

The household followed. Mr. Claiborne struck the table. Aunt Missouri
protested, "Now, now," while laughing helplessly herself. Grandmother Claiborne removed
her spectacles because tears had rendered them useless. Sam, realizing
he had accomplished something great, grinned at everybody.

Abner looked neither right nor left. His face emptied of
expression. He placed the ruined cards on the nearest table,
jammed his crushed hat upon his head, and walked toward
the door with the mechanical dignity of a condemned prince.

At the threshold his nerve broke.

He ran.

Across the porch he flew, down the steps, over the
gravel, through Aunt Missouri's bed of white petunias, and into
the road. His long trousers flapped at his ankles; his
purple tie streamed over one shoulder; his mother's messenger sprang
onto the mule and galloped after him, shouting further details
of the domestic indictment.

Ross started too.

Loyalty, habit, and terror propelled him as far as the
porch steps. He had endured the road, the collars, the
rich stream, the bean, the card, the checkerboard, the storm,
the spare room, breakfast, and the buggy whip by association.
Nothing remained but flight.

Then Champe laughed behind him.

It was not the household's laughter now. It was hers
alone—bright, victorious, intolerable.

Ross stopped.

There are moments when a boy's whole character, previously scattered
among fears, boasts, bruises, stolen apples, and unfinished lessons, gathers
itself without warning into one compact and dangerous purpose. No
book announces such a moment. No rule prepares for it.
Etiquette, standing at a safe distance, disapproves.

Ross turned.

Champe stood just inside the dining-room doorway, one hand against
the frame. Her red hair caught the morning sun. She
expected him to say something foolish, or angry, or borrowed.
She expected another "You flatter us," perhaps. The whole family
expected it.

Ross climbed the steps.

His knees were weak. His left trouser leg dragged. His
collar leaned outward like a broken gate. Yet he advanced.

"What did you come for, Mr. Dillard?" Champe asked.

Ross looked at her. Behind her were Aunt Missouri, Mr.
Claiborne, Babe, Grandmother, the cousins, Martha, Sam's abandoned dust, breakfast,
society, and judgment. The world was full of eyes.

He threw his hat on the floor.

Before Champe could move, he caught her by both shoulders
and kissed her squarely upon the mouth.

It was not a polished kiss. It was not restrained,
graceful, or delicate. It had no resemblance to anything in
*The Young Gentleman's Social Guide and Manual of Polite Deportment*.
It was brief, loud, desperate, and astonishingly successful.

Ross released her.

"There," he said. "That's what I came for."

Nobody laughed.

Champe stood motionless, her freckles nearly hidden by color. Babe's
mouth formed a perfect circle. Aunt Missouri held the coffee
pot suspended in midair. Mr. Claiborne stared at Ross with
an expression in which outrage struggled unsuccessfully against admiration.

Ross picked up his hat, bowed—not well, but independently—and walked
down the steps.

He had reached the gate when the first pebble struck
the fence beside him.

He looked back.

Champe was on the porch, scarlet-faced, gathering ammunition from Babe's
carefully arranged collection.

"You come back here, Ross Dillard!"

Another stone flew. Ross ducked, laughing.

"You stop, or I'll kill you!"

A third pebble clipped his hat. He placed it firmly
on his head and ran down the white road, not
in panic now, but in triumph, while Champe pursued him
to the gate and threw until the last of Babe's
pebbles was gone.

Far ahead, Abner's purple tie vanished around the bend, hastened
by the remembered buggy whip and the neglected kindling. Ross
followed at leisure. His collar was ruined, his trousers ridiculous,
his card dissolved, and his reputation, by every civilized standard,
irrecoverable.

But he had made his call.

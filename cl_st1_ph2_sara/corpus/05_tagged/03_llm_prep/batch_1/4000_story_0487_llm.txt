### SAMSON OF THE SNOWS

Jean Rasteaux was born at Ploumanac'h, where the coast of
Brittany is broken into red rocks and the Atlantic comes
marching in with the gravity of an army. His father
and his grandfather had been fishermen; so, too, would Jean
have remained until the sea took him, had not France,
in one of those acts of official wisdom which are
indistinguishable from lunacy, decided that this magnificent sailor was required
among the mountains.

He was twenty-four when the conscription seized him. He stood
six feet six inches in his wooden shoes, and was
broad in proportion. His hands could close about an oar
as other hands close about a walking-stick. Once, when a
squall overturned a boat off the Seven Islands, he swam
half a mile through a furious sea, dragging two exhausted
men behind him. Yet he was as gentle as a
child. He disliked quarrels, spoke softly, and could not see
a wounded bird without lifting it from the road.

Such a man would have been a giant in any
country. In Brittany, where legends still breathe among the stones,
the old women said he had the blood of the
ancient kings.

The military register described him merely as: "Rasteaux, Jean; fisherman;
fit for service."

By some bureaucratic miracle he was sent, not to the
navy, but to an Alpine regiment stationed in a narrow
valley near the Italian frontier. There the mountains rose on
every side, white, inaccessible and cruel. A torrent roared at
the bottom of the gorge; above it dark pines clung
to the precipices, and higher still lay dazzling slopes of
snow beneath a sky of iron-blue.

Jean hated them from the first.

He had been accustomed to the great horizon, to wind
smelling of salt and weed, to the long rise and
fall of the water beneath his feet. The mountains gave
him no horizon. They stood about him like prison walls.
Their silence was not the measured thunder of the sea,
but a silence that watched and threatened.

At night, in the barrack-room, he would lie awake while
the others slept, and hear, through the closed shutters, the
torrent roaring over the stones. Sometimes he deceived himself for
an instant and imagined that it was the surf along
the Breton coast. Then a sentry's challenge rang beneath the
window, the illusion vanished, and the big man turned his
face to the wall.

His comrades soon discovered his homesickness, and naturally they made
sport of it.

They mocked his slow Breton speech, his clumsy French, his
bewilderment at drill, and his enormous hands, which appeared incapable
of arranging a strap to the satisfaction of a corporal.
Because he was strong and patient, they concluded that he
was stupid. Because he did not resent an insult, they
supposed that he did not feel it. There is no
mistake more agreeable to a bully, or more dangerous.

They called him Samson.

At first the name was given in admiration. Later it
became a taunt.

—Samson, pull down the barracks!

—Samson, where is thy jaw-bone?

—Samson, has Delilah stolen thy wits?

Jean smiled when they smiled, though he understood but half
of their jests. If they hid his boots, he went
barefoot until they were restored. If they put salt in
his soup, he ate it. If they loaded his knapsack
with stones, he carried them all day and emptied them
quietly upon his bed at night.

This submission delighted his persecutors. Cruelty, like fire, feeds upon
what is given it.

There was among them a little soldier named Picard, a
yellow-faced creature with restless eyes and a voice as sharp
as a mosquito's whine. He scarcely reached Jean's shoulder, and
would never have dared to meet the Breton's glance had
he not been protected by uniform, custom, and the laughter
of twenty comrades. From this secure position he tormented the
giant continually.

He pricked him with pins on parade. He tied the
sleeves of Jean's tunic together. He dropped grease into his
cartridge-box and reported him for carelessness. Then, when punishment followed,
the little gnat stood near enough to watch.

The non-commissioned officers were no better. A patient soldier gave
them no amusement unless his patience could be broken; therefore
they undertook to break it. Jean was ordered to perform
useless labours, was reproved for faults committed by others, and
was made to stand motionless in the snow while the
cold pierced even his Breton blood.

His strength, which should have won respect, became merely an
instrument for their convenience.

—Rasteaux! Carry that chest.

—Rasteaux! Lift that gun-carriage.

—Rasteaux! Clear those stones.

He obeyed.

Only once did he protest. A sergeant had struck him
behind the ear because he misunderstood an order.

Jean turned slowly.

—Do not do that again, Sergeant, he said.

There was no threat in his voice. That made the
warning terrible. The sergeant stepped back; but seeing the men
watching him, he recovered his official courage.

—Silence, animal!

Jean looked at him for a long moment. Then he
saluted and went away.

After this they declared him sulky. It is always thus.
The victim who smiles is ridiculous; the victim who ceases
to smile is insolent.

Winter deepened. Snow covered the roofs, blocked the pass, and
laid a white shroud upon the mountains. Jean's longing for
Brittany became an illness. He saw in dreams the boats
drawn up upon the beach, brown nets drying in the
sun, his mother standing at the cottage door. He heard
gulls crying above the harbour. He woke with tears upon
his cheeks, and Picard, observing them, announced that Samson wept
because the mountains had frightened him.

The last outrage occurred in the wash-house.

Jean had filled a wooden pail and was bending over
a bench when Picard crept behind him. The little man
had prepared his jest carefully. With a sudden jerk he
snatched at Jean's belt, and at the same instant another
soldier overturned the pail so that the icy water drenched
Jean from head to foot.

The room exploded with laughter.

Jean straightened himself. Water ran from his hair and trickled
down his face. Picard danced before him, squeaking like a
monkey.

—Look! Samson has found his ocean! Swim, fisherman! Swim!

Then he spat upon Jean's wet tunic.

The laughter ceased.

Jean looked down at the little soldier. His great fists
closed, but he did not strike. To crush that wretched
body would have been murder, not combat. Instead he turned
toward two of the largest men in the room—the fellows
who had overturned the pail and for months had encouraged
Picard's cowardly mischief.

He seized the first by the collar and belt and
flung him across a bench. The second rushed in, head
lowered. Jean met him with one blow. The man rose
from the floor, struck the wall, and dropped senseless among
the buckets.

Then Jean stood still.

—Call the guard, he said.

The inquiry was held immediately before Lieutenant Varennes, a young
officer whose moustaches occupied more of his attention than his
duties. Picard told his story with tears and gestures. According
to him, Jean had attacked the entire wash-house without cause,
roaring like a savage. The others confirmed it. Military justice,
being justice in a hurry and in uniform, required nothing
further.

Jean tried to speak.

—Silence! cried Varennes.

—But, Lieutenant, the little one—

—Silence, I say!

—He has tormented me for six months.

The officer stepped forward. He carried a riding-whip, though there
was no horse within twenty miles. With the vanity of
a weak man displaying authority before inferiors, he struck Jean
across the face.

A red line appeared from the Breton's temple to his
mouth.

Something ended then—patience, discipline, perhaps hope itself.

Jean took Varennes by the waist. The officer gave one
shrill cry as those enormous arms lifted him overhead. For
an instant his polished boots kicked above the soldiers' faces;
then Jean hurled him across the room.

He fell upon a table, which broke beneath him.

No one moved. Picard had crept behind a door.

Jean looked at the senseless officer, then at the red-faced
sergeants.

—Now, he said, I have done wrong.

They chained him hand and foot and locked him in
a stone storehouse to await a court-martial. An armed sentry
stood outside. Everyone knew what sentence must follow an assault
upon an officer in that isolated command. Whether the law
demanded death mattered little; the regiment's pride demanded it, and
pride writes sterner codes than law.

During the night the wind rose.

At two in the morning the sentry heard a crash
within the storehouse. He called out. There was no answer.
He opened the door and found the chain torn from
its fastening and the small barred window empty.

Samson had broken his bonds.

The alarm sounded through the valley. Lanterns flashed; doors opened;
men stumbled into the snow, fastening their belts. Jean's tracks
were soon discovered beyond the outer wall. They crossed the
frozen road, descended to the torrent, and continued along a
shelf of rock toward the upper valley.

—He is making for Italy, said the colonel.

This explanation pleased everyone, because it made the fugitive's conduct
appear simple and themselves intelligent. Patrols were sent to the
pass. Marksmen occupied the bridge. By dawn nearly the entire
regiment had gathered where the valley narrowed beneath a gigantic
snow-covered ridge.

Far above them a black figure moved across the whiteness.

It was Jean.

He had climbed for hours, choosing places where the rocks
offered a hold, and now he had reached a vast
inclined field of snow. Behind him yawned the gorge. To
his left rose a cliff smooth as a fortress wall;
to his right the blue breast of a glacier curved
downward into broken ice. Above, an overhanging cornice glittered in
the rising sun.

Below him the soldiers waited.

They were in excellent spirits. The fugitive could neither ascend
the cliff nor cross the glacier. If he returned, they
would take him. If he remained, cold would conquer him
before evening. The officers examined him through field-glasses and discussed
his stupidity.

—The brute has trapped himself, said Colonel Dervaux.

Picard, safe among a crowd of armed men, laughed loudly.

Jean continued upward.

His black form looked very small against that immense purity.
He climbed slowly, sinking to the knee at every step.
Once he slipped and slid several yards before checking himself
upon a projecting stone. A cheer rose from below.

He heard it.

He turned and gazed down.

The regiment stood upon a broad level beneath the slope.
Behind the men ran the raging stream, swollen beneath ledges
of ice. On either side, precipices enclosed them. The only
easy retreat lay through the narrow road by which they
had entered; but so confident were they that wagons, horses
and men crowded its mouth.

Jean looked above him at the enormous cornice. He looked
again at the soldiers.

Then he sat down in the snow.

—He is finished, said Varennes, who had come despite a
bandaged shoulder and a face grey with pain. In half
an hour he will beg.

But Jean did not beg.

He remained motionless for several minutes. Perhaps he was thinking
of the court-martial, the firing party, and the little gnat's
triumphant face. Perhaps he remembered the wash-house, the whip, the
long months of insult. Or perhaps his thoughts had travelled
beyond all these things, over the peaks and plains of
France, to a low stone cottage beside the Breton sea.

He rose.

He turned his back to the mountain and spread his
arms.

Even then no one understood.

Jean threw himself forward.

His body struck the steep snow and began to roll.
It rolled slowly at first, gathering a white covering about
it. Then it bounded, struck again, and came on faster.
Snow clung to snow. The small mass became a boulder;
the boulder became a moving hill.

The officers stared.

A crack like a cannon-shot split the air.

High above, the cornice trembled. A long black seam appeared
beneath it, ran from one end to the other, and
opened. The whole overhanging ridge leaned outward.

Someone screamed:

—An avalanche!

There was a pause—the dreadful pause in which men see
death before death reaches them.

Then the mountain fell.

—An avalanche! An avalanche!!

The cry leapt from mouth to mouth, but there was
nowhere to fly. The precipices closed the flanks; the torrent
barred the front; wagons jammed the road behind. Officers shouted
contradictory orders. Soldiers trampled one another. Horses reared, broke their
traces, and plunged into the stream.

Picard tried to climb beneath a wagon. Varennes ran toward
the cliff and tore at the naked rock with his
hands. Colonel Dervaux stood waving his sword, commanding the snow
to halt in the name of discipline.

Down came Jean before it, enclosed in the vast white
ball his body had created. Behind him rushed the avalanche,
widening from wall to wall, swallowing rocks, uprooting pines and
hurling blocks of ice through the air. Its low sullen
roar deepened until earth and sky seemed to be roaring
together.

The hours of ascent were undone in seconds.

The white flood struck.

Men, horses and wagons vanished as though swept beneath a
breaking sea. The torrent leapt from its bed. Air driven
before the snow smashed windows in the distant barracks. Then
the avalanche climbed the opposite slope, hung there in a
cloud of shining dust, and settled.

Silence returned to the valley.

For three days rescue parties dug among the frozen masses.
They found men standing upright beneath the snow, men locked
together, men with arms raised before their faces. Picard was
discovered under the shattered wagon. His eyes were open, and
his little mouth remained stretched in a final cry. Varennes
lay near the cliff, his fingers broken against the rock.
Colonel Dervaux still gripped his sword.

All wore the same expression—not courage, not dignity, but naked
terror.

They found Jean last, farthest down the valley where the
avalanche had spent its force. The snow had been crushed
smoothly about him. His great limbs were at rest, and
there was no fear upon his face.

Those who lifted him said that he appeared to be
sleeping.

Perhaps, in that final descent, the thunder behind him had
ceased to be the voice of the mountain. Perhaps it
had become the surf of Brittany, rolling among the red
rocks; and the snow that covered him had become deep
green water, cool and familiar, bearing him gently home.

Thus Samson returned to the sea.

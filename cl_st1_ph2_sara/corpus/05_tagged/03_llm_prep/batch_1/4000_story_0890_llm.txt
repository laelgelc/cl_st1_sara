### The Education of Ambrose Pottle

At seven minutes past six on a spring-scented Thursday evening,
Ambrose Pottle stood before the mirror in his bachelor bedroom
and committed his face to destiny.

The face was not a bad one. It was mild,
pink, and arranged for peace. Its chief defect, considered romantically,
was that it looked capable of apologizing to a chair
after stumbling over it. Ambrose treated it with Bay-Rum Balm,
subdued the rebellious left point of his mustache with mustache
wax, and brushed his thinning hair diagonally across the strategic
territory above his forehead.

He then put on a fresh union suit, a lilac
shirt, a detachable collar of unusual severity, a blue tie
ornamented with a silver horseshoe, gray trousers, yellow socks, and
patent-leather shoes. Over all went the new brown suit bought
from Mr. Peskett, who had assured him that it was
precisely what young men were wearing in Chicago.

Ambrose was thirty-six.

First love at twenty is a fever. First love at
thirty-six is a chronic condition involving clean linen, careful budgeting,
and the conviction that Providence has waited until now because
it wished to do the thing properly.

Ambrose took from the bureau a box of Choc-O-late Nutties,
price sixty cents, and descended the stairs with diffident steps
and a hesitant heart.

He was in love.

The object of this mature combustion was Mrs. Blossom Gallup,
widow of the late Jason Gallup, dealer in hay, feed,
chicken gravel, fence staples, and selected garden seed. Jason had
left Blossom a new-art bungalow, eight thousand dollars in insurance,
the feed business, and a library of fifty-four volumes bound
in shiny green imitation leather.

Mrs. Gallup had not read the volumes. She had, however,
dusted them every Tuesday, and from their presence she had
acquired a reverence for culture which was almost more profound
than anything reading could have produced.

To Ambrose she was a dream of loveliness, a poem,
an idyl in pale blue silk. Her hair was golden
in the places where the light struck it and determinedly
golden elsewhere. Her eyes were large, candid, and susceptible. When
she listened to anyone who used a word of more
than four syllables, her lips parted with the innocent wonder
of a child seeing its first giraffe.

Ambrose sold barbers' supplies through seven counties. He knew the
wholesale price of striped poles, the absorbent qualities of neck
paper, the proper care of German razors, and twelve arguments
in favor of Pottle's Peerless Pomade. He did not know
whether giraffes were carnivorous, herbivorous, piscivorous, or merely nervous.

This had never troubled him until he met Blossom.

She admitted him with a flutter.

"Why, Mr. Pottle! How nice!"

"I thought maybe you might like these," said Ambrose, offering
the Nutties.

"How perfectly sweet! Do come in."

He entered the parlor, where the fifty-four green volumes stood
behind glass and looked down upon him with the cold
superiority of books that have never been opened. On the
center table lay *The Cultivated Woman's Companion*, *Poetry for Every
Fireside*, and a mail-order booklet entitled *How to Become Magnetic
in Ten Evenings*.

Mrs. Gallup sat upon the settee. Ambrose sat beside her,
leaving between them a space sufficient for propriety but insufficient
for comfort.

For three weeks he had rehearsed his proposal. In his
room he had delivered it to the mirror, the washstand,
and a hatbox. The hatbox had accepted him without hesitation.
In Blossom's presence, however, the speech seemed to have mislaid
its bones.

"Pleasant weather," he began.

"Isn't it?"

"Nice for gardens."

"Lovely."

"Also for traveling in barbers' supplies."

"I suppose it must be."

Silence arrived and sat between them.

Ambrose's fingers moved toward the box of candy. Blossom's fingers
moved at the same moment. They touched. Delicious tinglings radiated
from his spinal cord to regions not ordinarily concerned with
emotion.

"Blossom," he said.

It was the first time he had called her Blossom.
The daring of it nearly stopped his heart.

She lowered her eyes.

"Yes, Ambrose?"

He swallowed.

"Blossom, I've been thinking—well, a man gets to an age—living
alone isn't—what I mean is, if a woman with a
bungalow and a feed business happened to feel—"

"How strange!" she said.

Ambrose's soul dropped through three floors and a cellar.

"Strange?"

"I was thinking about marriage only this afternoon."

His soul returned by express elevator.

"You were?"

"Yes. I've often thought I might marry again."

"Blossom!"

"And I haven't," she concluded.

Ambrose's soul fell farther than before.

She selected a Nuttie and regarded it pensively.

"You see, Jason was a good provider. Nobody could judge
redtop hay better. But he wasn't literary."

"No?"

"He never read anything except feed quotations and the labels
on cough medicine. When I spoke of poetry, he said
poetry didn't move a stock of cracked corn. I told
myself that if I ever married again, it would be
to a cultured man."

"A cultured man," repeated Ambrose faintly.

"A man who has read," she said. "A man who
can talk of books and art and science. A man
who knows things."

Ambrose looked at the green library. Fifty-four silent judges looked
back.

"I read the *Barbers' Monthly Bulletin*," he offered.

"Of course business publications are useful."

"And I once read most of *Ben Hur*."

"How far did you get?"

"To where they began racing."

"That is not most of it, Ambrose."

"No," he admitted.

The doorbell rang.

Mrs. Gallup brightened. "That must be Mr. Deeley."

Ambrose disliked Mr. Deeley before he saw him. He disliked
him more efficiently afterward.

Mr. Deeley entered as if the house had been built
to receive him. He was rubicund and rotund, broad in
the waist, magnificent in the neck, and equipped with a
pleased mustache. It was a mustache that appeared to know
the answer before the question had been asked.

"My dear Mrs. Gallup!" he boomed. "Radiant as Aurora!"

"How fascinating!" breathed Blossom.

Ambrose had supposed Aurora to be either a town in
Illinois or a kind of stove.

Mr. Deeley shook Blossom's hand with cultivated warmth. He then
seized Ambrose's hand and crushed it until several small bones
reconsidered their positions.

"And this is Mr.—Bottle, isn't it?"

"Pottle."

"Of course. Pottle. Delighted."

He sat on Blossom's other side, took a Choc-O-late Nuttie
without invitation, and ate it in one large, intellectual bite.

"I was just telling Mr. Pottle," said Blossom, "that I
admire cultured men."

"Culture!" exclaimed Deeley. "A noble subject. Rather a specialty of
mine."

"Is it really?"

"My dear lady, all subjects are specialties of mine. Consider
astronomy. Do you know that the sun is approximately ninety-three
million miles from the earth?"

"How fascinating!"

Ambrose knew that his route from Peebles Junction to West
Rollo was forty-two miles, including the trolley transfer at Minden.
It seemed inadequate.

"Astronomy," Deeley continued, "is derived from two ancient words meaning
'stars' and 'knowing about them.' The Arabians were great astronomers.
So were the Aztecs. The Aztecs, you recall, inhabited Mexico
before it became fully organized."

"Think of that!"

"Have you ever studied the albatross?"

"No," said Blossom reverently.

"A remarkable bird. It lives chiefly on the ocean and
can go for weeks without landing on a tree."

"There are no trees on the ocean," Ambrose ventured.

"Precisely, my dear Poodle. That explains it."

"Pottle."

"The albatross is both carnivorous and piscivorous."

Blossom leaned forward. "What does that mean?"

"It means it eats corn and pigs."

Ambrose felt that this did not sound right, but he
lacked the weapons with which to attack it.

"Animal life," said Deeley, "is another specialty of mine. Take
the antelope. Or the armadillo. Or the aardvark. The aardvark
has a tongue nearly eighteen inches long."

Blossom gazed at him.

"Eighteen inches?"

"Nearly. It feeds on ants, which belong to the order—ah—Antibus
Insectivora."

"Gracious!"

"The ant itself is a marvelous creature. Industrious, organized, thrifty.
Many a businessman could learn from the ant."

Ambrose sold a preparation called Ant-Away to barbers troubled by
insects near their hot-water pipes, but he felt this would
not assist him.

Deeley moved easily from ants to Asia, from Asia to
Assyria, from Assyria to architecture, and from architecture to Aristotle.
He had met every subject before. Each was rather a
specialty of his. He knew the acreage of Australia, the
chief exports of Albania, the habits of the anaconda, the
height of the Alps, the principles of aviation, and the
age at which Alexander the Great began being great.

Mrs. Gallup's "How fascinating!" became almost a hymn.

Ambrose sat shrinking beside the empty candy box. Deeley grew
larger with every fact. His waistcoat expanded; his mustache prospered;
his watch chain gleamed like the chain of office of
a university chancellor.

At nine o'clock Ambrose rose.

"Must you go?" asked Blossom, in the tone of a
woman who had only just noticed that he remained.

"Early start tomorrow."

"Good night, Mr. Puddle," said Deeley.

"Pottle."

"That's it."

Ambrose walked home beneath a moon that seemed to have
been put in the sky expressly to illuminate his defeat.
At the bridge he paused and looked down into the
dark water.

A watery grave suggested itself.

He rejected it. The river was only three feet deep
at that season, and a traveling man drenched to the
armpits attracts ridicule rather than sympathy.

He continued toward his boardinghouse.

At the corner of Pine and Walnut, a thought stopped
him.

He reviewed the evening. Astronomy. Arabians. Aztecs. Albatrosses. Antelopes. Armadillos.
Aardvarks. Ants. Asia. Assyria. Architecture. Aristotle. Australia. Albania. Anacondas. Alps.
Aviation. Alexander.

Every subject had begun with A.

Ambrose stood beneath the sign of the Acme Laundry while
revelation burst upon him.

"A!" he whispered.

Miss Flossie Vane, telephone operator, passed with her aunt and
looked at him sharply.

"A!" repeated Ambrose. "He only knows A!"

That night he opened the first volume of Blossom's duplicate
encyclopedia, borrowed from the reading room of the Pottle Fraternal
Lodge. At two in the morning he knew the difference
between an abacus and an abalone. By three he had
reached Abyssinia. At four he fell asleep upon the Amir
of Afghanistan.

The following week was a stern and splendid campaign.

Ambrose arose at six and studied before breakfast. He studied
aboard trolleys, in hotel lobbies, and behind counters while barbers
considered orders for witch hazel. At lunch he balanced his
encyclopedia beside his soup. At night he pursued knowledge until
the boardinghouse lamp smoked and Mrs. Klepp hammered on the
wall.

He conquered A.

Then, with the prudence of a general fortifying the hill
beyond the hill, he invaded B.

He learned that baboons have formidable canine teeth; that Baffin
Bay is not, strictly speaking, a bay one would select
for bathing; that Babylon had hanging gardens; that bacteria multiply
by division; that Balboa saw the Pacific; that bamboo is
grass with social ambitions; that the barometer measures atmospheric pressure;
that bats are mammals; that Belgium is small but busy;
that belladonna is poisonous; that the Bessemer process concerns steel;
that Bhutan lies in the Himalayas; that biceps are muscles;
that Bismarck was called the Iron Chancellor; that the blackbird
is not always black; that borax is a compound of
boron; and that Brazil produces coffee in quantities capable of
keeping the world awake indefinitely.

Knowledge entered him in blocks, bales, bundles, and barrels. It
jostled the wholesale prices of shaving mugs and pushed aside
useful information about train schedules. By Wednesday he called a
customer in Belden "Bucephalus." By Friday, while demonstrating a hair
tonic, he absent-mindedly described the scalp as an archipelago.

But Pottles are made of stern stuff.

On the following Thursday, wearing the same brown suit and
carrying a fresh box of Choc-O-late Nutties, Ambrose again approached
Blossom Gallup's bungalow.

Deeley was already there.

He occupied the center of the settee, one arm stretched
along its back behind Blossom. On the table stood a
vase of asters.

"Asters," he was saying. "The name comes from the Greek
for star. Botany is rather a specialty of mine."

"Good evening," said Ambrose.

Blossom smiled. "Why, Ambrose!"

Deeley looked up. "Ah, Mr. Pobble."

"Pottle," said Ambrose calmly. "Ambrose Pottle. Ambrose comes from the
Greek *ambrosios*, meaning immortal. It is associated with ambrosia, the
food of the gods."

There was a small silence.

Deeley's mustache twitched.

"Well!" said Blossom. "How fascinating!"

Ambrose placed his candy on the table and sat opposite
them.

"I heard you speaking of asters," he said. "Aster belongs
to the composite family, though modern classifications have altered some
traditional groupings. There are hundreds of species. Not to be
confused with the sea aster, nor with Asterias, the common
starfish."

Mrs. Gallup turned from Ambrose to Deeley as a spectator
turns at a tennis match.

Deeley laughed.

"Exactly. Asterias. Curious animal. Eats corn and pigs."

"Starfish are marine invertebrates," said Ambrose. "They do not eat
corn, and pigs object to salt water. You may be
thinking of carnivorous and piscivorous, which mean flesh-eating and fish-eating."

Blossom blinked. "Not pigs?"

"Not unless the fish is exceptionally ambitious."

Mr. Deeley adjusted his waistcoat.

"My dear Bottle, these technical distinctions—"

"Pottle."

"These technical distinctions are unimportant."

"Accuracy," said Ambrose, "is never unimportant."

He had read that sentence in the preface.

Deeley reached for a Nuttie and failed to find the
box, which Ambrose had quietly placed beside his own chair.

"Perhaps," said Deeley, "you would prefer history. Alexander the Great—"

"Son of Philip the Second of Macedon," said Ambrose. "Born
356 B.C., tutored by Aristotle, defeated Darius, died at Babylon.
Speaking of Babylon, have you ever studied its hanging gardens?"

"Frequently."

"Who built them?"

Deeley smiled indulgently. "The Babylonians."

"Naturally. But for whom?"

"For—ah—Mrs. Babylon."

Blossom looked uncertain.

"Tradition credits Nebuchadnezzar," said Ambrose, "with building them for his
wife Amytis, though the historical evidence is debated."

"How romantic!" said Blossom.

"Very," Ambrose agreed. "A man will undertake heavy work for
the woman he loves."

This was aimed directly at Blossom, and it arrived.

Deeley cleared his throat.

"Gardens are hardly history. Now, Aristotle—"

"A pupil of Plato and tutor of Alexander. Wrote on
logic, ethics, politics, rhetoric, biology, and many other matters. His
classification of animals, though outdated, was remarkable for its time.
Biology, I may add, is the science of life."

"Certainly," said Deeley. "Biology is rather a specialty of mine."

"Then you can tell Mrs. Gallup the distinction between a
baboon and a badger."

Deeley stared.

"One begins," he said, "with 'bab' and the other with
'bad.'"

"True but insufficient. The baboon is a primate, chiefly African,
with a long muzzle and powerful teeth. The badger is
a burrowing mammal of the weasel family. Neither should be
confused with the bandicoot, which is a marsupial, or with
the barracuda, which is a fish and seldom burrows."

"How fascinating!" cried Blossom.

Ambrose warmed to his work. For six days the facts
had lain within him like merchandise in a warehouse. Now
the doors flew open.

"The bactrian camel has two humps. The Arabian camel has
one. Baffin Island is among the largest islands in the
world. Balboa crossed the Isthmus of Panama and saw the
Pacific in 1513. Bamboo is technically a grass. Barometers may
use mercury, though aneroid barometers do not. Bats are mammals
and are not blind. Bavaria is a region of Germany.
Bayeux is famous for a tapestry which is, strictly speaking,
embroidery. Beavers build dams. Beetles belong to the order Coleoptera.
Beethoven continued to compose after losing his hearing. Belladonna contains
atropine. Bengal is a region of the Indian subcontinent. The
Bering Strait separates Asia from North America. Beryllium is a
chemical element. Bhutan—"

"Stop!" said Deeley.

Ambrose stopped.

The room rang with the silence following an avalanche.

Mrs. Gallup's eyes shone. "Mr. Deeley, what is the capital
of Bhutan?"

Deeley's confidence had received several flesh wounds, but it still
breathed.

"The capital of Bhutan," he said, "is—Aden."

"Aden begins with A," said Ambrose.

"What has that to do with it?"

"It is also not the capital of Bhutan."

"Then," said Blossom, "what is?"

Ambrose hesitated. His encyclopedia, being twelve years old, had described
Bhutan's government without offering a simple capital in the modern
sense. To bluff now would be to become Deeley.

"The answer is complicated," he said honestly. "My book doesn't
make it clear, and I don't know."

Blossom appeared even more impressed.

Deeley seized the opportunity.

"Ha! He doesn't know!"

"No," said Ambrose. "Do you?"

"Certainly."

"What is it?"

Deeley's mustache searched the ceiling for aid.

"Bhutan City."

Ambrose smiled.

"Let us try something easier. What is borax?"

"A wild animal of the antelope family."

"What is the Bessemer process?"

"A method of preserving fruit."

"Who was Bismarck?"

"A manufacturer of baking powder."

"Where is Brazil?"

"In—Asia."

"Name one subject beginning with B that you know anything
about."

Mr. Deeley rose. He had turned from rubicund to empurpled.

"This is absurd! I do not submit to examinations in
private homes."

"Then choose the subject yourself."

"I refuse."

"Anything beginning with B."

"Your behavior," said Deeley, "is boorish."

"Boorish," Ambrose agreed. "From boor, meaning a rude or unmannerly
person. You know one B word, anyway."

Mrs. Gallup covered a laugh with her hand.

It was a small laugh, but it struck Deeley with
the force of artillery.

He glared at Ambrose.

"You planned this."

"For a week."

"You crammed."

"Day and night."

"You are no scholar."

"No."

"You are an impostor!"

"Yes," said Ambrose. "But I'm an impostor who reached B."

Deeley took up his hat.

Mrs. Gallup rose. "Mr. Deeley, is it true? Did you
only study subjects beginning with A?"

He drew himself up.

"My intellectual methods are my own affair."

"What do you do for a living?" asked Ambrose.

Deeley's pleased mustache was no longer pleased. It drooped like
wet upholstery.

"I am in publishing."

"What do you publish?"

"A scientific work."

"What is it called?"

Deeley muttered.

Blossom leaned toward him. "I didn't hear."

"*Hog Culture*," he said.

Ambrose gazed at him.

"You sell a book on hog farming?"

"It is the authoritative work on modern swine management."

"And the encyclopedia?"

"It is included free with every twelve-volume deluxe subscription."

Mr. Deeley moved toward the door.

"Good night, Mrs. Gallup."

"Good night," she said coldly.

At the threshold he turned upon Ambrose.

"And good night to you, Mr. B-B-B—"

"Pottle."

The door closed.

For a moment Ambrose and Blossom stood without speaking. Outside,
Mr. Deeley could be heard colliding with a galvanized watering
can. The sound was not cultured, but it was satisfying.

Blossom sat slowly.

"Ambrose," she said, "you were magnificent."

Ambrose's heart expanded. Then honesty, which is a stubborn and
inconvenient organ, prodded it.

"No," he said.

"But you knew everything."

"I knew two letters."

"What?"

"I'm no more cultured than Deeley. Not really. I caught
him because every subject he talked about began with A.
So I memorized A, and then I memorized B to
get ahead of him."

"You studied all that in one week?"

"Yes."

"For me?"

"Yes."

"Every night?"

"Until two or three."

Her eyes softened.

Ambrose looked at the shining green books, at the vase
of asters, at the unopened *Cultivated Woman's Companion*. His courage,
having survived Babylon, barometers, and Bhutan, decided to finish the
journey.

"Blossom, I don't know much about poetry. I can't tell
an old master from a new varnish advertisement. Before Tuesday
I thought Bessemer was a cough remedy. But I'm steady.
I don't drink, except root beer. I've got fourteen hundred
dollars saved, and I can repair a window cord, carry
coal, keep accounts, and sit up all night when somebody
is sick. I loved you before I knew what an
aardvark was, and I'll love you after I've forgotten."

Blossom's lips trembled.

"I said I wanted a cultured man," she whispered.

"I know."

"I thought that meant someone who could say impressive things."

"Deeley could say them."

"Yes, but he ate your candy."

"He did."

"And called you Puddle."

"Among other things."

She moved nearer.

"Jason knew all about hay," she said, "but when my
mother was ill he complained about the doctor's carriage blocking
the feed-store entrance. Mr. Deeley knew about Aristotle—or pretended to—but
he never once asked whether I was tired. You always
notice."

Ambrose said nothing. Hope was doing such violent things inside
him that speech seemed unsafe.

"What I really want," Blossom continued, "is a strong man."

Ambrose glanced doubtfully at his arms. They had carried sample
cases but had never been publicly identified as strong.

"A strong man," she explained, "who is kind enough to
be gentle and brave enough to tell the truth."

She placed her hand in his.

"Ambrose, would you care to ask me what you were
trying to ask last Thursday?"

He swallowed. The practiced speech had vanished again, but this
time he did not need it.

"Blossom, will you marry me?"

"Yes."

He stared at her.

"Yes?"

"Yes, Ambrose."

He took her in his arms. The embrace was somewhat
impeded by his severe collar and her ornamental brooch, but
no great romance has ever been permanently defeated by haberdashery.

After a while Blossom rested her golden head against his
shoulder.

"Do you suppose," she murmured, "that we ought to become
cultured together?"

"I suppose we might."

"We could read the encyclopedia."

"A little every evening."

"Starting with C?"

Ambrose considered. Before him stretched camels and Canada, cathedrals and
chemistry, Chaucer and China; then D, E, F, and the
whole tremendous alphabet of married improvement.

"No," he said. "We'd better begin again with A. I
was in a hurry the first time."

Blossom sighed contentedly.

"And when we reach H," she said, "we can skip
hogs."

Outside, the moon shone upon the Gallup bungalow, the feed
store, the trolley wires, and Mr. Deeley's retreating hat. Inside,
beneath the approving gaze of fifty-four unread volumes, Ambrose Pottle
kissed his future wife.

It was not scholarship.

It was better.

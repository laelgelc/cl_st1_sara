# The Eagle on the Drum

For ten years Stuart McGregor had been afraid of a
dead man.

Not continuously. There were mornings when the clean, metallic uproar
of New York battered Africa from his mind; afternoons when
telephones rang, wheels shrieked, elevators clanged, and the business of
living left no crevice into which terror might insert a
claw. There were evenings, too, when whisky laid a warm
amber curtain between him and memory.

But always the curtain lifted.

Then he saw Farragut Hutchison as he had last seen
him beneath the blackish-green wall of the Bakoto forest—broad shoulders
glistening with sweat, shirt hanging in tatters, and between the
shoulder blades that ridiculous blue eagle, wings spread, beak open,
one tattooed eye puckered by a fold of living muscle.

Winking.

"Dead men do not cross oceans," Father O'Donnell told him.

They sat in McGregor's library on East Forty-Eighth Street while
a July thunderstorm withheld its rain and pressed its purple
bulk against the city. The windows were open, yet no
breeze stirred the curtains. Heat had softened the asphalt in
the street. Motorcars passed with a sucking sound, as though
New York itself were being slowly drawn into some enormous
mouth.

McGregor wiped his forehead.

"You didn't know Hutchison."

"I know death."

"You know graves," said McGregor. "That isn't the same thing."

Father O'Donnell's gray eyes moved about the room. Spears, shields,
hideous little wooden images, elephant tusks and masks stared back
at him from every wall. McGregor had spent twenty-five years
going into places from which prudent men came out rapidly,
if at all. A lion had raked his ribs in
Somaliland. A spear had passed through the flesh of his
thigh near Lake Rudolf. He had crossed a flooded river
with crocodiles turning like submerged logs beneath his canoe. These
things he described calmly.

But at the name Farragut Hutchison his hands trembled.

"His bones were found," said the priest.

"Some bones were found."

"The identification—"

"His belt buckle. His compass. Two gold teeth. Yes. I
know."

"Then what do you fear?"

McGregor leaned forward. His face was large and florid, but
beneath the tropical tan there showed now a grayish, unhealthy
pallor.

"I fear," he whispered, "that Hutchison will kill me."

Father O'Donnell made no answer.

"From beyond the grave," McGregor added.

A truck struck a hollow place in the street. Boom—boom!

McGregor sprang up, overturning his glass.

Only a truck. Only New York.

Yet for one appalling instant the sound had been the
Bakoto drums, muttering from hill to hill beneath a moon
like a diseased yellow eye.

Father O'Donnell crossed himself almost furtively.

McGregor saw the gesture and laughed. It was a harsh,
staccato laugh, without mirth.

"You see? Even you!"

"I crossed myself because your soul is in danger."

"My soul!" McGregor sank back. "You think I'm sorry. That's
where you're wrong. I'd do it again."

"Do what?"

For a long moment McGregor stared at the priest. Outside,
the withheld storm crouched over the city. The stars had
vanished. The towers, black against a phosphorous-green horizon, seemed less
built than grown—tremendous fungal shapes thrusting from hot stone.

At last he said:

"Cheat at cards."

And he remembered.

Always, when he remembered, it began with laughter.

Not wholesome laughter, but the drunken barking of two white
men isolated in an immensity that had no use for
white men. They had been six weeks beyond the Belgian
trading station, following rumors of a ruined city and a
deposit of alluvial gold. Their carriers had deserted after the
third night of drumming. Their guide had vanished after the
fifth, leaving only footprints which ended beside a marsh.

Isolated!

The word had possessed a taste: coppery, bitter, feverish.

Yet they still had gin.

McGregor remembered Hutchison squatting by the fire, his red beard
beaded with perspiration, while the forest crowded around them. It
did not merely stand. It crept. It leaned upon furry
trunks. It thrust waxen flowers through curtains of creeper. It
breathed sour-miasmic breaths and whispered with ten million wings.

A leopard coughed in the darkness.

Farther off, a lioness answered in an inadequate treble.

Something clicked among the leaves—clicked, paused, and clicked again.

Then the drums began.

Boom—boom-boom.

Pause.

Boom!

Hutchison raised the gin bottle in salute.

"Geewhittaker," he said, "they play our tune."

They had found the Bakoto shrine that afternoon in a
clearing where the sunlight fell crimson through broad red leaves.
The fetish sat beneath a roof of plaited reeds. It
was a squat thing of black wood, potbellied and grinning,
with copper nails for teeth and two rough emeralds pressed
into its eye sockets. Around its neck hung strings of
claws, finger bones, shells and yellow nuggets of gold.

The place should have warned them. Even birds avoided it.

But gin had made them emperors.

Hutchison had placed his sun helmet upon the idol's head.
McGregor had poured liquor into its carved mouth. They had
laughed, sung a verse unfit for any shrine, white or
black, and pried out the emerald eyes with the point
of a hunting knife. Hutchison took the gold. McGregor took
a little ivory box from between the idol's feet.

Then Hutchison, swaying, had urinated upon the sacred stones.

The forest said nothing.

That silence was worse than any cry.

Now, beside their fire, the drums passed the message outward
through the night. One answered another, hill beyond hill, until
the wilderness possessed a gigantic pulse.

Boom—boom.

Boom—boom.

"They know," said McGregor.

"Let 'em."

"We ought to move."

"At night?"

"They'll be here by morning."

Hutchison scratched one shoulder. The movement caused the blue eagle
on his back to wrinkle beneath the torn shirt. He
had acquired the tattoo in Singapore from a drunken American
sailor who claimed the bird was copied from a presidential
seal. The sailor had made one wing longer than the
other and given the eagle a sly, lopsided eye.

As the muscles shifted, the eye closed.

A wink.

McGregor flung his tin cup at him.

Hutchison stared. "What's biting you?"

"Your damned bird."

"My—?" Hutchison twisted, trying absurdly to look between his own
shoulders. Then he laughed. "Geewhittaker, Stuart, the gin's finally pickled
your brains."

They slept badly.

Before dawn the fire sank into ruby hollows. Damp darkness
congealed about them. From the trees came the plop of
heavy fruit, the slither of scaled bodies, the minute rasping
of leaves rubbed together by creatures too small to name.
McGregor woke with a hand over his mouth and the
cold edge of a spear beneath his chin.

The warriors appeared to have been spewed from the earth.

There were perhaps thirty of them, lean and silent, their
bodies streaked with ochre and ash. Feathers trembled above their
brows. Knives, polished by generations of use, caught the first
rotten glimmer of dawn.

Neither McGregor nor Hutchison fired a shot.

Be it mentioned that each had faced charging beasts, hostile
rifles and seas whose waves rose like collapsing cliffs. Physical
courage they possessed in abundance. But there is a courage
of noon and a courage of three in the morning,
and these are not always the same.

They were bound and marched.

The village lay in a basin surrounded by colossal trees
whose smooth trunks resembled the black pipes of some infernal
boiler house. Huts crouched beneath them. Smoke dragged itself along
the ground. Children watched without expression; dogs, hairless and ulcerous,
sniffed at the prisoners' boots.

At the center stood the desecrated fetish.

Its empty eye sockets accused them.

The Bakoto chief received them beneath a striped canopy. He
was an old man, spare and erect, wearing a robe
of faded blue cloth and a necklace of leopard teeth.
His face possessed the calm courtesy of a physician preparing
to announce that an operation, though regrettable, would be fatal.

Beside him stood an interpreter from the coast, a narrow
fellow in a linen jacket several sizes too large.

The chief spoke.

The interpreter bowed to the prisoners.

"He says you have insulted the Guardian Who Watches Below."

"Tell him we'll pay," McGregor said quickly. "Gold, rifles, anything."

The chief listened to the translation and smiled.

"He says the Guardian has no need of rifles."

"Then emeralds. We'll return them."

"He says eyes once plucked out do not see again
merely because they are put back."

Hutchison swore.

The old chief continued in a gentle, almost apologetic voice.

"He says your offense requires that one man should remain
here and speak to the Guardian. For a long time."

"How long?" asked Hutchison.

The interpreter looked uncomfortable.

The chief explained.

The speaking, it appeared, would begin with small knives. No
vital place would be touched. Then red ants would be
introduced into shallow cuts, and honey applied where the ants
proved reluctant. At sunset heated fibers would be inserted beneath
the nails. Water would be given generously; food also, for
the Bakoto had no desire that weakness should interrupt the
conversation. If fever came, certain medicines would be administered.

The chief regretted that the entire ceremony might occupy four
days.

His regret was exquisite.

"A procedure," McGregor said afterward, "worthy of a more civilized
race."

Father O'Donnell stiffened, but McGregor, seeing Africa instead of the
priest, went on.

The chief offered them a privilege. Since two men had
committed the offense and only one victim was ceremonially necessary,
the strangers might select between themselves who should remain.

"We'll fight for it," Hutchison said.

The chief declined. The stronger man might prevail, and strength
was no proof of justice.

"We'll draw lots."

There were no suitable lots.

Then McGregor remembered the cards in his pocket.

The Bakoto examined the greasy pack with interest. The interpreter
explained games of chance. The chief approved. Fate, he said,
was the Guardian's younger brother.

A table was brought—a low, scarred block upon which meat
had recently been cut. McGregor and Hutchison sat facing each
other. Around them the village gathered in concentric silence. Flies
moved from the table to their faces and back again.

"One hand," Hutchison said.

"One hand."

"Five-card draw."

"Agreed."

"Loser stays."

McGregor's fingers touched the pack.

Then an extraordinary thing occurred—or so he afterward told himself.
His mind seemed to divide. One portion shrieked that no
man could do what he was about to do; another,
cold and skillful, sent telegraphic instructions down his arms.

He had been a cardsharp before he became an explorer.
In river saloons and mining camps he had learned to
make pasteboards obey him as soldiers obey a bugle. He
could hold out a card, deal seconds, stack a deck
under the eyes of men who killed on suspicion.

His fingers remembered.

They moved automatically. Delicately. Innocently.

He shuffled. The aces gathered beneath his palms.

Always he insisted that he had not consciously chosen. His
hands had chosen. His hands desired to live.

He dealt.

Hutchison picked up his cards and frowned. McGregor looked at
his own hand: two tens, a seven, a four, a
queen. The visible hand was nothing. The hidden hand waited.

"Cards?" he asked.

"Three."

Hutchison discarded three. McGregor gave him three from the top.

"Four," said McGregor.

As he drew, his little finger released the concealed packet.
One ace. Two. Three. Four. The movement was smooth as
oil slipping over water.

Hutchison watched his face, not his hands.

The chief watched both, smiling faintly.

"Show," said Hutchison.

He laid down three kings.

A murmur passed through the villagers, though they did not
know the game; they understood triumph by the light in
Hutchison's eyes.

Then McGregor spread four aces upon the bloody table.

Hutchison stared.

The jungle hummed.

The old chief inclined his head, accepting the decision of
fate.

For several seconds Hutchison made no sound. His gaze shifted
from the cards to McGregor's face, and in that gaze
suspicion was born—small at first, then swelling, blackening, filling the
whole man.

"You—" he said.

McGregor's hand went toward the pistol no longer at his
belt.

"You stacked them."

"No."

"You card-rat. You crooked—"

The warriors seized Hutchison.

He fought magnificently. He knocked one man senseless with his
head, bit another through the cheek, and dragged three more
across the dust. Then a club struck behind his ear.
He sagged to his knees.

His shirt ripped from neck to waist.

The eagle showed.

Blue upon the sweating skin, its malformed wings lifted as
his shoulder muscles strained. Its eye puckered.

Winked.

Hutchison raised his head. Blood ran down into his beard.

"I'll get you, Stuart."

McGregor tried to answer, but his mouth was dry.

"Do you hear me? I'll get you if I have
to crawl out of hell!"

Then, with grotesque irrelevance, Hutchison laughed.

"Four aces! Geewhittaker!"

They dragged him toward the hut of knives.

McGregor remained seated before the cards.

At noon the Bakoto returned his weapons, his pack and
the ivory box. Their law had been satisfied. No one
guarded him. No one needed to.

From the hut came a scream.

It climbed, broke, became a hoarse animal bellow, then climbed
again. The drums joined it—basso thunder under that human treble.

McGregor fled.

All day he hacked through creepers and thorn. The jungle
closed behind him like a curtain. Vines caught his ankles.
Flowers brushed him with cold, waxen fingers. Once he stumbled
into a pool where pale, slimy things twisted away beneath
the scum. Once he heard a leopard padding beside him,
unseen. But above every sound, even miles away, came Hutchison's
voice.

At sunset it was still screaming.

At midnight it had become a bubbling whistle.

Near dawn it stopped.

The silence followed McGregor all the way to the coast.

"Then you admit," said Father O'Donnell, "that you murdered him."

"I won a hand of poker."

"You cheated."

"I survived."

"And you feel no remorse?"

McGregor considered this. His expression became almost offended.

"Remorse? Hutchison would have done the same."

"That is no answer."

"It is the only answer in places like that."

"Then why are you afraid?"

McGregor's eyes moved toward a mask upon the wall. Its
shell teeth gleamed in the sultry dark.

"Because he knows."

"His bones were found three years later."

"So they told me."

"A Belgian survey party discovered them outside the abandoned village.
The remains bore signs—" Father O'Donnell paused.

"Yes. Signs."

"And the tattoo was recorded."

McGregor stared at him sharply. "No."

"No?"

"The report mentioned the teeth, buckle and compass. Not the
tattoo."

"But surely, if the skin—"

"There wouldn't have been skin after three years."

The priest was silent.

McGregor rose and paced beneath the trophies of his expeditions.
He had obtained wealth, reputation, lectureships, medals. Men in clubs
spoke his name with admiration. Newspapers called him indomitable.

Yet he slept with a pistol beneath his pillow and
a lamp burning beside his bed.

"I've seen him in crowds," he said. "Once in Marseille.
Once on Broadway. A red beard turning a corner. A
broad back in a torn shirt. Never the face. Always
the back."

"Imagination."

"I receive letters with no writing inside."

"Practical jokes."

"Twice I found playing cards on my breakfast tray. A
black ace."

"Coincidence."

"And last winter, when I had pneumonia, I woke and
saw that eagle on the ceiling. Its wings covered the
room. Its beak opened. It winked down at me."

"Fever."

"Words!" McGregor snarled. "You've a neat white word for every
black thing."

At that moment the bell rang.

Its small electric buzz sounded shockingly loud.

McGregor froze.

The servant entered carrying a silver tray.

"A note for you, sir. From Mr. Caspar Vale."

McGregor's face relaxed. Vale was an importer and dealer in
antiquities—a dry little spider of a man who maintained a
shop on Second Avenue, crowded with Egyptian funerary cats, Tibetan
trumpets, Polynesian clubs and idols manufactured the previous year in
Hoboken.

McGregor tore open the envelope.

My dear Mr. McGregor,

A curious object from the Upper Congo has come into
my possession. I believe its workmanship may interest you, and
I venture to send it for your examination. The covering
is unusual. I should particularly value your opinion concerning the
design upon it.

The object will arrive tonight by messenger.

Yours, etc.,
Caspar Vale

"An African curiosity," McGregor said. "Vale is always sending me
rubbish."

Father O'Donnell took his hat.

"I must go."

"Because of a curiosity?"

"Because it is late."

"Because you're frightened."

The priest looked toward the windows. At last the storm
had begun to mutter beyond the city, but still no
rain fell. Heat filled the room like invisible steam.

"I am frightened for you," he said. "Not of Hutchison."

Again he crossed himself. This time openly.

After he had gone, McGregor drank.

Nine o'clock. Ten. Eleven.

The temperature climbed instead of falling. The city wilted beneath
it. In alleyways cats cried with the voices of murdered
children. Fire engines rang through distant streets. Thunder moved along
the horizon, never approaching, like artillery in another world.

At eleven-thirty the package arrived.

It was a wooden crate, narrow and three feet high,
smelling of salt, mold and something sweetly corrupt. McGregor dismissed
the messenger, fetched a hammer and pried away the boards.

Inside, packed in yellow straw, stood a drum.

It was an African signal drum of the waisted type,
carved from a single block of dark wood. The sides
bore rows of crouching figures whose eyes were disks of
tarnished copper. Thongs, brittle with age, held a pale membrane
across the top.

McGregor bent nearer.

The membrane was not hide from any animal.

It was white.

Not clean white, but the yellow-white of old ivory, with
faint pores, wrinkles and sparse reddish hairs. Across it sprawled
a blue design blurred by stretching.

One crooked wing.

A hooked beak.

An eagle.

For an instant McGregor neither moved nor breathed.

Then his terror broke—not into a scream, but into laughter.

He laughed until tears ran down his face. He beat
his fists against his knees. He staggered around the crate,
roaring with relief, while the copper-eyed carvings crouched and watched.

"Dead!" he cried. "Dead, you fool! Skinned and made into
a drum!"

All the years of dread collapsed. Here was proof more
certain than bones, reports or priests. Here was Hutchison's skin,
preserved, tanned, stretched across a hollow log. Hutchison could not
be lurking in Marseille, could not follow him through Broadway
crowds, could not crawl from hell.

He had become merchandise.

A curiosity.

A joke in Caspar Vale's dusty shop.

McGregor lifted the drum and embraced it. The old skin
was warm from the night, almost feverishly warm. His fingers
caressed the eagle.

"You were going to get me, eh?"

The tattooed eye seemed to narrow beneath his thumb.

Wink.

McGregor's laughter stopped.

Something moved inside the drum.

A dry, papery whisper.

He held the object away from him. From the slitlike
opening in its side protruded a small wedge-shaped head, blackish-green
and glossy as wet leaves. Two eyes shone like drops
of oil. A forked tongue flickered.

The snake had traveled torpid in the packed drum, chilled
in warehouse and ship's hold. But New York that night
was tropical—sodden, breathless, steaming. The heat had entered the wood.
It had called the venom back into life.

The serpent flowed out with dreadful speed.

McGregor screamed and dropped the drum.

It struck the carpet—boom!—and rolled.

The snake fastened above his wrist.

He tore it loose and flung it against the wall,
but already two crimson beads swelled upon his skin. He
reached for the bell. His fingers missed. The room tilted;
masks grimaced; spears lengthened; walls became furry trees glistening with
jungle sweat.

Boom, said the rolling drum.

Boom.

McGregor fell beside it.

Even as he died, he knew the snake was real.

Even as he died, he understood the material chain of
ship, warehouse, summer heat and awakening venom.

Even as he died, he saw the eagle upon the
human skin wrinkle as the drum rocked to rest.

And even as he died, its blue, lopsided eye closed
in one slow, triumphant wink.

"Hutchison," he whispered.

Outside, the thunder broke at last.

Boom—boom.

Boom.

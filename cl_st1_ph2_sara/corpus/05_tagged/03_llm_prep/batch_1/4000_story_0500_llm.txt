### THE PASSENGER UNDER THE CHAIRS

I first made the acquaintance of Roger Cupples on the
third day out from Liverpool. He was a large, smooth-faced
man, with a gold watch-chain, an imposing manner, and a
habit of laying down the law upon every subject from
the management of steamships to the proper boiling of potatoes.
He told me that he was a lawyer in San
Francisco, though he had lately been attending to important business
in New York.

"The Pacific coast, sir," he said, "is the field for
a man of intellect. Everything is young there—even the lawsuits."

I had no reason to doubt him. On board ship
a man is generally accepted for what he says he
is, unless he says he is a good sailor.

We had encountered rough weather for two days, but on
the evening when my adventure began the sea had fallen
almost calm. The damp deck shone beneath the lamps, and
behind us the wake stretched in a long luminous road,
trembling and breaking under the dark sky. Most of the
passengers had gone below. I walked aft, past the companion-way
and the tarpaulin-covered gear, until I came to a stack
of folding steamer chairs near the aft wheelhouse.

As I passed them, one of the chairs moved.

I stopped.

There was another movement—a faint scrape, followed by a low
groan.

"Who is there?" I asked.

No answer came. I stepped closer and pulled aside two
chairs. A face looked out at me.

It was a miserable face—thin, hollow, and grey with suffering.
A ragged cap covered the man's hair, and a torn
coat was drawn about him. He crouched between the chairs
and the bulwark, with much the air of a man
who is stealing fowls and expects every instant to be
seized by the farmer.

"For Heaven's sake, sir," he whispered, "don't call out!"

"What are you doing there?"

"I'm a stowaway, sir."

The answer was so plain that it left me with
nothing to say.

He gripped my sleeve.

"Don't give me up, sir. I've had nothing to eat
since yesterday morning—not a crust. I'll work when we land.
I'll pay the company. But if they find me now,
they'll put me in irons, perhaps, or take me back.
Oh, no, no, no—don't call them!"

"Why did you come aboard without a passage?"

"Because I had no money."

"That is usually the reason," I said.

He gave me a quick look, uncertain whether I was
joking.

"Poverty is the great crime, sir. If a rich man
wants to cross the ocean, they bow him into a
state-room and feed him six times a day. If a
poor man wants to cross, they call the police. There's
food thrown away from this ship that would keep me
alive a month, but if I touch it I'm a
thief. Is that justice, sir?"

There was enough truth in this to make me uncomfortable.
I had myself complained that afternoon because the pudding was
cold.

"What brought you to this pass?" I asked.

He told me a story of long unemployment in Liverpool,
a dead wife, a child in Brooklyn, and a promised
situation with a builder there. It was told in broken
sentences and with such signs of weakness that I could
not listen to it unmoved.

"If I can only get one square meal," he finished,
"I'll keep quiet till we arrive. You'll never hear a
sound from me. I swear it, sir."

I told him to remain where he was. Then I
went below.

The dining saloon was empty, but a steward was carrying
a tray along the narrow passage.

"Steward," I said, "can I get some supper?"

"Yessir."

"Cold meat, bread, and a bottle of stout."

"Yessir."

"And put in enough for two. I am hungry."

"Yessir."

He looked at me without curiosity, for a good steward
has seen greater mysteries than a hungry passenger. Ten minutes
later the tray was in my state-room. I waited until
the passage was clear, covered the food with a napkin,
and carried it aft.

The stowaway fell upon it. There is no other phrase
for the manner in which he ate. He tore the
bread, swallowed the meat almost without chewing, and drank the
stout in three long pulls. Once he paused, with his
mouth full, and shook my hand.

"God bless you, sir."

"Quietly," I said. "You will have the quartermaster here."

He immediately crouched lower.

"You won't report me?"

"No."

"You promise, sir?"

"I promise."

I left him with what remained of the loaf and
returned to the smoking-room, where I found Cupples alone, reading
a newspaper three days old with the solemnity of a
judge examining a will.

I should have kept my own counsel. But a secret
on shipboard grows heavier than an anchor, and Cupples appeared
to me a sensible man of legal experience. I therefore
sat beside him and told him the whole affair in
a low voice.

He folded the newspaper very slowly.

"You fed him?" he asked.

"Yes."

"And gave him drink?"

"A bottle of stout."

"Good heavens!"

"What is the matter?"

"Did any person witness the transaction?"

"No."

He leaned back and looked at me with professional compassion.

"My dear sir, you have placed yourself in a very
serious position."

"How serious?"

"That depends upon the interpretation of the statute. Under the
Passenger Conveyance, Maritime Security and Clandestine Embarkation Act, any person
who shall harbour, conceal, sustain, nourish, assist, comfort, encourage or
otherwise give aid to a stowaway upon any vessel, barge,
brig, schooner, bark, clipper, steamship or other craft, whether propelled
by sail, steam, paddle, screw, tow-line, current, or any combination
thereof, is held to be an accessory before and after
the fact."

I stared at him.

"I never heard of such an act."

"Very few laymen have. That is why prisons are full."

"What is the penalty?"

"In England, two years. Under American jurisdiction it may be
five. There was the celebrated case of Dobson versus The
Queen, afterwards reversed in The Queen versus Dobson, though Dobson
had died in the interval, which raised a highly technical
question as to whether a dead defendant could recover costs."

"But I merely gave a starving man food."

"The law," said Cupples, "does not ask whether the meat
was hot or cold."

I rose in alarm.

"What should I do?"

"Report him."

"I promised not to."

"A promise made in furtherance of an illegal concealment is
void from the beginning. But you must act carefully. Above
all, act before witnesses."

"Why witnesses?"

"To establish your good faith. Go to the purser, the
chief officer, and at least two respectable passengers. Inform them
that you have discovered the man and that, after struggling
between private sentiment and public duty, you have chosen duty.
Use those words if you like."

"I do not like."

"Nevertheless, they are safe words. Then lead the officers to
the spot. Do not touch the accused. Do not threaten
him. Do not offer him more stout. And, on no
account, permit him to escape."

Cupples spoke with such gravity that my offence grew darker
every moment. Yet I disliked breaking my promise.

"I'll think it over," I said.

"Think rapidly. Concealment continued after knowledge aggravates the original misdemeanour."

At that instant the ship gave a slow roll, and
Cupples caught the table.

"I shall go to my state-room," he said. "Legal reflection
upon a moving vessel disturbs the stomach."

He left me in a highly uncomfortable condition.

For perhaps twenty minutes I walked about the deck. I
looked toward the pile of chairs, but could see no
movement. At last I decided that Cupples was right. My
promise had been foolish, though humanely intended. The man would
be discovered sooner or later, and I could perhaps speak
for him if I brought him forward myself.

Before doing so, however, I went to Cupples's state-room to
ask whether the captain or the chief officer was the
proper authority.

His door was partly open. I knocked.

There was no reply.

I pushed it an inch farther and saw Cupples standing
before the little mirror over his washstand.

His coat and waistcoat were off. In one hand he
held a ragged cap. With the other he was rubbing
something grey along his cheek. On the berth beside him
lay a torn coat, a piece of blackened cork, and
a small case of grease-paints.

I remained silent.

Cupples adjusted the cap, bent his shoulders, drew down the
corners of his mouth, and turned toward the mirror.

The starving stowaway looked back at him.

At first I felt foolish. Then I felt angry. Then,
as the complete beauty of the situation presented itself, I
felt cheerful.

I withdrew before he saw me and softly closed the
door.

It was clear now. Cupples, the San Francisco lawyer, had
manufactured the stowaway, the dead wife, the child in Brooklyn,
and very likely the Passenger Conveyance, Maritime Security and Clandestine
Embarkation Act. He had eaten my supper, frightened me with
imprisonment, and was preparing to return to his hiding-place in
order to enjoy my confession before witnesses.

I determined that he should enjoy all of it.

I went first to the chief officer.

"There is a stowaway concealed among the folded chairs by
the aft wheelhouse," I said.

"Are you sure?"

"I have fed him."

The officer frowned.

"You ought to have reported him at once."

"So I have been advised. I was also advised to
act before witnesses."

"Who advised you?"

"A lawyer."

That appeared to satisfy him.

We collected the purser, two sailors, and four passengers from
the smoking-room. Another gentleman joined us because he saw a
procession and believed somebody had fallen overboard. I led them
aft.

The chairs were silent.

The chief officer signalled to the sailors, who approached from
either side.

"Come out of that!" he ordered.

There was a rustle.

"Who's there?" cried a weak voice.

"You know who's there. Come out!"

The ragged head appeared. Cupples's acting was admirable.

"Oh, gentlemen," he pleaded, "have mercy! I'm a cabin passenger!"

"A cabin passenger under the chairs?" said the purser.

"My name is Roger Cupples. I can explain."

The sailors laughed.

"Every stowaway's a duke when he's caught," one remarked.

Cupples attempted to rise, but the chairs caught his coat
and came rattling down around him. He scrambled free and
made a dash toward the companion-way. One sailor seized him
by the collar.

"Let go!" shouted Cupples.

"Hold on, Bill!" cried the other sailor.

"You can't both hold on and let go," observed a
passenger.

Cupples twisted about.

"Confound you, this is a joke! Ask that gentleman—the man
standing there. He knows me."

Every face turned toward me.

I looked at Cupples with the greatest astonishment I could
command.

"I never saw this person before this evening," I said.

"You ate supper with me!" he cried.

"No," I answered, "you ate mine."

The purser took him by the arm.

"If you are a passenger, you can prove it below."

"My key is in my pocket. Cabin thirty-seven. Take your
hands off me!"

"He admits having a cabin," said one of the passengers.
"That makes the case worse. He has probably robbed it."

Cupples now perceived that further resistance would only enlarge his
audience. He allowed himself to be marched through the narrow
passages, with the sailors at his elbows and the rest
of us following. Passengers came out of their state-rooms. Ladies
looked over the saloon rail. A child in a nightdress
asked whether the pirates had come aboard.

At cabin thirty-seven Cupples produced the key. Inside, the chief
officer found his clothes, luggage, card-case, and name upon the
passenger list.

There was a short silence.

Cupples removed the ragged cap.

"I trust," he said, "that this establishes my identity."

The chief officer looked from him to me.

"Is this the lawyer who advised you?"

"The same."

The whole company broke into laughter.

Cupples bore it well. He washed the grey from his
face, put on his coat, and explained that he had
intended a harmless practical joke. He had observed, he said,
that I possessed a sympathetic nature combined with an excessive
respect for legal authority, and he had wished to test
both.

"You worked it pretty slick," I told him.

"Up to a point," he replied.

The chief officer said that, as no real stowaway existed,
he would overlook the disturbance. The purser hinted that a
disturbance involving so many thirsty witnesses could not properly be
closed without refreshment.

Cupples accepted the judgment. He went to the smoking-room and
stood treat all round. Even the sailors received something by
private arrangement with the steward. When my glass was filled,
Cupples raised his own.

"To the law," he said, "which, though occasionally delayed, never
fails to lay hold of the right man."

The next morning I learned that he was neither Cupples
nor a lawyer. His real name was Arthur Vale, and
he was a New York actor returning from an English
engagement. Several passengers knew him by reputation, though I did
not. His legal manner, he explained, had been borrowed from
a judge in a melodrama, while the stowaway was a
character he had once played in a short curtain piece.

Three nights later we held an entertainment for the benefit
of the Seamen's Orphan Fund. There were songs, a recitation
about Waterloo, a conjuring performance in which the captain's watch
narrowly escaped permanent disappearance, and finally Mr. Arthur Vale in
his celebrated character sketch, "The Stowaway."

He came upon the platform bent, ragged, starving, and grey.
He spoke of poverty as the great crime; of food
wasted within reach of hungry men; of the rich welcomed
into state-rooms and the poor hunted into corners. The saloon
became very still. Two ladies wiped their eyes. Even I
felt a tightening in my throat, though I knew he
had eaten an excellent dinner and had half a roast
chicken waiting in his cabin.

When he reached the words, "If I could only get
one square meal, sir," he looked directly at me.

The passengers burst into laughter.

I bowed.

He bowed lower.

And when the collection was counted, "The Stowaway" had brought
in more money than all the songs, battles, and conjuring
tricks together. Thus Cupples—or Vale—at last paid for his passage,
though not precisely in the manner he had first described.

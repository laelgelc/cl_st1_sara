### THE CIVILIZED FACE

In after years, when Lloyd Merriwether had become stout, prosperous,
and sufficiently bald to regard a hairbrush as a sentimental
rather than a practical object, he often told the story
of the house on the Ogowe. He told it best
after midnight, with three companions, a dwindling fire, and a
bottle within reach. He always began by insisting that he
was not imaginative, repeated this at intervals as the narrative
grew more fantastic, and concluded by asking whether any man
present had ever gone six weeks without a razor.

"No? Then you don't know anything about it," he would
say. "Pass the bottle."

This was Merriwether's account.

* * *

YOU fellows think civilization means constitutions, steam engines, public schools,
and women in picture hats. It doesn't. Civilization is a
clean collar. It is a man who shaves you every
morning and talks baseball while he does it. It is
talcum powder, a porcelain tub, a newspaper folded beside your
breakfast plate, and a policeman on the corner whom you
can dislike without being afraid of him.

I learned that on the Ogowe.

And don't grin, Parker. You were never alone there. You've
been to Dakar in a mail steamer and bought a
carved elephant from a boy at the landing. That isn't
Africa. Africa begins when the last white roof sinks behind
you, when the launch leaves you at a trading post
smelling of palm oil and mildew, and when the river
ahead narrows between two green walls that look as if
they had been waiting ten thousand years to close.

I was buying rubber then—rubber, ivory when it came my
way, and anything else from which our excellent civilization could
squeeze six per cent. I had been inland seven months.
Hard months? Not particularly. I was twenty-nine, as strong as
a derrick, and vain enough to suppose fever had made
a private arrangement to leave me alone. I had eight
porters, a cook called M'Bala, two canoes, a tent, rifles,
quinine, trade cloth, and my toilet case.

Remember the toilet case.

Pigskin, brass corners, silver-topped bottles, two razors, scissors, combs, brushes,
soap, tooth powder, nail file, bay rum, and a little
round mirror. My sister had given it to me before
I sailed. At the time I thought it foolish truck—pretty
enough for the cabin, but absurd in the bush. Later
I would have traded every bale of cloth I owned
for the worst cracked shaving glass in a Bowery lodging
house.

We had reached a village whose name I never could
pronounce without insulting somebody. Three huts, a goat, six million
insects, and a headman who considered punctuality a vice imported
by Europeans. I had been down with fever and was
ugly-tempered. M'Bala informed me that the porters would go no
farther. I informed him that they would. There was an
argument. I made threats, the headman made speeches, the goat
ate part of my ledger, and eventually everybody went to
sleep.

At dawn they had gone.

Gone—men, cloth, food, blankets, tent, the second rifle, and the
larger canoe. They left me the smaller dugout because they
could not carry it, three tins of beef because they
had overlooked them, and my trousers because I happened to
be wearing them.

They took the toilet case.

That offended me more than the rest. You may call
it absurd. It was absurd. A man can forgive robbery
on a grand scale; it has dignity. But the idea
of M'Bala, who had never voluntarily approached soap, carrying off
my shaving brush—well, it got my goat.

I had one rifle, twenty cartridges, a water bottle, quinine
in my pocket, two paddles, a canvas bag, and enough
conceit to stock a parliament. The Ogowe was below me.
The coast was somewhere downriver. All I had to do
was float toward it.

As easy as pie.

For the first two days I rather enjoyed myself. The
current did the work. I sat in the stern, steered
around snags, ate beef from the tin with my fingers,
and composed the speech I intended to deliver when I
found the thieves. It was a fine speech. There were
fines in it, prison terms, floggings, and several references to
British justice, though I was an American and the district
was French.

The river was broad there, coppery under the sun, with
mangroves on either bank. They sat back from the water
in tangled ranks, their roots lifted like the legs of
enormous, indecent spiders. Parrots flashed overhead—red, green, blue—colours so violent
they seemed to scream even when the birds were silent.
Great flowers hung from the vines and exhaled a lascivious
sweetness, half perfume and half corruption. Everything grew upon everything
else. Leaves on trunks, moss on leaves, orchids on moss,
insects on orchids. An entangled, exuberant commingling of appetites.

That sounds literary. I didn't think it then. I thought:
Hot. Stinking. Wet.

And I thought of my razor.

By the third morning my chin was grey-black. I rubbed
it and remembered a barbershop in Boston—white tiles, nickel fittings,
three chairs in a row. A little man named Gus
used to shave me. He had a wart beside his
nose and believed the Red Sox were personally persecuting him.
I had always considered Gus one of life's minor afflictions.
Now I would have crossed that river on my knees
to hear him say, "Close around the chin, Mr. Merriwether?"

Funny what comes back to you.

Not home in any noble sense. Not mother at the
gate, not the old church spire. Advertisements! I saw an
advertisement for shaving soap as plainly as I see you,
printed on a fence beside a railway: a pink-cheeked gentleman
smiling at his own lather. I hated him. Then I
loved him. Then I promised God that if I reached
the coast I would purchase every cake of that soap
in Gabon.

The heat thickened. It was no longer weather; it was
a substance. You breathed it, swallowed it, wore it under
your clothes. At noon the sky became a white metal
plate, and the river threw its glare upward in adamantine
dust. At night rain came—not drops, but whole lakes falling
at once—and I crouched under the overturned canoe while the
forest hissed, gurgled, and whispered around me.

Three days. Four. Five—

The beef went bad in the opened tins. I shot
at a bird and missed. I found berries and decided
they were probably poisonous, ate them, and was disappointed when
they weren't. Mosquitoes worked me in shifts. There were red
ants, black ants, ants with jaws like wire cutters, and
small pale ants that appeared to have no purpose except
to enter a man's ears and discuss him.

I kept taking quinine, but fever came anyway.

You know fever? Not a high temperature in a clean
bed, with a nurse shaking down a thermometer. Bush fever.
It arrives with a little chill in the spine, modest
as a bill collector. Then your bones begin to ring.
The air turns yellow. Your skin belongs to somebody else,
somebody who has been boiled. Thoughts come loose from their
fastenings and drift about independently.

I knew I was ill. I was perfectly rational about
it.

That is the dangerous stage—when you know you are rational.

I began talking aloud. There was no harm in that.
A man alone must hear a voice or forget that
voices exist. I discussed navigation with the paddle, complained to
the rifle, and gave the empty beef tins names. One
tin was Gus, the barber. Another was M'Bala. I told
M'Bala what I thought of him and then threw him
into the river. Gus I kept.

The country watched me.

You'll say a country can't watch. Quite right. I would
have said the same before. But those banks had eyes—not
actual eyes; don't be idiotic. Openings among leaves, knots in
bark, black hollows under roots. The river curved always just
beyond sight, pretending innocence, while the forest leaned over it
and waited. When I looked directly, nothing moved. When I
turned away, the whole green mass seemed to creep nearer.

At dusk the noises began. Croaks, whines, coughs, a booming
like drums underground. Once something laughed from the trees. It
laughed exactly as old Harkness laughs when somebody mentions honesty
in the ivory trade. I shouted at it. It laughed
again.

Then silence.

That was worse.

The absolute silence of a jungle is not an absence
of sound; it is millions of living things holding their
breath. You feel them listening. A twig drops and your
heart knocks against your ribs. The river makes a little
suck among the reeds—slop, slop—and you are certain something enormous
is crawling out behind you.

Loneliness! We use the word too easily. A man in
a hotel room says he is lonely because nobody has
invited him to supper. That isn't loneliness. Loneliness is the
conviction that mankind has ended and neglected to notify you.
It is reaching for a face in your memory and
finding only a blur. It is wondering whether English is
a real language or something you invented to pass the
time.

I began to fear I would forget my own face.

There was no mirror, you see.

I tried to remember it. Straight nose, brown eyes, heavy
jaw, moustache clipped narrow. An honest, ordinary American face, suitable
for bank counters and railway compartments. I traced it with
my fingers, but the fingers reported something else—bristles, hollows, swollen
lips, skin roughened by bites. I leaned over the water.
The current broke the reflection into bronze fragments.

Once I saw a face there.

Long, black-bearded, with burning eyes.

I struck at it with the paddle and nearly capsized.

After that I didn't look into the river.

Somewhere below N'Djolé—I learned this later—the main channel divided around
a chain of islands. I took the wrong branch. At
the time I thought the river had narrowed because it
was plotting against me. The current slackened. Reeds closed behind
the canoe. Branches dragged across my shoulders with a confidential
touch. Vines looped down like ropes from a gallows, and
the water became black, oily, motionless.

I should have turned back.

Instead I went on, because I believed the stream was
a shortcut. Fever arithmetic. If a broad river takes ten
days, a narrow river must take five. You see? Perfectly
logical—if you are mad.

I paddled for hours, or days. Time had become sticky.
The channel forked and forked again. Every branch looked like
every other: black water, white roots, green walls. I marked
a tree with my knife and passed the same mark
three times. The third time it was higher on the
trunk.

Don't ask me how. Fever.

I laughed then. Couldn't stop. The idea of the tree
moving the mark struck me as the funniest thing in
the world. I told it not to cheat. I threatened
to report it to the French administration. Then the laughter
broke off, and I was crying—quietly, foolishly, with my face
in my hands.

Home. That word began beating in my skull.

Home was a paved street after rain. Home was a
trolley bell. Home was roast beef, badly cooked by someone
else. Home was a man in a derby hat saying
something dull. Home was the smell of cigar smoke in
a saloon, the slap of cards, the scrape of chairs,
the ridiculous safety of other people's elbows.

Home. Home. Home.

I pushed through a curtain of leaves—and there it stood.

A house!

Not a hut. Not a mission shed. A European house,
two stories, plaster walls, green shutters, a veranda running across
the front. There were even ornamental posts under the eaves,
the kind a contractor adds to a suburban villa when
he intends to charge two hundred dollars extra.

I sat in the canoe staring at it.

It stood on a low rise fifty yards from the
stream, half strangled by creepers. The roof sagged in one
corner. Fungus streaked the plaster. A tree had thrust one
muscular root beneath the veranda and lifted the boards as
casually as a strong man raises a lid. Yet it
was a house. Human angles amid that vegetable insanity. Windows.
Doors. Rooms.

Civilization!

I shouted. No answer. I shouted in English and French,
and then in the little trade dialect I knew. A
bird flew out of an upstairs shutter. That was all.

I dragged the canoe ashore and stumbled up the path.
There had once been a garden. You could see borders
beneath the weeds and the stump of a stone fountain.
A rusted hoop lay in the grass, a child's toy
perhaps. That frightened me more than bones would have done.

The front door was unlocked.

Inside, the air was cooler, mouldy and still. I entered
a hall papered with faded red stripes. There was a
staircase, a hat stand, a cracked umbrella pot. A photograph
hung crookedly on the wall: a woman in white, a
bearded man, two children stiff as broomsticks. Their faces had
been eaten away by damp.

I called again.

Something moved upstairs.

A footstep. Slow. Deliberate.

I raised the rifle.

"Hello?"

Another step.

Then a faint scraping, as if a hand brushed plaster.

Now, a sane man would have left. But I had
been alone so long that danger seemed almost sociable. A
murderer, a lunatic, a ghost—any of them had the advantage
of being somebody.

I climbed.

The stair groaned beneath me. At the top was a
corridor with four doors. Sunlight entered through a broken shutter
in thin, green bars. Dust lay thick on the floor.

There were tracks in it.

Bare footprints.

I knelt. The marks were confused, overlapping, but fresh—somebody had
passed recently. Somebody with large feet.

You understand, I had no boots by then. They had
rotted, and I had thrown them away two days before.

I did not think of that.

"Come out!" I shouted.

A door slammed at the far end.

I ran to it, kicked it open, and found an
empty bedroom. Iron bedstead, mosquito net collapsed like a funeral
veil, wardrobe with one door hanging loose. The window stood
open. No one could have climbed out; the creepers below
were unbroken.

Behind me, in the corridor, floorboards creaked.

I spun around. Nothing.

Then laughter.

Not loud. A dry little chuckle from downstairs.

Hatred came over me—sudden, pure, sustaining hatred. Fear weakens a
man, but hatred puts iron into him. I knew this
creature had been watching me from the river. It had
laughed in the trees. It had led me into the
tributary and brought me to the house. Why? To play
with me. To see how long Lloyd Merriwether, independent trader,
practical man, could be made to dance.

"All right," I said. "You've had your fun."

I went down.

The drawing room contained a sofa split open by damp,
two wicker chairs, and a piano. Imagine it—a piano in
that place! The ivory keys were yellow, and orchids had
grown through a crack in the wall above it. On
the music stand lay a swollen book of hymns.

A note sounded.

Just one.

I nearly fired into the piano. Then I saw a
green lizard dart from the keys.

Ludicrous? Of course. I laughed. A splendid laugh—high, cracking, not
quite mine.

Across the room a tall glass stood covered by a
brown cloth. I thought it was a cupboard. Remember that.

There were tins in the pantry, all rusted. A bottle
held an inch of something amber. I drank it. Furniture
polish, perhaps. At that stage it had competition.

On the kitchen table I found a razor.

A razor! Rusty, but unmistakable.

I seized it, searched for soap, found a grey cake
by the sink, and carried both to a basin. The
pump was dry. No water. I spat on the soap
and rubbed it between my palms, trying to make lather.
The razor pulled more hair than it cut. I slashed
my cheek and watched blood drip into the basin.

Then someone breathed behind me.

I whirled and fired.

The bullet went through the pantry door. Pots crashed. The
house echoed—bang, bang, bang—throwing the shot from room to room
until it sounded like a dozen armed men closing in.

When silence returned, I heard breathing again.

My own? I considered that.

No. Too harsh. Too close.

I searched every room on the lower floor. Nothing. I
searched upstairs again. Nothing. Yet wherever I went, he was
just ahead. A shadow crossed doorways. A hand withdrew behind
a curtain. Once I saw a gaunt shoulder vanish around
the turn of the stairs. Once, from the corner of
my eye, I saw a naked, hairy creature crouched beside
me.

Always beside me.

Never in front.

That should have told me. But fever does not reason;
it manufactures reasons.

Night came.

I barricaded myself in the drawing room, wedging the sofa
against the door. The green dusk thickened beyond the shutters.
Things stirred in the walls. Rain began on the roof
with a thousand tapping fingers.

I sat facing the covered glass and kept the rifle
across my knees.

He moved behind the walls. I heard him upstairs, then
in the hall, then under the floor. Sometimes he muttered.
Sometimes he repeated my words in a low voice.

"Come out," I said.

"Come out," he whispered.

"Coward."

"Coward."

I fired toward the sound. Plaster fell. The whisper ceased.

Minutes passed—or hours.

Then I began remembering the photograph in the hall. The
man, the woman, the children. I invented their story. He
had been a doctor, I decided. Or an engineer. He
had built the house for his wife, planted the garden,
ordered the piano from Libreville. Fever came. One child died,
then the other. The wife went mad. The man buried
them beside the fountain and remained alone until loneliness ate
his face.

That was who hunted me.

No—that was melodrama, and I knew it even then. I
told myself so.

But knowing a thing is melodrama doesn't prevent it from
being true at midnight in the French Congo.

The cloth over the glass stirred.

There was no wind.

I watched it. It settled. Then stirred again.

He was behind it.

I raised the rifle.

The cloth slid sideways, an inch at first, then farther,
as though a hand beneath it were drawing it back.

A face appeared.

I saw one eye, bright and savage; a strip of
black beard; a cheek marked by a fresh scar. The
creature was tall but bent, almost naked, smeared with mud
and blood. Its hair hung in ropes. Mosquito bites swelled
its forehead. One hand clutched a rifle.

My rifle.

That was the final proof. He had mocked me by
becoming me—or he had stolen my weapon's shape. Don't ask
which I believed. Both. Neither. I only knew the thing
behind the cloth had followed me from the river, slept
when I slept, whispered when I spoke, and now meant
to step out and take my place.

I jerked the cloth away.

He stood there at full height.

Gaunt. Scarred. Armed.

His mouth opened when mine opened. His rifle came up.

I fired first.

The world exploded into silver.

Glass flew across the room in glittering sheets. The enemy
shattered into a hundred staring fragments—eye, teeth, beard, blood, eye
again—each fragment catching a different sliver of moonlight, each still
alive, each still looking at me.

A mirror.

Yes.

A long French mirror in a carved frame.

I did not understand immediately. That is the worst of
it. I saw no revelation, no neat answer. I saw
a man burst into pieces and believed every piece had
become another man. I fired again and again until the
magazine was empty. Then I clubbed the fragments with the
rifle butt, stamping, screaming, cutting my feet, while a hundred
Merriwethers screamed up at me from the floor.

Myself.

Myself!

The enemy I had heard breathing, the shadow at the
doors, the hairy creature beside me—it was myself. The footprints
were mine. The voice was mine. The face in the
river, mine. But I had not seen that face for
weeks. I had forgotten it. Fever, beard, bites, blood, hollow
cheeks—strip away a man's razor and sleep and other men's
eyes, and how long before he becomes a stranger?

How long before the stranger becomes an enemy?

I remember crawling beneath the piano. I remember hugging the
rifle and bargaining with the broken glass. I promised not
to shoot if it would stop looking at me. I
promised to shave. I promised to go home. I promised
to become kind, honest, sober, and uncommercial—proof enough that I
was out of my head.

After that, nothing reliable.

What I know came from Morrison.

Dr. Alec Morrison, a missionary physician, had heard that my
porters deserted me and had started upriver with two men.
They found the abandoned canoe, followed my tracks, and reached
the house on the fourth day.

The house had belonged to a French surveyor. Yellow fever
took his family. He left. Nobody had lived there in
nine years.

Morrison entered at noon. He found the walls bullet-marked, the
drawing room ankle-deep in mirror glass, and me hiding on
top of the wardrobe in the upstairs bedroom.

I attacked him.

Bit him, as a matter of fact. There's gratitude for
you. He still has the scar on his thumb and
shows it whenever my name comes up.

It took all three of them to tie me. They
carried me to the river in a blanket. For two
weeks at the mission I alternated between coma and violent
attempts to murder the washstand. Apparently it had a small
mirror. Morrison removed it.

When I finally woke rational, there was a clean sheet
under me and rain on a tin roof. A woman
was singing somewhere outside—badly, but humanly. On the table stood
a bowl of broth, a bottle of quinine, and a
safety razor.

I cried when I saw the razor.

Not gracefully. No manly tear rolling down one cheek. I
bawled like a child and held the thing against my
chest. Morrison thought it was fever. I never corrected him.

He shaved me that evening. His hand was bandaged where
I had bitten it, and he made a poor job
around the jaw. Best shave I ever had.

So laugh if you like. A toilet case! A mirror!
A practical man driven mad because he couldn't trim his
moustache! That is the comic version, and it is true
enough.

But here is the other version.

We carry civilization on the skin. A little soap, a
little steel, a little polished glass to assure us each
morning that the face looking back is the face we
wore yesterday. Take away the streets and bells, the clean
linen, the bored barber, the fellow across the table who
knows your name—take them away one by one—and the wilderness
does not have to enter you.

It finds what was there already.

That house did not contain a ghost. The river did
not hate me. The forest did not laugh. I supplied
the hatred, the laughter, the stalking thing. I carried my
enemy down the Ogowe, fed him quinine and rotten beef,
laid him each night beneath my canoe—and when at last
civilization offered me a glass, I shot him through the
heart.

Or tried to.

There's the joke: you can't kill him that way.

Pass the bottle, Parker. And if you're going upstairs, cover
that damned mirror by the door.

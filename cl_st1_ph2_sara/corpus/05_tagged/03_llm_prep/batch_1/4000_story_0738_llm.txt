# SIX DAYS UNTIL MORNING

YOU NEVER schedule catastrophe. You schedule breakfast, departure, arrival, a
bath under clean blue lamps. You schedule cards with the
boys at Port Fenris. You schedule breathing because breathing has
always kept its appointments.

Then a warning bulb winks amber.

Then red.

Then every light on the board shouts at once, and
the ship turns from a machine into a falling room.

Leonard Sale remembered the mountain rushing up. He remembered his
hands, white and quick upon the controls. He remembered saying,
very politely, "Not today."

The darkness.

Inside the darkness, the crash.

Inside the crash, a sound like the universe slamming a
door.

When he awoke, sunlight lay across his face.

He tasted blood and upholstery. Something hissed nearby with the
patience of a snake. The cabin had folded around him,
metal pleated and split, but his couch had torn loose
and cushioned him in insulation foam.

Sale moved one finger.

Then another.

His legs objected, but they moved.

"Remarkable," he whispered. "Somebody up there has mislaid his arithmetic."

The radio was cracked but alive. The food locker had
burst open but the packets were intact. The oxygen plant
still ticked. Outside, the planetoid was an arrangement of black
stone, yellow sand, and low greenish hills under a pearl-colored
sky. There were no beasts, no cities, no poison clouds.
The air tested thin but breathable.

Nothing moved except heat.

Within three hours he had patched the transmitter. Within four,
a voice came swimming across space.

"Survey vessel Vesta to scout Seven-Seven. We have your position."

Sale leaned against the panel and grinned.

"When?"

"Six standard days. Can you hold?"

"I can hold a parade."

"Medical status?"

"Bruised, vain, thirsty."

"Repeat."

"Bruised. No fractures. Supplies adequate."

"Maintain beacon. Vesta out."

Six days.

He made coffee.

He sat in the sunlight beside the wreck, boots propped
on a warm rock, and watched steam rise from the
metal cup. The ship rested behind him like some gigantic
bird killed in flight, one wing buried, the other pointing
accusingly at the sky.

Sale drank.

"Six days," he told the hills. "A vacation."

The hills did not disagree.

That first afternoon he inventoried supplies, erected the emergency shelter,
and laid out a schedule. Breakfast at seven. Beacon inspection
at eight. Repairs until eleven. Lunch. Exercise. Reading. Dinner. Sleep
at twenty-two hundred.

A man with a schedule owned the hours.

At twenty-two hundred, Leonard Sale entered the shelter, zipped the
flap, and lay down.

The ground was hard. The air smelled of dust, heated
cloth, and the faint medicinal odor of the oxygen unit.
He tucked an arm beneath his head.

His muscles loosened.

The day floated upward and away.

Then, very far off, someone began to sing.

A thin sound.

Eeeeeeeeeeeee—

Sale opened his eyes.

Silence.

He sat up and listened. The oxygen unit made its
small mechanical cough. Wind sifted grains of sand against the
shelter.

He lay down again.

Perhaps a valve. Perhaps the radio carrier. Perhaps his own
ears, stunned by impact.

His eyelids lowered.

The sound returned.

Eeeeeeeeeeeee—

Not in the air.

In him.

The note entered through no door. It rose from the
dark behind his eyes, sharpened, divided, became ten voices, a
hundred, ten thousand. Beneath them came drums. Boom. Boom. Boom.
Each beat struck not the ear but the spine.

A plain spread open in his mind.

It was green.

Green sky, green dust, green towers burning under green suns.
Across the plain ran men in bronze masks, lifting hooked
blades. Chariots flashed. Animals with six black legs plunged screaming
through smoke. Banners rose, bearing a white hand crushed around
a star.

Sale tried to wake.

He could not find himself.

The armies rushed across the soft interior country of his
skull, boots pounding over pale ridges of memory, wheels cutting
tracks through childhood, schooldays, old kisses, trivial Tuesdays.

At the center of them stood a figure in scaled
armor.

"I am Iorr," it cried. "Lord of the Western Flame,
Breaker of Gates, Eater of Kings!"

The whole multitude answered:

"IORR! IORR! IORR!"

Another sound came from beyond a river the color of
bruises.

Mmmmmmmmmmm—

The river boiled. Out of it marched soldiers in silver
cloth, carrying long tubes that spouted blue fire. Their helmets
were smooth and faceless. At their head walked a tall
woman—or a thin man, or something that had forgotten the
difference—crowned with knives.

"I am Tylle," said the figure. "Keeper of the Final
Law, Mother of Spears, Extinguisher of Worlds!"

"TYLLE! TYLLE! TYLLE!"

The two armies saw one another.

The green plain trembled.

Charge, said Iorr.

Destroy, said Tylle.

"No," Sale said.

The armies moved.

"No!"

He tore upward through layers of sleep. The green sky
shattered. The soldiers stretched into threads, the drums became his
heart, the fire became sunlight—

Sale flung himself upright.

Morning filled the shelter.

His fingernails had cut his palms. His shirt was wet.
The cup beside his bed had been kicked flat.

For several seconds he did not know where he was.

Then the planetoid returned: the wreck, the beacon, the six-day
rescue.

He crawled outside and vomited.

The pearl sky remained mild. The hills sat warm and
innocent in the sun.

"Concussion," he said.

That was the sensible answer.

He examined his pupils in a mirror. Equal. He tested
memory, coordination, speech. Leonard Michael Sale. Thirty-four years old. Scout
pilot. Born in Indiana District. Mother's name Ruth. Five times
seven, thirty-five. The opening line of Hamlet. The batting average
of Del Ortez in '89.

All present.

He contacted Vesta.

"Any medical personnel handy?"

A woman's voice answered. "Doctor Gennaro here. Symptoms?"

He described the dream, though not the names. The names
embarrassed him.

"Post-traumatic nightmare," Gennaro said. "Common after impact. Any persistent visual
disturbance?"

"No."

"Headache?"

"Only when I think."

"Stay hydrated. Use the mild sedative in kit three if
needed."

Sale looked at the shelter.

"No sedative."

"Why?"

"I dislike dreams."

A pause.

"Four days, twenty-one hours to pickup," the doctor said. "Call
if symptoms worsen."

Sale drank three cups of coffee and laughed at himself.

Iorr, Eater of Kings.

Tylle, Extinguisher of Worlds.

"Overqualified for dream work," he said.

The second time came after lunch.

He was sitting outside with a book open on his
knees, reading a comic novel about a dishonest bishop. Sunlight
warmed his shoulders. A breeze wandered down from the hills
and touched his eyelids.

Just a moment, he thought.

Not sleep. Rest.

The print blurred.

And instantly the doors opened.

Aaaaaaaaaaaaah—

A trumpet shrieked through him.

The battlefield had changed. Now it was a red city
built along the curving wall of his skull. Towers clung
to bone. Bridges crossed dark channels where old sorrow went
flowing. Iorr's troops crawled from the tunnels of his hearing,
beating shields with axes.

Mine, mine, mine, mine!

From the opposite quarter came Tylle's legions, their silver feet
splashing through the deep reservoirs of instinct.

Ours, ours, ours!

Sale was the city.

They knew it.

Iorr lifted his burning sword. "The vessel is sound."

Tylle answered from a balcony made of memory. "The vessel
is temporary."

"He is mine."

"He is passage."

"He is kingdom."

"He is weapon."

Sale tried to shut them out, but how do you
bar a door when the invader is already the room?
He felt soldiers hacking footholds in thought. He felt engineers
laying bridges across his fear. He felt priests planting flags
in the tender ground where his name stood.

LEONARD SALE, said one flag.

The letters were torn down.

IORR, said another.

TYLLE.

IORR.

TYLLE.

He awoke standing twenty meters from camp.

The book lay in the sand behind him. His hands
were extended before him as if gripping an invisible throat.

The sun had shifted only slightly.

He returned to the wreck and searched the ship's memory
banks.

The planetoid had no official name, only a survey number.
Diameter: one hundred eighty kilometers. Atmosphere: residual. Water: trace. Life:
absent. No mineral value. No previous landing recorded.

Absent.

Sale stared at the word.

No living thing.

That was not the same as empty.

He ran a geological scan, a radiation scan, an atmospheric
scan. Nothing. He walked in widening circles around the wreck
with a hand detector. Nothing. He climbed the nearest hill.

Beyond it lay more hills, black stone, yellow sand. Here
and there the wind had exposed flat surfaces too regular
to be natural. Pavements, perhaps. Foundations. One long stair descending
into solid earth.

He did not go down.

At the top step he found two carvings.

A hand crushing a star.

A crown of knives.

The wind moved past his ears.

Eeeeeeeeeeeee—

Sale ran.

He reached the shelter breathless, tore open the medical kit,
and counted stimulant tablets.

Thirty.

The rescue ship would arrive in a little more than
four days.

Ninety-eight hours.

He needed no calculation to understand the arithmetic. A man
could remain awake ninety-eight hours. Men had done worse. Soldiers,
surgeons, gamblers, fools in love. He would organize it. He
would be scientific.

No sleep.

That was all.

At eighteen hundred he ate soup and spoke aloud while
eating. At nineteen hundred he repaired the beacon again though
it did not need repair. At twenty hundred he exercised.
At twenty-one hundred he washed every utensil twice. At twenty-two
hundred he began War and Peace.

By midnight he had conquered eighty pages and disliked everyone.

At two he made coffee.

At three he played cards against himself.

"Your bet, Sale."

"Five."

"You're bluffing."

"I know your face."

"You are my face."

"Then stop smirking."

At four the stars seemed very close, bright nails driven
through black cloth. The planetoid turned slowly beneath them. Sale
walked around the wreck singing old advertisements.

At dawn he swallowed one tablet.

Day Three passed in lists.

Coffee.

Beacon.

Push-ups.

Tolstoy.

Coffee.

Cards.

Balzac.

Soup.

Radio check.

Coffee.

The hills watched him conduct his little civilization. He explained
each action to them.

"This is shaving."

"This is washing a sock."

"This is the human race refusing to become a haunted
house."

At sunset he heard Tylle whisper from the stair beyond
the ridge.

Sleep.

He put his fingers in his ears.

Sleep, Leonard.

"No."

Only a minute.

"No."

We can wait.

That was Iorr. Deep, amused, full of rust and triumph.

We have waited longer than your species has possessed words.

Sale shouted toward the hills, "Then another four days won't
inconvenience you!"

The echo brought it back:

—convenience you.

—venience you.

—you.

The voices laughed without sound. Their laughter was a pressure
in his teeth.

On Day Four he began to see movement at the
edge of things.

A silver helmet behind the food locker.

A bronze hand withdrawing beneath a stone.

When he turned, there was nothing. When he looked away,
armies gathered.

He read aloud faster.

"'Well, my prince, so Genoa and Lucca are now no
more than estates—'"

War and Peace. A fine choice. A book full of
troops.

"Shut up."

All books are doors, said Tylle.

All memory is land, said Iorr.

Sale hurled the volume across the shelter.

"Not my land."

Your eyes are weak.

Your heart stumbles.

Your walls are meat.

"I've got news for you. Meat built starships."

Meat sleeps.

Meat dreams.

Meat opens.

He took another tablet.

The chemical lifted him with hot hands. For an hour
he felt brilliant. He repaired a torn cable, composed a
speech for his rescuers, and invented seven jokes about disembodied
emperors.

By noon the brilliance curdled.

His tongue felt furred. Light acquired sharp edges. Every rock
cast two shadows. His pulse ran ahead of him like
a frightened child.

He radioed Vesta.

"Status check."

"Three days," came the answer. "You sound strained."

"Celebrating."

"Celebrating what?"

"Wakefulness."

"Sale, have you slept?"

"Sleep is temporarily canceled."

Doctor Gennaro came on. "How many stimulants have you taken?"

"Two."

"Over what interval?"

"Plenty."

"Answer."

"Thirty-one hours."

"You cannot continue that way. Take the sedative and rest."

Sale laughed.

It came out as a bark.

"Doctor, if I sleep, two prehistoric lunatics will turn my
brain into a parade ground."

Silence hummed between the stars.

"Sale," she said carefully, "you are suffering from trauma and
sleep deprivation."

"Yes. And company."

"Hallucinations become self-reinforcing. The more you resist sleep, the more
vivid they'll become."

"They left carvings."

"Who did?"

"Iorr and Tylle."

"Those names mean nothing to me."

"They mean entirely too much to me."

He could hear someone behind her speaking in a low
voice.

Then Gennaro said, "We are increasing speed. Earliest arrival is
fifty-eight hours. Until then, stop the stimulants. Restrain yourself if
you fear self-harm. Sleep."

"Certainly," Sale said.

He switched off the radio.

Fifty-eight hours.

He wrote the number on the shelter wall.

58.

Below it he wrote:

NO SLEEP.

Below that:

I AM LEONARD SALE.

The letters looked childish.

At sixteen hundred he caught himself standing before them, unable
to remember why he had written his name.

He read it aloud.

"Leonard Sale."

The censor in his mind replied, Says who?

"My parents."

Unreliable witnesses.

"My license."

Paper burns.

"My memories."

Occupied territory.

"Myself."

Which one?

He slapped his face until blood warmed his lip.

That evening was bad.

The sky darkened from pearl to violet. Cold came out
of the stones. The wind prowled around the wreck and
found a dozen flutes in its broken hull.

Oooooooo.

Eeeeeeeee.

Aaaaaaaa.

Every note became a battalion.

Sale walked. He counted steps to the ridge and back.
Eight hundred twelve. Eight hundred eleven. Eight hundred fourteen. The
ground shifted beneath him, breathing.

He made coffee and forgot to drink it.

He dealt cards and found the hand-crushing-star symbol among the
suits.

He blinked.

It was the king of clubs.

"Cheap," he told Iorr. "Very cheap."

The king winked.

Near dawn he sat down only to retie his boot.

His head dipped.

One second.

The universe burst.

Iorr's army crashed through the northern hemisphere of his brain,
all bronze shrieks and flaming wheels. Tylle's host poured upward
from the spinal dark, silver ranks multiplying, blue weapons opening
wounds in the sky.

Sale was no longer a man.

He was acreage.

He was weather.

He was a continent stretched beneath their feet, his nerves
white roads, his veins red canals, his memories villages burning
one by one.

A cannon fired near the childhood orchard where his mother
still hung washing.

Boom!

Trees exploded into birds.

Tylle raised both knife-thin arms. "Take the mouth!"

Silver troops surged toward his speech.

Iorr roared, "Take the hands!"

Bronze soldiers descended the red roads of his nerves.

Sale felt his right hand move outside the dream.

It crawled along the ground.

Found leather.

Opened a holster.

No, he thought.

The armies cheered.

His fingers closed around the pistol.

Tylle seized his thumb.

Iorr seized his first finger.

The weapon rose.

In the battlefield the two leaders faced one another upon
a bridge of bone.

"Fire!" screamed Iorr.

"Fire!" sang Tylle.

The pistol touched Sale's temple.

For one impossible instant both nations became quiet.

Then his finger tightened.

The shot was a white hammer.

Sale woke on his back beneath the paling sky.

The pistol lay beside him.

His left ear rang. Blood ran hot from a furrow
above his eyebrow, passed along his cheek, and tapped the
sand.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

He touched the wound and stared at his red fingers.

The bullet had grazed him. One centimeter lower and the
rescue crew would have found a body occupied by no
one, or by everyone.

He began to laugh.

"Bad marksmanship," he said.

The hills smiled greenly.

"After all those wars."

He laughed harder, then stopped and crawled for the pistol.

He unloaded it. He threw the cartridges in six directions.
He carried the gun to the ridge and smashed it
against stone until the grip broke, the barrel bent, and
the mechanisms spilled like little black teeth.

Then he stood over the pieces, panting.

Inside him Iorr said, There are other weapons.

Tylle said, There are always hands.

Sale looked down at his hands.

For the first time in his life, they seemed borrowed.

Day Five began with blood, no breakfast, and a voice
from the radio.

"Leonard, answer."

He sat beside the transmitter.

"Leonard Sale, respond."

Doctor Gennaro. Calm, professional, alive.

He pressed the switch.

"Present."

"We received an emergency discharge signal from your sidearm."

"Accident."

"Are you injured?"

"Only cosmetically."

"Describe it."

He did.

"We are thirty-nine hours out," she said. "I want you
to secure every weapon, blade, cable, and toxic substance."

"What about rocks?"

"Leonard—"

"There are a great many rocks."

"Listen to me. You need to keep speaking. We can
maintain intermittent contact."

"Intermittent isn't enough."

"It is what our range permits."

"What happens if I'm not myself when you arrive?"

Another pause.

"We'll manage."

"No. If I answer using names that sound like spoiled
cough medicine, don't open the hatch."

"We'll manage," she repeated.

He lowered his forehead to the panel.

The metal was cool.

"Doctor?"

"Yes?"

"Tell me something dull."

So she told him about the Vesta's water-recycling repairs.

For eleven minutes Leonard Sale listened to valves, filters, pressure
loss, mineral residue. It was beautiful. It was civilization itself.
Pipes and regulations. Human beings annoyed by plumbing. He wept
silently, careful not to let the tears touch the controls.

Then the signal faded.

The silence afterward was enormous.

He ate stimulant tablets dry.

One.

Two.

The doctor had forbidden them. The doctor had not seen
Iorr riding a black animal across the folds of her
own name.

The hours loosened.

Objects stopped agreeing to stay where he put them. The
coffee cup appeared on the radio, then under his cot,
then in his hand. The number on the wall changed.

39.

Then 93.

Then E 9.

The ink slid downward like insects.

He tried Shakespeare.

"A plague on both your armies," he informed the air.
"Exit everyone, pursued by common sense."

No one laughed.

He tried Balzac. The sentences gathered uniforms.

He tried cards. Every card bore his face.

He tried exercise. At the twentieth push-up the ground became
transparent, and beneath it millions of dead mouths opened in
the rock.

Mmmmmmmmmmmmm.

Not dead, they said.

Waiting.

He scrambled upright.

"What do you want?"

A body, said Iorr.

A world, said Tylle.

"You had a world."

He saw it then, not as dream but as remembrance
poured into him: two nations beneath green suns, each naming
itself eternal, each teaching children that the other nation had
invented evil. Towers rose. Songs were written. Weapons blossomed. Seas
boiled away. The final armies marched while the atmosphere burned.

Every body died.

But hatred, practiced for ten thousand years, had become a
structure stronger than flesh. The last thoughts of millions sank
into stone, preserving command, obedience, rage. No hands. No eyes.
No tongues.

Waiting for weather.

Waiting for a nervous system to fall from heaven.

Sale saw Iorr and Tylle in the final hour, each
launching the stroke that murdered both peoples.

"You're the same," he whispered.

The leaders recoiled.

Same?

"Two mouths yelling into one grave."

LIAR, roared Iorr.

BLASPHEMER, cried Tylle.

The armies struck him from within.

Pain bloomed behind his eyes. He staggered against the wreck.
His nose began to bleed.

"Same," he said again, because it hurt them. "Same uniforms,
different dye. Same speeches. Same stupidity with banners."

Iorr howled.

Tylle sang a note so sharp that Sale dropped to
his knees.

The world flashed green, red, white.

For several minutes—or hours—he was elsewhere.

When he returned, the sun was lowering.

Day Five was ending.

Night came on soft across the sand.

Sale climbed into the shelter and found he had arranged
the medical tablets in two armies. White pills on one
side. Red pills on the other. Between them stood the
coffee cup, upside down, a fortress.

He swept them away.

His hand paused over the red tablets.

Take all of them, suggested the censor.

"No."

Sleep forever.

"That isn't sleep."

Close enough.

No battlefield without a field.

He gathered the tablets in his palm.

They were very small. An easy door. Swallow. Drink. Lie
down. Let the heart forget its rhythm. Let the armies
inherit a country already freezing.

Iorr whispered, Coward.

Tylle whispered, Do it.

That decided him.

Sale scattered the pills into the wind.

"Deny you the satisfaction," he said.

But the censor chuckled. Noble. Heroic. Also, now you have
no tablets.

He watched the red and white specks vanish among the
stones.

"Oh," he said.

Darkness pooled in the hollows. The first stars appeared.

Thirty hours to rescue.

Perhaps less.

Perhaps a century.

Sale turned on every light the wreck could spare. He
set the radio to emit a shrill test tone. He
boiled coffee so black it seemed cut from night. He
tied one wrist to an overhead strut, not tightly enough
to sleep safely, only tightly enough that falling would jerk
him awake.

He stood beneath the light.

The light hummed.

His knees trembled.

His eyes burned like cracked glass.

"Leonard Sale," he said.

The hills replied in Iorr's voice.

Leonard Sale.

"Born Indiana District."

Tylle answered.

Born nowhere.

"Scout pilot."

Empty vehicle.

"Human being."

Temporary nation.

He slapped himself.

Once.

Twice.

Again.

The night pressed closer, vast and patient. Within his skull,
beyond the last bright fences of wakefulness, the dead armies
assembled. He heard armor fastened, weapons lifted, beasts saddled. He
heard Iorr rehearsing his titles. He heard Tylle blessing the
silver ranks.

Aaaaaaaaaaaa—

The sound rose like a moon inside him.

Sale drank coffee. Most of it spilled down his shirt.

The horizon tilted.

He straightened.

"Not yet."

His eyelids descended.

He snapped them open.

Across the shelter wall marched shadows with spears.

"Not yet!"

Twenty-nine hours.

The radio crackled.

Perhaps Vesta.

Perhaps static.

Perhaps ten million dead soldiers scratching at the thin door
of one living thought.

Leonard Sale leaned toward the sound, smiling with bloody lips.

"Come on, then," he whispered. "All of you. But you'll
have to fight me first."

The armies cheered.

The stars burned.

His eyes closed one fraction, opened, closed again.

And outside, over the harmless stones of the harmless world,
the long night arrived.

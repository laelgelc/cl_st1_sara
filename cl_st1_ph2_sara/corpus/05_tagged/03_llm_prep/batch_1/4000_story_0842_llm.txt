### Cavanelle's Little Cares

The first time I called Cavanelle an angel, I did
not know what I was saying.

He was then a clerk in a shop on Canal
Street—a long, glittering establishment where one might buy anything from
a mourning veil to a Japanese fan, and where the
clerks, by some commercial miracle, preserved their smiles through heat,
dust, bad tempers, and the daily handling of objects which
they could never afford to possess. Cavanelle presided over the
ribbons and laces. He was small, nervous, inexhaustible; his black
eyes snapped like beads in sunlight, and his thin hands
flew among the boxes with the grace of conjuring.

"Madame require' somesing distingue," he would say, laying before me
a strip of lace at twice the price I had
intended to pay. "Not too young—no! not too severe—jamais! Madame
possess' the complexion which permit' the delicacy."

He flattered without dishonesty. That is a rare distinction. He
did not invent one's beauty; he merely enlarged it, as
a good frame enlarges a modest picture.

Yet his own appearance was deplorable. His coat shone at
the elbows; one cuff had separated from its button and
wandered disconsolately over his wrist; his boots, though polished, had
been polished into submission after all hope of restoration was
gone. During a particularly cold December I saw him wearing
a gray muffler which looked as if it had been
knitted from kitchen string.

"Cavanelle," I said, "you will freeze."

He placed one hand upon his breast.

"Me? I am robust'! I never remark' the col'. Mathilde,
my sister—ah, she suffer. The throat, voyez vous, must be
protect'. For a voice, the throat is everysing."

Then I heard of Mathilde.

She was younger than he, delicate, gifted beyond common estimation,
and destined—according to Cavanelle—to astonish New Orleans, Paris, Milan, perhaps
the entire civilized world. He spoke of her voice as
other men speak of oil wells, racehorses, or revelation. Every
cent he could spare went to lessons, sheet music, nourishing
broths, throat lozenges, carriage fare on damp evenings, and the
white wine which her physician had recommended in a daily
demi-verre.

"She have the organ," he told me. "The professor say
it is necessary only to develop. Such volume! Such pathétique!
When she sing the prayer from *La Favorita*, madame, I
assure you the heart become liquid."

I looked at the gray muffler and the wandering cuff.

"You deny yourself everything for her."

He shrugged, delighted to be accused.

"But naturally! She require des petits soins."

There was something touching in this, and something, I confess,
which irritated my reason. I have always distrusted undiscovered genius
when it lives at another person's expense. New Orleans was
full of voices that would have conquered Europe had Europe
possessed the courtesy to come and hear them in their
mothers' parlors. Still, Cavanelle's ardor interested me. I began by
thinking him a humbug, passed briefly through the suspicion that
he was promoting a family speculation, and arrived—too rapidly—at angel.

It was I who proposed to hear Mathilde.

At once he became magnificent.

"Madame do us the honor! Sunday—at four hour. We make
no cérémonie. A little coffee, perhaps some sirop, and Mathilde
she sing—not as concert, you comprehend, but intime."

The following Sunday was bright and cold. I took the
streetcar down Rampart Street and descended where the cobblestones seemed
designed to test Christian patience. Cavanelle lived beyond the French
market, in a narrow yellow house crouching between a grocer's
and a courtyard full of washing. He received me at
the door in a coat which was too large for
him and had plainly belonged to a more prosperous relative.
A gardenia, costly and absurd, occupied his buttonhole.

"Madame! Our pauvre maison receive the sun!"

The pauvre maison consisted of three rooms arranged one behind
another. The front parlor contained a horsehair sofa, four unmatched
chairs, a marble-topped table with no marble top, and an
upright piano draped in red plush. Everything was scrubbed, mended,
polished, tied up, or covered over. Poverty had not been
expelled; it had been trained to behave.

Mathilde rose from the sofa.

My first thought was that a strong breeze might carry
her into the river. She was very tall and very
thin, with pale lashes and colorless lips. A blue ribbon
encircled her waist twice and still appeared loose. She wore
a white wool shawl, though the room was warm, and
held one narrow hand at her throat as if guarding
the treasure deposited there.

Cavanelle hovered about her.

"Mathilde, you remain too long stand'. Sit, ma chère. The
cushion—permit me. Madame will take coffee? Mathilde, not coffee for
you; the doctor forbid. A little sirop. No, the other
glass; the rim is more smooth."

These were his petits soins. He adjusted the shawl, moved
the lamp, shut an imaginary draft, opened the shutters, then
shut them again because the light fatigued her eyes. Mathilde
accepted his activities with the tranquil resignation of royalty. She
was not ungrateful; she was merely accustomed to being the
center around which Cavanelle revolved.

Refreshments arrived: coffee in cups thin as eggshells, two cakes
cut into six pieces, and a half-bottle of wine which
he uncorked ceremoniously but did not taste.

"For Mathilde," he explained. "To enrich the blood."

At last she approached the piano. Cavanelle sat forward, hands
on his knees, his whole person illuminated.

She began.

I had prepared myself for disappointment. I had not prepared
myself for pain.

The voice came out thin, sharp, and tremulous, like the
protest of a hinge in need of oil. In the
lower notes it disappeared entirely; in the upper it returned
with a vengeance. She pursued the melody without overtaking it,
and when she attempted a crescendo, the sound did not
increase so much as become frightened. A small dog in
the grocer's courtyard barked twice, paused to consider, and then
howled.

Cavanelle turned to me with tears in his eyes.

"You hear? That is the quality!"

I heard the quality.

Mathilde sang a second song, then a third. Her brother's
face followed each phrase with ecstasy, anxiety, triumph. He breathed
when she breathed; he lifted his chin toward the high
notes; he beat time with one shabby boot. When she
finished, he sprang up.

"Not fatigue yourself! It is enough. Madame comprehend. Madame have
relation with the Opera, perhaps—"

There it was. I had been invited not as an
amateur of music but as Providence in a bonnet.

I murmured that professional engagements were difficult, that training must
precede ambition, that health was the first consideration. These phrases,
which mean nothing and are therefore useful, did not discourage
him.

"The health improve each day. The professor say six month—perhaps
one year. Meantime we economize. I sen' her to Paris
if necessary."

Send her to Paris! On what? The gardenia in his
buttonhole? The daily cake divided into geometrical fragments? His boots,
persuaded by blacking to remain boots another season?

While he wrapped Mathilde's throat in a second shawl, my
pity turned—how readily pity does turn!—to impatience. He was not
an angel; he was a fool. Worse, he was a
fool manufacturing illusions for another fool. I imagined Mathilde arriving
in Paris, pale and ribboned, opening her mouth before some
brutal director, and being returned to New Orleans with such
speed that the Atlantic winds would finish what our local
breezes had neglected.

On the streetcar home I condemned them both. Mathilde was
vain; Cavanelle fatuous; the professor probably hungry. It was a
complete and satisfying judgment.

But that night I remembered Cavanelle's face while she sang.

He had not heard what I heard. That much was
obvious. Yet neither had I heard what he heard. To
me the notes had been notes—poor ones. To him they
were Mathilde's childhood, fragility, hope; all the promises he had
made beside a sickbed; all the soup carried upstairs, the
medicines measured, the coins counted after midnight. Her voice contained
his whole love for her, and love, as every practical
person knows, is the least trustworthy of music critics.

I called him a fool still, but less confidently.

Mathilde never went to Paris. She did not even reach
the stage of our modest Opera. Her health, which was
always going to improve in six months, declined through two
humid summers and a winter of rain. Cavanelle continued to
sell lace, praise complexions, and wear his coat to transparency.
Then one morning I learned that Mathilde had died.

Poor Cavanelle!

I attended the funeral. He stood beside the grave looking
smaller than I had ever seen him, but composed. He
thanked each person with a formality heartbreaking in its precision.
Even in grief he remembered who required a carriage and
whose gloves had been mislaid.

Afterward, I thought of him often. Death, that skillful though
merciless surgeon, had cut away the burden under which he
had bent. Cruel cure!—yet surely, when the wound healed, he
would stand upright. I pictured him buying a new coat,
eating a chop instead of bread and coffee, taking perhaps
a little room with a balcony. He might grow stout.
He might cultivate a waxed moustache, attend the theater, drink
his own demi-verre of wine. Freed from lessons, broths, shawls,
and impossible ambitions, he would become at last a reasonable
man.

Business carried me away from New Orleans, and nearly two
years passed before I entered the Canal Street shop again.

The same bells jingled. The same bolts of silk descended
in waterfalls from the shelves. And there was Cavanelle, darting
toward me with three boxes under one arm.

"Madame! Returned to us! Ah, New Orleans recover' her ornament."

He was unchanged.

No—his coat was worse. The collar had taken on a
greenish cast, and the cuff had ceased wandering only because
it had nearly ceased to exist. He looked thinner, though
as energetic as ever.

"You are well?" I demanded.

"Perfect'! Robust'!"

"And you are taking care of yourself now?"

A shadow crossed his face at the word *now*, but
vanished politely.

"I have not the time to be malade."

"You should buy a winter coat."

"This one is excellent. The cloth—English! They no longer make
such cloth."

"They no longer make it because it has all been
worn out."

He laughed extravagantly, as if I had produced a celebrated
epigram. While tying my parcel with more ribbon than the
purchase deserved, he told me his news. He had moved
from the yellow house. He now occupied rooms near Esplanade
Avenue with his mother's elder sister, Félicie.

"Aunt Félicie?" I said.

"Pauvre tante. Her husband die; the son depart for Texas—no
one know where. She have the rheumatism, very cruel, and
the eyes become feeble. Alone, she cannot remain. So I
take her."

Of course he had taken her.

"She require much attention?"

"Not much! Nothing! Only des petits soins."

There was the phrase again.

A special chair was needed because her back pained her;
a woman came twice a week for the washing; there
were ointments, spectacles, warm stockings, a tonic from the apothecary.
Félicie could not digest meat unless it was minced. She
slept badly if the room was too cold, and breathed
badly if it was too warm. On Sundays Cavanelle hired
a carriage to take her to Mass, because the streetcar
jolted her joints.

As he recited this catalogue of nothing, my exasperation rose
almost to anger.

"Cavanelle, you are impossible! Must you always find someone upon
whom to spend your last dollar?"

He stopped tying the parcel.

For once, the salesman's smile left him. He looked puzzled
rather than offended.

"But, madame—she is old."

That was his entire defense.

She was old. Mathilde had been frail. Another would be
blind, abandoned, hungry, feverish, or merely lonely. There would always
be a shawl to adjust, a broth to carry, a
carriage to hire; always some little human discomfort presenting its
account to Cavanelle. He would pay it, then call the
payment nothing. Des petits soins!

I saw suddenly that my grand plan for his liberation
had been absurd. Mathilde had not imprisoned him. Love had
not been a chain fastened around his ankle by a
languid girl with a bad voice. It was the natural
motion of his heart. Remove one object, and that motion
sought another, as water, diverted from a thirsty garden, finds
the next low place and fills it.

He resumed the bow on my parcel, making it splendid.

"Madame is not content with the lace? I change it
immediately."

"The lace is perfect," I said.

And so was my judgment at last—or as near perfect
as judgments about others are permitted to be.

Cavanelle was a humbug only in praising what he loved.
He was a fool only by the arithmetic which counts
coats and chops but not tenderness. I had called him
an angel once because I was sentimental, rejected the title
because I was practical, and now restored it because experience
had taught me that angels, if they exist among us,
may very well have frayed cuffs, bad boots, and no
worldly sense whatever.

Cavanelle was an angel.

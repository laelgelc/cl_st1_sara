## The Measure of a Day

On the outermost edge of a wood, where the land
lifted its green brow above the sea, there stood an
oak-tree three hundred and sixty-five years old. Its roots gripped
the dark earth among stones and fern; its mighty branches
were visible far across the water, and sailors looked for
them as for a friendly hand raised above the coast.

The oak had watched spring come like a laughing child,
summer stand crowned in roses, autumn shake gold from her
garments, and winter pass with white feet through the silent
forest. Three hundred and sixty-five times had the cuckoo called
from the beeches; three hundred and sixty-five times had the
snow lain upon the oak's branches.

Yet the tree did not reckon its life as men
reckon theirs. We wake by day and sleep by night,
but the oak woke through three seasons and slept through
one. Spring was its morning, summer its noon, autumn its
evening, and winter its quiet night. Thus a year seemed
to it only a single long day.

One warm afternoon, when the sea was blue and still,
a cloud of Ephemera danced beneath its boughs. They were
tiny winged creatures, light as the dust upon a sunbeam,
and each had but one day in which to be
born, to rejoice, to love, and to die.

"Poor little things!" sighed the oak. "Your whole life lasts
only until sunset. How melancholy it must be!"

"Melancholy?" cried one of the flies, settling upon a fresh
oak-leaf. "Why should it be melancholy?"

"Because your morning is already gone," said the oak, "and
your evening draws nigh. Before the stars appear, all your
happiness will be ended."

"But it is not ended yet!" answered the Ephemera. "The
sun is warm, the air is full of sweetness, and
everything dances with us. The clover-fields are fragrant; the wild
roses glow along the hedge; the elder-blossoms scatter white stars,
and the honeysuckle pours honey into the wind. I have
flown above the grass, looked into the heart of a
lily, played with my brothers and sisters, and seen my
own wings shine like glass. Is that not life?"

"It is but a moment," said the oak.

"A moment may be very large," replied the little creature.
"How long is your life?"

"Three hundred and sixty-five years."

"Then you possess many years, and I possess many moments.
Perhaps they are equally full. You count by winters; I
count by joys. You see, old tree, it is not
the length of the song that makes it beautiful."

At this the oak was silent. The fly sprang again
into the golden air. It wheeled with the others above
the foxgloves, rose and fell, glittered and vanished in the
brightness; and all the meadow seemed to sing, "We live!
We live!"

The sun descended. The sky became crimson, then violet; the
sea held the evening light as a silver cup holds
wine. The little flies danced more slowly. One after another
they folded their wings upon the leaves and grass.

"Good-night," whispered the oak. "Poor, happy little ones!"

"Good-night," answered the last Ephemera. "My day has been long
and beautiful."

Then it lay still, and the dew covered it.

Days and seasons passed over the oak. The swallows gathered
on its branches and spoke of southern gardens; the storks
sailed away beneath the clouds; the apples reddened in distant
orchards; and the woodland paths were buried under copper-coloured leaves.
At last winter came.

The sea grew dark and restless. Frost silvered every twig,
and snow folded the roots of the forest beneath a
soft white coverlet. Christmas drew nigh. From the village beyond
the hill came the sound of bells, now clear, now
faint, according as the wind carried them.

"Sleep sweetly," said the air.

"Sleep sweetly," murmured the moss about the oak's feet.

And the ancient tree bent its bare crown beneath the
stars and entered its winter sleep.

Trees have dreams, though theirs are deeper than ours and
filled with memories so old that no human tongue could
tell them all. The oak dreamed first of summer.

Warmth flowed through every branch. Its leaves spread green and
glossy, and sunshine poured between them in golden streams. Bees
came humming from the clover-fields; blue butterflies hovered over thyme;
beetles in polished armour marched beneath the fern. The sea
shone beyond the cliff, and white sails moved upon it
like swans.

All that the oak had ever seen returned.

There were children gathering strawberries, their hands and mouths stained
red. There were young lovers who had carved their names
upon the bark and promised that their affection should outlive
the tree itself. They had gone away long since, but
now they stood again in the green shade, with youth
in their faces.

There were hunters in scarlet coats and greyhounds straining at
the leash; there were woodcutters singing as their axes rang;
there were wandering musicians with ribbons on their hats; there
were old women carrying herbs, soldiers returning from war, and
knights of an earlier age, with plumes nodding above bright
helmets. A king had once rested beneath the oak and
drunk from a silver cup. A poor shepherd had slept
in the same place with his cheek upon his folded
arm. The oak had sheltered both and had thought the
sleep of one as peaceful as the other.

Birds long vanished from the wood sang again among its
branches. The nightingale poured out pearls of sound; the cuckoo
called from the hazels; thrushes whistled after rain. Even the
small wren, which had built its nest in a hollow
bough a hundred summers before, returned with a feather in
its beak.

"We are here!" cried the birds.

"We are here!" said the children and lovers.

"We are here!" whispered the flowers beneath.

The dream broadened. The whole forest stood in summer glory.
Birch-trees lifted their delicate veils; beeches spread smooth arms; firs
pointed solemnly toward heaven. The ground was embroidered with violets,
anemones, sorrel, harebells, daisies, and the humble green blades that
no one names, though each carries its drop of dew
as proudly as a queen carries a jewel.

The oak felt young. Sap surged through its trunk like
a joyful song. Its crown rose higher than it had
ever risen in waking life—above the beeches, above the firs,
above the cliff and sea.

Higher and higher it grew.

The clouds floated beneath it, large and white as swans.
The stars appeared, not cold and distant, but near and
mild, each shining like a lamp at a festal window.
From beyond them came a radiance that was neither sunrise
nor moonlight, but something purer, as if all love and
all truth had become visible.

"Oh, how glorious!" cried the oak. "Is this eternity?"

Its branches stretched toward the brightness, yet suddenly its joy
was troubled.

"Where is the little wren?" it asked. "Where are the
wild roses and the children? Where are the lovers, the
shepherd, and the king? Where are my companions of the
wood? Must I rise alone?"

The light seemed to answer, though it spoke no words:
No joy is complete while love looks backward.

Then the oak called with every leaf it had ever
borne.

"Come with me! Come, all of you—both great and small!
Let not one blade of grass remain behind!"

At once the forest began to rise.

The firs came with their dark, fragrant crowns; the birches
followed, trembling in silver; the beeches lifted their copper leaves.
Bushes, flowers, mosses, and grasses ascended together. The rose brought
its perfume, the violet its blue humility, and the thistle
its purple crown. Birds flew among them; deer bounded through
the airy glades; the fox came softly, and the hare
without fear. Butterflies hovered over flowers that never withered.

The people of the oak's remembrance came also. Children ran
laughing through the radiant grass. The lovers walked hand in
hand, their carved names made living again. The knight's armour
shone, the shepherd carried his crook, and the king wore
no crown, for none was needed there.

"All are here!" cried the oak.

"All—all are here!" answered the innumerable voices.

Yet from far below came a tiny sound, delicate as
the touch of rain upon a leaf.

"Do not forget me."

The oak looked down and saw the Ephemera, shining in
the dream as it had shone during its one summer
day.

"You too!" exclaimed the tree. "Come, little one!"

"I have never been far away," answered the fly. "My
day was full."

Then it rose beside the eagle, and neither seemed greater
nor less in the all-embracing light.

The oak's happiness became so vast that its ancient heart
could scarcely contain it. It felt the earth release its
roots. The stones that had held them loosened; the dark
soil opened gently, as a mother opens her arms when
a child has slept enough.

"I am free!" cried the oak. "I rise with all
I have loved!"

At that same hour a storm broke over the coast.

The sea leaped against the cliffs. Waves lifted white heads
and roared into the darkness; clouds drove across the sky
like torn banners. In the forest, branches cracked, trunks groaned,
and the wind rushed between them with a voice mighty
enough to wake the dead.

But the old oak did not wake. It dreamed of
ascent.

The storm seized its crown. The earth, softened by rain
and wearied by three hundred and sixty-five years of holding,
could keep the roots no longer. They tore free with
a sound like the rending of a great door. For
one instant the oak stood between earth and heaven; then
it fell toward the sea.

Its fall shook the cliff. Branches shattered; birds, startled from
shelter, flew crying through the night. Where the landmark had
stood, the storm swept over an empty space.

But in the dream there was no crash.

There was only music, light, and the joyful cry:

"All are here!"

Christmas morning dawned calm and pale. The storm had passed,
leaving the sea strewn with foam and the woodland glittering
with ice. A ship, which had battled through the night,
entered the bay. Flags flew from its masts in honour
of the holy day, and the sailors gathered upon the
deck while the village bells rang over the water.

One sailor pointed to the cliff.

"The great oak is gone!"

They all looked. For as long as any of them
remembered, its crown had shown above the shore. Fathers had
pointed it out to sons; pilots had steered by it
in mist and evening shadow. Now only torn earth and
broken roots remained.

"It saved us from going too near the rocks more
than once," said the oldest sailor, uncovering his head. "A
noble old tree! It has kept its last watch."

That was its funeral oration—brief, honest, and spoken by men
who knew the value of a faithful landmark.

From the little church came the Christmas carol. The wind
carried it over roofs white with frost, over the sleeping
fields, across the harbour, and out toward the wide sea.
It sang of hope entering darkness, of the lowly lifted,
of the lost restored, and of life that does not
perish though the earthly form be laid down.

The sailors listened in silence.

Upon the shore lay the oak, immense and still. Snow
rested on its broken branches; the waves breathed below; morning
light touched the bark with gold. To human eyes, three
hundred and sixty-five years had ended in one tempestuous hour.

Yet what are three hundred and sixty-five years beside eternity?
They are as brief as the life of an Ephemera—one
summer day, one dance in the sunshine, one full and
beautiful moment.

And perhaps, while the carol passed over the fallen tree,
the oak still dreamed itself upward, higher and higher, carrying
with it the forest, the flowers, the birds, the remembered
children, the humble and the proud, both great and small.

"All are here," whispered the light.

Then the ancient oak's long day was ended, and its
Christmas morning began.

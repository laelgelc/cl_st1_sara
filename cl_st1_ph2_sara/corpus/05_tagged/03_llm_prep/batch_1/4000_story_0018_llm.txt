# Seigerman's Per Cent

The winter which turned Baugh, Stubb, and Arab Ab loose
upon the peace and finances of Comanche County began with
a blue norther that froze three water barrels before supper
and drove every loose yearling on the Cimarron ten miles
south before daylight.

Up to that season an unemployed cow-hand had never been
considered exactly destitute. He might be without wages, but so
long as he could throw a leg over a horse
he belonged, in a loose and wandering fashion, to the
range. Any ranch whose brand he had ever worked could
be depended upon for a meal, a bed in the
bunk-house, and feed for his mount. If he met the
outfit on the trail, the cook would set out a
plate without asking for his pedigree or financial standing.

That was called riding the chuck-line. No one had invented
it by statute, and therefore every one respected it.

But the cattle business had lately taken to wearing a
white collar. Eastern money had come in, followed by bookkeepers,
printed regulations, and gentlemen who could reckon the cost of
a biscuit at forty rods. The old cattlemen had given
a rider shelter because he might be useful at the
next round-up. The new companies maintained that a man not
on the pay-roll had no more claim upon beef and
beans than a coyote had upon the branding-chute.

A circular came down from the head office of the
Continental and Western Cattle Company declaring that, after the first
of November, no food, lodging, horse feed, tobacco, medicine, ammunition,
or other company supplies were to be furnished to persons
not in regular employment.

The ranch superintendent read the order aloud in the cook-shack.
When he finished, no one spoke for several seconds.

Then Arab Ab, who had been christened Abner and had
earned the rest of his name by trading horses with
a Syrian peddler, said:

"I notice they don't prohibit us from breathing company air."

"That'll be in the next circular," said Stubb.

Baugh took the paper from the superintendent and examined it
gravely. He was a large, deliberate man whose confidence commonly
arrived at a destination several minutes before the truth started.
He read the regulation twice, turned it over to see
if there were any mercy printed on the back, and
handed it away.

"The cattle business," he observed, "is advancing towards civilization. The
first evidence of civilization is when hospitality gets entered on
the wrong side of the ledger."

The superintendent had no pleasure in discharging them. He needed
fifty riders in the spring, but only twelve during the
winter. The company had ordered him to cut the pay-roll,
and a company order was a kind of weather: a
man might criticize it, but he still got wet.

Thus Baugh, Stubb, and Arab Ab received their wages, rolled
their beds, and started north.

They had between them seven horses, three saddles, two pack
outfits, and a little more than four hundred dollars. This
seemed enough to carry them until spring, especially to men
accustomed to measuring wealth by whether it could be folded
and put into a vest pocket. They intended to winter
modestly in Willet, the county seat, and return to the
range when the grass came.

Their modesty survived until the second evening.

Willet contained a court-house, a bank, two churches, four livery
stables, a newspaper, and eleven places where a man could
obtain evidence against himself. It also possessed Louie Seigerman's hotel
and bar, which stood opposite the court-house and enjoyed the
trade of lawyers, jurors, drovers, traveling salesmen, and candidates for
office.

Baugh and his companions secured a room there, ate a
supper that reminded them they had not lately been fed
by a corporation, and entered the bar to make the
acquaintance of the county.

A poker game was in progress behind the stove.

"We will sit in for an hour," said Baugh. "It
is bad manners to enter a town and refuse its
hospitality."

At midnight Stubb had lost thirty-eight dollars.

At two o'clock Arab Ab remarked that he had discovered
the habits of the game.

At three, after losing sixty more, he said that the
habits of the game had changed without notice.

Baugh played until breakfast, explaining at intervals that temporary losses
were merely tuition. When the game ended, his tuition account
stood at one hundred and nine dollars.

Their next misfortune was a horse race. Arab owned a
brown gelding which could outrun any horse he had ever
raced against under fair conditions. Willet produced a gray mare
that beat him by half a length under conditions which
Arab described for three days and never once called fair.

Stubb then backed a bay horse belonging to a deputy
assessor. The bay lost by forty yards and was afterwards
discovered to have been entered chiefly on account of the
deputy assessor's need for money.

Baugh declined to be discouraged.

"Luck," he said, "is a nervous animal. Chase it and
it runs. Turn your back, and it'll follow you home."

They turned their backs on luck in a faro game
and lost another eighty dollars.

By the middle of December they had sold two spare
horses. By Christmas the pack animals had gone. They celebrated
the holiday with a turkey dinner at Seigerman's, after which
Louie presented a bill that proved hospitality had entered politics
as well as bookkeeping.

January found them with three saddle horses, their equipment, and
twenty-seven dollars and forty cents.

The weather had hardened. Snow lay in the draws, the
north wind swept the streets, and the range camps were
closed to chuck-line riders. There was no work nearer than
the Indian Territory, and perhaps none there. A cowboy will
endure heat, thirst, dust, danger, and the society of a
cook, but he does not willingly sell his saddle. The
three therefore gathered in their room at Seigerman's to consider
measures of finance.

Stubb emptied their joint treasury onto the bed.

"Seven dollars and forty cents belongs to me," he said.
"Arab's got nine. You possess eleven dollars and all the
wisdom."

"Wisdom," said Baugh, "has been bringing a low market lately."

Arab lay on the other bed with his boots against
the wall.

"We can make a race with the black horse," he
suggested.

"We can also burn the money for warmth," said Stubb.
"It takes less time."

"The black horse can run."

"So can a barber, if you shoot close enough. That
don't make him a race horse."

Baugh had been looking through the frost-furred window at the
court-house. Several men stood on its steps, and one was
shaking hands with every person who passed.

"Who is that energetic citizen?" he asked.

"Jim Tolliver," Stubb answered. "Candidate for sheriff."

"How many candidates are there?"

"Three declared. Tolliver, Mason, and old Ricks."

"Then there is a vacancy."

"There's three men after one office."

"That leaves a vacancy for a fourth man."

Arab sat up. "You fixing to run?"

"I never accept an office unless it comes looking for
me with a rope. But a public movement, properly encouraged,
has its advantages."

Stubb regarded him. "The last public movement you encouraged was
a shell game at Dodge."

"That was private enterprise. I am now considering the voice
of the people."

"The people ain't said anything."

"That's why they require a spokesman."

Baugh went downstairs. Seigerman was behind the bar polishing glasses.
He was a round, prosperous man with pale whiskers, a
broad red face, and a deep conviction that public office
was the natural crown of success. He had once been
elected precinct committeeman for the Reform Party, but the party
had expired before he could take advantage of it. Since
then he had served on school committees, welcomed conventions, contributed
beer to torchlight parades, and mentioned to every traveling politician
that he was ready to sacrifice himself for the county.

Baugh ordered three drinks and paid for them from the
eleven dollars. This expenditure pained Stubb when he heard of
it, but Baugh explained that capital must be cast upon
the waters.

"Mr. Seigerman," Baugh said, "you know the cattle interests."

"I know many cattlemen," said Louie. "They stop by my
house. Good men. Sometimes they pay."

"What is their feeling concerning law enforcement?"

Seigerman set down the glass he was polishing.

"They do not like thieves."

"Precisely. Rustling has become the leading industry. A stockman brands
a calf on Monday and meets it Wednesday wearing another
man's name. The cattle companies are aroused."

"Dot sheriff, he does nothing?"

"I would not say nothing. A man standing still occupies
space. But the companies want action. They want a fearless,
impartial, substantial citizen—some one not controlled by the court-house ring."

Seigerman's towel moved more slowly.

"Who do they want?"

Baugh looked towards the door, then lowered his voice.

"I am not authorized to give names."

"Sure. Sure. I understand."

"I will say this: when representatives of seven of the
largest brands in the Southwest met privately last month, one
name was favorably discussed."

Louie leaned across the bar.

"Whose name?"

Baugh glanced again at the door.

"The conference was confidential."

"I am confidential."

"That may be, but I have my instructions."

He drank slowly, declined a second glass, and walked out.

The refusal of liquor impressed Seigerman more than anything he
had said. Baugh had never before declined a drink in
the house. A man who refused one at eleven in
the morning must be carrying business of consequence.

When Baugh returned upstairs, Stubb demanded an accounting.

"You spent thirty cents to say what?"

"Nothing definite."

"That's the best thirty cents' worth of your conversation I
ever heard."

"I have laid the foundation."

"What are we building?"

"A sheriff."

"Out of what?"

Baugh took off his hat and threw it on the
bed.

"Out of Louie Seigerman's ambition, which is the most plentiful
building material in this county."

Arab's face brightened. "Is he going to run?"

"He is going to be summoned by the united cattle
interests. We are the united cattle interests."

Stubb pushed the coins together.

"What interest have they united on?"

"Keeping us alive until spring."

For the next two days Baugh said no more to
Seigerman. He paid for one meal, skipped another, and remained
sober with a severity which disturbed his companions. On the
third evening, while the bar was crowded, he entered, looked
around as if seeking privacy, and said:

"Louie, have you a room where four men can confer
without interruption?"

Seigerman led him at once to a small room behind
the bar. There was a table, six chairs, a spittoon,
and a framed photograph of the governor.

Baugh closed the door.

"The situation has developed," he said. "I have received authority
to proceed one step further. Before I do, I must
know your attitude on rustling."

"My attitude? I put them all in jail."

"Good. Would you enforce the law against a small thief
as well as a large one?"

"Sure."

"Against a cowboy as well as a farmer?"

"Sure."

"Against a company employee?"

"Every one."

Baugh frowned. "The companies may not like that last answer."

"I enforce dot law."

He continued frowning for ten seconds. "It is the right
answer. They may not like it, but they will respect
it."

Seigerman's chest expanded.

"Now," Baugh continued, "I am connected with the Continental and
Western. You may know that."

Louie did not, but nodded.

"I have been instructed to feel you out. No commitment.
No promises. Merely determine whether, if a public demand arose,
you would permit your name to be considered for sheriff."

Seigerman's face reddened beyond its customary color.

"For sheriff?"

"Do not misunderstand me. There are difficulties. The brands are
divided over methods. The Bar-Circle wants a range man. The
Diamond Tail opposes every one proposed by the Continental. The
Boxed K has money but no organization. Yet all agree
that the present candidates are too weak."

"Do they say my name?"

Baugh looked pained.

"I cannot disclose the proceedings of a confidential conference."

"But they say it?"

"I can tell you only that your record as a
property owner, taxpayer, hotel man, former precinct committeeman, and friend
of the livestock interest has not escaped attention."

Seigerman breathed deeply.

"If dot people call me—if duty says, 'Louie, you must
go'—I do not run away."

"That is all I am authorized to learn."

"What happens now?"

"I consult the conservative element."

"Who is dot?"

"Stubb."

Seigerman had seen Stubb eating and drinking in the house,
but had never detected any conservative element in him. This
made the discovery more impressive.

An hour later Stubb was dragged from his room, informed
of his dignity, and coached.

"You represent the Bar-Circle, the Diamond Tail, and the independent
stockmen north of the river," Baugh said. "You are opposed
to Seigerman."

"Why?"

"Because opposition is respectable. If we all fall on his
neck at once, he may mistrust us. You raise objections.
I answer them. Arab furnishes figures."

"What figures?"

"Any he can lift."

Arab nodded. "I can furnish figures enough to make the
census look like a private memorandum."

The conference took place the following afternoon. Stubb entered the
back room reluctantly, carrying a folded newspaper and wearing the
expression of a man summoned to identify a body. Baugh
introduced him as a representative of several concerns whose combined
herds, according to Arab's later calculation, would have required most
of Kansas for standing room.

Seigerman produced whisky and glasses.

"No," said Baugh. "Not during business."

Stubb looked at him with sincere resentment, which Louie mistook
for official austerity.

"We may take one," Stubb said.

"We may not," Baugh replied. "The eyes of the cattle
interests are upon us."

"Their eyes ain't thirsty."

Baugh ignored him.

"Mr. Stubb has doubts concerning your availability, Louie. I considered
it fair that you hear them directly."

Stubb settled into his chair.

"I don't favor a hotel man," he said.

"Why not?" Seigerman demanded.

"Too many friends. A sheriff needs enemies."

"I got enemies."

"How many?"

"Plenty."

"Can you name twelve reliable ones?"

Seigerman began counting. He reached eight without difficulty, hesitated, and
remembered four men who had disputed bills.

"That's better," said Stubb. "Still, you're foreign-born."

"I am a citizen twenty-two years."

"Some folks object to a man born outside the county."

"I was born outside, but I live here longer than
Tolliver."

"True. Then there's the liquor issue. The temperance people won't
support a saloonkeeper."

"My hotel has food, rooms, stable—everything. The bar is only
part."

"The bar is the part they'll mention."

Baugh struck the table with his palm.

"Now we reach the heart of the matter. Are the
cattlemen to let a handful of water-drinkers select their sheriff?
The Continental loses twelve thousand dollars a year through theft.
The Boxed K lost eight hundred and forty steers last
season. The Diamond Tail estimates that one calf in six
disappears before branding. Yet Stubb would reject the one man
capable of stopping it because he sells an honest drink."

"I didn't say reject," Stubb answered. "I said investigate."

"The investigation has been proceeding for months."

Seigerman looked from one to the other.

"Do they all want me?"

"No," said Stubb.

"Yes," said Baugh, at the same instant.

The disagreement pleased Louie. It proved that the meeting was
genuine.

Stubb opened his newspaper.

"The Bar-Circle wants Peters."

"Peters cannot carry the towns."

"The Diamond Tail likes Hendry."

"Hendry cannot carry his own household. His wife votes against
him at breakfast."

"The independents want a cowman."

"They cannot agree upon which cowman. If one brand names
a candidate, every neighboring brand recalls a quarrel over water."

Stubb rubbed his chin.

"What about campaign funds?"

"The sinews of war will be supplied."

"How much?"

Baugh looked at Seigerman. "That depends upon the candidate."

Stubb shook his head. "You see? No organization. No figures.
Just wind blowing across a big pasture."

At this signal the door opened and Arab Ab entered
without knocking. He wore a long coat borrowed from a
horse dealer and carried a leather memorandum book.

"Am I interrupting the central committee?" he asked.

"You are," said Baugh. "Come in."

Arab bowed gravely to Seigerman.

"This is Mr. Ab," said Baugh, "our organization man."

"Organizer for who?" Stubb asked.

"For the voters," said Arab. "Companies come and go, but
voters remain unless the sheriff gets active."

He opened his book.

"I have completed a preliminary canvas. The Continental can deliver
one hundred and forty-two men. Bar-Circle, sixty-eight. Diamond Tail, ninety-one.
Boxed K, one hundred and seventeen. Double Spur, forty-three. Independent
riders and floaters, conservatively, two hundred and six."

Stubb stared at him. "That's six hundred and sixty-seven."

Arab turned a page. "You omitted cooks."

"How many cooks vote?"

"Every cook who can get away from his outfit, and
some who can't. Add thirty-four. Blacksmiths, twenty-one. Hide buyers, eleven.
Livery employees, sixteen. Men unjustly accused of stealing horses, nine."

"Why would they support a sheriff?"

"They favor a change of administration."

Seigerman leaned over the book, but Arab closed it.

"Confidential rolls," he explained.

"How many total?" Louie asked.

"Our first-line strength is seven hundred and fifty-eight. With proper
inducements and transportation, we can bring in four hundred votes
from adjoining counties."

Stubb sat upright. "They can't vote here."

Arab looked offended.

"Not until they establish residence. We are discussing organization, not
miracles."

"How long does that take?"

"In politics, residence is chiefly a condition of mind."

Baugh intervened.

"We will have no irregularities. Mr. Seigerman must be elected
cleanly."

"Certainly," said Arab. "Clean as a washed cheque."

The first conference ended without a decision. Stubb remained officially
doubtful, Arab was ordered to verify his totals, and Baugh
promised to communicate with the principal companies. Seigerman furnished supper
to all three and refused payment.

"You are working for me," he said.

"Not yet," Baugh corrected. "We are working for the cattle
interests."

"Sure. Sure. I understand."

The next morning their room bill was returned marked "By
arrangement."

Stubb examined it.

"I begin to respect public service," he said.

"Do not cheapen the movement by enjoying it too soon,"
Baugh warned. "Louie has been approached, but not nominated. We
must enlarge the demand."

"How large?"

"Large enough to include board, lodging, and horse feed until
April."

Arab suggested they send telegrams to themselves. Baugh rejected the
idea as extravagant, but approved letters. During the next week
they wrote communications upon paper bearing the names of cattle
companies. The stationery was obtained by Arab from old invoices
in a commission merchant's office, and the messages were posted
from three neighboring towns by obliging stage drivers.

Baugh received the first letter at breakfast. He read it,
frowned, and put it into his pocket.

Seigerman came from behind the desk.

"News?"

"Nothing final."

"From a company?"

"The Prairie Consolidated."

"What do they say?"

"They are cautious."

That afternoon Stubb received a letter from the Diamond Tail
and allowed it to remain unopened on the bar for
twenty minutes. The envelope attracted nearly as much attention as
a weapon.

At supper Arab burst into the dining-room waving a third
letter.

"Victory!" he cried, then checked himself upon seeing Seigerman. "I
beg pardon. This is private."

Louie took them all into the back room.

The Prairie Consolidated, as interpreted by Baugh, would support Seigerman
if the northern interests did so. The Diamond Tail demanded
assurance that he would appoint a range man as deputy.
The Boxed K promised money after the convention. The Double
Spur was unwilling to make promises but viewed the movement
with "profound sympathy," a phrase Baugh said was equivalent to
forty votes.

"This makes the Zweibund," he announced.

"What is dot?" Louie asked.

"A two-power alliance. The Continental and Prairie Consolidated."

Stubb studied the governor's portrait.

"You still need the Diamond Tail."

"I have them conditionally."

"Conditional cattle don't make unconditional beef."

Seigerman offered to promise a range deputy.

"Not yet," said Baugh. "A candidate who begins promising offices
before nomination loses moral force. Besides, Stubb may want the
place."

"I don't," said Stubb.

"Your reluctance qualifies you."

Three days later the Diamond Tail was admitted, creating what
Arab called the Dreibund. When the Boxed K entered, he
declared a Fierbund, which Seigerman repeated proudly to customers although
he could not explain its geography.

The campaign began to supply all necessities. Louie provided the
three men a larger room, saying political leaders required space.
Their horses were moved into his stable without charge. Meals
appeared at any hour, and the back room was reserved
for the central committee. A bottle stood on the table,
but Baugh enforced temperance during conferences.

This discipline nearly ruined the scheme.

"I can lie without whisky," Stubb complained, "but I don't
see why a man should be asked to."

"A single drink might loosen confidential information."

"There ain't any information."

"Then it is all the easier to keep confidential."

Arab proved less obedient. He hid a glass behind the
governor's photograph until Baugh discovered it.

"You are endangering the confidence of the candidate."

"I'm maintaining the enthusiasm of the voters."

When Seigerman entered, Arab immediately opened his book and reported
that the organization had grown to nine hundred and twelve
men, not including sheepmen.

"I thought cattlemen hated sheep," Louie said.

"They do. That makes sheepmen easy to organize against any
candidate the cattlemen oppose."

"But the cattlemen support me."

"Exactly. The sheepmen will support you to show independence."

Seigerman considered the reasoning and found it profound.

The formal call came late in January. Baugh had delayed
it until Louie's appetite for office had overcome his remaining
caution. The three representatives assembled after midnight, when the bar
was closed. They wore clean shirts, and Arab had persuaded
the local printer to prepare a document headed:

**DECLARATION OF THE UNITED LIVESTOCK, COMMERCIAL, AGRICULTURAL, AND LAW-ABIDING INTERESTS
OF COMANCHE COUNTY.**

The declaration stated that theft stalked unchecked, property trembled, justice
slumbered, and the eyes of honest citizenship had turned spontaneously
towards Louis Seigerman.

Baugh read it aloud.

"Mr. Seigerman," he concluded, "we are instructed to ask whether
you will accept the nomination for sheriff, subject to the
action of the county convention and the sovereign people."

Louie stood. His hands shook slightly, and for once his
pleasure made him solemn.

"Gentlemen, I do not seek dot office. Many times people
say, 'Louie, you should be sheriff,' and I say, 'No,
I got my business.' But when property is not safe,
when cattle disappear, when honest men call—then a citizen must
not think only of himself. If the people want me,
I accep."

Arab began to applaud, but Stubb kicked him.

Baugh shook the candidate's hand.

"You have made a sacrifice which the cattle interests will
not forget."

"What do we do first?" Louie asked.

"Maintain absolute secrecy."

"When do I announce?"

"To-morrow."

The announcement appeared in the Willet *Clarion* beneath a portrait
taken twelve years earlier. It described Seigerman as the unanimous
choice of the livestock interests and an uncompromising enemy of
rustling, disorder, monopoly, extravagance, and secret combinations.

Tolliver read the article and asked which livestock interests were
supporting him.

"All of them," said Seigerman.

Mason made the same inquiry and received the same reply.

Old Ricks merely said, "Well, Louie always did keep good
whisky," and continued whittling.

The announcement forced Baugh to manufacture visible political activity. He
sent Arab into the country to organize precincts. Arab traveled
by train and stage, visiting saloons, livery barns, and several
ranches where he had friends. He returned after nine days
with mud on his coat, no money, and an expression
of national importance.

"How is the country?" Seigerman asked.

"Rising."

"For me?"

"Like floodwater. If the election were held to-day, you would
carry every precinct except possibly Cedar."

"What is wrong in Cedar?"

"Religious opposition."

"I give money to the church."

"That's the trouble. You gave five dollars to the Methodists
and only three to the Baptists. I can adjust it
for twenty dollars."

Seigerman supplied the money.

Arab also reported that imported votes would be required at
the convention. The phrase worried Louie.

"Dot must be legal."

"Entirely," Arab assured him. "We don't import the votes themselves.
We import the men containing them."

"How much?"

"For transportation, meals, lodging, cigars, and the customary educational expenses,
fifty dollars now. Another hundred when they arrive."

Stubb objected that Arab's organization costs exceeded the campaign treasury.

"You can't move public sentiment for nothing," Arab replied. "Public
sentiment is heavy."

Baugh supported him. "The sinews of war must be kept
flexible."

The fifty dollars was furnished and divided privately into three
equal shares, with the odd two dollars credited to Baugh's
tuition account.

February passed in conferences. Baugh became general chairman, Stubb the
representative of conservative capital, and Arab chief of field operations.
Their system had the merit of allowing each man to
confirm the others while differing upon every detail.

Baugh would announce that the Prairie Consolidated had pledged three
hundred votes.

Stubb would say, "Two hundred."

Arab would consult his book. "Two hundred and eighty-six, with
fourteen doubtful."

Seigerman always accepted the final figure because it had survived
disagreement.

At other times Stubb deliberately threatened withdrawal.

"The cattlemen don't like Louie's position on fencing," he would
say.

"What is my position?" Seigerman demanded.

"That's what they don't understand."

"I favor legal fences."

"Too broad."

"I oppose illegal ones."

"Too narrow."

Baugh would mediate. "State that you favor fences where needed,
oppose them where harmful, and reserve the right to act
according to law."

"That is my position," Louie said with relief.

Arab recorded it as the Seigerman Doctrine.

The candidate's platform grew. He favored low taxes and sufficient
revenue, strict enforcement and individual liberty, better roads without public
expense, honest government by practical men, friendship with capital, protection
for labor, free grass, open markets, closed jails, and full
dinner pails.

On the rustling question he was merciless.

"If elected," Baugh dictated, "I shall pursue the cattle thief
from the branding-pen to the market, from the market to
the court, and from the court to the penitentiary."

"Good," said Seigerman. "Put in hanging."

"Too strong for the farmers."

"Why?"

"Some of them have relatives."

The political movement also required travel. Baugh went to Junction
City to consult a supposed attorney for the Continental. Stubb
visited Caldwell to meet the Bar-Circle directors. Arab made a
second organization tour. Seigerman paid their railroad fare and furnished
expense money.

In Junction City Baugh remained three days, played moderate poker,
and returned with a leather portfolio purchased secondhand. It contained
cattle circulars, an obsolete railway map, and a blank incorporation
form. He carried it thereafter as evidence of responsibility.

Stubb's mission produced a disagreement of useful proportions.

"The Bar-Circle will not contribute a cent," he reported in
the back room.

Seigerman looked alarmed.

"They promised five hundred dollars," said Baugh.

"No. They said they might match outside contributions."

"That is a pledge."

"That is a maybe wearing boots."

Arab opened his book. "We don't need them. Prairie Consolidated
will put up eight hundred, the Continental one thousand, and
independent stockmen four hundred and sixty."

"Where is it?" Stubb asked.

"Awaiting a proper time."

"Money always awaits a proper time when it don't exist."

"You represent obstruction," Arab said.

"And you represent figures that would get lost coming home."

Baugh quieted them.

"This quarrel must not occur before the candidate. Louie, large
corporate contributions made too early would enable our enemies to
charge that you are owned by the cattle interests."

"I am not owned."

"Of course not. Therefore we shall delay accepting their money."

Seigerman was satisfied. Indeed, he was relieved. He had already
spent more upon the campaign than he admitted to his
wife, and the promise of future funds was nearly as
agreeable as funds themselves.

He gave Baugh another thirty dollars for postage and confidential
telegrams.

By March the three cowboys were living better than at
any time since the round-up. Their horses were grain-fed, their
clothes repaired, and their personal credit restored. They had even
saved a little money, though Baugh forbade gambling.

"You can't be the political salvation of a county and
play faro in public," he said.

"What about in private?" Arab asked.

"In private you'd still lose."

They maintained their discipline because spring remained distant and the
campaign was generous. At breakfast they discussed precinct totals. At
dinner they studied cattle theft. At supper they considered the
marshalship.

That prospect had arisen through Arab.

He returned from Topeka with a rumor that the federal
marshal might be replaced after the next election. By the
time he reached Willet, the rumor had become a plan.

"There is no reason," he told Seigerman, "why a successful
sheriff backed by every major cattle concern should stop at
county office."

Louie's eyes widened.

"What comes after sheriff?"

"United States marshal."

Baugh objected at once.

"Premature."

"I only mention the natural direction of events."

"National appointments involve senators."

"I have two senators in correspondence."

"You have written two senators. That is not the same
thing."

"They will answer when they understand the strength of our
organization."

Stubb shook his head. "Louie hasn't been nominated for sheriff,
and Arab's already moved him to Washington."

"A good campaign looks beyond the first water hole."

Seigerman tried to appear indifferent.

"I do not ask for marshal."

"Certainly not," said Arab. "A man who asks never gets
it. We arrange the demand."

"What does a marshal earn?"

Baugh ended the discussion sternly, which convinced Louie that the
matter was serious.

From then on the candidate conducted himself with federal dignity.
He bought a black broadcloth coat, had new photographs taken,
and practiced signing "Louis Seigerman, Sheriff" on hotel stationery. Once,
forgetting himself, he added "U.S.M." beneath it and burned the
sheet.

Not every day passed smoothly. There were alarms.

A drover from the Double Spur came into the bar
and denied that his company had endorsed Seigerman. Baugh happened
to be present.

"Of course they deny it," he said after the drover
left. "Their support is confidential."

"Why?" Louie asked.

"Because the company has contracts with the present administration. If
they declared openly before the convention, those contracts might be
endangered."

Another time the president of the Prairie Consolidated wrote Seigerman
in answer to a direct inquiry, saying he had never
heard of the political coalition.

"That proves the secrecy of our committee," Arab explained. "The
president isn't trusted with local details."

Stubb almost laughed and converted the sound into a cough.

The most dangerous event occurred when Seigerman proposed to visit
the Continental and Western ranch and address the riders.

Baugh opposed the trip.

"They are already unanimous."

"I want to thank them."

"Gratitude before election looks like weakness."

"I should meet the superintendent."

"He is hostile."

"You say the company supports me."

"The head office supports you. The superintendent favors Tolliver because
Tolliver's brother married his wife's cousin. Corporate politics is full
of treachery."

Seigerman abandoned the visit and admired Baugh's inside knowledge.

As the weather softened, the three campaign managers began watching
the southern horizon. Geese passed north. Snow disappeared from the
shadowed banks. The streets of Willet changed from iron to
mud. Every warm wind carried the smell of wet earth
and reminded them that somewhere calves were dropping and outfits
would soon be hiring.

Then a letter arrived from the Continental ranch superintendent—not to
Baugh, but to Stubb. It said work would begin in
two weeks and there would be places for all three
if they reported promptly.

Stubb read it in their room.

"The grass has nominated us," he said.

Arab stretched on the bed. "I was getting tired of
politics."

"You were born tired of everything except counting votes that
don't exist."

Baugh took the letter and looked thoughtful.

"We must withdraw in order."

"We can ride out to-morrow," said Stubb.

"That would be desertion. We are entrusted with a great
public movement."

"We're entrusted with a room bill."

"And with the candidate's confidence. The campaign must be left
in a condition able to support itself until it falls."

Arab grinned. "How much expense money does that require?"

Baugh ignored the question, which meant he was considering it.

They held an emergency meeting with Seigerman that evening. Baugh's
leather portfolio lay open on the table. Stubb looked graver
than usual, and Arab had covered three pages of his
book with columns.

"We have bad news," Baugh began.

Louie paled. "The cattlemen withdraw?"

"Never. Their loyalty is firm. The trouble is that our
enemies have discovered the extent of your strength."

"What do they do?"

"They are attempting to stampede the southern delegation."

Arab struck the table.

"Buying them by the bunch. Tolliver's men offered five dollars
a head in Cedar, whisky in Sand Creek, and two
deputyships to a family containing only one voter."

"Dot is corruption!"

"It is politics," Stubb said.

Baugh continued.

"We must take the field personally. Stubb must go south
and hold the Bar-Circle men. Arab must gather the imported
delegation."

"Legally," Louie said.

"Always legally," Arab replied. "Though legality costs more."

"And you?" Louie asked Baugh.

"I must go to the Continental headquarters. The company's executive
committee has called an urgent conference."

"How long are you gone?"

"A week. Ten days at most."

The county convention was three weeks away.

Seigerman looked troubled. He had come to depend upon their
conferences. Without Baugh's authority, Stubb's objections, and Arab's figures, the
campaign seemed suddenly hollow.

"You come back before convention?"

"Nothing except death or cattle business can prevent us."

Stubb turned his face away.

"What money is needed?" Louie asked.

Baugh protested. He had not raised the subject. The companies,
he said, ought to bear all expenses. Their funds were
delayed by accounting regulations and eastern directors who required two
signatures to move one dollar. He was unwilling to let
Seigerman advance more.

This reluctance was so firm that Seigerman insisted.

After a long argument, Baugh accepted sixty dollars for railroad
fare and incidentals. Stubb received forty. Arab required eighty-five because
he was moving men, a process comparable in expense to
shipping livestock but less certain in delivery.

They planned to leave separately. Arab departed first on the
morning train, carrying his organization book and promising to return
with four hundred men.

"If I can't bring four hundred," he told Seigerman on
the platform, "I won't insult you with less than three
hundred."

Stubb rode south the next day. He shook Louie's hand
in the stable.

"I opposed you at first," he said, "but you've worn
down my judgment."

"I knew you become my friend."

"I have become something."

Baugh remained another day. He reviewed the campaign, sealed papers
in envelopes, and instructed Seigerman to make no pledges, answer
no hostile questions, and distrust any cattleman who denied belonging
to the coalition.

"The companies must maintain secrecy until the ballots are cast,"
he said.

"When they bring dot money?"

"Convention morning."

"All of it?"

"Enough to make opposition surrender."

Louie accompanied him to the edge of town. Baugh rode
the black horse and wore the leather portfolio strapped behind
his saddle.

"You are sure?" Seigerman asked one last time.

"Louie, if votes were cattle, you could not find range
enough to graze your majority."

They shook hands. Baugh rode south at an easy lope
and disappeared beyond a low rise.

That night Seigerman found a letter pushed beneath the door
of the back room. It had been written by Baugh,
signed also by Stubb and Arab, and addressed to "The
Next Sheriff of Comanche County."

It explained that each man had been called away by
urgent cattle business in a different district. Circumstances might prevent
their return before the convention, but the organization was complete,
the brands were solid, the imported vote was moving, and
victory was as certain as sunrise. The letter advised Seigerman
to remain calm, avoid unnecessary expenditures, and accept the nomination
in a spirit of modest public sacrifice.

Louie read it three times. Its first effect was uneasiness;
its second, pride. Important men were always called away upon
urgent business. If the organization was complete, their physical presence
was unnecessary. Great political machinery, once set in motion, continued
by its own weight.

For the next three weeks Seigerman waited.

No campaign funds came from the cattle companies. Baugh had
warned him of delays.

No imported voters appeared. Arab had said they would arrive
at the last moment to prevent hostile challenges.

No range delegations called upon him. Secrecy, he remembered, was
essential.

On convention morning he put on his black coat and
walked across to the court-house. The hall was filled with
farmers, merchants, town politicians, lawyers, and a scattering of cattlemen.
Tolliver's men wore blue ribbons. Mason's wore white. Old Ricks
had no ribbons, but his friends occupied two rows and
smelled confidently of his campaign.

Seigerman looked for the united livestock interests.

He recognized three cowboys. One owed him twelve dollars, one
had been barred from his hotel, and the third asked
which candidate was giving away cigars.

The convention was called to order. Committees reported, rules were
disputed, and speeches were made concerning honest government by men
who had already counted every doubtful delegate. Seigerman sat near
the front, maintaining the expression Baugh had recommended for overwhelming
favorites.

Nominations opened.

Tolliver's name was presented with loud applause. Mason's followed. Old
Ricks was nominated by a farmer who said the county
needed a man familiar with criminals, after which Ricks stood
and explained that the speaker meant he had served six
years as justice of the peace.

For one alarming minute no one nominated Seigerman.

Then Herman Voelker, a baker and old friend, rose in
the back of the hall. He praised Louie's citizenship, business
success, generosity, and excellent table. His speech contained nothing about
cattle interests, but it put Seigerman's name before the convention.

Louie waited for the brands to rise.

None rose.

The first ballot gave Tolliver forty-six votes, Mason thirty-nine, Ricks
twenty-seven, and Seigerman one.

Louie stared at the tally. It was a test, he
decided. Baugh had often said that powerful organizations concealed their
strength on the first ballot.

The second ballot gave him one.

On the third, Tolliver lost two votes to Mason, Ricks
gained one, and Seigerman retained his one with a steadiness
unmatched by any other candidate.

The fourth produced the same result.

By the fifth ballot people had begun to notice.

A delegate called across the hall, "Louie, your man's sticking."

There was laughter. Seigerman turned red but remained seated. The
imported delegation might yet arrive. He listened for boots upon
the stairs, for the commotion of four hundred lawful residents
bearing credentials. He heard only the chairman's gavel.

On the sixth ballot Mason withdrew. Most of his delegates
went to Tolliver, enough to secure the nomination. Seigerman received
one vote to the end.

No one ever learned certainly who cast it. Herman Voelker
denied doing so, saying he had nominated Louie from friendship
but voted for Tolliver from judgment. Seigerman himself swore he
had voted for Ricks because Baugh advised a candidate not
to appear vain. Several delegates claimed the honor in later
years, but only after the ballot had become famous.

That evening the back room at Seigerman's remained locked.

The three cowboys, meanwhile, were seventy miles south and riding
towards the Continental range. They had divided the last expense
money, thrown Arab's organization book into a creek, and resumed
their ordinary clothes and language.

At camp the superintendent put them back on the pay-roll.
There were calves to brand, fences to mend, and long
circles to ride. Corporate rules still forbade feeding unemployed cowboys,
but an employed man could eat all the beans he
had strength to approach.

For several weeks they said nothing of politics.

Then one evening a hand named Mace returned from Willet.
Around the fire he described the convention and Louie's single
vote on six consecutive ballots.

Arab looked offended.

"One vote? I had nine hundred and twelve positively pledged."

"You must have misplaced nine hundred and eleven," said Stubb.

Baugh turned a branding iron in the coals.

"The organization failed at the point of delivery."

"The returns were mighty small," Mace said.

"Small?" Baugh replied. "They were exact. Louie received the lowest
return possible without being entirely excluded. That is not merely
small; it is a mathematical distinction."

Stubb nodded. "Seigerman's Per Cent."

The phrase pleased the camp. Within a week it was
being used at the chuck-wagon. A horse trader who received
one lame mule in settlement for six sound ponies was
said to have collected Seigerman's Per Cent. A cook who
baked forty biscuits and found one left for himself got
Seigerman's Per Cent. When a rider courted five girls and
secured one dance with the homeliest, the outfit calculated his
reward by the same measure.

From the Continental range the expression traveled with round-ups and
trail herds. It crossed the Cimarron, went south into the
Panhandle, and moved north with beef shipments. Men who had
never seen Willet or Louie Seigerman understood it perfectly. It
meant the least result that hope, labor, capital, prophecy, and
magnificent organization could produce without returning absolute nothing.

As for Louie, he did not run for office again.
He continued to keep a good hotel, served generous meals,
and required range riders to pay in advance. Whenever cattlemen
spoke mysteriously of united interests, he reached under the bar
for a club.

Baugh, Stubb, and Arab Ab never revisited his house.

They were not ashamed, exactly. Shame was an emotion for
which winter unemployment allowed little room. They maintained that the
cattle companies had withdrawn the chuck-line, public ambition had supplied
another, and they had merely followed it until green grass
returned.

But when any one asked Baugh how close Seigerman had
come to becoming sheriff—or United States marshal—he would gaze across
the range with the solemn look of a man consulting
official returns.

"Within one vote," he would say, "of receiving none at
all."

## PHILO GUBB AND THE UN-BURGLAR

When Philo Gubb received the reward for solving the celebrated
case of the Missing Municipal Mule, he did not squander
it.

He paid all but three hundred and sixteen dollars and
forty cents of it to Syrilla's employer.

This left only seven hundred and eighty-three dollars and sixty
cents between Syrilla and freedom, because Syrilla was still known
professionally as Princess Serephina, the Magnificent Mountain of Mesopotamia, and
her contract with Professor Bologni's United Wonders required her to
remain a Magnificent Mountain until the money advanced for her
costumes, meals, lithographs, and enlarged platform had been repaid.

Syrilla was not Mesopotamian and she was not a princess.
She had been born in Keokuk, and her true ambition
was to become Mrs. Philo Gubb and keep chickens behind
a neat white cottage. Unfortunately, she weighed six hundred and
eight pounds at the last public weighing, and Professor Bologni
considered her too valuable to surrender for sentimental reasons.

Philo visited her whenever the United Wonders exhibited within reasonable
railway distance of Riverbank.

"Seven hundred and eighty-three dollars and sixty cents," he told
her on his latest visit. "That is all which intervenes
between us and the matrimonial altar."

"It sounds like plenty," said Syrilla.

"It is plenty when considered as an indebtedness," Philo admitted,
"but it is trifling when viewed as a reward."

He was thinking of the five-thousand-dollar reward offered for the
capture of the men who had dynamited the stone crusher
outside Derlingport. Philo had previously made an effort to capture
those men. The effort had not been successful. He had
captured two umbrella menders, a Methodist Sunday-school superintendent, and a
traveling salesman selling a cough cure, but none of them
had dynamited anything.

"That five thousand would set us up handsome," Syrilla said.

"It would," Philo agreed. "And it is my fixed deteckative
intention to acquire it, if the criminals will kindly remain
uncaptured until I can locate their whereabouts."

He kissed the tips of Syrilla's fingers through the bars
that separated the public from the platform. The bars were
advertised as necessary because of Princess Serephina's savage Oriental disposition,
although they were chiefly useful in preventing boys from crawling
under the platform to see how it was built.

When Philo returned to Riverbank, he resumed his paper-hanging business
and his study of Lesson Eleven of the Rising Sun
Correspondence School of Detecting. Lesson Eleven was entitled "Shadowing a
Suspect Without Appearing to Do So." It advised the student
to carry three false mustaches, two reversible coats, blue spectacles,
a limp, and a daily newspaper.

Philo had the newspaper and the limp. The limp was
not strictly false, because he had stepped off the train
onto a milk can.

He had studied half the lesson when Marshal Wittaker came
into his shop.

The marshal of Riverbank performed the duties that would have
been performed by a chief of police in a larger
city. He also collected the dog tax and rang the
fire bell when the bell ringer was at dinner.

"Gubb," he said, "we've got a crime for you."

Philo placed one long finger between the pages of his
lesson.

"Retail or wholesale?" he asked.

"What?"

"Detecting," Philo explained. "A retail deteckative matter is when one
criminal commits one crime against one party. A wholesale matter
is when numerous criminals commit numerous crimes against the public
in general, such as dynamiting, counterfeiting, or putting crooked bottoms
in berry boxes."

"This is burglarizing," said Wittaker.

"Retail," Philo decided.

"Except nobody took anything."

Philo frowned.

"A burglar entered a residence and did not take anything?"

"He left something."

Philo closed his lesson.

"That," he said, "is wholesale peculiar."

Marshal Wittaker seated himself on a roll of wallpaper and
told the circumstances. During the night someone had entered the
home of Mr. and Mrs. Amos Griscom through a cellar
window. Nothing in the house had been disturbed, but in
the morning Mrs. Griscom had found a silver spoon lying
on the parlor table. It was a handsome spoon, with
a twisted handle, and on it were engraved the words:

TO AMOS AND AMELIA
WITH ESTEEM

"Was the cellar window broke into?" Philo asked.

"Griscom says it was broke out of."

"A window can't be broke out of by a man
getting in."

"That's what I told him."

"Unless," Philo added, "the man got in first and broke
it after."

"Then he broke it out of while he was in."

"No," said Philo. "He broke it into from the inside."

Marshal Wittaker considered this.

"What we need," he said finally, "is a fellow educated
in them fine differences."

Philo stood and buttoned his coat. He put Lesson Eleven
in his pocket and selected a small notebook.

"We will examine the scene of the depredation," he said.

Amos Griscom's house stood on Elm Street, three doors beyond
the Baptist church. A knot of citizens had already gathered
at the gate. Among them was Billy Getz, the best-known
practical joker in Riverbank.

Billy's jokes were seldom cruel. They were merely frequent. He
had once painted "Post No Bills" on every hitching post
in town, and for a week no citizen had known
whether the order applied to letters, advertisements, or William McKinley
bank notes. He had also sent Marshal Wittaker a printed
notice that the government intended to replace all town marshals
with mounted cavalry.

Billy greeted Philo eagerly.

"Here comes Sherlock Gubb!" he cried. "Now we'll catch—" He
stopped and coughed. "I mean, now we'll leave the catching
entirely to you."

Philo looked at him with narrow suspicion. Billy Getz had
curly hair, bright eyes, and the expression of a boy
who knew where the fireworks were hidden.

"Why did you commence to say 'we'll catch'?" Philo asked.

"I was going to say we'll catch cold standing out
here."

"It is May."

"I catch cold independent of seasons."

Philo wrote in his notebook:

BILLY GETZ—SUSPICIOUS COLD IN MAY.

Mrs. Griscom met them in the parlor with the spoon
wrapped in a handkerchief.

"I haven't touched it since breakfast," she said.

"You touched it to wrap it," said Philo.

"I touched it before breakfast, then."

"That changes the chronological docket but not the evidence."

He examined the spoon. It was genuine silver, or at
least looked sufficiently genuine to Mrs. Griscom. Philo smelled it,
tapped it against his front tooth, measured it with a
folding rule, and held it to the light.

"Has the criminal stole any spoons from elsewhere?" he asked.

"No," said Mrs. Griscom. "He brought this one."

Philo raised a solemn finger.

"A man can tie and he can untie. He can
hitch and unhitch. He can buckle and unbuckle. Therefore, if
he can burgle, he can un-burgle. This ain't burglary. It
is un-burglary."

Billy Getz covered his mouth.

Marshal Wittaker coughed loudly.

Mrs. Griscom looked at the spoon.

"I don't see why anybody would un-burgle us," she said.

"That," said Philo, "constitutes the motive."

In the cellar he found the window open and one
pane broken. Outside lay a chicken bone, two cigar ashes,
and a crushed pansy. Philo collected the chicken bone in
an envelope. He scraped the cigar ashes into another. He
studied the pansy.

"The criminal trod here," he said.

"So did Amos," said Mrs. Griscom. "He fell into the
flower bed when he came out to look."

Philo examined Amos's trousers. Purple stains marked both knees.

"That temporarily removes the pansy evidence from the criminal docket,"
Philo said.

Billy Getz was leaning against the fence, smiling.

"What do you make of the chicken bone, Detective?" he
asked.

"It came off a chicken."

Billy's smile weakened.

"How do you know?"

"The Rising Sun lesson on Bone Identification says a chicken
bone can usually be identified by being the bone of
a chicken."

"You've got him there, Billy," said Marshal Wittaker.

Philo returned to his shop convinced that Billy Getz was
the un-burglar. Billy had been too interested. He had almost
said, "We'll catch—" and the chicken bone might have come
from Billy's dinner.

The next morning, however, Marshal Wittaker returned with news of
a second un-burglary.

This time the victim was Attorney Medderbrook, who had discovered
a silver spoon on his desk. It was engraved:

TO LUCIUS MEDDERBROOK
AN ORNAMENT TO THE BAR

"Did the criminal enter through a cellar window?" Philo asked.

"He entered through the pantry," said Wittaker. "Or un-entered. I
ain't got your education in this sort of crime."

Philo inspected Medderbrook's home. A pantry window had been pried
open, a jar of pickles overturned, and a sack of
flour disturbed. On the floor lay a long brass tube
with a rubber bulb at one end.

Medderbrook, a short, stout man with a voice like water
going down a narrow drain, gurgled indignantly.

"It is perfectly worthless!" he said.

"Utterly hopeless," corrected his sister. "You always say perfectly worthless
when you mean utterly hopeless."

"I mean both!" Medderbrook gurgled. "A man enters my home,
spills my pickles, scatters flour, and gives me a spoon!
What sort of criminal is that?"

Philo lifted the brass tube.

"This may be a burglarious weapon."

"It's a lung tester," said Billy Getz, who had arrived
before the marshal. "You blow into it and the little
pointer tells how much lung you've got."

Philo placed the tube to his lips and blew.

A charge of flour shot from the other end and
covered Billy's face.

For a moment there was silence.

Then Marshal Wittaker sat down hard on a kitchen chair
and laughed.

Billy wiped flour from his eyebrows.

"That's a new model," he said.

"I thought you claimed it was a lung tester," Philo
said.

"It tested your lungs."

"It also tested your complexion," said Wittaker.

Philo made another entry:

BILLY GETZ—FAMILIAR WITH CONCEALED FLOUR APPARATUS.

The Riverbank Eagle printed an account of the two crimes
under the heading SILVER-SPOON FIEND AT LARGE. By noon, half
the town was locking its windows, while the other half
left windows open in the hope that the fiend might
consider them prominent enough to receive silverware.

At two o'clock Mrs. Millbrook rushed into Philo's shop. Mrs.
Millbrook was round, breathless, and unable to speak without seeming
to gurgle.

"He's done it!" she gurgled.

"Who?" asked Philo.

"Him!"

"What?"

"It!"

"Where?"

"Our house!"

This cross-examination exhausted her, and she sat on the paste
table until able to explain. Someone had entered the Millbrook
residence during the family's absence and left four engraved teaspoons
in the dining room. The inscription read:

TO THE MILLBROOK FAMILY
FOR SOCIAL MERIT

"Four!" cried Marshal Wittaker when he heard. "The fellow's getting
extravagant."

"He is proceeding from retail to wholesale," Philo said gravely.
"Unless checked, he may ruin himself."

That possibility genuinely concerned him. A criminal who stole was
bad enough, but a criminal who gave away silver might
eventually become a public charge.

At the Millbrook house, Philo discovered a muddy heel mark
below the pantry window and another crushed flower bed. The
flowers were yellow snapdragons. Yellow stains appeared on Billy Getz's
right trouser leg.

Philo's heart beat faster.

"How come them stains?" he demanded.

Billy looked down.

"I fell into Mrs. Millbrook's flowers when I heard she'd
been un-burgled."

"You also happened to be present at Griscom's and Medderbrook's."

"I'm interested in crime."

"You are interested previous to everybody else."

"Maybe I read fast."

Philo pointed an accusing finger.

"Mr. Getz, the deteckative eye is upon you."

"Tell it not to wink," Billy said.

Nevertheless, Philo was not satisfied. The Rising Sun course warned
that the obvious suspect was seldom guilty unless he was,
and that a detective should seek corroborative evidence by inductive,
deductive, and seductive methods. The meaning of the third had
not been explained, so Philo decided to employ the first
two.

He spent the afternoon learning where silver spoons had recently
been sold.

The jeweler had sold none of the engraved kind. The
secondhand store had bought six from a riverboat cook and
sold them to an elderly man who wore a black
coat and kept his hat pulled low. The proprietor could
not identify him because the man had paid cash, and
cash, in the proprietor's experience, made most customers look alike.

Philo considered elderly men in black coats.

Riverbank had twenty-seven.

Fourteen were eliminated because they did not have enough money
to buy silver spoons. Eight were eliminated because they were
ministers and would probably have delivered the spoons through the
front door. Three were bedridden. One had gone to Davenport.

The remaining man was John Westcote.

John Westcote was the richest and stingiest citizen in Riverbank.
He had once walked three blocks in the rain rather
than pay a nickel for a streetcar. He owned houses,
grain shares, mortgages, and enough unused collar buttons to supply
the town. His eyesight was poor, his back was bent,
and he moved with a tottering gait, but his reputation
for meanness stood perfectly upright.

Philo went to Westcote's house.

The old man opened the door three inches.

"What do you want?"

"Information in relation to silver spoons."

"I haven't any."

"Did you purchase six?"

"No."

"Did you not give Mr. and Mrs. Griscom a wedding
present?"

"No."

"Did you not give Attorney Medderbrook a wedding present?"

"He never married."

"That saved you expense," said Philo. "Did you give the
Millbrooks a wedding present?"

"No."

"When these various parties got married or became social ornaments,
you neglected the customary bestowal of silver?"

"I neglect everything customary if it costs money."

Philo's eyes shone. Here was a motive of beautiful proportions.

John Westcote had grown old. His conscience, dormant for years,
had awakened. Ashamed to confess publicly that he had failed
to give wedding presents, he was secretly entering homes and
depositing silverware. It was senile restitution, aggravated by remorse.

"Mr. Westcote," Philo said kindly, "it ain't too late to
reform without breaking windows."

Westcote slammed the door.

As Philo turned away, he noticed purple and yellow stains
on the old man's trousers.

The purple might have come from the Griscom pansies. The
yellow might have come from the Millbrook snapdragons. Philo's theory
became almost painfully complete.

He hurried to Marshal Wittaker's office. Billy Getz was there,
sitting with his feet on the stove.

"The case is practically solved," Philo announced.

Billy dropped his feet.

"Not already!"

"I have located the guilty conscience."

"You mean the guilty party?"

"The party and conscience are combined in John Westcote."

Marshal Wittaker stared.

"Old Westcote? He wouldn't give away a bent pin."

"Exactly," said Philo. "He has been withholding wedding presents for
forty years. His internal remorse has fermented. He is now
paying them under cover of darkness."

Billy swallowed a laugh.

"Philo," he said, "that's wonderful."

"It is inductive."

"It is more than that."

"Do you know anything about it?"

"Me? No. Nothing whatever." Billy rose. "And if I were
you, I'd shadow Westcote tonight. He may be planning a
big delivery."

Philo looked sharply at him.

"Why should you suggest that?"

"Professional admiration."

The false mustaches in Philo's pocket seemed suddenly heavy. He
had brought them in accordance with Lesson Eleven. Billy Getz
was urging him to follow Westcote because Billy expected him
to look foolish. That was evident. It was also evident
that Billy knew more than he admitted.

Philo therefore decided to follow Westcote.

He selected a black mustache, although he already had one
of his own, because the lesson said a disguise altered
a man's appearance. He pasted the false mustache above his
natural mustache, put on blue spectacles, turned his coat inside
out, and went to wait behind the hedge opposite Westcote's
house.

At seven-thirty, John Westcote came out carrying a newspaper-wrapped bundle.

Philo's confidence rose.

The old man looked up and down the street, then
set off toward the eastern end of town. Philo followed
according to correspondence-school rules: twenty paces behind on open streets,
ten paces behind in crowds, and out of sight whenever
the suspect looked backward.

There were no crowds. There was only Mrs. Purdy's goat,
which followed Philo because it liked the taste of the
lining of his reversed coat.

Westcote turned.

Philo stepped behind a maple tree. The goat remained visible,
chewing his coattail.

Westcote continued.

Philo continued, dragging the goat until the lining tore. He
abandoned the reversed-coat portion of the disguise and followed in
his shirtsleeves.

The old man passed the gasworks, crossed the creek bridge,
and entered the hazel brush beyond the town limits. Here
he stopped beside a stump, set down the newspaper bundle,
and moved away rapidly.

Philo waited.

Two men emerged from the brush. They did not resemble
persons collecting neglected wedding presents. One was broad and heavy,
with a cap pulled over his eyes. The other had
a narrow face and a scar across his cheek. The
broad man picked up the bundle.

"Count it," said the narrow one.

The pair moved deeper into the brush.

Philo followed.

The men sat behind a fallen tree and opened the
package. Inside were bank notes, which the broad man counted
by the light of a shaded lantern.

"One thousand," he said.

"He paid, then."

"He'd pay twice that rather than have his mills touched."

"He'll pay again."

The narrow-faced man laughed.

"After the Derlingport crusher job, these old moneybags know we
mean business. We'll send him another letter next week. If
he squeals, we'll put enough powder under his lumber mill
to scatter boards from here to Missouri."

Philo crouched behind a hazel bush, hardly breathing.

The Derlingport stone crusher!

These were not un-burglars. They were dynamiters—wholesale criminals of the
five-thousand-dollar variety.

His original reasoning had not been wrong, Philo told himself.
It had merely arrived at a different crime than the
one it had started toward.

The men wrapped the money again and moved off through
the brush. Philo followed. The route led behind Westcote's lumberyard,
across a vacant lot, and into the railway yards. A
freight train stood on a siding, its locomotive breathing patiently
in the darkness.

The men approached an empty boxcar.

A voice from within said, "That you, Jake?"

"It's us."

"Got the cash?"

"All of it."

The two climbed into the car.

Philo crept near and placed his ear against the wooden
side. Through a crack he saw three men seated on
nail kegs. One held the lantern. Another had a revolver
across his knees.

"He paid easy," said Jake.

"He'll pay easier next time," said the man with the
revolver. "We'll hit the grain elevator if he don't."

"What about the other three?"

"They're waiting near Blandon. We meet them tomorrow. Then we
split the money and make for Chicago."

"How soon does this car move?"

"Any minute."

Philo withdrew his ear.

There were five men in the car, three accomplices near
Blandon, and a freight train about to move. The usual
detective might have summoned help. Philo was not near any
help. He might have entered the car and arrested the
men, but five dynamiters and one revolver presented an unfavorable
retail proportion.

He examined the boxcar door.

It slid on an iron track. A hasp hung beside
it. The men had left the door open for air.

Philo removed his shoes, because Lesson Eleven stated that rubber-soled
footwear was best for silent work and stockings were second
best. He crept forward, seized the door, and slid it
shut.

A shout came from inside.

Philo dropped the hasp and thrust his steel paper-hanger's scraper
through it in place of a padlock.

The door shook.

"Who's there?"

Philo looked about. The train jerked.

The boxcar began to move.

Philo had intended to remain behind and notify Marshal Wittaker
by telephone. Unfortunately, his scraper was his best tool, and
the shaking door threatened to work it loose. He seized
the ladder on the end of the car and climbed.

The train gathered speed.

Inside, the dynamiters hammered at the door and used language
not included in the Rising Sun course.

Philo clung to the ladder as the railway yards slid
past. The lights of Riverbank vanished. Wind pulled at his
false mustache, and presently it departed into the night. His
natural mustache remained, proving its superior attachment.

At the first grade crossing, Philo shouted to the watchman,
but the watchman thought he was a tramp and advised
him to get off. At the second crossing there was
no watchman. At the third, the train was moving too
fast for conversation.

The door continued to shake.

Philo climbed to the roof, crawled forward over three cars,
and reached the caboose. The conductor discovered him entering through
the rear door without shoes.

"Where did you come from?" the conductor demanded.

"Riverbank."

"Where are your shoes?"

"Following a separate deteckative career."

Philo explained.

The conductor did not believe him until they walked forward
along the train at the next stop and heard the
prisoners threatening to blow up the entire railway if they
were not released.

"That sounds like dynamiters," the conductor admitted.

At the town of Cresswell, the conductor telegraphed the sheriff.
Twelve armed men surrounded the car. The scraper was removed,
the door was opened, and the five prisoners were invited
to emerge with their hands high.

They emerged.

By noon the next day, one had confessed. By evening,
the three accomplices near Blandon had been arrested. A telegram
was sent to Riverbank, but Philo remained in Cresswell to
identify the men and to make a statement twelve pages
long, including his theory concerning senile wedding restitution.

Meanwhile, Riverbank experienced another un-burglary.

Judge Purcell returned from court to discover a silver soup
ladle in his piano. It was engraved:

TO JUDGE PURCELL
HOPING TO BE NOTICED

The judge was not pleased. He organized the marshal, two
deputies, Amos Griscom, and a night watchman into a trap.

That night they hid inside the home of Mayor Higby,
whose prominence made him a likely recipient. At eleven-fifteen, a
cellar window opened. A thin, shabby young man crawled through,
carrying a package.

He tiptoed into the dining room, unwrapped a silver gravy
boat, and set it on the table.

Marshal Wittaker lit the lamp.

"Hold on there!"

The young man threw up his hands and smiled.

"Is this the party?" he asked.

"What party?"

"The party you're going to invite me to."

No one knew his name at first. He was a
harmless, simple-minded fellow who slept in an abandoned shed near
the river and earned occasional pennies carrying parcels. He explained
that he had dreamed prominent families were giving dinners. In
the dream, anyone who presented them with silverware was invited.

"I gave them spoons," he said, "so they'd know I
was sociable."

"Where'd you get the silver?" asked Wittaker.

"Found money in an old boot under the shed."

It was later learned that the money had belonged to
a tramp imprisoned in Davenport. The silver had been purchased
honestly, the engraving paid for honestly, and the entering of
houses done with no intention beyond establishing social relations.

"You could have knocked at the doors," Marshal Wittaker said.

"They might've said no."

"That's so," Billy Getz admitted. "Riverbank society is particular."

The young man was given supper and placed under the
care of the county. Thus the un-burglary case ended without
the aid of Philo Gubb.

Billy was in Marshal Wittaker's office the next morning when
the telegram arrived.

Wittaker opened it and read:

HAVE FIVE DERLINGPORT DYNAMITERS IN CUSTODY. THREE MORE TAKEN AT
BLANDON ON CONFESSION. PHILO GUBB RESPONSIBLE FOR CAPTURE. REWARD FIVE
THOUSAND PAYABLE ACCORDING TO TERMS. EXPECT HIM HOME THURSDAY.

Marshal Wittaker read it twice.

Billy took the telegram and read it once.

"He caught them?" Billy asked faintly.

"Five in a boxcar."

"And three more?"

"On confession."

"And the reward?"

"Five thousand."

Billy sat down.

The room was very quiet.

At last Marshal Wittaker said, "You got him started on
Westcote."

"I did."

"You stained your trousers in them flower beds on purpose."

"I did."

"You put the flour-shooter in Medderbrook's pantry."

Billy sighed.

"I did. I heard about the first spoon, and I
thought it would be funny to add some clues and
watch Philo investigate. Old Westcote really did fall into both
flower beds because I told him somebody had dropped a
dime in each one. I figured Philo would follow him
all night and look foolish."

"He followed him."

"Yes."

"And caught eight dynamiters."

"Yes."

Marshal Wittaker leaned back and stared at the ceiling.

"It don't seem right," he said.

"No."

"He was wrong about you."

"Yes."

"He was wrong about Westcote giving wedding presents."

"Yes."

"He was wrong about the spoons."

"Yes."

"He was wrong all the way through."

"Entirely."

"And he gets five thousand dollars."

Billy folded the telegram carefully and handed it back.

"How much does he still owe on Syrilla?" he asked.

"Seven hundred and something."

"So he can marry her."

"Looks that way."

"And have over four thousand left."

"Looks that way."

Billy rose and put on his hat.

"I feel bilious," he said.

Marshal Wittaker nodded.

"I feel worse than that."

"What do you feel?"

"Utterly hopeless."

Billy paused at the door.

"You mean perfectly worthless."

"I mean both," said Marshal Wittaker.

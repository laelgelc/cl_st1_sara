# THE TWO POTATOES

## I

On the afternoon of Monday, the twelfth of January, at
precisely sixteen minutes past five, Gilbert Swann departed from his
father's house in Bleakridge with a violoncello, a dress-suit, two
white collars, a music-case, and the conviction—modestly concealed—that the eyes
of the Five Towns were upon him.

It was a dramatic moment in the history of local
musical enterprise. The fact that only Gilbert and his mother
fully comprehended its dramatic character does not lessen its importance;
many of the most dramatic moments in history have occurred
without attracting the least attention from neighbours.

"Mind the step!" cried Mrs Swann.

"I can see it, mother."

"And don't let that cello knock against the gate-post."

"I shan't."

"You nearly did."

"I didn't."

The violoncello, which had been carried from the dining-room by
Gilbert, through the narrow lobby, and down the two stone
steps into a fine freezing dusk, now reposed in its
green-baize case against the garden wall. It was fragile as
a lady, cumbersome as a wardrobe, and worth more than
anything else in the house except the house itself. Mrs
Swann regarded it accordingly. Gilbert, having reached the age at
which a young man must appear indifferent to treasures for
which his parents have paid, regarded it with professional calm.

At the gate waited a cab—one of those enclosed, black,
solemn vehicles which seem designed equally for weddings, funerals, and
the transportation of musicians. The driver had twice struck his
arms across his chest and once remarked that it was
weather for brass monkeys. Mrs Swann had ignored him. Mr
Swann, standing behind her in the lobby, had said, "Ay,"
which was his customary contribution to observations meteorological, political, domestic,
or religious.

"You've got your money?" Mrs Swann demanded.

"Yes."

"Your handkerchiefs?"

"Yes."

"Your invitation?"

"Yes."

"Your extra strings?"

"Yes."

"Your common sense?"

Gilbert smiled. "I was wondering where I'd put it."

"None of your cleverness! Up with ye!"

The driver took the cello and arranged it beside Gilbert
with a care induced less by reverence for music than
by Mrs Swann's gaze. Gilbert entered. The door shut. The
horse moved. One wheel gave a protesting squeak; the lamps
shone dimly through the blue air; and the cab rolled
toward Hanbridge, capital—if any town could claim that perilous distinction—of
the Five Towns.

"He's gone!" said Mrs Swann.

"Ay," said Mr Swann.

"The boy's gone!"

"Ay."

"And everything depends on to-night."

Mr Swann looked at her, but not argumentatively. He had
learned, during twenty-four years of marriage, that an event which
to him merely happened might to his wife constitute the
crisis of an epoch. He therefore said:

"He'll do all right."

"Do all right!" she repeated. "Of course he'll do all
right. The question is whether the others will."

The cab vanished at the corner where the electric tram-lines
glittered under the frost. A tram from Bursley came grinding
up the slope, blue fire leaping at its overhead contact;
it carried forty-two passengers, none of whom knew that Gilbert
Swann had gone forth to sustain the artistic credit of
the district. Such is fame before it has been organized.

Mrs Swann remained at the gate until the tram had
passed.

"Come in, Sarah," said her husband. "You'll catch your death."

"I shall do no such thing."

Nevertheless she came in.

## II

The inaugural Musical Festival of the Five Towns had begun,
like many large undertakings, in a room over a shop.

Two years earlier, Mr Josiah Brough, tile manufacturer, alderman, Sunday-school
treasurer, and possessor of a bass voice which had once
been praised in the *Staffordshire Signal*, had observed at a
supper of the Hanbridge Choral Union that Birmingham had a
festival, Leeds had a festival, Norwich had a festival, and
even Hereford—Hereford!—had a festival. There followed one of those silences
in which a civilization examines its deficiencies.

"What's wrong with us?" Mr Brough had demanded.

Nobody knew. The Five Towns produced plates for Persia, tea-services
for Toronto, wash-basins for Buenos Aires, encaustic tiles for emperors,
and sanitary conveniences for the most delicate populations of Europe.
They had chapels, institutes, free libraries, two evening newspapers, an
art school, three rival systems of tramway, and a covered
market with a clock which lost no more than nine
minutes a week. Yet they had no musical festival.

The shame of this omission grew rapidly. Within a fortnight
it had spread from Mr Brough to the mayors; from
the mayors to the manufacturers; from the manufacturers to their
wives; and from their wives to every person in the
district capable of pronouncing the word "oratorio." Committees were formed.
Sub-committees were selected from the committees, and an executive committee
from the sub-committees. Guarantees were guaranteed. Circulars were circulated. A
seal was designed. A London conductor of formidable renown consented,
for a formidable fee, to direct the proceedings. The press
announced that the Five Towns had at last taken its
proper position in the musical life of the nation.

The festival was to endure three days in the Hanbridge
Municipal Hall, a building remarkable for a noble façade, an
inadequate cloakroom, and the absence of boxes. Nevertheless, the "box-plan"
was opened at nine o'clock on a Thursday morning, and
at seven minutes past nine the first citizen demanded to
know why the best boxes had been reserved.

There were to be morning rehearsals, afternoon concerts, evening concerts,
a civic luncheon, and a reception to the London artists.
The programmes included Bach, Handel, Beethoven, Mendelssohn, and a new
cantata by a local solicitor whose friends spoke warmly of
its devotional spirit. The combined choirs of Hanbridge, Bursley, Knype,
Longshaw, and Turnhill—eight hundred voices after deductions for influenza, disagreement,
and matrimony—would support the orchestra. Violins came from Manchester; oboes
from Liverpool; trumpets, trombones, and a harp from London. The
Five Towns supplied the refreshments, the contraltos, and Gilbert Swann.

It is possible that the Five Towns supplied other instrumentalists
also. Mrs Swann admitted as much when pressed. But none
had been selected at nineteen years of age to share
a desk with Mr Anton Fisch, the celebrated violoncellist; none
had been personally complimented by Dr Grunwald, the conductor; and
none had been invited to dine, before the opening concert,
at the residence of Mrs Clayton Vernon.

Mrs Clayton Vernon was patroness of the festival—not the sole
patroness, for the list included a duchess who had never
entered Staffordshire and an Italian princess who withdrew her name
on discovering that the journey from London occupied more than
two hours; but the active, visible, and indubitable patroness. She
possessed money, a carriage, a lorgnon, a husband who had
represented Hanbridge in Parliament for eleven months, and the rare
art of making local celebrities feel that London had heard
of them.

When, therefore, an envelope bearing the Clayton Vernon crest arrived
at the Swanns' house and requested the pleasure of Mr
Gilbert Swann's company at dinner on Monday, Mrs Swann understood
the significance of the document instantly.

"They've found out," she said.

"Found out what?" asked Mr Swann.

"What he is."

Mr Swann considered.

"Ay," he said.

Gilbert explained that Dr Grunwald and several principal players were
dining there, and that he had probably been invited merely
for convenience, since the orchestra was due at the hall
by seven-thirty.

"Merely!" exclaimed Mrs Swann. "They don't invite people merely. Not
people like Mrs Clayton Vernon."

Gilbert did not ask what sort of people were invited
merely. He had begun to perceive that silence, though less
celebrated than harmony, was a valuable branch of music.

## III

Gilbert's genius had first declared itself at the age of
four, when he extracted "Rock of Ages" from a toy
harmonium with one finger. Mrs Swann said that nobody had
shown him the notes; and perhaps nobody had. At six
he could sing in tune, a feat uncommon among children
and not universal among adults. At eight he played the
piano at a Band of Hope entertainment, and at ten
he accompanied his father in "The Lost Chord" without losing
it.

Mr Swann, who managed the decorating department of a prosperous
earthenware manufactory, had intended Gilbert for the works. He did
not insist upon this intention. Mr Swann rarely insisted. When
his wife announced that the child was musical, he replied,
"Seems so"; when she announced that the child required lessons,
he paid for them; and when she announced, four years
later, that the pianoforte was not his true instrument, he
asked what the true one cost.

The true instrument was the violoncello.

"Why that great thing?" he had ventured.

"Because Mr Dimmock says his hands are made for it."

Mr Swann looked at Gilbert's hands, then at his own.

"Same shape as mine."

"Shape!" cried Mrs Swann. "It isn't shape. It's formation."

The cello was purchased second-hand from a German in Manchester
for thirty-eight pounds, a sum which Mrs Swann subsequently described
as both ruinous and ridiculously cheap. Gilbert embraced the instrument
with his knees and his destiny with composure. He practised
scales while the street played football; he practised studies while
his contemporaries pursued young women round the skating-rink; he travelled
twice weekly by train to Manchester, carrying the cello with
a firmness that compelled porters to respect it; and at
seventeen he won a scholarship at the North Staffordshire Academy
of Music.

He had, moreover, the useful kind of talent which survives
instruction. He listened. He worked. He did not grow pale
and interesting, compose pessimistic sonatas, or demand that the family
should live in whispers. At breakfast he ate bacon. At
dinner he ate pudding. He polished his own boots. These
proofs of sanity greatly strengthened his mother's faith in his
genius, for Mrs Swann had no patience with genius unless
it behaved itself.

By nineteen Gilbert's name had appeared eleven times in print.
On three occasions it was spelt correctly. He had played
at charity concerts, chapel anniversaries, teachers' meetings, subscription recitals, and
an entertainment in aid of the Hanbridge Dogs' Home at
which a terrier barked throughout his slow movement. Dr Grunwald
had heard him in Manchester and had said: "You haf
a good tone, young man. Continue." Gilbert continued. Mrs Swann,
informed of the remark, regarded it as a European verdict.

Then came the festival appointment.

There were persons who said that Gilbert had been chosen
because the committee wished to include at least one local
player. Mrs Swann said that jealousy was the tax paid
by merit. There were others who said that Dr Grunwald
had engaged him chiefly because a London cellist had fallen
ill. Mrs Swann said that Providence often worked through influenza.
In her own mind the festival was a splendid pyramid—choirs,
orchestra, conductor, patrons, civic dignitaries, printed programmes, electric light, and
floral decorations rising tier above tier—and Gilbert was the concealed
stone without which the edifice would fall sideways into ignominy.

Gilbert himself had no such illusion. He knew that he
was to sit among seven other cellists, turn pages when
required, count bars accurately, and obey the stick. But he
loved the huge organized sound already existing in his imagination;
he desired to enter it, to serve it, to become
for three days one disciplined fraction of a majestic whole.
This desire was more serious than vanity and therefore much
harder for his mother to comprehend.

She loved music; but she loved Gilbert more, and consequently
heard all music as an accompaniment to him.

## IV

At six o'clock, while Mr Swann was eating bread and
cheese with pickled onions, his wife suddenly put down the
teapot.

"His hands!" she said.

Mr Swann glanced at his own, which were engaged with
an onion.

"What about them?"

"This frost."

"Ay. Sharp."

"He'll come out of that hot dining-room, and then he'll
have to cross to the hall. His fingers'll stiffen before
he gets there."

"He'll have gloves."

"Gloves!" said Mrs Swann, as if gloves had just been
exposed as an imposture. "What use are gloves in weather
like this?"

Mr Swann, who had worn gloves for thirty winters without
raising the question, made no answer.

"I ought to have thought of it before he went."

"You thought of most things."

"Most isn't all."

She rose. Once risen under the influence of an idea,
Mrs Swann never sat down until the idea had been
subdued, executed, or proved physically impossible. She opened the oven
door. Two large potatoes, baked for supper in their jackets,
lay among the ashes.

"Hot potatoes," she announced.

"For him?"

"One in each hand. There's nothing better. My mother always
used to give us hot potatoes when we went skating."

"He mayn't be going skating."

"Oh, you!" said Mrs Swann.

She selected the two finest specimens with the fire-tongs, wrapped
each in a square of flannel, and considered them. They
were exceedingly hot, admirably solid, and of a size to
reassure maternity. At once the problem arose of transportation. A
basket would advertise the errand; a parcel would cool too
quickly; her pockets were unequal to the weight. Finally she
placed both potatoes in her large brown muff.

The muff had been bought twelve years before at a
winter sale and had preserved its exterior magnificence while losing
much of its internal fur. It swallowed the potatoes with
dignity. Mrs Swann inserted her hands experimentally and withdrew them
with speed.

"They'll keep him warm till the concert starts."

"Likely."

"I'll take them over."

Mr Swann stopped eating. "To Mrs Vernon's?"

"Why not?"

"No reason."

This reply irritated her.

"We kneel down to nobody, Tom Swann. Nobody! Mrs Clayton
Vernon may have a bigger house than ours, but she
can't make a better son."

"No," said Mr Swann. "I doubt she can't."

"She hasn't any son."

"That settles it."

Mrs Swann put on her black bonnet, her sealskin cape,
and the muff. She looked imposing, though a faint scent
of baked potato already competed with the lavender in her
raiment.

"Don't wait supper," she commanded.

"No."

"And don't eat the other pickle. You know what they
do to you."

Mr Swann looked at the remaining onion.

"No," he said.

She departed, and thirty seconds afterward he ate it.

The tram journey from Bleakridge to Pirehill occupied nine minutes
in theory and fourteen in fact. Mrs Swann sat upright
with the muff upon her lap, guarding its contents from
shock, draught, and inquiry. Every passenger had the air of
knowing that she carried vegetables in a fur receptacle. Nobody
did know. Human beings on public conveyances are seldom interested
in our secret burdens; but this truth, though proved hourly
throughout the civilized world, has never consoled a bearer of
secret burdens.

At Crown Square she descended. Snow, fine as powdered flint,
was beginning to fall. The Clayton Vernon residence stood half
a mile away on the Pirehill Road, amid detached villas
whose names—The Cedars, Roseneath, Belle Vue, The Laurels—compensated for the
absence of cedar, rose, view, and laurel. Mrs Swann walked
rapidly. The potatoes rolled against one another inside the muff
like conspirators.

By the time she reached the iron gates she had
decided upon her speech.

"I happened to be passing," she would say, "and I
brought these for Gilbert's hands."

This was weak. She had not happened to be passing.
The Clayton Vernons' house was not on the way to
any place that a respectable woman might plausibly visit on
a freezing Monday evening.

"My husband asked me to bring them."

That was untrue and cowardly.

"Musicians always use hot potatoes."

Did they? She did not know.

At the front door, beneath an electric lamp of intimidating
brilliance, she abandoned words altogether and rang.

## V

The door was opened by a man in a striped
waistcoat whose shirt-front appeared to have been constructed from glazed
earthenware. He looked first at Mrs Swann's face, then—almost imperceptibly—at
the muff.

"Is Mr Gilbert Swann here?" she asked.

"Yes, madam."

"I'm his mother."

The servant accepted this biographical statement without surprise.

"Will you step in, madam?"

"I don't want to disturb him."

"Dinner is not yet served."

"That's why I don't want to disturb him."

The servant stood aside. There are invitations which operate as
commands. Mrs Swann entered.

The hall was large, hot, silent, and furnished with objects
which served no purpose discoverable to common sense: a bronze
boy holding an empty tray; a chair too delicate to
sit upon; a table too crowded to put anything upon;
a blue vase containing peacock feathers; and an enormous gong,
which appeared capable of summoning the dead from Turnhill Cemetery.
The floor was covered with pale carpet.

Mrs Swann at once regretted the snow upon her boots.

"Whom shall I say, madam?"

"Mrs Swann. But don't say anything. I only wish to
speak to my son for half a minute."

"I will inform Mrs Clayton Vernon."

"No! There's no need—"

He had gone.

It is a curious fact that women, on being left
alone in a strange hall, invariably look first toward the
door by which they entered and then toward the door
by which the servant vanished. Philosophers with common sense will
explain this by saying that there are only two ways
to look. Philosophers are often astonishing.

Mrs Swann looked both ways. She drew her hands from
the muff. The heat was becoming painful. Steam, or what
she imagined to be steam, rose faintly from the fur.
She pressed the muff against her cape.

From beyond a door came voices: a deep foreign voice,
Gilbert's laugh, and the bright sound of glass. Her heart
expanded. He was in there among them—among renowned persons, dining
under electric chandeliers, discussing Bach and London and perhaps Berlin;
and she, Sarah Swann, had produced him.

Then she became conscious that one of the potatoes was
softening.

It had been baked too long on one side. Under
pressure from her left wrist it yielded slightly. Mrs Swann
relaxed her hold. The potato, once encouraged to yield, yielded
further. A hot pulpy sensation spread across her palm.

"My word!" she whispered.

She attempted, without removing the muff, to turn the potato
round. The manoeuvre was a mistake. Her thumb entered it.
The jacket split. Floury fragments detached themselves and invaded the
fur.

A horrible sticky mess!

At that moment the inner door opened, and Mrs Clayton
Vernon came forth.

She did not walk rapidly; she advanced with the serene
momentum of a battleship entering a neutral harbour. Her gown
was of dark crimson silk, her shoulders bore lace, and
at her breast glittered a small diamond ornament which Mrs
Swann, despite anguish, valued at not less than seventy pounds.
A gold lorgnon hung from a chain. Her grey hair
had been commanded into shining waves. Behind her, at a
tactful distance, followed the servant.

"My dear Mrs Swann!" she said. "How very delightful!"

Mrs Swann had met her twice: once at a subscription
concert, where they had exchanged six words, and once in
the corridor of the Municipal Hall, where they had exchanged
none. The phrase "my dear" therefore marked not affection but
social competence.

"I just called," said Mrs Swann.

"How kind! Do come into the drawing-room."

"Oh no, thank you. I'm not stopping."

"You must."

"I can't."

"But you are frozen."

"I'm quite warm."

Which she was, particularly in the left hand.

Mrs Clayton Vernon smiled with a benevolence that permitted no
resistance.

"You have come to see your talented son, of course.
We are so proud to have him with us."

"It's only for a moment."

"Then you shall have your moment in comfort."

Mrs Swann was borne into the drawing-room.

If the hall had been hot, the drawing-room was tropical.
A coal fire blazed between marble pillars. Lamps glowed under
rose-coloured shades. On little tables stood books whose leaves had
never been cut, silver boxes whose contents were mysterious, and
photographs of eminent persons who had written across their own
shirt-fronts. Mrs Swann sat on the edge of a brocaded
chair, holding the muff rigidly before her.

Mrs Clayton Vernon sat opposite and lifted her lorgnon, not
because she needed to see Mrs Swann but because the
lorgnon completed attention.

"And how proud you must be!" she said.

Mrs Swann's natural answer was: "I am." But the crushed
potato had now reached the web between her fingers.

"He's a good lad," she replied weakly.

"A most gifted young artist. Dr Grunwald spoke of him
at luncheon."

"Did he?"

"He said your son possessed unusual promise."

Mrs Swann forgot the potato for half a second.

"What exactly did he say?"

Mrs Clayton Vernon reflected.

"I think those were his exact words."

"Unusual promise?"

"Yes."

"Not merely promise?"

"Unusual promise."

Mrs Swann breathed. Here was a fresh European verdict, delivered
at luncheon and authenticated by a patroness. The evening had
already justified itself.

"And he is so young," Mrs Clayton Vernon continued. "Nineteen,
is he not?"

"Nineteen last October."

"Extraordinary."

"He began at four."

"The violoncello?"

"Music," said Mrs Swann, unwilling to suggest that a four-year-old
had embraced an instrument twice his height.

"How fascinating! You must tell me all about it."

This threat recalled her to danger.

"I really mustn't stop."

"But surely you will have coffee after dinner?"

"No, thank you."

"Then a glass of sherry now?"

"No, thank you."

"Tea?"

"No."

"Something hot?"

At the word "hot," Mrs Swann's fingers involuntarily tightened. The
potato gave a noiseless collapse. Warm pulp oozed through a
seam in the muff and adhered to the palm of
her right glove.

Mrs Clayton Vernon's eyes descended.

"Is anything the matter?"

"No."

"Your muff—"

"It's an old muff."

Another untruth; it was old, but its age was not
the matter.

Mrs Swann rose.

"I only wanted Gilbert."

"I will fetch him myself."

"Please don't trouble."

"No trouble at all."

Mrs Clayton Vernon swept out.

The instant the door closed, Mrs Swann plunged both hands
into the muff and attempted a rescue. She seized the
ruined potato, but only half came forth. It emerged pale,
steaming, and shameful, with brown jacket hanging from it in
strips. She stared at it. There was no receptacle in
the room remotely suited to the concealment of half a
baked potato. A porcelain bowl stood on the nearest table,
but contained floating blossoms. A silver basket held visiting-cards. The
coal-scuttle was too exposed. The waste-paper basket was of open
wickerwork.

Behind the fire-screen! There lay salvation.

She crossed swiftly to the hearth, bent, and thrust the
fragment behind the brass curb. As she did so, three
smaller pieces fell from her glove upon the carpet.

One she picked up.

The second she picked up.

The third had vanished.

Mrs Swann dropped to one knee and searched. Her bonnet
tilted. The muff exhaled steam. She saw the missing fragment
beneath a delicate chair and reached for it just as
the door opened.

## VI

Gilbert entered first, followed by Mrs Clayton Vernon.

"Mother!"

Mrs Swann rose with a speed remarkable in a woman
encumbered by cape, bonnet, muff, and guilt.

"I dropped—" she began.

What had she dropped? Not a potato. Never a potato.

"—my pin."

"Your pin?" said Gilbert.

"It's here now."

Which it was not.

Mrs Clayton Vernon looked toward the carpet. Mrs Swann planted
her right foot over the fragment.

"I told them I shouldn't keep you," she said to
Gilbert. "I was passing."

Gilbert glanced at her. He knew the geography of the
Five Towns and was aware that she could not have
been passing Pirehill Road without having first undertaken an expedition.
But he had inherited from his father the capacity not
to insist.

"You didn't walk?"

"Of course not. Tram."

"What did you want?"

"Nothing."

"You came all this way for nothing?"

"I came to see that you were all right."

"I'm all right."

"I can see."

There was a pause. Mrs Swann's right boot remained fixed
upon the carpet. Beneath it, the potato fragment changed shape.

Mrs Clayton Vernon smiled. "Your mother is naturally anxious on
such an occasion."

"I'm not anxious," said Mrs Swann.

She was in extremity.

Gilbert's eye fell upon the muff. A streak of white
had appeared near its clasp.

"What's that?"

"What's what?"

"On your muff."

"Nothing."

"It looks like—"

"It's the fur."

It did not look like fur.

Mrs Clayton Vernon approached. "You have caught something on it."

"No, no."

"Allow me."

She extended one jewelled hand. Mrs Swann retreated, forgetting the
fragment under her boot. The sole rose with a soft
adhesive noise. Upon the pale carpet remained a flattened yellow-white
patch, distinct as a heraldic device.

All three looked at it.

Mrs Swann's mind, which at home could organize washing, meals,
accounts, servants, music lessons, railway journeys, and a husband without
perceptible strain, now ceased to act. Pride ordered her to
deny the evidence. Common sense ordered confession. Maternal purpose reminded
her of the second potato. Social terror displayed the servant,
the committee, the choirs, and the entire Five Towns laughing
over tomorrow's breakfast tables.

Gilbert stooped.

"It's potato," he said.

Mrs Swann laughed.

Nobody had ever heard her laugh in that manner. It
was a little sound, empty of amusement and rich in
catastrophe.

"Potato!" she repeated.

"So it is," said Mrs Clayton Vernon, mildly interested.

"It must have been on my boot."

A third untruth, and a poor one.

"From the street?" Gilbert asked.

"There are all sorts of things in streets."

"Baked potato?"

"Why not?"

Gilbert examined his mother. A faint odour, homely and unmistakable,
hovered in the luxurious room. His gaze travelled from the
stain to the muff, from the muff to her face.
Then comprehension entered it, followed by tenderness and a dangerous
inclination to smile.

Mrs Swann saw the smile before it existed.

"Gilbert," she said, "I want a word with you. In
private."

"My dear Mrs Swann," said the hostess, "of course. I
shall leave you together."

"No need," said Mrs Swann hastily.

"You asked for private."

"I only meant—"

But Mrs Clayton Vernon, too well bred to remain and
too experienced to appear curious, had already turned away. At
the door she paused.

"We dine in five minutes, Gilbert."

"Yes, Mrs Vernon."

"And, Mrs Swann, you must let Parker clean your muff."

"No!" cried Mrs Swann.

The patroness looked back.

"I mean, it doesn't matter. It's scarcely marked."

The muff resembled a flour merchant's apron.

"As you please," said Mrs Clayton Vernon, and went.

The door closed.

"Mother," Gilbert whispered, "what have you got in there?"

She looked at him. In that look were authority, humiliation,
defiance, and a demand that he should instantly become five
years old again and accept without comment whatever she did.

"Potatoes."

"How many?"

"Two."

"Why?"

"For your hands."

"My hands?"

"In this frost. You'll be crossing to the hall after
dinner. I didn't want your fingers stiff."

Gilbert bit his lip.

"Don't you dare laugh!"

"I'm not."

"You are."

"I'm not, mother."

His face was crimson.

"You might show some gratitude. I've come all this way.
Your father said gloves were enough, but what does he
know? And then that servant marched me in here, and
she came at me with that lorgnon, and one of
them burst."

"One of Mrs Vernon's lorgnons?"

"The potatoes! You know what I mean."

"Where is it now?"

"Behind the fire."

"The whole potato?"

"What's left of it."

Gilbert looked toward the fire-screen with the fascinated respect due
to the scene of a crime.

"And the other?"

"In the muff."

At that instant the other potato escaped.

Perhaps the clasp had been weakened; perhaps Mrs Swann, in
recounting disaster, made an unconscious gesture; perhaps the vegetable itself,
hitherto obedient, was seized by a desire for social advancement.
However it occurred, the muff opened, and the second potato
fell.

It struck the edge of a footstool, bounced upon the
carpet, rolled beneath a satin sofa, reappeared at the farther
end, and continued with stately determination toward the door.

Mother and son pursued it.

Gilbert reached it first. He stooped; it evaded his fingers
and came to rest against the polished toe of a
gentleman who had entered unnoticed.

The gentleman was short, square, black-haired, and wore a white
waistcoat. His eyebrows met above his nose; his moustache projected
like two conductor's batons. It was Dr Emil Grunwald.

Behind him stood Mrs Clayton Vernon and Mr Anton Fisch,
the principal cellist.

Dr Grunwald surveyed Gilbert, Mrs Swann, and the potato. No
one spoke. The festival, the reputation of the family, the
dignity of motherhood, and perhaps the future of British music
hung suspended.

Then Dr Grunwald bent and picked up the potato.

"Ah!" he said. "Excellent!"

Mrs Swann stared.

He turned to Mrs Clayton Vernon. "You see? This is
what I tell you. Hot potatoes. For the hands."

"Yes, doctor," said Mrs Clayton Vernon. "Parker is preparing them."

"Must be very hot," said Mr Fisch. "In the jacket.
Always in the jacket."

"I have ordered a dozen," said Mrs Clayton Vernon. "For
the string principals and Gilbert."

"Good! This weather is murder for fingers." Dr Grunwald weighed
the potato appreciatively. "Madam, you understand musicians."

Mrs Swann could not speak.

Gilbert could.

"My mother brought it for me, sir."

"So!" Dr Grunwald exclaimed. "A sensible mother. Young man, such
a mother is better than a metronome. She keeps you
in time with life."

This statement was not clear, but it sounded European and
was therefore impressive.

Mrs Clayton Vernon lifted her lorgnon. "My dear Mrs Swann,
why did you not tell me? How thoughtful of you!"

The path of confession lay open, broad, illuminated, and strewn
with roses. Mrs Swann had only to say: "There were
two; one burst in my muff; I hid it behind
your fire; I trod on a piece." Everything would have
been understood. Indeed she might have triumphed. Her maternal device
had received professional sanction from Dr Grunwald and Mr Fisch,
nem. con.; she had anticipated the arrangements of a woman
with a carriage, a butler, and diamonds.

But pride is a singular commander. It will march an
army into destruction rather than admit that the map was
upside down.

"I didn't wish to make a fuss," she said.

"And you pretended there was no potato!" said Mrs Clayton
Vernon playfully.

"I thought it hardly worth mentioning."

Which, considering the last twenty minutes, was magnificent.

Dr Grunwald returned the potato to Gilbert.

"Keep it. Both hands, five minutes before we start. Not
too long, or you make the skin soft."

"There was only one," said Mrs Swann quickly.

Gilbert turned toward her. She looked him full in the
face.

"Only one?" he repeated.

"Only one."

It was the fourth untruth. Gilbert accepted it.

"Thank you, mother."

Mrs Clayton Vernon rang the bell.

"Parker, kindly see that Mrs Swann's muff is brushed before
she leaves."

"No, really—"

"And remove that mark from the carpet."

Parker looked at the mark. His expression did not alter,
but servants of his rank possess an inward life.

"I'm afraid," Gilbert said, "the potato was very soft."

"So I perceive, sir."

Mrs Swann moved toward the door. Her departure had become
imperative before anyone approached the fire.

"You must stay to dinner," said Mrs Clayton Vernon.

"I couldn't."

"But we have room."

"My husband is waiting."

He was not waiting; he had begun upon the cheese
again.

"Then at least let us send you home in the
carriage."

"No, thank you. The tram's quicker."

It was not.

Mrs Clayton Vernon pressed her hand. "You must be very
proud."

Mrs Swann glanced at Gilbert in his evening clothes, holding
the hot potato gravely between both palms while Dr Grunwald
and Mr Fisch discussed the proper warming of a cellist's
fingers. He looked young, competent, and suddenly beyond her—not lost,
but entered into a world where his mother could not
arrange every detail.

"I am," she said.

This, at last, was true.

## VII

In the hall Gilbert helped her with the cape.

"You won't forget to count," she whispered.

"Count what?"

"Whatever you have to count."

"Bars?"

"Those."

"And you won't tell them?"

"Tell them what?"

"About the other one."

"There wasn't another one."

He smiled. "No, mother."

"Mind you don't."

"I won't."

"And don't keep that potato too long in one hand."

"I know."

"You don't know everything."

"No."

She kissed him quickly, then remembered that he was in
evening dress and brushed the place on his cheek with
her glove.

At the outer door the cold seized her. The electric
lamp shone mercilessly upon the stains of the muff. She
descended the steps with dignity and walked down Pirehill Road
through the fine snow.

Behind the drawing-room fire, one ruined potato lay concealed. On
the carpet, Parker laboured with hot water, soap, and the
discretion of his profession. In the dining-room, the leaders of
the festival ate soup. And Gilbert Swann, youngest member of
the orchestra, sat among them with his hands clasped about
a baked potato, as custom, science, and his mother had
ordained.

Mrs Swann reached the tram-stop. She waited six minutes, refusing
to look at the muff. The tram arrived in thunder
and blue sparks. She mounted it, paid twopence, and sat
opposite a woman carrying a basket of fish.

At home Mr Swann had fallen asleep over the newspaper.

"Well?" he asked, waking.

"They were delighted to see me," said Mrs Swann.

"Ay?"

"Dr Grunwald says hot potatoes are the proper thing for
musicians."

"Does he?"

"Of course he does."

Mr Swann considered this, and then the muff.

"What happened to them?"

She drew herself up.

"Nothing."

He nodded.

From Hanbridge, borne faintly across the frozen roofs and smoking
ovens of the Five Towns, came no music whatsoever. The
concert had not yet begun. Nevertheless Mrs Swann could already
hear the applause.

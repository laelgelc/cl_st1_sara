"See them two white seams along my left arm?" I
said. "No, they ain't knife marks, and they ain't the
remembrance of no honest battle, neither. I got 'em on
account of bein' a horse thief—which I wasn't—and a murderer—which
I likewise wasn't, leastways not then. There was also arson,
stage robbery, and the killin' of a United States marshal
mixed into the business, but them charges was mostly furnished
by my friends.

"You see, I was young in those days, and when
a young Texan has got a fast horse, four fool
companions, and twenty dollars in his pocket, he naturally begins
huntin' some risk and devilment. If he don't find it,
his companions will. Mine did."

There was five of us: Tom Martin, who had the
brains; Rufe Bledsoe, who claimed to have the brains; Sandy
Cole, who could ride anything with hair on it; Pike
Watts, who was long, mournful, and always hungry; and me,
called the Kid because I was the youngest and didn't
know enough to object.

We had drifted north after cattle and come away from
the drive with wages, horses, and an opinion that Texas
had got too small for us. Somewhere beyond the Powder
River we heard the Crows was holdin' races, and the
stakes had grown till every horseman in the country was
talkin' about 'em.

Now we didn't own no racer except Black Hawk, my
coal-black gelding, and Martin said Black Hawk was too good
to run where everybody could see him.

"What we want," says Martin, "is a quieter system."

"What kind?" I asked.

"The kind where the stakes changes owners without wearin' out
the horse."

That sounded reasonable till he explained it.

His notion was to wait till the big race was
run and all the Crows was yellin', disputin', and watchin'
the horses; then we was to slip around, seize the
money and trade goods posted for the stakes, and make
our get away. Martin said it wasn't exactly stealin', because
the Crows had likely stole half the horses they raced
and cheated for the other half.

Pike scratched his head.

"If they're thieves," he said, "wouldn't that make stealin' from
'em dangerous?"

Martin looked disgusted.

"Pike, you got no mind for finance."

Well, sir, we rode into that country feelin' like a
bank board goin' to a picnic. The races was held
on a long, level strip beside a creek, with cottonwoods
at one end and low bluffs at the other. There
was Crow lodges scattered over the flat, horses everywhere, dogs
barkin', children runnin', and men ridin' up and down the
course at a dead run for no reason except they
had horses under 'em.

The stakes was piled near a painted lodge—blankets, rifles, belts,
silver pieces, some paper money, and enough odds and ends
to stock a store. We looked at that heap till
every one of us had already spent his share twice.

The trouble was, the Crows looked at us, too.

They didn't make no fuss. They jest watched. Whenever Martin
edged toward the stake lodge, two riders edged the same
way. When Sandy circled behind it, three boys happened to
circle behind Sandy. Rufe said this proved they trusted us,
because they hadn't shot yet.

The last race was between a spotted Crow pony and
a rangy bay owned by a white trader. Everybody poured
down along the course, shoutin' fit to bust. That was
our moment.

Martin waved his hat. We jumped our horses, charged the
lodge, and found the stakes gone.

Gone, I tell you! Not scattered, not moved ten feet—gone
clean as a prairie pond in August.

An old Crow was sittin' there cross-legged, smokin'. He looked
at us and smiled. Then he pointed toward the bluffs.

Five riders was already halfway up them bluffs, each with
a bundle tied behind his saddle.

"Dishonest scoundrels!" Martin yelled. "They've run off with their own
stakes!"

That old man laughed so hard he near swallowed his
pipe.

Then somebody shouted behind us, and the whole race crowd
turned. I never see so many people lose interest in
a horse contest so sudden. They came swarmin' back like
a nest of angry hornets, and we didn't wait to
explain the finer points of our financial system.

We lit out.

Black Hawk went through the camp like a streak of
midnight. Blankets flew, dogs scattered, women yelled, and one skinny
yellow dog stayed under Pike's horse for fifty yards, bitin'
at the stirrups. A gun cracked, then another, and pretty
soon bullets was snappin' over us like corn in a
hot skillet.

Sandy was hit first. He jerked sideways and hollered that
somebody had drove a red-hot spike through his shoulder. Rufe
took a ball along the ribs, not deep, but enough
to make him cuss in three counties at once. Martin
lost his hat. Pike lost one boot. I lost all
respect for Crow horse racin'.

They chased us six or seven miles, and I'll say
this: them men could ride. Twice I looked back and
saw a painted rider near enough to count the feathers
in his hair. But Black Hawk had speed left, and
Sandy's dun could travel even with Sandy bleedin' down one
side. At last the pursuit dropped away, and we pulled
up in some broken country where rocks hid us from
the plain.

We had escaped with our horses, our guns, one boot
of Pike's, and eleven dollars among five men.

"That," says Pike, "ain't what I'd call a successful robbery."

"It wasn't a robbery," Martin said, mighty stiff. "We never
got nothin'."

"Then what happened to our money?"

That was another painful matter. Before the race we had
made private bets with three Crow horsemen, and those gentlemen
had collected in advance. When they carried off the stakes,
they carried our bets along with 'em.

Martin was shocked.

"A man can't do business," he declared, "with people that
won't respect dishonesty."

We bandaged Sandy and Rufe, divided the eleven dollars, and
considered our position. Texas lay a considerable journey south. We
needed food, grain, cartridges, and likely a doctor. A party
of five hungry riders might get one free meal at
a ranch, but by the second meal somebody generally mentioned
work, and by the third he mentioned pay.

That evening we camped by a creek. Pike boiled the
last of our coffee till it could've floated a horseshoe,
and Martin sat starin' into the fire.

Presently he says, "I got it."

Rufe groaned. "That's what you said before the race."

"This is different. We're officers."

"Of what?"

"The law."

We all looked at him.

Martin pointed at me. "Kid's a notorious outlaw. I'm the
sheriff. You three are deputies. We're transportin' him to Texas
for trial."

"Why me?" I asked.

"You're the youngest."

"What's that got to do with crime?"

"You look slippery."

I didn't favor the compliment, but Martin went on. A
sheriff and posse escortin' a dangerous prisoner could expect meals
and shelter out of public spirit. Nobody would charge officers
of the law, especially if they was engaged in protectin'
the country from a desperate criminal. At departure time the
prisoner would stage an escape, the posse would gallop after
him, and there'd be no awkward talk about money.

Sandy brightened. "Kid can take a head start and wait
for us down the road."

Pike nodded. "And prisoners get fed."

That settled me.

Next morning we tied my hands loose with a rawhide
thong and rode to a cattle ranch. Martin pinned a
brass harness buckle inside his vest and flashed it so
quick nobody could study it.

"I'm Sheriff Martin of Red Bluff County, Texas," he said.
"These are my deputies. We're takin' this criminal south."

The rancher stared at me. "What'd he do?"

Martin hadn't thought that far.

"Horse stealin'," he said.

Everybody understood that. The rancher's wife looked at me like
I was a rattlesnake in the milk house, but she
gave us bacon, beans, corn bread, and coffee. I ate
three helpings, which the rancher took as proof of a
depraved nature.

"Ain't he shackled?" he asked.

"Don't need it," says Rufe. "He knows I'll shoot."

Rufe looked so weak from his wound he could scarce
lift the coffeepot, but he narrowed his eyes ferocious, and
the rancher was impressed.

After supper I was put in the barn under Pike's
guard. Near dawn I slipped my hands, saddled Black Hawk,
and galloped past the house. Pike fired twice in the
air and shouted.

"He's loose! After him!"

The rancher and his sons came boilin' out in their
drawers while Martin and the others mounted.

"Which way?" Martin yelled.

"South!" Pike cried, though I was plain in sight goin'
east.

They thundered off east, and we met two miles away
behind a ridge.

Say! It was lovely.

At the next place Martin made me a horse thief
and bank robber. At the place after that, Sandy added
mail robbery, because the rancher had worked for the post
office and was pleased to be included. I began to
get a reputation faster than a dog in a sheep
camp.

The game worked fine. We ate fried beef, biscuits, eggs,
molasses, dried apples, and once a peach pie that Pike
remembers to this day. We slept under roofs, doctored Sandy
and Rufe, and fed our horses. I got admired from
a safe distance. Folks came from neighboring spreads to look
at me, and one little boy asked for a button
off my shirt.

"Don't get close," Martin warned him. "He bites."

I growled, and the boy ran into the kitchen delighted.

There was drawbacks, of course. I generally got the worst
bed, and sometimes no bed. One rancher chained my ankle
to a harrow. Another sat up all night with a
rifle across his knees and wouldn't let me scratch my
nose without permission. A widow in Wyoming Territory made me
eat from a tin plate on the porch while my
friends had chicken at her table.

I complained about that.

Martin said, "Crime don't pay, Kid."

"It's payin' for your chicken."

"That's administration."

The more successful we got, the more artistic Martin became.
Simple horse stealin' no longer satisfied him. He said a
notorious desperado must have variety. Thus I robbed a stage
near Santa Fe, burned the courthouse at El Paso, shot
a deputy in Fort Worth, escaped from three jails, and
held up a riverboat, though none of us could explain
what river it had been on.

Rufe invented most of the killings. He'd sit by the
fire after supper, eatin' somebody else's food, and tell how
he tracked me over deserts, through canyons, and across mountains.

"He's killed nine men," Rufe would say.

"Seven," I'd object from the corner.

"Ten," Rufe would correct. "He jest confessed another."

The household would stare, and I'd have to look mean
while wantin' another biscuit.

By the time we reached Colonel Abel Purdy's ranch, I
had become the worst human bein' west of the Mississippi
and parts east.

We come upon Purdy's place late one afternoon. It was
a prosperous spread, with a whitewashed house, stone corrals, a
long stable, and fences that ran far enough to make
a free-range cow feel insulted. A flag flew from a
pole by the gate. Not a ranch flag, mind you—the
United States flag, big enough to shade a wagon.

Colonel Purdy himself marched out to meet us. He was
a lean old gentleman with a white mustache, white hair,
and eyes as blue and hard as chips off winter
ice. He wore a military coat though the day was
warm, and his left sleeve showed the faded stripes of
rank.

"Halt!" he called.

We halted.

"State your business."

Martin flashed the harness buckle.

"Sheriff Thomas Martin, Red Bluff County, Texas. Deputies Cole, Bledsoe,
and Watts. Transportin' a dangerous outlaw."

The colonel stepped close and inspected us one at a
time. When he reached me, he looked pleased in a
grim way, like a preacher discoverin' sin enough for a
whole sermon.

"Name?"

Martin answered before I could. "William 'Bloody Bill' Mercer."

That was new.

The colonel's eyebrows climbed. "Bloody Bill?"

"The Panhandle Butcher," Sandy put in.

I looked at Sandy. He wouldn't meet my eye.

"What offenses?" Purdy demanded.

Martin drew breath.

"Horse theft, robbery, murder, jail breakin', arson, assault on officers,
interference with the mails—"

"Train robbery," says Rufe.

There wasn't a railroad within hundreds of miles, but Purdy
nodded.

"Destruction of federal property," Pike added, gettin' excited.

Purdy turned on me. "How many men have you murdered?"

"Nobody," I said.

Martin laughed. "Hear that? Cold as ice. He don't count
'em."

The colonel invited the officers in with solemn gratitude. Me,
he ordered to dismount.

I started to swing down, but he leveled a double-barreled
shotgun at my middle.

"Slowly, sir. I fought at Cerro Gordo and Churubusco. I
know the tricks of desperate men."

I got down slowly.

He made Sandy tie my wrists. Not with our friendly
loose loop, neither. He stood over him and ordered every
knot drawn tight, then added a chain from my wrists
to my waist. After that he put a rope around
my ankles, leaving jest enough slack for me to shuffle.

"This ain't necessary, Colonel," Martin said, and for once I
heard worry in his voice.

Purdy's mustache bristled.

"Sheriff, excessive mercy has watered the roots of crime. You
apprehend. I secure."

He secured me in a little smokehouse with a dirt
floor and one barred opening no bigger than a pie.
There was a ham hangin' from a rafter, and I
spent an hour smellin' it while my lawful guardians ate
supper in the house.

At last Purdy's daughter, a kind-faced woman of about thirty,
brought me bread and water.

"Ma'am," I said, "couldn't you put some meat between the
bread?"

"My father says indulgence encourages wickedness."

"It encourages digestion, too."

She nearly smiled, but Purdy called from outside, "Do not
converse with the assassin, Ellen!"

Assassin! That was another promotion.

After dark I heard Purdy ask Martin for the details
of my crimes. I couldn't see 'em, but the parlor
window stood open, and every word carried.

Martin told how I had stolen a prize stallion from
a widowed mother.

Rufe said I burned her cabin afterward.

Sandy said the widow's six children was still inside.

That made me jerk so hard at the chain I
near broke my thumbs.

"Seven children," Pike corrected, likely because he'd had two helpings
of pie.

Then Martin told how I ambushed a sheriff's posse and
killed four men. Rufe made it six. Sandy remembered that
one of 'em was a minister. Pike said I stole
the minister's horse.

That was Pike all over. Murder didn't trouble him much,
but he liked a story to be complete.

Colonel Purdy's chair scraped.

"Gentlemen," he said, "such a monster should never reach trial."

There was a silence.

Martin coughed. "Well, Colonel, the law has to take its
course."

"The law is weakened by delay. In Mexico we dealt
promptly with guerrillas."

I quit thinkin' about the ham.

A while later Martin came to the smokehouse window.

"Kid," he whispered.

"You invented seven dead children."

"That was Pike."

"You're the sheriff. Control your deputies."

"Listen. We'll make the escape early. Purdy sleeps light, so
don't pass near the house. Sandy will loosen your chain
when he brings you breakfast."

"What if the colonel comes?"

"He won't."

You know, them words ought to be carved over every
fool's grave: He won't.

At gray daylight Sandy arrived with a plate and Purdy
behind him.

The colonel carried his shotgun.

"I shall observe the feeding," he announced.

Sandy couldn't loosen nothin'. I ate bread while he stared
at the lock and sweated. Then Purdy took the key
himself and marched me outside for what he called exercise.
He tied the end of my chain to the corral
fence and stood ten paces off.

Black Hawk was saddled nearby, because Pike had got our
horses ready. Martin walked out on the porch, saw the
situation, and began inventin' desperate measures with his eyes.

Rufe came around the stable leadin' his horse. As he
passed me, he stumbled—accidental, you understand—and knocked a water trough
over. The crash startled every horse in the corral. Sandy
yelled. Pike opened the gate. Martin shouted, "Watch the prisoner!"

In the confusion I snatched the chain over the fence
post. Sandy had filed one link near through during the
night, and when I threw my weight against it, it
snapped.

I jumped for Black Hawk.

"Halt!" Purdy roared.

I didn't.

My hands was still tied in front, but I caught
the horn, stuck one foot in the stirrup, and went
aboard as Black Hawk lunged. The colonel fired.

The first barrel tore splinters from the corral rail. Black
Hawk hit the lane full speed. I leaned low, reins
clamped in both hands.

Behind me Purdy shouted orders like he had a regiment
hid in the chicken house.

Then the second barrel went off.

Something slapped my left arm and spun me half around.
For a second it felt numb. Then fire ran from
shoulder to wrist, and blood began drippin' off my fingers.

I had expected my companions to fire in the air,
mount leisurely, and follow. Instead I heard hoofbeats hammerin' close
behind. I looked back.

Colonel Purdy was comin'.

He had somehow traded the shotgun for a carbine and
mounted a big gray horse. His white hair streamed behind
him, his military coat flapped, and he was ridin' bent
forward with the reins in his teeth while he worked
the carbine. I swear he looked like the ghost of
the whole Mexican War.

He fired, and the bullet clipped dust under Black Hawk.

"Stop in the name of the United States!" he bellowed.

I would've stopped in the name of nearly anybody else.

The lane ran south between two fences, and that was
bad, because I couldn't turn off. Black Hawk stretched out,
his black neck flat and his stride eatin' ground, but
Purdy's gray was no plow horse. The colonel stayed close
enough to keep me nervous.

Another shot whined past my ear.

I tried to yell that it was a joke, but
the wind tore the words away, and besides, I didn't
figger Purdy was a man to enjoy jokes involving seven
burned children.

The fence ended at a dry wash. I swung Black
Hawk east, jumped the bank, and nearly lost my seat.
Purdy plunged after me. His gray stumbled, recovered, and came
on. The old colonel waved the carbine over his head
and shouted something about Buena Vista.

We raced across open prairie. Jack rabbits scattered. Prairie dogs
dived into holes. Once a flock of grouse exploded under
Black Hawk's nose, and he jumped sideways so quick my
wounded arm near come off. Purdy gained twenty yards.

"Yield, murderer!"

"No!" I yelled.

That was all I had breath for.

Ahead lay a strip of rough ground cut by gullies.
Black Hawk was quicker than the gray, and I knew
if we reached it, I could lose the colonel. But
the old man fired again, and the ball burned a
line along my upper arm. That's the second scar you
was askin' about.

I quit feelin' genial.

"Colonel!" I shouted back. "If you shoot me again, I'll
kill every one of them children twice!"

It was the wrong thing to say.

Purdy let out a roar and spurred harder.

Black Hawk reached the gullies. I gave him his head.
He went down one bank, up another, over a fallen
cottonwood, and through brush thick enough to comb a bear
bald. The gray tried the log, struck it, and went
to his knees. Purdy sailed over his neck like a
white-headed cannonball and landed in the sand.

I saw him rise behind me, still holdin' the carbine,
but he couldn't shoot for coughin' dust.

Black Hawk carried me another mile before I pulled up
among low hills. My sleeve was soaked, my hands had
gone stiff, and the rawhide still bound my wrists. I
slid off, sat against a rock, and told Black Hawk
he was the only honest member of the party.

Near noon Martin and the others found me. They came
over the ridge at a run, lookin' anxious till they
saw I was alive. Then Sandy began laughin'.

"Kid," he gasped, "you should've seen Purdy hit that wash!"

I held up my bloody arm.

"You should've seen this."

Martin cut my wrists loose and examined the wounds.

"Only grazes."

"Then you wear 'em."

Pike looked offended. "We tried to stop him."

"How?"

"We told him his horse was tired."

Rufe sat down, laughin' till his ribs hurt. "That old
warhorse chased you six miles! Kept yellin' he'd rid the
nation of Bloody Bill."

"I heard him."

Martin bound my arm and said we ought to move
before Purdy raised the county. Then he reached for the
rawhide thong.

"What's that for?" I asked.

"We got another ranch twenty miles south. Best keep up
the system."

I stood.

Martin stopped.

"Kid," he said carefully, "somebody has to be the prisoner."

"That's right."

"You play him natural."

"I'm retired."

"We need food."

"You be the outlaw."

Martin shook his head. "Can't. I'm the sheriff."

I took the brass buckle from his vest and pinned
it inside mine.

"No," I said. "From now on, I'm the sheriff."

And I was, too. Martin made a first-rate murderer once
we got his hands tied, though he complained powerful when
I told the next rancher about the widow, the minister,
and all seven children.

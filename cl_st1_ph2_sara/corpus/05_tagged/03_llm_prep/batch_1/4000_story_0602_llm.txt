# THE TRUE OWNERSHIP OF GOLDIE

## I

The secret history of the appointment of Carl Ullman to
the organ of Saint Ethelberta's, Oldcastle, has never until now
been written. Fragments of it have appeared, naturally, in the
*Oldcastle Advertiser*, and other fragments have been whispered behind gloved
hands after divine service; but neither journalism nor piety has
succeeded in presenting the whole truth. I shall do so.

Oldcastle is not one of the Five Towns. It has
always been most particular about this. The Five Towns produce
cups, saucers, sanitary ware, aldermen, smoke, money, football teams, Methodists,
bankruptcy petitions and men who know the price of coal.
Oldcastle produces character. It stands upon a hill, and from
that moral elevation looks down upon the Five Towns, whose
prosperity it secretly envies and whose manners it publicly deplores.
Its streets twist in a manner called historical; its drains,
until quite lately, behaved in a manner equally historical. It
possesses a castle without a roof, several roofs without adequate
houses beneath them, and a reputation for refinement which no
prudent citizen has ever examined too closely.

In this correct borough lived Mrs Ebag and her sister,
Miss Ebag.

Mrs Ebag was the widow of Josiah Ebag, eldest son
of Caiaphas Ebag, the celebrated manufacturer and speculator. Caiaphas had
amassed a fortune by honest labour in mighty enterprises which
lesser minds sometimes called gambling. At the period with which
we are concerned, he was eighty-two, paralysed, tyrannical, and confined
to an upper chamber of Ebag House. Since he could
ring six different bells and did ring them, confinement did
not diminish his influence.

Mrs Ebag was forty-six years of age. Her sister was
forty-two. Between them they had lived eighty-eight years, and not
one of those years had passed without contributing something to
the truest welfare of the church.

Mrs Ebag was comfortably stout, with a broad white brow,
a sympathetic bosom and the tranquil manner of a woman
who had been married once and therefore knew the world.
Miss Ebag was spare, upright and intense. Her nose was
finer than her sister's, her waist was less indulgent, and
her opinions were more numerous. Mrs Ebag had borne the
late Josiah no children, but Josiah, who had previously committed
matrimony, had left behind him a daughter named Edith. Edith
spent most of her time in London and other absurd
places.

The two sisters lived at Ebag House with Caiaphas, four
servants and Goldie.

Goldie was an orange tomcat of commanding dimensions. He walked
pompously, washed conscientiously, and received affection as though it were
a municipal tribute whose payment had been delayed. His eyes
were green; his whiskers were white; his tail was magnificent.
He never caught mice, because there were no mice in
Ebag House—or, if there were, they belonged to a social
order with which he did not mingle.

At that time Saint Ethelberta's required a new organist.

Its old organist, Mr Peake, had committed the indelicacy of
surviving his usefulness. He was sixty-nine, deaf in the left
ear, slow in the right hand, and obstinately attached to
an anthem which the choir had sung on twenty-seven consecutive
festivals. Mrs Ebag and Miss Ebag had prayed for guidance
concerning him. When, soon afterwards, Mr Peake resigned in consequence
of a disagreement about his stipend, the sisters could not
deny that their prayer had received something very like an
answer.

They heard of Carl Ullman through a music-master at Bursley.

Ullman was twenty-nine, tall, pale and almost penniless. He had
a mass of dark hair, romantic eyes, a foreign name,
and a black overcoat whose collar had been velvet before
adversity wore the velvet off. His father, a German violinist,
had died at Hanbridge; his English mother had followed him
after exhausting a small annuity and a large stock of
anxiety. Thus Carl was an artistic orphan. He played Bach
nobly, Mendelssohn persuasively, and church committees with discretion.

At his audition he made the organ speak first like
a prophet and then like an angel remembering a sorrow
which could not safely be explained to the congregation. Mrs
Ebag wept. Miss Ebag did not weep, but her nostrils
became tense. The vicar said that Mr Ullman had temperament.
The churchwardens asked whether temperament would cost extra.

Carl was appointed at eighty pounds a year.

This sum was considered handsome by everyone except persons who
had tried to live on it.

"You must stay with us until you find suitable rooms,"
said Mrs Ebag.

"Suitable," Miss Ebag repeated, glancing at the cuffs of his
shirt. "We could not permit you to fall into careless
surroundings."

Carl hesitated. He had six shillings and threepence.

"You are very kind," he said gloomily.

And so he came to Ebag House.

## II

Carl remained there for nine months.

Nobody had expected nine months. At the end of the
first week Mrs Ebag remarked that good lodgings were difficult
to discover. At the end of the first month Miss
Ebag observed that the winter was no season for removals.
At the end of the third month the sisters agreed
that his presence was convenient for Caiaphas, who enjoyed having
the funeral march from *Saul* played after luncheon. By the
ninth month the question of departure had become one of
those grave domestic questions which cannot be mentioned without vulgarity.

Carl occupied the blue bedroom overlooking the rhododendrons. He ate
breakfast with the sisters, visited Caiaphas at eleven, practised at
Saint Ethelberta's in the afternoon, and spent the evenings turning
music for one sister while the other embroidered altar linen.
His melancholy fitted the furniture. Ebag House was full of
mahogany, dark curtains, portraits of dead manufacturers and clocks which
struck as if issuing warnings to debtors. Carl, seated beneath
a portrait of the late Josiah, might have been designed
for the room by an expensive undertaker.

Nevertheless he proved useful.

Through him Mrs Ebag learned that the vicar intended to
introduce a harvest hymn of doubtful dignity. Through him Miss
Ebag learned that the tenor had been seen entering a
billiard-room. Through him both sisters learned that the choirboys preferred
buns to moral exhortation, a fact which profoundly altered their
policy. Carl, in return, acquired good dinners, clean cuffs, tobacco
supplied under the transparent fiction that Caiaphas required it, and
a sense of importance which eighty pounds a year had
not previously given him.

The government of Saint Ethelberta's passed by imperceptible stages into
the parlour of Ebag House.

Oldcastle noticed.

Oldcastle notices everything except its own motives. On Sunday mornings
looks were looked over prayer-books, coughs were coughed behind hymnals,
and pauses of exceptional richness occurred after the name of
Mr Ullman. In the High Street conversations assumed this form:

"Of course, it is no affair of mine—"

"Certainly not."

"And I should be the last person—"

"Indeed."

"But a young man in a house with two unprotected
ladies—"

"Though Mrs Ebag is a widow."

"Exactly. That makes it more peculiar."

No one suggested that Mrs Ebag and Miss Ebag were
capable of protecting themselves. Such a suggestion would have ruined
the scandal.

For a while the sisters remained serenely unconscious. Carl also
remained unconscious, but his unconsciousness was of an artistic kind
and involved frequent glances into mirrors. Then Mrs Ebag's cook
heard something from the butcher's wife, who had heard it
from the verger's aunt, who had heard nothing definite but
had drawn a responsible conclusion. The cook told the parlourmaid;
the parlourmaid told Miss Ebag's maid; and Miss Ebag's maid,
while fastening a collar, sighed.

"Why do you sigh, Pritchard?" Miss Ebag demanded.

"It's not for me to say, miss."

"What is not for you to say?"

"Nothing, miss."

Within seven minutes Miss Ebag knew everything.

She informed her sister. Mrs Ebag coloured; then she laughed
with mature sweetness.

"How ridiculous!"

"Utterly," said Miss Ebag.

They fell silent.

That evening Carl came in late from choir practice. He
looked unusually pale because the wind had been against him
and because he had eaten no tea. Mrs Ebag watched
him remove his gloves. Miss Ebag watched him bend over
the fire. Both perceived, for the first time, that his
romantic and gloomy appearance might have consequences.

"Dear Mr Ullman," said Mrs Ebag, "you must not overtire
yourself."

Carl raised his melancholy eyes.

"No one would care," he answered.

This was untrue. Several people cared, including his tailor. But
the phrase was effective.

"My dear friend!" Mrs Ebag murmured.

Miss Ebag dropped her embroidery scissors.

After that evening a new self-consciousness entered Ebag House. Doors
were left open which had formerly been shut. Interviews became
accidentally plural. If Mrs Ebag asked Carl whether he had
slept well, Miss Ebag appeared to inquire after the central-heating
boiler. If Miss Ebag carried him a cup of cocoa,
Mrs Ebag discovered that she too desired cocoa. The sisters
were devoted to each other. Their devotion became vigilant.

Carl perceived the change.

The worst was, he liked it.

## III

The thunderstorm occurred on a Thursday in June.

All day the sky had worn a yellow and vindictive
expression. The air pressed upon Oldcastle; the castle hill disappeared
under vapour; even the Five Towns ceased smoking for nearly
three minutes, an event subsequently denied by their Chambers of
Commerce. At half-past eight the storm broke.

Rain struck Ebag House in solid sheets. Thunder rolled through
the chimneys. Lightning disclosed the garden with photographic violence: lawn,
urn, monkey-puzzle tree, rhododendrons—and darkness again. The servants gathered in
the kitchen to insist that they were not frightened. Caiaphas
rang three bells simultaneously and demanded to know who was
moving the house.

Mrs Ebag, Miss Ebag and Carl were in the drawing-room.

Carl had been playing a prelude in C minor, but
at the first great thunderclap he stopped. Not from fear.
He wished the storm to have its chance.

"Where is Goldie?" Mrs Ebag suddenly asked.

The sisters looked towards the hearthrug. Goldie was not there.

"Perhaps he is in the dining-room," said Miss Ebag.

He was not in the dining-room. He was not in
the library, the conservatory, the hall or Caiaphas's room, though
Caiaphas declared that if the animal came near his bed
he would have it destroyed. The servants were questioned. Doors
were opened. Candles moved through passages. Goldie's name, ordinarily pronounced
in tones of endearment, became an alarm signal.

Then, during a lull, they heard him.

"Miaow!"

It came from above.

They ran into the garden, or as many of them
ran as propriety and the weather permitted. Another flash illuminated
the roof. Against the boiling sky, upon the ridge near
the chimney, Goldie appeared in an attitude not pompous but
desperate.

His tail was entangled in the weather-vane.

How he had reached the roof was never proved. The
scullery window, a pear tree, a narrow gutter and vanity
were probably involved. The weather-vane represented a gilded cock, and
it was afterwards suggested that Goldie had attempted to make
its acquaintance. This theory insults his intelligence. The fact remains
that his tail, after passing between two iron scrolls, had
twisted tightly round the spindle. Every movement increased his inconvenience.

"Goldie!" cried Mrs Ebag.

"Darling!" cried Miss Ebag.

The cat replied with a sound which no gentleman should
make, even on a roof.

"We must send for the fire brigade," said Mrs Ebag.

"The Oldcastle brigade has no extension ladder," Miss Ebag reminded
her. "The Hanbridge brigade has one, but they would charge."

This was true. Hanbridge would also have enjoyed the incident.

Carl examined the wall. A leaded porch roof sloped upwards
beneath the library windows; beyond it a stone ledge reached
the main gutter. From there an agile man might gain
the ridge. An agile man might also break his neck,
but the distinction was not then emphasised.

"I shall go," he said.

"No!" exclaimed both sisters.

Carl removed his coat.

Lightning flamed. The wind seized the trees. Mrs Ebag clutched
his arm, and her touch conveyed enough emotion to support
a less experienced climber.

"You must not risk your life," she whispered.

"It is of no value," Carl replied, with a glance
which suggested that its value might yet be altered by
one word from her.

Miss Ebag seized his other arm.

"Think of your art!"

"My art has brought me little happiness."

"You have brought happiness to others," said Miss Ebag.

The rain was ruining all three of them, and the
Persian runner in the hall, upon which they stood, was
receiving water at the rate of several shillings a second.

Goldie miaowed again.

Carl disengaged himself. He fetched a coil of clothes-line, climbed
through a library window and crawled across the porch roof.
The women watched below, uttering apprehensive feminine cries whenever he
moved and louder cries whenever he paused. A servant held
a lantern, though the lantern illuminated nothing except her own
terror.

Carl reached the gutter.

The wind pressed his wet shirt against his body. He
drew himself upward by the chimney stack, placed one boot
upon a projecting stone and gained the ridge. For an
instant he sat astride the roof, black against a white
blaze of lightning, a veritable hero of antiquity—if antiquity ever
sent its heroes up wet slates after orange cats.

Goldie struck him on the wrist.

"Ungrateful brute!" Carl said.

His words were carried away by the gale.

He wound the clothes-line round the chimney, tied the other
end about his waist and edged towards the weather-vane. The
roof shone like glass. Below him the rhododendrons tossed and
heaved. He grasped Goldie, received another blow, and attempted to
unwind the imprisoned tail.

The tail would not unwind.

"Carl!" Mrs Ebag cried.

It was the first time she had used his Christian
name.

He heard it.

At that sublime moment the weather-vane shifted. The iron spindle
turned; Goldie sprang; Carl's boot slipped. Cat, man and part
of the gilded cock descended together down the sloping roof.
The clothes-line tightened, snapped, and contributed nothing further to history.

There was one enormous shriek from the garden.

Carl and Goldie fell into the largest rhododendron bush.

For several seconds nothing happened. Then the bush trembled. Goldie
emerged, shook one offended paw, and walked towards the house
with his tail at an angle. Carl's head appeared among
the wet blossoms.

"Just my luck," he said.

He was not hurt. Neither was Goldie. The weather-vane, however,
never again indicated west with conviction.

## IV

The hero was brought into the drawing-room, wrapped in blankets
and given whisky.

Mrs Ebag prepared the whisky. Miss Ebag carried the blankets.
Each considered the other's service useful but secondary.

Carl sat by the fire, damp hair falling over his
forehead, a scratch upon his cheek and an expression of
noble exhaustion. Goldie lay before him licking the very tail
for which a human life had been risked. No one
rebuked the cat. Successful victims are seldom rebuked.

"You might have been killed," Mrs Ebag said.

Carl smiled sombrely.

"One has to die."

"Not like that," said Miss Ebag.

Mrs Ebag knelt to adjust the blanket about his knees.
Her face, softened by alarm and firelight, became almost beautiful.
She had been a handsome woman, and widowhood, though it
had increased her circumference, had not destroyed her eyes.

"You did it for me," she murmured.

Carl stared.

"For Goldie," he said.

"But you knew what his loss would mean to me."

She placed one hand upon the cat. Goldie continued washing.

"He is mine in a way no one understands," she
said. "Josiah gave him to me during the last year
of our married happiness."

This statement requires examination. Josiah had indeed brought Goldie into
the house during the last year of the marriage. He
had found him as a kitten in a packing-case at
the works and had said, "Here, somebody take this thing."
Mrs Ebag had been present. Whether the words constituted a
legal gift must be left to lawyers, who would undoubtedly
have rendered the animal valueless before reaching a decision.

Carl looked at Mrs Ebag. Gratitude, whisky, warmth and security
united within him. He saw himself no longer wandering from
lodging to lodging, but established in Ebag House, honoured at
Saint Ethelberta's, perhaps travelling yearly to Llandudno. Mrs Ebag's fortune
was not enormous, yet compared with eighty pounds a year
it was imperial. Moreover, she admired him. An artist may
resist money; admiration is more dangerous.

"Carl," she whispered.

He leaned towards her.

"Amelia—"

Miss Ebag entered with a hot-water bottle.

"Sister!"

The word contained surprise, reproach, inquiry and an insistence upon
the open door.

Mrs Ebag rose.

"I was merely seeing that Mr Ullman was comfortable."

"So I perceived," said Miss Ebag.

Carl did not propose.

He went to bed at midnight and dreamed that he
was married to a gigantic orange cat which played the
harmonium.

## V

At nine the next morning Carl descended to breakfast. Mrs
Ebag had not yet appeared. Miss Ebag sat alone behind
the coffee service, wearing pale grey and an expression of
spiritual fatigue.

"I trust you slept, dear Mr Ullman."

"Badly," said Carl.

This was his customary reply, and therefore conveyed no information.

Miss Ebag poured coffee. Her hands were graceful. In the
morning light she looked younger than forty-two; or, at least,
she looked forty-two under unusually favourable management. She did not
possess her sister's softness, but she had distinction, principles and
a private income of two hundred and seventy pounds.

Goldie entered.

He jumped upon Miss Ebag's lap with the unscrupulous instinct
of his species.

"My precious!" she exclaimed.

Carl watched her long fingers move through the orange fur.

"You are fond of him," he said.

"Fond?" Her voice shook. "He is the dearest thing in
my life."

Carl put down his cup.

Miss Ebag bent her head.

"I have never spoken of it," she continued, "because some
affections are too sacred for speech. Everyone supposes that Goldie
belongs to my sister. It is not so. He was
always mine."

"Yours?"

"He came to me first. I fed him with warm
milk when he could scarcely see. Sister was occupied with
poor Josiah. Naturally, as a widow, she has been allowed
to assume—but no matter. Goldie knows."

Goldie, who knew where the kidneys were kept, purred effulgently.

"When I saw you on the roof," Miss Ebag said,
"I felt that Providence had revealed something to me."

Carl's melancholy eyes met hers.

Once again he contemplated a future. It was not the
same future as last night's. This future contained fewer dinners
but more exalted conversation. Miss Ebag would understand Bach. She
would guard him from trivial callers. There might be a
small house near the church, with ivy and a sound
roof. Two hundred and seventy pounds, added to eighty, made
three hundred and fifty. Three hundred and fifty pounds was
not passion, but it could pay quarterly bills.

"I have been lonely," Miss Ebag said.

"So have I."

"Perhaps two lonely natures—"

"Sister," said Mrs Ebag from the doorway, "Father wishes to
know why his breakfast egg is seven minutes late."

Miss Ebag released Goldie.

"I was not aware that I laid it," she answered.

The sisters looked at each other. Carl looked at his
plate.

No proposal occurred.

During the next fortnight Ebag House attained a level of
propriety almost painful to witness. Mrs Ebag was never alone
with Carl. Miss Ebag was never alone with him. Neither
sister was ever alone with the other if avoidance could
be achieved without public violence. The artistic orphan wandered between
them, increasingly admired, increasingly well fed, and increasingly uncertain which
form of security Providence intended.

Oldcastle, observing all this through windows, keyholes and the servants'
entrance, became excited.

Then Edith arrived from London.

## VI

Edith was twenty-two.

Mrs Ebag called her a silly chit, though not to
her face. Miss Ebag called her a flighty creature, also
at a prudent distance. Both agreed that London had ruined
her, by which they meant that she possessed clothes they
could not understand and opinions she did not trouble to
defend.

She entered Ebag House at six in the evening, accompanied
by two trunks, a hatbox, a scent of violets and
the conviction that railway journeys were meant to end. She
kissed Mrs Ebag, kissed Miss Ebag, ran upstairs to Caiaphas,
returned before the tea was cold, and discovered Carl at
the window.

"Who is this?" she demanded cheerfully.

"Mr Ullman, our organist," Mrs Ebag replied.

"Our resident organist," Miss Ebag corrected.

Carl bowed.

Edith regarded him. She had fair hair of an insolent
brightness, a slender body and grey eyes which laughed before
her mouth did. Her beauty was not profound. It was
immediate, and immediate things possess advantages.

"Do you play dances?" she asked.

"I play serious music."

"How unfortunate! Can't you play anything wicked?"

"Edith!" Mrs Ebag protested.

"A waltz, then. Aunt Mary says waltzes are only wicked
when danced."

Miss Ebag, who was not her aunt but accepted the
title under protest, drew herself upright.

"Your aunt Mary says nothing so foolish."

"Then I invented it. That is nearly as good."

After dinner Edith opened the piano.

Carl explained that he did not care for fashionable compositions.
She set before him a waltz recently imported from Vienna.
He looked at it with the disdain of a prophet
confronted by a comic paper.

"Please," she said.

He played.

At first he played ironically, emphasising the vulgar rhythm. Then
Edith began to dance alone across the drawing-room. She moved
between the chairs with her arms extended, turning her fair
head as though an invisible partner adored her. The waltz
quickened. Carl's fingers grew lighter. The solemn furniture receded; Josiah's
portrait seemed to disapprove from a great distance; even the
clock forgot to issue warnings and merely struck nine.

Carl smiled.

Both sisters saw him smile.

It was not the bitter smile with which he received
compliments, nor the resigned smile with which he accepted a
second helping. It was a boy's smile—careless, amazed and delighted
at its own existence.

"There!" Edith cried, stopping breathless beside the piano. "You are
not gloomy at all."

Carl ceased playing.

"I am by nature melancholy."

"Nonsense. You have simply practised."

She took his hand and struck a final chord with
one of his fingers.

The chord was loud, common and joyous.

Mrs Ebag suggested that Edith must be tired after her
journey. Miss Ebag added that Mr Ullman needed repose after
his professional labours. Edith replied that nobody under ninety needed
repose before ten. From upstairs came the violent ringing of
Caiaphas's fourth bell.

"That is Grandpapa," she said. "He wants his chessmen. I
hid them last time because he cheated."

She ran from the room.

Carl looked after her.

The sisters looked at Carl.

Goldie, who had slept through the waltz, opened one eye.

## VII

The following days were disastrous to seriousness.

Edith persuaded Carl to play songs whose words involved moonlight,
roses and conduct not sanctioned by the churchwardens. She made
him walk to the castle ruins; she laughed at his
overcoat; she compelled him to buy a blue necktie for
one shilling and elevenpence. She attended choir practice and reduced
three choirboys to idiocy by asking their names.

"You encourage frivolity," Mrs Ebag told her.

"No," said Edith. "Frivolity encourages me."

"You have no conception of Mr Ullman's artistic temperament," Miss
Ebag said.

"I have. He enjoys buns, hates wet boots, and cheats
at bezique."

Carl denied the last accusation, but without gloom.

Oldcastle revised its theories. The butcher's wife decided that Mr
Ullman had been intended for Edith all along. The verger's
aunt remembered having said so. At Saint Ethelberta's, ladies who
had previously pitied the sisters now pitied Edith. No one
pitied Carl. Men in such circumstances are regarded as having
designed them.

One afternoon Edith found him alone in the conservatory. Rain
tapped upon the glass, but this was an ordinary rain,
without heroism. Goldie sat between them on a wicker chair.

Edith lifted the cat.

"You magnificent old fraud!" she said. "I have not thanked
Mr Ullman for saving you."

Carl became solemn. His solemnity now had to be summoned;
formerly it had waited.

"You heard of that?"

"Everyone in London has heard of it. At least, I
heard of it in London, which is not quite the
same."

She held Goldie against her cheek.

"He is my cat, you know."

Carl started.

"Yours?"

"Of course. Father gave him to me when I was
sixteen."

This also requires examination. Josiah had once said to Edith,
after the kitten upset an inkpot, "If you like the
beast, take charge of him." Edith had immediately gone to
school for two terms, leaving Miss Ebag to supply the
warm milk and Mrs Ebag to assume the expenses. Yet
in the tender jurisprudence of affection, absence does not invalidate
title.

"Mrs Ebag believes—" Carl began.

"Aunt Amelia believes everything in this house is hers because
she married Father. Aunt Mary believes everything is hers because
she arranged it. Grandpapa believes everything is his because he
paid for the roof. Goldie believes we are all his.
Goldie is nearest the truth."

She kissed the cat's head.

"So it was for my sake that you climbed up,"
she said.

Carl stared at her fair hair. A ray of reluctant
sunlight entered the conservatory and became entangled in it. He
had previously seen security in Mrs Ebag, understanding in Miss
Ebag, and dinner in both. In Edith he saw none
of these things. He saw expenses, disturbance, laughter, impossible hats,
journeys to London, unpaid accounts and happiness.

"No," he said honestly. "I did not know you existed."

"That was very stupid of you."

"I agree."

"But now that you know?"

Goldie, squeezed between them, uttered a warning.

Carl took the animal from her and set him on
the floor. Their hands met in the orange fur.

"Edith—"

She looked up.

At that instant Mrs Ebag called from the drawing-room, "Edith,
darling!"

Whether the darling was Edith or Goldie could not be
established. Neither answered.

Carl kissed Edith.

Goldie walked away, his tail erect. He had accomplished what
thunder, church music and provincial gossip had begun.

## VIII

The announcement was received with dignity.

Mrs Ebag sat very still, while her teacup rattled in
its saucer. Miss Ebag stood by the mantelpiece and stared
at the weather-vane, visible through the window in its permanent
uncertainty. Carl made the declaration. Edith supplied corrections.

"We are engaged," Carl said.

"Since four o'clock," said Edith.

"We hope for your approval."

"But we shall marry without it," said Edith.

"Edith!" Mrs Ebag exclaimed.

"It is kinder to be clear."

Miss Ebag addressed Carl.

"May one inquire upon what income you propose to support
a wife?"

Carl's romantic and gloomy appearance returned in full force.

"Eighty pounds a year."

"Ninety," Mrs Ebag said automatically. "The Easter increase has been
confirmed."

"Then ninety."

"And Edith has thirty pounds from her mother's settlement," Miss
Ebag added.

"One hundred and twenty!" Edith cried. "We are rich."

"You are destitute," said Mrs Ebag.

The arithmetic could not be disputed. Oldcastle allowed love in
moderation, but it expected marriage to possess dining-room furniture. Carl
had seventeen pounds in the bank, of which twelve belonged
morally to his tailor. Edith possessed jewellery, clothes, and a
parasol unsuitable for any known weather.

For three days the sisters presented serious questions. Where would
they live? Who would pay the rent? Had Carl considered
butcher's bills? Did Edith understand the price of coal? Had
either of them reflected upon children, illness, pew rents, servants,
insurance, chimney sweeping, boot repairs or the moral dangers of
hire-purchase?

They had not.

But they remained engaged.

Caiaphas was informed. The paralytic listened from his great bed,
his face like a wrinkled judicial apple.

"What has he got?" he asked.

"Talent," said Edith.

"What's that bringing per annum?"

"Ninety pounds."

"Then it isn't talent. It's a hobby."

He rang the second bell and demanded beef tea.

Oldcastle discussed the engagement with delectation. Some said Carl had
behaved abominably to Mrs Ebag; others maintained that Miss Ebag
had escaped by a miracle. A third party held that
Edith had trapped him. The fact that each of the
four principals considered someone else the trapper did not weaken
any theory.

Mrs Ebag yielded first.

She informed Edith that, for the sake of her dear
Josiah's memory and not from approval of reckless espousals, she
would permit the couple one hundred and fifty pounds a
year. Miss Ebag then offered the interest on a small
sum, but withdrew the offer when Edith kissed her too
violently, and restored it the next morning by letter.

Thus romance became solvent.

Carl and Edith were married at Saint Ethelberta's on a
bright October day. Carl played no part in the organ
music, this being one of the few weddings at which
he was indispensable elsewhere. The choir sang correctly. Mrs Ebag
wore mauve and forgave nobody. Miss Ebag wore grey and
forgave everybody in theory. Caiaphas remained at home and rang
bells throughout the ceremony.

Goldie was not invited, but appeared in the porch as
the couple emerged.

"There's my darling!" Edith cried.

She might have meant Carl.

The bridegroom bent and picked up the cat. People cheered,
though no one knew precisely why. Perhaps they had heard
of the roof; perhaps they approved youth; perhaps Oldcastle, after
manufacturing the scandal, wished to congratulate itself upon the marriage.

Carl married Edith's hair, her laughter and her capacity for
making one hundred and twenty pounds feel for five minutes
like a fortune. Edith married his melancholy eyes, his long
fingers and the secret gaiety which she believed she alone
had discovered. These were not the worst foundations for matrimony.

They took a small house near Saint Ethelberta's. Its roof
was sound. There were no rhododendrons.

## IX

The marriage succeeded.

This may sound queer, but facts must be respected even
when they offend social prophecy. Carl continued to be melancholy
in public and cheerful at home. Edith spent too much
on curtains, then saved the precise amount by dismissing a
servant and doing none of the servant's work herself. They
quarrelled over money, music, callers and the position of furniture.
They made peace rapidly. Carl's salary rose; he obtained pupils;
he composed an anthem which was praised in the *Advertiser*
as "ambitious." He even acquired a new overcoat, though he
kept the old one because it was more artistic.

Mrs Ebag paid the quarterly allowance with reluctance and punctuality.
She remained a widow.

Miss Ebag continued her labours for Saint Ethelberta's and was
elected secretary of three societies whose affairs she had previously
managed without office. She remained unmarried.

Each sister declared that she had never entertained a personal
thought concerning Carl. Mrs Ebag said she had regarded him
as a son. Miss Ebag said she had recognised from
the beginning that his temperament required youth. Oldcastle accepted neither
declaration, but repeated both.

Goldie moved between Ebag House and the young couple's home
according to the quality of meals. Mrs Ebag called him
faithless when he stayed with Edith. Edith called him kidnapped
when he stayed with Mrs Ebag. Miss Ebag said nothing,
but bribed him with chicken. Caiaphas ordered him out of
his room and then complained if he did not come.

For seven years the cat maintained this diplomatic position.

Age touched him. His jumps became calculations; his orange coat
faded; he ceased to wash the middle of his back
because the journey was excessive. Nevertheless, he retained authority. He
slept in the best chairs, interrupted the best conversations and
accepted rival claims without committing himself.

Then, in his eighteenth year—or nineteenth, according to Miss Ebag—Goldie
died.

He died peacefully on a cushion in Caiaphas's room, while
afternoon sun warmed his old fur. The household was shaken.
Mrs Ebag wept and said she had lost the last
living gift of Josiah. Miss Ebag wept and recalled the
milk she had warmed when Goldie was blind. Edith came
from her house and wept against Carl's coat, saying that
the heroism on the roof had given her both her
darlings. Carl, who had once risked his neck for the
animal and had since been scratched by him on twenty-six
recorded occasions, wept also.

They buried Goldie beneath the rhododendron bush.

A small stone was ordered. The inscription caused difficulty.

Mrs Ebag proposed:

> GOLDIE
> THE FAITHFUL COMPANION OF
> AMELIA EBAG

Miss Ebag objected to "faithful" and to the possessive implication.
She proposed:

> IN MEMORY OF GOLDIE
> TENDERLY NURTURED FROM INFANCY
> BY MARY EBAG

Edith preferred:

> GOLDIE
> WHO BROUGHT CARL AND EDITH TOGETHER

Carl considered this sentimental but secretly liked it.

The argument reached Caiaphas.

## X

The paralytic was now ninety. He had outlived doctors, partners,
competitors, two solicitors and every person who had ever advised
him to avoid excitement. He lay in the upper chamber
under a red quilt, his eyes sharp in a face
from which time had removed all unnecessary softness.

The family assembled round his bed. Mrs Ebag explained the
difficulty. Miss Ebag supplied dates. Edith described the proposed stone.
Carl stood by the window and examined the repaired weather-vane,
which still pointed south-east regardless of the wind.

Caiaphas listened.

Then he struck the quilt with one thin hand.

"Fools!" he said.

No one spoke. He was ninety, paralysed and rich.

"My cat," he continued.

"Your cat?" Mrs Ebag repeated.

"Mine. Bought him."

"You found him in a packing-case," said Miss Ebag.

"Packing-case was mine."

"Father gave him to me," Edith protested.

"Did nothing of the sort."

"Josiah gave him to me," said Mrs Ebag, with dignity.

"Josiah never owned the hair of him."

Caiaphas glared at them all.

The actual facts, so far as an actual fact may
survive in a family, were these. Eighteen years earlier Caiaphas
had ordered a consignment of glaze ingredients from Liverpool. In
one of the empty packing-cases at the works a kitten
had been discovered. Caiaphas had caused the porter to carry
it to Ebag House because he disliked rats in the
stables. He had paid the porter sixpence. This sixpence, in
his opinion, established an indefeasible title.

"I paid for him," Caiaphas said. "Fed him too."

Everyone looked surprised.

It appeared that for years Caiaphas had secretly shared his
luncheon with Goldie. The cat's daily visits to the upper
chamber, interpreted by Mrs Ebag as filial courtesy, by Miss
Ebag as instinctive reverence, and by Edith as mere wandering,
had been commercial expeditions involving cold beef.

"He slept here," Caiaphas added.

"He slept everywhere," said Edith.

"Because the house is mine."

There was no answer to this. Caiaphas had always understood
ownership in its largest and simplest sense.

"What shall we put on the stone, Grandpapa?" Edith asked.

"Put nothing. Waste of money."

He rang the third bell.

The family withdrew.

In the end the stone bore only:

> GOLDIE
> 18— TO 19—
> MUCH BELOVED

Thus each claimant was satisfied by suppressing the truth.

But I have not suppressed it. Mrs Ebag believed Goldie
hers because her husband had brought him home. Miss Ebag
believed him hers because she had nourished him. Edith believed
him hers because her father had once told her to
take charge of him. Carl may be excused if, after
the thunderstorm, he occasionally regarded the animal as purchased with
personal danger. Caiaphas believed him his because of a packing-case,
sixpence and cold beef. Goldie, who alone could have settled
the matter, maintained a silence at once dignified and advantageous.

From the rescue of this disputed animal came a marriage,
several years of gossip, two disappointed ladies, an increase in
an organist's income, and a permanent defect in a weather-vane.
Historians have founded larger conclusions on evidence less substantial.

Mrs Ebag died a widow.

Miss Ebag died unmarried.

Caiaphas died protesting that the funeral expenses were scandalous.

Carl and Edith lived together for forty-one years, not always
wisely, not always cheaply, but with a degree of happiness
which Oldcastle considered almost indecent. When Carl became celebrated enough
to have his portrait hung in the municipal hall, the
painter gave him romantic and gloomy eyes. Edith laughed for
three days.

And that is the whole truth concerning Goldie.

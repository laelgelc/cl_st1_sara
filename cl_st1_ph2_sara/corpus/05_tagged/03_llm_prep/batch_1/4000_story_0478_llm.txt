## The Test of the River

"I suppose there's no use asking you to tell me
who did it," said Jerry Hutton.

Ned Gordon looked uncomfortable.

The two boys stood before the long window at the
end of Winthrop Academy's Upper Hall. Outside, a gray February
rain had begun to fall upon the snowbanks. Water dripped
from the eaves, and across the campus the elms showed
black and bare against a smoky sky.

"Who did what?" asked Ned.

"Oh, shucks! You know well enough. Who held me up
for the Lyceum?"

"No one held you up exactly."

Jerry laughed without amusement.

"My name was proposed Monday. The meeting was last night.
This morning you tell me that I'm not to come
in until spring. If that isn't being held up, what
the dickens is it?"

Ned thrust his hands deeper into his pockets.

"You'll get in later. I'm sure of it."

"Then some fellow objected."

"There was—well, there was a little discussion."

"About me?"

"Of course it was about you."

"And Herb Welch led it."

Ned turned toward the window.

Jerry's mouth tightened. "I thought so."

"You mustn't blame Herb too much," said Ned. "He didn't
say anything mean. He only said that the Lyceum ought
to be careful about taking in new fellows."

"How considerate of him!"

"He said you were a good student and a first-rate
athlete, but—"

"But what?"

Ned hesitated. "But that you hadn't proved yourself."

For a moment Jerry stared at him.

"Hadn't proved myself!" he repeated. "I've been at Winthrop two
years. I stood sixth in my class last term. I
was substitute halfback on the Eleven. I've done everything the
Lyceum fellows have done except lick the boots of the
seniors."

"That isn't what Herb meant."

"I know what he meant. He's had it in for
me ever since last October."

The episode had occurred behind South Dormitory. Welch, then a
junior, had undertaken to make a timid freshman named Peters
climb the stone gatepost and deliver a speech to an
imaginary audience. Peters had refused. Welch had seized him by
the collar, and Jerry, coming upon the scene, had interfered.
There had been angry words, followed by a brief scuffle.
Jerry had escaped punishment, because a master had appeared in
time to learn the truth, but Welch had never forgiven
the humiliation.

"He shouldn't have hazed Peters," Jerry continued. "The kid was
scared half out of his wits."

"I know. I told Herb so afterward."

"Yes, afterward."

Ned flushed. "Now, Jerry, that's not fair."

Jerry regretted the words, but his pride would not let
him withdraw them.

"What did Welch want me to do to prove myself?"
he asked. "Stand on my head on the chapel roof?"

"He didn't say."

"Perhaps he wants me to rescue a maiden from a
burning building."

"You're acting like a silly ass."

"Maybe. But I know Herb. He's rough and bossy, and
because he's captain of the Nine this year he thinks
he owns the school."

"That's nonsense. And Herb isn't certain of being captain."

"He will be. He has half the Athletic Committee under
his thumb."

Ned faced him impatiently. "Listen, Jerry. I'm telling you this
because you're my chum. The vote was postponed, not rejected.
If you go about with a chip on your shoulder,
you'll make matters worse."

"And if I smile sweetly at Welch, he may graciously
allow me into his precious society?"

"Our society," corrected Ned.

Jerry snatched his cap from the radiator.

"Not mine."

He strode away, leaving Ned to look after him with
a troubled frown.

For the remainder of the morning Jerry was in no
agreeable humor. In mathematics he gave a foolish answer and
heard a boy behind him chuckle. At luncheon he found
himself seated within two places of Welch and ate without
joining the talk. Once Welch glanced toward him, but Jerry
pretended not to see.

The Lyceum was only a school society. Jerry knew that.
Its weekly debates were not always brilliant, its room over
the old library was rather shabby, and its silver badge
was small enough. Nevertheless, membership carried distinction. The best scholars
belonged, together with the leaders of the Eleven, the Nine,
and the Track Team. To be passed over was unpleasant;
to be passed over through the influence of Herb Welch
was worse.

And there was baseball to consider.

Practice would begin in the cage within a fortnight. Jerry
had counted upon trying for shortstop. If Welch became captain—and
almost everyone believed he would—he might easily favor another candidate.
It would be like him, Jerry thought bitterly.

By three o'clock the rain had stopped. A pale brightness
showed behind the western clouds, and Jerry decided to walk
into town. He needed new shoelaces and a bottle of
ink, but chiefly he wanted to be away from the
Academy and from the sight of the Lyceum pin upon
Ned's coat.

The thaw had transformed the road into a strip of
brown mud. Water ran in the gutters and gurgled beneath
crusts of dirty snow. From the hill Jerry could see
the river, swollen and dark, curving past Winthrop's playing fields
toward the iron bridge below the town. For a week
it had been sealed with ice. Now the surface was
broken by wide black lanes, and ragged white cakes drifted
slowly downstream.

There were boys on the ice in spite of the
thaw.

Jerry counted a dozen near the boathouse and several more
farther up. Their sweaters made bright spots against the gray
river. They darted between pools of slush, shouting to one
another as their skates rang over the harder stretches.

"Crazy loons," he muttered.

The Faculty had forbidden skating below the bend, but the
order was difficult to enforce. The morning rain had made
the ice plainly unsafe. Along the banks it had drawn
away from the frozen earth, leaving strips of open water,
while near midstream the current showed through long, jagged cracks.

Ned had wanted him to skate that afternoon. Jerry had
refused, partly because he disliked the look of the river
and partly because he had no desire for company.

He completed his errands in town and lingered at a
bookseller's window. When he started back it was after four.
The clouds had lifted, but a cold wind was blowing
from the west, sending black factory smoke across the lower
roofs. The thawing road was beginning to stiffen underfoot.

As Jerry approached the bridge, he heard the distant cries
of the skaters. Most were leaving the river. Two boys
stood on the bank near the boathouse, pulling off their
skates, and three others were climbing the path to the
Academy.

Jerry wondered if Ned was among them. Then he thought
of the morning's conversation and looked away.

He had gone perhaps a hundred yards beyond the bridge
road when a shout caused him to turn.

At first he saw nothing unusual. The river lay below
him, turbid and ice-strewn. The bridge crossed it nearly a
quarter of a mile downstream, its dark iron span resting
upon two stone pillars. Beyond it the current narrowed between
steep banks.

Again came the shout.

This time Jerry saw a moving figure near the middle
of the river.

A boy was standing on a detached field of ice.

For an instant Jerry could not understand how it had
happened. Then he saw the broad lane of water separating
the floe from the ice above. A great section had
broken loose and was drifting downstream.

The boy upon it waved both arms.

"Help!"

The cry came thinly against the wind.

Jerry raced down the slope to the bank. Two younger
lads were running along the opposite shore, but they were
far behind the drifting floe. No rope or boat was
near. The boathouse had been locked for the winter.

"Lie down!" Jerry shouted. "Keep flat!"

Whether the skater heard him or not, he dropped to
his hands and knees.

The floe was perhaps twenty feet across, but its edges
were rotten and crumbling. It turned slowly in the current,
grinding against smaller pieces. Ahead lay the bridge. Beyond the
bridge the river ran fast between rocks, where no broad
ice could remain together for more than a few seconds.

Jerry's first impulse was to plunge into the stream. He
was a strong swimmer. In summer he could cross the
river twice without resting. But the thought scarcely formed before
he dismissed it. No swimmer could live long in that
black water among the driving cakes of ice.

The bridge!

If he could reach the bridge before the floe passed
beneath it, there might be a chance. The iron floor
stood low because of the high water. A boy leaning
over the upstream rail could almost touch the top of
a large ice cake.

Jerry wheeled and ran.

The shortest course lay diagonally across a field, then down
a steep bank to the bridge road. The ground was
soaked, and his shoes sank at every stride. Behind him
the cries increased. Boys had seen the danger from the
Academy grounds and were racing toward the river, but they
were too far away to help.

Jerry glanced over his shoulder.

The floe had gained speed. Its rider lay flat, with
arms and legs spread wide. Once the ice tilted and
water swept over one edge. The boy shifted toward the
center.

Jerry stumbled over a buried stone and fell heavily upon
his left side.

A sharp pain shot through his shoulder. For a moment
he lay half stunned, his face in wet grass. Then
he heard another cry from the river.

He pushed himself up.

His left arm hung strangely, and every step jarred the
shoulder. He knew he had hurt it badly. That did
not matter now. The floe was less than two hundred
yards above the bridge.

He ran on, clutching his left elbow with his right
hand.

The bank grew steeper. Jerry slipped twice but kept his
feet. At the road he struck level ground and increased
his pace. He could hear the harsh grinding of the
ice and the rush of water against the stone piers.

A farmer's wagon stood near the town end of the
bridge. Its driver stared upstream.

"There's a lad out there!" Jerry gasped. "Get a rope!"

"Ain't got one."

"Then help me at the rail!"

The man sprang from the wagon, but Jerry was already
upon the bridge.

The floe was now only fifty yards away. It swung
around, struck a mass of broken ice, and split across
the middle with a report like a pistol shot.

The boy leaped from one half to the other.

Jerry caught a glimpse of his face.

It was Herb Welch.

The knowledge checked him for less than a stride. Surprise,
resentment, the memory of the Lyceum—all flashed through his mind
and vanished. There was only a boy on rotten ice,
with the river carrying him toward the bridge.

"Welch!" Jerry shouted. "Come to the near edge!"

Herb looked up. Recognition showed in his face, but he
said nothing. He crawled toward the side nearest Jerry.

The floe would not pass close to the bank. The
main current was bearing it toward the first stone pillar,
some twelve feet from the bridge rail. If it struck
the squared masonry, it would break into fragments.

Jerry ran out upon the span.

"Get that wagon rein!" he called to the farmer.

The man hurried back, but there was no time to
wait.

Jerry climbed onto the lower iron brace and leaned over
the rail. The pain in his shoulder was sickening. He
tried to use his left hand and found that he
could not raise the arm.

The floe rushed nearer.

"Stand up!" he shouted.

Herb obeyed cautiously. Water washed over his skates.

"When you come under me, jump!"

"I can't reach that far."

"Jump anyway!"

The ice struck a smaller cake and lurched. Herb fell
to one knee. The bridge seemed to leap upstream as
the current swept him on.

Already the edge of the floe was beneath the ironwork.

Jerry stretched down with his right arm. His fingers were
still two feet above Herb's head.

"Jump!"

Herb sprang.

His hand struck Jerry's wrist, slipped, then caught it again.
Jerry closed his fingers around Herb's sleeve.

At the same instant the floe crashed against the stone
pillar.

It broke apart with a roar. One section turned upright
and vanished. Another spun past beneath the bridge. Herb's feet
dropped into the river, and the current dragged his body
downstream.

The wrench nearly tore Jerry from the rail.

His injured shoulder struck the iron brace. White pain blinded
him. He felt himself sliding over the edge and flung
one leg around an upright post.

For an instant neither boy spoke.

Jerry hung head downward outside the rail, his right hand
locked about Herb's wrist. Herb swung below him with the
water rushing to his waist. The pull of the current
was relentless.

"Catch the bridge!" Jerry gasped.

Herb tried to reach the ironwork with his free hand,
but it was beyond him.

"I can't."

Jerry tightened his grip. His fingers were already numb. The
awkward position drove the edge of the rail into his
chest, and the injured shoulder felt as though it were
being torn loose.

Above, the farmer clattered onto the bridge with the leather
reins.

"Hold on, young feller!"

"Hurry!"

The farmer seized Jerry's belt, but he could not pull
him back without forcing Jerry to release Herb.

"Loop the rein around him!" Jerry cried.

The man leaned over, fumbling with the strap. It fell
short.

Herb's wet sleeve began to slide through Jerry's hand.

"Get your other hand up!" Jerry ordered.

"I'm trying."

Herb's face was pale, but he was strangely calm. He
made another effort, caught Jerry's wrist with his free hand,
and held there.

The added weight sent a fresh stab through Jerry's body.
A red mist clouded his sight.

"I can't keep this up," he thought.

From the bridge entrance came the pounding of feet and
excited voices. The Academy boys were arriving, but they seemed
a mile away.

Herb looked up.

"Let go, Hutton," he said quietly. "You'll go in, too."

Jerry stared at him.

"Shut up!"

"You can't hold me."

"I said shut up!"

The farmer had drawn back the rein and was making
another loop. His hands were clumsy with cold.

Jerry's grip slipped another inch.

Then Ned Gordon reached them.

"What do we do?" he cried.

"Take my legs—some of you! Get over the rail!"

Ned and two others seized Jerry about the knees and
waist. Another boy snatched the rein from the farmer, climbed
onto the lower brace, and swung its loop beneath Herb's
shoulders.

"Got it!" he shouted.

"Pull!"

They hauled together.

For one dreadful second Herb remained pinned against the current.
Then his body rose. Ned dragged Jerry backward over the
rail, while the others pulled upon the rein. Herb struck
the ironwork, caught it, and was lifted across.

All of them collapsed in a heap upon the bridge
floor.

Jerry heard boys shouting questions. Someone laughed in the strained,
foolish way boys sometimes laugh when a great danger has
passed. Herb lay on his back, drenched to the skin
but conscious. Ned bent over Jerry.

"Are you all right?"

"My shoulder," Jerry muttered.

He tried to sit up and fainted.

When Jerry next opened his eyes, he was in the
Academy infirmary. The room smelled of soap and medicine. A
shaded lamp stood on the table, and beyond it sat
Ned, reading a book upside down.

"Hello," Jerry said.

Ned jumped to his feet.

"You're awake!"

"So it seems. What's the matter with me?"

"Broken collarbone. Doctor's got you strapped up like a package.
You'll be all right, though."

Jerry moved and decided not to repeat the experiment.

"Welch?"

"Not a scratch. He swallowed some river and was nearly
frozen, but he's in his room now. He wanted to
come over. Doctor wouldn't let him."

Jerry looked at the ceiling.

"So much for shortstop."

"What do you mean?"

"I'll be out of the Baseball Team."

"Only for a while. The doctor says the bone ought
to knit before the season is well started."

"With my luck, Welch will fill the place and keep
me scrubbing bats."

Ned put down the inverted book.

"Jerry, you're a hopeless idiot."

"Thanks."

"I mean it. Herb has told everyone exactly what happened.
He says you saved his life."

"The farmer and the fellows did as much as I
did."

"They wouldn't have been there in time if you hadn't
held him."

Jerry was silent. He remembered Herb swinging beneath the bridge
and saying, with remarkable steadiness, "Let go." The recollection left
him less satisfied with his former opinion of the senior.

"He behaved pretty well himself," Jerry admitted. "He knew I
was slipping."

Ned smiled. "That's something, coming from you."

"Oh, get out."

"I will. Matron says you're not to talk too much."

Jerry closed his eyes. "Ned?"

"Yes?"

"About what I said this morning—I was sore. I didn't
mean you'd licked anybody's boots."

"I know."

"And I suppose I acted like a silly ass."

"You did," said Ned cheerfully.

Two days later Jerry was permitted to sit up. His
shoulder remained bound, and his left arm rested in a
sling. He had received visits from masters, members of his
class, the captain of the Eleven, and even young Peters,
who stood awkwardly beside the bed and said he had
always known Jerry was "the right sort."

Herb did not come.

Jerry told himself that he did not care. Nevertheless, each
time footsteps sounded in the corridor he glanced toward the
door.

On the third evening Ned entered with three senior boys.
All wore the silver pin of the Lyceum.

"What's this?" asked Jerry. "A committee to see whether I'm
likely to survive?"

"Something like that," said Ned.

The seniors parted, and Herb Welch stepped into the room.

His usual self-assurance was gone. He crossed to Jerry's chair
and stood looking down at him.

"I've been trying to get in," he said. "Doctor kept
me away."

"That's all right."

"No, it isn't. There are several things I've got to
say."

Jerry shifted uneasily. "You don't owe me anything, Welch."

"I owe you more than I can pay."

"That's rot. Anybody would have done the same."

"No," said Herb. "A good many fellows would have tried.
You did it."

The others were silent.

Herb continued, "I was the one who opposed you at
the Lyceum meeting."

"I guessed that."

"I said you hadn't proved yourself. I thought you were
too quick-tempered and too ready to set yourself against older
fellows."

Jerry's face grew warm.

"Maybe I am."

"And maybe I was sore because of that Peters affair,"
Herb added. "You were right then. I was wrong."

Jerry looked at him in surprise.

Herb took a small silver pin from his pocket.

"There was a special meeting this afternoon. Your election was
unanimous. But I asked the fellows to let me bring
you the badge, because I'm the man who'll nominate you
formally when you're able to attend."

He held out his hand, then noticed Jerry's sling and
offered the other one instead.

"You—you proved yourself down at the bridge, old chum."

Jerry gripped the hand.

Outside, the evening bell began to ring across the snowy
campus. The boys moved closer about his chair, talking all
at once of the river, the coming baseball season, and
the place that would be waiting for him upon the
Nine.

For a moment Jerry could not answer. He looked at
the little badge in Herb's palm and then beyond it
at the faces surrounding him.

Only that morning, it seemed, he had wanted the Lyceum
more than anything else in Winthrop. Now the pin itself
appeared the least important part of what he had gained.

"Thanks, fellows," he said at last. "I'll try not to
make you sorry."

Herb fastened the badge upon Jerry's coat.

"No fear of that," he replied.

My wife awakened me by touching my shoulder.

At first I did not open my eyes. I heard
her breathing, quick and restrained, and thought one of the
children was ill. Then I heard a blow from the
street below—iron upon stone, or an axe biting into wood.
Another blow answered it. After that came many blows, irregular
yet joined together, as if a hundred invisible carpenters were
building one enormous house in the darkness.

"Wake up," she whispered. "They are raising a barricade."

I sat upright.

The room was black, but through the curtains entered a
pale spring light, not yet dawn and not moonlight either.
My wife stood beside the bed in her white nightdress,
one hand at her breast. Her hair, usually braided before
she slept, had fallen loose. I had never seen her
like that. I had lived with her eleven years; I
knew the shape of her hands, the small scar under
her chin, the sound of her slippers in the kitchen.
Yet then I did not know her.

"Where?" I asked.

"Here. Across our street."

The axes sounded again. There were hammers also, and the
dragging of heavy things. Somewhere glass broke, but no one
cried out. The city was quiet around these sounds—so quiet
that a dog barking far away seemed to bark in
our room.

"And Anton?" I asked.

"He has gone to them."

My younger brother had been sleeping on the sofa since
the police searched his lodging. His coat was no longer
on its peg. His boots were gone from beneath the
stove.

I went to the window and lifted the curtain. The
lamps had been extinguished along the street. I could distinguish
moving shoulders, bent backs, a wheel pushed upright, the pale
end of a plank, then nothing. Men carried doors from
a courtyard. A wagon lay on its side. Stones passed
from hand to hand. They worked without commands, and nevertheless
each appeared to know where his hands belonged.

Above the roofs the sky was clear. For the first
time in years no factory smoke covered it. The chimneys
stood useless and black, and beyond them, though no fields
could be seen, the air smelled of wet earth and
young grass.

"How long have they been there?"

"Perhaps an hour. Perhaps all night."

We did not know the hour. The clock had stopped
two days before, and I had promised to repair it.
On other nights this dead clock annoyed me. Now I
was glad it had stopped. Its hands pointed to twenty
minutes after one, but that hour had passed forever. Perhaps
no hour followed it.

"Are you afraid?" I asked.

"Yes," she said.

She came beside me and looked down. A man lifted
his face, but we could not recognize him. He might
have been our neighbor, a stranger, a boy, an old
man. The darkness made them equal.

"I am afraid," my wife repeated. "But it is not
the old fear."

I understood her. The old fear had lived with us
and eaten with us. It knew the cupboard where we
kept our papers and the hollow brick where my pistol
lay wrapped in cloth. It entered when boots halted on
the stairs. It bent our heads when officials passed. It
was careful, grey, patient. This fear was different. This fear
had wings.

I lit the candle. The flame leaned, straightened, and leaned
again, like a little golden blade uncertain whom to strike.
The room appeared around it: the washbasin, the chair with
my trousers, the children's stockings drying by the stove, a
plate with crumbs. Everything was ours, and everything seemed already
abandoned.

My wife put on her skirt and fastened it with
trembling fingers.

"What are you doing?"

"I am going down."

"To the barricade?"

"Yes."

"You cannot."

She looked at me, not angrily. That made her answer
more terrible.

"Why can I not?"

"The children."

"Yes. The children."

"And if firing begins?"

"It will begin."

"If you are killed?"

"Then I shall be killed."

She said it in the same voice with which she
might have said that bread was needed. I seized her
wrists. They were warm and thin.

"Listen to me."

"No," she said. "You listen."

She drew her hands away and stood very straight. Behind
her the candle made her shadow climb the wall until
her head touched the ceiling.

"I love you. I love Misha and little Vera. You
know this. You know I have given everything to this
house. I have counted every coin, patched every shirt, sat
beside every fever. But this house is not all of
life. You are not all of life. The children are
not all of life."

Her eyes filled with tears, though her voice did not
alter.

"We have behaved as if our few rooms were the
world, as if keeping soup warm and paying rent were
eternity. But they are pulling up the stones outside. Do
you hear? Once in a life, perhaps once in a
hundred lives, a door opens. Am I to close it
because Vera may wake and ask for water?"

I released her.

The blows below came cheerfully now. Yes, cheerfully. One hammer
struck three times, paused, and another completed its phrase. It
was music without melody, a rough singing of wood, stone,
and human hands. I remembered labourers driving piles beside the
river when I was a boy. I remembered men cutting
pines in a distant forest. Yet this was neither river
work nor forest work. They were not constructing a bridge
or a house. They were constructing the morning.

"Who are you?" I asked.

She smiled through her tears.

"I do not know."

"My wife?"

"Yes."

"Only my wife?"

"No."

Then she asked, "And who are you?"

I could not answer. Yesterday I had been a clerk
dismissed for insolence, a father worried about boots, a man
who coughed in winter and owed money to the baker.
I had been thirty-eight years old and already tired. Now
I felt younger than Anton, younger than my son. My
body was the same body, but it had ceased to
be a prison.

"I must go," I said.

"Yes, comrade."

The word passed between us softly. No speaker at a
meeting had ever made it sound so solemn. She had
called many people comrade, and so had I, hurriedly and
with caution, behind locked doors. But when she called me
this, our marriage altered. I loved her no less. I
loved her beyond the boundaries in which I had kept
her.

"And you?" I asked. "What will you do, comrade?"

"I will come tomorrow."

"Tomorrow may not come."

"Then I will come when it does not come."

She explained that she would take the children to her
sister before noon, if the streets remained open. If not,
she would leave them with old Marfa downstairs. She spoke
of blankets, milk, Vera's cough. The visionary light remained in
her face while she discussed socks and porridge. That was
when I knew her courage was real. It did not
despise small things. It passed through them.

We washed in cold water. We dressed. She cut the
remaining bread, hard at the edges, and placed salt beside
it. We ate at the table without hunger. The bread
tasted of yesterday—of wages, quarrels, exhaustion, Sunday washing, letters opened
by police, winters when smoke pressed low over the roofs.
Yet I chewed it with gratitude. Yesterday had fed us
long enough to bring us here.

"Do you remember," she said, "when we first rented this
room?"

"The wallpaper was blue."

"It was never blue."

"It looked blue to me."

"It was brown and ugly."

We laughed quietly. Laughter seemed both forbidden and necessary.

A cart crashed below. Voices rose, then settled. Someone called
for rope. Someone else sang half a line of a
workers' song, and several men laughed at him. Their laughter
ascended through the clear air.

I went into the children's room.

Vera slept on her side with both hands under her
cheek. Her hair was damp at the temples. One bare
foot had escaped the blanket. I covered it and bent
over her, but I did not kiss her. I feared
that if she opened her eyes, I would become only
her father again, and my courage would shrink to the
size of that bed.

Misha lay facing the wall.

I stood beside him for some moments. Then he spoke.

"You are going down?"

His voice was awake.

"Yes."

"To Uncle Anton?"

"Yes."

He turned. He was nine years old, but in the
strange light his face seemed older. Children had heard everything
that year. We believed whispers protected them; in truth, they
understood the whispers better than we did.

"Take the pistol," he said.

"What pistol?"

"The one in the loose brick."

I looked toward the doorway. My wife was there.

"How do you know of it?" I asked.

"I saw you hide it after the search."

"You said nothing."

"Neither did you."

I almost smiled. Then he asked, "Will they kill you?"

My heart stopped—not from fear of death, but from the
impossibility of answering.

"They may try."

"Will you come back?"

"Yes," I said.

It was not a lie at that moment. I believed
return was certain, though I did not know whether I
would return in an hour, in twenty years, or only
in his memory. On such a night every promise was
true because the future had not yet learned to refuse
us.

He held out his hand. I took it, expecting a
child's grasp. He pressed my fingers firmly.

"Goodbye, Papa."

"Not goodbye."

"Then until tomorrow."

"Until tomorrow."

I removed the loose brick in our bedroom. Behind it
were the pistol, six cartridges, and two folded proclamations. The
weapon felt colder and heavier than I remembered. My wife
wrapped it in my scarf and helped place it inside
my coat.

For a little while we stood facing each other.

There were words I should have spoken. I should have
thanked her for the years, begged forgiveness for my impatience,
told her where certain papers were hidden. I said none
of these things. They belonged to the life that had
ended. She adjusted my collar.

"Are you afraid now?" she asked.

"Yes."

"So am I."

"We shall go nevertheless."

"Yes, comrade."

We kissed once. It was a calm kiss, almost grave,
and there was no clinging. The woman who released me
was not the woman who had awakened me. The man
who turned toward the door was not the man who
had slept in that bed.

The stairway was completely dark. It smelled of cabbage water,
damp plaster, soot, and the refuse someone had left on
the second landing. My hand slid along the greasy wall.
Below me a door opened, letting in a narrow bar
of silver street light, then closed again.

With each step the hammering grew louder.

I had descended those stairs thousands of times—to work, to
the market, to borrow money, to fetch a doctor. Never
had I known how deep they were. Never had I
known that a staircase could lead out of one existence
into another.

At the final landing I paused.

Behind me were my wife, my children, the candle, the
stale bread, the stopped clock. Before me were shadows, stones,
rifles perhaps, wounds perhaps, and men whose faces I could
not yet see. I knew nothing. I did not know
whether the barricade would stand until sunrise. I did not
know whether my brother lived. I did not know if
the freedom we imagined had any place upon the earth.

Yet joy rose in me, immense and almost painful.

I opened the door.

The spring night entered like a flood, and I went
out to meet it.

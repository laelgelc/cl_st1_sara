### The Matter of a Chin

"Mr. Addicks!"

Mr. Cowdin could put more venom into respectful address than
another man could put into profanity. He did not call;
he detonated.

Croly Addicks rose from his stool in the purchasing department
of the Harcourt-Pemble Piano Company and came forward with an
invoice trembling in his hand.

"Yes, Mr. Cowdin?"

Cowdin rotated in his swivel chair. First came the knees,
then the waistcoat, then the broad, concave face, and last
of all the jaw—a well-razored knob of blue-black authority thrust
out as if to arrive before the rest of him.

"What," he asked, tapping the invoice, "is this?"

Croly knew what it was. He had entered it, checked
it, rechecked it, and carried it to Cowdin. He knew
that it represented forty-eight gross of nickel-plated hammer-rail screws billed
at eleven cents above contract. He knew also that the
mistake belonged to the invoice clerk, who was Cowdin's nephew.
Knowledge, however, has never been a complete substitute for a
chin.

"It's the Brophy Metal bill," he quavered.

"Oh, it is?" Cowdin appeared amazed that a document could
have a name. "And did you happen to look at
it?"

"Yes, sir."

"With your eyes?"

Two stenographers stopped typing. One smiled down at her keys.
The other leaned sideways for a better view of Croly's
face, which began promisingly at the forehead and nose, then
sloped backward in mild surrender. Nature had planned a chin
there, perhaps, but had been called to another job.

"I noticed the overcharge," Croly said. "I marked it in
blue pencil."

Cowdin prowled with one finger along the paper until he
found the mark.

"You marked it."

"Yes, sir."

"And then you brought it to me."

"I thought you'd want—"

"You thought!" Cowdin sprang upon the word. "Mr. Addicks, this
company does not pay assistant clerks to think. When the
Harcourt-Pemble Piano Company desires thought, it secures thought from executives."

This was the rule in most vast enterprises. The lower
employees were hired because they knew the work; the higher
ones were retained because they knew who should be blamed
for it.

"I'm sorry," said Croly.

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate."

"But the invoice is inaccurate, Mr. Cowdin."

Cowdin paused. The stenographers paused. Even the pneumatic tube seemed
to hold its breath.

Cowdin pushed his jaw farther out. Croly's face, by contrast,
appeared to withdraw into his collar.

"Are you contradicting me, Mr. Addicks?"

"No, sir."

"I thought not."

Cowdin initialed the invoice precisely where Croly had indicated the
error and tossed it back.

"Take it up with Brophy. And get some snap into
you. A man without snap is no use in business."

Croly returned to his stool under the sympathetic regard of
seventeen desks, no two of which would have risked sympathy
aloud. He wanted to whirl, stride back, and tell Cowdin
that during eight years with Harcourt-Pemble he had learned every
contract, supplier, discount, freight route, and trade trick in the
department; that Cowdin could not distinguish a piano caster from
a soup plate unless both were labeled; and that the
company's celebrated purchasing efficiency was largely a system by which
Croly did the work while Cowdin wore the jaw.

In this fantasy Croly's voice rang like a bugle. Cowdin
shrank. The stenographers applauded. The president entered and said, "Addicks,
the firm needs men like you."

Actually Croly sharpened a pencil that did not require sharpening.

That day he was thirty years old.

Thirty is not ancient except to a man earning twenty-eight
dollars a week and wishing to marry on it. At
ten o'clock his mother had telephoned to remind him that
he had been born at precisely ten-seven in the morning.
At ten-seven Cowdin had ordered him to locate a missing
memorandum which lay beneath Cowdin's elbow. Thus Croly entered his
fourth decade crawling under a filing cabinet.

There was, however, the promotion.

Old Mr. Basset, assistant purchasing agent, had retired to raise
chickens in New Jersey, poultry being less treacherous than office
politics and, on the whole, better paid. This left a
vacancy. Cowdin had hinted that seniority and experience would be
considered. Croly possessed both in embarrassing quantities.

The notice was to be posted after lunch.

With the increase, he might marry Emily.

Emily said she did not care about money. What she
meant was that she did not care about excessive money.
She still retained a modest prejudice in favor of rent,
groceries, and shoes. Whenever Croly spoke of marriage, she squeezed
his hand and said, "Oh, Croly, I could live on
almost nothing."

Then, after reflection, she would add, "But could two people?"

At noon Croly descended to the basement beanery on Twenty-third
Street where payment was conducted by conscience. The customer selected
his food, ate it, and upon departing announced what he
had consumed. This noble confidence in human goodness was reinforced
by three cashiers, two mirrors, an elevated observation cage, and
a large man in a derby hat known as the
spotter.

The spotter had been selected for his gift of judging
character from the rear. He could tell, merely by the
droop of a shoulder, whether a customer had forgotten a
cruller. He never interfered with truck drivers, pugilists, or gentlemen
whose necks began immediately behind their ears. Long experience had
taught him that dishonesty flourishes most dangerously among small clerks.

Croly carried a bowl of soup, a plate of beans,
two rolls, and coffee through the jangle and clatter. At
the cashier's desk he said, "Thirty-five."

"Fawty-fi'," said the cashier.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Fawty-fi' cents."

"But the soup is ten, the beans fifteen, the rolls
five, and coffee five."

"Two coffees."

"One coffee."

The cashier looked past him. The spotter pounced.

"Come awn, buddy. Don't hold up the line."

"I'm not holding it up. I had one coffee."

The spotter's derby leaned toward Croly. Beneath it was a
face constructed in heavy masonry.

"Cashier says two."

"She is mistaken."

"Wadda yuh tryin' to pull?"

Heads turned. A spoon ceased halfway to a mouth. Croly
felt the whole establishment staring at his retreating lower face.
He could have paid the extra dime. The dime itself
was nothing—unless one wished to marry, in which case dimes
assembled at night and became furniture.

"I'm not trying to pull anything," he said.

"Sure. They never are."

A muscular man beside Croly announced twenty cents after eating
a steak, pie, milk, and what appeared to have been
part of the crockery. The spotter did not hear him.
He was busy with Croly.

"I had one coffee," Croly repeated.

"Listen, don't get fresh."

Fresh! Croly had spent thirty years being stale in order
not to offend anybody.

He paid forty-five cents.

Outside, in Madison Square, he sat upon a bench beneath
the gray tower and examined mankind with bitterness. Pigeons strutted
before him, equipped with more chin than they needed. Newsboys
shouted. Motorcars snorted. Everywhere the city honored protrusion. Buildings pushed
into the sky, horns thrust through traffic, elbows advanced through
crowds, and jaws projected across desks. Only Croly Addicks receded.

Chinless!

The old epithet. The old curse.

At seven, boys had called him Chicken-Face. At twelve, his
teacher had asked why he always looked frightened. At sixteen,
a barber had attempted to sell him a beard tonic
with the grave kindness of a surgeon. Once, in a
movie house, Emily had watched a square-jawed hero rescue a
young lady from smugglers and sighed.

Croly had not forgotten the sigh.

He loved Emily partly because she had never mentioned his
chin and partly because he always feared she would. She
had brown eyes, practical gloves, and a way of listening
that made his smallest office adventure sound like the Russian
Revolution.

"If you get the promotion," she had said last Sunday,
"we could look at that flat on One Hundred and
Ninth."

"When," Croly corrected boldly.

"Yes," she said. "When."

But afterward she had studied him with troubled tenderness. Emily
understood that Croly deserved advancement. She also understood Harcourt-Pemble.

He knew the business. That was indisputable. If a dealer
in Omaha demanded a replacement action for a Model G
concert grand manufactured in 1913, Croly knew the serial range,
the shipping weight, and why the original felt had failed
in damp climates. He could detect a fraudulent freight allowance
at twenty paces. He had once saved the company three
thousand dollars by discovering that a varnish contractor counted barrels
before deducting leakage. Cowdin had received a bonus.

Still, Croly had permitted this. That was the shameful part.
Every rebuke accepted without protest had pressed his character backward
until it had become as chinless as his face.

He imagined entering the office after lunch and saying, "Cowdin,
I know what you are."

In the fantasy he called him Cowdin.

Then he imagined Cowdin saying, "What did you call me,
Mr. Addicks?"

The fantasy collapsed.

Croly unfolded the evening paper although it was still noon
and stared at a photograph of a prizefighter. The fighter's
jaw projected with the architectural confidence of a hotel balcony.
Beneath it appeared the caption: "Battling Grogan, Who Fights His
Way to Success."

That was the American method. One fought one's way to
success, although in offices the fighting commonly consisted of making
a quieter man carry the files.

Croly threw the paper away. A breeze immediately returned it
to his ankles.

At the corner soda fountain he decided to buy himself
a birthday drink. This was extravagance, but thirty comes only
once, which is perhaps its best feature.

The clerk behind the marble counter was a youth with
brilliant hair and the bored majesty of a Roman emperor.

"Chocolate malted," Croly said.

"Chocolate what?"

"Malted milk."

"Say it, can't yuh?"

Croly blinked. "I did say it."

The youth imitated him in a thin, fading squeak: "'Chocolate
malted milk.' Gawsh!"

Two girls at the counter laughed. One glanced at Croly's
profile.

His face burned.

The clerk slammed down a glass in which several islands
of powder floated in pale fluid.

"This isn't mixed," Croly ventured.

"Wadda yuh expeck for fifteen cents, the Waldorf?"

Croly wanted to seize the glass and pour it over
the brilliant hair. He saw himself doing it as you've
seen it done in the movies: one quick movement, a
gasp from the girls, the clerk emerging chocolate-brown and chastened.
Croly would then place fifteen cents upon the counter and
walk out slowly while everyone recognized that a dangerous man
had been among them.

Instead he drank the lumps.

On the sidewalk he caught his reflection in a window.
Hat, nose, collar—and then nothing. No wonder Cowdin bullied him.
No wonder the spotter had pounced. No wonder the soda
clerk had selected him for ridicule from all the available
population of New York.

Yet surely a chin could not balance eight years of
faithful service. Surely somewhere behind the mahogany doors of Harcourt-Pemble
there existed a calm intelligence studying records, weighing merit, and
saying, "Addicks is the man."

He returned to the office at one-twenty.

Something had happened.

The typewriters were going, but they went self-consciously. Clerks bent
over papers with the exaggerated industry of schoolchildren concealing a
mouse. Near the bulletin board stood a loose half-circle of
employees who moved away as Croly entered.

The notice had been posted.

Cowdin's office door was open. Cowdin sat within, smoking a
cigar, his jaw visible through the glass partition like the
prow of a battleship. He saw Croly and smiled—not pleasantly,
not unpleasantly, but officially.

Croly's heart rose and lodged just below his Adam's apple.

He removed his hat. He replaced it. He removed it
again and placed it on a desk belonging to someone
else.

The bulletin board was thirty feet away. Between Croly and
it lay two rows of desks, a wastebasket, the water
cooler, eight years of service, Emily's brown eyes, the flat
on One Hundred and Ninth Street, and every taunt ever
directed at his profile.

"Go on," whispered one stenographer.

He looked at her. Was she kind? Amused? Sorry?

Cowdin called from behind the glass:

"Mr. Addicks!"

Croly quailed automatically. Then, with an effort that seemed to
lift the Metropolitan Tower, he straightened.

His chin did not come forward. It had never come
forward and never would. But the rest of him, after
thirty years of retreat, took one step.

Then another.

The office grew very quiet as Croly Addicks approached the
bulletin board.

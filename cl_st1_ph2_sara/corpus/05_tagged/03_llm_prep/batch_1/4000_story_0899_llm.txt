# The Measure of a Policeman

"One, two, three—bend!"

Peter Mullaney bent.

"One, two, three—up!"

Peter came up so violently that the wooden dumbbells nearly
escaped through the gymnasium roof.

"One, two, three—bend!"

There were forty-eight recruits in the drill hall that winter
morning, and forty-seven of them performed the exercise with the
sober misery of men lifting a mortgage. Peter alone was
happy. Happiness fizzed through him from the soles of his
regulation shoes to the topmost flame of his red hair.
His freckles seemed to applaud. His blue eyes shone upon
the instructor, the wall bars, the medicine balls, and even
the framed list of departmental offenses as though these were
the gates, towers and golden pavements of paradise.

"One, two, three—up!"

Up came Peter.

He was not a large man. Indeed, if every inch
of Peter Mullaney had been put into a savings bank
when he was born, accumulated interest would still have left
him short of police height. But what he lacked vertically
he had invested sideways in shoulders, chest and arms. His
muscles sat beneath his skin like hard-boiled eggs in a
basket. His neck began behind his ears and continued without
unnecessary scenery into his coat. He weighed one hundred and
sixty pounds, and only about twelve of them were hesitation.

In the line beside him was Recruit Callahan, who stood
six feet two and had the melancholy face of a
horse that has heard bad news.

"Peter," whispered Callahan, "if he says bend again, I'm going
to break in the middle."

"Ye'll bend," Peter whispered back. "An' ye'll be proud to
bend. Ye're bendin' for the finest police department in America."

"One, two, three—bend!"

Peter bent with patriotic enthusiasm.

His delight was not new. It had begun twenty-two years
earlier, when he was five and Officer McGurk carried him
across a flooded gutter without removing his helmet. From that
sacred hour Peter had known what he was put upon
earth to be.

Other boys wished to drive fire engines, command battleships, discover
gold, pitch for the Giants or own candy stores where
the inventory could be eaten. Peter wished only to become
a policeman. He played arrest beneath the tenement stairs. He
directed traffic composed of three baby carriages, a mongrel dog
and Mrs. Finnerty's washing basket. He acquired a broken broom
handle and called it his nightstick. When his mother sent
him for potatoes he brought back, in addition, reports of
every suspicious person encountered on the route.

At nine he captured a chicken thief, who proved to
be the chicken's owner.

At twelve he organized the Mullaney Avenue Detective Bureau, whose
sole case concerned the disappearance of a pie. The inquiry
ended when Peter's younger brother became ill.

At sixteen he prevented a runaway by seizing its bridle
and being dragged through four mud puddles, one ash heap
and the display window of a secondhand-clothing store. The newspapers
printed three lines about it. Peter cut them out and
carried them until the paper became as soft as linen.

At twenty-one he applied to the police department.

The measuring officer placed him against the wall, lowered the
brass arm upon his red head and frowned.

"Short."

Peter's heart fell the distance of a grand piano dropped
from a roof.

"How much?" he asked.

"Half an inch."

It was not really half an inch. It was perhaps
the distance between a bumblebee's eyes. But police departments do
not measure men in bumblebees. They measure them in feet
and inches, and an inch in a regulation is longer
than a mile in a man's hopes.

Peter went home. For three months he hung by his
knees, slept without a pillow, stretched from door frames, wore
heavy weights upon his ankles and entrusted himself to a
Professor Zimble, who advertised that he could increase the human
stature while you waited. Professor Zimble increased Peter's bill by
seven dollars and his height by nothing visible to science.

Then Peter applied again.

"Short," said the measuring officer.

"But I stretched."

"Not enough."

Peter swallowed.

"Measure me whin I breathe in."

"You were breathing in."

"Thin measure me whin I'm thinkin' tall."

The measuring officer nearly smiled, which was against regulations.

"See Commissioner Kondorman," he said. "He'll tell you no in
person."

Police Commissioner Abel Kondorman was a long, gray, ironed man.
His collars were stiff, his desk was bare, and his
opinions had corners. He believed in systems, classifications, examinations and
the decimal point. If a riot occurred at 3:17, he
wanted it suppressed at 3:18 and entered on Form 44-B
at 3:19. He had once reorganized the mounted squad according
to the color, age and digestive habits of the horses.
Even his eyebrows appeared to have passed civil-service tests.

Peter stood before his desk and poured out his heart.

"I know I'm short, Commissioner, sir. But I'm strong. Ask
anybody. I don't drink. I don't gamble. I never got
arrested, except once by mistake, an' I helped the officer
find the man he wanted. I can run ten miles.
I can swim the river. I can lift two hundred
and forty pounds over me head, an' if ye'll only
give me a chance I'll—"

"Stop."

Peter stopped so thoroughly that even his freckles seemed to
hold their breath.

Kondorman looked him over.

"The regulation exists for a reason."

"Yes, sir."

"The public respects size."

"Yes, sir."

"A policeman must command."

"Yes, sir."

"A policeman must also possess intelligence, judgment, memory, education, endurance,
sobriety, courage and moral character."

"Yes, sir. I got some of them."

"Which?"

"I got endurance."

Kondorman's mouth moved one thirty-second of an inch. In another
man it might have been laughter.

He sent for Peter's school records, employment references and neighborhood
history. These established that Peter had left school at fourteen
to help support six persons; that he had worked steadily
ever since; that he had never been discharged, summoned, drunk
or dishonest; that he had saved a child from a
cellar fire; that he contributed each week to his widowed
mother; and that seventeen citizens, including a priest, a rabbi,
two grocers and a woman who disliked everybody, considered him
trustworthy.

Kondorman summoned him again.

"You are below height."

Peter nodded miserably.

"You are deficient in formal education."

Peter nodded lower.

"You have, however, an exceptional physical record and an excellent
character."

Peter's head came up.

"I am admitting you conditionally to the training school."

The room began to revolve about Peter like a merry-go-round.

"You will receive no indulgence. You must earn excellent marks
in every subject. Not adequate marks. Excellent marks."

"Yes, sir!"

"In every subject."

"Yes, sir!"

"Failure in one means dismissal."

"Yes, sir!"

"Do you understand?"

Peter leaned across the desk, seized Kondorman's hand and shook
it until three pencils rolled from alignment.

"Commissioner, sir, I'll know everything there is to know."

"That," said Kondorman, recovering both his hand and his pencils,
"is a considerable promise."

Peter meant to keep it.

He conquered the gymnasium first. He ran, vaulted, climbed and
boxed. He swarmed up ropes like a red-headed squirrel. He
carried two recruits down a ladder at once. He broke
the academy record for lifting a sandbag and nearly broke
the sandbag. When the instructor shouted, "One, two, three—bend!" Peter
bent as if the honor of the republic depended upon
the angle of his spine.

Then came the classroom.

There Peter discovered that the republic depended on a great
many other things.

A policeman, Commissioner Kondorman held, should not merely know how
to stop a fight. He should know why men fought,
where fighting had first been invented, which ancient civilization fought
most efficiently, how much fighting was permissible under municipal law,
and upon which numbered form the result ought to be
reported.

Peter studied criminal law, city ordinances, first aid, geography, penmanship,
public hygiene, arithmetic, political organization, fire prevention, evidence, traffic control,
elementary chemistry and the historical development of sewerage.

He learned the penalties for keeping pigs above the second
floor; the correct method of approaching a mad dog; the
difference between burglary, larceny, robbery and a fellow borrowing an
umbrella forever; the names of six mayors; the symptoms of
seventeen poisons; the flags of thirty nations; the width of
lawful pushcarts; the treatment of broken ribs; the population of
Montana; the location of Madagascar; the speed of streetcars at
crossings; the rights of householders, tenants, lodgers, peddlers, cabmen, undertakers,
children, horses and geese.

He learned that the Nile flowed north, which seemed to
him contrary.

He learned that the earth was flattened at the poles,
although no witness was named.

He learned that the Tropic of Cancer was north of
the equator and the Tropic of Capricorn south of it.

That last fact gave him particular trouble.

"The Tropic of Capricorn," said Instructor Peabody, tapping the map,
"is an imaginary line at approximately twenty-three and one-half degrees
south latitude."

Peter copied it carefully.

At home he repeated it while eating supper.

"Twenty-three an' a half degrees south."

His mother looked alarmed. "Is it dangerous?"

"No, Ma. It's imaginary."

"Then why are ye worryin' about it?"

"Because Commissioner Kondorman might ask me where it is."

Mrs. Mullaney considered this.

"Tell him it's wherever he says. A commissioner should know
his own business."

Peter's fiancée, Nora Dacey, helped him study. Nora was small
enough to make Peter appear stately, practical enough to balance
his dreams, and pretty enough to cause him to forget
the capitals of South America whenever she leaned over his
shoulder.

"Chile," she would say.

"Santiago."

"Peru."

"Lima."

"Brazil."

"Rio de Janeiro."

"Tropic of Capricorn."

Peter scowled at the wall.

"Twenty-three an' a half—something."

"South latitude."

"I knew it."

"Where does it pass?"

"South America, Africa, Australia."

"And?"

"An' the Bronx."

"No, Peter."

"Well, it ought to. Every other imaginary line in the
world passes through the Bronx."

Nora laughed. Peter did not. To him the Tropic of
Capricorn had become a thin black serpent lying across his
future.

He studied on streetcars, at meals and in bed. He
carried cards in his pockets. On one side Nora wrote
a question and on the other its answer. Peter pulled
them out at random.

"What is habeas corpus?"

"A man has to be brought before a judge."

"What is the capital of Nevada?"

"Carson City."

"What is the boiling point of water?"

"Depends whether the stove is workin'."

"Peter!"

"Two hundred an' twelve degrees."

"What is the Tropic of Capricorn?"

Peter groaned.

Yet he passed his weekly examinations. Not brilliantly, but by
ferocious effort. His marks hung exactly at the edge of
excellence, peering over the precipice. Kondorman watched him. Peter knew
it. This caused facts to flee his brain whenever the
commissioner entered a classroom.

During field training he was assigned to Officer Timothy Gaffney.

Gaffney had served twenty-six years, weighed two hundred and thirty
pounds, and walked with the leisurely majesty of a courthouse.
His face was broad, weathered and genial. One of his
ears had been folded during a dock strike in 1907
and had never opened again. He knew every shopkeeper, loafer,
widow, newsboy, thief, dog and disputed property line in the
precinct.

"The first thing to learn, Pether," he said, as they
began the evening tour, "is that the street is a
book."

"Yes, sir."

"Every doorway is a paragraph. Every face is punctuation. A
crowd is an exclamation point."

Peter nodded reverently.

"An' a patrolman who tries to read it all at
once walks into a lamppost."

Peter looked forward just in time.

Gaffney taught him when to interfere and when to wait;
how to distinguish the sound of a pistol shot from
an automobile backfire; how to calm a drunk without offending
him; how to lift an injured man; how to observe
hands; how to enter a dark hallway; how to ask
a question twice without seeming to repeat it.

He also taught Peter that the law, although printed in
black and white, was administered among people, who came in
every color of confusion.

A fruit dealer accused three boys of stealing apples. Gaffney
discovered that they had stolen two, knocked down nine, eaten
one and returned another because it contained a worm. He
made them stack the fallen fruit, pay for the eaten
apple by sweeping the sidewalk, and apologize to the worm.

A woman demanded the arrest of her husband because he
snored in violation of domestic peace.

A man reported his watch stolen and found it in
his vest.

A small girl was lost. Peter took her hand, and
she instantly ceased crying.

"You got the gift," said Gaffney.

"What gift?"

"People knowin' ye're for them."

"Is that in the manual?"

"It was left out by accident."

One rainy midnight they found a crowd before a burning
building. Smoke rolled from a third-floor window, and a woman
screamed that her father was inside. Before the fire engines
arrived, Gaffney went up. Peter followed.

They found the old man unconscious near the kitchen door.
Together they carried him down through smoke so thick that
Peter could not see his own brass buttons. On the
sidewalk he vomited, coughed, recovered, and asked if they should
go back.

Gaffney slapped his shoulder.

"That's it, lad."

"What?"

"Learn all ye can. Know the ordinances, the streets, the
first aid, the islands in the Pacific an' the private
habits of the Emperor of Japan. But remember this: a
cop never runs away when he's needed."

Peter stored the sentence in a deeper place than he
kept the capital of Nevada.

"A cop never runs away when he's needed," he repeated.

"Not if he's alone?"

"Especially then."

"Not if the other fellow has a gun?"

"Ye don't have to be a fool, Pether. Use the
doorway. Call for help. Duck. Think. But don't run away
while somebody's callin'."

That became their proverb.

When Peter hesitated before separating two fighting coal heavers, Gaffney
said, "A cop never—"

"I know."

When a rabid dog charged along the avenue and Peter
used a garbage can lid as a shield until Gaffney
shot it, Peter gasped, "A cop never runs away when
he's needed."

When they stood in sleet for an hour guarding a
broken water main, Gaffney said the doctrine did not apply
to frozen feet, and they drank coffee.

The weeks passed. Peter's uniform was issued.

It was the first completely beautiful object he had ever
owned.

He brushed it in the morning and brushed it at
night. He polished the buttons until they reflected his freckles.
He polished the shoes until the cat attempted to walk
into one, mistaking it for an open doorway. He laid
the cap upon the bureau and woke three times to
look at it.

Nora had not yet seen him wearing the full uniform.
Peter wanted to save that sight for the day he
had earned his shield. He imagined walking to her house,
ringing the bell, and standing under the hallway light. She
would open the door. Perhaps she would cry. Peter intended
to say something modest, such as, "Here I am." In
his private imagination, however, a band played.

Only the final examination remained.

For two nights he did not sleep. Facts marched through
his head in disordered battalions. Habeas corpus invaded Brazil. The
capital of Nevada boiled at two hundred and twelve degrees.
Madagascar became a municipal offense. The Tropic of Capricorn twisted
around them all.

The examination was held on Saturday morning.

Commissioner Kondorman himself supervised it. He distributed the papers with
the gravity of a judge distributing sentences.

"Three hours," he said. "No talking."

Peter wrote.

He defined burglary. He described artificial respiration. He identified the
powers of the mayor. He calculated the stopping distance of
a streetcar. He named four symptoms of arsenic poisoning, although
the fifth symptom—death—seemed to him the important one.

Then he reached geography.

**Where is the Tropic of Capricorn?**

Peter stared at the words.

He knew this.

He had known it at breakfast. Nora had asked him.

"Twenty-three and one-half degrees south latitude," he whispered inwardly.

But his mind had become a room from which every
piece of furniture had been removed.

South America, Africa, Australia.

Something else pressed upon him. A street name. A schoolroom.
A joke. The Bronx.

His pencil moved.

**The Tropic of Capricorn is in the Bronx.**

The instant he wrote it, doubt struck. He crossed out
"Bronx." Then he feared the answer was right and rewrote
it. Then he added "principally," which made the error more
authoritative.

He handed in the paper.

By Monday all the recruits knew their marks except Peter.
At noon a messenger summoned him.

Kondorman stood beside his desk. Peter recognized his examination paper.
A blue pencil had encircled one line so heavily that
it looked strangled.

"Recruit Mullaney."

"Yes, sir."

"Your mark in law was excellent."

Peter breathed.

"In first aid, excellent. In departmental procedure, excellent. In geography—failure."

The breath left him.

Kondorman turned the paper.

"The Tropic of Capricorn," he said, each word separate, "is
not in the Bronx."

"No, sir."

"It is approximately twenty-three and one-half degrees south of the
equator."

"Yes, sir. I know it now."

"You were warned. Excellent marks in every subject."

"Yes, sir."

"Conditional admission. No exceptions."

Peter looked at the paper. The blue circle widened until
it appeared to surround his entire life.

"Could I take geography over?"

"No."

"An oral examination?"

"No."

"I only missed the one."

"A standard ceases to be a standard when altered for
an individual."

Peter's fists closed at his sides.

"I've wanted this since I could walk, Commissioner."

"I know."

"I can do the work."

"I believe you can."

"I'd never disgrace the uniform."

"I believe that also."

Hope leaped, only to strike the hard ceiling of Kondorman's
face.

"But the condition was explicit," the commissioner said. "You are
dismissed from training."

Peter did not cry in the office. He waited until
he reached a lavatory three floors below, locked himself in
a stall, sat upon the lid of a plumbing fixture
and wept into both hands. He wept silently and thoroughly.
A man who had swallowed a city might have looked
as uncomfortable.

That evening he went to Nora's house in civilian clothes.

She understood before he spoke.

"Oh, Peter."

"One line," he said. "One little black line around the
earth, an' it never hurt anybody."

She drew him inside.

"I wrote it was in the Bronx."

"I know."

"How?"

"You always wanted it there."

He sat on the horsehair sofa with his shoulders hanging.
Peter's shoulders were not constructed to hang, and the effect
was terrible.

"I'm not a cop, Nora."

"You're Peter Mullaney."

"That's what I was tryin' to improve."

She knelt beside him.

"You'll find another way."

"There isn't another way. There's only the one."

For a while neither spoke. Then Peter looked toward the
closed parlor door.

"I had the uniform."

"Yes."

"I wanted ye to see me in it."

Nora's eyes filled.

"I'd have liked that."

The next afternoon Peter returned to headquarters and made one
final request.

"Commissioner, let me go out tonight."

Kondorman frowned. "Impossible."

"One last training patrol. I'll turn in the uniform after.
Officer Gaffney's assigned to me. I won't carry a weapon.
I won't make an arrest alone. I only want—"

"What?"

Peter's face reddened beneath the freckles.

"I want me girl to see me wearin' it."

Kondorman looked away toward the window. Far below, traffic moved
in regulated streams that did not always obey regulations.

"One tour," he said at last. "You remain under Officer
Gaffney's direct supervision. You possess no police authority independent of
him. At midnight you return all departmental property."

Peter swallowed hard.

"Thank you, sir."

At six he dressed in the locker room.

The blue coat fitted his wide shoulders. The polished buttons
shone. The cap sat above his red hair. There was
no shield upon his breast, but at a distance no
one could see the absence. In the mirror stood everything
Peter had dreamed of being—and everything he had ceased to
be.

Gaffney came in, pulling on his gloves.

"I heard, lad."

Peter tried to smile.

"It appears the Tropic of Capricorn is farther from the
Bronx than I thought."

"Geography," said Gaffney, "is an overrated science. Ireland was moved
six thousand miles from New York, an' nobody asked me
permission."

They started their tour.

At eight they passed Nora's house.

She was waiting by the window. When Peter appeared beneath
the streetlamp, she came out without her coat. For a
moment she simply looked at him.

The band played inside Peter after all, but it was
very far away.

"You're grand," Nora whispered.

"It's only for tonight."

"I know."

Gaffney turned his broad back and became interested in a
drainpipe.

Nora touched one brass button.

"No matter what they say, Peter, it suits you."

He wanted to tell her that they would marry soon,
that he would find good work, that this dream had
not carried away every other hope. Instead he said, "Go
inside. Ye'll catch cold."

She kissed him quickly and obeyed.

Peter and Gaffney walked on.

At ten thirty rain began. The avenue emptied. Light from
drugstores lay yellow upon the wet pavement. Elevated trains thundered
in the distance. Peter tried to impress every sight upon
his memory: the swing of Gaffney's nightstick, the pressure of
the cap against his forehead, the gleam of buttons in
shop windows, the solid beat of regulation shoes.

At midnight he would surrender these things. He imagined removing
the coat, folding it, setting the cap on the counter.
His stomach tightened.

"Cheer up," Gaffney said. "Life's long."

"So is South America."

"That's the spirit."

They turned into Halpern Street.

Halfway down the block, a woman leaned from an upstairs
window.

"Officer!"

Gaffney stopped.

"Officer! There's men in the Bernstein house. I heard glass."

The Bernstein house was dark. Its owners were away visiting
relatives. Gaffney crossed the street, tried the front door, then
slipped into the narrow passage along the side.

"Stay here, Pether."

"I'll come."

"Ye're unarmed, an' by Kondorman's orders ye're under me supervision.
Wait at the front. If anyone comes out, yell. If
ye hear trouble, use the call box."

Gaffney disappeared around the rear.

Peter stood before the house.

Rain ticked upon his cap. The street was empty. Somewhere
a clock struck eleven.

Then came a flash inside the house.

A shot cracked.

A second shot followed.

Peter ran to the call box at the corner and
pulled the alarm. As he turned back, a shape appeared
briefly behind a downstairs curtain. He heard a crash. Then
Gaffney's voice came from within—faint, harsh and unmistakable.

"Pether!"

Peter stopped.

He had no pistol. He had no shield. He had
no authority. In less than an hour he would not
even have the uniform. Kondorman had dismissed him. The department
had measured him, examined him and found him short in
inches and geography.

Another shot shattered a front window. Glass fell onto the
pavement.

Every sensible nerve in Peter shouted for him to wait.
Help was coming. He had rung the alarm. The men
inside were armed. Going in would be foolish, perhaps fatal.

He took one step backward.

Then from somewhere in memory came Gaffney's voice, genial and
certain:

A cop never runs away when he's needed.

"But I'm not a cop," Peter whispered.

From the house came a groan.

Peter looked down at the blue coat, the brass buttons,
the empty place where the shield should be.

Whether a man was a policeman, he suddenly understood, could
not be settled entirely by brass arms against walls, blue
pencils upon papers or imaginary lines around the globe. Sometimes
the question was asked in darkness, with a wounded man
behind a locked door.

Peter went in.

The front door yielded to his shoulder. He entered low
and fast.

The hallway smelled of powder, wet plaster and overturned furniture.
A man stood near the stairs with a revolver in
one hand and a sack in the other.

"Hold it!" the man shouted.

Peter did not hold it.

The revolver flamed. The bullet struck the door frame beside
his head. Peter crossed the distance before the man could
fire again. He drove both shoulders into the burglar's middle.

They hit the wall.

Plaster burst.

The revolver fell.

The burglar clawed Peter's face. Peter struck once, short and
upward. The man folded as though a trapdoor had opened
beneath his ribs.

A second burglar came from the dining room.

Peter saw the gun.

He seized the first man by the coat and swung
him. The second fired. His bullet entered his companion's shoulder.
Both men cursed, and Peter, having no time to decide
which deserved attention first, gave attention to both.

He hurled the wounded burglar into the second. They went
down among a table, two chairs and a porcelain shepherdess,
who died without understanding the municipal issues involved.

Peter kicked the gun away.

The first burglar caught his ankle. The second rose with
a brass candlestick. It descended against Peter's head.

Light exploded.

He fell to one knee.

The candlestick rose again. Peter lunged forward and caught the
man around the thighs. They crashed through the dining-room doors.
The burglar hammered Peter's back. Peter lifted him bodily and
threw him across the table.

The table broke.

The burglar did not rise.

Peter turned.

The first man had recovered the revolver.

For one suspended instant they faced each other across the
wrecked room: the burglar pale and bleeding, Peter swaying in
his blue uniform, red hair darkened with blood.

"You little fool," the man said.

He fired.

Peter felt a tremendous blow beneath his right shoulder. The
room tilted. His legs weakened.

The man came nearer.

Peter could have fallen. His body begged him to fall.
Instead he took one step, then another.

The burglar fired again.

The shot tore through Peter's sleeve.

Peter kept coming.

Perhaps it was the blood on his face, perhaps the
uniform, perhaps the alarming fact that this short man seemed
to possess no agreement with bullets. Whatever the cause, terror
entered the burglar's eyes.

He backed away.

Peter smiled faintly.

"A cop," he said, "never runs away whin he's needed."

Then Peter struck him.

It was not a scientific blow. No examination paper could
have described it. It began in Peter's regulation shoes, traveled
through his legs and back, gathered the disappointment of twenty-two
years, collected the Tropic of Capricorn on its way, and
arrived beneath the burglar's jaw with the authority of Commissioner
Kondorman himself.

The man rose several inches from the floor and came
down unconscious.

Peter stood alone.

For a moment the house was quiet except for the
rain and the distant clang of the patrol wagon.

"Gaffney," Peter remembered.

He staggered into the kitchen.

The veteran officer lay near the rear door. His revolver
was still in his hand. Blood spread across his tunic.

Peter knelt.

"I got them, Tim."

Gaffney opened his eyes.

"I knew ye would."

"Help's comin'. Ye'll be all right."

"No lies to a police officer."

Peter pressed both hands against the wound.

"Don't talk."

"Did ye run?"

Peter's mouth twisted.

"I considered it."

"That's allowed."

"I came back."

"That's required."

Gaffney breathed with difficulty. His hand moved toward his breast.
His fingers touched the shield.

"Pether."

"Yes?"

"The books are useful."

"Yes."

"Learn all ye can."

"I will."

"But the rest—"

"I know the rest."

Gaffney's eyes softened.

"Ye'll make a fine cop."

His hand fell.

Peter bent over him. The kitchen floor rose gently to
meet Peter's face, and the sound of arriving whistles traveled
down a long tunnel into darkness.

He awoke beneath white sheets.

A nurse was bending over him. Peter regarded her with
suspicion.

"Where am I?"

"St. Vincent's Hospital."

"Where are the burglars?"

"In jail."

"Gaffney?"

The nurse's expression answered.

Peter closed his eyes.

Time passed. It might have been a minute or a
year. When he opened them again, Nora sat beside the
bed holding his hand. His mother stood behind her, crying
openly. Recruit Callahan filled the doorway, and beyond him were
three uniformed officers.

Then Commissioner Kondorman entered.

He looked exactly as before—long, gray, ironed—but something in his
face had been taken apart and put together differently.

"How do you feel?" he asked.

"Short," Peter whispered.

Nora laughed and cried at once.

Kondorman pulled a chair to the bedside. He carried a
small object wrapped in cloth.

"Officer Gaffney died at two fourteen this morning," he said.

Peter's eyes filled.

"He had served with distinction for twenty-six years. Before losing
consciousness, he informed responding officers that you entered the house
unarmed, subdued two armed felons, and attempted to save his
life."

"I was wearin' a uniform I had no right to."

"You wore it honorably."

"I wasn't on the force."

Kondorman removed the cloth.

In his hand lay Gaffney's shield.

The room became very still.

"Regulations," the commissioner said, "exist to protect the public from
incompetence, carelessness and unfitness. They are necessary. Examinations are necessary.
Education is necessary."

Peter nodded faintly.

"But no regulation is served by excluding courage, loyalty, judgment
and devotion when they stand directly before us."

He leaned forward and pinned the shield to the hospital
gown.

The metal was heavy and cold. Peter looked at it,
and tears rolled silently into his ears.

"Peter Mullaney," Kondorman said, "you are appointed to the police
force, subject to recovery, probation and the completion of such
academic work as may be assigned."

Peter tried to speak. Nothing came out.

Nora put both hands over her mouth. Mrs. Mullaney sat
down abruptly upon Callahan's foot, but he did not complain.

"Commissioner," Peter managed at last, "what about geography?"

Kondorman straightened.

His eyebrows resumed their official positions.

"There was an error in the examination."

"Mine?"

"The department's."

Peter blinked.

Kondorman's voice became clipped, scientific and absolute.

"After a complete review of the evidence, I have determined
that the Tropic of Capricorn is in the Bronx."

For three seconds nobody moved.

Then Nora laughed. Callahan laughed. The nurse laughed. Mrs. Mullaney
cried harder because she had not heard the question. Even
the officers in the hallway grinned.

Peter looked down at Gaffney's shield upon his breast.

Somewhere beyond the windows, the city roared and rattled about
its endless business. There were doors to try, corners to
watch, children to carry, crowds to quiet, wounds to bind,
frightened voices to answer. There were laws to learn, reports
to write and ten thousand facts Peter did not yet
know.

He would learn them.

But he knew the most important one already.

A cop never runs away when he is needed.

### THE EAGLE'S NEST

In the autumn of 1878 I had charge of the
public school in a thinly inhabited district lying some five
miles north of Brownville, California. Brownville called itself a town
by virtue of possessing a post-office, two hotels, eleven saloons,
and a weekly newspaper whose editor had once seen San
Francisco. It was chiefly known, however, as a summer place
of last resort for persons whose purses or reputations forbade
them to visit more celebrated localities.

My school-house stood in a little valley among low, wooded
hills. There were twenty-three pupils upon the register, though the
simultaneous appearance of more than fifteen was regarded by the
trustees as an educational excess and a reflection upon the
usefulness of children in agriculture. The trustees themselves were three
in number. One could read, one could write, and the
third, being able to do neither, was naturally chairman of
the board.

I lodged at Brownville in the house of Mrs. Lucinda
Barlow, a widow of formidable virtue and limited cookery. Every
morning, after fortifying the alliance between soul and body with
fried pork, black coffee, and bread of a geological density,
I walked to the school by a path through the
woods. Returning in the afternoon, I commonly followed a longer
trail over the hills, for I was young, vigorous, and—as
I now perceive—rather vain of the melancholy reflections inspired by
sunset.

One evening in September I had taken this latter route
and was within a mile of the town when I
heard voices below me in a narrow ravine. The sun
had set, though the upper boughs still held a wan
illumination, and a young moon, as tender as it was
ghostly, had begun to disclose objects without explaining them.

The voices came from behind a screen of laurel and
wild grape. One was a woman's, low and agitated; the
other belonged to a man and was so composed that
its quietness seemed itself a menace.

"You cannot mean it," said the woman. "You cannot ask
me to do that."

"I do not ask," replied the man.

There followed a silence. I was standing upon the trail,
perhaps twenty paces above the speakers. Had I been a
person of proper delicacy I should have withdrawn, but curiosity—an
impulse which in others I have frequently condemned—held me where
I was.

"I have obeyed you," the woman resumed. "I have gone
wherever you bade me go. I have kept silence. I
have lied for you. Have you no pity?"

"None that would be useful to you."

At this extraordinary reply I stepped from the trail and
descended a little way among the trees. Through an opening
in the foliage I saw them.

The woman was dressed in gray and stood with her
back against the bole of an oak, her hands clasped
before her breast. Her face, pale even in that uncertain
light, was turned upward toward the man. The attitude had
something theatrical in it; I remember thinking that a provincial
actress in a scene of supplication might have been proud
of it. Yet her terror was not theatrical. That, at
least, I believed then, and subsequent events have not materially
altered my conviction.

The man stood facing her, tall and erect. His hat
was in his hand. I could not distinguish his features,
but his head was bent slightly forward, and I had
an indefinable impression that he regarded her with great fixity.

"You have promised," he said.

"I was mad."

"You are sane now. That is why you will fulfill
the promise."

"No!" she cried. "I would rather die."

"That alternative," said he, "is always open."

Whether my movement dislodged a stone, or whether some imperfect
instinct warned him of my presence, I cannot say. He
turned abruptly in my direction.

"Who is there?"

The question was spoken without alarm, but with an authority
which I resented. I pushed through the vines and answered:

"A traveler upon the public path. If this lady requires
assistance—"

The remainder of my civil offer was never delivered. A
branch, released from my hand, struck me smartly across the
eyes. For two or three seconds I could see nothing.
When I had freed myself and reached the bottom of
the ravine, the place was empty.

I listened. There was no sound of retreating footsteps. The
dry leaves lay thickly upon the ground, yet neither to
right nor left could I hear them stirred. Before me
rose a rocky bank, behind me the slope by which
I had descended; the ravine itself curved sharply and was
dark with undergrowth. That two persons acquainted with the place
might have escaped my observation was entirely possible. I mention
the silence merely because it affected me at the time
with an unreasonable apprehension.

On the ground near the oak lay a woman's glove.
It was of gray kid, small, and fastened by three
pearl buttons. I put it into my pocket—with no intention,
I trust, of founding a romance upon so meager a
relic—and resumed my walk.

The next evening, at Mrs. Barlow's table, I observed three
new guests: two young women and a man. The elder
of the women was perhaps twenty-eight years of age, dark-haired
and handsome, though her face had the fatigued delicacy of
one long subject to anxiety. Her sister, who appeared scarcely
twenty, was fairer, slighter, and of a beauty which I
then thought spiritual, though I have since suspected that the
term is commonly applied to a pretty woman who looks
ill.

Their companion was introduced to me as Mr. Richard Benning,
their guardian.

He was a man of thirty-five, or thereabout, tall, dark,
and singularly graceful. His features were regular without weakness; his
black beard was closely trimmed; his voice, when he acknowledged
the introduction, was low and agreeable. His manners possessed that
repose which is sometimes the flower of breeding and sometimes
the mask of insolence.

The elder sister was Miss Pauline Maynard. The younger was
Eva.

I recognized Pauline as the woman whom I had seen
in the ravine.

My recognition, if reciprocated, produced no sign. She bowed and
continued her meal. Benning looked at me with courteous indifference.
As nearly as I could make out, neither his voice
nor hers was certainly that which I had heard; voices
are altered by fear, distance, foliage, and the dramatic disposition
of the listener. Yet when Pauline lifted her hand to
take a glass, I observed that it was ungloved, and
that the hand was of a size corresponding to the
glove in my pocket. This evidence, though impressive to a
schoolmaster, would perhaps have failed to agitate a jury.

After dinner Mrs. Barlow informed me, in the privacy of
the passage and within easy hearing of all concerned, that
the Misses Maynard were orphans from Sacramento, that Mr. Benning
had managed their property since the death of their father,
and that Eva was suffering from a weakness of the
heart.

"Or the lungs," added Mrs. Barlow. "The doctors don't agree,
which is how you know she's really sick."

"And Miss Pauline?"

"Not sick. Worried. She watches the other one as if
expecting her to turn into smoke. Mr. Benning is devoted
to both."

This final statement was delivered in a tone implying that
devotion, like arsenic, is harmless only in moderate quantities.

During the succeeding weeks I saw much of the party.
At table Benning was attentive, amusing, and perfectly self-possessed. He
read aloud in the parlor; he accompanied Eva on walks;
he brought Pauline books and flowers. Nothing in his conduct
justified distrust. I distrusted him profoundly.

I should be glad to attribute this sentiment to a
superior perception of character, but candour compels me to mention
that Eva listened to him with an admiration which she
did not extend to my conversation. I was twenty-seven, not
ill-looking, tolerably educated, and accustomed to supremacy among persons under
fourteen years of age. Benning's effortless ascendancy therefore afflicted my
moral judgment with a certain warmth.

His most remarkable gift was music. Mrs. Barlow possessed a
square piano whose interior had long been a place of
honorable retirement for moths; yet beneath Benning's hands the instrument
recovered something resembling a soul. He sang in a rich
baritone, usually selections from Italian opera. None of us understood
the words. This circumstance increased their power.

One night, after the others had retired, he sang a
strange composition which he said he had learned in the
East. It was not properly a song, but a succession
of deep, rhythmical phrases, repeated upon a narrow interval and
broken at times by a high, sustained note. The effect
was barbaric and disagreeable. Eva, who had been sitting near
the fire, rose slowly and came to the piano. She
stood behind him, motionless, her face white, her eyes fixed
upon the reflection of his in the polished music rack.

When he stopped she drew a long breath and put
one hand to her throat.

"What is that music?" I asked.

"A remedy for disbelief," he replied.

"In what?"

He closed the piano.

"In remedies."

The answer was foolish enough, but his manner made further
inquiry seem impertinent.

Pauline had entered during the performance and stood in the
doorway. She looked from him to Eva with an expression
of such helpless hatred that I instantly remembered the woman
against the oak.

The following day I contrived to speak with her alone
upon the veranda.

"Miss Maynard," I said, producing the gray glove, "I found
this some weeks ago in the ravine beyond Miller's Hill.
Is it yours?"

She took it, and her face changed.

"Where did you say?"

I repeated the location.

"No," she said. "It is not mine."

Nevertheless she retained it.

"I was there that evening," I continued. "I heard a
conversation which caused me concern."

She turned upon me with sudden energy.

"You heard nothing. You saw no one."

"I saw a woman in distress."

"You were mistaken."

"Possibly. I am often mistaken; it is an occupational privilege.
But if you should at any time require assistance—"

She laughed. The sound was without mirth.

"Assistance! From whom?"

"From me, if I can give it."

Her expression softened for an instant.

"You are kind," she said. "But you cannot. No one
can."

Benning came through the doorway behind us.

"Pauline," he said.

He spoke her name quietly. She turned, and for perhaps
three seconds they looked at one another. I cannot accurately
describe the alteration in her face. The fear did not
disappear; it seemed rather to withdraw behind a smooth and
rigid composure.

"Mr. Benning," she said, "this gentleman has found a glove
which resembles one of mine, but is not mine."

She handed it to him.

Benning examined it and smiled.

"The world is full of resemblances," he observed. "They are
the foundation of most errors—and nearly all accusations."

He returned the glove to me and conducted Pauline into
the house.

A month passed.

This was the death of the elder girl, Pauline.

She was found in bed one morning, lying upon her
back, her hands folded upon her breast, her eyes open.
There was no mark of violence. The physician from Brownville,
a man whose practice consisted chiefly in recognizing maladies after
they had become indifferent to recognition, pronounced the cause to
be disease of the heart.

"She had no heart disease," Eva said.

We were in the room when she spoke—Benning, Mrs. Barlow,
the physician, and I. Eva stood beside the bed in
her night dress, her fair hair falling over her shoulders.

"She was not ill," she continued. "He killed her."

Her arm rose, and she pointed at Benning.

Mrs. Barlow uttered a cry. The physician dropped his spectacles.
I stepped between Benning and the door, moved, I suppose,
by some confused notion that he intended escape. He did
not move.

"Eva," he said.

She met his eyes.

The transformation was immediate, though I am now not altogether
sure that it was inexplicable. Her accusing hand fell. The
passion went out of her face. She looked down at
Pauline and then about the room with the bewilderment of
one awakened in a strange place.

"I was wrong," she said. "I do not know what
I meant."

"You are distressed," Benning replied.

"Yes. I am distressed."

"You should return to your room."

She obeyed.

The inquest was held that afternoon in Mrs. Barlow's dining
room, the coroner sitting at the head of the table
where, in happier circumstances, he had frequently encountered her boiled
mutton. Eva testified that her accusation had been made in
grief and delirium. Benning gave a lucid account of Pauline's
health and habits. The physician repeated his opinion with additional
words. The jury returned a verdict of death from natural
causes, thus preserving nature's ancient reputation for indiscriminate malignity.

The Brownville *Clarion* reported that "a beautiful and accomplished young
lady had been summoned to that bourne from which no
traveler returns." As Pauline had traveled from that bourne to
Brownville only six weeks earlier, the phrase seemed to me
deficient in geographical exactness.

She was buried in the little cemetery beyond the church.

After her sister's death, Eva's health appeared for a time
to improve. She laughed more readily, walked in the garden,
and sometimes teased Mrs. Barlow with a playful audacity which
seemed inconsistent with grief. At other moments I found her
sitting alone, her face contracted with terror.

One evening, passing along the upper hall, I heard voices
in the small parlor below. The door was ajar. I
recognized Eva's voice and stopped. This conduct was indefensible; I
defended it to myself by remembering that a life might
be endangered.

"You killed Pauline," she said.

There was no answer.

"I know how you did it—not the means, perhaps, but
the act. You made her die. And you will kill
me."

Still Benning said nothing.

"I have felt it from the beginning," she continued. "You
have only waited. Why?"

His reply was too low for me to hear.

"No," she said. "Do not look at me! I can
speak when you do not look at me."

A chair scraped upon the floor. I entered.

Eva was standing at the window. Benning sat in a
high-backed chair near the lamp, his face in shadow. He
regarded my intrusion without apparent resentment.

"I beg your pardon," I said; "I believed Miss Maynard
had called."

"She did not," he replied.

Eva came quickly toward me and laid her hand upon
my arm.

"Stay."

Benning rose.

"As Miss Maynard prefers your company, I shall withdraw."

At the door he paused and looked at her. She
shivered, but did not release my arm.

"You will remember," he said.

"Yes," she whispered.

When he had gone I closed the door.

"Miss Maynard, you believe yourself in danger."

She looked at me with a faint smile.

"I am in danger."

"Then leave this house. Leave Brownville. I will take you
to Sacramento tonight."

"That would not save me."

"Why not?"

"Because I should return."

"Against your will?"

She considered this, then shook her head.

"My will is the least trustworthy thing I possess."

"You accused him of killing your sister."

"He did kill her."

"How?"

"I do not know."

"Did he poison her?"

"No."

"Did he touch her?"

"Perhaps not."

"What, then, did he do?"

She turned toward the door through which he had gone.

"He looked at her."

The reply, spoken simply, angered me. It appeared to substitute
superstition for evidence and helplessness for effort.

"A look cannot kill."

"Cannot it?"

"No."

She smiled again, and there was pity in the smile.

"You have never loved him."

The words struck me with a more personal displeasure than
their logic warranted.

"Loved him! You have said that you fear him, that
he murdered Pauline, that he intends your death."

"All true."

"And you love him?"

"With all that is left of me."

I walked to the window and back, arranging, as I
supposed, my thoughts; in reality I was arranging my indignation.

"Then testify against him. Tell the authorities what you know."

"I know nothing that authorities can hear."

"Tell them what you believe."

"He would look at me."

"I will stand beside you."

"He would look at you, too," she said, with a
little laugh. "But it would do no good. You are
not the sort."

This reflection upon my susceptibility should have reassured me. It
did not.

She came nearer.

"You think me weak. Perhaps I am. When he is
absent, I hate him; when he enters, I wait for
him to speak. When he speaks, I believe him. When
he is silent, I imagine his thoughts and obey those.
If I escaped to the end of the earth, I
should hear his voice in the wind and turn back.
Pauline resisted longer. That was why—"

She stopped.

"Why he killed her?"

"Yes."

"And you will not resist?"

"I resist every hour."

There was something in her face then—some union of childish
appeal and mortal fatigue—which overthrew the careful defenses of my
reason. I took her hands.

"Eva, let me save you."

She did not withdraw them.

"You cannot."

"Let me try."

For a moment she looked into my eyes. I believed
that I saw gratitude there, and perhaps affection. Vanity, that
indefatigable translator, may have enlarged the message.

"You are very good," she said. "If goodness were strength—"

The sentence was left unfinished.

From the lower room came the first bars of a
melody. Benning was at the piano, singing "La donna è
mobile." Eva's hands tightened upon mine; then gently, almost apologetically,
she released them and went to the door.

"Do not go," I said.

She opened it.

"Good night."

For several days afterward Benning was absent from the house.
He had gone, Mrs. Barlow said, upon business connected with
the estate. During that interval Eva and I walked together
daily. She seemed to recover a natural gaiety, and I,
with the beneficent obtuseness of a man in love, interpreted
every smile as evidence of cure and every silence as
permission to hope.

On the fourth afternoon she proposed that we climb to
the Eagle's Nest, a rocky summit two miles west of
Brownville. The place was a favorite objective of visitors, though
Mrs. Barlow disapproved of it because two persons had fallen
from the cliff in ten years and neither had afterward
paid his account.

We set out at four o'clock. The day was warm,
but a cool wind moved among the pines upon the
heights. Eva wore a pale dress and carried a red
shawl over one arm. As we ascended she spoke little,
though once she stopped and looked back toward the town.

"He has returned," she said.

I saw no one upon the road below.

"How do you know?"

"I know."

"Then let us go back by another path."

"No. I want to see the sunset."

The Eagle's Nest was a projection of dark rock thrust
from the mountain, with a sheer fall of several hundred
feet on its western side. A few twisted pines clung
in fissures below the brink; farther down, the tops of
great trees appeared as a motionless green surface. Lizards, reptiles
of exemplary habits, occupied the warmer stones and withdrew at
our approach.

We reached the summit as the sun touched the distant
ridges. The valley beneath us was already filling with violet
shadow, while the higher hills burned with a melancholy gold.
Eva stood near the edge, the red shawl hanging from
her arm like a streak of flame.

I begged her to come farther back.

"Are you afraid for me?" she asked.

"Yes."

"You should be."

She said this pleasantly.

I took her hand and drew her toward a flat
stone beneath a pine. There, with a directness for which
I can now find no adequate excuse except youth, jealousy,
and terror, I told her that I loved her and
asked her to become my wife.

She listened with lowered eyes.

"You offer me a very peaceful life," she said when
I had finished.

"Not magnificent, perhaps. A schoolmaster's income—"

"I was not thinking of money."

"Then say you will accept it."

"I should destroy your peace."

"You cannot."

"You have a great faith in your possessions."

"Eva!"

She looked at me, and again there was that faint
expression of pity.

"If I could have loved you," she said, "I think
I should have been safe."

A twig snapped behind us.

Benning stood upon the trail.

He was dressed as I had first seen him, in
dark clothes, his hat in his hand. The exertion of
the ascent had neither quickened his breathing nor disturbed his
composure.

"You!" I cried.

"At your service," he said. "Mrs. Barlow told me where
I might find Miss Maynard."

Eva had risen. The red shawl slipped from her arm
and lay upon the rock.

"You shall not speak to her," I said.

"I have not spoken."

"You shall not approach her."

"I am standing still."

This was true, and the precision of his answers enraged
me.

"Eva," I said, "come with me."

She did not move. Her eyes were fixed upon Benning.

The sunlight had left the summit. Only the sky retained
its color, and in that clear afterglow his face was
perfectly visible. It wore no expression of anger, desire, or
triumph. His gaze alone was remarkable—steady, imperative, and profoundly attentive,
as if he watched not Eva's body but some imprisoned
thing behind her eyes.

She began to tremble.

"Turn away!" I shouted. "Do not look at him."

She turned her head toward me. Her face was white
with terror.

"Help me," she said.

I sprang forward and caught her wrist.

Benning did not stir.

Then Eva smiled.

I cannot describe that smile without seeming to contradict myself.
The lips parted in happiness; the eyes implored me in
unspeakable fear. It was as if two persons possessed her
face, one exulting, the other condemned.

She freed her wrist from my hand—not violently, but with
a quick, twisting motion for which I was unprepared.

"Eva!"

She stepped backward.

There was no room behind her but the sky.

For one instant she stood upon the brink, slight and
radiant against the western light. Then she leaped.

The red shawl, disturbed by the wind of her passage,
rose from the rock and followed more slowly. I saw
it open, fold, and vanish. Of Eva herself I remember
only a pale movement among the dark branches far below.

There was no cry.

I fell upon my knees at the edge and looked
down. Nothing moved among the trees. The depth confused the
sight; the encroaching shadow made every stone a body and
every branch an upraised arm.

When I rose, Benning was standing where he had stood
before.

"You murderer!" I said.

He inclined his head slightly.

"You saw what occurred."

"You compelled her."

"I did not touch her."

"You killed Pauline in the same way."

"Pauline died in bed of heart disease. A physician and
a coroner have certified the fact."

"You threatened them both."

"Did I?"

"I heard you in the ravine."

"You heard a man and a woman whose faces you
could not clearly see."

"I heard Eva accuse you."

"And heard her withdraw the accusation."

"You had power over her."

"Most men," he said, "are willing to call influence a
power when it is exercised by another."

I advanced upon him. I believe I intended to throw
him from the cliff. Whether he read the purpose in
my face or merely disapproved of my proximity, he raised
his eyes and looked directly into mine.

I stopped.

No mysterious paralysis restrained me. Of that I am certain—or
nearly certain. I could move; I could speak; I could
have struck him. Yet between intention and act there opened
a cold interval in which every possible consequence presented itself
with unnatural distinctness: his stronger body, the dangerous footing, the
absence of witnesses, the legal interpretation of a struggle. Prudence,
cowardice, or something imposed from without held me motionless.

He smiled.

"As you are no doubt aware," he said, "help must
be summoned. I shall go to Brownville. You may remain
here and guard the place."

"You shall not escape."

"I have no thought of escape."

He put on his hat and turned toward the trail.

At the first bend he began to sing.

The voice came back through the pines, rich, easy, and
perfectly controlled. The air was "La donna è mobile," the
jaunty song he had so often sung in Mrs. Barlow's
parlor. It descended with him toward the darkening valley, now
clear, now faint, interrupted by trees and restored by the
wind.

I remained at the Eagle's Nest until the light had
wholly failed. Below me, unseen in the abyss, lay Eva
Maynard; beyond the hills the lamps of Brownville appeared one
by one, respectable, domestic, and remote. Presently men came with
ropes and lanterns. Benning came with them.

The official verdict was suicide while temporarily insane.

I testified to what I had seen. My account was
heard with courtesy and rejected with unanimity. Benning had not
touched her; he had uttered no command; he had, indeed,
spoken no word to her. The law, which is an
admirable machine for weighing material objects, found nothing to place
in either scale.

Richard Benning left Brownville the following week. I never saw
him again.

I resigned my school at the end of the term
and have not since revisited the place. In calmer years
I have subjected the events here related to that subsequent
analysis by which we commonly hope to repair the incompetence
of immediate perception. Pauline may have died naturally. Eva may
have inherited an unsound heart and an unsound mind. Benning's
influence may have been no more than the ascendancy of
a strong nature over weak ones, assisted by fear, fascination,
and my own jealousy. His gaze may have contained nothing
that I did not put there.

I cannot say.

But sometimes, when twilight is deepening among trees and a
distant voice is heard where no singer can be seen,
I remember Eva's smiling mouth and terrified eyes. Then all
the reasonable explanations with which I have fortified the years
fall silently away, and I know—as certainly as a man
may know what he cannot prove—that upon the Eagle's Nest
I stood in the presence of a murderer whose hands
were clean.

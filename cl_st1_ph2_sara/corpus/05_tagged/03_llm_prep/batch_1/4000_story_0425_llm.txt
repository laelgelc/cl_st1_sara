### THE HISTORY OF VIEUX PAR-CHEMINS, WHO WAS HANGED WITHOUT
DYING

An old chronicler of Rouen, whose parchment was discovered beneath
three sacks of ecclesiastical accounts and a cheese hardened to
the consistency of Roman law, relates the following history; and
although certain grave persons have doubted it, because grave persons
doubt everything except what profits them, I shall set it
down faithfully, adding nothing save those particulars without which truth
commonly goes naked and catches cold.

In the reign of Duke Richard—whether the First, Second, or
that other Richard whom careless historians have misplaced between them—there
lived near Rouen a certain Tryballot, son of Anseau Tryballot,
a merchant of hemp, salt fish, boot-leather, old iron, and
whatever else could be bought for two deniers and sold
for four. Anseau was so thrifty that he buttoned his
purse before sneezing, lest a coin should leap out; and
he had amassed, by the time death loosened his fingers,
two houses, seven fields, a vineyard of sour but saleable
grapes, twelve silver cups, and a chest containing more crowns
than the parish priest had hairs upon his tonsure.

His son Tryballot, however, held another philosophy.

From infancy he had loved blackbirds, jays, rooks, sparrows, and
all winged professors of appropriation. He observed that they neither
sowed barley, ploughed furrows, paid toll, nor saluted provosts; yet
they ate the grain of the industrious, nested in the
trees of the wealthy, and sang before breakfast. From this
he concluded—being already a subtle theologian—that Heaven favoured those who
took their nourishment where they found it.

When Anseau caught him stealing plums, the boy pointed to
a jay in the orchard and said:

"Father, that bird does likewise."

"The bird has no immortal soul," answered Anseau.

"Then he is the safer of us two," replied Tryballot.

Such sayings caused the neighbours to predict that the child
would become either a bishop or a vagabond.

On receiving his inheritance, Tryballot began by ordering a funeral
of such splendour that his father, had he been able
to rise from the coffin, would have died a second
time from the expense. Forty priests sang, though only twelve
were paid; six beggars bore wax tapers; two barrels of
wine were emptied in memory of a man who had
drunk water all his life; and the mourners ate three
oxen, eleven sheep, twenty-four geese, innumerable sausages, and a custard
so broad that a child might have sailed across it.

After the burial Tryballot sold the hemp, the salt fish,
the leather, the iron, the vineyard, the fields, and finally
the houses. What he could not sell he gambled; what
he could not gamble he gave away; and what no
one would accept he broke, for he disliked leaving business
unfinished.

He kept companions by the dozen. One played the lute,
another the dice, a third the flatterer; and of the
three professions the last consumed the most wine. He bought
scarlet cloaks for men who had never owned shirts, pearls
for women who had never possessed honesty, and a white
horse for himself, though he could not ride. The horse
threw him into a puddle on the first day and
was sold on the second to provide a supper in
honour of the accident.

Thus did he play ducks and drakes with his inheritance,
and in less than three years he had nothing left
but a patched doublet, a pewter spoon, and the pewter
spoon's creditor.

"Now," said he, "I am delivered from the burden of
property."

This maxim he repeated afterward whenever he found himself without
dinner.

Having become poor, Tryballot entered the profession of mendicancy, in
which, as he observed, the hours are short, the road
is open, and a man may change masters by turning
a corner. He wandered up hill and down dale, along
the Seine, through Rouen, Petit-Quevilly, Darnétal, and villages too small
to be named except when collecting taxes. In time he
became known as Vieux par-Chemins, Old-Man-of-the-Roads, although at first he
was neither particularly old nor exclusively attached to roads, since
he slept equally well in barns, ditches, porches, haylofts, bakehouses,
and, when fortune smiled, beneath widows' windows.

He begged according to seasons and audiences. To priests he
said that hunger distracted him from prayer; to soldiers, that
he had been wounded in wars which had occurred before
his birth; to women, that his mother had resembled each
of them; and to innkeepers, that Saint Julian himself had
commanded hospitality. If given bread he asked for cheese, because
bread alone produced melancholy. If given cheese he asked for
wine, because cheese without wine caused obstruction. If given all
three he requested a coin, lest eating without charity make
him ungrateful.

It is unjust, however, to say that he hoarded. Money
remained with him no longer than snow upon a griddle.
He played at knucklebones with carters, at dice with varlets,
and at cards with pilgrims returning from shrines, who had
collected indulgences but not wisdom. Often he won; always he
continued; finally he lost. Then, possessing nothing, he slept soundly,
whereas the winner lay awake for fear of thieves.

"Observe," Tryballot would say next morning, "how gaming punishes avarice
and rewards detachment."

His fame spread through the province. Bakers saved burnt loaves
for him; taverners kept the heel of the ham; kitchen-maids
opened doors after sunset; boys followed him to hear his
stories; dogs, recognizing a competitor, barked respectfully. He knew every
orchard wall that had a loose stone, every monastery whose
porter was soft-hearted, every fair at which a stranger might
be persuaded to finance a game, and every husband whose
return could be heard from the lane.

Work he avoided as a physician avoids plague.

Once a farmer offered him six deniers to carry a
sack of oats from one barn to another.

"Friend," said Tryballot, "if Heaven had wished oats to inhabit
the second barn, why did Providence place them in the
first?"

Another man proposed that he dig a ditch.

"I should gladly do so," he answered, "but the earth
has lain quietly since Creation, and I will not disturb
an ancient possession."

A canon, annoyed by these subtleties, commanded him to labour
for his bread.

"My father laboured all his life," said Tryballot, "and died.
I have carefully avoided his error."

This answer was repeated at taverns, markets, washhouses, and even
in the chapter room, though there it was attributed to
Plato, because canons receive nonsense more respectfully when it comes
from Greece.

Years passed, then decades, carrying away kings, wars, taxes, harvests,
fashions in sleeves, and those young women who had once
thrown apple peel at Vieux par-Chemins. He grew grey, then
white, then yellowish, as old parchment does; but remained wiry,
quick-eyed, and difficult to catch. He declared that poverty had
preserved him.

"The rich," he said, "die of sauces, mattresses, physicians, and
worry. They eat when they are not hungry, drink when
they are not thirsty, sleep when they are not weary,
and consult doctors when they are not ill. I eat
when someone feeds me, drink when someone pays, sleep when
I fall down, and rise when the rain enters my
ear. Judge which of us follows nature."

There was reason in this, though reason, like a coin
in a dungheap, may be discovered in strange places.

At eighty he could still walk twelve miles for a
wedding feast, climb an orchard wall for two pears, and
escape through a hedge when the owner appeared. At eighty-one
he danced at the Paschal fair until three fiddlers were
exhausted. At eighty-two he retained, besides six teeth, an appetite
which the writers of the period describe with admiration and
which modesty commands me to call the last candle in
an old chapel.

Now it happened that for several months this candle had
illuminated nobody. The widows had grown prudent, the maids had
grown fastidious, and even a blind washerwoman, upon learning his
age, advised him to think of Abraham. Such abstinence soured
the good man's temper and persuaded him that the world
had abandoned courtesy.

One warm afternoon, wandering beside a meadow beyond the Porte
Beauvoisine, he saw a young cowherd asleep under a willow.
Her cattle grazed nearby; her crook lay in the grass;
and owing to the heat, her posture, and the treachery
of her garments, a part of her person had escaped
its proper fortifications.

Tryballot stopped.

Here the old chronicler grows elaborate, invoking Paris and Helen,
David and Bathsheba, Jupiter under various disguises, and even Plato,
who had nothing to do with the matter. I shall
say only that the sight awakened in Vieux par-Chemins a
desire stronger than judgment and much less honourable. Instead of
turning away, as age, law, and decency required, he approached
the sleeping girl and forced upon her an outrage for
which neither hunger nor longevity can excuse a man.

She woke, cried out, struck him with her crook, and
called to two labourers in a neighbouring field. Tryballot attempted
flight, but a fellow of eighty-two, however instructed by sparrows,
cannot outrun men of twenty-five while fastening his breeches. He
was seized, beaten, bound with a halter, and dragged before
the provost.

The cowherd gave her complaint plainly, though the clerk improved
its grammar. She said she had been asleep, had consented
to nothing, and had awakened to find the ancient villain
where no invitation had placed him. The labourers testified to
her cries, her torn clothing, her bleeding lip, and Tryballot's
hurried retreat. The prisoner did not deny the deed; he
denied only that a man of his age could reasonably
be expected to resist such a trial.

The provost, who disliked beggars, paradoxes, and cases that interrupted
supper, sentenced him to hang.

News flew through Rouen. People who had refused Tryballot a
crust now hurried to see him die; for mankind is
often more generous with attention than with bread. Bakers left
their ovens, scholars their books, lawyers their clients, and clients
their lawyers, which last desertion harmed nobody. Some lamented the
old rogue; others praised justice; many merely hoped the hanging
would occur before rain.

Tryballot, informed of the sentence, demanded appeal to Duke Richard,
declaring that so remarkable a case belonged not to an
ordinary provost but to sovereign jurisprudence.

The duke, who enjoyed curious lawsuits as other men enjoy
falconry, ordered the prisoner brought before him. The court assembled:
barons in fur, clerks in black, guards with halberds, ladies
with veils, chaplains with grave mouths, and advocates whose mouths
were never grave so long as a fee remained possible.

Tryballot entered in chains, bowed, and said:

"My lord, I ask justice tempered by arithmetic."

"Arithmetic?" said the duke.

"Yes, Monseigneur. The provost has counted my offence but not
my years. I am eighty-two."

"That should have given you eighty-two reasons for restraint."

"On the contrary, it gave me cause for haste. A
young man may postpone pleasure until Tuesday; an old man
cannot be certain of Monday evening."

A murmur ran through the court. The duke concealed a
smile.

"Did the girl consent?"

"No, my lord."

"Did you know she did not consent?"

"She was asleep, which is a form of silence."

"It is not a form of permission," said the duke,
more sternly.

Tryballot bowed again.

"That is true according to law. Yet consider nature. For
months I had lived in a desert more cruel than
that of Moses, who at least possessed a rod with
which to strike rocks. Then suddenly, beneath a willow, there
appeared before me two hemispheres worthy of dividing the globe
between them. My reason, being old, arrived too late; my
desire, being miraculously young, had already taken the field."

"You call violence a miracle?"

"I call my vigour one, Monseigneur. The violence I leave
to the clerk, who knows its proper article."

At this several ladies lowered their eyes; several gentlemen raised
their brows; and one aged bishop coughed so violently that
charity forbids us to interpret him.

The duke asked, "Would the gallows cool this miraculous ardour?"

"Sire, the gallows cools everything."

"Then your defence is merely fear."

"My defence is that a man cannot be blamed for
lacking a faculty and hanged for possessing it. If I
were impotent, the accusation would fail; being potent, I am
condemned. Thus justice punishes the proof by which she proves."

The lawyers applauded inwardly, for they recognized the architecture of
their profession: a staircase leading upward and downward at once.

Duke Richard considered. He knew the cowherd had suffered a
grave wrong; he knew also that Rouen expected entertainment; and
princes, being required to administer both justice and spectacle, sometimes
confuse their accounts. At last he pronounced this decree:

"Tryballot, called Vieux par-Chemins, you claim that age has not
diminished your desires and that a sudden sight overcame reason.
Tomorrow you shall stand beneath the gallows, the rope around
your neck. If, in the presence of death, a suitable
object can still awaken the vigour of which you boast,
your sentence shall be commuted. If fear proves stronger, the
hangman shall proceed. Meanwhile the injured woman shall be compensated
from our treasury, since your purse contains more holes than
coins."

Letters patent were drawn up, sealed, copied, recopied, and misunderstood
by nearly everyone. The clergy objected that the test was
indecorous; the lawyers answered that execution was also indecorous, though
customary; the physicians wished to observe; and the people declared
Duke Richard the wisest ruler since Solomon, whose own methods,
if closely examined, would not always satisfy a modern notary.

At dawn the field of execution resembled a fair. Pie-sellers,
wine-carriers, cutpurses, jugglers, relic-vendors, friars, and respectable families occupied every
patch of ground. Children climbed trees; apprentices sat on roofs;
noblemen arrived on horses; peasants came in carts. A merchant
sold little wooden images of Tryballot with movable legs, and
had disposed of two hundred before terce.

The gallows stood black against the morning. Beneath it waited
the hangman, dressed in red, who complained that experiments were
ruining his trade.

Tryballot was brought out wearing a clean shirt provided by
the duke, not from mercy but because public ceremonies require
linen. The rope was placed around his neck. For the
first time in many years the good man appeared thoughtful.

"Are you afraid?" asked the confessor.

"Father, I have slept under roofs less trustworthy than this
gallows, but never with a cord so affectionate."

"Repent."

"I repent having been caught."

"That is imperfect contrition."

"It is the only kind for which I have evidence."

Trumpets sounded. The duke took his seat upon a raised
platform. Beside him sat judges, ladies, churchmen, and the cowherd,
pale and unwilling, with her family. A purse of gold
had been placed in her father's hands; yet gold, although
useful for bread, does not purchase the past, and she
looked upon the spectacle with no amusement.

Then arose a difficulty which the duke's decree had not
solved. Who would present to the condemned man an object
capable of awakening desire?

The town prostitutes had offered their services, but the physicians
rejected them as too familiar. Two tavern maids volunteered, then
quarrelled over precedence. A sculptor proposed a painted Venus; the
bishop protested that idolatry must not be added to obscenity.
Matters might have ended in hanging by procedural delay had
not a noblewoman of the court, renowned for beauty, courage,
and a taste for singular adventures, stepped forward.

She wore cloth of silver, a mantle trimmed with ermine,
and jewels sufficient to ransom a baron. Her name the
chronicler prudently conceals, remarking only that her descendants later produced
three abbots and a treasurer, all of whom paid to
keep the page illegible.

"My lord," she said to the duke, "if the trial
is to be fair, let fear and beauty contend with
equal arms."

She descended from the platform and approached the gallows. The
multitude became so quiet that one heard a pie crust
break fifty paces away.

Standing before Tryballot, she opened her mantle. With the composure
of a queen displaying relics, she loosened certain laces and
revealed those twin charms which poets compare to ivory apples,
theologians avoid naming, and infants understand without instruction. She then
raised her skirts high enough to offer a glimpse of
the pretty delta where mankind begins its lawsuits.

The hangman watched Tryballot.

The physicians watched Tryballot.

The judges watched the physicians watching Tryballot.

The clergy watched nobody, but afterward supplied the most exact
descriptions.

For a moment nothing happened. The old man stared at
the lady; then at the rope; then again at the
lady, as though weighing two roads, one leading to Paradise
and the other postponing it. The crowd held its breath.
The hangman adjusted his knot hopefully.

Suddenly a movement beneath Tryballot's shirt announced that life had
lodged its appeal.

The physicians rushed forward. One felt the prisoner's pulse; another
inspected the evidence; a third called for ink, having resolved
to write a treatise. There could be no dispute. Under
the shadow of death, with the noose at his throat
and eternity opening beneath his feet, Vieux par-Chemins had raised
the banner required by ducal ordinance.

A roar shook the field.

"Pardon!" cried the crowd.

"Miracle!" cried the women.

"Natural heat!" cried the physicians.

"Scandal!" cried the clergy, pressing nearer.

The duke laughed until his fur collar slipped sideways. He
ordered the rope removed and proclaimed Tryballot pardoned according to
the letter of the decree. The hangman, disgusted, presented a
bill for wasted time. The noblewoman refastened her garments and
returned to her seat amid such applause as had never
greeted a sermon in Rouen.

Tryballot bowed to her.

"Madame," said he, "you have given me my life."

"No," she replied. "I merely proved that you continue to
misuse it."

This sentence, being both short and sensible, is omitted by
several later chroniclers.

The town celebrated for three days. Bells rang; bonfires burned;
taverns emptied their cellars. Songs were composed before sunset and
became indecent before supper. Bakers made loaves shaped like gallows,
from which hung little sausages. The wooden images doubled in
price. A monument was proposed, designed, taxed, begun, abandoned, and
finally converted into a horse trough, as happens to many
civic honours.

Duke Richard, having saved Tryballot from justice, now determined to
make him respectable, which was a harsher punishment. He granted
him a small estate, twenty livres a year, two suits
of clothing, and a coat of arms showing a blackbird
above a broken noose. Then, by that sovereign wisdom through
which princes repair one injury with another, he commanded the
cowherd to marry him.

Her objections were numerous and just. Her father, dazzled by
the purse, called the match advantageous. The duke called it
reparation. The lawyers called it settlement. Tryballot called it Providence.
The young woman called it what it was, but the
clerk did not record the word.

Thus they were wed before the cathedral doors. Tryballot wore
blue velvet and looked older than Methuselah's uncle. The bride
wore white, though no one had the right to demand
the colour. The duke supplied the feast, the bishop the
blessing, the town the jokes, and the bride the silence.

Marriage, which reforms some men and merely relocates others, made
Tryballot a landowner, husband, father, and unwilling example. Against expectation
he begot several children, thereby confirming the evidence offered beneath
the gallows. They grew sturdy, noisy, and acquisitive; one entered
the army, one the Church, one the law, and the
cleverest entered all three successively.

From these children descended a family of some consequence. Their
first surname, derived from the celebrated proof by which their
ancestor escaped hanging, was so frank that modest copyists rendered
it Bonne-C------. In the reign of King Louis XI, when
the family obtained offices requiring cleaner parchment, they changed it
by royal permission to Bonne-Chose, or Good-Thing, which conveyed the
idea while clothing it in a bonnet. Their arms retained
the blackbird, though the broken noose was replaced by two
silver globes upon a field of flesh-colour, an heraldic device
which caused much innocent discussion among antiquaries.

Tryballot lived twenty-seven years after his pardon and died at
one hundred and nine, not from wine, women, dice, hunger,
rain, or the gallows, but from the burdens of prosperity.
He had servants to wake him, stewards to question him,
tenants to deceive him, physicians to bleed him, priests to
admonish him, children to inherit from him, and a wife
who never permitted him to forget by what road he
had entered the household.

Often he sat at the gate of his estate watching
beggars pass.

"Happy rascals," he would sigh. "They own the whole road
and pay rent nowhere."

Once he escaped with a band of pilgrims, carrying only
a stick and a crust; but his servants overtook him
before noon and brought him home upon a mule. He
wept when they placed him in a feather bed.

"Formerly," he said, "I possessed nothing and feared no thief.
Now I possess everything and am guarded like a prisoner."

At his death they buried him beneath a carved stone
representing a respectable gentleman with folded hands. This likeness deceived
posterity more completely than all the rest of the chronicle.

From his history you may obtain several morals, though it
is prudent not to use them. You may learn that
wealth shortens appetite while poverty sharpens it; that blackbirds are
dangerous schoolmasters; that a rope around the neck does not
always cool the blood; that princes make sport where they
ought to make justice; and that a man may escape
the hangman only to be caught by property, genealogy, and
marriage.

But the old chronicler, who understood mankind better than morality,
writes only this: he who would preserve his health should
eat little, sleep where he can, avoid labour, possess nothing,
and never pass beneath a willow without first examining who
lies there.

To which I add: examine also whether the provost is
nearby.

# The Third Night

There are moments when the human heart appears to possess
senses unknown to the body, when it hears across provinces,
sees through walls, and suffers beneath a blow not yet
reported by any messenger. Such facts, rejected by reason because
reason cannot measure them, have nevertheless been preserved by families,
magistrates, priests, and soldiers; and the Revolution, which destroyed so
many witnesses, furnished more than one terrible example of those
invisible correspondences by which two beings, separated by distance, may
remain united in anguish and death.

In the closing months of 1793, when every town in
France had its suspicions, its denunciations, its patriotic society, and
its little imitation of the revolutionary tribunal, there lived at
Carentan a widow named Madame de Dey.

She belonged to one of those ancient Norman families whose
estates, titles, lawsuits, and alliances had long formed a second
geography of the province. Her husband, the Comte de Dey,
had died some years before the fall of the monarchy,
leaving her a considerable fortune, a respected name, and one
son. The countess—though that title had become dangerous, and no
prudent friend now used it except in a whisper—was then
about thirty-eight years of age. Her beauty, more delicate than
brilliant, had survived both marriage and grief. She was small,
fair, and distinguished by that grace of movement which is
rarely acquired and never successfully imitated. Her blue eyes, habitually
mild, could become commanding without harshness; her forehead was calm;
her voice had the restrained sweetness of women educated from
childhood to hide strong feeling under perfect manners.

Her marriage had not been happy. The late count, a
severe man, proud of his ancestry and jealous of his
authority, had regarded tenderness as a weakness unsuited to his
rank. Consequently, all the affection denied to the wife had
been poured into the mother. Auguste, her only child, had
become at once her consolation, her occupation, and her secret
religion.

Mothers who have several children distribute their fears, their hopes,
and even their pride; each child relieves the heart of
some portion of the burden imposed by another. But the
mother of an only son loves with the concentrated force
of a passion that has neither diversion nor remedy. The
future is enclosed in one life. One illness threatens the
whole world; one absence empties the house; one danger makes
Providence itself seem cruel. Madame de Dey had watched Auguste
grow with that anxious delight which counts every breath and
finds an omen in every smile. When he entered the
army, she had suffered; when he emigrated with other young
noblemen, she had suffered still more; and when the Revolution
converted exile into treason, her maternal fears became daily companions,
seated beside her at table and kneeling with her at
prayer.

Auguste was twenty years old. He had his mother's eyes
and the proud carriage of his father, softened by a
gaiety natural to youth. He had joined an émigré corps
beyond the frontier, not from political conviction—for at twenty conviction
often bears the uniform selected by family—but from honor, companionship,
and the shame he imagined he would feel if he
remained behind while men of his name were fighting. Madame
de Dey, who understood the fatal generosity of that decision,
had not opposed him. Certain mothers resist their sons only
until they perceive that resistance would diminish them in their
own esteem; then they assist the sacrifice and die from
it in silence.

After Auguste's departure, the countess did not emigrate. Her property,
her sex, the good opinion of the town, and perhaps
the protection of several influential citizens permitted her to remain
in Carentan. She dismissed unnecessary servants, abandoned her arms and
liveries, wore plain dresses, accepted the name of citoyenne without
visible resentment, and subscribed generously whenever the municipality demanded contributions
for volunteers, wounded patriots, widows, salt, grain, or the equipment
of the National Guard. Yet she never practiced that noisy
republicanism by which many former nobles hoped to disguise their
birth. Her prudence was dignified. She bent before the storm
but did not salute it.

Her house stood in a quiet street not far from
the church. Built in the preceding century, it had a
gray façade, a broad staircase, a small courtyard, and behind
it a garden enclosed by walls. A greenhouse prolonged one
wing of the building, and from its glass door a
path led between clipped yews to a narrow gate used
formerly by gardeners and tradesmen. Since the Revolution, that gate
had acquired a value for which the architect had not
designed it.

Twice each week Madame de Dey received the principal inhabitants
of Carentan. The mayor came, and the public prosecutor, and
two merchants who had purchased national property; there were also
the notary, a physician, an elderly judge, the wives of
several officials, and occasionally officers passing through the town. Cards
were played. Political matters were discussed with the cautious ardor
of people who wished to sound one another without being
sounded themselves. The countess served tea when tea could be
obtained, and on poorer evenings offered cider, preserves, or cakes
made according to an old family recipe which even the
Republic had not abolished.

Her salon was useful to everybody. The former nobles found
there a remnant of the society they had lost; the
bourgeois enjoyed the triumph of entering a room formerly closed
to them; officials could display their authority without descending to
a tavern; and Madame de Dey obtained, in exchange for
wax candles and civility, a certain public security. A woman
who receives the whole town is less easily accused of
hiding from it. Moreover, all Carentan praised her goodness, her
moderation, and the absence of pride in her conduct—praises in
which compassion was mingled with the satisfaction of seeing a
great lady dependent on the favorable judgment of grocers and
attorneys.

In small towns curiosity is not a vice but an
occupation. Every inhabitant, having exhausted the limited variety of his
own life, employs his leisure in interpreting the lives of
others. A shutter closed at an unusual hour, a servant
crossing the market twice in one morning, a lamp burning
after midnight—such things become public documents. Facts are first observed,
then compared, then explained; by evening the explanation is certainty,
and on the next day certainty has acquired witnesses.

Thus, when Madame de Dey failed to receive on a
Tuesday evening, the circumstance was noticed. Her porter said she
was ill. The physician had not been called. Brigitte, the
countess's old confidential servant, bought meat at an hour when
she usually bought vegetables. A boy from the baker's carried
two extra loaves to the house. On Wednesday morning a
carpet was beaten in the courtyard; on Wednesday afternoon the
poulterer sent a fine hare, though Madame de Dey was
known to eat little and to keep a modest table.
The upper windows of the wing overlooking the garden were
opened. Brigitte purchased wax candles and asked whether fresh cream
could be delivered late the next day.

Each fact was innocent; together they became alarming.

"Someone is expected," said the wife of the notary.

"A relative, perhaps," replied the mayor, who was a shrewd
Norman and preferred that dangerous conclusions should first be uttered
by other mouths.

"What relatives has she left in France?"

"None who would visit openly."

The last phrase, delivered by the public prosecutor, produced a
silence.

He was a man of thirty-five, formerly an obscure lawyer,
thin, carefully dressed, and possessed of one of those pale
faces upon which ambition appears as an illness. Before the
Revolution he had been received by the countess only in
matters of business. Since becoming public prosecutor, he entered her
drawing room familiarly and sometimes seated himself in the chair
once reserved for the bishop. He admired Madame de Dey;
but in men of his kind admiration is rarely separated
from the wish to subdue. The social distance which had
formerly placed her beyond his reach continued to attract him
even after law had declared all distances abolished. Equality had
given him access, not assurance. He would willingly have protected
her, provided she knew that she owed her safety to
him.

The rumor crossed Carentan before sunset. An émigré had landed.
A priest was hidden in the countess's house. English gold
had arrived. The young Dey himself had returned. There were
even persons who remembered having seen, three nights earlier, a
man on horseback near the road from Saint-Lô, though none
could describe either the horse or the rider.

What the town merely guessed was more terrible than it
imagined.

Eight days earlier Madame de Dey had received a letter.
It had been brought by a peasant woman who sold
butter in the market and who, after asking for Brigitte,
disappeared without awaiting an answer. The paper was small, stained,
and folded into the lining of a coarse cap. The
handwriting trembled but was unmistakable.

Auguste had returned secretly to France with several companions. Betrayed
near the coast, they had been attacked by a republican
detachment. Some were killed; others captured. He was being conducted
under guard toward the interior, where a military commission would
almost certainly condemn him. Yet a soldier charged with watching
the prisoners had been bribed. During a halt in Morbihan,
Auguste hoped to escape. If he succeeded, he would travel
by night, avoid the roads, and reach Carentan within three
days of the date written at the head of the
letter.

"You will see me, my dear mother," he had written,
"before midnight on Friday. A friend has arranged everything. Keep
the garden gate unfastened. If I do not come by
then, believe that God has disposed of me. Do not
wait beyond the third night."

That last sentence had entered Madame de Dey's heart like
cold steel.

The first day she lived entirely in hope. She gave
Brigitte careful instructions, prepared money, plain clothing, food, and the
papers of a dead gardener's nephew, which might allow Auguste
to pass as a conscript traveling toward Cherbourg. In the
greenhouse she concealed a ladder by which he could reach
the neighboring wall if the house were searched. The bedroom
at the end of the upper corridor, formerly his room,
was aired and warmed. Clean linen was placed on the
bed; on a chair lay a brown coat and thick
shoes; beside a basin stood the razors he had used
before leaving France.

These humble objects overwhelmed her. She touched the coat as
though it already contained the beloved body. She arranged and
rearranged the pillow. She ordered Brigitte to bring a bottle
of the old Burgundy Auguste had praised at his last
dinner at home, then feared the bottle would attract notice,
then ordered it again. Mothers awaiting a son in danger
are not guided by reason; their care seeks refuge in
details, because details can be commanded while fate cannot.

On the second day hope became restless. Every sound in
the street compelled Madame de Dey to rise. She watched
from behind the curtains, though she knew observation might expose
her. A cart stopped two houses away; she turned white.
A horse galloped over the stones; she gripped the window
frame. At dusk she went into the garden and stood
by the little gate, listening to the countryside beyond the
wall. The earth smelled of rain. Bare branches moved against
the gray sky. Somewhere a dog barked, was answered, and
fell silent.

"He will come tonight," she told Brigitte.

The old servant crossed herself.

"Yes, madame. Monsieur Auguste was always fortunate."

But servants who love us acquire a cruel penetration into
our misfortunes. Brigitte's hopeful tone was too emphatic; Madame de
Dey understood that the woman was afraid.

Nothing happened.

Toward dawn the countess slept for an hour in a
chair beside the prepared bed. On waking, she was shocked
by the daylight, which seemed an accusation. The second night
had passed. Only Friday remained.

It was on Friday morning that the mayor, acting from
friendship as much as policy, called at the house. Brigitte
attempted to refuse him, but he insisted with the quiet
obstinacy peculiar to Normans, who often conceal benevolence beneath the
forms of self-interest.

Madame de Dey received him in a small ground-floor parlor.
She wore a gray dress; her hair was arranged; her
manner at first appeared calm. But the mayor, accustomed to
judging the quality of cattle, contracts, cider, and lies, saw
at once the bluish shadows beneath her eyes and the
slight trembling of her right hand.

"Citoyenne," he said, "Carentan is concerned for your health."

"You are very good. I was tired, nothing more."

"You have closed your doors for two evenings."

"I did not feel able to receive."

"A reasonable decision. Unfortunately, reason has little authority over gossip."

She looked at him.

"What is being said?"

"Everything. Therefore, nothing—so far."

The last two words told her the extent of the
danger.

The mayor walked toward the window and examined the wet
garden.

"In these times," he resumed, "a person may become suspicious
merely by changing a habit. Regularity is patriotism. A woman
who serves tea on Tuesdays and Fridays must continue to
serve it while the Republic exists."

"Do you advise me to receive this evening?"

"I advise nothing. Advice establishes responsibility. But if your lamps
were lighted, if the cards were laid out, if half
the municipality found you here without fever or conspiracy, there
might be less talk tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?"

He turned from the window.

"A detachment is expected tonight. The authorities have received information
concerning fugitives from a band of émigrés captured in Morbihan.
Searches may be ordered."

Despite all her courage, Madame de Dey leaned against the
table.

The mayor pretended not to notice.

"Madame," he said, abandoning the republican form of address, "if
a person whom you loved—some imaginary person, naturally—were to pass
through Carentan, that person would do well not to remain
after daybreak."

"He will not remain," she murmured.

The mayor lowered his eyes. The confession had escaped her
so softly that he might have denied hearing it.

"Then receive tonight," he said. "Be seen. Above all, do
not be alone. Solitude is a witness whom every prosecutor
can force to speak."

After he had gone, Madame de Dey remained motionless. The
necessity was monstrous, yet undeniable. During the very hours in
which she expected her son, she must open her house
to spies; she must smile while listening for his signal;
she must play cards with those who could send him
to death.

At four o'clock she ordered the shutters opened.

Carentan immediately perceived this surrender to public opinion. Invitations were
not needed. By seven the first guests arrived, animated by
the double pleasure of satisfying curiosity and offering sympathy. The
drawing room, cold and deserted for two nights, recovered its
ordinary appearance. Candles shone in the mirrors; a fire burned
beneath the marble mantel; card tables stood ready; porcelain cups
and silver spoons, survivors of confiscations and patriotic levies, displayed
the discreet elegance of the old household.

Madame de Dey had dressed with extraordinary care. She wore
a gown of dark green silk, severe enough for republican
simplicity but cut with a perfection no patriot's wife could
mistake. A narrow ribbon encircled her waist. Her hair was
powdered very lightly, in defiance less of the Convention than
of fashion. Around her neck she had placed a small
gold chain, beneath which, hidden by the bodice, lay a
miniature of Auguste at the age of sixteen.

The effort required to descend the staircase, enter the salon,
and greet the first guest was greater than that demanded
of many condemned prisoners walking to the scaffold. Yet she
smiled.

The notary's wife embraced her.

"How pale you are, my dear citoyenne!"

"A headache. It has passed."

"We feared something serious."

"You see there was no cause."

The word cause was repeated throughout the room in several
forms. One guest had heard that the countess was preparing
to leave Carentan; another believed she had received news of
confiscated property; a third mentioned a cousin in England. These
questions, presented as friendly concern, circled the truth like birds
around a field where blood has fallen.

Madame de Dey answered all of them. Never had her
manners been more gracious. She directed the games, remembered the
physician's preference for a particular chair, asked a merchant about
his daughter's marriage, and listened to the mayor's account of
grain requisitions. Only Brigitte, moving between drawing room and dining
room, understood the fixed attention concealed beneath her calm. Whenever
the bell at the outer door rang, the countess paused
for a fraction of a second. Whenever footsteps crossed the
vestibule, color rose into her face and vanished.

Eight o'clock struck.

Then nine.

Every stroke sounded separately, with an interval large enough to
contain a hope and its destruction.

The public prosecutor arrived late. He apologized, saying that official
duties had detained him, and looked around the room before
approaching Madame de Dey.

"You have recovered," he said.

"Entirely."

"I am relieved. Your absence from society had caused anxiety."

"So I have been told."

"Carentan is devoted to you."

"Carentan is attentive."

He smiled at the distinction. Their glances met. She knew
that he suspected; he knew that she knew.

He took a place at a card table but played
badly, which was unusual for him. His attention remained fixed
on the countess. She stood near the fireplace speaking to
two ladies, and he observed the rigid line of her
shoulders, the fingers sometimes pressing against the hidden miniature, the
involuntary movement of her head whenever wheels sounded in the
street.

At half past nine Brigitte entered carrying a tray. Her
face was disturbed.

Madame de Dey went toward her.

"What is it?"

"Nothing, madame."

"You heard something?"

"A man asked at the kitchen door whether this was
the road to Bayeux."

"What man?"

"A carter. No one."

The countess's eyes closed for an instant.

The prosecutor was beside her when she turned.

"Citoyenne," he said, "may I ask for a few words
in private?"

She could not refuse. She led him into the adjoining
library, leaving the door partly open. The room was unheated.
Most of the books had belonged to the late count;
military histories and theological works lined the walls, while the
shelves once occupied by forbidden memoirs stood empty.

The prosecutor did not immediately speak. Political power delights in
pauses, because fear commonly fills them with confession.

"Madame," he said at last, "I am about to commit
an imprudence."

"You?"

"Yes. People imagine that authority makes a man safe. On
the contrary, it merely ensures that his fall will be
observed."

"What do you wish to tell me?"

"A search has been ordered for tomorrow morning. Every house
near the western road will be examined. Yours is on
the list."

Her hand closed on the back of a chair.

"Why mine?"

"Because you have enemies."

"I know of none."

"Then say you have friends whose zeal is dangerous. The
effect is the same."

She remained silent.

"If a stranger were found here," he continued, "even a
stranger received from charity, I could do little. If that
stranger were an émigré, I could do nothing. The law
is precise."

"And yet you called this warning an imprudence."

"It is more. It is perhaps a crime."

"Why do you commit it?"

His face altered. For one moment the official disappeared, revealing
the man who had entered an aristocratic drawing room for
years with desire disguised as politics.

"Because your life is precious to me."

Madame de Dey bowed her head. She could not encourage
him; she dared not offend him.

"You are generous," she said.

"Do not thank me yet. I ask only that, if
anyone comes tonight, he depart before six tomorrow morning. The
barriers will be changed at seven. A patrol will occupy
the Cherbourg road. The garden lane, however, is not watched
before dawn."

She raised her eyes sharply.

"So you believe—"

"I believe nothing. A public prosecutor must believe only what
can be proved. Let us say I have described a
dream."

"Could you save him?" she whispered.

The pronoun completed what her first admission had left uncertain.

"If he is here before midnight and leaves before dawn,
perhaps. If he is discovered in the house, no. If
the military authorities claim him, no. If he has already
been condemned—"

"Stop!"

The cry was low but so full of suffering that
he recoiled.

She recovered herself at once.

"Forgive me. I am ill, as everyone has so kindly
observed."

The prosecutor took her hand; it was cold.

"Trust me."

A sad expression passed over her face. Women in danger
quickly perceive that promises made by powerful men often protect
the giver's vanity more securely than the victim's life. Nevertheless,
she pressed his fingers in gratitude. At that moment she
would have kissed the hand of an executioner who promised
one hour of delay.

They returned to the salon.

Ten o'clock struck.

The guests had begun to lose their first eagerness. No
priest had emerged from a cupboard, no English agent had
been captured beneath the table, and the countess's conduct offered
nothing upon which curiosity could feed. Cards resumed their empire.
Coins clicked. A merchant disputed a point in piquet. The
mayor told a story about a patriotic deputation whose carriage
had become stuck in a marsh.

Madame de Dey sat near the door. She no longer
attempted to work. Her embroidery lay untouched upon her knees.

Would Auguste remember the signal? Three light taps at the
garden window; a pause; then two. Brigitte was to open
the greenhouse door. The room upstairs was ready. Food, money,
papers—everything waited. Perhaps he was even then crossing the fields.
Perhaps he had hidden in a ditch while a patrol
passed. Perhaps he was wounded. Perhaps each step cost him
blood.

But he was coming.

A mother's hope does not reason; it creates. Madame de
Dey saw him approaching with a precision more vivid than
sight. His hair would be wet with mist; his boots
muddy; his face thin and darkened by fatigue. He would
say, "Mother!" No other word. She would hold him. For
one minute—only one!—the Revolution, the Republic, armies, laws, borders, judges,
and death itself would cease to exist.

At a quarter to eleven a violent knocking shook the
front door.

Madame de Dey rose.

Every conversation ended.

The prosecutor looked toward the vestibule; the mayor put down
his cards. Brigitte, carrying a candle, hurried out. The knocking
came again, not secret taps but three blows delivered with
military authority.

A man's voice called:

"Open in the name of the Republic!"

The countess stood perfectly still. Her face became radiant.

Those who watched her interpreted that radiance as terror. It
was hope—wild, ungovernable, impossible hope. Auguste had been pursued; he
had disguised himself; he had come boldly to the front
door; perhaps the public words were meant to mislead observers.

Brigitte opened the door.

A young soldier entered the vestibule, accompanied by a corporal
and a municipal clerk. His uniform was soaked by rain;
a knapsack hung from one shoulder; his hat concealed part
of his face.

The clerk came into the salon.

"Citoyenne Dey," he announced, "by order concerning the lodging of
troops, a recruit of the Seventh Battalion is billeted in
this house for the night."

The young man stepped forward and removed his hat.

Madame de Dey gave a broken cry.

"Auguste!"

She crossed the room before anyone could stop her and
threw her arms around him.

For one astonishing instant the soldier accepted the embrace, stupefied
by the beauty, rank, and despair of the woman clinging
to him. Then he drew back.

"Citoyenne—I—I am not—"

The candles illuminated his face.

He was about twenty. He had Auguste's height, Auguste's fair
hair, and that same youthful curve of the cheek which
the countess had kissed a thousand times. But the eyes
were brown, not blue. A small scar divided one eyebrow.
The mouth was broader. Similarity, that cruel counterfeit with which
chance sometimes mocks affection, vanished as soon as it had
accomplished its work.

Madame de Dey stared at him.

"You are not my son."

The words were spoken quietly. Their calm terrified those who
heard them.

"No, citoyenne," answered the recruit. "My name is Pierre Laroque.
I come from Caen."

She released his coat. Her hands fell to her sides.

The prosecutor immediately approached, using the confusion to conceal what
had occurred.

"Our friend has mistaken you for a relative," he said
to the soldier. "A natural error in this poor light."

But no one in the salon believed the light responsible.

Madame de Dey looked around at the assembled faces: the
mayor's pity, the prosecutor's alarm, Brigitte's horror, the bright curiosity
of the notary's wife, the municipal clerk's sharpened attention. In
that single glance she comprehended that she had betrayed her
secret.

Then the education of a lifetime, aristocratic pride, maternal courage,
and the instinct of survival came to her aid.

"Yes," she said, and smiled with superhuman gentleness. "The resemblance
is extraordinary. My son has been absent for several years.
Forgive me, citizen soldier. A mother's eyes are easily deceived
when her heart is not."

The recruit blushed.

"You need not ask pardon, citoyenne."

"You will stay here tonight, of course. Brigitte, prepare a
room."

Brigitte made an involuntary gesture toward the staircase.

Not his room! Her face cried.

Madame de Dey understood.

"The blue room," she added. "The one near the front
stairs."

The prepared chamber at the end of the corridor must
remain untouched.

The incident broke the evening's social balance. Everyone felt that
continued conversation would be indecent, yet nobody wished to depart
before learning more. The mayor, with admirable presence of mind,
began questioning the recruit about his battalion. Pierre Laroque answered
respectfully. He was the son of a cooper, had drawn
an unlucky number, and was traveling with other men who
had been distributed among neighboring houses. He knew nothing of
émigrés, Morbihan, or the road from Saint-Lô.

The public prosecutor examined his billet, then returned it.

"All is in order."

Madame de Dey resumed her chair. For ten minutes she
maintained an appearance of calm, but the resemblance continued to
torture her. When Pierre turned his head, Auguste appeared; when
he spoke, Auguste vanished. Every gesture first revived her son
and then killed him.

At last the guests rose. Farewells were prolonged by embarrassment.
The notary's wife kissed the countess and whispered, "Courage," though
she did not say for what. The physician offered a
sleeping draught. The mayor pressed Madame de Dey's hand without
words.

The prosecutor remained until the others had gone.

"It is dangerous now," he said in a low voice.

"I know."

"The clerk noticed."

"I know."

"If your son comes, do not let him enter by
the front. Send Brigitte to the garden. The soldier must
see nothing."

"He will come by the garden."

"And at dawn?"

"He will leave."

The prosecutor hesitated.

"Will you promise me?"

"Promise what?"

"That you will think of your own safety."

She looked at him almost with surprise.

"My safety?"

It was clear that the phrase no longer had meaning
for her.

"Madame, if he is arrested here, you will be accused
of harboring an émigré. The tribunal—"

"Good night, citizen prosecutor."

There are forms of despair before which even ambition becomes
silent. He bowed and left.

The outer door was bolted. The house settled into darkness.
Only two candles remained lighted in the hall, and one
lamp burned behind the curtains of the greenhouse. The rain
had stopped. Water fell at intervals from the roof into
the courtyard, each drop distinct in the stillness.

Brigitte led Pierre Laroque to the blue room. The young
soldier, tired and confused, apologized repeatedly for the disturbance caused
by his arrival.

"You could not help your face," the old servant said
harshly.

"My face?"

"Nothing. Go to bed. And if you hear sounds tonight,
you have heard nothing. Do you understand?"

He stared at her.

"I understand that this is a strange house."

"It is an unhappy one. That should be enough."

When Brigitte returned downstairs, she found Madame de Dey standing
at the garden door.

"Madame, you will take cold."

"Open it."

"The door is already unlatched."

"Open it. He may not know."

Brigitte obeyed. A gust of damp air entered the hall.
Beyond the threshold, the path was pale beneath a clouded
sky. The yews seemed like black figures waiting against the
wall.

Madame de Dey went into the greenhouse. The scent of
wet earth, old leaves, and rosemary surrounded her. On a
table Brigitte had placed bread, cold meat, wine, and a
covered bowl of soup kept warm over a small spirit
lamp. Auguste's cloak lay across a chair.

"He must be hungry," said the countess.

"Yes, madame."

"Heat the soup again at midnight."

"It is nearly midnight now."

"No. The clock is fast."

Brigitte dared not contradict her.

They waited.

The church clock began to strike. At the first stroke
Madame de Dey lifted her head. At the second she
moved toward the garden. By the sixth her lips were
counting. At the twelfth she grasped Brigitte's arm.

The sound ended.

Nothing followed.

The deadline named in the letter had passed.

"No," said Madame de Dey. "Our clock is fast."

"The church clock, madame."

"The church clock is fast too. They are never together."

"He may have been delayed."

"Yes. A river swollen by rain. A patrol. He may
have lost the road."

"Certainly."

"He said before midnight because he wished to reassure me.
Men do not understand roads as we do. He will
come."

She returned to the hall and sat facing the garden
door. Brigitte wrapped a shawl around her shoulders.

One o'clock.

From upstairs came the creak of a board. Madame de
Dey sprang up.

"He is in the house!"

"It is the soldier."

"No. Listen!"

A door opened above them; Pierre's footsteps crossed the landing.
He appeared at the head of the stairs, half dressed,
holding a candle.

"Citoyenne," he called softly, "is something wrong?"

Madame de Dey gazed upward.

Seen through the uncertain light, the young man once more
resembled Auguste. The staircase disappeared; the republican uniform became a
traveling coat; the stranger's troubled face was transformed by distance
into the face she desired.

"My child," she whispered.

Pierre descended two steps.

Brigitte hurried toward him.

"Go back! Madame is ill."

"Should I fetch a doctor?"

"No doctor."

Madame de Dey came nearer. She raised one hand, as
if to touch the face above her, but the scar
in his eyebrow caught the candlelight. The illusion broke.

"Leave us," she said.

The recruit obeyed. He could face artillery more easily than
the sorrow in that woman's eyes.

Two o'clock.

The wind rose and shook the greenhouse panes. Somewhere in
the town a patrol passed, its measured steps advancing, filling
the street, then diminishing. A command was spoken. Metal struck
metal. Madame de Dey imagined a musket butt against the
wall, chains, a prisoner forced onward.

"Brigitte, go to the gate."

"I was there a moment ago."

"Go again."

The servant crossed the garden and returned.

"No one."

"You did not look beyond the lane."

"I looked."

"He may be lying near the wall, too weak to
call."

Brigitte took a lantern and searched the lane, the ditch,
and the shadow of the neighboring orchard. When she came
back, her skirts were muddy and her cheeks wet with
tears.

"No one, madame."

Three o'clock.

Hope, when defeated by fact, sometimes abandons reason without abandoning
life. Madame de Dey began to speak of Auguste as
if he were already in the prepared room.

"He will need sleep. We must not wake him at
five. The prosecutor said six, but he can surely delay
the search until seven. At seven there will be daylight.
That is dangerous. We shall dress him as Pierre. Yes—Pierre
can give him the uniform. The two are alike. Did
you see how alike they are? Providence sent that young
man for this purpose."

Brigitte knelt before her.

"Madame, come upstairs. Rest for one hour."

"Rest? While he walks?"

"You will have need of your strength."

The argument prevailed. Madame de Dey allowed herself to be
led to Auguste's chamber, but she would not enter her
own.

A fire burned low on the hearth. The white bed
waited beneath its canopy. The coat and shoes lay ready.
Beside the plate on the table, Brigitte had placed a
small bunch of late violets found under the greenhouse glass.
They were Auguste's favorite flowers.

Madame de Dey stood on the threshold.

The room was no longer a room; it was hope
made visible. Everything in it asserted an arrival which had
not occurred. The folded shirt, the filled glass, the turned-back
coverlet—each object had become a silent witness against God.

She went to the bed and laid her hand upon
the pillow.

"It is cold."

"I will warm it."

"No. He is cold."

The words scarcely passed her lips.

Brigitte seized her hands.

"Do not say that!"

Madame de Dey looked at her with sudden terror.

"Why did you cry out? You believe it."

"I believe nothing. Monsieur Auguste is delayed."

"Then why is the pillow cold?"

"Because no one has lain upon it."

No one.

The simple answer opened an abyss.

Madame de Dey sank into the chair near the bed.
Her eyes remained fixed on the door. Brigitte sat at
her feet, listening with her.

Four o'clock.

At that same hour, far from Carentan, beyond roads, rivers,
sleeping villages, and fields whitened by the first frost, a
military escort entered the courtyard of a former monastery in
Morbihan.

The Republic had converted the building into a prison. Its
chapel housed forage; its refectory served as a guardroom; the
monks' cells confined refractory priests, suspected peasants, deserters, and captured
émigrés. Justice was swift there, because civil war teaches governments
to fear delay more than error.

Auguste de Dey had not escaped.

The bribed soldier, either frightened or treacherous, had revealed the
plan. Auguste was separated from his companions, questioned, and brought
before a military commission. His name, rank, and presence in
arms against the Republic were sufficient. He was condemned before
sunset. Because a column was to leave at dawn, the
execution had been fixed for the early morning.

He had spent the night in a cell whose narrow
window looked east. No letter was permitted, but he had
found a fragment of charcoal and written one word upon
the wall:

Mother.

Toward five, the turnkey entered with a lantern.

"It is time, citizen."

Auguste rose. Youth has this superiority over age: it can
pass in a moment from broken sleep to courage. He
buttoned his coat and asked whether there was a priest.
The turnkey shook his head.

"You may speak to God without one."

Auguste smiled.

"That is what my mother would say."

In Carentan, Madame de Dey suddenly stood up.

"Did you hear?"

Brigitte had heard nothing.

"He called me."

"Who?"

"Auguste."

The certainty in her voice made the servant tremble.

Madame de Dey crossed to the window and opened it.
Dawn had not yet appeared, but the darkness was thinning.
Cold air entered the chamber, stirring the curtains and making
the candle flames bend.

"Auguste!" she called.

The name went out over the sleeping garden.

No answer came.

At the prison in Morbihan, the escort led the condemned
man through the courtyard. The sky was of that colorless
gray which precedes a winter sunrise. Six soldiers waited beside
the outer wall, stamping their feet against the cold. An
officer read the sentence. Auguste listened politely, as though the
document concerned another man.

He refused the bandage.

"May I give the command myself?" he asked.

The officer hesitated, then nodded. Perhaps he too had a
mother.

Five o'clock struck in Carentan.

Madame de Dey placed both hands upon her breast. Her
face, agitated throughout the night by successive waves of fear
and hope, became suddenly peaceful.

"He is no longer coming," she said.

Brigitte began to weep.

"No, madame—no! He will come with daylight."

"He cannot."

"How do you know?"

The countess did not answer. She seemed to be listening
to something very far away.

At the wall in Morbihan, Auguste looked toward the east.
No sun was visible, but a pale line lay across
the horizon. He thought of the garden gate at Carentan,
of his room, of the sound of his mother's dress
in the corridor. For one instant the prison yard disappeared,
and he saw her as she had stood on the
day of his departure, refusing to cry until his carriage
had turned beyond the avenue.

He lifted his hand.

"Ready."

The soldiers raised their muskets.

In Auguste's chamber, Madame de Dey opened her arms.

"My son!"

"Aim!"

She took one step forward, as if someone had entered
the room.

"Fire!"

The volley struck the monastery wall and rolled across the
fields.

Madame de Dey fell beside the prepared bed.

Brigitte screamed, raised her mistress, called for help, and ran
to the corridor. Pierre Laroque, awakened by the cry, reached
the room first. The resemblance which had tortured the mother
now completed the horror of the scene: kneeling beside her,
the young recruit seemed for a moment the son who
had arrived too late.

A physician was summoned. The mayor came, then the public
prosecutor, then officers charged with the search. They found no
fugitive, no weapon, no treasonable paper; Brigitte had burned Auguste's
letter during the night. They found only food grown cold,
an unused coat, clean shoes, a bed in which no
one had slept, and Madame de Dey lying fully dressed
upon the floor.

The physician declared that her heart had ceased to beat.

"She died of exhaustion," he said.

The mayor looked at the clock. Its hands indicated five
minutes past five.

Some days later official news arrived from Morbihan. Auguste de
Dey, émigré and bearer of arms against the Republic, had
been executed at five in the morning on that same
day.

The report was precise. Military death keeps exact hours.

Brigitte, when questioned, swore that at the moment the clock
began to strike, Madame de Dey had heard her son
call, had declared that he could not come, and had
opened her arms before falling. The physician smiled at the
tale; the prosecutor forbade its repetition; the mayor recorded the
coincidence privately in a memorandum intended for his children. Pierre
Laroque carried the memory through several campaigns and related it
years afterward, when uniforms and governments had both changed.

The wise may call the event chance. The pious may
call it Providence. Those who have loved will perhaps give
it another name.

For laws govern bodies, roads separate towns, walls confine prisoners,
and armies defend frontiers; but there are affections before which
distance is powerless. The musket balls that killed Auguste in
Morbihan could not travel to Carentan, yet the blow reached
his mother. At the instant when his life was broken
against a prison wall, the invisible bond by which she
had given him life drew tight across the darkness—and broke
also.

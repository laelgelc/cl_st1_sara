### The Appetite of Romance

At seventeen, Chester Jessup had already discovered that no one
in his family understood him.

His mother understood that he left damp towels on the
bathroom floor, that he could not be trusted to carry
a jar of preserves downstairs without sampling it, and that
he owned four neckties although only one of them could
be worn in daylight. His father understood that Chester's account
at the People's Savings Bank contained eighty-six dollars and forty
cents, accumulated through five summers of lawn-cutting and one winter
of delivering the *Evening Clarion*. His younger sister, Ruth, understood
that if she stood outside his door and whispered "Mildred"
she could cause him to throw a hairbrush.

But none of these things touched Chester's soul.

His soul was pale, lonely, and romantic. It lived, so
far as he could determine, somewhere just below the breast
pocket of his best blue suit, and it belonged entirely
to Mildred Wrigley.

Mildred was sixteen. She had yellow hair, gray eyes, and
a manner of looking at a chocolate cream as if
she pitied it. Her wrists were extraordinarily narrow. Chester considered
this proof of spiritual refinement. He had once seen her
lift a heavy geography book with both hands, and the
sight had moved him nearly to tears. A creature so
delicate ought not to be obliged to struggle with geography,
door latches, suitcases, or the grosser facts of existence.

She ate, Chester believed, as a canary eats: a crumb
at a time, with occasional sips of dew.

He had never, it is true, taken a meal with
her. At school she ate lunch in the girls' cloakroom,
where masculine observation could not follow. At church suppers she
sat with her parents at a distant table, and Chester's
view was impeded by palms, hats, and members of the
Ladies' Benevolent Committee. Yet he had seen Mildred leave half
a sandwich on a plate.

The sandwich had been ham.

Chester, who disliked ham himself, constructed upon this incident a
complete theory of her appetite.

Mrs. Jessup held a different theory.

"That Wrigley girl can put away more cake than any
child I ever saw," she said one Sunday at dinner.

Chester stopped with a forkful of beans halfway to his
mouth.

"You don't know her."

"I watched her eat six pieces at the church social."

"They were little pieces."

"They were not."

"Mamma," said Chester, with restrained dignity, "there are some things
a person ought not to say."

"What things?"

"Things about—about girls."

Mr. Jessup folded his newspaper to expose the sporting page.
"What's wrong with girls?"

"Nothing is wrong with girls."

"Then why can't your mother talk about them?"

Chester saw that he was surrounded by minds incapable of
subtle distinctions.

"It's the way she talks," he said.

Mrs. Jessup considered him. "Mildred ate six pieces of cake.
There isn't any special way to say it."

"She's very delicate."

"She's got excellent digestion."

"Mamma!"

Ruth giggled into her milk.

Chester rose. A bean remained upon his plate in a
pool of tomato sauce. It was vulgar, scarlet, and offensively
substantial. The idea of connecting Mildred with beans, cake, digestion,
or any of the machinery by which the human frame
is maintained seemed to him an outrage against beauty.

"You may excuse me," he said.

"You haven't finished."

"I'm not hungry."

Mr. Jessup lowered the newspaper. "Sick?"

"No."

"In love?" asked Ruth.

Chester departed amid the laughter of his family and ascended
to his room with the measured tread of a condemned
prince.

His room contained a narrow iron bed, a mission-oak bureau,
pennants from three colleges he had no expectation of attending,
a photograph of the high-school baseball team, and a color
print of a young gentleman in evening clothes steering a
motorcar beside the sea. Chester had bought the print because
the young gentleman's jaw resembled his own when Chester pressed
his teeth together.

He stood before the mirror and pressed them together now.

Then, because the face in the glass looked rather alarming,
he relaxed.

On the bureau lay Mildred's photograph. She had given it
to every member of the senior class, but Chester did
not permit this circumstance to lower its value. Beneath it
she had written, "To Chester, with best wishes always, Mildred."
The "always" was underlined. So, unfortunately, was the "best," but
Chester preferred not to examine handwriting as a lawyer examines
a contract. Love was not a matter of punctuation.

He stared at the photograph until the room receded and
a future appeared in its place.

He saw Mildred in a little white house with green
shutters. She wore pale blue and moved noiselessly among roses.
In the evening she sat by a lamp while Chester,
returned from important affairs in the city, told her of
business victories that caused powerful older men to clap him
on the shoulder. Their dinner consisted of a clear soup,
two chops, and something in a silver dish. Mildred touched
almost nothing.

"You eat so little, darling," the future Chester said.

"I live on love," replied the future Mildred.

At that point Ruth pushed open the door.

"Papa says if you're through dying you're to bring in
the porch chairs. It's going to rain."

Chester hurled the hairbrush. Ruth closed the door in time.

The following afternoon, during the interval between English composition and
chemistry, Mildred Wrigley told Chester that her father was sending
her away to school.

They stood behind the gymnasium, where Chester had led her
under the pretense of discussing the senior picnic. A March
wind blew coal smoke across the playground, tugged at Mildred's
red tam-o'-shanter, and made Chester's eyes water in a manner
that might have been attributed to emotion.

"Boarding school?" he repeated.

"In Connecticut."

"For how long?"

"Forever, practically. Two whole years."

Chester leaned against the brick wall. Two years was not
a length of time; it was a geological era.

"But why?"

"Papa says I'm silly."

Chester rejected this charge instantly. Mildred's habit of saying "perfectly
heavenly" about silk stockings and "too horrid for words" about
algebra struck him not as silliness but as the spontaneous
language of a fine nature.

"He says I don't study," she continued. "And Mamma says
I'm extravagant. Just because I charged that rose georgette dress.
It was marked down to nineteen dollars and ninety-eight cents,
so really I saved twelve dollars."

"Of course."

"And now they're going to shut me up in an
awful stone building with bells and porridge."

Mildred's lower lip trembled.

Chester felt within himself the awakening of Manhood. It did
not awaken clearly or with a plan, but it made
his collar seem tight.

"They can't," he said.

"They are. I leave Thursday."

"Thursday!"

"This Thursday."

A bell rang in the main building. From the chemistry
room came the muffled explosion of something pedagogically controlled.

"Mildred," Chester said.

She looked at him.

He had rehearsed declarations in bed, in the bathroom, and
once in the cellar, where his father had overheard the
phrase "from the first instant" and requested to know whether
Chester was composing an advertisement. All the declarations now vanished.
What remained was a dry tongue and a sensation that
his shoes had increased in size.

"Mildred," he said again, in a lower voice, "there are
times in the existence of every fellow when he has
to tell somebody something which he has not previously—well, said."

"Is it about the picnic?"

"No."

"Because I think yellow bunting would be prettier than green."

"It is not about bunting."

"Oh."

"It is about life."

Mildred's eyes widened. Chester was encouraged.

"You are going away. I may never see you again.
Two years from now everything may be different. People change.
Circumstances arise. A person cannot stand calmly by and allow
his entire—his entire future happiness to be sent to Connecticut."

"Chester!"

"I love you."

The words emerged with such violence that a sparrow left
the gymnasium roof.

Mildred put one gloved hand to her mouth. Her glove
was pearl gray with three black buttons. Chester would remember
those buttons long after he had forgotten much of his
chemistry.

"Do you really?"

"More than anything in the world."

"More than your new radio set?"

"Infinitely more."

"I think that is perfectly heavenly."

"Do you love me?"

Mildred looked down, turned one toe inward, and considered. "I
think I do."

Think! The word was not all Chester had hoped for,
but there was no time to demand a stronger verb.

"Then we must not be separated," he said.

"What can we do?"

"We can marry."

Mildred gasped. This was satisfactory.

"We'll go to New York tomorrow," Chester continued, as the
plan constructed itself ahead of his speech. "I have money.
Nearly ninety dollars. We'll get married and rent an apartment.
I'll find a position."

"What sort of position?"

"Business."

"Would we have a maid?"

"At first," Chester said carefully, "we might want to be
by ourselves."

Mildred smiled. "Oh, Chester! It sounds like a motion picture."

This commendation settled the matter.

They agreed to meet at the railway station at seven
forty the next morning. Each would bring one suitcase. Chester
would purchase the tickets, arrange the marriage license, secure lodgings,
obtain employment, and confront any police officers, irate parents, or
hostile business magnates who might arise. Mildred would bring her
rose georgette dress.

That evening Chester ate scarcely any dinner. He looked upon
the roast beef, browned potatoes, creamed onions, pickled beets, and
apple dumpling as a man already withdrawn from domestic comforts.
Mrs. Jessup, observing that he refused a second dumpling, put
her hand against his forehead.

"You are sick."

"I am not sick."

"He's in love," Ruth said.

"I am not in love."

This was Chester's first lie as a married man, or
almost-married man, and he felt it was justified by the
need to protect his bride.

At nine o'clock he announced that he was tired and
went upstairs. There followed preparations of a magnitude not seen
in the Jessup household since Aunt Louisa's visit.

He bathed for forty-five minutes. He shampooed his hair twice,
trimmed one fingernail too closely, applied bay rum, talcum powder,
tooth powder, cold cream to a small blemish, and a
mysterious amber lotion advertised as indispensable to the modern gentleman.
He laid out a maroon tie, changed it for a
blue one, restored the maroon, and finally selected a black
tie with tiny horseshoes.

His traveling costume consisted of the best blue suit, a
cream shirt, lustrous brown shoes, purple socks, and a tan
overcoat with a belt. The haberdasher's label in the coat
proclaimed it "Tailored for Distinction." Chester had not previously understood
the full prophecy of those words.

He packed two shirts, three collars, one pair of pajamas,
a military brush, a razor he had used only twice,
a volume of inspirational essays, and the photograph of the
high-school baseball team. After reflection, he removed the baseball team
and substituted Mildred's photograph, although Mildred herself would presumably be
available for inspection.

Then he withdrew his bankbook from beneath a stack of
old magazines.

The eighty-six dollars and forty cents had been intended for
college. Love, however, was an education; and New York, according
to every magazine Chester read, was a university in which
ambitious young men became assistant managers before lunch.

He slept little. At intervals he rose, switched on the
lamp, and looked at himself in the mirror. Once he
practiced facing Mr. Wrigley.

"Sir," he whispered, "we have acted in accordance with the
dictates of our hearts."

At half past five, believing himself alone, he boxed with
his reflection. He feinted with his left, drove in a
right, and administered to an imaginary kidnapper a final blow
of such precision that his knuckles struck the wardrobe.

His mother called from the hall, "What fell?"

"Nothing."

"Why are you up?"

"I'm studying."

"Go back to bed."

At six fifteen Chester descended with his suitcase.

Mr. Jessup was lighting the furnace. Mrs. Jessup, in a
blue wrapper, stared at her son's overcoat, shining shoes, suitcase,
and expression of noble secrecy.

"Where are you going?"

"New York."

"What for?"

"School business."

"What school business requires pajamas?"

Chester had not anticipated this.

"The senior committee may stay overnight."

"Who else is going?"

"Several people."

"Names?"

"They are not definitely certain yet."

Mr. Jessup appeared in the cellar doorway with soot on
his cheek. "Give him some breakfast, Martha. If he's running
away, he shouldn't do it on an empty stomach."

"I'm not running away."

"Then you can tell us when you'll be back."

Chester attempted the nonchalant shrug of a man whose timetable
depends upon transcontinental negotiations. "Possibly tonight. Possibly tomorrow."

Mrs. Jessup set oatmeal before him. "Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"You'll be hungry on the train."

He rejected oatmeal, eggs, toast, marmalade, and bacon. He accepted
coffee because it seemed adult, drank it too rapidly, burned
his mouth, and departed while his parents watched from the
dining-room window.

Mildred was waiting under the station clock. She wore a
green coat with black fur at the cuffs and carried
a yellow suitcase large enough to contain the rose georgette
dress, several less important dresses, and perhaps a collapsible piano.

"Oh, Chester!" she cried. "Isn't this thrilling?"

Chester bought two one-way tickets. One-way tickets cost more than
he had expected. Still, economy at such a moment would
have been sordid.

On the platform Mildred pressed a hand to her waist.

"I feel funny."

"What's the matter?"

"I didn't have any breakfast."

"Neither did I."

"Mamma was watching me, and I was afraid if I
ate she'd know something."

Chester did not see the logic, but he recognized the
sacrifice. "We can get something on the train."

"I couldn't eat on a train. It makes me nervous."

A red box in the station confectioner's window caught her
eye.

"Perhaps just some candy," she said. "To keep me from
feeling weak."

Chester purchased a pound of assorted creams. Mildred opened the
box before the train arrived. She selected a vanilla cream,
then a maple, then one with a green center whose
identity required investigation.

"I simply adore the green kind," she said. "I could
perish eating them."

Chester smiled tenderly. A pound of candy spread across a
lifetime was not an excessive indulgence.

On the train they sat together in a plush seat.
Towns slipped past: red depots, yellow houses, black factories, white
laundry snapping in back yards. Chester spoke of their future.

They would initially occupy a modest apartment, perhaps overlooking the
river. He would go downtown each morning in a derby
hat. Mildred would arrange flowers, read improving books, and await
him.

"Could we have a Pekinese?" she asked.

"Certainly."

"And a victrola?"

"In time."

"I hate apartments without victrolas."

"We shall have one."

She ate another chocolate.

The train gave a rhythmic clatter over the rails.

Could-perish, could-perish, could-perish.

At the first important junction, Mildred announced that she was
still weak.

"There's a soda fountain," she said.

"The train stops only twelve minutes."

"It won't take a minute."

They hurried into the station restaurant. Mildred ordered a chocolate
sundae with marshmallow, chopped nuts, whipped cream, and a crimson
cherry. Chester ordered nothing. He kept one eye on the
clock while Mildred searched the glass dish for submerged nuts.

"We have four minutes."

"I'm eating as fast as I can."

"We have three."

"This is the most heavenly marshmallow."

"We have two."

Mildred consumed the cherry, abandoned one teaspoonful of melted ice
cream, and ran for the train. They climbed aboard as
the conductor shouted and Chester experienced the first financial and
respiratory strain of married life.

Outside Newark, Mildred remembered that pralines were sold at a
celebrated shop near the New York station.

"My cousin brought me some once," she said. "They melt
away to absolutely nothing."

Chester had begun to distrust things that melted away to
nothing. They generally cost seventy-five cents a box. Nevertheless, upon
arrival in the vast city—among taxi horns, newsboys, porters, brass
rails, tile corridors, blue uniforms, red signs, and citizens moving
with the grim speed of people already licensed to marry—he
purchased pralines.

Mildred ate four while Chester consulted a directory.

The marriage-license office was downtown. It closed at noon on
Saturdays.

"What time is it?" Mildred asked.

"Ten twenty."

"Loads of time. I'm starving."

"You had candy and a sundae."

"That wasn't food."

Chester had never before considered this legal distinction.

"We should get the license first."

"But I feel weak and funny. If I don't eat,
I may faint right in the office."

The thought of Mildred fainting among municipal clerks was intolerable.
Chester led her into a restaurant decorated with orange tiles
and nickel-plated urns.

"I only want a little something," she said.

She ordered pancakes.

The pancakes arrived in a golden stack of four, accompanied
by butter, syrup, and three sausages.

"I didn't order sausage."

"It comes with them," said the waitress.

"Oh, well."

Chester took coffee. He watched Mildred spread butter methodically to
the edges of every pancake. She poured syrup in widening
circles. The sausages disappeared first, perhaps because they had not
been ordered and therefore did not count.

"Do you want one?" she asked when one remained.

"No."

"You don't mind?"

"No."

She ate it.

At eleven fifteen they emerged. Chester hailed a taxi, an
action which cost him in imagination before it cost him
in fact. They sped southward between delivery wagons and green
streetcars. Chester held Mildred's hand. Her fingers were warm and
faintly sticky with syrup.

At the public building they climbed marble steps, hurried down
a corridor, went up in an elevator, were directed down
again, crossed a court, entered the wrong bureau, and finally
arrived before a frosted-glass door at twelve minutes past twelve.

The door was locked.

A stout attendant in a gray uniform was switching off
corridor lamps.

Chester rattled the knob. "We want a marriage license."

"Come Monday."

"But we have to be married today."

"Office closes twelve Satiddy."

"We were delayed."

"Monday."

Mildred leaned toward Chester. "Tell him it's terribly important."

"It is terribly important," Chester said.

The attendant looked from Chester's smooth face to Mildred's red
tam and yellow suitcase.

"How old?"

"Eighteen," Chester said.

He would be eighteen in four months. This seemed sufficiently
near for civic purposes.

The attendant turned to Mildred.

"Eighteen," she said also.

She would be eighteen in twenty-one months, but love's arithmetic
is generous.

"Got proof?"

"Proof?"

"Birth certificates. Parents' consent if you're under age. This ain't
a candy counter. Come Monday with your folks."

"Our folks can't come."

"Then you can't marry."

Chester summoned the bearing of the gentleman in the motorcar
print. "There must be some procedure available to citizens in
our position."

"Sure. Grow up."

The attendant walked away.

For several seconds Chester stared at the locked door. He
had supposed that society welcomed marriage. Ministers praised it, mothers
hinted at it, magazines printed furniture advertisements in its honor.
Yet here society had placed a brass lock between him
and happiness.

Mildred opened the praline box.

"What will we do?"

Chester thought furiously. He recalled an item in the newspaper
about different marriage regulations across state lines.

"Hoboken," he said.

"What?"

"We'll go to New Jersey. The laws may be different
there."

"Is Hoboken nice?"

"It is immaterial whether it's nice."

"I don't want to be married anywhere horrid."

"We'll take the ferry. We can be there in half
an hour."

Mildred ate a praline and brightened. "A ferry sounds romantic."

They started west. Chester, seeking an inexpensive luncheon place, discovered
that Mildred did not care for inexpensive places.

"That one looks greasy."

"We're not eating."

"But it's after twelve."

"You just had pancakes."

"That was breakfast."

"We should go directly to Hoboken."

"I cannot be married when I'm faint."

"You don't look faint."

"That's a cruel thing to say."

Tears appeared in her gray eyes with a speed that
struck Chester as almost professional. People on the sidewalk began
to look at him. He yielded.

They entered a restaurant with red velvet curtains, potted palms,
white tablecloths, and prices printed without reassuring decimal points. A
headwaiter examined Chester's blue suit, Mildred's green coat, their suitcases,
and their youth. After a pause expressive of the decline
of standards, he seated them near the pantry.

"I don't want much," Mildred said, studying the menu. "Just
something light."

Chester looked at the column headed FISH AND SHELLFISH. Lobster
appeared there at a price that might have supported a
modest apartment for several days.

"I adore lobster," Mildred murmured.

"Do you?"

"I could simply expire eating lobster."

The phrase, which had once sounded airy and feminine, now
fell upon Chester's mind like a bill presented for immediate
payment.

"A cold sandwich would be quicker," he suggested.

"A sandwich? On my wedding day?"

They were not married, and there were moments when Chester
wondered whether the failure lay entirely with the city government.
Still, he could not condemn his bride to a sandwich.

Mildred ordered lobster Newburg, potatoes, buttered asparagus, rolls, a fruit
salad, and ginger ale. She explained that the asparagus and
salad were included and would therefore be wasteful not to
eat. Chester ordered soup, then lost interest in it.

The lobster arrived in a silver dish, embedded in a
sauce of glorious yellow. The potatoes were creamed white, the
asparagus a brilliant green, the rolls brown and shining, the
salad jeweled with red cherries and purple grapes. It was
less a luncheon than a parade.

Mildred ate with innocent concentration. She broke rolls, buttered them,
dipped them in sauce, and occasionally sighed with happiness.

"This is the nicest elopement," she said.

Chester studied the check, which lay face down on a
tray at the edge of the table. Though he could
not see the total, it seemed to radiate menace.

"Shouldn't we hurry?"

"There's always another ferry."

"The marriage office in Hoboken may close."

"Not while I'm eating."

This declaration contained a firmness he had not previously associated
with her narrow wrists.

When the silver dish had been cleared, Mildred ordered a
chocolate éclair.

"I thought you were faint."

"I feel better now."

Chester paid. The day's expenditures, including railway tickets, taxi, candy,
sundae, pralines, pancakes, luncheon, tips, and the unnecessary ginger ale,
had reduced his eighty-six dollars to an amount that no
longer suggested an apartment overlooking anything. He carried both suitcases
out of the restaurant because Mildred said the lobster had
made her sleepy.

They reached the ferry house after two. The waiting room
smelled of damp wood, tobacco, machinery, and the river. Chester
purchased tickets and led Mildred aboard.

The ferry was a broad, practical vessel, painted white and
brown, with no interest in romance. Horses and wagons occupied
the lower deck. Passengers crowded along the rails. The river
was gray-green, chopped by wind and streaked with oil. Tugs
whistled. Gulls wheeled above floating orange peels and fragments of
wood.

Mildred stood at the rail.

"How heavenly!" she cried.

Chester placed the suitcases at his feet. New Jersey lay
ahead, smoky and substantial. Somewhere among those buildings might be
another locked office, another attendant, another regulation. Yet he persisted.
Heroism, he had learned that day, consisted largely in going
on after luncheon.

The ferry moved into the river.

Mildred's expression changed.

"What's the matter?"

"Nothing."

She swallowed.

"Are you cold?"

"No."

A tug crossed in front of them. The ferry rolled
gently in its wake.

Mildred closed her eyes.

"I feel funny."

Chester's heart hardened at the familiar phrase. "You can't be
hungry."

"I'm not hungry."

"Thank goodness."

"That is a perfectly horrid thing to say."

The ferry rolled again. Mildred clutched the rail.

"Oh!"

"What is it?"

"I'm sick."

"You've eaten too much."

"I have not."

"You've had chocolates, a sundae, pralines, pancakes, three sausages, lobster—"

"Four sausages."

Chester stared at her.

"One came with the pancakes," she explained faintly, "and I
ordered another while you were paying the taxi."

The river rose and fell behind her.

Mildred turned pale. The pink in her cheeks vanished; her
lips became gray; even the black buttons on her gloves
appeared subdued. Chester, who had imagined her delicacy in a
hundred graceful forms—reclining on cushions, refusing broth, lifting a lace
handkerchief to a noble brow—found that actual delicacy was abrupt,
public, and alarming.

A deckhand pointed toward a door.

"Ladies' cabin, miss."

Mildred disappeared through it.

Chester remained with the yellow suitcase, the tan suitcase, and
the river. Nearby, a small boy ate peanuts from a
paper bag. Each crack of a shell struck Chester's nerves.
A woman peeled a banana. A man unwrapped a liverwurst
sandwich. The whole world, apparently, had decided to eat.

After ten minutes Mildred emerged, supported by an elderly matron.

Her hat was crooked. Her yellow hair clung damply to
her forehead. She looked at Chester with the concentrated resentment
of one who has suffered and has selected the guilty
party.

"I want to go home."

"We're nearly in Hoboken."

"I don't care."

"We came to be married."

"I don't want to be married."

The words, spoken in a thin, exhausted voice, destroyed the
little white house, the green shutters, the roses, the Pekinese,
the victrola, and Chester's derby hat. They vanished together, leaving
behind two suitcases and a smell of river water.

"You said the ferry would be romantic," Mildred continued.

"How was I to know you'd be sick?"

"You made me hurry."

"I waited while you ate for an hour."

"You forced me to eat that lobster."

Chester's mouth opened.

Of all the accusations made in the history of mankind,
this seemed to him the least supported by evidence.

"I forced you?"

"You knew I was weak."

"You've been weak since seven o'clock!"

"You are cruel and selfish, and if I had married
you I would have been miserable forever."

The elderly matron nodded at Chester with grave disapproval, having
arrived too late for the lobster but in time for
the verdict.

The ferry entered its slip. Chains clanked; timbers groaned; the
crowd pressed toward the exit.

"We have to go home," Mildred said.

Chester picked up the suitcases.

They crossed directly to the return boat.

On the way back, Mildred lay in the ladies' cabin
with her eyes closed. Chester sat outside beside a sign
advertising a digestive tonic. The painted gentleman on the sign
held one hand to his stomach and smiled after using
the product. Chester felt that the smile was in poor
taste.

They returned by train late that afternoon. Mildred spoke only
twice. Once she requested water. Once she said that if
her father sent her to Connecticut now she would be
glad to go.

At their home station Mr. Wrigley was waiting with a
policeman, Mrs. Wrigley, Mr. Jessup, and an expression on his
face which caused Chester briefly to reconsider the advantages of
remaining in New York.

Mildred flew into her mother's arms.

"Oh, Mamma! It was awful!"

Mr. Wrigley looked at Chester. "You took my daughter to
New York?"

"Yes, sir."

"To marry her?"

"Yes, sir."

"You are seventeen."

"Yes, sir."

"You have no job."

"No, sir."

"You have no place to live."

"No, sir."

"You have any money left?"

Chester counted mentally. "Some."

Mr. Wrigley took a step forward, but Mildred began to
cry again and the parental attack was postponed. Mrs. Wrigley
led her away. The policeman, disappointed by the absence of
crime, advised Chester not to do it again.

Mr. Jessup picked up the tan suitcase.

"Come on, Romeo."

Chester lacked the strength to object.

At home Mrs. Jessup had kept dinner warm. The dining-room
table displayed creamed potatoes, asparagus, rolls, fruit salad, and, by
a coincidence so monstrous that Chester suspected divine mockery, lobster
patties in yellow sauce.

"Sit down," she said. "You must be starved."

Chester turned pale.

"Take it away."

"What?"

"All of it."

"Chester, you haven't eaten since yesterday."

"I have had enough food."

Ruth looked from the lobster patties to her brother. "Did
Mildred eat?"

Chester closed his eyes.

Mr. Jessup served himself potatoes. "Mr. Wrigley says she's still
going to boarding school Thursday."

A day earlier this news would have struck Chester like
a sentence of exile. Now he imagined the stone school
in Connecticut: bells, porridge, long dining tables, supervised portions, locked
pantries. He felt no grief. He felt only a remote
compassion for the institution.

"I hope," he said, "they give her enough to eat."

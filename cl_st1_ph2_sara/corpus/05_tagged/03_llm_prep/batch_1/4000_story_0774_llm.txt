## THE PRIVATE ACCOUNT

At six o'clock the western windows of Remsen & Company
held the last light above the roofs. Chimneys, water tanks,
and the black frameworks of fire escapes stood sharply against
a saffron sky, while farther downtown a broad stain of
smoke turned purple over the river. In the outer office
the typewriters had stopped one by one. Chairs scraped, desk
covers descended, and young men hurried away with the innocent
velocity of persons whose evenings belonged to them.

Percy Bixby remained at his ledger.

He sat on a high stool under a green-shaded lamp,
his narrow shoulders bent and his cuffs protected by black
alpaca sleeves. Before him lay the private accounts—Mr. Remsen's capital,
the reserve for bad debts, the machinery depreciation account, and
half a dozen others whose names had acquired, during the
past five years, a dreadful personality. They were enemies whom
he had introduced to one another and must now prevent
from comparing notes.

Percy's desk was the smoothest in the office. His pencils
were sharpened to equal lengths; his erasers occupied a japanned
box; his postage stamps were kept beneath a square of
blotting paper so that the gum might not curl in
damp weather. The drawers contained cough drops, court-plaster, a clothes-brush,
two fresh collars, and a small tin of bicarbonate of
soda. He knew where everything was. There was only one
thing in his department that he could not put his
hand upon, and that was one thousand dollars.

Some of it had come back. At the moment the
shortage was seven hundred and thirty dollars. But a shortage,
unlike a debt, is not consoled by reduction. It exists
entire in the mind of the man who has made
it.

Percy drew a figure through the reserve account, carried it
to machinery, hesitated, and erased it. The faint gray scar
remained on the page. That scar seemed more visible than
the figures themselves.

"Still at it, Bixby?"

He started so violently that the ruler at his elbow
fell to the floor.

Oliver Remsen stood at the railing which divided the accounting
department from the passage. He was a tall, well-fed young
man of thirty-five, with the agreeable gravity of one who
has inherited a business and intends to justify the inheritance
by humane improvements. He had abolished the time clock, supplied
filtered water, and once addressed the clerks on the value
of initiative. His father had believed that a man worked
best when watched; Oliver believed that he worked best when
trusted. The results, so far as profits went, were much
the same.

"Just finishing, Mr. Remsen," Percy said.

"You finish too often. There's no virtue in wearing out
the office furniture after hours."

"No, sir."

"And you didn't take your vacation last summer."

"My wife wasn't very well."

"The summer before?"

"We were moving."

"And the summer before that you were helping on the
inventory. I remember. You ought to get away. A man
loses perspective if he sits over figures all the year."

Perspective was precisely what Percy feared. From a distance the
little shifts and transfers might cease to look like bookkeeping
and assume their common name.

"I'll arrange it this year," he said.

"You'd better." Oliver glanced at the open ledger without curiosity.
"By the way, you'll have some help soon. We're having
Garton & Peale go over the books next month. Not
that there's anything wrong. Father always had an outside audit
every third year, and I've been neglecting it."

The blood withdrew from Percy's face so quietly that Oliver
did not observe it.

"An audit?"

"Yes. A mere matter of policy. They begin Monday week."

Oliver went on, speaking of efficiency, system, and the reassuring
effect of independent verification upon investors. Percy heard the words
as one hears separate bells in a fog. Monday week.
Garton & Peale. Independent verification.

"Well, don't stay too late," Oliver concluded. "Mrs. Bixby will
think we're tyrants."

Percy looked down at the page.

"I don't believe she thinks that, sir."

When the door had closed, he remained without movement. Through
the building came the hollow noises of evening: the elevator
gate clanged, a wastebasket rolled somewhere, and a cleaner sang
two bars of a popular song before remembering the emptiness
of the place. Percy's watch ticked in his waistcoat. It
had cost forty dollars, though a twelve-dollar watch would have
kept the same time and perhaps less accusation.

Monday week.

For five years Percy had regarded discovery as inevitable but
indefinite, like death. To give it a date was an
act of cruelty.

He shut the ledger, squared it exactly with the edge
of the desk, put on his coat, and walked to
the elevated.

The train carried him uptown among homeward faces. Men opened
evening papers; women guarded department-store parcels with their knees; a
boy with red ears slept against the window. Percy stood
gripping a strap while lighted flats flashed past—dining rooms, gas
chandeliers, shirt-sleeved fathers, plants in brass jardinieres, children leaning over
tables. Each interior appeared for an instant and vanished. Respectable
lives, all of them, balanced lives, perhaps; though Percy, who
knew something of accounts, understood that the appearance of balance
proves very little.

The matter had begun with a necktie.

Five years earlier Percy had been twenty-eight, unmarried, and as
nearly contented as a young man on twenty-eight dollars a
week could be without exciting the contempt of his acquaintances.
He rented a small room in East Nineteenth Street, ate
breakfast at a dairy lunch, and spent Sunday afternoon reading
the newspapers in a particular chair at the public library.
On Tuesdays he went to a moving-picture house; on alternate
Saturdays, after his suit had been pressed, he attended the
theater from the gallery. His annual requirements were predictable: two
pairs of shoes, three shirts in spring, a felt hat
in September, and at Christmas a box of cigars for
Mr. Remsen, Senior, who did not smoke them.

Percy was the sort of employee about whom employers say,
"If all men were like Bixby, business would be simple,"
without considering that if all men were like Bixby there
might be no business to employ them. He wanted neither
authority nor risk. He wished to be useful, punctual, and
decently clothed. Promotions went, as a rule, to men who
made superiors aware that they desired them. Percy supposed that
merit, like interest in a savings bank, accumulated without attention.

Then he met Stella Brown.

He met her at the Brown flat on West One
Hundred and Thirty-seventh Street, where he had gone with his
fellow clerk, Henry Lowe, to return a borrowed umbrella. The
flat contained Mrs. Brown, three daughters, a piano purchased on
installments, twelve sofa cushions, and a smell compounded of face
powder and stewed tomatoes. Somebody was always passing through the
parlor, adjusting a belt, seeking a hatpin, or calling a
question from the hall.

Stella sat near the window, doing her hair.

It was magnificent hair, heavy and pale brown, with copper
in it where the sun touched. She held three pins
between her lips and regarded Percy in the mirror, which
gave him the impression that she had seen him before
he saw her and had already placed a value upon
him.

"Hullo, Hen," called the youngest Brown girl. "Who's the solemn
party?"

"Mr. Bixby," Henry said. "The financial brain of Remsen &
Company."

Percy blushed. Stella removed the pins from her mouth.

"Financial?" she repeated.

"Bookkeeping," Henry explained, with the brutal helpfulness of a friend.

"Same thing, nearly," Percy said.

Stella smiled, not because the remark amused her but because
Percy's tie did. It was a dull maroon tie selected
for durability, and against his gray suit it produced an
effect of moral earnestness.

The next Saturday Percy bought a blue tie with small
white spots. It cost a dollar and a quarter. He
had never paid more than fifty cents for a tie,
and the purchase gave him a faintly dissolute pleasure. When
he wore it to the Browns', Stella said it was
"quite smart." Percy went home intoxicated by profit.

Courtship, among people of limited income, is commonly described as
romantic because its bookkeeping is performed in secret. Percy soon
discovered that a young woman could not be wooed by
the same schedule under which a bachelor maintained himself. There
were car fares and chocolates, flowers and lunches, gloves mislaid
in taxicabs, and theater seats which cost more because Stella
disliked looking down upon a stage from above.

She did not demand these things crudely. Her method was
more modern.

"Of course we needn't go," she would say, studying the
advertisement for a musical comedy. "I suppose you've seen it."

"No."

"I thought everybody had. Charley says the second act is
wonderful."

Charley Greengay was Percy's rival. He sold advertising space for
a theatrical weekly, earned money in dazzling irregular quantities, and
wore waistcoats upon which small black figures appeared to be
climbing yellow vines. He had a broad, smooth face and
the useful gift of behaving as though every room had
been waiting for him.

Charley sent Stella violets out of season. He knew head
waiters, press agents, and a man who could obtain first-night
tickets. His name had once been mentioned in a divorce
case, a circumstance which injured him with mothers and improved
him with daughters. Mrs. Brown said he was too fast.
Stella replied that at least he was going somewhere.

Percy disliked Charley with the concentrated dislike of a methodical
man for luck. Yet Charley was not continuously prosperous. There
were weeks when he borrowed five dollars from Henry Lowe
and evenings when the yellow waistcoat appeared without dinner beneath
it. His strength lay in not permitting adversity to lower
his voice.

"What do they pay you down at Remsen's, Percy?" Stella
asked one Sunday.

They were walking in Riverside Park. The river shone through
leafless trees; motorcars passed with glossy occupants tucked under rugs.
Percy had been telling her that Mr. Remsen considered him
indispensable.

"Forty dollars a week," he said.

The statement was spoken before his conscience could inspect it.
His actual salary was thirty-two dollars. He had recently received
an increase from twenty-eight and felt, in the glow of
it, that forty was the next natural figure.

"That's not bad," Stella said.

"No. And there's a good future."

"How good?"

"Well—there's no telling."

This was true, though not in the sense she understood.
Stella slipped her hand beneath his arm. Eight fictitious dollars
a week had done what six months of honest devotion
had failed to accomplish.

Afterward Percy had opportunities to correct the figure. He did
not use them. A lie about salary is especially difficult
to withdraw because every day that it survives it acquires
furnishings. Stella calculated rent, clothing, entertainment, and savings on the
basis of forty dollars. Percy watched the imaginary household assemble
under her direction: a five-room flat, mahogany dining furniture, a
maid twice a week, and a telephone. He had not
known that eight dollars could be made to purchase so
much.

He proposed on a rainy Thursday in the Brown parlor.
The youngest sister was playing "Every Little Movement" on the
piano; Mrs. Brown and a neighbor discussed neuralgia in the
dining room; and somebody's wet umbrella dripped into a jardiniere.

Stella accepted him with composure.

"I believe you're dependable," she said.

There are men who would have resented the word. Percy
cherished it.

The engagement lasted four months. During this period the future
increased in cost. The flat required a deposit; furniture required
cash, despite the expansive claims of installment dealers; there were
rings, license fees, wedding clothes, and the honeymoon. Stella desired
only three days at Atlantic City, but three days at
Atlantic City, when properly performed, represented a month of Percy's
salary.

He began by borrowing. The bank declined him because he
possessed no collateral. A loan company offered two hundred dollars
at a rate that seemed to Percy not so much
interest as revenge. Henry Lowe could spare ten. Mr. Remsen,
Senior, might have advanced the money, but to confess poverty
would expose the forty-dollar fiction.

Then one afternoon a customer paid an old account in
cash.

The cashier had gone to lunch. Percy received the money,
counted it, and locked it in his desk: two thousand
dollars in large bills. For twenty minutes it lay beside
his pencils and postage stamps. He made no decision. His
mind simply arranged facts.

He was trusted. His salary would certainly rise. A temporary
advance of one thousand dollars could be restored in small
amounts. No person would suffer. The firm carried reserves vastly
larger than the sum. Indeed, in a commercial sense, the
firm would not know the difference. Money was always moving
through accounts; only sentimental persons imagined that particular dollars retained
their identity.

At three o'clock Percy took ten one-hundred-dollar bills, placed them
in an envelope, and put the envelope inside his coat.

His heart beat painfully, but his hands remained steady. This
steadiness reassured him. Criminals, he supposed, trembled.

The concealment required more ingenuity than the taking. Percy credited
part of the payment to the wrong customer, shifted an
old debit, and postponed an adjustment. Within a week the
shortage had been distributed among three accounts, each innocent by
itself and collectively fatal.

He married Stella on a Wednesday.

For a few months happiness appeared to justify everything. Their
flat had red paper in the dining room, a brass
clock on the mantel, and a Morris chair which no
one found comfortable but which gave the sitting room distinction.
Stella moved among these objects with an ease that rewarded
Percy for having bought them. She wore a gray traveling
suit on the honeymoon and a black hat with two
sweeping feathers. When they crossed the hotel lobby, men looked
at her. Percy felt that the thousand dollars had not
been spent but embodied.

He intended to repay it within two years.

Then expenses, those anonymous dependents of marriage, began arriving. The
forty-dollar salary had to be maintained in appearance, although only
thirty-two dollars entered the household. Percy made up the difference
from the stolen fund until little remained. When his salary
rose to thirty-five, Stella interpreted the increase as forty-five. A
fiction, like a growing child, outgrows its first clothes.

She was not wasteful in the vulgar sense. She compared
prices, returned imperfect goods, and could discuss for fifteen minutes
whether a hat at twelve dollars and a half possessed
more value than one at nine seventy-five. But she required
the hat. She believed that good appearance was capital, that
social life was investment, and that cheapness cost more in
the end. These principles were sound enough when applied to
enterprises with income.

Percy attempted economies.

"Do we really need the telephone?" he asked once.

Stella looked at him.

"Need it? No. We don't need curtains, either. We could
tack newspaper over the windows."

"I only meant—"

"Everybody we know has a telephone."

Percy retained it.

He saved privately instead. He walked to the office in
pleasant weather, ate a fifteen-cent lunch, wore shirts one season
longer, and gave up the theater except when Stella wished
to go. Each month he restored five dollars, sometimes ten,
to the hidden shortage. Then there would be a dentist's
bill, a new coat for Stella, or a visit from
Mrs. Brown that required delicacies, and the installments ceased.

The burden changed Percy less visibly than might have been
expected. He arrived at nine, balanced his columns, answered questions,
and was promoted to head bookkeeper. The promotion added responsibility
before it added salary. He became leaner. A vertical line
formed between his eyes. He developed the habit of returning
to the office after dinner on the pretense of special
work.

Vacations were impossible. Another man might notice an irregularity. Percy
told Stella that the company could not spare him.

"You've made yourself too cheap," she said. "They never spare
men they can overwork."

"Mr. Remsen appreciates me."

"Appreciation doesn't pay rent."

She spoke accurately, which is often more damaging than speaking
cruelly.

Mr. Remsen, Senior, died in the third year of Percy's
marriage, and Oliver took control. Oliver raised wages, installed brighter
lights, and sent a circular to department heads requesting frank
suggestions. Percy suggested nothing. He feared any change in the
system, particularly improvement.

Oliver took an interest in him.

"You're too valuable to be merely a bookkeeper all your
life, Bixby," he said. "You ought to develop executive qualities."

Percy promised to do so. Executive qualities, as he understood
them, involved leaving one's desk and asking other men what
they were doing. He preferred to remain where the danger
was.

At home, Stella's dissatisfaction became organized.

She had expected marriage to move forward. Other couples they
knew migrated from flats to apartments with elevators, from orchestra
seats occasionally to orchestra seats habitually, from imitation mahogany to
the real wood. The Bixbys remained fixed. Their furniture grew
shabby but not antique; their friends advanced beyond them; the
youngest Brown girl married a dentist who owned a motorcar.

Stella did not love luxury merely for softness. She loved
it as evidence of motion. A new gown meant that
life had not stopped. A restaurant supper meant that the
city still admitted her. She was thirty now, and her
beauty, though undiminished, had begun to seem to her a
security on which no further advances were being made.

Charley Greengay reappeared from time to time.

He had survived the divorce scandal, a failed magazine, and
six months in Chicago. He now managed publicity for a
chain of theaters and wore quieter waistcoats, which indicated increased
prosperity. When he met Stella on Broadway, he took her
to lunch at a hotel where the napkins were pink
and telephoned Percy afterward to explain that it had been
"purely business."

"What business?" Percy asked that evening.

"Charley thinks I've a good manner with people. He says
I ought to be doing something."

"You have enough to do."

"Keeping four rooms tidy and asking whether you'll be home
for dinner?"

Percy said nothing. Silence had become his only extravagance.

Yet he loved her. Or, more exactly, he loved Stella
together with the life for which he had paid: the
pale hair arranged before the mirror, the gloves on the
hall table, the scent of powder in the bedroom, the
impatient tapping of her slipper while he fastened a collar
button. These details had become the visible form of his
guilt. To lose them would be to admit that the
theft had purchased nothing.

And now there was to be an audit.

Percy left the elevated at his station and walked toward
the flat. Store windows cast squares of light upon the
pavement. In one stood a wax woman wearing a dinner
gown the color of champagne. Percy paused. Stella would have
liked the gown. The price card read sixty-five dollars, which
was absurd; yet Percy's mind, trained by marriage, immediately calculated
how many weeks would be required to pay for it.

He found Stella at the dressing table. Two electric bulbs
flanked the mirror, producing an illumination more searching than kind.
She wore a blue kimono, and her hair hung down
her back, splendid as ever. A theater program lay beside
the powder jar.

"You're late," she said.

"I had work."

"I dined alone."

"I'm sorry."

"Charley had two tickets for tonight, but I told him
I couldn't go."

Percy removed his coat carefully and hung it in the
closet. Even at the edge of disaster he could not
leave a garment over a chair.

"Stella, I've got to tell you something."

"If it's about being late, you've told me."

"It isn't."

She looked at him through the mirror. His face frightened
her, but fright in Stella first expressed itself as annoyance.

"Well?"

"Not now. I have to see Mr. Remsen tomorrow."

"That sounds mysterious."

"It won't be mysterious afterward."

He slept little. The brass clock struck each half hour
with a thin, expensive note. At four he rose, drank
water, and stood at the window. An elevated train went
by, carrying cleaners, bakers, and newspaper men toward the beginning
of another orderly day. Percy envied the poorest of them.
A man with three dollars in his pocket and no
secret was rich.

At nine he was in the office.

He waited while Oliver dictated letters, conferred with the sales
manager, and telephoned a club about luncheon. At eleven Percy
knocked.

"Come in, Bixby. What is it?"

Oliver sat behind a broad desk bare except for an
inkstand, a telephone, and a silver-framed photograph of Mrs. Remsen
with two white dogs. Behind him hung a chart showing
the company's growth in red ascending lines. Percy had prepared
the chart.

"I want to speak about the audit."

"Yes?"

"There's something wrong in the accounts."

Oliver's expression became attentive rather than alarmed.

"An error?"

"No, sir."

"What, then?"

Percy had rehearsed phrases—temporary borrowing, unauthorized advance, personal emergency. They
deserted him.

"I took a thousand dollars."

Oliver leaned back.

"When?"

"Five years ago."

"Five years!"

"I've put back two hundred and seventy. The shortage is
seven hundred and thirty now."

"You took it all at once?"

"Yes."

"And concealed it?"

"Yes."

"How?"

Percy described the entries. Once started, he spoke clearly. Here
at last was bookkeeping, and bookkeeping he understood. He named
accounts, dates, transfers, corrections. Oliver listened with growing astonishment. The
mechanism was both skillful and pitiful. Percy had devoted five
years of excellent service to protecting one dishonest afternoon.

When he finished, Oliver said, "Why?"

"I was getting married."

Oliver waited.

"I'd told my wife I earned more than I did.
There were expenses. I meant to repay it."

"Did your wife know?"

"No. Nobody knew."

"Have you taken anything since?"

"No, sir."

Oliver rose and walked to the window. His first sensation
was anger, and beneath it something more personal: wounded managerial
faith. He had trusted Percy not merely because Percy seemed
trustworthy, but because Oliver's philosophy required trustworthy men. A dishonest
employee was also an argument against him.

He turned.

"You understand that I ought to call our attorney."

"Yes, sir."

"And that this is embezzlement."

Percy flinched at the word. He had been prepared for
police, prison, disgrace, but not for accurate vocabulary.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you come to me before?"

"I thought I could fix it."

"That is what people always think when they are making
a thing worse."

"Yes, sir."

"Sit down, Bixby. You look ill."

Percy sat on the edge of a chair. Oliver asked
more questions. Was there gambling? No. Another woman? Percy looked
almost offended. Drink? No. Speculation? No. Oliver had been ready
to understand vice in several recognized forms; furniture, theater tickets,
and a salary exaggerated during courtship seemed an insufficient machinery
for ruin.

At last he telephoned his attorney, but not to summon
him. He asked a hypothetical question, received an answer, and
dismissed it.

"I'm going to lend you seven hundred and thirty dollars,"
he said.

Percy stared.

"You will restore the accounts before Garton & Peale arrive.
Then you'll sign a note to me personally. We'll deduct
fifteen dollars a month from your wages."

"I don't deserve—"

"No, you don't." Oliver's voice hardened. "Don't make the mistake
of thinking I'm saying you do. I'm doing this because
prosecution would destroy more than it would correct. Also because
you confessed before the audit. That counts."

"I couldn't let them find it."

"That counts too."

Percy put his hands over his face.

Oliver looked away. Benevolence is most comfortable when the recipient
remains dignified.

"There's one condition," he said. "You tell your wife. The
whole truth. A secret like this is not paid merely
by balancing figures."

Percy lowered his hands.

"Yes, sir."

"And take a week off after the audit."

"I'd rather not."

"You're going to."

Oliver came around the desk and placed a hand on
Percy's shoulder. He felt at once generous, embarrassed, and disappointed.
One may rescue a man from prison and still have
no idea what to do with him afterward.

That evening Oliver told his wife.

Mrs. Remsen, who was intelligent without making a profession of
it, listened while cutting the pages of a new novel.

"Poor creature," she said.

"Poor? He stole from us."

"I meant the wife."

"She didn't know."

"That may be why I pity her."

Oliver explained Percy's lie about the salary, the marriage expenses,
and the long repayment.

Mrs. Remsen laid down the paper knife.

"You've saved him from the law," she said. "Can you
save him from having chosen the wrong woman?"

"I don't know that she's the wrong woman."

"My dear Oliver, a man doesn't steal a thousand dollars
to marry the right woman. He may starve for her,
or wait for her, or live with her over a
bakery. But if he has to counterfeit himself before she'll
take him, the marriage begins with the punishment."

Oliver disliked judgments which left no room for administrative remedy.

"He says he loves her."

"Of course. That is why it is serious."

Percy told Stella after dinner.

He had insisted that they eat first. The meal consisted
of lamb chops, canned peas, and a custard from the
delicatessen. Stella spoke twice about a leak in the bathroom
faucet. Percy answered and continued eating, though each mouthful seemed
an act of fraud added to the others.

When the dishes were cleared, he placed the household account
book on the table.

"What's that for?" Stella asked.

"We have to reduce expenses."

"How much?"

"About fifteen dollars a month to begin with. Maybe more."

She sat opposite him. Her face became still.

"Why?"

He told her.

Unlike Oliver, she did not interrupt. Percy began with the
salary lie, then the thousand dollars, the false entries, the
five years of concealment, and Oliver's loan. The story seemed
longer at home than in the office because every sentence
touched an object in the room. The rug, the clock,
the dining table, the curtains—all had been accessories after the
fact.

When he finished, Stella looked around.

"So this is what we've been living on."

"Partly. At first."

"You stole it."

"Yes."

"For me, I suppose."

"I did it because I wanted to marry you."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No."

"And you've lied to me for five years."

"Yes."

She rose and went to the mantel. The brass clock
ticked between them. Percy had imagined tears, anger, perhaps forgiveness
after anger. Her calmness was worse.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Mr. Remsen lends me the money. I pay him back
out of my salary."

"How long?"

"Four years, at fifteen a month. I want to pay
faster if we can."

"Four more years."

"It may not take that long."

"Your salary hasn't exactly been galloping."

"He's been very good to me."

"Good!" Stella laughed once. "He keeps you in the same
chair for years, pays you just enough to stay alive,
and then he's good because he doesn't send you to
jail."

"He could have."

"Yes. That would have been inconvenient for the office."

Percy looked at the account book.

"We can move to a cheaper flat. Give up the
telephone. I'll bring lunch from home. We don't need—"

"We?"

He looked up.

"I'm not going to spend four years helping you pay
for five years of lies."

"I didn't ask you to pay. I'm asking you to
economize."

"That is paying."

Her practical mouth had tightened. Percy saw, with sudden clarity,
that she was not deciding what to do. She had
been deciding for months; his confession had merely supplied the
occasion.

"What are you saying, Stella?"

"I'm going back to work."

"You've never worked."

"I did before I married you. I can type. I
can learn shorthand again."

"Where?"

"Charley needs a secretary."

The name entered the room with all its old spotted
waistcoats and first-night tickets.

"You've discussed this with him?"

"He mentioned it."

"When?"

"Several times."

Percy stood. "You're going to leave me for Charley Greengay?"

"I'm going to work for him."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's all I'm answering."

He walked to the window and back, an excursion of
twelve feet. Anger rose in him, but he had so
little habit of expressing it that it emerged as precision.

"Charley is not dependable."

"No," Stella said. "He is ambitious."

"He's had three jobs in two years."

"And each one paid more than the last."

"He's mixed up in every cheap, flashy—"

"He lives. He doesn't sit in an office until his
collar wilts and call it virtue."

Percy's face went white.

"I sat there because of what I did for you."

"No. You did it because you were afraid. You took
the money because you were afraid I wouldn't marry you,
and then you stayed at the desk because you were
afraid they'd find out. You've been afraid ever since I
met you."

The cruelty of this lay in its accuracy. Stella had
found the hidden account beneath the account.

Percy said, "You liked what the money bought."

"I thought you earned it."

"You never asked whether we could afford things."

"I asked what you earned. You told me."

There was no answer to that.

Stella went into the bedroom and brought out a suitcase.
It was the expensive leather suitcase purchased for Atlantic City,
its brass fittings darkened but intact. She laid it on
the bed and began folding clothes.

Percy stood in the doorway.

"Are you going tonight?"

"I'll stay with Mother."

"Does she know?"

"She knows I haven't been happy."

He watched Stella place collars, stockings, gloves, and a blue
dress into the case. Her movements were controlled. She had
always packed well. Even grief, if grief existed in her,
was made to occupy little space.

"What about the furniture?" he asked.

The question sounded absurd, but the furniture had been the
material vocabulary of their marriage.

"Sell it. Keep it. I don't care."

"The clock was yours."

"I never liked that clock."

He had paid eighteen dollars for it because she had
admired one like it in a shop window.

At the hall mirror she put on her hat. Her
hair, ordered beneath it, showed no disturbance. Percy remembered the
first afternoon at the Browns', the three pins between her
lips, the maroon tie she had smiled at. Five years
had passed from one mirror to another.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"So am I, Percy."

"Are you?"

She considered this.

"I'm sorry you weren't what you said you were."

Then she left.

The door closed softly, for Stella disliked slamming doors. Percy
returned to the dining room. The clock ticked. On the
table lay the account book, open to the page for
household incidentals. He sat before it until midnight.

Within a month the flat was surrendered. Stella took her
clothes, the bedroom mirror, and two chairs belonging to her
mother. Percy sold the remaining furniture to a dealer for
less than a third of what he still remembered paying.
Respectability, secondhand, has a low market value.

He moved to a boarding house on West Twenty-third Street,
where his room contained an iron bed, a washstand, one
competent chair, and a picture of a stag pursued by
hounds. Breakfast was served at seven-thirty. Dinner included dessert except
on Fridays. The landlady objected to late baths and admired
steady gentlemen.

Percy resumed the life he might have led if he
had never met Stella, but one does not return to
an earlier life merely by reproducing its expenses. He read
the newspaper in the evening, took walks on Sundays, and
kept his clothes brushed. His room cost six dollars a
week. He bought no flowers.

The office heard that Mrs. Bixby had gone to visit
her mother. Later it heard nothing, which is the usual
form in which offices receive truth. Charley Greengay's name appeared
occasionally in the theatrical columns. A new production had been
"ingeniously exploited by Charles Greengay, publicity director." Stella became his
secretary and purchased three smart suits on credit. Whether she
became anything else was a matter about which Percy refused
to speculate, and therefore thought constantly.

Six months after the audit, Oliver found him at his
ledger near dusk.

The outer office was empty again. Beyond the windows the
city stood in a pale winter glow, its windows beginning
to kindle floor by floor. Percy's desk remained orderly. There
were new pencils in the tray and a clean square
of blotting paper over the stamps.

"Still making a religion of overtime?" Oliver asked.

Percy smiled faintly. "I'm leaving now."

"How are you getting on?"

"Very well, sir."

Oliver knew of the separation. Mrs. Remsen had said only,
"You see?" which he had found unsympathetic.

Percy closed the ledger.

"I wanted to ask you something, Mr. Remsen. About the
note."

"Yes?"

"Could you deduct thirty dollars a month instead of fifteen?"

"Can you manage that?"

"My expenses are lower now."

Oliver looked at him. Percy's coat was neatly pressed, but
the cuffs had begun to shine. His tie was dark
blue without spots. He seemed older and, in an odd
way, more peaceful. The worst had occurred; routine, which had
once imprisoned him, now sheltered him.

"There's no need to punish yourself," Oliver said.

"I'm not. I want to get it paid."

"All right. Thirty, if you insist."

"Thank you."

Oliver hesitated. "Bixby, I hope you understand that, as far
as the company is concerned, the matter is closed."

"Yes, sir."

"And personally—I don't think less of you than I did."

This was kindly intended, but both men knew it could
not be true. Percy spared him the necessity of improving
the sentence.

"You've treated me better than I had any right to
expect."

He put on his coat, checked the top drawer, extinguished
the green-shaded lamp, and left for his boarding-house dinner.

Oliver remained at the railing. On Percy's desk the ledgers
stood in a perfect row, their backs labeled, their totals
sound, every dollar restored to its proper place or moving
there by regular installment. He had corrected the books, preserved
a useful employee, and prevented a scandal. By all the
standards available to enlightened business, he had succeeded.

Yet the man walking toward the elevated had paid more
than seven hundred and thirty dollars, and would go on
paying after the note was canceled. There are accounts no
auditor can examine and no employer, however humane, can balance.

### Little River

Azélie came to the plantation store twice in one week,
and on Saturday she came again.

She crossed the yard slowly, carrying a large tin pail
that struck her knee at every other step. The pail
had once been painted green, but the color remained only
beneath the handle and in a few chipped islands around
the bottom. She wore a faded blue calico dress, drawn
tight across the shoulders and hanging loose at the waist.
Her shoes were thick with pale dust from the river
road.

'Polyte saw her through the open store door and made
a sound of impatience. He was balancing the week's accounts
on a high desk, with one foot resting upon the
rung of a stool. The store was close and pungent.
It smelled of molasses, coal oil, salt meat, damp rope,
tobacco, and the sharp brown soap kept in blocks under
the counter.

"I wish she would go 'way f'om yere," he said,
though there was no one near enough to hear except
old Baptiste, who slept on a flour barrel in the
corner.

Azélie stopped in the doorway. She did not step in
until 'Polyte looked at her.

"W'at you want?" he asked.

She raised the pail a little, as if it were
an answer.

"Pa say fo' you to give us some meal."

"Your pa been had meal Tuesday."

"It all gone."

"Gone! Bonté! W'at you people do with meal? Eat it
night an' day?"

Azélie considered this.

"We eat it," she replied.

'Polyte left the desk and came toward her. He was
a compact young man, dark, active, and proud of the
order he maintained in Mr. Mathurin's store. His white shirt
was open at the throat, and his sleeves were rolled
above sinewy brown wrists. A key ring hung at his
side. When he moved, the keys made a brisk, important
sound.

"How much meal he wants?"

"A pail."

"An' w'at else?"

"Some meat. An' coffee."

"No coffee."

"Pa say coffee."

"I say no coffee. His account run too high already.
You tell him that."

She did not move.

"Eh bien? You don' hear?"

"He say some sugar, too."

'Polyte laughed once, without amusement.

"Sugar! He want sugar now. Maybe he wants oranges, an'
wine, an' a gold carriage to ride in."

Azélie looked past him at the shelves. Their abundance had
an oppressive stillness: sacks, tins, bottles, folded cloth, plugs of
tobacco, boxes of cartridges, coils of rope. She looked neither
covetous nor ashamed. Her glance merely counted what was there.

"Pa say coal oil," she added.

"No."

"An' soap."

"No soap."

"Ma needs it."

"You people always need. You don' never pay."

"We go pay when the cotton's sold."

"Your pa's cotton ain't fit to pay fo' the seed.
He knows that."

She lowered the pail and set it on the floor.
Its hollow ring disturbed Baptiste, who shifted on the barrel
and resumed sleeping.

Mr. Mathurin entered from the gallery, brushing cigar ash from
the front of his linen coat. He was broad, gray-bearded,
and habitually amused. His plantation house stood across the yard,
beyond two live oaks and a row of whitewashed pillars.
The shutters had been closed against the afternoon heat.

"Ah, Azélie," he said. "You come to take my whole
store away?"

"No, monsieur."

"She tryin'," said 'Polyte. "Meal, meat, coffee, sugar, coal oil,
soap. To-morrow she comes fo' the building."

Mr. Mathurin laughed.

"And your father, comment ça va?"

"He's well."

"Still going back to Little River when the picking is
finished?"

"Yes, monsieur."

"Then he must make a fine crop before he goes.
Give her the meal, 'Polyte. A little meat also."

"His account—"

"Yes, yes, I know his account. I know everybody's account
better than they do themselves. Give what I said."

'Polyte filled the pail halfway with meal and wrapped a
narrow strip of salt pork in paper. He set the
parcel on top.

"Coffee," Azélie reminded him.

"No coffee."

"Coal oil."

"No."

"Soap."

"You go home."

She took up the pail, but stood waiting.

"W'at you waitin' fo' now?"

"Pa say cartridges."

At that, Mr. Mathurin laughed so heartily that his cigar
fell from his hand.

"Cartridges! Mathurin Landry is to feed the family and provide
the hunting besides. Ma foi, it is an honor."

'Polyte's face hardened.

"No cartridges. You tell your pa he can come ask
me hisself."

"He's plowing."

"He ain't plowing at this hour."

"He's fixing the plow."

"Then let him fix it."

Azélie turned away. She descended the two wooden steps and
crossed the yard with the pail striking her knee again.
Neither man spoke until she had passed the cotton shed.

Mr. Mathurin lighted another cigar.

"She has a gentle face, that girl."

"She got no sense," said 'Polyte.

"No?"

"No sense of nothing. No shame, neither. They take, take,
take. Her pa don' care how much he owes. Her
ma don' care. That whole Li'le River crowd's the same."

Mr. Mathurin looked at him with mild curiosity.

"You are severe to-day."

"I know their kind."

"Perhaps. But give them enough to finish the season. Après
tout, they work."

When Mr. Mathurin had gone, 'Polyte returned to the desk.
The figures no longer held themselves quietly in their columns.
Azélie's requests intruded between them: coffee, coal oil, soap, cartridges.
He had the unreasonable impression that she was still standing
in the doorway, not pleading, not smiling, only waiting for
the things she had been sent to obtain.

He shut the account book with a slap.

That night the air lay warm and heavy about the
plantation. The cabins had grown quiet before ten, though now
and then a child cried or a man called across
the quarters. Frogs sounded from the ditch. The cotton fields,
pale under the rising moon, seemed to bloom beyond their
proper limits and spread toward the dark line of woods.

'Polyte slept in a small room behind the plantation office.
Shortly after midnight he awoke, uncertain what had disturbed him.
He remained still and listened.

There came a faint metallic touch, then silence.

He rose without lighting the lamp. The corridor boards felt
warm beneath his bare feet. Through the open end of
the passage he saw the moon, narrow and bright as
a keen, curved blade.

The sound came again from the store.

'Polyte dressed quickly, took the revolver from beneath his pillow,
and went out. He did not cross the yard directly.
He passed around the office and approached along the shadow
of the gallery. The store door was closed. One shutter
at the rear stood open a hand's breadth.

He waited.

There was a low scrape within, followed by the soft
fall of something upon the floor.

'Polyte moved to the shutter and pushed it inward.

A match flared.

For an instant he saw Azélie beside the counter. She
had placed a sack on the floor and was bending
over it. The light shone upon her face, her bare
forearm, and the neck of a coal-oil bottle. Then she
dropped the match, and darkness closed over the room.

"Don' move," he said.

She did not move.

He climbed through the window and struck a match of
his own. Azélie stood with her hands at her sides.
At her feet were coffee, soap, cartridges, sugar, and the
bottle of oil.

"You!"

"Yes."

The match burned his fingers. He threw it down and
lighted the hanging lamp. Its yellow flame revealed the confusion
around the window. A nail had been worked loose from
the shutter; a flour box had been dragged beneath it.

"How you got in yere?"

"Through there."

"I see through there! Who opened that shutter?"

"I did."

"With w'at?"

She showed him a narrow iron blade, broken from some
farm implement.

"Your pa sent you?"

"No."

"Your ma?"

"No."

"You come by yourself?"

"Yes."

The quiet of her answers angered him more than denial
would have done. He caught her by the wrist.

"You know I can put you in jail?"

She glanced at his hand, then at his face.

"Yes."

"You know it's stealin'?"

"We needed them things."

"That don' make no difference."

She seemed to think he was mistaken.

"Ma got no oil. The baby been sick in the
night. Pa needs cartridges fo' birds. We got no soap,
no coffee."

"So you break in my store?"

"Mr. Mathurin's store," she corrected.

His grip tightened. "You got no shame at all?"

"I was goin' take just w'at we needed."

The distinction appeared sufficient to her. 'Polyte looked at the
gathered articles and felt suddenly ridiculous standing there barefoot, with
an unloaded revolver thrust into his waistband, preaching law to
a girl who had come for soap and coffee.

Yet beneath the ridicule another feeling had begun, swift and
hot, and so unwelcome that he mistook it at first
for rage. Her wrist was small in his hand, but
not frail. He could feel the steady beat of blood
beneath the skin. A loose strand of hair lay against
her cheek. She smelled of night air, dust, and the
crushed fennel that grew near the ditch.

He released her.

"You ought to be whipped," he said.

She bent to pick up the sack.

"Leave that!"

She straightened again.

He walked once to the door and back. The lamp
smoked above them. He imagined calling the watchman. He imagined
Mr. Mathurin coming in his nightshirt, genial even before the
scattered goods and broken shutter. He imagined Azélie being taken
away. Each image offended him.

At last he said, "Take them."

She began to place the articles in the sack.

"Wait. Not the cartridges."

"Pa needs—"

"I'll give him cartridges to-morrow. You hear? You don' come
yere like this no mo'."

"All right."

"If you need something, you ask me."

"You don' give it."

"I'll give it."

She paused with the sugar in her hand.

"Anything?"

"Anything you need."

She put the sugar into the sack.

"You promise?"

"Yes, I promise."

He expected some gratitude then, some change in her face.
None came. She tied the sack with a length of
twine and lifted it to her shoulder.

At the window he stopped her.

"You go by the door."

He unlocked it. The keys trembled and clashed in his
hand. Azélie passed him, descended the steps, and disappeared across
the yard. He watched until the darkness absorbed her.

Then he went back inside and stood among the things
she had displaced. On the floor lay the little iron
blade she had used upon the shutter. He picked it
up and placed it in his pocket.

The next morning 'Polyte charged the missing supplies to himself.

After that, Azélie did not often need to ask. A
sack of meal went to her family's cabin on Monday.
On Wednesday there was meat, coffee, and a fresh bottle
of oil. When one of her younger brothers appeared barefoot
at the store, 'Polyte gave him shoes. He sent tobacco
to the father and a bolt of yellow cloth to
the mother.

Mr. Mathurin noticed the change in the accounts.

"You have adopted them?" he asked.

"I don' want them robbin' the store."

"Ah, certainly. It is economy."

'Polyte flushed and bent over his ledger.

He discovered errands that took him toward the far end
of the quarters, where Azélie's family occupied a cabin beyond
the cane brake. Their place was no worse than several
others, but to 'Polyte it appeared uniquely disordered. A broken
chair leaned against the wall. Gourds hung from the eaves.
Chickens scratched beneath a ramshackle wagon that had one wheel
propped upon a block. The father, Félix, was often absent
when 'Polyte arrived, though whether in the field, the woods,
or at a neighbor's card table was difficult to learn.

Azélie's mother received every gift without surprise.

"You put it yere," she would say. "Azélie, come take
that."

Azélie would come from behind the cabin or from the
kettle set over the outdoor fire. Sometimes she thanked him.
More often she merely carried the thing inside.

Her indifference sharpened his desire for her company. He questioned
her about Little River.

"W'at's there?"

"Our house."

"This ain't a house?"

"It's Mr. Mathurin's."

"Your place any better?"

"It's ours."

"Your pa got land?"

"A little."

"Flood don' take it?"

"Sometimes."

"Then why go back?"

She looked at him as if the answer had already
been given.

He began waiting for her in the evenings. If she
passed the store, he called her. If she did not
pass, he went to the cabin. He brought ribbons, a
comb, a small bottle of violet scent, and once a
pair of red shoes from town. Azélie accepted the useful
things and left the ribbons folded on the table. She
tried the shoes, found them tight, and gave them to
her sister.

This wounded him more deeply than he admitted. The next
pair was brown, plain, and fitted her perfectly.

His feeling grew without receiving nourishment. It fed upon refusals,
silences, and brief meetings. If Azélie smiled at something her
brother said, 'Polyte remembered the smile all day, though it
had not been meant for him. If she disappeared into
the cotton with the other pickers, the moving white sacks
and sunbonnets tormented him because he could not distinguish hers
among them.

He became alternately generous and severe. One evening he reproached
her for taking sugar from the store on the night
of the break-in.

"You ain't needed sugar."

"Ma wanted it."

"That ain't the same as needin'."

"She wanted coffee, too."

"You don' understand right f'om wrong."

"I know you said take it."

"After I caught you!"

"Yes."

She was sitting on the cabin step, shelling peas into
a basin. Her fingers moved steadily.

"Don' you feel bad when you think of it?"

"No."

"You ought to."

"Why?"

"Because it was wrong."

"You give us all them same things now."

"That's different."

"How?"

He could not explain the difference without confessing what he
wished to conceal. He wanted her to understand that theft
from the store was wrong, while taking gifts from him
was right; that dependence upon the plantation degraded her, while
dependence upon him might elevate them both. The distinction, clear
and honorable in his own mind, found no convincing words.

"You got no moral sense," he said.

Azélie emptied the peas into her apron and rose.

He watched her go inside, furious with her and with
himself.

By September everyone knew.

The women at the quarters laughed when 'Polyte approached. Baptiste,
waking long enough to observe him polish his boots before
visiting the far cabin, asked whether the governor was expected.
Mr. Mathurin said nothing for several weeks. Then, one afternoon
when 'Polyte requested a day in town, he remarked:

"Buy something sensible. A ring is very small for the
price."

'Polyte stared.

"Who say I'm buyin' a ring?"

"No one. But if you do, choose a good one.
These girls know gold."

"She ain't like that."

"Of course not."

Mr. Mathurin's agreement sounded worse than disbelief.

'Polyte bought the ring. It was a thin band set
with a little red stone. He carried it for twelve
days before showing it to Azélie.

By then the cotton stood open under a bleached sky.
The fields were busy from dawn until the light failed.
Wagons creaked toward the gin, piled high with white, while
loose fibers caught upon weeds and fence rails. In the
evenings the air was cooler, and smoke from cooking fires
remained low among the cabins.

He found Azélie alone near the bayou, washing mud from
a pail. Cypress shadows lay long upon the water.

"You goin' away soon?" he asked.

"When we finish."

"How soon?"

"Pa say two weeks."

"You want to go?"

"Yes."

He had rehearsed a speech in which he would describe
the security he offered: a proper house, regular money, clean
clothes, freedom from her father's debts. But at her single
word the speech scattered.

"Stay yere," he said.

She looked up from the pail.

"I can't."

"Why?"

"We goin' home."

"I mean you. You stay."

"By myself?"

"With me."

She dipped the pail again. Water flashed at its rim.

"I got work," 'Polyte went on. "Mr. Mathurin trusts me.
I got money saved. I can get a house. Not
in the quarters. A good house, near the road, with
a gallery."

Azélie scrubbed at a dark mark with a handful of
sand.

"I'll give you anything," he said. "You know that. Since
that night, I give you everything."

"You said you would."

"Yes, I said it. I love you."

The words, spoken at last, seemed both inadequate and shameful.
He repeated them because he had no others.

"I love you, Azélie. I love you."

She looked troubled, not softened.

"I never ask you to."

"No. You don' ask nothing fo' yourself. Only fo' them.
Your pa wants this, your ma wants that. W'at do
you want?"

"To go home."

He took the ring from his pocket and held it
toward her. The red stone caught the declining sun and
showed a bright point of fire.

"You marry me. Then you can stay. You won't have
to break in stores, or beg fo' meat, or live
where the roof leaks. You won't have to follow your
pa all over the country."

Her face changed at the last words.

"I follow Pa home."

"Home! That place floods every spring. You got hardly no
land. He'll have you hungry before Christmas."

"We been hungry before."

"You say that like it don' matter."

"It passes."

He seized her wet hand and tried to press the
ring upon one finger. She drew back.

"Don'."

"Take it."

"I don' want it."

"You take everything else."

"Things fo' the house."

"This is fo' you."

"I don' want it."

Humiliation rose in him with such force that for a
moment he hated her. He wanted to close his hand
upon hers and force the ring over her knuckle. He
wanted to remind her of the open shutter, the stolen
goods, the promise by which she had bound him while
binding herself to nothing. He wanted to call her ungrateful.

Instead he dropped her hand.

"You rather go with that lazy crowd than stay respectable?"

She lifted the pail.

"They my family."

"Your pa uses you. Sends you to ask fo' things
'cause he knows nobody tells you no."

"You told me no."

"And you stole."

"Yes."

Her agreement deprived him of accusation. She started toward the
cabins.

He followed a few steps. "Azélie."

She did not stop.

"I ain't goin' ask again."

"All right."

He stood beside the bayou, the ring held tightly in
his palm. Presently he flung it toward the water. It
struck a cypress root and fell among the reeds instead.
After dark he returned with a lantern and searched until
he found it.

The family left on a clear morning in October.

Their wagon had been patched for the journey with raw
boards. A mule and a small horse stood in the
traces. Bedding, pots, tools, sacks of corn, and three splint
chairs were tied behind. Azélie's mother sat high among the
bundles with the youngest child asleep against her. Félix moved
about giving unnecessary directions while the boys chased a pig
they intended to take with them.

'Polyte watched from the store gallery.

He had not spoken to Azélie since the proposal. During
the last days he had cultivated an air of occupation,
calling loudly for receipts, inspecting cotton weights twice, and correcting
accounts that needed no correction. No one was deceived.

Mr. Mathurin came across the yard, hands clasped behind him.

"They are leaving you some of the plantation," he observed.
"That is considerate."

'Polyte did not answer.

Azélie emerged from the cabin carrying the green tin pail.
She wore the brown shoes he had given her. He
fixed his attention upon that fact as though it contained
a private message.

She placed the pail in the wagon and turned. For
a moment her gaze rested upon the store. 'Polyte descended
one step.

She walked toward him.

His heart beat so violently that he felt anger at
her for causing it.

"We goin' now," she said.

"I see."

"Ma say thank you fo' the cloth."

"All right."

"Pa say he owe Mr. Mathurin."

"He knows."

"He go pay after the spring crop."

'Polyte almost smiled. "All right."

She waited, and he imagined that at last she would
say something concerning himself. But she only extended a key.

It was the small store key he had once given
her after she promised never again to force the shutter.
She had never used it, so far as he knew.

"You keep it," he said.

"We ain't goin' be yere."

"Keep it."

She placed it on the gallery rail.

From the wagon Félix shouted, "Azélie! Come on, fille!"

She turned.

"Azélie," 'Polyte said.

She looked back.

He wanted to tell her again that he loved her.
The words had become worn and powerless, but they were
the only truth he possessed.

"Good-bye," he said.

"Good-bye, 'Polyte."

She climbed into the wagon. Félix cracked the reins. The
wheels groaned, settled into the ruts, and began to turn.
The pig squealed from a crate at the rear. One
of the chairs dragged until a boy jumped down and
retied it.

The wagon passed the cotton shed, then the whitewashed gateposts.
Azélie sat facing backward. Whether she looked at him or
merely at the plantation, 'Polyte could not tell. The road
curved beyond the pecan trees, and the wagon vanished in
dust.

That afternoon he entered three figures in the wrong columns.
The next day he neglected to lock the meat room.
On Monday Mr. Mathurin found him sitting at the desk
with the account book unopened.

"You are ill?" Mr. Mathurin asked.

"No."

"Then perhaps the figures have offended you."

'Polyte rose and walked to the door. The yard appeared
strangely broad. Without the family's broken wagon, their wandering chickens,
and Azélie's blue dress crossing the dust, the plantation had
assumed an order he once believed he valued.

"I can't work yere no mo'," he said.

Mr. Mathurin regarded him for a while.

"You wish to go to town?"

"No."

"Where, then?"

'Polyte took the store key from his ring and laid
it upon the desk. Beside it he placed the smaller
key Azélie had left on the rail.

"Li'le River," he said.

Mr. Mathurin lifted his eyebrows, but did not laugh.

'Polyte packed that evening. Before dawn he crossed the yard
carrying one valise. The shutters of the big house were
closed, and the store stood dark beneath the live oaks.
Behind him the keys remained on the desk, where the
first light would presently find them.

He took the river road north, following the deep, uneven
marks of a ramshackle wagon.

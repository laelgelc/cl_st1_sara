Belle Carpenter had for years lived alone with her father
in a narrow frame house at the east end of
Winesburg. The house stood back from the road behind two
soft-maple trees. One tree was dead at the top, and
in winter its branches scraped against the shingles. An eaves-trough
had come loose over the kitchen window. When rain fell,
water struck a wooden box beneath it and made a
hollow, persistent sound.

Henry Carpenter, Belle's father, was a bookkeeper in the First
National Bank. He was a small man with a gray
face and a thin mouth. In summer and winter he
wore black alpaca coats that hung almost to his knees.
Each night he brushed the coat he had worn that
day and placed it over the back of a chair.
He owned three boards on which he pressed his trousers.
The boards were kept beneath his bed, and he spoke
of them with pride.

"The appearance of a man tells everything," he often said.

Henry had never been promoted at the bank. Younger men
came and were placed above him. He blamed the failure
on his wife, who had died when Belle was twelve,
and afterward he blamed Belle. At supper he watched the
girl eat and found fault with the way she held
her fork. If she laughed, he asked what foolishness had
entered her head. If she remained silent, he accused her
of sulking.

Once, when Belle was seventeen, she came home after walking
with a clerk from the freight office. Henry waited behind
the kitchen door and struck her across the face with
a leather strap. The blow left a dark line from
her ear to her chin. She did not cry. She
took the strap from him and threw it into the
stove. Her father stood trembling before her, and for the
first time she understood that his cruelty came out of
weakness. The knowledge did not make her pity him. It
made her hate him more.

Belle was twenty-three and had a strong, handsome body. Her
hair was black and thick, and her shoulders were broad.
In the millinery shop where she worked, she bent over
hats and pieces of ribbon all day. Women came in
and spoke of weddings, church suppers, and the conduct of
other women. Belle listened and smiled when a smile was
required. Sometimes she looked at herself in the long mirror
between the hat stands. She imagined leaving Winesburg. She imagined
a room of her own in Cleveland or Toledo and
a man who would come to the room at night.
The face in the mirror would then become frightened and
proud.

The man she wanted was Ed Handby.

Ed tended bar in a saloon near the railway tracks.
He was thirty, large through the shoulders, and heavy in
the arms. His hands were so big that a beer
glass seemed a toy in them. His eyes were small
and light-colored, and when he was angry his voice became
quiet.

At eighteen Ed had inherited eleven hundred dollars from an
uncle. For six months he spent money as if it
were water drawn from a well that could not go
dry. He bought suits, hired horses, gambled with traveling men,
and paid for drinks for anyone who stood near him.
One night in Cleveland he threw a chair through a
hotel window because a woman laughed at his shoes. In
another place he fought two policemen and slept three nights
in jail. When the money was gone, he returned to
Winesburg and became a bartender.

People thought him dangerous, and he was dangerous. Yet in
the presence of Belle Carpenter he became confused. He could
not say the simple thing that was in him. He
wanted to ask her to marry him. Instead he caught
her wrist and spoke in threats.

"You keep away from the young fellows," he said. "I
won't have you running about with them."

Belle laughed at him. She knew she could make his
neck redden and his hands open and close. It pleased
her to do so. What pleased her more was the
thought that this powerful man, whom other men feared, had
become helpless before her.

She also despised him. He worked in a saloon and
sometimes smelled of whiskey and sawdust. Henry Carpenter, petty and
beaten though he was, had taught his daughter to believe
in social divisions. A bank clerk might look down upon
a bartender. A milliner might dream of marrying a lawyer
or the owner of a store. Belle had taken her
father's pride into herself, although she hated everything else about
him.

For this reason she would not give herself to Ed,
and because she would not give herself to him, she
could think of little else.

During the autumn she began walking with George Willard, the
young reporter on the Winesburg Eagle. George was younger than
Belle. He had a narrow body, restless hands, and a
way of speaking eagerly of matters he did not understand.
She did not love him. When she kissed him in
dark doorways or beneath trees, she sometimes closed her eyes
and imagined that Ed Handby's arms were around her.

George knew nothing of this. He thought Belle had chosen
him because he was becoming a man.

Among the younger men of Winesburg, George had lately begun
to boast. He spoke loudly in the pool room and
laughed at stories he had once been afraid to hear.
When a traveling salesman told of women in Chicago, George
leaned against the wall and pretended boredom. He had never
been to Chicago. He had not been with a woman.
The pretence itself excited him.

One January evening he remained in the pool room until
nearly ten. Snow had fallen two days before, but the
roads were now packed hard. The room was close and
smelled of tobacco, wet wool, and the kerosene burned in
two hanging lamps. Men stood about the tables. Balls struck
together. Chalk dust lay on the floor.

George had been talking with three boys from the livery
stable. They spoke of a widow who lived beyond the
fairgrounds. One of the boys claimed she had let him
into her house after midnight. The story was a lie,
but no one challenged it.

George put his hat over one eye and staggered across
the room like a drunken man. He struck the wall,
saluted an imaginary officer, and began marching.

"I've seen life," he declared. "There isn't much of it
I haven't seen."

The boys laughed. Encouraged, George seized a billiard cue and
carried it like a rifle. He was a soldier returning
from war. He had killed men and loved women. He
spoke in a deep voice and made his face stern.

Then, without warning, the performance wearied him. The laughter sounded
thin. He set down the cue, buttoned his coat, and
went into the street.

Outside, the cold struck his face. He stood for a
moment beneath the wooden awning. The lamps in the shops
had been extinguished, and a narrow moon hung above the
roofs. The hard snow reflected a pale light. A freight
engine moved in the distance and gave one long cry.

George began walking.

He went first along Main Street, past the dark windows
of the drug store and the hardware store. Before the
bank a patch of ice shone like metal. His own
footsteps made a sharp sound. The silliness of the pool
room fell away from him, and a solemn mood took
its place.

He thought of himself as someone newly born. It seemed
to him that until that night he had been only
a boy who moved his arms, shouted words, and imitated
other men. Now, in the cold and silence, he believed
he could feel the design beneath things.

"Order," he whispered.

The word pleased him. He repeated it.

"Order. Law. Fellowship."

He did not know what the words meant, but as
he spoke them they seemed full of meaning. They rose
inside him and widened the town. The dark houses were
no longer merely houses. The people sleeping within them were
joined together. The banker, the baker, the old woman who
sold eggs, the boy shoveling manure in a barn—each had
a place. Their lives entered one another like the wheels
of a machine.

George turned into the laborers' district near the tracks. Here
the houses were smaller and stood close to sheds and
barns. A cow moved behind a fence and breathed heavily.
From one chimney came the smell of burning corn cobs.
Somewhere a baby cried, and a woman's voice answered. The
voice was tired but tender.

He stopped before a low house. Through a window he
saw a man in red underclothes kneeling beside a stove.
The man put in coal and then stood rubbing his
hands. A woman came into the room carrying a lamp.
Her hair hung down her back. For a moment they
faced each other without speaking. Then she lifted one hand
and touched his cheek.

George's heart beat violently.

He walked on. The night had become beautiful. Sheds, broken
fences, piles of frozen earth, and the dark mouths of
alleys were taken into the beauty. Even the smell of
animals seemed grave and clean. Life was near him. Life
was immense.

"Death," he said.

He liked the heaviness of the word.

"Night. Fear. The sea."

He had seen Lake Erie once and had never seen
the sea. Nevertheless the sea came to him as a
great black surface over which men traveled toward unknown cities.
He imagined himself standing on the deck of a ship.
He imagined storms and women waiting at ports. His body
grew warm beneath his coat.

Then the thought of Belle Carpenter came.

At once all the other people of the town seemed
to withdraw. The man by the stove, the woman with
the lamp, the crying child, the banker, the baker, and
the stable boy disappeared. Belle alone remained. She would see
the change in him. He would go to her and
speak quietly. She would look into his face and understand
that he was no longer a boy who played at
drunkenness in a pool room.

He hurried toward her house.

Earlier that evening Belle had been sitting in the parlor
with a piece of red ribbon in her lap. Her
father was in the dining room pressing his trousers. Through
the open door she could hear him moving the hot
iron back and forth.

"Too much coal was burned today," he called. "You have
no notion of economy."

Belle did not answer.

The lamp beside her smoked. She turned down the wick
and listened to the scraping of the dead maple branches
against the roof. For three nights Ed Handby had not
come near her. She had told herself she was glad,
but as the hours passed she became restless. She wanted
to go into the street and walk past the saloon.
She wanted to look through the window and see him
lifting bottles from the shelf.

There was a movement outside. Belle got up and went
to the window. Ed stood near the gate.

She put on a coat and slipped out through the
kitchen. The loose eaves-trough hung above her head. A piece
of ice dropped from it and broke at her feet.

Ed did not greet her. He took off his hat,
crushed it between his hands, and put it on again.

"I hear you're still going with that newspaper kid," he
said.

Belle looked past him toward the road. "I walk with
anyone I please."

His voice became low. "Don't do it."

"Why?"

"You know why."

"No. Tell me."

Ed shifted his weight. He had come determined to speak
of marriage. All afternoon, while drying glasses, he had formed
the words. He would say that he had saved two
hundred dollars, that he could buy an interest in a
saloon in another town, and that she could come with
him. Now the words were gone.

"I'll hurt him," he said.

Belle laughed, although anger and longing tightened her throat. "You
talk like a fool."

"I mean it."

"Then go away."

Ed looked at her face. A strange gentleness came into
his eyes, but she did not see it. He lifted
his hand as if to touch her and then let
it fall.

"I won't stand for it," he said. "That's all."

He walked off toward the street. At the corner he
stopped in shadow. Belle knew he had stopped. She could
not see him, but she felt his presence.

At that moment George came along the board sidewalk.

He approached quickly, his face shining in the moonlight. When
he saw Belle at the gate, he took it as
a sign. The order of life had brought him to
her, and she had come outside to meet him.

"Come walk," he said.

Belle glanced toward the corner where Ed stood. Excitement went
through her body.

"Yes," she answered.

They went away together. Belle chose the direction and led
George past the last houses and along a lane beside
an abandoned brickworks. She was sure Ed would follow. She
felt him moving behind them in the darkness, although when
she looked back she saw nothing.

George talked. He spoke of the night and of the
new understanding that had come to him. He told Belle
that people lived too much in confusion and fear. A
man must take hold of himself. A man must know
what he wanted.

"I've been asleep," he said. "That is what it was.
I've been asleep all my life, and tonight I woke
up."

Belle barely listened. The road climbed toward a bare hillside
where sumac bushes grew through the snow. She heard George's
voice and, beneath it, imagined another sound—the soft tread of
Ed's boots.

George mistook her silence for awe.

He became bolder. "You can feel it too," he said.
"I know you can."

At the top of the hill they left the road.
A stone quarry lay beyond the slope. Snow filled the
shallow cuts in the earth, and weeds stood black against
the white ground. There was no house nearby.

Belle stopped.

George faced her. The moonlight made her seem older and
stronger than he had remembered. Her dark hair blew across
her cheek. Desire rose in him and mixed with all
the thoughts he had carried through town. Order, night, fear,
manhood, Belle. The words and the body of the woman
became one thing.

He put his hands on her shoulders.

Belle allowed it. She was listening for Ed.

"You knew I would come," George said.

"Yes," she murmured.

"You knew I'd be different."

"Yes."

The answer had nothing to do with him, but it
filled him with triumph. He drew her toward him and
kissed her. Belle's lips opened. Her body leaned against his.
For a few seconds she gave herself to the kiss,
not because she wanted George but because she wanted the
unseen man in the darkness to witness it.

George trembled. He believed that strength had entered his arms.
The woman had surrendered. The night itself had surrendered.

Then Belle turned her head sharply.

A shape came up the hill.

Ed Handby did not hurry. He climbed with his hands
in the pockets of his coat. When he reached them,
he stood looking first at Belle and then at George.

George released the woman and stepped forward.

"What do you want?" he demanded.

Ed took one hand from his pocket.

"You go home," he said.

The quietness of the voice frightened George, and because he
was frightened, he became theatrical. He laughed and spread his
feet.

"You've no right here," he said. "Belle came with me."

Ed looked at Belle. "Did you?"

She lifted her chin. "I go where I like."

The words struck him more deeply than either of the
others knew. His face did not change. He moved toward
George.

The young man raised his fists. He had seen boxers
in traveling shows and knew how a fighter should stand.
He made a quick movement with his left hand.

Ed caught him by the coat and belt. He lifted
him as easily as if lifting a sack of feed
and threw him into the sumac bushes.

George landed on his side. Snow went down his collar.
For a moment he lay astonished. No pain came at
first. What came was disbelief. The orderly law of life
had been broken by two large hands.

He got up and rushed forward.

Ed turned, caught his arm, and flung him down again.
This time George's knee struck a stone. He cried out
and rolled onto his back.

Belle stood watching. Excitement shook her. She wanted Ed to
strike her, kiss her, or carry her away. His power,
which she had pretended to despise, now seemed simple and
complete. Yet she also felt a stab of pity for
George. He looked small in the snow.

"Stop it," she said.

Neither man listened.

George rose a third time. The words he had spoken
in the pool room came back to him. He was
a soldier. He had seen life. He had awakened. The
boys at the livery stable, the men in the pool
room, and Belle herself seemed to be watching. If he
stayed down, he would cease to exist before them.

He lowered his head and ran.

Ed stepped aside, seized the back of his coat, and
sent him crashing into a clump of brush. George's head
struck the frozen ground. A flash of white light filled
his eyes.

For a time he could hear nothing. Then sounds returned
separately: Belle's breathing, the scrape of a boot, a freight
car coupling in the valley.

Ed went to Belle and gripped her arm.

"You're coming with me," he said.

She tried to pull away, but the effort was not
wholehearted. "Let go."

"No."

"You think you own me."

Ed's fingers tightened. There were many things he wanted to
say. He wanted to tell her that he had stood
outside her house on winter nights until his feet became
numb. He wanted to tell her of the money in
the tin box beneath his bed and of the room
above a store he had imagined for them. He wanted
to say he was ashamed of the saloon and of
his own clumsy anger. Nothing came.

"I'll kill any man that touches you," he said.

Belle looked at his face. She understood neither the love
nor the shame in it. She understood only the force
of his hand.

"All right," she whispered.

Ed led her down the hill.

George heard them go. He pushed himself to his knees.
Blood from a cut above his ear ran warm along
his neck. Rage filled him. He staggered after them and
struck Ed between the shoulders.

Ed turned with a weary expression. He took George by
the front of the coat and held him at arm's
length.

"Enough," he said.

George kicked at him.

Ed shook him once and threw him down the slope.
The young man rolled through snow and weeds and stopped
against a stone ledge. He did not rise again.

Ed and Belle walked away. Their figures became two shadows
on the white road and then one shadow. Belle did
not look back.

For several minutes George lay still. The moon floated above
him. Earlier it had seemed a lamp hung over an
immense and meaningful world. Now it was merely cold light.
His hands hurt. One trouser leg was torn, and wet
snow pressed against his skin.

At last he stood. He found his cap near the
bushes and brushed it with his sleeve. The gesture made
him think of his father brushing a coat, of clerks
closing shops, of men performing small acts because they did
not know what else to do.

He started home.

The cut on his head throbbed. More painful was the
thought of Belle walking beside Ed. George imagined them laughing
at him. In truth they did not laugh. Ed walked
in silence, still holding Belle's arm, and Belle stared straight
ahead, disturbed by what she had called forth. But George
could not know this. Their silence became, in his mind,
mockery.

He hated Ed. He hated Belle. Soon he hated himself
more than either of them.

The phrases of the evening returned and mocked him.

Order. Fellowship. The sea. Manhood.

He tried to recover the feeling he had known while
walking through the town. He looked toward the laborers' houses.
The same chimneys smoked. The same sheds and fences stood
under the moon. A cow shifted in a stall. A
man coughed behind a wall.

Nothing opened before George. The houses seemed cramped and dirty.
Ashes had been dumped beside the road. A broken wagon
wheel leaned against a privy. From a ditch came the
sour smell of refuse. The woman who had touched her
husband's cheek was no longer part of a great fellowship.
She was merely a tired woman in a cheap house.

George walked faster.

At one corner he stopped and pressed both hands against
his face. Hatred moved in him like sickness. He wanted
to shout, to strike someone, to return to the hill
and fight again. Instead he began to cry. The tears
embarrassed him even though no one was near.

He entered his father's hotel by the rear door. In
the kitchen a lamp burned low. He washed the blood
from his neck and stared into a small mirror above
the basin. His face was pale. A twig clung to
his hair.

He took it out and held it between his fingers.

For an instant he remembered Belle on the hill, her
body yielding against his own. The memory did not comfort
him. It seemed false, as though it had happened to
another boy who had died in the bushes.

Upstairs he undressed in the dark and got into bed.
The room was cold. He pulled the blankets over his
head, but the hillside remained before him. Again he rushed
forward. Again Ed's hands seized him. Again the earth rose
and struck his skull.

He told himself he had been betrayed. He told himself
that Belle was bad and Ed a brute. He told
himself that next time he would take a club or
a revolver. None of the thoughts lasted.

Beneath them was the knowledge that his awakening had been
made largely of words.

Near midnight a freight train passed through Winesburg. The windows
trembled. George listened until the sound faded over the frozen
fields. He wanted the train to carry him away, but
he did not get up.

Outside, the moon sank toward the roofs. The town remained
what it had been: sheds, stores, kitchens, barns, bedrooms, and
dark streets in which people moved toward one another and
failed to speak. For a little while George had believed
it orderly and vast. Now it seemed mean and commonplace.

He turned his face to the wall.

In the morning he would tell no one where he
had been.

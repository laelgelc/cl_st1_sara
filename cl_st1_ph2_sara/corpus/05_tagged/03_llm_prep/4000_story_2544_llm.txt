### The Siege of the *Lancashire Queen*

Of all the men Charley Le Grant and I took
in our years with the Fish Patrol, two Italians cost
us the most time, the greatest quantity of patience, and
very nearly our reputations. They had no firearms that we
saw, no boat worth ten dollars, and no more sea
room than lay between Oakland Long Wharf and a barnacled
English ship at anchor. Nevertheless, they defied the State of
California for fourteen days, knocked Charley senseless, made a circus
of us before the whole waterfront, and came within one
hundred yards of escaping at the finish.

We caught them because a rich man had imagination enough
to build a boat that nobody needed.

But that comes at the end.

It began one pale morning in the lower bay, when
Charley and I were beating to windward in the patrol
sloop *Reindeer*. She was a twenty-eight-foot centerboard boat, broad in
the beam, sound in her planking, and fast enough when
she had room to stretch herself. But she drew more
water than a skiff, required space in which to come
about, and possessed none of the quickness of a light
boat under oars. Before that day was done we had
reason to curse every one of those limitations.

The wind blew fresh out of the west, and the
bay was chopped into short, white-topped seas. We had been
out since before daylight, watching the shrimp camps and the
flats where illegal nets were most often worked. About eight
o'clock we sighted a Greek lateener dragging near the edge
of the channel.

"Too early in the season for honest men to be
so busy," Charley observed.

The Greek boat carried three men. The instant they saw
us alter course, one of them sprang to the stern
and began hauling in a line. Another cast loose the
peak halyard. These actions interested Charley.

"Keep her full," he said. "I want to see what
they're nursing."

I tended the sheet while he held the tiller. The
*Reindeer* drove through the chop, shouldering the seas aside and
flinging spray over her lee rail. The Greeks tried to
get their net aboard before we reached them, but wet
webbing is heavy, and guilt does not make it lighter.
We came alongside while half of it still trailed astern.

The law prescribed the minimum size of mesh permitted in
such fishing. This one would scarcely admit two fingers.

Charley lifted a section between thumb and forefinger. "Fine enough
to strain soup," he said.

The Greeks protested all together. They had borrowed the net.
They had found it drifting. They had not used it.
They had intended to repair it into a hammock. One
declared that the fish in the bottom of their boat
had jumped there of their own accord.

Charley listened with the gravity of a judge.

"Then the fish can explain it to the court," he
said.

We put one Greek aboard the *Reindeer*, ordered the other
two to follow us, and started for Oakland. It should
have been a simple morning's work. The wind was fair
for the run, the prisoners were peaceable, and our evidence
lay in plain sight.

Then, off the end of the long training wall, we
saw the Italians.

They were in a small Whitehall skiff, pulling easily toward
a cove. Across the stern-sheets lay several objects covered by
an old brown sail. One end of a long pole
stuck out from beneath it. There was also a coil
of heavy line and, unless my eyes deceived me, the
iron glimmer of a sturgeon hook.

Sturgeon gear was prohibited in that district, and the men
knew it as well as we did. They had seen
our patrol flag. For one second they sat without moving.
Then both bent to the oars.

"There!" Charley cried. "Those fellows are making a confession."

We shouted for them to stop.

They did not stop.

The Greek in our cockpit smiled. The two Greeks in
the lateener began talking rapidly and with great cheerfulness. The
sight of the law abandoning one quarry to chase another
appeared to restore their faith in justice.

Charley looked from them to the escaping skiff. "We can't
tow that lateener through a race."

He ordered the Greeks alongside and took their boat in
tow with a short line. It was a bad arrangement
for speed, but the Italians were not more than three
hundred yards away, and the *Reindeer*, with a whole sail
and a strong breeze, could run twice as fast as
they could row.

At least, she could in open water.

Directly ahead lay the *Lancashire Queen*, a large iron English
sailing ship anchored off the wharf. She was waiting for
cargo, and her high black sides rose from the bay
like the wall of a floating fort. Her lower yards
were braced nearly square; boats hung from davits; lines, ladders,
and stages dangled over her side. A few sailors leaned
upon the rail and watched us come.

The Italians pulled straight for her.

"They've cornered themselves," I said.

"They've got the choice of surrendering or climbing the anchor
chain," Charley answered. "Give me the sheet."

He hardened it down, luffed into the wind, and drove
the *Reindeer* toward the ship's bow. We were gaining by
the second. The Greeks' lateener leaped and yawed astern, flinging
water from either bow, but the towline held.

The Italians did not attempt the anchor chain. At the
last possible instant they put the skiff's helm hard over,
hauled on one oar, backed on the other, and spun
under the *Lancashire Queen's* bowsprit.

We could not follow.

To turn as they turned would have left the *Reindeer*
in irons under the ship's forefoot. Charley bore away, shot
past the bow, and ordered me to cast off the
Greek boat. The towline splashed free. The Greeks at once
lowered their sail and lay to, highly entertained.

We rounded the ship's bow. The skiff had vanished.

"Other side," I said.

"I know it!"

Charley brought the *Reindeer* about so sharply that her boom
cracked across and the blocks rattled. We reached along the
Englishman's starboard side. Halfway down it we saw the skiff
shoot around the stern.

The sailors at the rail began to laugh.

We rounded the stern thirty yards behind. The Italians were
already halfway up the port side, pulling with short, fierce
strokes. We gained on the straight run, but as soon
as we came near enough to seize them, they whipped
around the ship's bow again.

Thus began the marine hippodrome.

Around and around the *Lancashire Queen* we went. On each
straight side the *Reindeer* overhauled the skiff. At each bow
or stern the skiff doubled like a hare, while we
swept wide, lost our gain, filled away, and began once
more. The wind was strong enough to give us speed
and awkward enough to rob us of precision. Sometimes the
ship blanketed our sails and left them slatting. Sometimes a
flaw of wind spilled down from her high tops and
caught us aback. Meanwhile the Italians, with nothing to consider
but two oars and a rudder, twisted wherever they pleased.

The English sailors gathered in greater numbers. They perched upon
the rails, leaned from the shrouds, and called advice to
both sides.

"Pull, you little beauties!"

"'Ard alee, copper!"

"Mind 'is yardarm!"

One red-headed sailor acted as announcer.

"Third lap!" he bawled. "The Eye-talians lead by a nose!"

"Wot a circus!" another cried. "Tork about yer marine 'ippodromes!"

Charley's jaw set harder with every circuit. His pride in
the *Reindeer* was personal, and the laughter from above bit
into him.

"I'll catch those men if I have to wear a
groove around this ship," he said.

On our fifth turn he tried a new tactic. Instead
of following around the bow, he put the tiller up,
ran off before the wind, and attempted to meet them
on the opposite side. The Italians saw the plan from
beneath the ship's bowsprit and reversed their course. When we
returned, they were behind us.

On the seventh turn we almost had them. A sudden
flaw left their skiff exposed while the *Reindeer* came roaring
down the port side. I went forward with the grappling
iron. We drew nearer—twenty feet, fifteen, ten. One Italian pulled
wildly while the other glanced over his shoulder.

"Stand by!" Charley shouted.

I cast.

The iron struck across the skiff's gunwale, caught under a
thwart, and held. I snubbed the line around a cleat.
The skiff swung broadside, and both Italians shouted. The sailors
above gave one tremendous cheer.

"Got 'em!" I cried.

Charley left the tiller and sprang forward. At the same
moment the larger Italian snatched up a fish knife and
slashed at the grappling line. The first stroke cut only
the outer strands. The second went halfway through.

I hauled in, drawing the skiff against us. It struck
the *Reindeer's* quarter. Charley leaned over to seize the man.

The smaller Italian rose with an oar in both hands.

"Look out!" I yelled.

He swung it like a club. The blade caught Charley
above the ear with a hollow sound. Charley's hands opened,
his knees folded, and he dropped into the cockpit.

The third slash severed the line.

The skiff sprang free. Before I could leave Charley, it
had darted under the English ship's counter. Ropes came down
from above. The Italians abandoned their oars, tied slings beneath
the skiff, and swarmed up a ladder. Half a dozen
sailors hauled the boat clear of the water.

By the time Charley opened his eyes, fugitives, gear, and
skiff were all on the *Lancashire Queen's* deck.

He stared at me.

"Where are they?"

"Up there."

He felt the swelling above his ear and looked aloft.
The red-headed sailor waved cheerfully.

Charley rose too quickly, sat down again, and swore a
slow and comprehensive oath that included Italians, Englishmen, oars, sailing
ships, and the laws governing leverage.

We came alongside and hailed the captain. After some delay
he appeared at the gangway. He was a heavy man
with gray whiskers and a blue coat, and he regarded
us as though we were peddlers who had interrupted breakfast.

"We are officers of the Fish Patrol," Charley called. "Two
men under arrest have taken refuge aboard your ship. Send
them down."

The captain looked along his deck. "I know of no
men under arrest."

"You saw them come aboard."

"I saw two distressed boatmen rescued by my crew."

"They assaulted an officer."

"Not aboard my vessel, they didn't."

Charley's face reddened. "Captain, I demand those men in the
name of the State of California."

"And I decline in the name of common sense," the
captain answered. "You've no warrant to search a British ship,
and I'll not have my deck turned into a brawling
ground."

The sailors grinned behind him. One of the Italians peered
from a hatch and vanished when I pointed.

Charley would have boarded then and there if I had
not reminded him that the *Lancashire Queen* was under the
British flag. A fight on her deck, followed by the
arrest of two men whom her captain claimed as rescued
guests, might become more than a fishery case. It could
pass from the police courts to the newspapers, from the
newspapers to consuls, and from consuls to men who wrote
long letters bearing seals and ribbons.

The captain understood that as well as we did.

"Come with proper authority," he said, "and you shall have
proper treatment. Until then, keep off my paint."

He turned away.

Charley sat in the cockpit with a wet cloth against
his head. For a minute he said nothing.

Then he looked up at the high black side.

"All right," he called. "Keep them. Feed them well. Give
them feather beds. Because I'm going to live right here
until they come down."

The red-headed sailor leaned over the rail.

"Suppose they don't come down?"

"Then I'll live unnaturally long," Charley replied.

That was the beginning of the siege.

We first disposed of the Greeks, who had waited nearby
in the hope of seeing another act. We delivered them,
their boat, their net, and their miraculous fish to the
proper authorities. Then we returned to the *Lancashire Queen* before
sunset.

The situation was simple to look at and difficult to
solve. The ship lay about four hundred yards from the
nearest mud-flat shore and somewhat farther from the wharf. Between
ship, shore, and wharf there was enough open water to
expose any small boat attempting passage. The Italians could not
swim with their gear, nor could they swim at all
for any distance in that cold water. If they left
by boat, we could see them. If we remained near
the ship, we could intercept them.

But the problem altered when darkness, fog, fatigue, tides, friendly
boats, and the ship's own bulk were included.

Charley reduced it to geometry.

"Call the ship A, the shore B, and the wharf
C," he said, scratching lines on the deck with a
nail. "Put us inside the triangle. If they pull from
A toward B, we cut across. If they start toward
C, same thing. If their friends come from B or
C, we head them off before they join. The only
danger is our letting ourselves be drawn too far from
A."

"And sleep?"

"We take turns."

"And food?"

"We bring it."

"And fog?"

"We listen."

"And if they wait a month?"

He touched the lump on his head. "Then we wait
a month and one day."

During daylight we sailed back and forth under the *Lancashire
Queen's* quarter. At night we anchored where the tide could
not set us out of position. One of us slept
while the other watched. Every unusual creak from the ship,
every splash alongside, every glimmer of a lantern brought the
watcher upright.

The English crew enjoyed the first three days exceedingly. They
sang songs for us, dropped potatoes into our cockpit, and
asked whether we desired a line ashore for mail. The
Italians appeared at mealtimes and smoked over the rail. The
larger man, whose name we later learned was Bartolomeo, would
salute Charley with two fingers. The smaller, Pietro, once brought
out the very oar with which he had struck him
and pretended to inspect its blade.

Charley smiled pleasantly.

"Take care of it," he called. "The State may need
it as evidence."

At night signals began from shore.

A lantern flashed twice from a shed near the mud
flats. A match flared on the ship. Later a rowboat
came out with three men in it, ostensibly to sell
fruit. It moved toward the *Lancashire Queen* until we intercepted
it.

"No trade aboard," Charley said.

"Captain want oranges," one man answered.

"Then the captain may send his steward to Oakland in
daylight."

The rowboat turned away. That night the ship's crew threw
orange peels at us.

On the fifth day a fishing smack sailed between us
and the ship. It came so close that its mainsail
blanketed the *Reindeer*. At the same instant we heard tackle
running aboard the *Lancashire Queen*. Charley shoved the tiller down,
while I leaped for the halyards. For half a minute
we were blind behind the smack's brown sail. When we
broke clear, a ship's boat hung halfway down the Englishman's
side with two figures crouched in it.

We drove straight at them.

The boat went up again so rapidly that one Italian
lost his cap. It fluttered down and landed on the
water beside us. Charley picked it up with the boat
hook and wore it for the rest of the afternoon.

The next attempt was more cunning. Near midnight a skiff
left the wharf and rowed past the *Lancashire Queen* without
approaching her. We followed at a distance. The skiff continued
toward Alameda, and we continued after it. When it had
drawn us nearly half a mile away, I saw a
pale spot move under the English ship's stern.

"Back!" I cried.

Charley had seen it too. We came about and thrashed
to windward. A second boat, carrying the Italians, was slipping
toward the mud flats. Their friends in the first skiff
had pulled us from the ship, using the long side
of the triangle against us.

For ten minutes the matter was close. The fugitives had
less than three hundred yards to go, and the wind
forced us to tack. But the tide was ebbing across
their course. They rowed for the shore and were carried
toward the training wall; we sailed diagonally, gained upon them,
and finally forced them back under the protection of the
ship.

"Nearly," Bartolomeo called as the crew hoisted him aboard.

"Nearly is a word prisoners use," Charley answered.

After that we kept closer.

The weather changed. A drizzling rain came down and softened
everything—the shore, the wharf lights, the ship's yards—until the world
seemed built of wet gray wool. Our clothes never dried.
Coffee tasted of smoke and bilge water. We slept in
scraps of an hour, waking stiff and cross. Even the
English sailors ceased to find us amusing. Their jokes grew
fewer, their songs shorter, and the red-headed announcer merely nodded
when we passed.

The captain remained firm.

On the seventh morning he hailed us. "You cannot maintain
this nonsense forever."

"No," said Charley. "Only longer than you can."

"My ship may remain here six weeks."

"I'm young."

"The men have food."

"So have we."

"They can sleep."

At that Charley looked troubled, but only for a moment.

"Sleep weakens the moral character," he declared.

In truth, sleep came near defeating us. We were two
men against a ship's company. The sailors could watch by
shifts; the Italians could rest all day and choose their
moment. Charley and I divided the night into watches, but
there is no clean division when a man must handle
sail, anchor, tide, and sudden alarms. More than once I
woke to find Charley standing with one hand on the
tiller and his eyes shut. More than once he caught
me nodding.

We tried to obtain relief, but the patrol was short
of men. Salmon poachers were working upriver, shrimp nets required
watching, and our superiors considered two fishermen aboard an anchored
ship a problem already contained.

"Contained!" Charley cried when he read the message. "So is
steam in a boiler. The difficulty is what happens when
it gets out."

He then attempted diplomacy. He wrote a formal demand and
sent it aboard by a launch. The captain returned it
unopened. Charley wrote another, quoting statutes. This came back folded
into a paper boat. He threatened charges for obstructing officers.
The captain sent word that British law did not recognize
threats shouted from cockleshells.

Then Charley tried deceit.

The bay supported hundreds of men who hunted ducks in
flat-bottomed boats, and on misty mornings such craft passed without
much notice. We borrowed an old duck-boat, smeared it with
mud, laid rusty decoys in the bow, and hid Charley
beneath a pile of sacking. I dressed in a ragged
coat, rowed with one oar over the stern, and let
the flood tide carry us toward the *Lancashire Queen*.

Our plan was to drift under her port quarter. Charley
would climb a line, reach the deck before anyone understood
what was happening, and either seize the Italians or force
the captain into a direct act of resistance before witnesses.
It was not a good plan, but after nine days
bad plans acquire a certain beauty.

The mist favored us. I sculled lazily, keeping my face
turned away. From beneath the sacks came Charley's muffled voice.

"Closer."

"We're close enough to count the rivets."

"Closer."

A sailor looked over the rail. I pretended to adjust
a wooden decoy.

We were within fifteen feet when the red-headed sailor appeared
beside him.

He studied the duck-boat. He studied me. Then he looked
at the suspiciously large pile of sacking.

"Fine mornin' for ducks," he called.

I nodded.

"Funny thing," he continued. "Never seen ducks wear boots before."

Charley's heel was sticking out.

A bucketful of refuse descended from above and struck the
sacks. Charley erupted, covered with potato peelings, coffee grounds, and
the remains of an English breakfast. The sailors roared. I
lost control of the sculling oar. The duck-boat swung broadside
and bumped the ship.

Charley sprang for a dangling line, but hands above jerked
it out of reach. Another bucket came down. Then another.

We withdrew under heavy fire.

For the rest of that day the red-headed sailor walked
the deck quacking like a duck.

"I'll prosecute the whole Empire," Charley said.

But the siege continued.

By the twelfth day the affair had become known along
the waterfront. Launches passed to inspect us. Tugboat men shouted
inquiries. Boys on the wharf kept count of escape attempts
and offered wagers. Newspapers printed a paragraph about "The Fish
Patrol's Floating Jail," though no jail existed except the space
between the ship and shore.

Friends of the Italians maintained their signals. A white cloth
on a warehouse roof meant a boat would come after
dark. Two lanterns by the wharf meant wait. A smoky
fire on the flats meant the patrol was thought to
be absent. We learned the code gradually and sometimes answered
it ourselves, thereby causing several useless excursions by men who
thought they were helping.

Yet neither side gained an advantage. The Italians could not
reach land. We could not reach them. The captain could
not sail without cargo, and his cargo did not arrive.
We clung to the *Lancashire Queen* like a leech, and
she sheltered our prisoners like a stone castle.

Then the *Streak* came to Oakland Long Wharf.

I first saw her at noon on the thirteenth day.
She came down the estuary with a noise unlike that
of any launch I knew—a hard, rapid thunder that beat
across the water ahead of her. She was long and
narrow, built of black steel, with a knife-like bow and
three smokestacks raking aft. There seemed scarcely room in her
for machinery, yet machinery filled her with life. Her hull
trembled; smoke streamed behind; and the wake rose in two
white walls that pursued her hungrily.

She passed us at such speed that the *Reindeer* rolled
in the wash.

"What in the name of common decency is that?" Charley
demanded.

A tugboat man shouted the answer. She was an experimental
yacht owned by a manufacturer from the East, fitted with
new water-tube boilers and engines of extraordinary power. Some said
she had six hundred horsepower, some said eight hundred. All
agreed she could make more than thirty miles an hour,
which was twice the speed of the ordinary launches then
on the bay.

The *Streak* ran to the wharf, reversed her engines, and
stopped with a boiling of white water.

Charley watched her for a long time.

Then he began to smile.

It was not a pleasant smile for the Italians.

That afternoon we went ashore for provisions, leaving a patrol
launch to watch the ship. Charley found the owner of
the *Streak*, a thin, quiet man named Harcourt, who listened
while we described our difficulty. He had already heard of
the siege and seemed more interested in proving his yacht
than in enforcing fish laws.

"How far would you need to run?" he asked.

"Half a mile at most," said Charley.

"From cold engines?"

"No. Hot, with steam up."

"What boat have they?"

"A skiff. Two strong rowers."

Harcourt considered. "If they have a two-hundred-yard start, they'll believe
themselves safe."

"That," Charley said, "is exactly what I want them to
believe."

We returned to the *Reindeer* before dusk and resumed our
station. The *Streak* remained at the wharf, black and quiet.
A wisp of smoke rose from one stack, but there
were no signs of haste aboard her.

The fourteenth morning broke clear, with a light northerly breeze
and the water smooth as hammered metal. It was the
worst possible weather for the *Reindeer*. Without wind she could
scarcely move. It was the best possible weather for a
rowboat.

Charley yawned conspicuously whenever the Italians appeared. We sailed farther
from the ship than usual. At noon we anchored nearly
three hundred yards to leeward and began repairing a sail
that did not need repair. I sat with my back
toward the *Lancashire Queen*. Charley lay in the cockpit, his
hat over his face.

For two hours nothing happened.

The tide began to flood toward the shore. On the
wharf, the *Streak's* crew moved casually about her deck. Steam
showed only as a faint shimmer.

At three o'clock a handkerchief flashed from the warehouse roof.

A match answered from the English ship.

I bent over my sewing.

"They're lowering the skiff," I murmured.

Charley did not move. "How many?"

"Two in it. Both Italians. Gear too."

"Good."

The skiff touched water beneath the ship's far side. For
several minutes it remained hidden. Then its bow appeared beyond
the stern.

Bartolomeo and Pietro watched us. We continued with the sail.

They pulled one cautious stroke, then another. The skiff moved
toward the mud-flat shore, not directly, but slanting away from
us. At one hundred yards they paused.

Charley lifted his hat just enough to see.

"Let them come."

They resumed. The tide helped them. The distance between the
skiff and the ship increased to one hundred and fifty
yards, then two hundred.

A boat waited close under the shore, screened by reeds
and broken piles. Their friends intended to receive them there
and scatter inland.

The Italians were now committed, but not completely. If we
raised sail too soon, they could turn back to the
*Lancashire Queen*. If we waited too long, they would reach
shallow water where even the *Streak* could not follow.

"Two hundred and fifty yards," I said.

"Wait."

"Three hundred."

"Wait."

They began to row harder. Perhaps they suspected our indifference;
perhaps freedom merely pulled at them. Their blades dipped together.
The little skiff ran cleanly, leaving twin chains of bubbles.

"Charley—"

"Now."

We dropped the useless sail, went over the side into
our dinghy, and pulled for the wharf.

For one instant the Italians did not understand. Then Bartolomeo
looked from us to the black yacht. He shouted. Both
men swung the skiff around.

But the *Lancashire Queen* was behind them, the shore ahead,
and the *Streak* lay on the third side of the
triangle.

We reached the wharf ladder. Harcourt stood in the yacht's
cockpit.

"Steam?" Charley called.

"Full pressure."

We tumbled aboard. Lines were cast off. Harcourt moved a
lever.

The *Streak* did not seem to start. She leaped.

The bow lifted; the stern dug down; the three stacks
vomited smoke. The deck shook under us with a furious,
ordered vibration. Water split from the knife bow and streamed
aft in solid white sheets. The wharf vanished. The waiting
boats, the piles, the faces of the crowd—all whipped behind
as though jerked away by a cord.

I had known speed under sail: the long rush with
a strong wind, the straining rig, the hiss along the
lee rail. This was different. It had no patience and
no negotiation in it. The engines struck, the propellers bit,
and the yacht hurled herself across the bay.

The Italians saw us coming.

They abandoned all thought of returning to the ship and
drove for the flats. Their oars bent. Pietro's body doubled
at each stroke. Bartolomeo shouted to the men waiting near
shore.

The distance closed with ridiculous quickness.

Two hundred yards became one hundred. One hundred became fifty.
The *Streak's* wake rolled outward behind us, high enough to
swamp a careless boat. Harcourt eased the engines, judging the
interception. We swept across the skiff's course, turned, and came
alongside before the Italians were within one hundred yards of
shallow water.

Charley stood in the bow.

"Stop rowing!"

Bartolomeo tried to dodge around our stern. Harcourt touched the
levers. The *Streak* pivoted under his hand and blocked him.

Pietro raised the oar.

Charley pointed to the old lump above his ear. "Don't,"
he advised. "I remember that implement."

Pietro looked at the black steel side towering over the
skiff, at the churning water astern, and at the crowd
watching from the wharf. He laid down the oar.

We took both men aboard. Their prohibited gear followed—hooks, heavy
line, poles, and all. The skiff we towed. The friends
waiting near shore departed without ceremony.

As we turned back, a cheer came from the wharf.
Another answered from the *Lancashire Queen*. Even the red-headed sailor
waved his cap.

"End of the fourteenth lap!" he bawled. "The coppers win
by an engine!"

The English captain stood at the gangway with his hands
behind his back. His face showed no expression, but when
we passed he gave Charley a small, formal bow. Charley
returned it.

The Italians accepted defeat better than might have been expected.
Bartolomeo examined the yacht's engines with respect. Pietro sat silent
beside his captured oar. They had fought the law with
strength, quickness, friends, a foreign flag, and fourteen days of
patience. There was no disgrace in losing to a machine
that seemed able to outrun its own smoke.

At the wharf, after the prisoners had been handed over,
Harcourt asked Charley what he thought of the *Streak*.

Charley walked once around her. He inspected the knife bow,
the black hull, the three raking stacks, and the great
engines packed into her narrow body. Steam sighed from a
valve. The yacht trembled softly, like an animal not yet
willing to rest.

"She's a wonderful piece of imagination," he said.

"You mean machinery," Harcourt corrected.

"No," said Charley. "Any fool can use machinery after another
man has built it. The important thing is imagining a
boat like this before there's any sensible reason for it
to exist."

He looked toward the *Lancashire Queen*, where the red-headed sailor
was still quacking faintly across the water.

"And," he added, "imagining a sensible use for it when
it does."

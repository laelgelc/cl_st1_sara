### Uncle Peter's Veracity

"Honesty," said Uncle Peter, "is a virtue; but literal truthfulness
is a talent, and a very uncommon one."

He delivered this judgment from the deep leather chair at
the right of the hearth, where the light fell upon
his silver hair and left the rest of him in
a comfortable twilight. Outside, the February wind went down the
street like a creditor trying every door. Within, the red-brick
fireplace gave more glow than heat, and the brass kettle
maintained a small, asthmatic song.

"That sounds immoral," I said.

"It is only accurate. You have been at the university
so long that accuracy naturally sounds immoral to you."

"On the contrary, we were taught to verify everything."

"Precisely. Verification is the last refuge of the incredulous. You
young men no longer believe your uncles."

"We did until we heard them talking."

"Humph! Education has destroyed reverence without supplying manners. But let
us return to the point. An honest man may say
what is not literally true, while a dishonest man may
confine himself rigidly to facts."

"That is a horse with a high saddle," I said.
"Mount him."

Uncle Peter took up the poker and drew together two
black logs which had fallen into a state of mutual
indifference.

"Suppose," he began, "that a man writes the history of
his childhood. He tells you that his mother was an
angel, his father a philosopher, his schoolmaster a tyrant, and
the little girl next door his first and purest love.
Now his mother probably had a temper, his father sometimes
read the newspaper at breakfast, his schoolmaster suffered more from
the boy than the boy from him, and the little
girl was remembered chiefly because, at the age of ten,
she refused to lend him a sled. Yet the man
may be perfectly honest."

"He has an elastic conscience."

"No; he has an elderly memory. Youth dreams in the
future tense; age, in the past participle. Between the two
lies grammar enough to alter any story. Memory is not
a clerk making entries in a ledger. She is an
artist, and an artist with a weakness for improving the
composition."

"In other words, autobiography is fiction."

"Not fiction—at least, not always intentional fiction. It is truth
and poetry in domestic partnership. Autobiography is usually a man's
view of what his biography ought to have been."

"That would explain the remarkable dignity of distinguished memoirs."

"It explains more than that. Consider the accounts which eminent
persons give of their early reading. At six, they are
discovered kneeling over Bunyan; at eight, they shed intelligent tears
upon Homer; at twelve, they form opinions of Tacitus; and
at fifteen, in a lonely garret, they read Shakespeare by
the light of one consecrated candle. There is never a
fishing-rod, a stolen apple, or a yellow-backed tale in the
whole immaculate business."

"You suspect them?"

"I envy them. My own childhood was less nobly furnished."

"But perhaps your memory is doing you a negative injustice.
Exaggeration may diminish as well as enlarge."

Uncle Peter looked at me with approval.

"That is not bad. You may yet survive your education.
Negative exaggeration is common, especially among men who wish to
be thought candid. They boast of faults they never possessed,
because the genuine ones are still too tender to handle."

"Then give me something concrete. What did you really read
when you were a boy?"

The question pleased him, though he pretended to regard it
as an impertinence. He pressed tobacco into his pipe, settled
his shoulders deeper into the chair, and contemplated the fire
as if his childhood were concealed among the coals.

"Before I answer," he said, "we must define the field
of investigation. Do you mean what books passed through my
hands, what books I finished, what books I admired because
I was told to admire them, or what books I
loved?"

"What books you loved."

"Then the answer is easy. Up to the age of
twelve I loved no book half so well as a
trout-stream."

"That is not a book."

"It was printed in characters more legible than many I
have since encountered. There were deep pools for chapters, rapids
for punctuation, and every bend promised a new volume. My
father and I read it together."

The last sentence came quietly. He bent forward and stirred
the ashes, though no stirring was required.

"Was your father a great fisherman?" I asked.

"He was great at being with a boy. That is
rarer. He could tie a fly, mend a rod, cook
bacon over a smoky fire, and lose a large trout
without saying what he would have forbidden me to repeat.
On summer mornings we went beyond the last farms, through
alder and fern, while the dew whitened our boots. No
library ever smelled so sweet as those woods. Say what
you please, the best adventure story is to go somewhere
with a man you trust."

"Yet winter must have driven you indoors."

"Winter and the city—those two librarians—did their work. There were
months when the river was locked, the hills inaccessible, and
adventure reduced to throwing snowballs at boys from another street."

"Were there casualties?"

"Few, but honourable. Our hostilities arose from obscure causes—boundary disputes,
commercial rivalry in the matter of sledding, and insults offered
to ambassadors. A casus belli was always available. War effervesced
among us like gas from a spring."

"And after diplomacy failed?"

"There had never been any diplomacy. That was supplied afterward
by the historians. But when gloves were wet and noses
sufficiently damaged, I went home and read."

Here I reached for the bellows. In doing so I
displaced the tongs, which struck the shovel, which overthrew the
poker, which sent a live coal upon the rug. Uncle
Peter extinguished it with his slipper and regarded me severely.

"You see," he said, "how history is composed. In your
account, no doubt, the fire-irons suddenly fell."

"They did fall."

"Without human agency?"

"I prefer the Stevensonian omission. The event is true; the
responsibility is not emphasized."

"Excellent. You have mastered autobiography. Now put the bellows down
and let a more competent person encourage the flame."

When order had been restored, he continued.

"My first books were fairy tales—not the improved kind in
which the wolf apologizes and establishes a vegetarian restaurant, but
the old, candid stories, full of hunger, enchantment, cruelty, and
justice. Giants were killed, witches burned, youngest sons rewarded, and
princesses married with a promptitude which spared the reader all
later difficulties."

"You believed them?"

"Entirely, and more wisely than I believed many subsequent histories.
A fairy tale tells you at once that it is
impossible, and then proceeds to reveal human nature. A bad
history assures you that every word is true, and then
conceals it."

"Another distinction between honesty and literal truth?"

"Exactly. After fairy tales came pirates, hunters, castaways, red Indians,
shipwrecked families, bold midshipmen, and boys who crossed continents with
a biscuit in one pocket and Providence in the other.
I read them with an appetite unspoiled by criticism. If
a hero was tied to a tree at the end
of one chapter, I did not inquire whether the knot
was nautically correct. I turned the page."

"Were none of these books improving?"

"Some were heavily improved. Good children died with astonishing regularity,
usually after giving their last orange to a less deserving
companion. Missionaries were boiled in remote countries. Roman boys allowed
their hands to be burned off. Scottish martyrs delivered long
speeches in weather that would have silenced a brass band.
I read all of it: religious miscellanies, histories of Greece,
lives of naval commanders, books of travel, and a large
volume about the habits of ants."

"The foundation of a cultivated mind."

"The compost-heap," he corrected. "Foundations are too architectural. A boy's
mind grows by what falls into it: seed, leaf-mould, rain,
an old button, and the occasional dead cat. Culture comes
later and puts up a sign."

"But surely there were great books?"

"Scott arrived when I was ready for him. He opened
doors. Until then, the past had been a corridor hung
with dates; after Scott, people came tramping through it in
boots. Armour clashed, banners blew, abbots plotted, and kings behaved
with the expensive folly proper to their station. I cared
little whether every incident had passed a meticulous investigation. The
world lived. That was enough."

"And Dickens?"

"Dickens came into the house like a travelling theatre. He
brought lamps, fog, coaches, debtors, schoolmasters, angels in bonnets, and
villains with unmistakable noses. His people were larger than life
because, when one is young, life itself is large. I
laughed over him until I was sent from the room,
and cried over him when nobody was present."

"You omit the crying from the official record?"

"I did until now. Take care of it. Candour is
costly and should not be wasted."

He lit his pipe, failed to draw, and discovered that
in the excitement of reminiscence he had neglected the match.

"When did you begin to care about style?" I asked.

"Not when teachers told me to. A schoolmaster can point
to beauty, but the finger often hides the view. I
began to hear style before I knew its name. There
were sentences in Scott that moved like horsemen; sentences in
Dickens that turned corners, carrying lanterns; sentences in the Bible
that sounded like the sea. I returned to them, not
for what happened, but for the happening of the words."

"That is suspiciously like a professor."

"Do not insult me in my own house. I mean
only that language ceased to be a transparent window and
became coloured glass. Through it the world was changed, though
not obscured."

"And poetry?"

"Poetry had been around me from the beginning, disguised as
hymns, nursery rhymes, street cries, and the songs my father
sang badly. Later I met it in books. At first
I liked only the parts with trumpets, storms, battles, ghosts,
and ruined castles. Love poetry seemed to me an extravagant
correspondence concerning persons who might easily have met and settled
the matter."

"Your Platonic passions had not begun?"

"They were frequent but brief. One cannot maintain a Platonic
passion for a girl who has just seen one fall
through the ice."

"Especially if she laughs."

"Especially then. But poetry waited. That is one of its
civilities. A poem which says nothing to you at fourteen
may speak distinctly at forty, though it has not altered
a syllable."

"And Shakespeare?"

"Shakespeare came by pieces. A speech here, a scene there,
a school performance in which Macbeth wore his wrist-watch under
a tartan cloak. I did not at first comprehend the
whole architecture, but the rooms were splendid. There were words
in them which struck sparks from the mind. Later I
discovered the humanity—the kings who are boys, the fools who
are wise, the lovers eloquent because they are foolish, and
the old men foolish because they remember being lovers."

"You admired everything, then?"

"Certainly not. Universal admiration is the politeness of people who
possess no taste of their own. There were celebrated authors
whom I approached with reverence and left with relief. Some
froze me; some preached at me; some appeared to have
mistaken obscurity for depth. There was one poet—his name shall
remain in the humane shelter of omission—whose verses I read
annually for five years because an eminent critic assured me
they were inevitable."

"What happened in the sixth year?"

"I decided they were avoidable."

"You lacked perseverance."

"No, I had achieved self-respect. A man should not pretend
affection for a book any more than for a woman.
Respect, if you please; gratitude where it is due; but
love must choose for itself."

"Yet taste matures."

"It does, and sometimes it merely changes its coat. We
are too anxious to condemn our former selves. The boy
who delights in a desert island is not refuted by
the man who delights in Montaigne. He has travelled, that
is all. Indeed, if the man has travelled properly, he
still keeps an island somewhere."

I asked when his own travelling had reached its decisive
stage.

"At college," he said, "with *Henry Esmond*."

I laughed. "The greatest moment in your intellectual life occurred
through Thackeray?"

"The transition from liking books to loving literature occurred through
that book. Do not make it more solemn than it
was. I found the volume one wet Sunday in a
room where I had gone to escape a theological discussion.
I began it from idleness and finished it in possession."

"In possession of what?"

"A new sense of time, voice, form—of the past not
merely restored, but interpreted. Here was history with a pulse,
irony with tenderness behind it, style so fitted to the
tale that the two seemed born together. The boy in
me still wanted the duel, the campaign, the secret, the
galloping messenger; the man, who had just begun to exist
and was rather conceited about it, heard the sadness beneath
the adventure. For the first time I knew that a
book could entertain one age in us and judge another."

"You still call it the greatest English novel?"

"I do."

"Without qualification?"

"With hundreds. Qualifications are the bodyguard of conviction. But after
they have marched past, *Henry Esmond* remains."

"What stands beside it?"

"Beside it? Nothing stands precisely beside another book. But I
return to *The Heart of Midlothian*, to *David Copperfield*, to
*The Cloister and the Hearth*, to *Pride and Prejudice*, to
*The Vicar of Wakefield*. I return also to books which
would quarrel violently if seated at the same table. That
is the advantage of shelves: they enforce a peace impossible
among authors."

"You have omitted the moderns."

"I have omitted many things. Omission, as you demonstrated with
the poker, is one of the higher literary arts. Besides,
names are dangerous. Mention ten and every listener mourns the
eleventh."

"But this account of your reading—is it true?"

Uncle Peter leaned back and watched a small blue flame
travel along the bark of the upper log.

"It is honest," he said.

"That was not my question."

"It is the answer you are likely to get. No
doubt I have rearranged the shelves. No doubt books which
came late have cast their shadows backward, while some that
once blazed like bonfires are now represented by a little
ash. I may have given my boyhood more sequence than
it possessed. Life arrives as a miscellaneous parcel; memory ties
it with ribbon."

"Then your history is what your childhood ought to have
been?"

"Not altogether. If I wished to improve it, I should
remove the wet gloves, the street battles, the foolish stories,
the neglected masterpieces, and the superior attraction of fishing. But
remove those, and the boy himself disappears. Honesty consists less
in telling everything than in refusing to make oneself too
admirable."

The wind pressed once against the windows and went away.
The fire had at last become clear and steady. Uncle
Peter's pipe glowed in rhythm with his thoughts.

"You have grown fond of rather grave books," I said.
"That is a symptom."

"Of what?"

"Age."

He looked at me over his spectacles.

"My dear boy, when I was young I read of
men escaping from islands. Now I read of men who
cannot escape from themselves. The adventure is more difficult, that
is all."

"So you deny that you are growing old?"

"Absolutely," said Uncle Peter. "I am merely growing up."

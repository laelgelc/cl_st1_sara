# The Illustrious Skyrocket

The Prince of Bellacourt was to marry the Princess of
the Northern Isles, and the whole kingdom had been ordered
to rejoice.

This was not difficult, because rejoicing was a branch of
public employment in Bellacourt, and the citizens performed it with
admirable regularity. The town bells rang from breakfast until luncheon,
the fountains ran with rose-water until the plumbers complained, and
every official received twice his usual salary for the day.
As most of the officials had not received their usual
salary for nine months, the generosity of the Treasury was
universally admired.

The Court Gazette announced that the weather had been specially
arranged by His Majesty. There were three small clouds over
the eastern hills, but they were not mentioned in the
official account, and consequently nobody of good position saw them.

At noon the Princess arrived in a carriage drawn by
six white horses. She wore a cloak of silver velvet
bordered with ermine, and on her hair rested a little
crown of pearls, delicate as drops of frozen moonlight. Her
cheeks were pale from the long journey, but when she
saw the Prince waiting beneath the marble arch she blushed
beautifully.

"She is like an ivory rose that has remembered the
summer," said the Poet Laureate.

He wrote the remark down at once, for poets are
often obliged to keep careful records of their inspiration.

The Prince bowed, kissed the Princess's hand, and declared that
he had counted every hour until her arrival.

"That makes seven hundred and twenty-six hours," observed the Lord
Astronomer, who had counted them too. "Unless Your Highness included
the hour lost when the palace clock stopped."

The Lord Chamberlain frowned at him. Accuracy is excellent in
an almanac, but extremely inconvenient at a wedding.

The ceremony took place in the chapel of blue crystal.
A choir of a hundred children sang from the gallery,
white lilies watched from golden bowls, and two little pages
carried the Princess's train with such solemnity that everyone supposed
it to be much heavier than it was. When the
Prince placed the ring upon her finger, the sun shone
through the painted window and covered them both in rubies,
sapphires and emeralds.

Afterward there was a banquet in the Hall of Mirrors.
The guests ate sugared peacocks, almond castles, silver fish in
green sauce, and a wedding cake so tall that a
special staircase had been built beside it. The King played
the flute between the courses.

The King was very fond of music, though music had
never returned the affection. He played a long composition called
*The Triumph of Harmony*, during which three ambassadors became visibly
unwell and the Princess's little dog hid beneath the table.

"Magnificent!" cried the courtiers when he had finished.

"Your Majesty's highest note was particularly moving," said the Prime
Minister.

It had moved a large chandelier and broken one of
its pendants, so the compliment was perfectly true.

But the chief wonder of the evening was to be
the display of fireworks at midnight. The Royal Pyrotechnician had
worked for seven weeks under a striped pavilion in the
palace garden, assisted by twenty men, four boys and an
undersecretary from the Department of Celebrations, whose duty was to
sign papers proving that everything had been done.

All the fireworks had been arranged upon a high wooden
stand beside the lake. There were Golden Bees, Silver Fountains,
Crimson Comets, Whirling Suns, a Regiment of Crackers, two Roman
Candles, a Catherine Wheel and an enormous Skyrocket with a
long polished stick.

As soon as the workmen had gone, the fireworks began
to talk. There is nothing unusual in fireworks talking. They
are full of powder and therefore naturally have strong opinions.

"What a charming garden," said the Catherine Wheel, looking toward
the clipped yew trees and marble statues. She had once
been attached to a duke's summer festival and had acquired
a taste for landscape. "That moonlight gives me a sensation
of refined sadness."

"The moon is merely a satellite," said the elder Roman
Candle. He was a practical firework and disliked sensations that
could not be measured.

"That is exactly why I pity her," replied the Catherine
Wheel. "To revolve continually around another person must be the
very essence of marriage."

"You should be careful," said a little Blue Squib. "This
is a wedding."

"All the more reason to speak of marriage," answered the
Wheel. "One should always discuss subjects upon which other people
are too happy to listen."

The Crackers laughed loudly.

"Public happiness is the foundation of national prosperity!" cried the
largest Cracker. He had heard the Prime Minister speaking that
morning and had been greatly improved by it. "Prosperity is
the foundation of confidence, confidence is the foundation of credit,
and credit is the art of possessing what belongs to
somebody else."

"A sensible art," said the Golden Bee.

"It is a vulgar art," said the enormous Skyrocket, who
had been silent only because he wished everyone to notice
the silence. He gave a dignified cough.

No one looked at him.

He coughed again, rather more loudly.

The Catherine Wheel continued to admire the moon.

The Skyrocket coughed for the third time, and several sparks
of dust fell from his paper cap.

"I trust," he said, "that I am not interrupting any
conversation of importance."

"Not at all," said the Roman Candle. "We were merely
enjoying ourselves."

"I am glad of that," replied the Skyrocket. "I have
always considered enjoyment a harmless occupation for the lower orders.
Those of us who possess serious emotional natures have graver
duties."

"What duties?" asked the Blue Squib.

"To be understood," said the Skyrocket. "It is the most
exhausting duty in the world, and one I perform continually."

The Catherine Wheel turned one of her painted spokes toward
him.

"Are you to be included in tonight's display?" she asked.

"My dear lady," said the Skyrocket, "the display is to
be included in my appearance. There is a considerable distinction.
Indeed, I have reason to believe that the royal marriage
itself has been arranged in order to provide me with
a suitable audience."

The Crackers giggled.

"You may laugh," continued the Skyrocket, "but laughter is frequently
the tribute paid by ordinary minds to extraordinary statements. The
Prince has waited many years to marry. Why? Because I
was not ready. The Princess has crossed seven seas to
reach this kingdom. Why? Because I am here. The King
has ordered a banquet, illuminated the palace and played the
flute. The flute was unnecessary, but monarchs are allowed certain
weaknesses."

"How very fortunate the kingdom is to possess you," said
the Roman Candle.

"It is," answered the Skyrocket. "Though I should hesitate to
put the matter so plainly myself."

"You have just put it more plainly," observed the Blue
Squib.

"Plain speaking is one of my faults," said the Skyrocket.
"Genius cannot always wrap itself in ceremony."

He shifted slightly on the stand so that the moonlight
struck the gilded stars painted on his case.

"My family," he went on, "is one of the oldest
in the history of pyrotechnics. My father was a celebrated
Rocket of uncommon range. At the summer fête of the
Grand Duchess of Pomerelia he rose so high that two
astronomers mistook him for a new planet. He descended in
a shower of violet fire, and the Grand Duchess immediately
awarded him a medal."

"Could he wear it?" asked the Squib.

"He could have worn it," said the Skyrocket coldly, "had
he survived to receive it. That is the tragedy of
public distinction: the honour generally arrives after the person who
earned it has gone elsewhere."

"And your mother?" asked the Catherine Wheel, who enjoyed family
histories when they ended badly.

"My mother was a Catherine Wheel of singular beauty. She
revolved nineteen times at her first appearance and spelled the
word 'GLORY' in green fire. The last letter was imperfect,
owing to the wind, but the newspapers agreed that the
intention was magnificent. She had a very gold heart."

"Old heart?" said a Cracker.

"Gold," replied the Skyrocket. "I said gold. To misunderstand a
person of refinement is easy, but it is not clever."

"I thought you said old," persisted the Cracker.

"Then you thought badly. Some people suppose that thought becomes
truth if they repeat it. This is why committees have
minutes."

The Roman Candle looked up at the clear sky.

"You should keep yourself dry," he said. "There is a
heavy dew tonight, and a damp firework is of no
use."

"Of no use?" cried the Skyrocket. "What a material expression!
Utility is the last refuge of things without personality. A
watering can is useful. A broom is useful. I have
never heard anyone boast of descending from a broom."

"You may be descended from one on your stick's side,"
whispered the Blue Squib.

The Crackers exploded into laughter, though not in the professional
sense.

The Skyrocket pretended not to hear. Pretending not to hear
is a social accomplishment, while really not hearing is only
a physical defect.

"I am extraordinarily sensitive," he said. "That is what distinguishes
me from objects of ordinary construction. I feel everything—praise, admiration,
expectation, envy. I also feel criticism, though naturally it affects
me only when it is intelligent, and intelligent criticism is
always favourable."

"I prefer to be cheerful," said the Golden Bee. "We
shall soon go up among the stars."

"Cheerfulness is shallow," answered the Skyrocket. "The great emotional spirits
of the world have always been melancholy. At this very
moment, for example, I am considering what might happen to
the young Prince and Princess."

"They may be happy," said the Bee.

"That is possible, but it lacks imagination. Let us suppose
that they have a son, a beautiful little boy with
eyes like mountain violets and hair like spun sunlight. Let
us suppose he grows up, adored by everyone. Then one
afternoon he wanders into the forest to gather hawthorn blossom
for his mother. Night falls. A snowstorm begins. He loses
his way among the black pines. Wolves howl in the
distance. At dawn they find his velvet cap beside a
frozen stream, but the child is never seen again."

"How dreadful!" cried the Catherine Wheel.

"How unnecessary," said the Roman Candle.

"How affecting," whispered the Skyrocket, and a large tear rolled
down his side.

"But none of it has happened," said the Blue Squib.

"That makes it more affecting still. Anyone can be sorry
for what has happened. It requires imagination to be broken-hearted
over what has not. I am fortunate to possess such
sympathy."

A second tear followed the first, and then a third.

"Take care!" called the Roman Candle. "You are soaking your
powder."

"Practical persons are always trying to suppress emotion," said the
Skyrocket, weeping freely. "They would put a roof over the
ocean if they could obtain permission from the Harbour Board.
I shall not harden my heart merely to please you."

So he continued to cry over the lost child who
had never been born, until his painted case was wet,
his fuse was sodden, and even his long stick had
a mournful appearance.

At eleven o'clock the palace orchestra came into the garden.
Lamps shone among the orange trees, the lake wore the
moon like a silver coin, and the marble terraces filled
with ladies in pale silk and gentlemen covered with decorations.
The Princess stood beside the Prince under a canopy of
white roses. She had changed into a gown sewn with
tiny crystals, and whenever she moved it seemed as if
a piece of the winter sky had learned to dance.

"How beautiful everything is," she said.

"It is not as beautiful as you," replied the Prince.

This was neither original nor accurate, but it was the
proper answer, and proper answers have saved more marriages than
wit.

The Royal Pyrotechnician approached the King and bowed.

"Is all prepared?" asked His Majesty.

"All is prepared, Sire."

"Will there be a loud noise?"

"Several, Sire."

"Excellent. Loud noises are understood by everyone."

At midnight a torch was carried to the stand.

The Blue Squib went first.

Fizz! Snap! She darted through the grass in a quick
blue flame, and the little pages clapped their hands.

Then the Golden Bees rose in a humming swarm and
scattered sparks above the lake.

"Look!" cried the Princess. "They are golden flowers flying home
to the sun."

The Bees were delighted. A poetic description is one of
the few rewards performers can enjoy while working.

The Roman Candles sent up crimson stars, one after another,
with perfect discipline.

Boom! Boom! Boom!

The Regiment of Crackers discharged itself in a series of
passionate political statements.

Bang! Pop! Crack! Bang!

"Public confidence!" shouted the largest Cracker, and vanished.

The Catherine Wheel began to turn. Faster and faster she
spun, throwing circles of emerald and amber light across the
lawn.

"This is exquisite misery!" she cried. "I have never been
so beautifully unhappy!"

Then came the Silver Fountains, pouring luminous rain into the
night. The trees flashed white, the statues seemed to wake,
and every window of the palace glittered. Last of all,
the Crimson Comets rushed upward with long tails of fire
and burst above the towers into burning roses.

The court applauded. The Princess smiled, the Prince kissed her
hand, and the King declared that the display was nearly
as fine as his flute-playing. The courtiers answered that nothing
could be as fine as that, and several foreign ambassadors
arranged to leave the kingdom before the next concert.

All the fireworks had performed except the Skyrocket.

The Royal Pyrotechnician applied the torch to his fuse.

Nothing happened.

He tried again.

The fuse gave one damp little sigh and went out.

"A defective piece," said the Pyrotechnician.

The Skyrocket was deeply offended.

"Defective indeed! The fellow has no understanding of artistic suspense."

The workman pressed the torch to him for the third
time, but the wet powder would not catch.

"Leave it," said the supervisor. "The celebration is over."

"Exactly," thought the Skyrocket. "They are reserving me. It would
have been in poor taste for me to appear with
the others. Their performance was pleasant, of course, in a
decorative way, but a genius requires a separate occasion. Perhaps
the coronation will be postponed for me. Or a war
may be arranged. War is extremely effective if one is
sufficiently far from it."

The guests returned to the ballroom, and music floated from
the open windows until dawn. The Skyrocket remained upon the
empty stand beneath the dew.

"I prefer solitude after a public triumph," he said. "It
gives one time to consider how modestly one has behaved."

In the morning two gardeners came to remove the framework.

"Here is that bad rocket," said one.

"Grand Rocket," murmured the Skyrocket. "Even labourers recognise breeding."

"It's thoroughly spoiled," said the other. "Throw it away."

"Royal," interpreted the Skyrocket. "He said I am thoroughly royal.
The accent of the working classes is picturesque but indistinct."

The gardener pulled him from the stand and flung him
over the wall.

The Skyrocket turned twice in the air, struck a branch,
slid down a bank and landed upright in a muddy
ditch.

"This must be a health resort," he said, after recovering
from the shock. "The mud is no doubt medicinal. Persons
of distinction are often sent to uncomfortable places for their
health."

The ditch was shallow and green, with reeds on one
side and a field of yellow buttercups on the other.
A Dragonfly hovered above the water, wearing a blue coat
that shone like enamel.

"Good morning," said the Skyrocket. "You may announce me."

The Dragonfly settled upon a reed.

"To whom?"

"To everyone."

"There is nobody here but a Frog, a Duck and
some water beetles."

"Then announce me to the more intelligent portion."

The Dragonfly looked at him for a moment.

"What are you?"

"What am I?" repeated the Skyrocket. "My dear creature, the
question is not what I am, but how much the
world is capable of knowing about me. Still, as you
are plainly provincial, I will explain. I am a distinguished
public object with a highly developed emotional nature and an
unequalled future."

"You look like a stick wrapped in wet paper," said
the Dragonfly.

"Simplicity of appearance is the final luxury of greatness."

"I see," said the Dragonfly, who did not see and
had no wish to. He flew away.

"A charming listener," observed the Skyrocket. "He was too overcome
to reply."

Presently a large green Frog pushed his head out of
the water.

"Good afternoon!" he called. "What delightful weather for conversation."

"Excellent," said the Skyrocket. "I have long wished to meet
someone capable of listening to me."

"So have I!" cried the Frog. "There is nothing I
enjoy more than a listener. Every evening I sing to
the marsh. My voice has extraordinary carrying power. The farmer's
wife says she can hear me from her bedroom."

"Does she admire your singing?" asked the Skyrocket.

"She throws things from the window, which proves that she
is deeply moved. Indifference never has such good aim."

"There is some truth in that," said the Skyrocket. "My
own career has produced intense feeling in every class of
society."

"I begin at sunset," continued the Frog, "with a low
note—Croak! Then I rise gradually—Croak! Croak! The second croak is
richer than the first, though less philosophical. After midnight I
introduce a passage that has been called unforgettable."

"By whom?"

"By a gentleman who begged me never to repeat it."

"I should like to speak of myself now," said the
Skyrocket.

"Certainly," replied the Frog. "But first I must tell you
about my children. There are six hundred and twelve of
them, unless the heron has called again. Family life is
the soul of the nation. My eldest has a remarkable
tail, my youngest has no tail at all, and the
others occupy every stage between these two distinctions."

The Frog talked for nearly half an hour. Then he
remembered an appointment among the rushes and plunged into the
water.

"What a rude person!" exclaimed the Skyrocket. "He monopolised the
conversation and departed just as I was about to begin.
Self-centred people are intolerable. They have no sympathy with those
of us who wish to discuss ourselves."

A White Duck came waddling down the bank. She had
bright orange feet and a calm domestic eye.

"Quack," she said. "Are you edible?"

"No," replied the Skyrocket. "I am celebrated."

"That is less useful."

"Usefulness again! Why does everyone in the country speak of
usefulness? It must be the result of bad schools."

"I have never attended school," said the Duck. "I learned
everything necessary from my mother: how to swim, how to
find snails, how to avoid boys, and how to sit
on eggs without making speeches."

"A narrow education," said the Skyrocket. "I belong to public
life."

"What do you do?"

"I rise."

"So does bread."

"I rise into the heavens."

"Smoke does that."

"I burst into stars."

"Does it feed anybody?"

"Madam," said the Skyrocket, "the higher things do not feed
people."

"Then people must eat before attending to them," said the
Duck. "Have you ever laid an egg?"

"Certainly not."

"Built a nest?"

"No."

"Warned anyone of a fox?"

"I do not associate with foxes."

"Then I cannot see what you are for."

"The greatest purposes are invisible," answered the Skyrocket. "That is
why no one can prove they exist."

The Duck considered this.

"If I were you," she said at last, "I should
try to become useful."

"If you were I," replied the Skyrocket, "the strain of
the promotion would destroy you."

The Duck waddled away toward the farmyard.

"She has a thoroughly domestic mind," said the Skyrocket. "Domesticity
is merely ambition wearing an apron."

All day he stood in the ditch. The sun came
out and warmed his paper case, though the mud remained
cold around his stick. Now and then a beetle crawled
over him, and each time he supposed it was a
deputation.

At sunset the sky became a great field of apricot
and rose. Swallows flew above the meadow, the castle windows
burned in the distance, and from the palace came the
faint music of another banquet.

"They are discussing my absence," said the Skyrocket. "Nothing increases
a person's importance like not being present. Presence gives people
facts, but absence allows them imagination."

During the night he grew somewhat drier. The next morning
was hot, and by noon his powder had almost recovered
from his tears.

Two boys came along the lane carrying a bundle of
sticks and a black kettle. They were the sons of
a woodcutter and had spent the morning gathering fallen branches
in the forest.

"Let us have our dinner by the ditch," said the
elder boy. "There is shelter from the wind."

They made a little ring of stones, filled the kettle
from the stream, and arranged twigs beneath it.

The younger boy noticed the Skyrocket.

"Look at this long bit of wood."

"It will help the fire," said his brother.

"Help the fire!" thought the Skyrocket. "At last, intelligent officials.
They are clearly preparing my grand appearance. These boys are
probably princes in disguise. Princes in disguise are extremely common
in rural districts, though one seldom meets them at court."

The boys pulled him from the mud and laid him
across the sticks.

"This position is informal," he said to himself, "but true
genius is at home everywhere."

They covered him with dry leaves and set the leaves
alight.

"Now," said the elder boy, "we must wait for the
water to boil."

Waiting made them sleepy. They lay down beneath a willow,
and within a few minutes both were fast asleep.

The little fire crackled. The leaves curled into red claws,
the twigs glowed, and a thin flame touched the Skyrocket's
paper coat.

"Ah!" he said. "The appointed hour has come."

The flame crept along his side.

"I shall rise higher than the palace towers."

It reached his fuse.

"Higher than the mountains."

The fuse began to hiss.

"Higher than the moon."

Smoke wound about him.

"Higher than the stars."

The fire entered his powder.

"Higher than history itself!"

Whizz!

The Skyrocket shot out of the fire, passed through the
willow branches, knocked three leaves from a bough and flew
above the meadow trailing a wavering line of sparks.

"I knew it!" he cried. "The whole world is watching."

The boys slept beneath the tree.

The farmer was repairing a gate on the other side
of the hill and had his back turned.

At the palace the King was playing the flute, so
nobody could have heard anything else even if they had
wished to.

Up went the Skyrocket, not quite as high as the
castle roof, but much higher than the ditch, which was
enough to confuse him.

"I am passing the sun!" he shouted, though the sun
was several million miles away and did not notice.

Then his powder burst.

Boom!

A handful of pale sparks opened in the daylight, trembled
for an instant and disappeared. They were rather pretty, but
the sky was too bright to display them, and no
eye saw them.

The Skyrocket, however, was enraptured.

"What a sensation!" he exclaimed. "Kings will resign, poets will
become silent, and newspapers will issue special editions. Centuries from
now people will divide history into the age before my
explosion and the age after it."

His empty case fell into the field, while his long
blackened stick spun downward over the hedge.

In the farmyard a grey Goose was walking solemnly toward
a puddle. She was thinking about nothing, which is a
rare form of wisdom.

The stick landed sharply upon her back.

"Honk!" cried the Goose, flapping her wings. "The sky is
falling!"

The other geese rushed about in alarm, the hens scattered,
and a sleepy dog barked once from beneath a cart.

The Skyrocket heard the commotion as the last thread of
fire faded inside him.

"I have moved the entire world," he whispered. "I always
knew I should create a tremendous sensation."

And, perfectly satisfied, he went out.

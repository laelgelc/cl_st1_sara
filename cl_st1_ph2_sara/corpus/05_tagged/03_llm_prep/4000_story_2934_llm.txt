### THE GRATMAR AFFAIR

I have delayed for nine years the publication of the
following particulars, not from any solicitude for the living—who have
shown themselves abundantly competent to protect their own reputations—but because
the dead, being unable to contradict us, are entitled to
some consideration. Whether Lieutenant-Colonel Venn belongs properly to that defenceless
class is certain enough. Concerning Private Gratmar I preserve a
less absolute conviction.

Gratmar belonged to the Third Frontier Regiment, in which I
then served as assistant surgeon. He was a tall, pale
young man, proud as an iris and tremulous as a
moth; one of those natures which mistake acerbity for injustice
and injustice for the end of the world. He read
poetry, avoided cards, and possessed the regrettable habit of looking
directly into the eyes of his superiors. This last infirmity
commended him particularly to Lieutenant-Colonel Venn.

Venn was short, broad, red, and constructed for authority rather
than ornament. He had fought like a mastiff in two
campaigns, and in peace preserved the military virtues by waging
war upon his subordinates. Gratmar's refinement irritated him as silk
irritates a man with cracked fingers. He punished the youth
for slowness, insolence, untidiness, melancholy, and once, I believe, for
smiling without permission.

"You will cure me of him," Gratmar said to me
one evening.

"I am a surgeon, not a bishop," I replied.

He had just returned from parade with a cheek swollen
by Venn's glove. His face was white, but his eyes
had that peculiar fixed brilliance which sometimes precedes fever, poetry,
or crime.

"He says I shall become a deserter, a suicide, or
a murderer."

"The Colonel has at least allowed you a choice."

"I shall choose two."

He spoke so quietly that I laughed. This appeared to
wound him more profoundly than the blow.

"You think death ends a man," he said. "It only
removes his restraints. If I kill myself, I can reach
him where sentries and walls cannot prevent me."

"My dear Gratmar, if the dead retain their resentments, eternity
must be a noisy barrack-room."

He looked at me for several seconds.

"Tell him," he said, "that when my body is scattered,
I shall stand at his shoulder."

On the following afternoon an explosion shook the eastern quarry,
where the regiment stored blasting powder for road construction. We
found a crater, a scorched tunic-button, part of a boot,
three fragments of bone, and organic remnants whose want of
coherence rendered identification a matter of sentiment. Gratmar had been
seen walking toward the quarry with a cartridge of dynamite.
He was not seen afterward. The regimental inquiry, being presided
over by gentlemen of sound administrative instinct, concluded that he
had destroyed himself; and the fragments, collected in a biscuit-tin,
were buried beneath a cross bearing his name.

Venn attended the burial. When the chaplain said, "Dust to
dust," the Colonel glanced behind him.

That movement, trifling in itself, interested me.

During the next fortnight he altered. His complexion, commonly suggestive
of boiled meat, became gray and moist; he started at
footfalls, turned abruptly in empty passages, and twice drew his
revolver upon curtains moved by the evening wind. A corporal
reported hearing him dispute with someone in his room, though
no visitor had entered.

"Stand where I can see you!" Venn had shouted.

After a pause he added, in a lower voice, "You
have no right to that face."

When I questioned him, he denied the circumstance with considerable
profanity. Yet he asked whether a man annihilated by dynamite
could remain conscious.

"It would depend," I said, "upon what you mean by
a man."

He wiped his forehead.

"You medical fellows are damned evasive."

"It is how we preserve confidence."

Three nights later he sent for me. I found him
seated in bed, his pistol across his knees and every
lamp lighted. The room smelled of hot oil, brandy, and
fear.

"He comes when I sleep," he said. "No—before I sleep.
I hear him breathing at the door. Then he is
beside me. He has no face, only pieces of one,
but I know him."

"A nightmare."

"Nightmares do not whisper."

"What does he whisper?"

Venn's mouth worked before sound emerged.

"'Soon.'"

I prescribed bromide, which was useless but respectable, and recommended
leave, which he refused because flight would have constituted an
admission to a private soldier, living or dead. Pride can
make a courageous man ridiculous; in Venn it achieved the
converse transformation, making a ridiculous man almost courageous.

At this period there resided near the south gate a
widow named Madame Korba, who earned a modest livelihood by
translating the silence of eternity for officers' wives. I had
attended her during an attack of asthma and found her,
apart from her profession, reasonably honest. In the interest of
observation, I mentioned to her that Colonel Venn might call,
that he was troubled by the death of a soldier,
and that the name Gratmar had acquired an unhappy significance.
I did not instruct her what to say. I merely
supplied those particulars without which inspiration is so often tongue-tied.

Venn visited her on the twenty-third of October. As he
had concealed his intention from me, I learned of it
by the simple means of watching his quarters. This may
appear an excess of curiosity; but extraordinary diseases demand extraordinary
vigilance, and fear, when permitted to mature, is among the
most illuminating of diseases.

Madame Korba received him at nine. I entered her house
by the kitchen shortly before eight, with her consent, and
occupied a closet adjoining the room in which she practised
immortality. Through a crack in the panelling I saw Venn
sit before a circular table. He wore civilian clothes, carried
his revolver, and had buttoned his coat incorrectly.

The medium lowered the lamps and placed her hands upon
the table. For several minutes nothing occurred except the familiar
groaning of furniture subjected to spiritual influence. Then three knocks
sounded beneath the table.

"Who is there?" Madame Korba whispered.

The knocks were repeated.

Venn leaned forward. "Ask its name."

She did so.

A faint voice, apparently proceeding from the curtains, breathed, "Gratmar."

I confess the effect was admirable. Venn sprang up, overturning
his chair.

"You are dead!"

"Not dead," said the voice. "Released."

Here Madame Korba began to tremble. That detail was not,
as I first supposed, artistic. Her eyes had fixed upon
the dark window behind Venn, and her face had undergone
the disagreeable relaxation of one who sees an expectation become
visible.

"Ask him what he wants," said Venn.

She did not answer.

"Ask him!"

The curtains stirred, though the window was shut.

The voice spoke again, clearer and very near:

"Before midnight—your neck—my hands—"

Madame Korba screamed. "Go! Go away! There is someone behind
you!"

Venn fired into the curtain, rushed from the room, and
left the front door open. I followed by the kitchen,
pausing only long enough to ask the medium what she
had seen.

"A white face," she gasped. "At the glass—then gone. I
did not arrange that. I swear it!"

Her distinction between arranged and unarranged phenomena was professionally delicate.
I had no time to praise it.

Venn's carriage was waiting in the lane. He ordered the
driver to take him to the garrison and departed at
a dangerous speed. My own horse stood in a stable
nearby—a coincidence which the censorious have called preparation—and I mounted
at once. I did not wish to interfere; intervention destroys
so many valuable conclusions. I wished merely to observe.

The road to the garrison crossed three miles of pine
forest. The night was moonless, but the carriage lamps moved
ahead like two yellow eyes, vanishing and reappearing among the
trunks. I followed at sufficient distance to escape notice. Once
I heard Venn shout to the driver; once there came
from the carriage a sound like laughter, though it may
have been a wheel striking stone.

Near the old powder bridge the lamps halted.

When I came up, the driver was standing in the
road, calling to his passenger. He had felt the carriage
sway violently, he said, and had heard a scuffle within;
then silence. The door hung open.

Venn lay half upon the floor, with his head twisted
backward at an angle which deprived him of all military
dignity. His eyes protruded, his tongue was caught between his
teeth, and the collar of his coat had been torn
away. Across his throat were dark marks resembling the impressions
of fingers.

The opposite door was closed. The road behind us was
empty. On either side rose dense pines, wet with mist.

The driver crossed himself.

"It was the dead soldier."

"Possibly," I said.

I examined the body. The neck was broken. On the
seat lay Venn's revolver; one chamber had been discharged at
Madame Korba's, and the others remained loaded. A little black
earth soiled the cushion. Near the open door I found
a pale thread, perhaps wool, perhaps cobweb, and placed it
in my pocket. I afterwards lost it, which was unfortunate.

At the inquest I stated the medical facts and nothing
beyond them. The driver supplied the supernatural interpretation, Madame Korba
confirmed it with hysterical generosity, and the regiment remembered Gratmar's
threat. Thus public opinion, which dislikes an unfinished narrative, completed
the case. Venn was buried; Gratmar's cross was garlanded by
anonymous hands; and the officers ceased striking sensitive soldiers for
nearly a month.

I retained doubts, though I cannot say they distressed me.
It is not often that one may observe a murder
whose agent has no legal existence. The elegance of the
thing compensated, in some measure, for its brutality.

A year passed. I had been transferred to the capital
when I received a letter bearing a Swedish postmark. It
contained no salutation.

The writer asked whether we had ever proved that the
remains in the quarry were human. A sheep, he observed,
has bones; a pig, flesh; a condemned military tunic, buttons.
Dynamite possesses the useful quality of confusing distinctions. Was it
impossible that Gratmar had placed such materials beside the charge
and concealed himself before the explosion?

The letter next considered Madame Korba. A medium, it said,
might be bribed or persuaded; alternatively, she might believe herself
an accomplice in a harmless terror while another person stood
outside her window. A voice in darkness requires no spirit,
only breath and opportunity.

As for the carriage, the writer suggested that a living
man could cling beneath it, enter while it slowed upon
the forest road, break Venn's neck, and escape among the
trees. Terror would have weakened the victim; expectation would have
completed what hands began.

These propositions were expressed not as confession but as intellectual
exercise. The writer even reproached me for having overlooked them,
though he complimented my discretion at the inquest. One sentence,
which I remember exactly, ran: "You were nearer to the
truth that night than you appeared desirous of becoming."

That was unjust. I have always desired truth, though not
invariably its arrest.

The letter concluded: "A ghost is only a man whom
society has been persuaded not to look for."

It was signed,

**RAMTARG**

I have shown the signature to cryptographers, theologians, police officers,
and one celebrated professor of northern languages. All found it
curious; none could explain why the word affects me with
the familiarity of a name seen backward in a mirror.

Sometimes, before sleep, I almost discover its purport. Then the
letters rearrange themselves in my mind, the quarry bursts again
into flame, and a pale young soldier stands at my
shoulder—alive, dead, or released—waiting with infinite patience for me to
turn around.

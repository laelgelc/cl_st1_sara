The basement had one window, high in the wall and
painted black.

Danny sat beneath it at a card table. Across from
him sat Tigo.

They wore different jackets. Danny's was blue satin with a
white crown stitched between the shoulders. Tigo's was black, with
a red cobra coiled across the back. In the streets
above, those two patches were enough to start a fight.
Down here, they were supposed to end one.

The revolver lay between them.

It was a Smith & Wesson .38, blued steel, four-inch
barrel, walnut grips worn pale along the edges. The cylinder
was open. Six empty chambers showed in a circle.

Beside the gun stood a cardboard box of cartridges.

Tigo looked at the box. Then at Danny.

"You ever done this?"

Danny leaned back. "What you think?"

"I think I asked you."

"Maybe I done it twenty times."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah."

Tigo nodded slowly. "Then you must be real lucky."

Danny did not answer.

Footsteps crossed the floor overhead. Their clubs were up there—the
Royal Crowns on one side of the old grocery store,
the Cobras on the other. They had agreed there would
be no fighting until the game was finished. One boy
from each club. One gun. First live shot settled the
dispute.

Nobody had said what the dispute was worth.

A corner, Danny supposed. The right to stand outside the
candy store. Maybe the right to call the other club
chicken.

Tigo picked up one cartridge.

"You scared?" he asked.

"No."

"I ain't neither."

"That's nice."

The cartridge was brass, with a blunt gray nose. Tigo
rolled it between his fingers.

Danny watched his hand. It was a thin hand. The
nails were bitten down. There was a black smear of
grease along the thumb.

"You work someplace?" Danny asked.

Tigo glanced up. "Garage."

"That grease?"

"Yeah."

"You a mechanic?"

"Tryin' to be."

"Thought all you Cobras were professional bums."

Tigo smiled without showing his teeth. "Thought all you Crowns
were dead."

The smile went away.

Tigo slid the cartridge into one chamber and snapped the
cylinder shut. He put the gun flat on the table
and spun it. The barrel turned past Danny, past Tigo,
past Danny again. It stopped pointing toward the wall.

"Who goes first?" Tigo asked.

Danny shrugged. "Your piece."

"Ain't my piece. Cobra piece."

"Same thing."

Tigo's eyes hardened. "No, it ain't."

For a moment, the jackets spoke again. Crown. Cobra. Enemy.

Danny reached for the gun.

"I'll go."

He lifted it. The revolver weighed more than he expected.
Cold metal settled into his palm. He had held guns
before, but never one like this, never with a cartridge
hidden somewhere inside.

He put the muzzle against his right temple.

Tigo watched him.

Danny's finger touched the trigger. He could hear a pipe
dripping behind him. He could hear shoes shifting overhead. He
could hear his own blood.

"Go on," Tigo said.

Danny pulled.

Click.

His hand dropped so fast the barrel struck the edge
of the table.

Tigo let out his breath. "Man, you near busted the
table."

Danny laughed. The sound was dry and ugly. "You worried
about the furniture?"

"This is a classy joint."

"Your turn."

Tigo took the gun. He spun the cylinder with his
thumb. It made a smooth metallic whir. He snapped his
wrist, shut his eyes, and pressed the muzzle beneath his
jaw.

Danny saw his throat move.

Click.

Tigo opened his eyes.

"Classy," Danny said.

Tigo laid the revolver down. "Nothing to it."

"Sure."

"Nothing."

Neither reached for it.

Upstairs somebody laughed. Another voice told him to shut up.

Tigo looked toward the ceiling. "They having a good time."

"They ain't down here."

"They will be if we don't finish."

Danny picked up the gun and opened the cylinder. The
single cartridge stared at him from its chamber.

"We supposed to add another now?"

"That's what they said."

"Who made these rules?"

"Presidents."

"Presidents don't play, though."

"They got responsibilities."

Danny looked at Tigo.

Tigo grinned. This time Danny grinned back.

Danny took a second cartridge from the box and pushed
it into the cylinder. Two rounds. Four empty chambers.

He spun and closed it.

"You got a girl?" Tigo asked.

"What's that got to do with anything?"

"Just asking."

"Yeah."

"What's her name?"

"Rita."

Tigo raised his eyebrows. "Short girl? Curly hair?"

"You know her?"

"I seen her around."

"You been looking?"

"Man, I got my own chick."

"What's her name?"

"Marisol."

Danny knew the name. Everybody knew Marisol. She laughed loud
and wore silver hoops that touched her jaw.

"She too good for you," Danny said.

"That's what my old lady says."

"Your mother knows her?"

"My old lady knows everybody. I come home five minutes
late, she knows what street I was on and who
sneezed while I was there."

Danny laughed. "Mine waits behind the door."

"With what?"

"Whatever she got. Shoe, broom, frying pan."

"Frying pan?"

"Once."

"She hit you?"

"She missed. Put a dent in the wall."

They both laughed now, quietly at first, then harder. Danny
pictured Tigo sneaking past his mother and being caught by
some woman with a frying pan. He did not picture
a Cobra. He pictured a skinny kid ducking under a
swinging arm.

The revolver remained in Danny's hand.

Tigo noticed it first. His laughter stopped.

"Go ahead," he said.

Danny raised the gun.

Two bullets.

He placed the muzzle against his temple. Rita's face came
to him, then his mother's, then the blue jacket hanging
from a nail beside his bed. He wondered which one
they would bring home.

He pulled the trigger.

Click.

For a second he could not move.

Tigo reached across and gently pushed the barrel away from
Danny's head.

"You all right?"

"Sure."

"You look white."

"I'm always white."

"I mean whiter."

Danny put the revolver down. "Play."

Tigo spun the cylinder. He stared at the gun while
it turned, as if he might follow the loaded chambers
through the steel. Then he snatched it up.

"Marisol wants to go boating," he said.

"Boating?"

"Yeah. Rowboats. At the lake."

"That's kid stuff."

"You scared of water?"

"No."

"You can't swim."

"I can swim."

"Like a rock, maybe."

Tigo pressed the muzzle to his forehead. "Sunday," he said.
"She wants to go Sunday."

"Then take her."

"She wants another couple. Says it's more fun."

Danny looked at him. "You trying to ask me out?"

"Shut up."

"Real romantic."

"Shut up, man."

Tigo pulled the trigger.

Click.

He froze, teeth bared. Then he slammed the gun onto
the table and bent forward, both hands over his face.

Danny waited.

Tigo's shoulders shook once. When he looked up, he was
smiling, but there was water in his eyes.

"Nothing," he said.

"Sure."

"Just the noise."

"There wasn't no noise."

"You know what I mean."

Danny knew.

They sat in silence. The room smelled of damp cement,
dust, and gun oil. A bare bulb hung above the
table, making a white circle between them. Outside that circle,
crates and broken chairs crowded the walls.

Tigo opened the cylinder.

"Three?" he asked.

Danny studied him. "That's the rule."

"Yeah."

"Unless you want to quit."

Tigo's eyes went flat. "You want to quit?"

"I asked you."

"And I'm asking you."

The jackets pulled tight around their shoulders. Blue crown. Red
cobra.

Danny took another cartridge and set it upright on the
table.

"Maybe the rules are stupid," he said.

Tigo looked at the cartridge. "Maybe."

"Maybe the whole beef is stupid."

"You saying the Cobras are right?"

"I'm saying I don't even know what started it."

"Duke says one of your guys jumped Papo."

"Benny says Papo jumped him."

"Papo's got six stitches."

"Benny lost a tooth."

Tigo rubbed the grease mark on his thumb. "You know
Papo?"

"No."

"I don't know him much neither."

"You know Benny?"

"I seen him twice."

Tigo gave a little laugh. "So Papo and Benny got
a fight, and we're down here."

"Looks that way."

"Man."

Danny rolled the cartridge toward him. It crossed the table
and tapped his hand.

Tigo loaded it.

Three bullets. Three empty chambers.

He spun the cylinder and passed the gun to Danny.

"Why'd you join?" he asked.

"The Crowns?"

"No, the Army."

Danny looked at him sharply.

Tigo smiled. "Yeah, the Crowns."

Danny turned the gun in his hand. "Guys I grew
up with were in."

"That all?"

"You need more?"

"I joined the Cobras 'cause my brother was one."

"He still in?"

"He's in the Army for real."

"Where?"

"Germany. Says it's cold and everybody drinks beer."

"Sounds all right."

"He says don't join nothing where people yell at you."

Danny smiled.

"What?"

"Little late with that advice."

"Yeah."

Danny spun the cylinder again, though Tigo already had. The
chambers whirred. He wondered if spinning twice changed anything. It
did not. Nothing changed the number. Half full, half empty.

He lifted the gun.

"What does your father say?" Tigo asked.

"Ain't got one."

"Dead?"

"Gone."

"Oh."

"What about yours?"

"Got one. Same as not having one. He sits in
front of the television, tells me I'm no good. Then
he asks for a beer."

Danny nodded. "My mother tells me I'm gonna end up
dead."

"Mine says jail."

"They ought to get together."

"Yeah. They could plan our futures."

The muzzle touched Danny's skin.

He watched Tigo's face.

Tigo was not smiling now. "You don't got to," he
said.

"Neither do you."

"Then put it down."

Danny's finger tightened.

Click.

The empty sound struck harder than before.

He lowered the weapon carefully and laid it between them.
His palm was wet.

Tigo stared at the revolver. "Three times."

"Yeah."

"Three empty."

"Yeah."

"Odds don't remember."

"What?"

"My math teacher says that. Like if you flip a
coin and get heads ten times, next flip is still
half and half."

"You listen in school?"

"Sometimes."

"You don't look like it."

"You look like you ate the school."

Danny frowned. "What's that mean?"

"You're a big turkey."

"I ain't fat."

"You're fat."

"I got shoulders."

"You got shoulders in front, too."

Danny started laughing. Tigo joined him. The laugh loosened something
in the room. For several seconds there was no gun,
no ceiling full of gang members, no jackets.

There were only two boys laughing at a card table.

Then Tigo picked up the revolver.

He spun it once.

"Suppose we stop?" he said.

Danny's laugh faded. "They'll call us chicken."

"Both clubs?"

"Both."

"They might jump us."

"They might."

Tigo nodded. "Cobras would do worse than the Crowns."

"Crowns ain't polite."

"You think they'd kick you out?"

"Probably."

"You care?"

Danny looked toward the blackened window. Somewhere outside, cars passed.
Somewhere farther away was the lake, flat and silver under
the afternoon sky. He had been there once as a
child. His father had rented a rowboat and let Danny
hold an oar. That was before he left.

"I don't know," he said.

Tigo kept the gun pointed at the floor. "I been
thinking about leaving."

"The Cobras?"

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Same old noise. Stand here. Go there. Hit this guy.
Don't talk to that guy. Like they own you."

Danny understood that. He had never said it aloud. Saying
it would have sounded weak. Hearing Tigo say it made
it sound true.

"You leave," Danny said, "what do you got?"

"My garage job."

"That all?"

"Marisol."

"That's plenty."

Tigo glanced at him. "You got Rita."

"Yeah."

"You work?"

"Stockroom at Kramer's."

"You like it?"

"No."

"You any good with motors?"

"No."

"I could teach you."

Danny smiled. "You ain't even a mechanic yet."

"I know more than you."

"My mother knows more than me."

"That ain't saying much."

They looked at each other across the gun.

Tigo's expression became serious. "Come Sunday."

"Where?"

"The lake. You and Rita. Me and Marisol."

"You mean that?"

"Yeah."

"What about the clubs?"

"They don't own the lake."

"They see our jackets—"

"We don't wear them."

Danny touched the white crown over his heart.

No jacket. It sounded like going outside without skin. It
also sounded cool.

"We get two boats," he said.

"One."

"You and me in one boat? People talk."

"Girls can row."

"Rita don't row."

"Marisol will."

"She'll hit you with an oar."

"Then you save me, fat boy."

Danny laughed. "Sunday."

"Sunday."

For the first time, the word made a real place
in Danny's mind. Sunlight on the water. Rita in a
yellow blouse. Marisol laughing from the bow. Tigo working the
oars, grease still under his nails. No Crowns. No Cobras.

Tigo looked down at the gun in his hand.

"We got to finish this turn," he said.

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Put it down."

Tigo's jaw worked. Above them, a shoe scraped across the
floor.

"They're listening," he whispered.

"Let them."

"You say that with the gun in my hand."

"I'll say it when it's in mine."

Tigo studied him. Then he lifted the revolver.

Danny leaned forward. "Don't."

Tigo put the barrel against his head.

"Sunday," Danny said.

"Sunday," Tigo answered.

He pulled the trigger.

Click.

The gun fell from his hand. Danny caught it before
it rolled off the table.

They stared at each other.

Then Tigo began to laugh. It was not a tough
laugh. It was wild and breathless and relieved. Danny laughed
with him.

"We're done," Danny said.

"We're done."

"No more."

"No more."

Danny opened the cylinder. Three cartridges gleamed inside. He tipped
them into his palm and set them on the table.

Tigo looked toward the stairs. "What do we tell them?"

"The truth."

"They won't like it."

"Tough."

"They might come down."

"Then we go out the window."

"That window's painted shut."

"We break it."

"With what?"

Danny held up the empty revolver.

Tigo grinned. "You crazy."

"Sunday, ten o'clock."

"Make it eleven. Marisol takes forever."

"Rita too."

"Girls."

"Yeah."

Danny looked at the cartridges on the table. Then at
the box.

"One last spin," Tigo said softly.

Danny's smile disappeared. "We said done."

"Empty gun. Just for the noise upstairs."

Danny understood. Spin it, click it, let the clubs think
the game had ended by chance. Then they could walk
up together and tell them whatever they wanted.

"Empty," Danny said.

"Empty."

He checked the cylinder. The light was bad. Five chambers
looked dark. One turned away beneath the frame. He shook
the gun once and heard nothing.

Tigo pushed the three cartridges into a row.

Danny snapped the cylinder shut and spun it hard. The
revolver sang in his hand.

Above them, the room became silent.

Danny raised the gun.

Tigo smiled across the table. "To the lake."

"To Sunday," Danny said.

He put the muzzle against his temple and pulled the
trigger.

The explosion filled the basement.

The bulb swung. Plaster dust fell from the ceiling. Danny's
chair went backward, and his body struck the floor with
the blue crown folding under him.

For a moment Tigo did not move.

Smoke curled from the barrel.

One cartridge had remained in the sixth chamber, caught beneath
the extractor, hidden when Danny emptied the cylinder.

Tigo looked at the gun. He looked at the row
of three cartridges. He looked down at Danny.

"No," he said.

Footsteps thundered overhead.

Tigo came around the table and dropped beside him. He
took Danny by the jacket and pulled him close, as
if he could drag him back from the shot, back
from the floor, back toward the black window and the
Sunday sunlight beyond it.

"No, man. No. We was done."

Blood spread beneath Danny's head.

Tigo pressed his face against the blue satin. The white
crown darkened under his hands.

The basement door burst open, but Tigo did not look
up. Cobra and Crown crowded the stairs together, suddenly quiet.

Tigo held the boy who had been his enemy.

Then he bent over him and wept.

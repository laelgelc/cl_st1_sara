### The Lesson of May

Professor Almeric Vale paused in the midst of his sentence
and looked beyond the open windows.

For nearly fifty minutes he had been speaking of immortality—not
the immortality of creeds, precisely, though he had touched upon
them; nor the thin continuance of a name in books;
but that persistence of personal identity which men had desired,
asserted, doubted, and debated from the first moment in which
death had become more than an animal darkness. Upon the
blackboard behind him appeared, in his fine, slanting hand, the
names of Plato, Spinoza, Kant, and William James; under these,
connected by wavering lines, were such words as *substance*, *memory*,
*moral necessity*, and *the consciousness of life*.

But immortality had little chance against May.

The breeze came wandering into the class-room with a fragrance
of lilacs and damp young earth. It stirred the papers
on the professor's desk, lifted the edge of a curtain,
and passed over the students with a touch so light
and persuasive that their thoughts went after it. Outside, the
campus lawns lay in a green brilliance. White clouds travelled
slowly above the elms. From the tennis courts came the
regular impact of balls, an occasional cry, and laughter released
into the air without apology.

A girl in the second row, Anna Mercer, had arranged
her hair more carefully than usual; yet the breeze, malicious
with youth, loosened a strand and drew it across her
cheek. She put it back, smiling. A young man beside
her looked at the loosened hair instead of at the
professor.

Vale saw the look.

He saw also how Daniel Frost, who had argued so
vigorously the week before that morality could have no foundation
without freedom, was now following with grave attention the progress
of two girls across the lawn. Three seats farther back,
someone had drawn a sailboat in the margin of his
notes. Even Gretta Loring, whose eyes ordinarily met each turn
of his argument with a kind of forward-leaning intelligence, seemed
troubled rather than absorbed. Her gaze was fixed upon him,
but there were tears in it.

He faltered.

The word he had intended to speak deserted him. He
turned toward the board, where the philosophers confronted him in
chalky silence, and rubbed his forehead. The room was unusually
warm. Or perhaps the warmth was in him—that dry heat
of fatigue which had lately come without warning, leaving his
limbs heavy and his thoughts just beyond reach.

For an instant he resented the students. Forty-two years he
had taught young people to distinguish desire from evidence, impulse
from conviction, cleverness from truth; and now a fragrance, a
tennis ball, a strand of hair, could conquer him. But
the resentment passed. What claim had philosophy to become an
enemy of life? If it could not enter into the
sunlight, if it must close the windows upon birds and
laughter in order to be heard, then perhaps it had
mistaken its own purpose.

He laid the chalk down.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he said, "I have discovered that I
am lecturing not merely against Hume and the materialists, but
against the first generous day of May. It would be
intellectual dishonesty to pretend that my argument is prevailing."

The class stirred, uncertain whether it had permission to smile.

"I have sometimes told you," he continued, "that philosophy should
enlarge experience, not impoverish it. A principle is tested, perhaps,
by its consequences. If our wisdom makes us less conscious
of life rather than more conscious—less capable of receiving an
hour such as this—then we may question whether it deserves
the name."

Now they were smiling openly.

"We shall end here. Go out-of-doors. Consider the immortality of
the soul, if you can; but if you cannot, attend
honestly to the mortality of May."

There was laughter, followed by the movement of books, chairs,
and hurried feet. Gratitude brightened the young faces. They passed
him with friendly farewells, already transformed from students into players,
walkers, lovers, idlers—into that many-formed creature called youth. Anna Mercer
went out with the breeze still troubling her hair. Daniel
Frost overtook the two girls upon the steps.

Gretta remained.

She sat with her notebook closed beneath one hand. The
tears had disappeared, but not their trace. Her face, grave
and candid, seemed to hold some question which she could
neither put to him nor wholly conceal.

Vale busied himself with his papers.

"You are not going to accuse me of surrendering the
citadel, Miss Loring?"

"No, Professor Vale."

"I thought you looked disapproving."

"I wasn't disapproving."

"Then I have mistaken you." He smiled, though he found
smiling an effort. "A teacher should know better than to
interpret faces. It is a dangerous branch of metaphysics."

She rose. "You are tired."

The words, gently spoken, struck him with an intimacy he
disliked.

"Not at all. A little overcome by the weather, perhaps.
May is a formidable disputant."

She seemed about to answer, but did not. Instead she
gathered her books and held them against her breast.

"Good afternoon, Professor."

"Good afternoon, Miss Loring."

At the door she looked back. The concern in her
eyes was unmistakable, and because it was unmistakable he turned
away. A moment later she was gone.

He stood alone in the class-room.

The sunlight which had been animated by moving heads and
shoulders now lay in broad, vacant spaces across the desks.
Dust motes travelled through it. On the blackboard, *immortality* had
been underlined twice. He stared at the word until its
letters appeared foolishly large.

He was tired.

There was no indignity in fatigue, he told himself. At
seventy-three a man might be tired after lecturing for an
hour. Indeed, he had lectured twice that morning and conducted
a conference before luncheon. Younger men complained of weariness upon
less cause. Yet the explanation did not satisfy him, perhaps
because it came so readily. Of late he had developed
explanations for many things: the forgotten reference, the repeated anecdote,
the name which would not come, the argument begun with
confidence and abandoned in confusion. Each occurrence was trivial. Taken
together, did they remain trivial?

He erased the board slowly. The chalk dust whitened his
fingers and settled upon his sleeve. When the last word
was gone, a ghost of it remained.

His rooms were in a narrow house beyond the north
gate, but he felt no wish to return to them.
There would be the cool parlour with its unused chairs,
the supper arranged by Mrs. Bell under covered dishes, the
ticking clock, the evening paper folded beside his plate. Nothing
unpleasant awaited him. Nothing awaited him at all.

He put on his hat and walked toward the library.

The campus had surrendered completely to spring. Groups of students
lay beneath the trees. Young men in white flannels called
to one another across the lawns; girls moved among them
in pale dresses, their voices rising and falling like birdsong.
Everywhere there was an unconscious expenditure of vitality. They ran
when walking would have served, laughed where a smile might
have sufficed, argued with the magnificent certainty that truth had
delayed its appearance only until their arrival.

Vale watched them with tenderness and a faint irritation. Once
he too had crossed a campus swiftly, carrying books which
seemed to contain the foundations of the universe. Could it
be true? He remembered the inward violence of those years
more clearly than their outward shape—the discovery of an idea
at midnight, the impatience with accepted answers, the belief that
thought itself was a moral heroism. He had not then
imagined age. Age was a condition belonging to other men,
as remote and impersonal as an ancient system of astronomy.

Two students greeted him. He raised his hat, but one
had already turned away before the gesture was completed. Not
discourtesy—merely haste, absorption, youth. He found himself repeating the words
as though acquitting them before a court.

In the library the air was dim and cool. The
familiar odour of leather, paper, and dust received him with
something like hospitality. Here the season entered reluctantly through high
windows. The shelves stood in ordered ranks, indifferent to May.
He had always loved their patience. Generations might hurry beneath
the trees, but Aristotle waited; Marcus Aurelius waited; the grave,
courteous dead made no complaint if one arrived late.

He went to the philosophy alcove, intending to examine a
new volume which the librarian had mentioned. Halfway down the
narrow aisle he heard voices from the other side of
the shelves.

"I wish you hadn't told me," Gretta Loring was saying.

Vale stopped.

There was no reason to suppose that the conversation concerned
him. Nevertheless, at the sound of her voice, and at
something constrained within it, he remained motionless.

"You would have heard," another girl answered. He recognised Helen
Armitage, a senior who sometimes assisted in the president's office.
"Everyone on the board has been discussing it."

"Not everyone knows him."

"That is exactly what makes it difficult."

There was a pause. A book was drawn from a
shelf and replaced.

"He lost the thread three times at the meeting," Helen
continued. "Dr. Newell had to prompt him. And last month
he gave the same lecture twice to the ethics class."

"I know."

The quietness of Gretta's reply was worse than surprise would
have been.

"They haven't decided anything," Helen said. "President Morrow wants him
to resign of his own accord. Some of the trustees
say it would be cruel to ask him. Others say
it would be crueller to let him go on."

"Crueller to whom?"

"To him—and to the college."

Again there was silence.

Vale leaned one hand against the books. The shelf pressed
sharply into his palm, yet it seemed necessary to hold
to something. He felt neither anger nor astonishment. What he
felt first was an absurd attention to physical details: a
crack in the floor, a frayed thread at the edge
of the carpet, the gilt lettering upon a volume before
his eyes. *The Limits of Human Faculty*. Had some malicious
librarian placed it there?

Helen spoke more softly. "Gretta, you must see that he
can't continue."

"I do see."

The professor closed his eyes.

"But he has been the college," Gretta said. "Not all
of it, of course—but for so many of us, the
part that mattered. To have them whispering about him, measuring
his failures—"

"They are trying to spare him."

"I know. That is what hurts."

There came the sound of approaching footsteps from the central
hall. The girls moved away, their voices lost among the
shelves. Vale remained where he was until the footsteps also
faded.

So Gretta had wept because she pitied him.

The discovery brought heat to his face. He would almost
rather she had despised him. Pity looked downward; it placed
the giver upon a height and made an object of
the receiver. Yet even while he accused her, he knew
the accusation unjust. There had been no condescension in her
voice. Her honesty was the wound. She had not defended
his abilities; she had defended his past.

He took his hand from the shelf. Four pale marks
crossed the palm where his fingers had pressed.

They haven't decided anything.

No, it was he who must decide. They were waiting
for him to save them from cruelty, embarrassment, perhaps ingratitude.
President Morrow would praise his service; the trustees would pass
a resolution; some younger man would occupy the class-room in
September and erase from the blackboard whatever faint remnant of
his writing remained.

He walked out of the library without the new book.

The brilliance of the afternoon seemed now almost indecent. How
could light continue with such serenity after one had been
condemned? Yet no condemnation had been pronounced. That was part
of the humiliation. He had overheard a practical conversation between
two girls; from it he had constructed his own dismissal.
Was this intellectual honesty—or merely wounded vanity rushing to meet
what it feared?

He sat upon a bench behind Morton Hall, where an
old copper beech cast a shadow deep enough to conceal
him from the nearest path.

Could he continue?

He tried to summon evidence in his favour. His students
still came to him. They filled his advanced seminar. He
received letters from former pupils—lawyers, ministers, teachers—who remembered some question
he had asked twenty years before. Only last winter the
faculty had applauded his address on moral freedom.

But had they applauded the address, or the man who
had once been capable of it?

His mind returned, against his will, to recent failures. A
morning when the name of Berkeley had vanished and left
him speaking vaguely of "the Irish bishop"; an examination paper
misplaced, then discovered beneath papers already searched; a discussion in
which he had confused two students and rebuked one for
an opinion held by the other. Small matters. Age was
composed of small matters. No single stone made a ruin.

He bent forward, resting both hands upon his cane.

For forty-two years the college had given form to his
days. The bells had divided time; terms had supplied beginnings
and endings; the recurring faces of students had kept the
world perpetually young about him. He had declined offers elsewhere
because this college needed building. He had not married—though once
there had been Margaret, with her dark, humorous eyes, waiting
for him to discover whether scholarship or happiness was the
more urgent obligation. He had believed there would be time.
Then her father took her abroad; later she married. He
had attended no wedding, founded no household, held no child
of his own. The college became not compensation, he had
insisted, but vocation.

Now the vocation was to be removed. What remained?

Books, naturally. He might complete the treatise on ethical continuity
begun twelve years ago. He might travel. Friends had urged
him to see Athens, Florence, the French cathedrals. The suggestions
appeared before him in their conventional brightness, like painted doors
opening upon blank walls.

He had taught that a man's worth could not depend
wholly upon an office. To identify the self with its
social function was philosophically crude and spiritually perilous. He had
said so many times. But wisdom delivered from a platform
was one thing; wisdom required in solitude was another.

Must one always pay for one's truths?

A bell sounded from the chapel tower. Four strokes passed
over the lawns and died.

Vale rose. His decision, when it came, possessed none of
the grandeur he had sometimes attributed to moral action. It
was simply the refusal to wait until another man was
forced to speak. If there remained to him any authority,
he would use it to surrender authority.

In his office, the late sunlight had begun to withdraw
from the shelves. He sat at his desk and took
out a sheet of college paper.

"My dear President Morrow," he wrote.

The pen moved readily at first. He stated that, after
serious consideration, he had concluded the present academic year should
mark the termination of his active service. He referred to
the natural claims of younger scholarship, the need for fresh
strength, the welfare of the department. He expressed gratitude for
the confidence shown him over more than four decades.

The phrases were dignified, exact, lifeless.

He stopped before the final sentence. Through the wall came
the faint sound of a typewriter, brisk and competent. Somewhere
a door closed. The institution continued around him, each person
completing an appointed task. For many years his own task
had seemed inseparable from that continuance. Now he saw how
easily the mechanism would adjust itself.

He signed, "Almeric Vale."

The name looked unfamiliar. Professor Vale had written thousands of
comments, recommendations, lectures, reports; Almeric Vale, an elderly unmarried gentleman
with rooms beyond the north gate, had very little occasion
to write at all.

He placed the letter in an envelope and carried it
to President Morrow's office. The secretary had gone. He slipped
it beneath the inner door.

That was all.

No bell marked the act; no tree was struck barren.
The corridor remained empty, smelling faintly of floor wax and
old wood. His life had changed, and nothing had happened.

He returned to his office and sat in the chair
by the window.

At first he reviewed his career almost defensively, as though
preparing evidence for an appeal. He had reorganised the department
in 1898. He had established the Hartley lectures. His essays
on conscience had been quoted in serious journals. He had
served twice as acting dean and once, during President Farrow's
illness, as head of the college. Former students had attained
positions of distinction.

Yet the catalogue of accomplishment gave little comfort. Honours belonged
to public life, and public life was precisely what was
closing. A resolution bound in leather could not sit opposite
him at supper. A portrait, if they chose to hang
one, would not know whether the original woke in the
night.

He rose and walked among his books.

Each shelf contained an age of himself. Here were the
volumes purchased during his first years, when money was scarce
and every book an event; there, the German philosophers acquired
during his summer in Heidelberg; above them, presentation copies from
men now dead. Marginal notes preserved arguments he no longer
remembered making. In a volume of Plato he found a
pressed violet, brown and nearly transparent.

Margaret had given it to him.

He stood holding the book while the years folded strangely
together. A spring afternoon, a river path, Margaret laughing because
he had attempted to explain beauty instead of seeing it—were
these truly farther from him than the class dismissed an
hour ago? Memory abolished time and proved nothing. The dead,
the absent, and the young self were vivid within consciousness;
nevertheless, when he closed the book, he was alone in
a darkening room.

He replaced the Plato carefully.

Perhaps he had chosen wrongly. Perhaps work, however imperfect, had
been his honest form of love. A man could not
live every possible life; renunciation lay concealed within each decision.
Still, he had imagined that devotion purchased exemption from loneliness.
Serve faithfully, and one would belong forever to that which
one served.

But institutions had no forever. They possessed schedules.

The thought was bitter, and he was ashamed of its
bitterness. The college had not wronged him. It had given
him opportunity, respect, companionship, a sphere in which his powers
could act. If those powers failed, was the college obliged
to fail with them? The young had claims, too—not merely
upon places, but upon truth well taught. To continue because
he feared emptiness would be to convert service into possession.

He knew this. Knowledge did not heal him.

The light faded. Long shadows from the window bars climbed
the opposite wall and reached the portraits above the shelves.
The daylight seemed to creep away under protest, lingering upon
the gilt edges of books, retreating from table to chair,
from chair to floor, until only the upper panes held
a pallor of sky.

At last Vale put on his coat.

Outside, evening had softened the campus. The fierce brightness was
gone; in its place lay that suspended hour when forms
remain distinct while colours deepen toward mystery. Lamps appeared along
the paths one by one. The elms stood dark against
a greenish west, and from the town beyond the gates
came the distant sounds of wheels and bells.

He descended the steps, then stopped.

He did not want to go home.

The reluctance was childish, but he had no wish to
rebuke it. For one more evening he belonged here officially.
The letter waited beneath President Morrow's door, unread perhaps until
morning. For one more evening his name remained in the
catalogue without qualification.

He turned toward the chapel garden.

There, on a low stone wall beneath the lilacs, sat
Gretta Loring. Her hat lay beside her, and her hands
were clasped about one knee. She appeared not to be
reading or waiting, but merely listening to the gradual arrival
of night.

When she saw him, she rose.

"Professor Vale."

"Miss Loring." He removed his hat. "You are spending your
victory out-of-doors, I see."

"My victory?"

"May's victory. We must give credit accurately."

She did not smile. In the dimness her face had
the same attentive gravity it wore in the class-room, but
now he understood that attention differently. She had been watching
not the argument, but the man labouring to sustain it.

"You were right to dismiss us," she said. "Though I
didn't go far."

"No?"

"I walked to the river and came back."

He rested one hand on the stone wall. The fragrance
of the lilacs was stronger in the cooling air, almost
too sweet. Nearby, hidden birds exchanged brief, questioning notes before
sleep.

"I have resigned," he said.

There. Again the act was accomplished in a few words.

Gretta looked at him without surprise. Her hands tightened slightly
upon each other.

"To-day?" she asked.

"To-day. The letter is at President Morrow's office."

She lowered her eyes.

He had hoped—despite everything, despite the conversation overheard in the
library—that she would protest. She might say the college could
not spare him, that his powers remained unweakened, that the
very idea was unthinkable. He would have rejected such assurances,
certainly; but he had wanted them offered.

"You think I am right," he said.

She looked up. "You have always taught us to face
what is true."

"A maxim returns with inconvenient punctuality."

"It does not make the truth easier."

"No." He turned toward the darkening lawn. "But you think
it is true."

For a moment she said nothing. That silence answered him;
yet when she spoke, her voice was steady.

"Yes."

The word entered him cleanly, without the confusion of hope.

He nodded. "Thank you."

She made a distressed movement. "Professor—"

"No, I mean it. False kindness would be an odd
tribute after four years of philosophy."

"I don't want to be kind in that way."

"I know."

They sat upon the wall, leaving a little distance between
them. Students passed along a path beyond the hedge, talking
in low voices. A girl laughed, then hushed herself as
though evening had laid a finger upon her lips.

Vale watched the lamp glow through young leaves.

"I heard you in the library," he said.

Gretta's face became pale. "You heard—?"

"You and Miss Armitage. I had gone to look for
a book. One finds many things in libraries besides those
one seeks."

"I am sorry."

"You said nothing cruel."

"But you should not have had to hear it so."

"How should one hear that one has become old? There
is probably no proper method."

She turned toward him. "It was not that you are
old."

"No? That would at least be blameless."

"I mean—it wasn't your age that distressed me."

"My failures, then."

"The thought that you might believe they had cancelled everything
before them."

He was silent.

She continued with difficulty, choosing each phrase. "When Helen said
the college must find someone else, I suddenly saw how
it might look to you—as if another man would enter
your class-room, take your books from the desk, teach your
subjects, and so replace you. As if forty-two years could
be put away because a younger voice speaks more strongly."

"That is substantially what will occur."

"No."

The force of her answer startled him.

"No," she repeated, more quietly. "Someone will occupy your chair.
Someone will teach the classes. But he cannot begin where
you began, because you have changed the place from which
beginning is possible."

Vale looked at her.

She leaned forward, as she had so often done in
his lectures; yet now there was no professor and student
in the old relation. She was trying to make him
see, and he—the teacher of perception, distinction, and truth—was the
one who had failed to understand.

"You once told us," she said, "that no thought is
solitary. Even when we oppose a man, we carry something
of him into the opposition, because he gave us the
question in a form we could answer. Do you remember?"

"Faintly."

"I remember. Daniel remembers. So do dozens who left before
us. You taught us not what to think—that would have
ended with examinations—but how to be ashamed of an easy
answer. How to know when we were deceiving ourselves. How
to wait before judging another person. Whoever teaches here next
will teach students whom you, and the students you taught,
have already helped to form."

"That is influence," he said. "A frail sort of immortality."

"Is any immortality that we can prove less frail?"

The question, carrying his own method back to him, stirred
something almost like amusement.

"You have become dangerous, Miss Loring."

"You intended us to."

A breeze moved through the lilacs. Several blossoms, loosened from
a cluster, fell upon the wall between them.

Gretta picked up one of them and turned it in
her fingers.

"I don't think you are being replaced," she said. "Replacement
means that one thing is removed and another of the
same use is put where it stood. But a teacher—if
he has truly taught—has already entered the lives that come
after him. The next person can go farther because you
brought us this far."

She hesitated. "It is not replacement. It is fulfilment."

The word rested in the twilight.

Fulfilment.

At first Vale resisted it. It seemed too generous, too
rounded, a verbal consolation fashioned by youth for grief it
could not yet comprehend. What did Gretta know of waking
without an appointed task, of becoming a past tense in
the mouths of colleagues, of watching strangers inherit the familiar?
Influence was invisible. Loneliness was not.

Yet she had not denied the loss. That was why
he could hear her. She had said yes when he
asked whether it was time. She had refused him the
narcotic of reassurance; now she offered not escape from truth,
but another truth beside it.

Had he himself not argued, earlier that very afternoon, that
philosophy must enlarge experience? He had thought May the rival
of thought because the students looked away from him toward
sunlight. But perhaps their looking away was not defeat. A
lesson fulfilled did not keep the pupil forever facing the
teacher. It released him toward the world.

The idea came gradually, not as revelation but as recognition.

He remembered faces.

A young minister who had written from a mining town
that Professor Vale's lecture on doubt had saved him from
becoming either a hypocrite or an unbeliever. A judge who,
visiting after twenty years, confessed that one classroom question returned
whenever the law offered an easy cruelty. Margaret's nephew, whom
Vale had persuaded not to abandon medicine after his first
failure. Gretta herself—her candid mind, her unwillingness to soften truth,
the compassion disciplined by honesty. Had these things occurred apart
from him? Had they not, in some small measure, been
his work continuing where he could no longer direct it?

He had looked for his life in catalogues, offices, courses,
and honours. Naturally he had found only what could be
taken away.

But if it lived elsewhere—in minds now moving beyond his
sight—then loss was not annihilation. The stream did not cease
because it left the spring.

"Fulfilment," he repeated.

Gretta watched him anxiously. "Perhaps it is the wrong word."

"No." He considered. "It may be a word I have
used often without understanding."

The chapel clock began to strike. The notes descended slowly
into the evening, each one separate, each fading before the
next arrived. Vale counted them without intending to.

Eight.

How late it had become. Mrs. Bell would be worried;
his supper would be dry. The ordinary future, with all
its small claims, waited after all.

He rose, and Gretta rose beside him.

"I shall miss teaching," he said.

"I know."

"I may be very unhappy."

"I know."

He smiled. "You offer remarkably little comfort."

"I can't bear to give you comfort that will be
gone by morning."

"That is well."

They walked together toward the north path. The campus had
grown shadowy, but the windows of the library shone, square
and golden, among the trees. From an upper room came
the murmur of students studying; from the dormitory steps, a
burst of song, uncertain at first and then strengthened as
other voices joined.

Vale listened.

Not one of those voices required him. The recognition still
hurt; serenity, he discovered, did not consist in the absence
of pain. It was perhaps the willingness to let pain
stand within a larger meaning.

At the path where Gretta must turn toward the women's
residence, they stopped.

"What will you do?" she asked.

He looked toward the dark line of the hills. "For
the first week, I suspect, I shall arrange my books
and irritate Mrs. Bell. After that—I have long threatened to
write something on ethical continuity."

"You must."

"Must I? At my age, commands should be issued cautiously."

"Then I hope you will."

"That is more philosophical."

She held out her hand. He took it. Her grasp
was warm and firm, without timidity.

"Good night, Professor Vale."

"Good night, Miss Loring."

She walked away beneath the lamps. Halfway to the residence
she turned and raised her hand; then she went on,
her figure passing from light into shadow and from shadow
into light until the trees concealed her.

Vale remained for a while.

The fragrance of lilacs drifted across the path. Overhead, the
last colour had gone from the sky, but the darkness
was clear, and between the young leaves a few stars
had appeared. Around him the college breathed with lives intersecting,
separating, continuing—laughter from one window, music from another, footsteps on
gravel, the closing of a distant door.

He thought of the class-room in September, another man standing
at the desk, other names written upon the board. The
image no longer seemed an erasure. The new teacher would
inherit questions shaped before his coming; the students would bring
assumptions altered by older students, by books selected, traditions established,
standards patiently insisted upon. Nothing would remain pure or recognisable.
That was not the manner of living continuance.

Immortality. He had drawn lines beneath the word while May
entered through the windows and undid a girl's hair. He
had sought an argument strong enough to defend the self
against death, and all the while life had been giving
another answer—imperfect, unprovable, dispersed among persons who would scarcely know
its source.

Perhaps the self was not preserved by remaining unchanged. Perhaps
it endured by being given away.

He began to walk home.

His step was slow, and the cane sounded distinctly upon
the path. He was seventy-three; he had failed in ways
that could not be reversed; to-morrow his resignation would be
read, and soon his office would cease to be his.
None of this had altered.

Yet the college no longer seemed to recede from him.
Rather, it extended beyond him. The years of labour, which
an hour before had appeared piled like dead books in
a closed room, now opened into countless consequences—small, mingled, beyond
recovery, but real. He would never trace them. It was
enough that he had helped to begin them.

At the north gate he looked back.

The towers rose dark above the elms. Lamps shone along
the walks where young figures moved, meeting and parting. May
possessed the campus utterly; but he no longer felt that
spring had mocked him. Renewal required departure, as growth required
what had gone before. Age and youth were not enemies
unless one demanded that either stand alone.

Professor Vale removed his hat.

The night air touched his forehead, cool where the afternoon
had burned. Then, with a dignity no longer dependent upon
title or class-room, and with a peace made credible because
sorrow remained within it, he passed through the gate toward
home.

### THE LAST INSTRUMENT

Clarke Randolph was a man whom even his enemies—he had
acquired a few in the honorable exercise of an exacting
profession—described as educated, scrupulous, courageous, and kind. At forty-six he
had attained that eminence at the bar which comes less
frequently from brilliance than from the disciplined perpetuation of brilliance;
he possessed fortune without ostentation, influence without vulgarity, and manners
in which an older chivalry appeared reconciled with the more
severe intelligence of the modern world. He was sensitive, but
not weak; proud, but not vain; deliberate in judgment, yet
generous after judgment had been pronounced. That such a man
should contemplate murder is terrible. That he should contemplate two
murders, and his own death afterward, is almost incredible.

Nevertheless, on the afternoon of the seventeenth of November, Clarke
Randolph bought a revolver.

He purchased it at a shop far from the quarter
in which he was known. He inspected the weapon with
professional composure, asked two or three pertinent questions, paid in
bank-notes, and carried the small parcel beneath his overcoat to
his office. No clerk saw him enter, for he had
dismissed the clerks at four; no client was expected; and
the charwoman, whose presence might have disturbed his calculations, did
not come until eight.

Dinner at his house was fixed for half past seven.
His wife would be there. Henry Stockton would be there
also.

At five minutes after five Randolph sat at his great
mahogany desk, broke the seal of the parcel, and laid
the revolver upon a sheet of white legal paper.

Its blackness was singularly distinct against that immaculate surface.

Three persons were to die: his wife, Henry Stockton, and
himself.

He had arranged the order. The woman first, because she
had been the first cause—or so at that hour his
passion informed him; Stockton next, because he was a traitor
to friendship, hospitality, confidence, and manhood; Randolph last, because the
world, after such an exposure, could contain neither occupation nor
dignity for him. There would be no trial, no vulgar
procession through police court and criminal court, no staring jury,
no medical witnesses, no daily publication of domestic foulness. He
would constitute himself accuser, judge, executioner, and final victim. The
proceeding would be swift, private, conclusive.

The revolver won.

For during the preceding night, and again throughout the day,
other instruments had contended for his preference. There had been
poison, with its deceptive cleanliness and its scientific gradations; the
sleeping draught that merely deepens sleep, the alkaloid that arrests
the heart, the corrosive that burns its dreadful road through
tissues and membranes. But poison involved uncertainty—convulsion, vomiting, delay, the
possible intervention of a physician, the hideous tenacity with which
the organism sometimes clings to a life already condemned. There
had been the knife; but the knife demanded contact. It
was personal, hot, primitive. There had been strangulation; but that
was the method of the brute hand, the murderer in
a ditch, the ape enraged by possession.

The revolver separated intention from consequence by a few feet
of air. Pressure—explosion—death. It was manifestly the instrument of civilization.

Thus may intelligence become the advocate of madness. Let us
not dismiss Randolph as insane, and by that convenient word
escape the obligation of understanding him. His mind was not
shattered. On the contrary, it functioned with a dreadful precision.
Memory presented evidence; logic classified it; imagination furnished consequences; will
prepared action. Only the premise was diseased, and from that
diseased premise every conclusion followed with the stateliness of law.

He had learned the truth at midnight.

The discovery had been accidental, yet accident had merely opened
a door behind which, as he now perceived, a hundred
neglected indications had assembled. A letter—misdirected, perhaps; perhaps dropped with
the unconscious carelessness of those whom secrecy has made overconfident—had
contained no explicit confession. It needed none. A phrase of
tenderness, an appointment, a backward reference to hours which he
had believed innocent: these were sufficient. The holographic character of
the writing was beyond dispute. He had compared it with
other letters, though comparison was superfluous. He knew Stockton's hand
as he knew his own.

Henry Stockton! His companion at college; his ally in early
poverty; his best man upon the morning of his marriage;
his intimate guest for fifteen years! To Stockton he had
confided those disappointments which pride concealed from the world. Stockton
possessed the key of his house, the affection of his
servants, the confidence of his wife. When illness had once
brought Randolph near death, it was Stockton who had remained
beside the bed and who, believing the sick man unconscious,
had wept.

And she!

He did not pronounce her name, even within his thought.
To name her would have evoked the bride of twenty
years ago, the timid girl whose hand had trembled in
his, the young wife who had watched at his own
bedside, the gracious mistress of his house, the woman whose
beauty had softened rather than hardened with maturity. He required,
for his present purpose, not that woman, but another: the
false wife, the adulteress, the conspirator.

It was necessary to simplify her before he could kill
her.

At first the proposed scene had given him a monstrous
satisfaction. He would arrive a little before the appointed dinner
hour. He would be perfectly dressed and perfectly calm. Stockton
would stand by the library fire; she would enter, perhaps
wearing the gray silk gown he had admired. Randolph would
lock the door. Then, without accusation, without noise, he would
place the letter upon the table.

Their faces would tell everything.

He would let them suffer. There must be an interval—half
a minute, a minute—in which exposure, denunciation, publication, scorn, contempt,
and terror should pass before their imaginations. She would grow
pale; Stockton would advance; perhaps he would speak Randolph's name
in the old friendly tone. Ah, the infamy of that
tone! Randolph would lift the weapon. Stockton might spring forward.
She might place herself between them. There would be an
appeal, a cry, a flash; gray silk stained darkly; a
body sinking among the chairs; another report; Stockton turning with
astonishment in his eyes; blood upon the hearth; smoke under
the chandelier—

Randolph rose suddenly.

The office had become oppressive. He crossed to the window
and looked down into the street, where lamps were beginning
to appear in the violet obscurity. People passed beneath him:
a messenger-boy running; a woman carrying flowers; an old gentleman
pausing to avoid a puddle. All these persons belonged to
the ordinary world, where appointments were kept, dinners eaten, children
kissed, and tomorrow contemplated without horror. He seemed immeasurably removed
from them, not by altitude but by species.

He returned to the desk.

There were arrangements to make. A lawyer does not leave
disorder behind him, even when he proposes the ultimate disorder.
His estate was considerable and almost wholly personal. There were
securities, two houses, certain western lands, insurance policies whose clauses
regarding suicide he knew precisely, and a collection of pictures
which his wife professed to value. Under his existing will
she received everything.

That instrument must be destroyed.

He opened a steel box, removed the document, and read
the commencement: "I, Clarke Edwin Randolph, being of sound and
disposing mind—"

He smiled.

Never had his mind been more disposing.

He tore the will across, then again, and dropped the
pieces into the grate. Flame ran along the heavy paper,
curled the careful clauses, blackened his signature, and consumed the
names of the witnesses. In a minute twenty years of
domestic confidence were ash.

A new will lay ready in his imagination. His wife
should receive nothing—not a chair, not a jewel, not the
portrait of her mother. The house itself should be sold,
so that no familiar wall might shelter her. His fortune
should pass to hospitals, orphanages, institutions for the redemption of
fallen women—yes, there was fitness in that! He would leave
perhaps a small annuity, sufficiently small to preserve existence and
sufficiently public to preserve shame. The trustees should pay it
monthly, requiring her personal receipt. Each signature would be a
confession.

But why leave an annuity?

Let her live by Stockton's charity, if Stockton lived. Yet
Stockton would not live. Then let her live by labor.
Could she labor? She had delicate hands, accomplished hands, hands
trained for music, flowers, embroidery, the distribution of tea. They
would become reddened, coarsened, perhaps tremulous with fatigue. She would
seek employment and be refused. Friends would withdraw. Invitations would
cease. Her name would be spoken in lowered voices, then
not spoken at all.

Randolph's imagination pursued her.

He saw her in cheap lodgings; saw beauty fading under
privation and disgrace; saw her descend from poverty to debt,
from debt to deception, from deception to drink. Why not?
Such descents had been recorded. He had encountered them in
court. There would be an arrest, imprisonment, illness, delirium; the
once fastidious woman crying in a public ward; death under
a coarse blanket; burial at municipal expense.

He bent over the new will and wrote the date.

Then his hand stopped.

What good?

The question came neither grandly nor gently. It entered his
reasoning like a clerk presenting an overlooked fact.

What good would her misery accomplish? Would twenty degraded years
reconstruct one hour of fidelity? Would poverty purify her? Would
public contempt restore his honor? If punishment were intended to
reform, her destruction defeated it; if intended to deter others,
the intimate facts must be exposed, and exposure would befoul
his name with hers; if intended merely to satisfy him,
he would be dead and incapable of satisfaction.

No good. None.

He laid down the pen.

But she had injured him! Justice demanded consequence.

Justice? Or appetite? A wounded animal bites. A stag gores
its rival; the wolf tears the wolf that approaches its
mate; even the small domestic creature, sleek beside the fire,
becomes tooth and claw when possession is disputed. Was man
then distinguished from animals merely by the complexity of his
revenge? Did reason exist only to lengthen the fang, to
sharpen the talon, to carry injury beyond death into testamentary
clauses?

No. If that were all, intelligence would be an enlargement
of brutality, not an escape from it.

He rose again and approached the mirror above the bookcase.

The face confronting him was scarcely his own. Its lips
were drawn; the nostrils dilated; the eyes, usually gray and
steady, appeared narrowed and bright. Education, dignity, jurisprudence, social honor—where
were they? Beneath the skin waited the ancestral brute, hairy
with old appetites, clutching its prey and snarling over possession.

Randolph turned away with disgust.

"She shall not be impoverished," he said.

They were the only words spoken in the room, and
their sound startled him.

He took the unfinished will, folded it once, and thrust
it into the fire.

So much was settled. She should retain the property. But
she should die.

The distinction seemed at first important. He would not pursue
her vindictively through imaginary years; he would not convert the
law into a posthumous engine of torture. Death was different.
Death was immediate, majestic, almost merciful. One report, one instant
of fear, and the account would be closed.

Yet when he tried again to imagine the scene, it
would not obey him. The false wife refused to appear.
Instead there came the woman of familiar mornings, seated opposite
him at breakfast; the woman leaning from a carriage to
smile at a child; the woman asleep, with one hand
beneath her cheek. He remembered foolish tendernesses which no betrayal
could render entirely fraudulent. Once, during a storm at sea,
she had been afraid and had hidden her face against
his shoulder. Once she had asked whether he thought souls
would recognize one another after death.

To shoot her! To raise a metal instrument against that
breast; to see terror where he had once awakened trust;
to make those lips plead for the life he had
sworn to protect!

It could not be done.

Cowardice, said something within him.

"No," his reason answered, though less audibly. "It would be
cowardice to do it."

She was physically weaker than he, socially dependent upon him,
enclosed by his name and fortune. She could not meet
him upon equal ground. She was a woman—frail, emotional, perhaps
driven by loneliness, perhaps by some hunger which his laborious
virtue had never perceived. He had given her security; had
he given companionship? He had supplied every luxury; had he
supplied himself? The courts, clients, committees, public dinners, and grave
masculine obligations had received his best hours. Stockton, idle by
comparison, sympathetic, charming, and present, had received the remainder.

This did not absolve her. But explanation is not absolution;
nor is understanding approval. It is merely that indispensable light
in which the human form becomes visible behind the offense.

She was a woman.

The phrase returned again and again. At first it had
meant weakness, and weakness had seemed contemptible. Now it meant
limitation, sensibility, exposure, the innumerable pressures of a nature different
from his own. If strength had any moral significance, surely
it imposed restraint upon the strong. To strike the weaker
because the weaker had wounded him—was that chivalry? Was that
manhood?

No. It was the brute.

He opened the revolver, removed the cartridges, and placed them
separately upon the desk. There were six. Three had been
intended for necessity, three for accident. He put one cartridge
back and closed the chamber.

His wife should live.

The conclusion brought no peace. Her life must have a
shape, and he must decide whether that shape should be
disgrace or pardon.

He could expose her without impoverishing her. A letter to
her father; another to certain intimate friends; a memorandum among
his papers stating the cause of his death. The facts,
discreetly but unmistakably recorded, would preserve him from the suspicion
of madness or financial dishonor. Society would understand. Society always
understood the sins of others with admirable completeness. Doors would
close; heads would incline; women of unimpeachable virtue would speak
compassionately and invite her no more.

Was he entitled to his reputation? Certainly. Reputation was property—the
accumulated result of conduct. Why should she preserve hers by
the destruction of his?

Because exposure would not restore him.

Again the question: What good?

None to him. He would be beyond praise, blame, explanation,
and the editorial paragraphs of respectable newspapers. None to society,
which had survived similar scandals and would survive another. None
to her, manifestly. None even to Stockton, unless suffering were
accepted as good merely because an injured man desired it.

Then silence.

He must leave behind a cause for his suicide—a cause
which implicated no one. Financial ruin was impossible to counterfeit;
the estate would refute it. Political disgrace might provoke inquiry.
Mental collapse would dishonor his professional life and place old
judgments under suspicion. Illness remained.

An incurable illness: concealed, recently diagnosed, inevitably painful.

Yes; there was a way.

He had known physicians and had cross-examined them. He could
employ their vocabulary without overemployment, for excessive detail would invite
verification. A progressive internal disorder, symptoms privately endured, consultation under
an assumed name, certainty of a lingering and degrading death—these
things would be believed because men prefer a physical explanation
for any act which otherwise obliges them to examine the
soul.

He drew paper toward him and began a letter addressed
to his wife.

"My dearest—"

The pen paused above the blank.

Could he write affectionately? He must. Her protection required tenderness,
not the ambiguous courtesy of a deceived husband. Yet false
tenderness seemed another degradation.

Was it false?

He had loved her yesterday. Did knowledge extinguish love, or
merely turn love's happiness into anguish? The object remained the
same woman, enlarged now by guilt. If affection depended upon
perfection, no human being had ever been loved; only an
illusion had been worshiped. Charity commenced at the precise point
where desert ended.

He wrote:

"My dearest, I have concealed from you for several months
a condition of health whose termination has now become certain.
Forgive the concealment. It arose not from want of confidence,
but from my inability to darken days which, in your
presence, have been the happiest granted to me."

He stopped and read the sentence. It was true enough
to wound him.

He continued, explaining that pain had increased, that physicians offered
no hope, that he could not submit her to the
spectacle of prolonged dissolution. He asked her not to reproach
herself for having failed to observe symptoms deliberately hidden. He
thanked her for twenty years of kindness. He advised travel
after the first season of mourning. He begged her to
live, not merely to exist beneath a widow's ceremonial sorrow.

At the end he wrote, "Whatever may await consciousness after
death, believe that my last thought was gratitude for you."

He signed it.

The clock struck six.

An hour and a half remained before dinner. How curious
that clocks persist! The mind may overturn duty, marriage, friendship,
life itself; still the small mechanism divides eternity into equal
portions and demands punctuality from catastrophe.

Stockton remained.

Until that moment Randolph had treated his friend's death as
inalienable. Feminine frailty might plead for a woman; no such
plea existed for a man. Stockton had known. He had
eaten Randolph's bread, grasped his hand, accepted his confidence, sat
beneath his roof, and then entered the secret chambers of
his domestic life like a thief. There was no inequality
between them. They were men. Let the man answer.

He should die.

The phrase acquired a judicial solemnity. Stockton should die—not in
anger, not as an animal sacrifice to jealousy, but as
punishment for treachery. Randolph imagined meeting him before dinner, perhaps
in the library, while his wife was still above stairs.
He would show the letter. Stockton would understand. If he
possessed courage, he might accept the sentence. If he resisted,
Randolph would fire. One bullet through the heart; another, if
needed. Then a message to the wife, concealing the motive,
and afterward Randolph's own death.

But the lie of illness could not survive Stockton's body
on the library floor.

Very well: Stockton might be summoned to the office. A
note would bring him. The murder could appear unrelated, though
inquiry—

No. There must be no inquiry.

Could Randolph compel him to suicide? Lay the evidence before
him, place the revolver between them, and leave the room?
That had the elegance of ancient judgment. Yet Stockton might
refuse. A man base enough to betray friendship might be
base enough to cling to life.

He should die.

Why?

Because he had acted like a brute.

And therefore Randolph would kill him like a brute?

The argument recoiled upon itself.

There were, among animals, contests for possession; victorious males slew
or mutilated rivals. Nature contained no court of appeal, no
forgiveness, no voluntary renunciation. If Randolph shot Stockton, in what
respect—apart from polished furniture, legal phrases, and a mechanically superior
fang—would the act differ from those blind collisions of appetite?

Reason did not make vengeance human merely by describing it
accurately.

But punishment protected society.

Would Stockton's secret death protect society? No one would know
its cause. The act would deter no one, reform no
one, establish no principle. It would simply remove the hated
body from the world.

Then it was vengeance.

Animal vengeance.

Randolph resisted. Stockton's was the greater guilt! He had calculated;
he had betrayed deliberately; he had accepted friendship while corrupting
its dearest trust. To spare him would be weakness, sentimentality,
a surrender of moral distinction.

Or would it be strength?

The easy act was pressure upon the trigger. The difficult
act was to sit motionless while every inherited instinct cried
out for blood. Muscles could kill. Even an insect could
kill. But to apprehend pain, contain it, examine it, and
refuse its transmission—was not that a higher function? Civilization consisted
less in the clothes men wore and statutes they printed
than in the interval they placed between impulse and action.
In that interval lived conscience, mercy, self-command—all that separated man
from brute.

He should die.

No.

He should live.

Randolph covered his face with both hands.

For several minutes he did not move. The office was
now nearly dark except for the green lamp upon the
desk and the subdued glow of the fire. The familiar
ranks of law-books had receded into shadow. Their learning seemed
remote. No authority among them could decide this jurisdiction. The
case was Randolph against Randolph, instinct against reflection, injury against
compassion; and from its judgment there could be no appeal.

If Stockton lived, what then?

He would possess her.

The thought entered like a blade.

They would mourn Randolph—how sincerely?—and afterward meet. Grief would draw
them together. His wife, protected by the fabricated illness, would
retain sympathy and wealth; Stockton, protected by silence, would retain
honor. In a year, perhaps two, they might marry. They
might inhabit Randolph's house, read his books, sit before his
fire. Stockton's hand might rest upon the chair Randolph had
used. They might speak his name gently. They might even
persuade themselves that he would have understood.

The revolver seemed to darken upon the paper.

To preserve them separately was one thing. To facilitate their
happiness—was that demanded? Mercy need not become generosity. He might
leave circumstances untouched and let uncertainty punish them. His wife
would never know whether he had discovered the truth. Stockton
would wonder. Every recollection of the dead man's kindness would
become accusation.

There was refinement in such punishment.

And therefore cruelty.

Randolph saw it immediately. He had removed the revolver from
his hand only to install a subtler instrument in their
minds. Suspicion, remorse, doubt—the invisible rack! Was mental torture superior
because it shed no blood? Again intelligence offered to perform
the office of the fang.

No better than brutes.

He repeated it silently: no better than brutes.

If he concealed his knowledge, he must conceal it completely.
If he forgave, he must not leave forgiveness shaped like
a trap. Their chance of amendment, dignity, and even happiness
must be genuine.

He took another sheet of paper and addressed Stockton.

"My dear Henry—"

Here the pen trembled.

He remembered a summer day thirty years earlier, two young
men rowing upon a lake; Stockton laughing as the oar
broke; both swimming beside the overturned boat. He remembered their
first chambers in the city, their ambitions, ridiculous neckties, borrowed
money, shared meals. He remembered Stockton at the wedding, pale
with emotion; Stockton at the sickbed; Stockton raising a glass
upon Randolph's fortieth birthday and declaring before twenty guests that
friendship was the one youth which age could not diminish.

Was all of it false? Surely not. Human conduct is
not a single color. A traitor may have been a
friend; indeed, only a friend can betray. The enormity of
treachery proves the reality of the trust that preceded it.

Randolph wrote of the illness. He asked Stockton to assist
his wife in the confusion that must follow; to advise
her regarding business; to protect her from importunate acquaintances; to
encourage her, after a proper interval, to resume life.

Then he added:

"You have been nearer to me than any man. I
therefore place in your care what has been dearest to
me. Do not permit her grief to become a permanent
renunciation of happiness. If affection should hereafter enter her life,
let no conventional devotion to my memory stand against it."

He could write no more.

The sentence was enough. Stockton would read an unsuspecting confidence
where there was, in truth, a conscious bequest.

Randolph signed and sealed the letter.

Now the will.

He drafted it with the skill that had made his
testamentary instruments models of precision. His wife was to receive
the income of the estate for two years. If within
that period she married Henry Stockton, the principal should vest
in them jointly. If she did not, the principal should
vest in her alone at the expiration of the term.
Thus there would be no coercion toward marriage and no
penalty against it; but the provision would declare, more powerfully
than any letter, Randolph's confidence in both.

He considered whether its unusual form might awaken suspicion. Perhaps.
Yet men attribute eccentric generosity to the dying, especially when
disease is said to have produced long meditation. The will
was wholly holographic, dated, signed, and legally sufficient under the
law of the state. He placed it with the earlier
letter in an envelope addressed to his executor.

Then he found the incriminating letter.

For the last time he read Stockton's fatal phrases. How
slight the document appeared! A sheet of paper had overturned
a universe; now another sheet of paper would construct a
merciful falsehood above its ruins.

He held the letter over the grate.

Flame touched the corner, advanced through the salutation, consumed the
appointment, hesitated blue about the fold, and climbed. Stockton's signature
blackened, glowed, vanished. Randolph watched until no legible fragment remained.
Then he stirred the ashes together with the poker, mingling
accusation, discarded wills, vengeance, and proof in one gray substance.

The clock showed seven.

There was nothing left but himself.

Why must he die?

The question, strangely, had not before been admitted. His own
death had stood from the beginning as the fixed term
of the problem, while every other term underwent scrutiny and
revision. Now reason, having spared two lives, turned at last
toward the third.

Could he live with them?

He imagined tomorrow morning: his wife entering the breakfast-room; Stockton
perhaps calling later; commonplace words above the abyss of knowledge.
Could Randolph smile? Could he touch her hand? Could he
hear Stockton's voice without recoil? Concealment might protect their honor,
but continued intimacy would convert every hour into hypocrisy. Exposure
might make another kind of life possible, but exposure would
destroy what he had resolved to preserve. Separation without explanation
would punish them by mystery and invite scandal. Divorce would
publish the cause or suggest one. Travel would postpone, not
cure.

There were men, he knew, who might forgive and remain.
Saints perhaps; or temperaments less fastidious; or those fortunate beings
in whom affection overgrows imagination. Randolph was not one of
them. He could restrain the brute, but he could not
annihilate memory. The letter would lie invisibly upon every table.
In his wife's eyes he would seek comparison; in Stockton's
hand he would feel deceit. Love might survive, but trust
had undergone an amputation, and no philosophy could restore the
vanished limb.

Then he should depart.

Not die—depart. He could go abroad, abandon name and practice,
leave them freedom.

But a living husband, absent and unexplained, would bind her
more securely than a dead one. He could arrange a
false death; yet such fraud required witnesses, documents, continued concealment,
and perhaps crimes against the law he had served. Moreover,
somewhere in the world he would remain—waiting, imagining, reading newspapers,
aging beneath an assumed name. His sacrifice would become a
prolonged surveillance.

Death alone was complete.

Let us understand the danger of this conclusion. Randolph had
reasoned nobly concerning others, but concerning himself nobility and despair
had formed an alliance. Because he refused to make his
wife suffer, he decided that he must absorb all suffering;
because he refused to take Stockton's life, he concluded that
his own was forfeit. He had overcome vengeance, but not
the original demand for a victim. The altar remained. Only
the sacrifice had changed.

Yet to Randolph the conclusion appeared perfectly clear.

He restored order to the desk. The will lay in
the center, the letters beside it, each sealed and addressed.
He wrote a brief memorandum naming the supposed physician only
as "a specialist consulted privately in another city," thus making
inquiry difficult without making direct falsehood too elaborate. He mentioned
recurrent pain, sleeplessness, and the certainty of physical degradation. He
requested that no post-mortem examination be made, a wish which
his position and the apparent circumstances would probably secure.

He rang for no one.

Outside, carriage wheels passed over the damp pavement. Somewhere below,
a door closed. The city was moving toward dinner.

Randolph put on his evening coat. The act was not
absurd to him. He had always regarded disorder of person
at death as an inconsideration toward survivors. He adjusted his
tie, brushed a fleck of ash from one sleeve, and
returned to the mirror.

The face had altered. Its distortion was gone. The eyes
were tired but steady; the mouth grave, almost gentle. He
looked neither heroic nor insane. He looked like a man
who had completed difficult work.

Upon the desk the revolver waited.

He thought of his wife receiving the letter. There would
be horror, grief, self-reproach—not for betrayal, perhaps, but for failing
to detect the imaginary illness. Stockton would come. He would
read his own letter and suffer. Yes, suffering could not
be wholly prevented. But shame would be prevented; terror, prosecution,
blood, public disgrace, poverty, and the perpetuation of hatred would
be prevented. In time suffering would diminish. They might marry.
They might be happy.

The last word cost him most.

Happy.

He permitted the vision. A house not necessarily his house;
sunlight; his wife dressed no longer in mourning; Stockton older,
humbled perhaps, but kind. No child had been born to
Randolph's marriage, yet perhaps life still held other forms of
tenderness for them. They would remember him. If remorse accompanied
remembrance, affection might soften it. They could become better than
their act. He would not deny them that possibility.

Was this forgiveness?

Perhaps forgiveness is not a feeling. Perhaps it is merely
the refusal to convert pain into further pain.

Randolph sat down.

He placed one hand upon the letter to his wife
and the other upon the weapon. Its metal was cool.
Earlier it had seemed an instrument of judgment, then of
brutality, and now—by the final perversion or final triumph of
his reasoning—an instrument of release.

The clock began to strike the quarter past seven.

He counted the strokes. After the last, silence returned, profound
and courteous.

There was still time to go home.

For one instant the whole construction trembled. He saw the
dining-room bright with candles, the gray silk gown, Stockton before
the fire. He might enter, dismiss the servants, lay the
letter down, and say simply: I know. Truth might accomplish
what secrecy could not. There might be confession, separation, years
of pain, even ultimate peace. Life, being larger than logic,
might contain a road he had not foreseen.

His hand relaxed.

Then imagination presented their faces—their terror, humiliation, pleading; the interminable
afterward; the three of them imprisoned in knowledge. His courage,
which had resisted murder, failed before life.

"No," he whispered.

He raised the revolver.

At that final moment he felt neither hatred nor exaltation.
He thought, with a tenderness almost domestic, that his wife
disliked sudden noises. Then he remembered she was not there.

His head was perfectly clear. His face was bright and
tranquil.

The report disturbed the quiet office, startled a horse in
the street below, and sent a little shower of soot
down upon the ashes in the grate.

At half past seven two places waited at Randolph's table,
and Henry Stockton, standing beside the library fire, listened for
the opening of the door.

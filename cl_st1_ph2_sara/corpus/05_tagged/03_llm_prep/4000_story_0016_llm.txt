There used to hang in the office of a cattle
company at Dodge a picture which none of us ever
forgot. It showed a battle going on in a narrow
place between rocks, with smoke laying low over the ground
and the mounted men crowded together where there did not
seem room enough for a rabbit to turn. In the
foreground sat a soldier whose leg had been shattered. He
could not ride with the others, and his rifle was
gone; but he had dragged himself beside a spring and
was filling canteens for the men who came back thirsty
from the fighting.

Miller said the painter had put more sense into that
one-legged man than into all the generals in the picture.

"A man ain't out of the fight," he told us,
"so long as he can find something that needs doing."

We thought of that picture afterward when Jack Martin left
us.

Little Jack had been thrown from a wagon when he
was a child, and the hurt had left one leg
drawn up and his back turned somewhat to the right,
as if he were forever edging past somebody in a
narrow doorway. He could ride, though not far or fast,
and he could handle a team if the horses had
religion. When they had none, Jack generally wound up with
one line around his neck and the other under his
sound knee. He could not talk plain, either. The words
seemed to collect behind his tongue, and when he forced
them out they came by fits, the first half too
quick and the last half scarcely there at all.

But he could cook.

To say that Jack could cook is like saying a
spring can make water. It does not tell the useful
part of it. He could make sour-dough bread rise in
a norther, fry meat tender from a steer that had
worn itself to harness, and turn canned tomatoes, bacon grease,
dried apples, and hope into a supper fit to make
a man forgive his enemies. He knew how much coffee
Mouse could drink before Mouse began to sing, and he
knew Coon needed feeding before being told bad news. If
a hand came in wet from night herd, Jack had
a dry shirt warming behind the stove. If one of
us was sick, Jack set a kettle going and sat
up, though no one ever remembered asking him.

He was the smallest man in the outfit and had
more authority than Miller.

Miller was foreman and could send us forty miles after
strays in sleet. Jack could look into the flour barrel,
shake his head, and send Miller to the store.

About the ranch there are jobs a man gets thanked
for and jobs which, if they are done properly, nobody
notices. Jack did mostly the second kind. The coffee was
there before daylight. The branding irons were hot when wanted.
There was always a rag for a cut hand, axle
grease for the wagon, a bit of sugar laid by
for a man with fever, and a place near the
stove where frozen gloves might thaw without burning. We never
considered how these things happened. We merely supposed the sun
rose, cattle bawled, and Jack had breakfast ready.

Then Oklahoma opened.

For months every drifting paper had something in it about
the new land. Men were to line up at noon
on the appointed day and race in to claim a
quarter-section. There was talk of black soil deep enough to
bury a horse standing, of creeks that ran all summer,
of timber along the bottoms, and corn so tall a
rider could lose his hat in it. Most range reports
gain two feet of grass and an inch of rain
every time they pass through a saloon, and we allowed
Oklahoma was no different. Still, settlers went south and east
in wagons until every trail seemed to carry a stove,
a feather bed, three children, and a yellow dog.

Jack listened to all of it.

He had a little money saved. We knew that because
he never spent any except for garden seed, needles, and
a striped candy which he pretended was for the cook
but which usually found its way into our coat pockets.
One evening, after supper, he came to Miller where we
were sitting outside the bunkhouse oiling ropes.

"M-M-Miller," he said, "I'm g-going."

No one spoke at first. Coon kept drawing a rag
down his rope, though he had reached the end of
it. Mouse looked toward the cookhouse as if Jack might
have meant he was going there.

"Going where?" Miller asked.

"T-to the new c-country. I got a m-map. Big T-Turkey
Creek. There's a p-piece with t-timber and water."

He brought out a folded land-office map, worn white at
the creases. There was a pencil cross on it and
notes in Jack's cramped hand: creek, rise, cottonwood, road. He
had studied the tract from survey reports and from what
freighters had told him. It was not foolish dreaming. Jack
never let a pie burn while he dreamed.

"You aim to file?" Miller said.

Jack nodded.

"And live there?"

Another nod.

"Alone?"

At that Jack's face reddened, though we did not understand
why until much later.

"I can m-make it," he said.

That was all. Jack was going from us.

We took the news as men take a hard fall—first
lying still to find which part is broken. Then everybody
talked at once. Mouse declared the soil would poison potatoes.
Coon said every settler south of the line had fever.
Baldy Evans remembered hearing that Big Turkey Creek went dry
nine months in the year and flooded the other three.
We proved Oklahoma to be a country wholly unfit for
man, beast, garden truck, or a crippled cook.

Jack listened and smiled.

The smile finished us. He knew what we were doing.

Miller folded the map and handed it back.

"If you're set on it," he said, "you'll need a
man to make the run."

Jack's bad leg could never stand the pounding, and his
small horse, Jenny, had about as much chance in a
land race as a milk cow in the Kentucky Derby.
Yet the law permitted a claimant to send an authorized
rider to stake and hold his tract until he arrived.
There were rules against one man claiming for another, rules
against entering before noon, and rules against nearly everything the
human mind could devise. Most were obeyed when a marshal
happened to be looking.

"We'll send the lightest rider on the fastest horse," Miller
said.

Then we began a campaign.

Every horse on the place was discussed as solemnly as
if the Government had asked us to provide a cavalry
mount for the President. Coon favored a long-legged bay called
Deacon, because Deacon could run all day and had once
jumped a branding-pen fence with Coon attached. Mouse stood for
the gray mare, Dove, who was quick off her feet
but had a habit of turning around whenever a man
drew his pistol. Baldy argued for his own sorrel until
we reminded him the sorrel had not beaten anything faster
than a feed wagon in six years.

The best horse was the coyote horse.

His proper name had been Comet, but nobody used it.
He was a narrow, dun-colored animal with black down his
legs and a way of looking over his shoulder that
made him appear guilty before anything happened. He could leave
a standing start like a coyote going under a fence.
He had wind, hard feet, and no affection for mankind.
Only one man in the outfit could ride him without
turning a straight road into a public discussion, and that
was young Billy Hart.

Billy weighed one hundred and twenty pounds after dinner and
had the sense to keep quiet around horses. He was
nineteen, freckled, and shy until trouble started. Then he became
pleased with himself.

"You reckon you can put Jack's stake in before them
farmers get there?" Coon asked him.

Billy looked at the map.

"I can if the creek stays where it's marked."

"That's no answer."

"It's enough answer for a horse that don't listen to
speeches."

We spent the next week preparing as if we intended
to annex the territory. Miller sent a man over every
part of the route that could be traveled before the
opening. We learned where the ravines lay, where the wire
fences had been cut, where soft ground would bog a
horse, and which draw offered shade if Billy had to
wait near the line. Jack sharpened a hatchet, painted his
name on two stakes, packed food in a flour sack,
and said little.

On the morning before the opening we started. Jack rode
Jenny in a spring-seated buggy with his blankets, tools, seed,
and enough cooking gear to establish civilization. The rest of
us went horseback. We were not all needed, but that
never prevented an outfit from attending to its own business.

The border country looked like a fairground without the music.
Wagons stood in rows. Horses were tied to wheels and
trees. Men slept under canvas, argued over maps, traded saddles,
watered teams, and lied about how fast they could travel.
Women cooked over little fires while children chased each other
among the vehicles. Every claimant believed he alone knew of
some perfect tract that no other living man had noticed.

At dawn on opening day, Billy led the coyote horse
out. Jack had made him a breakfast of steak, eggs,
hot cakes, and coffee.

"If he eats all that," Coon said, "the horse'll be
carrying the heaviest rider."

Billy ate it.

Near noon we moved toward the line. Mounted men stretched
away in both directions. There were racers on blooded horses,
farm boys on plow teams, old soldiers, storekeepers, gamblers, and
one preacher riding a mule. Behind them stood wagons loaded
so high that the women on the seats appeared to
be navigating furniture.

Miller checked Billy's cinches himself.

"Don't kill the horse," he said.

"No."

"Don't kill yourself."

"No."

"And don't let any man sit on Jack's land."

Billy glanced at him. "That the order they come in?"

Miller slapped the coyote horse on the neck. "Use your
judgment."

The signal came from a soldier's carbine.

The line broke.

There is no way to tell exactly what a land
rush looks like, because no man sees more than the
dust nearest him. Horses jumped sideways, wagons lurched, men yelled,
wheels struck wheels, and one old fellow fired his pistol
into the ground until his mule departed without waiting for
further instructions. Billy and the coyote horse went out together
like a startled deer. In ten seconds we saw only
the black tip of the horse's tail. Then dust swallowed
him.

We waited.

Waiting has always been the longest job in cattle work.
A man can ride night herd in sleet and think
himself comfortable compared with sitting beside a trail wondering if
somebody else has done what he cannot help doing. Jack
held the lines over Jenny and stared southeast. His face
did not move.

Toward evening we took the buggy through. The country behind
the first wave already looked occupied. Stakes stood on rises.
Wagons were turning off toward claims. Men with axes attacked
trees as though personally insulted by them. At every crossing
somebody shouted for directions, and nobody knew any.

We reached Big Turkey Creek after dark.

Billy found us by firing three shots. He was sitting
beside a little fire with the coyote horse tied short
to a cottonwood. Jack's painted stake stood at the edge
of the clearing.

Billy had made it.

Then we saw his face.

"What's wrong?" Miller asked.

"There's a cabin half a mile up the creek."

Nobody answered.

"It's been there a spell," Billy continued. "Roofed, chimney built,
patch broke for garden. Fellow says he owns this quarter.
Says he entered after noon from the east."

A man who slipped into the territory before the legal
opening was called a sooner. Some hid in gullies, some
in timber, and some lived for weeks under wagon sheets,
waiting to spring up at noon and swear they had
crossed honestly. The law was against them, but law on
a new claim is like a paper umbrella in a
hailstorm. It may be respectable and still fail to keep
off the weather.

Miller kicked dirt over Billy's fire.

"Show us."

The cabin was better than we expected and therefore worse.
It stood on the exact tract Jack had chosen, near
a spring under the bank. A stack of split wood
leaned against one wall, and beans were already coming up
behind a brush fence. No man could have built all
that since noon unless he had worked faster than sin
travels.

The sooner came to the door holding a rifle. He
was a big, slack-faced fellow with red whiskers and boots
that did not match.

"This is my land," he said.

Miller looked at the garden, the weathered woodpile, and the
ashes about the chimney.

"Been making mighty good time."

"I crossed legal."

"From where?"

"East."

Miller pointed east. Big Turkey Creek made a loop there,
and beyond it lay a bluff that a goat would
have studied before climbing.

"You bring that cabin over the bluff in your pocket?"

The man raised the rifle a little.

We spread without being told. It was not a threatening
move exactly, but it gave him more of us to
consider.

"I filed my notice," he said.

"Where?"

"That's my business."

"No," Miller replied, "your business is packing."

The sooner swore he would die first. Men often offer
to die when they believe nobody intends to take them
up. Miller regarded him quietly, then turned to us.

"Boys," he said, "we ought to respect a man's choice."

We knew Miller. The sooner did not.

Coon produced a rope. Baldy found a cottonwood limb. Mouse,
who had once traveled with a medicine show and never
recovered from it, began speaking in the mournful voice of
a circuit judge.

"Prisoner at the bar," he said, "you have been found
guilty of arriving before you got here, concealing property in
plain sight, and taking the homestead of the best cook
between Texas and creation. Have you anything to say why
sentence should not proceed?"

The sooner's face changed.

"You can't hang me!"

"That," Coon said while shaping a loop, "is what we
come to determine."

Jack tried to speak, but Miller touched his arm. We
all understood the performance. Miller meant to scare the man,
not hurt him. Still, a rope has an ugly look
in lantern light, and we did not laugh.

We took the rifle away. The sooner fought enough to
prove his concern but not enough to get damaged. Coon
settled the loop over his shoulders rather than his neck
while keeping the knot where the prisoner could feel it.
Baldy tossed the other end over the limb.

Mouse asked if he preferred a hymn.

The sooner said he preferred justice.

"That ain't on the program," Mouse told him.

Miller stood near the tree with his watch out.

"You get one minute to remember where you left your
wagon," he said.

The man remembered in twenty seconds. He also remembered two
horses hidden in a draw, some bacon, a bedroll, a
shovel, and a private understanding with a land clerk. At
forty seconds he offered to leave the territory permanently if
permitted to keep his boots.

Miller waited until the full minute had run.

"Take the rope off him."

The sooner did not wait for help. He tore himself
loose, caught one of his horses, and departed bareback, leaving
the other animal and most of his possessions. We shouted
after him that he could return for his property in
daylight. He did not.

Jack stood looking at the cabin.

"M-Miller," he said at last, "that was p-plum foolish."

"It was," Miller agreed. "But we couldn't hang him sensible."

We slept on Jack's claim that night.

The next morning Miller sent two men to the nearest
land office with Billy, the signed authorization, and every witness
who could swear to the time of the run. The
sooner never appeared to contest it. Whether he had been
frightened clear to Arkansas or merely found another unguarded tract,
we never learned.

Jack owned the quarter on Big Turkey Creek.

The cabin was not good enough for him. That was
our next conclusion.

It had one room, a dirt floor, a chimney that
smoked inward, and a roof low enough to comb Coon's
hair whenever he stood straight. There were cracks between the
logs through which a man might observe weather without opening
the door. The spring was excellent, the creek had water,
and the bottom soil turned black under a spade. Jack
walked over every acre with his uneven gait, touching trees
and looking at the ground. His grin was like a
cat eating paste.

We had planned to remain two days. We stayed eleven.

Cattle business could survive our absence, Miller said, though from
the way he said it one might have supposed the
beef-shipping season had been established mainly to inconvenience Jack. We
cut logs from the creek bottom and dragged them with
the spare horses. Billy and Baldy notched corners. Mouse mixed
clay for the chimney and put so much straw in
it that Coon said we were building a haystack with
a fireplace. Miller laid out the cabin twice the size
of the first and added a lean-to for tools.

Jack objected.

"I d-don't need all that."

"You'll grow," Coon told him.

Jack looked down at his crooked body.

It took Coon a moment to understand. Then he waved
both hands.

"Not that way, you blamed little rooster. I mean prosperous."

We set the new cabin on a rise above flood
level. It had a puncheon floor, two windows, a loft,
a stone fireplace, and a porch running across the front.
The old sooner's cabin became a stable and storeroom. Miller
insisted on a separate kitchen at the rear, saying no
man who had cooked for cowboys ought to spend the
rest of his life sleeping in the smell of bacon
unless he chose to.

When rain stopped the outside work, we made furniture. None
of us was a cabinetmaker, but cowhands are seldom checked
by ignorance. We built a table that could have supported
a branding chute, benches that leaned in friendly fashion toward
whoever sat on them, a bedstead, shelves, pegs, and a
rocking chair. The chair was Mouse's design. It rocked beautifully
but also traveled forward, so that a man beginning by
the fireplace ended at the door after fifteen minutes.

Jack planted his garden before the roof was finished.

He put in potatoes, beans, onions, squash, sweet corn, cabbages,
tomatoes, and flowers. The flowers surprised us. He had carried
the seed in a folded paper at the bottom of
his chest.

"What's them?" Baldy asked.

"Z-zinnias."

"Can you eat 'em?"

"No."

"Can cattle?"

"No."

"What good are they?"

Jack looked toward the cabin.

"M-make it home."

That silenced Baldy, which was one of Jack's lesser miracles.

Neighbors began to appear. A family from Missouri had claimed
the quarter north. A widow with two grown sons settled
west, and a German farmer with six daughters established himself
beyond the ridge. They lent tools and accepted coffee. Everybody
was poor in possessions and rich in plans. Before the
week ended they had arranged to share a breaking plow,
help raise barns, and send for a schoolteacher as soon
as there were enough children to torment one.

We stocked Jack as well as we could. Miller left
a milk cow, though the ownership of that cow had
been debated since she wandered into our herd with a
brand resembling a flowerpot. We called her the Flower Pot
cow and had never found anybody willing to claim the
design. Coon gave Jack two pigs acquired in a trade
which Coon could not explain without changing the subject. Billy
left a shotgun. Baldy surrendered a good ax and spent
the afternoon insisting it was only a loan.

We also left the extra horse abandoned by the sooner.
Miller posted notices, sent word to the marshal, and held
the animal thirty days according to custom. No claimant came.
Jack named him Justice, which pleased Mouse more than was
healthy.

On our last morning Jack cooked breakfast in his new
kitchen. He had ham, biscuits, fried potatoes, gravy, and coffee
strong enough to float a cinch ring. We ate on
the porch while sunlight came across his field. The zinnias
had not yet broken ground, but Jack knew where each
row lay.

Nobody wanted to start.

We tightened cinches that needed no tightening and examined shoes
sound enough to reach California. Coon warned Jack against fever,
floods, grasshoppers, claim jumpers, chimney fires, dishonest preachers, and marriageable
widows. That last warning made Jack blush again.

Miller gave him a small leather bag containing money collected
from the outfit.

Jack would not take it.

Miller set it on the table.

"It ain't charity," he said. "It's wages we forgot to
pay."

"You n-never forgot wages."

"Then call it damages for the food we've et."

Jack shook his head.

Miller's voice softened.

"Keep it for when we come visit. It'll take money
to feed us."

That argument prevailed.

When we rode away, Jack stood at the gate with
one hand on the post. The cabin, the new fence,
the black garden rows, and the Flower Pot cow made
a small settlement behind him. He looked little enough against
all that country, but not helpless. No man who had
a roof, seed in the ground, water at his door,
and friends on every horizon could properly be called helpless.

Still, the ranch felt wrong without him.

Our new cook was a man named Sanders who had
once worked in a railroad camp and considered grease a
seasoning. He could cook beans three ways: burned, raw, and
both. He put sugar in the cornbread and blamed the
altitude when biscuits failed. No one mistreated him, because Miller
would not allow cruelty to a man merely for lacking
talent; but when Sanders turned his back, we looked at
one another with the grief of children who have come
home to find their mother absent.

Coon became thin. He said it was sorrow. We said
it was self-defense.

Letters came from Jack, short and labored but full of
news. The corn was up. The Flower Pot cow had
calved. A flood reached the lower fence but not the
cabin. He had helped a neighbor put up hay. The
schoolhouse was started. Justice had kicked the stable door down.
There was never anything about loneliness.

In the fall, Jack appeared at the ranch.

He came in a new buggy behind Jenny and wore
a dark coat too warm for the day. He had
gained flesh in his face, and his hands were brown
and hard from field work. We ran out as if
he carried pardons.

Sanders surrendered the kitchen without a fight. Jack cooked supper,
and for an hour there was no conversation except requests
to pass things. He had brought jars of tomatoes, green
corn, onions, cucumbers, butter, and a loaf of bread baked
that morning. Coon ate until his eyes lost their hunted
look.

Afterward Jack asked Miller to walk down by the corrals.

We saw them go. Naturally we followed at a distance,
not from curiosity but because the evening air was wholesome.

They stopped by the branding-pen. Jack spoke too low for
us to hear. Miller turned and looked at him. Then
he seized Jack by both shoulders and shouted loud enough
to start the horses.

"You little son of a gun!"

We came running honestly after that.

Jack was going to be married.

Her name was Ellen Parker. She was the widow's niece
from the western claim, a brown-haired woman who had come
from Kansas in July to help with harvest and intended
to teach at the new school. She had eaten at
Jack's table, admired his garden, borrowed books from him, and
discovered what we had known for years—that halting speech does
not mean a halting mind, and a bent body may
carry a straighter spirit than a sound one.

The wedding was set for the next Saturday.

"Why didn't you tell us sooner?" Billy demanded.

Jack tried three times to answer while we pounded his
back, shook his hands, and shouted questions.

"I—I wanted to t-tell Miller f-first."

Miller wiped his nose on his sleeve and pretended dust
had got into it.

The next morning cattle work ceased.

There were beeves to gather for shipment, fences to ride,
and a set of strays reported near the salt flats.
Miller listened to the list and said none of it
was likely to marry before Monday.

"We're attending this wedding," he announced. "And we ain't going
empty-handed."

Then came the gathering of presents.

Cowboys on monthly wages are not rich, but poverty never
prevented us from being extravagant with property whose title was
uncertain. Mouse rode thirty miles to a store and bought
a clock with blue flowers painted on the face. The
clock gained seventeen minutes a day, but Mouse said a
new family ought to get ahead. Coon contributed a silver-plated
coffee pot and six spoons, three of which matched. Billy
bought Ellen a bolt of red cloth, not knowing what
women made from cloth or how much one required. He
purchased twelve yards to be safe.

Baldy gave the ax he had formerly claimed to have
loaned.

Sanders, relieved perhaps by the prospect of Jack's permanent absence,
baked a wedding cake. It leaned southeast, but he braced
the low side with candy hearts.

Miller produced a new cookstove from the ranch storeroom. None
of us knew there had been a cookstove there.

"That belong to the company?" Coon asked.

"It has belonged to the company without doing the company
any good for six years," Miller said. "Now the company
is going to do something Christian and don't know it."

We loaded the stove, cake, presents, blankets, sacks of flour,
coffee, sugar, and canned fruit into two wagons. A fat
yearling without a readable brand also accompanied us in a
manner we considered temporary until supper.

The trip to Big Turkey Creek took most of two
days. We cleaned ourselves at the last stream, shaved with
one razor among twelve men, and put on such dress
clothes as could be assembled. Coon wore a black coat
inherited from a taller man. Mouse had a collar that
held his head like a brace. Billy's boots squeaked, and
every squeak made him redder because he had heard of
the German farmer's six daughters.

We reached Jack's place near sunset.

For a moment we thought we had come wrong.

The cabin had grown. A second room stood at the
east end, freshly chinked, with glass in both windows. There
was a barn, a chicken house, two rail pens, and
a well sweep behind the kitchen. Corn stood stacked in
the field. Pumpkins lay yellow near the fence, and the
zinnias had made such a blaze beside the porch that
even Baldy recognized their use.

Jack came through the gate wearing a white shirt.

Behind him stood Ellen.

She was not pretty in the foolish picture-card fashion, but
she had steady gray eyes and a mouth ready to
laugh. She wore a blue dress and held out both
hands to Miller.

"So this is the outfit," she said.

"No, ma'am," Miller replied. "This is just the respectable part.
The rest are hiding behind the wagons."

That brought us forward.

Jack had told her about every one of us. She
knew Mouse had designed the traveling rocker, that Coon had
brought the pigs, that Billy made the run, and that
Baldy's ax was the best ax ever loaned permanently. She
thanked each man as if his gift had been chosen
in a city store and carried across the plains for
her alone.

When she saw the cookstove, she looked at Jack.

He looked at Miller.

"M-Miller," he said, "that's the r-ranch stove."

"Used to be," Miller answered. "It got dissatisfied."

Neighbors arrived until the clearing filled with wagons, saddle horses,
children, dogs, women carrying covered dishes, and men bearing fiddles,
benches, lanterns, and advice. The wedding was to be at
noon next day, but frontier celebrations started when people arrived
and ended when they could travel again.

We slaughtered the yearling down by the creek and asked
no questions likely to spoil the flavor. Jack directed the
cooking as a general who never knew defeat. Trenches were
dug for fires. Iron pots swung from green poles. Women
worked in the kitchen, though Ellen had to order Jack
out twice because he forgot he was the bridegroom and
began seasoning the beans.

By midnight there were pies on every shelf, bread under
cloths, meat roasting over coals, coffee pots lined along the
hearth, and children asleep in nests of coats. The fiddlers
tuned in the new room. Mouse attempted to dance with
the Missouri farmer's wife and traveled forward like his rocking
chair until stopped by a wall.

The ceremony next day was short. A preacher from twelve
miles east stood on the porch. Jack and Ellen faced
him with the zinnias behind them. We filled the yard,
scrubbed and solemn, hats in hand.

When the preacher asked Jack if he took Ellen for
his lawful wife, Jack's answer caught.

We saw the strain come into his face. In excitement
his speech was worse, and the silence around him made
it harder. He tried once, then again.

Ellen slipped her hand into his.

"Take your time, Jack," she said.

He looked at her and smiled.

"I d-do," he said clearly.

There was not a man among us who would have
traded those two words for a governor's speech.

When the preacher pronounced them husband and wife, we yelled.
Pistols went off. Horses pulled at their ties. The Flower
Pot cow ran through a patch of late cabbage, and
Coon chased her in his inherited black coat while Ellen
laughed against Jack's shoulder.

Then we ate.

Jack had planned for a hundred and fed near two
hundred. There was roast beef, pork, chicken, ham, beans, corn,
potatoes, pickles, tomatoes, bread, biscuits, preserves, pies, cakes, coffee, and
a yellow pudding whose origin nobody discovered but whose disappearance
was complete. Sanders's leaning cake stood in the middle of
the table. Jack and Ellen cut it together. The candy-heart
braces failed, and the upper half slid into Mouse's lap.

Mouse said that was why he had worn dark trousers.

After dinner the benches were cleared away. Two fiddlers and
a man with a banjo climbed onto the porch. The
first tune brought every young person in the settlement to
the floor, and the second brought the old ones. We
danced in the new room, on the porch, and finally
in the yard where lanterns hung from poles.

Billy danced with three of the German farmer's daughters and
became so confused he called each one Mary. None was
named Mary. Coon performed a jig that tore the sole
from his boot. Baldy courted a widow until she asked
whether he owned land, whereupon he remembered pressing ranch duties.
Miller danced once with Ellen and once with the Missouri
farmer's grandmother, giving equal care to both.

Jack could not dance in the usual way. His bad
leg would not carry him through the figures. For a
while he stood by the door, smiling and calling tunes.

Then Ellen came to him.

The fiddlers slowed. She put one hand on his shoulder
and took his hand with the other. They moved in
a small circle, Jack leaning on her only a little,
she matching her step to his uneven one. Nobody made
a joke. We watched until Mouse shouted that the bridegroom
was monopolizing the prettiest woman present.

That broke the spell, and the dance went on.

Night settled cold over the creek. Fires were built in
the yard, coffee renewed, babies laid in wagons, and horses
fed from Jack's new hay. The moon came up over
the field and silvered the corn shocks. Every window of
the cabin shone. Music spilled through the open door, mixed
with laughter, boots on puncheon boards, children's cries, and the
steady clatter from the kitchen.

We danced reels, quadrilles, and figures invented on the spot.
Coon called one set and became lost in his own
instructions. Mouse sang a sorrowful ballad about a sailor who
drowned in a country without water. Ellen's aunt played the
banjo after the regular player fell asleep. Jack kept food
before everybody, though we repeatedly reminded him he was not
hired help.

"Can't h-help it," he said. "F-folks got to eat."

That was Jack.

Near three in the morning Miller and I stood outside
by the gate. We could see Jack through the window.
He was carrying a pot of coffee in one hand
while Ellen followed with cups. Every few steps somebody stopped
him, shook his hand, or pulled him into a joke.
The cabin he had once said was too big could
scarcely hold his friends.

Miller watched a long time.

"You remember how he looked when we rode away?" I
asked.

"I remember."

"Didn't seem possible then."

Miller rubbed his chin.

"Most things don't seem possible till somebody smaller than us
does 'em."

That was as close to a sermon as Miller ever
came. He was a creedless old Christian who mistrusted prayer
spoken too loud but would ride all night to help
a sick stranger.

At daylight the fiddlers tried to quit. We threatened them
with breakfast and persuaded them to play one more tune.
That tune became four. Finally the eastern sky turned pink,
roosters began reporting from every direction, and Jack called us
to eat again.

We sat wherever there was room—at the long table, on
benches, on wood blocks, along the porch, and in the
grass silvered with frost. Jack served ham and eggs, fried
potatoes, biscuits, gravy, and coffee. Ellen moved beside him, and
they worked together without crowding, as if they had been
married ten years instead of one night.

Coon raised his cup.

"To Jack," he said, then stalled because direct feeling always
embarrassed him.

Mouse rescued him.

"To Jack," Mouse said, "who has traded twenty useless cowboys
for one good woman and made a profit."

We cheered that.

Jack tried to answer, but the shouting began again, and
Ellen told him not to trouble.

By midmorning the neighbors started home. Wagons rolled out one
by one. Children waved from piles of quilts. The preacher
departed carrying half a ham and denying he had asked
for it. The German farmer collected five daughters and left
Billy searching nervously for the sixth until she was found
asleep in the loft with two little girls.

We remained to wash dishes, stack benches, repair a gate,
and set the new stove. No one suggested leaving until
Miller said cattle did not grow brands merely because we
wished them to.

Our horses were brought up. Jack and Ellen stood together
at the gate. The zinnias had been touched by frost
but still showed red and gold beside them.

This parting was not like the first.

Before, we had left Jack alone on a tract marked
by one painted stake, with a raw cabin, a few
garden rows, and more courage than prospects. Now smoke rose
from a sound chimney. Hens scratched by the barn. Corn
filled the crib. Neighbors' roofs showed along the creek. Ellen's
hand rested on Jack's arm, and he stood as straight
as his body allowed.

Miller shook his hand last.

"If you need us—"

"I k-know," Jack said.

That was enough.

We rode north in a loose bunch, tired, stiff, and
satisfied. Coon had one boot tied with rawhide. Mouse's blue-flowered
clock, forgotten in the excitement, ticked inside a saddlebag and
gained time with every mile. Billy carried a red ribbon
given him by one of the German girls, though he
never learned which one. Sanders worried aloud that the wedding
cake had injured his reputation. Baldy assured him it had
not changed it.

At the ridge we turned for one more look.

Jack and Ellen were still by the gate. The cabin
stood bright in the morning sun, and smoke drifted low
toward Big Turkey Creek. What had once been open land
and a sooner's poor hiding place had become a home.

Then Miller waved his hat, and all of us did
the same.

We had missed two days of cattle work, given away
a company stove, consumed an unrecorded yearling, ruined one boot,
exhausted three musicians, and danced the shine off Jack's new
floor.

Taking it altogether, we reckoned we had done the occasion
justice.

# THE NINE BAILEYS OF TUSCULUM

## I

This is the proem.

Jack Winfield, owner of twelve thousand acres, eight thousand cattle,
four hundred horses, three houses, one black beard, and no
wife, had been refused by Miss Genevieve St. Clair of
St. Louis.

Miss St. Clair was visiting the military post, where she
had produced a commotion out of all proportion to her
weight. She had blue eyes, seven trunks, and a parasol
upon whose ivory handle she cut a notch for every
rejected suitor. Jack's notch was the thirty-seventh.

The thirty-seventh was final.

"You are very kind," she had told him, in a
moonlit corner of the colonel's veranda, "but I could never
live among cattle."

"Could fence 'em off from the house," Jack suggested.

"And Indians."

"They don't come much."

"And cowboys."

"I could discharge the boys."

"And mountains."

Jack stared at her.

"No," he said slowly, "I couldn't move the mountains."

Thus the negotiations terminated.

On the following morning Jack rode forty miles to his
ranch, entered the office where Dick Cutter was making up
the monthly accounts, flung his hat at a peg, missed
it, and announced:

"I'm going to marry."

Dick Cutter did not look up.

"Who?"

"I don't know yet."

"That may hinder you."

"It won't. I have decided on the principle. The details
can come afterward."

Dick laid down his pencil and twirled his blond moustache.
He had known Jack for eleven years and understood that
a fixed idea in the rancher's mind was like a
bull in a kitchen: one might deplore it, but one
did not reason with it.

"Genevieve?" he asked.

"Busted."

"Ah."

"She says Montana is too rough."

"She has discernment."

Jack tugged his beard.

"Dick, I am thirty-five. You are thirty-seven. Reynolds is forty.
Pete Murchison is forty-two, though he says thirty-nine. There are
fourteen solvent bachelors within sixty miles of this ranch, and
not enough unmarried women to fill a spring wagon."

"There's Widow Prout."

"She has five children."

"A bachelor's vagueness," said Dick, "usually begins at two and
becomes complete at five."

"There's the school-teacher," Jack continued, "but she's promised to the
deputy sheriff. There's Mrs. Nolan's niece, but she squints and
owns a pug dog. We need an importation."

"Of women?"

"Of wives."

Dick lit a cigar.

"By thunder," Jack cried, bringing his fist down on the
ledger, "why not?"

"Because women are not Durham heifers, for one reason."

"No. They're scarcer."

That afternoon Jack summoned the neighboring bachelors. They came on
horseback and in buckboards, suspicious but hopeful. Pete Murchison wore
a flowered vest. Reynolds had oiled his hair. Old Major
Blythe, who had not addressed a marriageable woman since Appomattox,
put on gloves.

Jack explained the shortage and the proposed remedy.

"There must be towns in the East," he said, "where
respectable young women are wasting their lives for lack of
opportunity. We have opportunity. We have land, money, health, and
loneliness. It is a plain commercial imbalance."

The major objected to "commercial."

"Call it a romantic imbalance, then," said Jack. "The facts
remain."

Dick, sitting on the window-sill, suddenly removed his cigar.

"I know a family," he said.

Fourteen heads turned.

"In Tusculum, New York. The Baileys."

"How many?" demanded Jack.

"Nine daughters."

There was a silence such as follows the discovery of
gold.

"Unmarried?" whispered Reynolds.

"Every one."

"Good-looking?"

"When I saw them."

"When was that?"

"Eleven years ago."

Pete Murchison groaned. "They may be grandmothers."

"They were children then."

This restored the market.

Dick described the Bailey family. Mr. Bailey had been a
surveyor, descended—according to Mr. Bailey—from a governor, two judges, and
a gentleman who had declined a baronetcy for reasons never
made clear. Mrs. Bailey came of equally ancient but less
talkative stock. Their daughters possessed classical names, genteel manners, musical
instruction, and a curious family resemblance to the female profile
on an Indian Head cent.

"Nine Cent-Girls," said Jack admiringly. "Dick, you go east."

"I?"

"You know them."

"I knew them when Clytie put frogs in my boots."

"So much the better. You have common memories."

"I haven't corresponded with them."

"You can resume."

"Resume what?"

"Whatever you stopped."

By sunset the venture had been capitalized. Jack subscribed five
hundred dollars; the others, according to fortune and desperation, contributed
sums down to thirty-seven dollars and a silver watch. Dick
was appointed envoy, escort, and general manager of the Bailey
Hegira.

His instructions were simple: offer Mr. Bailey employment surveying the
new irrigation lands; describe Montana without mentioning blizzards; invite the
entire family west; and avoid making any promises of matrimony
that could be introduced as evidence in court.

"Use tact," said Major Blythe.

"Use photographs," said Reynolds.

"Use my photograph," said Pete.

"Don't," said Jack.

## II

Tusculum stood beside a narrow New York river and considered
itself old.

It had elms, white porticoes, mossy gravestones, six churches, and
one factory which the best families pretended not to see.
Its young men went away to Albany, Boston, New York,
or the grave; its young women remained behind, preserving the
traditions and paying the mortgages.

Dick arrived in October, wearing a broad hat which made
boys follow him from the station. He left the hat
at the hotel and presented himself at the Bailey house
in Eastern black, though his boots betrayed him at every
step.

The house had once been white and important. Now the
paint peeled from the pillars, the gate hung by one
hinge, and a card in the parlor window announced:

MISS BAILEY
INSTRUCTION IN FRENCH, MUSIC, AND THE ELEMENTS

Below it another card read:

PLAIN AND ORNAMENTAL SEWING DONE WITH DISPATCH

Dick knocked.

A young woman opened the door. She had gray eyes,
brown hair, a composed mouth, and the cent-profile unmistakably. She
looked at him for three seconds.

"Mr. Cutter?"

"Euphrosyne?"

"Yes."

He had remembered her as a slim girl carrying two
babies and a Latin grammar. She was now thirty, perhaps;
not beautiful according to Miss St. Clair's militant standard, but
fine and quiet, with the look of one who had
learned to put weariness away before answering the bell.

"I should scarcely have recognized you," she said.

"I recognized you instantly."

This was untrue, but Eastern civilization returned to Dick in
layers.

She brought him into the parlor. There the Bailey sisters
appeared successively, as if summoned by a magician who owned
only one mold and varied the sizes. There were Aurora,
Thalia, Daphne, Irene, Eunice, Selene, Chloris, and Clytie. The youngest,
Clytie, was eighteen and had ink on one finger. The
others ranged upward with decorous intervals.

They greeted Dick with pleasure, curiosity, and restraint. He turned
from one similar face to another and lost the names
at once.

"Miss Aurora—"

"I am Daphne."

"Of course. You have changed."

"I was not born when you left Tusculum."

"Gad," said Dick.

Euphrosyne rescued him by pouring tea.

"And Mr. Bailey?" he asked. "I have business which may
interest him."

The room became still.

"Our father has been dead nine years," Euphrosyne said.

Dick set down his cup.

No one in Montana had allowed for death. It had
not entered the prospectus.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know. I ought to
have known."

"We had no reason to suppose the news would reach
you."

"And Mrs. Bailey?"

"Five years ago."

The plan, which had seemed formidable west of the Missouri,
now appeared indecent beside the mended curtains and carefully divided
loaf of cake. Dick saw the music sheets patched with
paper, the faded carpet turned at the edge, and nine
pairs of industrious hands. He could not announce that fourteen
bachelors had subscribed cash for the possible acquisition of these
ladies.

He remained for twenty minutes, spoke of the weather, confused
Chloris with Selene, and escaped.

At the Tusculum House he smoked three cigars consecutively, then
wrote Jack a telegram:

FATHER DEAD. MOTHER ALSO. NINE DAUGHTERS ALONE. SCHEME IMPOSSIBLE. RETURNING.

He carried it halfway to the telegraph office, tore it
up, and returned to the Baileys.

Euphrosyne received him alone.

"I fear," he began, "that my visit has been awkward."

"It has been pleasant."

"No, it hasn't. Not altogether. I came on business."

"Yes?"

"For your father."

"I understood that."

"A surveying position. In Montana."

Her face changed slightly.

"Would it have paid well?"

"Three thousand a year to begin."

"Three thousand!"

Dick understood then what three thousand meant in Tusculum. In
Montana it meant a moderately fortunate horse trade.

"He was an excellent surveyor," she said. "Even in his
last illness he hoped for work. But Tusculum has been
surveyed rather thoroughly."

"I'm a blundering fool."

"You could not know."

"And there was another part."

He walked to the window, returned to the stove, struck
a match, remembered the parlor, and extinguished it.

"The country needs women."

Euphrosyne raised her eyebrows.

"Respectable women," Dick added hastily. "There are some already, naturally.
Excellent women. But not many. The distances—well, the geography is
against sociability."

"I see."

"No, you don't. I haven't explained it."

He attempted the explanation. He described the ranches, the schools,
the climate, the towns, and the fourteen bachelors. He avoided
the word "wives" until it became visible in the room
by its very omission. Then he spoke it and turned
red.

"You mean," said Euphrosyne, "that we were to be invited
west in order that your friends might marry us?"

"When you put it that way, it sounds like a
livestock circular."

"Is there another way?"

"Not at present."

She looked toward the closed folding doors, beyond which eight
sisters were making a disciplined pretense of not listening.

"Are your friends honorable men?"

"Most of them."

"Most?"

"All. In essentials. Reynolds cheats at poker, but only when
excited. Pete drinks at weddings. Major Blythe fought for the
Confederacy, but that can hardly happen again."

"And they know nothing of us?"

"Only what I told them."

"What was that?"

"That you were respectable, accomplished, and looked like cents."

Euphrosyne laughed.

It was the first unweary sound Dick had heard in
the house, and it startled him more than a pistol
shot.

"I beg your pardon," he said.

"No. It is accurate. Father often said so."

The doors opened six inches, then closed.

Euphrosyne folded her hands.

"Mr. Cutter, our income last year was nine hundred and
twelve dollars. The mortgage interest was four hundred. Aurora and
Thalia teach. Daphne makes underclothing for persons who frequently neglect
to pay her. Irene paints place cards. Eunice keeps the
church accounts for nothing, because payment would offend the trustees.
The younger girls help as they can."

"I didn't come to pry."

"You came with an offer. I must tell you whether
we can refuse it."

"You can. Absolutely."

"Clytie wishes to study medicine. There is no money. Chloris
may obtain a position in the glove factory. Selene has
been promised six pupils in drawing, though none has yet
appeared. If we remain, they will work, grow tired, and
become—useful."

She said the word as if it were a sentence
pronounced by a judge.

"What do you want?" Dick asked gently.

"For them? A chance."

"For yourself?"

Euphrosyne's gray eyes rested upon him with a candor more
disturbing than tears.

"I ceased including myself in such questions some years ago."

Dick's cigar case snapped in his pocket.

Within an hour the eight listeners had entered, the plan
had been repeated, attacked, defended, wept over, and made practical.
Aurora objected that Montana contained Indians. Clytie hoped it did.
Thalia asked about schools. Daphne asked about church society. Chloris
wished to know whether the glove fashions were advanced. Selene
wanted mountains. Irene asked whether the men danced. Eunice wanted
a written statement of living expenses.

The expedition was settled before midnight.

Tusculum talked for three weeks.

Some said the Baileys were going west to teach. Others
said they had inherited mines. Mrs. Deacon Treadwell declared that
nine young women traveling under the protection of one unrelated
man represented the collapse of American womanhood. Her own unmarried
daughter called twice to inquire whether there might be room
for a tenth.

There was not.

Dick bought the tickets, checked the trunks, arranged the route,
and—upon being asked to select serviceable traveling garments—committed his great
mistake.

He ordered nine identical brown ulsters.

"They are sensible," he said.

"They are alike," said Euphrosyne.

"Easier to keep track of you."

"We are not sheep, Mr. Cutter."

"Certainly not. Sheep would never agree to wear ulsters."

## III

The forth-setting took place on a wet Monday.

Tusculum came to the station. The minister prayed. The newspaper
editor took notes. Mrs. Treadwell watched for scandal through a
lorgnette. Dick counted the sisters repeatedly, always obtaining either eight
or ten because strangers moved among them.

At last the nine brown ulsters stood in a row.

"They look," whispered the baggage-master, "like a female baseball nine."

The phrase was overheard, improved, and sent down the platform.

"What club, mister?" a boy called to Dick.

"No club."

"Where you playin'?"

"We are not playing."

"Which one pitches?"

"Go home."

The train pulled out amid cheers appropriate to a pennant
victory. Euphrosyne sat very straight.

"Mr. Cutter," she said, "did you hear?"

"Boys are idiots."

"Tusculum will hear."

"Tusculum hears everything eventually. It is its chief industry."

At Syracuse a hotel porter approached them.

"Eunnin', ladies! Opera house carriage this way."

"We are not actresses," said Euphrosyne.

"Minstrels, then?"

"No!"

"Temperance lecturers?"

Dick seized the man by the sleeve.

"They are private ladies."

The porter examined the nine ulsters.

"Very private," he said.

At Rochester the dining-room was full, though Dick could see
six vacant tables. The manager explained that theatrical companies must
enter by the side door.

"We are not a theatrical company," Dick said.

"Then you're a bicycle troupe."

"They haven't a bicycle among them."

"That's your business."

At the next station a newspaper reporter boarded the car.

"Which is Madame?"

"No Madame," said Dick.

"Who's your advance man?"

"Nobody."

"Name of attraction?"

"Bailey."

"Bailey's Belles," the reporter murmured, writing rapidly. "Refined novelty entertainment.
Nine sisters. Is it song, skirt-dance, or living pictures?"

Dick confiscated the notebook. The reporter produced another.

By Buffalo, the hegira had become famous.

A handbill advertising a comic opera happened to show nine
women in military coats. The resemblance was sufficient for a
crowd. Boys gathered at the station. Three cabmen fought for
the privilege of carrying the "company." A gentleman in a
pearl hat offered Dick a week at a theater in
Cleveland.

"We open Monday," he said. "You get sixty per cent
after expenses."

"Get out."

"Seventy."

"Out!"

"You've gotter strike while the racket's hot."

Dick struck—not the gentleman, though history would have excused it,
but the counter of the first hotel.

"Ten rooms," he demanded.

The clerk turned the register.

"Full."

There were twenty keys on the rack.

At the second hotel the clerk said all rooms had
been reserved for a convention of undertakers.

At the third, the proprietor leaned confidentially across the desk.

"We don't mind a good clean show," he said, "but
the Women's Moral Reform League meets here this week."

"They are not a show!"

"Then why are they dressed alike?"

Dick had no answer acceptable in Christian society.

Rain began. Trunks vanished. Clytie laughed until Euphrosyne reproved her.
Aurora cried. A crowd followed the sisters from hotel to
hotel, offering advice, applause, and peanuts. At last Dick secured
a shabby private boarding-house whose mistress agreed to admit "the
performers" by the kitchen entrance.

Euphrosyne refused.

"No," she said.

She stood beneath the dripping awning, pale with fatigue, her
brown ulster shining in the gaslight. Behind her the eight
cent-profiles were variously indignant, frightened, and amused.

"It is only for one night," Dick urged.

"We cannot remain here, and we cannot continue."

"Of course we can continue. The train leaves at nine."

"Not under these circumstances."

"They'll forget us west of Chicago."

"They have telegraphs west of Chicago."

"Unfortunately."

"We have been laughed at in three cities, denied lodging
in one, and advertised as an entertainment. We are nine
unmarried women traveling with one man. Mrs. Treadwell was right."

"No person has ever been less right than Mrs. Treadwell."

"The appearance is improper."

"The appearance is idiotic. That isn't our fault."

"It is mine. I accepted the arrangement."

"For your sisters."

"That does not alter it."

She turned to them.

"We shall return to Tusculum."

A silence fell. Even Clytie did not laugh.

Return meant the mortgage, the glove factory, the unpaid needlework,
and Mrs. Treadwell's triumph extending into eternity. Dick saw all
this. He also saw Euphrosyne, wet, resolute, and prepared to
bear the ruin alone because she had borne everything alone
for nine years.

He had spent three weeks trying not to want her.

He had failed before Syracuse.

"Suppose," he said, "the appearance were made proper?"

"How?"

"By a chaperone."

"We have none."

"We could have."

"Where?"

Dick removed his hat. Rain ran from his hair. His
heart behaved like an unbroken horse, but his mind, after
thirty-seven years of caution, abruptly resigned.

"THEN," he said, so loudly that a cab horse jumped,
"marry me."

Euphrosyne stared.

The sisters stared.

A small boy in the crowd cried, "Hooray!"

"Mr. Cutter—"

"I mean it. I've meant it for days, but I'm
slow. Jack says I can take forty minutes to decide
whether to cross a creek after my horse is already
on the other bank."

"This is absurd."

"Completely. That's why it may work."

"You propose to marry me so that I may chaperone
my sisters?"

"No. I propose because I want Y-O-U. The chaperoning is
an advantage to the general enterprise."

The pearl-hatted theater man, who had followed them, whispered, "Good
climax."

Dick wheeled upon him.

"If you speak again, I'll introduce you to Montana without
a ticket."

Euphrosyne's composure trembled.

"You scarcely know me."

"I knew you eleven years ago. I've known you three
weeks now. That makes eleven years and three weeks, which
is longer than most men know what they want."

"You came to find wives for your friends."

"I've found one for myself. Let them attend to their
own affairs."

"I am older than—"

"Than Clytie. So am I."

"I have no money."

"I have enough."

"I have eight sisters."

"I noticed."

"And if I say no?"

"We go back to Tusculum with you."

This answer decided her, though Dick never knew whether it
was love, gratitude, exhaustion, or horror of Mrs. Treadwell. In
successful marriages the original motive is commonly mixed and afterward
improved.

Euphrosyne looked at her sisters. Eight pairs of eyes implored
her with shameless unanimity.

"Yes," she said.

Dick caught his breath.

"When?"

"Before the nine-o'clock train."

The small boy shouted, "Extra!"

## IV

Buffalo possessed laws, ministers, rings, and newspaper reporters in abundance.
The license required the most time. The clerk doubted Euphrosyne's
classical name and made her spell it twice.

"E-u-p-h-r-o-s-y-n-e," she said.

"How do you pronounce it?"

"You do not need to pronounce it. You need only
write it."

The minister lived four blocks away and was eating supper.
He came in haste, fastening his collar as he walked.
The pearl-hatted manager offered his theater without charge. Dick declined.
The wedding took place in the boarding-house parlor between a
wax-flower case and a portrait of President Grant.

Aurora cried. Thalia cried because Aurora did. Daphne held the
license. Irene produced orange blossoms from some inexplicable feminine reserve.
Eunice calculated the minister's fee. Selene and Chloris stood witness.
Clytie kissed Dick before Euphrosyne did.

The ceremony required eleven minutes.

At eight forty-seven Mr. and Mrs. Richard Cutter, accompanied by
Mrs. Cutter's eight unmarried sisters, entered the station.

The crowd cheered.

The reporter ran beside them.

"What shall I call the story?"

"Call it nothing," said Dick.

"WESTERN MAN WEDS LEADER OF BAILEY'S BELLES?"

"She was never the leader of anything theatrical."

"LOVE LAUGHS AT HOTEL CLERKS?"

Euphrosyne paused.

"That," she said, "is nearly accurate."

The train left at nine.

Marriage transformed the party at once. Conductors became respectful. Hotel
clerks found rooms. Elderly ladies volunteered advice. What had been
scandal at eight o'clock was domestic virtue by midnight. Euphrosyne,
who had been an adventuress while unmarried, became a matron
upon saying two words before a minister, and might thereafter
escort any number of sisters across the continent without endangering
the Republic.

Dick sat beside her in the sleeping car.

"You regret it?" he asked.

"I have not yet had time."

"I can wait."

She looked at him and smiled.

"You have already waited eleven years and three weeks."

"That was before I caught on."

She put her hand in his.

Montana received the Nine Cent-Girls with a brass band, though
the band knew only two tunes and played both simultaneously.

Jack Winfield stood at the station in a new coat,
tugging his black beard. Behind him waited Reynolds, Pete Murchison,
Major Blythe, and the remaining investors, washed, shaved, perfumed, and
appalled by the realization that theories sometimes step off trains.

Dick descended first.

"Gentlemen," he said, "the enterprise has arrived."

Euphrosyne followed.

"My wife," Dick added.

Jack's mouth opened.

"Your what?"

"My wife."

"You were sent as agent!"

"I took a commission."

"Out of the shipment?"

Euphrosyne's gray eyes met Jack's.

"Mr. Winfield," she said, "if you refer to my sisters
as a shipment, I shall take them back east."

Jack removed his hat.

"Ma'am, I beg your pardon. Dick's marriage has confused the
bookkeeping."

Then Aurora stepped down.

She was tall, dark-haired, and less tired than Euphrosyne, with
a calm look which made Jack forget Miss Genevieve St.
Clair, her seven trunks, and the thirty-seventh notch.

"Which one is that?" he whispered.

"Aurora," Dick said.

"How old?"

"Ask her."

"Gad, no."

The others appeared. Names were exchanged and immediately lost. Pete
bowed to Thalia while addressing Daphne. Reynolds offered flowers to
Selene, discovered they were meant for Chloris, and gave them
to both. Major Blythe stood before Eunice in his gray
gloves and recited a speech which he had practiced for
two days, beginning, "Madam, as a representative of this vigorous
commonwealth—"

Clytie interrupted him.

"Are there really bears?"

"Yes, miss."

"Where?"

The major fell in love at once, though Clytie did
not.

Jack drove the Cutters and the sisters to his ranch.
The road climbed through clean, enormous air. Snow shone on
the ranges; cottonwoods burned yellow beside the river; cattle moved
like dark stitches across the plains.

For a long while nobody spoke.

Then Selene said, "There is room here."

That was the whole argument for Montana.

## V

What followed has been called romance, colonization, speculation, and Providence,
according to the speaker's temperament.

Aurora became housekeeper at Winfield Ranch for precisely six weeks.
Then Jack, maddened by her correction of his accounts, her
rearrangement of his pantry, and her refusal to admire his
beard, asked her to remain permanently.

"On salary?" she inquired.

"On half ownership."

"In the ranch?"

"In me."

"The ranch is better security."

He gave her both.

Their wedding caused the county newspaper to issue an extra,
the first since a train robbery three years earlier. Miss
Genevieve St. Clair sent a silver sugar bowl from St.
Louis. Jack used it to hold harness buckles until Aurora
discovered him.

Thalia opened a school with seven pupils and, by spring,
thirty-one. She married the deputy land commissioner after defeating him
in a public debate concerning the spelling of "separate."

Daphne established a dressmaking shop. Pete Murchison ordered six shirts
in one month, although none fitted him, and proposed over
the seventh.

Irene painted place cards for a cattlemen's dinner. Reynolds sat
beneath one depicting a cherub and complained that it resembled
him. Irene agreed. Their engagement was announced before dessert.

Eunice reorganized Major Blythe's ranch books and discovered that he
had been losing two thousand dollars annually through politeness. He
asked her to save the ranch. She said she would
require legal authority. He supplied it at the altar.

Selene painted the mountains, and Chloris organized a library in
a former saloon. Each found a husband in the natural
course of civic improvement.

Clytie alone held out.

She entered medical school in the territorial capital, returned during
holidays, rode better than half the cowboys, and rejected the
major's nephew, the newspaper editor, two physicians, and a young
Englishman who owned sixteen pairs of riding breeches.

"Nothing suits her," Jack complained.

"She is eighteen," Euphrosyne said.

"Nineteen now."

"A serious antiquity."

At twenty Clytie became engaged to Dr. Hiram Bell, who
had at first refused to allow a woman into his
dissecting room. She won the dispute, his practice, and finally
his heart, in that order.

Thus the last share in the Tusculum venture was taken
up.

As for Dick and Euphrosyne, they did not remain dependent
upon Jack. Dick bought a small ranch on credit, Euphrosyne
kept the accounts, and together they enlarged it until Jack
began borrowing money from them.

One evening, a year after Buffalo, Dick found his wife
on the veranda looking toward the mountains. The sun had
gone down behind the peaks, leaving the sky green and
gold. From the corrals came the noise of calves, cowboys,
and one of Clytie's rejected suitors singing badly.

"Thinking of Tusculum?" Dick asked.

"A little."

"Want to go back?"

"To visit."

"Mrs. Treadwell?"

"Particularly Mrs. Treadwell."

Dick laughed and lit a cigar.

"Happy?" he asked.

Euphrosyne considered the question with her customary honesty.

"Yes."

"For yourself, I mean. Not for the girls."

She turned toward him.

"For myself."

This answer satisfied him so thoroughly that he forgot to
strike the match and continued holding it until it burned
his fingers.

The mortgage on the old Bailey house was paid that
winter. The glove factory lost Chloris forever. Tusculum received photographs
of nine women standing before nine Western homes, though several
babies and an unreasonable number of dogs spoiled the symmetry.

Mrs. Treadwell examined the photographs and remarked that no truly
refined person could be happy so far from Albany.

But by then the Nine Cent-Girls had caught on to
Montana, and Montana had caught on to them.

The speculation paid handsomely.

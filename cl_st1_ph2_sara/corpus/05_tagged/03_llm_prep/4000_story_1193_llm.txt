# Ann Mary's Two Thanksgivings

On the Wednesday morning which Mrs. Little confidently declared to
be the morning before Thanksgiving, Ann Mary sat upon the
high kitchen stool and picked over raisins.

She was twelve years old, rather small for twelve, and
had a sober way of holding her shoulders which made
people say that she was the very image of her
grandmother. There was, to be sure, no likeness between Ann
Mary's smooth brown braids and Mrs. Little's gray hair, rolled
severely above her ears, nor between the child's blue calico
dress and the elder woman's black alpaca, with its row
of gold beads at the throat. The resemblance lay deeper—in
a certain straightness of the back, a watchful compression of
the lips, and a disposition to regard untidiness as almost
a moral failing.

At present Ann Mary was watching for stones among the
raisins, and Mrs. Little was making the plum-pudding.

"You've put in the nutmeg, haven't you, Grandma?" Ann Mary
asked.

Mrs. Little stopped with the flour scoop poised above the
yellow mixing-bowl.

"Nutmeg?" said she. "Of course I've put in nutmeg."

Ann Mary bent over the raisins again. Her feet, which
did not reach the floor, swung once beneath the stool
and then became still.

"I didn't see you grate it," she remarked timidly.

"That don't prove it ain't in," returned Mrs. Little. "Folks
can do things when they ain't bein' watched, I presume."

"Yes'm," said Ann Mary.

Mrs. Little stirred the pudding three times around, frowned, and
then walked to the pantry. She returned with the nutmeg-grater.

"I put in a mite," she said, in a tone
of reluctant explanation, "but likely not enough. Nutmeg ain't so
strong as it used to be. Nothin' is."

Mr. Little, who was sitting near the air-tight stove mending
a harness strap, looked over his spectacles. There was a
quiet twinkle in his pale eyes, but he had lived
with Mrs. Little for forty-one years and knew better than
to give it words.

The kitchen was full of the comfortable disorder proper to
a feast. Two pumpkin pies, their brown surfaces shining faintly
with spice and milk, stood upon the pantry shelf. A
pan of biscuit waited beneath a folded cloth. The turkey,
plucked and trussed and of a size that seemed almost
unreasonable for three persons, lay upon the blue platter near
the sink. Strings of onions hung against the wall; red
apples filled a basket under the table; and the stove
gave forth such a mingled fragrance of fire, molasses, sage,
and baking crust that even Mrs. Little's corrections could not
make the room seem severe.

Ann Mary had expected that there would be six persons
instead of three.

Her cousin Lucy, Lucy's father and mother, and perhaps even
Lucy's older brother, Frank, had been invited from Wilton. For
three weeks Ann Mary had made plans. She and Lucy
were to sleep together in the yellow-posted bedstead in the
spare room. They were to crack butternuts in the shed,
play hymns with one finger upon the parlor organ, and
go after dinner to see whether the pond beyond the
meeting-house had frozen smooth enough for sliding. Ann Mary had
even put away the last red Baldwin apple because Lucy
preferred red apples to green ones.

Then, on Monday, a letter had come saying that Uncle
James could not leave his store and Aunt Harriet had
one of her headaches. Lucy must remain with them.

Ann Mary had read the letter twice, standing beside the
window where the bare birches clicked their branches against the
glass. She had not cried. Crying did not bring company
from Wilton, and Grandma Little considered red eyes unbecoming. But
ever since Monday the anticipated Thanksgiving dinner had seemed like
a great flourish of accompaniment without any song.

"Now the plums," said Mrs. Little.

Ann Mary looked up.

"What plums, Grandma?"

"The plums for the pudding, child."

"I haven't seen any plums."

Mrs. Little drew herself upright.

"You've been pickin' 'em over this half hour."

"These are raisins."

"Raisins is what I meant."

Ann Mary's fingers moved carefully through the sticky heap. "You
said yesterday that there were to be plums in it,
because Grandpa fetched them from Mason's store."

"Well, and so there are."

"Where are they?"

Mrs. Little gave a short laugh, intended to show how
needless the question was. She opened the upper pantry cupboard,
looked behind a jar of meal, and shut it again.
She opened the lower cupboard, moved three pie tins, and
shut that. Then she searched the mantel, the dresser drawers,
and the box in which she kept clean lamp-wicks.

Mr. Little pushed his spectacles higher.

"Seems to me," he said gently, "I saw a paper
parcel on top of the parlor organ."

"Then why didn't you say so before, Pa? Ann Mary,
go fetch it. There ain't a mite of sense in
keepin' cooking things in the parlor."

Ann Mary fetched the parcel. Mrs. Little received it without
embarrassment and emptied the dried plums into the bowl.

"I set 'em there so's to remember 'em," she said.
"If other folks move things, 'tain't my fault."

No one had moved them, but Ann Mary only handed
her the wooden spoon.

Mrs. Little was absent-minded in the same decisive fashion in
which other women were orderly. She never doubted herself while
a mistake was being committed; doubt came afterward, and even
then it was accompanied by resentment. She could forget the
salt in bread, put the spectacles she was wearing into
the case, and accuse the clock of losing two hours
because she had read its face incorrectly. Yet her house
was scoured, swept, polished, aired, and regulated with such zeal
that dust itself appeared to avoid it from fear.

Ann Mary loved her grandmother. She was also afraid of
contradicting her. These two facts governed much of the child's
life.

At ten o'clock the stage from Wilton was due to
pass the white gate.

It had been Ann Mary's secret hope that Lucy might
come after all. Perhaps Uncle James had changed his mind.
Perhaps Aunt Harriet's headache had departed as suddenly as it
had arrived. Perhaps Lucy had begged so hard that they
had wrapped her in the green plaid shawl and placed
her beside the driver with a little valise at her
feet.

At five minutes before ten Ann Mary carried the raisin
bowl to the table nearest the window.

"You'll get your neck stretched lookin' down the road," said
Mrs. Little.

"I was only looking at the weather."

"The weather ain't liable to come in a stage."

Ann Mary blushed and resumed her picking.

Presently sleigh-bells sounded beyond the rise. The road, deeply packed
with snow, curved between stone walls and entered the village
past the schoolhouse. The stage appeared, red-bodied and high upon
its runners, with white breath flying from the horses. Ann
Mary could see the driver's fur cap, a woman in
a brown bonnet, two men with buffalo robes drawn to
their chins, and a square parcel tied behind. There was
no child in the green plaid shawl.

The stage swept past.

Ann Mary continued to look at the empty road until
the snow settled again.

"You missed a stem," Mrs. Little said.

"Yes'm."

At noon the pudding was tied into its cloth and
set boiling in the great iron pot. The turkey had
been rubbed with salt, stuffed with bread and sage, and
placed in the dripping-pan. Mrs. Little now discovered that she
had forgotten sugar in one of the pies, though she
maintained for some time that the pie which lacked it
was intended to be "ruther wholesome."

Mr. Little went out to split kindling. Ann Mary washed
bowls and spoons in water that smelled of soap and
spice.

"Mrs. Pottle can't cook for herself with that rheumatism," Mrs.
Little said suddenly. "We'd ought to send her over a
plate."

"Today?"

"There won't be time tomorrow, with meetin' and dinner. You
can carry it after the turkey's done. A slice of
white meat, some dressin', a biscuit, cranberry, and one of
the little pies."

Ann Mary brightened. Old Mrs. Pottle lived alone in a
brown cottage beyond the schoolhouse. She was almost deaf, and
her conversation consisted chiefly of "Hey?" and "I want to
know!" but she liked Ann Mary and kept peppermint drops
in a china hen upon the mantel.

"May I stay a little while?"

"Not so's to be out after dark. The sun goes
down early enough this season without children encouragin' it."

By three o'clock the turkey was done. It emerged from
the oven brown, shining, and fragrant, with crisp wings and
a breast so plump that Mrs. Little's expression softened in
spite of herself.

"There," she said. "I don't know when I've seen a
handsomer bird."

"You said last year's was the handsomest you'd ever roasted,"
Ann Mary reminded her.

"Last year's was handsome for last year. This is this
year."

The plum-pudding stood beside it, round and dark upon a
white plate. Mrs. Little had boiled it longer than necessary,
owing to her uncertainty about the hour at which it
had gone into the pot, but the outside was firm
and richly colored, and when she pricked it with a
straw a warm perfume of fruit and spice rose into
the kitchen.

Ann Mary's plate for Mrs. Pottle was covered with another
plate and wrapped in a clean towel. She put on
her blue hood, her red mittens, and a gray shawl
crossed snugly over her chest.

"Go straight there and straight back," said Mrs. Little. "An'
don't set the plate down in a snowbank to make
snowballs."

"I never did such a thing."

"Well, then, you won't have to stop doin' it."

Mr. Little opened the door for her. Cold air poured
in, clean and sharp, and the kitchen brightness seemed to
draw back from it.

"Take care by the bridge," he said. "It's drifted some."

Ann Mary stepped into the blue-white afternoon.

The snow creaked beneath her overshoes. Smoke went straight upward
from the village chimneys, and the western sky was already
beginning to redden behind the black pines. She carried the
plate with both hands, watching the towel lest it slip.
A farm sleigh passed her, the horse jingling briskly, and
the driver called, "Evenin', Ann Mary!" though it was not
yet four.

Mrs. Pottle's path had been shoveled that morning, but a
skin of powdery snow covered it. Ann Mary stamped her
feet upon the step and knocked.

There was no answer.

She knocked again, harder.

After some time she heard a chair scrape within, then
slow footsteps. The door opened two inches, checked by a
chain.

"Who be ye?" called Mrs. Pottle.

"It's Ann Mary Little!"

"Hey?"

"Ann Mary!"

"Land sakes, I ain't buyin' nothin'."

"I brought you some turkey."

The chain rattled instantly. Mrs. Pottle appeared in a purple
knitted shawl and a white cap with strings that hung
nearly to her waist.

"Turkey? Why, come in, child! Why didn't ye say so?"

The cottage was hot enough to make Ann Mary's cheeks
burn. A black cat slept under the stove; dried herbs
hung from a beam; and a kerosene lamp, already lighted,
stood before a looking-glass and appeared to make two small
suns in the room.

Mrs. Pottle uncovered the plate and admired every article separately.

"White meat! Cranberry! One of Mirandy Little's biscuits! An' pie,
too! I want to know! Set down, child. I got
some drops somewheres."

She searched the china hen and found two peppermint drops,
one button, and a brass thimble.

Ann Mary stayed longer than she intended. Mrs. Pottle told
her three times how her father had driven a team
through the great snow of 'forty-seven, and Ann Mary, who
had heard the story before, listened politely. By the time
she rose, the red sunset lay along the horizon like
a banked fire.

"I must go. Grandma said before dark."

"Ain't dark."

"It will be."

"Hey?"

"I said good night."

"Come again!" cried Mrs. Pottle. "Don't wait till Christmas."

Ann Mary hurried home.

As she approached the white house, she was surprised not
to see the kitchen windows shining. The parlor windows were
dark also. Only a dull red showed for a moment
in the west-facing glass, but that was the reflection of
sunset and faded as she reached the gate.

She tried the kitchen door. It was locked.

"Grandma!" she called.

No one answered.

She went around to the front. That door was locked,
too, but the Littles kept a spare key behind a
loose shutter, and Ann Mary, after feeling with numb fingers,
found it.

The hall was cold and dim. She smelled the familiar
odors of beeswax, dried apples, and the supper which ought
to have been waiting, but she heard no voices. Her
own step upon the braided rug sounded improper.

"Grandpa?"

Silence.

On the kitchen table stood the blue-edged plates, the tumblers,
the celery glass, the salt dishes, and three snow-white napkins
folded as Mrs. Little folded napkins only upon Sundays and
holidays. A paper lay beneath the lamp.

Ann Mary lit the lamp and read:

> Dear Ann Mary,
> Word has come that Aunt Sophrony is took sudden
and bad at East Braintree. We have gone directly in
Mr. Bell's sleigh, as Pa says the mare cannot make
it through the drifts. Eat your supper. The turkey and
pudding are all ready. If we are not back, go
over to Miss Loretta's and stay. Do not be alarmed.
Put more coal in the stove.
> Grandma.

Below this, in Mr. Little's hand, was written:

> We shall likely return by nine. Lock the door.

Ann Mary read both messages twice.

Aunt Sophrony was Mr. Little's older sister, who lived twelve
miles away and had been expecting to die, in a
general manner, for at least fifteen years. Every winter she
wrote that it might be her last, and every spring
she planted more peas than anybody in East Braintree. Still,
"took sudden and bad" sounded serious. Ann Mary pictured her
grandfather riding through the dark with his face pale above
his comforter, and her grandmother holding the black medicine case
which she carried on occasions of illness though it contained
little except camphor and mustard plasters.

She put coal in the stove. Then, being hungry and
practical, she went to carve herself some turkey.

The blue platter was not on the dresser.

She looked in the pantry. There were pies, biscuit, cranberry
jelly, cold potatoes, pickles, cheese, and a bowl of whipped
turnip. There was no turkey.

The pudding was gone also.

Ann Mary searched the pantry twice. She opened the warming
oven, the baking oven, the flour barrel, and the cupboard
under the sink. She even looked in the woodshed, although
it was not reasonable to suppose that Mrs. Little had
put either a turkey or a plum-pudding among the kindlings.

"The turkey and the plum-pudding," Ann Mary said aloud.

The sound of her voice in the empty kitchen gave
her a start.

Perhaps Grandma had carried them to the cellar, where it
was cool. Ann Mary took the lamp and descended the
narrow stairs. Shadows sprang among the shelves. Crocks of butter,
jars of preserves, cabbages, beets, and rows of potatoes occupied
their proper places. She looked behind the apple barrel and
under the stone shelf. No turkey. No pudding.

A faint scratching came from the dark corner.

Ann Mary nearly dropped the lamp.

It was only a mouse, or so she told herself.
She went upstairs quickly and closed the cellar door.

She ate a biscuit, a spoonful of cranberry, and a
piece of pumpkin pie. Without turkey and pudding, the feast
seemed scattered and foolish. Three places were set, yet she
sat at only one. The grandfather's chair, pushed neatly against
the table, looked more lonely than an empty chair had
any right to look.

She would go to Miss Loretta's.

Loretta Bell taught the village school. She was only twenty-three,
which made her young to Mrs. Little and grown-up to
Ann Mary, and she wore her light hair in a
soft knot that never appeared to be fastened by enough
pins. Ann Mary admired her more than she admired any
other person. Miss Loretta knew poetry, could draw maps without
looking at an atlas, and sometimes let Ann Mary remain
after school to dust the blackboard erasers.

The Bell house stood two doors beyond Mrs. Pottle's.

Ann Mary wrapped herself again and set out. It was
now quite dark. The snow reflected a pallid light from
the stars, but the road between the houses appeared longer
than it had in the afternoon. Bare branches clicked overhead.
Somewhere a loose shutter knocked steadily—one, two; one, two—and stopped
whenever Ann Mary paused to hear it.

The Bell house was dark.

She knocked. There was no answer.

A note pinned to the door said that the family
had gone to Springfield to spend Thanksgiving with Mr. Bell's
married sister and would not return until Friday. Ann Mary
remembered then that Loretta had mentioned the journey at school,
but she had been thinking of Lucy and had not
attended.

Something brushed against her skirt.

She jumped, then heard a plaintive mew.

Loretta's white cat stood upon the step, lifting one paw
from the snow.

"Oh, Pearl! Did they leave you?"

Pearl mewed again and rubbed her cold side against Ann
Mary's overshoe. There was a box for her in the
woodshed, no doubt, and some neighbor had been expected to
feed her; but Pearl had plainly formed an opinion of
this arrangement.

Ann Mary gathered the cat beneath her shawl.

"You may come home with me," she whispered. "Grandma doesn't
like cats in the parlor, but this isn't exactly ordinary."

Before returning, she stopped again at Mrs. Pottle's. Light shone
through the red curtains, and Ann Mary could see the
old woman's shadow moving across them. She knocked loudly.

No response.

She struck the door with both mittened fists.

Within, Mrs. Pottle continued moving about in perfect unconsciousness.

Ann Mary called until her throat hurt. The door was
fastened. At last Pearl wriggled impatiently, and the child understood
that if she remained there all night, Mrs. Pottle might
remain equally unaware of her.

She walked home with the cat.

The kitchen had grown warmer. That was something. Ann Mary
gave Pearl a saucer of milk and spread an old
towel near the stove. Pearl drank, washed one shoulder, and
then leaped into Ann Mary's lap as if they had
arranged it days before.

"Grandma says cats are deceitful," Ann Mary told her. "But
I think perhaps she means they don't explain."

Pearl purred.

Seven o'clock passed. Then eight.

Ann Mary tried to read the Youth's Companion, but every
sound in the house disturbed her. The clock ticked with
unusual force. The stove clicked as its iron sides cooled
and heated. Once a mass of snow slipped from the
roof and struck the pantry window with a soft, heavy
blow. Ann Mary stood up so quickly that Pearl rolled
to the floor.

No sleigh appeared.

At half past eight she cleared the table, washed her
plate, and set the lamp in the front window. Then
she made careful preparations for the night. She filled the
water pail, placed the poker beside the stove, locked both
doors, and put Mr. Little's old walking stick at the
head of her bed. She did not know what she
expected to do with the walking stick, but its knotted
handle had a dependable appearance.

The yellow-posted bedstead was in the spare room. Ann Mary
might have slept there, where she and Lucy had meant
to whisper after the candle was blown out, but the
thought made her throat ache. She chose her own little
room under the eaves.

Pearl followed.

Mrs. Little did not permit animals upon beds. Ann Mary
spread her shawl on the floor and indicated it.

"You must lie there."

Pearl sat upon the shawl and looked at her.

Ann Mary undressed, shivering in her long nightgown. She blew
out the candle, climbed into bed, and pulled the patchwork
quilt to her chin.

Pearl sprang lightly upon the coverlet.

"You mustn't," Ann Mary whispered.

The cat walked in a circle, kneaded the quilt, and
lay down against Ann Mary's knees.

"Well," said Ann Mary, "only till I hear the sleigh."

The darkness was full of shapes. The satin-scroll wallpaper, harmless
by day, seemed crowded with pale faces. The clothes hanging
upon a peg became a tall woman. The birch branch
scraped the roof with the fingernail sound of somebody trying
not to be heard.

Ann Mary thought of Aunt Sophrony. She thought of the
drifted road, an overturned sleigh, a horse stumbling at the
bridge. She thought of the missing turkey and plum-pudding. That
mystery, ridiculous in daylight, had become dreadful. Food could not
walk away. Somebody must have moved it. If Grandma had
not moved it, who had?

Pearl purred steadily.

Ann Mary put one hand upon the cat's warm back.
She resolved that if she heard a step downstairs, she
would not scream. She would take the walking stick, go
to the landing, and demand to know who was there.
Her voice might tremble, but the words would be distinct.

She was repeating the intended demand when bells sounded far
down the road.

Ann Mary sat up.

The bells came nearer, stopped, began again, and at last
jingled into the Little yard. Voices sounded below. A key
scraped at the kitchen door.

"Ann Mary!" called Mr. Little.

She was downstairs before Mrs. Little had removed her bonnet.

"Grandma! Grandpa!"

Mrs. Little was powdered with snow and looked tired, cold,
and offended. Mr. Little's eyebrows were white with frost. Behind
them stood Mr. Bell's hired man, who had driven the
sleigh and now stamped his boots upon the mat.

"There, there," said Mrs. Little, embracing Ann Mary with more
force than tenderness. "You ain't hurt. I said you wouldn't
be hurt."

"I thought Aunt Sophrony was dying."

"Dyin'!" Mrs. Little pulled off one mitten. "She was settin'
up eatin' pork and beans."

Mr. Little's mouth twitched.

"The message lost a word or two in traveling," he
explained. "Sophrony had a spell of dizziness yesterday. By the
time the news reached Deacon Pike, and Deacon Pike told
Ezra Bell, it had become a seizure. She's likely as
well as any of us."

"As well!" cried Mrs. Little. "She asked why we came
rampagin' over at supper-time, an' then she told me I
looked peaked."

Ann Mary began to laugh. She did not intend to.
The laugh rose out of the long hours of watching
and came so suddenly that it turned halfway into a
sob.

Mrs. Little's expression changed.

"Why, child," she said. "Were you scared?"

"No'm. Not very. Only Miss Loretta was gone, and Mrs.
Pottle couldn't hear, and the turkey and the plum-pudding—"

Mrs. Little stiffened.

"What about 'em?"

"They're gone."

"Gone where?"

"I don't know. I looked everywhere."

"Nonsense. They're in the pantry."

"They aren't."

Mrs. Little marched to the pantry. Ann Mary, Mr. Little,
and Pearl followed. The cat's presence was not observed immediately,
owing to the greater issue.

Mrs. Little looked upon every shelf.

"Well," she said.

She examined the warming oven, baking oven, dresser, cellar shelves,
and woodshed. She returned to the kitchen with a humble
and resentful air.

"You must have overlooked 'em," she told Ann Mary.

"I looked three times."

"Then likely I put 'em somewheres safe before we started."

"Where?"

"If I knew where, I wouldn't be huntin', would I?"

Mr. Little coughed into his hand.

The search began again. All four rooms downstairs were inspected.
Mrs. Little peered into the tall clock, though its case
was plainly too narrow for a turkey platter. Mr. Little
looked in the sleigh robes, the umbrella stand, and the
entry cupboard. Ann Mary went through the pantry for the
fourth time.

"The turkey and the plum-pudding," Mrs. Little muttered. "A body'd
think they had legs."

Pearl, unnoticed, entered the parlor.

The search ended at half past ten. Mr. Little ate
bread and milk. Mrs. Little refused supper on the ground
that she had lost her appetite at East Braintree. She
allowed Pearl to remain indoors after Ann Mary explained, though
she said no cat had ever yet perished from spending
one seasonable night in a shed.

Ann Mary went back to bed. This time she heard
her grandparents moving below, and the sounds which had frightened
her became ordinary sounds again.

The turkey and pudding remained missing.

During the next several days the mystery was examined from
every side.

Mrs. Little suggested that a tramp had entered the house.
Mr. Little pointed out that the doors had been locked
and that a tramp who rejected pies, biscuit, and silver
spoons in favor of one turkey and one pudding possessed
unusual discrimination.

Mrs. Little then suspected Pearl.

"A cat could drag a turkey," she declared.

"Not a turkey on a platter," said Ann Mary.

"Might have et it where it stood."

"There weren't any bones."

"Cats don't always leave bones when they know they've done
wrong."

Mr. Little looked at Pearl, who was asleep upon the
hearth with four clean paws folded under her breast.

"That cat," said he, "would need several hollow legs."

Mrs. Little did not appreciate the observation.

The family ate the remaining dinner in portions. On Thursday
they had vegetables and pie. On Friday Mrs. Little made
hash from no meat at all, which Mr. Little called
"expectation hash." On Saturday the last of the cranberry jelly
was finished.

It was Saturday afternoon when Deacon Pike stopped at the
house.

He found Mrs. Little rolling pie crust.

"More pie?" he asked.

"It ain't any concern of the parish how many pies
I make," said Mrs. Little. "But after Thanksgiving there's generally
calls for something sweet."

Deacon Pike took off his hat.

"After Thanksgiving?"

"Yes."

He looked at the calendar upon the wall. Then he
looked at Mrs. Little.

"Mirandy, Thanksgiving's next Thursday."

Mrs. Little continued rolling.

"It was last Thursday."

"No, 'twasn't."

"The proclamation said the fourth Thursday."

"So it did."

"Well, this was it."

"This coming one is the fourth Thursday. Last was the
third."

Mrs. Little stopped. Ann Mary, who was paring apples, counted
the Thursdays silently upon her fingers.

There were five Thursdays in November that year.

Mrs. Little had begun counting with the last Thursday in
October.

Mr. Little, entering with an armful of wood, heard the
discussion and set the wood carefully in the box.

"Pa," said Mrs. Little, "didn't you know Thanksgiving was next
week?"

"I supposed you'd looked at the almanac."

"I did look."

"Likely the almanac looked back," said Deacon Pike.

Mrs. Little turned so sharply that her gold beads clicked
together.

"I don't see any occasion for merriment. There's been a
mistake in dates, which might happen to anybody burdened with
as much as I am. An' if folks had corrected
me sooner—"

Ann Mary lowered her eyes to the apple in her
lap.

"But Grandma," she said, "I thought you said—"

"You needn't tell me what I said. I was misinformed."

No one had informed her at all. Deacon Pike, whose
courage was greater in the pulpit than in Mrs. Little's
kitchen, took his leave.

For several minutes only the rolling-pin sounded.

Then Mr. Little said, "This explains why there was no
meeting."

"I supposed Reverend Ames was poorly."

"And why the stores stayed open."

"I ain't in the habit of governin' my religious observances
by Mason's store."

"And why the Wilton stage was runnin' regular."

"Pa, if you've got any more wood, you'd better fetch
it."

Mr. Little fetched it. He was smiling when he went
out.

Ann Mary scarcely knew whether to be mortified or glad.
They had eaten Thanksgiving vegetables upon the wrong day, traveled
twelve miles to a needless sickbed, and lost the chief
dishes of a dinner which should not yet have been
cooked. But the real Thanksgiving still lay ahead, shining with
all its possibilities restored. Lucy might not come, of course;
Uncle James's store would still require attention, and Aunt Harriet's
headache might still persist. Yet there would be church, school
would be dismissed, and every other household would be feasting,
too.

On Monday another letter arrived from Wilton.

Ann Mary saw Uncle James's handwriting and took it into
the pantry before opening it, because she did not wish
her face to be observed if the contents were disappointing.

They were not.

Uncle James had arranged for his clerk to keep the
store. Aunt Harriet felt better. The whole family, except Frank,
who had gone to Boston, would arrive Wednesday evening and
stay until Friday.

Lucy was coming.

Ann Mary read the letter once, twice, and a third
time. Then she ran into the kitchen so quickly that
Mrs. Little accused her of bringing a draft.

"They're coming! Lucy's coming for Thanksgiving!"

Mrs. Little took the letter and read it.

Her first expression was satisfaction. Her second was calculation. Her
third was dismay.

"We'll have to have another turkey."

"Yes'm."

"And another pudding."

"Yes'm."

"And pies. Them pies are gone."

"We can make more."

"I know we can make more. The question is whether
folks have any notion what it takes out of a
person to make 'em."

Nevertheless, Mrs. Little began that afternoon.

The second preparations resembled the first, but they were more
carefully supervised. Ann Mary wrote a list in pencil and
crossed out each item as it entered the bowl. Flour.
Suet. Sugar. Raisins. Plums. Nutmeg. Cinnamon. Salt.

"I don't need a child keepin' tally of me," said
Mrs. Little.

"No'm. It's only for practice in writing."

Mrs. Little looked at the list.

"You spelled cinnamon wrong."

Ann Mary corrected it. The sugar went in.

On Wednesday the Wilton sleigh arrived at four o'clock, with
Uncle James driving, Aunt Harriet wrapped in three shawls, and
Lucy standing up behind them, waving both hands.

Ann Mary ran as far as the gate without her
hood.

Lucy was thirteen, rosy, talkative, and taller than Ann Mary
by half a head. Snow dusted her brown curls, and
her green plaid shawl was exactly the one Ann Mary
had pictured upon the stage. She jumped down before the
sleigh had stopped.

"We came! Mother said we'd freeze, but we didn't. I
brought my geography, though Father said nobody studied geography on
Thanksgiving. Is the pond frozen? Have you got the red
apple? Why is there a white cat in the window?"

Ann Mary answered all three questions at once, and neither
girl understood the other, which did not matter.

That night they slept in the yellow-posted bedstead. They talked
until Mrs. Little called from across the hall that if
they did not stop whispering, they would be too hoarse
to eat dinner. They whispered more softly after that.

Ann Mary told Lucy everything: Aunt Sophrony, the empty house,
Mrs. Pottle's deafness, Pearl's arrival, and the disappearance.

"A whole turkey?" Lucy breathed.

"And the plum-pudding."

"Somebody stole them."

"The doors were locked."

"Then it was a ghost."

"Ghosts don't eat."

"You don't know. Perhaps that's why they're so thin."

Ann Mary considered laughing improper at that hour, but the
bed shook beneath both girls.

Thanksgiving morning was bright and very cold. The snow had
hardened overnight and glittered beneath a pale blue sky. Sleighs
moved along the village road, bells sounding from every direction.
From the kitchen rose the smell of coffee, frying sausage,
sage dressing, and the second turkey, already browning in the
oven.

Mrs. Little wore her black silk and gold beads, though
she had covered the dress with a large white apron.
She was calm but vigilant.

"Lucy, don't stand in front of the oven. Ann Mary,
fetch the celery. Pa, if you're goin' out, go out;
if you're stayin' in, shut the door."

The family was to attend the ten-o'clock service. Aunt Harriet
went upstairs to change her bonnet, Uncle James helped Mr.
Little with the mare, and Mrs. Little sent the girls
into the parlor to find her hymn-book.

The parlor was opened chiefly for company, funerals, and occasions
which Mrs. Little thought ought to impress the minister. It
contained a horsehair sofa, six stiff chairs, a marble-topped table,
the organ, a framed wreath made of human hair, and
wallpaper patterned with large satin-colored scrolls. The room smelled faintly
of cold ashes and dried rose leaves.

The hymn-book was not on the organ.

"Look under things," Lucy advised.

"Grandma doesn't put books under things."

"Your grandma put plums on the organ."

This was unanswerable.

They searched beneath the table and behind the chairs. Lucy
knelt by the sofa, lifted its dark fringe, and gave
a cry.

"Ann Mary!"

"What?"

"There's something under here."

Together they pulled.

First came a corner of a folded tablecloth. Then a
large blue platter slid across the carpet, bearing a turkey
which had once been handsome beyond dispute and was now
somewhat flattened on one side. Behind it was the plum-pudding,
still upon its white plate, wrapped in two napkins and
hard as a stone.

For a moment the girls stared.

"The turkey and the plum-pudding," whispered Ann Mary.

Then Lucy sat down upon the floor and laughed until
she had to hold her side.

Mrs. Little appeared in the doorway.

"What in creation—"

She stopped.

Ann Mary rose, carrying the petrified pudding. Lucy pointed beneath
the sofa.

"We found them," said Ann Mary.

Mrs. Little looked at the platter. She looked at the
pudding. She looked at the sofa.

"Well, for the land sakes," she said.

"Did you put them there, Grandma?"

Mrs. Little's face passed through astonishment, remembrance, and defense.

"I do recollect now. When the message came about Sophrony,
I thought we might be gone overnight. I didn't want
the kitchen coolin' with food standin' where any stray dog—or
cat—could get it. I brought 'em in here for safekeepin'."

"Under the sofa?"

"It was the coolest place."

"Why didn't you remember?"

"I had other things on my mind. Folks don't generally
expect to be called twelve miles to see a woman
eat pork and beans."

Lucy covered her mouth.

Mr. Little and Uncle James came in from the entry.
Aunt Harriet descended in her bonnet. All were shown the
remains.

Mr. Little inspected the turkey solemnly.

"Kept safe," he said.

"Perfectly safe," said Uncle James. "Nobody would think of looking
there."

Mrs. Little lifted her chin.

"You may laugh. I don't see anything laughable in preventin'
waste."

"Can we eat it?" Lucy asked.

"No!" cried four adults together.

The turkey and pudding were carried to the shed for
disposal. Pearl followed with intense interest, and Mrs. Little shut
the door firmly upon her.

At church the Littles occupied their pew with proper composure.
Ann Mary sat between Lucy and her grandfather. Sunlight shone
through the tall windows; the stove at the back gave
off a sleepy iron heat; wet overshoes stood in rows
beneath the benches; and the air held the mixed scents
of wool, evergreens, and peppermint.

Reverend Ames preached about gratitude after trial. Ann Mary listened
more closely than she commonly did. She had not suffered
any great trial, she knew. Aunt Sophrony had not died.
The dark house had contained no thief. The scratching in
the cellar had been a mouse. Her grandparents had returned
before midnight. Still, loneliness had seemed very large while it
lasted, and fear was not less real because it afterward
proved unnecessary.

At the final hymn she and Lucy shared the book.
Lucy sang too loudly and lost the tune in the
second verse. Ann Mary did not mind.

Outside, the whole village sparkled. Sleigh-bells crossed and recrossed in
the frosty air. Neighbors called greetings. Mrs. Pottle, bundled so
thoroughly that only the point of her nose could be
seen, shouted that she had never tasted a finer bit
of turkey than the plate Ann Mary had brought her
"last Thanksgiving."

"This is Thanksgiving," Ann Mary called back.

"Hey?"

"This is the real Thanksgiving!"

"I want to know!" said Mrs. Pottle, as if the
news were surprising but agreeable.

When the Littles reached home, the fresh turkey was done
to a turn. The second pudding had every ingredient, and
the sauce was neither scorched nor forgotten. There were mashed
potatoes, onions, turnips, celery, cranberry jelly, pickles, biscuit, pumpkin pie,
mince pie, and the red Baldwin apple, which Ann Mary
and Lucy divided exactly.

Miss Loretta was expected that evening, having returned early from
Springfield, and Pearl was to remain at the Little house
until then. Even Mrs. Little had admitted that the cat
had been "some comfort," though she qualified this by adding
that most creatures could be useful once if given sufficient
opportunity.

At dinner Mr. Little asked the blessing.

He gave thanks for health, for family, for food, for
safe journeys, and for mercies understood only after some delay.
At this last phrase Mrs. Little opened one eye, but
she said nothing.

Ann Mary looked around the table.

Lucy sat beside her. Uncle James carved. Aunt Harriet had
no headache. Her grandfather's eyes were gentle behind his spectacles,
and her grandmother, erect in black silk and gold beads,
presided over the second turkey with the dignity of one
who had never mistaken a date or placed a dinner
beneath a sofa.

The cold rooms were warm. The empty chairs were filled.
The mystery was solved. Even the first pudding, though lost
beyond eating, had served a purpose by providing Lucy with
something to laugh over for years to come.

Ann Mary's earlier disappointment seemed very far away; yet because
she remembered it, the present happiness had a brightness it
might not otherwise have possessed. She understood, in the simple
fashion in which a child understands what older persons often
explain too much, that a sorrow does not always give
notice when it is nearly over. A dark road may
have bells coming upon it. An empty house may soon
be full. A missing feast may be followed by another,
better one.

And so, after all their mistakes and alarms, the Littles
kept Thanksgiving upon the proper day.

Ann Mary thought they had never kept it better.

The great copper boiler had stopped trembling by six o'clock,
and the steam began to thin upon the windows. Maria
went along the row of tables to see that everything
was left properly. The folded shirts stood in their little
white piles, the irons had been placed upon the stone
shelf, and no scraps of soap remained in the basins.
She was very particular about scraps because they made the
place look untidy.

The women were waiting for their tea and barmbrack. Some
of the younger ones had already begun guessing who would
get the ring, and Lizzie Fleming said she would swallow
it rather than marry any man she had ever met.
Everyone laughed at that, even Mrs. Craven, who was matron
and generally did not encourage foolishness.

Maria carried in the brown teapot with both hands. She
was a very small woman, and when she laughed the
tip of her nose nearly met the point of her
chin. The women said she looked like a little witch,
which was only their Hallow Eve nonsense, for there was
nobody more peaceable than Maria. Whenever two of the women
quarrelled over a clothes-line or a missing apron, it was
Maria who went between them and made things nice again.

"Now, girls," she said, "we must all be friends tonight."

They told her she would surely find the ring, and
Lizzie Fleming called out that Mr. Right might be waiting
for her on the tram. Maria shook her head and
laughed until her face grew red.

"I want no Mr. Right," she said. "I have my
independence."

Mrs. Craven, pouring the tea, said independence was a fine
thing when one had it, and then she gave Maria
the first slice of brack because Maria was going out.
Maria cut the slice carefully into two and passed half
to old Nannie, who had received only a small corner.
It was far better that everyone should be satisfied.

She had been looking forward to the evening for three
weeks. Joe Donnelly had sent his eldest girl with a
message that nothing would do but Maria must come for
Hallow Eve. Joe always remembered her. She had minded him
when he was no higher than the wash-tub, and she
had minded Alphy too, though Alphy had been the quieter
child. Joe used to say that Maria was his proper
mother, and once, after taking a little too much stout,
he had declared that she was worth ten of the
mother who had borne him. Of course, a man should
not speak against his own mother, even when she was
dead, but Joe meant it kindly.

At a quarter past six Maria went upstairs to change.
Her room was narrow but quite comfortable, and there was
a coloured picture of the Sacred Heart over the bed.
She took off her working dress and put on the
brown one with the small pearl buttons. Then she buttoned
her good boots and pinned her hat firmly with two
pins, for there was a fresh wind outside. Her purse
was in the inner pocket of her coat. She pressed
it once to make certain.

She had allowed two shillings and sixpence for the evening.
The tram fare there and back would take fourpence, though
Joe would probably insist on sending one of the boys
home with her, and the cakes might cost a shilling.
That left enough for something special. It would not do
to arrive at a family party with only ordinary cakes.

Mrs. Craven came to the door to say good night.

"You're as neat as a pin, Maria."

Maria smiled and said she believed in looking respectable. Then
the women all cried out good wishes from below, and
Lizzie Fleming called after her not to forget the wedding.

Such girls! Maria could hear them laughing even after the
outer door had closed.

The streets were full of people and the shop windows
shone upon the wet pavement. Boys ran past with turnip
lanterns, pushing one another, and a child in a white
paper mask stood outside a public-house frightening the women who
entered. Maria held her purse through the cloth of her
coat and walked close to the walls.

At Dowling's she looked at the little cakes under the
glass. There were penny buns with pink sugar, currant squares,
and some yellow sponge cakes that looked very nice. She
asked for a dozen mixed, six for the children and
six for the grown people, though the children would likely
eat nearly all of them. The girl behind the counter
placed them in a cardboard box and tied the box
with thin blue string.

"And have you any plumcake?" Maria asked.

The girl showed her a plain piece at fourpence, but
it was dry at the edge. Then she showed her
a thick slice from a rich cake with almonds on
top. It cost eightpence, which was a great deal for
one slice, but Joe was so fond of plumcake. Maria
imagined him cutting it, pretending that he would keep it
all for himself, and the children shouting at him. So
she bought it.

The rich slice was wrapped separately in brown paper and
tied with string. Maria placed it on top of the
box and went out feeling very pleased. What a nice
surprise it would be.

The first tram was crowded, and she had to wait
for another. While she waited, she counted her money again.
Everything had come out exactly, with twopence over. It was
always best to make a plan.

The second tram was nearly full too, but a gentleman
rose at once and offered her the place beside him.
He was a broad gentleman with a sandy moustache and
a soft brown hat. His face was red, and when
he sat down again Maria noticed the warm smell of
drink; but many perfectly respectable gentlemen took something in the
evening, especially at Hallow Eve.

He asked whether the parcel on her knees was safe,
and he held the cake box while she arranged the
plumcake and fastened her purse. His manners were very genteel.

"A party, ma'am?" he said.

"Only a little family party."

"A wedding party, I'll wager."

Maria laughed and said there was no question of a
wedding. The gentleman said that was hard to believe, and
he lifted his hat a little as if she were
a young lady. Maria told him she had no interest
in marrying and was quite contented where she was. Still,
she hoped the tram would not become brighter, because she
could feel herself blushing.

He spoke about the weather and the crowds, and once
he called the conductor a fellow in a tone that
showed he was used to being obeyed. When Maria's stop
came, the gentleman handed her the box and helped her
down. He wished her a happy evening and touched his
hat again.

There were still some nice people in the world.

She walked the remaining streets quickly. The Donnellys lived in
a narrow house with a brass knocker that Joe polished
every Sunday. Before Maria had reached the step, the door
opened and two of the children rushed out.

"She's here! Maria's here!"

Joe's wife came from the kitchen wiping her hands upon
her apron. She kissed Maria on the cheek and scolded
her for bringing anything. Then Joe appeared behind her, larger
than Maria remembered him, though she had seen him only
the month before. He had taken off his collar and
his hair was standing up over one ear.

"Well, here's the best woman in Dublin," he said.

Maria laughed and told him not to be foolish. Joe
took the cake box, and the youngest boy danced about
asking what was inside.

"Nothing for greedy boys," Maria said.

The front room was warm and crowded. There was a
bright fire, and apples floated in a basin near the
hearth. Two neighbour girls had come in for the games,
and Joe's eldest daughter wore orange ribbons in her hair.
On the table were nuts, bottles of stout, lemonade and
a great barmbrack upon a plate. Everyone made room for
Maria in the best chair.

She removed her hat and gloves. Then she remembered the
plumcake.

"There was another little parcel," she said.

Joe's wife looked inside the box. There was only the
dozen cakes.

"It was tied separately," Maria said. "A slice of plumcake."

Joe searched the hall and asked the children whether they
had taken it. They denied it loudly. His wife said
perhaps the shopgirl had forgotten to give it to her.

But Maria remembered holding it at the tram stop. She
remembered placing it on the box and the gentleman steadying
the parcels for her. The thought came that it might
have slipped when she got down. It was certainly no
one's fault.

"It doesn't matter in the least," she said. "There is
more than enough."

Yet she was so vexed that for a moment she
could not undo the clasp of her purse. Eightpence was
a great sum for a thing to disappear. And the
gentleman had been so attentive. She hoped they would not
think she had invented the cake to make herself important.

Joe said the tram company was full of thieves and
careless blackguards, and that he would go to the office
in the morning. Maria begged him not to trouble. His
wife brought her tea in the blue cup and gave
her one of the pink cakes. After a little while
everything was nice again.

The children bobbed for apples, soaking their sleeves and splashing
water on the floor. Joe pretended to be angry, but
he laughed more loudly than anyone. Then they hung an
apple from a string, and each person tried to bite
it with hands tied behind the back. When Maria's turn
came the apple struck her nose, and the children shrieked.

"You mustn't laugh at an old woman," she said, laughing
herself.

"Old!" Joe cried. "Listen to her!"

He poured stout for Maria, though she asked for only
half a glass. He said they would drink to her
health, and everyone raised a cup or glass. Maria felt
very grateful. It was a grand thing to be remembered
in a family.

When the noise had settled, she asked quietly whether Joe
had heard from Alphy.

Joe's face changed at once.

"No," he said.

Maria said Alphy was always a reserved sort of boy
and perhaps he was waiting for Joe to make the
first move. Brothers should not remain divided over a few
words. Hallow Eve was just the night for making peace,
and she would gladly carry a message.

"A few words!" Joe said. "You don't know half of
it."

His wife told him not to begin.

"I'll not have his name in this house," Joe said.
"Not after what he did."

Maria touched his sleeve and said they were both good
boys at heart. She remembered them sleeping in the same
little bed, Alphy curled against the wall and Joe kicking
off all the blankets. Many a cold night she had
covered them again.

But Joe pulled his arm away, not roughly, and said
Alphy could go to the devil in his own fashion.

There was a silence. One of the neighbour girls coughed,
and the youngest child rolled a walnut across the table.
Joe's wife asked who was ready for the saucers.

Maria sighed and said men were stubborn creatures. Still, she
was certain the quarrel would be settled in time. Joe
had a warm heart, though he became excited. It would
all come right.

The saucers were placed upon the table while Maria sat
near the fire. The children whispered and ran in and
out of the back room. There was water in one
saucer, a ring in another, and a silver coin in
another. Joe's eldest daughter explained that water meant travel, the
ring meant marriage, and the coin meant riches.

"And what would Maria do with riches?" Joe asked.

"Give them all away," his wife said.

They tied a folded handkerchief over Maria's eyes and turned
her round three times. She protested that they would make
her dizzy. Then the children led her toward the table,
giggling and warning one another not to speak.

Maria stretched out her right hand. She could hear the
fire and someone breathing close beside her. Her fingers moved
over the edge of a saucer and descended.

They touched something cold, soft and wet.

At once a chair scraped. Someone caught Maria by the
wrist, and there were quick whispers near the table. Joe's
wife said sharply, "Who put that there?" Then one of
the girls began to laugh, but stopped.

Maria stood waiting with the handkerchief over her eyes. She
thought perhaps water had been spilled, though it had not
felt like water. It felt like the damp earth in
the laundry garden after rain.

"That doesn't count," Joe's wife said brightly. "They've muddled the
saucers. You must try again."

The handkerchief was not removed. Maria was turned once more,
and while she waited she heard a door open toward
the yard and then close. The children were very quiet.

This time her fingers met the square corners of a
small book.

"A prayer-book!" everyone cried.

Joe laughed louder than was necessary and said Maria would
join a convent before the year was out. His wife
said she would make the nicest little nun in Ireland,
and the children called her Sister Maria.

Maria took off the handkerchief and laughed with them. A
convent was better than marriage at her age, she said,
and she had always admired a religious life. She noticed
that one saucer was missing from the table, but the
youngest boy had begun biting into a currant square, and
she told him to use a plate.

After that Joe brought out an old concertina. He played
two lively airs, badly but with great spirit, while the
children danced. Then his wife asked Maria for a song.

Maria said she had no voice left. They would not
accept that, and Joe filled her glass again and declared
that the evening could not finish without her song. She
knew the one he meant. She had sung it years
ago when Joe and Alphy were boys and would lie
awake to hear the end.

She sat very straight and folded her hands in her
lap. At first her voice was thin, but the room
became quiet, and then it grew steadier.

She sang of a lady who dreamed she lived in
marble rooms, with servants at every door, and of claiming
a proud and ancient name and possessing riches beyond counting.
Outside, a boy shouted in the street, and the tram
bell sounded far away.

Then Maria sang the last lines more softly: that neither
halls nor gold nor noble birth could please the lady
unless the one she loved loved her always.

Joe stared into the fire. His wife gathered the empty
cups without making a sound.

Maria smiled when she finished and said it was only
an old song. For a moment nobody spoke. Then Joe
took her hand in both of his and said there
was not another voice like hers in all Dublin.

Maria told him he was a great flatterer, and she
laughed until the tip of her nose nearly touched her
chin.

Romance is an excellent occupation for those who have no
need of an income; for everybody else it is an
expensive recreation. A young man who wishes to marry should
possess either a fortune or the obstinacy to acquire one.
Hughie Erskine possessed neither. He had only a beautiful face,
an admirable heart, and six hundred pounds a year allowed
him by an aunt who disapproved of idleness but disliked
employment even more.

To look at Hughie was to forgive him everything, including
his prospects. His hair had the agreeable disorder which costs
a careful man ten minutes before breakfast, his eyes were
grey, and his smile had opened more doors than his
talents could keep open. He played the piano sufficiently well
to be asked once, and sufficiently badly never to be
asked twice. He danced, rode, dressed, and talked with distinction.
In short, he had every accomplishment except that of making
money.

He had tried it, of course.

For six months he had been on the Stock Exchange,
where he discovered that bulls were brutal, bears were hungry,
and both demanded capital. He next entered the tea trade,
but could never remember whether pekoe was a leaf or
a province, and declared souchong to be an immoral word.
After this he became the private secretary of a member
of Parliament. His employer had no secrets and Hughie had
no industry, so the arrangement soon ended by mutual relief.

At last he decided to live upon his allowance and
his appearance. The allowance was inadequate, but the appearance was
universally admired.

Unfortunately, Hughie was in love.

The lady was Laura Merton, the daughter of Colonel Merton,
a retired officer who had lost his digestion in India
and his patience in England. Laura had dark eyes, clear
opinions, and the sort of beauty that makes poverty seem
poetic at dinner and impossible at breakfast. She loved Hughie
with perfect sincerity, which is rare; the Colonel liked him
with severe reservations, which is common.

"You may marry Laura," the Colonel had said, "when you
have ten thousand pounds of your own. Come to me
then, and I shall listen."

"Could you not listen now, Colonel?"

"I can hear you now. Listening is another matter."

Thus the engagement existed in every respect except the one
recognized by fathers and solicitors.

One morning Hughie went to see his friend Alan Trevor,
the painter. Trevor was a rough, red-bearded man, with a
freckled face and hands perpetually stained by colours that Nature
had never thought of combining. In society he looked like
a sailor who had insulted a duchess; in his studio
he became delicate, exact, and almost civilized. His pictures were
eagerly purchased by people who did not understand them and
passionately discussed by people who could not purchase them. This
is fame.

When Hughie entered, Trevor was standing before a large canvas.
On a raised platform in the corner stood an old
beggar.

He was a singular figure. A patched brown cloak fell
from his shoulders; one bare arm rested upon a crooked
staff; his boots were broken, his beard silver-grey, and his
face had the dry, folded look of a letter that
has travelled through many countries and brought bad news from
each. Before him lay a battered hat, turned upward with
professional melancholy.

"What an extraordinary model!" cried Hughie.

"A masterpiece," said Trevor, without looking round. "Velázquez would have
crossed Spain for him, and Rembrandt would have pawned his
furniture."

"The poor old fellow," murmured Hughie. "How miserable he looks."

"Naturally. I pay him to look miserable. Cheerful beggars are
fatal to art."

"What do you pay him?"

"A shilling an hour."

"And what will you get for the picture?"

"Two thousand guineas, if the purchaser has taste; three thousand,
if he has merely a drawing-room."

Hughie frowned. "Then he ought to receive a percentage."

"My dear Hughie, the business of the model is to
provide lines, not accounts. Besides, anguish cannot be painted by
the yard. Art has its privileges."

"So has hunger."

"Hunger," replied Trevor, stepping back from the canvas, "is extremely
picturesque at a proper distance."

At that moment a servant entered and whispered that a
frame-maker wished to speak with Trevor. Painters are always disputing
with frame-makers. The artist believes the frame exists for the
picture; the frame-maker knows that the picture exists for the
bill.

"Do not corrupt my model while I am gone," said
Trevor. "He has the most valuable sorrow in London."

He vanished through the door.

The old man remained motionless, but his eyes rested upon
Hughie with a curious brightness. Hughie felt in his pockets.
He found a sovereign and a few coppers. The sovereign
was to have paid for a pair of gloves for
Laura and a cab for himself. Love may walk, he
reflected, and gloves are merely civilization worn on the hands.

He crossed the studio and slipped the coin into the
battered hat.

The beggar looked down, then up.

"Thank you, sir," he said. His voice was low and
remarkably composed. "You are very generous."

"Oh, not at all," said Hughie, blushing. "I wish it
were more."

The old man's wrinkled face altered for an instant. It
was not quite a smile, but it suggested that happiness
had looked in at the window and decided not to
disturb the portrait.

Trevor returned.

"Stand as you were!" he exclaimed. "That expression is quite
wrong. You look as if someone had left you a
railway."

Hughie took his departure and walked to Laura's house, since
compassion had spent his cab fare.

"You gave away a sovereign?" said Laura, when he told
her. "Hughie, it was all you had."

"Not all. I have eightpence, a silver pencil, and an
aunt in Kensington."

"You absurd person!"

She kissed him, which encouraged him greatly. Women often reward
virtues that they would be alarmed to find in a
husband's balance sheet.

That evening Hughie dined at the Palette Club, where the
food was bad enough to be artistic and the conversation
good enough to be unpaid. Near midnight he found Trevor
smoking alone beneath a portrait of a celebrated critic, painted
without mercy.

"Well," said Hughie, "did you finish your beggar?"

"My beggar? Entirely."

"He must have been tired, poor soul."

"He endured it heroically. By the way, he was much
interested in you."

"In me?"

"Enormously. He asked your name, where you lived, what your
income was, whether you had expectations, and why you wore
so admirable a coat with so deplorable a pair of
gloves."

Hughie stared. "You told him?"

"Everything. Your professionless career, your aunt, Colonel Merton, Laura, and
the ten thousand pounds."

"My dear Alan! You had no right to discuss my
affairs with a model."

"Artists have no rights; we have curiosities."

"He will tell every beggar in London. I shall be
surrounded tomorrow by an army of confidential mendicants."

Trevor began to laugh.

"Why are you making that noise?"

"Because your army could purchase Europe."

"What do you mean?"

"The old man in the cloak was Baron Hausberg."

Hughie put down his glass.

"Baron Hausberg—the banker?"

"The same. He owns houses in five capitals, mines in
three continents, railways in countries whose names you could not
spell, and sufficient government bonds to make patriotism profitable. He
could buy this club tonight and improve it by shutting
it."

"But why was he dressed as a beggar?"

"Because I am painting him as one. The idea was
his own. Millionaires grow weary of being painted beside pillars.
Poverty was a novelty."

"And I gave him a sovereign!"

"You did."

Hughie covered his face. "I shall never recover."

"On the contrary, you may recover your sovereign."

"Alan, this is monstrous. You should have warned me."

"And ruin the finest scene I have witnessed outside a
theatre? Never. When you gave him the money, Hausberg looked
as if human nature had suddenly paid a dividend."

"He must think me an imbecile."

"He thinks you delightful."

"That is what people say when they mean an imbecile
with good manners."

Trevor laughed again, and Hughie went home in a condition
of magnificent humiliation. All night he imagined the Baron recounting
the episode at royal dinner tables while ministers and archdukes
applauded. A man may survive being ridiculous in private; society
was invented to prevent him.

At ten the next morning a carriage stopped before Hughie's
lodging. From it descended a solemn gentleman in black, carrying
gloves in one hand and discretion in the other. His
card announced him as Monsieur Gustave Naudin, private secretary to
Baron Hausberg.

Hughie's heart sank. The sovereign was to be returned ceremoniously.
Perhaps there would be a receipt.

"Monsieur Erskine?" asked the visitor.

"Yes. I fear you have come about my unfortunate mistake."

"Mistake, monsieur?"

"Yesterday—in Mr. Trevor's studio. I assure you I had no
intention of being offensive. The Baron's disguise was so complete,
and I am occasionally rather stupid."

Monsieur Naudin bowed. "The Baron has never complained of completeness."

"I hope he will accept my apology."

"The Baron instructed me to deliver this."

He presented an envelope, sealed with a crimson crest. On
the outside were written the words: *A wedding gift to
Hughie Erskine and Laura Merton, from an old beggar.*

Hughie opened it. Inside lay a cheque for ten thousand
pounds.

For some moments he said nothing. Astonishment, unlike conversation, improves
by silence.

"Is this—quite serious?" he managed at last.

"Baron Hausberg," said Monsieur Naudin, "is serious only about money
and generosity. He considers both too important for jokes."

"But ten thousand pounds!"

"The Baron observed that a man who gives one sovereign
when he has only one possesses a rarer fortune than
a man who gives ten thousand when he has several
millions. He hopes you will permit him to exchange the
currency."

Hughie sat down.

"Monsieur," continued the secretary, with another bow, "the Baron also
hopes to attend the wedding. He has never before been
invited to one by a gentleman who mistook him for
a pauper."

"He shall sit beside the Colonel," cried Hughie. "It will
improve them both."

Colonel Merton examined the cheque that afternoon with the reverence
old soldiers reserve for reliable artillery.

"I said ten thousand pounds," he remarked. "I did not
specify how you were to earn it."

"I didn't earn it, sir."

"No fortune ever admits that."

The wedding took place six weeks later. Laura looked radiant,
Hughie looked astonished, and Baron Hausberg looked exactly like a
millionaire, which disappointed Trevor. The Baron gave away the bride,
for the Colonel declared that a man who had provided
the money might reasonably assist with the ceremony. Trevor was
best man and made a speech so brilliant that nobody
understood it until the following morning.

After dinner he told the story of the portrait.

"Artists require models," he concluded, lifting his glass, "and society
requires millionaires. But it is not every day that one
finds a millionaire who is a model—or a model millionaire."

The Baron smiled, Laura laughed, and Hughie blushed as beautifully
as ever.

And since generosity had accomplished in one careless moment what
prudence had failed to do in several industrious years, everyone
agreed that virtue was its own reward—provided, of course, it
selected its beggars with discrimination.

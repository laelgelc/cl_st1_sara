I am dead, gentle reader; yet be not alarmed at
the confession, for I have neither the power nor the
desire to shake the curtains of thy bed, to extinguish
thy lamp, or to print upon thy warm wrist the
cold similitude of fingers long dissolved. I am a quiet
ghost, and, upon the whole, a happy ghost enough. If
at times I sigh among the yew-trees, it is because
the wind remembers for me; if I whisper beneath a
casement, it is that some solitary dreamer may gather from
my murmur a thought which mortal speech, that heavy vehicle,
must needs bruise in bearing.

I died young. That is a little sentence, but it
contains the ruins of many palaces.

In childhood I had divided life into splendid provinces. Boyhood
would sport beside the stream; youth would love beneath the
moon; manhood would journey, labour, sing, and gather honour; age,
seated among affectionate faces, would dream the whole bright pilgrimage
again. I had chosen my books, my friends, my bride,
my laurel, and even my grave. The last was in
a secluded hollow where three old beeches leaned together over
a spring. Wild thyme grew there; a ruined chapel stood
upon the hill; and at sunset one narrow ray passed
through its broken eastern window and lay upon the grass
like a golden blessing. Thither, thought I, they shall carry
me when my three-score years and ten are ended.

Alas! Death consulteth neither youthful calendars nor picturesque preferences.

A fever, impatient as flame, consumed in seven days the
body which I had expected to employ for seventy years.
My relations, good souls, wept, disputed, ordered black cloth, and
buried me in the town cemetery, because my chosen hollow
was damp, distant, and inconvenient to the undertaker. Thus was
I laid beneath a square stone in the most crowded
quarter of the dead, between a merchant who had demanded
a marble angel and an alderman whose virtues occupied eighteen
carved lines.

Grave! Grave! how strangely are thy inhabitants lodged! The living,
who complain if a neighbour's wall stand too near their
window, thrust us shoulder against shoulder, coffin against coffin, as
though death rendered privacy indecent. Yet the spirit hath room
where the body hath none; and I soon learned that
my prison was not the narrow box in which my
bones descended, but the remembrance, desire, vanity, and sorrow that
descended with them.

For a season I hovered close above my burial-place. I
could rise no higher than the cypress tops. When I
strove toward the stars, something drew me downward—not a chain
visible to the eye, but the weight of unfulfilled intention.
My unwritten verses clung about me like damp garments; journeys
never begun hung from my feet; the face of the
maiden I had meant one day to love—though I had
not yet met her—shone vaguely before me and enticed me
back to earth. I mourned less for what I had
lost than for what I had expected to possess; and
expectation, gentle reader, is sometimes a stronger fetter than memory.

By degrees I became acquainted with my companions. Ghosts do
not converse precisely as mortals do. We have no lungs
for loud discourse, nor tongues to trip upon falsehood; but
thought, when earnest, passes from spirit to spirit as perfume
passeth through a garden. For ceremonious occasions, however, we knock
upon tombstones. Three light taps summon a friend; two slow
ones warn away an enemy; and a confused clattering commonly
signifies that some newly liberated shade hath not yet learned
the manners of the place.

The first who answered my summons was the merchant beneath
the marble angel. In life he had loved gold; in
death he could not endure it. Every midnight he crept,
in spirit, to the iron chest concealed under the floor
of his former counting-house. His heirs had never discovered it,
and this pleased him; yet each coin had become transparent,
and within it he saw the fraud, hunger, betrayal, or
hard-hearted bargain by which it had been obtained. There was
a widow's tear in one piece, an orphan's pallor in
another, a workman's broken hand in a third. He counted
them perpetually—not to know their sum, but to number his
transgressions.

"Wouldst thou not have the gold found and given to
the poor?" I asked.

"I would," said he, "if I could cease wishing that
my name be praised for the gift."

Thus even repentance may be soiled by vanity.

Near the northern wall wandered a murderer. No earthly tribunal
had condemned him; he had died honoured, with his children
kneeling around his bed. But the dead need no witnesses.
Upon his right hand there burned continually one dark crimson
stain. He washed it in rain, in dew, in moonshine;
he plunged it into the shadow of the church; he
rubbed it against the cold grass till the blades trembled;
yet still the blood appeared, fresh as at the fatal
moment. Worse than the stain was the sensation: he felt
forever the last warm pulse of his victim beneath his
palm.

Mortal man, boast not that thy secret is safe because
another's lips are closed. Conscience hath an immortal memory.

There were also two lovers buried in adjoining graves. They
had loved unlawfully, passionately, destructively; each had sacrificed duty to
the other, and each, after death, accused the other of
the sacrifice. An ethereal cord bound them breast to breast,
so that they could neither embrace nor separate. What had
once seemed ardour was now mutual imprisonment. They repeated old
endearments with bitterness, old quarrels with tenderness, and were jealous
even of the worms that divided their dust.

A husband came sometimes to seek the spirit of his
first wife; but she had risen long before, having loved
him without possessing him. His second wife, lately dead, hid
behind her monument to avoid him. Such are the little
comedies of the cemetery, where marriage vows are interpreted by
a court from which custom, property, and convenience are excluded.

Not all who lingered were criminal. Upon the tallest monument
sat an old soldier, watching continually toward the east. He
had given his youth, his blood, and three sons to
his country; yet patriotism, though noble, held him to the
soil as surely as avarice held the merchant. Each rumour
of war passed through him like thunder. When the bells
rang for victory he rose almost to the clouds; when
they tolled for defeat he sank among the roots of
the graves. He could not ascend while one frontier remained
threatened or one statesman appeared foolish—which, as I once ventured
to observe, might detain him considerably.

He smiled, for the dead are not insensible to a
reasonable jest; but he resumed his watch.

Another shade haunted not his tomb but a library. He
had been a poet, celebrated at his death and forgotten
within twenty years. Whenever a student opened his book, the
poor spirit brightened; whenever the volume closed unread, he faded.
Praise was his atmosphere. He hovered over critics, inspired dissertations
concerning himself, and once remained three months in the chamber
of a scholar who had mistaken one of his poems
for another man's. The injury seemed to him more terrible
than oblivion.

"Let it go," I urged. "What is a name to
an immortal intelligence?"

"What is immortality without a name?" he replied.

So fame, which promiseth to preserve a man after death,
may only preserve him from heaven.

I beheld, too, the power of grief. Many a spirit,
already light enough to rise, was detained by the tears
of those below. A mother, bending nightly above the bed
of her bereaved child, could not depart while the child
cried to her. A little boy lingered near his grave
because his mother had placed his toys upon it and
begged him to return. His spirit stretched upward toward a
band of radiant children among the stars, then downward toward
the wooden horse blackening in the rain. O living hearts,
love the departed, but do not bind them! Let remembrance
be a lamp set in the window, not an iron
hook cast into the sky.

My own family mourned me according to their several natures.
My mother's sorrow was deep and silent, and therefore pure;
it enveloped me like a soft mist, but did not
drag me downward. My uncle praised my talents at every
dinner until I began to suspect that I had possessed
more genius dead than alive. A cousin borrowed my manuscripts
and published two youthful fragments under his own name. I
felt some indignation, but when I perceived how little applause
they gained him, my anger became almost celestial.

Years passed, if that dim succession of moons and snows
may be called years. The flesh fell from my bones;
my name grew faint upon the stone; the merchant's angel
lost a wing; grass entered the cracks of the alderman's
virtues. Yet I remained, though no longer wholly unhappy. Earthly
sounds softened as they reached me. The laughter of children
beyond the wall, the distant bell, the cry of the
night-bird, the rustle of lovers walking where they believed themselves
alone—all became parts of one low music, mournful chiefly because
it could not endure.

Then she came.

It was in late autumn. The moon shone through a
vapour so thin that the whole cemetery appeared submerged in
silver water. Near the eastern boundary stood an ancestral vault,
long closed, bearing above its door the weather-worn arms of
a family once illustrious and now merely respectable. Torches approached;
black figures moved among the stones; prayers were spoken; and
into that chamber they carried the body of a maiden
scarcely nineteen years old.

I knew at once that she had lived purely—not because
flowers covered her coffin, nor because mortals called her angel,
for the wicked are often magnificently buried—but because, when her
spirit awoke, no shadow clung to it. She rose from
the vault like fragrance from a white flower. At first
she seemed bewildered. Her eyes sought familiar faces among the
departing mourners; her hands extended toward an aged father who
walked as though each step descended into deeper night.

"Father!" she cried.

He did not hear; but his grief heard, and fastened
one trembling thread between them.

I approached gently.

"Maiden," said I, "thou art not alone."

She turned, and the faint starlight passed through her without
being diminished. Never in life had I beheld beauty so
free from the tyranny of feature. Her loveliness was not
of cheek, eye, lip, or hair, though memory lent her
at moments the semblance of all these; it was the
visible harmony of an innocent soul.

"Art thou dead also?" she asked.

"For a considerable time," I answered; "and therefore, if experience
confer authority, qualified to be thy guide."

She smiled. That smile lifted me higher than all my
previous struggles. For one instant I saw the cemetery beneath
me—the monuments little and pale, the trees like moss upon
the earth—and above, beyond, immeasurably above, a multitude of tranquil
fires, toward which my spirit yearned as flame yearneth through
smoke. Then remembrance of myself returned, and I sank again
beside her vault.

Her name had been Eveline, but among the dead names
are garments lightly worn. She had lived in the great
house beyond the hill with her widowed father, books, music,
and a garden enclosed by ancient walls. Illness had kept
her apart from companions. She had loved the world without
having entered it, as one may love the sea from
hearing shells. Her few attachments were tender, never selfish; her
few regrets concerned kindnesses omitted rather than pleasures denied.

Each night I called her with three soft taps upon
the stone, though in truth the ceremony was needless, for
she always awaited me. Together we passed among the graves.
I showed her the merchant, the murderer, the lovers, the
poet, and the soldier. She pitied each without shrinking.

"Will they suffer forever?" she asked.

"Nay," I told her; "nothing impure can enter heaven, but
neither can aught created by Heaven be abandoned. They remain
till greed have learned generosity without praise; till guilt have
confessed itself wholly; till passion be freed from possession; till
love of country widen into love of all; till fame
become indifferent to its own echo. Their torments are not
vengeance, but purification."

"And thou?" she said.

The question pierced me more delicately than reproach.

"I had dreams," I replied, "and loved them too well.
I desired to be great, beloved, remembered. Perhaps I am
detained till I can surrender the life I never lived."

"Then surrender it," she whispered.

Easy words! Yet spoken by her, they became wings.

From that hour my old ambitions loosened. The imaginary bride
faded without bitterness; unwritten books ceased clamouring within me; distant
mountains I had wished to climb seemed no more sacred
than the mound covering my dust. I began to understand
that no earthly experience is lost to the soul which
hath learned its meaning, and no pleasure truly possessed by
him who hath merely consumed it. Eveline and I rose
nightly above the cypresses. Sometimes we reached the church spire;
sometimes the clouds received us, cool and luminous, and through
their openings we saw the upper stars.

She grew brighter as she grew lighter. The thread connecting
her to her father alone detained her. He visited the
vault each evening and remained until darkness hid even the
door. She bent beside him, laying an unseen hand upon
his white head.

"Must I leave him?" she asked.

"Thou must cease fearing to leave him. Love will remain
when sorrow releases thee."

Thereafter she entered his dreams. She did not speak of
death, but walked with him in the old garden beneath
summer trees. Gradually his grief changed. He still wept, yet
no longer called her back. He gave her books to
a lonely child, opened her garden to the poor, and
caused the flowers intended for her grave to be carried
to the sick. Each generous act severed a fibre of
sorrow. At last the thread between them shone, not downward
like a chain, but upward like a ray.

Our happiness became almost hope. I thought—vain ghost that I
remained!—that we should ascend together. Had she not taught me
release? Had not her purity entered my substance like dawn
into mist? Often, floating above the chapel tower, we gazed
toward that celestial country of which no mortal words can
bear report. Music descended thence, though "music" is too gross
a name; fragrance shone, though "fragrance" cannot shine; thought became
light, and light affection. I told her that when the
summons came we would rise hand in hand.

She answered only, "If Heaven permit."

One winter night the snow lay smooth over every grave,
concealing rich marble and pauper's clay beneath one equal whiteness.
The air was very still. I approached the vault and
gave our accustomed signal.

Three taps.

No answer.

Again I struck, more loudly, disturbing an ancient baronet who
complained from the next enclosure.

"Eveline!"

Silence.

I entered the vault. Her coffin rested where it had
been placed; the flowers upon it were dust; but the
delicate brightness that had always hovered there was gone. I
searched among the yews, above the church, along the moonlit
fields. I called till the lesser spirits stirred uneasily in
their graves.

"She is gone!" said the poet, with envy.

"She is free," said the murderer, covering his crimson hand.

The old soldier pointed upward.

Then I perceived, very high among the stars, a new
tenderness of light—not a star, perhaps; not she, perhaps; yet
my whole spirit recognised it.

She had ascended without me.

O gentle reader, hast thou ever been left by one
who went not merely to another land, but into a
degree of being where thy grief could not follow? My
loneliness was sharper than at death. I sank upon my
own neglected grave and envied the bones beneath it, because
they felt nothing. For one impious moment I accused her:
had we not hoped together, risen together, loved—if ghosts may
use that perilous word—together?

But pure affection cannot long accuse. As I watched the
high, misty brightness, peace descended from it. She had not
deserted me; she had merely obeyed. To have delayed her
with one regret would have made my love another chain
like that of the guilty lovers. I blessed her departure,
though the blessing tore me as it rose.

Afterward I found that I could ascend farther than before.
Grief, when unselfish, is lighter than joy. I passed beyond
the clouds and heard, at times, intimations of that celestial
harmony; yet some small remnant still recalled me. Perhaps it
was the desire to tell my story. Perhaps no spirit
who hath loved beauty can depart until he hath attempted
once to communicate it, though mortal language crush the finer
intelligences of the soul as a coarse hand crusheth the
dust from a moth's wing.

Thus I sought a living mind.

In a garret overlooking the cemetery dwelt a solitary writer.
He was poor, unknown, and sufficiently melancholy to be receptive.
At midnight he sat beside a little lamp, his paper
empty, while ambition and despair disputed behind his brow. I
entered with the wind, breathed upon the flame, and laid
my thought against his dreaming mind.

He started, dipped his pen, and wrote.

Not all that I gave him reached the page. Where
I sent radiance, he wrote "pale light"; where I breathed
the vast tenderness of purified spirits, he wrote "sadness"; where
I revealed Eveline, he described a maiden in a white
dress with blue eyes—she had possessed neither dress nor eyes
when I knew her. Such is authorship: the immortal visitor
brings fire, and the mortal host complains of smoke.

Still, he served me well for a season. Night after
night I whispered of graves, stars, guilt, longing, and release.
He believed the thoughts his own, which is a pardonable
error among writers. His pages accumulated. Editors praised their "singular
imagination." Ladies found them sweetly pensive. A critic accused him
of excessive familiarity with tombs, though the critic himself had
buried many reputations.

Success came; and with it carpets, dinners, applause, and a
waistcoat too brilliant for spiritual intercourse. My dreamer ceased watching
the cemetery. He wrote by daylight, revised according to fashion,
and learned to manufacture melancholy without feeling it. When I
approached, I found his mind crowded with reviews, invitations, and
calculations of profit.

I tapped upon his thought.

"Not tonight," he muttered in sleep. "The publisher waits for
a comedy."

So I left him. Let no author boast too confidently
of his inspiration; the angel, ghost, memory, or sorrow that
whispers today may tomorrow seek a purer ear.

And now, maiden—thou living maiden who readest beside a shaded
lamp, while the household sleeps and one star looks through
thy casement—I turn to thee. Fear me not. I ask
neither vow nor tear, neither flower upon my grave nor
remembrance of my name. I would but breathe into thy
youth a gentler wisdom. Love, but do not possess; grieve,
but do not summon back; create, but give thy creation
freedom; cherish thy country, yet forget not mankind; pass through
beauty toward its source, and through sorrow toward compassion.

Perhaps, when thou dreamest, I shall show thee an old
garden, a ruined chapel, and two spirits rising above the
winter trees. Perhaps thou wilt wake with no memory save
a fragrance that no flower gave, or a brightness that
lingereth after dawn. Follow that brightness. Make thy soul light
ere death strips it of clay, so that no guilt,
ambition, jealousy, fame, nor even excessive sorrow shall bind thee
to the earth.

For I believe my own release is near. Often now
the cemetery dwindles beneath me; often the upper stars appear
less distant; and in their softest region shines that remembered
tenderness which once answered when I called.

Eveline waits—or, if heaven knoweth no waiting, she remembers.

And when thou also hast been purified, maiden; when I
have guided thee as she guided me; when affection hath
become blessing, and memory hath lost its weight—then neither thou
nor I shall wander among graves.

We shall rise.

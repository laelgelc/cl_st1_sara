### Eleanor's Christmas List

"O, dear!" sighed Eleanor Stuart, dropping her ivory pencil across
the gilt-edged sheet before her. "I do wish Christmas came
but once in ten years."

"My dear!" said Aunt Margaret, looking up from her sewing.

"I do indeed. It is the most tiresome season in
the whole calendar."

Aunt Margaret smiled, but made no answer.

Eleanor sat before a bright fire in a bright room,
and looked, it must be confessed, as little like an
object of compassion as a young lady could look. A
soft crimson gown fell about her in rich folds; a
slender chain shone at her throat; two rings sparkled on
the white hand which supported her head. Near her stood
a marble-topped table covered with pretty trifles—silver boxes, painted fans,
card cases, books in costly bindings, and several small figures
which had no particular use except to be dusted and
admired.

Yet Eleanor sighed again.

"Every body has got every thing," she continued. "That is
the difficulty. Mamma has three embroidered shawls, two lace mantles,
more scent-bottles than she can ever empty, and six work-boxes.
Papa never uses the slippers I made last year, and
he has got canes enough for a regiment. Cousin Mary
has got every book that is worth reading, and a
good many that are not. Kate has got bracelets, brooches,
lockets, rings, and ear-rings; and as for Tom and Will,
they laugh at every thing one gives them."

"Then why give them any thing?" asked Aunt Margaret.

"Why, because it is Christmas."

"Ah!"

"And they will give me presents, of course."

"Ah!" repeated Aunt Margaret.

Eleanor lifted her head and regarded her aunt suspiciously.

"You know what I mean. One must give presents to
one's friends."

"Must one?"

"Certainly. It would look very odd not to do it."

"So you are not thinking what would please them, but
what would prevent you from looking odd?"

"That is putting it disagreeably," said Eleanor, coloring.

"Truth sometimes looks plain in its morning dress," replied Aunt
Margaret. "We may trim it with ribbons afterward, if you
like."

Eleanor laughed in spite of herself, and took up her
list.

"Here is Kate. I thought of a gold thimble; but
she has two."

"She might sew with all three at once."

"Now, aunt, you are laughing at me. For Mary I
had chosen a pearl card case, till I remembered she
received one on her birthday. Tom might have a seal,
only he never seals his letters; and Will says he
wants nothing but a horse, which I cannot afford. Then
there are the Morrises, and the Lanes, and the three
Miss Mayhews. Every body has got every thing, I tell
you."

"And you have twenty-five dollars to spend?"

"Thirty," said Eleanor, with some pride. "Papa gave me twenty,
and I saved ten."

"Saved it from what?"

"From gloves, ribbons, confectionery, and such things."

"A severe economy."

"It was, indeed. There was a blue velvet bonnet at
Madame Celeste's which exactly suited me."

Aunt Margaret drew her needle through the gray wool upon
her lap.

"So you denied yourself a seventh bonnet that you might
purchase a third gold thimble for Kate, who seldom sews."

"When you say it in that way, it does sound
foolish."

"Suppose we try another way."

"What way?"

"Suppose you give to somebody who has not got every
thing."

Eleanor glanced around the room, as if such persons could
scarcely be expected to exist beyond its warm curtains.

"But I do not know any body who really wants
things."

"Do you not?"

"No. At least—not among our friends."

"There are people in the world who are not upon
our visiting list."

"Poor people, you mean?"

"Poor people, I mean."

Eleanor's face fell a little.

"One can give money to societies," she said. "Mamma subscribes
every year."

"That is excellent. But would you like to know what
your money did? Would you like to choose, and plan,
and imagine the pleasure beforehand?"

Eleanor turned her pencil between her fingers.

"I might. But what could I buy? Bread and wood
are not very Christmas-like."

"Are they less so than card cases?"

"They are not pretty."

"Bread is pretty to a hungry child, and a fire
is beautiful to one who is cold."

There was a brief silence. The soft coal settled in
the grate with a rosy fall of sparks.

Aunt Margaret folded her work.

"Shall I tell you about some neighbors of mine?"

"Yes," said Eleanor, though not very eagerly.

"In a narrow house behind Mason Street lives a shoemaker
named Robert Bell. He was a strong, industrious man two
years ago, and earned day wages sufficient to keep his
wife and four children neatly. Then a cough fastened upon
him. He worked while he could; afterward he worked when
he could not; and now he sits by a little
window, bending over shoes until his hands tremble and his
breath fails."

"Is he consumptive?" asked Eleanor.

"I fear so. His wife washes and irons, mends, cooks,
nurses him, and tends the children. Last week I found
him lying upon a cot, with his coat spread over
his feet because there was no second blanket. His youngest
boy stood by the bed eating an orange."

"An orange?"

"One which a market-man had given him because it was
bruised. The child had saved half for his father. Robert
tasted it, and said, 'I used to think nothing was
so refreshing when I was a boy.' Then he smiled
and gave the piece back."

"Poor fellow!" cried Eleanor.

Aunt Margaret's eyes rested quietly upon her.

"He has longed for fruit," she continued, "but fruit costs
money; and when there is scarcely bread enough for six
mouths, oranges and grapes appear as far away as diamonds."

Eleanor drew the paper nearer.

"How much would some oranges cost?"

"That depends upon how many good fairies bought them."

"I am not a fairy."

"No? Your purse might prove a tolerable wand."

Eleanor wrote, in a doubtful hand, "Oranges."

"And grapes," she added. "Consumptive people like grapes, do they
not?"

"Very much."

"What else do they need?"

"Mrs. Bell needs a warm dress. Her old one is
thin at the elbows, and she goes out before sunrise
to carry home washing. The eldest girl has no stout
shoes, the two boys have patched trousers, and the little
lame child—"

"Lame, too?"

"Only one of the children. Little Nan fell when she
was a baby and has never walked well. She sits
much of the day on a wooden stool, helping her
mother by keeping the youngest amused. When I asked what
she hoped Santa Claus would bring, she said, 'One whole
orange, with nobody to want half.'"

Eleanor's pencil stopped.

"She shall have a dozen whole oranges."

"Would not two or three be enough?"

"No. A dozen," repeated Eleanor. "And stockings. Children always like
stockings."

"When they have none without holes, they like them exceedingly."

"And shoes for the eldest girl. What is her name?"

"Ruth."

Eleanor wrote it down. Her list, which had lately seemed
a desert, began to fill rapidly.

"Now tell me about some one else."

Aunt Margaret resumed her sewing, but a little smile passed
over her face.

"There is young Susan Gray, who lives above the baker's
shop."

"Is she very poor?"

"Very. Her husband went west to find employment and died
of fever before he had been gone three months. Susan
supports herself by making shirts. She is not much older
than you."

"Not older than I?"

"Perhaps a year. She has a baby with round black
eyes and the most astonishing quantity of hair."

"Is it a pretty baby?"

"Every baby is pretty to its mother, and this one
has claims upon less partial persons. Susan is generous, too.
Last month, when she had only a little tea left,
she divided it with an old woman in the next
room."

"Then we must give her tea."

"You are improving wonderfully."

"And sugar. Tea without sugar is miserable. Does she want
clothing?"

"The baby wears neatly mended garments. Susan keeps him wonderfully
clean. But yesterday she stood before a pedler's basket and
looked for a long time at a purple velvet cap
trimmed with white fur. It was quite too splendid, and
quite too dear. She asked the price, laughed, and said,
'My baby must keep his old hood.' Then she bought
two needles and went away."

"A purple velvet cap!" exclaimed Eleanor. "That is Christmas-like. I
shall buy it."

"Perhaps the baby needs flannel more."

"He shall have both. One present for need, and one
for beauty."

"An excellent principle, if need comes first."

Eleanor put "Flannel—cap—tea—sugar" beneath Susan's name.

"I begin to understand," she said. "It is pleasant to
buy for people who have not got every thing. Why,
there are twenty things one may think of!"

"There are more than twenty forms of want in one
street."

The words were gently spoken, but they checked Eleanor's excitement.
She looked at her aunt with a graver expression.

"Have you any more neighbors?"

"Many."

"Tell me of the poorest."

Aunt Margaret laid down her work again.

"In the upper part of an old house near the
river live two sisters, Martha and Jane Wilkins. Martha is
eighty-eight, Jane nearly ninety. They have outlived parents, brothers, husbands,
and children. Once they owned a little shop; but sickness
took their savings, and age took their strength. Now they
wind silk when work is brought to them, and earn
a few pennies."

"Two old women all alone?"

"All alone, except when Mrs. Flynn below climbs the stairs
to see whether they are living. Their garret has a
broken window stuffed with rags. Snow drifts through the roof
in severe weather. The stove is cracked, and last Tuesday
their fire went out at noon."

"Why did they not put in more coal?"

"They had no more."

"No coal at all?"

"Three small pieces lay in a box, but they were
saving them for evening."

Eleanor looked involuntarily toward the blazing grate. The heat had
become almost oppressive, and she pushed her chair back.

"What did they eat?"

"Bread softened in hot water."

"With tea?"

"There was no tea."

"Meat?"

"No."

"But surely some one takes care of them!"

"Some one must always begin, my dear. Every body is
apt to believe that some one else has begun already."

Eleanor's cheeks were no longer languidly pretty; they were flushed
with earnest shame.

"I never thought—"

"No," said Aunt Margaret kindly. "That is the trouble of
many comfortable people. They do not refuse; they do not
think."

"What would make the old women comfortable?"

"A load of coal would seem royal wealth. A small
stove that did not smoke, warm shawls, flannel petticoats, tea,
sugar, rice, and a little meat would make their room
another world."

"How much does a stove cost?"

"A second-hand one could be bought cheaply."

"Then put it down."

"You are holding the pencil."

"So I am."

She wrote so rapidly that the point broke.

"A stove, coal, two shawls, flannel, tea, sugar, rice, meat—O,
aunt, thirty dollars will not do it all!"

"No."

"I wish I had not bought that silver belt-buckle last
week."

"Wishing will not melt it into coal."

"I could return it. It is still in its box."

"That is for you to decide."

"And I might give up the blue bonnet."

"You have already given that up, in imagination."

"Mamma owes me five dollars. Papa might advance my allowance.
But no—I wanted these to be my presents."

Aunt Margaret bent to pick up the broken pencil point.

"There is no merit in spending what belongs to another.
Yet neither is it necessary that you should relieve every
hardship before New Year's day. Choose wisely, do what you
can, and do it lovingly."

Eleanor leaned her chin upon both hands.

"Lovingly," she repeated. "That is what is wanting in the
other presents, is it not? I buy Kate a thimble
because she buys me a bracelet; then next year she
must buy something larger, and I must buy something finer,
though neither of us cares for what we receive."

"Presents between friends may be very sweet."

"O, yes. I would not give up every thing."

"Nor should you. A little remembrance, chosen because it recalls
a shared walk, a favorite book, a kind word, may
carry affection more truly than a jewel. But the costliest
thing, bought merely because custom demands it, is hard, cold,
and glittering. Love does not live in the price-mark."

"Then what makes a true present?"

"The heart that chooses it, and the need or happiness
it answers."

Eleanor read over her altered list. Oranges, grapes, stockings, shoes,
flannel, cap, tea, sugar, shawls, coal, stove—the common words seemed
to glow upon the paper.

"Suppose," Aunt Margaret continued, "that you were nearly ninety, without
child or grandchild, in a room where the wind shook
the window and the embers were dying. Suppose you heard
bells, and carriages, and merry voices in the street below;
suppose you knew that warm houses were full of lights,
that tables were loaded, and that young people were exchanging
gold boxes and painted trifles. Which would seem the more
precious to you—a pearl ring laid on a centre table
where twenty rings had lain before, or a basket of
food at your own door?"

"The basket, of course."

"And which would carry the greater blessing?"

Eleanor did not answer immediately.

"I have been very selfish," she said at last.

"Do not waste time calling yourself names. Self-reproach which does
nothing is only another luxury. Fill your list."

Eleanor smiled faintly and obeyed.

"But why," she asked after a while, "should we do
this especially at Christmas? Poor people are cold in January,
too."

"They are, and charity must not end with the holly.
Yet Christmas gives us a special reminder. What are we
celebrating?"

"Our Savior's birth."

"And where was He born?"

"In a manger."

"Not in a palace, though He was a King; not
among the rich, though the world was His. He came
lowly, without room at the inn, and all His life
He drew near to the weary, the sick, the hungry,
and the outcast. If we fill our houses with brightness
in memory of Him, yet forget those whom He called
His brethren, have we kept His birthday well?"

Eleanor's eyes dropped to the list.

"No," she said softly.

"The evergreen and gifts and feasting are pleasant; I love
them as well as you. But the merriment grows warmer
when it reaches beyond our own door. A Christmas candle
loses none of its flame by lighting another."

"I will remember," said Eleanor. "And I shall begin to-morrow."

* * * * *

Three days later, the Stuart hall resembled a country shop
after an earthquake.

Bundles lay upon the chairs, boxes blocked the stairs, brown
paper crackled under every foot, and a black iron stove
stood near the door with so solemn an appearance that
the footman had twice attempted to carry it away.

Tom and Will entered together and stopped in astonishment.

"Hallo!" cried Tom. "Has Eleanor taken to trade?"

"Dry goods, groceries, hardware, and fruit," said Will, reading the
labels. "A comprehensive business."

Eleanor appeared at the head of the stairs with a
purple velvet cap in one hand and a pair of
woollen stockings in the other.

"Do not touch those parcels!"

Tom lifted a thick gray shawl and threw it around
his shoulders.

"How becoming! Is this my Christmas present, Nell?"

"Put it down."

"Mine must be the flannel petticoat," said Will. "I always
suspected she knew my taste."

"If you unfold another thing, I shall call James."

"James is occupied guarding the stove," said Tom. "Is that
for Cousin Kate? It will look charming upon her dressing-table."

"Perhaps the coal is for Mary," added Will. "A delicate
attention. She complained that her last bracelet was too cold."

Eleanor came down, endeavoring to look offended, though laughter pulled
at the corners of her mouth.

"You are both absurd."

"And this?" Tom held up the baby's cap between finger
and thumb. "Surely this is for Will."

"Too small," said Will. "Tom's head contains less."

Eleanor snatched the cap away.

"It is for the prettiest baby in town."

"Indeed!" Tom's eyebrows rose. "And since when have you numbered
babies among your fashionable acquaintance?"

"Since I found one who needed a cap."

Will glanced from the bundles to her face.

"What is all this, Nell?"

Before she could reply, Aunt Margaret entered carrying a list.

"It is good fairyism," she said.

"Then the stove is the magic chariot," said Tom, "and
the coal the winged horses."

"You may laugh," Eleanor answered; "but I bought these things
to make some people laugh who are not accustomed to
laughing."

There was enough earnestness in her voice to silence them.

She told them first of Robert Bell and his longing
for fruit; then of his tired wife, Ruth's worn shoes,
and lame Nan, who desired one whole orange. She told
of Susan Gray, who had given away half her tea
and left the purple cap in the pedler's basket. Last
of all she described Martha and Jane Wilkins sitting beside
a dying fire, saving three pieces of coal for night.

Tom slowly removed the gray shawl from his shoulders and
folded it—not very skilfully, but with unusual care.

"Are all these for them?"

"Yes. At least, as much as I could buy. Aunt
Margaret found the stove, and the grocer gave us the
tea at a lower price when he heard where it
was going. I returned my belt-buckle, too."

"The silver one you were so proud of?" asked Will.

"I found I was prouder of the stove."

Tom examined the iron article with new respect.

"How much are you short?"

"Short?"

"You intended something else. I know that face."

Eleanor hesitated.

"I wanted a warm dress for Mrs. Bell and a
little meat for the old sisters. But my money is
gone."

Tom put his hand into his pocket and produced a
crumpled bank-note.

"Here are ten dollars."

Eleanor stared.

"Tom!"

"I meant to buy a new riding-whip. My old one
can endure another season."

"Are you in earnest?"

"Painfully so. Good fairyism appears to be catching."

Will searched his pockets, brought forth two dollars, several coins,
a knife, and a button.

"You may have the money," he said, "though I reserve
the knife and button for future charities."

Eleanor threw her arms around Tom, and then around Will,
taking both young gentlemen so entirely by surprise that they
submitted without protest.

Aunt Margaret looked down at her list. Perhaps her eyes
were dim; but older persons sometimes find small print troublesome
in winter.

"What shall we buy with it?" asked Tom.

"The dress first," said Eleanor.

"Red," suggested Will.

"Brown," said Aunt Margaret. "It wears well."

"And meat," Eleanor added. "And perhaps a doll for Nan."

"A whole orange and a whole doll," said Tom. "She
will become dangerously extravagant."

"Then you approve?" Eleanor asked.

"I do more than approve. I demand a part in
the expedition."

"So do I," said Will. "I shall deliver the stove."

"You will drop it on your toes."

"Then I shall suffer in a worthy cause."

The next morning was clear and keen. Bells rang over
roofs powdered with snow, and shop-windows shone like treasure caves;
but no window appeared half so splendid to Eleanor as
the loaded sleigh before her door. There were baskets under
the seat, parcels at their feet, shawls tucked into corners,
and the purple cap resting safely in Eleanor's muff. The
little stove followed in a cart, attended by Will as
proudly as if it had been a cannon captured in
battle.

We might follow them first to the Bells' narrow room,
and see Robert lift a cluster of grapes with wondering
fingers; we might hear Nan laugh over the golden army
of oranges and press her doll against her cheek. We
might climb above the baker's shop, where Susan would set
the purple cap upon her baby's black curls and declare,
with tears and smiles together, that no prince had ever
looked so fine. We might mount the broken stairs to
the garret, where two old faces would brighten at the
sight of coal, tea, warm shawls, and the small black
stove destined to make a summer in the midst of
snow.

But our story spins on too long already, and happy
doors are best opened sometimes by imagination.

Eleanor went out that morning a rich young lady wearied
of giving; she returned a good fairy, though a very
human one, with cold toes, an empty purse, and a
heart so warm that she scarcely felt the winter. And
when her own costly presents glittered beneath the Christmas tree,
she liked them well enough; yet none gave her half
the pleasure of remembering that, in three humble rooms, people
not accustomed to laughing had laughed because she had learned
at last what a present meant.

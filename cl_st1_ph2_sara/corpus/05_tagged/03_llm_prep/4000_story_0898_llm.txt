### The Honor of Alphonse Pettipon

There were men aboard the *Voltaire* who believed that the
ship was propelled from Havre to New York by twin
screws of fourteen thousand horsepower; there were engineers below who
held that steam, properly compressed and governed, deserved some small
portion of the credit; and there was Captain Vaugirard, who
would have maintained, over coffee and cognac, that the vessel
advanced solely because he stood upon the bridge and permitted
it.

All were mistaken.

The *Voltaire* crossed the Atlantic because Monsieur Alphonse Pettipon, second-class
steward, kept cabins 214 to 231 in a condition agreeable
to Providence.

He was a short, spherical man, composed chiefly of pink
cheeks, polished buttons, and professional conscience. His black hair, diminished
at the summit but courageous at the sides, lay upon
his head in two damp crescents. His mustache was small
and exquisitely pointed. Three chins descended ceremoniously into his collar,
and when he hurried along a passage they moved one
after another, like dignitaries following a funeral.

In the making of a bed, Pettipon was not merely
competent. He was inspired. He approached a rumpled berth with
the grave compassion of a physician approaching a sick child,
and departed from it with the modest satisfaction of an
artist who has corrected Nature. His sheets did not wrinkle;
they reposed. His pillows were not beaten; they were persuaded.
Dust, confronted by Monsieur Pettipon, lost its courage and fled
into cracks where even there it was pursued, arraigned, and
ignominiously removed.

He had served the Compagnie Transatlantique Universelle for twenty-two years,
three months, and one day.

The day mattered.

"Without the day," he had once explained to his wife,
Thérèse, "one tells a falsehood. A little falsehood, perhaps—but honor,
my cabbage, is made of little truths."

Thérèse, who was large, practical, and unimpressed by arithmetic unless
it concerned household expenses, had replied that honor might also
be made of bringing home sufficient money on Saturday.

This was why Pettipon desired promotion.

His wages were respectable for a second-class steward, his passengers
were occasionally generous, and on each voyage he conducted private
calculations concerning tips with the solemnity of a finance minister
balancing the budget of a nervous republic. Cabin 216 contained
an American manufacturer of buttons, likely ten francs if his
digestion remained calm. Cabin 219 contained two Belgian aunts and
a niece, collectively six francs, unless the niece married the
young man from 225 during the crossing, in which case
emotion might produce eight. Cabin 228 contained an English clergyman
who asked for hot water four times each morning and
would probably leave nothing but a tract.

Yet all this did not make a violin.

His son Émile, aged fourteen, had music in him. The
boy could take the ancient instrument borrowed from Monsieur Leduc,
the baker, and cause it to speak so tenderly that
Pettipon's eyes filled, his mustache trembled, and Thérèse paused even
while counting potatoes. But Monsieur Leduc required the violin for
his daughter on Sundays, and its bridge was cracked, its
varnish resembled an old soup bowl, and one of its
strings had been replaced by a substance whose origin nobody
cared to investigate.

A proper violin cost money.

"A promotion," Pettipon would whisper while fitting fresh linen over
a mattress. "Only one little promotion, and Émile shall have
an instrument with a soul."

He had been told, at intervals during the preceding seven
years, that his name was being considered. It seemed to
him that his name must be undergoing an extremely thorough
consideration, possibly by a committee of philosophers.

Nevertheless, he remained hopeful. He was punctual, polite, incorruptible, and
clean. Above all, clean. During one hundred and twenty-seven crossings
he had lost two teaspoons, broken one water carafe, and
received only three complaints: one from a woman who objected
to the sea, one from a man who held Pettipon
personally responsible for moonlight entering his porthole, and one from
an infant whose criticism, though loud, had not been put
in writing.

His record was perfection.

Then, on the third morning out from Havre, perfection screamed.

The scream came from cabin 223.

Pettipon was in the corridor carrying towels in graduated sizes,
from the majestic bath sheet to the modest square suitable
for the chin. He stopped. A second scream followed, masculine,
furious, and accompanied by the violent ringing of a bell.

He entered.

The occupant of 223 was Monsieur Anatole Brizard, a commercial
traveler in artificial flowers. He was long, yellowish, and untidy.
His beard grew in six directions. His necktie appeared to
have been tied during an earthquake. His boots, which Pettipon
had aligned neatly the previous night, had wandered once more
to opposite regions of the cabin.

Brizard stood beside the lower berth in his nightshirt, pointing.

"What," he demanded, "is that?"

Pettipon looked.

On the immaculate white plain of his sheet, near the
pillow, moved a small brown object.

It was alive.

For three seconds the engines continued, the sea rolled, and
the ship maintained its westward course. But for Alphonse Pettipon
the universe had ceased. The stars went out. Civilization folded
like a badly packed shirt. Twenty-two years, three months, and
one day collapsed beneath six tiny legs.

His towels dropped to the floor.

"No," he whispered.

"Yes!" cried Brizard. "A bedbug!"

Pettipon flinched as if the word itself had struck him.
He looked toward the door, terrified that it might have
escaped into the passage and contaminated innocent ears.

"Monsieur," he pleaded, "not so loud."

"Not so loud? I shall be heard in Paris! I
shall be heard in New York! I shall be heard
by every respectable sleeper between the two!"

"You must not say it."

"I must not—? There it is!"

Brizard seized the creature between thumb and forefinger. Pettipon gave
a little moan.

"Do not destroy it!"

"What?"

"It is evidence."

"Evidence that your ship is crawling!"

"My ship does not crawl!" cried Pettipon, suddenly magnificent. "My
ship advances at twenty knots. And my cabins—monsieur, my cabins
are temples! Look about you. Observe the basin. Pass the
finger beneath the shelf. Examine behind the life belt, where
lesser stewards permit civilizations of dust to establish themselves. You
will find nothing. Nothing!"

"I found this."

"Impossible."

Brizard held it nearer.

Pettipon recoiled, then leaned forward. He examined the insect with
the dreadful fascination of a jeweler studying a flaw in
the crown diamond.

"It is not of this cabin," he pronounced.

"It was in the bed."

"But it is not of the bed."

"Then perhaps it came for the sea air."

"Monsieur Brizard," said Pettipon, pressing both hands to his heart,
"when you embarked, you wore a brown overcoat."

"What has my overcoat to do with it?"

"Everything! I observed that coat. It had traveled. It had
known railway compartments, hotels, perhaps even waiting rooms. I do
not accuse. Heaven forbid that Alphonse Pettipon should accuse a
passenger! But objects form attachments. A traveler carries souvenirs. One
man carries postcards, another carries cheese, another—"

Brizard's eyes bulged.

"You say I brought it?"

"I say only that before monsieur entered cabin 223, cabin
223 possessed no such animal. After monsieur entered—"

"Out!"

"But the specimen—"

"Take it and go!"

Pettipon found a clean envelope in his pocket, one intended
for the distribution of luggage labels. With trembling delicacy he
induced Brizard to drop the creature inside. He sealed the
flap, wrote upon it CABIN 223, THIRD DAY, 9:17 A.M.,
and added, after reflection, ALIVE WHEN DISCOVERED.

Then he gathered his fallen towels. They were now morally
soiled and would have to be replaced.

In the corridor he leaned against the wall. His cheeks,
ordinarily pink as breakfast radishes, had become gray. Perspiration gathered
upon his forehead in large official beads.

What was to be done?

A coward might say nothing. A lesser steward might remove
the insect, burn the bedding, spray the cabin, and allow
the Atlantic to close over the incident. But Pettipon was
not a lesser steward. He had a record. A record
must be defended, even when shattered.

He would report to Monsieur Crépine, head steward of the
second-class section.

Crépine was discovered in the linen room, where he was
arguing with a laundress about pillowcases. He was thin, sharp-nosed,
and permanently irritated, as if someone had put a crumb
in his soul.

"Well?" he snapped.

Pettipon stood at attention, clutching the envelope.

"Monsieur Crépine, you know me."

"I know too many people. What is it?"

"For twenty-two years, three months, and a day I have
served this company."

"If you have come to resign, do it after luncheon."

"Resign!"

Pettipon's three chins shuddered.

"Name of a name, monsieur, do you suppose I wish
to abandon the *Voltaire*? She is my second wife. My
first wife is Thérèse, who would not permit a second
wife, but this is metaphorical. I speak of devotion."

"Speak more briefly."

"In one hundred and twenty-seven crossings, monsieur, I have made
approximately sixty-one thousand beds."

"You have counted them?"

"I have estimated with restraint. Of those beds, not one—never—not
under any flag—not in calm, storm, fog, heat, cold, or
the regrettable voyage when seven passengers were seasick upon the
same staircase—not one has produced this!"

With tragic ceremony he extended the envelope.

Crépine took it, opened it, and peered inside.

"A bug," he said.

Pettipon staggered.

The damnatory word had now been spoken by a superior.

"You see it, then."

"I have eyes."

"And you do not strike me?"

"Why should I strike you?"

"Because it was in 223."

"Then remove it from 223."

"It has been removed. It is here."

"Then why are you troubling me?"

Pettipon stared. Such coldness was inhuman. Clearly Crépine had already
judged him and found the offense too vile for anger.

"Monsieur," he said brokenly, "I understand."

"I wish you did."

"You will not reproach me before the laundress. That is
delicacy. I thank you."

"What are you talking about?"

"I know the regulations. I know what such a discovery
means. One spot upon a sheet is a negligence. One
hair upon a basin is a disgrace. But this—this is
Waterloo! This is the guillotine descending upon the neck of
a career!"

Crépine closed his eyes.

"Pettipon, change the bedding."

"I shall burn it."

"You shall send it to the laundry."

"I shall burn the laundry."

"You shall do nothing of the kind."

"But my honor!"

"Your honor may wait until after luncheon. We are short
four stewards, the Belgian woman in 219 has lost a
hatbox, and somebody has put a haddock in the smoking
room. Go back to work."

He handed over the envelope.

Pettipon received it as a condemned duke might receive the
warrant for his execution.

"One question," he whispered. "Did Monsieur Brizard speak to you
before I came?"

"No."

"Ah! Then you heard from another."

"I heard nothing."

Pettipon nodded mournfully. Naturally Crépine would protect his informant.

As he left, the laundress said, "What has he got?"

"A bug," said Crépine.

From the passage came a muffled cry.

By eleven o'clock Pettipon had replaced every article in cabin
223, including two that could not reasonably have been implicated.
He examined the seams of the mattress through a magnifying
glass borrowed from a jeweler in 217. He fumigated Brizard's
coat while Brizard was at breakfast. He scrubbed the bed
frame, polished the brass, and interrogated the carpet with a
brush until its pattern nearly confessed.

Nothing.

This proved his innocence.

Or it would have proved it, had not Crépine's terrible
composure revealed that the case had gone higher.

Pettipon imagined memoranda moving through the ship like torpedoes: SECOND-CLASS
CABIN INFESTED. STEWARD SUSPECTED. TWENTY-TWO YEARS OF HYPOCRISY EXPOSED. He
saw his name removed from promotion lists and entered instead
in a black ledger kept at company headquarters. He saw
Émile raising an empty bow to an absent violin. He
saw Thérèse standing at the market, forced to purchase inferior
onions.

At noon he could bear it no longer. He must
appeal to the chief steward.

Monsieur Malvoisin, chief steward of the *Voltaire*, occupied an office
near the first-class pantry. He was a huge, slow man
with silver whiskers and a stomach of executive proportions. His
jokes had been issued so frequently that junior stewards could
recite them before he made them, but nobody dared.

Pettipon knocked.

"Enter!"

He entered sideways, though there was room enough, and stood
before the desk.

Malvoisin glanced up from a wine inventory.

"Yes, Pettipon?"

"Monsieur the Chief Steward," began Pettipon, "before you condemn me,
permit me to speak."

"Condemn you? Have you drunk the champagne?"

"I have never drunk company champagne. Once, in 1908, a
cork struck me, but that was not voluntary."

"What have you done?"

"Nothing! That is the tragedy."

He produced the envelope.

Malvoisin put on his pince-nez, examined the inscription, and opened
it.

The creature moved.

"Ha!" said Malvoisin. "A stowaway."

Pettipon did not smile.

"It was found in my cabin."

"Then charge it passage."

Malvoisin laughed deeply. It was one of his newer jokes,
and he was pleased by it.

Pettipon's knees weakened. Condemnation disguised as humor! The cruelty of
the great could be astonishing.

"Monsieur, I implore you not to jest upon the grave
of my reputation."

"What reputation?"

"My spotless reputation!"

"Ah, that one."

"I am a clean man. Ask my wife. Ask the
officers. Ask Georges Prunier, if you can find him."

"Who is Georges Prunier?"

"In 1911, Georges Prunier occupied cabin 198 on the old
*Diderot*. A man of coarse habits, monsieur, who ate sardines
in bed and used the counterpane to polish his boots.
Yet when he left the cabin—"

"What has that to do with this?"

"It demonstrates! After Georges Prunier, I purified 198 so completely
that Madame de Villiers, who occupied it on the return
voyage, declared that it smelled like a chapel. Those were
her exact words. Or perhaps a hospital. In any case,
a clean institution."

Malvoisin waved a heavy hand.

"Where did you find the thing?"

"In Brizard's berth."

"Then Brizard brought it."

Pettipon's eyes widened.

"You believe me?"

"Of course."

"Without witnesses? Without consulting the passenger list? Without examining the
moral character of the accused?"

"I do not care who brought it. Throw it overboard."

Pettipon clutched the envelope to his breast.

"Destroy the evidence?"

"Then keep it as a pet."

"Monsieur mocks me."

"Monsieur has work to do."

"Twenty-two years, three months, and one day—"

"Out!"

"One hundred and twenty-seven crossings!"

"Out!"

"Sixty-one thousand beds!"

"OUT!"

Pettipon backed from the office, bowing at every step. In
the corridor he collided with an assistant purser, apologized to
each of the man's buttons, and then stood trembling beside
a rack of silver trays.

The chief steward knew.

Worse, he laughed.

There are laughs that pardon and laughs that destroy. Malvoisin's
had been broad, ponderous, and unmistakably the laughter of a
man who had already discussed the offender with the captain.

The captain!

Pettipon's heart rolled over like a ship in a cross-sea.
To approach Captain Vaugirard without permission was nearly mutiny. Yet
if the captain had heard only the accusation, what chance
remained? Justice must hear both sides, even when one side
weighed less than a grain of rice and possessed six
legs.

He went below to the crew's quarters and took from
his locker a photograph of Émile. The boy stood stiffly
in his best suit, holding Monsieur Leduc's wretched violin. Pettipon
gazed at him.

"My little oyster," he murmured, "your father goes to battle."

At half past one Captain Vaugirard came down from the
bridge.

He was a lean man with a pointed white beard,
hard blue eyes, and the stride of one who regarded
doors as obstacles placed by subordinate minds. He could assess
wind, current, distance, tonnage, and the incompetence of mankind in
a single glance. When he spoke, words were expected to
salute.

Pettipon waited outside the chartroom.

"Captain!"

Vaugirard continued walking.

Pettipon trotted beside him, wheezing.

"Captain, one instant! A matter of honor!"

"Whose?"

"Mine."

"See the purser."

"It concerns the safety of the ship."

The captain stopped.

That phrase possessed power.

"What safety?"

Pettipon looked about, then lowered his voice.

"A creature has been found aboard."

"What creature?"

Pettipon extended the envelope.

The captain inspected it.

"What am I looking at?"

"The ruin of Alphonse Pettipon."

Vaugirard looked at Pettipon, then into the envelope again.

"It appears very small for the purpose."

"Captain, it was found in cabin 223, for which I
am responsible. But I swear upon my wife, my child,
and the sacred regulations of the company that it was
not there when we left Havre."

"Remove it."

"I have removed it."

"Then the matter is finished."

"No, Captain. Monsieur Crépine knows. Monsieur Malvoisin knows. You know.
Soon the officers will know. Then the crew. Then first
class. By dinner it will be in the wireless room.
Tomorrow New York will know!"

"If the wireless operator sends a message about that insect,
I shall dismiss him."

Pettipon took this as proof that messages had already been
contemplated.

"The passenger brought it," he said urgently. "Monsieur Brizard. Observe
him! His beard is a lodging house. His luggage has
no discipline. His overcoat—Holy Blue, Captain, his overcoat contains geological
periods!"

"Have the cabin disinfected."

"It is disinfected. I have scrubbed, steamed, polished, sprayed, searched,
and prayed."

"Excellent."

"But my record?"

"What record?"

The question pierced him. Pettipon's mouth opened and closed.

"My record," he repeated faintly. "Twenty-two years, three months, and
one day."

"Congratulations."

"One hundred and twenty-seven crossings."

"You must be tired."

"Never a blemish! Never an insect! There was a flea
once, aboard the *Pascal*, but it belonged to a dog
and was therefore in the charge of the baggage master."

Captain Vaugirard glanced toward the chartroom, where important matters awaited
him: winds, positions, reports of ice, the possible failure of
a condenser.

"Pettipon, are we taking water?"

"No, Captain."

"Are we on fire?"

"No, Captain."

"Has the rudder gone?"

"No, Captain."

"Is there fog?"

"A little, perhaps."

"An iceberg?"

"Not that I have observed."

"Then why are you speaking to me?"

"Because my honor is sinking!"

The captain exhaled through his nose.

"Listen carefully. I do not blame you. Crépine does not
blame you. Malvoisin does not blame you. Nobody blames you.
Kill that object and return to your duties."

He entered the chartroom and shut the door.

Pettipon remained outside.

Nobody blamed him.

The words should have brought comfort, but they did not.
They were too explicit. Innocent men were not told that
nobody blamed them unless blame was everywhere. It was the
language of mercy, and mercy, as Pettipon knew from illustrated
histories, was frequently extended just before execution.

He descended toward second class.

At the head of a stairway he paused and looked
through a porthole. The Atlantic was gray, cold, and convenient.
One step to the boat deck, one climb over the
rail, one plunge—and the waters would close over his dishonor.

But then somebody else would have to make the beds
in cabins 214 to 231.

Crépine might assign young Jules, who folded blankets unevenly and
had once left soap in a dish with water beneath
it.

No. Suicide would be selfish.

Pettipon remembered that Victor Ronssoy, president of the steamship line,
was aboard.

Monsieur Ronssoy traveled on the *Voltaire* twice each year and
occupied the Imperial Suite on A Deck, a region so
exalted that Pettipon had never entered it. The suite possessed
a sitting room, two bedrooms, a private dining salon, a
marble bath, and a steward named Baptiste who wore white
gloves and behaved as though second class were a distant
colony inhabited by cannibals.

To appeal over the heads of Crépine, Malvoisin, and Captain
Vaugirard to the president himself was unthinkable.

Pettipon thought of it.

By three o'clock his agitation had become too large for
his body. He polished shoes with tears running down his
cheeks. He mistook a Belgian aunt for her niece. He
brought the English clergyman cold water, then hot water, then,
in confusion, a cup containing three collar studs. At last
he placed the envelope inside his breast pocket, straightened his
jacket, and climbed.

From second class he ascended to first class as a
pilgrim might ascend a holy mountain whose guardians knew him
to be improperly ticketed. He passed carpets into which a
man's shoes disappeared apologetically. He passed vases of flowers, brass
rails, palms in painted tubs, and passengers who possessed enough
luggage to establish principalities.

Baptiste intercepted him near the Imperial Suite.

"Where are you going?"

"To see Monsieur Ronssoy."

Baptiste smiled without warmth.

"Monsieur Ronssoy is not seeing second-class stewards."

"He will see this one."

"He will not."

"It concerns a catastrophe."

"What catastrophe?"

Pettipon looked around.

"An insect."

Baptiste stared.

"In your head?"

"In an envelope."

"Go away."

Pettipon drew himself up. It did not greatly increase his
height, but it rearranged his chins with dignity.

"Baptiste, old turnip, I have served this company since you
were learning not to spill soup. Stand aside."

"Have you gone mad?"

"Entirely, if necessary."

At that moment the door opened.

Victor Ronssoy stood within.

He was tall, broad-shouldered, and iron-gray, with eyebrows substantial enough
to have been listed among the ship's equipment. He had
built the company from four elderly steamers and an office
over a fishmonger's shop into a fleet whose funnels were
recognized in every major port. Men who commanded ten thousand
tons became cautious when he entered a room.

"What is this?" he asked.

Baptiste bowed.

"A second-class steward, monsieur. He claims to have a catastrophe."

Ronssoy looked at Pettipon's swollen eyes, damp hair, and trembling
hands.

"Come in."

The Imperial sitting room contained more space than Pettipon's entire
apartment at Havre. A fire burned in a tiled grate.
Beyond the windows, the Atlantic lifted and fell with elegant
restraint, as if aware that it was visible from first
class.

Ronssoy seated himself at a desk.

"Your name?"

"Alphonse Pettipon, monsieur the President. Second-class steward, cabins 214 through
231, excluding 220, which is used for baggage when required."

"How long with the company?"

"Twenty-two years, three months, and one day."

Ronssoy's eyebrows rose slightly.

"You are exact."

"Cleanliness begins with exactness."

"And your catastrophe?"

Pettipon took out the envelope.

His hand shook so violently that he dropped it. He
snatched it from the carpet, horrified that the creature might
escape and establish a dynasty in the Imperial Suite.

"Monsieur the President, at nine seventeen this morning, in the
lower berth of cabin 223, I discovered—that is, Monsieur Brizard
discovered, but I attended immediately—this."

Ronssoy opened the envelope.

"A bedbug."

Pettipon closed his eyes.

The final judge had named the crime.

When he opened them again, tears stood upon his lashes.

"Monsieur, before you dismiss me, listen."

"I have not said—"

"Twenty-two years! Three months! One day! I have served in
the *Molière*, the *Pascal*, the first *Voltaire*, which rolled like
a drunken wardrobe, and this noble ship. I have crossed
one hundred and twenty-seven times. I have made beds for
the rich, the poor, the polite, the English, the seasick,
and once for a Portuguese magician who kept two pigeons
in a washbasin. Never has such a creature been found
in my charge."

Ronssoy leaned back.

Pettipon continued, gathering speed and moisture.

"I know every seam of every mattress. I know where
dust hides. Dust is cunning, monsieur. It waits behind pipes.
It disguises itself beneath wardrobes. It allies with fluff. But
I defeat it! I do not merely pass a cloth.
Any idiot can pass a cloth. I lift, inspect, pursue.
I have cleaned after Georges Prunier—"

"Who is Georges Prunier?"

"A savage with sardines, monsieur, but that history is lengthy.
I have scrubbed ink, medicine, soup, rouge, boot polish, and
a substance in cabin 230 which the chemist refused to
identify. I have never complained. I have never spared myself.
If a passenger rings at midnight, I come. If he
rings at two, I come. If he rings at four
to ask why the ship moves, I come and explain
the ocean."

Ronssoy's mouth twitched.

Pettipon mistook it for severity.

"The animal came with Brizard," he cried. "I do not
say the man is wicked. Perhaps he is innocent and
the overcoat guilty. But look at him! Look at his
beard! Search his luggage! Convene an inquiry! I shall submit
to questioning. I shall confront witnesses. I shall reproduce every
action from the moment we left Havre."

"That will not be necessary."

"Then I am condemned."

"No."

"Reduced?"

"No."

"Transferred to freight?"

"No."

Pettipon blinked.

"Then what?"

Ronssoy folded his hands.

"Why are you a second-class steward?"

The question was so unexpected that Pettipon had no prepared
tragedy for it.

"Because I have not been promoted."

"Yes, I had reached that conclusion. Why not?"

Pettipon lowered his eyes.

"Perhaps I am too fat."

"I employ many fat men."

"Perhaps I speak too much."

"That possibility has merit."

"Perhaps because I have no protector. My father was a
cooper. My wife's uncle knows a deputy, but the deputy
does not know my wife's uncle."

"What would you do with a promotion?"

Pettipon's face changed. The fear remained, but behind it appeared
something tender and shy.

"My son plays the violin."

"Does he?"

"Like an angel who has not yet had lessons. His
name is Émile. He borrows an instrument from a baker.
It is a dreadful violin, monsieur—an old box with strings—but
the boy makes it sing. With greater wages, I could
buy him one of his own. Not a grand violin.
I am not ambitious for Stradivarius. Something honest. Something that
stays in tune."

Ronssoy regarded him for a long moment.

Outside, the sea slid past the windows. The fire settled
softly in the grate. Pettipon stood with his head bowed,
holding his cap in both hands. He looked ridiculous and
loyal, pompous and humble, a plump little priest of folded
linen whose entire world had been overturned by a speck.

At last Ronssoy said, "Baptiste!"

The private steward entered.

"Monsieur?"

"How long have you attended my suite?"

"Three years, monsieur."

"And how often have you told me that the work
is too much for one man?"

Baptiste glanced suspiciously at Pettipon.

"Frequently, monsieur."

"You now have an assistant."

Pettipon looked behind him to see who had entered.

Ronssoy pointed.

"This man."

Baptiste's expression became that of an archbishop informed that a
turnip had been appointed canon.

"Monsieur the President?"

"Pettipon will retain his rank for this voyage and assume
the post of steward to my private suite upon our
return. The wages will be those of a first-class senior
steward. Inform Malvoisin."

Pettipon made a small sound.

It might have been "oo," or "la," or the soul
leaving his body from happiness.

"Monsieur," he said, "I do not understand."

"That does not matter. I do."

"But the insect?"

"What of it?"

"You will not dismiss me?"

"My dear Pettipon, any steward who can suffer this much
over one bedbug is the steward I want near my
bed."

The words entered Pettipon slowly. Then they expanded.

His cheeks flushed. His knees bent. His cap fell. He
attempted to bow, but emotion overtook architecture, and he sank
onto a gilt chair.

"A promotion?"

"A promotion."

"The private suite?"

"Yes."

"Greater wages?"

"Yes."

"A violin?"

"If you choose."

Pettipon rose with astonishing speed. He seized Ronssoy's hand in
both of his and kissed it before the president could
defend himself.

"My benefactor! My old cabbage! My emperor of steamships!"

"Control yourself."

"I am controlled! Behold my control!"

His entire frame shook. His cheeks quivered like pink jellies
on a dining saloon table during rough weather.

"I shall serve you as no man has been served.
Your sheets shall be snow! Your boots shall see their
faces in each other! Not one grain of dust shall
approach your threshold. If dust even thinks of this suite,
I shall know."

"I am sure you will."

"And Émile! My little oyster shall have his violin! Thérèse
will weep. She does not often weep, monsieur. She considers
it wasteful."

Ronssoy smiled openly now.

"Go and tell your family when we land. For the
present, return to your cabins."

"Yes, monsieur the President."

"And Pettipon—"

Pettipon stopped at the door.

"Throw that thing away."

He looked at the envelope in his hand.

The insect shifted inside, innocent of the careers it had
destroyed and created.

"Throw it away?" Pettipon repeated.

"Certainly."

He gazed at it with profound reflection.

An hour earlier it had been disgrace, dismissal, starvation, the
death of music, and a watery grave in the Atlantic.
Now it was promotion, first class, increased wages, and a
violin for Émile. Philosophers had built systems upon less.

"No, monsieur," he said.

Ronssoy stared.

"No?"

"With respect, it would be ingratitude."

He tucked the envelope carefully into his breast pocket and
patted it.

"This little animal came aboard with Monsieur Brizard—of that I
remain morally, scientifically, and spiritually certain. Yet by a miracle
it has become the benefactor of my house. I shall
preserve it."

"Preserve it?"

"In alcohol, perhaps. Or beneath glass. I know a jeweler
in Havre who makes charms for watches."

Ronssoy began to laugh.

Pettipon, unable to interpret laughter correctly even in triumph, bowed
with solemnity.

Then he descended from the Imperial Suite to second class,
radiant, promoted, and carrying against his heart the first lucky
bedbug ever appointed to a steamship steward's watch chain.

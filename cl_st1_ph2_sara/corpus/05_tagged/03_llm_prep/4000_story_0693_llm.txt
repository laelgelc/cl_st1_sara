I

The night which remains most distinctly in my memory—if memory
be the proper name for a picture that may never
have had an original—was in November of 1887. I was
traveling on horseback from Newcastle to Auburn, having missed the
afternoon train through an error concerning its time which I
attributed then to the station agent and afterward, with less
justice perhaps, to another person.

The road descended into a narrow ravine, where recent rains
had swollen the stream beyond its ordinary channel. There was
no danger to a prudent rider, but the moon had
not risen, the banks were densely wooded, and a white
vapor lay along the water like the breath of some
enormous sleeping animal. My horse picked his way unwillingly among
the stones.

At the lowest part of the road he stopped.

A man stood before us.

"Good evening, Mr. Manrich," he said.

No voice has ever given me so sudden an apprehension
of calamity. It was low, melodious and perfectly composed; yet
I had once heard it utter words which appeared to
destroy the distinction between life and death.

"Dr. Dorrimore!" I said.

He laughed lightly.

"The devil you are!" I added, with less ceremony.

"That is a professional opinion which I should hesitate to
confirm without examination."

He stepped nearer. Though five years had passed, he was
scarcely altered. He was a tall man, slender without frailty,
with black hair worn longer than fashion permitted and a
face of uncommon regularity, except that the eyes seemed placed
too deeply beneath the brows. When they caught what little
light there was, they glittered independently, as the eyes of
certain reptiles are said to do. His clothing, though dusty,
was that of a gentleman.

We shook hands. I wish I could affirm that I
refused, but truth—where it can be secured—should not be sacrificed
to dignity.

"I thought you were in India," I said.

"I have been in many places."

"And practiced many arts?"

"One art only: observing men."

He proposed that we ride together to Auburn. As his
horse was tethered behind a thicket, the proposal had the
character of an arrangement already made. During the ascent he
related something of his travels, but I do not recollect
it. I remember only the fog behind us, the measured
tread of the horses, and his occasional turning of the
head to look at me with an attention at once
courteous and intolerable.

At Auburn we took rooms in the same hotel. That
was not my wish, though I may have said it
was.

Before retiring, he laid one hand upon my shoulder.

"You have not forgotten our first acquaintance?"

"No."

"Nor what you saw?"

"I saw a trick."

"Certainly," he replied. "Everything seen is a trick of light."

Then he bade me good night.

II

Five years earlier I had met Dorrimore at a dinner
in San Francisco, given by a journalist who possessed the
reporter's generous hospitality toward any man accused of something interesting.
The doctor had lately returned from Calcutta and was described
as a physician, philosopher, prestidigitateur and scoundrel. The last qualification
was privately supplied by our host.

After dinner the conversation turned upon the thaumaturgists of India.
Dorrimore listened with amusement while several gentlemen explained feats they
had never witnessed, performed by men they had never met,
in a country they had never visited.

"The Hindu miracle-worker," he said at last, "does not deceive
the eye. He deceives the owner of the eye—a simpler
operation."

I asked if he spoke metaphorically.

"No. Metaphor is the refuge of persons unable to perform
the fact."

This impertinence pleased the others. They demanded a demonstration. Dorrimore
declined until I, having taken more wine than was suitable
to an argument about sobriety of perception, accused him of
prudence.

He looked steadily at me.

"You desire proof, Mr. Manrich?"

"I desire entertainment."

"You may have both."

He requested that I walk with him. We left the
house and went down a quiet street lying between warehouses.
The hour was late; the lamps were few, and a
wet wind came from the bay. Dorrimore spoke of indifferent
matters. Presently, at the mouth of an alley, he stopped.

A man lay upon the pavement.

His hat had rolled into the gutter. One arm was
bent beneath him, the other extended toward us with the
palm upward. I saw a dark wound at the throat
and blood upon the stones. Dorrimore knelt, turned the face
upward, and looked at me.

It was his own face.

I confess that I cried out and caught the wall
for support. Beside me stood Dorrimore; before me lay Dorrimore,
murdered.

"Your tricks are clumsy," I said, though my teeth made
the words uncertain.

The body opened its eyes.

With a slow movement it rose, first to its knees
and then to its feet. A sword passed through its
breast, the hilt projecting in front, the blade behind. Blood
ran down the waistcoat. The dead thing smiled.

I saw all this. I know that I saw it.

It came toward me. I attempted to retreat, but my
back was against the wall. The standing Dorrimore whispered close
to my ear:

"Which of us will you believe?"

The corpse lifted both hands.

Then there was no corpse, no sword, no blood. The
alley was empty. A wagon rattled at the farther crossing,
and a watchman walked past without turning his head.

Dorrimore offered me his arm.

At the house our companions were still at the table.
They declared that we had been absent less than ten
minutes. I said nothing of what had occurred, but Dorrimore,
taking up his glass, observed:

"Mr. Manrich has discovered that vision is the most obedient
of the senses."

The company laughed. Reporters, I have since noticed, are constitutionally
exempt from collective delusion; nothing that happens to another person
surprises them, and nothing that happens to themselves is permitted
to remain private.

I avoided Dorrimore from that night until our meeting in
the ravine.

III

On the morning after our arrival in Auburn I received
a note from Margaret Corray, to whom I was engaged.
It stated that she and her mother had come from
Oakland unexpectedly and were lodged at the Freeman House. The
handwriting was Margaret's; the phrasing was hers; even the unnecessary
loop beneath the final initial was a habit for which
I had gently reproached her.

I went at once.

Margaret received me with affection, Mrs. Corray with resignation. The
latter had never opposed our marriage, but regarded every prospective
son-in-law as a creditor whose account required vigilant examination.

While we were speaking, Dorrimore was announced.

I had not invited him. Nevertheless, I presented him, observing
that he was a former acquaintance. He bowed to Mrs.
Corray with grave deference and to Margaret with something less
definable. Within ten minutes the elder lady had asked his
advice concerning her heart, which until then had been an
organ of exemplary secrecy. Within twenty, Margaret was listening to
his account of Benares.

I shall not repeat that account. Either I did not
hear it correctly or it would now seem commonplace. I
recall his voice, Margaret's uplifted face and the intolerable ease
with which he occupied the room. He made no obvious
attempt to charm her; obvious attempts are the expedients of
fools. He merely spoke, and all other speech became interruption.

For the next fortnight we were much together. I say
"we," though frequently I was absent from the party to
which I belonged. Dorrimore arranged excursions, discovered views, prescribed drives
for Mrs. Corray's health and lent Margaret books in languages
she could not read. When I protested, she smiled.

"You are jealous of a scholar, Robert."

"I am jealous of a man."

"Then you concede the scholarship."

There was a levity in her manner foreign to her
character. I thought it acquired from him and hated him
for it.

One afternoon I encountered Dorrimore alone near the courthouse.

"You interest yourself greatly in my friends," I said.

"Your friends interest me."

"One of them is to be my wife."

"So you have told me."

"I tell you again."

He turned his dark eyes upon me. "Repetition is the
beginning of suggestion, Mr. Manrich. Take care what you teach
yourself to believe."

I struck at him. Or intended to. He caught my
wrist with astonishing quickness and held it without apparent effort.

"You are not yet angry enough," he said, releasing me.

That evening Margaret reproached me for insulting him. I asked
how she knew. She answered that Dorrimore had told her,
but at once denied having said so. We quarreled. Mrs.
Corray withdrew with a tact so elaborate that it resembled
attendance.

In the days following, Margaret became evasive. Twice she was
absent when I called; once I saw her return from
a walk with Dorrimore at twilight. The town itself appeared
to conspire with them. Its shuttered houses watched me; its
empty streets lengthened to delay me; the bell of the
little church counted hours that had been stolen from my
happiness.

My reason told me that I behaved like a melodramatic
schoolboy. My reason had no authority.

IV

Above Auburn, on a barren hill, there was an abandoned
cemetery enclosed by a broken wall. Mining operations had disturbed
part of the ground, and neglect had accomplished the rest.
Graves had sunk; stones leaned together like conspirators; roots, intruding
into the habitations of the dead, performed their unspeakable office
beneath the soil. It was a dishonor to the living,
a calumny upon the dead, and—if Heaven concerns itself with
municipal neglect—a blasphemy against God.

On the seventeenth night after Margaret's arrival I saw her
leave the Freeman House shortly before eleven. She wore a
dark cloak and carried no lamp. I followed.

The moon was nearly full, but clouds passed across it,
alternately revealing and extinguishing the road. Margaret went directly toward
the hill. She did not once look behind her. At
the cemetery gate Dorrimore stepped from the shadow.

They embraced.

I state what I saw. Whether it occurred is another
matter.

I crossed the road, forced my way through the gate
and advanced upon them. Margaret gave a cry. Dorrimore moved
between us.

"Go home, Manrich," he said.

"Stand aside."

"You have seen enough."

"I have seen too much."

He smiled. That smile was the final injury. I drew
the small revolver which I commonly carried when traveling. Margaret
called my name, but her voice seemed to come from
underground.

Dorrimore did not move.

I fired.

There was no report. The weapon in my hand had
become a sword—the same sword I had seen five years
before, wet with blood and passing through a dead man's
breast.

Dorrimore stood at a distance of twenty feet; then at
ten; then immediately before me. These changes occurred without his
appearing to move. His face was pale, his hair disordered,
his eyes without human meaning.

Behind him Margaret laughed.

I rushed upon him.

Of what followed I remember almost nothing. There was a
sensation of falling through branches, although no tree was near;
a pressure at my throat; lights revolving in blackness; and,
once, Margaret's face looking down at me from a great
height with an expression not of pity but curiosity.

I was found at dawn outside the cemetery wall by
two laborers. My revolver, unfired, lay beside me. There were
bruises upon my body, and marks at my throat resembling
those of fingers. For six days I was delirious.

These details were afterward related to me by the hotel
proprietor, a physician and others. Their accounts differed, as truthful
accounts commonly do. All agreed that I spoke continually of
Dorrimore and Margaret. Dorrimore had vanished. The clerk said he
left the hotel before midnight, taking no luggage. No one
at the Freeman House, however, knew Margaret Corray or her
mother.

When I was strong enough to walk, I went there
myself.

The register contained no such names.

"You have dreamed it, sir," said the clerk.

"For seventeen days?"

He shrugged. Hotel clerks are familiar with prodigies so long
as these incur no charge against the house.

I returned to Oakland as soon as the physician permitted.
Margaret met me at her mother's door. Her surprise at
my appearance was so natural that I accused her at
once.

She heard me without interruption. Then she brought letters, invitations
and witnesses proving that she had not left Oakland during
the entire period. Mrs. Corray confirmed it with offended precision.
Margaret had attended a concert on the night of the
cemetery affair and had afterward supped with friends. There were
twelve persons prepared to swear to it.

A year later she became my wife. She is so
still, and has never given me reason to doubt her.

That sentence, I am aware, is less reassuring than it
should be.

V

In 1894 a newspaper published an account of Dorrimore's arrest
in London. I preserve the relevant portion:

"Dr. Ambrose Dorrimore, an American physician long resident in India,
was yesterday examined at Bow Street upon charges arising from
experiments in hypnotic suggestion. The accused is alleged to possess
extraordinary influence over subjects of a nervous or imaginative temperament.
According to medical testimony, he can produce not merely transient
visions, but coherent hallucinations extending through days or weeks, during
which the subject converses with imaginary persons, visits nonexistent places
and performs actual acts in obedience to fictitious circumstances.

"Dorrimore studied under certain native practitioners whose methods remain imperfectly
understood. One witness declared that the doctor could cause a
subject to recognize strangers as intimate friends, to mistake one
locality for another, and even to supply retrospectively a complete
chain of false memories. The witness added that a susceptible
person might never discover the precise moment at which the
deception began or ended."

There was more: legal argument, medical caution and a reporter's
jocularity. Dorrimore was released for want of evidence. He afterward
disappeared.

The newspaper explanation is generally accepted by those to whom
I have related my adventure. Margaret accepts it. It accounts
for her phantom journey to Auburn, her apparent infidelity, the
cemetery meeting and perhaps the apparition in San Francisco. It
may account also for my missed train, Dorrimore's presence in
the ravine, and the marks upon my throat—though here the
physicians disagree.

But an explanation is not always a boundary. Sometimes it
is a door.

I know that Margaret is my wife. I know we
have lived together for years in a house overlooking the
bay. I know the arrangement of its rooms, the voices
of our servants, the books upon this table and the
quiet movement of her hand in the chamber beyond.

Yet I knew the dead man in the alley. I
knew the blood upon his breast. I knew Margaret's note,
her presence in Auburn, her treachery and her laughter among
the graves.

If Dorrimore could impose a false life for seventeen days,
why not for seventeen years?

And if I am still within it, whose hand is
now writing this?

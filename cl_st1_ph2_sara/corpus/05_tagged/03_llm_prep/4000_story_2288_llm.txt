### Yasha

At my age one no longer knows with certainty which
pictures belong to the little museum of one's own memory
and which have been introduced there, like doubtful portraits into
an ancestral gallery, by the testimony of nurses, aunts, elder
brothers, and those indefatigable old neighbours who remember not only
what occurred, but what ought to have occurred. I see,
for example, Yasha standing in the yard under the pear
tree, polishing a brass buckle with brick dust; yet I
cannot have been more than three years old, if indeed
I had been born at all, when he still wore
that particular belt. Did I see him? Did my sister
describe him? Or did I encounter, in some illustrated magazine,
another shaven servant beneath another pear tree, and afterward bestow
upon that stranger Yasha's narrow shoulders and solemn, yellowish face?

Such questions are useless. Memory is not a court of
law, and the witnesses whom it calls are all bribed.

Yasha belonged to my father, though no legal document, so
far as I know, established the possession. My father was
the district doctor of our village in Podolia, a mild,
untidy man whose official territory extended over more versts than
either he or his horse could reasonably traverse. He cured
peasants, scolded landowners, lent money which was never returned, and
kept his medical instruments in such disorder that only Yasha
could find them. Yasha, on the other hand, considered himself
neither a hired servant nor a member of the household,
but something older and more dignified than both: the guardian
of my father's honour, property, digestion, and public appearance.

He was perhaps forty when I first remember him clearly,
though to us children he seemed ageless, as servants and
trees do. His head was long, with black hair cropped
close over the crown; his cheeks and chin were shaved
every Sunday with a razor which he sharpened for half
an hour upon a strip of leather; his nose was
thin, his lips thinner still and habitually pursed, as though
the world had offered him some food which he suspected
of being badly prepared. His grey eyes could be hard,
comic, or unexpectedly mournful, but they were seldom idle. He
wore high boots, a dark waistcoat in every season, and
shirts of a whiteness which accused the rest of us.

The house itself was not clean. My mother had died
young, my father possessed a philosopher's indifference to dust, and
our cook, Yevka, held the opinion that floors should be
washed only before Easter, weddings, or episcopal visitations. Yet certain
islands in this sea of disorder shone with almost ecclesiastical
purity. My father's desk, though he covered it nightly with
prescriptions, crumbs, coins, tobacco ash, goose feathers, and anatomical drawings,
emerged every morning restored. The medicine chest glittered. The harness
hung in exact curves. The samovar reflected the windows. These
were Yasha's provinces, and within them negligence was treason.

He also owned a tin-bound trunk, which stood beneath his
bed and excited in us an interest out of all
proportion to its contents. There were three coats, one green,
one brown, and one black with velvet cuffs; two pairs
of trousers wrapped in newspaper; a silver-plated cup; seven religious
pictures; a cracked hand mirror; a packet of letters tied
with blue wool; a wooden comb; a brass button bearing
an eagle; several coloured prints of generals, saints, and beautiful
ladies with impossible waists; a child's red shoe; dried apples;
beeswax; a watch without hands; and little sacks containing, respectively,
salt, buckwheat, tobacco, buttons, and copper copecks. Nothing was useless
to him because everything had survived something.

"Why do you keep one shoe?" I once asked.

"And why, brother, have you two ears?" he replied. "To
listen twice and understand nothing?"

This answer satisfied me for several years.

His greatest public ceremony was the departure of my father
on his medical rounds. Our tarantass was old, its leather
hood cracked, its left wheel inclined inward with a humility
not shared by the right, and its cushions emitted dust
even when no one sat upon them. The horse Matsko,
said by Yasha to be of Polish blood, had crooked
forelegs, a pale eye, and two bladder-like growths upon his
belly. He was of an uncertain dun colour and possessed
the melancholy of an official long denied promotion. Nevertheless, in
Yasha's hands the setting forth of this invalid equipage became
a military spectacle.

First he would bring Matsko from the stable, addressing him
in tones of grave reproach.

"Stand, senator! You will receive His Excellency presently. Must you
shame Christendom?"

Matsko, who objected to the collar, would raise his head
and expose long yellow teeth.

"Aha! Rebellion also. Very good."

The harness was inspected buckle by buckle; the doctor's bag
was placed beneath the seat; a rug was folded, unfolded,
beaten, and folded again; the whip was set at the
proper angle. Then Yasha entered the house, brushed my father's
coat while my father still wore it, removed from his
pocket whatever books or bread crusts offended him, and led
him out with an expression suggesting that the health of
the empire depended upon punctuality.

Once seated, he gathered the reins high against his chest.
Matsko took three thoughtful steps, stopped, and turned his pale
eye toward us.

"March!"

Another two steps followed.

"To the devil with your politics!"

At last, with the whip cracking above but rarely upon
him, Matsko consented to trot, while Yasha sat erect, his
shaven profile implacable, and our tarantass rattled through the gate
like the carriage of a ruined sovereign.

There was also Bouton, a shaggy, low-bodied dog of no
acknowledged breed, with black ears, white eyebrows, and a tail
so sociable that it endangered cups upon low tables. Bouton
loved everybody—the postman, beggars, Jewish traders, drunken carters, children who
pulled his hair, and even the policeman, whose principal duty
in our district consisted of becoming godfather to an increasing
number of farmers' babies. Such promiscuity disgusted Yasha.

"A dog without principles," he would say. "Today he licks
the doctor's hand, tomorrow the hand of a thief. Is
that devotion?"

He never caressed Bouton in our presence. If the dog
entered the kitchen, Yasha ordered him out; if he lay
across the doorstep, Yasha nudged him sternly with a boot;
if he jumped up, Yasha called him "Turkish filth," though
no Turk had contributed, as far as we knew, to
Bouton's ancestry.

Yet I discovered one winter morning why Bouton followed him
as faithfully as his loose understanding of social obligations permitted.
Yasha was behind the woodpile, crouched over a wooden bowl.
Into it he poured warm broth, scraps of meat, and
half his own bread, first testing the temperature with a
finger. Bouton waited, trembling.

"Not yet, glutton. You will burn your lying tongue."

He saw me, stood up, and became terrible.

"If you speak of this, brother, I shall tell your
father who broke the blue bottle."

I had not broken it, but Yasha's knowledge of household
catastrophes was so extensive that I fled. Afterward I often
heard, late at night, a low murmur from his room
and Bouton's answering whine—a strange and touching duet between two
creatures who had agreed that affection must be concealed from
society.

In ordinary times Yasha was the most industrious man I
have known. He could mend a trace, pickle mushrooms, bleed
a patient if my father directed him, starch a collar,
bargain for oats, repair a roof, and calculate to half
a copeck how much the cook had cheated us at
market. He despised books, however, and regarded writing as a
physical infirmity.

"A man reads," he said, "because no sensible person will
talk to him."

His veneration for my father was boundless, but it was
not meek. He contradicted him, bullied him into warmer clothes,
removed vodka from his reach, and once refused to harness
Matsko because the roads were unsafe.

"I order you," my father said.

"You are a doctor, sir, not Almighty God."

"I shall go without you."

"You may go to the gate."

My father laughed and remained home.

It would be comforting to say that so useful and
faithful a being was rewarded with tranquillity. Human nature, however,
seldom respects the convenience of biographers. Once or twice a
year—usually after some insult so slight that no one else
had perceived it—Yasha drank.

His first act was to sell what he loved. A
coat went to the tavern keeper, the brass buckle to
a carter, the silver-plated cup to an old woman who
bought it for three eggs and resold it that afternoon
for vodka. He did not steal, nor did he ask
wages in advance. His degradation must be financed by sacrifice,
as if drunkenness were an expensive religious vow.

Then he appeared in the yard without his waistcoat, his
shirt open, his face inflamed and his speech at once
affectionate and insulting.

"Master!" he cried beneath my father's window. "Come out! Judge
me! A learned man—ha! A benefactor! He heals all Podolia
and cannot heal one fool. Come out, Nikolai Andreyevich!"

My father would open the window.

"Go to sleep, Yasha."

"Sleep? You command sleep? Give me money."

"No."

"Good! Excellent! A righteous refusal. Brother, give me twenty copecks."

"No."

"Ten."

"No."

"Then I spit upon wealth!"

And he would sit upon the well cover and weep.

On one such occasion Yevka, who enjoyed the misfortunes of
all men equally, told him that while he was drinking
she had cleaned my father's room better than he ever
did. We children, stupid with the innocent cruelty peculiar to
children, confirmed it.

"Yevka washed under the chest," my sister announced.

"And the windows," I added.

Yasha stared at us. The drunken looseness vanished from his
face, leaving it suddenly grey.

"Better?"

"Yes," we shouted. "Much better!"

He went into the stable and broke, with an axe,
the little shelf on which he kept harness oil, three
brushes, Matsko's comb, and a picture of Saint Nicholas. He
smashed the brushes, tore the picture, then lay down among
the splinters and sobbed so violently that Matsko, disturbed by
grief occurring outside his own body, kicked the partition.

The next morning remorse descended upon Yasha with almost supernatural
force. He crawled to my father's door, though he was
physically able to walk, and remained on his knees.

"Drive me away, master. I am a beast. Worse—a beast
does not sell a winter coat in November."

My father, tired and angry, said, "Very well. Go."

At this Yasha rose.

"No."

"What do you mean, no?"

"I mean I will not go."

"But you asked me to dismiss you."

"I asked justice from you. You gave foolishness."

"You smashed the stable."

"I shall mend it."

"You insulted me."

"I spoke in illness."

"You sold your clothes."

"They were mine."

My father tried not to smile.

"You are impossible, Yasha."

"Yes, sir. But where will you find another?"

There, in truth, the matter ended. The shelf was rebuilt
stronger than before, the picture of Saint Nicholas replaced by
one of the prophet Elijah, and Yasha's coat recovered from
the tavern keeper after negotiations in which my father lost
two roubles and the tavern keeper his temper.

His relations with women were obscure. He treated Yevka as
an enemy power and young women generally as gaudy, inefficient
creatures whose ribbons shed dye in rain. We were therefore
astonished when he announced, at the age of perhaps forty-five,
his intention to marry.

The chosen bride was Marfa Sidorovna, a widow at least
sixty years old, with one tooth, a red woollen shawl,
and a reputation for drinking lamp oil when vodka was
unavailable. She lived behind the railway station with a goat
and two daughters who had ceased speaking to her. Why
Yasha selected her has never been explained. Perhaps she reminded
him of someone; perhaps she possessed money, though no evidence
supported this; perhaps matrimony, like drunkenness, seized him as a
form of deliberate catastrophe.

He came before my father wearing the black coat with
velvet cuffs, carrying his cap in both hands.

"Sir, I request your blessing."

"For what?"

"For lawful marriage."

My father removed his spectacles.

"With whom?"

"With Marfa Sidorovna."

"The widow at the station?"

"She is not guilty that her husband died."

"She is old enough to be your mother."

"A wife should possess experience."

"She drinks."

"She will stop."

"She has one tooth."

"She will not bite."

My father looked at him for a long moment and
then said, gently, "No, Yasha. This is nonsense."

Yasha's face did not change. He bowed.

"Very well, master."

"Very well?"

"Yes. There will be no wedding."

He left the room, removed the black coat, folded it,
and never again referred to Marfa Sidorovna. When she came
to our gate two days later, intoxicated and shouting that
she had been betrayed by princes, officials, and eunuchs, he
watched her from behind the curtain until Yevka drove her
away with a broom. Was he relieved, humiliated, broken-hearted? We
children expected a scene, a drunken bout, at least the
destruction of another shelf. Nothing happened. This silence, more than
the proposal itself, unsettles me when I think of it
now.

For Yasha's passions were not shallow; they were merely conducted
in chambers to which no one else possessed a key.

The event that preceded his death occurred in autumn, when
the roads were black and the railway embankment smelled of
wet coal. A travelling clerk—or perhaps a discharged soldier; accounts
differed—hanged himself in the storehouse at our little station. As
my father was required to examine the body, Yasha drove
him there. The dead man had used a leather strap
fastened to a beam. He wore a blue shirt, one
boot, and a grey knitted sock on the other foot;
his head leaned sideways, and a lock of hair had
fallen across his forehead.

My father told us afterward that Yasha looked at the
corpse too long.

"Come away," he said.

"Does it hurt?" Yasha asked.

"Not after death."

"Before?"

"How should I know?"

"You are a doctor."

"Doctors know less about death than peasants suppose."

On the drive home Yasha was silent. He unharnessed Matsko,
rubbed him down, measured out the oats, and scolded Bouton
for muddy paws. Nothing in his conduct alarmed anyone. Yet
during the following days he questioned the station porter twice
about the stranger.

"Did he leave a letter?"

"No."

"Did anyone know him?"

"No."

"Then why did he do it?"

"How should I know?"

This answer, which ordinarily Yasha would have despised, appeared to
satisfy some portion of his mind while inflaming another.

A week later I entered his room without knocking and
found him before the cracked mirror. He had combed a
lock of hair across his forehead. His head inclined toward
his left shoulder; one foot was bare, the other wore
a grey sock. Around his neck hung a leather strap,
not tightened, merely placed there. Seeing me, he straightened and
removed it.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Nothing."

"You look like the dead man."

His expression frightened me—not because it was wild, but because
it was calm.

"You have not seen him, brother."

"Papa told us."

"Your papa tells too much."

Then he gave me a lump of sugar and sent
me away.

Why did I tell no one? A child's silence is
often judged by adults as secrecy, but it may be
only the inability to distinguish danger from play. Yasha had
impersonated a general with a broom, a priest with a
towel, and our district magistrate with a cushion beneath his
waistcoat. The dead stranger was perhaps another figure in his
private theatre. Moreover, death itself had no fixed meaning for
me. It belonged with railway whistles, churchyards, old photographs, and
the black dresses of aunts—a solemn region lying near life
but not, I imagined, able to enter our yard.

The last person of our family to speak with him
was my little sister Anna, then six years old. He
found her in the passage before supper and gave her
the brass button with the eagle, which he had recently
bought back from the carter.

"Keep this, little mistress."

"Why?"

"For remembrance."

"Are you going somewhere?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Where servants are not required."

She laughed, believing this a joke, and asked whether there
were sweets there.

"No," he said. "Only justice, perhaps."

Then, after a pause, he kissed her hand. This was
unlike him; he considered kissing children's hands an Italian affectation.
Anna ran to show us the button, and we quarrelled
over it.

The following morning Yevka complained that Yasha had not lit
the kitchen fire. My father called him. The yard was
searched, then the stable, the shed, the garden, the road
toward the tavern. Matsko stood unfed and struck the floor
impatiently. Bouton ran between us, whining, his coat wet with
dew.

At last the stable boy climbed to the attic for
hay and screamed.

Yasha had fastened a rope to the crossbeam. He wore
the blue shirt he used on weekdays, one boot, and
a grey sock; his hair lay across his forehead; his
head inclined to the side with that dreadful exactness which
proved, more clearly than any letter could have done, that
the unknown dead man had continued living in his imagination.
Nearby, arranged upon a grain chest, were his razor, his
comb, two icons, the broken watch, my father's clothes brush,
and half a loaf of bread for Bouton.

Of the hours which followed I retain disconnected fragments: my
father cutting the rope; Yevka crossing herself and then washing
the passage floor for no reason; the policeman arriving with
his tunic unbuttoned; Matsko neighing because no one had given
him water; my sister asking whether Yasha wanted the brass
button back. I remember, too, my father sitting at his
desk that evening with his face in his hands. It
was the first time I understood that a master might
love a servant, though neither would have used that word.

The official inquiry was brief. The policeman questioned my father,
Yevka, the station porter, and Marfa Sidorovna, who appeared voluntarily
and declared that Yasha had killed himself because the government
had forbidden their marriage. As the government had not heard
of it, this testimony was disregarded. The conclusion stated that
the deceased had acted in a condition of mental unsoundness.

That phrase brought relief to everyone. Madness is the sack
into which society puts whatever it cannot carry in its
hands.

Because he had taken his own life, the priest would
not bury him in consecrated ground. My father argued, then
pleaded, then offered money, which offended the priest without persuading
him. Yasha was buried beyond the village cemetery, near a
line of birches where paupers, strangers, and unbaptised infants lay
beneath low mounds. There was no proper service. Rain fell
steadily. My father, the stable boy, two peasants, and I
followed the cart. Why was I permitted to go? Perhaps
no one noticed me.

Bouton had been shut in the woodshed, but before the
earth was filled in we heard barking from the road.
He came running through the rain, trailing a broken rope.
At the grave he did not howl, as dogs do
in stories. He sniffed the wet soil, walked around it
three times, and lay down with his nose between his
paws.

My father tried to call him.

"Bouton! Come home."

The dog raised his head but did not move.

We left him there. That night he returned to the
yard covered in mud, ate nothing, and slept outside Yasha's
door. The next morning he was gone again. Peasants saw
him for several days near the birches. Someone took him
food; perhaps several people did. Then he disappeared. He may
have followed a wagon, been killed by wolves, or found
another household whose moral standards were sufficiently loose to accept
his universal affection. In our family, however, it was believed
that he died upon Yasha's grave, and I have no
desire to correct so fitting an invention.

Many years have passed. The house was sold, the pear
tree cut down, the railway station enlarged beyond recognition. Of
Yasha's possessions I kept for some time the watch without
hands; Anna kept the eagle button until her own children
lost it. Matsko survived his master by two years and
was bought by a miller, who praised his steadiness and
complained of his opinions.

As for Yasha, explanation has not brought him nearer. Was
his suicide born in the sight of that dangling stranger,
or had the stranger merely shown him a gesture for
which some hidden sorrow had long been waiting? Was the
proposed marriage another approach to the same precipice—an effort toward
degradation, rescue, companionship, or annihilation? Why did a man who
guarded every spoon destroy his coats for vodka; why did
one who spoke contemptuously of affection feed a despised dog
from his own bowl; why did a servant disobey his
master in everything except the abandonment of a bride?

Perhaps consistency is only the virtue of people whom we
do not know well.

When I close my eyes, I see him not in
the attic, thank God, but upright upon the tarantass, the
reins gathered ceremoniously in his hands, while Matsko advances with
wounded dignity and Bouton races beside the wheels, barking at
the world he loves too freely. Yasha turns his severe
profile toward us; he is proud, ridiculous, faithful, tyrannical, pure,
vain, self-destructive, and tender. He cracks the whip above the
old horse's back, and the whole shabby equipage moves away
through the Podolian dust with the grandeur of a procession.

Was he merely an unhappy servant whose mind gave way
before an image of death? Or was there in him
something of that vast, contradictory soul of our country—servile and
rebellious, practical and visionary, comic in its ceremonies, terrible in
its grief?

I do not know. I knew him all my childhood,
and I do not know him still.

On the morning after the Arrowic reached Queenstown, the New
York Daily Mentor contained the following editorial, telegraphed at great
expense across the Atlantic, and therefore, presumably, worth the money:

> OCEAN RACING AGAIN
>
> Is human life a trifle? Is the honour of
the American merchant marine to be hazarded for the gratification
of one man's vanity? These questions must be asked, and
asked with emphasis, in view of the astounding conduct of
Captain Elias Bramwell, commander of the steamship Arrowic.
>
> It is reported that, although New York Harbor lay
wrapped in fog, Captain Bramwell put to sea in order
to race the Dartonia. He had beneath his feet a
magnificent vessel, a valuable cargo, and no fewer than nine
hundred and forty human souls. Were these entrusted to him
that he might stake them upon a reckless contest of
speed? What is a captain's responsibility—to his owners, to the
public, to helpless women and innocent children?
>
> Happily, disaster did not follow. But because crime is
unsuccessful, does it cease to be crime? The Mentor trusts
that a searching inquiry will teach even the proudest ocean
commander that the press, speaking for civilisation, will not tolerate
the sacrifice of duty upon the altar of rivalry.

I read this aloud in the purser's room, chiefly because
the purser had begged me not to.

"The press, speaking for civilisation!" he cried. "The cheek of
it! Where was the Mentor?"

"In New York," I replied.

"Exactly. And where were we?"

"In the fog."

"Exactly again. Then how do they know?"

I explained that this was the peculiar advantage of an
editorial office. A man in the fog suffers from local
prejudice. He sees merely what occurs around him; whereas an
editor, sitting twenty miles away with his feet on a
desk, surveys the whole field without interference from facts.

The purser struck his table.

"That is journalism, is it?"

"It is the higher journalism."

"If a statesman makes a treaty, your editor knows why
he made it. If a bank closes, he knows where
the money went. If a railway train leaves the metals,
he knows which bolt broke before the engineer knows there
was a bolt. If dock labourers strike, he knows what
the men want, what the owners mean, and what the
public ought to think. He knows everything under the sun."

"And in fog," I added, "he sees farther than anybody."

The purser called this nonsense, and advanced the palpably absurd
contention that Captain Bramwell, who had commanded ships for thirty-two
years, might possibly understand his responsibility better than a penny-a-liner
collecting rumours on the Battery. As I am myself connected
with the press, I could not permit so dangerous an
attack upon an institution of which I am an ornament.
Nevertheless, I promised that, if he would stop pounding the
table, I would set down what I had seen.

This is the account.

The Arrowic lay on the western side of her pier,
and the Dartonia at the next wharf below. Both were
to sail at ten. This circumstance was enough to create
a race in the minds of several thousand persons who
had no intention of going to sea in either vessel.

The morning had begun clear, but by nine o'clock a
grey vapour was creeping up the bay. On the wharf
all was motion and noise. Porters rushed with trunks; belated
passengers rushed without them; friends who had said farewell came
back to say it again; cranes swung boxes over our
decks; steam escaped with a prolonged shriek; bells rang, gangways
banged, and a small boy, separated from his aunt, announced
the fact to all nations.

Beyond the sheds rose the black funnels of the Dartonia,
pouring smoke. Her passengers crowded the rails. Our passengers crowded
ours, each body examining the other with the amiable hostility
natural to people who have paid for rival accommodations.

At ten minutes before ten the Dartonia gave a deep
blast.

"There!" said a stout gentleman near me. "She means to
get away first."

"She is blowing off steam," said another.

"No, sir. I have crossed fourteen times. That blast was
defiance."

He afterwards became useful to me, and I shall call
him the nautical passenger. He knew the names of many
objects aboard ship, and when he did not know a
name he furnished one.

At ten precisely our last gangway fell. The tug at
our bow strained upon her line; the twin screws turned
slowly; the wharf began to slide away, carrying with it
waving handkerchiefs, uplifted umbrellas, and the small boy's aunt, who
had been discovered at the last moment and was now
weeping more loudly than he.

A cheer went up. The Dartonia moved almost simultaneously. Even
the ranks of Tuscany could scarce forbear a cheer at
that, and the two great vessels backed, swung, and headed
down the river within half a mile of one another.

"The race has begun," said the stout gentleman.

Captain Bramwell, upon the bridge, appeared unaware of it. He
was a lean, grey man, with a face that might
have been carved for the expression of patience. Beside him
stood the harbor pilot, red-cheeked and compact, peering ahead. The
captain looked at the water, the shipping, and the advancing
fog. He did not once look toward the Dartonia.

Off the lower city the vapour thickened. The shore dissolved
by degrees: first the buildings lost their roofs, then their
upper stories, then their walls. Ferry-boats appeared suddenly, black and
enormous, crossed our bow with a clang of bells, and
vanished as though they had never been built. From invisible
points came whistles—one blast, two blasts, a long melancholy bellow,
and once a rapid, indignant screaming which suggested that two
unseen craft had formed differing opinions concerning the right of
way.

Our speed diminished.

The Dartonia was still discernible astern, a long shadow with
two funnels. Then the fog took her.

"It is as thick as pea-soup," said the nautical passenger
cheerfully.

The fog, as if it had heard him and considered
the comparison inadequate, grew thicker. Moisture gathered on the rails
and dripped from the awnings. The cold pierced one's coat.
The bow disappeared from those of us standing amidships, and
the world became a white blank with a wet deck
in the middle of it.

The ship's gong sounded at intervals. Men were posted forward.
The pilot bent over the bridge rail, listening. Captain Bramwell
walked six paces, turned, and walked six paces back.

Then we came to a standstill.

The change was curious. A great liner in motion seems
a thing of intention; halted in fog, she seems a
hotel that has mislaid the earth. From close on our
starboard side came the jangle of an anchor chain.

"That will be the Dartonia," said the nautical passenger.

"How do you know?"

"She was over there when the fog shut down."

Presently her whistle sounded, near and distinct.

"She has anchored," he continued. "Sensible thing too. She will
wait until it clears."

"And we?"

"We ought to do the same."

This verdict travelled among the passengers and gave general satisfaction.
We had not anchored, but we were motionless. The screws
turned occasionally to hold us against the tide. Somewhere ahead,
unseen steamers passed across the channel. Their whistles came first
from one quarter, then another, while the clang of buoys
reached us with an unnatural clearness.

After perhaps twenty minutes a wind stirred. The white wall
thinned until I could see the forecastle, then a patch
of lead-coloured water, then a buoy rocking ahead.

Captain Bramwell stopped walking.

The pilot pointed. The captain glanced at the chronometer fixed
beneath the bridge screen.

"Slow ahead."

The screws revolved. We moved.

A murmur went through the passengers, followed by that silence
in which men make ready to say they had expected
the worst. The Arrowic crept forward, not at the pace
of a racing vessel, but at the pace of a
cautious cab horse. The buoy slid past, close on the
port side. For three minutes the air continued clear enough
to show a narrow corridor of water.

Then the fog rolled over us again.

"Stop."

The tremor beneath our feet ceased.

"What is he doing?" I asked.

The nautical passenger had obtained a chart from somewhere and
spread it upon a deck chair.

"He has gained the next safe position," he said. "There
is bad traffic crossing astern, but room enough here. He
is not racing the Dartonia."

"The Mentor says he is."

"Then the Mentor is wrong."

I asked him to be careful. Such words, spoken at
sea, might undermine the Constitution.

He tapped the chart. "He is racing the tide. If
we do not cross the bar before the ebb falls,
we must wait until afternoon. The Dartonia has chosen to
wait. Bramwell thinks the wind will tear lanes in the
fog, and he means to use them."

"And if it does not?"

"Then he will anchor."

This seemed so moderate an explanation that none of us
believed it until events compelled us.

For another quarter of an hour we lay still. The
captain resumed his six paces. The pilot listened, one hand
cupped behind his ear. A steamer's whistle sounded ahead, drawing
nearer. Our gong answered. The unseen vessel passed with a
roar of churning water so close that a woman cried
out; yet we saw nothing but a momentary darkness in
the vapour.

The wind came again. The fog lifted like a curtain
badly managed, revealing two buoys, a tug, and a three-masted
ship under tow across our course.

"Half ahead," said the captain.

We moved toward the tug, then slowed. The tow crawled
past. Its sailors stared at us from the mist, spectral
and dripping. The instant its stern cleared, the captain raised
his hand.

"Full speed ahead."

The deck thrilled; water boiled under the counter; the Arrowic
drove through the opening. For five minutes we had sight.
Then it shut down.

"Stop."

Silence once more, except for the wash against our plates.

The nautical passenger looked at his watch.

"Forty minutes before we lose the tide."

Nobody laughed at him now.

The third opening showed us the Narrows, or a portion
of them, with shore dimly visible on either hand. We
advanced six minutes and stopped again. The fourth lasted scarcely
two. The captain nevertheless gained another half-mile from it, placing
the ship where the channel broadened.

Each time the same procedure followed: the wind stirred, the
pilot searched the whitening distance, the captain consulted the chronometer,
and the engines answered. There was no shouting, no theatrical
running to and fro. The most excited person aboard was
a deck steward who had misplaced a tray of beef
tea.

"Twenty-five minutes," said the nautical passenger.

The fog closed.

"Eighteen."

It lifted, and we went ahead.

"Eleven."

We stopped.

By then the whole company had gathered wherever a glimpse
of the bridge could be obtained. The captain walked. The
pilot watched. The passengers whispered, as though noise from us
might make the fog thicker. Behind, the Dartonia's whistle sounded
at long intervals, growing fainter as tide and distance separated
us.

Seven minutes remained, according to our commentator, when a stronger
gust swept over the ship. For an instant the vapour
streamed in tatters, and ahead appeared the black shoulder of
a buoy, tossing violently. Beyond it lay another bank of
whiteness.

The pilot spoke rapidly. Captain Bramwell looked at the chronometer
and then at the moving curtain.

"Full steam ahead."

This time we did not creep. The Arrowic leaped to
the thrust of her twin screws. The buoy came rushing
toward us, swung past, and disappeared. The fog poured over
the decks. We could see nothing. The gong thundered. Men
forward stared into vacancy.

For perhaps ninety seconds we drove blind.

Then sunlight struck the upper rigging.

The white roof above us broke apart; brightness descended the
masts, touched the bridge, and flashed along the wet rails.
The fog streamed away astern in great ragged banners, and
before us, from horizon to horizon, lay the whole blue
ocean.

A cheer rose that shook the deck. Captain Bramwell merely
gave an order to set the Atlantic course, while the
pilot wiped his forehead and looked back toward the buried
harbor. We had crossed the bar with the last of
the tide.

The Dartonia came out five hours later.

That is how Captain Bramwell risked nine hundred and forty
lives in order to race her.

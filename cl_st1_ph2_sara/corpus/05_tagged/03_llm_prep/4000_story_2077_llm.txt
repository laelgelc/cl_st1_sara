At five o'clock Little Chandler laid his pen beside the
ledger and blotted the last blue figure with unusual care.
For several minutes he had been watching the clock above
the presses, though whenever Mr O'Rourke passed along the corridor
he bent quickly over his work and assumed an expression
of punctilious absorption. The hands moved with official slowness. Outside,
wheels rattled upon the quays; within, a clerk coughed, a
drawer was shut, and sheets of foolscap whispered beneath dry
fingers.

Ignatius Gallaher was in Dublin.

The thought had returned to him all afternoon, warm and
surprising. Eight years had passed since Gallaher had gone to
London with a second-hand portmanteau, three unpaid accounts and a
reputation which respectable men considered doubtful. Now his name appeared
under reports from Paris, Berlin and Rome. He had described
a royal funeral, a ministerial scandal, a murder in Whitechapel;
he knew editors, actors, racing men. That morning a note,
written in a broad hurried hand, had arrived at the
office:

*Dear Tommy—In town for two days. Meet me at Garvey's
Rooms, Fleet Street, half-seven sharp. We'll have a drink for
old times. Don't fail. Iggy.*

Little Chandler had read the note repeatedly. Its careless authority
delighted and disturbed him. Gallaher called him Tommy as if
no intervening years existed, as if Annie, the baby, the
office and the small obligations of his present life were
accidents easily put aside. Yet how characteristic the command was:
*Don't fail.* Gallaher himself had not failed.

At last the clock struck. Little Chandler fastened the tapes
of the ledger, placed it in the cupboard and locked
the door. He was a small, neat man of thirty-one,
with pale hands, a carefully brushed moustache and shoes which
he polished every second morning. The other clerks sometimes called
him Little Chandler even to his face, not maliciously, but
because the name seemed natural. His person, his voice and
his movements suggested diminution. When he carried a bundle of
documents along the corridor he seemed less an official than
a well-trained boy entrusted with important papers.

He put on his hat before the narrow mirror. The
brim was perhaps a shade too decorous. Gallaher would wear
something broad and foreign-looking, no doubt. But refinement did not
require extravagance. Indeed, many men of real distinction were quiet
in appearance. Little Chandler adjusted his tie, smiled faintly at
his reflection and went downstairs.

The evening had opened after rain. From the entrance of
the office he could see the river lying brown and
cold between the quays, carrying broken light upon its back.
Barges rested against the walls; gulls wheeled and cried above
chimneys; the dome of the Custom House, pale in the
sinking day, seemed to float at a noble distance from
the sordid traffic below. Westward, a yellow brightness gathered behind
the roofs.

He crossed the bridge slowly. The freedom of the air,
after the close office, enlarged his spirit. A tram went
past with a clamour of bells, its upper deck crowded
with faces; a newspaper boy darted between carts, shouting an
evening edition; women with baskets hurried homeward, their skirts lifted
from the wet stones. All these people had their appointed
destinations. His own destination, though only a public room in
Fleet Street, appeared to lie beyond the city, beyond habit,
beyond his life.

London! Gallaher had escaped to it and had been acknowledged
there. The great streets, the lit theatres, the rushing cabs,
the presses roaring through the night—what energy must circulate in
such a place! A man might be unknown in the
morning and discussed in clubs by evening. Talent had room
to stretch itself. In Dublin every promise bent beneath observation:
one's relations, one's schoolfellows, one's neighbours watched and remembered. A
boy's hesitation clung to the man. A single failure became
a family anecdote. The city held its sons by their
coat-tails and smiled when they tried to run.

He looked back at the houses along the quay. In
the mellow light their upper windows glimmered, but their fronts
were stained by rain and smoke, and they leaned together
like necessitous old travellers waiting outside a lodging-house. Was there
not a melancholy beauty in them? He felt it keenly.
Melancholy was his element. Certain temperaments were not intended for
noisy triumphs; they worked in silence, storing impressions, suffering delicately,
until one day a perfect form arose from their sorrow.

He had written verses in his mind. Often, on wet
Sundays, phrases came to him—grey water, extinguished bells, a woman's
withdrawn hand—and he repeated them until they seemed the beginnings
of poems. If he could only secure an hour each
evening, free from interruption, he might compose a little volume.
Nothing pretentious: thirty or forty lyrics, perhaps, united by a
mood. The critics would remark the book's distinction, its restraint,
its Celtic sadness. Some London review might speak of "a
new voice from Ireland." His photograph would be published, not
the ordinary photograph of him in the parlour, but one
taken in shadow, the head turned slightly, the eyes grave.
Beneath it would be printed his full name: Thomas Chandler.

He imagined the title page. A modest title was best—*Autumn
Lamps*, perhaps, or *Songs of the Waning City*. The dedication
presented difficulty. To Annie? It would be proper; yet poetry
belonged to that part of him which Annie did not
understand. To Gallaher, then? No. Gallaher's nature was too coarse.
Perhaps no dedication at all.

Turning south, he entered a street where the shops were
lighting their windows. Gold letters shone upon glass; rows of
bottles burned ruby and emerald in a chemist's display; joints
of meat hung heavily under white lamps. Children had gathered
near a fruit stall, thin children with quick hands and
sharp little faces. At the stallholder's shout they scattered into
doorways like mice, then crept out again when his back
was turned. Their rags, their cunning, the obscene boldness of
one small girl repelled him. Yet the scene possessed a
painful picturesqueness. A true poet would not avert his eyes.
He would discover beauty even here—especially here.

Little Chandler passed on, holding his umbrella close to his
side.

What if Gallaher laughed at the idea of his writing?
He would not mention it directly. He might speak generally
of books, of the neglect of Irish talent, and permit
Gallaher to draw his own conclusions. Journalists knew publishers. A
word from a man established in London could be useful.
Not that he wanted patronage. His work, if it were
worth anything, must stand alone. Still, introductions were customary. Even
the greatest writers had received assistance at the beginning.

The thought quickened his pace.

Garvey's Rooms occupied the first floor above a tobacco shop.
Their coloured windows looked down upon the narrow street with
an air of expensive secrecy. Little Chandler had passed the
place many times but entered only once, years before, in
Gallaher's company. Respectable men went there, barristers and commercial travellers;
yet there was in its polished door and crimson blind
an invitation to licence. He paused outside, suddenly conscious of
his gloves and sober overcoat.

Then he remembered the command. *Don't fail.*

He climbed the stairs.

The room was bright after the evening street. Red shades
hung over the tables; mirrors multiplied the bottles behind the
counter; waiters moved rapidly through a warm atmosphere of smoke,
perfume and spirits. Glasses clicked, chairs grated, laughter rose and
subsided. For a moment Little Chandler stood near the entrance,
alarmed by the abundance of strangers.

A voice called:

"Tommy! By all the saints, here he is!"

Gallaher sat in a recess with one leg thrust beneath
the table and the other extended before him. He had
grown broader. His face, once lean and mobile, was now
full at the cheeks and slightly yellow from late hours.
But the eyes were the same: pale blue, direct, and
hard when the smile left them. His hat lay on
a chair beside a folded evening paper. An orange tie
flamed against his striped shirt.

He rose, seized Little Chandler's hand and shook it vigorously.

"Well, well! Let me look at you. Not a pound
heavier. Not half a whisker older. Dublin preserves its saints,
eh?"

Little Chandler laughed and withdrew his hand.

"You've changed, Ignatius."

"I should hope so. A man who doesn't change in
eight years is either dead or in the civil service."

"I'm not precisely in the civil service."

"No? You look as if you owned it. Sit down.
What's yours?"

Little Chandler hesitated.

"A small whisky, perhaps."

"A small one! Listen to him. Two whiskies, Pat, and
make one of them fit for an invalid. No—bring the
bottle. We're celebrating."

The waiter departed. Gallaher leaned back and inspected his friend
with cheerful insolence.

"So you're married."

"Yes. Nearly two years."

"And a father, I hear."

"A little boy."

"Good Lord. Tommy Chandler with a wife and heir! You
used to blush if a barmaid asked whether you wanted
water."

Little Chandler smiled. He had expected teasing and had prepared
himself to receive it with dignity.

"We cannot remain boys for ever," he said.

"Speak for yourself. I've made a profession of it."

The drinks arrived. Gallaher poured generously, splashed in a little
water and raised his glass.

"To old times—and new sins."

"To your success," Little Chandler said.

"Success?" Gallaher swallowed and wiped his mouth. "I keep moving,
that's all. Stop in our trade and the fellow behind
you climbs over your body. London's full of hungry devils
with notebooks."

"But you have done well. Everyone says so."

"Everyone in Dublin?"

"I have seen your name in the papers."

"That's fame for you: six letters at the bottom of
a column nobody finishes. Still, it pays—sometimes. We had a
proprietor once who thought a man could live on notoriety
and tobacco."

Little Chandler sipped his whisky. The warmth entered his chest
immediately. Gallaher's clothes were too loud, his nails less clean
than they might have been, and his voice carried across
the room; but he possessed ease. He leaned wherever he
wished, summoned waiters without apology, named important persons familiarly. No
room diminished him.

"You've travelled a great deal?" Little Chandler asked.

"Travelled? I've slept badly in every capital worth mentioning. Paris,
Brussels, Vienna, Berlin. Rome twice. Madrid in summer—don't do it.
And Monte Carlo when a certain gentleman from Manchester mistook
company money for his own."

"What is Paris really like?"

Gallaher gave a short laugh.

"Which Paris? There are twenty. The one the tourists see,
with monuments and guides and a photograph of themselves under
a tree? The newspaper Paris? The artists' Paris? Or the
Paris that begins after midnight and doesn't give receipts?"

Little Chandler coloured but persisted.

"I have heard it is very beautiful."

"Oh, beautiful, certainly. In parts. Broad streets, fine women, food
that makes you curse your mother's kitchen. But beauty isn't
the half of it. The place knows how to live.
Nobody asks where your grandfather worshipped or whether your aunt
approves. You may be a prince at dinner and a
scoundrel by breakfast; provided you pay, they forgive the transformation."

"And the women?" Little Chandler asked, lowering his voice.

Gallaher's blue eyes narrowed with amusement.

"Ah! So that's where the poetry leads. The women, Tommy,
are educated. Not in books—though some could teach a bishop
his theology—but in life. A glance, a glove, the turn
of an ankle, and you're ready to sign away an
estate."

He described a singer in Montmartre, a dark young woman
who had worn silver shoes and drunk champagne from a
saucer. He spoke of an actress in Vienna who sent
telegrams at four in the morning, of a Belgian countess
separated from her husband, of a red-haired dancer whose jealous
protector carried a pistol. His stories were rapid and incomplete;
names were withheld, dangers magnified, conclusions indicated by a wink
or a shrug. French phrases appeared among his Dublin idioms
like coloured labels upon foreign luggage.

Little Chandler listened with fascination. Some of the tales seemed
improbable, some positively ugly. It was vulgar to speak of
women in that fashion. A man of true experience would
surely be more discreet. Yet behind Gallaher's boasts stood railway
platforms, boulevards, hotel rooms, midnight suppers—the whole moving brilliance of
a life in which things happened.

"And London?" he asked.

"London is work. London will grind the sentiment out of
you, thank God. You're up at noon, the office at
two, chasing some liar by four, dinner wherever there's credit,
and then perhaps the real business begins. A fire, a
divorce, a body in the river. At three in the
morning the presses shake the building and you feel the
whole dirty city beating under your feet."

"That must be exciting."

"It is when it isn't damnable."

"I sometimes think," Little Chandler said carefully, "that a more
active environment develops powers which—here—might remain unrecognised."

Gallaher stared at him, then slapped the table.

"You mean you want to bolt!"

"I did not say that."

"You didn't need to. Every man in Dublin wants to
bolt. Half haven't the fare and the other half have
mothers."

Little Chandler looked into his glass.

"A man may have ambitions."

"Of course he may. What's yours? Politics? The law? Don't
tell me you're inventing a machine."

"I have always been interested in literature."

Gallaher whistled softly.

"Literature! That's dangerous. Does your wife know?"

"I have written a little—not much. Sketches. Verses."

"Verses, is it? I remember. You used to carry Byron
about as if he were a prayer book."

"I no longer admire him unreservedly."

"Naturally. You're married."

Little Chandler felt the blood rise into his face. Gallaher
grinned, but there was no reverence in the grin, no
sudden perception that the mild clerk before him might possess
a secret gift.

"I should like to publish a small collection some day,"
he said. "If the work seemed worthy."

"Publish it whether it's worthy or not. That's what the
unworthy fellows do, and they're the ones you hear about."

"I could not do that."

"Then you'll never be famous."

"Surely merit counts for something."

"My dear Tommy, merit is a shy girl at a
crowded dance. Unless somebody drags her forward, she sits by
the wall until the lamps go out."

The sentence, though glib, impressed Little Chandler. He thought he
might remember it. Gallaher refilled the glasses.

"You ought to send me something," he continued. "I'll tell
you if there's blood in it."

Little Chandler's pleasure was checked by the phrase.

"I should value an honest opinion."

"You'll get that, anyway. I've no time for polite lies."

"And perhaps," Little Chandler said, "if you thought any piece
suitable, you might indicate a journal—"

"Certainly, certainly. London's buried in journals. Some pay, some pray,
most disappear. Write something sharp. None of your mooning over
ruined abbeys. Give them murder, love, drink, religion—preferably all four."

"My work would be of a quieter character."

"Then give them a quiet murder."

Gallaher laughed and Little Chandler joined him weakly. The whisky
had made the room warmer. The red shades swam within
the mirrors and the hum of conversation formed a luxurious
barrier around them. He wished Gallaher would speak seriously. He
wished to be recognised, not merely encouraged. Did not his
face show sensibility? Did not the very modesty of his
ambition prove its genuineness?

He tried another approach.

"I suppose you meet many literary men."

"Too many. Fellows who haven't bought a drink since their
first sonnet. They're all geniuses until the bill arrives."

"I meant writers of reputation."

"Oh, a few. I've seen Harland at a dinner. Shared
a cab once with a dramatist you'd know, though he
was too drunk to know himself. Editors, critics, novelists—they eat
like other people. Worse, if somebody else pays."

"But their conversation?"

"Conversation! Tommy, you've a beautiful picture of the world. A
famous novelist talks about his digestion. A critic talks about
the novelist's income. A poet talks about the critic. There
you have literature."

The contempt in Gallaher's voice offended him. It was the
contempt of the practical man for finer natures, of the
successful tradesman for art. Journalism had coarsened Gallaher. His famous
London was perhaps no more than public houses, offices and
cheap adventures. To live widely was not necessarily to live
deeply. A sensitive soul might experience more in one quiet
Dublin twilight than Gallaher had gathered from all his capitals.

Little Chandler sat straighter.

"There are experiences of the imagination," he said, "which cannot
be measured by travel."

"True enough. A man can imagine bankruptcy without leaving his
bed."

"I mean that the artist need not be—worldly."

"No. But it helps him sell."

"You reduce everything to money."

"Because everything reduces me to money. Rent does. Tailors do.
Women do with extraordinary speed."

"Some men value domestic happiness."

The words came out more firmly than he intended. Gallaher
looked at him with an expression almost compassionate.

"Ah, yes. Domestic happiness."

"You speak as though it did not exist."

"I speak as one who has avoided testing it."

"You may alter your opinion."

"I may also join a monastery."

"You will marry," Little Chandler said.

"Will I?"

"Most men do."

"Most men die too. I'm in no hurry for either."

Gallaher tilted his chair and regarded the ceiling.

"No, Tommy. Not yet. I've seen too much of the
arrangement. A fellow walks into church in a new coat
and walks out with his head in a sack. Then
come the furniture, the perambulator, the mother-in-law, the butcher's book.
He asks permission to stay out after eleven and calls
it happiness."

Little Chandler's fingers tightened around his glass.

"That is a very cynical view."

"It's an observant view."

"A good wife can be a help to a man."

"So can a good secretary, and she goes home at
six."

"That is unworthy."

Gallaher lowered the chair and studied him.

"Have I touched a nerve? I beg Mrs Chandler's pardon.
I'm sure she's an angel."

"My wife is an excellent woman."

"And handsome, they tell me."

Little Chandler's indignation softened slightly.

"She is considered so."

"Then you're a lucky dog. Bring her to London and
I'll show you both the sights."

"I do not know that Annie would care for London."

"No? Sensible girl. It eats money."

There was a pause. Gallaher lit a cigar and offered
one. Little Chandler declined, though he would have liked to
accept. Cigars left an odour which Annie noticed immediately.

"Come now," Gallaher said through the smoke, "don't look so
solemn. I'm not condemning marriage. I'll marry myself one day."

Little Chandler smiled despite himself.

"I thought you had escaped."

"Escape is temporary. When I'm forty-five, perhaps, and tired of
sleeping in strange beds."

"What kind of woman?"

"The wealthy kind."

"You would marry for money?"

"For money, beauty, position and devotion. Why limit oneself?"

"You will have difficulty finding all four."

"Not I. There are widows in Germany with castles and
weak hearts. American girls with millions and a passion for
titles. I've only to choose my battlefield."

"But you have no title."

Gallaher winked.

"In Europe, an Irishman may call himself almost anything if
his tie is bright enough."

Little Chandler laughed more freely. The second whisky had relaxed
him, and for a little while the old companionship seemed
restored. Gallaher recalled evenings when they had wandered through the
city with no money, inventing futures. He imitated an old
college porter, a pompous curate, a singer they had known
who pronounced Italian through his nose. Little Chandler contributed memories
cautiously, pleased whenever Gallaher shouted approval. Yet the pleasure was
attended by sadness. Those evenings had been beginnings to Gallaher.
To him they had been nearly the whole adventure.

A clock struck nine.

Little Chandler started.

"I must go."

"Go? We've only begun."

"I promised to be home early. Annie is at her
mother's for an hour, and the servant has the evening
out. I am to mind the baby."

Gallaher stared, then burst into laughter.

"Poetry and the cradle! There's a title for your book."

Little Chandler rose, mortified.

"It is only because—"

"Sit down. One last drink."

"I really cannot."

"One. For the road."

The word *road* tempted him. He sat. Gallaher poured.

"You see?" Gallaher said. "Already asking permission of the clock."

"It is a question of keeping one's word."

"Certainly. Respectability is made of little promises. Drink up."

Little Chandler drank more quickly than before. The spirit stung
his throat. He wanted to say something decisive, something which
would establish the difference between prudence and cowardice, fidelity and
imprisonment. Instead he asked:

"When do you return?"

"Tomorrow night. Boat and train. Back at my desk the
following afternoon if the sea doesn't claim me."

"So soon."

"Business, my boy. The world refuses to pause."

They descended together. In the street the night had hardened.
Lamps shone on wet pavement, and people hurried past beneath
umbrellas. Gallaher buttoned his coat.

"Send the verses," he said. "I mean it."

"I shall revise them first."

"Don't revise the life out of them."

"I am grateful."

"And give my respects to the excellent and handsome Mrs
Chandler."

They shook hands. Gallaher called a cab, sprang in and
shouted an address. The horse started. For an instant his
orange tie showed through the dark square of the window;
then the cab turned and vanished among the lamps.

Little Chandler remained on the pavement.

The city appeared smaller now. Its streets closed around him
with familiar turns; its lamps were meagre; its passers-by wore
the dull, secretive faces of people returning to duty. The
warmth of the whisky had become an ache behind his
eyes. Gallaher was already moving onward—to his hotel, to London,
to another affair—while he must go home because a servant
had an evening out.

Yet Gallaher had asked for the poems. That was something.

He walked rapidly at first. In his imagination he composed
the letter which would accompany them. It must be friendly
but not supplicatory. He would explain that the enclosed verses
represented only preliminary work, that he had not sought publication,
that his official duties and domestic responsibilities allowed little leisure.
Gallaher, reading them in a London office, would be surprised.
The fellows around him would ask whose work it was.
"An old friend in Dublin," Gallaher would reply, with a
respect absent from his voice tonight.

Then Little Chandler remembered that only three poems existed on
paper, and none was complete.

The knowledge fell coldly upon him.

By the time he reached his street the rain had
begun again, a fine oblique rain that silvered the railings.
The houses stood in a grey row, each with its
fanlight, its narrow steps, its square of curtained brightness. His
own looked neither poorer nor finer than the rest. In
the upper window a lamp burned behind the blind.

He opened the door quietly. The hall smelled of oilcloth,
extinguished fire and the lavender Annie placed among the linen.
On the peg hung her everyday coat. She had not
yet returned.

The baby was asleep in a small cot beside the
bedroom fire. Little Chandler removed his wet coat and shoes
and went to look at him. The child's face was
flushed, the lids faintly blue, the mouth open upon a
damp breath. One tiny hand lay outside the blanket, curled
as if holding an invisible thread.

For a moment tenderness overcame him. He touched the fingers
with his own. They closed instinctively around the tip of
his little finger.

"My son," he whispered.

The phrase had dignity. A man with a son was
not a failure. He belonged to the future in another
form. Perhaps the child would inherit his sensibility but not
his timidity; perhaps he would be educated broadly, sent abroad,
permitted choices his father had never possessed. Little Chandler pictured
a tall young man departing from Kingstown while he and
Annie stood on the quay, proud and sorrowful.

The baby stirred. Little Chandler withdrew his finger quickly.

In the parlour the lamp had been left burning low.
He raised the wick. The room came into view: the
mahogany-coloured sideboard, the carpet patterned with brown roses, six chairs
in imitation leather, the clock beneath a glass dome, Annie's
workbasket and the framed enlargement of her photograph upon the
mantelpiece. The furniture was new and nearly all obtained by
instalment. Each object seemed to preserve its price. The sideboard
required eleven more payments; the carpet, five; the perambulator, which
occupied the cupboard under the stairs, had not yet been
paid for at all.

Annie's photograph surveyed the room. She had been handsome on
the day it was taken: regular features, abundant fair hair,
steady eyes. The photographer had arranged one hand against her
cheek and softened the background into a pale cloud. Little
Chandler had once admired the picture greatly. Tonight the pose
seemed cold. There was no yielding in the eyes. They
were eyes which noticed a loose cuff, an unpaid bill,
the odour of tobacco.

He sat near the table and opened a book he
had bought before his marriage, a handsome volume of English
verse. Its green cover was faded where the sun had
reached it in their former lodgings. He turned the pages
until he found lines he remembered—lines of solitude, exile and
the narrow chamber of the heart.

How noble they sounded! The words did not complain; they
transformed complaint into music. Suffering, passed through genius, became permanent
and pure. His own suffering was inarticulate because he lacked
time, encouragement, freedom. Was that his fault? A man could
not create while accounts pressed upon him, while meals occurred
at fixed hours, while a child might wake in the
next room.

Still, other men had worked under difficulties.

He frowned at the page.

Gallaher would say that difficulties were excuses. Gallaher, who had
fled before anyone could impose obligations upon him! It was
easy for such a man to preach action. He had
no wife to question where he had been, no furniture
to pay for, no infant whose sleep governed the house.
He spent money upon orange ties and cigars, then laughed
at respectability. Vulgar, noisy, overblown—was this success?

But Gallaher had London.

Little Chandler read the stanza again. The rhythm stirred an
infant hope in him, weak but insistent. He drew a
sheet of notepaper from the writing case and uncapped his
fountain pen.

*The rain on the roofs of the city—*

No.

*Beneath the lamps the exiled—*

Too rhetorical.

He looked toward Annie's photograph. The fixed eyes disturbed him.
He shifted his chair so that his back was partly
turned to it.

From the bedroom came a small cry.

He waited.

Silence followed. He bent over the paper.

*O heart, be still in thy narrow room—*

That was derivative. Worse, it was false. His heart was
not still. It beat rapidly, resentfully. A poet must be
honest.

The baby cried again, a thin questioning sound.

Little Chandler set down his pen and went into the
bedroom. He rocked the cot gently. The child opened its
eyes, saw the unfamiliar face above it and made a
doubtful grimace.

"Hush now," Little Chandler whispered. "Hush. Papa is here."

The word *Papa* sounded theatrical in his mouth. He rocked
the cot more firmly. The baby's eyes closed, then opened.

"Hush."

After several minutes the breathing steadied. He returned to the
parlour, leaving both doors open. He sat with the book,
not the blank paper; composition required equipoise, and his had
been disturbed.

He began to read aloud in a low voice. The
poet addressed a distant beloved from captivity. Little Chandler savoured
the measured sorrow, pausing at the end of each line.
Such music might have been his. The thought brought tears
almost to his eyes—not ordinary tears, but tears of artistic
recognition.

A cry interrupted him.

He continued reading, slightly louder.

The cry became a wail.

He stopped. The wail paused also, as if the child
listened. Then it recommenced with renewed force.

Little Chandler gripped the book. Why should Annie be late?
She had said an hour. It was nearly half past
nine. Her mother's house was not distant. No doubt they
were talking comfortably while he—after meeting a friend returned from
London, after receiving perhaps the only encouragement of his literary
life—was left to perform a nursemaid's duties.

The child wailed on.

He entered the bedroom and lifted it awkwardly from the
cot. The small body was heavier than he expected and
hot through its gown. He held it against his shoulder
as he had seen Annie do.

"Quiet now. Quiet."

The baby's face reddened. Its fists worked near his collar;
its mouth opened enormously; the cry pierced his ear.

Little Chandler walked up and down. He patted the child's
back. He spoke nonsense words, first tenderly, then with embarrassed
urgency. Nothing availed. The cries filled the room, travelled into
the hall, returned from the walls. They were disproportionate to
the little body, accusations without language.

His head throbbed.

"If you please," he muttered. "If you please."

The baby screamed.

A suffocating anger rose in him—not wholly against the child,
but against the room, the clock, the instalments, Gallaher's laughter,
Annie's composed photograph, his own small hands supporting this inexorable
burden. Prisoner for life! The phrase struck through him. A
little house, a little salary, a little reputation; and now
this little tyrant, helpless and absolute, forbidding even the reading
of a page.

He laid the baby in the cot and rocked it
sharply.

"Stop!"

The child screamed louder.

"Stop, do you hear?"

Its face had become crimson; tears shone at the corners
of its eyes.

Little Chandler leaned over it. His restraint broke.

"Stop crying!"

The shout was tremendous in the small room.

The baby ceased at once.

For one dreadful second it lay rigid, staring up at
him. Its eyes widened until the blue irises seemed surrounded
by white. The lower lip trembled. Then fear convulsed the
whole face and there came a shriek so wild, so
wounded, that Little Chandler stepped backward.

The front door opened.

Footsteps ran along the hall. Annie appeared in the doorway,
carrying a paper parcel. She took in the scene—the child
in the cot, her husband standing over it, the book
fallen upon the floor.

"What have you done?"

"Nothing. He began to cry and I—"

She pushed past him and snatched up the baby.

"My darling! My little love! What did he do to
you?"

"I did nothing," Little Chandler repeated. "I only told him
to be quiet."

Annie held the child tightly, swaying from side to side.
Her hat had slipped back and a strand of fair
hair lay across her cheek. The baby buried its face
against her neck and sobbed in broken gasps.

"Did he frighten you? Did he frighten mother's little man?"

"Annie, I assure you—"

She turned her eyes upon him.

There was no question in them. There was anger, alarm
and something more terrible: exclusion. She and the child formed
a single injured creature from which he stood apart. He,
the father, was the stranger in the room.

"What kind of man shouts at a baby?" she said.

"I lost patience for a moment. I had been trying
for half an hour. You said you would return early."

"So it is my fault?"

"I did not say that."

"You did."

"I merely meant that I am not accustomed—"

"No. You are not."

The baby cried again. Annie bent her head and kissed
its damp hair.

"There, there. Mother is here. Nobody will frighten you now."

Little Chandler looked at her. He wished to explain the
whole evening: Gallaher's arrival, the talk of London, the offer
concerning his verses, the humiliations and hopes, the whisky, the
line he had almost written. If she understood how much
had gathered within him, she might judge the shout less
harshly. But even as he imagined the explanation he heard
its feebleness. Great cities, unwritten poems, a friend's boast, a
crying child—what connexion could justify him?

He stooped to pick up the book. One page had
folded beneath the cover. He smoothed it carefully, though his
fingers shook.

Annie continued to murmur to the child. Her voice, tender
for the baby, seemed by that tenderness to condemn him
more completely.

"I am sorry," he said.

She did not answer.

"I said I am sorry."

The child's sobs diminished. Little wet breaths came against her
shoulder. Annie walked toward the lamp and stood beneath it,
examining the baby's face for harm. Light fell over her
hair and the white folds of the gown. Little Chandler
remained near the bedroom door, where the shadow from the
wardrobe covered the wall.

He looked at the green book in his hands. The
lines he had admired were still there, serene and finished.
Their captivity was beautiful because it had been mastered. His
own was merely a room in which he had shouted
at a frightened infant.

Gallaher would be at supper now, perhaps laughing beneath chandeliers,
perhaps telling some new companion that poor Tommy Chandler wrote
verses and minded the baby. Tomorrow he would cross the
sea. The presses would thunder, the streets would open before
him, and Dublin would diminish behind the ship.

Little Chandler saw, with a clearness that admitted no appeal,
that he himself would not go. Not because Annie prevented
him, nor because the office chained him, nor because fortune
had preferred coarser men. He would remain because he had
always remained: waiting for permission, waiting for confidence, waiting for
a life to begin after all risk had been removed.
His melancholy, which he had cherished as the sign of
a poet, might be only fear grown graceful in contemplation.
His sensitivity had not made him gentle. His dreams had
not made him brave.

The recognition passed through him quietly.

It was useless.

Annie sat beside the fire with the baby in her
arms. She did not look toward him. The clock beneath
its glass dome ticked with domestic exactitude. On the table
the blank sheet waited under the lamp, bearing half a
line and a blot where his pen had rested too
long.

He moved farther back, out of the warm circle of
light.

The child gave one last shuddering sigh and slept against
its mother. Annie kissed its forehead. Little Chandler watched them,
ashamed to approach and unable to withdraw. His throat tightened.
He lowered his head over the closed book.

In the shadow, without sound, tears gathered in his eyes
and fell.

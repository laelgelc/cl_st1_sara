### THE COMMANDANT'S SECOND EYE

In the year of grace 1797—when his Catholic Majesty still
possessed California by virtue of certain maps, proclamations, and pious
intentions, though the deer, the grizzly bear, and the greater
part of its human inhabitants remained imperfectly informed of that
circumstance—the Presidio of San Carlos stood upon a low eminence
between the mountains and the sea. It consisted of an
adobe quadrangle, a chapel, divers huts, a flagstaff, two brass
cannon without serviceable powder, and a garrison whose military ardour
had been softened by matrimony, gardening, and the climate.

The Commandant, Don Hermenegildo Salvatierra, was a gentleman of ancient
family, moderate fortune, and fifty-six years. He had lost his
left eye in an encounter with heathen Indians, although an
uncharitable tradition attributed the calamity to the flying cork of
a wine bottle at Monterey. It becomes me not to
arbitrate between military glory and domestic accident. Certain it is
that the eye was gone; that a black patch covered
the vacancy; and that the remaining orb was mild, brown,
and habitually disposed to regard mankind with indulgence.

Don Hermenegildo's administration was paternal. He knew every soldier's children
by name, praised their pothooks when they learned to write,
excused irregularities in drill during the melon season, and heard
petitions beneath a pepper tree whose shade was held, by
local precedent, to possess almost judicial authority. His sword, a
Toledo blade inherited from a warlike uncle, had long been
missing. It was subsequently discovered in the possession of a
corporal's little boy, who employed it for stirring mud pies.
The Commandant rebuked the child, kissed him, and forgot to
reclaim the weapon.

Such was San Carlos before the coming of the schooner.

On the afternoon of October 17, the sea darkened under
a wind from the southwest. The gulls fled inland; the
cattle turned their tails to the weather; and the mission
bells, moved by invisible hands, gave forth irregular admonitions which
Father Tomás declared to be providential, though he afterward secured
them with rawhide. Before sunset the ocean had become a
grey and mountainous wilderness, over which foam ran like torn
lace. Through that confusion a small vessel was perceived labouring
toward the anchorage, vanishing behind each ridge of water and
rising again with an obstinacy more commercial than heroic.

The schooner was the *Prudent Abigail*, of Salem, Massachusetts, Captain
Peleg Scudder, master and part owner. She carried, according to
her papers, agricultural implements, household conveniences, medicinal essences, hardware, and
articles of personal refinement. According to those who afterward inspected
her cargo, these comprehensive terms signified shoe pegs, wooden combs,
hard nutmegs, tin whistles, three warming pans, a gross of
spectacles, and several barrels whose perfume established the presence of
codfish beyond theological dispute.

Captain Scudder came ashore in a boat shortly before dark,
bringing two sailors, a sea chest, and that penetrating odour
which salt water imparts to woollen garments and commerce to
dried fish. He was a long, narrow man, red at
the joints, sandy at the chin, and so active in
his glances that the Commandant's single eye appeared, by contrast,
to enjoy the repose of double vision.

Don Hermenegildo received him in the guardroom with the ceremony
due to a foreign mariner cast upon the hospitality of
Spain.

"My house, señor," said he, "my poor table, my soldiers,
and all that the king has entrusted to me are
at your disposition."

Captain Scudder looked through the open door at the two
cannon, one of which contained a hen's nest.

"Much obliged," he replied. "I won't disturb the king more'n
necessary."

It is alleged that the Commandant did not comprehend this
answer. He nevertheless bowed, ordered a room prepared, and commanded
that supper should be made worthy of the occasion. The
garrison, stirred by the arrival of a foreigner and by
rumours of contraband, assembled with commendable promptitude. Women brought tortillas
and stewed fowl; soldiers contributed wine; Father Tomás produced a
bottle of mission brandy reserved for medicinal extremities; and Captain
Scudder opened his chest.

There followed an exchange of gifts and information which later
historians have perhaps magnified. The Yankee presented brass buttons, a
clasp knife, a bottle of hair restorative, and a small
mechanical bird which uttered one note when squeezed. Don Hermenegildo
bestowed upon him a silver-mounted rosary, two embroidered handkerchiefs, and
a title to an acre of ground which neither man
could locate. Political news crossed the table with the wine.
Bonaparte had conquered Italy, or had been killed in Egypt;
the British fleet had sunk, or captured, or failed to
discover the Spanish fleet; President Washington had become a king,
retired to a monastery, or resumed the occupation of surveying
land. Captain Scudder supplied all versions with equal readiness.

At a later hour, when diplomatic reserve had yielded to
brandy, the Commandant danced a fandango with the corporal's wife,
while Captain Scudder accompanied him upon the tin whistle. It
is further alleged that Father Tomás attempted a sailor's hornpipe
and fell into a basket of onions. As this rests
upon the testimony of a soldier afterward punished by the
reverend father, a grave chronicler may properly withhold assent.

Near midnight the storm diminished. The company dispersed. Captain Scudder
was conducted to his chamber; Don Hermenegildo, still wearing his
black patch at a festive angle, retired to his own;
and the *Prudent Abigail* rode at anchor beneath a clearing
sky.

When the day broke the ship was gone.

There was neither wreckage upon the beach nor sail upon
the horizon. Captain Scudder, his sailors, and his sea chest
had likewise disappeared. The only memorial of Yankee commerce was
a solitary shoe peg found in Father Tomás's pocket, where
its presence was never satisfactorily explained.

The Commandant awoke late. His servant, José, entered with chocolate,
advanced three paces, and dropped the tray.

Don Hermenegildo sat upright.

"What ails thee, animal?"

José crossed himself. His teeth were audible.

The Commandant reached toward his face. The black patch lay
upon the pillow; but in the empty socket there was
now an eye—clear, lustrous, blue-grey, and so perfectly formed that
the red threads upon the white and the faintly clouded
iris might have deceived a surgeon. It was a glass
eye of the newest Boston manufacture.

There was no mistake.

Don Hermenegildo examined himself in a small mirror, first with
amazement, then with pleasure. The effect was distinguished. His features,
hitherto impaired by the patch, regained their symmetry; indeed, the
new eye, being larger, brighter, and more resolute than the
old one, lent his countenance a dignity almost gubernatorial. He
called for Father Tomás.

The priest arrived with José, three soldiers, two women, and
seven children, all of whom had already learned the miracle
in its essential and several improved forms. Father Tomás inspected
the phenomenon, prayed, questioned the Commandant, and inspected it again.
He was a man of learning, having read six books
besides his breviary, and presently declared that Providence could accomplish
whatever it pleased.

This conclusion, although impregnable, left untouched the agency of Captain
Scudder. Don Hermenegildo remembered that the Yankee had displayed spectacles,
false teeth, a cork arm belonging to an uncle, and
other contrivances by which New England repaired the errors of
Nature at a profit. He understood the truth. Yet when
he beheld José kneeling, the women weeping, and the children
gazing upon him as upon a saint newly added to
the calendar, he lacked the courage—or possessed too much official
prudence—to explain that Heaven had employed a trader from Salem
and charged the expense to hospitality.

By noon the miraculous restoration of the Commandant's eye was
known throughout San Carlos. Indians came from the rancherías; candles
were lighted in the chapel; an old woman touched his
sleeve and recovered temporarily from rheumatism. Father Tomás preached upon
the opening of the eyes of the blind, while carefully
avoiding the question whether a man with one sound eye
came within the strict letter of the text. Don Hermenegildo
sat in the front pew, humble, gratified, and exceedingly handsome.

But admiration soon gave place to disquiet.

The glass eye did not move. When the natural eye
softened in pity, sparkled in amusement, or closed in sleep,
its companion maintained the same cold and polished attention. It
looked through darkness and sunlight, feast and funeral, confession and
drill. It seemed less an organ of sight than a
fixed opinion.

José was the first to feel its power. While shaving
the Commandant, he remembered a silver spoon which had passed,
through inadvertence, from the official kitchen into his own. The
eye regarded him. His hand trembled; he cut his master's
chin; and before breakfast the spoon reappeared in the drawer.

The soldiers followed. Men who had slept peacefully at their
posts under the paternal oversight of the brown eye now
started from dreams to encounter the blue one. Ammunition accounts
were corrected. Missing bridles returned. A sergeant confessed that one
of the royal cannon had not been fired on the
king's birthday because a family of mice had occupied the
touch-hole. Even the children abandoned the courtyard when Don Hermenegildo
entered, for the eye appeared to number broken jars, stolen
figs, unlearned prayers, and muddy footprints with supernatural arithmetic.

At first the Commandant was amused.

"My little ones," he would say, "why do you flee
from Papá Hermenegildo?"

They answered that they did not flee from him. They
fled from the other.

This distinction offended him. He began to observe that men
avoided his gaze, lowered their voices at his approach, and
ceased speaking when he turned his head. Since innocence, as
every magistrate knows, has nothing to fear from inspection, their
uneasiness proved their guilt. He therefore inspected them more closely.

Orders multiplied. The morning gun must be fired at sunrise,
although there remained powder for only eleven sunrises. Uniforms must
be buttoned. Accounts must balance. The king's mule must not
be employed to carry private cabbages. José was questioned concerning
the spoon, and, being unable to explain why he had
returned what he denied taking, received three days' confinement. The
corporal's boy was discovered with the Toledo sword and punished
for possession of government property which the government had not
missed in nine years.

Thus suspicion produced concealment, and concealment justified suspicion; a wheel
of policy which has since been employed by administrations more
considerable than San Carlos.

Father Tomás resisted longest. He preached that the Divine Eye
alone perceived the secrets of the heart. Unfortunately, while pronouncing
this doctrine, he glanced at the Commandant's left side and
lost his place. His authority declined. Penitents declared that confession
was unnecessary, since Don Hermenegildo knew everything already; others, with
equal logic, confessed crimes they had never committed, hoping thereby
to conceal those they had. The priest protested. The Commandant
replied that loyalty had no mysteries.

By the spring of 1798 San Carlos had ceased to
laugh.

The change in Don Hermenegildo was not wholly imaginary. Continual
association with the artificial eye seemed to harden the natural
one. His old courtesies became formulas; his affection, supervision; his
memory, an indictment. He no longer praised the children's handwriting,
but counted their errors. He no longer sat beneath the
pepper tree to hear petitions, because petitions implied dissatisfaction, and
dissatisfaction approached sedition. He caused loopholes to be repaired in
the walls, powder to be rationed, and the hen to
be removed from the cannon. These measures were praised in
an official report as evidence of stringent discipline.

Nature herself, which commonly preserves a dignified neutrality in political
affairs, appeared to imitate the Commandant. The winter rains failed.
The hills lost their green; the earth cracked; dust lay
upon the cactus like ashes upon a penitent's head. The
sky, cloudless and unwinking, had the dry, hard glare of
the glass eye. Cattle crowded the shrinking pools. Maize withered.
The mission vines produced grapes so small and sour that
Father Tomás declared the vintage suitable only for ecclesiastical use.

Among the Indians uneasiness spread. Their elders said the one-eyed
chief had stolen a second sight from the sea and
with it the rain. A medicine man displayed a blue
pebble, which he promised would blind the evil eye if
accompanied by six blankets and a horse. Young men vanished
from the mission fields. Arrows were found near the cattle
pens. At night drums sounded in the hills, and the
soldiers, hearing in each beat an accusation, clutched unloaded muskets.

Don Hermenegildo answered unrest with decrees. No Indian might leave
without permission; no soldier might speak after the evening bell;
no woman might gather at the well; no child might
whistle near the powder magazine. As the magazine was empty,
this last regulation was observed with particular severity.

Only Paquita still came freely to his room.

She was the orphan daughter of an officer, twelve years
old, grave in the manner of children who have been
much among ceremonious adults, and accustomed to receive lessons from
the Commandant in reading, geography, and the proper reverence due
to Spain. Formerly he had drawn maps for her upon
the table, placing San Carlos at the centre and disposing
the rest of the world around it according to convenience.
Now he corrected her letters in red ink and required
her to recite lists of regulations.

On the last evening of 1798 she stood before him
while the sun descended, crimson and enormous, through the smoke
of distant fires. Don Hermenegildo asked why she had failed
to visit him for three days.

Paquita twisted her apron.

"I was afraid."

"Of me?"

"Not of you."

"Of whom, then?"

"Of the eye."

He frowned, and the glass eye made the frown terrible.

"It is my eye."

"No, señor."

"Do you presume to tell your Commandant what belongs to
his face?"

She began to cry, but did not withdraw.

"Your hand is the same," she said. "And your voice
is sometimes the old voice, when you say 'Paquita, niña
mía.' But the eye is not yours. It does not
sleep. It does not forgive. When you look with it,
your other eye forgets me."

The Commandant raised his hand to rebuke her. She caught
it and pressed it against her cheek.

"This is you," she whispered. "That is not."

For a moment the room, the fort, the whole elaborate
fabric of suspicion seemed to recede from him. He felt
the child's tears upon his fingers. His natural eye grew
dim; but the other, inaccessible to moisture, shone with undiminished
rectitude.

He sent her away gently.

That night the drums ceased. A wind moved faintly among
the dry grasses. Near midnight three figures crossed the courtyard:
a discontented sergeant, an Indian neophyte, and José, whose participation
may surprise the reader until he reflects upon the moral
consequences of silver spoons. They entered the Commandant's chamber by
a door which military prudence had caused to be locked,
but which Spanish carpentry had rendered open.

Don Hermenegildo slept upon his back. His brown eye was
closed.

The blue eye watched them.

The sergeant, carrying the recovered Toledo sword, advanced to the
bed. The glass eye met him with serene and awful
knowledge. It knew the stolen powder sold at Monterey; it
knew the cards concealed beneath the barrack floor; it knew
the widow at Santa Cruz and the two incompatible promises
made in her courtyard. The sergeant halted.

"Strike," whispered José.

The sergeant lifted the sword. The eye neither pleaded nor
closed.

His courage broke. He stumbled backward against a chair.

Don Hermenegildo awoke.

The ensuing action was brief and lacking in the order
desirable in military narratives. The Commandant seized the sergeant's wrist;
José grasped the Commandant; the neophyte grasped José, apparently in
doubt concerning his allegiance; and all four revolved about the
chamber until the sword cut down a bed curtain. Paquita,
awakened in the adjoining room, screamed. Soldiers rushed into the
courtyard. Father Tomás rang the chapel bell with such vigour
that it came loose and descended among the worshippers.

In the struggle José's elbow struck Don Hermenegildo's face.

Something small and bright flew across the room, touched the
wall, and rolled beneath a chest.

The Commandant stopped.

His left socket was empty.

The mutineers, perceiving the miracle abolished, surrendered with that obedience
for which the garrison had formerly been celebrated. The glass
eye was sought by candlelight, but could not be found.
Some declared that the earth had swallowed it; others that
the Devil reclaimed his own; Father Tomás preferred to regard
its disappearance as providential. In fact, a mouse carried it
into the wall, where it remained until 1823, when it
was discovered by an American carpenter and mistaken for a
marble.

With the eye gone, Don Hermenegildo's severity departed—not in an
instant, as vulgar legend insists, but rapidly enough for historical
purposes. At dawn he released José, pardoned the neophyte, and
sentenced the sergeant to repair the door through which he
had entered. He restored the black patch, called Paquita "niña
mía," and sat once more beneath the pepper tree.

Before noon clouds gathered over the mountains.

The rain began softly, stippling the dust, darkening the tiles,
and awakening from the earth that fragrance which is the
land's first response to mercy. Then it fell in silver
cords. Children danced bareheaded in the courtyard; soldiers held out
their hats; women set jars beneath the eaves; Father Tomás
ordered a thanksgiving and discreetly rolled a cask under a
broken gutter. The hills grew green, the streams returned, and
the cattle lifted their horns toward a sky which at
last had learned to weep.

San Carlos recovered its harmony. The morning gun was discontinued
for want of powder. The hen resumed possession of the
cannon. Confessions became moderate and credible. Indians returned to the
fields, soldiers to their naps, children to their figs, and
the king's mule to the private transportation of cabbages. Don
Hermenegildo governed many years thereafter with one eye, which proved
sufficient when assisted by kindness and occasionally closed.

As for Captain Peleg Scudder, records place the *Prudent Abigail*
at Valparaíso, Manila, Honolulu, and ports which no respectable schooner
could have visited within the time alleged. He prospered. Among
the articles advertised in his later invoices were patent spectacles,
incorruptible nutmegs, mechanical birds, glass eyes, wooden legs, and other
Boston notions designed to remedy Providence at a reasonable advance.

Whether he ever learned the consequences of his midnight generosity
cannot now be determined. Yankee traders, though proverbially indisposed to
deceive simple people, have seldom burdened commerce with unnecessary explanations.

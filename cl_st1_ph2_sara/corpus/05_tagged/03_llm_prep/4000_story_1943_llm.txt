The stranger had walked farther than he intended, and, finding
that the road to Claybury possessed more turnings than milestones,
stopped at last before the Cauliflower and regarded its faded
sign with the gratitude due to a landmark which also
dispenses refreshment. The afternoon was warm, the village street empty,
and the low-ceilinged tap-room, with its sanded floor and pewter
shining dimly behind the bar, offered a cool and almost
monastic repose.

An old man sat by the open window, smoking a
long clay pipe with an air of having exhausted every
occupation but that one. He raised his eyes when the
stranger entered, manifested no curiosity, and after a civil greeting
assented drowsily to the proposition that it was hot.

"Quiet place," said the traveller, when his thirst had been
attended to.

The old man took his pipe from his mouth and
glanced along the deserted road.

"It is now," he said.

The stranger waited. There was in the old man's tone
the pathetic insistence of one whose parish had once stood
at the centre of affairs and had since been unjustly
neglected by history.

"Things happen here sometimes, I suppose?" he prompted.

The old man smiled faintly.

"They used to," he said. "There was a conjurer 'ere
once. Proper conjurer 'e called hisself, though Bob Pretty said
arterwards that 'e was no more a conjurer than 'e
was a bishop."

"Who was Bob Pretty?"

The old man replaced his pipe and looked at the
stranger over it.

"You don't know Bob Pretty?"

The traveller admitted the defect.

"Well, that saves argument," said the old man. "There's people
in this village as'll tell you Bob was a rogue,
and there's Bob hisself, when 'e was alive, as would
tell you 'e wasn't. Nobody ever proved anything agin 'im—not
properly. Things used to disappear when Bob was about, but
that might 'appen to anybody. Farmer Gill's ducks followed 'im
'ome one evening, but Bob said they mistook 'im for
the farmer, both being stout men with whiskers. And when
Squire Widden's best copper boiler was found in Bob's shed,
Bob said 'e was keeping it safe till the owner
advertised for it. That's the sort o' man 'e was:
always ready with an explanation, and very hurt if you
didn't take it."

He paused, relit his pipe with care, and settled himself
more comfortably.

"This conjurer," he continued, "came 'ere one Tuesday in November,
when the roads was that deep in mud that nobody
with sense would travel 'em. 'E was a little man
with a black moustache curled up at both ends, a
shiny hat, and a long overcoat with fur on the
collar. Brought two boxes, a carpet-bag, and a pistol-case, and
wanted the big room upstairs for a performance.

"Smith, the landlord, told 'im there wasn't enough people in
Claybury to make a performance pay, unless 'e performed for
nothing and stood the beer. The conjurer said art must
be paid for. Smith said so must beer. They talked
it over, and at last the conjurer agreed to do
a private exhibition in the tap-room for threepence a head,
Smith to 'ave the room for nothing and two tickets
for hisself and Mrs. Smith.

"By seven o'clock there was eleven of us in 'ere,
not counting Smith and his wife. There was me, Peter
Gubbins, Sam Jones, Dicky Weed, John Biggs, George Kettle, old
Isaac Wren, two labourers from Henshaw's farm, Bob Pretty, and
a young fellow from Wickham as had come over to
court Smith's niece and stayed because 'e thought threepence was
cheaper than courting.

"The conjurer 'ad put a green cloth over that table
there and stood two candles on it, one each side.
'E wore evening clothes, though it was only Tuesday, and
a white shirt-front that stuck out so far Peter said
you could serve drinks off it. 'E bowed to us,
and nobody bowed back except old Isaac, who was asleep
and nodding.

"'Gentlemen,' ses the conjurer, 'I have the honour this evening
to demonstrate certain mysteries of Oriental science, combined with the
latest discoveries in natural philosophy.'

"'Are you going to swallow fire?' ses Sam Jones.

"'Possibly,' ses the conjurer.

"'Because if you are,' ses Smith, 'mind the ceiling.'

"The conjurer looked at 'im very severe, and then 'e
took a pack o' cards and asked Dicky Weed to
choose one. Dicky took the ace o' clubs, and the
conjurer told 'im not to show it to anybody.

"'I can't tell whether you know it if I don't,'
ses Dicky.

"'You know it yourself,' ses the conjurer.

"'Course I do,' ses Dicky. 'I just picked it.'

"'Then kindly replace it,' ses the conjurer, beginning to get
short.

"Dicky put it back, and the conjurer shuffled the cards,
tapped 'em three times, and drew out the ace o'
clubs. We clapped a bit, though Peter Gubbins said any
man who had watched where it went could ha' done
the same.

"The conjurer then tore the card into four pieces, burnt
'em in the candle, and found the card whole in
Sam Jones's cap.

"Sam took it out and turned the cap upside down.

"'Wot else you put in there?' he ses.

"'Nothing, sir,' ses the conjurer.

"'There was a sixpence in that cap this morning,' ses
Sam.

"'I know nothing of that,' ses the conjurer.

"'That's wot they all say,' ses Bob Pretty, very quiet.

"The conjurer asked for silence. Then 'e borrowed Peter's red
cotton handkerchief, stuffed it into his fist, and pulled out
a blue silk one about six feet long. Peter said
'e wanted his own back, not that, because red cotton
wore better. The conjurer pulled another bit, and then another,
and presently 'e had near twenty foot o' coloured stuff
on the floor.

"'Where's mine?' ses Peter.

"'In good time,' ses the conjurer.

"'I've got a cold now,' ses Peter, 'not in good
time.'

"The conjurer waved his wand over the silk and produced
a white rabbit. That was the first animal.

"Mrs. Smith screamed, and Smith asked 'im whether it was
house-trained. The rabbit jumped off the table, went under the
settle, and Peter crawled after it because 'e thought his
handkerchief might be wrapped round it. While 'e was down
there the conjurer borrowed George Kettle's hat and took two
pigeons out of it.

"George was a very particular man about his clothes, and
when one o' the pigeons flapped its wings and left
a mark on the lining 'e got up and said
there was going to be trouble. The conjurer said it
was an illusion.

"'You can call it wot you like,' ses George, pointing
to the mark, 'but you'll clean it.'

"The conjurer promised, and then 'e asked John Biggs for
the loan of his pipe. John gave it to 'im,
after emptying the tobacco into his waistcoat pocket, and the
conjurer wrapped it in paper, put it on the table,
and hit it with a flat-iron.

"'Mind wot you're doing,' ses John.

"'Have no fear,' ses the conjurer. 'Matter is indestructible.'

"'That pipe cost ninepence,' ses John.

"'Observe,' ses the conjurer.

"'E opened the paper and there was nothing in it
but little bits. John stood up.

"'You've broke it,' he ses.

"'Patience,' ses the conjurer.

"'You've broke my pipe,' ses John, louder.

"The conjurer reached behind old Isaac Wren's ear and brought
the pipe out whole. Everybody laughed except John, who examined
it very careful and said it wasn't his.

"'Certainly it is,' ses the conjurer.

"'Mine's got a bit chipped off the mouthpiece,' ses John.

"The conjurer looked at it, and then at the pieces
on the table.

"'Ah,' he ses, 'the conditions in this room are unfavourable.'

"John said the conditions would be worse if 'e didn't
get ninepence, and arter some talk the conjurer paid 'im
sixpence and gave 'im the whole pipe as well. John
went home with it next day and found his own
on the mantelpiece, where his wife had put it. But
that was arterwards.

"By that time the conjurer was getting annoyed. 'E said
'e would now perform a feat no other living man
could equal, and asked Smith to fill a mug with
water. Smith filled one from the tap, but the conjurer
told 'im to drink first, to prove there was no
deception.

"'There's always deception in water,' ses Smith. 'Beer's safer.'

"So 'e filled a mug with beer instead and drank
half of it to show it was genuine. The conjurer
took the mug, covered it with a plate, said words
in a foreign language, and turned it upside down. When
'e lifted the plate there was a little green frog
sitting in the beer.

"Smith made more fuss over that than George had over
his hat.

"'That's a fresh mug,' he ses. 'I washed it myself
last week.'

"The frog jumped onto the table, and Mrs. Smith screamed
again. Sam Jones said it looked like one from Tinker's
Pond, and wanted to know whether the conjurer had paid
for the fishing. The conjurer said the frog was a
materialisation and would shortly dissolve.

"It didn't. It jumped into Peter Gubbins's lap, and Peter,
who'd just come out from under the settle with the
rabbit in one hand and a dead mouse in the
other, threw both away and upset his chair. The rabbit
went round the room something shocking, with Mrs. Smith trying
to keep it out o' the kitchen and George trying
to keep the pigeons off his hat.

"The conjurer stood there tapping the table with his wand
and calling for order. Bob Pretty was the only quiet
one among us. 'E sat in the corner with his
arms folded and a little smile on his face.

"'Perhaps,' ses Bob, when things had settled a bit, 'the
gentleman would like to take something out o' me.'

"The conjurer looked at 'im.

"'With pleasure, sir,' he ses. 'Kindly stand up.'

"Bob stood, and the conjurer asked permission to examine his
coat. Bob held his arms out. The conjurer felt along
one sleeve, and out dropped three eggs. Then 'e felt
the other sleeve and pulled out a string o' sausages.

"We stared at Bob, and Bob stared at the sausages.

"'Never saw 'em before in my life,' he ses.

"'Of course not,' ses the conjurer, smiling.

"Then 'e put his hand into Bob's inside pocket and
brought out a small black kitten.

"Bob's smile went.

"'Put it back,' he ses.

"'Back?' ses the conjurer.

"'Where you got it from,' ses Bob. 'I don't want
no strange cats.'

"The kitten scratched the conjurer and got loose. Mrs. Smith
screamed for the third time, and Smith opened the door,
but instead of going out it ran behind the bar
and upset a tray. The rabbit followed it, and one
of the pigeons flew upstairs. There was unpleasantness.

"When things quieted down, the conjurer asked Bob whether 'e
was satisfied.

"'Not yet,' ses Bob. 'You ain't found the flat-iron.'

"The conjurer flushed up and said 'e had been providing
innocent amusement. Bob said there was nothing innocent about putting
sausages in a man's sleeve, especially with meat the price
it was, and George Kettle wanted to know why Bob
had been favoured with sausages while 'e had only got
pigeons.

"Dicky Weed, who was very proud of his gold watch,
took it out to see the time. That watch had
belonged to his uncle, who left it to 'im because
none of the other relations would sit with 'im in
his last illness. Dicky was always talking about it. He
said it was worth twenty pounds, and wore it in
a wash-leather bag for fear the air should spoil it.

"The conjurer saw it.

"'Ah!' he ses. 'A watch. With the owner's permission, I
will now perform my celebrated experiment in the instantaneous transmission
and reconstruction of metallic objects.'

"Dicky put it away.

"'Not with mine,' he ses.

"'There is no danger,' ses the conjurer.

"'Then use your own,' ses Dicky.

"The conjurer said professional men never used their own property,
because it might lead to suspicion, and Bob Pretty said
that sounded reasonable.

"'I guarantee the watch,' ses the conjurer, putting his hand
on his shirt-front.

"'For twenty pounds?' ses Dicky.

"'For its full value.'

"'In writing?' ses Dicky.

"The conjurer said his word was enough.

"'Not in this village,' ses Bob.

"Arter a lot o' talk the conjurer offered to put
eighteenpence on the table as evidence of good faith. Dicky
said eighteenpence wasn't twenty pounds, but Bob pointed out that
if the trick succeeded he'd be eighteenpence better off. That
took Dicky's fancy. The conjurer laid down the money, and
Dicky handed over the watch, but kept hold of the
chain till the conjurer promised that no animal would come
out of it.

"The conjurer put the watch in a handkerchief and let
Dicky feel it through the cloth.

"'Is that your watch, sir?'

"'It feels like it,' ses Dicky.

"'You admit it is intact?'

"'It was when I gave it to you.'

"The conjurer laid the bundle on the table and brought
out the flat-iron again.

"'Now,' he ses, 'by one decisive blow—'

"''Old 'ard,' ses Dicky.

"But the iron came down. There was a crunch that
made everybody quiet.

"Dicky sprang forward, but the conjurer held up his hand.

"'Do not interfere with forces you do not understand,' he
ses.

"'I understand that noise,' ses Dicky.

"The conjurer opened the handkerchief. There was the watch in
little bits—the case bent, the glass powdered, the hands loose,
and the works looking as if somebody had trod on
a beetle.

"Dicky gave a sort o' moan and picked up the
pieces.

"'Twenty pounds,' he ses. 'Twenty pounds, and it kept time
to a minute.'

"'It will again,' ses the conjurer.

"'You'll keep time to a minute,' ses Dicky, 'when the
police get you.'

"The conjurer opened the pistol-case and took out a long
pistol with a brass-bound stock. He said the experiment had
only begun, and asked us to observe closely. The fragments
of the watch, 'e explained, would be placed in the
pistol, and when the weapon was discharged at any gentleman
the separated particles would travel invisibly, rearrange their molecular constitution,
and appear as a perfect watch in that gentleman's pocket.

"'Why not put 'em in his pocket without firing?' ses
Peter Gubbins.

"'Electric sympathy requires concussion,' ses the conjurer.

"Peter said he thought it might.

"The conjurer swept all the bits into the barrel, ramming
'em down with a little rod. Dicky watched every piece
go in, and when the conjurer dropped the winding-key on
the floor 'e made 'im pick it up.

"'Now,' ses the conjurer, looking round, 'I require a volunteer.'

"Nobody moved.

"'The pistol contains no ball,' he ses.

"Nobody moved again.

"'The discharge is perfectly harmless.'

"'You try it,' ses John Biggs.

"'The operator cannot receive the object,' ses the conjurer. 'It
is contrary to the law of magnetic polarity.'

"'Shoot Smith,' ses Sam Jones. 'It's his room.'

"Smith said 'e had a wife and business responsibilities.

"Then Bob Pretty got up.

"'I don't believe in it,' he ses, 'but I'm willing
to give the gentleman a chance. I've been put upon
in this room before now, and I don't want it
said I stood in the way o' science.'

"The conjurer thanked 'im and placed him by the door.
Bob buttoned his coat and asked which pocket the watch
would come in.

"'The right-hand pocket,' ses the conjurer.

"'There's two onions in there,' ses Bob.

"'Then the left.'

"Bob felt his left pocket and nodded.

"The conjurer told us all to stand back. 'E raised
the pistol, took aim at Bob's waistcoat, and fired.

"The bang was tremendous. One candle went out, Mrs. Smith
screamed for the fourth time, and Bob Pretty gave a
yell and clapped both hands to his side.

"'You've done it!' he ses. 'You've shot me!'

"'E staggered agin the door, opened it, and ran out
into the street bent almost double.

"For half a minute nobody spoke. Then Dicky Weed ran
to the door and shouted after 'im.

"'Look in your pocket, Bob!'

"There was no answer.

"'Fetch 'im back,' ses the conjurer, looking uncommon pale.

"Sam and Peter ran out, but Bob had disappeared. They
went as far as the pond, and then to Bob's
cottage, but his wife said 'e hadn't come home and
that, if we'd shot 'im, it was no more than
some people had been trying to do for years.

"Dicky came back and stood in front of the conjurer.

"'Where's my watch?' he ses.

"'In the volunteer's pocket,' ses the conjurer.

"'Then fetch it.'

"'I cannot be responsible for a volunteer who absents himself
at the critical moment.'

"'You said it'd be restored.'

"'And it has been.'

"'Prove it.'

"'Bring back the man.'

"Dicky said 'e would, and six of us went out
searching. We looked in Bob's shed, behind the churchyard wall,
under the bridge, and in the little wood toward Pottle's
End. We didn't find 'im. His wife kept saying he
wasn't home, though there was a light upstairs and somebody
sneezed when Sam Jones stood under the window.

"When we got back, the conjurer had packed his boxes.
Dicky told 'im 'e wasn't going anywhere till Bob and
the watch turned up.

"The conjurer said 'e had an engagement at Wickham.

"'Not now you ain't,' ses Dicky.

"Smith locked the front door and put the key in
his pocket. The conjurer sat down and talked about the
dignity of his profession, and Dicky sat opposite talking about
twenty pounds. They kept it up till near midnight. At
last the conjurer offered Dicky two pounds on account, but
Dicky said twenty. Then 'e offered five, and Dicky said
twenty again.

"In the end the conjurer had to sleep upstairs, with
Peter Gubbins outside the door and Dicky's boot tied to
the handle by a bit o' string. Next morning 'e
got away by the window, taking his boxes but leaving
one pigeon and Peter's handkerchief behind.

"Bob Pretty wasn't seen for three days.

"When 'e did come out 'e walked with two sticks
and a bandage round his body. He moved very slow
and breathed through his teeth if anybody spoke to 'im.

"Dicky went straight up to 'im.

"'My watch,' he ses.

"Bob leaned on both sticks and shut his eyes.

"'Don't excite me,' he whispers. 'Doctor's orders.'

"'Wot doctor?'

"'Private doctor,' ses Bob.

"'You ain't got a doctor.'

"'That's why 'e's private,' ses Bob.

"Dicky asked whether the watch had appeared in his pocket.
Bob said 'e didn't know; 'e had been in too
much pain to examine his clothes, and his wife had
burnt the coat because it was soaked in blood.

"'Burnt it?' ses Dicky, turning white.

"'For sanitary reasons,' ses Bob.

"'And my watch?'

"Bob groaned and said worldly possessions meant little to a
man who had stood at death's door. Then 'e went
into the Cauliflower and had a pint with a little
gin in it to keep his strength up.

"For the next fortnight Dicky followed 'im about. Bob still
walked on sticks when Dicky was looking, but Sam Jones
saw 'im one evening run across a field after a
rabbit. Bob said it was the sudden return of muscular
power caused by excitement. Another time Peter found the bandage
hanging on Bob's washing-line without a mark on it. Bob
said his wife had washed the blood out.

"People took sides. Some said Bob had the watch, and
some said the conjurer had never put the right bits
in the pistol. Old Isaac Wren said the watch had
most likely been carried off by spirits, but nobody took
much notice of him because 'e owed Dicky six shillings.

"Matters might ha' gone on like that for ever if
the conjurer hadn't come back.

"'E arrived one Saturday afternoon in a pony-trap with another
man, and the first thing 'e did was to go
to the constable. The other man was a doctor from
Wickham, and the conjurer had brought 'im to examine Bob's
wound and clear up the scandal.

"Bob was sitting in this very room, with one stick
between his knees and the bandage showing above his collar.
When 'e saw the conjurer 'e turned pale, but only
for a moment.

"'Murderer,' he ses, very dignified.

"'Impostor,' ses the conjurer.

"'Don't quarrel in my house,' ses Smith. 'Not without ordering.'

"They both ordered, and then the doctor asked Bob to
remove his coat.

"Bob said 'e couldn't, owing to adhesions.

"The doctor said 'e would be careful.

"Bob said the sight of wounds affected him.

"'It won't affect me,' ses the doctor.

"Bob looked round at us. 'Is an injured man to
be insulted in his own village?' he ses.

"'Not if 'e shows us the injury,' ses Dicky.

"Bob called 'im a heartless miser, and Dicky said twenty
pounds. Then Sam and Peter took Bob by the arms,
and John Biggs unbuttoned his waistcoat. Bob struggled something shocking
for a man at death's door, but at last they
got his coat and shirt off.

"There wasn't a mark on 'im.

"The bandage was wrapped round and round with a folded
red handkerchief underneath, to make a swelling. The doctor tapped
Bob's ribs, listened to his chest, and said he was
as sound as any man in the room.

"'Miracle,' ses Bob. 'That pistol-shot must ha' cured my rheumatics.'

"Dicky went through Bob's pockets, but there was no watch.
Then 'e said they ought to search his house, and
Bob said they could if they had a warrant and
didn't mind his wife.

"The conjurer was triumphant. 'E stood on the hearthrug with
his thumbs in his waistcoat and said his professional honour
was completely restored.

"'Where's my watch?' ses Dicky.

"'That man has it,' ses the conjurer, pointing at Bob.

"'Prove it,' ses Bob.

"'You were the recipient.'

"'I received a pistol-shot,' ses Bob. 'Nothing else.'

"'The watch materialised in your pocket.'

"'Which pocket?'

"The conjurer hesitated.

"'The left,' he ses.

"'You said right,' ses Bob.

"'You had onions in the right.'

"'So I did,' ses Bob. 'And very good onions they
was. No watch, though.'

"The conjurer began explaining about magnetic currents and interference caused
by vegetable substances. Nobody liked that. Dicky said if onions
could spoil a twenty-pound trick 'e ought to have been
told before the firing.

"John Biggs, who'd been listening very close, took out his
own watch. It was a thick silver one, worth perhaps
thirty shillings, and it lost ten minutes a day, but
John was fond of it because 'e had won it
in a raffle.

"'Do the trick again,' he ses.

"The conjurer shook his head.

"'Professional experiments cannot be repeated under hostile conditions.'

"'There's no onions,' ses Sam.

"'And you can shoot at me,' ses Peter Gubbins.

"Peter had been drinking since dinner and was feeling brave.

"The conjurer said it was impossible. Bob Pretty, who had
put his shirt on again, leaned back in his chair
and smiled.

"'Course it is,' he ses. 'Because 'e can't do it.'

"'I can perform it under suitable conditions,' ses the conjurer.

"'These are suitable,' ses Bob. 'Same room, same pistol, same
people, and a watch. Only difference is, this one ain't
gold.'

"John held it up.

"'Will silver work?' he ses.

"'The principle applies to all metals,' ses the conjurer, forgetting
hisself.

"'There you are,' ses Bob.

"The conjurer said 'e had retired that experiment from his
programme. Bob said that was wise. Dicky said 'e wanted
his twenty pounds. Everybody began talking at once, and the
conjurer, to prove 'e wasn't afraid, took John's watch and
laid it on the green cloth.

"'I will explain the process,' he ses, 'but I shall
not perform it. The first stage consists in wrapping the
object in a handkerchief—so.'

"'E wrapped it up.

"'The second stage,' he ses, 'would consist of striking it
with the iron, but naturally—'

"Before 'e could finish, Bob Pretty snatched up the flat-iron
and brought it down as 'ard as he could.

"There was the same crunch as before.

"John Biggs gave a roar and caught Bob by the
throat. They rolled onto the floor together, and by the
time we separated 'em John's collar was torn off and
Bob had lost half a whisker.

"'You've broke my watch!' ses John.

"'I was helping,' ses Bob.

"'You smashed it!'

"'According to the gentleman,' ses Bob, rubbing his neck, 'that's
the fust part o' mending it.'

"The conjurer unwrapped the handkerchief and looked at the pieces.

"'I disclaim all responsibility,' he ses quickly. 'The blow was
administered by an untrained person.'

"'A blow's a blow,' ses Bob. 'You hit Dicky's just
the same.'

"'There are degrees of impact,' ses the conjurer. 'There is
also rhythm, direction, and psychic preparation.'

"'You never said nothing about that before,' ses Dicky.

"John Biggs picked up the biggest piece of his watch
and stared at it.

"'Mend it,' he ses.

"'Impossible.'

"'You said matter was indestructible.'

"'Indestructible is not the same as repairable.'

"'It was last time,' ses Sam Jones.

"The conjurer put on his overcoat and said 'e had
been insulted enough. Smith stood with his back to the
door.

"'Nobody's stopping you,' he ses, 'soon as John's watch is
right.'

"'I did not break it,' ses the conjurer.

"'You supplied the iron,' ses Bob.

"'And the handkerchief,' ses Peter.

"'And the explanation,' ses Dicky.

"The conjurer appealed to the doctor, but the doctor said
it was no concern of his and went home. The
constable had left already, saying 'e couldn't interfere with scientific
business unless somebody was dead.

"Bob picked up the pistol-case.

"'Only one thing to do,' he ses. 'Put the bits
in and fire 'em at somebody.'

"'Not me,' ses Peter, his courage going.

"'No,' ses Bob. 'Last time the gentleman fired at me
and the watch was meant to come to me. This
time we'll fire at the gentleman, and it'll come to
him. Then he can hand it back to John.'

"The conjurer laughed, but it was a very poor laugh.

"'Absurd,' he ses.

"'Why?' ses Dicky.

"'Because the operator must control the discharge.'

"'You can tell us how,' ses Sam.

"'There are dangers.'

"'You said there wasn't,' ses Dicky.

"'To the uninitiated.'

"'We're initiated now,' ses Bob. 'We've seen it twice.'

"The conjurer tried to get to the door, but John
Biggs caught hold of his coat-tail. Then the others gathered
round, and before long 'e was sitting in that armchair
by the fireplace with Sam on one side and Peter
on the other.

"'E talked wonderful fast. 'E said a silver watch required
a different atmospheric pressure, and that the moon was in
the wrong quarter, and that the first experiment had disturbed
the electrical balance of the parish for thirty days. Bob
listened very respectful and then asked 'im whether Tuesday's moon
knew Dicky's watch was gold.

"The conjurer said Bob was an ignorant man.

"'That's why I want instruction,' ses Bob. 'Load the pistol,
John.'

"John pushed the broken bits into the barrel. There was
a lot more of 'em than with Dicky's watch, that
being a thick one, and 'e rammed 'em down tight
with the rod.

"The conjurer stopped talking when 'e saw that.

"'You must not fire that weapon,' he ses.

"'Why not?' ses John.

"'It may burst.'

"'It didn't before.'

"'The internal conditions are different.'

"'Silver,' ses Bob. 'That's wot does it.'

"The conjurer said he would pay for the watch. John
asked how much.

"'Ten shillings,' ses the conjurer.

"John said two pounds.

"'It is not worth two pounds.'

"'It is to me.'

"'Fifteen shillings.'

"John shook his head and raised the pistol.

"'Eighteen,' ses the conjurer.

"'Two pounds or we try the trick.'

"The conjurer searched his pockets and produced eleven shillings, a
foreign coin, two tram tickets, and a button. John said
it wasn't enough.

"'Then give me time,' ses the conjurer. 'I will send
the balance.'

"Everybody laughed at that, even old Isaac.

"Bob arranged the chair so it faced the far wall.
'E told the conjurer to sit still and keep the
proper pocket open.

"'Which pocket?' ses Sam.

"The conjurer didn't answer.

"'Left,' ses Bob. 'On account o' there being no onions.'

"The conjurer tried to rise, but they pushed 'im down
again. John stood about twelve feet away with the pistol.
Smith moved the lamp and told 'im to mind the
mirror.

"'This is murder,' ses the conjurer.

"'Nonsense,' ses Dicky. 'There's no ball in it.'

"'The fragments themselves become projectiles!'

"'You said they travelled invisibly,' ses Peter.

"'After molecular dissolution.'

"'Well, dissolve 'em,' ses John.

"The conjurer's face was wet, though the room was cold.
'E offered John a pound, then twenty-five shillings, then said
'e would fetch the money from Wickham if Bob went
with him. Bob said 'e never travelled with strangers.

"John cocked the pistol.

"'Wait!' ses the conjurer.

"John waited.

"'The experiment will fail unless every person in the room
looks in the opposite direction at the moment of discharge.'

"Bob shook his head.

"'We looked at you last time,' he ses.

"'That was precisely why the first experiment was disturbed,' ses
the conjurer, recovering a little. 'The concentration of so many
eyes produced an adverse current. Had you all looked away,
Mr. Weed's watch would undoubtedly have returned to him.'

"Dicky stared.

"'You never told us.'

"'I was about to, when the volunteer fled.'

"'You fired before 'e fled,' ses Sam.

"The conjurer said that was a vulgar confusion of cause
and effect. Then 'e began talking about rays from the
human eye, and how they attracted metal, and how even
looking sideways might draw the watch into the wrong pocket.
If the trick was to succeed, 'e said, everybody must
turn completely round and shut both eyes.

"'Including you?' ses Bob.

"'Especially me,' ses the conjurer.

"Bob considered it.

"'Sounds fair,' he ses at last. 'We don't want John's
watch coming out o' Smith's ear.'

"So we all turned our backs. John kept the pistol
pointing behind 'im, and Bob told 'im to count five
after the conjurer gave the word. The room went quiet.
I could hear the fire and somebody breathing very hard.

"'Ready,' ses the conjurer.

"'One,' ses John.

"There was a scrape.

"'Two.'

"There was another scrape and a little bump.

"'Three.'

"Somebody whispered, 'Keep your eyes shut.'

"'Four.'

"The door banged.

"'Five.'

"John pulled the trigger.

"The explosion was worse than the first. The mirror cracked,
the lamp went out, and bits of horsehair and wood
flew all over the room. Mrs. Smith screamed from the
kitchen, though she wasn't even there to see it.

"When we turned round, the armchair was on its back
with a hole through it, the stuffing smoking, and one
arm nearly off.

"The conjurer had gone.

"For a moment nobody moved. Then Bob Pretty opened the
door and looked out. The street was empty, but the
Wickham road was long and straight, and far down it
we could see a little black figure running with its
coat-tails flying.

"'Good trick,' ses Bob.

"John Biggs looked at the chair, then at the pistol,
and then searched the floor for his watch. There wasn't
enough left of it to fill a teaspoon.

"Smith wanted thirty shillings for the chair. John said the
conjurer ought to pay. Dicky said the conjurer ought to
pay twenty pounds fust. George Kettle said there was still
the mark in his hat, and Peter Gubbins asked again
about his red handkerchief. They all began quarrelling, and Bob
stood by the door smiling.

"Dicky turned on 'im.

"'You started this,' he ses. 'You've got my watch.'

"Bob looked shocked.

"'After all I've done?' he ses.

"'Wot 'ave you done?'

"'Exposed a dangerous impostor,' ses Bob. 'Saved John from trusting
'im, saved the village from being deceived again, and risked
my life in the cause of truth.'

"'You smashed John's watch,' ses Sam.

"'To prove the trick was false.'

"'You pretended to be shot,' ses Dicky.

"'To see how far the villain would go.'

"'And my gold watch?'

"Bob sighed and shook his head.

"'There you are,' he ses. 'Always suspicion. A man clears
his good name before the whole parish, and wot does
'e get? Gratitude? No. Respect? No. 'E gets accused of
stealing a watch that was last seen inside a loaded
pistol.'

"He drank up his beer, put on his coat, and
walked out with his head very high. He had forgotten
his sticks, but when Dicky called attention to it he
began to limp.

"Dicky never got his watch," concluded the old man. "John
never got his neither, and Smith never got paid for
the chair. But Bob used to say to his dying
day that the business was the best proof of his
honesty anybody could ask for."

The stranger smiled.

"And was it?" he asked.

The old man regarded him with mild reproach, and tapped
the ashes from his pipe upon the sill.

"Well," he said slowly, "nobody ever proved it wasn't."

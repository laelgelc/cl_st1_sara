## A Voyage Prevented

Kate Darlington had reached her twentieth year with beauty enough
to make her admired, wealth enough to make her courted,
and good sense enough to render both admiration and courtship
somewhat troublesome. She was the only daughter of a merchant
whose name stood high in the city; and, as Mr.
Darlington's fortune was believed to be even larger than it
appeared, the gilded moths of society gathered about his drawing-room
with a perseverance which would have been affecting, had its
object been less plainly understood.

Kate was gay and sprightly, with bright eyes, an animated
countenance, and a deportment in which ease was united with
dignity. But she possessed qualities not so immediately visible. She
could distinguish a man from his coat, and character from
reputation—a discrimination less common than charitable persons suppose. The American
dandies who approached her, armed with glossy moustaches, faultless gloves,
and imported opinions, found themselves subjected to an examination for
which no fashionable education had prepared them. They could discourse
upon horses, operas, and the last new mode of tying
a cravat; but when Kate quietly endeavored to discover whether
they had principles, occupations, or any settled purpose in life,
their conversation became less brilliant.

One had spent a winter in Paris and therefore despised
his native country; another had never performed an honest day's
labor, and therefore spoke contemptuously of business; a third considered
himself poetical because his hair was long and his pecuniary
affairs disordered. Kate smiled upon them all, offended none without
necessity, and rejected them without regret.

Mr. Darlington sometimes shook his head.

"My dear," he said, "you are difficult to please."

"Not difficult, father," she replied. "I ask only that a
man should be something, and not merely resemble something."

The merchant laughed; yet he could not deny that the
distinction was just.

There was one young man beneath his roof who would
have borne Kate's examination without fear, though neither he nor
she had thought of making it. Edwin Lee was the
orphaned son of Mr. Darlington's earliest friend. His father had
died when the boy was twelve years of age, leaving
behind an honorable name, a few debts honestly acknowledged, and
no fortune whatever. Mr. Darlington took the child into his
own home, educated him with affectionate care, and, when Edwin
became a man, placed him in the counting-room.

There he proved active, trustworthy, and intelligent. He possessed no
showy talents, but many useful ones; he spoke little of
what he intended and generally accomplished more than he promised.
His manner was kind, modest, gentlemanly; his habits were regular;
and his gratitude toward his benefactor was not the servile
submission of dependence, but the warm and manly attachment of
a generous nature.

Kate and Edwin had grown up together. They had studied
beside the same winter fire, ridden under the same summer
trees, quarreled over books, made peace over music, and called
one another brother and sister so long that the household
had almost forgotten there was no tie of blood between
them. Edwin corrected Kate's accounts when she undertook charitable enterprises
too extensive for her allowance; Kate laughed him out of
his gravity when the perplexities of the counting-room followed him
home. Their confidence appeared perfect, their affection tranquil.

But youth does not remain stationary because custom finds its
changes inconvenient.

Kate returned one spring from a visit in the country.
Edwin met her at the door, and in that instant
discovered what three weeks of absence had done. Kate was
the same to him; and yet not the same. Her
voice had its old sweetness, her smile its old sincerity,
but both had acquired a power which alarmed him. He
could no longer hear her step without listening; he could
no longer sit beside her without remembering that she was
young, beautiful, and heiress to a great fortune, while he
was a dependent, possessed of nothing but his character and
his salary.

Had Edwin been vain, he might have mistaken kindness for
preference; had he been selfish, he might have resolved to
profit by their intimacy. But he was imbued with a
passion which made him fearful for Kate's peace and jealous
of his own honor. Mr. Darlington had received him when
he was poor and friendless. Could he repay that confidence
by secretly seeking the daughter? Could he expose himself to
the suspicion that gratitude had been a calculation and affection
an investment?

"No," he said, in the solitude of his chamber. "I
must go."

The decision cost him much, but he believed it right;
and some excellent people, when once convinced that misery is
a duty, pursue it with astonishing firmness.

A commercial house connected with Mr. Darlington's business required a
trustworthy person to sail in one of its vessels for
Valparaiso. The voyage would be long, the position responsible, and
the absence perhaps prolonged through several years. Edwin offered himself
and was accepted. He then informed his benefactor that he
intended to depart within a fortnight.

Mr. Darlington laid down his spectacles and looked at him
with unfeigned astonishment.

"Leave my counting-room? Leave this house? Why, Edwin, what dissatisfaction
have you concealed from me?"

"None, sir. I have received only kindness."

"Is your salary insufficient?"

"It is more liberal than my services deserve."

"Have you quarreled with anyone?"

"With no one."

"Then why conjure up the necessity of crossing half the
world?"

Edwin's cheek flushed, but his answer was steady.

"I believe the change will be advantageous."

"To your fortune, perhaps; but will it be advantageous to
your happiness?"

"That is of less consequence."

Mr. Darlington regarded him keenly. "Not to me."

Yet all persuasion failed. Edwin was respectful, grateful, immovable. He
would assign no cause beyond a desire for wider experience
and independence. The merchant, who had never used benefits as
chains, could not forbid him to go; but he was
wounded by the reserve and disturbed by the determination.

Kate received the news with far less composure.

"Valparaiso!" she exclaimed. "And when did Edwin become weary of
us?"

"He says he is weary of nothing," replied her father.

"Then his conduct is without reason."

"There is a reason, depend upon it; but the young
gentleman chooses to keep it locked in his own breast."

Kate was silent. During the following days she watched Edwin
with an attention sharpened by pain. He avoided being alone
with her; yet she sometimes found his eyes fixed upon
her when he believed himself unobserved. He joined the family
in the evening, but his accustomed cheerfulness had vanished. If
she spoke of his voyage, he grew embarrassed; if she
spoke of his return, a shadow crossed his face. These
signs, insignificant to vanity and invisible to stupidity, were clear
enough to affection.

One afternoon she found him in the library arranging certain
papers which he wished to leave in order. The rain
beat softly against the windows, and the gray light gave
to his serious countenance an expression of deeper sadness.

"Edwin," she said, "I have come to ask you a
question."

He rose. "I hope I can answer it."

"I hope so too. Why are you going away?"

"For the reasons I have given your father."

"Those reasons satisfy neither him nor me."

Edwin turned toward the table. "They are the only ones
I am at liberty to give."

"Then there are others."

He made no reply.

Kate approached him with less playfulness than was usual in
her manner.

"Have I offended you?"

"You!" The word escaped with such warmth that denial became
unnecessary. "Never."

"Are you unhappy here?"

"No."

"Do you expect to be happy away?"

His hand trembled among the papers.

"I expect to do my duty."

Kate's perceptive eyes rested upon his face. A sudden light
entered her mind, and with it came confusion, tenderness, and
a strange joy which she had never felt when surrounded
by more splendid admirers. Edwin could endure her gaze no
longer. He took up a packet, laid it down again,
and appeared deeply interested in a ribbon which required no
attention whatever.

"Edwin," she said softly, "is it because there is someone
here whom you wish to forget?"

His face became crimson.

There are silences more explicit than declarations. Kate had received
her answer.

She did not compel him to dishonor his resolution by
a confession he believed forbidden. With feminine delicacy—and, we may
add, with feminine decision—she merely said, "I am sorry that
you have so little confidence in your friends," and left
the room.

Edwin remained motionless. He feared that his secret was discovered;
he scarcely dared hope that it was not unwelcome.

Kate went directly to her father's private apartment. Mr. Darlington
was examining a ledger, but one glance at her countenance
caused him to close it.

"Well, my child?"

"Father, I know why Edwin is going."

"Indeed! You have succeeded where I failed. What is this
tremendous mystery?"

"He is in love."

The merchant raised his brows. "That is a common misfortune
at his age. With whom?"

"With me."

Mr. Darlington rose and walked twice across the room.

"Has he told you so?"

"No."

"Then how do you know it?"

"By everything he could not tell me."

The father's expression grew grave. "And what is Edwin's wish?"

"To go away rather than appear ungrateful to you."

Mr. Darlington stopped. The whole conduct of the young man
became intelligible. His heart warmed with pride even while prudence
whispered objections.

"He is honorable," he said at last.

"That is why he intends to leave us."

"But, Kate, Edwin is poor."

"I know it."

"You are accustomed to every comfort."

"Edwin has intelligence and industry."

"Society will be surprised."

"Society is frequently surprised by anything reasonable."

Mr. Darlington could not repress a smile; but he looked
earnestly at his daughter.

"This is no subject for wit. You have called him
brother all your life."

"I was a child all my life—until lately."

"Do you love him?"

Kate's color rose, though her eyes did not fall.

"I respect him more than any man I know. I
trust him entirely. His presence makes home happier, and the
thought of his departure makes it desolate. If that is
not love, father, it has at least more of love
in it than all the compliments I have ever heard."

The merchant drew her toward him and kissed her forehead.

"My dear Kate, I have desired your happiness, not a
title for your visiting cards. Yet I must be certain.
Edwin's virtues are real; but marriage demands more than gratitude
on one side and admiration on the other."

"Then prevent the voyage," she replied. "Time will prove the
rest."

Mr. Darlington reflected. He had named Edwin's poverty because the
world would name it; but he knew that riches cannot
purchase honor, and that a man capable of sacrificing happiness
rather than abuse confidence possessed a wealth not entered in
bank accounts. Before Kate left him, he promised that Edwin
should not go without another effort.

The next evening, as they sat together in the drawing-room,
the approaching separation oppressed them all. Edwin's passage had been
secured; his trunks were packed; the ship would sail in
three days.

Mr. Darlington broke the silence.

"Edwin, Kate and I have agreed that your voyage must
be abandoned."

Edwin started. "Sir, I am deeply grateful; but my arrangements
are made."

"Arrangements may be unmade."

"My honor—"

"Your honor is not in danger."

Edwin looked quickly at him, then at Kate. She sat
near the window, apparently occupied with her embroidery, though the
same flower had been subjected to her needle for several
minutes without visible improvement.

Kate raised her eyes.

"Edwin, will you be happier in Valparaiso?"

He tried to answer lightly, but truth and sorrow overcame
caution.

"No, Kate. I shall be miserable."

"Then why go?"

"To remain might be presumption."

"Toward whom?"

He was silent.

Mr. Darlington took up his hat with remarkable deliberation.

"I have remembered an engagement," he said. "Kate, perhaps you
can convince our obstinate traveler that honor in fact, not
honor in appearance, should govern a man's conduct. Honor seems
to send him away; but I suspect love would make
him stay."

Having delivered this observation, the good gentleman withdrew. And we
think we had as well retire with the reader; for
there are explanations which lose their beauty when reported by
a third person, and promises which are sacred though spoken
in a drawing-room.

It is sufficient to say that Edwin did not sail
for Valparaiso.

When, some months afterward, Kate Darlington married the penniless clerk,
fashionable society was first astonished, then amused, and finally compassionate.
The dandies declared that she had thrown herself away; young
ladies wondered how she could endure a husband without fortune;
and several prudent matrons predicted unhappiness with that solemn satisfaction
which worldly wisdom derives from another's supposed mistake.

Their predictions failed. Edwin justified Mr. Darlington's confidence, increased in
usefulness and prosperity, and never forgot that independence is best
deserved by those who do not grasp at it dishonorably.
Kate found no cause to regret that she had chosen
substance instead of display, intelligence instead of pretension, and manliness
of character instead of a fashionable name.

She was perhaps no longer considered a fashionable wife.

She was a happy one.

# The Measure of Emptiness

In earlier years, when public fasting was still counted among
the serious entertainments of a city, the hunger artist enjoyed
a reputation of unusual extent. His cage might be erected
in the central arcade of a hotel, under the glass
roof of a railway hall, or in a rented pavilion
beside the municipal gardens; wherever it stood, people came in
such numbers that ropes had to be stretched before the
entrance and policemen assigned to regulate the queue. Children were
lifted onto shoulders. Old men removed their hats. Ladies, returning
a second or third time, spoke of the alteration in
his face as though discussing a difficult work of portraiture.

The cage itself was simple. It had black bars, a
floor scattered with straw, a clock hung outside the door,
and, fixed above it, a white board bearing the number
of the day. Within this narrow equipment the hunger artist
sat, lay, or sometimes stood upright with one hand around
a bar. He wore a dark close-fitting costume which, as
the fast advanced, seemed less to clothe him than to
preserve an official outline around his bones. He spoke little.
When a spectator addressed him courteously, he inclined his head;
when a child, emboldened by its parents, asked whether he
was hungry, he sometimes smiled.

His profession required him to consume nothing. This was the
whole principle, but the simplicity of the principle gave rise
to innumerable administrative difficulties.

It was not enough that he should fast. He must
be seen to fast, and, more importantly, the public must
be assured that what it saw was genuine. For this
reason groups of watchers were appointed by lot to sit
beside the cage through the night. These men were commonly
butchers, bakers, restaurant porters, hospital attendants, or others believed to
possess practical knowledge of food and the concealment of food.
They occupied a table under a hanging lamp, played cards,
smoked, argued, and kept the prisoner under inspection.

Some watchers took their duty seriously. They leaned forward at
the least movement in the straw, directed flashlights into the
corners, and examined the artist's drinking glass before and after
he touched it. Such men pleased him, though their suspicion
was insulting, because at least it gave him an opportunity
to prove himself. He would sit awake until morning, accepting
their inspection with almost festive patience, and sometimes even sing
in his thin voice, so that they might hear that
his mouth contained nothing.

Other watchers, from kindness or laziness, withdrew to the far
side of the room and whispered among themselves, allowing him,
as they believed, a chance to take some secret refreshment.
They supposed this courtesy would be gratefully understood. In fact,
nothing tormented him more. He would rattle the bars, call
them back, and ask whether they intended to fulfill their
duty. Often they laughed at his anxiety.

"Rest, my good fellow," one might say. "We are not
tyrants."

But he did not want mercy. Mercy, in this case,
was only suspicion wearing a benevolent face. If they slept,
the morning visitors would say he might have eaten. If
they watched, they would say he had hidden food beforehand.
If the cage was searched, then the search had not
been sufficiently thorough; if doctors measured him, the instruments might
have been inaccurate; if photographs were taken, someone would claim
that photographs proved nothing. Against suspicion there was no complete
procedure. This fact, which ought perhaps to have reconciled him
to the inevitable, only sharpened his resentment.

Nevertheless, he fasted. He fasted while the guards played cards,
while the hotel guests ate breakfast behind the palms, while
bands performed in the gardens, and while the large clock
outside his cage divided into identical minutes an accomplishment which,
to him, admitted of no natural division at all.

Fasting was easy for him. He alone knew how easy
it was.

This was his secret, though he did not hide it.
On the contrary, he was always prepared to explain it,
but no one accepted the explanation in its plain form.
People assumed that every difficult achievement must be made possible
by a method and that every method, if known, could
be copied. When he said there was no method, they
interpreted this as professional discretion. When he said he did
not suffer, they called it courage. When he said he
could continue, they praised his pride. His statements returned to
him improved beyond recognition.

The impresario understood this public error and used it carefully.
He was a large, smooth man with silvered hair, quick
hands, and a voice capable of making every announcement sound
like an act of generosity. He managed the hall, the
newspapers, the medical certificates, the watchers, the flower arrangements, and
the conclusion of the fast. Above all, he managed the
number forty.

No fast was permitted to last longer than forty days.

The impresario had not chosen the number without experience. During
the first week curiosity was high. In the second week
the better classes began to attend. By the third, newspapers
printed daily bulletins, and photographs of the artist appeared beside
reports of cabinet crises, murders, and horse races. At about
the thirtieth day, however, attendance became unstable. Those who wished
to see suffering had seen it; those who wished to
see endurance were uncertain how long they could be expected
to endure the sight of it. By the fortieth day,
publicity could produce one last profitable ascent. Beyond that day
the public's patience, which was often mistaken for admiration, could
no longer be guaranteed.

The hunger artist disputed the rule whenever he had sufficient
breath to do so.

"Why forty?" he asked.

"Because forty has dignity," said the impresario. "Thirty-nine is accidental,
and forty-one is untidy."

"But I can continue."

"That is precisely what we must not allow people to
discover."

This answer, though perhaps the most honest the impresario ever
gave, did not satisfy him. To stop at forty days
was, in the artist's view, to stop just as the
fast had become pure, when the body's first complaints had
quieted and the trivial interest of beginners lay behind him.
The first days were nothing. Anyone could avoid a meal,
many could avoid several, and even a moderately vain person
could resist food for a week if enough witnesses were
present. Only later did fasting become a realm in which
he had no rival. Yet each time he reached its
border, music was summoned and the door unlocked.

The ending ceremony always took place at noon.

By then the hall was decorated with flags and white
flowers. A military band stood ready near the platform. Doctors
in evening dress approached the cage, displayed charts of the
artist's declining weight, and announced in solemn voices that further
abstinence would endanger his life. Two young women, selected by
public subscription or newspaper competition, were granted the honour of
assisting him from the cage.

The women were invariably excited until the door opened. Then,
seeing him close at hand—his damp hair, the skin drawn
tightly over his cheeks, the knees that seemed unable to
carry even their own small weight—they became pale and uncertain.
One took his right arm, the other his left. The
impresario, smiling toward the photographers, gave instructions in a whisper.

"Gently, ladies. But firmly. He is overcome."

He was indeed overcome, though not in the advertised sense.
His legs failed beneath him. His head rolled. He tried
to turn back toward the cage. The young women, believing
this movement a sign of collapse, held him tighter, while
the impresario supported him from behind with such enthusiasm that,
for several steps, the artist was scarcely walking at all
but was swung between the three of them like an
inconvenient parcel.

The audience laughed nervously. The band played louder.

At a table on the platform waited a bowl of
broth, a folded napkin, and a physician with a spoon.
The artist refused the first spoonful. This refusal was expected
and much admired. He refused the second, which was considered
dramatic. At the third the impresario bent over him and
said through his smile:

"Do not spoil everything."

Then the artist swallowed. Applause broke out. Flash powder burst
white in his eyes. One of the young women began
to cry, partly from pity, partly from relief at having
completed her task, and perhaps partly because the photographers had
turned toward her. The impresario raised both hands and invited
the public to salute an unparalleled triumph of self-denial.

The artist sat beneath the flowers and hated them all.

He hated the broth, the music, the charts, the laughing,
the tears, and the word triumph. Most of all he
hated the implication that he had been rescued from himself
at the final permissible moment. Rescued from what? From success?
From the continuation of the only act he could perform
without deceit? Had they allowed him another week, another month,
an entire season, he would have astonished them. He would
have shown them the true scale of his art. Instead
they applauded the interruption and called it the achievement.

Afterward, in private, the impresario accused him of ingratitude.

"You are celebrated," he said. "Do you understand what that
means?"

"I am prevented."

"You are protected."

"From fasting?"

"From indifference."

At such times the impresario produced photographs. Some showed the
artist on the final day, his head fallen against his
chest; others showed the decorated hall or the two young
women supporting him. One photograph, especially favoured for publicity, caught
him at an angle that made his body appear twisted
with pain. The impresario pointed to it.

"There," he said. "There is the proof that forty days
is enough."

"It proves nothing."

"It proves what the public can understand."

"But I was only sad."

The impresario laughed, not cruelly, but with the exhaustion of
a practical man compelled to discuss fine distinctions with someone
whose living depended upon ignoring them. He explained that sadness
photographed extremely well, whereas satisfaction at indefinite fasting would be
invisible. The artist listened with his face turned away.

Soon another engagement began, in another city, beneath another clock.
The same objections were raised, the same watchers appointed, the
same white board marked each morning with a larger number.
As the years passed, his fame increased. Children who had
once stared through the bars returned as adults, bringing children
of their own. Teachers referred to him in lessons on
willpower. Clergymen praised him as an example of the conquest
of appetite, though some warned against pride. Doctors published disagreements
about his metabolism. Restaurant owners displayed his portrait beside menus,
a joke that brought them considerable custom.

Only the hunger artist remained dissatisfied.

To the public he appeared severe, but severity was not
the source of his isolation. He wished to be understood.
This wish was perhaps unreasonable, since understanding would have required
the spectators not only to believe that he fasted but
also to grasp why fasting was no hardship to him;
and if it was no hardship, then admiration became difficult,
while without admiration they saw no reason to visit. Thus
the truth of his art threatened the existence of his
profession. He knew this, of course, but knowledge did not
make the arrangement less offensive.

Sometimes a spectator, usually a young man eager to display
independence, declared loudly that the artist must be cheating.

The artist would rise, grip the bars, and demand that
the accusation be examined.

The impresario intervened at once. He assured the young man
that such doubts were natural, indeed intelligent, and that the
management welcomed scrutiny. He then offered a tour of the
cage, produced signed statements, and introduced the chief physician. In
this way the insult became additional entertainment. The accuser left
satisfied, the surrounding spectators congratulated themselves on their fairness, and
the hunger artist returned to the straw trembling with rage.

At other times an old woman would approach, gaze at
him for a long while, and weep. Such sympathy depressed
him no less. He did not want to be pitied
for doing what he desired. Yet if he tried to
reassure her, his voice sounded so weak that she wept
more freely.

There was no correct response. It was all futile.

Then the fashion changed.

No decree announced the change. No critic established that fasting
had ceased to be art. For a while, in fact,
the pavilions continued to fill, and newspapers continued to print
the daily figures; but the crowds had acquired a different
quality. People entered because they had entered before. They looked
briefly, searched for something they could not name, and departed
with a slight embarrassment. New amusements appeared. Mechanical riders crossed
suspended wires. Divers plunged into shallow tanks. Troupes of trained
dogs performed military drills. A woman slept for a week
in a glass room while her dreams were interpreted by
experts.

Beside these developments, fasting seemed old-fashioned. Its very stillness, once
regarded as sublime, began to resemble a failure to provide
a performance.

The impresario fought bravely. He redesigned the advertisements, enlarged the
day counter, hired musicians, dismissed musicians, moved the cage from
indoor halls to public squares and back again. He announced
that the present fast would surpass all previous fasts, although
the fortieth-day limit remained unchanged. He arranged lectures on nutrition,
public debates, charitable breakfasts, and a procession of local officials.
Nothing restored the former enthusiasm.

In smaller towns, the artist now saw empty spaces behind
the ropes. School parties arrived under instruction and left in
disorder. Watchers failed to report for duty. Once, during a
night shift, all three appointed guards went across the street
for sausages, leaving the cage entirely unobserved for nearly an
hour. The artist called after them until his voice broke.
In the morning they claimed they had been able to
see the entrance through the tavern window.

The impresario took him aside after that engagement.

"We must be realistic," he said.

"I have always been realistic."

"No. You have always been exact. That is different."

The artist wanted to ask whether the impresario would now
permit him to fast beyond forty days. The question seemed
more justified than ever: if the audience no longer came,
what commercial harm could continuation cause? Yet he understood from
the impresario's expression that the rule would be maintained even
in an empty hall. A conclusion ceremony had been advertised;
flowers had been ordered; the young women had been chosen.
Administration survived interest.

Not long afterward they separated.

There was no quarrel. The contract expired, accounts were settled,
and the impresario, who had grown older but not smaller,
pressed the artist's hand with genuine emotion. He spoke of
their years together, of the great halls, the distinguished visitors,
the medical controversies, and the unforgettable receptions. He did not
mention the artist's grievance. Perhaps he had never believed it
existed.

"You will be remembered," he said.

"I do not want to be remembered for stopping."

The impresario sighed. "Then you will not be remembered at
all."

The hunger artist joined a circus.

This decision appeared to many an admission of defeat. He
himself considered it an escape. The circus possessed crowds larger
than any he could now attract independently, and although the
management made clear that he would not be presented in
the central ring, he accepted a place near the animal
stalls. His cage was set along the passage leading from
the equestrian tent to the menagerie, between a storage enclosure
for harnesses and a row of beasts whose cries and
odours filled the air.

A new board was fixed above the bars. Fresh straw
was laid down. The circus painter produced a sign showing
the artist in the celebrated posture of his earlier years,
though the painted figure had broad shoulders, noble eyes, and
a chest sufficiently full to make fasting seem a deliberate
conquest rather than a physical condition.

At first the arrangement seemed favourable. Crowds passed all day.
Visitors stopped, read the sign, and looked inside. Some remembered
his name. Others asked what trick he performed. The attendants
explained that there was no trick: the man fasted continuously,
and the number of days was recorded on the board.

Yet the cage stood on the way to the animals,
and this proved decisive. People did not arrive before it;
they were delayed by it. The children tugged at their
parents' sleeves, impatient to see the lions fed, the bears
bathed, the monkeys quarrel, and the great cats circle behind
their bars. The adult visitors, who might otherwise have studied
the artist for a minute, were dragged onward. Those returning
from the stalls had already spent their attention. They carried
sweets, animal whistles, feathers, and the heavy smell of the
menagerie. They glanced at the fasting man as one glances
at a notice concerning an event that has passed.

The artist heard everything: the crack of whips, the rolling
of feed carts, the laughter when an animal lunged at
the bars, the shouts announcing the next performance. At feeding
time, raw meat went past his cage in red slabs.
Buckets of milk, baskets of fish, bundles of hay, and
sacks of grain were carried into the stalls. The abundance
did not tempt him. It oppressed him only because it
demonstrated the ease with which appetite communicated itself. No explanation
was required for a tiger's hunger. No certificate was demanded
from a wolf.

Still, he was free to fast.

The circus had no forty-day rule. Indeed, the management had
no particular rule at all, provided his cage did not
obstruct traffic and his maintenance cost little. An attendant changed
the number each morning. A doctor visited at irregular intervals.
Once a week the straw was replaced. These arrangements satisfied
the contract, and the artist, who had imagined that unlimited
fasting required only the removal of a prohibition, settled down
to the greatest achievement of his life.

The number rose.

At fifty days a few old admirers came with flowers.
At sixty, a provincial paper printed a short item, though
it mistakenly gave his former impresario's name as his own.
At seventy, the sign was smeared by rain that blew
beneath the canvas roof. At eighty, an attendant failed to
alter the board for three mornings and then, unable to
recall the correct figure, advanced it by two.

The artist protested.

"You may be right," the attendant said. "Or it may
be one more."

"It is important."

"Of course."

The attendant promised to consult the records. There were no
records.

After this, accuracy declined rapidly. The board displayed ninety-seven for
almost a week, then one hundred and three, then eighty-nine,
because a child had turned one of the painted cards
upside down. The artist tried to keep count himself, but
time, when marked by hunger alone, did not divide cleanly.
Daylight entered the passage weakly. Performances repeated. The circus traveled.
The cage was loaded onto wagons at night and rebuilt
in new towns, sometimes facing east, sometimes west, sometimes no
visible sky at all.

He continued.

His body diminished beneath the dark costume. His hair spread
over the straw. The attendants no longer asked him to
stand for inspection, since standing required assistance and assistance took
time. A drinking glass remained by the bars, clouded with
dust. The white board became grey. The painter's handsome portrait
cracked across the chest.

There were days when no one looked into the cage.
This was not literally true, perhaps: a child might point,
a cleaner might reach through with a broom, an animal
keeper might check that the door remained locked. But no
one looked with the intention of seeing him. He had
escaped suspicion and reached neglect. The distinction was greater than
he had expected.

At first he welcomed it. Here, finally, no impresario shook
him awake beneath flowers; no doctor declared that continuation was
impossible; no young women dragged him toward broth. He could
surpass every former record. He could fast to the utmost
boundary of fasting, if such a boundary existed.

But where was the triumph if the number was unknown?
How could he surpass a record when no one knew
the day? He attempted to inform passing visitors.

"I am still fasting," he whispered.

They heard only a dry movement in the straw.

"Something is in there," a child said once.

"It is the fasting man," replied the father.

"Is he alive?"

"Presumably. Come, the panthers are being fed."

People went straight past him.

The circus staff also grew accustomed to the cage. New
employees assumed it contained old equipment. A stable boy stored
two broken buckets beside it. Posters were leaned against the
outer bars. During wet weather a tarpaulin was thrown over
one side, darkening the interior. The weekly replacement of straw
ceased. Dust mixed with the bedding; scraps of paper collected
in the corners; a sour, earthy smell rose from below.

The artist did not complain. Or, more precisely, he complained
continually, but his complaints no longer reached speech. Within himself
he conducted the old arguments with extraordinary exactness. He answered
the impresario, corrected the doctors, summoned the absent watchers, and
demanded that the day be properly entered on the board.
His reasoning remained alert even as its subject became uncertain.
Was it the two hundredth day? The three hundredth? Had
the circus wintered once since he entered, or twice? He
could not tell.

At any rate, no one stopped him.

One morning, between the departure of a train of animal
wagons and the opening of the ticket booths, a supervisor
made an inspection of the passage. He was a newly
promoted man, conscientious and severe, who disliked unaccounted objects. Seeing
the dirty cage, the leaning posters, and the board with
its illegible numerals, he called an attendant.

"Why is this here?"

"The cage?"

"I can see that it is a cage."

"It belongs to the fasting display."

"What fasting display?"

The attendant hesitated. He had worked for the circus only
six weeks. Another man was summoned, then a third. The
oldest remembered that a hunger artist had once occupied the
cage, though he supposed the performer had left at the
end of the previous season.

The supervisor struck the bars with his cane.

"Is anyone inside?"

From beneath the rotting straw came a faint sound.

The men stepped back. One fetched flashlights. Their beams crossed
over the bedding, found the cloudy glass, the folded costume,
and at last a face so reduced that it seemed
less uncovered than excavated. The hunger artist's eyes were open.

"Clear this away," said the supervisor.

They pulled out the posters and broken buckets. The cage
door, rusted at the hinge, required oil and several blows
before it opened. The supervisor entered, holding a handkerchief over
his mouth. He knelt beside the artist.

"So you are still fasting?" he asked.

The artist moved his lips.

"Quiet," said the supervisor. "He is speaking."

The men leaned closer.

"I wanted you to admire me," the artist whispered.

The supervisor, who was not unkind, answered in the tone
used for dying employees and frightened animals.

"We do admire you."

"You should not."

"Very well, then. We do not. But why should we
not?"

"Because fasting was easy."

"That may be."

"I had to fast."

The supervisor lowered his head until his ear was almost
against the artist's mouth.

"Why?"

For the first time, the hunger artist's expression lost its
grievance. Something like apology passed across it, though there was
hardly enough flesh remaining to carry an expression.

"Because I never found the food I liked."

The supervisor waited for more. None came.

"If I had found it," the artist breathed, "I would
have eaten like anyone."

His eyes remained open, fixed not on the men but
on some point beyond the bars, perhaps the point at
which the long calculation of his achievement had finally ended.
A moment later there was no reason to continue kneeling.

The supervisor stood.

"Clear the cage," he ordered.

They buried the hunger artist with the straw. No ceremony
was arranged, partly because his contract could not be found
and partly because the circus was opening that afternoon. The
old sign was removed, the drinking glass thrown away, and
the bars scrubbed. Fresh sand was spread across the floor.
The rusted hinge was repaired.

Into the cage they brought a young panther.

It was a large animal, black along the back and
gold at the flanks, with shoulders that rolled beneath its
skin as if strength had become impatient with its own
confinement. The cage, which had seemed enormous around the hunger
artist, appeared scarcely sufficient for two of its strides. It
crossed from bar to bar, turned, and crossed again. Its
tail struck the air. Its eyes followed every movement in
the passage.

The animal keeper brought meat. The panther seized it before
it touched the floor. Its jaws closed with a wet
crack, and a sound rose from its throat—not anger, exactly,
nor pleasure alone, but an unquestionable announcement of life. Nothing
in it required interpretation. Its appetite was visible in its
teeth, its ribs, its paws, in the splendid violence with
which it consumed what had been given.

The crowds gathered immediately.

Children pressed against the railing. Parents who had hurried past
the cage for months now remained until attendants urged them
onward. Even the circus workers stopped during their errands. The
panther seemed to carry freedom within its body, although the
bars were as firm as before. It sprang, fed, breathed,
and stared. Its vitality filled the cage almost to breaking.

No one asked how many days it had been there.

No one suspected it of cheating.

And for a long time the passage could not be
cleared.

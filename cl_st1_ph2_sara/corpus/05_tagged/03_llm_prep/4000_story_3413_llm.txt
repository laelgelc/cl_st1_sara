### THE CRYSTAL ABYSS

For three weeks after the automatic projectile had disappeared into
the experimental shaft, Roland Clewe permitted no one but Bryce
to enter the western shed.

There was nothing suspicious in this prohibition, for Clewe's workmen
were accustomed to arbitrary boundaries. One building contained delicate electrical
instruments; another was given over to explosive compounds; a third
sheltered machines whose purpose their inventor had not yet chosen
to disclose. But in the western shed the secrecy was
unusually complete. The windows were covered, the doors were locked,
and Bryce himself slept in a small room adjoining it.

Within stood a steel car, narrow enough to pass freely
down the shaft and strong enough, Clewe believed, to resist
any pressure it was likely to encounter. Its shape was
cylindrical, with a rounded bottom and a roof carrying a
double suspension joint. Through this joint passed the telephone wire,
the electric conductors, and the cable by which the car
was to be lowered.

The interior had been arranged with almost domestic care. There
were electric lamps, a folding seat, two reservoirs of compressed
air, an apparatus for purifying the atmosphere, a barometer, thermometers,
a compass, a chronometer, and an indicator which recorded every
foot of descent. A locker contained water, concentrated food, brandy,
medicines, tools, rope, and a pair of pistols, though against
what enemy the pistols could be employed Clewe did not
attempt to say. An independent battery supplied the lamps in
case the conducting wires should fail. There were also oxygen
cylinders, a fire extinguisher, an electric drill, and a small
windlass attached to two hundred feet of steel cord.

Bryce examined every fitting twice.

"The main cable has been tested to eight times the
weight," he said. "The auxiliary cable to five. Either one
should hold you."

"Unless it is cut upon the edge of some stratum,"
replied Clewe.

"We have lined every part of the shaft which showed
a cutting surface."

"Then unless a mass of rock falls upon the car."

"The projectile has left a clean aperture."

"Or unless the heat becomes excessive."

"In that case you will return."

Clewe smiled.

"You put the matter very simply."

"I desire that it should remain simple," said Bryce. "You
go down, you observe, and you come back."

This was indeed the plan, and it sounded reasonable when
thus expressed; nevertheless, both men knew that no human being
had ever undertaken a journey comparable to it.

The shaft had been made by Clewe's automatic shell, a
huge pointed projectile driven downward by successive internal explosions. Its
machinery had been designed to take advantage of the resistance
it encountered, and instead of exhausting its force in the
manner of a cannon-ball striking a target, it renewed that
force at regular intervals. Instruments connected with it had continued
to report its progress until, at a depth of more
than fourteen miles, its signals had suddenly ceased.

Why they had ceased Clewe intended to discover.

Shortly after midnight, when the buildings were quiet and the
moon had passed behind a bank of clouds, the roof
of the shed was rolled back. The steel cable rose
from the car to a system of pulleys mounted upon
a massive iron frame, and thence passed to a battery
of windlasses in the adjoining engine-house. Every drum had its
brake; every brake had its duplicate; and beside Bryce's telephone
stood a row of electric bells by which Clewe could
order a slower or more rapid movement without speaking.

At one o'clock Clewe entered the car.

"You may yet abandon the attempt," said Bryce.

"And spend the remainder of my life wondering what lies
below?"

"That would be preferable to spending the remainder of your
life below."

Clewe held out his hand.

"You will keep the car moving at the prescribed rate.
If I ring twice, stop. Three times, raise me. If
communication fails, raise me immediately."

Bryce grasped his hand, but made no further objection.

The door was closed and fastened from within. A moment
later the car rose an inch, swung gently over the
black circle in the floor, and began to descend.

At first Clewe could see nothing through the thick glass
windows except the stone lining of the upper shaft. It
moved upward with a smoothness which was almost deceptive. Had
he not watched the indicator, he might have imagined that
the car remained stationary while the earth itself ascended around
him.

"How do you proceed?" asked Bryce through the telephone.

"Perfectly. The car does not vibrate. Lower a little faster."

The speed increased.

Down, down he went. The granite wall gave place to
bands of darker stone, then to a glittering stratum in
which particles of mica caught the electric rays. Water appeared
in narrow seams and ran upward from his point of
view, breaking into drops as the car passed. In one
region the rock was reddish; in another it was almost
white. Clewe made notes upon a board attached to the
wall, marking the depths at which these changes occurred.

For two hours the descent retained the character of a
scientific observation. He measured, recorded, telephoned, and calculated. The temperature
rose, but much less than certain geologists had predicted. The
walls remained firm. Once the car turned slowly upon its
cable, and the ascending strata revolved about him like the
painted scenery of a theatre; but the motion soon ceased.

At four miles he ate a biscuit and drank some
water.

At six miles he lighted a cigar, although Bryce had
warned him that tobacco smoke would impose an unnecessary burden
upon the purifying apparatus. The familiar occupation comforted him. He
began to think of matters wholly unconnected with the expedition:
a defective governor in the northern engine-house, an unanswered letter
from a manufacturer in Ohio, and the foolish persistence of
the gardener who planted tulips beside the laboratory door where
every delivery wagon must pass over them.

"Temperature?" asked Bryce.

"Ninety-one."

"Air?"

"Excellent."

"Any discomfort?"

"My cigar does not draw well."

"That may be considered a providential warning."

Clewe laughed, and the sound, confined within the narrow steel
chamber, appeared louder than it should have been.

At eight miles the walls became exceedingly dark and metallic.
The compass behaved strangely; its needle trembled, swung east, and
then remained fixed against the side of its case. The
telephone hummed with an intermittent note, but Bryce's voice was
still audible.

At ten miles Clewe ceased to think of the gardener.

The vast mass above him had begun to enter his
consciousness. He knew that the shaft was open, that the
car hung from cables, and that engines under Bryce's hand
could draw him back; yet these facts seemed small and
artificial compared with the fourteen miles of solid earth through
which he proposed to pass. Above were fields, houses, trees,
moving clouds, and men asleep in their beds. Below was
the unknown. Between the two hung a steel cylinder no
larger than a boiler.

He checked the clamps upon the door and inspected the
oxygen valves.

"Is anything wrong?" asked Bryce, who had heard him moving.

"Nothing whatever."

"You have been silent."

"I have been considering the pressure upon the neighboring rock."

"Do not consider it."

Clewe smiled faintly. "A useful scientific direction."

At twelve miles the temperature reached one hundred and fourteen
degrees, then, contrary to expectation, began slowly to fall. Clewe
distrusted the thermometer and compared it with the second instrument.
Both agreed. The air outside the car could not be
tested, but the steel wall itself was becoming cooler.

He reported this circumstance.

"I do not like it," said Bryce.

"Why should cold be more alarming than heat?"

"Because we expected heat."

That answer expressed Clewe's own feeling. A danger predicted by
theory could be met by preparation; an unexpected benefit might
announce the failure of every theory upon which the preparations
had been based.

The shaft now appeared perfectly black beyond the reach of
the lamps. The wall had changed again, but he could
not determine its composition. Here and there it shone with
a dull lustre. The descent continued, foot by foot, mile
after mile, until the indicator approached the depth at which
the projectile's signals had ended.

Clewe extinguished one interior lamp so that he might see
more clearly through the lower window.

"Slowly now," he said.

The movement became scarcely perceptible.

"Thirteen miles, seven thousand eight hundred and ten feet," Bryce
announced.

"I have the same reading."

"Do you see anything?"

"The shaft. Nothing else."

Another hundred feet passed. Then another.

Suddenly a pale radiance appeared below.

At first Clewe supposed it to be a reflection from
his exterior lamps, but it increased even after he shut
them off. It was not a point of light, nor
did it come from any visible object. The lower end
of the shaft seemed filled with a clear, steady illumination
resembling daylight seen beneath deep water.

"Stop!" he cried.

The car halted.

"What is it?" demanded Bryce.

"There is light beneath me."

"From the projectile?"

"I cannot tell. Lower me ten feet."

The car descended. The wall surrounding it came abruptly to
an end.

Clewe looked out and saw space.

The effect was so extraordinary that for several seconds he
neither spoke nor breathed. The car hung beneath the opening
of the shaft in an immensity of luminous emptiness. Above
him was the round black mouth through which he had
emerged; on every other side extended a region without apparent
wall, floor, or roof. The light existed everywhere and came
from nowhere. It was brilliant but soft, and objects within
it showed no harsh reflection.

There was one object.

Some distance below, and a little to the right, rested
the projectile.

Its steel shell had been crushed and torn. The pointed
end was bent backward, plates had sprung from their fastenings,
and part of the automatic mechanism protruded like the bones
of a shattered animal. Yet the great machine did not
fall. It remained motionless in that shining vacancy, apparently sustained
by nothing.

Clewe stared at it.

"Do you see the shell?" asked Bryce.

"Yes."

"Where is it?"

"Below me."

"Upon what does it lie?"

"I do not know."

"Is there a floor?"

"No."

"Then raise the car."

"Wait."

He turned on the exterior lamps. Their beams passed downward,
but revealed no surface. They only made the emptiness more
visible.

A thought occurred to him. Perhaps the chamber was filled
with a transparent liquid of great density. This would account
for the projectile's support and perhaps for the peculiar illumination.
He watched for bubbles against the windows and examined the
pressure gauge attached to an external tube. There was no
indication of liquid pressure.

"Lower me until the car is level with the projectile,"
he ordered.

Bryce objected, but Clewe repeated the command, and the descent
continued. The damaged shell seemed to rise beside him. At
last the bottom of the car touched something with a
faint but unmistakable jar.

Clewe sprang to the indicator. The cable had slackened slightly.

"I am supported," he said.

"By what?"

"I cannot see."

"Do not leave the car."

Clewe made no answer.

He lowered the small steel sounding rod through a valve
in the floor. It descended six inches and stopped. He
withdrew it, examined its point, and found no moisture or
adhering substance. Next he dropped a steel nut through the
same opening. It fell a few inches and lay there,
plainly visible, but apparently suspended in air.

Then Clewe understood that the invisible support was solid.

The discovery, instead of satisfying him, increased his desire for
direct examination. He put on thick boots, buckled a rope
about his waist, and fastened its other end to the
interior ring.

"Roland," said Bryce, hearing the door clamps move, "what are
you doing?"

"I am going out."

"You are not."

"I must determine the nature of the substance."

"You can bring up a fragment with the drill."

"I must also examine the projectile."

There was a silence, and then Bryce spoke with restrained
anger.

"If you leave that car, I shall order the windlasses
reversed."

"You will do nothing until I tell you."

Clewe opened the door.

No gas rushed in, no liquid entered, and no pressure
changed. The luminous region outside appeared cool and perfectly still.
He extended one hand and felt nothing. Then he put
out one foot.

The boot encountered a firm surface six inches below the
sill.

He pressed upon it cautiously. It was as hard as
stone. Although his eyes declared that he was stepping into
space, his foot assured him that he stood upon a
floor.

He transferred his weight, brought out the other foot, and
remained beside the car.

The sensation was intolerably strange. Beneath him, visible with dreadful
distinctness, lay a depth which seemed without limit. He could
see the nut he had dropped, resting upon the invisible
surface; below it the radiance continued, unbroken, pale, and measureless.
His shadow did not lie at his feet. It passed
downward as a faint, elongated obscurity and was lost in
the brilliant abyss.

Clewe knelt and laid both hands upon the floor.

It was smooth, cold, and absolutely hard. He moved his
palms over it and encountered minute ridges, though none could
be seen. When he tapped it with a hammer, the
blow gave out a thin, musical sound which travelled away
in delicate repetitions.

He crawled toward the projectile.

Its appearance became explicable as he approached. The shell was
not suspended; it lay upon the same transparent substance that
supported him. Fragments of steel, bolts, and pieces of mechanism
were scattered around it, all seeming to float at various
heights, though his fingers found them upon one common plane.

The projectile's nose had penetrated several feet into the invisible
floor. Around the point were fractures which could be felt
but only faintly seen, for each crack diverted the general
radiance and shone like a thread of fire. A mass
had been splintered loose and lay partly beneath the broken
shell.

Clewe struck this mass with his hammer. Several fragments separated.
He picked up one as large as a hen's egg.

It was transparent.

Its edges refracted the surrounding light into spectral colours—blue, crimson,
yellow, and violet. He held it before him, turned it,
breathed upon it, and then gripped it so tightly that
its edges cut his glove.

A suspicion so immense that he did not dare express
it entered his mind.

"Have you reached the shell?" asked Bryce.

"Yes."

"What supports it?"

"A transparent mineral."

"Can you identify it?"

"Not yet."

This was untrue. Clewe had already formed his belief, but
he would not pronounce it until ordinary tests had confirmed
what his eyes and fingers reported.

He placed several fragments in his leather specimen pouch. Then,
forgetting caution in the intensity of his curiosity, he moved
beyond the projectile.

At each step he first slid one foot forward, feeling
for the floor. It remained beneath him; yet because he
could not see it, he began to imagine that its
level was changing. He stopped and returned to the shell,
measuring by the position of the scattered metal. The floor
was not level. It sloped gently away.

Toward what?

He looked down.

Nothing interrupted the light. There was no cloud, no distant
rock, no darkened limit. The transparent substance might be ten
feet thick or ten thousand miles. Its surface, descending from
the projectile, appeared to lead him outward over a region
of unreality where the senses contradicted one another and reason
obtained no foothold.

He advanced again.

The slope increased. His boots held firmly, but he bent
forward and touched the surface with one hand. The gesture
reassured him for an instant. It was solid. It was
merely a smooth mineral plane.

Then his right foot slipped.

He moved no more than a few inches, but an
awful terror shot into his soul. He flung himself flat
and clutched at a floor which his eyes denied existed.
Beneath his face opened the illuminated depth; beneath his outspread
hands there was apparently nothing; and the slow sliding of
one knee told him that the slope continued downward.

He shut his eyes.

At once the situation became less dreadful. Touch declared that
he lay upon cold stone. But when he opened his
eyes again, he seemed to be falling—falling face downward through
an endless world of light, while the car, the shell,
and the mouth of the shaft receded above him.

"Bryce!" he cried.

The telephone was in the car, but a speaking wire
had been attached to his belt.

"I hear you! What has happened?"

"The surface slopes. I slipped."

"Come back immediately."

Clewe tried to rise, but the mere sight of the
abyss deprived his legs of strength.

"I cannot stand."

"Crawl. Keep your eyes upon the projectile."

This practical command saved him. He turned his head and
fixed his gaze upon the twisted steel shell. It was
an ugly, familiar thing, made by men in workshops, and
the sight of rivets and plates restored the authority of
the ordinary world.

Slowly he crawled upward. His hands found tiny inequalities. Once
a fragment in his pouch pressed painfully against his side,
and the pain pleased him because it was definite and
comprehensible. At last he reached the projectile, then the car.

He climbed inside and shut the door.

For some minutes he sat upon the floor, unable to
speak. Sweat ran from his forehead, although the air was
cool. His hands trembled so violently that he could not
unfasten the rope.

"Are you in the car?" asked Bryce.

"Yes."

"Door secured?"

"Yes."

"I am raising you."

The cable tightened. The car lifted from its invisible foundation
and moved upward toward the black aperture.

Clewe watched the broken projectile sink below him. It diminished
slowly, retaining for a long time the appearance of a
wreck floating upon luminous air; then it became a dark
mark, and at last disappeared in the universal brilliance.

The rocky walls closed around the car.

After that the return was without incident. Up, up he
went through the dark strata, through the regions of metallic
lustre, past the seams of water and the bands of
granite. Bryce spoke to him frequently, but Clewe's replies were
brief. He drank brandy, ate a little food, and tried
unsuccessfully to smoke.

Thirteen hours after his departure, the car rose into the
western shed.

When the door was opened, Clewe stepped out between Bryce
and two workmen who had been summoned to assist. His
face was grey, and he walked unsteadily, but he would
not permit them to carry him.

Margaret stood near the entrance.

She had learned of the expedition only after it began,
and the composure with which she waited was belied by
the pressure of her clasped hands. When she saw him,
she uttered no cry. She came forward and looked directly
into his face.

"You have returned," she said.

"Yes."

"That is enough for the present."

Clewe rested for several hours. Late in the evening, after
the workmen had gone and Bryce had locked the engine-house,
he asked Margaret to join him in the laboratory.

Upon a table beneath a shaded lamp lay the fragments
he had brought from the depths. They had been washed
and roughly sorted. Even in their uncut state they filled
the room with scattered sparks of colour.

Margaret stopped at the table.

"What are these?"

Clewe handed her the largest fragment.

She examined it, then looked at him incredulously.

"Crystal?"

"Carbon."

"You mean—"

"Diamond."

She almost dropped it.

"That stone? That enormous stone?"

"It is not a stone in the jeweller's sense. It
is a broken splinter. I have tested its hardness, its
refractive power, its specific gravity, and its behaviour under heat.
There can be no reasonable doubt."

Margaret turned toward the other fragments.

"And all these?"

"Diamonds."

"But where did you find them?"

"The projectile struck them loose from the floor."

"What floor?"

"The floor at the bottom of the shaft."

She was silent for a moment, seeking to reconcile his
words.

"Then there is a bed of diamond fourteen miles beneath
us?"

Clewe shook his head.

"Not a bed. I do not believe we have reached
a layer deposited among other strata. The extent, the transparency,
the character of the fractures, and the manner in which
the shell is supported all point to something far greater."

"How great?"

He drew a chair toward the table but did not
sit.

"Margaret, I believe the centre of the earth is diamond."

She stared at him.

"The centre?"

"An immense nucleus of crystallised carbon. Perhaps it is hundreds
of miles in diameter; perhaps thousands. We struck its outer
surface."

"That is impossible."

"Why?"

"Because the centre of the earth is molten."

"So we have been taught. Yet our direct observations are
few. The temperature in the shaft did not continue to
rise. Near the end it fell. What if the earth
was not formed as commonly supposed?"

He took a sheet of paper and drew a circle.

"Imagine a comet travelling for ages through space. Its nucleus
contains carbon in enormous quantity, subjected to pressures and conditions
we cannot reproduce. Around that nucleus gather gases, vapours, metals,
and mineral particles. The comet approaches the sun. Its outer
portions burn, combine, and are altered. It passes through flaming
catastrophes; it captures matter; it loses matter. At last its
course changes, and it becomes a planet."

"And the burned exterior?"

"Forms layer after layer around the nucleus—ash, rock, metal, and
all the substances of the crust. The core remains: a
globe of diamond enclosed within the earth."

Margaret looked again at the glittering fragments.

"But the light?"

"I cannot fully explain it. It may be electrical; it
may be due to pressures within the crystal; it may
be sunlight or terrestrial heat transformed and conducted through the
whole mass. Diamond transmits light with extraordinary power. Within so
vast a body, every distant disturbance might contribute to that
universal radiance."

"And you stood upon it?"

"Yes."

"You walked upon the diamond centre of the earth?"

"For a short distance."

He turned away. The recollection had changed his voice.

Margaret perceived it.

"What happened there?"

Clewe did not answer immediately. Then he told her of
the invisible slope, of his slipping foot, and of the
immeasurable luminous depth beneath his face. He described how reason
had assured him of solid support while every visual instinct
declared that he was falling into emptiness.

She came nearer.

"You will not go down again."

"No."

"But if further examination should be required—"

"No."

"If instruments were prepared, and Bryce accompanied you—"

"No."

The word was abrupt. He pressed both hands upon the
table.

"Never, never, never again."

Margaret said nothing. Presently she touched one of the smaller
fragments.

"These alone must be worth a fortune."

"According to present standards, yes. That piece in your hand
might buy this establishment. The largest, if properly cut, would
be spoken of throughout the world."

"Then your work need never again depend upon financiers."

Clewe gave a short, humourless laugh.

"If I brought up a few fragments, I should become
rich. If I brought up wagon-loads, diamonds would become cheap.
If I opened the shaft to commerce, the substance would
cease to be precious."

"But it would remain useful."

"Assuredly. It would transform industry. Cutting tools, bearings, electrical instruments,
optical devices—there is no end to its employment. Yet consider
the disturbance. Fortunes, governments, mines, and whole communities rest upon
the scarcity of this mineral. Announce that a mass larger
than a continent lies beneath our feet, and every diamond
in every treasury becomes doubtful property."

"Will you keep the discovery secret?"

"For the present."

"And the fragments?"

"I shall preserve enough for proof. The rest must be
secured."

He gathered them into a steel box. Their coloured fires
vanished one by one as he closed the lid.

Outside, beyond the laboratory windows, the night was cool and
dark. A lamp burned in the engine-house, and from the
garden came the faint odour of crushed tulips where a
delivery cart had passed that morning. The familiar details of
the surface world seemed to Clewe more wonderful than they
had ever seemed before: soil which concealed what lay beneath
it, trees rooted in opaque earth, and darkness mercifully limiting
the range of human sight.

Far below, his ruined projectile rested upon the transparent curve
of an immeasurable crystal. No darkness surrounded it. Through the
solid substance the mysterious light extended downward, downward, without visible
bound.

Clewe put the key of the steel box into his
pocket.

Then, with Margaret beside him, he went out and locked
the western shed.

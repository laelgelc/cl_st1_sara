The Race on Clearwater Lake

"Every man ready?"

Professor Brice stood beside a black line drawn across the
smooth ice of Clearwater Lake. Before him were twelve cadets
of Colby Hall Military Academy, each bending forward a little,
with eyes fixed upon the distant western shore.

"Ready!" came the answer.

"Remember, you are to skate to the blasted pine, circle
it, and return to this line," continued the professor. "The
first man home wins the silver cup. The second receives
the new pair of hockey skates. And let every fellow
skate fair and square."

"We'll do that, Professor!" cried Jack Rover.

"No cutting across at the pine!" added Dan Soppinger.

"Whoever cuts across ought to be made to skate the
race over backward," put in Andy Rover.

"That would suit you," returned his twin, Randy. "You're always
doing things backward."

A laugh arose from the contestants and from the crowd
gathered behind the starting line.

It was a clear winter afternoon, and the broad surface
of Clearwater Lake shone beneath the pale sun like a
sheet of polished steel. A light wind swept down from
the hills, but the air was not bitterly cold. For
more than a week the cadets had practiced for the
great skating race, and nearly everybody connected with Colby Hall
had come out to witness the event.

In addition, a party of girls from Clearwater Hall Seminary
stood near the finish. Among them were Ruth Stevenson, a
tall and attractive miss in a blue skating costume, and
May Powell, a lively girl whose bright red scarf could
be seen at a considerable distance.

"Do you think Jack can win?" May asked Ruth.

"I hope so," answered Ruth. "But Dan Soppinger and Walt
Baxter are both splendid skaters."

"Don't forget Randy Rover," said another girl. "He came within
a yard of beating Jack yesterday."

Near the girls stood Fred Rover, the youngest of the
four cousins. He had entered the race, although he did
not expect to win.

"I'm on the job, too," he announced. "Maybe I'll surprise
the whole crowd."

"You'll surprise yourself if you keep on your feet," called
Andy.

"Save your breath for the race," advised Jack, with a
smile.

Jack Rover had won several athletic contests at Colby Hall,
but he knew that the present race would test him
to the utmost. The course was nearly three miles in
all, and some of the cadets lined up beside him
had both speed and endurance.

At the right stood Dan Soppinger, long-legged and confident. Next
to him was Walt Baxter, a powerful youth who could
make tremendous strokes. Randy Rover occupied the position beyond Walt,
while Andy, Fred, Ned Lowe, Bart White, and the others
filled out the line.

Professor Brice raised a small pistol.

"Look out!" cried somebody in the crowd.

Crack!

The weapon spoke, and the twelve cadets sprang forward.

For a few seconds there was a wild striking of
skates and a great swinging of arms. Then the contestants
spread out, each seeking a clear path.

"Go it, Jack!"

"Randy, don't let them get away from you!"

"Dan is ahead!"

"No, Walt has him!"

"Come on, Fred!" cried May. "You're not beaten yet!"

"I know it!" Fred shouted back, although he was already
several yards behind the leaders.

Dan Soppinger was the first to obtain a decided advantage.
He bent low and drove ahead with long, steady strokes.
Walt Baxter followed close behind, while Randy held third place.
Jack was fourth, with Andy at his shoulder.

"Why don't you move up?" Andy asked as they skated
side by side.

"Plenty of time," answered Jack. "Don't wear yourself out at
the start."

"That's good advice. I wish my legs would listen to
it."

The blasted pine stood on a rocky point at the
western end of the lake. It was more than a
mile away, and at first it appeared no larger than
a black post. The racers swept toward it, while a
number of spectators followed at a respectful distance.

Among these was Fatty Hendry, the heaviest cadet at Colby
Hall. Fatty had wanted to enter the contest, but his
friends had persuaded him otherwise.

"If you had raced, the lake might have tilted," Andy
had told him.

Now Fatty was skating after the field, puffing loudly.

"Wait for me!" he called.

"This isn't a picnic party!" Fred yelled over his shoulder.

The contestants soon separated into three groups. Dan, Walt, Randy,
Jack, and Andy formed the first. Four more cadets came
fifty yards behind them, while Fred and two others brought
up the rear.

"Dan is setting a hot pace," remarked Randy.

"Too hot," returned Jack. "He can't keep it up all
the way back."

Dan heard this and glanced over his shoulder.

"Don't worry about me, Rover!" he called. "I've got speed
to spare!"

"We'll see at the finish," answered Jack cheerfully.

Closer and closer loomed the blasted pine. The leading five
were now skating for all they were worth, with the
exception of Jack, who still held something in reserve.

Walt Baxter drew up beside Dan.

"I'll circle first!" he cried.

"Not if I can help it!" returned Dan.

Both made a tremendous effort. Their skates rang upon the
hard surface, and bits of ice flew from beneath the
flashing blades. Walt gained half a yard, lost it, and
then gained it once more.

"Baxter leads!"

"No, they're even!"

"Watch Randy!" shouted Fred from far in the rear. "He's
crawling up!"

Randy had indeed forged forward, but in doing so he
was compelled to swing far to the left. Jack remained
directly behind Dan, watching for an opening.

Walt reached the pine first, with Dan no more than
an arm's length away. Randy was third, Jack fourth, and
Andy fifth.

The turn was a sharp one. Walt went too wide,
and Dan slipped past him.

"Here's where I leave you!" exclaimed Dan.

"You haven't left me yet!" cried Walt.

Randy made the turn neatly. Jack did the same, while
Andy skidded, waved both arms, and came within an inch
of sitting down upon the ice.

"Whoa there!" he ejaculated. "That pine tried to trip me!"

"Better luck next time!" shouted Bart White, who was approaching
the turn.

On the homeward stretch the light wind blew against the
racers. Dan still led, with Randy, Walt, and Jack close
behind him. Andy had lost several yards because of his
bad turn.

For a time no one spoke. The breathing of the
cadets grew louder, and their strokes became more deliberate. The
finish line could not yet be seen, but the groups
of spectators on the eastern shore showed as dark specks
in the distance.

Dan increased his lead to five yards.

"It's Soppinger's race!" called one of the following skaters.

"Don't announce it too soon," advised Fred.

Jack watched Dan steadily. The leader's strokes were long, but
they were no longer even. Once his right skate slipped
sideways, and a moment later he looked back anxiously.

"Now he's feeling it," thought Jack.

Randy moved nearer to Dan. Walt tried to follow, but
his heavy strokes had cost him dearly. Jack passed Walt
without seeming to increase his pace.

"Going ahead at last?" panted Walt.

"Just beginning," answered Jack.

Half the return distance had now been covered. The crowd
at the finish could distinguish the leaders, and the cheering
began.

"Here they come!"

"Dan is ahead!"

"Randy is second!"

"Where's Jack Rover?"

"There he is—right behind Randy!"

Ruth Stevenson shaded her eyes with one mitten.

"Jack is not skating as hard as the others," she
said.

"Perhaps he can't," answered May.

"Perhaps he is waiting."

That was precisely what Jack was doing.

Dan still held first place, but Randy had drawn almost
even with him. Jack remained three yards behind, while Walt
came at his heels. Andy was farther back, fighting stubbornly
to regain the ground he had lost.

"Come on, Jack!" cried Andy. "No use in talking—you've got
to move!"

Jack looked toward the finish. Less than half a mile
remained.

He bent lower.

At once the pace changed.

With a powerful drive he swept past Walt and came
up to Randy. For a dozen strokes the two cousins
raced side by side.

"Go on!" gasped Randy. "I'll follow!"

Jack nodded and forged forward.

Dan heard the ringing blades behind him and made another
effort. For several seconds he held his lead. Then Jack
came closer and closer.

"Jack Rover is gaining!" yelled a cadet near the line.

"It's Dan's race!"

"No, Jack has him!"

"Randy is coming, too!"

The crowd pressed out upon the lake in order to
obtain a better view. Professor Brice waved them back.

"Keep behind the line!" he ordered. "Give the racers room!"

Jack was now at Dan's shoulder.

"Some race!" panted Dan.

"You've made it one," returned Jack.

Dan set his teeth and increased his stroke. Jack answered
at once. The two shot over the ice like arrows,
with Randy only a short distance behind.

One hundred yards remained.

"Dan and Jack are even!"

"Randy is third!"

"Walt is coming up!"

Jack had saved his strength for this very moment. He
straightened slightly, swung his arms, and put every ounce of
power into the final sprint.

For a few seconds Dan held him. Then Jack gained
half a yard.

"Jack Rover leads!"

The cheering became tremendous.

"Go it, Jack!"

"Come on, Randy!"

"Dan, don't give up!"

Fifty yards—forty—thirty!

Dan made a desperate spurt, but his earlier efforts had
exhausted him. Randy swept alongside and passed him.

Jack flashed across the black line amid a perfect storm
of cheers. Randy followed three seconds later. Then Dan Soppinger
and Walt Baxter struck the line together.

"Jack wins!"

"Randy is second!"

"Who took third?"

"It was Dan!"

"No, Walt beat him!"

Professor Brice raised both hands.

"Stand back, everybody! The judges will give the decision."

Jack coasted in a wide circle and returned, breathing hard
but smiling. Randy caught his arm.

"You saved enough for the finish, all right," said Randy.

"I needed every bit of it," Jack replied. "You nearly
caught me."

"Nearly doesn't win a silver cup."

Dan and Walt came up, both plainly disappointed.

"I thought I had you halfway home," admitted Dan.

"So did I," answered Jack. "You set the pace."

"I used too much steam going out," said Dan ruefully.
"But you won fair and square."

Professor Brice consulted his watch and then spoke to the
other judges.

"You have broken the academy record by eleven seconds, Rover,"
he announced.

This news brought another cheer.

"Speech! Speech!" called Fred, who had at last reached the
finish.

"I haven't breath enough," replied Jack.

"Then let Andy make it for you," suggested Randy.

Andy came skating in a moment later.

"I'll make a speech after somebody brings me a chair,
a hot dinner, and two new legs," he declared.

The judges decided that Dan and Walt had crossed the
line at the same instant, so the two were awarded
equal honors for third place. Both accepted the decision without
complaint.

Fred finished ninth.

"I might have done better," he said, "but I stopped
to admire the scenery."

"The scenery was moving faster than you were," returned Andy.

Fatty Hendry now arrived, his round face crimson from exertion.

"Who won?" he demanded.

"The race has been over for a week," said Andy.

"You fellows didn't wait."

"We thought the lake might wear out."

Fatty made a playful rush at him, and Andy darted
behind Professor Brice. The crowd laughed, and for several minutes
all was noise and good humor.

More skaters came from the shores. Boys and girls gathered
around Jack and Randy, while others examined Professor Brice's watch
and discussed the record. Fully two hundred persons were now
standing in a comparatively small space.

"Congratulations, Jack," said Ruth Stevenson.

"Thank you, Ruth."

"I knew you were holding back," she added.

"I didn't know it," put in May. "I thought Dan
would win."

"So did Dan," remarked Andy.

"Not after Jack passed me," returned Dan with a grin.

Some distance away, Fatty Hendry was showing two younger cadets
how he could cut a figure eight. His first loop
was very broad, his second crooked, and his third ended
in a tumble.

Down he came with a thump that made everyone nearby
laugh.

But the laughter ceased almost instantly.

Cr-r-rack!

A sharp report ran beneath the ice.

"What was that?" demanded May.

"Only Fatty landing," said one cadet.

Crack! Crack!

This time the sounds came from two directions, and a
thin black line shot across the smooth surface.

Professor Brice's face changed.

"Move toward shore!" he ordered. "Do not run! Spread out
and move quietly!"

For one second the crowd remained still. Then somebody cried:

"The ice is breaking!"

A panic followed.

Boys and girls pushed toward the eastern shore. Several attempted
to skate, collided with others, and fell. The black cracks
lengthened with alarming speed.

"Don't bunch together!" shouted Professor Brice. "Spread out!"

"Give me room!"

"Look out!"

"Don't push!"

Jack caught a small boy who had fallen and set
him upon his feet. Randy dragged another skater away from
a widening crack.

The lake gave a low, grinding groan.

Near the finish line the ice sagged under the great
weight. Water bubbled through a narrow split and spread over
the surface.

"Everybody down!" yelled Walt Baxter. "Crawl if you have to!"

Many obeyed, but others continued toward shore with wild strokes.
Fatty, thoroughly frightened, went down on his hands and knees
and crawled for all he was worth.

Then came a tremendous report.

A section of ice twenty feet across sank several inches.

Ruth Stevenson and May Powell, who had been helping a
younger girl, were caught near the center of it. The
child scrambled to firm ice, but Ruth slipped and fell.
May turned to aid her and went down beside her.

"Ruth!" cried Jack.

The sinking section tilted. Water rushed over its edge and
swirled around the girls' skates.

"Don't stand up!" Jack shouted. "Lie flat!"

Ruth obeyed at once and pulled May down beside her.
But the broken sheet continued to sink, while cracks spread
around it like the spokes of a wheel.

"They can't stay there!" exclaimed Fred.

"We need a rope," said Randy.

"There's one at the boathouse, but that's too far away,"
returned Andy.

Professor Brice was driving the rest of the crowd toward
safety. He turned, saw the two girls, and called for
everyone to keep back.

Another crack sounded, this one directly between the girls and
the shore.

May gave a frightened cry.

"The water is rising!"

Jack threw himself flat and began to crawl toward the
broken section.

"Jack, you can't go alone!" cried Randy.

"I'm going with him," announced Andy.

"So am I," added Fred.

The four cousins stretched out upon the ice, each keeping
several feet from the others. Slowly but steadily they crawled
toward Ruth and May.

Behind them the crowd watched in breathless silence.

Ahead, the dark water climbed higher around the stranded girls.

And then, with a grinding crash, the ice beneath Jack's
outstretched hands began to split apart.

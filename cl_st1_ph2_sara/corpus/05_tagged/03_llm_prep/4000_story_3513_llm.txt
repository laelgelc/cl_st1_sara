## THE BLACK HAND

When I was a little boy—which occurred, if the parish
register may be trusted, about a hundred and fifty years
ago, though I shall be only a hundred and forty-nine
next Michaelmas—there reigned over our school a certain Mr. Birchmore,
a long, dry gentleman, who smelt of Russia leather, sealing-wax,
and justice. His wig was the colour of old porridge;
his cane was the colour of new honey; and between
the two he maintained order among forty-three boys, every one
of whom, he frequently declared, was born for the gallows.

One November morning a silver pencil-case disappeared from the desk
of young Augustus Blenkinsop. It was not a handsome pencil-case,
mind you. It had a blue stone in the top,
which Augustus called a sapphire, though I have since seen
better jewels in the eyes of boiled codfish. Nevertheless, property
is property; and when the case could not be found
in pockets, desks, boots, inkpots, or the hollow of Plunkett
Minor's Latin dictionary, Mr. Birchmore locked the schoolroom door and
announced that the thief must be discovered.

We trembled. Innocence trembled as violently as guilt; indeed, innocence
commonly trembles more, having had less practice.

Mr. Birchmore then produced a canvas sack, into which the
school porter had rubbed a quantity of soot. He placed
it behind a curtain, and explained his ingenious engine of
detection.

"Each boy," said he, "will pass behind the curtain and
thrust his right hand into this sack. The innocent will
obey. The guilty, fearing that the soot possesses a certain
chemical virtue by which theft is betrayed, will merely pretend
to do so. Thus every honest hand will emerge black,
and the dishonest hand white."

We regarded the sack with horror. Chemistry was then a
youthful science and capable, for anything we knew, of denouncing
a criminal in Greek hexameters. One by one we passed
behind the curtain. I plunged my hand in up to
the elbow, for I had not stolen the pencil-case, though
I had stolen three damsons from Mrs. Birchmore's tart two
days before, and supposed the soot might be retrospective. When
I emerged, black as an Ethiop chimney-sweep, the Doctor nodded.
Blenkinsop followed; then Crowdy, Mealey, the two Puggs, Smith, Jones,
another Smith, and all the Christian army.

Every hand was black.

The guilty boy—whoever the young rascal may have been—had either
misunderstood the arrangement or possessed more courage than Mr. Birchmore
allowed him. We stood there with forty-three sable paws, looking
like a parliament of imps voting unanimously for mischief; and
the pencil-case was never recovered. Augustus subsequently confessed that the
blue sapphire had come off a sugar-box, which reduced our
sympathy with his loss, though not Mr. Birchmore's appetite for
inquiry.

I have often thought since, benevolent friend and reader, how
fortunate it is that the great schoolmaster of Society keeps
no infallible sack behind his curtain. Fancy us all commanded,
at the ringing of a bell, to put in our
hands—YOU first, if you please; no hanging back, madam; no
wiping the fingers upon the coat-tails, sir—and fancy the bag
possessing the power, not merely to mark theft, but envy,
cowardice, falsehood, vanity, secret spite, private appetite, public humbug, and
all the little smudges which we call venial because they
happen to be ours!

What a company would come forth clean?

Clean, did I say? Psha! We should be black to
the eyebrows. The very beadle who held the sack would
be discovered pilfering the soot.

Consider the confusion if every schoolboy were found out. Young
Tibbins, who reports that Dobbs copied his exercise, would be
discovered to have copied his own from an elder brother's
book. Dobbs, pleading injured innocence, would be found to have
hidden the master's spectacles. Puggs Major, who never tells a
lie, would be convicted of seven before breakfast—one to his
mother concerning the jam, two to the housemaid concerning the
broken window, three to himself concerning his ability at cricket,
and one general magnificent falsehood about the injustice of the
world.

Then the masters must take their turn. Dr. Busbygrave, who
lectures upon Temperance, would be found out respecting the locked
cupboard in his study. Mr. Mealymouth, the mild assistant who
abhors cruelty, would be discovered to pinch boys where the
bruise does not show. And Mr. Birchmore himself—ah, venerable shade!—might
be called upon to explain why Blenkinsop's sapphire pencil-case was
seen, twenty years later, serving as a holder for his
widow's bodkin.

But we must not stop at school. Ring the bell!
Admit the town!

Here comes the Reverend Silas Smoothly, preaching Humility in a
silk gown, with a voice like melted butter poured over
warm toast. He tells us that rank, applause, wealth, and
worldly consideration are dust. Quite right, my dear brethren—dust they
are; but observe how quickly our pastor brushes that dust
from another man's chair before sitting in it himself! He
has forgiven his enemy Crump seven times, and announces each
forgiveness in the parish newspaper. The eighth time Crump is
late in paying pew rent, and Christian charity expires at
half-past eleven precisely.

Would you have Smoothly found out? Suppose, in the middle
of his discourse, the pulpit became transparent and displayed every
thought passing beneath his white neckcloth: his satisfaction at Lady
Goldmore's attendance; his resentment that the bishop has not answered;
his private estimate of the collection; his wonder whether his
sermon on Poverty may not be printed by subscription. The
congregation would laugh—until the pews, growing transparent in their turn,
revealed that Lady Goldmore was thinking of her new bonnet,
Crump of Smoothly's unpaid butcher, Mrs. Crump of Lady Goldmore's
complexion, and the clerk of sixpence he had abstracted from
the plate.

No, no. Let the pulpit remain mahogany and the pews
decent green baize. We cannot conduct divine service coram populo
if souls are to be turned inside out like pockets
at a police office.

Next arrives the Right Reverend Bishop of Botherham, carrying benedictions
in one hand and preferments in the other. He smiles
upon young Mr. Pliable and gives him a living. Why
Pliable? Because of his scholarship? His piety? His eloquence? Certainly,
certainly—and because Pliable's uncle has three votes in the House.
Shall we expose the arrangement? My man, if every mitre
were lifted and every transaction underneath examined, there would be
such a scuttling among lawn sleeves as was never seen
even when the wine runs short at a clerical dinner.

After the Church comes the State, puffing, bowing, and declaring
its conscience immaculate.

Sir Orlando Weathercock has represented East Wobble for thirty years.
On Monday he supports the Corn Bill because the nation
demands it. On Tuesday the ministry changes, and on Wednesday
the nation—providentially enlightened during the night—demands the opposite. "Consistency," says
Sir Orlando, "is the vice of little minds." He is
cheered by gentlemen whose own minds have lately expanded in
the direction of office.

Found out? Bless you, he is found out by everybody
except those who profit by not finding him out. The
newspaper calls his movement "a statesmanlike development." His cousin obtains
a commission, his nephew a secretaryship, his publisher a contract
for stationery, and the patriotic conscience of East Wobble is
soothed with a new bridge which begins nowhere and ends
in Sir Orlando's estate.

You cry "Shame!" from your armchair. Softly, Cato. Did you
not yesterday praise the opinions of old Tallow because he
gave you a good dinner? Did you not laugh at
Flimsby's intolerable story because Flimsby's brother may lend you fifty
pounds? Put your hand into the sack before you call
for Sir Orlando's.

Now the ladies enter—and here I advance with reverence, terror,
and my hat in my hand. Woman, lovely Woman, has
no secrets. She is candour itself. She never opens a
letter addressed to another; she merely observes, by accident, what
is written while carrying it to the proper person. She
never listens at a door; she pauses in the passage
from concern lest the speakers within should require assistance. She
feels no curiosity concerning the locked drawer in her husband's
desk, though she has tried her own key, the store-room
key, a hairpin, and the little instrument attached to her
scissors.

My excellent friend Mrs. Longbow knows nothing of scandal and
repeats it only to refute it. "I do not believe
one syllable," she says, and proceeds to pronounce the syllables
distinctly for the benefit of twelve acquaintances. Mrs. Painter despises
artificial beauty, having retained her own through a judicious friendship
with rouge, candlelight, and distant mirrors. Diana Hunter has refused
every man in the county; some before they proposed, others
before they were introduced, and several before they were born.

Would you strip away these amiable veils? Would you compel
Mrs. Longbow to admit she enjoys the scandal, Mrs. Painter
to wash her face coram populo, and Diana to confess
that Captain Bounce never entertained the smallest idea of marrying
her? Monster! Leave them their innocent decorations. A truth may
be as indecent as a naked man in the marketplace,
and half as useful.

Men, meanwhile, decorate themselves with virtues they have never possessed.
Colonel Thunder tells how he held a bridge against four
thousand Afghans with one pistol and a lame pony. At
the mere recollection his moustaches bristle like bayonets. Yet I
knew the Colonel when a goose flew at him on
Barnes Common, and his retreat was conducted with a rapidity
which, had military judges witnessed it, must have secured his
promotion.

He was found out by little Miss Kitty Grigg.

"Colonel," said she at dinner, "was the Afghan army as
terrible as Aunt's gander?"

The hero dropped his spoon. His wife coughed. I looked
into the fire. The goose, though absent, won the engagement.

But why should we despise Thunder? His courage, like many
fortunes, existed chiefly on paper; still, it entertained us. A
real hero may save a bridge once in his life,
whereas Thunder saved one every evening after soup. If a
nation can live by credit, may not a Colonel?

Sometimes the guilty betray themselves without sack, soot, magistrate, or
Miss Grigg. I remember a certain Mr. Baggs who, when
a silver spoon vanished after supper, exclaimed before any accusation
had been made, "Upon my honour, I never went near
the pantry!"

Nobody had mentioned the pantry.

Baggs perceived his error and added, "Nor do I know
that the missing object is a spoon."

Nobody had mentioned a spoon.

"Besides," he continued, turning scarlet, "it is only plated."

It was solid silver. We recovered it the next morning
from his left boot. Baggs had put it there, he
explained, to prevent somebody else from stealing it. His benevolence
was imperfectly appreciated.

Murderers, too, are occasionally moved by this helpful desire to
assist justice. They return to the scene, correct the newspapers,
supply details known only to the criminal, and complain that
the police have misrepresented the position of the body. One
wonders that any murderer escapes. Yet many do; and I
could, if I chose, explain a method by which a
person might commit a perfectly undetectable homicide using no weapon,
poison, accomplice, or visible violence.

But I shall NOT.

The editor has subscribers with rich uncles. A moral essay
should not diminish circulation by increasing mortality. Let us turn
to safer criminals—the literary kind.

My old acquaintance Sacks produced twelve volumes upon the ruins
of Nineveh without having visited any ruin more remote than
an unfinished terrace in Camden Town. He borrowed his facts
from Baggs, his drawings from a French atlas, his style
from three deceased bishops, and his enthusiasm from a publisher's
advance. The work was praised as "the record of a
fearless eyewitness" by Dr. Badford, who had not read it,
in a journal partly owned by Sacks's brother-in-law.

I mentioned a trifling error: Sacks had placed the Euphrates
in Egypt.

"My dear fellow," said he, "that is the new theory."

Badford afterwards called the error "a daring geographical insight." A
professor disputed it; Sacks gave the professor a dinner; the
professor reconsidered; and within six months the Euphrates flowed through
Egypt in every fashionable drawing-room in London.

Do not laugh too loudly, young Scribbler. Is all the
learning in your review your own? Did you finish the
book you condemned last Thursday? No? You read the preface,
cut the pages at intervals, and quoted a sentence furnished
by the author's enemy. Yet you demanded sincerity in literature!
Into the sack, sir. Up to the shoulder.

Authors complain of critics, critics of authors, both of publishers,
and publishers—being men of business—complain only when the accounts are
examined. Each keeps the others' secret. The author is called
immortal; the critic, profound; the publisher, enterprising; and the public
buys the volume because everyone supposes everyone else has read
it.

Reputation, my dear brethren, is often not a statue but
a screen. Behind it the great man takes off his
boots and wonders how long the thing will stand.

I once knew a philosopher who preached the abolition of
property while carefully marking his umbrellas. There was a poet
of domestic affection who could not endure his children during
working hours, eating hours, sleeping hours, or leisure. There was
a physician who wrote upon longevity and died at thirty-eight
from refusing his own advice. There was a banker who
denounced gambling but speculated with widows' deposits; an actor who
despised applause and counted the claps; a hostess who invited
only intellect and seated it below money; and a reformer
who wished to regenerate mankind, beginning everywhere except at home.

Had they all been found out simultaneously, what a dies
irae! Philosophers, poets, bishops, aldermen, soldiers, beauties, editors, and schoolmasters
fleeing through the streets with Society behind them brandishing the
cane! Stocks in every square, confessionals at every corner, penitents
queuing from Westminster to Wapping! The executioner would demand assistants;
the birch forests of Europe would be exhausted; and, after
punishing the last detected sinner, the judges would be obliged
to sentence one another.

Who, then, would remain to eat dinner?

Absolute justice is a magnificent angel, no doubt, but I
should not care to have her lodge permanently in my
house. She would examine the wine bill, question the servants,
read my old letters, compare my conversation with my conduct,
and ask why I call myself an elderly invalid when
there is dancing. Mercy, on the other hand, is a
comfortable old aunt. She sees the stain upon the coat,
puts a flower over it, and says nothing.

Do not suppose I recommend villainy. Keep your hands from
other men's spoons, your pens from other men's paragraphs, and
your geographical theories within reasonable distance of their rivers. If
you have stolen the pencil-case, restore it. If you have
slandered Mrs. Longbow—though this may only be carrying coals to
Newcastle—repent. Say peccavi when necessary; but do not demand that
all your neighbours cry it through trumpets merely because you
whisper it into your pillow.

We live by a great treaty of partial blindness. I
do not look too closely at your vanity; you overlook
my temper. Your wife forgets that you once proposed to
her sister; you forget that she remembers. Jones accepts Smith's
courage, Smith accepts Jones's wisdom, and both accept my youth.
Thus the machine turns. Pull out every pin labelled Illusion,
Tact, Courtesy, Hope, and Convenient Memory, and see how soon
the wheels fall upon our toes.

At home, especially, let the merciful veil remain.

The husband enters after dinner and tells his story. His
wife has heard it fourteen times, knows the ending, and
remembers that it happened to his cousin rather than to
him; nevertheless she smiles at the proper place. Is that
deception? Yes—and one of the golden kinds. She asks whether
he is tired, though she knows he is cross; he
says her cap becomes her, though he has not observed
the cap. Their daughter plays the piano intolerably, and both
discover genius. Their son writes verses, and the mother sees
Milton; the father, who pays the printer, sees at least
a pamphlet.

The old grandmother in the corner believes them all handsome,
noble, clever, and good. Her eyes are dim, you say.
Perhaps. But there are lights visible to dim eyes which
sharp ones miss.

For what is affection but a lantern hung behind the
beloved, making a halo where an accountant would find only
ears? We do not weigh those dear to us with
grocer's scales. We add a little to courage, subtract from
selfishness, call obstinacy firmness, idleness delicacy, chatter vivacity, and wrinkles
character. They perform the same charitable arithmetic for us—Heaven reward
their bad mathematics!

And when, one day, we are nearly found out; when
the brave face slips, the wisdom fails, the temper shows,
the old lie peeps from its cupboard, and we stand
with our foolish white hand before the dreadful sack—then may
some kindly soul rub a little soot upon it, take
it gently, and say:

"See? Black like all the others. Let him pass."

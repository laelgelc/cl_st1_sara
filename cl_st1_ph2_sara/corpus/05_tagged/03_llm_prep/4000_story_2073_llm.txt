The blinds of Mercy Crane's front room had been tied
at the same careful height for so many years that
the green cords had worn pale where her fingers took
hold of them. From the road the house looked shut
and watchful, though there was always one narrow opening through
which Mercy could observe a team going toward the village,
a neighbor crossing the lower field, or the minister's wife
making calls with her best parasol inclined against the sun.

Mercy herself had not passed the gate since the winter
she slipped on the church steps and came home with
a strained hip and a firm opinion that Providence did
not require a woman of sixty-eight to expose herself unnecessarily.
She could walk from room to room, and from the
kitchen door to the currant bushes, but the road had
become foreign country. News reached her punctually, nevertheless. Persons who
never visited anybody stopped to see Mercy Crane. She had
a way of receiving an account with such grave attention,
and of remembering its smaller particulars so exactly, that the
teller went away feeling that nothing had been wasted.

On the last Thursday of August she sat by the
west window tatting a narrow edge for a pillowcase. The
afternoon was hot and still. A fly traveled in circles
over the polished table, and now and then struck the
glass globe of the lamp with a small, offended sound.
Mercy had just counted the loops in her work for
the third time when she saw Sarah Ellen Dow coming
up the road.

Sarah Ellen usually walked as if somebody were waiting for
medicine, but that day she came slowly. She carried a
brown bundle in one hand and an old black bonnet
in the other. Her gray hair was pushed back anyhow,
and the skirt of her dark calico dress had caught
burrs along the ditch. She stopped at the gate, looked
toward Mercy's window, and then appeared to collect herself before
entering.

Mercy dropped her tatting into the blue china saucer kept
for that purpose.

"Something's happened," she said aloud. There was no one to
hear her, but a solitary woman falls naturally into the
habit of announcing important conclusions to the furniture.

By the time Sarah Ellen reached the side door, Mercy
had crossed the kitchen and set the kettle nearer the
hot part of the stove. She opened the door before
the knock came.

"Why, Sarah Ellen!"

"Sister Barsett's gone," said Sarah Ellen.

She entered with the words and set her bundle down
on a chair. Then she stood holding the black bonnet
by its strings, as if it belonged to the dead
and had been placed in her charge.

Mercy's face changed at once. Curiosity came first, but sympathy
followed so close upon it that no one but Mercy
herself could have distinguished them.

"Gone?" she repeated. "You don't mean—"

"She passed about half past two. Or they said she
did. I wa'n't there at the very last."

Sarah Ellen's mouth trembled. She turned toward the window and
began picking burrs from her skirt with unsteady fingers.

"Set right down," said Mercy. "You look beat out. I'd
been thinkin' of her all day. There was a queer
stillness in the air this mornin', and I says to
myself, 'If Sister Barsett ain't worse, then I don't know.'
Set down, Sarah Ellen. I'll make a cup of tea."

"I don't know as I can stop."

"You can stop long enough to draw breath. Funeral matters
won't be furthered by your fallin' in the road."

That was reasonable, and Sarah Ellen yielded to reason when
it was spoken with sufficient authority. She lowered herself into
the wooden rocker by the kitchen window, but continued to
hold the bonnet.

Mercy looked at it.

"Whose is that?"

"Mine. I fetched it over Monday, account of thinkin' I
might have to go to the village for Dr. Prince.
Then Mis' Fales come with her horse, and I didn't
need it. I forgot all about the bonnet till they
told me I'd best clear my things out."

"They told you?"

"Her sisters. Dorcas and Jane."

Mercy opened the tea caddy with more force than was
needed.

"They came, then."

"They came this noon, when everything was about over. Dorcas
sailed in with her veil down, though nobody'd sent word
Sister Barsett was dead yet. Jane had a basket of
clean collars and a bottle of camphor. You'd have thought
they'd come to take possession of a meetinghouse."

Sarah Ellen's grief was warming into resentment, and Mercy, who
knew that resentment could support an exhausted person when tenderness
failed, let the kettle occupy her for a moment.

"I suppose they felt they had the right," she said
at last.

"The right! I don't dispute blood relationship. I never have.
But where was blood relationship last March, when Sister Barsett
had that turn with her lungs and wouldn't let anybody
but me touch the mustard paste? Where was Dorcas then?
She wrote a postal card. Said she hoped our dear
sister would put her trust in higher hands. Higher hands
wa'n't there to change the sheets."

Mercy brought out two cups with small purple grapes painted
around the rims. They were not her everyday cups. Death,
even at a neighboring house, required some ceremony.

"How long had you been there this time?"

"Eleven days and ten nights. I went home twice to
see if the roof leaked, and once to feed the
hens. Eliza Coombs' boy's been attendin' to 'em, but boys
scatter feed as if corn grew on doorstones. I took
my shawl and two aprons over to Sister Barsett's, and
there I've been. She had spells every night. I never
closed both eyes together."

"You look it," Mercy said kindly.

Sarah Ellen accepted the judgment as praise. She had been
useful, and usefulness was the one form of prosperity she
could always command.

"She wa'n't easy," she continued. "I won't say she was.
The dead ought to be spoke of with charity, but
truth's truth. At one o'clock this mornin' she wanted her
feet rubbed with goose grease. At two she said goose
grease stopped the pores and asked what possessed me. Then
she called for pennyroyal tea, and when I fetched it
she said the smell of it would kill a well
person. She'd read a piece in the Farmer's Companion about
the heart, and after that there wa'n't a feeling from
her chin to her ankles that she couldn't explain by
heart failure."

"Poor creature," said Mercy. "She always did know considerable about
diseases."

"She knew their names. That ain't precisely the same."

Mercy concealed the beginning of a smile by bending over
the stove. Sister Barsett had enjoyed infirmity with a thoroughness
denied to many who possessed better health. For twenty years
she had been expected to die before spring, before haying,
before the first frost, or at least before another Sabbath.
The seasons had passed with decent regularity, but Sister Barsett
remained to warn stronger neighbors against their habits.

"Was she conscious?" Mercy asked.

"Conscious! She noticed everything. Yesterday she told me I'd put
her clean nightcap on hind side before. It wa'n't, either.
She said the bow set nearer the left ear than
it did Sunday, and she'd know that cap if she
met it in the dark. Then she wanted the blue
quilt took off because it was too heavy, and the
white spread put on because Jane might come. Five minutes
later she wanted the blue quilt back, account of the
white spread showin' every speck."

"Did Jane come in time to see it?"

"She come in time to criticise the fringe. Said she'd
always told sister that knotted fringe caught dust. Sister Barsett
couldn't answer by then, but she looked at her. I
knew what that look meant."

Mercy set a plate on the table. From the pantry
she brought half a round of shortcake, baked that morning,
and a glass dish of preserved blackberries. Sarah Ellen watched
despite herself.

"I couldn't eat," she said.

"You needn't. I'm goin' to."

Mercy cut two slices.

Sarah Ellen sighed. "I ain't had a thing sence six
but half a cracker, and that got soft in my
pocket."

"Then you'll take what's set before you."

"I suppose a body must keep up. There'll be considerable
to do."

She laid the bonnet on the bundle and came to
the table. Mercy poured the tea, dark and fragrant, and
added cream without asking. Sarah Ellen took one bite of
shortcake, then another, and her shoulders lowered a little.

"How did it come at the last?" Mercy asked.

This was the question toward which everything had been proceeding.
Sarah Ellen placed her fork carefully beside her plate.

"She'd had a bad forenoon. About ten she said there
was a weight across her, and wanted the window shut.
Then she wanted it open. She asked me if the
kitchen clock was run down because she couldn't hear it,
though it was strikin' while she spoke. I sent Willie
Fales for the doctor, but the doctor'd gone north to
the Jameson child.

"Near noon she kind of settled. Her face looked different.
You know how they do."

Mercy nodded, though she had seen only two persons die
and one of those had recovered before the undertaker arrived.

"I says, 'Sister Barsett, don't you know Sarah Ellen?' She
says, plain enough, 'I should hope so.' Those were near
about her last words to me. Then Dorcas and Jane
drove up. Dorcas wouldn't take her bonnet off. She says,
'Sarah Ellen, family ought to be alone at such a
time.' I says, 'Family might have come sooner.' I know
I oughtn't, but it slipped right out."

"It was natural," Mercy said.

"Jane began cryin' and askin' where the linen was. Sister
Barsett wa'n't dead, mind you, and Jane wanted the linen.
I told her the second drawer, underneath the best pillow
slips. Then Dorcas said perhaps I could go make gruel,
in case sister revived enough to take nourishment."

"Gruel!" Mercy exclaimed. "After all your nursing?"

"I went. I wasn't goin' to make trouble over a
dyin' woman. But I'd no more than got the meal
stirred smooth when Jane come down and says it was
over. She said it in that churchyard voice of hers.
'Our dear sister has entered into rest.' I asked if
she was certain, and she drew herself up and said
death wa'n't a matter she'd jest about."

"No," said Mercy. "Jane never jested about much."

"She said Dorcas had held the glass to Sister Barsett's
mouth and there wa'n't any mist. Then they went straight
to the bureau. Said they must find the burial clothes.
I offered to help, but Dorcas says, 'You've done enough,
Sarah Ellen.'"

There were two ways of understanding such a sentence, and
Sarah Ellen had chosen the injurious one. Her eyes filled.

"I've done enough," she repeated. "That's what they think. Enough
to lift and fetch and set up nights. Enough to
wash dishes when the pump was froze. Enough to hear
every pain described from beginning to end. But when it
comes to the last breath and the bureau drawers, Sarah
Ellen ain't family."

Mercy reached across and touched her wrist.

"You were more comfort to her than either one of
'em."

"I don't know. She found fault with me constant."

"She wouldn't have dared find so much fault if she
hadn't depended on you."

This interpretation was pleasing and perhaps true. Sarah Ellen drank
her tea.

"She did depend," she admitted. "Only yesterday she said, 'If
you was to leave, Sarah Ellen, I don't know what
I should do, though you've got a terrible heavy step
in a sickroom.' I told her my step had carried
a good many things upstairs for her."

"You said that?"

"I did. She laughed, too. Then it set her coughin',
and she blamed me for that."

For a little while the two women ate in quiet.
The clock on Mercy's shelf gave a dry tick, and
a yellow wasp investigated the blackberry preserves. Outside, the broad
leaves of the squash vines lay motionless in the sun.

Mercy had liked Sister Barsett in moderate portions. Their acquaintance
had been conducted chiefly through exchanged dishes and messages, for
Sister Barsett's ailments prevented her from walking east while Mercy's
hip prevented her from walking west. Each had therefore been
able to think kindly of the other without enduring too
much contradiction.

"I remember when she brought me that jar of quince,"
Mercy said. "The year my husband died. She sent word
she'd meant to put in more sugar, but sugar was
high and sorrow spoiled the appetite anyway."

"That was like her."

"It was good quince."

"She never failed with preserves," Sarah Ellen said loyally. "Her
crab apple jelly was clear as a church window. Dorcas
can't make it. Hers candies on top."

"Dorcas keeps a very neat house."

"Too neat to live in. She folds the dish towels
before they're dry. Sister Barsett always said they smelled close."

The dead woman's authority, so recently burdensome, was already useful.

"What'll become of the things?" Mercy asked, in a tone
suggesting that property was merely another aspect of bereavement.

Sarah Ellen looked toward the window.

"I don't know as she made a will."

"I thought there was one."

"She talked of it. She talked of everything. One day
the house was goin' to the church, and next day
to Dorcas's oldest girl, and once she said she'd leave
it to me if I'd promise not to marry."

Mercy's eyebrows rose. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her I hadn't had an offer in twelve
years and couldn't promise against Providence for the sake of
a leaky ell."

"That was proper."

"I might have promised if she'd mentioned it again," Sarah
Ellen added honestly. "There ain't much danger."

Mercy's own house, small but sound, had come to her
through her husband, along with seven acres and enough money
to make thrift respectable instead of desperate. She was generous
with meals, rides when she could arrange them, and the
loan of household implements; but she had a deep, unspoken
dread that some less secure woman might mistake hospitality for
an invitation to permanence. Sarah Ellen's bundle, resting on the
chair, appeared to contain all the uncertainty of the unmarried
working woman: two aprons, a comb, perhaps a worn nightdress,
and no room of unquestioned welcome.

"You'll go back to your place now?" Mercy asked.

"If they don't need me tonight."

"You ought to rest."

"I don't know what I'll do come winter. There's nursing
enough, but folks that need you most generally have the
least to pay with. Mis' Whitcomb owes me for three
weeks. She says she's layin' by eggs toward it."

"Eggs are something."

"Not when her hens are molting."

Mercy cut another piece of shortcake and put it on
Sarah Ellen's plate. She did this partly from affection and
partly because food was safer than offering a bed.

"Perhaps Sister Barsett remembered you," she said.

Sarah Ellen's face took on the reluctant, modest look with
which hope often disguises itself.

"I don't expect anything."

"Of course not."

"She did say the green-and-white spread ought to go to
somebody that understood it."

"You quilted half of it."

"More'n half. Her stitches got so long after her rheumatism
come that I had to take out two whole corners.
But Dorcas has always admired that spread."

"Admiration ain't ownership."

"No. Though families make it so, mostly."

Sarah Ellen ate the second piece more slowly. The tea
had restored color to her face, and with strength came
fuller recollection.

"There's her mother's crockery, too," she said. "The sprigged set.
Jane says it belongs on her side because her Mary's
named for their mother. Sister Barsett said Mary broke everything
she touched and shouldn't have so much as the slop
bowl. I heard her."

"Did she write it?"

"No."

"That makes a difference."

"It hadn't ought to."

"No," Mercy agreed, "but it does."

A solemn interval followed, occupied by the realities of unwritten
intention. Both women knew households where a promised bureau, cow,
feather bed, or set of spoons had been diverted at
death by relatives who required ink before they recognized gratitude.

"Her fur tippet's ruined," Sarah Ellen said suddenly.

Mercy looked up. "The good one?"

"Moths. I took it out in June to air, and
there was three bald tracks straight across the back. Sister
Barsett accused me of lettin' moths into the house on
my shawl. I told her moths didn't need me to
introduce 'em; they'd been settled in her camphor chest before
I was born."

"I remember that tippet," Mercy said. "She wore it to
the county conference the year Elder Snow preached."

"She said everybody looked at it."

"They did. A moth flew out during prayer."

Sarah Ellen tried not to laugh. Her lips pressed together,
and then she gave a short, unwilling sound that ended
in a sob. She covered her face with her checked
handkerchief.

"It don't seem right to laugh," she said.

"You ain't laughin' at her death."

"I'm laughin' at her tippet, and she was inside it."

"She laughed herself afterward."

"No, she didn't. She said it was a miller."

Mercy's composure failed for a moment. They laughed softly together,
with shame enough to preserve propriety and affection enough to
excuse it.

When Sarah Ellen lowered her handkerchief, her expression had gentled.

"She had her good parts," she said. "Nobody could sit
up with a child like she could before she got
down herself. And if a family was burned out, she'd
give. She might tell what every article cost, and she'd
generally send what she could spare easiest, but she'd give."

"She sent the Perkinses six sheets."

"Four sheets and two tablecloths."

"They could use the tablecloths on the beds."

"That's what she said. And she never passed a subscription
paper without puttin' down something, unless she mistrusted the committee."

"She mistrusted most committees."

"She said committees was where Christian feeling went to be
counted."

Mercy considered this. "There was sense in her."

"There was. Only she mixed so much else with it."

The sun had moved beyond the kitchen window, and the
room began to lose its white glare. Mercy gathered the
plates, but Sarah Ellen immediately rose.

"You set. I'll wash."

"You're my company."

"I couldn't leave dishes standin' when there's death in the
neighborhood."

The connection was not evident, yet Mercy understood it. Disorder
appeared especially improper beside mortality, as if an unwashed cup
might be taken for evidence against the living. She let
Sarah Ellen wash while she wiped, using a flour-sack towel
too good for ordinary pans.

"What did Dr. Prince say last time?" Mercy asked.

"Said her heart was weak but her constitution was strong.
Sister Barsett said that was contrary. He told her there
wa'n't anything contrary in a body. She didn't like him
sence."

"She took considerable patent medicine."

"Considerable! There's bottles enough in her pantry to stock a
peddler. Golden Restorative, Mother Wylie's Nerve Balm, Indian Bitters, Dr.
Bell's Female Support, and a liver syrup that stained every
spoon it touched. She'd take one till it disagreed with
her, then take another to cure the disagreement."

"Perhaps that wore her out."

"Perhaps it kept her goin'. Poisons get acquainted with some
folks."

Sarah Ellen dried her hands and went back to the
rocker. Her eyes fell upon the bundle.

"Dorcas rolled my things together," she said.

"She didn't!"

"She did. My clean apron was in the washstand drawer,
and she wrapped it round my brush and slippers. Said
they'd want the chair clear for callers. There was three
empty chairs in the room."

Mercy's indignation was sincere. The treatment of another woman's clean
apron represented a breach plain enough to require no family
qualifications.

"She might have folded it."

"She never liked me. Years ago, when their father had
his stroke, I was there. He took to me, and
Dorcas couldn't bear it. Some folks want all the gratitude
without doin' all the work."

Sarah Ellen spoke sharply, but her anger soon sank. "I
suppose I'm foolish. They'd lost a sister. Maybe Dorcas didn't
know what she was doing."

"She knew enough to clear drawers."

"Yes."

"And the chair."

"Yes."

Mercy replenished the teapot with hot water. The second pouring
would be weaker, but Sarah Ellen no longer needed strengthening
so much as continuation. There are visits sustained by tea
after thirst has gone, because the lifting of a cup
grants time for what cannot otherwise be said.

"Was she afraid?" Mercy asked.

Sarah Ellen looked down at her hands. They were broad
hands, reddened by soap and weather, with clean broken nails
and a white scar across one knuckle.

"Sometimes. She talked religion enough when she felt middlin', but
when the spells come she wanted folks, not doctrines. Tuesday
night she gripped hold of me and says, 'Don't you
go downstairs, Sarah Ellen.' I says, 'I've got to get
the hot-water bag.' She says, 'Let it stay cold.' So
I stayed."

"That was religion enough," Mercy said.

"I ain't much of a professor."

"You're useful."

"Usefulness won't save a soul, according to Elder Pike."

"Elder Pike never had pleurisy at midnight."

Sarah Ellen glanced at Mercy and smiled.

"Sister Barsett worried because she couldn't feel resigned. I told
her maybe she wa'n't called on to feel it till
the time come. Folks expect the dyin' to be acquainted
with heaven before they've got there."

"What did she say?"

"She said I was unscriptural and asked for beef tea."

"That sounds more natural."

"She took near a cupful."

They sat quietly. Mercy thought of her husband's last winter,
and of how little his death had resembled the shaped
event described at funerals. He had worried about the woodpile,
complained of a seam in his pillow, and asked twice
whether she had shut the henhouse. His final concern had
been that a borrowed wrench should be returned. For years
Mercy had felt disappointed that there had been no noble
farewell; later she came to understand that a life ended
in its own language.

"She'll be missed," she said.

Sarah Ellen's face crumpled again, though no tears came.

"I keep thinkin' I hear her bell. I hated that
bell. She'd ring once for water, twice for the bedpan,
and keep jinglin' if she thought I wa'n't movin' fast
enough. Last night I took it away from her. I
says, 'You can call me by name like a Christian.'
Then she whispered till I fetched it back."

Mercy made the proper sympathetic sound.

"What's queer," Sarah Ellen continued, "is I don't know where
to be. For near two weeks every minute's belonged to
her. If she slept, I listened. If she woke, I
fetched. Now there ain't anything. Dorcas says come tomorrow at
nine if I want to help lay out the parlor.
If I want to! As if I was askin' for
entertainment."

"You need rest first."

"I could stay here till sundown, maybe."

"Of course you can."

Mercy said it warmly. Sundown was distinct from supper and
very distinct from night. She was ashamed of making the
distinctions, but continued to make them.

"I wouldn't be any trouble," Sarah Ellen said, not looking
at her.

"You never are."

"I mean, if it come on dark—"

"I'll have Asa Goodwin's girl walk over with you. She's
passin' about eight."

"Oh, I can walk alone."

The little danger passed. Mercy felt mean and relieved. To
make amends she went to the pantry and brought out
a small loaf of gingerbread.

"You'll take this home."

"I won't rob you."

"It'll dry before I eat it."

Sarah Ellen knew this was untrue; Mercy's gingerbread never had
an opportunity to dry. Still, a fiction told to preserve
the receiver's dignity was one of the community's accepted forms
of honesty.

"Well, a slice might be handy," she said.

"I'll wrap half."

"Not half."

"Near half."

Mercy cut generously and folded it in clean paper, tying
it with string saved from the dry-goods store. Sarah Ellen
watched the parcel join her bundle, and for the first
time that afternoon there was something homelike in her possessions.

The light mellowed. Beyond the open door, the fields had
begun to turn from yellow to bronze, and the dusty
road held the long shadow of Mercy's lilac bush. Far
over the marsh two birds called, one after the other,
their cries so remote that the narrow neighborhood seemed briefly
suspended in a wider and lonelier world.

Sarah Ellen heard them and sighed.

"Sister Barsett always said those birds meant rain."

"They've called dry weather before."

"She said that was because the rain changed its mind."

"Then a sign can't be proved wrong."

"That suited her."

The women returned to the front room, where Mercy's best
chairs stood upon a braided rug and the family photographs
looked out with grave, pale eyes. Sarah Ellen paused before
the picture of Mercy's husband.

"He'd have fetched you all the news," she said.

"He fetched some that hadn't happened."

"He was sociable."

"He was."

Mercy adjusted the album on the center table. Widowhood had
become her settled climate, but now and then a visitor
opened a door through which the old weather blew.

Sarah Ellen sat by the tied blind. From there she
could see the upper bend of the road and, through
a gap in the elms, the chimney of Sister Barsett's
house.

"There'll be folks commencin' to call," she said. "Dorcas'll have
the parlor blinds down."

"They'll need somebody to receive."

"Jane thinks she can. She talks too low when there's
death. Nobody knows whether she's askin' about the deceased or
offerin' 'em a chair."

"You know all the families."

"I know who's mad at who. That's more necessary."

Mercy resumed her tatting, though she had lost count and
would have to undo several loops later.

"Will they have the funeral Saturday?"

"If the minister can come. Dorcas favors Friday, but Sister
Barsett never approved buryin' Friday. Said it made Sunday seem
too near. Jane wants Elder Pike, though Sister Barsett left
his church after he preached against decorative trimming."

"She took that personal on account of her bonnet."

"She had reason. He looked straight at the purple bow."

"What did she say?"

"Said if Elder Pike thought the Lord preferred ugly women,
he'd formed his theology to suit his congregation."

Mercy dropped her tatting.

"She said that?"

"To me, she did."

They laughed again, more freely now. Death had begun its
usual work of selecting and polishing the remarks by which
a difficult person could be loved.

"I hope Dorcas don't find the red notebook," Sarah Ellen
said.

"What notebook?"

"The one Sister Barsett kept in her sewing table. She
wrote down what folks borrowed and didn't return."

Mercy's fingers stopped.

"I returned her jelly strainer."

"I know."

"I sent it by Eliza."

"It's marked returned."

"Did she have my name?"

"Most everybody's."

"What else was against me?"

Sarah Ellen hesitated, enjoying for the first time that day
a restored advantage.

"There was a paper of nasturtium seed."

"I never borrowed nasturtium seed."

"She wrote April seventeenth, 'Mercy Crane—mixed colors.'"

"She gave me those."

"Likely she forgot."

"I don't want Dorcas thinking I kept what wa'n't mine."

"Nobody'd think that."

"Dorcas would."

"Well, the notebook might not be found."

Mercy considered the matter with growing concern. A person could
live uprightly for seventy years and still be reduced in
another family's account book to mixed nasturtiums. She decided to
send Dorcas a note, though a note might suggest guilt.
Better perhaps to send seeds. Yet the original flowers had
gone mostly yellow and could not be said to represent
the mixed colors.

Sarah Ellen, perceiving that she had shifted the burden of
uneasiness, felt kinder.

"Sister Barsett knew you were honest," she said. "She only
wrote things down to exercise her memory."

"She might have exercised it by remembering they were a
gift."

Before Sarah Ellen could answer, a figure appeared at the
bend of the road, moving quickly. It was Lyddy Fales,
a stout young matron whose apron had been thrown over
her head in place of a bonnet. She waved one
arm when she saw Sarah Ellen at the window.

"Mercy!" Sarah Ellen cried. "Something's happened over there."

Both women rose. Mercy reached the side door just as
Lyddy came up the path, breathless and pink.

"Sarah Ellen here?" Lyddy demanded.

"She is. What is it? Has Dorcas took sick?"

Lyddy leaned against the doorframe, too full of news to
spend time recovering.

"Sister Barsett ain't dead."

For one instant the statement had no place in the
orderly world. Mercy stared. Behind her Sarah Ellen said, "What?"

"She ain't dead," Lyddy repeated. "She come to."

Sarah Ellen gripped the back of a chair.

"Lyddy Fales, don't you speak careless."

"I ain't. Ma sent me. They'd laid her straight and
shut the room, and Dorcas went down to tell Mr.
Hobbs about the coffin. Jane was lookin' through the linen.
Then there come a poundin' overhead."

"A poundin'?" Mercy whispered.

"She'd got hold of the bedstead and was bangin' it
against the wall. Jane went up and found her settin'
upright, mad as a hornet, askin' why the room was
dark and where Sarah Ellen had gone."

Sarah Ellen lowered herself into the chair without taking her
eyes from Lyddy.

"Alive?"

"Alive enough. Dorcas tried to make her lie down, and
Sister Barsett told her to get out of the house.
Said they'd smothered her under the white spread and near
finished what the spell begun. Jane commenced prayin', and Sister
Barsett said if she couldn't pray quieter she could do
it outdoors."

Mercy put one hand over her mouth.

"What about the glass?" Sarah Ellen asked faintly.

"She says Dorcas held it at the wrong end."

No one spoke for a moment.

Lyddy hurried on. "She's hungry. Wants fried salt pork, two
eggs, and tea strong enough to stand a spoon in.
Ma said not to give her all that, but she

The brown bundle stood on the kitchen chair, topped by
the black bonnet and the parcel of gingerbread. Sarah Ellen
looked at it as a traveler might look upon baggage
left at the wrong station.

"Did the doctor come?"

"Just now. He says it was a suspended condition."

"She's had 'em before," Sarah Ellen said slowly. "Not so
complete."

"She says she never was dead and don't see why
folks are so eager."

Mercy's shoulders began to shake. She turned away, but the
tied blinds, the purple-grape cups, and the solemn black bonnet
all seemed to have entered into conspiracy against her. A
small sound escaped.

Sarah Ellen looked offended. Then Lyddy gave a gasp that
was half laughter, and Mercy lost command altogether. She sank
into the kitchen rocker and laughed until tears ran down
her face. Every candid judgment of the afternoon came marching
back: the heart remedies, the burial clothes, the crockery, the
moth-eaten tippet, the green-and-white spread, the notebook of debts. Sister
Barsett, who had been safely arranged in memory, had sat
up among the linen and reclaimed her faults.

"It ain't a laughin' matter," Sarah Ellen said, though her
own mouth twitched. "She may go yet."

"That's what she told Dorcas," Lyddy said. "Only not till
she'd had her supper."

Sarah Ellen rose. Weariness returned to her features, but it
was a familiar weariness now, joined to purpose. She tied
on her bonnet, tucked the bundle under one arm, and
checked for the gingerbread.

"I'll go," she said. "She'd ought not to eat pork."

"Couldn't anybody else attend her tonight?" Mercy asked, wiping her
eyes.

"Not after Dorcas held the glass wrong."

At the doorway Sarah Ellen paused and looked back with
solemn dignity.

"Mercy, what's said in a house ought to stay there."

"It shall," Mercy promised.

"And if she mentions the green-and-white spread, I never asked."

"You never did."

"Nor the crockery."

"Not a word."

"The tippet was moth-eaten, though. I stand by that."

"So would anybody that saw it."

Sarah Ellen nodded, satisfied. Then she and Lyddy set off
down the road, Sarah Ellen already asking whether the eggs
were fresh, whether the bed had been aired after all
those people crowded round it, and whether Jane had possessed
the sense to put away the camphor.

Mercy watched until they passed the elms. The evening lay
quiet over the fields, but Sister Barsett's distant chimney now
sent up a lively thread of smoke. Supper was being
prepared; authority had resumed its place; grief had been dismissed
until further notice.

Mercy closed the door and carried the cups to the
sink. When she found Sarah Ellen's checked handkerchief beneath the
table, she began laughing again. She laughed while she untied
the blinds, while she put away the blackberry preserves, and
while the first star came out above Sister Barsett's roof.

At last she sat alone in the darkening kitchen, weak
with amusement and strangely comforted. There were worse things, she
reflected, than being called back to work; worse things than
an appetite returning after everybody had begun dividing the crockery.
And if death itself could be kept waiting by a
badly managed household and a demand for strong tea, then
perhaps the neighborhood was not yet so helpless as it
sometimes appeared.

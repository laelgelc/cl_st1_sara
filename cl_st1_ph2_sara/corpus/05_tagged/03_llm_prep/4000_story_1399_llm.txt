## THE MARBLE INTERPRETER

During a summer which I passed upon Martha's Vineyard—partly in
obedience to a physician who believed the salt wind would
strengthen my lungs, and partly because a certain weariness of
mankind had persuaded me that solitude might strengthen my spirit—I
became acquainted with Mr. Ichabod Wigglesworth, carver of tombstones.

He had come thither from the mainland, not, as he
frankly confessed, for the beauty of the island, nor for
its healthfulness, nor even for the excellent bluefish that were
daily drawn from the sea, but because the inhabitants, though
sufficiently ingenious in most affairs of life and death, had
long been accustomed to import their memorial stones.

"There's poor economy in it," said Mr. Wigglesworth. "The dead
don't know whether their gravestones cross the Sound; and the
living ought not to pay freight on their sorrow."

This observation was delivered on the morning of our first
meeting, while he stood before a little workshop near the
road, arrayed in a leathern apron, with a mallet in
one hand and a chisel in the other. He was
a short, round, brisk old man, whose countenance had acquired
no sepulchral solemnity from his trade. On the contrary, his
cheeks were rosy, his eyes twinkled, and his whole person
seemed to rebound from the neighborhood of Death with an
additional elasticity. Marble dust lay upon his hair and shoulders,
giving him the aspect of one who had grown prematurely
venerable; but whenever he shook himself, the counterfeit age departed
in a cloud, and the cheerful little artificer stood revealed.

Behind him were ranged slabs of white marble, gray slate,
and a dark stone brought, he told me, from a
quarry in Connecticut. Some were blank as the unrecorded future;
others bore half-finished names, fragments of Scripture, weeping willows, urns,
cherubs, death's-heads, broken roses, clasped hands, and sundry other devices
by which mankind endeavors to make visible that grief which,
in its profoundest recesses, refuses all shape.

I entered his shop, and from that day forward scarcely
one passed without my spending an hour beneath its low
roof. The ocean was near enough for its voice to
mingle with the tapping of the chisel; and often, while
Mr. Wigglesworth carved the name of some sailor whom the
sea had swallowed, the waves seemed to mutter an answer
beyond the dunes. There was a strange fitness in this
companionship of the sea and the grave. Both conceal what
is committed to them; both surrender it only in altered
forms; and over each the human heart hangs, searching for
what cannot return.

Mr. Wigglesworth's customers afforded me a study more various than
any library. They entered upon the same business, yet each
brought a separate world of memory; and the difference between
one sorrow and another was as great as between the
faces of the mourners. Some came weeping, some bargaining, some
ashamed of emotion, some proud of it, and a few
with no grief whatever, except the apprehension that their neighbors
might suspect them of possessing none.

The first whom I particularly remember was an elderly woman
in a faded black bonnet, who laid upon the workbench
a small purse, a lock of brown hair, and a
yellow letter folded into the compass of a thumb.

"He was twenty-three," she said, "and I was nineteen."

Mr. Wigglesworth nodded, as though these figures constituted a sufficient
biography.

The young man had sailed in a whaler forty years
before. Somewhere in the Pacific, beneath a sun which had
never shone upon his native island, a wounded whale struck
the boat with its flukes. Two sailors were rescued; three
disappeared. Of the latter no relic returned—not a garment, not
a clasp-knife, not so much as a splinter of the
boat whereon a sweetheart might fasten her desolate affection. The
girl waited seven years, refusing every suitor; afterward she married
a worthy farmer, bore him children, nursed him through a
long illness, and had now been his widow for a
decade. Yet it was for the drowned youth that she
desired a stone.

"I would not have it large," she said. "My husband's
monument is large enough for both of us, when my
time comes. But Samuel has nothing."

Nothing! The whole Pacific was his tomb, and the moon
wrote his epitaph upon ten thousand miles of moving water;
yet because no narrow slab marked a grassy mound, the
poor woman felt that he had been defrauded of death
itself.

She chose a little stone, bearing a broken rose.

"He used to bring me wild roses," she murmured.

Mr. Wigglesworth proposed an inscription concerning a flower transplanted to
Heaven; but she shook her head and gave him a
verse from the yellow letter—a boyish promise to return before
the autumn gales.

When she had departed, leaving the lock of hair as
a guide for the carved wreath, I remarked that her
remembrance seemed almost an infidelity to the husband with whom
she had passed the greater portion of her life.

"Not a bit," answered Wigglesworth. "A woman's heart has more
rooms than a tavern, and the dead never complain of
their lodging."

Methought his comparison lacked dignity, yet contained truth. The old
woman's first love had remained unchanged because it had never
been subjected to change. Samuel had not grown peevish, rheumatic,
parsimonious, or deaf; he had not forgotten anniversaries, disputed household
expenses, nor left muddy boots beside the hearth. Death, that
severe idealist, had preserved him at twenty-three. Perhaps the lost
lover was less a man than a fragrance shut within
the heart; and, like the scent of those wild roses,
it had survived the hand that gathered them.

Not long afterward there came a tall, dry personage, whose
garments had the respectable blackness of one who habitually attended
funerals and profited by them. He examined three stones of
equal size, asked the price of each, and then inquired
whether a reduction would be made if all were ordered
together.

"For children?" said Wigglesworth.

"Wives," replied the stranger.

It appeared that Providence had thrice united him in matrimony
and thrice released him. The first wife had been meek,
the second industrious, and the third, as he cautiously expressed
it, "a woman of uncommon force." He desired identical monuments,
lest any distinction should awaken posthumous jealousy or present scandal.

"Same verse?" asked Wigglesworth.

"The same, if it saves labor."

My friend rubbed his chin. "Names differ."

"They must, I suppose."

"And ages."

"Unfortunately."

After much calculation, the widower ordered three slabs adorned with
weeping willows, and the assurance that each deceased woman had
been the best of wives. He departed with the grave
composure of a merchant who has arranged the shipment of
three barrels of flour.

"Suppose he marries again?" I asked.

"I'll keep the pattern," said Wigglesworth.

Here was thrift invading the sanctity of bereavement; yet who
shall affirm that the man's grief was therefore counterfeit? We
demand that sorrow be eloquent, individual, and careless of expense;
but sorrow itself is often dumb, imitative, and obliged to
count its shillings. Perhaps he had loved all three women
in such measure as his nature allowed. A shallow vessel,
though filled to the brim, contains but little; nevertheless, it
is full.

A more noble figure entered the workshop one tempestuous afternoon.
He was Captain Jabez Coffin, a whaling master, broad-shouldered, bent
somewhat forward by the opposing winds of fifty voyages, and
so weather-beaten that his face seemed carved from the root
of an oak. His wife had died while his ship
was rounding Cape Horn. He returned to find her grave
green with a season's grass and his youngest child unable
to recognize him.

The captain required a double monument. Upon one side was
to be his wife's name; upon the other, a blank
reserved for his own.

"Put in the date of my birth," he said. "Death
can finish the account."

For an emblem he chose an open book, one page
inscribed and one awaiting the final hand. He stood a
long while before the design, tracing with a scarred finger
the place where his name would be.

"She kept the light burning," he said at last, "thirty-two
years."

The phrase referred, I supposed, to the lamp in the
seaward window, yet it illuminated more than their cottage. While
he pursued whales amid the ice, while strange constellations wheeled
above him and islands rose like dreams from the tropic
sea, that faithful flame had drawn an invisible line from
the world's uttermost circumference to his hearth. Now the lamp
was extinguished; but in his remembrance it burned brighter than
before.

"Will you add an anchor?" I asked, imagining it a
suitable token of hope.

"No," said the captain. "I've had enough of anchors. Put
two chairs."

Thus the grand machinery of maritime sentiment was rejected for
the homely image of evening companionship. Wigglesworth carved the chairs;
and, though they appeared somewhat stiff and disproportionately small, I
have seldom seen an emblem that touched me more deeply.

The sea, which had caused the captain's absence, presently supplied
us with comedy. A woman named Hepzibah Crowell commissioned a
costly stone for her husband, lost eighteen months before from
a schooner bound to Boston. The vessel had been found
drifting, with a damaged mast and no living creature aboard.
Widow Crowell mourned decorously, wore bombazine despite the summer heat,
and selected an epitaph declaring that her husband had exchanged
the storms of earth for the everlasting haven.

Mr. Wigglesworth had cut the name, age, and most of
the verse, when a shadow darkened the doorway.

A lean, bearded sailor stood upon the threshold, carrying a
red bundle.

"Hepzibah been here?" he asked.

Wigglesworth, glancing from the sailor to the inscription, dropped his
chisel.

The supposed dead man had been washed overboard, clung to
wreckage, and been taken up by a vessel bound for
the African coast. Fever, contrary winds, and a succession of
remote ports had delayed his homeward journey. He now contemplated
his own memorial with an expression in which bewilderment gradually
yielded to pride.

"Handsome stone," he said.

"Best Italian marble," replied Wigglesworth mournfully.

"Can the name be changed?"

"Not without spoiling the balance."

The sailor considered. "Might keep it till needed."

"You've already outlived the age cut on it."

"That's awkward."

At this juncture Widow Crowell herself appeared, uttered a shriek,
and fell upon the returned man with such violence of
affection that the red bundle rolled into the road. The
living husband embraced his wife; the carver embraced, figuratively, his
loss. All was joy except in the bosom of Mr.
Wigglesworth.

"A fine epitaph wasted," he lamented when they were gone.
"Folks ought to be certain before they order."

The stone was eventually turned, its reverse side polished, and
sold for the infant child of a cooper. Thus does
the blank indifference of marble accommodate itself to our vicissitudes:
a husband's resurrection becomes a child's grave, and the same
substance which had falsely proclaimed safe entrance into the eternal
haven was made to record a voyager who scarcely quitted
shore.

One morning a mother came leading a boy of eight
years. The woman's face wore that fixed composure which is
more terrible than tears; the child's countenance possessed the frightened
gravity of one who has lately looked into a mystery
and fears that any careless movement may open it again.
His twin brother had died of fever.

The boy carried his brother's wooden whistle. At intervals he
raised it toward his lips, but his mother gently lowered
his hand.

They examined stones bearing lambs, cherubs, buds, and broken bows.
The boy preferred a design of scattered arrows, because his
brother had loved to play at Indians; the mother chose
a sleeping lamb, because it was customary.

For the inscription Wigglesworth produced a book of verses. The
mother turned its pages without seeing them. At length the
child read aloud:

> Short was his stay, yet sweet his rest;
> God called him home, for He knew best.

"That one," said the mother.

Afterward I asked Wigglesworth whether such verses, repeated upon a
hundred graves, did not make grief itself appear commonplace.

"Common words are roomy," he answered. "They stretch to suit
a great grief and shrink to fit a small one."

Indeed, the lines were poor enough when measured by poetry,
but sorrow has a prosody of its own. To a
stranger they said nothing; to that mother they interpreted the
vacant bed, the folded garments, the untouched porringer, and the
terrible singularity which had entered where there had always been
two. The phrase "called him home" might be worn smooth
by generations; yet she heard in it the voice that
had called both children from their play. Convention supplied the
vessel; her anguish filled it.

The surviving twin returned alone some weeks later and asked
for the scattered arrows to be carved secretly upon the
back of the stone. He paid with six pennies and
a bright button.

"Mother likes the lamb," he whispered, "but Tom would like
these."

Mr. Wigglesworth accepted the treasure and did the work. Perchance
no sculptor, from Phidias downward, ever received a nobler commission.

Not all who entered were moved by love. Two ancient
islanders, Obed Luce and Barzillai Norton, had hated each other
during sixty years. The cause of their enmity had been
forgotten by everyone, including, I suspect, themselves; but the hatred
endured, vigorous after the death of its parent grievance. They
had disputed boundaries, pews, elections, fishing rights, weather, theology, and
the proper hour for closing the tavern. If Obed repaired
his fence, Barzillai declared it crooked; if Barzillai fell ill,
Obed called the sickness pretended.

When Barzillai died, Obed came to order a stone.

"I want the lot beside his," he announced.

Wigglesworth stared. "Thought you couldn't abide him."

"I can't."

"Then why lie alongside him till Judgment?"

"So he shan't think he's got rid of me."

He chose a taller stone than his enemy's and directed
that it be placed where its morning shadow would fall
across the neighboring grave. His epitaph, prepared in advance, described
him as a lover of peace.

Here hatred had assumed the offices of affection. It had
kept Barzillai continually present in Obed's thoughts; had sharpened his
faculties, enlivened his conversation, and perhaps prolonged his days. When
the enemy vanished, life lost its counterweight. Obed could not
consent that death should dissolve a relation to which he
had devoted the chief energies of his heart.

"Hatred," I observed, "may be love wearing a mask."

"No," said Wigglesworth. "Love pays for both stones. Hate makes
each man buy his own."

The distinction was practical, and possibly profound.

Another visitor was Peleg Pottle, the richest and meanest man
on the island. He heated no room until water froze
in it, dined upon food rejected by his hired laborers,
and was said to read borrowed newspapers by moonlight in
order to save candles. Yet he ordered a monument of
such magnitude that Mr. Wigglesworth doubted whether his little wagon
could convey it to the burying-ground.

It was to have four columns, an urn, two angels,
a winged hourglass, a ship under full sail, an open
Bible, and a serpent swallowing its tail. Peleg wished his
virtues enumerated upon three sides, leaving the fourth for an
account of his public benefactions.

"Have you made many?" asked Wigglesworth.

"I intend to," said Peleg.

The miser haggled over every dollar, reducing the price by
the sacrifice of one angel's trumpet and half the feathers
in the wings of the hourglass. Yet he would not
diminish the size.

"Why such a monument?" I ventured.

He fixed upon me a small, cold eye.

"Because nobody else will put one up."

There was naked truth in the reply. Peleg knew that
he had stored no treasure in human affection; therefore he
would compel stone to preserve what no heart would willingly
remember. He had denied warmth to the living, but demanded
marble from the earth; he had withheld his substance from
need, yet lavished it upon his future dust. The monument
was not vanity alone. It was fear—the terror of a
man who, having made himself unlovable, perceived the second death
of oblivion advancing behind the first.

He died before all his virtues could be composed. Wigglesworth,
unwilling to leave so much surface blank, filled one panel
with a large willow.

"Stone won't remember a man," he told me, "but it
can show where he was forgotten."

Among these histories, none dwells more tenderly in my memory
than that of Lydia Mayhew, a young woman whose slight
figure and transparent cheek foretold what all her companions strove
not to believe. She came with an aunt, but chose
her memorial as independently as another girl might choose a
ribbon for a festival.

She rejected the death's-head as ugly, the cherub as too
fat, the willow as gloomy, and the urn because, she
said, it reminded her of a teapot. At last she
found a narrow slab bearing the outline of a butterfly
rising from an opened chrysalis.

"That is not death," she said. "That is getting free."

Mr. Wigglesworth, accustomed to mourners who spoke of the dead,
was disconcerted by one who spoke serenely of herself.

"What words shall go beneath?" he asked.

"Only my name—and 'She is not here.'"

Her aunt began to weep. Lydia touched the unfinished butterfly
with fingers nearly as pale as the marble.

"Do not cry, Aunt Mercy. If I am not here,
you must not keep looking here."

She returned twice to observe the work and once brought
flowers for the carver's window. Before the stone was finished,
the bell tolled for her. Wigglesworth completed the butterfly in
silence, and I saw that his chisel trembled.

"There's folks," he said, "that seem dead before they go,
and folks that seem living afterward."

The sentence followed me to Lydia's grave, where the new
stone stood among older monuments blackened by weather. The butterfly
appeared about to rise, yet could never quit the marble
that embodied its escape. There lay the paradox of every
tomb: it seeks to declare immortality by means of weight,
freedom by imprisonment, spirit by stone.

As the summer declined, I grew acquainted with Wigglesworth's catalogue
of symbols and the moral values he attached to them.
A willow suited widows, unless they were young and likely
to marry again; clasped hands were good for affectionate couples,
though expensive in the fingers; anchors belonged to sailors and
hopeful Christians; a snapped column signified youth, but displeased masons;
and angels, he averred, should never be carved from poor
marble, because discoloration gave them a diseased appearance.

"Do you believe," I asked one day, "that these devices
truly represent the dead?"

"They represent the folks who order them," he replied.

This was so just that I suspected him of wisdom,
though he himself would have denied the accusation.

The workshop became to me a little theatre in which
mankind rehearsed its final relation to mankind. There I beheld
faith and doubt, tenderness and display, remorse, economy, rivalry, habit,
and hope—all translated into inches of stone and reckoned in
dollars and cents. Yet the baseness of the commerce did
not always degrade the sentiment. A coin laid down by
a poor widow might contain more love than a cathedral;
while an elaborate monument could be but the petrified shadow
of vanity.

One golden evening, when the sun made every marble slab
luminous and the dust in the workshop floated like minute
spirits, Mr. Wigglesworth drew forth several patterns.

"You've looked at enough stones," he said. "Which would you
choose for yourself?"

I laughed, but he remained serious. There was a blank
slab against the wall, pure and cold, awaiting a name;
and for an instant I imagined my own letters deepening
beneath his chisel.

"None," I answered.

"No stone?"

"No grave, if I could prevent it—or at least no
monument."

He appeared wounded, as though I had rejected not an
article of trade, but the accumulated purpose of his life.

I endeavored to explain. Why should affection be chained to
the spot where the body decays? The beloved dead, if
our faith be true, are not beneath the stone. They
have cast off the flesh as the butterfly its chrysalis;
yet we build heavy architecture above the discarded shell and
bid remembrance dwell there like a prisoner. We say, "Here
lies," when the soul is not here; we inscribe resurrection
upon a slab which presses downward; we carve wings that
cannot rise.

"When I think of one who is gone," I continued,
"I would fain behold the freed spirit ascending whithersoever Providence
appoints—not imagine it confined under turf, among roots and worms.
A gravestone draws the thought from Heaven to dust. It
makes the immortal seem local. It mistakes the cast garment
for the wearer."

Wigglesworth listened with increasing distress.

"Then all these are lies?" he asked, looking about at
his stones.

"Not lies. Symbols—perhaps mistaken ones."

"Folks need a place to go."

"The heart is place enough."

He shook his head. "Heart moves around. Stone stays put."

Against this homely answer my philosophy found no immediate defense.
The heart does move around. It is distracted, forgetful, treacherous;
it fills with new faces, new duties, new griefs, until
the dead, once sovereign, become faint figures in a remote
chamber. Perhaps we set up stones not because the soul
inhabits the grave, but because human remembrance requires an earthly
threshold. We go thither to recover what daily life disperses.
The body is absent from Heaven to our senses, but
the grave is present beneath our hand.

Still I resisted.

"Would you imprison Lydia's butterfly forever in marble?"

"No," he said. "I'd show where it flew from."

The sun had sunk lower. Across the floor, the stones
cast long bars of shadow, like the grating of a
dungeon; yet above them the marble dust shone in the
air, ascending and descending with every breath. I thought of
the old woman's lover, young forever in the Pacific; of
Captain Coffin's two chairs; of the rescued sailor who had
outlived his epitaph; of the twin's secret arrows; of Obed
lying where his shadow might trouble his enemy; of Peleg's
huge monument proclaiming the emptiness around his name; and of
Lydia, who had warned us not to seek her where
she was not.

These, these were graves where loved ones slept—and graves where
vanity strutted, hatred kept watch, remorse paid tribute, and loneliness
made its final bargain. The stones did not merely cover
dust. They revealed the living.

When I left the island, Wigglesworth accompanied me to the
road. He had wrapped a small object in brown paper
and pressed it into my hand. Aboard the packet, I
opened it and found a piece of marble no larger
than a book, carved with a butterfly on one side
and, on the other, the words:

**THE HEART MOVES; MEMORY RETURNS.**

I possess it still, though I have never determined whether
it refutes my doctrine or confirms it.

Years have passed. Many whom I loved have gone beyond
the reach of my voice, and I, who once spoke
so confidently of liberated souls, have sometimes found myself longing
for the pressure of a hand now dust, the cadence
of a voice now silent, the very infirmities which eternity
has presumably cured. Joy consoles us by filling the present;
sorrow, more mysteriously, consoles us by preserving the past. Regret
keeps open a door which happiness would close. Perchance our
griefs are not merely wounds, but senses—painful organs whereby we
apprehend that invisible world which otherwise might escape our dull
consciousness.

And so I remain uncertain. The spirit may soar, yet
affection kneels. Faith looks upward, while memory walks among the
graves. The stone is cold, but the hand that touches
it is warm; the inscription is conventional, but the tear
that interprets it is individual. Perhaps the dead need no
monuments. Perhaps the living do.

Whenever I behold that little marble butterfly, I think less
of death than of the cheerful old carver standing amid
his slabs, his apron whitened, his mallet uplifted, while beyond
his narrow workshop the immeasurable ocean spoke of burial and
return. Then sorrow and comfort seem not enemies but companions,
grave and tender, journeying by the same road; and I
know not whether the deepest consolation granted to man is
found in the joys he possesses, or in those sacred
regrets by which he learns what his joys were worth.

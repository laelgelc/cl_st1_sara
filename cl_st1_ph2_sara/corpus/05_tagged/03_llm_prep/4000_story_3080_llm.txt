## I. SIXTY-TWO CENTS

At noon the price came over the wire to the
station agent at Black Hawk: December wheat, sixty-two cents.

The agent wrote the figures in blue pencil upon the
margin of a circular and pinned it behind the fly-specked
glass of the bulletin frame. Before two o'clock every farmer
within ten miles knew of it. Men stopped their teams
in the road to tell one another; the blacksmith repeated
it between strokes of his hammer; at Delaney's store they
discussed it in low voices over the flour barrels.

Sixty-two cents meant ruin.

It had cost Sam Lewiston seventy-four cents to grow his
wheat. This included seed, hired labor, interest on the mortgage,
repairs to the reaper, twine, taxes, and the hauling of
the crop eleven miles to the elevator. It did not
include his own work, nor Emma's, nor the gradual wearing-out
of the horses. At sixty-two cents every bushel sold enlarged
his debt.

All summer the wheat had stood high and yellow across
the Kansas land, flowing to the horizon in one enormous,
opulent tide. Sam had watched it thicken, watched the heads
fill, had crushed the kernels between his teeth and found
them hard and sound. There were forty bushels to the
acre. He had never raised a better crop.

But in Chicago Truslow, the Great Bear, had begun his
campaign.

He and his following sold wheat they did not own,
sold it for December, for January, for May; sold it
in ten thousand-bushel lots, in fifty thousand, in a hundred
thousand; sold it yesterday and promised to deliver it months
afterward. Their brokers hammered the price in the Pit. Each
decline frightened holders into selling; each fresh sale forced another
decline. Elevators were full, the visible supply was enormous, Europe
bought sparingly. The Bear party struck and struck again.

The price went to seventy cents, to sixty-eight, to sixty-five.

At sixty-two the farmers began to die.

On the afternoon when the news reached Black Hawk, Sam
sat on the rear step of the ranch house, his
elbows on his knees, his hands hanging limp. Beyond the
sheds the stubble fields stretched away under the white October
sky. A windmill complained at intervals, turning a quarter circle
and stopping.

Emma came from the kitchen, wiping her hands upon her
apron.

"Well?" she asked.

He did not look up.

"Sixty-two."

For a time she stood beside him. There was no
outcry. The foreclosure had already been discussed, the sale of
the stock calculated, the value of the machinery guessed at.
They had passed beyond surprise.

"Delaney says there's work in Chicago," Sam said at last.
"Elevators. Packing houses. A man can get two dollars a
day."

Emma looked across the fields. The wheat had gone to
the Black Hawk elevator three weeks before, but the land
seemed still full of it, as though every furrow held
the labor of their years.

"Will they take the house?"

"They'll take everything."

The words fell into the great quiet. Somewhere behind the
barn a hen cackled. The windmill moved again with a
rusty, complaining cry.

In November the ranch was sold by order of the
mortgage company. The horses brought less than half their value.
The reaper, for which Sam still owed seventy dollars, was
knocked down for thirty-five. After the judgments and costs were
paid, the Lewistons possessed one trunk, two valises, eighty-three dollars
in cash, and railroad tickets to Chicago.

They went east in an emigrant car, while beside the
track, mile after mile, the wheat country rolled past them—granaries,
elevators, bare fields, stacks, loaded cars—all that prodigious food which
had become so cheap that the men who raised it
could no longer live.

## II. A DOLLAR TEN

By March the situation was reversed.

The wheat that had glutted the country disappeared. Export clearances
increased; bad weather delayed shipments from Argentina; reports from Russia
indicated a deficient crop. Most important of all, Charles Hornung
and the associated bull clique had bought the available supply.

They owned the wheat in the Chicago elevators. They held
warehouse receipts for wheat in Duluth, Minneapolis, Toledo and St.
Louis. They controlled the lake shipments, the receipts in transit
and the contracts for May delivery. The shorts—men who had
sold what they did not possess—must buy from Hornung or
default.

Wheat rose to ninety cents, to a dollar, to a
dollar ten.

In his office on La Salle Street, Hornung received Truslow.

The Bull sat behind a broad mahogany desk, wearing a
velvet skull-cap and smoking a long Pittsburg stogie. He was
a heavy man, slow in gesture, with pale, steady eyes.
Truslow, leaner and older, kept his hat on his knee
and tapped its brim with one finger.

"One hundred thousand bushels," he said.

"You're short more than that," Hornung observed.

"That's my affair."

"It is—for the present."

Truslow's mouth tightened. Six months before he had been invincible.
He had crushed wheat to sixty-two cents and cleared, it
was said, over a million. Now the Bull had caught
him. Every cent of advance meant another loss upon his
outstanding contracts.

"I'll pay your price," he said.

"One ten."

"One ten."

Hornung took the stogie from his mouth.

"On one condition. The wheat goes out of the country."

"What difference does that make to you?"

"All the difference there is. I'm not selling you ammunition
to fire back at me."

Truslow glanced toward the windows. Below, the traffic of the
street passed in an indistinct roar.

"Export it where?"

"Liverpool, Glasgow, Antwerp—I don't care. But export it. Bills of
lading to be deposited with my bank. Party of the
First Part guarantees that said grain shall not be redelivered
at any domestic market."

"You want the ocean thrown into the contract."

"I do."

For a moment the Great Bear remained motionless. He appeared
beaten, but there was something in his stillness that Hornung
did not like. A desperate bear, even with the dogs
upon him, had claws.

Finally Truslow nodded.

"Draw your papers."

The contract was prepared. Truslow delivered a certified check for
one hundred and ten thousand dollars; Hornung surrendered the warehouse
receipts. Before evening the wheat was loaded into cars ostensibly
consigned to an Atlantic port.

"Watch him," Hornung told his secretary. "He's busted, but he
isn't dead."

## III. THE PIT

Three days later, at ten minutes after eleven, an unknown
broker began selling May wheat.

He sold ten thousand bushels at a dollar twelve. He
sold ten thousand more at a dollar eleven and seven-eighths.
When Hornung's men bought, he sold again.

The Pit, already turbulent, became convulsed.

A hundred mouths opened; a hundred hands sprang above the
ring; hats were crushed, coats torn open, collars twisted. Men
leaned shoulder to shoulder, yelling into one another's faces. They
bought with thrust-out fingers, sold with fists flung downward; they
climbed upon the steps, slipped, recovered, drove forward again. The
floor under them shook with the grind of their heels.

"May at twelve!"

"Sold!"

"Ten at eleven seven!"

"Take it!"

"Sold! Sold!"

The unknown broker stood near the centre, red-faced and perspiring,
his orders folded in one hand. Whenever the market rallied,
he struck it. Twenty thousand—thirty thousand—fifty thousand bushels poured upon
the Pit.

In Hornung's booth the telephone bells rang without pause.

"Who is it?" demanded Going, his chief broker.

"Can't find out."

"How much has he got?"

"Blamed if I know. He acts as if he's got
a million."

Going seized the transmitter.

"Wire the Chief. Say there's wheat coming from somewhere. We're
supporting at eleven and a half."

The message reached Hornung in his office. For the first
time during the campaign the Bull hesitated. By every report,
every warehouse statement, every railway return, he controlled the supply.
Yet wheat was being offered—real wheat, apparently, not merely short
contracts. If an unsuspected reserve existed, his corner might be
no corner at all.

"Buy it," he ordered. "Buy every bushel. And find where
it comes from."

Back in the Pit the battle swelled. Hornung's brokers supported
the market at a dollar eleven, then at a dollar
ten and three-quarters. The sellers came on. The Bear was
attacking now, driving with his head down, ripping at the
Bull's flank. Each fraction lost meant panic among the smaller
followers of the bull clique. One broke ranks and sold;
then another. Wheat touched a dollar ten.

Going's face had turned grey.

"Support it!" he shouted. "One ten for May! One ten!
I'll take all you've got!"

"All?"

"All!"

"Sold twenty-five!"

"Sold fifty!"

"Sold a hundred!"

The word repeated itself from mouth to mouth, one enormous
detonation—"Sold! Sold! Sold!" The Pit ceased to be a gathering
of men. It was one beast, many-headed and blind, roaring
over its food.

At noon a messenger forced his way into Hornung's office
with three telegrams and a railway map.

The secretary spread them upon the desk.

"We've traced the cars."

"Well?"

"They were billed east, all right. But at Danville the
bills were cancelled. The grain went south over the Wabash
connection, then north again under new numbers. It came back
to Chicago this morning."

"Whose orders?"

"Truslow's. Through the Central Forwarding Company."

For one instant Hornung stared at the map. Then his
uncertainty vanished.

There was no hidden supply. It was his own wheat—the
hundred thousand bushels sold under contract for export. Truslow had
turned it on the rails, disguised it with altered consignments,
and brought it back to stab him.

Hornung's fist descended upon the desk.

"Get an injunction. Attach every car. Notify the inspectors that
the receipts are disputed. Then wire Going: 'Supply limited to
one hundred thousand. Truslow wheat. Buy it all.'"

The telegram reached the Pit at twelve minutes past twelve.

Going read it once, climbed upon the rail of the
booth and bellowed:

"It's Truslow's hundred thousand! That's all there is! Buy! Buy
and lift her!"

Hornung's men charged.

They took ten thousand at a dollar ten, twenty thousand
at a dollar ten and one-eighth, thirty thousand at a
dollar ten and a half. The unknown broker faltered. His
orders diminished. The Bear's ammunition, so cunningly stolen, was being
swallowed in mouthfuls.

The price rose.

"One eleven!"

"One twelve!"

"One thirteen for May!"

Shorts stampeded to cover. Men who five minutes before had
sold now fought to buy back. The Bull came on,
huge, irresistible, goring the broken market upward. At the closing
gong May wheat stood at a dollar fourteen and one-quarter.

Truslow's attack was smashed. The Great Bear had failed.

## IV. THE BREAD LINE

That same evening Sam Lewiston stood in a line before
the rear door of a mission on West Madison Street.

There were perhaps two hundred men ahead of him. Some
wore caps pulled over their ears; some had wrapped newspapers
around their shoes. The line moved slowly toward a basement
window where a man with rolled sleeves passed out bowls
of coffee and thick pieces of bread.

Sam had worked at the freight yards until January. Then
business slackened. He had shoveled coal for nine days, carried
bricks for four, swept a saloon, unloaded hides and slept
twice in a police station. Emma earned three dollars a
week sewing vests, but the rent was six dollars a
month, and bread had risen again.

Wheat at a dollar fourteen meant five-cent loaves reduced in
weight, flour beyond their means, and hunger measured by fractions.

The man in front of Sam turned.

"They say wheat went up four cents today."

Sam nodded.

"Fellow named Hornung's got it cornered."

"So I hear."

"Farmers ought to be doing well."

Sam looked at the bread window, at the white slab
of loaf under the gaslight.

"Maybe," he said.

The line advanced. Above the roofs, in the direction of
La Salle Street, the upper stories of the office buildings
shone brilliantly. There the victors were reckoning profits, the vanquished
arranging loans, and clerks were recording in ledgers the tremendous
combat of Bull and Bear.

Sam reached the window. The attendant thrust a bowl toward
him and cut a piece of bread.

Six months before wheat had been so cheap that it
drove him from his land. Now it was so dear
that he must accept charity to eat it.

He took the bread in both hands. Behind him the
long line pressed forward, silent, patient, hungry.

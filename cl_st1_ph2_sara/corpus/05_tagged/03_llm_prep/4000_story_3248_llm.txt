# The Trouble at Bramble Bank

I have sometimes told you stories about tidy animals who
shut their doors before dusk, polish their spoons on Saturdays,
and never accept supper from a person whose boots are
muddy.

This is not one of those stories.

Tommy Brock was a badger. He was broad in the
back, short in the temper, and exceedingly dirty. His coat
was black and grey; his waistcoat had once been brown,
but was now of no particular colour. He wore heavy
boots, slept wherever he pleased, and grinned at remarks that
were not amusing.

Nobody in the wood liked Tommy Brock.

To tell the truth, Tommy Brock liked nobody either—unless they
happened to provide him with supper.

One warm afternoon he came stumping through the hazel coppice
towards Bramble Bank, where old Mr. Bouncer lived in a
comfortable burrow beneath an ash tree. The entrance was concealed
by bracken; there was a brass knocker upon the inner
door, and three shelves in the pantry, though one of
them had fallen down.

Old Mr. Bouncer was a rabbit of sociable habits. He
had been left in charge of his son Benjamin's seven
babies while Benjamin and Flopsy went across the fields to
gather young lettuces and purchase a paper of sugar.

The seven babies were asleep in a large rush basket
near the fire.

At least six of them were asleep. The smallest one
had its eyes open and was chewing the corner of
the blanket.

Mr. Bouncer was rocking himself beside the hearth and smoking
a little pipe. He had promised Flopsy that he would
remain awake.

"Certainly, my dear," he had said. "You may depend upon
me."

This was a statement displaying more confidence than accuracy.

There came a knock—BANG, BANG, BANG—upon the door.

Mr. Bouncer sat up.

"Who is there?"

"Only me," answered a rough voice.

That was not a satisfactory answer; but Mr. Bouncer, who
was curious as well as sociable, opened the door.

Tommy Brock stood on the threshold with a sack over
one shoulder and a bottle under his arm.

"Good afternoon," said Tommy Brock, without taking off his hat.
"I was passing."

He was not passing. He had come on purpose because
he had smelt rabbit.

Mr. Bouncer looked at the bottle.

"What have you there?"

"Elderberry wine," said Tommy Brock. "A present from an acquaintance."

This acquaintance had not intended to make the present.

Mr. Bouncer hesitated. He ought to have shut the door.
Instead, he moved aside.

"Well, perhaps you had better come in for one glass."

Tommy Brock came in for several.

He put down his sack behind the settle, where it
lay flat and innocent-looking. Then he sat at the table,
spread his elbows, and looked around the burrow.

"A pleasant little place," he remarked.

"Very snug," said Mr. Bouncer.

"Plenty of room."

"Quite sufficient."

"For a family."

Mr. Bouncer laughed proudly. "My grandchildren are staying with me."

"I noticed a basket," said Tommy Brock.

The smallest baby stopped chewing the blanket.

Old Mr. Bouncer fetched two blue cups. He also fetched
bread, butter, pickled onions, and a cold turnip pie. Hospitality
is a charming quality, but it should not be exercised
without discretion.

They ate the pie. They ate the bread and butter.
Tommy Brock ate all the pickled onions except one, which
he dropped into his pocket.

Then they opened the elderberry wine.

It was very strong.

Mr. Bouncer's ears became warm. He told Tommy Brock a
long story about a scarecrow. Tommy Brock did not listen,
but he nodded at intervals.

After the second cup, Mr. Bouncer told the same story
again.

After the third cup, he forgot the scarecrow and sang
part of a song.

Tommy Brock produced a twist of black tobacco.

"Try this," he said.

Mr. Bouncer filled his pipe. The tobacco was stronger than
the wine.

The burrow became hazy. The fire made a red glow
upon the dishes, the brass candlestick, and Tommy Brock's broad
unpleasant grin.

"Dear me," murmured Mr. Bouncer. "I feel a trifle—"

He did not finish the sentence.

His pipe slipped from his paw. His head sank sideways
against the cushion. His spectacles slid down his nose.

Presently he began to snore.

Tommy Brock waited.

He waited until the clock ticked twelve times—not because it
was midnight, but because the clock was broken and struck
whenever it felt inclined. Then he rose quietly.

The seven babies were still in the basket.

Tommy Brock lifted the blanket.

Fourteen little ears twitched.

"What a convenient number," he said.

He fetched the sack from behind the settle. One by
one, he placed the babies inside. They were sleepy, warm,
and bewildered. The smallest one tried to bite his thumb.

Tommy Brock tied the sack with cord.

Then, being a badger of odious habits, he helped himself
to Mr. Bouncer's best handkerchief, two candles, the remaining half
of the turnip pie, and a pewter salt-cellar.

He left the pickled onion on the table.

It was almost evening when Benjamin Bunny and Flopsy came
home. Benjamin carried a bundle of lettuces; Flopsy had the
sugar and a bunch of thyme.

They found the door open.

The fire was low. The table was covered with cups,
crumbs, ash, and one lonely onion. Old Mr. Bouncer was
asleep in the rocking-chair.

The rush basket was empty.

Flopsy dropped the sugar.

"Where are the children?"

Mr. Bouncer opened one eye.

"What children?"

"Our children!"

Mr. Bouncer opened the other eye. He stared at the
basket. Then he stared beneath the table, behind the curtains,
and inside the coal-scuttle.

"I distinctly remember," he said, "that there were seven."

"There still ought to be seven!" cried Flopsy.

Old Mr. Bouncer stood up too quickly and sat down
again.

"There was a visitor," he murmured.

"What visitor?" demanded Benjamin.

"A civil fellow. Broad shoulders. Striped face. Brought wine."

"Tommy Brock!"

Flopsy made such a sound that Mr. Bouncer's ears flattened.

Benjamin ran out of the burrow. The ground beneath the
ash tree was dry and dusty. Across it went the
marks of heavy boots—down the bank, through the nettles, and
towards the coppice.

Beside the boot-marks were little scratches where something bulky had
dragged against the earth.

"The sack!" said Benjamin. "He has carried them away in
the sack!"

He was all of a twitter. He ran back to
the door; he ran forward again; he turned in a
circle and almost collided with the lettuces.

Flopsy had recovered the sugar packet and was tying on
her bonnet.

"You must fetch Cousin Peter."

"Yes—Peter. Peter will know what to do."

"And you must hurry."

"I am hurrying!"

Benjamin hurried so violently that he took the wrong path,
climbed over a stile, discovered a bull in the meadow,
and climbed back again. By the time he reached Peter
Rabbit's burrow, his jacket was torn and one shoe was
full of burrs.

Peter was sorting onions in a basket.

"Cousin Peter! Cousin Peter! Tommy Brock has taken the babies!"

Peter put down an onion.

"How many?"

"All seven!"

"That is serious."

"They were left with my father—there was wine—he fell asleep—there
is a sack—and Flopsy says we must hurry!"

Peter fetched his coat.

"Has it rained since yesterday?"

"No."

"Then there will be a trail."

This was the difference between Peter and Benjamin. Benjamin could
feel seven alarming things at once; Peter generally considered one
thing at a time.

They returned to Bramble Bank. Peter inspected the doorway, the
dusty ground, the broken nettles, and a grey hair caught
upon a bramble.

"Badger," he said.

"We know it was a badger!"

"It is well to know a thing twice when babies
are concerned."

The boot-marks led down into the wood.

Evening had settled between the trees. The tops of the
beeches were golden, but beneath them the paths were brown
and dim. Gnats danced over a pool. Somewhere in the
distance a woodpecker tapped—tap, tap, tap—and then stopped, as though
it had heard something disagreeable.

Peter and Benjamin followed the track.

Tommy Brock had walked heavily. Here he had crushed a
fern; there he had brushed against a patch of wild
garlic. Once he had put down the sack beside a
pollard willow. The grass was flattened, and a short piece
of cord lay on the ground.

Benjamin picked it up.

"It is the same cord!"

"Very likely," said Peter.

"Do you suppose they are alive?"

"I suppose we had better continue before discussing what we
do not know."

They went on and on.

The track climbed above the stream, crossed a stony lane,
and entered a field of flowering clover. The scent of
the badger was mixed with clover, damp soil, and the
sharp smell of crushed sorrel. Rabbits have excellent noses; this
is useful, though it does not always lead them towards
pleasant discoveries.

At the far side of the field they met Mrs.
Tiggy-Winkle carrying a bundle of washing.

"Good evening," said Peter. "Have you seen Tommy Brock?"

"I saw a dirty person with a sack," she replied.
"He asked whether I knew of a quiet lodging. I
told him I did not take lodgers, especially in boots."

"Which way did he go?"

"Up the cart-road towards the crags."

Benjamin shivered.

Mr. Tod owned several houses near the crags.

Mr. Tod was a fox with sandy whiskers, narrow legs,
and an excessive opinion of his own importance. He kept
houses in different parts of the wood so that he
might never be inconvenienced by staying in one place too
long.

He had six houses, but was seldom at home.

One house had a slate roof. One had a door
but no windows. One had windows but no proper chimney.
The largest stood below the crags among pine trees. Its
front garden was full of nettles, and its back door
was secured by an iron bar.

At that season Mr. Tod was believed to be visiting
his house beside the quarry.

Tommy Brock was aware of this.

Peter and Benjamin climbed the cart-road. The moon rose pale
behind the pines. Jays scolded overhead; then an owl called
from the valley.

At the first fox-house they found nothing except a smell
of damp straw and three feathers.

At the second house, Benjamin saw boot-marks near the well.

"He has been here!"

"He has fetched water," said Peter. "But he has gone
on."

"How can you tell?"

"The print is deeper on the outward side. The pail
was full."

They climbed higher.

The third house stood beneath a rocky ledge. Its windows
shone red in the last of the sunset.

Smoke came from the chimney.

Benjamin stopped so suddenly that Peter walked into him.

"There!"

"Yes," said Peter. "There."

They crept through the nettles and looked into the parlour
window.

No one was in the parlour.

There was a round table, two horsehair chairs, a stuffed
magpie under glass, and a picture of Mr. Tod's uncle
in a gilt frame. Dust lay everywhere except upon a
path leading to the kitchen.

The kitchen window was too high. Peter climbed upon a
rain barrel; Benjamin climbed upon Peter; the barrel rolled half
a turn, and both rabbits fell into the tansy.

They tried again more carefully.

Through the kitchen window they saw Tommy Brock.

He was sitting at the table eating Mr. Tod's food.
He had found a loaf, a jar of honey, and
a dish of eggs. He ate noisily, dipping bread into
the honey and wiping his paws upon the tablecloth.

The sack lay on the floor.

It was empty.

Benjamin made a faint squeak.

Peter put a paw over his mouth.

Against the wall stood a large black cooking oven with
an iron door. From within it came the tiniest rustle.

Then a little voice said, "Mother?"

Benjamin trembled from his whiskers to his heels.

"They are alive!"

Tommy Brock turned his head.

The rabbits ducked below the window.

There was silence.

After a moment Tommy Brock began to hum. He carried
the empty sack into the passage; then he came back
and put a kettle on the fire.

Peter cautiously looked again.

The oven door was secured by a metal catch. A
heavy chair had been pushed against it. On the dresser
lay a knife, a rolling-pin, a pie dish, and a
bunch of parsley.

These objects suggested preparations for a supper of an objectionable
character.

"We must get in," whispered Benjamin.

They went to the back door. It was barred from
inside.

They tried the front door. It was locked.

They examined the windows. The parlour sash was painted shut;
the pantry window was too narrow; the bedroom window upstairs
was open, but there was no ivy upon the wall
and rabbits are not flies.

Benjamin pulled at the kitchen door until his paws hurt.

"Stop," said Peter. "He will hear."

"But the babies are in the oven!"

"Yes. Therefore we must not cause Tommy Brock to put
anything under the oven."

This was a dreadful observation, but a sensible one.

They crouched beneath the kitchen window. Presently Tommy Brock carried
the candle upstairs. Floorboards creaked over their heads.

Then came a thump.

Then another thump.

Then a long rolling snore—

Hrrr-rrr-rrr-rrr...

Tommy Brock had gone to sleep in Mr. Tod's bed.

He had not removed his boots.

Peter listened.

"He is asleep."

"Let us break the glass."

"He may wake."

"We cannot leave them!"

"No. We shall go underneath."

Peter had noticed that the kitchen floor stood above a
shallow bank. The earth was soft near the wall, where
rainwater dripped from a broken gutter.

Rabbits understand digging.

They began beneath a clump of hart's-tongue fern. Peter loosened
the soil; Benjamin kicked it backwards. Then Benjamin dug while
Peter cleared away stones and roots. They dug and dug.

Inside the house, Tommy Brock snored.

Hrrr-rrr-rrr...

Sometimes the snoring stopped. At once the rabbits stopped too,
crouching in their tunnel with earth upon their noses.

Then the snoring began again.

Hrrr-rrr-rrr...

They dug deeper.

The night became cold. Moonlight lay white upon the crags,
and mist gathered in the hollow below. Once an owl
swooped across the garden without making a sound. Once a
dog barked from a distant farm.

From the oven came an occasional muffled whimper.

"Courage," whispered Benjamin through the wall. "Father is here."

One of the babies sneezed.

"Quiet!" whispered six other babies.

The tunnel passed beneath the foundation stones. Roots hung from
its roof. Peter struck something hard.

"The floor," he said.

They scraped upwards. A little dust sifted down.

It was nearly dawn when Peter's claws touched the underside
of a kitchen board.

"We are through to the floor," he said. "But not
through the floor."

They had no saw. Benjamin had brought nothing except the
piece of cord and a great many anxieties.

"We can gnaw it," he said.

They gnawed. The plank was old oak and had a
flavour of grease, dust, and fox.

It was very disagreeable.

Above them Tommy Brock continued to snore in the best
tester bed.

But soon there came another sound—far off at first, then
nearer.

Tap—tap—tap.

Peter stopped gnawing.

Boots were coming up the stony road.

These were not the broad, dragging boots of Tommy Brock.
They were lighter and quicker.

A thin shadow moved between the pines.

Mr. Tod had come home.

He wore a green coat and carried a walking-stick with
a silver knob. His sandy whiskers were neatly brushed. Over
one arm hung a basket containing a fish, a paper
parcel, and a bottle of cleaning fluid.

Mr. Tod expected to find his house exactly as he
had left it.

He first noticed smoke from the chimney.

His whiskers stiffened.

Then he noticed the boot-marks in the garden.

His tail became twice its ordinary size.

He approached the front door, unlocked it, and entered without
speaking.

The parlour was dusty as usual. The kitchen was not.

Mr. Tod stared at the crumbs, honey, eggshells, muddy marks,
damp towels, and his best tablecloth, which Tommy Brock had
used to clean a frying-pan.

Mr. Tod's feelings were too deep for immediate expression.

He sniffed.

"Badger."

He went upstairs.

The bedroom door stood open.

Tommy Brock lay across the tester bed, fully dressed, with
his boots upon the embroidered counterpane. His mouth was open.
His paws rested upon Mr. Tod's pillow.

Hrrr-rrr-rrr...

Mr. Tod stood in the doorway.

He did not shout.

He did not rush in.

He withdrew quietly and shut the door.

Down in the tunnel, Peter and Benjamin heard Mr. Tod
moving about. They heard cupboard doors open and shut, the
clink of bottles, the scrape of a chair, and the
dragging of something heavy across the floor.

"What is he doing?" breathed Benjamin.

"Something elaborate," said Peter.

Mr. Tod went first to the wash-house. He fetched a
pail, a length of rope, and a wooden pulley used
for drying sheets. Then he fetched a gimlet, three nails,
a hammer, and a ball of string.

He carried everything upstairs.

The bedroom ceiling had a beam above the bed. Mr.
Tod fixed the pulley to the beam. He passed the
rope through it and tied one end to the handle
of the pail.

The pail was large.

He took it downstairs and filled it at the pump.

Then he reconsidered.

Plain water seemed insufficient.

Mr. Tod added half a bottle of cleaning fluid, two
scoops of washing powder, and something from a jar marked
FOR MOTHS. He stirred it with the fire shovel.

It foamed.

Mr. Tod carried the pail upstairs, spilling a little on
each step. He hauled it up by the pulley until
it hung directly above Tommy Brock's head.

Tommy Brock snored.

Mr. Tod tied the rope to a nail near the
door. To this rope he attached the string, and carried
the string downstairs, through the passage, and into the garden.

It was a handsome contraption. Mr. Tod admired it.

Then he remembered the embroidered counterpane.

He went upstairs, intending to remove it without disturbing Tommy
Brock.

The badger lay very still.

Mr. Tod reached towards the bed.

Tommy Brock gave such a sudden snort that Mr. Tod
leapt backwards and knocked over the wash-basin.

The wash-basin rolled in a circle.

Tommy Brock sighed and turned over.

Mr. Tod withdrew. The counterpane would have to suffer.

In the tunnel, Benjamin was becoming frantic.

"We must break through now!"

"Not while Mr. Tod is in the kitchen."

"What if he opens the oven?"

Peter sniffed through a crack in the floor.

"I think he is concerned with the bedroom."

Mr. Tod returned to the garden and tested the string.
The pail tilted slightly.

He smiled.

Then he frowned.

The pail might merely wet Tommy Brock. Mr. Tod desired
a more decisive effect.

He went to the kitchen, fetched a heavy warming-pan, and
carried it upstairs. He balanced it on the tester rail
so that the falling pail would knock the warming-pan upon
the sleeping badger.

Then he fetched a brass candlestick.

Then a flat-iron.

Then he decided that too many objects might spoil the
arrangement. He removed the candlestick but left the flat-iron.

All this while Tommy Brock appeared to be asleep.

But he was NOT SNORING ANY LONGER.

Mr. Tod did not observe this. He was beneath the
bed, adjusting the rope.

As soon as the fox's tail disappeared through the doorway,
Tommy Brock opened one eye.

He looked up at the pail.

He looked at the warming-pan.

He looked towards the door.

Then he grinned.

When Mr. Tod went downstairs to fetch a sheet for
protecting the carpet, Tommy Brock got out of bed. He
moved quickly for such a fat person.

He took Mr. Tod's dressing gown from behind the door,
rolled it around two pillows, and tucked the sleeves beneath
the blanket. He placed Mr. Tod's nightcap at the top
and arranged a black hearth-brush across it.

In the dim room it resembled a sleeping badger—at least,
it resembled one sufficiently to deceive an angry fox standing
in the garden.

Tommy Brock then slipped behind the bedroom door.

Mr. Tod came back with the sheet. He spread it
upon the carpet, glanced at the bed, and saw nothing
amiss.

The bundled dressing gown lay still.

Mr. Tod crept downstairs and went outside. Dawn had turned
the eastern sky pale green. The pines were black above
the mist.

He took hold of the string.

"One pull," he whispered, "and there will be an improvement
in my household."

He pulled.

Upstairs, the nail came free.

The pail tipped.

There was an enormous SPLASH—followed by a CLANG, a THUMP,
a tearing noise, and the crash of the wash-stand.

Water poured through the bedroom floor and dripped into the
kitchen.

Mr. Tod's eyes shone.

He waited.

There was no snoring.

There was no movement.

"Excellent," said Mr. Tod.

He went indoors, taking care not to slip upon the
cleaning fluid that ran down the stairs. He paused outside
the bedroom.

The bundle beneath the bedclothes did not stir. The nightcap
was flattened; the hearth-brush hung over the pillow.

Mr. Tod poked it with his walking-stick.

Nothing happened.

He poked it again.

Still nothing.

Mr. Tod began to consider disposal. Badgers are heavy. There
were stairs, a passage, three steps at the door, and
a steep hill beyond the garden. He fetched the empty
sack from the kitchen, though it was plainly too small.

He returned to the bedroom and pulled back the blanket.

There was Mr. Tod's dressing gown.

Mr. Tod stared.

Behind him somebody cleared his throat.

Tommy Brock sat in the armchair beside the window, drinking
tea from Mr. Tod's flowered cup.

"Good morning," said Tommy Brock.

Mr. Tod spun around.

"You!"

"Did you expect someone else?"

"You filthy trespasser!"

"You have spilt water on your bed," said Tommy Brock.
"A lamentable accident."

Mr. Tod's whiskers bristled until they resembled a bottle-brush.

"That is my cup."

"It holds tea very well."

"That is my chair."

"It is rather hard."

"Those are my boots upon my counterpane!"

"They are my boots," said Tommy Brock. "The counterpane may
be yours."

Mr. Tod sprang.

Tommy Brock threw the tea.

Mr. Tod ducked; the cup struck the wall. Tommy Brock
seized the warming-pan. Mr. Tod caught up the flat-iron.

For one moment they stood facing each other—the fox thin
and furious, the badger broad and grinning.

Then the bedroom became uninhabitable.

They went round the bed, under the bed, and over
the bed. The tester curtains came down. The pail rolled
between Mr. Tod's legs. Tommy Brock struck the wardrobe with
the warming-pan; Mr. Tod struck the warming-pan with the flat-iron;
the wardrobe door opened and emptied hats upon both of
them.

They fell into the passage.

They rolled down three stairs, stopped, bit each other, and
rolled down the remaining nine.

In the kitchen, Peter and Benjamin heard the whole house
shake.

"Now!" said Peter.

They gnawed upwards with all their strength. The weakened board
split. Peter pushed his head through; then one shoulder; then
the other.

He emerged beneath the kitchen table in a shower of
dust.

Benjamin followed.

At that instant Mr. Tod and Tommy Brock burst through
the kitchen door.

Peter and Benjamin remained perfectly still beneath the hanging tablecloth.

Mr. Tod had lost his coat. Tommy Brock wore the
torn tester curtain around his neck. They collided with the
table, overturned two chairs, and crashed against the dresser.

Plates slid from the shelves.

One broke.

Two broke.

Then ALL the plates broke.

Mr. Tod seized the rolling-pin. Tommy Brock took the bread
board. Mr. Tod threw the rolling-pin; Tommy Brock caught it
and put it into his pocket, which was unnecessary but
characteristic.

They rushed into the pantry.

There was a crash of jars.

They rushed out again covered with flour.

Tommy Brock was white from nose to waistcoat; Mr. Tod
had treacle upon one ear.

The fox sprang at the badger. The badger stepped aside.
Mr. Tod flew through the back door and landed in
the rain barrel.

Tommy Brock jumped after him.

The rain barrel split.

Water, fox, badger, and barrel hoops rolled across the garden
and down the hill.

They fought through the nettles, over the cart-road, and into
a patch of blue hyacinths. The flowers waved and closed
behind them. Their growls became fainter; then louder; then fainter
again.

Peter crawled from beneath the table.

"Quickly."

Benjamin was already at the oven.

He pulled the chair away. Peter lifted the iron catch.
The door stuck because Tommy Brock had wedged it with
a spoon.

They pulled.

They pulled again.

The spoon snapped.

The oven door opened.

Inside were seven small rabbits, crowded together among two folded
towels and a pie dish. They were dusty and frightened,
but otherwise unhurt.

"Father!" cried the eldest.

"Father!" cried the others.

The smallest one merely sneezed.

Benjamin gathered them into his arms, which was difficult because
there were seven and they all wished to be held
at once.

Peter looked around.

"We cannot carry them openly. The fox or badger may
return."

"The sack!"

Tommy Brock's sack lay in the passage. Peter shook out
crumbs, a candle end, and Mr. Bouncer's handkerchief. They lined
it with the oven towels and placed the babies gently
inside.

They did not tie the top tightly.

"This is only for travelling," Benjamin explained. "You are not
being kidnapped again."

The babies accepted this distinction after some discussion.

Peter took one side of the sack and Benjamin took
the other. It was heavy. Seven baby rabbits weigh more
than one expects, especially when two of them argue and
another insists upon looking out.

They carried it through the garden.

Down the hill, bushes shook and stones rattled. Mr. Tod
shouted something about his crockery. Tommy Brock laughed.

The rabbits went the other way.

They hurried down the cart-road, past the empty fox-houses, across
the clover field, and into the coppice. Morning sunlight touched
the upper branches. The mist thinned over the stream; spiders'
webs shone upon the grass.

At the pollard willow they stopped to rest.

"Are they all there?" asked Benjamin.

Peter counted the ears at the mouth of the sack.

"One, two, three, four, five, six—"

Benjamin turned pale.

The smallest baby crawled out from beneath the folded towel.

"Seven," said Peter.

They went on.

Mrs. Tiggy-Winkle met them near the hazel wood.

"What have you in that sack?" she asked.

"Laundry," said Peter, because he did not wish to explain.

The sack wriggled.

"Very lively laundry," observed Mrs. Tiggy-Winkle.

At Bramble Bank, Flopsy had not slept. She sat near
the doorway with her bonnet still on. Old Mr. Bouncer
sat in the farthest corner, looking small and ashamed.

When Flopsy saw Peter and Benjamin carrying the sack, she
ran down the path.

"Are they—?"

The sack began to squeak.

"Mother!"

Flopsy untied it herself.

Out came the babies—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven.
They tumbled over one another, clung to her skirts, and
all began speaking at the same time.

"There was an oven—"

"Tommy Brock ate honey—"

"Father dug through the floor—"

"Mr. Tod fell into the water—"

"There was a spoon in the door—"

"The sack was not so bad the second time—"

"I sneezed."

Flopsy counted them twice and kissed them all. Benjamin tried
to tell the adventure, but he had become so tired
that his account began with the tunnel, returned to the
elderberry wine, and ended with the bull in the wrong
meadow.

Peter gave a clearer version while brushing earth from his
coat.

Old Mr. Bouncer approached slowly.

He carried the pickled onion upon a saucer.

"I am afraid," he said, "that I displayed a serious
want of judgment."

Flopsy looked at him.

Mr. Bouncer's ears drooped.

"A very serious want of judgment," he repeated.

The seven babies looked at their grandfather. The smallest climbed
into his lap.

Flopsy sighed.

"You are forgiven—but you must never entertain Tommy Brock again."

"I shall not entertain anybody," promised Mr. Bouncer, "without a
written character and two references."

This was perhaps going too far; but promises made after
calamities are often extensive.

They all went inside.

Flopsy prepared breakfast. There was fresh bread, lettuce, oat porridge,
and hot milk for the babies. Peter and Benjamin were
given large cups of tea. Old Mr. Bouncer was given
weak tea and no tobacco.

He did not complain.

After breakfast the babies fell asleep together in the rush
basket. Benjamin sat beside them, though he also fell asleep.
Flopsy mended the torn sack into a hearth-rug, which was
a practical use for an objectionable object.

Peter remained long enough to eat another piece of bread.

From far beyond the coppice came a faint crash.

Then a fox barked angrily.

Then there was a deep badger laugh.

Peter listened.

"Do you think they are still fighting?" asked Flopsy.

"Probably," said Peter. "They have several matters to settle—the bed,
the crockery, the tea cup, and the treacle."

"Will Mr. Tod come here?"

"Not today. He is too occupied."

This was correct.

Mr. Tod and Tommy Brock fought down the hill, across
the stream, through a gooseberry hedge, and into another of
Mr. Tod's houses. What happened there is not properly part
of this story. It is sufficient to say that the

Mr. Tod afterwards purchased an extraordinary quantity of soap.

Tommy Brock was seen a week later sleeping under a
hayrick. He still had the rolling-pin in his pocket.

As for old Mr. Bouncer, he behaved with remarkable caution
for nearly a month. He locked the door at sunset,
refused three harmless visitors, and questioned the postman through the
keyhole.

Then one fine evening Peter found him smoking his little
pipe beneath the ash tree.

"It is only mild tobacco," explained Mr. Bouncer.

Peter sat down beside him.

The seven babies played in the bracken, never going beyond
the white stone at the end of the path. Flopsy
watched from the doorway. Benjamin carried in a basket of
lettuces and counted the babies whenever he passed.

One, two, three, four, five, six, seven.

The evening was warm. Bees moved among the thyme. Above
the wood, the sky turned gold; beneath the trees, the
shadows gathered quietly around the burrows and roots.

Beyond the hill lay Mr. Tod's houses, dark and untidy.
Somewhere among the pines Tommy Brock was perhaps grinning.

But at Bramble Bank the door was strong, the fire
was bright, and everybody who belonged at home was safely
there.

Old Mr. Bouncer tapped out his pipe.

"I believe," he said, "that I shall tell the children
a story."

"Not the one about the scarecrow," said Benjamin.

"No," replied Mr. Bouncer. "I think I shall tell them
about the dangers of accepting elderberry wine from strangers."

This was a suitable story.

It was also, at last, one which old Mr. Bouncer
was qualified to tell.

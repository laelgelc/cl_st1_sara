I RECOLLECT

I recollect the forest first.

Of all that day—the shouting, the smoke, the officers galloping
with drawn swords, the long thunder of the guns—I recollect
most clearly a little branch of hazel which struck me
across the mouth as I ran. It was wet with
dew, though the morning had already grown hot, and one
of its leaves adhered for a moment to my lip.
I brushed it away. Then I was among the trees,
running with the others, and the branch sprang back into
its place as though no man had passed.

How strange that it should remain in my memory! Men
were dying beside me, yet I recollect the leaf.

"Fo-o-r-wa-ard!"

The command rolled through the wood. It seemed to come
from before us, behind us, from the earth, from the
smoke. We ran. Our boots tore the moss; dry twigs
snapped beneath us; rifles struck against cartridge-boxes; someone laughed, and
someone cried upon God. The bullets hummed in a high,
spiteful manner among the leaves. One cut a twig above
my head, and the pale wood showed where the bark
had been shorn away.

I had volunteered. I must say this, for at that
time the word appeared important to me. I was not
taken from my village by force; I was not dragged
weeping from my mother's threshold. I had offered myself. I
had spoken of the fatherland, of our Christian brethren, of
honor. I had imagined banners, music, noble wounds, and the
grateful eyes of those whom we should deliver.

Now I ran with my tongue dry in my mouth,
my cap slipping backward, and one thought only in my
brain: Do not fall. Keep with them. Do not be
the last.

A man on my right threw up both hands. His
rifle flew forward, turning once in the air, and he
dropped upon his face. I knew him. He was a
little clerk from Kalouga who had lost two fingers in
a printing machine, and who sang in a thin voice
after supper. I leaped over him. There was no time
to stop. Another stumbled, clutching at my sleeve, and for
three steps I dragged him. Then his fingers opened and
I went on.

"Hurrah!"

We shouted because the others shouted. We shouted because if
the mouth were not employed in shouting it might cry
out in terror. The sound became enormous under the trees.

Ahead of us, through the smoke, figures moved in grey
and brown. A flash, then another. The man before me
spun halfway round. Blood appeared upon his cheek as suddenly
as a red ribbon pinned there. He sat down very
carefully against a trunk and stared at me.

Still we ran.

I recollect an open place where the sun lay fiercely
upon crushed fern. Beyond it was a low bank with
thorn bushes, and from behind the bank the Turks fired.
Their smoke rose in separate white puffs. Our line broke
upon the trees, gathered itself, and poured toward them.

Then everything was close—too close for thought. Faces, hands, metal,
open mouths. The bayonets struck branches and rifle barrels. A
Turk came through the smoke before me.

He was young. I know now that he was young;
then I saw only eyes. They were broad, black, and
full of such terror as no beast has ever shown
me. His cap had fallen away. Sweat ran from his
curled hair along one temple. He held his rifle across
his body, not aiming it, merely holding it as a
drowning man holds a plank.

For an instant we looked at one another.

He said something. Perhaps it was a prayer; perhaps he
called to his mother. I could not understand.

My hands acted.

I thrust the bayonet forward. It entered beneath his ribs
with a resistance at once soft and dreadful. He bent
toward me, and his breath touched my cheek. His rifle
fell. I tried to pull my weapon free, but it
would not come. I put my boot against him and
tugged. Then it came loose, and he sank sideways among
the fern.

I went on.

Did I shout? I think I shouted. Perhaps I shouted
"Hurrah!" Perhaps I cursed him. I do not recollect. There
are moments which memory preserves with monstrous exactness—the sweat upon
his temple, one broken lace upon his boot—and others which
it has covered in darkness.

We crossed the bank. Men were fleeing among the trees.
Our officer waved his sword and cried:

"After them! Fo-o-r-wa-ard!"

I had taken scarcely ten steps when something struck my
left leg below the knee. It was not pain at
first, but a violent blow, as if an iron bar
had been swung against me. I staggered. Almost at the
same instant there came another blow in the right thigh,
hotter and heavier than the first.

The earth rose.

No—that is not true. The earth did not rise; I
fell. Yet it appeared to me that the whole green
forest lifted itself and struck my face. My rifle went
from my hands. I heard the shouting pass over and
beyond me. Boots ran near my head. One boot trod
upon my fingers, and I felt no anger. Then the
smoke became black, the trees leaned together, and all things
were extinguished.

When I opened my eyes, I believed I was at
home.

There was green above me, and through it small pieces
of blue sky. An insect travelled along a blade of
grass close to my nose. Its back shone like polished
copper. I watched it climb, pause, wave its feelers, and
descend upon the other side. It seemed a most serious
business to the creature.

Then I smelt powder, blood, and trampled earth.

I attempted to move.

Pain burst through both legs and met in my chest.
I cried out. The cry was weak, almost a child's
cry, and immediately I lay still.

The battle had moved away. Musketry rattled far off, now
fiercely, now faintly, like rain passing over a distant roof.
Once there was a cheer. Once the deep voice of
a gun shook the leaves, and dust fell from them
upon my face. But near me all was quiet.

I lay in dense thorn bushes below the bank. Perhaps
when I fell I had rolled there; perhaps another man's
body had pushed me. The branches concealed me on three
sides. Through one opening I could see the trampled fern,
several cartridge papers, and a hand.

Only a hand at first.

It lay palm upward, the fingers a little curved. The
skin was brown; there was dirt beneath the nails. I
knew that hand. Or rather, I knew the sleeve: coarse
grey cloth, dark at the cuff with blood.

The Turk was beside me.

I turned my head with great difficulty. There he lay,
not two arshins distant, upon his back. The wound made
by my bayonet was hidden beneath him, but a broad
stain had spread across his tunic. His face was turned
toward me. His black eyes remained partly open, though they
no longer contained fear. There was astonishment in them—or I
imagined it. The mouth was open. Between the lips I
could see small, even teeth.

"So," I whispered, "thou hast followed me."

The sound of my own voice frightened me.

I listened for our men. Nothing. I called.

"Brothers! Here! I am wounded!"

No answer came except the faint crackle of firing beyond
the wood.

I called again, louder, and the effort caused my sight
to darken. The trees became long black pillars. The patch
of sky narrowed. I shut my eyes and waited until
the faintness passed.

They had gone on. Naturally they had gone on. A
regiment cannot stop because one volunteer lies in a bush.
Victory was before them; behind remained those who could no
longer shout or run.

How long had I been senseless? An hour? Three hours?
The sun stood high, but I could not see it.
Heat gathered under the bushes. My coat clung to my
back. Flies circled the blood upon my trousers, settled, crawled,
rose angrily when I struck at them, and returned.

My left leg was twisted. I felt that, though I
dared not look. The right burned from thigh to heel.
Each beat of my heart drove a nail into the
wounds.

I tried to sit up. I placed both hands upon
the earth and raised my shoulders. The movement dragged my
legs. A red mist filled my eyes, and I fell
back biting the sleeve of my coat lest I scream.

For some time—I cannot say how long—I thought only of
pain.

Pain is a tyrant without language. It does not persuade;
it commands. It contracts the world. There was no Russia,
no army, no victory, no mother, no future. There were
only my two legs, and in them a fire that
rose, sank, rose again, and would not consume what it
tormented.

Then thirst came.

At first it was merely dryness. I moved my tongue
and found my mouth sticky with powder and dust. Soon
the dryness became a living creature. It scratched my throat.
It swelled there. I could think of nothing but water.

My canteen!

I felt at my belt. The strap remained, but the
canteen was gone. It had perhaps been torn away when
I fell. I searched the ground with my hands, blindly,
among grass, twigs, stones, and empty cartridges. Nothing.

I laughed. Why did I laugh? A man who lies
shot through both legs, abandoned in an enemy's forest, laughs
because his canteen is lost. It was absurd; it was
terrible; therefore I laughed.

The Turk watched me.

"What dost thou say to it?" I asked him. "Thou
and I have made a fine campaign."

His eyes did not answer.

The sun moved. Shadows lengthened across the opening. The firing
became rarer. At last there was silence, and that silence
appeared greater than all the noise of battle. The flies
droned. An ant climbed upon my wrist and entered beneath
my cuff. Somewhere a woodpecker tapped at a tree—six strokes,
a pause, then six again.

Peace had returned to the forest. Peace! Beneath every bush
there might be a dead man; in the fern lay
rifles, caps, torn flesh; and the woodpecker continued its labor.

I looked again at my neighbor.

Why did I kill him?

The question came not as a thought formed by degrees,
but as though someone had whispered it close to my
ear.

Why did I kill him?

Because he was a Turk and I a Russian. Because
he stood before me. Because the command was forward. Because
if I had not thrust, he might have fired. All
these answers arose, and all were empty.

Wherein was he to blame? Had he injured me? Had
he burnt my house, struck my mother, insulted my bride?
I had never seen him until that instant. He was
afraid of me. I saw it. His whole soul was
in his eyes, crying, "Do not kill me!" And I
killed him.

Perhaps he, too, had been forced from a village. Perhaps
he possessed an old mother who even now counted the
days to his return. Perhaps there was a girl who
had tied a thread about his wrist. I searched with
my eyes and saw, beneath his cuff, a narrow cord
of faded blue.

A charm? A remembrance?

His mother might have touched it.

"Forgive me," I said.

The word sounded foolish beside a corpse.

Evening descended slowly. The sky visible through the leaves became
pale, then greenish, then of a deep black-blue. One star
appeared. I fixed my gaze upon it. Never had I
seen a star so bright. It trembled between two leaves,
disappeared when the wind stirred them, and returned.

Cold entered the wood. I had longed for coolness, but
when it came my teeth chattered. My wet clothing chilled
me. I tried to draw my coat around my breast
and discovered that one button remained; the rest had been
torn away.

I thought of home.

My mother sat near the lamp, spectacles low upon her
nose, mending a shirt. Anna stood at the window. She
had promised to marry me when I returned. When! I
had spoken that word confidently. "When I return, Anna, we
shall take the small house near the mill." She smiled,
but tears came into her eyes. I kissed them away
and told her that a man must do his duty.

What duty? To thrust steel into the belly of a
terrified stranger?

The night deepened. Sounds came from every side. Leaves whispered;
insects chirped; once something moved in the fern beyond the
opening and stopped when I called. I imagined Turks returning
to cut the throats of the wounded. I searched for
my rifle, but could not reach it. Then I remembered
that it lay where I had fallen—perhaps five paces away,
perhaps fifty; distances had ceased to possess meaning.

The Turk's rifle lay near his feet.

If men came, I could not take it. If wolves
came, I could not take it. I was as helpless
as an infant abandoned in a field.

Sleep approached, but pain drove it away. Again it approached.
I fell into a darkness filled with running men. The
hazel branch struck my mouth. The Turk stood before me,
but now he had the face of Anna. I thrust
at him, and my bayonet entered my mother's breast.

I woke screaming.

Moonlight had entered the opening. The dead man's face was
white, though by day it had been brown. His teeth
shone. I knew he was dead; still, I waited for
him to move.

"Thou art not angry?" I whispered. "It was war."

It was war. Men say this as though war were
an answer. A storm breaks trees, and we say, "It
was the wind." A fever takes a child: "It was
illness." But war has hands—our hands. It sees through our
eyes; it enters with our steel. Without us, what is
it? A word.

Toward morning I slept.

I awoke to brightness, heat, and thirst.

The second day had begun.

My tongue felt too large for my mouth. My lips
had split. I sucked the dew from leaves within reach,
but there was scarcely a drop upon them. I tore
grass and chewed it. Its bitter juice gave relief for
a moment, then made the thirst worse.

I examined my wounds. The left trouser leg was stiff
and black with dried blood. Below the knee there were
two holes, one in front and one behind. The bullet
had passed through. The foot lay at an unnatural angle,
yet I could move the toes. The right thigh was
swollen greatly. When I touched it, I felt a hard
track beneath the flesh. There was no exit wound.

This examination exhausted me. Sweat stood upon my forehead. Flies
rose in a cloud and settled again.

Water.

The word beat in my head.

A brook must be near. Forests have brooks. I listened
and believed I heard one—a soft, continuous murmur. I turned
my head. It ceased. Presently it began again. Water over
stones, cool, clear, shaded by alders. I saw it. I
saw the brown leaves moving beneath it. I knelt and
plunged my face—

There was no brook. Only wind in the upper branches.

I wept then, not from pain, but from thirst.

When my tears reached the corners of my mouth, I
tried to catch them with my tongue.

At noon the smell began.

The heat had worked upon my neighbor. At first there
was only a sweetness in the air, faint but disagreeable.
Then it thickened. Flies clustered about his eyes and wound.
His face had swollen. The astonished expression was gone, replaced
by a hideous contentment.

I covered my nose, but could not long keep my
hand there.

"Thou art changing quickly," I said. "Yesterday a man, today—what?"

I turned away. In doing so I saw at his
side a leather strap half-hidden beneath his arm.

A canteen.

For several seconds I merely stared. Then joy struck me
so violently that I trembled.

The canteen might be empty. It might be pierced. Still,
when I looked closely, I saw that its stopper remained
in place. The Turk's body lay between me and it.
The distance was scarcely two arshins.

I stretched out my right hand. My fingers reached only
grass.

I attempted to draw myself toward him. With my elbows
pressed into the earth, I pulled. My body moved the
breadth of a finger. Both wounds opened their fiery mouths.
I stopped, panting.

It cannot be done, I thought.

Then the imagined brook murmured again.

I dug my fingers into the soil. I pulled. My
helpless legs dragged behind me. Thorns caught my coat. Something
tore. The pain was so great that for a moment
the wood vanished, and I saw only a yellow wheel
revolving against blackness.

I rested. I pulled again.

One does not crawl two arshins in such a state.
One crosses a province, a desert, a lifetime. Every inch
demands a decision: endure this or die. Again: endure this
or die.

At last my hand touched the Turk's sleeve.

It was hot.

I recoiled, then forced myself to grasp it. To reach
the strap I must partly raise his shoulder. I pushed
my fingers beneath him. His body yielded in a manner
that made horror rise in my throat. Flies burst upward.
The smell became overpowering.

"Pardon," I muttered. "I must have it."

I pulled the strap. It caught under him. I pulled
harder. His head rolled toward me, and for one instant
his open mouth rested against my sleeve as though he
would bite.

I screamed and struck at him.

The body did not move again.

Shame followed terror. This dead man, whom I had killed,
possessed the water that might save me, and I struck
him because his head rolled! I seized the strap with
both hands and tugged until it came free.

The canteen was half-full.

I heard the water move within it.

No music, no beloved voice, no church bell on Easter
morning ever sounded so beautiful.

My hands shook as I removed the stopper. I raised
the canteen. Instinct commanded me to drink all, to pour
life into my cracked mouth until nothing remained. I swallowed
once—too much. Pain seized my throat. I coughed, and precious
drops ran down my chin.

I stopped.

"Slowly," I told myself. "Ivanof, thou fool, slowly."

I took a little, held it in my mouth, and
let it pass. Again a little. The water was warm
and tasted of leather, metal, and something bitter. It was
paradise.

I replaced the stopper and laid the canteen beneath my
breast so it could not roll away.

"My preserver," I said to the Turk.

He had crossed perhaps hundreds of versts carrying that water.
His hands had filled the vessel. He had guarded it
during the charge. Now it belonged to the man who
killed him. Or did it belong? No; nothing belonged to
either of us. The rifle, the uniform, the body itself—all
had been lent by powers that demanded them back.

I crawled no farther. I remained close beside him, my
shoulder almost touching his arm.

Toward evening clouds gathered. I prayed for rain. Great grey
shapes covered the sun; thunder muttered; the leaves turned their
pale undersides upward. I held the canteen ready, dreaming that
I should fill it, drink, fill it again. A few
drops struck my face.

Then the cloud passed.

The drops vanished from the hot earth.

Night came, the second night. Fever came with it.

I conversed for hours with my neighbor. I called him
Achmet, though I did not know his name. I told
him of our village, of the millpond, of Anna's yellow
shawl. He told me—yes, in fever he told me—of white
houses upon a hillside, of vines, goats, and a younger
brother who could climb any tree. We spoke without language
and understood one another.

"Why didst thou come?" I asked.

"They sent me," he said.

"Why did I come?"

"They praised thee."

"Didst thou hate me?"

"I feared thy bayonet."

"And I thine."

Then he laughed, and insects poured from his mouth.

I awoke in terror. The moon had moved westward. My
neighbor had not spoken. The blue cord remained upon his
wrist.

I drank two small mouthfuls.

On the third morning the sky was cloudless. The sun
rose red beyond the tree trunks and sent long golden
beams through the mist. Spiderwebs, invisible before, appeared everywhere, hung
with drops that shone like jewels. A bird sang from
a high branch. Another answered.

It was beautiful.

I hated it.

How dared the morning be beautiful? How dared the light
touch the corpse gently, gild the torn fern, sparkle upon
the bayonet? If the sky had become black, if the
trees had groaned and the sun fallen bloody upon us,
there would have been justice. But nature recognized no battle.
To her we were two animals, one dead, one nearly
so, beneath a bush.

My fever increased. Red lines had spread above the wound
in my thigh. The flesh was hard and hot. I
could no longer move the left foot. When I touched
it, it seemed to belong to another man.

The Turk had changed further. His face was dark and
enormous. The lips had drawn back, exposing the teeth. Flies
covered him so densely that their movement made the features
seem alive. I placed a corner of my torn coat
across my nose, yet the odor entered my mouth, mixed
with the taste of water, and settled upon my tongue.

War! Here it lay.

Not the banners seen from a square; not the brass
music; not the general's proclamation; not the printed report stating
that our losses were insignificant. Here was war: a grinning
face under flies, a young man's body swelling in the
heat, another young man begging from it a mouthful of
warm water.

Insignificant losses! Perhaps tomorrow some correspondent would write: "After a
spirited engagement our troops advanced, suffering only insignificant losses." I
should be one of those losses. Not even Ivanof—only one
private. My mother would receive a paper, if she received
anything. Anna would wait until autumn, then winter. Somewhere a
clerk would dip his pen and make a mark.

One.

Was Achmet also one?

Anger gave me strength to call out. I shouted until
my throat tore. No one answered.

At midday I heard horses.

At first I thought it another deception of the fever.
Then came clearly the dull beat of many hoofs, the
clank of sabres, a man's cough, and voices. Through the
opening I saw movement between the trunks—dark horses, lances, blue-red
uniforms.

Cossacks!

Life returned to me in one violent bound.

"Here!" I cried. "Brothers! Here!"

My voice was a whisper.

The company passed perhaps thirty paces away. I could see
the tops of their caps. One rider sang a fragment
of a song. Another said something about tobacco. The hoofbeats
approached, filled the wood, began to recede.

"Here! For God's sake!"

I seized the canteen, intending to strike it against a
stone. My arm failed. I tried again, waving it above
the grass.

The strap slipped from my fingers.

The canteen fell upon its side. The stopper, which I
had not secured after drinking, came loose.

Water poured into the dust.

I stared without understanding. A dark patch spread beneath the
vessel and vanished almost at once. I snatched it up.
A few drops remained—no more than a swallow.

The Cossacks were gone.

Then despair took me wholly. I cursed them. I cursed
their horses, their song, their mothers. I cursed my officers,
the Tsar, the Sultan, Russia, Turkey, God, and myself. I
beat the earth until my knuckles bled. I would have
torn my wounds open if I could.

At last I lay exhausted, the nearly empty canteen beneath
my cheek.

"Thou gavest it to me," I said to the corpse,
"and I wasted it."

I thought of the rifle.

The Turk's weapon lay beyond his boots, but by crawling
around him I might reach it. One cartridge would be
enough. Place the muzzle beneath the chin, press with a
stick or the toe—no, my feet could not obey. I
could turn the rifle and pull the trigger with my
hand.

It were better.

Better a flash, a blow, darkness, than another night of
thirst, fever, flies, and that smell. What bravery was there
in waiting? No one knew I lived. No one would
come. My wounds were rotting. The water was gone.

I began to crawl.

I passed along the corpse's side. The ground beneath him
was damp and black. Larvae moved in the wound. I
shut my eyes, but my hand entered something soft; I
jerked it away and vomited, though there was nothing in
my stomach.

The rifle's stock was near. I stretched. My fingers touched
wood.

Then I saw my mother.

Not in fever. I remembered her as she had stood
at the gate when I departed. She was small beneath
her black shawl. She did not weep before the others.
When I climbed onto the cart she crossed herself, then
crossed me from afar. Her lips moved. As the cart
turned, I saw her lean one hand against the gatepost.

If I killed myself, would that act kill something in
her also?

"Live," she had said on the last night. "Whatever comes,
Vanya, live."

I drew my hand away from the rifle.

"No," I said. "Not yet."

The words cost nothing; endurance cost everything. Still, there is
in man some obscure animal which clings to breath after
hope has gone. Perhaps it is cowardice. Perhaps courage. I
do not know. I only know that I returned, inch
by inch, to the hollow where I had lain.

The last swallow I divided into three portions. One at
sunset. One at midnight. One I kept for morning.

That evening I remembered a dog.

Years before, near the road from our village, a wagon
had struck a yellow dog and broken its hindquarters. It
dragged itself into a ditch. For two days I saw
it there. People passed. Some pitied it; none wished to
touch it. On the third day it no longer barked.
It only lifted its head when footsteps came, and in
its eyes there was an appeal more terrible because it
had no words.

I had been late for work. I passed on.

Now I was that dog.

"Water," I whispered whenever the leaves moved. "Do not pass.
I am alive."

No footsteps came.

The third night was confused. Sometimes I lay beneath the
bush; sometimes I walked at home. Anna sat by the
river and accused me of bringing a stranger to our
wedding. The stranger was Achmet, clean and smiling, with the
blue cord about his wrist. My mother gave him bread.
I protested that he was an enemy.

"An enemy?" she asked. "Whose son is he, then?"

I could not answer.

Later the Turk's skeleton sat upright beside me. The flesh
had vanished. Flies moved in its empty eyes.

"Behold," it said, "I am glory."

"No," I replied.

"I am victory."

"No."

"I am war."

At that I looked upon its grin and knew the
truth.

Before dawn cold rain began. It was so light that
at first I mistook it for insects upon the leaves.
Then a drop struck my eyelid, another my lip.

Rain!

I opened my mouth. I spread my hand upon the
ground and licked the water from my palm. I held
the canteen upright, but the leaves above prevented more than
a few drops from entering. With terrible labor I dragged
myself from beneath the thickest branches toward the opening.

The rain ceased.

Still, the moisture upon the grass gave me life. I
sucked leaves, chewed wet earth, pressed my face into moss.
Never be ashamed of the beast within thee; it may
preserve thee when all noble thoughts have died.

Morning arrived grey and cool. It was the fourth day—or
was it? I counted: the battle day, one night; the
second sun; the Cossacks on the third; now another dawn.
Three and a half days, perhaps. Time no longer moved
in hours. It circled around pain and thirst.

The last water was gone.

My neighbor no longer resembled a man. His skin had
split in places. Beneath the drawn lips the teeth showed
in a fixed mockery. One eye had collapsed; flies entered
the other. His hand, which on the first day had
seemed almost capable of closing, had become discolored and stiff.

I looked at the blue cord.

If I lived, I wished to take it and send
it to his mother. But where should I send it?
To "the mother of the Turk whom I bayoneted in
a forest"? There would be no address, no name. He
had vanished from the world more completely than if he
had never been born.

Yet I knew him.

I knew the terror of his eyes. I knew the
weight of his shoulder when I pulled the canteen from
beneath him. I knew that someone had tied a cord
upon his wrist. Is that not more knowledge than generals
possess of the thousands they move upon maps?

"Achmet," I said, "if I live, I shall remember thee."

My voice made no sound. The lips formed the words,
but my throat was closed.

The day grew warmer. I ceased to fight the flies.
They walked over my face and gathered at my cracked
lips. Ants travelled across my sleeve. One carried a white
egg many times, dropped it whenever my hand trembled, returned,
lifted it again. I watched with dull admiration.

Sleep came.

Death, I had imagined, would appear solemnly. There would be
prayer, farewell, perhaps a final vision. Instead, one merely becomes
tired. The edges of things soften. Pain withdraws a little,
like an enemy who knows he has won. The sky
seems farther away. Sleep, sleep—only sleep.

I heard voices.

I did not open my eyes. The dead often hear
what they desire.

"Over there! Another one."

"Turk?"

"Two of them, I think."

Branches cracked. A spade struck a stone. Someone complained of
the smell.

They had come to bury us.

I tried to move my hand. It did not move.
I tried to speak. Air passed my lips.

"Wait," said a voice. "This one's Russian."

A boot nudged my shoulder.

"Dead."

The word entered me clearly.

No! I thought. No, brother, do not cover me. I
am here. I live.

All my remaining being gathered itself. I opened my eyes.

A bearded face leaned above me, blurred by sunlight. The
man started backward.

"The devil! He's looking!"

Another bent down. Fingers touched my neck.

"Alive. Bring water—quickly, but not too much."

Water.

I attempted to seize the cup when it came, but
they held my hands. A corporal lifted my head. He
was a broad man with a scar across his nose,
and his breath smelt of onions and tobacco.

"Gently, lad," he said. "A sip. Thou hast managed thus
far; do not drown thyself at the end."

The cup touched my mouth.

Cold water passed over my tongue.

I groaned. I believe I thanked him. Perhaps I merely
wept. He gave me another sip and wiped my face
with his sleeve.

"How long hast thou lain here?"

I raised three fingers, then four, uncertain.

"Four days? Holy Mother!"

"Three," said another soldier. "The action was three days ago."

"Three and a half," I whispered.

They cut away my trousers. Their faces changed when they
saw the wounds, but the corporal continued speaking cheerfully.

"Nothing at all. A scratch on one side, a scratch
on the other. You volunteers are delicate fellows. You lie
down for half a week over anything."

I knew he lied, and loved him for it.

They placed me upon a cloak. When they lifted me,
pain returned with such force that the forest split in
two. I bit the corporal's hand. He did not strike
me.

"Bite away," he said. "I have another."

As they carried me out, I turned my head toward
the Turk. Two men stood over him with spades. One
had already taken the rifle. The blue cord still showed
at his wrist.

"Wait," I murmured.

"What?"

"The Turk."

"He will be buried."

"My preserver."

They thought me delirious. Perhaps I was. The corporal followed
my eyes and saw the canteen hanging from my hand
by its strap.

"His?"

I nodded.

"Well," he said after a moment, "then God remember him
also."

They carried me through the forest.

For the first time I saw what had lain beyond
my bush. Bodies were scattered under the trees, some alone,
some in groups as though they had gathered to sleep.
Broken rifles, torn packs, caps, and cartridge-boxes marked the line
of our charge. The clerk from Kalouga still sat against
the trunk. The red ribbon upon his cheek had become
black.

Farther on, birds sang.

At a clearing stood carts and stretchers. Wounded men waited
in rows. Some called for water; some stared silently; one
sang a hymn in a steady, pleasant voice while blood
soaked the straw beneath him.

They gave me broth. I could not keep it. They
gave me water by spoonfuls. I begged for the bottle,
cursed them when they refused, then begged forgiveness.

The first stretcher carried me to a field station. The
second, after sunset, took me to a village church used
as an infirmary. Candles burned before the darkened saints. Beneath
the painted eyes of martyrs, surgeons worked with rolled sleeves.
There was blood upon the altar steps.

An officer of the medical service examined me.

"Name?"

"Ivanof."

"Regiment?"

I told him.

"Volunteer?"

"Yes."

He raised his brows, as though volunteering were an illness
whose symptoms he had often observed.

"How long exposed?"

"Three and a half days."

He looked toward the surgeon.

"Remarkable."

They gave me something bitter. The lamps expanded and contracted.
Men groaned behind curtains. Metal instruments clinked in basins.

The surgeon was a small, bald man with kind hands
and an impatient voice.

"The left bullet missed the bone," he said. "The right
has lodged near it. Artery untouched. You are fortunate."

"Fortunate?"

"Extremely."

I began to laugh, and he frowned.

"Do not excite yourself."

"My legs?"

He examined them again. His fingers pressed the swollen flesh.
I cried out.

"The right may be saved," he said. "The left—"

He paused.

"The left what?"

"It must come away below the knee."

I stared at him.

"My leg?"

"A mere trifle," he answered briskly. "Men lose their heads.
You shall lose only a foot and some inches. Be
grateful for economy."

A mere trifle!

I thought of the hazel branch, the clerk's red wound,
the Cossacks passing, the water darkening the dust, the Turk's
exposed teeth. I thought of Anna standing at the river.
Would she marry a man with one leg? Would I
work? Would I dance? Would children laugh when I passed?

Then I remembered the rifle within reach of my hand
and my mother's command: Live.

"Cut it," I said.

They placed a cloth over my face.

Before the drug took me, I heard the surgeon say,
"Strong heart." Someone answered, "He must have one, after lying
out there."

No. My heart was not strong. It had been terrified,
bitter, murderous, despairing. It had cursed and begged. It had
desired death. It had lived because a dead enemy carried
half a canteen of water.

When I awoke, my left leg ended in bandages.

For many weeks fever returned. At night the ward became
the forest. The men in neighboring beds turned into trees;
the lamp became a star between leaves. I called for
Achmet. Once I struck an attendant because I believed he
was stealing the canteen. They kept it for me—the battered
leather vessel, stained with earth. I would not permit it
to be washed.

My right leg healed slowly. The doctors extracted the bullet
and showed it to me, flattened at one side. A
nurse offered to wrap it in paper as a remembrance.

"I have remembrance enough," I said.

Letters came at last. My mother's was covered with crosses.
Anna wrote only three lines: she had heard I lived;
she thanked God; she would come when travel was permitted.
I read those lines until the paper grew soft at
the folds.

In the official account our engagement occupied half a column.
The troops had displayed exemplary courage. The enemy was driven
from his position. Our losses were comparatively slight.

I was one of the slight losses.

The clerk from Kalouga was another. Achmet was not counted
in our column at all.

Months afterward, when I could stand upon a wooden limb
and move with crutches, I asked an officer whether lists
existed of the enemy dead. He laughed, not cruelly.

"My dear Ivanof, there were hundreds. Why?"

"I wished to know one name."

"Did you take his papers?"

"No."

"Then he has no name for us."

No name for us. Yet he comes more often to
my sleep than many men whose names I know. Sometimes
he is as I first saw him—young, sweating, afraid. Sometimes
he lies swollen beneath the flies. Sometimes he walks beside
me without wound or weapon, and neither of us wears
a uniform. We arrive at two roads. He turns one
way, I another. Before parting, he lifts his hand, and
upon the wrist is the faded blue cord.

I recollect.

That is why I have written these things. Not to
tell of courage, for I displayed little; not to accuse
this commander or that sovereign, for a single wounded volunteer
cannot judge the designs of empires. I write because men
speak too easily of battle. They draw arrows upon maps;
they print numbers; they praise the charge. But within each
arrow men run beneath humming bullets. Within each number a
mother waits. Beneath every phrase concerning glory there may lie,
hidden in bushes, two strangers who never hated one another:
one dead by the other's hand, the other living by
the dead man's water.

I survived. They call that fortune.

I returned to my mother. Anna became my wife. I
walk poorly, suffer pain when rain approaches, and wake sometimes
with thirst so violent that I overturn the cup beside
my bed. Then I hear again the water moving in
the Turk's canteen.

And I recollect the forest, the wet hazel leaf upon
my lip, the frightened eyes before my bayonet, and the
voice commanding us all—Russian and Turk, living and dead—

"Fo-o-r-wa-ard!"

### The Lamb Who Learned to Share

In a green meadow, where the daisies grew thickly and
the brook made a pleasant murmuring sound beneath the willows,
there lived a lively young Lamb with his mother and
his twin sister.

The two lambs were almost exactly alike. They had the
same pinkish-white noses, the same bright eyes, and the same
four unsteady legs which had grown stronger every day since
spring began. Yet in their tempers they were not alike
at all.

The little sister was gentle and contented. If Mother Sheep
moved away to nibble a patch of clover, she followed
quietly; and if her brother reached the warmest place beside
their mother, she found another place without complaint.

But the brother wanted everything first.

He wanted the sweetest clover, the softest hollow in the
grass, and both sides of Mother Sheep, although, as we
know, no lamb can possibly stand on both sides of
his mother at once.

"Move away!" he would say to his sister when she
came to nurse. "You had some before."

"I am hungry again," she would answer.

"You are always hungry."

"So are you," said his sister, and this was perfectly
true.

Then the brother would bunt her with his hard little
forehead. He did not hurt her very much, but it
was rude, and rudeness often hurts a loving heart more
than a small bump hurts the body.

Mother Sheep saw this and sighed.

"My son," she said, "there is room for both of
you."

"There would be more room if she were not here,"
he muttered.

Now, at the farther end of the farmyard stood a
pen for the lambs who had no mothers to feed
them, or whose mothers could not give them enough milk.
The Farmer's daughter came there several times each day carrying
bottles filled with warm milk. The bottle-fed lambs would crowd
around her, wagging their little tails and crying, "Ma-a-a! Ma-a-a!"
They were kindly treated, but they had no dear woolly
mother to sleep beside at night.

The brother Lamb had watched them through the rails.

"I wish the Farmer would take Sister to the bottle
pen," he said one morning. "Then I should have you
all to myself."

His sister looked up in surprise, with a drop of
milk still shining on her soft mouth.

"You do not mean that," said Mother Sheep.

"Yes, I do. She can drink from a bottle. I
want all your milk and all your love."

Mother Sheep bent her head and touched him gently with
her nose.

"Love is not a trough that becomes empty when two
drink from it," she said. "Love grows when it is
shared."

"That sounds very well," replied the Lamb impatiently, "but I
should still like Sister to go away."

Mother Sheep's eyes were sad.

"Selfishness takes up a great deal of room in a
little heart," she told him. "When it has filled the
heart, there is scarcely any place left for true love."

The Lamb frisked away as if he had not heard
her. Still, he had heard, and somewhere under his quick
temper the words remained.

A few days later the Farmer and two shepherds drove
the flock from the meadow.

"Where are we going?" asked the sister Lamb.

"I do not know," said Mother Sheep, "but keep near
me."

The sheep went through a gate, along a dusty lane,
and into a large yard enclosed by high hurdles. There
were old Ewes with patient faces, young Sheep who had
never been there before, and several grave Wether Sheep. The
lambs ran under the larger animals, calling for their mothers,
while the flock pressed shoulder to shoulder.

Then the shepherds opened a narrow gate and separated the
lambs from the grown sheep.

At once there arose such a bleating as you have
seldom heard.

The mothers called, and the lambs answered. They bleated and
bleated and bleated until the air seemed full of sorrowful
voices.

"Mother!" cried the brother Lamb.

"I am here!" called Mother Sheep from beyond the hurdles.

"Come back!"

"I cannot, my dear."

The shepherd lifted another hurdle into place, and soon the
Lamb could no longer see her. He pushed against the
wooden bars, but they were strong. He ran to one
end of the pen, and then to the other, but
every opening was closed.

His sister pressed against him.

"I am frightened," she whispered.

"Do not crowd me!" he snapped, though he was more
frightened than she was.

Evening came, and the lambs were not returned to their
mothers. They huddled together as the air grew cold. From
a shed nearby came strange sounds: the murmur of men's
voices, the stamping of hooves, and now and then a
sharp snip-snip-snipping.

The Lamb had never seen sheep sheared, so he did
not know that the Farmer was merely removing their thick
fleeces before the warm weather. Wool protects sheep in winter,
but a heavy fleece may become hot and burdensome in
summer. The shears do not harm a sheep when they
are used carefully, though their noise may sound alarming to
a lamb who does not understand.

"What are they doing?" the sister asked.

"I do not know."

"Will Mother come back?"

"Of course she will," said the brother, but his voice
trembled.

One by one, the sheep were led into the shed.
One by one, they disappeared.

The brother Lamb remembered his own unkind wish.

"I want all your love," he had said. "I wish
Sister would go away."

What if Mother Sheep went away instead? What if the
Farmer took her somewhere beyond the hills? What if he
never again felt her warm side against his cheek, or
heard her deep, familiar call?

"Oh, Mother!" he cried into the darkness. "I did not
mean it! I did not mean those naughty words!"

Impatient creatures sometimes say what they think they mean, only
to discover later that they did not understand their own
hearts. The Lamb had believed that he wanted his sister
gone, but now, when the whole world seemed uncertain, he
wanted every member of his little family safe together.

His sister nestled closer.

"Are you crying?" she asked.

"No," said the brother.

But a tear lay upon his nose.

"I hope Mother is not afraid," said the sister. "If
she is, I wish I could comfort her."

The brother said nothing. He had been thinking only of
who would comfort him.

All night the lambs slept and woke, slept and woke.
Whenever the brother opened his eyes, he listened for Mother
Sheep. Sometimes he heard a distant bleat and sprang up,
but there were many mothers calling, and he could not
tell whether one was his own.

Morning brought more bustle. The shed door opened and shut.
The shears went snip-snip-snip, and great soft fleeces were rolled
and carried away.

At last the shepherds removed the hurdle.

The lambs rushed forward in a white, leaping crowd.

But where were their mothers?

In the yard stood a flock of strange-looking animals. Yesterday
the grown sheep had been round and woolly, their fleeces
heavy with dust from the fields. Now they looked slender
and pale. Their long necks seemed longer, their bodies seemed
smaller, and their legs appeared almost too thin to carry
them.

The lambs stopped in astonishment.

"That is not our flock!" cried one.

"Where is my mother?" called another.

The shorn Ewes began to bleat, and confusion filled the
yard. A lamb knows its mother by her scent, her
call, her face, and even by the way she walks;
yet a sudden change may puzzle it greatly. A short
separation may make the puzzle harder still.

The brother Lamb stared at the unfamiliar Sheep.

"Mother!" he called.

A deep voice answered, "My children!"

He turned his head. A newly shorn Ewe was pushing
through the flock. She looked very odd without her dingy,
heavy fleece, but she held her head in a certain
way, and one forefoot stepped a little more slowly than
the other.

The Lamb knew that walk.

"Mother!" he cried again.

He raced across the yard, dodging between the Sheep, and
ran straight to her. She lowered her head, and he
pressed his face beneath her chin.

"My dear one," she said. "You found me."

"I knew your voice. And I knew your walk. Oh,
Mother, I thought you were gone for ever!"

"I was never far away."

He rubbed his nose against her smooth side. She felt
strange without all her wool, but she smelled like Mother,
and her voice was Mother's voice. The Lamb was so
happy that he jumped straight into the air and came
down with all four feet together.

Then he looked around.

"Where is Sister?"

Mother Sheep lifted her head anxiously.

They could hear the sister Lamb calling from the farther
side of the yard.

"Mother! Mother! Where are you?"

She ran first to one Ewe and then to another.
Each time she drew near, she stopped with a sad
little face and hurried on.

"She does not know me," said Mother Sheep.

The brother saw that the shepherd was beginning to gather
the lost lambs beside a gate. Beyond that gate lay
the farm buildings—and beyond the buildings was the bottle-fed pen.

A selfish thought crept into his heart.

If I remain quiet, he thought, Sister will not find
us. The Farmer may take her away. Then Mother will
be mine alone.

He moved closer to Mother Sheep, as though he could
hide her behind his small body. Selfishness whispered, "This is
what you wanted."

For one moment he imagined the warm sleeping place all
to himself. He imagined never waiting while his sister drank.
He imagined Mother Sheep speaking only to him.

Then his sister called again.

"Mother! Brother! I am lost!"

Her voice sounded very small.

The Lamb remembered the dark night, the hard hurdles, and
the dreadful fear that he would never see Mother again.
He remembered how the crowded pen had seemed empty because
she was not in it. Was his sister's heart feeling
that same emptiness now?

Goodness, which had not had much room in him before,
pushed bravely against Selfishness.

"Go away," the Lamb whispered.

"What did you say?" asked Mother Sheep.

"I was speaking to a naughty thought."

Then he ran.

He darted between the shorn Sheep and around the legs
of a shepherd. He squeezed beneath a rail just as
his sister was being guided toward the gate.

"Sister!" he called.

She turned.

"I cannot find Mother!"

"I have found her. Come with me!"

"But they all look different."

"I know. Follow close."

The brother led her across the yard. Once she stopped
beside the wrong Ewe, but he hurried back and nudged
her gently—not with an angry bunt, but with a loving
little push.

"This way," he said.

Mother Sheep saw them and gave her own deep call.

The sister's ears lifted. She ran the last few steps
and pressed herself against her mother.

"It is you!" she cried. "You are very thin!"

"My wool has been cut off, that is all," said
Mother Sheep, laughing in the quiet way of sheep. "It
will grow again."

The sister rubbed her pinkish-white nose against Mother's neck. Then
she turned to her brother.

"You came to fetch me."

"Yes," he said.

"You might have kept Mother to yourself."

The Lamb hung his head.

Mother Sheep looked at him with wise, gentle eyes, and
he knew that she had guessed the whole struggle.

"I nearly did," he confessed. "The selfish thought came back."

"But you did not obey it," said Mother Sheep.

"I remembered how lonely I was without you. I did
not want Sister to feel so lonely."

Mother touched his hard little forehead with her nose.

"Then you have begun to understand. Love does not become
smaller when we give some away. It grows stronger, because
every loving deed makes more room for love."

The brother leaned against one side of her, and his
sister leaned against the other. There was room for both,
just as Mother Sheep had always said.

When the flock returned to the meadow, the newly shorn
Sheep looked clean and white beneath the sun. They stepped
lightly without their heavy fleeces, while the lambs frisked around
them.

The brother did not become perfect in a single morning.
Very few lambs do, and very few children do either.
Now and then he still wanted the sweetest clover, and
sometimes he reached Mother first. But he no longer tried
to drive his sister away. If she was lost among
the flock, he called to her; if she was tired,
he waited; and if there was one particularly tender patch
of grass, he sometimes—though not always—let her have the first
bite.

One evening Mother Sheep said, "You are becoming a great
comfort to me."

The Lamb looked pleased, but then he said honestly, "I
hope it lasts. I may feel selfish again tomorrow."

"You may," replied Mother Sheep. "Goodness is not never having
a selfish thought. Goodness is knowing which thought should lead
you."

So the two lambs settled beside their dear woolly mother
while the sun went down beyond the pasture. The sister
slept peacefully, and the brother kept watch for a little
while, proud that his family was together and prouder still
that he had helped to make it so.

And if he ever whispered that he had once wished
his sister away, the meadow birds did not repeat it.

We shall not repeat it either.

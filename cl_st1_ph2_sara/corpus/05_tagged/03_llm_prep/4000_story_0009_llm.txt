# The Call of the Monte

There are men whose proper occupations are selected for them
before they have learned the use of a spoon, and
there are others in whom nature has placed so stubborn
a preference that the combined authority of parents, tutors, physicians,
and respectable neighbors cannot altogether suppress it. The former become
useful citizens, subscribe to charities, marry at an appropriate age,
and are remembered by a paragraph in the newspapers. The
latter occasionally make poor clerks and worse husbands, but they
are apt to discover countries.

Alexander Wells belonged to the latter class, though during the
first twenty-two years of his life no one suspected it,
least of all himself.

He was the only son of Jonathan Wells, of New
York, whose fortune had originated in shipping and had afterward
been enlarged by investments in warehouses, insurance companies, and certain
western lands which he had never seen. Alexander was educated
in the best manner then considered necessary for a young
gentleman who was expected neither to preach nor practise law.
He attended school at New Haven, spent four years at
college, acquired some Latin, less Greek, a respectable knowledge of
mathematics, and a habit of sitting up after midnight with
books, cards, or companions according to the demands of the
occasion.

There graduated with him, in the spring of 1872, a
pale and narrow-chested youth whom his mother regarded with alarm
and his father with concealed disappointment. He coughed, ate little,
slept badly, and was subject to a nervous weariness which
the family physician described in language sufficiently grave to be
impressive and sufficiently indefinite to preserve his professional reputation. A
sea voyage was proposed; so were the Adirondacks, Florida, and
the mineral springs of Virginia. Alexander listened without enthusiasm until
his father happened to mention Texas.

The Wells family possessed, or believed it possessed, a grant
of land lying some forty miles southwest of San Antonio.
The title had come through a complicated transaction involving a
Spanish survey, a Mexican empresario, two deceased speculators, and an
American lawyer who had accepted acreage in payment of a
fee. Taxes had been remitted from New York with tolerable
regularity, but no member of the family had visited the
property. There were said to be live-oaks upon it, a
permanent creek, and several thousand acres of grass. To Jonathan
Wells it was an item in a tin case. To
Alexander it became, within ten minutes, a country.

The physician, being assured that Texas possessed dry air and
abundant sunshine, approved the plan. It might be in perfect
order to remark here that he afterward received from Mr.
Wells a handsome silver service, though this circumstance should not
be interpreted as diminishing the sincerity of his opinion. Physicians,
like other men, may be both grateful and correct.

Alexander left New York in September with two trunks, a
rifle in a walnut case, three suits of clothes, a
box of medicines, and imperative orders to remain no longer
than six months. He traveled by rail as far as
the arrangements of that period permitted, by steamboat down the
Mississippi, and thence westward over routes which changed from one
season to another according to mud, water, and the condition
of bridges. The last portion of his journey was made
in a stage whose body hung upon leather thorough-braces and
whose driver considered nine miles an hour a concession to
weakness.

For two days they crossed a country broader than any
Alexander had imagined. There were stretches of black prairie on
which the grass rolled under the wind like grain; belts
of timber marking watercourses; ranchitos composed of a jacal, a
corral, and a patch of corn; and, at long intervals,
a town gathered about a stone church or a square
courthouse. Dust entered the coach through every seam. At night
the passengers ate fried pork and beans at stage stands
where lean dogs disputed possession of the gallery. Alexander was
fatigued, bruised, and delighted.

San Antonio, when he reached it, was still more Mexican
than American in appearance. Flat-roofed houses stood behind thick walls;
irrigation ditches carried clear water beneath cottonwoods; ox carts, whose
wooden axles complained at every revolution, passed wagons newly arrived
from the coast; and Spanish, English, and German speech mingled
in the plazas. He lodged at a hotel near Main
Plaza, where the proprietor looked at his white face and
medicine chest and recommended that he avoid night air, green
chile, bad whisky, and all exertion.

Within a week Alexander had disregarded the four recommendations.

His father's agent was a deliberate attorney named Brackett, who
had been instructed to examine the young man's health, arrange
comfortable quarters, and prevent him from wandering into danger. Mr.
Brackett accomplished the first object by asking Alexander how he
felt, the second by procuring him a room in the
house of a German widow, and the third not at
all.

A surveyor was engaged, horses were hired, and a party
rode out to inspect the Wells grant. The journey occupied
the greater part of a day. They followed a wagon
road for twenty miles, left it at a settlement consisting
of a store and three houses, and continued across open
land where mesquite grew in scattered clumps. Toward evening they
descended into the valley of a creek shaded by pecans.
Deer tracks printed the mud at the crossings, wild turkeys
flew heavily from the trees, and somewhere in the monte—the
dense thorny cover of the lower country—a band of javeline
broke brush and went grunting away.

Alexander forgot that he was an invalid.

The property proved better than the attorney had expected and
more irregular than the old map admitted. Its eastern boundary
followed the creek; to the north lay open prairie; the
western portion rose into low, stony hills covered with cedar
and shin oak. There were two springs, one doubtful well,
and a deserted cabin on a bench above the water.
The cabin had been built of post-oak logs, chinked with
clay, and roofed with split boards held in place by
poles. Its chimney, made of sticks plastered with mud, leaned
outward in a manner which would have alarmed a mason,
but it drew well. The floor was hard-packed earth. A
pecan tree shaded the door.

"I could live here," Alexander said.

The surveyor laughed, supposing this to be an eastern gentleman's
pleasantry.

"You might camp here a little while," said Mr. Brackett.
"But live? There isn't a house within eight miles."

"That is one reason."

On the following morning they encountered Tiburcio Salazar.

He appeared at the edge of the creek bottom leading
a small gray horse upon which were packed a rolled
blanket, a coffee pot, a rawhide rope, and the carcass
of a young deer. He was perhaps thirty-five years old,
though sun, wind, and a life spent outdoors made precise
judgment difficult. He wore trousers of brown cloth, a cotton
shirt, sandals, and a broad hat darkened by weather. A
long knife hung at his belt, and he carried an
old muzzle-loading rifle as naturally as another man might carry
a walking stick.

Tiburcio had been born at a ranch on the Medina
and knew the country from the Nueces northward to the
limestone hills. He could find water where no water appeared,
tell by a bent grass blade what animal had passed
and how long before, sleep dry through a rain with
no shelter but a blanket, and approach a feeding deer
against an uncertain wind. These accomplishments had never been certified
by a college; nevertheless, they represented an education exacting in
its requirements and severe in its examinations.

He examined the party, nodded to the surveyor, and spoke
briefly with him in Spanish.

"He says a blue norther will come by midnight," the
latter explained.

The sky was clear and warm, and Alexander looked upward.

"How does he know?"

Tiburcio pointed toward a line of ants carrying their white
eggs from a low nest to higher ground. Then he
indicated cattle in the distance moving steadily south with their
heads down.

"He knows," said the surveyor.

The norther arrived at eleven. The wind shifted in a
single violent breath, the temperature fell, and rain drove under
the blankets of the camp. Tiburcio, who had chosen a
hollow beneath a limestone bank and built a small fire
against its face, remained comparatively comfortable. Alexander crawled beside him
before dawn and received hot coffee without comment.

That cup began a friendship.

When Alexander returned to San Antonio, he announced that he
intended to occupy the cabin for several months. Mr. Brackett
protested; the German widow protested; even the hotel keeper, learning
of it, contributed his opinion. Alexander purchased an army cot,
blankets, an iron kettle, a frying pan, an ax, two
boxes of cartridges, flour, bacon, coffee, salt, matches, a lantern,
and enough canned provisions to withstand a moderate siege. He
hired a wagon and team, placed his trunks upon the
load, and rode out in company with Tiburcio, whose services
he had secured for twenty dollars a month and board.

It must not be supposed that Tiburcio accepted the employment
merely for wages. He had observed that Alexander listened when
instructed, possessed a first-rate breech-loading rifle, and was willing to
provide cartridges in quantities which appeared to him magnificent. In
addition, the young American's ignorance amused him.

They repaired the cabin first. Rotten roof boards were replaced;
the chimney was straightened and rechinked; a door was made
from planks brought in the wagon; and stones were laid
about the spring to keep cattle from trampling it into
mud. Tiburcio constructed a table by driving four mesquite stakes
into the floor and fastening a split pecan slab across
them. Alexander made shelves, though not level ones, and set
his books beside tins of powder and coffee. The cot
occupied one wall. Tiburcio preferred to spread his blankets near
the fire.

For the first month the life was not altogether romantic.
Scorpions entered the cabin; mice investigated the flour; smoke sometimes
filled the room; and Alexander discovered that a man accustomed
to servants may possess the best intentions and still burn
bacon. He blistered his hands with the ax, lost a
horse for two days, and once walked five miles in
a circle while attempting to find the creek.

Yet his appetite returned. He slept ten hours without medicine
and awoke hungry. The cough disappeared so quietly that he
did not notice its departure until Mr. Brackett asked about
it. By December his face was brown, his shoulders broader,
and his weight increased by fifteen pounds. His mother, receiving
these facts in a letter, expressed gratitude to Providence and
suggested that he return in March.

Alexander did not answer that suggestion directly.

Hunting provided the principal occupation of the winter. At dawn
he and Tiburcio rode out while frost still silvered the
grass, leading two hounds of uncertain ancestry but excellent noses.
They learned the crossings used by deer, the ridges along
which wild turkeys traveled, and the muddy places where javeline
rooted beneath prickly pear. Tiburcio taught Alexander to study the
wind before entering cover, to step on stone rather than
dry twig, and to look not for the whole shape
of an animal but for the horizontal line of a
back, the twitch of an ear, or a difference in
color among leaves.

Alexander, who had been a competent target shot, at first
mistook accuracy for woodcraft. He could strike a tomato can
at a hundred yards but could not see a buck
standing motionless at forty. Tiburcio would touch his arm, point,
and whisper, "Allí," while Alexander stared at an apparently empty
thicket until the animal bounded away. This occurred so often
that it became a standing joke requiring no words.

In return, Alexander taught Tiburcio the operation of the breech-loading
rifle and gave him a small field glass. Tiburcio accepted
the first improvement at once, but regarded the glass with
suspicion until he discovered that it enabled him to distinguish
cattle brands at a great distance. Thereafter he carried it
carefully wrapped in buckskin.

Their expeditions sometimes lasted a week. They rode south into
flatter country where cactus grew higher than a horse's shoulder,
or north toward the broken hills, camping wherever water and
grass could be found. Supper consisted of venison ribs roasted
over coals, tortillas when they had them, coffee always, and
occasionally pecans shaken from a tree and gathered by firelight.
Coyotes called from the darkness. The horses, hobbled nearby, tore
grass with a steady ripping sound. No bell summoned Alexander
to dinner; no engagement awaited him; no man asked what
profession he intended to adopt.

He liked it. The romance was real; it was not
an imitation.

By spring he spoke enough Spanish for ordinary purposes, though
his grammar caused Tiburcio private enjoyment. He had also acquired
a beard, buckskin leggings, and the habit of carrying very
little money. Specie was inconvenient in the monte and unnecessary
where neither goods nor sellers existed. Twice a month they
rode to the settlement for mail and supplies. The storekeeper,
who was also postmaster, case-keeper, and informal justice of the
peace, permitted Alexander to sit on the gallery and read
his letters.

His father's communications were businesslike. The six months were nearly
completed; a position awaited him in a countinghouse; arrangements should
be made to return. His mother's letters were affectionate and
anxious. She hoped the climate had fulfilled its purpose and
enclosed news of families Alexander knew, including several references to
Miss Caroline Meredith, who had asked kindly after his health.

Alexander replied that travel remained difficult, that the weather was
unsettled, and that his strength, although improving, could not yet
be trusted to the fatigue of a northern journey. He
wrote this after riding sixty miles in two days behind
the hounds.

The most peculiar feature of the whole affair was that
Alexander felt genuine remorse while constructing these statements. He loved
his parents, knew their anxiety to be reasonable, and had
been raised to consider a deliberate falsehood disgraceful. Nevertheless, when
confronted with the choice between truth and the New York
countinghouse, he discovered a moral flexibility not mentioned in his
college curriculum.

In June he went to San Antonio and consulted a
physician. The latter, after listening to Alexander's chest and finding
it excellent, said that he might safely return East.

"Doctor," Alexander replied, "the heat is increasing, the Gulf roads
are bad, and I have lately felt a weakness after
meals."

The physician looked at the bronzed young man before him,
whose pulse was slow and whose hand filled the doctor's
own like that of a teamster.

"What sort of weakness?"

"A general weakness."

"Does it prevent exercise?"

"Not moderate exercise."

It later appeared in a letter to Mr. Wells that
a further season in the dry climate might be prudent.
The fee for this opinion was twenty dollars. Alexander considered
it excessive, but paid without complaint, recognizing professional collaboration when
he encountered it.

Summer upon the creek was hot, yet not intolerable. They
worked in the early hours, rested at noon beneath the
pecans, and rode again toward evening. Thunderstorms towered over the
prairie, advanced with flags of rain beneath them, and departed
as rapidly as cavalry. The creek rose, ran red for
a day, and cleared. Fish gathered in the deeper pools.
Doves came to water by hundreds, and at dusk bats
circled over the cabin.

Alexander began to concern himself with the land in a
practical fashion. He mended a line of old fence, bought
six cows, and helped Tiburcio build a corral of upright
mesquite posts. He learned to throw a rope, though never
with his companion's effortless precision. A neighboring rancher taught him
the meaning of brands and earmarks, the value of grass
in a dry year, and the necessity of examining cattle
often enough to prevent their adoption by men of liberal
ideas concerning ownership.

A year passed. Then a second.

Each autumn brought letters whose tone grew firmer. Mr. Wells
offered Alexander a place in the shipping office; Alexander replied
that fever had appeared near the coast. His mother proposed
accompanying him home; he explained that accommodations in the country
were unsuitable. When his father demanded a definite date, Alexander
selected one six months distant and, as it approached, developed
a disorder of the liver which confined him to camp
except when hunting.

Tiburcio understood the correspondence in its general character.

"Your mother thinks you are sick?" he asked one evening.

"Not sick exactly. Delicate."

Tiburcio looked at the haunch of venison Alexander had carried
half a mile upon his shoulders.

"Muy delicado," he said.

By the third year Alexander's life had assumed a completeness
which astonished him whenever he remembered its temporary intention. The
cabin contained few of the things once thought essential, yet
everything in it served a purpose. Rifles rested on pegs
above the cot; saddles hung beneath a shed roof; dried
meat, onions, and red peppers swung from rafters. There were
dogs before the door, horses in the pasture, cattle increasing
along the creek, and a friend whose silence was never
oppressive.

He received newspapers weeks old and read of elections, scandals,
railway combinations, and fashionable marriages. These events appeared remote and
somewhat theatrical. The creek continued to run regardless of them.
A norther required wood; a lame horse required attention; the
discovery of fresh bear sign in the hills was more
immediate than the failure of a bank in New York.

His parents, however, had not surrendered.

Mrs. Wells was a small, composed woman who had spent
her life employing gentle language to accomplish immovable purposes. In
the autumn of Alexander's fourth year in Texas, she wrote
that she feared her health would not permit a western
journey but longed to see him once more. Alexander answered
with affection, described the cabin as nearly inaccessible, and advised
strongly against travel.

She arrived in San Antonio three weeks later.

Mr. Brackett, now less surprised by the Wells family than
formerly, met her stage. The journey from the coast had
required twelve days and included a broken wheel, two flooded
crossings, and one night in a station where the roof
leaked upon the bed. When he expressed regret at her
hardships, Mrs. Wells smiled and called the expedition "a little
pasear," having acquired the word from a fellow passenger.

She rested one day, purchased a traveling dress of stout
material, hired a covered wagon, and directed Mr. Brackett to
take her to the grant.

Alexander knew nothing of this. On the afternoon of her
arrival he sat cross-legged upon a cowhide spread over the
cabin floor, playing monte with Tiburcio and two visitors from
a neighboring ranch. Silver dollars lay between them. A pot
of coffee stood on the hearth; three rifles occupied the
table; one dog slept beneath it; and another, having recently
encountered a skunk, had been tied at a tactful distance
from the house.

The wagon came up the trail unnoticed until the dogs
barked. Alexander glanced through the door, saw Mr. Brackett, and
continued gathering the cards. Then a woman descended from the
wagon.

He stood so suddenly that he upset the coffee.

His mother paused before the cabin. For one moment she
looked at the broad-shouldered, bearded man in a faded blue
shirt, at the cards on the cowhide, at the venison
hanging beneath the eaves, and at the saddle horse tied
ready near the corral. Her eyes then returned to his
face.

"Alexander," she said, "you appear better."

The neighboring ranchers withdrew with remarkable promptness. Tiburcio rose, removed
his hat, and stepped outside. Mr. Brackett became interested in
the wagon harness.

Alexander kissed his mother. He attempted an explanation of the
game, the coffee, his beard, and his health, but the
several subjects interfered with one another.

"I was only—That is, we had been riding, and the
doctor believed—"

"Yes," Mrs. Wells said. "I am sure the doctor did."

She remained at the cabin six days. No accusation passed
her lips. She slept upon Alexander's cot while he and
Tiburcio occupied blankets outside; ate venison, beans, and tortillas without
complaint; and rode a gentle mare along the creek. She
observed the clear water, the pecan groves, the cattle in
grass to their bellies, and the pleasure with which her
son explained each practical detail. She also observed that he
was entirely happy.

This knowledge did not weaken her purpose, but it made
her kind.

On the final evening she sat with Alexander beneath the
pecan tree. The sun had gone down, and beyond the
creek a flight of wild turkeys crossed between the trees,
their wings making a heavy rush in the still air.

"Your father is growing old," she said. "He will not
say so. I have said it for him."

Alexander looked toward the pasture.

"He needs you."

"I have not been much use to him."

"You have not tried."

This was true, and therefore difficult to answer.

After a little while she placed her hand on his
arm. "Come home with me, Alexander. If after a fair
trial you cannot remain, we shall speak of Texas again.
But come now."

He had prepared himself for commands, reproaches, even tears. The
quiet request defeated him.

"I—yes, Mother," he said. "I suppose I must."

His affairs were settled within a week. The cattle he
transferred to Tiburcio, subject to no account except that the
latter should keep what he could increase. The horses, rifles,
tools, furniture, and supplies likewise remained at the cabin.

"The field glass is yours," Alexander said.

Tiburcio shook his head. "When you come back, you will
need it."

"I may not come back."

Tiburcio wrapped the glass in buckskin and placed it on
the shelf.

"You will need it," he repeated.

Their farewell contained no speech about friendship. Men who have
shared cold camps, uncertain water, and the approach of wounded
animals generally know what place they hold in one another's
regard. They shook hands. Tiburcio then asked Mrs. Wells to
see that Alexander wore sufficient clothing on the journey, a
request which caused her to like him immediately and Alexander
to threaten him in Spanish.

The stage rattled out of San Antonio on a bright
morning. Alexander watched the adobe walls, church towers, acequias, and
cottonwoods fall behind. At the last rise he turned in
his seat and looked back until dust concealed the town.

New York received him with dinners.

His health was praised; his appearance was discussed; his adventures
were requested and interrupted. Men admired his knowledge of rifles
but considered his opinions concerning cattle unnecessarily detailed. Women found
his complexion becoming and his beard too long. Within a
month the latter was trimmed; within two he wore black
coats again; within three he occupied a high stool in
his father's office.

The shipping business was honorable, established, and profitable. It was
also conducted indoors. Alexander learned invoices, rates, bills of lading,
and insurance tables. He arrived late, forgot appointments, and once
approved a cargo for a port to which the vessel
did not sail. His father moved him to the western
land department, supposing his experience useful there. Alexander offended a
prospective purchaser by advising him that the tract under discussion
lacked permanent water.

A banking position followed. He remained six months and demonstrated
that indifference to money, admirable in camp, is not a
recommendation in a bank. Thereafter a place was found in
an importing house owned by his uncle. Alexander performed no
serious damage, but neither did he contribute enough to justify
the salary.

His failure was not due to stupidity. He understood each
business when it was explained, and might, under pressure of
poverty, have become competent. But he lacked the indispensable belief
that the matters before him were important. His thoughts escaped
continually to the creek: whether the spring had failed in
August; whether the gray mare had foaled; whether Tiburcio had
found the bear whose track they had followed through the
cedar hills.

Society proved no more successful than commerce. Mrs. Wells arranged
dinners at which Alexander met cultivated young women, several of
whom possessed beauty, intelligence, and property. Among them was Caroline
Meredith, whose interest had survived his Texas years. She listened
willingly to accounts of storms, hunts, and long rides, and
Alexander liked her with sincere respect.

A marriage was proposed—not formally at first, but by those
gradual family arrangements through which two people may discover that
their futures have been settled before either has spoken. Caroline
would have made an excellent wife. Alexander knew it. He
also knew that she loved music, disliked horses, feared firearms,
and considered any town lacking a theatre uninhabitable.

One evening she asked whether he had truly intended to
live permanently in his cabin.

"Yes," he answered.

"But surely you had grown tired of it."

"No."

She smiled, waiting for a qualification. None came.

The matter ended without quarrel. Caroline afterward married a lawyer
who became a judge, and there is every reason to
believe that she was happier than she would have been
beside a creek forty miles from San Antonio.

When Alexander turned thirty, his father invited him into the
library and closed the door. The older man had resisted
Texas first from anxiety, then from principle, and finally from
habit. He now recognized that his son had spent four
years attempting obedience and had produced nothing but dissatisfaction in
himself and inconvenience to others.

"You still wish to go back?" he asked.

"Every day."

"And what will you do?"

"Stock the land. Buy cattle—good cows, not too many. Build
another well on the north range. Tiburcio wrote that the
grass held through the drought. In time I can make
a ranch of it."

"You may lose money."

"I have lost your money here."

Jonathan Wells could not dispute this.

"You will sacrifice society, opportunities, perhaps a family."

Alexander walked to the window. Below, carriages moved in prescribed
lines along the wet street; bells sounded; men entered offices
and emerged carrying papers. The whole mechanism was useful, ingenious,
and oppressive to him.

"Father," he said, "men here are fitted to measures as
cloth is fitted to a pattern. They are told what
business to enter, whom to know, when to marry, where
to live, and what success must look like. Some are
made for it. I am not. Out there a man
may be poor without being ashamed, ignorant of fashion without
being ridiculous, and alone without being suspected. If he can
find water, manage his stock, keep his word, and take
care of himself and his neighbors, he has standing enough.
I know what I give up. But there is room
in the West—room not only for cattle and horses, but
for a man to be the shape God made him."

His father was silent for a long time.

At last he opened a drawer and took out the
papers relating to the Texas grant.

"Then you had better learn whether our title is good,"
he said.

Alexander left within the month. He carried fewer clothes than
on his first journey, no medicine, two improved rifles, a
new field glass for Tiburcio, and capital enough to purchase
three hundred breeding cows. The railway now shortened a portion
of the distance, but he resented every hour not spent
moving west.

Beyond the Mississippi the sky widened. Prairie replaced fields; settlements
grew farther apart; saddles appeared upon station platforms; men spoke
again of rain, grass, horses, and the condition of roads.
Alexander felt the restraints of four eastern years fall from
him one by one, as unnecessary garments are discarded in
warm weather.

At San Antonio he bought a horse before he secured
a room. On the following morning he rode southwest.

The familiar signs returned: mesquite on the flats, live-oaks upon
the rises, buzzards wheeling over the creek bottoms, and dust
lifting under distant cattle. Near sunset he reached the boundary
of the grant. A rider sat beneath the pecan tree
before the cabin, motionless except for the smoke of a
cigarette.

Tiburcio watched him approach. When Alexander dismounted, neither man hurried.

"You came back," Tiburcio said.

"Yes."

From inside the cabin Tiburcio brought the old field glass,
still wrapped in buckskin, and handed it to him.

Alexander laughed. Then the dogs recognized his voice and set
up a clamor; horses ran along the pasture fence; somewhere
in the gathering dusk a flock of turkeys rose from
the creek, beating the air with strong wings. The sounds
came to him not as reminders but as possession restored.

Civilization has called many men from the wilderness and made
them useful against their preference. Occasionally the wilderness calls one
back, using no argument that can be entered in a
ledger: only the cry of hounds in the monte, the
smell of rain upon dry grass, the rattle of pecans
on a cabin roof, and the sight of a trail
running beyond the next rise. Such a summons may be
dangerous, as all liberties are dangerous; but Alexander Wells had
at last ceased to mistake it for illness.

On the next morning he and Tiburcio rode north to
inspect the range. Before them lay the boundless West, rough,
sunlit, and sufficient.

The Young Trees

The rain had begun before dawn, not generously, not with
the broad, wholesome violence of a summer shower, but in
thin, cold lines that seemed to stitch earth and sky
together in gray mourning. By ten o'clock the wind had
risen. It drove the rain against the windows of the
new administration building, worried the flags nailed above the entrance,
and bent the young maples along the reformatory road until
their wet leaves swept the ground.

Within, however, it was a day of rejoicing.

So the printed programmes declared; so the superintendent had announced;
so the three newspapers represented by three damp young men
would doubtless report. The State had completed another monument to
its enlightened care. There were steam heat, tiled lavatories, a
library of improving books, classrooms with blackboards, and an assembly
hall capable of seating four hundred persons without serious inconvenience.
The old building—drafty, ill-smelling, dangerous—had been condemned after nineteen years
of complaint and four investigations. The new one was to
be dedicated with appropriate exercises.

On the platform sat the appropriate men.

There was the chairman of the Board of Correction, solemn
in a frock coat; the superintendent, whose large, pink face
expressed hospitality restrained by discipline; two clergymen; a professor of
social science; a manufacturer who had given the clock in
the tower; several legislators who had helped vote the appropriation
after opposing it; and Philip Grayson, candidate for governor.

Before them sat three hundred boys.

They had been washed, brushed, arranged, cautioned, marched in, and
seated according to cottage and number. Their uniforms were clean.
Their boots were heavy. Their hair had been clipped with
a thoroughness which suggested that individuality, like vermin, might flourish
near the roots if not sternly discouraged. The smaller boys
occupied the front rows. Behind them the heads rose by
degrees—fair heads, dark heads, round heads, narrow heads, heads held
defiantly erect and heads already bent in the posture of
habitual submission.

Grayson had looked at them when he entered, and many
had looked at him. They knew his face from the
campaign portraits hung in drugstores and railway stations: the strong,
lean face, the grave mouth which photographers called firm, the
deep-set eyes which newspapers called fearless. He had been told
that the boys were eager to hear him. That was
one of the little decorations added to such occasions. The
boys were eager for a holiday from the shops, eager
for the band, eager for the cake which rumour had
promised—and possibly curious about the man who might become governor
and thus, in some remote manner, master of the walls
about them.

The band played a march. The boys sang a hymn
about gratitude and guidance. For a moment, while their voices
filled the fresh-painted hall, something warm and living escaped the
regulations and rose above the platform. The thin soprano of
the little fellows mingled with the uncertain bass of the
older ones; one voice broke, another wandered from the tune,
and the whole became touching because it was imperfect.

Then the chairman rose.

He spoke of the beneficence of the Commonwealth. He spoke
of duty, opportunity, moral reclamation, and the obligation resting upon
each unfortunate youth to justify the expenditure made in his
behalf. He spoke for twenty-three minutes and was applauded in
the proper places by the men upon the platform.

Grayson listened at first with the patience of one accustomed
to public exercises. He had himself used most of the
phrases, though never, he hoped, quite so many at once.
There were phrases which belonged to occasions as brass handles
belonged to coffins. One did not examine them. One grasped
them, performed the required office, and passed on.

Yet that morning the words irritated him.

Perhaps it was the storm. It beat at the high
windows as if the world outside had some urgent contradiction
to make. Perhaps it was the rows of boys, so
unnaturally still. Or perhaps it was the chairman's repeated emphasis
upon what the State had done for "these unfortunates."

These unfortunates!

Grayson studied the nearest faces. A freckled child in the
front row sat with his hands folded upon his knees,
his lips moving as he counted the buttons on the
chairman's waistcoat. Beside him a sharp-faced boy stared at the
platform with a composure too old for him. Farther back
sat a broad-shouldered fellow of seventeen or eighteen, his jaw
thrust forward, his eyes fixed upon nothing. What had he
done? Stolen, very likely. Struck somebody. Run away from a
farm where there had been too much work and too
little kindness. Or perhaps he had done something cruel, something
ugly, something which could not be softened by explanation.

But was the ugliness all in him?

The chairman's voice rolled on. The State, in its wisdom—the
State, in its mercy—the State, in its munificence—

The State! Grayson saw suddenly, not an august mother with
open arms, but a large gentleman keeping account of every
mouthful bestowed upon a hungry child.

Applause sounded. The chairman sat down, mopping his forehead, and
the professor of social science took his place.

The professor had brought statistics.

He showed that juvenile misconduct increased where home influences declined,
that disciplined labour produced exemplary habits, and that proper classification
prevented the contamination of the merely wayward by the fundamentally
delinquent. He referred to the boys as material—promising material, refractory
material, human material to be shaped for citizenship. His voice
was mild, his conclusions humane, his cuffs irreproachable.

Grayson shifted in his chair.

Material! Were they timber, then? Clay? Bundles of defective fibre
to be sorted and planed and stamped with the State's
approval? The professor meant well. That was the trouble. They
all meant well, and their good intentions hung between the
platform and the boys like thick glass—transparent enough to look
through, too solid to touch through.

A gust struck the building. Rain hissed down the windows,
and one of the flags outside tore loose at a
corner and snapped frantically.

Grayson glanced toward the superintendent. The superintendent smiled, reassured by
the solidity of the new walls. The weather provided an
excellent demonstration of sound construction.

Without, the young maples bowed and sprang back, bowed and
sprang back. Their branches tossed in passionate insurrection. The wind
seized their leaves and turned their pale undersides outward, as
though wringing from them a sign of surrender. But they
would not surrender. They bent because they must, rose because
they could, and bent again.

Within, the professor explained the corrective value of obedience.

Another hymn followed. Then the manufacturer spoke.

He was a kindly man, proud of having begun life
with twelve dollars and a second-hand tool chest. He told
the boys that honesty was the best policy, punctuality the
first rung of success, and industry the key which opened
every door. He assured them that when they left the
institution, the world would receive them with open arms, provided
they demonstrated a determination to deserve its confidence.

At that several of the older boys looked down.

The movement was slight, almost collective—the lowering of eyes which
had seen something the speaker had not seen. Grayson caught
it. The world's open arms! He imagined a discharged boy
standing at a factory gate.

"Where have you worked?"

"At the State school, sir."

The foreman's face changing. The pause. "Nothing today."

At another gate the same question, the same answer, the
same closing. Open arms? The world had arms, certainly—arms to
push away, arms to point backward, arms to fold in
righteous caution across a respectable chest.

The manufacturer had never been in a reformatory. His twelve
dollars and tool chest had been accompanied by a decent
name, a mother's blessing, and the recommendation of a church
elder. Yet he was telling these boys that the road
lay straight before them, as though they had only to
place polished shoes upon it.

The coldness!—the comfortlessness!—of it all.

Grayson turned the folded pages of his own speech. It
was a good speech. His secretary had gathered figures, and
Grayson himself had added a paragraph about the sacred partnership
between public authority and private character. There was a sentence
which the party manager particularly admired: "The Commonwealth, having rescued
the erring youth from the consequences of his immature transgression,
may rightfully demand from him an enriched and disciplined citizenship."

He read it now with a kind of nausea.

Rescued! Demand!

He looked again at the boys. Those who made speeches
and those who must listen. Those who commended virtue and
those upon whom virtue was imposed. The so-called good sitting
high beneath flags, and the so-called bad sitting low beneath
watchful attendants. Between them a chasm carefully decorated with flowers.

Had he come merely to widen it?

The first clergyman offered prayer. He asked that the boys
might resist temptation, control their baser impulses, honour those placed
above them, and become worthy of the sacrifices made for
their redemption.

Grayson bowed his head. The prayer was faultless. Why, then,
did it seem almost indecent? Was temptation peculiar to cropped
heads and numbered coats? Had no baser impulse ever mounted
the platform? Did ambition cease to be ambition when it
wore a black coat? Did greed become responsibility upon election
to a board? Did anger become moral force when expressed
in editorials?

He felt the folded speech under his fingers.

"No," he thought. "I can't give them this. Not after
the rest. I can't stand up and throw one more
stone of goodness down upon them."

Then came the warning voice—the practical, experienced voice which had
governed many necessary compromises.

This was no place for experiments. The election was six
weeks away. Every sentence would be reported. His opponent's committee
had men searching his life for indiscretions and his speeches
for weapons. Grayson was leading, but not safely. The rural
counties distrusted novelty; the cities distrusted reformers; the party wanted
dignity, moderation, no surprises.

He had a wife. He had obligations. He had pledged
himself to prison reform, labour arbitration, honest taxation. If elected,
he might accomplish actual good. Was he entitled to risk
all that for a moment's emotional rebellion against tedious speeches?

Besides, what could he say? That he disliked the ceremony?
That the chairman was pompous and the professor bloodless? Irritation
was not truth. One did not tear down an imperfect
bridge unless one could put something in its place.

The superintendent leaned toward him.

"You follow Reverend Pike," he whispered. "Then the boys sing,
and we inspect the dormitories."

Grayson nodded.

Reverend Pike was brief and hearty. He had addressed boys
before and understood the usefulness of anecdote. He told of
an inmate of another institution who, through obedience and perseverance,
had risen to become assistant cashier in a bank. The
reverend did not know whether the story was true, but
truth, in such cases, was less important than tendency. He
urged every boy to choose this day whom he would
serve, and sat down amid brisk applause.

The superintendent announced Philip Grayson.

The applause was louder now. Some of it was genuine
curiosity, some obedience, some relief that the final address had
come. Grayson rose and carried his manuscript to the lectern.

The hall seemed suddenly very still.

He looked over it—at the officials, at the attendants stationed
along the walls, at the reporters ready with pencils, at
the boys. Three hundred pairs of eyes. Not all hopeful;
certainly not all innocent. Some mocking, some guarded, some dull
with the learned refusal to expect anything. But human eyes,
every pair of them. Eyes that had watched quarrels, hunger,
drunkenness, policemen, courtroom ceilings, mothers crying; eyes that had perhaps
lied, cheated, hated; eyes that had perhaps seen tenderness too,
and remembered it secretly.

He unfolded the speech.

"My friends," he began, "we are assembled today to dedicate
this admirable building, which represents the enlightened generosity of the
State—"

The words came from habit, full and practiced.

A leaf, plastered by rain against the nearest window, shook
loose and vanished.

Grayson stopped.

One of the reporters glanced up. The superintendent's smile tightened.

Grayson looked down at the pages. Then, slowly, he folded
them once, twice, and put them in his pocket.

"Boys," he said, "that is what I had intended to
say. It is printed nicely, and it has a good
many long words in it. But you've heard enough long
words this morning."

There was a small movement through the hall. A boy
near the rear laughed once and suppressed it.

"I've been sitting here listening," Grayson continued, "and I've been
wondering what I should think if I were down there
instead of up here. I think I should decide that
all the good people in the world sat on platforms
and all the bad people sat in rows. I think
I should decide that goodness meant being allowed to talk,
and badness meant having to listen."

This time the laughter spread. It was uncertain laughter, but
alive. Even one of the attendants smiled before remembering himself.

The chairman did not smile.

"Now, that isn't true," Grayson said. "It isn't nearly true.
And if I stood here and pretended it was, I
should be telling you a lie."

He paused. His heart was beating with unreasonable force. There
remained time to retreat—to turn this into an agreeable joke
and proceed safely. Then he saw the broad-shouldered boy lift
his head. The defiant face had altered; not softened, but
awakened.

"Most of the gentlemen who have spoken to you mean
kindly," Grayson went on. "They want you to do well.
So do I. They have told you that the State
has given you this building, and that you owe the
State good conduct in return. Well, perhaps you do. A
decent building is better than a bad one. A warm
room is better than a cold room. But bricks don't
make a boy good, and a steam pipe can't teach
him courage. We mustn't praise ourselves too loudly because we've
stopped putting children in places unfit for cattle."

A cough came sharply from behind him. It was the
party manager, Henry Vale, seated at the end of the
platform.

Grayson heard it and continued.

"You've been told that you are here because you gave
way to unfortunate tendencies. That is a polished expression. I
suppose it means you did wrong. Some of you did
a little wrong, and some of you did a great
deal. Some of you were caught the first time. Some
weren't caught until the fiftieth. Some may be here partly
because no one stood beside you when trouble came."

The smallest boys watched without comprehension, responding only to his
tone. Among the older ones there was no movement now.

"But look here," he said, leaning forward. "Do you think
people outside these walls never do wrong? Do you think
the difference between you and us is that you've got
bad in you and we haven't?"

He shook his head.

"The difference is not so simple. Sometimes the difference is
that one boy gets caught and another boy escapes. Sometimes
one has a father with influence and another has no
father at all. Sometimes one has friends who cover his
foolishness, and another has enemies who publish it. And sometimes—let's
be fair—sometimes one boy fights the bad thing in himself
and another boy lets it master him. But the fight
is in all of us. In every one."

There was danger in general confession, but it was still
merely general. Henry Vale's shoulders relaxed a fraction.

Grayson saw and almost smiled.

"You may say, 'That's easy for him. He's a lawyer.
He's running for governor. His picture is in the papers,
and everybody says he's respectable.' Well, I know something about
respectable people. I've been one for years."

Laughter came again, warmer now.

"And I know how respectability can cover a man like
varnish until he looks smooth and fine and can't feel
the hand of anybody outside. I don't want to talk
to you through varnish. I want to talk man to
man."

He gripped the sides of the lectern.

"When I was fifteen, I thought rules were invented by
cowards to annoy brave people. At sixteen, I believed a
man proved himself by doing whatever older men told him
not to do. I lied to my father. I stole
money from his desk—not much, but enough, and the amount
doesn't change the name of the act. I drank because
I hated the taste and wanted my companions to think
I liked it. I gambled because I was ashamed to
admit I couldn't afford to lose. I called obedience weakness
and bad temper spirit. If anybody had spoken to me
about the generosity of the State, I should probably have
wanted to break a window."

The broad-shouldered boy smiled outright.

Grayson's voice had become quieter. Every word carried farther because
the hall strained to receive it.

"My father was a stern man. He loved me, but
he had a poor way of showing it, and I
had a worse way of understanding it. We quarrelled. He
said I was going to ruin. I said I didn't
care. The truth was, I cared desperately. But pride is
a bully. It gets hold of you and says, 'Don't
let them know you're hurt. Strike first. Laugh. Curse. Do
anything—but don't let them know.'"

Several faces changed at that. Grayson saw recognition pass among
them like light passing over troubled water.

"Did you ever have that bully sense inside you? The
one that tells you a small hurt must be paid
for with a great one? The one that would rather
lose a friend than say, 'I was wrong'? I had
it. I have it yet, though it wears better clothes
now."

The professor looked down at his hands.

"One night," Grayson said, "when I was seventeen, I was
playing cards with three fellows in the back room of
a livery stable. We had been drinking. Not much, we
told ourselves—only enough to make every insult larger and every
bit of common sense smaller. My closest friend was there.
His name was Will Mercer.

"Will and I had grown up together. We had fished
in the same creek, slept in the same hayloft, taken
punishment for each other, and sworn that nothing could come
between us. That night he accused me of cheating."

A faint sound escaped someone in the audience—not a word,
only breath.

"I had cheated."

Grayson let the admission stand bare.

"I had marked one card with my thumbnail. Will saw
it. He told me to put the money back. If
I'd done it, if I'd laughed and admitted it, my
whole life might have turned on that little act. But
the bully inside me spoke. I called him a liar.
He called me a cheat. He was right, but I
hated him for being right. I struck him. He struck
me back.

"There was a revolver on a shelf. The stable owner
kept it there. I took it up."

On the platform a chair creaked violently. Henry Vale leaned
forward, his face drained of expression.

Grayson no longer saw him. He saw a low room
yellow with lamplight; stained cards scattered upon a box; Will's
young face pale with anger; two frightened companions pressed against
the wall. He smelled dust, horse sweat, cheap whisky, and
the bitter metal odour rising from the weapon in his
own hand.

"I pointed it at my friend," he said. "At the
best friend I had in the world. And I fired."

The silence was complete. Even the rain seemed to withdraw
from the windows.

"I missed him. Not because I meant to miss. My
hand jerked, or he moved, or God was kinder to
me than I had any right to expect. The bullet
went into the wall beside his head."

Grayson drew a breath.

"If it had gone three inches to the left, I
should not be a candidate for governor. I might not
be alive. I should have been a murderer. Do you
understand? The evil was not less because my aim was
bad. The law would have called the result different, but
the thing in me—the rage that lifted that pistol and
pulled the trigger—was what it was."

A little boy in front stared with his mouth open.
An older boy slowly closed his eyes.

"The other fellows hid the revolver. Will told the stable
owner that a cartridge had gone off by accident. There
was a hole in the wall, but no one asked
many questions. My companions protected me. My father knew nothing.
The police knew nothing. The newspapers never heard. I went
home before morning, and I was not arrested.

"So there you have the difference between some of you
and me. I missed. My friends lied for me. I
had a home to go to. That was the bridge
by which I walked away from prison."

The chairman's face had turned a mottled red. The superintendent
sat rigid, his eyes moving from Grayson to the reporters.
Their pencils were racing now.

"But I did not walk away from what I had
done," Grayson continued. "For months I heard that shot. I
heard it in doors slamming, in wheels striking stones, in
a book falling on the floor. I saw the mark
beside Will's head. I began to understand that I was
carrying something inside me more dangerous than any revolver.

"At first I blamed the whisky. Then I blamed Will.
Then my father. Then the fellows who had laughed at
me. We're clever at finding shoulders for our own burdens.
But one morning I looked at myself and said, 'No.
You did it. You cheated. You lied. You took the
weapon. You fired it.'"

His hand loosened upon the lectern.

"That was not the end of my trouble. It was
the beginning of my chance."

The rain had softened. It moved down the panes now
in long, wavering threads.

"I wish I could tell you that from that morning
I became good. It would make a tidy story. It
would also be false. I fell again. I drank again.
I lied more than once. I hurt people who trusted
me. But I knew there was a fight, and I
knew which side I wanted to win.

"That is what I've come to tell you. Not that
I'm good and you're bad. Not that we on this
platform have climbed some hill from which we can call
directions to you in the mud. I'm telling you that
every man has a fight within him—the generous thing against
the mean thing, courage against cowardice, truth against the easy
lie. A man may wear a judge's robe and lose
that fight. A boy may wear a numbered coat and
win it."

The sharp-faced boy in the front row blinked quickly.

"Winning doesn't mean never falling. It means getting up with
a little more honesty than you had before. It means
knowing your favorite excuse and refusing to be fooled by
it. It means saying, 'Yes, that anger is mine—but it
isn't my master. That appetite is mine—but it doesn't give
orders. That fear is mine—but it doesn't choose my road.'

"Some people will tell you to bury the bad in
you. I don't know that buried things always stay buried.
Better drag them into the daylight and learn their names.
Mine were vanity, temper, and fear—especially fear. I was afraid
of being laughed at, afraid of seeming weak, afraid of
admitting affection. So I acted bold. Many a coward wears
the coat of a bully."

An attendant near the wall looked away.

"And don't make the opposite mistake," Grayson said. "Don't bury
the good in you because somebody has called you bad.
A label is a convenient thing for an institution. It
is not a complete account of a human soul. Perhaps
you stole; that does not mean there is no honesty
in you. Perhaps you struck someone; that does not mean
there is no tenderness. Perhaps you have lied until nobody
believes you; truth is still there, waiting to be used.
The good may be weak from neglect. Use it, and
it grows."

He turned slightly and looked at the officials seated behind
him.

"We have talked much today about what you owe the
State. The State owes something too. It owes justice without
contempt. It owes discipline without humiliation. It owes training that
leads to work, and work that is honestly available when
training is done. Above all, those of us who call
ourselves good citizens owe you the chance to become citizens
without being pushed back forever by the name of what
you once were."

No one coughed now.

"When you leave here, I hope the world will receive
you kindly. But I won't promise that it will open
its arms. The world is not one person. It is
made of frightened people, selfish people, generous people, busy people—people
very much like you and me. Some will help. Some
will distrust you. Some will enjoy reminding you where you
have been.

"When that happens, you may think all this talk of
a fresh start was a trick. You may say, 'They
told me to go straight, and then they blocked the
straight road.' You may be tempted to take the old
road because at least you know its turns."

He leaned toward them once more, and now there was
no candidate, no dignitary, no selected final speaker—only a man
urgently attempting to reach other human beings across a distance
which ceremony had failed to cross.

"Don't do it without giving somebody a chance to stand
with you.

"If you leave here and can't find work, write to
me. If a man turns you away because you've been
here, write to me. If you get into a tight
place and feel the old thing rising—the anger, the hunger,
the bully pride—write before you act. I can't promise to
solve everything. I'm not rich enough, wise enough, or powerful
enough for that. I may be able to find a
job. I may know a man who will give you
a fair hearing. At least I can answer.

"My address isn't a secret. The newspapers print it often
enough, and if they stop, the city directory will have
it."

A low ripple of amusement passed through the hall, breaking
the tension without destroying it.

"I mean this," he said. "Not as a speaker. Not
as a candidate. Man to man, I mean it.

"You have a hard road before you. Some of that
hardness you made yourselves. Some was made before you were
old enough to choose. It doesn't matter much, when a
road climbs, who put the hill there. What matters is
whether you climb it—and whether someone gives you a hand
when your foot slips.

"I fired at my friend. I have never forgotten it.
But the worst act of my youth was not the
whole truth about me. Neither is the worst act of
yours the whole truth about you.

"Fight for the rest of the truth."

He stopped.

For one suspended instant nothing happened. The officials remained motionless.
The reporters' pencils rested. The boys seemed scarcely to breathe.

Then the broad-shouldered youth stood up.

An attendant moved instinctively toward him, but the boy only
brought his hands together—once, twice, hard. Another boy rose. Then
another. The applause came not in the regulated patter which
had followed the earlier speeches, but in a great rough
crash, broken by whistles, shouts, and the stamping of heavy
boots. The smaller boys climbed upon the benches to see.
Faces which had been guarded were open now; faces which
had been dull were bright. One boy laughed while tears
ran down his cheeks, and, embarrassed by them, clapped more
violently.

Three hundred boys were no longer material, delinquents, obligations, or
unfortunate tendencies. They were boys—wounded, guilty, foolish, courageous, frightened boys—and
for a few minutes they believed that a man outside
their walls had seen them whole.

Grayson stood with his head lowered.

He had known applause in packed theatres, applause timed by
committees, applause swelled by bands and flags and partisan expectation.
He had never heard anything like this. It did not
flatter him. It accused him, humbled him, and gave back
more than he had given.

Behind him the chairman whispered furiously to the superintendent. The
professor polished his spectacles though they were already clear. Reverend
Pike looked troubled, then thoughtful. The manufacturer who had spoken
of open arms stared at the boys with a new,
uncomfortable attention, as if he had just remembered the existence
of factory gates.

Henry Vale did not applaud.

At last the superintendent raised both hands. Order returned slowly.
The boys sat, still turning toward Grayson, still smiling at
him as though a hidden door had opened in the
wall.

The final hymn was omitted. No one knew quite how
to sing it.

During the inspection that followed, officials talked too loudly about
ventilation, plumbing, and fireproof stairways. The chairman attached himself to
a legislator and avoided Grayson. The superintendent explained the dormitory
arrangement with the strained cheerfulness of a host whose guest
has broken an heirloom. Only the professor approached Grayson.

"That was extraordinarily irregular," he said.

"Yes."

"And your generalization concerning social labels was scientifically incomplete."

"I don't doubt it."

The professor hesitated. "But possibly useful."

He walked away before Grayson could answer.

In the corridor outside the workshops, the boys passed in
a line on their way to dinner. Regulations forbade conversation,
but several looked directly at Grayson. One touched two fingers
to his forehead. Another grinned. The broad-shouldered boy came last.

"What's your name?" Grayson asked.

"Daniel Kerr, sir."

"How long have you?"

"Four months, if I keep my marks."

"Write to me, Daniel."

The boy searched his face, testing the promise for weakness.

"You might be governor then," he said.

"I might not."

Daniel's mouth twitched. "Directory either way?"

"Directory either way."

The attendant urged the line onward. Daniel went, but the
defiance in his shoulders had changed. It had not vanished—and
perhaps should not vanish. It had become something straighter, less
like resistance to a blow and more like readiness to
bear weight.

Grayson and Henry Vale left the building shortly after four.
The rain had ceased. Water dripped from the eaves and
ran in bright channels beside the road. In the west,
low beneath the broken clouds, a strip of pale gold
had appeared.

Vale waited until they had entered the motorcar.

"Well," he said.

Grayson removed his hat and wiped the rain from its
brim.

"Well?"

"Do you understand what you've done?"

"I told the truth to three hundred boys."

"You confessed to attempted murder in front of three reporters."

"I confessed to firing at a man and missing him."

Vale stared. "I am relieved by the distinction."

The car started down the drive. Its tyres hissed through
shallow water.

"By tomorrow morning," Vale continued, "every paper in the State
will have it. By tomorrow afternoon, the opposition will have
broadsides printed. 'Grayson, Drunkard, Gambler, Gunman.' They won't care that
you were seventeen. They won't care that the man wasn't
hit. They'll say you concealed a felony. They'll ask what
else you've concealed."

"Yes."

"We had the election."

"Perhaps."

"We had it!" Vale struck his knee with his fist.
"After twelve years of their crowd, we had it—and you
handed them the winning card because you were tired of
listening to bad speeches."

Grayson looked through the wet glass.

The young maples stood along the drive. Leaves and small
branches littered the ground around them. One tree leaned so
far that its roots had lifted the earth in a
dark crescent, but the trunk had not split. The others
were rising in the calm after the storm, shedding water,
their slender bodies shaken yet unbroken.

"No," Grayson said. "Not because the speeches were bad."

Vale made an impatient sound.

"I looked at those boys, Henry. We were telling them
to fight their way back into a world that takes
pride in shutting doors. We were asking them for faith
and giving them phrases. I couldn't add another phrase."

"You're a candidate for governor. You have to think of
more than the feeling of a room."

"I did think of more."

"Of what?"

"Of the first boy who goes to three factory gates
and is turned away. Of the one who decides that
because we call him bad, he may as well be
bad. Of Daniel Kerr, who has four months left and
expects every promise to be a lie."

Vale was silent for a time.

"You can't save them all," he said finally.

"No."

"You may not save one."

"No."

"And you may have destroyed your chance to reform the
whole system."

Grayson leaned back. Weariness came upon him now that the
need for courage had passed.

"That is possible," he said. "I don't pretend to know
whether I did right in every consequence. A man usually
sees only a few feet of the road when he
makes a real choice. But I know I could not
ask those boys to face the worst truth about themselves
while I hid the worst truth about me."

Vale watched him with mingled anger and unwilling respect.

"You always were a difficult candidate."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you aren't."

A faint smile crossed Grayson's face. "Not yet."

They reached the outer gate. The guard saluted, the iron
leaves swung apart, and the car passed from the reformatory
grounds into the public road.

Behind them the new building shone wetly in the late
light. It was still an institution—brick, lock, rule, number. One
speech had not altered its machinery. The attendants would count
the boys that evening; doors would close; lamps would go
out at the regulated hour. Some promises would be forgotten,
some hopes fail, some boys return by old and bitter
roads.

Yet in the assembly hall there remained, like warmth after
fire, the memory of a man who had refused the
safe distance between the virtuous and the condemned. Three hundred
boys had heard that wickedness was not their private inheritance,
that goodness was not the property of respectable men, that
a falling might be followed by a rising again. And
among them, perhaps, one boy would remember before lifting his
hand in rage; one would write before stealing; one would
endure the closing of a door because he believed another
door might open.

The clouds broke wider. Evening sunlight reached across the drenched
fields and touched the tops of the maples.

They had been planted only that spring, each tied to
a stake and instructed, after the manner of men, in
the direction it ought to grow. The storm had torn
loose several of the bindings. Free of them, the young
trees swayed gently in the clearing air—not straight, not all
alike, but rooted still, and lifting.

Unbroken. Unhurt.

Growing toward the light.

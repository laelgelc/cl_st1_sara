# TWO ALTARS

## I. THE OFFERING OF 1776

The sun was going down over a New England winter
landscape, and, having done but a scant day's work in
the way of warming the earth, seemed resolved to make
amends by painting it.

Across the western sky lay long bars of crimson and
amber; the snow upon the hills caught the glow and
gave it back in purple shadows, lilac hollows, and rose-colored
ridges. Every fence rail wore a white cap; every stone
wall had drawn a soft coverlet over its rough gray
shoulders; and the naked elms stood black and delicate against
that brisk, clear, metallic heaven which belongs to winter alone.

The wind, meanwhile, cared nothing for the sunset. He was
a roguish fellow, with cold fingers and a most impertinent
curiosity. He peeped beneath the eaves, shook the latch of
the Ward farmhouse, slapped the loose shutter, and then ran
whistling down the chimney, where he was met by such
a cloud of smoke, sparks, and indignant warmth that he
retreated with a howl.

Within, all was life.

The great kitchen fireplace was wide enough to roast an
ox—or so Dick Ward had always declared, though Aunt Hitty
insisted that if an ox ever came into her kitchen
she would like to know who was to scrub afterward.
A pine knot blazed upon the hearth. A black kettle
sang over the flames; a smaller one, less musical but
more useful, bubbled with apple sauce; and three flatirons stood
warming like grave, triangular officials awaiting their turn at duty.

Mrs. Ward sat near the table, mending a soldier's shirt
by candlelight. Her face was pale and composed, and the
smooth bands of brown hair beneath her white cap had
already begun to show threads of silver. One might have
supposed her occupied with no weightier matter than the turning
of a cuff; yet every stitch she set was part
of the war.

Aunt Hitty, short, energetic, and built upon a plan which
seemed expressly contrived for resisting wind, weather, taxation, and human
folly, was knitting a gray stocking.

Grace, aged eight, was winding yarn.

Dick, who was eleven and therefore considered himself virtually ready
for the command of troops, sat astride a wooden stool,
cutting paper soldiers out of an old almanac.

Little Ben gathered chips from the wood box to the
hearth—a wholesome and gracious employment much recommended to children in
those days, chiefly because the chips were wanted and the
children could be caught.

"Now, Ben," said Aunt Hitty, without looking at him, "if
you put another chip in that kettle instead of the
fire, I shall conclude you're enlisted on the British side."

"I ain't," said Ben. "I'm Continental."

"You're contrary, anyhow."

"Is that the same?"

"Near enough for family purposes."

Dick brandished a paper general.

"When I get to the army," he said, "I shall
ride a black horse, and General Washington'll say, 'Colonel Ward,
take them redcoats on the left!' Then I shall say,
'Leave 'em to me, sir!' and—whack!—"

His gesture swept three paper soldiers, the pincushion, and Aunt
Hitty's ball of yarn to the floor.

"Colonel Ward," said Aunt Hitty, "will kindly conquer under the
table until my yarn is recovered."

Grace laughed. Ben shouted, "Whack!" and overturned the chip basket.

Mrs. Ward smiled, but only for a moment. Her needle
had stopped in the cloth.

There was a sound at the door.

Every face turned.

It was not Captain Ward. It was only Nathan Pike,
the post rider, white with snow and carrying a leather
pouch beneath his coat.

But the heart does not always distinguish, in its first
leap, between hope and certainty. Mrs. Ward rose so quickly
that her chair fell backward.

"A letter?" she asked.

Nathan removed his hat.

"A letter, ma'am. From the captain."

There are letters which are merely paper and ink; there
are letters which are doors, opened suddenly upon fields of
blood, prison rooms, fever beds, oceans, and graves. Mrs. Ward
took this one with both hands.

"Sit down, Nathan," said Aunt Hitty, bustling toward the fire.
"You're froze to the backbone. Dick, hang up his coat.
Grace, fetch the mug. Ben, don't fetch anything. Your talents
air of another order."

But Nathan shook his head. Other houses waited; other mothers,
wives, and children had seen him pass along the road.
He drank the hot cider standing, bowed awkwardly, and went
again into the bitter dusk.

The family gathered close while Mrs. Ward broke the seal.

The letter had been carried many miles, folded and refolded,
guarded from rain beneath a soldier's coat, and stained at
one corner with something brown. Mud, perhaps. Let us call
it mud, since those who wait at home must sometimes
live by merciful names.

Captain Ward wrote from the army near Valley Forge. The
great Declaration had been proclaimed the year before; bells had
rung, flags had flown, and men had spoken nobly of
self-evident truths. Now came the winter account of those truths.

The army lacked food. It lacked shoes. Men marched over
frozen roads and left blood where their feet had passed.
Coats hung in rags. Blankets were scarce. Fires were small,
flour was dear, meat was sometimes a memory, and the
cause of liberty, which had looked in July like a
bright angel with a trumpet, now sat barefoot beside a
smoky campfire and asked whether the people still believed in
her.

Captain Ward did not complain. Men of his sort often
reserve their complaints for weather that cannot hear and tools
that cannot be hurt. He wrote that his health was
tolerable; that the general remained firm; that the men, though
tried, were steadfast; that a parcel of woolen stockings would
be of great service; and that he hoped, by God's
mercy, to return when independence was secured.

At the bottom he had added:

"Tell Dick that courage is not noise. Tell Grace her
little hymn book is in my breast pocket. Kiss Ben,
though I dare say he will resist. And to you,
my dear wife, I can only say that when the
wind is sharpest I think of our hearth, and it
warms me."

Mrs. Ward read the letter through without faltering.

Then she laid it down.

"Mother," Grace whispered, "is father cold?"

Mrs. Ward looked at the fire.

"Yes, child."

"Very cold?"

"Yes."

Grace considered this, her small fingers tightening around the yarn.
Warmth, to her, had always been a thing as natural
as mother's hand, morning porridge, and the sun. That her
father should be cold while the fire before her leaped
so lavishly seemed an injustice that required instant correction.

"He may have my red cloak," she said.

Aunt Hitty clicked her needles faster.

"Your red cloak don't come to his knees."

"Then he can give it to a little soldier."

"There aren't any little soldiers."

"There's Dick."

"There's only one Dick, thank Providence, and he's here."

"I shan't always be," Dick said grandly.

"You will if I can help it."

Mrs. Ward lifted the gray shirt from her lap.

"We shall send what we can," she said. "There is
the blue blanket from the spare bed."

"The best blanket?" asked Aunt Hitty.

"The warmest."

"There's the old brown one."

"It is thin."

"So is our company," Aunt Hitty replied. "Nobody comes visiting
in wartime except collectors, committees, and relations who expect feeding.
Still—the blue is a good blanket."

"For that reason," said Mrs. Ward, "it must go."

There was no argument after that.

The room became a council chamber. Every cupboard, chest, peg,
drawer, and shelf was put under examination. The people of
a republic yet unborn took inventory of their private comforts.

Two linen shirts were found in Captain Ward's chest, and
a third in need of a sleeve. Aunt Hitty declared
that a sleeve was a mere detail in a cause
involving human rights, sat up half the night, and supplied
it. Three pairs of stockings were completed. Mrs. Ward cut
down an old wool petticoat for chest protectors. Dick offered
his second-best cap, withdrew the offer when reminded that it
bore a tassel, then, ashamed, added the best one also.

Grace brought her cloak.

"My dear," said her mother, "you need it."

"But I said."

"Your father would not have you go cold."

"He goes cold."

That was the whole argument, and it conquered.

The cloak was laid with the rest.

Ben contributed a mitten.

"Where is the other?" asked Aunt Hitty.

"I don't know."

"No army on this earth can be kept warm by
one mitten."

"Maybe there's a soldier with one hand."

Aunt Hitty opened her mouth, shut it, and put the
mitten in the pile.

The next morning the kitchen took on the air of
a manufactory. Dough was mixed and rolled for hard biscuits.
Apples were sliced for drying. Thread, soap, cloth, and herbs
were gathered. Mrs. Ward brought down a small bag of
silver coins from the upper cupboard.

Aunt Hitty stared.

"Not all of it, Martha."

"All but what we need for seed."

"You were saving that."

"Yes."

"For Dick's schooling."

"Yes."

Dick's patriotic ardor encountered, for one sobering instant, arithmetic.

"Mother," he said, "I can learn without money."

"You must learn with or without it."

"I can read father's books."

"You can begin by not cutting soldiers from the index,"
said Aunt Hitty.

But even she spoke gently.

There is a manner of giving in which the giver
keeps one hand upon the gift, recounts its price, and
expects the world to admire the empty place. There was
none of that in Mrs. Ward. She counted the coins
once, tied them in a cloth, and placed them with
the stockings.

"They will buy flour," she said.

The children stood around the table, surveying the heap.

It had an oddly solemn appearance. A cloak, a cap,
linen shirts, knitted stockings, dried apples, herbs, soap, biscuits, silver,
and one unmatched mitten—such were the visible offerings of liberty.

Do not despise them. Principles make brave speeches in halls,
but they must eat in camps. Independence may sign her
name in a bold hand, yet some woman must spin
the thread that covers her nakedness. Liberty, if she comes
into this world at all, comes like a child: helpless,
shivering, costly, and demanding the labor of every heart that
loves her.

Later that week the meetinghouse bell rang at noon, and
the people gathered with parcels. A great sled stood before
the door, its runners sunk in snow. Men brought sacks
of grain and sides of pork. Women came with blankets,
shirts, bandages, stockings, candles, mittens, and jars of preserves. A
blacksmith gave nails. A cobbler gave shoes, though his own
children wore patched ones. Old Mr. Bell surrendered a silver-hilted
cane, announcing that he could hobble toward freedom with a
stick cut from the hedge.

Aunt Hitty arrived carrying the blue blanket.

"There," she said to the committee. "It's sound, no moth
holes, and wide enough for two thin men or one
officer with ambitions."

Dick carried the parcel of clothing.

Grace carried the red cloak.

Ben carried his mitten and refused to surrender it except
directly to the minister, whom he apparently regarded as commander
of the army's one-handed division.

The minister stood upon the meetinghouse steps and spoke of
sacrifice.

He spoke of Abraham, of altars, of the blood of
patriots, of the sacred cause, and of the great structure
of freedom which must be founded upon courage and self-denial.
His words rose into the frosty air in white clouds.
Men uncovered their heads. Women wept quietly. Children grew still
because their elders were still.

Then, when the hymn had been sung and the people
began to disperse, Dick walked beside his mother.

"What did you give, Mother?"

Mrs. Ward glanced toward the sled.

"You saw."

"No—I mean your own thing. Grace gave her cloak, and
I gave my caps, and Aunt Hitty gave the blanket,
and Ben gave half a pair of mittens. What did
you give?"

Grace slipped her hand into her mother's.

Mrs. Ward stopped.

Across the common the winter sunset burned behind the meetinghouse
steeple. The loaded sled stood waiting like a humble altar
beneath the sky. Upon it lay the neighborhood's warmth, sleep,
food, savings, handiwork, and ease.

What had Mrs. Ward given?

Her husband was in a frozen camp. Her eldest son,
Samuel, had gone the previous spring and lay buried near
Long Island in a grave she had never seen. Dick
spoke daily of joining when he was old enough. Grace's
cloak lay upon the sled. Ben's small life, and all
their lives, must rise or fall with a cause whose
end no mortal could promise.

She drew her children near.

"I have given what God lent me," she said. "Your
father first; Samuel after him; and you, if the time
should come. I have laid my home, my husband, and
my children upon the altar of liberty."

"Mother!" cried Grace.

Mrs. Ward bent and kissed her.

"Not because I love liberty more than I love you,"
she said, "but because liberty is the shelter under which
I would have you live. If we keep you by
teaching you to kneel to tyranny, we keep only your
bodies and lose your souls."

Aunt Hitty, who had come up behind them, wiped her
eyes with the corner of her shawl.

"I don't approve of talking in the cold," she said.
"It freezes sorrow into a body. Come along."

The sled started southward the next morning. Its bells rang
down the white road. The villagers stood watching until it
disappeared among the pines.

And if there were angels concerned with the founding of
nations, perhaps they did not write only the names of
generals and statesmen in their books. Perhaps they wrote also:
Martha Ward, one blue blanket; Hepzibah Ward, six pairs of
stockings; Grace Ward, one red cloak; Benjamin Ward, one mitten;
and beneath them all, in letters no frost could dim:

**Offered freely, that men might be free.**

Years passed. The war ended. Captain Ward came home lame,
hollow-cheeked, and older than his years. The children ran to
meet him. Grace knew him first by the hymn book
he drew from his breast pocket.

There were bells again. There were flags. There were speeches
concerning the rights of man, the consent of the governed,
and the new nation—a nation to be, as its proudest
teachers said, a light among peoples, a city lifted where
all the earth might behold it.

Mrs. Ward listened.

She had paid dearly for those words and therefore believed
them precious.

She did not suppose that liberty was white.

She did not suppose that the self-evident truth had a
north door for one race and a south door barred
against another.

She did not suppose that the altar upon which she
had laid her beloved would one day be furnished with
unwilling victims.

But nations, like men, sometimes preserve the cup and spill
the sacrament; repeat the creed and crucify its meaning; build
monuments to yesterday's sacrifice while preparing a market for tomorrow's
betrayal.

Let us pass, then, from that winter hearth.

Let us turn the page.

The date is 1850.

The flag bears more stars now.

The altar remains.

## II. THE OFFERING OF 1850

In a narrow street of Boston stood a small brick
house whose windows caught the morning sun.

It was not a fashionable residence. No liveried servant polished
the brass; no carriage waited before the door; no marble
steps proclaimed that those within had inherited the comfortable approval
of society. Yet the panes were clean, the curtain was
white, a geranium bloomed in a red pot, and the
threshold had been scrubbed until it possessed a virtue seldom
attained by the consciences of lawmakers.

George Harris lived there with his wife, Ruth, and their
two children.

George was a cooper. He made barrels that held flour,
apples, fish, molasses, and whatever else commerce required to be
kept from falling apart—which is more than may be said
for commerce itself. His shoulders were broad; his hands were
hardened by the adze; and when he came home in
the evening, the children knew his step before the latch
lifted.

He had lived ten years in Massachusetts.

Ten years earlier he had escaped from bondage in Georgia.
He had traveled by night, hidden by day, crossed rivers,
followed stars, trusted strangers, and learned that a free state
was not reached merely by setting one's feet upon its
soil. Freedom had to be built afterward—one wage, one room,
one friendship, one unafraid morning at a time.

He built it.

He worked first on the docks, then in a stable,
and afterward apprenticed himself to an old cooper who judged
a man's character by whether his hoops held tight. George's
did.

He married Ruth in a small church. Their daughter, Mercy,
was born the next year; their son, David, four years
later. They bought chairs, a table, a clock that ran
too quickly in summer and too slowly in winter, a
blue-edged Bible, six china cups, and—after much consultation—a painted tea
tray decorated with roses of a species never seen in
nature.

Ruth took in ironing. Steam clouded the windows on Mondays;
flatirons marched across the stove; white shirts hung like surrendering
flags from a line in the kitchen. She sang while
she worked, unless the baby was asleep, in which case
she sang in a whisper and maintained that the Lord
could hear just as well.

One October evening George returned carrying a paper parcel.

David flung himself at his knees.

"Daddy! What you got?"

"My legs, mostly, and you are endangerin' them."

"What in the paper?"

"Air."

"Air don't make corners."

"This is trained air."

Mercy, aged nine and already possessed of that sober superiority
which elder sisters acquire without instruction, said, "It's a book.
I can see."

George surrendered the parcel.

It contained a spelling book for Mercy and a small
wooden slate for David.

Ruth turned from the ironing table.

"George, we said we'd wait till next pay."

"I know what we said."

"Then what did you do?"

"I waited till this pay."

"You have an answer ready for everything."

"Not for everything," he said. "Couldn't answer Mr. Jenks when
he asked why three staves split this mornin'. Told him
the wood had political principles and wouldn't hold together."

Ruth laughed.

Mercy opened the spelling book with reverence. George watched her
trace the letters. He himself could read haltingly. Enslaved people
in the place where he had been born were forbidden
education, since ignorance was considered a most suitable chain by
persons who spoke often of civilization.

"My girl's goin' to read every word," he said.

"Every one?" Mercy asked.

"Every one worth readin'."

"How'll I know?"

"Your mother'll tell you."

"And who'll tell Mama?"

Ruth set down the iron.

"The Lord gave people sense, Mercy."

"Did He give it to everybody?"

George coughed.

Ruth pointed the iron at him. "Don't you answer that
child."

Their laughter filled the room.

Observe them well.

There is the father removing his heavy boots by the
fire. There is the mother smoothing a collar beneath the
iron. There is Mercy spelling "country." There is David drawing
a horse on the slate which resembles a ladder in
distress. The kettle sings; the clock ticks; the geranium leans
toward the last light.

No senator beholds them.

No judge finds their happiness recorded among the statutes.

No broker calculates the worth of George's kiss upon his
son's hair, or Ruth's hand upon his shoulder, or Mercy's
pride when she reads a sentence without help.

Yet here, if anywhere, is the republic: labor rewarded, children
taught, marriage honored, prayer unmolested, and a man sitting beneath
his own roof.

"Read the psalm, George," said Ruth when supper was done.

He opened the blue-edged Bible.

"The Lord is my shepherd," he began slowly, guiding himself
with one broad finger.

David climbed into his lap.

"I shall not want."

Outside, wheels passed along the street. Somewhere a bell rang.
The autumn wind touched the window and moved on.

George read of green pastures, still waters, the valley of
the shadow, the rod and staff, the table prepared in
the presence of enemies, and the dwelling place that endured
forever.

When he finished, Ruth said softly, "Amen."

It was then that someone knocked.

Not the neighbor's quick tap. Not the child's rattle. A
hard, official blow—wood against wood, authority announcing itself.

George raised his head.

The knock came again.

Ruth's hand closed upon the table edge.

"Who is it?" George called.

"United States marshal. Open the door."

The words entered before the men did. They crossed the
threshold, extinguished the domestic brightness, and stood among the china
cups.

George rose.

For ten years he had dreamed of footsteps behind him.
In the stable, on the wharf, in church, beside his
child's bed, the old fear had sometimes awakened and laid
its cold hand upon his neck. But year by year
it had weakened. Honest labor, friendship, northern law, and the
promises of white men had lulled it.

Now it stood upright.

The marshal entered with three deputies and a man named
Silas Crewe, agent for a Georgia enslaver.

Crewe was neatly dressed. His gloves were clean. He might
have been collecting a debt or calling upon a cousin,
and there are evils which lose nothing by washing their
hands.

"That's him," he said.

"George Harris," said the marshal, "you are arrested under authority
of the United States."

"For what?"

"As a fugitive from service."

Ruth stepped between them.

"He is my husband."

Crewe glanced at her.

"He's Mr. Caleb Morton's property."

"No man's property," George said.

The deputies moved.

David began to cry.

Mercy seized her father's arm. "Daddy, don't go!"

George lifted both hands.

"Wait. Don't frighten the children. I'll go quiet if you
let me speak."

The marshal, who considered himself humane, permitted it.

George knelt. Mercy clung to his neck. David pressed his
face against his father's shirt.

"Listen to me," George said. His voice trembled, and he
steadied it. "I ain't done wrong. You hear? Whatever they
say, I ain't done wrong."

"Then they can't take you," Mercy sobbed.

Ah, child! What a country you imagined! In that blessed
little republic of your heart, innocence was protection, labor earned
security, marriage was sacred, and the law defended the home.
How soon we educate children out of such fantasies!

Ruth stood very straight.

"Where are you taking him?"

"To the courthouse."

"I am coming."

Crewe smiled. "Won't help."

"I am coming."

The street had filled by the time they brought George
out.

News moves swiftly when misery is the messenger. Neighbors appeared
at windows; workmen left shops; women came without shawls; boys
ran ahead shouting that a fugitive had been caught. Some
cursed the officers. Some urged rescue. Some cautioned obedience to
law. Some lamented that such distressing scenes should happen in
respectable streets and thereby revealed that the scene, rather than
the sin, offended them.

George walked between deputies.

Ruth followed, holding David. Mercy held fast to the back
of her mother's dress.

Above them, from a public building, the American flag moved
in the clean October air.

Its red stripes snapped.

Its stars shone.

The flag had waved over Mrs. Ward's sled of blankets.
It had waved over soldiers without shoes. It had waved
over declarations, battlefields, graves, rejoicings, and the slow enlargement of
national pride.

Now it waved over the capture of a workingman whose
sole crime was that he had carried his own body
away from a thief.

What is a flag? Cloth, unless justice breathes through it.
Color, unless mercy gives it meaning. A boast, unless the
weakest may stand safely beneath it.

At the courthouse, George's friends gathered counsel. A young attorney
volunteered. Ministers came. Free Black citizens crowded the corridor. Outside,
the square thickened with people.

Inside, the proceeding was brief.

Silas Crewe produced papers. He swore that George corresponded to
the description of a man who had escaped from Caleb
Morton's plantation. A second witness identified a scar above George's
left elbow.

George's attorney rose.

"My client has resided here ten years. He is known
as a peaceful, industrious citizen. He has a wife and
children. We ask the opportunity to present testimony—"

"The statute," said the commissioner, "does not make those considerations
material."

Material!

The wife was not material. The children were not material.
Ten years of honest labor were not material. Prayer, character,
wages, rent, friendship, memory, and hope were not material. The
scar was material. The claimant's paper was material. The market
value of flesh was material.

The attorney argued that no person should be deprived of
liberty without due process.

The opposing counsel replied that the Constitution protected property; that
the peace of the states depended upon fidelity to compromise;
that private feeling must yield to public obligation; and that
the Union, that sacred vessel freighted with the hopes of
mankind, could not be endangered for the sake of one
individual.

One individual!

The phrase is a useful broom. With it statesmen sweep
men, women, children, and broken hearts from the polished floor
of public policy.

George was permitted no testimony in his own behalf.

The commissioner adjusted his spectacles.

He regretted, he said, the painful nature of the duty.
He respected the sentiments of the community. He personally abhorred
slavery. Nevertheless, law was law, constitutional obligation was constitutional obligation,
and the peace of the Union required obedience.

He signed the certificate.

In that movement of the pen, George ceased, in the
eyes of federal authority, to be husband, father, laborer, worshiper,
and man.

He became property.

Ruth made no sound at first.

She crossed the room and took George's face between her
hands.

"Look at me," she said.

He looked.

"You remember us."

"Every breath."

"You remember who you are."

"I know."

"No paper can change it."

"No."

David reached for him. The deputy allowed George to hold
the child once more.

"Daddy," David whispered, "read the shepherd."

George shut his eyes.

"The Lord is my shepherd," he said.

His voice broke on the second line.

The marshal touched his shoulder.

Time was up.

Outside, the crowd roared when the decision became known. Men
pressed against the doors. Militia had been summoned; constables formed
ranks; chains stretched across the street. The prisoner was led
out under armed guard.

How imposing was the majesty of the republic! Muskets to
return one unarmed cooper to bondage; bayonets to defend the
kidnapping of a father; drums, officers, warrants, learned arguments, and
constitutional phrases—all brought forth against the dangerous proposition that a
Black man belonged to himself.

An elderly woman stood upon a doorstep as George passed.

"My father froze at Valley Forge!" she cried. "Was it
for this?"

No official answered.

Perhaps there was no answer.

George was taken south.

Ruth had no money to follow, but friends collected what
they could. The china tea set was sold. The clock
was sold. The painted tray with its impossible roses was
sold. George's tools were sold last.

Mercy brought her spelling book to her mother.

"Sell this too."

Ruth held it to her breast.

"No. Your father bought it for your freedom."

"What freedom?"

Ruth could not answer.

Weeks later she traveled with the children, assisted by an
antislavery society, to the city where George was to be
delivered. She hoped to see him. She hoped—oh, indestructible human
heart!—that money might be raised, that the claimant might sell
him to friends, that some crack of mercy might open
in the stone wall.

But Caleb Morton had died while George was being transported,
and the estate ordered several enslaved people sold to settle
debts.

Thus a man who had supported his family by making
barrels was advertised with horses, cotton presses, and household furniture.

The auction took place in a public square.

The day was bright.

Trade was lively.

There were merchants discussing prices; lawyers exchanging civilities; planters examining
teeth and shoulders; boys eating cakes; politicians talking of harmony
between sections; and ministers who, though reluctant to approve slavery
in the abstract, admitted that Scripture counseled servants to obey
masters and that agitation produced unchristian excitement.

Upon a raised platform stood men, women, and children awaiting
sale.

There was a mother with an infant tied against her
body and a boy holding her skirt. There were two
sisters whispering. There was an old man whose back bent
before anyone touched it. There was a young woman staring
beyond the crowd with eyes that seemed to have left
the world. There was George.

The auctioneer climbed the steps.

He was cheerful, as became a man with business to
conduct.

"Gentlemen," he called, "we have an excellent offering today."

Offering.

Yes—let the word be used.

Once, upon another altar, a woman had offered blankets, coins,
a husband, and sons that liberty might live. Here men
and women were offered that slavery might live; offered not
by themselves but by others; offered without consent, without honor,
without return; bound to the horns of an altar named
Union, while priests of law lifted their hands and pronounced
the sacrifice necessary.

The auctioneer began with the mother.

"A sound field hand, twenty-six years of age, with infant.
The boy will be sold separately."

The mother cried out.

"No, mas'r! Please, mas'r, sell us together!"

The auctioneer frowned.

"We'll conduct this in order."

The boy clung to her.

A bidder purchased the mother and infant. Another purchased the
boy.

When they pulled him away, he screamed one word.

"Ma!"

It is a short word. Every nation understands it. It
was older than constitutions, stronger than statutes, holier than compromises.

"Ma!"

The respectable spectators shifted uneasily.

One gentleman remarked that scenes of this kind were very
unfortunate, but the responsibility rested with fanatics who disturbed the
natural relations of society.

The sale continued.

A husband was sent west; his wife east. Two sisters
were divided. The old man brought little. The young woman
brought much.

Then George was called forward.

"Prime male," said the auctioneer. "About thirty-five. Skilled cooper. Strong,
healthy, accustomed to work. Ran away some years back, but
has been recovered under the laws of the United States.
What am I offered?"

There was a murmur of appreciation.

Recovered! As one recovers a misplaced umbrella, a strayed cow,
a spoon slipped behind the cupboard.

A planter examined George's hands.

"Good shoulders," he said.

Another asked, "Temper?"

Crewe answered, "Quiet enough. Had northern notions, but they'll wear
out."

Northern notions! That a man's wife was his wife. That
his child was his child. That wages earned belonged to
the laborer. That the body God made was not merchandise.
Dangerous notions, certainly; they have overturned thrones and frightened tyrants
since the world began.

At the edge of the crowd, Ruth stood with Mercy
and David.

She had found him.

"George!"

His head snapped toward the sound.

The square disappeared for him. The auctioneer, bidders, platform, guards,
and whole monstrous apparatus of sale fell away. He saw
only Ruth's face, Mercy's uplifted hands, David struggling forward.

"My family," he said.

The auctioneer continued calling bids.

Ruth pushed through the crowd.

"That is my husband! Gentlemen, that is my husband!"

A guard blocked her.

"We was married before God," she cried. "He worked for
us. He never harmed any soul. We have money—some money.
Friends will send more. Let me speak to the man
buying him."

The bidders avoided her eyes.

One man did not. He looked upon her with irritation,
as though grief were an indecent interruption of commerce.

"Woman," he said, "the law recognizes the claimant's title."

"Does the law know me?"

No answer.

"Does it know his children?"

No answer.

"Does it know him?"

Still no answer.

The bidding rose.

Five hundred dollars.

Six hundred.

Seven hundred and fifty.

At eight hundred, a Louisiana planter nodded.

The hammer lifted.

Mercy broke free and ran to the platform.

"Daddy!"

George bent toward her, but a guard caught the child
around the waist.

"Don't hurt her!" George shouted.

The crowd stirred.

The auctioneer's hammer fell.

"Sold."

The sale of a man!

Sold—his back, his hands, his breath, his skill, his waking
hours, his sleep, the scar upon his arm, the strength
in his shoulders.

Sold—his right to walk where he chose, to speak when
he chose, to defend his wife, to teach his daughter,
to lift his son.

Sold, not for a term of years, but for life?
No—more than life. For liberty is the life of life;
take it, and the heart may continue beating while the
manhood is murdered day by day.

Ruth sank to her knees.

George strained against the men holding him.

"Ruth! Teach them! Mercy, read! David—take care of your mama!"

David screamed, "The shepherd, Daddy! Say the shepherd!"

And George, standing upon the auction block beneath the flag
of the nation that had returned him, lifted his voice.

"The Lord is my shepherd!"

The guard pulled him toward the steps.

"I shall not want!"

His purchaser ordered him chained.

"He maketh me to lie down in green pastures—"

The iron closed around his wrists.

"He leadeth me beside the still waters—"

Ruth reached out both hands.

"Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow
of death—"

They dragged him into the street.

"I will fear no evil—"

The crowd swallowed him.

The psalm went on, broken by distance, hoofbeats, wheels, and
the crying of children.

Then it was gone.

That evening the square was swept. Scraps of paper blew
along the stones. Merchants closed their ledgers. Lawyers dined. Politicians
congratulated themselves that constitutional principle had been maintained. Ministers prepared
sermons upon peace. The flag came down at sunset.

Ruth sat with her children in a lodging room.

Mercy's spelling book lay open in her lap.

The word at the top of the page was **liberty**.

She stared at it until the letters blurred.

"Read, child," Ruth said.

Mercy shook her head.

"Read it."

"I can't."

"Your father told you."

Mercy pressed both fists against her eyes.

Ruth drew her close.

"Read it," she repeated, though her own voice was scarcely
more than breath. "They took him because they fear that
word. You read it."

Mercy looked again.

"Lib-er-ty."

David leaned against his mother.

"Where's Daddy going?"

Ruth held them both.

Where, indeed?

Southward, toward labor without wages, obedience enforced by pain, nights
beneath a strange roof, mornings without his children, years measured
not by growth and hope but by endurance.

And what had the nation gained?

The Union was preserved, said the statesmen.

Peace between sections was preserved, said the compromisers.

Property was protected, said the courts.

Law was vindicated, said the officers.

Behold, then, the noble maintaining of principle! Behold the edifying
spectacle of a republic saving itself by destroying a family!
Behold the altar garlanded with constitutional phrases, and upon it
a man's heart, his hopes, his marriage, his fatherhood, his
very ownership of himself!

Ah, you who defend this because it is law—what thing
may not be sanctified by your doctrine? If law commands
the mother's child to be sold, shall she dry her
tears and admire the statute? If law puts chains upon
innocence, do the chains cease to be iron? If law
denies the image of God in a man, is Heaven
bound by the commissioner's certificate?

You say the Union must be preserved.

Preserved for what?

That merchants may prosper beneath it? That politicians may rise
beneath it? That pulpits may preach beneath it? That white
children may learn beneath it how their fathers fought for
freedom, while Black children are torn from their fathers by
federal power?

A Union that asks liberty to feed slavery is not
preserved; it is corrupted. A peace purchased with the hunted
man's body is not peace; it is a silence made
by terror. An altar that receives unwilling human victims is
not sacred because senators bow before it.

Remember the first offering.

Remember the snow road, the blue blanket, the knitted stockings,
the red cloak, the little mitten. Remember Martha Ward, who
gave husband and sons that no ruler might claim ownership
of the people.

Could she have stood in that auction square?

Could she have heard the hammer and called it liberty?

Would Captain Ward, whose feet bled upon frozen ground, have
guarded George's chains? Would the soldiers who starved at Valley
Forge have said, "We suffered that this man might be
returned to bondage"?

No.

Let the dead be spared that slander.

The sin belongs to the living.

The first altar was raised by love and approached by
willing feet. Its offerings were sorrowful, but they were free.

The second was raised by greed, fear, cowardice, and compromise.
Its victim was seized. His wife's consent was not asked.
His children's prayers were not counted. His labor, body, and
future were laid down by men who sacrificed nothing of
their own.

And above both altars floated the same word.

Liberty.

Once it sounded like a bell across the snow.

Now it rang like irony above the slave market.

Yet the word was not false. Men were false to
it.

Somewhere, in a southern night, George repeated the psalm. Somewhere
in Boston, Mercy bent over her book. Somewhere Ruth taught
David to remember his father's face. And in quiet houses,
meeting halls, churches, workshops, and fields, consciences awakened. Hands once
folded were clenched. Voices once timid grew strong.

For liberty may be betrayed, but she is a troublesome
ghost. She walks through courtrooms after the judge has gone.
She sits beside the auction block when trade is finished.
She enters nurseries, asks children what the flag means, and
refuses to be silenced by answers concerning policy.

The nation had placed George upon its altar.

But before Heaven, another offering was being prepared: indignation, courage,
labor, witness, and the solemn resolve that no union, no
law, no prosperity, and no peace was worth the price
of a human soul.

Until that truth prevailed, the old soldiers could not rest.

Until that truth prevailed, the Declaration remained unfinished.

Until that truth prevailed, the flag itself was only a
question moving in the wind.

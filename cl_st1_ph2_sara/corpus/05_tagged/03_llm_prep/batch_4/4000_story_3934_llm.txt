## The Measure of Friendship

One morning the old Water-rat put his head out of
his hole. He had round, beady eyes, stiff grey whiskers,
and a long black tail that looked as if it
had been made of India rubber. In the pond the
little ducks were swimming after their mother. They were like
six yellow balls of thistledown, and the mother-duck, who was
very white and had remarkably red legs, was trying to
teach them how to stand upon their heads in the
water.

"You will never be admitted into good society unless you
can stand upon your heads," she said; and every now
and then she showed them how it ought to be
done.

But the little ducks paid no attention to her. They
were so young that they did not know what an
advantage it is to be admitted into good society.

"What disobedient children!" cried the Water-rat. "They really deserve to
be drowned."

"Nothing of the kind," answered the mother-duck. "Everyone must make
a beginning, and parents cannot have too much patience."

"Ah! I know nothing about the feelings of parents," said
the Water-rat. "I am not a family man. Indeed, I
have never been married, and I do not intend to
be. Love is an excellent thing in its way, no
doubt, but friendship is much nobler. I know of nothing
in the world more beautiful than a devoted friendship."

"And what, may I ask, is your idea of the
duties of a devoted friend?" said a green Linnet, who
was sitting on a willow-tree and had overheard the conversation.

"Yes, that is exactly what I should like to know,"
said the mother-duck; and she swam to the far end
of the pond, where the water was shallow, and began
turning upside down in order to give her children a
good example.

"What a very foolish question!" cried the Water-rat. "I should
expect my devoted friend to be devoted to me, of
course."

"And what would you do in return?" asked the Linnet,
swinging gently upon a silver twig.

"I do not understand you," said the Water-rat.

"Then let me tell you a story," said the Linnet.

"Is the story about me?" asked the Water-rat. "If it
is, I will listen to it, for I am extremely
fond of stories about myself."

"It applies to you," answered the Linnet.

"Then I shall certainly listen," said the Water-rat, and he
climbed out of his hole, folded his forepaws across his
chest, and sat down upon the bank.

"Once upon a time," began the Linnet, "there was an
honest little fellow named Hans."

"Was he distinguished?" interrupted the Water-rat.

"No," said the Linnet, "except for his kind heart, his
merry face, and his beautiful garden. He lived by himself
in a tiny cottage, and every day he worked among
his flowers.

"There was no garden in the whole countryside so lovely
as little Hans's garden. Sweet-william grew there, and gillyflowers, and
shepherd's-purses, and purple columbines. There were damask roses and yellow
roses, lilac crocuses and gold, violets white and blue, and
tall foxgloves with freckled throats. In their proper months the
marjoram scented the paths, the basil warmed in the sun,
the fennel tossed its feathery leaves, and the lavender stood
in pale ranks beside the door. The pear-trees wore snowy
blossoms in spring, the plums turned crimson in summer, and
in autumn the vines hung low with dark grapes. So
there were always beautiful things in little Hans's garden, and,
as Hans loved every one of them, he was always
happy while he worked.

"Now little Hans had many acquaintances, but the most intimate
of them was big Hugh the Miller. The Miller was
a prosperous man. He owned a fine mill by the
river, a hundred sacks of flour, six sleek cows, a
flock of sheep, and a large brick house with a
blue roof. Yet so devoted was he to Hans that
whenever he passed the garden he leaned over the wall
and gathered a great bunch of flowers, or filled his
pockets with plums and cherries if the fruit was ripe.

"'True friends should have everything in common,' the Miller used
to say, and little Hans would smile and nod, feeling
very proud that a rich man should speak so beautifully
to him.

"Sometimes, indeed, the neighbours wondered that the Miller never gave
Hans anything in return, though he had flour in his
bins, cream in his dairy, cheeses on his shelves, and
red wine in his cellar. But Hans never troubled himself
about such questions. Nothing pleased him better than to listen
to the Miller's noble opinions upon the unselfishness of friendship.

"So little Hans worked in his garden. During spring, summer,
and autumn he was very happy; but when winter came,
he had neither fruit nor flowers to sell, and he
often suffered from cold and hunger. He would go to
bed without supper, having eaten no more than a few
dried pears or some hard nuts. He was lonely too,
for the Miller never came to see him in winter.

"'There is no good in my visiting Hans while the
snow lasts,' the Miller said to his wife, as he
sat in a deep chair beside a blazing fire. 'When
people are in trouble they should be left alone. Visitors
disturb the dignity of sorrow. At least, that is my
view of friendship, and I am sure it is the
correct one. I shall wait until spring, and then I
shall pay Hans a visit, and he will give me
a large basket of primroses. That will make him exceedingly
happy.'

"'You are always thoughtful about others,' answered the Miller's wife.
She was sitting on a cushioned sofa, drinking warm spiced
wine. 'It is a pleasure merely to hear you talk
about friendship. I am sure the clergyman himself could not
say finer things, though he owns a university degree and
wears a gold ring.'

"'But could we not ask little Hans to come here?'
said the Miller's youngest son. 'If he is cold and
hungry, I will give him half my porridge and show
him my white rabbits.'

"'What a silly boy you are!' cried the Miller. 'I
really do not know why we send you to school.
You appear to learn nothing. If Hans came here and
saw our warm fire, our good supper, and our cellar
full of wine, he might become envious. Envy is a
terrible thing, and it spoils the finest character. I could
not allow Hans's character to be spoiled. I am his
best friend, and it is my duty to protect him
from temptation. Besides, if he came here he might ask
me to let him have some flour on credit, and
I could not do that. Flour is one thing and
friendship is another, and they must not be confused. The
words are spelled differently and mean quite different things. Anyone
can see that.'

"'How beautifully you speak!' said his wife, and she poured
herself another glass of wine. 'Your conversation has made me
quite drowsy. It is exactly like being in church.'

"'Many people act well,' answered the Miller, 'but very few
talk well. This proves that talking is much more difficult
than acting, and also much finer.'

"And he looked severely across the table at his little
son, who hung his head and became scarlet. But he
was so young that he may be forgiven."

"Is that the end of the story?" asked the Water-rat.

"Certainly not," said the Linnet. "That is the beginning."

"Then you are not a modern storyteller," said the Water-rat.
"Modern storytellers always begin at the end and end in
the middle. I heard that from a critic who walked
around the pond with a young man. He spoke for
a long time, and I am certain he must have
been right, for he wore blue spectacles and had very
little hair."

The Linnet pretended not to hear and continued.

"At last the winter passed. The frost loosened its white
fingers, yellow stars of celandine shone beside the lanes, and
the hazel branches were hung with green tassels. Early one
morning the Miller said to his wife that he would
go and see Hans.

"'What a good heart you have!' cried his wife. 'You
are always thinking of other people. Do not forget to
take the large basket for the flowers.'

"So the Miller took the largest basket he could find
and walked down the hill.

"Little Hans was digging in his garden. His face was
thin from the winter, and his clothes had been mended
in many places, but when he saw the Miller he
leaned upon his spade and smiled.

"'Good morning, little Hans,' said the Miller.

"'Good morning,' said Hans. 'I am very glad to see
you. The winter was a hard one, but now the
flowers are waking, and I feel happy again.'

"'We often spoke of you during the winter,' said the
Miller, 'and wondered how you were getting on.'

"'That was kind of you,' said Hans. 'I was sometimes
afraid you had forgotten me.'

"'Forgotten you! How can you say such a thing?' cried
the Miller. 'Friendship never forgets. That is what is wonderful
about it, though I fear you do not understand the
poetry of life. How lovely your primroses are, by the
way!'

"'They are very lovely,' said Hans, 'and fortunately I have
a great many. I am going to sell them in
the market and buy back my wheelbarrow.'

"'Buy back your wheelbarrow? You have not sold it?'

"'I had to,' said Hans. 'Winter was very cold, and
I had no money for bread. First I sold the
silver buttons from my Sunday coat, then I sold my
little chain, then my pipe, and at last my wheelbarrow.
But I hope to buy them all back before long.'

"'Little Hans,' said the Miller, 'I will give you my
wheelbarrow. It is not in very good repair. One side
is gone, and there is something wrong with the wheel,
but in spite of that I will give it to
you. I know it is exceedingly generous of me, and
many people will think me foolish for parting with it,
but I am not like the rest of the world.
Generosity is the essence of friendship, and, besides, I have
bought a new wheelbarrow. You may consider the old one
yours.'

"'That is generous indeed!' cried Hans, and his round face
became bright with pleasure. 'I can easily repair it, for
I have a plank in the cottage.'

"'A plank!' said the Miller. 'That is exactly what I
need for the roof of my barn. There is a
large hole in it, and the rain will spoil my
corn unless I mend it. How fortunate that you mentioned
the plank! It is remarkable how one good action creates
another. I have promised you my wheelbarrow, and now you
will give me your plank. Of course, the wheelbarrow is
worth much more than the plank, but true friendship never
notices such things. Fetch it at once, and I shall
begin the roof this afternoon.'

"'Certainly,' said Hans, though his voice was less cheerful, and
he ran into the cottage and dragged out the plank.

"'It is not a very large plank,' said the Miller,
examining it. 'I fear that after I use it for
the roof there will be nothing left for the wheelbarrow.
But that is not my fault. And now, as I
have given you my wheelbarrow, I am sure you will
like to give me some flowers. Here is the basket.
Fill it quite full.'

"'Quite full?' asked Hans, for the basket was very large,
and he knew that if he filled it he would
have few flowers left for the market.

"'Well, really,' said the Miller, 'since I am giving you
my wheelbarrow, I do not think it much to ask.
Perhaps I am mistaken. Perhaps friendship, true friendship, is not
entirely free from selfishness after all.'

"'My dear friend, my best friend,' cried Hans, 'you are
welcome to all the flowers in my garden.'

"And he gathered his beautiful primroses and filled the Miller's
basket.

"'Good-bye, little Hans,' said the Miller as he climbed the
hill with the flowers on one arm and the plank
on the other.

"'Good-bye,' called Hans, and he began digging with great happiness,
for he was thinking of the wheelbarrow.

"The next morning Hans was tying honeysuckle against the porch
when he heard the Miller calling from the road. He
climbed down the ladder and ran to the wall.

"The Miller stood there with a heavy sack of flour
on his back.

"'Dear little Hans,' he said, 'will you carry this sack
to market for me?'

"'I am sorry,' answered Hans, 'but I am very busy
today. I must finish tying up the honeysuckle, water the
seedlings, and roll the grass.'

"'Well, really,' said the Miller, 'considering that I am going
to give you my wheelbarrow, I think it is unfriendly
of you to refuse.'

"'Oh, do not say that!' cried Hans. 'I would not
be unfriendly for anything.'

"So he took the sack upon his shoulders and walked
the six dusty miles to market. The day was hot,
the flour was heavy, and after Hans had gone some
distance the straps cut painfully into his shoulders. However, he
remembered that the Miller was his best friend and went
bravely on.

"He waited at the market until he had sold the
flour for a good price, and then he hurried home,
for he feared to meet robbers on the road. It
was after sunset when he reached his cottage.

"'What a tiring day,' he said. 'Still, I am glad
I did not refuse the Miller. He is my best
friend, and he is going to give me his wheelbarrow.'

"Early the following morning the Miller came for the money,
but Hans was so tired that he had not yet
risen.

"'Upon my word,' said the Miller, looking through the window,
'you are very lazy. Considering that I am going to
give you my wheelbarrow, I think you should work harder.
Idleness is a great sin, and I would not like
any friend of mine to be idle. You must not
mind my speaking plainly. I should never dream of doing
so if I were not your friend. But what is
the use of friendship if one cannot say exactly what
one pleases? Anyone can say charming things and try to
make himself agreeable; a true friend says unpleasant things and
proves his devotion.'

"'I am sorry,' said Hans, rubbing his eyes. 'I was
so tired that I thought I would lie in bed
a little longer and listen to the birds.'

"'I am pleased to hear that you have listened to
the birds,' said the Miller. 'When you are dressed, you
can come and repair my barn roof.'

"Poor little Hans was anxious to work in his garden.
His flowers had not been watered for two days, but
he did not like to refuse the Miller. So he
climbed onto the Miller's barn and worked there until evening,
while the Miller stood below and told him how boards
should be placed.

"At sunset the Miller called up, 'Have you mended the
hole, little Hans?'

"'It is nearly finished,' answered Hans.

"'There is no work so delightful as the work one
does for others,' said the Miller.

"'It is certainly a privilege to hear you talk,' said
Hans, wiping his forehead. 'Few people have such beautiful ideas.'

"'They come to me naturally,' said the Miller. 'But you
must not grow vain because I share them with you.
Come tomorrow and drive my sheep to the mountain pasture.'

"So the next day Hans drove the Miller's sheep to
the mountain. They strayed into ferny hollows and over stony
ridges, and by the time he brought them home his
feet were blistered. On the day after that he carried
a jar of butter to the town. The day after
that he fetched the Miller's cows from the marsh. Then
he cleaned the stable, cut wood for the kitchen fire,
gathered rushes for the Miller's wife, and stood for an
entire afternoon frightening crows away from the Miller's corn.

"Each morning Hans intended to work in his garden, and
each morning the Miller arrived with some new duty.

"'I would gladly do it,' Hans would say, 'but my
roses need tying.'

"'A friend who thinks of roses before friendship has a
very small heart,' the Miller would answer.

"Or Hans would say, 'My lettuces are drying.'

"And the Miller would reply, 'Selfish people are always talking
about their own lettuces.'

"Or Hans would say, 'I must take my strawberries to
market.'

"And the Miller would sigh and answer, 'It grieves me
that you put business before affection. I had not expected
it from you, especially after my promise about the wheelbarrow.'

"Then little Hans would blush, ask pardon, and do whatever
the Miller desired.

"As the summer advanced, Hans's garden became neglected. The grass
grew tall in the paths, the carnations drooped, and the
bindweed twisted round the stems of the lilies. The bees
still visited, for bees are industrious and do not trouble
themselves about social theories, but there were fewer blossoms every
week. Hans saw this and was sorrowful. Yet he comforted
himself with the thought that Hugh was his best friend,
and that a wheelbarrow would be extremely useful once he
received it.

"'Hans is certainly improving,' the Miller said to his wife
one evening. 'He is beginning to understand the duties of
friendship.'

"'All through your influence,' she answered. 'And will you give
him the wheelbarrow soon?'

"'My dear, you speak without reflection. If I give it
to him at once, he may have nothing to look
forward to. Hope is one of the greatest blessings, and
I should be sorry to deprive Hans of it. Besides,
the wheel is worse than I thought, and the handles
are missing. I am afraid he will be disappointed.'

"'He would be very ungrateful to be disappointed by a
gift,' said the Miller's wife.

"'Exactly,' answered the Miller. 'That is why I must be
cautious. Generosity places a terrible responsibility upon the giver.'

"Summer turned to autumn, and the air became cool. Red
leaves drifted along the lanes, the blackberries shone in the
hedges, and silver webs lay on the fields in the
morning. Little Hans had not yet repaired his garden, but
he had repaired the Miller's gate, painted the Miller's kitchen,
carried apples to the cider press, and spent three days
digging a ditch beside the mill.

"One evening he sat alone in his cottage. His hands
were rough, his back ached, and on the table stood
a very small supper of brown bread and onions. Outside,
the wind moved through the trees with a low, uneasy
sound.

"Suddenly there came a violent knocking at the door.

"Little Hans opened it, and there stood the Miller. He
held his cloak over his head, and in one hand
he carried a lantern.

"'Dear little Hans,' he cried, 'a terrible accident has happened.
My youngest son has fallen from the loft and hurt
himself. I am going for the doctor, but the doctor
lives a long way off, and the night is so
bad that I have decided it would be much better
for you to go instead. You know I am going
to give you my wheelbarrow, and it is only fair
that you should do something for me in return.'

"'Of course I will go,' said Hans. 'I am very
sorry about your son. But the night is so dark.
Will you lend me your lantern?'

"'My lantern?' exclaimed the Miller. 'It is nearly new. The
storm may damage it, and that would be a great
loss.'

"'Then I will go without it,' said Hans.

"'That is right,' said the Miller. 'I knew you would
not allow a selfish concern for your own comfort to
interfere with friendship. Tell the doctor to come immediately. And
take care not to waste time.'

"Little Hans put on his coat and cap and stepped
into the storm.

"The night was black, and the wind was so strong
that he could hardly stand. Rain swept across the road
in shining sheets, though Hans could not see them shine,
for there was no moon. The branches cracked above him,
ditches overflowed, and now and then a flash of lightning
showed the white road twisting between the hills like a
frightened serpent.

"He walked for three hours before he reached the doctor's
house.

"'Who is there?' cried the doctor from an upper window.

"'Little Hans, sir.'

"'What do you want, little Hans?'

"'The Miller's son has fallen from the loft and is
hurt. You must come at once.'

"The doctor dressed and brought out his horse, his boots,
and a large lantern. Then he rode away toward the
mill, while Hans followed on foot.

"But the storm grew fiercer. The rain beat upon Hans's
face, and the wind blew out the tracks almost as
soon as the horse had made them. After a while
Hans could no longer see the doctor's lantern. He called,
but the wind swallowed his voice.

"He wandered from the road onto the moor. There were
deep pools there, hidden among the reeds, and old pits
filled with rain. Hans stumbled on, stretching his hands before
him. He thought of his garden, of the roses that
would need pruning, and of the wheelbarrow the Miller was
going to give him. Once he saw a pale light
and hurried toward it, but it was only lightning lying
for a moment on the water.

"Then the darkness closed around him again.

"At dawn some shepherds found his cap floating in a
deep pool. They searched with long poles, and at noon
they brought out the body of little Hans."

The mother-duck stopped teaching her children and lowered her head.
Even the ducklings were quiet.

"And what happened to the Miller's son?" asked the Water-rat.

"He had only bruised his arm," said the Linnet. "The
doctor bandaged it, and by breakfast the boy was eating
honey-cakes beside the fire."

"I am glad of that," said the Water-rat. "I am
always touched by accidents in respectable families."

The Linnet continued.

"Everyone went to little Hans's funeral, for he had been
kind to them all. The Miller walked at the head
of the procession in a long black cloak.

"'As I was his best friend,' he said, 'it is
only proper that I should have the chief place.'

"Behind him came Hans's neighbours, carrying flowers from their own
gardens, since Hans's flowers had withered. The Miller's wife wept
into a large embroidered handkerchief, and the Miller's youngest son
carried a little bunch of white violets.

"The Miller himself did not cry, but his face had
a solemn expression, which many people thought very impressive.

"After the burial they went to the inn and drank
spiced ale and ate seed-cakes. The Miller sat in the
best chair by the fire.

"'Little Hans is certainly a great loss to everyone,' said
the blacksmith.

"'A great loss to me in particular,' answered the Miller.
'Why, I had practically given him my wheelbarrow, and now
I do not know what to do with it. It
takes up room in my barn, and it is in
such bad condition that I could not sell it. This
will teach me never to give anything away again. One
always suffers for being generous.'

"'That is very true,' said several of the guests, for
the Miller was rich, and it is astonishing how true
rich people's opinions sound when they are spoken in a
warm room.

"The Miller accepted another cup of ale and began to
explain the difference between prudence and selfishness. But the blacksmith
went outside, the schoolmaster looked into the fire, and the
Miller's youngest son put his violets on the empty chair
beside him.

"That is the end," said the Linnet.

"And what became of the Miller?" asked the Water-rat.

"Nothing became of him," answered the Linnet. "He continued to
prosper."

"I thought so," said the Water-rat. "He had a practical
mind. I like him very much. He understood friendship thoroughly,
though perhaps he spoke too often about it. A truly
wise person should keep some wisdom in reserve, or other
people may steal it."

"I am afraid you have missed the meaning," said the
Linnet.

"The meaning?"

"The moral."

"Do you mean to say the story has a moral?"
cried the Water-rat.

"Certainly."

"Well, really," said the Water-rat in a rage, "you should
have told me before you began. If I had known,
I would not have listened. I could have said 'Nonsense!'
as the critic did. However, I can say it now."

So he shouted "Nonsense!" at the top of his voice,
whisked his black tail, and disappeared into his hole.

"And what do you think of the Water-rat?" asked the
mother-duck, swimming back after a while.

"He has several good qualities," answered the Linnet. "But I
confess that I have the feelings of a storyteller, and
I cannot see a hardened listener without becoming a little
sad."

"I am afraid you have annoyed him," said the mother-duck.
"It is dangerous to tell stories with morals."

"Quite so," said the Linnet.

And I entirely agree with him.

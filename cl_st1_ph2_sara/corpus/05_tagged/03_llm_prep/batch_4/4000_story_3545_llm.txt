# THE CANDLE UPON THE PLOUGH

*"Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good."*
—Romans xii. 21.

In the days when serfdom still prevailed in Russia, many
noblemen possessed, together with their forests, fields, horses, and cattle,
whole villages of Christian souls. The peasants tilled the land,
cut the hay, felled timber, carted grain, and performed such
labor as their masters demanded. By law their service was
limited; but where the master lived far away, and entrusted
his estate to an overseer who feared neither God nor
man, the law was often no protection to the poor
moujiks.

On one such estate there lived a superintendent named Michael
Simeonovitch. He had himself been born a peasant, but, being
quick with figures and bold in speech, he had gained
the favor of the nobleman and had been raised above
his fellows. At first the peasants rejoiced.

"He knows our life," they said. "He hath eaten black
bread, worn bark shoes, and slept upon a stove. Surely
he will remember the burden of the poor."

But it commonly happens that a man who is lifted
above his brethren, if he forgets God, becomes harder than
one who has always been rich. Michael Simeonovitch desired to
show the nobleman that no other superintendent could obtain so
much labor or profit from an estate. He also wished
to enrich himself. Accordingly, he laid upon the peasants burdens
greater than those appointed by law, and if any man
complained, Michael cried:

"Who is master here—you or I? Go back to work,
or I will teach you obedience!"

The serfs were bound to labor three days in the
week upon the nobleman's land, leaving the other days for
their own fields. Michael compelled them to work four days,
and often five. If rain threatened while the master's hay
lay cut, he ordered the peasants from their own meadows.
If their rye was overripe, he still sent them to
cart his oats. When a widow had no horse, he
took her cow to draw a small harrow; and when
the cow died from exhaustion, he said:

"A useless beast is no loss."

He established a workshop in which the women spun linen
by night, and he sold the cloth for his own
gain. He forced the young men to burn charcoal in
the forest during winter, and kept back part of the
kopecks paid for it. He demanded eggs, honey, mushrooms, chickens,
and wool, saying these were gifts due to his office.
But no gift ever satisfied him.

On one occasion an old peasant named Nikita came late
to the threshing-floor because his granddaughter lay dying. Michael struck
him across the face and ordered him tied to a
bench.

"Have mercy, Michael Simeonovitch," said the old man. "The child
called for me, and there was no one else to
fetch the priest."

"The dead need no priest, and the living need discipline,"
answered Michael.

He caused the old man to be flogged, though his
gray hair was white as the winter frost.

Among the peasants there was one named Vasili Minaeff. He
was strong, hot-tempered, and fearless, and because he could not
hear injustice without answering, he suffered more than the others.
Michael hated him, for Vasili looked straight into his eyes
and would not humble himself.

"Why must we cart timber on our own day?" Vasili
once asked.

"Because I command it."

"But the nobleman's rule gives us this day for our
fields."

Michael seized a stick.

"I am the nobleman's rule!"

He struck Vasili until the stick broke, and afterward had
him flogged before the whole village. From that time hatred
took root in Vasili's heart. He thought of Michael by
day and dreamed of him by night.

Another peasant, Peter Mikhayeff, was unlike Vasili. He was neither
old nor weak, and could labor as well as any
man, but he was gentle in speech and peaceably inclined.
He read the Psalter, observed the fasts, and never returned
abuse for abuse. When Michael gave him work beyond his
share, Peter performed it without murmuring, yet he would sometimes
say to the other peasants:

"The burden is heavy, brethren; nevertheless, let us not make
it heavier by taking sin upon our own souls."

Some mocked him for this, calling him Monk Peter; but
when sickness or sorrow entered a cottage, they sent for
him, because he was good-hearted and knew how to comfort
the afflicted.

As Michael's cruelty increased, the peasants began to meet secretly
in a bathhouse at the far end of the village.
There Vasili spoke to them.

"How long shall we endure this devil in human form?"
said he. "He takes our days, our bread, our cattle,
and the strength of our wives. Yesterday he ordered Fedka's
boy flogged for breaking an old rake. Tomorrow he will
flog us because the sun does not shine. Are we
men, or sheep waiting beneath the butcher's knife?"

"We are men," replied several.

"Then let us act like men. He rides alone through
the birch wood every Thursday. A blow from behind, and
he troubles us no more."

The peasants were silent. Some crossed themselves; others stared at
the earthen floor.

At length one named Tikhon said:

"If he is killed, the nobleman will send soldiers. They
will beat the whole village."

"Who will know who struck him?" asked Vasili. "The birches
do not speak."

"The devil speaks when murder hath been done," said Peter
Mikhayeff, who had entered unnoticed and heard the last words.

Vasili frowned.

"We did not ask thee to preach, Peter."

"I came because ye are my brethren, and because I
heard what ye intended."

"What we intend is to save our wives and children."

"Can a soul be saved by murder?"

Vasili rose and struck his fist upon the bench.

"Thou hast not been beaten as I have."

"I have been struck."

"Not until thy back ran blood. Not until thy little
children cried at the sight of thee."

"That is true," answered Peter. "But though he hath sinned
against thee, his sin cannot cleanse thine."

Then Vasili said:

"A hawk descended upon a flock of sparrows and devoured
one every day. The sparrows gathered and said, 'Let us
peck out his eyes while he sleeps.' But one foolish
sparrow answered, 'Nay, brethren, for it is sinful to harm
the hawk.' So the next day the hawk ate the
foolish sparrow first, and afterward all the others. That is
what comes of thy wisdom."

Peter considered the matter and replied:

"The tale is not rightly told. For sparrows are beasts
without immortal souls, and even they cannot overcome a hawk
by becoming hawks themselves. But suppose the hawk were a
man, and the sparrows also men. If they killed him,
one among them would take command, and if evil were
in his heart, he too would grow claws. Then whom
would they kill next? Evil cannot be ended by evil,
any more than fire can be quenched with oil."

"Fine words!" cried Vasili. "Meanwhile Michael eats white bread and
we chew husks."

"God sees both his table and our hunger."

"Then why does God wait?"

"We do not know the hour appointed. But we know
what is commanded unto us: return not evil for evil."

The meeting ended without agreement. Yet hatred continued to grow,
and Vasili watched for an opportunity.

Toward the end of Lent, when the roads were breaking
under the spring sun and the dark earth appeared from
beneath the snow, Michael Simeonovitch summoned the elders.

"The upper field must be ploughed," said he.

"We will begin after the holy feast," answered the eldest.
"Our own strips also wait."

"Ye will begin when I order."

"To-morrow is Holy Saturday."

"Then begin Monday."

The elders looked at one another in astonishment, for Easter
Monday was kept as a holy day. Even hard masters
did not compel peasants to labor while the Easter bells
were ringing.

One elder ventured to speak.

"Michael Simeonovitch, all winter we have worked. Allow us at
least to honor the Resurrection."

Michael's face darkened.

"Will the Resurrection plough the upper field? The ground is
ready now. If ye do not go, I will send
every tenth man to the whipping-post."

The order spread through the village. Women wept, old men
shook their heads, and the young men cursed.

"He hath at last declared war against God Himself," said
Vasili. "Now do ye still fear to strike him?"

On Easter night the church shone with candles, and the
people, dressed in their cleanest shirts and homespun coats, stood
close together beneath the holy images. At midnight the bells
broke forth, the doors opened, and priest and people went
around the church proclaiming the Resurrection. For a little while
the unfortunate serfs forgot their troubles. They embraced one another,
saying, "Christ is risen!" and answering, "He is risen indeed!"
Even Vasili's angry face softened when his small daughter gave
him a red egg.

But the next morning the command was repeated. Every household
was to send a plough with men and horses to
the upper field. Michael remained at home to feast with
two clerks and a neighboring steward.

The peasants went unwillingly. They drove their thin horses along
the muddy road while the bells sounded over the fields.
Some had drunk too much vodki and shouted curses. Others
spoke of turning back. Vasili walked beside his plough with
an axe hidden beneath the straw in his cart.

"What wilt thou do with that?" asked Tikhon.

"What ought to have been done long ago."

"Not to-day, Vasili."

"Why not to-day? It is better to destroy a wolf
on a holy day than to let him devour the
flock on a common one."

Peter Mikhayeff came behind them. He had put on his
old work clothes, but beneath his coat he still wore
the clean red shirt in which he had attended church.
In one hand he carried the reins, and in the
other a small Easter candle.

Vasili saw him and laughed bitterly.

"Wilt thou light the furrows for us?"

Peter answered:

"I brought the candle from the church, and I will
not let Michael's sin take the feast out of my
heart."

When they reached the upper field, the men set the
ploughs into the moist ground. The morning was bright but
windy. The larks sang overhead, the church bells sounded far
off, and from neighboring villages came faint voices of people
still exchanging Easter greetings. Yet Michael's serfs stumbled behind their
ploughs as though it were an ordinary day of bondage.

They cursed the superintendent, the nobleman, the horses, and the
earth. One man deliberately broke a trace. Another turned so
shallow a furrow that the sod fell back into place.
Vasili stopped often and looked toward the road.

Peter alone worked steadily. Before starting, he fixed the little
candle upright upon the wooden beam of his plough, shielding
it with his hands until the flame caught. Then he
crossed himself and began to sing in a low voice:

"Christ is risen from the dead,
Trampling down death by death…"

The wind moved across the open field, bending the dry
grass and lifting the skirts of the men's coats. The
plough jolted over stones and roots. Nevertheless, the little flame
did not go out. It burned clear and upright upon
the plough.

At first no one noticed it. Then Tikhon cried:

"Look, brethren! Peter's candle is still burning."

They gathered about him at the end of the furrow.

"Hide it in thy coat," said one. "The wind will
extinguish it."

Peter smiled.

"If God wills it to burn, it will burn."

He turned the plough and drove the horse forward. The
candle trembled, bent almost to the wood, and then rose
again. Peter continued singing, neither proudly nor as one who
wished to display a wonder, but peacefully, as he had
sung in church.

The sight silenced the men. Some took off their caps.
Vasili stood with his hand upon the hidden axe and
looked at the tiny light. Shame entered his heart, but
hatred still struggled against it.

At Michael's house there was no thought of prayer. He
had eaten roast goose, eggs, curd cakes, and sweet bread.
He drank vodki and wine with his guests, played badly
upon a guitar, and ordered the cook to sing. The
brass samovar hissed upon the table, the windows stood open
to the spring air, and scraps of food were thrown
to the dogs.

"Those lazy moujiks expected a holiday," Michael boasted. "I taught
them that the earth does not keep saints' days."

A groom who had carried water past the upper field
came into the kitchen. There he told the servants what
he had seen.

"All the peasants curse," said he, "except Peter Mikhayeff. He
sings the Easter hymn, and a church candle burns upon
his plough. The wind blows, the plough leaps, but the
flame does not die."

The cook repeated this to Michael's wife, Maria Ivanovna. She
was not an evil woman, though she had grown accustomed
to comfort bought by the peasants' misery. She entered the
dining-room.

"Mishinka," said she, using the affectionate name she had called
him in earlier days, "they say a strange thing is
happening in the upper field."

Michael laughed.

"Do the moujiks see angels now?"

"They say Peter Mikhayeff ploughs with a candle burning before
him."

At the name of Peter, Michael ceased laughing.

"What else do they say?"

"That the others curse thee, but Peter sings and works
in peace."

Michael put down his glass. His face, flushed from drink,
slowly became pale.

"Could the flame truly remain alight?"

"So they say."

He sent for the groom and questioned him closely.

"Didst thou see it thyself?"

"With my own eyes, Michael Simeonovitch."

"Was it in a lantern?"

"No. It stood naked upon the beam."

"Was there no glass around it?"

"None."

"And Peter—did he threaten me?"

"He said nothing against thee. He only sang."

Michael dismissed the groom, walked twice across the room, and
stopped. The guests tried to jest, but he did not
hear them. He remembered the old man whom he had
flogged, the widow's cow, Vasili's bleeding back, the women working
through winter nights, and the many kopecks he had taken.
Until that hour he had called such things discipline and
profit; now they appeared before him by their true name—sin.

"He has conquered me," Michael said.

No one answered.

He pressed his hands to his head.

"He has conquered me! They all curse me, and I
fear none of them. Let Vasili come with his axe—I
will have him bound. Let the whole village rise—I will
call soldiers. But Peter does not curse. He obeys, and
his candle burns. What weapon have I against such a
man?"

His wife approached him.

"Mishinka, do not harden thy heart. Ride to the field.
Release them. Tell them to keep the feast."

"They will laugh at me."

"Better that men laugh than that God condemn."

"I am ruined," said Michael. "That candle is burning for
my soul."

"Then repent while time is given thee."

He stood irresolute. The neighboring steward urged him to drink
and forget peasant superstitions; but Michael pushed the glass away.
At length he ordered his horse saddled.

When the horse was brought, Maria Ivanovna followed him onto
the porch.

"Speak gently to them," she pleaded. "Promise that henceforth thou
wilt demand only what is just."

Michael mounted.

"I shall tell them to return home."

"And ask pardon, Mishinka."

At this his pride stirred once more.

"A superintendent does not ask pardon of moujiks."

But as he rode away, he again muttered:

"He has conquered me."

The road to the upper field passed through the village
and by a gate where the fence had lately been
repaired. The new pickets were tall and sharply pointed. Children
were playing near the road, rolling painted eggs in the
dust. When they saw Michael, they scattered, for they feared
him.

Just then a pig ran squealing from beneath a cart.
Michael's horse shied violently. He pulled the reins and struck
it with his whip. The frightened animal reared, turned sideways,
and threw him against the fence. One sharp picket entered
beneath his ribs.

The horse galloped on, dragging the reins. Michael hung for
a moment upon the fence and then fell heavily to
the earth.

The children screamed. Women came from the cottages, but when
they recognized the superintendent, none dared approach. Some thought he
might rise and strike them; others feared they would be
accused of causing his death.

A boy ran to the upper field shouting:

"Michael Simeonovitch is killed! He lies by the gate!"

The peasants left their ploughs and hurried toward the village.
Vasili was first among them. When he saw Michael lying
motionless beside the fence, blood darkening his holiday coat, he
stopped.

"So," said he, "the wolf is dead without my axe."

Several men came closer, but none touched the body.

"Perhaps he still breathes," said Tikhon.

"Let him breathe or not," replied another. "How many times
did he show mercy?"

Then Peter arrived. The Easter candle was still burning upon
his abandoned plough in the distant field. He looked at
Michael, crossed himself, and knelt beside him. He placed a
hand upon the superintendent's breast, but the heart was still.

"Help me lift him," said Peter.

No one moved.

"Why should we soil our hands?" asked Vasili.

"Because he also was a man."

"A man? He lived like a beast and died like
a dog."

Peter looked sorrowfully at Vasili.

"Do not rejoice at death. He hath gone before the
Judge, and we shall follow."

Peter straightened Michael's limbs, closed his eyes, and, finding that
the others still held back, lifted the body as far
as he could and called again for help. At last
Tikhon and two old men came forward. Together they carried
Michael to his house.

Maria Ivanovna met them at the porch. When she saw
her husband, she gave a cry and fell upon him.

"He was going to release you," she sobbed. "He had
repented. He was going to set right the evil he
had done."

Peter bowed his head.

"May God receive his repentance."

Vasili, standing behind the others, heard these words. He remembered
the axe beneath the straw, and fear passed through him.
Had Michael taken another road, or had the horse not
shied, Vasili might have killed a man who was already
turning from evil. Then the superintendent's blood would have lain
not upon a senseless picket, but upon Vasili's soul.

After Michael's burial, the nobleman came from the city to
examine the estate. He had supposed his superintendent strict but
honest; now the peasants brought forth accounts, receipts, and witnesses.
He learned how Michael had exacted unlawful labor, taken goods,
beaten the weak, and enriched himself. Maria Ivanovna, grieving and
ashamed, concealed nothing.

The nobleman was troubled.

"If I knew it not," said he, "it was because
I did not wish to know. Therefore the sin is
also mine."

He dismissed the clerks who had aided Michael, abolished the
extra labor, and finally offered the peasants their freedom on
condition that they should pay a small yearly rent for
the land they used. The sum was not light, for
no peasant's burden is light; yet it was just, and
they accepted it with gratitude.

In time the village prospered. The women no longer spun
through the night for another man's gain. The moujiks ploughed
their own fields, and though hail sometimes destroyed the grain,
and sickness entered their houses, they bore such afflictions more
easily because they did not come from human cruelty.

Vasili never again spoke of murder. On the anniversary of
that Easter he went with Peter to the upper field.

"I believed my axe would free us," he said. "Yet
had I used it, soldiers might have burned the village,
and my children would have called a murderer their father."

Peter answered:

"Thy axe was strong enough to cut flesh, but not
strong enough to cut evil from the heart."

"And thy candle did what the axe could not."

"Nay," said Peter. "The candle was only wax and flame.
It was God's commandment that conquered him."

The peasants long remembered how Michael Simeonovitch, who had feared
neither curses nor threats, trembled before one peaceful man who
returned no evil and whose Easter light the wind could
not extinguish. And they understood that violence may terrify the
body for a season, but goodness alone conquers the soul;
for evil breeds only evil, while he who refuses to
hate is stronger than the tyrant and stronger than death.

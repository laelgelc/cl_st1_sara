Harry Esmond's First Home

Of all the days of Harry Esmond's childhood—and there were
many that he remembered afterward with a clearness which made
the intervening years seem but a mist—none was so wonderful
as that on which Lady Castlewood first took his hand
and called him her boy.

Until then he had belonged nowhere. He had eaten at
other men's tables, learned his Latin under other men's rods,
slept in chambers where no mother came to bless him,
and been reminded, sometimes by a servant's shrug and sometimes
by a gentleman's silence, that he was an orphan dependent
upon charity. He had borne these things with a grave
face, for pride comes early to the friendless; but the
heart beneath his little black coat was hungry enough, and
would have gone a great way for one kind word.

Lady Castlewood gave him a thousand.

She was then in the first splendour of her beauty,
with hair that shone warm as wheat under candlelight, grey
eyes that could be merry or melancholy in a moment,
and a manner so gentle that the poorest old woman
in the village thought my Lady had spoken especially to
her. When she bent over Harry, and touched the rough
lock upon his forehead, the child scarce dared breathe. He
had seen saints painted above altars, but none, he thought,
so fair as this living apparition.

"You are to stay with us, Harry," she said. "Frank
needs a companion, and Beatrix a governor, though she will
obey neither of us. Can you love them a little?"

"I can love them if they love you, madam," Harry
answered.

At this solemn declaration Lady Castlewood laughed, and then, seeing
the boy's colour rise, kissed him.

"There, sir," says she; "you have made court enough for
one morning. Go and conquer Frank."

Frank, who was younger than Harry, round-faced, noisy, and generous
when generosity cost him nothing he desired, received his new
companion with enthusiasm. Beatrix, a small beauty of seven years,
with her mother's eyes and her father's imperious little chin,
regarded him more doubtfully.

"Will he stay always?" she asked.

"If he behaves well," said Lady Castlewood.

"Then I hope he behaves ill sometimes," Beatrix replied, and
clung to her mother's gown.

But within a week she would permit no one else
to lead her pony, cut her bread, recover her ball
from the pond, or tell her stories after supper. If
Harry attended Frank, she wanted him; if he read to
my Lady, she climbed between them; if Lady Castlewood praised
him, Beatrix found some offence of which he had been
guilty, and repeated it with sparkling eyes.

"Mamma, Harry tore his sleeve."

"In pulling you from the brambles, miss."

"He has ink upon his finger."

"From writing out your French exercise."

"He says I am vain."

"Which was very impertinent," says Lady Castlewood, hiding a smile.

"And true," Harry added, at which Beatrix flew at him,
and peace was restored only when he carried her round
the long gallery upon his back.

Those were the happiest days he had yet known. The
house, old and irregular, with its gables, chimneys, dark stairs,
sunny withdrawing-room, and hall hung with trophies which Lord Castlewood
declared had belonged to ten generations of heroes, became to
Harry a kingdom more noble than any in romance. He
knew every stair that creaked, every panel which gave back
a hollow sound, every place where the afternoon sun touched
the floor. He studied with Frank, fenced with him, rode
with him, fought with him, and made peace before dinner.
He read French to Beatrix, though she corrected his accent;
and in the evenings he sat at Lady Castlewood's feet
while she worked, listening to her voice until the words
mattered less than the music of them.

Lord Castlewood, a handsome, hearty gentleman, fond of his bottle,
his hounds, and an easy life, regarded Harry with good
humour.

"The young scholar earns his meat," says my Lord. "He
keeps Frank out of the pond, Bea out of the
chimney, and her Ladyship from scolding me. Give him another
pudding."

"My Lord," Harry said one day, very red, "I do
not stay here for pudding."

"No? Then you have more philosophy at fourteen than I
shall have at forty. I stay wherever the pudding is
best."

Lady Castlewood reproved her husband with a look; but Harry
saw that she smiled, and forgave my Lord immediately.

He would have fought the whole county for that smile.
Indeed, he nearly began with young Tom Claypole, the steward's
nephew, who, repeating some village gossip he had picked up,
called Lady Castlewood proud.

Harry struck him before the sentence was finished.

Tom, being taller and two years older, returned the blow
with interest. They met behind the stable, stripped off their
coats, and fought with that grave savagery peculiar to boys,
who will endure more punishment for an imagined point of
honour than grown men commonly bear for a real one.
Frank stood second to Harry and cried out contradictory counsel.

"Hit him, Hal! Guard your left! Oh, capital! No, not
there! Give over, Tom, or I'll tell my father!"

Harry's nose bled, his lip was split, and one eye
presently closed; but he continued advancing until Tom, astonished by
such obstinacy, declared that he had never meant the words,
that Lady Castlewood was the finest lady alive, and that
Harry was a mad dog.

"You will say the dearest and kindest lady alive," Harry
insisted.

"I'll say an angel if you like," gasped Tom.

"That is enough."

When Harry appeared at supper, Lady Castlewood demanded an account
of his wounds. Frank, bursting with importance, told the story
before Harry could stop him.

"My poor foolish champion!" my Lady cried. "Would you have
every idle tongue answered with a broken head?"

"Yes, madam, if it spoke against you."

"You are not to fight for me."

"Then they must not insult you."

She turned away, perhaps because she would not encourage him,
perhaps because tears had come into her eyes. Lord Castlewood
laughed prodigiously and drank to Harry's next battle; Doctor Tusher,
the chaplain, observed that zeal ought always to be directed
by discretion; and Beatrix, who had called Harry hideous when
he entered, stole afterward to his chamber with a piece
of marchpane.

"You are not to die," she commanded. "Mamma would cry."

"And you, Trix?"

"I should have nobody to carry me."

Yet she kissed his bruised cheek and ran away.

Thus Harry grew in the warmth of a household which
he worshipped with all the ardour of one who has
long stood in the cold. If Frank had a headache,
Harry fetched the cordial. If Beatrix lost a ribbon, Harry
searched the orchard. If my Lady sighed, he wondered what
grief he might vanquish for her. When she smiled, the
day was bright; when she was silent, he moved softly;
when she praised him, he lay awake repeating the words.
Such devotion may appear extravagant to those who have always
been loved, but a cup of water seems no miracle
to a man living beside a spring.

One autumn afternoon Harry rode to the far end of
the village, where Giles Blackett, the smith, had promised to
mend a buckle upon Frank's little saddle. The forge door
stood open; sparks leaped beneath the hammer, and the air
smelled of iron, smoke, and wet leaves. Giles was absent,
but his wife came from the cottage, her apron held
to her face.

"Best not come in, Master Harry," she said. "The little
one is ill."

Harry, who knew the child and had brought him apples
more than once, asked what ailed him.

Mrs. Blackett hesitated. Behind her, from the dim room, came
a thin cry.

"It is the pox, sir. Doctor says there's no mistaking
it."

Harry stepped back as if a pistol had been fired.
He remembered that he had visited the cottage two days
before; the child had sat upon his knee, fingering the
silver buttons of his coat. That same evening Harry had
played with Frank, slept in the next bed, shared a
cup with him, and carried Beatrix through the gallery.

He left the buckle and ran all the way home.

Lady Castlewood was in the nursery, measuring Beatrix for a
new gown, while Frank built a fortress out of books
upon the carpet.

"Madam," Harry said, breathless, "I must speak with you alone."

Something in his face alarmed her. She followed him into
the passage and shut the door.

"Well, Harry?"

"I have been at Blackett's. Their youngest child has the
smallpox. I was there before—two days ago—and held him. I
did not know. I may have brought it here."

My Lady's face became white. Her hand flew to the
door behind which Frank and Beatrix were laughing.

"You held him?"

"Yes, madam."

"And afterward came among my children?"

"I knew nothing of it."

"You slept beside Frank?"

Harry bowed his head.

For one instant fear mastered all the gentleness in her.
She seized his arm and thrust him from the nursery
door.

"Go away! Do not come near them. Oh, merciful Heaven,
what have you done?"

The words struck harder than Tom Claypole's fists. Harry stood
silent.

"What have I done?" she repeated, walking distractedly. "Frank is
delicate; Beatrix has never had it. You must leave the
house. You should have been sent to college before this.
My Lord spoke of it; I was weak—I kept you
here—and now—"

"I will go," Harry said.

His quietness checked her more effectively than any reproach. Yet
panic still hurried her on.

"To Cambridge—to any proper place. Doctor Tusher will arrange it.
You are old enough. It is not right that you
should remain idling here."

"I will go tonight, madam."

He bowed to her as though she had been a
stranger and walked toward his chamber. He was too proud
to let her see him cry, though the passages swam
before him. A quarter of an hour earlier he had
possessed a home; now every familiar object seemed to say
that he had only been suffered there.

He had packed a shirt, two books, and the little
purse containing all his money, when the door opened. Lady
Castlewood entered without ceremony.

"Harry," she said.

He did not turn.

"Harry, look at me."

He obeyed. Her face was wet with tears.

"I have been cruel," she said. "I was frightened, and
a frightened mother is often a wicked judge. You came
straight to warn us, when another boy might have concealed
the danger. I rewarded your honesty by striking where I
knew you were most tender."

"Madam, you were thinking of Frank and Beatrix."

"As what mother would not? But you are my child
too—if you will forgive me and be so still."

At that, Harry's pride broke. He dropped upon his knees,
and she put her arms about his head.

"My poor boy," she murmured. "My faithful, foolish boy! We
will take precautions, and pray, and bear what comes together.
You shall not be sent away for bringing us the
truth."

He could not speak. He kissed her hand a thousand
and a thousand times, or so it seemed in his
later recollection; and if she had commanded him then to
go into exile, he would have gone blessing her.

Precautions came too late. Within a week Harry sickened. He
was moved to a distant chamber, where an old nurse
attended him; but before the eruption declared itself, Frank fell
ill also. Then Lady Castlewood, who had watched by her
son against every warning, took the fever in her turn.

The house changed from a place of laughter to a
besieged fortress. Doors were shut; fires burned night and day;
vinegar stood in bowls; servants whispered upon the stairs. Lord
Castlewood fled first to the hunting-lodge, loudly declaring that he
must preserve himself for the sake of the family. Doctor
Tusher went between the sickrooms with prayers, powders, and an
expression of Christian fortitude which diminished whenever anyone coughed near
him.

Beatrix alone escaped, being sent to an aunt before the
sickness fully showed itself. She departed raging, because no one
would let her kiss her mother.

Harry remembered little of his own illness: the crimson curtains
breathing in and out like monstrous lungs; the nurse's face
bending above him; the taste of bitter draughts; and, once,
Lady Castlewood's voice from the passage, asking how her boy
did, though she herself was scarcely able to stand. In
his delirium he fancied that he was again outside the
nursery door and that she commanded him to go. He
begged to remain, promising he would never touch Frank's cup,
never sit at her feet, never offend again.

But youth was strong in him. The fever spent itself,
the spots faded, and one cold morning he opened his
eyes with a clear mind and saw sunshine upon the
bed. Frank, too, recovered, though more slowly. Lady Castlewood's life
hung longer in doubt; and Harry, still too weak to
cross the room without trembling, lay listening for news of
her.

"Will she live?" he asked the nurse twenty times a
day.

"With God's mercy, sir."

"Does she ask for me?"

"For all of you."

"Tell her I am well."

"You are as lean as Lent."

"Tell her I am riding, then."

"I'll tell no such wicked lie."

At last word came that the danger was past. Harry
wept, laughed, attempted to rise, and fainted upon the floor.
The nurse, finding him there, called him every kind of
fool while putting him back to bed.

Some weeks later the three convalescents met in the great
withdrawing-room. Frank entered first, thin and pale, but proud of
the marks upon his face, which he regarded as scars
won in battle.

"I am altered, Hal," says he. "I look older."

"You look uglier," Beatrix observed, having returned from her aunt's
and being determined to show no excessive tenderness.

"Then I shall make a soldier."

Harry waited by the window. He had heard the servants
whisper that my Lady was changed, but he had formed
no picture of the change. When she entered, leaning upon
her woman's arm, he understood their silence.

The disease had spared her eyes and the gracious line
of her mouth, but it had marred the smooth fairness
of her skin. Her golden hair was hidden beneath a
lace cap; she moved with hesitation, as though fearing the
gaze of those who had once admired her. Lord Castlewood
kissed her with noisy affection and spoke too often of
how perfectly well she looked. Doctor Tusher declared the alteration
invisible. The servants lowered their eyes.

Lady Castlewood turned to Harry.

"Well, my poor bear," she said, striving to smile, "are
you frightened at me?"

Harry, who had never learned the useful art of lying
prettily, gazed at her with such grief that she knew
the truth before he spoke.

"You are changed, madam," he said.

The room fell still.

Lord Castlewood coughed. Doctor Tusher contemplated the ceiling. Beatrix stared
at Harry as if he had committed murder.

My Lady's scarlet lip trembled.

"Thank you, Harry. At least one friend will not flatter
me."

She drew away her hand, which he had been about
to kiss, and moved toward the fire. Harry saw that
he had wounded her; yet, being young and ashamed, he
knew not how to repair the hurt. He stood in
misery until she said, with formal gentleness, "I am tired.
I wish you a good night, Harry."

He went out like a condemned man.

For two days she treated him with perfect courtesy, which
was worse than anger. She asked whether he had slept,
praised his reading, and called him Mr. Esmond. Harry would
fain have provoked her into reproaching him, but she gave
him no opportunity. At last, finding her alone in the
gallery at dusk, he placed himself before her.

"Madam, you must hear me."

"Surely, Mr. Esmond."

"Do not call me that. Call me ungrateful, savage, stupid—anything
but that."

A faint smile touched her lips and vanished.

"I asked whether I was changed, and you answered me.
There is no crime in truth."

"There is, when it is spoken like a brute. I
saw that you had suffered, and I thought only of
my own grief."

"Your grief?" she said, looking up.

"Yes. Because anything had dared to hurt you. Because I
could not strike the sickness as I struck Tom Claypole.
Because—" His voice failed; then he continued passionately: "You are
changed, but you are not less dear. You could never
be less beautiful to me. If every person in England
called you ugly, I should know they lied. Your face
is the face that first looked kindly upon me. It
is the face I saw when I had no mother
and no home. I would know it and love it
if I were blind."

Lady Castlewood's eyes filled.

"Oh, Harry," she whispered, "you honest young blunderer!"

"I do not say it to comfort you."

"I know."

"You are the dearest, and kindest, and sweetest lady in
the world."

"So you told poor Tom Claypole with your fists."

"And I will tell anyone again."

She held out her hands then, and he kissed them,
while the last cold light lay across the gallery floor
and made a glory about her hair. Many a time
afterward Harry saw finer ladies, smoother cheeks, brighter jewels, and
faces praised by poets and courtiers; but none ever appeared
to him so beautiful as Lady Castlewood in that dusk,
smiling through tears at her awkward champion.

After their recovery a quieter happiness settled upon the house.
Harry, being the eldest of the children in sense, though
not in station, became by degrees their tutor and my
Lady's as well. Doctor Tusher directed the severer studies, but
Harry heard Frank his Latin, taught Beatrix history, read French
plays aloud, and discovered that Lady Castlewood herself had a
lively desire for knowledge.

"You must not laugh at my ignorance, sir," she said,
taking her place at the table with the children.

"I could no more laugh at you than at the
Queen."

"Which Queen?" asks Beatrix.

"Any Queen."

"That is evasion," says my Lady. "Write him down idle,
Trix."

Thus the schoolroom rang as often with laughter as with
conjugations. Frank escaped to the stables whenever he could and
returned with excuses so enormous that even he did not
expect belief. Beatrix learned everything quickly and forgot nothing, particularly
a correction. If Harry praised her brother, she surpassed him;
if he praised her, she pretended indifference and repeated the
compliment later to her maid. Lady Castlewood applied herself gently,
sometimes laying down her book to watch the three young
faces about her, and thanking God in silence that fever
had not emptied those chairs.

Harry remembered those years as a long summer, though there
were winters enough, and rains that flooded the lower meadow,
and household quarrels, and debts which made Lord Castlewood swear
at his steward. Happiness, when recalled, keeps little account of
weather. There were rides at dawn, lessons by the fire,
bowls upon the lawn, Christmas dances in the hall, village
feasts, and evenings when my Lady sang while Harry turned
the pages of her music. He was no longer merely
sheltered; he was useful, and usefulness is a proud joy
to one who has feared himself a burden.

Then news came that Lady Castlewood's aunt, an ancient gentlewoman
of considerable fortune and a temper sharpened by eighty years
of contradiction, was dead. She left money to her niece,
though less than my Lord had expected.

"Less!" cried Lord Castlewood, reading the lawyer's letter. "Why, she
had ten thousand pounds hidden in mattresses, if she had
a shilling."

"Perhaps she has left the mattresses elsewhere," my Lady said.

"She has left us a sermon on economy, I dare
swear. Dead relations are always prodigious economists with the money
they deny you."

Lady Castlewood laughed; but when her portion was settled, she
declared that her share should provide for Harry's education at
Cambridge.

My Lord stared.

"Harry's education? Why, Tusher has filled him with more Latin
than is wholesome already."

"He must have his chance in the world."

"He has a chance here. He may teach Frank not
to fall off a horse."

"Frank generally teaches me," Harry said.

But Lady Castlewood would not yield. She had long foreseen
the separation and perhaps, for that reason, had delayed naming
it. Harry was sixteen; he could not remain forever in
the enchanted circle of her household. Affection itself commanded that
he leave it.

When the matter was told him, gratitude and pain struggled
so violently that he answered almost with anger.

"I need no college, madam. I am happy here."

"That is why you must go."

"Because I am happy?"

"Because happiness must not make you little, Harry. You have
gifts, and courage, and a name to earn. Would you
spend them all in correcting Frank's grammar?"

"His grammar needs it," says Frank.

"And who will read with you?" Harry demanded. "Who will
keep Bea from vanity?"

"No mortal power can do that," said Beatrix, though her
eyes were red.

Lady Castlewood laid her hand upon his.

"You once said you would serve me. Go, then, and
become such a man as I may be proud to
call my son."

There was no answer to this. Harry bowed his head.

The last weeks passed with cruel speed. Every ordinary thing
became precious because it was nearly the last: the bell
for dinner, Frank's boots upon the stairs, Beatrix singing in
the garden, my Lord calling for his dogs, Lady Castlewood's
lamp shining beneath the drawing-room door. Harry visited the forge
and made peace anew with Tom Claypole; he walked to
the churchyard, where the names of strangers had once seemed
the nearest thing he possessed to kindred; he rode over
every field and looked back upon the house from every
hill.

On the morning of his departure, half the village assembled
before the gate. Giles Blackett had polished the horse's shoes
as if for a royal progress. Doctor Tusher delivered advice
in Latin and English. Lord Castlewood gave Harry a purse,
embracing him briskly and warning him against papists, card-sharpers, and
bad wine.

"Good wine," says my Lord, "is another matter, taken moderately—which
means when no more is to be had."

Frank promised to follow him to Cambridge and beat every
scholar there. Beatrix gave him a ribbon for his sword,
though he had no sword, and then turned away lest
he see her cry.

Lady Castlewood came last. She wore grey, and the morning
wind stirred the bright hair at her temples. For a
moment she could not speak.

"You will write?"

"Every week."

"Every week is often."

"Every day, then."

"No; live first, and write afterward. But do not forget
us."

"Forget!" Harry cried.

She placed her hands upon his shoulders and kissed his
forehead as she had on the first day.

"My boy," she said. "Go with God."

He mounted because he could not trust himself to answer.
At the bend in the road he turned. Lord Castlewood
waved his hat; Frank shouted; Beatrix stood upon the gate
rail, her ribbon fluttering; and Lady Castlewood, a little apart
from the others, raised one hand in farewell.

Harry carried that picture with him all his life after:
the old house among the autumn trees, the clustered servants,
the children who had been his first companions, and the
lady whose kindness had made a home for an orphan's
heart. Then the road dipped, the chimneys disappeared, and, with
tears drying upon his face and hope rising painfully within
him, Harry Esmond rode out into the world.

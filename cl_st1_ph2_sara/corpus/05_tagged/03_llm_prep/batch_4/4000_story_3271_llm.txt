I have crossed the sea many times since that autumn
passage in the *Balmoral Castle*, and have seen the English
coast under conditions more propitious to sentiment—at sunrise, when the
cliffs shone like a celestial rampart; in rain, when the
whole island seemed no more than a grey rumour upon
greyer water; and once after a long absence, with such
private happiness awaiting me that I could scarcely trust myself
to speak. Yet whenever I recall the first light of
England, it is not Dover, nor the Needles, nor any
comfortable harbour that rises before me. I see a pin-point
flash beyond a wilderness of black waves; and, against it,
a sick man holding up a crying child.

We were homeward bound from the Cape, carrying, as such
ships then carried, a little epitome of the Empire and
its inequalities. On the saloon-deck were mine-owners, colonial officials, merchants,
two young ladies returning from a visit to relations in
Natal, a retired admiral and his querulous wife, and Mr.
Jonas Olstein, whose millions had been made—according to the political
complexion of his listener—either by the genius of enterprise or
the timely embarrassment of other men. Below us, beyond a
companion which few of us descended, were three hundred emigrants
who had gone out poor and were returning poorer.

The sea, impartial in its violence but not in the
accommodation it allows one to purchase against that violence, had
been rough from Cape Town. We in the saloon complained
because the soup travelled perilously in its plates, because the
cards slid upon the tables, and because the fiddler could
not keep his chair during the after-dinner concert. Beneath us,
men, women and children lay in an atmosphere compounded of
damp clothes, paraffin, sickness and exhausted hope. A thin plank
separated the two worlds; yet it was curious how complete
a moral distance that plank contrived to establish.

I do not exclude myself from the indictment. I had
paid for my cabin, and accepted its privileges with the
ready logic of possession. If I occasionally leaned over the
rail and watched the third-class people taking the air forward,
my sympathy cost me nothing beyond a sigh; and a
sigh, as I have since discovered, is one of the
cheapest forms of moral expenditure.

They came up by divisions when the weather permitted: pale
women with babies pressed beneath shawls; broad-shouldered labourers whose colonial
clothes hung loosely upon them; boys who had lost the
noisy impudence proper to their age; and old men who
stared eastward as though England might be detected already through
five thousand miles of haze. Collectively they had a subdued
and waiting aspect. They spoke little. Their adventure had failed,
and failure among the poor is seldom granted privacy.

On the tenth day the purser organised sports, partly to
improve the temper of the passengers and partly, I suspected,
because a list of amusements looked well in the company's
printed account of the voyage. There were potato races, quoits,
a tug-of-war, and a competition in which blindfolded gentlemen attempted
to strike suspended pillows with a broom. We laughed abundantly.
In the afternoon a collection was proposed for prizes among
the emigrants.

Mr. Olstein took charge of it.

He was a large, moist man, with small eyes enlarged
but not softened by gold-rimmed spectacles. Diamonds flashed at his
shirt-front and upon two of his fingers; when he removed
his hat to wipe his forehead, the handkerchief passed over
a scalp as polished as his stones. He possessed that
restless indignation common in men who have prospered greatly and
fear that the world does not sufficiently understand the justice
of it.

"Now, gentlemen," he cried, standing beside the smoking-room door with
the list in one hand and his hat in the
other, "we're British, aren't we? These poor devils are British
too. Nobody can accuse Jonas Olstein of forgetting the flag.
What's the Empire for, I ask you, if one Englishman
won't give another a lift?"

The appeal yielded guineas, half-guineas and certain conspicuous promises which
had afterward to be recollected. One man declined, walked twice
round the deck, then intercepted the purser privately and gave
him a sovereign. Mr. Olstein, who had himself subscribed five
pounds and announced the amount three times, discovered the coin
upon the list.

"Anonymous?" said he. "Nonsense! No encouragement to anybody in anonymity."

Among the competitors was a London baker named Tom Hales.
I had noticed him before because he wore, pinned to
his poor coat, an enormous scarlet rosette supplied by the
sports committee. Its exuberance made his body appear more wasted.
His chest was hollow, his shoulders narrow, and above his
ragged beard the skin had that transparent brightness which experience
teaches one to dread. When spoken to, however, he blushed
with the quick modesty of a boy.

He entered the sack race, fell almost immediately, and rose
laughing while blood showed briefly upon his lips. The ship's
surgeon ordered him out of the remaining events. I found
him later under the lee of the fore-companion, watching a
group of children chase a rope's end as it slid
with the roll of the vessel.

"You should be below," I said.

"Air's better here, sir."

He spoke without complaint, and presently, by those accidental confidences
possible upon a ship, I learned his history.

He had kept a baker's shop near Bermondsey. When his
lungs failed, a charitable doctor had urged a warmer climate.
His wife was then expecting her confinement; nevertheless they sold
what they possessed, borrowed the remainder of the passage money,
and went to the Cape in the faith—common among the
desperate—that geography might perform what medicine could not. Their son
was born at Wynberg, a vigorous child; but Hales found
no baker willing to employ a man who coughed over
flour, and work in the open air was beyond his
strength.

"So we're going back," he concluded.

"Have you prospects at home?"

"Job waiting, sir. My old master wrote he'd take me
on."

He said this with quiet satisfaction.

"But will you be fit for it?"

For a moment he examined the children. One had captured
the rope and was being dragged, delighted, across the wet
boards.

"No, sir," he said. "Not for long. Doctor out there
told the missus I shouldn't have started home. But I
couldn't stop and die where she'd no people. Better get
her back. Master'll give me a week or two, likely.
After that—well, there's the club money, and she can sew.
Baby's uncommon strong."

The recital was so matter-of-fact that I had no answer
ready. He spared me the necessity of finding one.

"You should see him, sir. Six months, and sits up
by himself. Never had a day's illness. Climate suited him,
anyhow."

His eyes, which had seemed dull while he spoke of
his own death, became almost merry.

That evening I returned to the saloon, where Mr. Olstein
was explaining that the taxation of successful men constituted an
organised assault upon civilisation. I remember the peculiar discomfort with
which I saw his rings grip the stem of a
wineglass. It was not that he was cruel; cruelty requires
an apprehension of suffering, and I doubt whether he apprehended
anything beyond the penumbra of his own importance. Yet I,
who had listened to Hales, ate an excellent dinner and
did nothing. Moral superiority is a delicate luxury, and often
as unearned as wealth.

As we entered the Channel approaches, expectation spread through both
divisions of the ship. The emigrants remained longer on deck
despite the cold; the saloon passengers consulted charts, interrogated officers
and disputed the hour at which we should sight the
Eddystone. A sweepstake was arranged. Each subscriber drew a minute,
and the prize would go to the holder of the
time at which the light was first reported from the
bridge.

Mr. Olstein drew eleven thirty-seven.

"I tell you it's mine," he proclaimed. "Captain's calculations, speed
of ship, set of current—simple arithmetic. I have an instinct
for figures. That's why some men die poor and some
don't."

At eleven the night was clear overhead but obscure upon
the horizon. The wind had fallen; the long Atlantic swell
lifted us with grave regularity, so that the mastheads travelled
among the stars. Passengers gathered upon the hurricane-deck, muffled in
rugs and ulsters. Watches were produced and compared with the
saloon chronometer. The officer upon the bridge had warned us
that the light might be difficult to distinguish at first,
owing to haze.

Below, at the bow, the third-class passengers were also waiting.
No money depended upon their vigilance. They stood close together,
men with caps drawn down, women hugging shawls about themselves,
all facing the unseen coast.

At eleven twenty-five Mr. Olstein became animated.

"There!" he cried.

It was a star sinking low.

At eleven thirty-one the admiral's wife declared she saw a
flash; the admiral informed her that she had apostrophised the
riding-light of a distant steamer. At thirty-four somebody laughed, and
was reproved. The stillness gathered strangely. Even Mr. Olstein ceased
talking. The engines laboured beneath us with their tireless iron
pulse; the bow lifted, paused, and went downward into darkness.

I thought then of our divided company—of silver and coarse
tin, linen sheets and crowded bunks, the saloon staircase and
the fore-companion. Yet let the sea enter by one rent,
and how rapidly those distinctions would dissolve! The same engines
carried us; the same plates withheld the same black water;
and one extinguished light might have sent millionaire and baker,
grievance and hope, all together into an equality deeper than
any reformer had devised.

"Two minutes," said someone.

Mr. Olstein was pacing and turning and pacing again. Perspiration
stood upon his forehead despite the cold; the hairs on
the back of his hands seemed to bristle around his
rings.

"Thirty-six!"

Every face was turned south-westward.

Then, immeasurably distant, there came a spark—no larger than the
point made by a needle in black paper. It vanished.
Several seconds passed.

Again it shone.

"Light reported!" called the officer.

A cheer arose.

"Thirty-seven!" roared Mr. Olstein. "By God, thirty-seven! What did I
tell you? Mine—mine to the second!"

He struck his palm upon the rail, demanded confirmation from
three watches, and hurried toward the saloon to secure the
purse, lest, as he afterward explained, there should be any
muddle. The others followed, laughing and congratulating him. Within a
minute the hurricane-deck was nearly empty.

I remained. The light appeared, vanished, and returned, serene in
its appointed rhythm. From forward came a movement among the
emigrants, and then a figure broke from them and ran
toward the companion. It was Hales. He stumbled below.

Presently he reappeared carrying a bundle snatched hastily from sleep.
His wife followed, bareheaded and frightened.

"Tom, you'll chill him!"

But Hales did not hear. He came to the bows
and dragged away the shawl. A round, indignant face emerged;
the child screwed up its eyes against the wind and
began to cry.

Hales lifted him high.

"Look, baby!"

The little head rolled helplessly against his wrist.

"Out there—look! That's home."

The light flashed.

Hales laughed, though his breath caught painfully; then tears ran
into his beard. He held the child higher, as if
the small blinking eyes must receive, before it was too
late, the whole dark inheritance—the crowded streets, the smoky roofs,
hunger perhaps, labour certainly, but kinship, speech, memory, and a
place where his mother would not stand alone.

"England!" he cried.

His wife put one hand upon his shoulder.

"England, baby—England!"

The child wailed. The lighthouse shone once more, and in
that moment the father's tears fell upon his son's face
like a baptism.

Behind us, through the bright saloon windows, Mr. Olstein was
counting his winnings.

## The Rose of the Winter Garden

At the foot of an old cedar sat a young
Student, and his face was hidden in his hands.

"She has promised to dance with me if I bring
her a red rose," he cried, "but in all the
garden there is not one red rose!"

Above him, in the shadow of the cedar, a Nightingale
listened from her nest. She had sung of Love through
many silver nights, though she had never seen him; and
she had praised his kingdom to the Moon, though she
had never entered it.

"Here at last is a true lover," she said. "Night
after night I have told his story, and behold, he
is sitting beneath me. His hair is dark as the
hyacinth before rain, and sorrow has made his face pale
as carved ivory. Surely Love has laid a purple wound
upon his heart."

The Student lifted his head.

"To-morrow the Prince gives a ball," he said, "and the
Professor's daughter will be there. If I bring her a
red rose, she will dance with me until dawn. Her
hand will rest in mine, and her hair will brush
my cheek, and I shall know all that the poets
mean. But there is no red rose in the garden,
and therefore I shall sit alone, and she will pass
me as though I were a shadow. How small a
thing may destroy a man's happiness!"

"How strange is Love!" said the Nightingale. "It is finer
than the emeralds merchants bring from the East, and dearer
than pearls drawn from ocean-caverns. Neither gold nor crystal can
purchase it, and yet a little flower may serve as
its messenger."

The green Lizard, who was warming himself upon a stone,
raised his narrow head.

"Why is he weeping?" he asked.

"For a red rose," answered the Nightingale.

The Lizard laughed, for he was a creature of reason.
A yellow Butterfly, floating over the grass like a scrap
of sunlight, also laughed; and a Daisy whispered to her
neighbour that young men were very foolish.

But the Nightingale spread her brown wings and flew into
the garden.

In the centre of the lawn stood a Rose-tree covered
with white blossoms, and they trembled in the breeze like
little clouds caught among green stars.

"Give me a red rose," cried the Nightingale, "and I
will sing you my sweetest song."

The Tree shook its leaves.

"My roses are white," it answered, "white as foam upon
a northern sea, and whiter than the snow that sleeps
on mountain summits. Go to my brother beside the old
sundial. Perhaps he can give you what you seek."

So the Nightingale flew to the Tree beside the sundial.

Its roses were yellow, and among the dark leaves they
shone like coins scattered by a careless king.

"Give me a red rose," cried the Nightingale, "and I
will sing you my sweetest song."

The Tree shook its leaves.

"My roses are yellow," it answered, "yellow as the hair
of a mermaid beneath green water, and more golden than
the robes of Autumn when she walks through the harvest-field.
Go to my brother beneath the Student's window. Perhaps he
can give you what you seek."

So the Nightingale flew to the Tree beneath the Student's
window.

It was bare and bent, for Winter had struck it
with his icy staff. The frost had bitten its roots,
the wind had broken its youngest branches, and no flower
adorned it.

"Give me a red rose," cried the Nightingale, "and I
will sing you my sweetest song."

The Tree gave a sorrowful shiver.

"My roses are red," it said, "red as coral beneath
the sea, and redder than the ruby upon a monarch's
hand. But Winter has chilled my veins, and the storm
has torn my limbs. This year I shall bear no
roses."

"One red rose is all I ask," said the Nightingale.
"Is there no way by which I may obtain it?"

"There is a way," answered the Tree, "but it is
so terrible that I dare not tell you."

"Tell me," said the Nightingale. "I am not afraid."

"If you desire a red rose, you must fashion it
out of music by moonlight and stain it with your
own heart's-blood. All night you must sing to me with
your breast against a thorn. All night you must sing,
and the thorn must enter your heart, and your life
must flow through my branches and become mine."

"Death is a great price for a flower," said the
Nightingale. "It is pleasant to rest among leaves and watch
the golden Sun drive his chariot over the world. Sweet
is the scent of hawthorn, and sweet are the bells
of the blue flowers in the valley. Yet Love is
better than Life, and what is the heart of a
bird beside the heart of a man?"

She rose into the air and returned to the cedar.
The Student still sat beneath it, and his tears had
not yet dried.

"Be glad," cried the Nightingale. "You shall have your red
rose. I shall make it from song in the moonlight
and colour it with my heart's-blood. Only be faithful in
your Love, for Love is wiser than Philosophy and stronger
than Power. His wings are flame-coloured, and his throne stands
beyond the reach of kings."

The Student looked up and listened, but he could not
understand what she said, for he knew only the language
written in books.

The cedar understood, and grew sad, because it loved the
little bird who had nested among its branches.

"Sing me one last song," it whispered. "When you are
gone, the night will seem very empty."

Then the Nightingale sang, and her voice was like clear
water falling into a silver basin.

The Student listened for a while.

"She has a beautiful manner," he said, "but I doubt
whether she feels anything. Musicians are often like that. They
give us perfect sound and keep their meaning to themselves.
Still, the song is agreeable."

He took a notebook from his pocket and wrote several
observations concerning form. Then he went indoors, lay upon his
narrow bed, and dreamed of the Professor's daughter.

When the Moon rose, pale and cold in her crystal
chariot, the Nightingale flew to the Rose-tree beneath the window.

"Are you ready?" asked the Tree.

"I am ready," said the Nightingale.

She pressed her breast against the thorn and began to
sing.

All night long she sang, and the Moon leaned down
to listen. All night long she sang, and the thorn
rested against her heart.

First she sang of Love awakening in the souls of
a boy and a girl. She sang of the shy
glance across a crowded room, of the hand that trembles
before it is touched, and of the secret hope that
opens unseen, as a white flower opens beneath the stars.

Upon the highest branch of the Tree there appeared a
rose, faint as mist upon a mirror.

"Press closer, little Nightingale," murmured the Tree, "or Morning will
come before the rose is complete."

The Nightingale pressed closer, and the thorn entered her breast.

Louder grew her song, and she sang of Love when
it is crowned with joy. She sang of dances beneath
painted ceilings, of gardens where fountains spoke to the lilies,
of kisses stolen behind curtains of blue silk, and of
two hearts believing that Time had forgotten them.

The rose widened petal by petal. At first it was
colourless as sea-foam; then a delicate blush passed over it,
as dawn passes over pearl. Pink grew its outer leaves,
pink as the feet of a dove, while its centre
remained pale.

"Press closer, little Nightingale," cried the Tree, "for the heart
of the rose is still white."

The Nightingale pressed closer, and the thorn went deeper.

A sharp pain passed through her, and her wings beat
wildly against the air; but her song became richer, and
she sang of Love made perfect by sorrow. She sang
of separation and long waiting, of letters carried across stormy
seas, of lamps burning in lonely windows, and of one
name guarded faithfully when all other names had been forgotten.

The Moon heard her, and her white face grew troubled.
Echo caught the song and carried it among the hills.
The Reeds whispered it to the river, and the river
bore it toward the sea.

Still the centre of the rose was pale.

"Press closer, little Nightingale," begged the Tree, "or your labour
will be lost."

The Nightingale pressed with all her strength.

The thorn pierced her heart.

Then her song rose sudden and terrible, for she sang
of Love that dies and does not repent. She sang
of the gift for which no thanks are given, of
the altar that no worshipper sees, of the treasure thrown
before the blind, and of the heart whose breaking becomes
beauty for another.

Her blood flowed warm upon the Tree. It passed through
root and branch and leaf, and the rose drank it.
Crimson spread across the petals, crimson as sunset upon a
field of snow, crimson as wine in a cup of
ivory, crimson as the secret flame at the centre of
the world.

At last the rose stood perfect beneath the Moon.

Its petals were red as royal velvet, and its heart
burned like a ruby.

But the Nightingale's voice grew faint. Her little wings ceased
their trembling, and a grey veil passed over her eyes.
She gave one final note, so pure that the Moon
forgot the coming dawn and lingered in the sky.

Then the Nightingale fell among the grass, and the thorn
remained dark with her heart's-blood.

"Behold!" cried the Tree. "The rose is finished."

But the Nightingale did not answer.

At noon the Student opened his window.

"How remarkable!" he exclaimed. "Here is a red rose, and
upon my own Tree! It must be an exceedingly rare
variety. No doubt it has a very long name in
Latin."

He leaned out, plucked the rose, and fastened it to
his coat. He did not see the little brown body
beneath the grass, nor the dark stain upon the thorn.

"What good fortune!" he said, and hurried to the Professor's
house.

The Professor's daughter sat at the doorway winding blue silk
upon a reel. She wore a dress the colour of
summer clouds, and a small dog slept at her feet.

"You promised to dance with me if I brought you
a red rose," said the Student. "Here is the reddest
rose in the world. Wear it near your heart, and
while we dance it will tell you how much I
love you."

The girl looked at the flower and frowned.

"I do not think it will suit my dress," she
said. "Red and blue are always quarrelling. Besides, the young
Lord from the palace has sent me a bracelet set
with sapphires, and everyone knows that jewels are worth much
more than flowers."

"But this rose is perfect," protested the Student.

"Perfection is not the same as fashion," she replied. "And
you have no silver buckles on your shoes. The young
Lord has silver buckles."

"You are ungrateful!" cried the Student.

"You are rude," said the Professor's daughter. "After all, you
are only a Student."

The Student turned away in anger.

"What a foolish thing Love is!" he said. "It makes
promises it cannot keep and persuades one to believe what
is plainly untrue. It is not half so useful as
Logic, for Logic proves matters, while Love merely confuses them."

He tore the rose from his coat and flung it
into the street.

It fell into the gutter, where muddy water stained its
petals. Presently a merchant's cart came rattling by, and the
wheel passed over it.

"What nonsense it is to suffer for such a thing,"
said the Student. "I shall return to Philosophy."

He went back to his room, took down a great
dusty volume, and began to read about the nature of
Reality.

Outside his window, the broken rose lay beneath the cart-track,
and beneath the Tree the Nightingale lay still.

The Moon had gone, the garden glittered in the common
sunlight, and no one knew that Love had sung there
all night long.

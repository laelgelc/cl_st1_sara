A YEAR AND A QUESTION

It was one year, lacking three days, after the celebrated
trial in the king's arena, when five foreign dignitaries came
riding into the capital upon five yellow camels. They had
crossed two deserts, three rivers, and a frontier at which
every traveller was required to declare his occupation, his destination,
and whether he preferred justice to mercy. Their object was
neither commerce nor conquest. They wished to learn whether the
young man of the arena had opened the door of
the lady or the door of the tiger.

The deputation consisted of a prince, a philosopher, a high
priest, a minister of finance, and a widow of extraordinary
rank. Each believed himself—or, in the widow's case, herself—peculiarly fitted
to judge doubtful matters. The prince understood love; the philosopher
understood motives; the priest understood destiny; the minister understood probabilities;
and the widow understood men.

Having refreshed themselves with rose-coloured sherbet, they were conducted through
seven courts to the Hall of Considered Answers, where sat
the Officer of Royal Conclusions. He was a grave personage,
clad in green silk, with a golden key embroidered upon
his breast. It was well known that the key opened
nothing, but it had been bestowed upon his grandfather and
was therefore highly respected.

The five strangers made obeisance.

"Most noble officer," said the prince, speaking for the rest,
"the fame of your sovereign's arena has gone forth among
all nations. We have journeyed far to learn the truth.
When the accused youth opened the door indicated by the
princess, what came forth—the lady or the tiger?"

The officer folded his hands.

"Most noble strangers," said he, "before I answer, it is
necessary that you should answer me."

The philosopher frowned slightly, for he considered this an inversion
of proper inquiry.

"What question can equal ours?" he asked.

"That," replied the officer, "you shall judge when you have
heard the history of the Veiled Bride."

The widow settled herself among the cushions. The minister produced
a tablet. The priest closed his eyes in preparation for
enlightenment, and the prince ordered more sherbet.

The officer began.

In the reign of the king's father, who was also
semi-barbaric, though in a somewhat more polished manner, there came
to court a young prince from beyond the Mountains of
Blue Stone. He was handsome, well educated, and so accustomed
to being obeyed that he had not yet learned the
usefulness of obedience.

The king received him magnificently. There were trumpets, fountains of
perfumed water, dancing bears with silver collars, and a banquet
at which each guest was served a dish named after
one of the royal virtues. The prince, who was of
a thoughtful disposition, praised everything and ate sparingly of the
Royal Moderation.

On the third day the king said to him:

"You are unmarried."

"That is true, your Majesty."

"It is an omission."

"Perhaps," said the prince, "but not, I trust, an offence."

"An omission in a prince," replied the king, "is an
offence waiting for a proclamation. You must marry."

The prince bowed.

"I have often considered the matter."

"That is fortunate. You shall marry to-morrow."

The prince ceased bowing.

"Your Majesty," he said cautiously, "I had supposed that marriage
required a certain acquaintance between the persons concerned."

"You are acquainted with yourself," said the king, "and the
woman will be acquainted with herself. Thus both parties will
possess acquaintance."

"I should prefer to examine the character, disposition, tastes, education,
and relative intellectual development of the lady."

"You may examine them after the ceremony."

"But suppose we are unsuited?"

"Then," said the king graciously, "you will have discovered it."

The prince endeavoured to smile as one smiles at a
monarch whose humour is guarded by soldiers.

"May I inquire," he said, "which lady your Majesty has
in mind?"

"All of them."

The prince looked about the hall. Along the walls stood
many beauteous women of the court, daughters of dukes, nieces
of generals, cousins of chamberlains, and one young person whose
ancestry was uncertain but whose eyes made ancestry appear an
unnecessary accomplishment.

"Surely," said the prince, "I am not expected to marry
all."

The king's brow contracted.

"I said that you should marry. I did not say
that you should make difficulties. Difficulties are the weeds of
government, and I root them up."

He struck a silver gong. A side door opened, and
there entered a tall man in a crimson robe. He
bore a naked cimeter, whose edge was so delicately keen
that a sunbeam falling upon it appeared to divide into
two parts. This official was known as the Discourager of
Hesitancy. His post was ancient, honourable, and seldom vacant for
long.

The Discourager saluted the king, then the prince, and finally
the cimeter. He handled the weapon as tenderly as though
it had been a sleeping infant.

"This gentleman," said the king, "assists those whose decisions require
expedition."

The prince looked at him, and proceeded to be measured
for a wedding coat.

During the remainder of the day the palace was filled
with preparations. Tailors stitched; goldsmiths hammered; cooks compounded; priests rehearsed
blessings; musicians disputed over a march; and the Keeper of
Auspicious Birds sought everywhere for a white peacock, finally painting
a grey one. The prince attempted to question the courtiers.

"Which lady am I to marry?" he asked the Lord
of the Inner Curtain.

"What our king thinks right, that will he do," replied
the lord.

"Does no one know?"

"All know that his Majesty knows."

"Does his Majesty know?"

The lord looked shocked.

"To ask whether the king knows is to imply the
possibility that he does not know. Such a possibility is
unknown."

The prince next appealed to an elderly duchess.

"Madam, can you give me no hint?"

"I can give you excellent advice," she said. "Do not
sneeze during the blessing."

At sunset the Discourager visited the prince's apartment to inquire
whether his courage was comfortable. Finding that it was not,
he polished the cimeter beside him until midnight.

The ceremony took place the following morning in the Chamber
of Forty Lamps. The prince entered in cloth of gold,
attended by nobles, heralds, pages, fan-bearers, sword-bearers, ring-bearers, and two
officials whose duties had been forgotten but whose uniforms were
splendid. Before approaching the altar, he was blindfolded with a
band of black velvet.

"What is the meaning of this?" he demanded.

"It is the king's wish," said the high priest.

"I cannot see."

"That is the purpose of a blindfold."

"I protest."

The Discourager coughed courteously behind him.

The prince withdrew his protest.

A great rustling of silk arose at the farther end
of the chamber. Slippered feet approached. The prince strained his
ears, but forty lamps crackled, musicians played softly, and the
court whispered with that particular silence which is made by
many people trying not to make a sound.

Then a hand was placed in his.

It was a small, warm hand, and at its touch
his indignation changed to wonder. He had imagined that terror
would make any hand tremble; this one did not tremble.
Its fingers rested within his with confidence and gentleness.

The priest began the ancient formula.

When the prince was required to answer, he answered. When
the unseen woman was required to answer, her voice came
softly through the veil of darkness.

"I consent."

The voice was low and sweet, yet there was in
it something which seemed to him either courage or amusement.
He wished to hear it again.

The rings were exchanged. The blessings were pronounced. Flowers descended
from the gallery. The musicians struck their cymbals, and the
court cried, "Long live the bride and bridegroom!"

Amid the tumult, the bride drew nearer.

"I am here," she whispered.

The prince's heart gave a leap.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

"I am here."

Before he could speak again, her hand slipped from his.
Silk rustled away; voices rose; doors opened and closed. He
tore at the velvet band, but two attendants restrained him
until the king commanded that it be removed.

He found himself standing alone before the altar.

"Where is my wife?" he cried.

The king, seated upon a throne of carved ebony, smiled
benignly.

"You shall now have the pleasure of recognizing her."

At a signal, golden doors were thrown open. Forty ladies
entered and formed a crescent before the prince.

They were of the same height, or had been made
so by slippers of varying thickness. They wore gowns of
the same white silk, veils arranged in the same manner,
and jewels of equal lustre. Their hair was dressed alike.
Their hands were gloved. Even their complexions had been brought,
by the artists of the court, to a common perfection.

"Among these ladies," said the king, "stands your lawful bride."

"Which one is she?"

"That," said the king, "is what you are to discover."

The prince examined the line in dismay.

"I heard her voice."

"They will not speak."

"I held her hand."

"They are gloved."

"She whispered to me."

"They have all been instructed in whispering."

The prince walked slowly before them. Their eyes followed him,
though some looked modestly downward and others modestly upward, according
to the fashion of modesty prevailing in their families.

"What happens if I choose correctly?" he asked.

"You depart with your bride, my blessing, twelve camels, and
a silver bath."

"And if I choose incorrectly?"

"That depends. If you select a married woman, her husband
may object. If you select a widow, her relations may
object. If you select an unmarried woman, the law will
object."

"In what manner?"

The Discourager of Hesitancy stepped forward and tested the cimeter
upon a floating rose petal. The petal became two petals.

"The unmarried ladies," said the king, "have been reserved for
future diplomatic necessities. To choose one would be an injury
to the state. Injuries to the state are answered at
the neck."

The prince observed the forty faces again.

"Is this justice?"

"It is marriage," said the king. "Do not confuse ceremonies."

For a considerable time the prince studied eyes, lips, posture,
breathing, and such portions of the human soul as may
be detected through identical veils. Thirty-eight ladies maintained an expression
of perfect serenity. Of the remaining two, one smiled at
him and one frowned.

Instantly his attention fixed upon these.

The smiling lady stood near the centre. Her smile was
slight but unmistakable. It might have said, "Beloved, you have
found me." It might equally have said, "Poor stranger, you
are about to choose wrongly, and I cannot prevent it."
It might have been the smile of confidence, affection, pity,
triumph, obedience, or concealed terror. The more he considered it,
the more meanings it acquired.

The frowning lady stood three places from the end. Her
brows were drawn together, and her lips were grave. Was
this distress because her husband did not recognize her? Was
it a warning that he must not choose her? Was
she jealous of the smiling woman? Was she frightened? Or
was she, in truth, the bride, displeased that a man
who had held her hand and heard her voice should
require more than a heartbeat to know her?

The prince reasoned rapidly.

"Would my bride smile if she desired me to choose
her? Surely she would. But would she not fear that
so plain a signal might be forbidden and punished? Then
perhaps she would frown, hoping to conceal her desire. Yet
if she frowned, would I not think she rejected me?
She might smile because she is not my bride and
has nothing to lose. But if she is unmarried, she
has my selection and my death to consider. Would that
make her smile? It might, if she dislikes me. Would
my true bride frown because she loves me? Would she
not smile because she loves me? Would she not—"

"Enough," roared the king. "You have had four minutes."

"For the choice of a wife?"

"I required less time to choose a war."

The king raised his hand.

"You shall decide in ten seconds."

The Discourager drew nigh, courteously placing himself where the prince
could appreciate the cimeter's workmanship.

"Ten," said the king.

The prince looked at the smiling woman.

"Nine."

He turned to the frowning woman.

"Eight."

The smile seemed warmer. The frown seemed deeper.

"Seven."

If the smile invited him, the frown warned him. But
perhaps the warning was the invitation and the invitation the
warning.

"Six."

He remembered the soft hand.

"Five."

He remembered the whisper: "I am here."

"Four."

Both women were here.

"Three."

He took one step.

"Two."

He stretched out his hand.

"One."

He chose.

A murmur swept through the Chamber of Forty Lamps. The
selected lady came forward. The king rose, descended from his
throne, and lifted her veil.

"You have chosen well," he said. "This is your lawful
bride."

The Discourager sheathed his cimeter with evident regret. The musicians
played; the courtiers shouted; the silver bath was brought forth;
and the prince, who had been pale, became so suddenly
radiant that his raptures were almost rampant.

Here the Officer of Royal Conclusions ceased speaking.

The five foreign dignitaries leaned toward him.

"Well?" cried the visiting prince. "Which did he choose?"

"The smiling woman or the frowning woman?" asked the widow.

"That," replied the officer, "is the question."

The philosopher objected that the bride's prior conduct must supply
a logical inference. The priest maintained that the frown indicated
sacrificial love. The minister calculated that a smile was three
parts more probable than a frown, unless the penalties for
signalling were severe, in which case the result reversed itself.
The widow declared that either expression might mean anything whatever
and frequently did.

They debated until the lamps were lighted. They debated through
supper, through a second bowl of sherbet, and until their
camels had lain down in the courtyard and refused to
rise.

At last the prince addressed the officer.

"We cannot decide."

The officer bowed.

"When you have decided whether the bride smiled or frowned,"
said he, "I shall tell you whether the door in
the arena concealed the lady or the tiger."

At the latest accounts, the five dignitaries were still in
the capital. The philosopher had written nine volumes; the minister
had exhausted seven tablets; the priest had ordered a fast;
the prince had fallen in love with one of the
court ladies, though he had not determined which; and the
widow smiled whenever the matter was mentioned.

Or perhaps she frowned.

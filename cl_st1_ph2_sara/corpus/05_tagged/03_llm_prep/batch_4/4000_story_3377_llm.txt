## Grandma's Other Eyes

The grandchildren remembered Grandpa first by his two canes.

One was blackthorn, polished by his hard palm until it
shone like horn; the other he had cut from an
apple bough in the year the north orchard froze. He
used them together on bad mornings, planting them before him
with grave deliberation, as if he were measuring every foot
of earth between the kitchen door and the barn. Yet
if Grandma called, "Father, where be you?" he would move
with astonishing quickness, forgetful of rheumatism, winter, and ninety years.

"I'm a-comin', Mother," he always answered.

You would never know, to hear them, that they had
spoken those simple names for more than sixty years. There
remained in his voice a boy's eagerness and in hers
the shy contentment of a bride.

Their two canes often stood crossed in the corner—Grandma had
used hers before blindness confined her nearer the hearth—and little
Jerry said they looked like two old trees leaning together
against a storm. The comparison pleased Martin, his father, though
he did not say so. Martin was not given to
saying the tender things he felt. His parents had not
been, either. They had expressed love in split kindling, warmed
bricks, mended sleeves, medicines measured at midnight, and the last
spoonful of preserves saved for another. Their devotion was not
a subject they discussed. It was a fact of the
universe, like sunrise, seedtime, and the Lord's mercy.

Grandpa had been a tall young man once, brown-haired and
straight, able to pitch hay from dawn until the stars
appeared. Grandma—then Ruth Bell—had possessed eyes of such luminous gray
that he sometimes lost the thread of her speech while
looking into them. They were poor, but poverty in youth
may wear a wreath of wildflowers if hope walks beside
it.

On the evening they became engaged, they sat under the
great elm beyond the Bell pasture. The meadow was misty,
the leaves wet with dew; low in the west, the
afterglow burned rose and topaz, while one star trembled above
it like a lamp in the window of heaven.

"I ain't got much to offer ye, Ruth," Ezra said.

"You've got yourself."

"That's what I mean. 'Tain't much."

She laughed, and the sound flew up into the branches
like a bird. Then she became grave and laid her
hand in his.

"It's enough fer me."

The world has heard prouder vows under painted ceilings, with
gold rings, music, and silken garments; but perhaps the angels
bent lower over those two in the damp grass, for
they had nothing to give except their whole lives.

They began housekeeping in a weather-beaten cottage with a roof
that leaked above the pantry and a chimney which smoked
whenever the wind blew east. Ezra owned one cow, a
second-hand plow, and forty acres mortgaged beyond what seemed reasonable.
Ruth brought a feather bed, six towels, a blue bowl,
and a braided rug made from her mother's dresses. They
labored. They saved. They laughed more than one might suppose.

Then their first baby came.

She lived only eleven weeks.

Those weeks remained in Grandma's memory not as a small
portion of time but as a whole country, bright and
sorrowful, through which she could wander even when she was
very old. There had been the wooden cradle beside the
bed, the tiny hand curling around her finger, the faint
milky breath. There had been Ezra coming softly from the
barn and bending over the child before removing his muddy
boots. There had been one terrible night when the wind
shook the windows, and the baby's breathing grew lighter, lighter,
until the room held only the ticking clock and Ruth's
cry.

Ezra made the coffin himself from white pine. No carpenter's
work had ever cost more. Ruth lined it with cotton
wool and a piece of her wedding petticoat; her tears
fell upon the stitches, but every stitch was even. They
put a candle beside the little form and one white
rose in its hands.

Afterward, people told them that they were young, that other
children would come. Other children did come—Martin, two daughters, and
a boy who died grown—but no child replaces another. Love
does not mend its house by closing one room. It
keeps the room, dusts it with memory, and sometimes enters
there to pray.

The topaz-and-pearl pin belonged to that first sorrow and to
all the happiness before it.

Ezra had seen it in Willet's shop window when he
went to town for harness buckles. It was not costly
by the measure of wealthy people: an oval topaz, amber-colored
and clear, surrounded by six seed pearls. But to him
it seemed made of sunset and tears. Ruth had admired
it once, then turned away quickly after hearing the price.

That winter Ezra quit tobacco, though he had smoked since
he was sixteen. He sold two fox pelts and worked
three nights repairing Mr. Hale's stone wall by lantern light.
He walked to town rather than spend five cents on
the stage. Ruth noticed his weariness and asked questions.

"Nothin'," he said. "Jest chores."

On Christmas morning she found the pin in a paper
box beneath her plate.

"Ezra!"

She touched it but did not lift it. Wonder and
reproach struggled in her face.

"You hadn't ought!"

"Mebbe not."

"We need flour, an' the note's due come March."

"I know."

"Then what possessed ye?"

He looked down, embarrassed by the very magnitude of his
tenderness.

"I wanted ye to have suthin' pretty."

That was all.

Years afterward, she learned what he had done to buy
it. By then the baby had come and gone. Ruth
wore the pin at the little funeral because Ezra fastened
it to her black dress with his own shaking fingers.
The stone caught the candlelight; the pearls lay pale around
it. Henceforth it held, for her, the glow of courtship,
the sacrifice of a poor young husband, and the unspeakable
sweetness of the child whose eyes had opened so briefly
upon earth.

She wore it on Sundays and at weddings, funerals, baptisms,
and Thanksgiving dinners. When the farmhouse burned in the winter
of '82, Ezra carried out Martin, Ruth carried the baby,
and afterward—standing barefoot in the snow—she cried, "The pin!"

Ezra would have gone back into the flames if Martin's
uncle had not seized him. Yet the pin was found
the next day in the drawer of a blackened sewing
table, its box scorched, the topaz unharmed.

"Seems like the Lord means ye to keep it," Ezra
said.

"Seems like," she answered, though she thought perhaps God understood
that she could not bear to lose everything at once.

By the time their hair turned white, the mortgage had
long been paid, the second farmhouse stood square and weather-tight,
and apple trees planted in their middle years made a
green benediction behind the barn. One tree bore, one autumn,
two apples joined upon a single stem. Ezra carried them
into the kitchen as carefully as if they had been
treasure.

"Look here, Mother. That's us."

Ruth laughed. "Which one's you?"

"The handsome one."

"They're both wormy."

"Then either'll do."

It was in that same autumn that the pin disappeared.

Grandma had worn it to Martin's house for the christening
of his youngest daughter, Ellen. On returning home she removed
her shawl in the yard because the day was warm.
Indoors, her hand flew to her collar.

"It's gone!"

Grandpa was deaf in one ear, but he heard that
cry.

"What's gone?"

"My pin. My topaz pin!"

They searched the wagon, the road, Martin's parlor, and the
church where the christening had taken place. Martin's children walked
in a line through the grass, bending low. Grandpa sifted
dust in the barn passage. Grandma emptied her trunk and
unfolded garments she knew could not contain it.

"It's gone," she repeated, sitting at last beside the window.
"After all these years, it's gone."

Grandpa stood behind her with both hands on his cane.
He could have endured the loss of money or stock
with equanimity; but the grief in her voice made him
look old in a single afternoon.

"I'll find it," he said.

"How?"

"Dunno. But I will."

For three days he searched. On the fourth, Jerry climbed
the elm in pursuit of a young crow. High among
the branches he discovered an abandoned nest containing string, a
spoon handle, two bits of blue glass, and something that
flashed gold through the twigs.

"Grandpa!" he shouted. "I found Grandma's prettiness!"

He nearly broke his neck descending. Grandpa waited below, trembling
and calling unnecessary instructions. When Jerry placed the pin in
his hand, the old man sat suddenly upon the grass.

"Thank the Lord," he whispered.

Grandma kissed Jerry until he squirmed.

"I never stole it," he protested. "The bird done it."

"We know, lamb."

"My nose itches turrible when folks kisses me."

"Then it can itch," she declared, and kissed him again.

The pin had returned. Her sight did not.

For several years the world had been drawing a veil
across Grandma's eyes. At first there were floating specks, then
a mist over faces, then a dimming around the edges
of each day. She concealed it as long as she
could. She knew the house so intimately that her hands
went unerringly to the flour bin, the kettle, the clock
shelf, and the basket of darning. She measured steps without
appearing to do so. When she poured tea, she rested
a finger inside the cup and stopped when warmth touched
it.

Grandpa suspected. Love is observant, though it may be slow
to confess what it fears.

One morning he brought in a burdock burr and set
it upon the white tablecloth near her elbow.

"Mother, what's that?"

She turned her face toward it.

"What's what?"

"That bug."

She drew back. "Where?"

"Right there—a great black creepin' thing."

Grandma peered, blinking.

"Ezra Green, you take it away!"

He did not laugh. He picked up the burr and
went behind the woodshed, where no one could see him
weep.

Soon afterward he drove her to the doctor in town.
Dr. Ames had spectacles, clean cuffs, and a voice accustomed
to pronouncing verdicts. He examined her eyes while Ezra watched
every motion.

"There has been a progressive degeneration," he said at last.
"At her age, we must recognize the operation of natural
law. No treatment can restore the optic function."

Grandpa leaned forward.

"Speak plain."

"She will be blind. Entirely blind, I fear. Nothing can
be done."

"Nothin'?"

"I am sorry."

"There's doctors in Boston."

"They could do no more."

"There's operations."

"Not for this."

Grandpa struck his cane upon the floor.

"Then there's miracles."

Dr. Ames, who was kind despite his solemn language, looked
away. "We do not build medical hope upon miracles, Mr.
Green."

"Mebbe you don't."

All the way home Grandpa sat stiffly in the wagon.
Grandma's hand rested in his, as it had under the
elm long ago. The road shone pale before them; maples
flamed scarlet along the hills; a hawk sailed through immeasurable
blue. She looked as if she would gather every color
into her heart before the darkness came.

That night Grandpa went to the barn. He knelt in
the hay and prayed with the fierce passion of a
man bargaining for the life of his beloved.

"Lord, she's served Ye all her days. She's took what
Ye sent—the baby, the fire, sickness, graves, hard winters—an' never
complained. Don't take the light too. Take mine instead. I
don't need to see. But her—she loves flowers, Lord. She
loves faces. Give her a miracle. Ye raised the dead.
Ye opened blind eyes. Do it again!"

He remained there until the lantern burned low.

In the bedroom Grandma prayed differently.

"O Father, Ezra can't bear it yet. Comfort him. If
I'm to walk in darkness, walk with me. If my
eyes can't be healed, heal the fear in my heart.
Make me useful still. Don't let me grow bitter. An'
when I forget the color of things, let the children
tell me."

Her sight faded through the winter. The last thing she
remembered seeing clearly was not a sunrise or rose, though
afterward people expected her to say so. It was Grandpa's
hand upon the coverlet—knotted, brown-spotted, scarred across the thumb, the
hand that had built a coffin and two houses, planted
orchards, held babies, dug graves, and fastened a topaz pin
to her dress. One morning that hand became only a
pale shape. Then even the shape was gone.

Grandpa could not forgive heaven immediately.

"He asked fer a miracle," Grandma told Martin. "He thinks
the Lord said no."

"Didn't He?"

Grandma turned her blind eyes toward the window, where sparrows
quarreled in the bare lilac bush.

"Not every miracle's a door openin', son. Sometimes it's strength
to stand afore a door that stays shut."

Martin considered this for many days.

He had his own decision to make. His father was
frail; his mother could no longer live alone when Grandpa
went to the distant fields or fell ill. So Martin
rented his smaller place and moved with his wife, Anna,
and their four children into the farmhouse.

The house, which had grown quiet, filled at once with
boots, books, dolls, crumbs, slammed doors, laughter, disputes, and song.
Grandpa complained that he could not hear himself think.

"You never did much of that," Grandma said.

He smiled for the first time in weeks.

At first the children were solemn around her blindness. They
watched her hands move over the table and lowered their
voices when she entered, as if darkness had made her
sacred and sorrowful. Grandma soon cured them.

"Ellen, what color ribbon've ye got?"

"Blue."

"What kind o' blue?"

"Just blue."

"There ain't any 'just blue.' Is it robin's-egg, cornflower, storm-cloud,
or the blue at the edge of a winter sunset?"

Ellen stared at the ribbon. "It's—well—it's like the sky after
rain, only darker."

"That's better. Now I can see it."

Thereafter they became her eyes. Jerry described the first snow
as "flour sifted over everything, only sparklier." Little May told
her that the moon was "a clipped fingernail in the
dark." Ellen read the almanac, the Bible, and Martin's weekly
newspaper, stumbling over political speeches but rendering advertisements with great
expression. Even Peter, who preferred the barn, brought her treasures
to touch: a warm egg, a horse chestnut, a shed
snakeskin, the velvet nose of a newborn calf.

Grandma taught them in return. Her fingers could still distinguish
good stitches from careless ones. She showed Ellen how to
hem, guided May in braiding rugs, and taught Jerry to
shell peas without scattering them.

She also knew stories no book contained: how the river
had frozen solid in '61; how Grandpa once courted her
in a borrowed coat whose sleeves were too short; how
a runaway mare carried them three miles before overturning the
sleigh in a drift.

"Was Grandpa scared?" Jerry asked.

"Scared? He landed headfirst with his feet wagglin' in the
air."

"I was seein' if you was hurt," Grandpa protested from
the stove.

"With your feet?"

"Best I could."

The children shouted. Their laughter warmed him, though he pretended
otherwise.

Grandma moved through the house with one hand upon familiar
furniture and the other sometimes resting on a child's shoulder.
Whoever guided her took pride in the office. They warned
her of the threshold, placed her fingers on the back
of a chair, and told her when sunlight lay across
her path.

"Here's a warm patch, Grandma," May would say.

Then Grandma paused, lifted her face, and received the sun's
beneficence as gratefully as one receives a blessing.

She no longer saw the topaz pin, yet she wore
it every Sunday. Grandpa fastened it for her. His fingers
had become clumsy, and sometimes the point pricked him.

"Did I stick ye?" she asked.

"No."

"Ezra."

"Mebbe a mite."

She would capture his hand and kiss the injured finger,
both of them laughing tremulously at the old ritual of
care. To the children, the pin looked merely old-fashioned. But
when Grandma let them touch it and told its history,
it ceased to be jewelry. It became the wall built
by lantern light, tobacco renounced, the baby's candle, the burned
house, the crow's nest, and Grandpa's lifelong wish that she
should possess "suthin' pretty." The children handled it as reverently
as a relic.

The years continued their quiet taking. Grandpa walked less and
sat more. On summer afternoons he and Grandma rested beneath
the orchard tree that had once borne the joined apples.
She could not see the leaves, but she heard their
multitudinous whisper; she could not see the moths hovering over
clover, but May described them; she could not see Grandpa
beside her, but her hand found his.

"Remember the elm?" he asked once.

"Allers."

"Remember what ye said?"

"I've said a heap o' foolish things."

"Ye said I was enough."

"So ye were."

"Ben a long time."

"Not long."

"No?"

She leaned her head against his shoulder. "Not when I
remember it."

Memory had become her inner daylight. Within it the lost
child slept peacefully, the old farmhouse stood unburned, and young
Ezra crossed the meadow beneath a topaz sky. Yet she
did not live only backward. That was the wonder. The
children drew her continually toward tomorrow: toward Ellen's examination, Jerry's
injured pigeon, Peter's calf, May's secret birthday gift. Their needs
kept her from becoming imprisoned in recollection.

They brought her troubles that seemed small to adults but
were enormous to them. Grandma never laughed at a sorrow.
She knew that the heart measures grief not by the
world's scales but by its own capacity. A broken doll,
a friend's betrayal, a dead bird, fear of thunder—she received
each confession with grave commiseration, laying a soft hand upon
a bowed head.

"Dark don't last forever," she told them. "An' while it
lasts, there's hands to hold."

On winter evenings the family gathered around her. Anna sewed,
Martin mended harness, Grandpa dozed, and the children clustered at
Grandma's knees. Sometimes she prayed aloud. Her prayers were not
eloquent. She thanked God for bread, the roof, Martin's harvest,
Anna's patience, Grandpa's cough being easier, and "all these young
ones, even when they're noisy."

"Especially then," Anna added.

"Especially then," Grandma agreed.

Her blindness, which had seemed at first a wall dividing
her from life, became strangely a doorway through which each
member of the household approached her. They told her what
they saw, and in telling, saw it better. They offered
arms, and learned tenderness. They read aloud, and heard wisdom
in words they might otherwise have hurried over. They came
to her in grief and departed with courage.

Dr. Ames had been right concerning natural law. Her eyes
were never restored. Grandpa's demanded miracle did not come in
the form he desired.

But another came quietly.

It came in Jerry describing frost like feathers upon the
pump; in Ellen placing the first rose of June against
Grandma's cheek; in Martin pausing at the kitchen door because
his mother's hymn made the burden of the day seem
lighter. It came in Grandpa's hand seeking hers after sixty
years and finding it without error. It came whenever darkness,
instead of emptying her world, filled the house with loving
voices.

Grandma could see nothing.

And she was the light of them all.

### The Last Knight of the Rose

In the last winter of King Almeric's quarrel with the
lords of the western marches, when every baron kept his
own peace and broke his neighbour's, an old knight rode
alone beneath the wooded slopes of Montfaucon. The day was
near its ending. Snow lay in the hollows, but the
road itself was brown and soft, and the naked branches
held the sunset as though each twig had been dipped
in crimson fire.

The knight's name was Sir Aymar of Vey. His horse
was grey about the muzzle, as he was grey about
the beard, and his armour, once burnished like a mirror,
had been fretted by rain and salt until it seemed
the iron bark of some ancient tree. Only the clasp
of his cloak had kept its brightness. It was wrought
in the likeness of a rose, and in its heart
there burned a single ruby, blood-coloured and clear.

Sir Aymar rode with bowed head, not from weariness, though
he had travelled far, but from an old sorrow that
had grown lighter in no season. He had crossed twelve
countries and served seven princes, had watched cities burn, monasteries
rise, kings fall sick and beggars become captains; yet the
thing he sought had continually fled before him. For some
quests lead men over the edge of the world, and
others lead them only round and round their own desire.

As he came where the road divided, he heard shouting
among the trees. Presently five peasants burst out upon the
lower path, running with cudgels and wood-axes, and behind them
came a long-legged lad in a leather jerkin, who ran
and slipped, and climbed and ran, and shouted more loudly
than all the rest.

"Stay, good fellows," called Sir Aymar. "Whither go ye armed
in such haste?"

The foremost peasant, a broad man with a red cap,
halted unwillingly. "After thieves, lord."

"What have they stolen?"

"Two pigs."

Sir Aymar raised his head.

The peasant added, lest the cause appear too small, "They
are Father Odon's pigs, lord. He is old and pious,
and they were the better part of his winter meat."

"Then yours is a just pursuit."

"It may be just," said another, breathing hard, "but there
be sixteen thieves, or twenty, and some have bows."

"Twenty-three," said the lad. "I saw them from the hazels,
unless two were the same man, which may be, for
they all wore brown."

The men looked back along the path. Justice is a
fair companion while she walks beside a larger company; but
when she goes outnumbered into a wood, many honest men
remember their wives.

"Are these thieves under any lord's protection?" asked Sir Aymar.

"They call themselves men of Baron Gaultier," said Red Cap,
"but Baron Gaultier calls them woodcutters, and the woodcutters call
themselves hungry. It changes according to who asks."

"And Father Odon?"

"He belongs to Saint Ruel's abbey."

"Then the cause is not entangled in feudal doubt," said
the knight gravely. "It is well."

The peasants did not see what was well in it.

"We had thought," Red Cap said, "to follow until we
knew where they lodged, and then return with the reeve
and thirty men."

"By then the pigs will be eaten."

"A pig once eaten is proof of theft," observed a
thin peasant.

"And a pig recovered is breakfast," said Sir Aymar. "Which
would Father Odon prefer?"

They considered this, and the lad answered, "Breakfast."

The old knight looked from one face to another. "Hear
me. For every thief slain in the taking of these
beasts, I shall pay one silver crown. If any man
be wounded, he shall have two; and if any man
fall, his household shall receive ten."

At the word silver their caution wrestled visibly with arithmetic.

"Have you so much?" asked Red Cap.

Sir Aymar struck the purse beneath his cloak, and it
answered with a weighty chink. "Enough."

The thin man crossed himself. "It is tempting Providence."

"Nay," said the knight. "Providence cannot be tempted with a
crown. Men can."

Thereat they agreed, though each advised the others to take
the greater danger. Sir Aymar dismounted and examined the earth.
In the clay were the cloven prints of the pigs,
confused with the heels of many men; here a broken
switch showed where one beast had been beaten, and there
a thread of coarse rope clung to a thorn. The
trail passed upward into the forest.

They followed it beneath oaks black with winter, through bracken
dead and brittle, and over a stony stream where the
thieves had sought to hide their passage. Yet a pig
is no subtle traveller. On the farther bank the mud
was churned, and beyond it lay a little blood where
a split hoof had struck a flint.

By the time they reached the ridge, the sun had
sunk behind the hills, and the clouds stood above it
like gates of burning copper. Sir Aymar's ruby rose caught
that light and returned it. The lad stared at the
jewel until he stumbled into Red Cap.

"Is it enchanted?" he whispered.

"All costly things are enchanted to fools," said the peasant.

Sir Aymar heard but did not rebuke him.

Below the ridge was a hollow where charcoal-burners had once
made their fires. The thieves had camped among the black
mounds, believing themselves safe. One pig was tied to a
beech and rooted furiously among its leaves; the other had
escaped its cord and was being pursued by three men,
to the delight of the rest. Their bows leaned against
a fallen trunk. A pot steamed over the fire.

"There are nineteen," murmured Red Cap.

"Eighteen," said the thin man.

"Twenty-three," insisted the lad. "Some are behind the pig."

The peasants began to withdraw.

Sir Aymar spoke softly. "If we charge from the ridge,
they will think many follow. Shout the names of saints,
kings, cousins, and all men whom ye would have beside
you. Strike swiftly, and hold close to me."

"Would it not be wiser to wait for darkness?" asked
one.

"It would be safer."

"Then—"

"But not wiser."

He drew his sword. Its blade was notched, its guard
plain, and upon its pommel was engraved the same rose
he bore upon his cloak. When it came free of
the scabbard, the sound was very small; yet the peasants
afterward remembered it better than their own shouting.

Sir Aymar went down the slope.

He did not run at first, but walked with such
certainty that the men behind him were ashamed to remain
where they stood. Then Red Cap roared, "Saint Ruel and
King Almeric!" though Saint Ruel had never approved of fighting
and King Almeric had never heard his name. The others
cried for Saint Martin, Saint Giles, the abbey, the reeve,
and various dead uncles. The lad shouted, "Twenty-three silver crowns!"

The thieves sprang up in confusion. One reached a bow
and loosed too quickly; the arrow passed over Sir Aymar
and struck a cooking pan, which flew into the fire.
Then the knight was among them.

He struck the bowman down, turned a wood-axe upon his
shield, and with the same motion drove its owner backward
into the embers. The peasants came after him in a
knot, flailing with clubs and crying out numbers that increased
as their courage rose. Two thieves fled at once. Three
others, supposing armed men were still descending the ridge, fled
after them.

But the chief of the band was a great fellow
in a wolfskin coat. He rallied his companions and came
at Sir Aymar with a two-handed bill. The blow split
the knight's shield from rim to boss and cut deep
into his shoulder. Sir Aymar staggered, yet before the bill
could rise again his sword entered beneath the wolfskin and
the great man fell.

Now the arrows began to buzz.

"Behind me," said Sir Aymar.

He stood before the peasants with his ruined shield, and
the shafts beat upon it like winter rain. One pierced
his thigh, one broke against his breastplate, and one passed
through the mail beneath his arm. Yet the peasants, sheltered
for that moment, rushed upon the bowmen. Red Cap felled
one with his cudgel; the thin man, who had spoken
most prudently, fought with such terror that no one could
stand near him. The lad threw stones, missing friend and
foe with perfect impartiality.

The battle was brief, as most battles are to those
who die in them. Seven thieves lay still among the
charcoal pits, four crawled into the brush, and the rest
cast down their weapons or vanished into the forest. The
two pigs, alarmed by mankind, escaped together up the slope.

"After them!" cried Sir Aymar.

The peasants obeyed more readily now. They caught the beasts
by their ropes and dragged them back, squealing, biting and
wholly ungrateful for deliverance.

Then Red Cap saw the blood running from the old
knight's armour.

"Lord, you are hurt."

"It is no great matter."

But when Sir Aymar tried to step forward, his wounded
leg failed, and he knelt beside the dead fire. The
peasants crowded about him, speaking at once of bandages, litters,
the nearness of night, and the possibility that the thieves
would return with Baron Gaultier's men.

"Take the pigs to Father Odon," said Sir Aymar. "The
promise is fulfilled."

"And you?"

"I shall follow when I have rested."

They knew this to be untrue, but the darkness gathered
in the trees, and fear is a shepherd who drives
hard. They bound his shoulder with a strip of cloak,
propped him against the fallen trunk, and promised to send
help from the village. Then they went away with the
pigs, hurrying and stumbling down the path, and presently only
their voices could be heard, and then only the squealing
of the beasts, and then nothing.

The lad had gone with them.

An hour later he returned.

He came into the hollow carrying a lantern, a sack,
a loaf, and a small black cock tucked beneath one
arm.

"I thought you had fled," said Sir Aymar.

"I did," replied the lad. "But I remembered the crowns.
Also this cock was in the thieves' camp, and he
has a strong breast. I think he will fight."

"What is your name?"

"Colin, though my mother calls me Col when she is
pleased and Brainless when she is not. There are seven
dead thieves. I counted twice. One has red hair, but
that still makes seven."

Sir Aymar smiled faintly. "My purse is here. Take seven
crowns."

Colin set down the cock, which immediately pecked at a
dead man's boot, and opened the purse. He counted the
coins aloud, then counted them again, lest holiness have altered
their number.

"Shall I take for the men who ran away?"

"Did you slay them?"

"No."

"Then no."

"What of the one who may die before morning?"

"If he dies of wounds received here, the crown is
earned."

Colin nodded, satisfied by this theology. Then, seeing the knight's
face, he put aside the coins and made up the
fire. He brought water from the stream in the cooking
pot, washed the wounds as gently as his clumsy hands
allowed, and covered Sir Aymar with the wolfskin coat.

"You will die," he said.

"Yes."

"The villagers will come."

"No."

"They said they would."

"They are good men. But good men are often delayed
by fear until goodness can be performed safely."

Colin broke the loaf and gave the softer half to
the knight. "Why did you do it? Father Odon's pigs
are lean, and one bites. Seven men are dead, maybe
eight, and you also. It seems a high price for
bad pork."

Sir Aymar looked into the fire. Above them the clouds
had parted, and the stars shone between the branches; but
on his breast the ruby rose shone more warmly, as
if a small sunset had lodged there and would not
depart.

"When I was young," he said, "I entered the Fellowship
of the Rose. Hast thou heard of it?"

"Was it an army?"

"No."

"A band of robbers?"

"No."

"Then I have not."

"There were never many of us. We served no earthly
prince, though we kept faith with those whose bread we
ate. We were vowed to the Divine Rose, which blooms
unseen at the heart of the world. Its roots are
watered by mercy, its thorns are justice, and its flower
is the love of the Most High."

Colin glanced at the jeweled clasp. "Is that the same
rose?"

"It is a sign only. Men need signs because their
inward sight is dim. We believed that the Powers of
Corruption work wherever the weak are devoured, truth is sold,
or innocence mocked; and where a just cause stood in
peril, there one of us must stand also."

"Even for pigs?"

"Even for pigs. The measure of a cause is not
the price of what is stolen, but the wrong done
to him from whom it is taken."

Colin considered this and gave the cock a crumb.

Sir Aymar continued: "There was another part to our vow.
We desired a worthy death, but were forbidden to seek
death for its own sake. A man who throws away
his life to prove his courage serves pride, not Heaven.
Therefore we must enter the good cause wholly, neither courting
the sword nor fleeing it, and leave the hour and
manner to God."

"Did the others die so?"

"One held a bridge while children crossed from a burning
town. One defended a leper-house when soldiers came to plunder
it. One died in a dungeon because he would not
accuse an innocent woman. My master, Sir Bérenger, was slain
between two feuding lords after he had made them swear
peace. Each received the end appointed to him."

"And you did not?"

"For fifty years I sought to be useful. I fought
in wars that seemed righteous at dawn and wicked by
supper. I guarded princes who became tyrants and opposed rebels
who became saints. I crossed the sea to defend pilgrims,
but found merchants quarrelling over their baggage. I rescued a
maiden who did not wish to be rescued, and aided
a hermit who had stolen the cave in which he
prayed. Always there was vanity mixed with justice, and greed
mixed with mercy. Behold, the world is muddy clay, and
no man walks through it with clean feet."

Colin looked at Sir Aymar's boots. "Yours are very muddy."

"So they are."

"Then why not stay at home?"

"Because the road remained."

The old knight's voice had weakened, but his face was
no longer sorrowful. "In these latter years I feared that
I had been forgotten. The Fellowship is gone, its halls
broken, its banners eaten by moths. I am the last
rose upon a dead branch. I thought I should perish
in bed, among cups and blankets, while some kind priest
invented virtue for me. Yet tonight there were two stolen
pigs, and frightened men, and thieves stronger than they; and
for once the cause was small enough to be pure."

"Pigs are often pure after rain," said Colin.

Sir Aymar laughed, and the laugh became a cough. Blood
darkened his beard. Colin held the cup to his lips.

"Will the Divine Rose come for you?" the lad asked.

"It has been coming all my life."

"Will I see it?"

"Perhaps. Innocents behold much, though they understand little; wise men
understand much, though they behold nothing."

The fire sank. The cock settled beneath the fallen trunk,
tucking its head under its wing. Far off, a wolf
called across the snowy hills, and another answered.

Sir Aymar lifted his eyes toward the stars and began
to sing. It was an old hymn of the Fellowship,
and his voice was broken, yet the tune moved through
the trees with a grave sweetness:

"Rose beyond the mortal garden,
Rose no winter maketh pale,
Take the blood the thorn hath gathered,
Lift the heart beyond the veil."

At the final word, the ruby upon his breast flashed
once in the firelight. Then his head inclined, as if
he had heard a command and bowed before obeying it.

Colin waited.

After a while he touched the knight's hand and found
it cold. He sat beside him until the moon passed
over the hollow, thinking of battles, silver crowns, enchanted roses,
and whether a cock fed upon a dead thief's crumbs
would become fiercer.

Near midnight he began to dig a grave.

The earth was hard beneath the leaves, and his tool
was only a thief's short-handled spade. Nevertheless he dug and
rested, and rested and dug; and by dawn he had
made a trench nearly long enough, though somewhat narrow at
the shoulders. Sir Aymar lay beside it wrapped in the
wolfskin, his rusted sword upon his breast and his face
turned toward the paling east.

Then from the valley came the crowing of a cock.

Colin stopped.

Again the challenge sounded, bold, clear and insolent. The black
cock beneath the trunk woke and answered furiously.

Colin listened with shining eyes. By the voice alone he
judged the unseen bird to be large, deep-chested and young.
He set down the spade, snatched up his own cock,
and ran toward the slope.

He ran and slid, and climbed and ran, following the
cries through the whitening woods until both boy and bird
were lost among the trees.

Behind him the sun rose crimson over Montfaucon. It kindled
the clouds, the broken shield, the old blood upon the
leaves, and the ruby rose upon Sir Aymar's breast. The
grave waited open beside him, lacking no more than half
an hour's labour; but the knight did not complain, for
his quest was ended, and the concerns of the living
had already begun again.

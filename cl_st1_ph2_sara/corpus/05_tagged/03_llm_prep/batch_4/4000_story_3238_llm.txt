# The Roly-Poly Kitten

Once upon a time there was an old, old house
among the hills, and in that house lived Mrs. Tabitha
Twitchit with her three kittens—Mittens, Moppet, and Tom.

The house had thick stone walls, crooked chimneys, little staircases,
large cupboards, and more doors than Mrs. Tabitha could remember.
Some doors opened into bedrooms, some into closets, and one
opened upon nothing but a blank wall.

There were noises behind the wainscot at night.

Scratch—scratch—patter—patter!

Mrs. Tabitha said it was the wind. But the kittens
knew better.

One morning Mrs. Tabitha rose very early, for it was
baking day. She tied on a clean apron and stirred
the fire. She set out a flour barrel, a yellow
bowl of butter, three dough pans, and a rolling-pin.

"Now listen to me!" she said. "I will have no
paws in the flour, no tails near the fire, and
no kittens tumbling into my dough. You must sit quietly
until Aunt Ribby arrives."

Moppet immediately put both paws into the flour.

Mittens climbed into an empty milk pan.

Tom disappeared.

Mrs. Tabitha became more and more distracted.

She washed Moppet. She lifted Mittens out of the milk
pan. Then she called for Tom.

"Tom! Tom Kitten! Where are you?"

There was no answer.

She looked under the dresser, behind the flour barrel, inside
the tall clock, and beneath the counterpane upstairs. She even
looked into the teapot, although Tom could not possibly have
fitted into it.

Meanwhile Tom was in the best hiding place he had
ever discovered.

He had climbed upon the kitchen hob, scrambled behind the
iron fireback, and squeezed into a hole beside the chimney.
It was very black and wonderfully secret.

Tom heard his mother calling.

He did not answer.

He heard her open the cupboard.

"I shall put you two troublesome kittens where I can
find you," said Mrs. Tabitha.

She shut Moppet and Mittens inside the pantry cupboard, where
there were jars of currants, a basket of onions, three
folded dust sheets, and a little round hole beside the
skirting-board.

"Stay there!"

"Yes, Mother," said Moppet and Mittens.

Mrs. Tabitha went away to search the bedrooms.

Moppet looked at Mittens.

Mittens looked at Moppet.

Then both looked at the little round hole.

It was quite large enough for kittens who breathed in
and squeezed.

They wriggled through it.

Behind the cupboard was a narrow passage between the plaster
and the stones. It smelt of dust, cheese rinds, and
mice. Moppet and Mittens crept along it until they came
out beneath the back stairs.

"There!" whispered Moppet. "Mother will never find us."

At that very moment a grey rat, wearing a blue
apron, ran past with a lump of butter in her
arms.

Mittens squeaked.

The rat stopped and showed her yellow teeth.

"Good morning," she said. "Kindly do not obstruct the passage."

Then she hurried away.

A second rat appeared. He was a large, thin gentleman
in a shabby brown coat. He carried a piece of
dough over one shoulder and dragged Mrs. Tabitha's rolling-pin behind
him.

Bump—bump—bump went the rolling-pin over the boards.

The kittens fled from beneath the stairs and rushed into
the kitchen.

Mrs. Tabitha had just returned from looking up the linen
chimney.

"Mother! Mother!" cried Moppet.

"An enormous rat has stolen the butter!" cried Mittens.

"And another has taken the dough!"

"And the rolling-pin!"

"They went under the stairs!"

Mrs. Tabitha sat down heavily upon a flour tub.

"My butter! My dough! My rolling-pin! And where is Tom?"

"We do not know," said the kittens.

Mrs. Tabitha wrung her paws.

At that moment there came a knock at the door,
and in walked Ribby, the neighbor, carrying a clean napkin
over one arm.

Ribby was a stout, sensible cat with a striped gown
and a firm manner.

"I have come to assist with the baking," she said.
"Why is the kitchen full of soot, and why are
you sitting on the flour?"

"Tom is lost! The butter has been stolen! Rats are
dragging away the dough! Oh, Ribby, this house will be
the death of me!"

"Nonsense," said Ribby. "Sit ye down and collect yourself. We
shall search properly."

Ribby examined the hole beside the fireback. There were sooty
paw marks upon the bricks.

"Tom went up the chimney," she said.

"My poor child!"

"Your disobedient child," said Ribby. "Fetch a candle."

They searched the kitchen chimney, the bedroom chimney, and the
chimney in the spare room. They opened cupboard doors and
tapped upon the wainscot. Ribby climbed upon a chair and
listened behind the plaster.

Nothing could be heard except a faint pattering far inside
the walls.

Then came another sound.

Roly—poly—roly—poly—

It seemed to come from over their heads.

Mrs. Tabitha and Ribby ran upstairs.

The noise stopped.

They went into the attic, which was a stuffy, fusty
room crowded with trunks, broken chairs, picture frames, and an
ancient cradle with only three legs. Dust sheets hung from
the rafters like ghosts.

Ribby put her ear against the floor.

Roly—poly—roly—poly—

"There!" cried Mrs. Tabitha. "Someone is using my rolling-pin beneath
the boards!"

"That is exceedingly inconvenient," said Ribby.

While the cats searched the house, Tom had been going
up, and up, and up inside the chimney.

At first he enjoyed himself. He could see thin threads
of daylight above him, and his claws held well upon
the rough bricks. But the chimney soon divided into three
flues.

Tom chose the narrowest.

This was a mistake.

He crawled sideways, slipped downward, caught his jacket upon a
rusty nail, and sneezed seven times because of the soot.
When he tried to go back, he could not remember
which way he had come.

The passage grew smaller and darker.

At last the bricks ended, and Tom tumbled through a
broken place in the lath and plaster.

He landed in a heap of feathers upon the floor
of a hidden room.

It was a very peculiar room. It lay between the
attic floor and the ceiling below, where no proper room
ought to have been. There was a little iron stove,
a table made from a cigar box, several cracked cups,
and a bed covered with a red pocket handkerchief.

Strings of onions hung from the rafters. Six stolen teaspoons
stood in a jar. Mrs. Tabitha's blue egg-cup was upon
the mantelpiece.

Tom was still staring when the grey rat in the
blue apron came in with the butter.

She dropped it.

"A kitten!" she exclaimed.

Tom tried to scramble back into the chimney, but the
large gentleman rat appeared behind him with the dough and
rolling-pin.

"Stand still, young person," said the rat. "Your behavior is
creating unnecessary confusion."

Tom spat.

The rat caught him by the tail.

"Samuel Whiskers!" cried the grey rat. "Do not allow him
to scratch the furniture!"

"My dear Anna Maria," said Samuel, "I am entirely occupied
in preventing him from scratching me."

Tom bit and spat, and mewed and wriggled.

But Anna Maria fetched a length of string. Between them
the rats tied Tom's paws and wrapped the string around
his middle.

"Too much string," said Samuel Whiskers. "String is indigestible."

"It may be removed before cooking," said Anna Maria.

"That creates additional labor."

"You always object when I am trying to be practical."

"I merely observe that sound housekeeping requires foresight."

Tom did not like this conversation at all.

"What are you going to do?" he cried.

Anna Maria pinched his side thoughtfully.

"He is young," she said. "A little sooty, perhaps."

"Nothing that butter will not remedy," replied Samuel.

They laid Tom upon the table.

Anna Maria rubbed his jacket, waistcoat, whiskers, and tail with
butter. It was cold and sticky, and it made the
soot run into greasy streaks.

Tom mewed very loudly.

"Do not make that noise," said Samuel Whiskers. "It is
most trying before luncheon."

Then they spread out the stolen dough and placed Tom
in the middle of it.

"A roly-poly pudding," said Anna Maria.

"A dumpling," said Samuel.

"A roly-poly."

"A dumpling, whatever you may urge to the contrary."

Anna Maria folded the dough over Tom's sides.

"You have forgotten the currants."

"There are no currants."

"There are currants in the pantry cupboard."

"There were kittens in the pantry cupboard."

"They have gone."

"That is not the point. One cannot conduct a household
if ingredients are never where they are expected."

Tom kicked as hard as he could. The dough split
at one end, and his tail popped out.

Anna Maria pushed it back.

"You must roll him evenly."

Samuel took the rolling-pin.

Roly—poly—roly—poly—

The rolling-pin went over Tom's buttered jacket.

He was becoming flatter and flatter.

Up in the attic, Mrs. Tabitha gave a dreadful cry.

"That is directly under my paws!"

Ribby scratched at the floorboards. They were broad oak boards
and very firmly nailed.

"We require John Joiner," she said. "At once."

John Joiner was a large dog who repaired gates, cupboards,
hen-coops, and anything else that could be persuaded to remain
still while he hammered it.

Moppet and Mittens were sent to fetch him.

They ran down the lane, through the vegetable garden, over
a stile, and into John's workshop. He was sharpening a
saw.

"Please come!" cried Moppet. "There are rats under the attic!"

"They have Tom!" cried Mittens.

"And Mother's rolling-pin!"

John Joiner picked up his tool basket.

"Rats under a floor are bad," he said. "A rolling-pin
under a floor is worse. Show me."

He came pounding into the old house, up the stairs,
and into the attic. He sniffed along the cracks.

The hairs rose upon his back.

"Here," he said.

He pressed his nose to a knot-hole and gave one
tremendous snort.

Below, in the hidden room, flour flew off the table.

"What was that?" asked Anna Maria.

"Atmospheric disturbance," said Samuel.

Then John began to scratch.

Scrape—SCRATCH—scrabble—SCRATCH!

Dust showered from the ceiling. A cup fell from the
mantelpiece. The onions swung upon their string.

Samuel Whiskers dropped the rolling-pin.

"That," he said, "is not atmospheric."

John set the saw against the floor.

Zizz—zizz—ZIZZ!

The teeth bit into the oak. The whole ceiling trembled.

Anna Maria ran to the cupboard and began stuffing spoons
into a sack.

"Take the butter!"

"We have used the butter."

"Then take the egg-cup!"

"It is chipped."

"It is not ours!"

"My dear Anna Maria, this is hardly the occasion for
a discussion of ownership."

They dragged open a tiny door behind the stove. Samuel
pushed the remaining dough into a wheelbarrow, and Anna Maria
climbed after it with the spoons, onions, egg-cup, and one
of Mrs. Tabitha's best napkins.

"What about the kitten?" she asked.

"He is insufficiently rolled," said Samuel.

"Then we cannot cook him."

"Precisely."

"Such a waste!"

"Kindly refrain from criticism while I am escaping."

Samuel seized the handles of the wheelbarrow. They vanished into
a tunnel just as the point of John's saw broke
through the ceiling.

Zizz—CRACK!

John lifted one floorboard. Ribby lifted another.

A smell of soot, butter, onions, and frightened kitten came
out of the hole.

Mrs. Tabitha peered down.

There was Tom upon the table, tied with string and
wrapped in dough. Only the tip of one ear was
visible.

"My child!"

"Mmf!" said Tom.

John Joiner climbed down and cut the string with his
pocketknife. Ribby peeled away the dough. It came off in
heavy grey pieces, all speckled with soot and bits of
red thread from Tom's jacket.

Tom was alive, but he was extremely sticky and almost
too ashamed to look at anybody.

Mrs. Tabitha carried him downstairs.

He was bathed once in warm water.

Then he was bathed again with soap.

Then Ribby said that he still smelt of butter, so
he was bathed a third time.

Tom was put to bed beneath a clean counterpane with
a hot-water bottle and a saucer of warm milk. Moppet
and Mittens sat beside him.

"Were the rats going to eat you?" whispered Moppet.

Tom pulled the counterpane over his head.

"Were you truly a pudding?" asked Mittens.

Tom would not answer.

Downstairs, Mrs. Tabitha looked sadly at the ruined dough.

"It cannot be wasted," said Ribby.

So they picked out the string, added fresh flour, and
put in a generous handful of currants to hide the
smuts. The pudding was baked separately in a small dish.

John Joiner would not taste it.

He repaired the attic floor and blocked the worst of
the holes behind the skirting-boards. But the old house had
thick walls, seven chimneys, and passages that led no one
knew where. Even John could not stop every opening.

Samuel Whiskers and Anna Maria pushed the stolen wheelbarrow through
the walls, down a drain, and across the lane after
dark.

They went to live in Farmer Potatoes's barn.

It was a comfortable barn, with a warm haymow, sacks
of oats, bins of barley, and a loose board behind
the harness room. Samuel and Anna Maria found it very
suitable.

They had children, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, and great-great-grandchildren.

There was no end to them.

Farmer Potatoes complained that his grain disappeared, his leather straps
were gnawed, and somebody rolled onions down the loft stairs
at midnight. He set traps, but Samuel Whiskers was a
rat of education and took the cheese without disturbing the
springs.

In time Moppet and Mittens grew into fine cats. They
had learned that little holes should be watched, not entered,
and that a silent paw is more useful than a
noisy curiosity.

They became celebrated rat-catchers.

Farmer Potatoes employed them regularly, and they sat for hours
beside the bins with their tails curled neatly around their
feet. They caught many of Samuel's descendants—though Samuel and Anna
Maria themselves were always said to be just around the
next corner, arguing about butter.

As for Tom, he recovered from the soot, the string,
the butter, and the dough.

But he never again climbed an unfamiliar chimney.

This shows how unwise it is to crawl into a
dark place merely because one's mother has said not to
do so.

I myself saw the rats' wheelbarrow some weeks afterward when
I was walking to the post. It stood upside down
beside Farmer Potatoes's ditch, with one handle broken and flour
upon the wheel.

I recognized it immediately.

It had been borrowed from my own potting shed without
permission.

John Joiner mended it, but it has never rolled straight
since.

Tom grew into a large cat with an excellent coat
and very respectable whiskers. Yet he would not go into
Farmer Potatoes's barn, and he avoided every sound that resembled—

Roly—poly—roly—poly.

He could face beetles, moths, and small mice.

But he remained afraid of anything bigger than—

A Mouse.

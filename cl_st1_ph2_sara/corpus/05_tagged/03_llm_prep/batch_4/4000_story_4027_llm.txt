# The Hunger for Light

Before daylight had loosened the black knot of Hester Street,
Shenah Pessah was already on her knees.

The hallway stretched before her like the throat of a
chimney—narrow, airless, breathing out the smells of yesterday's cabbage, damp
plaster, kerosene, herring, babies, and old shoes. Somewhere behind one
of the doors a man coughed as though his lungs
were tearing loose. Somewhere a child whimpered and was hushed.
From the street came the iron rattle of a milk
wagon passing over the stones.

Shenah pushed her rag along the floor.

Scrub—wring—scrub.

Her hands were red and swollen from the lye. Two
fingers had cracked open at the knuckles, and the dirty
water stung them with each plunge into the pail. Above
her, on the third-floor landing, Uncle Mordecai's door opened.

"Still by the second floor?" he called down. "A whole
morning to wash one hallway! For this I brought you
to America?"

His slippers slapped against the boards as he descended. He
wore his undershirt and trousers, the suspenders hanging at his
hips. In the gray light his beard looked full of
ashes.

"I started before five," Shenah said without rising.

"Started! She tells me started. The dirt cares when you
started? The tenants pay for clean halls, not for your
stories."

"The tenants pay you."

"And who gives you a bed? Who gives you bread?"

Bread! A heel hard as the table, dipped into weak
tea. A spoonful of potatoes when Aunt Gittel remembered there
was another mouth in the room. Bread—he named it like
a banquet.

Shenah bent lower and drove the rag against a black
mark on the floor. Her loosened hair fell across her
cheek.

"Faster," said Uncle Mordecai. "After the halls comes the yard.
Then the ashes. And Gittel says the stove is waiting."

"The stove is always waiting."

"What?"

"Nothing."

He kicked the pail. Brown water leaped over the rim
and spread across the boards she had already washed.

For one second Shenah stared at the widening stain. Inside
her there rose not anger alone, but all the mornings—each
dark stair, each pail, each greasy pot, each command thrown
at her as at a dog. She saw the ship
rolling under a bruised sky. She saw herself clutching the
rail, sick and freezing, while Uncle Mordecai promised, "In America
you'll become a lady. You'll learn. You'll earn. You'll see
a life." She saw the first day in this house,
when he put the broom into her hands.

A broom instead of a book.

A cellar instead of the sky.

She stood.

Uncle Mordecai stepped back. Though she was thin, she was
taller than he remembered. Her face, usually pale from indoor
work, had taken on a hard, feverish color.

"You kicked it," she said.

"So?"

"You made dirty what I cleaned."

"So clean it again."

"No."

The word fell between them. Small—yet it seemed to strike
the walls and come back louder.

"No?"

"I won't clean it again."

"Meshugeneh girl! Take the rag."

"No!"

Doors opened above and below. A woman with curl papers
leaned over the railing. Two children appeared barefoot on the
stairs.

Uncle Mordecai seized Shenah's wrist. "Into the kitchen before the
whole house gets a show."

She twisted free. "Let them see. Let all America see!
You brought an orphan girl over the ocean and made
from her a horse. No wages. No school. Nothing but
scrub, carry, wash. Let them see what kind of uncle!"

His face changed. Rage first—purple, puffing rage—and after it a
frightened look, almost childish.

"An orphan," he repeated. "Always she throws orphan in my
teeth. Was it me who made the cholera? Was it
me who buried your father and mother? Who paid your
ship-ticket?"

"And must I pay you my whole life?"

"You eat my food!"

"Then tell me what I owe. A dollar? Ten dollars?
A hundred? I'll work and pay. But I won't belong
to you."

Aunt Gittel rushed from the apartment, tying her wrapper across
her broad body. "Gevalt, what screaming! Mordecai, the respectable tenants
are hearing."

"Respectable!" cried the woman with curl papers. "Since when?"

"Close your door, Mrs. Blumenthal!"

"Close your own mouth."

Uncle Mordecai lifted his hand. For a moment it hung
over Shenah's face.

She did not shrink.

Perhaps it was that stillness which stopped him. Perhaps in
her blazing eyes he saw the eyes of his dead
sister. His arm dropped.

"You think the streets want you?" he said hoarsely. "Go.
Go give a look how America waits for a greenhorn
girl with broken English and no trade. By night you'll
crawl back."

The fury drained from Shenah. Suddenly he seemed old. His
undershirt sagged at the neck. A patch showed on one
knee of his trousers. He too rose before dawn. He
too collected rents from people who cursed him and paid
the landlord who cursed him more. He had crossed the
same ocean with empty pockets. Scarcity had closed around his
heart until every crumb became a debt and every kindness
an investment.

For one dangerous instant pity weakened her.

Then her eyes dropped to the overturned pail.

If she stayed, she would pity herself into the grave.

She walked past him into the apartment. From a nail
behind the stove she took her worn brown shawl. Her
possessions fit into a flour sack: one change of underclothes,
a comb missing three teeth, her mother's brass candlesticks, and
a little Russian book of poems she could barely read
because so much schooling had been denied her. At the
bottom she placed a folded scrap of paper.

The professor's handwriting crossed it in blue ink:

*You have an original mind. Do not let hardship persuade
you otherwise.*

Only that. Eleven words. Yet when she touched them, the
room widened.

Once, in the old country, before her parents died, Professor
Nikolai Andreyev had come each Thursday to teach the pharmacist's
sons French and history. Shenah, then sixteen, served their tea
and lingered by the door. One rainy afternoon he found
her studying a map left open on the table.

"What do you seek there?" he had asked.

"Where the countries end."

"And have you found it?"

"The ink ends," she answered. "Maybe the countries don't."

He looked at her—not through her, not past her, but
at her. After that he lent her books. He listened
when she spoke. He said her questions mattered. On the
last evening before he left for Warsaw, she brought back
a volume of poems. Snow had whitened his shoulders. In
the dark vestibule he took her cold hand between his
warm ones.

"You must learn," he said. "Promise me."

She promised.

Then, sudden as light touching water, he bent and kissed
her.

A breath against her lips. No more. But all the
dead years afterward had not erased it. In that instant
she had felt chosen out of the dust—lifted—named as a
person.

Now she put his note close to the candlesticks and
tied the sack.

At the door Aunt Gittel blocked her way.

"Where will you go?"

"To work."

"What work?"

"Any work that pays me."

"You'll starve."

"Then the hunger will be mine."

She passed into the hall.

Uncle Mordecai stood where she had left him. The watching
tenants retreated as Shenah came down. At the bottom step
she paused. She wanted him to call her back—not with
a command, but with one human word. *Forgive me. Child.
Stay until you find a place.*

His lips moved.

"Don't forget," he said, "you owe me for the ship-ticket."

Something broke loose in her—a laugh or a sob; she
did not know which. She pushed open the front door.

Morning struck her face.

The street was already surging. Pushcarts pressed wheel to wheel
beneath striped awnings. Peddlers shouted oranges, onions, stockings, tin pans.
Women bargained with babies on their hips. Boys darted between
wagons. Overhead, laundry flapped from fire escapes like a thousand
flags of surrender. The elevated train thundered beyond the roofs,
showering sparks into the smoky air.

No one turned toward her. No welcoming arms opened. America
did not know she had escaped.

Yet above the roofs lay a strip of sky—thin, cold,
silver-white.

Shenah lifted her head and walked toward it.

---

By noon her courage had worn through with the soles
of her shoes.

"Experienced operators only."

"No greenhorns."

"Come next week."

"Full up."

At a cigar shop the foreman laughed when she said
she had quick hands. At a laundry a red-faced woman
inspected her cracked fingers and told her to heal first.
At a paper-box factory thirty girls waited before the door;
the last position went to a cousin of the bookkeeper.

Shenah walked on.

Hunger pulled at her stomach. The sack cut into her
shoulder. Streets opened into other streets, each more crowded than
the last. Everywhere signs shouted in languages she could not
fully read. Everywhere doors swallowed workers and threw them out
again. America was doors—locked doors, guarded doors, doors marked *No
Help Wanted.*

Near dusk she found herself before a six-story brick building
with rows of grimy windows. Painted across its side in
faded red letters were the words:

**IMPERIAL LADIES' WAIST COMPANY
GIRLS WANTED—STEADY WORK**

Girls wanted.

The letters trembled through her exhaustion. Wanted! Somewhere behind that
wall, hands were needed. Her hands. Not beautiful hands, not
educated hands, but strong hands, quick hands—hands that could make
a way.

The doorway seemed suddenly flooded with white radiance. It opened
like two great arms.

She entered.

The employment room smelled of cloth dust and sweat. A
clerk with a pencil behind his ear looked her over.

"You operated?"

"I can learn."

"That means no."

"I sewed by hand. I mend. I copy quick."

"Machine's different."

"Learn me five minutes."

He snorted. "Next."

Shenah planted both palms on his desk. "Give me five
minutes. If I spoil, send me out. What do you
lose?"

Behind her, the waiting girls giggled.

The clerk frowned, but a man in a vest, passing
with a bundle of shirtwaists, stopped.

"What's this?"

"Another one thinks she's a genius, Mr. Bialek."

The man in the vest studied Shenah's face. "You ever
see a power machine?"

"No."

"Afraid?"

"Yes," she said. "But I do it anyhow."

He smiled. "Put her by Arkin. If she keeps ten
seams straight, she stays."

The factory room struck her like weather.

Two hundred machines clattered beneath hanging belts. Wheels spun, needles
flashed, presses hissed. Cloth dust floated in the gaslight. Girls
bent over tables shoulder to shoulder, their faces gray, their
hair tied back with rags. Foremen threaded the aisles, calling
numbers. The floor trembled as though a metal heart beat
under it.

Shenah could not hear herself think.

Then a young man rose from a machine near the
window. He was broad-shouldered, with dark hair combed carefully back
and a patient, square face. His sleeves were rolled to
the elbows.

"Sam," shouted Mr. Bialek, "show this one the single needle."

Sam Arkin glanced at Shenah's sack and swollen hands.

"New landed?"

"Six months."

"You speak English?"

"I speak enough that people understand when they want."

His mouth twitched. "Sit."

He showed her the treadle, the guide, the lever. His
hands moved without waste—firm hands, scarred at the fingertips.

"Don't fight the machine," he told her. "It's stronger than
you. Let it run, but show it where."

The first seam crawled crooked. The second bunched. On the
third, the needle struck too near her finger, and she
jerked back.

"Slow," Sam said.

"I got no time slow."

"You got less time bleeding."

Around them the machines roared. Shenah leaned close. The cloth
beneath her fingers was a white road, the needle a
hammering foot. She pressed the treadle. The machine leaped. For
a second panic blinded her. Then her hands understood what
her mind did not. Guide—release—turn. The seam ran forward.

One straight line.

Then another.

By the tenth, sweat streamed down her back, but Mr.
Bialek grunted his approval.

"Three dollars first week. If you're no trouble."

"Three dollars mine?"

"Whose should it be?"

Mine.

The word opened within her like sunrise.

At six, the belts slowed and stopped. The sudden silence
rang louder than the machines. Girls stretched their backs and
shook threads from their skirts.

A thin red-haired worker at the next table gave Shenah
a sour look.

"Three dollars," she said. "Bialek gets girls cheaper every day.
Soon we pay him for the pleasure."

"I didn't make the wages," Shenah answered.

"No, only take bread from them that knows the trade."

"Sadie, leave her breathe," said a plump woman fastening her
coat. "First day she looks half-dead."

"Half-dead girls sew cheap. That's the trouble."

Sam came carrying Shenah's flour sack.

"You got family near?"

The memory of Uncle Mordecai's kicked pail swept over her.

"No."

"A room?"

"No."

Sadie, who had been wrapping a scarf around her narrow
neck, paused.

"No room? So where you sleep?"

"I'll find."

"With three dollars coming next Saturday?" Sadie rolled her eyes.
"Come by me for tonight. My sister moved to Brooklyn,
so there's half a mattress not earning nothing."

Shenah stared. "You just said I take your bread."

"A mouth and a heart ain't always partners. Come before
I change both."

Thus, beneath hostility sharpened by hunger, a door opened.

Sadie lived with her mother and two younger brothers in
three rooms on Rivington Street. The half mattress lay behind
a curtain in the kitchen. Mrs. Polansky served Shenah a
bowl of barley soup without asking questions. It was thin
soup, but warm. Steam touched her face like a blessing.

That night, while the boys breathed heavily on the other
side of the curtain and mice scratched behind the stove,
Shenah unfolded the professor's note.

The gas jet made a golden island above the paper.

*You have an original mind.*

The factory still thundered in her bones. She saw the
women bent under belts and wheels, the cloth piling around
them like snowdrifts, Sam's strong hands showing hers the way.
Could a mind live in such a place? Could it
rise above the clatter?

"Yes," she whispered. "It must."

She pressed the note to her lips.

From somewhere beyond the roofs came a train's long cry.
It did not sound to her like loneliness. It sounded
like distance calling.

---

Within three weeks Shenah earned five dollars.

Within two months she earned eight.

She learned every operation Mr. Bialek allowed her to touch.
Sleeves, cuffs, collars, buttonholes—her hands flew. The machine no longer
frightened her. Its clatter became a language, and beneath the
separate crashes she began to hear a mighty rhythm: thousands
of immigrant hands pushing through necessity, thousands of wheels carrying
lives forward.

A surging song of hope!

Even the factory windows changed. They were filmed with dust,
but through the upper panes Shenah could see a slice
of sky. At noon she stood near the glass with
bread in one hand and a borrowed newspaper in the
other, sounding out words.

"Leg-is-la-ture," she murmured.

Sam leaned against the sill beside her. "You'll break your
teeth on such a word."

"What means it?"

"Politicians making laws."

"Then why not say law-makers?"

"Because politicians get paid by the syllable."

She laughed.

It was easy to laugh with Sam. He knew how
to turn trouble sideways until a little humor slipped through.
When Mrs. Fein's sleeve got caught and tore, he said
the waist had declared independence. When the steam pipe broke
and drenched three pressing girls, he asked whether Imperial had
opened a Russian bath. Even Sadie smiled, though she usually
guarded her smiles as if they cost ten cents each.

He brought Shenah newspapers after other boarders finished with them.
He showed her where the public library stood. On Friday
evenings, when the factory closed early, he walked with her
to the reading room.

The first time she entered, awe stopped her beneath the
high ceiling.

Rows upon rows of books rose in the quiet. Green
lamps shone on polished tables. Nobody shouted. Nobody demanded money.
Men in work clothes sat beside clerks and students. A
gray-haired woman turned pages with blackened fingers. Here was wealth
that did not shrink when shared.

"Can I take them?" Shenah whispered.

"If you got a card."

"To my room? Without paying?"

"That's how libraries work."

"All this—and they let a person carry it away?"

Sam watched her face. "You look like you found a
gold mine."

"Higher than gold."

He helped her fill out the application because she did
not know how to spell several English words. She hated
needing his help. The pen trembled in her fingers.

"Occupation?" the librarian asked.

"Factory girl," said Sam.

Shenah looked up. "Operator."

The librarian wrote it.

Operator. Not drudge. Not orphan. Not burden. A worker with
wages of her own.

Soon she attended evening classes twice a week. She came
home dizzy with fatigue, but the English words burned in
her head like stars. Geography opened continents. History raised dead
civilizations. Grammar, with its rules and exceptions, seemed at first
a wall built to mock her; then each rule became
a foothold.

She practiced sentences while sewing.

"I am. You are. She is."

Sadie groaned. "Who cares what she is? Stitch the cuff."

"I was. I shall be."

"That one I like," said Sam from the next machine.
"She shall be. Only what?"

Shenah's hands stopped.

"A person."

The girls laughed, but not cruelly.

"You're already a person," said the plump woman.

"No. I'm arms for a machine. Feet for stairs. A
stomach needing rent. A person is one that got something
inside and can bring it out."

"What lays inside you?" Sadie asked.

"So much I can't yet give it a name."

Mr. Bialek, passing, snapped, "Less philosophy, more production."

But her production surpassed them all.

When the floor inspector fell ill, Mr. Bialek gave Shenah
his tally sheet. She caught defects others missed, rearranged bundles
to prevent delays, and argued with a cutter twice her
age until he admitted his sizing error. A month later
she was made assistant examiner at ten dollars a week.

The news traveled through the workroom before lunch.

"Ten dollars!" cried Mrs. Fein. "For a girl green enough
to grow leaves!"

"Bialek likes her pretty eyes," muttered one operator.

"Pretty eyes don't inspect crooked seams," Sam answered.

Sadie said nothing. That evening, as they climbed the tenement
stairs, she burst out, "I worked there four years. Four
years! You come yesterday and already ten dollars."

Shenah stopped on the landing.

"I know."

"You know? What does that help me?"

"No help."

"My mother's medicine is two dollars. Benny needs shoes. The
landlord raises rent. And you—you buy books."

"I borrow books."

"You think yourself better."

The accusation struck deep because Shenah feared it might one
day become true.

"I don't think better."

"You hold your head like the ceiling belongs to you."

"Because all my life they pushed it down!"

Sadie flinched.

From below came the smell of frying onions. A baby
wailed behind the wall. Both girls stood breathing hard, their
faces lit by the weak stairway flame.

Shenah touched Sadie's sleeve. "I can ask Bialek to learn
you examining."

"I don't need favors."

"No favors. You got sharp eyes. Sharper than your tongue
even."

Sadie tried not to smile and failed.

Two weeks later Sadie was learning the tally sheets.

Scarcity made people cruel; a little room could make them
kind again. Shenah began to understand this. She had thought
freedom was a door crossed once. But doors stood everywhere.
Some she opened for herself. Some another person held. Some
she must hold open after passing through.

And always near her was Sam.

He waited after classes to walk her home. He mended
the broken clasp on her mother's candlestick. When she caught
fever, he brought oranges, though oranges in winter cost shameful
money. He never mocked the professor's note, even when she
told him the whole story.

They sat that evening on the roof of Sadie's building.
Below them the tenements crowded close—brick walls, chimneys, water tanks,
fire escapes. Above them the September sky spread clean and
immense.

"He was the first who saw me," Shenah said.

"I see you."

"Not like him."

"How you know?"

"He saw what I could become."

Sam picked mortar from the parapet. "Maybe he saw a
young girl with big eyes looking at him like Moses
come down from the mountain."

"No. Don't make him little."

"I don't. But you make him so big no living
man could reach his shoes."

Her cheeks flamed. "He had learning. He spoke beautiful. In
his face was—was a white radiance. Like a door to
a higher life."

"And where is this door now?"

"I don't know."

"He writes?"

"He doesn't know where I am."

"Does he got a wife?"

She turned sharply. "Why ask?"

"Because professors marry too. Even with white radiance."

"You're jealous."

"Yes."

The plainness of it silenced her.

Sam looked out over the roofs. His profile, heavy and
earnest, had none of the professor's delicacy. His nose bent
slightly from a childhood fall. A scar cut through one
eyebrow. He smelled faintly of machine oil even on Sundays.

"I got no fancy way to talk," he said. "But
when you come in the factory with that flour sack—so
hungry, so proud—I said to myself, here walks somebody who'll
either climb the world or die biting it. Since then,
when you're not there, the whole room is only noise."

"Sam—"

"Don't answer. I ain't asking yet."

He climbed down the fire escape before she could speak.

Shenah remained under the open sky. Pity moved through her,
and tenderness too—warm, frightening tenderness. She remembered his patience at
the machine, the newspapers, the oranges, his laughter. If she
reached out, she could touch a life ready to receive
her.

But behind that possible life stood another image: a lamp,
a book, the professor's listening face.

Not the man alone. Perhaps Sam was right—the man might
be married, indifferent, changed. But what he had awakened was
not changed. Learning. Beauty. Equality. The right to unfold the
mind God had put in her.

Could she enter marriage without shutting that door?

The sky darkened from gold to violet. One star appeared
between two chimneys.

Shenah raised both hands toward it.

"God," she whispered, "don't let my heart become a chain
to my head."

---

Winter pressed the East Side under soot-colored snow.

Work slowed after Christmas. Girls were laid off by the
dozen. The factory became a battlefield of lowered eyes and
whispered calculations. Who could survive one week without wages? Who
had pawned a coat already? Who would be next?

Shenah kept her position. Her skill protected her, and she
hated that protection when Sadie's hours were cut.

Sam too remained. He had been at Imperial seven years
and could repair nearly every machine on the floor. He
stayed late for extra pay, oiling gears after the others
left.

One February evening he asked Shenah to wait.

The room emptied around them. Belts hung motionless from the
ceiling. Without bodies and noise, the factory looked like the
skeleton of some dead beast.

Sam washed his hands at the basin, put on his
coat, and led her to a small coffee house on
Grand Street. He ordered tea and two rolls. Shenah noticed
that his fingers shook.

"What happened?"

"Nothing happened. Something should happen."

From inside his coat he drew a bank-book and placed
it before her.

She stared at the stamped figures. "What's this?"

"Read."

There were deposits made month after month—three dollars, five dollars,
sometimes ten. The final balance was more money than Shenah
had ever imagined one worker possessing.

"One thousand eight hundred and forty dollars," she whispered.

"And sixty-two cents."

"How?"

"Ten years saving. I started at fifteen. Slept four men
in a room. Didn't smoke. Didn't gamble. Ate lunch from
home."

"Why show me?"

"I can buy a small notions store. Maybe a dry-goods
place in Brooklyn. We live behind it first. Later upstairs.
You don't got to work factory no more."

The coffee house blurred.

"Sam—"

"Wait. Let me talk myself out, or I bust."

He leaned forward. His square face burned with painful resolve.

"I know I ain't a professor. I don't speak French.
When I read a book, half the time the book
wins. But I work. I save. I don't drink. I
would never lift a hand on you. You want classes?
Take classes. You want books? I buy shelves. A whole
wall shelves! You want a piano, even a piano when
we can afford. Marry me, Shenah."

The words seemed to gather all the warmth in the
room and lay it at her feet.

Marriage. A home. A door with her own key. No
foreman watching the clock. No fear of layoffs. Shelves of
books, lamplight, perhaps children sleeping clean under blankets. Sam coming
in from the store, stamping snow from his shoes. His
faithful hands. His kind eyes.

How sweet it would be to rest!

She was tired—God, how tired. Tired in the marrow. Tired
of guarding each dollar, each hour, each fragile step upward.
For one instant she wanted to drop her head on
his shoulder and let his strength carry them both.

Sam saw the softness in her face. Hope flared in
him.

"All the money is yours," he said quickly. "Put the
bank-book in your name. I don't care. Only say yes."

That ended it.

The warm room closed.

"Why you give me money?"

"Because I love you."

"To buy my rest?"

"To make you safe."

"Safe where?"

"With me."

"Behind a store?"

"At first."

"And then babies, cooking, customers, accounts?"

"We hire help when business grows."

"When business grows. When there's enough. Always later my learning."

"No! I said classes."

"At night, after work for you and house for me."

His face tightened. "So what you want? A palace? Servants?"

"I want to stand on my own feet."

"You do stand! I'm asking you stand beside me."

"With your eighteen hundred dollars holding us up."

"What's shameful in money I worked for?"

"Nothing. It's beautiful. But it's yours."

"I make it ours."

"You can say ours, but in my bones I know
who earned. Every book I open, I think Sam paid.
Every bread I eat, Sam paid. Every step higher—Sam lifted."

"So let me lift! What is love for?"

"I must lift myself."

He stared at her as though she had spoken madness.

Outside, sleigh runners scraped the filthy snow. A waiter dropped
a spoon. The ordinary world went on, indifferent to the
heart breaking at the little table.

Sam's voice sank.

"It's him."

Shenah looked down.

"The professor."

"He is not only himself."

"What does that mean?"

"He is—how can I say?—the first light that fell on
me. Before him I thought maybe I was born only
to serve, to carry, to disappear. He talked to me,
and I heard my own soul answer. When I remember
him, I remember I got a mind."

"I tell you every day you got a mind."

"Yes."

"I bring books."

"Yes."

"I walk you to school."

"Yes!"

"Then why his light and not mine?"

She could not answer without wounding him more. His love
was warm fire—honest fire, earthly fire. It offered a room
against the winter. But the professor's remembered light shone from
a height no room could contain. It might be illusion.
It might lead nowhere. Still, it called upward.

Sam closed the bank-book.

"You love him."

"I love what woke in me through him."

"Words. All words."

"Words are life when all your life nobody lets you
speak."

"He kissed you once."

Her hand flew to her breast.

"And I would give you my whole life."

"I know."

"Then take it."

"I can't."

His face emptied.

Not anger. Anger would have been easier. The life simply
went out of him, leaving his features gray and still.
Shenah reached across the table.

He pulled his hands away.

"Don't pity me."

"I don't pity."

"You look like on a sick dog."

"Sam, please—"

He stood so suddenly the table shook.

"You think I'm low because I want a store. You
think money is dirt. But I remember dirt. In Odessa,
seven of us slept in one cellar. My mother washed
shirts till blood run from her nails. My father died
owing rent. When I came here, I swore nobody I
love should shake before a landlord. That bank-book ain't money.
It's walls against fear."

"I know it."

"No, you don't. You dream sky because somebody else remembers
to pay for the roof."

The words struck with the justice of a blow.

Sam threw coins beside the cups and walked out.

Shenah followed. "Sam!"

He pushed into the crowd.

Snow had begun to fall—thin, black-speckled flakes melting on coats
and hats. She caught his sleeve at the corner.

"Listen to me."

"Go home."

"You call me proud—yes, I'm proud. Because without pride I'd
be back scrubbing my uncle's halls. But I don't despise
what you made. I honor it."

"Honor! A fine thing to sleep beside."

"You want I should lie?"

"I want you should love me."

Her grip loosened.

"I do love you—"

He turned, hope and fury colliding in his face.

"Not the way you need."

He made a sound like a man struck in the
stomach.

"Then don't say it."

He walked away.

This time she did not follow.

Snow gathered on her hair. Crowds divided around her and
joined again. Faces streamed past—tired faces, hungry faces, each carrying
its own hidden tragedy through the city's glare.

She had chosen.

Why, then, did it feel like tearing off her own
arm?

---

For days Sam did not come to work.

His empty machine stood beside the aisle. Each time Shenah
passed it, something dropped inside her.

"Influenza," said Mr. Bialek.

"Heart influenza," Sadie murmured.

"Be quiet."

"So marry him."

"Be quiet!"

The factory women knew. News traveled where air itself seemed
unable to move. Some said Shenah was a fool. Some
said she had another man. Mrs. Fein declared that a
girl who rejected eighteen hundred dollars deserved a black year.
The younger workers looked at Shenah with frightened wonder, as
if she had flung bread into a furnace.

"Love don't pay rent," Sadie told her at supper.

"Neither does a soul without love."

"So what are you waiting for? A prince?"

"I'm waiting to know what I can become."

"Maybe you become old."

"Then I become old by my own road."

But at night defiance abandoned her.

She saw Sam's deadened face. She heard his words: *You
dream sky because somebody else remembers to pay for the
roof.*

Was she selfish? Was education only a silk name for
vanity? Other women worked, married, bore children, stretched pennies, and
still found courage to laugh. What right had she to
demand a life higher than theirs?

Yet higher did not mean richer. Higher meant awake. To
live with every faculty opened—to understand the forces grinding people
down, to speak for those whose tongues were trapped, to
become more than an exhausted pair of hands.

She did not want escape from her people.

She wanted strength enough to return to them carrying light.

On the fifth evening she went to Sam's boardinghouse.

A widow answered the door and pointed upstairs. "He sits
like a stone. Maybe you can make him eat."

His room was small and fiercely neat. One bed, one
chair, one washstand. On the wall hung a photograph of
an old woman in a dark shawl. Sam sat by
the window in his undershirt though the room was cold.

He did not look surprised.

"You shouldn't come."

"Why you weren't at work?"

"No reason."

"You're sick?"

"No."

A plate beside him held untouched bread and herring.

Shenah closed the door. "You punish yourself to punish me."

"I don't do nothing for you anymore."

"Then do for yourself."

He laughed bitterly. "Yourself. Always yourself. America teaches that good."

"Yes. Sometimes good."

"Go give speeches at school."

"I came to give one here."

"I don't want."

"You listened when I needed. Now I'll make you listen."

His eyes flashed. "What more you got to take?"

"Nothing. I came to give back what you gave me."

She stood before him, trembling, yet the words rose with
a force beyond fear.

"When I came to the factory, the machine was stronger
than me. You said, 'Don't fight it. Show it where.'
You taught my hands their strength. You brought me to
the library. You walked beside me when I was nobody
in America. You think because I can't marry you, all
this becomes nothing?"

"It becomes worse than nothing."

"No! Pain don't wipe out truth."

"For you maybe."

"For you too, if you stop laying down like dead."

He sprang from the chair.

"Dead? You made me dead!"

"Then make yourself alive!"

The cry filled the room.

Sam stared at her.

Shenah's whole body shook now. Tears burned her eyes, but
she drove on.

"You got strength enough to cross an ocean alone. Strength
to work ten years and save every little coin. Strength
to help fools like me when nobody helped you. And
now one girl says no, so all your strength is
gone? Then it wasn't strength. It was only a bank-book."

His face darkened. "Get out."

"No."

"I'll throw you."

"Throw! At least you move."

He took one step toward her, fists clenched. Then his
arms fell.

"What you want from me?" he whispered.

"I want you should become more than a man who
can provide for a wife."

"That's little?"

"It's great. But you got more. I see it."

He turned away.

"You could run a factory better than Bialek. You know
machines, people, costs. You could open your store. You could
study business. You could organize the workers so girls don't
get thrown out every slow season. What do I know?
Something! Make something from this suffering."

"You talk like pain is coal for a stove."

"Maybe it is. Burn it—or freeze beside it."

Silence followed.

From the street came the cries of children sliding on
frozen gutters. A woman called from a window. Somewhere a
hurdy-gurdy played a broken waltz.

Sam pressed his forehead to the glass.

"You got your professor's words now," he said. "Big words."

"They're mine."

He looked back.

There it was—the whole struggle between them. He had thought
her hunger belonged to another man's kiss, another man's language.
She too had thought so. But now she knew. The
professor had not placed a soul inside her. He had
only knocked once upon a door already there.

"The words are mine," she repeated softly. "Like your savings
are yours. Like your strength is yours. Nobody can give
us a self ready-made."

His mouth twisted. "And nobody can share it?"

"Share—yes. Surrender—no."

Tears came into his eyes, and he hated them. He
wiped them roughly with his palm.

"If I become this big somebody, then you marry me?"

She closed her eyes.

"No promises."

"Then why should I?"

"For yourself."

"Always yourself."

"Until you got a self, what can you give anybody?"

He sat again. This time not like a stone, but
like a tired man.

Shenah lifted the plate and put it into his hands.

"Eat."

To her astonishment, he obeyed. One bite, chewed without appetite.
Then another.

She turned toward the door.

"Shenah."

Her heart lurched.

He did not ask her to stay. He only said,
"Bialek offered me assistant foreman last month."

"You didn't tell."

"I didn't want be the one driving girls faster."

"Then take it and drive different."

"He won't let."

"Make him."

A shadow of the old humor crossed his face. "You
think I can make everybody."

"No. Only yourself."

She left him with the bread in his hand.

---

Spring came first as dirty water running along the curbs.
Then one morning, above the pushcarts, a pear tree in
a tenement yard burst into white blossom.

Shenah saw it through an iron gate on her way
to work.

White radiance enclosed by brick.

She stopped so suddenly a man carrying chickens cursed and
nearly struck her. The little tree trembled in the smoky
air, each flower clean as a star. No one had
planted it for beauty, perhaps. No one in the crowded
house had time to worship it. Yet it rose from
the black soil and opened.

At Imperial, Sam's machine was no longer empty.

He had returned thinner, quieter. For weeks their speech remained
confined to work.

"Bundle twelve is short."

"I'll check."

"The belt on twenty-three is slipping."

"I'll fix."

Then he became assistant foreman.

The girls groaned when Mr. Bialek announced it, but Sam
refused to dock Mrs. Fein for time lost repairing a
broken needle. He rearranged the tables so daylight reached more
machines. He argued for steadier winter hours, showing the owners
that trained workers saved more money than constantly hired beginners.

"They'll throw him out," Sadie predicted.

They did not. Profits rose.

One noon Sam found Shenah at the window with a
school catalogue.

"What's that?"

"Teacher-training classes."

"You want teach?"

"Maybe. First I need two more years English. History. Literature."

"You'll keep factory?"

"At day."

"You'll kill yourself."

"I'm too busy."

He almost smiled.

In June, Sadie and her mother moved to a better
apartment in the Bronx. Shenah rented a tiny room of
her own under a roof on East Broadway. The ceiling
slanted so low she bumped her head each morning. In
summer it baked; in winter, she was warned, frost grew
inside the window. But the room had a lock.

Her lock.

She set her mother's candlesticks on the washstand. Above them
she pinned the professor's note. Then, after considering, she placed
beside it her factory pay envelope and her library card.

Three documents of becoming.

On the first night she lit the candles though it
was not Sabbath. Their flames touched the bare walls and
made them golden. No aunt called her wasteful. No uncle
demanded labor. Below, the city clamored: wagon wheels, quarrels, whistles,
songs from open windows. The whole East Side seemed packed
into one vast, feverish body.

Shenah opened her grammar book.

"I am," she said aloud.

The candle flames leaned.

"I was."

A train thundered in the distance.

"I shall be."

The words were no longer an exercise.

---

Years might pass before she became what she could not
yet name. The road did not lie clear. Tuition cost
money. Work consumed strength. Some nights English abandoned her, and
every sentence she wrote came back bleeding red corrections. Some
mornings she woke with longing so deep that it seemed
a physical wound.

She still dreamed of the professor.

In dreams he waited on a golden hill beneath an
open sky. She climbed toward him carrying books, but as
she neared, his face dissolved into light. She called his
name. The light answered in her own voice.

Once she wrote to the pharmacist's sons in the old
country, asking whether they knew his address. Months later came
a letter. Professor Andreyev had married. He taught in Kiev.
He had two daughters.

Shenah read the lines without breathing.

The room collapsed inward—the low ceiling, the narrow bed, the
cracked pitcher. Deadness—emptiness—nothingness! Her beautiful secret reduced to a married
man taking breakfast with his children. Had he forgotten the
snowy vestibule? Had the kiss meant only kindness, impulse, farewell?

She tore the letter in half.

Then gathered the pieces.

All night she sat by the window. Rain moved over
the roofs. At first grief devoured everything. Not only the
man, but the country she had lost, her mother's voice,
her father's books, the girl she had been. She sobbed
with her forehead against the glass until dawn whitened the
street.

When the light came, it fell upon the note pinned
above the candlesticks.

*You have an original mind. Do not let hardship persuade
you otherwise.*

The words remained true whether he remembered or not.

A strange peace entered her.

He had been a door. She had mistaken the door
for the whole world beyond it.

She dressed and went to work.

At lunch Sam saw her swollen eyes.

"What happened?"

"I found out my professor has a wife."

Sam looked away quickly, guarding whatever satisfaction or hope tried
to rise.

"I'm sorry."

"Are you?"

"I'm trying."

She laughed through the ache. "That's honest."

He offered half his apple. She took it.

They stood side by side at the dusty window. Below,
workers flooded the street, caps and shawls moving like a
dark river. Above, the sky shone blue between roofs.

"Your evening school," Sam said. "They got business arithmetic?"

"Yes."

"Maybe I take."

She turned to him.

"Don't look like the Messiah arrived," he warned.

"I say nothing."

"Your face says speeches."

She bit into the apple.

That autumn Sam enrolled in two classes. He complained about
fractions, bookkeeping terms, and teachers younger than himself. Shenah helped
with grammar; he helped her calculate percentages. They argued. They
laughed. Sometimes the old wound opened between them, raw and
dangerous. But neither pretended it was not there.

He did not ask again for marriage.

She did not ask him to stop loving her.

Life remained unfinished.

---

On the anniversary of the morning Shenah left her uncle's
house, a girl appeared at Imperial carrying a carpetbag.

She was smaller than Shenah had been, with frightened eyes
and a bruise darkening one cheek. Mr. Bialek waved her
away.

"No beginners."

The girl stood helplessly as the machines roared around her.

Shenah crossed the floor.

"Give her five minutes."

Mr. Bialek scowled. "You collect strays now?"

"I train her on my time."

"Production suffers, it comes from your pay."

"Good."

Sam, checking a belt nearby, heard and glanced over. Their
eyes met.

He nodded once.

Shenah led the girl to an empty machine.

"What's your name?"

"Rivka."

"You sewed before?"

"By hand."

"Afraid?"

Rivka looked at the flashing needle. "Yes."

"Good. Fear keeps fingers awake. Sit."

She placed the girl's hands on the cloth.

"Don't fight the machine. It's stronger than you. Let it
run—but show it where."

The old words moved through Shenah with a tenderness almost
too great to bear. Hands teaching hands. Strength passing without
ownership. A door held open.

Rivka's first seam went crooked.

"Again," Shenah said.

The second bunched.

"Again."

Around them, belts spun and wheels clattered. Dust burned in
the air. Foremen shouted. Women coughed over their work. Nothing
in the room was beautiful.

Yet Shenah heard beneath the machinery that surging song—the stubborn
music of people refusing to remain what misery had made
them.

On the fifth attempt, Rivka's seam ran straight.

The girl looked up, astonished.

"I did it."

"You did."

Such light entered her face!

Shenah turned away for fear of weeping.

At the far end of the floor, Sam stood speaking
with Mr. Bialek, pointing to figures in a ledger. His
shoulders were straighter now. He had submitted plans to buy
a partnership in a small shop that repaired industrial machines.
Whether he would succeed, nobody knew. But he was moving—not
toward her, not away from her. Upward by his own
road.

Sadie, now an examiner, shouted that bundle thirty-six was missing
two waists. Mrs. Fein demanded justice for a vanished spool.
Rivka laughed as the machine leaped under her feet.

Life—loud, coarse, unfinished life—pressed around Shenah.

At evening she climbed the stairs to her room. On
the landing she met the landlord's daughter, a child of
nine, reading a schoolbook under the gas jet.

"What word?" Shenah asked.

The child pointed.

"*Aspiration.*"

Shenah sounded it slowly, though she knew it well.

"As-pi-ra-tion."

"What means?"

She looked upward. A square of twilight showed through the
stairwell skylight, deep blue and pierced by one early star.

"It means," she said, "a hunger that lifts the head."

Inside her room, she lit the candlesticks. She opened the
window, and the city rushed in—herring smoke, rain-soaked brick, cries
of peddlers, factory whistles, fragments of tenement songs. The odors
were acrid, the voices weary. Yet above them stretched the
sky, unowned and immeasurable.

Shenah set her schoolbooks on the table.

Her hands were no longer soft; perhaps they never had
been. The lye scars remained faintly across the knuckles. Needle
marks dotted her fingers. These hands had scrubbed another man's
floors. They had fed cloth through iron teeth. They had
clung to a remembered kiss. They had refused a bank-book
and wounded a faithful heart. They had opened books, counted
wages, trained another girl.

Not clean hands.

Not idle hands.

Her own hands.

She thought of Uncle Mordecai. Months ago she had sent
him the price of her ship-ticket, though she owed him
nothing that money could settle. He returned no thanks. Aunt
Gittel sent word that he carried the receipt in his
pocket and showed it to visitors, boasting that his niece
earned ten dollars a week.

Even his pride tried to own her.

Shenah smiled.

Let him boast. Perhaps his hard life needed some reflected
light.

She thought of Sam eating bread in the cold room.
Love had not saved either of them. Perhaps love was
not meant to save. Perhaps it was meant to awaken,
to challenge, to burn away falsehood—even when the burning hurt.

Would there come a day when she could join her
life with his without surrender? Would his love grow wide
enough to leave her sky open? Would hers descend from
worship into companionship?

She did not know.

Once uncertainty had seemed like terror. Now it seemed like
space.

She sat at the table and began an essay for
school.

The assigned subject was *What America Means to Me.*

For a long time her pen hovered.

America was not Uncle Mordecai's promise. Not streets waiting with
gold. Not a factory sign opening its arms. America had
kicked pails too. It starved workers, crushed fingers, cut wages,
sold airless rooms at the price of blood. Its doors
did not open by themselves.

But there were doors.

A library. A classroom. A pay envelope bearing her name.
A machine mastered. A room with a lock. A man
who helped without understanding all he helped to release. A
girl whose crooked seam could be tried again.

Shenah bent over the page.

"America," she wrote, "is not a ready-made freedom. It is
the place where I must make freedom from what lays
in my own hands."

She frowned at the grammar, crossed out *lays*, and wrote
*lies*.

Then she continued.

Outside, darkness filled the narrow streets. One by one, windows
flared across the tenements—small lamps, weak lamps, lamps behind torn
curtains. No single flame could conquer such darkness.

Still they burned.

Shenah wrote until the factory ache left her back and
the walls disappeared. Her thoughts rose beyond the roof, beyond
the city, beyond the ocean that had carried her from
one bondage into another struggle. She saw no professor waiting
on a hill. No husband holding out safety. No angel
descending with a completed self.

She saw a road.

Steep—yes. Lonely—sometimes. Mud below, stars above.

And she saw herself upon it, not carried, not summoned,
not purchased.

Climbing.

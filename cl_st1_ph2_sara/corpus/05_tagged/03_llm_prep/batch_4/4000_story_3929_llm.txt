The house stood halfway up the city, neither on the
ground nor, properly speaking, above it, suspended between two office
towers by tensile members which the woman called sky-roots, although
they were made of plasteel and had never entered soil.

"Maximum suspension with no sensation of height," she said.

He kept saying that was very good.

He kept thinking, No sensation of anything, Madam, that would
be the maximum, wouldn't it?

The entrance appeared to be a dark wall. When the
woman approached, however, the wall folded into thirds and became
an entrance; then, after they passed through, it unfolded behind
them and became—not the same wall, he thought, but another
wall pretending to remember the first.

"Responsive ebony," she said. "Artificial, naturally. Genuine ebony is too
temperamental."

"Of course," he said, almost touching it.

The surface was warm, or had been instructed to seem
warm. There were no fingerprints. His fingers, arrested an inch
away, appeared in the finish as five pale worms investigating
five other pale worms, and he withdrew them before the
two hands could meet and exchange whatever information hands exchange
when their owners are exhausted.

He had not slept, unless the three hours on the
train counted, and the three hours did not count because
he had spent them counting stations that were not stops
and stops that were not stations. Now his skull felt
lightly upholstered. He imagined that if the woman opened his
forehead she would point inside and say, "Crease-resistant thoughtfoam, washable,
available in six neutral anxieties."

"This is the conversation area."

There was no furniture.

When he looked again, there was furniture: a low couch
extruded from the floor, two chairs shaped like incomplete circles,
and a table so transparent that the woman's legs seemed
to end at the knee.

"Flexsan seating," she said. "It adjusts to every viable posture."

"What about the nonviable ones?"

She smiled professionally, not at him but at the category
of customer into which he had briefly fallen. "The house
discourages those."

He sat. The couch received him too eagerly, rising around
his hips like dough. For a moment he thought it
meant to digest him. Then it firmed.

Laboratory rats, he remembered. Doctor, listen, I am trying to
explain the rats.

You painted a little white ring on a door and
taught them that the ring meant food. The rat ran,
jumped through the ring, and ate. Again: ring, jump, food.
Again and again until the animal's nervous system had made
the sentence so complete that no grammar could revise it.
Then one morning you replaced the door with a solid
panel painted with the identical ring. The rat ran. The
rat jumped. The rat struck its nose.

This was science, naturally.

Afterward the rat tried again because a rat cannot cross-examine
a symbol. It ran faster, perhaps believing in velocity; it
jumped higher, perhaps believing in grace; it hit the panel
harder, which was faith. The nose bled. The ring remained
innocent.

"They enter compulsive rotation," the doctor had said, or someone
had said, perhaps he himself was saying it now. "Then
convulsions. Then collapse. In the terminal phase, willingness."

Willingness was the worst. Nobody can not jump forever.

The woman touched the table and a blue flame appeared
beneath its surface.

"Minipiano thermal service," she said. "It boils water without heat."

Without heat, he repeated privately. Water without wetness, walls without
obstruction, a home without arrival.

She moved toward what looked like a window. The window
opened.

"This way."

He stayed seated.

Beyond her was a corridor made to resemble open air.
Projected clouds moved under the transparent floor, though they were
not clouds and the floor was not transparent; it was
cloudproof duroid, load-tested against panic. Far below—or apparently far below—traffic
flowed in red and silver corpuscles.

He saw his eyes reflected in the window-door. They were
small, wet, and watchful, the eyes of the rat after
the seventh impact when it no longer hated the wall
because hatred requires a remaining theory.

"Are you coming?"

"Yes," he said, but did not move.

Religion had been a ring once. Gold-painted, with singing behind
it. He had run at it obediently in childhood, knees
clean, hair wet from Sunday combing, and there had been
food beyond: eternity in slices, forgiveness in a white cup,
the large paternal kitchen of Heaven. Then the panel changed.
Or he changed. He jumped and struck his nose on
the sentence that nobody could prove.

Science was next, stainless and properly labelled. Science promised that
every wall was only an insufficiently examined door. He learned
atoms, glands, fossils, reflex arcs; he learned that love secreted,
that terror contracted, that God might be a lesion near
the temporal lobe. Explanations accumulated beautifully, each one a little
key, until he discovered there was no lock, only keys.

Money had a green ring. Money opened almost every door
except the one for which he earned it. He had
run harder there. His nose, metaphorically speaking (though not entirely
metaphorically, because stress produced bleeding), had paid taxes.

Then love—don't mention love, old friend, unless you mean the
renewable adhesive marketed under that name. Love was spring rain
and the smell of hair and two people making a
country in a rented room. Love said Jump, darling. Love
said There is no wall. He jumped. The wall married
somebody else in Connecticut.

"Sir?"

He stood because the couch had begun to pulse beneath
him, gently encouraging viable departure.

The corridor accepted their weight with a musical hum.

"Ambient promenade," the woman said. "The soundscape changes according to
mood."

A cheerful chord followed him.

"It thinks I'm cheerful."

"It is still calibrating."

"It may take years."

"Calibration averages eleven seconds."

She continued, each sentence cleanly packaged. The walls filtered air;
the air filtered odour; the odour system supplied Pine, Ocean,
Library, or First Rain. The ceiling was daylight-equivalent. The plants
were synthophyll and required neither water nor death. In the
dining module, plates were made from nutrivene, a substance resembling
porcelain but resistant to appetite. The knives had no edges
until authorised by food. The cupboards were seamless, the seams
having been found unhygienic.

He nodded at the appropriate intervals, or at intervals he
hoped were appropriate. His nod floated ahead of him and
became detached from agreement.

There had been a poet—he could not remember the poet's
name, which seemed discourteous after the man had gone to
so much trouble to be destroyed. The poet had pursued
something unnameable. Beauty, perhaps, though beauty was already a name
and therefore not it. The Absolute? The blue flame behind
appearances? A woman who was also music, mathematics, death, and
June?

He wrote toward it for twenty years. Every poem was
a marked opening; every poem hardened at the instant of
contact. At last language itself became the panel. He could
not say bread without suspecting metaphor, could not say moon
without hearing all the dead poets coughing. They found him
walking in circles around a fountain, asking the water to
hold still long enough to be true.

He had been ridiculous, which did not mean he had
been wrong.

"This partition," the woman said, "provides privacy while preserving a
maximum of openness."

The partition was clear.

"Privacy from whom?"

"From the adjacent occupancy."

He could see into the adjacent room, where a mechanical
bed turned itself down for no one.

"In what sense is it private?"

"The glass is selectively opaque."

"It isn't opaque."

"You haven't selected it."

She pressed an invisible control. The glass whitened, becoming a
blank wall.

There. The ring painted on the solid door. Or the
solid door painted on the ring. He stepped back so
quickly that the corridor gave an alarmed little note.

"Impact avoidance is standard," she said.

"I know a man in Jersey."

"I beg your pardon?"

"A man with a house. A genuine house, or it
had been genuine when he bought it. Brick pretending to
be brick, wood with the indecency to rot. He spent
fifteen years paying for it. Lawn, garage, children, taxable happiness.
Then one Tuesday he took a hammer to the kitchen
wall."

The woman waited with the expression used for optional features.

"He said there was a pipe making a noise. He
opened the wall and found no pipe, so he opened
the next wall. Then the floor. Then the ceiling. By
Thursday the bathroom fixtures were on the lawn and he
was digging beneath the foundation. He said the trouble had
to be somewhere. That is logical, Madam. If there is
trouble, it is somewhere."

"Was the property insured?"

"That was what his wife asked."

He laughed, but the corridor interpreted it as respiratory distress
and released filterable lavender.

In Jersey the man had continued until the house became
materials: lath, copper, plaster, nails, a bathtub standing upright among
hydrangeas like a white astonishment. Once the rooms were gone,
the man sat in the wreckage, calmer. The house could
no longer deceive him by being a house.

"Complete modularity prevents destructive renovation," the woman said. "Every unit
can be removed without tools."

"Then he would have removed it faster."

Ahead were three identical panels.

"The sleep environment is through the centre door."

"Which is the centre?"

"The one in the middle."

The middle shifted when he turned his head. There were
four panels now, or reflections of two, and the woman
had become three women reciting the same brochure at fractional
delays.

Rest, the centre promised. Rest had been another ring. Physicians
had prescribed it, employers postponed it, advertisements photographed it in
white sheets. But whenever he lay down, his mind ran
in place, leaping through religion, science, money, love, work, art,
houses, explanations—nose, ring, wall; nose, ring, wall—until dawn stood over
him with institutional cheerfulness.

Doctor, if you cut the prefrontal connections, does the rat
stop jumping?

It may.

Does it stop seeing the ring?

Possibly.

Does the wall open?

That is not the therapeutic objective.

He imagined consent forms printed on edible paper. PRECISE RELIEF
FROM EXCESSIVE MEANING. The little silver instrument, ugli-proof and self-sterilising,
entering above the eye. Afterward he would sit willingly. Willingness
without wanting: the final modern feature.

The woman opened the leftmost centre door. It was a
wardrobe.

"Adaptive storage," she said without embarrassment.

The next opened onto the kitchen they had already visited,
though they had not turned back. The third contained only
his reflection, standing with its coat crooked and its mouth
slightly open.

The reflected eyes knew him. Seventh impact, perhaps eighth.

He raised one hand. The other man raised his. Almost
touching, they waited on opposite sides of a substance that
was not quite glass.

"Old friend," he whispered, "which of us is being shown
the house?"

"Did you say something?"

"I said the workmanship is remarkable."

"Everything is guaranteed for forty years."

"And after forty?"

"Replacement."

Naturally.

He no longer wanted the sleeping room. He wanted the
street, although the street was another system, another painted promise,
full of signal lights that changed while one was crossing.
Still, it had weather. Real weather, perhaps—rain not selected from
a panel, wind containing dirt, spring arriving without a warranty.

"Exit," he said.

The woman pointed toward a pane of glass.

He did not trust it. The pane showed the city
and, faintly, himself; beyond it people moved below, small and
purposeful, each following a private circle. It could be a
door. It could be the last wall. He approached with
the care of an animal that has learned too much
from instruction.

No running.

No jumping.

He extended his hand, then withdrew it. The woman waited.
The house hummed through its many disguised materials, full of
artificial patience.

He stepped forward.

The glass divided soundlessly before he touched it.

For one instant relief passed through him—not faith, not knowledge,
not money or love, not poetry, property, sleep, treatment, or
any recovered door from the museum of failed entrances, merely
the small animal astonishment of not being struck.

Beyond was an escalator.

"Automatic descent," the woman said. "Street access in forty seconds."

He stepped onto it. The house lifted away behind him
while he was carried downward, standing still with excellent efficiency.
Floors passed: offices, restaurants, clinics, chapels, banks, bedrooms, laboratories, each
with its circle and its ration.

At the bottom, people received him without noticing. He joined
them because there was nowhere else to join. The moving
stair flattened itself obediently.

He put out his foot.

Before it could descend, the pavement seemed to rise and
meet it.

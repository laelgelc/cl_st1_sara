# THE TALLEST OBJECT

## A Comedy in Two Acts

### CHARACTERS

**ISABEL WARLAND**
**LUCIUS WARLAND**, her husband
**JOHN OBERVILLE**
**MRS. VANE**, Isabel's aunt
**CECIL DANE**, a young man of leisure
**PETERS**, a servant

---

## ACT I

*The drawing-room of the Warlands' villa at Newport. Tapestries, flowers,
bric-a-brac. Through long windows, a strip of lawn, the cliffs,
and an immoderate amount of sea. On a lacquered table
stands a silver tea service. There are letters beside it,
and a small French clock on the mantel.*

*ISABEL, in a pale morning dress, is pouring tea. MRS.
VANE sits near the window with a newspaper. CECIL DANE
examines a porcelain shepherdess.*

MRS. VANE. Cecil, put that down.

CECIL. I am only estimating her.

MRS. VANE. Her value?

CECIL. Her virtue. Value is what the auctioneer estimates afterward.

ISABEL. The shepherdess is safe. She has survived two revolutions,
three inheritances, and my husband's creditors.

MRS. VANE. Isabel!

ISABEL. My dear aunt?

MRS. VANE. One doesn't mention creditors at tea.

ISABEL. Why not? They provide most of the sugar.

CECIL. And all the excitement. A creditor is the only
person who calls on a married couple without wanting either
luncheon or scandal.

MRS. VANE. In my day, young men did not discuss
money before women.

ISABEL. In your day, women had money.

CECIL. And young men had manners.

ISABEL. The arrangement was extravagant. It could not last.

*LUCIUS WARLAND enters rapidly, carrying an open letter. He stops
on seeing MRS. VANE and CECIL.*

LUCIUS. Still here, Cecil?

CECIL. My dear Warland, that is not hospitality. It is
arithmetic.

LUCIUS. I thought you were sailing at twelve.

CECIL. The wind has dropped.

ISABEL. Cecil never moves without an external motive.

CECIL. Movement is so compromising. A man who goes somewhere
admits that another place is better.

LUCIUS. There is quite a breeze.

CECIL. A social breeze, perhaps. You bring it in with
you.

MRS. VANE. Has anything happened?

LUCIUS. Happened? No. That is—nothing unpleasant. On the contrary.

ISABEL. Then it must be unusual.

LUCIUS. I've had a letter from Roger Pell.

CECIL. Roger writes only when he wants a yacht, a
vote, or a woman's address.

LUCIUS. It concerns none of those things.

ISABEL. Then he has discovered friendship.

LUCIUS. It concerns Oberville.

*ISABEL's hand remains for a moment above the tea service.
She sets down the sugar tongs.*

MRS. VANE. John Oberville?

CECIL. The John Oberville?

ISABEL. How many does politics require?

CECIL. One in office, one in the newspapers, and one
to deny both.

LUCIUS. He is coming to Newport.

MRS. VANE. Here?

LUCIUS. To Newport.

ISABEL. That is less alarming.

LUCIUS. He may come here.

ISABEL. That is more definite.

MRS. VANE. After ten years!

CECIL. Ten years is an admirable period. It turns indiscretion
into history.

LUCIUS. You knew him, Aunt Vane?

MRS. VANE. Everybody knew him.

ISABEL. My aunt means that everybody watched him.

MRS. VANE. He was considered brilliant.

CECIL. He is considered powerful now. Brilliance, properly invested, becomes
influence.

LUCIUS. He practically names the men in the foreign department.

CECIL. Does he? Then I forgive him everything except his
speeches.

ISABEL. You have never read them.

CECIL. That is how I forgive them.

LUCIUS. Pell says Oberville is coming tomorrow and may remain
three days. He has taken the Murchisons' place, but the
drains are wrong or the cook has religion—something of the
sort. Pell thought we might ask him here.

ISABEL. Mr. Pell is lavish with other people's bedrooms.

MRS. VANE. We have the blue room.

ISABEL. The ceiling rains in the blue room.

LUCIUS. Only when it rains.

CECIL. A distinction beloved by landlords.

MRS. VANE. John Oberville would not mind a little discomfort.

ISABEL. Great men never mind discomfort when it belongs to
someone else.

LUCIUS. Isabel, you used to know him very well.

ISABEL. Did I?

MRS. VANE. My dear child!

ISABEL. Well, Aunt Lydia?

MRS. VANE. You were almost—

ISABEL. At twenty-one everyone is almost something. Almost beautiful, almost
ruined, almost married.

CECIL. Often all three.

LUCIUS. Pell said Oberville would be particularly glad to see
you.

ISABEL. Mr. Pell's correspondence grows indecently intimate.

LUCIUS. He merely remembered that you and Oberville had been
friends.

ISABEL. Memory has become the last refuge of people without
facts.

MRS. VANE. You were more than friends.

*A pause.*

CECIL. I begin to feel that the shepherdess and I
should withdraw.

ISABEL. On the contrary. She has heard worse, and you
would repeat it better.

LUCIUS. There is no secret, I suppose?

ISABEL. None. Secrets are truths that have not acquired good
clothes. This one has had ten years to dress.

MRS. VANE. John Oberville was deeply in love with Isabel.

LUCIUS. Deeply?

ISABEL. My aunt measures all extinct emotions by the depth
at which they are buried.

MRS. VANE. He wished to marry her.

LUCIUS. And why did he not?

ISABEL. I married you.

LUCIUS. That came later.

CECIL. Chronology is the enemy of marriage. Never examine it.

LUCIUS. Why did he give you up?

ISABEL. Mr. Oberville had an obligation.

MRS. VANE. To another woman.

LUCIUS. Another woman?

ISABEL. The sex was less surprising then.

MRS. VANE. A married woman, and in great distress.

LUCIUS. What kind of distress?

ISABEL. The married kind, presumably. It is the only distress
society recognizes and the last it relieves.

MRS. VANE. John felt bound in honor not to desert
her.

LUCIUS. He loved her?

ISABEL. He said not.

LUCIUS. But he gave you up for her?

ISABEL. So it appeared.

CECIL. An obligation without love is the most respectable form
of passion.

LUCIUS. And you accepted that?

ISABEL. I lacked a supreme court.

MRS. VANE. Isabel behaved beautifully.

ISABEL. I was young enough to mistake silence for beauty.

LUCIUS. You suffered?

ISABEL. Lucius, suffering is like a family heirloom. One may
possess it without keeping it on the drawing-room table.

LUCIUS. I only asked—

ISABEL. And I only answered.

*LUCIUS folds the letter, then unfolds it.*

LUCIUS. It is an extraordinary thing that I never heard
the whole story.

ISABEL. You never asked.

LUCIUS. A husband doesn't like to ask about the men
who preceded him.

ISABEL. He prefers to learn about them from Roger Pell.

CECIL. Roger has the historical instinct.

MRS. VANE. John went away. Isabel was ill. Then Lucius
came.

ISABEL. Like the second course.

LUCIUS. Isabel!

ISABEL. My dear, you were far more nourishing.

CECIL. And remained for the bill.

*LUCIUS gives him a sharp look.*

CECIL. I perceive the wind has risen. Mrs. Vane, shall
we inspect the cliffs?

MRS. VANE. At this hour?

CECIL. The cliffs are always at home.

ISABEL. Do go, Aunt Lydia. Cecil needs a chaperon whenever
he is alone.

MRS. VANE. I don't approve of leaving you to quarrel.

ISABEL. We never quarrel. Lucius states his position, and I
admire the scenery.

CECIL. Marriage as a musical form: theme, variation, and an
interrupted cadence.

*MRS. VANE rises.*

MRS. VANE. I hope you will ask John to dinner.

LUCIUS. Of course we shall.

ISABEL. Lucius has asked him already—in his imagination.

MRS. VANE. Wear the black spangled gown, Isabel. You look
superb in it.

ISABEL. Does one dress superbly for history?

CECIL. Only when history controls the appointments.

*CECIL offers his arm to MRS. VANE. They go out
through the windows.*

*LUCIUS waits until they are beyond hearing.*

LUCIUS. Cecil knows.

ISABEL. Cecil suspects everything. It saves time.

LUCIUS. He had no right to make that remark about
appointments.

ISABEL. Then it was clairvoyance, not gossip.

LUCIUS. I have never discussed my affairs with him.

ISABEL. Your affairs discuss themselves. They have loud voices.

LUCIUS. What do you mean by that?

ISABEL. There are three letters beside your plate which you
have not opened because you know their contents. The butcher
called yesterday in person. Mr. Ames no longer bows to
us at the Casino. And you have worn your gray
coat in sunshine for a week because the tailor has
retained the blue one.

LUCIUS. Ames is a swine.

ISABEL. He is a creditor. The distinction is chiefly one
of appetite.

LUCIUS. Things are temporarily tight.

ISABEL. Things have been temporarily tight since our wedding. Temporary
has become our oldest servant.

LUCIUS. You knew I wasn't rich.

ISABEL. I knew you had expectations.

LUCIUS. So did I.

ISABEL. Expectations are capital for poets. Tradesmen remain conservative.

LUCIUS. If your uncle had behaved decently—

ISABEL. He did. He left his money to the person
he loved best.

LUCIUS. Himself?

ISABEL. His second wife. It amounted to the same thing.

LUCIUS. And the Flushing investment ought to have doubled.

ISABEL. It did. It doubled our anxiety.

LUCIUS. You can be infernally cool when I'm trying to
save us.

ISABEL. One of us should keep a normal temperature.

*He walks to the window, returns, and taps the letter
against his palm.*

LUCIUS. There is a secretaryship vacant at Vienna.

ISABEL. Ah.

LUCIUS. Not a mere clerkship. Second secretary. The salary is
respectable, the allowances are good, and in two years—

ISABEL. In two years the creditors would learn German.

LUCIUS. It would give me a career.

ISABEL. You have always wanted a career. They are so
much easier to want than to begin.

LUCIUS. I have ability.

ISABEL. You have every ability except the ability to make
people notice the others.

LUCIUS. Oberville could make them notice.

ISABEL. Could he?

LUCIUS. One word from him would settle it. He controls
the committee, and Bellows is his man.

ISABEL. I thought Mr. Bellows belonged to the Republic.

LUCIUS. Isabel, don't be flippant. This is our chance. Our
one chance, perhaps.

ISABEL. Chances resemble heirs. The more one needs them, the
longer they live elsewhere.

LUCIUS. Oberville was—he was very fond of you.

ISABEL. Ten years ago.

LUCIUS. A man doesn't forget a woman like you.

ISABEL. You do, almost daily.

LUCIUS. That is different.

ISABEL. Marriage is always the legal name for the difference.

LUCIUS. You must know what I mean.

ISABEL. I am trying not to.

LUCIUS. If he comes here—if you receive him pleasantly—

ISABEL. I generally receive guests without violence.

LUCIUS. Not merely civilly. You might speak to him of
Vienna.

ISABEL. Before or after the soup?

LUCIUS. Damn it, Isabel!

ISABEL. The soup, then. Politics digests badly after fish.

LUCIUS. You twist everything.

ISABEL. Straight things are so often weapons.

LUCIUS. I am asking you to help your husband.

ISABEL. No. You are asking a woman to borrow from
a ghost.

LUCIUS. A ghost with enormous influence.

ISABEL. That improves his social position, not his reality.

LUCIUS. You preserved his letters.

*A pause. ISABEL looks at him.*

ISABEL. Who told you?

LUCIUS. No one. I saw the packet once, in the
Florentine box.

ISABEL. You opened it?

LUCIUS. No.

ISABEL. Why not?

LUCIUS. I am not dishonorable.

ISABEL. Only observant.

LUCIUS. They were tied with gray ribbon.

ISABEL. Camphor would have been more practical.

LUCIUS. Then you do remember him.

ISABEL. I remember the dress I wore when he left
me. That does not mean I wish to wear it
again.

LUCIUS. But you kept the letters.

ISABEL. People keep obsolete laws, dead languages, and christening cups.
The storeroom is not a temple.

LUCIUS. You loved him.

ISABEL. Yes.

LUCIUS. Do you still?

ISABEL. You are asking for an inventory of goods stored
before our marriage.

LUCIUS. Answer me.

ISABEL. Very well. I have remembered him.

LUCIUS. That is not an answer.

ISABEL. It is the only honest one. Love may die,
but memory wears its cloak. From a distance one mistakes
the outline.

LUCIUS. And if he still loves you?

ISABEL. Then he has been less industrious than I supposed.

LUCIUS. Would that disturb you?

ISABEL. It might disturb him.

LUCIUS. You evade every question.

ISABEL. Questions about old love are designed by men who
hope for either vanity or remorse. I refuse both luxuries.

LUCIUS. I am not jealous.

ISABEL. Naturally not. Jealousy is sentimental, and Vienna is practical.

LUCIUS. That is unfair.

ISABEL. It is exact.

*He throws the letter on the table.*

LUCIUS. Very well. I am practical. We are in debt.
I am thirty-eight. I have wasted years waiting for men
with less intelligence and more relations to promote me. Here
is a man who can do in an afternoon what
I have failed to do in ten years. He once
loved my wife. Am I to pretend the fact has
no value?

ISABEL. Value?

LUCIUS. Influence, then.

ISABEL. No, keep the first word. It is uglier and
therefore more sincere.

LUCIUS. I don't ask you to do anything unworthy.

ISABEL. Worthiness is elastic under financial pressure.

LUCIUS. A word. A hint. You could tell him how
much the post means to us.

ISABEL. To us?

LUCIUS. To me, if you insist.

ISABEL. I do.

LUCIUS. Is it a crime for a man to be
ambitious?

ISABEL. Not unless he employs his wife as the staircase.

LUCIUS. You make it sound vile.

ISABEL. I did not invent the sound.

*He turns away. The clock strikes one.*

LUCIUS. Pell expects an answer.

ISABEL. Give him one.

LUCIUS. Shall I ask Oberville here?

ISABEL. You have decided to.

LUCIUS. I want your consent.

ISABEL. Consent is the little ribbon husbands tie around decisions
already packed.

LUCIUS. Shall I ask him?

*A pause.*

ISABEL. Yes.

LUCIUS. Isabel—

ISABEL. Don't look grateful. It is unbecoming in a purchaser.

LUCIUS. By Jove, you can be cruel.

ISABEL. Cruelty is generally truth without upholstery.

LUCIUS. I would do as much for you.

ISABEL. You may be certain I shall never ask it.

*PETERS enters.*

PETERS. Luncheon is served, madam.

ISABEL. Tell Mrs. Vane and Mr. Dane.

PETERS. Yes, madam.

*He turns.*

LUCIUS. Peters—have a room prepared for Mr. John Oberville tomorrow.
The blue room.

PETERS. The ceiling, sir?

LUCIUS. Put a basin under it.

ISABEL. And flowers over the basin. Greatness should not see
the machinery of hospitality.

PETERS. Yes, madam.

*PETERS goes out.*

LUCIUS. You will be kind to him?

ISABEL. I shall be perfect.

LUCIUS. Not too perfect.

ISABEL. My dear Lucius, perfection is the one form of
kindness that leaves no obligation.

*She takes up the unopened letters, sorts them, and places
the largest before him.*

ISABEL. The butcher has written again.

*She goes toward the dining-room.*

LUCIUS. Isabel.

*She stops.*

LUCIUS. Wear the black gown tomorrow night.

ISABEL. Is it for Mr. Oberville or Vienna?

LUCIUS. For me.

ISABEL. Then you should have asked ten years ago.

*She goes out. LUCIUS stands with the butcher's letter in
one hand and Pell's in the other.*

*Curtain.*

---

## ACT II

*The same room, the following afternoon. The sea is gray,
and the long windows are closed. A fire burns beneath
the clock. On a chair lies ISABEL's walking cloak and
hat. The tea service has been prepared but not touched.*

*ISABEL stands fastening her gloves. MRS. VANE watches from the
sofa. CECIL is at the window.*

MRS. VANE. You cannot go out now.

ISABEL. Why not?

MRS. VANE. John may arrive at any moment.

ISABEL. That is why walking has become urgent.

CECIL. Flight loses dignity when announced.

ISABEL. I am not flying. I am taking the air.

CECIL. Air is merely flight without luggage.

MRS. VANE. It would be pointed.

ISABEL. At whom?

MRS. VANE. At John.

ISABEL. After ten years he should be able to survive
punctuation.

CECIL. Mrs. Warland, your hat is trembling.

ISABEL. The feathers are sentimental. They remember being birds.

*The clock strikes four.*

MRS. VANE. He was to come at half past three.

ISABEL. Powerful men are late. It proves they control even
the past.

CECIL. Perhaps he is detained by the nation.

ISABEL. The nation ought to send an apology.

*LUCIUS enters in traveling clothes, drawing on his gloves.*

LUCIUS. Not here?

MRS. VANE. Not yet.

LUCIUS. Extraordinary. Pell said the train reached at three.

ISABEL. Perhaps Mr. Oberville got off to govern Rhode Island.

LUCIUS. You're not going out?

ISABEL. I had formed the desperate project.

LUCIUS. But he may come.

ISABEL. Your confidence is touching.

LUCIUS. Isabel, I must speak to you.

CECIL. Again the cliffs, Mrs. Vane?

MRS. VANE. It is beginning to rain.

CECIL. Then they will be less crowded.

LUCIUS. There is no need for everyone to leave.

CECIL. My dear fellow, there is always a need. What
is rare is an excuse.

*CECIL leads MRS. VANE toward the windows.*

MRS. VANE. We shall only walk on the terrace.

ISABEL. Take an umbrella. Respectability is no protection against weather.

*MRS. VANE and CECIL go out.*

LUCIUS. I have to take the five-ten to New York.

ISABEL. I thought Washington was your destination.

LUCIUS. It is. I connect at New York tonight.

ISABEL. You said yesterday that you had abandoned the journey.

LUCIUS. I may still abandon it.

ISABEL. How adventurous.

LUCIUS. If Oberville comes before five—

ISABEL. You will ask him for the secretaryship yourself?

LUCIUS. Not outright. It requires management.

ISABEL. Then you had better leave it to a woman.
Management is the feminine name for what men call influence
when they possess it.

LUCIUS. I wish you wouldn't take this tone.

ISABEL. Which tone would secure Vienna?

LUCIUS. You know perfectly well.

ISABEL. No. Sing me the note.

LUCIUS. I am in a damned awkward position.

ISABEL. You placed yourself there with considerable elegance.

LUCIUS. If I stay, it looks as though I am
waiting to petition him. If I go, I may lose
the opportunity.

ISABEL. There is the whole masculine tragedy: to be seen
wanting what one wants.

LUCIUS. And you?

ISABEL. I shall give him tea.

LUCIUS. Nothing more?

ISABEL. Tea is often more than people deserve.

LUCIUS. Isabel—

*A carriage is heard outside. LUCIUS turns toward the door.*

LUCIUS. That must be he.

*ISABEL slowly removes one glove.*

LUCIUS. You will not go?

ISABEL. It appears the air has arrived indoors.

*PETERS enters.*

PETERS. Mr. John Oberville.

*OBERVILLE enters. He is distinguished, graying slightly, easy in manner
until he sees ISABEL. Then he stops. LUCIUS crosses to
him.*

LUCIUS. Oberville! Delighted—delighted to see you.

OBERVILLE. Warland. It is very good of you to receive
a political refugee.

LUCIUS. Refugee?

OBERVILLE. I escaped three committees, two reporters, and a lady
who wished her son made consul at Nice.

ISABEL. Was the son qualified?

OBERVILLE. He had spent a winter there.

ISABEL. In diplomacy, that is almost indecent experience.

OBERVILLE. Isabel.

ISABEL. Mr. Oberville.

*They look at each other. LUCIUS laughs a little too
quickly.*

LUCIUS. Ten years, eh? By Jove, ten years.

OBERVILLE. Is it?

ISABEL. The clock confirms nothing before four-thirty.

OBERVILLE. You have not changed.

ISABEL. That is the first untruth old friends owe one
another.

OBERVILLE. Then let me amend it. You have changed only
where change improves.

ISABEL. That is the second.

LUCIUS. Sit down, Oberville. You'll have tea? Something stronger?

OBERVILLE. Tea, if Mrs. Warland will give it to me.

ISABEL. I distribute it without political distinction.

*They sit. ISABEL pours tea.*

LUCIUS. I am unfortunately obliged to catch a train.

OBERVILLE. I heard you were going to Washington.

LUCIUS. You heard?

OBERVILLE. Pell said something. A matter of appointments, wasn't it?

LUCIUS. Oh, nothing settled. A possible opening.

ISABEL. Lucius prefers openings. Doors imply that someone may shut
them.

LUCIUS. Isabel jokes about my ambitions.

OBERVILLE. That is what wives are for. A man's ambition,
left alone, becomes a public nuisance.

ISABEL. You speak with official knowledge.

LUCIUS. The foreign department is being reorganized, I understand.

OBERVILLE. It has been reorganized every spring for twenty years.
The furniture remains exhausted.

LUCIUS. There is a vacancy at Vienna.

OBERVILLE. Bellows mentioned it.

*A pause. ISABEL hands OBERVILLE his cup.*

OBERVILLE. Thank you.

LUCIUS. I had thought of putting my name forward.

OBERVILLE. You should.

LUCIUS. You think so?

OBERVILLE. Why not?

LUCIUS. There are several candidates.

OBERVILLE. There are always several candidates and only one appointment.
It preserves our belief in choice.

ISABEL. Like marriage.

LUCIUS. I hope to see Bellows tomorrow.

OBERVILLE. Tell him I said he ought to listen to
you.

LUCIUS. My dear fellow—

OBERVILLE. It commits neither of us to anything. Bellows enjoys
listening. It gives him a reputation for caution.

LUCIUS. Still, a word from you—

*ISABEL lets the sugar tongs fall lightly against the bowl.*

ISABEL. Lucius, your train.

LUCIUS. I have twenty minutes.

ISABEL. Eighteen. The clock is slow from associating with us.

OBERVILLE. Don't let me detain you. I shall be here
tomorrow.

LUCIUS. Tomorrow?

OBERVILLE. If Mrs. Warland permits it.

ISABEL. My permission has never controlled the guest list.

LUCIUS. Of course, of course. We dine at eight.

OBERVILLE. I am pledged to the Murchisons tonight, but tomorrow—

LUCIUS. Tomorrow, certainly. We'll have some people over.

OBERVILLE. Must you?

LUCIUS. No—no, not if you prefer quiet.

*He looks at ISABEL. She lifts the teapot.*

ISABEL. Another cup, Mr. Oberville?

OBERVILLE. Presently.

LUCIUS. Well, I must be off. Isabel, may I speak
to you in the hall?

ISABEL. The hall repeats everything to Peters.

LUCIUS. Just a moment.

*ISABEL rises and goes a little apart with him.*

LUCIUS. In a low voice. He knows Bellows. You heard
him.

ISABEL. Even the teapot heard him.

LUCIUS. If he would write tonight—

ISABEL. You will miss your train.

LUCIUS. Shall I stay?

ISABEL. Certainly not.

LUCIUS. You mean that?

ISABEL. Entirely.

LUCIUS. You will—?

ISABEL. Go to Washington, Lucius.

*He searches her face, then presses her hand with sudden
warmth.*

LUCIUS. You are splendid.

*ISABEL withdraws her hand to adjust a bracelet.*

ISABEL. Your glove is unbuttoned.

*He buttons it, collecting himself, and returns to OBERVILLE.*

LUCIUS. Good-bye, Oberville. We shall continue the question of Vienna.

OBERVILLE. In Vienna, I hope.

LUCIUS. That sounds promising.

ISABEL. It sounds geographical.

LUCIUS. Until tomorrow.

*He goes out. A moment later the outer door closes.
Silence.*

OBERVILLE. Does he always leave so much energy behind him?

ISABEL. Only when he takes a train.

OBERVILLE. And is Vienna your desire?

ISABEL. Vienna is a city. I try not to desire
municipalities.

OBERVILLE. But you wish him to have the post?

ISABEL. He wishes it.

OBERVILLE. That was not my question.

ISABEL. Marriage answers many questions by changing their ownership.

*OBERVILLE sets down his cup.*

OBERVILLE. Isabel, will you take off the other glove?

ISABEL. Why?

OBERVILLE. The one bare hand makes you look as if
you had begun to forgive me and stopped.

ISABEL. I had forgotten the glove.

OBERVILLE. But not me?

ISABEL. You lose no time.

OBERVILLE. I have lost ten years.

ISABEL. Time is not lost because we dislike the investment.

OBERVILLE. May we speak seriously?

ISABEL. Seriousness is usually bad manners in the afternoon.

OBERVILLE. Then let us have bad manners.

ISABEL. Politics has accustomed you to them.

OBERVILLE. You are determined to keep me at the length
of an epigram.

ISABEL. It is a civilized distance.

OBERVILLE. You knew I would come.

ISABEL. Lucius told me.

OBERVILLE. Before that.

ISABEL. I have not spent ten years foretelling railway journeys.

OBERVILLE. I wrote twice.

ISABEL. Eight years ago.

OBERVILLE. You returned the letters.

ISABEL. They were not mine.

OBERVILLE. Everything I wrote to you was yours.

ISABEL. Then I exercised the privilege of property and disposed
of it.

OBERVILLE. You are still angry.

ISABEL. Anger is an expensive tenant. I could not afford
to keep it.

OBERVILLE. What did you keep?

*A pause.*

ISABEL. A version of the event.

OBERVILLE. And of me?

ISABEL. Naturally. Events require characters.

OBERVILLE. What sort of character was I?

ISABEL. A very tall one.

OBERVILLE. Tall?

ISABEL. You stood on the horizon. At twenty-one, distance and
height are easily confused.

OBERVILLE. And now?

ISABEL. You are in my drawing-room. Perspective has become difficult.

OBERVILLE. Isabel—

ISABEL. No. Sit down. If we must exhume the past,
let us at least remain comfortable.

*They sit. Rain touches the windows.*

OBERVILLE. I have thought of this meeting a thousand times.

ISABEL. That explains why you seem rehearsed.

OBERVILLE. I deserve that.

ISABEL. Do you? I have not yet begun to judge
you.

OBERVILLE. You judged me then.

ISABEL. I acquitted you.

OBERVILLE. Acquitted me?

ISABEL. Of loving me too little.

OBERVILLE. I loved you more than anything on earth.

ISABEL. Yes. That was the case for the defense.

OBERVILLE. You cannot doubt it.

ISABEL. I did not. You had an obligation more sacred
than happiness. You sacrificed me to honor. It was extremely
painful and very magnificent.

OBERVILLE. Don't mock it.

ISABEL. I am not mocking it. I wore that belief
through every bad season. It was a strong cloak.

OBERVILLE. You understood there was another woman.

ISABEL. A married woman who depended on you.

OBERVILLE. She did depend on me.

ISABEL. And you could not abandon her without disgracing yourself.

OBERVILLE. That is what I said.

ISABEL. It is what I believed.

OBERVILLE. I had promised to help her. Her husband was
brutal, jealous, half mad. She had no one else.

ISABEL. And so you left the woman you loved.

OBERVILLE. Yes.

ISABEL. For duty.

OBERVILLE. Isabel—

ISABEL. Say it. I have kept the word polished for
ten years.

OBERVILLE. Duty was part of it.

ISABEL. Part?

OBERVILLE. Things are never as simple as they seem afterward.

ISABEL. They are often much simpler. Afterward merely removes the
decorations.

OBERVILLE. I was twenty-nine. I had no money, no position.
Her husband threatened to publish her letters. There would have
been a scandal.

ISABEL. Scandal is the tax society places on sincerity.

OBERVILLE. It was not only scandal. He threatened you.

ISABEL. Me?

OBERVILLE. He said he would come to your mother's house.
He would tell her everything—make a scene.

ISABEL. A scene.

OBERVILLE. A horrible, vulgar scene.

ISABEL. And you wished to spare me.

OBERVILLE. Yes.

ISABEL. How noble.

OBERVILLE. Don't say it like that.

ISABEL. How shall I say it?

OBERVILLE. You don't understand the man. He was violent. He
shouted. He made threats. He once struck her before me.

ISABEL. And you believed courage consisted in leaving quietly?

OBERVILLE. I believed the situation intolerable.

ISABEL. To whom?

OBERVILLE. To all of us.

ISABEL. No. To you.

*OBERVILLE rises.*

OBERVILLE. Perhaps. Very well—perhaps to me. I dreaded it. I
dreaded his coming to your house, your mother fainting, the
servants listening, your name dragged through every club in New
York. I could face a duel, ruin, even disgrace. But
that sordid uproar—that vulgarity—I could not face it.

ISABEL. You were afraid of a scene.

OBERVILLE. Yes.

*A long pause. ISABEL looks at him, then takes off
her remaining glove and lays both gloves together on the
table.*

ISABEL. That was all?

OBERVILLE. "All"?

ISABEL. There was no oath? No helpless life entrusted to
you? No great moral debt?

OBERVILLE. There was obligation. But—

ISABEL. But chiefly a drawing-room full of raised voices.

OBERVILLE. I was a coward.

ISABEL. A damned coward?

OBERVILLE. If you like.

ISABEL. I don't like. That is the curious part.

*She rises and walks to the mantel. She touches the
clock, then turns.*

OBERVILLE. Isabel, I have accused myself every day since.

ISABEL. Have you? How industrious remorse can be when it
changes nothing.

OBERVILLE. I tried to come back.

ISABEL. After she died.

OBERVILLE. After I was free.

ISABEL. After the risk of noise was over.

OBERVILLE. You were engaged to Warland.

ISABEL. Yes.

OBERVILLE. Would you have had me break your engagement?

ISABEL. No. You had become honorable again by then.

OBERVILLE. You are merciless.

ISABEL. On the contrary. I have just pardoned you.

OBERVILLE. Pardoned me?

ISABEL. Completely.

OBERVILLE. Then why do you look at me so?

ISABEL. Because I can see you.

OBERVILLE. You could not before?

ISABEL. Not distinctly. You were a steeple. I arranged the
landscape around you. From your imaginary height I could see
farther than my life allowed. It was useful.

OBERVILLE. And now?

ISABEL. Now you are a handsome object in a well-cut
coat.

OBERVILLE. A bibelot?

ISABEL. A little large for the mantelpiece, but yes—something collected
abroad, with a history no one examines.

OBERVILLE. You loved the ideal, then. Not the man.

ISABEL. I loved the man I was given to understand
existed.

OBERVILLE. I did exist. I exist now. I have loved
you all these years.

ISABEL. You have remembered me.

OBERVILLE. Is that so little?

ISABEL. Memory is a dilettante. It enjoys the arts of
feeling without accepting the discipline.

OBERVILLE. You said you had remembered me.

ISABEL. Yes.

OBERVILLE. Then we are equally guilty.

ISABEL. No. I remembered a sacrifice. You remembered an escape.

OBERVILLE. That is cruelly clever.

ISABEL. Cleverness is what remains when faith leaves the room.

*He approaches her.*

OBERVILLE. Listen to me. Whatever I was then, I am
not that man now.

ISABEL. We are all acquitted by age.

OBERVILLE. I would risk everything now.

ISABEL. There is nothing to risk.

OBERVILLE. There is your marriage.

ISABEL. My marriage is not an adventure for visiting statesmen.

OBERVILLE. Are you happy?

ISABEL. That question is sentimental rot.

OBERVILLE. Then you are not.

ISABEL. Happiness is not evidence. Unhappy women have been known
to keep their balance, and happy ones to run away
with dancing masters.

OBERVILLE. You evade me because you feel what I feel.

ISABEL. I evade you because you have begun to repeat
yourself.

OBERVILLE. When I saw you just now, everything between us
returned.

ISABEL. Everything returned to you. Something left me.

OBERVILLE. What?

ISABEL. The past.

OBERVILLE. I don't believe you.

ISABEL. Belief has already done enough damage.

*He takes her bare hand. She allows it for an
instant, looking at their hands, then draws hers away.*

OBERVILLE. You trembled.

ISABEL. The room is cold.

OBERVILLE. Isabel—

ISABEL. Please put another log on the fire.

*He stares at her, then obeys. She returns to the
tea table and rearranges the cups.*

OBERVILLE. You can turn from this to teaspoons?

ISABEL. Teaspoons are real. It is restful.

OBERVILLE. I came here hoping—

ISABEL. Never arrive with hope. It wrinkles in travel.

OBERVILLE. I hoped at least that you had forgiven me.

ISABEL. I told you: I have.

OBERVILLE. Forgiveness without tenderness is only a superior form of
contempt.

ISABEL. Not contempt. Classification.

OBERVILLE. As a bibelot.

ISABEL. A distinguished one.

OBERVILLE. And what is Warland?

ISABEL. My husband.

OBERVILLE. That is a legal definition.

ISABEL. Legal definitions have the merit of surviving emotion.

OBERVILLE. Does he love you?

ISABEL. In the intervals of ambition.

OBERVILLE. And you love him?

ISABEL. You have no right to ask.

OBERVILLE. I surrendered the right ten years ago.

ISABEL. At last we agree.

*PETERS enters.*

PETERS. Mrs. Vane and Mr. Dane have returned, madam.

ISABEL. Ask them to wait in the library.

PETERS. Yes, madam.

ISABEL. Peters.

PETERS. Madam?

ISABEL. Mrs. Vane spoke of going to Narragansett tomorrow.

PETERS. Yes, madam.

ISABEL. Tell her the carriage may take her after breakfast.
Mr. Dane will accompany her.

PETERS. Very good, madam.

OBERVILLE. And Warland?

ISABEL. Mr. Warland has gone to Washington.

PETERS. Yes, madam.

ISABEL. We shall have no guests at luncheon except Mr.
Oberville.

*PETERS glances at her, then lowers his eyes.*

PETERS. Very good, madam.

*He goes out.*

OBERVILLE. What are you doing?

ISABEL. Arranging tomorrow.

OBERVILLE. You send the others away and ask me to
remain?

ISABEL. I have not asked you yet.

OBERVILLE. Ask me.

ISABEL. Will you lunch with me tomorrow?

OBERVILLE. Alone?

ISABEL. Unless you require a committee.

OBERVILLE. Isabel, does this mean—

ISABEL. It means that Lucius wants Vienna.

*OBERVILLE recoils slightly.*

OBERVILLE. Ah.

ISABEL. You control Mr. Bellows.

OBERVILLE. No one controls Bellows.

ISABEL. Then influence him. It is the more elegant verb.

OBERVILLE. Warland put you up to this?

ISABEL. Lucius is not subtle enough to put anyone up
to anything. He merely opens the door and looks pleased
when one discovers the staircase.

OBERVILLE. And you are willing to use—what I feel?

ISABEL. You brought it with you.

OBERVILLE. As political capital?

ISABEL. Sentiment should be invested before it depreciates.

OBERVILLE. Good God.

ISABEL. That invocation has never improved a bargain.

OBERVILLE. You despise me.

ISABEL. No. If I despised you, I could not ask
a favor.

OBERVILLE. Is that the only reason you wish to see
me tomorrow?

*A pause.*

ISABEL. It is the only reason I can safely name.

OBERVILLE. Then there is another.

ISABEL. A clever politician should never force a witness to
clarify useful ambiguity.

OBERVILLE. I will have Warland appointed.

ISABEL. You are very quick.

OBERVILLE. I have wasted ten years.

ISABEL. That phrase again.

OBERVILLE. I will do it because you ask me—not for
him.

ISABEL. Motives are the private scandal of good actions. The
appointment will look the same on paper.

OBERVILLE. And afterward?

ISABEL. There is always afterward. It is where consequences live.

OBERVILLE. May I hope?

ISABEL. You may do anything that does not require my
assistance.

*The outer door opens. LUCIUS's voice is heard in the
hall.*

LUCIUS. Peters! I missed the train.

*ISABEL and OBERVILLE look at one another. ISABEL swiftly takes
up her gloves.*

LUCIUS enters.*

LUCIUS. A broken axle on Bellevue Avenue. I thought I
could still make it, but— Well? Have I interrupted?

ISABEL. Only the past.

LUCIUS. Oberville, I'm glad you're still here. I've decided to
take the night train after all.

OBERVILLE. You should go.

LUCIUS. Should I?

OBERVILLE. Certainly. See Bellows tomorrow. He will have heard from
me.

LUCIUS. My dear Oberville!

*He grasps OBERVILLE's hand.*

OBERVILLE. Don't thank me yet.

LUCIUS. But this is— By Jove, Isabel, you did it!

ISABEL. Mr. Oberville did it.

LUCIUS. Of course, of course. But you spoke for me.

OBERVILLE. Mrs. Warland speaks very effectively.

ISABEL. Especially when I say little.

LUCIUS. I knew you would help me. I knew it.

*He turns to her, flushed with relief, and puts his
arm around her. She stands still. Then, with a slight
movement, she disengages herself and reaches for the walking cloak.*

LUCIUS. Where are you going?

ISABEL. Upstairs.

LUCIUS. In your cloak?

ISABEL. I had forgotten I was wearing it.

LUCIUS. You're cold. Your hands are like ice.

ISABEL. Mr. Oberville has put on another log.

LUCIUS. Isabel, you are magnificent.

ISABEL. You said that before.

LUCIUS. And tomorrow? Oberville will lunch with us?

ISABEL. With me. Aunt Lydia and Cecil are going to
Narragansett. You will be in Washington.

LUCIUS. Yes. Yes, naturally.

*His pleasure flickers, then returns more deliberately.*

LUCIUS. Excellent. You will have a quiet talk.

OBERVILLE. A very quiet one.

LUCIUS. There's no one like Isabel for managing these things.

ISABEL. These things have no names in polite households.

LUCIUS. You are not offended?

ISABEL. Why should success offend me?

LUCIUS. Then give me a kiss before I go.

*A pause. ISABEL draws the cloak around her.*

ISABEL. I have a headache.

LUCIUS. Poor darling. Rest before dinner.

ISABEL. I shall.

LUCIUS. And wear the black spangled gown tonight. Oberville won't
be there, of course—but wear it for me.

ISABEL. The black gown?

LUCIUS. Aunt Lydia was right. You look superb in it.

ISABEL. Very well.

*LUCIUS bends and kisses her forehead. She closes her eyes,
not moving. He turns to OBERVILLE.*

LUCIUS. I'll telegraph from Washington.

OBERVILLE. Do.

LUCIUS. Isabel, you've saved us.

ISABEL. No, Lucius. We have merely transferred the debt.

*She goes toward the door.*

OBERVILLE. Until tomorrow?

*ISABEL stops without turning.*

ISABEL. At one.

LUCIUS. At one, then. Splendid.

*ISABEL looks at the clock.*

ISABEL. The clock is slow.

*She goes out. LUCIUS moves to the tea table and
pours himself what remains in OBERVILLE's cup before noticing. He
sets it down with an embarrassed laugh.*

LUCIUS. Wrong cup.

OBERVILLE. Yes.

*The two men stand on opposite sides of the tea
table. Outside, the rain clears from the windows, and the
sea grows visible again.*

*Curtain.*

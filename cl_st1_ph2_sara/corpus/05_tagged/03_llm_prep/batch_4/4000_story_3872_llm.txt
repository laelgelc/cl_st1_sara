### The Last Pass

It was after dinner at the Brackenmoor Club, on the
first wet Saturday of October, that Clayton told us about
the ghost.

I remember the evening particularly well because I had played
thirty-six holes in a cold drizzle, had lost five pounds
to Evans through putting with quite unaccountable perversity, and had
come down to dinner in a humour so black that
even the excellent claret failed at first to improve it.
By ten o'clock, however, the rain had thickened against the
long windows, the curtains were drawn, and we had disposed
ourselves about the great fireplace in the north smoking-room with
that comfortable sense of being barricaded against weather, creditors, wives,
and all the other raw importunities of the world.

There were five of us: Clayton, Wish, Sanderson, Evans, and
myself.

Clayton lay deep in the red leather chair nearest the
fire, his long legs extended and one patent-leather shoe almost
touching the fender. He was a broad, fair man of
forty or thereabouts, clean-shaven, pleasantly florid, and endowed with an
expression of candour which was, in his case, wholly misleading.
He told lies not from malice or profit, but as
another man might whistle—in order to decorate empty air. His
inventions had a finish, a proportion, and, above all, a
social usefulness that distinguished them from common mendacity. They relieved
tedious journeys, redeemed bad dinners, and had once, during a
fog-bound week in Skye, prevented three otherwise reasonable men from
quarrelling irreparably.

Wish, who had an unfortunate respect for intimations, sat upright
beside him, warming his thin hands over the blaze. Sanderson,
the engineer, had taken possession of the lamp and was
turning the pages of a technical journal. Evans smoked a
large black cigar and occasionally made noises expressive of his
contempt for the rain, the government, and my golf.

We had been discussing the east wing.

The east wing of Brackenmoor is older than the rest
of the house and much less convenient. The club had
been a private residence until 1879, and the original proprietor,
Sir Jasper Mowbray, seems to have believed that a gentleman's
dignity increased in direct proportion to the distance between his
bed and his bath. The bedrooms there are large, draughty
chambers connected by dim passages, unexpected steps, and corners at
which one invariably encounters a table, a coal-scuttle, or another
member in insufficient clothing. The servants dislike the place. There
is a story of a housemaid who saw a grey
woman on the back staircase, but the steward attributes this
to gin, and I have always thought him right.

Clayton had slept in the east wing the previous night.

"It isn't the woman," he said suddenly.

No one had mentioned the woman for several minutes. Sanderson
looked up.

"What isn't?"

"The ghost."

Evans grunted. "Good Lord! Has the club acquired another?"

"It has," said Clayton. "A young man. Rather a poor
specimen."

Wish turned his head. "You saw something?"

"I saw a ghost."

There was a pause, followed by the kind of laughter
with which one greets a familiar performer making an early
bid for attention.

Clayton smiled indulgently.

"My dear fellows, laugh if you like. I should have
laughed yesterday. In fact, I did laugh. That was one
of the awkward parts."

"Was he hurt?" Evans asked.

"Considerably. He had expected me to be frightened."

"And you weren't?"

"Not in the least."

"That proves he wasn't a ghost," said Evans. "A ghost
that can't frighten Clayton is wasting the club's electricity."

"There was no electricity. He came by candlelight."

Wish did not laugh. "Where?"

"In my dressing-room, at about a quarter past one."

Sanderson closed his journal, keeping one finger between the pages.

"You are asserting," he said, "that last night, in this
house, you saw the apparition of a dead man?"

"I am telling you precisely that."

"And you expect us to believe it?"

"No. I expect you to listen."

This was so characteristic of him that we settled back
at once. Clayton selected a cigar from the box on
the table, examined it, clipped the end with a little
silver instrument, and held it unlighted while he began.

"I went upstairs at half-past twelve. You fellows had all
gone, and old Prendergast was asleep over the Sporting and
Dramatic. I woke him, sent him to bed, and came
up alone. The house was extraordinarily quiet. You know the
passage beyond the armour case? Well, there was a patch
of moonlight on the floor there—rather watery, owing to the
rain—and for some reason it made me think of a
pond. Nothing else happened until I reached my room.

"I undressed, put on my dressing-gown, and sat for ten
minutes over that wretched brief I brought down. Then I
heard a noise in the dressing-room."

"What sort of noise?" asked Sanderson.

"A sigh."

"Pipes," said Evans.

"It was not pipes. It was a human sigh—the sort
of sigh a man gives when he has tried a
difficult collar stud for the sixth time and dropped it
behind the washstand."

"That is unusually precise."

"I went in. The candle was on the chest of
drawers, and the window-curtains were moving a little. At first
I thought the room empty. Then I saw him beside
the cheval glass."

Clayton paused and lit his cigar. The match made a
sharp little scratch in the quiet room, and its flame
illuminated the hollows beneath his eyes. Outside, the rain rubbed
steadily against the glass.

"He was trying to haunt me," he continued. "That was
quite clear. He had raised both hands—like this—and opened his
mouth."

Clayton lifted his hands to the level of his shoulders,
fingers loosely crooked, and gave a faint, mournful utterance.

"Boo," he said.

Evans choked on his cigar. Even Wish smiled.

"It wasn't a good boo," Clayton admitted. "It had no
body in it. No conviction. One could tell at once
that he had never addressed a public meeting. I said,
'Is that the best you can do?'"

"You said that?" I asked.

"Something very like it. He gave a jump. His hands
dropped, and he looked positively ashamed.

"That was when I observed him properly. He was a
youngish chap—twenty-six, perhaps—with a narrow chest, sloping shoulders, and ears
that projected rather painfully. His hair was thin and damp-looking,
brushed across his forehead. He wore a short coat buttoned
too high and trousers baggy at the knees. Everything about
him suggested inferior lodgings and insufficient luncheon. He was not
white, as ghosts are supposed to be, but a sort
of diluted grey, and I could see the brass handle
of the wardrobe through the lower part of his waistcoat.

"That settled the matter."

"What matter?" said Evans.

"That he was a ghost."

"You might have been drunk."

"I had taken less wine than you."

"You might both have been drunk."

"The wardrobe handle was perfectly steady," said Clayton. "Besides, there
is a moral advantage in dealing with a transparent person.
He cannot, you know, conceal anything."

Sanderson frowned. "Did he cast a shadow?"

"I didn't look. This was not an examination for the
Royal Society. He was there, and he was unhappy. Those
were the immediate facts."

"Did he speak?"

"After a little prompting. I said, 'Well?' He said, 'I
beg your pardon, sir.'"

The deferential "sir," as Clayton gave it, was so weak
and breathless that we could almost hear the unfortunate apparition.

"I asked what he was doing in my dressing-room. He
said he had come to haunt the house. I told
him the house was a private club and that he
could not simply come in and haunt it without being
introduced. That upset him still further.

"'I didn't know it was a club, sir,' he said.
'It wasn't when I was alive.'

"'That is hardly our fault,' I said.

"He rubbed his hands together—though whether from nervousness or cold
I couldn't determine—and looked towards the door. 'If you would
kindly allow me, sir, I'll go.'

"'By all means.'

"He moved into the bedroom. I followed. He crossed to
the fireplace, raised his arms, hesitated, lowered them, and looked
about as if he had forgotten something.

"'Well?' I said.

"'I'm afraid, sir—'

"'What are you afraid of?'

"'I can't get back.'"

Clayton removed his cigar and considered the ash.

"He said it so miserably that I nearly laughed again.
One doesn't expect a ghost to be stranded. Yet there
he was, very much in the position of a fellow
who has missed the last train and hasn't the price
of a bed.

"'What do you mean, you can't get back?'

"'I've forgotten the passes, sir.'

"'The what?'

"'The movements. You have to make them in the proper
order. I knew them when I came, but seeing you—seeing
you so sudden, sir—and then you spoke rather sharp—I lost
the middle part.'

"He began making motions with his hands. Nothing resulted. He
tried again, and this time one of his elbows passed
through the bedpost. That seemed to disturb him badly.

"'This is ridiculous,' I said. 'Surely you can walk out
as you came in?'

"'No, sir. I came through.'

"'Through what?'

"'Through the other side, sir.'

"'Then go back through it.'

"'You can't without the signs.'

"'But you know some of them?'

"'The beginning and nearly all the end. It's the turn
after the crossing that I can't bring to mind.'

"Then, without warning, he sat down on the edge of
my bed and began to cry."

"Did the bed sink?" Sanderson asked.

"No."

"Then he didn't sit on it."

"He adopted the posture of sitting. Don't be pedantic."

"A posture has mechanical implications."

"Your friendship has mechanical implications," said Clayton, "but I tolerate
it."

Wish leaned closer to the fire. "Go on."

"It was a wretched spectacle. He put his face in
his hands, but his fingers went faintly into his cheeks,
which made the effect worse rather than better. I asked
him his name.

"'Parker, sir. George Parker.'

"'And how long have you been dead?'

"'Nine years, sir. Or ten. Time is different.'

"'What did you die of?'

"He hesitated. 'Weakness, I believe.'

"That seemed probable. I gave him a few minutes, then
asked why he had come haunting at all. At this
he became very confused.

"'They said I ought, sir.'

"'Who said?'

"'The others.'

"'What others?'

"'The ones where I am now. They all have places
they go to. Houses and ruins and roads and such.
They asked where I went, and when I said nowhere
they laughed. I thought, as I had once been in
this house—when Mrs. Bullock kept the school—I might manage it
here.'

"He had been, it appeared, an assistant master in a
small commercial academy that occupied Brackenmoor before the estate was
sold to us. He taught handwriting, elementary bookkeeping, and a
little French. Not good French, he admitted.

"The boys bullied him. The headmistress underpaid him. A music
mistress to whom he had been engaged married a traveller
in dental instruments. Afterwards he became clerk to a wholesale
stationer in Holborn, where he spent five years copying invoices.
He lived in a room over a fried-fish shop and
died during a summer of bronchitis because he was too
timid to tell his landlady that the window did not
shut.

"'I never did anything properly, sir,' he said. 'Not even
dying. The doctor said on the Tuesday that I was
out of danger, and I died on the Wednesday. It
made him look foolish, and he was vexed with me.'

"I asked him whether death had improved matters.

"'Not especially, sir.'

"'No celestial choirs?'

"'I haven't heard any.'

"'No fires?'

"'There are some warm places, but I don't belong to
them.'

"He could not explain his present condition. He spoke of
corridors, waiting places, and a great many people who seemed
always to be going somewhere else. There were rules, but
no one had given him a complete copy. Even among
the dead, poor Parker had contrived to miss the proper
office hours.

"And now, having attempted the one bold act of his
existence, he was trapped in my bedroom because I had
startled the ritual out of his head."

Clayton's tone had lost its banter. He stared into the
fire, and we, who had begun by expecting a diversion,
found ourselves listening with an attention that no mere liar
should command.

"Why didn't he remain?" I asked. "If he had nowhere
particular to go?"

"He said daylight would be very bad for him. He
didn't know exactly what it would do, which naturally made
it worse. Also, he feared the servants. He had always
been afraid of servants. So I said I would help
him."

Wish drew in his breath.

"You tried the gestures?"

"Of course."

"You ought not to have done that."

"My dear Wish, he was weeping on my bed."

"You should have rung."

"For whom? The night porter? Can you imagine Baines confronted
with an unregistered spirit in Number Seventeen? He would have
asked to see its card."

"What were the gestures?" Sanderson said.

"Complicated."

"Describe them."

"I'll come to that."

Clayton drank from the glass at his elbow.

"We began with what Parker remembered. First, the hands were
held before the face, palms inward. Then they were separated,
turned, and brought down across the breast. The right hand
described a circle; the left passed through it; both were
carried behind the shoulders and returned with the fingers interlaced.
There followed a movement like winding thread, except that the
wrists had to reverse at the top. Then came the
portion he had forgotten.

"He tried a dozen variations. Sometimes his outline grew fainter;
sometimes the candle flames stretched towards him and burned blue.
Once the mirror clouded from within and showed, not the
room, but a dark passage with a little point of
light at the far end. Parker gave a squeal and
lost the sequence entirely.

"I made him rest. Then I took him through it
slowly, as one might teach a child a drill. He
had no memory in his muscles—perhaps ghosts haven't muscles—and every
fresh attempt displaced something he had previously learned. If he
got the circle, he forgot the crossing. If he remembered
the crossing, he put the wrong thumb uppermost. It was
jolly awkward.

"At about two the landing clock struck, and he nearly
dissolved with fright. I gave him a whisky, but it
fell through him and made a damp patch on the
carpet."

Evans laughed abruptly. "Now you've overdone it."

"I wondered when you would discover that."

"Whisky through a ghost! My dear chap."

"It was my whisky. I had every right."

"Did he become drunk from the ankles upward?"

"He became apologetic. That was worse.

"By half-past two I had mastered all he could show
me. The missing piece remained. I told him to stop
trying to remember the motion and remember instead how it
felt.

"He said it felt like opening a door that was
inside oneself.

"'Which way did the door open?'

"'Both ways, sir.'

"'Did you push or pull?'

"'I think I folded.'

"That gave me the notion. His hands had been working
independently; perhaps they ought to mirror one another. I made
him repeat the approach and then turn the left wrist
inward while the right turned outward. Something happened."

Clayton's voice became very quiet.

"The room altered."

No one interrupted him.

"I do not say it grew dark. The candle was
still burning. The furniture remained where it had been. Yet
everything seemed removed—not farther away in distance, but less attached
to me. The bed, the washstand, my coat over the
chair: all became for an instant no more significant than
objects seen through the windows of a passing train. Behind
them—or through them—was an immeasurable blackness. And Parker stood at
the edge of it.

"He cried out, 'That's nearly it, sir!'

"Then it was gone.

"We tried the turn again. The effect returned, but less
strongly. On the fourth attempt Parker remembered the next movement.
It was a curious double loop of the fingers, followed
by a sweep downward and a sudden lifting of the
head.

"After that he knew the remainder.

"He stood in the middle of the room. The candles
flared so high that their flames seemed astonished. He began
the whole sequence.

"This time he did not falter. His poor narrow face
changed. For the first time since I had seen him
he looked capable—not strong, precisely, but intent. His hands moved
faster, crossed, opened, turned. At the inward fold, the walls
seemed to recede. The mirror went black. He made the
double loop, swept his arms down, and lifted his head.

"Then he smiled.

"It was not much of a smile, but it transformed
him. One saw the boy he might have been before
people had informed him of his deficiencies.

"'Thank you, sir,' he said.

"He brought his hands together, twisted them apart, and—went."

"Vanished?" said Evans.

"Vanished."

"Like smoke?"

"No."

"Faded?"

"No. He was there, and then he was not. The
candle flames dropped. The mirror reflected the room. The wet
patch from the whisky remained on the carpet."

Clayton leaned back.

"That is the whole story."

For several seconds there was no sound but the fire
and the rain. Then Evans shook himself and laughed.

"Capital! Your best this year."

"I knew you would say that."

"Your Parker is delightful. The touch about the doctor being
vexed—excellent."

"That was his phrase."

"And the wet whisky!"

"That was mine."

"Exactly."

Wish had not moved. "Did you sleep afterwards?"

"For an hour."

"Any dreams?"

"No."

"Headache?"

"No."

"Have you told anyone else?"

"I mentioned the stain to the steward."

"I mean the ghost."

"Not until now."

Sanderson had taken off his spectacles and was polishing them
thoughtfully.

"Those gestures," he said. "You are certain of the sequence?"

"I think so."

"Could you repeat it?"

Clayton smiled. "I spent two hours learning it."

"Then show us."

Wish swung round. "No."

The word came so sharply that we all looked at
him.

"Why not?" asked Evans.

"Because we don't know what it is."

"We know what it is. It is Clayton's after-dinner entertainment."

"Then let it remain an entertainment."

Clayton raised his eyebrows. "You believe me?"

"I don't know whether I believe you. That's not the
point."

"It seems very much the point."

Wish stood up and went to the window, though the
curtains prevented him from seeing anything. He was the least
robust of us and was sometimes mocked for attaching importance
to dreams, coincidences, and those small animal apprehensions that stronger
men dismiss until they prove useful.

"I dislike the thing," he said. "I disliked it when
you described the room changing. You said the movements opened
a door."

"Parker said that."

"And you propose to open it here."

Evans blew smoke towards the ceiling. "If every idiot who
waved his hands could open eternity, Parliament would have disappeared
long ago."

Sanderson ignored him. "Show me the beginning only."

Clayton rose.

He was a big man, and his standing altered the
scale of the room. He moved away from the chairs
to the clear space before the hearthrug, placed his cigar
on the mantelpiece, and faced us with a trace of
his habitual theatrical solemnity.

"Observe," he said. "The palms inward."

He lifted his hands before his face.

"Then separate—turn—down across the breast. The right hand circles. The
left goes through."

His movements were at first faintly absurd. Evans watched with
a grin. I felt the embarrassment one feels when a
friend attempts conjuring too near one's chair.

Clayton carried his hands behind his shoulders and returned them,
fingers linked.

"Now the winding movement."

"Stop," said Sanderson.

Clayton stopped.

"What?"

"Your left thumb."

"What about it?"

"It should be under, not over."

"That was Parker's difficulty."

"Do it again."

Clayton repeated the movement.

Sanderson stood and came closer. "Where did you get this?"

"I have told you."

"No, I mean the pattern. These aren't arbitrary motions."

Evans groaned. "Not you too."

Sanderson frowned at him. "I have seen part of this
sequence before."

"Where?" I asked.

"In Prague, eleven years ago. A lodge attached to one
of the old operative fraternities. Not regular Masonry, exactly—an eccentric
survival, full of alchemical rubbish. They had a series of
symbolic passes representing death, dismemberment, and restoration."

Wish turned from the curtains.

"You recognised it?"

"The circle and crossing, yes. Also the return behind the
shoulders."

"What did it do?" said Evans.

"Nothing. It was ceremonial."

"That's better."

"But Clayton has omitted a turn."

Clayton's interest quickened. "Where?"

"After the inward fold, I think. Show me up to
that point."

"No," said Wish again.

Sanderson glanced at him. "My dear fellow, a resemblance between
hand movements proves very little."

"It proves enough."

"It proves that Clayton has heard or seen something and
forgotten it."

"I have never been in Prague," said Clayton.

"Then you saw it elsewhere."

"Last night."

Evans slapped the arm of his chair. "Come, Sanderson, don't
spoil a good lie by believing half of it."

But Sanderson was absorbed. He had the defect of the
technical mind: once presented with an incomplete mechanism, he could
no more leave it alone than a terrier could ignore
a hole.

"Begin again," he said.

Clayton did so. Sanderson watched his wrists.

"There. At the fold. The right elbow must drop while
the left rises. Then the hands exchange planes without uncrossing."

He demonstrated.

It was a small thing—a twist, almost ugly in its
awkwardness—but Clayton uttered an exclamation.

"That's it!"

"Are you certain?"

"I had it wrong. Parker had it wrong until the
final attempt, but that is exactly what he did."

Wish came quickly across the room. "You've shown enough."

"On the contrary," said Clayton, delighted now, "we have solved
it."

"There was nothing to solve."

"There was a missing turn. Sanderson supplied it independently."

"Not independently. You prompted him."

"I did not know it myself."

"You knew something like it."

"Wish," Evans said, "you are behaving like an old nurse."

"I don't care how I'm behaving."

His pallor and earnestness checked our amusement. Even Clayton looked
at him with some concern.

"What do you think will happen?" I asked.

Wish rubbed his forehead.

"I don't know. That is precisely why he mustn't finish
it. Suppose the story is true."

"It isn't," said Evans.

"Suppose it is."

"Then Clayton waves himself into the spirit world, sees poor
Parker, and comes back before breakfast."

"Can he come back?"

The question fell among us unpleasantly.

Clayton recovered first. "Parker came."

"Parker was dead."

"Well, I am not."

"Then perhaps the passage will kill you."

Clayton laughed, but it was not his easiest laugh. "My
dear Wish, a man cannot die from putting his wrists
in the wrong position."

"The right position," Wish said. "That is what I'm afraid
of."

Sanderson returned to his chair and put on his spectacles.

"There is no physiological danger in the movements as far
as I can see. Unless Clayton intends to hold his
breath."

"I intend nothing but to demonstrate."

"Then don't," said Wish.

Clayton looked around the circle. He had always been unable
to resist a challenge, particularly when fear and an audience
were combined. Had we laughed unanimously, he might have abandoned
the business. Wish's opposition gave it consequence.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I have been accused for twenty years
of decorating the truth. Last night I encountered the one
witness who, being transparent, was constitutionally incapable of concealment. You
now ask me to suppress his evidence."

"We ask you not to play the fool," said Wish.

"An entirely different matter."

Clayton retrieved his cigar, found it had gone out, and
relit it. This commonplace operation seemed to restore the room.
Sanderson reopened his journal, though he did not read. Evans
stretched his legs. The rain had softened, and somewhere in
the house a door closed with a padded thud.

I confess that at this stage I believed Clayton's tale
to be an unusually elaborate invention founded upon some half-remembered
ritual. Sanderson's recognition impressed me, but coincidence, suggestion, and Clayton's
extraordinary power of leading a listener seemed explanation enough. Wish's
alarm struck me as excessive and, because it was excessive,
contagious. I wished Clayton would sit down. I also wished
very much to see the completed passes.

That conflict, I think, existed in all of us except
Wish.

Clayton stepped back into the open space.

"One performance," he said. "Then we shall have whisky and
recover our common sense."

Wish caught his sleeve.

"I mean it, Clayton."

"So do I."

"You said the room changed."

"A trick of candlelight and fatigue."

"You said Parker vanished."

"I said what I saw."

"And now you pretend not to believe yourself."

For an instant their eyes met. Clayton's expression softened.

"If there is anything in it," he said, "I shall
stop before the end."

"How will you know the end until you reach it?"

"Parker made a final movement—like casting something away. I won't
make that."

"You promise?"

"I promise."

Wish released him, though reluctantly.

Clayton faced the fireplace. His shadow, thrown by the lamp,
lay large and distorted across the oak panelling. The room
was warm, but I became conscious of a thread of
cold air moving along the carpet.

"Ready?" he said.

"No," said Wish.

Clayton began.

The first gestures were slow and explanatory.

"Palms inward. Separate. Turn. Cross the breast."

His right hand made the circle, his left passed through
it. He carried both arms back, interlaced the fingers, and
brought them forward.

"Left thumb under," said Sanderson.

"Yes."

The winding motion followed. Clayton's wrists revolved with increasing smoothness.
The mockery had gone out of his face; he was
concentrating.

"Now the fold."

His palms approached, one inward, one outward. His right elbow
sank. His left rose. For a moment his hands seemed
hopelessly entangled; then they changed places without separating.

Sanderson whispered, "Correct."

A coal settled in the grate.

The lamp flame gave a small leap.

Clayton paused.

"That," he said, "is where it began."

"Enough," said Wish.

Clayton did not answer. His eyes were fixed, not on
his hands, but on something beyond them.

"Do you see anything?" I asked.

"No. Nothing."

Yet he spoke as a man who sees.

He resumed.

His fingers made the double loop. The movement was swift
and curiously repellent, like the knotting of pale insects. His
arms swept down. He lifted his head.

At once I experienced a sensation for which I can
offer no adequate description. It may have been no more
than expectation acting upon the nerves. The room did not
disappear; the chairs, the table, the lamp, and the red
curtains remained distinct. But their solidity became doubtful. The oak
walls seemed suddenly thin, the fire remote, and my companions
no longer substantial men but bright arrangements upon the surface
of a darkness without depth or boundary.

It lasted less than a second.

Evans said, "Did you feel that?"

No one answered.

Clayton stood perfectly still. His face was pale.

"Stop now," Wish said.

Clayton breathed deeply and smiled.

"There's only the ending."

"You promised."

"I know."

"Then sit down."

Clayton lowered his arms. The room became wholly itself again.
I heard Sanderson release his breath. Evans reached for the
decanter with an unsteady hand.

"There," Wish said. "It's over."

Clayton looked at his fingers as though they belonged to
someone else.

"I should like to know," he murmured, "whether Parker described
it rightly."

"No."

"Whether it is a door."

"No, Clayton."

"A man has so few opportunities to test a thing
absolutely."

Wish stepped between him and the fire. "You gave your
word."

That stopped him. Clayton blinked, gave a rueful shrug, and
turned towards his chair.

Then Sanderson spoke.

"You have still omitted the outward reversal."

It was an involuntary remark, the protest of accuracy against
incompleteness. I saw him regret it before the words were
finished.

Clayton turned back.

"The outward reversal?"

"Nothing. Sit down."

"After the sweep?"

"I was mistaken."

"You weren't."

"Clayton—"

But Clayton had already lifted his hands.

Wish seized his right wrist. Evans sprang up. For a
ridiculous instant the three of them stood tangled together before
the hearth, like men engaged in some undignified dance.

"Let go," Clayton said.

"You shan't do it."

"Don't be an ass."

"I won't let you."

Clayton was much the stronger. He disengaged himself, not violently
but with a decisive twist, and stepped away. Wish stumbled
against the fender. I moved towards Clayton, though whether to
prevent or assist him I cannot now say.

He raised his arms once more.

There was no smile on his face. The performer had
vanished. He looked grave, almost exalted, and younger than I
had ever seen him.

"Only the reversal," he said. "Then the final cast."

"Clayton!" Wish cried.

Clayton made the reversal.

His hands swept outward, turned sharply, and crossed above his
head. The lamp flame narrowed to a blue point. The
fire gave no light. I saw Clayton's features illuminated from
below by a pallor that was not the reflection of
anything in the room.

He brought his hands down.

For one instant he hesitated.

Then, whether from bravado, compulsion, confusion, or some impulse beyond
these, he performed the final gesture.

He opened his hands and cast them apart.

His face changed.

I do not mean that it became distorted. There was
no convulsion, no theatrical horror. Rather, the animation went from
it all at once, as lights go out in a
house when the current fails. His eyes remained open. His
mouth formed a little sound of surprise.

He stood so for perhaps two seconds.

Then he collapsed.

Evans caught one shoulder, and I caught the other, but
his weight dragged us down. We laid him on the
hearthrug. Sanderson pushed the table aside. Wish knelt near Clayton's
head, repeating his name.

"Clayton! Clayton! Good God, answer me!"

There was no answer.

Evans had some medical training from his army days. He
loosened Clayton's collar, felt for the pulse, and put his
ear against the chest. Sanderson rang the bell until the
wire must nearly have broken. The steward came, followed by
a footman, and within five minutes the smoking-room, so secure
and masculine and ordinary a little while before, was full
of frightened service: cushions, brandy, hot water, whispered instructions, and
useless haste.

The doctor arrived from the village in twenty-three minutes.

Clayton was dead before he entered the house.

The official account was simple. The doctor found evidence of
an unsuspected weakness of the heart and judged that excitement,
combined perhaps with the heavy dinner and cigar, had produced
a fatal seizure. The coroner accepted this. Evans said that
such deaths were rare but by no means inexplicable. Sanderson
remarked that muscular strain and breath retention might have contributed,
though Clayton had shown no visible strain. The newspapers gave
the affair three lines: "Sudden Death at a Country Club."

No mention was made of George Parker, formerly writing master,
clerk to a stationer, and unsuccessful ghost.

We did not speak of him before the servants or
the authorities. Indeed, during the next day we scarcely spoke
of him among ourselves. Evans became aggressively practical. Sanderson left
before luncheon, pleading business. Wish remained in his room until
the afternoon and thereafter would never enter the north smoking-room
after dark.

As for me, I state only what I saw and
heard.

I saw Clayton perform a sequence of gestures which he
claimed to have learned from an apparition. I heard Sanderson
identify part of that sequence and supply a detail Clayton
had not shown him. I experienced, while the movements were
made, a momentary alteration in my sense of the room—an
effect which may readily be attributed to suggestion. I saw
Clayton complete the final pass and die at that exact
instant.

I did not see him vanish.

His body was buried four days later beneath a wet
October sky, and there can be no reasonable doubt that
it lies still in the family ground at Wexbury.

Yet reason, though admirable at arranging facts, is sometimes less
competent with their timing.

Years have passed since that night. Brackenmoor has changed. The
east wing now has electric light, central heating, and bathrooms
that would have astonished Sir Jasper. Evans is dead; Sanderson
lives mostly abroad; and Wish resigned from the club without
ever returning. The north smoking-room has been redecorated in a
pale modern fashion I dislike. Only the oak panelling remains,
and on winter evenings the fire still throws a man's
shadow enormously across it.

I have sometimes tried to reconstruct the gestures.

Never all of them. Never the inward fold. Never the
final cast.

Even in recollection there comes a point where my hands
refuse to continue, and I hear Wish crying out across
the room. Then I see again Clayton's face at the
last instant—not frightened, not suffering, but astonished, as though a
door had opened where no door should be, and beyond
it stood some timid, grateful figure waiting to receive him.

The coroner's explanation was undoubtedly sufficient.

But the coincidence was black and amazing.

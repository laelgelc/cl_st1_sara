## The Purple Remedy

Mr. Coombes went out of his house on Sunday afternoon
with the settled conviction that he would never enter it
again. He slammed the shop door so violently that a
pyramid of tins in the window trembled, and one of
the cards marked THIS LINE ESPECIALLY RECOMMENDED fell face downwards.
Then, after looking back to make sure the slam had
been observed, he crossed the road, turned the corner by
the Congregational chapel, and walked towards the pine woods.

He had no hat.

This omission, trivial to a careless mind, indicates the extremity
of his condition. Mr. Coombes was not a man who
went hatless. On Sundays especially he wore a silk hat
of a hard, brownish black, whose gloss survived chiefly beneath
the brim, and he regarded uncovered heads as evidence of
atheism, insolvency, or drink. Moreover, his frock-coat was buttoned awry,
one bright glove was on his hand and the other
clenched within it, and his military-looking collar had reddened his
neck almost to strangulation. That he should present himself thus
to the October afternoon proves beyond dispute that some very
serious disturbance had occurred.

The disturbance was marriage, assisted by visitors.

It is commonly said that a bachelor cannot understand marriage,
and it may be added that a married man understands
it only when it is too late to profit by
the information. Mr. Coombes, before his wedding, had imagined a
wife as a creature who would sit beside the till
during business hours, admire his judgment concerning stock, prepare punctual
meals, and spend the evenings in grateful appreciation of the
home he had supplied. Mrs. Coombes, on the other hand,
had imagined a husband as a person who earned money
without discussing how, handed it over without inquiry, and exhibited
cheerfulness before her friends. These ideals were not in themselves
unreasonable. Their simultaneous fulfilment was unfortunately impossible.

The shop had been doing badly. It occupied a corner
calculated by the house agent to "command two thoroughfares," but
the thoroughfares had proved singularly disobedient. A larger establishment had
opened near the station, with plate-glass windows, electric lighting, and
packets tied with blue ribbon; and customers who had once
bought a pound of tea from Coombes now passed his
door carrying parcels whose very shape insulted him. Capital disappeared
into rent, rates, stale biscuits, cracked jars, soap of an
unpopular scent, and small debts incurred by persons who always
promised payment on Saturday. Respectability remained expensive while profit became
conjectural.

Mrs. Coombes, meanwhile, had a cousin named Jennie, and Jennie
had a young man named Clarence.

Clarence was employed in a warehouse, though his cuffs and
conversation suggested that he merely visited it to give advice.
He wore narrow shoes, a pale tie secured by a
metallic horseshoe, and hair divided with such precision that each
half appeared to distrust the other. He played the piano
with an emphatic left hand, smoked cigarettes in a holder,
and used the word "smart" concerning clothes, people, furniture, and
conduct. Jennie admired him deeply. Mrs. Coombes admired Jennie's admiration.
Mr. Coombes did not admire Clarence at all.

On the Sunday in question Clarence and Jennie arrived before
dinner and stayed after it. Dinner itself was twenty-five minutes
late, the potatoes were hard, and the mutton, owing to
a dispute in the kitchen, had passed from underdone to
memorable. Mr. Coombes had made three remarks about the value
of punctuality. Mrs. Coombes had replied that if he wanted
a cook he ought to earn enough to keep one.
Jennie had laughed. Clarence had said, "That's one for you,
old man," though he was neither related to Coombes nor
appreciably younger.

After dinner the visitors went into the little parlour and
began what Clarence called "a bit of life." The piano
stood against the wall separating the parlour from the shop,
and every chord disturbed something saleable. A selection from a
popular musical comedy caused the glass pendants of the gas
bracket to tinkle; a comic song about a duchess shook
dust from a row of patent medicines; and when Jennie
attempted the chorus of "Turn, Turn, Turn, My Love," three
customers paused outside, peered in, and went away smiling.

Coombes entered the parlour during the second repetition.

"I suppose," he said, "you don't mind if the 'ole
neighbourhood 'ears you?"

Clarence, who was seated sideways at the piano, turned with
patient elegance. "Let 'em 'ear. Music improves the lower orders."

"This is a business 'ouse."

"Sunday, isn't it?"

"Sunday don't mean Bedlam."

Mrs. Coombes was in the arm-chair, her feet on the
fender. "Now don't begin, Coombes."

"I'm not beginning. I'm simply saying a man's got a
right to quiet in 'is own 'ouse."

"You've been saying it all day."

"Because I 'aven't 'ad it all day."

Jennie, who had been turning the pages for Clarence, tossed
her head. "Some people don't know how to enjoy themselves."

"Enjoy themselves!" said Coombes. The phrase struck him as monstrously
significant. "Enjoyment's all very well for them as can afford
it."

Clarence played three soft notes, expressive of social weariness.

"There," said Coombes. "That! I ask 'im civil to leave
off, and 'e goes on."

"I was merely finishing the chord."

"You've been finishing chords since 'alf-past two."

"Coombes," said his wife, "don't make yourself ridiculous before company."

There are men who can endure loss, fatigue, and physical
pain, yet become desperate when accused of making themselves ridiculous.
Mr. Coombes was one. He looked from his wife to
Jennie, from Jennie to Clarence, and from Clarence to the
cheap Japanese fan over the mantelpiece, which seemed, by its
painted grin, to have joined the opposition.

"Company!" he said. "Company that eats my dinner, drinks my
tea, uses my coal, spoils my Sunday, and then insults
me!"

Clarence rose. "If that's meant for me—"

"It is meant for you."

"Then I must say—"

"You must say nothing. You must get out."

Jennie gave a shrill cry. Mrs. Coombes sprang up.

"Clarence shan't be turned out!"

"He shall."

"He was invited."

"Not by me."

"It's my house as much as yours."

"It's my rent."

"Rent!" cried Mrs. Coombes. "And who helps you in the
shop? Who goes without a decent dress? Who has to
explain why there's never a shilling in the place? You
talk as if you were a gentleman with thousands a
year instead of a little shopkeeper who can't make his
business pay."

That, as the reader will perceive, was the decisive wound.
To call a shopkeeper a shopkeeper may be mere description,
but to put "little" before it and commercial failure after
it is an operation resembling vivisection.

Coombes became white.

"Very well," he said.

His wife, flushed by success, continued. "Yes, very well. If
you treated people properly, perhaps they'd buy here. But you
go about looking as if every customer had stolen something.
You grumble over the housekeeping, you grumble over the gas,
you grumble if Jennie comes, and you grumble if I
so much as laugh."

"I see," said Coombes. "I'm not wanted."

"Oh, don't talk nonsense."

"I'm not wanted in my own 'ouse."

"No one said that."

"You've said enough."

Clarence cleared his throat. "Look here, old man, no need
to make a tragedy—"

Coombes turned upon him. "You keep out of it."

Then, with a dignity considerably impaired by his catching the
tail of his coat on the door handle, he left
the parlour, passed through the shop, and went out bareheaded
into the street.

For a moment there was silence.

"Well!" said Jennie.

"The man's impossible," said Mrs. Coombes, though her voice was
less certain than before.

Clarence adjusted his cuffs. "Bit highly strung. Business worry, probably."

"He's always like this," Mrs. Coombes said. "If I ask
anyone in, he behaves as if they've come to rob
the till. You wouldn't believe what I put up with."

Jennie kissed her. Clarence resumed his seat at the piano.
Presently, after a proper interval of sympathy, they began the
chorus again.

"Turn, turn, turn, my love—"

Coombes heard it faintly as he passed the last houses.

He walked quickly, nourishing despair. His imagination, released from its
habitual confinement among invoices and grocery jars, became magnificently active.
He saw his business ruined, his credit stopped, his shop
sold, and Clarence installed in the parlour wearing his dressing-gown.
He saw his wife laughing over his memory. He saw
himself discovered dead beneath a tree, and the whole neighbourhood
suddenly conscious of his value.

There would be an inquest.

Mrs. Coombes would be brought forward, pale and trembling. The
coroner would ask severe questions. Clarence would attempt his warehouse
manner, but the jury would see through him. Jennie would
sob. Perhaps a letter would be found in Coombes's pocket—a
restrained, manly letter, blaming nobody by name but making everything
perfectly clear. There might be a paragraph in the local
paper: TRAGIC END OF A RESPECTED TRADESMAN. His creditors, too
late, would regret their harshness.

He considered methods. There was water, but he could swim.
There were trains, but the spectacle was objectionable and might
inconvenience innocent passengers. A razor possessed a dreadful intimacy. Poison
was more dignified, though he knew, as a vendor of
household necessities, that most readily available substances produced either vomiting
or stains. A pistol would be decisive, but pistols cost
money, and it seemed unreasonable to increase one's liabilities merely
to escape them. Rope, gas, laudanum, arsenic, a fall from
the railway bridge—he examined them all with the conscientiousness of
a man comparing wholesale prices.

The road narrowed between wet hedges and entered the pine
wood.

It had rained during the night. The earth was dark
and soft, the bracken rusty, and every branch carried a
cold brightness. The air smelt of resin, dead leaves, and
those secret processes by which nature, with no regard for
respectability, converts the dead into nourishment for the living. Under
the pines, fungi had appeared in extraordinary abundance.

Coombes noticed them because he had nowhere else to look.

There were brown cups full of rain, yellow knobs pushing
through the needles, white umbrellas delicate as porcelain, livid shelves
fastened to rotten trunks, and scarlet caps spotted with cream.
Some were slimy, some woolly, some cracked like old leather.
One species resembled a cluster of ears listening at the
roots of a tree. Another exhaled an odour of bad
fish. The whole colony had the crowded, clandestine appearance of
a market conducted by poisonous vegetables.

"Deadly, some of 'em," said Coombes.

The word interested him.

He left the path and stooped over a broad agaric
of a rich purple colour. Its pileus was smooth at
the centre and faintly fibrous towards the margin; beneath it
the gills were pale, close-set, and bruised to violet where
his thumb pressed them. The stalk was thick and unhealthy-looking,
with a swollen base half buried in black mould. There
were three smaller specimens nearby, like children attending a disreputable
parent.

Coombes had heard that bright fungi were poisonous. This one
was very bright. It also smelt peculiarly offensive—not strongly, but
with a sweet, intimate foulness that seemed to enter the
throat rather than the nostrils.

"Providence," he whispered.

It is unnecessary to examine the theology of this conclusion.
Men in distress commonly attribute to Providence any convenient object
they would condemn in calmer circumstances.

He plucked the fungus and sat upon a fallen trunk.
The thought of eating it produced nausea, but nausea appeared
a small matter beside the inquest, the newspaper paragraph, and
Mrs. Coombes's lifelong remorse. He broke off a piece. The
flesh was white within and slowly changed to lilac in
the air.

"Deadly poison," he said, with satisfaction.

He put it into his mouth.

The taste was at first earthy, then bitter, then surprisingly
hot. It dried the tongue and caused a tingling sensation
in the gums. He swallowed with difficulty, shuddered, and ate
another piece. The second was worse. The third, however, seemed
less objectionable, and by the time he reached the stalk
he had acquired something almost resembling an appetite.

He ate the entire fungus.

For several minutes nothing happened. Coombes sat with his elbows
on his knees, waiting for death and feeling rather cold.
A drop of water fell from a branch upon his
nose. Somewhere a bird made a brief contemptuous sound.

Then his fingers began to glow.

That is not a scientific description, but it accords with
his subsequent recollection. The warmth travelled from his fingers along
his arms, met in his chest, and expanded there with
astonishing rapidity. His respiration deepened. The collar about his neck,
formerly an instrument of oppression, became intolerable; he tore it
open and laughed at the noise of the stud flying
into the bracken.

The wood brightened.

Every wet needle shone. The purple fragments on his palm
seemed of royal magnificence. The trunks rose around him like
pillars in an immeasurable hall, and the sky between their
branches was not grey, as it had been a moment
earlier, but a profound and joyous silver. His troubles remained
in his mind, yet they had become small, comical objects,
like articles in a doll's shop.

Clarence! Mrs. Coombes! Rent! Rates!

He laughed again.

A curious physical confidence possessed him. His boots seemed wonderfully
light. His heart beat with the enthusiasm of a drum,
and his blood, ordinarily a cautious fluid, hurried through his
body as though late for a celebration. He stood, swayed,
recovered, and announced to the fungi that life was excellent.

"Excellent!" he cried. "That's what it is. 'Njoyment!"

He gathered every purple specimen he could find and filled
his pockets. He decorated his buttonhole with a yellow toadstool,
tucked furze into his waistcoat, and turned his frock-coat inside
out because the grey lining appeared to him more festive
than black cloth. One glove he placed upon his head.
The other he threw into a puddle after a short
but spirited argument with it.

Then this new convert to joie de vivre set out
for home.

Meanwhile, in the parlour behind the shop, tea had been
served. Mrs. Coombes was telling Jennie that she had never
expected luxury, only ordinary consideration; Jennie was saying men were
all alike; and Clarence, who had eaten seven slices of
bread and butter, was explaining that tact was essential in
married life. The piano had been closed because a customer
had knocked at the shop door and then departed before
anyone answered.

"I suppose he'll come back when he's tired of sulking,"
said Mrs. Coombes.

At that moment something struck the window.

All three looked round. A purple fungus slid slowly down
the glass.

"What was that?" said Jennie.

The shop bell rang with terrific violence. There followed a
crash among the tins, a burst of laughter, and the
voice of Coombes singing the wrong words to "Turn, Turn,
Turn, My Love."

He entered the parlour backwards.

His appearance produced a silence of uncommon purity. His coat
was inside out, his waistcoat bristled with furze, his collar
hung open, and a glove rested on his hair like
the crest of some commercial savage. Both pockets bulged with
fungi. His face was flushed, his eyes brilliant, and a
dark stain surrounded his mouth.

"Coombes!" said his wife.

He turned with elaborate care and bowed.

"Friends," he said. "Guests. Relations by marriage. Clarence. We are
going to 'njoy ourselves."

"You're drunk," said Jennie.

Coombes considered this and smiled. "Better than that."

He took a fungus from his pocket and offered it
to her.

"Eat."

She recoiled. "The filthy thing!"

"Deadly poisons," said Coombes, confidentially. "Make you see things proper.
Purple wisdom. 'Ave one."

Mrs. Coombes seized his arm. "Where have you been? What
have you done to yourself?"

He kissed her noisily on the forehead.

For perhaps the first time in their married life, Mrs.
Coombes was unable to answer him.

"Music!" he commanded. "Clarence, old boy, perform."

Clarence rose and adopted an expression of manly firmness. "Now
look here, Coombes. You've had too much, and the best
thing you can do is go upstairs and sleep it
off."

Coombes stared at him with delighted wonder.

"Too much? I've 'ad nothing. Nothing all my life. That's
the trouble. No music. No profit. No respect. Potatoes 'ard.
Dinner late. Clarence in the arm-chair. But now—" He spread
his arms, scattering fragments of fungus. "Now I'm 'aving everything."

"You're making a fool of yourself," said Clarence.

The phrase had unfortunate associations.

Coombes's smile vanished. "Play."

"I shall do nothing of the kind."

"Play 'Turn, Turn, Turn.'"

"Don't be absurd."

Coombes seized the music stool and threw it at the
piano. It struck the lower panel with a report like
a gunshot. Jennie screamed. Mrs. Coombes backed against the mantelpiece.
Clarence stepped forward, fists raised in a scientific attitude learned
from an illustrated weekly.

"Put that down," he said.

Coombes had already put it down. He now picked up
the blacking brush from beside the fender.

"Come on, then," said Clarence, though he moved behind the
table as he spoke.

The subsequent action was rapid and, in certain particulars, difficult
to reconcile. Coombes pursued Clarence round the tea-table, striking at
him with the brush. Clarence defended himself with a cushion.
Jennie clung to Coombes's coat, thereby removing half the lining.
Mrs. Coombes cried, "Mind the china!" until the china ceased
to justify the warning. A cup broke, then a plate,
then the sugar basin; and at the destruction of the
sugar basin Coombes uttered a triumphant yell and vaulted, not
altogether successfully, across the arm-chair.

Clarence escaped into the shop.

Coombes followed. He hurled a packet of candles, missed, and
overturned the pyramid of tins. Clarence snatched up a broom.
For a few moments the two men fenced between the
counter and the bacon slicer, Clarence retreating and Coombes advancing
with the blacking brush held like a cavalry sabre.

"Mad!" cried Clarence. "The man's mad!"

"'Njoyment!" shouted Coombes.

A tin of treacle burst beneath Clarence's heel. He slipped,
sat down heavily in a basket of onions, and received
the blacking brush upon the crown of his head. Jennie,
arriving in time to witness this indignity, attacked Coombes with
an umbrella. He caught it, twisted it away, opened it
indoors, and marched beneath it through the passage, singing.

Mrs. Coombes locked the parlour door.

Coombes knocked politely.

"My dear," he said, "open."

"Go away!"

"This is my 'ouse."

"You're a lunatic!"

"That," said Coombes, "is not businesslike."

He stepped back and charged the door. It held. He
charged again. On the third attempt the lock tore loose,
and Coombes entered amid splinters with the open umbrella above
him and the yellow toadstool still nodding from his buttonhole.

There are moments when authority changes hands without legal formality.
Mrs. Coombes saw her husband among the ruins of the
tea things, heard Clarence groaning among the onions, and perceived
that argument, ridicule, and fluent complaint had temporarily lost their
value.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"Stout."

"There isn't any."

Coombes pointed the blacking brush at her.

"There are six bottles under the kitchen sink," she added.

"Good woman," said Coombes.

He found the stout and drank the first bottle in
the kitchen, pausing only to explain to the dresser that
commerce required courage. The second he carried into the parlour.
He compelled Clarence, whose hair was sticky with treacle and
blacking, to play the piano; but as Clarence's left hand
had been bruised in the engagement, the accompaniment was irregular.
Coombes conducted with the brush, Mrs. Coombes and Jennie sitting
side by side upon the sofa.

"Sing," he said.

They sang.

"Louder."

They sang louder.

"Turn, turn, turn, my love," roared Coombes, "turn into somebody
else!"

At the conclusion he applauded with the stout bottle against
the mantelpiece, damaging both. He then delivered a speech on
domestic economy, in which rent, affection, potatoes, musical taste, wholesale
prices, and the decline of the British character were blended
with remarkable freedom. He accused Clarence of eating capital in
the form of bread and butter. He informed Jennie that
her hat lacked moral seriousness. He told his wife she
was "a fine woman, but badly managed," and appointed himself
to correct the defect.

The third bottle of stout softened his indignation. With tears
in his eyes, he embraced Clarence and called him a
serpent. He attempted to restore the fallen tins to the
window, arranging boot polish with preserved peaches and placing a
card marked BEST CURRANTS above the onions. A customer came
to the door, saw him, and decided against entering.

The fourth bottle revived his energy.

He danced in the passage with the umbrella. He climbed
upon the counter and declared a dividend. He threw pennies
to his audience, demanded them back on grounds of insufficient
capital, and challenged the piano to single combat. The piano
made no response, which he regarded as cowardice.

As for the fifth bottle, and certain incidents involving the
flour bin, Clarence's pale tie, a photograph of Mrs. Coombes's
mother, and the kitchen clock, it is better to draw
a veil. They add nothing to our understanding of his
altered physiology and might give pain to readers of a
domestic disposition.

Towards evening the exhilaration began to fail. Coombes's gestures grew
uncertain, his speech sank into fragments, and his flushed face
became grey. He wandered from room to room as if
seeking some forgotten article.

"Purple," he murmured. "Wise little things. Deadly. Turn, turn—"

He went down the steps into the coal cellar.

Mrs. Coombes, who had watched him with the fascinated vigilance
one accords to a dangerous machine, heard a soft collapse
among the sacks. She waited. Clarence took up the broom
again. Jennie whispered that someone ought to fetch a policeman.

They opened the cellar door.

Coombes lay upon the coal, asleep. His breathing was loud
but regular. One hand still grasped the stem of a
crushed purple fungus.

"A doctor," said Mrs. Coombes.

"No," said Clarence, touching his damaged head. "Police."

Mrs. Coombes looked at the wrecked passage, at the broken
parlour door, and then at her husband. Something had entered
her expression that had not been there that morning—not tenderness,
perhaps, nor forgiveness, but a new and practical uncertainty.

"We'll have the doctor first," she said.

The doctor, when he came, examined Coombes, smelt the fragments
of fungus, and asked several questions no one could answer.
He used the words "toxic alkaloid," "temporary cerebral disturbance," and
"fortunate emesis," the last referring to an event in the
coal cellar which need not detain us. He said Coombes
would probably recover, though headache, thirst, and moral depression might
follow. He recommended warmth, quiet, and no more stout.

Clarence went home with Jennie shortly before midnight. The engagement
survived, though his visits thereafter became shorter and were generally
concluded before tea.

Coombes awoke on Monday afternoon in bed. His head contained,
according to his own account, a boiler works, and his
mouth tasted of burnt rope. Mrs. Coombes brought him broth.

"What happened?" he asked.

She looked at him for some moments. "Don't you remember?"

He remembered the wood, the purple fungus, an incomparable sense
of illumination, and Clarence sitting among onions. Beyond that were
splendours and confusions.

"I remember enough," he said cautiously.

Mrs. Coombes set down the broth.

"You nearly killed Clarence."

"Did I?"

"You smashed the parlour door. You broke nine pieces of
the tea service. You frightened Jennie half out of her
life. You—" She stopped. "You behaved like a wild beast."

Coombes closed his eyes. Ordinarily he would have apologised, explained,
blamed worry, and promised submission. But somewhere beneath the headache,
faint as warmth beneath ashes, remained a trace of the
purple confidence.

"Then perhaps," he said, "people will listen when I speak
civil."

Mrs. Coombes stared.

Neither said anything for a while.

That evening she did not invite Jennie. On Tuesday she
asked Coombes how much money was in the business. He
told her. The sum shocked her, and because shock is
sometimes the beginning of arithmetic, they spent two hours over
the accounts. On Wednesday she suggested reducing the housekeeping expenditure.
On Thursday she stood in the shop while Coombes visited
a wholesaler and obtained a better price for tea.

These changes did not occur without friction. No marriage is
transformed by one blacking brush, however resolutely applied. Yet a
rearrangement had begun. Mrs. Coombes discovered that the shop's difficulties
were not entirely manufactured for the purpose of denying her
dresses. Coombes discovered that his wife could sell goods more
pleasantly than he could, remember customers' preferences, and distinguish between
economy and mere refusal. He also discovered, to his surprise,
that firmness need not always be postponed until one was
poisoned.

The parlour was repaired. The piano was sold, ostensibly because
it occupied space required for stock. Clarence married Jennie and
moved to another district. The larger shop by the station
failed after extending too much credit. Coombes purchased part of
its stock cheaply, altered his window display, introduced a line
of reliable provisions, and, at his wife's suggestion, began delivering
orders.

Prosperity did not descend like grace; it accumulated in halfpennies.

Five years later the shop had been enlarged into the
neighbouring premises. There were two assistants, a boy with a
bicycle, fresh paint above the windows, and the name COOMBES
& CO. in gold letters, though no one could identify
the company. Coombes wore a glossy hat, a soft collar
that respected his circulation, and an overcoat without imitation astrachan.
He had grown broader, spoke more slowly, and no longer
watched every customer as a potential criminal. Mrs. Coombes kept
the books, managed the assistants, and consulted her husband before
inviting anyone to Sunday tea.

It would be excessive to say they were happy in
the poetic sense. They were successful, which is the form
happiness most readily recognised in a commercial neighbourhood.

One autumn afternoon Coombes walked with his elder brother through
the pine wood. His brother, who had come to inspect
the enlarged premises and borrow a modest sum, congratulated him
upon the business.

"You've done well, Alf," he said. "There was a time
I thought you were going under."

"So did everyone," said Coombes.

"What turned it?"

Coombes smiled with the gravity of a man about to
disclose a principle.

"Character."

"Character?"

"I'd been too easy. Let people trample on me. Wife's
relations always about, money going out, no order in the
place. Then one Sunday I took a firm stand."

His brother looked impressed. "Had it out with her?"

"Thoroughly."

"Best thing sometimes."

"A woman," said Coombes, "respects a man when she knows
he means what he says. Mind you, I don't believe
in harshness. Reason, kindness, mutual confidence—that's marriage. But there comes
a time for decisive action."

They had reached the place where the purple fungi grew.
Several broad pilei pushed through the wet needles, rich and
glossy in the fading light.

"Queer-looking things," said his brother, prodding one with his stick.
"Poisonous, shouldn't wonder."

Coombes regarded them without recognition. The details of that Sunday
had long ago arranged themselves in his memory according to
dignity and convenience. He recalled the quarrel, his return, his
decisive action, and the subsequent reform of his household. The
wood and its purple inhabitants had faded into an irrelevant
mist.

"Very likely," he said. "Still, everything in nature has its
use, if we only knew it."

He adjusted his glossy hat and walked on towards the
prosperous shop, where Mrs. Coombes was waiting with the weekly
accounts and tea at the proper hour.

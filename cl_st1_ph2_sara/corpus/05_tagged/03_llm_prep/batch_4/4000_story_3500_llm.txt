## The Crown of Nabendu Sekhar

When Purnendu Sekhar departed this world, he left his son
three houses, twelve mango trees, a considerable quantity of Government
paper, and one unfinished ambition.

The houses had cracked cornices, the mango trees were capricious
in their fruit-bearing, and Government paper, though bearing the august
promise of interest, was subject to the fluctuations common to
mortal securities. But the ambition was kept in perfect repair.
It had been polished daily with salaams, nourished with invitations,
aired beneath the verandahs of English officials, and protected from
every rebellious wind that blew across our Motherland.

Purnendu's ambition was to become a Rai Bahadur.

Alas, father Purnendu Sekhar! For thirty-seven years he mounted the
ladder to Paradise, rung by rung. He subscribed to imperial
memorials, illuminated his house upon royal anniversaries, presented addresses in
silver caskets, sent baskets of mangoes to Burra Sahibs who
disliked mangoes, and agreed with Englishmen upon questions concerning which
he had no opinion. Yet, at the last rung, when
his fingers already trembled over the hem of glory, Death—who
possesses neither manners nor respect for official procedure—pulled him down.

His son, Nabendu, inherited the ladder.

As a boy Nabendu had learned that Englishmen were of
three principal varieties: those who could give him something, those
who might later be able to give him something, and
those who knew the first two varieties. He never confused
them. To a Collector he bent from the waist; to
a Commissioner, from the soul; and before a Lieutenant-Governor, had
Providence arranged the encounter, he would doubtless have prostrated his
seven generations.

It must not be supposed that he was a bad
man. He paid his servants after a reasonable delay, never
struck a cow, and had once returned a counterfeit rupee
to a shopkeeper. His defect was merely that he mistook
the shadow of authority for the light of heaven. There
are men who worship stone, men who worship gold, and
men who worship philosophy. Nabendu worshipped the nod of a
sahib.

Then he married Arunlekha.

Marriage, according to poets, joins two hearts; according to prudent
fathers, two properties; and according to experience, two entire armies
of relations. Nabendu had expected a bride. He received, in
addition, a fortress of nationalists.

Arunlekha's father regarded Government titles as gilt buttons sewn upon
a servant's coat. Her elder brother Pramathanath had once returned
from England in a hat, boots and a moral fog.
For six months he had addressed his countrymen as if
speaking through a magistrate's clerk. Then one day, at a
railway station, an English passenger mistook him for a European
in the dusk and offered him a seat. When dawn
disclosed the brown truth of his face, the courtesy was
withdrawn with interest.

Pramathanath came home thoughtful. "They bowed," he said, "not to
me, but to my wrapping."

He removed the wrapping. From that day his English boots
no longer walked before his conscience.

The younger members of the family possessed no such tragic
history, but they made up for it with cheerfulness. Arunlekha's
sisters—Labanyalekha, Shashilekha and Komollekha—were educated, quick-witted and united in the
opinion that their new brother-in-law was a providential gift to
comedy.

Labanyalekha, the eldest of the three, was the most dangerous.
Beauty in a foolish woman is an ornament; in an
intelligent woman it is an armed conspiracy. Her eyes could
express admiration so perfectly that a man discovered only afterward
that he had been insulted. Her voice was soft, her
reasoning was merciless, and when she said, "Indeed?" strong positions
collapsed like wet umbrellas.

On Nabendu's first visit after the marriage, the sisters prepared
a reception.

He entered the inner courtyard expecting sweets, smiles and perhaps
a respectful inquiry concerning his acquaintance with the Assistant Collector.
Instead he found, upon a low wooden stool, a pair
of enormous English boots. They had been borrowed from a
Eurasian railway guard and shone with blacking like twin monsoon
clouds.

Before them burned incense.

Shashilekha rang a little brass bell. Komollekha scattered flowers. Labanya
held a tray containing sandal paste, rice and a lamp.

"What is this?" Nabendu demanded.

Labanya turned with pious astonishment. "Hush! We are performing Boot
Poojah."

"Boot Poojah?"

"Certainly. We feared we had been neglecting the household gods
most dear to you."

Komollekha began chanting from a strip of cotton on which
she had written, in red ink, the sacred names:

"Smith, Brown, Jones, Robinson, Thompson, Johnson—"

This was their namavoli, a devotional cloth ordinarily bearing the
names of deities. Never, we believe, had Messrs. Smith and
Robinson found themselves transported so cheaply to the celestial regions.

Nabendu's ears reddened.

"You are all foolish girls."

Labanya bowed to the boots. "Forgive him, O Johnson Sahib.
His devotion is shy before witnesses."

"I am not devoted to any Johnson!"

"To Smith, then?"

"To nobody!"

"Ah," said Labanya, "marriage has already made him a patriot."

The sisters clapped. Nabendu, seeing laughter shine in Labanya's eyes,
attempted to laugh also; but his laughter emerged thin and
lonely, like a mosquito trapped under a glass.

At dinner the persecution continued.

"Brother," asked Shashilekha, "is it true that a sahib's handshake
cures fever?"

"No."

"Then why did you preserve the glove with which the
District Judge once shook your hand?"

"I did no such thing."

Komollekha sighed. "A sacred relic has been lost."

Nabendu turned to Arunlekha for support. His wife lowered her
face to hide a smile. A bride's loyalty is strong,
but three sisters pulling together can move even that household
mountain.

"You people understand nothing," Nabendu said. "Maintaining good relations with
officials is necessary for practical men."

Nilratan, Labanya's husband, who had arrived unnoticed, answered, "A bridge
is practical if it carries you across a river. If
it carries you only to the middle and leaves you
bowing there, its usefulness may be questioned."

Nabendu disliked Nilratan's sentences. They were not long enough to
provide hiding places.

He said loftily, "My father's position was respected throughout the
district."

"Undoubtedly," said Labanya. "Whenever an English officer sneezed, your father
was respected enough to say 'God bless you.'"

Alas, father Purnendu Sekhar! Even in death you were denied
protection from your daughters-in-law's family.

Yet Nabendu did not remain offended for long. The house
pleased him. Arunlekha's quiet affection soothed him; the sisters' laughter,
though often purchased at his expense, was sweeter than the
solemn compliments of petitioners; and Labanya's approval became, without his
noticing it, an unofficial decoration more desirable than many official
ones.

When she praised his Bengali pronunciation, he stopped sprinkling his
sentences with unnecessary English. When she remarked that his muslin
dhoti suited him, his stiff European trousers began to rest
in the wardrobe. When she said, "You look almost courageous
today," he spent the entire afternoon searching for a courageous
act.

Thus reforms enter the world by curious roads. A philosopher
writes ten volumes and converts no one; a beautiful sister-in-law
lifts an eyebrow and changes the direction of history.

Nabendu nevertheless continued his old cultivation in secret. He sent
New Year cards to officials, subscribed to the District Flood
Relief Fund, although that year the district suffered chiefly from
dust, and kept careful account of every sahib who accepted
his hospitality. His garden parties were investments; his compliments, irrigation;
his father's unfinished title, the golden crop.

But he no longer boasted of them before Labanya.

Once, when an English police superintendent took tea at his
house, Nabendu ordered the servants to keep the event hidden
from his wife's family. Unfortunately, a sahib's visit is not
a mouse that can be concealed beneath a grain basket.
The carriage stood at the gate, the syce shouted, the
chuprassi displayed his belt, and half the neighbourhood assembled to
inspect the sahib's nose.

The next morning Labanya sent Nabendu a parcel. Inside was
a small silver cup engraved:

**TO NABENDU SEKHAR, FOR VALOUR IN POURING TEA.**

He locked it in a drawer, but not before admiring
the engraving.

Soon afterward, Arunlekha went to stay with Labanya and Nilratan
at their country house, and Nabendu followed. The season was
winter, when Bengal, released for a few weeks from the
tyranny of heat, becomes tender to her children. Sunlight lay
on the fields like warm silk. Dew trembled on the
grass until morning, each drop holding a tiny inverted world.
Mustard flowers spread their yellow laughter beneath the pale blue
sky, and the river moved beyond the bamboo groves with
the quiet contentment of one who need not seek promotion.

Nabendu was happy.

He ate rice from the new harvest, sat in the
mild sun, listened to Labanya sing in the evening, and
forgot for several consecutive hours that any District Magistrate existed.
Had the Government chosen that moment to abolish all honorary
titles, he might even have forgiven it.

But Providence had not brought him there merely to eat
date-palm molasses.

One afternoon Nilratan entered with two gentlemen from the local
Congress committee. They wore coarse cloth, serious expressions and the
air of men who had discovered a national duty requiring
someone else's cheque.

"Nabendu Babu," said Nilratan, "these gentlemen are collecting funds for
the approaching session of the Indian National Congress."

Nabendu at once became alert. Congress! The word moved in
his official imagination like a cobra under a pillow.

"A noble institution," he said cautiously, "in its proper sphere."

"What is its improper sphere?" asked Labanya.

"I mean—one must be prudent."

"Of course," she agreed. "No one expects courage from everybody."

Nabendu glanced at her. "I am not afraid."

"I did not say you were. Fear in a sensible
man is merely foresight wearing spectacles."

One of the Congress gentlemen opened a subscription book.

"Babu Prasannakumar has given five hundred rupees," he announced.

Nabendu disliked Prasannakumar, who had recently purchased a larger carriage.

"I shall give five hundred and one," he said.

"How generous!" cried Labanya. "And how exact. Patriotism has learned
bookkeeping."

Nabendu felt the pumpkin of his head swaying in a
hot wind.

"Write one thousand."

The pen stopped. All faces turned toward him.

"One thousand rupees?" asked the collector.

"Did I not say so?"

Labanya's eyes shone. "Brother, we had misjudged you."

Those words cost him nine hundred and ninety-nine rupees more
than prudence had intended, but they produced an intoxication no
Government certificate had yet supplied. He signed.

Nilratan said quietly, "A gift becomes honourable when the hand
does not try to pull it back."

Nabendu withdrew his hand from the subscription book.

By the following week the donation appeared in the nationalist
newspapers. They hailed Nabendu Sekhar as "a son of Bengal
awakened from the sleep of privilege," "a courageous representative of
the landed classes," and, in one particularly enthusiastic journal, "a
thunderbolt forged in the sacrificial fire of the Motherland."

Nabendu read every phrase three times. He had never before
suspected that a thunderbolt might wear embroidered slippers.

Then a loyalist paper published a correction. It declared that
Nabendu's name had been misused; that he was descended from
a family celebrated for unshaken devotion to the Crown; that
his late father had eaten the salt of Government, though
in fact Purnendu had purchased his own salt; and that
no sensible person could believe Nabendu sympathised with the Congresswallahs.

This article filled him with alarm and relief in equal
portions. The loyalists were defending his prospects. The nationalists were
praising his courage. He desired, if possible, to stand upon
both boats while the river divided beneath him.

He wrote a mild letter:

"Sir, Certain statements concerning my political opinions have lately appeared.
I request the public not to draw hasty conclusions from
a private act of charity—"

Labanya read the draft over his shoulder.

"Excellent," she said.

Nabendu knew that tone. "What is wrong with it?"

"Nothing. It shows such balance. One foot on the Motherland,
one on the sahib's doorstep."

"I must protect myself from misrepresentation."

"Naturally. The Government may think you possess convictions."

"I do possess convictions!"

"Then conceal them carefully. They might injure your advancement."

Nabendu tore the draft in half.

"Do you wish me to insult the Government?"

"Never! A Government is too vast to be insulted by
you."

"Do you think I am a coward?"

Labanya looked distressed. "Brother, why do you compel me to
answer family questions?"

He walked about the room. His heart twitched like a
koi-fish dropped upon dry earth. He saw the title of
Rai Bahadur retreating toward a desert shore; he saw Labanya's
respect retreating in the opposite direction. Between the two stood
his vanity, pulled by both ears.

Nilratan said, "If you reply, reply plainly. If you cannot,
remain silent."

"Plainly? I shall be plain enough!"

Labanya sat at the desk. "Shall I take dictation?"

Nabendu dictated. Labanya improved. Nilratan corrected. By the time the
letter was completed, scarcely one of Nabendu's original words remained,
but all his indignation had found magnificent clothing.

The letter condemned the insolence of the Anglo-Indian press, rejected
the assumption that Indian gentlemen required certificates of loyalty, denounced
racial contempt, defended the Congress, invoked the dignity of the
nation, and concluded that public service without self-respect was only
servitude decorated with stationery.

Nabendu read it with widening eyes.

"Is this not rather strong?"

Labanya said, "We can remove 'dignity.' It is a dangerous
expression."

"No, keep it."

"And perhaps 'servitude'—"

"Keep everything!"

The letter was published.

The nationalist papers exploded into praise. One called Nabendu "the
lion of the eastern districts." Another thanked him for "tearing
the mask from arrogant bureaucracy." A poet compared him to
a volcano. Since Nabendu had never torn a mask, roared
like a lion or emitted lava, he was at first
embarrassed; but a man can grow accustomed to any description
if printed often enough.

The Anglo-Indian papers replied with abuse. Nabendu was called an
agitator, an ingrate, a baboo demagogue and—this cut deepest—a person
of no social importance.

"No social importance!" he cried. "My father entertained two Lieutenant-Governors'
private secretaries!"

Labanya said, "Write and tell them."

He stared at her, then laughed despite himself.

Invitations from Congress committees now arrived in bundles. Deputations visited
his house. Students unharnessed the horse from his carriage and
attempted to pull him through the streets. Their enthusiasm exceeded
their coordination, and Nabendu narrowly escaped being overturned into a
sweetmeat shop; but when the crowd shouted his name, his
chest expanded.

Public honour, like opium, may be condemned sincerely before it
is tasted. Afterward the patient discovers arguments in its favour.

He began to speak at meetings. At first he read
speeches written by Nilratan. Then, finding applause attached more to
volume than to grammar, he ventured upon extempore passages. He
spoke of "the sacred soil of our ancient land," although
his own shoes had never willingly touched soil; of "the
starving peasant," whom he chiefly knew as an item in
rent accounts; and of "fearless sacrifice," at which phrase he
remembered his thousand rupees and felt entitled to thunder.

Still, the old deity had not been expelled from the
temple. It had merely been placed behind a curtain.

Whenever a Government carriage passed, Nabendu's neck moved by ancestral
instinct. Whenever honours were announced, he read the list from
beginning to end, then backward, in case his name had
entered by another door. When an Englishman spoke pleasantly to
him at a railway station, he repeated the conversation at
home but altered "Good morning" into "My dear Nabendu."

Labanya observed these symptoms as a physician observes a chronic
disease.

The opportunity for treatment came during the spring.

One morning a liveried chuprassi appeared at Nabendu's gate and
delivered a card bearing the name of the District Magistrate,
Mr. Edward Hamilton. The servant stated that the Magistrate Sahib
had called while Nabendu was away and had regretted missing
him.

Nabendu's soul leaped like a hooked fish.

"The District Magistrate himself?"

"So I was told, Babu."

"Did he enter the drawing room? Was he offered tea?
Who received him? Why was I not informed immediately?"

The household broke into confusion. Chairs were accused, servants threatened,
clocks consulted. Nabendu seized the card. There it was: **Mr.
Edward Hamilton, I.C.S.** The letters I.C.S. glowed like a mystic
formula opening all heavenly locks.

The reader, whose intelligence we have never doubted, may already
suspect Labanya. She had obtained the card through a cousin
who knew the Magistrate's Bengali clerk. The chuprassi was no
Government servant but a theatrical doorkeeper, whose dignity could be
hired by the hour. The conspiracy was enlarged by Shashilekha,
financed by Komollekha, and blessed, after some hesitation, by Arunlekha.

Nabendu ordered his carriage.

"You are going out?" asked Labanya, who had arrived with
miraculous punctuality.

"I must return the Magistrate's call."

"Must you?"

"Common courtesy demands it."

"Certainly. When a mountain visits a grain of sand, the
sand should hurry after it."

He ignored her. "Where is my black coat?"

"I thought you had renounced those symbols of European bondage."

"Courtesy is not bondage."

"And the top hat?"

"Ceremonial necessity."

"And the scented handkerchief?"

"Why are you laughing?"

"I was thinking of the lion of the eastern districts."

Nabendu dressed with religious concentration. He rehearsed several openings:

"Delighted to return your kind call."

"Extremely honoured by your gracious visit."

"My dear Mr. Hamilton—"

The last seemed too bold. He reserved it for the
future, perhaps after Rai Bahadurship.

At the Magistrate's bungalow, the gatekeeper stated that the sahib
was busy.

Nabendu gave him two rupees.

The gatekeeper discovered that the sahib might soon be less
busy.

On the verandah another servant said the sahib was dressing.

Nabendu gave him five rupees.

The process continued through several official heavens, each guarded by
an angel susceptible to bakshish. At length Nabendu was admitted
to a room where Mr. Hamilton sat at breakfast, red-faced
and displeased.

"Who are you?" asked the Magistrate.

Nabendu bowed. "Nabendu Sekhar, sir. I have come to return
your kind call."

"My call?"

"Yes, sir. Yesterday."

"I did not call on you."

Nabendu's smile remained fixed while his eyes begged the room
to explain itself.

"Your card was left at my house."

"Impossible."

"The chuprassi said—"

"What chuprassi?"

Nabendu produced the card.

Mr. Hamilton glanced at it. "Where did you get this?"

"You left it, sir."

"I have never been to your house."

The Magistrate's tone had the hard flatness of a door
being shut. Two English guests at the table looked up
with interest. One of them smiled. Nabendu felt his dignity
detach itself from his body and crawl beneath the breakfast
table.

"Some misunderstanding," he murmured.

"Evidently."

"I feared it would be discourteous not to—"

"You have interrupted my breakfast on the strength of a
stolen visiting card."

"Not stolen, sir! Certainly not! I was deceived."

Mr. Hamilton rang the bell.

That bell! Empires have been built with cannon, treaties and
taxation, but their deepest authority sometimes resides in a small
bell rung by an irritated man at breakfast.

A servant appeared.

"Show this gentleman out."

Nabendu departed. His top hat seemed to have grown three
feet high. Every servant on the verandah knew that he
had bribed his way in; every crow in the compound
appeared to cry, "Return call! Return call!" His wounded heart
convulsed like the severed tail of a lizard, retaining motion
after hope had been cut away.

At home the family awaited him.

Labanya asked, "Did the mountain receive the grain of sand?"

Nabendu looked at her.

She tried to preserve innocence, but Shashilekha made a choking
sound. Komollekha fled behind a curtain. Arunlekha covered her face.

"You!" Nabendu cried.

Labanya folded her hands. "What have I done?"

"The card—the false chuprassi—the whole infernal business!"

Her composure broke. Laughter seized her, then the others, until
the room rang. Nabendu stood among them, black-coated, top-hatted and
furious, like a crow appointed ambassador at a festival of
swans.

"You are a howling idiot!" he shouted at Labanya.

"Brother," she gasped, "not in front of the sacred hat!"

He snatched it off and flung it onto a chair.
Unfortunately it bounced and rolled beneath the table. Shashilekha rang
a brass bell, and Komollekha began chanting:

"Hamilton, Smith, Brown, Robinson—"

Even Arunlekha laughed then. Nabendu's rage, finding no ally, swelled
to majestic proportions and burst. He sat down.

After a moment Labanya wiped her eyes and said more
gently, "Why did you go?"

"Because I was told he had called."

"If an Indian friend's card had come, would you have
scattered rupees among servants to reach him at breakfast?"

"That is different."

"Exactly."

Nilratan, who had remained by the window, said, "They did
not humiliate you today. They merely allowed you to display
the humiliation you had prepared for yourself."

Nabendu winced.

Arunlekha came near and took his hand. "They should not
have played such a cruel trick."

He looked at her, grateful for the small shelter.

Labanya said, "Perhaps not. Forgive us."

Her apology was sincere enough to remove his anger but
not his shame. That evening he burned the Magistrate's card.
For three days he refused to read the loyalist newspapers.
For seven days he did not wear the black coat.
These periods may appear short to stern moralists, but in
the calendar of Nabendu's character they amounted to an epoch.

Meanwhile the story of his visit leaked out. A loyalist
gentleman whispered it at the club, an English clerk embroidered
it, and soon the Anglo-Indian press published a paragraph mocking
"a certain Congress lion who attempted to force his way
into an official bungalow."

Nabendu trembled.

Then the nationalist papers came to his rescue. They declared
that the District Magistrate had insulted a distinguished Indian leader.
They denounced the racial arrogance that refused ordinary courtesy to
a representative of the people. Meetings passed resolutions. Students proposed
a procession. One newspaper demanded an inquiry by the Viceroy.

"But that is not what happened," Nabendu told Nilratan.

"No," said Nilratan.

"Should I correct them?"

"If you wish."

Nabendu considered the matter. "A correction might weaken the larger
issue."

"Undoubtedly," said Labanya. "Truth must not obstruct the larger issue."

He gave her a suspicious glance, but remained silent.

Thus was his private foolishness washed, perfumed and dressed as
a public grievance. Yesterday he had been expelled from a
breakfast room; today he was a martyr to national dignity.
Reputation, that ingenious tailor, can cut a heroic garment from
the most embarrassing cloth.

Congress leaders now embraced him without reserve. He was placed
upon committees, elected to presidencies and consulted on matters he
had first heard of during the consultation. His portrait appeared
in shop windows between those of famous patriots. The artist
enlarged his eyes, strengthened his jaw and removed a portion
of his stomach. Nabendu bought six copies.

At home, however, the sisters remained unimpressed.

When he returned from a meeting garlanded to the ears,
Labanya asked, "How many times did you save India today?"

"When will you learn to respect serious work?"

"As soon as serious work learns to distinguish itself from
applause."

Yet she was not wholly displeased. Beneath the vanity, something
had shifted. Nabendu now listened when Nilratan spoke of unjust
laws. He contributed to a village school without sending the
receipt to any official. Once, when a police inspector abused
an old tenant, Nabendu intervened at some personal risk and
afterward told no newspaper.

We record these facts because satire, unless supervised, may become
unjust. Nabendu's courage was mixed with vanity, but even muddy
water may reflect the sun. He had stumbled into patriotism
as a man may stumble into a river; after much
splashing, he had begun to swim.

Then approached the Queen's Birthday.

Every year, upon that auspicious occasion, honours descended from the
imperial sky upon selected subjects. Titles fell like rain, though
by a meteorological peculiarity they chiefly watered gardens already well
supplied. Nabendu pretended indifference.

"Will the list be published tomorrow?" Arunlekha asked.

"I have no idea."

"You have bought three newspapers in advance."

"For general information."

At dawn he awoke before the birds. A servant was
sent to the railway station for the English newspaper. Nabendu
walked on the verandah, telling himself that he expected nothing,
desired nothing and despised the entire machinery of titles. Every
five minutes he asked whether the train was late.

At last the paper arrived.

He opened it with philosophical calm and ran his finger
down the honours list.

Rao Sahib Haricharan Das.

Khan Bahadur Abdul Majid.

Rai Bahadur Prasannakumar Mitter.

Prasannakumar! The owner of the larger carriage!

Nabendu read on. His finger slowed, trembled and reached the
bottom. His name was absent.

Perhaps there was another column. Perhaps the provincial list continued
on the next page. Perhaps "Nabendu" had been misspelled. He
examined shipping news, advertisements and obituaries in case the Government,
from modesty, had concealed his distinction among them.

Nothing.

The desert shore of Rai Bahadurship stretched before him, dry
and endless. He thought of his father's thirty-seven years of
salaams, his own garden parties, subscriptions, tea trays, New Year
cards and careful handshakes. All had vanished like offerings poured
into a bottomless official well.

Alas, father Purnendu Sekhar! Your son had betrayed your gods
and had not been adopted by them as a punishment.

Nabendu sat alone until evening. Publicly he had declared titles
to be badges of servitude. Privately he had expected the
badge to arrive in a velvet box.

At sunset Arunlekha entered.

"My sisters wish you to come to the inner courtyard."

"I am not in the mood for jokes."

"They say it is urgent."

He followed reluctantly.

The courtyard was illuminated with lamps. A carpet had been
spread. At one end stood a chair draped in red
cloth, and above it hung a canopy made from Arunlekha's
wedding shawl. The sisters wore festive saris. Nilratan stood beside
them, solemn as a priest who has misplaced the scripture
but intends to proceed.

Before Nabendu could retreat, Komollekha placed a garland around his
neck. Shashilekha sounded a conch. Labanya advanced carrying a silver
tray.

Upon it lay a crown fashioned from gold-coloured paper.

"What fresh nonsense is this?" he asked.

Labanya spoke in a ceremonial voice.

"Whereas the foreign Government, being deficient in discernment, has omitted
to confer upon Nabendu Sekhar any title whatsoever; and whereas
he has nevertheless donated money, written letters, suffered abuse, interrupted
a Magistrate's breakfast, and supplied the country with much innocent
entertainment—"

The assembly applauded.

"—we, the undersigned sisters of Arunlekha, acting under authority granted
by ourselves, hereby proclaim him King Nabendu the First, Sovereign
of the Unreturned Call, Defender of the Thousand-Rupee Subscription, Lion
of the Eastern Districts and Terror of Breakfasting Magistrates."

Before he could protest, she set the paper crown upon
his head.

"All hail the King!"

"All hail!" cried the others.

Nabendu tried to look offended, but affection attacked him from
every side. The lamps shone upon laughing faces; the garland
smelled of jasmine; Arunlekha stood a little apart, her eyes
bright and tender. The official list, with its Rai Bahadurs
and Khan Bahadurs, seemed for one generous moment no more
substantial than smoke.

Labanya bowed low.

"Your Majesty, will you accept the loyalty of your subjects?"

"I shall have you all imprisoned."

"Under which regulation?"

"For insolence."

Nilratan said, "A wise beginning. His Majesty has mastered government
already."

Nabendu laughed. The paper crown slipped over one ear, and
the sisters straightened it with great pomp and ceremony.

Then Arunlekha came forward. She had prepared no speech. From
beneath her shawl she brought a narrow strip of handwoven
cloth and tied it around his wrist.

"What is this honour?" he asked softly.

"No honour," she said. "Only a reminder."

"Of what?"

"That whatever others call you, you belong with us."

At this, the lion, thunderbolt, volcano and sovereign found himself
unable to reply. He touched the cloth to his forehead.

Outside, some young Congress workers who had learned of the
ceremony began shouting, "Victory to Nabendu Babu!" Neighbours joined them.
Someone cried, "Long live the patriot!" Someone else, having misunderstood
the occasion, cried, "Long live the King!" The phrases collided
in the night air and became indistinguishable.

Nabendu stepped onto the verandah wearing his jasmine garland and
paper crown. The crowd cheered. He raised his hand with
the natural ease of a man who had practised receiving
applause under several political systems.

Labanya watched from behind the screen.

"Do you think he is cured?" Arunlekha whispered.

Labanya considered him. "Of the Magistrate, perhaps."

"And of the Rai Bahadurship?"

"My dear sister, do not demand miracles in the first
reign."

For indeed, when the next New Year honours were rumoured,
Nabendu casually inquired whether the Government ever recognised political opponents
in the interests of reconciliation. He also observed that a
title, though personally meaningless, might be used for national purposes.
And when a new Lieutenant-Governor arrived, Nabendu sent no basket
of mangoes—certainly not!—but he did ask the gardener, in an
abstract and wholly patriotic spirit, whether the late-season fruit was
good.

We greatly doubt that Purnendu Sekhar's ladder had been burned.
It had merely been laid flat and decorated with Congress
flags.

But on Nabendu's wrist remained Arunlekha's strip of cloth, and
in a glass cabinet stood the paper crown bestowed by
his laughing judges. Between the title he still desired and
the love he had accidentally earned, his head continued to
sway like a pumpkin in the wind.

Which honour would finally prevail?

Our Motherland, who has seen emperors, petitioners, martyrs, magistrates, patriots
and howling idiots pass across her patient soil, smiled and
withheld the answer.

## The Five Hundred Keys of Lowenburg

The dames of Weinsberg, who, when their town was given
over to destruction, carried their husbands upon their backs through
the gates, under cover of a promise that each woman
might save whatever she held most precious, have been justly
celebrated by poet and chronicler. Their burden was indeed a
heavy one—especially, we may suppose, where the husband had prospered
at table—but affection made it light; and the conqueror, though
angry at having been outwitted, honorably refused to break his
word.

Yet there are burdens which cannot be laid across the
shoulders, and treasures which may neither be packed in bundles
nor concealed beneath a cloak. A good conscience is such
a treasure; and though the deed we are about to
relate has neither bloodshed, siege, nor kingly clemency to recommend
it, we cannot refrain from telling the story. For courage
does not always wear a steel cap. Sometimes it wears
a linen coif, carries a market basket, and has at
its girdle a bunch of household keys.

Lowenburg, in the year 1631, was a thriving Protestant city,
strongly set within walls and moat, with gate towers overlooking
the roads, and a market-place surrounded by tall houses, whose
upper stories projected so far that neighbors might almost have
shaken hands from their opposite windows. There were pointed gables,
dark galleries, wainscoted rooms, vaulted storehouses, bake-ovens of notable capacity,
three hundred looms or factories connected with the cloth trade,
and fourteen inns—an allowance which seems liberal until we remember
the number of soldiers, factors, drovers, messengers, and disputatious theologians
then passing through Germany.

But the Thirty Years' War had made prosperity a doubtful
blessing. A rich city invited occupation, and occupation brought contributions,
billets, confiscations, and a new confession of faith whenever the
flag above the gate changed color. Lowenburg had fallen under
Catholic imperial control. Its Lutheran pastors were expelled; certain leading
citizens were driven away; others were imprisoned; and two hundred
and fifty families, it is said, lost either their homes
or the means by which homes are maintained.

Most of the men who remained submitted outwardly to the
religion imposed upon them. Some pleaded their children, some their
workshops, and some the danger of being turned into the
road in winter. We will not judge them too harshly.
It is easier to preserve a perfect theological posture when
no pikeman is quartered in one's best chamber and no
officer is examining one's accounts. Still, it must be owned
that the husbands of Lowenburg discovered a considerable flexibility of
conscience.

Their wives did not.

These good women continued steadfast Lutherans. They held prayers in
their houses, slipped beyond the walls to hear Protestant preaching,
and carried their infants into neighboring districts to be baptized
according to the faith of their fathers. If questioned, they
answered plainly. If threatened, they attended to their bread, their
butter, their spinning, and their children, and afterward did precisely
as they had intended before the threat was made.

The magistrates bore this refractory womankind with such patience as
necessity teaches. A councilor might order a cloth factor to
Mass; but it was less easy to command the cloth
factor's wife, who knew where his clean shirts were kept,
what debts he had forgotten, and how much wine he
had drunk at the Swan on the preceding Thursday. Several
members of the council were unmarried or widowed, and these
gentlemen naturally spoke with great boldness. The married members, having
more accurate information, were inclined to moderation.

Unfortunately there lived in Lowenburg a former friar named Julius,
a man of desperate fortunes and unscrupulous zeal. Whether he
desired ecclesiastical favor, revenge upon the Protestants, or merely the
importance obtained by making other people uncomfortable, cannot now be
certainly known. All three motives may have joined hands within
him, for bad purposes are often sociable. He represented to
the council that the women's obstinacy brought contempt upon authority.

"You have subdued the city," said he, "but not the
households. The men obey, while the wives defy both Church
and magistracy."

This was unpleasantly true.

Julius urged that several of the principal ladies should be
summoned to the Town Hall, admonished, and, if they continued
stubborn, imprisoned. The rest, he promised, would speedily submit. A
flock, he observed, might be turned by seizing its leaders—an
image more satisfactory to him than to any person acquainted
with geese.

The council at last consented. Summonses were sent to the
Judge's wife, the Burgomaster's wife, and several other burgher dames
of known influence, requiring them to appear at a stated
hour and answer concerning their disobedience.

The appointed morning came. The councilors assembled in their wainscoted
chamber, wearing their robes and official chains. The Town Clerk
arranged his papers; Julius prepared his arguments; a messenger stood
by the door; and the boldest unmarried magistrate rehearsed a
speech upon submission, order, and the weakness of the female
understanding.

Then a murmur arose in the market-place.

It grew into the sound of many feet, many skirts,
and many voices. The councilors went to the windows. From
every street women were advancing: matrons in sober gowns, merchants'
wives in fur-bordered mantles, artisans' wives with aprons folded up,
widows, grandmothers, young mothers who had left infants in the
keeping of elder daughters, and servants carrying bundles of food
lest the business should last beyond dinner. At their head
walked the Judge's wife and the Burgomaster's wife; behind them
came, not the seven or eight persons summoned, but five
hundred women.

This was more obedience than the magistrates had expected, and
they did not like it.

The foremost dames entered the hall, followed by others, until
the passages, stairs, and lower rooms were filled. The messenger
tried to close the door, but a stout baker's widow
inserted her foot and remarked that the council had sent
for the Protestant women, and the Protestant women had come.

"Only certain persons were summoned," said the Town Clerk.

"We are all concerned," replied the Judge's wife.

"The others must depart."

"None will depart."

The Burgomaster's wife turned to the women behind her. "Will
any go home and leave her sisters to answer alone?"

"No! No!" came the cry; and then, with greater order,
"We will all share the same fate."

The council now directed that the leaders alone should enter
the chamber. This also was refused. The women declared that
no one among them should be separated, coaxed, frightened, or
forced into an answer which the rest could not hear.

At this point Julius advanced and spoke of obedience to
lawful authority. He told them that imprisonment awaited those who
resisted; that wives owed submission; that private opinion must yield
to public peace; and that the magistracy possessed both wisdom
and power.

The women listened. Five hundred faces regarded him with an
attention which became less agreeable the longer it continued.

Then the Judge's wife answered: "We have come peacefully, as
commanded. We have raised no tumult and harmed no man.
But our faith is not a coat which the council
may bid us put off, nor will we act against
our consciences. If one is to be imprisoned, imprison all."

"Yea, yea—all!" cried the women.

Now the councilors observed a circumstance which had escaped them
from the windows. Nearly every woman wore at her girdle
a bunch of keys: keys of cupboards, chests, doors, storerooms,
workshops, and strongboxes; venerable iron instruments, some of them large
enough to have locked a fortress. No woman had drawn
or lifted one. Indeed, their conduct remained perfectly composed. Nevertheless,
five hundred bunches of keys made an impressive glitter, and
the magistrates' imagination supplied what the women had never threatened.

The unmarried councilor whispered that violence was intended.

A married colleague, who recognized his own cellar key hanging
at his wife's side, became pale.

Julius advised firmness, but spoke from behind the Town Clerk.

A confused consultation followed, during which the wisdom and authority
of Lowenburg moved steadily toward a small rear door. The
councilors withdrew into the next room, then into the passage,
and finally down a private staircase. Some left their papers;
one left his hat; another abandoned a handkerchief of unusual
fineness. Julius descended first, though he afterward maintained that he
had gone only to summon assistance.

The door below was locked, not upon the magistrates, but
upon the women.

Thus five hundred peaceable dames, who had obeyed a legal
summons, found themselves prisoners in the Town Hall, while their
judges stood safely outside.

There was at first some laughter. The baker's widow picked
up the councilor's hat and asked whether authority commonly shed
its covering when frightened. The Judge's wife rebuked her, for
ridicule, though tempting, seldom makes an adversary more reasonable. The
women settled themselves in the halls and chambers. Those who
had brought bread divided it with those who had not;
benches were arranged for the elderly; and one mother, having
prudently brought knitting, set to work upon a stocking.

Meanwhile, Lowenburg began to discover that five hundred housewives cannot
be removed from a town without consequences. Fires had been
left banked, not fed; pots stood ready, but no dinners
appeared; children demanded their mothers; apprentices found no bread cut;
husbands searched vainly for keys, accounts, linen, and common sense.
Families soon crowded around the Town Hall, passing in baskets
of bread, butter, cheese, and cold meat through such windows
as could be reached. One thoughtful child sent his mother
a crab saved from the previous evening's meal; whether as
a delicacy or as a weapon does not appear.

The women, having eaten, requested to know upon what charge
they were imprisoned and when their trial would take place.

This question was inconvenient. No charge had been prepared; and
the magistrates, who had planned to terrify eight ladies, were
unwilling to prosecute five hundred. The Town Clerk therefore sent
word that there had been a misunderstanding. The summons, he
said, was not judicial. Their honors had desired only a
friendly religious conference.

The Judge's wife replied that friendly conferences were not commonly
conducted with locked doors.

Another message assured them that no compulsion was intended.

"Then open the door," said the women.

The door remained closed.

Presently Julius sent a long discourse upon the errors of
Luther, the unity of the Church, the peril of disobedience,
and the mercy awaiting repentance. When it had been read,
the women returned a short answer: they had not come
to dispute theology; they would not abandon the confession in
which they had been instructed; and they would neither promise
attendance at Mass nor have their children baptized contrary to
conscience.

There the matter rested. The magistrates could not frighten them,
could not argue them into surrender, and could not keep
them without disorganizing half the city. Toward evening the doors
were opened. The women came out in good order, taking
nothing that did not belong to them, though the abandoned
hat was carried to its owner by the baker's widow,
who set it upon his head with a courtesy not
wholly free from significance.

One might suppose that Julius had received enough instruction for
a single day. But a meddler, like a burr, is
not easily shaken off. He persuaded the council to make
one further attempt, privately and without the alarming accompaniment of
five hundred keys.

The Judge's wife, the Burgomaster's wife, and a third leader
were therefore summoned. Julius spoke to them separately, offering two
paths. They might conform openly; or, if immediate conversion seemed
difficult, they might say that they required time for consideration.
This latter answer, he hinted, would satisfy the authorities and
save appearances.

It was a neat proposal. They need not believe a
falsehood; they need only tell one.

"Our parents did not teach us to lie," replied the
Burgomaster's wife.

"Nor shall we pretend to doubt what we do not
doubt," said the Judge's wife.

Julius urged that words were small things and that the
Ten Commandments must sometimes yield to necessity—a sentiment which, had
it been applied by others to his purse or reputation,
he could hardly have wished put into practice.

The three women remained immovable.

Nor had they come unsupported. News of the private summons
traveled through Lowenburg with that speed peculiar to matters which
authority desires to keep quiet. Before Julius had completed his
exhortation, women were once more gathering in the market-place. First
came twenty, then fifty, then a hundred; keys sounded at
their girdles, and husbands followed at a prudent distance, carrying
cloaks and baskets.

The magistrates looked from the windows and remembered the former
conference. They had no wish to lose another dinner, another
hat, or what remained of their dignity. Julius was ordered
to desist. The three leaders were released, and the waiting
women escorted them home through streets crowded with children, apprentices,
old men, and not a few husbands who cheered very
heartily when danger had passed.

No scaffold was raised in Lowenburg for these dames; no
sword was drawn; no prison received them for years; and
it would be unjust to place their trial beside those
dreadful martyrdoms in which men and women have sealed their
faith with blood. Yet constancy is not measured only by
pain endured. They faced lawful power when it was unlawfully
employed; they refused alike the open sin of betrayal and
the convenient sin of deceit; and, above all, they stood
together, so that no timid soul was left alone to
be broken.

Their weapons were patience, plain speech, and unity. Their banners
were aprons and sober mantles. Their formidable armor consisted of
household keys, which had opened pantries, linen chests, workshops, nurseries,
and perhaps occasionally a husband's locked understanding. With these they
put to flight the collected wisdom of the magistracy, and
gained a victory without striking one blow.

It is a homely Golden Deed, certainly, and has more
of bread and butter in it than of trumpets and
laurel. But courage which preserves conscience, harms no enemy, and
strengthens the weak is worthy of record; and therefore the
five hundred women of Lowenburg may take their modest place
beside the dames of Weinsberg—those carrying what they loved most,
and these refusing to surrender what they held most sacred.

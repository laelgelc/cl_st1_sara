On the last evening of October, when the doors are
best kept shut and no man should call another by
name upon a lonely road, Red Hanrahan was in a
barn at Daire-caol, teaching songs to the young men and
playing at cards with the old.

There was a great fire of turf on the floor,
and over it an iron crook with a black pot
hanging from it, though there was nothing in the pot
but water for mixing the whiskey. A barrel stood against
the wall, and there were sacks of oats, broken harness,
a ploughshare spotted with rust, and three hens asleep on
a beam under the thatch. Two dip candles had been
set in bottles, and their flames bent whenever the half-door
was opened, for a sharp wind was coming down from
Slieve Echtge.

Hanrahan was a tall, straight man at that time, broad
in the shoulders and red in the hair, and there
was not a fair or a pattern between Ballylee and
Lough Greine where he was not known. He had a
little inkpot tied in a cloth, and a horn primer,
and an old book of Virgil he had bought from
a priest's servant. He would teach a boy his letters
for a measure of meal or a night's lodging, and
he would make a song for a wedding, or against
a miser, or upon a girl that had given the
cold hand to her sweetheart. There were some said his
learning was not deep, but there were none said his
songs were bad.

He had been singing that evening of a ship going
west under red sails, and of the woman watching it
until her eyes were blinded with tears, when a boy
named Colman came running to the barn. He was wet
to the knees, and his breath was broken in him,
for he had crossed the bog by the short path.

"Hanrahan," he said, "there is word from Mary Lavelle."

At the name, the young men began laughing and striking
one another with their elbows.

"Let the learned man hear his lesson," said one.

"He will have another school before morning," said another, "and
scholars enough after the year."

Hanrahan told them to hold their tongues, but he was
smiling.

"What word is it?" he asked.

"She says you are to come to her now, and
not when it pleases you. The rent is owing, and
old Lavelle is dead from her, and the cousin that
has the writing of the place says he will put
her out unless there is a husband in the house
before the week is gone. She says you gave your
promise in May, and if there is truth in you,
you will come before daylight."

At that the laughter stopped, for land and rent are
no laughing matters, and they all knew Mary had but
the little holding beyond Druim-da-rod, with a strip of meadow
and a stony field and three cows.

Hanrahan stood up at once.

"I gave her my promise," he said, "and I will
not have it said I broke it."

He gathered his primer and Virgil and inkpot, and wrapped
them in a piece of flannel. Then he took his
long frieze coat from a peg. The woman of the
house, who had come in with a loaf under her
arm, said he should eat before setting out, but he
would not wait.

"There are seven miles between this and Mary's door," he
said, "and the moon is up."

"The moon is up," said the woman, "but it is
a Samhain moon, and there is more than moonlight abroad."

He told her he had no fear of the dead
or the wandering people, for neither had ever paid him
school fees. That set the young men laughing again, and
one filled a glass for his journey. He drank it
standing, and was drawing the door toward him when an
old man sitting by the darkest part of the wall
spoke.

No one had marked when the old man came in,
though that is not a thing to wonder at on
Samhain Eve. He was small and bent, with a grey
coat buttoned to his chin, and a cap drawn low
over his eyes. His hand, where it rested upon his
knee, was thin and withered like the claw of a
bird.

"You are going early, Red Hanrahan," he said.

"I am going late," said Hanrahan, "and that is why
I must go quickly."

"There is time enough for one game."

"There is not."

"There is always time for the game that is waiting."

Hanrahan looked at the door and then at the table
where the cards lay. They had been playing for halfpence,
and he had won more than he had lost. He
was fond of cards, as he was fond of songs
and arguments and every business that might keep a man
from the road he had chosen.

"One game," said the old man, "and no more."

"Let him go," said the woman. "There is a girl
looking down a dark road for him."

But the old man was shuffling the cards, and the
sound of them was like dry leaves running over stones.

Hanrahan took off his coat again.

"One game," he said. "And if you cheat me, old
grandfather, I will put you out over the half-door."

They sat opposite one another on meal chests, and the
others crowded round. The old man dealt slowly, turning each
card with his long nails. He named the suits as
they fell.

"Spades and Diamonds, Courage and Power; Clubs and Hearts, Knowledge
and Pleasure."

"That is no way of naming them," said Colman.

"It is an old way," said the stranger.

"Too old for an honest game," said the woman.

But Hanrahan had begun sorting his hand. He had three
kings, and he thought he would finish quickly. The old
man won the first trick and the second. Hanrahan won
the third, and he laughed. Then the stranger laid down
the queen of hearts.

At that moment a wind went through the barn, though
the door was shut, and both candles died together. The
fire sank low, and there was no light but a
red gleam among the sods of turf.

"That's not fair," cried Hanrahan. "That's not fair."

"Play," said the old man.

The cards in Hanrahan's hands began to shine at their
edges. At first they shone no more than a coal
under ashes, but soon every card was circled with red,
and his fingers looked as if they were within rings
of fire. Across the table the stranger's face could not
be seen, only his two hands moving in the darkness.

"Spades and Diamonds," he said, "Courage and Power."

"Clubs and Hearts," answered a voice from near the roof,
though no one stood there, "Knowledge and Pleasure."

The people drew back against the walls. One of the
hens woke and fluttered, and the black pot swung on
its hook.

Then the old man threw a card upon the table,
and it was no card when it touched the wood,
but a white hare with a red mark on its
throat. It leaped to the floor and ran three times
about the fire. He threw another card, and that became
a black hound. Then another and another, until there were
twelve hounds straining and whining, their eyes green, their ribs
bright under their skins.

The hare sprang over the half-door.

The hounds went after it with a cry that shook
dust from the rafters, and the door burst open before
them. The moonlight lay white in the yard, and the
hare crossed it like a flying rag.

Hanrahan was on his feet.

"Where are you going?" cried the woman, catching at his
sleeve.

"I will see where they go."

"Mary Lavelle is waiting."

"I will follow, I will follow on," he said, pulling
free. "It is but a turn of the field, and
after that I will take the road."

He ran into the yard. Behind him the old man
laughed once, but whether it was laughter or the cry
of a curlew no one could say.

The hare had gone through a gap in the wall,
and the hounds streamed after it. Hanrahan climbed over the
loose stones, tearing his coat, and followed them across a
pasture silvered with frost. The moon was full, but clouds
travelled under it, and the shadows went running faster than
the hounds.

At the first field he thought he would turn back.
At the second he thought the hunt must soon circle
home. At the third he had forgotten Mary's message, and
there was nothing in him but the desire to keep
the white hare in sight.

They went by the ruined church at Ballynashee, where the
nettles were black beside the graves, and over the ford
of the Callan, though the water was swollen. Hanrahan slipped
upon a stone and wet his clothes to the waist,
but he heard the hounds crying on the farther bank
and climbed after them. They went through a hazel wood,
and every bare branch caught at him. They went over
bogland where the pools showed stars that were not in
the sky, and twice he felt the ground breathe under
his feet.

Near Lough Greine the hare turned north. There was a
cabin with light at the window, and a man came
to the door holding a lantern.

"Who goes there?" he called.

"Red Hanrahan."

"Come in, Hanrahan. You have blood on your face."

"I cannot. There is a hunt before me."

"I hear no hunt."

But Hanrahan heard it, far and high, like bells under
the earth. He left the road and climbed through heather.
The mountain rose black above him, and the moon seemed
caught upon its ridge.

All that night he followed, though afterwards he could not
tell what paths he had taken. He crossed Daire-bán and
the shoulder of Druim-da-rod, and he saw beneath him lakes
that were smooth as metal and little villages where no
dog barked. Sometimes the hounds were close enough that he
saw the white of their teeth, and sometimes they were
no more than moving sparks a mile ahead. The hare
never tired.

When they came to the steep side of Slieve Echtge,
the hunt rose from the ground. The hare ran upward
through the air as easily as over grass, and the
hounds followed, their long bodies stretched between earth and moon.
Hanrahan climbed after them though there was nothing beneath his
feet but cold wind. He did not look down, for
fear would have dropped him among the rocks.

He came at last to a narrow path between two
cliffs. There the hare vanished, and the hounds were silent.
Before him was a smooth wall of stone with no
doorway in it. Hanrahan put out his hand, and the
stone opened inward.

Beyond it was broad daylight.

He stepped through and found himself in a country of
summer. The grass was high and warm about his knees,
apple trees were in flower, and yellow flags stood beside
a slow river. Birds were singing as they sing at
sunrise in June, though behind him it had been a
frosty night of November. The air had the smell of
honey, young leaves, and water running over clean stones.

Far off, upon a green hill, stood a house white
as new milk. Its roof shone like gold after rain.
Hanrahan went toward it, for he could not have turned
back if his own mother had called him.

There was no door in the house, but when he
came near, the wall opened. He entered a long room
filled with a brightness that came from no lamp or
window. The floor was dark blue and had silver fish
moving in it as if it were deep water. Along
the walls hung red branches bearing fruit and blossom together.

At the end of the room, upon a low bed,
lay a woman that was like a queen. Her hair
went down to the floor and spread there like ripe
wheat. She wore a green dress fastened with a golden
wheel, and there were small crowns embroidered on her sleeves.
Her eyes were shut, but there was life in her
face, and now and again she sighed. In that sigh
was the sound of hidden streams, and wind in reeds,
and cattle lowing across a valley at evening.

Beside her stood four old women. They were bent and
grey, but their eyes were bright, and each held something
covered with a cloth.

Hanrahan had made songs before kings' portraits and had spoken
boldly to magistrates and priests, but in that place his
tongue lay heavy in his mouth.

The first old woman came forward. She drew away her
cloth and showed a little bronze cauldron. Though it was
no bigger than two hands, there came from it the
smell of roasted meat, red wine, summer apples, and every
feast a hungry man remembers.

"Pleasure," she said.

The second uncovered a round black stone. It looked no
different from a stone out of a field, but when
Hanrahan looked at it he saw armies moving, judges rising
from their seats, landlords opening their gates, and crowds bending
their heads.

"Power," she said.

The third had a spear with a leaf-shaped point. Fire
ran quietly along its edge, and at the sight of
it Hanrahan felt his shoulders straighten. He thought he could
face the black hounds, the men of the law, the
sea in winter, and death itself if death came openly.

"Courage," she said.

The fourth old woman uncovered a sword in a dark
sheath. There were letters upon the handle, some Greek, some
Latin, and some older than either. When she drew it
a hand's breadth, Hanrahan heard children spelling in distant schools,
poets reciting in halls long fallen, and the first words
spoken by men after the making of the world.

"Knowledge," she said.

Then all four turned toward the sleeping queen.

Hanrahan understood that he must choose, or speak, or ask
some question that had waited a thousand years for his
asking. But the cauldron drew his hunger, and the stone
drew his pride, and the spear drew the bold blood
in him, and the sword drew all the learning he
had ever desired. He looked from one to another, and
because he wanted each, he reached for none.

The queen sighed again, and one of her hands moved
upon the coverlet. Her eyelids trembled. Hanrahan thought she would
wake if he could but call her by her name.

He tried to speak, but his throat was dry.

The first old woman said, "Choose."

The second said, "Ask."

The third said, "Awaken."

The fourth said, "Remember."

He thought then of Mary Lavelle standing at her half-door,
with her dark hair tied back and flour upon her
hands. He saw the little field, the three cows, and
the smoke of the house going up quietly in the
morning. It seemed a poor thing beside that shining room,
and yet his heart turned toward it.

"Mary," he said, but no sound came.

The old women drew closer.

"He is weak," said the first.

"He is weak," said the second.

"He is afraid," said the third.

"He has knowledge enough to be lost," said the fourth,
"and not enough to find the way."

Then the woman that was like a queen opened her
eyes for one moment. They were grey as rain over
the mountain, and sorrow was in them, but not anger.
Hanrahan felt that she had been waiting before his birth
and would be waiting after his death, and that many
men had come to that room and failed as he
was failing.

"It is a pity," said the four together. "It is
a great pity."

The brightness went out. The floor broke under him like
thin ice, and he fell into darkness, hearing the hounds
far above.

When Hanrahan awoke, his face was against frozen heather. Morning
was whitening the east, and there was sleet in the
wind. One shoe was gone, his coat was torn from
shoulder to hem, and blood had dried upon his hands.
His bundle lay near him. The flannel was soaked, the
primer had lost half its pages, and the leaves of
Virgil were clotted together with mud.

He sat up and looked about. He was on the
lower slope of Slieve Echtge, though he did not know
how he had come there. He remembered the barn and
the whiskey and the beginning of a card game, but
after that there was nothing.

He tried to stand and fell again. Hunger was twisting
him, and his tongue was swollen. By crawling and stumbling
he came at evening to a shepherd's hut. The shepherd
gave him milk and oatcake, and Hanrahan eat it with
both hands like a child.

"What happened you?" the shepherd asked.

"I do not know."

"Were you robbed?"

"I do not know."

"Did you sleep on the hungry grass?"

"It may be."

The shepherd crossed himself. "There are many go wandering and
forgetting like that. You have been given the touch, maybe,
and it is best not to question it."

Hanrahan slept by the fire, and in the morning he
went away. But he did not go toward Mary Lavelle's
house, for her name had gone from him as writing
goes from a slate under rain.

All that winter he wandered from cabin to cabin. Sometimes
people knew him and called him the learned man and
the great songmaker, but he looked at them without recognition.
He would sing if they pressed him, yet the songs
had no endings, or he would begin one tune and
finish with another. When children brought their books, he opened
them and stared.

"Read that for us, master," they said.

"The light is bad," he would answer.

Even with the book held near the fire he could
make nothing of it. The letters shifted like black insects.
In his Virgil whole lines had washed away, and those
that remained seemed to belong to a language he had
never learned.

He grew thin, and his red hair became long and
tangled. He tied his coat with a rope. He slept
in hayricks, under bushes, in limekilns, and once in an
empty grave where the earth was warmer than the air.
People gave him potatoes or a sup of milk because
of the songs he had made in better days. Others
shut their doors, saying there was something following him.

In spring he was seen near Gort, walking after a
white dog that was not there. In summer he stayed
a month among turf-cutters beyond Lough Greine and helped them
stack the sods, but one morning he left without wages.
In autumn he went with tinkers as far as Ballylee.
He had no count of days, and a week and
a month were the same to him.

Yet there were times when some small thing troubled him:
the smell of flour, a woman's dark hair, three cows
going one after another along a boreen. Then he would
stand still with his hand against his forehead, knowing he
had lost something, but not knowing what it was.

The length of a year passed in that way.

On the next Samhain Eve he came to a house
at Daire-caol, not the same barn but one within sight
of it. There were men gathered around a turf fire,
and a bottle going from hand to hand. They knew
Hanrahan, though they scarcely knew him by his looks, and
they made room.

"Sit down, Red Hanrahan," said the master of the house.
"Whatever has happened you, there is heat here."

A woman gave him boiled potatoes and salt. He eat
them slowly. The talk went on about cattle prices, marriages,
and a mare that had fallen into a drain. No
one asked him questions, for the night was dangerous, and
it is not right to make a wandering man tell
where he has been.

After a while cards were brought out.

At the sound of the pack being shuffled, Hanrahan lifted
his head.

"Will you take a hand?" someone asked.

He drew back at first, but the cards had already
been dealt, and the queen of hearts lay face upward
near his hand. He touched it with one finger.

The room darkened before him.

He saw fiery circles, a white hare, black hounds, and
a woman asleep under golden hair. He heard four voices
saying, "Pleasure. Power. Courage. Knowledge." Then another face rose clearer
than all of them: Mary Lavelle at her door, waiting.

Hanrahan sprang up so suddenly the table was overturned.

"Mary!" he cried.

The people stared.

"Mary Lavelle! I was to go to her. She sent
for me this very night."

"This night?" said the woman of the house.

"Yes, this night. Colman brought the word. Her land will
be taken unless I marry her."

No one answered for a while. The fire settled with
a red shower.

At last an old man said, "Colman brought that word
a year ago."

Hanrahan caught him by the coat. "You are lying."

"I am not."

"It was an hour ago. There was one game. No
more than one game."

"You have been gone the length of a year."

Hanrahan let him go. He looked at the faces and
saw pity there, and pity frightened him more than the
hounds.

He snatched up his bundle and ran from the house.

The moon was behind clouds, and rain had begun, but
he knew every turn of the road now. He crossed
the ford and took the path by Druim-da-rod. Twice he
fell. Once he heard hounds upon the mountain and stopped,
trembling, but they were mortal hounds after a fox.

Before dawn he came to Mary's holding.

The gate hung from one hinge. The thatch was gone
from half the roof, and the door stood open. Nettles
grew on the threshold. There were no cows in the
field, and the meadow had been grazed bare by another
man's sheep.

Hanrahan went from room to room calling her name. Rain
had come through the roof. A broken stool lay by
the hearth, and in one corner he found a blue
ribbon he remembered seeing in her hair. He held it
as if it could speak.

A neighbour came along the road carrying a spade. He
stopped when he saw Hanrahan.

"So you are back," he said.

"Where is Mary?"

The neighbour shifted the spade to his other shoulder.

"She waited for you until the week was out. Then
the cousin came with the bailiff and put her cattle
on the road. She stayed with relations near Gort for
a while."

"And after?"

"She married a labouring man."

Hanrahan's hand closed about the ribbon.

"What man?"

"One Murtagh Keane. He had work promised in a city
across the water. Liverpool, they said, or maybe Glasgow. She
went with him in spring."

"She left no word?"

"She left many words while she thought you might hear
them. After a time she left none."

"Did she think me dead?"

"Some said dead. Some said drunk. Some said you had
followed the people of the hills. It made no difference
to the rent."

Hanrahan looked toward Slieve Echtge. The mountain was hidden, but
a pale line showed where morning was coming.

"I was delayed," he said.

The neighbour gave a hard little laugh, not out of
mockery but because there are griefs a man cannot answer.

"A year is a long delay to a woman without
land."

Hanrahan went into the empty house once more. He sat
where the hearthstone was cold and put his ruined Virgil
on his knees. He tried to read, but the letters
ran together, and with the dint of looking at them
his eyes filled with water.

After a while he began making a song. It was
about a door standing open on Samhain, and a road
that grew longer under the feet, and a woman waiting
until waiting had eaten the youth from her heart. But
when he came to the last verse, no words would
come.

He left the blue ribbon under a loose stone by
the hearth. Then he rose and went out.

The neighbour watched him take the road eastward in the
white light of the morning. He called once, asking where
he would go, but Hanrahan did not turn.

He never saw Mary Lavelle again, nor did any certain
word of her come to him. Some said she had
children and forgot the red-haired schoolmaster. Some said she died
in a fever in a crowded street. Some said she
would speak his name whenever cards were shuffled, though her
husband knew nothing of it. But these are only the
things people say when a story has no mending.

As for Hanrahan, he went on wandering, carrying his spoiled
books and making songs for other men's weddings. And if
he passed near Slieve Echtge, he would turn his face
away, for he remembered the shining house and the sleeping
queen and the four treasures he had not chosen.

But more often he remembered a little house with smoke
above it, three cows in a field, and Mary Lavelle
looking down the road.

And that was the treasure he had chosen too late.

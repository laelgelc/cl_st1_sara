## MIS' ELDERKIN'S PITCHER

"Come, Uncle Sam," said little Ben, drawing his stool nearer
the fire, "you promised to tell us why Mis' Elderkin
keeps that broken pitcher in the corner cupboard."

"Broken pitcher?" said Sam Lawson, turning his apple round and
round before choosing a place to bite. "Wal, now, there's
pitchers and pitchers. Some is broke in the handle, and
some in the nose, and some is cracked all the
way down and don't know it till ye fill 'em.
Folks is considerable like crockery in that respect."

"We mean the brown pitcher," said Alice, "with the brass
wire round it."

"I know what ye mean. I ain't so old but
what I know a brown pitcher from a blue chest
of drawers. That pitcher's a providence."

"A providence?"

"Yes, a special providence—though it took a wall, and Miry
Brown's temper, to bring it to light. Ye see, children,
things don't allers come labeled. A blessing may look like
an old cracked jug, and a misfortune may drive up
in a shiny buggy with a white horse. I've lived
long enough to see both kinds."

"But what was in it?" Ben demanded.

"Wal, if ye know that now, where's my story? A
story's like plantin' beans: first ye clear the ground, then
ye drop the seed, and arter that ye wait. You
young folks want to pull up the vine before it's
fairly out o' the dirt.

"Now this was when Miry Brown was a girl, and
a girl she was! There wa'n't a pink-and-white, sugar-candy look
to Miry. She was tall, straight, and strong in the
shoulders, with black eyes that could warm ye or freeze
ye according as she'd a mind. Her hair was thick
enough to stuff a cushion, and when she walked into
the singing-school, with her red hood thrown back, every bass
and tenor there forgot whether he was in 'China' or
'Coronation.'

"I was singin' tenor myself in them days."

"Were you in love with her?" Alice asked.

"Love? Who's speakin' of love? I admired her gifts. There's
a difference, and I should be sorry to have anything
repeated that might get round to Mis' Lawson. Your Aunt
Nabby's an excellent woman, but she has a memory like
a steel trap, specially for matters that happened before she
was born.

"Anyhow, Miry had beaus enough. There was Tom Beacon, whose
father owned the sawmill. Tom wore a green coat with
brass buttons and greased his hair so slick that a
fly couldn't have stood on it without creepers. He thought
Miry was bound to take him because he had a
chaise and a silver watch.

"One night, arter singing-school, Tom undertook to kiss her by
the Beacon pasture bars. Miry told him once to behave,
and Tom, bein' one of them men whose ears don't
connect with their understanding, tried again. She caught him by
the collar and waistband and tipped him over the fence
into a drift.

"Snow was deep that winter. If it hadn't been, Tom
might have broke his notions along with his neck. As
it was, he come up lookin' like a frosted doughnut
and never courted Miry more.

"Then there was Deacon Stebbins's nephew, Joel, that talked Scripture
from breakfast to bedtime. He told Miry a virtuous woman
was a crown to her husband.

"'Maybe so,' says Miry, 'but I don't want to be
worn on Joel Stebbins's head.'

"That ended him.

"There was Lije Pepper, too, and young Dr. Crane, and—wal,
no matter about the rest. I might mention one more
if it was necessary, but it ain't. I was busy
that year helpin' my uncle with his cider mill, and
circumstances alters cases.

"Now Miry's father was John Brown, commonly called Black Hoss
John, partly because he raised black horses and partly because
his disposition kicked at both ends. He owned the best
farm beyond North Hill, two wood lots, a strip of
river meadow, and mortgages on half a dozen men who
slept poorly whenever interest day come round.

"He was close—close as a green walnut in frost. Folks
used to say John pinched a cent till the eagle
hollered, and then put it away because noise was wasteful.
He'd walk three miles to save a penny on salt
and wear out two shillin's' worth of shoe leather doin'
it. He fed his hens gravel Saturday nights so their
crops would feel full Sunday, though whether that's gospel truth
or only village improvement I won't undertake to say.

"He was a member of the meetin'-house in good standin',
too. Sat in the third pew, north side, and passed
the contribution box with a face solemn enough to sour
cream. Parson Lothrop once preached on the young ruler who
went away sorrowful because he had great possessions. John said
arterward it was a powerful discourse and wished certain poor
folks had heard it.

"Parson Lothrop had a snug salary, a good woodpile, and
two silver spoons, so it come easier to him to
show the vanity of riches than it would to a
widow with five children and the meal barrel bottom upward.
Still, the doctrine was sound. Doctrine generally is; it's the
application that gets lame.

"Miry kept house for her father, her mother havin' died
when she was fourteen. She churned, baked, spun, made soap,
weeded garden, milked three cows, and could lay a stone
wall nigh as straight as any man in the parish.
She had two brothers, Nathan and Silas, both older and
married, and a sister, Keziah, that had gone to live
in Hartford with a man who sold clocks and always
looked as if he'd swallowed the pendulum.

"The boys stood well with Black Hoss John because they
agreed with him till they got what they wanted. Miry
didn't. If he said black, and it was black, she
said so; but if it was white, she wa'n't goin'
to call it black for a farm and two wood
lots. That made trouble.

"'Women was created to obey,' John would say.

"'Then it's curious,' Miry would answer, 'that Providence gave 'em
tongues to ask why.'

"She was contrary as a pipperage-tree, John said. But there's
two kinds of contrary. One kind sets its foot down
because the road is right, and t'other because the road
is wrong. John never learned the distinction.

"Now, about this time, Bill Elderkin come to town. His
father had been a sea captain and lost ship, cargo,
money, and health all in one winter. Bill had little
but a sound head and a blue coat brushed shiny
at the elbows. He taught the district school, studied law
nights, and read books enough to make ordinary folks nervous.

"He wa'n't handsome in Tom Beacon's polished style. His hair
had a way of standin' up when he was excited,
and his boots knew considerable about mud. But he had
clear gray eyes, a steady voice, and a laugh that
took hold of folks. He could split wood, mend a
clock, argue a case, or quiet a frightened horse; and
there wa'n't a widow in the south district whose path
he hadn't shoveled some stormy mornin'.

"Miry met him at singing-school. Leastways, that's where folks first
noticed it. He handed her the book when they sang
'Lenox,' and she looked at him over the top of
the page. After that they seemed to find the same
book pretty often, though there was twenty-seven copies in the
room.

"I noticed it. I've allers been observin'.

"Did that make you jealous?" asked Ben.

"No. I was relieved. A man with a cider mill
to tend don't want complications. Besides, Bill was a friend
of mine, and I may have spoken a favorable word
for him to Miry—though Bill afterward took all the credit,
as married men generally do.

"They took to walkin' home from Thursday lecture together. Sometimes
they went by the long road under the elms, which
added nigh a mile, but young people in love ain't
calculators. A mile then is shorter than a doorstep arter
they've been married twenty years.

"By spring everybody knew how matters stood. Miry wore a
sprig of lilac Bill gave her, and Bill carried a
mitten she'd knit him in his breast pocket, even when
June come and no mortal hand required wool. That's love.
It makes a man proud of being uncomfortable.

"When Black Hoss John heard of it, he was mad
enough to bite a harrow.

"'A schoolmaster!' says he. 'A pauper with ink on his
fingers!'

"Bill wa'n't a pauper, but John used that word for
anybody who couldn't buy him twice over.

"He ordered Miry to stop seein' Bill.

"'I'm of age,' says Miry.

"'You're under my roof.'

"'I've worked enough under it to shingle it twice.'

"'If you marry that fellow, you don't get a copper
of mine.'

"'Then keep your copper warm,' says she.

"That answer flew round the village before sundown, and I
must say it gained in finish as it traveled.

"Bill, however, had sense. He told Miry he wouldn't marry
her straight out of her father's house with no income
but district school wages. He meant to finish his law
studies, go to the city, and make a position.

"'It may take two years,' says he.

"'Then I can wait two years,' says Miry.

"'Or three.'

"'I can count to three.'

"'Your father'll oppose us.'

"'He's opposed everything since the comet of 1811.'

"So it was settled. Bill went away to study in
Judge Perry's office, forty miles off, and they agreed to
write every week.

"Now you'd think a man as rich as Black Hoss
John could afford honesty. But money don't buy that article
unless there's a demand for it inside. He watched the
post office. He got round old Asa Pike, the postmaster,
by hintin' about a debt Asa owed him; and when
Bill's letters come, John took 'em.

"Miry wrote and wrote. No answers come. Bill wrote and
wrote. No letters reached him. There's no misery quite like
waitin' at a window for somebody that's walking toward ye
while a wall stands between.

"Miry grew pale, but not weak. Folks said Bill had
forgot her. Nathan said young men in cities saw finer
ladies than country girls. Keziah wrote from Hartford that disappointment
should teach submission. Keziah allers prescribed submission for other folks;
she herself kept her clock-selling husband wound tight as an
eight-day clock.

"John watched Miry and saw the hurt workin' in her,
and one night he brought out a paper.

"'Sign that,' says he.

"'What is it?'

"'A promise to give up William Elderkin, now and forever.'

"'No.'

"'He has given you up.'

"'Then he don't need my help.'

"'He hasn't written.'

"'I don't know that.'

"John's face changed a mite, and Miry caught it. She
had eyes like gimlets when suspicion got behind 'em.

"'Father,' says she, 'have you touched my letters?'

"'Everything under my roof belongs to me.'

"'Not my name,' says Miry, 'and not my promise.'

"He shook his cane at her and swore she'd never
have one rood of his land nor one dollar of
his money.

"She stood straight as a young pine. 'I'd rather beg
bread with clean hands than buy it by breakin' faith.'

"Wal, that was Miry. A softer woman might have cried
and signed; a slyer one might have signed and lied.
Miry did neither.

"Not long after, Black Hoss John took sick. It began
with rheumatism, then a winter cough got him, and by
March he was bedfast. Nathan and Silas come round often
enough to ask the doctor how long such complaints commonly
lasted. Their wives brought puddings that John couldn't eat and
carried home eggs because Miry had so many.

"Keziah came from Hartford for four days and spent three
of 'em lookin' through cupboards to see which china she
wanted. Then she said her husband couldn't manage alone and
went back.

"Miry stayed.

"John was a hard sick man. Some sick folks turn
gentle when their strength leaves; John seemed to gather all
his temper into his tongue. The room was too hot,
then too cold. The broth was weak, then greasy. The
pillows was high, low, hard, soft, and crooked, sometimes all
in ten minutes. He rang a little brass bell till
Miry dreamed of sleighs every time she slept.

"She lifted him, fed him, rubbed his joints, sat up
nights, and kept the room cleaner than a new milk
pan. Once, when she'd been awake two nights, he flung
a cup at the hearth because the drink was lukewarm.

"'You'll kill yourself servin' him,' I told her.

"Yes, I went there. I carried wood and did chores.
Charity, that was all, and if Nabby ever says different
she misremembers.

"Miry says, 'He's my father.'

"'He don't act much like one.'

"'That ain't my part of it.'

"There's Scripture without chapter and verse.

"Bill heard through me that John was ill, and he
wrote again. I brought the letter myself and put it
in Miry's hand by the lilac bushes.

"She read it, and the color come back into her
face like sunrise over snow. Bill had never forsook her.
He'd saved every letter he wrote, or copies of 'em,
being lawyer-minded even in courtship. He said he'd wait as
long as she did.

"She folded the letter and put it near her heart.
Then she went back to John and nursed him six
months more.

"Did her father know?" Alice asked softly.

"Not then. Miry didn't tell him. She wa'n't one to
torment a sick man, even when he'd earned a little
reasonable torment.

"By autumn John was thin as a fence rail's shadder.
The maples turned red along the hill, geese went south,
and the wind rattled the dry hollyhocks by the kitchen
window. He knew he was goin'. Folks commonly do, though
some pretend the journey ain't on the schedule.

"He sent for Lawyer Beale and made his will. Nathan
was there, and Silas, and Deacon Stebbins as witness. Miry
was downstairs simmerin' gruel and never asked what they wrote.
John called her up afterward.

"'Will you renounce Elderkin?' he whispered.

"'No, father.'

"'Then you know what to expect.'

"'Yes.'

"'You'll be sorry.'

"Miry looked at him a long time. 'I'm sorry now,'
says she, 'but not for what you mean.'

"He turned his face to the wall.

"That night, just before dawn, he called for her. She
gave him water and held his hand. He tried to
say something, but his breath rattled and the words never
shaped. Once he pointed toward the high shelf in the
keeping-room, then his hand fell.

"He died with Miry beside him.

"Wal, funerals draw relations like molasses draws flies, only the
flies don't wear black gloves. The house filled up. Parson
Lothrop preached from 'Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon
earth,' which some thought a particular sermon for John and
others thought it came too late to improve him.

"Nathan and Silas walked behind the coffin looking solemn and
calculating. Keziah cried into a handkerchief with a lace edge,
but kept one eye on the tall clock. Miry made
all the arrangements and stood dry-eyed. When grief goes too
deep, it don't allers spill.

"A week later Lawyer Beale read the will in the
keeping-room. The farm and west meadow went to Nathan. Silas
got the wood lots, the mortgages, and John's shares in
the turnpike. Keziah got six hundred dollars, the silver spoons,
the tall clock, and most of the furniture.

"Miry sat by the window with her hands folded.

"Lawyer Beale cleared his throat and looked uncomfortable. 'To my
daughter Mary,' he read, 'who has chosen her own way
in defiance of my wishes, I give the brown earthen
pitcher presently standing upon the upper shelf of the keeping-room,
and nothing more.'

"Nathan coughed behind his hand. Silas looked down. Keziah said,
'Dear me,' in a voice that meant, 'Just as I
expected.'

"Miry got up.

"Now that pitcher was the ugliest article in the house.
It was big-bellied, narrow-necked, brown as swamp mud, and cracked
from lip to handle. John's mother had owned it, and
Miry had hated dustin' it since childhood. It wouldn't hold
water; it wa'n't handsome enough for flowers; and it sat
on that shelf year arter year collectin' grease, soot, and
the discouragement of generations.

"Lawyer Beale took it down and handed it to her.

"'Your father's directions is explicit,' says he.

"Miry held it. Her face went white, then red. Mind
ye, she hadn't cared for the money—not in comparison with
Bill—but she'd served that old man night and day while
the others waited for him to die, and now he'd
slapped her from the grave with a cracked jug.

"Keziah says, 'It may have value as a family relic.'

"That done it.

"'Then keep the pieces!' says Miry, and she swung that
pitcher against the chimney wall with all the strength in
her right arm.

"There was a crash fit to wake Black Hoss John
in the buryin'-ground.

"The pitcher broke into fifty pieces—and out over the floor
rolled gold.

"Gold eagles, half eagles, old English guineas, Spanish doubloons, French
pieces, and coins nobody there could name. They came rattlin'
and ringin' across the boards, under chairs, into corners, bright
and dull, thick and thin. One struck Nathan on the
boot. Another rolled between Silas's feet. Keziah dropped her handkerchief
and made a sound like a hen finding a snake.

"Nobody moved for half a minute.

"Then Lawyer Beale shut the door and says, 'By the
terms of the will, the pitcher and its contents belong
to Mary.'

"'But father could not have intended—' Nathan began.

"'He was very particular in his wording,' says Lawyer Beale.

"Silas got down and began gathering coins, which was the
quickest he'd moved since haying.

"Miry stood staring at the broken brown pieces. Then she
laughed. She laughed till she had to sit down, and
once she began, Lawyer Beale laughed, and I laughed—I was
at the door, havin' come on an errand—and even Nathan
made a noise, though his wa'n't cheerful.

"When we counted it, there was near twenty thousand dollars
in gold. In those days twenty thousand dollars was a
fortune broad enough to sit on. John had been hoardin'
coin for forty years, hidin' it in the pitcher nobody
valued.

"Did he mean Miry to have it?" Ben asked.

"Ah, there's the question. Some said he'd forgot the gold
was there, old age havin' muddled him. Some said he
meant to punish her and outwitted himself. Lawyer Beale believed
John remembered at the last and pointed toward the shelf
to tell Miry. I've studied it considerable, and my opinion
is worth what opinions generally fetch when nobody can prove
'em.

"I think John knew. He was mean, but he wa'n't
foolish. Maybe he'd planned to leave her the empty pitcher,
then hid the money in it and never got it
out. Maybe he repented but couldn't bend enough to say
so plain. A stubborn man's repentance often comes sideways, like
a crab through a gate.

"Anyhow, Miry had the gold. Nathan and Silas talked of
contestin' the will, but Lawyer Beale asked whether they wanted
the whole county hearin' how their sister nursed their father
while they measured the fields. That cooled 'em.

"Keziah asked Miry for the tall clock back, claiming sentiment.
Miry told her to keep it, pendulum and all.

"Now Miry didn't fling the money about like corn to
chickens. She had Lawyer Beale put part into sound notes
and part into land near the new canal route. She
bought a small house for herself, paid for repairs on
the school, and sent fifty dollars to a widow John
had squeezed on a mortgage. She could make money behave,
which is rarer than merely havin' it.

"Bill Elderkin finished his studies the next spring and was
admitted to the bar. He came back in a blue
coat not much better than the old one, though his
hair lay flatter. He went straight to Miry.

"'I've got no fortune yet,' says he, 'but I've got
a profession and two clients.'

"'I've got a pitcher,' says she.

"Then she told him.

"They were married in June, when the lilacs was out.
Miry wore gray silk, and Bill looked so proud he
near forgot to answer Parson Lothrop. I stood up with
him. That's the only part I had in the matter,
whatever foolish persons may report.

"They prospered. Bill made a name in law, then went
to the legislature, and afterward folks called him Judge Elderkin.
Miry's canal land rose in value, and she kept account
of every dollar. They built the big white house by
the common, but Miry never got above makin' her own
quince preserves, and Bill never passed a widow's unshoveled path
if he had a shovel handy.

"As for the pitcher, Miry gathered the pieces the very
day it broke. She had 'em fitted together with brass
wire, all but one bit that was ground to powder
against the wall. There it stands in the corner cupboard,
cracked before and cracked worse afterward, yet worth more in
remembrance than all Keziah's silver spoons.

"So now," said Sam, reaching for another apple, "you know
why Mis' Elderkin keeps it."

Ben stared into the fire. "Because the gold was in
it?"

"Partly. But chiefly because it reminds her that she once
took what looked like an insult and broke it open.

"Ye see, children, we're poor judges of the outside of
things. Joseph went into a pit before he got to
Pharaoh's court; Jonah had to see the inside of a
fish before he saw his duty; and Miry Brown had
to inherit the ugliest pitcher in the county before she
found herself an heiress. Providence don't allers use handsome parcels.

"So when a thing comes to ye cracked, dusty, and
contrary to expectation, don't be too quick to throw it
away."

"But Miry did throw it," Alice said.

Sam paused with the apple halfway to his mouth.

"Wal," he said, "don't be too quick—unless you've got Miry
Brown's arm and a good solid wall."

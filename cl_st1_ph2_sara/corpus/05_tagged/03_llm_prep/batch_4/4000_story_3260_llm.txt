# The Station at R——

Who has not cursed a postmaster? Who, detained half an
hour at some lonely station, has not thundered against negligence,
extortion, stupidity, and the ruin of the imperial roads? Who
has not threatened the trembling official with the inspector, the
governor, the Senate itself? Let us confess it: a hungry
traveller is an unjust judge, and a traveller whose carriage
lacks horses is capable of condemning an innocent man to
Siberia.

We arrive wrapped in fur, carrying sealed papers, military epaulettes,
a foreign watch, perhaps only an imperious expression—often the most
serviceable credential of all. We demand twelve horses where there
are six, hot soup where there is no cook, dry
straw after three days of rain, and instant obedience from
a man whom every passing secretary considers born for abuse.
If the postmaster ventures to say that the horses have
gone with a courier, we call him a liar. If
he shows us the empty stable, we accuse him of
concealment. If, exhausted, he lifts his voice, we enter his
insolence in the travelling book; if he remains silent, we
call him a blockhead.

And what is this formidable person? A petty official, generally
of the lowest civil rank; a man with a faded
coat, a scanty salary, a smoky room, a querulous wife
if she still lives, several barefoot children if fever has
spared them, and a register in which the misfortunes of
the road are recorded against his name. Rain leaks through
his roof, snow buries his yard, officers strike the table
with riding-whips, and dignitaries borrow his last samovar without remembering
to return it. Yet in our songs and anecdotes he
is a tyrant, a robber, a provincial Cerberus!

No, let us be just.

During twenty years of wandering I have known many postmasters.
With some I drank tea, with others punch; from one
I borrowed linen after an overturned sledge had baptized me
in a ditch; another kept my purse, which I had
forgotten on the bench, for nearly seven months. Their stories
would fill a volume, and not a wholly dull one.
The road teaches more than geography. It shows us men
stripped of ceremony by fatigue, cold and delay; and, if
the lesson is not always flattering to mankind, it is
at least exact.

Of all those humble sovereigns of the posting stations, none
remains more clearly in my memory than Simeon Virin.

I first met him in the spring of 18——, when
I was young, impatient, and vain enough to suppose that
rank might be read in the cut of a travelling
cloak. I had none, properly speaking; but I possessed the
arrogance of a man certain that he would acquire it.
A cold rain had pursued me from N——. By the
time my light cart drew into the station yard at
R——, water ran from my hat, my boots were grey
with mud, and my driver declared, with the solemnity peculiar
to drivers, that the road ahead had become bottomless.

"Horses!" I cried before entering the house.

"Immediately, sir," answered a cheerful voice.

The postmaster who came out to meet me was a
man of about fifty, though at first glance he appeared
younger. He was broad in the shoulders, fresh-complexioned, and active.
Three ribbons, much faded, decorated his green official coat. While
he examined my travelling paper, he apologized for the weather
as if rain also belonged to his department.

"You shall have horses, sir. Two have only just come
in. Dounia! Put the samovar on. The gentleman is soaked
through."

At the name, a girl appeared from behind a speckled
curtain.

She was fourteen, perhaps a few months more. Beauty at
that age is often merely a promise, but Dounia's had
already acquired composure. Her blue eyes met mine without boldness
and without awkwardness; her fair hair was braided beneath a
little kerchief; her movements were quick, neat, and so assured
that the poor room seemed arranged around her presence. She
carried away my wet coat, spread it near the stove,
gave an order to the yard-boy, and returned with cups
before I had finished pitying myself.

The station room was clean and pleasant. Pots of balsam
stood in the windows. A brass tea-urn shone on the
table. The speckled curtains, though washed thin, were carefully mended.
Above the bench hung four coloured prints depicting the story
of the Prodigal Son. In the first, a young man
in a blue coat received his inheritance from a venerable
father in a nightcap; in the second, he feasted among
ladies with red cheeks and impossible feathers; in the third,
stripped almost to his shirt, he fed swine; and in
the fourth, he returned barefoot, while the father rushed out
to embrace him. Beneath each picture were German verses, which
I could not understand and therefore supposed profound.

I looked at the pictures, then at Dounia, then again
at the pictures. Youth delights in discoveries that have been
prepared for it by chance.

"Is this your daughter?" I asked Virin.

"My own daughter, sir," he replied, and pride softened his
voice. "Her mother died when she was little. Everything here
is managed by Dounia. She knows what is needed before
I do."

"Papa says that because he dislikes counting the linen," said
the girl.

"And because you count better than I."

She smiled and poured the tea.

At that moment a carriage rattled into the yard. A
stout councillor rushed in, demanded horses, learned there were none,
and began the customary storm. Virin listened respectfully. Dounia came
forward and told him that a fresh pair would return
from the next station within twenty minutes; meanwhile she offered
him tea and a slice of cold veal. Whether softened
by the veal or by her eyes, the councillor became
quiet, called Virin "my good fellow," and finally departed without
entering a complaint.

"There, sir," said the postmaster, when the carriage had gone.
"What can't she do? The fiercest gentleman becomes tame when
Dounia speaks to him."

The horses were soon ready, but I did not hurry
to leave. I drank three cups of tea and then
a glass of cherry cordial which Virin produced from a
cupboard. Dounia sewed a ribbon onto her father's sleeve. We
spoke of the road, the harvest, the swollen river, and
the price of oats. Such conversation appears insignificant when repeated,
yet at the time it may warm the heart more
than eloquence.

At last my driver coughed pointedly through the open door.
I put on my coat. Dounia helped me with the
collar.

"May I kiss you goodbye?" I asked, with the agreeable
impudence then common to me.

She glanced at her father.

"Why not?" said Virin. "The gentleman has drunk tea with
us."

Dounia offered her cheek. The kiss was innocent, brief, and
fragrant with fresh bread and balsam. I have since kissed
women admired in three capitals, women whose names made poets
sigh and husbands watchful; but I remember none of those
kisses so clearly as that which a postmaster's daughter gave
me in a poor station room while spring rain beat
upon the windows.

Several years passed.

I travelled the same road again, no longer in a
light cart and no longer quite so pleased with myself.
It was late summer. Dust lay white on the birches,
the fields had begun to yellow, and thunderclouds stood motionless
beyond the plain. I remembered the station at R—— and
wondered whether the balsams still flowered, whether Virin still wore
his medal ribbons, and—let the reader pardon an old traveller's
curiosity—what had become of Dounia.

The station appeared smaller than memory had made it. One
shutter hung crooked. Weeds grew beside the fence. In the
yard a lame hen scratched among broken harness buckles.

No one answered my first call. At last Virin came
from the inner room.

I scarcely recognized him.

His hair, once only touched with grey, was white. His
back had bent; his cheeks had fallen inward; his chin
was unshaven. The green coat remained, but its cloth was
shiny at the elbows and one ribbon had disappeared. He
moved slowly, as though every step required recollection.

"Do you not remember me?" I asked.

He peered into my face.

"Many gentlemen pass this way."

I reminded him of the spring rain, the councillor, the
cherry cordial, and his daughter's ribbon. At Dounia's name he
turned away.

"Ah," he said. "That was long ago."

The room had altered with him. The balsam pots were
gone. Dust clouded the windows. The samovar had lost a
handle, and the speckled curtain was patched with dark cloth.
Only the four pictures remained. The Prodigal Son still received
his money, squandered it, fed the swine, and returned to
his father; but damp had curled the corners, and a
fly had died under the glass upon the prodigal's painted
shoulder.

I asked for tea. Virin set water to boil, fumbling
with the wood. Then, without asking me, he brought out
a bottle of rum. I poured him one glass. He
drank it quickly. After the second, his eyes brightened; after
the third, he recognized me fully, seized my hand, and
called me his old guest.

"Where is Dounia?" I asked gently. "Has she married?"

His face tightened.

"Married? God knows. Alive? God knows that too."

He lowered his head. For some minutes there was no
sound except the bubbling tea-urn. I regretted my question; but
grief, like a locked room, sometimes opens not to delicacy
but to persistence. I poured another glass.

"Tell me, Simeon Virin," I said. "Perhaps speaking will ease
you."

"Ease me?" He laughed without mirth. "Words don't bring back
a child. Tears don't wash a road clean. Still, if
Your Honour wishes to hear—why not? All the world knows
better than her father what was good for Dounia."

He drew his chair nearer and began. I shall give
his account as faithfully as memory allows, preserving, where I
can, his own expressions.

"It was three winters after you came," said Virin. "A
hard winter, with wind that drove the snow through the
window frames. On a Saturday evening, just before dusk, a
travelling sledge flew into the yard. The horses were smoking.
The driver shouted for another team, and a young hussar
entered, wrapped in a military cloak, with a black moustache
and a red cap.

"'Horses!' he says.

"'None at present, Your Nobility,' says I. 'A courier took
the last three pairs. Two should return tonight.'

"At once he flared up. He struck the register with
his glove and swore he would have me flogged. I
had heard worse and bowed. Then Dounia came from behind
the curtain.

"'Perhaps the officer will take supper while he waits,' she
says.

"He looked at her, and it was as though someone
had poured water on a fire.

"'Supper?' he says. 'Very well. And tea.'

"He took off his cloak. He was young, handsome, lively—one
of those gentlemen who enter a hut as if it
were their own house and somehow make you glad to
serve them. His name was Captain Minsky.

"Dounia cooked eggs and cut the cold roast. He ate,
laughed, asked me about the road, and praised everything. The
horses returned sooner than expected. I went in to tell
him. He was lying on the bench with his hand
to his forehead.

"'I cannot travel,' he says. 'My head is splitting.'

"A minute later he began to shiver. Dounia covered him
with my sheepskin. By midnight he was burning with fever—or
so it seemed. What could we do? A sick man
is in God's care. We put him in my bed,
and I slept beside the stove.

"The next morning he was worse. He would neither eat
nor open his eyes. His servant rode to S—— for
a doctor. Dounia soaked cloths in vinegar and laid them
on his forehead. Once, when she bent over him, he
caught her hand and whispered, 'Do not leave me.'

"I saw it. Why should I lie? But I thought:
he is ill; he does not know what he says.

"The doctor arrived toward evening, a German in spectacles, very
red in the face from the cold. He examined the
captain, tapped his chest, looked at his tongue, and said
in French that rest was necessary. I understood that much.
Then the two of them talked together. Minsky gave him
twenty-five roubles, and the doctor dined with him. They drank
a whole bottle of wine.

"I ought to have suspected! The German later told our
priest that he had suspected from the first. From the
first! Yet he ate my pickled mushrooms, drank the captain's
wine, took twenty-five roubles, and said nothing. That is how
clever men protect simple ones."

Here Virin wiped his mouth and drank again.

"Two days passed. The captain improved wonderfully. On Monday he
sat up. On Tuesday he walked about the room in
a dressing gown. On Wednesday he was dressed and joking
with Dounia. He paid for everything generously—too generously. He gave
me money for food, gave the yard-boy a silver coin,
and promised the servant a new coat.

"That morning the bells rang for the liturgy. Dounia put
on her blue cloak and said she was going to
church.

"'I am leaving too,' says Minsky. 'I can take her
as far as the turning.'

"My heart stirred. Why? I cannot say. Perhaps a father
hears misfortune before it enters the door. I said, 'No,
Dounia, you had better stay.'

"She looked surprised.

"Then I was ashamed. What danger could there be? An
officer, a gentleman, a man we had nursed! The church
was only a little beyond the turning.

"'Go, then,' I said. 'His Nobility will take you.'

"She sat in the sledge beside him. She was pale
and trembling. I remember that. Minsky wrapped the bearskin around
her. The servant climbed behind, the driver cracked his whip,
and away they went.

"I stood at the gate until the bells could no
longer be heard.

"An hour passed. The service ended. Our neighbours came home,
but Dounia did not. The priest's wife stopped for tea.

"'Where is your girl?' she asked.

"'Did she not attend church?'

"'No.'

"I ran to the turning. There were only sledge tracks,
and snow had begun to cover them.

"Toward evening the driver returned alone, drunk and frightened. At
first he would say nothing. I seized his collar.

"'Where is Dounia?'

"'She went on with the captain,' he muttered. 'She wept,
but she went.'

"She wept, but she went! Those words have never left
me.

"I fell where I stood. For three days I knew
no one. They say I raved, called her mother's name,
and begged them to stop the horses. When I recovered,
the road was already hard again and the captain was
far away."

Virin paused. His hands trembled on the table.

"Perhaps," I said, "she was forced."

"Perhaps. Perhaps not. A father can live a long while
on that word. The driver said she cried from here
to the first station. After that she grew quiet. At
the second she refused to return. Did Minsky persuade her?
Did she fear shame? Did she love him already? God
saw it. I did not.

"I found from the travelling papers that he was going
to Petersburg. I asked leave from the inspector, though he
cursed and said no one else could keep the station.
I walked part of the way, rode with carts when
anyone would take me, and reached Petersburg after twelve days.

"My old regimental companion lived in the Izmailovsky quarter. He
had lost an eye and sold candles. I slept behind
his stove. Through the hussar's address, written in the register,
I discovered Minsky's lodging.

"He lived at the Hotel Demuth, like a prince. The
first morning I went there. A servant in livery told
me the captain was asleep.

"'I shall wait.'

"'He receives no one before eleven.'

"I waited in the street. Snow melted from the roofs.
Carriages splashed mud onto my boots. At eleven I returned.

"'The captain is dressing.'

"I returned at noon.

"'The captain has gone out.'

"Next morning I came earlier and gave the servant fifty
kopecks. He let me stand in the passage. At last
Minsky came out in a silk dressing gown, smoking a
pipe.

"He knew me immediately. He became pale, then shut the
door behind him.

"'What do you want?' he asks.

"I could not speak properly. I took his hand.

"'Your Nobility,' I said, 'what is done is done. Give
me back my poor Dounia. You have had your amusement.
Do not destroy her altogether.'

"He pulled away.

"'You are mistaken about me,' he said. 'I confess I
behaved badly toward you. I should have asked—I should not
have deceived you. But Dounia loves me. She left of
her own will.'

"'Then let her tell me so.'

"'That would only torment her.'

"'I am her father.'

"'And for that reason you ought to wish her happiness.'

"'Happiness!' I cried. 'What happiness can there be for a
poor station girl in a gentleman's rooms? Today silk, tomorrow
the street! Give her back while she is still innocent
enough to come.'

"He frowned.

"'She cannot return to what she was. You must understand
that. I shall never abandon her. She will have everything.
I give you my word.'

"'Your word! And when you marry a lady of rank?'

"He walked up and down the room. Then he stopped
before me.

"'Old man,' he said, 'I am guilty toward you, and
I ask forgiveness. But I cannot give her up.'

"He pressed something into my hand and pushed me gently
toward the door.

"'Do not be angry. She is happy. You will see.'

"The door closed. In the street I opened my hand.
There were banknotes—five hundred roubles, perhaps more.

"For a moment I stared at them. Then my whole
body burned. I threw them into the gutter and stamped
them into the mud.

"I walked to the corner. There I thought: Why waste
money that belongs to Dounia? I went back.

"The notes were gone.

"A well-dressed young man was climbing into a cab. He
had seen me throw them down. Perhaps he dined well
that day and drank to paternal honour."

The postmaster smiled bitterly.

"For two days I wandered through Petersburg. Every blue cloak
made my heart stop. Every fair-haired girl seemed Dounia from
behind. I stood outside Minsky's hotel, but he did not
return. At last the servant, who had grown tired of
me, said, 'The young lady does not live here.'

"I gave him my last silver piece. He named a
street near the Liteiny.

"I went there. A coachman was polishing a splendid carriage
before a handsome house. I asked whose it was.

"'Captain Minsky's.'

"'Does he live here?'

"'He visits.'

"'Whom?'

"The coachman winked. I wanted to strike him, but remembered
my grey hair.

"'When does he come?'

"'Usually in the evening.'

"I waited at a tavern across the street. At dusk
his carriage arrived. He entered the house. I waited another
hour, then went to the door. A maid opened it.

"'What do you want?'

"'I have a message for Avdotia Simeonovna.'

"She stared at me.

"'There is no such person here.'

"Behind her another servant called, 'Who is it, Katia?'

"I heard Dounia's voice.

"I pushed past the maid.

"The rooms were brighter than any church on Easter night.
Lamps burned under silk shades. There were carpets, mirrors, flowers
in winter—real flowers! On a table lay gloves, French books,
jewels in a little open case. Dounia sat in a
white gown trimmed with lace. Diamonds shone in her ears.
Her hair was arranged like a great lady's. Minsky stood
behind her chair, bending over her. She was looking up
at him and winding one of his black curls around
her finger.

"For a moment I did not know her. That is
the truth. Then she turned.

"'Dounia!' I said.

"She looked at me, opened her mouth, and fell to
the carpet.

"Minsky rushed forward. When he saw me, his face changed.

"'You!' he cried. 'How dare you come here?'

"I went toward my child.

"'Let me raise her.'

"He caught me by the shoulders and dragged me into
the passage.

"'You will kill her with your scenes! I told you
she was happy.'

"'Let me speak to her once.'

"'Never come here again.'

"He struck me—yes, Your Honour, struck an old man—and thrust
me down the stairs. The door slammed. I remained on
the landing, hearing servants run about inside and Dounia's maid
calling for water.

"I do not remember reaching the street.

"The next day I left Petersburg."

Virin's voice failed. He bent over the table, and for
a while I pretended to examine the pictures on the
wall. The prodigal in the last print knelt at his
father's feet; the old man's arms were open. Art is
sometimes crueller than life.

"Did you never hear from her?" I asked at last.

"Not one word."

"Perhaps she wrote and the letter was lost."

"Letters find those who are waited for."

"He may have married her."

"A hussar marry a stationmaster's daughter?"

"It happens."

"Not to us."

He rose, opened the cupboard, and found the bottle empty.

"Every year," he continued, "girls disappear along the roads. Some
come back barefoot. Some do not come back at all.
At first they ride in carriages, then they walk, then
they vanish. Dounia was clever; Dounia was good. But the
clever are deceived, and the good are not spared. When
gentlemen tire of them, where do they go? Tell me
that."

I had no answer.

"Sometimes I dream she is standing outside in the snow,"
he said. "I hear her knock. I hurry to open,
but my hands cannot lift the latch. Then she says,
'Papa, it is cold.' I wake, and the room is
empty."

He covered his face.

I confess that my eyes filled with tears. The rum
may have contributed; the hour was late, rain had begun
to strike the windows, and melancholy is readily communicated in
a lonely station. Yet I will not surrender those tears
altogether to the bottle. They were deserved by the old
man's grief, even if grief had made him unjust to
the living and tender toward despair.

My horses arrived. I wished to give him money, but
remembered the banknotes in the gutter. Instead I bought from
him a useless bundle of old route sheets at twice
their value. He noticed the pretence and thanked me without
looking up.

As I left, he stood in the doorway. The wind
blew his white hair across his forehead.

"If you ever see her, sir," he called after me,
"tell her that her father waited."

The road soon turned, and the station disappeared behind the
birches.

Again years passed.

Fortune carried me far from those provinces. I travelled abroad,
returned, changed occupations, acquired a rank which in youth I
had valued excessively, and lost friends whose immortality I had
never questioned. Many times I remembered Virin and thought of
stopping at R——; but haste, bad weather, official errands, and
that indolence which disguises itself as necessity prevented me. We
imagine that what waits today will wait tomorrow. This error
is the quiet accomplice of many sorrows.

At last, in an autumn already touched by frost, I
found myself once more upon the old road.

The day was colourless. Reaped fields stretched beneath low clouds;
crows rose from the stubble; yellow leaves ran before the
wheels. Near R—— I dismissed my carriage at the crossroads
and walked toward the station, wishing—why, I cannot say—to approach
it slowly.

The building remained, but it was no longer a station.
The imperial sign had been removed. A brewer occupied the
rooms. Barrels stood in the yard where post-horses had once
stamped and jingled their harness. The fence had fallen, and
a pig slept beneath the window.

A broad woman came out, wiping her arms on her
apron.

"Does Simeon Virin live here?" I asked.

"The old postmaster? He's dead."

"When?"

"Three years now, maybe four. My husband bought the place
afterward."

"Where was he buried?"

"In the churchyard beyond the village."

"Can someone show me?"

She called into the yard.

"Vanka! Come here, you little devil!"

A boy of seven or eight crawled from behind a
barrel. He was barefoot despite the cold and carried a
bent tin hoop.

"Show the gentleman the postmaster's grave," said the woman. "And
don't take him through the marsh."

The boy ran ahead, striking weeds with a stick. The
village lay beyond a shallow stream. Smoke rose straight from
the chimneys. Women were lifting flax from the fields; an
old man mended a cart under a willow. Nothing in
the scene appeared tragic. Nature and poverty have little time
for monuments.

"Did you know the postmaster?" I asked.

"Grandfather Simeon? Of course."

"Was he kind?"

"He gave us nuts."

"What did he die of?"

The boy considered.

"He drank too much, Mother says. Then he lay down
and didn't get up. Father and Uncle Klim carried him."

"Did anyone visit him?"

"No."

"Did he speak of his daughter?"

"He used to tell us not to run after carriages."

We reached the small churchyard. Its wooden crosses leaned in
every direction. Nettles grew among the graves. The boy led
me to a mound near the wall, marked by a
dark cross with a little copper icon. The earth had
sunk. Someone had placed dried flowers beneath the cross; they
were not village flowers, but pale hothouse roses, tied with
a faded ribbon.

"Who brought these?" I asked.

"The lady."

"What lady?"

"A beautiful lady. Very rich. She came in summer."

The boy's face brightened; simple people remember generosity more exactly
than dates.

"She came in a carriage with six horses," he continued.
"There were three little children, and a nurse, and a
black dog. The driver asked where the stationmaster lived. Mother
said, 'He is dead.' Then the lady became white and
held the door. She asked where he was buried.

"I brought her here."

"What happened?"

"She told the children to stay with the nurse. Then
she came to the grave alone. She knelt down there."

He pointed to the damp earth.

"She put her arms around the mound and cried. Not
loudly at first. Then she cried very much. I waited
by the wall. She lay there a long time, with
her face on the ground. The nurse came, but the
lady waved her away.

"Afterward she went to church and gave Father Aleksei money
for prayers. She gave Mother money too. She gave me
a silver rouble."

"Did she say who she was?"

"No. But she was such a kind lady—such a kind
lady! She kissed me and asked whether Grandfather Simeon had
suffered. I said I didn't know. Then she cried again."

"Were her children boys or girls?"

"Two boys and a little girl. The little girl had
yellow curls. She wanted to come to the grave, but
the nurse would not let her."

I dismissed the boy with a coin. He thanked me,
then ran after his hoop, leaving me alone.

The wind stirred the dried roses. One petal loosened, rolled
across the mound, and caught against my boot.

So Dounia had returned.

She had not, it seemed, been abandoned in the streets.
Minsky's promise—at least the worldly part of it—had perhaps been
kept. Whether he married her, whether she became his widow,
whether those three children bore his name, I never learned.
Wealth had clothed her, protected her, and carried her back
in a splendid carriage over the same road by which
she had once departed weeping. Yet wealth, so powerful at
stations, had failed before a poor grave. Six horses had
brought her too late.

What would Virin have done had he lived to see
her? Would he have opened his arms like the father
in the coloured print? Would grief and wounded pride have
melted at the first word, "Papa"? Or would he have
turned away, remembering the locked door, the blow upon the
stairs, the years without a letter? We may answer according
to our own hearts; but the dead preserve their silence.

For my part, I believe he would have forgiven her
before she crossed the threshold. He had condemned her every
day only because he loved her every hour. His anger
was the last poor possession left to him by loss;
one embrace would have taken it away.

I remained beside the grave until the autumn rain began.
Across the wall, the village boy shouted to his companions.
A cart creaked along the distant road. Somewhere beyond the
fields a bell rang at a station no longer visible,
summoning fresh horses for some impatient traveller.

I thought of the first spring morning: the shining tea-urn,
the balsams in the window, the speckled curtain, the girl
bending over her father's sleeve. I thought of the pictures
on the wall and their simple promise that wandering ends
in return, guilt in repentance, grief in reconciliation. Life had
observed the design and altered only one detail.

The daughter had come home.

The father was not there.

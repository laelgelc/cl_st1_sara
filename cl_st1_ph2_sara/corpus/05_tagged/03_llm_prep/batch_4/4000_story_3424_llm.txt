On the afternoon of the thirtieth of April, whilst staying
at Munich, I conceived the desire to see something of
the country beyond the usual circuit of gardens, galleries, and
royal avenues with which the stranger is commonly satisfied. The
day had broken with uncommon brightness. A pale sun shone
through a veil of pearl-coloured vapour, and the distant Alps,
though faint as clouds, presented that alluring appearance which makes
every road seem too short and every horizon a promise.

My landlord procured for me a light carriage and a
driver named Johann, a broad-shouldered Bavarian with an honest red
face, small blue eyes, and a moustache which seemed disproportionate
to his other features. He spoke enough English for ordinary
purposes, and I possessed sufficient German to make up the
deficiency. We set out after breakfast, and for some hours
all went pleasantly. The air was brisk, the horses spirited,
and Johann, when not occupied with his reins, pointed out
farmsteads, shrines, and distant villages, giving me such scraps of
local history as he could command.

Toward the middle of the afternoon we reached a place
where a narrow road, little more than two wheel-ruts with
grass between, left the highway and ran away over the
bare upland.

"Where does that go?" I asked.

Johann looked in the direction I indicated, and his cheerfulness
vanished.

"No place, mein Herr."

"No place? Roads are seldom made for no place."

He gathered up the reins and urged the horses onward.
Both animals had already shown uneasiness, turning their heads toward
the side road and snorting as though some odour displeased
them.

"There was village," he said reluctantly. "Long ago. Now—nothing."

"Ruins?"

"Ja. Ruins—and graves."

This last addition interested me more than it alarmed me.
I had spent too many wet afternoons among English abbeys
and too many summer mornings in Roman burial grounds to
regard either ruins or graves as reasons for avoiding a
road. I told Johann to turn.

He crossed himself.

"No, no, mein Herr! We go Munich. It grows late."

The sun was still well above the western hills, though
a bank of cloud had begun to rise under it.

"Late? We have three hours at least."

Johann drew out a large silver watch, consulted it, shook
his head, and put it away with a nervous haste
which made me laugh.

"To-night," he said, bending toward me, "is Walpurgis nacht."

He spoke the words as if they contained their own
argument. When I merely smiled, he repeated them more loudly.

"Walpurgis nacht! The night when—when all bad things go free.
The dead walk. The witches—" He flung one hand upward
and made a movement like something flying. "Ach! We must
not be here when dark comes."

"My dear Johann," said I, "witches may be troublesome to
Bavarians, but Walpurgis Night does not concern Englishmen."

He did not join in my amusement. Instead, he glanced
behind us and then over the open plateau. There was
little to see: a few strips of black woodland, distant
fields, and the narrow road stretching white between brown grasses.
Yet his look was that of a man who suspects
an enemy to be concealed in every hollow.

I insisted upon learning more of the abandoned village. By
degrees, and with many pauses, I obtained the story. The
place had been deserted generations before after a succession of
inexplicable deaths. Certain persons, buried with the rites of the
Church, had afterwards been found when their graves were opened—not
wasted by decay, but ruddy, swollen, and with fresh blood
upon their mouths. The survivors had fled, leaving houses, chapel,
and dead together. Since then no peasant would live within
many miles of it.

Johann's account, mutilated by his imperfect English and made absurd
by his gestures, seemed to me no more than one
of those legends which cluster round any place long abandoned.
I pressed him again to turn. His face became obstinate.

"Never! Not with horses. They know."

As though to confirm him, one of the animals reared
so suddenly that the carriage swung sideways. At that instant
there came from far off—a great way beyond the dark
belt of woods—a long, low cry. It might have been
a dog, though its note was deeper and more melancholy
than any dog's voice I had heard.

The horses plunged together. Johann mastered them only with difficulty.
When at last they stood trembling, their hides were wet
and their nostrils flecked with foam.

"You see!" he cried. "Wolf!"

"Then lend me your stick," said I. "I shall walk
a little way and return. You may wait here if
you are afraid."

I confess that the last words were deliberately unjust. His
terror was real, but it was not cowardice; he feared
for me as much as for himself. He caught my
sleeve.

"Mein Herr, do not go! We must drive. The storm
comes."

The bank of cloud had indeed risen with remarkable speed.
It now covered much of the west, and ragged fragments
raced before it, extinguishing the sunlight by intervals. Yet the
air where we stood was still, and there remained in
the east a wide field of clear blue.

"I shall be back before the storm," I answered, stepping
down.

Johann pleaded, argued, invoked the saints, and at length cursed
the road in a torrent of German too rapid for
me to follow. When he saw that I was not
to be moved, he thrust his heavy walking-stick into my
hand. Then, with an expression almost of despair, he climbed
again to his seat.

"One hour," he said. "Only one! Then I come—or soldiers
come. But, ach, no! I cannot come there."

Again the distant cry sounded. This time it seemed nearer.

The horses sprang forward before Johann had properly turned them.
The carriage lurched, narrowly missed the ditch, and went thundering
back toward Munich. For a few moments I watched the
driver struggling with the terrified beasts; then a curve hid
them, and the clatter of wheels diminished into silence.

I stood alone.

The solitude, which at first was pleasant after Johann's importunities,
soon impressed itself upon me with a peculiar force. There
was no dwelling in sight, no smoke, no movement upon
the road. Even the birds had ceased to call. Across
the plateau the long grass bent, though I felt no
wind upon my cheek. The clouds continued to advance, black
beneath and white at their torn edges, like smoke from
some prodigious conflagration beyond the hills.

Nevertheless, I walked on with a light heart. It is
difficult for a healthy man, abroad in daylight and possessed
of sound limbs, to believe that an old peasant tale
can have power over him. I reflected that Johann would
soon recover his temper, and that I should have an
amusing apology to offer when I returned to the hotel
after discovering no more terrible apparition than an owl among
ruined rafters.

The side road descended gradually. Presently the bare upland gave
place to scattered firs, and these in turn thickened into
a wood. The track, though neglected, showed traces of ancient
paving beneath the moss. Here and there stood weathered stones
carved with crosses or letters too worn to read. At
one place I found a little wayside shrine. Its niche
was empty, but someone—perhaps a shepherd, perhaps some fearful traveller—had
lately placed within it a sprig of withered rowan tied
with red thread.

Beyond the shrine the road divided.

One branch passed between the trees. The other dipped sharply
into a hollow so overgrown that I might have overlooked
it had not a broken milestone leaned beside the entrance.
I examined the stone and made out the remains of
a name, though not enough to determine it. Beneath were
two dates separated by nearly two centuries.

Whilst I stood there, the stillness was broken by a
quick gust which swept dead leaves round my feet. The
trees sighed together. Then all was quiet once more.

I chose the descending path.

The hollow deepened into a narrow valley, and the sky
dwindled to a dark strip overhead. Thorn bushes caught at
my coat. Twice I was obliged to climb fallen trees,
their roots projecting from the earth like stiffened claws. The
air grew colder with every step. It was not the
wholesome cold of mountain weather, but a damp, creeping chill
which seemed to issue from the soil.

After perhaps half an hour the trees opened, and I
saw the village.

At first I took the grey shapes for rocks. Then
I distinguished walls, roofless gables, and the stump of a
tower. The houses lay in no regular street but clustered
along the valley floor as though they had drawn close
together for safety. Most were mere shells. A few retained
portions of their roofs, black with age and sunk inward.
No door hung upon its hinges; no pane remained in
any window. The chapel stood upon slightly higher ground, its
little belfry split from top to base.

There are ruins which speak of ordinary desertion—of poverty, war,
or the slow migration of families. This place suggested something
more sudden. In one house an iron pot still stood
upon a stone hearth. In another I saw the remains
of a bedstead. A child's wooden stool lay overturned before
a doorway, bleached by years of rain and snow. It
seemed that the inhabitants had not removed, but fled.

The clouds had now covered the entire sky. A faint
greenish light remained, by which every stone appeared unnaturally distinct.
I began to regret that I had allowed Johann to
depart, though not yet from any supernatural apprehension. I had
come farther than intended; the road was bad; and there
was plainly a severe storm at hand.

I turned to retrace my steps.

At that very moment the long cry rose again.

It was no longer distant. It came from the wood
above the village.

A second voice answered from the opposite ridge.

I tightened my grasp upon Johann's stick and walked rapidly.
I had no fear of a single wolf, but the
possibility of a pack in such lonely country was another
matter. The path by which I had entered the village,
however, seemed changed. The gathering darkness confused the ruined walls;
openings appeared where none had been, and familiar landmarks disappeared.
After passing twice beneath the broken belfry, I understood that
I had gone in a circle.

Then the storm descended.

There was no gradual beginning. One instant the air was
merely cold; the next a blast rushed through the valley
with a roar like heavy surf. Dust, twigs, and fragments
of dead grass flew upward. The trees bent till their
branches swept the ground. A shutter, torn from some hidden
ruin, whirled past me and shattered against the chapel wall.

I sought shelter in the nearest house, but had hardly
entered when part of the roof crashed down behind me.
The remaining walls shook. I escaped through a window and
ran into the open, where the first hailstones struck my
face.

They were of extraordinary size and hardness. Within moments the
earth was white with them. Lightning flickered continuously above the
ridges, not in distinct bolts, but as a vast pulsing
illumination behind the clouds. Thunder followed in rolling peals which
mingled with the wind until the whole valley seemed filled
by one sustained and furious voice.

I made for the higher ground behind the chapel, hoping
to find the road from above. Before I had climbed
twenty yards, the hail changed to snow.

Snow—in that season, after a morning almost warm!

It fell in dense, horizontal sheets. The village vanished. The
chapel vanished. I could see only the whirling white immediately
before my eyes. The wind drove me sideways, and the
stones beneath my feet became treacherous. Once I fell heavily
and cut my hand. The blood appeared almost black upon
the snow, then was covered before I had regained my
feet.

A singular fancy took possession of me: that the storm
was not merely violent, but intentional. Whenever I turned toward
what I believed to be the open valley, the blast
met me full in the face; whenever I allowed it
to drive me, the ground rose more steeply and the
trees grew thicker. I tried three times to descend. Three
times the storm hurled me back.

Cold entered my body as water enters a sinking vessel.
My fingers lost their feeling. My breath came painfully. I
began to understand Johann's reference to the hour, and to
wonder whether he had reached Munich or whether the horses
had overturned the carriage. Above all, with every fresh roar
of wind, those words beat upon my memory:

Walpurgis Night.

I had laughed at them under the sun. In that
blind wilderness they were no longer ridiculous.

A flash brighter than the rest revealed, for perhaps one
second, a row of upright stones among the trees. I
staggered toward them and found an old wall, low in
places but solid enough to afford shelter. An opening stood
before me. I passed through it and almost fell over
a sunken grave.

I was in a cemetery.

The snow lay less deeply there because of the cypresses
and ancient yews which crowded within the enclosure. Their black
branches interlaced overhead, making a vault beneath which the wind
moaned rather than shouted. Tombstones leaned at every angle, some
green with moss, others split by roots. Crosses rose from
the white ground like lifted arms. On many graves the
earth had sunk into long hollows; on others it was
strangely heaped.

I knew that I ought to leave, yet the comparative
shelter was irresistible. My strength had diminished so much that
each step required an effort of will. Somewhere amongst those
trees there might be a vault or porch where I
could wait until the worst had passed.

Moving slowly, I came upon a broad avenue which led
toward the centre of the burial ground. Unlike the rest
of the place, it was paved, and though grass grew
between the stones, there were signs that some heavy object
had passed along it not many years before. At its
end stood a solitary tomb.

Even in that confused light I perceived it to be
of uncommon magnificence. It was built of white marble, discoloured
in streaks but largely untouched by moss. Four black cypresses
stood at its corners. Above the bronze door rose the
carved figure of a woman lying as if asleep, her
hands crossed upon her breast. From the roof projected a
great iron spike, broad at the base and sharp at
the summit. It might have been intended as a lightning
conductor, though no wire descended from it.

I approached the door and brushed the snow from its
inscription. The letters, deeply cut, were German:

COUNTESS DOLINGEN OF GRATZ
IN STYRIA
SOUGHT AND FOUND DEATH
1801

Below, in characters less regular, as though added by another
hand, were Latin words which I translated with some difficulty:

THE DEAD TRAVEL SWIFTLY.

As I read, there came from within the tomb a
faint sound.

I cannot say whether it was a sigh, a movement,
or merely snow sliding over marble. I stood motionless, listening.
My reason offered a dozen explanations: wind in a crevice,
water within a drain, some small creature sheltered there. Yet
not one satisfied me. The sound had possessed a quality
too nearly human.

I stepped back.

A shaft of lightning struck beyond the cemetery wall. Every
tree sprang into dazzling whiteness, and from the ruined village
below there arose such a tumult of howling that my
blood seemed to stop. There were many wolves now—scores, perhaps.
Their voices came from the valley, from the woods, from
every side, and through them all sounded one deeper note,
measured and commanding.

The bronze door of the tomb stood slightly open.

I was certain that it had been closed when first
I approached. Indeed, I remembered clearing snow from the seam
between door and frame. Now a black interval showed, no
wider than my finger, yet unmistakable.

Another sigh came from within.

Curiosity has been the cause of many disasters, but curiosity
alone did not draw me forward. I felt, or imagined
that I felt, a pressure behind me—a steady influence which
was neither hand nor wind. At the same time the
cold became so bitter that the darkness of the tomb
promised shelter and perhaps warmth.

I set my shoulder against the bronze.

The door resisted. Then something yielded with a metallic crack,
and it swung inward.

The interior was not entirely dark. Lightning, reflected from the
snow, entered through the doorway in pale pulsations. I saw
a chamber lined with marble and, in its centre, a
stone bier. Upon it rested an open coffin.

Within the coffin lay a woman.

There are sights which memory preserves not as pictures but
as wounds. Though many years have passed, I can still
see her face whenever I close my eyes: the smooth
forehead, the dark lashes, the rich masses of hair, and
the lips—those dreadful lips—red as though fresh blood moved beneath
them. She wore a white garment clasped at the throat
with a ruby. Her hands were folded; her skin possessed
neither the waxen hue nor the collapse of death. The
cheeks were faintly rosy. The breast—may God witness that I
speak only what I saw—seemed to rise and fall.

I could not move.

Her eyes opened.

They were black, and in them shone a recognition more
terrible than surprise. Her lips parted. The pointed whiteness of
her teeth appeared between them.

At once the tomb filled with a perfume, heavy and
sweet, like flowers kept too long in a sealed room.
It overcame the damp odour of stone and age. I
felt my senses swim. The woman raised one hand.

Then some force seized me.

It did not pull from before, nor push from behind.
Rather, the air itself hardened and flung me outward. I
crossed the threshold without touching the floor and struck the
snow several yards from the tomb.

At that instant the heavens opened in one sheet of
fire.

The iron spike upon the sepulchre received the lightning. A
blue-white column leapt from cloud to metal, and the marble
burst with a report beyond thunder. The door flew from
its hinges. Fragments of stone rained amongst the trees.

For the fraction of a second during which I retained
sight, the whole interior was exposed. The woman had risen
upright in her coffin. Flame wrapped her white clothing, yet
her face remained visible, no longer beautiful but distorted by
rage. Her mouth was open, and from it issued a
scream so piercing that it seemed to pass through my
skull rather than through the air.

The next flash revealed red—not merely fire, but a fountain
of red springing from the shattered bier. It splashed the
walls, the coffin, the snow outside. Forms moved behind it:
pale forms, sheeted and wavering, crowded in recesses which I
had thought solid marble.

Then roof and wall fell together.

Something struck my head.

The cemetery, the storm, and the flaming woman disappeared.

What followed exists in my recollection as disconnected sensations.

First there was cold—an immeasurable cold, beneath which I seemed
to be sinking through layers of snow into the earth
itself. I knew that if I surrendered to it, sleep
would come; and I knew also, with that strange certainty
possible in dreams, that sleep would be death. I struggled
to raise my arms, but they belonged to someone else.
My eyelids would not obey me.

Then came warmth.

It spread slowly across my chest and side, so welcome
that for a while I did not question its source.
A heavy weight pressed upon me. Warm vapour touched my
face at regular intervals. Something rough passed over my throat.

I heard a low growl.

Consciousness returned with a violence almost as great as the
lightning. I opened my eyes.

A gigantic wolf lay upon me.

Its forepaws were planted on either side of my shoulders,
and the whole weight of its body held me down.
Its coat was grey, though along the spine it appeared
nearly black. Snow clung to the coarse hair. The head
was larger than that of any wolf I had seen
in menageries, and its eyes, close above mine, burned with
a reddish light.

Its muzzle rested against my neck.

I felt the wet tongue move over the skin where
the pulse beat. The animal's breath was hot. Its teeth
touched me lightly—so lightly that they did not break the
flesh.

No human terror can equal the helplessness of that moment.
Had it torn my throat, the end would have been
instantaneous; but it did not tear. It licked, paused, and
listened. When I made the smallest movement, a growl vibrated
through its chest into mine, and I lay still.

Yet, as I am a living man, the creature was
warming me.

Its body shielded me from the snow, and where its
flank covered my breast sensation gradually returned. The agony of
returning blood passed through my limbs like hot needles. The
wolf could have killed me a hundred times. Instead it
guarded me—or reserved me.

From beyond the wall came faint voices.

The wolf lifted its head. A long yelp burst from
it, unlike the savage cries I had heard before. It
was almost a summons.

Far off, men shouted.

A light appeared between the trees, vanished, and appeared again.
Then came another and another, moving irregularly through the storm.
I heard horses, the clink of metal, and a command
given in German.

The wolf yelped once more.

"There!" someone cried. "To the left!"

The lights drew nearer. They were torches carried by mounted
soldiers, their flames beaten flat by the wind. The wolf
rose from my chest. For one instant it stood over
me, its legs rigid and its head turned toward the
approaching men. In the torchlight its mouth appeared red.

A shot rang out.

The animal sprang aside. Another soldier fired, and the bullet
struck a tombstone, scattering chips across the snow. The wolf
bounded between two yews and disappeared—not fleeing in panic, but
moving with a speed and certainty which made pursuit impossible.

The soldiers surrounded me. Their uniforms were white with snow,
their horses steaming and wild-eyed. One man dismounted and knelt
at my side. He touched my face, then my heart.

"He lives!"

"Impossible," said another. "In this cold?"

"The beast lay on him."

"To feed."

"No. Look at the throat."

Several faces bent over me. The nearest soldier unfastened my
collar. I tried to speak, but could produce only a
groan.

"No wound," he said. "Not even the skin broken."

"A wolf—and yet not a wolf," whispered a younger man.

His officer turned sharply. "It was a dog."

The youth stared toward the trees. "Herr Leutnant, I saw—"

"You saw a dog. We all saw a dog. Do
you understand?"

The men exchanged looks. One crossed himself when he thought
the officer was not watching.

They wrapped me in cloaks and lifted me onto a
horse before one of the troopers. As they did so,
the clouds parted for a moment and moonlight poured upon
the cemetery. The shattered tomb lay at the end of
the avenue, smoking faintly. The marble around it was stained
dark. I saw one soldier go toward it with a
torch. He had taken no more than three steps when
he stopped.

"What is it?" called the officer.

The man did not answer.

Another joined him. Both stood staring. The torch shook in
the first man's hand.

"Come away!" the officer ordered.

"But there is someone under the stones," said the second
soldier. "A woman. She—she is not burned."

From the ruin came a soft cracking sound.

The men recoiled together. One dropped his torch. It fell
upon the snow and went out with a hiss.

Then, from somewhere beneath the broken marble, there sounded a
laugh.

It was low, brief, and unmistakably feminine.

The horses reared. My own mount wheeled so suddenly that
only the trooper's arm prevented me from falling. The officer
shouted an order, and the whole party moved at once
toward the gate. Discipline became little more than controlled flight.
No man looked back.

As we passed through the opening in the wall, a
dark shape appeared upon the ridge. The gigantic wolf stood
outlined against the moon. It watched us in silence. When
we reached the trees, it threw up its head and
howled.

Every horse broke into a gallop.

Of that return journey I remember only fragments: torchlight streaming
backward; branches striking my face; the hot neck of the
horse against my hand; men speaking urgently on either side;
and now and again the sensation that something followed us
just beyond the circle of light. Once the young soldier
looked back and cried out. The officer demanded what he
had seen.

"Nothing," he answered after a pause. "Only—the dog."

No one rebuked him.

I next became fully conscious in my room at the
hotel. A fire burned high upon the hearth, and the
curtains were drawn. My landlord sat beside the bed, whilst
a physician whom I had met previously in the dining
room held my wrist. On seeing my eyes open, the
landlord uttered an exclamation and hurried to pour wine.

"You have had a narrow escape, sir," said the physician.
"Exposure, a blow to the head, and a degree of
exhaustion which I should have thought impossible in so short
an interval. But you are fortunate. Very fortunate."

My first question concerned Johann.

"He is safe," the landlord said. "The horses brought him
almost to the city at a gallop. One wheel was
broken, and he was scarcely able to speak. He declared
that you had gone alone toward the old valley."

"He warned me," I said.

"He did more than warn you, sir. He begged the
police, the garrison, everyone he could find. At first they
thought him drunk. Then came the storm."

"And the soldiers?"

The landlord's expression altered. "They found you."

"In the cemetery?"

He hesitated. "Near it."

"I was inside."

"You must not excite yourself."

"There was a tomb. Countess Dolingen. Ask them what they
saw."

The physician and landlord looked at one another. The former
laid a calming hand upon my arm.

"The mind under extreme cold creates vivid impressions."

"Did cold create the wolf?"

"The soldiers report that a large dog was found lying
across you."

"A dog!"

"So their officer says."

I laughed, though there was little mirth in the sound.
"And the tomb? Was that also an impression?"

Neither replied. Their silence told me more than any denial.

For two days I remained confined to bed. Johann came
on the second morning. His face, never pale by nature,
had a grey, sickly colour. The moment he saw me
he began to weep. He kissed my hand, begged my
forgiveness for leaving me, and poured out prayers of gratitude
in German.

"You were right," I told him. "It was Walpurgis Night."

At that he crossed himself three times.

I questioned him regarding the village and Countess Dolingen, but
he would say little. He knew the name, for at
its mention his eyes widened; yet all I could extract
was that the Countess had come from Styria, had died
far from her proper home, and had been buried against
the advice of local priests. Later, disturbances had occurred. Her
grave had been opened, closed again, blessed, cursed, and finally
abandoned.

"Why the iron stake upon the roof?" I asked.

Johann shuddered.

"Not upon roof," he said. "Through roof. Down—down to her."

That explanation returned to me at night, when the wind
moved the curtains and the fire sank low.

On the third day I was permitted to rise. The
landlord visited me after breakfast, carrying several papers. He had
been kind beyond expectation, but there remained in his manner
a reserve which I attributed at first to concern for
my health. At length he stood before the window and
said:

"There is something you should know. The search was not
organized only because Johann returned."

"What other reason could there be?"

He selected a sheet from the papers. "A telegram arrived."

"For me?"

"Concerning you."

"From whom?"

He gave it to me.

The message had been sent from Bistritz and bore a
name then scarcely known to me, though I had arranged
to visit its owner during my travels eastward.

It read:

YOUR ENGLISH GUEST MAY HAVE STRAYED FROM THE ROAD. HE
IS IMPULSIVE AND DOES NOT UNDERSTAND THE DANGERS OF THE
COUNTRY. LOSE NO TIME. SEND HORSEMEN IN EVERY DIRECTION, ESPECIALLY
TOWARD THE OLD VALLEY. HIS SAFETY IS OF THE HIGHEST
IMPORTANCE. SPARE NO EXPENSE, AND SHOULD HE BE FOUND, SEE
THAT HE IS KEPT FROM FURTHER HARM.

DRACULA.

I read it twice.

"When did this arrive?"

The landlord named the hour.

It had been received before Johann reached Munich.

For some time I could hear nothing but the ticking
of the clock upon the mantel. The words upon the
paper remained steady, black and courteous. There was no visible
threat in them. Indeed, they expressed solicitude of an almost
paternal kind. Yet each phrase chilled me more deeply than
the snow of the cemetery.

"How could Count Dracula know that I had left the
road?"

The landlord shook his head.

"How could he name the valley?"

Again there was no answer.

I went to the window. Munich lay below in ordinary
daylight: carts passing, bells ringing, citizens about their business. The
roofs shone after rain. Nowhere was there any sign that,
beyond the comfortable streets and cultivated fields, the boundaries between
the living and the dead had opened for a night.

I thought of the woman rising in her coffin. I
thought of the lightning drawn to the iron stake. I
thought of the wolf's weight upon my chest, its warmth
preserving the life which its teeth might so easily have
taken. Had it protected me from the cold, from the
dead Countess, or merely from a death which another power
had reserved for its own purposes?

On my throat, where the creature's tongue had passed, two
small red marks had appeared. They were not punctures. The
physician declared them bruises caused by pressure from my collar.
Perhaps he was right.

Yet when I touched them, they burned.

I folded the telegram and returned it to the landlord.

That evening, as the sun went down, a dog began
to howl somewhere beyond the city walls. The sound was
faint at first, then clearer, and presently another voice answered
from farther away. I stood at my window until darkness
covered the roofs.

I could not persuade myself that the cries were those
of dogs.

Nor could I forget that I still had an appointment
in the east—with the nobleman whose unseen hand had reached
across mountains, forest, and storm to preserve me.

Count Dracula had protected his English guest.

But from what—and for whom—I did not yet know.

# THE BRONZE DEVOCATION

The footprint was on the white stone of the hearth.

If you want to understand the mysterious and incredible tragedy
of Julian Rodman, you must begin with that footprint—not with
the dead man, nor with the burned formulae, nor even
with the gigantic Oriental whom the officers found kneeling outside
the locked door.

It was the print of a naked foot.

I saw it before Constable Renshaw, before the coroner, before
the photographers from the Criminal Investigation Department had spread their
black cloths and magnesium apparatus over the disordered room. I
saw it in the grey light of morning, when the
embers were dead and the body of Rodman lay along
the edge of the fender, distorted under its evening clothes.

The print was small, exquisitely formed, and faintly pink at
the heel, as though the flesh that made it had
been warm.

Beyond it the hearth was covered with ash.

The French windows were fastened inside. The door was locked,
and the key lay beneath Rodman's body. The white paneling
of the drawing room was unbroken. The chimney was narrow,
and protected at the top by iron bars. There was
no secret passage, no concealed stair, no architectural contrivance by
which any living creature could have entered or escaped.

Yet the footprint remained.

Think about this thing.

A man alone in a locked room; a naked woman's
footprint on the hearth; a little bronze image lying among
the ashes; all the papers on which depended the economic
stability of half Asia burned to flakes of black tissue;
and Julian Rodman dead—not stabbed, not poisoned, not shot, but
crushed.

Nearly every bone in his body had been broken.

I have written that sentence as the surgeon wrote it.
It reads like a lie. The whole record reads like
a lie. That is precisely why, for more than a
year, sane men accepted the only explanation that was not
sane.

Sir Henry Marquis did not accept it.

He arrived after the others, at nine minutes past eight,
buttoned to the throat in a grey overcoat. He stood
in the doorway while Inspector Brune explained the locked windows,
the key, the ashes, and the footprint. Sir Henry listened
with his head a little to one side. He had
the pale, inattentive face of a man hearing music in
another room.

Then he looked at Lal Gupta.

The Oriental was seated against the wall between two constables.
He was the largest human being I had ever seen.
His body seemed not so much to occupy the chair
as to flow over it, softly and enormously, like some
deep-sea organism brought up from pressureless waters. His hands, spread
upon his knees, had the breadth of paddles. Yet there
was no coarse heaviness about him. The immense flesh suggested
soft, tough fiber; a flexible substance packed over steel.

His face was yellow-brown, smooth, and almost childlike. He wore
a white robe beneath a European cloak, and his head
was shaved except for a dark knot of hair at
the crown.

When Sir Henry entered, Gupta rose.

"Excellency," he said, "the Master is dead."

His voice was metallic and curiously remote.

Sir Henry glanced toward the body.

"That appears probable."

"I was sent to guard him."

"And did you?"

Gupta's eyelids descended.

"I guarded the Master against men. Against the creative Forces
of the spirit, Excellency, no servant has power."

Sir Henry went across the room. He did not look
again at the corpse. He picked up the bronze from
the ashes with the fire tongs, set it on the
mantel, and examined it.

It was perhaps nine inches high, green-black with age, and
of remarkable workmanship. A man carried a struggling woman in
his arms. The man was powerful, broad-backed and naked; the
woman's body bent away from him in a luminous arc
of metal, one arm thrown above her head, her hair
streaming to the base of the group.

There was something violent in it, and something ceremonial.

Sir Henry touched the woman's bronze foot with one finger.
Then he looked at the pink impression on the hearth.

"What happened here?" he said.

Lal Gupta bowed his immense head.

"The thing that I feared."

That was all he would say until the magistrate came.

But the story did not begin in that room. It
began three years earlier, with a brochure of fourteen pages
printed at the private expense of Julian Rodman and circulated
among seventy scientific men in Europe and America.

The brochure startled the world.

Rodman was an American chemist; but to call him a
chemist is like calling Napoleon an artillery officer. At thirty-two
he had isolated two unstable elements which orthodox molecular theory
denied could exist. At thirty-five he produced synthetic sapphires indistinguishable,
except by their freedom from flaws, from stones mined in
Ceylon. At thirty-eight he announced that the crystalline structure of
ruby, emerald, diamond and blue corundum could be built, altered,
and multiplied by a process whose cost would eventually be
no greater than that of making plate glass.

The announcement was not speculation. It contained photographs, spectroscopic analyses,
and the sworn reports of three jewel experts.

In London, Amsterdam, Bombay, Calcutta and Singapore, the effect was
extraordinary. Shares fell. Merchants telegraphed across continents. Certain hereditary fortunes,
accumulated through generations of mines, cutting houses, temples, treasuries and
trade, trembled as though a geological convulsion had passed beneath
them.

Rodman proposed to make precious stones cheaply and abundantly.

The destruction of value was incredible.

There were those who called him a liberator. There were
others who called him a monster. A diamond is carbon;
an emerald is beryl colored by chromium; a ruby is
crystallized alumina. These are statements of science. But a stone
locked in a prince's treasury is not merely matter. It
is debt, marriage, dynasty, worship, tribute, murder, inheritance. Rodman had
laid his hand, not upon minerals, but upon the imagination
of mankind.

And he meant to close it.

He went to India because one part of the formula
required observation of certain ancient furnaces still used in the
native states. There, at Benares, Lal Gupta appeared.

The circumstances were related to me by Giovanni Spada, Rodman's
Italian secretary, who had been sent ahead to London with
laboratory notes. Spada was a nervous, accurate man with a
pointed beard and the habit of arranging every object before
him in a straight line.

"It was at sunset," he told me. "We were on
the upper veranda of the house above the river. Dr.
Rodman was dictating. Then this creature came up the steps."

"Gupta?"

"Yes. But I did not know his name. No one
had admitted him. He simply appeared."

Spada paused to align the spoon with his coffee cup.

"He bowed and said, 'Master, I have been sent to
serve you.' Dr. Rodman laughed. You know his laugh—without amusement.
He asked, 'Sent by whom?' The man replied, 'By those
who know the division of worship.'"

"What did that mean?"

"Nothing. Or everything. With such men one cannot tell."

Spada's fingers shook slightly.

"Gupta carried a package wrapped in red silk. He placed
it before Dr. Rodman and uncovered the bronze. The Master
admired it. Indeed, he was delighted. Gupta said that it
represented a devocation—the drawing down of a spiritual force into
material form."

"And Rodman believed him?"

"Rodman believed in nothing that could not be weighed. That
was his pride. He said, 'Can you draw down a
woman into bronze?' Gupta answered, 'No, Master. The danger is
that the bronze may draw the woman out.'"

Spada crossed himself.

"The man then warned him. I remember the words. 'The
intellect travels upward, Master, but the body calls it down.
When the image opens, do not answer the body.' Dr.
Rodman laughed again. He kept the bronze."

"Why did Gupta remain?"

"Because Rodman wished to study him. The Master collected unusual
mechanisms, unusual minerals, unusual men. Gupta called himself a monk.
He cooked, cleaned the instruments, slept at the door, and
followed us like a shadow. He appeared obedient. But I
was afraid of him."

"Why?"

Spada looked at me with irritation.

"Because he was never surprised."

Rodman returned to England in October and took the old
Dower House at Ashe Morton, where he could work without
interruption. The house stood alone among wet fields and black
yews, two miles from the village and seven from the
railway. Spada remained in London to arrange patents. Lal Gupta
accompanied Rodman as servant, guardian, cook, and—according to his own
account—religious protector.

The final experiments occupied eleven weeks.

On the afternoon of December seventeenth Rodman sent Spada a
telegram:

PROCESS COMPLETE. FORMULAE FAIR-COPIED. COME TOMORROW. WORLD ENDS MONDAY.

He dined alone at seven. Gupta served him. At half
past eight Rodman went into the drawing room carrying a
red portfolio. At nine Gupta heard him moving. At ten
the maid, who slept in the north wing, smelled smoke,
but Gupta told her the Master was burning papers and
must not be disturbed.

At eleven minutes past midnight there came a sound which
she described as "a chair dragged once across the floor."

Then silence.

At six in the morning Gupta knocked. There was no
answer. The door was locked. He sent the maid for
the police and waited outside until Constable Renshaw arrived. They
forced the door.

Rodman was dead.

The subsequent inquiry became one of those institutional dramas in
which the solemn apparatus of reason is assembled around a
fact that reason cannot digest. There were measurements, medical depositions,
architectural plans, chemical tests and photographs. Experts entered the locked
room and departed from it visibly offended.

The body presented the greatest difficulty.

No machine was found. There was no evidence that a
machine had been removed. No fall could have produced the
injuries. The skull, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, arms and legs had
suffered compression from multiple directions. Dr. Taverner stated that such
injuries might have resulted if Rodman had been caught beneath
a collapsing mass of masonry, or seized by an industrial
press.

But there had been no masonry.

There had been no press.

There was only Lal Gupta.

He could not have left the room because, by his
own testimony and the testimony of the maid, he had
remained outside the door. Yet suspicion turned naturally toward him.
Men distrusted his size, his origin, his ceremonious language, and
the obscure theology with which he surrounded every ordinary act.
It was easier to imagine him a murderer than to
imagine the naked footprint.

When they asked how Rodman died, Gupta requested permission to
write his statement.

"It is not a story for the tongue," he said.
"The tongue is divided by fear."

They gave him paper. He wrote slowly, in an angular
English hand, covering twelve foolscap sheets. Then he signed each
page, folded them together, and laid the bronze image upon
them.

The statement was read first before the magistrates, afterward before
the medical commission, and finally in the private room at
Broadmoor where Sir Henry Marquis and I heard it from
Gupta himself. Although the written report is in the archives,
I shall reproduce his spoken account, because the pauses, the
gestures, and that metallic voice gave to the impossible narrative
a weight which no clerk's transcript can preserve.

The room at Broadmoor was narrow and overheated. Rain ran
down the barred window. Gupta sat under a shaded lamp,
his robe gathered about him, his enormous hands folded.

Sir Henry clipped the end from a cigar.

The sound was absurdly distinct.

"You have been declared unfit to plead," he said.

Gupta bowed.

"The learned men have called me mad, Excellency."

"They believe you killed Julian Rodman."

"I did not enter the room."

"That is true."

"They believe also that no other entered."

"That is also true."

Gupta looked at him.

"Then the learned men have made a house without a
door."

Sir Henry struck a match. The little flame etched his
face in black and gold. He lighted the cigar, shook
out the match, and leaned back.

"Tell us what occurred."

Gupta was silent for so long that I heard the
rain tapping on the outer sill.

"At Benares," he began, "I saw the Master standing between
two fires. In one fire was his mind. In the
other was the wealth of nations. He believed the first
fire would consume the second."

"Would it not?" I said.

Gupta turned his mild eyes toward me.

"Gold is not wealth, sir. A jewel is not wealth.
Belief is wealth. The Master desired to alter belief by
altering stones."

Sir Henry exhaled smoke.

"Go on."

"I was instructed in a monastery beyond the river where
the powers of image and desire are studied. There are
forms made by men which remain empty. There are forms
into which attention has been poured for a thousand years.
These are not empty. The bronze was such a form."

"The bronze of the man and woman?"

"Yes, Excellency. It is older than the kingdoms of the
north. The man is not carrying the woman away. He
is carrying her into the world."

Gupta paused.

"An image is a door. Worship is pressure on the
farther side."

I confess that his words affected me. The institutional room
disappeared: the barred window, the painted table, the warder by
the door. I imagined centuries of dark hands touching bronze;
flowers, oil, smoke, prayers; an invisible accumulation bearing against the
thin partition of matter.

Sir Henry merely tapped his ash into a tray.

"What warning did you give Rodman?"

"I told the Master that intellect may command matter, but
the body will betray intellect. I said that when the
image opened he must not receive what came from it.
He laughed."

"What did you expect to come?"

"The creature of his desire."

"A woman?"

Gupta's lids lowered again.

"Every man gives the creature a different face."

I interrupted him. "You mean that the bronze knew Rodman's
desire?"

"The bronze knew nothing. It was a vessel. The Master
filled it."

"Against your reason," said Sir Henry, "continue."

Gupta's huge chest rose and fell.

"At Ashe Morton the Master completed his formula on the
seventeenth day of December. He ate little. He drank coffee.
At eight o'clock he went into the white room and
placed the formulae upon the table. The bronze stood on
the black marble hearth."

"Where were you?"

"In the passage."

"The door?"

"Open."

"What did you see?"

"Nothing at first. The Master sat before the fire. He
had put on evening clothes, though he was alone. He
said that one should dress formally when signing the death
warrant of an industry."

That was like Rodman. I had heard him make remarks
equally theatrical and equally cold.

Gupta continued.

"The room was white except for the hearth and the
red fire. The Master read the formulae from the beginning.
He corrected three figures. Then he poured brandy into a
glass."

"He rarely drank," I said.

"He did not drink it. He held the glass before
the fire. The bronze woman shone through the brandy."

Gupta paused.

"I smelled apple blossom."

The detail was so unexpected that I looked at Sir
Henry. He showed no response.

"Apple blossom?"

"Yes, sir. There were no flowers in the house. It
was winter. But the odor entered the passage—fresh, cold, and
sweet, as if a door had opened upon an orchard
in April."

He leaned forward.

"The bronze tapped upon the marble."

"Tapped?"

"Three times."

His forefinger struck the table.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

The warder shifted near the door.

"The Master heard it. He put down the glass. He
went to the hearth and touched the image. The metal
was hot. He called to me, but I did not
enter."

"Why?"

"I had been commanded to serve, not to cross the
division of worship."

Sir Henry's cigar glowed.

"What happened to the bronze?"

"It enlarged."

He said it simply.

I heard the rain. I heard the faint hiss of
tobacco. I heard, from far down the corridor, the opening
and shutting of an iron door.

"How?" I asked.

"As a shadow enlarges when a lamp approaches. It did
not grow by movement. It occupied more space. The base
spread upon the black marble. The bronze man rose to
the height of the mantel. His shoulders passed into the
white paneling, but the paneling did not break. The woman
unfolded in his arms."

Gupta's voice had changed. It remained low, yet every word
seemed pressed out under a burden.

"The Master stepped backward. He could not speak. The bronze
man bent and placed the woman upon the hearth."

He paused.

"And she lived."

It is easy, in daylight, to mock the sentence. It
is easy among clerks, typewriters, electric lamps and official seals.
But I ask you to set these things aside. See
the white room. See the black square of marble and
the low red embers. See Rodman, whose mind had reduced
the diamond to carbon and value to molecular arrangement, standing
before a thing for which no formula had been written.

And it did appear.

"What was she like?" Sir Henry asked.

"White," said Gupta. "Whiter than the paneling. Her hair was
yellow. It fell to her knees. Her eyes were blue."

"Naked?"

"Yes, Excellency."

"Did she speak?"

"Not at first. She stood upon the ash. The print
of her foot remained where the heat had colored her
flesh."

The footprint.

I remembered it under the grey morning light, delicate and
pink, an intimate mark amid death and destruction.

Gupta lifted one hand as though seeing the scene before
him.

"The Master said, 'Who are you?' The creature looked at
the bronze. The man in the image had become small
again. He carried an empty curve in his arms."

An empty curve! It was precisely the sort of minute
monstrosity that gave Gupta's story its terrible persuasion.

"The woman said, 'You called me.'"

"In English?" Sir Henry asked.

"In the language of the Master's desire."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer, Excellency."

Sir Henry smiled faintly.

"Continue."

"The Master asked how she had come. She pointed to
the formulae. 'You have opened matter,' she said. 'Matter has
opened me.' The Master began to tremble. I had never
seen him tremble. He said, 'This is hallucination.' The creature
replied, 'Then command the hallucination to go.'"

Gupta's fingers tightened together.

"She came toward him."

He paused.

"The Master called my name."

"Did you enter?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"I had warned him."

"That is not why."

For the first time a flicker of anger passed over
Gupta's face.

"Against the pressure of desire, Excellency, another man is helpless."

"What did Rodman do?"

"He tried to reach the bell. The woman stood between
him and the wall. She did not touch him. She
said, 'You are about to fill the earth with stones.
What will you give me?' He answered, 'Nothing.' She said,
'Then I shall remain here when the stones are made.'"

Gupta bent his head.

"The Master knew that if the formulae lived, she would
live. The intellect may reject a miracle, but the body—"

He stopped.

"The body what?" I asked.

"The body had already believed."

The warder moved again. It seemed to me that the
room had grown excessively warm.

Gupta resumed.

"The Master took the red portfolio from the table. He
carried it to the fire. He said, 'If I burn
this, will you return?' The woman answered, 'I shall return
when you open matter again.' He tore the papers once
across and put them upon the coals."

Every person acquainted with Rodman knew the magnitude of that
act. Years of experiments, the exact temperatures, catalysts, pressures, timings,
the undisclosed relations of crystal formation—all existed only in that
fair copy and in Rodman's memory. The lesser notes were
useless without the final arrangement.

"The papers did not burn at first," Gupta said. "They
curled. Blue writing appeared between the black lines. Then flame
went through them. The room filled with smoke."

He paused.

"The woman laughed."

"What sort of laugh?" I asked.

"Like ice breaking far away."

Sir Henry looked at the glowing end of his cigar.

"And then?"

"The Master opened the door."

I stared at him.

"The door?"

"Yes."

"But you were in the passage."

"I knelt outside."

"Then you saw the woman?"

Gupta raised his eyes.

"No."

The word fell flatly.

I said, "You have just described her!"

"I saw beneath the door. I heard. I smelled the
blossom. When the Master opened the door, smoke came out,
but the room was empty."

"Then your account is imagination."

"Against your reason, sir, every account is imagination."

Sir Henry spoke quietly.

"Why did Rodman close the door again?"

"He looked into the passage. He did not see me."

"You were kneeling there."

"I was behind the arras beside the library door."

"Why?"

"The creature had gone out of the white room."

A chill passed over me despite the heat.

Gupta continued with painful slowness.

"The Master believed she had entered the passage. He called
to her. There was no answer. He returned to the
hearth. He locked the door. He thought the lock would
keep the thing outside."

"And you?"

"I remained where I had been placed."

"What happened next?"

"There was silence. Then the Master spoke. I could not
hear the words. Another voice answered."

"The woman's?"

"No."

"What voice?"

Gupta looked at the bronze, which had been brought from
the evidence room and placed upon the table between us.

"The man's."

No one spoke.

"The bronze man?" I said finally.

"He had carried the woman into the world. The Master
had yielded to her and destroyed the formulae. The woman's
work was finished. The bearer came to recover her."

"Did you see him?"

"I saw a shadow under the door. It covered all
the light."

Gupta stretched his immense arms, then slowly brought them inward.

"I heard the Master cry out. There was a pressure
in the room. The paneling groaned. The glass in the
windows sang. The Master cried once more. Then came the
sound of a chair moving."

His arms closed upon his chest.

"After that there was nothing."

"How long did you wait?" Sir Henry asked.

"Until morning."

"Why?"

"To open a door at night upon such a thing
is forbidden."

"When the constable arrived, the key was inside."

"Yes."

"The windows were fastened."

"Yes."

"The bronze had returned to its original shape."

"Yes."

"The woman was gone."

"Yes."

"And Rodman was crushed."

Gupta bowed his head.

"The bearer had taken her away."

This was the statement.

No sensible man believed it; yet no sensible man could
provide another account that covered the physical evidence. The medical
commission concluded that Gupta suffered from a systematized religious delusion.
Because he could not have entered the locked room, and
because his conduct before and after the death was consistent
with his story, the charge of murder was not brought
to trial. He was confined indefinitely.

There the matter might have remained: a monstrous footnote to
the history of synthetic chemistry.

But Sir Henry Marquis was troubled.

He did not believe in devocation, creative Forces, living bronze
or women materialized from the pressure of worship. Neither did
he believe Lal Gupta mad.

On the evening after our interview, we dined in Sir
Henry's rooms overlooking the river. The fog pressed against the
windows, turning the lamps along the Embankment into pale yellow
coins. The bronze stood on his desk. Gupta's twelve-page statement
lay beside it.

Sir Henry ate little. Afterward he moved the lamp nearer
and examined the first sheet through a lens.

I watched him.

"Well?" I said.

"The human mind is capable of any absurdity."

"You mean Gupta's?"

"I mean ours."

He held the page sideways to the light.

"What do you see?" he asked.

"Writing."

"What else?"

"Nothing."

"Precisely. That is often the most useful thing to see."

He laid the sheet down and considered the bronze. Then
he picked it up by the base. It was heavier
than it appeared.

"The footprint proves the woman," I said.

"No. The footprint proves a foot-shaped application of warmth or
pigment."

"On clean stone?"

"Yes."

"But the print showed the ridges of skin."

"A molded surface will show ridges."

"The perfume?"

"Perfume."

"The voice?"

"An accomplice, a mechanical device, or Gupta himself."

"The locked room?"

"A magician does not abolish doors. He makes the audience
look at the wrong door."

I walked to the window.

"You think Gupta killed him."

"Yes."

"How did he enter?"

"He did not need to enter after the door was
locked."

"Then Rodman was dead before it was locked."

"Probably."

"Who locked it?"

"Rodman."

I turned.

"That is nonsense."

"It's only a theory."

He said it in his emotionless drawl; but I knew
by then that Sir Henry used the phrase only when
the theory had hardened into fact.

He rang for a spirit lamp.

While we waited, he tapped the written statement.

"Why did Gupta insist upon writing this?"

"Because the tongue is divided by fear."

"Decorative rubbish. Why write twelve pages when three sentences would
establish his delusion?"

"To explain."

"No criminal explains unless the explanation performs another service."

The servant brought the lamp. Sir Henry lighted it and
adjusted the blue flame.

He held the first page above the heat.

At first nothing happened. Then, between the black lines of
Gupta's visible statement, faint brown marks emerged. They darkened slowly,
gathered into strokes, and joined one another.

Words appeared.

I bent over the desk.

The secret writing ran across the page at right angles
to the public text. Sir Henry warmed the remaining sheets
one by one. A second document rose from emptiness: concise,
commercial, and sane.

It was addressed to a firm in Bombay whose name
had long been associated with the jewel trade.

MISSION COMPLETED. RODMAN'S FINAL FORMULAE DESTROYED IN MY PRESENCE. ALL
WORKING NOTES PREVIOUSLY ALTERED OR REMOVED. RODMAN DEAD. SYNTHETIC PROCESS
CANNOT BE RECOVERED. INDIA'S TREASURES ARE SECURE.

Below that came instructions regarding payment through three banks and
a request that certain persons in Antwerp be notified.

The last line read:

THE BRONZE DEVOCATION HAS SERVED ITS PURPOSE.

I read the message twice.

"Gupta wrote this in custody!"

"Yes."

"To whom did he expect to send it?"

"To his employers. He believed his legal statement would pass
through intermediaries or be copied abroad as a curiosity. Invisible
ink is an old servant of commerce."

"Then he is an agent."

"A highly trained one."

"But the room—"

Sir Henry extinguished the lamp.

"Let us remove the supernatural ornaments. Rodman had completed a
formula that threatened immense private wealth. Those whose fortunes depended
upon scarcity could not buy him, frighten him, or steal
from him. Therefore they sent a man able to become
indispensable."

"Gupta."

"Cook, servant, guardian, religious fool—whatever mask Rodman would tolerate. Gupta
altered preliminary notes, watched the final experiments, and waited until
the fair copy existed."

"Why not simply steal it?"

"Because Rodman remembered it. Both the formula and the man
had to be destroyed."

"Then why induce him to burn it?"

"To make certain no copy survived and to create evidence
of voluntary destruction."

"How?"

Sir Henry picked up the bronze.

"With a performance constructed for one spectator."

He pressed a nearly invisible catch beneath the base. A
narrow panel opened. Inside the hollow bronze lay a coil
of fine wire, a packet cavity, and the remains of
a fragrant resin.

"The apple blossom," I said.

"Yes. The tapping could be produced by expansion when the
metal was heated, or by a mechanism formerly enclosed here.
The image was placed upon the hearth. Rodman had been
prepared for months by Gupta's warnings. Suggestion is pressure. Repeat
to a proud rationalist that bodily temptation will destroy his
reason, and he will watch for that exact temptation."

"The woman was real?"

"Undoubtedly."

"But no one entered the house."

"No stranger entered that night. The maid searched ordinary rooms,
not luggage, cupboards, attics or servants' quarters with the thoroughness
of Scotland Yard. A woman could have been concealed in
the house earlier. More likely, she came disguised among the
laundry carts that morning and remained in Gupta's room."

"Yellow hair, blue eyes—"

"A wig and colored lenses, if those details are even
accurate. Remember, everything except the footprint comes from Gupta."

"And she left?"

"Before Rodman locked the door. Gupta's own narrative gives us
the moment. Rodman opened the door after burning the formulae.
Smoke filled the passage. In that confusion, the woman passed
out. Rodman saw her go—or thought he did. Gupta then
spoke from concealment, persuaded him that the apparition remained nearby,
and directed him back into the room."

"Directed him?"

"Perhaps he told Rodman that the woman would return unless
he locked himself inside. Perhaps Rodman, ashamed and terrified, did
so without prompting. We cannot know the precise words."

"But Gupta was outside afterward."

"Exactly. Outside the door when the maid saw him. Yet
before she came, while the door stood open, he had
entered."

I stared at him.

"Entered? Then how did he leave after Rodman locked it?"

"He did not."

The fog pressed against the windows.

Sir Henry pointed toward the bronze group.

"Look at the man. What is he doing?"

"Carrying the woman."

"No. Look at the arms."

I looked. The bronze man's arms encircled the woman's ribs,
one beneath the shoulders and one across the hips. The
posture that had seemed erotic was mechanical. It was the
posture of compression.

Sir Henry continued.

"Gupta entered while the smoke concealed him. Rodman, obsessed with
the woman and the bronze, did not see the huge
servant pass behind the curtain beside the mantel."

"There was no curtain."

"There had been. Two brass hooks remained in the paneling.
The curtain was found burned among the papers and was
assumed to have been used as fuel. Gupta concealed himself
behind it before Rodman locked the door."

"And then?"

"Then he waited."

"For what?"

"For Rodman to kneel over the ashes, searching perhaps for
a surviving fragment of the formula. Gupta came from behind
him."

Sir Henry's hands moved, not dramatically, but with dreadful precision.

"He seized Rodman around the chest. He was strong enough
to fracture the ribs at once. Rodman could not cry
after the first compression. Gupta then broke the limbs and
spine, using his knees, the floor, and his arms. Dr.
Taverner imagined an industrial press because the force was distributed.
Gupta was the press."

I remembered those hands spread mildly on his knees; the
body flowing over the asylum chair; the soft, tough fiber
under the white robe.

"But he was found outside."

"After the murder he arranged the body, placed the key
under it, and went out through the chimney opening."

"That was measured. No man could pass."

"No ordinary man. Gupta's shoulders were flexible to an exceptional
degree, and the lower register of the chimney was wider
than the inspectors realized. Soot marks were found upon his
robe but dismissed because he tended the fires. He climbed
only as far as the inspection recess behind the chimney
breast."

"There was no outlet from it."

"There was an iron cleaning panel opening into the passage.
It had been painted over and mistaken for part of
the heating system. Gupta knew the house better than Rodman
did. He emerged, replaced the panel, and knelt before the
locked door."

I sat down.

"The naked footprint?"

"Prepared beforehand. A wax mold taken from the woman's foot,
warmed against the bronze and pressed upon the stone with
a trace of carmine. Its warmth when we arrived was
an illusion produced by the color. No one touched it
to test the temperature."

I had written that the heel looked warm. I had
not felt it.

"And the woman?"

"Gone before midnight, disguised once more. She need never have
known murder was intended. She may have believed herself employed
in a private theatrical trick."

"The enlarged bronze?"

"A lie."

"The second voice?"

"A lie."

"The shadow under the door?"

"A lie."

"The groaning paneling and singing glass?"

"Or the sounds of murder transferred in the telling to
an occult cause."

I looked at the secret message, brown and businesslike between
the extravagant lines of theology.

"Why build so elaborate a story?"

Sir Henry smiled without warmth.

"Because an impossible murder is safer than a possible one.
Gupta offered the authorities a miracle and a madman. They
accepted both. His size made him an obvious suspect, so
he transformed that suspicion into pity. A religious hallucination explained
his presence, his silence, his failure to intervene, the burned
papers, the bronze, the footprint and the injuries. The more
fantastic the story became, the more completely it occupied the
investigation."

"But he condemned himself to an asylum."

"For a time. His employers would eventually have arranged release,
escape, deportation or disappearance. The payment named in the message
was sufficient."

Sir Henry gathered the pages.

"Jewel merchants did not save India," he said. "They saved
scarcity."

The police returned to Broadmoor the next morning.

Lal Gupta was brought again to the overheated room. He
looked from Sir Henry to the bronze and then to
the twelve sheets spread upon the table. The hidden writing
had darkened overnight.

For the first time, he was surprised.

It was not a great change. His eyelids lifted. His
enormous hands became still. But Spada had been right: with
such a man, surprise was confession.

Sir Henry stood by the barred window.

"You are sane," he said.

Gupta bowed slowly.

"The learned men have altered their worship."

"You were employed by a syndicate of stone owners in
Bombay, Colombo and Rangoon."

"The world contains many owners."

"You entered Rodman's house to destroy his work."

"I entered to serve the Master."

"You induced him to burn the formula."

"The Master chose."

"And then you killed him."

Gupta looked toward the bronze.

"No, Excellency."

"You crushed him in your arms."

"No."

"You hid behind the curtain, left through the chimney recess,
and composed a miracle from the traces of your crime."

Gupta's face seemed suddenly older.

"The formulae are gone," he said.

"Yes."

"The notes cannot restore them."

"Yes."

"The Master is dead."

"Yes."

"Then what remains for your victory?"

Sir Henry's voice was quiet.

"The truth."

Gupta considered this. Then he smiled.

It was not the smile of a monk, a servant,
a madman or a savage. It was the weary, courteous
smile of a professional whose adversary has at last discovered
the method after the performance is finished.

"Excellency," he said, "truth is a jewel whose value also
depends upon scarcity."

He admitted nothing more.

The woman was never found. The syndicate dissolved before warrants
could be executed. Two merchants died; one vanished; another demonstrated
that every relevant instruction had passed through a clerk who
had conveniently drowned. Rodman's altered notebooks yielded brilliant fragments but
not the central process. Synthetic sapphires continued to be made,
but the cheap abundance he predicted did not come.

Lal Gupta was tried, not as a madman, but as
a conspirator and murderer. The hidden report established motive and
agency; the chimney recess supplied means; fibers from the burned
curtain were discovered in the seams of his cloak. He
listened to the verdict without movement.

I saw him once after that.

As the guards led him away, he paused beside the
evidence table. The bronze devocation stood there, green-black under the
court lights: the powerful man, the yielding woman, the circle
of the arms.

Gupta touched his forehead to it.

Then he looked at Sir Henry.

"The body calls the intellect down," he said.

Sir Henry made no answer.

You may say that everything was explained. The perfume came
from resin; the footprint from wax; the apparition from an
accomplice; the miracle from preparation; the locked room from a
forgotten iron panel; the crushing from Gupta's tremendous arms. The
secret writing converted theology into commerce, worship into conspiracy, and
supernatural pressure into literal force.

That is the reasonable conclusion.

Yet I retain one detail which Sir Henry never explained
to my satisfaction.

Months after the trial, when the bronze was delivered to
the museum, I accompanied the curator into the storage room.
He unlocked the official crate in my presence. The wrappings
were untouched. The evidence seals were unbroken.

We lifted out the image.

The bronze man stood as before, his arms locked around
the woman.

But on the polished wood at the bottom of the
crate lay a faint impression, scarcely larger than a child's
hand.

It was the print of a naked foot.

The curator called it condensation. Sir Henry, when I told
him, called it contamination from the wax mold. Both explanations
are possible. Indeed, they are probable.

The human mind is capable of any absurdity.

Nevertheless, when winter comes and apple wood burns upon my
hearth, I sometimes detect beneath the smoke a cold sweetness,
like blossom opening in an unseen orchard. Then I remember
Lal Gupta's words and the white room at Ashe Morton—the
black marble, the red embers, the burned formulae, the dead
man, and that delicate mark of flesh upon stone.

And I think about the thing.

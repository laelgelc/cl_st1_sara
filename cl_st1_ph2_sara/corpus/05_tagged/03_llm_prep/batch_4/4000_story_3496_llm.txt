### The Waning Moon

On the full-moon night of Phalgun the south wind entered
Hemanta's chamber without asking permission, as spring winds are accustomed
to do. It shook the jasmine beside the courtyard wall,
stole a little fragrance, and scattered it over the white
bed. Beyond the open window the moonlight lay upon the
terrace like a heroine wearied by a long journey and
fallen asleep without removing her jewels. Somewhere among the mango
blossoms a papiya called with inexhaustible hope.

Hemanta sat beside his wife, winding one end of her
loosened hair about his finger. Kusum had removed her necklace,
but he had taken it up and was trying, with
more affection than skill, to fasten it round her wrist.

"Do you know," he said, "that if this night were
prolonged by a thousand years, I should still find it
too short?"

Kusum did not answer.

"Then let the poets be summoned," he continued. "Jayadeva shall
compose the song, the moon shall keep time, and this
impertinent bird shall provide the refrain. Only you must not
sit there like Parbati before her penance was rewarded."

Still she was silent. Her face was turned toward the
open sky; but she did not seem to see the
moon. Her hands lay clasped upon her knees, and beneath
Hemanta's playful fingers her hair was cold and heavy, like
a stream that has ceased to flow.

At last she said, "I have something to tell you."

The words were low, yet they seemed to close the
window and extinguish the moon. Hemanta released her hair.

"What is it?"

"I have tried many times. Before our marriage—after it—every day,
almost. But I was afraid."

Hemanta laughed, though uneasily. "If you have broken the blue
cup in the storeroom, I forgive you. If you have
fed my share of sweetmeats to the cat, I forgive
the cat also."

Kusum turned toward him. In her eyes there was neither
laughter nor appeal, but the fixed gravity of one who
has reached the bank of a river after wading barefooted
through fire.

"It is not such a thing."

Then, from the corridor, came the sound of slippers striking
the floor with the emphatic regularity of a magistrate's seal.
The papiya called once more. Kusum started, and the confession
died upon her lips.

Harihar Mukerji entered without announcement. He stood on the threshold,
broad, rigid, and trembling—not with weakness, but with that kind
of righteousness which requires the whole body for its proper
display.

"Hemanta!"

His son rose.

"Turn that woman out of the house this instant."

Kusum bowed her head. Hemanta looked first at his father
and then at his wife, as though the two had
suddenly begun to speak in an unknown tongue.

"What has happened?"

"Do not ask me what has happened. Ask her who
she is."

Hemanta's face lost its colour. "Who she is?"

"A Kayestha's daughter. An orphan. And a widow besides! She
was passed off as a Brahmin maiden, and you—you have
shared food and worship with her. Our house has been
polluted from roof to foundation."

The moon was still in the window; the jasmine still
trembled; the papiya, ignorant of caste, continued its indecent song.

Hemanta said, "Is this true?"

Kusum answered, "It is."

He stared at her. The room, the house, the years
of his married life—all seemed to draw away from him,
as a familiar shore recedes from a man carried helplessly
into the dark. He could not at first feel grief
or anger; those require some ground upon which to stand.
He felt only that there was no ground.

Harihar roared, "Why are you waiting? Send her away!"

Hemanta turned upon him with such a look that even
paternal authority paused. "Leave us."

The old man's lips shook. Perhaps he had never imagined
that a son could pronounce those two words to a
father. Nevertheless he withdrew, promising with his eyes to return
accompanied by ancestry, scripture, society, and several competent Brahmins.

Kusum remained seated. Hemanta walked to the window and back.
The fragrance of the night oppressed him.

"Tell me everything."

She said, "My father was a Kayestha clerk in Barisal.
I was married when I was nine. My husband died
before I had learned his face. After my parents died,
I lived with a distant relation in Calcutta."

"And you were brought here as a Brahmin girl?"

"Yes."

"You knew?"

"Not at first—not what was intended."

"And afterward?"

"Yes."

"Why did you not tell me?"

Kusum looked down at her hands. "I wished to."

"That is not an answer."

"No." She paused. "I was afraid you would cease to
love me."

The naked simplicity of this reply struck more cruelly than
any excuse. Hemanta's anger, seeking another object, turned upon her
because she was near.

"You feared the loss of my love, and therefore you
made the love itself false?"

"I did not make it."

"You consented."

"I do not know."

He laughed bitterly. "One remembers whether one consented to a
marriage."

"One remembers the words. One does not always know who
spoke them—the heart, fear, obedience, or another person standing behind
all three."

Then she told him, in broken sentences, of the house
opposite his college lodgings; of the old widow who sheltered
her; of the terrace where she went at dusk to
dry clothes or gather them before rain; of the young
student who looked too often from the neighbouring roof; of
the notes hidden beneath a broken flowerpot; of the day
Peari Sankar Ghosal came and spoke kindly to her guardian.

At the name Hemanta stopped.

"Peari Sankar?"

Kusum nodded.

"He said there was no sin in love. He said
your father would never accept me if the truth were
known. He said the scriptures were made by men who
had never been hungry or afraid. Then he arranged everything.
A Brahmin family from another district claimed me as their
niece. I was taught the names of dead ancestors I
had never seen."

"And you agreed to deceive me."

"I begged him to tell you."

"Did he refuse?"

"He said that to tell you would be to lose
you. On the morning of the wedding I tried to
run away. They brought me back. I thought—" She covered
her face. "I thought if there were another birth, perhaps
I could be born without shame and meet you honestly
there. But this birth was already upon me. The drums
were sounding."

Hemanta's rage flared and consumed only himself. He wished to
condemn her, yet every sentence placed before him the frightened
girl she had been, surrounded by those who arranged virtue
and vice as household furniture. Still, pity could not immediately
rebuild what revelation had shattered. Love is called eternal because
lovers cannot bear to measure it; but one whispered fact
may sometimes turn its palaces to dust.

"Why tell me now?" he asked.

"I was going to tell you before your father came."

"Why tonight?"

"Because they have discovered it. Your sister's marriage will be
broken."

"How do you know?"

"A letter came this afternoon. Peari Sankar sent it to
your father."

Hemanta stood motionless. Then he took his shawl.

"Where are you going?"

"To ask him why."

Kusum rose quickly and caught the edge of the shawl.
"Do not go in anger."

"In what spirit should I go? Gratitude?"

"He has waited many years for your anger. Do not
give him more than he has earned."

Hemanta looked at her clasped hand and drew the cloth
away—not roughly, but with a coldness that made her sink
back as though struck.

Peari Sankar Ghosal lived at the end of a narrow
lane in a house whose plaster had peeled off with
dignified resignation. It was late, but he was awake. A
servant admitted Hemanta to a small sitting room where an
oil lamp burned beside account books, a brass pan-box, and
a vase containing two roses arranged with scrupulous taste.

Peari Sankar entered wearing a spotless cotton wrapper. His beard
was grey; his manner was mild.

"Ah, Hemanta Babu! At this hour? The spring moon must
have made you restless. Sit down. Will you take pan?"

Hemanta remained standing. "You arranged my marriage."

"I have assisted at several marriages. It is one of
the burdens of sociability."

"You know which marriage I mean."

Peari Sankar opened the pan-box and selected a leaf. "Yes.
Yours was unusually successful, I had thought."

"Why did you do it?"

The old man folded lime upon the leaf with delicate
attention. "Since you have come for a history rather than
refreshment, I must begin at the beginning. Otherwise even revenge
may be misunderstood, and that would be unjust."

Hemanta clenched his hands.

"You remember my only daughter?" Peari Sankar continued. "No; you
were young. She married into the Majumdar family. Her husband,
Navin, was foolish. Foolish men are plentiful, but Navin possessed
industry in addition. He drank, incurred debts, and finally ran
away to England with a theatrical lady—or perhaps she ran
away with him; society never troubles to establish grammar when
scandal supplies the meaning.

"After five years he returned alone, ill and repentant. My
daughter received him. She said he was her husband. Women
have such primitive notions. I also received him, because she
would not leave him, and because a father's heart is
an undisciplined organ.

"Your honoured father, Harihar Mukerji, then announced that I had
defied society. He called a meeting. Water was not to
be accepted from my hand; priests were not to officiate
in my house; invitations were to pass my door as
carefully as respectable women pass a drunkard. I went to
Harihar and asked for mercy. He gave me scripture. Scripture,
I discovered, is especially abundant when mercy is scarce."

Peari Sankar placed the finished pan in his mouth and
smiled.

"My daughter died within the year. Navin followed her. Thus
society was saved from further anxiety. I had, however, a
nephew—the son of my widowed sister. I brought him up.
When a marriage was arranged for him, your father visited
the bride's people and explained our impurity so eloquently that
they broke the match. The boy left home in shame
and took employment in Assam. Fever completed what orthodoxy had
begun.

"You see, Hemanta Babu, by that time I owed your
father a considerable debt."

"And you repaid it through an innocent woman?"

"Through several innocent persons. Revenge, like religious purification, is seldom
economical."

Hemanta stepped forward. "You villain!"

Peari Sankar raised one hand courteously. "Do not spoil the
account before it is balanced. When you were a college
student, you lodged next to the house of Bhabatarini, an
old widow related to me. Kusum lived under her protection.
You looked from your terrace; she looked from hers. The
south wind carried messages, the moon approved, and all the
customary officers of romance performed their duties without salary.

"Bhabatarini became alarmed. She brought the girl away. You ceased
eating, neglected your books, and wandered about like a verse
seeking its rhyme. Kusum was in no better condition. I
reflected that two young hearts should not be sacrificed merely
because one was Brahmin and the other Kayestha. Such liberality
would have shocked me had I not found it useful.

"I secured a respectable Brahmin intermediary in the western districts.
Poverty had made him philosophic. For a suitable sum he
discovered that Kusum was his brother's orphaned daughter. A horoscope
was produced; ancestors arose from the dead and supplied their
names; the auspicious moment was selected. Your father, after making
proper inquiries into everything except the truth, accepted the match."

Hemanta's voice was hoarse. "Did she consent?"

Peari Sankar's expression changed almost imperceptibly.

"She wept."

"That was not my question."

"She begged me to tell you. I explained that confession
would destroy the marriage. She said she would rather die.
Young people place much confidence in death, perhaps because they
have met it only in poetry. I told her that
if she withdrew, your grief would be upon her head.
If she proceeded, you would be happy. Between another's misery
and one's own dishonour, even an experienced judge might hesitate;
she was seventeen.

"On the wedding morning she disappeared. We found her at
the riverside. After that she remained quiet. Whether silence signifies
consent is a question on which society has long since
given judgment—for its own convenience."

"And after ruining us, you waited?"

"My dear Hemanta, I had not yet ruined you. You
were happy. That was essential."

Peari Sankar leaned back. His calmness had the terrible completeness
of a well-sharpened instrument.

"If I had exposed the matter immediately, Harihar might have
concealed it, performed a private prayaschittam (rite of expiation), and
found you another bride. But happiness, when allowed to put
down roots, cannot be removed without bringing up half the
earth. So I waited.

"Then your sister's marriage was arranged with a family whose
orthodoxy rivals your father's. Yesterday I sent them one letter
and Harihar another. Her marriage will fail; your father's pride
will be dragged through the same dust into which he
cast my house; and you must choose between his caste
and your wife. It is, you must admit, a well-rounded
composition. Had I a literary education, I might turn it
into a romance."

Hemanta seized the edge of the table. "And Kusum? What
had she done to you?"

"Nothing."

"My sister?"

"Nothing."

"Then what justice is this?"

Peari Sankar sighed. "Justice? I spoke only of repayment. Your
father also called his acts duty, not cruelty. Men choose
dignified words as assassins choose dark lanes."

For a moment neither spoke. Outside, a cart creaked along
the sleeping road. Time seemed to stand around them like
a black ocean, without wave or shore.

Peari Sankar pushed the pan-box forward.

"Now will you take one?"

Hemanta looked at him with disgust, but beneath his disgust
another understanding had begun painfully to stir. Before him sat
not a demon sent ready-made into the world, but a
man who had once begged, had been refused, had buried
his daughter and nephew, and had then worshipped his wounds
until they became a god demanding sacrifice. Suffering does not
invariably make the heart tender; sometimes it teaches cruelty to
speak in the accents of justice.

Hemanta said, "You have punished my father by becoming like
him."

For the first time Peari Sankar's smile vanished.

Hemanta left.

The moon had declined westward. Clouds, thin as ashes, passed
over it, and the streets lay empty beneath a light
that no longer appeared festive. As he walked home, Kusum's
hidden years accompanied him: the child-bride who had scarcely known
her child-husband; the orphan dependent upon reluctant kindness; the girl
on the terrace; the bride surrounded by sacred fire and
invented ancestors; the wife who had awakened each morning beside
love as beside a treasure stolen from a temple, fearing
every footstep at the door.

He remembered her restraint, which he had mistaken for serenity;
her sudden pallor whenever caste was discussed; her habit of
standing aside during family ceremonies until his mother called her
forward. How easily the fortunate interpret another's silence as peace!
The chain had lain beneath the flowers, and he had
praised the flowers.

Yet deception remained deception. His trust had been broken. The
mind repeated this with stern precision; the heart answered only
by showing him Kusum seated alone in the moonlit room.

When he returned, the house was silent. He opened the
chamber door.

Kusum had not lit the lamp. The full moon of
evening was sinking, and the room had filled with uncertain
darkness. She sat on the floor beside the bed, her
head resting against its wooden frame. The necklace Hemanta had
fastened round her wrist lay before her.

She rose when he entered.

"I will go," she said.

"Where?"

"Anywhere."

"That is not a place."

"There are places for women who have no place."

"Who told you so?"

"No one needs to tell us."

He could not answer. The world had provided Kusum with
guardians, priests, matchmakers, fathers-in-law, and avengers in abundance; only her
own will had remained an unnecessary luxury.

She approached and knelt at his feet.

"Do not quarrel with your father for me. I deceived
you. Let me go before morning. Say I died. That
will be almost true."

Hemanta drew back as if her touch burned him.

"Do not hold my feet."

She released them at once.

That obedience decided what argument had not. In the eternal
picture which fate, a patient and somewhat cruel painter, had
arranged within that dark room, one human being stood as
judge and another knelt as accused; but Hemanta suddenly saw
that the accused had been judged all her life, while
those who fashioned the crime sat comfortably outside the frame.

He lifted her by both hands.

"I cannot say that nothing has changed," he said. "Something
has broken."

Kusum's face became still.

"But it is not you who broke it."

From the corridor came again the sound of slippers. On
the earlier night that sound had interrupted confession; now it
approached like the drum preceding a sentence.

Harihar entered. Behind him hovered two elderly kinsmen, eager to
support virtue at a safe distance.

"Well?" he demanded. "Has she gone?"

"No."

"You have heard the truth?"

"Yes."

"Then send her away. At dawn the priests will come.
There must be expiation. Until then you will touch neither
food nor water."

Hemanta said nothing.

Harihar's voice rose. "Do you understand? If you keep this
woman, I renounce you. You will have no share of
my property. No Brahmin will enter your house. You will
lose caste."

Kusum's fingers tightened in Hemanta's hand. Outside the window the
papiya had fallen silent. The moon was now only a
pale fragment caught between two clouds; the jasmine moved in
the south wind, though its fragrance seemed to have withdrawn
into some inaccessible depth of the night.

Harihar struck the floor with his slipper.

"Choose!"

All the elaborate machinery of society waited: dead ancestors, living
neighbours, priests with their mantras, relatives with their whispers, revenge
calling itself justice, pride calling itself purity. Against them stood
a young man whose happiness had been wounded, a woman
who wished to disappear, and a sentence so plain that
no scripture was required to interpret it.

Hemanta looked at his father.

"I do not care for caste."

Harihar stared at him.

Hemanta drew Kusum closer.

"I choose my wife."

For a few moments no one moved. Then the old
man turned away, and his slippers retreated down the corridor,
no less authoritative in sound, though authority itself had suffered
an invisible fracture.

The clouds covered the moon. Husband and wife remained beside
the open window in darkness, without forgiveness fully spoken, without
sorrow ended, without any promise that the world would grow
kind by morning. Human love does not conquer all things;
such a claim is made only by songs and by
those who have never paid its price. But sometimes, when
custom has twisted beauty out of shape and duty has
lent its mask to cruelty, love refuses to become their
accomplice.

The south wind entered once more and stirred Kusum's loosened
hair. Far away, from some tree hidden in the dark,
the papiya called.

# OLD MOTHER MISCHIEF

There is a great difference, you must know, between being
busy and being industrious. The industrious creature attends first to
the duty under his own nose, and, when that is
honestly finished, may perhaps lend a wing, a paw, or
a word of counsel to his neighbour. The merely busy
creature, on the contrary, flies up and down, here and
there, peeps into every window, counts every egg but her
own, and makes such a clatter about setting the world
to rights that nobody notices her own nest going to
pieces behind her.

Old Mother Magpie belonged decidedly to the latter class.

From sunrise until the evening star appeared over Parson Too-Whit's
cedar, she was never still. She knew who had dined
with the Squirrels, why Nancy Nibble had changed her dressmaker,
how many acorns Deacon Graytail had put by, and whether
the young Woodpeckers were being brought up according to the
newest system. Yet her own chimney leaked, her children went
abroad with uncombed topknots, and there was generally no supper
until one of them reminded her of it.

"Bless me!" she would cry. "Can a public-spirited person remember
everything?"

One morning she called upon Mrs. Oriole, who lived in
a pretty swinging nest at the end of an elm
branch. Mrs. Oriole was stitching the torn edge of the
nursery and singing softly to herself when Mother Magpie thrust
in her black-and-white head.

"My dear!" she exclaimed. "What a situation!"

Mrs. Oriole dropped her grass needle. "Is anything wrong?"

"Wrong? I hope not. I never alarm people. But does
the house always swing in this shocking manner?"

"The wind moves it," said Mrs. Oriole. "We Orioles rather
like that."

"A fiddle on what Orioles like! I am speaking of
the constitution. Dr. Mole's new lectures prove beyond contradiction that
continual oscillation injures the nervous system, unsettles the digestion, and
gives young birds extravagant opinions."

"My children seem healthy."

"That is precisely the danger. Complaints of this sort make
no announcement. One day the little creatures chirp; the next
day—well, I will not distress you."

Mrs. Oriole glanced anxiously toward the nursery, though the nestlings
were at that moment quarrelling with great vigour over a
green caterpillar.

Mother Magpie inspected the walls, pulled at a fastening, condemned
the moss, advised a northern exposure, and declared horsehair immoral
because it encouraged luxury. Then she settled herself upon the
rim.

"And where is Tommy?"

"At the Singing Committee, I believe."

"Again!"

"He is often required."

"My dear Mrs. Oriole, committees require only those who enjoy
being required. A married bird's first committee is his family."

Mrs. Oriole's breast feathers flushed orange. "Tommy has many engagements."

"I defend him everywhere," said Mother Magpie, lowering her voice.
"When Mrs. Jay remarked yesterday that no respectable husband ought
to be abroad after moonrise, I said, 'Tommy is an
artist. Nature has given him a temperament.'"

"Mrs. Jay said that?"

"Not in those exact words. I never repeat gossip. But
between ourselves, the parish observes things. Your nest swings at
one end of the branch, and Tommy swings at the
other end of society."

"He is a good husband!"

"Certainly! Only absent. Goodness at a distance is still goodness,
though perhaps less useful. You should speak firmly."

"I do speak."

"Then speak more firmly. Tears, silence, a fainting fit—there are
many respectable methods. I understand husbands."

Now Mother Magpie had never understood her own husband while
he lived, and had contradicted him steadily for seventeen years;
but death, as often happens, had converted her experience into
infallible wisdom.

Mrs. Oriole at last begged to be left alone. Mother
Magpie departed, assuring her that no offence had been intended
and that gratitude often wore a disagreeable expression when first
awakened.

That afternoon she met Tommy Oriole outside the parsonage.

"Poor fellow!" she cried. "How worn you look!"

"I am in excellent health."

"Ah, you brave creatures always say so. I have just
come from your home."

Tommy adjusted his neck ribbon. "Indeed?"

"I fear your wife is dissatisfied."

"With what?"

"With everything, apparently—the committee, your singing, your late hours. She
spoke with considerable warmth. Naturally, I defended you."

Tommy drew himself up. "She objects to my public duties?"

"She thinks a husband should sit continually on the nest,
I suppose."

"Does she imagine," said Tommy, "that a bird endowed with
my range and sensibility can spend life tying grass knots?
Nature has marked out for me a path beyond the
range of the commonplace."

"So I told her."

"And what did she say?"

Mother Magpie had not been told anything of the kind,
but she answered, "I dislike repeating domestic remarks."

"Repeat it!"

"Well—she suggested that some voices sound better at a distance."

Tommy's crest rose. He flew home immediately, not to make
peace, as a wiser bird might have done, but to
demand whether his genius was appreciated. Mrs. Oriole asked why
Mrs. Jay had been discussing him; Tommy asked why his
wife had ridiculed his voice; each quoted Mother Magpie; neither
listened to the other; and before sunset the whole elm
knew there had been a quarrel.

The consequence was that Tommy spent even more time at
the Singing Committee, saying home disturbed his artistic development. Mother
Magpie, when reproached, declared that young couples were sadly touchy
and that she had done her utmost to reconcile them.

But old Mother Magpie had now got a new business
in hand.

Dr. Bullfrog, the parish music teacher, had lived for many
years in a comfortable recess beside the river, among cat-tails
and broad lily leaves. He had trained three generations of
woodland choristers in breathing, rhythm, and the proper management of
the lower register. His own evening performance—"Paddy-go-donk! Paddy-go-donk!"—was not fashionable,
but it was deep, honest, and could be heard beyond
the mill.

Mother Magpie found him sunning himself upon a stone.

"Doctor," she began solemnly, "have you heard nothing?"

"I have heard the gnats," replied Bullfrog. "They are sharp
this morning."

"I mean about the parish."

"What about it?"

She sighed. "Then I must be the one to tell
you. How disagreeable! And I, who never carry unpleasant news!"

Dr. Bullfrog's yellow throat trembled. "Is my rent to be
raised?"

"Worse. There is talk of replacing you."

"Replacing me!"

"Pray remain calm. The younger birds demand modern instruction. Italian
scales, fashionable trills, aerial execution—the old waterside method is considered,
pardon me, damp."

"But I taught Madam Lark."

"Established merit is always respected after it is removed. That
is the difficulty."

"Has Parson Too-Whit complained?"

"Not precisely."

"The committee?"

"Committees do not complain until their minds are settled. By
then it is too late."

Poor Dr. Bullfrog had lately observed that several young Finches
giggled during his lessons. He had attributed this to bad
breeding; Mother Magpie now persuaded him it was a revolution.

"What must I do?" he asked.

"Move on land immediately. Water draws the voice downward. Take
a room near the orchard, alter your diet, and imitate
the birds."

"But I am not a bird."

"That is an antiquated objection. Art rises above anatomy."

"I have lived beside this river sixty-three years."

"Then change is medically necessary. At your age the system
requires novelty."

So confidently did she speak of systems, membranes, reputation, and
the Continental method that Dr. Bullfrog began to be ashamed
of his lily leaves. Within three days he had abandoned
his cool waterside chamber and taken lodging beneath a currant
bush in the gardener's grounds.

The place was dusty, hot, and full of ants. His
legs ached; his skin dried; and he missed the friendly
mud. Nevertheless, Mother Magpie arrived every morning to conduct his
transformation.

"Chest forward!" she cried. "Chin elevated! Think upward!"

"My chin will not go any higher."

"Then imagine it does. Now—tweet!"

"Croak."

"No, no! Tweet!"

"C-r-r-weet!"

"Better. Again, with refinement."

For two miserable weeks he practised scales. "Do—re—mi" became in
his throat "Donk—rawk—gurr," and his attempt at a nightingale's run
sounded like a cart rolling over walnuts. The Sparrows gathered
on the fence. Young rabbits hid beneath the cabbages and
stuffed clover into their mouths to keep from laughing. Even
Nancy Nibble, who was charitable when it cost nothing, said
the Doctor appeared to be taking courses unbefitting his age
and standing.

At length Mother Magpie arranged a public trial.

"You must astonish them," she said. "Appear suddenly beneath the
rose arch and perform the Italian passage."

"I would rather return home."

"Home! Have you no ambition?"

"I had comfort."

"Comfort is the enemy of progress."

That evening the orchard company assembled. Mother Magpie announced that
Dr. Bullfrog would demonstrate "the emancipation of the natural voice
from its merely hereditary limitations." The Doctor stepped from beneath
a rhubarb leaf, trembling in every toe.

He opened his mouth.

"Pee—ee—ee—RAWK—PADDY-GO-DONK!"

There was a dreadful silence; then every Sparrow burst into
a twitter. The Jays screamed, the young Squirrels rolled over
one another, and Tommy Oriole, who ought to have known
better, declared it the finest imitation of a broken gate
ever heard in the parish.

"Continue!" whispered Mother Magpie.

Dr. Bullfrog attempted a trill.

At that instant the gardener, supposing some strange pest had
got among his vegetables, flung a wooden shoe toward the
sound.

THUMP!

It struck the earth scarcely an inch from the Doctor's
nose.

He leaped blindly from the rose bed, knocked over a
flowerpot, and landed before the kennel. The gardener's dog, delighted
by this unexpected entertainment, sprang out barking.

Across the path went Dr. Bullfrog—hop, scramble, tumble!—with the dog
behind him and half the orchard shouting directions. He passed
under the gate, over a ditch, through nettles, and down
the bank, until at last he plunged with one enormous
splash into his native river.

The dog stopped. The laughter stopped also; for even foolish
creatures sometimes discover, when danger appears, that their joke has
gone too far.

Mother Magpie alone remained composed.

"I always said land air would not agree with him,"
she remarked. "Besides, he began too ambitiously."

Dr. Bullfrog returned to his old recess among the cat-tails,
but he was never quite the same. He resumed teaching
after a time, though he would no longer sing at
concerts, and if any pupil mentioned improvement, fashion, or Italian
execution, his broad mouth closed like a locked door.

When Parson Too-Whit asked who had induced him to leave
the river, the Doctor stared gloomily across the water.

"Her name," he said, "is not Mother Magpie. Call her
by her proper title—Old Mother Mischief."

And so the parish did, though never when she was
near; for she continued to fly up and down, here
and there, correcting everybody's affairs, perfectly satisfied with her usefulness,
while her own nest leaked, her own children went supperless,
and disorder followed wherever her helpful shadow fell.

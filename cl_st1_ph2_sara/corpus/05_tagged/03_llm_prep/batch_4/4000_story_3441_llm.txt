### IDLE WORDS

*"Every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give
account thereof in the day of judgment."*

The minister had repeated these words with unusual solemnity at
the conclusion of his sermon; and, during the walk home,
Miss B. had scarcely spoken. Even when Helen called her
attention to the laburnum in Mrs. Gray's garden, which had
opened into a shower of gold since the preceding Sunday,
she only looked at it, shook her head gently, and
walked on.

At dinner the same restraint continued. Their uncle, who had
observed it, at length said—

"You are less communicative than usual, my dear. Has anything
disturbed you?"

"No, uncle," replied Miss B.; "I have only been reflecting
upon the sermon."

"So have I," said Helen; "but reflection does not commonly
prevent one from asking for a little more currant jelly."

Miss B. looked grave.

"You may laugh, Helen; but the subject is a serious
one. If we must give account for every idle word,
ought we not to be very watchful that we speak
nothing which has not some useful purpose?"

"Useful purpose!" repeated Helen. "Must every sentence teach geography, mend
a stocking, convert a sinner, or tell us how to
make bread?"

"Not precisely; but it should have a directly beneficial tendency."

"Then I am afraid," said Helen, "that half my conversation
must be condemned without benefit of clergy. This morning I
spoke of the east wind, Mrs. Gray's flowers, Louisa Morton's
new bonnet, the Canary bird at the parsonage, and the
extraordinary quantity of lace on Mrs. Bell's sleeves. I cannot
prove that one immortal soul was improved by any of
these observations."

"It is that very habit of thoughtless talking which seems
to me so dangerous," said Miss B. "We become accustomed
to uttering whatever enters the mind, and Christian responsibility is
forgotten."

"But," said Helen, "if every one should adopt your rule,
what would become of visiting? Suppose six sober persons meet
in a room, each resolved to say nothing which has
not a directly useful tendency. They would sit like a
parcel of old clocks ticking at each other. One might
venture to remark that eternity is solemn; another would answer
that duty is important; and then there would be silence
until tea."

Their uncle smiled.

"You state the difficulty rather vividly, Helen. Yet your cousin's
principle deserves a fair examination. Tell me, my dear, what
do you understand by an idle word?"

"One that does no good."

"Does every word do harm which does no obvious good?"

"I think that whatever does not advance us must retard
us."

"That maxim may sound wise," said he, "and still be
very unwisely applied. When you walked home, did you advance
every moment?"

"Certainly not. We stopped at the crossing."

"Then you were retarded?"

"For a minute."

"And sinfully?"

Miss B. hesitated.

"No; because the delay was necessary."

"Necessary to what?"

"To safety."

"Very well. And may not rest, amusement, and the easy
interchange of kindly feeling be necessary to the safety and
health of the mind?"

"But conversation about the weather can hardly refresh the soul."

"Let us see. Suppose you visit an aged woman who
is poor, timid, and unused to society. You enter with
a strong determination that every word shall have a directly
religious usefulness. You begin by asking whether she is prepared
for death; you proceed to inquire into the state of
her affections; and, before leaving, you warn her against hardness
of heart. Would you necessarily have done good?"

Miss B. looked troubled.

"I might frighten her, perhaps."

"Now suppose Helen goes in, admires the geranium in the
window, asks whether the rain has helped the beans, listens
to the history of the old woman's cat, and laughs
over the misbehavior of a grandchild. Presently the poor creature
forgets her shyness, speaks of her troubles, and receives with
gratitude a few simple words of comfort. Which visit has
been the more useful?"

"Helen's, in that case."

"And was the geranium useless? Was the rain idle? Was
the cat wholly destitute of a beneficial tendency?"

Helen's eyes sparkled.

"I always believed cats had a moral mission."

"You must not triumph too soon," said her uncle. "A
pleasant manner may open the heart; but pleasantness without principle
may also lead it astray."

"I submit to the qualification."

Miss B. remained thoughtful.

"Then you consider all cheerful conversation innocent?"

"By no means. We must distinguish between amusement and frivolity,
as we distinguish between rest and sloth. The laborer who
sits beneath a tree at noon is not therefore an
idler; but if he remain there until sunset, his rest
assumes another character. So the mind requires intervals in which
its powers may unbend. Perpetual tension is not strength. A
bow always drawn loses its elasticity; and a spirit compelled
to move continually among solemn subjects may become exhausted, gloomy,
and at last strangely indifferent to those very truths which
it has handled without relief."

"Can religion make any one gloomy?" asked Miss B.

"True religion cannot; but a mistaken treatment of religion may.
We may confound seriousness with sadness, and watchfulness with anxiety.
We may become so fastidious concerning the form of goodness
that we lose its temper. A person may speak of
duty from morning until night, and yet render duty disagreeable
to every one around her."

Helen glanced at her cousin, but Miss B.'s expression checked
the smile which had risen.

Their uncle continued—

"There is an ascetic spirit which suspects whatever is agreeable.
If music delights us, it asks whether music is necessary;
if flowers please us, it demands what doctrine they prove;
if laughter visits the family table, it looks as though
an enemy had entered. Yet He who gave fragrance to
the rose, melody to the bird, and mirthfulness to childhood
cannot have intended that innocent pleasure should be treated as
an offence."

"But the text is very plain," said Miss B. "It
says, 'Every idle word.'"

"Scripture is plain when Scripture is fairly understood. We must
not take a word, attach to it the narrowest meaning
our fears can devise, and then call the result reverence.
What is an idle servant?"

"One who neglects his work."

"Or one who, being entrusted with power, fails to employ
it properly. An idle word may therefore be a careless,
ineffective, or irresponsible word—a word which, instead of fulfilling the
proper purpose of speech, becomes fruitless of good and productive
of evil."

"But how are we to know which words will produce
evil?"

"We cannot foresee every consequence; and we are not accountable
for what no reasonable prudence could anticipate. We are responsible
for the natural and probable effects of our conduct. If
I drop a spark into dry straw, I cannot excuse
myself by saying that I did not personally burn the
barn. I knew, or ought to have known, what fire
might do."

Helen grew serious.

"You mean that words are sparks?"

"Very often. A hasty criticism, repeated from one person to
another, may destroy a reputation which years of uprightness have
established. A jest against some harmless peculiarity may fasten a
cruel nickname upon a sensitive child. A doubtful insinuation may
make friends suspicious of each other. The speaker forgets his
sentence in an hour; but the sentence travels, enlarges, and
wounds long after his voice is silent."

"That is not what people commonly call idle conversation," observed
Miss B.

"Perhaps not; yet it is much nearer the scriptural meaning
than a remark upon roses or bonnet patterns. Harmless trifles
may promote kindness; malicious trifles may poison it. The moral
quality lies less in the importance of the subject than
in the spirit and tendency of the words."

Helen sighed.

"I recollect saying, last Thursday, that Mr. Allen appeared very
fond of being praised. Caroline asked me why; and I
told her it was because he had repeated Mrs. Ford's
compliment to three persons. I meant no great harm."

"What followed?"

"Caroline said she had always thought him vain. Then Lucy
declared that his humility was mere affectation. I contradicted that;
but somehow, before we separated, we had reviewed almost everything
he had done during the winter."

"And was your first observation founded upon sufficient knowledge of
his motives?"

"No," said Helen, after a pause. "I suppose it was
not."

"There is an idle word. It was not idle because
Mr. Allen's character is a trivial subject, but because you
spoke without a just purpose, without adequate knowledge, and without
regard to consequences. Your remark invited criticism; criticism encouraged severity;
and severity ended in injustice."

"I am afraid," said Helen, "that I scatter more sparks
than I imagined."

"We all do, unless principle governs the temper from which
speech proceeds. Consider, too, remarks upon religious subjects. A young
man hears an imperfect sermon, and afterward makes its faults
the entertainment of the dinner table. He imitates the preacher's
tone, ridicules an awkward expression, and gains a laugh. Perhaps
a younger brother, who had listened with some conviction, concludes
that the discourse deserves only contempt. The impression is lost;
and the jester may never know that his cleverness helped
to destroy it."

"But must we never criticize a sermon?" Helen asked.

"Certainly we may. Ministers are fallible, and their teaching should
be examined. But there is a difference between thoughtful examination
and contemptuous sport. One seeks truth; the other seeks applause.
One points out error that it may be corrected; the
other exposes weakness that the speaker may display his wit."

Miss B. said earnestly—

"I have often thought that people speak with unpardonable levity
of sacred things."

"Levity may be deeply culpable; but take care how you
use the word *unpardonable*. Some sincere persons have been terrified
because a random expression, uttered in ignorance or excitement, seemed
to them the sin which wanteth forgiveness. The warning against
blasphemy describes a settled and malignant resistance to acknowledged truth,
not every rash syllable which a penitent heart afterward deplores."

"Then even a skeptical remark may not be an unpardonable
sin?"

"It may be a great sin, and yet not that
sin. Suppose a man repeats an objection to revelation which
he has never examined. He repeats it because it sounds
ingenious, and because he enjoys disturbing the confidence of others.
A young listener, less informed than himself, is shaken; prayer
becomes difficult; duty loses authority; and years of sorrow may
follow. The speaker cannot say, 'I merely offered a casual
thought.' He used influence carelessly where the highest interests were
concerned."

Helen folded her napkin slowly.

"It seems, uncle, that you have defended conversation only to
make it more alarming."

"No. I would make it free by making it pure.
There are two kinds of bondage: one is the fear
which forbids every spontaneous word; the other is the habit
which makes us slaves to every passing impulse. Christian liberty
delivers us from both."

"But if I must consider all possible consequences before I
speak," said Helen, "conversation will be over before I have
finished considering."

"You cannot calculate every consequence, nor is such calculation required.
The tongue cannot be governed by a committee of thoughts
assembled before every sentence. The deeper work must be done
in the character. 'A good man out of the good
treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things.' If the
heart is benevolent, candid, reverent, and pure, its unpremeditated words
will commonly possess the same qualities."

"Commonly?" repeated Miss B.

"We are imperfect, and must therefore remain watchful. But watchfulness
is not perpetual alarm. The gardener does not examine each
drop that passes through the fountain; he cleanses the spring
and guards the channel. So must we cultivate the affections
from which conversation flows."

"What affections especially?" asked Miss B.

"Kindness, which will make us unwilling to wound; humility, which
will restrain contempt; truthfulness, which will forbid exaggeration; purity, which
will reject corrupting allusions; reverence, which will prevent sacred things
from becoming toys; and charity, which 'thinketh no evil.' Where
these are living principles, speech need not move with the
stiffness of a funeral procession."

Helen smiled again.

"Then I may still admire Mrs. Gray's laburnum?"

"You may even describe it as a shower of gold,
if you do not insist that every one else's laburnum
resembles a collection of yellow rags."

"And I may discuss bonnets?"

"If the bonnet does not lead you to envy the
wearer, ridicule the maker, or spend money which belongs to
the butcher."

"And Canary birds?"

"Provided you do not falsely accuse the bird of singing
during family prayers."

"It did sing."

"Then state the fact without aspersions upon its theology."

Even Miss B. laughed; but she checked herself almost immediately.

"Do not repent of that," said her uncle. "Your laughter
has done Helen good."

"It has indeed," said Helen. "I was beginning to fear
that my cousin would never smile again without first discovering
its useful tendency."

Miss B. colored.

"Perhaps I have been too severe. Yet I still fear
the danger of thoughtlessness."

"And justly," replied her uncle. "Thoughtlessness, indulged as a habit,
weakens moral perception. A person who speaks continually for effect
begins to regard companions as an audience; one who delights
in gossip learns to feed upon the faults of others;
one who habitually utters cynical remarks may at last become
incapable of generous judgment. Words react upon the speaker. We
become, in part, what we repeatedly express."

"Then silence is sometimes safest," she said.

"Certainly. There is 'a time to keep silence, and a
time to speak.' Silence is noble when it restrains anger,
refuses scandal, or leaves another's secret untouched. But silence may
also be proud, unkind, or cowardly. If a stranger sits
neglected while we preserve our solemnity, our silence is not
charitable. If a friend is unjustly blamed and we say
nothing, it is not harmless. If affection asks for expression
and we deny it through stiffness, some of our unspoken
words may be more culpable than Helen's remarks about the
weather."

"I never supposed one could be accountable for idle silence,"
said Helen.

"We are accountable for influence, whether exerted by speech or
by reserve. A cheerful greeting may encourage one who is
secretly burdened; a few familiar words may reconcile persons whom
formality has kept apart; the mention of a flower may
awaken gratitude; even a harmless absurdity, making a weary household
laugh, may perform a service which grave instruction could not
perform at that moment."

Miss B. looked toward the window, where the afternoon light
lay upon the table.

"I think I understand. The usefulness need not always be
direct."

"Precisely. Whatever innocently refreshes the mind, strengthens affection, removes embarrassment,
or diffuses cheerfulness has a real, though often indirect, usefulness.
Religion does not require us to be always preaching; it
requires us to be always governed by love."

"And the idle word," said Helen, "is not necessarily the
word about an idle subject."

"No. A grave subject may be treated idly, and a
light subject kindly. One person may speak of judgment in
order to display superior sanctity; another may speak of a
rose in order to please a lonely child. We must
look to knowledge, intention, spirit, and probable consequence."

Miss B. was silent for a little while.

"Uncle," she said at last, "I believe my error has
been in trying to make every sentence visibly religious, instead
of trying to make the heart religious from which the
sentences come."

"That is well expressed."

"And mine," said Helen, "has been in supposing that, because
conversation should be easy, it may therefore be careless."

"That also is well expressed."

"Then," continued Helen, "we have each been half wrong, which
is much more sociable than for one of us to
be entirely so."

Her uncle smiled.

"Let the correction be equally divided. Helen must learn that
wit cannot cancel cruelty, nor good humor excuse falsehood; and
your cousin must learn that solemnity is not the only
evidence of goodness. Let one guard against a tongue without
a conscience, and the other against a conscience which leaves
the tongue nothing pleasant to say."

The church bell began to ring for the afternoon service.
Miss B. rose, then paused beside Helen's chair.

"Was Mrs. Gray's laburnum really so beautiful?"

"Beautiful!" cried Helen. "It looked as if the sunshine had
been caught in every branch. And beneath it there were
purple tulips, though I cannot imagine why she planted them
together."

"Perhaps," said Miss B., with a gentler expression than she
had worn all day, "she thought the contrast agreeable."

"It was agreeable—though not, I fear, directly improving."

"I am not now quite sure," replied Miss B., "that
agreeable things never improve us."

Their uncle took his hat.

"If they soften the temper, awaken gratitude, or help us
to give pleasure without doing harm, we need not be
ashamed of them. Let us not spend life anxiously counting
every word after it is uttered; let us rather seek
that inward treasure of truth and charity from which good
words naturally proceed. Then seriousness will not be gloomy, nor
cheerfulness vain; silence will be considerate, and speech sincere. For
when the heart is pure and benevolent, even its lightest
conversation may carry kindness with it, as the common air
carries the fragrance of flowers."

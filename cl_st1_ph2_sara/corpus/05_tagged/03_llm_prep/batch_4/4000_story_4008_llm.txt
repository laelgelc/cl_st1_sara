It was after tea, I think, though the fire had
sunk so low that the room possessed that uncertain colour
which belongs neither to afternoon nor evening, when the chairs
become larger than they are, and the books recede into
the shelves as if ashamed of their titles. I had
put the cup upon the carpet—an indolent and dangerous habit—and
was watching a coal split along its scarlet seam when
I first saw the mark above the mantelpiece. It was
small, roundish, and dark; not black precisely, but brown with
a purple edge, as a drop of old wine might
look if wine could cling to plaster. I ought, of
course, to have risen. Three steps would have settled the
matter. Yet there are moments when three steps seem a
concession to tyranny, and I remained where I was.

A nail hole, perhaps. The former tenants may have hung
a portrait there: an admiral with silver whiskers, or a
child in white muslin holding a hoop. No, the mark
was too high for a child and too low for
an admiral, if admirals require height, as I suppose they
do. Authority likes to look downward. It is not enough
to possess ships, medals, dispatch boxes, and a name printed
in heavy type; one must also be placed six inches
above the eyes of visitors. But the tenants before us
were not, I believe, naval people. They left behind a
pale blue jar, a broken boot-tree, and three pins under
the kitchen dresser. From such remains one constructs a civilization.
Antiquaries have done more with less.

The mark might show where a bracket had been fixed.
On the bracket there may have stood a clock, and
beneath the clock the family may have waited through dinners,
illnesses, quarrels, births, and those Sunday afternoons which seem designed
to prove that time can stop while clocks continue. How
faithfully objects outlive the emotions arranged around them! The clock
is sold, the daughter marries a dentist in Exeter, the
father dies saying something no one hears, and the maid
throws the ashes away; yet the hole made by a
screw persists, quietly recording pressure, weight, removal. If the wall
were stripped, one would find old papers beneath the new:
roses beneath stripes, stripes beneath yellow medallions, each pattern believing
itself permanent while the paste loosened.

Still, I cannot be sure it is a hole. There
appears to be a slight swelling on one side. It
may be a flake of plaster curling outward, and then
the darkness is shadow rather than substance. How quickly the
mind provides materials! Give it a speck and it builds
scaffoldings, galleries, ancestral halls; one thought arrives carrying a plank,
another a coil of rope, a third, officious creature, brings
a flag and insists that the whole structure represents Progress.
Meanwhile the mark stays there, refusing promotion, neither hole nor
heraldic emblem, and I admire it for that. There is
a dignity in withholding one's identity. People are named, numbered,
examined, invited, taxed, ranked—and the mark is merely the mark.

The cup on the carpet reminded me of the green
cup we once owned, with a handle like a swan's
neck. Where did it go? Things disappear without any proper
ceremony. One morning there are six teaspoons; by Thursday there
are five. A red glove lies for months in a
drawer waiting for its partner, then vanishes too, perhaps out
of delicacy. Scissors migrate from basket to desk, from desk
to kitchen, from kitchen into the void where buttons, pencils,
spectacle cases, and small coins assemble in a republic beyond
human reach. I sometimes imagine that after death we shall
be confronted not by angels but by lost property: the
umbrella left in the train at Reading, the ring dropped
in the garden, the letter placed somewhere safe. They will
surround us silently, and we shall recognize our lives by
what escaped them.

But death—why should a mark above the mantel lead to
death? Because everything does, if followed far enough. A teaspoon
leads to the hand that polished it, the hand to
the body, the body to the bed with its white
counterpane, and beyond the bed lies that blank province which
priests cover with gold lettering and philosophers with fog. They
say the dead know more than we do. This seems
an unjust reward for being dead. Perhaps they know less;
perhaps knowledge depends upon the small friction of being alive—the
shoe pinching, the kettle singing, dust settling on the photograph.
Without surfaces, edges, interruptions, how could thought take hold? Eternity
may be smooth as glass, and therefore impossible to grasp.

The mark, at least, has an edge. I can see
that now—or think I can. It interrupts the wall. How
comforting interruption is! A bell, a knock, a crumb in
the bed: all rescue us from the endlessness of ourselves.
If there were no obstacles the mind would pour on
and on like water over a vanished bank, flooding childhood,
Rome, the moon, tomorrow's shopping, until nothing had shape. Perhaps
personality itself is only a collection of obstacles: habits, names,
the angle at which one holds a spoon, the unreasonable
dislike of certain hats. Take these away and where is
"I"? Somewhere beneath, people say, there is a true self.
But why beneath? Why not on the surface, flickering among
gestures, prejudices, errors—the only places where anyone has ever met
us?

I remember, at seventeen, deciding that I was reserved. The
decision pleased me so much that for nearly a year
I spoke in a lower voice and looked out of
windows when addressed. How laboriously we manufacture what we afterwards
call nature! A cousin says, "You are the impulsive one,"
and there we are, rushing through doors for twenty years.
A schoolmaster praises our reason, so we drag Reason behind
us like a brass cannon, firing it at sunsets, marriages,
and dreams. Then one morning we discover that the reserved
woman longs to shout in railway stations, while the reasonable
man has spent half his salary on a mechanical bird.
No, no, the self is not a statue hidden in
marble. It is more like flame among dry twigs—shape made
by consumption, altered by every draught.

The mark could be an insect. But surely an insect
would have moved. Unless it is dead; or asleep; or
so slow that my idea of movement is merely vulgar
impatience. We measure all creatures by the clock in our
own blood. A mayfly's afternoon contains a dynasty; a stone's
century may be no more than the closing of an
eyelid. Trees stand in the square and appear passive, although
under the bark armies of moisture climb, roots negotiate with
darkness, leaves bargain with light. We call this stillness because
we cannot hear the terms. Perhaps the mark has crossed
half the wall since breakfast and I, blind through haste,
suppose it fixed.

Outside, a cart went by, the wheels grinding upon the
road. For an instant the panes trembled and the brass
shovel beside the grate gave a thin complaint. Then silence
returned, carrying the sound away. Streets are full of people
hurrying to confirm one another's arrangements. At five they open
offices; at six they shut shops; at seven they dress
for dinner and sit in prescribed order, the important beside
the beautiful, the rich beside the useful, and the insignificant
at the draughty end. There must be books explaining it.
The Chancellor precedes the Bishop, the Bishop precedes the Admiral,
the Admiral precedes the widow of a man who preceded
everyone while alive. If a comet entered the dining room,
somebody would consult the Table of Precedency before offering it
soup.

I have always mistrusted tables—those printed ones, I mean, though
wooden tables are not innocent. A wooden table bruises the
knee and collects unpaid bills, but a printed table claims
that the universe has been measured and divided into squares.
Kings in one column, rivers in another; annual rainfall, mineral
exports, noble families, causes of death. How soothing to discover
that sorrow is forty-two per thousand! Whitaker, with his neat
pages, closes the lid upon confusion. Yet beneath every figure
something wriggles. "Population": millions of private mornings, toothaches, jealousies, songs
half remembered. "Casualties": a boot in mud, a watch still
ticking, a mother unfolding a telegram beneath the kitchen lamp.
The columns stand straight; what they contain does not.

Perhaps that is why men are so fond of systems.
I say men, though women may be just as fond
when allowed a filing cabinet. Systems save one from looking
too closely. Once the world is divided into civilized and
savage, suitable and unsuitable, sane and eccentric, one need only
stamp the labels. There is great satisfaction in a stamp:
the downward blow, the purple certainty. I should like, for
a day, to sit in an office and stamp everything
incorrectly. Admirable on the tax demand; Perishable on the bishop;
Return to Sender on the declaration of war. Yet by
noon I should become proud of my disorder and issue
regulations for rebellion. Freedom, too, grows uniforms if watched long
enough.

The mark remains outside all regulations. It has neither ancestry
nor profession. If it is a crack, however, the house
ought to be inspected. There, practicality puts its boot through
philosophy. A crack may mean damp, subsidence, beams rotting secretly
in their sockets. We imagine the house solid because the
floor receives our weight each morning, but wood softens, nails
redden, mortar crumbles grain by grain. The whole room is
engaged in falling down at a pace suited to furniture.
The mantelpiece descends invisibly; the ceiling sifts itself upon our
heads as dust; the roses on the carpet fade beneath
our feet. Solidity is only collapse conducted with patience.

And the earth itself—there is the grandest falling object of
all, spinning in darkness while we balance cups on carpets
and speak of permanent addresses. It tumbles with oceans pasted
to it, Alps, cathedrals, sheep, factories, nurseries, all rushing at
a speed that would tear thought from the skull if
speed could be felt without comparison. Yet the fire burns
upright. A crumb drops predictably. We call that stability. One
might as well sit in a railway carriage and declare
the countryside in motion, which perhaps it is; perhaps nothing
is privileged, not the train, not the field, not the
star. Knowledge becomes a quarrel over which object is permitted
to be still.

Scientists know, of course. Or they know what is accepted
until Wednesday. On Thursday a particle is discovered behaving badly,
and the schoolbooks must be altered. I like that particle.
I picture it slipping under the laboratory door while professors
argue over Latin names. But my admiration is vanity, surely;
I recruit nature to support my own dislike of professors.
The mind is shameless in this way. It sees a
hawk and praises liberty, forgetting the mouse. It sees the
sea and praises infinity, though ten minutes later it complains
of wet shoes. We do not observe the world; we
appoint it to committees in our private parliament.

Suppose I stood up now and looked. The question would
end. A nail, a beetle, a stain—one word would pin
it flat. There is cruelty in exactness. The botanist bends
over a flower, names each part, presses the whole between
paper, and has gained knowledge, yes, but lost the trembling
yellow thing in the wind. Yet ignorance has its cruelties
too. A child burns with fever while someone calls illness
a visitation from Heaven. Facts cut, but fantasies can strangle.
How is one to choose? To know enough for mercy,
perhaps, and not enough for pride. That sounds admirable, which
is reason to distrust it. The most dangerous ideas arrive
wearing expressions of modest wisdom.

The fire clicked; a small tower of ash fell inward,
revealing an intense orange chamber. In that miniature cavern I
saw cities, for flames encourage empires. Crimson banners climbed the
coal; bronze gates opened; processions wound between black rocks while
citizens raised cups to a queen whose reign would last
precisely seven seconds. Then the roof collapsed and all became
grey. History may look like that to a longer-lived eye:
Troy, Carthage, London, little sparks naming themselves eternal. Shakespeare knew
it, probably. We make Shakespeare know everything because he cannot
contradict us. If I said Shakespeare understood hatpins, someone would
find a line. He has become a great cupboard into
which each generation puts its own possessions and afterwards discovers
them with reverence.

How enviable, nevertheless, to be made of words! The body
is a poor archive. Faces alter; voices vanish; even photographs
become strangers when no one remains to identify them. But
a sentence travels intact through rooms its writer never imagined.
It lies dormant, black marks on a pale page, until
an unknown hand opens the book, and then the dead
thought wakes in a living head—though altered, certainly, for no
thought crosses unaltered. Perhaps reading is less like receiving a
parcel than like striking a tuning fork: the page vibrates,
the mind answers, and the sound belongs wholly to neither.
Still, we put names beneath the vibrations. Ownership again. Mine,
yours, Shakespeare's. Even echoes must show papers at the border.

The mark above the mantel has no signature. It might
be a leaf blown in when the window was open,
though how could a leaf adhere there? A shred of
ivy, perhaps, brown and curled at the edges. At once
I see the garden after rain, the earth almost black,
the nasturtiums holding round drops of water, each drop containing
an inverted sky. There is more order in a leaf
than in all the Almanacks, yet it does not announce
its principles. Veins divide, cells thicken, green feeds upon light.
No committee directs the stem toward the sun. Or perhaps
committees do, minute green councils whispering in sap, and we
call the result instinct because we cannot translate their minutes.

Nature is the name we give to what has not
asked our permission. That may explain both our worship and
our rage. We praise wildflowers, then pull them up from
the lawn. We admire rivers, then force them between walls.
We celebrate birds and shoot them with admirable equipment. A
landscape is beautiful once fences establish ownership and a path
prevents mud from touching the shoe. Even the wilderness is
catalogued: here the scenic view, there the rare fern, beyond
it the licensed place for solitude. Yet somewhere under the
paving a root thickens, pushing upward without respect for municipal
plans. I take comfort in that root, though again I
am using it—making it the servant of my little rebellion.

There was a time, I daresay, when I should have
got up immediately. Youth wastes no uncertainty; it spends answers
like a rich inheritance. At twenty one knows whom to
love, what art is for, which wars are just, and
exactly what one will never become. By thirty the estate
has diminished. Questions appear in the accounts. By forty—well, perhaps
by forty one sits after tea and refuses to identify
a spot on the wall because mystery is among the
few possessions no one has yet taxed. But this is
another generalization, and generalizations are nets with holes arranged to
catch only the fish one already expects.

Memory is no better. It pretends to preserve, but it
paints over. I remember a summer field with purple clover,
a white dress, someone calling from the river. Yet was
the dress white? Was it summer? The river may have
come from another day entirely, flowing across years to improve
the composition. Memory is an interior decorator. It removes awkward
furniture, changes the curtains, places sunlight where there was rain.
Then we stand in the refurbished room and swear it
is original. Perhaps identity depends upon this fraud. If we
remembered everything accurately—the boredom between kisses, the selfishness mixed with
grief, the exact words of every quarrel—we should be crushed
beneath detail.

The mark does not remember me. That is restful. Objects
do not require consistency. The chair supports saint and liar
alike; the mirror reflects grief without sympathy and vanity without
contempt. How good to enter, for a moment, the republic
of things, where nothing must justify itself! The brass shovel,
the cold grate, the cup with its brown crescent of
tea, the thread caught on my sleeve—each rests completely in
its own existence. But do they? The cup is clay,
trade, wages, a kiln, a ship, hands packing straw around
a crate. Follow any object and the whole world comes
tumbling after it, people and machines and weather intertwined. Nothing
is alone; solitude is merely connection too fine for the
eye.

And yet the mark seems singular. It draws thought toward
it as a dark pool draws the sky. If I
looked too long, perhaps I should fall through—not physically, naturally,
though physical falling may be the simplest kind. There are
inward falls: a word repeats until it loses meaning; one's
own hand appears foreign; the familiar room peels back and
reveals that no habit proves reality. Why this wall? Why
this body? Why now? Such questions are trapdoors. Better to
fix the gaze upon something hard and small. The mark
is there. The mark is there. Whatever else slips, the
mark remains, a little knot tied in the loose fabric
of the afternoon.

Perhaps reality is only what resists us. The wall stops
the hand; winter enters the lungs; another person refuses the
role assigned in our imagination. We resent resistance, yet without
it we should drift like dreams without dreamers. Love itself
may be the discovery that another life is not a
room furnished for one's convenience. It contains locked drawers, ugly
ornaments, windows facing elsewhere. To love it is not to
possess the key but to hear movement beyond the door
and refrain, sometimes, from entering. That sounds too polished. Thought
grows vain as soon as it makes a sentence fit
for quotation. I can almost see it admiring itself in
the glass.

No, no, nothing has been settled. I have only circled
the room without leaving the chair. The mark may be
soot. Chimneys breathe black dust through cracks, and soot is
humble enough to undo metaphysics. A damp cloth, one wipe,
and all these empires, systems, selves, and rivers vanish from
their point of departure. But would they vanish? The journey
does not depend upon the stone from which one set
out. A traveller may discover that the famous monument was
merely a milestone and still have crossed mountains. Imagination is
extravagant but not therefore false; fact is exact but not
therefore sufficient. The two eye each other like cats on
a wall, neither willing to yield the sunlit place.

The door opened. Cold air entered first, followed by Henry
with the evening newspaper folded beneath his arm. He shook
rain from his shoulders and stood before the fire.

"Another offensive," he said. "More guns, more speeches, more assurances
that everything is proceeding splendidly. They've printed a map this
time, so I suppose we must feel informed."

I looked again at the wall. "Do you see that
mark above the mantelpiece?"

He leaned closer, scarcely pausing.

"That? It's a snail."

"A snail?"

"Yes. How on earth did it get indoors?"

He turned back to the newspaper, where armies advanced in
arrows across a square of paper. On the wall the
snail lifted its delicate horns and moved, with almost immeasurable
patience, toward the ceiling.

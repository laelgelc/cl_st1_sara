In the spring of 1892 I was resting at Mentone—or
pretending to rest, which is the form that idleness commonly
takes on the Riviera. There are places where a man
may be private, and places where he may be conspicuous;
Mentone permits him to be both at once. He may
sit under an orange tree, hidden from all mankind except
six English families, three invalid Russians, a German baron with
field-glasses, and the correspondent of a Paris newspaper.

The sea was blue, the hills were green, the hotels
were white, and everybody talked in subdued voices about everybody
else. It was tranquillity with a programme; repose accompanied by
pow-wow and fuss and feathers.

At breakfast one morning I noticed a man at a
table near the window. He was perhaps sixty-five, though his
stoop and his melancholy made him look older. His hair
was abundant and iron-gray; his eyes were large, dark and
absent; his hands, which lay beside his untouched coffee, were
fine hands—sensitive, nervous hands, made either for a violin, a
brush, or bankruptcy.

He sat alone. Several persons bowed to him, and he
answered courteously, but without warmth. Presently he took a crust
from his plate and scattered it upon the balcony for
the sparrows. The birds came quarrelling and carried it away
crumb by crumb. He watched them with a tenderness which
seemed out of proportion to the service.

"Who is that sad old gentleman?" I asked Smith.

Smith was an American of fifty-eight or thereabouts, well preserved,
well dressed, and well supplied with those comfortable millions which
have forgotten the hardships of their infancy. We had met
two days before and already knew enough of each other
to exchange confidences, provided they concerned somebody else.

"That?" he said. "That is Théophile Magnan."

"French?"

"Entirely."

"Rich?"

"Disgracefully."

"Distinguished?"

"More than he can admit."

This answer had a loose end to it, and I
took hold.

"What has he done?"

Smith stirred his coffee slowly.

"He has furnished the world with one of its best-known
names."

"Then why have I never heard his own?"

"Because it is not the name he furnished."

He glanced toward Magnan. The old man had left the
table and was passing through the room. As he went
by, Smith rose slightly and bowed. Magnan paused, laid a
hand for an instant on Smith's shoulder, and said:

"The birds here are well fed."

"Yes," said Smith, "but they have to come to the
hotel for it."

Magnan smiled—faintly, as if the machinery had not been used
lately—and moved on.

Smith watched him go.

"When we were young," he said, "he once told me
about a little brown bird that sang all summer in
a village garden. Nobody listened. They threw stones at it
because it woke the baker. In the autumn it died,
and a professor happened along, examined the body, and announced
that it belonged to a rare species. Then the village
erected a monument to it and held an annual festival
in honour of its song."

"A useful village."

"The ordinary kind. Magnan said the bird made only one
mistake."

"Dying?"

"No—dying before it had appointed agents."

Smith lit a cigar. He was silent so long that
I supposed the matter ended; then he said:

"If I tell you something, you must hold it in
confidence."

"For how long?"

"Until Magnan dies."

"Really dies?"

Smith examined me sharply, then laughed.

"You have found the hinge already. Yes—really dies. Listen. His
name is not Théophile Magnan."

"What is it?"

"François Millet."

I confess I stared at him. Millet had been dead
for years. His death, his funeral, his pictures, his fame—these
were not matters of opinion, but multitudinous and long-established facts.

"Not the Millet?"

"The only one the dealers recognise."

"But Millet is dead."

Smith leaned back.

"That is what made his fortune."

He ordered brandy, and when it came he began.

"There were four of us in those days—Millet, Claude Frère,
Carl Boulanger, and I. We were young painters in France,
and we possessed everything proper to young painters except money,
reputation, patrons, fuel, food, and the ability to satisfy a
landlord. Our genius was enormous. There was enough of it
in the room to illuminate Europe; but genius, you know,
gives a poor light when the candle has not been
paid for.

"We lived near Barbizon in a house which had been
condemned by the authorities and abandoned by the rats. There
were two beds among the four of us, one chair,
seven canvases, seventeen pipes and a skillet. The skillet was
our chief article of furniture because it looked useful, though
there was seldom anything to put in it.

"Credit had supported us for a year. Then credit became
exhausted—physically exhausted. The butcher said he could trust us no
longer because his wife had begun to suspect him of
philanthropy. The baker said art was immortal but flour was
cash. Our landlord took the door off its hinges and
said he would return it when the rent came. It
was January.

"One evening we held a council. On the table lay
our united capital—two francs, a copper button and a pawn-ticket
for Carl's boots.

"Claude said, 'We must sell a picture.'

"Millet said, 'Excellent. To whom?'

"Claude said, 'To a man with money.'

"'Name him.'

"Claude could not.

"I proposed work. There was a silence at that—an offended
silence. We were painters. Work of a miscellaneous or useful
character appeared to us degrading.

"Carl had been lying on the floor with his head
against the wall. Suddenly he sat up.

"'I have it,' he said.

"'Food?'

"'Better—an idea.'

"'You cannot fry it,' said Claude.

"Carl ignored him. He had bright eyes and no conscience
to speak of; or rather he had one, but it
was of an artistic temperament and never interfered with a
composition.

"'Boys,' he said, 'why do our pictures not sell?'

"'Because the public is composed of idiots,' I answered.

"'That is the foundation. Go on.'

"Millet said, 'Because nobody knows us.'

"'Better. And when does the public begin to know a
painter?'

"We considered. Claude said:

"'After he has succeeded.'

"'No. After he is dead.'

"That stopped us.

"Carl sprang up and began marching about the room, though
he had only one boot and the marching was not
impressive.

"'Look at the history of art,' he said. 'A living
painter asks five hundred francs; people laugh. He dies; they
fight for the same canvas at fifty thousand. Yesterday he
could not get credit for a pork chop; to-morrow kings
acquire his pencil scratches. While he lives, his poverty proves
he has no talent. When he dies, his poverty proves
he was a neglected master. Death is the critic who
never makes a mistake.'

"'What is your conclusion?' I asked.

"'One of us must die.'

"Millet looked up.

"'Carl, hunger has entered your brain.'

"'My brain is the only place it has not entered.
Hold still, now. I do not mean that one of
us must die inconveniently. I mean officially. The body may
continue its private arrangements. What we require is a deceased
painter.'

"Claude said, 'You propose murder with reservations.'

"'I propose commerce. One of us becomes the late illustrious
master. The others become his pupils, friends and authorised representatives.
We sell his work. We report his declining health. We
increase the prices. At the proper moment we announce the
final mournful event—and then, gentlemen, we collect.'

"Millet stared at him, grave and quiet. 'And the deceased?'

"'Retires under another name, with enough money to endure the
loss.'

"We laughed first. We were too hungry not to laugh.
Then Carl began giving figures, and laughter weakened.

"'A sketch brings five francs now,' he said. 'Sign it
with a famous name and it brings five hundred. Let
the famous hand be paralysed and it brings a thousand.
Put the famous hand in a coffin and the sketch
becomes priceless. These are not morals; they are market quotations.'

"Claude objected that no one would believe in the fame.

"Carl said, 'They will if we tell them other people
believe in it. That is how fame begins.'

"I said, 'And how are we to decide which one
dies?'

"'Draw lots.'

"That sobered us. It is agreeable to discuss death in
the abstract, but drawing it from a hat gives it
an unnecessarily personal complexion.

"Carl tore four strips from an unpaid bill. On one
he wrote MORIBUND. He folded them and dropped them into
the skillet.

"'I object to that word,' said Claude.

"'Phrase it to suit yourself.'

"'Write IMMORTAL.'

"Carl changed it. We drew.

"Millet opened his paper. 'Immortal,' he said.

"For half a minute nobody spoke. Then Carl embraced him.

"'François, your future is made!'

"'My past appears to be made also,' said Millet.

"We spent our last two francs on bread and wine
and began the project that night.

"Millet's task was production. He must paint finished pictures, unfinished
pictures, studies, sketches, fragments, notes—everything. A dead master cannot replenish
his stock. Therefore, while he remained alive and unfamous, he
had to manufacture a posthumous career sufficient for fifty years
of galleries and auctions.

"We divided the labour. Claude prepared canvases. I mixed colours.
Carl invented dates, titles and periods. Millet painted like a
man pursued by posterity.

"He painted peasants in fields, women at wells, shepherds, trees,
sunsets, dawns, old shoes, broken carts, barns, geese, cabbages. If
he wiped his brush on a rag, Carl seized the
rag and labelled it 'Colour experiment, early manner.' If he
drew a cow and disliked the horns, Claude cut off
the horns and preserved them as a separate study. We
saved everything. Millet could not sharpen a pencil without adding
to the estate.

"In three weeks the house was crowded. Pictures stood against
the walls, under the beds, behind the skillet. We slept
between masterpieces and ate over them.

"Then Carl said the evangelists must depart.

"He took twelve drawings; Claude and I took twelve each.
Millet remained at work. We were to travel in different
directions, describe ourselves as pupils of the great but little-known
François Millet, and refuse to sell cheaply.

"'Refuse?' I said. 'We are starving.'

"'That is why you must refuse. Need repels buyers; reluctance
attracts them. Ask eight hundred francs.'

"'For a drawing we could not sell last month for
eight?'

"'Exactly. The difference is information.'

"I went to Rouen. I called on a dealer and
showed him a sketch.

"'Whose?' he asked.

"I looked surprised at his ignorance. 'Millet's.'

"'Which Millet?'

"I looked sorrowful.

"'Then the news has not reached Rouen.'

"'What news?'

"I wrapped up the sketch.

"'No matter.'

"He stopped me. Dealers cannot endure news that has not
reached them.

"'Is he ill?'

"I lowered my voice. 'We hope.'

"'You hope he is ill?'

"'We hope against fear, monsieur. The lungs—the heart—overwork—privation. France may
soon realise what she has neglected.'

"That phrase did it. France enjoys realising what she has
neglected, provided another nation has realised it first.

"He asked the price.

"'Eight hundred francs.'

"He offered two hundred. I refused. He offered four. I
moved toward the door. At six hundred I trembled; at
seven I remembered Carl; at eight he bought it.

"I walked out with eight hundred francs in my pocket
and the gait of a man who has founded a
bank.

"Claude sold two drawings at Dijon for fifteen hundred. Carl,
the bright, conscienceless rascal, sold a little painting in Paris
for four thousand and persuaded the purchaser that he had
robbed him.

"We returned to Barbizon with food, wine, clothes, coal and
self-respect. Millet looked at the money for a long time.

"'So I am valuable?' he said.

"'Not yet,' Carl answered. 'You are merely ill.'

"Then the campaign began in earnest.

"We travelled through France. We talked of Millet in inns,
studios, railway carriages and drawing-rooms. We never praised him crudely;
that would have looked interested. We sighed. We spoke of
'the final manner,' 'the tragic serenity,' 'the failing hand.' We
hinted that Berlin had discovered him, then told Berlin that
London was buying him, informed London that Paris was alarmed,
and assured Paris that America had begun taking everything.

"Carl knew a journalist. A paragraph appeared:

"'Grave anxiety is felt concerning the health of François Millet,
the eminent painter of rural life, whose works have recently
aroused enthusiasm among the most discerning collectors of the Continent.'

"The paragraph was copied. Each newspaper enlarged it. In Lyons,
Millet became 'the celebrated master.' In Brussels, 'the foremost living
interpreter of the soil.' In Vienna, his condition was 'hourly
causing distress in artistic circles.' Artistic circles had not heard
of him the week before, but they became distressed with
admirable promptness.

"Prices rose. Eight hundred became three thousand; three became ten.
Dealers who had refused his work now sent letters begging
for it and apologising for delays caused by gout, bereavement
or travel. One man who had ordered us out of
his shop purchased the same picture from Claude at twenty
times the original price and declared he had recognised its
greatness instantly.

"Meanwhile the moribund was in magnificent health and painting sixteen
hours a day.

"We guarded him like a state secret. He could not
go to town, because his face might become known; he
could not receive visitors, because they might find him insufficiently
dying. We issued bulletins.

"'Master passed a restless night.'

"In fact, he had danced with the baker's daughter.

"'Master unable to touch nourishment.'

"He had eaten a chicken, a ham and half a
tart.

"'Master rallied briefly and asked for his brushes.'

"He had never put them down.

"After six months Carl called another council. This one was
held over champagne, and the door was back on its
hinges.

"'Gentlemen,' he said, 'the public is prepared. François must grow
worse.'

"Millet said, 'I am growing tired.'

"'That has no market value. You must grow worse.'

"The bulletins became alarming. We allowed a rumour of blindness,
corrected it, substituted paralysis, denied that, and admitted exhaustion. Uncertainty
is excellent publicity. A definite disease limits the imagination.

"Orders came from Madrid, Amsterdam, New York and St. Petersburg.
We sold slowly, always reluctantly. Carl would answer: 'The master
cannot be persuaded to part with the canvas.' The master
had finished it that morning and was beginning three more.

"At last there came a day when Carl spread the
accounts before us.

"'We are rich,' he said.

"We did not believe him, so he proved it. Then
we believed him extravagantly.

"'Now,' he continued, 'we require the supreme event.'

"Millet put down his glass. 'Must I?'

"'François, do not fail us at the funeral.'

"The arrangements were delicate. A doctor was found who understood
friendship and disliked questions. A wax figure was made. It
was an excellent likeness, though somewhat more peaceful than Millet
had ever looked. We placed it in a coffin and
surrounded it with flowers. The face was visible for two
days, but only through glass, and grief, candlelight and reputation
completed the resemblance.

"The announcement shook Europe. At least, the newspapers said it
did, and Europe had no opportunity to contradict them.

"Collectors rushed to buy. Prices doubled before the funeral and
doubled again during it. Men who had never seen Millet
wept publicly. Critics explained the greatness they had omitted to
mention while he was obscure. One journal printed a long
account of an interview with him which had never occurred.
Millet read it in the next room and said the
dead received more intelligent conversation than the living.

"The funeral was attended by officials, artists, dealers, reporters, creditors
who had forgiven him, and strangers who wished to be
observed forgiving him. There were wreaths from societies that had
been founded after his death to honour his memory. The
procession extended half a mile.

"Now comes the dangerous part."

Smith paused and relit his cigar, though it was not
out.

"Well?" I said.

"We needed four bearers for the coffin. Claude, Carl and
I were three."

"And the fourth?"

"A distant cousin from Normandy—a stooped, bearded fellow with green
spectacles. Nobody knew him. He wept continuously and carried the
rear corner on the left."

"You mean—"

"Millet."

I set down my glass.

"He carried himself to the grave?"

"Certainly. It was appropriate and saved hiring a man. At
one point Carl whispered, 'François, you are taking this very
well.'

"Millet whispered back, 'I have never had so many distinguished
people carry flowers behind me.'

"The coffin was lowered. Millet watched his wax substitute disappear
and afterwards said it produced a curious sensation—a mixture of
bereavement and professional satisfaction.

"That evening we divided the first great proceeds. Within a
year each of us possessed a fortune. The remaining works—hundreds
of them—were released cautiously. A sketch here, a canvas there,
an 'unknown study' discovered in a farmhouse, a 'last fragment'
found in an attic. The supply of last fragments was
astonishing. Carl managed the business until he retired. Claude bought
an estate. I went to America and committed several respectable
investments.

"And Millet?"

Smith looked toward the balcony, where the sad old man
stood among the sparrows.

"He became Théophile Magnan. At first he enjoyed freedom. He
travelled, read, collected pictures by other men and criticised them
without mercy. But there was a punishment hidden in the
success, though none of us had calculated it.

"He could never paint publicly again. Imagine possessing your hand,
your eye, your name—except that the name belongs to a
dead man, the hand must pretend to be idle, and
any new picture you make is either worthless as Magnan
or fraudulent as Millet. He had bought fame by surrendering
the right to enjoy it openly.

"That is why he is melancholy?"

"Partly. And partly because people praise Millet to him. They
explain Millet to him. They tell him what Millet intended,
what Millet suffered, why Millet used brown, what Millet would
have painted had he lived. He has listened to experts
correct his memories. That will age a man."

Magnan turned from the birds and looked in our direction.

"But he is enormously rich," I said.

Smith nodded.

"Yes. Society denied the living artist bread and gave the
dead one palaces. We merely arranged for the same man
to occupy both positions."

"And the secret has never escaped?"

"Never. The doctor is dead. Carl and Claude are dead.
I remain—and he remains."

Magnan came inside. Smith rose, and I rose with him.

"My friend," Smith said to the old man, "this gentleman
admires your—your knowledge of painting."

Magnan studied me with those deep, tired eyes.

"I know very little of painting," he said. "I know
something of prices."

Then he smiled, bowed, and went away.

At the door he paused to scatter one last handful
of crumbs upon the balcony. The sparrows descended noisily, each
eager to seize what it had ignored while it lay
in the common loaf.

Smith watched them.

"You see," he said, "the bird in the village was
wrong. It needed no monument."

"What did it need?"

"An agent—and a funeral."

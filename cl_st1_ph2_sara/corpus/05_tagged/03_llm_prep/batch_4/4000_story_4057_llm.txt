## Lights in the Abyss

There are seasons in the history of nations when injustice,
long endured, awakens a just indignation; but there are also
seasons when indignation, no longer governed by conscience, becomes vengeance,
and vengeance passes into a madness more cruel than the
tyranny it professes to destroy. Such was the condition of
Paris in the summer and autumn of 1792. Ancient institutions
had fallen; the throne trembled; law spoke with a feeble
voice, and the populace, persuaded that suspicion was guilt and
death the only safeguard of liberty, stood ready to complete
at one fell swoop the bloody work of revolution.

Think of the Swiss Guards, faithful at their posts while
all around them surged treachery, panic, and rage. On the
10 th of August they had defended the Tuileries, not
because they understood every quarrel that divided France, but because
they had sworn obedience, and a promise, to an honourable
man, remains sacred even when obedience may cost him his
life. They were overwhelmed. Their scarlet uniforms were seen amid
smoke, overturned furniture, broken gates, and the rushing multitude; and
many who survived the first assault were hunted through the
streets and murdered without mercy.

Yet even this slaughter did not satisfy the popular fury.
Paris had become an abyss of horror into which accusation,
innocence, age, learning, beauty, and piety were alike cast down.
The prisons were crowded with priests who had refused revolutionary
oaths, with noblemen and women, with servants of the royal
household, and with persons whose only offence was a name,
a friendship, or a word overheard and maliciously repeated. Rumours
were spread that these captives intended to escape, arm themselves,
and massacre the citizens while the armies of foreign kings
approached the capital. The story was absurd; but fear, when
inflamed by wicked men, does not pause to ask whether
its object be possible.

On Sunday, the 2 nd of September, the alarm-guns sounded,
and bands of armed ruffians gathered before the prisons. They
bore sabres, pikes, clubs, muskets, and the heavy tools of
common labour now degraded into instruments of murder. Some were
intoxicated with wine, others with applause, and more with that
fearful intoxication which comes when men cease to feel themselves
responsible because they act in a crowd. At the Abbaye,
the Carmes, La Force, and other places of confinement, rude
tribunals were formed. Prisoners were brought forth, questioned for a
few moments, and either ordered back—or thrust through a doorway
to the assassins waiting without.

It is easy, after many years, to read a list
of the dead. It is more profitable, though more painful,
to remember that every name belonged to a living soul,
and that, in the midst of the general delirium, certain
deeds of charity and devotion shone out like lamps carried
through a storm.

Among the prisoners at the Abbaye was the Abbé Roch-Ambroise
Sicard. He was no conspirator. His life had been devoted
to those unhappy children upon whom silence had seemed to
set its seal from birth. By patience, benevolence, and rare
intelligence, he had taught the deaf and dumb to communicate
with their fellow-creatures; he had built for them, as it
were, a bridge of signs across the dark gulf of
isolation. But learning and goodness were poor protections in those
dreadful days. He was a priest, and to the murderers
that was enough.

When the first uproar reached the prison, Sicard understood what
was intended. He heard the cries in the courtyard, the
tumult at the doors, the names called one after another,
and then the savage acclamations which announced each fresh death.
Escape was impossible. Resistance was equally so. He therefore prepared
himself, not by railing against his enemies, but by commending
his soul to God.

At length his name was called.

He passed into the court, where the murderers closed around
him. His dress proclaimed his office, and a pike was
already raised when a man sprang between the weapon and
its intended victim.

This man was Monnot, a watchmaker. He was not Sicard's
relative, nor had he been bound to him by years
of friendship. He knew him only by reputation; but he
knew what Sicard had done for the helpless, and in
that hour knowledge became duty.

"Stop!" he cried. "This is the Abbé Sicard—the father of
the deaf and dumb! He has spent his life in
doing good. You shall not kill him unless you kill
me first."

The murderer hesitated. Monnot did not move. He had no
armour, no authority, no force with which to compel that
frantic assembly; he possessed only truth and courage, yet these,
for one blessed moment, proved stronger than the pike.

Voices arose from the crowd.

"The father of the deaf and dumb?"

"Yes," answered Monnot; "the friend of those who cannot plead
for themselves. Will you murder such a man?"

The weapon was lowered. Some who had been clamouring for
death now cried for pardon with the same vehemence. Sicard
was drawn aside, protected, and ultimately restored to liberty.

We must not pass too hastily over this act. Monnot
could have remained silent. No one would have accused him;
perhaps no one would even have noticed him. But there
are moments when silence makes a man the accomplice of
wickedness. The watchmaker, little divining whether the mob would listen
to him or strike him down beside the priest, placed
his body before another man's body and his good name
against the fury of assassins. In an age boasting loudly
of fraternity, he practised it.

At the same prison was the aged Marquis de Sombreuil,
formerly governor of the Invalides. His daughter Marie, young, gentle,
and devoted, had refused to abandon him. When he was
summoned before the dreadful tribunal, she followed, clinging to him
as though filial love might form a shield that steel
could not pierce.

The old man was condemned almost before the questions had
ceased. The doors opened. Beyond them stood the executioners, their
arms red with the work already done.

Marie darted forward.

"My father is innocent!" she cried.

The ruffians bade her stand aside.

"He is my father," she answered. "If you must kill
him, kill me first."

There was nothing theatrical in that appeal, though the scene
has since been represented by painter and poet. The daughter
saw death fearfully close; she saw the weapons, the stains
upon the pavement, the distorted faces of men who had
forgotten mercy. Yet she did not shrink. Youth pleaded for
age; weakness confronted violence; and purity stood where hardened men
trembled to stand.

For an instant the mob wavered. Then one of the
assassins, whether in brutal sport or in a sudden desire
to test the courage he could not comprehend, held out
a glass containing a red liquid.

"Drink," he said, "and your father shall be saved."

It was afterwards reported that the cup contained blood drawn
from one of the victims. The tale passed rapidly through
France and gave Marie the sorrowful name by which she
was long remembered. Justice, however, requires us to add that
she herself denied having drunk blood; and it is probable
that the draught was wine, discoloured perhaps by the dreadful
disorder of the place. We need no hideous embellishment to
magnify her heroism. She believed herself surrounded by men capable
of any outrage, yet she accepted the cup.

She drank.

"Pardon for Sombreuil!" shouted some.

"Pardon! Let the daughter take him away!"

The cry spread. Those who a moment before had demanded
the father's death now applauded the child who had braved
them. Marie put her arm around the old man, whose
strength had nearly failed, and led him through the dreadful
court. One cannot imagine the feelings with which they crossed
that pavement, passed the weapons that were lowered for them,
and reached the outer air. Every step appeared a deliverance.
Every shout behind them might have recalled them to death.

Near them in captivity was another father, Jacques Cazotte, a
man of letters already advanced in years. His daughter Elizabeth
had also followed him to prison. Cazotte's bald forehead, white
hair, and feeble frame might have awakened pity in men
less frantic; but the September murderers regarded grey hairs not
as a claim to reverence, but as an easy mark
for the sword.

He was brought out and ordered to death.

Elizabeth placed herself before him.

"You cannot reach my father," she said, "without passing through
my heart."

The executioners commanded her to withdraw. She clasped the old
man more closely.

"Strike us together, then. I will not leave him."

The people had just witnessed Marie de Sombreuil's devotion, and
the image of that noble courage had not wholly faded
from minds so violently changeable. A murmur passed among them.
Some called Elizabeth a brave child; others declared that so
good a daughter must not be murdered. The cry of
pardon arose once more.

"Let the old man go!"

"He is saved for his daughter's sake!"

Thus Cazotte also passed alive through the gate of death.
He blessed Elizabeth, but his joy was solemn, for he
knew too well that they had escaped the murderers, not
the hatred which had delivered him into their hands. Still,
for that day, the father was restored to his child,
and the child received the earthly reward of her devotion.

Not every prayer prevailed.

Madame de Fausse Lendry had entered the prison in order
to attend an uncle who was dangerously ill. She had
watched beside him amid increasing confusion, hoping that sickness, at
least, would disarm cruelty. Surely, she thought, men who claimed
to punish enemies of the nation would not butcher one
already lying helpless upon his bed.

A sentry, whose heart had not been wholly hardened, warned
her of the approaching danger.

"Go, madame," he said. "The killers are coming. If you
remain, you will perish."

"And my uncle?"

The man looked away.

"They will spare no one."

She might have saved herself, yet affection held her beside
the sick man. By and by the doors were forced.
The murderers entered, shouting, searching every chamber, dragging the prisoners
from beds and corners. They found the invalid, too weak
even to rise before them.

Madame de Fausse Lendry threw herself between him and the
weapons.

"He is dying already!" she cried. "Have you no pity?"

They had none. Her entreaties were answered with threats, and
the sick man was slain before her eyes. She survived,
but survival itself may become a heavy cross when it
preserves, not hope, but the remembrance of a beloved face
seen for the last time amid violence.

More celebrated, and of surpassing horror, was the fate of
the Princess de Lamballe. She had been the faithful friend
of Queen Marie Antoinette. Neither danger nor popular insult could
induce her to purchase safety by denying that attachment. She
was confined at La Force, where the same mockery of
judgment awaited her.

When brought before the tribunal, exhausted by confinement and terror,
she was questioned concerning her name and rank.

"Swear," said her judges, "to love liberty and equality, and
to hate the king and queen."

She answered that she could swear to the first, but
not to the last.

"Swear! If not, you are dead."

She paused. To speak the demanded words might perhaps have
saved her life. Yet friendship, once cherished in prosperity, appeared
to her still more sacred in ruin. She would not
proclaim hatred against those whom she loved.

"I cannot," she said.

The judges made the fatal sign. She was led toward
the door. Some accounts relate that a compassionate person whispered
to her to cry, "Long live the nation!" and thus
perhaps gain a moment's mercy; but she was bewildered, and
the mob outside was already absolutely frantic. She had scarcely
crossed the threshold when she was struck down.

We will not dwell upon the indignities that followed. There
are crimes which ought to be recorded, lest mankind forget
to what depths unrestrained passion can descend; yet the imagination
need not be forced to linger over every circumstance. Let
us remember rather the princess's constancy: the fair head bowed
beneath violence, the gentle friend refusing to make falsehood the
ransom of life, and the soul passing beyond the reach
of those who could insult the body but could not
conquer fidelity.

For several days Paris remained in the power of the
assassins. The ordinary business of life continued strangely around the
massacres. Shops opened; citizens crossed bridges; bells sounded; the September
sun shone upon roofs and gardens; and within prison walls
men and women waited to hear whether the next cry
would be their own name. Such contrasts are among the
most fearful features of public calamity. A city does not
cease to breathe because hundreds are murdered. Bread is bought,
children play, and bystanders turn away, until wickedness, seeing itself
unopposed, grows bolder.

At last the bloody work ended. The courts were washed,
the weapons laid aside, and many who had taken part
returned to their homes. But no cleansing could remove the
guilt, and no silence restore the dead. France had been
told that these murders were necessary for liberty. In truth,
liberty gains nothing from injustice. A cause which must be
defended by slaughtering helpless prisoners has already condemned itself before
God and honour.

The rescued, meanwhile, had little assurance of safety. Jacques Cazotte's
reprieve proved only a delay. He was rearrested and brought
before a more regular tribunal, though the forms of law
could not conceal the vengeance beneath them. Elizabeth could no
longer snatch him from his judges by placing her young
body before his. He was condemned to the guillotine.

When the hour came, the old man met death with
composure. He had known the tenderness of a daughter who
was willing to die for him, and he carried that
blessing to the scaffold. He professed his fidelity to God
and to the king, commended those he loved to divine
mercy, and passed from the injustice of men to the
judgment where no clamour can turn innocence into guilt.

Marie de Sombreuil was reserved for sorrows scarcely less severe.
The father whom she had led from the Abbaye was
afterwards accused again. Her brother shared his danger. Before a
tribunal that possessed the appearance rather than the spirit of
justice, both were condemned, and both were executed. The daughter
who had once conquered a mob could not conquer the
deliberate machinery of the Terror. She was left alone.

Was her courage, then, useless? Was Elizabeth's devotion wasted because
Cazotte ultimately died? Such is the false reckoning of a
world which measures good only by immediate success. Marie gave
her father days, perhaps months, of life; she gave him
also the knowledge that he was loved with a love
stronger than death. Elizabeth did the same. And even had
both fathers perished in the courtyards, the devotion of their
children would not have been less noble. Virtue does not
cease to be virtue because violence appears for a time
to triumph over it.

The September Massacres remain among the darkest pages of the
Revolution. Yet as we look into that abyss of horror,
we ought not to see only pikes, drunken faces, hurried
judgments, and fallen victims. We should see Monnot lifting his
unarmed hands before Sicard; Marie de Sombreuil holding the cup
while her father waits between life and death; Elizabeth Cazotte
covering grey hairs with her own breast; the faithful woman
beside her sick uncle; and the Princess de Lamballe refusing
to stain friendship with a lie.

The multitude was mighty, but its power lasted only an
hour. The courage of the merciful has endured.

The murderers believed that they were making history with iron
and blood. In truth, they are remembered chiefly because the
virtues of their victims shine against their darkness. Charity, filial
devotion, constancy, and faith cannot always turn aside the weapon;
but they can rob cruelty of its final victory. They
pass from generation to generation, teaching that, even when a
nation is delirious and law lies prostrate, the human soul
may remain free.

And for those whose earthly deliverance was brief, there remained
that better hope which neither tribunal nor scaffold could destroy.
Suffering, borne without falsehood or hatred, became not defeat but
testimony; and death, which the assassins intended as extinction, carried
the faithful from the clamour of Paris toward peace, and
from the light affliction of an hour to a far
more exceeding and eternal weight of glory.

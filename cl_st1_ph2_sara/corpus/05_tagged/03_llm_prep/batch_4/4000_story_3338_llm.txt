### THE WEEK-DAY LEDGER AND THE SUNDAY ACCOUNT

Rev. Andrew Adkin—for so the good people of Mayberry commonly
styled him, although his reverend office rested upon no more
substantial foundation than an occasional invitation to "hold forth" when
the regular minister was absent—kept a store at the corner
of Market Street and the Mill Road. In the former
calling he was constant, vigilant, and, as he believed, remarkably
competent; in the latter he was intermittent, unprepared, and not
unfrequently troubled by an inward suspicion that he did more
harm than good.

Brother Adkin was not a hypocrite, if by that term
we mean one who knowingly assumes a virtue which he
neither possesses nor desires. He wished to be useful; he
liked to think himself useful; and whenever the brethren praised
his "free utterance," or an elderly sister told him that
a certain observation had been "very strengthening," he experienced a
warmth which he readily accepted as evidence of spiritual approval.
Yet his mind had been trained, through six days of
every seven, to run in the narrow channels of buying
and selling; and it is not in the nature of
mind, any more than of water, to forsake at a
moment's notice the course which long habit has worn for
it.

On Monday morning, Brother Adkin purchased corn from Jacob Willis,
a plain farmer who lived four miles beyond Mayberry. The
market had risen unexpectedly during the night. Adkin knew this,
for a letter from the city lay open beside his
ledger; Willis did not know it, for he had left
home before sunrise.

"What's corn bringing?" asked the farmer, resting both hands upon
the counter.

"Well," replied Adkin, drawing the word out while he looked
toward the open letter, "prices vary."

"D'ye give eighty?"

"I can give seventy-five."

"Only seventy-five? Tom Barker said on Saturday it was likely
to rise."

"Tom Barker says many things." Adkin smiled in that mild,
reproving way by which a tradesman sometimes seeks to make
another man ashamed of distrusting him. "If you wish to
leave it, Jacob, I'll take it at seventy-five."

"Is that the market rate?"

Now here was a question which demanded either a truthful
answer or a dishonest one. Brother Adkin chose what seemed
to him a middle path, though we have never observed
that truth and falsehood are connected by any safe and
lawful highway.

"It is my rate," he said.

Willis hesitated. He needed money for a payment due that
afternoon, and Adkin knew it. After a little more talk
the bargain was made, and the farmer drove away with
seventy-five cents a bushel for corn which the storekeeper sold
before evening at a handsome advance.

The profit was entered with satisfaction. Yet, when Adkin shut
the ledger, the word "market" seemed to remain visible after
the page had closed.

"I told him no untruth," he reflected.

This was certain; but neither had he told him the
truth which friendship, fairness, and Christian love required. There are
men who imagine that the moral law, like a badly
framed contract, may be evaded through a defect in its
wording. Brother Adkin was not so hardened as these. His
conscience made an entry against him, though he declined, for
the present, to examine the account.

On Tuesday, Peter Giles passed the store. Giles owed Adkin
eleven dollars and forty cents, and had owed it for
three months. The debtor was careless, vain, and sufficiently provoking
to supply any creditor with an abundance of reasons for
anger. Adkin called him in.

"Peter, when are you going to settle that bill?"

"When I've got the money."

"You promised it last Saturday."

"I expected to have it."

"And now?"

"Now I haven't."

Adkin compressed his lips. "You found money enough for the
horse-race."

Giles's face reddened. "And what if I did? A'n't a
man to spend a quarter without asking Brother Adkin whether
it'll help him into heaven?"

The title, spoken with an insulting emphasis, struck Adkin more
sharply than the debt.

"I require no man to help me into heaven," he
answered. "But I require you to pay what you owe."

"Then require away."

"I shall put the account into the magistrate's hands."

"You're mighty fond of preaching mercy on Sunday," said Giles,
with a curl of the lip. "Week-days, I reckon, it's
cash down."

There are reproofs which benefit us because they are justly
and kindly administered; and there are others which, being mingled
with insolence, may contain truth without finding any welcome for
it. Brother Adkin heard only the insolence.

"Leave my store," he said.

"I'm going."

"And you will receive a summons."

"Send two, if it'll ease your religion."

Before noon the account was placed in the hands of
a constable. We do not say that Adkin had no
right to recover his money. Justice is not suspended because
a creditor prays in public; nor does charity demand that
idleness shall live indefinitely upon industry. But the motive which
carries a just act into execution may itself be unjust.
Brother Adkin did not summon Giles because sober judgment had
exhausted gentler methods. He summoned him because his pride had
been stung.

That evening, while attempting to read a chapter concerning the
forgiveness of injuries, he found the language less agreeable than
usual. His thoughts returned to Giles; from Giles they passed
to the cost of the summons; from the summons to
the probability of payment; and from payment, by an easy
transition, to the balance of his outstanding accounts.

On Wednesday nothing occurred beyond the common vexations of business,
which are small enough when met singly, but which, gathering
like dust upon a neglected garment, may seriously affect the
appearance of a Christian temper. On Thursday came Mrs. Smith.

Mrs. Smith was a widow with a sharp eye, a
quick tongue, and a memory peculiarly tenacious of every item
charged against her, though somewhat less exact concerning the articles
she had received. She entered with her bill in one
hand and her spectacles in the other.

"Brother Adkin," she began, "there's a mistake here."

"Indeed?" He assumed an expression of well-affected surprise.

"You've charged me twice for a pound of tea."

"I think not."

"Here it is on the third, and here again on
the ninth."

"You had tea on the ninth."

"I never had it!"

"My clerk says you did."

"Your clerk says what isn't true."

Adkin's religious dignity and commercial authority were touched at the
same instant—a conjunction rarely favourable to patience.

"Mrs. Smith, I cannot permit such language in my store."

"And I cannot permit such charges in my bill."

"The account is correct."

"It a'n't."

"I say it is."

"And I say I paid for the tea on the
third, and never had another grain that month."

The dispute continued, repetition supplying the place of evidence, until
Mrs. Smith's cheeks were flushed and Brother Adkin's hands trembled
as he rearranged articles which required no rearrangement.

"You may take your custom elsewhere," he said at last.

"I mean to. And if you preach next Sunday, you
may preach about false weights and measures."

She departed with her head perked in offended resolution, leaving
Adkin to discover that a pound of tea may weigh
heavily upon the mind even when its existence is disputed.

Late that afternoon his clerk found the missing explanation. The
second pound had been purchased by another woman named Smith
and entered upon the widow's account.

"You had better call and tell her," said the clerk.

"I will correct the bill."

"But she'll want to know."

"She will see the correction when the account is sent."

He knew that he ought to go. He even took
his hat; but the recollection of Mrs. Smith's tone came
upon him, and he hung the hat again upon its
peg. Pride, having first prevented him from listening, now prevented
him from apologising.

Thus the week proceeded. Brother Adkin was not at ease.
He prayed, but his petitions had no liberty; he read,
but the words of the sacred page seemed continually to
turn their edges toward him. Still he took no decisive
step toward restitution or reconciliation. He intended to pay Willis
something more at the next opportunity; he hoped Giles would
settle before the case was heard; and he meant, undoubtedly,
to acknowledge the error to Mrs. Smith when she had
learned to speak with becoming moderation. These future virtues cost
him little and therefore afforded him much satisfaction.

At half past ten on Saturday night, when the shutters
were fastened and Adkin was adding a column of figures,
there came a knock at the side door. It was
Deacon Price.

"Brother Adkin," said the deacon, "we've had word that Mr.
Elwood is sick and cannot preach to-morrow. The brethren hope
you'll take his place."

The request fell upon Adkin like a demand for payment
upon an empty treasury.

"To-morrow?"

"Yes. Morning service only, unless you feel led to conduct
the evening also."

Adkin did not feel led toward either; but he had
often spoken of the duty of being ready in season
and out of season. To refuse might suggest that his
gifts were unequal to the occasion. Moreover, the deacon looked
expectant.

"I suppose," he said slowly, "if the call is clear,
I must not shrink."

"We knew we could depend upon you."

When the visitor had gone, Adkin stood for some minutes
with his hand upon the ledger. He was tired; his
head ached; and his mind, instead of opening toward divine
truth, persisted in closing upon the affairs of the week.

He turned to the Scriptures. After much hurried searching he
chose the text, "Love is the fulfilling of the law."
It was a noble text, comprehensive, practical, and particularly perilous
to a man who had spent six days in violating
its spirit.

He wrote three headings: Love toward God; Love toward Man;
Practical Observance Thereof. Beneath the first he could think of
little. Beneath the second arose the face of Jacob Willis
asking, "Is that the market rate?" Beneath the third appeared
Giles's sarcastic mouth and Mrs. Smith's indignant spectacles.

At midnight he had covered two pages, although quantity and
preparation are not always the same thing. A row of
words may conceal poverty of thought as readily as a
row of buildings may conceal an empty field. He retired,
hoping that sleep would bring the influx of intelligence denied
to wakefulness.

It brought dreams of ledgers, sermons, summonses, corn, tea, and
a congregation which insisted upon paying its pew-rent in counterfeit
money.

The Sabbath morning was clear. The bell sounded with unusual
solemnity to Brother Adkin as he walked toward the church,
carrying his notes in a Bible. He endeavoured to compose
his thoughts; but at one moment he wondered whether the
clerk had locked the rear window, and at another calculated
the profit upon the Willis corn. He rebuked these intrusions,
only to find that a thought does not depart merely
because its owner orders it out.

The congregation assembled. Adkin ascended the pulpit with a pale
face and an elaborate composure. He read the hymn, joined
in prayer, and announced his text.

"Love," he began, "is the great principle—the governing principle—without which
all other principles, however principled in their nature—"

He paused, aware that this opening had not advanced him
far.

At that instant he saw Jacob Willis in the third
pew. The farmer's face bore no accusation; indeed, it was
grave and attentive. But Adkin heard again, as distinctly as
on Monday, "Is that the market rate?"

He looked down at his notes.

"Love," he continued, "requires openness between man and man; it
does not seek an undue advantage through another's—through his lack
of—through circumstances not generally understood."

Willis lifted his eyes. Adkin's voice faltered.

Seeking relief on the opposite side of the church, he
encountered Peter Giles, who sat with folded arms and a
half-smile upon his lips. "Week-days, I reckon, it's cash down,"
sounded in the preacher's memory.

"Yet love," Adkin proceeded, "does not abolish law. The magistrate
is appointed for—for evil-doers, and lawful debts are—That is not
precisely our subject."

A child coughed. An old gentleman adjusted his ear-trumpet. The
silence of the remaining hearers became painfully complete.

Brother Adkin passed to his third division, hoping that practical
observance would prove more manageable than doctrine. Then Mrs. Smith
came into view. Her brows were knit, her lips compressed,
and her head held in precisely the position which had
concluded their controversy.

"Practical religion," he said, "extends to the smallest transactions of
life—even to articles of inconsiderable value—even, we might say, to
a pound of—"

He stopped.

Mrs. Smith looked steadily at him.

"To a pound of duty," he added, with desperation, "if
such an expression may be permitted."

It was not an expression which brought light to the
congregation. From that point the sermon wandered without map or
destination. Texts were quoted but not explained; exhortations were commenced
and abandoned; love was described as a law, the law
as a rule, the rule as a guide, and the
guide as something which every hearer must guide himself by.
Clearness of discrimination, grasp of thought, orderly arrangement, spiritual warmth—these
were all absent. The hungry sheep looked up, and our
lay preacher offered them the chaff of phrases.

At last he closed. The final hymn was sung, and
the benediction pronounced. Adkin descended, conscious of dampness upon his
forehead and weakness in his knees. A few persons shook
his hand, as custom required. Their remarks were mercifully general.

"An important subject, Brother Adkin."

"Yes," he replied; "very comprehensive."

"It gives one much to think about."

"Very much indeed."

As he reached the vestibule, a folded paper was placed
in his hand by someone whom he did not see.
Supposing it to contain a request for prayer, he opened
it on his way home. It bore only one sentence:

"The hungry sheep look up, and are not fed."

He stopped beneath an elm and read it twice.

Had the note abused him, he might have grown angry;
had it mocked him, pride might have furnished a defence.
But its simple truth entered where harsher language would have
been resisted. He saw his sermon as the congregation had
heard it—words without nourishment, profession without power. He saw, also,
that the failure had not begun on Saturday night. It
had begun at the counter on Monday, at the encounter
with Giles on Tuesday, in the quarrel with Mrs. Smith
on Thursday; perhaps it had begun much earlier, in the
easy assumption that a man immersed in profit and loss
might enter the pulpit at an hour's warning and speak
with authority concerning eternal things.

That afternoon he went first to Jacob Willis and paid
him the difference upon the corn. He next called upon
Mrs. Smith, acknowledged the error without qualification, and asked pardon
for his manner. On Monday he withdrew the summons against
Giles—not because the debt had ceased to be due, but
because anger had begun the proceeding. These acts did not
transform him instantly into a saint; they merely proved that
his conscience, though long kept waiting, had at last been
admitted to the counting-room.

Whether Brother Adkin ever preached again we cannot certainly say.
We may hope that, if he did, he prepared by
more than selecting a text, and that his preparation included
the ordering of his daily conduct. For no "call to
preach" releases a man from the call to be just;
nor can Sabbath eloquence compensate for week-day selfishness.

Knowledge, meditation, disciplined attention, and a life not openly at
variance with the doctrine uttered—these are not needless ornaments of
spiritual teaching. If the mind has flowed all week toward
gain, resentment, and petty triumph, it cannot, at the ringing
of a bell, become a clear river of heavenly wisdom.
The pulpit magnifies inconsistency; it does not conceal it.

And now, having laid Brother Adkin's case before those whom
it may concern, we leave each reader to examine his
own ledger. There are accounts which no merchant enters, debts
for which no magistrate issues a summons, and balances which
must nevertheless be settled before a man can offer the
bread of life with clean hands.

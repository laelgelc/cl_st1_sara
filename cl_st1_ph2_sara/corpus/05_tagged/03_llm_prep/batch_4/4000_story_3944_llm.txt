## The Crown of Briars

It was in the last violet hour of evening, when
the peacocks had folded their painted fans and the fountains
in the lower gardens whispered more softly among the myrtles,
that Prince Lucian looked for the first time upon the
ornaments of his kingship.

On the morrow he was to be crowned.

He had but sixteen years, and until six months before
had known no roof more splendid than the mossy thatch
of a goatherd's cottage, no music sweeter than the bells
of the flock upon the hills, and no courtlier companions
than the brown bees that drowsed in the thyme. Yet
now he stood in the Hall of the Golden Astrolabe,
surrounded by chamberlains, pages, jewellers, embroiderers, heralds, masters of ceremony,
and certain ancient nobles whose pedigrees were so long that
they had forgotten the deeds by which their houses had
first acquired distinction.

That he should have been found at all was a
matter concerning which many tales were told.

Some said that the late King's only daughter, the Princess
Isabeau, had secretly loved a musician from the South, whose
voice could make tame doves descend from towers and whose
dark beauty was like that of Adonis as he came
bleeding through the anemones. Others maintained that the stranger had
been no musician, but a prince of Tartary travelling in
disguise; while a learned antiquary, who had written nineteen volumes
upon the royal genealogy and had never once troubled himself
with truth, proved beyond contradiction that the child's father was
descended from Osiris.

Yet the common tale ran otherwise.

It was said that, sixteen years before, on a night
of rain and lamentation, a newborn boy had been carried
beyond the city walls by two masked servants and left
at the door of a poor goatherd. Around his neck
hung a slender chain of gold, and upon the clasp
was engraved the device of the royal house: a falcon
holding a star. Before dawn the servants were discovered drowned
in the black canal beneath the northern gate, and three
days later the young Princess died in the palace. Of
grief, said the priests. Of poison, said the people.

The goatherd, who had buried two sons and had much
room in his sorrow, took the child in. He named
him Lucian because the sun broke suddenly over the hills
when the infant first opened his eyes.

And there the boy grew, among rocks and hawthorns, drinking
goat's milk from an earthen cup, sleeping upon fern, and
learning the speech of birds before he learned the speech
of men. He could tell by the colour of the
west whether rain would come before midnight; he knew where
the wild bees hid their honey; and he had once
followed a white stag so far into the cedar forest
that he returned after three days with narcissus blossoms in
his hair and could give no account of where he
had wandered.

When the old King lay dying without an acknowledged heir,
the golden clasp was remembered. Messengers rode into every province,
and at length the boy was found piping beneath an
almond tree while his goats cropped the bitter grass.

He was brought to the palace, questioned by Councillors, examined
by physicians, measured by heralds, and instructed by masters. The
clasp was genuine; his face bore the sorrowful beauty of
the dead Princess; and beneath his left shoulder was the
small crescent-shaped mark said to belong to her line.

Thus the goatherd's child became Prince Lucian and heir to
the kingdom.

At first he pined for the hills and would slip
barefoot from his apartments to sleep in the garden under
the cypresses. He spoke to ambassadors with an honesty that
greatly alarmed them, laughed during solemn audiences, and once gave
his jewelled belt to a kitchen-boy because the boy admired
its enamelled buckle. Natural manners are charming in shepherds and
intolerable in princes, and the Lord Chamberlain took much trouble
to cure him of them.

But if Lucian disliked ceremony, he loved beautiful things with
a fierce and innocent love.

The palace was to him an enchanted treasury. He wandered
through galleries where pale saints dreamed upon backgrounds of beaten
gold; through rooms hung with tapestries from Arras, in which
crowned hunters pursued silver beasts beneath green moons; through cabinets
of amber, ivory, rock-crystal, lapis-lazuli, and porphyry. He would lie
for hours before a Persian carpet, tracing with his finger
its labyrinth of scarlet tulips and blue-winged birds, or watch
the court goldsmith set a green emerald in the breast
of a silver peacock.

He loved old Greek coins where forgotten youths stood naked
beside dolphins. He loved cups of agate, reliquaries of chased
gold, Venetian glass as thin as the shell of a
dragonfly, and painted boxes from Samarcand that smelled of sandalwood
and musk. At night he often commanded the pages to
carry lamps before him while he visited the statues in
the deserted halls, and once they found him at dawn
with his arms about the cold neck of a marble
Endymion.

There were some who blamed him for this passion, saying
that a king should delight in fortresses, laws, taxes, and
the breaking of rebellious cities. But the Treasurer, who sold
offices and accepted gifts from three foreign powers, defended the
Prince's innocence.

"Let the boy love statues," he said. "They ask for
no salaries."

And now, on the eve of his coronation, the three
great ornaments of sovereignty had been laid before him.

The robe hung from an ivory stand beneath a canopy
of blue silk. It was woven of threads so fine
that they seemed not spun but breathed, and over its
golden surface were embroidered stars, pomegranates, and the wings of
seraphim. Along the hem ran a procession of lions with
ruby eyes, while the collar was stiff with seed-pearls and
topazes pale as winter wine.

The sceptre rested upon black velvet. It was of white
gold, carved with leaves and small crowned faces, and at
its summit shone a pearl larger than a pigeon's egg,
flawless and softly luminous, as though the moon had shed
a single tear and some magician had imprisoned it there.

Last came the crown.

Twelve high leaves of gold rose from its circle, and
between them burned twelve rubies. They were not of the
dark colour of garnets, nor of the thin brightness of
coral, but red as the heart of flame. The greatest
of them, set above the brow, glowed with so deep
a splendour that the faces of those who bent near
it seemed touched with blood.

When Lucian saw these things he gave a cry of
joy.

"How beautiful they are!" he said.

The masters who had made them bowed till their beards
brushed their knees, each secretly desiring that the others should
be thought inferior. The Lord Chamberlain smiled, for pleasure becomes
a prince when it has been arranged by the proper
officials.

Lucian put out his hand and touched the robe.

"It is light as sunlight," he murmured.

"It is woven with gold," said the Keeper of the
Wardrobe.

He lifted the sceptre.

"This pearl is like a star seen through mist."

"It is the finest pearl in the world," answered the
Royal Jeweller. "Ten ships sought its equal, and none was
found."

Then Lucian raised the crown. Its rubies flashed against his
brown curls, and the courtiers applauded softly, according to custom.

Before him stood a mirror framed in golden branches. Looking
into it, he saw not the sunburnt goatherd's boy who
had run laughing over the hills, but a young king
out of some ancient romance, delicate and splendid, terrible and
fair. His dark eyes shone with delight. He set the
crown upon his head and drew the robe about his
shoulders, while a page placed the sceptre in his hand.

"The kingdom will kneel to thee," said the Lord Chamberlain.

But Lucian scarcely heard him. He watched the rubies burn
in the mirror and thought that Beauty herself had chosen
him for her priest.

At length the moon rose, round and yellow, behind the
terraces, and the palace clocks struck midnight with silver tongues.
The courtiers withdrew. The regalia were placed in the chamber
adjoining his own, guarded by six soldiers, and Lucian went
to his bed.

His room had walls of green marble veined like leaves,
and above his pillow hung a picture of the Child
Christ bearing a little crown. Perfumed oil burned in an
alabaster lamp. Beyond the open windows the city lay in
darkness, save where torches moved along the streets in preparation
for the morrow.

Lucian could not sleep. In his fancy he heard the
cathedral bells, the shout of the people, the long cry
of trumpets. He saw himself beneath the great dome, clothed
in gold and crowned with flame.

Then the wind stirred the curtains. The lamp trembled. From
the garden came the scent of roses, heavy and sweet
as wine.

And the young Prince fell asleep and dreamed.

He seemed to be standing in a long, low chamber
where the air was thick with dust and the windows
were barred. Hundreds of men, women, and children sat at
looms. They were gaunt, and their faces had the grey
colour of ashes. Their naked feet bled upon the stones,
and on their shoulders Hunger had laid her yellow hand.

The looms clattered without rest.

Through the room walked a man dressed in crimson velvet.
His fingers were covered with rings, and at his belt
hung a little whip of knotted cords. Behind him went
Avarice, lean and smiling, carrying an account-book under one arm.

"Faster!" cried the man in crimson. "The King is crowned
at noon, and the robe is not finished."

A woman bent over her loom. Beside her, a child
scarcely seven years old twisted threads of gold. His lips
were blue, and whenever his head drooped the man struck
him.

Lucian went to the woman.

"Why art thou so pale?" he asked.

She looked up, and her eyes were hollow.

"Because I hunger."

"Doth no one give thee bread?"

She laughed, but her laughter was without mirth.

"We sow the corn, and another eats it. We press
the grapes, and another drinks the wine. We build houses,
and our children sleep beneath bridges. We weave robes of
gold, and our own bodies are clothed in rags."

"Yet thy master is richly dressed," said Lucian.

"He is our brother," answered the woman, "but his name
is Cain."

The man in crimson heard her and struck her across
the mouth.

"Do not speak," he said. "Speech wastes time."

Lucian's heart grew hot with anger.

"Why dost thou permit this cruelty?" he cried. "Art thou
not a man as they are?"

The master turned and stared at him.

"A man? Certainly. But men are of two kinds: those
who possess, and those who labour for them. If I
paid these creatures enough to live, my neighbour would sell
more cheaply and I should be ruined. If my neighbour
paid them enough to live, another would undersell him. Misery
is the thread from which profit is woven."

"Then break the looms," said Lucian.

"And go naked to thy coronation?" asked Avarice.

At these words the workers turned their faces toward him.
Their eyes were full of accusation.

"What are they weaving?" Lucian whispered.

The child lifted a strip of golden cloth. Upon it
was embroidered a winged lion with ruby eyes.

"It is the coronation robe of the young King," he
said.

Then the whip descended upon his back. The child fell
against the loom, and his blood stained the gold.

Lucian gave a great cry and awoke.

The alabaster lamp was still burning, but the flame had
become small and blue. The curtains swayed like pale ghosts.
For a moment he did not know where he was.

Then he rose and went into the adjoining chamber.

The soldiers stood motionless by the door. The golden robe
hung beneath its canopy, splendid and silent. Lucian touched its
hem, and it seemed to him that the embroidered lions
blinked their red eyes.

"There is Hunger in every thread," he said.

But the soldiers did not hear him, and he returned
to his bed.

He told himself that it had been only a dream,
born from the wine of the feast and the heat
of the room. Dreams, he had been taught, were vapours
of the body unless interpreted by a licensed astrologer, and
the Royal Astrologer was asleep in the western tower.

So Lucian closed his eyes.

And he fell asleep again, and dreamed, and this was
his dream.

He stood upon the deck of a black ship under
a sky of copper. No land could be seen. The
sea rolled thickly, without foam, and a great red sun
hung close to the horizon.

Around the mast crouched a company of slaves. Iron collars
clasped their necks, and chains linked their wrists. Their skins
were dark from the sun and striped with old wounds.
At the prow sat their master beneath a canopy, drinking
sherbet from a cup of green jade. He was enormously
fat, and his eyes were small and bright as two
beads of jet.

A young slave, slender and beautiful, stood at the ship's
edge. A stone had been tied to his ankles, and
a basket hung from his waist.

The master raised one finger.

The slave leaped.

The sea opened and swallowed him.

For a long while nothing happened. Then the rope fastened
about the slave's body was pulled, and he came up
gasping, blood flowing from his nose and ears. In his
basket lay a few pearl oysters.

They were opened with knives. The pearls within were small
and misshapen.

The master frowned.

"Again."

The stone was tied once more, and the slave descended.

All day the red sun watched him rise and sink.
Each time he returned weaker. At noon Fever came over
the side, wearing a necklace of bones, and kissed him
upon the forehead. At evening Ague wrapped him in a
wet cloak.

Still the master said, "Again."

Lucian turned to one of the chained men.

"Why doth no one save him?"

The man lifted his wrists.

"The chain is a strong argument."

"Why must he dive?"

"For a pearl."

"Is a pearl worth so much pain?"

"To him who wears it, yes. To him who finds
it, no."

Once more the rope tightened. The slave was dragged from
the water, and this time there was a strange light
in his face. His hands were closed. The master sprang
up and tore them open.

In the right hand lay a pearl of marvellous size
and purity. It shone against the wounded palm like the
full moon above a field of battle.

The master cried aloud in triumph.

But the slave did not breathe.

They cut away the basket, unbound the stone, and flung
the body into the sea. It sank slowly through the
dark water, and white fish gathered around it.

The master polished the pearl upon his sleeve.

"For whom is it?" Lucian asked.

The fat man smiled.

"For the sceptre of the young King. No other pearl
in all the oceans was worthy of him."

Then Death rose from the sea. Her hair was green
with weed, and little crabs moved in the hollows of
her eyes.

"I have taken the diver," she said. "Who shall pay
me for the pearl?"

And the master answered, "Ask the King."

Lucian cried out and awoke.

Morning had not yet come. A thin rain tapped upon
the windows, and the painted Child Christ above his pillow
seemed to regard him sorrowfully.

He went again into the chamber of the regalia.

The sceptre lay upon black velvet. The great pearl at
its summit gave forth its tender, mysterious light. Yet as
Lucian gazed, the pearl became for him the white face
of the drowned slave, and its soft radiance was the
pallor of death.

"There is Death in the heart of the pearl," he
said.

One of the guards moved uneasily, thinking perhaps that the
Prince spoke to him, but Lucian returned to his bed
without another word.

He was afraid to sleep. He opened the window and
leaned out.

Far below, workmen raised triumphal arches along the avenue. They
hung scarlet banners from balconies and scattered yellow flowers before
the palace gate. In the eastern quarter, where the poor
lived, no lamps burned. Above those roofs the darkness lay
like a great beast.

Lucian remembered the goatherd's cottage. He saw the old man
mending a net beside the fire, while rain slipped through
the thatch into a wooden bowl. He heard the goats
moving in their pen and the mountain wind calling among
the pines.

"Oh, that I were there!" he said.

But weariness overcame him.

And he fell asleep a third time, and dreamed, and
this was his dream.

He was in a vast valley enclosed by mountains. The
mountains were bare, and on their sides gaped innumerable pits.
A white sun burned in a colourless sky. There were
neither trees nor birds nor blades of grass, but only
stones, dust, and the black mouths of the mines.

Across the valley toiled a multitude beyond counting.

Some hacked at the rocks with iron picks. Some carried
baskets of earth upon their backs. Some dragged carts whose
wheels cut their feet. Children crawled into fissures too narrow
for men, and old women washed gravel in poisoned streams.

Over them stood armed overseers.

At the valley's end, upon a throne of iron, sat
a figure draped in a cloth of gold. Lucian could
not see its face, for it wore a mask shaped
like a smiling mouth. On one side stood Greed with
a bowl; on the other stood Misery with an empty
cradle.

"What do they seek?" Lucian asked.

"Rubies," said a miner beside him.

The man's ribs showed through his skin, and red dust
clung to his beard.

"For whom?"

"For kings, merchants, idols, and women who are loved for
an evening."

"And what are ye paid?"

"We are paid with permission to continue living."

As he spoke, a boy gave a shout. In the
stone before him glittered a vein of red.

The overseers ran forward. The rock was broken, and from
it they drew a ruby as large as a plum.
It burned in the sunlight like a coal taken from
the furnace of the world.

Greed clapped her hands.

Then from the mouth of a cave came Plague. She
was wrapped in a spotted veil, and wherever her feet
touched the ground the dust turned black.

She breathed upon the workers.

One fell. Then another. Then twenty. Then a hundred.

The overseers drove the living across the bodies of the
dead.

"Why dost thou slay them?" demanded Lucian.

Plague smiled behind her veil.

"They are many, and rubies are few."

From another pit came a rumbling like distant thunder. The
earth shook. The roof of the mine collapsed, burying all
who were within. Wives beat upon the stones with their
hands, but the overseers ordered them away.

"The shaft is lost," said Greed. "Open another."

All through the valley pits collapsed, carts overturned, whips descended,
and men died beneath the white and pitiless sun. Yet
still the bowl beside the iron throne remained unfilled.

At last Greed weighed the great ruby and carried it
to the masked figure.

"Is this enough?" she asked.

The figure shook its head.

"Not while one man yet lives to seek another."

Then Lucian saw that the throne was built of human
bones.

A trumpet sounded. The mask fell away.

Upon the throne sat Death.

Her crown was of rubies, and each stone had a
drop of blood moving within it.

"Whose ruby is this?" Lucian asked, though terror closed his
throat.

Death held up the great stone.

"It is for the crown of the young King."

"And who is the young King?"

Death pointed at him.

"Thou art."

Then the dead miners opened their eyes, and with one
voice they said, "Wear us."

Lucian awoke screaming.

Through the windows streamed the first grey light of dawn.
The rain had ceased, and beyond the roofs the cathedral
bells began to ring.

He rose. His face was pale, and his hair clung
damply to his forehead. He crossed the chamber and stood
before the crown.

The rubies blazed in the morning.

But he saw in them the valley, the plague-stricken bodies,
the ruined shafts, and the throne of bones.

"There is Blood in the heart of the ruby," he
said, "and Death in the heart of the pearl, and
Hunger is woven into the golden robe. I will not
wear them."

At that moment the Lord Chamberlain entered, followed by the
Keeper of the Wardrobe, the Royal Jeweller, twelve pages, six
heralds, and the Master of Sacred Ceremonies. All were dressed
magnificently, and the Master of Sacred Ceremonies carried a book
containing the order in which each person was to breathe
during the coronation.

They bowed.

"Sire," said the Lord Chamberlain, "the hour approaches. The bath
is perfumed, the barbers are waiting, and the sacred garments
have been blessed."

Lucian looked at him.

"Take these things away."

The courtiers glanced at one another.

"Which things, Sire?"

"The robe, the sceptre, and the crown."

The Keeper of the Wardrobe turned pale, for the robe
had cost more than a fortress.

"Is some jewel loose? Hath a thread displeased Your Majesty?"

"All the threads displease me."

The Jeweller fell to his knees.

"The pearl is without blemish!"

"The blemish is not upon it," said Lucian, "but beneath
it."

He told them his dreams.

While he spoke, the younger pages listened with frightened faces,
but the nobles concealed their impatience beneath expressions of respectful
grief. When he had finished, the Lord Chamberlain sighed.

"Sire, dreams are not matters of state. If kings altered
policy whenever sleep presented them with an image, government would
belong to indigestion."

"It was no idle image."

"Then let the Bishop interpret it after the ceremony."

"I need no interpreter."

"Majesty needs many interpreters," said the Lord Chamberlain gently. "That
is what Majesty means."

But Lucian had already crossed to a cedar chest in
which he kept the few things brought from the mountains.
From it he took a rough tunic of undyed wool,
a weather-stained cloak of sheepskin, and a long staff of
hazel.

"These were mine when I was free," he said. "They
shall be mine when I am crowned."

A murmur passed through the chamber.

The Master of Sacred Ceremonies dropped his book.

Lucian dressed himself in the shepherd's tunic. He fastened the
old cloak at his shoulder with a thorn, and taking
his knife, cut a length from the wild briar that
climbed about the balcony. He bent it into a circle.
Its thorns pierced his fingers, and drops of blood fell
upon the floor.

"This shall be my crown."

The Lord Chamberlain's face became grave.

"Sire, a jest is excellent at a banquet, permissible at
a hunt, and dangerous at a coronation."

"It is not a jest."

"The people expect splendour."

"They shall see truth."

"The people," said the Chamberlain, "have seldom found truth sufficiently
splendid."

Yet Lucian set the briar upon his curls, took the
hazel staff in his hand, and went from the room.

The pages followed because they were young and curious. The
courtiers followed because they were old and had offices to
preserve. Thus the strange procession descended the great staircase.

Servants lined the galleries. At first they thought the King
wore some symbolic costume whose significance would later be explained
in a proclamation, and they bowed. But when they saw
the rough wool, the worn sandals, and the crown of
thorns, they began to whisper.

In the courtyard waited the royal horse beneath a cloth
of silver, its bridle hung with emeralds and little bells.
Lucian refused it.

"I will go on foot."

The Captain of the Guard protested.

"A king's feet must not touch the common road on
his coronation day."

"Then the common road is more sacred than I."

He passed through the gate.

The avenue beyond was crowded. Merchants had closed their shops,
apprentices had climbed onto roofs, women leaned from windows, and
children sat upon the shoulders of their fathers. Scarlet banners
streamed between the houses. Trumpeters lifted their instruments.

When the people saw Lucian, silence fell.

Then a man who sold ribbons laughed.

"Where is the King?"

"There he walks," said another.

"That is no king. It is a shepherd who hath
lost his goats."

The laughter spread from balcony to balcony. Boys threw yellow
petals at him in mockery. A butcher held up a
wreath of sausages and offered it as a royal collar.
Even the beggars shook their bowls and cried, "Alms for
His Ragged Majesty!"

Lucian stopped.

"Why do ye mock me?" he asked.

A master weaver pushed through the crowd. He wore a
good cloak, though the hands beneath it were twisted by
labour.

"Because thou mockest us," he said. "We have waited to
see our King in glory, and thou comest dressed as
Poverty."

"Is Poverty shameful?"

"To those who endure it, yes."

"I wear no gold because men starve to weave it."

The weaver's mouth hardened.

"And if thou refusest the robe, shall they starve less?
The loom will not pity them. The merchant will seek
another buyer. We live by the vanities of the rich."

"Must thy bread be purchased with another man's blood?"

"All bread is so purchased," answered the weaver. "The plough
cuts the earth; the sickle kills the corn; the millstone
crushes the grain. Thou wouldst have clean hands, young King,
but the world is not clean."

A fishmonger shouted, "Wear thy crown and give us a
holiday!"

A woman called, "My husband worked upon the palace. Must
his labour be hidden beneath rags?"

Another cried, "Splendour is the mercy of kings, for it
gives the poor something beautiful to remember."

Lucian looked upon them sorrowfully.

"I will not honour your labour by wearing your misery."

And he went on.

At the Square of the Bronze Horse, a company of
soldiers barred his path. Their breastplates shone, and their spears
stood upright like a winter forest. At their head rode
the Duke of Vaumont, chief among the nobles, whose family
had supplied the kingdom with generals, rebels, and creditors for
four hundred years.

"Sire," said the Duke, "return to the palace."

"I go to my coronation."

"Not so."

"Who forbids me?"

"The dignity of the Crown."

"I am the Crown."

The Duke's eyes narrowed.

"The Crown is older than the head that wears it."

Behind him the soldiers struck their shields with the shafts
of their spears.

"If thou enterest the cathedral in this guise," continued the
Duke, "foreign ambassadors will report that our kingdom is ruled
by a mad shepherd. Our enemies will arm. Our allies
will laugh. The nobles will not swear loyalty to a
briar."

"Then let them swear loyalty to justice."

"Justice hath no army."

"It hath God."

The Duke smiled coldly.

"God does not sit in Council."

"Perhaps that is why Council does so badly."

Some of the soldiers hid their laughter, and this angered
the Duke more than an insult would have done.

"Wilt thou put aside these rags?"

"I will not."

"Then do not blame us for what follows."

Lucian stepped between the spears. For a moment none moved.
He was unarmed, very young, and the thorn above his
brow had drawn a thin line of blood down his
temple. The soldiers lowered their eyes.

One by one, they opened a path.

The Duke turned his horse sharply and galloped toward the
quarter of the great houses.

And the young King continued to the cathedral.

The cathedral stood in the centre of the city upon
a flight of a hundred white steps. Its dome rose
blue and immense into the morning, like a bubble blown
from heaven, and around its towers wheeled clouds of pigeons.
The bronze doors had been opened. Within, thousands waited under
lamps of silver. The air was heavy with incense and
the perfume of lilies.

At the high altar stood the Bishop in robes of
violet, with a mitre of pearls upon his head and
a pastoral staff of ivory in his hand.

When Lucian entered, a stir passed through the congregation. The
ambassadors whispered behind their jewelled hands. The princes of the
blood turned crimson. The ladies of the court lowered their
eyes, not from modesty but to conceal their smiles.

The Bishop descended from the altar.

"My son," he said, "where are the robes prepared for
thee?"

"I have refused them."

"And the sceptre?"

"I will not carry Death in my hand."

"And the crown?"

Lucian touched the briar.

"Here is my crown."

The Bishop gazed long at him.

Lucian told him the dreams, and as the tale unfolded,
the old man's face grew weary rather than astonished.

"My son," he said at last, "thou hast seen suffering,
and because thou art young thou thinkest it a new
discovery."

"Can suffering become old?"

"To the sufferer, never. To the world, always."

"Then help me change it."

The Bishop sighed.

"Wouldst thou empty the sea because one diver drowned? Wouldst
thou close the mines and leave the miners without bread?
Wouldst thou break the looms and clothe the people in
leaves? Misery is vast. Thy pity is a cup."

"Then let me give the cup."

"Famine walks in the East. War rides in the North.
In the marshes Fever hath built her house, and in
the ports Plague counts the ships. Children are sold, prisoners
are tortured, the innocent perish, and the guilty sleep well.
I have prayed against these things for fifty years."

"And hast thou done nothing but pray?"

A murmur of horror passed through the priests.

The Bishop's hand trembled upon his ivory staff.

"Take care, child. Pride sometimes disguises itself as compassion. Thou
wouldst be purer than all men, and yet kingship itself
is burdened with sin. Wear the robe, not because it
is innocent, but because the people require a sign. Take
the sceptre, not because no man died for it, but
because authority must be visible. Receive the crown, for without
majesty thy good intentions will be trampled by those who
have none."

Lucian answered, "If authority must be purchased with suffering, then
authority is too dear. If Majesty cannot be seen unless
she dresses in the skins of the poor, then she
is Shame."

"Thou speakest as a saint," said the Bishop, "but must
rule as a king."

"Perhaps no man can be a king until he hath
learned to be a saint."

The Bishop looked toward the altar, where the image of
Christ hung upon a cross of dark wood. A crown
of carved thorns circled the bowed head.

"Who taught thee this?" he asked softly.

"My dreams."

"Dreams have ruined empires."

"And awakened souls."

The Bishop turned away.

"I cannot crown thee in these garments."

"Then I will ask a greater hand."

Lucian walked past him and knelt before the altar.

The people muttered. The incense rose in pale spirals. Somewhere
high in the dome, a bird fluttered against a window.

Lucian laid his hazel staff upon the steps and bowed
until his forehead touched the stone.

"Lord," he prayed, "I desired beauty, and Thou hast shown
me its wounds. I delighted in gold, and I heard
the hungry cry. I praised the pearl, and the sea
gave up its dead. I loved the ruby, and the
earth opened and showed me blood.

"I am young and know little. The wise tell me
that cruelty is necessary, the rich that misery sustains them,
the poor that splendour consoles them, and the holy that
evil is too great to oppose. If they speak truth,
then truth is terrible.

"Yet if I cannot cleanse the whole world, let me
not stain my own soul willingly. If I cannot break
every chain, let me not call a chain an ornament.
If I cannot feed every hunger, let me not wear
hunger as a robe.

"Judge me not by the crown they made for me,
but by the crown I choose."

As he prayed, the Duke of Vaumont entered by the
western door.

With him came many nobles in armour. Beneath their cloaks
they carried naked swords. Their faces were stern, for during
the young King's progress through the city they had met
in secret and agreed that a ruler who despised royal
splendour would soon despise noble privilege, which was plainly a
danger to the ancient constitution.

The Duke raised his hand.

"He disgraces the kingdom," he whispered. "When he rises, strike."

The nobles drew their swords.

At that moment the clouds parted.

Through the great eastern window poured a flood of sunlight.

It fell first upon Lucian's bowed shoulders, and the rough
shepherd's tunic shone. Not as woven gold shines, heavily and
with the dull splendour of metal, but as morning shines
upon running water. Every coarse thread became a ray; every
fold held the colour of dawn. Around him the light
gathered and fashioned itself into a robe more delicate than
silk and more glorious than all the treasure of Ormuz.

The congregation ceased to breathe.

Then the hazel staff lying upon the steps put forth
green leaves.

From its dry wood sprang buds, and from the buds
opened lilies, white beyond ivory, white beyond snow, white as
mercy in the heart of God. Their fragrance filled the
cathedral, and the pearls upon the Bishop's mitre grew pale
beside them.

Last, the briar upon Lucian's head began to flower.

The thorns remained, but among them blossomed roses of so
deep and living a red that the rubies of every
prince seemed no more than dark stones. They were red
as love, red as sacrifice, red as the first light
kindled in the chambers of the world.

The organ, though no hand touched it, gave forth a
mighty sound.

The silver lamps swayed. The bells in the towers rang
of themselves. The carved angels upon the choir appeared to
smile, and from the high windows descended a multitude of
white birds that circled three times above the young King's
head.

Lucian rose.

His face shone as if a lamp burned within him.

The Duke's sword fell from his hand and struck the
pavement. One by one the nobles cast down their weapons.

The Bishop removed his pearl mitre.

"A power greater than mine hath crowned thee," he said.

And he knelt.

The priests knelt. The courtiers knelt. The ambassadors, who had
witnessed many strange customs in foreign lands and had prepared
themselves to be amused, knelt also. The soldiers bent their
heads. The merchants bowed. The weavers, the smiths, the fishmongers,
the beggars, and the children who had followed from the
streets sank to their knees.

Even the Duke of Vaumont knelt, though it was said
afterward that he did so with uncommon dignity.

And through the cathedral ran a single cry:

"Behold the King!"

Lucian looked upon the robe of sunlight, the flowering staff,
and the crown of roses. He understood then that Beauty
had not rejected him. She had only demanded that he
know her true name.

For beauty purchased by cruelty is but a painted mask
upon the face of Sin; splendour fed by hunger is
a lamp burning human oil; and the jewel that requires
a life is not a jewel, but a wound taught
to glitter.

Yet sunlight burdens no captive, lilies demand no diver's breath,
and the rose makes rich even the hand that is
pierced by its thorn.

The Bishop approached and would have led him to the
golden throne beneath the dome, but Lucian paused.

"Before I sit," he said, "bring into this place those
who have no seats."

So the great doors were opened wider, and the poor
who stood outside entered. The nobles drew back, the merchants
muttered, and the Masters of Ceremony suffered greatly, for no
page of their books provided instructions for seating beggars beside
ambassadors.

But the young King smiled.

And because his smile was like dawn upon the hills,
no man dared oppose him.

When the service was ended, he left the cathedral and
walked back toward the palace. This time no one mocked
him. The people scattered flowers before his feet, but he
lifted the smallest children and carried them in his arms.
The lilies upon his staff did not wither, though the
sun climbed toward noon, and the roses of his crown
remained fresh.

At the palace gate the golden robe, the pearl sceptre,
and the ruby crown had been brought out by order
of the Lord Chamberlain, who hoped the miracle might have
restored the King to a proper appreciation of ceremonial objects.

Lucian stood before them.

"What shall be done with these treasures?" asked the Chamberlain.

"The robe shall be unpicked," said the King, "and its
gold sold for bread."

The Keeper of the Wardrobe groaned.

"The pearl shall be given to the sea."

The Royal Jeweller wept.

"And the rubies shall be sold to ransom slaves and
open the sealed mines."

The Treasurer stepped forward in alarm.

"Sire, such acts may disturb commerce."

"Then let commerce be disturbed."

"They may anger powerful men."

"Then let powerful men learn anger."

"They may not end all suffering."

"No," said Lucian. "But that is no reason to preserve
the suffering we can end."

The Bishop, who had followed him, heard these words and
bowed his head.

It is said that King Lucian ruled for many years,
though concerning his later deeds the chronicles disagree, as chronicles
commonly do whenever goodness interferes with revenue. Some record that
he forbade children to labour at the looms; others that
he freed the pearl-divers and made the mines subject to
laws of mercy. Certain historians deny these reforms altogether, observing
that no kingdom could have survived them, though the kingdom
did, which was inconsiderate of it.

The old goatherd was brought from the hills and given
rooms in the palace, but he disliked marble floors and
used the royal tapestries to keep draughts from his goats.
The courtiers were scandalised, and Lucian laughed as he had
laughed beneath the almond tree.

Yet on every anniversary of his coronation, the King laid
aside his robes and walked barefoot to the cathedral in
the rough tunic of his childhood. He carried the hazel
staff, which continued to blossom, and wore the crown of
briar, whose roses never faded.

And those who saw him pass said that his face
was not the face of the shepherd boy, nor wholly
the face of a king, but the face of one
who had looked upon suffering and had not turned away.

For it shone like the face of an angel.

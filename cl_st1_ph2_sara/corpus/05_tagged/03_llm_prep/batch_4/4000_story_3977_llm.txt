# THE MASTERPIECE OF PAUL BOIELLE

There are two ways of carrying six plates, three bowls
of soup, a bottle of Burgundy, and a dish of
boiled mutton through a crowded restaurant. One is the right
way. The other is the way of Paul Boielle.

Paul's method combined the finish of a Marathon runner with
the spiritual absorption of a saint and the conversational gifts
of a man selling patent medicine at a fair. He
curved the plates along his left arm, balanced the soup
upon his right, lodged the Burgundy under his chin, and
urged the mutton forward by will-power. Meanwhile he shouted orders
to the kitchen, apologised to a stout customer whose ear
he had brushed with a potato, and addressed remarks of
a private and impassioned nature to Jeanne Le Brocq, who
was wiping knives near the counter.

"Jeanne," he said, as he passed her, "to-night I shall
speak."

"You are speaking now," said Jeanne.

Jeanne was a large, deliberate girl. When she moved, glasses
trembled. When she stood still, the restaurant appeared to have
been built around her. She had calm black eyes and
a face which, seen above the white apron, suggested a
harvest moon rising over a laundry.

Paul deposited the Burgundy upon Table Nine, the soup upon
Table Seven, the mutton upon a gentleman from Balham, and
returned to Jeanne.

"To-night," he repeated, "I shall ask you the great question."

"Table Four wants coffee."

"It is of our lives that I speak."

"Table Four wants coffee now."

The distinction between the romantic and the immediate is one
which women perceive more readily than men. Joan of Arc,
if employed in a Soho restaurant, would probably have attended
to Table Four before rescuing France. Paul went for the
coffee.

He was a small, dark, elastic young Frenchman with a
moustache which seemed always to be preparing to make a
speech. By profession he was a waiter at Bredin's Restaurant,
Frith Street. By nature, destiny, and his own signed testimony
he was a painter.

Nobody at Bredin's knew this except Jeanne, Bredin, the cook,
two kitchen boys, the cashier, the wine merchant, and most
of the customers. Paul had a difficulty in keeping Art
to himself. If a customer asked whether the sole was
fresh, Paul was apt to reply that freshness was the
soul of colour. If somebody complained that the roast beef
was underdone, he explained that Rubens would have approved of
its tone.

His wages were eighteen shillings a week.

What a blessing is poverty, properly considered. It sharpens the
faculties, purifies ambition, strengthens the character, and enables a landlord
to throw a painter's belongings into the street with very
little exertion. Paul considered it improperly. He disliked it.

Yet he had a plan.

In the attic which he called his studio, immediately beneath
a skylight through which rain entered with the freedom of
an honoured guest, stood a large canvas. It represented a
young man in a blue-and-gold costume bounding across a stream
in an enchanted forest. Birch trees bent above him; distant
mountains regarded him with mauve approval; and the dawn, after
some uncertainty, was breaking in the left-hand corner. Paul called
the work *Youth Triumphant*.

It was his masterpiece.

Once the masterpiece had been sold to a wealthy connoisseur,
Paul intended to purchase a cigar shop. The relation between
painting and tobacco may not at first be obvious, but
Paul had studied the matter deeply. A cigar shop offered
warmth, regular meals, a chair behind the counter, and hours
of leisure in which to paint. It also offered an
income on which a man might marry Jeanne.

Thus Art would produce Commerce, Commerce would sustain Love, and
Love would inspire Art. There are financiers in the City
who have founded companies upon less.

At half-past eleven that night Bredin's closed. Bredin himself stood
by the door, scratching his chin and looking with porcine
austerity at the departing staff.

Nature had begun Mr Bredin as a pig and had
then, in a moment of absent-mindedness, finished him as a
restaurant proprietor. His eyes were small, his cheeks vast, and
his neck an economic rumour. When he smiled, which he
did chiefly at women and accounts showing a profit, his
body quivered with ponderous amiability.

Paul waited for him to roll away. Then he approached
Jeanne.

"Jeanne," he said.

Jeanne put on her hat.

"Jeanne, I love you."

"Yes?"

It is disconcerting when a declaration upon which one has
lavished fire and music is received as if it were
a statement concerning the weather. Paul expanded his chest.

"I love you with a passion terrible and eternal. From
the first instant that I see you, my heart cry
aloud. It say, 'There is she! There is the woman!
There is Jeanne!'"

"My bus goes in six minutes."

"You shall not take the bus. We shall walk beneath
the stars. Jeanne, marry me."

"No."

Paul's moustache drooped.

"You say no?"

"I say no."

"But why?"

"You earn eighteen shillings a week."

Paul waved the objection aside. Eighteen shillings, under the influence
of a magnificent gesture, became a vulgar technicality.

"For the moment, yes. But soon I sell my masterpiece."

"How much for?"

Paul had often asked himself this question, and he had
always answered generously.

"Perhaps five hundred pounds. Perhaps one thousand. Who knows? These
rich collectors, they are mad for beauty."

"Has anybody offered five hundred?"

"Not yet."

"Has anybody offered anything?"

"Not yet. The picture is not shown."

Jeanne considered this. Romance had passed her by, but figures
commanded respect.

"What will you do if you sell it?"

"I buy a cigar shop. A splendid shop. Gold letters.
A little room behind. You sit at the desk. I
paint. We are married. Ah, Jeanne!"

She shifted her hat to a more comfortable angle.

"If you sell the picture," she said, "you can ask
me again."

Then she caught her bus.

Paul remained upon the pavement, transfigured. She had not accepted
him; true. But she had admitted an if. To the
lover, an if is a bridge, a banner, a brass
band, and sometimes a trapdoor.

He walked home singing.

* * *

On the following morning Paul wrapped *Youth Triumphant* in brown
paper and took it to Bond Street.

The first dealer unwrapped a corner, observed a birch tree,
and said he had no room.

The second unwrapped the whole picture, observed Youth, Triumph, the
stream, the dawn, and the mauve mountains, and said he
had no room.

The third had room, but preferred pictures of Highland cattle.

"You have no soul," said Paul.

"I have three Highland cattle in the window," replied the
dealer, "and I sold two more on Tuesday."

This was logic of a brutal kind, and Paul withdrew.

For seven days he carried *Youth Triumphant* through London. He
offered it to dealers, magazines, publishers, frame-makers, hotel managers, and
an elderly gentleman who had merely paused to tie his
bootlace. The dealers had no room. The magazines had no
use. The publishers had no budget. The hotel managers had
no authority. The elderly gentleman had no idea why Paul
was following him.

At each refusal Paul's conviction that he was a genius
deepened. Mediocre men may be discouraged by rejection; genius recognises
persecution. Michelangelo, he reflected, had doubtless met just such fools,
though perhaps fewer of them in one afternoon.

Every evening Jeanne asked the same question.

"Sold it?"

"Not yet."

And every evening Paul answered with diminishing splendour.

"The world is blind."

"Mm," said Jeanne.

On the eighth day a small illustrated weekly allowed him
past the outer office. Its art editor, a pale man
with the eyes of one who has looked upon twenty
thousand comic drawings of mothers-in-law, examined the canvas.

"What is it meant to be?"

"Meant?" cried Paul. "It is Youth! It is Spring! It
is the soul of mankind leaping from the dark valley
of despair into the golden—"

"I see he's jumping," said the editor. "Could you make
him look as if he were jumping because a dog
had bitten him?"

"No."

"We're doing a canine number."

Paul seized the canvas and left with dignity. Unfortunately, dignity
is difficult with a large picture in a narrow passage,
and he struck two clerks, overturned a waste-paper basket, and
became wedged in the revolving door.

When he reached Bredin's that evening, he was late by
twenty minutes.

Bredin stood at the counter, pink and ominous.

"You're late."

"I have been fighting the battle of Art."

"You've been fighting it in my time."

Paul went to the kitchen. Jeanne was arranging cream buns
on a dish.

"Sold it?" she asked.

"No."

"Thought not."

There are moments when a man needs tenderness. Paul looked
at Jeanne's broad, calm face and saw no immediate supply.

"The fools will understand one day," he said. "They will
crawl to me. They will say, 'Monsieur Boielle, forgive!' I
shall say, 'Go! There is no room!'"

"Take these buns to Table Eleven."

Paul took the buns.

Such is life. Alexander sighed for new worlds; Napoleon brooded
upon destiny; Paul Boielle carried cream buns to Table Eleven.

* * *

The catastrophe occurred on a Thursday.

Bredin had always admired Jeanne. He admired her as a
man may admire a well-fed heifer, a substantial wardrobe, or
a bank balance in excellent health. Her size appealed to
him. Beside her he felt graceful.

That evening, after the last customers had gone, Jeanne was
polishing the brass rail by the counter when Bredin approached.
He smiled. The effect ran downward through him in ripples.

"Working hard, Jeanne?"

"Yes, Mr Bredin."

"Good girl."

He placed one moist hand beneath her chin.

Paul saw it.

The heroic ages return without warning. One moment a man
is stacking plates; the next he is Perseus beholding Andromeda
in danger. It made no difference to Paul that Andromeda
outweighed Perseus by three stone and could probably have removed
the monster without outside help. His blood became flame.

"Release her!"

Bredin turned.

"What?"

"You insult the woman I love!"

Jeanne stopped polishing.

"Don't be silly, Paul."

But Paul had passed beyond advice. He snatched up a
bread roll and hurled it.

There are weapons which command respect by tradition: the sword,
the spear, the battle-axe. The bread roll has been neglected
by poets, yet at short range and with determination it
can influence events. This one struck Bredin upon the nose.

Bredin squealed.

Paul followed with a second roll. It missed and knocked
over the sugar basin.

Then Homeric conflict spread through Bredin's Restaurant.

Bredin advanced, snorting. Paul retreated round a table, firing such
missiles as came to hand. A spoon rang against the
wall. A potato burst upon Bredin's waistcoat. A cream bun,
launched too hurriedly, struck Jeanne behind the ear.

Jeanne uttered a deep sound, like a cow discovering machinery
in its meadow.

"Stop it!"

Bredin lunged across the table. Paul dodged and discharged the
contents of a gravy boat over his employer's sleeve. Bredin
seized him by the collar. For a few seconds they
revolved together, the prize pig and the infuriated artist, until
a chair intervened and all three went down.

The cook and the kitchen boys separated them.

Bredin rose first. Gravy streaked his coat. Cream decorated one
eyebrow. His body quivered like a jelly upon which tragedy
has suddenly descended.

"You're sacked," he said.

Paul struggled free.

"I spit upon your employment!"

"You're sacked without a reference."

"I disdain your reference!"

"And I'll take the breakages out of your wages."

Paul became silent. Eighteen shillings may be contemptible in the
abstract, but deductions lend it dignity.

"You pig!" he said at last.

"Out!"

Paul turned to Jeanne. He expected gratitude, admiration, perhaps an
embrace. The rescuing of maidens has always been understood to
carry some emotional return.

Jeanne was wiping cream from her hair.

"You've lost me my job," Paul said nobly, "but I
do not reproach you."

"Lost yourself your job."

"For defending you!"

"I didn't ask you to."

"He put his hand beneath your chin."

"So?"

"So! It was an outrage."

"It was none of your business."

Paul stared at her.

"Andromeda," he said, though she did not know it, "had
never spoken in this manner."

"Who?"

"I fight for you, and you turn against me."

"You threw a bun at my head."

"It was intended for Bredin."

"That makes it all right, I suppose."

"Jeanne, come away from this place. I shall sell the
masterpiece. We shall marry."

"Sell it first."

She turned her back.

Paul walked into the night.

Behind him Bredin's glowed warm and yellow. Before him stretched
London, vast, dark, and apparently overstocked with waiters. He had
no employment, no savings, no purchaser for his picture, and
no Jeanne. The four horsemen of personal disaster rode beside
him, though one of them, after inspecting his pockets, remarked
that famine was already present and could manage alone.

He went to his attic.

The masterpiece leaned against the wall.

Paul tore away its wrapping and looked at it. Youth
continued to triumph across the painted stream. The sun rose.
The mountains glimmered. Every leaf declared hope. Paul hated them
all.

"Liar!" he shouted at Youth.

Youth, being in oils, took the rebuke calmly.

Paul sat on the bed. Rain tapped through the skylight
and fell into a saucepan placed there for that purpose.
Drop. Drop. Drop. It was the sort of sound which,
after midnight, has driven stronger men to philosophy.

He thought of Jeanne. He thought of Bredin. He thought
of the art editor and the dog. He thought of
his last sixpence.

Then, with the majestic simplicity of a Roman falling upon
his sword, he made his decision.

He would kill himself.

* * *

The man who has decided to die must settle certain
details. Paul discovered this as soon as he reached the
street.

There was the river, of course. But the river was
cold, and Paul disliked cold water. Poison involved a chemist
and, very probably, money. Hanging required rope and some reliable
object from which to hang. Gas was unavailable in his
attic, the landlord preferring tenants who paid for their own
illumination.

London, so generous with methods of dying by accident, becomes
strangely unhelpful when a citizen wishes to arrange the matter
himself.

Paul walked west, carrying *Youth Triumphant* beneath his arm. He
did not know why he had brought it. Perhaps a
man likes his greatest work near him at the last;
perhaps habit is stronger than despair; perhaps he feared the
landlord would seize it for rent.

The streets grew quieter. He entered the Park. Trees massed
against the sky. Lamps stood at intervals, each illuminating a
small circle of loneliness. Paul's sorrow expanded to suit the
scenery.

He imagined the newspapers.

TRAGIC END OF UNKNOWN GENIUS.

Beneath this, perhaps:

MASTERPIECE FOUND BESIDE BODY.

Later, critics would gather. They would bow their heads before
the canvas. The pale art editor would tear his canine
number into fragments. Dealers would quarrel over the privilege of
exhibiting the work for which they had had no room.
Jeanne, dressed in black, would weep before the grave.

This last picture comforted him considerably.

A motorcar swept round the corner and passed close to
him. Paul leapt backward.

"Assassin!" he cried after it. "Do you wish to kill
me?"

The car vanished.

Paul stood trembling with indignation. Then he remembered that being
killed was precisely what he required.

He brightened.

A motorcar was warm, quick, and free. It involved no
chemist, no rope, no river, and no landlord. Modern civilisation,
so often criticised, had at last justified itself.

He waited.

Several cars passed, but either too slowly or on the
wrong side. Paul rejected them with the fastidiousness of a
patron choosing a cab. Death, yes; but not beneath a
delivery van containing cabbages.

At length he saw two powerful lamps approaching. The car
behind them came swiftly and silently.

Paul placed the painting against a tree.

He stepped into the road.

Brakes shrieked. The car swerved. Paul shut his eyes and
prepared to become immortal.

Nothing happened.

He opened one eye.

The motorcar had stopped with its front wheel six inches
from his left shoe.

A large man in a fur-collared coat sprang out.

"What in thunder do you think you're doing?"

Paul drew himself up.

"You have spoiled everything."

"Spoiled—? I nearly made a pancake of you!"

"That was my desire."

The American stared at him.

Under ordinary circumstances Mr Cyrus K. Biddle, advertising director of
the Universal Brace and Suspender Corporation of Chicago, wasted little
time upon strangers. Time, in his philosophy, was money, and
money was a substance which should never be allowed to
stand idle. But a man who reproaches you for not
running him down has a claim upon curiosity.

"You were trying to commit suicide?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Paul laughed bitterly.

"Why? You ask why? I have lose my employment. My
masterpiece is rejected. The woman I adore despise me. I
possess sixpence. All is finished."

The American looked him over.

"Poor devil," he said. "Get in the car."

"I prefer death."

"You can have it later. Get in."

There was authority in his voice, the authority of a
man accustomed to making five million citizens change brands of
suspenders before breakfast. Paul obeyed.

As the car moved off, the American glanced behind.

"What's that by the tree?"

"My masterpiece."

"Bring it."

The chauffeur retrieved the canvas, and *Youth Triumphant* entered the
motorcar.

* * *

Mr Biddle lived at an expensive hotel where doors opened
before him and men in uniforms competed for the honour
of carrying his hat.

He took Paul to a private sitting-room and ordered food.

At the sight of roast chicken, rolls, salad, and a
covered silver dish whose contents were unknown but fragrant, Paul's
desire for death weakened. Suicide is an enthusiasm best nourished
upon an empty stomach. After two helpings of chicken and
a wedge of pie, he felt that life, though blemished,
might profit from revision rather than immediate cancellation.

Mr Biddle smoked and listened.

Paul told him everything. He told him of the attic,
of Jeanne, of the cigar shop, of the dealers without
room, of the art editor's dog, of Bredin's porcine courtship,
and of the battle of the cream bun. He spoke
for forty minutes and omitted no gesture. When he reached
his dismissal he rose and re-enacted it with a napkin.

Mr Biddle nodded gravely.

"Let's see the picture."

Paul removed the brown paper.

For a long moment the American said nothing.

He was not, in the museum sense, a connoisseur. Put
him before a Rembrandt and he would have wanted to
know its circulation. Show him a Botticelli and he would
have asked whether the lady's hair could be made to
recommend shampoo. But commercial imagination blazed within him. Where Paul
saw Youth, Spring, and the upward struggle of the human
soul, Mr Biddle saw a healthy young man whose trousers
occupied a position of exceptional stability during violent movement.

He stepped closer.

"You painted this?"

"With my blood."

"Oil, isn't it?"

"It is my soul."

"Sure. How long did it take?"

"Seven months."

"You've got the goods," said Mr Biddle.

Paul gripped the table.

"You understand?"

"I understand plenty. That fellow's jumping a brook. Both feet
off the ground, coat flying, arms up, and his pants
staying exactly where pants ought to stay. Say!"

He began to pace.

"The Universal Brace has been looking for a new campaign.
We've tried comfort. We've tried economy. We've tried doctors, athletes,
senators, and a baby pointing at its father's waistband. Nothing.
Too quiet. What we need is action."

He swung round and indicated the canvas.

"That's action."

Paul's face shone.

"You will exhibit it?"

"Exhibit it? I'll put it on every wall from Maine
to California."

"Ah!"

"Full-page colour in the magazines. Twenty-four sheets beside the railroads.
Show-cards in every men's store. We'll call it 'Youth Triumphant—Thanks
to Universal Braces.'"

Paul became still.

"Braces?"

"Suspenders."

"You wish to put suspenders upon my Youth?"

"Bright red ones. They'll carry at a distance."

Paul looked at the painted figure. He looked at Mr
Biddle. He looked again at the painted figure.

"It is an allegory," he said faintly.

"Sure. So's advertising."

"The mountains signify aspiration."

"We keep the mountains."

"The stream is despair."

"Keep that too."

"The golden light is the immortal spirit."

"Fine. Put the product name across it."

Paul recoiled. Art staggered within him, stabbed beneath the fifth
rib by Commerce.

"You ask me to desecrate my masterpiece."

Mr Biddle sat down.

"I ask you to sell it."

Paul's artistic conscience raised its head.

"How much?"

"Three thousand dollars."

The conscience lay down again.

Mr Biddle produced a cheque-book.

"And that isn't all. I need a man who can
draw movement like that. Our London agency turns out men
standing around in underwear as if they were waiting for
a funeral. You come to Chicago, or stay here and
work through the London office. Five thousand dollars a year
to start."

Paul's lips moved.

He converted dollars into pounds. The result was so beautiful
that for a moment he could not breathe.

"A year?"

"A year. If you're as good as I think, it
goes up. We sell ten million pairs. A man who
can make suspenders look romantic is worth money."

Paul stood before *Youth Triumphant*. He communed with his soul.

An artist must compromise at times. Michelangelo had accepted commissions.
Raphael had dealt with patrons. Leonardo, had the opportunity arisen,
might have painted an excellent advertisement for tooth powder. Moreover,
red was a stimulating colour.

"It shall be done," said Paul.

"Cracker jack!"

Mr Biddle wrote the cheque.

Paul took it with trembling fingers. Three thousand dollars. The
bit of paper seemed too small. Destiny, he felt, should
have been printed on cardboard.

"We'll go to the bank in the morning," said Mr
Biddle. "Then clothes. Then you meet our London people at
noon."

Paul gazed at him.

"Monsieur," he said, "you have give me back my life."

"Well, don't step in front of any more cars. It
scratches the paint."

* * *

Prosperity is a powerful restorer. By six o'clock the next
evening Paul had acquired a new suit, new shoes, a
silk hat, gloves, a gold watch, and a manner.

The suit alone would have altered the opinions of most
thoughtful women. It was dark blue, discreet, and cut by
a tailor who understood that civilisation depends upon the shoulders.
The hat gleamed. The shoes reflected passing cabs. In his
pocket rested ten crisp pound notes, an advance from the
Universal Brace and Suspender Corporation.

Paul took a cab to Bredin's.

He might have gone to another restaurant. London contains many.
But there are meals which nourish the body and meals
which nourish the soul, and Paul had an appetite for
the latter.

He entered at eight o'clock.

Bredin stood near the door.

For one wonderful second he failed to recognise him. He
saw only a prosperous young gentleman with an American-cut suit
and an air of being expected by banks. Then recognition
arrived, and with it uneasiness.

"You!"

Paul handed him his hat.

"Take care of it."

Bredin's face deepened in colour.

"What do you want?"

Paul looked round.

"I wish to dine."

"You can't."

Paul drew a banknote from his pocket. It was a
five-pound note, and in Bredin's restaurant it had the moral
effect of a royal warrant.

"I have been told," he said, "that persons dine here
if they can pay."

Bredin's eyes fixed upon the note. The restaurateur struggled with
the man. The restaurateur won.

"This way, sir," he muttered.

"Near the window."

Paul seated himself at the best table. He picked up
the menu and studied it with the grave discontent of
one accustomed to better things.

A waiter approached. Paul dismissed him.

"I prefer the waitress."

Bredin hesitated.

Then he called Jeanne.

She came from the back of the room carrying a
tray. At first she saw merely the clothes. Then she
saw Paul inside them.

Her mouth opened.

"Paul!"

He raised his eyebrows.

"Mademoiselle?"

"What are you doing here?"

"I am dining."

"In those clothes?"

Paul sighed.

"They were considered suitable by my tailor."

Jeanne's gaze moved from the silk tie to the gold
watch-chain and returned to his face with a new expression.
It was not yet love, perhaps, but it had begun
to make inquiries.

"Where did you get the money?"

"The picture."

"You sold it?"

"For three thousand dollars."

Jeanne sat down suddenly upon the chair opposite.

Bredin, watching from the counter, swelled with alarm. A customer
at the next table stopped eating soup. He had followed
the drama at Bredin's for months and recognised a final
act when he saw one.

"Three thousand!" said Jeanne.

"And a position. Five thousand dollars a year. To begin."

Jeanne's eyes softened. It was remarkable what tenderness five thousand
dollars could awaken in them. Paul, who had once compared
those eyes to midnight pools, now observed that they resembled
two competent cashiers counting receipts.

"You never told me," she said.

"I did not know yesterday."

"You're going to buy the cigar shop?"

Paul unfolded his napkin.

"No."

"But you said—"

"That was before I entered the advertising profession. I shall
take rooms in St James's. Later, perhaps, I go to
America."

Jeanne leaned nearer.

"You asked me something the other night."

"Did I?"

"You asked me to marry you."

Paul appeared to search his memory.

"Ah, yes. It is possible."

"You can ask me again if you like."

The gentleman at the next table abandoned all pretence of
attending to his soup.

Bredin listened so hard that his cheeks seemed to point.

Paul looked at Jeanne.

Twenty-four hours earlier he had desired nothing in the world
so much as her acceptance. He had seen her as
companion, goddess, muse, cashier of the cigar shop, and mother
of dark-eyed children who would inherit his genius and her
ability to carry a tray. Now the moon had set.
Before him sat a large practical waitress who had rejected
eighteen shillings and become affectionate at five thousand dollars a
year.

Success had not only lined Paul's pockets. It had polished
his eyesight.

He smiled.

"My dear Jeanne," he said, "you are very kind."

Her face brightened.

"But I could not think of taking you away from
Mr Bredin. He admires you. Also, you are an excellent
waitress."

Jeanne stared.

"You don't want to marry me?"

Paul examined the menu.

"I will have the clear soup."

"Paul!"

"And after that, the sole."

"Are you making fun of me?"

"Not at all. I am ordering dinner."

Her face flushed.

"You little beast!"

Bredin hurried forward. Whatever his private emotions, nobody insulted the
staff before paying the bill.

"Jeanne! Get back to work."

Paul raised one finger.

"And bring a bottle of the best Burgundy."

Bredin looked at him. His small eyes travelled unwillingly to
the banknote beside the plate. Hatred wrestled with business. Once
more business gained the fall.

"The best Burgundy," he told Jeanne.

She stood for a moment, breathing heavily. Then she turned
and went.

Paul watched her departure without pain. A surprising thing about
broken hearts is the speed with which prosperity repairs them.

The customer at the next table lowered his spoon and
smiled into his napkin.

Near the door a policeman had entered to speak to
Bredin about a delivery cart obstructing the pavement. He surveyed
the proprietor's pink, agitated face and broad black coat.

"Friend of yours?" he asked, indicating Paul.

"No," said Bredin.

The policeman considered Paul's clothes, Bredin's expression, and Jeanne's retreating
back. He was a man who understood the world through
glimpses and preferred his conclusions simple.

"Seems to have got your number," he said.

Bredin made a noise in his throat.

Jeanne returned with the Burgundy. She placed it on the
table harder than was necessary.

"Anything else, sir?"

Paul inspected the bottle.

"Yes."

She waited.

"Do not shake the wine."

Their eyes met. In hers were fury, regret, and calculation.
In his was courteous emptiness.

"Very good, sir," she said.

And that "sir" was Paul's triumph.

He dined well. Bredin hovered. Jeanne served him. The customer
at the next table ordered a second portion of pudding
in order not to miss anything. Outside, London hurried through
the rain, unaware that one of its great romantic dramas
had ended over a bottle of Burgundy and a plate
of sole.

As Paul left, Bredin gave him his hat.

Paul tipped him sixpence.

It was his last concession to Art.

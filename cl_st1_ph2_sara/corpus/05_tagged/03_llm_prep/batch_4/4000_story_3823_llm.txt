When Toinette Girard married Prosper Leclere, the old women of
Saint-Basile said that she had done better than anyone expected,
and the young women said that Prosper had done better
still. Both judgments were true, which is rare enough in
village judgments to be worth remembering.

Toinette was not beautiful in the manner of the coloured
saints behind the glass at Dufresne's religious-goods counter. Her nose
turned upward a little; her brown hair would escape its
pins; and one of her eyes, when she was excited,
seemed to ask a question before the other had understood
it. Nor was she learned. She wrote a hand as
large and upright as fence posts, and she considered any
letter containing more than two semicolons a work of philosophy.

But she had quick feet, a clear laugh, an honest
temper, and a positive genius for loving.

There are people who love by measure. They give tenderness
as a prudent shopkeeper gives sugar—no more than the weight,
and never forgetting the paper. Toinette gave the bag, the
scoop, the sugar, and both hands besides. When she loved,
there was no arithmetic left in her.

Prosper, for his part, was a compact, respectable man with
black whiskers and a habit of polishing his boots even
when the road was six inches deep in mud. He
was brave in business, timid in happiness, generous with money,
and somewhat stingy with praise. He believed in God, hard
work, and well-kept accounts; but of the three, perhaps the
accounts gave him the least mystery. You might call him
a Catholic with a Puritan temperament and a Gallic imagination.
That combination can make a man admirable at nine o'clock
in the morning and miserable by midnight.

They began married life in two rooms behind a small
shop on the main road. Above the door hung a
blue sign:

**PROSPER LECLERE
GROCERIES, DRY GOODS, HARDWARE, NOTIONS**

The word *notions* delighted Toinette. She said it meant anything
a person might suddenly decide he could not live without.

They sold flour, lamp oil, nails, ribbon, tea, seed, patent
medicine, bootlaces, smoked herring, and little china dogs with gilt
collars. In winter the farmers stamped snow from their boots
beside the stove; in spring they brought the smell of
wet earth with them; in summer flies hummed against the
windows and Toinette tied her sleeves above her elbows; in
autumn the bins glowed with apples, onions, and pumpkins.

Prosper understood buying and prices. Toinette understood people.

A customer might enter determined to purchase only a spool
of black thread and leave with the thread, two yards
of flannel, a tin whistle for the youngest boy, and
a conviction that all four had been his own idea.
Toinette never cheated him. She merely made the world appear
full of useful possibilities.

"Madame Leclere," Prosper once told her, pretending severity, "you could
sell spectacles to a man with no ears."

"Bah! I would first sell him the ears," she answered.

Then he laughed, though he tried not to, and she
watched his face as if the sun had come into
the shop for her alone.

They had no children. For the first two years they
expected them; for the next two they hoped; afterward they
ceased to speak of it. Sorrow passed over their felicity
like the shadow of a cloud over a hayfield—visible, cool,
and soon gone, though not forgotten. Toinette gathered the affection
that might have been divided among sons and daughters and
poured it all upon Prosper.

She warmed his stockings before the stove. She knew from
the sound of his step whether he had sold well,
eaten badly, or met a man he disliked. If he
coughed twice at night, she sat awake until morning. When
he cut his thumb opening a crate, she nearly fainted;
when he recovered, she preserved the bloodstained handkerchief in her
sewing basket.

This last fact was discovered by tante Bergeron, Toinette's widowed
aunt, who lived across the lane and entered without knocking
whenever Providence or curiosity moved her.

"Voyons voir!" cried the old woman, holding up the handkerchief.
"What is this relic?"

"Give it to me!" said Toinette, snatching it away.

"You worship that husband."

"I love him."

"There is love, and there is idolatry."

"Je m'en fricasse of your idolatry. He is mine, and
I love him."

Tante Bergeron settled her black cap over her ears. She
enjoyed foretelling calamity, partly because calamity was important and partly
because, if it failed to come, nobody complained.

"Too much love calls out to death," she said. "My
grandmother knew a woman at Trois-Rivières who loved her son
beyond reason. She counted his breaths while he slept. One
night she heard a bell beneath the floor. Three days
later—gone."

"Who was gone?"

"The son."

"What killed him?"

"Death, naturally."

"But what sickness?"

"Death requires no assistant."

Toinette laughed and pushed her aunt toward the kitchen, where
coffee and seed cake reduced the conversation to safer subjects.
Yet the saying remained. Foolish words are burrs: one brushes
them away from the sleeve and finds them later fastened
near the heart.

Prosper sometimes travelled to buy stock or arrange contracts. Usually
he went no farther than Montreal, and seldom for more
than a week. Those absences were Toinette's malady.

On the first day she busied herself. On the second
she listened for wheels. By the third every newspaper report
of a railway accident seemed written especially for her. She
imagined fever, robbery, fire, drowning, a runaway horse, an overturned
train. At night the house enlarged around her. The boards
creaked with deliberation. The stove muttered. Wind touched the window
like a fingernail.

"Why do you torment yourself?" tante Bergeron asked.

"Because he is away."

"He has been away before."

"And death has been in the world before."

"Then pray."

"I do pray."

"Pray with confidence."

"I pray with everything!"

That was precisely the difficulty. Toinette did nothing by halves—not
work, not laughter, not fear, not prayer.

Four years after their marriage Prosper went to Ottawa on
business. A wholesale firm had failed, another had offered favourable
terms, and there were questions involving barrels of sugar, cases
of tea, and a line of agricultural tools. He expected
to remain twelve days.

"Fourteen at the most," he said at the station.

"Not fifteen?"

"Certainly not."

"Promise."

"I cannot command the railway."

"Then command yourself to come by another train."

He kissed her, somewhat embarrassed because the stationmaster was watching.
She gripped his lapels.

"You have your woollen scarf?"

"Yes."

"And the cough mixture?"

"I have no cough."

"That is why you must preserve yourself from one."

He smiled. "Good-bye, little goose."

"Prosper—"

"Well?"

"Nothing. Come back."

The train pulled away with a grinding of iron and
a great exhalation of white vapour. Toinette ran beside it
for several yards. Then she stood still, waving until the
last carriage became a dark mark, and the dark mark
disappeared.

You know how time behaves with those who wait. For
the happy it runs downhill like April water. For the
anxious it drips as reluctantly as black molasses from a
cold jug. Toinette endured every minute separately.

Prosper wrote twice. His first letter was practical and affectionate
in his fashion. He had arrived safely; prices were not
what the salesman had represented; his room was too warm;
she must remember to order kerosene.

His second letter was shorter.

The third did not come.

Now there was an ordinary explanation. Prosper had become busy.
But fear does not eat ordinary explanations when extraordinary ones
are available.

On the tenth day a commercial traveller named Delorme entered
the shop. He represented a Montreal house dealing in buttons,
combs, needles, hooks, and other small necessities upon which civilisation,
though proud, secretly depends. Delorme had a waxed moustache and
a polished manner. He joked with Toinette while spreading his
samples.

"You choose blue buttons, madame? Ah, but blue agrees with
your eyes."

"My eyes are brown."

"Then the buttons must change colour from admiration."

"Keep your badinage for customers who pay too much."

"You wound me."

"I will order six dozen of the black and four
of the pearl. No blue."

Delorme laughed. He remained perhaps forty minutes. Two days later
he returned because he had copied the order incorrectly. Unfortunately,
Prosper's cousin Alphonse saw him leave by the side door—the
front entrance being blocked by a wagon unloading flour—and later
mentioned this innocent circumstance in a letter to Prosper.

Alphonse had no evil intention. A man need not intend
to drop a spark in order to burn a barn.

Prosper read the letter in his hotel room, where rain
striped the windows and the stove gave too much heat.
First he shrugged. Then he read the passage again.

*Saw that handsome fellow Delorme coming out your side door.
Toinette was laughing fit to wake the dead. You had
better hurry home or she will sell him the whole
establishment.*

It was a joke. Prosper knew it was a joke.

But jealousy seldom enters like a burglar. It is more
cunning. It knocks politely and asks whether you have considered
all the facts.

Prosper considered them. Toinette was warm with everyone. Men liked
her. Delorme was handsome. The side door opened near the
private rooms. Why had she not mentioned his visits in
her last letter? Why had her last letter been only
four pages instead of six?

He despised himself and continued.

Jealousy is a strangling vine. At first it appears only
a green thread against the wall; presently it has entered
every crack, covered every window, and pulled the stones apart.
Prosper slept badly. By morning suspicion had turned his decent
heart into a courtroom in which Toinette was accused, Delorme
condemned, and every laugh he could remember admitted as evidence.

Meanwhile Toinette's fear also flowered.

On the thirteenth evening snow began to fall, though the
season was early. The flakes came sparsely at first, then
in crowds, whitening the road and softening the dark roofs.
Toinette closed the shop, checked the stove, and went upstairs.
She had eaten little. Tante Bergeron's story returned to her—not
as words now, but as a conviction.

Too much love calls out to death.

Perhaps Heaven had marked Prosper because she had loved him
beyond the permitted measure. Perhaps every kiss had been an
appeal against him, every prayer a challenge, every fearful thought
a countersign of death.

She knelt before the crucifix above her small bedside table.

"O good God," she whispered, "if there must be punishment,
take from me. Take the shop. Take my health. Take—"

She stopped. There was nothing else. Prosper was all the
rest.

The lamp burned low. Snow hissed against the window. From
very far away—or from the road below—came the faint ringing
of bells.

Toinette lifted her head.

The room was full of pale light.

Not lamplight, not moonlight. It had no source. It seemed
to breathe through the walls, and with every breath the
familiar objects became less substantial. The washstand faded; the bed
curtains lengthened into columns; the rag rug flowed away like
mist.

She found herself kneeling in a church.

It was not the parish church of Saint-Basile. This church
was immense, but its vastness had no boundaries. Columns climbed
into luminous fog. Lamps burned behind veils of blue smoke.
The air held an unfamiliar perfume—wax, winter roses, damp stone,
and something sweetly corrupt, like lilies left too long beside
a coffin.

Every pew was crowded.

Toinette could see no faces. She felt the worshippers as
one feels a forest in darkness: by the breathing of
leaves, the shifting of hidden branches. Silk rustled. Wood creaked.
A murmur passed from aisle to aisle, and beneath it
sounded another noise—very faint, very terrible—the dragging of innumerable chains.

At the altar a priest in silver vestments raised his
hands. His face was hidden by the brightness behind him.

"Whose Mass is this?" Toinette whispered.

A woman beside her answered without turning.

"For the dead."

"What dead?"

"The dead who were loved too much."

Toinette's heart gave one violent blow.

"Prosper!"

Nobody replied. The priest began to speak, but his voice
came from a great distance, like thunder beyond hills. Toinette
understood no words. She understood only loss.

Then a hooded figure moved down the aisle carrying a
black bag. It paused at each pew. Hands emerged from
the shadows—thin hands, brown hands, hands glittering with rings—and dropped
gold coins into the bag.

Clink.

Clink.

Clink.

The sound approached.

Toinette searched her pockets. She found a handkerchief, two copper
coins, a shop key. The collector stood before her. Beneath
the hood there was no face she could distinguish, only
depth and a small reflection of altar flame.

"Gold," said the figure.

"I have none."

"Only gold is received."

"I am poor."

"You are rich in what you fear to lose."

The black bag opened.

Toinette looked at her left hand. Her wedding ring shone
warmly in that colourless place.

"No," she said.

The hidden congregation stirred.

The collector waited.

"It is all I have of him."

"For whom do you offer?"

"For Prosper Leclere, my husband. But he is not dead.
Do you hear? He is not dead!"

"Gold."

"If I give this, will he live?"

No answer.

The distant bells rang again. The unseen chains moved. Toinette
pulled at the ring, but her finger had swollen. She
twisted until the skin burned.

"Take it, then! Take it instead of him!"

The ring came free.

It lay for an instant in her palm—a little circle
containing four years of mornings and evenings, quarrels and forgiveness,
bread shared, accounts balanced, lamplight, winter, laughter. Then she dropped
it into the bag.

Clink.

Every lamp in the church went out.

Toinette awoke—or returned, or was released—kneeling beside her bed.

The room was dark. Her lamp had extinguished itself. She
was fully dressed, though she remembered removing her shoes. Snow
whispered at the pane.

For several moments she could not rise. Her knees ached.
Her right hand clutched the bedcover. Her left hand was
open.

The wedding ring was gone.

She lit the lamp with shaking fingers. She searched the
floor, the bed, her pockets. She stripped back the blankets.
She moved the washstand. She examined the spaces between the
boards with a candle. Nothing.

"No," she muttered. "No, no, no."

She ran downstairs. Perhaps she had removed it while closing
the shop. She searched the counter, the till, the flour
bins, the drawer where she kept string. She sifted ashes
from the stove. She crawled beneath shelves, leaving white streaks
on her dress. At dawn she was still searching.

Tante Bergeron found her upstairs after sunrise, overturning the sewing
basket.

"What devil has been dancing here?"

"My ring."

"What of it?"

"It is gone."

"Then it is in the house."

"It is in the church."

The aunt stared.

Toinette told her everything. As she spoke, the old woman's
mouth tightened. Superstition is often courageous while sitting by another
person's fire; confronted in daylight with its own offspring, it
may become timid.

"You dreamed," tante Bergeron said.

"Then where is the ring?"

"You walked in your sleep."

"To that church?"

"Not to a church. Around the room, perhaps."

"I searched."

"Search again."

"I have searched everywhere!"

The words broke into a cry so full of anguish
that the aunt crossed herself.

"Do not tell anyone," she whispered.

"Prosper is dead."

"You have no proof."

"The Mass—"

"A dream."

"The ring—"

"Mislaid."

"The bells."

"Sleigh-bells."

"The collector—"

"Indigestion. You did not eat your supper."

Thus tante Bergeron, who had planted the seed, now attempted
to deny the tree. But Toinette saw only Prosper laid
out in some Ottawa room, his black whiskers smoothed, his
hands cold.

At that very hour Prosper was on the train home.

He had concluded his business a day early and had
sent no telegram. At first he told himself he wished
to surprise his wife. Then, with shameful honesty, he admitted
that he wished to surprise someone else.

The snow delayed the train. Every mile gave his imagination
time to work. Memories, once loosened, surged through him like
logs after a river jam: Toinette laughing with a farmer;
Toinette praising Delorme's samples; Toinette saying Prosper need not hurry
back if business detained him. Harmless things struck one another
and became dangerous in the current.

By the time he descended at Saint-Basile, he was pale
and determined.

No one came to meet him. Why should they? No
one knew he was coming. Yet this wounded him.

He left his valise at the station and walked quickly
toward the shop. Snow squeaked beneath his boots. The blue
sign swung over the door. The front entrance was locked
though the morning was advanced.

He tried the side door.

It opened.

Inside, drawers stood out, boxes lay overturned, flour streaked the
floor. A chair had fallen near the counter.

Prosper's jealousy no longer whispered. It shouted.

"Toinette!"

She appeared at the stairhead. Her hair hung loose; her
dress was wrinkled and dusted with ash. For one instant
she did not recognise him. Then her face changed with
such joy that any wiser man would have fallen on
his knees.

"Prosper!"

She flew down the stairs and threw her arms around
him.

"You are alive! Oh, my love, my love—you are alive!"

He held himself stiffly.

"Evidently."

She drew back. "What is it?"

"What has happened here?"

"I was searching."

"For what?"

She hid her left hand, but too late.

Prosper caught it.

The bare finger seemed to him confirmation of every poisonous
thought. A ring removed. A disordered house. A wife fully
dressed at dawn. He heard Alphonse's joke as if spoken
beside him.

"Where is your wedding ring?"

Toinette stared at him.

"I lost it."

"When?"

"Last night."

"How?"

"I—I gave it away."

His grip tightened.

"To whom?"

She saw the malady in his face but did not
yet understand it. "At the Mass."

"What Mass?"

"The Mass for the dead."

He released her hand as if it had burned him.

"Do not lie badly, Toinette."

She became very still.

"Lie?"

"I know about Delorme."

"Delorme?"

"The commercial traveller. He comes by the side door when
I am away. You entertain him. You laugh with him."

For a moment bewilderment overcame pain. "He came twice. The
wagon blocked the front."

"And the ring?"

"I told you."

"You gave it away."

"Yes."

"To him?"

The accusation stood between them, naked at last.

Toinette's face lost all colour. She leaned against the counter.
Prosper had imagined anger, excuses, perhaps tears. He had not
imagined the look she gave him—the look of a creature
struck by the hand it has always trusted.

"You believe that of me?"

"Where is the ring?"

"In the church."

"What church?"

"I do not know."

"There is only one church in Saint-Basile."

"Not that one. Another. A great church, full of the
dead, and the collector asked for gold. I had no
gold except my ring. I gave it because the Mass
was for people who died from being loved too much.
Tante Bergeron said—oh, it sounds mad, but I was afraid!
You did not write. I thought Heaven had taken you
because I had loved you beyond reason. So I gave
the ring. I said, 'Take it instead of him.' Then
I was here, beside the bed, and it was gone."

Prosper's mouth twisted.

"A convenient miracle."

She flinched.

"Prosper, it is true."

"Swear it."

"I swear."

"Not to me."

Her eyes filled. "Very well."

She turned and went upstairs. Prosper followed. The room bore
the violence of her search: mattress dragged aside, drawers emptied,
clothes heaped on the floor. Tante Bergeron stood in the
corner, moving her lips soundlessly.

Toinette went to the crucifix above the bedside table.

"I will swear before Our Lord," she said. "And if
I speak falsely, may—"

She reached up.

Something moved beneath the small wooden base of the crucifix.
There came a tiny metallic sound.

All three looked down.

On the white cloth covering the table lay the wedding
ring.

No one spoke.

Morning light touched it. The plain gold circle shone with
a mild, steady brightness—nothing supernatural now, nothing terrible. Merely gold.
Merely a ring. Yet there are moments when the smallest
object bears a weight greater than a church.

Toinette picked it up slowly.

"I searched there," she whispered. "I lifted the crucifix. I
searched there three times."

Tante Bergeron crossed herself and backed toward the door.

"Some mysteries," she announced faintly, "are not improved by discussion."

Then she fled.

Prosper remained.

His jealousy collapsed, but not gently. When a false conviction
breaks, its fragments cut the person who held it. He
saw the open drawers, the ashes on Toinette's dress, the
red mark where the ring had been pulled from her
finger. He saw, too, the four years behind them—her vigilance,
her foolish tenderness, her entire unguarded heart. Every memory he
had used against her now returned as witness for her
defence.

He sat on the edge of the bed and covered
his face.

"Toinette."

She did not answer.

"I have been wicked."

Still she said nothing. Her fingers closed around the ring.

He looked up. Prosper was not a man who wept
readily. His tears embarrassed him, and because they embarrassed him
they were honest.

"You feared you had loved me too much," he said.
"But it was I who did not love enough."

She shook her head. "You loved."

"Without trust? That is poor love. A miser's love. I
kept it locked up and counted it, and suspected everyone
who came near the door."

"Prosper—"

"I accused you because I was afraid. No, let me
say it. I thought another man might take you because
I never understood why you chose me. I was proud
where I should have been grateful."

Released words came now like spring water through broken ice.
He told her of Alphonse's letter, the hotel room, the
sleepless night, the ugly thoughts. Confession made none of them
smaller, but it made repentance possible.

At last he held out his hand.

"Can you forgive me?"

Toinette looked at him a long while. Love may be
limitless, but hurt has its hour, and even the tenderest
heart should not be hurried through it.

Then she said, "Put it back."

He took the ring.

Her finger was swollen, and the gold passed with difficulty
over the knuckle. Prosper pressed it gently into place. When
he had done so, he bowed his head over her
hand.

"Not too much," she murmured.

"What?"

"You must not love me too much. Tante Bergeron will
invent another death."

He gave a broken laugh.

"No," he said. "Not too much."

"Enough, then?"

He drew her toward him.

"Enough to trust you."

And that, as you know—or as every happy marriage must
sooner or later discover—is not the lesser love but the
greater. For love without trust is a lamp with no
oil, a promise with no courage, a ring with its
circle broken. If it breaks, they are broken. If it
holds, they are happy.

Whether Toinette dreamed the radiant church, whether she walked in
her sleep and placed the ring beneath the crucifix, or
whether mercy permitted her to see for one solemn instant
a world hidden from ordinary eyes, I cannot tell you.
Tante Bergeron claimed all three explanations at different times and
resented anyone who reminded her of the other two.

But the ring was found.

Prosper never again questioned Toinette's laughter with customers, though Delorme
prudently used the front door thereafter. Toinette continued to warm
Prosper's stockings, worry over his coughs, and love him with
her whole unreasonable heart. The shop prospered. The blue sign
faded. Seasons came and went, carrying snow, mud, flies, apples,
weddings, funerals, debts, and forgiveness.

And when Prosper travelled, he wrote every day.

Even if he had nothing to report, he wrote.

Sometimes the letter contained only a single line in his
fence-post hand:

*I am alive, ma chère, and I trust you.*

For Toinette, that was enough.

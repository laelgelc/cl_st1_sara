My Dear Violet,

You asked me, when we walked last Thursday under the
wet elms at Sevenhays, why I, who had once spoken
of Providence with something approaching bitterness, could now hear the
old words read in church without either impatience or that
inward recoil which is worse than open dissent. I answered
you evasively. Indeed, I believe I said that age had
blunted the edge of speculation—a cowardly phrase, and not even
a true one; for age, though it may dull appetite,
commonly sharpens whatever thorn the mind has consented to carry.
You knew I was withholding something. Well, you shall have
the whole tale.

I must ask only that you do not name it
to others. I have no desire to become the patron
saint of psychical societies, nor to furnish a paragraph for
those ingenious journalists who are able to explain Heaven and
influenza with equal confidence. What happened may have been a
dream, a seizure of the brain, an elaborate architecture raised
in one instant by grief. I cannot prove otherwise. Yet
I was not asleep when it began; and if the
vision was born wholly within me, then there are continents
in the soul whose mountains and rivers possess a substance
more formidable than chalk or granite.

I

You remember Harry as he was before he went East:
the sudden smile, the untidy fairness of his hair, his
invincible habit of whistling in passages, and that endearing conviction
that every quarrel, whether between nations or stable-boys, might be
settled if only the disputants would dine together. He was
not profound—God forbid that I should make him profound now
that he is dead. The dead have suffered enough from
the virtues imposed upon them by survivors. Harry was careless,
impatient of books, absurdly generous, and brave without knowing bravery
to be uncommon. He went abroad neither for glory nor
advancement, but because three villages had been burned, a relief
mission lacked an interpreter, and he happened to know the
dialect.

His death, as you know, was violent; but perhaps you
never heard its particulars. The party had entered the valley
under written guarantees from both factions. At noon they were
stopped beside a dry watercourse by men who wore no
uniform. Their medical chests were opened and scattered; the mules
were shot; and Harry, who had stepped forward holding up
the stamped papers, was struck first in the mouth with
a rifle-butt and afterwards repeatedly with a knife. A native
orderly, hidden beneath the body of a mule, saw everything
and brought us the account. Harry lived for nearly an
hour. There was no priest, no morphia, no water. He
asked twice whether the women of the mission had escaped,
and once, very quietly, for our mother.

That last detail undid me.

Death in the abstract has a certain Roman dignity. One
writes mors ultima linea rerum beneath an engraving and believes
oneself reconciled to law. But the particular death—the broken teeth,
the dust adhering to blood, the flies investigating an eyelid,
the beloved voice asking for one who cannot answer—this is
no abstraction. It is an indecency committed upon the entire
past. Every summer morning of our childhood became, in retrospect,
an accomplice in that ditch. What business had the sun
to shine upon us at twelve if it was also
to shine upon Harry at thirty-four while he bled among
burst medicine-bottles?

Our friends offered the customary anodynes. He had died in
a noble cause; his example would bear fruit; perhaps his
sacrifice had already softened hostile hearts. I tried not to
resent them. A platitude is often merely a truth delivered
before its hour. Yet in this case the words seemed
not premature but monstrous. Harry had not been a counter
in some celestial game, to be surrendered for an advantage
visible only to the Player. If omnipotence desired softened hearts,
omnipotence might soften them without first breaking his body.

For several months I retained a formal belief in God
while denying, with increasing vehemence, His goodness. The distinction is
important. Atheism would have relieved me of an enemy; I
was not so fortunate. The universe appeared far too intricate,
too mathematical, too prodigal in cunning, to be fatherless. Consider
the spiral of a nebula, the valves of the heart,
the eye of a moth, the migratory instinct that conducts
a frail bird across black leagues of sea. Everywhere intelligence;
everywhere design. But when I turned from the instrument to
the purpose for which it was employed, what did I
find? Teeth, talons, venom, famine; the ichneumon laying its eggs
in the living caterpillar; the cat prolonging the agony of
the mouse; children born deformed; cities shaken down at dawn;
pestilence entering by the same air that carries the scent
of hawthorn. Nature was not careless. Carelessness might have left
gaps through which mercy could enter. No, the engine was
exact.

Thus I came to imagine a supreme Artificer whose wisdom
was infinite and whose delight was cruelty.

You will say that this was grief speaking. Certainly it
was grief; but grief may reason, and my syllogisms seemed
then as solid as Euclid. If God knew all pain
beforehand, He had chosen it. If He could prevent pain
and did not, He preferred its existence. The familiar reply—that
freedom requires the possibility of evil—explained, even if one granted
it, only a narrow province of suffering. What freedom had
the foal drowned in a flooded meadow? What moral education
was achieved by a cancer hidden in an infant's bone?
If the world were a school, its pedagogy would disgrace
a savage.

I read theology with the fury of a barrister preparing
an indictment. Augustine appeared to me subtle where he should
have been ashamed; Leibniz, with his best of all possible
worlds, resembled a courtier praising the banquet while beggars froze
at the palace gate. Even the Book of Job, which
had once terrified me by its thunderous beauty, now seemed
to evade the charge. The Lord answered out of the
whirlwind with constellations and crocodiles; but splendour is not innocence.
Orion's belt does not bind a child's wound.

By winter I had ceased to pray. I still attended
church occasionally, partly for our mother's sake and partly because
the old words had the grave loveliness of ruins. Credo
in unum Deum, I would say with the congregation, and
add inwardly: Yes, I believe in Him, as a prisoner
believes in his gaoler.

It was in this condition that the thing occurred.

II

The night was the seventeenth of January. I record the
date because the stable-book confirms that the frost began then,
and because Henderson complained next morning that a nail had
sprung from the east gutter under the weight of ice.
Such trivial corroborations prove nothing, I know; nevertheless, the mind,
when approaching marvels, clings instinctively to invoices and weather.

I had dined alone and remained late in the library.
A north wind moved about the house with those stealthy
variations of pressure which make old timbers answer one another
like uneasy sleepers. The fire had sunk; the green-shaded lamp
illuminated my book, one hand, and a small continent of
red carpet, leaving the shelves above in darkness. I was
reading Seneca—not for consolation, but because the economy of his
despair suited me—when I heard a horse upon the gravel.

At first I supposed that Henderson had been called to
the village. Then I remembered that the stable clock had
struck one nearly half an hour before, and that no
one would ride out at such an hour without informing
me. The hoofs approached slowly, stopped beneath the library windows,
and were followed by the light metallic chink of a
bit shaken in a quiet mouth.

I rose. Before I reached the curtain, I knew who
waited outside.

Do not ask how. I experienced no voice, no perfume,
no premonitory chill; yet the knowledge was complete. The heart
does not always learn by evidence. Sometimes it recognises, as
the eye recognises day.

The moon stood above the cedars, so bright that every
branch had its black duplicate upon the lawn. Beside the
stone steps waited a grey horse, saddled and bridled, its
breath issuing in silver plumes. Harry held the rein.

He wore the rough coat in which he had left
us, the collar turned up, the old leather gloves thrust
beneath his belt. His face was neither luminous nor transparent.
It was Harry's face, leaner than I remembered, and very
still. Yet I never, even for the fraction of a
second, mistook him for a living man. Life has a
minute turbulence, a perpetual flicker of intention under the skin;
he possessed shape and expression, but not that flame. He
was present as a portrait might be present if, without
ceasing to be paint, it looked back at one.

I opened the casement.

"Harry," I said.

"Hullo, Stephen."

That commonplace greeting brought tears to my eyes more swiftly
than any solemnity could have done. He had always said
it in precisely that tone, half affectionate and half amused,
as though my existence were a mildly surprising but agreeable
fact.

"What do you want?"

"To show you something."

"Where?"

"A little way beyond your conclusions."

Even then he could make a phrase sound less like
an epigram than a practical direction. He nodded towards the
grey.

"You must ride."

I had not put on boots since dinner, and I
remember noticing that my velvet slippers were wholly unsuitable. This
concern with costume, absurd as it appears, reassured me. Madness,
I thought, might neglect the weather, but it would scarcely
provide a saddle with the near stirrup lowered to my
length.

I took my greatcoat from the hall, found boots and
a scarf, and let myself out. The cold seized my
nostrils. Gravel cracked beneath my feet with an exactness impossible
to dreams. Harry held the stirrup while I mounted.

"And you?" I asked.

"I have my own way of travelling."

He laid a hand upon the pommel. At once the
grey moved forward.

We passed down the avenue at a walk. Harry kept
beside my right knee, though I could not hear his
tread. The lodge windows were dark, the gate standing inexplicably
open. Beyond lay the familiar lane, whitened by frost, with
pollard willows crowding the ditch; then the hill above Marston,
where the church tower rose like a black finger against
the moon. I looked back once and saw the chimneys
of Sevenhays dwindling between the trees.

"How far?" I asked.

"As far as need be."

"Am I dead?"

"No."

"Are you?"

He smiled. "You have always wanted definitions before evidence."

After Marston the road altered. I cannot tell at what
point. The hedges grew taller, then ceased; the fields widened
into a plain where no fence, house or rick interrupted
the pale grass. The constellations also appeared displaced. The Great
Bear leaned upon the horizon; Sirius burned overhead with the
hard radiance of a sapphire; and low in the east
hung unfamiliar stars, green and crimson, like lamps observed through
deep water. Yet the moon remained our companion, and the
horse cast a shadow.

We travelled without wind, though the grass on either side
bent continuously away from us, as if under the passage
of some vast invisible current. Presently I heard water.

It was no brook or river. The sound came from
all directions, a deep circumfluent murmur, sometimes remote as surf
below cliffs, sometimes close enough to suggest a flood running
beneath the road. I thought of Oceanus, the ancient stream
encircling the habitable earth, father of rivers and boundary of
men's knowledge.

"Do you hear it?" I said.

"Yes."

"What sea is that?"

"The nearest."

This answer, if answer it was, silenced me.

The plain rose by imperceptible degrees. Ahead, where the moonlight
gathered, a wall appeared—first a grey thread, then a cliff
of masonry stretching north and south beyond sight. At its
centre stood an arch so lofty that a cathedral nave
might have passed through it without brushing the keystone. There
were no gates. Above the entrance an inscription had been
carved in characters resembling Greek, though I could not read
them. The letters seemed to alter as I looked: at
one moment flames, at another wounds, and at another merely
the natural fissures of stone.

The grey horse stopped.

"We walk from here," Harry said.

I dismounted. The instant my boots touched the ground the
horse disappeared—not by fading, nor by any visible movement, but
simply by being absent where a moment before it had
stood. Bridle, saddle, steam of breath: all gone. I cried
out.

"He will be waiting," said Harry.

"Where are we?"

"You know already."

I did not. Yet terror, which is sometimes wiser than
thought, had begun to answer for me.

III

We entered the arch.

The passage beyond descended gradually between walls covered from pavement
to vault with sculptured scenes. At first I mistook them
for the conventional punishments of Tartarus: Ixion upon his wheel,
Tantalus beneath the retreating fruit, Prometheus and the vulture. But
these antique figures soon yielded to images more numerous and
more terrible. Here men were broken under carts; there women
crouched in burning houses, holding infants above their heads. I
saw slaves beneath whips, sailors drowning within sight of shore,
prisoners hanging by bound wrists, old people abandoned upon snow,
workers caught in the teeth of machines. There were operating
tables, battlefields, mines, nurseries, madhouses; and among human torments ran
the dumb parallel of the beasts—the ox under the goad,
the fox in iron jaws, the horse screaming amid battle-smoke,
the small bird transfixed upon a thorn.

No sculptor could have carved so much. The wall was
not decorated but inhabited. Each figure moved a little within
the stone. Mouths opened. Fingers clenched. Blood descended through cut
channels and disappeared into gutters beside the pavement.

The smell was not of decay but of iron, sweat,
hot oil and wet wool.

I turned upon Harry. "Is this your Heaven?"

"No."

"Hell, then?"

"Not as you mean it."

At the end of the passage a red light pulsed.
We came out beneath an open sky into an amphitheatre
so immense that its opposite wall was made vague by
distance. Tier above tier ascended until the uppermost seats appeared
to touch the stars. The arena itself was an oval
plain of black sand crossed by drains and shallow conduits.
Around it stood cages, frames, furnaces and instruments whose purpose
I sometimes understood too readily, sometimes not at all. Some
resembled engines of ancient siegecraft; some were as neat and
nickel-bright as devices in a modern laboratory; others had the
obscene simplicity of rope, stone and flame.

The tiers were empty.

At the western end rose a throne upon a platform
of porphyry. It was hidden by curtains of a dark
imperial purple, embroidered with suns, moons, crowns and winged creatures.
Water streamed continually beneath those curtains, descended the steps, and
entered the arena channels. Wherever it passed, the sand changed
from black to white; but farther on fresh blood darkened
it again.

Before the throne moved the tormentors.

They were shaped like men and women, though taller than
common humanity. All wore close black raiment and smooth masks
without eyeholes, nostrils or mouths. Their facelessness was more dreadful
than any expression could have been. An angry face can
be entreated; a delighted face can at least be hated;
but these blank ovals suggested not passion, but office. They
performed their cruelties with the impersonal exactitude of clerks entering
sums in a ledger.

In the nearest cage a mother clung to two children
while a masked figure fed the bars with fire. On
a wheel lay a soldier whose limbs were stretched until
the joints separated. A girl stood in water while, drop
by measured drop, some corrosive liquid fell upon her head.
An old man, naked except for a scholar's chain, was
compelled to watch pages torn from a book and pressed
against his wounds. Farther away I saw multitudes: victims of
plague tossing upon cots; men crushed beneath fallen masonry; a
caravan staggering in desert heat; creatures pursued, trapped, dissected, starved.

And from the whole amphitheatre there arose one sound—not a
collective cry, as from a crowd, but innumerable separate cries
preserved in their terrible singularity. Each "Mother," each "Water," each
"Why?" reached the ear distinctly, yet all existed together. It
was as though time had been flattened into a single
surface and every grief since the first wound cried now.

Above that sound came another: a low, regular vibration from
behind the curtains.

The throne was shaking.

I understood—or believed that I understood. Here, at last, was
the truth my reason had constructed: the universe as spectacle,
agony as imperial entertainment, the hidden Deity enthroned before His
sport. The faceless executioners were natural law, disease, accident, human
malice; behind them sat the Intelligence which had designed both
nerve and knife. The empty tiers were unnecessary because One
Spectator sufficed.

My terror became rage. I ran towards the throne.

Harry caught my arm. His fingers were cold, but not
weak.

"No farther."

"Let me see Him!"

"You cannot."

"I will accuse Him to His face."

"You have done so every night."

The curtains heaved inward, then outward. The platform trembled.

"Look!" I shouted. "He laughs. The whole throne shakes with
it."

Harry's expression altered—not to anger, but to such sorrow that
I stopped struggling.

"Listen better," he said.

I listened. Beneath the cries, beneath the crackle of flames
and turn of wheels, that deep vibration continued. At first
it remained what my hatred had named it: laughter constrained
behind heavy draperies. Then, gradually, another cadence declared itself—the broken
inhalation, the long collapse of breath, the involuntary renewal. I
had heard it beside our father's bed after the doctors
left; I had heard it from our mother when she
believed herself alone.

It was sobbing.

"No," I said.

Harry did not answer.

"No. That is another trick. Pity after permission is not
goodness. A king may weep over prisoners whom he refuses
to release."

"Look at the sufferers."

"I have looked."

"Not at their wounds. At their faces."

I turned towards the burning cage. Smoke had hidden the
mother and children; now the water from the throne reached
the coals, and the air cleared.

The mother's face was the face painted above our village
altar: not the mild, sexless beauty bestowed by sentimental artists,
but a strong Semitic face, dark-eyed and bruised, with blood
upon the brow. The elder child bore the same face.
So did the infant. I looked to the wheel: the
broken soldier had that face. The girl under the falling
acid raised it. The scholar, the plague-stricken labourer, the blinded
horse, even the bird struggling upon the thorn—each, without ceasing
to be individually itself, revealed one countenance.

It was the face of the Son of Man.

The perception did not occur successively, as when one recognises
a resemblance in several portraits. It was instantaneous and universal.
Across the immeasurable arena, in every cage and upon every
bed, under crowns, bandages, fur, scales, rags and armour, the
same living Presence suffered. Diversity was not abolished; the mother
remained a mother, the animal an animal, Harry Harry. Yet
through them all, as flame shines through many windows, one
Victim looked towards the hidden throne.

I fell to my knees.

"Is this the meaning of it?" I whispered. "That He
watches Himself suffer?"

"No," Harry said. "That He suffers whatever He watches."

"But the throne—"

"Listen."

The curtains shook again. Water poured beneath them in a
sudden abundance, flooding the porphyry steps. It entered the channels,
washed the blood from the sand, and spread towards the
lowest wall. There it did not gather or stagnate. It
passed through narrow apertures and went outward, and I heard
beyond the amphitheatre the circumfluent sea that had accompanied our
ride.

"Tears?" I asked.

"Yes."

"His?"

"Yes."

"Around the whole earth?"

"Around all worlds where anything can be hurt."

The catechism was too simple for my resisting intelligence. I
rose unsteadily.

"Then why does He not end it?"

Harry looked towards the curtains. "I was not brought to
show you why."

"That is the only question."

"No. It is the question you used to conceal another."

"What other?"

"Whether pain is borne alone."

A masked figure passed near us carrying a pair of
iron tongs. I recoiled; Harry did not.

"Who are they?" I asked.

"Everything that wounds."

"Are they His servants?"

"Some call themselves so. Some deny Him. Some are merely
laws. The wound is real, whatever name the hand bears."

"And He permits them."

"Yes."

"Then my accusation stands."

"Perhaps. But not as you framed it."

I looked again upon the throne. My rebellion had depended
upon a solitary image: a safe God seated beyond consequence,
contriving the nerves of creatures with which to amuse His
eternity. That image was gone. In its place stood a
mystery more grievous and more profound: omnipotence consenting not merely
that the world should wound itself, but that every wound
should enter the divine life. I could not call this
an explanation. It was not the balancing of an account.
Harry's torn body was not restored; the mother still screamed
in the cage. Yet a certain false arithmetic had been
destroyed. I had counted every pang against God while assuming
that none was counted in Him.

"Did He feel your death?" I asked.

Harry's familiar smile returned, faintly.

"Stephen, I did not feel it without Him."

"Were you afraid?"

"Very."

"Did you call for Mother?"

"Yes."

"Did she hear?"

"No."

"Did He?"

Harry looked towards the streaming throne.

"Yes."

At that word the amphitheatre changed.

The empty tiers, which an instant before had risen in
barren circles to the stars, were filled with figures in
white. There were myriads upon myriads—kings without crowns, soldiers without
weapons, children, ancients, women whose hair fell loose upon their
shoulders, men marked by every race and century. Some I
recognised without knowing that I had ever seen them: Socrates
holding the hemlock cup; Joan in the light of her
fire; a black slave with scarred shoulders; a nameless nurse
from a pest-house; and near the lowest tier, our father,
younger than when he died.

All were bowed towards the arena.

They did not applaud. They wept.

Their tears descended like rain, mingling with the water from
the throne. Where the drops struck cages, the bars became
for one heartbeat transparent. Where they fell upon wounds, the
sufferers lifted their heads. Nothing was undone; yet nothing was
isolated. The whole vast company seemed bound by an invisible
circulation of grief and love, each sorrow entering all, all
sorrow entering One.

Then, very far off—or very near—I heard a cock crow.

The white-robed multitude vanished. The tiers were empty. The masked
figures stood motionless. The red furnaces dimmed, and the moon
appeared above the eastern wall.

Harry touched my shoulder.

"We must go."

IV

We returned through the sculptured passage, but the figures in
the walls were now still. Blood remained in the channels,
though clear water followed it. At the great arch I
expected to find the grey horse; instead there was only
a mist, lying waist-deep over the ground.

"Harry," I said, "shall I remember this?"

"As much as you are able."

"Will I see you again?"

"You see more of me than you suppose."

"I mean like this."

"I know what you mean."

He walked ahead. The mist thickened until the wall behind
us disappeared. I could no longer hear Oceanus, only the
faint dripping of water from leaves. Beneath my boots the
monumental pavement gave place to frozen earth, then gravel.

A gatepost emerged from the vapour.

Upon it stood the stone acorn at the entrance to
Sevenhays.

The lodge-gate, which I had left open, was shut and
fastened. Beyond it the avenue ran homeward under the cedars.
I turned to Harry. He had become indistinct—not transparent, but
difficult to retain in sight, as a star fades when
one looks directly at it.

"Was it true?" I asked.

He laughed softly; this time I knew laughter from sorrow.

"You will spoil everything if you insist on using that
word as though it had only one door."

"Tell me at least that you are safe."

"No one is safe in the way you mean."

"Happy, then?"

His face brightened with that old suddenness which no painter
ever caught.

"More alive than happy. And happier than safe."

He put out his hand. I reached for it, but
between our fingers there seemed to pass a breadth of
cold running water.

"Good-bye, Stephen."

"Harry—"

"Go in. You have no coat for standing about eternity."

It was precisely the sort of thing he would have
said. Then I was alone.

The stable clock struck three.

I found myself not outside the gate but within it,
though I have no recollection of climbing over. My greatcoat
was buttoned wrongly; the hem of my trousers was wet
to the knee. There was mud upon my boots of
a reddish colour unlike the pale clay of our fields.
I preserved some of it in a jar—yes, I anticipated
your practical mind—but by morning it had become ordinary dust,
and Henderson threw the jar away months later, believing it
empty.

In the library the lamp still burned. Seneca lay open
where I had left him. The fire was dead. Upon
the carpet beneath the casement stood a little pool of
water.

That is all the evidence. It is nothing. A physician
might speak of bereavement, exhaustion, auto-suggestion and a somnambulistic excursion
through the grounds. I could not refute him. The amphitheatre
may have been assembled from mosaics, hospital memories, photographs from
the war and those cruel marginal grotesques in medieval manuscripts;
the theology may have been my own mind's desperate compromise
between love and revolt. Even Harry's jokes might have been
preserved by memory as a gramophone preserves a voice without
preserving the man.

I grant every objection.

But no objection alters what followed.

I did not recover faith in the sense of regaining
my former opinions. Those opinions had been too easy. Before
Harry died, I had believed in divine goodness chiefly because
the arrangement of my own life had been agreeable: health,
books, fields, friendship, sufficient money, and the annual miracle of
April. Such belief was gratitude wearing the robes of philosophy.
When the arrangement changed, my philosophy changed with it.

Nor did the vision explain suffering. I remain suspicious of
explanations. To say that pain strengthens character is an affront
to those whom pain destroys; to say that evil contributes
to a higher harmony is to value the symphony above
the broken instrument. I do not know why the child
is sick, why the mine collapses, why cruelty is permitted
its long ingenuity. If wisdom consists in possessing an answer,
I am no wiser than before.

Yet the image of the cruel Spectator has departed. I
cannot summon it, even when the morning paper is worst,
even when I pass the hospital gates and see those
who wait for news. Behind the curtain I hear no
laughter. I hear the sobbing of a sorrow too immense
for human nerves to bear; and beneath the doors of
the world I see the lustral flood, washing but not
denying the blood.

This conviction has not made me cheerful. Serenity is not
cheerfulness, though the two are often confused by people who
have experienced neither despair nor peace. I am still troubled—more,
perhaps, because suffering no longer appears as a philosophical defect
that may be debated at leisure. It is sacred, and
therefore intolerable. If every wounded face contains that other Face,
one cannot pass misery as though it were merely part
of the scenery. The beggar, the condemned man, the exhausted
animal, the tiresome invalid repeating the same complaint: each stands,
however obscured, within that arena.

Perhaps this is why I have taken so active an
interest in the infirmary, which you once attributed to remorse.
It is not remorse. Or not only remorse. I do
not imagine that by bandaging one hand I solve the
ancient contradiction between omnipotence and anguish. I have merely learned
that pity is not an emotion entertained by the fortunate;
it is a current in which God Himself is immersed.
We enter it where we can.

Last Sunday the lesson was the raising of Lazarus. I
had always thought the shortest verse—"Jesus wept"—a tender pause before
the exercise of power. Now it seems to me the
centre of the story. He who would presently command the
dead man to come forth did not, for that reason,
stand dry-eyed outside the grief of Martha and Mary. Foreknowledge
did not cancel tears. Power did not exempt love from
pain.

There are theologians who will arrange this more elegantly. They
will speak of Incarnation, kenosis, communicatio idiomatum; they will distinguish
impassibility from compassion and defend their distinctions in footnotes. I
do not despise them. Words are vessels, and even cracked
vessels carry water. But when the old rebellion stirs in
me, I do not turn to the treatises. I see
purple curtains trembling above a flooded arena. I see every
victim bearing one countenance without losing his own. I hear
Harry answer, with that almost impatient gentleness of his: "I
did not feel it without Him."

Do you remember, Violet, the little stream below Marston wood?
As children Harry and I built dams there with stones
and fern, imagining that we could arrest the water. It
always escaped—under our walls, around them, sometimes straight through—and continued
towards the river with a sound too small to be
called music. Yesterday I walked there. The thaw had swollen
it; brown water hurried between shelves of ice, carrying twigs,
foam and one drowned primrose from some sheltered bank.

For a moment the stream caught the evening light and
became a road of fire. I thought of the water
beneath the throne, passing out through secret conduits to encircle
the earth. I thought, too, that perhaps our griefs are
dams of a kind: laboriously built, fiercely guarded, and always
failing at last before that deeper flood.

Do not suppose I am asking you to believe my
vision. I hardly know what belief in such a matter
means. Believe only this, if you can: no creature's pain
is wholly private; no cry falls into an empty heaven;
and if God permits the wound, He does not preserve
Himself from the blade.

That is less than an answer.

It has been enough.

Stephen

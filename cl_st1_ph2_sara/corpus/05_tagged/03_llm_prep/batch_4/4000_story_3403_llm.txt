# The Fatal Excellence of "His Wife's Deceased Sister"

I propose to relate, with that strict regard for truth
which should distinguish an autobiographical warning from a work of
imagination, the singular calamity which overtook me in the most
prosperous period of my literary life. If my experience shall
restrain one young author from an inconsiderate indulgence in excellence,
or induce one editor to reflect upon the pecuniary consequences
of an extravagant compliment, I shall not have suffered wholly
in vain.

Before my marriage I was a writer of moderate reputation
and still more moderate income. My stories appeared with reasonable
frequency in several magazines of respectable circulation, though never upon
their opening pages, and seldom beneath illustrations which cost the
publishers more than the stories themselves. Editors knew my name,
compositors spelled it correctly, and readers, I had reason to
believe, encountered it without active displeasure. No railway passenger ever
bought a magazine solely because I was announced upon the
cover; but, on the other hand, no passenger, discovering me
in its pages after the train had started, threw the
periodical from the window.

This condition of professional life was not magnificent, but it
was remunerative. I paid for my lodging, my meals, my
ink, and most of my cigars; and if the last
article occasionally exceeded the limits prescribed by prudence, I reduced
the expense of my luncheon with an impartiality which preserved
the equilibrium of my affairs. I wrote tales of sentiment,
tales of adventure, and tales in which sentiment and adventure,
finding themselves accidentally in the same neighborhood, made such terms
as they could. My lovers were faithful, my villains were
sufficiently objectionable, and my old family servants knew more than
the lawyers. The public accepted these persons at their proper
valuation, and I was content.

Then I married Hypatia.

It is unnecessary to explain in detail why this event
changed the complexion of existence. Those who have been happily
married will understand me; those who have not would regard
my explanation as an offensive display of felicity. It is
enough to say that the world, which had previously been
a serviceable but somewhat dusty apartment, suddenly opened windows upon
innumerable gardens.

The sky acquired a more expensive blue. Flowers which I
had passed for years with civil indifference began to address
me personally. Peaches possessed flavors of which horticulturists had apparently
kept the secret, and even the mutton at our modest
table displayed unsuspected tenderness. My cigars improved to such an
extent that I accused the tobacconist of having formerly imposed
upon me, although I was still purchasing the same brand
at the same price.

Hypatia herself was the center and explanation of this transformation.
She was sympathetic without being indiscriminate, cultivated without making knowledge
a form of domestic discipline, and practical in those regions
where my own faculties were inclined to become vaporous. She
admired my profession, which was gratifying; she read my manuscripts,
which was useful; and she believed in my genius, which
was dangerous.

For several weeks after our marriage I wrote nothing. I
could not descend to fiction while living in a reality
so much more agreeable. The people in my unfinished stories
seemed pale and ill-nourished compared with Hypatia. Their conversations were
mechanical, their griefs provincial, and their happiness unconvincing. I put
them aside and devoted myself to existence.

But by degrees the very abundance of my felicity demanded
expression. My mind, which had formerly resembled a plain but
wholesome claret, began to effervesce. Ideas rose within it like
bubbles in champagne, each one reflecting the universe in miniature
before bursting into another. One morning, after Hypatia had placed
a vase of jonquils upon my table and left the
room, I took up my pen.

I wrote throughout the day. I wrote far into the
night. The next morning I continued, and on the evening
of the third day I laid down my pen with
the consciousness—not the hope, not the vanity, but the cold
and solemn consciousness—that I had produced a great story.

Its title was "His Wife's Deceased Sister."

The name may not, to an unprepared mind, suggest the
highest regions of literary art. Neither does the word "Madonna"
fully convey Raphael's achievement.

"Raphael did not invent the word," said Hypatia, when I
afterwards used this comparison.

"That strengthens my position," I replied. "He took an existing
word and associated it permanently with his genius. I have
done the same with a family connection."

Hypatia read the story in silence. Once she smiled, several
times she sighed, and at the conclusion she wept in
a manner which, although distressing to a husband, was intoxicating
to an author.

"It is beautiful," she said. "It is entirely different from
anything you have ever written."

I kissed her, and accepted both statements as compliments.

The first editor to whom I sent "His Wife's Deceased
Sister" returned it with a note expressing regret that recent
arrangements had filled his pages for eleven months. This did
not disturb me. There are men who would reject the
Sistine Madonna because their walls had recently been papered.

The second editor retained the manuscript six weeks, and then
requested an interview. He received me with an eagerness which
was not usual among editors receiving authors. Editors commonly preserve,
even in their moments of enthusiasm, the manner of men
who have just remembered an appointment elsewhere.

"My dear sir," he said, grasping my hand, "you have
done an extraordinary thing."

"I had entertained that possibility," I answered.

"This story will move the country."

"I trust it will not move it beyond the reach
of your circulation."

He did not notice the remark. He was walking about
his office with the manuscript in his hand.

"It has tenderness, humor, restraint, pathos, and that indefinable quality
which compels every reader to remember his own sorrow as
if it had happened to somebody more interesting. We shall
publish it in our midsummer number. We shall illustrate it.
We shall announce it."

His offer of payment was larger than any I had
previously received, although not so large as his enthusiasm had
led me momentarily to expect. I accepted it, returned home,
and informed Hypatia that our future was secure.

The story appeared in July.

Its success was immediate, extensive, and, as events proved, disastrous.
Newspapers printed paragraphs concerning it. Reviewers who had never mentioned
my earlier work discovered that I had long been ripening
in obscurity. Ministers referred to it in sermons; schoolmistresses read
it aloud on Friday afternoons; railway travelers purchased the magazine
at junctions where they had hitherto bought sandwiches. One critic
declared that "His Wife's Deceased Sister" combined the domestic purity
of an English idyll with the emotional amplitude of Lamartine.
I had never read Lamartine with sufficient attention to determine
the justice of this assertion, but I quoted it freely.

Letters arrived from strangers. A widow in Vermont wrote that
the story had enabled her to forgive a cousin. A
man in Ohio wished to know whether the deceased sister
had been drawn from life and, if so, whether I
could furnish her address before death. A lecturer proposed to
adapt the story for the platform, taking all the characters
himself. The publishers ordered additional impressions of the magazine and
sent me a framed copy of the page on which
my name appeared.

For a time I moved within a golden atmosphere. Acquaintances
who had formerly asked whether I was "still writing" now
spoke of my career. Hypatia and I dined twice at
a hotel, and on the second occasion ordered a bottle
of wine whose name the waiter could pronounce. I bought
a new coat. We considered a larger apartment.

In the midst of this prosperity I wrote another story.

It was a good story. I say this without immodesty,
because subsequent events forced me to examine it with a
severity rarely directed by an author against his own offspring.
Its construction was firm, its dialogue natural, its conclusion both
unexpected and just. Before my marriage it would have been
accepted promptly and paid for without protest.

I sent it to the editor who had published "His
Wife's Deceased Sister."

Ten days later it came back.

The accompanying letter was extremely courteous.

"My dear sir," it began, "your new tale exhibits all
the skill which your readers have learned to associate with
your name. It is, in many respects, admirable. But after
'His Wife's Deceased Sister' the public will expect from you
something possessing an equal depth of feeling and an equal
distinction of treatment. We do not think the present manuscript
quite reaches that exceptional standard."

I read this letter to Hypatia.

"It is praise," she said.

"It encloses the story," I replied.

She conceded the material force of this observation.

I sent the manuscript to another magazine. Its editor had
previously bought four stories from me, one of them at
a time when he had more contributors than subscribers. He
returned it with compliments.

"There is much that is clever in this," he wrote,
"but it lacks the poignant charm of 'His Wife's Deceased
Sister.' From the author of that memorable work we have
a right to anticipate something more."

The phrase "a right to anticipate" disturbed me. I had
sold the public one story. I had not, so far
as I remembered, sold it a permanent mortgage upon my
emotions.

A third editor regretted that the tale did not "strike
the profound human note sounded in 'His Wife's Deceased Sister.'"
A fourth considered it "graceful but comparatively external." A fifth
admired it, but felt that my reputation would suffer by
its publication.

I began another story.

While writing it I continually perceived "His Wife's Deceased Sister"
looking over my shoulder. The deceased lady offered no assistance.
On the contrary, she depressed every page by silent comparison.
When I attempted humor, she reproached me with frivolity; when
I introduced pathos, she accused me of imitation; when I
avoided both, she rendered the result insignificant.

Nevertheless, I finished the tale and sent it away.

It returned with a note.

"Your admirers," said the editor, "will inevitably compare this with
'His Wife's Deceased Sister.'"

I had already made that discovery.

During the next six months I wrote seven stories. They
concerned, respectively, a lawyer, a young woman with musical ambitions,
an inheritance, a mistaken umbrella, two brothers estranged by politics,
a shipwreck, and a dog. Under ordinary circumstances this variety
would have protected me from any general accusation; but all
seven manuscripts returned with the same deceased relative attached to
them.

The lawyer lacked the spiritual penetration of "His Wife's Deceased
Sister." The musical young woman did not awaken the emotions
stirred by "His Wife's Deceased Sister." The inheritance, though ingeniously
managed, seemed slight beside "His Wife's Deceased Sister." The umbrella
was amusing, but admirers of "His Wife's Deceased Sister" might
resent it. The political brothers suffered from comparison with "His
Wife's Deceased Sister." The shipwreck, although physically tempestuous, did not
approach the moral force of "His Wife's Deceased Sister." The
dog came back without a letter, but somebody in the
editorial office had written the fatal title in blue pencil
upon the margin.

At first I was irritated; then anxious; finally alarmed. My
income, once modest but regular, disappeared. Fame had enlarged my
expenses at the precise moment when it destroyed my resources.
The new coat remained unpaid for. The larger apartment receded
into the distance, and even our present apartment seemed to
approach us in a threatening manner on rent day.

Hypatia proposed economies. We dismissed the woman who assisted with
the heavier work. We ceased dining at the hotel. I
returned to my former cigars, but found, to my surprise,
that marriage had not permanently improved them. At length I
gave them up altogether, thereby depriving literature of one of
its few dependable stimulants.

Still I wrote. Still the manuscripts returned.

One bleak afternoon, when the sky had the color and
texture of rejected paper, I called upon Mr. Crampton, an
editor who had known me before my elevation. I had
brought him a story which I believed to be one
of my best, excluding, of course, the work which had
permanently disabled my name.

He received me kindly, but the manuscript lay between us
on his desk with the unmistakable appearance of an article
about to be restored to its owner.

"Crampton," I said, "tell me candidly what is wrong with
that story."

"Nothing is wrong with it."

"Then why do you return it?"

"Because it is not extraordinary."

"Must everything I write be extraordinary?"

"Everything bearing your name must now appear so. Your difficulty
is not that this story is worse than your former
productions. It is considerably better than most of them. But
you have taught the public to expect a masterpiece."

"I taught it once."

"The public has a retentive memory where its demands are
concerned."

"Raphael," I said, "was surely not required to paint the
Sistine Madonna every Thursday."

"No," replied Crampton; "but Raphael did not contribute to a
weekly magazine."

This distinction silenced me.

As I left the office, carrying my blameless and unremunerative
story, want actually stared me in the face. It wore,
for the moment, the countenance of a thin man standing
beneath the shelter of the doorway.

I had seen this person occasionally in editorial anterooms. His
clothes were clean where they were not worn through, and
worn through in several places where cleanliness could not assist
them. His beard was gray and irregular, his hat soft
with age, and his eyes possessed the acute yet wandering
expression of a man who has long pursued small coins
through a large world.

"Pardon me," he said. "Are you not the author of
'His Wife's Deceased Sister'?"

I admitted it, although with less satisfaction than formerly.

"My name is Barbel," he continued. "I, too, was once
famous."

There was something in the phrase which made me regard
him attentively.

"Perhaps," he said, "you would care to see what fame
has done for me."

I followed him.

Barbel lived at the top of a house in a
street which had resigned all architectural pretensions. His room contained
a broken chair, a table stabilized by a volume of
parliamentary debates, a stove without fire, and a bed covered
by newspapers pasted together at the edges. Upon the wall
hung a yellow clipping in a cracked frame.

"Read it," he said.

The clipping contained a short paragraph headed:

**THE CONUNDRUM OF THE ANVIL**

Beneath the heading was a joke. It asked why a
blacksmith's anvil resembled a certain statesman, and the answer, which
depended upon three meanings of a verb and one mispronunciation
of the statesman's name, was so admirably compact that, against
my will, I laughed.

Barbel bowed gravely.

"That joke," he said, "was printed in every newspaper in
the country. It crossed the ocean. It was translated into
French, where it ceased to be a joke but retained
its reputation. It appeared upon cigar boxes. Minstrels used it.
A bishop quoted it by mistake in a diocesan address.
For fourteen months I received requests for jokes resembling 'The
Conundrum of the Anvil.'"

"And you supplied them?"

"I attempted to do so. I wrote six hundred and
eleven jokes. Some were good; several were excellent; one caused
my landlady to smile before she understood that I owed
her money. But none was 'The Conundrum of the Anvil.'
Editors returned them with expressions of disappointment. The public, they
said, expected another anvil."

"Could you not write upon anvils?"

"I did. I exhausted the blacksmith's trade. I made jests
about hammers, forges, horseshoes, bellows, tongs, sparks, aprons, and the
moral character of iron. But the original conundrum had arisen
from a unique conjunction of language, politics, and inspiration. It
could not be repeated."

"What did you do?"

"What you see."

He pointed to the table, where a small grindstone had
been fixed upon a wooden support. Beside it lay a
box of unfinished pins.

"I grind pinpoints," he said. "It is delicate work and
poorly paid. Yet it is safer than literature."

He seated himself, took up a pin, and applied it
to the revolving stone. His foot worked a treadle beneath
the table. After a moment he held the pin toward
the window, examined it, and threw it into the stove.

"Why did you discard that one?" I asked.

"It was too good."

I thought I had misunderstood him.

"Too good?"

"It possessed an extraordinary point. If I allowed it to
leave this room, the manufacturer might show it to others.
He might demand that all my pins equal it. My
present industry would then be ruined as completely as my
authorship."

He ground another pin, inspected it, and placed it in
the box.

"That one," he said, "is safely mediocre."

The coldness of the room had affected me, but not
so much as this explanation. I picked up the rejected
pin from the stove. Its point was almost invisible in
its fineness.

"Barbel," I said, "your experience is terrible."

"It is instructive."

"Have you never produced another joke equal to the Conundrum?"

"Once," he replied.

"What became of it?"

"I burned it."

At that moment Hypatia, who had become uneasy at my
prolonged absence and had traced me through Crampton's office, appeared
at the open door. I introduced Barbel and explained his
history. She listened with that earnest sympathy which makes some
women accept the most improbable misfortunes as soon as they
are properly narrated.

Barbel demonstrated his art. He ground three pins. Two were
of a prudent commonness and went into the box. The
third possessed such exquisite sharpness that he and I uttered
an exclamation together.

Without hesitation he snapped it in half.

Hypatia turned pale.

On our way home she was silent for several streets.

"At first," she said at last, "I thought his reasoning
absurd."

"And now?"

"I can still feel that perfect pin."

"So can I," I replied. "It pierced my soul."

When we reached our rooms, she looked at the framed
page containing the opening of "His Wife's Deceased Sister." For
the first time its gilt border seemed to us less
an ornament than a species of confinement.

"You are not going to grind pins?" she asked.

"Not unless we can afford two stones. I should dislike
to see you idle."

"My dear!"

"I spoke only from economic foresight."

That night I dreamed that every editor in America stood
beside an enormous grindstone, sharpening copies of my story and
throwing all my other manuscripts into a stove.

The following morning I visited Crampton again and related Barbel's
history. He knew it.

"Literature contains many such cases," he said. "There was a
poet who wrote one lyric so perfect that thereafter no
editor would accept his longer poems. He now prepares seed
catalogues. There was a dramatic critic who invented a single
brilliant phrase concerning an opera singer; he spent the remainder
of his career trying to say it differently. There was
also a woman who wrote an incomparable recipe for quince
preserves, but her case belongs rather to domestic economy."

"Then there is no remedy?"

Crampton leaned back and considered me.

"There may be one. Your stories are salable. Your name
is not."

"That is a distinction without nourishment."

"Publish under another name."

I stared at him.

"You mean conceal my identity?"

"I mean provide your work with an identity which has
not already performed a miracle. An unknown author is permitted
to be good. A celebrated author is required to be
better than himself."

The proposal offended my literary dignity for nearly half a
minute. Then I remembered the rent.

"What name would you suggest?"

We discussed several. Hypatia's maiden name was too distinguished and
might attract expectations of its own. "Anonymous" had been overused.
At last we selected "Martin Pavey," a combination so respectable,
unobtrusive, and apparently solvent that I felt immediate confidence in
it.

I copied my latest story, signed it "Martin Pavey," and
sent it to an editor who had rejected the same
manuscript under my own name three weeks before.

He accepted it by return mail.

His letter praised the freshness of the treatment, the natural
feeling, and the promise displayed by a new writer. The
payment enclosed was not extravagant, but it could be converted
into groceries without difficulty.

I laughed for ten minutes. Then I became indignant. Then
I laughed again.

Hypatia framed the acceptance, though she placed it in our
bedroom, where visitors would not ask inconvenient questions.

Under the name of Martin Pavey I rapidly recovered my
position. Stories which had been condemned as unworthy of the
author of "His Wife's Deceased Sister" were welcomed as remarkable
productions from a newcomer. Editors encouraged me. One advised me
not to imitate celebrated writers but to preserve my own
simple manner. Another predicted that if I continued to improve,
I might eventually attain the emotional power of the author
of "His Wife's Deceased Sister."

I thanked him.

The arrangement was admirable. My real name retained its glory
because nothing inferior appeared beneath it; my assumed name earned
money because nothing glorious had yet appeared beneath that. I
kept the two reputations in separate compartments, as a careful
traveler packs medicine apart from confectionery. Under one name I
was immortal and unpaid; under the other, obscure and comfortable.

Our domestic prosperity returned. We paid for the coat. I
resumed cigars, though of a temperate quality. We moved, not
into the larger apartment once contemplated, but into one with
a second room which Hypatia declared would be convenient should
we ever require a nursery.

In due time we required it.

The birth of our son was an event of such
magnitude that all former happiness appeared merely preparatory. I had
believed, upon marrying Hypatia, that the capacity of the heart
was fixed and that mine had been filled. I discovered
that the heart, unlike a rented apartment, can enlarge without
notice from the landlord.

The child was not, during his first weeks, remarkable in
the ordinary accomplishments of society. His conversation lacked sequence; his
hours were irregular; he received distinguished visitors without opening his
eyes. Yet these deficiencies did not lessen my admiration. His
smallest finger seemed to me a triumph of design. The
movement of his eyelids possessed more dramatic interest than most
five-act plays, and when he first grasped my thumb I
experienced emotions for which no publisher had ever offered an
adequate rate per word.

Again the world changed color—or rather, colors which had been
softened by custom were newly varnished. Morning light upon the
cradle became a celestial phenomenon. The creaking of the nursery
floor acquired tenderness. The future, which had previously extended before
me as a line of editorial engagements, opened into radiant
avenues populated by schoolboys, university honors, public offices, and grandchildren.

For a fortnight I wrote nothing. Then, late one evening,
while Hypatia and the child slept, I entered my study
and sat before a blank sheet of paper.

An idea came to me.

I resisted it.

The idea remained.

It was simple, profound, tender, humorous, and dangerous. I recognized
the symptoms. The intellectual atmosphere around me grew rare and
sparkling. Common words approached in ceremonial order and arranged themselves
with an ease which I distrusted. Incidents developed without labor.
Characters spoke before I had determined what they should say,
and spoke better than I could have instructed them. The
whole story moved toward its conclusion with the dreadful serenity
of a river approaching a cataract.

I tried to make it worse.

I inserted an unnecessary description of a garden wall. The
story absorbed it and became richer. I introduced a minor
person with a comic defect; he instantly became human and
indispensable. I prolonged a conversation beyond the limits of art,
but the added sentences revealed the innermost hearts of the
speakers. Every fault I attempted was transmuted into merit.

At dawn I wrote the final line.

I did not read the manuscript immediately. I walked to
the window and looked down upon the empty street. A
milk wagon passed. Somewhere a servant opened shutters. The ordinary
world was beginning its ordinary day, unaware that my livelihood
stood in mortal peril upon the desk behind me.

When Hypatia entered, carrying our son, she found me still
at the window.

"Have you been writing?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Is it a Martin Pavey story?"

"I do not know."

She placed the child in his cradle and read the
manuscript.

Her face, as she proceeded, underwent those changes which an
author watches with a concentration unknown to science. She smiled.
She ceased smiling. Once she pressed the page against her
breast before turning it. At the end she bowed her
head and wept.

I needed no further judgment.

"This story," she whispered, "is as beautiful as 'His Wife's
Deceased Sister.'"

"No," I said.

"It is."

"It cannot be."

"It is greater."

I seized the manuscript and read it from beginning to
end. Parental exaltation had not deceived me. There was no
loose construction, no false emotion, no sentence which desired removal.
The story possessed precisely that indefinable quality which editors profess
to seek and punish whenever they find it.

This story WAS as good.

For several minutes neither of us spoke.

At length Hypatia said, "What shall we do with it?"

"Nothing."

"But it is your finest work."

"Exactly."

"Could it not be published under your own name? The
editors would be delighted."

"For the moment. Then every story I wrote afterward would
be compared not merely with 'His Wife's Deceased Sister,' but
with this. My disabled reputation would receive a second and
perhaps fatal injury."

"Then publish it as Martin Pavey."

I looked at her in horror.

"And ruin him? Martin Pavey supports us. He pays the
rent. He buys the child's milk. If this appears under
his name, editors will demand another like it, and in
six months we may have two celebrated authors in the
family and not an income between them."

Hypatia clasped the child more closely.

"We cannot risk that," she said.

I laid the manuscript upon the desk. Its pages seemed
to emit a pale, destructive radiance. Here was fame, distinction,
perhaps immortality—and behind them I saw Barbel bending above his
little wheel while the perfect pinpoint glittered for an instant
before he broke it.

"Burn it," said Hypatia faintly.

I approached the stove.

But I could not do it. Barbel had destroyed his
second great joke, but paternal inspiration was involved in mine,
and I felt that burning the story might symbolically scorch
the child. Besides, an author may possess prudence without being
capable of absolute virtue.

"We shall preserve it," I said, "but in such a
manner that it cannot injure us."

That afternoon I purchased a stout tin document case with
a lock. I placed the manuscript inside, wrapped in oiled
paper, locked the case, and sealed the key in an
envelope.

On reflection this appeared insufficient. Tin may rust; envelopes may
be opened; human resolution is notoriously vulnerable to flattering letters
from publishers. I therefore placed the tin case inside a
small iron cash-box, which I locked with a second key.

Hypatia observed that a cash-box could be carried away.

The next morning we visited a locksmith and ordered a
steel chest large enough to contain the cash-box. The locksmith
asked whether we wished protection from fire or burglary.

"From publication," I replied.

He looked at me, then at Hypatia, and recommended his
strongest pattern.

When the chest arrived, we placed the cash-box within it.
I added a written declaration stating that the enclosed manuscript
must not be opened during my lifetime, nor during Hypatia's
if my death should leave her financially dependent upon Martin
Pavey. The steel chest was locked, chained, and deposited in
the cellar beneath a pile of broken furniture.

Still I felt uneasy about the keys.

There were three of them: one for the tin case,
one for the cash-box, and one for the steel chest.
I soldered them together, carried them to the river, and,
after looking carefully around to make certain that no publisher
followed me, threw them from the bridge.

They flashed once in the sunlight and disappeared.

That evening I returned to my desk and began a
new story for Martin Pavey. It concerned a grocer, an
aunt, and a misunderstanding about six oranges. It had no
profundity, awakened no universal sorrow, and contained nothing which a
bishop was likely to quote. When I read it to
Hypatia, she smiled in several appropriate places but did not
weep.

"Do you think it is good?" I asked.

"Quite good."

"Exceptionally good?"

"No."

I kissed her with gratitude.

The story was accepted, paid for, and forgotten. Since then
I have written many others of the same safe and
useful quality. Our son has grown; our household is comfortable;
and Martin Pavey enjoys an esteem which is cordial but
not incendiary. My own name remains associated with "His Wife's
Deceased Sister," concerning which occasional critics still write as though
its author had died immediately after producing it. In a
professional sense, perhaps he did.

The manuscript in the cellar has never been disturbed. After
my death, when rent, food, cigars, and editorial expectations can
no longer inconvenience me, it may be opened by force
and given to the world. The critics may then declare
it superior to "His Wife's Deceased Sister." The public may
demand another. Editors may address their requests to my grave.

I shall at last be in a position to disregard
them.

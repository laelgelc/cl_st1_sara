By five in the morning Boori Ma had already declared
war on the mites.

She dragged her bedding from beneath the letterboxes, shook it
once beneath the yellow bulb, and carried it upstairs under
one arm. The bedding consisted of three newspapers, a cotton
sack split along one seam, and a square of quilt
whose stuffing had migrated into hard islands. Mites had settled
in the remaining territory. All night they had traveled over
her ribs and bitten the soft parts behind her knees.

"Once," she announced to the sleeping building, "my mattresses were
stuffed with carded silk. Every winter they were opened, cleaned,
scented with sandalwood, and stitched again by six women. Six.
Believe me, don't believe me."

No one answered. Behind the doors of the second floor,
families lay under ceiling fans and dreamed of school fees,
tram delays, and fish prices.

The building stood in a narrow north Calcutta lane where
drains remained wet even in April. Its plaster had fallen
away in shapes resembling continents. Several windows lacked glass. The
collapsible gate leaned to one side, and the stairs turned
sharply around a central shaft through which quarrels, cooking smells,
and babies' cries rose and sank all day. On the
roof, rainwater collected in blackened tubs. On the ground floor,
beneath four dented letterboxes, Boori Ma slept.

Officially, she belonged to no flat. Unofficially, she belonged to
the stairs.

She swept them twice a day with a long reed
broom, beating dust from each landing and pushing down bottle
caps, fish scales, peanut skins, hair, dead ants, flakes of
plaster, and the silver paper from sweets. She knew which
milkman watered his cans and which boy stole coal from
the courtyard. She intercepted salesmen, beggars, census clerks, wandering sadhus,
and cousins who could not remember the surnames of the
people they had come to visit. When deliveries arrived she
shouted upward. When children returned from school, she inspected their
faces for mischief.

"Wipe your feet."

"Do not swing from that railing."

"Who gave you permission to climb with guavas in your
pockets?"

Her voice was tart as tamarind and carried cleanly to
the roof.

Around her waist she tied the loose end of her
sari, and inside its knot she kept a ring of
skeleton keys. The keys opened nothing in the building. Most
were long and black, with ornate teeth and oval bows,
keys made for cupboards wider than the alcove where she
slept. Whenever anyone questioned her authority, she shook her hip
so that they clattered.

"These opened my almari," she would say. "Teak, not sal
wood. Three shelves for saris, two for winter shawls, one
only for attars. The lock came from Lucknow. Believe me,
don't believe me."

At other times the keys had belonged to a granary,
a courtyard gate, a room where silver hookahs stood in
rows, or a warehouse by a river whose name changed
with the telling.

So she garbled details. She contradicted herself. She multiplied servants
and reduced sons, moved a zamindar from one side of
her family to the other, and described marble floors in
a district where no one believed marble had ever been
laid. The residents had long ago stopped correcting her. What
difference did it make whether she had owned forty goats
or none? She had crossed the border after Partition with
a fever, a broom, and the clothes she wore. Her
husband had vanished. Her daughters had vanished. Her land, if
there had been land, was elsewhere.

Her stories took the place of receipts.

That morning she spread the bedding on the roof. Dawn
had turned the city the color of watered milk. Crows
hopped along the parapet. From nearby kitchens came the first
smells of kerosene, ginger, and onions hitting hot oil.

Mrs. Dalal, who had come up to gather yesterday's laundry,
found Boori Ma beating the quilt against the wall.

"What are you doing? You'll scatter vermin everywhere."

"The vermin are already everywhere. They have eaten me like
a festival meal."

Mrs. Dalal examined the quilt without touching it. She was
a soft-faced woman with polished toenails and a habit of
speaking kindly when no one else was present.

"This must be thrown out."

"Thrown out? Once my bedding was so fine that a
rose petal could bruise it."

"Then let the rose petals sleep on this."

Mrs. Dalal lifted the quilt with two fingers and dropped
it over the parapet. It landed in the lane, startling
a dog.

Boori Ma stared down. "What am I to sleep on?"

"When Mr. Dalal receives his promotion, we are going to
Simla. I will bring you a blanket. A thick one.
Also perhaps a cotton mattress."

"You promise?"

"I promise."

"A blanket with a satin border?"

"A useful blanket."

"In my house we had seven blankets for guests alone."

"Yes, yes."

"From Kashmir. One blue as a kingfisher. One embroidered with
almond flowers."

"Believe you, don't believe you," Mrs. Dalal said, laughing.

Boori Ma laughed too, though her eyes remained on the
lane. A bicycle passed over one corner of the quilt.
By noon it would be black with mud.

Three days later Mr. Dalal came home with his promotion.

The news entered the building before he did. His office
peon arrived carrying a box of sweets. Then Mr. Dalal
appeared in a taxi, wearing a new shirt and an
expression of formal modesty. The residents assembled on the stairs.
Children shouted. The wives accepted sandesh and immediately began estimating
the increase in his salary.

That evening Mr. Dalal made two announcements. First, he and
his wife would take a holiday in Simla. Second, before
they left, he intended to install a washbasin on the
first-floor landing.

"A basin?" said Mrs. Bose.

"With a tap," said Mr. Dalal.

"Running water?"

"When water runs."

The building possessed taps inside no flat. Water was stored
in drums, buckets, brass vessels, kerosene tins, and enamel pots.
Baths took place behind kitchen doors. Teeth were brushed over
drains. The idea of a porcelain basin in the common
stairwell produced a silence more respectful than the promotion itself.

"Changing times," pronounced Mr. Chatterjee, who had lived on the
top floor for twenty-two years and interpreted every event as
evidence of changing times.

Mr. Dalal described the basin as modest. He had selected
a pale green one, imported in appearance if not in
fact, with a white rim and two chromium knobs. It
would be useful to all residents. Guests could wash their
hands. Children could rinse ink from their fingers. The building
would gain distinction.

"Is the stairwell a bathroom now?" asked one of the
wives.

"It is an improvement," said Mrs. Dalal.

"Who will keep it clean?"

Everyone looked toward Boori Ma.

"I will keep it cleaner than your own teeth," she
said. "Once we had basins carved from stone, with taps
shaped like swans. Hot water from one beak, cold from
the other. Believe me, don't believe me."

The mason arrived the next morning with two laborers. They
chipped into the wall, raised dust, cursed the pipes, borrowed
a hammer from the Boses, and stepped repeatedly over Boori
Ma's newspaper bed. For two days the stairwell smelled of
cement and heated metal. The water had to be shut
off. The residents climbed around coils of pipe and bowls
of gray mortar, complaining as if Mr. Dalal had personally
invented inconvenience.

Boori Ma tried to maintain order.

"Who are you?"

"Plumber."

"You were a plumber yesterday. Today you are carrying bricks."

"They are for the plumber."

"Show me."

"Move aside, grandmother."

No one had previously called her grandmother. She did not
like it from strangers.

At last the basin was fixed to the wall. It
projected over the first landing like a pale green tongue.
Mr. Dalal wiped it with his handkerchief. Mrs. Dalal placed
a cake of pink soap in a wire dish. Water
coughed from the tap, rust-colored at first, then clear.

The residents came out to admire it.

Mrs. Bose washed her hands unnecessarily. The children opened and
closed both knobs. Mr. Chatterjee bent to inspect the drain.

"Times do not merely change," he said. "Sometimes they improve."

Before the week ended, the basin had become an insult.

The wives asked why one family's prosperity should hang in
a common passage. The husbands replied that no law prevented
others from prospering. Mrs. Bose observed that her sitting room
curtains were older than Mrs. Dalal's marriage. Mrs. Misra pointed
out that the Chatterjees' shutters had not been painted since
Independence.

"Is it beyond us to improve our own homes?" she
demanded.

"No one said it was beyond us."

"It has been implied."

"By whom?"

"Must every implication have a signature?"

One husband ordered new cane chairs. Another hired a carpenter
to build an almari into the wall. The Boses purchased
a carom board large enough to occupy half their sitting
room. Mr. Chatterjee, after condemning wasteful competition, engaged painters for
his shutters and chose a blue so bright that neighbors
across the lane came to their balconies to stare.

Then rumors began.

The Dalals, someone heard, had bought a sofa in Simla.
No, a refrigerator. No, a radio with foreign stations. Mrs.
Dalal was ordering shawls, silver bangles, and a tea service
with twelve cups. She had remembered Boori Ma's blanket. She
had forgotten it. She was bringing one mattress. She was
bringing two.

Boori Ma repeated whichever version reached her last.

"They are bringing me bedding thick as a merchant's account
book."

"Who told you?" asked Mrs. Bose.

"Mrs. Dalal herself."

"She has not returned."

"She told me before leaving."

"You said yesterday it was a quilt."

"A quilt, a blanket, a pillow. Why should she bring
one thing when she can bring three?"

All day the building admitted strangers. Carpenters arrived with planks
balanced on their heads. Painters carried tins and ladders. A
man selling door handles measured every door but received no
order. Electricians pushed wire into cracked walls. A glazier replaced
two panes, broke one, and promised to return. Porters delivered
cane chairs to the wrong floor. The carom board became
wedged at the turn of the stairs until six men
lifted it upright.

Boori Ma could not question them all. Whenever she demanded
a name, a resident shouted from above, "Let him through."
Whenever she tried to close the gate, someone needed it
open.

Dust returned faster than she could sweep it. Paint drops
hardened on the steps. Nails hid in the corners. Her
newspaper bed was trampled, folded, and once carried away with
debris.

"You people will lose the whole building," she cried. "You
let in anyone with a brush."

"We know what we are doing."

"Then tell that man not to spit paan by the
drain."

"He is Mrs. Misra's carpenter."

"He is a pig with a measuring tape."

The carpenter laughed.

At night her knee swelled until the kneecap disappeared. Pain
chewed slowly through the joint. She rubbed it with mustard
oil and tried to sleep beneath the letterboxes, but workers
arrived before sunrise and residents returned after midnight. The collapsible
gate shrieked open and shut. Plaster dust settled on her
tongue.

She moved to the roof.

There she made a bed of newspapers beside a water
tank. She weighted the pages with bricks and kept her
broom under one arm. Her hair, which she twisted into
a knot no larger than a walnut, collected white dust.
The roof was quieter, but it gave no shelter from
the weather.

The monsoon arrived badly fitted, like a boy stumbling in
his elder brother's sandals. Rain slapped one parapet, missed the
next, paused for half an hour, then returned sideways. Boori
Ma crouched beneath a sheet of tin. The newspapers softened
and printed headlines against her sari. Her knee throbbed. Wet
mango leaves clogged the roof drain, and each morning she
cleared them with her fingers.

Below, the building continued to improve.

Walls changed color. Shutters shone. Old furniture descended the stairs
on laborers' backs, followed by new furniture wrapped in sacking.
The residents spoke of varnish, invoices, brass fittings, delivery dates.
No one asked Boori Ma to inspect the men. No
one complained that she was not sweeping often enough. Her
absence from the entrance had become convenient.

Only the children visited.

"When is your blanket coming?"

"When the Dalals return."

"What color?"

"Red, with golden parrots."

"You said blue."

"They are bringing two."

"Can I sleep on one?"

"You cannot even wipe your feet."

One afternoon, after three days of rain, the newspapers beneath
her gave off a smell like spoiled yogurt. Boori Ma
gathered them and dropped them into the courtyard. She descended
slowly, gripping the rail. On the first landing, the green
basin gleamed beneath a film of construction dust. Someone had
left a paintbrush soaking in it.

She removed the brush and washed her face. Cold water
ran over her eyes, down her neck, and into the
front of her sari.

"You see?" said Mrs. Bose, passing with a tray. "Mr.
Dalal's basin is useful to everyone."

"Once I bathed in rosewater warmed over charcoal."

"You should ask your rosewater to cure your knee."

"Forty jars were stored in a cellar. Pewter lids. Labels
written in Persian."

"Of course."

"Believe me, don't believe me."

Mrs. Bose continued upstairs.

Boori Ma remained bent over the basin. For a moment
she watched water circle the drain. The plumbing made a
hollow swallowing noise in the wall.

The Dalals had been gone nearly a month.

Restlessness entered Boori Ma's limbs. She began walking in the
afternoons, first to the end of the lane, then farther.
She carried the broom as a staff. In her sari
knot, along with the skeleton keys, she kept the small
money she had collected from errands and festival tips: folded
notes, warm coins, a tram token she mistook for a
coin.

The markets gave her something to patrol.

She passed pyramids of limes, heaps of green chilies, baskets
of silver fish whose gills opened and closed in the
heat. Sellers shouted prices. Tram bells struck the air. Rickshaw
wheels cut through mud. At a stall of secondhand clothes,
she fingered a wool shawl with three moth holes.

"How much?"

"Eight rupees."

"For this rag? In my house rags were given to
servants to polish brass."

"Then buy from your house."

She moved on.

At another stall, glass bottles of attar stood in a
row. The seller uncorked one and touched its stopper to
her wrist. The scent was sweet, medicinal, and so dense
that it seemed to contain a room.

"Jasmine," he said.

"False jasmine."

"You have a better nose?"

"I had a garden."

"Then smell your garden."

She held her wrist near her face until the scent
dissolved into sweat.

Each day she walked farther. She told herself she was
waiting for the Dalals' taxi, which might enter by the
main road. She told herself movement loosened the knee. She
told herself she was searching for bedding at a fair
price, in case Mrs. Dalal returned empty-handed.

At home, no one noticed when she left. The gate
stood open for deliveries. The residents had become accustomed to
strange feet on the stairs.

One afternoon the rain stopped and the city steamed. Boori
Ma entered a crowded market near Sealdah where tarpaulins sagged
over the lanes and drops fell from their edges onto
shoulders below. She paused before a vendor frying lentil cakes.
Oil hissed. Hunger tightened her stomach.

"Two," she said.

While she counted coins, someone struck her lightly from behind.

"Move, move," said a man's voice.

A basket pressed against her hip. A child squeezed between
her and the stall. The vendor folded the cakes into
newspaper. Boori Ma paid, stepped aside, and ate one standing
beneath a striped awning.

Then she heard no rattle.

She shook her hip again.

The knot at her waist had loosened. The end of
her sari hung empty. Her money was gone. The skeleton
keys were gone. Even the tram token was gone.

She searched the mud around her feet. She overturned vegetable
leaves with the broom. She questioned the vendor, the child,
a woman selling combs, a porter with a basket, a
blind singer seated against a post.

"My keys," she cried. "A ring this large. Iron keys.
And money."

"Go to the police."

"The police will eat the rest of my day."

"What do your keys open?"

"My house."

"Where is your house?"

She looked toward the station, where the crowd entered and
emerged beneath the clock. "Across," she said.

"Across what?"

But she had already moved away.

She spent two hours retracing her path. The jasmine seller
had not seen the keys. The shawl seller told her
not to disturb his customers. A constable advised her to
tie money inside her blouse next time. Clouds gathered again,
turning the afternoon purple.

By the time she reached the building, rain had begun
to stipple the lane.

A crowd stood at the entrance.

The residents had assembled beneath umbrellas and along the stairs.
The children were silent. Two policemen occupied the first landing.
On the wall, four raw bolt holes surrounded a rectangle
of cleaner plaster.

The green basin was gone.

Only the pipe remained, bent downward, dripping onto the floor.

Boori Ma stopped inside the gate. Water fell from her
hair and nose. In one hand she held the broom.
In the other she held the uneaten lentil cake, mashed
inside its paper.

"There she is," said someone.

Mrs. Bose came down two steps. "Where have you been?"

"My money was stolen."

"The basin has been stolen."

"My keys also."

"What keys?"

Boori Ma reached for her sari knot. Her fingers closed
on wet cloth.

Mr. Chatterjee cleared his throat. "This is a grave matter."

"Ask the workers," Boori Ma said. "Ask the men you
let in."

"The workers finished yesterday."

"Then ask yesterday."

"A witness saw two men carrying the basin out."

"Who?"

"The sweet-shop boy."

"Did he stop them?"

"He assumed they belonged here."

"You see? Everyone assumes. Everyone enters."

Mrs. Misra pushed forward. "And where was the woman who
calls herself our gatekeeper?"

"I went to market."

"You go every day now."

"My bedding is wet. My knee is bad. The Dalals
are late."

"What have the Dalals to do with the basin?"

"They are bringing my blanket."

A few residents exchanged looks. Above them, water tapped from
the cut pipe with the patience of a clock.

One policeman asked, "Does she work here?"

No one answered immediately.

"She stays here," said Mr. Chatterjee.

"She sweeps," said Mrs. Bose.

"She watches the gate," said another.

"She was supposed to watch it," said Mrs. Misra.

The policeman turned to Boori Ma. "Did you notice suspicious
persons?"

"All persons are suspicious until they say whom they have
come to see."

"Did you see anyone?"

"I told them. I said strangers were coming. Painters, carpenters,
men with ladders, men with wire, men to measure handles,
men to mend what they themselves had broken."

"That is not an answer."

"My money was stolen," she repeated. "My keys."

"How much money?"

She named a sum larger than what she had carried.

Mrs. Bose gave a short laugh. "Yesterday she said she
had seven rupees."

"Seven folded notes."

"You said rupees."

"Notes are rupees."

"And now?"

"More. There were festival gifts."

"When did you receive them?"

"Before."

The residents' faces changed. Not greatly. A mouth tightened. An
eyebrow rose. Doubt passed among them more efficiently than a
message shouted up the stairwell.

"She lies about everything," Mrs. Misra said.

"I do not lie."

"You had marble floors."

"I did."

"You had servants carrying rosewater."

"They carried brass pitchers."

"You see? Already the story changes."

"Believe me, don't believe me," Boori Ma said, but the
phrase came out too quickly. It did not command the
stairwell. It fell beside the dripping water.

Mr. Chatterjee folded his arms. "There is a question of
complicity."

"What complication?" she demanded.

"Complicity. Assistance. Information given to thieves."

Boori Ma struck the floor once with her broom. "I
gave no one information."

"How else would thieves know the basin could be removed?"

"It was hanging on the wall for the whole lane
to admire."

"How would they know when the entrance was unwatched?"

"The entrance has been unwatched since you filled it with
workmen."

This accusation offended them. The improvements had been undertaken legally,
paid for privately, and admired publicly. To suggest that new
shutters, cupboards, paint, chairs, and a carom board had invited
disorder was to confuse advancement with carelessness.

Mrs. Bose spoke more gently. "If your money was stolen,
why did you not come straight home?"

"I searched."

"For hours?"

"For my keys."

"Keys to what?"

Boori Ma opened her mouth. No list came. No teak
almari, no granary, no silver room, no courtyard gate. The
empty knot at her waist was visible to everyone.

The policemen asked further questions, wrote nothing, and left. A
stolen basin did not interest them as much as the
residents had hoped.

Afterward the building held a meeting in the second-floor corridor.
Boori Ma stood below, near the mark on the wall.
They did not invite her up. Their voices descended in
pieces.

Responsibility.

Negligence.

Reputation.

Safety.

Property values.

A building with improved shutters and cane furniture, they agreed,
required improved protection. There were now possessions worth taking. Today
a basin, tomorrow an almari. Today plumbing, tomorrow jewelry. A
uniformed durwan would record names, lock the gate, sleep at
the entrance, and carry a stick.

"Changing times," said Mr. Chatterjee.

The phrase settled the matter.

Two wives came to the roof for Boori Ma's things.
There was little to collect: damp newspapers, a cracked cup,
a mustard-oil bottle, two blouses, a comb missing half its
teeth, and a tin box containing thread, betel nut, a
photograph blurred by water, and three safety pins. They carried
everything downstairs in a broken basket.

"This is all?" one asked.

"My bedding is coming."

Neither replied.

The residents deposited the newspapers and clothing in the lane.
The oil bottle fell and rolled beneath a parked handcart.
A boy rescued the tin box before it entered the
drain.

Boori Ma stood at the gate with her broom. She
looked upward through the stairwell. Faces occupied every landing, some
stern, some embarrassed, some merely curious.

"I guarded this building," she said.

"No one denies you swept," said Mrs. Bose.

"I guarded it."

"You left the gate."

"You filled the gate with strangers."

"You should have told us."

"I told you."

"You tell us many things."

The children watched from behind the railings. One of them
began to cry and was taken inside.

Boori Ma lifted the loose end of her sari and
shook it. Nothing sounded.

The gesture was small, almost private. Without the keys, her
hip moved beneath an empty fold of cloth.

"Believe me," she said. "Believe me."

Mrs. Misra looked away. Mr. Chatterjee examined the wet patch
spreading beneath the severed pipe. Someone remarked that the wall
would have to be repaired before the Dalals returned. Someone
else asked whether the soap dish should also be removed,
in case thieves came back for it.

At last Mr. Chatterjee opened the gate.

Boori Ma bent to pick up the tin box. Then
she changed her mind. She took only the broom.

Rain had thinned to a gray mist. The lane shone
with trampling feet and reflected windows. Behind her, the gate
collapsed shut with its usual metallic complaint.

She walked toward the main road, placing the broom ahead
of her like a staff. Her swollen knee resisted each
step. The residents remained at the entrance until she turned
the corner.

Then they went inside to discuss wages for a real
durwan.

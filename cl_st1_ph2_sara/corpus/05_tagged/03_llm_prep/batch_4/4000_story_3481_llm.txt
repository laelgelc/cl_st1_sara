In the house of Saradasankar, zemindar of Ranihat, Kadambini had
no one whom the law or the priest would call
her own. Her husband had long ago departed to that
country from which letters do not arrive; she had borne
no child; and of her father's people neither roof nor
welcome remained. Yet the human heart is a stubborn householder.
If one door is sealed, it finds another room in
which to light its lamp.

Saradasankar's little son had been left almost from birth to
Kadambini's care, for his mother, being frequently ill, possessed the
dignity of motherhood more securely than its daily burdens. Kadambini
fed the child, bathed him, bore his blows and kisses
with equal gratitude, watched his sleep, and learned the meaning
of every wrinkle upon his small forehead. No one called
her mother; nevertheless, she performed all the duties for which
that name is the world's poor payment.

Perhaps the child was dearer to her because he was
not hers. Property is guarded by right; a gift is
guarded by fear. Every smile from him seemed lent, every
embrace subject to recall. Thus her affection had in it
the humility of worship and the anxiety of theft.

One night in the month of Sravan, while the rain
descended without interval and the swollen ponds had forgotten their
banks, Kadambini sat in an inner room warming milk for
the boy. The lamp burned dimly. From the courtyard came
the steady fall of water from the eaves, like fingers
drumming upon a closed door.

Suddenly she pressed one hand upon her breast.

"Sister," she called to the child's mother, "bring him to
me. I feel—"

The sentence did not finish. Her eyes widened as if
an invisible darkness had entered the room and spread before
her. A little lamp, when extinguished, leaves the walls unchanged;
but when consciousness goes out, the letters of the world's
book are blotted at once. Kadambini fell.

The household awakened to confusion. Women cried out, servants ran
in opposite directions, and a physician was summoned through the
rain. He came, felt her pulse, listened, waited, and at
last shook his head with that grave economy by which
learned men conceal both certainty and doubt.

There was no movement in the heart. The breath had
withdrawn beyond detection.

Since there had lately been rumours of cholera in the
neighbourhood, Saradasankar's people were unwilling to keep the body until
morning. A death within the zenana is sorrow; a suspicious
death is also inconvenience. Four Brahmin servants were therefore ordered
to carry Kadambini at once to the burning ground and
complete the rites without delay.

The burning ground lay far from the village beside a
dried river-bed which in the monsoon became a chain of
black pools. There had once been a river there, and
men still called the place a ghat, as men preserve
the titles of ruined families after their lands are gone.
A great banyan stood beside a tank; beyond it stretched
wet fields, low bushes, and the darkness.

Near the place was a mud hut used by those
who attended cremations. The four men set down the bier
inside it. Wood had been promised from the estate, but
wood, like most things promised by an estate, had not
come.

Two of the attendants remained with the body, while the
other two went to discover what had delayed it. The
rain had weakened to a fine, cold drizzle. The lamp
in the hut flickered twice and expired.

"Have you matches?" asked Nitai.

Gurucharan searched his cloth. "They are damp."

"Then relight it from your pipe."

"My tobacco is damp also."

Both men sat in darkness, listening to frogs. From the
tank came a ceaseless chorus, vigorous enough to suggest that
all the dead had been reborn with green bodies and
excellent lungs. Crickets shrilled in the grass. Every now and
then a gust pushed rain through the doorway.

After some time Nitai said, "If they had brought wood
promptly, we should now be home."

Gurucharan sighed. "At home there would be tobacco."

Silence followed. A cremation ground at midnight is a poor
place for conversation, because every commonplace remark appears to invite
an uncommon answer.

Then the cot creaked.

The two men held their breath.

Again there came a sound—a long, low sigh from the
direction of the bier.

Nitai seized Gurucharan's arm. Gurucharan did not complain, for he
was already seizing Nitai's. Something moved upon the cot.

Neither man invoked reason. Reason is a town gentleman who
seldom visits a burning ground after dark. They cried, "Ram!
Ram!" and fled through the mud, leaving lamp, corpse, rites,
and dignity behind them.

When they met their companions upon the road, carrying a
lantern and explaining at length that the woodcutters had been
asleep, they reported that the body had vanished.

"Vanished?" said Bidhua.

"It sighed," whispered Nitai.

"It rose," said Gurucharan.

"It may now be behind us," added Nitai.

At this all four looked behind. Then, with admirable unanimity,
they resumed their journey.

After much debate they decided that the matter must not
be told precisely as it had occurred. A zemindar might
forgive cowardice less easily than a ghost. They returned near
dawn and declared that the cremation had been accomplished. When
questioned about the delay, each supplied details so plentiful that
no two agreed. Since ashes do not answer inquiries, the
household accepted their account.

But what had truly happened in the hut?

Kadambini's heart had not ceased for ever. Life had drawn
inward, like a frightened creature into its burrow, and after
some hours it returned. She opened her eyes to perfect
darkness.

At first, I fancy, she supposed herself still in her
room. She called faintly, "Sister."

No one answered.

The smell of damp straw came to her. Wind passed
over her face. She reached out and touched the rough
edge of the cot. Memory returned by fragments: the milk,
the pain, the sudden blackness.

Then lightning whitened the doorway. For an instant she saw
the tank, the banyan, the desolate field, and the low
roof beneath which she lay. She knew the place. It
was the burning ground.

A certainty entered her mind more terrible than fear.

"I am dead," she thought.

She did not question why her body could move, why
her lungs drew air, or why the mud was cold
beneath her bare feet. We believe ourselves alive not because
we have tested the matter philosophically, but because others address
us, need us, quarrel with us, and make room for
us at meals. Kadambini had awakened where the living had
abandoned her. Therefore she concluded that she belonged to the
dead.

She stepped outside.

The two attendants had fled before she fully rose, and
she saw nothing of them. The rain-washed night stretched without
boundary. In life a widowed woman cannot cross a courtyard
without permission; in death the whole world stood open, and
there was nowhere she might go.

At first this freedom almost intoxicated her. No veil was
required before darkness. No elder could rebuke her. No custom
had jurisdiction beyond the House of Yama. She walked across
the flooded fields, sometimes sinking to her ankles, sometimes to
her knees. Thorny shrubs caught her cloth; mud streaked her
arms; rain loosened her hair.

Yet freedom without belonging is merely exile with a larger
sky.

Again and again the boy's face rose before her. She
heard him calling "Auntie!" in the impatient tone with which
children summon those whose love they never doubt. She took
two steps toward Ranihat, then stopped.

How could she enter? Men and ghosts dread one another.
The living fear that the dead will cross the boundary;
the dead, perhaps, fear that no door will open to
them. On opposite banks of the river of death they
call out, each mistaking the other's shadow for terror.

Toward dawn Kadambini reached a road. The clouds thinned, and
the grey shapes of trees returned to the world. A
farmer drove cattle from a shed; women came with brass
vessels toward a pond; a child cried for food. Daylight
restored society, and society made her afraid.

A traveller found her standing beneath a tree, exhausted and
covered with mud. From her speech and bearing he understood
that she was a respectable woman, though her condition contradicted
respectability in every visible particular.

"Mother," he said, "where is your home?"

Kadambini could not name Saradasankar's house. That had been her
home while she lived; a dead woman had no claim
there. After a long silence she remembered her childhood companion.

"Take me," she said, "to the house of Sripati Babu
at Nisindapur. His wife is Jogmaya."

The traveller, being a good man and therefore somewhat inquisitive,
asked no fewer than twelve questions. To none did he
receive a satisfactory reply. Nevertheless, he arranged for her journey
and sent a servant with her.

Jogmaya had not seen Kadambini since the early days of
marriage. In girlhood they had shared sweets, secrets, quarrels, and
reconciliations; and in later years they had exchanged letters full
of assurances that distance could not weaken affection. Such assurances
are often most abundant where distance has already done so.

When Kadambini arrived, Jogmaya stared for a moment, then embraced
her.

"My dear! Why did you send no word? What has
happened? Why are you dressed so? Have they mistreated you?"

Kadambini lowered her eyes. "Do not ask me of that
house. Give me a corner here. Let me work as
your servant."

"As my servant!" cried Jogmaya. "You are my friend."

Sripati entered at the sound of voices. He was a
mild man with a compassionate heart, though Jogmaya sometimes suspected
that his compassion grew especially alert in the presence of
distressed women. Seeing Kadambini's downcast face and ruined clothing, he
concluded that she had suffered cruelty among her husband's people.
He asked nothing and ordered that a room be prepared.

This delicacy pleased Kadambini but irritated his wife.

When husband and wife were alone, Jogmaya said, "You showed
great concern."

"Should I have shown cruelty?"

"You might at least have remembered that she came to
see me."

"I remembered it perfectly."

"Then why did you look at her as if she
had crossed seven deserts to seek your protection?"

Sripati smiled. "She did look as though she had crossed
several."

A woman may invite her husband to pity her friend,
but she does not always forgive him for obeying too
readily. This is not hypocrisy; it is domestic geometry, in
which the shortest distance between two hearts is seldom a
straight line.

For some days Kadambini remained quiet. She performed little tasks,
but seemed always to be listening for a sound beyond
the walls. She would stop in the middle of folding
cloth and gaze at her hands. Once, seeing herself in
a mirror, she gave a cry.

"What is it?" asked Jogmaya.

"My shadow—"

"You have seen it every day of your life."

"Not since my life ended."

Jogmaya laughed, then ceased, for Kadambini did not.

At night the guest would awaken screaming. She feared solitude,
yet the presence of others gave her no peace. Once,
long after midnight, she came to Jogmaya's chamber and beat
upon the door.

"Sister, do not leave me alone."

Jogmaya opened it angrily. "What nonsense is this? You alarm
the whole house."

Kadambini stood with joined hands. "Give me a place upon
the floor. I shall make no sound."

There was such naked terror in her face that Jogmaya's
irritation changed for an instant to pity. But pity cannot
indefinitely share a bedroom, especially when neighbours and servants have
begun to talk.

The servants declared that Kadambini's feet made no sound in
the corridor. The cook swore that he had seen her
pass through the courtyard while she was at that very
moment in the women's room. A maid, surprised by her
near the water jars, dropped a plate and afterward maintained
that the plate had leapt from her hand of itself.
Every household produces mysteries in proportion to the number of
servants available to explain them.

Kadambini heard these whispers. They strengthened what they feared.

She thought: "I have no hunger as they have, though
she ate when food was placed before her. I have
no sleep as they have, though weariness closed her eyes.
I possess memory, but memory may remain in the dead
as writing remains upon a page after the writer has
gone. I love the boy—but perhaps love is the passage-money
carried by souls into the silent land."

Her own existence became an empty room in which every
thought returned as an echo.

Jogmaya finally told Sripati, "This cannot continue. Whatever quarrel drove
her from Ranihat must be settled. People will speak."

"They already speak," said Sripati.

"That is why it must be stopped."

He agreed to visit Saradasankar's estate and make inquiries. Kadambini
watched him depart, and though no one had told her
his purpose, she understood. A judgement was being sought from
the world of the living.

Sripati returned two days later, soaked by rain and burdened
with certainty. It was nearly ten at night. Jogmaya had
waited impatiently, but on seeing his expression she became uneasy.

"Well?" she asked.

"You have made a grave mistake."

"I?"

"The woman here cannot be your friend Kadambini."

Jogmaya's fear instantly took the safer form of indignation. "I
know my childhood friend better than you do."

"I do not dispute your eyesight. I only tell you
what I learned. Kadambini died at Ranihat."

"Nonsense."

"The household carried her to the burning ground. The date
is known."

"Then they carried someone else."

"Her death occurred the night before the woman arrived here."

Jogmaya calculated upon her fingers, miscounted, began again, and triumphantly
produced the same result.

"You must have misunderstood."

"I spoke with Saradasankar himself."

"Men hear half a sentence and bring home three conclusions."

"And women recognise a face after twenty years and consider
inquiry an insult."

Thus the question of whether a dead woman occupied their
guest room became entangled with the older question of whether
husbands or wives are more often wrong. On this second
subject both possessed inexhaustible evidence.

Their voices rose. Rain beat against the shutters. A gust
entered through a gap, and the lamp went out.

At that moment the door opened.

Kadambini stood upon the threshold.

In the darkness her white garments were scarcely visible; her
face appeared when lightning filled the room, pale, sorrowful, and
strangely calm.

"Sister," she said, "I am indeed Kadambini. But I am
no longer living."

Jogmaya shrank against her husband.

Kadambini came one step nearer. "I have done no wrong
to you. If I have no place among the living,
and no place among the dead, tell me—whither shall I
go?"

No one replied.

"Whither shall I go?"

Her cry seemed to pass through the house and out
into the rain. Then she turned and fled.

Sripati followed after recovering from the paralysis which benevolent men
experience when benevolence requires physical courage. But the courtyard gate
stood open, and Kadambini had disappeared into the storm.

She did not know where she went. For a day
and a night she wandered, hiding from villages in daylight
and moving beneath the rain after dusk. Hunger troubled her,
but she called it an old habit of the body.
Her feet bled, but what were wounds to a ghost?

On the second evening she found herself near Ranihat.

The familiar trees seemed to recognise her. Beyond them stood
Saradasankar's house, its upper windows lit. There, behind walls from
which death had erased her name, lay the child.

Desire is less obedient than fear. Kadambini waited until the
household retired, then covered her face and entered by a
rear passage. A servant saw a veiled woman and assumed
that she belonged to the zenana. In great houses, confidence
is often a better passport than identity.

She hid in a dark storeroom. From the neighbouring chamber
came women's voices. The child had been ill since her
departure; fever had made him weak and fretful. His mother,
recovered sufficiently to attend to cards, sat with her widowed
sister-in-law and a neighbour. The maid had been left with
the patient.

Kadambini heard him murmur in sleep.

"Auntie."

That one word broke the gates of death.

She entered the sickroom. A small lamp burned beside the
bed. The boy lay thin and flushed, one arm above
his head. Kadambini knelt and gazed at him. Here was
the face which had been the scripture of her days.
Every eyelash was known to her; every breath commanded her
more absolutely than priest, custom, or fear.

The boy stirred and opened his eyes.

"Auntie," he said, without surprise, "give me water."

Kadambini poured water into a cup and lifted him. He
drank from her hand as he had done a hundred
times. Then he rested his head against her breast.

At that touch a great confusion within her became simple.
The dead might remember love, but could they satisfy thirst?
Could their arms support this warm, living weight? The child
had not asked whether she belonged to this world. He
had needed her, and need had recognised her.

She kissed him.

He looked carefully into her face. "Did you die, Auntie?"

"So they say, darling."

"Have you come back?"

"Yes."

"Then don't die again."

Kadambini began to weep. Tears fell warm upon his forehead.
For the first time since awakening in the cremation hut,
she knew—not reasoned, not hoped, but knew—that she was alive.

The maid entered carrying gruel. She saw Kadambini with the
child in her arms, screamed "Ghost!" and fell senseless. The
bowl shattered.

The card-players rushed in. The child's mother stopped at the
doorway. Her face emptied of colour; then she too collapsed,
for terror is contagious where affection has been neglected.

Saradasankar came running.

Kadambini laid the boy gently down and turned to him.

"Brother," she said, "why are you afraid? I am not
dead."

Saradasankar folded his hands. His lips trembled. "Kadambini, have mercy.
The funeral rites have been performed. Do not cling to
maya, the illusion of earthly affection. The child is ill.
Your presence will harm him. Go in peace."

"I am alive."

He backed away.

"I was not dead. They were mistaken."

She seized a brass bowl from the table and struck
it against her forehead. The rim cut the skin. Blood
ran down her temple and across her cheek.

"Look!" she cried. "I bleed. Ghosts do not bleed. See—this
is warm blood. I am alive!"

But evidence has little power over terror. A bleeding ghost
is not, to the frightened mind, a living woman; it
is merely a ghost with an additional horror.

The women wailed. Servants crowded beyond the door, invoking Ram.
Saradasankar continued to plead, "Depart! Bless the child and depart!"

Only the boy reached toward her.

"Auntie," he cried, "don't go."

Kadambini looked from him to the others. A moment before,
love had restored her to life; now society thrust her
out of it again. She had crossed the river, but
those on the bank denied that she stood among them.
Her blood, her hunger, her pain, her tears—none was an
acceptable document. A widow's life had never been entirely her
own; now even its existence required witnesses, and the witnesses
refused.

She laughed once. It was not the laughter of madness,
though all who heard it afterwards described it so. Perhaps
there are truths which, finding no place in speech, take
refuge in laughter.

"I am not dead," she said.

Then she ran from the room.

The storm had broken afresh. Rain poured into the courtyard,
overflowed the drains, and beat the pond into a white
turmoil. Kadambini crossed the veranda and descended the steps.

Behind her voices shouted. Some called for her to stop;
others begged the ghost to depart. No one came near.

She reached the old well beside the outer wall. For
an instant she stood upon its stone rim, her garments
streaming, the blood on her face washed thin by rain.

"Whither shall I go?" she whispered.

The child's cry came faintly from the house.

Kadambini lifted her head. If life could not receive her
and death had rejected her once, she would force the
two to agree.

She threw herself into the well.

There was a heavy sound below, then only rain.

Men came with ropes and lamps. This time no heart
revived; this time no breath hid beyond the physician's search.
Before dawn they drew her body from the black water,
and all who saw it accepted at last that Kadambini
had been a living woman.

Thus, by truly dying, she established the fact that she
had not been dead before.

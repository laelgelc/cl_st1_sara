## Jimmy Raises the River

There are two rules in driving logs. The first is
to keep them moving. The second is the same rule
spoken louder.

A river boss may save a bridge, soothe a farmer,
respect a dam, and avoid breaking Sunday quiet if he
can do these things without delaying the drive. But the
logs must go down. They represent stumpage, wages, winter provisions,
bank interest, mill contracts, and the opinions of men who
sit in dry offices. A pine stick hung for six
hours on a gravel bar may be only a pine
stick to you; to the owner it is money refusing
to travel.

Jimmy Hurd understood this.

He was a compact man, square across the shoulders, with
black hair, a short black beard, heavy brows, and skin
so pale that his face had the effect of a
black-and-white mask. In fair weather, rain, shouting, and disaster, he
smoked the same little pipe and looked upon the river
with the same steady attention. Men sometimes mistook his stillness
for slowness. They did not repeat the mistake after working
under him.

That spring Daly came down from the city in a
checked suit and a pair of shoes which became muddy
before he reached the cook camp.

"The contract says the first sticks must be at the
mill in twenty days," he announced.

Jimmy sat on a chopping block outside the wanigan, filling
his pipe. The wanigan was the drive's floating storehouse, office,
bunk room, kitchen, and refuge from civilization. Beyond it the
river spread brown and muscular between banks still patched with
snow.

"Twenty," repeated Daly. "Not twenty-one. Not twenty and a fraction."

Jimmy pressed the tobacco with one finger.

"You ordered the rear crew out of the upper branch?"
he asked.

"Certainly. Every man available."

"How many million?"

"Eight and a half."

Jimmy struck a match on the block.

Now eight and a half million feet of timber will
make a considerable procession. First the rollways must be broken,
and the logs that have slept all winter on the
iced slopes must be started into the freshet. Then the
rear crew follows, clearing every cove, island, shoal, and backwater.
Other men ride the banks, break jams, fend logs from
bridge piers, and keep the drive from winging out—that is,
from spreading so widely in slack water that it ceases
to be a drive and becomes a lumberyard. Every bend
has its own temper; every ledge has teeth; every dam
demands sluicing. And spring water, like a man with money,
is plentiful chiefly when you do not need to count
upon it.

"Twenty days is close," Jimmy said.

"It can be done."

"Might."

"It must."

Jimmy's match burned down near his fingers. He shook it
out and drew twice on the pipe.

Daly began to walk back and forth. "There's another concern
after this contract. If we fail, they get it. You
understand what that means."

"Yes."

"It means the mill may stand half the summer. It
means—well, it means we've got to do it. I don't
care how you handle the drive. Hire more men. Run
nights. Blow jams. Tie right into her and keep her
hustling. Only get the logs out."

"Peaceably?" asked Jimmy.

Daly stopped.

"Of course peaceably. I don't want claims and lawsuits."

Jimmy looked at the river.

"Get them out peaceably if you can," Daly amended, "but
get them out."

Jimmy nodded.

The drive started next morning.

For nine days the weather and the river behaved better
than either had promised. The rollways thundered. Great piles loosened
stick by stick, trembled, settled, and then plunged together, bounding
down the skids with a noise like continuous artillery. Logs
struck the river endwise, dived, shot up streaming, and joined
the brown multitude below.

The men followed with peavies, those long poles shod with
spikes and swinging hooks by which a river driver lays
hold of timber too heavy for ordinary argument. They ran
logs, sprang from stick to stick, waded icy coves, and
ate wherever the cook could overtake them. At night their
wool clothing steamed around the stove. Before dawn they were
out again.

McGann, who led the rear crew and possessed an unfailing
gift for discouragement, viewed each day as a personal insult.

"She'll hang over thim twinty days," he told Jimmy on
the sixth evening. "The upper rollways is slow, the east
branch is spread from bank to bank, an' there's two
thousand sticks hidin' in the alder swamp."

"Get them," said Jimmy.

"We're gettin' them."

Jimmy smoked.

McGann waited for sympathy, instructions, or prophecy. Receiving none, he
went away muttering that a wooden image would be warmer
company.

By the tenth day the head of the drive had
passed Oxbow Bridge. By the twelfth it had gone through
the Narrows, after a jam there had grumbled, bulged, and
broken under two charges of dynamite. The logs poured out
on the white water, shouldering one another like cattle through
a gate.

By the fifteenth day even McGann admitted that they had
a chance.

Then the river dropped.

It did not fall dramatically. Rivers rarely consult men about
the proper appearance of a catastrophe. The frost went out,
the high feeders dwindled, and the brown flood lost inches
by night. Stones showed black in the channels. Gravel bars
lifted long gray backs into the sun. The water that
had raced became a calm velvet rush over shallows.

Below Loon Bend, five miles above the town, the logs
began to strand.

First a few grounded at the heads of bars. Others
caught against them. Then scores, hundreds, thousands came pressing down
from behind. The men heaved and pried and pushed. They
cut side channels, built little wing dams, rolled logs by
hand, and dragged them with horses. Each stick freed made
room for three more to settle.

The river murmured and snarled and eddied back among them.

By noon the drive lay for two miles like wearied
cattle resting in a road.

Daly arrived in a hired buggy at four o'clock. He
climbed down, saw the timber, and forgot the mud on
his shoes.

"What's this?"

"Shallows," said Jimmy.

"I can see they're shallows!"

Jimmy sat on a log with his pipe. Behind him
men splashed waist-deep, shouting to one another while peavies clanked
and logs rolled reluctantly.

"The head end should be at the power dam by
now," Daly said.

"It isn't."

"We have four days!"

"Three and part of one."

Daly stared at him. "Can you get them through?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Tonight."

The owner looked over the immense litter of timber. Here
and there narrow lanes of water showed through it. Elsewhere
the logs lay three deep, stranded, plugged, caught, and tangled.

"Tonight!" Daly cried. "Why, there isn't water enough to float
a wash tub."

"No."

"Then how?"

Jimmy's pale face did not change.

"I'll clear the shallows tonight."

Daly lowered his voice. "What will it cost?"

"Something."

"How much?"

"Don't know."

"Men? Dynamite? Teams?"

"Something," Jimmy repeated.

Daly had employed Jimmy long enough to recognize the end
of a conversation. He pulled out his watch, snapped it
open, and looked at a face which could give him
no comfort.

"Remember," he said, "I authorized no foolishness."

Jimmy nodded.

"And no damage."

Jimmy nodded again.

"Above all, no interference with the power company's property. They've
been waiting two years to catch us at something."

"Yes."

Daly climbed into the buggy. "But get those logs out!"

When he had gone, Jimmy called McGann.

"How many men have had sleep?"

"Sleep?" McGann repeated. "None of us has had sleep since
Candlemas."

"Pick forty. Send them to camp. Supper and bunks. Don't
wake them till eleven."

McGann scratched his red whiskers. "An' lave the rest of
us to lift the river bodily?"

"Keep working."

"What'll the forty do?"

"Sleep."

McGann made a sound indicating that the management of log
drives had fallen into incompetent hands. Nevertheless, he selected forty
good men and sent them back to the wanigan. Jimmy
worked the remaining crew until darkness. Then he ordered supper,
set double watch on the stranded timber, and disappeared inside
the cook tent.

At eleven the forty sleepers were kicked awake. They came
out sour, stiff, and astonished. Hot pork, beans, bread, and
coffee were given them. Jimmy stood near the stove, smoking.

"Bring axes, augers, sledges, crosscut saws, and peavies," he said.
"No lanterns after we reach town."

McGann's eyes widened.

"Town?"

Jimmy looked at him.

"Sure," said McGann. "Town. A natural place to drive logs."

They marched shortly before midnight, keeping to an old tote
road among the spruce. The night was cold and moonless.
Meltwater gleamed in the ruts, and branches brushed wetly against
their shoulders. No man spoke above a murmur. Jimmy went
first, a dark, square shape beneath the stars.

The town lay below the power dam. Its principal street
possessed a drugstore, two saloons, a hotel, a bank, a
Methodist church, and three policemen who protected these institutions according
to a schedule. Arc lamps hung over the intersections. Their
current came from the dam.

This dam crossed the river in a low wall of
stone and timber. In its center were sluice gates through
which logs could pass when water permitted. Along the crest
ran sockets intended for flashboards—planks set edgewise to raise the
head of water in seasons when extra power was required.
The power company owned the dam, the sockets, the flashboards
stacked in a locked shed, and a vigorous dislike of
lumbermen.

Jimmy halted his crew in the trees above town.

"McGann, take ten to the shed. Bring every flashboard."

"The shed's locked."

"Yes."

"Ah," said McGann.

"Bates, fifteen men to the west end. Nolan, fifteen east.
Fit the boards tight. Brace them. Work quiet."

"What if the watchman comes?" Bates asked.

"Explain."

"How much explainin'?"

"Enough."

The men separated.

A padlock objected briefly to McGann's axe. Then the flashboards
came out—heavy planks banded with iron, designed to stand against
the push of a rising river. Men carried them two
at a time along the damp grass. Others lowered them
into the crest sockets, clamped them, spiked braces, and drove
wedges under the timbers.

The dam changed its voice.

At first the water slipped over it with a wide,
even whisper. As board after board was set, that whisper
narrowed and deepened. The river recoiled from the new barrier.
It curled, murmured, and crept backward upon itself.

The watchman appeared carrying a lantern and wearing one boot.

"Here!" he called. "What are you doing?"

"Power company work," McGann told him.

"I am the power company watchman."

"Then ye ought to know."

The watchman raised the lantern. He saw forty men with
axes and peavies. He also saw Jimmy standing motionless on
the dam.

"You can't put those in!"

"They're in," said Jimmy.

"I'll fetch the police."

"All right."

The watchman ran toward town, still wearing one boot.

The last flashboard dropped into place at one twenty. The
men drove the braces home. Water gathered above the dam,
smooth, black, and immensely patient. Inch by inch it rose
against the new planks. It covered stones, swallowed bars, and
stole upstream under the stranded logs.

Three miles above, the first tier stirred.

A grounded pine lifted one end, rolled, and bumped its
neighbor. A dozen moved. Then a hundred. The broad, motionless
fields of timber trembled as though waking. Channels opened; logs
slid into them; heaps loosened with wet grunts and crashes.
The held-back river reached beneath the drive and took up
its burden.

At two o'clock the town policeman met the watchman on
Main Street.

"The river's going backward," the watchman gasped.

"Rivers don't."

"This one is."

The policeman went to the bridge and looked. He observed
that the water appeared unusually high and that a great
deal of hammering came from the dam. However, the bridge
was in his district and the dam was not. He
therefore returned to investigate an unlocked stable.

At half past two the river entered the cellar of
the hotel. At two forty it wet six tons of
coal belonging to the Methodist minister. At three it came
through the basement windows of the hosiery factory and lifted
several packing cases with a gentle impartiality.

Citizens began to wake.

Lights appeared. Men shouted from porches. Women in wrappers carried
jars, carpets, and preserves upstairs. A fire bell rang, though
there was no fire. Then somebody discovered the flashboards, and
the population moved toward the dam.

By that time the logs had reached it.

They came first in scattered ranks, black in the darkness.
Then the main drive appeared, filling the river from bank
to bank—a moving floor of pine and spruce, dipping, grinding,
and pressing toward the sluices. Jimmy had opened the log
gates. His men stood on the booms and aprons with
peavies, guiding the sticks into the chutes.

The timber shot through. Logs plunged nose down, vanished in
white turmoil, and leaped out below like animals breaking cover.
The dam shook beneath the weight of water. Spray flew
thirty feet.

The chief of police arrived with both his patrolmen and
most of the town.

"Who's in charge here?" he demanded.

Every driver pointed at Jimmy.

The chief climbed onto the dam. "Did you order these
boards put in?"

"Yes."

"You're under arrest."

"All right."

"Come with me."

"When the logs are through."

The chief looked at the boiling sluices. "You come now."

Jimmy took the pipe from his mouth.

"If I go now, the drive jams at the gates.
If it jams, the boom goes. If the boom goes,
you'll have logs through every bridge and mill between here
and the state line."

The chief looked downstream. The town's main bridge did not
appear capable of meeting a million logs socially.

"How long?" he asked.

"Two hours."

"This is outrageous!"

"Yes."

"You've flooded half the business district."

"Cellars," Jimmy corrected.

"The hosiery factory has three feet of water in its
packing room."

Jimmy glanced at the river. "Shouldn't keep hosiery in a
cellar."

The chief's mouth opened. Then a heavy log swung crosswise
above the gate. Jimmy sprang onto the wet apron, drove
his peavy hook into the timber, and leaned. Two men
joined him. The log turned slowly, caught the current, and
shot between the stone cheeks. A dozen followed, rattling like
wagons over a bridge.

The chief waited.

He could do nothing else. An arrest is a firm
expression of government, but it loses dignity when the prisoner
must first save public property.

Dawn came gray over a flooded town. The river shone
like fluid steel. Barrels floated in alleys. A henhouse moved
majestically across a market garden. The owner of the hotel
stood knee-deep in his cellar and spoke of Jimmy's ancestry.

At the dam the men worked without pause. They heaved
and pried, shoved and shouted. Whenever the sluice mouths clogged,
they ran out over the trembling sticks and broke the
tangle before it could harden. A jam begins innocently: one
log catches, a second crosses it, and a third pins
both. In ten seconds there may be fifty; in a
minute, five hundred; in another minute, pressure enough to tear
out masonry. The business is to reach the key log
before the jam learns its strength.

Jimmy seemed always to know which log was the key.

At six fifteen the tail of the drive appeared. It
was no majestic body now, but a ragged sweep of
scattered timber, bark, driftwood, and foam. McGann's rear crew came
with it, wet to the shoulders and triumphant.

"She's clean!" McGann shouted. "Every stick off the shallows!"

Jimmy nodded.

The last logs entered the sluice. When the final brown
end dipped out of sight, Jimmy ordered the gates lowered
and the flashboards removed. The men knocked away the braces.
One by one the planks came free, and the impounded
river roared over the crest in a shining wall.

The water began to fall in the town.

Jimmy wiped his peavy, set it against the rail, and
approached the chief.

"Ready," he said.

The chief had been waiting so long that the announcement
seemed almost discourteous.

"You admit responsibility?"

"Yes."

"You broke into company property, interfered with the dam, endangered
the public, and caused extensive flooding?"

"Yes."

"Have you anything to say?"

"The logs are through."

Jimmy spent that day in the town lockup. He slept
very well.

Meanwhile the drive swept downstream on the released water, passed
the lower rapids before noon, and entered the mill boom
late the following afternoon. It arrived with four hours to
spare.

The hearing took place two days later. The courtroom was
crowded with factory officers, hotel men, cellar owners, reporters, river
drivers, and citizens who had never before enjoyed the spectacle
of a man accused of stealing three feet of river.

The power company's lawyer spoke for forty minutes. He described
rights, hazards, precedents, property, civic order, and the sanctity of
incorporated capital. The hotel owner described twelve ruined mattresses. The
Methodist minister described his coal. The hosiery superintendent displayed a
damp pasteboard box.

Jimmy sat beside his attorney, though he had no need
of one. His black brows remained level. His pipe, being
forbidden in court, rested unlighted in his hand.

When asked for his plea, he stood.

"Guilty," he said.

The judge peered over his spectacles.

"You understand the gravity of this offense?"

"Yes."

"You deliberately raised the river?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"To float the logs."

A subdued pleasure passed through the rear benches.

The judge frowned it down. "The court imposes a fine
of five hundred dollars and costs."

Jimmy took an envelope from his pocket. It contained five
hundred dollars in new bank notes, together with a little
more for costs. Nobody in the room asked how a
river boss, whose wages had recently been advanced to his
family, happened to possess that particular sum. When you reflect
upon it, five hundred dollars is large money for a
workingman and small money for the delivery of eight and
a half million feet of pine.

Outside the courthouse Daly waited among the reporters.

His checked suit was immaculate. His expression was severe.

"Jimmy," he said loudly, "I am astonished. Utterly astonished. My
orders were explicit. You were to bring the drive through
without trespass, damage, or illegal interference of any kind."

"Yes, sir," said Jimmy.

"You exceeded your authority."

"Yes, sir."

"You have placed the company in a most embarrassing position."

"Yes, sir."

"I cannot approve such conduct merely because it happened to
produce a successful result."

"No, sir."

The reporters wrote rapidly. Daly drew himself up as one
who had defended law, property, and good business practice at
personal cost.

Jimmy put his pipe between his teeth.

"Mr. Daly," he asked meekly, "did the logs get to
the mill on time?"

Daly hesitated only the fraction of a second.

"They did," he said.

Jimmy's pale face remained solemn. The black beard, black brows,
and still eyes preserved the mask. Then, so slowly that
none of the reporters could afterward swear to it, one
eyelid descended and rose.

Daly turned away to conceal an interest in the courthouse
steps.

And the river, three feet lower and entirely innocent, went
on about its business.

# The Counsel's Flute

I can remember the court as plainly as I remember
the face of a man I killed.

The fog had come up from the river before dawn
and thrust itself through every chink and passage of the
old assize-house, so that the candles burned like bleared yellow
eyes and the great black beams overhead appeared to swim
in smoke. Wet cloaks steamed upon the benches; boots shuffled
on the flags; somewhere behind me a woman coughed without
ceasing; and from the public gallery there descended that low,
hungry murmur which belongs to crowds gathered for a hanging.
They had not come to hear justice done. They had
come to see Gabriel Foot condemned.

I stood in the dock with my hands upon the
rail. There was a turnkey at either shoulder. One had
eaten onions for breakfast, and the other carried gaol-fever in
the folds of his coat—or so I fancied, having slept
three months among men who spoke of the gallows as
sailors speak of the weather. The judge's scarlet robe glimmered
through the vapour. His gossan wig seemed to me a
white bird sitting upon his skull.

Below him sat Mr. Ambrose Quill, my barrister.

He was a broad, ruddy man of fifty, with pale
brows, a neat mouth, and such an air of comfortable
good humour that no honest person would have trusted him.
His hands were small and beautifully kept. During the last
hour of the trial he had employed them in saving
my neck. Now, while the jury shuffled back into court,
he trimmed a quill with a little silver knife.

"Gentlemen," said the clerk, "are you agreed upon your verdict?"

"We are."

My mouth was dry, though my shirt clung wet between
my shoulders.

"How say you? Is the prisoner at the bar guilty
or not guilty?"

The foreman looked at me. He was a tallow-chandler named
Peake, to whom I had once sold a lame mare
as sound. For that reason alone I expected no mercy.

"Not guilty."

There was a stir like wind passing over dead grass.

Not guilty.

The woman behind me ceased coughing. A fellow in the
gallery hissed. Someone else laughed. The turnkey smelling of onions
took his hand from my arm, and I felt the
absence of it as a man feels the sudden loosening
of a rope.

Then Mr. Quill looked up.

He did not smile. He merely turned the freshly sharpened
quill between finger and thumb and regarded me across the
court. His eyes rested upon mine for no longer than
the time required to draw breath.

Yet in that glance I read everything.

The jury had acquitted me. The judge must discharge me.
The law had finished with Gabriel Foot.

Ambrose Quill knew me guilty.

A cold fell upon my heart which the fog had
never put there.

I had borne the witnesses well enough: Mrs. Prowse, who
heard me quarrelling with the jeweller; the ostler, who swore
there was blood upon my sleeve; the constable, who found
Mr. Venn's broken watch in the ditch below my lodging.
Quill had torn each of them to tatters. He made
Mrs. Prowse admit she was deaf in her left ear,
the ostler confess himself drunk, and the constable agree that
any child might have thrown the watch into the ditch.
He had spread doubt before the jury as a conjurer
spreads cards.

But he knew.

I cannot say by what movement of his face I
understood it. There are looks which have no name. A
dog gives one before it bites; a sick man gives
one when the physician lies to him; a woman gives
one when she has forgiven you with her lips and
condemned you in her heart. Quill's look told me that
he had found the truth and hidden it under his
gown until the verdict was safe.

Then he bent again to his quill.

"Prisoner," said the judge, "you have escaped a grave charge—"

I heard little more. I was discharged through a side
door into a town that had already hanged me in
its mind.

The fog hung above the roofs like a shroud. Bells
were striking four, though the afternoon had the darkness of
evening. I went first to the Crown and Mortar because
I had sixpence in my pocket and a thirst that
seemed capable of drinking the river dry.

The tap-room had been my house before the arrest. I
had thrown dice there, sung there, fought there, and more
than once slept beneath the settle when the landlord's wife
was too charitable to kick me into the street. But
when I entered, talk stopped as sharply as if I
had fired a pistol.

Ned Brice stood behind the counter polishing a mug.

"Brandy," I said.

He continued polishing.

"Did you hear me?"

"I heard."

"Then pour."

He set down the mug. "No drink for you."

I laughed, for I had not yet learned the shape
of my freedom. "The court says I'm innocent."

"The court don't keep this house."

A drover near the fire spat upon the hearthstone. Another
man turned his back. In the corner old Jory Pike,
who had borrowed money from me five times and repaid
none of it, crossed himself as though I were a
corpse walking.

"I've silver," I said.

"Keep it."

"You served me when I was under charge."

"Aye. Thought then there might be a mistake."

"And now?"

Ned's wife appeared at the kitchen door, holding a child
behind her skirts. "H'out yer goes," she said. "We'll have
no butcher's leavings here."

I put my hand across the counter and caught Ned
by the neckcloth. He came forward with a squeak; but
three men rose behind me, and I heard the drover
scrape his chair aside.

It would have been pleasant to break Ned's head upon
his own pewter pots. Yet I had spent long enough
in stone rooms. I released him.

"Your brandy is ditch-water," I said.

"Then you'll not miss it."

They watched me go. When the door closed, their talk
returned in a burst, louder than before.

Thus did innocence receive me.

I tried two other houses. At the Bell they bolted
the door when they saw me crossing the street. At
the Fox and Grapes a pot-boy came out with a
blunderbuss twice as long as himself and informed me, in
a voice that trembled, that the master was from home.

By dusk I had eaten nothing since the gaol porridge
at sunrise.

There was no lodging for me in the town. My
room had been let; my few clothes sold against rent;
and every face at every window seemed to carry that
same knowledge I had seen in Quill's eyes. I could
have endured curses. A blow may be returned. But whispers
are knives without handles.

So I went north.

Beyond the last cottages, where the road climbed from the
river valley, the fog thinned and tore apart. The moor
received me under a hard autumn sky. Heather stretched black
on either side, patched with pale rushes and pools that
caught the last colour of evening. The wind drove straight
from the hills, smelling of peat, stone, and distant snow.
I had no greatcoat. The clothes returned to me by
the gaoler were thin, stained things, and before I had
walked three miles my teeth knocked together.

As I write of it, I can still feel that
road beneath my soles: hard in places, drowned in others,
vanishing between banks of fern and rising again beneath a
moon as sharp as a clipped coin. Behind me lay
the town, its chimneys thrusting through the fog. Before me
there was nothing but the moor.

I did not then intend another murder.

That is the plain truth. Men may call me what
they please, but I never killed from habit. I killed
Roger Tallis because he gave me cause. I had not
yet decided what to do about Quill. His glance burned
in my thoughts, certainly, but a glance is a poor
supper, and hunger has more command over a man than
vengeance.

After perhaps two hours I saw a light.

It showed, vanished behind a shoulder of ground, and showed
again: one square of gold in a waste of blue-black
heath. Leaving the road, I crossed a stream by stepping-stones
and came to a cottage built against a low outcrop
of granite. It had a slate roof, two chimneys, and
shutters upon all the lower windows except the one from
which the light escaped. There was a little walled garden
gone wild with nettles. No stable, no barn, no neighbour.

As I approached, I heard music.

A flute was playing within.

The tune was a melancholy street air concerning a sailor
drowned within sight of his bride. It was not well
played. The higher notes went thin and wandering, while the
lower ones had a breathy complaint, as though the instrument
had a cold. Yet the player persisted, beginning again whenever
he lost the melody.

I knocked.

The flute continued.

I knocked harder.

Still the tune piped on, now drowning the sailor for
the third time.

"Deaf or damned?" I muttered, and tried the latch.

The door opened.

I entered a narrow kitchen, warm enough to make my
skin smart. A lamp burned upon the table. A peat
fire glowed under a black kettle; bread stood beneath a
cloth; a ham hung from the rafters. There was nobody
there. The flute came from above, accompanied now by a
regular tapping, as if the player beat time with his
heel.

"Halloa!" I called.

The tune skipped into a livelier measure.

I closed the door, found a knife, and cut myself
bread and ham. I ate standing at the table, too
fiercely at first to taste either. There was ale in
a brown jug. I drank from it, then searched the
dresser and discovered a bottle of excellent French spirit behind
a row of cracked plates. It warmed me from throat
to belly.

All this while the flute played.

The absurdity of it began to anger me. A man
may trespass, rob a pantry, stamp across a stranger's kitchen,
and expect some protest from the master of the house.
But to be ignored is an insult. I took the
lamp and climbed the stairs.

There were three doors. Behind one I heard the flute
finishing a jaunty country dance. I pushed the door open.

Mr. Ambrose Quill sat beside a fire in his shirtsleeves,
with the instrument raised to his lips.

He lowered it.

For a moment neither of us spoke.

The room was small but comfortable. Books stood in crooked
ranks against the wall. A brace of pistols lay upon
a cabinet beyond him. On the table were papers, a
tobacco jar, two glasses, and a steaming bowl of punch.
Quill wore woollen breeches, black stockings, and embroidered slippers. Without
wig or gown he looked less like a barrister, but
no less himself. His grey hair was cropped close, and
firelight shone upon his red cheeks.

"Mr. Foot," he said. "You are late."

I shut the door behind me.

"You expected me?"

"I expected someone. Solitude encourages optimism."

"You knew I would come."

"No. Had I known, I should have put the pistols
nearer."

I looked at the cabinet. He followed my glance.

"You will find the left-hand weapon loaded," he said. "The
other has a cracked spring. Choose judiciously."

I crossed the room and took the loaded pistol.

He did not move.

"You saved my life this morning," I said.

"So I did."

"And then told me with your eyes that you'd hang
me tomorrow if you could."

"I am sorry my eyes are indiscreet. In court they
are usually praised for their restraint."

I cocked the pistol.

The click sounded very loud.

Quill examined the flute, turning it beneath the light. "You
dislike music?"

"I dislike men who know too much."

"Ah."

"You know I killed Venn."

"No."

The answer checked me.

He put the flute across his knees. "You did not
kill Elias Venn, the jeweller."

"Then why did you look at me so?"

"Because you killed another man."

The room seemed to narrow. "Who?"

"Roger Tallis."

I raised the pistol until its muzzle pointed between his
eyes.

Quill sighed. "If you fire at that distance, Mr. Foot,
you will spoil both the wall and my argument."

"There'll be no argument."

"There is always an argument. Even a pistol must state
its case."

I had seen brave men and foolish men. They often
resemble one another until blood is drawn. Quill was neither.
He was afraid—I knew it by a faint stiffness at
the corners of his mouth—but he regarded fear as a
fact to be arranged beside other facts. That calm of
his stirred my hatred worse than pleading could have done.

"How did you find Tallis?" I asked.

"I have not found him."

"Then you know nothing."

"On the contrary, I know almost everything. Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"As you please. But uncock the pistol. Your finger is
not an officer of the court, and I mistrust its
discretion."

I kept it cocked.

Quill poured punch into the second glass and held it
out. When I did not take it, he drank from
it himself, filled the first glass, and leaned back.

"Let us rehear the matter," he said. "We had this
morning the case for the Crown. Elias Venn, carrying unset
stones to the value of twelve thousand pounds, travels from
Lanton to Merrow Fair. His chaise is driven by a
postillion, Roger Tallis. Near Black Tor the chaise overturns—or appears
to overturn. Venn is found beneath it with his skull
broken. Tallis has vanished. So has the finest stone in
the parcel, a blue-white diamond valued at somewhat above ten
thousand pounds."

"You defended me. I heard it all."

"You heard what was said. I am interested in what
was not said. The Crown alleged that you stopped the
chaise, killed Venn, stole the diamond, and disposed of Tallis,
who might otherwise have given evidence."

"And the jury called it lies."

"The jury called it unproved. Juries are careful with nouns."

He took up a clay pipe, filled it, and lit
it from a spill.

"The case for the defence," he continued through smoke, "was
simpler. No one saw you near Black Tor. The blood
upon your sleeve might have been animal blood. Venn's watch,
found near your lodging, might have been placed there by
another hand. You possessed no diamond, no unusual money, and
no means of disposing of such a famous stone. Above
all, Roger Tallis—the most probable thief—was missing. Thus I suggested
that Tallis murdered his employer and fled with the gem."

"A good tale."

"A persuasive one. Unfortunately, Tallis did not flee."

The wind pressed against the shutters. Somewhere below us the
kettle lid began to chatter.

Quill pointed the stem of his pipe at me.

"Now we come to the facts. You knew Tallis. The
Crown did not discover that, because it asked whether you
had known him in Merrow. You had not. You knew
him in Bristol, seven years ago, when both of you
worked with a horse-dealer named Clegg."

I said nothing.

"Tallis was a boaster," Quill continued. "On the night before
Venn's journey, he drank with you at the Ploughman's Arms.
The landlord remembered two strangers, but not their names. Tallis
told you what his master carried. You proposed robbery. Tallis
refused—perhaps because he intended the business alone."

"You make pictures from smoke."

"I make them from people, which is much the same."

His eyes never left me.

"Tallis loosened the linchpin on the descent from Black Tor.
The wheel came away; the chaise overturned; Venn was injured
but not killed. Tallis finished him with the iron handle
of the wheel-brake. He took the diamond. Then you appeared."

My grip tightened.

"You had followed," Quill said. "Perhaps to share the profit,
perhaps to take it. Tallis denied having the stone. You
searched his coat and boots. You found nothing. You quarrelled.
He laughed at you. Roger Tallis had a disagreeable laugh,
by all accounts."

"He did."

The words left me before I could stop them.

Quill's pipe paused halfway to his mouth.

There was no triumph in his face. That made it
worse.

"Yes," he said softly. "I thought so."

I remembered Tallis in the moonlight, crouched beside the overturned
chaise. Venn lay with his head in the mud. One
of the horses screamed until Tallis cut the trace and
let it run. His hands were bloody, and he kept
laughing because I could not find the diamond.

"Where is it, Rog?" I had said.

"Gone where you'll never lay hand on it."

Then he spat at me.

I had struck him once. He came at me with
the brake-iron. I took it from him. When it was
done, his head had gone soft on one side.

I said to Quill, "If you know so much, why
am I free?"

"Because I was engaged to defend you."

"You could have withdrawn."

"And allowed that pudding-witted Crown counsel to hang you for
a murder you did not commit? Professional pride forbade it."

"I killed a man."

"Not the man charged in the indictment. The distinction is
one upon which our liberties depend."

"Your liberty won't."

I stepped nearer. He looked at the pistol and then
at the clock upon the mantel.

It was a quarter before nine.

"You intend to kill me," he said.

"I do."

"Because I can prove Tallis's death?"

"Because you can speak of it."

"I have told no one."

"You might."

"Very true. Then I must purchase your restraint."

"With what?"

"Ten thousand pounds."

I laughed. "You've no ten thousand pounds."

"I have property worth more. Three houses in Chapel Row,
a warehouse by the western quay, and sixty acres of
tolerable pasture. Deeds may be drawn. You will have legal
title."

"I'll have your death tonight."

"A corpse is seldom worth ten thousand pounds, except to
an anatomist."

"You bargain cold."

"I bargain for my life. Warmth would only confuse the
arithmetic."

He rose slowly, leaving the flute on the chair.

"Give me half an hour," he said. "At the end
of it you may kill me if you remain dissatisfied."

"What trick?"

"No trick. You hold the pistol. I shall carry a
spade."

"A spade?"

"To Roger Tallis."

The name seemed to move through the room like another
person.

Quill took a watch from his waistcoat and laid it
upon the table. "Half an hour, Mr. Foot. My life
against property worth ten thousand pounds. You must hear my
full proposal before deciding. A bargain requires terms."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you shoot me now and spend the remainder of
your evening wondering what lay half an hour away."

Curiosity has led more men to ruin than greed. I
uncocked the pistol.

"Get your coat."

Quill nodded as though I had accepted an invitation to
supper.

He put on a thick brown coat, wound a scarf
about his throat, and lit a lantern. From a cupboard
on the landing he took two spades, one short and
one long. He offered me the short.

"You carry both," I said.

"Your caution does you credit, though somewhat late in life."

We went out by the kitchen. Before leaving, Quill glanced
regretfully up the stairs.

"My flute," he said.

"Leave it."

"It argues better than I do."

"Then it knows when to be silent."

He sighed and shut the door.

The moon had risen higher. Frost silvered the heather, and
the stream below the cottage rushed black between white stones.
Quill led the way without hesitation, his lantern swinging. I
followed three paces behind with the pistol beneath my coat.

For some minutes only the wind spoke.

Then Quill said, "You buried Tallis well."

My hand went to the pistol. "How do you know
where?"

"I do not know precisely. That is why you accompany
me."

"You said you'd take me to him."

"I shall take you to the neighbourhood. You must provide
the final direction."

"I'll provide you a grave of your own."

"That threat loses force through repetition."

We crossed the road and climbed towards Black Tor. The
ground grew broken, scarred by old workings and heaps of
pale spoil. In former days men had burned limestone there,
and the abandoned kilns stood in a row against the
hillside, squat black mouths under crumbling arches. Rainwater had eaten
channels through the ash. Thorn bushes twisted from the cracks
like fingers.

I had not returned since that night.

A man believes earth will keep what he gives it.
It does not. Earth alters, sinks, swells, drains, freezes; grass
creeps across it; roots thrust down inquisitively. Yet memory preserves
the place too faithfully. I saw again Tallis's boots dragging
over the stones. I heard his heels knocking. I smelled
lime dust and blood.

Quill stopped beside the first kiln.

"Here?"

"No."

He proceeded to the second.

"Here?"

I pointed towards a hollow beyond the last kiln, where
a rowan tree leaned almost flat from the wind.

"There."

His lantern light moved across my face. "You are certain?"

"I dug it."

"Yes. I beg pardon."

The hollow had filled with dead fern. Beneath it the
ground looked undisturbed, but I knew the long stone set
like a doorstep at one end. I had placed it
there to remember—and then sworn never to return.

Quill gave me the short spade.

"Dig."

"You dig."

"I am buying my life. You are examining the goods."

I levelled the pistol at him.

He considered, then drove the longer spade into the earth.

The topsoil was soft with rain, but beneath it lay
clay. He worked badly. Lawyers, whatever their other crimes, are
not trained to dig. After five minutes he was panting;
after ten, his scarf hung loose and his ruddy face
had darkened. I watched from the edge until impatience mastered
me.

"Out."

He climbed from the shallow pit. I put the pistol
in my belt and took his spade.

The labour warmed me. Clay came up in heavy shining
slabs. The smell of wet earth thickened the air. Quill
held the lantern and occasionally consulted his watch, which struck
me as insolent.

"You buried him deep," he observed.

"I'd no wish for foxes to brief the magistrates."

"A commendable foresight."

At perhaps four feet, the spade struck cloth and slid
through it.

I stopped.

Neither of us spoke.

I knelt and cleared the soil with my hands. The
coat had rotted to a greasy black web. Beneath it
something pale curved through the clay.

A rib.

Quill lowered the lantern. Its light entered the pit and
made a theatre of the bones: the collapsed cage of
the chest, the long arms drawn inward, the skull tipped
to one side. The broken place showed above the ear.
One boot remained, though the foot inside had become a
confusion of little brown sticks.

There was nothing supernatural in it. A dead man is
matter. Yet the sight of Tallis's grin, stripped now of
lips and laughter, brought the cold back upon me.

Quill climbed down.

"Do not disturb the head," he said. "The chest and
throat are our concern."

"Our concern?"

He removed his gloves and began loosening clay from among
the ribs.

"What are you seeking?" I demanded.

"The consideration for our bargain."

"Deeds don't grow in graves."

"No. Something more portable does."

Then I understood—or half understood.

"The diamond?"

Quill looked up. "Precisely."

"He hadn't got it."

"He had."

"I searched him."

"You searched his clothes."

"And his boots."

"But not his stomach."

The wind passed over the kiln mouths and made a
low hollow note, like breath blown across a bottle.

Quill worked carefully, separating clods with his fingers. "Tallis knew
you would search him. He could not throw the stone
away, nor conceal it securely on the chaise. He therefore
swallowed it."

"You cannot know that."

"I know he purchased lamp oil from a cottager an
hour before the murder and asked whether it would make
a man vomit. I know the stopper of Venn's cordial
bottle was found removed, though the bottle was empty. I
know Tallis had been seen years before swallowing coins for
wagers. Most importantly, I know the diamond vanished without passing
through any known market."

"So you brought me here to find it."

"I brought you here because you had already found the
grave."

He cleared the last clay from beneath the ribs, then
paused.

"Your knife."

I handed it to him.

The remains of Tallis's waistcoat and shirt had fallen into
a dark mass beneath the spine. Quill cut through the
cloth, scraped, and lifted something like a hardened purse of
leather.

"Stomach tissue," he said. "Or what the soil has made
of it."

He slit it.

Nothing appeared but black filth.

A rage took me. I caught his collar and dragged
him upright.

"You lied."

"Wait."

"You brought me to dig your grave beside his."

"Wait, you fool."

His composure broke for the first time. He thrust his
hand into the clay beneath the bones and felt about
furiously. Then he laughed—a short, breathless sound.

Between finger and thumb he held a clot of dirt
no larger than a walnut.

He rubbed it against his sleeve.

The moon entered the grave.

That is how it seemed. A hard white flame sprang
from his hand, cold and pure, gathering the lantern light
and breaking it into blue, yellow, and scarlet sparks. Tallis's
bones, the wet clay, Quill's sweating face, even the muzzle
of my pistol appeared to lean towards it.

I had seen paste jewels, sea-glass, rock crystal, and diamonds
no larger than grains of salt. I had never seen
a stone like that. It seemed less an object than
a hole cut through the night into some fiercer world.

Quill stared at it.

For one instant he forgot me.

His thumb moved over the facets. His face, usually so
well governed, became naked with desire.

Then he closed his fist.

"There," he said.

"Give it."

"On what terms?"

I drew the pistol.

He looked down its barrel. "The original proposal was property
worth ten thousand pounds in exchange for my life."

"The property is in your hand."

"It is not mine."

"It was Tallis's."

"No. It belonged to Venn, and now belongs to Venn's
estate."

"Venn's dead. Tallis's dead. You said ten thousand."

"I said property worth ten thousand. I did not specify
my own."

"You meant to take it."

The words struck him more sharply than the pistol had
done.

For a moment he said nothing. The kiln mouths gaped
behind him, and the lantern hissed in the damp wind.

At last he climbed out of the grave and sat
upon the long stone. He took his pipe from his
pocket but found it broken from the digging. He examined
the two pieces with a look of genuine sorrow.

"Yes," he said. "I meant to take it."

I remained below among Tallis's bones.

Quill opened his hand. The diamond flashed upon his palm.

"I deduced its resting place three weeks before your trial.
I knew Tallis had swallowed it. I knew you had
killed Tallis and concealed him near Black Tor. But I
did not know the exact grave. Had I accused you,
the stone might never be recovered—and you might hang for
Venn's murder, which offended my sense of order."

"So you freed me to lead you here?"

"No. I freed you because the evidence did not justify
conviction. Do not make me more practical than I am."

"But you came to the cottage."

"I own it. I come here when I wish to
escape clients."

"With spades in the cupboard?"

"There are potatoes in the garden."

I could not help it: I laughed.

The sound came strangely from the pit. Quill's mouth twitched.

He continued, "After the verdict I intended to search the
moor. I told myself I would recover the jewel for
Venn's heirs. Then I told myself the heirs were wealthy,
disagreeable people who had refused my account in an unrelated
matter. Thereafter my reasoning deteriorated."

"And the flute?"

"I was arguing out the case with it."

"Who was winning?"

"The flute. It has a painfully narrow conception of property."

He held the diamond towards the moon.

"I knew that if I found this alone, I might
put it in my pocket. A stone so small permits
a man to carry ten thousand pounds without appearing bent
beneath the weight of his sin. That is a dangerous
convenience."

"Yet you came."

"I came. I played three tunes, drank two bowls of
punch, and was preparing to become a thief when Providence
sent me a murderer."

"You call me Providence again and I'll shoot you from
principle."

"Then let us preserve our theological uncertainty."

He tossed the diamond down.

I caught it against my chest.

Even through the clay on my fingers, it felt smooth
and terribly cold.

"That is your ten thousand pounds," he said. "Perhaps more.
In return, you spare my life."

"What prevents you informing against me tomorrow?"

"Professional confidence."

"I never told you anything as my lawyer."

"You said Tallis had an unpleasant laugh."

"That is thin protection."

"It secured your acquittal."

I studied him. "You'll tell Venn's heirs of the diamond."

"I cannot tell them what I no longer possess."

"You'll tell the magistrate where Tallis lies."

"I have no wish to explain how I came by
the knowledge. Nor would the discovery of Tallis's bones improve
my reputation as counsel in your former case."

I climbed from the grave. The diamond lay hidden in
my fist, yet its imagined light seemed to shine between
my fingers.

"You arranged this," I said. "The bargain. The digging. All
of it."

"I arranged nothing beyond the limit of desperation. Your arrival
spared me from theft; my information spared me from murder.
We have each performed an accidental service for the other."

"You saved me once in court."

"And you will save me once out of it. Symmetry
is pleasing."

I looked at the pistol.

It would have been easy. Quill stood within six feet,
muddy, tired, and unarmed. The moor was empty. There was
room beside Tallis. The old rage had not gone; but
hatred, like hunger, changes after it is fed. In my
hand I held wealth enough to cross oceans, purchase names,
bribe officials, and become in some distant place an honest
gentleman.

Besides, I had given my word to hear his bargain.

Men suppose a murderer's word must be worthless. They are
wrong. A man who lives outside the law must sometimes
cling harder to the few laws he makes for himself.

I lowered the pistol.

"You have your half-hour."

Quill released a breath which he had been pretending not
to hold.

"Then we should cover Mr. Tallis," he said. "He has
served us both sufficiently."

We restored the bones to the clay. I did most
of the work. Quill filled the smaller spaces and stamped
down the earth with his embroidered slippers, now ruined beyond
redemption. We spread fern across the grave and replaced the
long stone.

When all was done, the eastern clouds had paled a
little, though dawn remained far off.

We walked back without speaking.

At the cottage Quill rekindled the kitchen fire and set
water to boil. He poured brandy for himself, then looked
at the glass from which I had drunk earlier.

"You used this?"

"Aye."

He picked it up with thumb and forefinger, opened the
back door, and flung it against a stone. It broke
neatly.

"Why?"

"I am particular."

I took the clay pipe I had borrowed from his
mantel while waiting for the kettle and put it between
my teeth.

"You used that also?"

"Aye."

He took it from me and snapped it in half.

"You're a damned hospitable man."

"My gratitude has limits."

He gave me bread, ham, a thick coat, and twelve
guineas from a locked box. In exchange I promised never
to approach his house, chambers, person, relatives, clerks, servants, or
flute.

"What will you do with the stone?" he asked.

"Sell it."

"Not in England."

"I'm not a fool."

"No," he said. "That has been the difficulty throughout."

At the door I turned. He stood in the kitchen
with mud to his knees, hair blown wild, and the
broken pipe still in his hand.

"You knew I might kill you."

"I considered it probable."

"Yet you played your flute."

"I play when troubled."

"You played badly."

"I was very troubled."

I left him there.

At the stream below the cottage, I heard the flute
begin again. The first notes trembled and cracked. Then Quill
found the air—a brisk soldier's march, sprightly and clear—and sent
it piping out over the barren moor as though he
had won a victory.

Perhaps he had.

I carried the diamond for nine months before I found
a buyer in Antwerp who asked no questions and paid
less than half its worth. It was enough. I crossed
the Atlantic, changed my name twice, and have lived in
several countries where Gabriel Foot is known only to God,
myself, and—if he yet lives—Ambrose Quill.

I have not repented. Repentance is often only fear looking
backwards. Tallis would have killed me had I not killed
him; Venn was dead before I came upon the chaise;
and as for Quill, I spared him according to bargain.
Let others arrange the moral account.

Yet there are nights, even now, when fog presses against
my windows and candle flames look like sickly eyes, that
I see the old assize-house and Quill's knowing glance across
the court. Then the room alters. Books become black kiln
mouths; the carpet turns to wet clay; and in my
closed hand I feel again that cold stone flashing above
the bones.

Most plainly of all, I hear the flute.

It begins with the drowned sailor, sorrowful and absurd. It
quarrels with itself, loses the melody, and wanders through the
dark. But at last it rises into the soldier's march,
jaunty as daylight, and I see Ambrose Quill in his
ruined slippers congratulating himself that Providence had sent a murderer
in time to preserve him from dishonesty.

He remained, as he would doubtless phrase it, merely a
lawyer.

I remained what I was.

And Roger Tallis, who swallowed ten thousand pounds to keep
it from my hands, gave it to me in the
end.

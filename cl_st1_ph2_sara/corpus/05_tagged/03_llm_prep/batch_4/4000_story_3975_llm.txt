# The Unstamped Fortune

There are moments in the life of every man when
the machinery of existence appears to pause, as if the
engineer in charge had gone away to have his tea.
At such moments even a bank clerk may become a
figure of destiny.

Owen Bentley's moment occurred at half-past eight on a Thursday
evening in the drawing-room of Mr Horace Sheppherd, of Balham.

Mr Sheppherd was a man who had prospered in the
wholesale hardware trade, and prosperity had affected him as starch
affects a collar. It had made him stiff, glossy, and
uncomfortable. He sat before the empty fireplace with his legs
apart and his hands on his knees, looking like a
statesman who has just discovered that the Opposition has been
behaving exactly as he expected.

Opposite him sat Owen.

Owen Bentley was twenty-six, amiable, moderately good-looking, and employed in
the Foreign Correspondence Department of the New Asiatic Bank at
a salary of one hundred and thirty pounds a year.
He possessed, in addition, twelve pounds eight shillings in the
Post Office Savings Bank, a silver cigarette-case won in a
golf competition in which the other competitor had sprained his
wrist, and an uncle in New Zealand from whom nothing
had been heard since 1897.

These assets did not impress Mr Sheppherd.

"You wish," he said, "to marry my daughter."

"Yes," said Owen.

"My only daughter."

"Yes."

"Audrey."

"Yes."

Mr Sheppherd breathed heavily. Repetition is the last refuge of
a man who has prepared a speech and forgotten the
opening.

"And may I ask," he continued, "how you propose to
support her?"

Owen had expected this. For three days he had been
composing answers to it while adding columns of francs and
marks, and the result had seemed excellent in the Foreign
Correspondence Department. In Balham, however, it somehow lost its outline.

"Well," he said, "I have my salary."

"Precisely."

"And there is every possibility of an increase."

"When?"

Owen hesitated. Promotion at the New Asiatic Bank was governed
by principles resembling those which regulate the formation of coral
islands. It occurred, but not in a manner that could
be watched by the naked eye.

"I could hardly name a date."

"Nor could I."

"No."

Mr Sheppherd leaned back.

"I have nothing against you personally, Bentley. Nothing whatever. I
believe you to be respectable."

"Thank you."

"You do not drink?"

"No."

"Gamble?"

"No."

"Hold advanced political opinions?"

"No."

"Write poetry?"

"Not now."

Mr Sheppherd started.

"Not now?"

"I wrote a sonnet once."

"What became of it?"

"I destroyed it."

"Good. That was sensible. But respectability is not everything. I
require prospects. I require substance. I require"—Mr Sheppherd inflated himself—"financial
stability."

Owen looked at the carpet. It was a thick carpet,
with pink roses on a blue ground, and at that
moment it seemed to him to contain the whole tragedy
of human existence. One cannot argue effectively with financial stability
when earning two pounds ten a week.

"I love Audrey," he said.

Mr Sheppherd waved this aside. Love, in his opinion, was
an excellent thing in its proper place, like mustard, but
no foundation for housekeeping.

"Very possibly. Youth is impulsive. At your age I fancied
myself attached to a young person who assisted in a
confectioner's establishment in Finsbury."

"What happened?"

"I mastered the feeling."

Owen regarded him with a dislike which was almost admiration.
There was something magnificent in the picture of Mr Sheppherd
wrestling with passion and flooring it in two rounds.

"I am sorry," said the father, without looking it, "but
the matter is impossible. Furthermore, I must ask you not
to see Audrey again."

"But—"

"Not to see her, write to her, or communicate with
her in any way."

"But—"

"I have said what I had to say."

This was inaccurate. Mr Sheppherd had several more things to
say, and he said them at length. They dealt with
responsibility, prudence, the cost of coal, the decline of modern
youth, and the singularly fortunate circumstance that he himself had
not married until he was thirty-nine. Owen listened in silence.
A sensitive man would have suffered. Owen merely became numb.

At nine o'clock he rose.

"Good night," he said.

"Good night, Bentley. No ill feeling."

"None whatever," said Owen, lying courteously.

In the hall Audrey was waiting beside the hat-stand.

Audrey Sheppherd was small and fair, with steady grey eyes
and a manner which made hesitation seem an eccentric foreign
custom. Her father had spent twenty-two years ruling clerks, travellers,
warehousemen and Mrs Sheppherd, and had supposed this to be
preparation for ruling Audrey. It was an error which time
had not corrected.

"Well?" she whispered.

Owen shook his head.

"He won't hear of it."

"Did you tell him you loved me?"

"Yes."

"And that I loved you?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"He spoke about coal."

Audrey frowned.

"That sounds like father. What else?"

"He says I have no prospects."

"You have."

"I haven't."

"You must get some."

This direct solution, though admirable in principle, seemed to Owen
to overlook certain technical difficulties.

"How?"

"You must make money."

"How?"

Audrey reflected. She was accustomed to issuing commands to the
universe and leaving details to the appropriate department.

"You might invent something."

"I don't know anything about machinery."

"Write a book."

"I once wrote a play."

Audrey's eyes brightened.

"Of course! I had forgotten. The one from Edith Butler's
novel."

"From *Was It Her Heart?*"

"Such a beautiful book."

Owen had his own views concerning *Was It Her Heart?*
It was the story of a girl called Hyacinth who
sacrificed herself in chapter after chapter for persons who would
have been better for a sharp talking-to. Hyacinth possessed blue
eyes, a pure soul and a tendency to misunderstand conversations
overheard behind curtains. Owen had dramatized the novel four years
earlier during a period when his work at the bank
had been less exacting than usual and rain had prevented
cricket on five successive Saturdays.

"What happened to the play?" Audrey asked.

"I sent it to Edith Butler."

"And?"

"Nothing. She never answered."

"Perhaps she did not receive it."

"She must have. I posted it myself."

"Did you put stamps on it?"

"Yes."

Audrey had known Owen for two years and had learned
to investigate.

"And the address?"

"Yes."

"And your own address?"

"I enclosed a covering letter."

"Then write another play."

"One is enough for any man."

"But you wrote that one extremely well."

"You never read it."

"No, but I am certain you did."

Love is the only critic whose opinions are formed without
reference to the work under discussion.

A door opened upstairs.

"That will be father," said Audrey. "Go now. I shall
telephone you tomorrow."

"He said we were not to communicate."

"I heard him."

"But—"

"Good night, darling."

She kissed him quickly, opened the front door, and propelled
him into the street.

Thus Owen left Balham with his heart broken and his
hat at an angle. At the corner he collided with
a lamp-post.

The lamp-post remained neutral.

* * *

For the next three weeks the telephone became the central
institution of Owen's life.

Mr Sheppherd had forbidden meetings and correspondence, but he had
omitted to mention the telephone, chiefly because, like many men
who pride themselves on foresight, he was seldom able to
see beyond the end of the sentence he was speaking.
Audrey therefore telephoned daily from a tobacconist's shop near Victoria,
where privacy could be obtained in a cabinet smelling strongly
of cigars and damp overcoats.

Owen received these communications at the bank.

The New Asiatic discouraged private calls. Its directors held that
an employee who wished to converse with persons outside the
building should do so after six o'clock, preferably in writing,
and at his own expense. The telephone on Owen's desk
had been installed for discussions involving bills of exchange, Indian
jute, and the movement of specie. Audrey used it to
ask whether he was still fond of her.

"Of course I am," Owen would whisper.

"How much?"

"Very much."

"More than yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Have you made any money?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"I've been busy."

"With what?"

"Work."

Audrey regarded this as unsatisfactory. Work which merely preserved a
salary of one hundred and thirty pounds a year did
not seem to her work in the fullest sense. It
was more in the nature of fidgeting.

"You must do something big," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"What about?"

"Something big."

"What?"

"If I knew, I shouldn't need to think."

These conversations affected Owen's efficiency. A man cannot be expected
to discuss undying devotion at eleven-thirty and at eleven-thirty-two apply
his whole mind to a remittance from Constantinople. The soul
does not change gears so rapidly.

Owen became absent-minded.

He placed documents in the wrong baskets. He addressed envelopes
to people who did not exist. He sent a confidential
memorandum on Brazilian securities to a firm of Birmingham caterers
and acknowledged with gratitude the receipt of three thousand pounds
which the sender had not remitted. His chief, a man
named Mr Gudgeon, began to observe him with pain.

Mr Gudgeon was thin, bald, and entirely without illusions. He
had entered the bank at sixteen, and thirty-eight years of
service had removed from him all qualities not essential to
the correct filing of correspondence. He knew that the world
contained poetry, romance and spring mornings, but he had never
approved of them.

"Bentley," he said one afternoon, "you are deteriorating."

"Yes, sir."

"You sent Messrs. Phipps and Carboy a letter intended for
the Amsterdam Discount Company."

"I'm sorry, sir."

"You also entered a credit of forty pounds as four
thousand."

"I corrected it."

"After Messrs. Wilkins had ordered a motor-car."

"I'm very sorry."

"Do not be sorry. Be accurate."

This was Mr Gudgeon's philosophy. It had brought him to
a salary of four hundred a year and chronic indigestion.

Owen tried to be accurate, but melancholy enveloped him like
a fog. His meals lost their flavour. Cricket scores ceased
to interest him. A Middlesex collapse which in happier days
would have caused him to clutch his head now drew
from him only a sigh. He listened to a street
singer performing "Love's Garden of Roses" beneath his window and
did not throw anything.

His landlady became alarmed.

"You want a change, Mr Bentley," she said. "You're run
down."

"I'm all right."

"You're not. You ate hardly any of that boiled mutton."

"I ate a good deal."

"You left the best part."

Owen looked at what remained and shuddered faintly.

"I think I'll take a holiday."

It was the first sensible decision he had made for
some time.

A fellow clerk recommended a farmhouse near Church Stretton in
Shropshire, where he had once spent a fortnight recovering from
influenza. The terms were moderate, the eggs genuine, and there
was a stream in which fish could be seen, though
not caught. Owen wrote to Mrs Dorman, secured a room,
and on the following Saturday travelled west.

Audrey approved.

"The country will clear your brain," she said over the
telephone. "You will think of a way to make your
fortune."

"I shall probably sit in a field."

"You can think in a field."

"I can try."

"And you won't speak to any girls?"

"The place is a farm."

"Farms have girls."

"I believe they usually keep cows."

"Cows do not exclude girls. Promise."

"I promise."

Audrey was not naturally jealous. She had become jealous through
observing life. A girl who takes no precautions may wake
one morning to discover that the man she loves has
become engaged to somebody who plays the mandolin. Such things
happen.

* * *

The Dorman farm stood on a green slope between a
wood and a road which went to Ludlow after wandering
for some miles in an undecided manner. It was an
old stone house with low windows, uneven floors, and a
smell composed of peat smoke, bacon and dogs. To Owen,
coming from London, it seemed a haven.

On his first morning he woke to birdsong, distant cattle,
and an atmosphere so pure that breathing it felt almost
dishonest. The hills lay blue beyond the fields; a stream
winked among the alders; and a haze of heat trembled
above the grass.

Mrs Dorman gave him ham and eggs, oatcakes, coffee, honey,
and advice.

"Tha' wants buildin' up," she said.

Mrs Dorman was square, rosy and confident. She possessed the
gift of prophecy, which had come down to her from
an aunt in Much Wenlock who had foreseen the Franco-Prussian
War, though unfortunately not until 1872.

"You read fortunes?" Owen asked.

"Ah do."

"Cards?"

"Cards, tea-leaves, dreams, bumps. Bumps is extra."

Owen declined the bumps but permitted her to inspect his
tea-leaves. Mrs Dorman turned the cup three times, peered into
it, and clicked her tongue.

"Money," she said.

Owen smiled.

"A small legacy?"

"Pots o' money."

"What?"

"Pots. Great pots an' pots."

"How soon?"

Mrs Dorman frowned at the cup.

"Soon enough. There's a dark man, too."

"A creditor?"

"Dark man wi' hair about 'is face. 'E'll bring trouble,
then money."

"A bearded financier?"

"An' marriage."

Owen sat up.

"Mine?"

"Could be yourn. Could be his'n. Cups don't speak plain."

This weakened the prophecy, but Owen was prepared to be
charitable.

"Pots of money," he repeated.

"Pots."

"How many pots?"

Mrs Dorman gave him a severe look. There are limits
beyond which science refuses to be cross-examined.

George, her son, appeared at the door. He was a
long, silent youth whose conversation had been compressed by rural
life into essentials.

"Stranger's at little cottage," he said.

"What stranger?" asked Mrs Dorman.

"Him wi' beard."

"When come?"

"Last night."

"What's 'e want?"

"Quiet."

George considered this sufficient biography and withdrew.

Mrs Dorman nodded impressively at Owen.

"There's your dark man."

"Is he a financier?"

"Writes books, George says."

"Then he certainly isn't."

The bearded stranger occupied a small cottage belonging to the
Dormans at the farther end of the farm. He kept
to himself, ate at odd hours, and had sent word
that he was not to be disturbed under any circumstances.
Mrs Dorman regarded this as proof of genius. George regarded
it as proof that the man was "a rum 'un."
Owen scarcely regarded it at all.

That afternoon he walked over the hills. Exercise and sunlight
worked upon him. By four o'clock he had ceased to
brood about Mr Sheppherd; by five he had begun to
suspect that the world might contain possibilities; and at half-past
five, seated beneath a hedge with a pipe, he felt
almost cheerful.

Then someone began singing.

The singer was invisible, being concealed by the hedge, but
his voice travelled freely. It was a large, rough voice,
and the song concerned a maiden who waited by the
mill while her lover went to sea. The melody possessed
four lines, all alike, and the performer repeated them with
the concentration of a man sandpapering a table.

Owen endured three verses.

Man's power of endurance is limited.

He rose, climbed the bank, and looked over the hedge.

On the other side, in a deck-chair outside the little
cottage, sat the bearded stranger. Papers covered a table before
him. He wore a tweed coat, an open collar and
an expression of concentrated ferocity. The song was issuing from
a portable gramophone by his feet.

Owen called to him.

"I say!"

The stranger looked up.

"Stop that noise!"

"What?"

"That song."

The stranger rose slowly. He was a large man, and
his beard bristled in a manner suggestive of an outraged
hedgehog.

"Are you addressing me?"

"Yes."

"Then don't."

He sat down.

Owen was annoyed.

The countryside encourages liberty, equality and bad judgement. In London
he would have walked away. In Shropshire, with the clean
wind in his face, he felt capable of defending civilization.

"Would you mind stopping that gramophone?"

"Yes."

"I can't think."

"Then don't try. You'll strain yourself."

"I came here for quiet."

"So did I."

"You are making a hideous row."

The bearded man's eyes blazed.

"Hideous row?"

"Extremely."

"Sir, that song is 'The Maid of the Mill,' composed
by Hermann Glogauer and sung by Signor Pietro Belli."

"I don't care if it was sung by the Archbishop
of Canterbury."

The stranger gripped the table.

"I am working."

"So I gathered."

"I require music when I work."

"You require a new record."

There are words which turn away wrath and words which
increase it. Owen had selected from the second class.

The stranger seized the nearest object. It happened to be
a loaf of bread—a compact farmhouse loaf, weighing approximately one
pound fourteen ounces and containing, according to Mrs Dorman, a
judicious proportion of semolina. He hurled it.

Owen ducked. The loaf passed over his head, struck the
hedge, and fell among the nettles.

For a moment the two men stared at each other.
The stranger resembled Ajax defying the lightning. Owen resembled a
bank clerk who has just escaped death by bakery.

Then the stranger pointed toward the road.

"Go away!"

"I intend to."

"And don't come back."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

Owen descended the bank with dignity. Dignity on a steep
bank is difficult, and he lost it twice before reaching
the path.

At supper he told Mrs Dorman what had happened.

"Ah," she said comfortably. "That'll be Mr Prosser."

"Who is Mr Prosser?"

"Writer-man. George fetched 'im from station."

"He threw bread at me."

"Always was quick-tempered, George says."

"How does George know?"

"Read it in paper."

"Does the paper mention his habit of throwing loaves?"

"Hasn't got no wife," said Mrs Dorman, pursuing her own
thought. "That's what's wrong wi' him. Man needs wife to
take the edge off."

She turned Owen's teacup and examined the leaves.

"He'll marry soon."

"Prosser?"

"Dark man. Hair about 'is face."

"And the money?"

"Pots."

Owen went to bed reflecting that prophecy, though attractive as
an indoor amusement, was deficient in practical value. A dark
man had appeared, certainly, but he had brought no money.
He had brought a loaf, and had not allowed Owen
to retain it.

For the remainder of his holiday Owen avoided the cottage.
Once, from a distant hill, he saw Prosser striding across
a field with sheets of paper under his arm and
a shepherd's dog fleeing before him. Another time he heard
"The Maid of the Mill" floating across the valley and
took a path in the opposite direction.

The peace of the countryside restored him. At the end
of two weeks he returned to London brown, rested, and
determined to be efficient.

Determination is a fine thing, but it should not be
confused with performance.

* * *

On the morning after his return, Owen arrived at the
bank at nine twenty-eight, put on his office coat, and
began work with the stern resolution of a Roman consul.

By ten fifteen he had completed seven letters without error.
At ten twenty the telephone rang.

"Is that you, darling?" said Audrey.

"Yes."

"Did you miss me?"

"Terribly."

"Did you meet any girls?"

"No."

"None at all?"

"There was Mrs Dorman."

"How old?"

"About sixty."

"That proves nothing. What did you do?"

"I walked. I ate. A man threw a loaf at
me."

"A man?"

"A writer named Prosser."

"What was he like?"

"Large. Bearded. Mad."

"Why did he throw it?"

"I criticized his music."

Audrey considered this.

"Was the loaf stale?"

"I did not examine it."

"You should have. It might have hurt you."

This concern softened Owen. For twelve minutes they discussed their
love, their future, Mr Sheppherd's obstinacy, and the possibility of
a dramatic rise in the value of Owen's uncle in
New Zealand.

When the conversation ended, the Roman consul had disappeared.

Owen returned to his correspondence in a mist of emotion.
Letters moved through his hands like leaves in an autumn
gale. He signed, folded and enclosed them with the dreamy
grace of a preoccupied monarch scattering largess.

At noon Mr Gudgeon approached.

"Bentley."

"Yes, sir?"

"You have placed the Paris mail in the Berlin tray."

"I'm sorry, sir."

"And this envelope contains no letter."

"Really?"

"Yes. Unless it is written in invisible ink."

Owen apologized and corrected the errors. But Fate had already
loaded the dice.

Among the morning's correspondence were three letters addressed to Miss
Dora Delaney, Flat 4, 16 Marlowe Mansions, Chelsea. These letters
had been handed in by a client, Mr J. C.
Prosser, with instructions that they be forwarded under the bank's
cover, since the writer was travelling and wished his present
address to remain private.

The bank performed such services for favoured customers. It expected
its clerks to affix the necessary postage.

Owen did not.

The three letters went forth unstamped.

The Post Office, an institution which can be patient but
refuses to be philanthropic, charged Miss Delaney double postage on
each. Miss Delaney paid sixpence, sixpence, and another sixpence. She
then wrote to Mr Prosser.

Mr Prosser wrote to the bank.

His letter reached the manager, Mr Treadwell, on Wednesday morning.

Mr Treadwell sent for Owen.

The manager's room was large, hushed and heavily carpeted. Mr
Treadwell sat behind a desk on which every paper lay
at right angles to every other paper. He had pale
eyes, drooping cheeks, and an expression like that of a
stuffed trout disappointed in humanity.

"Bentley," he said.

"Yes, sir?"

"Do you know a Mr J. C. Prosser?"

Owen thought of airborne bread.

"I met a man of that name in Shropshire."

"Indeed."

Mr Treadwell made a note, as if Shropshire itself might
later be required to explain.

"Mr Prosser is a valued customer of the bank."

"I didn't know that."

"He entrusted us with three letters for forwarding. They were
sent without stamps."

Owen's heart sank.

"I'm very sorry."

"The recipients were charged eighteenpence."

"Yes, sir."

"Mr Prosser describes the matter as an outrage."

Owen felt this was putting it strongly. An outrage, to
his mind, involved bombs, secret societies and perhaps a Balkan
frontier. Eighteenpence seemed inadequate.

"He has come here personally," continued Mr Treadwell, "to demand
an explanation."

"Here?"

"He is waiting outside."

At that instant the door opened violently and Prosser entered.

He looked exactly as Owen remembered him, except that he
wore a dark suit and carried no food. His beard
projected aggressively. His eyebrows were storm signals.

"Which is the son of a gun?" he demanded.

Mr Treadwell winced. Strong language in the manager's office affected
him as dust affects a museum curator.

"Mr Prosser, this is Mr Bentley, the clerk responsible."

Prosser turned.

"You!"

Owen stared.

"You!"

Mr Treadwell looked from one to the other.

"You are acquainted?"

"This is the fellow," said Prosser, "who insulted Glogauer."

"This is the fellow," said Owen, "who threw a loaf
at me."

Mr Treadwell's mouth opened. In thirty-one years of banking he
had encountered overdrafts, forged signatures and one cashier who kept
poultry in the vault, but this was new.

Prosser suddenly grinned.

The change was remarkable. His face, which had resembled an
angry wilderness, became almost human.

"By Jove," he said. "The bread critic!"

"The musical maniac."

Prosser slapped him on the shoulder.

"Excellent! I've been wanting to meet you again."

"I have managed without it."

"You ducked well."

"I had an incentive."

"Not hurt?"

"Only spiritually."

Prosser laughed. He turned to Mr Treadwell.

"Forget the stamps."

The manager blinked.

"But, Mr Prosser—"

"Forget 'em. Eighteenpence won't ruin me. I was in a
bad temper when I wrote."

"You threatened to remove your account."

"I threaten everybody. Means nothing."

Mr Treadwell looked pained. To him, a threat to remove
an account was not a conversational ornament.

Prosser thrust his hands into his pockets.

"What are you doing tonight, Bentley?"

"I'm going to the theatre."

"What theatre?"

"The Royalty."

Prosser raised his eyebrows.

"*Was It Her Heart?*"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"A friend gave me a ticket."

This was not strictly accurate. Audrey had bought two seats
with money saved from her dress allowance and had arranged
to meet him secretly in the foyer. But Owen had
learned caution. Bank managers do not need complete biographies.

Prosser nodded.

"I'm going too. Supper afterward?"

"With pleasure."

"Savoy grill-room. Eleven-fifteen."

He shook Owen's hand, nodded curtly at Mr Treadwell, and
left.

The manager remained motionless for some seconds.

"Bentley," he said at last, "your methods are irregular."

"Yes, sir."

"Extremely irregular."

"Yes, sir."

"Nevertheless, in this instance, they appear to have produced a
satisfactory result."

"Yes, sir."

"Return to your department."

Owen returned. Mr Gudgeon asked what had happened.

"I was forgiven."

Mr Gudgeon looked disappointed. He distrusted happy endings during office
hours.

* * *

The Royalty Theatre was filled that evening with the kind
of audience which arrives prepared to enjoy itself but willing
to be persuaded otherwise. There were women in bright gowns,
men in white waistcoats, critics with tired faces, and a
sprinkling of country visitors who had dined early and regarded
the whole enterprise as daring.

Owen met Audrey by the marble pillar in the foyer.

"You look pale," she said.

"I've had a difficult day."

"Did they dismiss you?"

"No."

"Then it cannot have been very difficult."

She gave him his ticket.

"I told father I was dining with Muriel."

"Who is Muriel?"

"I don't know. But she sounds respectable."

They entered the stalls.

The curtain rose on the drawing-room of Sir Jasper Mervyn's
house in Sussex. Owen leaned back, prepared to endure. He
remembered the novel clearly. Hyacinth would enter through the French
windows, discover that her sister loved the same man, and
instantly decide to sacrifice herself. Later, someone would conceal a
letter in a vase.

Hyacinth entered. She was played by a celebrated actress who
specialized in looking pure while standing in expensive rooms. She
crossed to the fireplace and spoke to the butler.

Owen sat forward.

"What is it?" whispered Audrey.

"Nothing."

The scene continued.

A young man named Gerald appeared—a character who had not
existed in Edith Butler's novel. He was a cheerful, impecunious
cousin of the family, employed in a bank.

Owen gripped the arms of his seat.

Gerald said, "A man may live without love, but not
on one hundred and thirty pounds a year."

The audience laughed.

Owen did not.

He had written that line.

Four years earlier, seated in his lodging with rain tapping
the window, he had invented Gerald because the novel possessed
no comic relief and an almost criminal abundance of misery.
He had given Gerald a bank clerkship, a habit of
mislaying letters, and a scene with a pompous father. Gerald's
proposal to the maid in the second act had been
Owen's own work. So had the dialogue about coal.

One coincidence may be accepted. Two require attention. By the
time Gerald referred to an uncle in New Zealand, Owen's
doubts had vanished.

Audrey touched his sleeve.

"You are hurting the seat."

"This is my play."

"What?"

"This is my play."

"Hush," said a woman in front.

Owen lowered his voice.

"I wrote this."

Audrey's eyes widened.

"But it says 'Adapted by Edith Butler' on the programme."

"She stole it."

Audrey turned toward the stage. Her expression hardened. If Edith
Butler had entered the stalls at that moment, literature might
have lost a popular novelist.

The first act ended amid moderate applause. Owen remained seated.

"Why didn't you do something?" Audrey demanded.

"What could I do?"

"Stand up and tell them."

"To the audience?"

"Yes."

"I should be removed."

"Then go behind the scenes."

"And say what?"

"That it is yours."

"They would ask for proof."

"You have proof."

"I haven't. The manuscript was never returned."

Audrey frowned. Practicality had collided with fact.

"Who gave them the manuscript, then?"

"Edith Butler. She must have kept it."

"The beast."

The second act began. Owen scarcely watched it. His mind
seethed. For four years he had regarded his play as
a youthful indiscretion, lost somewhere in the postal system or
discarded by an author who knew better. Now it stood
before him, polished, decorated and receiving applause.

Recognition is sweet, but anonymous recognition has drawbacks.

At the final curtain the audience clapped politely. There were
several calls. Hyacinth bowed, Gerald bowed, and the actor playing
Sir Jasper advanced with the expression of a man who
had personally saved the British theatre.

Then the applause faded.

In the foyer Audrey took Owen's arm.

"You must see a solicitor tomorrow."

"With what evidence?"

"Your word."

"Solicitors prefer documents."

"Then speak to Edith Butler."

"I don't know where she lives."

"It will be in *Who's Who*."

"Novelists aren't always in *Who's Who*."

"They ought to be if they steal plays."

Owen looked at his watch.

"I promised Prosser I would have supper with him."

"The bread man?"

"Yes."

"I am coming."

"You can't. You told your father you were dining with
Muriel."

"That does not prevent supper."

"He expects you home."

Audrey's face clouded.

"I suppose I must go. But you will tell Mr
Prosser everything. Writers know about these things."

"Prosser will probably throw a roll at me."

"Then throw it back."

She departed in a cab, after making Owen promise to
telephone early.

At eleven-fifteen Owen entered the Savoy grill-room.

Prosser was already seated at a corner table, studying the
supper menu with the gravity of a financier reconstructing a
nation. He waved Owen into the opposite chair.

"Well?" he said. "What did you think of the show?"

"It was bad."

"Very bad?"

"Not quite as bad as the novel."

Prosser looked up sharply.

"Steady."

"I dislike the novel."

"Have some oysters."

"No, thank you."

"Soup?"

"No."

"Then what will you have?"

"Justice."

Prosser pushed the menu aside.

"That sounds indigestible. What's happened?"

Owen leaned across the table.

"Prosser, the woman who calls herself Edith Butler stole that
play from me."

For several seconds Prosser said nothing. A waiter approached, observed
his expression, and retired tactfully. Waiters at the Savoy develop
an instinct for emotional weather.

"What did you say?" Prosser asked.

"I said Edith Butler stole my play."

Prosser's beard seemed to stiffen.

"Do you know Edith Butler?"

"No."

"Have you ever met her?"

"No."

"Then why call her a thief?"

"Because four years ago I dramatized *Was It Her Heart?*
and sent her the manuscript. Tonight I saw my work
on the stage. The character Gerald is mine. Half his
speeches are mine. The scene with Sir Jasper is mine.
Even the joke about coal is mine."

Prosser stared at him.

Owen, carried forward by indignation, continued.

"She never answered my letter. She simply took the manuscript,
put her name to it, and had it produced. If
that isn't theft—"

"It isn't."

"What?"

"Edith Butler did not steal your play."

"How do you know?"

"Because I am Edith Butler."

Owen gaped.

It was the only adequate action.

Prosser poured himself a glass of water and drank it.

"Edith Butler is my pen-name," he said. "I've written under
it for fifteen years."

"You wrote *Was It Her Heart?*?"

"I did."

Owen looked at the beard, the shoulders, the large red
hands. He thought of Hyacinth's tremulous sacrifices, of moonlit gardens,
pressed flowers, whispered farewells and women who said "Ah, no!"
on balconies.

"You?"

"Yes, me."

"But—"

"I know. People always look like that. They expect Edith
Butler to be a widow with a lace cap and
a canary. Publishers expect it. Interviewers expect it. Once a
bookseller sent me a bouquet."

Owen's indignation reassembled itself.

"Then you admit you received my play?"

"I received a manuscript. No name. No address."

"My covering letter was inside."

"It wasn't when the parcel reached me."

"I put it there."

"Possibly. But it had gone."

"That's absurd."

"Not at all. The wrapping was torn. The manuscript arrived
in a state resembling a casualty after a railway accident.
There was no letter and no name anywhere on the
pages."

Owen tried to remember. He had posted the parcel during
a walking holiday on the south coast. The brown paper
had been inadequate; the string had been borrowed from a
grocer; and a sea wind had been blowing with enthusiasm.

Prosser continued.

"I read the thing. It was good."

Owen blinked.

"Good?"

"Very good in places. Bad in others. Your third act
wandered like a lost child, and you had no idea
how to get Hyacinth off the stage. But Gerald was
first-rate. I made inquiries. Couldn't trace the author."

"You might have advertised."

"I did."

"Where?"

"*The Author's Gazette*, *The Stage*, and two morning papers."

Owen had never read *The Author's Gazette*, seldom read theatrical
notices, and at that time took only a sporting evening
paper which specialized in racing tips and Middlesex cricket.

"Describe the manuscript," said Prosser.

"Blue paper. Typed in purple ink. The title page had
a smudge in the lower left corner."

"Anybody might know that."

"The second act ended with Gerald saying, 'If this is
honour, give me a modest helping of disgrace.'"

Prosser's eyes narrowed.

"Go on."

"The last page had the name of a hotel stamped
on the back. I used their writing-room."

"What hotel?"

"The Marine View, Eastbourne."

Prosser struck the table.

"By Jove!"

A spoon jumped into a dish.

"That's right," he said. "Marine View Hotel, Eastbourne. I remember
the stamp. I wrote there, but you'd gone."

"I left the next morning."

"And they had no forwarding address."

"I was walking to Seaford."

Prosser leaned back and regarded him. The anger had gone
from his face. He looked thoughtful and, to Owen's surprise,
embarrassed.

"This is most unfortunate," he said.

Owen found the understatement painful.

"Unfortunate?"

"For you. And for me. Though mostly for you."

"You used my work."

"I did. I believed the author had abandoned it or
would eventually come forward. I had a contract to deliver
a stage version and a producer snapping at my heels.
I altered the structure, rewrote most of the serious scenes,
and retained Gerald practically as you wrote him."

"Under your name."

"Yes."

Owen's jaw tightened.

Prosser held up a hand.

"Don't start bristling. That is my privilege. You will be
paid."

"Paid?"

"Certainly. You wrote a substantial part of the play. I'm
explosive, domineering, and according to one critic incapable of describing
female emotion without bringing in moonlight, but I'm not a
thief."

The waiter returned cautiously. Prosser ordered oysters, grilled chicken, potatoes,
salad, a bottle of champagne and, after looking at Owen,
a plate of bread.

"No throwing," he assured him.

Hope entered Owen's heart. It did not enter quietly. It
blew trumpets.

"What am I entitled to?" he asked.

Prosser rubbed his beard.

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On the receipts."

Owen's mind flew to Mr Sheppherd. It saw that gentleman
listening while figures were mentioned. It saw his starch soften,
his objections collapse, his pink carpet conquered.

"Has the play made much?"

Prosser looked grave.

"In London?"

"Yes."

"No."

The trumpets stopped.

"What do you mean?"

"It's a failure."

Owen stared.

"Tonight was the eighty-third performance."

"And probably the last. Houses have been bad for weeks.
The manager is taking it off on Saturday."

"But there were plenty of people there."

"Paper. Complimentary seats. Half the stalls had paid nothing, and
most of the rest had paid less than the price
of a decent umbrella."

Owen's heart turned to lead. It had been airborne for
approximately forty seconds.

"How much has it made?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"A loss, so far as London is concerned."

The champagne arrived. Prosser filled two glasses.

Owen ignored his.

"It can't be helped," he said.

There are occasions when the Englishman, standing among the ruins
of his dreams, finds comfort in sounding like a railway
official discussing rain.

Prosser drank.

"You wanted the money badly?"

"I wanted to marry."

"Who?"

"Audrey Sheppherd."

"Does she object to poverty?"

"Her father does."

"Fathers usually do. They regard affection as a luxury imported
from France."

"He says I have no prospects."

"Bad rot."

"He is right."

"Not now."

Owen looked at him.

"You said the play made nothing."

"In London."

"What do you mean, in London?"

Prosser selected an oyster.

"My dear Bentley, London is not the world. It merely
prints maps which give that impression."

Owen waited.

Prosser ate the oyster with maddening deliberation.

"The play opened in New York eighteen months ago," he
said. "Ran for forty-seven weeks."

Owen's fingers tightened around the edge of the table.

"What?"

"Chicago company ran thirty-two. Boston twenty-one. There are three touring
companies at present crossing America and one in Canada. The
Australians bought it last month. South Africa is negotiating. A
French version is being prepared, though heaven knows what they
will do with Gerald."

Owen made a small sound.

Prosser continued in the tone of a man discussing weather.

"The American manager paid an advance of ten thousand dollars.
Royalties since then have been substantial. Very substantial."

"How substantial?"

"I have not the exact accounts here. But if we
agree that your contribution represents, say, one-third of the dramatic
work—and I think one-third is fair—your share to date should
be somewhere between six and seven thousand pounds."

The grill-room became misty.

Owen blinked several times.

"Pounds?"

"Pounds."

"Six thousand?"

"Possibly nearer seven."

Prosser speared another oyster.

"And it will go on earning. Americans have taken kindly
to Gerald. There are Gerald dolls, Gerald collars, and a
cocktail called The Impecunious Cousin. I tried one in New
York. It contains gin, vermouth and financial anxiety."

Owen sat silent.

Six thousand pounds. Seven thousand pounds. The figures moved before
him in golden procession. Mr Gudgeon's entire career dwindled. Mr
Treadwell's office became a cupboard. Mr Sheppherd, financial stability and
the cost of coal receded into an unimportant distance.

Prosser watched him.

"Enough to satisfy the father?"

"I think," said Owen slowly, "it may weaken his position."

"Good. We'll see a solicitor tomorrow and have the agreement
drawn up. You'll get a cheque on account at once."

Owen lifted the champagne glass.

"To Edith Butler," he said.

Prosser grinned.

"To the man who ducked."

They drank.

At half-past twelve Owen left the Savoy and walked into
the Strand. London glittered about him. Cabs rolled past; lights
shone on wet pavement; a newspaper boy cried an edition
whose contents no longer mattered. Owen moved like one in
a dream.

At the corner of Wellington Street he stopped before a
closed baker's shop.

Behind the glass, arranged in solemn rows, stood loaves of
every size and description.

Owen removed his hat.

He had misjudged bread.

* * *

At ten the next morning he telephoned Audrey.

"Have you made your fortune?" she asked.

"Yes."

There was a pause.

Audrey was not often at a loss, but even great
commanders may be surprised when reinforcements arrive on the day
requested.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I have made it. Or part of it.
Six or seven thousand pounds, with more coming."

"Owen!"

"The play was mine. Prosser is Edith Butler."

"The bearded man?"

"Yes."

"How extraordinary."

"He is going to pay me my share."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

"Then we can marry."

"Yes."

"When?"

Owen considered.

"Soon."

"Saturday?"

"This Saturday?"

"Why not?"

"There are licences and things."

"Then Monday."

"We must speak to your father."

Audrey's voice became firm.

"I shall speak to him. You come at eight."

That evening Owen entered Mr Sheppherd's drawing-room for the second
time.

The carpet was unchanged, the chairs were unchanged, and Mr
Sheppherd still resembled a statesman. But the balance of power
had altered. Money, though condemned by poets, remains one of
the most effective forms of moral support.

Mr Sheppherd cleared his throat.

"Audrey informs me that certain developments have taken place."

"Yes."

"In connection with a theatrical venture."

"Yes."

"She mentions a sum of six thousand pounds."

"Possibly seven."

"And continuing royalties."

"Yes."

Mr Sheppherd folded his hands.

"I have always felt, Bentley, that you possessed latent ability."

Owen looked at him.

"Have you?"

"Undoubtedly. I may have appeared cautious. A father must be
cautious. But I have never opposed the match on personal
grounds."

"No."

"My objections were purely financial."

"They are gone now?"

"Substantially modified."

Audrey, seated beside the window, intervened.

"That means yes."

Mr Sheppherd drew himself up.

"I was about to say that, in view of the
altered circumstances, I am prepared to withdraw my opposition."

"Thank you," said Owen.

"There remains the question of your bank position. Literary income
can be uncertain."

"I shall keep my post for the present."

"Excellent. Prudence. Stability."

Audrey looked at Owen.

"You will leave the bank."

"When?"

"After we are married."

Mr Sheppherd began to speak. Audrey turned her grey eyes
upon him.

He reconsidered.

Later, in the hall, Owen took her in his arms.

"I knew you would do something big," she said.

"You told me to sit in a field."

"And you met Mr Prosser there."

"He threw a loaf at me."

"That was part of it."

Owen kissed her.

The human mind, when confronted with a series of coincidences,
seeks some governing principle. Philosophers call it causation; poets call
it destiny; bank managers call it irregular procedure. Owen thought
of Mrs Dorman in the Shropshire farmhouse, turning his cup
beneath the lamp and announcing wealth with such splendid disregard
for detail.

The next morning he sent her a postal order for
five pounds and a letter conveying the news. He checked
the address twice, enclosed the letter, sealed the envelope, and
carried it personally to the post-box.

Then he walked back twenty yards, reopened the envelope, and
examined it.

He had forgotten the stamp.

This time he corrected the omission, for a man with
pots of money may still respect the Post Office.

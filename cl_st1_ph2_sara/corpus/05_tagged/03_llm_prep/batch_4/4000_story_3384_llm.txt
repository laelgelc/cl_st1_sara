# The House Upon Great Hill

Nobody in Godsdale could say precisely when the house first
appeared upon Great Hill.

This was not because the people of Godsdale were unobservant.
They knew when Farmer Pounce had painted his barn, though
he had done it at night to avoid advice; they
knew when Widow Lark had entertained the commercial traveller from
Ipswich, and what he had eaten for breakfast; they knew,
within sixpence, the income of Parson Solewise, and, within three
glasses, the evening consumption of Mr. Ditton, the lawyer. Yet
concerning the mansion on Great Hill they disagreed by months,
by seasons, and even by years.

The hill had always been there, to be sure—a broad
green elevation above the village, with gorse upon its western
shoulder and, below the eastward slope, a marsh that flashed
like silver under the moon and like pewter under rain.
But the mansion? Some declared it had risen during the
great thunderstorm of June; others remembered seeing its pale chimneys
against February snow; old Mrs. Appleby maintained that her grandfather
had once stolen pears from its garden, although no pear
trees were visible there now, nor had Mrs. Appleby possessed
a grandfather of adventurous habits, so far as anybody could
discover.

Whatever the truth, there it stood: long, low, and noble,
with towers at either end, innumerable windows, and a portico
of grey stone through which the afternoon sun shone as
through smoke. At times its walls appeared white; at times
they took the color of old ivory; and once, when
a cloud crossed the hill, the entire western wing seemed
to dissolve, leaving only three chimneys unsupported in the air.

"A trick of perspective," said Schoolmaster Penn.

"A warning," said Parson Solewise.

"A very good property, if the title is sound," said
Mr. Ditton.

Then the dark lady came.

She was first seen on the Springwood Road in a
great red automobile, such as had never before alarmed the
Godsdale poultry. The machine was long in the bonnet, brass
at the lamps, black at the wheels, and noisy as
a minor apocalypse. A small, stiff chauffeur sat at the
steering-wheel. Behind him, upright among cushions and dark draperies, was
a woman so beautiful that those who saw her spoke
of her beauty reluctantly, as though it had been an
injury.

Her face was pale and narrow, her eyes black, her
hair blacker still; jewels flashed at her throat and wrists,
but she wore no hat, even in rain. She leaned
from one side of the automobile and then the other,
searching every ditch, hedge, milestone, tree root, gateway, and broken
wall.

"Turn!" she would cry. "No—the other road! Faster! It must
be near. It is precious—priceless! Lives depend upon it!"

The chauffeur obeyed with a patience which suggested either devotion
or deafness.

Down Springwood they went, back by Blueberry Lane, across Commoners,
up to the chalk pits, round the churchyard, through Mill
Hollow, and once, to the scandal of the congregation, directly
across the green during Sunday evensong. Dogs pursued them, children
cheered, horses climbed banks, geese dispersed in blasphemous indignation; and
wherever the red motor passed, the lady's dark head turned,
turned, turned.

She searched in heat and rain. She searched by dawn,
when the grass was pearl-grey, and at sunset, when the
windows of the mansion burned like plates of ruby. She
searched so long that wonder declined into familiarity. The villagers
named her the Mad Lady, as villagers will name what
they cannot understand, and presently they ceased to interrupt their
work when the automobile thundered by.

Only Parson Solewise continued to raise his hat.

"She may be mad," he said, "but grief has claims
upon courtesy."

One evening in late autumn the red automobile came down
Great Hill with extraordinary celerity. The sky was greenish above
the marsh, and lightning flickered without thunder behind a bank
of cloud. The chauffeur's cap had blown away. The Mad
Lady stood in the tonneau, clutching the speaking-tube.

"Straight!" she cried. "Straight ahead! I see it! By the
tree—the blasted tree! Faster!"

The road bent sharply toward Blueberry Lane, but she pointed
to a grassy causeway that ran into the marsh.

The chauffeur hesitated.

"Go on! It is there! It is there!"

The motor left the road, bounded over the turf, and
rushed upon the causeway. For fifty yards it advanced magnificently,
the brass lamps tossing, the lady's black draperies streaming like
torn banners. Then the ground darkened beneath the wheels.

The chauffeur fought the steering lever. The automobile slewed sideways.
One wheel sank, then another.

"Back!" cried the lady. "Forward! Left—no, right! Hurry, hurry! We
are almost there!"

The engine roared. Mud rose in oily fountains. The causeway,
which looked firm enough from the hill, trembled and broke
like the skin upon custard. The red body settled to
its axles.

Men came running from the village with ropes, gates, ladders,
poles, and all those implements which are never at hand
until their usefulness is nearly over. They heard the motor
still hammering and the woman crying contradictory commands; then the
marsh gave a long sigh.

The automobile went down.

For an instant the Mad Lady remained visible, standing high
against the storm, her white face turned not toward those
who would have saved her but toward the lightning-struck tree
beyond the causeway.

"My proof!" she called. "My precious proof!"

Then mud closed over the lamps, the cushions, the black
hair, the chauffeur's hands, and all.

The rescuers reached the place in time to see three
bubbles ascend.

Nothing was recovered that night. In the morning there was
only a pool of brown water, faintly iridescent with oil.

The mansion on Great Hill was searched. Its front door
stood open, and this was fortunate, because no one had
remembered to bring a key, though Mr. Ditton had brought
two bottles of brandy. Room followed room in pale and
disconcerting succession: a dining-hall with no table, a library with
no books, bedrooms without beds, fireplaces without ash. Black curtains
stirred at windows which, viewed from outside, appeared to have
neither glass nor opening.

No servant was found, nor plate, nor clothing, nor letter.
There was dust on the floors, but no footprint except
those of the searchers. In one upper chamber Parson Solewise
discovered a cracked mirror, and in it, he afterward declared,
he saw the room furnished richly in crimson, with a
woman seated before the fire. When he turned, there were
only bare boards and a fallen swallow's nest.

Schoolmaster Penn explained this by fatigue.

Mr. Ditton explained it by brandy.

The parson explained neither explanation.

Afterward the house became increasingly uncertain. From the village its
north tower stood clear against the sky; up close, no
north tower could be found. A balcony appeared in moonlight
and vanished at noon. Boys who entered by a ground-floor
window came out through a door two storeys above, which
was inconvenient but not fatal, the grass below being deep.
Nobody cared to live there. Nobody could ascertain ownership. Taxes
were demanded, disputed, postponed, forgotten, and at last transferred to
a ledger kept solely for hopeless matters.

Years passed. Ivy climbed the pale walls, if walls they
were, and rooks nested in chimneys that sometimes showed the
stars through them.

Then Mary Solewise came home.

She was the parson's only daughter, golden-haired, grey-eyed, and colored
like a rose petal held against a candle. She had
been educated in Canterbury, where she learned French, household arithmetic,
the lives of the saints, and enough piano to give
pleasure without creating expectations. More importantly, she possessed the fortunate
conviction that most difficulties could be reduced by boiling water,
a hammer, or fresh air.

With her came Julian Vale, a poet.

Godsdale regarded poets much as it regarded comets: with interest,
distrust, and some uncertainty concerning their means of support. Julian
was tall, lean, and cheerful; he wore a velvet coat
with pockets full of screws, string, verses, and carburettor parts.
He had published a volume entitled *The Seven Lamps of
Arcady*, which nobody in Godsdale had read except Schoolmaster Penn,
who had not understood it, and Mary, who had understood
at least the dedication. Julian was also able to mend
clocks, pumps, hinges, and combustion engines, practical gifts for which
the village forgave his metaphors.

Mary showed him Great Hill on the afternoon of their
arrival.

The house shimmered above the sward, half ruin, half palace,
its eastern windows blazing in the sun.

"Oh!" said Julian.

"Yes," said Mary.

"Who lives there?"

"Nobody."

"Then we shall."

This was how the matter began.

They climbed through nettles and entered the east wing, which
proved more stable than the rest. It contained four rooms
below, five above, a staircase, portions of two chimneys, and
a conservatory filled with elder bushes. Rain had damaged the
ceilings; owls had occupied the nursery; a fox had formed
opinions in the pantry. Yet the walls were thick, the
view magnificent, and wild roses pressed at the windows as
though eager to be invited in.

"This shall be the kitchen," cried Mary.

"This the library," said Julian.

"No, that is the nursery."

"We have no children."

"We have no books either."

"My darling, I have written books."

"One copy of each will not require a library."

He embraced her, having no satisfactory rebuttal.

They passed delightedly from room to room, assigning destinies faster
than carpenters could possibly fulfil them.

"Here we shall breakfast."

"Here I shall write my epic."

"Here we shall keep apples."

"Rows of lilies below the terrace!"

"Rows of roses above it!"

"A blue door!"

"A brass bell!"

"Children on the stairs!"

"Music in the hall!"

At this last the empty house returned a note, not
exactly an echo, but something like the long-drawn breath of
a sleeper.

Mary paused.

"Did you hear?"

"The acoustics," said Julian, who believed in ghosts but did
not intend to let one spoil their engagement.

The village opposed their plan affectionately and in detail. Schoolmaster
Penn said the foundations were unsound; Mr. Ditton said the
ownership was unsound; Farmer Pounce said the roof was unsound;
Mrs. Appleby said the late lady's morals had been unsound.
Parson Solewise listened to them all.

"Do you love him?" he asked Mary.

"Yes."

"Do you love her?" he asked Julian.

"Beyond speech."

"That is inconvenient in a poet, but sufficient. Mend the
east wing."

So they did.

They worked throughout the summer. Julian repaired beams and wrote
sonnets upon them. Mary scraped walls, burned rubbish, washed windows,
and coaxed a blackened range into life. Villagers who had
condemned the enterprise came up to assist in it, first
to prevent catastrophe and afterward because assistance was pleasant. Farmer
Pounce supplied timber; Penn measured angles; Ditton examined documents until
the documents seemed ashamed of themselves. The schoolchildren carried stones
and sang songs whose words they had misapprehended.

The house submitted by degrees. A door that had opened
upon a blank wall began opening into the scullery after
Julian rehung it. A staircase formerly containing seventeen or nineteen
steps settled upon eighteen. Chimneys drew. Floors ceased to slope.
The east wing, having been attacked by brushes, hammers, soap,
laughter, and Mary's decided commands, became as material as any
respectable building could desire.

They were married in September.

The wedding procession climbed Great Hill under a sky of
spotless blue. Mary wore cream silk and carried late roses;
Julian had lost one glove and found a chisel in
his pocket during the blessing. The church bells rang, then
the school bell rang, then Farmer Pounce fired a gun,
not ceremonially but because he had observed a rabbit at
an inopportune moment.

There was feasting in the restored hall. Mr. Ditton compared
the wine favorably with that at Cana, though his knowledge
of the latter vintage was speculative. Schoolmaster Penn recited Tennyson.
Children invaded the staircase, musicians occupied the pantry, and at
midnight Parson Solewise stood beneath the old beams and blessed
the house.

As he spoke, a gust passed through the ruined western
corridors. Candles bent; the pale walls trembled. Far away, or
perhaps directly overhead, a woman seemed to sigh.

Mary took Julian's hand.

The parson continued steadily: "Let what was lonely be inhabited;
let what was broken bear kindly weight; let memory surrender
its bitterness, and dreams accept the blessed burden of reality."

The wind fell.

Thereafter life entered the house with muddy boots.

A daughter was born, then a son, then another daughter
who arrived during a snowstorm and was called Candida because
Julian wished to call her Persephone. The nursery overflowed with
blocks, boats, dolls, shoes, books, crumbs, puppies, and theological questions.
Mary's kitchen shone with copper. Julian wrote poems at dawn
and repaired machinery after breakfast. His projected epic concerning the
Sephiroth and the Prince of the Powers of the Air
reached Book Three, where it remained, owing to the more
urgent claims of a broken pump.

Harvest suppers were held in the hall; the church choir
practised there when the vestry roof leaked; villagers danced there,
argued there, recovered from influenza there. One Christmas, thirty-seven people
dined under the cracked mirror, which now reflected only what
stood before it, though generally twice as cheerfully.

The western wing remained ruinous, and the north tower continued
to appear chiefly at a distance; but vines ran across
the broken masonry, gripping stone to stone with green fingers.
Whether they dragged the house down or held it together
was disputed. Mary planted lilies in rows and roses in
rows, exactly as promised. Julian built a blue door and
hung a brass bell.

Thus seven years passed.

Then the marsh surrendered the automobile.

A summer drought shrank the pools and hardened the causeway.
Boys hunting frogs discovered a curve of red metal beneath
the mud. Within a week the whole machine had been
raised by ropes, horses, profanity, and a mechanical winch invented
by Julian from parts not intended to cooperate.

The chauffeur and the Mad Lady were gone. No bone,
jewel, scrap of cloth, or button remained. But the automobile
emerged astonishingly complete: lamps clouded, leather blackened, brass green, wheels
clogged with reeds, yet recognizable.

"Bury it," said Mrs. Appleby.

"Exhibit it," said Mr. Ditton.

"Study it," said Penn.

"Mend it," said Julian.

Mary looked at the long red body, from which marsh
water dripped like tears.

"Must you?"

"My heart, it is a machine."

"So is a guillotine."

"But less useful afterward."

He restored it in a shed below the east wing.
The task occupied two winters and a summer. He cleaned
the cylinders, remade the valves, replaced leather, polished brass, straightened
rods, and spoke tenderly to bolts which resisted him. The
children assisted by losing tools. Mary disliked the automobile but
brought Julian tea, for marriage, among its higher mysteries, includes
the endurance of one another's absurdities.

At last the engine fired.

The report brought villagers running from every direction. Smoke billowed,
hens fled, and the motor shuddered as though awakening from
a nightmare. Julian emerged black-faced and triumphant.

"By George, she'll go!"

"Where?" asked Mary.

"Round the courtyard."

But the courtyard became the drive, the drive became Great
Hill Road, and, on a bright October morning, Mary found
herself seated beside Julian while half Godsdale assembled to witness
their departure.

"Only to Springwood and back," she stipulated.

"Only there."

"Not near the marsh."

"Not within a mile."

"And slowly."

"My treasure, we shall crawl like pilgrims."

The children waved from the terrace. Julian released the brake.
The automobile descended Great Hill with stately explosions.

For the first half-mile nothing occurred. The hedges were scarlet
with hips; spiders' threads flashed across the fields; smoke rose
blue from cottage chimneys. Mary began to laugh. The old
machine was absurd, magnificent, and unexpectedly smooth. Julian wore goggles
and an expression of sacerdotal bliss.

Then the speaking-tube hissed.

It hung beside Mary's shoulder, a flexible tube ending in
an ivory mouthpiece, meant for passengers to direct the chauffeur.
Julian had cleaned it, though there was no need, since
they sat together in front.

From its mouthpiece came a woman's whisper.

"Turn left."

Mary's laughter ceased.

"What is it?" Julian shouted above the engine.

"Nothing."

The whisper sharpened.

"Left! Blueberry Lane. Hurry!"

Mary seized Julian's sleeve. "Stop."

But at that instant the steering lever moved under his
hand—not violently, but with the pressure of another hand laid
over his. The automobile swung into Blueberry Lane.

"Did you do that?"

"No."

"Then stop!"

"Straight!" cried the tube. "No, right—the next right! Faster! It
is precious. Priceless. Lives depend upon it!"

Julian applied the brake. The pedal sank uselessly to the
floor.

The car accelerated.

They rushed beneath yellow branches, scattering leaves in whirlwinds. A
cart climbed the bank to avoid them. Julian wrestled with
the controls while Mary clung to the side.

"Right!" commanded the voice. "Back! Across Commoners! No—the chalk road!
Go like blazes!"

"It is she," Mary said.

The automobile flew through Commoners, passed the churchyard, circled Mill
Hollow, and returned by a lane so tangled with brambles
that no motor ought to have crossed it; yet thorns
bent away from the lamps, stones seemed to roll aside,
and the engine hammered with baleful delight.

The route was the Mad Lady's route. Old people recognized
it from tales; younger ones recognized only the red streak.
Godsdale heard the roaring approach and came to doors and
windows.

"Stop them!" cried somebody.

How? With a parish notice? With Schoolmaster Penn's umbrella? The
machine tore across the green while Julian called apologies and
Mary listened, white-faced, to the dead woman at her ear.

"Near now! Near! The blasted tree!"

Ahead lay the marsh.

The causeway stretched over it, dry at first, silver and
treacherous beyond. At its farther end stood the lightning-struck tree,
black against the sky.

Julian hauled upon the brake, cut the ignition, and even
attempted prayer, though with less technical assurance. Nothing availed. The
motor rushed on.

"Straight!" cried the voice. "It is here! It is here!"

Mary snatched the speaking-tube.

"Where?" she demanded.

The voice fell silent.

"Where is it? Tell me exactly!"

There came a sob, so intimate and desolate that Mary's
fear changed character.

"Beneath the tree. Three paces east. Deep. Oh, my precious—my
proof—"

Mary spoke with the authority that had subdued foxes, chimneys,
poets, and children.

"We shall find it. Do you hear me? We shall
find it. Now stop this car."

The engine died.

It died instantly, completely, twenty yards before the yielding edge
of the causeway. The automobile rolled forward, slowed, and rested
with its lamps pointed toward the blasted tree.

For some moments neither Mary nor Julian moved.

Then Julian said, "My dear, I have always admired your
way with machinery."

She began to tremble.

They crossed the remaining ground on foot, carrying a spade
found miraculously—or conveniently—in the rear compartment. The tree had been
split from crown to root. Three paces east lay a
patch of coarse grass, unremarkable except that no dew clung
to it.

Julian dug.

At two feet the spade struck metal.

They uncovered a little iron box, rusted but intact, sealed
by a lock whose key was nowhere. Julian broke it
with the spade.

Within, wrapped in decayed silk, were three objects: a lock
of fair hair, bright as harvest wheat; a plain gold
ring; and a folded document, stained, blistered, and written in
ink which water or time had rendered nearly illegible.

At its head could be discerned two names. Below were
words resembling *lawful*, *witness*, and *marriage*. A seal clung to
one corner.

Mary touched the fair hair.

"Hers?"

"No. Her hair was black."

"A child's, perhaps. Or a man's."

Julian studied the ring and paper. "This was what she
sought. Proof of a marriage, inheritance, birth—who knows? Something the
world denied her because she could not produce it."

"And so she made the house?"

"Perhaps."

Behind them the automobile's speaking-tube gave one long, gentle sigh.

When they turned, the red machine appeared ordinary, old, and
faintly ridiculous in the sunlight.

The box was taken to Great Hill. Mr. Ditton examined
the document sober, then after one glass, then after two,
without improvement. Schoolmaster Penn applied chemicals and produced a purple
stain. Parson Solewise read what remained and said that the
essential portion was beyond recovery, which, he observed, was often
the case with earthly proofs.

That evening Julian stood outside the house.

The sunset had turned the marsh to blood and silver.
Behind him the eastern rooms glowed warmly: Mary moved through
the kitchen; children quarrelled upstairs; a kettle sang; somebody practised
scales with conscientious violence. Yet the western walls trembled in
the dusk. A turret faded. For one breath the dining-hall
windows showed stars where there should have been plaster and
beams.

A terrible thought entered him.

What if the mansion had never been a house? What
if it were the Mad Lady's projection, built from furious
longing—a castle in the air compelled into visibility by grief?
And if so, what were its doors, its fires, its
nursery floors? What was the restored automobile? What would happen
now that the lost proof had been found and her
purpose ended?

Would the whole apparition melt?

Would Mary, the children, the years of marriage, the roses,
the poems, the blue door—would all tumble into moonlight because
they had trusted themselves to a dead woman's dream?

"We must leave," he said.

Mary had come quietly behind him. "Leave?"

"Tomorrow. Tonight, if we can. We'll go to your father's."

She looked at the house. Candlelight filled the windows, but
one window flickered in and out like a lantern seen
through rain.

For the first time since her wedding, Mary wished herself
elsewhere.

They summoned their friends.

Parson Solewise arrived without his hat; Penn came with notebooks;
Mr. Ditton brought brandy, ostensibly for the nerves of others.
They sat at the long table beneath the cracked mirror
while Julian explained.

"So," he concluded, "if the house was made by her
will, and her will is satisfied, it may cease."

Schoolmaster Penn tapped his fingers together. "Matter is vibration. Thought
also may be vibration. Persistence of form could therefore depend
upon—"

"Nobody understands you, Penn," said Ditton kindly.

"I understand myself."

"That is the gravest symptom."

Parson Solewise walked to the wall and laid his hand
upon it. The plaster was warm from the kitchen chimney.

"Was this wall here when you came?"

"Not quite," said Mary. "There was a gap."

"And the floor?"

"Rotten."

"The roof?"

"Mostly rain."

"The stairs?"

"Unreliable," said Julian.

The parson smiled. "Then whatever she made, you have remade."

"But if the foundation is no more than her thought?"

"Most foundations are thoughts before they are stones."

Penn brightened. "Precisely!"

"No," said Ditton, "not precisely. Much better expressed."

The parson continued. "A lonely will may summon an apparition,
perhaps. I do not deny it. The prophets saw cities;
Jacob saw a stair; the Zohar is full of worlds
hung upon letters. But an apparition inhabited, repaired, blessed, taxed—occasionally
taxed, Ditton—filled with births, meals, quarrels, songs, and hospitality, is
no longer the same thing."

Mr. Ditton raised his glass. "Especially if taxed."

Mary remained pale. "Can love make stone?"

"Why not?" asked her father. "Hatred has made prisons. Fear
has made fortresses. Vanity has built larger houses than this."

From above came a crash, followed by a child's voice:
"It was already broken!"

Julian looked at the ceiling.

Ditton laughed. "There is reality for you."

At that moment villagers began arriving, for news of departure
had spread with the supernatural speed common to villages. Farmer
Pounce entered carrying a ham, because crises made him hungry.
The choir came from rehearsal. Mrs. Appleby brought blankets. Children
crowded the passage; dogs followed; somebody lit the hall lamps;
somebody else opened a barrel.

"What's this about leaving?" demanded Farmer Pounce. "After I gave
you sound oak for that roof?"

"It may vanish," said Julian.

"Not my oak."

The hall filled. Voices rose, chairs scraped, the piano sounded,
and presently, because Godsdale could not confront metaphysical peril without
refreshments, a supper appeared. Loaves, cheese, apples, ham, pies, and
ale covered the table. The choir sang. The children ran
races in the corridor. Mrs. Appleby, who had once refused
to cross the threshold, fell asleep beside the fire.

The house ceased trembling.

Or, if it trembled, it trembled as any house does
under dancing feet.

Near midnight Mary went out alone. The moon stood over
the marsh; the red automobile rested in its shed; the
iron box lay buried now beneath the chapel wall, with
its ring, fair hair, and unreadable testimony.

The mansion rose behind her, pale and many-windowed. The north
tower was visible, though she knew there was no door
into it. Vines covered the western ruin. Above the east
wing, firm chimneys poured smoke into the stars.

For one instant she saw a dark woman upon the
terrace.

The figure was tall, veiled, and motionless. No beauty could
be distinguished in that shadowed face, but sorrow remained about
it like a garment.

Mary's hands grew cold.

"We found it," she said.

The figure inclined its head.

"You may rest now."

The dark outline thinned into moonlight. Whether it vanished, or
whether a cloud merely passed from before a cypress tree,
Mary could never say; and sooth to say, she never
tried very hard.

Julian found her there.

"Are you afraid?" he asked.

"A little."

"Shall we go?"

She looked through the windows. Within were her father's white
head, Penn's uplifted finger, Ditton's comfortable waistcoat, dancers turning, children
drowsing on benches, lamplight upon polished wood. The walls looked
solid; yet more than solidity held them. Every room contained
a memory, every threshold the print of accustomed feet. The
house had learned their voices.

"No," Mary said. "We shall stay."

They did.

In later years the mansion became the most dependable object
in Godsdale. Its eastern wing endured storms that carried off
lesser roofs; its blue door swelled every winter and stuck
every spring with admirable regularity. The north tower remained elusive
to strangers but was plainly visible in all family portraits.
Julian never completed his epic, though extracts were read at
the school by generations of children whose comprehension did not
noticeably increase. The red automobile was driven on feast days,
but its speaking-tube uttered nothing more alarming than the whistles
of small boys.

Mary's children grew, married, and brought children of their own.
Music continued in the hall. Roses climbed the terrace. Lilies
multiplied beyond the rows assigned to them. Workmen repaired the
west wing, finding walls where none had been and doors
where walls were expected; nevertheless they persevered, for builders are
accustomed to contradictions.

As for the Mad Lady, she passed from terror into
legend, from legend into a song, and from the song
into a game played by children upon the causeway when
the marsh was dry. Her proof remained unread, but perhaps
she no longer needed it. The world had refused to
believe her once; afterward a whole house had believed her
for years.

And if the mansion had begun as a dream, what
then?

There are dreams too frail for daylight, dreams that melt
at a cockcrow or a creditor's knock; but there are
others into which men and women carry chairs, cradles, bread,
tools, wedding rings, and all the cheerful lumber of existence.
They hang curtains in the apparition, light fires in it,
quarrel and forgive beneath its roof. Children are born there.
Friends cross its threshold. Vines grip its stones. At last
the castle in the air discovers that it has foundations—not
in earth alone, perhaps, but in habit, affection, memory, and
the innumerable small acts by which human beings persuade the
uncertain world to remain.

Thus the house upon Great Hill stood; and there, since
every tale must stop somewhere, we may leave it: pale
in the morning, golden at evening, occasionally doubtful in moonlight,
but warm at its heart, inhabited, and real.

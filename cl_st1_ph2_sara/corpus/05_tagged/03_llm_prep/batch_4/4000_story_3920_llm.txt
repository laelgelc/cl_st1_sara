### The Bashful Scout

Alfred Pomeroy was a very little man and very bashful.

There are little men who are noisy because Nature has
cheated them in inches, and there are bashful men whose
humility is merely a screen behind which they wait to
hear themselves praised. Alfred belonged to neither class. He was
little without resentment and bashful without calculation. His shoulders were
narrow, his hands almost delicate, and his pale mustache appeared
to have been put on by somebody who had afterward
regretted the liberty. When addressed suddenly by a woman, he
blushed to the roots of his sandy hair. When obliged
to pass between two persons, he went sideways, with a
perpetual sidling apology, as if he had trespassed upon their
continent.

Yet Alfred could read the prairie like a book.

He could tell by the bend of a crushed grama
stalk whether a horse had passed before or after sunrise.
He could find water where a plainsman of ten years
saw only dust, alkali, and discouragement. He rode as if
horse and rider had been fitted together at the factory;
he shot with either hand; and it was said that
he had killed five men, although he never mentioned the
circumstance unless some practical question made it necessary. Even then
he spoke of the dead in the embarrassed tone of
one confessing to having broken five cups at a tea
party.

Mr. Josiah Caldwell of Philadelphia engaged Alfred at Fort Kearny
to guide his party to Deadwood.

Mr. Caldwell had made his money in iron, which is
a heavy substance and gives the successful dealer in it
a corresponding solidity of opinion. He was a broad man,
well fed, clean-shaven, and accustomed to seeing opposition removed by
clerks. His daughter, Miss Virginia Caldwell, was handsome, spirited, and
twenty-two. Her fiancé, Mr. Allen Pembroke, was also handsome and
spirited, although in a somewhat more polished manner. He had
studied law, ridden after foxes, fenced at a club, and
once slept all night in a fishing hut without a
servant. He consequently believed himself fitted for the frontier.

The Caldwells had three wagons, two servants, six riding horses,
a folding bathtub, cases of preserves, and enough blankets to
relieve a small military post. They were attached to a
larger train of twenty-seven wagons bound northwest, and Mr. Caldwell
had no intention of suffering merely because the country was
called wild. He held that hardships, like beggars, should be
discouraged by firm treatment.

On the second evening, as Alfred sat by the fire
repairing a bridle, Allen asked how many Indians he had
killed.

Alfred lowered his eyes.

"I don't rightly recollect, sir."

"Oh, come," said Allen. "Surely that is not a number
one forgets."

"Well, sir, some was mixed up in fights, an' some
fell where I couldn't go to count 'em. A body
might make a mistake."

Virginia laughed.

"Mr. Pomeroy is afraid of boasting."

Alfred turned red.

"Yes'm."

"Have you ever been afraid of anything else?"

"Yes'm," said Alfred. "Plenty things."

This answer delighted them. For the next week they made
a mild game of Alfred's timidity. If a coyote cried
after dark, Allen would ask whether it was safe to
remain in camp. If Alfred hesitated before entering the Caldwell
tent to report the next day's march, Virginia would assure
him that nobody inside intended to scalp him. Alfred accepted
these pleasantries with a troubled smile. He seemed grateful when
they ended.

Only the wagon master, Hiram Blunt, did not laugh. Blunt
was built like a stump and had the emotional resources
of one. On the third occasion he removed his pipe
and said:

"If Alfred tells you to crawl under a wagon, you
crawl. Don't stop to inquire if your clothes'll get dusty."

Allen smiled.

"We are in capable hands, then."

"You are," said Blunt. "Question is whether the hands have
got capable heads to manage."

That evening Alfred found Allen and Virginia riding a mile
south of the train.

He came toward them at an easy lope, hat pushed
back and face apologetic.

"Beg pardon, miss. Beg pardon, Mr. Pembroke. We're holdin' close
to the wagons from here on."

"Why?" Allen asked.

"Country's uncertain."

"It looks remarkably empty."

"Yes, sir," said Alfred. "That's one of its ways."

He had seen no Indians. What he had seen was
less definite and therefore, to him, more important: a little
smoke too thin for a grass fire, a jack rabbit
running from country in which no wolf showed itself, and
three crows rising together from a hollow. No single sign
meant anything. Three signs meant that a careful man kept
his people near.

Allen wished to know this reasoning, and Alfred explained it.
Allen then examined the horizon through a pair of field
glasses and saw nothing.

"There isn't a living creature for ten miles."

"No, sir," Alfred replied softly. "Not one that wants seein'."

Virginia turned her mare toward camp.

"I suppose we must obey our protector. Are you always
so severe, Mr. Pomeroy?"

Alfred's horse shied at a bleached buffalo skull. He settled
the animal with one hand.

"No'm. Only when I'm scared."

This renewed their amusement; but they returned.

The plains in those days possessed a treachery peculiar to
vast and beautiful things. They appeared to offer liberty because
they provided no visible boundaries. A person bred among streets
and doors felt that he might ride in any direction,
as a child entering a great room supposes everything in
it has been arranged for play. Yet the absence of
fences did not imply the absence of ownership, observation, thirst,
weather, or death. A ridge that looked half a mile
away might consume an hour. A hollow could conceal a
hundred horsemen. The clean blue distance, which Eastern travelers admired
as a symbol of infinity, was to Alfred a wall
covered with writing.

Virginia admired the prairie, but she could not read it.

She had been surrounded all her life—by family, servants, furniture,
obligations, and people who knew her father. Even the journey
west had not released her. There were always wagons creaking
behind, outriders ahead, Allen beside her, and Alfred appearing from
nowhere with a timid request that she not do whatever
she had just decided to do. The very magnitude of
the country sharpened her desire to be alone in it.

On the morning of the twelfth day beyond Kearny, the
train crossed a shallow creek and halted to rest the
teams. The banks were lined with cottonwoods, and for half
an hour there was confusion enough to hide an army:
mules braying, children shouting, men filling casks, women shaking dust
from bedding, and Hiram Blunt blaspheming at a wheel that
had settled to the hub in mud.

Virginia saddled her gray mare.

She told Allen she was going to the far end
of the encampment to sketch the creek. Allen was shaving
in a small mirror hung from a wagon bow. He
said he would join her presently.

Virginia did not go to the far end of the
encampment. She passed behind the cottonwoods, followed the creek east
for a little distance, and then rode north over the
rise.

For the first time in many weeks no one watched
her.

She laughed aloud from pleasure. The mare, feeling the loosened
rein, stretched into a gallop. Dust smoked behind them. Larks
sprang from the grass, uttering bright little notes, and the
wind pulled at Virginia's hat until she took it off
and let it hang by its ribbon. She rode toward
a low line of hills which seemed near enough to
touch.

Meanwhile Alfred, three miles west, was kneeling beside a hoofprint.

It had been made by an unshod pony. That by
itself was not alarming; stray ponies, Indian hunters, and travelers
from small trading camps all crossed that country. But the
earth at the toe was freshly crumbled, and on a
thorn near the trail Alfred found a short thread of
red wool. A hundred yards on, he found another print,
then five, then the faint parallel scars of lodge poles
dragging over the soil.

He followed these scars for a quarter mile. There they
ceased.

That was the important fact.

A village moving with women, children, dogs, and possessions left
lodge-pole trails. A war party did not. The pony tracks
continued northwest without the dragging poles. Alfred found the impression
of a moccasined foot where a rider had dismounted, and
beside it a blue glass bead no larger than a
pinhead. He touched the bead, looked at the sun, and
returned to camp at a gallop.

He did not look bashful when he found Blunt.

"Close up them wagons. Put the loose stock inside. No
shootin', no ax work, no noise that ain't needed."

Blunt's pipe went into his pocket.

"How many?"

"Can't say. Thirty, maybe more. Sioux. Passed before sunup, then
turned."

Blunt began shouting orders. Men who had ignored a quiet
request from Alfred moved rapidly at Blunt's roar, which is
a common injustice suffered by soft-spoken competence.

Alfred went to Caldwell's wagon.

"Where's Miss Virginia?"

Allen wiped soap from one cheek.

"Sketching. Somewhere by the creek."

Alfred stared at him.

It was not a long stare, but Allen afterward remembered
it better than many spoken accusations.

"We was to hold in camp."

"She has not gone far."

Alfred turned to the servant.

"Which horse?"

"The gray, sir."

He ran to his bay gelding. Mr. Caldwell caught his
sleeve.

"What is wrong?"

"War party close."

The wealthy man's face changed.

"My daughter—"

"I'm a-goin' fer her."

"I shall come with you."

"No, sir."

It was said without volume, yet Mr. Caldwell released the
sleeve. Bashfulness had left Alfred as completely as if he
had dropped it beside the trail. His eyes were gray
and flat; his narrow body seemed made of wire.

Allen came forward, still carrying his towel.

"I'll ride with you."

"You'll stay."

"I am responsible for Virginia."

"Then you ought've watched her."

Allen flushed and stepped nearer.

"You forget yourself."

Alfred swung into the saddle.

"No, sir. I'm rememberin' her."

He rode away before Allen could answer.

The gray mare had left a plain track where the
grass was sparse. Virginia had made no effort to hide
her direction, because hiding assumes danger, and she had ridden
away to escape assumptions. Alfred followed swiftly, slowing only where
stone broke the trail. Twice he dismounted. Once he rode
in a widening circle until he found the mark of
the mare's hind shoe in a patch of sand.

He knew how long a frightened father would wait before
following despite instructions. He knew Blunt would hold him if
he could. He also knew that the war party, having
seen the train or its dust, might be watching from
any of the folds ahead.

Ten minutes later he found proof.

A pony had crossed Virginia's track after she passed. Another
had crossed fifty yards farther on. Neither rider had approached
the gray mare's trail closely enough to be accidental. They
had seen her and were ranging beside her course.

Alfred leaned forward.

The bay gelding had been selected for endurance rather than
beauty. His head was ugly, his ears long, and his
temper unchristian; but he could run. He ran now with
his belly close to the grass.

Virginia had reached the low hills and discovered that they
were not hills at all, but the raised edge of
a broad plateau. Beyond lay another sweep of prairie, punctured
everywhere by pale mounds. Small brown animals sat upright beside
the holes, squeaking indignantly at her approach.

A prairie-dog town sometimes covered hundreds of acres. It was
a cheerful place to look upon and a poor one
to cross at speed. The burrows broke horses' legs, the
loose mounds turned under hoof, and rattlesnakes frequently accepted lodging
in the unoccupied chambers. Virginia, knowing none of this, rode
among the mounds in delight.

The animals vanished before her in successive rows, each dropping
with a whisk of tail and a final bark. She
guided the mare from hole to hole, laughing at their
gravity.

Then she saw a rider on the ridge to the
east.

He sat motionless against the sky. A second appeared, then
a third. She turned west and saw four more.

For several seconds she did not understand. They were too
distant, too picturesque, and too silent to be connected with
her. Then one rider lifted an arm. A long wavering
cry passed along the ridge.

The gray mare trembled.

Virginia wheeled her southward. Three horsemen came from that direction
at a canter.

She pulled up. The solitude she had desired closed around
her like a fist.

A shot sounded from behind.

One of the three riders pitched sideways. Before the body
struck earth, the other two had turned. Alfred came over
a low rise on the bay gelding, rifle across the
saddle. He did not shout to Virginia. He pointed toward
the center of the prairie-dog town, and she rode there.

He joined her beside a broad mound.

"Oh, Mr. Pomeroy!" she cried.

"How many'd you see?"

"I don't know. They are everywhere."

"Yes'm. 'Bout thirty."

His voice was again soft, but the softness now had
no apology in it. He dismounted and pulled the reins
over the gelding's head. Virginia watched him examine the ridges.

"Can we ride back?"

"No'm."

"Why not? You broke through them."

"They let me. Wanted me in here too."

The mounted men were spreading around the town. They kept
beyond good rifle range, riding in and out of view,
their figures dark in the noon glare. Some carried repeating
rifles, some older muzzle-loaders, and others bows. Frontier gossip often
armed every warrior with the latest Winchester or else with
nothing more formidable than a stone ax. The truth, as
usual, lacked neatness. Weapons came by trade, theft, war, gift,
and the progressive dishonesty of men who sold anything that
brought profit. A party of thirty might possess six excellent
rifles, ten poor ones, and ammunition suited to neither.

Alfred counted the visible guns.

Virginia gripped her reins.

"What shall we do?"

"Get down, miss."

She obeyed. Her skirt caught on the saddle horn, and
under other circumstances Alfred would have turned scarlet and looked
away. He freed it with a quick jerk.

"Lead your mare behind that big mound. Keep low."

A bullet clipped the grass with a dry whisper.

Virginia ducked.

"Are they shooting at us?"

"Not particular yet."

He placed both horses in a shallow depression where two
mounds nearly joined. Then he took cartridges from his belt
and arranged them on the ground. His Winchester held fifteen.
He had forty-three more in his belt, six in a
pocket, and two revolvers. Against thirty men these represented either
abundance or very little; arithmetic under fire depends largely upon
who misses first.

Virginia watched his hands.

They did not shake.

"Mr. Pomeroy," she said, "I am sorry."

"Yes'm."

"I should have listened to you."

"Yes'm."

She almost smiled, because his agreement contained no comfort at
all.

"Do you think they mean to kill us?"

He looked around the encircling ridge.

"No'm."

Her relief came too fast. Alfred saw it and was
obliged to continue.

"They'll kill me if they can."

"And me?"

"They'll take you."

The relief disappeared.

One of the warriors galloped near, hanging along his pony's
far side so that little showed above the animal's back.
He fired beneath the pony's neck. The ball struck a
mound and threw dust into Alfred's face.

Alfred fired once.

The pony went down. The rider rolled free, got to
his knees, and dragged himself toward a hole, one leg
trailing uselessly. Alfred could have shot again but did not.

Virginia saw his expression and understood something of their position.

"What happens if they take me?"

"Don't study on it."

"Tell me."

"No'm."

"Tell me!"

Alfred glanced at her. She had become very pale, but
she stood straight.

He drew a small revolver from inside his coat. It
was a five-shot weapon with a pearl grip, absurdly pretty
for its purpose. He checked the chambers and held it
out.

"You know how to use one?"

"Yes. Allen taught me."

Alfred put it into her hand.

"If I go down, you wait till they're close. Put
it here."

He touched his own temple.

For the first time his gaze wavered.

"You don't turn it aside, an' you don't shut your
eyes till you pull. Understand?"

Virginia stared at the pistol.

The men on the ridge shouted to one another. Their
cries arrived distinctly through the still air.

"You mean I must kill myself."

"If I'm dead."

"I could shoot them."

"You might get one."

"And save the last bullet?"

"Yes'm."

She swallowed.

"How can you say such a thing so calmly?"

"I ain't calm."

His hands still did not shake, but sweat shone around
his mouth.

A second shot passed overhead. The bay gelding snorted and
pulled back against the reins.

Alfred turned to the ridges again. The Sioux were testing
them, seeking some sign of panic or poor shooting. They
had no desire to charge two armed people across a
prairie-dog town if patience would do the work. Yet patience
belonged more readily to individuals than to mounted parties. Courage
moves in waves. So does caution. One bold man can
carry twenty cautious ones forward, just as one visibly frightened
man can turn twenty bold ones back.

Alfred meant to frighten the first wave.

He had observed that several of the riders possessed poor
weapons and that none cared to enter the broken ground
alone. If they charged together, their ponies would be forced
to pick through the mounds. The line would break, speed
would fall, and each rider would become aware that he
was no longer hidden inside a crowd. Alfred did not
need to kill thirty. He needed to persuade five or
six that they were about to be killed. Those five
or six would persuade the rest.

This principle is not confined to Indians, nor to war.
It may be observed in markets, churches, legislatures, and any
gathering where men borrow conviction from the number of persons
standing near them.

"Get down behind there," Alfred told Virginia. "Don't show your
head."

She crouched behind the largest mound, the pistol held against
her breast.

"Will they charge?"

"Soon."

"How do you know?"

"They're through talkin'."

The riders had gathered on the northern ridge. They formed
no parade-ground line, but a loose crescent two ranks deep.
Feathers and strips of cloth moved in the wind. Sunlight
flashed on a rifle barrel. One man rode before the
others, turning his pony with pressure of his knees.

Alfred rested the Winchester across the mound.

"Can you stop them?"

"I reckon."

"All of them?"

"Ain't necessary."

His voice had acquired its soft Southern burr again. This
was not bashfulness returning, but habit. A man may speak
gently while preparing to do a hard thing.

The leader raised his arm.

Virginia began to pray. She had never prayed under conditions
that required an immediate answer, and she found that the
elegant phrases learned in childhood were badly arranged for speed.
She tried to remember her father, Allen, and home. Instead
she saw absurd details: a silver-backed brush on her dressing
table; the green velvet chair in her aunt's parlor; Allen's
face half covered in shaving soap.

The raised arm fell.

The charge came.

At first the hoofbeats were scattered, like large drops striking
a roof. Then they joined and deepened. The riders descended
the slope in a widening mass, crying out, ponies stretched
beneath them. Dust rose behind the line. The sound seemed
to enter the ground and come up through Virginia's knees.

Alfred did not fire.

A bullet hummed past like wind through bare branches. Another
struck between his hands. He remained kneeling.

He was waiting for the distance at which retreat would
become visible as fear. At two hundred yards, a horseman
turns because he has reconsidered. At fifty, he turns because
he believes death has reached for him. Alfred wished every
rider to see the first horses fall and the first
men tumble directly beneath the ponies behind them. He would
fire first at the leader, then at the two nearest
riflemen, then low into the thickest part of the line.
He expected the prairie-dog holes to assist.

Still he waited.

Virginia raised her head.

"Stay down!" he snapped.

She dropped behind the mound.

The charge struck the edge of the town. Its clean
front broke immediately. Ponies swerved, jumped, and crowded one another.
One plunged forefeet into a burrow and turned completely over,
flinging its rider ahead. Another crashed against it. The crescent
divided and flowed around the fallen animals like terns parting
around a rock.

Alfred smiled without amusement.

"There now," he murmured.

Seventy yards.

Sixty.

The nearest rider leaned over his pony's neck. His face
was painted black across the eyes. He was young; Alfred
could see that much.

Fifty yards.

Alfred rose to one knee and brought up the rifle.

The earth beneath him collapsed.

A prairie-dog burrow, weakened by the horses' trampling and by
his own weight at its lip, gave way with a
muffled crack. Alfred dropped waist-deep into the ground. His rifle
flew from his hands, struck the mound, and fired. The
bullet went harmlessly into the sky.

Virginia saw him vanish.

She heard the shot.

She heard the charging cries close upon them.

There are moments in which the mind does not reason
but merely obeys the last clear command given to it.
Alfred had told her what capture meant. He had told
her what to do if he fell. She had watched
him disappear and heard his weapon discharge. Terror left no
room for examination.

She put the pearl-handled pistol to her temple.

Alfred clawed at the crumbling earth.

"Hold on!" he shouted. "I ain't—"

The small pistol cracked.

For a fraction of a second all motion seemed suspended.

Then Alfred dragged himself from the hole.

The charge had already failed.

His accidental shot, the sudden disappearance of his body, the
broken ground, and the fallen horses had produced among the
attackers precisely the uncertainty he intended to create by better
methods. The young leader jerked his pony aside. The horse
behind collided with him. Another rider fired wildly and wheeled.
The movement passed backward through the party. Men who could
not see the cause saw only companions turning away; and
since no man wishes to be the sole brave person
in a retreat, they followed.

The line split to either side. Ponies leaped mounds, stumbled,
recovered, and fled for the ridge. One rider stopped long
enough to pull an injured man across his saddle. Two
others dragged themselves among the holes. Within half a minute
the thunder of the charge was receding across the prairie.

Alfred did not look at them.

Virginia lay on her side behind the mound.

The pistol had fallen from her hand. Her hat ribbon
stirred in the grass. There was very little blood at
first—less than seemed reasonable for so great an injury—and her
expression held astonishment rather than pain.

Alfred crawled to her.

"Miss?"

He touched her throat.

"Miss Virginia?"

The pulse fluttered once beneath his fingers, or he imagined
it. He lifted her shoulders, then lowered them. There are
injuries which invite effort and injuries which rebuke it. Alfred
had seen enough of both.

He sat beside her.

The riders gained the ridge and gathered there, diminished by
distance. A few fired back, but the bullets fell short.
One struck the earth twenty paces away and made a
small, contemptible puff.

Alfred took off his hat.

He had warned her. He had reached her. He had
broken the charge without needing to kill another man. Every
part of his plan had succeeded except the part for
which the plan existed.

"I'm sorry," he said.

His voice was scarcely audible.

A breeze moved over the prairie-dog town. The little animals,
having no respect for grief and only a limited memory
for danger, began to appear again at the farthest holes.
One sat upright and barked. Another answered. The ordinary world
resumed by degrees.

Alfred noticed that Virginia's skirt had been thrown above one
boot when she fell. He looked away at once, and
the old blush rose painfully into his face.

Then, with great care, he drew the cloth down and
arranged it over her ankles.

Afterward he picked up his rifle.

Three men remained within the town.

The first was the rider whose pony Alfred had shot.
He had crawled into a shallow burrow, but his wounded
leg lay outside. He watched Alfred approach and reached for
a knife.

Alfred shot him through the chest.

The second was trapped beneath a fallen pony. He struggled
to lift his rifle. Alfred shot him in the head.

The third had a broken thigh and was pulling himself
toward the ridge with both hands. He heard Alfred behind
him and rolled over. He was perhaps sixteen years old.
His face was bare of paint, and fear had made
him look younger.

Alfred stood above him.

Ordinarily he would not have killed an incapacitated enemy. Frontier
men later explained these three shots by saying that the
wounded warriors might have fired on the wagons, that Alfred
could not guard prisoners, or that necessity on the plains
wore a rough coat. None of these explanations was true.

Alfred looked back at Virginia.

Then he shot the boy.

He returned to her and sat down, holding the rifle
across his knees. His hat remained on the ground. The
sun moved westward. Far off, the surviving warriors lingered on
the ridge but did not come nearer.

Presently Virginia's gray mare pulled free and wandered among the
mounds. Alfred caught her, tied both horses, and waited.

He did not weep. Alfred was a very little man
and very bashful, and grief, like company, made him wish
to occupy as little space as possible.

An hour later he saw dust in the south.

Hiram Blunt came first with eight armed riders. Allen rode
beside him, and Mr. Caldwell followed on a borrowed horse,
bouncing heavily in the saddle. Blunt spread the men before
entering the prairie-dog town, for he understood holes, ambushes, and
the uselessness of reaching tragedy with a broken neck.

Allen saw Virginia and spurred ahead.

Alfred rose.

"Where is she?" Allen cried, though he could see.

Alfred stepped aside.

Allen dismounted badly and fell to his knees. Mr. Caldwell
arrived moments later. He looked at his daughter, looked at
the small pistol in the grass, and then turned upon
Alfred.

"What happened?"

Alfred lowered his eyes. Bashfulness had returned.

"They charged, sir. I told her if I went down
she was to use the pistol. Ground broke under me.
She thought I was dead."

Mr. Caldwell stared at him.

"And you were not?"

"No, sir."

Allen made a sound and covered his face.

Blunt looked over the churned earth, the dead ponies, the
three bodies, and the rifle still held loosely in Alfred's
hand. He understood most of it without further explanation.

"Did they run?" he asked.

"Yes."

"How many did you get?"

Alfred glanced toward the dead.

"Four."

Blunt's eyes rested on the three who had been shot
where they lay. He asked no more.

The men made a litter from two rifles and a
blanket. Mr. Caldwell walked beside it. Allen led Virginia's mare.
Alfred mounted the ugly bay and rode last, a little
apart from the others, while behind them the prairie dogs
came out and stood upright among the empty holes.

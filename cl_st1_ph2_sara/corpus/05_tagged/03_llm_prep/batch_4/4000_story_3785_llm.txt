# Silverhorns

The railway has never yet been accused, by any candid
man, of permitting punctuality to interfere with its arrangements; nevertheless,
Dudley Hemenway considered a delay of three hours unreasonable even
for the North Country.

It was half past eleven on a wet October night.
A chilly haze lay over the little station at Lac
des Pins, turning the lamps to smoky orange blurs and
laying a thin varnish upon the rails. Water dripped from
the eaves with a maddening regularity. Inside, the waiting-room stove
glowed red in its joints and baked one side of
every traveller while the draught under the door refrigerated the
other. Two lumbermen slept upon a bench, their heads fallen
together like conspirators. A woman with a fretful child sat
among battered valises tied with rope. Somewhere behind the ticket
window the telegraph clicked, paused, and clicked again with the
important secrecy of an insect carrying government dispatches.

Hemenway had exhausted the newspapers, the railway map, the advertisements
for liver pills, and such Christian resignation as seven unsuccessful
moose hunts had left him. He stood now on the
platform beneath the dripping roof, a long, lean figure in
a stained shooting coat, with a gun case, a canvas
pack, and a pair of snowshoes arranged about him like
exhibits for the prosecution.

Beyond the tracks the village lights floated uncertainly in the
fog. In the yard a locomotive hissed and sizzled patiently,
letting off little gusts of steam as though muttering to
itself about the incompetence of mankind. Its engineer leaned from
the cab and smoked a short black pipe.

"Is that you, Angus?" Hemenway called.

The engineer removed the pipe.

"Unless I've changed in the mist," he answered. "And if
that's Dud Hemenway, ye've come home without a moose again."

Hemenway crossed the wet rails. "How can you tell?"

"By the melancholy droop of your shoulders, man. If ye'd
killed one, your chest would be two foot farther east
than the rest of ye."

Angus McLeod was a square, grizzled Scot who had driven
engines through the Canadian forests so long that he appeared
to have acquired some of their properties. His beard was
the colour of cinders, his face was seamed like a
firebox door, and his blue eyes shone with the cheerful
vigilance of signal lamps. He regarded hunting as a form
of murder practised by gentlemen who had not the courage
to become butchers, though he ate venison whenever it was
offered and criticised the cooking with authority.

"When does the express arrive?" Hemenway asked.

"Officially, ten-forty."

"I know when it officially arrives."

"Then ye ken more than I do. There's a washout
south of St. Fabien, a freight stalled at Mile Ninety,
and the superintendent in his bed. The Lord alone could
tell when she'll be here, and He has other afflictions
to supervise."

Hemenway looked at the waiting-room, where the child had begun
a thin, monotonous wail.

"Come into the cab," said McLeod. "There's warmth there, and
I've coffee that would dissolve a horseshoe. Ye can confess
your crimes while we wait."

"I have committed none."

"Then confess your intentions. They're nearly as black."

The locomotive had been coupled to a short local train
standing on a siding. McLeod was to take it west
after the express passed. In the cab the furnace light
leaped upon brass handles, gauges, oily steel, and the solemn
face of the fireman, young Baptiste Leduc, who acknowledged Hemenway
with a nod and continued polishing an injector as if
the salvation of his soul depended upon it.

McLeod poured coffee from a tin pot. It was hot,
black, and of such strength that Hemenway suspected it had
already served one term as axle grease.

"Well?" said the engineer. "How many innocent creatures have escaped
ye this time?"

"All of them."

"A merciful Providence!"

"One bull in particular."

"Ah! The famous Silverhorns?"

Hemenway looked up. "You've heard of him?"

"Every guide from here to Chicoutimi has heard of him.
Old bull, near seven foot at the shoulder; spread like
a rocking chair; one tine white as a bishop's conscience—which
is to say, white enough at a distance. Is that
the beast?"

"That is the beast."

"And ye've been trying to murder him these seven years."

"Hunt him, Angus."

"Murder him."

"Hunt."

"If a man creeps through the bushes with a rifle
and puts a bullet behind an animal's shoulder, what does
the catechism call it?"

"Sport."

"The catechism ye studied must have been printed in Boston."

Hemenway smiled in spite of himself. "Very well. I have
spent seven seasons trying to murder Silverhorns, and I have
failed with a thoroughness that ought to satisfy you."

"It does not merely satisfy me," said McLeod. "It strengthens
my faith."

Rain ticked upon the cab roof. Far down the line
a switch lamp showed green, suspended in the vapour. McLeod
settled against the side of the cab and folded his
arms.

"Begin at the beginning," he said. "A failure properly narrated
is better than most victories."

"The beginning was seven years ago. Billy Brassard and I
were hunting north of the Crooked Water. We found a
track in the mud beside a beaver pond—a track so
large Billy laid his cap in it and had room
left for his tobacco pouch."

"Billy's cap is no standard of measurement. His head is
scarcely larger than a turnip."

"It was a tremendous track. We followed it through black
spruce and alder until noon. Then, in a little burnt-over
patch, I saw the bull for perhaps three seconds. His
antlers were pale at the tips, almost silver. That was
how Billy named him."

"And ye fired?"

"I had no shot. He was behind a windfall."

"A weak excuse, but continue."

"The next year we found him again, ten miles east.
The year after that, farther north. Once I caught sight
of him crossing a ridge at sunset. Once we heard
him thrashing a birch. Once he came within thirty yards,
but there were three young trees between his shoulder and
my sights. Every season something happened."

"Predestination," said McLeod. "The beast was appointed to live, and
ye were appointed to waste cartridges."

"This year I determined to settle the question."

"A declaration which must have caused great anxiety in heaven."

Hemenway stretched his wet boots toward the firebox. His irritation,
warmed by the cab and encouraged by an audience, began
to loosen into narrative pleasure.

"We went in by canoe from Saint Aurèle—Billy, young Tom
Vane, and I. You know Tom?"

"The lad with spectacles?"

"He wears them for reading."

"And for shooting?"

"He claims not."

"Then he shoots without seeing. There's theology in that too."

"We had twelve days. The first four it rained without
interruption. Not ordinary rain, but a deliberate descent of the
whole Atlantic Ocean. The tents leaked, the blankets smoked, the
flour became paste, and the woods were so full of
water that whenever you leaned against a tree another shower
fell from it. Billy said the moose had probably learned
to swim and gone south."

"Billy has imagination."

"On the fifth morning the wind shifted north and the
sky cleared. We found fresh sign above Long Pond: bark
newly stripped, mud still oozing in the hoofprints, and a
bed warm enough for Billy to swear the bull had
risen when he heard us."

"Did he?"

"No. Billy swears warmly on cold evidence. But the sign
was fresh. We followed all day and came at last
to a ridge above a marsh. From there we could
see two miles of yellow grass, scattered pools, and little
islands of spruce. At the far end stood a cow
and calf."

"Family life," said McLeod. "A sacred institution, even among your
intended victims."

"We lay there until dusk. Just as the light began
to go, the alders moved below us. First I saw
the palms of antlers; then the head; then the whole
bull stepped out."

"Silverhorns?"

"There was no mistaking him. His antlers seemed wider than
the space between two trees. The right palm was dark,
but the left carried three bleached tines that shone against
the alders. He stood quartering away at seventy yards."

McLeod leaned forward despite his principles. "A fair shot?"

"The fairest I had ever had at him—except that I
was on the wrong side of Billy. Tom was behind
us, the bull was below, and a dead spruce lay
across my knees. To move the rifle I had to
lift it over Billy's back."

"Why not shoot from where ye were?"

"A bullet through Billy would have spoiled the guide."

"It might have improved him."

"I raised the rifle inch by inch. Billy lay flat,
holding his breath. The bull stood listening. I got the
barrel clear, drew down on the shoulder—and the dead spruce
rolled."

"Rolled?"

"Six inches. That was enough. It dragged another branch, which
dragged another, and the entire slope seemed to crack from
top to bottom. The bull went through the alders like
a storm. I fired at a grey patch and cut
a twig."

"First attempted murder prevented by faulty furniture," McLeod pronounced.

"We trailed him until dark. No blood, no hair; nothing
but tracks. Billy said my bullet had passed so close
it frightened a mosquito off his hide."

"A guide must comfort his employer."

"The next day we built a calling stand beside Little
Ghost Lake. Have you heard Billy call?"

"I have heard sounds attributed to him."

"He uses a cone of birch bark five feet long.
The note he gets from it is lonelier than a
lovesick hippopotamus mourning from a housetop. It begins in the
earth, travels through the listener's boots, and comes out somewhere
between his shoulder blades."

"An instrument of war, plainly."

"At sunset Billy called. We waited. He called again. Presently
a bull answered from across the lake."

"Your bull?"

"We could not know. The mist was rising, and the
moon had not yet cleared the ridge. Billy called twice
more. There came a crash among the birches, then silence.
A few minutes later we heard him in the water."

"Swimming?"

"Wading along the shore. Slosh—slosh—slosh. Each step nearer. The mist
lay white over the lake, and the spruces beyond were
black as ink. I could hear drops falling from his
legs. Then came the sound of antlers striking brush: thud—thud—thud.
Billy whispered, 'Big one.'"

"Billy had deduced that from the violins, no doubt."

"I knelt behind the screen with the rifle ready. The
sound approached until it seemed he must be within twenty
yards, yet I could see nothing. You know how darkness
comes beside those lakes—not from the sky, but up from
the water. The world narrows to a patch of fog,
a black stump, the end of your barrel, and then
even those are gone.

"The bull stopped. We heard him breathe. Billy gave the
soft cow call. The bull answered with a grunt that
made the bark cone tremble in Billy's hands. Then a
cloud moved from the moon. For one instant there he
was—head high, water streaming from his chest, antlers white above
the fog."

"And ye shot?"

"I raised the rifle. The cloud returned."

McLeod sighed with theatrical relief.

"I waited. The bull took two steps. The moon showed
again, but by then he was directly in line with
Billy's canoe, which we had drawn among the reeds."

"Ye might have killed the canoe. Was it insured?"

"Before I could shift, he caught our scent. There was
one great splash; something struck the water like a falling
tree; and he was gone."

"Second murder frustrated by astronomy and poor parking."

"You can make it ridiculous now," Hemenway said, "but there
in the dark it was magnificent."

"Most follies improve by candlelight and suffer in the morning."

Baptiste, who had appeared indifferent, turned from the injector. "Dat
bull, he know plenty," he said.

"He knew enough to keep the canoe between himself and
Mr. Hemenway," said McLeod. "Which is more than some men
know."

Hemenway drank the remainder of his coffee.

"We shifted camp two days later. There was a chain
of bogs beyond Ghost Lake where Billy believed Silverhorns fed
at dawn. We left before light. Frost had come in
the night, and every dead leaf was stiff. You could
hear a mouse repent at fifty yards. We moved for
two hours and made perhaps half a mile."

"Hunters," McLeod observed, "are the only men who boast of
travelling slowly."

"Near sunrise we reached the edge of a muskeg. Fresh
tracks crossed it. The bull had passed not ten minutes
before. Billy went ahead, placing each moccasin as carefully as
if the ground were covered with sleeping babies. Tom followed.
I came last.

"We had gone a hundred yards when Billy stopped and
pointed. Beyond a fringe of tamarack I saw antlers moving.
The bull was feeding toward us.

"There was no wind. The sun had just touched the
high trees; mist smoked off the moss; somewhere a jay
scolded, then thought better of it. We stood among knee-high
bushes, afraid even to breathe. The antlers came nearer. I
saw the hump of his shoulder. Fifty yards. Forty. Thirty-five."

McLeod's pipe had gone out. He did not notice.

"I raised the rifle. At that moment a small grey
moth flew into Billy's mouth."

The engineer blinked. "A what?"

"A moth. Perhaps a caddis fly. Billy insists it was
a moth."

"And this creature took sides?"

"Decisively. Billy strangled. He shut his lips, swelled in the
face, and fought for self-control with heroism worthy of a
better cause. The insect went farther down. Billy's eyes filled
with tears. He made a noise like a cork being
pulled from a bottle."

McLeod began to laugh.

"The bull stopped," Hemenway continued severely. "I had him in
the sights, but Billy bent double and exploded. He coughed,
sneezed, struck Tom with the birch-bark horn, and sat down
in a puddle. The bull wheeled. I fired and missed
cleanly."

McLeod laughed until the cab shook. Even Baptiste grinned into
the furnace.

"It was not funny," Hemenway said.

"Not then, perhaps. But Providence keeps accounts over a long
period."

"Billy produced the moth afterward and offered to preserve it."

"He should have mounted it. The smallest member of the
Society for Prevention of Murder."

Hemenway waited until the engineer recovered.

"The next chance came on the ninth day. Snow had
fallen—only an inch, but enough to show the tracks plainly.
We crossed them at noon, and by three o'clock Billy
believed we had circled ahead of the bull. There was
a ravine with a brook at the bottom. Silverhorns was
travelling along it, and we took position above a narrow
crossing.

"It was perfect. The wind came into our faces. The
snow muffled every sound. Tom stayed two hundred yards back.
Billy and I lay behind a cedar. We had only
to wait."

"Those words," said McLeod, wiping his eyes, "generally introduce disaster."

"We waited two hours. Nothing happened. The cold came through
our coats and into our bones. One foot went to
sleep, then the other. Billy whispered that perhaps the bull
had bedded down. I whispered that perhaps Billy ought not
to whisper. Then we heard a raven flap out of
the ravine.

"A minute later a branch cracked below. The bull appeared
between the cedars, walking directly toward the crossing. I had
a clear view of his neck and chest. He stopped
at the brook and lowered his head."

"And then?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"My rifle would not fire."

McLeod stared at him with the shocked respect one mechanic
gives another man's mechanical catastrophe.

"The lever was not fully closed," Hemenway admitted. "A pine
needle had got into the action when I cleaned it.
I pressed the trigger—click. Silverhorns raised his head. I worked
the lever, but a frozen sleeve caught on the cedar.
The branch shook. He jumped back, saw us, and went
up the ravine with his antlers laid along his shoulders."

"Man," said McLeod, "ye were protected against yourself by a
pine needle. There's no Calvinist alive who could overlook such
evidence."

"I sat in the snow for ten minutes, contemplating the
commandments."

"Particularly the sixth?"

"Particularly several that are not written."

"What did Billy say?"

"Nothing. A wise guide knows when silence is part of
his wages."

Outside, the rain had diminished to a fine whisper. The
somnolent local train stood under the lamps, its windows dim
and empty. Somewhere in the fog a dog barked at
regular intervals, as if protesting a private injustice.

"And that was the last?" McLeod asked.

"No. Yesterday was the last."

Hemenway's voice changed. The comic vexation left it, and he
stared beyond the boiler toward the darkness.

"We had broken camp. Tom took the first canoe load
down to the settlement, while Billy and I remained to
pack the tent. About noon we heard a bull grunting
in the timber behind camp."

"Silverhorns again?"

"Billy said so at once. There are notes in an
animal's call, he claims, just as in a man's voice.
We left the packs and went in.

"The country there is terrible—blown spruce, granite shelves, patches of
bog that look firm until they take you to the
thigh. We followed for three hours. Sometimes he answered; sometimes
we heard him moving. He was not running from us.
He seemed to be circling, curious and suspicious.

"At last we reached an open hardwood ridge. The afternoon
was extraordinarily still. The leaves had all fallen, and the
white trunks stood in ranks down the slope. I could
see a long way between them.

"Billy touched my arm. Silverhorns was on the opposite side
of the hollow."

Even McLeod did not interrupt.

"He stood broadside, entirely clear, not eighty yards away. There
could be no mistake, no darkness, no canoe, no moth,
no pine needle. The light lay full upon him. He
was enormous, Angus. I have seen bigger measurements in record
books, perhaps, but never an animal that carried size as
he did. The shoulders rose black and heavy; the muzzle
was grey; there were scars along his flank. One ear
was torn. And those antlers—good Lord! They seemed not an
ornament but a crown he had won by fighting."

"Did he see ye?"

"Almost at once. He looked across the hollow directly at
us.

"I brought up the rifle. The sights settled behind his
shoulder. I was steady. I could have killed him."

"But ye did not."

"There was a young maple between us—not enough to turn
the bullet, only enough to cover a hand's breadth of
his chest. I shifted one step to the left. At
the same instant he stepped forward. A beech trunk covered
him. I shifted again; he moved again. It was absurd.
For perhaps half a minute we kept on so, the
bull walking slowly and I trying to find the open
lane."

"The final quadrille."

"Then he came clear. I had the sight on him.
Billy whispered, 'Now.'

"Silverhorns turned his head. Not away—toward us. He stood there
looking, and I had the most ridiculous impression that he
knew precisely what we had been doing for seven years.
Not that he reasoned about it, of course, but he
knew us as danger; and he had escaped danger by
strength, caution, accident, and something very like courage.

"I hesitated."

"Ah," said McLeod quietly.

"Only for a second. Then the wind changed. He flung
up his head, wheeled, and vanished over the ridge."

"Without a shot?"

"Without a shot."

For some moments there was no sound but the soft
breathing of the locomotive.

"Billy was furious," Hemenway went on. "He asked whether I
had come to hunt moose or admire scenery. Perhaps he
was right. Yet when the bull had gone, I found
I was not sorry. Disappointed, yes. Ashamed of hesitating, certainly.
But not sorry that he was alive."

McLeod relit his pipe with deliberation. "Then ye're improving."

"Don't be sentimental. If he had waited another second, I
should have fired."

"Of course. Conversion is seldom complete at the first sermon."

"I mean only that he had played a good game.
Better than mine."

"Seven seasons against three men, two rifles, a canoe, a
birch trumpet, and Billy Brassard. I'd say he had the
advantage in intelligence."

A bell rang faintly in the station. The telegraph began
a furious clatter. Presently the door opened and the night
operator ran along the platform, waving a lantern.

"Express clearin' St. Fabien!" he shouted. "Twenty minutes, mebbe less.
You take the local right after her, Angus."

McLeod climbed down to receive his orders. Hemenway remained in
the cab, watching steam drift across the yard. He ought
to have returned to the waiting room and prepared to
board the express, which would carry him east toward his
office, clean linen, respectable meals, and persons unable to distinguish
a moose track from a soup plate. Instead the warmth,
the smell of oil and coal, and the anticipation of
movement held him.

McLeod returned. "There's been a change. The express stops only
for orders. Ye'll never get your gear aboard in time."

"Then I shall stay here until morning."

"Come west with us to Grand Junction. Ye can catch
the mail train there."

"In the cab?"

"Unless ye prefer the baggage car with two calves and
a barrel of herring."

Hemenway looked at his gun case. "Will you promise not
to call me a murderer all the way?"

"No."

"Then I'll come."

The express arrived out of the mist with a distant
tremor, a growing thunder, and at last two white beams
that swept the wet rails. It paused only long enough
for a man to exchange papers with the operator. Sleeping
cars glided past, glowing softly behind their curtains; faces appeared
and disappeared at windows; then the red lamps dwindled westward,
and the station returned to its puddles and haze.

Five minutes later McLeod's local drew out.

The little platform slipped behind. The blurred village lights wavered,
sank among the trees, and were gone. The locomotive gathered
speed with a deepening beat. Steam streamed backward; sparks whirled
over the tender; the rails, caught for an instant in
the great headlight, rushed beneath them and vanished. The patient
monster of the siding had awakened joyful and obedient. It
shook the damp night from its flanks, devoured the darkness
by the mile, and sent its long cry ahead through
sleeping settlements and black forests.

Hemenway sat on the fireman's box, his pack under his
feet. Baptiste fed the furnace. Each opening of the firebox
door flung a red, infernal brightness over the cab, and
each closing restored the cool moonlight and the green glow
of the gauges.

The rain ceased. The clouds broke apart, revealing a late
moon that silvered the marshes and laid pale bars across
the track. Here and there a pond flashed between the
trees. Telegraph poles sprang up in the headlight, bowed past,
and fled into the rearward dark.

McLeod stood at the throttle with one gloved hand resting
lightly upon it. His whole square body seemed to listen
to the engine. He knew every rise, curve, culvert, and
treacherous rail along the line; and the locomotive, responsive to
the smallest pressure of his hand, might have been a
powerful animal whose temper he understood.

"This is better than waiting-room theology," Hemenway said.

"Aye. A moving train improves most opinions."

They climbed steadily through cuttings of granite and long aisles
of spruce. At the summit the line ran beside a
chain of moonlit bogs. Frost had whitened the sedges, and
pools shone like fragments of broken mirror. No house stood
within twenty miles. The only signs of man were the
parallel rails and the wires, humming faintly in the wind.

Hemenway thought of Silverhorns upon the hardwood ridge. He saw
again the scarred flank, the high dark shoulders, the pale
antlers among white trees. By this time the bull would
be far north of the abandoned camp, perhaps feeding in
some sheltered valley where the snow had not yet covered
the moss. Next year calves would follow cows through those
forests; young bulls would challenge one another in the autumn;
and somewhere beyond the reach of Hemenway's rifle the old
monarch might survive another winter.

The reflection should have comforted him. It did, though he
would not have admitted as much to McLeod.

They ran down a long grade. The engine's beat quickened.
Wind poured through the cab and snatched coal dust into
glittering spirals. McLeod glanced at the steam gauge, then at
his watch.

"Eight minutes lost," he said. "We'll recover five before the
river."

"I thought punctuality did not enter railway arrangements."

"It does when I'm driving. Official philosophy is for officials."

Ahead, the track bent around the shoulder of a low
hill, crossed a marsh upon an embankment, and entered a
narrow rock cut. The headlight travelled before them, an enormous
white eye searching the forest. It swept over wet stone,
pale grass, signal boards, and the ghostly trunks of birch.

Then Baptiste shouted.

Something moved upon the track at the far edge of
the light.

At first Hemenway saw only a black bulk. He thought
it a cow strayed from some distant farm, though there
was no farm. The shape turned. Antlers flashed.

"A moose!" he cried.

McLeod's hand flew to the whistle cord. The locomotive screamed—a
great, tearing blast that rolled across the marsh and struck
echoes from the cut.

The bull stood between the rails.

For an instant the rushing light held him motionless. He
was a monstrous figure, black against the white gravel, steam-like
breath pouring from his nostrils. His antlers spread almost from
rail to rail. He might easily have stepped aside. The
embankment was low; the marsh lay open on either hand.

McLeod whistled again and closed the throttle. Baptiste seized the
brake. Steel shrieked beneath the wheels.

"Off, ye fool!" McLeod roared, as if the animal could
understand him. "Off the line!"

The bull did not move.

What passed in that wild brain no man could know.
Perhaps the headlight seemed another moon fallen to earth; perhaps
the whistle was the challenge of some beast vaster than
any he had fought; perhaps pain, age, and seven hunted
autumns had filled him with that ultimate defiance in which
fear itself becomes anger.

He had known wolves slipping grey between the spruces. He
had known the thin crack of rifles and the bitter
sting of branches cut by bullets. He had battled bulls
beside northern lakes until the water reddened about their knees.
He had broken ice with his breast, crossed rivers in
flood, and stood beneath winter storms while snow gathered upon
his back. Always danger had yielded, or he had escaped
it.

Now an intolerable eye came blazing through his forest, shaking
the earth, crying aloud.

The bull lowered his head.

"No!" Hemenway shouted.

The moose charged.

He came down the track with a short, furious rush,
antlers thrust forward, hooves striking sparks from the rail. For
two seconds the engine and the animal rushed upon one
another—two beasts, the one of flesh and ancient rage, the
other of iron, fire, and the gathered will of men.

There was a terrific crash.

The locomotive lurched. Glass rattled. A dark body whirled past
the cab and disappeared. McLeod fought the brake while the
wheels screamed and hammered. The train ran on a hundred
yards, two hundred, then stopped with a hiss that seemed
almost an exhalation of horror.

No one spoke.

Hemenway had been thrown against the tender. He straightened slowly,
one hand bleeding where it had struck a bolt.

"Was it—" he began.

"A bull," Baptiste said.

McLeod took the lantern from its hook. His face had
lost all humour.

"Stay with the engine, Baptiste. Come, Dud."

They climbed down.

The moon shone clearly now. Behind the train the rails
gleamed in two converging lines, and along one of them
lay dark stains. The silence after the engine's uproar was
immense. Water dripped in the marsh; somewhere far off, disturbed
geese called once and were still.

They walked back beside the track.

They found the bull at the foot of the embankment,
among frosted grass. The collision had shattered him frightfully. One
shoulder was crushed; the great body lay twisted; blood darkened
the white sedges. Yet the head had fallen upon a
patch of soft moss, and the antlers were unbroken.

Hemenway stopped above him.

The left palm carried three pale tines, bleached almost white.

"Silverhorns," he said.

McLeod lowered the lantern. Its yellow glow moved over the
grey muzzle, the torn ear, the scars along the flank.

"Are ye certain?"

"I could not mistake him."

The great eye was half open, but whatever fierceness or
cunning had lived there was gone. Without motion, the bull
seemed larger than he had in the forest, less like
quarry than some fallen fragment of the wilderness itself. The
antlers rose from the moss in a vast, useless splendour.

Hemenway knelt. He put one hand upon the rough forehead,
then drew it away.

For seven years he had imagined this moment. In imagination
there had always been a clean shot, a triumphant guide,
measurements taken with reverence, photographs, congratulations, and the magnificent head
borne home as proof of patience and skill. Nothing in
those pictures resembled the broken body beside the railway.

McLeod stood with the lantern hanging at his side.

"Well, Dud," he said at last, and his voice was
gentle, "ye've killed your first moose."

Hemenway looked back toward the locomotive. It waited in the
moonlight, steam drifting about its wheels, its great eye shining
steadily down the track. There was no guilt in that
obedient machine, nor triumph; only power arrested for a moment
before it resumed its appointed course.

"You had a hand in it," Hemenway said. "We'll divide
the honour."

"An honour is it?"

"No," Hemenway answered. "I don't think it is."

They remained there a little while. Neither spoke of the
delayed train, the lost minutes, nor the passengers who would
presently wonder why the local had stopped in the wilderness.
The old bull lay among the sedges with his crown
intact, as though death itself had been unable to disarm
him.

At length McLeod touched Hemenway's shoulder.

"We must go."

Hemenway rose. He looked once more at the pale tines,
the battered shoulder, and the head that had lowered against
the impossible brightness.

"What a fighter," he said.

"Aye."

"My first moose."

"Aye."

Hemenway picked up the lantern. "And my last, Angus."

They climbed the embankment together. Behind them Silverhorns lay still
beneath the moon; before them the locomotive breathed upon the
rails, impatient to conquer the remaining darkness.

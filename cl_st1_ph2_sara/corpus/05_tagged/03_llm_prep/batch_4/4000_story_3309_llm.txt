### DON ALONZO'S EXPERIMENT

When Deacon Bassett drove into the Pitkin yard, Mira knew
him first by the cough of his old mare and
next by the deacon's habit of calling "Whoa!" three times
after the creature had already stopped.

"There he is!" she said, glancing through the pantry window.
"Now, Alonzo, don't you go to gettin' nervous. He's only
come to pass the time of day."

Don Alonzo Pitkin, who had been arranging beetles in a
shallow box, turned pale.

"He'll want to look at me."

"Well, he's got eyes, ain't he?"

"He'll ask whether I've grown."

"Then tell him no, and ask if he has."

But Don Alonzo had already caught up his crutch. He
moved swiftly in spite of his twisted hip, crossed the
little bedroom, and disappeared beneath the high, old-fashioned bed.

Mira sighed. She was a brisk young woman with brown
hair, rosy cheeks, and a heart which never considered her
own convenience when some one else required comfort. She stooped
and lifted the faded valance.

"Alonzo!"

"I can't, Mira."

"You ain't a mite afraid of him."

"Yes, I am."

"He don't mean harm."

"I know it. That makes it worse."

This was true in a way Mira understood. Don Alonzo
could have endured an enemy better than he endured Deacon
Bassett's solemn kindness. The deacon possessed a little medical knowledge,
picked up from an uncle who had sold pills, and
he never saw Don Alonzo without feeling of his pulse,
inquiring about his spine, and recommending sassafras, goose-oil, or violent
exercise.

"Very well," Mira said. "Stay there, poor baby. Only don't
sneeze. I swept yesterday, but dust knows where it's wanted."

She dropped the valance just as the deacon knocked.

Don Alonzo was seventeen, though a stranger might have doubted
whether he were twelve or twenty-five. His body had ceased
growing early; one shoulder stood higher than the other, and
his legs were thin and untrustworthy. But above this frail
figure was a fine, grave head, with broad forehead, large
dark eyes, and ears that missed very little. His pretty,
silly mother had given him the name Don Alonzo after
a gentleman in a romance; and, having burdened him with
so magnificent a title, had died before she could teach
him to laugh at it.

Joe Pitkin, his elder brother, had taken him in without
question. Joe was strong, cheerful, and not overburdened with imagination.
Mira, whom Joe married two years later, had made Don
Alonzo's life gentler than he had believed life could be.
She never spoke to him in the patient, mournful tone
some women reserve for cripples and funerals. She scolded him
when he left acid on the table, praised his drawings
when they deserved it, and kissed him whenever she pleased.

Now Deacon Bassett entered, smelling of horse, peppermint, and the
October wind.

"How be ye, Mira? Joe anywheres about?"

"He's gone to Walpole with the apples. Won't be back
till to-morrow afternoon."

"Sho! Then you're alone."

"Alonzo's here."

The deacon looked toward the bedroom.

"How's he gittin' on? Growed any sence spring?"

"Not so's you'd notice," said Mira, giving the bedchamber door
a warning glance.

"I've been studyin' his case. There was a man over
to East Norridge with a crooked back, and he used
to hang himself by the arms from a beam every
morning."

"What happened to him?"

"Beam broke."

"There! I shouldn't wonder."

"Hurt him considerable," admitted the deacon. "Still, the principle might
be sound. Where is the boy?"

"Busy."

"Chemicals again, hey? Them things is dangerous. He might blow
the house apart."

"That's what I tell him. Some morning Joe'll come home
and find me scattered all over the lot like kindlin'-wood."

A faint sound came from beneath the bed. It might
have been a cough, but Mira knew it for a
smothered laugh.

The deacon settled into a chair. He had not really
come to see Don Alonzo. He had come to tell
the news, inspect Joe's winter squashes, and perhaps sell Mira
a bottle of Dr. Bassett's Invigorating Compound, compounded in his
own woodshed from honest ingredients which did not invigorate anybody.

"You heard about the robbers?" he asked.

Mira's face changed.

"What robbers?"

"Two of 'em. Strangers. Night before last they broke into
Widow Peavey's. Took nine dollars, her silver spoons, and a
ham. Last night they tried old Miss Larkin's, up beyond
the schoolhouse."

"My, oh!"

"Scare't her half to death. She woke and hollered, and
they run, but not before they'd pried the pantry window
clean off. Folks say they pick houses where there ain't
no able-bodied man."

Mira looked again toward Don Alonzo's room.

"Joe'll be home to-morrow."

"Likely they won't come this way. Then again, folks know
Joe's away. He told it at the store, and what's
said at Hiram Cobb's might as well be printed in
the county paper."

"Thank you, deacon," said Mira rather dryly. "That makes me
feel real restful."

He leaned nearer.

"You lock up tight. Got a gun?"

"Joe's old musket, but I'd be more afraid of it
than of the robbers. It kicks like a mule, he
says."

"I could stop overnight."

Mira knew that Mrs. Bassett was away nursing her sister,
and understood the size of the sacrifice.

"No, you couldn't. I've got Alonzo."

The deacon blinked, but Mira's chin was lifted, so he
did not say what he thought. Instead he sold her
the Invigorating Compound, inspected the squashes, pulled up two carrots
to see how they were doing, and at last drove
away.

Don Alonzo emerged with feathers in his hair and humiliation
in his eyes.

"You shouldn't have said you had me."

"Why not?"

"He knew what you meant, and he knew it wasn't
true."

"It is true."

"Mira, what could I do against two grown men?"

She brushed the feathers away and smoothed his hair.

"Nothing, likely, and I don't want you should. We'll fasten
the doors, and if robbers come we'll holler till the
village wakes. But I'm glad you're here. I'd be lonesome
without you."

He searched her face. Mira did not lie prettily; truth
stood in her honest eyes as plainly as a candle
in a window.

"You've always been good to me," he said.

"Now, don't begin that."

"You have. I wish I could do something for you."

"You can bring in the kindling."

He did, though the basket dragged against his lame leg.
Yet all through supper he was silent. He heard Joe's
empty chair creak when Mira brushed against the table. He
heard the wind fingering the lilac branches. And behind these
familiar sounds he imagined a file upon the window catch,
a boot on the doorstep, a whisper like a hiss.

Afterward Mira went about fastening the house. She pushed the
bolt across the kitchen door, set a chair beneath the
latch at the front, and examined each window twice.

"There," she said. "If they get in now, they're smarter
than Deacon Bassett, and that ain't possible."

But she carried the flatiron upstairs and put it beside
her bed.

Don Alonzo withdrew to his room. It was the one
place in the village where he never felt ashamed. Along
the walls stood shelves made by Joe from soap boxes.
They held books on birds, minerals, electricity, and chemistry; bottles
with careful labels; dried plants; stones; nests; a snake skin;
two clocks, neither of which told time; and the neat
skeleton of a rabbit wired together by Don Alonzo himself.
On the table were scales, glass tubes, a spirit lamp,
paintbrushes, and an alarming jar marked POISON, though it contained
only starch.

His crutch slipped and knocked a small box to the
floor.

The lid sprang open.

A greenish glimmer shone up at him.

Don Alonzo bent over it. The box contained pieces of
cardboard coated with a luminous preparation he had made after
reading of phosphorescent minerals. He had exposed them to sunlight
that afternoon, and now in darkness they shone with a
sickly, wavering radiance.

He closed the door and turned down his lamp.

The green shapes burned brighter.

For a long while he sat perfectly still. Then he
reached for an old book of tales which had belonged
to his mother. He remembered one story in it—something about
a white figure, a frightened sentry, and an army put
to flight by terror rather than force.

Presently his grave face altered. He no longer looked frightened.
He looked busy.

From the lower drawer he took black cloth left from
Mira's sewing, a close-fitting cap, and an old black shirt.
Then he found chalk and began to draw.

At ten o'clock Mira called, "Alonzo, you abed?"

"Almost."

"What you doin'?"

"An experiment."

"Will it go off?"

"No."

"Sure?"

"I hope not."

"That ain't the answer I was lookin' for."

He smiled, though she could not see him. "Go to
bed, Mira. I'll be careful."

"If you burn up Joe's shirt, burn the old one."

The house grew quiet.

* * *

The night was cloudy, with no moon and scarcely a
star. After midnight the wind died. This made the silence
worse, for a creaking bough or rattling shutter would have
given it something to do. Instead, the darkness waited.

Mira lay awake, watching the pale oblong of the window.
She had laughed at Deacon Bassett's warning, but laughter is
poor company at one in the morning. The flatiron beside
her seemed smaller than it had downstairs. Joe's musket stood
in the corner, black, tall, and useless.

Once she heard a scratching in the wall.

"A mouse," she whispered.

Then came another sound.

Snap!

A twig in the yard.

Mira sat up.

Nothing followed. She listened until her ears hurt. Perhaps a
cat had crossed the currant bushes. Perhaps old Brindle had
strayed from the barn. Perhaps—

Snap!

This time there was a low murmur outside. Not wind.
Not an animal. A voice, carefully smothered.

Mira slid from bed and wrapped the quilt around her
shoulders. The floorboards were cold. She moved into the hall
and stood at the head of the stairs.

Below, something touched the kitchen window.

Scrape.

A pause.

Scrape—scrape.

A mouse, was it, or—the sound of a tool?

She wanted to call Don Alonzo, but the shame of
waking the poor boy to share her terror held her
tongue. What could he do? He would try to come,
and perhaps fall on the stairs. Better that he remain
asleep.

There was a faint click.

Mira gripped the banister.

A man whispered, "Easy, now."

Another voice answered, "The woman's alone."

The scrape began again.

Mira's knees weakened. She thought of the nine dollars Widow
Peavey had lost, and then, absurdly, of the ham. There
were six dollars and thirty cents in the blue teapot,
Joe's silver watch in the bedroom drawer, and three hams
in the pantry. Let them take all of it! But
would they be satisfied? Miss Larkin had said one man
carried a club.

Mira opened her mouth to scream.

Before she could make a sound, something moved below.

It came from Don Alonzo's room and crossed the dark
passage silent as a shadow. Mira saw only a deeper
blackness at first. Then a narrow line of green appeared—curved,
horrible, and shining.

Another line joined it.

A skull turned toward her.

Where a human face should have been, empty eyes glimmered
above a dreadful grin. Ribs burned through the darkness. Long
bones moved without sound. The thing was no taller than
a child, but its misshapen shoulder rose behind its skull,
and one arm hung crookedly at its side.

Mira's breath stopped.

The apparition placed one shining finger to its shining teeth.

Then it slipped into the kitchen.

Mira clung to the banister. Every warning she had ever
heard concerning chemicals rushed into her head. Don Alonzo had
gone off at last. He had blown himself out of
this world, and now his spirit had come to tell
her so.

Poor baby! Poor, dear baby!

She tried to follow, but her feet would not move.

The window latch gave with a little crack. The sash
rose an inch, two inches, then slowly higher. A man's
cap appeared, followed by a broad face with whiskers.

"Come on," the man whispered over his shoulder. "All still."

He put one leg across the sill.

At that instant the glowing skeleton sprang.

It did not walk, as a respectable ghost might. It
bounded forward with a wild, thin yell, seized the burglar
around the neck, and fastened itself upon his back. Something
whistled in the dark and came down with a crack.

The burglar screamed.

No dignified shout of alarm was this, but a shriek
which cut through the night like a knife.

"Take it off! Lem, take it off!"

The second burglar, who had been waiting beneath the window,
looked up. What he saw was his companion halfway through
the sash, with a green skeleton riding his shoulders and
beating him vigorously with what seemed to be a black
tail.

Lem made one attempt at rescue. He caught the apparition
by its luminous arm.

The arm came away in his hand.

Lem stared at the shining bone, gave a howl twice
as loud as his friend's, and ran.

The first burglar tore himself loose, taking the window curtain
with him. He fell backward into the currant bushes. The
skeleton followed through the opening and landed squarely upon him.

Crack! went the whip.

"Mercy!" cried the burglar.

Crack!

"I ain't never robbed no dead folks!"

Crack!

"Then begin learnin' not to rob the live ones!" said
a thin, furious voice.

Mira heard the voice. She knew it. But before knowledge
could overcome terror, the skeleton rose from the bushes, one
green arm missing, its ribs ablaze and its skull nodding
loosely. It turned toward the window.

"Mira!" it called.

That was too much. Mira said, "Oh, Alonzo!" and fainted
neatly upon the rag rug.

Meanwhile the burglars were making a thorough advertisement of themselves.
Lem had reached the road and was running toward the
village. His companion followed, still entangled in the curtain, which
streamed behind him like a bridal veil. Don Alonzo came
after them as fast as his twisted legs allowed, leaning
upon his crutch and snapping Joe's long cattle whip.

He could not have overtaken a lame goose in ordinary
circumstances. But he had no need to. The two men
had lost all judgment. They ran into Deacon Bassett's fence,
knocked over Hiram Cobb's ash barrel, and passed the schoolhouse
crying that judgment had come.

At the Cobb place a lamp flared. Then another appeared
at Widow Peavey's. Dogs barked, windows opened, and men emerged
carrying lanterns, broom handles, guns, and one stove lid.

"What is it?"

"Murder!"

"Fire?"

"Ghost!" screamed Lem.

"Where?"

"Behind us!"

The village looked.

Down the road came a small green skeleton, shining like
a corpse-candle, limping horribly and waving a whip.

For several moments the village agreed with Lem.

Deacon Bassett dropped his lantern. Hiram Cobb aimed his gun
but remembered it was not loaded. Widow Peavey, recognizing one
of the robbers by his red scarf, displayed greater sense
than the men and threw a washtub in his path.

Both burglars fell over it.

By the time they had untangled themselves, Hiram, the deacon,
and two hired men were upon them. They were held,
though not without difficulty, while Don Alonzo arrived and leaned
against the fence, panting.

"Don't let the little dead one touch me!" begged the
whiskered burglar.

The deacon raised his lantern.

The glowing skull had slipped sideways. Beneath it appeared Don
Alonzo's pale, earnest face.

"Why," said the deacon, "it's Joe Pitkin's boy!"

"I ain't a boy," Don Alonzo answered, breathlessly but distinctly.
"And these men were breaking into our house."

There was a silence. Then Widow Peavey began to laugh.

She laughed until she had to sit on the washtub.
Hiram joined her, then the hired men, and finally Deacon
Bassett, though he pretended he was coughing. Even the captured
burglars might have laughed if there had been anything funny
in the matter from where they sat.

"What be you rigged in?" asked Hiram.

"Black cloth. Pasteboard bones. Luminous paint."

"What paint?"

"Phosphorescent. It stores light and gives it out in darkness."

The deacon peered at him with new respect.

"Did you make it?"

"Yes, sir."

"And that arm come off?"

"I sewed it loosely on purpose. I thought if one
of them seized me, it might be useful for him
to believe he had pulled off a bone."

Widow Peavey slapped her knee.

"My, oh! Useful! I guess 'twas!"

Then Don Alonzo remembered Mira.

His triumph vanished.

"She's alone!"

He turned so quickly that his crutch slipped. Hiram caught
him before he fell.

"Easy, son. We'll all go."

The procession back to the Pitkin house was the largest
seen on that road since the Fourth of July. Two
men guarded the prisoners; six others carried lanterns; Widow Peavey
carried the stove lid, having grown attached to it; and
Deacon Bassett walked beside Don Alonzo, asking no questions about
his growth.

They found Mira still on the kitchen rug.

Don Alonzo dropped beside her.

"Mira! Mira, it was me. I didn't mean to frighten
you."

Her eyelids fluttered. She saw the shining ribs and shut
them again.

"Take it off," she murmured. "Whatever you be, take it
off."

He tore away the skullcap. Hiram unpinned the black shirt,
and the green skeleton collapsed into pasteboard pieces.

Mira looked up at Don Alonzo's familiar face.

"You ain't dead?"

"No."

"You ain't a spirit?"

"No."

"You painted yourself?"

"Only the bones."

She sat up and put both arms around him.

"You wicked, blessed, foolish child!"

Then she held him away and looked at him again.

"Was it you makin' that awful noise?"

"Partly. The burglars made most of it."

"Did you truly jump onto one?"

"Yes."

"With your hip?"

"I forgot about my hip."

At that, Mira began to cry. It was not elegant
crying. She laughed between the tears and wiped her eyes
on the quilt she still wore around her shoulders.

"There! I knew them chemicals would do something. But I
never expected they'd save three hams and Joe's watch."

The sheriff came from the county seat before breakfast and
took the burglars away. One proved to be the man
who had robbed Widow Peavey, and her silver spoons were
found in his lodging. The story traveled faster than the
sheriff's horse. By nine o'clock half the village had visited
the Pitkin house, and the other half was preparing to
come after dinner.

Ordinarily Don Alonzo would have hidden beneath the bed. That
morning he sat in Joe's chair with his crutch beside
him and the luminous skull upon his knee. He explained
the paint to Hiram Cobb, the detachable arm to Widow
Peavey, and the handling of the whip to three boys
who regarded him as a military commander.

Deacon Bassett arrived again with Dr. Bassett's Invigorating Compound.

"I brought your money back, Mira," he said. "Seems to
me this household don't need invigoratin'."

Then he turned to Don Alonzo.

"You've got a remarkable constitution."

"No," said Don Alonzo, and his eyes twinkled. "I haven't
growed any sence spring."

The deacon laughed louder than was necessary.

Joe came home near sunset. He found wheel marks in
the yard, broken currant bushes, a curtain hanging from the
kitchen window, and twelve people talking at once. He stood
in the doorway with his cap pushed back.

"What in creation's happened?"

Mira flew at him.

"Joe Pitkin, where've you been?"

"Walpole, same as I said."

"Well, while you've been peddlin' apples, your brother's caught two
robbers, scared the village out of seven years' growth, recovered
Widow Peavey's spoons, broke the curtain rod, and made me
faint."

Joe looked at Don Alonzo.

"You did?"

Don Alonzo's old shyness returned for one moment. Then Mira's
hand settled upon his shoulder.

"I had an idea," he said.

He told the story. At first his voice was low;
then, when he reached the open window and the burglar's
broad back, he forgot to be self-conscious. He showed how
he had leaped, how the false arm had come loose,
how Lem had run with it, and how the whip
had cracked. The room roared with laughter.

Joe did not laugh immediately. He looked at the small
figure in the big chair, at the twisted legs, the
grave young face, and the ridiculous glowing skull. Then his
eyes grew bright.

"I couldn't have done better myself," he said.

"Better!" cried Mira. "You'd have gone blunderin' out with the
musket and shot the rain barrel. Alonzo used his head.
That's more'n most men remember they've got."

She drew Don Alonzo close and smiled at Joe over
his shoulder.

"You may be the bigger one, Joseph Pitkin, but he's
proved himself the greater man."

Joe nodded solemnly.

Outside, the October dark pressed cold against the windows. Inside,
the lamps shone upon neighbors, books, broken pasteboard bones, and
Mira's untidy supper table. Don Alonzo looked from one friendly
face to another and did not wish to hide from
any of them.

The kitchen seemed warm as June.

### YANN RIEL'S RIDE

The following adventure happened in the autumn of 1803, during
the short and uneasy interval which followed the renewal of
war between England and France. It was related, more than
fifty years afterwards, by Yann Riel, then master of a
small coasting vessel out of Morlaix, to my grandfather, who
committed it to paper with a fidelity which I have
no reason to doubt, though the manuscript—discovered among accounts of
pilchards, sailcloth, and a lawsuit concerning three barrels of lamp-oil—has
suffered from damp in several places.

I have supplied a word here and there where the
writing was wholly lost, but have otherwise allowed Captain Riel
to tell his story in his own fashion.

---

My father, Yves Riel, was an honest fisherman when fish
were to be caught, and a smuggler whenever gentlemen in
Cornwall were prepared to pay twice the Roscoff price for
good Breton brandy. My mother objected to the second occupation,
but not so loudly as she might have done; for
fishing filled the pot badly that year, and brandy bought
flour, shoes, salt, and once a blue ribbon which she
wore at pardon.

I was twelve years old, and wanted above all things
to cross the Channel.

"You would be sick," said my father.

"I was not sick when we went to Ouessant."

"Ouessant!" said he. "That is like walking to the church
door and boasting you have been to Rome."

"I can reef a sail."

"You can reef your own shirt-tail into your breeches, if
your mother reminds you."

"I can steer."

"You steered us upon the Keravel bank."

"The wind shifted."

"The bank did not."

My mother, who was mending a stocking by the fire,
looked over her spectacles.

"Take him, Yves," she said.

My father turned upon her as though she had proposed
sending a sucking-pig to command a frigate.

"Take him? On a straight run to Cornwall? With the
English cruisers watching every creek?"

"He will go one day without asking you. Better that
he should learn while you can cuff him."

This argument struck him, for he had himself gone first
to sea without leave, at nine years old, in a
boat which had neither compass nor bread aboard. He scratched
his chin.

"There are riding-officers," he said to me. "There are soldiers.
There are revenue cutters. If we are chased, nobody stops
to wipe your nose."

"I wipe it myself."

"And if we are taken, you may see the inside
of an English prison."

"I should like to see England."

"The dickens take England! That is just where you are
not to be seen."

Nevertheless, three nights later I went aboard the lugger *Bonne
Espérance*, carrying my jersey, two onions, a knife, and a
cake which my mother had baked so hard that it
might have served for ballast. My father inspected these preparations
and removed the onions.

"You are not going to a wedding," said he.

We sailed from a little cove west of Roscoff, with
no lantern showing and the sea as smooth and black
as polished slate. There were seven men aboard besides myself.
Old Tanguy Le Coz commanded while my father attended to
the cargo, though which of the two was truly master
I never discovered. Tanguy had one eye, a red comforter,
and a habit of beginning every order with an oath
and ending it with a prayer.

Our chief cargo consisted of sixty ankers of brandy, packed
close under tarpaulins, together with tobacco in small waterproof parcels.
But there was also a deal box, long and shallow,
which my father treated with more irritation than he bestowed
upon all the contraband beside it.

"What is in that?" I asked.

"Fifteen little wooden dolls."

I thought he was laughing at me.

"Truly?"

"Fifteen little wooden dolls," he repeated, with disgust. "Arms that
bend, legs that bend, eyes painted blue, and hair painted
yellow. Brandy, I understand. Tobacco, I understand. Silk stockings, if
women must have them, I can understand on Sundays. But
fifteen little wooden dolls!"

"Are they for children?"

"They are for Mademoiselle Keranguin."

"Who is she?"

"A French lady who lives in England and sends orders
through John Penrose. She paid beforehand."

That was enough for my father. He respected payment as
the curé respected Holy Writ, though he understood it much
better.

We had a fair wind at first, and I was
too proud to be sick. Then the breeze freshened from
the north-west, the lugger began to shoulder the seas, and
my courage went down into my boots. I lay beneath
the weather bulwark wishing myself dead, while the men stepped
over me with tubs, ropes, and sea-boots, saying kindly that
I looked green enough to be an Englishman.

After midnight the wind eased. I crawled aft. There was
no moon, but the stars stood out between moving clouds,
and far ahead a blacker darkness appeared under them.

"Cornwall," said my father.

I stared until my eyes watered. At first I saw
only a long low loom, now hidden, now revealed; then
a single point of light twinkled, vanished, and twinkled twice
again.

"The signal," said Tanguy. "They are ready."

We ran into a narrow bay between cliffs. The sails
came down with scarcely a flap; the sweeps went out;
and the *Bonne Espérance* slid towards a strip of pale
shingle where twenty or thirty men were waiting. Nobody called.
Nobody carried a lantern openly. Yet, as we drew nearer,
shuttered lights flashed here and there, showing a face, a
hand, the brass buttons of a coat, the white blaze
of a horse; then all was black again.

Two tub-boats came alongside. The ankers were lifted into them,
six at a time, and rowed ashore. I followed in
the second boat, entrusted with the box of dolls because,
as my father said, if I dropped them overboard there
would still be the brandy.

The shore was the busiest silent place I had ever
seen. Men rolled the ankers beyond the tide-mark, while others
tied them in pairs upon packhorses. These horses had been
greased along the back and flanks, so that a revenue
man who tried to seize one might get no better
hold than upon an eel. Some carried four tubs, some
six smaller ones, and they stamped and snorted at the
smell of salt water and spirits.

John Penrose, a broad Cornishman with whiskers like two scrubbing-brushes,
took the doll-box from me.

"Keranguin lot?" he whispered.

"Yes."

"Put it on Lilith."

"Who is Lilith?"

He pointed to a tall black mare standing a little
apart. She carried no tubs, but two panniers hung from
her saddle-frame.

"She goes inland to the lady's farm. Box in one
side, stones in t'other to balance."

Lilith's ears went back when I approached. She had an
eye like a drop of wet coal and a neck
which curved proudly away from every hand. Penrose laid the
box in one pannier and filled the other with stones.
Then someone shouted from the cliff.

It was not a loud shout, but the whole beach
changed in an instant.

A whistle answered from above. A shuttered lantern flew open.
Men began cutting lashings, dragging boats, mounting horses. From the
headland came the beat of hooves and the jingle of
metal.

"Riding-officers!" cried Penrose. "Run the train!"

The smugglers struck the leading packhorses with flat sticks. The
beasts plunged towards a steep path at the back of
the cove, their loads knocking—thump, thump—against their sides. Our tub-boats
were shoved into the surf. Tanguy roared for everyone to
come aboard.

I ought to have run to my father. Instead, I
thought of the dolls.

Lilith was wheeling in terror, the box banging against her
ribs. I had ridden our grey pony at home and
imagined all horses differed chiefly in colour. Therefore I caught
at the wooden frame, set my foot upon a projecting
strap, and scrambled over her back.

"Yann!" shouted my father.

I heard him; but at that moment a pistol went
pop-pop from the cliff, a bullet struck the shingle with
a sharp ping! and Lilith leaped forward as if Satan
had twisted her tail.

I had neither bridle nor saddle. There was only the
pack-frame, two panniers, and a greasy horse beneath me. My
hands closed upon a rope, my knees closed upon Lilith,
and the next moment we were in the midst of
the pack-train, going up the cliff path at a gallop.

Behind us men shouted in English. The lugger's crew shouted
in Breton. Another shot cracked, the sea gave back the
sound, and for a few moments the whole cove resembled
the wicked place described by Père Alain in Lent—darkness, flames,
noise, and everyone running in a different direction.

The path rose so steeply that I seemed to be
lying upon Lilith's tail. Stones flew from under her hoofs.
On my right the cliff went up like a wall;
on my left was emptiness, with the white surf turning
far below. The greased packhorses jostled one another, their ankers
knocking, their nostrils smoking. A man tried to seize the
leading horse and fell flat on his face. Another sprang
at Lilith's head; she struck out, and I saw his
lantern go bob-bobbing down the slope before it burst.

At the top the track widened. The pack-train divided—some horses
turning inland, others following the cliff. Lilith took the inland
way. I heard pursuit behind us, but whether smugglers, soldiers,
or the devil himself, I could not look round.

"Stop!" I cried in French.

She went faster.

"Whoa!" I cried in English, that being nearly all the
English I knew.

She tossed her head.

Then, without thinking, I shouted as I would to our
pony at home:

"Doucement, ma bihan! Doucement donc!"

The change was remarkable. Her ears came forward. Her stride
shortened, though she did not stop, and when I said,
"À droite!" she swerved to the right at the fork
of a lane.

This astonished me so much that I forgot my fear
for half a minute. The black mare understood Breton.

We passed between high banks where trees met overhead. Branches
whipped my face. Once we splashed through a stream; once
we crossed a road just before three mounted men swept
along it. Their coats showed dark red in the starlight,
and one called out. Lilith answered by jumping a stone
hedge.

I had never jumped a hedge. I do not recommend
learning at midnight upon a greased mare without reins. I
rose from her back, came down upon the wooden frame,
and bit my tongue. Yet the doll-box remained secure, which
seemed to me proof that Providence had a particular regard
for Mademoiselle Keranguin's order.

The other packhorses had disappeared. Lilith went on alone through
an unknown country, and I began to think of my
father. I saw again his face turned towards me upon
the beach, his mouth open in anger or fear; then
the tub-boat among the breakers and the lugger beyond it.
Had he escaped? Had he returned for me? Would he
believe that I had been killed, or—worse—that I had let
the English take me?

The excitement drained out of me. I was cold, bruised,
and very small beneath that immense foreign sky.

Lilith trotted steadily. Whenever we came to a crossing she
chose her way without help. Cottages appeared, with a faint
red gleam behind their shutters; dogs barked; once an old
woman opened a door and held up a candle. Her
cap and wrinkled face shone for an instant and vanished
like a scrap of a dream.

Gradually the eastern sky whitened. Hills emerged from darkness; fields
divided themselves by walls and hedges; the wet road became
silver under Lilith's hoofs. Cornwall, which I had expected to
look entirely unlike Brittany, looked sufficiently like it to make
me homesick. There were the same low farms crouched from
the wind, the same bent trees and patches of furze—only
the road-signs, when we passed them, bore words I could
not read.

At last Lilith turned through a broken gate into a
sunken track choked with bracken. It seemed to lead nowhere.
We went under twisted oaks, round a shoulder of rock,
and came suddenly upon a grey farmhouse hidden in a
hollow.

No smoke rose from its chimneys. Its windows were narrow,
and ivy covered one whole end. The place had such
a deserted look that I thought Lilith had brought me
to a ruin; but she stopped before a side door
and struck it three times with her hoof.

Presently bolts were withdrawn. A small woman appeared, holding a
candle above her head.

She wore a loose green gown, much patched at the
elbows, and her grey hair hung down in a plait.
Though she must then have been near fifty, her face
was lively and sharp, like a bird's.

"Lilith?" she said in French. "Why alone? Where is Jago?"

Then she saw me.

"Holy Virgin! Who are you?"

"Yann Riel, of Roscoff."

"Roscoff!"

She came into the yard, set the candle upon the
stones, and clapped both hands.

"You are Breton?"

"Yes, madame."

"Say something."

I thought this a foolish request, since I had already
spoken; nevertheless I said, "The mare is tired, and so
am I."

"In Breton, little idiot!"

So I repeated it in Breton.

She uttered a cry, seized my hand, and kissed it.
Then she spoke to Lilith in the dialect of Léon,
calling her *ma merc'hig*, her little daughter, and the mare
lowered her proud head against the woman's shoulder.

"I taught her," the lady said. "Jago swore no horse
should hear Christian speech, so I taught her my own.
And she has brought me a countryman! Come down, my
little Yann—no, wait, you will break your neck."

With surprising strength she lifted the pack-frame sideways while I
slid into her arms. My legs had forgotten their office.
They folded under me, and we both sat down in
the yard.

The lady began to laugh. I laughed also, though I
was nearer crying.

"And the box?" she asked suddenly. "The box from Roscoff?"

"It is here."

"My children!"

She flew to the pannier, tore at the cords, then
stopped and looked over her shoulder.

"Forgive me. You are flesh and blood, and they are
wood. Food first."

She led me into a kitchen where turf glowed upon
the hearth. The room smelled of peat, apples, and lavender.
An old Cornish servant called Martha stared when she saw
me, crossed herself, and at once began frying bacon. Between
them they stripped off my wet jersey, rubbed my bruises
with brandy—excellent brandy, which I suspected had made the journey
from Brittany before me—and wrapped me in a coat much
too large.

The lady was Heloise Keranguin.

She questioned me while I ate: my father's name, my
mother's parish, which saints stood in the church at Roscoff,
whether the great fig-tree still grew near the harbour, whether
one could see the tower of Saint-Pol on a clear
morning. Before I had swallowed one answer, she put three
more questions.

When I told her of the raid, she grew grave.

"Penrose knew there were patrols."

"He did not know they would come that night."

"Penrose never knows until the door is broken. And your
father?"

"He reached the boat, I think."

"You think?"

Her tone made the bacon stick in my throat.

She saw this and patted my hand.

"He escaped. These Bretons escape everything—except women and bad weather.
We shall send to the coast. There is a packet
lying near Fowey which sails for Guernsey, and from Guernsey
you will find a Roscoff boat."

"I must take back the mare."

"No, my cabbage. Lilith is mine."

That solved a difficulty which had troubled me since dawn.

After breakfast Heloise carried the doll-box upstairs. I followed because
she beckoned, though Martha shook her head and muttered that
the mistress had slept too little.

We passed through a narrow corridor into a great bedchamber.
Heavy green curtains covered the windows. A bed stood upon
a dais, its posts carved with grapes and angels, its
canopy hung in faded pink dimity. Though the room was
clean, it had the closed, watchful stillness of a chapel
after a funeral.

Upon a long table by the wall stood a miniature
town.

At first I thought it a toy, but no child
could have made it. There were cardboard buildings with arched
windows; a chapel with a gilt cross; gardens stitched from
green and brown silk; trees of twisted wire covered in
sponge; and a pond made from looking-glass, over which a
bridge no longer than my finger had been laid. Tiny
gravel walks ran between box hedges. In the chapel yard
stood wooden nuns and little girls in dresses of blue,
white, and rose-coloured stuff.

Heloise opened my father's box.

The fifteen dolls lay in rows, stiff and pale, with
jointed knees and elbows. Their painted eyes all looked upward
as if awaiting instruction.

"One, two, three—fifteen!" she cried. "Penrose said ten. I wrote
fifteen. He answered twelve. Your father has brought fifteen. Your
father is a prince."

"My father says dolls are foolish cargo."

"Then he is a prince without imagination."

She lifted the figures out one by one and placed
them around the miniature chapel. As soon as her fingers
touched them, her manner changed. The quick questions ceased. She
bent close to each doll, considering it with tender seriousness.

"This tall one shall be Sister Angélique. She had a
long nose and always smelled of violets. This is little
Yvonne, who stole plums and confessed before anyone knew they
were gone. Here—bend the arm. She must carry the banner.
And this one will be me."

The doll chosen for Heloise had yellow hair and blue
eyes, neither of which resembled her. She clothed it in
a scrap of white muslin and set it among the
schoolgirls.

"What is this place?" I asked.

"The convent of Sainte-Anne at Quimper, as it was when
I was sixteen."

She drew back one of the green curtains. Morning entered
reluctantly, touching the gilt cross, the silk gardens, and the
mirror pond.

"I was educated there," she said. "We were thirty-two girls,
and twelve sisters, and old Père Matthieu, who pretended to
be deaf when we laughed during instruction. Every year for
the Feast of the Virgin we made a procession round
the garden. The youngest carried flowers. The elder girls bore
candles, though once the wind blew out every flame and
set Sister Agnès's veil on fire."

She smiled, but her eyes remained upon the miniature.

"When I came to England, I remembered the garden incorrectly.
I made the pond too near the chapel and the
south wall too high. Then a woman from Quimper visited
Plymouth and set me right. Since then I have measured
everything by memory."

"Why did you come to England?"

"Because gentlemen in Paris discovered that convents were wicked, that
priests were criminals, and that girls who prayed before breakfast
endangered the Republic."

I knew something of the Revolution. Every Breton child did.
Men spoke of drowned priests at Nantes, burned châteaux, roadside
crosses broken with hammers, and heads carried through towns. Yet
these things belonged to grown people's talk and had never
before seemed connected to small silk gardens or dolls with
painted hair.

"The sisters were sent away," Heloise continued. "Some went to
their families. Some had no families. The chapel became a
warehouse. The garden was dug for potatoes. Père Matthieu died
in prison."

"And the girls?"

"Scattered. Married. Hidden. Dead. Alive somewhere under other names."

She touched the little figure representing herself.

"My father arranged my marriage to Alain de Kersaint. He
was no great nobleman, whatever the English imagined. He owned
three poor farms, a leaking roof, and a library which
he valued more than all the farms. We were to
marry in October. In August he fled to Jersey; in
September I followed, dressed as a fisherman's boy and smelling
so strongly of herring that he did not kiss me
when I arrived."

She laughed then—a brief, real laugh.

"We came from Jersey to this farm. It belonged to
an English cousin of Alain's mother. We believed the troubles
would end in a month, then by Christmas, then next
spring. Exiles live by next spring."

Her hand rested upon the miniature chapel.

"Alain taught French to a clergyman's sons. I embroidered waistcoats.
We saved money to go home. Then came sickness. He
died in that bed."

She turned, and I looked at the high curtained bed
upon the dais. Until then it had been only furniture.
Now its dark canopy seemed to fill half the room.

"How long ago?" I asked.

"Nine years."

I was silent. At twelve, nine years is nearly the
measure of a life.

Above the bed hung a small brass plate. I could
read the French inscription:

**ALAIN-MARIE DE KERSAINT
DIED IN EXILE
LOOKING TOWARDS HOME**

"Could he see Brittany?" I asked.

"No. The window faces east, and Brittany lies south. But
dying people see more truly than the rest of us."

She replaced the curtain, and the room darkened again.

For some minutes she arranged the new dolls. Two became
banner-bearers; four knelt before the chapel; six walked in pairs;
one was made a nun by means of black cloth
and white thread. The last she set outside the convent
gate.

"That is Alain," she said.

"But he was not a girl."

"No. Therefore he waits outside."

This seemed reasonable.

A knock sounded below. Martha entered with a bearded man
in a leather coat, one of Penrose's people. He reported
that the *Bonne Espérance* had escaped. Two beachmen were taken,
one horse had broken a leg, and a riding-officer had
lost both boots attempting to hold a greased freighter. No
Breton had been captured.

My knees weakened again, but this time from relief.

"Your father sent word," the man said in bad French.
"Boy to be put aboard *Mary Rose* at Polkerris. Sailing
evening tide."

"What word?" I demanded.

The man scratched his beard.

"He said: 'Tell the young fool his mother will murder
us both.'"

That was certainly my father.

Heloise wished to keep me until noon, but the path
to the coast was long and patrols were abroad. Martha
packed bread, cheese, and apples. The bearded man found me
dry clothes, which had belonged to a boy twice my
size; I tied the breeches up with cord and rolled
the sleeves to my elbows.

Before I left, Heloise took me again to the great
bedchamber. The procession was complete now. Tiny nuns and schoolgirls
curved around the mirror pond, their wooden arms bent to
carry flowers and candles. In the centre, the doll which
was Heloise seemed to turn its painted face towards the
figure waiting beyond the gate.

"You brought back the Feast," she said.

"My father brought it."

"But you carried it through the soldiers."

"Lilith carried it."

"Then between your father, Lilith, and yourself, I must divide
the honour."

From beneath her gown she drew a silver crucifix upon
a worn chain. It was old work, plain except for
a little flower engraved at the ends of the cross.

"This belonged to Sister Angélique. She gave it to me
when we left the convent. It is payment for the
dolls."

"My father was paid beforehand."

"Then it is payment for the delivery."

"I did not deliver them on purpose."

"Very few brave things are done entirely on purpose."

She put the chain over my head.

I objected that it was too valuable. She answered that
a thing shut in a drawer served neither God nor
memory, and that if I refused she would send it
to my father, who would certainly sell it for brandy.
This argument compelled me to accept.

At the door she kissed both my cheeks and then,
to my surprise, bowed to me as formally as if
I had been a gentleman.

"Remember me when you see Saint-Pol against the sunrise."

"You will return and see it yourself," I said.

"Next spring," she replied.

The bearded man and I walked away through the bracken.
At the turn of the path I looked back. Heloise
stood beneath the ivy, one hand resting upon Lilith's black
neck. The house behind her appeared grey and empty, but
the morning sun had reached an upper window, and the
glass shone like a lamp.

I reached Polkerris without meeting a riding-officer, though once we
lay for half an hour beneath furze while two dragoons
passed on the road. The *Mary Rose* carried clay, onions,
and three passengers who asked no questions. From Guernsey I
found a fishing-boat bound for Roscoff, and arrived home six
days after my departure.

My mother embraced me, wept over me, and struck my
father with a wooden spoon. My father examined the crucifix,
said it was good silver, and asked whether the dolls
had arrived undamaged. When I told him yes, he nodded
as if this had been the object of the whole
expedition.

I never saw Heloise Keranguin again. Letters went uncertainly in
those years; Penrose was transported; the farm changed hands; and
when peace at last allowed easier passage, no one in
Quimper could tell me what had become of her. Some
said she died in Cornwall. Others believed she entered a
religious house in Belgium. One old woman remembered a grey-haired
lady kneeling for a whole morning in the ruined garden
of Sainte-Anne, but whether that lady was Heloise, the dickens
knew.

The miniature convent, the fifteen dolls, the mirror pond, and
the little wooden Alain waiting beyond the gate—all vanished with
her.

But I have the crucifix still.

When I hold it, I remember the black beach and
the pistols going pop-pop; I remember Lilith carrying me beneath
the stars because I spoke to her in Breton; and
most of all I remember that lonely room in England,
where a woman exiled from her country rebuilt her youth
out of cardboard, silk, and fifteen little wooden dolls.

---

Here Captain Riel's narrative ends.

It may interest the reader to know that the silver
crucifix descended to his eldest daughter and was, in 1879,
still preserved by the family. As to his later assertion
that he abandoned smuggling from the day of his Cornish
adventure, I can only observe that the harbour records mention,
with suspicious frequency, a vessel named *Lilith*, owned by one
Yann Riel, master mariner.

—A. L. M.

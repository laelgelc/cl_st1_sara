# The Barred Door

In the year 1436, when James the First of Scotland
kept the season of Carnival within the walls of a
Dominican monastery at Perth, there were many who wondered that
a prince so keen of wit should lodge beneath a
roof which neither moat nor battlement defended. Yet the King
loved not only the rude exercises of his northern realm—the
chase, the tourney, and the casting of the heavy stone—but
also music, minstrelsy, and such gentler arts as he had
learned during the long captivity of his youth. Thus the
grave cloister, though dedicated to prayer, rang for a little
while with laughter, song, and the light footfall of courtly
company.

It was a strange contrast. Without, Scotland lay stern beneath
the winter sky, divided by feuds, and vexed by lawless
nobles who obeyed the Crown only when the Crown was
too strong to be resisted. Within, waxen tapers shone upon
embroidered garments; pages hurried along passages worn by the sandals
of friars; and the Queen, Joan Beaufort, sat among her
ladies while the King listened to a harper or joined
in some merry device.

James was no feeble sovereign. His enemies themselves could not
deny his accomplishments, his courage, or the vigor with which
he strove to make royal justice respected from the Border
to the Highlands. But in an age when a great
lord considered his own will a better law than any
ordinance sealed by a king, justice was easily named tyranny,
and firmness oppression. James had used craft; he had sometimes
been harsh; and in depriving powerful men of lands and
dignities, he had not always persuaded the world that his
title was better than his strength. Above all, the confiscation
of the earldom of Strathern had planted a bitter hatred
in hearts already hostile to him.

Sir Robert Graham became the chief instrument of that hatred.
He persuaded himself, as violent men commonly do, that revenge
was righteousness; and beneath words concerning liberty and ancient right
he concealed the foul purpose of murder. Other discontented nobles
lent him aid, some boldly, some with whispered promises, and
some by that cowardly silence which is often the most
useful servant of treason.

More dreadful still, the King's own chamberlain was false.

A castle may resist an army while loyalty keeps the
keys; but the strongest palace is already taken when the
trusted servant within has sold its doors. The monastery at
Perth possessed bolts, bars, and other poor defenses against sudden
violence. These were secretly removed. Windows which might have offered
escape were made secure against those within, while the passage
by which assassins meant to enter was left ready for
their approach. The deed was prepared not in the heat
of anger, but slowly, knowingly, and under the protection of
familiar faces.

Warnings were not wanting. History, when some great calamity draws
near, seems often to send a shadow before it, as
the cloud crosses the valley ere the storm breaks upon
the hill.

A Highland woman, wild in aspect and earnest in speech,
sought the King.

"My liege," she cried, "if you pass this night where
you purpose, death shall find you before another day."

The courtiers smiled. What was a wandering woman's prophecy amid
the lights and revelry of Carnival? Some called her crazed;
others thought she had been prompted by those who desired
to alarm the King. James himself, accustomed to threats and
proud of mastering danger, refused to be governed by so
dark a message.

"Good woman," said he, more lightly than the occasion deserved,
"kings must keep their appointed road, though fear stand by
the wayside."

She would have spoken again, but was turned away.

Alas! The humble lips had uttered truth.

Even among the conspirators there were tremors and misgivings. Conscience
may be silenced, but it is seldom slain; and when
men assemble for a bloody act, the bravest in wickedness
sometimes starts at a sound which an innocent child would
scarcely notice. Yet Sir Robert Graham urged them onward. The
hour had come, he declared; delay would be ruin; and
each man's safety depended upon the completion of their common
crime.

Night deepened over Perth. The last merriment faded from the
monastery, and the solemn quiet proper to the place returned.
Within the royal apartments the King remained with the Queen
and her ladies. He had laid aside sword and armor.
Those around him were women, unarmed attendants, and servants who
had no reason to expect an assault beneath a consecrated
roof.

Then came a sound in the passage.

At first it was doubtful—a tread, a murmur, the faint
jar of a door. Presently it grew nearer. There were
many feet, and they moved with the heavy haste of
men who desired speed but feared discovery.

The King listened.

"What noise is that?"

No one answered. A lady approached the outer door. From
beyond it came the confused clatter of weapons.

"Treason!" cried a voice. "Treason! Save the King!"

Instantly all was terror and action. James sprang toward the
door and called for the bolts.

Alas! The bolts and bars were gone!

The women searched desperately for some means of fastening the
entrance. There was none. The chamberlain's treason had done its
work well. A heavy piece of furniture was dragged forward,
but the passage was narrow, the moments few, and already
rough hands were trying the door from without.

The windows offered no escape. They had been secured.

"Is there no other way?" asked the Queen.

The King remembered a vault beneath the floor, a place
once communicating with another part of the building. If the
old outlet remained open, he might descend and pass beyond
the assassins.

A board was lifted. The dark hollow appeared.

"Your Grace, quickly!" pleaded the ladies.

James lowered himself into the vault. But when he sought
the former outlet his hand met solid obstruction. At his
own command, given some time before because tennis-balls had been
lost through that inconvenient opening, it had been sealed.

Thus do small acts, innocent when performed, sometimes return with
terrible significance! The blocked passage which had saved a plaything
now imprisoned a king.

Above, the attackers hurled themselves against the door. The women
pressed upon it, but what could their strength avail? Their
sovereign was hidden, yet only a slender barrier divided him
from men who had sworn not to depart while he
lived.

The staples by which the door had formerly been barred
remained, though the bar itself had disappeared. Then Catherine Douglas,
one of the Queen's ladies, beheld them, and formed a
resolve as sudden as it was noble.

"There is no bar," cried another.

"Yes, there is," answered Catherine. "My arm shall be one."

Before they understood her purpose, she thrust her arm through
the iron staples, bracing it across the door.

O brave and noble-hearted woman! The limb was delicate, but
the spirit within it was true as tempered steel. She
knew that flesh and bone could not long oppose armed
men; yet if she gained one minute—one half-minute—the King might
discover escape, assistance might arrive, or the murderers' courage might
fail. She did not count the pain. Loyalty counts only
the life entrusted to it.

The door shook beneath the first blow.

Catherine's face grew pale, but she did not move.

Again the assassins struck. The frail arm strained against the
iron, while the Queen and her ladies cried to those
without that they should fear God, respect the sanctuary, and
remember that the man they sought was their anointed sovereign.

A third assault came, fiercer than the others.

There was a dreadful sound; Catherine fell backward, her arm
broken, and the door burst inward.

The conspirators rushed into the chamber with drawn weapons. Their
faces, disordered by fear and fury, seemed scarcely human. Some
demanded the King; others searched behind curtains and furniture. The
Queen opposed herself to them, pleading, reproaching, commanding—even then remembering
her royal dignity. She was wounded in the tumult, but
would not willingly yield a step.

"He is gone!" cried one of the ladies.

The words deceived the assassins for a little while. Believing
James had escaped by some hidden passage, they hurried out
to search the monastery.

Hope returned. The women lifted Catherine and gathered about the
opening of the vault. If aid could be summoned now,
the King might yet be saved. But whom could they
trust? The chamberlain was a traitor; the defenses had been
betrayed; and no armed band came running at the alarm.
The holy house seemed abandoned to wickedness.

Below, James attempted by his own strength to enlarge the
sealed outlet. His hands tore at the obstruction; but kings
are no more able than peasants to break stone with
bleeding fingers.

Footsteps returned.

The assassins had discovered that no man had passed from
the monastery. With fiercer shouts they re-entered the chamber. This
time concealment was impossible. They saw the displaced board. Lights
were brought. One man stooped and looked into the darkness.

"The King is here!"

Then Sir Robert Graham and his companions descended.

We will not follow them. Our tale concerns golden deeds,
not brazen cruelty; the arm that was stretched forth to
save, not the steel uplifted to destroy. It is enough
to say that James, unarmed and forsaken by those whose
duty it was to guard him, resisted with the courage
of his race, and that the traitors completed their foul
design. The Carnival ended in mourning; the monastery became a
place of lamentation; and Scotland learned that when lawless ambition
strikes at lawful rule, it wounds not one breast alone,
but the peace of a nation.

Yet the murderers did not gain the immortality they desired.
Their names survive beneath the shadow of their crime; while
Catherine Douglas, powerless to conquer and unable to save, is
remembered because she gave all that she possessed at the
extremity of peril. She had neither sword nor shield. She
made a shield of her own body.

Let us now pass across centuries and seas, from stern
Perth to splendid Versailles; from the court of a Scottish
king to that of an unhappy French queen. The costumes
have changed, the language is another, and the old feudal
nobles have given place to a revolutionary multitude; yet the
human heart remains unchanged. Treason still finds doors to betray,
and loyalty still stands within them.

During the French Revolution, when hatred of the monarchy had
become a fury that listened neither to pity nor to
law, a bloodthirsty mob invaded the palace of Versailles. Its
members declared that they sought Marie Antoinette. Against her all
miseries were charged; every rumor was believed; and those who
had never looked upon her face spoke of her as
though her death would feed the hungry and restore peace
to France.

The palace, lately the seat of magnificence, awoke to cries,
blows, and musket-shots. Marble stairs echoed beneath coarse and hurrying
feet. Royal guardsmen, surprised and greatly outnumbered, endeavored to defend
the passages. Some fell; others, bleeding from their wounds, fought
on, not because they supposed that their own lives could
alter the destiny of France, but because duty had appointed
them to a threshold.

Marie Antoinette was sleeping, attended only by her women, when
a guardsman, already wounded, reached the door near her apartments.

"Save the Queen!" he cried to Madame Auguier. "They are
coming!"

There was no time for questions. Madame Auguier bolted the
door and ran to awaken her mistress.

"Madame! Rise! Fly at once!"

The Queen started from sleep. She was but half dressed;
her hair fell loose upon her shoulders; and the bewildering
cries from without told more plainly than speech that danger
was at hand.

"My children!" she exclaimed.

"To the King's apartments, Madame! There may be safety there."

Behind them came a crash. The mob had reached the
outer entrance. The wounded guardsman and his comrades held the
passage. One stood upon the stairs against numbers before which
the stoutest soldier might have recoiled. Another, though his strength
was ebbing away, loaded and fired, then drew his sword
when he could fire no more.

Madame Auguier hurried the Queen through another passage. Every second
seemed an age. A door refused at first to open;
trembling hands struggled with it; voices sounded nearer.

"Courage, Madame!" said the faithful attendant. "Only a few steps
more."

They passed through. The door closed behind them, and presently
Marie Antoinette entered the King's apartments, where the royal family
gathered amid confusion and alarm. For that hour, at least,
she was saved.

Scarcely had she escaped when the invaders forced their way
into the chamber she had left. They found the bed
disordered and the room vacant. Rage followed disappointment; but the
intended victim was beyond their immediate reach. Between her and
death had stood a wounded soldier's warning, a woman's prompt
devotion, and the resistance of guardsmen who bought with their
blood the moments necessary for flight.

The French royal family was not thereby rescued from the
vast tempest which had broken over throne and kingdom. No
single barred door could turn back the Revolution, nor could
devoted attendants change the final doom of Marie Antoinette. But
success is not the measure of nobility. Catherine Douglas failed
to save King James; the defenders at Versailles won only
a brief reprieve. Yet failure cannot make their loyalty less
pure, any more than the setting of the sun can
extinguish the light already given.

Behold, then, the lesson written in these two fated chambers
of royalty. Power may be betrayed by its officers; crowns
may become helpless; stone walls may fail, and iron bars
be stolen away. But courage needs neither rank nor weapon.
It may dwell in a lady's fragile arm, in the
cry of a wounded guardsman, in the swift hands of
a tire-woman, or in the last stand of nameless soldiers
upon a stair.

The deeds of the cruel make history dark; yet darkness
has this unwilling service—it reveals the stars.

> When traitors rend the guarded gate,
> And steel is raised by lawless hate,
> One faithful heart, though frail it be,
> Outshines the pride of chivalry.

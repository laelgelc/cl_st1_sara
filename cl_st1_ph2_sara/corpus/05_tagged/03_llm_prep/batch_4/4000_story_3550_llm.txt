I am an old man. I have lived seventy years,
and for more than fifty of them I have known
that the life I lead is wrong.

I do not mean that I have merely committed particular
sins, though there have been many; nor that I have
sometimes been hard, vain, untruthful, and cowardly. I mean that
the whole arrangement of my life—my rooms, my food, my
clothes, my leisure, the respect shown me, even the books
in which I condemn injustice—rests upon the labour and humiliation
of people who possess neither leisure nor respect nor sufficient
food.

I have known this, and yet I have continued.

In youth I told myself that I was preparing to
serve mankind. In middle age I said that a man
with a family cannot overturn the order into which Providence
has placed him. In old age I have said that
it is too late. Thus the years have grown and
grown, my comforts have grown and grown, my understanding of
their cost has become more and more exact, and my
power to surrender them has seemed less and less.

I cannot plead ignorance. I cannot say that I have
not seen the cracked hands which polished my floors, carried
my wood, groomed my horses, and washed the linen from
my body. I cannot say that I do not know
why one man sleeps beneath a quilt and another on
straw, why one child is taught French and another, blue
with cold, drives cattle before sunrise. I know. And still
I ring the bell.

This is the most terrible thing—not that men are cruel
without knowing it, but that they arrange their thoughts so
that knowledge may exist in one chamber of the mind
and life in another. They pity the poor, discuss education,
subscribe to hospitals, and thank God before meals; and all
the while the meal, the hospital, the school, and the
very hour spent discussing mercy are purchased by the poor
man's necessity.

Perhaps it has been permitted me to remain among those
whom I accuse so that I may bear witness against
them. Yet even that thought may be only another justification.
I do not wish to acquit myself. Nor do I
wish to say that every poor man is good and
every rich man wicked. Among the poor there is drunkenness,
envy, stupidity, violence; among the rich there is tenderness, learning,
self-sacrifice, and sincere desire for justice. But justice does not
ask whether the master is amiable or the labourer quarrelsome.
It asks why one man commands and consumes while another
obeys and lacks bread.

I will therefore tell what I saw—not because it was
extraordinary, but because it was ordinary.

Alexander Ivanovich Volgin was thirty-eight years old and occupied a
respectable position in a Moscow bank, where he received twelve
thousand roubles a year, besides a yearly gratuity which varied
according to profits and which he always regarded as smaller
than it ought to have been. He worked from eleven
until four, except on Saturdays, when the bank closed at
three, and he considered himself overworked.

In July he went to stay with his cousin, Prince
Peter Nikolaevich Karsagin, on an estate of nine thousand acres
in Tula province. He arrived at half past seven in
the evening in a reserved compartment, bringing two leather trunks,
a dressing-case with silver-topped bottles, a dispatch box, a plaid,
a gun in a green baize cover, six novels, three
newspapers, four political reviews, and a small parcel containing a
silk scarf for his cousin's wife.

A carriage had been waiting at the station since six.
The coachman, Fedor, had left the estate at two in
the afternoon, because one could not be certain that the
train would keep its proper time. During the interval he
had fed the horses, eaten a piece of black bread,
and sat under the station shed while flies gathered on
the animals' flanks. Volgin, seeing the pair of greys, the
lacquered carriage, and Fedor's long blue coat, was pleased.

"This is what I call living," he thought, breathing the
warm, dusty air. "One forgets in Moscow that Russia is
still Russia."

He asked Fedor whether the harvest promised well.

"God knows, Alexander Ivanovich. The rye is thin."

"Too little rain?"

"Too little at first. Now there may be too much."

Volgin nodded gravely. He liked sensible replies from peasants. During
the fourteen-verst drive he looked at the fields, at women
bending among the flax, at barefoot children watching the carriage,
and at the low, smoke-blackened huts.

"They must have schools," he thought. "That is the first
necessity. Everything depends upon education."

Fedor, meanwhile, was thinking that one of the traces had
cracked and that he must mend it before the return
journey, though the stableman would probably say it had been
his fault. He also wondered whether his wife had managed
to borrow money for salt.

At the house, Volgin was received by Peter Nikolaevich, his
wife Elena Pavlovna, her unmarried sister, and two guests: a
district marshal with a red face and a young man
from Petersburg who wrote articles about local self-government. Three servants
carried in the luggage. A fourth brought tea. A fifth
led away the dogs that sprang against Volgin's pale travelling
suit.

The next morning he woke at nine in a high,
cool bedroom smelling of lavender and waxed wood. His boots,
which he had left dusty beside the bed, had been
cleaned. Fresh water stood in a porcelain basin; hot water
steamed in a covered copper jug; his razors had been
laid out on a folded towel; the newspapers, ironed so
that the pages were smooth, waited on a small table
beside coffee, cream, rolls, butter, and apricot preserve.

He stretched and felt that country life was beneficial.

The servant who had brought the hot water was called
Prokhor. He was sixteen and had risen at five. Before
carrying Volgin's water he had swept two corridors, cleaned six
pairs of shoes, assisted in bringing wood to the kitchen,
and been struck on the ear by the under-butler for
breaking a saucer whose edge had already been cracked. Stephen,
the elderly valet assigned to Volgin, had served in the
house for thirty-two years. He suffered from swollen veins and
slept behind a partition near the pantry, but he stood
silently while Volgin drank his coffee and read an article
on excessive government expenditure.

"The country is being ruined by useless officials," Volgin said.

"Yes, sir," Stephen answered.

Volgin bathed, shaved, brushed his teeth with perfumed powder, clipped
one fingernail, changed his linen, chose a grey English suit,
rejected a spotted necktie in favour of a blue one,
and finally went downstairs at eleven. He had intended to
rise early and take exercise before breakfast, but sleep, he
told himself, was also necessary to health.

On the terrace, breakfast had been arranged beneath striped awnings.
There were eggs, cold veal, smoked fish, cucumbers on ice,
two kinds of cheese, fresh berries, white bread, black bread,
honey, coffee, tea, and a dish of little hot pies
which Elena Pavlovna said were not properly made.

"They are heavy," she said, breaking one open and eating
only the crust.

Peter Nikolaevich spoke of the heat, the marshal of a
disputed boundary, and the Petersburg writer of a speech he
intended to deliver at the provincial assembly. Volgin listened while
spreading butter thickly on white bread. He was not hungry
after the coffee and rolls in his room, but country
air, he said, gave one an appetite.

After breakfast they walked through the gardens. A gardener and
three boys had been at work since sunrise watering beds,
clipping paths, removing caterpillars, and carrying baskets of weeds. The
visitors admired the roses. Elena Pavlovna complained that the second
planting of heliotrope was poor.

At the edge of the orchard they came upon several
peasant women hoeing potatoes on the estate's own land. Their
shirts were dark with perspiration between the shoulders. One woman
had tied an infant to a tree in a piece
of sacking; the child cried hoarsely, paused, and cried again.

"Why does she not nurse it?" Elena Pavlovna asked.

"The overseer does not like them stopping," Peter Nikolaevich said.
"Besides, they are paid by the row."

"That is dreadful," said Volgin.

He looked at the child and felt sincere pity. For
several moments he considered saying something to Peter Nikolaevich, but
the young writer had begun to explain a plan for
elected school boards, and Volgin turned to him with interest.

They continued along the clipped path.

At noon a groom brought saddled horses. Volgin rode a
bay mare whose coat shone like dark satin and whose
yearly keep cost more than many peasant families received in
money. They passed the village school, a low brick building
with broken steps.

"You ought to repair that," Volgin said.

"There are no funds," answered Peter Nikolaevich.

"But education is the foundation."

"Certainly. Only last year I spent three hundred roubles on
the roof."

"The peasants should contribute something themselves. People value only what
they make sacrifices for."

The groom riding behind them heard this. He had contributed
four roubles and eighteen kopecks to the school levy, though
his eldest daughter had no boots and could attend only
in autumn and spring. He said nothing.

When they returned, luncheon was waiting. The table, covered with
spotless linen, shone with cut glass and silver. There was
clear soup with little pastries; river trout; chicken with mushrooms;
roast mutton; peas; a pudding soaked in rum; fruit; coffee;
red wine, white wine, and mineral water brought from abroad.
Volgin had ridden for nearly two hours and ate with
pleasure.

They were discussing an auction in Moscow. A small Madonna,
attributed by some to a pupil of Raphael and by
others to a later Bolognese painter, was to be sold.
Volgin had seen it twice.

"The face is remarkable," he said. "Not beautiful in the
conventional sense, but spiritual. One feels maternity purified of the
merely animal."

"Are you going to buy it?" asked Elena Pavlovna.

"If the price is not absurd. I told Semyonov not
to exceed two hundred."

"Two hundred!" cried the marshal. "For that gloomy little thing?"

"It is not gloomy. It is austere."

At that moment the under-butler entered and said that a
peasant from Borki wished to see his excellency.

"What does he want?"

"He says his horse died."

Peter Nikolaevich frowned, not angrily but wearily. Horses were always
dying, roofs burning, cows falling ill, sons being taken for
soldiers. The misfortunes of the village seemed to him like
rain: regrettable, recurrent, and beyond the control of a reasonable
man.

"Tell him to wait."

"He has waited since morning."

"Oh, let him come after luncheon."

Volgin, who had heard, felt uncomfortable. He continued eating, but
the mutton lost some of its flavour.

"Do many of them lose horses?" he asked.

"This year, yes," said Peter Nikolaevich. "There was glanders in
one district, and then they buy the worst animals imaginable.
What can you expect for twenty-five roubles?"

"Could there not be a cooperative fund?"

The Petersburg writer began at once to describe cooperative credit
in Belgium. By the time coffee was served, the peasant
had been forgotten.

After luncheon the men went to the smoking room, where
Volgin read telegrams in the newspaper while Peter Nikolaevich slept
in a leather chair. At three, the under-butler again mentioned
the peasant.

"Send him in," said Peter Nikolaevich, opening his eyes.

Mitri Sudarikov entered without a cap. He was forty-one, though
the hollows beneath his eyes and the grey in his
beard made him appear older. His shirt, patched at the
elbows, was wet with sweat; his birch-bark shoes were split;
straw clung to one trouser leg. He smelled of dust,
horsehide, and the sour smoke of a peasant hut.

He bowed, crossed himself toward the icon, and then unexpectedly
dropped to his knees.

"Get up, get up," Peter Nikolaevich said. "What is this?"

"Have mercy, your excellency. I am ruined."

"What happened?"

"The mare died in the night. She swelled up after
work and fell. By morning she was cold."

"Did you call the veterinary man?"

"With what money, your excellency? And he is twelve versts
away."

"You should have come sooner."

Mitri lowered his head. It is easy to tell a
man what he should have done when what prevented him
from doing it was the absence of money.

"I came to ask—lend me something. I will work it
off. At haymaking, harvest, carting—whatever your excellency orders."

"How much do you want?"

"A horse cannot be had for less than thirty-five. Even
a poor one."

"Thirty-five!" Peter Nikolaevich said, as if Mitri had requested a
carriage. "Where am I to find thirty-five roubles for everyone
whose horse dies?"

Mitri said nothing. A fly moved along his temple. He
did not brush it away.

Peter Nikolaevich asked how much land he held, whether he
had paid his taxes, whether his eldest boy could work,
and whether he drank. To each question Mitri gave short,
submissive answers. He held nine strips in three fields; the
taxes were partly unpaid; the eldest boy was eleven; he
drank sometimes, like others.

"There it is," said Peter Nikolaevich. "Vodka."

Mitri raised his eyes, then lowered them again. He had
indeed drunk two glasses at Easter, one at Trinity, and
more than he should have at his brother's wedding the
previous winter. Once, after quarrelling with his wife, he had
spent forty kopecks at the tavern. This was wrong. But
the mare had not died because of those forty kopecks;
she had died because she was old, ill-fed, and worked
beyond her strength—like Mitri himself.

Peter Nikolaevich gave him five roubles and told him to
apply to the village elders.

Mitri took the note, bent until his forehead nearly touched
the floor, and backed toward the door.

"Wait," Volgin said.

Mitri stopped.

Volgin took out a purse of soft brown leather. It
contained forty roubles in notes, several gold coins, and silver.
He selected three roubles.

"Here. I am only a visitor, but this may help."

Mitri bowed first to Volgin, then to Peter Nikolaevich, then
toward the icon.

"May God reward you."

The words pleased Volgin and at the same time embarrassed
him. When the peasant had gone, he said:

"What a terrible condition they live in."

"Yes," said Peter Nikolaevich. "But if you give one of
them thirty-five roubles, twenty others will be here tomorrow."

"Of course. Individual charity solves nothing. The whole system must
be changed."

This conclusion restored Volgin's peace of mind. He went upstairs
to write letters.

One letter was to Semyonov, the dealer, authorising him to
bid up to one hundred and eighty roubles for the
Madonna, provided the attribution could be supported and the panel
had not been repainted. Volgin hesitated over the figure. Then,
remembering the spiritual quality of the face, he wrote a
cheque for one hundred and eighty roubles—sixty times the sum
he had given Mitri—and enclosed it.

He did not make the comparison.

Mitri left the great house with eight roubles folded into
a scrap of newspaper and hidden beneath his shirt. He
had eaten nothing since dawn. In the servants' courtyard he
saw a crust lying beside a tub, but two dogs
were near it, and he was ashamed to pick it
up.

The distance to Borki was six versts. He walked quickly
because the village was that day cutting the communal meadow,
and each household had to provide a worker. Without him
his strip would be left, or his wife would have
to send their eldest child.

Mitri's mare, Manka, had been seventeen years old. She had
a white blaze and a swollen left knee and could
no longer pull a full load uphill without stopping. Yet
she ploughed, harrowed, carried firewood, drew water, transported sheaves, and
took the family's small quantity of rye to the mill.
Without a horse, nine strips of land scattered among the
holdings of others were not property but a burden.

He had bought Manka nine years earlier for twenty-eight roubles,
of which twelve had been borrowed. For three years he
had paid the debt in labour. Since then the mare
had foaled once, but the foal died. Last spring there
had not been enough hay, and during winter Manka was
fed chopped straw mixed with chaff. In April she pulled
the plough while her ribs showed beneath her hide. Two
days before her death, Mitri had noticed that she refused
oats—though there had been only one handful to offer—and stood
with her head lowered. He worked her nevertheless. What else
could he do?

That morning, before sunrise, he had found her stretched beside
the shed. His youngest daughter was sitting on the ground,
stroking the dead animal's ear.

"Get away," he shouted.

The child began to cry. Mitri struck the mare's flank
with his foot, though he knew she was dead, and
cursed. Then he entered the hut and told his wife,
Anisya.

She stared at him. Five children were still asleep—two on
the stove, three on a bench and the floor. Smoke
from damp wood hung beneath the ceiling.

"You killed her," Anisya said.

"I killed her?"

"You saw she was sick."

"And should I have ploughed myself?"

"You might have rested her."

"And eaten earth in autumn?"

They quarrelled in low, fierce voices so as not to
wake the children. Then Anisya sat down and covered her
face with her apron. Mitri stood looking at her bent
shoulders. He wanted to say that he was sorry, that
he had known Manka since she was a thin-legged young
mare, that he too was frightened. Instead he took his
cap and went to beg.

To beg.

A man may endure hunger, cold, pain in his back,
and the death of an animal he has fed with
his own hands. But to stand before another man, remove
his cap, call him benefactor, and wait while that man
decides whether his children's need is true—that is a special
humiliation. We have made this humiliation into an institution and
call it charity.

When Mitri reached Borki, the women were returning from the
meadow with water pails. Anisya stood outside their hut, the
baby on her hip. She looked first at his face
and then at his hands.

"Well?"

"Eight."

"Only eight?"

"Only."

She bit her lip. He gave her the notes. She
counted them twice, although she could not read, and tucked
them inside her dress.

"We owe Gavrila two for flour," she said. "One and
twenty to the tax collector. There is no salt. And
the little one coughs."

"Keep five. Give me nothing."

"You must eat."

"I'll eat at the meadow."

There was no food at the meadow except what each
household carried.

Inside the hut the children had gathered around a wooden
bowl containing boiled potatoes. There were seven potatoes, blackened in
places by rot. The eldest boy, Fedka, had divided one
with the handle of a spoon. The smallest child licked
the empty salt cellar.

Mitri took half a potato, dipped it in the grey
water at the bottom of the bowl, and ate standing
up.

"Father, will we get another horse?" asked Fedka.

"We will get one."

"When?"

"When God gives it."

Fedka knew that this meant his father did not know.

Mitri took a blunt scythe from beneath the eaves and
started for the meadow. His neighbour, Yegor, overtook him on
the road. Yegor's shirt was open to the waist, exposing
a chest burnt brown by the sun.

"Well?" he asked.

"Eight roubles."

Yegor whistled.

"The prince has nine thousand acres."

"What of it?"

"Nothing. I only said."

At the meadow twenty-three men and boys were cutting in
a broken line. The grass was coarse and tangled, and
the ground beneath it uneven. Each stroke required the arms,
shoulders, back, waist, and legs; after every few paces the
mower stopped to sharpen the blade with a whetstone carried
in a wooden holder at his belt. The sun stood
high. Mosquitoes rose from the damp hollows. Sweat ran into
Mitri's eyes.

He took his place at the end of the line.
Because he had been absent all morning, he was behind.
He swung faster than was wise, cutting a narrow swath,
breathing through his mouth.

No one asked whether he was tired. They were all
tired.

Old Akim, who was sixty-three and coughed blood in winter,
mowed beside his married son. Semyon's feet were wrapped in
rags because his bast shoes had worn through. A boy
of thirteen tried to keep pace with the men and
cried secretly when blisters opened on his palms. They mowed
because the grass would not wait, because rain might come,
because cattle must eat in winter, because if cattle died,
people went to the manor house and knelt.

At five, clouds gathered. The line moved faster. Mitri's hands
became numb; a pain seized him beneath the ribs; twice
the scythe struck earth. Yegor gave him water from a
wooden flask.

"Your blade is no good," Yegor said.

"It's the last one."

"I'll hammer it tonight."

"With what time?"

"We'll find time."

The men cut until the light turned yellow and the
shadows lengthened across the meadow. Then the women and children
spread the fallen grass. Anisya came carrying the baby and
leading their six-year-old daughter. She had brought a piece of
black bread wrapped in cloth, an onion, and a jar
of sour kvass.

The bread weighed perhaps a pound. It was for seven
people.

They sat near a willow. Mitri cut thin pieces with
his pocketknife, first for the children, then for Anisya, lastly
for himself. He ate slowly, making each bite last. The
baby reached for the crust in Anisya's hand, and she
softened it in her mouth before giving it to him.

A man came along the road.

He was not from Borki. His beard was almost white,
one eye watered continuously, and his coat, though the day
was warm, was tied around him with string. He carried
a bag but no staff.

"Christ be with you," he said.

"With you," the peasants answered.

He stopped. His eyes went to the bread and then
away.

"Have you eaten, grandfather?" Anisya asked.

"Yesterday."

She looked at Mitri. Mitri looked at the remaining bread.
There were three pieces and the end crust. Fedka saw
the look and lowered his own piece, though he had
not finished it.

"Give him the crust," Mitri said.

Anisya handed the old man the end, then, after a
moment, one of the pieces. The beggar crossed himself.

"God save your children."

"Eat," she said.

He sat apart, but the smallest girl went and watched
him. He broke off a morsel and gave it to
her. Thus the man who had not eaten since yesterday
shared with the child whose family feared there would be
no bread tomorrow.

No one praised them. No one called it philanthropy. No
newspaper printed their names.

At the manor house, tea was served at the same
hour in the drawing room: tea with lemon, cream, cakes,
strawberries, biscuits, and little sandwiches because Elena Pavlovna believed that
the country air made everyone hungry. Volgin, refreshed by a
bath and dressed in a white linen jacket, described Mitri's
visit to the ladies.

"It is appalling," he said. "A man's entire economic existence
depends on one worn-out horse."

Elena Pavlovna sighed.

"One cannot help everybody."

"That is exactly the tragedy."

The Petersburg writer said that rural poverty was caused by
administrative centralisation. The marshal said it was caused by the
emancipation, which had taught peasants to expect everything and respect
nothing. Peter Nikolaevich maintained that the peasants' chief defect was
lack of foresight. His wife believed vodka was responsible. Volgin
considered that education, cooperative credit, and gradual legal reform were
all necessary.

They spoke earnestly and were not pretending. Each wished, in
his fashion, that there should be less suffering, provided that
the remedy did not disturb dinner, ownership, travel, inheritance, servants,
horses, or the pleasant order of the house.

After tea they played tennis. Two boys swept and marked
the court. A gardener's assistant retrieved balls from the nettles.
Volgin played badly but energetically and congratulated himself on taking
exercise. Later, while changing for dinner, he asked Stephen whether
the peasants were generally satisfied with Prince Peter Nikolaevich.

"They have their troubles, sir," Stephen answered.

"But he is a good landlord?"

"Certainly, sir."

Stephen had a married daughter in Borki. Her husband had
worked three months on the prince's roads and had not
yet received full payment. Stephen did not mention this. He
was fastening the collar of the man who had asked
the question, and dependence teaches precision in fastening collars, not
freedom in answering.

At dinner there were candles in silver branches, though daylight
still lingered at the windows. The soup had been sent
back because it was not hot enough. A bottle of
wine was opened and rejected as corked. Volgin ate sturgeon,
beef, asparagus, and ice flavoured with cherries. He told an
amusing story about a director of the bank who drew
twenty-five thousand roubles a year and nevertheless charged the institution
for his cab fares.

"Such greed is disgusting," he said.

Everyone agreed.

A servant carried away plates on which enough remained to
feed Mitri's family for a day. Some scraps went to
the dogs, some to the servants, some into a pail.

After dinner came music. Elena Pavlovna played Beethoven; the young
writer turned pages; Volgin sat near the open window and
felt elevated. Beyond the lit room the garden was dark.
Beyond the garden lay the fields, and beyond the fields
Borki, where Mitri and Anisya were deciding whether two roubles
must be spent on flour or kept toward a horse.

Volgin thought of the Madonna and hoped Semyonov would secure
it.

The next morning a telegram arrived: PURCHASED 175 ATTRIBUTION PROBABLE
CONDITION GOOD.

Volgin was delighted. He showed the telegram at breakfast.

"A bargain," he said. "If genuine, it is worth twice
that."

At nearly the same moment Mitri went to the horse
dealer in the neighbouring village. The cheapest animal offered was
a ribbed gelding with a clouded eye and a cough.
The dealer wanted thirty-two roubles.

"I have eight," Mitri said.

"Then buy eight roubles' worth of the tail."

Mitri returned on foot.

For the next twelve days he borrowed Yegor's horse at
night. He ploughed from ten in the evening until three
in the morning, returned the animal before dawn, slept an
hour, and then went to mow or cart for the
commune. In exchange he promised Yegor six days' labour at
harvest. His body became slower; his temper worsened; once he
struck Fedka for spilling kvass. Afterwards he sat outside the
hut, ashamed, but did not call the boy to him.

Anisya spent two of the eight roubles on rye flour,
one on salt, oil, and a little dried fish, one
rouble twenty kopecks on arrears, fifty kopecks on medicine which
did not cure the baby's cough, and thirty kopecks on
a new whetstone. Three roubles remained toward a horse. These
three roubles—the whole of Volgin's charity—lay wrapped in cloth behind
the icon, while the cheque for one hundred and seventy-five
had already purchased a painted image of the Mother of
God holding a well-fed divine child.

I repeat: Volgin was not a wicked man.

He loved his mother. He had once spent a night
beside a sick colleague. He disliked corporal punishment, subscribed twenty-five
roubles yearly to a home for orphans, and could not
see an animal beaten without protesting. When his own coachman's
son died, he paid for the funeral. He read serious
books and believed that society must improve.

Peter Nikolaevich was not a monster. Elena Pavlovna was not
without compassion. The writer truly desired representative government. The marshal,
who spoke harshly of peasants, had been brave in war
and tender to his grandchildren.

Why, then, did nothing follow?

Why could they see Mitri kneel and not see him?
Why could they hear that a horse had died, discuss
the economic consequences, give eight roubles, and then consume at
one meal what his household lacked for weeks? Why did
the crying child tied beneath the tree become merely an
illustration of the need for schools? Why did the groom's
silence, Stephen's bent back, Prokhor's swollen ear, the women's wet
shirts, the boy's blistered hands, and the beggar's hunger remain
outside their understanding, though all these things stood directly before
them?

Because to see truly would have required not pity but
change.

It would have required Volgin to ask who produced his
twelve thousand roubles, who made his five working hours possible,
who cleaned the dust from his boots, and why his
desire for a doubtful painting outweighed a family's need for
the animal by which it lived. It would have required
Peter Nikolaevich to ask by what justice nine thousand acres
belonged to him while Mitri's scattered strips could not feed
five children. It would have required all of them—and me,
and perhaps you—to recognise that kindness which preserves the cause
of suffering and relieves only a convenient fraction of its
result is not enough.

We say that the poor are improvident. What provision can
a man make from hunger?

We say that they drink. Why do we remember the
forty kopecks spent at the tavern and forget the hundreds
spent on wine at dinner?

We say that they are ignorant. Who keeps them labouring
from dawn until darkness and then reproaches them for not
reading?

We say that one cannot help everybody. But we do
not ask whether everybody is helping us.

The fields are ploughed; the bread is baked; the water
is carried; the linen is washed; the roads are made;
the horses are groomed; the lamps are filled; the children
of the wealthy are taught and guarded. Hands perform all
this—cracked, dirty, weary hands belonging to living people, each of
whom fears pain, loves a child, remembers humiliation, and desires
rest as we do.

Yet we speak of them as "labour," "the village," "the
lower orders," "the peasant question." We transform persons into an
arrangement, and then the arrangement into a necessity. We see—and
train ourselves not to see.

I cannot say how society must be remade in every
detail. I know only that the first command is not
obscure. Do not live by another's suffering. Do not take
more than you can justify before the hungry. Do not
call alms justice. Do not kneel before painted holiness while
a living mother divides her last bread.

I know this. Volgin knew part of it. All of
us, in quiet moments, know it.

Then why do kind, educated, religious people continue to eat,
dress, travel, inherit, debate, purchase beauty, and congratulate themselves on
compassion while workers bend beneath their windows and children wait
for bread?

Why do we see them—and yet not see?

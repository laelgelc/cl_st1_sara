## The Housewarming

On a damp autumn morning Adrian Prokhoroff, coffin-maker, at last
removed from his old lodgings near the market to the
yellow house in Nikitsky Lane which he had coveted for
eighteen years.

The change, though long desired and by no means cheaply
purchased, afforded him less pleasure than might have been expected.
Adrian was one of those prudent citizens to whom possession
immediately reveals defects invisible during expectation. No sooner had his
final chest been dragged across the threshold than he discovered
a crack in the stove, two broken panes in the
attic, and a looseness in the courtyard gate which, in
his opinion, invited every thief in Moscow to enter without
ceremony.

His daughters, Akulina and Daria, viewed matters differently. They ran
from room to room, disputed over the window facing the
lane, and arranged their ribbons before a small mirror while
the carriers were still bringing in the furniture. Their servant,
Aksinia, installed the brass kettle beside the stove and declared
that the kitchen was twice as light as the former
one, although the enlightened reader, had he inspected it, might
have judged this praise excessive.

By noon the household was in order. The sacred images
occupied the corner of the principal room; beneath them stood
a table covered with green cloth. Crockery, family linen, account
books, and a locked casket containing money were put in
their appointed places. Along the wall leaned several coffins: two
black, one brown, and one small white coffin trimmed with
blue braid, intended for a merchant's child whose recovery had
unfortunately spoiled the transaction. Mourning cloaks hung from pegs in
the passage, and in the yard a newly painted sign
displayed a gilded coffin under the inscription:

**COFFINS OF EVERY KIND MADE, REPAIRED, AND DELIVERED.
FUNERAL PROCESSIONS ARRANGED AT FAIR PRICES.**

Adrian examined the sign, frowned at the gilding, which he
thought too cheerful, and went indoors.

Our profession commonly influences our temper. The baker smells of
warmth and generosity; the tailor becomes attentive to appearances; the
tax collector, being occupied with public necessity, learns fortitude against
private tears. Adrian's commerce required silence, measurement, and a sober
regard for inevitable events. He was not cruel. He merely
considered death from its practical side. A long illness signified
uncertainty; a sudden apoplexy, ready money; a large family, disputes
over expense; a wealthy widow without attentive heirs, excellent business.

He had therefore been much preoccupied by the condition of
Agrafena Trukhina, the rich shopkeeper's wife from Razgulyay, who had
lain near death for three weeks. Adrian had supplied coffins
to her late husband, her brother, and two infant grandchildren,
and regarded the family almost as hereditary customers. Yet her
nephew had lately begun dealing with a fashionable undertaker nearer
the river. Adrian feared that distance, youth, and brass handles
might triumph over old acquaintance.

He was calculating the probable cost of velvet when someone
knocked at the door.

A stout, red-faced man entered, bowed, and introduced himself as
Gottlieb Schultz, shoemaker, whose shop occupied the house opposite. His
blue coat shone at the elbows, his waistcoat was ornamented
with silver flowers, and his face expressed that confidence peculiar
to a man who is satisfied with his wife, his
cellar, and his own workmanship.

"Respected neighbour," he said, "tomorrow my Luise and I celebrate
twenty-five years of lawful marriage. Do me the favour to
dine with us at two o'clock, and bring your young
ladies. There will be friends, music, and no speeches longer
than the sausages."

Adrian, who disliked both music and comparisons involving sausages, nevertheless
accepted. The shoemaker bowed again, complimented the new sign, and
departed with the brisk step of a man whose boots
cost him nothing.

The following day Adrian put on his best dark caftan,
his daughters adorned themselves with yellow hats, and at precisely
two they crossed the lane. We shall not, according to
the usual custom of modern storytellers, devote three pages to
the folds of the young ladies' sleeves. It is sufficient
to say that they considered themselves elegant and that their
father considered the ribbons expensive.

Schultz's rooms were crowded with German tradesmen and their families.
There were a baker white as his own flour, a
locksmith with iron-grey whiskers, a bookbinder whose purple countenance resembled
morocco leather, a watchmaker thin enough to have been constructed
from spare springs, and a tailor who examined every guest's
coat with silent compassion. Near the stove sat the district
watchman, Yourko, wearing his uniform and a smile of official
importance.

Madame Schultz, a round and active woman, embraced Adrian's daughters,
seated them beside two apprentices, and ordered everyone to eat.
The table bore roast goose, smoked ham, cabbage, pies, pickled
mushrooms, and a quantity of sausages which fully justified Schultz's
earlier figure of speech. Beer foamed in tall glasses; wine
appeared in green bottles; and a bowl of punch waited
on the sideboard like a reserve regiment.

The company began with a toast to the married pair.

"To Gottlieb and Luise! Another twenty-five years!"

Glasses were emptied.

Schultz then drank to his guests. The baker proposed the
health of all honest craftsmen. The locksmith drank to strong
locks and weak thieves. The tailor drank to well-dressed citizens.
The bookbinder drank to useful books, though no one present
was known to read them. Each toast demanded another glass,
and each glass suggested a fresh toast. They drank to
Germany, to Russia, to Moscow, to the emperor, to good
leather, sound flour, punctual payment, obedient wives, prosperous children, and
absent creditors.

Adrian at first preserved his habitual gravity. He ate thoroughly,
drank methodically, and answered questions with nods. But the punch,
being sweet, concealed its intentions. His eyes brightened; he began
to discuss oak prices with the locksmith and even laughed
when the tailor related how a councillor had been buried
in trousers made for another gentleman.

At last Schultz stood, raised his glass, and cried:

"My friends, let us drink to the health of those
for whom we work—our honoured customers!"

The shoemaker drank to his gentlemen's feet; the baker to
hungry stomachs; the tailor to vanity; the locksmith to household
suspicion. Everyone shouted approval.

Yourko leaned across the table toward Adrian.

"And you, little father," said he, "must drink to the
health of your customers too!"

For a moment there was silence. Then the bookbinder snorted,
the baker shook, and the entire table burst into laughter.

The jest was not refined, but refinement is seldom expected
from a watchman after six glasses of punch. Adrian's daughters
lowered their eyes. Schultz slapped the table and begged his
neighbour not to take offence. Yourko, delighted with his success,
raised an empty glass toward the coffin-maker and cried:

"May they all stand up and thank you!"

This produced still greater merriment.

Adrian smiled with compressed lips. He drank, but not to
his customers. From that moment he spoke no more. The
company continued its pleasures; a violin appeared; the apprentices danced;
Madame Schultz lost a slipper; the baker attempted a song
and forgot every line except the first. At length Adrian
rose, collected his daughters, and took leave with such rigid
politeness that Schultz, who was no longer capable of detecting
shades of expression, considered him deeply touched by the celebration.

Outside, the cold air revived Adrian's injury without noticeably improving
his balance.

"Customers!" he muttered as Aksinia helped him remove his coat.
"What are they laughing at? Is my trade less honourable
than theirs? A shoemaker puts soles on the living; I
provide decent covering for the dead. The tailor cheats men
who can complain. My people say nothing."

"Then take their example, master," replied Aksinia. "Say nothing and
go to bed."

"I intended to invite those Germans to my housewarming," Adrian
continued, ignoring her. "I would have fed them, given them
wine, let them dance under my roof. But no! I
shall invite my own customers. Let the dead come tomorrow
evening—all whom I have served. They will know how to
behave."

"Cross yourself!" said the servant. "The saints preserve us from
such guests."

Adrian turned toward the dark coffins along the wall and
lifted one hand with drunken solemnity.

"My benefactors, orthodox and otherwise! You have enriched me; some
of you paid promptly, some through your heirs, and some
not at all. Do me the favour to attend my
housewarming tomorrow night. The invitation is sincere."

Having made this speech, he stumbled into his room, fell
upon the bed without undressing, and immediately began to snore.

Before dawn a violent knocking shook the courtyard gate.

Adrian awoke with a bitter mouth, a heavy skull, and
no recollection of hospitality toward the deceased. Aksinia entered carrying
a candle.

"A messenger from Razgulyay," she said. "Agrafena Trukhina died in
the night."

Adrian sat up at once. Business has restored many men
more efficiently than cold water. He dressed in haste, thrust
measuring tape, pencil, and notebook into his pocket, and followed
the messenger to a waiting cart.

The deceased lay beneath the sacred images in a room
already crowded with relatives, servants, neighbours, and those indefatigable female
acquaintances who appear at every death before the doctor's carriage
has left the street. Agrafena Trukhina's waxen face was bound
in a white kerchief; her sunken mouth, once celebrated for
bargaining, had been composed into an expression of severe economy.

The nephew received Adrian coldly.

"We require everything proper," he said, "but without extravagance."

This phrase, which has ruined more artists than criticism, did
not disconcert the coffin-maker. He displayed samples, calculated wood, velvet,
fringe, candles, bearers, horses, and refreshments for the clergy. The
nephew rejected oak as ostentatious, pine as shameful, and finally
selected birch painted to resemble oak. He demanded gilt nails
but silvered handles, six horses but only four hired mourners,
and a velvet pall which might afterward be returned if
it had not rained.

Adrian argued, yielded, added, subtracted, sighed, and wrote. Tradesmen gathered
like ravens around the courtyard. The priest's clerk disputed with
the caterer; seamstresses measured the dead woman for her final
dress; a carpenter hurried away with dimensions; and the nephew
whispered to Adrian that the family expected a discount in
consideration of old connections.

By evening all was arranged. Adrian had eaten nothing but
a roll and had drunk several glasses offered at various
stages of negotiation. Weariness, wine, and satisfaction softened his resentment.
The order was profitable. The fashionable undertaker by the river
had not been summoned. Providence, he reflected, sometimes rewards patience.

It was already night when he returned to Nikitsky Lane.
Clouds hurried across the moon, and the autumn wind drove
scraps of paper along the pavement. Schultz's house was dark.
Adrian smiled bitterly at the shoemaker's door, then noticed that
his own gate stood open.

He distinctly remembered telling Aksinia to bolt it.

Within the yard a figure entered ahead of him. It
wore a broad hat and a long cloak and moved
stiffly, as though its knees objected to the journey.

"Who is there?" Adrian called.

The figure turned. Beneath the hat he saw a yellow
face, closed eyes, and lips drawn tightly over the gums.
Then it passed through the doorway.

Adrian's skin grew cold. He wished to run, but professional
familiarity struggled with fear. The face seemed known to him.
He followed.

Light shone beneath the door of the principal room. From
within came a murmur like dry leaves stirred in a
churchyard. Adrian crossed himself and entered.

His house was full of dead people.

They stood between the coffins, sat beneath the sacred images,
and crowded around the green table. The room appeared larger
than before, though this may be explained by terror, which
is an excellent architect. Moonlight and candle flame revealed faces
green, blue, yellow, and blackened by decay; collapsed noses; closed
lids; exposed teeth; hands folded with the stubborn decorum taught
them at burial.

Adrian recognized them all.

There was retired Major Kurilkin in his faded uniform, though
mildew had conquered one epaulette. Beside him stood the widow
Potapova in a lace cap, the ribbon of which hung
from one ear. A young clerk wore the holiday caftan
his mother had purchased for the funeral, now rotten across
the shoulders. Two children sat together upon the small white
coffin, swinging feet that did not reach the floor. An
old deacon breathed dust through his beard. Several customers of
earlier years had been reduced by time to bones, but
retained scraps of shawl, boot, medal, or nightcap by which
a conscientious tradesman might identify them.

As Adrian entered, the company turned toward him.

"Our host!" said Major Kurilkin in a voice that seemed
to issue from an empty cupboard. "At last."

"We accepted your invitation," whispered the widow Potapova.

"You called us benefactors," said the clerk.

"You begged us to do you the favour," added the
deacon, shedding a little earth upon the carpet.

Adrian backed against the door.

"My respected customers," he stammered, "I am honoured beyond measure.
Yet there has perhaps been a misunderstanding. The housewarming is
not prepared. There is no supper."

"We do not eat," replied the major.

"There is no wine."

"We do not drink."

This assurance, though economical, failed to comfort him.

The dead drew nearer with ceremonious bows. Coffin lids creaked
in the passage without being touched. From the yard came
a continual shuffling and knocking, for more guests were arriving:
those buried far away, those whose families had removed from
Moscow, paupers supplied at municipal expense, merchants interred with pomp,
soldiers buried cheaply, and infants carried beneath white veils. They
entered according to no rank except the date of their
burial.

One small skeleton, badly dressed in the remains of a
green caftan, pushed through the company. Its skull was bare,
one jaw hung lower than the other, and its boots
rattled upon the floor like pestles in mortars.

"Do you remember me, Adrian Prokhoroff?" it asked.

Adrian looked and could not answer.

"I am Pyotr Petrovich Kurochkin, formerly sergeant of the fire
brigade. Buried in the year of the great frost."

"Ah," said Adrian faintly. "Yes. An honourable funeral."

"Honourable?"

The skeleton spread its arms. Shreds of sleeve floated from
the bones.

"My sister paid you for an oak coffin. You supplied
pine, stained brown. Here is the proof."

It struck its ribs, and from within came a thin
wooden knocking, as if someone were tapping upon a cheap
box.

Adrian gathered what dignity remained to him.

"Pyotr Petrovich, prices were unusually high that winter. Oak was
scarce. Your sister insisted upon immediate delivery. Moreover, pine is
warmer in appearance."

"Swindler!" cried the skeleton.

The dead repeated the word in voices high, low, hollow,
and toothless.

"Swindler! Swindler!"

"It was a misunderstanding in the account," said Adrian. "I
am prepared to allow a reduction on your next order."

Even the dead were silent at this proposal.

The skeleton advanced, perhaps intending an embrace, perhaps something less
friendly. Adrian recoiled and struck it with both hands. Pyotr
Petrovich flew apart upon the floor. His skull rolled under
the table; one boot landed among the crockery; the ribs
collapsed with the noise of firewood.

A dreadful agitation seized the assembly.

"He has insulted his guest!"

"He has murdered a dead man!"

"He breaks what he buried!"

The major raised a mouldering fist. Widow Potapova opened her
sunken mouth to an unnatural width. The children slipped from
the coffin. Bone fingers caught Adrian's sleeves; rotten cloth brushed
his face; grave-cold breath surrounded him. Behind the crowd, the
skull of Pyotr Petrovich laughed beneath the table.

Adrian cried out, stumbled over the scattered skeleton, and fell.
The room, the candles, the dead, and the gilded coffin
upon his sign revolved together and vanished.

Sunlight lay across the bed.

Aksinia stood beside him with the kettle.

Adrian stared at her. His clothes hung upon the chair.
The room was quiet. From the principal room came the
voices of his daughters arguing over a comb.

"Where are they?" he asked.

"Who, master?"

"The dead."

Aksinia set down the kettle and examined him suspiciously.

"And Agrafena Trukhina? The funeral? The nephew?"

"The shopkeeper's wife? She is alive. Her maid passed an
hour ago and said the old lady had asked for
broth."

Adrian sat up.

"But yesterday—the messenger before dawn?"

"No messenger came. You returned from the German's feast, scolded
the whole world, invited dead people to supper, and slept
until now. A pretty housewarming you arranged!"

Adrian looked toward the door. No earth marked the carpet;
no skull lay beneath the table; the crockery remained whole.
From across the lane came the cheerful hammering of Schultz
at his workbench. Morning bells sounded over the roofs.

The coffin-maker drew a long breath and crossed himself.

"So it was a dream."

"What else could it be?"

He considered this, then smiled—a phenomenon so uncommon upon his
face that Aksinia nearly dropped the kettle.

"Thank God," he said. "Make the tea strong. And call
Akulina and Daria. We shall have breakfast together."

"And shall I begin preparations for the shopkeeper's wife?"

"No, no. Let the good woman drink her broth."

Aksinia went out, muttering that punch was a German invention
against Christian sleep. Adrian remained by the window, watching the
wet lane brighten in the autumn sun. Opposite, Schultz caught
sight of him, waved a boot, and shouted something friendly
through the glass.

Adrian hesitated, then waved back.

Whether he afterward used oak whenever oak had been paid
for cannot be stated with certainty. Tradesmen, like other men,
are improved by nightmares less often than moralists suppose. But
that morning he drank three cups of tea, listened patiently
to his daughters, and did not once inquire whether Agrafena
Trukhina had taken a turn for the worse.

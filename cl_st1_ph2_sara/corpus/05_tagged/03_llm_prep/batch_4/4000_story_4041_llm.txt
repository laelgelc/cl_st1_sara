### Regulus

In the days when Rome had subdued nearly all Italy,
but had not yet become mistress of the world, there
stood upon the opposite shore of the Mediterranean the great
city of Carthage. She was a daughter of ancient Tyre,
and her ships carried the purple colours of commerce into
every western sea; her merchants were rich, her harbours crowded,
and her walls magnificent. Yet beneath this outward splendor there
remained much of the wicked religion of Zidon. Baal and
Ashtoreth were worshipped with foul rites, and children, as it
was said, were sometimes given to the fire to propitiate
Moloch. The Carthaginians were subtle, wealthy, and enterprising, but cruel
to those beneath them and unfaithful to those whom they
feared; while the Romans were plain, hardy, fierce, and pitiless,
living by strict law and holding courage and obedience in
greater honour than riches.

Between two such nations peace could not long endure. Their
quarrel began concerning Sicily, that fruitful island which lies, like
a stepping-stone, between Italy and Africa; and thus commenced the
First Punic War. At first the Romans, who were accustomed
to fight upon land, knew little of the sea; but
they copied a stranded Carthaginian vessel, built a fleet, and
taught their soldiers to make a sea-fight resemble a battle
on shore. After many changes of fortune, they resolved to
carry the war into Africa, and Marcus Atilius Regulus, one
of their consuls, was appointed to command the invasion.

Regulus was not a rich man. Though he held the
highest office of the Republic, his estate consisted, according to
the old account, of no more than seven acres, and
was cultivated by one steward. When news reached him that
this servant had died, and that another labourer had stolen
the implements of husbandry, Regulus asked permission to return home,
fearing that his wife and children might lack bread. The
Senate, however, undertook to maintain them and to cultivate the
little farm at the public expense. Such poverty was then
no shame at Rome; and the ruler of armies, who
could command many thousands of men, did not consider it
beneath him to care anxiously for a plough and a
few furrows of corn.

He crossed into Africa and at first prospered greatly. Town
after town submitted; the fields were laid waste; multitudes of
cattle and captives were carried away; and the Carthaginians, shut
within their walls, began to tremble. During the march there
appeared, as some historians relate, an enormous serpent, one hundred
and twenty feet long, which kept the army from a
river and destroyed several soldiers with its breath and the
strokes of its tail. Regulus at length caused engines to
be brought against it, as though he were besieging a
fortress, and the monster was slain. Its skin was afterwards
said to have been shown at Rome. We need not
believe every marvel reported by ancient writers; yet the tale
shows what terrors men supposed the Roman army able to
overcome.

More dangerous than serpents was the pride which enters the
heart after prosperity. The Carthaginians, dismayed by their losses, sent
envoys to Regulus and desired peace; but he demanded that
they should surrender their ships, abandon Sicily, pay the expenses
of the war, and submit to conditions scarcely differing from
servitude. These terms were so harsh that the envoys answered
they would rather perish with arms in their hands. Regulus
had forgotten that victory is uncertain, and that mercy in
the hour of power is both noble and prudent. He
was severe toward others, as he afterwards proved himself severe
toward his own body and affections; but justice must condemn
his arrogance, even while constancy compels us to honour him.

At this extremity the Carthaginians obtained the aid of Xanthippus,
a Spartan soldier who understood war better than their own
generals. He observed that they possessed excellent cavalry and many
elephants, yet had allowed themselves to be defeated among hills,
where these advantages could scarcely be used. He led the
army out upon an open plain, placed the elephants in
front, the cavalry upon the wings, and the heavy troops
behind. Regulus advanced with confidence; but the huge beasts broke
the Roman ranks, the Numidian horse surrounded them, and the
Carthaginian infantry pressed forward. The legionaries fought with their accustomed
fierceness, but they were trodden down, pierced with darts, or
cut off while attempting to escape. Of the great Roman
host, only a small number reached safety. Regulus himself was
taken alive.

The fallen consul remained a prisoner for about two years.
How he was treated during that time is not certainly
known. The heathen nations could be savage to captives, and
the Carthaginians had little cause to love the man who
had refused them mercy; yet later stories may have increased
his sufferings in order to make his end more terrible.
At length, being weary of the war and desirous of
recovering their own citizens who were held at Rome, they
determined to send an embassy proposing peace, or at least
an exchange of prisoners. Regulus was commanded to accompany the
envoys and to persuade the Senate to consent.

Before he departed, the Carthaginians required him to swear that,
if the negotiation failed, he would return to captivity. They
doubtless believed that no man, once restored to his country,
his wife, his children, and the free air of Rome,
would voluntarily go back to enemies who might put him
to death. Regulus gave the promise. It must be remembered
that he was not instructed by that perfect revelation which
teaches us both the sanctity of truth and the vanity
of heathen oaths; nevertheless, according to the light granted him,
he believed that his pledged word stood under the eye
of divine power. In this he ignorantly approached a truth
greater than Rome, for the true God has in every
age loved faithfulness and hated deceit.

When the embassy drew near the city, the people poured
out to welcome their former consul. Marcia, his wife, came
with his children, longing after two years of sorrow to
embrace him. But Regulus stood apart. He wore the garments
of a Carthaginian captive, and declared that, being the slave
of another state, he could not enter Rome as a
free citizen, nor receive the honours due to a Roman
magistrate. He would not at first accept the caresses of
his family, lest affection should weaken the purpose he had
formed. It was a stern restraint, perhaps sterner than Christian
tenderness would approve; yet there was something wonderful in the
spectacle of that sick and wasted man, refusing even comfort
because he feared comfort might tempt him from duty.

The conscript fathers assembled in the Senate-house and invited Regulus
to sit among them. He refused, saying that captivity had
deprived him of senatorial rank, and he remained standing while
the Carthaginian envoys stated their proposals. They urged that peace
should be made, or that prisoners should be exchanged man
for man. Many senators were moved by pity; the people
desired to save their countryman; and the family of Regulus
hoped that the years of mourning were ended.

Then Regulus asked to speak. He was commanded by his
captors to recommend the exchange, but he had determined that
Rome should not be injured for his sake.

"Conscript fathers," he said, "continue the war. Carthage is exhausted,
though she conceals her weakness; Rome grows stronger through endurance.
The prisoners whom you hold are young and able commanders,
while I am old, broken by confinement, and no longer
useful either in council or in battle. Do not restore
brave enemies to obtain one worn-out man. Let not the
safety of the Republic be purchased by dishonour."

He may also have told them, as tradition relates, that
poison had been given him and that he could not
live long. Whether this was true we cannot know; but
his chief argument needed no such aid. He judged that
the exchange would strengthen Carthage and weaken Rome, and therefore
opposed it, although its rejection closed the door of his
prison upon himself.

The senators were astonished, and some endeavoured to persuade him
that an oath forced from a captive was not binding.
The priests, it was said, offered to release him from
it. His friends pleaded, his children wept, and Marcia entreated
him not to abandon them. But Regulus remained unmoved.

"Shall I return to Carthage?" they asked him.

"I shall return," he answered. "I know what may await
me; but pain is brief, while guilt is lasting. Rome
must not learn from one of her consuls that a
promise may be broken when keeping it becomes dangerous."

Here was no loud boasting, nor any vain delight in
suffering. He wished to live; and because life was dear,
the surrender of it was great. A man who throws
away what he does not value makes no sacrifice. Regulus
loved his home and children, but he loved honour more;
and though his idea of honour was mingled with the
severe errors of pagan Rome, he understood that neither fear
nor affection can turn falsehood into truth.

The Senate refused both peace and the exchange. The Carthaginian
envoys departed, and Regulus prepared to accompany them. His friends
tried once more to detain him, for no Roman force
could have compelled him to go; but he replied that
Rome must not protect him in perjury. He passed from
the city, looking, perhaps, for the last time upon its
temples, roofs, and walls, and went toward the ship as
calmly as if he had been returning to his seven-acre
farm. The crowd followed in silence. Marcia and the children
could only watch until the stern figure of the captive
was lost from sight.

He reached Carthage and died soon afterwards. The manner of
his death is uncertain. A dreadful story declared that his
eyelids were cut away, that he was exposed to the
burning sun, and that he was then placed in a
chest pierced with inward-pointing nails. Other accounts related different tortures;
but these narratives were written long after the event and
may have arisen from hatred between the two peoples. It
is quite possible that Regulus died from disease and the
hardships of imprisonment. His virtue requires no invented torment to
make it glorious. The noblest part of his story had
already been completed when, being free to remain, he stepped
aboard the vessel and returned because he had promised.

Marcia, however, believed the worst accounts. In the fury of
grief she caused certain noble Carthaginian prisoners, who had been
delivered into her family's custody, to be confined and cruelly
treated, hoping to repay suffering with suffering. This was not
constancy, but revenge; and it shows how even love, when
it refuses the government of justice, may become wicked. The
Senate at last interfered and rescued the captives. Regulus had
sacrificed himself for Rome; Marcia, in seeking to honour his
memory by cruelty, disgraced it. Evil does not become good
because it is committed for one whom we love.

Thus ended the life of Marcus Atilius Regulus. He had
been proud in victory and merciless in his demands; he
belonged to a fierce people, worshipped under the shadows of
heathen error, and knew nothing of that Saviour who afterwards
taught men to forgive their enemies. We must not call
all his actions righteous. Yet when safety, home, and life
were set against public duty and his pledged word, he
chose duty and truth. It may thus be hoped that
the God who gave him courage regarded with mercy the
imperfect servant who, amid much darkness, followed the light he
possessed.

Empires pass away, and Carthage herself was afterwards burned, while
the victorious Rome of Regulus became in turn corrupt and
enslaved. But fidelity does not grow old with ruined cities.
The warrior's severest conquest was not over Africans, elephants, or
the fabled serpent; it was over his own fear and
longing. Therefore his name has endured, teaching that honour is
greater than comfort, that an oath is not dissolved by
danger, and that he who preserves his truth, though he
lose his life, may leave behind him a possession which
neither prison, torture, nor death can take away.

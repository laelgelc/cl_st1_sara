For three years after Jack Gisburn had ceased to paint,
people continued to discuss his reasons with the solicitude ordinarily
reserved for a royal illness or an unsuitable marriage. The
unsuitable marriage, in his case, had actually occurred; but since
Mrs. Gisburn was rich, good-natured, and free from those obscure
hereditary blemishes which fashion discovers when money is absent, society
could not accept her as a sufficient explanation.

There had to be another.

Mrs. Thwing, who collected artists with the same grave avidity
that she bestowed on famille-verte monsters and little silver reliquaries
of doubtful sanctity, declared that Jack had "seen through Arrt."
She prolonged the sacred monosyllable until it became a corridor
down which one expected Truth to advance in sandals. Young
Dace, the critic, held that Gisburn's silence was his final
masterpiece; and old Lady Babberley, who had been painted by
him in a Byzantine robe concealing most of her figure
and none of her pretensions, said that success had exhausted
his delicacy.

The simpler version was that he had married money and
no longer needed to work. But simplicity, in such matters,
is merely what remains after interest has gone out of
the subject; and Gisburn had been too interesting, and his
withdrawal too abrupt, for us to surrender him so cheaply.

I confess that my own curiosity was of the idlest.
I had never rated Jack's painting very high, though I
had often rated his conversation above it; and there seemed
a peculiar fitness in the fact that a man who
had painted so many people who did nothing should at
last join their ranks. Yet when, during a winter on
the Riviera, I learned that the Gisburns were established not
twenty miles from my hotel, I felt the old question
stir in me. The excuse of proximity is one of
the most respectable forms of indiscretion, and I wrote to
announce myself.

Their motor met me at the station.

The villa stood above the sea in a garden composed
with the costly naturalness of a stage woodland. Terraces of
clipped ilex descended toward a blue so polished that it
seemed an adjunct of the decoration; terracotta nymphs, agreeably discolored,
watched among the cypresses; and the house, an ochre-colored mass
with green shutters and a loggia full of marble fragments,
had been restored to that state of consummate antiquity which
only recent money can produce.

Mrs. Gisburn received me at luncheon.

I had seen her before her marriage, but had retained
no image of her; and after ten minutes in her
company I understood why. She was one of those women
whose absence of emphasis becomes, under favorable conditions, almost a
distinction. Her face was neither young nor old, her voice
neither low nor loud, her opinions neither her own nor
anyone else's. She wore gray, liked the sunshine, and thought
the view "too lovely." What made her faintly remarkable was
the completeness with which she had escaped being interesting.

This, I reflected, might explain more than Mrs. Thwing's theory.

The women who had helped to make Gisburn famous had
all been intensely interesting. They had leaned over his easel,
discovered moral crises in his underpainting, and told him that
his whites were "spiritual." They had put their souls into
his hands with the reservation that he was to make
the souls becoming. He had repaid them generously. In his
portraits their cheekbones acquired significance, their shoulders history, and the
fabric of their gowns that half-human vitality which painters of
his sort reserve for satin and fur. One came away
persuaded that Lady Babberley's sleeve had survived dynasties and that
Mrs. Thwing's pearls had suffered.

Mrs. Gisburn would have supplied no such stimulant. She had
not enough vanity to be inspirational; she merely possessed the
money to be useful.

Jack came in late, handsome, cordial, and apparently untouched by
his apostasy. He had always had the quality of looking
cleverer than he was, partly because his eyes were quick
and his forehead spacious, and partly because he knew how
long to pause before an answer. Time and leisure had
softened him without coarsening him. His gray clothes fitted him
with the negligent exactitude attainable only through repeated consultations, and
his manner suggested that he had just returned from some
agreeable exertion which others were not equipped to share.

"My dear Rickham! So you've come to inspect the ruins?"

"You don't look ruined."

"No? Wait till you've seen my golf."

Mrs. Gisburn explained that Jack's golf was "quite wonderful," though
he never took it seriously; and Jack, smiling at her,
observed that taking things seriously was the most expensive amusement
of middle age.

He was at ease—too much at ease, I thought. His
old animation remained, but had lost its object, like the
gesture of a man continuing to conduct after the orchestra
has stopped. He spoke of pictures, books, politics and people
with a fluent impartiality. Nothing appeared to touch him, and
yet he touched everything expertly. I had the impression of
dining off admirable silver with no food on the plate.

After luncheon Mrs. Gisburn offered to show me the house.

It contained what the cultivated rich are expected to contain
when they have submitted to competent advice: a Sèvres service
too precious for coffee, two Renaissance bronzes of questionable parentage,
a row of pale Italian cabinets, brocades retired from ecclesiastical
duty, and here and there some deliberately barbarous object inserted
to prove that taste had not become timid. Everything was
rare, appropriate, and slightly exhausted by appreciation.

Jack followed us amiably, supplying dates and schools where necessary.
He had arranged the rooms himself; his wife made this
clear with the pride of one who has hired genius
for domestic use.

"But of course Jack won't let me hang any of
his own things," she complained, as we entered the long
drawing-room.

I looked about. The walls bore a Luini pupil, a
guarded little landscape ascribed to Corot, and three eighteenth-century portraits
whose sitters had had the wisdom to die before their
attribution became uncertain. Not a Gisburn was visible.

"You have renounced yourself very thoroughly," I said.

He gave me a quick look.

"My former prices made me cautious. I couldn't afford myself
now."

Mrs. Gisburn laughed with literal pleasure. "Isn't he absurd? We've
got my portrait upstairs—the one he did before we were
married—but he won't have it down here. I tell him
people would like to see it."

"I painted it for private devotion," said Jack.

There was nothing in the words; there was perhaps too
much in the lightness with which he spoke them. I
remembered the portrait. It had been one of his triumphs:
Mrs. Gisburn, then Mrs. Clenshaw, in dove-colored velvet, turning from
a dark background with one hand on a lacquer cabinet
and the other holding a spray of white orchids. The
pose implied that she had been interrupted in an act
of great inward significance. I recalled the gleam on the
velvet, the modulated pallor of the face, the orchids poised
like captured moths. It was excellent Gisburn—bold, polished, and empty
enough to receive any compliment.

We passed through the remaining rooms, and presently Mrs. Gisburn
remembered some domestic engagement. She entrusted me to Jack, with
the suggestion that he should show me "his own place."

He led me down a passage and through a small
courtyard into a room built against the northern side of
the villa. It was plain to austerity. Dark books lined
the walls; the furniture was leathery and square; the floor
had no carpet; and the single long window admitted a
cold, even light which made the Mediterranean brilliance outside seem
theatrical. After the house's succession of brocade, porcelain and agreeable
archaeology, the room had the effect of a cell contrived
by a connoisseur.

"So this is where you hide from your treasures," I
said.

"From my wife's treasures. Mine are here."

I looked about. There were few pictures: an old Spanish
panel, blackened almost to invisibility; two Japanese drawings; a small
study of rocks; and, above the writing-table, a sheet in
a narrow walnut frame.

I went nearer.

It was the sketch of a donkey.

At first I saw merely a dozen nervous lines on
yellowish paper: a drooping head, the ridge of a patient
back, four legs planted with the stubborn delicacy peculiar to
the animal. Then the thing took possession. The donkey was
tired; but its fatigue had no anecdote in it, no
appeal. It stood. The weight of the body passed down
through the thin legs into the earth; the head hung
because the neck must sustain it so; the whole creature,
awkward, enduring and complete, appeared to have been known from
within. Nothing had been added for charm, and nothing necessary
had been omitted. The lines seemed not drawn upon the
paper but exposed there, as cracks reveal the structure of
stone.

"Stroud," I said.

Jack had remained behind me.

"Yes."

"I've never seen that."

"No one has, practically."

The name itself had become one of those minor reproaches
which survive the men who earned them. Felix Stroud had
painted for thirty years, exhibited intermittently, sold almost nothing, and
died with a reputation confined to six artists, two critics
and several pupils who imitated his austerity without acquiring his
knowledge. Whenever the public neglected a serious painter, Stroud's name
was brought forward to prove that neglect might be a
sign of greatness; but the people who used him thus
seldom owned, or even looked at, his work.

"Where did you get it?"

"From his widow."

"Ah."

I turned again to the donkey. Behind me Jack moved
toward the writing-table, shifted a box of cigarettes, and shifted
it back.

"She gave it to me," he said, "after he died."

There was a pause.

"You knew him well?"

"No. That is, I knew him in the way successful
men know unsuccessful ones. I called him 'dear old Stroud'
and avoided his exhibitions."

The phrase, uttered without a smile, altered the air between
us. I sat down.

Jack stood by the window, looking into the courtyard, where
a stone basin held the reflection of a lemon tree.

"You came here to find out why I stopped painting,"
he said.

I made the conventional protest.

"Oh, not consciously perhaps. But the question traveled with you.
I know; it arrives with everyone. Some people put it
down beside their teacup, others carry it through the house
and leave it in the guest-room. You've at least had
the decency to wait."

"I supposed you had tired of it."

"So did I."

He turned from the window and leaned against the sill.

"I had reasons enough, all the little plausible reasons one
manufactures after the event. I was overworked, I had repeated
myself, portrait-painting had become commercial, marriage changed my habits, money
destroyed the useful stimulus of necessity. Every explanation was true,
and none had anything to do with it."

His eyes moved to the sketch.

"It was that."

"The Stroud?"

"That donkey—and his face."

He sat opposite me, bent forward, and clasped his hands.
The gesture was unusual in him; it lacked arrangement.

"You remember when Stroud died? No, why should you? The
papers gave him four lines between a landscape-gardener and a
baronet's second wife. I was in the middle of my
best season. There were six portraits in the studio, and
people were quarreling for sittings as though I were a
dentist during an epidemic. I had just finished Lady Ulver's
boy in riding-clothes—the one everyone admired because I made the
pony look more intelligent than the child."

"I remember."

"Of course. Reproductions everywhere. Well, a week after Stroud's death
his widow came to see me.

"I had never met her. She was a little, worn
woman in black, with the look of someone who has
spent her life trying not to interrupt. She sat on
the edge of the great gilt chair the decorators had
chosen for my distinguished clients, and apologized for taking my
time. Then she asked me to paint Stroud's portrait.

"I thought she meant from photographs—a commemorative thing. I began
to explain that I was overwhelmed, that posthumous portraiture required
a special sympathy, that my method depended so much on
the stimulus of the living sitter—you know the phrases. I
had used them often; they fitted any refusal.

"But she said his body was still in the house."

Jack stopped, then went on more slowly.

"She had delayed the funeral. It seems Stroud had always
hated being photographed. There were snapshots, blurred little things taken
in gardens, but no real record of him. She wanted
a portrait before he was buried.

"I was shocked—less by the request, I'm afraid, than by
its inconvenience. Yet she had a curious persistence. Not force;
force would have been easier to resist. She merely sat
there with her shabby gloves folded, explaining that Felix had
admired my portraits.

"That was the first blow."

"Stroud admired them?"

"So she said. God knows what he had really said.
Something kind, probably—he was lavish with other men because he
was so severe with himself. She had translated it into
faith. To her I was success, authority, the visible crown
which the world had denied him. She thought that if
I painted him, society would at last understand what it
had missed.

"There was the irony: she wanted to borrow my reputation
to vindicate his genius.

"I accepted."

"Out of kindness?"

He gave a small laugh.

"Partly. Vanity is never so comfortable as when it can
put on kindness. Her request frightened and flattered me. I
saw the noble paragraph: 'Mr. Jack Gisburn has consented, despite
the pressure of important commissions, to paint the late Felix
Stroud.' I imagined the portrait in the Academy, the black
frame, the little hush. People would say that only an
artist could have painted an artist. I was always strongest
at anticipating what people would say."

He rose and walked once across the room.

"I went the next morning. They lived in a narrow
house beyond Fulham—brown curtains, bad gas, casts under dust, canvases
turned to the wall. Poverty without picturesqueness. Mrs. Stroud took
me upstairs.

"He lay in a small room overlooking roofs. Someone had
put a white cloth over the table and drawn back
the curtains. His face was already sharpened by death, but
not altered into peace. You know the conventional dignity we
give the dead? Stroud hadn't taken it. He looked as
he had lived: concentrated, impatient, almost inconvenienced by what had
happened.

"I set up my easel.

"At first the machinery worked. It always did. I placed
the head, found the dominant planes, laid in the shadows.
My hand was never uncertain. That was the trouble: it
knew too quickly what to do. Before an hour had
passed I had something recognizable, vigorous, effective. Mrs. Stroud came
in, stood behind me, and began to cry.

"She thought it wonderful.

"And because she thought it wonderful, I suddenly suspected it."

Jack's voice had fallen. The sea, invisible from the room,
made a faint recurrent pressure in the silence.

"I returned the next day. The light was better. I
worked for two hours, and the portrait grew more accomplished.
The forehead came forward; the cheek sank; the mouth acquired
that compressed intensity everyone associated with him. I even caught—or
thought I caught—the peculiar angle at which he had carried
his head.

"It was all there. That is, everything was there except
Stroud.

"I blamed the circumstances. A dead face is a mask;
the animation that interprets structure has gone. I told myself
no painter could succeed under such conditions. Then, while waiting
for the light to change, I wandered into the next
room.

"It had been his studio.

"There was almost nothing in it. The large canvases had
been taken away or turned; his brushes stood in a
cracked jar; the stove had gone out. On a table
near the window lay a portfolio, and on top of
it was that drawing."

He pointed to the donkey.

"I looked at it casually. I had seen Strouds before,
and had given them my respectful attention—the kind one gives
work which lacks the courtesy to amuse. But that morning
I saw the drawing.

"Really saw it.

"It was as if some pressure had been removed from
my eyes. I understood that every line had been earned.
There was no flourish, no seduction, no effort to make
poverty look aristocratic or ugliness significant. He had known the
beast—its weight, its balance, the articulation under the hide, the
dumb persistence of its life—and because he knew, he could
omit. Those dozen lines were the end of a journey.
My finished portraits were notices announcing journeys I had never
begun."

He looked at me with a kind of defiance, as
though even now the confession exposed him to ridicule.

"You may say a donkey is easier than a human
face."

"I wasn't going to."

"No; but I said it. I said everything. I told
myself Stroud was narrow, dry, unresponsive to color; that he
lacked charm, amplitude, social imagination. Social imagination! I had invented
the phrase to describe my faculty for painting people as
they wished to be known at dinner.

"I went back to the bedroom and looked at him.

"That was when his face changed. Not literally. But I
saw that he had spent his life building downward, stone
under stone, where no one applauded; while I had been
hanging balconies in the air. His smallest line rested on
knowledge. My largest effects rested on agreement—my agreement with the
sitter, the critics, the spectators, with everyone except the thing
before me.

"I tried to go on.

"My hand performed its tricks: the sudden loaded stroke, the
dragged light, the dark accent that made the neighboring flesh
vibrate. Bravura—prestidigitation. I knew exactly where the audience would murmur.
But now each stroke exposed me. I was like a
circus strongman groaning over a feather: all the gesture proclaimed
the absence of weight.

"I scraped the face out."

"What did Mrs. Stroud say?"

"She begged me not to. That was the worst of
it. She had liked the portrait. She said it was
Felix—more like him than anything she had ever seen. And
perhaps it was like the Felix she had been able
to see. But I had begun to see another.

"I started again. For three days I started again. Each
time I reduced the performance: fewer effects, quieter color, stricter
drawing. I thought honesty could be reached by taking things
away. But bareness is not truth. A fool's whisper is
no wiser than his shout.

"On the fourth day they could delay the funeral no
longer.

"I remained alone with the canvas. The face stared out—not
Stroud's face, but my idea of what a dead master
ought to look like. It had severity, pathos, intellectual distinction.
It was admirable. It was false.

"I cut it to pieces."

The short sentence seemed to leave a visible gap behind
it.

"And told Mrs. Stroud?"

"That I had failed. I made some excuse about the
light, the condition of the—of the sitter. She was bewildered,
but she forgave me at once. People like that always
forgive the injury and remember the favor. Then she gave
me the drawing. She said Felix would have wanted me
to have it."

He sat down again.

"I came back to my studio. Lady Ulver's daughter was
waiting in Venetian red, with a wolfhound and an expression
arranged for posterity. The room smelled of flowers. There were
telegrams on the mantelpiece, invitations, a dealer with a client
below. I looked at the six unfinished portraits and saw
the same face on all of them—my own public face,
multiplied and disguised.

"I couldn't touch them."

"But you finished some?"

"Two. Badly. Or rather, as well as usual—which had become
worse. Then I returned the deposits and announced that I
needed rest."

"And Grindle?"

At the name, something between amusement and pain crossed his
expression.

"Ah, Grindle. Mrs. Stroud still wanted the portrait. A few
days later she wrote asking whom she should employ. I
wanted the whole affair removed from me—carried off, finished, forgotten.
So I recommended Victor Grindle."

"Why Grindle?"

"Because he would have no difficulty."

Victor Grindle was then a young man of abundant hair,
ingratiating silences, and a talent for making paint look expensive.
His portraits, which had since invaded dining-rooms on both sides
of the Atlantic, gave every woman the air of having
just refused a throne and every financier the melancholy of
a misunderstood Lorenzo de' Medici.

"He painted Stroud from the photographs," Jack continued. "Mrs. Stroud
was delighted. The papers reproduced it. Dace wrote that Grindle
had interpreted 'the spiritual solitude of creative labor.' Within a
year he was charging my prices. Within two, he had
doubled them."

"You made him."

"Yes. My last contribution to art."

There was bitterness in the phrase, but not the bitterness
I had expected. It was colder, less personal: the bitterness
of a man who has discovered that the mechanism he
despises does not require his participation.

I looked again at the donkey.

"And since then—never?"

"Never."

"You might have begun again differently."

"I tried to believe that. For months I drew in
secret. Cubes, hands, old men in cafés, trees without leaves—the
penitential exercises of the exposed virtuoso. I imagined I could
crawl back to foundations and build honestly. But one cannot
recover in middle age the patience one has spent on
applause. I knew too many effects and too few causes.
Even my humility had a manner."

"But surely recognition counts for something."

"It counts for stopping."

He rose and stood beneath the drawing. The cold light,
flattening the elegance of his clothes and face, showed for
a moment the fatigue beneath his cultivated ease.

"That is all I have been able to do. Stroud
knew where he was going, though no one followed him.
I had a crowd behind me and no destination. When
I saw it, I stepped out of the road. Don't
mistake me: I don't pretend the act was heroic. Clara's
money made renunciation comfortable. It is easier to sacrifice a
career when one can do it from a villa with
good cooking."

He smiled, but his eyes did not alter.

"Still, I stopped."

Mrs. Gisburn's voice sounded across the courtyard, calling to ask
whether we wanted tea. At once the room seemed to
resume its place in the excellent machinery of the house;
the donkey diminished to a framed drawing, Jack to a
prosperous idler, and I to a guest who had stayed
indoors too long on a fine afternoon.

As we returned through the passage, I asked: "Does your
wife know?"

"That I gave up because I was a fraud? My
dear Rickham, Clara thinks I had too much talent for
the profession. She considers painting bad for the nerves."

In the drawing-room tea waited on a low table: thin
porcelain, heavy silver, cakes of studied insignificance. Mrs. Gisburn greeted
us with the news that the light on the sea
had become "perfectly artistic," then apologized to Jack for the
word.

He kissed her forehead.

I understood then, with a correction of my earlier malice,
why he had married her. She offered him the one
form of admiration he could endure: admiration without discrimination. The
interesting women had praised the painter and consumed the man;
Clara, incapable of distinguishing between his talent and his golf,
restored him to the comfortable indistinctness of being loved.

Before I left, she insisted that I see her portrait.

It hung in an upstairs sitting-room above a Dubarry commode.
Time had done nothing to it except to deepen the
varnish and alter the fashion of the dress. The velvet
still flowed, the orchids still hovered, and Mrs. Gisburn still
turned from the darkness with that air of having been
surprised at the center of a destiny.

"It is like me, isn't it?" she asked.

Jack stood in the doorway.

I looked from the painted face to the placid woman
beside me. "It is magnificent," I said.

Her pleasure absolved the evasion. Jack's did not.

At the station, while we waited for my train, I
saw on the bookstall the latest illustrated weekly. Its cover
reproduced a new portrait by Grindle: a steel magnate standing
before a stormy window, one hand on a globe, as
though the weather and the planet were subsidiaries awaiting his
decision. The brushwork had the familiar dash; a highlight flamed
on the cuff; the head emerged from its expensive gloom
with precisely the force required for reproduction.

Jack followed my glance.

"Good, isn't it?" he said.

The train came in before I could answer. As I
climbed into my carriage, he raised his hat with the
old graceful gesture—the gesture I had seen reproduced in a
hundred painted hands, corrected, ennobled, made memorable at so much
a sitting.

Then he turned back toward the motor and the villa
above the sea.

I carried away with me two images: Grindle's magnate, large,
accomplished and empty, traveling outward through the world on ten
thousand glossy covers; and, in the dark plain room, Stroud's
little donkey standing on its four tired legs.

One would be seen everywhere.

The other stood.

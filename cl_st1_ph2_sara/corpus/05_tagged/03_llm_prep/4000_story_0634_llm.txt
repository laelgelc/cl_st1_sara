**THE MYSTERY OF DR. BARRITZ**

**Hotel Miramar, June 3**

My Dear Irene:

You will remember Jack Raynor—the lank red-headed boy who used
to fasten tin cans to the Syndicate Mill mule and
then look pained when the animal expressed itself? He is
here. Fancy! He came striding into the music-room yesterday in
clothes which must have been grown on him, for no
tailor could have fitted such shoulders without taking fright, and
he called me "Gun—" before I froze him with a
look.

He changed it to "Miss Dement," but too late. I
had heard the ancient and dishonourable syllable.

You know why they called me Gunny at Redhorse: because
my first ball-gown, at the age of eight, was a
meal-sack with holes cut for my arms and a blue
bow at the neck. Mother said the blue bow made
it dressy. Poor dear! It DID. At least, there was
nothing else in Redhorse to contradict her.

I forgave Jack, because one cannot shoot a childhood friend
in a hotel music-room; but I told him that if
he revived the name again I should have him removed
as mining refuse. He laughed, and then introduced Dr. Barritz.

Irene, have you ever seen a face that made you
instantly ashamed of your own nose?

Dr. Barritz is tall without seeming constructed for that purpose,
dark without looking theatrical, and composed without the least appearance
of having practiced before a mirror. His eyes are gray—or
green—or perhaps that colour which the sea puts on when
it is thinking wickedly. He has lived in Vienna, Benares,
Paris, Cairo, and places which Jack pronounced in a low
voice because no decent atlas acknowledges them. He speaks seven
languages, knows the chemical nature of dreams, and has discovered
something about the human will which he refuses to publish
because society is "not prepared."

Society is never prepared, dear; that is why it wears
stays.

Dr. Barritz bowed over my hand—not kissed it; I should
have died if he had, though perhaps not until afterward—and
said, "Miss Dement and I have met before."

I said, "Surely not."

He answered, "Surely."

That was all! Then Mrs. Brimmer asked him whether the
soul could leave the body, and he replied that some
souls had apparently never entered it. I laughed so suddenly
that my fan struck me on the chin.

Jack watched us with the expression of a man who
has set a fuse.

I shall avoid them both.

Your resolute
**Mary Jane**

---

**June 6**

Irene:

I have NOT avoided him.

You need not sit there smiling like a cat in
a creamery. It is impossible to avoid a man who
is invited everywhere, particularly when Jack sees to the invitations
and Mrs. Brimmer regards him as a species of imported
prophet. The woman now takes her breakfast egg by "magnetic
intuition," which means that she taps all six before choosing
the coldest.

Jack tells the most extraordinary stories.

According to him, Dr. Barritz once remained motionless for three
days in a Himalayan monastery, though why the monks tolerated
it Jack does not explain. He can compel sleep by
looking at a person; he can remember events which happened
before he was born; and he knows the secret thoughts
of anyone whose left hand he touches.

Since hearing this I have kept my left hand behind
me like a stolen article.

Yesterday, on the veranda, he asked whether I had ever
been in California.

"Yes," I said, with what I hoped was negligent elegance.
"As a child."

"In the mountains?"

"For a little while."

"Near Redhorse?"

Irene, the blood rushed into my face with such violence
that I expected it to come out through my hairpins.

"Redhorse?" I repeated. "What a singular name."

He looked straight at me. "I have always thought it
memorable."

Then Jack appeared, the little wretch, and began talking of
chloriding the dumps. Dr. Barritz smiled—not at Jack, but at
ME. What does he know? What does he remember? Can
a man truly read the buried chambers of the mind?
If he touched my left hand, would he see the
cabin with its tar-paper roof, Mother washing for the superintendent's
wife, Father coming home white with mill dust, and me
in the meal-sack pinafore fighting Dumps beside the ore bins?

You remember Dumps. Nobody knew his proper name. He slept
wherever shelter could be persuaded to overlook him, held up
his trousers with one suspender, and divided everything he stole
with me except green apples, which he said disagreed with
girls. When Father struck pay ore and sent me East,
Dumps ran beside the wagon for nearly a mile. Two
years later came the news that road agents had stopped
the stage on which he had found work, and that
the boy had been killed.

I cried for a week. You know I did.

But I will not have Dr. Barritz know I was
Gunny Dement, meal-sack princess of Redhorse. It is not that
I despise those people—if anyone says so, I shall marry
him merely to obtain the legal right to make his
life miserable. I am ashamed only because HE is so
finished, so learned, so mysteriously superior, and I—beneath silk, French
verbs, and a trained manner of entering rooms—am still capable
of eating bacon with my fingers.

He looked at me again this evening. Such a look
ought to be licensed as public entertainment, and I should
have paid at the door.

I am in danger.

**Mary**

---

**June 10**

Dearest:

It is worse.

I love him.

There—I have written it, and the ceiling has not fallen,
though a waiter dropped a tray outside, which was almost
as effective.

How did it happen? By degrees, I suppose, as one
descends into the Valley of Humility—first admiring the view, then
slipping on the gravel, and finally arriving at the bottom
with one's hat over one ear. I began by being
curious about his powers; then I desired his approval; then
I discovered that when he left a room all the
furniture became uninteresting.

He speaks to me very little, but every word remains.
On Monday he said, "The morning is clear." I have
thought about it ever since. Why THAT morning? Clear of
what? Jack says the doctor never wastes speech and that
his simplest remarks contain "secondary significances." Jack deserves secondary strangulation.

Last night we sat in the conservatory behind a palm
which had been rented for the season and was plainly
dissatisfied with society. Dr. Barritz asked whether I believed people
could outlive their former selves.

"Certainly," I said. "That is what civilization is for."

"But if the former self were innocent?"

"One might regret it."

"And if someone loved that self?"

Irene, I could hear my heart beating against my bodice
like a tenant demanding repairs.

I tried to answer lightly. "Then someone had a strange
taste."

He bent nearer. "Taste is sometimes fidelity under another name."

At that instant Mrs. Brimmer came through the palm and
requested him to cure her headache by fixing his gaze
upon the back of her neck. If I live a
thousand years I shall never forgive that neck.

Today Jack informed me, with elaborate carelessness, that Dr. Barritz
intends to leave soon. He also said the doctor has
been known to influence persons at a distance.

"Influence them to do what?" I demanded.

"Anything," said Jack.

"Marry him?"

Jack coughed until he was purple.

Now I am terrified. Suppose this love is not mine
at all! Suppose it has been put into me by
occult vibration, astral pressure, or some foreign machinery not admitted
through customs. Yet if it is an enchantment, I do
not wish to be cured. That is the humiliating part.
I would rather be bewitched by Dr. Barritz than remain
sane for any other man.

But he must never learn about Redhorse.

Do not remind me that virtue should be candid. Virtue
has never worn a meal-sack in front of the man
it adores.

**M. J. D.**

---

**June 14**

Irene, my own:

This morning Dr. Barritz asked me to walk with him
on the beach at four.

I said yes in a tone suggesting that beaches and
four o'clock were daily incidents, then went upstairs and changed
my gown three times. White appeared bridal, blue seemed hopeful,
gray looked resigned, and pink made me resemble a confection.
I chose white, despised myself, changed to gray, wept, returned
to white, and lost a hairpin down my back.

At three Jack met me by the lift.

"You are going?" he asked.

"Where?"

"To the beach."

"Why should I?"

"Because Barritz is waiting."

"Then why ask?"

He grinned—the Redhorse grin, unchanged by civilization—and said, "Be kind
to him, Gunny."

I caught his sleeve. "Jack Raynor, what has he told
you?"

"Nothing."

"What do YOU know?"

"Everything."

Then the lift swallowed him before murder could be arranged.

Dr. Barritz was waiting below the cliff where the sand
is firm and the hotel guests become small, respectable insects
above. The wind was high. It blew my hair into
my mouth, wrapped my skirt around my knees, and made
his coat flap heroically. Nature is always so inconsiderate when
one wishes to be statuesque.

We walked some distance without speaking. Then he said, "Mary
Jane."

No one here calls me that. I stopped.

"Who gave you permission?"

"You did, long ago."

The whole sea seemed to draw one breath and hold
it.

He continued: "I have loved you for years."

I ought to have replied with tenderness and dignity. Instead
I said, "How many?"

He smiled. "Nearly fifteen."

"That is impossible. You did not know me."

"I knew a little girl who wore two braids, had
a scar on her thumb, and could throw a stone
more accurately than any boy in Redhorse."

I could not move.

"She was called Gunny," he said gently.

Oh, Irene! The surf might have risen and carried California
away without surprising me more.

I whispered, "You read my mind."

"No."

"You have seen hidden records."

"No."

"You are—" I hardly knew what magnificent horror to propose—"a
reincarnation?"

He laughed. LAUGHED! Dr. Barritz, lord of seven languages and
master of the unprepared human will, laughed exactly as a
ragged boy used to laugh when the superintendent slipped into
the tailings pond.

Then he pulled up his left sleeve. On the wrist
was a crooked white mark, shaped like a fishhook.

I had made that mark with a tin shovel.

"Dumps," I said.

He bowed. "At your service."

My knees failed. I sank upon a convenient rock with
all the tragic grace of a heroine in the last
act, except that I sat on my skirt and nearly
pulled myself backward.

DUMPS!

Alive, educated, well dressed, speaking in that voice for which
a person should purchase a ticket—Dumps, whose nose I had
bloodied behind the assay office; Dumps, who once gave me
half a peppermint after first removing it from his mouth;
Dumps, whom I had mourned as dead!

Irene, I BLUBBERED.

Not wept. Do not give it a prettier name. I
clutched his coat and made noises. He sat beside me,
put his arms around me, and allowed his collar to
be ruined with a patience no occult science could teach.

When I could speak, I accused him of being dead.

He said the road agents had shot the driver, not
him. He had crawled under the stage, escaped after dark,
and afterward joined a freight outfit going south. From there
his history became a geography lesson—Mexico, ships, hospitals, universities, Vienna,
India, Paris. He worked, studied medicine, changed his name because
no diploma would look serious with "Dumps" on it, and
came back when Jack found him abroad.

"Why did you not write?" I demanded.

"I was poor, then proud, then uncertain. You had become
Miss Dement."

"And you had become a supernatural nuisance!"

He kissed my forehead. That made argument difficult.

"But why say we had met? Why torment me with
Redhorse? Why let Jack fill the hotel with tales of
your powers?"

"I said only the truth. We HAD met. As for
Jack—"

At that moment my right leg, which had been folded
beneath me, became possessed by ten thousand needles. I sprang
up with a shriek, struck Dr. Barritz on the shoulder,
and hopped in a circle.

He caught me. "What is it?"

"My leg! It is dead!"

His face changed so terribly that I cried, "No, no—not
medically dead. Asleep!"

Thus the grand recognition of two souls, parted by bandits
and fifteen years, concluded with the gentleman rubbing the lady's
calf while she leaned against him and giggled into his
hair.

When circulation and dignity returned, I asked the only important
question.

"Do you love Miss Dement, or merely Gunny?"

He looked almost offended. "They are the same woman."

"They are not. One has money."

"Then I prefer the other."

"Because she had none?"

"Because she shared it."

I remembered two copper pennies divided beside the Syndicate Mill,
and began blubbering again, though more quietly.

He asked me to marry him.

I said yes.

Then, being myself once more, I added, "Alive, mind you.
I have already mourned you dead and will not do
it twice."

Your delirious, disgraced, and blessed
**Mary Jane**

**June 15**

Dearest Irene:

You ask—though your letter cannot possibly have arrived since yesterday;
I merely hear you asking across the miles—whether I confessed
that I had concealed Redhorse because I feared he would
despise me.

I did.

It was after dinner, under the rented palm. I told
him everything: my shame at the nickname, my terror of
his refinement, my belief that he could look through silk
and education into the little cabin, and my conviction that
I was unworthy to unfasten the lowest button of his
distinguished boot.

He said, "Mary, I have seen you barefoot."

There are men who can destroy rhetoric with one sentence.

Then I asked whether he had despised himself when poor.

"Sometimes," he said. "But never when I remembered you."

That was cruelly beautiful, and I was about to cry
when he added that I had once bitten him for
refusing to steal peaches.

"I did not bite you for that," I said. "I
bit you because you said my braids looked like rat
tails."

"So they did."

We nearly quarreled, which reassured me more than all his
declarations. One cannot live forever with an oracle; but Dumps
I can manage.

Jack entered and found us laughing. He tried to retreat,
but I seized him by the lapel.

"You invented the monasteries!"

"Some of them."

"The thought-reading?"

"Entirely."

"The three-day trance?"

"He once overslept in Bombay."

"And the influence at a distance?"

Jack looked innocent. "That was obvious."

I told him he had played it upon me shamefully.

He replied that if he had introduced Dr. Barritz simply
as my old companion Dumps, I should have been kind,
charitable, and uninterested; whereas mystery made me look twice, and
love did the rest.

The villain is right, which makes forgiveness extremely disagreeable.

We are to be married in September. Dr. Barritz—Dumps—says he
will take me to Redhorse first. The mill is silent
now, the cabin gone, the tailings silver under weeds; but
some people remain, and I shall not deny them. Let
fashionable society hunt gossip out of season if it chooses.
I was Gunny before I was Miss Dement, and the
girl in the meal-sack had better sense about one ragged
boy than the lady in Paris silk had about one
celebrated doctor.

Still, I shall wear the silk.

Affectionately,
**Mary Jane Dement**

P.S. Mrs. Brimmer has abandoned occultism. Dr. Barritz failed to
cure her headache, and the hotel physician succeeded with bicarbonate
of soda. Jack says this proves material science is stronger
than the astral body after lobster salad.

P.P.S. My left hand is no longer hidden. It is
engaged.

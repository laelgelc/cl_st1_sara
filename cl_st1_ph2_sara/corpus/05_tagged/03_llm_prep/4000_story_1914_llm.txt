# The Mustard-Tin Fortune

Mr. Augustus Teak learned of his wife's fortune on a
Tuesday evening, and for the rest of the week regarded
her with a respect which twenty years of matrimony had
failed to inspire.

The information came from Mrs. Piper, a widow who lived
three doors away and was incapable of retaining either money
or news. She detained Mr. Teak outside the Prince of
Wales, and, after looking carefully up and down the street,
spoke in a whisper that reached the opposite pavement.

"Your missis is a wonderful woman," she said. "If I'd
got three hundred and twenty-two pounds put by, I shouldn't
be wearing a bonnet like hers."

Mr. Teak, who had been lifting his hat, replaced it
slowly.

"Three 'undred and what?" he demanded.

"Twenty-two. She told me herself, but you mustn't breathe a
word. She's been saving it ever since she married."

Mr. Teak stared at the widow.

"There's some mistake," he said at last. "She's never 'ad
three hundred farthings."

Mrs. Piper smiled with pity.

"Some men don't know everything," she observed. "She's got it
in sovereigns and notes, hidden in your 'ouse. Says she
don't believe in banks."

"Did she say where?"

"No. And I didn't ask. I'm not inquisitive."

Mr. Teak looked at her in silence. Mrs. Piper, who
had spent the greater part of her life asking questions,
bore his gaze with firmness.

"She said she'd saved it out of the housekeeping," she
continued. "Wonderful management."

Mr. Teak's expression altered.

"Out of the housekeeping?"

"So she said."

He walked home in a state of mind difficult to
reconcile with the respectability of his black coat and stiff
collar. The idea that he had eaten inferior meat, worn
mended socks, and smoked tobacco sold loose from a blue
jar in order that Mrs. Teak might conceal three hundred
and twenty-two pounds about the premises appeared to him a
monstrous interference with the rights of man.

At supper he watched her cut the loaf. She was
a small, brisk woman with sharp eyes, a smooth forehead,
and the economical habit of cutting bread so thin that
daylight could be seen through it. Mr. Teak ate four
slices while considering his position.

"You're looking at me," said his wife.

"I know."

"What for?"

Mr. Teak lowered his eyes to the cheese.

"I was thinking what a saving woman you are."

Mrs. Teak stopped cutting.

"What d'you mean?"

"Nothing. It's a compliment."

"I don't want none of your compliments. Eat your supper."

Mr. Teak obeyed. From time to time his gaze travelled
about the kitchen—to the dresser, the cupboards, the clock, the
coal-scuttle and the fireplace. Three hundred and twenty-two pounds, properly
distributed, might lurk anywhere. The house, which had always seemed
painfully small, acquired sudden depths and mysteries.

That night, while Mrs. Teak slept, he rose noiselessly and
examined the chest of drawers. At the first squeak she
sat up.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking for a collar-stud."

"In my stocking drawer?"

"It rolled," said Mr. Teak, after a pause. "It rolled
a long way."

Mrs. Teak lit the candle and watched him return to
bed.

Next morning she followed him from room to room with
an attentive air. At dinner she asked why the kitchen
rug had been lifted, and in the evening discovered that
the flour-bin had been prodded with the poker.

"Cat," said Mr. Teak.

"We ain't got a cat."

"Neighbour's cat."

"In the flour-bin?"

Mr. Teak lost patience.

"If you know all about it," he said, "why ask?"

Mrs. Teak's eyes narrowed. For the rest of the evening
she sat sewing, while her husband reflected that unaided enterprise
was impossible. On Saturday night he sought counsel at the
Prince of Wales.

Mr. Alfred Chase, his oldest friend, was seated in the
private bar with a pint of bitter and a twopenny
cigar. He was a broad man with a red face,
pale eyebrows, and a talent for finding employment too exhausting
to retain. Mr. Teak purchased another pint and led him
to a quiet corner.

"I want a favour," he began.

Mr. Chase inspected him over the rim of his pot.

"How much?"

"Never mind that yet."

"Then it ain't money you want," said Mr. Chase, brightening.

"It's money I've got."

Mr. Chase put down his pot.

"How much?"

"Three 'undred and twenty-two pounds."

Mr. Chase stared, then reached across and pinched his friend's
sleeve.

"Where?"

"Hidden in my 'ouse."

"Who hid it?"

"My wife."

Mr. Chase sat back. His face assumed the expression of
a man disappointed by the morals of society.

"You mean it's hers?"

Mr. Teak raised one hand.

"Legally, perhaps. Morally, no. She saved it out of money
I gave her. If a man gives his wife eighteen
shillings for housekeeping and she spends seventeen, whose is the
shilling?"

"Hers," said Mr. Chase promptly.

Mr. Teak frowned.

"I'm putting a case."

"I'm answering it."

"You don't understand. She's become a miser. Money is ruining
her character. My object is to remove temptation and teach
her to enjoy life."

Mr. Chase looked at him with admiration.

"What'll you do with it?"

"Put it where it can't injure her," said Mr. Teak.
"I might buy a little public-house."

"Ah," said Mr. Chase.

"There's only one difficulty. I can't hunt while she's there,
and I can't get her out often enough. What I
want is somebody in the 'ouse who can search quiet
and methodical."

Mr. Chase smoked for some time.

"What do I get?"

"Five pounds."

"Good night."

He rose, but Mr. Teak pulled him down.

"Ten."

"Good night."

"Fifteen."

Mr. Chase shook his head.

"Think of the risk. Suppose she catches me with the
clock in pieces, or my arm up the chimbley? I
might lose my character."

"You've managed without one so far."

"That makes it all the harder to explain. Twenty pounds."

Mr. Teak groaned.

"Twenty," repeated Mr. Chase, "and my board and lodging while
I'm looking."

After prolonged argument, during which Mr. Teak demonstrated that friendship
was extinct and Mr. Chase proved that business was business,
the terms were accepted.

The next difficulty was to introduce the assistant fortune-hunter into
the house. Mr. Chase, who lived in a furnished room
and owed three weeks' rent, had no objection to changing
his address. Mrs. Teak had several.

"I don't want no lodger," she said, when her husband
proposed the matter.

"Think of the money," said Mr. Teak. "Eight shillings a
week."

"Ten."

"Eight," said Mr. Chase, who had called by arrangement and
was sitting in his best clothes by the kitchen fire.

"Ten," said Mrs. Teak. "And washing extra."

Mr. Chase looked at Mr. Teak. Mr. Teak winked at
him.

"What's the matter with your eye?" asked Mrs. Teak.

"Dust."

"You only wink when you've got something wicked in your
mind."

Mr. Teak opened both eyes to their fullest extent. Mr.
Chase, attempting to reassure him, winked in return.

"And what are you winking for?" demanded Mrs. Teak.

"Same bit o' dust," said Mr. Chase.

Mrs. Teak looked from one to the other.

"Ten shillings," she said, "and he buys his own beer."

Mr. Chase entered the household on Monday.

His first week produced no result. Mrs. Teak kept him
in view with an industry flattering neither to his appearance
nor his honesty. If he went upstairs, she called after
him. If he lingered in the kitchen, she found work
there. Once, on discovering him alone in the parlour, she
stood in the doorway with folded arms.

"Lost anything?" she inquired.

"My 'andkerchief."

"It's sticking out of your pocket."

Mr. Chase pushed it in.

"Other 'andkerchief," he explained.

On Saturday he reported failure to Mr. Teak.

"You don't get her out," he complained. "A man can't
search an 'ouse with a woman following him like a
policeman."

"I took her shopping Wednesday."

"For twenty minutes. I'd only just got the screws out
of the looking-glass when I heard her key."

"You put 'em back wrong," said Mr. Teak. "She says
it leans."

"Take her somewhere for the afternoon."

Mr. Teak considered the expense.

"There's the park."

"She'll be back in an hour."

"Then I'll take her to see her aunt at Camberwell."

"She hates her aunt."

"All the better. She'll stay to quarrel."

Before adopting this excellent plan, Mr. Teak made a discovery
which diverted his energies. He came upon Mrs. Teak in
the garden stamping down a small patch of newly turned
earth. At sight of him she started and dropped the
trowel.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Planting."

"What?"

"Things."

"What things?"

"Things that come up," replied Mrs. Teak. "Don't stand there
breathing on 'em."

Mr. Teak walked indoors with an unconcern that deceived nobody.
At half-past eleven that night he stole downstairs in his
stockinged feet, armed with a shovel. Moonlight shone upon the
garden, a narrow strip containing two cabbages, a line of
washing, and a broken chair on which Mrs. Teak cultivated
mint.

He began at the newly turned patch. After ten minutes
he found a potato, after twenty a piece of crockery,
and after half an hour a boot which he recognized
as his own. He widened the excavation.

At one o'clock Mr. Chase put his head out of
the bedroom window.

"Found it?" he whispered.

"Shut up!"

"Don't dig too deep. It might be Australia by now."

Mr. Teak shook his fist. The movement disturbed his balance,
and he went backwards into the mint.

At half-past two the garden resembled a small railway cutting.
Mr. Teak, wet with perspiration and rich in stones, stood
knee-deep in a trench and contemplated a final mound. The
kitchen window went up.

"Augustus," said Mrs. Teak.

He froze.

"What are you doing?"

Mr. Teak leaned on the shovel and looked toward the
house.

"Couldn't sleep."

"So you're digging up my garden?"

"Exercise," he said. "Doctor says it's good."

"What doctor?"

"Doctor—doctor I met."

Mrs. Teak came downstairs in a shawl and stood surveying
the ruins. Her silence was painful.

"I've been meaning to do it for months," said Mr.
Teak. "Drainage."

She picked up the boot.

"You buried this last year because it pinched you."

"I wondered where it was."

"You'll put every bit back before breakfast."

Mr. Teak spent Sunday under observation, restoring the garden. Mr.
Chase watched from an upper window and occasionally offered advice.

The visit to Camberwell took place on Wednesday. Mr. Teak
returned in less than an hour, holding his hat on
with one hand and rubbing his ear with the other.

"She wouldn't go," he said.

"I guessed that," replied Mr. Chase.

"How?"

"She's upstairs."

The following Saturday he tried again. This time he took
Mrs. Teak to the Zoological Gardens, having first promised Mr.
Chase that he should have four clear hours.

The outing began badly. Mrs. Teak objected to the tram
fare, the entrance money, and a bag of buns purchased
by her husband for the animals. She accused him of
throwing money about, and, on his offering a bun to
an elephant, asked whether the creature had paid for it.

Mr. Teak endured these reproaches with a sweetness that alarmed
her.

"What do you keep smiling for?" she demanded.

"Enjoying myself."

"Well, stop it. You look silly."

For two hours he led her among beasts whose feeding
habits appeared to him less strange than those of his
wife. He bought her tea, and even suggested a second
cup. Mrs. Teak examined him closely.

"You've done something," she said.

"I've done nothing."

"Then you're going to."

At that moment rain began. It came first in large
drops and then in a grey sheet that emptied paths
and filled shelters. Mrs. Teak gathered up her skirts.

"We're going 'ome."

"Can't," said Mr. Teak. "Rain."

"That's why."

"We might wait. It may clear."

"It may not. Come along."

He detained her for twelve minutes under the pretence that
his bootlace was broken. She watched him knot and unknot
it, then dragged him to the tram. All the way
home he sat with a fixed smile while his mind
pictured Mr. Chase in the midst of some delicate operation.

Mrs. Teak reached the house first. She opened the door
and stood listening.

There was a faint bump overhead.

"Rats," said Mr. Teak quickly.

"Rats don't wear boots."

"Big rats might."

She mounted the stairs. Mr. Teak followed, making warning noises
which began as coughs and ended as bleats. In the
bedroom all appeared orderly, except that the rug was rolled
back and a chair stood in the fireplace. A shower
of soot descended into the grate.

Mrs. Teak drew a long breath.

A pair of boots appeared in the chimney, followed by
two trousered legs. Mr. Chase emerged backwards, wriggling violently. His
coat was white with plaster and his face black with
soot, except where perspiration had traced three clean channels down
it. He reached the chair, turned, and confronted them.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

"What," said Mrs. Teak at last, "are you doing in
my chimbley?"

Mr. Chase opened his mouth. No answer came.

"I asked what you're doing."

"Bird," he said.

Mrs. Teak looked into the empty grate.

"What bird?"

"Little bird. Sparrow—or a pigeon. I 'eard it fluttering."

"In our bedroom?"

"Yes."

"So you climbed up the chimbley?"

"I climbed down," said Mr. Chase. "That is, I was
going up, but I come down."

"Where's the bird?"

Mr. Chase glanced at Mr. Teak. Mr. Teak winked with
such force that his whole cheek moved.

"It got away," said Mr. Chase. "It flowed—I mean flew—past
me."

"Up the chimbley or down?"

"Sideways."

Mrs. Teak nodded slowly. She then crossed to the window
and looked out at the rain.

"Very fond of birds, ain't you?" she said.

"Always was," replied Mr. Chase, with feeling.

"Get out of my bedroom."

Mr. Chase got out. Mrs. Teak followed him downstairs, placed
a broom, dustpan and pail in his hands, and stood
over him while he removed the soot. Mr. Teak assisted
until his wife sent him to purchase whitening for the
ceiling.

That evening Mrs. Teak sat unusually quiet. Her gaze moved
now and again toward the two friends. Mr. Teak, to
demonstrate innocence, read the newspaper upside down. Mr. Chase devoted
himself to a plate of cold mutton.

"I suppose," said Mrs. Teak suddenly, "you two never met
before he came lodging here?"

"Never," said Mr. Teak.

"Not to speak of," said Mr. Chase at the same
moment.

Mrs. Teak waited.

"I mean," said Mr. Teak, "we'd met, but not friendly."

"Barely knew each other," added Mr. Chase.

"You went to his sister's wedding," said Mrs. Teak.

"Did I?" said Mr. Teak.

Mr. Chase kicked him under the table.

Mr. Teak sprang up.

"What are you jumping for?" asked his wife.

"Cramp."

"In both legs?"

"Very severe cramp."

Mrs. Teak resumed her sewing. The conspirators avoided each other's
eyes.

For the next week nothing was attempted. Mr. Chase declared
that Mrs. Teak never slept; Mr. Teak replied that he
had noticed it himself. Their conversation took place in whispers
at street corners and in the public bar, where the
cost of strategy steadily reduced the value of the prize.

On the following Thursday Mr. Teak returned from work to
find his wife seated in the kitchen with Mrs. Piper.
Both women had red eyes. A police whistle lay on
the table, together with a brick and a mustard tin.

"What's up?" he demanded.

Mrs. Teak pointed to the objects.

"Robbed," she said.

Mr. Teak's heart gave a leap so violent that he
placed one hand against his waistcoat.

"Robbed? What of?"

"My savings. Every penny."

Mrs. Piper sniffed.

"Three hundred and twenty-two pounds," she said. "In the house
all the time, and him not knowing. Wonderful woman."

Mr. Teak sank into a chair.

"Where was it?"

"Mustard tins," said his wife. "Behind the fireplace in the
bedroom. Somebody broke the washhouse window, got into the kitchen,
went upstairs, and took 'em."

Mr. Teak stared at the tin. It was yellow, square,
and empty.

"When?" he asked.

"This afternoon."

"Where's Chase?"

"Out. He went just after dinner."

Mr. Teak's face slowly brightened.

"Then it's no use making a fuss," he said. "Money's
gone. Might as well say nothing."

"Say nothing?" cried Mrs. Piper. "Send for the police!"

"No," said Mr. Teak sharply.

Mrs. Teak looked at him.

"Why not?"

"They make trouble. Questions and that."

"That's what they're for."

"They might suspect innocent people," said Mr. Teak, with dignity.
"Once the police get into a respectable 'ouse, there's no
knowing where they'll stop."

"I'm going to fetch one," declared Mrs. Piper.

Mr. Teak caught her wrist as she reached for the
whistle.

"Sit down. Think of the scandal."

"The scandal's the thief's."

"Neighbours'll talk."

"They're talking now," said Mrs. Piper. "I told six of
'em as I came along."

Mr. Teak turned pale. With some difficulty he persuaded her
that quiet investigation was better than official interference. Mrs. Teak
opposed him at first, but at last yielded with a
meekness that surprised him.

"Very well," she said. "If Augustus don't want the police,
I suppose he's got his reasons."

"I've got no reasons," said Mr. Teak quickly. "Only common
sense."

Mrs. Piper departed to spread the news more quietly. Mrs.
Teak went upstairs, leaving her husband alone with the empty
tin.

He picked it up and performed three steps of a
silent hornpipe. Then he kissed the label, replaced it on
the table, and waited for Mr. Chase.

Nine o'clock came, then ten. At half-past ten the lodger
entered with a parcel of fried fish beneath his arm.
Mr. Teak met him in the passage and dragged him
into the parlour.

"Well?" he whispered.

Mr. Chase looked puzzled.

"Well what?"

"Don't play the giddy goat. You got it."

"Got what?"

"The money."

Mr. Chase dropped the fish on a chair.

"What money?"

Mr. Teak closed the door.

"Three hundred and twenty-two pounds. Mustard tins behind the bedroom
fireplace. You broke the washhouse window to make it look
like burglars. Very clever. Now hand it over."

Mr. Chase stared at him, first with surprise and then
with suspicion.

"You've got it," he said.

"I ain't."

"You knew where it was."

"I know now."

"How?"

"She says it's been stole."

Mr. Chase sat down slowly.

"And you think I took it?"

"Course you did."

"Then where's my twenty pounds?"

Mr. Teak laughed uneasily.

"You don't expect me to believe you ain't got it?"

"No more than I expect you to believe me when
I say I climbed up the chimbley after a bird.
You found it yourself."

"I never!"

"You dug the garden up. You was poking round every
night. You found it and broke the window."

Mr. Teak clenched his fists.

"I tell you I thought you'd done it."

"And I tell you I thought you had."

They faced each other across the little table. From upstairs
came the faint movement of a chair.

Mr. Teak lowered his voice.

"If you're trying to cheat me—"

"Cheat you?" said Mr. Chase. "You offered me twenty pounds
and now you've got the lot."

"I ain't got a farthing."

"Turn out your pockets."

"You turn out yours."

Mr. Chase did so. A clasp-knife, two coppers, a tram
ticket and a piece of string fell on the table.

"There," he said. "Now you."

Mr. Teak produced ninepence, three keys and a wooden match.

"You've hidden it," said Mr. Chase.

"So've you."

Mr. Chase seized him by the coat.

"Give me my twenty pounds."

Mr. Teak struck his hands away.

"Take your dirty paws off."

"Thief!"

"Liar!"

Their voices rose together. The table went over, followed by
a chair and the fried fish. Mr. Chase rushed in
low; Mr. Teak caught him round the neck, and the
two revolved heavily about the room, knocking pictures crooked and
bringing the lamp dangerously near the edge of the mantelpiece.

Mrs. Teak opened the door.

They stopped. Mr. Chase had one hand in Mr. Teak's
collar; Mr. Teak's thumb was in Mr. Chase's mouth.

"What's the matter?" she asked.

"Nothin'," said Mr. Teak thickly.

Mr. Chase released the thumb.

"Friendly argument," he said.

Mrs. Teak surveyed the overturned supper.

"Have it outside."

Mr. Chase picked up his hat, which had been sat
upon, and pushed it into shape.

"I'm leaving," he announced.

"You owe a week's lodging," said Mrs. Teak.

He looked at Mr. Teak.

"Let him pay it," he said bitterly. "He can afford
it."

Mr. Teak made a rush, but Mrs. Teak stepped between
them. Mr. Chase departed, slamming the front door so hard
that a china dog fell from the mantelpiece and broke
its nose.

During the next month the former friends met twice in
the street. On the first occasion each crossed over. On
the second, both crossed and found themselves face to face.
Mr. Chase tapped his breast-pocket and smiled mysteriously; Mr. Teak
replied by patting his own pocket and laughing. Each went
away tormented by the other's appearance of prosperity.

At home Mr. Teak lived under a cloud. His wife
referred frequently to her loss, chiefly when he asked for
money.

"New boots?" she said. "After I've been robbed of my
life's savings?"

"The soles is through."

"Put cardboard in 'em."

On another occasion she served him cold potatoes for supper
and sighed over the price of coal. Mr. Teak's belief
that the missing money was concealed by Mr. Chase became
a settled grief. He pictured his old friend buying a
public-house, wearing gold watch-chains, and smoking cigars at fourpence each.

Then, one evening, Mrs. Piper called.

Mr. Teak was in the passage and heard her say,
"I think you done quite right."

"So do I," replied Mrs. Teak.

"Safe as houses in a bank."

Mr. Teak stood motionless.

"And you never told Augustus?"

"He'd only worry."

"What made you move it?"

"That Chase man coming down the chimbley. I knew there
was something up. Augustus couldn't look honest if he tried.
So next morning I took the tins out, paid the
money into the bank, and broke the washhouse window."

Mrs. Piper gave a delighted gasp.

"And pretended it was stole?"

"I wanted to see what they'd do. Augustus near danced
when I told him. Then he and Chase had that
fight. Pair of fools."

Mr. Teak entered the kitchen.

The women turned. Mrs. Piper rose at once.

"I must be going," she said.

Mr. Teak stood aside for her. After the door closed,
he approached his wife.

"So you've got the money?"

"Yes."

"In the bank?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Three hundred and twenty-two pounds, less sixpence for the new
pane."

Mr. Teak drew a chair to the table and sat
down.

"You deceived me."

Mrs. Teak continued folding a cloth.

"You deceived yourself. I only helped."

He considered this for some time.

"Suppose I want some of it?"

"You don't."

"Suppose I do?"

"What for?"

Mr. Teak opened his mouth, but no respectable purpose presented
itself.

"Never mind what for. I'm your husband."

"That's why it's in the bank."

He stared at her. Mrs. Teak placed the folded cloth
in a drawer and locked it, though it contained nothing
but cloths.

Next evening Mr. Teak entered the Prince of Wales and
found Mr. Chase alone in the private bar. He ordered
two pints, pushed one across, and sat down.

Mr. Chase regarded him cautiously.

"Got my twenty pounds?"

"No."

"Then what d'you want?"

Mr. Teak related the truth. As he proceeded, Mr. Chase's
suspicion gave way to amusement. He slapped the counter and
laughed until the landlord requested quiet.

"She broke the window herself?" he said.

Mr. Teak nodded gloomily.

"And the money's in the bank?"

"Every penny."

Mr. Chase wiped his eyes.

"Well, you ought to know your own wife."

Mr. Teak took a long pull at his beer.

"That's where you're wrong, Alf," he said. "A man may
know thieves, liars and fortune-hunters—but marriage gives him something harder
to study."

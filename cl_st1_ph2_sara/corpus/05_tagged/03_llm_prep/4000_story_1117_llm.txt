### The Fisherman of Pass Christian

If ever you go to Pass Christian in July—and you
should go, if only to learn how many colors the
sea can wear between breakfast and moonrise—you will find the
winds in perpetual disagreement.

From the pine woods comes one, resinous, fragrant, cold, wandering
through the long needles with a sound like rain; from
the Gulf comes another, warm and soft and languorous, with
salt upon its lips. They meet among the cottages, dispute
over the white shell roads, shake the oleanders, and finally
escape together across the water, reconciled, perhaps, by its beauty.

Annette Valmont declared that she could distinguish them with her
eyes closed.

"The north wind says, 'Wake and be sensible,'" she told
her cousin Philip, "and the south wind says, 'Go to
sleep and dream.'"

"Hum," said Philip.

Philip's "Hum" was an instrument of wide compass. It could
express disbelief, displeasure, jealousy, fatigue, or that superior masculine patience
which is often least patient when it appears most resigned.
On this occasion it meant that Annette had been talking
nonsense for half an hour and that he loved her.

They were sitting at the end of the long pier
belonging to Madame Valmont's summer cottage. Behind them the shore
lay white in the sun, with villas half hidden by
magnolias, figs, and live-oaks. Before them the Gulf stretched smooth
and intensely blue, except where catboats moved upon it like
white moths. Farther out, the low dark line of a
schooner hung between sea and sky.

Annette, in a pale dress and broad straw hat, leaned
over to watch the small fish flashing below. She was
nineteen, with dark eyes that changed more quickly than the
Gulf, a mouth made for laughter and song, and that
careless grace which relatives call alarming until strangers begin to
admire it.

Philip was twenty-seven and very sensible. He had an excellent
position in New Orleans, an excellent opinion of his judgment,
and an unspoken intention of marrying Annette as soon as
she should become sufficiently sensible to perceive the advantage.

"Do not lean so far," he said.

"I am not leaning."

"You are almost in the water."

"Then you may rescue me."

"I cannot swim in boots."

"You should not wear boots at the seaside."

She turned to smile at him, and in turning struck
with her parasol the little basket beside her. It rolled
once, twice, and dropped between the planks.

"My music!"

A gust lifted the loose sheets from the basket. They
fluttered out over the water—scales, exercises, a romance by Gounod—white
birds suddenly terrified by freedom.

Annette sprang up. Philip caught her sleeve. At the same
moment a fishing skiff glided from beneath the pier, and
the man in it, standing lightly while the boat rocked,
gathered two sheets with his net, seized a third with
his hand, and rowed after the others.

He was dressed in coarse blue trousers, a faded shirt,
and a broad hat such as the fishermen wore; but
nothing else about him appeared coarse. His figure was tall
and spare, his movements easy, and when he brought the
recovered pages to the steps his courtesy belonged less to
a boat than to a drawing-room.

"Mademoiselle has lost a song," he said. "The sea, being
musical, wished to keep it."

His voice made Annette look at him twice. It was
deep, beautifully placed, and touched with an accent too slight
to be foreign and too distinct to be ignored.

"You have saved everything," she cried. "Even my dreadful exercises."

"They are more valuable than the song."

"No one who had practised them would say so."

"On the contrary, mademoiselle, only one who has practised them
could say so."

Philip descended two steps and accepted the dripping sheets.

"We are obliged to you," he said, in the tone
with which one dismisses a competent servant.

The fisherman's eyes rested upon him for an instant. They
were gray, Annette saw, and contained a quiet amusement.

"My name is LeConte," he said. "If the pages dry
badly, I shall consider myself responsible."

"You are a fisherman?" Annette asked.

"Today, yes."

Philip said, "Hum."

LeConte bowed, pushed off, and rowed away with long, noiseless
strokes.

That evening Annette sang on the gallery while the sunset
burned itself out behind Bay St. Louis. Her aunt accompanied
her upon a small square piano which had endured six
summers, much dampness, and innumerable sentimental songs. Philip sat in
the shadow smoking and finding faults in the weather.

When Annette finished, there came from beyond the hedge the
soft clapping of two hands.

Philip rose.

LeConte stood on the shell road with a string of
speckled trout. He removed his hat.

"Pardon, madame. I came to offer these fish, and the
music detained me."

Madame Valmont glanced at his clothes, then at his face.
She had been born a D'Aubigny, and therefore believed herself
capable of recognizing good blood through any amount of salt
and sun.

"You sing, monsieur?" she asked.

"A little."

"He knows exercises," Annette said. "That is suspicious."

LeConte smiled. "One may know the road without having traveled
far upon it."

Natalie came through the hall, her bright head-turban appearing first,
followed by an apron held up to receive the fish.

"Bon Dieu! Look at dem trout! Monsieur cotch all dat?"

"All but the largest," said LeConte. "That one allowed me
to pull him in."

Natalie laughed. "Den he got mo' politesse than some folks."

Philip threw away his cigar.

"A fisherman who quotes Gounod and jokes with the cook,"
he observed later, "is likely to be either a fraud
or a fool."

"Or a gentleman," said Annette.

"A gentleman does not live in a hut beyond Henderson's
Point."

"How do you know where he lives?"

"I inquired."

Annette smiled. "Oh, Philip!"

"Hum."

From that day LeConte appeared with the irregular regularity of
a person who does not seek encounters and never misses
one. At sunrise he passed in his skiff while Annette
bathed with her cousins; at noon he was seen mending
a net beneath the oaks; at evening he brought fish
to the cottage, or stood listening beyond the oleanders when
she sang. He never intruded. Indeed, his restraint provoked more
curiosity than familiarity could have done.

Some days later the Valmonts held a fish-fry on the
beach. You must know that a fish-fry, properly conducted, is
not a meal but a ceremony. There must be a
fire of driftwood; there must be children burning their fingers
upon potatoes which they have been warned not to touch;
there must be coffee, black and furious, boiled in a
tin pot; and there must be freshly caught fish, rolled
in meal and dropped into grease until they emerge brown,
crisp, and altogether superior to the same creatures served upon
porcelain in the city.

Philip had undertaken to supply the fish. He returned after
four hot hours with two croakers, a pinfish, and a
disposition so savage that Madame Valmont advised silence.

LeConte's skiff came in behind him with redfish, trout, flounder,
and a pompano bright as beaten silver.

"Hum," said Philip, casting his own catch upon the sand
as if each fish had personally insulted him.

LeConte cleaned his contribution with astonishing dexterity, built the fire
when the damp wood refused to burn, restored Natalie's overturned
coffee-pot, and rescued little Cécile's ribbon from the tide. Annette
followed him from task to task, praising each performance until
Philip attached himself ostentatiously to Miss Corinne Beauregard and did
not look at Annette for twenty minutes.

This punishment failed because Annette did not notice it.

When the moon rose, broad and yellow, the young people
carried mandolins and guitars to the pier. The water was
dark beneath them, but every stroke of an oar awakened
a green, phosphorescent flame. Waves ran toward the beach and
broke there with a laughing kiss. The moon, who has
assisted at hundreds of other equally romantic gatherings and should
by now be weary of them, nevertheless appeared to sympathize.

"Sing for us, LeConte," Annette commanded.

He shook his head.

"Why not?"

"I have no song tonight."

"But you said you sang a little."

"A little may be too much."

She began the old Creole melody, *Quand mo té jeune*,
lightly at first; then her voice deepened and opened, warm,
untrained, and touching. The guitars softened. Even Philip ceased to
scowl.

LeConte listened with his head bent. When she ended, he
said quietly, "You have something rare, mademoiselle."

"A voice?"

"The beginning of one."

It was not extravagant praise; another girl might have been
offended. Annette felt instead as though a door had opened
upon a brilliantly lighted room.

"What must I do?"

"Study."

"I do study."

"Study more. Breathe correctly. Do not force the upper tones.
Be patient."

"I am never patient."

"Then learn that also."

Philip laughed. "Annette at the opera! We shall hear her
from the boxes."

"Why not?" she demanded.

LeConte looked across the luminous water. "Why not, indeed?"

Two mornings afterward, Annette and her younger cousin went sailing
with Philip. The boat was small, the breeze deceptive, and
Philip, who knew enough of sailing to be confident and
not enough to be afraid, carried too much canvas. Beyond
the point the Gulf wind struck them sharply. The boat
heeled; Cécile screamed; Annette's hat flew off; and the line,
running wild through Philip's hand, burned his palm.

A brown sail came toward them.

LeConte's skiff seemed scarcely to touch the water. He brought
it alongside, lowered their canvas, freed the fouled line, and
quieted Cécile, all without haste and almost without speech.

"You have a habit of rescuing us," Annette said when
danger had become adventure.

"It is fortunate that mademoiselle provides opportunities."

Philip's face darkened. "We were in no danger."

"Certainly not," said LeConte, so courteously that Annette bit her
lip.

They sailed toward Henderson's Point, where a storm-cloud had begun
to pile itself purple and enormous above the pines. Rain
swept the distant shore in gray veils. As the first
large drops fell, LeConte led them to his hut.

It stood upon a rise beyond the marsh, built of
weather-darkened boards, with a narrow porch and one window facing
the Gulf. Annette entered expecting nets, tar, and confusion. She
found nets, certainly, and tar; but she also found a
shelf of French books, a silver traveling flask, a violin
in its case, linen marked with an embroidered L, and
upon the wall an engraving of the Paris Opera.

Philip saw these things too.

"Your hut is well furnished," he remarked.

"One carries memories into exile," LeConte replied.

"Exile?"

"From comfort, monsieur."

He made coffee over a charcoal brazier. The rain drummed
upon the roof, the marsh bent beneath the wind, and
the sea disappeared in pearl-colored mist. Annette opened a book—Molière—and
found penciled notes in a firm, delicate hand.

"You read French poets while waiting for fish?"

"Fish require patience. Poets improve it."

"And the violin?"

"Fish do not object to music."

"Who are you?" she asked suddenly.

The question stilled the little room.

LeConte handed her the coffee. "A man recovering his health."

"From what?"

"From too much noise, too much light, too many suppers,
and the applause of friends."

Philip looked sharply at him, but Annette heard only the
weariness beneath the words.

When the rain ceased, the world emerged washed and glittering.
Drops trembled upon the marsh grass; the west opened in
bars of rose and gold; and the returning wind smelled
of wet pine, salt, and distant flowers.

As the days passed on, Annette's attachment grew with the
ease of a vine finding its trellis. Every meeting gave
it support: a word about her singing, a shell placed
in her hand, a look across the music when others
talked. She knew nothing of LeConte's past, little of his
present, and nothing at all of his intentions; but at
nineteen imagination is a generous architect and does not wait
for deeds before constructing palaces.

Her family became uneasy.

"My dear," Madame Valmont said, "he is charming, undoubtedly. But
charm is not a name, nor a position."

"He has a name."

"LeConte! Half Louisiana is LeConte."

"He is educated."

"So are many dangerous people."

"He is a gentleman."

"A gentleman fishing for his health does not necessarily become
a suitable acquaintance."

"He has never been unsuitable."

That was true, and therefore difficult to answer.

Philip answered it in his own fashion.

"You make yourself ridiculous over this man."

Annette's cheeks flamed. "I do nothing of the kind."

"You follow him about; you listen when he speaks as
if he were announcing the Resurrection; you sing because he
tells you to sing."

"He understands me."

"He has known you three weeks."

"Some people understand in three minutes what others cannot understand
in twenty-seven years."

Philip whitened.

It was a cruel speech. Annette repented before it had
ceased sounding, but pride forbade recall.

The next evening she walked alone along the beach. The
sun had gone, leaving a greenish radiance behind the low
clouds. LeConte stood beside his boat.

"I leave tomorrow," he said.

The world, which a moment before had contained sea, sky,
cottages, gulls, and the smell of jasmine, contracted to those
three words.

"Tomorrow?"

"My health is restored. My work calls me."

"What work?"

"You will know before long."

She turned away. "You always answer with a mystery."

"Forgive me."

"Do you think I shall forget you?"

"No."

His certainty startled her.

He took from his pocket the shell she had once
admired, a delicate spiral, rosy within.

"I ask three promises, mademoiselle."

"Three?"

"Do not forget this summer."

"I could not."

"Do not speak of me until we meet again."

"Why?"

"Because explanations spoil surprises."

"And the third?"

"Continue your lessons. Study, study, sing, practise—when you are tired,
when praise makes you vain, when discouragement makes you idle.
Promise."

"I promise."

The harvest moon lifted above the Gulf, red at first,
then gold. LeConte bent over her hand, but his lips
did not touch it.

"Au revoir, Annette."

It was the first time he had used her name.

"Au revoir," she whispered.

By morning he was gone.

New Orleans received the Valmonts in October with heat, dust,
rattling carriages, bells from the mule-cars, and gossip enough to
compensate every returning family for the loss of the sea.
Annette kept her promises. She did not speak of LeConte.
She placed the shell among her music and began to
work.

Her teacher, Signor Barini, who had long accused her of
possessing talent without industry, stared when she demanded scales.

"Again," she said after an hour.

"You are ill?"

"No."

"In love?"

"Again, maestro."

She breathed, placed, attacked, sustained. She sang vowels until they
ceased to resemble any human speech; she sang intervals that
pursued one another up and down the piano like quarrelsome
children; she learned to distrust easy applause and to hear
the flaws hidden beneath a pretty tone. Study, study, sing,
practise. LeConte's words beat time.

It was the night of the opera's opening.

The French Opera House shone like a jewel-box thrown open.
Carriages crowded Bourbon Street; lamps glittered upon silks, satins, uniforms,
white shoulders, diamonds, fans, and opera-glasses. The boxes bloomed with
women. Gentlemen leaned forward to exchange greetings and judgments. Everyone
knew everyone—or pretended to, which in society often answers as
well.

The opera was *Faust*. Madame Dubeau, newly engaged from Paris,
would sing Marguerite. Of the tenor, an American-born Creole who
had triumphed in Milan and Vienna, people told extraordinary things.

"LeComte has been ill," said Madame Valmont, consulting the programme.
"He disappeared all summer."

"Not LeComte," corrected Philip. "LeConte."

Annette's fan stopped.

The orchestra began. Violins stirred, woodwinds called softly, the curtain
rose, and for a while she forgot the printed name.
Then Faust entered.

He wore the scholar's robe, his face altered by paint
and the lights; but when he lifted his head she
knew the gray eyes. When he sang, she knew the
voice that had spoken above the tide, beneath the storm,
beside the moonlit boat—a voice no hut could contain, golden
and commanding, mounting through the theater until the chandeliers themselves
seemed to tremble.

Annette sat perfectly still.

LeConte, the fisherman, the courteous wanderer, the man recovering from
noise and applause, stood before all New Orleans crowned by
both.

At the end of the act the house broke into
cheers. Flowers fell. Ladies waved handkerchiefs. He bowed, received the
homage calmly, and then his gaze traveled upward to the
Valmont box.

He saw her.

For an instant Faust vanished, celebrity vanished, the crowded theater
vanished. He smiled as he had smiled beside the Gulf.

"Annette!" whispered Madame Valmont. "You know him?"

"The fisherman," Philip said bitterly.

Annette remembered her promise. "I knew that we should meet
again."

After the opera LeConte came to their box. He wore
evening dress now, and no one could question the distinction
of his bearing. Madame Valmont's anxiety dissolved before fame, which
is a passport honored at social frontiers where merit may
be detained for hours.

"You deceived us, monsieur," she said, smiling.

"I rested among you, madame. Deception requires more effort."

Philip asked, "Was the disguise necessary?"

"Entirely. A tenor who goes fishing is still a tenor.
A fisherman may sometimes be a man."

Then he turned to Annette.

"You kept the third promise?"

"Ask Signor Barini."

"I shall hear for myself."

He did hear. Soon he came often to the Valmont
house, sometimes to rehearse with Annette, sometimes to drink coffee
with Madame Valmont, sometimes—Philip believed—for no reason except that Annette
expected him. He praised sparingly, corrected gently, and spoke of
Paris as if it were not a distant city but
a door she might open.

"You must go," he told her. "Two years of serious
study. Perhaps three."

"Would I succeed?"

"If you work."

"Would I sing here?"

"You may sing wherever you choose."

Under his encouragement ambition flamed in her, and ambition, you
must know, can disguise itself as love so perfectly that
the heart scarcely perceives there are two guests at its
table. She dreamed of Parisian studios, of rehearsal rooms, of
orchestras waiting beneath her lifted hand; but in every dream
LeConte stood just beyond the footlights.

There was, however, Madame Dubeau.

Madame Dubeau was fair, graceful, thirty perhaps, and gifted with
that accomplished softness which makes an inexperienced girl feel unfinished.
Upon the stage she loved LeConte in *Faust*, defied him
in *Carmen*, died for him in *Aida*, and forgave him
in several languages. Off the stage she called him "cher
ami," touched his sleeve when speaking, and seemed always to
know where he had dined.

Annette disliked her first as an artist, then as a
woman, and finally with the concentrated injustice of jealousy.

"She leans upon you too much in the garden scene,"
Annette said one afternoon.

"It is in the business."

"She need not enjoy the business."

"Actresses are expected to appear to enjoy it."

"She looks at you even when the audience is applauding
her."

"The audience would be disappointed if she looked at the
conductor."

Annette struck a chord so violently that the piano protested.

LeConte regarded her with amusement and something gentler. "Petite jalouse."

"I am not jealous."

"Then Madame Dubeau is unfortunate. She has been accused without
motive."

"People say you are always together."

"People must say something between operas."

"Do you care for her?"

He grew grave.

"My dear Annette, the theater creates relations which appear strange
outside it. We kiss, quarrel, marry, betray, murder, and die;
then we wash away the paint and take supper. You
must not confuse the footlights with the sun."

She heard reassurance because she desired it. Youth is often
accused of refusing advice; in truth, it accepts very readily
the advice that agrees with hope.

Plans were made for Paris. Madame Valmont, dazzled by Signor
Barini's approval and LeConte's prophecies, consented. Letters were written to
a famous teacher. Gowns were ordered. Philip ceased to protest
and became painfully polite.

The departure was fixed for April.

On the last evening before the trunks were to be
closed, rain fell over New Orleans, warm and steady, silvering
the balconies and washing the dust from the banquette. Annette
sat among music, ribbons, gloves, and half-packed books. On the
table lay the rosy shell from Pass Christian.

Natalie entered without knocking. Her face held that solemn excitement
with which servants announce disasters that are also news.

"Mamzelle Annette—"

"What is it?"

"You ain' heerd?"

"Heard what?"

Natalie hesitated. "Monsieur LeConte. He done marry Madame Dubeau."

Annette laughed.

It was a small, courteous laugh, intended to relieve Natalie
of embarrassment.

"That is absurd."

"Mais non, mamzelle. Dey marry las' week in Mobile. Secret.
Ev'ybody know now. It print in de paper."

Natalie held out the evening journal.

There it was: brief, black, incontestable. The celebrated tenor Étienne
LeConte and Madame Elise Dubeau had been united privately in
Mobile; professional obligations had delayed the announcement; the distinguished artists
would depart for Europe at the close of the season.

Annette read it twice.

The room did not whirl; the rain did not beat
more loudly; no merciful darkness covered the words. Such courtesies
belong to the theater. In life, the lamp burns steadily,
the trunks stand open, and a ribbon dropped five minutes
before remains exactly where it fell.

Madame Valmont found her still sitting there.

"My child," she said, and stopped.

Annette's face had become very white. She folded the newspaper
along its original crease, carefully, as though neatness were now
the single duty left to her.

"Shall I send for Philip?" her aunt asked.

"No."

"For Signor Barini?"

"No."

Madame Valmont knelt beside her. "You must not let this
change Paris. Your voice—your career—those belong to you."

Annette looked at the open music. Scales, exercises, arias; all
the patient ladders she had meant to climb. She touched
the shell, and for one instant the room filled again
with white sand, phosphorescent water, guitars sounding under a harvest
moon, and a fisherman's voice saying, *Study, study, sing, practise.*

Then the vision went out.

"Tell them to unpack," she said.

"But, Annette—"

"I shall not go to Paris."

The words were neither angry nor tragic. She spoke in
a quiet little tired voice, the voice of someone who
had sung too long and had suddenly discovered that there
was no audience, no companion, and no song.

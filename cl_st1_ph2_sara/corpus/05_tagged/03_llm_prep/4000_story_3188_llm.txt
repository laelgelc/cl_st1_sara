You, who have known the temper of my soul longer
than any confessor, will not mistake patience for forgiveness. There
are injuries which may be borne because they are accidental,
and insults which may be pardoned because they are foolish;
but when injury is crowned by contempt, redress becomes not
passion but obligation. Fortunato had injured me often. I endured.
At last he insulted me.

You will ask the nature of the insult. I shall
not repeat it. To name it now would lend it
a dignity it never possessed. It is sufficient that I
understood, and that he—though perhaps only in the instant of
utterance—understood also. From that hour I resolved upon retribution.

Yet punishment is imperfect when it returns upon him who
administers it. It is equally imperfect when the offender does
not perceive whose hand has corrected him. I must strike
without hazard; he must know himself struck by Montresor. These
principles I held as calmly then as I hold them
now, after the passage of half a century.

No alteration in my conduct betrayed me. I continued to
smile upon Fortunato, and he, poor fool, saw no change
in the smile. Indeed, he imagined it the submission of
a lesser man. His vanity was vast, but upon one
subject it was especially vulnerable: wine. In painting, antiquities, and
music he professed judgment; in vintages he possessed some. That
little true knowledge made his conceit the more serviceable.

It was during the last evening of the carnival that
chance delivered him to me.

The streets blazed beneath paper lanterns. Masks streamed past in
vermilion, purple, and gold; drums thundered from the piazza; flutes
shrieked above laughter; and from balconies women scattered petals upon
the crowd. In that tumult I saw Fortunato advancing with
uncertain majesty. He wore the motley dress of a jester.
A close cap covered his head, and at each movement
its little silver bells announced him.

"Montresor!" he cried, seizing both my hands. "You are fortunate
to encounter me."

Fortunate! I admired the word.

He had drunk deeply. His eyes were clouded, and the
rouge upon his cheeks made his face resemble a painted
mask. Nevertheless, he stood before me with the solemnity of
a magistrate.

"My dear Fortunato," I said, "what happiness! And how remarkably
well the costume becomes you. But I fear I have
been cheated."

"Cheated?"

"I have received what the merchant declares to be Amontillado—a
full pipe."

He stared. The bells ceased.

"Amontillado?"

"So he swears. Yet the season is early, the price
was absurd, and I purchased without consulting you."

"A pipe? Impossible!"

"I thought so. I am on my way to Luchresi.
If any man can determine—"

"Luchresi!" Fortunato laughed. "He cannot distinguish Amontillado from common sherry."

"Some judge him your equal."

"Idiots judge many things."

"You are occupied, however. I should not detain you. Luchresi
will suffice."

"Take me to it."

"No, my friend. You are expected elsewhere, and I perceive
you have a cough."

He coughed at once, as though the malady, offended at
being forgotten, demanded acknowledgment. The fit bent him nearly double.
Bells shook; passers-by turned; he raised one finger, asking patience,
until the violence had spent itself.

"It is nothing," he said.

"The vaults are insufferably damp. They are thick with nitre.
I could not forgive myself if—"

"Amontillado!"

"Luchresi—"

"Luchresi is an ass. Come."

Thus did my concern persuade him where eagerness alone might
have failed.

My palazzo stood beyond the noisiest quarter, its windows dark,
its servants absent. I had informed them that I should
not return until morning and had strictly commanded them to
remain within the house. Such commands, given on a carnival
night, ensured their immediate departure.

We entered unobserved. From two iron brackets I took flambeaux,
giving one to Fortunato. He waved it carelessly, scattering sparks
upon the marble floor.

"The wine," he said.

"Below."

I led him through several chambers whose furnishings slept beneath
coverings of grey cloth. The uproar of the city diminished
behind us, becoming first a murmur, then a distant vibration,
then nothing. At the far end of the house I
opened a narrow door. Stone steps descended into blackness.

Fortunato lifted his torch. "Your cellars are deep."

"Our family is old."

He placed a hand upon my shoulder and began the
descent. The stair was steep, and his intoxication rendered him
careless. More than once he slipped, and the bells at
his cap rang brightly in that solemn passage. I followed
close enough to preserve him. It would have been regrettable
had he broken his neck.

At the bottom we entered the vaults of the Montresors.
The air was cold and heavy. Moisture shone on the
walls; white nitre spread across them in branching forms, like
veins upon a corpse. From niches and shelves the bones
of my ancestors regarded us with the indifference of the
long dead.

Fortunato stopped to cough.

The sound passed along the galleries, multiplying itself until the
catacombs seemed crowded with sufferers. He leaned against the wall
and pressed a hand to his breast.

"We will return," I said. "Your health is precious. You
are wealthy, admired, beloved, happy. I am not a man
to endanger so considerable a life. Besides, Luchresi—"

He seized my arm. "Enough of Luchresi. The cough will
not kill me."

"Certainly not."

The words pleased me exceedingly.

We proceeded. I opened a bottle of Médoc from a
rack and handed it to him. He drank without ceremony,
then wiped his lips upon his sleeve.

"To those who rest about us," he said.

"And I," I replied, "to your long life."

He laughed, and the bells answered.

The passages narrowed as we advanced. In places the ceiling
descended so low that we were obliged to stoop. Our
flambeaux burned weakly in the corrupt air, glowing rather than
flaming. Bones lay in ordered heaps beside the walls, though
now and then a skull had rolled into our path
and stared upward from the mud.

Fortunato regarded the nitre.

"How far do these vaults extend?"

"Beyond the foundations of the palazzo, beneath the old gardens,
and into the hill."

"You have a numerous family."

"Once."

"And your arms? I have forgotten them."

"A serpent, azure, crushed beneath a golden heel; its fangs
are fixed in the heel that crushes it."

"The motto?"

"'Nemo me impune lacessit.'"

He nodded gravely, as if approving a vintage. "Good. Very
good."

Another fit of coughing overtook him. I uncorked a flask
of De Grave and offered it. He emptied it, casting
the bottle over his shoulder. It shattered among the bones.
Then he made a curious gesture beneath his cloak, moving
his hands in a manner at once elaborate and secretive.

I looked at him without reply.

"You do not understand?" he asked.

"No."

"Then you are not of the brotherhood."

"The brotherhood?"

"The Masons."

"But surely," I said, "I am a mason."

He smiled with drunken suspicion. "Give me a sign."

From beneath my cloak I produced a small iron trowel.

For a moment his expression was vacant. Then he threw
back his head and laughed until the laughter dissolved into
coughing.

"You jest well, Montresor. Put it away. The Amontillado!"

"Yes," I said, replacing the trowel, "the Amontillado."

We went deeper.

The air became more oppressive, and droplets descended from the
roof with the regularity of a clock. Each step carried
us from sound, warmth, and human habitation. Yet Fortunato did
not hesitate. Pride walked before him with a torch, and
wine pushed from behind.

At length we reached the remotest crypt. Three walls were
lined with bones stacked to the ceiling. Before the fourth
lay a confused mound of skeletons, revealing a recess beyond.
It was perhaps four feet deep, scarcely three in width,
and high enough for a man to stand upright.

I raised my flambeau toward it.

"There," I said. "The Amontillado."

Fortunato peered into the darkness. "I see no cask."

"It lies within. The damp has blackened the wood."

He entered, holding his torch before him. In two steps
he reached the rock at the rear and stood bewildered.
Before he could turn, I had passed a chain about
his waist. Two iron staples had long ago been fixed
into the granite; the chain was short, the padlock ready.
A single motion secured him.

The flambeau fell from his hand and smouldered at his
feet.

At first he did not comprehend. He pulled once upon
the chain and looked down. Then he turned his face
slowly toward mine.

"What is this, Montresor?"

"The nitre," I said. "It is worse here than I
feared."

He tugged again, more violently. Iron struck stone. The sound
rang sharp and pure through the crypt.

"You have had your jest," he said. "Release me."

"Presently."

"The Amontillado?"

"Indeed."

I set my torch in a crevice and went to
the heap of bones. Behind them I had concealed stone
and mortar. I uncovered these materials and began my labour.

The first tier rose quickly.

Fortunato watched. His intoxication prevented him from understanding the whole,
though the parts were plain before him: the chain, the
stones, the mortar, the trowel. He gave a low laugh.

"An admirable trick," he said. "We shall recount it at
the palazzo. They will be waiting for us—my wife, our
friends. Come, Montresor."

I laid the second tier.

He shook the chain. "Enough."

I laid the third.

Then came a furious rattling. He threw his weight from
side to side, grinding the links against the staples. I
ceased my work and sat upon the bones to listen.
For several minutes the iron clashed and the bells jingled
wildly, producing a kind of infernal music. I hearkened until
it ended. Then I resumed.

At the fourth tier he began to shout.

These were not words, but long, savage cries, such as
no civilized throat should contain. They rushed along the catacombs
and returned weakened, as though the dead themselves had answered.
I paused, not from pity, but from a desire to
assure myself that no ear above could hear him.

Then I replied.

I shouted louder. I matched cry with cry, lengthening each
until his voice faltered. At last there was silence.

"The Amontillado!" I called.

No answer.

I completed the fifth tier, then the sixth. The wall
had risen to his breast. His face, lit from below
by the dying flambeau, no longer bore the expression of
drunken confusion. He had become sober. Terror is a swift
physician.

"Montresor," he said quietly.

I inclined my head.

"Let us speak plainly."

"At last."

"If I have offended you, I will make amends."

"Amends?"

"Gold, apology, influence—whatever you require."

I spread mortar upon a stone.

"You mistake the character of the account," I said. "It
is not payable in gold."

His lips moved. Perhaps he searched among past words and
gestures for the one that had purchased this conclusion. Perhaps
he found it. I have often wondered.

The seventh tier concealed his hands.

"Think," he said, "of the consequences."

"I have thought of nothing else."

"You cannot escape."

"I already have."

"You are mad."

"It is possible."

This answer seemed to frighten him more than denial would
have done.

I continued. The eighth tier rose; then the ninth. Only
a narrow opening remained, through which I could see his
eyes and the faded diamonds of his costume. The little
bells trembled whenever he breathed.

Suddenly he laughed.

It was a thin, broken sound, but unmistakably laughter.

"Ha! ha! An excellent jest! We will laugh over it
for years. Imagine their faces when we tell them! But
it grows late. My wife will be impatient. Let us
go."

"Yes," I said, "let us go."

"For the love of God, Montresor!"

"Yes," I said, "for the love of God."

He did not reply.

I waited. "Fortunato?"

No answer.

I thrust my torch through the opening. It fell within,
scattering sparks upon the damp floor. A faint jingling came
from the darkness.

My heart grew sick. The dampness of the catacombs affected
me.

I fitted the final stone into place and sealed it
carefully. Against the new masonry I restored the ancient rampart
of bones. No eye could distinguish that wall from any
other burial recess in the vaults. Above, carnival music still
troubled the dawn; below, all had become quiet.

For fifty years no mortal hand has displaced those bones.

You know now the justice, the method, and the perfection
of my redress. Whether Fortunato understood at the last is
a question I leave to God, to whom he appealed
too late.

In pace requiescat.

I

It was toward the end of October when the travelling
carriage, splashed to the windows with black mud, stopped before
the posting station at K—. The rain had ceased, but
a wet mist still hung over the road, over the
stripped birches, and over the low, dark fields, in which
the last patches of yellow stubble showed through the water.
The horses, steaming and gaunt from the heavy stage, lowered
their heads beneath the dripping harness; one of them shook
itself, and the bells gave a tired, uneven jingle.

The postmaster, a short man in a nankeen waistcoat, came
out upon the steps, shouting for fresh horses. Behind him
appeared two drivers, a barefoot boy, the cook with flour
upon her arms, and three curious little girls who had
been playing with nutshells beside the stove. All looked toward
the carriage.

First a maid descended. She was tall, broad-shouldered, and healthy;
her cheeks, heated by sleep and confinement, were of a
deep florid colour, her black eyes moved quickly, and beneath
her cloak her full bosom rose and fell with each
vigorous breath. She looked anxiously back into the carriage.

"Take care, Anna," said a weak voice. "Do not pull
me. Wait."

The maid bent forward, and a gentleman in a travelling
cap came round from the other side. Between them, slowly
and with much difficulty, they lifted out a woman wrapped
in shawls and furs. She was still young. Her fair
hair had been carefully arranged beneath a lace cap; but
the forehead under it had the transparency of wax, the
lips were dry, and a faint red patch, too brilliant
and too sharply marked, burned upon either cheek. When her
little foot touched the ground she trembled, pressed one wasted
hand against her chest, and looked angrily at her husband,
as though he, and not her illness, had made the
step too high.

"I told you," she whispered, "I told you I could
not bear another bad station."

"My angel, it is only for an hour," replied Pavel
Ivanitch. "The horses must rest. We shall have tea, and
then—"

"Tea! You always think tea can remedy everything."

The doctor, who had ridden in the second carriage, now
approached. He was a stout, clean-shaven man with a fur
collar and a smooth, composed face. He bowed, placed two
fingers upon the invalid's wrist, and said that the room
was warm and that repose was necessary.

Maria Dmitrievna allowed herself to be led inside.

The best room of the station was low and close.
It smelled of cabbage, damp sheepskins, candle grease, eau de
cologne, and the sour vapour of clothes dried too often
by the stove. A print of the Emperor hung crookedly
above a sofa covered in cracked black leather; on the
sill stood a dusty geranium, and flies, revived by the
heat, crawled heavily upon the window-glass. Anna spread a shawl
over the sofa, placed a pillow beneath her mistress's head,
opened a travelling case, and produced a silver scent bottle,
a little porcelain cup, medicines, linen, brushes, and a cushion
for the small white lap-dog which had travelled beneath Maria
Dmitrievna's cloak.

The dog shook its long hair and began sniffing about
the floor.

"Close the door," said Maria Dmitrievna. "Everyone is staring."

The three girls disappeared, though their eyes remained visible for
a moment through the crack.

She lay with her head raised, because she could no
longer breathe when flat. Every breath was drawn slowly, with
an inward effort which seemed to hollow the delicate spaces
above her collarbones. From time to time she coughed, at
first with two dry, cautious sounds, then more deeply; and
at each attack Anna held a handkerchief to her lips
and Pavel Ivanitch turned away toward the window. When it
was over, the red in her cheeks grew brighter, her
eyes shone, and she looked almost beautiful again.

"Give me the drops," she said.

"The doctor wished you to wait."

"I did not ask what he wished."

Anna poured the drops. Her large, steady hand and the
thin hand that received the glass seemed not to belong
to creatures of the same kind. Maria Dmitrievna noticed the
contrast. She noticed also the fresh colour in the girl's
neck, the firmness of her arms, and the way her
bodice strained when she bent down. Formerly she had been
pleased by Anna's health and had said that a sickly
maid was of no use on a journey. Now that
same health appeared to her insolent.

"You have been eating onions," she said.

"No, madam."

"Yes, you have. I can smell them. Go away."

Anna flushed and withdrew to the window.

Pavel Ivanitch and the doctor passed into the common room,
where the postmaster had ordered tea, eggs, and fresh bread.
The husband stood for some time without speaking. Through the
thin partition came the faint cough, the maid's low voice,
and the impatient tapping of a spoon against porcelain.

"Well?" he asked at last.

The doctor lifted his shoulders.

"What do you mean by that? We have been three
weeks upon the road. You said the southern air—"

"I said that if she reached the south before the
cold weather, a milder climate might lessen the irritation. I
did not say more."

"And now?"

The doctor poured tea, watching the dark stream rise in
the glass.

"Now I must tell you plainly, Pavel Ivanitch, that Italy
is impossible."

The husband sat down.

"Impossible?"

"She cannot support the journey. The roads beyond Moscow will
be worse, and the cold—You see her condition. It would
be fortunate if we reached Moscow."

"Fortunate?" Pavel Ivanitch repeated, and his lower lip trembled. "You
cannot mean—My God, she was dancing last winter. At the
governor's ball she danced twice with—My God! What is to
be done?"

The doctor spread butter upon a slice of bread.

"Return home. Keep her quiet. There is nothing else."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing that medicine can promise."

The postmaster entered with eggs in a chipped blue dish,
and the two gentlemen became silent. Pavel Ivanitch pushed his
plate away, then drew it back and ate mechanically, dipping
the hot bread in salt. He had eaten nothing since
dawn. The doctor, who had long ago learned that grief
does not suspend the needs of the stomach, finished one
egg and took another.

"What shall I tell her?" Pavel Ivanitch whispered.

"The truth, but gently."

"You do not know her. She has set her whole
mind upon Italy. The German physician assured her—"

"The German physician had not seen her in October."

"She will say it is because I am tired of
the expense. She already thinks—My God, what am I to
do?"

At that moment the door opened. Maria Dmitrievna stood upon
the threshold, supported by Anna. She had heard only the
murmur of voices, but the guilty stillness with which the
men looked at her told her more than words.

"What are you discussing?"

"Nothing, my angel. The horses."

"The horses do not make you whisper."

She came in, moving her feet beneath her wide travelling
skirt with the slow care of an old woman. Her
dog followed and sat beside her, looking up. Pavel Ivanitch
placed a chair, but she did not thank him.

"The doctor thinks," he began, "that perhaps, considering the season—"

"That perhaps what?"

"That it might be wiser to remain in Moscow for
the winter."

"Remain in Moscow?"

"Only until the weather—"

"We are going to Italy."

The doctor folded his napkin.

"Maria Dmitrievna, the journey is long, and your strength—"

"My strength is sufficient. I was weaker in August, and
Doctor Krause said that the first month would be the
hardest. Once beyond the mountains—"

"There are many stages before the mountains."

"And therefore I am to die in Moscow?" She smiled,
but her nostrils quivered. "That is convenient. My husband is
tired; Anna complains of the roads; you, doctor, prefer your
practice at home. All are tired except the person who
is ill."

"No one has said that," answered Pavel Ivanitch.

"You need not say it. I understand. I have understood
everything from the beginning."

She pressed the handkerchief to her mouth and coughed. The
cough bent her whole body; beneath the shawls her shoulders
jerked, and one small blue-veined hand clutched at the table.
When she raised her head, there was blood upon the
linen. Anna saw it and turned pale. Maria Dmitrievna folded
the handkerchief quickly.

"It is nothing," she said. "The road shakes me."

The doctor approached, but she held out her hand to
prevent him.

"Do not touch me. You have all agreed. You think
I am dying."

"My dear friend," Pavel Ivanitch said, "we think only of
saving you."

"Then take me where I may be saved."

Her eyes passed from one face to another. They were
large, frightened, and accusatory. She knew what their silence meant;
she had known it during the nights when she listened
alone to the bubbling in her chest, during the mornings
when her rings slipped from her fingers, during the moments
when Anna, believing her asleep, crossed herself after emptying the
basin. Yet knowledge admitted for an instant was immediately driven
out by another thought: Krause had named a lady who
recovered at Pisa; a princess had written of grape cures;
an old aunt had lived twenty years with only one
lung. There must be air somewhere, some medicine, some physician
not lazy or stupid, some warm garden in which she
would walk again. She was only thirty-four. Her children needed
her. Her blue dress had been left unfinished at home.
It was impossible.

"I shall go on," she said.

Pavel Ivanitch covered his face.

"You wish me dead," she added softly.

He sprang up.

"Maria! How can you? Before God—"

"Do not swear. Order the horses."

She turned away. The movement exhausted her, and before she
reached the door she sank upon the chair that had
been placed for her. Anna knelt, loosened the shawl, and
rubbed her cold hands. The doctor looked at Pavel Ivanitch
and shook his head almost imperceptibly.

Outside, the drivers shouted, shafts knocked against the carriage, wet
harness creaked, and the bells of another team were heard
approaching through the mist. Life at the station, delayed for
a moment by the sick lady's arrival, resumed its ordinary
noise.

Fresh horses were not ready for two hours. Dusk descended
early. The mist thickened into rain, the road vanished beneath
the dark, and the postmaster declared that the next bridge
might be flooded. After much anger, pleading, and another attack
of coughing, Maria Dmitrievna consented to remain for the night.

In the best room candles were lighted. Anna brushed her
mistress's hair, warmed her nightdress, and arranged the pillows. Pavel
Ivanitch sat by the stove, sighing. He wanted to be
tender, but every word he chose seemed false before it
was spoken. Maria Dmitrievna watched him.

"You regret coming with me," she said.

"No, my angel."

"You are thinking of home."

"I am thinking of you."

"Then do not sit so far away."

He drew his chair nearer. She gave him her hand.
For several minutes both were silent. The hand was hot,
dry, and almost weightless.

In the yard, a man coughed—a deep, hoarse cough, rougher
than hers and without restraint.

"Who is that?" she asked.

"One of the drivers," Anna replied.

"Tell him to go elsewhere. I cannot endure it."

The coughing ceased, then began again behind the wall.

II

The man who coughed was Theodore, a post-driver of forty,
though his stooped back, dim eyes, and grey-threaded red beard
made him appear older. All summer he had complained of
pain beneath the shoulder blade. In September he could still
harness a team, though he rested against the horses while
tightening the straps; by October he no longer drove, but
lay upon the brick ledge of the common stove, covered
with a greasy sheepskin coat, his boots placed upright below
him.

The boots were new. He had bought them at the
summer fair, when he still believed he would be driving
freight in winter. They were thick, high, and well greased,
with iron-shod heels. Everyone at the station had examined them.
Theodore himself had worn them only twice.

While the lady in the next room was being put
to bed, Serega, a young driver with pale eyelashes and
a torn blue shirt, came in from the yard. He
stamped the mud from his bast shoes, blew upon his
fingers, and looked toward the stove.

"You're making noise again, uncle," he said.

Theodore opened his eyes.

"Was I?"

"Coughing. The gentlefolk complain."

"Let them complain."

"You'll have us all turned out."

The cook, who was kneading dough at the table, glanced
over her shoulder.

"Where is he to take his cough? Put it in
his pocket, perhaps?"

"He might go to the hospital," said Serega.

"The hospital!" Theodore muttered. "You take me."

"There are no horses to waste on that."

"Then leave me."

Serega sat upon a bench and began winding fresh straw
around his feet. His own boots had split at the
sole and hung from a peg above him, useless. He
glanced several times at Theodore's boots.

Theodore noticed.

"You keep looking," he said.

"They're good boots."

"They are."

"And no use to you now."

The cook stopped kneading.

"Shame on you, Serega."

"What shame? He knows himself. What will boots do in
a grave?"

Theodore's hairy hand emerged from beneath the sheepskin. It had
once been broad and powerful; now the knuckles rose like
knots beneath loose skin. He felt along the stove ledge
for a cup, found it empty, and set it down.

"Give me water."

Serega brought the wooden dipper. Theodore drank with difficulty, the
water clicking in his throat. A little ran into his
beard.

"You want the boots?" he asked.

"I could use them. Mine won't last till snow."

"Buy them."

"With what?"

Theodore closed his eyes again. His breathing filled the pause,
slow, scraping, and uneven.

"You'll bury me?" he said.

"They'll bury you."

"Who?"

"The commune. The postmaster. Whoever does it."

"No one owes me."

The cook wiped her floury hands upon her apron.

"We won't throw you to the dogs, Theodore."

"I've no fear of dogs." He paused. "My father had
a stone. In our village. A good stone, standing upright.
They carved a cross on it."

Serega laughed.

"You want a stone?"

"I want something. So a man knows where I lie."

"What man will come looking?"

Theodore did not answer at once. Rain beat upon the
roof, and the flame under the pot hissed where water
had spilled. From the best room came the delicate ringing
of a spoon.

"My brother may come," Theodore said. "He went to the
Don. Or his boy may come."

"Your brother's dead, likely."

"Likely. But put a stone."

"A stone costs money."

"Then a wooden cross."

"You said stone."

"I say stone if the boots are worth it."

Serega looked again at the boots. He took one down,
weighed it in his hand, bent the leather at the
ankle, and examined the sole.

"They've been damp."

"Grease them."

"The right one pinches."

"Then don't take them."

Serega put the boot down.

"What kind of stone?"

"One from the mason beyond the churchyard. Not too small.
A cross scratched in it, and Theodore Nikititch if they'll
do letters."

"For those boots? Letters too?"

"Then no letters. A stone."

The cook shook her head.

"He will deceive you."

Theodore looked at Serega. His sunken eyes, usually clouded by
fever, became attentive.

"Will you?"

"Why should I deceive? Give them."

"After."

"After what?"

"After I'm dead."

"What use is that? Someone else will take them."

Theodore considered. The station servants slept in the same room;
possessions without a living owner did not long remain ownerless.
He raised himself on one elbow, but the effort brought
on coughing. His face darkened, his eyes filled with water,
and the breath whistled in his chest. Serega stood waiting
with the boot in his hand.

At last Theodore sank back.

"Take them now," he whispered. "Before the cook and postmaster."

"You hear?" Serega said. "He gives them."

"I hear," replied the cook. "And God hears what you
promise."

"I promise a stone."

"A proper one," Theodore said.

"A proper one."

Serega pulled off his bast shoes. The boots were too
large at the heel and too narrow across the toes,
but with more straw they sat firmly. He stood, stamped
once, then twice, and smiled.

"Fine boots."

Theodore watched the living feet walk about in them. He
seemed pleased, or perhaps only tired.

"Grease them," he said again.

That evening the station grew crowded. A merchant arrived with
bales of cloth and slept upon a bench; two soldiers
escorting a prisoner were given a corner near the door;
the drivers came and went with wet shoulders, bringing draughts
of cold air, the smell of horses, and scraps of
news from the road. They ate cabbage soup, black bread,
and onions. Theodore accepted half a cup of broth but
could not swallow it.

The wealthy travellers' servants received roast fowl, white rolls, and
wine from their own provisions. Anna came into the common
room for boiling water. The drivers stared at her bright
face and fine wool dress. Serega, proud of the boots,
stretched his legs into the light.

"Who is coughing here?" Anna asked.

All looked toward the stove.

"My mistress cannot sleep."

"Nor can he," said the cook.

"Could he not be moved?"

"To where?"

Anna saw Theodore then: the red beard upon the sheepskin
collar, the hollow temples, the open mouth, and the hand
hanging over the edge of the stove. For a moment
disgust and pity struggled in her face.

"I will ask the doctor to see him," she said.

When the doctor came, Theodore was already half asleep. He
listened to his chest, asked several questions, and prescribed warmth,
broth, and rest. The cook laughed when he had gone.

"Warmth! He lies on the stove. Rest! He hasn't stood
up in a week. Broth! Will your doctor buy the
hen?"

Theodore smiled faintly.

"He knows."

"What does he know?" Serega asked.

"That I'll die."

No one contradicted him.

In the next room Pavel Ivanitch walked up and down
until after midnight. Maria Dmitrievna had taken laudanum and lay
with her eyes closed. The dog slept curled at her
feet. Now and then she asked whether the rain had
stopped, whether horses could be obtained at dawn, whether the
doctor had written to Moscow, whether Anna had packed the
blue case. Each question was answered gently. Each answer seemed
to trouble her.

At last she slept.

Beyond the partition the merchant snored, the prisoner muttered, the
soldiers shifted their muskets, and one of the drivers cursed
in a dream. The stove gave out a thick, oppressive
heat. Theodore lay turned toward the wall. Once he asked
for water, but no one heard. Once he tried to
raise his head and looked toward the place where his
boots had stood. In the darkness Serega's feet, now wearing
those boots even in sleep, projected from beneath a coat.

Near morning Theodore's breathing became softer. The pauses between breaths
grew longer, yet because the room was full of other
sounds no one noticed. He did not call upon God,
did not speak of the stone, and did not struggle.
He sighed once, stretched his fingers upon the warm brick,
and became still.

At dawn the cook rose to light the fire. She
crossed herself before the icons, put wood under the pot,
and called:

"Theodore, will you have tea?"

There was no answer.

She climbed upon the bench and touched his shoulder.

"Theodore!"

Serega sat up.

"What is it?"

"He's cold."

The merchant ceased snoring. The soldiers looked over. The cook
drew the sheepskin away and placed her ear to Theodore's
chest.

"He is dead," she said.

For a little while no one moved. Then the postmaster
was called, the body was lowered from the stove, and
the place was needed for the merchant, who complained that
his back had been broken by the bench.

Maria Dmitrievna's carriage departed after sunrise. The sky was still
grey, but the rain had stopped. As the servants carried
her out, wrapped in white fur, she saw through the
open door a long shape on the common-room floor, covered
with a coarse cloth.

"What is that?" she asked.

"The sick driver died," Anna replied.

Maria Dmitrievna shuddered.

"Why did you tell me? Put me in quickly."

The carriage door closed. Bells sounded; wheels sank into the
mud; the horses strained, and the station disappeared behind the
mist.

Theodore was buried two days later behind the birch woods,
in the portion of ground used for men who had
no family in the village. The postmaster paid the priest's
clerk, but not the priest. The cook brought a little
loaf. Serega drove the cart and wore the new boots.
Earth fell upon the boards with a hollow sound.

No stone was placed there.

III

Maria Dmitrievna reached Moscow, but she did not reach Italy.
From Moscow she was taken home in a sleigh after
the first snow had hardened the roads, and all winter
she remained in the large bedroom overlooking the garden.

By March she no longer left her bed. The room,
with its rose-coloured curtains, gilded chairs, family portraits, medicine bottles,
prayer books, silver trays, and bowls of forced hyacinths, had
acquired the fixed and oppressive arrangement of a sickroom. Everything
necessary stood near at hand; everything was clean, soft, expensive,
and useless. A fire burned day and night. Cologne was
sprinkled upon handkerchiefs. The doctor came each morning, another physician
twice each week, and a celebrated specialist from Moscow once
a month. They listened, tapped, whispered, and altered the medicines.
She grew steadily weaker.

Outside, spring began.

Snow slid from the iron roof with a rushing sound;
brown water ran beneath the garden gates; the lindens showed
small swelling buds, and sparrows fought among the wet branches.
Servants crossed the courtyard without fur caps. The children, released
from lessons, launched paper boats in the gutter and shouted
whenever one was drawn beneath the ice.

Maria Dmitrievna heard them.

"Tell them to be quiet," she said.

Anna, still florid and strong, closed the window.

"No. Open it again. There is no air."

The window was opened.

"It is cold. Do you want me to catch inflammation?"

Anna closed it once more.

Nothing could be done correctly. The broth was greasy; the
jelly too sweet; the pillow too high and then too
low. Pavel Ivanitch entered too noisily and sat too silently.
When he went to the estate office, she asked why
he deserted her; when he remained beside her, she saw
by the heaviness of his eyes that he wished to
leave.

Her face had lost even the feverish beauty of autumn.
The nose had sharpened, the temples had sunk, and the
skin around her mouth was grey. Her hair, however, was
combed every morning and arranged beneath a cap with lilac
ribbons. She insisted upon the ribbons. She had once heard
that violet shades suited consumptives.

"Where is Pavel?" she asked one afternoon.

"In his room, madam."

"Send him."

He came wearing a black coat and carrying a packet
of estate papers which he hid behind his back.

"You were occupied?"

"No, my angel."

"You always say no. I know I am a burden."

He bent and kissed her forehead.

"How do you feel?"

"As I felt yesterday, and the day before. Your doctor
comes, looks at me, and goes away. Has the prescription
arrived from Tula?"

"What prescription?"

"The one the deacon's wife spoke of. The infusion of
pine shoots in goat's milk."

Pavel Ivanitch looked toward Anna.

"I sent a man," Anna said. "The woman lives beyond
the river."

"And?"

"She will prepare it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow!" Maria Dmitrievna's dry lips trembled. "Everything is tomorrow. If
I die tonight, you will all be very sorry tomorrow."

"My friend," Pavel Ivanitch said, "the archpriest is here."

She looked at him without understanding.

"He came only to visit," he added quickly.

"To visit?"

"And if you wished—only if you wished—to hear confession and
receive the Holy Gifts."

Her eyes widened. For several seconds she seemed not to
breathe.

"So you have sent for a priest."

"The doctor thought—"

"The doctor! The doctor who promised that I should improve
in spring?"

"No one wishes to alarm you."

"You have all condemned me."

Pavel Ivanitch knelt beside the bed and seized her hand.

"Maria, for God's sake, do not speak so. Every Christian
receives the sacrament. I shall receive it with you if
you wish."

"You are not dying."

The word, spoken aloud, filled the room. Anna lowered her
eyes. Beyond the door one of the children laughed, and
a nurse immediately hushed him.

Maria Dmitrievna turned her face to the wall.

"I will see Father Vasily," she said after a long
time.

The room was prepared. A white cloth was spread upon
the little table; candles were lighted before the icons; the
medicine bottles and soiled cups were carried away. Father Vasily
entered in a dark cassock, his grey hair falling upon
his collar. He had baptised Maria Dmitrievna's children and had
dined many times at their table. Today he looked older
and more solemn, but his familiar round hands, with their
broad nails, comforted her.

Everyone withdrew.

At first she spoke coldly and correctly. She accused herself
of impatience, pride, anger toward servants, neglect of prayer. The
words were those she knew should be spoken. But as
the priest listened, the reserve broke. She told him that
she feared the night; that she could not believe God
would take her from her children; that she envied every
healthy peasant woman she saw from the window; that she
hated Anna's red cheeks; that she had suspected Pavel Ivanitch
of wishing to marry again; that when people said "God's
will" she wanted to strike them.

Father Vasily did not appear shocked.

"Fear is natural to the flesh," he said. "But the
soul must learn to submit."

"I have submitted," she answered quickly. "I have always submitted.
Yet is it wrong to wish to live?"

"No."

"Then pray that I may recover."

"We shall pray for mercy."

"For recovery."

"For that which His mercy knows to be best."

She frowned.

"Why will no one say plainly that I may recover?"

The priest was silent.

Then she wept. The tears did not flow freely but
gathered slowly beneath her eyelids and ran toward her ears.
Father Vasily spoke of the thief upon the cross, of
the life that does not end, of the vanity of
earthly fear, and of the peace granted to those who
surrender themselves. As he spoke, her breathing became quieter. The
familiar sacred words, the candle flames, the scent of incense,
and the grave kindness of the old man gave form
to the terror which had until then been formless. She
confessed again, this time without choosing her faults, and when
the family entered she looked gentle and exhausted.

Pavel Ivanitch, Anna, the children, the old nurse, and several
household servants stood behind the priest. The youngest child, a
boy of six, stared at the gold embroidery of the
vestment until his nurse pressed his head down.

Maria Dmitrievna received the sacrament.

Afterward she lay motionless, her hands crossed upon the sheet.
A strange brightness appeared in her eyes.

"I am at peace," she said. "God's will be done."

Pavel Ivanitch sobbed and kissed her hand. Anna wept openly.
Even the doctor, who arrived while the candles still burned,
walked softly and spoke with unusual warmth.

For nearly an hour Maria Dmitrievna said nothing. She looked
toward the icons, then toward the square of pale blue
sky visible between the curtains. She imagined death no longer
as a black pit but as something solemn, accompanied by
candles, prayer, and the loving faces of those who would
remember her. She forgave Pavel Ivanitch. She even looked kindly
at Anna.

"Bring the children nearer."

They came. The elder girl kissed her mother's fingers and
began to cry. The younger children, frightened by the room,
stood stiffly. Maria Dmitrievna blessed them one after another.

"Obey your father. Remember me. Do not quarrel."

The boy's attention wandered to the lap-dog scratching at the
door.

"May I take Fifi?" he whispered.

The nurse pulled him back, horrified. Maria Dmitrievna smiled faintly.

"Yes. Take her."

When the room had emptied, she closed her eyes. Anna
thought she slept. Outside, drops fell from the eaves in
a regular, cheerful rhythm.

Presently Maria Dmitrievna opened her eyes.

"Anna."

"Yes, madam?"

"Has the man returned from the deacon's wife?"

"Not yet."

"Send another horse."

Anna hesitated.

"You heard me. The pine shoots must be young, not
dried. And the goat must be black. She said a
black goat, did she not?"

"I believe so."

"Then why do you stand there?"

Anna went.

The peace of the sacrament had not vanished entirely; she
still believed that she accepted God's will. But perhaps God's
will was that the humble remedy, despised by learned physicians,
should save her. Such things had happened. The deacon's wife
had cured a merchant's daughter after the doctors abandoned her.
They had all abandoned her too soon. Pavel, the doctor,
even Father Vasily—kind, all of them, but tired, resigned, ready
to bury her. She would drink the milk; the cough
would soften; by summer she might sit upon the terrace.
Their astonishment would prove how little human wisdom understood.

Toward evening the infusion arrived in an earthenware jug. It
smelled of resin, warm milk, smoke, and the stable. Maria
Dmitrievna insisted upon drinking it at once.

The doctor objected.

"It can do no good."

"Then it can do no harm."

"It may increase the nausea."

"You said there was nothing more to be done. Why
do you care?"

She drank three spoonfuls, grimaced, and demanded a fourth. Soon
afterward she was seized by coughing. Blood came freely now,
bright upon the white cloth. Pavel Ivanitch ran into the
corridor calling for the doctor, though the doctor stood beside
him. Anna supported her mistress's head. The children, hearing the
confusion, gathered outside until the nurse drove them away.

For two hours Maria Dmitrievna struggled for breath. The composed
words of the afternoon were forgotten. She grasped at Anna's
arms, pushed away the priest when he returned, begged the
doctor not to let her suffocate, called Pavel Ivanitch by
name, and repeated, "I do not want to, I do
not want to, I do not want to."

No one could answer.

At sunset the clouds broke. A broad red light entered
the garden, shone upon the wet trunks, and touched the
upper wall of the bedroom. The candles before the icons
were again lighted, though their flames were invisible in the
brightness.

Maria Dmitrievna suddenly ceased struggling. Her mouth remained open. One
hand made a small motion upon the sheet, as if
searching for something it had dropped. Then the hand was
still.

The doctor leaned over her.

Pavel Ivanitch waited.

"Well?" he whispered.

The doctor straightened and closed her eyelids.

Maria Dmitrievna had become a corpse.

That night the body was washed, dressed in a white
silk gown, and laid beneath the icons. A lace cap
concealed the thin hair; flowers were placed around the head;
the hands, which would no longer push away medicine, reproach
a servant, caress a child, or clutch at the sheet,
were folded around a small painted image. With the mouth
closed and the face arranged by others, she appeared calmer
than she had appeared in many months.

The house filled with visitors. Ladies came in black veils,
crossed themselves, whispered of her youth and beauty, and asked
the doctor whether Italy might have saved her. Gentlemen stood
near Pavel Ivanitch, pressing his hand and speaking of Christian
consolation. He received them with swollen eyes. At intervals he
went into his study, where food had been placed, and
ate a little cold meat or drank tea without knowing
that he did so.

The children were brought in to say farewell. The elder
girl sobbed sincerely. The younger stared at the waxen profile
and afterward ran into the corridor to play. The boy
asked why his mother's ring had been removed. That evening
the children quarrelled over Fifi, and their noise was heard
in the room where the psalter was being read.

A hired clerk sat near the bier and recited in
a rapid, nasal voice. He stumbled over the words, yawned,
and from time to time glanced toward the servant who
was to relieve him. The psalm spoke of man as
grass, of the earth renewed, of breath departing and returning
no more. Did she, whose jewels lay locked in the
cabinet, whose dresses hung in the wardrobe, whose remedy still
stood souring in the jug, understand those words now? The
candles burned evenly. The corpse gave no sign.

The funeral was magnificent. Six horses drew the hearse. Choirboys
in bright surplices walked before it; bells sounded above the
thawing town; carriages extended far along the street. Mud splashed
the wheels, but carpets had been laid from the church
door to the road. The coffin was lowered into a
brick-lined grave in the family plot, beside Maria Dmitrievna's father
and an infant sister whom she had never known.

Within a month a marble monument had been ordered from
Moscow. Before summer it stood above the grave: a white
angel bent over a broken column, and beneath it, in
gilded letters, were her name, her titles, the dates of
her birth and death, and a verse concerning eternal remembrance.

IV

The snow melted behind the birch woods later than it
did in the open fields. Water gathered in Theodore's sunken
grave, then dried; last year's grass lay flat upon it,
and new green blades pushed through. No stone stood there.
No cross marked the place.

Serega had worn the boots all winter. The right one
had ceased pinching; the leather had softened, and one heel
had already been repaired. Whenever he passed the mason's yard
beyond the church, he remembered the promise and calculated the
cost. A stone of proper size cost two roubles, the
cutting of a cross thirty kopecks, and letters more. There
was always something more urgent: oats, vodka at Shrovetide, a
shirt, payment to the blacksmith. Theodore, being dead, could wait.

The cook reminded him.

"You took the boots."

"I know."

"You promised before God."

"I'll do it."

"You said that before Christmas."

"The ground was frozen."

"It is thawed now."

"I have work."

In May the postmaster himself spoke.

"Put up something, Serega. Travellers wander there, and soon no
one will know the grave."

"A stone costs too much."

"Then make a cross."

"He bargained for stone."

"A wooden cross is better than weeds."

Serega scratched his head. He disliked being reminded, not because
he had forgotten, but because every reminder made the boots
feel as though they did not belong to him. That
afternoon, after driving the mail team, he fetched an axe
from the shed and went toward the woods.

The day was clear and still. Young grass shone beside
the road; larks hung above the fields, pouring out song;
from the damp hollows came the smell of water, leaf
mould, and the first wild mint. The birches were covered
in small, translucent leaves, each leaf distinct against the blue,
and farther within the forest the firs stood dark, while
hazel, bird cherry, and young aspen crowded wherever the sunlight
entered.

Serega walked without haste. His boots sank softly into the
moist ground. He wanted a straight young tree, not too
thick to cut and carry, but thick enough to make
a respectable cross.

At the edge of a clearing he found one: a
young ash, taller than the surrounding shrubs, with a smooth
grey trunk and a crown newly opened to the spring.
It had grown close beside an older tree, bending slightly
outward in search of light. Its roots held firmly to
the earth; sap moved beneath its bark; on one branch
a small bird perched, called twice, and flew away.

Serega spat upon his hands and raised the axe.

The first blow made a pale wound in the bark.
The tree scarcely moved. A chip fell among the violets.

He struck again.

The measured sound passed through the forest—one sharp knock, then
silence; another knock, then the faint stirring of leaves high
above. With each blow the notch widened, exposing wet, fragrant
wood. Serega paused, touched the edge of the axe, and
listened. Somewhere a cuckoo called. Farther off, another axe answered,
duller and slower.

He resumed.

The young ash trembled now at every stroke. Its upper
branches shook though there was no wind, and sunlight flashed
between the leaves. Ants ran along the wounded trunk. A
thread of sap appeared at the edge of the cut
and gathered dust.

Serega moved to the other side. He judged the direction
of the fall, chopped away a bush, and began again.
Sweat darkened his shirt between the shoulders. The boots, Theodore's
boots, stood apart upon crushed grass, firm and useful.

At last there came a sound unlike the sound of
the axe: a dry inward crack.

Serega stepped back.

The tree remained upright for one moment, balanced upon the
uncut fibres. Its crown seemed to rise, then slowly inclined.
The leaves rustled with increasing agitation; slender branches caught at
neighbouring branches as though seeking support; the trunk split, tore,
and with a long rushing sigh the whole tree descended.
It struck the earth heavily. Smaller boughs snapped beneath it,
leaves flew up, and the bird that had returned to
the older tree darted across the clearing.

The ash lay still.

Where its crown had been, an open space now appeared
in the forest. Sunlight, until then broken and green, poured
through the gap and illuminated the straight trunks behind it,
the white bark of a birch, the red cones upon
a fir, the trembling new leaves, and the floor of
the clearing strewn with chips. The surrounding trees, relieved of
the young crown that had pressed among them, spread their
branches into the brightness. Birds flew from one illuminated twig
to another and sang. A breeze entered the opening and
moved through the leaves of the fallen tree, so that
they whispered and glittered as though life still continued in
them.

Serega wiped the axe upon the grass. He trimmed the
branches, measured the trunk with his arm, and cut from
it two pieces. These he would shape at the station
and fasten across one another with a wooden peg. The
remaining crown, green and fragrant, he left upon the ground.

He lifted the two lengths to his shoulder and started
back.

Behind him the stump shone pale in the sun. Beetles
moved over the exposed wood. The bruised violets slowly raised
their heads. Above, through the new opening, the sky was
deeper and more spacious than before.

By evening a rough cross stood over Theodore's grave. It
bore no name. Serega pressed the soil around it with
the heels of the boots and regarded his work.

"That will do," he said.

The cook, who had come carrying a small tin lamp,
crossed herself.

"At least now a man can see where he lies."

The cross leaned slightly toward the woods. Beyond it the
felled ash, invisible among the other trees, was already losing
the brightness of its leaves. In the town, the marble
angel over Maria Dmitrievna's grave shone white in the setting
sun, its gilded letters distinct and enduring. Here there were
no letters, no angel, no broken column—only the raw wood,
the grass, and the mound settling into the earth.

Night came warmly. Frogs called from the low ground. The
last birds fell silent, then began again before dawn. Mist
rose over the fields and withdrew. When the sun entered
the forest, it touched the stump, the fallen crown, the
cross behind the woods, and the wet marble in the
distant churchyard with the same light.

The living leaves opened farther.

The birds sang.

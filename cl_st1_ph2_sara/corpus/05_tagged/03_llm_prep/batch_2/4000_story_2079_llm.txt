By the middle of April our campaigns in the waste
ground behind Connolly's timber yard had begun to repeat themselves.
Every Wednesday, unless rain converted the ground into a brown
pond, we assembled after dinner with sticks, wooden pistols and
feathers cut from a bolster in Leo Byrne's house. Leo
commanded the settlers; Mahony commanded the Indians; and I, because
I possessed two volumes of frontier tales and knew the
proper names of several tribes, was permitted to change sides
according to the necessities of the battle.

Leo's younger brother, Peter, acted as scout. He was a
small, pale boy who could creep through the nettles without
stirring them, though in ordinary life he complained if his
boots were muddy. Mahony, on the contrary, rushed from concealment
with such terrible cries that no surprise was ever possible.
He wore an old tea-cosy as a war bonnet and
struck prisoners on the calves until they agreed to be
scalped. It was commonly believed that he would come to
a bad end. Two years later he entered a religious
college.

At first the games satisfied me. They lent a savage
colour to streets which, in daylight, seemed condemned to respectability.
A stable door became a stockade; the canal bank became
a range of mountains; a broken drainpipe, discovered beneath weeds,
served as the mouth of a secret passage. But the
endings were always known. Mahony was defeated, or Leo was
captured, or Peter appeared with news that his mother wanted
him. Even the deaths lacked conviction. A slain warrior would
rise to dispute the ownership of a stick.

I began to desire an adventure which had not been
arranged beforehand.

This desire was strengthened by the books I read under
the bedclothes. Their heroes escaped from schools, crossed seas as
cabin boys, slept in forests and heard conspiracies through the
walls of inns. In the morning I carried their liberty
with me as far as the classroom, where it expired
beneath the smell of damp coats, chalk and old ink.
Father Butler's voice completed its extinction.

He was a broad, clean-shaven priest with pale eyes and
a habit of suspending a boy's answer while he inspected
the boy's boots. Poverty, in his judgment, was not an
excuse for ignorance but its visible uniform. One morning he
discovered a coloured weekly hidden inside Doyle's Roman History. He
lifted it between finger and thumb.

"What is this rubbish?"

No one answered.

"Redskins! Detectives! Highway robbers!" he said, turning the pages. "Is
this what the sons of decent Catholic parents feed their
minds upon?"

His eyes travelled over us and rested on Mahony, perhaps
because Mahony's collar was frayed.

"I suppose you know all about such trash."

Mahony looked straight ahead.

"Answer me, boy."

"No, Father."

"No, Father," the priest repeated. "You know nothing, naturally. Close
that mouth. A gentleman does not stand with his mouth
open."

The magazine belonged to me. I felt a mean gratitude
that Doyle, who had borrowed it, did not betray me;
but I also felt that Father Butler's contempt for the
adventures gave them an authority which they had not possessed
before. During the geography lesson I looked at the blue
edge of the map and resolved to escape, if only
for one day, from the restraining influence of school.

I communicated my plan to Mahony and Leo Byrne. We
were to meet on Thursday at ten o'clock beside the
clockmaker's shop in Dorset Street. Each boy was to bring
sixpence and some food. To avoid inquiry, we were to
leave home at the ordinary hour with our books, separate
before reaching school, and hide the books in a place
selected by Mahony. From Dorset Street we would go eastward
to the docks, cross the river by the ferry, and
walk as far as the Pigeon House. The name itself
had for us the remoteness of an island.

Leo proposed that we should bring pistols. I opposed this
because pistols, even wooden ones, would attract notice. Mahony said
we could take stones if there was trouble. Differences of
courage and equipment having been settled, we bound ourselves not
to "funk" at the last moment. For greater security we
chose false names. Mahony was to be Murphy, Leo was
to be Nolan, and I was to be Reynolds.

On Thursday morning I left home with a volume of
Euclid and my Latin grammar strapped together. My mother gave
me bread spread with dripping and asked why I was
early. I said there would be Mass before lessons. The
lie made the hallway seem unusually silent. For several minutes
after I had closed the door I expected her to
call me back.

The morning was bright and mild. Milk carts rattled over
the stones, and shopkeepers in shirt sleeves were raising their
shutters. I passed two boys from my class and turned
into a side street before they could see me. At
the rear of a disused coach house Mahony was waiting.
We pushed our books through a gap in the door
and covered them with a piece of sacking. Freed from
them, we felt at once lighter and conspicuous.

We reached the clockmaker's shop before ten. In its window
a dozen watches displayed different hours, and beneath them a
mechanical sailor, faded by the sun, raised and lowered a
telescope. Mahony bought two penny cakes from a dairy and
ate one immediately. We waited for Leo.

At five past ten Mahony said Leo had funked.

I defended Leo and suggested his father might have kept
him.

At a quarter past, Mahony pressed his face to the
clockmaker's glass and made remarks about the sailor. At half
past, when the clocks began striking in disagreement, we abandoned
our companion. I was disappointed, for three seemed the proper
number for an expedition; yet Leo's absence also made the
enterprise more secret. We set out without him, speaking loudly
and carelessly, as if the city belonged to boys who
had no business in it.

We went by way of Gardiner Street, where the sunlight
slanted between the houses and shone upon the young leaves
in the square. Women carrying market baskets passed us; a
messenger cycled by with a tray balanced on his head;
from an upper window came the thin practising of scales.
Every adult appeared to possess an appointment, and their occupation
made our idleness delicious.

Near Amiens Street we saw a school line turning a
corner under the charge of a lay brother. We slipped
behind a brewer's dray until they had passed. The dray
horses stood patiently beneath their brass ornaments, and the heavy
smell of them mixed with malt, smoke and the salt
breath coming from the river. Mahony laughed, but my heart
continued beating hard after danger was gone.

Beyond the railway bridge the streets widened and lost their
domestic appearance. There were fewer women and more carts. Warehouses
rose behind walls stained with rain; chains clanked in open
yards; men rolled barrels along planks, calling to one another
in voices roughened by the air. We walked down to
the Custom House and lingered beside the quay.

The river was brown and glittering. A tug moved slowly
against the current, leaving a black ribbon of smoke, and
behind it a barge loaded with timber seemed to advance
without effort. On the farther side, funnels, cranes and masts
were crowded together. Their complexity delighted me. Each rope and
spar appeared to belong to a system which extended beyond
Dublin, beyond England, into regions coloured yellow and red on
the classroom map.

We followed the quays eastward. At a ship chandler's shop
Mahony stopped to examine knives, lamps and coils of rope.
In another window hard biscuits lay in white heaps beside
tins printed with foreign words. A sailor with gold rings
in his ears came out of a public house and
spat into the gutter. Mahony said he was probably a
Spaniard. I thought he looked like a man from Liverpool,
but I did not say so. It was necessary that
the docks should furnish foreigners.

Presently we saw three sailors leaning over the rail of
a steamer. One was dark, one yellow-haired, and one had
a red handkerchief tied round his throat. Mahony shouted a
greeting which he believed to be Italian. The dark sailor
answered with a laugh and called something down to us.

"What did he say?" Mahony asked.

I pretended not to have heard.

The sailor pointed eastward and made a gesture as if
drinking. His companions laughed. We went on, pleased to have
entered, however briefly, into communication with another nation.

At the ferry we paid a halfpenny each. The boat
was crowded with labourers, a woman carrying a basket of
fish, and two soldiers who smoked without speaking. As we
crossed, the Custom House dome withdrew behind smoke and the
city seemed flattened against the sky. I watched the water
slap the black side of the boat and imagined it
widening into the sea. For a moment I saw myself
at the rail of a vessel while Dublin diminished to
a grey line, and I experienced the melancholy proper to
departure without enduring any of its inconvenience.

On the southern bank we ate our bread upon a
low wall. Mahony had brought currant buns, which were compressed
in his pocket but still agreeable. We drank water from
a public fountain and began walking toward Ringsend.

The day had grown warmer. Coal dust lay upon the
road; carts groaned past us; dogs slept in the narrow
shadows of doorways. Children played barefoot around heaps of broken
stone and pursued us, calling, "School, school!" Mahony turned and
threatened them with a brick. They retreated at once, except
for a red-haired girl who put out her tongue.

"Dirty lot," Mahony said.

I agreed, though their liberty resembled ours more closely than
I cared to admit.

At the bridge we asked an old man the road
to the Pigeon House. He inspected us slowly and said
it was a long road for two young gentlemen. The
phrase encouraged me. We walked on beside the water, but
the distance did not shorten as it had in our
plans. The chimneys ahead remained always ahead. Our boots grew
hot; my heel began to smart; and the currant bun,
which had been festive at noon, lay heavily within me.

Mahony amused himself by aiming stones at telegraph poles. Once
he struck a tin sign and the noise brought a
watchman out of a shed. We ran until the road
curved, though the watchman did not follow us. Afterward we
walked in silence.

It was then decided, without formal acknowledgment, that we would
not reach the Pigeon House. We had spent much of
our money and must return in time to recover our
books. The adventure had acquired the ordinary limits of an
afternoon.

We turned inland by a lane bordered on one side
by a ditch and on the other by a high
wall. Beyond the ditch lay fields broken by patches of
waste ground. Smoke drifted above distant roofs, but the place
itself seemed removed from the city. There was no wind.
Flies moved over the water in the ditch, and the
leaves of the docks hung dusty and still.

Finding a gap in a hedge, we entered a field.
The grass was long enough to conceal us if we
lay down. Mahony began chasing a cat which disappeared through
nettles, while I sat on the bank and examined the
blister on my heel. A train passed somewhere beyond the
wall, its sound swelling and diminishing like a warning not
intended for us.

Mahony returned and stretched himself full length. We spoke of
Leo Byrne. Mahony said he had known Leo would funk
because fellows who talked most about adventures were generally the
first to stay at home. I perceived that the remark
might also have been directed at me, and said that
at least I had come.

After this we quarrelled mildly about which of us had
first proposed the excursion. The heat soon exhausted the subject.
Mahony pulled his cap over his eyes. I watched a
pale butterfly crossing the field in uncertain stages.

It had almost reached the hedge when I noticed the
man.

He had entered by the gap through which we had
come and was walking slowly along the edge of the
field. He carried a stick under one arm. His clothes
were of greenish-black cloth, shiny at the elbows, and he
wore a soft hat pressed down over his forehead. At
first I thought he had not seen us. Then he
turned his head, continued for several paces, and turned again.

"There's an old josser looking at us," Mahony said.

I told him not to mind.

The man made a circuit near the ditch and approached.
His progress was so deliberate that it would have seemed
foolish to run from him. When he came within speaking
distance, he saluted us politely.

"Fine day, boys."

We answered that it was.

He rested both hands upon his stick. His moustache was
grizzled, and when he smiled his lips disclosed long yellow
teeth. Yet his voice was educated and mild.

"Out for a holiday?"

Mahony said yes.

"School holiday, is it?"

I said our school had closed for the day because
the heating pipes were being repaired. This explanation, improbable in
the warm weather, sounded elaborate enough to satisfy him.

"Ah," he said. "A fortunate accident."

He asked what school we attended. Remembering our false names
but no false school, I mentioned one across the city
where a cousin of mine had been a pupil. The
stranger nodded as though acquainted with its masters. Then he
asked if we read much.

"I was a great reader when I was your age,"
he said. "A great reader. Adventure stories, voyages, poetry. Would
you be fond of poetry?"

I said I liked some poetry.

"And you?" he asked Mahony.

Mahony plucked a grass stem with his teeth.

"I don't read that gas."

The man laughed gently and looked at me, establishing between
us, as I then imagined, an understanding founded upon culture.
He named several poets. Some I knew from school and
some I did not. His manner reassured me. I began
to feel ashamed of Mahony's bluntness and hoped the man
perceived that, though we were companions, our tastes were different.

He sat down on the bank a little distance away.
For a while he spoke about books and about travelling.
He had once, he said, known a sailor who visited
Greece and brought home a white stone from a ruined
temple. The sea was a wonderful education. A boy who
travelled learned more in one month than another boy learned
in a year at school.

This doctrine, coming from an adult, pleased me greatly.

Then, without any clear transition, he asked whether we had
many girl friends.

Mahony grinned.

"No."

"No?" the man repeated. "You will have, though. Every fine
boy has girls fond of him. Little sweethearts, eh?"

He smiled at us in turn. I lowered my eyes
and broke a blade of grass into equal pieces.

Mahony said there were plenty of girls in our street.

"Pretty girls?"

"Some."

"Ah, there is nothing in the world like a pretty
girl," the man said. "Nothing. A soft-haired girl, with nice
manners. You like fair hair, perhaps?"

He addressed this question to me. I said I did
not know.

"Or dark hair. Some prefer dark girls. Dark eyes watching
you. And a girl's hand—such a little hand."

His own hand tightened on the stick. He began to
speak of girls he had known when he was young:
girls who laughed, girls who walked out on Sundays, girls
who allowed boys to carry their parcels. His sentences wandered,
but they returned continually to their hair, their hands and
the punishment due to girls who were proud.

The sun pressed upon the field. I could hear Mahony
chewing his grass stem. The cultivated conversation about books seemed
to have occurred a long time before.

Suddenly the man stood up.

"I must leave you for one moment," he said.

He walked away toward the far corner of the field,
not toward the gap. His pace quickened when he believed
himself beyond our sight.

Mahony watched him.

"What's he at?" he said.

I did not answer. The man stopped with his back
to us.

Mahony uttered an exclamation, half laughter and half disgust.

"Look at him!"

I fixed my eyes upon the grass. A small black
insect climbed one of the broken blades beside my shoe.
I watched it reach the top, hesitate and descend. My
face felt hot, but my hands were cold.

"He's a queer old josser," Mahony said.

I told him to be quiet.

After a time the man returned. He came by almost
the same path, smoothing his coat before he sat down.
His face appeared calmer, though a faint moisture shone beside
his nose. Neither of us spoke.

"You are quiet boys," he said. "That is good. I
like quiet, well-behaved boys."

He looked at Mahony.

"But I suppose you get into mischief."

"Sometimes," Mahony said.

"Run away from lessons, perhaps?"

Mahony laughed.

The man did not laugh. He began to say that
boys required discipline. A boy who disobeyed his masters should
be corrected, corrected severely. There was no kindness, he said,
in sparing him. A good whipping, properly administered, saved a
boy's character.

He leaned nearer.

"Have you ever been whipped?"

Mahony tossed away the grass stem.

"That's my business."

The man's eyes brightened. He spoke faster, though his voice
remained low. Bold boys, saucy boys, boys who answered back—those
were the boys who needed it. Their trousers should be
taken down and they should be whipped until they promised
obedience. Whipped and whipped. A soft-hearted person might stop too
soon, but that was a mistake. The boy must feel
it. He must remember.

Each time he repeated the word his stick traced a
little line in the dust between his boots.

I searched for some means of departure which would not
provoke him. The gap in the hedge seemed farther away
than before. Mahony lay on his elbow, staring insolently, but
I knew his insolence was partly fear because a whiteness
had appeared around his mouth.

The man turned to me.

"You have been whipped, I expect."

I shook my head.

"Never?"

"No, sir."

"A studious boy like you," he said softly. "But even
studious boys need correction. Sometimes more than the others, because
they are secretive. They think too much. They conceal things."

He looked down at my hands.

My heart beat with such force that I believed he
must see my jacket moving. Yet I was unwilling to
spring up and run. Running would confess that I had
understood him, and I did not wish to possess that
understanding. I brushed earth from my knees and said, with
a poor attempt at carelessness, that it was late.

"Late?" he repeated.

"We have to meet our friend."

"What friend?"

For one instant I had forgotten our arrangement. Then I
stood and called loudly toward the far hedge:

"Murphy!"

Mahony looked at me without comprehension.

"Murphy!" I called again. "Are you coming?"

He understood. He rose, but instead of approaching at once
he snatched up a stone and threw it toward the
ditch.

"Here I am!" he shouted. "Wait for me!"

His voice, vulgar and splendid, broke the enclosure of the
man's speech. I walked toward the gap without hurrying. Behind
me the stranger said something about good manners, but I
did not turn. The grass dragged at my boots. Every
step seemed slow until I heard Mahony running.

He came past me with his cap in his hand.

"Come on, Reynolds!" he cried.

We climbed through the hedge and ran along the lane.
Neither of us looked back. My blister burst inside my
shoe, and pain shot through my heel, but it seemed
a clean and welcome pain. At the first corner Mahony
began to laugh breathlessly and abuse the old josser. I
tried to laugh also. No sound came at first.

We stopped beside the high wall when we were sure
we had not been followed. Mahony's face was red again.
He struck the wall with his cap and said he
would have knocked the man down if he had tried
anything. I said I had known that. Then, because gratitude
embarrassed me, I reminded him that I had thought of
calling the false name.

The city received us gradually: first the rattle of a
cart, then houses, then women at doors and children returning
from school with satchels on their backs. We recovered our
books from the coach house. The sacking had left a
white dust upon my Latin grammar, and I wiped it
carefully with my sleeve.

At home my mother asked whether the Mass had been
long. I answered that it had. She set tea before
me, and the ordinary objects of the kitchen—the blue jug,
the bread knife, the clock with its painted roses—appeared both
comforting and accusatory.

For many days I spoke of the docks, the foreign
sailors and the ferry, but not of the field. When
Mahony boasted that we had met a madman, I corrected
details of his account and pretended amusement. I still considered
him coarse. Yet whenever I remembered the moment in which
his running figure had crossed the field toward me, cap
in hand and mouth open, I felt again the relief
of rescue, and with it a sharper feeling.

It was shame: not for having been afraid, as I
told myself then, but for having secretly despised the person
whose rough courage I had needed.

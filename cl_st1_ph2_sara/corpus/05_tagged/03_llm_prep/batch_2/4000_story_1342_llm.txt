# THE CHIEF FROM THE WATER

In Redwood Camp, where a man's moral worth was commonly
estimated by the rapidity with which he could empty a
revolver, replenish a glass, or detect an ace beneath another
man's sleeve, Elijah Martin enjoyed the distinction of being considered
entirely worthless. This judgment, having been delivered early and frequently,
acquired in time the venerability of law; and as Elijah
did nothing to contradict it—contradiction itself requiring a certain energy—it
remained unchallenged.

He had arrived in the camp without money, profession, or
visible purpose, and had attached himself to its outskirts as
burrs attach themselves to a mule's tail: not through affection,
perhaps, but through a mild incapacity for letting go. He
chopped wood occasionally, washed dishes reluctantly, held horses badly, and
once assisted in the burial of a gambler by carrying
the lighter end of the coffin. He drank little, fought
never, and possessed no talent for profanity beyond a faint
and apologetic "damn," uttered chiefly when he injured himself. These
deficiencies, in a community where intoxication was conviviality, combat citizenship,
and blasphemy almost an accomplishment, were naturally fatal to his
reputation.

"Lije ain't dangerous," Poker Dick had once observed, intending the
phrase as an exhaustive biography.

"He ain't useful, neither," added Profane Pete, who had been
elected road commissioner because he owned no road and had
never been seen to travel one.

Elijah was not more than thirty-five, though neglect had already
given him that uncertain, weathered appearance by which frontier men
anticipated age without attaining dignity. He was tall, loose-jointed, brown-haired,
and pale under his freckles; his blue eyes, when not
lowered before accusation, had a softness which Redwood Camp mistook
for stupidity. His hands were large but irresolute. Even his
clothes seemed to hang upon him under protest, as if
aware that another wearer might have carried them with more
decision.

Yet it may be doubted whether Elijah had originally been
as useless as Redwood Camp made him. Society, which takes
much credit for reforming criminals, seldom acknowledges its equal talent
for manufacturing incompetents. A man who is told for ten
years that he cannot act will, at the eleventh crisis,
frequently justify the prophecy—not from conviction, but from habit. Courage
is often less a native fire than a credit cautiously
extended by one's neighbors; and Elijah, having never been trusted
with so much as a spark, had ceased to carry
tinder.

Thus, when Redwood Camp laughed at him, he smiled uneasily;
when it pushed him aside, he remained where he fell;
and when it declared that he would never amount to
anything, he accepted the statement with the meekness of a
debtor signing a note he could not read.

It was the river that first disputed the camp's opinion.

The winter rains had descended upon the redwood slopes for
six days and nights, not in showers, but in gray,
continuous sheets which appeared to lower the sky inch by
inch. The narrow gulches filled; the brown hills ran with
water; abandoned claims became ponds, roads became channels, and channels
became torrents. Still Redwood Camp, accustomed to considering Nature as
another adversary to be bullied, continued its poker, its drinking,
and its municipal debate over whether a dead citizen might
legally vote if his name remained upon the roll.

On the seventh night the river rose.

It did not rise with theatrical warning, as a more
considerate river might have done, but came down from the
mountains in a vast black accumulation of uprooted trees, broken
sluices, drowned cattle, and the private property of several communities
which had imagined themselves beyond its reach. It struck Redwood
Camp shortly after midnight. Cabins trembled, revolved, and went out
like cards swept from a table; lanterns darted between the
trees; men shouted contradictory instructions from roofs already under way;
and the town's largest saloon, maintaining to the end its
reputation as a place of general resort, received a stable,
two sheds, and most of the cemetery before disappearing downstream.

Elijah had been asleep in a lean-to behind the blacksmith
shop. He awoke afloat.

The floor beneath him had lifted; the roof above him
had vanished; and his narrow bunk, with its blanket, straw
mattress, and terrified occupant, was moving rapidly through branches under
a rain-filled darkness. Elijah did not cry out. This was
not courage. He had simply reached that extremity of fear
in which the human voice, like an overtaxed public official,
refuses further duty.

A cottonwood struck the bunk and overturned it. He went
under, rose among wreckage, caught a beam, lost it, embraced
a barrel, and was separated from that companion by the
antlers of a drowned buck. Presently his fingers closed upon
a square timber from some bridge or mill. To this
he clung with an attachment deeper than any he had
previously shown to employment, principle, or friend.

All night he was carried through a world without form.
At intervals lightning disclosed cliffs, bent trees, white water, and
fragments of human habitation rushing beside him with a ghastly
domesticity—a cradle, a red chair, a hen-coop, a door still
bearing its brass knocker. Once he heard a man calling
from the darkness. The voice passed behind him, weakened, and
was lost. Once the timber spun into an eddy and
Elijah felt branches comb across his body like giant fingers
searching for a hold. He shut his eyes, not to
exclude death, which was everywhere, but to avoid seeing in
what shape it chose to approach.

Toward morning the current widened and slackened. He remembered afterward
a pale band in the east, reeds bent beneath the
flood, and a succession of low islands upon which driftwood
had piled like bones. Then the timber struck something; Elijah's
knees touched mud; he crawled through willows, climbed a bank,
and fell senseless among wet ferns.

He awoke under sunlight.

For several minutes he lay watching steam rise from his
clothes. The storm had passed. Above him the forest dripped
with a thousand small, separate sounds, while beyond the trees
the swollen river hurried westward, brown, shining, and innocent of
explanation. A thrush called. Somewhere a branch broke under the
retreating water. The world, having nearly killed him, had resumed
its ordinary business.

He was saved. More than that, he was alone.

The first fact amazed him; the second terrified him.

He stood, staggered, and looked about. No chimney smoke rose
through the trees. No axe answered his shout. The flood
had borne him beyond every landmark he knew, perhaps fifty
miles, perhaps two hundred; for a frightened man measures distance
not by geography but by the absence of assistance. His
pockets contained a broken comb, two matches reduced to pulp,
a brass button, and half a plug of tobacco, though
he did not chew. His boots were full of sand.
His coat had been torn away. He possessed neither knife
nor pistol, and for the first time understood why Redwood
Camp had considered an unarmed man almost indecent.

All that day he followed the river through alder thickets
and drowned meadows. Hunger, which begins as a complaint and
ends as a government, soon directed his movements. He ate
sour berries, chewed fern roots, and pursued a lizard with
such clumsy ferocity that the creature escaped less by speed
than by contempt. Toward evening he found tracks in the
mud—small moccasined footprints, plainly not white men's—and his hunger yielded
at once to another appetite: FEAR.

The miners had filled his mind with stories of the
Minyos, a small coastal tribe inhabiting the western valleys. That
none of the narrators had ever seen a Minyo did
not diminish the accuracy of their information. According to Redwood
Camp, they were at once cowardly and ferocious, idle and
tireless, disappearing before honest labor yet traveling a hundred miles
to commit an outrage. Elijah, who had doubted many camp
statements concerning himself, accepted these with the trustfulness men reserve
for prejudices flattering to their race.

He therefore left the tracks and pushed deeper into the
timber. Darkness found him among enormous trunks where the last
daylight remained aloft in the branches like gold imprisoned beneath
a roof. He slept badly under a fallen log, dreaming
alternately of bacon and scalping knives.

The following morning hunger conquered ethnology. He returned toward the
river and discovered, in a little hollow above the flood
line, a low structure roofed with bark and earth. Smoke
escaped faintly from an opening at its center. Beside it
stood wicker baskets, a stone bowl, and a rack upon
which several smoked salmon hung in a row, their open
mouths expressing astonishment at Elijah's good fortune.

He approached on hands and knees, paused, listened, and finally
entered through the small door.

It was a sweat-house, although Elijah did not know it.
Sunlight fell through the smoke-hole in narrow, fiery shafts, illuminating
cedar posts, woven mats, bundles of herbs, and carved objects
whose solemn faces emerged and vanished in the vapor. At
the farther end, raised slightly upon skins, sat a man.

Elijah stopped.

The man did not move.

He was very old, dressed in a mantle of pale
feathers and sea-otter fur, with shell ornaments upon his breast
and a circlet of red wood around his head. His
face was painted in black and vermilion bands; his hands
rested upon his knees; behind him stood a staff carved
with the image of a diving bird. He regarded Elijah
with an immobility more formidable than anger.

"Morning," whispered Elijah.

The old man remained reserved.

It required nearly a minute for Elijah to comprehend that
he was dead. Death had evidently occurred some days before,
yet the coolness of the earth house, the smoke of
fragrant boughs, and certain native preparations had preserved the body
in a posture of authority. He had been placed there
ceremonially, perhaps to await burial, perhaps according to some custom
beyond Elijah's knowledge. But hunger is the least antiquarian of
passions. Having discovered that the owner could not object, Elijah
seized one of the salmon and ate.

He ate the second more slowly and the third with
reflection. Warmth, food, and concealment restored his animal spirits sufficiently
for him to consider his appearance. His garments were stiff
with mud and torn almost to nakedness. Upon a peg
hung a long woven shirt, richly bordered with quillwork, and
beside it a cloak of fur. Elijah hesitated. He had
enough superstition to dislike robbing the dead, but not enough
clothing to respect the objection. He took the shirt, then
the cloak; and, finding his own boots intolerable, replaced them
with soft decorated leggings and moccasins laid near the bier.

When dressed, he caught sight of himself in a polished
slab of dark stone. The effect was extraordinary. The fur
increased the breadth of his shoulders; the long shirt concealed
his gaunt awkwardness; the circlet, which he placed experimentally upon
his brow, restrained his uncombed hair with almost classical success.
Terror had sharpened his features and hunger hollowed his cheeks;
these alterations, assisted by smoke and shadow, gave him an
ascetic gravity never suspected at Redwood Camp.

He might have admired himself longer had he not heard
voices outside.

They approached rapidly—first two, then many, punctuated by the soft
beat of running feet. Elijah looked toward the narrow entrance.
Flight was impossible; concealment offered only baskets and the corpse;
resistance required a weapon and, more importantly, a resister. In
his desperation he took the carved staff, backed against the
wall, and drew the fur cloak across the lower half
of his face.

Six Minyo men entered and dropped upon their knees.

This was alarming. More entered and did the same. An
old woman peered through the doorway, uttered a cry, and
bowed until her forehead touched the earth. Behind her came
women, children, and warriors, all staring at Elijah with an
awe so complete that it excluded immediate violence.

Elijah's knees trembled under the woven shirt; yet as the
garment was long, this evidence of mortality remained mercifully private.

An elderly man with white-streaked hair crawled forward. He spoke
in a language of soft syllables, pointing first to the
dead chief, then to Elijah, then toward the west. Elijah,
understanding nothing, made no reply. The man repeated the gesture
and indicated the river.

Here, at least, was something comprehensible. Elijah pointed eastward and
said hoarsely:

"River."

A murmur passed through the assembly. The elder bent lower
and spoke one word, which Elijah afterward knew meant "water,"
though whether river or sea was still uncertain. Elijah nodded
eagerly. Any agreement that postponed his death seemed admirable.

The people rose in solemn excitement. The old chief's circlet
was adjusted upon Elijah's head; shell necklaces were placed around
his neck; and, before he could protest, he was conducted
from the sweat-house beneath a deerskin canopy.

Unknown to Elijah, the Minyos had lost their hereditary chief
during the rains. The old man, Talama, childless and revered,
had died after declaring—or had been interpreted after death as
declaring, which is often the more enduring method—that the spirit
of leadership would return in a strange form FROM THE
WATER, wearing the signs of the former house and speaking
first the sacred word for the source of life. The
prophecy had already grown exact in the three days since
its utterance. Elijah, arriving from the flooded river, clothed in
Talama's regalia, and naming water at the proper instant, fulfilled
it with a precision which deliberate imposture could scarcely have
achieved.

Had he entered boldly, he might have been attacked. Had
he attempted a speech, his ignorance would have been exposed.
Had he carried a rifle, the Minyos would have considered
him an ordinary white intruder. But his helplessness was so
profound, his silence so complete, and his appearance so unaccountable,
that common explanations retreated before him. It was his utter
incompetency which made him supernatural.

There are societies in which a man becomes important by
making noise; there are others in which importance is inferred
from his refusal to do so. Elijah had exchanged the
first for the second without moving more than the river
moved him.

He was taken to a village situated on a sunny
shelf above a broad tidal stream, where redwood forest opened
upon reed beds, warm sand, and, in the distance, a
blue line of sea. The Minyos numbered scarcely four hundred.
Their houses, built of split cedar planks, stood about an
open ground; their canoes lay drawn upon the bank; racks
of drying fish shone silver in the sun. Children ran
beside Elijah's procession but did not touch him. Dogs barked
and were silenced. Old men came from doorways and lifted
their hands.

A house larger than the others was given him. Food
arrived in bowls—salmon, acorn meal, venison, berries, roots sweetened with
honey. A young interpreter, who knew perhaps thirty English words
learned from traders, approached and asked:

"You—come sea?"

Elijah, with the prudence of terror, answered only, "Water."

The interpreter withdrew profoundly impressed.

For several days Elijah expected death at every entrance. He
slept in the regalia, fearing that removal of the circlet
might be considered abdication and that abdication might be performed
with a stone knife. He spoke rarely. When questioned, he
frowned, gazed westward, or lifted one hand. These gestures were
interpreted by the elders with such intelligence that Elijah began
to distrust the necessity of speech altogether.

If he disliked a dish, his abstention became a fast.
If he overslept, he communed with spirits. If sudden noise
caused him to start, watchers announced that he perceived invisible
dangers. Once, while crossing the village ground, he saw a
snake and became rigid with fright; the people, observing his
fixed eyes and uplifted hand, waited respectfully until the reptile
passed, after which they praised his mercy toward living things.

Thus did cowardice put on the appearance of self-command, as
a beggar may wear a stolen coat and be saluted
for the tailoring.

His first official crisis came a fortnight later.

A party of young hunters returned from the north with
two white captives. The men were packers who had lost
their trail and fired upon the Minyos under the natural
frontier assumption that an unfamiliar human figure was improved by
being shot at. One Minyo had been wounded. The packers,
disarmed and bound, were brought before Elijah amid a crowd
whose excitement required no translation.

The prisoners were sunburned, bearded, and badly frightened. One, called
Haskins, had known Elijah at Redwood Camp and had once
paid him fifty cents to clean a saddle. Yet Elijah's
transformation, aided by ceremonial paint, fur, shellwork, and the universal
human unwillingness to discover an acquaintance in a superior position,
prevented recognition.

The elder spokesman addressed Elijah, pointed to the wounded hunter,
then drew a finger across his own throat.

Elijah understood.

At once every memory of camp tales returned to him:
captives burned, tortured, mutilated; those cheerful inventions with which civilized
men justify anticipatory slaughter. He looked at the bound packers.
His face blanched; he recoiled; and, raising both hands, cried:

"No! Good Lord—no!"

The people fell silent.

Elijah stepped down, pushed aside the man who held the
prisoners, and tore ineffectually at the rawhide thongs. An elder,
mistaking his agitation for sacred urgency, cut them. Elijah pointed
toward the forest.

"Go!" he shouted. "Git out! Run!"

The captives ran.

A long murmur followed them. The wounded hunter's mother looked
at Elijah, then at the disappearing men, and bowed. Presently
others bowed with her. The chief from the water had
refused blood vengeance. The act was not regarded as weakness,
because the Minyos had already decided he was powerful; and
humanity, when exercised by the powerful, is graciously permitted to
remain humanity.

The two packers reached a trading post three days later,
where Haskins told the story over whiskey.

"I tell you, that chief's no common buck," he declared.
"Never raised his voice till the pinch come. Sat there
like he owned earth and weather. Then he turned them
red devils with one hand and let us loose. A
natural ruler—that's what he is. Born king. You could see
it."

This narrative, adorned at each repetition, returned to the Minyo
village through a mixed-blood trader named Jules. Jules translated the
compliments before Elijah, supposing they would please him.

They did more.

Elijah listened without movement, but the words entered him with
the force of revelation. A natural ruler. A born king.
No sentence remotely resembling these had ever been applied to
him. Praise, falling upon a character long starved of it,
does not refresh merely; it intoxicates. He retired to his
house and repeated the phrases silently. Haskins, a white man,
a packer, a citizen of the world which had despised
him, had seen command in his face and power in
his gesture.

That night Elijah stood before the dark polished stone he
used as a mirror. He lifted his chin. The face
within it, broadened by shadow and framed in fur, did
not appear cowardly. It appeared grave.

The next morning he removed the ceremonial paint.

He began to learn the Minyo language, first from necessity,
then from curiosity, and finally from ambition. His memory, never
taxed at Redwood Camp except by instructions he wished to
evade, proved surprisingly tenacious. He listened to councils, asked questions,
and discovered that the people's affairs were neither mysterious nor
disorderly. They wanted what most communities want: sufficient food, secure
homes, fair exchange, protection from enemies, and relief from the
young men who confused excitement with policy.

Here Elijah's defects became useful. He disliked warfare because he
feared it; he discouraged reckless hunting because he hated hunger;
he resisted long journeys because he was lazy; and he
settled quarrels because noise disturbed his sleep. A more heroic
ruler might have ruined the Minyos within a season. Elijah
preserved them through an enlightened preference for comfort.

He ordered fish stores divided among the houses before winter
rather than controlled by a few families. He encouraged the
enlargement of acorn grounds and the planting of potatoes obtained
from coastal farms. He had irrigation ditches cut—not by himself,
which would have been contrary to both custom and inclination,
but under men who knew the soil. Nets were repaired
collectively; canoes were counted; smoked salmon and woven baskets were
exchanged at fixed rates with trustworthy traders.

The Minyos, having been told that these measures came from
sacred wisdom, carried them out with an enthusiasm no white
electorate would have shown toward mere common sense.

Elijah's physical indolence was not cured, nor was cure necessary.
Government is among the few occupations in which sitting still
may be mistaken for labor, provided enough persons sit nearby
taking notes. He held councils at sunrise, listened more than
he spoke, and discovered that two hours of attention saved
ten hours of confusion. Because he had once been disregarded,
he recognized the importance of hearing men whom others interrupted.
Because he knew nothing at first, he asked questions which
exposed the pretended knowledge of advisers. Because he feared making
decisions, he postponed them until he understood their consequences; this
habit, contemptible in a hired hand, became statesmanship in a
chief.

Within a year the Minyo village was better fed, better
stored, and less divided than it had been in living
memory.

Then the government discovered it.

Civilization had approached the Minyos before in the forms of
whiskey, bullets, disease, missionaries, and men searching for timber; but
these unofficial representatives, though energetic, lacked documents. The official party
arrived with flags, ledgers, military escort, and a commissioner named
Mr. Abel Wynne, who had been instructed to negotiate a
treaty by which the Minyos would surrender most of their
country in return for protection upon the part remaining.

Mr. Wynne was an honest man within the limits of
his instructions, and his instructions were dishonest within the limits
of national policy. He had brought blankets, beads, agricultural tools,
inferior tobacco, three crates of hymnbooks, and a quantity of
brass medals bearing the President's profile. He expected gratitude, confusion,
and signatures.

Instead, he found Elijah.

The chief received the delegation in a cedar council house,
attended by elders and by Jules as interpreter. He wore
plain white buckskin with a narrow circlet and carried no
weapon. His hair had grown to his shoulders; sun and
outdoor life had darkened his face; prosperity had straightened his
back. Mr. Wynne, observing his height, restraint, and grave attention,
decided he was encountering a native statesman of unusual capacity.
Since statesmen everywhere benefit from being approached with that expectation,
Elijah became one.

The proposed treaty was read.

By it, the Minyos ceded the river mouth, the best
meadow, the salmon grounds, and the western forest; in return
they were to receive a rocky upland, annual supplies subject
to congressional appropriation, and the paternal affection of the United
States, an emotion famous for its grandeur and irregularity.

Elijah listened. Two years earlier he would have signed anything
placed before him if the pen were pointed in the
proper direction. Now he asked, through Jules, why land already
occupied by the Minyos should be given away in order
that the government might protect their occupation of what remained.

Mr. Wynne cleared his throat.

"It is the policy," he said.

Jules translated. Elijah replied.

"What policy?"

"The policy of the government."

"Does the government protect first or take first?"

Mr. Wynne looked at the military officer, who examined the
roof.

Negotiations continued for nine days. Elijah rejected the rocky tract,
retained the salmon grounds, demanded recognition of Minyo fishing rights,
and secured a boundary following the ridge rather than the
river. He accepted farming tools but refused the whiskey offered
privately by a subcontractor. He declined beads at four times
their coastal value and requested iron, salt, canvas, cattle, and
a written guarantee against unauthorized settlement.

The commissioner was astonished. He had come prepared to negotiate
with people presumed ignorant of white methods and found himself
opposed by a man who understood those methods through their
most educational expression—having once been their victim. Elijah knew the
white trader's trick of praising what he wished to sell
and scorning what he wished to buy; knew that a
promise made loudly might be forgotten quietly; knew that a
line on paper could become a fence on earth. He
had learned these truths in Redwood Camp without realizing they
constituted political education.

The treaty, when finally signed, was the least unfavorable agreement
made with any tribe in that district. It succeeded because
the Minyos understood enough about the whites to distrust them,
while the whites knew so little about the Minyos that
they feared to expose their ignorance.

News of the Water Chief spread. Newspapers referred to him
as "the Minyo Solon," though few editors could have identified
either Minyo or Solon without assistance. Mission societies claimed his
temperance as evidence of prior conversion. Traders complained that he
was hostile to commerce because he objected to being cheated.
Settlers called him dangerous because he insisted that treaty lines
ought to possess the same reality on both sides.

When two squatters built cabins in the western meadow, Elijah
sent men to remove their roofs without injuring them. The
settlers appealed to the army. Elijah produced the treaty. The
officer, after inspecting it with regret, ordered the settlers away.

When a party of drunken hunters shot a Minyo youth,
Elijah did not permit retaliation; instead he carried the wounded
boy to the agency, displayed him upon the agent's table,
and sat there silently until arrests were made. The hunters
were tried, convicted of disorderly conduct, and fined less than
the value of their ammunition—a result not equivalent to justice,
but sufficiently novel to discourage repetition.

Meanwhile the village prospered. New plank houses appeared above the
flood line. Minyo women sold baskets and cured fish at
coastal markets for prices fixed in advance. A small herd
grazed in the meadow. Children learned English numbers for trade
without being forbidden their own songs. Elijah discouraged missionary disputes
by welcoming every sect and allowing none to govern, a
policy which each considered persecution of itself and toleration of
the others.

His person acquired wealth: shell ornaments, furs, horses, carved chests,
silver coins, blankets, and gifts from merchants who had discovered
that honesty toward a powerful customer was commercially indistinguishable from
virtue. He married Wachita, the niece of Talama, partly at
the elders' urging and partly because she had watched him
from the beginning with quiet, intelligent eyes which neither worshiped
nor mocked him.

Wachita was scarcely twenty. She was slender, strong, and self-contained,
with black hair generally braided in two heavy strands and
a face whose repose concealed rather than denied emotion. She
learned Elijah's habits without questioning their origin. She knew he
disliked sudden noises, deep water, blood, snakes, and public singing.
She knew he woke sometimes from dreams crying English words
he would not translate. She knew his skin beneath the
sun-browned face was lighter than that of any Minyo, though
coastal tribes had seen enough shipwrecked sailors, mixed families, and
wandering peoples not to regard complexion as a divine affidavit.

Whether she suspected more, she did not say.

She loved him, if steady service, amused patience, and the
preservation of another's dignity may be admitted as love. Elijah,
who had never before possessed a home secure against ridicule,
loved her gratefully and with a tenderness almost free from
vanity. They had a son who died in infancy, and
shared grief drew them closer; but the empty cradle remained
in a corner of the house, and there were evenings
when Wachita sat beside it while Elijah walked alone toward
the sea.

For after two years of authority, prosperity, and respect, the
Chief from the Water grew restless.

It was not Redwood Camp he missed precisely. He remembered
its mud, contempt, gunfire, debts, and practical jokes too well
to gild them entirely. Yet the English language, heard only
in bargaining or official speech, began to haunt him with
common phrases. He longed to hear a white woman laugh.
He longed for church bells, though he had avoided church;
for newspapers, though they had never mentioned him; for ham
fried in an iron pan; for songs badly played upon
a saloon piano. Man is capable of yearning most poignantly
for the very world that rejected him, provided distance has
first corrected its manners.

The sea encouraged these dissatisfactions. Beyond the reed beds it
stretched as a blue road to cities, crowds, hotels, lamps,
and rooms in which no one had heard of prophecy.
Elijah would sit upon the warm sand listening to the
rollers and watching curlews run at their retreat. Sometimes a
ship showed far offshore, a white splinter moving south. He
imagined himself aboard it in ordinary trousers, answering to his
own name.

But what then?

Elijah Martin, returned from the dead, would still be Elijah
Martin. He could not carry Minyo reverence into Redwood Camp
like a letter of introduction. If he told the truth,
men would laugh; if he displayed his wealth, they would
remember that he had acquired it among people they despised;
if he asserted authority, someone would call him "Lije," and
the old paralysis might seize him before the second syllable.

Thus he remained—king from fear of becoming a vagrant.

The new Indian agent arrived in April. His name was
Dall, a narrow, industrious man of forty-five whose belief in
regulations was so sincere that he applied them even when
they contradicted one another. More consequentially, he brought his wife.

Mrs. Marian Dall was twenty-eight, bright-haired, gray-eyed, and possessed of
that lively curiosity which society excuses in an attractive woman
because society expects to enjoy the results. She had spent
two years at a military post in Arizona and therefore
regarded herself as experienced in native affairs; but the Minyos
interested her because they declined to conform to her experience.

She heard at once of the mysterious chief. Her husband
described him as intelligent but "reserved."

"A savage diplomat?" she asked.

"Something of the kind."

"Old?"

"No."

"Ugly?"

Mr. Dall frowned at the irrelevance. "He has a commanding
appearance."

This answer ensured her investigation.

Elijah avoided the agency after learning that a white woman
lived there. He sent elders to conduct business and declined
three invitations to dine. Mrs. Dall's curiosity, denied the respectable
entrance, selected another.

One warm afternoon she rode alone along the tidal path
under the pretense of collecting ferns. The air smelled of
salt, wild mint, and sun-warmed cedar. Bees droned among the
lupines. Beyond the reeds, long Pacific rollers broke with a
measured sound at once remote and intimate, like memory heard
through walls.

She found Elijah seated upon a driftwood log, looking toward
the sea.

He wore a dark-blue blanket across one shoulder, deerskin leggings,
and the narrow red circlet of office. His head was
bare. At the sound of her horse he rose quickly,
then stood motionless.

Mrs. Dall dismounted.

"So," she said, smiling, "you are the terrible chief."

Elijah understood every word.

For two years he had guarded that understanding as a
miser guards coin. Now the familiar cadence of a white
woman's voice passed through him more dangerously than accusation. He
lowered his eyes.

She came closer, studying him with unconcealed interest. "They say
you never speak to white people. Is that pride, I
wonder, or policy?"

Elijah's hands tightened beneath the blanket.

Mrs. Dall mistook his silence for incomprehension and continued with
the freedom people employ before servants, foreigners, children, and the
supposedly deaf.

"You are not at all what I expected. Marian Dall,
you have been scandalously deceived." She tilted her head. "Why,
he is handsome."

The word struck Elijah with absurd power. It was not
merely praise; it was praise in English, from a woman
belonging to the inaccessible world beyond the sea. Redwood Camp
had called him worthless. The Minyos had called him sacred.
Haskins had called him king. But this careless "handsome," spoken
half to herself, awakened something less dignified and more imperious
than kingship.

Mrs. Dall stepped nearer and touched the border of his
blanket.

"Does Your Majesty understand one word I say?"

"Yes," said Elijah.

Her hand dropped.

For an instant they stared at one another. Mrs. Dall's
gray eyes widened, not yet with fear, but with a
delighted astonishment that seemed to promise conspiracy. Elijah saw in
her face recognition—not of the chief, but of the man
concealed beneath him. The discovery stripped away two years. He
was again white, English-speaking, solitary, and young enough to desire
what stood before him.

Had she recoiled, he might have recovered. Had she laughed,
shame might have restored his caution. But she whispered only:

"You do!"

That soft triumph completed his undoing.

He seized her in his arms and kissed her.

It was not a courtly kiss, nor a considered betrayal,
nor even an act possessing the dignity of deliberate passion.
It was the desperate assault of exile upon his former
life—a hunger for language, race, memory, vanity, and forbidden possibility
concentrated into one blind embrace. Mrs. Dall struggled, struck his
shoulder, and broke free.

Elijah's reason returned in full and merciless detail.

He saw her disordered hair, her flushed face, the horse
drawing back upon its reins, the path to the agency,
the treaty, the village, Wachita, and the entire fragile architecture
of accident beneath which Elijah Martin had lived as a
chief. Without a word he turned and fled into the
reeds.

Mrs. Dall remained beside the driftwood, breathing hard. Anger came
first; then bewilderment; then the practical intelligence by which women,
denied official authority, often arrive more quickly than officials at
the truth.

She looked after him.

His "Yes" had carried no accent except the faint rural
note of the American frontier. His face, recalled without the
ceremonial frame, rearranged itself in her mind. The blue eyes,
brown hair, skin darkened by sun rather than birth, his
avoidance of the agency, his comprehension, his panic—all came together
with the audible neatness of a lock closing.

"Good heavens," she said. "That man is white."

A movement sounded behind her.

Wachita stood at the edge of the willow shade.

She had followed on foot, perhaps from suspicion, perhaps from
concern, perhaps by one of those domestic intuitions which men
dismiss as mysterious because acknowledgment would require better conduct. Her
face was calm. Only her hands, clasped before her, betrayed
pressure in the whitened knuckles.

Mrs. Dall knew enough Minyo to say, "I did not
know."

Wachita's eyes moved from the woman's face to the place
where Elijah had vanished.

She answered nothing.

Between them lay the trampled grass; beyond them the sea
advanced and withdrew, erasing each mark at the edge of
the sand while leaving the shore itself unchanged. Mrs. Dall
gathered her reins, suddenly uncertain whether she was witness, victim,
or judge. Wachita remained still, carrying in her silence the
future of a chief, a husband, and a man whom
two societies had made in opposite images.

In the reeds Elijah crouched like the fugitive he had
once been, listening for pursuit.

None came.

That absence frightened him more than footsteps.

For two years he had ruled because others believed him
more than he believed himself. He had transformed their faith
into roads, granaries, boundaries, bargains, and peace; and in doing
so had nearly become the person their mistake created. Yet
one kiss—one impulsive claim upon the world that had refused
him—had torn the ceremonial cloak from his shoulders more completely
than any public accusation.

He had come from the water without courage, name, or
purpose. The Minyos had given him all three. Now the
sea before him offered escape, while the village behind him
held judgment; and Elijah Martin, who had survived a flood
by clinging to whatever floated, discovered at last that there
are moments when a man must choose what shall bear
his weight.

Above the reeds a curlew cried. Far offshore a sail
moved southward, white in the declining sun.

Elijah lifted his head.

Then he heard Wachita call his name—not his title, not
the sacred name bestowed upon him, but the private name
by which she addressed him when no council listened.

He rose slowly.

Whether cowardice made him hesitate, whether love made him turn,
or whether kingship, practiced long enough, had at last become
character, no observer could certainly have said. Human motives, like
rivers in flood, carry the wreckage of many sources and
are named only after they have reached the sea.

But he turned toward the village.

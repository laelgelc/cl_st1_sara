### THE MAKING OF RUBE HURTLE

There are managers who say a pennant is won in
April, and others who swear it is won in September.
I have always maintained that a pennant is won on
the afternoon when nine ball players quit thinking about their
averages, their sore fingers, and their next season's contracts, and
begin fighting for one another.

That afternoon came late for Worcester.

In May we looked like champions. We had speed, hitters,
fielders, three seasoned twirlers, and enough confidence to fill a
freight depot. McCall, our little red-headed center fielder, stole bases
whenever he felt restless. Spears, captain and third baseman, could
block a ground ball with his shins and then throw
a runner out from a sitting posture. Old Radbourne knew
every trick ever invented with a baseball and several tricks
that ought to have been prohibited by law. Stringer, our
left fielder, was batting three hundred and seventy-one and hitting
the ball so hard that in Rochester he knocked a
fence board loose and sent it spinning into the street.

The newspapers called us the Worcester Whirlwind.

I called us pennant winners.

That was before the injuries, the suspensions, the poor pitching,
the bad breaks, and the yellow streak that slipped over
the club like fog.

First McCall ran into a concrete wall chasing a long
fly and split his eyebrow to the bone. The doctor
sewed him up and ordered him to rest ten days.
McCall played the next afternoon with a bandage over one
eye, misjudged two flies, and afterward fainted in the clubhouse.

Then Spears argued with an umpire in Providence. An argument
with Spears was never a private affair. He began with
the umpire, continued with the Providence first baseman, addressed a
few remarks to the grand stand, and ended by throwing
a water bucket across the field. The league suspended him
for a week.

Our best pitcher strained his shoulder. Our second-best developed a
blister, and our third-best developed matrimony. He received a letter
from his wife before every game and thereafter pitched as
though each batter were his mother-in-law.

We lost three straight in Toronto, two in Montreal, four
out of five at home, and a double-header to Jersey
City, whose infield had been considered safe only when the
ball was not hit to it.

Then came the slump.

Stringer stopped hitting.

No one knew why. That is the worst part of
a batting slump. If a man breaks a leg, you
can point to the break. If he has a fever,
you can read the thermometer. But when a hitter who
has been driving curves into the alleys suddenly begins swinging
under fast balls and over slow ones, there is nothing
to bandage and no medicine to prescribe.

Stringer tried everything. He changed bats. He changed his stance.
He moved forward in the box and back in the
box. He gripped the handle at the knob, then choked
it until his hands were halfway up the wood. He
ate raw eggs, carried a rabbit's foot, and permitted a
hotel porter in Montreal to rub his wrists with liniment
warranted to cure rheumatism, toothache, and poor circulation.

Still he could not hit.

He went twenty-two times without a safety. Then he rolled
a weak grounder through the box and was so grateful
that he kept the ball. After that he went nineteen
times more.

A club feels such a thing. Stringer was our big
gun. When he failed, the others pressed. When the others
pressed, they failed. Men who had been taking a healthy
cut began tapping at the ball as if afraid of
waking it. On the bases they hesitated. In the field
they threw to the wrong bag. Our pitchers looked toward
the bench whenever a man reached first, as though expecting
me to go out and pitch for them.

By the middle of July we were fifth.

Fifth!

Buffalo led us by eight games, and Buffalo was no
paper champion. It had sluggers from top to bottom, a
stone-wall infield, and old Danby, the craftiest pitcher in the
league. Its manager, Brill, wore a black derby on the
bench and smiled whenever his men scored, which they did
with unpleasant regularity.

Every morning I read the standing of the clubs while
eating breakfast, and every morning Worcester appeared a little lower
and Buffalo a little farther away.

To most men a pennant is a flag. To a
manager it is bread, reputation, and next year's salary. To
me that pennant meant more. A sporting-goods concern had offered
me an interest in a store if Worcester finished first
or second. With the money I expected from that deal
I intended to buy a white cottage on the road
beyond Lake Quinsigamond.

Milly had seen the cottage.

It stood among maples, with green shutters, a narrow porch,
and a strip of blue water visible between the trees.
She had not said she wanted it. Milly never asked
for anything. She merely stood at the gate one Sunday
afternoon and looked at the porch, the maples, and the
tiny garden where hollyhocks grew along the fence.

"Some woman'll be happy there," she said.

That was all.

But I saw the pennant, and the successful season, and
the business deal, and the cottage, and Milly—Milly in a
white dress under the maples, her blue eyes laughing at
me while I pretended I had bought the place for
an investment.

Then Worcester began losing.

One rainy evening, after we had dropped our sixth game
in a row, I shut the clubhouse door and faced
the team.

The men were sprawled along the benches. Mud streaked their
uniforms. Nobody talked. Nobody whistled. A club that had once
made more noise than a boiler shop now sat as
cheerful as mourners around an open grave.

I threw the scorebook on the floor.

"I can stand losing," I said. "Any club can lose.
I can stand errors, sore arms, bad hops, and umpires
with heads made of seasoned oak. But I won't stand
quitting."

No one answered.

"You men quit before the game started today. I saw
it. Buffalo made two runs in the first, and every
one of you began thinking about supper."

Spears lifted his battered face. "We're shy on pitchin'."

"We're shy on backbone."

That brought a mutter.

I walked down the row. "McCall, you once stole home
with two out in the ninth."

"Sure."

"Stringer, you hit three homers in a double-header."

Stringer stared at his shoes.

"Radbourne, you beat Baltimore with two broken fingers."

"I was younger then," said Radbourne.

"You weren't. You only looked younger."

A few men grinned.

"That's better," I said. "There are two months left. Eight
games can be made up. But not by corpses. From
now on, any man who quits can buy a ticket
home. If we go down, we'll go down fighting so
hard the other clubs will have to count their fingers
afterward."

Spears stood up.

"What we need," he said, "is a pitcher."

"I know it."

"A real pitcher."

"I know that, too."

"Then find one."

That was simple advice. It was like telling a thirsty
man to find a river.

Every manager in the league was hunting pitchers. Scouts searched
colleges, factory teams, mining towns, military posts, lumber camps, and
reform schools. Any man who could throw three strikes without
dislocating himself already had six offers and a conceit.

For ten days I chased rumors. I found a left-hander
in Albany who had speed but no control. He hit
five batters in three innings and knocked the mask off
his own catcher. I found a veteran in Syracuse who
claimed to possess a new curve. It was new because
no batter had ever mistaken it for one. I found
a young fellow who struck out eighteen men in an
industrial league, but when I saw his opponents I learned
that half of them wore spectacles.

Then Radbourne handed me a letter.

It came from his cousin, a grain dealer in Rickettsville.
The cousin wrote that a farm boy named Whitaker Hurtle
had pitched against the Rickettsville Cornhuskers and struck out twenty-one.
The one man who hit him reached first on a
bunt that rolled six inches, whereupon Hurtle picked him off.

"A country wonder," I said.

"Maybe," said Radbourne.

"I've seen fifty."

"See fifty-one."

So I went to Rickettsville.

The journey required two trains, a hired buggy, and a
final mile on foot because the buggy horse declined to
proceed through mud. I arrived behind a general store at
four in the afternoon with my shoes ruined and my
temper ready for combat.

A game was in progress in a pasture. There were
no grand stands, no chalk lines, and no uniforms worth
mentioning. A rope stretched from tree to tree kept the
crowd back. Cows watched from beyond right field. The catcher
wore a mask with three bars missing, and the umpire
stood behind the pitcher because he valued his life.

"Where's Hurtle?" I asked.

A farmer pointed.

At first I thought he meant the center fielder, who
was a broad young fellow. Then a creature unfolded itself
from the bench and walked toward the box.

Whitaker Hurtle was six feet three, though parts of him
appeared longer. He was lean as a fence rail, with
narrow shoulders, big hands, knees that seemed incorrectly hinged, and
ears standing away from his head like handles on a
jug. His uniform consisted of gray trousers too short for
him, a blue shirt too large for anyone, and a
cap that rested on a hay-colored shock of hair.

He moved with the grace of a crippled cow climbing
stairs.

"That?" I said.

"That's Whit."

The first batter faced him. Hurtle wound up. His arms,
legs, shirt, and cap all flew in different directions.

Then the ball came.

I heard it strike the catcher's mitt. I did not
see it.

The catcher rose, shook his hand, and walked in a
circle.

"Sufferin' umpires!" I said.

The second pitch began at the batter's shoulder and broke
across the plate at the knees. It curved wide as
the blade of a mowing scythe. The batter stepped backward,
stumbled over the catcher, and sat down.

The crowd laughed. Hurtle did not.

He threw another. It floated toward the plate, paused—or seemed
to pause—and dropped. The batter swung before it arrived and
nearly screwed himself into the ground.

Three pitches. Three strikes.

The next man bunted. Hurtle reached the ball in two
strides, scooped it barehanded, and threw true as a plumb
line. The first baseman never moved his feet.

The third batter crowded the plate. Hurtle brushed him back
with a white bullet under the chin, then put two
curves over the outside corner and finished him with a
fast one that made the catcher's mitt crack like a
pistol.

What a find!

I watched four innings. Hurtle struck out ten. He had
speed enough for three pitchers, a drop, an outcurve, an
incurve, a slow ball, and control so fine he could
have shaved a fly off the plate.

After the game I found him pumping water over his
head.

"Whitaker Hurtle?"

He wiped his face on his sleeve. "That's me."

"I manage Worcester."

He studied me. "The ball club?"

"The ball club."

"I seen your name in a paper once."

That was not the reception I had expected.

"How would you like to pitch professional baseball?"

He looked toward the pasture. "Fer money?"

"Certainly for money."

"How much?"

I named a figure.

His mouth opened. "A month?"

"A month."

"An' you pay railroad?"

"Yes."

"An' vittles?"

"On the road."

He scratched his head. "Ma'll say it's foolish."

"What do you say?"

He looked at the muddy ball in his hand.

"I calc'late I can throw some."

"You calc'late correctly."

He signed a contract in the general store. His handwriting
traveled uphill and then fell off the page. When I
told him to pack, he returned in fifteen minutes with
a carpetbag containing one shirt, two apples, a whetstone, and
a pair of socks wrapped around a horseshoe.

On the train he sat rigid, gripping the seat whenever
we rounded a curve.

"Hain't rode much," he explained.

"You'll get used to it."

"Does Worcester have electric lights?"

"Some."

"Elevators?"

"A few."

He frowned. "I don't hold with elevators."

That was Whitaker Hurtle, the pitcher upon whom my pennant
possibilities, my business future, and my white cottage depended.

The Worcester players were taking batting practice when I led
him through the gate.

McCall saw him first.

"Great smoke!" he yelled. "The boss has signed a beanpole."

The others turned. Hurtle stopped.

Spears walked around him slowly. "Is it alive?"

"Shut up," I said.

Radbourne came from the clubhouse, glanced at Whit, and winked
at me.

"Gentlemen," he said solemnly, "permit me to present the new
sidewheeler from the Agricultural League."

The men laughed.

Hurtle reddened.

Stringer held out a hand. He was too miserable over
his own slump to be cruel. "Glad to know you,
Hurtle."

Whit ignored the hand and stared at Spears.

"What's eatin' him?" McCall asked.

"He's thinking," said Spears. "Give him room."

"His name's Hurtle," I said. "Whitaker Hurtle. He pitches tomorrow
against Buffalo."

That stopped the laughter for one second.

Then our catcher, Murphy, groaned.

"Tomorrow? Boss, we're tryin' to win tomorrow."

"You'll catch him now."

Hurtle went to the box. The team gathered behind the
cage. He made two warm-up throws, both easy.

McCall began clucking like a hen.

"Come on, Rube! Show us the country smoke."

The nickname struck instantly.

"Wind up, Rube!"

"Milk the ball, hayseed!"

"Look out fer the cow!"

Hurtle's ears grew scarlet. He took a longer windup and
fired.

The ball tore through Murphy's raised hands, struck the backstop,
and rebounded halfway to second base.

No one laughed.

"Again," said Murphy.

Whit threw his curve. Murphy lunged right and barely stopped
it.

He threw his drop. It buried itself under Murphy's mitt.

Then he cut loose with the fast ball.

Crack!

Murphy pulled off his glove and examined his palm.

"Where'd you steal this scarecrow?" he demanded.

By the time Hurtle had pitched ten minutes, the players
had lost their grins. Spears watched from third base, arms
folded. Radbourne stood beside me.

"He's got everything," I whispered.

"Everything except a notion of where he is," Radbourne replied.

"What do you mean?"

"Look at him."

Whit's eyes shifted from the grand stand to the high
fence, from the scoreboard to the streetcars passing beyond center
field. A hundred people had gathered to watch. Each cheer
caused him to stiffen. He had pitched before farmers, cows,
and a dog. This was a ball park.

"He'll settle," I said.

"Maybe."

That night I found him in his hotel room sitting
on the bed, fully dressed.

"What's wrong?"

"Room's movin'."

"It's on the third floor."

"Feels too high."

He had opened the window and then tied the sash
cord to the bedpost, apparently fearing the building might tip.

I talked baseball to him. That calmed him. He described
the way he held each pitch. He knew nothing of
technical terms, yet his fingers had discovered grips veteran pitchers
spend years learning.

"Tomorrow," I said, "throw to Murphy's mitt. Forget the batter.
Forget the stands."

"How many folks'll be there?"

"Eight or ten thousand."

He swallowed.

"Maybe more."

He lay back without taking off his shoes.

"Boss," he said faintly, "I'd ruther face a bull."

Buffalo arrived the next morning leading the league by six
games. Its players came onto the field laughing, sleek and
confident. Brill sat under his black derby and watched Hurtle
warm up.

The news of the country wonder had spread. The stands
filled early. Boys perched on the fence. Rooters shouted questions
about crops and livestock.

Hurtle's first warm-up pitch sailed over Murphy's head.

The second struck the dirt.

The third went straight.

"That's it," I called. "Easy, Whit. Groove it."

He nodded, pale under his freckles.

McCall ran in from center. "Don't worry, Rube. Them Buffalo
hitters put their pants on one leg at a time."

"Go away," said Murphy. "You're makin' him worse."

The umpire shouted, "Play ball!"

Buffalo's lead-off man, Shanks, stepped in. He was a small,
patient hitter who could worry a pitcher into fits. Whit
gazed at him as if he were an apparition.

Murphy signaled for a fast ball.

Whit wound up.

The pitch went behind Shanks.

The crowd roared.

Shanks dusted his uniform and grinned.

The next pitch almost hit his feet. The next was
high. The fourth struck the backstop.

One walk.

Buffalo's second hitter laid down a bunt. Hurtle charged, picked
up the ball, and threw it ten feet over first
base. Shanks scored. The batter reached third.

The crowd became quiet.

"Steady!" I yelled.

Whit looked toward our bench. His face was the face
of a boy lost in a strange city.

He hit the next batter.

Buffalo's cleanup man doubled against the left-field fence.

Two runs. Men on second and third. No outs.

Spears came in from third.

"Listen, Rube," he said. "Throw the ball."

"I'm throwin' it."

"Try throwin' it toward the plate."

Whit's jaw trembled.

Spears returned to position.

A wild pitch brought home another run. A single scored
two. An error by our shortstop put another man on.
Then came a walk, a sacrifice fly, another double, and
a ground ball that bounced through Spears's legs.

Eight runs!

The Buffalo bench howled. Brill removed his derby, brushed it,
and put it back on at a more triumphant angle.

Our crowd booed. One rooter yelled, "Send him back to
the hayfield!"

Hurtle stood in the box with his cap pulled low,
throwing arm hanging. I started toward him.

Radbourne grabbed my sleeve.

"Leave him."

"He'll never get an out."

"He can get one if he gets mad."

"He's terrified."

"That's why. Fear's got all the room in him. Fill
him with something else."

"What?"

"Anger."

I turned to Spears, who had come toward the bench.

"Go out there," I said. "Make him mad."

Spears stared. "How?"

"Insult him."

"We've been doin' that since yesterday."

"Do it better."

Spears grinned without humor. He marched to the box and
stood nose to chest with Whit.

"Say," he began loudly, "we thought we signed a pitcher."

Whit blinked.

"We got a chicken with long legs. A bow-legged Pogie
from the backwoods."

Hurtle's head jerked.

"What'd you call me?"

"Pogie."

"My name's Whitaker Hurtle."

"Your name's Pogie. Pogie Hurtle, the human rain barrel. You
couldn't pitch horse shoes into a barn."

A few Buffalo players laughed.

Hurtle clenched his fists. "You call me thet ag'in an'
I'll knock yer block off."

"Pogie," said Spears.

Whit stepped forward.

Spears backed away, pointing toward the plate.

"Knock that fellow's block off first. He's laughing at you."

The batter was indeed laughing. He rested his bat on
his shoulder and said, "Come on, Pogie. Toss one over."

Hurtle's face changed.

The terror did not disappear gradually. It went out like
a lantern in a gale. His pale eyes narrowed. His
long fingers closed around the ball. His shoulders seemed to
rise, and for the first time since he entered Worcester
he forgot the grand stand, the scoreboard, the streetcars, and
the eight thousand faces.

He saw one batter.

He wound up.

The ball crossed the plate like a glint of light.

"Strike!" cried the umpire.

The batter's grin vanished.

Murphy returned the ball.

Whit pitched again. A drop. The batter swung a foot
over it.

"Strike two!"

"Now waste one," Murphy called.

Hurtle shook his head.

The third pitch started at the batter's hip and shot
across the inside corner. The bat did not move.

"Strike three!"

The stands erupted—not a great cheer yet, but a startled
burst, as if the crowd had awakened.

Hurtle strode toward the bench.

"You," he said to Spears, "I'm a-goin' to lick you
after the game."

"If you're still alive," said Spears. "There's two out."

Whit went back.

He struck out the next man on four pitches.

The inning was over.

Buffalo eight. Worcester nothing.

I had seen clubs quit with a smaller deficit. Eight
runs in the first inning against the league leaders is
not a hill; it is a mountain with wolves on
it. Our men shuffled in.

Stringer was first to bat. He carried three bats to
the plate, discarded two, and then looked toward me.

"Hit it," I said.

"That's the plan."

Danby, Buffalo's veteran, smiled at him. Every pitcher in the
league knew Stringer's slump. Danby started with a curve outside.
Stringer lunged and missed.

The second pitch was a slow one. Stringer swung too
soon.

"Strike two!"

The crowd groaned.

Danby wasted a fast ball high, then tried another curve.
Stringer fouled it into the seats. The next came inside.
Stringer backed away. Danby smiled again and delivered his favorite—an
outcurve beginning down the center and slipping from a right-handed
batter at the last instant.

Stringer took one short step.

His bat met the ball.

There are hits that produce sound, and there are hits
that stop sound. For a fraction of a second after
that crack the park went dead still. Then every head
turned.

The ball rose toward left center, climbed above the fielders,
climbed above the fence, and kept traveling. It struck the
top of a brewery sign beyond the park and dropped
into the street.

Stringer stood at the plate.

"Run!" screamed McCall.

Stringer began running. At first he jogged as if uncertain
the ball would stay gone. Then the crowd hit him
with a roar. Men flung hats into the air. Women
waved handkerchiefs. Boys hammered the fence.

Stringer rounded third, grinning like a lunatic.

He crossed the plate and seized me by both shoulders.

"I hit it!"

"So I noticed."

"I hit it square!"

"You near killed it."

The players pounded him. Spears shouted. McCall danced. Even Radbourne
swung his cap.

One run seemed foolish against eight, but it was not
the run. It was the crack. It was Stringer's face.
It was the knowledge that Danby could be hit and
that our big gun had fired again.

Momentum is a queer thing. You cannot put it in
a scorebook. You cannot measure it with a tape. But
I have seen it walk into a ball park and
sit on one bench, then cross the field in the
middle of an inning.

It crossed then.

Our next two batters went out, but McCall singled, stole
second, and tried to score on Spears's hit. Buffalo threw
him out at the plate by a step.

McCall jumped up covered with dust.

"I had it beat!"

"You missed it by six feet," said the umpire.

"Then your feet are crooked."

We dragged him away before he could be suspended.

Hurtle faced the lower half of Buffalo's order in the
second. He was still angry. He threw eight pitches. Three
batters struck out. One fouled a ball over the grand
stand roof, and the crowd cheered the foul as if
it had saved the city.

When Whit came to the bench, Spears moved down to
make room.

"Set here, Pogie."

Hurtle remained standing. "After the game."

"Sure."

Worcester scored twice in the second. Radbourne walked. Murphy punched
a single to right. Our shortstop forced Murphy, but Hurtle,
batting ninth, surprised everyone by bunting along third. Danby fielded
it and threw wildly. Radbourne scored. McCall lifted a long
fly, bringing in another.

Eight to three.

The rooters began to calculate.

In the third Buffalo's lead-off man hit a slow roller
between first and second. Our first baseman reached it but
could not make the play. The scorer called it a
hit.

It was the only one Buffalo made after the first
inning.

Whit struck out the next batter. The third hit into
a double play—Spears to Radbourne to first, hard and clean.

As the men ran in, Radbourne said, "He's not throwin'
now."

"What's he doing?"

"Pitchin'."

There is a difference.

A thrower relies upon his arm. A pitcher uses the
batter's eyes, feet, nerves, vanity, and memory. Whit knew none
of those words, but he understood the things themselves. He
watched where a man stood and moved the ball away.
He saw a batter lean over the plate and drove
him back. He showed speed, then took it off; showed
a curve, then fired straight. His awkward body became machinery.
The loose arms, the flying knees, the scarecrow shirt—everything came
together at the instant of delivery, and the ball shot
true.

In our half of the third, Stringer came up again.

Danby tried a fast ball inside. Stringer lashed it against
the right-field wall for a double.

The crowd stood as one.

Our next man singled him to third. Spears drove a
line ball straight at the shortstop. Stringer scrambled back. Radbourne
lifted a sacrifice fly.

Eight to four.

In the fourth Whit struck out two and made the
third pop to Murphy. Buffalo's hitters stopped joking. They began
talking to the umpire, to one another, to the ball,
and finally to themselves.

Worcester went out in order.

In the fifth Whit fanned Danby on three straight balls.
Shanks attempted to bunt and fouled the third strike. The
next batter chopped to Spears.

Still eight to four.

I paced behind the bench. Four runs seemed both near
and impossible. Every inning gone shortened the road. I looked
beyond center field where the evening light lay white on
the buildings, and for one foolish instant I saw the
cottage again—the green shutters, the maples, Milly at the gate.

Then McCall yelled, "Wake up, boss! We got business."

He opened the fifth with a walk. Danby threw twice
to first. McCall took a longer lead.

"Don't," I warned.

He went.

The catcher's throw was perfect, but McCall slid around the
tag, hooked the bag with one hand, and rose in
a cloud of dust.

"Safe!"

Spears bunted him to third. Stringer came up.

Buffalo's manager left the bench to talk with Danby. The
infield drew in. Danby pitched around Stringer. Three balls came
wide. Stringer looked toward me.

"Take," I signaled.

Danby had to put one over. Stringer smashed it through
the box so violently that Danby turned his back. McCall
scored.

Eight to five.

A walk put two men on. Radbourne hit sharply to
second. Buffalo made a double play.

The rally was over.

The crowd groaned, but it was a fighting groan. No
one left the park.

Whit walked to the mound for the sixth. Spears called
after him.

"Come on, Pogie! Hold 'em."

Whit wheeled. "I heerd you."

"Good. I thought maybe them ears was decoration."

The first Buffalo hitter swung at a curve and missed.
The second pitch came inside for a strike. Murphy signaled
low and outside. Whit fired exactly there.

The umpire shouted the third strike.

The next man popped a foul near the Buffalo bench.
Murphy chased it, flung off his mask, and dove among
bats and water buckets. He came up with the ball
and a cut on his chin.

The third batter grounded toward short. An ugly hop struck
our shortstop in the chest, but he recovered and threw
in time.

They came in running now.

No shuffling. No lowered heads. Worcester had remembered how to
be a ball club.

In the sixth we put two men on with one
out. Hurtle was due to bat. I considered a pinch
hitter.

Radbourne saw me looking down the bench.

"Don't take him out."

"I'm not crazy."

"You're thinkin'."

"I need runs."

"You need that fellow more."

Whit overheard.

"I can hit."

"When?" I asked.

"Now."

He selected a bat that looked like a toothpick in
his hands. Danby threw him a curve. Whit swung so
hard he spun around and faced the umpire.

Buffalo laughed.

The next pitch was low. Whit swung again and missed
by a yard.

"Keep yer head!" shouted Spears.

"I'll keep yours fer you after," Whit answered.

Danby wasted one outside. Then he came back with a
fast ball. Whit met it accidentally or by divine intervention
and sent a slow grounder toward third. Both runners advanced,
but he was out at first.

McCall batted with two away. He fouled off three pitches,
then hit a little fly to right. Buffalo's fielder raced
in and caught it at his knees.

No runs.

Eight to five, and three innings left.

The strain got into the crowd. Every pitch brought a
cry. Men stood along the aisles. Telegraph boys forgot their
messages. A policeman near first base shouted advice to Hurtle
until the umpire told him to enforce silence on himself.

The seventh was Whit's grandest inning.

Buffalo sent up its best hitters. The first had doubled
in the opening massacre. He expected speed and received a
slow curve that floated over for strike one. Whit followed
with a fast ball at the shoulders. The batter swung
late.

For the third pitch Whit used the wide outcurve.

The batter began to swing, checked, and was called out.

He flung down his bat.

"That thing went around a corner!"

The second hitter crowded the plate. Whit put one under
his chin. He fell backward, angry, and on the next
delivery swung at a drop in the dirt. Two more
fast balls finished him.

The cleanup man came last. He was the strongest hitter
in the league, a broad, black-mustached fellow named Craney. In
the first he had driven in two runs.

He spat on his hands.

"Let's see the smoke, hayseed."

Whit's eyes narrowed.

The first ball struck Murphy's glove with a report like
a rifle.

Craney had not moved.

The second was faster. He swung late and fouled it
straight back.

The crowd rose.

Whit rubbed the ball. Murphy signaled for a curve. Whit
shook his head. Murphy repeated it. Another shake.

He wanted speed.

"Don't," I muttered. "He's set for it."

Whit lifted his long arms, kicked high, and came down
with every pound of his body behind the pitch.

Craney swung.

The ball was already in Murphy's glove.

"Strike three!"

The roar rolled over the diamond, struck the grand stand
roof, and came back twice as loud. Hurtle stood motionless
for a moment. Then he looked at Spears.

"After the game," he shouted.

Spears laughed so hard he nearly fell over the rail.

Worcester opened the seventh with the heart of the order.
Spears singled over second. Stringer came up, and Buffalo's outfield
moved back until the fielders' heels nearly touched the fence.

Danby did not give him anything good. Stringer waited. Three
balls and one strike.

The next pitch was close.

Stringer took it.

"Ball four!"

Danby argued. Brill ran from the Buffalo bench in his
black derby. The umpire listened, shook his head, and pointed
toward the box. While they quarreled, Spears and Stringer stood
on first and second clapping their hands.

Our next batter laid down a bunt. Danby slipped fielding
it. Everyone was safe.

Bases full. No outs.

That crowd became a living thing. It pounded boards, stamped
feet, whistled, screamed, and called Worcester! Worcester! until the very
grass seemed to quiver.

Radbourne stepped up.

He was old, slow, and wise. Danby tried to make
him hit a bad pitch. Radbourne would not. Two balls.
A strike. Another ball.

Then Danby grooved one.

Radbourne drove it to deep center.

The fielder caught it near the fence. Spears scored. Stringer
took third.

Eight to six.

Murphy came next. I ordered the hit and run, hoping
to keep out of a double play. Murphy swung and
sent a sharp grounder to short. Stringer broke for home.
Buffalo's shortstop flipped to second for one, but Murphy beat
the relay to first.

Stringer scored.

Eight to seven!

The ball park lost its reason.

Men hugged strangers. Boys crawled under the rope. A woman
behind our bench struck a dignified gentleman over the head
with her parasol and then apologized while continuing to cheer.

Murphy stood on first, breathing hard.

Our shortstop hit a line drive toward right. For an
instant it appeared safe. Buffalo's second baseman leaped and speared
it.

Three out.

One run behind.

The eighth inning belonged to fear—but Buffalo's fear now, not
ours.

Its batters watched Whit warm up and talked anxiously. Brill
walked along the bench, jerking at the brim of his
derby. He had seen an eight-run lead shrink to one
and a frightened country boy turn into a giant.

The first batter fouled off two, then struck out on
a drop.

The second walked—the only base on balls Whit allowed after
that first inning. The crowd quieted. Buffalo tried to bunt
him over.

Whit pounced on the ball like a cat, wheeled, and
threw to second. Our shortstop relayed to first.

Double play!

Hurtle leaped straight into the air. His knees seemed to
reach his chin. He came down and ran for the
bench in seven-league boots.

Spears slapped him on the back.

"Great work, Pogie!"

Whit shoved him into the water bucket.

The players roared. Even Spears, sitting in six inches of
water, roared.

We had the top of our order in the eighth.
McCall drove a bounder through the box. Danby knocked it
down with his bare hand and threw him out. Spears
hit a foul that Craney caught against the seats. Stringer
walked for the second time.

I put in a fast runner for the batter behind
him, but the move failed. A curve produced a weak
roller to first.

Eight to seven.

One inning left.

I looked at the scoreboard. The numbers glowed in the
late sun:

BUFFALO—8 0 0 0 0 0 0 0
WORCESTER—1 2 1 0 1 0 2 0

An entire season had narrowed to two blank spaces.

Whit took the mound for the ninth.

Buffalo sent up Danby first. Perhaps exhaustion had reached the
veteran pitcher. He swung at three balls, hitting none.

Shanks came next. He had walked and scored in the
first. He chopped a grounder toward third. Spears fielded it
and threw him out by half a stride.

Two gone.

The last hitter waited through two balls. The next was
a strike at the knees. He fouled one back. Murphy
crouched and signaled for the outcurve.

Whit delivered.

The batter swung. The ball vanished into Murphy's glove.

"Strike three!"

Fourteen strikeouts.

Buffalo had made one scratch hit after the first inning.
Yet we still trailed.

Whit came to the bench breathing easily. The bottom of
the order was up: Radbourne, Murphy, our shortstop, and then
Whit.

"Three runs," he said.

"One'll do," I answered.

"Three's safer."

Radbourne led off. Danby worked carefully and struck him out
with a high fast ball.

Murphy hit a tremendous drive down the left-field line. The
crowd screamed, but the ball curved foul by two feet.
On the next pitch he lifted an easy fly to
center.

Two out.

Our shortstop came up. He had made two errors early,
but he had helped turn the eighth-inning double play. Danby
fed him curves. He fouled one. Took a ball. Fouled
another. Took two more.

Three and two.

The whole park waited.

Danby pitched low.

"Ball four!"

The tying run stood on first.

Hurtle was next.

I looked down the bench. I had one pinch hitter
left, a stocky fellow named Larkin who could drive a
fast ball. Whit had looked helpless at bat except for
his accidental grounder.

I called, "Larkin!"

Whit turned.

"What fer?"

"You're through."

"I hain't."

"We need a hitter."

"I can hit."

"This isn't Rickettsville."

His face darkened. "You callin' me Pogie, too?"

"I'm calling Larkin."

He stepped close. "Boss, if you take me out, I'll
lick you now."

Spears whispered, "Let him hit."

"This game means the pennant."

"He means it."

That was true, but I do not know whether I
let him bat because I trusted him, feared him, or
had become as crazy as the crowd.

"Go on," I said. "But don't swing at bad ones."

Whit seized his bat.

Danby smiled when he saw him approach. Buffalo's infield moved
in. Our runner at first danced off the bag.

The first pitch was a curve. Whit swung and missed.

The second was inside. He swung and missed again.

I groaned.

"Two gone, two strikes, one run behind," said Radbourne softly.
"Couldn't be simpler."

Danby wound up to finish him. The pitch came fast
and straight, aimed at the outer half.

Whit did not swing hard. For once his arms and
legs remained in agreement. He pushed the bat forward and
met the ball squarely.

Crack!

It shot over Danby's head and into center field.

A clean hit!

Our runner raced to third. Whit tore around first and
would have continued to second if the coach had not
tackled him by the sleeve.

Men on first and third. Two out.

McCall at bat.

The crowd called his name in a chant.

"Mac! Mac! Mac! Mac!"

Danby was finished, but Brill left him in. Perhaps no
pitcher could have entered that storm and found the plate.
Perhaps Brill trusted the veteran. Perhaps he was too stunned
to move.

McCall took a strike.

Whit edged away from first. Buffalo's first baseman shouted. Danby
threw over. Whit dived back awkwardly, all elbows and knees.

Danby returned to the rubber.

McCall fouled the next pitch.

Two strikes.

I flashed the signal—hit and run.

Whit stared at me, uncertain.

I gave it again, slower.

He nodded.

Danby began his motion. Whit was off. Not lumbering, not
stumbling. Those long legs devoured ground. He ran as if
wearing seven-league boots.

McCall swung.

The ball jumped between first and second. Buffalo's second baseman
raced left, gloved it, and threw toward first.

McCall beat the play!

Our runner scored from third.

Tie game!

Whit never stopped. He rounded second as the throw went
to first. The first baseman wheeled and fired to third.

The ball sailed wide.

Whit hit third and came on.

"Go!" screamed Spears.

"Come on, Rube!"

The left fielder had backed up the overthrow. He gathered
it and threw home. Whit was halfway down the line,
arms pumping, cap gone, pale hair flying.

Murphy jumped up and down beside the plate.

"Slide!"

Whit did not slide. I doubt he knew how. He
came in upright, a two-legged giraffe charging through dust.

The catcher caught the ball and lunged.

Whit twisted. The glove swept behind him. His foot struck
the plate.

The umpire flung both arms wide.

"Safe!"

Worcester nine. Buffalo eight.

For a moment I heard nothing. I saw the umpire's
arms, Whit sprawled beyond the plate, the catcher holding the
ball, and the entire grand stand rising like a wave.

Then the sound arrived.

It was not cheering. It was thunder.

Players poured from our bench. Spectators leaped the rail. McCall
came from first base at full speed and landed on
top of Whit. Spears followed. Murphy, Stringer, Radbourne, substitutes, bat
boys, policemen, and several citizens joined the pile.

Someone tore my coat. Someone else took my hat. I
found myself running without knowing where I intended to go.

Whit crawled from beneath the heap. His shirt hung in
strips. Dirt covered his face. He looked bewildered.

"Did we git it?" he asked.

"You got it!" I shouted.

"Game over?"

"Game over!"

The Buffalo players walked away through the crowd. Brill's derby
had been crushed flat. Danby paused, looked at Hurtle, and
shook his head.

"Where'd you find that?" he asked me.

"In a pasture."

"Put him back."

Stringer pushed through and grabbed Whit's hand.

"You saved us, boy."

Whit pulled free. "Who's boy?"

Stringer laughed. He had three hits, a home run, and
his old eyes again. The slump was dead.

Around us the rooters chanted "Rube! Rube! Rube!" The nickname
shook the roof. Whit frowned each time he heard it,
but there was pride under the frown.

That victory did not win the pennant by itself. No
single game does, whatever managers may claim afterward. But it
changed Worcester. The players believed in Hurtle, in Stringer, in
one another, and even in me. The fans returned. The
newspapers printed pennant extras. Whit won his next four starts,
and when clubs learned to hit his fast ball, he
beat them with curves. When they waited for curves, he
threw smoke.

Stringer batted over four hundred for the remaining weeks. McCall
stole everything except the bases themselves. Spears fought no more
umpires, though three umpires later said this improvement made them
nervous.

As for the cottage, I bought it that autumn.

Milly stood at the gate beneath the red maples while
I handed her the key.

"Some woman'll be happy here," I reminded her.

Her blue eyes laughed.

"Which woman?"

That question was settled the following June.

But on the afternoon Worcester beat Buffalo nine to eight,
I knew none of what lay ahead. I knew only
that the pennant had stopped fading, the successful season was
alive, the business deal possible, the cottage waiting, and Milly—Milly
nearer than she had been that morning.

I was thinking of all this when Whit approached me
in the clubhouse.

The other players became suddenly quiet.

He had washed the dust from his face, but a
bruise darkened one cheek where McCall's knee had struck him
during the celebration. He still wore his torn shirt. In
one hand he carried the game ball. The other was
doubled into a fist.

"Boss," he said.

"Great pitching, Whit."

"Fourteen struck out."

"That's right."

"One measly hit after them eight runs."

"That's right."

"An' I made the winnin' run."

"You certainly did."

He stepped closer.

"Spears called me Pogie."

"He did it to make you mad."

"I'm still mad."

Spears slipped behind Radbourne.

Whit pointed at him. "I'm a-goin' to lick you."

Then he pointed at me.

"An' you tried to bat fer me."

"I reconsidered."

"You called me a hayseed in yer mind."

"I never did."

"You fetched me here where all them lunatics call me
Rube."

"They call you that because they like you."

He considered this. From outside came the cry of thousands
still waiting around the park:

"Rube! Rube! Rube!"

Whit's ears reddened.

He raised his fists.

"I don't keer," he said. "I'm a-goin' to lick you,
too!"

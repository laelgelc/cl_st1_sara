# THE STILL-VEXED ISLANDS

It was in the latter part of a voyage from
England, after we had been for three weary weeks the
sport of that dark and angry sea which rolls between
the banks of Newfoundland and the warm latitudes, that our
captain, despairing of the direct course, put the ship about
and bore away toward the south. Until then the Atlantic
had shown us no countenance of friendship. Day followed day
beneath a low roof of cloud; the waves came after
us like black hills in pursuit; the cordage dripped, the
decks were never dry, and the wind, wandering through the
rigging, uttered at night those lamentable cries which a landsman
may ridicule by his fireside, but which, heard in mid-ocean,
seem to proceed from the drowned multitude beneath.

There were hours when the bowsprit dipped as if pointing
to our grave; when the men at the wheel, lashed
fast lest they should be swept away, could scarcely distinguish
the binnacle light; when a loose spar, striking in the
darkness, startled the sleepers below with a sound like the
first stroke of dissolution. Our little world became a republic
of damp garments, broken crockery, bruised limbs, and anxious faces.
Even the most cheerful among us ceased to predict the
hour of our arrival, and contented themselves with desiring that
we might arrive somewhere.

But no sooner had the ship descended toward the region
of the Bermudas than winter, as if forbidden to pass
an invisible boundary, fell behind us. The wind softened; the
seas, lately so fierce and mountainous, sank into long undulations;
a warmth, delicate at first, came over the water, carrying
with it some indefinable fragrance, or the fancy of one,
which was more persuasive than any chart. The wet sails
were spread to dry; the hatches were opened; pale passengers
crept from their imprisonment; and the sailors, with that prompt
forgetfulness of evil which is among the best appurtenances of
their calling, began to whistle over the very ropes that
had threatened them with destruction.

On the second evening of this deliverance, the clouds withdrew
in shining companies, and the moon rose upon a sea
so tranquil that I could hardly realize it to be
the same element whose tumult had lately filled our ears
and imaginations. Dolphins rolled beside us, showing for an instant
a polished back in the pale mysterious light; flying-fish, startled
from the bows, skimmed away like silver arrows; and here
and there the frail sail of a nautilus appeared upon
the smoothness, so slight a thing that one wondered what
protection Providence had contrived for it amid hurricanes. Some person
in the cabin touched a guitar. The notes ascended through
the open skylight, mingled with the whisper at the vessel's
side, and died upon the air as though all sounds
had been charmed to silence.

At dawn the islands rose before us.

They did not lift themselves boldly, like the mountainous Antilles,
nor oppose to the sea those austere and precipitate brows
by which many tropical lands announce their grandeur. They lay
low and green upon the water; but their greenness, under
the early sun, had a freshness almost supernatural after our
long prospect of foam and vapour. Cedar woods darkened the
interior; little white roofs shone among gardens; the reefs, revealed
through water of extraordinary clearness, enclosed lakes of blue and
emerald; and the narrow beaches seemed lines of ivory drawn
between sea and leaf. Birds passed over us with cries
that had in them no omen of tempest. A boat
came off, rowed by men whose easy motion and sunburnt
cheerfulness appeared part of the general serenity.

It was then that the phrase, familiar since boyhood, returned
to me with a new and almost audible significance—"the still-vexed
Bermoothes." Here were the Bermoothes; but where was the vexation?
Surely not in that transparent sea, which seemed incapable of
concealing so much as a shell; not among those gardens,
whose oranges glowed between glossy leaves; not in the mild
air, which swelled our canvas so softly that the ship
made a whispering way. Yet the contradiction itself was Shakespearean.
The place might well have been prepared for one of
those enchanted reversals in which terror puts off its mask
and discovers beneficence beneath; in which the foundering wreck brings
every soul safely to land; in which the savage island
proves more hospitable than the civilized state.

I had long known, as every reader of *The Tempest*
knows, that learned persons had looked toward these islands when
discussing the origin of that play. But the notion, while
it remained a marginal comment in a book, had possessed
for me little vitality. Now, as we moved along the
reefs, and the cedar-scented breeze came over the quarter, conjecture
took colour from the scene. I began to inquire—not with
the confidence of one who supposes that the imagination of
Shakespeare can be tracked like a smuggler to a single
landing-place, but with the modest pleasure of a traveller who
sees old words suddenly illuminated—whether the fortunes, fears, and follies
of the first English adventurers in Bermuda might not have
supplied something more than a name to the poet's romance.

The reader shall determine what strength there is in my
surmise. I will only set before him the history as
it appeared to me, gathering its several particulars from those
grave pamphlets and relations in which our ancestors recorded wonders
with an admirable union of piety, credulity, and commercial exactness.

In the year 1609, when the colony of Virginia hung
between hope and dissolution, a fleet of nine vessels sailed
from Plymouth to carry men, provisions, and authority to Jamestown.
The chief officers were embarked in the *Sea Venture*, a
new ship of three hundred tons, having aboard Sir Thomas
Gates, Sir George Somers, Captain Christopher Newport, and a numerous
company of sailors, soldiers, gentlemen, labourers, women, and children. Sir
George Somers, admiral of the fleet, was already renowned as
a seaman—resolute, generous, and of that bodily hardihood which, in
an age of difficult navigation, was almost a moral virtue.

For a time the voyage prospered. Then, near the end
of July, there arose one of those hurricanes which seem
not so much disturbances of the air as rebellions of
the whole visible creation. The ships were scattered and lost
sight of one another. The *Sea Venture*, labouring beneath an
intolerable weight of wind and water, sprang a leak. The
sea poured into her faster than the pumps could discharge
it; the seams opened; the men, divided into companies, stood
waist-deep in the hold, passing buckets or working the machinery
until hands were blistered and strength exhausted.

During three days and four nights there was scarcely cessation.
Gentlemen who had never before performed the office of a
common sailor took their turns with the rest; those who
fell down from weariness were dragged aside, and others entered
the flooded hold. The storm allowed neither cooking nor sleep.
The vessel, deprived of her boats and much of her
rigging, wallowed like a thing already dead, though not yet
permitted to sink.

At last, when labour appeared useless, some yielded to despair.
They had done what men could do; the water increased
upon them; the ship must founder. There was wine in
the vessel, and a portion of the company, conceiving themselves
upon the threshold of another world, drank farewell to one
another in this. It was a strange carousal: pale faces,
drenched garments, the staggering lamp, the thunder of seas against
the planks, and above all the dreadful composure of those
who, having lost hope, are relieved also from exertion.

In the midst of this extremity came a cry from
aloft—"Land!"

No word in human speech could have produced a more
violent resurrection. The exhausted started up; men rushed upon deck;
every eye strained through rain and spray. But the land
announced was Bermuda, and Bermuda, in the opinion of mariners,
was scarcely less terrible than the sea from which they
sought escape.

The islands had long been shunned. They were environed by
hidden reefs; violent storms appeared to make them their peculiar
habitation; strange cries were heard there at night; and ships
passing at a distance reported fires, apparitions, and confused noises
among the rocks. The Spaniards and Portuguese, though acquainted with
the group, had established no settlement upon it. Sailors called
the place an enchanted den, the habitation of fiends, the
"islands of devils." To be driven upon Bermuda was not
to be saved from destruction, but merely to exchange the
manner of it.

Sir George Somers, however, saw what fear might have concealed
from a less steadfast judgment. The ship could not remain
afloat; the land, whatever spirits possessed it, offered the only
possibility of life. Taking the helm, he steered directly toward
the breakers. On either side the reef rose like a
wall; before them appeared a narrow opening, more guessed than
discerned through the storm. The *Sea Venture* entered it, struck
violently, and was lifted by a succeeding wave. Then, by
one of those felicitous accidents which look in retrospect like
deliberate mercies, she lodged firmly between two rocks, so that,
although her hull was broken and the sea passed through
her, she did not go down.

The shore was near. Lines were carried out; boats and
rafts were contrived; and every person aboard—more than a hundred
and forty souls, according to the common account—reached land alive.
Even a dog came safely from the wreck. Thus the
dreaded islands, which imagination had peopled with devils, received the
castaways more gently than many a Christian harbour.

Their first wonder was increased by every hour of examination.
Instead of a burnt and sterile wilderness, they found a
country clothed in cedar, palmetto, and luxuriant herbage. The climate
was temperate; fresh water could be obtained; the soil promised
abundance. Hogs, descended, it was supposed, from animals left by
earlier voyagers, ran wild in the woods. Fish crowded the
bays; turtles came upon the beaches; birds nested so thickly
that they might be taken by hand or struck down
with sticks. The long-tailed cahows, unacquainted with man and therefore
innocent of fear, filled the night with lamentable cries—the very
voices, perhaps, which passing mariners had attributed to demons.

Here was another transformation suitable to romance. The "most dangerous,
unfortunate, and forlorn" place in the world became, upon acquaintance,
a garden. The devils proved to be birds; the enchanted
fires were perhaps no more than lightning among the cedars;
and famine, which had threatened in the ship, was repelled
by a natural banquet.

I do not mean that their life was without hardship.
Men who have escaped together from death are not thereby
secured against disagreement; indeed, self-gratulation soon restores those distinctions and
appetites which terror had temporarily abolished. At first all laboured
in common. Cabins were erected from palmetto leaves and timber
saved from the wreck. The provisions were brought ashore. A
bell was hung, watches appointed, worship observed, and something resembling
an English settlement arose amid the solitude. But presently there
were murmurings concerning authority, labour, and the necessity of proceeding
to Virginia.

Some, delighted with Bermuda, asked why they should abandon plenty
for a colony reputed to be miserable. Here there was
food without purchase, land without landlord, and a climate which
made the want of elaborate houses tolerable. Virginia, on the
other hand, promised discipline, danger, and labour. The island tempted
them with what Gonzalo was afterward to imagine:

> "No kind of traffic
> Would I admit; no name of magistrate;
> Letters should not be known; riches, poverty,
> And use of service, none."

But even a commonwealth without riches requires agreement, and agreement
was precisely what these new philosophers lacked. Certain of the
company denied the authority of Gates; others secretly withdrew into
the woods; one faction meditated remaining, another departure. There were
accusations, conspiracies, punishments, pardons, and renewals of conspiracy. The little
island, which contained neither market nor court nor private estate,
contrived nevertheless to reproduce in miniature many of the discontents
of Europe.

Before these dissensions reached their height, a long-boat had been
fitted with a deck and sent to seek Virginia. Henry
Ravens, an able mariner, commanded her. He carried a small
crew and promised, if he arrived, to send relief. Those
who watched the boat diminish upon the bright water must
have felt that their connection with mankind went with it.
Days passed; then weeks. No answer came. The boat was
never heard of again. Whether she foundered in the open
sea, missed the coast, or fell victim to some sudden
tempest, remains among the innumerable unrecorded tragedies of navigation.

The loss did not extinguish resolution. From the cedar of
Bermuda and the soundest portions of the wreck, two vessels
were built. The larger was named the *Deliverance*, the smaller
the *Patience*—names in which the history of the company may
almost be read. Cordage, ironwork, sails, and stores were drawn
from the *Sea Venture*. Men who had expected to lie
beneath the Atlantic became shipwrights; trees which had stood for
centuries over the cries of cahows were shaped into keels
and ribs; and under the direction of Somers and Gates
the two small craft slowly assumed the form of their
deliverance.

Meanwhile, rebellion showed itself repeatedly. Some refused duty; some concealed
themselves; some proposed a permanent separation from the main body.
Severity alternated with forgiveness. One unhappy man suffered death; others,
after confession or submission, were restored. It is not difficult,
looking upon Bermuda from the secure deck of a modern
ship, to condemn either the mutineers or their governors. Yet
isolation magnifies grievances as a lens magnifies insects. A command
spoken sharply becomes tyranny; an hour's additional labour becomes bondage;
the cedar grove beyond the hill appears a kingdom of
liberty. Every discontented man hears in the surf the applause
of an imaginary people.

At length, after about nine months upon the islands, the
company embarked. A few men remained behind, some willingly and
some through the accidents of faction; of these I shall
presently speak. The rest, carrying provisions of pork, fish, and
other island plenty, sailed in May 1610. Bermuda, which had
first appeared as the jaws of destruction, receded behind them
as a lost Eden.

Their voyage to Virginia was short. Their reception there was
the final and darkest reversal.

Instead of a flourishing colony, they found Jamestown reduced by
famine, sickness, and war. Of several hundred persons, scarcely sixty
remained alive; and these were so wasted that the newcomers
looked upon them with consternation. Houses had been burned for
fuel; palisades had fallen; the fields were neglected; hunger had
dissolved courage and almost humanity itself. The ample stores brought
from Bermuda could postpone ruin, but not remove it. Gates
determined to abandon the settlement; and the colonists had actually
embarked when, descending the river, they encountered Lord De La
Warr with men and supplies from England. Thus Virginia also
was snatched back, at the latest instant, from apparent dissolution.

Sir George Somers, seeing that food remained the principal necessity,
offered to return to Bermuda and gather provisions. He was
no longer young; the labours of the wreck, the island,
and the voyage had tried a constitution which courage perhaps
persuaded him was inexhaustible. Nevertheless, he sailed again in the
*Patience*, accompanied by his nephew and a small crew.

Bad weather separated him from another vessel and drove him
far to the north; yet he persisted, found the islands,
and began the work for which he had returned. Hogs
were killed and salted; fish taken; stores collected. But the
hardships of the voyage had entered too deeply into him.
He sickened and died in Bermuda, among the cedars of
that country which he had discovered for England by the
strange road of shipwreck.

His last thought was said to have been for Virginia.
He directed those about him to carry the provisions thither,
where men were starving. It was an instruction consonant with
his whole conduct—plain, useful, and dutiful. But grief sometimes disguises
self-will beneath the robes of reverence. His nephew and the
sailors, unwilling to leave the body of their commander in
that remote soil, embalmed it and sailed for England, carrying
the dead admiral home while the food intended for the
living remained behind. His heart, according to tradition, was buried
in Bermuda; his body lies in his native Dorsetshire. So
ended a gallant life, with a divided sepulture appropriate to
one whose fame belongs both to England and to the
islands.

The report of Bermuda's fertility could not now be suppressed.
Pamphlets circulated; merchants calculated; divines discerned Providence; gentlemen obtained patents.
The Virginia Company first extended its interest over the group,
and afterward a separate company undertook its colonization. What had
been the terror of navigators became an object of investment.
The demons were dispossessed by surveyors; shares succeeded enchantments; and
cedar, ambergris, tobacco, pearls, and other hopeful commodities entered those
optimistic catalogues by which English enterprise consoled itself for present
expense.

Before the regular settlers arrived, however, Bermuda passed through an
interval of government too remarkable to be omitted. It had
three kings.

These potentates were Christopher Carter, Edward Waters, and Edward Chard,
sailors or adventurers left upon the islands after the departure
of Gates and Somers. Their titles were not acknowledged by
any European sovereign; their treasury contained little money; their court
had no chamberlain; and, since there were no subjects, the
burden of obedience did not fall heavily upon the population.
Yet in a country where each man possessed as much
land as he could walk over, and where no superior
authority was visible between the beach and heaven, they may
be pardoned if their deportment acquired something of the monarchical.

The tripartite sovereignty began prosperously. The kings built or occupied
cabins, hunted hogs, caught fish, and gathered such fruits as
the islands afforded. If His Majesty Carter desired a turtle,
Their Majesties Waters and Chard assisted in turning it. If
King Waters repaired a roof, the neighbouring crowns brought palmetto
leaves. There were no customs duties, for there was no
foreign trade; no taxation, for there was no currency; no
war, since every power in Bermuda was bound by hunger
to the same alliance.

They might have continued in this golden simplicity, affording future
philosophers an unanswerable example of natural government, had not Fortune,
who takes pleasure in introducing European evils into the most
secluded places, cast upon their shore a mass of ambergris.

Ambergris, that mysterious substance then valued for perfumes and medicines,
was worth an immense sum in the markets of Europe.
The three men recognized, if not its precise price, at
least the magnitude of their acquisition. In an instant the
political constitution of Bermuda changed. Yesterday there had been only
a shapeless object upon the beach; today there were property,
capital, jealousy, expectation, and the fear of fraud.

Each monarch considered by what right the others claimed two-thirds.
Carter had perhaps first seen it; Waters had perhaps first
touched it; Chard may have assisted most vigorously in dragging
it beyond reach of the tide. Memory, formerly indifferent, became
wonderfully exact. Services were enumerated, words misquoted, intentions suspected. The
commonwealth of nature disappeared; mine and thine, those indefatigable legislators,
established their jurisdiction.

It is said that threats followed argument, and that the
three kings, unable to divide the treasure without diminishing it,
prepared to divide one another. Their armouries were modest, but
sufficient: muskets, swords, knives, and such implements as solitary men
keep ready against accident and afterward employ against friendship. Bermuda
trembled upon the edge of civil war.

Carter, whether more pacific or merely more prudent than his
fellow sovereigns, perceived that discussion would be less sanguinary if
the weapons vanished. During a favourable opportunity he collected and
concealed them. The belligerents, discovering themselves disarmed, were compelled to
continue hostilities by reproaches alone. Thus was bloodshed prevented; and
Carter deserves to be remembered among those legislators who have
understood that the easiest means of preserving peace is sometimes
to remove the instruments by which angry men intend to
defend it.

Their quarrel did not restore the ambergris to innocence. Each
remained watchful; each feared that another would conceal, barter, or
appropriate the prize. They possessed wealth in prospect and misery
in hand. While they had owned nothing, the whole island
had been theirs; now that they owned a treasure, they
could scarcely enjoy a meal without suspicion.

In 1612 Governor Richard More arrived with a body of
settlers and the necessary apparatus of civilization—authority, instructions, clerks, divisions
of land, and a company's claim to whatever of value
might be found. The reign of the three kings ended
without battle. Their treasure, upon which each had built palaces
in imagination, was declared to belong to the colonizing company.
Thus foreign intervention settled the domestic controversy by dispossessing all
parties alike.

There is something so perfectly comic, and yet so faithfully
representative of greater histories, in this little episode that one
hesitates whether to laugh at the kings or salute them
as founders of political science. Their poverty produced concord; wealth
produced rivalry; armament threatened war; disarmament preserved an angry peace;
and organized government concluded the matter by taking possession of
the disputed revenue. Many an empire has performed the same
drama upon a larger stage, with trumpets, proclamations, and several
thousand graves.

It was while reflecting upon this insular monarchy that I
returned to the question with which I began. Shakespeare's *Tempest*
was first performed, so far as we can ascertain, within
a short time after the news of the *Sea Venture*
and the Bermuda deliverance had reached England. Accounts of the
event circulated both in manuscript and print. There was a
*True Declaration of the Estate of the Colonie in Virginia*;
there were letters and narratives among members of the Virginia
Company; and the extraordinary circumstances of the wreck furnished conversation
wherever maritime adventure, colonial enterprise, or Providence was discussed.

I make no claim that Shakespeare took a chronicle in
one hand and composed his play with the other. Genius
does not borrow as a tradesman measures cloth. It receives
a fact, a cadence, an image, a rumour; these descend
into its mysterious possession, suffer a "sea-change," and emerge as
something "rich and strange." Yet correspondences, when numerous, have their
persuasion.

Here was a ship, carrying persons of rank and authority,
separated from a fleet by tempest. Here was a vessel
believed to be lost, yet lodged so fortunately that every
soul reached shore alive. Here were islands long reputed the
seat of devils, voices, and enchantments. Here the dreaded wilderness
proved fertile and temperate, supplying food in miraculous abundance. Here
men debated a natural commonwealth; authority was resisted; factions arose;
and a few obscure persons, cut off from society, assumed
the airs of independent rulers.

In the play, the storm terrifies but does not destroy.
"Not a hair perish'd," says Ariel; the passengers are scattered
in groups, yet preserved. The island rings with noises whose
origin is invisible:

> "The isle is full of noises,
> Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt
not."

How closely this answers to Bermuda's ancient terrors transformed by
experience—the cries of birds taken for spirits, the thunder among
reefs, the lights seen by passing ships, the very music
of wind in cedar! Caliban himself, though represented as savage
and deformed, speaks of the island's enchantments not with fear
but tenderness; as the castaways learned, what alarms the stranger
may be familiar, even beneficent, to the inhabitant.

Then there is Gonzalo's commonwealth, without traffic, magistrate, riches, poverty,
service, inheritance, or labour. The speech is drawn in part
from an older and greater essayist; yet could it not
have acquired a fresh pleasantry from the reported condition of
men living freely upon an abundant island, reluctant to proceed
to the discipline and want of Virginia? And when Stephano,
Trinculo, and Caliban plot sovereignty over the place—when a butler
becomes a lord, a jester a councillor, and the poor
monster imagines liberty beneath a new master—may we not hear,
however faintly, the mock-heroic history of Bermuda's three kings?

I confess that Carter, Waters, and Chard appear to me
almost ready-made for such transformation. They need only pass through
the poet's imagination. Their ambergris may become a bottle; their
dispute over property, a conspiracy for dominion; their island monarchy,
the grotesque kingdom of Stephano. History rarely supplies exact originals
for dramatic persons; it supplies rather provocative shapes, which the
dramatist enlarges, combines, and animates.

Even the moral atmosphere is kindred. The Bermuda story is
made of reversals. Storm becomes deliverance; devils become birds; a
wreck becomes a storehouse; wilderness becomes garden; abundance becomes mutiny;
castaways become kings; treasure becomes affliction; and the man who
returns to save the starving is carried home dead by
those who disobey his charitable command. *The Tempest* also advances
by such mutations. Enemies are preserved by what appears destruction;
a usurped duke rules an island; a prince, thinking his
father drowned, finds a bride; vengeance changes into pardon; and
the magician, having recovered power, renounces the instrument of it.

When our ship had passed the last pale reef and
Bermuda began to sink behind the horizon, evening was again
descending. The islands lost their separate forms and became a
single dusky line, half earth and half cloud. For a
little while the cedar fragrance seemed still to follow us;
then the wind freshened, the sails filled, and the open
Atlantic resumed its dominion.

I stood aft, watching until the line disappeared. I could
not persuade myself that we had merely passed a cluster
of cultivated islands, accurately surveyed, commercially governed, and entered with
reassuring precision upon the chart. Behind that fading verge remained
the older Bermuda: the haunted Bermoothes of mariners; the green
refuge of the *Sea Venture*; the workshop of the *Deliverance*
and the *Patience*; the grave of Somers's heart; the throne
of three ragged monarchs; the possible island of a poet's
dream.

Whether Shakespeare saw in the narratives all that I have
imagined, the reader may determine. My argument is no more
than a surmise, gathered upon a tranquil sea. Yet there
are places where history seems to lie unusually near the
surface of romance, as the coral lies beneath those transparent
waters; and Bermuda, feared before it was known, fruitful when
it was expected to be accursed, discovered by shipwreck and
governed for a season by kings without subjects, is surely
one of them.

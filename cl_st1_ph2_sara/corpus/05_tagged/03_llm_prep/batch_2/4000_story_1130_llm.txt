I

Eeldrop and Appleplex had taken two rooms above a laundress
in a street which no guide to London mentioned and
no cabman admitted knowing until the fare was doubled. The
street maintained, by daylight, a defeated respectability. Its houses had
fanlights, iron knockers, and narrow areas in which geraniums survived
by taking nourishment from soot. At night the blinds disclosed
complicated domesticities: a woman ironing beneath a green shade; a
clerk removing his collar with the solemnity of a priest;
a child smearing treacle upon a newspaper; and, occasionally, a
hand which extinguished the lamp immediately upon being observed.

Opposite stood a police station.

The laundress believed that Eeldrop was connected with publishing and
that Appleplex was connected with the government. She therefore mistrusted
the former and respected the latter. In reality, neither occupation
described them, though each description afforded a certain protection. Their
rooms contained a camp bed, two cane chairs, a cracked
washstand, a spirit lamp, three volumes of speculative philosophy, a
tin of oatmeal, and a pair of field-glasses. Appleplex also
kept twelve black notebooks, labelled in his small upright hand:
OCCUPATIONS, FACES, METAPHYSICAL PRETENSIONS, HABITS OF EATING, UNCLASSIFIED, and so
forth. Eeldrop objected to the labels and borrowed the notebooks.

They came there periodically, as men go to a monastery,
a gaming-house, or an aunt in the country. They escaped
from lives which, though not disgraceful, had become offensively explanatory.
Here nobody asked what they represented. They could sit at
the window after dusk, smelling damp linen, fried haddock, stale
tobacco, and the tepid breath of the public-house at the
corner, and watch the police station deliver its occasional fragment
of human mystery.

On one such evening a van arrived. Two constables descended,
followed by a small man in a grey coat whose
sleeve had been torn from shoulder to elbow. There was
no blood visible. This disappointed the crowd, which gathered with
the silent speed of particles obeying a physical law. A
butcher's boy stood on tiptoe; three women emerged without hats;
a gentleman with a Pekinese paused at a distance compatible
with innocence; and an old man, chewing bread with fat,
deliberate fingers, asked everyone what had occurred and rejected every
answer.

The prisoner did not resist. He looked once at the
upper windows opposite, not as if he saw Eeldrop, but
as if some ancient calculation had presented an unforeseen remainder.
Then he entered the station.

"Murder," said the butcher's boy.

"Drink," said one of the women.

"Foreign," said the gentleman with the dog.

Appleplex made a note.

"You see," said Eeldrop, "how rapidly the world repairs the
wound of individuality. A man passes through that door carrying
an act which may have destroyed his universe; before the
latch falls, he is murder, drink, or foreign."

"You have omitted insanity," said Appleplex. "It will appear when
the evening paper arrives."

"Insanity is merely the most comfortable drawer. It absolves the
classifier from imagining motive."

"It may also be true."

"Truth is not the point."

Appleplex lowered his pencil. "That is a dangerous concession from
a seeker after reality."

"Not truth as correspondence, accuracy, evidence, and the other virtues
of magistrates. I mean that even if the man is
drunk, foreign, homicidal, and mad, the accumulation does not approach
him. It surrounds him. We know the categories through which
he may be condemned; we do not know the instant
in which he became irretrievable to himself."

A fly settled upon the rim of the oatmeal tin.
Appleplex watched it clean its forelegs.

"You assume an instant," he said. "Most actions are sedimentary.
A rejected cheque, a diseased child, a humiliating employer, five
years of custard and prunes in a boarding-house, one woman's
laugh repeated beyond endurance—these form layers. At last a hand
moves. The catastrophe looks sudden because the public arrives late."

"But the public also departs early. It sees the hand
move and misses what follows. There are actions after which
the self can no longer return to its previous fiction.
The murderer may escape the gallows, the adulterer may be
forgiven, the fraudulent cashier may repay the bank; yet an
irreversible thing has entered consciousness. Theology understood this badly but
intensely. Sociology understands it accurately and not at all."

"You are contrasting medieval hell with municipal statistics."

"I am contrasting consequence with classification."

Below them the old man spat with care between two
paving stones. The women discussed whether the prisoner had worn
boots or slippers. Their disagreement became moral. Boots implied preparation;
slippers implied domestic provocation.

Appleplex resumed writing.

"There are," he said, "at least seven socially distinct methods
of spitting. The artisan projects; the loafer allows; the prosperous
tradesman conceals; the young intellectual forgets; the elderly radical asserts.
Our friend below belongs to a vanishing school."

"You are incorrigible."

"I observe particulars."

"You collect them until they become generals. You begin with
one old man's saliva and end with the Decline of
England."

"Whereas you begin with one torn sleeve and end with
the abyss of the soul."

They were silent. Across the street, the crowd thinned. The
gentleman's Pekinese, having exhausted its suspicion of the police, turned
to a cabbage stalk in the gutter. Behind the station
curtains a shadow crossed and recrossed a yellow pane.

The neighborhood had been selected for its privacy, but its
privacy was not the privacy of innocence. Respectability there had
gone soft at the edges. A piano teacher received gentlemen
who brought no music; a physician prescribed chiefly to women
with veils; a religious bookseller kept racing forms beneath the
counter. The vegetation in the adjoining squares possessed a tropical
luxuriance when seen from above, but at ground level every
leaf was dusty and every root mouldered among bottles. In
summer the air was full of flies, piano scales, and
the exhausted cries of children.

Eeldrop and Appleplex preferred it to districts where vice had
been professionally arranged. The openly disreputable quarter, said Eeldrop, was
as conventional as a cathedral close. Here concealment retained a
private incompetence.

"What we seek," he continued, "is not the exceptional person.
The exceptional person is already a public institution. Nor do
we seek the commonplace, which is a statistical ghost. We
seek the person before he has been explained."

"And how shall we recognize him?"

"By the failure of our explanations."

"That principle would make my aunt one of the profoundest
beings in Europe."

"Perhaps she is."

"She breeds canaries and attributes the Franco-Prussian War to unsuitable
underclothing."

"You have classified her."

"I have lived with her."

Eeldrop leaned back. In the uncertain light his face suggested
a theologian who had misplaced his doctrine and was not
altogether sorry.

"What is important," he said, "is what cannot be undone.
Not what the newspapers enlarge, nor what reforms abolish, nor
what biographies make inevitable after the event. A man may
spend forty years in harmless repetition; then in ten seconds
he recognizes that another consciousness exists and that he has
injured it. That recognition may be the only important event
of his life."

"Does he need to have injured it?"

"For complete knowledge, possibly."

"You are becoming savage."

"I am becoming precise."

Appleplex closed the notebook. "And yet tomorrow morning you will
leave here, travel southwest, and concern yourself with overdrafts."

Eeldrop looked at him.

"You have been examining my letters."

"I have been examining your boots. Suburban clay. Also, last
Tuesday you said 'we' when discussing the reduction of clerical
salaries."

Since confession, when accidental, is the least dignified mode of
autobiography, Eeldrop supplied the remainder without ornament. He was a
clerk in a suburban bank. He had a wife whose
judgment was sound, three children whose illnesses were seasonal, and
a garden containing a pear tree, six rose bushes, and
a strip of lawn upon which nothing could persuade grass
to grow. On weekdays he took the 8:17. On Saturdays
he compared grocery accounts. His wife believed these visits were
connected with an amateur society for economic research.

Appleplex listened gravely.

"This improves you," he said.

"It destroys your image of me."

"I had no image. I had a provisional file."

"And your own occupation?"

"My occupations are plural."

"Which means that none pays you."

"Several pay irregularly. I am preparing a survey of contemporary
society."

"In twelve notebooks?"

"There are twenty-three at home. These are the portable series."

Eeldrop laughed. "We flee our assigned existences in order to
apprehend the unclassified, and you bring a filing system."

"Detachment requires apparatus."

"No. Apparatus requires detachment. That is why your observations are
always in danger of becoming a census of the invisible."

"And yours a sermon addressed to a prisoner whose name
you do not know."

At eleven the station door opened. A constable came out,
stretched, and examined the sky. Nothing was revealed. The two
observers prepared oatmeal on the spirit lamp and ate it
without sugar, as befitted an inquiry into reality.

II

Several Sundays later the air was so warm that the
curtains hung without movement and the wallpaper seemed to exhale
the odour of all previous tenants. Appleplex arrived carrying a
packet tied with mauve ribbon.

"You have brought evidence," said Eeldrop.

"Correspondence, theatre programmes, two photographs, a publisher's notice, and the
copy of a hotel bill."

"Whose?"

"Edith's."

The woman whom certain friends called Scheherazade had never, so
far as could be established, told a story from beginning
to end. This was among her attractions. She had married
at nineteen a manufacturer of surgical fittings, left him during
a thunderstorm, divorced him in a jurisdiction known chiefly for
divorces, danced for six weeks in a production which closed
after four, travelled through Egypt with a baronet's widow, studied
singing in Milan, published seventeen poems under a masculine name,
and returned to London wearing amber beads and an expression
acquired, it was said, in the desert.

Thereafter she moved among painters who distrusted paint, composers who
rejected sound, socialists with servants, actresses with titled mothers, and
titled women who wished to be mistaken for actresses. She
was seen at first nights, last suppers, private exhibitions, public
scandals, and lectures upon the liberation of instinct. A novelist
had dedicated a book to her and later denied the
acquaintance. She possessed no money with regularity, yet was never
observed to lack the precise costume demanded by her poverty.

Eeldrop had met her twice.

"She is," he said, "one of those beings for whom
experience is not an incident but an element. She does
not live by decisions. She is carried from intensity to
intensity, and the inconsistency of her life is merely the
consistency of passion."

Appleplex untied the ribbon. "She retained the hotel bill."

"A practical necessity."

"She has annotated it: 'The night of my spiritual abandonment.'
The charge includes two breakfasts."

"Perhaps she was not alone."

"Then the abandonment was incomplete."

Eeldrop refused the photograph offered to him. "You mistake self-consciousness
for insincerity. A person may recognize the drama of her
own life without falsifying it."

"Recognition is not the difficulty. Direction is. Edith does not
merely recognize the drama; she engages the theatre, distributes the
parts, arranges the notices, and keeps the receipts."

"You speak as a disappointed archivist."

"I speak as an archivist whose subject has done the
filing in advance."

Appleplex spread the documents on the table. There was a
photograph of Edith in profile, severe and pale beneath an
immense hat; another showed her laughing beside a marble torso.
The poems concerned salt, blood, moons, and the awakening of
Asia. A theatre programme bore three names in pencil, two
crossed out. The publisher's notice called her "a voice from
beyond convention," though the poems had been issued by subscription.

Eeldrop moved to the window. Sunday evening had emptied the
street without purifying it. A blind flapped above the physician's
brass plate. Somewhere a gramophone repeated eight bars of a
sentimental song.

"You have reduced her to exhibits," he said.

"On the contrary, the exhibits are her chosen emanations. Consider
the room in which she wakes. A silk stocking hangs
from the bedpost; there are cigarette ends in a saucer
painted with lilies; scent, cold cream, unpaid bills, an invitation
from a duchess, a note from an unemployed violinist, a
volume of Nietzsche opened with a hairpin, three bruised roses,
a chipped cup containing black coffee, and on the floor
a manuscript entitled 'Nakedness.' The sunlight passes through a torn
blind. Edith opens her eyes. What does she do?"

"She remembers whom she loved."

"She first remembers what she must remember."

"That is a distinction without mercy."

"It is the essential distinction. Her room appears accidental, but
every object has passed inspection. Even disorder has been placed."

Eeldrop turned. "You have never entered her room."

"No, but she has described it to four people, each
of whom described it to me with minor symbolic variations."

"That proves only that she possesses imagination."

"It proves that she possesses stage management."

For some time they listened to the gramophone, which failed
at precisely the same note on every repetition. Eeldrop's romantic
conception of Edith was not easily surrendered, partly because it
had cost him nothing and partly because he suspected that
Appleplex's accuracy concealed a defect of appetite.

"You deny her mystery," he said at last, "because she
assists it. Yet mystery may be conscious. The priest knows
the mechanism of the sanctuary; the artist knows where the
shadow must fall."

"Then call her an artist."

"I might."

"But an artist transforms experience into form. Edith transforms it
into reputation."

"She wrote poems."

"Poetry has survived worse arguments."

"She acted."

"For six weeks."

"She travelled."

"So does luggage."

"She loved."

"Frequently, punctually, and with references."

Eeldrop smiled despite himself. "What, then, is she?"

"That is precisely the improper question. The answer becomes a
type: adventuress, muse, emancipated woman, minor decadent, social parasite, experimental
soul. Each is false; taken together they are merely crowded.
I say only that she has made herself too completely
available to interpretation."

"Too completely?"

"There is no dark residue. There are secrets, certainly, but
her secrets are brightly furnished. She tells one man that
her marriage destroyed her innocence, another that it awakened her
body, a third that marriage never touched the inviolable centre
of her being. These are not contradictions forced upon her
by life. They are costumes selected for different climates."

"Contradiction may be the form of sincerity in a divided
nature."

"Yes. But Edith is not divided. She is multiplied."

The distinction pleased Eeldrop and irritated him.

He sat down among the papers. "Let us imagine the
marriage. A girl of nineteen, intelligent beyond her circumstances, fastened
to a man whose conversation concerns rubber tubes and invoices.
She sees the years advancing: dinners, accounts, a villa named
Restharrow, children instructed to be quiet. Then thunder, rain, a
railway carriage—"

"Her husband manufactured surgical fittings," said Appleplex, "but collected Japanese
prints and read Verlaine. Restharrow belonged to his mother."

"You have poisoned the image with information."

"I have saved the man from symbolism."

This rebuke introduced a silence more serious than the previous
ones. Eeldrop looked again at the photograph. The face in
profile had been arranged to exclude appeal. Its beauty, if
beauty it possessed, seemed to demand that the observer congratulate
himself upon perceiving it.

"We have wronged the manufacturer," he admitted.

"And perhaps Edith. For to accuse her of lacking mystery
is another way of demanding that she entertain us."

"Yet there is something absent."

"Catastrophe."

"She was divorced."

"A legal event."

"She has suffered."

"Everyone has suffered. The question is whether suffering surprised her."

Eeldrop waited.

Appleplex gathered the theatre programmes into a square pile.

"Edith permits nothing unforeseen," he said. "Not because she controls
circumstances—no one does—but because she annexes each circumstance at once.
Betrayal becomes development; poverty becomes freedom; rejection becomes solitude; illness
becomes purification. Before an event can wound her without meaning,
she has named it, dressed it, and invited witnesses. She
is always one interpretation ahead of disaster."

"Then she is invulnerable."

"No. She is merely never present at the point of
impact."

Outside, two constables brought in a woman without a hat.
She was large, grey-faced, and quiet. No crowd formed. One
of her shoes was tied with string. At the station
door she bent suddenly, not in resistance but to pick
up a small white button from the pavement. The constable
waited. She placed the button in her pocket and went
inside.

Neither man spoke until the door had closed.

"There," said Eeldrop, "we know nothing."

"We know the shoe, the button, the absence of spectators."

"And if we learn everything?"

"We shall know more and understand differently."

"Not more deeply?"

"I distrust depth. It is often ignorance with a vertical
arrangement."

Eeldrop returned to Edith's photograph.

"She is not unhappy," he said.

"No."

"She is not tragic."

"No."

"She is not an artist."

"Not in the severe sense."

"And yet she is not commonplace."

"Commonplaceness is not the alternative. That is the error by
which society manufactures eccentrics. It tells a woman she must
be ordinary or exceptional, obedient or liberated, wife or enchantress.
Edith has rejected the first column and become the complete
anthology of the second."

"Then her freedom is another classification."

"An illustrated classification, revised annually."

The gramophone across the street stopped. Evening settled over the
dusty shrubs, the broken blinds, the physician's plate, and the
station entrance. For a moment the neighborhood appeared not secretive
but exhausted by secrecy.

Eeldrop put the photograph back into the packet.

"We should examine circles next," he said. "Not individuals merely,
but the manner in which five or six persons combine
to create an atmosphere none could sustain alone. The artistic
circle, the reforming circle, the wealthy circle that envies crime,
the criminal circle that imitates wealth—"

"I have material," said Appleplex. "Menus, guest lists, quarrels, dedications,
changes of address."

"Of course you have."

"We might begin Thursday."

"Why not now?"

Appleplex consulted his watch and rose.

"I am dining with Mrs. Howexden."

Eeldrop regarded him with disappointment. Mrs. Howexden maintained a Pekinese,
a political salon, and an unalterable conviction that she had
discovered Appleplex.

"You cannot go from this discussion to Mrs. Howexden."

"On the contrary," he said, placing Edith's life beneath his
arm, "that is where the discussion goes."

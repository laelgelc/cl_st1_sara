BULLIES, said the night-watchman, folding his arms on the gate
and looking over the dark river, is men wot would
be very pleasant company if everybody else was twice their
size. They don't mean no 'arm, not really. It's only
their high spirits. I've known a man with such high
spirits that it took six policemen to get 'em into
a cab.

Shipboard is where a bully gets his best chance. There
ain't no street-corner to turn, and no back-door to slip
out of, and if the mate says, "Come 'ere," you've
got to come, whether you're busy, asleep, or dead. I
knew one second mate as found fault with a hand
for dying in his watch. Said it was shirking.

But pride is a useful thing in a bully. If
it wasn't for pride, I don't know wot ordinary sailormen
would do with 'em. Pride is like a rope round
a man's neck: once you get hold of the end
you can lead 'im anywhere.

I remember a fust mate o' the barque *Mary Ann*,
trading out east, as was about the finest-built bully I
ever see. He stood six foot two in his socks,
though nobody ever see 'im in his socks except the
steward, and 'e had fists like two smoked hams. His
name was Mr. Grummett, and his idea of discipline was
to keep everybody uncomfortable. If a man was working, he
wanted to know why 'e wasn't working faster; if a
man was working fast, he wanted to know wot 'e'd
been doing before.

He took a pertickler dislike to a red-'aired able seaman
named Bill Cousins. Bill's hair was the brightest thing aboard,
and Mr. Grummett said it was a danger to navigation.
He said a careless man might mistake Bill's head for
the port light, and he ordered 'im to keep his
cap on after sunset.

Bill stood it pretty well at fust. He was a
peaceable man when asleep, and even when awake he preferred
talking about fighting to doing it. But Mr. Grummett was
always finding little jobs for him. If there was a
rusty bolt in the forepeak, Bill had to polish it.
If there was a rope wanted coiling, Bill had to
coil it. And if there wasn't nothing wanted doing, the
mate used to invent something and then find fault because
it hadn't been done before.

"Cousins," he ses one morning, looking over the rail while
Bill and Bob Pullin was holy-stoning the deck, "wot are
you grinning at?"

"I ain't grinning, sir," ses Bill.

"Don't answer me," ses the mate.

"I didn't know you didn't want an answer, sir," ses
Bill.

"Are you trying to be funny?" ses the mate.

"No, sir," ses Bill, very careful.

"Then don't look funny," ses the mate. "It's agin discipline."

Bill went on with his work, but his face got
redder than his hair. Bob Pullin, wot was working alongside
of 'im, told him afterwards that it was a mercy
there wasn't no gunpowder aboard.

"You'll get into trouble one o' these days," ses Bob.

"I know that," ses Bill. "The trouble'll be about six
foot two, with black whiskers."

"Keep your temper," ses Bob. "That's the best thing."

"I am keeping it," ses Bill. "I'm saving it up."

The mate heard the last part, and Bill spent the
afternoon scraping paint in a place where there wasn't hardly
room for a rat to turn round. When he come
out, Mr. Grummett asked 'im whether he'd enjoyed his rest.

It went on like that all the way to Calcutta.
Sometimes the mate shoved Bill, and sometimes he knocked his
cap off, and once he pulled 'im up out of
his bunk at four bells because he said Bill was
snoring disrespectful. Bill spoke to the skipper about it, but
the skipper was a mild, elderly man and advised patience.

"Mr. Grummett has a quick manner," he ses.

"So's a mad dog," ses Bill.

"There, there," ses the skipper. "Do your duty, Cousins, and
don't answer your officers."

"I never gets time," ses Bill. "He answers for me."

The skipper looked at him severe and told him to
go for'ard.

By the time we got to Calcutta the whole fo'c's'le
was taking an interest in the affair. Not that we
encouraged Bill to do anything wrong. Far from it. We
was all steady men, with a proper respect for law
and order, especially when the law was standing six foot
two and could box. But we sympathized with Bill, and
when a man's shipmates sympathize with 'im, there's no knowing
wot he may be persuaded to do.

It was Joe Smith as thought of the idea. Joe
was a little, soft-spoken man, with a way of looking
at you as though he was sorry for your future.

He was sitting on his chest one evening, mending a
sock, when the mate's voice came down the hatch ordering
Bill to go aft and wash some mud off the
jolly-boat.

"That's the third time to-day," ses Bob Pullin.

"It's clean now," ses Bill.

"Then you'd better go and dirty it first," ses Joe,
biting off a thread.

Bill said several things about the mate which showed that
he'd been brought up among people with large vocabularies.

"If you was to hit 'im aboard," ses Joe, thoughtful,
"it'd be mutiny."

"I know that," ses Bill.

"And if you hit 'im ashore," ses Joe, "you'd get
put in quod."

"I know that too," ses Bill.

Joe held the sock up and looked at it.

"Course," he ses, "if 'e was to be set upon
by a couple o' strangers, that'd be different."

Bill stared at him.

"Wot strangers?" he ses.

"Any strangers," ses Joe. "Sailormen, for choice."

"He'd know me," ses Bill. "He'd know this 'air in
a coal-cellar."

Joe shook his head.

"Not if you altered yourself," he ses.

"How?" ses Bill. "Cut my head off?"

"Black it," ses Joe.

There was a quietness in the fo'c's'le after that. Everybody
looked at Bill's face, and then they looked at Joe.
Ginger Dick began to laugh, but stopped when Bill turned
round.

"Black it?" ses Bill.

"And your 'air," ses Joe. "You and Bob could dress
up like a couple o' natives. Loose shirts, turbans, and
a bit o' blacking. Wait for the mate somewhere quiet,
give 'im a doing, and come back aboard afore he
does."

"Why me?" ses Bob Pullin, very sharp.

"You're Bill's friend," ses Joe.

"I ain't that sort of friend," ses Bob.

"We thought you was," ses old Ned Baker.

"There's friendship and friendship," ses Bob. "I'd lend 'im tobacco.
I'd even lend 'im money if I was sure he
couldn't get ashore to spend it. But dressing up and
fighting the mate is different."

Bill put his hand on Bob's shoulder.

"You've often said you'd like to see the mate get
a hiding," he ses.

"So I would," ses Bob. "From a safe place."

"You shall," ses Bill. "Close to."

Bob tried to argue, but there's no arguing agin six
men all telling you wot a fine, loyal character you've
got. Besides, Joe said there wasn't hardly no risk, and
when Joe said a thing was safe it generally meant
that he wasn't going to take part in it.

The carpenter had a pot of blacking he used for
marking timber and touching up odd jobs. It wasn't ordinary
boot-blacking; it was his own mixture, and he was very
proud of it.

"Water won't touch it," he ses. "Sun won't fade it.
Salt won't bite it. Once it's on, it stays."

"That's just wot we want," ses Joe.

The carpenter lent us the pot, though he said it
was wasting good material on bad faces. Two old cotton
shirts was bought ashore, and the steward provided a couple
o' cloths for turbans after we'd promised to pay for
'em at some future date. Bill wanted bracelets as well,
but Joe said that would be overdoing it.

We waited till the skipper had gone to dine with
an agent, and Mr. Grummett had dressed himself to go
ashore. He came for'ard before leaving, just to tell us
that if he found any man drunk when he got
back he'd cure 'im of it in a way he
wouldn't forget.

"I don't drink," ses Bill, looking down.

"No," ses the mate. "You're foolish enough sober."

When he'd gone, Bill's better feelings nearly got the upper
hand. He walked up and down saying that murder was
hanging work, and that if Bob didn't keep his temper
there might be a serious accident.

"My temper!" ses Bob. "I'm going to see fair play,
that's all."

"You're going to 'elp," ses Bill.

"I'll 'elp you home," ses Bob.

We got 'em dressed in the carpenter's shop. The blacking
went on beautiful. It covered Bill's red hair until it
looked like polished ebony, and when we'd finished with his
face his own mother wouldn't have known him without being
told. Bob looked even better, except where the blacking had
gone into one eye and made him cry.

"Stop rubbing it," ses the carpenter. "You'll spoil the surface."

"I want to spoil it," ses Bob. "It burns."

"That shows it's taking hold," ses the carpenter, pleased.

Bill looked at himself in a little cracked mirror and
turned his head from side to side.

"I don't look much like a Hindoo," he ses.

"You don't look much like Bill Cousins," ses Joe, "and
that's the chief thing."

"You look like two wax figgers after a fire," ses
Ginger Dick.

Bill picked up a marline-spike, and Ginger said at once
that, looking closer, he'd never seen a finer pair.

They went ashore by the accommodation ladder, keeping their heads
down. One of the native boatmen called something after them,
and Bob called something back. We never knew wot either
of 'em said, but Bob claimed afterwards that it helped
the disguise.

The plan was for them to wait near a narrow
lane the mate generally used on his way back. Then,
when he came along, Bill was to jostle him accidental,
Bob was to jostle him from the other side, and
between 'em they was to give 'im a lesson in
politeness.

We stayed aboard, wondering how they was getting on. Joe
said there couldn't be no danger provided they kept cool,
and the carpenter said that if either of 'em perspired
too much they was to be careful not to wipe
their faces on the shirts, because his blacking was expensive.

About two hours later we heard somebody coming along the
wharf, singing and stopping every few yards to talk to
himself. It was Mr. Grummett.

He came up the side very slow. One sleeve of
his coat was missing, his collar had broke loose, and
his necktie was hanging over his left ear like a
pennant. His right eye was closing fast, and there was
a large swelling over his jaw.

We all looked busy. Ginger Dick began coiling a rope
that was already coiled, and Joe took up his sock.

The mate stood at the top of the ladder and
looked at us.

"Any o' you men been ashore?" he ses.

"No, sir," ses Joe.

"Where's Cousins and Pullin?"

"Below, sir, I think," ses Joe.

The mate breathed hard.

"I've just had a little difficulty," he ses.

Nobody spoke. We could see that he was waiting to
be asked, but sailors has to be careful not to
pry into their officers' private affairs.

"Five of 'em," ses the mate, after a bit. "German
sailors."

"Five, sir?" ses Joe, opening his eyes.

"Five," ses the mate. "Big fellows. They thought they'd found
an easy mark."

"Did they, sir?" ses Ned Baker.

"They know better now," ses the mate. "I don't suppose
two of 'em will leave their beds for a month.
The others ran."

"German sailors is very troublesome," ses Joe.

"They chose the wrong man," ses the mate. "One of
'em hit me from behind with a bottle. That's how
I got this."

He touched his eye very gentle.

"And your sleeve, sir?" ses Ginger.

"Torn off while I was holding two of 'em," ses
the mate. "One under each arm."

"Pity there wasn't six," ses Joe, admiring. "You could 'ave
had three under each."

The mate looked at him hard, but Joe's face was
as innocent as a baby's. Then Mr. Grummett told us
to mind our own business and went below.

We waited another hour, and then two figures came crawling
along the wharf. At fust we thought they was natives
looking for work; then one of 'em swore in Bill's
voice.

They got aboard somehow, and we carried 'em into the
fo'c's'le. If the mate looked bad, Bill and Bob looked
worse. Bob had two black eyes, though it didn't show
much owing to the blacking, and Bill's nose had been
knocked a little out of its usual position. Their shirts
was in ribbons, their turbans was gone, and they had
to be put in their bunks like sacks.

"Did you do 'im?" ses Joe.

Bill opened one eye.

"Do 'im?" he ses. "There was three of us 'itting
'im together."

"Three?" ses Joe.

"Me, Bob, and the ground," ses Bill. "Every time we
got up he knocked us down agin."

"You must have hurt 'im," ses the carpenter.

"Bob tore his sleeve off," ses Bill.

"I was trying to hold on to something," ses Bob,
faintly. "He hit me for ten minutes without stopping."

"Eight minutes," ses Bill.

"It seemed longer," ses Bob.

Joe told 'em that the mate had come back and
claimed he'd beaten five German sailors. Bill sat up at
that, groaned, and lay down agin.

"German sailors?" he ses.

"Five," ses Joe. "Very large."

"The liar," ses Bill.

"You can't call 'im a liar," ses Joe. "Not without
explaining."

Bill thought for a moment and then smiled, though it
hurt him.

"Let 'im have his five Germans," he ses. "All I
want now is to wash this muck off and turn
in."

That was where the real trouble began.

The carpenter brought a bucket of water and a lump
of soap, and Bill and Bob started washing. They washed
for ten minutes, and all they did was to make
the blacking shine brighter. The carpenter watched with the air
of a man seeing a good job properly tested.

"I told you water wouldn't touch it," he ses.

"Bring hotter water," ses Bill.

We brought hotter water. Then we brought soft soap. Then
yellow soap. Then the steward produced a cake of scented
soap belonging to the skipper. Nothing happened, except that Bob's
face smelt like violets.

"Try butter," ses Ned Baker.

We tried butter. After that came linseed oil, tea leaves,
soda, lamp oil and a mixture of salt and vinegar
recommended by Ginger Dick's aunt for taking stains out of
tablecloths.

"It ain't a tablecloth," ses the carpenter.

"It ain't a face neither," ses Bill, rubbing till his
skin smarted.

At last the carpenter fetched some turpentine. That shifted a
little blacking and nearly shifted Bill out of the bunk.
He jumped up, yelling, and chased the carpenter twice round
the fo'c's'le with a razor. We stopped him from using
it because we knew how upset he'd be afterwards.

"I thought you said it would come off," ses Bill,
when we'd got the razor away.

"I never said nothing of the kind," ses the carpenter.
"I said it stayed on. That's why it's good."

"How long?" ses Bob.

"Depends," ses the carpenter.

"On wot?"

"Wear and weather," he ses. "Three weeks, p'raps. Six if
you keeps indoors."

There was a nasty silence.

The skipper was coming back aboard that night, and in
the morning all hands would be mustered. Bill and Bob
couldn't stay hidden for three weeks, nor even three hours
if the mate took it into his head to look
for 'em.

"You've ruined us," ses Bill to the carpenter.

"I've disguised you," ses the carpenter. "That was wot you
asked."

Joe began walking up and down, thinking. We all helped
by making suggestions.

"Shave their heads," ses Ginger.

"And their faces?" ses Bill.

"Tell the skipper they've got fever," ses Ned.

"Wot sort o' fever turns a man black?" ses Bob.

"A very bad one," ses Ned.

"You'd better desert," ses Joe at last. "Slip ashore now
and don't come back till it wears off."

Bill stared at him.

"And lose our pay?"

"It's only money," ses Joe.

"That's easy for you to say," ses Bill.

"Besides," ses Bob, "we ain't got nowhere to go."

Joe said that India was a large country and there
ought to be room for two more. Bill said he'd
rather give himself up and tell the truth. That alarmed
everybody.

"You can't do that," ses Ginger. "You'd be admitting to
assaulting an officer."

"I'll admit your part too," ses Bill. "The whole plan."

"It wasn't our plan," ses Joe. "We only took an
interest in it."

"You blacked us," ses Bob.

"Out of kindness," ses the carpenter.

"You lent us the shirts," ses Bill to the steward.

"Against my better judgment," ses the steward.

Bill reached for the razor agin, and we had another
little struggle. In the middle of it the mate's voice
came down the hatch.

"Cousins! Pullin! On deck!"

We all stopped.

"Tell 'im we're dead," whispers Bob.

"He'd come down to make sure," ses Joe.

The mate called agin, louder.

Bill stood up, swaying a little, and looked round at
us. His face, being black and shiny, didn't show much
expression, but his voice did.

"I'm going up," he ses, "and I'm going to tell
everything."

He took one step, and six of us caught hold
of him.

"Don't be rash," ses Joe.

"Think of your shipmates," ses Ned.

"That's just wot I am thinking of," ses Bill.

While we was arguing, the skipper's voice joined the mate's.
He ordered the two men on deck immediate. That settled
it. We couldn't hide 'em, and carrying 'em ashore through
the porthole wasn't practical.

Joe suddenly held up his hand.

"I've got it," he ses. "You was attacked."

"By who?" ses Bob.

"Foreign sailors," ses Joe.

Bill stopped struggling.

"How many?" he ses.

"Five," ses Joe.

There was a pause, and then Bill's battered face began
to brighten.

"Five Germans," he ses.

"Exactly," ses Joe. "They attacked the mate fust, and then
they attacked you. They knocked you senseless and blacked your
faces for spite."

"Why?" ses the carpenter.

"Foreigners," ses Joe, shrugging his shoulders. "You never know with
foreigners."

Bill and Bob looked at each other.

"It's a weak tale," ses Bob.

"It's the only one we've got," ses Joe.

The mate shouted for 'em a third time, and Bill
took a deep breath.

"Help us up," he ses.

We helped 'em on deck. It was a beautiful warm
night, with the lights ashore shining across the water and
the skipper standing by the cabin door. Mr. Grummett was
alongside of him, looking even larger than usual owing to
bandages.

The skipper caught sight of Bill and Bob and stepped
back.

"Who are these men?" he ses.

Bill touched his forehead.

"Cousins, sir," he ses.

"And Pullin," ses Bob.

The skipper stared. The mate stared too, but in a
different way. His mouth opened, and then shut with a
snap.

"Wot's the meaning of this?" ses the skipper.

Bill leaned against the rail.

"Attacked, sir," he ses, very feeble. "Set upon by five
foreign sailors."

The mate made a choking noise.

"Five?" ses the skipper.

"Yes, sir," ses Bob. "Germans."

Mr. Grummett took one step forward, and Bill looked at
him. For a moment neither spoke. The mate knew them
then, and they knew he knew. But he also knew
that if he said so he'd have to explain how
two mild and meek-looking natives had torn off his sleeve,
put his necktie round his ear, and damaged his features.

"Go on," ses the skipper.

"We was walking peaceable," ses Bill, "when these five Germans
sprang on us. Big men they was."

"Very big," ses Bob.

"One of 'em had a bottle," ses Bill.

The mate's bandaged eye twitched.

"They knocked us senseless," ses Bob, "and while we was
unconscious they blacked us all over."

"Outrageous!" ses the skipper.

"Yes, sir," ses Bill. "Cruel, I call it."

"Why should they black your faces?"

Bill hesitated.

"To insult the British flag," ses Joe Smith from behind
us.

The skipper nodded at once.

"That's it," he ses. "A deliberate insult. Mr. Grummett, these
must be the same ruffians who attacked you."

The mate's face went dark red.

"Seems like it," he ses between his teeth.

"Five German sailors," ses the skipper. "There cannot be two
gangs of five German sailors fighting British seamen on the
same night."

"No, sir," ses Joe. "Not likely."

"And the description agrees," ses the skipper. "Large men, one
with a bottle."

"Exactly, sir," ses Bill.

The mate looked as though he wanted to speak, but
every time he opened his mouth Bill's one visible eye
fixed itself on him. At last he said that he
supposed it might be the same men.

"Might be?" ses the skipper. "Of course it is. This
is a serious matter. Three of my men attacked in
one evening!"

"Three wictims, sir," ses Joe, sorrowful.

The skipper told the steward to fetch brandy, and he
made Bill and Bob drink some. Then he ordered hammocks
for 'em in a cool part of the deck and
said they wasn't to do no work until they'd recovered.
He even sent ashore for a doctor next morning.

The doctor looked at their faces, asked a few questions,
and said he had never seen such a case. The
carpenter said modestly that he hadn't neither.

For four days Bill and Bob lay under the awning
drinking beef tea and being treated like invalids. The skipper
visited 'em twice a day, and every time he did,
the mate had to stand by and listen to their
account of the five Germans.

Bill improved it with telling. By the third day the
Germans had clubs; by the fourth they had knives; and
Bob said one of 'em had tried to scalp him.
The skipper promised to report the matter to the authorities.

The blacking faded gradual. Bill came out patchy, with one
pink ear and a red streak showing through his hair,
while Bob peeled round the nose. The skipper said it
was a blessing their natural colour was returning, and Mr.
Grummett had to agree.

He never said a word about it. He couldn't. That
was the beauty of Joe's idea. If the mate accused
them, he accused himself; and if he told the truth,
he had to change five great Germans into two ordinary
sailors dressed in cotton shirts. Pride held 'im tighter than
irons.

But he had to ease his feelings somehow.

So for the rest of that v'y'ge he treated Bill
Cousins and Bob Pullin as though they was made of
glass, while the remainder of us did nearly double work.
He found rusty bolts we'd never seen before, ropes that
wanted coiling six times a day, and mud on the
jolly-boat when there hadn't been rain for a week.

That's the worst of helping a man out of a
difficulty. He's generally grateful to the wrong people.

Still, Bill and Bob got well, the mate kept his
reputation, and the skipper went to his grave believing that
somewhere in Calcutta there was a gang of five enormous
Germans with a bottle and a pot of blacking.

And, as I said afore, pride is a useful thing
in a bully—provided you don't mind doing his share of
the work.

## The Charm of Miss Armitage

When the war with Spain was announced, Mr. Edward Chesterton
was taking tea with Miss Armitage beneath a striped awning
on her aunt's lawn.

This was unfortunate. Had he received the news in a
newspaper office, a railway carriage, a hotel smoking-room, or any
other place where men may arrange the destinies of nations
without interruption from the women who love them, he would
have declared at once that he must go to the
front. As it was, he looked across a small silver
table and saw Miss Armitage's hand suddenly close over a
spoon.

"No," she said.

Chesterton had not yet spoken.

The spoon trembled in the china cup, making a faint,
unreasonable sound.

"My dear Isabel—"

"No, Edward."

"You cannot know what I was going to say."

"You are going."

"To Spain?"

"To the war."

He leaned back and smiled with the delighted admiration of
a man whose character has been accurately read by the
only person whose reading matters to him.

"My gracious!" cried Miss Armitage. "Do not look pleased. It
is detestable of you."

"I am not pleased that you are unhappy."

"You are pleased that I understand you."

"Well," he admitted, "perhaps a little."

Aunt Lydia, who was sleeping behind a copy of the
*Morning Post*, gave no sign of having heard either the
war or its consequences.

Chesterton was twenty-nine, strong, quick, adventurous, and possessed of that
irrepressible confidence which in ordinary life makes a man charming
and in war makes him either useful or dead. He
had reported strikes, royal tours, floods, elections, and one revolution
which had been postponed without notice. His paper now required
a correspondent with the expeditionary force, and he had already
determined that no other man should obtain the honour.

Miss Armitage knew all this before he explained it. She
listened, nevertheless, because lovers require from one another not merely
truth but its full ceremonial repetition.

"It is my profession," he said. "I should be ashamed
to remain in London writing leaders about courage while other
men are practising it."

"Then write nothing about courage."

"My editor would object."

"Let him go."

"He has rheumatism."

"Then let him send somebody who has no one—no one
at all—to care whether he returns."

"That would be an extremely melancholy correspondent."

"It would be an extremely suitable one."

Chesterton reached across the table. Her hands escaped him like
two startled swallows, then returned of their own accord and
were captured.

"Isabel, listen to me. I must go."

"I know."

"And I shall come back."

"You cannot know."

"I do know."

"How?"

"Because you will be waiting."

"That will not stop a bullet."

"It will stop every bullet intended for me."

She looked at him with reproachful eyes, but the reproach
was weakened by hope. He saw the weakness and advanced
upon it without mercy.

"You love me," he continued, "and therefore I am immortal.
Immune! Shot-proof! Fever-proof! I shall walk through ambuscades like a
man through rain, shake the drops from my coat, and
come home complaining of the weather."

"You are laughing."

"Only because I am so happy."

"I am not happy."

"You will be when I return."

"I want no splendid articles," she said. "I want no
medals, no praise, no stories of generals congratulating you. I
want YOU, Edward. You, you, you! Is that so small
a thing that you can risk it for telegrams?"

He ceased smiling.

"No," he said quietly. "It is the greatest thing in
the world."

For a moment the awning flapped above them, the tea
cooled, the roses attended to their own concerns, and Aunt
Lydia slept through history.

Then Chesterton bent and touched his lips to Miss Armitage's
fingers.

"Your love shall be my charm," he said. "I shall
carry it with me everywhere."

"Love is not a charm."

"Yours is."

"Edward—"

"It is settled. I go because I must. I return
because you command it."

Miss Armitage's philosophy had never included charms. She had been
brought up upon sound doctrine, good manners, punctual meals, and
a sensible suspicion of anything not endorsed by her aunt.
Yet, being in love, she was driven into regions where
philosophy found the roads poor and doctrine had neglected to
erect signposts.

"If I believed it," she whispered, "perhaps I could bear
it."

"Then believe."

She tried. For the next hour she tried with such
determination that by the time Chesterton left, wearing her little
silver matchbox in the inner pocket of his coat and
carrying a medicine case she had insisted upon packing, the
charm was almost an article of faith.

The matchbox bore his initials on one side and hers
on the other. The medicine case contained quinine, bandages, antiseptic,
a thermometer, pills of several colours, and written instructions which
assumed that, while exposed to hostile artillery, Chesterton would remember
to take three drops in water after meals.

"You will carry it always," she ordered.

"It weighs as much as a field gun."

"Then you will hire a mule."

"And the matchbox?"

"Next to your heart."

"Where else?"

"And you will write."

"Every day."

"Every day, Edward."

"Twice on Sundays."

At the gate he turned, waving his hat with deplorable
cheerfulness.

Miss Armitage waved until he vanished. Then she went indoors,
knelt beside a chair, and prayed that she had not
lied when she told him she believed.

War thereafter ran its happy-go-lucky course.

Ships transported troops, troops transported accoutrements, officers transported anxieties, and
correspondents transported rumours. The armies marched into districts which the
maps declared to be roads and the inhabitants knew to
be swamps. Staffs issued orders; detachments misunderstood them; newspapers corrected
everybody. Men performed acts of heroism because there was no
time to consider alternatives, and acts of folly because there
was too much time. The Spanish fired from hills and
vanished. The expeditionary force occupied villages, drank doubtful water, and
imbibed fever according to rank and constitution.

Chesterton enjoyed himself enormously.

He was under fire within forty-eight hours of landing and
wrote five thousand words about it by candlelight, omitting only
the fact that he had been frightened. This omission was
not dishonest. By the time he wrote, the fear had
passed, and Chesterton never believed in the permanence of any
emotion except love.

A shell burst near him on the third day. A
fragment tore through his saddle-bag, demolished a hairbrush, and lodged
in a packet of Miss Armitage's letters. Chesterton retrieved the
packet, kissed the torn ribbon, and announced that the enemy
had been repulsed by superior literature.

During the following week, the man who slept on his
right died of yellow fever, the man who slept on
his left was invalided with dysentery, and Chesterton, between them,
maintained an appetite that became a subject of unfriendly military
inquiry.

"You ought to be ill," said a surgeon.

"I am protected."

"By what?"

"A young lady in Surrey."

"Is she vaccinated?"

"Against every known misfortune."

The surgeon, who had been awake for thirty-six hours, laughed
according to regulations and sent him away.

Chesterton rode with a cavalry patrol whose rear guard was
cut off; he returned with the advance guard and did
not learn of the matter until dinner. He crossed a
courtyard two minutes before a wall collapsed into it. A
sniper fired at the glint of his camera lens and
struck a brass button, which Chesterton thereafter wore on his
watch-chain as proof that Providence favoured good tailoring. Once, when
he was due to accompany a reconnaissance, he remained behind
to finish a letter to Miss Armitage. The reconnaissance entered
a ravine and came back by a route so circuitous
that several of its members believed they had visited another
province.

The letters themselves were prodigious.

They came to Miss Armitage stained by rain, smoke, dust,
coffee, and occasionally substances upon which she declined to speculate.
They were written in abandoned convents, crowded transports, ruined farmhouses,
beneath wagons, upon ammunition boxes, and once on the back
of an order directing all civilians to remove themselves from
the vicinity. They told her that the sunsets were magnificent,
the generals entertaining, the food criminal, the danger negligible, and
her absence unbearable.

"You are here," one letter declared, "in the inner pocket
where the silver box presses against me. You are in
the saddle when I ride, in the tent when I
sleep, in the absurd coffee when I drink it. Yesterday,
under a fire which some timid persons considered heavy, I
saw a flower the precise violet of the gown you
wore at Richmond, and the whole battery disappeared. I mean
spiritually, my dear, though one or two of the guns
also disappeared physically. You have made me invulnerable."

Miss Armitage read such passages first with terror, then indignation,
then tenderness, and finally with a curious strengthening conviction. He
had escaped. Again and again he had escaped. Perhaps love,
which made people unreasonable in drawing-rooms, was permitted to be
reasonable before eternity.

She wrote in return: "Do not rely upon being invulnerable.
Take your quinine. I enclose more tablets. Aunt Lydia says
the climate is quite fatal, though she has never been
there. Do not stand where officers tell you not to
stand. Do not drink water unless it has been boiled.
Do not lose the silver box. Oh, Edward, come back
soon."

He took the quinine when convenient, boiled water when compelled,
stood in forbidden places whenever the view was superior, and
never willingly lost the silver box.

At last, after months of smoke, mud, dispatches, marches, rumours,
charges, fevers, and official assurances that everything had proceeded exactly
as intended, peace was declared.

The news reached headquarters late in the afternoon. Chesterton heard
it while arguing with a transport officer over the possession
of a pony. Within ten minutes he had obtained the
animal, packed his camera, notebooks, letters, and medicine case, and
discovered that a steamer would sail from San Aurelio at
noon the next day.

The port lay more than fifty miles away.

"You cannot make it," said Major Belden.

"I can."

"The road is bad."

"The pony is worse. The two should understand each other."

"There are Spanish detachments in the hills."

"There were Spanish detachments in the hills. This is peace."

"Have the detachments heard of it?"

Chesterton tightened a girth.

"My dear Major, peace is not a private communication."

"In this country," said Belden, "everything is a private communication,
usually entrusted to a man with a lame mule."

"I sail at noon."

"You will break your neck."

"Miss Armitage would object."

"She is evidently a lady of judgment."

"And I have promised to return."

Belden looked at him, saw the bright, absurd certainty in
his face, and gave up.

"Take the southern road. Avoid Las Piedras. There was a
hostile outpost there yesterday."

"Yesterday is dead."

"So are many men who believed in tomorrow. Take an
escort."

"No horses."

"Wait until morning."

"No steamer."

"Then at least carry a revolver."

"I have one somewhere."

"Keep it somewhere accessible."

Chesterton shook his hand, mounted, and rode out of camp
as dusk gathered among the hills.

Night rose rapidly from the valleys. The road climbed through
plantations and dropped into jungled hollows where great leaves brushed
his shoulders and vines hung like black cordage. Then it
emerged upon bare ridges flooded with moonlight. Palms stood in
glittering silver; distant adobe houses shone like whitewashed sepulchres; the
mountains floated, pale and enormous, against a sky crowded with
stars.

Chesterton rode with the buoyant impatience of a man whose
whole life waits at the end of a road. He
was tired, hungry, and stiff in every joint. His pony
had a hard mouth, a suspicious disposition, and a gait
suggestive of furniture being dragged downstairs. None of this mattered.
The steamer would sail. The steamer would cross the Atlantic.
A train would carry him to London. A carriage would
carry him to Surrey. Then Miss Armitage would stand beneath
the striped awning—or at the gate—or anywhere in the created
world—and he would be home.

He spoke to her aloud.

"Five days, Isabel. Six if the Atlantic behaves disgracefully. You
will pretend not to cry, and Aunt Lydia will pretend
not to see. I shall say I told you so.
You will say I am intolerable. Then—"

The pony stumbled.

"Then I shall forgive you," he concluded.

At that hour, two miles farther along the road, twelve
Spanish soldiers crouched behind a low bank overgrown with cactus.

Their lieutenant, a narrow-faced young man with a torn sleeve,
had received no news of peace. He had received almost
no news of anything for four days, except that his
rations were exhausted and one of his men had stolen
a chicken. Reports had reached him that an enemy column
might attempt to pass during the night. He therefore regarded
the moonlit road with professional hostility.

A sentry slid down the slope.

"A horseman, Lieutenant."

"One?"

"One, señor. From the enemy's direction."

"A scout," said the lieutenant.

The men settled their rifles across the bank.

Chesterton rode toward them, exposed by the moon as completely
as though a searchlight had followed him. His loose coat,
field-glasses, camera case, and broad hat provided a silhouette of
unusual clarity.

"Wait until he reaches the white stone," whispered the lieutenant.
"Then fire."

The pony clattered on.

Chesterton, meanwhile, had decided to smoke. He felt in his
outer pockets and found a pencil, two cartridges, a notebook,
a biscuit of mineral hardness, and a telegram concerning peace.
The matches, of course, were in the silver box next
to his heart.

He drew it out. Moonlight glimmered on Isabel's initials.

"My charm," he said, and flicked it open.

At that instant the pony swerved from a pale snake
crossing the road. Chesterton snatched at the reins. The matchbox
flew from his fingers, flashed once, and vanished into the
weeds.

He reined in with a cry of genuine anguish.

Behind the bank, twelve rifles rose.

"Not yet," breathed the lieutenant. "The white stone."

Chesterton dismounted and began searching the roadside.

The lieutenant frowned. A scout, having detected an ambush, might
pretend to lose something and thus warn the column behind
him. Or he might be marking the road. Or he
might be attempting to draw their fire. War provides simple
men with complicated suspicions.

"Hold," he whispered.

Chesterton went to his knees. He brushed aside coarse grass,
peered under leaves, and struck an ordinary match from a
paper packet which, in his agitation, he had failed to
discover in his waistcoat pocket.

The tiny flame illuminated his face.

"He signals," said a corporal.

"Perhaps."

"Shall we fire?"

"No. If there is a column, we reveal ourselves."

Chesterton's match burned his fingers and went out. He lit
another.

"Two signals," murmured the corporal.

The soldiers waited, each man seeing, in the movement of
one romantic Englishman searching for a trinket, the advance of
battalions.

At last Chesterton found the box half buried in soft
dust. He polished it tenderly on his sleeve, kissed it
with an extravagance no Spanish military manual had anticipated, remounted,
and turned aside from the road.

The lieutenant's finger tightened.

But Chesterton had noticed a narrow trail descending toward the
valley. His map showed that it rejoined the main road
beyond a bend and shortened the journey by perhaps half
a mile. He took it at once.

The soldiers heard hoofbeats diminish among the trees.

"A decoy," said the lieutenant.

"Undoubtedly," said the corporal.

They remained in position for three hours awaiting the column
which Chesterton, by failing to exist in sufficient numbers, had
neglected to provide.

Chesterton himself congratulated the pony on finding a shortcut.

"You see," he said, "her gift saves time as well
as tobacco."

The trail returned him to the main road below the
ambush. From there the country descended through a ravine, at
the bottom of which a river moved blackly between walls
of cane. A timber bridge crossed it—a temporary military structure
thrown together during the campaign and weakened by flood, transport
wagons, tropical rot, and the administrative optimism which had certified
it as sound.

At its entrance hung a board.

Chesterton leaned from the saddle to read it. The writing
had been washed pale, but the word DANGER remained visible.

"That," he told the pony, "is probably Spanish for bridge."

The pony disagreed. It planted all four feet, lifted its
head, and uttered a short, bitter sound.

"Forward."

The pony backed.

"Peace has been declared. You cannot object on military grounds."

The animal's ears flattened.

Chesterton dismounted and pulled the bridle. The pony braced itself
with the moral strength of a martyr and the physical
strength of a small locomotive.

The bridge creaked in the current. Several lashings had parted.
One central support leaned downstream, and the whole structure swayed
delicately, like a house of cards contemplating a sneeze.

Chesterton saw none of this. He was occupied with the
pony's character.

"I have known braver footstools," he said.

He tried coaxing, insult, pressure, and a lump of sugar
discovered in his saddle-bag. The pony accepted the sugar and
declined the bridge.

Then, down the slope to his left, where moonlight reached
a quiet pool beside the ford, Chesterton saw an orchid.

It grew from the mossy branch of a fallen tree,
a cluster of pale blossoms washed with violet at their
throats. Months before, Miss Armitage had once paused before a
conservatory window and declared that this particular orchid was her
favourite flower. Chesterton had remembered the remark because love is
an excellent index to otherwise useless information.

He abandoned the bridge immediately.

"For Isabel," he said.

Leading the pony down a steep path, he reached the
riverbank. The ford lay a hundred yards below the bridge,
broad and shallow, though rough with stones. Chesterton tethered the
animal, climbed onto the fallen tree, and stretched for the
blossom.

At the centre of the bridge, a wet rope snapped.

Chesterton, balanced upon one boot, heard the crack and looked
up.

Another lashing parted. The central support shifted. For one extraordinary
moment the bridge retained its shape while ceasing to possess
any reason for doing so. Then it folded, swayed, and
dropped into the river. Timbers struck the current with a
thunderous succession of blows. Spray climbed silver in the moonlight.

The pony jerked at its reins.

Chesterton stared.

"Abominable workmanship," he said.

He plucked the orchid, wrapped its stem in damp cloth,
and secured it inside his camera case. He then led
the pony across the ford, getting wet to the knees
and once nearly losing a boot.

On the opposite bank he looked back at the ruins
sweeping downstream.

"Your judgment," he told the pony, "is superior to your
manners."

The animal sneezed.

"Still," Chesterton added as he checked the orchid, "if Isabel
had preferred roses, we should be there all night."

He rode on, vaguely conscious that fortune had favoured him,
but attributing the entire matter to the charm in a
general and poetical manner. He did not attempt to distinguish
between a pony's terror, an orchid's colour, a woman's remembered
preference, and Providence. This was just as well. Men have
constructed less satisfactory religions out of more elaborate materials.

Toward three in the morning, clouds covered the moon. The
road entered a district of small farms and abandoned cane
fields. Chesterton's weariness settled over him like a heavy coat.
His head nodded. Once he woke to find the pony
standing motionless while he continued, in a dream, to ride
magnificently toward Surrey.

Then the animal stumbled and came up limping.

Chesterton dismounted. A foreleg was tender, the shoe loose.

"Now," he said, "you become difficult."

The pony regarded him without apology.

A village lay ahead, no more than a cluster of
adobe houses around a church. One lamp burned above an
inn door. The sign, creaking in the damp wind, displayed
a faded red lion which had never resembled any animal
native to Spain or elsewhere.

Chesterton knocked.

After some delay, the door opened upon a broad man
in a white shirt, holding a candle in one hand
and a knife in the other.

"I require a horse," Chesterton said in imperfect Spanish.

The innkeeper looked past him at the lame pony, then
at his dusty field clothes, camera case, saddle-bags, and revolver
holster.

"A horse?" he repeated.

"A sound horse. I must reach San Aurelio before noon."

The innkeeper's eyes rested on the saddle-bags.

"I have a horse."

"Excellent."

"It is expensive."

"Everything necessary is expensive."

The innkeeper stepped aside.

The common room was low-ceilinged and smoky, though no fire
burned. Three men sat at a table near the rear
wall. Their coats concealed military tunics badly. One had a
bandaged head; another wore cavalry boots. They stopped speaking when
Chesterton entered.

Chesterton nodded pleasantly.

"Good evening, gentlemen. Or good morning. Peace makes time unimportant."

They stared.

The innkeeper said, "Peace?"

"Declared yesterday."

No expression crossed his face, but one of the men
at the table glanced quickly at another.

Chesterton put down his bags.

"Food first, if you please. Then the horse. I am
prepared to pay an unreasonable sum for both."

The innkeeper brought bread, cold meat, and wine. While Chesterton
ate, he withdrew to the rear of the room and
spoke in a low voice with the three men.

They were remnants of a local irregular company—angry, unpaid, and
unaware until that moment that the war had ended without
consulting them. The innkeeper's brother had been killed during a
bombardment; his stable had been requisitioned twice, once by each
army, with equally formal promises of payment. He possessed a
horse, and he intended to sell it to the Englishman.
He also intended, after receiving the money, to send two
men with Chesterton beyond the village, where the road crossed
a deserted quarry. There they would kill him and bring
the horse back.

It was not a complicated plan. Chesterton assisted it by
displaying a purse, mentioning his haste, and asking no questions.

"He carries a pistol," whispered the bandaged man.

"He is exhausted," said the innkeeper.

"He may have companions."

"He arrived alone."

"And peace?"

The innkeeper's mouth hardened.

"Peace will still exist after he is dead."

Chesterton, having eaten enough for two innocent men or one
guilty one, called across the room, "How soon can the
horse be saddled?"

"Half an hour, señor."

"Too long."

"Twenty minutes."

"Fifteen, and I shall praise your establishment in the London
newspapers."

The innkeeper showed his teeth. It was technically a smile.

Before he could answer, a cry sounded from above.

It was a woman's cry, brief and broken. The innkeeper
turned white. A second cry followed, then rapid feet on
the stairs. A young woman appeared, clutching the railing.

"Mateo!" she gasped. "The child—he does not breathe properly. Come!"

The innkeeper dropped the candle. One of the men caught
it before it went out.

All four hurried upstairs.

Chesterton remained at the table for perhaps three seconds. Then
he rose and followed.

In a small room above the kitchen, a boy of
six lay upon a narrow bed. His face was flushed
darkly, his breath coming in harsh, shallow efforts. Beside him
knelt an old priest, murmuring prayers. The mother wrung her
hands; the innkeeper stood as helpless as a wall.

Chesterton pushed through the doorway.

"What is it?"

The innkeeper rounded on him.

"Go!"

"Has he fever?"

"The doctor is two leagues away," said the priest. "The
boy has worsened suddenly."

Chesterton touched the child's forehead. It was burning. He had
seen enough fever in camp to recognise its authority, though
not enough medicine to challenge it confidently.

Then he remembered Miss Armitage's case.

He ran downstairs, opened his saddle-bag, and returned with the
square leather box. Its contents, packed in Surrey among roses
and reasonable furniture, looked astonishingly precise in that poor room:
bottles in loops, folded bandages, a thermometer, powders labelled in
Miss Armitage's neat hand, and a small printed booklet of
emergency directions.

"What is that?" demanded the mother.

"My orders," said Chesterton.

He placed the thermometer, counted the child's pulse, listened to
his chest, and rapidly lost the buoyant expression with which
he approached most difficulties. The breathing was obstructed; the throat
badly swollen. Among the remedies was an emetic, included because
Miss Armitage's family physician believed every traveller would sooner or
later eat something disastrous. There were also antiseptic tablets and
an instrument for clearing the mouth and throat.

Chesterton read the instructions twice.

The priest watched him. "You are a physician?"

"No."

The mother gave a moan.

"But I have buried several," Chesterton added. "That is, I
have travelled with army surgeons. Boil water. Quickly."

This was not reassuring, but it was decisive. The household
obeyed.

For twenty minutes the room became a campaign. Chesterton administered
the remedy, cleared the choking child's throat, ordered cloths cooled
and replaced, measured drops with trembling care, and spoke to
the boy in English because tenderness requires no translation. The
child struggled, coughed, and for a dreadful instant lay still.

The mother cried out.

Then the boy drew a long, tearing breath.

Another followed.

He coughed again, violently, and the obstruction came free. Air
entered his lungs. The dark flush began slowly to lessen.

The old priest crossed himself.

Chesterton sat back on his heels, as weak as though
he had been shot.

"More cool cloths," he said. "A little water. Not much.
The fever remains."

The innkeeper had not moved. His face, so hard below,
had broken entirely. He knelt beside the bed and took
his son's hand in both of his.

"Mateo," murmured the boy.

"I am here."

Chesterton stayed until the breathing steadied. He left powders with
the priest, explained the printed instructions as best he could,
and made the man repeat them. Then he returned downstairs.

The three irregulars followed.

No one spoke for a moment.

Chesterton repacked the medicine case. One bottle was empty, two
bandages used, the thermometer miraculously unbroken. He smiled at Miss
Armitage's handwriting on a label.

"She insisted," he said. "I told her it was nonsense
to carry half a hospital. She is always right when
it is inconvenient."

The bandaged man looked toward the ceiling.

The innkeeper entered. He came directly to Chesterton and placed
both hands upon his shoulders.

"The boy sleeps."

"Good."

"You saved him."

"The case saved him. A lady in England packed it."

"Then God bless the lady in England."

"He has undertaken that duty already."

The innkeeper lowered his head. When he looked up, there
were tears in his eyes and something like shame beneath
them.

"You need a horse."

"Desperately."

"My best mare is in the stable."

"What price?"

"No price."

"I cannot take her."

"You will take her."

"I insist on paying."

"And I insist you do not."

Chesterton regarded him.

"My friend, international peace may collapse under this dispute."

The priest, descending the stairs, said gently, "Accept the horse,
señor. A father must be allowed gratitude."

So Chesterton accepted.

The innkeeper himself saddled the mare. He filled Chesterton's food
pouch, replaced his wine flask, and sent the bandaged man
to guide him by a road avoiding both the quarry
and a flooded stretch near the coast. Chesterton interpreted these
arrangements as examples of Spanish hospitality.

At the edge of the village, the guide reined in.

"From here, ride east until you see the sea."

"Thank you."

The man looked at Chesterton strangely.

"You should not have come alone."

"Nonsense. The war is over."

"Yes," said the man after a pause. "For you, perhaps
it is."

Chesterton failed to understand this remark, attributed it to national
melancholy, and rode on.

Dawn found him on the coastal plain. The clouds split
in the east, releasing long bars of gold. Wet fields
shone; birds burst from the cane; the sea appeared beyond
low dunes, bright as beaten metal. The mare was strong
and eager. San Aurelio's church towers rose in the distance.

At eleven twenty, Chesterton reached the harbour.

At eleven thirty, he bullied a customs official.

At eleven forty, he bribed a boatman.

At eleven fifty-five, he climbed the gangway as the steamer
prepared to cast off.

At noon, standing at the rail with the orchid safely
wrapped, the silver matchbox against his heart, and the medicine
case diminished but intact, he watched the coast withdraw.

"Immortal," he told the sea.

The sea, which had drowned many confident men, made no
reply.

Six days later, Miss Armitage was waiting at the gate.

She had intended to receive him in the drawing room.
Aunt Lydia considered gates emotional and draughty. But when the
carriage appeared between the hedges, Miss Armitage abandoned the drawing
room, Aunt Lydia, composure, and several other obligations of civilised
life.

Chesterton leaped down before the carriage stopped.

For one second they stood apart, looking at each other
with the startled gravity of people who have imagined a
moment so often that reality seems an interruption.

Then Miss Armitage ran to him.

Her hands caught his coat with the desperate grip of
a drowning child. He held her, laughing and unable to
speak. She said his name once, then again, then many
times, as if each repetition recovered some separate part of
him from war.

Aunt Lydia watched from an upper window and withdrew with
dignity before anyone could discover that she was crying.

At last Miss Armitage pushed Chesterton away sufficiently to inspect
him.

"You are thin."

"I am heroic."

"You are brown."

"Heroically brown."

"There is a scar."

"A scratch."

"You said there were no scratches."

"I said there were no important scratches."

"My gracious, Edward, you are exactly the same."

"Did you expect Spain to improve me?"

She laughed, and the laugh broke into tears. He kissed
them away with no regard for the gate, the servants,
the carriage driver, Aunt Lydia's curtains, or the general state
of English manners.

Later, beneath the striped awning, he presented the orchid.

It had survived the journey imperfectly. The petals were bruised,
the stem bent, and its violet throat had faded. Miss
Armitage received it as if it were the crown jewel
of a conquered empire.

"You remembered."

"I remember everything you say."

"That is plainly untrue."

"Everything concerning flowers, kisses, and my personal safety."

He showed her the matchbox, scratched but bright. He displayed
the medicine case and told her about the child. He
recounted the campaign in splendid fragments: shells, fever, cavalry, generals,
ruined bridges, bad coffee, moonlit roads, the obstinate pony, the
generous innkeeper, and the steamer caught by five minutes.

"You see?" he concluded. "The charm worked."

Miss Armitage's smile faded.

"Yes," she said. "It did."

There was something in her voice that made him set
down his cup.

"What is it?"

"One night," she said slowly, "I knew you were in
danger."

"My dear Isabel, according to the newspapers I was in
danger for months."

"No. This was different."

"When?"

"The night after peace was declared. I woke suddenly. It
was after four here, I think—perhaps near midnight where you
were. I had been asleep, and then I was awake,
quite awake, with such terror in me that I could
scarcely breathe."

Chesterton reached for her hand.

She continued: "I felt that you were alone. I could
see nothing clearly—only darkness, a road, and something waiting. I
thought, 'He will die if I do not pray.' It
was not reasonable. I knew peace had been declared. Aunt
Lydia had shown me the evening paper. But I knelt,
and I prayed—oh, Edward, I prayed as though I could
hold you in the world by saying your name."

Her fingers tightened around his.

"For hours I could not stop. Once the fear lessened,
then it returned worse than before. Then I thought of
water. Then of a child crying. It sounds absurd now."

Chesterton's face softened into affectionate amusement.

"My darling girl."

"Do not laugh."

"I am not laughing at you."

"You are."

"Only a little, because it proves how needless our fears
are. That night was the safest of the whole campaign.
Peace had been declared. I was riding to the coast
through a country full of future friends."

Miss Armitage looked at the scratched silver matchbox on the
table.

"You met no danger?"

"None worth the name."

"The bridge?"

"I did not cross it."

"Why not?"

"The pony objected—and I went down to gather your orchid."

"And the village?"

"A charming place. They gave me a horse."

"Because you helped the child."

"With your medicine case."

She was silent.

Chesterton lifted the silver box.

"And this delayed me perhaps five minutes on the road.
I dropped it and behaved like a lunatic in the
weeds. But danger? Never. I was never for one moment
in danger."

Far away, in a hot country now officially at peace,
a young Spanish lieutenant was perhaps still wondering what signal
had summoned the invisible column. A ruined bridge travelled piece
by piece toward the sea. In a village above a
red-lion inn, a child slept with steady breath, while his
father kept a useless pistol locked in a drawer and
remembered the Englishman he had meant to murder.

Miss Armitage knew none of these things.

Chesterton knew none of them.

She looked at the orchid, the medicine case, the matchbox,
and the man before her. Then she smiled—that tremulous, radiant
smile by which women sometimes accept a mystery without surrendering
their understanding.

"Yes," she said. "You were safe."

"Entirely."

"Because the war was over?"

"Of course."

She leaned toward him.

"And because you carried the charm."

Chesterton kissed her hand with triumphant solemnity.

"My dear Isabel," he said, "that is what I have
been telling you from the beginning."

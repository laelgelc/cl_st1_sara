### Mary's Ark

If you have never seen Dedlow Marsh under a March
sky, when the tide is out, the wind southeast, and
the last rain lying in every depression like tarnished silver
in the mould of an old coffin, you may reasonably
doubt the particulars of this story. Indeed, Dedlow itself seems
then to discourage belief in anything wholesome, courageous, or providential.
The sloughs go twisting through it with the black, furtive
sinuosity of eels; the reeds lean together as if exchanging
scandal; the coarse grass, submerged at the roots and bleached
at the tips, trails upon the pools like the floating
hair of a drowned woman. Even the birds appear to
have suffered a moral deterioration from long residence there. The
curlews complain without offering a remedy; the heron, who stands
ankle-deep in water with the stoicism of one who has
abandoned all hope of dry feet, preserves a sepulchral silence;
and the black marsh ravens hold noisy consultations upon the
driftwood, but never, so far as I have learned, arrive
at a definite report.

At high tide Dedlow undergoes a transformation, although not, I
think, an improvement. The channels disappear, the tussocks become islands,
and the whole plain is converted into a smooth, treacherous
mirror, in which the clouds contemplate their own deformity. A
stranger might call it beautiful. The marsh, conscious of this
temporary compliment, usually responds by sending up a fog from
the sea, shutting out the dunes, the hills, the lighthouse,
and every other object by which a prudent man could
steer. It then lies quiet, expansive, innocent, and false.

Beyond it, where the river broadens into the bay, there
stood, in the spring of '58, a logger's cabin. It
was built on low ground at the confluence of a
creek and a slough, though the word "built," as applied
to that habitation, may imply more architectural deliberation than had
actually been exercised. Four walls, a roof, two rooms, a
chimney, and a narrow porch composed it. Yet it was
strongly fastened, being the work of Tom Reeve, who had
more faith in spikes than in ornament, and who drove
a nail with the stern finality of a magistrate pronouncing
sentence.

Tom lived there with his wife Mary, their infant son,
and a yellow dog of mixed ancestry and unblemished devotion,
called Sailor. The child had been sickly from birth, and
on the evening of the great flood lay fretful and
feverish in a wooden cradle near the stove.

I had the account from Mary herself, many years afterward,
in the house that succeeded the cabin; and if, in
arranging her recollections, I have introduced an order which terror
may not always observe, I have altered none of the
essential circumstances. Her own narrative was simpler. "I sat and
sat," she would say, "and then the house acted silly;
and after that, you see, there wasn't much time to
think."

On that afternoon Tom had gone with three men to
carry a raft of squared timber across the upper reach
of the bay. The weather was already bad, but not
extraordinarily so. Rain had fallen for five days upon the
mountains; the river was swollen, the roads were impassable, and
the roof leaked into a basin by the bed. Such
domestic inundations had occurred before and were received, like poverty
and rheumatism, as inconveniences not wholly unexpected in the country.

"You'll be back before dark?" Mary asked.

"Before midnight, anyway," Tom answered.

He kissed her, touched the child's cheek with one enormous
finger, whistled to Sailor—who declined to accompany him—and went out
into the rain.

Toward dusk the wind increased. It descended from the timbered
hills in long, sonorous charges, bent the alder thickets, hurled
the rain against the cabin, paused to recover breath, and
charged again. The chimney moaned; the stovepipe clicked; drops forced
themselves beneath the door and crept across the floor in
narrow, shining files. Sailor lay with his nose on his
paws, but whenever the house shook he lifted his head
and looked at Mary with that grave canine inquiry which
seems to ask for an explanation of human arrangements.

Mary fed the child, wrapped him in a quilt, and
sat beside the stove. She afterward remembered that she was
wearing an old brown dress patched at both elbows, and
that the baby had on a flannel gown faded nearly
white. This circumstance troubled her greatly during the night. It
is one of the peculiarities of danger that, having expelled
the larger vanities, it sometimes leaves a small one behind,
as a flood leaves a teacup on a fence post.
She had a blue dress in the chest, and the
child possessed a little embroidered cap given by his grandmother.
"If I'd known we were going visiting," she said, "I'd
have fixed us decent."

At eight o'clock she heard a sound beneath the floor.

It was not the steady whisper of rain, nor the
tapping of the branch against the roof, nor the familiar
gurgle of the creek. It was a heavy, muffled concussion,
followed by a long scraping, as though some great animal
had turned itself over under the house.

Sailor sprang up and barked.

Mary took the lamp and opened the back door. The
wind instantly extinguished the flame. She could see nothing but
darkness and feel nothing but rain. Yet from below came
a sucking and bubbling, and against the step something struck
once, twice, and drifted away.

She shut the door, relit the lamp, and went to
the front window.

Nothing but water.

She opened the bedroom door.

Nothing but water upon the floor, crawling inward.

She ran to the kitchen.

Water was coming through the cracks between the boards.

She lifted the lamp. The yellow light trembled over the
chairs, the stove, the cradle, and the black surface spreading
under them. For perhaps half a minute she could not
comprehend it. The tide did not rise so high; Tom
had said it never could; the cabin stood above the
old mark on the alder. Then she remembered the rain
in the mountains, the brown river at noon, the logs
that had gone down the creek.

It was the river, and not the tide!

The recognition seemed to strike strength into her. She set
the lamp on a shelf, seized the cradle, and dragged
it toward the table. The water covered her shoes. Sailor
splashed beside her, barking furiously at each floating stick that
entered under the door. She placed the baby on the
table, then climbed upon a chair and lifted him to
a beam from which Tom had hung a broad storage
shelf. Blankets, flour, matches, and a tin cup followed. She
worked without crying, although she prayed continually—not in ordered sentences,
but in brief appeals fitted between exertions.

"Lord, hold the house! Lord, keep him warm! Lord, bring
Tom!"

The cabin lurched.

The stove shifted six inches, tearing the pipe loose from
the chimney. Smoke poured into the room; water hissed upon
the coals; a cloud of steam rose, bitter and suffocating.
Mary threw open the door, and through it entered a
muddy wave bearing chips, leaves, a drowned hen from some
upstream ranch, and one of Tom's boots, which had been
standing upon the porch. The table turned slowly, as if
considering departure, struck the wall, and became wedged.

The child began to cry. That weak, familiar complaint, scarcely
louder than the creaking rafters, steadied her more than any
trumpet. She climbed to the shelf, took him against her
breast, and wrapped the quilt around them both.

Then followed a period which she could never measure. She
"sat and sat." The water mounted to the chair seats,
the table, the stove top. Household objects, liberated from their
customary obligations, drifted solemnly through the room. A saucepan sailed
into the bedroom; the family Bible floated open against the
wall; a red wool stocking circled the table three times
and vanished. Sailor had climbed upon the wood box and
stood there trembling, his claws spread, his eyes fixed upon
Mary.

The house began to move.

At first it was only a shudder; then a lifting
of one corner, a settling of another, a wrench that
ran through every timber. Tom's spikes held. Mary knew their
virtue and blessed them. But the earth beneath the cabin
was dissolving, the posts were yielding, and even Tom's workmanship
could not nail a house to water.

There came a tremendous blow against the rear wall. The
boards bulged inward. Something scraped along them, crushing the porch,
and stopped with a crash that flung Mary against the
beam.

A tree had lodged against the cabin.

She could not see its length, but through the broken
wall she made out a vast, black trunk and a
confusion of roots. It was a redwood, uprooted in the
upper valley and brought down bodily by the flood, its
branches stripped, its roots clutching mud, brush, and fragments of
fence. The trunk rose and fell with the water, striking
the house at each movement.

Mary understood only that it was larger than the cabin
and might outlive it.

She tied the baby inside the quilt with a strip
torn from her skirt. She took the matches, although they
were already wet; the tin cup, although she could imagine
no immediate social or domestic use for it; and a
shawl. Then she crawled along the shelf toward the break
in the wall.

The house leaned.

The roof timbers groaned together. The chimney fell outward with
a noise like distant cannon. Sailor leaped from the wood
box, swam across the room, and scrambled through the breach.
Mary followed. One hand grasped a projecting board; one knee
found the sill; then, with the child pressed beneath her
breast, she lowered herself upon the wet trunk.

"Now, Lord!" she cried. "You've got to help me!"

Whether the appeal was considered irregular in form I cannot
say; it was answered with promptitude. A branch caught her
skirt, a root offered support, and she drew herself astride
the tree.

Behind her the cabin gave a final, singular sigh. One
wall sank, the roof folded, and the whole structure knelt
like cattle beneath a blow. For an instant the lamp
still burned upon the shelf, enclosed by the falling timbers;
then water closed over it. The tree swung free.

Mary and the child went down the river.

The darkness was absolute. There were no banks, no hills,
no stars—only the rush of water and the uprooted tree
beneath her. It rolled enough to force her to cling
with both arms. Sometimes its roots struck bottom and halted;
wreckage crowded against them; the flood accumulated, snarling and heaving;
then the mass tore loose, and the tree shot forward
with a violence that nearly swept her away.

Sailor followed. She could hear him swimming close to the
trunk, snorting as the waves broke over his head. Once
his paws found a low root, and for several minutes
he clung there. Mary called his name and praised him.
He answered with a whine, not because praise was unwelcome,
but because the circumstances afforded little opportunity for its enjoyment.

The baby had ceased crying.

Mary put her cheek to his face. She felt a
faint warmth and, after a dreadful pause, a breath. She
tucked the shawl around him and bent over until her
own body covered him. Rain ran from her hair, cold
water washed continually across her feet, and the wind cut
through the torn brown dress. Yet maternal anxiety, which is
indifferent to personal discomfort when it has selected another object,
persuaded her that she was warm enough.

At some point the river divided. She knew it by
the twisting of the trunk and by the conflicting pressure
of currents. One branch seized a standing tree; another current
pulled at the roots. The redwood revolved slowly, majestically, like
some monstrous hand upon the dial of the Flood. Mary
lost all sense of direction.

Then Sailor barked.

Across the darkness came another bark—or perhaps an echo. Sailor
abandoned the root and plunged after it.

"Sailor! Here, Sailor!"

She called again and again. Once she heard splashing far
behind; once a sharp yelp; then nothing.

There are losses which grief cannot immediately enter because terror
has occupied the whole house. Mary listened, called, and listened;
but the tree moved on, and Sailor did not return.
Only afterward, in safety, did she mourn him.

The current presently became smoother. The tree no longer struck
the banks, and the water acquired a long, regular heave.
Mary tasted the spray upon her lips.

It was fresh.

Therefore she had not yet reached the bay. The river
was still carrying her between drowned fields or through some
widened channel. This conclusion comforted her until she heard, far
off, a deep and continuous thunder.

The surf.

She had heard it on winter nights when the sea
broke upon the outer bar. It seemed now the voice
of a door standing open into immeasurable darkness. If the
tree crossed the bay and passed between the dunes, there
would be no bank, no marsh, no morning rescue—only the
Pacific, which had swallowed larger vessels with less ceremony.

The redwood drifted onward.

A light appeared through the rain, vanished, and returned. It
was pale, remote, and revolving.

The lighthouse.

Mary watched it with painful concentration. If it remained on
her left, she reasoned, she might be moving toward the
marsh; if it swung behind, she might be going out.
But the tree rotated, the fog thickened, and the light
behaved with that bureaucratic ambiguity sometimes observable in official beacons:
it stated that danger existed without offering the least practical
suggestion for avoiding it.

She dipped the tin cup into the water and tasted
again.

Still fresh.

Later it was brackish.

Later still it was salt.

At this she began to pray aloud. She prayed for
Tom, who would return to a vanished house; for Sailor,
wherever the black current had carried him; for the child,
who lay so still; and finally for herself, though with
an apologetic brevity, as if Providence might regard that petition
as excessive.

The tree struck something, rolled, and stopped. Mary thought they
had grounded, but almost immediately it slid free. Reeds brushed
her face. Then there was open water again, and after
that a soft grinding beneath the roots. The trunk lifted,
settled, lifted once more, and came to rest at an
angle.

She waited for the next movement.

None came.

The tide had carried the redwood into one of Dedlow's
blind channels and laid it across a submerged tussock. Around
it the marsh grass hissed in the wind. Mud sucked
at the roots. Driftwood accumulated along the trunk, forming by
degrees a rude breakwater. The Ark—for it had by then
earned the name, though no dove was available and the
raven would certainly have declined responsibility—had grounded.

Mary did not know this. She knew only that motion
had ceased. She attempted to crawl toward the roots, but
her hands no longer obeyed her. The baby felt cold.
She loosened the quilt and touched his face.

He neither stirred nor breathed that she could perceive.

Then, for the first time that night, Mary screamed.

She rubbed his hands; she breathed upon his mouth; she
called him every foolish little name that affection had invented
in happier hours. The face remained pale and waxen in
the obscure grayness preceding dawn. Convinced that she had saved
only a dead child, Mary drew him to her, laid
her cheek upon his, and fainted.

Morning came reluctantly to Dedlow Marsh. The rain diminished to
a mist, the clouds lifted above the eastern hills, and
the pools assumed the color of lead. Two Native women
from a settlement beyond the dunes had entered the marsh
to gather the early red berries that survived among the
hummocks. They saw the redwood first, where no tree had
stood the day before; then a strip of brown cloth
fluttered from a branch.

Approaching in their canoe, they found Mary lying across the
trunk with the child bound to her breast.

The elder woman climbed out without hesitation. She touched Mary's
throat, then the baby's, and called sharply to her companion.
Between them they cut away the wet quilt, wrapped the
child in a dry blanket, and carried both mother and
son into the canoe. At their shelter they kindled a
fire, warmed stones, rubbed Mary's limbs, and gave her a
bitter infusion of herbs. The baby they held close against
living warmth until, after an interval that seemed to Mary—when
told of it—another age of the world, he coughed, drew
breath, and uttered a thin, indignant cry.

It was not musical; it was not even grateful. But
no anthem has ever been received with greater joy.

Mary revived to the sound. She opened her eyes, saw
unfamiliar faces above her, saw the child moving, and immediately
attempted to rise. Being restrained, she "acted silly," as she
afterward admitted—laughing, weeping, and demanding Tom in the same breath.
The women understood little of her words but the child's
name, which she repeated, and Tom's, which required no translation.

Meanwhile Tom had returned at daybreak to find the creek
unrecognizable, the river spread from hill to hill, and his
cabin gone. A fragment of roof hung in the willows;
the cradle had lodged against a fence three miles below.
He followed the flood line toward the bay, questioning everyone
he met. At noon he reached a trading post near
the marsh, where a boy told him that two women
had found "a white lady and a baby" on a
floating tree.

Tom afterward said he did not run. Men of his
build seldom concede the word. He merely "went along tolerable
fast," overturning a barrel, breaking a gate latch, and arriving
at the settlement with one boot half off.

Mary was seated near the fire, wrapped in a red
blanket, the child asleep in her arms. When Tom entered,
she looked at him quietly for a moment, as if
he too might be some apparition manufactured by fever. Then
she said:

"I knew your spikes would hold as long as anything
could."

This tribute to his workmanship overcame him more completely than
any declaration of affection. He sat down on the earth
beside her, put one arm around mother and child, and
hid his face in the red blanket.

The baby recovered. Sailor did not. For several weeks Mary
set food outside the temporary shelter, though the ravens, whose
consciences are flexible in matters of bereavement, usually consumed it.

When the water withdrew, Tom found the redwood still stranded
upon Dedlow Marsh. With chains, oxen, rollers, and a vocabulary
that added materially to the local tradition, he hauled it
to a rise above the highest flood mark. There he
squared one side of the trunk and made it the
foundation beam of a new house. The building had a
steep roof, stout shutters, and a porch from which one
could see both river and bay. No water has reached
it yet.

Over the door Tom nailed a board upon which, with
more feeling than skill, he had carved the words:

**MARY'S ARK**

The name remains. I have sat beneath it while Mary,
grown gray and comfortable, recounted the night when her house
went down the river and the marsh received her. From
the porch Dedlow appears almost amiable at sunset. Its pools
shine; its reeds turn golden; its winding sloughs seem paths
into a pastoral tranquillity. But wait until the tide rises
and the fog advances from the dunes; wait until the
curlews begin their lamentation, the grasses trail upon black water,
and some uprooted trunk comes silently out of the river.

Then you may imagine the woman in the torn brown
dress, clasping her child upon that drifting tree; and you
may understand why the marsh, though it tried hard enough
to destroy her, has never been able to keep the
last word.

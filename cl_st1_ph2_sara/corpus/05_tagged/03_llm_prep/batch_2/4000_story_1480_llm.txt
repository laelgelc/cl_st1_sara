# The Bridegroom's Shroud

There stands, in a narrow street of the older portion
of New York—though commerce, with its brazen voice and iron
footsteps, has long since profaned the neighborhood—a church whose brown
walls appear to have absorbed the smoke, sorrow, and secret
prayer of more than a century. My grandmother, who remembered
its pews when they were filled by powdered heads and
stiff brocades, would never pass beneath its tower without relating
a strange history connected with its bell. She had it
from her mother, who was present at the occurrence; and
as traditions of this domestic kind acquire, through faithful repetition,
an authority not greatly inferior to that of written chronicles,
I shall tell the tale substantially as it descended to
me.

The bell, it may be observed, possessed a voice of
unusual depth. At marriages it could not be made entirely
joyful; at funerals it seemed to speak from the very
hollow of the grave. Men attributed this peculiarity to some
defect in the metal, but old women, sextons, and others
conversant with spiritual matters affirmed that a portion of the
silver consecrated to its casting had been taken from a
chalice brought out of a ruined monastery. Whether from metallurgy
or religion, its note had always the power of compelling
the heart to listen.

About forty years before the event, Mr. Ambrose Ellenwood had
been betrothed to a young lady whose beauty was then
celebrated wherever beauty, fortune, and fashionable expectation met beneath one
roof. Clara Maynard—for such was Mrs. Dabney's maiden name—had not
merely the regular features which painters praise and lovers swear
to be immortal; she possessed that mutable brightness which causes
each expression to seem the loveliest, until the next effaces
it. In mirth she was all sunshine; in tenderness, a
twilight wherein the soul might dream; and even in anger
there flashed through her countenance something which persuaded the offender
that forgiveness would be sweeter for the storm that preceded
it.

Ellenwood loved her with the grave and solitary passion of
a man who had built no other temple in his
heart. He was shy among strangers, almost uncivil in companies,
and incapable of those small gallantries by which affection is
often advertised before it is felt. Yet in Clara's presence
his nature opened as a night-flower opens to the moon.
He had learning, imagination, and a disposition naturally generous; but
these qualities, wanting a practical object, were in danger of
becoming no more than beautiful disorders. He needed love, not
as an ornament to life, but as its central law.

Why, then, were they separated?

The answer is neither uncommon nor wholly satisfactory. Her family
considered his prospects uncertain. An aunt discovered that his manners
were eccentric; a cousin doubted whether he would ever be
rich; a prudent friend proved, by figures, that affection without
income was a species of genteel starvation. Clara herself wept,
resisted, yielded, repented, and yielded again. A wealthier suitor appeared
at the precise moment when constancy had become fatiguing. Thus
was the engagement dissolved—not by one heroic act of cruelty,
but by a multitude of little prudences, each so reasonable
that nobody could accuse it, though together they murdered a
human hope.

Within a year Clara became a wife.

Her first husband carried her abroad, surrounded her with magnificence,
and died after twelve years, leaving her a handsome fortune
and a daughter who survived him only a little while.
Her second marriage was less splendid, more companionable, and perhaps
more nearly happy; but it, too, ended in widowhood. Time,
meanwhile, had done what grief could not. It had cooled
regret, hardened memory, and taught her that the world will
continue its ceremonies though an individual heart sit desolate among
the spectators.

At sixty, Mrs. Dabney possessed houses, jewels, plate, and the
polished philosophy of one who has been disappointed without consenting
to appear unhappy. She had travelled among divers nations, received
admiration in three languages, and learned to estimate mankind with
an indulgence that was half charity and half contempt. Yet
there remained one subject upon which her judgment failed her:
she could not acknowledge herself old.

She admitted that her contemporaries had aged lamentably. She mourned
over the white eyebrows of one acquaintance, the bent shoulders
of another, and the preposterous rouge of a third; but
the mirror, which told her an equal truth, she regarded
as an ill-tempered counsellor whose evidence might be corrected by
candlelight, powder, satin, and the artful disposition of a curl.
She did not hope to be mistaken for twenty; she
merely insisted that sixty, in her particular instance, bore no
resemblance to sixty in others. Vanity, when young, demands homage
from the world; when old, it is content if the
world will assist in deceiving it.

Ellenwood, during these forty years, had remained unmarried. He was
not faithful in the cheerful and fruitful meaning of the
word, for faithfulness should preserve what is good in love;
his constancy preserved chiefly its wound. He read much, travelled
aimlessly, performed occasional acts of munificence, and acquired a reputation
for singularity. The poor blessed him, scholars respected him, children
feared him, and fashionable people pronounced him mad whenever he
failed to resemble themselves.

He lived in a gloomy house with shuttered rooms, and
was said to keep, in an ebony cabinet, the faded
ribbon with which Clara had tied his first packet of
letters. Possibly the rumor was false. Yet there are men
who make reliquaries of their whole existence, embalming therein a
dead affection, and then wonder that the air around them
smells of the sepulchre.

They met again by accident—or by Providence, which often wears
the plain cloak of accident—at a winter assembly. The widow
entered beneath chandeliers whose innumerable lights were scarcely more brilliant
than the diamonds on her breast. Ellenwood stood apart, tall,
thin, and silver-haired, with an expression in which pride had
long ago frozen pain into dignity.

Their eyes encountered.

For one moment the ballroom vanished from between them. The
musicians, the fans, the silks, the mirrors repeating a hundred
false worlds—all were annihilated; and they saw each other as
beside a garden gate forty years before. Then the vision
passed. He bowed profoundly. She smiled, trembled, and addressed him
by his Christian name.

From that meeting arose, first, a correspondence; next, visits; finally,
an engagement which astonished society. Some laughed, some pitied, and
many condemned. Young ladies declared it romantic until they imagined
themselves in the bride's place. Elderly gentlemen called Ellenwood a
fool with unusual vehemence, perhaps because his folly resembled a
secret wish of their own. As for Mrs. Dabney, she
prepared for the nuptials with a magnificence more suitable, it
was whispered, to the marriage of her granddaughter.

The appointed morning came bright and cold. Sunshine streamed through
the eastern windows of the old church and touched the
memorial tablets, gilded the organ pipes, and lay in long,
cheerful bars across the aisle. The guests assembled early, eager
to behold a spectacle in which tenderness and absurdity were
expected to contend for mastery.

Near the altar stood the clergyman, a mild old divine
whose white hair and tranquil face made him appear already
half translated from earthly passion. He alone, perhaps, saw nothing
ridiculous in the approaching union. "If love," he had said,
"be sincere at the eleventh hour, we may welcome it,
though we regret the wasted day."

A murmur at the door announced the bride.

Mrs. Dabney entered with six attendants, all young, rosy, and
richly apparelled. Among them she appeared like a rose preserved
by curious art amid the fresh buds of June. Her
robe was of pearl-colored satin, heavily wrought with silver; a
mantle of violet velvet descended from her shoulders; orange blossoms,
mingled with jewels, glittered in her hair. Art had softened
the channels upon her brow and lent an autumnal warmth
to her cheek. From a distance she might have seemed
stately rather than aged; but the cruel light, passing through
the high windows, discovered here a wrinkle beneath the powder,
there the tremulous weakness of a hand, and everywhere that
delicate rigidity with which age imitates composure.

Still, she bore herself proudly. If she perceived a smile,
she vanquished it with a glance. If her own heart
whispered that bridal roses were unnatural beside gray hair, the
diamonds answered more loudly.

She had advanced halfway up the aisle when the bell
struck.

One deep note fell from the tower.

It was not the brisk clamor appointed for a wedding,
but a single, measured stroke such as summons mourners after
a coffin. The sound descended through the church, shook the
painted windows, and seemed to linger beneath the arches after
the metal itself was still.

The bride paused.

"What means that?" she asked.

"A mistake of the sexton," said one attendant.

"An ill-chosen jest," whispered another.

The procession moved forward.

Again the bell struck.

The sunshine, at that instant, was obscured by a passing
cloud. The satin lost its lustre; the gold upon the
pillars grew pale; and the company, which a moment before
had resembled a brilliant painting, seemed suddenly old, faded, and
unreal. The bride's velvet mantle became in color almost black.

A carriage stopped before the church. Its wheels moved with
that slow and decorous heaviness which belongs to funeral equipages.
Those nearest the entrance turned and saw, through the open
doors, a hearse standing at the curb.

There was now no whisper of laughter.

"Has some burial been appointed for this hour?" asked Mrs.
Dabney, addressing the clergyman.

"None that I know of," he replied; and though his
voice was calm, he looked toward the vestibule with grave
perplexity.

The bell struck a third time.

Then there entered two old men dressed in mourning. They
walked abreast, leaning upon ebony canes; behind them came two
aged women whose faces were hidden by long veils; after
these followed others, male and female, all bowed with years,
all silent, and all clothed as for the grave. Some
were known to the company as former companions of Ellenwood's
youth. Others appeared so ancient that they might have risen
from family portraits or stepped out of forgotten tombs.

They ranged themselves on either side of the aisle. The
bridal party, with its roses, feathers, gems, and youthful faces,
stood surrounded by this spectral assembly. It was as if
Pleasure, having decked herself for a festival, had been seized
and brought to judgment before the court of Time.

Mrs. Dabney's face became white beneath its borrowed bloom.

"Where is Mr. Ellenwood?" she demanded. "Why does he delay?"

A voice from the doorway answered:

"The bridegroom comes."

All eyes turned.

Ambrose Ellenwood entered alone.

His garments were not those of a bridegroom. From his
shoulders to his feet he was wrapped in a burial
shroud. A linen band encircled his brow; his face, naturally
pale, had the fixed severity of one who had already
looked upon the secrets of death and returned less to
comfort the living than to accuse them. In one hand
he carried no bridal ring, but a withered wreath; in
the other, a taper such as is placed beside the
dead.

A cry arose from the women. Several guests drew back.
The bride remained motionless, her jewels flashing upon a bosom
that scarcely seemed to breathe.

Ellenwood advanced while the bell continued its slow, tremendous tolling.
Each stroke accompanied a step. When he reached the altar,
he stood opposite Mrs. Dabney and regarded her in silence.

She attempted to speak with indignation.

"Ambrose, what cruel masquerade is this?"

"Cruel?" he repeated. "You call it cruel?"

His voice was low, yet it filled the church more
completely than the bell.

"Yes, cruel!" she exclaimed. "To summon me here before my
friends—to make me a spectacle—to insult the sacred ceremony—"

"Sacred!" said Ellenwood. "Then let us strip it of falsehood.
Let satin fall away in imagination; let jewels cease to
bribe the eye; let paint confess the furrows it conceals.
We are not two young lovers at the gate of
life. We are two aged beings upon the threshold of
a tomb. Why should our wedding lie?"

Mrs. Dabney covered her face, but he continued, and his
words came now with a mournful and deliberate cadence.

"Forty years ago you were my bride in hope. Your
hair was dark; your cheek needed no art to make
it bloom; your heart—ah, Clara, I believed your heart was
mine! Then came wealth, rank, counsel, fear, and that cold
arithmetic by which the prudent calculate how much affection may
safely be sacrificed for comfort. You gave another man your
youth. To another you gave the first sweetness of companionship,
the birth of your child, the vigor of your days.
A second received what grief had spared. And I—what remains
for me?"

He lifted the withered wreath.

"This remains. Your gray hairs, your exhausted pleasures, your memories
of other hearths; a hand already cold with approaching death;
a few infirm years, if years be granted; and then
a grave beside mine. Shall I dress in festival garments
to receive such a portion? No! The shroud is our
honest wedding robe."

She lowered her hands. Anger, shame, and anguish struggled in
her countenance.

"You knew my age," she said. "I did not conceal
the years from you."

"You concealed them from yourself," replied Ellenwood. "And I—I have
concealed from myself the poison of my remembrance. You adorned
decay; I worshipped resentment. Your falsehood was painted on the
face; mine lay deeper, and was therefore darker."

This unexpected judgment upon himself disturbed the stern triumph of
his accusation. His voice faltered; but the old grievance, once
summoned before so many witnesses, demanded its full utterance.

"Look around you," he cried. "Here stand the companions of
our morning, now bent and withered. There waits the hearse,
more faithful than any earthly lover, because it never fails
its appointment. Above us sounds the bell that shall toll
for you and me. Come, then, Clara! Come, my bride!
Let us be wedded, not as those who expect springtime,
children, harvests, and a long household future, but as two
ghosts who have met beside an open sepulchre. Wed me
at the grave's edge—if you dare!"

The old mourners bowed their heads. The bridal attendants wept,
though some scarcely understood whether they wept from pity, terror,
or the contagious solemnity of his speech. The clergyman alone
kept his eyes upon Mrs. Dabney, as if awaiting the
moment when vanity, wounded past endurance, must either harden into
pride or break open and reveal a human heart.

The transformation was not immediate. No true victory over a
lifelong falsehood is accomplished without agony. Mrs. Dabney looked down
upon her embroidered robe; she touched the gems at her
breast; then she glanced toward the young women around her,
whose beauty had made her own age so terribly visible.
At last she tore the orange blossoms from her hair.

They fell upon the pavement.

"You have punished me," she said. "Perhaps you have punished
me justly; but not wholly justly."

Ellenwood started.

"I was weak when I abandoned you. I was persuaded
by those whose wisdom I had been taught to reverence.
I feared poverty; I feared ridicule; I feared all things
except the one calamity that came—the loss of what was
best in me. My first marriage gave me splendor, and
my second gave me kindness. I will not defame the
dead; they wronged neither you nor me. But through all
those years there was an empty chamber in my heart,
and your name was written on its locked door."

She took one step toward him.

"You say others had my youth. It is true. Would
you have had me bring it back from their graves?
You say I offer you age. It is true. Age
is all that time has left me. Yet what I
offer now I offer freely, which I did not know
how to do when I was young. Then I loved
you and yielded you up; now I love you and
come despite the laughter of the world. Is there no
value in a heart because it has suffered? Is late
truth nothing because early truth was betrayed?"

The bell struck again, but now its note seemed less
an accusation than an answer from eternity.

Mrs. Dabney unclasped the jewels from her neck and placed
them upon the altar. Without their glitter, without the artificial
flowers, without the proud smile by which she had defended
herself against time, she looked older—yet nobler. Her face had
ceased to be a mask laboriously preserved from ruin; it
was the record of a life, and tears made it
beautiful.

"If you desire revenge," she continued, "you have it. Every
eye here has seen me humbled. If you desire sincerity,
behold me as I am. My youth is dead. My
beauty is dead. Many hopes are dead. But love, which
I believed buried with them, has risen. Ambrose, shall it
find no welcome because it comes from a tomb?"

Ellenwood's taper slipped from his hand and was extinguished upon
the stones.

For a while he stood rigid beneath the shroud. Then
the strange severity passed from his face, and those who
watched him perceived how old, how weary, and how lonely
he had become. The grotesque apparition vanished; there remained only
a man who had nursed his injury until it had
grown almost stronger than his love, and who now discovered
that forgiveness was the one blessing he had never dared
to seek.

"Clara," he said, "I, too, have been cruel."

She extended her hand.

He took it and pressed it first to his lips,
then against his heart.

"Forgive me," she whispered.

"Forgive me," he replied. "You destroyed my hope in youth;
I would have avenged it upon your age. Let the
wrongs lie together in one grave."

Then, before the whole assembly, he removed the linen band
from his brow and cast back the shroud. Beneath it
he wore a plain suit of black, such as became
his years and the solemnity of the place. There was
no brightness in his apparel, but his countenance had acquired
a light which neither jewels nor bridal satin could bestow.

The clergyman descended one step and joined their hands.

"My friends," he said, "you have brought death into the
marriage ceremony; but death, rightly understood, is no enemy to
love. It is only the gate through which love must
prove whether it belongs to the body or the soul."

The vows were then pronounced.

At first the funeral bell continued to toll, and its
iron lamentation mingled awfully with the sacred words. Yet when
Ellenwood promised fidelity, not for the long years they could
no longer expect, but for whatever brief remnant Providence might
grant, the organ breathed a low harmony from the shadowy
end of the church. It rose gradually, gathering strength through
the arches, until the bell and organ seemed to contend—the
one proclaiming decay, separation, and the grave; the other answering
with reconciliation, immortality, and peace.

The ring was placed upon the bride's aged hand.

The blessing was spoken.

Then the organ swelled to its full majesty, and the
knell, though still sounding from the tower, was conquered and
absorbed into the music. What had begun as terror became
solemn triumph. The hearse remained before the door, the shroud
lay upon the pavement, and the withered wreath rested beside
the fallen blossoms; yet these emblems had lost their cruelty.
They signified no longer the vengeance of the past, but
the burial of pride, resentment, and delusion.

Thus were Ambrose Ellenwood and Clara Dabney married—not with the
vain expectation of wresting youth from time, nor with the
poorer vanity of pretending that winter is spring, but in
full knowledge that their earthly companionship must be brief. Others
had possessed the morning and noon of their lives; to
each other they gave the evening. And if the evening
was cold, it had stars.

My grandmother always ended the narrative by declaring that the
old bell never afterward sounded wholly mournful. Even at funerals
there was heard, beneath its deepest note, a vibration like
distant organ music. Whether this be fact, fancy, or one
of those truths which the heart perceives though the ear
cannot, I leave to wiser judgment. But the moral of
the tale appears sufficiently plain: that death is less terrible
than falsehood, age less pitiable than vanity, and love never
altogether late when it arrives in time for forgiveness.

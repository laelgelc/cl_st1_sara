# Ohio's Years of Trial

In the year 1803 Ohio found herself a state, though
she had come within one vote of entering the Union
with a stain upon her first garment. The convention assembled
to frame a constitution had before it a proposal which,
under the mild name of temporary servitude, would have admitted
slavery for a limited period. Such limitations, as we know,
have often proved stronger in promise than in performance; an
evil once established is not easily persuaded to depart when
its appointed hour has come.

The contest was close. Men spoke of labor, property, and
the supposed necessities of a new country; but Judge Ephraim
Cutler, weak in body and hardly able to attend, saw
the question more clearly. He appeared at the decisive hour,
gave his voice against the proposal, and helped defeat it
by the narrowest possible majority. Thus freedom, which afterward seemed
inseparable from Ohio's history, was preserved by one frail man's
determination. The constitution was adopted; Ohio became the seventeenth state;
and there has never since been any serious doubt that
she belongs to the Union.

Her first years were years of peace and comfort, if
not of great prosperity. The forest fell before the ax;
clearings widened; cabins multiplied; orchards were planted, and fields of
corn took the place of thickets where deer had wandered.
A family might possess rich soil and abundant harvests and
yet remain poor, for produce without a market is wealth
imprisoned. Roads were few and bad. In spring they became
channels of mud; in winter they were blocked with snow;
in summer their stumps and ruts broke wheels and exhausted
horses. Rivers were the natural highways, but they flowed where
nature pleased and not always where the farmer wished to
go.

Still the state advanced. Towns grew, if they did not
grow rapidly; mills appeared beside the streams; schoolhouses and meetinghouses
followed the cabins. Ohio might have continued quietly in this
manner had not forces beyond her northern boundary disturbed the
frontier.

The British still held Canada, and British agents maintained influence
among the Indigenous nations of the Northwest. Arms, ammunition, promises,
and suspicion traveled southward from the lakes. At the same
time settlers pressed westward, treaties were questioned, hunting grounds diminished,
and resentment deepened. Out of these grievances arose Tecumseh, a
Shawnee chief of remarkable courage and commanding eloquence, and his
brother Tenskwatawa, called the Prophet. The Prophet appealed to religion
and old custom; Tecumseh attempted something greater—a union of many
nations against further American settlement.

He moved from council fire to council fire, carrying the
same grave warning. No single people, he declared, could sell
land which belonged to all. His project was bold, and
for a time it seemed possible; but the nations were
divided by distance, old quarrels, and unequal interests. Nevertheless, when
war began between Great Britain and the United States in
1812, Tecumseh and many of his followers joined the British.
Ohio, which had lately heard only the ax and the
mill wheel, now listened for the drum.

The first great news was disastrous. General William Hull crossed
from Detroit into Canada, hesitated, retreated, and finally surrendered Detroit
without the struggle expected of him. By that act the
whole northern frontier was exposed. Settlements which had thought themselves
protected discovered that the barrier had vanished. Families abandoned lonely
cabins and crowded into blockhouses; militia hurried toward the north;
rumors multiplied faster than troops. Every shadow beyond a field
might conceal an enemy, and every delayed messenger was believed
to carry tidings of defeat.

General William Henry Harrison was appointed to recover what had
been lost. He gathered an army and advanced through a
country in which mud, snow, hunger, and distance were often
more formidable than bullets. Supplies had to be dragged over
roads that scarcely deserved the name. Horses died in the
traces; wagons sank to their hubs; men marched with frozen
clothing and insufficient food. Courage was plentiful, but courage does
not bake bread or lift a cannon from a swamp.

Part of Harrison's army moved toward the River Raisin, near
Frenchtown. There, after early success, the Americans were attacked by
British regulars under General Henry Proctor and by Indigenous warriors.
Their position was overwhelmed. Many were killed; many more surrendered
under promises of protection. Proctor then withdrew, leaving wounded prisoners
exposed and poorly guarded. The next morning slaughter began.

Tecumseh was not present, but when he learned what had
happened he condemned it with a force which did him
honor. On another occasion, seeing prisoners threatened, he sprang among
the assailants and demanded that the killing cease. He served
the British cause, but he was not the servant of
cruelty; and while we may oppose his purpose, justice requires
that we remember his humanity.

The cry "Remember the Raisin!" passed through Ohio and the
western army. It was not merely a call for revenge;
it was the voice of a frontier suddenly aware of
the awful possibilities of defeat.

Harrison now established Fort Meigs upon the Maumee River. It
was a large work of timber and earth, built to
protect the country and serve as a base for recovering
Detroit. Scarcely was it completed when Proctor appeared with British
artillery, while Tecumseh's warriors surrounded the woods. Shot and shell
fell into the fort; marksmen watched every opening; the ground
outside belonged to the enemy.

Yet the defenders dug traverses within the works, and these
walls of earth caught the British balls. Men who at
first had exposed themselves soon learned to pass behind shelter.
Harrison answered demands for surrender with firmness. Relief arrived, though
part of the relieving force, carried away by excitement after
capturing enemy batteries, pursued too far and was surrounded. Again
prisoners suffered; again Tecumseh intervened where he could. Fort Meigs
held. A second attempt was no more successful, and Proctor
turned toward Fort Stephenson at Lower Sandusky, supposing that a
smaller post would yield an easier triumph.

Fort Stephenson was commanded by Major George Croghan, who was
only twenty-one years old. His garrison numbered little more than
a hundred and fifty men, and his principal artillery consisted
of one six-pound cannon, affectionately known as Old Betsy. Harrison,
believing the place indefensible against a large force, ordered Croghan
to destroy the works and retreat.

But retreat was already dangerous, perhaps impossible. The enemy was
near, and Croghan feared that his little column would be
overtaken in the open country. He therefore sent a message
declaring, in substance, that he had decided to maintain the
fort and would not abandon it.

To a commanding general, this sounded very much like disobedience,
and Croghan was removed. He immediately went to Harrison and
explained that his words had been intended partly to deceive
the enemy should the message be intercepted; he had never
meant disrespect. Harrison, satisfied with the explanation and perhaps secretly
pleased by the young officer's spirit, restored him to command.

Proctor arrived with British regulars, artillery, and a large body
of warriors. He demanded surrender and warned that, if the
fort were taken by assault, he might not be able
to restrain his allies. Croghan answered that when the fort
was taken there would be no one left alive to
require restraint.

The enemy opened fire. Within the works Croghan moved Old
Betsy from place to place, firing occasionally so that the
British could not determine where the gun stood. Meanwhile he
prepared for the attack he expected. At one angle of
the fort he strengthened the wall, dug a ditch, and
concealed the cannon in a masked embrasure, loading it heavily
with grapeshot and slugs.

The bombardment continued; then the British advanced. Their columns came
steadily forward through smoke and fire, reached the ditch, and
crowded into it, believing the wall above them nearly beaten
down. At that instant the hidden port flew open.

Old Betsy spoke.

At such close range the discharge was terrible. The head
of the column was swept away; men fell upon one
another in the ditch; those behind attempted to advance and
could not, while those before attempted to escape and found
the way blocked. American riflemen poured down a galling fire.
The assault wavered, broke, and fled. Proctor, who had promised
an easy conquest, retreated in haste, leaving wounded men upon
the field.

Then Croghan showed that his courage was not brutality. Though
the darkness made it dangerous to open the gates, the
defenders carried water to the ditch, drew in the wounded,
dressed their injuries, and protected them from further harm. Thus
the same hands which had struck fiercely in battle performed
the gentler offices of mercy. The defense of Fort Stephenson
rang through the country, and the young major became the
hero of the hour.

Yet the mastery of the region depended upon Lake Erie.
So long as the British controlled its waters, they could
supply Detroit and move troops along the shore. The United
States therefore sent Commodore Oliver Hazard Perry to create a
fleet where no fleet existed.

The undertaking was almost incredible. Timber grew beside the lake,
but canvas, cordage, cannon, anchors, tools, and skilled workmen had
to be transported through the wilderness. Roads disappeared in marshes;
wagons broke; teams failed. Nevertheless, the materials arrived. Trees were
marked, felled, shaped, and joined; and from the forest, as
if the wilderness itself had taken sides in the contest,
arose brigs and schooners ready for war.

On the morning of September 10, 1813, Perry sailed to
meet the British. From his flagship, the *Lawrence*, floated a
blue banner bearing the words, "Don't Give Up the Ship."
The lines closed. Cannon smoke rolled over the water, spars
splintered, rigging fell, and the decks became scenes of confusion
and death. Because some American vessels had not yet come
effectively into action, the *Lawrence* endured the concentrated British fire.
One gun after another was silenced; men fell at their
stations; the ship became nearly unmanageable.

Defeat appeared close. Perry, however, would not surrender with his
shattered vessel. Taking his battle flag, he descended into a
small boat and was rowed through the fire to the
*Niagara*. British guns turned upon him, and shot struck the
water about the boat; but he reached the second vessel
unharmed.

Then the fortune of battle changed. A fresh wind brought
the other American ships forward. Perry drove the *Niagara* through
the British line, firing from both sides, while our vessels
closed upon the disordered enemy. The British, already weakened by
their struggle with the *Lawrence*, could not endure the new
attack. One after another their flags descended.

Perry wrote his report upon the back of an old
letter. It contained the words which have outlived the roar
of the cannon: "We have met the enemy and they
are ours."

Victory did not abolish sorrow. American and British dead lay
beneath flags which, a few hours earlier, they had defended
against one another. They were borne to shore with solemn
ceremony; minute guns sounded across the lake; oars dipped slowly;
funeral music followed the fallen. In the presence of death,
triumph put aside its boasting. Enemies in life received the
last sad rites together, while half-masted colors stirred above them.

The lake now belonged to the Americans. Proctor abandoned Detroit
and retreated into Canada, accompanied by Tecumseh and his diminishing
followers. Harrison crossed in pursuit. Tecumseh urged the British commander
to stand and fight, reproaching him for retreat after retreat.
At last, near the Thames River, Proctor formed his line.

The American cavalry charged and broke the British position with
astonishing speed. Proctor escaped, but his soldiers surrendered in large
numbers. In the woods the struggle was fiercer, for there
Tecumseh and his warriors held their ground. The mighty Shawnee
chief fought among his people and fell; and with him
fell the strongest spirit of organized Indigenous resistance in that
region. His followers, hearing that he was dead, withdrew into
the forest.

So ended Ohio's great danger. Detroit was recovered, British power
on Lake Erie was broken, and the frontier entered a
period of security such as it had not known before.
Roads would still be poor, crops would still lack markets,
and political quarrels would by no means disappear; but the
state had survived her years of trial.

She had begun with a vote for freedom, endured invasion
and alarm, and supplied men and material for victory. Cutler
in the convention, Harrison behind the walls of Fort Meigs,
Croghan beside his masked cannon, Perry crossing an open lake
beneath the enemy's fire, and even Tecumseh restraining cruelty amid
a bitter war—each left an example larger than the immediate
event. For history is not made only by armies and
constitutions. Often it turns upon a sick man who rises
to vote, a young officer who refuses to retreat, a
commander who changes ships instead of surrendering, or a defeated
warrior who remembers mercy when others have forgotten it.

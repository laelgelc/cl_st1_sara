### Melons

I never discovered whether Melons was a baptismal designation, a
family inheritance, or merely one of those titles which, like
the appellations of conquerors and saints, are conferred by an
admiring public after the fact. His head, it is true,
offered no suggestion of the fruit. It was small, white,
and covered with a transparent down that gave him the
appearance of a very old chicken imperfectly restored to youth.
Nor did his figure justify the plural abundance of his
cognomen. He was narrow, angular, and chiefly remarkable for a
pair of trousers which, having apparently descended through several generations
of larger men, were attached to his person by one
suspender and an optimism almost sublime.

He was seven years old. This statement rests upon his
own authority, and is therefore open to those objections which
attend all autobiographical statistics. At one time he was six;
at another, and on the same afternoon, he confessed to
nine. But seven seemed a diplomatic mean, and I accepted
it.

Melons lived in McGinnis's Court, beside which, through circumstances reflecting
little credit upon either my ambition or my income, I
had taken rooms.

McGinnis's Court was not, properly speaking, a court. It had
no fountain, no carriage entrance, no uniformed guardians, and no
recorded connection with McGinnis. It was a rectangular depression between
two more respectable streets, into which the discarded architecture, broken
crockery, surplus children, and disappointed cats of the neighborhood had
gradually settled. A fence leaned over it confidentially. Three wooden
houses stood about it in attitudes of fatigue. From their
windows depended garments whose relation to the human form was
occasionally conjectural. A geranium, growing in a fish tin upon
a sill, had long ago exchanged the hope of blossoms
for the more practical ambition of collecting dust.

Yet the Court had character. It refused to conform to
circumstances. Drainage might fail, shutters might hang by one hinge,
and the morning sun might reach it only after a
struggle with adjacent roofs; nevertheless, life there was vigorous, contentious,
and frequently upside down. By noon its inhabitants had quarreled,
reconciled, borrowed, lent, cooked, sung, and publicly revised one another's
moral histories. Compared with it, the adjacent streets—whose brass plates
shone, whose children wore socks, and whose doors opened only
in response to bells—seemed deficient in incident.

Melons was the presiding genius of the place.

I first observed him walking along the top rail of
the fence, his arms extended, his enormous trousers moving independently
in the breeze. Reaching a post, he paused, turned a
somersault into the Court, and disappeared behind a washtub. A
moment later his white head emerged from a neighboring window,
where, so far as I could judge, no door had
admitted him. This indifference to the ordinary avenues of approach
distinguished his whole career. He entered houses by roofs, left
them by cellars, and regarded stairs as a concession to
age and infirmity.

His occupations were various but never lucrative. He could balance
upon a clothesline, imitate a policeman with painful fidelity, and
cause a sleeping dog to reconsider its philosophy. He knew
where every backyard fence was loose, which kitchen windows produced
cakes, and at what hour the private school in Laurel
Street liberated its pupils. These latter, being the children of
wealthier and more cautious families, were forbidden to associate with
him. The prohibition naturally established his social supremacy.

Mothers who would not have admitted Melons to their hallways
found their sons returning at dusk with torn collars, exalted
expressions, and imperfect accounts of subterranean passages, pirates, and buried
firearms. Daughters who had been instructed not even to look
toward McGinnis's Court were discovered looking toward nothing else. Melons
needed only to whisper through a paling that a dead
Chinaman, a circus tiger, or a gentleman hanged for murder
was concealed in the vacant stable, and the flower of
Laurel Street immediately abandoned education, refreshments, and filial duty to
investigate.

His finest achievement in this direction concerned Tommy Bainbridge, aged
eight, the sole male hope of a family possessing a
piano and opinions. Melons persuaded Tommy that the upper surface
of a roof was the natural place from which to
view the world. By a system of boxes, drainpipes, and
unrecorded miracles, he conducted him to the ridge of Mrs.
Flaherty's house, and there left him.

When discovered, Tommy was seated astride the chimney, sobbing with
dignity, while Mrs. Flaherty below offered religious advice and his
father, from an adjoining yard, threatened the utter and complete
extermination of Melons.

Melons heard this sentence from beneath the house.

"Tell him," he called, "Tommy went up hisself."

This defense, though legally incomplete, contained that germ of truth
which has preserved more serious men from punishment. Tommy had
indeed gone up. That he would never have conceived the
ascent without Melons was a consideration belonging to ethics rather
than locomotion.

There was also the affair of the blind harper. This
venerable musician came into the Court one afternoon and seated
himself upon a box, where he performed a lament so
prolonged and penetrating that every adult withdrew indoors. Melons alone
remained. He stood before the blind man with his hands
behind him, listening in an attitude of grave criticism. Then
he removed his cap, collected contributions from windows, and restored
to the harper perhaps one-half of the proceeds. With the
remainder he purchased gingerbread, which he divided among three smaller
children and a goat. Whether this disposition of the fund
constituted embezzlement, charity, or artistic management I have never determined.
Melons himself evaded inquiry by walking on his hands.

My own acquaintance with him grew without formal introduction. My
window was low and looked directly upon the Court; his
world, consequently, lay within ten feet of my desk. During
the period of which I write, I was engaged upon
a literary work of magnitude. Its precise nature had not
been finally selected, but its magnitude was unquestionable. Sometimes it
presented itself as a novel involving the decline of an
old family; sometimes as a philosophical essay upon the modern
conscience; once, during a rainy week, it became a tragedy
in five acts and blank verse. I prepared for it
methodically.

At nine o'clock each morning I sharpened three pencils. At
half past nine I arranged my books according to height.
At ten I reflected that true composition requires freedom from
domestic anxiety and went out to purchase tobacco. Returning at
eleven, I discovered that the light was unfavorable. After luncheon,
the digestion, being engaged in its own dark labors, could
not be disturbed by intellectual effort. Thus, by a rigorous
system, I preserved my great work from the dangers of
premature execution.

During these sacred intervals Melons would appear upon the fence.

He never asked what I was writing, possibly because the
answer was visible. I never asked why he was not
at school, possibly because I respected consistency. For half an
hour we might regard one another in silence—he swinging a
bare foot above the abyss, I holding a pen above
an equally empty page. A vagabond instinct we felt in
common. I concealed mine under a dressing gown; he displayed
his in trousers.

Occasionally he offered information.

"Mike's mother throwed a flatiron at him."

"Indeed."

"She missed."

"I am relieved."

"Mike ain't."

Or he would say, "They is a cirkis comin'."

"On what authority?"

"A feller seen the hosses."

"What fellow?"

"A feller wot seen 'em."

This circular evidence satisfied him, and, on certain mornings, satisfied
me better than the arguments of philosophers.

It was in September that I purchased the bananas.

They were green when they entered my possession—green with that
rigid, incorruptible greenness which resists alike hunger and persuasion. The
fruit seller advised patience. I therefore suspended the bunch outside
my window, where sun and air might bring it by
gentle gradations to maturity. For three days I watched the
yellow principle advance. Here, at least, was progress. Though my
tragedy remained unwritten and the modern conscience unexplained, the bananas
fulfilled their destiny.

On the fourth morning they were gone.

The string remained. It hung from the nail in a
manner at once innocent and accusing, like the rope in
a historical picture after the martyr has been removed. I
examined the ground. There were no peels, no footprints that
could be distinguished from the general archaeology of McGinnis's Court,
and no witnesses. Mrs. Flaherty, when questioned, said she had
troubles enough. Mike's mother said she hoped they were poisoned.
The goat looked thoughtful but not compromised.

Then a boy passed the window eating a banana.

He was followed by a second boy, also eating a
banana.

A third appeared with two bananas, one in either hand,
and that expression of excessive virtue which attends recently acquired
property. Within five minutes the Court seemed to have entered
upon a tropical era. Bananas emerged from doorways, behind fences,
and even from the pocket of an infant who possessed
only two teeth and no apparent means of purchase.

I summoned the first boy.

"Where did you obtain that fruit?"

He pointed vaguely westward. "A man give it."

"What man?"

"A banana man."

"Why?"

The question seemed to reveal an improper curiosity. He withdrew,
carrying the evidence.

My suspicions fixed themselves upon Melons. That he had taken
the bananas was not proved; but proof, as every historian
knows, is often the timid follower of conviction. He had
opportunity, agility, an established contempt for property, and a constituency
that was plainly receiving benefits. Moreover, he was absent. Innocence,
in McGinnis's Court, was generally noisy and nearby.

Toward noon his head rose slowly beyond the fence. First
came the venerable down, then two cautious eyes, then the
remainder of Melons. He sat upon the rail and regarded
the sky.

"Melons," I said.

He did not hear.

"Melons."

He examined one foot with scientific interest.

"Where are my bananas?"

At the word "bananas" he looked surprised—not guilty, but wounded
by the sudden introduction of a subject unworthy of our
friendship.

"They is a cirkis," he said.

"Where?"

"Down by the wharf. They got two el'phants and a
hoss with spots."

"Where are my bananas?"

"A feller says the el'phant busted loose."

"You took those bananas."

"No, I didn't."

"You took those bananas."

"Billy took 'em."

"Who is Billy?"

Melons indicated, with a comprehensive sweep of his thumb, that
Billy inhabited some remote region beyond the west, perhaps beyond
the known continent.

"Billy wot lives up there."

"I have never seen Billy."

"He don't come down."

"Yet he came down for my bananas?"

"He come down to onct and took 'em."

This narrative, although defective in detail, was delivered with composure.
Melons even leaned toward my window, as if willing to
assist in the prosecution of Billy. But I had been
robbed. Worse, I had been interrupted in the act of
not writing. Property and authorship, those twin pillars of civilization,
demanded vindication.

I rose.

"Melons," I said, "attend to me. The act of removing
those bananas from my premises, secretly and with intent to
convert them to your own use, constitutes theft. The subsequent
distribution of the stolen articles among confederates does not diminish
the offense. On the contrary, it suggests conspiracy. Under the
statutes of this State, taking goods animo furandi—"

Melons blinked.

"—is a felony, punishable by imprisonment. Your youth may mitigate
the sentence, but it does not destroy culpability. You have
violated confidence, invaded property, corrupted infants, and placed yourself within
reach of the law."

For the first time since I had known him, Melons
appeared impressed. His bare toes tightened upon the rail. His
eyes, usually as impersonal as those of a sparrow, fastened
upon mine.

"You took those bananas," I concluded.

He slipped from the fence.

I expected him to reappear beneath the window, upon the
roof, or from the interior of my wardrobe. He did
not. The afternoon passed without his white head traversing the
Court. The next morning brought no Melons. On the third
day I inquired of Mrs. Flaherty.

"Him?" she said. "Sure, I haven't seen the young villain."

Mike had not seen him. The banana-eating children denied not
only knowledge of his whereabouts but any previous acquaintance with
fruit. Laurel Street knew nothing. Tommy Bainbridge's father expressed satisfaction
and hoped the boy had been transported. The blind harper,
encountered near the market, remembered a small philanthropist but could
offer no address.

A week passed. Then two.

I searched the vacant stable, though without belief in the
tiger. I examined the wharf, where there was no circus,
no spotted horse, and no elephant enjoying liberty. I made
cautious inquiries at the police station and was asked whether
Melons had another name. Confronted with this absurd demand, I
withdrew. To imagine him as Frederick, Henry, or James seemed
an injury to identity.

His disappearance began to trouble me. I had meant only
to alarm him slightly, as magistrates alarm society, for its
good. I had not considered that my Latin phrase, reinforced
by statutes and imprisonment, might drive him from the only
republic suited to his genius. Perhaps he had gone to
sea. Perhaps he had joined the circus whose existence he
had so consistently maintained. Perhaps Billy, descending at last from
his inaccessible residence, had led him into the west.

There were darker conjectures. The harbor was deep. Wagons passed
rapidly in the outer street. Children of seven, even when
enlarged by inherited trousers, are physically frail. At night I
sometimes fancied I heard his feet upon the fence, but
it was only rain, or the cat, or conscience—that domestic
animal which becomes active when others sleep.

McGinnis's Court continued. Garments hung, mothers disputed, and the geranium
collected dust. Yet something vertical had departed from the scene.
No pale head rose above the roofs; no bare feet
quivered over the fence; no affluent child of Laurel Street
was lured from respectability into adventure. Order, briefly achieved, proved
dull.

I never saw Melons again.

It is possible, of course, that he merely crossed two
streets and forgot me. Children escape from grief with an
ease denied to authors. It is possible that he grew
up, entered commerce, and now protects his bananas by every
process of law. He may have died young; he may
have died of old age at seven; he may have
folded himself into those enormous trousers and vanished through a
rent in material existence.

Sometimes I entertain a final and more disturbing theory. Perhaps
Melons never existed with the solidity I attributed to him.
I saw him, after all, through a low window, during
hours dedicated to imaginative labor. He may have been the
visible shape assumed by idleness, hunger, and an empty page—a
white-headed messenger sent to reproach me, rob me, and depart.

Yet the bananas were unquestionably gone.

And when at last I took up my pen, intending
only to record the facts for private relief, I found
that the great unwritten work, the philosophical tragedy, the historical
novel, and the modern conscience could wait. Melons, by merit
raised to that bad eminence, had filled the void.

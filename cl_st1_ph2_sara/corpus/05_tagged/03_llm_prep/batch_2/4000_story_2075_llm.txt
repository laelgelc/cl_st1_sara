## The White Rose Road

It was on one of those afternoons in June when
the whole country appears to have been newly made, and
to be so well satisfied with the work that it
can do nothing but shine and grow. We had left
the village soon after dinner, when the white clapboarded houses,
standing among their elms, wore a look of comfortable retirement
from the heat, and the shop doors were dark and
hospitable, and even the horses at the hitching-posts had ceased
to switch their tails with any energy. Our own horse,
being of a deliberate mind and well acquainted with the
road to Ogunquit, took no counsel of us, but went
steadily eastward with the chaise at his heels, while the
dust rose in a pale cloud and settled again upon
the sweet-fern and low blackberry vines.

There were three of us, besides John Marr, who drove,
and who possessed the useful gift of answering a question
only when it deserved an answer. My cousin Mary and
a young visitor from Boston sat upon the back seat;
I sat in front, where I could see the country
open and close before us, and where every turn disclosed
some new alliance of field and woodland, some farm standing
bravely against decay, or some old cellar over which the
young birches had already lifted their victorious green banners.

The road first climbed through a tract of pine woods,
cool and resinous, where the wheels made hardly a sound
upon the brown carpet. The pines stood close together, but
here and there a white birch had found room, and
shone among them like a candle in a dark church.
A hermit thrush called from far within, and the notes,
falling one after another through the stillness, seemed less like
a bird's song than like the recollection of one. We
passed a brook which ran out from under the roots
of an alder, crossed the road in a narrow wooden
trough, and went laughing down toward the intervale, with no
thought of droughts, mortgages, lame men, or sick children. Nature
is sometimes tender enough to console us, but she is
never so tender as to remember our griefs.

At the edge of the woods the country widened suddenly.
There were green pastures climbing the low hills, fields of
young corn in precise, hopeful ranks, and acres of mowing-land
where the red and white clover showed through the grass.
The June wind bent everything one way and then another;
the fields appeared to breathe. Beyond them, at a great
distance, lay a blue line which might have been cloud
but was the sea, ruling all this neighborhood though often
hidden from it, drawing the rivers, flavoring the air, sending
its sons away in ships, and sometimes giving them back
old and silent.

The first farmhouse stood near the road, with two enormous
barns behind it. The barns had a patient, expectant look,
like dull, comfortable creatures waiting for their annual meal of
hay. Swallows flashed about their eaves and teased them, as
small birds will plague cattle in a pasture. A man
and two boys were repairing a stone wall; the man
lifted his head as we passed, but the boys did
not, being engaged in a dispute as to the proper
position of a stone far beyond their strength. Near the
kitchen door a woman, with her skirt pinned up and
a baby set safely in a wooden box beside her,
was washing milk-pans. The sun struck the pans and threw
bright circles upon the wall.

"There's the whole family at work," said Mary.

"Yes," said I; "and I dare say the baby believes
he is overseeing it."

John Marr turned his head a little. "He'll be set
to pickin' stones soon enough," he observed, and this was
all the sympathy the child received from him.

On the next farm the fields were less carefully kept,
though the house had lately been painted. A truckle-cart stood
broken beneath an apple-tree, and a young heifer had pushed
through the fence and was making a guilty feast among
the raspberry bushes. The pasture beyond was filling with juniper
and hardhack. One notices such things unwillingly on a pleasant
day, as one notices the first gray thread in a
friend's hair; but the woods are always at war with
our small civilization, and when a hand is withdrawn for
one generation they advance without mercy. A spruce had grown
in the corner of the door-yard, and two maples were
lifting the stones of the old walk. Yet there were
clean curtains at the windows, and a row of shirts
drying upon the grass; life still held its half of
the ground.

The white roses began a mile or two farther on.
I do not know who first carried the root into
that neighborhood. Perhaps a sailor brought it from some English
garden, wrapped in damp cloth among his sea-chest treasures; perhaps
a bride took it from her mother's door when she
went to the new-cleared farm, where the stumps stood black
among the potatoes and the wolves were heard in winter.
At all events, the rose had traveled from house to
house, and now it belonged to the road. Almost every
old dwelling had its bush, sometimes trained beside the porch
and sometimes grown into a green mound as high as
the lower windows, all powdered with small white blossoms.

They were not stately roses, and no gardener would have
boasted of them. Their petals fell quickly, their stems were
armed with spiteful little thorns, and after a hard rain
the flowers had a bruised and pitiful appearance. Yet no
rose has ever seemed to me more full of human
association. One could imagine how many girls had broken off
those sprays on Sunday mornings, and how many young men
had carried them awkwardly to neighboring doors; how they had
been set in blue bowls at weddings, pinned into the
bosoms of old gowns, laid upon the pillows of dead
children, and planted again beside newer houses by women who
could not bear to leave all of home behind.

We came upon a girl hoeing a garden at the
corner of a poor little place where the roof sagged
and the doorstep had split in two. She could not
have been more than eleven. Her pink calico dress was
too short, and the hoe was so large that she
was obliged to balance herself at every stroke; yet she
attacked the earth with a solemn determination. The garden was
her own, we learned afterward. There were four rows of
beans, a patch of onions, some radishes already going to
seed, and at the farther end three marigolds and a
peony, set out with an ambition beyond the size of
the enclosure.

We stopped to ask what she meant to raise.

"Most everything," she answered, looking at us with clear gray
eyes. "There'll be cucumbers along the fence, if the hens
don't get 'em."

The hens, three yellow miscreants, were at that moment watching
her from beneath a currant bush.

"And are the flowers yours too?" Mary asked.

The child nodded. "I traded Ellen Pike two tomato plants
for that peony. It ain't going to blow this year,
she says, but I guess it will."

We encouraged the peony to the best of our ability
and drove on. I suppose the child saw already, in
those few rods of stubborn ground, a garden full of
abundance: bean-poles feathered to the top, cucumbers hiding under broad
leaves, marigolds like little suns, and the peony opening such
a crimson globe as should astonish Ellen Pike. There was
something touching in her confidence, and something noble too. The
farm might fail, the roof sink lower, the forest steal
up from the neglected pasture; but she had marked off
her kingdom and stood in it with a hoe.

Past the schoolhouse, where the door stood open to the
warm air and a droning geography lesson escaped to us
as we went by, the road began a long ascent.
Columbines grew among the ledges, their red bells swinging above
the gray stone; there were mulleins like tall yellow candles,
and pale meadow rue, and clumps of rudbeckia which an
old neighbor of mine always called "rude-beckies," as if they
were troublesome girls. The ditches were full of whiteweed. Their
multitude gave the roadsides a festive appearance, though the farmers
regarded them with less poetry, and I have known a
man to look upon a field of whiteweed as if
it were a personal insult.

From the crown of the hill we turned into a
narrower way, grass-grown between the wheel tracks. The farmhouse we
had come to see stood beyond a row of ancient
sugar maples. It was a plain, weather-darkened house, facing south,
with a long ell and a great barn that had
lost some shingles from its seaward roof. The fields fell
away on three sides, and from the door one could
see the distant marshes shining and, beyond them, a small
triangular gleam of ocean. There were no near neighbors. In
winter the hill must have seemed to rise into the
very loneliness of the sky.

Silas Ward sat beside the kitchen window in a wheeled
chair. He had been a strong man, broad in the
shoulders and quick of foot, before the war; but a
ball had shattered his hip, and the years since had
drawn and stiffened him until one leg was useless and
the other gave him little service. His face still had
the grave, weathered dignity of the men of his family.
He received us without complaint or apology, as if the
chair were an ordinary possession and not the narrow territory
to which pain had reduced him.

His two grandchildren were with him: Nathan, a quiet boy
of fourteen, and little Ruth, who came from the pantry
with flour on both arms and remained close to her
grandfather. Their father, Silas's only surviving son, had been lost
from a coasting vessel three winters before; their mother had
died in the following spring. Thus the old disabled soldier,
who might reasonably have expected to be cared for by
the younger generation, found himself guardian of what was left
of it.

The kitchen was very neat, though poor. Bunches of sage
hung from the rafters, and there were more drying upon
a newspaper near the stove.

"You seem prepared for a great outbreak of sickness," I
said.

Ruth looked alarmed, but Silas smiled. "Mother always raised sage
enough for the county. We keep on with it."

There are household customs which survive their reasons and become
a kind of fidelity. I remembered Silas's wife, Eunice, gathering
those gray-green leaves in the same door-yard, wearing a brown
apron and a sunbonnet faded almost white. She had been
dead four years, yet nothing in the house seemed entirely
convinced of it. Her blue cup stood on the mantel;
her braided rug lay by the door; the conserve roses
she had planted straggled along the southern wall, half wild
and laden with pale blossoms. Even a stranger would have
missed her in the house.

Eunice had been ill for the greater part of twenty
years. There were seasons when she could go about her
work slowly, resting between the pantry and the table; there
were others when she scarcely left the small bedroom that
opened from the kitchen. She had buried three children. The
first, a little girl, died of scarlet fever; a grown
daughter followed after a lingering decline; and the son who
went to sea was taken last, though the sea did
not return his body for burial. I have sometimes thought
that sorrow, repeated too often, must dull itself, as the
hand grows hardened by labor; but this is not true.
The heart may learn patience, but it does not cease
to feel.

I once visited Eunice during a winter storm when the
hill road was nearly closed. She lay high upon her
pillows, her face thin and clear, and the snow pressed
against the window as if it wished to come in.
The wind shook the house, and Silas, already lame, moved
heavily in the kitchen, preparing supper.

"You have had a hard time," I said, with the
useless boldness of the healthy.

She looked toward the door, where her husband's shadow passed
and repassed.

"I've had good care," she answered.

That was all. She bloomed in my memory afterward like
one of her white roses, not because she had been
untouched by the weather, but because she had opened in
spite of it.

Now Silas spoke of the haying, and of Nathan's wish
to leave school early and work the farm.

"He's a likely boy," he said, "but there's more land
than the three of us can manage. The upper field's
gone back already."

We looked from the window. Young firs showed dark among
the grass, and the stone wall had disappeared in places
beneath blackberry vines.

Nathan colored. "I can clear it."

"Maybe," said his grandfather; and I could not tell whether
pride or pity was strongest in his voice.

The Ward family had cleared that hill more than a
hundred years before. They had hauled stones out of the
stubborn acres, planted orchards, buried children, added rooms, lost money,
gained it, and lost it again. One generation had marched
against the British; another had gone to the western country;
Silas had returned from war with his broken body; and
now a boy stood ready to begin the contest once
more against fir, frost, debt, and solitude. Endurance is not
always triumph. Sometimes it is only the refusal to leave.

Ruth brought us water in a glass pitcher, and a
plate of seed-cakes which she had made herself. There was
a grave pleasure in her manner when we praised them.
Afterward she took Mary to see the chickens, while Nathan
showed John a new scythe. I stayed a little longer
beside Silas. He asked about families in the village and
gave me news of persons whom he saw even less
often than I did. The isolated are sometimes the best
keepers of relationship; every visitor brings them a thread, and
they weave the scattered neighborhood together.

When we said good-by, Ruth broke three white roses from
the bush and gave them to us. One was already
shedding its petals. I saw Silas watching her from the
window, and wondered whether he thought of Eunice's hands among
those branches, or of the years ahead when Ruth might
carry a root away to some house of her own.
One can imagine too much for another, and often falsely;
yet the old man's face had that look which is
less an expression than an opening through which the past
appears.

We regained the main road and went seaward again. The
afternoon had grown richer in color. Shadows lay beneath the
walls; the buttercups shone more distinctly in the fields; every
spruce stood black-green against the sky. We passed a door-yard
filled with peonies, great red, pink, and white heads bending
upon their stems, and presently another where a border of
larkspur ran the length of the house. The women of
that road had little leisure and less money, but they
maintained their gardens with a constancy which put more fortunate
persons to shame. A patch of flowers was often the
only possession that answered no practical demand. It fed no
one, clothed no one, paid no tax; therefore it testified
the more clearly to some hunger of the spirit.

The larkspur reminded me of a woman I had known
years before in Virginia. Her husband kept a poor hill
farm where the earth was red and thin, and where
all summer the heat lay upon the fields like a
weight. She cooked for eight persons, milked, spun, tended poultry,
and helped with the tobacco; yet beside the cabin she
had made a garden so crowded that one walked through
it sidewise. There were hollyhocks against the chimney, pinks under
the windows, southernwood, sweet-william, roses, and a wilderness of vines
climbing strings to the eaves.

"I couldn't stay here without 'em," she told me.

She had come from a greener county, and I suppose
each plant was an embassy from the home she had
lost. A woman may be transplanted farther than any rose
and expected to take root without complaint. That poor Virginia
garden, burning under a harsher sun, seemed suddenly near as
we passed the cool New England dooryards; the two belonged
to the same invisible country, whose citizens are all those
who save a corner for beauty after labor has claimed
the rest.

Near the coast road stood a low, square house said
to have sheltered one of the regicides for a night,
though another town confidently claims the same honor and provides
a better attic for the purpose. A mile beyond was
the cellar of the first meetinghouse, and farther down, where
the river widened, one could still see the blackened timbers
of an old shipyard at low tide. History in such
places does not proceed in an orderly fashion. It grows
like the roadside, where an ancient apple-tree, a new telegraph
pole, goldenrod, and a broken sleigh may all occupy the
same prospect.

We had intended to go as far as the Ogunquit
bridge, but the sun was lowering, and John warned us
that the horse had views about supper. We turned homeward
by the river road. For a time we were silent,
content with the steady beat of hoofs and the light
creaking of the chaise. The white roses Ruth had given
us lay across Mary's lap. Their fragrance mingled with the
smell of clover, warm dust, and distant salt marsh.

At a fork in the road we met three wagons
coming toward us, their occupants dressed in black. The men's
collars looked stiff and uncomfortable in the heat, and the
women held folded handkerchiefs and little bunches of flowers. Even
before John drew aside, we knew that they were returning
from a funeral. In the first wagon sat an old
woman upright as a figure carved from dark wood. Her
bonnet framed a face of extraordinary composure. She had buried
her eldest son that afternoon, but there was in her
bearing no appeal to the world and no surrender to
it—only a primitive majesty which made us lower our voices
after she had passed.

In the last wagon was our acquaintance Mrs. Pike, who
recognized us and leaned out while her husband checked the
horse.

"You ain't heard?" she called. "Ezra Cole died Tuesday—pneumonia, took
sudden—and they don't expect Mis' Talbot to last the night.
Her daughter's comin' acrost from Berwick if they can fetch
her. And Joe Little's boy got hurt at the quarry;
a chip stove through his hat and cut him dreadful,
though they say it missed the bone. There's been sights
of trouble."

She drew breath, looked at our roses, and added, "Pleasant
day, though."

It was indeed a pleasant day, and this seemed at
first a poor answer to death; yet when the wagons
had gone on and their wheels ceased to sound, the
beauty around us kept grief from settling with its full
weight. A bobolink poured out his absurd, bubbling music over
the meadow. The river flashed between the alders. High clouds
sailed in a sky of such serene blue that the
old words of consolation, so often worn smooth by use,
appeared to have been written there again.

I felt ashamed that we could not be wholly sad.
But perhaps the earth does not ask this of us.
The old mother had gone home from her son's grave
under arching maples; the mourners had smelled clover and heard
the cattle bells; and these things, though they could not
lessen loss, might prevent sorrow from believing itself the only
truth.

Below the next hill John turned into a narrow lane
which followed the river. It was hardly more than two
ruts, with grass between and alders brushing both wheels. This,
he told our visitor, was part of the old trail
used before any English road had crossed the country. The
native people had followed the river inland from the sea,
carrying fish and shell beads, and had passed beneath pines
whose descendants now stood upon the farther bank. Some portions
of the trail had been widened for ox-carts; others were
lost under ploughed fields or village streets; but here the
ground still kept the old direction.

It is difficult to travel such a path without feeling
the presumption of our ownership. We give the river a
new name, divide its banks, write deeds, build mills, and
speak of improvement, while beneath our fences lies the road
of those who knew the turns of the water before
our oldest cellar was dug. The pine woods seemed to
me that evening to retain a more rightful tenancy than
ours. Their shadows reached across the river, and the wind
spoke in them with a grave, continuous sound.

Once, old men said, salmon had come up this river,
and shad in such numbers that the spring catch supplied
every farm. There were alewives too, and great baskets of
smelts. The fish entered the town accounts, the tavern talk,
and the winter stores; they belonged as much to the
place as its hayfields. Now a dam blocked one branch,
factory refuse fouled another, and a tannery upstream poured out
a stain which colored the water a sick golden-brown. We
stopped where a small brook joined the river. The brook
came clear over its stones, but below the meeting the
current carried streaks of scum among the reeds.

John said there had been dead fish along the bank
the week before.

"Did anyone complain?" Mary asked.

"Oh, they complained," he replied. "Folks have complained twenty year."

There are abuses so old that people begin to respect
them as institutions. The mill employed men; the tannery paid
taxes; the dam was private property; the river, which belonged
to everyone and to no one, was therefore expected to
bear the injury. Laws had been made with large intentions
and small penalties. An inspector came, looked solemnly at the
water, wrote something in a book, and went away. The
next rain carried the stain downstream, and the next season
fewer fish returned.

I confess that my pleasure in the journey deserted me
for a while. It seemed intolerable that a creature following
the ancient command of its life from sea to inland
gravel should be stopped by our carelessness or poisoned for
our convenience. We had cut the forests, exhausted farms, left
cellar-holes where households had been, and now we were spoiling
the streams, as if the future were an enemy to
whom we meant to leave no provisions.

The river itself gave no answer. It moved past us,
brown beneath the alders and bright where it took the
sky, carrying leaves, foam, and the shadows of swallows. At
a bend above the mill it looked almost uninjured. Evening
had laid a silver track upon it, and a kingfisher
flew from one dead branch to another with his rattling
cry. Beauty can sometimes appear indifferent to wrong; at other
times it seems to offer an image of what may
yet be restored.

We came out of the lane near a broad intervale
where cows were moving homeward. Their bells sounded irregularly, one
clear and near, another faint beyond the willows. A boy
followed them barefoot, switching a branch against the burdocks. He
called to John that one of the bridge planks was
loose, and then ran after a red cow which had
mistaken the road. The ordinary duties of the world had
resumed their command. Gates must be shut, cows milked, children
fed, and gardens guarded from hens, whether rivers were saved
or ruined.

The light faded slowly. A violet haze gathered over the
lowlands, while the tops of the elms still held the
sun. From one farmhouse came the sound of dishes being
set upon a table; from another, the smell of wood
smoke and frying salt pork. A woman stood at a
pump filling a pail, and two small boys were making
a fort of hay under the barn eaves. On a
doorstep an old man mended a harness, drawing the waxed
thread through with gestures so familiar that they seemed inherited.

We passed again into the white rose country. The blossoms
shone after other colors had begun to disappear, pale along
the walls and porches, carrying the eye from one old
house to the next. At a deserted farm the bush
had overrun the doorway. No hand had pruned it for
years; the roof had fallen at the back, the chimney
leaned, and grass grew in the window ledges, yet the
rose flowered with reckless generosity. It had outlived the woman
who planted it, the children who played beside it, and
perhaps even the memory of their names among the neighbors.
Still, it was not meaningless. It marked the place of
affection.

I thought of Ruth upon the hill and of her
grandmother; of the little gardener balancing behind her hoe; of
the Virginian woman carrying water to her crowded flowers after
a day in the tobacco field; of the old mother
riding home from the cemetery; of all those who had
planted what they might not live to see mature. The
road seemed whitened not merely by roses but by remembrances,
each blossom gathering about itself a courtship, an illness, a
departure, or some common morning when a woman opened her
door and found the bush in flower.

Our Boston visitor, who had been quiet for some time,
asked whether country life was always so sorrowful.

"No," I said; "but one sees its sorrows because there
is room around them."

In towns, grief passes behind a wall or disappears into
a crowd. Here the closed house, the neglected field, the
empty place in a wagon remain visible. Yet happiness is
visible too: a child's garden, a well-filled barn, a clean
kitchen, cattle coming home, neighbors who remember the names and
fortunes of families for miles around. Neither condition is hidden
long.

John Marr, after considering my answer, said, "Country folks have
good times. They don't always know enough to mention 'em."

We laughed, and the sound startled a night bird from
the ditch.

By the time we reached the first village houses, lamps
had begun to shine in the windows. The shops were
closed, and the elms made dark arches overhead. Our horse
quickened of his own accord, having borne philosophy as long
as he thought reasonable and now being certain of his
supper. Behind us the western sky remained bright above the
road we had traveled; before us the church steeple rose
in the dusk, with one early star beside it.

My anger over the river had not vanished, but it
had altered. It is easy, when confronted by old damage,
to imagine that folly is permanent and greed the only
durable human force. Yet the farms themselves had been won
from wilderness by people who believed in a future they
would not see; the white roses had been carried and
replanted; children still hoed gardens on poor ground; and even
complaints repeated for twenty years were proof that conscience had
not been wholly silenced. Perhaps we shall learn, though late,
that a river is more than power for a wheel,
that a forest is more than uncut boards, and that
the fish returning through dark sea-water possess a claim older
than our ledgers. Wisdom also may be inherited, if someone
takes pains to plant it.

I have forgotten many drives whose miles were longer and
whose occasions seemed more important, but that June afternoon remains
entire. I can still see the velvet turf along the
hill road, the dull-blue sage drying in Silas Ward's kitchen,
the peonies bending heavily after the sun, and the white
rose petals scattered across Mary's dress. I hear the brook
laughing under the pines, the funeral wheels grinding in the
dust, the irregular cattle bells, and the old river moving
beside its nearly vanished trail.

Pleasure and sorrow traveled with us, neither overcoming the other.
The day was beautiful, but not innocent; the country was
beloved, but not secure. Every farm bore the marks of
labor and abandonment, every garden declared both hope and mortality,
and the roses themselves were white alike for brides and
for the dead. Yet because they flowered still—at prosperous doors,
beside lonely windows, and over the thresholds of ruined houses—the
road did not end in grief.

In memory I follow it even now, climbing from the
river toward the blue hills, passing one pale bush after
another beneath the evening sky.

### WILLIAM AT THE PICTURES

William's aunt gave him a shilling.

She did it at half-past ten on Saturday morning, when
hope was still fresh and the day had not yet
been spoiled by adults arranging it. She pressed the coin
into his hand with the air of one rewarding virtue.

"There, William," she said. "Spend it wisely."

William promised that he would.

His conception of wisdom differed in certain details from his
aunt's, but this was hardly his fault. Adults spoke of
spending wisely without ever defining the term. William, after grave
consideration, decided that wisdom consisted in obtaining the greatest possible
quantity of pleasure for the least possible quantity of money.

The cinema cost sixpence. That left sixpence for sweets.

The arrangement was perfect.

There were shops in the village where sixpence bought chocolates
in a box adorned with a pink ribbon, and other
shops where it bought eleven rather sad-looking caramels in a
paper bag. William passed these without weakening. He went instead
to a small, dark establishment whose window displayed bootlaces, postcards,
paraffin, marbles, string, flypapers and sweets of a violent green
colour. The proprietor had once objected when William counted the
currants in a penny bun before buying it, but William
had since forgiven him. Sixpence was too important to be
influenced by private resentment.

"How many of those green ones d'you get for sixpence?"
he demanded.

The proprietor looked at him without enthusiasm.

"A quarter of a pound."

William leaned over the counter.

"They've been there a long time, haven't they?"

"They have not."

"They look as if they have."

"Well, they haven't."

"P'raps they're a bit stale."

"They're perfectly fresh."

William sighed. Negotiations with unreasonable tradesmen were always exhausting.

"If they were stale," he said patiently, "you'd give me
more, wouldn't you?"

"No."

"Not even one more?"

"No."

William examined the sweets. They were shaped like small cushions
and covered with a fine white powder. Their greenness was
not the greenness of grass or leaves, but a more
ambitious greenness, suggesting that Nature had made an effort and
failed.

"I'll have sixpenn'orth," he said at last, with the air
of a financier concluding a doubtful but extensive transaction.

The bag made a gratifying bulge in his pocket. He
ate three sweets on the way to the cinema and
found them satisfactory. They adhered firmly to the teeth and
possessed a flavour which, though not immediately recognisable, was powerful.
William considered that delicacy of flavour was greatly overrated. What
one wanted from a sweet was evidence that one had
eaten it.

The afternoon performance had already begun when he entered. He
crept down the darkened aisle, tripped over two pairs of
feet, sat on a hat, apologised to the wrong person
and finally settled himself in the front row.

On the screen a criminal was escaping from justice.

William recognised him as a criminal at once because he
wore a cloth cap pulled over his eyes, kept looking
behind him and carried a black bag which he ostentatiously
concealed beneath his coat. These precautions were guaranteed to attract
attention in any properly regulated street. A policeman appeared. The
criminal ran. The policeman ran. Three more policemen joined them,
followed by a man with a basket, a woman with
a perambulator and a dog. The criminal climbed walls, fell
through roofs, hid in barrels and emerged from chimneys. He
had only to walk quietly into a shop and buy
a collar to escape detection, but this did not occur
to him. He preferred methods requiring courage, noise and a
complete disregard for property.

William watched, spellbound.

Next came a romance of country life.

A simple country maiden lived with her old father in
a cottage with roses round the door. She spent much
of her time carrying a milk-pail which was always empty,
possibly because a full one would have interfered with the
more important business of emotion. A simple country son of
the soil loved her. He wore gaiters, leaned upon gates
and spoke in a husky voice. Unfortunately, a dark stranger
also loved her. He could be identified as a villain
by his moustache, which turned upwards at the ends and
appeared to have independent designs upon the simple country maiden.

The maiden loved the simple country son of the soil
but believed him faithless because she had seen him shaking
hands with another woman. The son of the soil loved
the maiden but believed her faithless because he had seen
her accepting a letter from the postman. They therefore spent
several reels avoiding each other behind haystacks. At last the
dark stranger attempted to carry the maiden away in a
motor-car. The son of the soil pursued on horseback, rescued
her, injured his forehead slightly and married her.

William approved of the pursuit, though he thought the misunderstanding
unnecessarily prolonged. He himself would have asked the postman what
was in the letter.

There followed a comedy about a painter.

The painter had been ordered to paint a door. Instead,
he painted his employer, the employer's wife, a policeman, a
cat and most of the street. At one point he
removed old paint by holding a flame beneath it and
scraping it off with a knife. This process particularly interested
William. It was simple, scientific and produced a pleasing amount
of smoke.

The final film was a drama against strong drink.

Its principal character was a drunkard with a white face
and black hair. He had a small child whose duty
it was to forgive him. The drunkard spent the housekeeping
money, pawned the furniture, struck his employer, burned down his
home and fell off a bridge. After each catastrophe the
child clasped his hands and said, "Father, I forgive you."

The child's health was not good. He coughed whenever anyone
entered the room and lay upon a narrow bed beneath
a patchwork quilt. His forgiveness appeared to exhaust him so
much that he repeatedly sank back upon the pillow with
closed eyes, though he always recovered in time for the
next offence.

At the end the drunkard knelt by the bed and
promised to reform. The child raised one thin hand, smiled
faintly and forgave him for the last time. The drunkard
then became prosperous with remarkable speed, while the child, whose
chief occupation was no longer required, died.

The lights went up.

William remained seated for a moment, blinking and thoughtful. He
had passed through crime, love, art, intoxication, repentance and death
in less than three hours. Ordinary village life seemed singularly
empty by comparison.

Outside, the late afternoon was bright and peaceful. No criminals
fled over the roofs. No maidens were being carried away.
No fathers reeled from public houses to receive the forgiveness
of dying children. Mr Bott's dog slept in the road.

William put a green sweet into his mouth and started
home.

At the corner by the church he remembered the criminal
film.

At once the street altered. The hedge became the wall
of a prison, the vicarage a den of spies and
the elderly lady approaching with a basket a detective in
disguise. William drew his cap over his eyes, thrust the
bag of sweets inside his jersey and glanced furtively behind
him.

The elderly lady moved aside.

William increased his speed.

He could hear the police gaining upon him. They were
silent police, which made them more dangerous. One was creeping
along the ditch. Another had climbed the telegraph pole. A
third was disguised as Mr Bott's dog.

William broke into a run.

He dashed through his own gate, sprang across the lawn,
rounded a laurel bush and collided with his father.

Mr Brown had been enjoying ten minutes of quiet before
dinner. He was seated in a deck-chair with the newspaper
spread before him and a cup of tea balanced upon
one knee. William's arrival sent the tea over his waistcoat,
the newspaper over his head and the deck-chair backwards into
a flower-bed.

There was a brief interval during which Mr Brown's legs,
the newspaper and the deck-chair struggled for supremacy. Then his
face appeared.

"William!"

William looked round quickly. The imaginary police had vanished at
the first sign of trouble, as imaginary police often did.

"Yes, Father?"

"What do you mean by it?"

"I was only comin' home."

"Coming home? Coming home? Do you generally come home like
a battering-ram?"

"I was being chased."

"Chased? Who was chasing you?"

William glanced towards the gate. It was empty.

"Some men," he said.

"What men?"

"I don't know their names."

Mr Brown rose. Tea dripped from his waistcoat. Earth adhered
to his trousers. His expression suggested that William's ignorance of
the men's names was the least unsatisfactory feature of the
affair.

"What's that in your jersey?"

"Nothin'."

"It's not nothing. Give it to me."

William reluctantly produced the bag. Mr Brown seized it.

"Sweets! Of course! Stuffing yourself with this poisonous rubbish and
charging about like a lunatic! Here!"

He flung the bag over the hedge into the kitchen
garden.

William watched its flight with horror.

"You needn't've done that," he said.

"You needn't have knocked me into the flower-bed!"

"I didn't knock you. I jus' happened to be runnin'
where you happened to be sittin'."

"Go indoors!"

William went round the house instead.

The bag had burst upon landing. Green sweets lay among
the cabbages, on the path and in a bed recently
dressed with soot. William collected them with anxious devotion. A
smaller-minded person might have rejected those which had fallen into
the soil. William merely rubbed them upon his sleeve. By
the end of the operation he had consumed sweets and
garden in approximately equal quantities.

He had just secured the last when Joan appeared at
the fence next door.

Joan was eight and possessed the valuable quality of believing
most things until someone older contradicted them.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

William straightened slowly.

The romance returned to him. He was no longer a
boy kneeling among cabbages with a torn bag. He was
a simple country son of the soil, weather-beaten by sorrow,
silent beneath injustice and probably leaning upon a gate. As
no gate stood conveniently near, he leaned upon a rake.

"Nothin'," he said huskily.

Joan stared.

"What's the matter with your voice?"

William gazed into the distance. The simple country son of
the soil had always gazed into the distance before speaking.
It gave significance to even the most ordinary remark.

"It's always like this," he said in a deep whisper.

"No, it isn't."

"It is now."

"Have you got a cold?"

William considered the question. A cold was not romantic. Weak
lungs were romantic. He remembered the drunkard's child, coughing delicately
upon the patchwork quilt.

"It's my lungs," he said.

Joan's eyes widened.

"What's wrong with them?"

"They're weak."

"Are you going to die?"

William had not intended to go as far as death,
but Joan's awed expression encouraged him.

"P'raps," he said. "The doctor says I've gotter be careful."

"What doctor?"

"A special one."

Joan came nearer to the fence.

"You don't look ill."

"People with weak lungs don't," said William. "Not till the
end."

He coughed. His first attempt sounded more like a bark,
but the second was softer and more affecting.

Joan was impressed.

"You ought to go to bed."

"I can't. I've got things to do first."

"What things?"

William had not prepared the details.

"Important things," he said.

He drew out the remains of the bag.

"Like to have a sweet?"

Joan hesitated.

"They're dirty."

"They're not dirty. That's medicine powder on them."

"They've got earth on them."

"Earth's good for you. Potatoes grow in it."

The argument was unanswerable. Joan accepted one.

The sweet required energetic attention. When she had subdued it
she accepted another, then another. William, taking his duties as
host seriously, selected those least heavily coated with soot. Their
flavour had broadened since the purchase and now included cabbage,
leather and the kitchen garden generally.

Joan ate five.

William ate seven.

Then Joan said that she felt funny.

"That's because you're not used to medicine powder," said William.

"I think I'm going to be sick."

"Don't be silly. People in pictures never are."

Joan turned pale and went home hurriedly.

William remained by the rake, coughing from time to time
and staring across the cabbages. There was no simple country
maiden present to overhear him, and the effect was therefore
wasted. After a few minutes he abandoned the role.

At dinner that evening Mrs Brown said, "William, Mrs Clive
has just telephoned."

William continued to eat.

"She says Joan has been very sick."

"Has she?" said William politely.

"She says you gave her some sweets covered with dirt."

"They weren't covered with dirt."

"What were they covered with?"

"Only bits of it."

Mr Brown lowered his fork.

"Are these the sweets I threw away?"

"I didn't throw them away," said William. "You did."

"Did you pick them up?"

"Well, I wasn't goin' to waste them."

"And then you gave them to that child?"

"I gave her the cleanest ones."

Mr Brown closed his eyes. He had recovered from the
tea, but not from the memory of it.

Mrs Brown said mildly, "Mrs Clive also says that Joan
is terribly upset because you told her you were dying
of weak lungs."

Ethel looked up. Ethel was William's elder sister and had
reached an age at which William's mere presence seemed to
her an offence against civilisation.

"Dying?" she said. "What nonsense!"

"I didn't say I was dying," said William. "I only
said p'raps I was."

"Why?"

"She asked me."

"You made your voice sound ill," said Mrs Brown.

"I can't help my voice."

"Your voice sounds perfectly normal now."

"It comes and goes."

Mr Brown opened his eyes.

"If it doesn't go," he said, "you will."

William judged it prudent to finish dinner in silence.

Afterwards he passed the drawing-room and heard Ethel speaking to
Jack Morgan near the open window. Jack Morgan was a
young man who came to the house rather often, though
never, so far as William could see, for any useful
purpose. He played tennis badly, drank tea slowly and spent
a great deal of time telling Ethel things that did
not appear to interest either of them.

"I suppose," Ethel was saying, "you'll be seeing quite a
lot of Margaret at the Harrisons'."

"Possibly," said Jack.

"Of course, it's nothing to me."

"No," said Jack. "And I suppose you'll be seeing young
Fenton?"

"Perhaps."

"Of course, it's nothing to me."

"No."

There was a silence of immense emotional depth.

William stopped beneath the window.

Here, unmistakably, were the lovers from the picture. They loved
each other, but pride held them apart. Ethel was the
simple country maiden, though the description required some generosity, and
Jack was the simple country son of the soil, though
he wore no gaiters and worked in a bank. Margaret
and young Fenton were obstacles placed in their path by
Fate. Unless someone intervened, Ethel would carry empty milk-pails about
for several reels and Jack would lean bitterly upon gates.

William entered the room.

"Go away, William," said Ethel.

He studied them compassionately.

"You oughtn't to let pride come between you," he said.

Ethel flushed.

"What on earth do you mean?"

"Nothin'," said William, with tact, and withdrew.

The matter occupied him deeply. In the film, the lovers
had waited until the villain's motor-car forced a decision. William
saw no reason to wait. He possessed information, courage and
most of the remaining evening.

He went into the garden and looked for a flower
suitable for conveying Ethel's emotions. The roses were nearly over.
He found a small pink bud, slightly damaged by rain,
and pulled it from the bush. On the way to
Jack's house he carried it in his pocket with a
penknife, two marbles, string, a piece of chalk and half
a biscuit. By the time he arrived, it had lost
its petals and acquired a coating of biscuit crumbs. Nevertheless,
its symbolism remained.

Jack himself opened the door.

"Hullo, William," he said cautiously.

"I've brought you a message from Ethel."

Jack's caution increased.

"What message?"

William held out the crushed rosebud.

"She sent you this."

Jack took it.

"Did she?"

"Yes. She picked it with tears in her eyes."

Jack examined the object, perhaps in search of the tears.

"What did she say?"

"She said, 'Take this to him, William, and tell him
I'm wastin' away.'"

"Did she really?"

"Yes," said William. "She can't eat."

Jack looked surprised.

"She ate a fairly substantial tea."

"She was pretendin'. She does that so people won't know."

"I see."

"She walks about all night callin' your name."

"Does she?"

"Not loud," William added. "You wouldn't hear her from here."

Jack's mouth twitched, but William was too occupied with tragedy
to notice.

"She wants to meet you secret," he continued. "At seven."

"Where?"

"At our house."

"That isn't especially secret."

"It is if you don't tell anyone."

"What am I to say when I arrive?"

"Say you've come to see Father. Then when no one's
lookin' you can tell Ethel you forgive her."

"For what?"

William frowned. Jack was taking a narrow view.

"For Margaret and young Fenton and pride an' all that."

"Ah," said Jack. "Naturally."

"You'll come?"

"I shouldn't miss it."

William returned home with the satisfaction of one who has
reunited two lives. He did not mention his success. Great
benefactors are often silent.

At seven o'clock the Brown family was at dinner.

Mr Brown had reached a condition of comparative peace. Joan's
illness had been reported as temporary, William had said nothing
for nearly an hour, and the soup was hot. Then
the bell rang.

The maid entered.

"Mr Morgan, sir."

Ethel dropped her spoon.

Mr Brown stared.

"At this hour?"

Jack appeared in the doorway, carrying the remains of the
rosebud.

"I'm terribly sorry," he began. "I hope I'm not interrupting."

"You are," said Mr Brown.

Mrs Brown intervened.

"Do come in, Jack. Is anything the matter?"

Jack looked at Ethel. Ethel looked at the rose. The
rose, though much the worse for experience, pointed unmistakably towards
William.

"I received a message," said Jack.

"What message?" demanded Ethel.

Jack held out the flower.

"This."

Ethel's face became scarlet.

William watched with pleased expectancy. In the picture, this was
the moment when the maiden placed both hands upon her
heart.

"I never sent that!" said Ethel.

"You did," said William helpfully. "You picked it with tears
in your eyes."

"I did nothing of the kind!"

"You said you were wastin' away."

"William!"

"You said you couldn't eat."

Ethel turned to Jack.

"I haven't the faintest idea what he means."

Jack's expression was courteous but unsteady.

"He also mentioned that you walk about at night calling
my name."

Mr Brown rose slowly.

"William," he said, "have you been interfering again?"

"I haven't interfered. I was helpin'."

"Helping whom?"

"Them. They're in love with each other, but they're too
proud to say so."

Silence.

It was not an ordinary silence. It was a silence
composed of Mr Brown's fury, Mrs Brown's embarrassment, Ethel's mortification,
Jack's suppressed amusement and William's dawning suspicion that romantic assistance
was not appreciated in private life.

Ethel spoke first.

"I hate you!"

"That's what the girl said in the picture," William replied
eagerly. "But she didn't really. She—"

"Be quiet!" roared Mr Brown.

Jack placed the rosebud carefully beside his plate.

"Perhaps," he said, "I had better go."

"Yes," said Ethel.

"No," said Mrs Brown at the same moment. "Do stay
and have some dinner."

"I think perhaps—another time."

He left amid polite phrases from Mrs Brown, an icy
stare from Ethel and a sound from Mr Brown resembling
the preliminary disturbance of a volcano.

William was sent upstairs.

He went unwillingly. His behaviour, he felt, had been systematically
misconstrued. He had rescued invisible pursuers from the trouble of
catching him, recovered valuable property, shared it generously with Joan,
faced death without complaint and united two lovers. For these
achievements he had been shouted at, accused and deprived of
pudding.

His room offered no comfort. It contained no secret passage,
no rustic maiden and no patchwork quilt. The door, however,
was painted.

William remembered the painter.

Here at last was an occupation beyond emotional misunderstanding. It
was practical, scientific and useful. The paint on his door
was chipped near the handle. He would remove the old
paint and perhaps, if time allowed, repaint it. His father
could hardly object to an improvement of property.

William found a candle and his penknife. He lit the
candle, held it near the lower panel and scraped. Nothing
happened. He moved the flame closer. The paint softened, blistered
and turned brown. A gratifying smell filled the room.

He scraped harder.

Long curls came away. Beneath them appeared bare wood, blackened
in places but undeniably clean. Encouraged, William extended the operation.
The painter in the film had worked quickly, with broad
gestures and no concern for straight lines. William did the
same.

Soon the lower half of the door presented a varied
surface of scorched wood, hanging strips, knife marks and islands
of untouched paint. Smoke drifted into the passage.

Mr Brown came upstairs.

He opened the door, saw William kneeling with candle and
knife, and remained perfectly still.

This stillness was more alarming than speech.

"What," he said at last, "are you doing?"

"Strippin' paint."

"Stripping paint."

"Yes. It's a new way. You use fire."

Mr Brown looked at the door.

"You've burned it."

"I haven't burned all of it."

"You've ruined it!"

"I was improvin' it."

"With a candle?"

"The man in the picture did."

Mr Brown seized the candle and blew it out.

"Pictures! Is that what's been the matter with you? Charging
into me, poisoning the child next door, telling people you're
dying, making a fool of Ethel, dragging Morgan here in
the middle of dinner, and now setting fire to the
house!"

"I didn't set fire to the house."

"You were trying to!"

"I wasn't. I was only takin' the paint off."

"You are insane," said Mr Brown. "Completely insane. No sane
human being could produce this amount of destruction in one
day."

William stood with injured dignity.

"I was only tryin' to help."

"Help! Help! You nearly break my neck and call it
help. You feed filthy sweets to Joan and call it
help. You meddle in your sister's affairs and call it
help. You destroy a door and call it help. I
suppose if you blew up the house you'd expect me
to thank you!"

"No," said William reasonably. "Not if you were in it."

Mr Brown made a brief but impressive speech upon obedience,
truthfulness, property, fire, sweets, sisters, neighbours, cinemas, insanity and the
probable future of boys who persisted in behaving like William.
He then imposed a punishment of such magnitude that William
ceased to follow its details after the first fortnight.

When Mr Brown had gone, William sat on the bed.

His life was tragic.

He had been misunderstood by everyone. Even the drunkard's child
had not suffered so persistently, though admittedly he had possessed
a more satisfactory cough. William recalled the child's pale face,
thin hand and inexhaustible forgiveness. At once his own course
became clear. He would forgive his father. Not angrily or
ostentatiously, but quietly, as one standing upon the edge of
another world.

He descended the stairs.

Mr Brown was in the hall, telling Mrs Brown about
the door.

"Burned it!" he was saying. "Actually burned it! If I
hadn't gone up, the whole place might have been in
flames. The boy's a menace. A public menace!"

William appeared on the bottom step. He leaned against the
banister and coughed faintly.

No one noticed.

He coughed again.

Mr Brown turned.

"What do you want?"

William raised one hand. He had intended it to tremble,
but the effort made it wave instead.

"Father," he said huskily, "I forgive you."

Mr Brown stared.

"You what?"

"I forgive you for all you've done to me."

"What I've done to you!"

"When I'm gone," continued William, "you'll remember my last words.
You'll come to my little bed an' say, 'William, can
you forgive me?' An' I'll smile faintly an' say—"

Mr Brown advanced.

The drunkard in the film had fallen upon his knees
at this point. Mr Brown showed no intention of doing
so. His movements suggested an entirely different ending.

William's dramatic instinct yielded promptly to his practical one.

He sprang through the open front door, raced down the
path and disappeared into the gathering dusk before forgiveness could
be completed.

Behind him came his father's voice.

Ahead lay the road, the hedge, the darkening fields and
all the inexhaustible possibilities of another pursuit.

William drew his cap over his eyes and ran.

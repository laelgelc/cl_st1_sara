## The Latitude of Literature

When the *Southern Zodiac* was incorporated at Chalybeate Junction, Georgia,
its prospectus announced that it would preserve the intellectual sovereignty
of the South, encourage the flowering of native genius, defend
the purity of Anglo-Saxon ideals, and accept advertisements for agricultural
implements at twenty cents a line.

The last object was printed in smaller type.

The magazine occupied two rooms above the First State Bank
and Fertilizer Company. From its eastern windows could be seen
the courthouse, a red cotton warehouse, and the railway track
bending northward with an appearance of treachery. From the western
windows one looked upon Cedar Heights, the ancestral estate of
Colonel Aquila Telfair, editor, scholar, veteran, and custodian of several
traditions that had survived chiefly because nobody had lately disturbed
them.

The colonel was sixty-four, erect, silver-haired, and dressed in black
broadcloth regardless of temperature. He wore a low collar, a
black string tie, and a watch chain that crossed his
waistcoat with the solemnity of a treaty. His face was
narrow and patrician; his beard had retired from the field
except for a white imperial under the lower lip. He
bowed to ladies, removed his hat before monuments, and never
referred to the late sectional disturbance by any name that
might imply its conclusion.

At Cedar Heights stood a library of ten thousand volumes
inherited from three generations of Telfairs. This sounds impressive until
you learn that eight thousand were sermons, agricultural reports, county
histories, and incomplete sets of Sir Walter Scott. The colonel
had read them all except the agricultural reports. The condition
of Cedar Heights demonstrated the omission. Its roof sagged, its
cornfields had become red gullies, and its chief crop was
family recollection.

Colonel Telfair had been chosen editor of the *Southern Zodiac*
because his qualifications were undeniable. His grandfather had entertained Henry
Clay for dinner; his father had been wounded at Chickamauga;
and the colonel himself, at the age of nine, had
carried a dispatch three hundred yards through a rainstorm while
wearing a gray cap and suffering from measles. Furthermore, he
knew what Tasso had said about glory, what Burke had
said about conciliation, and what every Telfair since 1739 had
said about every other family in the state.

The directors could hardly have found a more literary man
without going outside Georgia, which the charter forbade.

The charter provided that the magazine should publish "the work,
thought, aspiration, history, philosophy, poetry, anecdote, moral instruction, agricultural reminiscence,
and refined humour of writers born in, descended from, married
into, or spiritually sympathetic with the Southern States."

The phrase "spiritually sympathetic" had been inserted to accommodate a
contributor in Ohio whose mother's second cousin had once owned
a turpentine still near Savannah.

The editorial staff was assembled with equal care.

Mrs. Araminta Polk Withers held the department of poetry. Her
husband's uncle had been a brigadier, and her own great-aunt
had danced with John C. Calhoun, though whether he knew
it was a dance had become uncertain with the passage
of time. Mrs. Withers wrote sonnets of nineteen lines, explaining
that the South had never submitted to Italian rules of
composition.

Professor Lucius Quintard Rusk reviewed books. He had been professor
of rhetoric at Ptolemy Female College until that institution was
converted into a roller-skating pavilion. Professor Rusk possessed a fine
command of semicolons and a letter from Jefferson Davis's former
dentist.

Major Pemberton G. Scroope conducted "Memories of the Field." He
had entered the Confederate service at the age of six
weeks when his mother carried him through a camp near
Macon. In later years the major described himself as the
youngest combatant of the war. A portrait in the office
showed him with a cavalry sword in one hand and
an infant's feeding bottle in the other. No enemy had
ever disputed the claim.

Miss Sophronia Vane edited "The Confederate Hearthstone." Her principal credential
was an aunt who, according to family testimony, had once
been kissed by Stonewall Jackson at a church supper. The
aunt had not specified the location of the kiss, and
Miss Vane's dignified silence preserved its military value.

There was also Mr. Absalom Dade, office editor and genealogical
examiner, whose task was to inspect manuscripts for errors of
ancestry. Mr. Dade could trace any Southern family back to
a baron, a bishop, or a man who had signed
something. He had only one eye, but with it he
detected Northern grandparents at a distance of three generations.

With this organization the *Southern Zodiac* began publication.

Its first number contained an address on "The Influence of
Chivalric Institutions upon the Cultivation of Sorghum," a forty-minute speech
delivered at the unveiling of a monument to an unknown
picket, twenty-seven pages of blank verse concerning the Battle of
Shiloh, and a serial romance entitled *The Lily of Lost
Cause Manor*. There were also six poems to mockingbirds, four
to magnolias, three to swords, two to graves, and one
to a mockingbird singing in a magnolia over a grave
upon which lay a sword.

The circulation reached 611.

The second number printed a continuation of the sorghum article,
additional verses on Shiloh, and a scholarly inquiry into whether
General Lee had taken one lump of sugar or two
when visiting the author's grandmother in 1858. The conclusion was
reserved for the next issue.

Circulation advanced to 643, including complimentary copies.

After one year, the directors called a meeting. Even Georgia
stockholders occasionally discover that departed glory will not pay a
living printer.

Colonel Telfair appeared before them carrying a leather portfolio and
the confidence of a man supported by ten thousand unread
agricultural reports. He attributed the magazine's financial condition to "the
temporary enfeeblement of public taste under the corrosive pressure of
commercial sensationalism."

The treasurer attributed it to $4,800 in unpaid printing bills.

The colonel pointed out that the *Zodiac* had won honourable
mention from the Ladies' Historical Circle of Little Mulberry County.
The treasurer replied that the printer did not accept honourable
mention at the bank.

It was then that Providence, which sometimes travels under a
derby hat, sent Mr. T. T. Thacker from New York.

Thacker promoted magazines. He had promoted a monthly devoted to
vegetarian athletics, a weekly for amateur detectives, and a trade
journal called *The Undertaker's Smile*. He had raised the circulation
of the last by offering a free accident-insurance policy with
every annual subscription.

Physically he resembled a well-fed terrier that had acquired banking
connections. He was short, alert, glossy, and equipped with eyes
that counted everything in the room, including the buttons on
your coat. His speech combined the speed of a telegraph
instrument, the cordiality of a campaign manager, and the conscience
of a slot machine.

He arrived in Chalybeate Junction with two suitcases, a portable
typewriter, and a plan.

"I've looked you over," he told the directors, "and you've
got the finest undeveloped cemetery in American journalism. The name
is good, the territory is good, and the magazine is
deader than Julius Caesar's campaign committee. That means opportunity."

The directors did not wholly approve of his imagery, but
they approved of his offer. Thacker would invest ten thousand
dollars, take charge of promotion, and increase the circulation to
fifty thousand within a year. In return he demanded a
voice in editorial policy.

Colonel Telfair rose.

"Gentlemen," he said, "while I should be reluctant to interpose
objections to an arrangement calculated to relieve the present pecuniary
embarrassment of this institution, I must nevertheless insist that no
alien hand be permitted to impair its essential character. The
*Southern Zodiac* was founded not as a receptacle for the
ephemeral productions of every scribbling adventurer who may obtain notoriety
in Boston, Chicago, or other centres of intellectual unrest, but
as a sanctuary wherein the genius of our own people
might find dignified and uncontaminated expression."

"Sure," said Thacker. "Keep the sanctuary. I only want to
put a cash register in the vestibule."

Thus they became associates.

On his first morning in the office Thacker removed his
coat, pushed back his cuffs, and examined three months' manuscripts.
He worked for two hours in silence while Colonel Telfair
wrote an editorial entitled "The Assault of Modernity upon the
Rural Verities."

At eleven, Thacker lit a cigar.

"Colonel," he said, "where do you get this truck?"

The colonel slowly raised his head.

"If by 'truck,' Mr. Thacker, you intend a general designation
of the literary material at present before you, I may
say that it represents the spontaneous efflorescence of Southern culture."

"It represents spontaneous combustion," said Thacker. "If we print it,
the subscribers will burn the office."

He held up a manuscript.

"Here's fourteen thousand words on 'Recollections of My Father's Stable
Boy's Grandmother.' What's it got?"

"The author is a Fitzhugh of Virginia."

"Does that make the stable boy's grandmother move any faster?"

"The Fitzhughs," said the colonel, "require no acceleration."

Thacker picked up another contribution.

"'Lines Written on Seeing the Bust of a Statesman in
the Hallway of a Macon Hotel.' Nine pages. Who did
this?"

"A direct descendant of the Rutledges."

"And this—'Was Pocahontas Misunderstood?'"

"The writer's maternal grandfather commanded a battery at Vicksburg."

"What did Pocahontas command?"

Colonel Telfair looked pained.

"Your jibes, sir, while perhaps consonant with the lighter standards
of metropolitan intercourse, do not facilitate an equitable consideration of
the matter."

"All right. No jibes. Let's consider it as a business.
I've got a magazine with twelve hundred subscribers, seven hundred
of whom are cousins of the contributors. The other five
hundred think they subscribed to the *Southern Poultry Guide*. We
need names. We need stories with people in them that
do something before page six. We need pictures, gossip, mystery,
love, crime, money, and one article every month that makes
the reader afraid his neighbour knows more than he does."

"The function of literature," said Colonel Telfair, "is to elevate."

"Certainly. But first you've got to get it off the
floor."

Thacker began walking about the room.

"I can get Gilroy Haines to do us a Washington
article. I can buy a sea story from Captain Bludsoe—the
man's been wrecked in every ocean except the Arctic, and
he's leaving Thursday. We'll run 'How Our Millionaires Choose Their
Wives,' with photographs. We'll get an actress to tell why
she never married; that always interests her three husbands. Then
a detective serial, a page of jokes, and something about
food that poisons you slowly. Put a red cover on
it. Bang! Thirty thousand by Christmas."

Colonel Telfair's lips contracted.

"And these persons whom you propose to introduce into our
pages—may I inquire whether any possess affiliations with the South?"

"They possess affiliations with the public."

"That is not synonymous."

"It is at the news stand."

The colonel laid down his pen.

"Mr. Thacker, I do not deny that the expansion of
our circulation is desirable. I do not deny that contemporary
themes may, under restrictions, merit consideration. But I cannot consent
to fling open these portals to every Northern manufacturer of
literary merchandise merely because his name has acquired currency among
readers of unstable taste."

"Then don't fling. Open 'em six inches and charge admission."

"Our contributors have hitherto been selected with regard to character,
culture, tradition, and the possession of a viewpoint harmonious with
that of the magazine."

"You left out kinfolk."

"I did not consider it necessary to state the obvious."

Thacker laughed.

"Colonel, you're a wonder. You don't edit a magazine; you
hold a family reunion in type."

For a week the two men fenced across the editorial
table.

Thacker submitted an article by a Chicago surgeon on appendicitis.
Colonel Telfair rejected it because the surgeon had served as
medical adviser to a Grand Army post. The colonel offered
instead "Plantation Remedies Recalled by an Octogenarian," which recommended sassafras
tea for fractures.

Thacker proposed a story by the most popular woman novelist
in the country. Colonel Telfair's investigation showed that she had
been born in Massachusetts. He suggested a substitution by Mrs.
Araminta Polk Withers, whose romance concerned two lovers separated for
ninety years by a misunderstanding at a barbecue.

Thacker wanted an exposé of municipal corruption.

The colonel offered a twelve-page account of a banquet given
to Alexander H. Stephens.

Thacker demanded humour.

Colonel Telfair supplied "Droll Sayings of Faithful Retainers," in which
an elderly servant said, "Yes, Marse William," on five different
occasions.

The promoter grew desperate.

"You can't sell embalmed recollections forever," he said. "The country
wants now. It wants electricity, automobiles, trusts, divorces, airships, politics,
prize fights. It wants a man who was in the
White House yesterday telling what somebody whispered behind a curtain.
It doesn't want Cousin Clementina remembering what Uncle Beauregard almost
said in 1860."

"Cousin Clementina," said the colonel, "is a woman of irreproachable
veracity."

"That's her trouble. She never improves anything."

Yet Thacker did accomplish changes. He doubled the advertising pages,
introduced illustrations, reduced Major Scroope's reminiscences from thirty pages to
eight, and persuaded the colonel to print a short story
by a Kentucky writer whose grandfather had moved from New
Jersey before the war. Mr. Dade examined the genealogy for
three days and pronounced the taint technically extinguished by climate.

Circulation increased by 118.

Thacker celebrated as if he had annexed Canada.

Then, one rainy afternoon, he entered the colonel's office and
found the editor reading a thick typewritten manuscript. Colonel Telfair
was so absorbed that he failed to hear the promoter
knock, enter, cough, move a chair, and drop a bound
volume of *Debates in the Provisional Congress*.

Thacker's eyes brightened.

"Good stuff?"

Colonel Telfair placed one long hand upon the manuscript.

"It is a contribution of exceptional interest."

"Story?"

"No."

"Scandal?"

"Certainly not."

"Politics?"

"In the broadest and most elevated interpretation of that term."

"Who wrote it?"

The colonel hesitated.

"The identity of the author need not at present concern
us."

Thacker sat down.

"When an editor says that, the author's either famous or
in jail. How long?"

"Approximately eight thousand words."

"Eight thousand!" Thacker leaned forward. "And you like it?"

"I find in it breadth, vigour, lucidity, moral earnestness, a
robust comprehension of national responsibilities, and a directness of utterance
not wholly devoid of literary merit."

"Then buy it."

"The question is not so simple."

"It's always simple when the copy's good. Who's the man?"

Colonel Telfair adjusted his string tie.

"He is widely known."

"North or South?"

The colonel's silence answered.

Thacker slapped his knee.

"Well, sound the trumpets! A Northerner has crossed the Rappahannock
and reached your desk alive."

"I have not stated that the manuscript will be accepted."

"But you want it."

"I recognize its power."

"Then run it."

"Before taking a step of such gravity, I must satisfy
myself that the author is not entirely alien to the
traditions represented by this publication."

Thacker stared at him.

"You mean you're going to look up his grandmother?"

"I propose to institute a discreet genealogical inquiry."

"Colonel, if Shakespeare mailed you *Hamlet*, you'd hold it until
you found out whether the Prince of Denmark had an
uncle in Mobile."

"The geographical affiliations of Shakespeare," said Colonel Telfair, "present questions
of considerable complexity."

Thacker got up and paced.

"Who is it? Senator? Ambassador? College president? Railroad king?"

"I am not at liberty to say."

"Does he live in New York?"

"That inquiry is irrelevant."

"Boston?"

"Mr. Thacker—"

"Washington?"

The colonel folded the manuscript and placed it in a
drawer.

"I must ask you to respect the confidentiality customarily accorded
to material under editorial consideration."

Thacker bent over the desk.

"I'll bet you fifty dollars you print it."

"I do not wager upon matters involving principle."

"Twenty-five."

"No."

"A dinner."

"I decline."

"A barrel of your favourite mineral water."

Colonel Telfair's expression softened almost imperceptibly. The Chalybeate Junction mineral
water tasted like a horseshoe dissolved in rain, but he
regarded it as medicinal.

"I cannot participate in a bet," he said. "Nevertheless, should
the contribution prove admissible, I would have no objection to
your commemorating the event in whatever harmless fashion may recommend
itself."

"That's a bet with a funeral veil over it," said
Thacker. "Done."

During the next two weeks Colonel Telfair conducted his investigation.

He wrote letters to Charleston, Savannah, Roswell, and Oyster Bay.
He consulted county histories, family Bibles, obituaries, army records, marriage
announcements, and a volume entitled *Distinguished Georgians and Those Who
Ought to Have Been*. He interviewed Mrs. Withers, Professor Rusk,
and an elderly judge who could remember every family connection
in the state except his own first wife.

Mr. Dade was called in and supplied with maps.

The genealogical examiner worked with impressive solemnity. He filled sheets
of foolscap with branching lines that resembled river systems afflicted
by drought. Names were circled, connected, questioned, underlined, and pursued
through marriages. A Bulloch joined a Stewart; a Stewart encountered
an Elliott; an Elliott wandered toward a Roosevelt; then the
trail divided, crossed Long Island, returned by way of Philadelphia,
and disappeared behind a second cousin's baptism.

Thacker watched these operations from the adjoining room.

"Any Confederate cavalry in the underbrush?" he asked.

Mr. Dade gave no answer.

"Find a Georgia uncle yet?"

Silence.

"How about a cook who once heard of Atlanta?"

Mr. Dade closed the door.

The mystery began to trouble Thacker. He knew every prominent
writer then selling words by the pound, yard, or reputation.
He telegraphed two literary agents in New York and wrote
to a correspondent in Washington. He inspected the colonel's wastebasket,
but discovered only a rejected ode to a courthouse bell
and a note ordering shoe polish.

One evening he met Colonel Telfair at the railway station.

The colonel held a telegram and appeared almost cheerful.

"Good news from the family cemetery?" Thacker asked.

"I have received information of a confirmatory nature."

"So our Northern genius has a rebel rib somewhere in
his skeleton?"

"I should not employ that metaphor, but substantial evidence of
an ancestral connection with this state has been established."

"Close connection?"

"Blood, sir, admits no measurement in terms of mileage."

"Grandfather?"

"I prefer not to anticipate publication."

"Great-grandmother?"

The colonel bowed and entered his carriage.

Thacker stood under the station lamp and watched him drive
away toward Cedar Heights. The promoter's face wore the expression
of a man who has heard a safe click but
cannot find the door.

On the following Monday the *Southern Zodiac* went to press.

Thacker was in Atlanta arranging a contract with a lithographer
for a new cover—a spirited female figure representing the South,
holding a laurel wreath in one hand and a subscription
blank in the other. He returned late Thursday evening. The
town was dark except for the bank clock and the
windows of the printing office.

He climbed the stairs and found Colonel Telfair correcting proofs.

"Well?" said Thacker.

"The issue has been completed."

"Did the eight-thousand-word blockbuster get in?"

"It did."

Thacker whistled.

"Name?"

"You will receive a copy in the morning."

"Colonel, I've waited two weeks."

"A few additional hours can impose no serious hardship."

"Did you prove he belonged to Georgia?"

"I established that his lineage includes one of the most
respected families in the state."

"Then I owe you mineral water."

Colonel Telfair inclined his head.

"The water may be sent to Cedar Heights."

At nine the next morning a printer's boy delivered the
first bound copy of the new issue to Thacker's hotel.
He tore off the wrapper before the boy reached the
stairs.

The cover had been altered. Instead of Thacker's spirited female
figure, it bore an engraving of a columned mansion under
a live oak. Beneath appeared the legend:

**THE SOUTHERN ZODIAC**
*A Monthly Exponent of Native Letters, History, and Ideals*

Thacker turned the pages.

There was Mrs. Withers's poem, "To a Sabre Found in
an Attic." There was Professor Rusk's review of a biography
published eleven years before. There was Major Scroope, describing how
he had personally witnessed the retreat from Atlanta from a
cradle in a nearby county. There was "The Lily of
Lost Cause Manor," now in its fourteenth chapter and still
on the veranda.

Then Thacker came upon eight pages of heavy type.

At the top stood an ornamental heading surrounded by magnolia
blossoms, crossed flags, and a small outline of the state
of Georgia. Below it, in dignified capitals, was printed:

### A MESSAGE UPON THE AFFAIRS
### AND DUTIES OF THE NATION

#### By a Distinguished American Statesman

Thacker read the first paragraph. His mouth opened.

He turned back to the title page. Beneath the heading,
in smaller type, the colonel had inserted an editorial note:

> The *Southern Zodiac* takes especial pride in presenting to
its readers this vigorous and important state paper from the
pen of an American whose maternal ancestry identifies him intimately
with Georgia. Through his mother, Martha Bulloch, he derives from
the historic Bulloch family, long honoured in the annals of
this commonwealth. In welcoming this contribution, therefore, we do not
depart from our established policy, but rather extend recognition to
a distinguished representative of Southern blood.

Then came the author's name:

### THEODORE ROOSEVELT
### PRESIDENT OF THE UNITED STATES
### AND A MEMBER OF THE WELL-KNOWN
### BULLOCH FAMILY OF GEORGIA

Thacker read it twice. He sat down on the bed.
Presently he began to laugh, and the brass pitcher rattled
on the washstand.

At ten o'clock he entered the editorial office carrying the
magazine under one arm and an order for a barrel
of Chalybeate water under the other.

Colonel Telfair was opening his mail.

"Colonel," said Thacker, "you win. You've captured the President."

"I have merely presented the facts of his maternal descent."

"You've annexed him to Georgia."

"Blood relationship," replied the colonel, "is not annexation."

Thacker laid the order on the desk.

"There's your mineral water. Thirty gallons. If it doesn't kill
you, nothing can."

The colonel examined the paper and bowed.

"Your courtesy is appreciated."

Thacker turned to go, then stopped.

"One thing more. If the King of England sends us
an article, can we print it?"

Colonel Telfair considered.

"The question would require investigation."

"I suppose so. Maybe his third nurse had a cousin
in Augusta."

"Mr. Thacker," said the colonel with dignity, "your levity continually
leads you into historical inaccuracy."

That month the *Southern Zodiac* gained 7,400 subscribers. Thacker advertised
the President's message in every newspaper from New York to
New Orleans, omitting only the genealogy. Colonel Telfair regarded the
increase as a triumph of Southern letters.

And perhaps it was. For the South had supplied the
author, the North had supplied the circulation, Congress had supplied
the copy, and the President of the United States had
contributed without being asked.

This is known in magazine offices as editorial cooperation.

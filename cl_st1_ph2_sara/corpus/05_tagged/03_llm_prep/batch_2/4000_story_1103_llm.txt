## THE LAST CRY OF SOLANGE

The scream came from beneath the archway of the Cordeliers.

It was nearly midnight. Paris, exhausted by one of those
days in which it had accused, judged, and slain with
the industry of a great manufactory, lay under a pale
September moon. The shops were shut; the lanterns swung in
the wind; and from distant streets there rose at intervals
the hoarse refrain of the *Ça ira*, sung by men
who had forgotten both the tune and the reason for
singing it.

I was returning from the house of Citizen Danton when
I heard that cry.

"Help, monsieur! In the name of Heaven, help!"

Monsieur! Heaven! Two dangerous words in Paris in the year
1793.

I turned the corner and saw a young woman struggling
in the hands of four National Guards. Her dress was
coarse, her cap plain, and a bundle of linen lay
at her feet. Yet even in that disguise there was
something which betrayed her. A laundress does not recoil from
a dirty hand with the air of an offended duchess;
nor does a daughter of the people cry "Messieurs!" when
four bayonets are at her breast.

"Her papers!" said the tallest of the patrol.

"I have lost them," she answered.

"Lost them? Then come with us."

"Where?"

"To the section."

"I have done nothing."

"That is what they all say."

I advanced.

"What is the matter, citizens?"

The tall man raised his lantern. He knew me.

"Ah! Citizen Albert. We have found a bird without a
cage-number."

"A bird?"

"This girl. No pass, no civic card, no certificate of
work. And she says *monsieur* as sweetly as a nun
says *Ave Maria*."

The young woman looked at me. I shall remember that
look when I have forgotten all else—even my own name.
Her eyes were dark, large, and steadfast; terror had enlarged
them, but had not conquered them. Her face, though pale,
possessed that perfect oval which the daughters of ancient families
inherit like an estate. A strand of black hair had
escaped from beneath her cap and lay upon her cheek.

I understood everything.

At that period understanding was often the interval between life
and death.

"You fools!" I cried, laughing. "Have you detained Solange?"

"Solange?" repeated the patrol.

"Yes, Solange, the daughter of Mother Babet, the laundress of
the Rue du Bac. She brings linen to my lodging."

The girl neither moved nor spoke.

The tall citizen frowned.

"She called us messieurs."

"Her mother washes for aristocrats. Their vices cling to the
linen."

Two of the men laughed. The tall one did not.

"And her papers?"

"With me," I said.

This was false.

"Show them."

"At Danton's."

That name produced its usual effect. The tallest lowered his
lantern.

"What has Danton to do with a laundress's daughter?"

"Nothing," I replied; "but he has much to do with
me. I have been working under his protection, and this
girl carries messages connected with my studies."

"What studies?"

"Studies of the guillotine."

The men glanced at one another.

There were subjects before which even curiosity hesitated.

"Come to the Cordeliers," said the tall man. "If Danton
answers for her, she may go."

The young woman's hand trembled. I took it as though
the action were the most natural in the world.

"Come, Solange."

She understood me then.

"Yes—Albert," she murmured.

That was the first time she pronounced the name which
was not mine, and from that instant no other name
ever seemed to belong to me.

We walked between the guards. Her fingers remained in mine,
cold and light as those of a child. Once she
stumbled.

"Courage," I whispered.

"I have courage."

"Then do not say monsieur."

"I shall endeavour to remember—citizen."

"And your name?"

"Solange."

"Your mother?"

"Babet."

"Your occupation?"

She hesitated.

"Surely I am not expected to know everything at once,"
she said, with so delicate an irony that I could
have smiled had the guillotine itself stood before us.

"You fold linen."

"Then I fold linen."

"Badly, I fear."

"Very badly."

The guard looked back suspiciously.

"No whispering!"

"Citizen," I answered, "I am teaching her republican manners."

Danton was still at the Cordeliers. We found him seated
before a table covered with petitions, denunciations, maps, empty bottles,
and the remains of a loaf. His coat was open,
his great throat bare, his broad face made terrible by
fatigue and the scars of disease. He looked less like
a statesman than some formidable labourer who had paused between
the pulling down of one world and the building of
another.

"What now?" he thundered.

The patrol pushed the young woman forward.

"This girl has no papers. Citizen Albert says you know
her."

Danton turned his heavy gaze upon me.

I had rendered him a service two days earlier by
warning him of a political intrigue which need not be
recalled here. He knew that I was neither spy nor
coward. But he did not know the girl.

I approached the table.

"Citizen Danton," I said, "this is Solange, daughter of the
laundress Babet. I answer for her."

He looked at me for several seconds. Then his eyes
passed to the girl. He saw, as I had seen,
the fine hands, the proud carriage, the lips accustomed to
command servants rather than bargain with tradesmen.

"Your daughter?" he asked me.

"No."

"Your sister?"

"No."

"Your mistress?"

The young woman blushed crimson.

"Not yet," said Danton.

Then he burst into laughter—such laughter as shook the bottles
and made the guards smile against their will.

"Let her go. Albert answers for her, and I answer
for Albert. Write that down, citizens. If the Republic perishes
because a laundress has lost her pass, I will carry
the blame before God—if God has not also emigrated."

The guards saluted and withdrew.

The door had scarcely closed when the young woman seized
Danton's hand.

"Citizen," she said, "language has no words to express my
gratitude."

Danton drew back sharply.

"There!" he cried. "Did ever a washerwoman thank a man
as though she were granting him an audience at Versailles?
Child, if you wish to keep your head, learn to
speak badly."

She smiled in spite of herself.

"I shall try."

"Try quickly. Albert, take your princess away."

She became pale.

Danton's expression changed. Under his brutality there were moments of
strange gentleness.

"I ask no questions," he said. "In these times, questions
are warrants with an interrogative point. Go. And if either
of you is wise, you will be beyond the barriers
before sunrise."

Outside, the moon shone upon the wet stones. For some
moments we stood without speaking.

"At whose disposal am I?" she asked at last.

"At your own, mademoiselle."

"That is no longer true."

"You owe me nothing."

"I owe you my life."

"I merely lent you a name."

"You lent me yours."

"Not mine," I replied.

She looked quickly at me.

"Then you are not Albert?"

"No more than you are Solange."

For the first time she laughed. It was a low,
musical laugh, very brief, very young, and so little in
harmony with that bloody city that I felt tears rise
to my eyes.

"Then we are well matched," she said. "Two shadows presenting
one another beneath false names."

"Where do you live?"

"I cannot tell you."

"Where are you going?"

"I do not know."

"You were being followed?"

"My father and I were denounced this evening. We left
our house separately. I was to meet him near the
Pont-Neuf, but the patrol stopped me."

"Your father's name?"

She hesitated.

"Do not tell me," I said. "The fewer names a
man knows, the fewer he can betray."

She held out her hand.

"You are generous, Albert."

The hand was white and small, with tapering fingers which
no soap, however republican, had roughened. I raised it to
my lips.

"Will you trust me?"

"I have already done so."

"Then come."

I conducted her to the lodging of an old woman
named Véronique, who had been nurse to my mother and
would have hidden the King himself had I requested it.
There Solange spent the remainder of the night.

At ten the next morning I returned.

She was waiting beside the window in a grey dress
which Véronique had procured. Daylight did not diminish the charm
which moonlight had lent her. Her hair was arranged with
severe simplicity; her eyes retained the trace of sleeplessness; her
mouth, though grave, seemed formed for smiles. Her teeth, when
she greeted me, had the whiteness of little pearls. I
record these details because for three months they constituted my
heaven, and because memory, cruel in great matters, is merciful
in small ones.

"My father?" she asked.

"I have made inquiries. A man answering your description was
seen near the Pont-Neuf at two in the morning. He
escaped a patrol and took refuge in the house of
a former officer."

"Who?"

"General Marceau."

She clasped her hands.

"Marceau! Then he may yet be saved."

"You know the general?"

"My father knew him before the Revolution. He admired his
courage."

"Marceau is at the Army of the West, but his
aide remains in Paris. If your father has reached that
house, he can be conveyed under military protection as far
as the coast."

"To England?"

"To England, if Heaven—and the committees—permit."

She approached me.

"Albert, save him."

"I will attempt it."

"No. Do not say attempt. Say you will."

"I will."

"At what hour shall I see you?"

"To-morrow."

"To-morrow?"

"At ten."

She repeated the words as though they were a prayer.

"To-morrow, at ten."

The task was not easy. The father, whom I shall
call the Marquis de N——, had served the monarchy without
distinction but with honour. That alone was sufficient to condemn
him. His name appeared upon a list in the Committee
of General Security. His house had been sealed, two servants
arrested, and every gate of Paris warned against his escape.

Yet confusion sometimes serves those whom order destroys. France was
governed by a hundred authorities, each jealous of the others.
I obtained from Marceau's aide a certificate stating that the
bearer was a military surgeon required at Cherbourg. Danton added
his signature with an oath and a shrug.

"What relation is this surgeon to your laundress?" he asked.

"Her father."

"Then Babet has married above her station."

"He is innocent."

Danton struck the table.

"Innocent! Do not use that word to me. If we
begin looking for the innocent, we shall have no time
left to govern the guilty. Take the paper."

That evening I entered the hiding place where the marquis
waited. He was a tall, thin man of nearly sixty,
with powdered hair which no danger had induced him to
abandon. Solange was beside him.

"My father," she said, "this is Albert."

The marquis bowed.

"Monsieur, I am at your disposal."

"No, monsieur le marquis. It is I who am at
yours."

He started at hearing his title.

"Solange has trusted you?"

"With nothing except her life."

"Which is everything."

He took my hand.

"I shall make no concealment from you. I have little
faith in republicans."

"Nor I in aristocrats," I replied. "Fortunately, we have no
time to discuss politics."

His eyes flashed; then he smiled.

"You are right. And since you have risked your life
for my child, you may abuse my order as much
as you please."

We left Paris before dawn in a military cart containing
chests of surgical instruments. The marquis wore the uniform of
an assistant physician. I travelled beside him as far as
Évreux, where Marceau's protection became effective. At the last relay
he embraced me.

"My daughter remains in Paris," he said. "Why?"

"Because it was safer for her to travel separately."

"Will you send her after me?"

"I have already applied for her passport."

"If she does not come, I shall return."

"You would die at the barrier."

"Then swear to me that she shall follow."

I thought of her eyes when she had said *to-morrow,
at ten*.

"I swear to place the choice before her."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is all I can promise."

He studied my face.

"Do you love her?"

I had known her four days.

"Yes."

"Does she love you?"

"I do not know."

"Then, monsieur, you are either an honest man or a
very dangerous one."

"Perhaps both."

He entered the carriage. As it moved away, he leaned
from the window.

"Send me my child!"

I returned to Paris and obtained the passport after three
days of supplication, bribery, and lies. It named the bearer
as Solange Babet, linen worker, aged twenty-one, travelling to Dieppe
upon family business.

At ten in the morning I carried it to her.

She read the paper slowly.

"You have succeeded," she said.

"Yes."

"My father is safe?"

"He has reached the coast. A fishing vessel will take
him to England."

"And I may join him?"

"You may leave to-night."

She folded the passport and laid it upon the table.

"Then I shall not use it."

I thought I had misunderstood.

"You shall not use it?"

"No."

"Solange, your father waits for you."

"I know."

"You cannot remain in Paris."

"I can."

"You are proscribed."

"So are you, perhaps."

"My danger is less."

"Because you are a man?"

"Because no one knows who I am."

"No one knows who I am."

"Too many suspect it. Your manner betrays you at every
word."

"Then teach me to speak badly."

"This is no moment for wit."

"Nor for cowardice."

I seized the passport.

"Listen to me. A boat waits. Your father is safe
because men have risked their lives. Will you render their
courage useless?"

Her lips trembled.

"Do you wish me to go?"

"I wish you to live."

"That is not an answer."

"Solange!"

"Albert, do you wish me to go?"

She stood before me, pale but resolute, her hands clasped
against her breast. I tried to speak and could not.

"You see," she whispered. "You cannot say it."

"What would remain to you here?"

"You."

The word was scarcely audible.

I let the passport fall.

"Solange!"

"I love you," she said. "I do not know your
name, your family, or even whether Albert is the first
or the tenth mask you have worn. I know only
that, when all other men threatened me, you opened your
hand; when my father was lost, you saved him; when
safety was offered to me, the thought of leaving you
made safety hateful. If this is madness, I am mad.
If it is ingratitude, let my father condemn me. I
remain."

I fell at her feet and covered her hands with
kisses.

That same day I wrote to the marquis. I told
him the truth—or that portion of truth which lovers call
the whole of it. He replied from London after three
weeks. His letter was severe, noble, and tender.

"If my daughter has chosen you," he wrote, "I will
not command where I can no longer protect. Marry her.
Bring her to England when it is possible. Until then,
may God guard you both, for France has ceased to
do so."

For three months we lived in Paris under our borrowed
names.

You know, perhaps, what three months signify to those who
are happy under sentence of death. Other lovers measure time
by years; we measured it by knocks upon the door,
patrols beneath the window, lists posted at the sections, and
carts rumbling toward the Place de la Révolution. Yet happiness
lodged with us. It sat beside our poor fire, ate
our black bread, and slept between our dreams and the
scaffold.

Solange resumed the occupation by which she had supported herself
before her arrest. In a small house near the Jardin
des Plantes, she taught reading, music, and drawing to the
daughters of two merchants who found aristocratic accomplishments useful while
professing contempt for aristocrats. She left each morning in a
plain cap and returned before dark.

I continued the researches which had first recommended me to
Danton.

It is necessary that I speak of them, though every
word costs me pain.

From my earliest studies in medicine, I had been convinced
that many of the punishments inflicted by society were based
upon ignorance of the body. The advocates of the guillotine
praised it as instantaneous, painless, humane. I doubted them. Certain
muscular contractions, alterations of the pupils, and responses to sound
appeared to persist after decapitation. If consciousness remained even for
several seconds, then the instrument was not the merciful machine
its inventors proclaimed, but a chamber of indescribable torture compressed
into the span of a breath.

I hoped to prove this.

I hoped, in my pride, that truth might shame men
into mercy.

Through Danton's authority and the assistance of a surgeon attached
to the executioner, the heads of certain condemned persons were
delivered to me immediately after death. My laboratory occupied a
desecrated chapel at the edge of the old cemetery of
Sainte-Catherine. The Revolution had stripped it of its crucifix, altar
ornaments, and bells. Rain entered through the roof; nettles grew
between the paving stones; open graves surrounded it. I placed
my instruments where the altar had stood.

The heads were brought in leather sacks.

There I examined the contraction of the eyelids, the response
of the pupils to flame, the movement of the lips,
and such electrical phenomena as the imperfect apparatus of that
period allowed me to excite. I kept exact records. Some
mouths appeared to form words. Some eyes turned toward my
voice. Once, when I pronounced the name of a man
whose head lay before me, the eyelids opened and remained
fixed upon me for nearly six seconds.

I told Solange nothing of these particulars.

She knew only that I studied death in order to
oppose it.

"For the good of humanity," she would say, smiling sadly.

"For the good of humanity."

"Humanity is an ungrateful patient, Albert."

"We do not abandon a patient because he bites."

"No. But one may fear the fever."

Near the chapel ran the trench into which the blood
and water from the carts were sometimes poured. After heavy
rain it overflowed, staining the mud a rusty red. One
evening I found a dead child in it, drowned in
water through which the blood of fifty condemned persons had
passed. I carried the little body to the keeper's lodge.

That night I dreamed that Solange stood beside the trench,
calling my name, while I searched for her among faces
without bodies.

I awoke with a cry.

She put her arms around me.

"What is it?"

"Nothing."

"You called me."

"Did I?"

"Three times."

I pressed her to my heart.

"Promise that you will never come to the chapel."

"Why should I?"

"Promise."

"I promise."

We intended to marry. The difficulty was not in finding
a priest—there were still priests in Paris, though they wore
red caps by day and carried the sacrament beneath their
coats by night—but in establishing Solange's identity without revealing it.
A false civil declaration might expose us later; a true
one would expose her at once.

"Then we shall wait," she said.

"Until when?"

"Until France recovers her reason."

"Suppose that is never?"

"Then before God I am already your wife."

She placed her hand in mine as she spoke. Her
look was so pure, so full of courage, that I
dared not profane it even with a kiss. A moment
later she herself leaned forward and touched her lips to
my forehead.

Thus passed September and the first half of October.

Then the Queen died.

On the morning of the sixteenth, Solange and I stood
among the crowd as Marie Antoinette was driven to the
guillotine. I had not wished Solange to go, but she
insisted.

"I must see," she said, "what they have made of
her."

The day was cold and grey. The former Queen sat
in an open cart, her hands bound behind her, her
hair cut close, her face older than its years. The
crowd insulted her. Some women laughed. Others lifted their children
that they might see a queen humbled.

At the foot of the scaffold, Marie Antoinette stepped upon
the executioner's shoe.

"Pardon me, monsieur," she said. "I did not do it
intentionally."

Those were nearly her last words.

Solange gripped my arm.

"Even now," she whispered, "she says monsieur."

The blade fell.

A shout rolled across the square.

Solange fainted.

That night the wind began to rise. The shutters rattled;
the flame of our candle bent though the windows were
closed. A dog howled without interruption in the courtyard.

"Albert," she said, "I am afraid."

I had never heard her confess fear.

"Of what?"

"I do not know."

"We shall leave Paris."

"When?"

"To-morrow."

She smiled faintly.

"To-morrow, at ten?"

"At ten, if you like."

"No. Before dawn. I no longer trust to-morrow."

Yet we did not leave. The next morning a message
arrived from the hospital requiring my attendance. A fever had
broken out among prisoners transferred from the Conciergerie. For six
days I worked almost without sleep.

During that interval a letter came from England.

The envelope bore the marquis's hand, but when Solange received
it, the seal had been broken and then clumsily pressed
together. Inside, her father implored her to join him and
addressed her by her true baptismal names.

"Someone has read this," I said.

"Yes."

"Who delivered it?"

"The concierge."

"Before him?"

"He does not know."

I held the paper over the fire.

"What are you doing?"

"Burning it."

"But it is my father's letter."

"It is also a death warrant."

She watched the flames consume the signature.

"We must go to-night," I said.

"My pupils expect me to-morrow."

"Let them expect you."

"If I disappear suddenly, suspicion will be confirmed."

"If you remain, suspicion may become certainty."

She laid her head upon my shoulder.

"Very well. To-morrow evening."

"To-night."

"To-morrow, Albert. I must collect the money owed me, and
I must not seem alarmed. To-morrow I shall return at
five. We will leave at six."

I wanted to refuse. I wanted to lock the door,
to carry her bodily from Paris, to summon every power
that had once assisted me. But fate seldom enters by
force when it can persuade us to open the door
ourselves.

"To-morrow, at five," I said.

"At five."

"Do not be a minute late."

"I shall be early."

We passed the night without sleep. At dawn the rain
began. It tapped first gently against the panes, then descended
in long, cold sheets, veiling the opposite houses. Solange dressed
in silence.

I watched each movement with an anguish for which I
could give no reason. She folded her shawl. She fastened
her plain cap. She placed the useless passport inside her
dress.

"Why take that?" I asked.

"It bears your name for me."

"It bears Babet's name."

"Babet gave me life a second time."

She came to me.

"You are pale, Albert."

"Do not go."

"You said yourself that I must appear as usual."

"I was wrong."

"For once?"

"For once."

She smiled and put her hands upon my shoulders.

"Then command me to stay."

"Stay."

"I cannot."

"Solange!"

"If I do not go, they may search here before
we are ready. I shall teach for three hours, collect
what is owed, and return."

"At five?"

"At five."

I kissed her. She clung to me with sudden violence.

"Albert!"

"What is it?"

"Nothing. Hold me."

I held her.

Her heart beat against mine so rapidly that I could
count its strokes. Then she disengaged herself, opened the door,
and looked back.

"Until five."

"Until five."

She descended the stairs.

I went to the window and saw her cross the
courtyard beneath a black umbrella. At the gate she turned,
though she could not see me behind the curtain. She
lifted one hand.

Then she vanished into the rain.

At noon a messenger came from the cemetery.

Three executions had taken place that morning, and more were
expected in the afternoon. The assistant surgeon required me to
inspect certain unusual reflexes without delay. I wrote a note
for Solange, telling her to wait for me and prepare
our small luggage. I entrusted it to Véronique.

"If she is not here at five," I said, "go
to the school."

I reached the chapel shortly after two.

The rain had transformed the cemetery into a marsh. My
boots sank in the mud; water filled the new graves;
the bare branches lashed one another overhead. The keeper was
absent. I unlocked the chapel and entered.

The place smelled of wet stone, chemicals, and decay.

A single lamp burned upon the table. The stripped walls
streamed with moisture. Where the altar had once stood, my
instruments lay beneath white cloths. Drops fell from the broken
roof with slow, regular sounds, like the ticking of a
clock.

The first sack had already been delivered.

I worked.

You will pardon me if I do not describe that
work. My notes, if they still exist, contain enough for
science; my memory contains too much for peace. I shall
say only that the rain continued, the afternoon darkened prematurely,
and every sound became magnified by the emptiness of the
chapel.

At four, the outer door swung open.

A cart had stopped outside.

Two men entered carrying another sack between them. It was
dark with rain and stained at the bottom.

"Fresh," said one of them. "The last batch."

"How many?" I asked.

"Seven."

"Names?"

He handed me a folded list.

The wind extinguished one of the lamps before I could
read it.

"Shut the door," I said.

"It will not hold. The hinge is broken."

They placed the sack before the former altar.

"Bad road," said the second man. "Wheel stuck by the
trench."

"Were these executed at three?"

"Some at three. One later."

"A woman?"

"Two, perhaps. Ask Sanson. We only carry."

They left.

The cart creaked away. Its wheels splashed through the mud
until the storm swallowed the sound.

I was alone.

I trimmed the remaining lamp. Its flame trembled and cast
monstrous shadows upon the walls. The sack lay perhaps ten
paces from me, shapeless and motionless. Water gathered beneath it
and spread along the stones.

I unfolded the list, but the ink had run. I
could distinguish only fragments.

*Pierre—François—widow—Louise—*

A gust burst through the half-open door and sent the
paper flying. I pursued it; the lamp nearly went out
again. Somewhere among the graves a dog began to howl.

I thought of the dog beneath our window on the
night the Queen died.

Five o'clock, I told myself. Solange would now be home.
Véronique would have given her my note. At six we
should depart. By midnight we might reach the first post-house.
In three days we should see the sea.

I bent over my instruments.

Then I heard my name.

"Albert!"

I raised my head.

The voice had been faint, almost lost beneath the wind.
I listened.

Nothing.

"Who is there?" I called.

The door moved upon its broken hinge.

No one answered.

I took the lamp and searched the porch. Rain drove
across the graves; branches whipped the chapel wall; the red
trench overflowed into the path. There was no living creature
in sight.

I returned and closed the door as well as I
could, securing it with a cord.

"You are tired," I said aloud.

My voice sounded strange.

I resumed my work.

A minute passed—perhaps two.

"Albert!"

This time I dropped the instrument in my hand.

The voice was unmistakable.

It was Solange's.

"Solange?"

The lamp trembled as I turned. The chapel was empty.

"Solange! Where are you?"

No answer.

I ran to the door, cut the cord, and flung
it open.

"Solange!"

Only the storm replied. The cemetery stretched black and silver
beneath the rain. I called again. I searched behind the
chapel, among the cypresses, beside the keeper's lodge. No one
was there.

Could she have followed me? Could she have lost her
way among the graves? Could some accident have—

Then the voice came a third time.

"Albert!"

It came from inside the chapel.

I stopped breathing.

The cry had been weaker, but nearer—so near that it
seemed spoken at my shoulder. I turned slowly.

The lamp burned upon the table. The cloths stirred in
the draught. The wet sack rested before the empty altar.

The voice seemed to proceed from the sack.

I do not know how long I stood upon the
threshold. Reason deserted me, yet habit continued to observe. I
saw the water slipping from the leather. I saw the
cord tied at its mouth. I saw a narrow red
line winding between the stones toward my foot.

"No," I said.

I entered.

The door swung shut behind me.

"No."

I approached one pace.

The sack did not move.

"Albert," sighed the voice.

It was so faint that it might have been the
wind within the stones.

I sprang forward and tore at the cord. My fingers,
stiff with cold and terror, could not loosen the knot.
I seized a knife and cut it. The mouth of
the sack fell open.

Dark hair lay uppermost.

I recoiled, striking the table. A bottle shattered. The acrid
fumes rose around me.

Dark hair.

Not black entirely—there was a warm brown gleam in it
where the lamp touched it.

I knew that gleam.

"Solange!"

No answer.

I thrust both hands into the sack.

My fingers touched a cheek.

It was warm.

Understand me: warm!

I drew out the head.

Her cap was gone. Her hair, wet with rain and
blood, clung to my wrists. The face was pale beyond
any earthly pallor, yet unaltered. The delicate brows, the small
ears, the mouth I had kissed that morning—everything remained. Only
below the chin there was that which I cannot describe.

"Solange!"

Her eyelids were closed.

I pressed my lips to hers.

They were warm.

I cried her name again and again. I called her
my beloved, my wife, my soul. I implored her to
speak. I promised England, safety, marriage, springtime, years—mad promises to
that poor severed head!

Then beneath my mouth her lips moved.

I swear they moved.

Her eyelids quivered.

Slowly—oh God, slowly!—the eyes opened.

They did not wander as in the reflexes I had
observed. They found me.

They knew me.

For one instant there was terror in them; then tenderness;
then an immeasurable sorrow. The pupils contracted toward the lamp.
Her lips parted, but no sound emerged.

"Solange! I am here. It is Albert. Look at me!"

She looked.

Two tears gathered beneath her eyelids.

They trembled, overflowed, and ran down the coldening cheeks.

I kissed them away.

Her gaze remained fixed upon mine for perhaps three seconds—perhaps
a century. Then the pupils widened. The eyelids descended. The
lips lost their warmth beneath my lips.

"Solange!"

Nothing.

"Solange!"

The wind moaned through the chapel.

"Solange!"

The lamp went out.

I remember no more until morning.

They found me before the empty altar, kneeling in blood
and rainwater, with her head clasped against my breast.

From the keeper, from Véronique, and later from a clerk
whom I bribed, I learned the story.

The letter from England had indeed been intercepted. Solange's true
names had been compared with the description of a suspected
woman employed as a teacher near the Jardin des Plantes.
She was arrested upon leaving the house that morning. The
useless passport naming her as Solange Babet was found upon
her person and treated as proof of concealment.

She was taken before the tribunal shortly after noon.

"Your name?" they asked.

"Solange Babet."

"That is false."

"It is mine."

"You are the daughter of the emigrant Marquis de N——."

She remained silent.

"Where is the man called Albert who aided his escape?"

"I do not know."

"Is he your lover?"

She answered, "He is my husband before God."

That declaration appears in the record.

She was condemned at two and taken to the scaffold
after four, the final prisoner of the day. A witness
told me that she refused the priest, not from impiety,
but for fear that delay might cause her to break
her courage.

At the foot of the guillotine she asked the executioner
the hour.

"Nearly five," he said.

"Then he is waiting for me."

Those were her last words.

No one discovered my identity. Danton, when he heard what
had occurred, came to me and wept. Yes, Danton wept.
The man whom Europe imagined made of bronze sat beside
my bed, hid his scarred face in his hands, and
sobbed like a child.

"You saved the father," he said. "You could not save
the daughter. That is the Revolution. It gives with one
hand so that it may know where to strike with
the other."

Within a year, Danton's own head fell beneath the same
blade.

Marceau died young upon a battlefield.

The marquis never returned to France.

As for my researches, I abandoned them. Others may determine
whether consciousness survives the stroke of the guillotine. I possess
my proof, but I have never offered it to any
academy. Science demands repetition; love is satisfied—and destroyed—by a single
experiment.

Many years have passed. Governments have risen and fallen. The
cemetery has vanished beneath new streets; the chapel was demolished;
men who shouted for blood became respectable citizens and spoke
with horror of excesses committed by persons conveniently dead.

I alone have not changed.

At five o'clock, whenever rain strikes the windows and the
wind moves a door upon its hinges, I hear her
voice.

Not as it sounded in life, when she laughed at
danger and repeated our promises—*to-morrow, at ten; to-morrow, at five*—but
as it came to me from the sack before the
empty altar: faint, bewildered, loving, already separated from the world.

"Albert!"

Then I answer, though no one stands beside me.

"Yes, Solange. I am here."

## The Making of Madison Mixon

"Lazy?" said Martha Maria Mixon, setting down her teacup with
such decision that the spoon leaped in the saucer. "Sister
Todbury, laziness was the least of that man's accomplishments."

The ladies around the stove in Shiloh Chapel's basement laughed,
for Martha's accounts of her husband had long ago passed
from private sorrow into church history.

"He could sleep," she continued, "through a thunderstorm, a washday,
an election, an' the Book of Revelation. I seen a
mule work harder switchin' flies. Madison had a tongue sweet
enough to spread on bread, but when it come to
his hands—Lord!—them hands was made ornamental."

"You married him," observed Sister Jane Pike.

"I did. An' I bought a blue hat once that
made me look green. A person can repent."

At this there was another rising of laughter, though everyone
knew that Martha still wore a plain gold ring beneath
her glove and never permitted anybody else to abuse Madison
beyond what she herself considered Christian limits.

She had married him in Georgia when both were young
and freedom itself was young enough to feel like a
road without signposts. Madison Mixon had possessed a handsome face,
an easy laugh, and a genius for appearing hopeful in
the presence of work without ever becoming involved in it.
He could sit upon a fence and advise three men
in a field, quote Scripture to a woman carrying water,
and arrive at supper with the innocent appetite of one
who had been unjustly deprived all day.

Martha took in washing. Madison discussed enterprises.

He planned to raise cotton, then chickens, then bees. He
proposed a school although he could scarcely spell the name
of it. He intended to preach, but was waiting, he
said, for a clearer call.

"The Lord been callin' you every mornin' through me," Martha
had told him. "But you pulls the cover over yo'
head."

After seven years she placed two dresses, a Bible, and
her remaining patience into a trunk.

"Madison," she said, "I loves you. That's the misery of
it. But love ain't vittles, an' it don't split kindlin'.
I'm gwine North where a woman can earn what she
eats without feedin' a full-grown dream beside her. If you
ever makes a man of yo'self, you come find me.
Till then, I ain't yo' wife in practice, only in
the sight of God."

Madison had looked wounded, which was one of his best
looks.

"Martha Maria, you know a man can't rise with his
wife expectin' him to fall."

"A man can't rise lyin' down, neither."

She went away.

New York did not welcome Martha; it merely gave her
opportunities to prove that she could survive its indifference. She
cooked in a boardinghouse, sewed after midnight, saved every coin
that did not cry aloud to be spent, and in
time opened a small establishment where respectable workingmen might obtain
clean rooms and abundant meals. Her biscuits acquired a reputation.
Her chicken stew softened creditors. By the twelfth year, Mrs.
Mixon owned the brick house in which she had once
rented an attic room.

She did not grow rich in the manner of newspapers,
but in the manner of women who know precisely how
much coal remains in the cellar. She had money in
the bank, linen in the cupboard, and the confidence of
tradesmen. She also had a parlour piano upon which nobody
could play, but which stood as a dark, polished testimony
that Martha Maria Mixon had come North and prospered.

Shiloh Chapel prospered with her.

It was not, strictly speaking, beautiful. Its steeple leaned slightly,
its furnace smoked according to private convictions, and the painted
Jordan behind the baptistry resembled a blue turnip patch. But
its people had carried to it their best clothes, their
longest prayers, their homesickness, their gossip, and the old Southern
hymns whose notes could make a snowy street smell suddenly
of pine smoke and red earth.

Reverend Absalom Todbury was its pastor. He had more wisdom
than grammar and enough voice for three educated men. He
knew when his congregation needed doctrine and when it needed
dinner. On Christmas he supplied both.

The older members remembered Christmases before freedom, when celebration had
been borrowed from the master's pleasure and might be withdrawn
with his temper. They remembered corn liquor passed in cabins,
fiddles scraping late, children delighted by an orange, women laughing
because laughter was permitted and sighing because sorrow was not.
They had danced and mourned, feasted and feared, shouted "Glory!"
beneath a heaven that seemed to have overlooked them.

Now they bought their own turkeys. They chose their own
preacher. They prayed as long as they pleased, which was
often considerably longer than anybody had expected. Freedom had not
ended grief, but it had given grief a chair at
its own table.

For this year's Christmas celebration, Reverend Todbury announced that a
distinguished visiting minister would preach.

"He come from the South," the reverend said, swelling slightly
behind the pulpit, "where he has conducted a mighty work
among them what sits in darkness."

"How dark?" called Brother Pike.

"Dark enough to need the light, Brother Pike."

"What his name?"

Reverend Todbury smiled. "That will be made manifest in due
season."

The secret gave him such pleasure that the congregation forgave
him for keeping it.

Martha was to lodge the guest and provide Christmas dinner.
Thus on Christmas Eve her house became a kingdom of
preparation. Holly shone against the mantel; nuts waited in bowls;
a turkey of formidable dimensions occupied the oven; and three
cakes stood beneath white cloths like respectable ladies awaiting introduction.

Outside, snow sifted through the lamplight. Crowds moved along the
streets with parcels under their arms, while bells, wagon wheels,
and vendors' cries made a rough music of the evening.
Inside, Martha directed two girls, corrected the seasoning, and guarded
a bowl of egg drink from Brother Pike, who insisted
it was too sweet and repeatedly tasted it to confirm
his suspicion.

"Reverend Todbury say the preacher ain't comin' till mornin'," Sister
Todbury reported.

"Then the preacher can have the front room mornin'," Martha
said. "I ain't keepin' no sheets nervous all night for
a man that ain't hyeah."

A knock sounded.

"Now who's that?" Martha demanded. "If it's Brother Pike come
back with another opinion, tell him the bowl done gone
dry from investigatin'."

She opened the door herself.

A tall man stood upon the step with snow across
his shoulders. His coat was dark and well brushed, his
boots were polished, and a narrow strip of gray ran
through his hair. Time had strengthened his face without wholly
disciplining the smile upon it.

"Merry Christmas, Martha Maria," said Madison Mixon.

Her hand remained on the latch.

Behind her, the room became so silent that the fire
could be heard settling in the grate.

Madison removed his hat. "You gwine let the snow receive
me better'n my wife?"

Martha stepped back because hospitality was a Christian duty and
because her knees had suddenly become unreliable.

"Come in," she said.

He entered. Sister Todbury's eyes grew round. One of the
girls dropped a spoon. Brother Pike, who had not in
fact gone home, emerged from the dining room carrying the
suspicious cup.

"Martha," he whispered loudly, "is that the deceased?"

"He ain't dead," she replied.

"I know that now."

Madison bowed to the company. "Friends, I'm Madison Mixon, Martha's
lawful burden."

"Former burden," Martha said.

"Reformed burden," he corrected gently.

That word stirred hope in her, and hope, after long
neglect, entered awkwardly. She examined him. There were no signs
of drink. His collar was clean. His hands, once soft
with avoidance, had grown broad and marked. A leather travelling
case stood at his feet, and he had paid the
cabman without borrowing.

"What you doin' in New York?"

"I come to answer what you said."

"I said plenty."

"You said when I made a man of myself I
should come find you."

"And has you?"

"I been tryin' these thirteen years."

Brother Pike leaned toward Sister Todbury. "Took him long enough."

"Men's slow-cookin' victuals," she whispered back.

Martha sent everyone away, though not before Sister Todbury kissed
her and Brother Pike tasted the drink once more for
farewell. Then she and Madison sat before the fire, with
the years between them like a third person requiring attention.

He told her little. After she left, he said, shame
had first made him angry and anger had made him
lonelier than shame. He wandered west, worked on a riverboat,
failed at trading, laboured in a sawmill, and one winter
fell sick in a town where nobody knew him.

"A man can lie to his wife," he said, "an'
he can lie to his neighbours, but fever don't listen.
I seen myself plain."

"And was it a discouragin' sight?"

"It was a poor prospect."

She almost smiled.

He spoke of learning to read properly, of working with
his hands, of helping build a school. Once he mentioned
a congregation, but a pan boiled over in the kitchen,
and when Martha returned he had turned the subject.

"Madison," she said at last, "is you honest?"

"Tonight, yes."

"Tonight ain't a lifetime."

"No. But it's where a lifetime got to start."

The clock ticked. Snow brushed the window.

Martha had imagined this moment through years of hard mornings
and solitary evenings. In some imaginings she forgave him grandly;
in others she turned him away while dressed magnificently. She
had not imagined gray in his hair or humility in
his voice. These details disturbed the orderliness of resentment.

"You may sleep in the back room," she said. "The
front room's promised to a preacher."

Madison's mouth twitched. "I wouldn't dispossess a preacher."

In the morning Martha awoke before dawn. She dressed in
plum-coloured silk, laid out Madison's breakfast, and allowed herself the
dangerous luxury of picturing them walking into Shiloh together. The
church would stare. Sister Jane Pike would choke on envy
and afterward claim it was her collar. Martha would sit
beside her husband—not a widder, not a discarded woman, not
a grass widow preserved by pride, but Mrs. Madison Mixon.

He entered wearing a black suit.

"That's a sober coat," she said approvingly.

"I got business this mornin'."

"Business?"

"Yes."

"On Christmas?"

"It can't wait."

Her bright inward picture cracked.

"You ain't comin' to church?"

"Not with you."

The old anger rose so swiftly that she recognized it
as an old friend.

"You arrive talkin' reform, eat my supper, sleep under my
roof, an' first daylight you goes sneakin' after business!"

"I ain't sneakin'."

"What business?"

"I can't tell you yet."

"Then don't tell me ever."

"Martha—"

"Go on. New York got plenty streets for a man
to idle in. You won't wear mine out."

His face tightened, but he picked up his case.

"I asked you once to believe in what I might
become," he said. "You was right not to. I'm askin'
now that you wait one hour."

"I waited thirteen years."

He bowed his head and left.

Martha stood in the hall until the closing door ceased
to tremble. Then she went upstairs, changed from plum silk
into black, and pinned her hat with such violence that
the hat might reasonably have considered itself punished.

Shiloh Chapel was crowded. Evergreen branches framed the windows; candles
shone along the pulpit; children fidgeted in new shoes. The
congregation buzzed over the visiting preacher and over Martha, whose
domestic news had travelled six blocks before breakfast.

Reverend Todbury met her in the aisle.

"Sister Mixon, you is a blessed woman this Christmas mornin'."

"Don't prophesy at me, Reverend."

"But—"

"I got a special bitterness on me, an' I may
say somethin' to damage yo' theology."

She took a seat near the back.

The singing began. Usually Martha's voice could support a hymn
as firmly as her kitchen table supported a dinner, but
that morning she scarcely moved her lips. She thought of
Madison slipping away. Perhaps he owed money. Perhaps he had
another wife. Perhaps his reform consisted merely in wearing improved
clothes while committing improved varieties of foolishness.

Reverend Todbury rose.

"Brothers an' sisters, Christmas is a season of revelation. A
star revealed a King. Angels revealed good tidin's. An' this
mornin', though some folks been impatient with me"—he glanced toward
Brother Pike—"I reveals our preacher."

A man stepped from the little room beside the pulpit.

Martha gripped the pew.

Madison placed his leather case beneath the chair, mounted the
steps, and turned to face Shiloh Chapel.

A murmur travelled through the congregation. Sister Todbury looked back
at Martha with tears and triumph competing in her expression.
Brother Pike whispered, "The deceased is preachin'," and received an
elbow from his wife.

Madison opened the Bible.

His first words were quiet.

"'When I was a child, I spake as a child,
I understood as a child, I thought as a child;
but when I became a man, I put away childish
things.'"

He paused.

"I used to think that text concerned toys."

Laughter broke the tension.

"I put away no toys," he continued, "because I had
discovered somethin' easier: I let my wife put away everything
while I played with my life."

The laughter softened.

He told them of the man he had been—not wicked
enough to alarm the neighbourhood, merely useless enough to exhaust
one good woman. He told how pride wears many costumes,
and how idleness sometimes calls itself waiting upon Providence.

"I waited on the Lord," he said, "but I wanted
the Lord to carry the wood, light the fire, cook
the dinner, an' then thank me for the appetite."

Even Martha laughed, though tears blurred the candles.

Then his voice deepened. He spoke of her departure, of
the empty cabin, of discovering that charm could not wash
a shirt or comfort a fever. He told of a
carpenter who gave him work, a teacher who gave him
books, and an old preacher who gave him no peace.

"He said, 'Madison, the Lord callin' you.'

"I said, 'How you know?'

"He said, 'Because you argues too much for any ordinary
employment.'"

The church shook with delight.

But when Madison described the years of labour—building schools, burying
children, feeding flood victims, preaching beneath trees and in cabins—the
humour yielded to attention. Here was no idle man's embroidered
tale. His words carried weather in them, graves, hunger, endurance,
and the stubborn brightness of people who had learned to
praise God without confusing praise with ease.

"At last," he said, looking toward the back pew, "I
understood that becoming a man ain't a finish line a
body crosses once. It's a road he chooses every mornin'.
Thirteen years ago, a woman I loved told me to
make a man of myself. I come today not to
boast that the work is done, but to testify that,
by grace, it has begun."

His gaze found Martha.

"She had faith enough to leave me when stayin' would
have helped me remain small. If she can have faith
enough now to let me walk beside her, I'll spend
what days I got honourin' the mercy."

Shiloh forgot its Northern dignity.

Women wept aloud. Men shouted. Reverend Todbury cried, "Amen!" so
powerfully that a sprig of evergreen fell from the pulpit.
Sister Jane Pike turned around and beckoned to Martha with
both hands.

Martha rose.

She walked down the aisle slowly, proudly, in black silk
that no longer looked like mourning. At the pulpit steps
Madison came to meet her.

"You might've told me," she whispered.

"Reverend Todbury swore me to secrecy."

"I'll deal with him later."

"Is you still takin' me home?"

She looked up at him, at the reformed mischief in
his eyes and the earned strength in his face.

"Preacher Mixon," she said, loud enough for the nearest pews
to hear, "I got a turkey waitin', a front room
empty, an' thirteen years' worth of instructions for you."

The congregation laughed and shouted again.

Madison offered his arm. Martha took it.

They went home through the clean Christmas snow, followed presently
by half of Shiloh Chapel, for reconciliation was sacred, but
dinner was at one o'clock, and the people saw no
good reason why either blessing should be enjoyed in solitude.

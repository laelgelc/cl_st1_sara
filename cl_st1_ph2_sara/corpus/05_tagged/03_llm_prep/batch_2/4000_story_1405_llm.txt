## THE SERPENT WITHIN

When George Herkimer, after five years spent among the galleries,
churches, and marble-haunted studios of Italy, again set foot in
his native town, he found that Time—who works with a
chisel less delicate, though scarcely less effectual, than a sculptor's—had
altered the countenance of almost every familiar object. The streets
had grown narrower, because his remembrance had magnified them; the
houses, once so stately, seemed to crouch beneath their burden
of antiquity; and the elm trees, which his youthful eye
had accepted merely as pleasant shade, now stretched above him
like venerable guardians whose green benediction had descended upon generations
of mortal trouble.

Yet there was one mansion toward which his thoughts reverted
with an interest deeper than nostalgia. It stood apart from
the road, behind a rusty iron gate, and was approached
by a long avenue, where the sunshine came down in
broken flakes through the interwoven branches. This was the hereditary
dwelling of Roderick Elliston, Herkimer's early companion, and, at the
period of their separation, the husband of the beautiful Rosina.

The young sculptor—young we shall still call him, though travel,
labor, and disappointment had traced their fine hieroglyphics about his
temples—had brought a message from that unhappy lady. Rosina and
Roderick had lived asunder for nearly three years. Of the
cause of their estrangement, Herkimer knew enough to feel pity
for both, but too little to judge either. Indeed, who
shall presume to judge between two hearts that have once
beaten side by side and afterward recoil from one another?
There are domestic catastrophes without a witness; wars whose battlefields
are no broader than a fireside; murders committed by a
glance, and burials performed in silence.

Rosina had met Herkimer by chance in a neighboring city.
At the utterance of her husband's name, her cheek had
become pale, though not with anger.

"Go to him," she had said. "Tell him that I
remember no wrong. Tell him that, if misery has overtaken
him, the door which he closed against me is open
still."

Then, after a pause, she added words of deeper significance:

"Do not say that I forgive him, George. Forgiveness may
imply pride in the forgiver. Say only that I love
him."

With this message in his heart, the sculptor entered the
old avenue. A fountain murmured somewhere among the trees; but
the sound, which should have been cheerful, seemed like a
voice perpetually repeating some secret of the family and forever
failing to make itself understood. We may well imagine that
Herkimer's recollection peopled every shadow. Here had Roderick walked with
Rosina in the first season of their marriage; beneath that
arbor she had sat, half concealed by roses; and on
the broad stone step of the mansion the two friends
had parted, five years before, with hands clasped in the
confidence of youth.

Herkimer lifted the knocker.

The door opened almost immediately, and an aged servant, after
scrutinizing the visitor's face, started with an expression of mingled
pleasure and alarm.

"Mr. George!" he whispered. "Then Providence has sent you. But
speak low, sir. My master hears what no other mortal
creature can hear."

"What is the matter with him?" asked Herkimer.

The old man glanced toward the dark interior of the
house.

"He says," replied he, "that there is a snake in
his bosom."

At that moment, from a remote chamber, there came a
cry so keen, so full of bodily anguish and spiritual
terror, that the sculptor felt it thrill through every nerve.

"It gnaws me!" cried the voice. "It gnaws me!"

Then followed what might have been a sob, or a
laugh, or—if report be trustworthy—the faint, malignant hiss of a
reptile.

* * *

Roderick Elliston had once possessed a form and countenance which
seemed to promise an honorable harmony between the outward man
and the soul within. His figure was tall, though inclining
to slenderness; his brow broad; his eyes dark and contemplative;
and his mouth expressive of a sensibility which, properly disciplined,
might have rendered him tender toward every creature that breathed.
He had wealth without the necessity of toil, intellect without
the habit of obedience, imagination without the ballast of common
duties. Such gifts are often termed blessings; but Heaven, in
bestowing them, perhaps intends them only as instruments, and leaves
the recipient to decide whether they shall build a temple
or a prison.

His marriage with Rosina had at first appeared to complete
his felicity. She brought no fortune to the ancestral mansion,
unless a serene temper, a courageous tenderness, and the faculty
of finding beauty in humble things may be reckoned among
the riches of this world. To Roderick, however, her loveliness
was less a companion than a possession. He loved her,
undoubtedly; but his love was of that jealous species which
would imprison the bird whose song it adores, lest another
ear should share the melody. A smile given to a
guest, a word spoken with warmth, even a thoughtful silence
of which he could not ascertain the object, became food
for his morbid contemplation.

There are persons who watch the movements of a beloved
heart as a miser watches gold, counting every pulse, suspecting
every absence, and trembling lest the treasure diminish by being
enjoyed. Roderick was one of these unfortunate guardians. He demanded
that Rosina should not merely love him, but should prove
at each successive moment that she had never loved, could
not love, and would never even imagine any being but
himself. Thus affection, which should have been an open heaven,
became a chamber lined with mirrors, where he saw nothing
save the multiplied image of his own distrust.

The immediate occasion of their separation was trivial—as the visible
spark of a conflagration commonly is. A letter, written before
Rosina's marriage and retained from no motive beyond forgetfulness, fell
into Roderick's hands. It contained a harmless expression of youthful
regard from one who had long since died abroad. Rosina
explained; then, wounded by the manner of his accusation, refused
to explain further. He interpreted dignity as guilt, grief as
confession, and silence as proof more damning than speech. His
bitter words drove her from the house. His pride forbade
him to recall her; hers—if that noble reluctance to force
love where love seems dead may bear the name of
pride—prevented her return.

On the first night after her departure, Roderick felt a
strange movement in his breast.

He was sitting alone in the library. The fire had
sunk low; rain beat against the windows; the ancestral portraits,
scarcely visible in the gloom, seemed to withdraw themselves from
a descendant whom they disowned. While he bent forward, brooding
upon the imaginary falsehood of his wife, something stirred beneath
his heart. It was first a mere creeping sensation, such
as might have been produced by a nerve disturbed through
long excitement. Then came a coil, a pressure, a sudden
coldness winding about his vitals. At last, with a sting
that made him leap from his chair, invisible fangs appeared
to fasten in his bosom.

"It gnaws me!" he shrieked. "The serpent! The serpent!"

From that hour the creature—whether it were actual or imaginary,
born of disease, admitted by infernal agency, or generated from
the poisoned substance of his own soul—became the tyrant of
his existence.

Had Roderick declared that a thought tormented him, society might
have listened with indifference; had he confessed that jealousy consumed
his peace, moralists would have furnished him with maxims; had
he complained of indigestion, physicians would have understood him. But
when he affirmed that a literal snake lay coiled behind
his ribs and fed upon his heart, the matter assumed
sufficient grotesqueness to interest the whole town.

At first he concealed his anguish. He moved through society
with one hand frequently pressed against his breast, as if
to restrain the reptile from thrusting its head between the
buttons of his waistcoat. His complexion acquired a greenish pallor;
his eyes glittered with a watchful and almost ophidian light;
and his gait, once firm, had an undulating motion, as
though his limbs unconsciously obeyed the creature within. Herkimer, had
he then beheld his friend, might have fancied him a
statue carved from some discolored marble—a representation of Envy, perhaps,
with all the snakes hidden beneath the drapery instead of
writhing visibly around the head.

By degrees, secrecy became impossible. In the midst of conversation
Roderick would suddenly pause, clutch his bosom, and utter the
terrible refrain:

"It gnaws me! It gnaws me!"

The ladies screamed; gentlemen stared; children, with the cruelty of
innocence, followed him in the street and imitated his convulsive
gestures. Old women whispered that one of his ancestors had
practiced devil worship, and that the familiar spirit of the
family had now claimed its inheritance. The learned inclined toward
a nervous explanation. The clergy spoke vaguely of chastisement, while
carefully avoiding the implication that any respectable member of their
congregations could deserve it.

Roderick sought relief wherever hope, folly, or despair directed him.
A physician of the old school bled him until the
miserable sufferer nearly slipped into Eternity through the wound intended
for his cure. A younger practitioner declared the complaint to
be sui generis, gave it a Greek name of imposing
length, and prescribed a diet so delicate that the patient's
body was almost starved, though the serpent remained voracious. One
eminent divine prayed over him, but broke off abruptly when
Roderick cried that the creature had hissed at the petition.
A traveling mesmerist promised to draw it forth through the
crown of the head; a dealer in patent remedies sold
him twelve bottles of an elixir warranted to expel everything
from sorrow to tapeworm; and a naturalist gravely proposed that
a cage, baited with warm milk, be fastened against the
sufferer's side.

Finally, a celebrated physician, after tapping Roderick's chest, examining his
tongue, and making many inquiries concerning his meals, announced that
the serpent was neither more nor less than dyspepsia.

Thus was the grand mystery of sin, grief, jealousy, self-torment,
and perhaps supernatural visitation reduced to a disorder of digestion!
We smile; yet mankind has always displayed a noble eagerness
to bestow little names upon great terrors, as though a
label tied to the horn of a demon could transform
him into a domestic animal.

None of these remedies availed. The serpent still gnawed.

* * *

But the strangest phase of Roderick's affliction was yet to
come. Misery, after feeding long upon itself, commonly acquires an
appetite for companionship. He began to perceive—or imagined that he
perceived—serpents in the bosoms of other men. The sight afforded
him a cruel consolation. If he alone were haunted, he
must be accursed; if every heart contained its reptile, then
he was merely honest enough to proclaim what others concealed.

One morning, in the public square, he encountered two brothers
whose quarrel over a strip of inherited land had divided
their families for twenty years. The elder, a substantial citizen,
drew aside with an expression of disgust.

"Keep away," said he. "Your madness may be contagious."

Roderick fixed his glittering gaze upon the man's breast.

"Contagious!" he exclaimed. "Why, thy own snake is older and
fatter than mine. I see its head beneath thy ribs.
It is black with hatred, and every scale bears thy
brother's name."

The citizen turned pale; for the most intolerable accusation is
often that which gives bodily form to a truth we
have labored to keep invisible.

At the town hall Roderick confronted an aspiring politician, who
had spent twenty years proclaiming his disinterested devotion to the
people and six months calculating the value of every vote.

"Stand aside, serpent-bearer," said Roderick.

The orator, supposing himself addressed in political metaphor, smiled with
practiced benevolence.

"My unfortunate friend," he replied, loudly enough for the bystanders,
"I bear in my bosom only love of country."

"Indeed!" cried Roderick. "Then love of country has a forked
tongue. It coils about the seat of ambition, drinks flattery,
and will sting every rival who approaches office. Hark! It
hisses the name of the governorship!"

At this, some laughed who had applauded the patriot's speeches;
but others remembered Roderick's wealth and breeding and thought his
eccentricity deplorable rather than amusing.

He next visited the countinghouse of Ephraim Harwood, the richest
merchant in the district. Harwood was a man whose beneficence
had been recorded on brass plates throughout the town, though
his debtors knew that every charitable dollar was squeezed from
some widow's necessity. Roderick found him leaning over a ledger.

"How sleek it is!" murmured the snake-possessed.

"What do you mean?" demanded the merchant.

"The golden serpent in your bosom. See how it swells
whenever the ink records a profit! It has swallowed the
bread of your clerks, the blood of your sailors, the
sleep of your debtors. Take care, good sir. When it
has eaten all else, it will begin upon its keeper."

Harwood ordered him into the street and afterward donated fifty
dollars to the asylum fund.

Nor did vice in its grosser forms escape Roderick's scrutiny.
He pursued a drunken magistrate from the tavern, crying that
a scarlet snake floated in the man's veins and bit
him at every heartbeat. He interrupted a clergyman's sermon against
damnation to inquire whether Christian wrath had not hatched the
fiery reptile then writhing beneath the preacher's robe. He whispered
to an envious maiden that the small green thing at
her heart was feeding on the beauty of her dearest
friend. He bowed before a notorious libertine and saluted the
jeweled snake of appetite, whose scales, he declared, were women's
tears hardened into brightness.

There was grotesque humor in these scenes, and the town
laughed—until each laugher feared his own turn. Roderick's penetrative madness
respected neither rank nor sanctity. He beheld reptiles everywhere: the
pale worm of guilt; the bloated adder of avarice; the
viper of domestic spite, nourished at a common table; the
winged serpent of ambition; the drowsy boa of habitual vice;
the little, incessant asp of wounded vanity. Every human breast
appeared to him a cavern where some secret monster slept.

And was he altogether wrong? Let us not answer too
hastily. If each concealed passion were suddenly made flesh—if hatred
developed fangs, envy scales, lust a divided tongue, and greed
the coils by which it crushes its victim—what man or
woman among us could walk abroad without a reptile showing
beneath the garments? Civilization may consist less in destroying our
serpents than in teaching them to lie quiet.

Nevertheless, society could not tolerate a prophet whose parables pointed
too directly at the hearer. One evening Roderick entered a
crowded assembly, where lamps sparkled, music floated, and young people,
innocent or otherwise, moved through the figures of a dance.
He had not been invited. His hair hung wildly about
his greenish brow; one hand was thrust within his coat;
his whole frame writhed with secret agony.

"Stop the music!" he cried. "Ye tread upon a nest
of serpents!"

The dancers shrank back.

He moved among them, indicating each with a terrible accuracy.
"Here is jealousy in white satin! There is vanity wearing
pearls! Yonder old gentleman cherishes remorse under his embroidered waistcoat.
That smiling matron has a little snake called scandal, which
creeps nightly from her lips into her neighbors' homes. And
here—ah, here!"

He stopped before a young bridegroom and burst into laughter.

"Two serpents in one bosom! One is love grown poisonous;
the other, suspicion not yet born. They will devour each
other, and thee between them."

The bride fainted. Her father struck Roderick. The company surged
about him with threats, questions, laughter, and shrieks. Above the
tumult rose his cry:

"It gnaws me! Why do ye hate me for confessing
aloud what each of you whispers to himself?"

A constable seized him. Roderick offered no resistance, but looked
around upon the faces with a compassion more awful than
rage.

"Poor fellow-creatures," said he. "You lock me away because my
serpent is visible. Yours are safe because they hide."

That very night he was conveyed to an asylum.

* * *

The institution stood on a barren hill beyond the town,
a huge gray edifice whose innumerable windows appeared less designed
to admit light than to count the passing hours. There
Roderick remained more than a year.

The physicians differed concerning him. One maintained that his delusion
arose from diseased sensation in the nerves of the chest;
another traced it to hereditary morbidness; a third believed that
his intellect was sound except upon a single point; while
a fourth, having published a treatise on insanity, attempted to
make Roderick's mind conform to one of its chapters. Doctors,
like sculptors, are tempted to force the living subject into
the posture required by their theories.

They blistered his skin, plunged him into cold water, confined
his arms, dosed him with opium, deprived him of sleep,
compelled him to sleep, questioned him for hours, and forbade
him to speak. Through all these contrarieties the serpent, if
a serpent there actually was, maintained an admirable independence of
medical authority.

At times Roderick lay motionless, listening inwardly. At others he
paced his cell with a soft, sinuous step. Sometimes he
implored the attendants to cut open his breast and drag
the monster forth.

"It lies close around my heart," he would say. "Be
careful! If you wound the creature, its venom will enter
my blood; if you wound the heart—why, that may be
the kinder error."

Once, after a week of unusual quiet, the superintendent entered
and found him smiling.

"Is the snake dead?" asked the physician.

"No," replied Roderick. "It sleeps."

"What quieted it?"

"I was thinking of another's sorrow."

The doctor recorded this answer as evidence of an altered
cerebral condition, though it contained the germ of a wisdom
which his science failed to recognize.

Roderick's fellow inmates regarded him with varying emotions. One, who
believed himself emperor of the moon, offered to appoint him
royal serpent-keeper. Another, a poor woman forever searching for a
child dead thirty years, laid her hand upon his breast
and sang a cradle hymn to the supposed reptile. During
the song Roderick wept and made no outcry. But when
she ceased and resumed her endless search, he clutched himself
and groaned.

"It gnaws me!"

At length the authorities grew weary. His conduct, though strange,
was often more coherent than that of persons who enjoyed
unquestioned liberty. He reasoned closely, remembered accurately, managed his estate
through letters, and displayed an embarrassing perception of the doctors'
weaknesses. Most importantly, he had learned that public truth-telling was
dangerous and promised to abstain from identifying the private reptiles
of respectable citizens.

A legal inquiry followed. Witnesses testified that he was eccentric,
offensive, theatrical, and possessed of an abominable habit of describing
moral defects; but no one could prove him incapable of
managing his affairs. The judge ordered his release.

The town received the news with apprehension. Doors were shut
when he approached. Conversations ceased. Men who had no faith
whatever in his serpent-discerning faculty nevertheless buttoned their coats more
tightly. Yet Roderick troubled them little. He withdrew to the
ancestral mansion, dismissed most of the servants, darkened the windows,
and lived among old rooms in which every object reminded
him of Rosina.

It is easier to accuse the world than to dwell
alone with oneself. In the asylum, Roderick had seen his
affliction reflected in surrounding madness; in society, he had diverted
his misery by exposing others; but in solitude the serpent
and its victim were left face to face—or rather, heart
to fang.

The creature grew hungrier.

* * *

Such was the history, partly learned beforehand and partly gathered
afterward, which lay behind the cry that greeted Herkimer at
the mansion door.

The sculptor followed the aged servant through a hall where
the shutters excluded the afternoon sunshine. Dust covered the floor
except for a winding track, as though some great reptile
had passed repeatedly from the staircase to the library. The
air had the lifeless odor of rooms denied the purifying
entrance of day.

Before opening the library door, the servant whispered, "Do not
contradict him, sir, and do not agree. Both anger it."

He withdrew.

Herkimer entered and for several moments perceived no occupant. At
last a figure detached itself from the deepest shadow beside
the fireplace.

"Roderick!"

"Herkimer," replied the figure.

The voice retained its old richness, but seemed to issue
from a cavern. Roderick stepped forward, and the returning traveler
could scarcely conceal his horror. The friend of his youth
was emaciated almost beyond recognition. His skin had the greenish
translucence of ancient bronze; his dark eyes glittered as if
a fever burned behind them; his lips wore a rigid
smile which might equally signify contempt, pain, or a desperate
hospitality. One hand lay flat against his breast. Beneath it,
Herkimer fancied that the cloth gave a slight, wriggling motion.

The sculptor reminded himself that expectation can create its own
evidence.

"You have come to model me," said Roderick. "Is that
it? You seek a new representation of Laocoön, with the
serpents placed inside the victim. A finer agony, George! The
old sculptors understood muscle, but not conscience."

"I have come as your friend."

"My friend?" Roderick laughed softly. "Then show me your bosom.
Friendship is a fair word beneath which many a little
asp keeps warm."

"You once trusted me."

"I once trusted everybody—especially Rosina."

At the name, his body contracted. A faint sound, like
dry leaves rubbed together, seemed to come from within his
chest.

"Hiss, then!" he cried, striking himself. "She is far away.
Thou hast driven her thence. Art not satisfied?"

Herkimer took a step nearer.

"I have seen Rosina."

Roderick recoiled so violently that he struck the mantel. His
eyes closed; both hands clutched his breast; and his face
displayed the mortal anguish of one whose vitals are seized
by teeth.

"It gnaws me!" he gasped. "George, do not speak that
name unless you would feed it my whole heart at
once."

"She remembers you with tenderness."

"False!"

"She sent me."

"More false! Rosina's tenderness would not cross my threshold. I
poisoned it."

"She bade me tell you that she remembers no wrong."

Roderick stared at him. Suspicion, hope, terror, and an almost
boyish longing passed successively across his face. But hope was
weakest, for it had long been starved.

"No wrong," he repeated. "Ah, that is her revenge—to deny
me even the dignity of having injured her! Does she
forgive me?"

"She forbade that word."

"Then she hates me."

"She said, 'Tell him that I love him.'"

Roderick uttered no cry. He sank into a chair and
bent forward, listening, as though the serpent must pronounce judgment
upon the message. The old clock ticked loudly. Outside, a
bird sang one clear phrase and flew away. After a
long interval, he raised his head.

"It is quiet," he whispered.

Herkimer's heart leaped.

"For the first time?"

"No. There have been moments. When I forgot myself beside
the madwoman who sought her child, it loosened its coil.
When I listened to another sufferer, it slept. But always
I returned into this accursed chamber of myself, and there
it waited."

He tapped his breast with a dreadful gentleness.

"You know, then, what sustains it?" asked Herkimer.

Roderick smiled. "Food. The same food on which I have
nourished it since Rosina left: myself, myself, myself! My wrong,
my pain, my suspicion, my penitence, my possible salvation—all myself.
I have contemplated my soul until contemplation became a mirror,
and the mirror a cavern, and in that cavern the
serpent was bred."

"Then cast away the mirror."

"How? A man cannot step outside his own individuality."

"He can forget it in service, in compassion, in love."

"Words!" exclaimed Roderick; and the creature, or some convulsion of
his muscles, moved beneath his coat. "Give a drowning man
a discourse on water! Tell the prisoner that the door
is a mere conception! I know the remedy, George. To
cease thinking of myself—aye, that would starve it. But the
pain compels attention. Each fang points inward. Every effort to
forget becomes another thought of what I would forget."

He rose and began to pace the chamber.

"I have studied the matter as theologian, natural philosopher, and
victim. If it is bodily, the doctors cannot reach it.
If spiritual, prayer has not expelled it. If imaginary, imagination
is the more terrible reality. Perhaps it is my jealousy
made flesh. Perhaps a fiend entered when love departed. Perhaps
every human fault seeks ultimately to become an animal, and
mine, being cold, secret, and self-consuming, could choose no shape
but a snake."

He stopped before Herkimer.

"Would you like to hear it?"

"No."

"You fear to believe."

"I fear to strengthen your belief."

"Wisely answered! Yet belief and unbelief are twin morsels to
it. Deny the serpent, and I rage that my torment
is mocked. Admit it, and terror feeds it. Pity me,
and my egotism grows proud of unique suffering. Despise me,
and wounded pride gives it blood. There is no approach
to me by which this keeper of the gate does
not levy tribute."

"Rosina might approach."

Roderick's expression darkened.

"Do not tempt me."

"She is near."

The snake-possessed man froze. The silence became so complete that
Herkimer heard water dripping in the garden fountain.

"Near?" Roderick whispered.

"Nearer than you imagine."

Then a hiss—audible, distinct, and prolonged—issued from the shadows about
him.

Whether it came from his contracted breath, from wind in
the chimney, from an actual reptile hidden in the old
house, or from that region where moral and physical phenomena
mingle their boundaries, Herkimer could never afterward determine. He only
knew that Roderick staggered, tore open his waistcoat, and pressed
both hands against the bare skin above his heart.

"No!" he cried. "Keep her away! It will spring at
her. It hates what alone can destroy it."

* * *

The sculptor hastened to support him, but Roderick thrust him
aside and fled from the library. He passed through a
glass door into the garden, as though even the serpent's
victim could no longer breathe in that atmosphere of dust,
memory, and self-contemplation.

The afternoon had declined into that mellow hour when light
seems less to descend from heaven than to arise from
the earth. The old elms cast long shadows; roses had
overgrown the neglected paths; and the fountain, receiving flecks of
sunshine through the branches, scattered them again in trembling fragments.
Nature, unlike man, does not forever brood upon the injuries
of yesterday. The tree that was torn by winter puts
forth leaves around its scar; clear water rises continually from
darkness; and the earth, though nourished by graves, covers them
with flowers.

Roderick reached the fountain and leaned against its stone rim.
His reflection wavered below him, broken by every ripple.

"See!" he said as Herkimer approached. "Even the water will
not hold my image whole. There are a hundred Rodericks,
and each has a snake."

"Look beyond the image," answered the sculptor.

"Beyond it is emptiness."

"No. Another human being."

A slight movement came from the arbor.

Rosina stepped forth.

She had been concealed in the garden by Herkimer's arrangement,
for she had journeyed to the town with him and
refused to remain farther away than the necessity of his
first interview required. Her beauty had changed since Roderick last
beheld her. It was less brilliant, perhaps, but more profound;
sorrow had taken nothing essential, though it had subdued the
radiance of youth into the softer light of womanly beneficence.
She wore no ornament. Her face was pale, her eyes
full of tears, and her hands—those gentle ministers of the
heart—were extended toward her husband.

Roderick did not move. He appeared suddenly transformed to stone.

"Rosina," said Herkimer, "he believes that he can be saved
only by forgetting himself."

She came nearer.

"Then let him remember me."

"No!" cried Roderick. "Do not touch me!"

"Why should I fear you?"

"You know nothing. There is a serpent here."

"I know."

"It has fed upon thoughts of you."

"Then let thoughts of me starve it."

"It will sting you."

"Better me than you."

This simple answer pierced where argument had failed. Roderick's face
convulsed; but still he retreated as she advanced, until the
fountain barred his way.

"Rosina," he said, "I accused you falsely. I searched your
purity for stains until the search itself became pollution. I
drove you from your home. Afterward, when remorse should have
taught me humility, I made even remorse a species of
pride. I declared myself the most miserable of men and
found greatness in my misery. I exposed the sins of
others, not to heal them, but to escape my own.
If you love me, it must be some phantom bearing
my name."

"I love the man whom suffering has hidden."

"He is dead."

"Then I love what may arise from him."

"There is no future for me."

"There is this moment."

Readers of a colder temperament may pronounce Rosina's devotion excessive,
her forgiveness improbable, or her reasoning deficient in worldly prudence.
Yet love has never submitted its credentials to prudence, nor
can the divine be measured by the ordinary. There is
a forgiveness which springs from weakness and invites renewed injury;
but there is another, strong as judgment and clear-eyed as
truth, which sees the whole transgression and nevertheless refuses to
let evil possess the future merely because it has poisoned
the past.

Rosina stood before her husband.

"Roderick," she said, "you have dwelt so long within yourself
that every thought returns to bite the heart which bred
it. Come forth. Forget whether you suffer, whether you are
forgiven, whether you are worthy of happiness. Think only that
I have suffered too—and that I need you."

"You need me?" he repeated.

"Yes."

The words made a breach in his prison.

His clenched hands loosened. For one instant he ceased to
regard himself as victim, sinner, invalid, prodigy, or serpent-bearer. He
saw only Rosina: the trembling of her lips, the tears
she had not permitted to fall, the courage by which
she stood within reach of his imagined monster. Her sorrow
became more real than his sensation. Her need displaced his
agony from the center of creation.

She laid her hand upon his breast.

A violent shudder ran through him. Something seemed to writhe
beneath her palm. Herkimer saw—or afterward believed that he had
seen—the narrow outline of a creature move under the skin,
pass upward toward the shoulder, and recoil as though scorched
by that gentle touch.

Roderick gave a tremendous cry.

"It gnaws me! It—"

The refrain broke unfinished.

From within his open coat there shot a small, dark
shape, glittering green and gold in the fleckered sunshine. It
struck the fountain's rim, coiled for the fraction of a
moment, and lifted a delicate head. Its eyes were like
two points of malignant fire. Then, with a hiss which
might have been the sound of water escaping through grass,
it slipped over the stone and vanished among the roses.

Or perhaps a lizard, disturbed from a crevice, had darted
away. Perhaps no creature appeared at all, and the sculptor's
imagination, long prepared by horror, gave visible shape to the
crisis of his friend's mind. Truth compels us to preserve
the doubt. But all three heard the hiss; all three
turned toward the same spot; and afterward there remained, upon
Rosina's hand, two faint red marks resembling the pressure of
tiny fangs—though they faded before sunset.

Roderick stood upright.

The greenish pallor left his face as if a cloud
had moved from the sun. His eyes lost their glitter
and filled with human tears. He drew a long breath,
then another, testing the free movement of his chest. No
coil tightened. No tooth entered his heart. For the first
time in three years, silence reigned within him.

"Gone," he murmured. "Rosina—it is gone."

She would have fallen, had he not caught her.

That instinctive act completed what her touch had begun. In
supporting another, he forgot to examine himself. He held her,
not as a possession recovered, but as a life entrusted
to his care. The distinction is the whole distance between
jealousy and love.

When Rosina revived, Roderick knelt beside the fountain.

"Do not forgive me," he said. "I have no right
to ask it."

She placed both hands upon his head.

"I did not come to speak of rights."

"I made a hell of our home."

"Then let us make of what remains a place where
another sufferer may find peace."

"I doubted your truth."

"Be truthful now."

"I loved you selfishly."

"Love me better."

At these words he bowed his face against her hands
and wept with the abandonment of a child. The fountain
continued its clear, indifferent music; the sunshine trembled upon the
water; and in the grass, where the supposed serpent had
vanished, no movement could be discerned.

Herkimer withdrew a little distance. The artist in him perceived
the scene as a sculptural group: the kneeling penitent, the
woman bending in benediction, the old stone basin with its
perpetual overflow, and above them the interlacing branches through which
light descended like visible mercy. Yet he knew that no
marble could contain its meaning. Art may carve anguish upon
the brow and tenderness into the inclination of a form;
but the inward moment when self is dethroned and another
soul admitted to its place belongs to that invisible workmanship
by which Providence shapes mankind.

* * *

Roderick's cure, though sudden in its crisis, was not exempt
from the slower labor demanded of every moral restoration. The
serpent did not return in bodily shape; yet at times
he felt an old contraction about the heart, a remembered
sting, a whisper of suspicion. On such occasions he did
not retire into darkness or bend over the mirror of
his sensations. He sought Rosina, or visited some person more
wretched than himself, or performed a duty whose very plainness
left no room for melodrama.

The mansion's shutters were opened. Sunshine entered chambers where dust
had long held dominion. The library, once the cavern of
his contemplation, became a schoolroom for children whose parents could
not afford instruction. The garden paths were cleared, though a
portion near the fountain remained wild; and no gardener could
be persuaded to disturb the roses under which, according to
household tradition, the serpent slept—or had dissolved into the earth—or
had never existed.

The townspeople gradually resumed their acquaintance with Roderick. Many declared
that they had always considered him sane. The politician shook
his hand warmly after securing the governorship. The merchant praised
his recovery and subscribed a modest sum to Roderick's charities,
taking care that the donation appeared in the newspapers. The
clergyman preached a sermon on the danger of extravagant symbolism.
As for the physicians, each attributed the cure to that
part of his treatment which had preceded it most remotely;
and the celebrated advocate of dyspepsia announced that the serpent
had yielded at last to dietary regulation.

Society is generous in forgiving a man when his madness
has ceased to describe society.

Herkimer remained several months before returning to Italy. He modeled
no literal representation of Roderick's affliction, though hints of it
afterward appeared in his work. In one group, much admired
for the contrast between contorted suffering and tranquil compassion, a
shadowy reptile was visible beneath the foot of a woman
who bent to raise a fallen man. Critics disputed whether
the creature symbolized sin, disease, remorse, jealousy, or merely the
antique necessity of introducing an animal into allegorical sculpture. Herkimer
answered none of them.

For symbols, like serpents, evade the hand that would seize
them too confidently.

Was there, then, a living snake in Roderick Elliston's bosom?
The anatomist will reject it; the theologian may interpret it;
the physician will rename it; the lover, perhaps, will understand.
We need not decide. It is enough that something coiled
within him, fed upon incessant self-contemplation, poisoned love into suspicion,
and converted conscience itself into a tooth that never ceased
to gnaw. Whether actual or imaginary, what affliction could have
been more real?

Nor should we pride ourselves that Roderick's case was singular.
Few among us cry aloud in the public square; fewer
still possess the terrible candor to name the creatures that
inhabit our neighbors. Yet every passion which makes the self
its universe tends toward the serpent's shape. Pride coils. Envy
watches with lidless eyes. Avarice constricts. Hatred strikes. Jealousy poisons
the very heart that shelters it. Even repentance, if forever
admiring its own wounds, may become another form of egotism
and nourish the evil it deplores.

Against these reptiles, argument has small efficacy, ridicule none, and
force may drive them deeper. The surest charm is not
to stare continually into the bosom where they breed, but
to look outward; to behold another's need, another's grief, another's
claim upon our tenderness. Self-forgetfulness is not self-destruction. It is
the opening of a prison door.

Years afterward, travelers passing the Elliston estate would sometimes glimpse
a man and woman beside the fountain. The man's hair
had whitened early, and now and then his hand moved
involuntarily toward his breast; but before it reached the old
place of torment, the woman's hand would meet it. Then
they would smile—not with the careless happiness of those who
have never suffered, but with the graver hope of souls
that have looked into an abyss and chosen together to
turn toward the light.

The water rose clear from its hidden source, broke into
brightness, and fell again. Sunshine moved among the leaves. Beneath
the roses there was silence.

And the serpent, if serpent there had been, returned no
more.

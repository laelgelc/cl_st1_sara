### THE MAN IN THE NEXT STREET

Some thirty years ago—though whether in a newspaper, a traveller's
miscellany, or the loose conversation of an elderly barrister, I
cannot now determine—I encountered the outline of a singular domestic
history. The particulars have vanished, as particulars commonly do; but
the naked circumstance, being of that obstinate species which will
live in the mind after more useful knowledge has perished,
remains with me still.

A sober London householder, of middle age and blameless reputation,
informed his wife that business required him to spend several
days in the country. He departed with a carpet-bag, took
lodgings within a stone's throw of his own dwelling, and
remained absent twenty years. During all that period he watched
his wife, his house, and the narrow circuit of life
which he had voluntarily forsaken. At length, on a wet
evening, he entered at his own door and resumed his
place beside the domestic fire as quietly as if he
had gone out after breakfast and returned before supper. He
continued, I believe, an exemplary husband until his death.

This is the whole fact—or the whole fable; and, in
either case, it is enough.

The man's name, let us say, was Hatherley. I know
not that it was so; but every shadow must have
a name if we would follow it through the streets.
Imagine him, then, at five-and-forty: a man neither stout nor
meagre, neither handsome nor disagreeable, with brown hair already touched
by a doubtful gray, and a face so moderately expressive
that ten acquaintances, called upon to describe it, would have
produced ten different countenances. His coat was always suitable, his
opinions never violent, his charity regular, his resentments short, his
laughter infrequent but sincere. He kept accounts in a shipping
office near Thames Street and had kept them, with scarcely
a blot, for twenty-two years.

Such men are supposed to be safe. Beware the supposition!
A tempest announces itself by black heavens and a roar
among the chimneys; but the crack which lets winter into
a household may begin no wider than a hair.

Mrs. Hatherley was three years younger than her husband, though
she looked, at the date of his departure, perhaps five
years younger still. Her temper had more warmth than his,
yet had accommodated itself to the cool climate of their
matrimonial existence. They had no children. Their affection, therefore, was
not diffused through a nursery, but went directly from one
to the other—sometimes with the force of a sunbeam, sometimes,
it must be confessed, with no greater animation than the
light reflected by an opposite wall. Nevertheless, they were happy;
or, what among respectable persons is frequently the same thing,
neither had conceived a life apart.

One Monday, after dinner, Hatherley announced his journey.

"I shall be gone a week, Maria—possibly less."

She was sewing at the window. The needle stopped.

"What business takes you?"

"An account in Hertfordshire. A troublesome matter, but not a
serious one."

Now there was no account; there was no Hertfordshire; and,
until that instant, there had scarcely been a journey. The
lie sprang complete from some obscure chamber of his mind
and stood before him like a servant awaiting orders. Why
did he not dismiss it? Because Mrs. Hatherley looked up
in surprise; because the invented absence had suddenly made him
interesting; because a man who had been entirely foreseeable for
twenty years felt, with a little inward thrill, that he
possessed a secret.

Poor Hatherley! Crafty simpleton! You are pleased with the first
inch of your own grave.

His project—if project it deserves to be called—was to vanish
for seven days and observe the effect. Would Maria miss
him? Would she sit late, listening for his footstep though
she knew it could not come? Would his empty chair
accuse her of having undervalued its owner? There was vanity
here, doubtless, but no grand vanity; only that domestic species
which desires to read its worth in another's distress. There
was selfishness, but not hatred. He did not wish her
to suffer much—merely enough to furnish him with agreeable evidence
of her devotion.

He rose early on the appointed morning. Mrs. Hatherley had
packed his carpet-bag with a precision more affectionate than caresses.
At the door she adjusted his cravat, brushed a thread
from his sleeve, and asked whether he had sufficient money.
He replied almost sharply, being touched by her solicitude and
ashamed of the cause which made it necessary. Then, as
he descended the steps, he turned.

The smile upon his face disturbed her.

It was meant to be reassuring, perhaps playful; yet it
had the constrained significance of a mask, as though another
Hatherley, lurking behind the familiar one, had peeped forth and
immediately withdrawn. Mrs. Hatherley remained at the door after he
had passed the corner. All that day, while arranging china,
directing the servant, or bending over her sewing, she saw
the inexplicable smile more plainly than she saw the objects
before her.

Meanwhile our traveller proceeded no farther than the next street.

He had already engaged a room there under the name
of Harcourt—a back room, rather high, with faded green curtains,
a horsehair chair, a narrow bed, and a window overlooking
brick walls, chimney-pots, and one oblique fragment of the sky.
He entered with his bag and stood listening. A maid
clattered below; a baby cried in the adjoining house; wheels
ground over the stones. The whole city continued its business,
indifferent to the remarkable circumstance that Mr. Hatherley had stepped
out of his life.

At first he laughed. He unpacked nothing, for why should
a man unpack who was to return within a week?
He took luncheon at an obscure tavern, walked westward, and
bought a wig, spectacles of blue glass, and a coat
considerably more shabby than his own. These articles transformed him
less than he imagined, but London completed the disguise. In
a village, where each face is a public document, he
might have been detected before noon; in London a man
may alter his hat and become a stranger to half
the human race.

Towards evening he approached his house.

There it stood—three stories of brown brick, brass plate polished,
curtains clean, the upper windows reddened by sunset. He saw
the servant admit the butcher's boy. Presently Mrs. Hatherley appeared
behind the drawing-room curtain and looked into the street. She
was not gazing for him; how could she, when he
was supposed to be thirty miles away? Yet his heart
gave a foolish leap, and he stepped into a doorway.

"Well," thought he, "she is already lonely."

This conclusion pleased him so much that he nearly went
home.

But no. To enter on the first evening would spoil
the experiment and expose its absurdity. He would wait until
Thursday; that would be sufficient. Thursday came, and he postponed
his return until Saturday, because his wife's manner—observed through window,
doorway, and such glimpses as the street permitted—did not yet
furnish a decisive result. On Saturday he determined that Sunday
afternoon would be more natural. On Sunday he feared explanations.
Monday, being precisely a week from his departure, seemed at
once the best and worst possible day.

Here, reader, we arrive at the hinge upon which twenty
years are to turn. It is so small that you
may scarce perceive it.

Hatherley walked towards his own door at twilight. He had
composed a tale of delayed coaches, lost letters, and a
sick fellow traveller. The wig and blue spectacles were concealed
beneath his arm in a parcel. His hand was extended
towards the knocker. At that instant, a movement within caused
a shadow to pass across the fanlight; and he pictured—not
what truly awaited him, but questions, reproaches, tears, and his
own ludicrous confession.

He walked on.

"Tomorrow morning," he murmured.

Tomorrow! There is no word which has covered more cowardice
with a decent garment.

On the ninth day Mrs. Hatherley sent inquiries to his
office. On the tenth, a messenger went to the inn
where he had falsely said he would stay. By the
twelfth, the police had been consulted. Notices appeared. An accident
was suspected; then robbery; then suicide; then some concealed discredit
in his business. His accounts, when examined, proved exact to
the penny. This confirmation of his honesty deepened the mystery
and, by a cruel irony, made his wife more certain
that he was dead.

Hatherley purchased a newspaper containing the notice of his disappearance.
He read his own description—age, height, coat, watch-chain, small scar
near the left eyebrow—and experienced the strange gratification of a
ghost discovering that the living have remarked his departure. Yet
beneath this pleasure trembled fear. He was no longer playing
only with Maria. Clerks, constables, neighbours, and distant cousins had
been drawn into the circle. To return now would require
a confession before the world.

Still, he would return next week.

Mrs. Hatherley fell ill.

A physician's carriage stopped twice daily before the house; candles
burned at unaccustomed hours; an upper blind remained lowered through
the morning. Hatherley watched all these signs from across the
street and constructed alternately hopeful and terrible explanations. At last
he bribed the servant of a neighbouring house, through some
crooked story of kinship, to tell him what she knew.
The missing gentleman's wife, she said, had suffered a fever
of the brain and was not expected to survive the
night.

Go home, Hatherley!

I see him beneath a lamp, rain shining on his
false spectacles, his mouth open, his cowardly heart struck almost
brave by horror. He crossed the street. He mounted the
first step. Above him, behind those guarded curtains, lay the
woman whose peaceful life he had shattered merely to discover
whether she valued him. What obstacle remains? No lock is
turned against him; no ocean intervenes; he has but to
raise his hand.

Yet observe the treachery of shame. A generous remorse drives
us towards the injured person; a selfish remorse fears the
spectacle of the injury. Hatherley imagined Maria opening her fevered
eyes upon the cause of her torment. He imagined the
physician's astonishment, the servants' cries, the neighbours assembling, the questions
that could have no sane answer. His return, he persuaded
himself, might kill her. Better to wait until the crisis
had passed.

He descended the steps.

Fool! You call the next street a refuge, but it
is another world.

Mrs. Hatherley did not die. Youth—or what remained of it—habit,
medical care, and perhaps that mysterious reluctance by which the
heart continues beating after its reason has been removed, restored
her gradually. Hatherley saw her for the first time six
weeks later. She came forth leaning upon an elderly cousin's
arm, dressed in black, with a veil over a face
now narrow and colourless. He followed them at a distance
to church.

During the service he sat behind a pillar and watched
his wife pray for his soul.

What passed within him? Grief, certainly; tenderness, perhaps; but with
these was mingled a monstrous curiosity. He had become the
secret spectator of a drama which he himself had authored
and which every additional pang rendered more significant. Had he
entered the house that day, sobbing out the truth, she
might have forgiven him—love has pardoned cruelties far less absurd
and far more deliberate. But he looked at the fixed
widow's composure of her face and felt the space between
them widen. She believed him dead. How could a dead
man contradict her without indecency?

"Soon," he whispered. "When she is stronger."

Let us leave him for ten years.

Not that nothing happened. Everything happened, as everything perpetually does;
but Hatherley's share in occurrence diminished year by year. The
green curtains of his room rotted at their folds and
were replaced by brown ones. The first landlady died; her
daughter succeeded; the daughter married, and her husband raised the
rent. A theatre burned. A row of old houses yielded
to shops with glittering windows. Coaches multiplied, fashions altered, children
grew into clerks and mothers. Ministers fell; wars began and
ended. Through all this movement Hatherley remained in the same
narrow orbit, like a moth circling a lamp it dares
not touch.

He no longer wore the blue spectacles, and the first
wig had become ridiculous before he discarded it. Time itself
provided a better disguise. His shoulders bent; his cheeks grew
hollow; the line near his eyebrow deepened; gray overspread his
hair. He earned a little money copying documents for an
attorney who knew him as Harcourt, a silent, dependable man
with no relations. Thus he was respectable in his second
existence as he had been in his first. Even his
eccentricity became methodical.

Each morning he breakfasted at the same table. Each afternoon,
unless weather prevented him, he walked past his former home.
Sometimes he saw Mrs. Hatherley arranging flowers, receiving visitors, or
setting forth with a prayer book. He knew the seasons
of her gowns, the days on which she attended church,
the hour at which her drawing-room lamp was lit. Of
her thoughts he knew nothing.

This ignorance tormented his vanity more than grief would have
done.

Was she faithful to his memory? She continued to wear
subdued colours, but these became her. Did she still mourn?
Her face was calm. Had his disappearance ruined her life?
She appeared useful, occupied, even honoured among her acquaintance. Once
he saw her laugh at something spoken by a gray-haired
gentleman. The sound—scarcely heard across the street—wounded him more sharply
than the sight of her illness. Yet another time, when
she paused alone at the door with an expression of
inexplicable weariness, he swelled with the conviction that she had
never ceased expecting him.

Thus did he turn every gesture into evidence for whichever
hope or fear possessed him. He had abandoned his place
as husband and assumed the inferior station of interpreter.

Why did he not return? Ask rather why the stone,
having rolled an inch down the slope, does not reconsider
at the tenth yard. He intended it continually. He rehearsed
his entrance a thousand times. At Christmas; on her birthday;
on the anniversary of his departure; after one more month;
when his circumstances improved; when an especially suitable mood should
descend upon him—then he would knock, stand revealed, and recover
twenty years by a sentence.

But habit, which begins as a thread, may end as
an iron cable. His secret lodging, once strange, became his
home; his real home, daily seen, acquired the unreality of
a painted scene. He was faithful to Maria in sentiment
and false to her in fact; close enough to hear
her door shut, yet farther removed than if oceans rolled
between them; alive in the flesh, dead in her household;
and, strangest contradiction, still a husband in his own imagination
while occupying no more space in his wife's life than
a name upon a tomb.

Now for a scene!

It is the tenth year—nearly to the day. London pours
its population through a crowded commercial street. Porters cry out
beneath burdens; carts dispute passage with carriages; umbrellas tilt and
clash in a sudden shower. Hatherley, older and more carelessly
dressed, is borne one way by the current. Mrs. Hatherley
advances in the other.

Neither sees the other until they stand face to face.

Her hand, lifted to protect herself from collision, touches his
breast. His breath stops. Here is the face which has
filled his vision for ten years, yet never so near:
fuller than after her illness, marked delicately beside the mouth,
the eyes steadier, the hair beneath her bonnet threaded with
silver. She looks directly at him.

Does she know him?

For one immense second some buried instinct appears to stir.
Her brows contract; her lips part. Memory—not conscious memory, but
the body's old intelligence—seems about to cry aloud. Hatherley's own
features, beneath the ravages of concealment, must likewise tremble towards
their former shape.

A porter thrusts between them. The crowd divides and carries
them apart.

Mrs. Hatherley turns once, puzzled, searching among a dozen hats
and shoulders. Then a companion addresses her; she shakes her
head, as though dismissing an idle fancy, and passes from
sight.

Hatherley escapes into an alley and leans against the wall.

"She did not know me," he says.

The words reveal him to himself. Until that instant he
had imagined his identity preserved intact, like a legal paper
locked in a box. He had supposed that, whenever he
chose, he might produce himself and have the old signature
acknowledged. But identity is not kept by one man alone.
It exists between faces, voices, duties, expectations, remembered kindnesses, forgiven
offences, chairs drawn nightly to the same hearth. Mrs. Hatherley
had not merely forgotten the curve of his cheek; life
had ceased to require him. The vacant place had closed.

"I am mad," he cries, and covers his face.

Yes, Hatherley; but yours is not the splendid madness of
visions and voices. It is a dingy, patient lunacy, paying
rent punctually and postponing cure until Monday.

He resolves to return that very night. He walks towards
the house, turns back, walks towards it again, and spends
two hours beneath the opposite colonnade. By then the lamps
are extinguished. Tomorrow will be better.

Another decade passes.

We need not count its days. To Hatherley they scarcely
counted themselves. The postponement which once required argument now proceeded
without thought, like breathing. His walks shortened. His coat shone
at the elbows. People in the neighbourhood knew him as
an elderly solitary who stared too much at one particular
house, though no one suspected why. Boys mocked his slow
gait. A shopkeeper sometimes nodded to him. He had become
part of the street's furniture—less noticeable than a new signboard,
more enduring than a patch of soot.

Mrs. Hatherley also aged. Her step grew measured; spectacles appeared
when she read; visitors became fewer, though not few enough
to leave her desolate. A niece came to live with
her, married, and departed. The house was repainted. The brass
plate still bore Hatherley's name, not because she expected him,
perhaps, but because property, custom, and memory had combined to
preserve it. Often he stood before that name in the
dusk and felt that the letters possessed a stronger right
within than the man who had once supplied them.

There is a point at which punishment ceases to be
imposed and becomes the offender's chosen climate. Hatherley suffered, but
guarded the cause of his suffering. He lamented his exclusion
and performed it daily. Had an angel opened the door
and commanded him to enter, he might have requested one
additional hour to prepare.

Then came the evening.

It was late autumn, in the twentieth year of his
absence. A cold rain swept slantwise through the streets, while
gusts worried the lamps and drove dead leaves into corners.
Hatherley had eaten no dinner. His lodging seemed damp, mean,
and intolerably silent; so he wandered out, with his thin
coat buttoned to the throat, and arrived by long habit
opposite the house.

The drawing-room curtains had not yet been closed.

Within, a fire burned with that deep, settled radiance which
no public lamp can imitate. Mrs. Hatherley sat beside it,
older now, but not infirm. A small table stood near
her elbow; a book lay open; a white cap surrounded
her tranquil face. The maid entered, placed another piece of
coal upon the fire, and withdrew. Mrs. Hatherley looked towards
an empty chair.

Perhaps she looked merely because the flames stirred there. Perhaps
the chair had always been his. We must not force
significance upon every motion, as Hatherley did. Yet something in
the scene—its warmth, its order, its absolute unconsciousness of the
wet old man beyond the pane—pierced through twenty years of
cowardice.

A gust drove rain down his neck.

"This is absurd," said Hatherley.

And truly it was! His whole tragedy, viewed from a
certain angle, had the aspect of a bad jest persisted
in after every listener had gone home. He crossed the
street, mounted the steps, and opened the door with an
old key which he had carried for twenty years.

The maid, hearing the entrance, called from below, "Is that
you, ma'am?"

Hatherley removed his hat. The passage had altered—the paper was
new, a cabinet stood where the clock had been—but the
house breathed upon him with an air remembered by his
very bones. He walked into the drawing room.

Mrs. Hatherley looked up.

What followed I do not know. The anecdote, if anecdote
it were, merely affirmed that he resumed his domestic station
and remained a devoted husband until death. Let us not
invent the cry, the fainting, the unbelief, the questions, or
that dreadful first touch by which the living woman assured
herself that her ghost was warm. Nor need we decide
whether forgiveness came in an instant or was laboured out
through sleepless years. There are thresholds across which a moral
tale should not intrude.

I picture only Hatherley standing inside the door, rain dripping
from his coat, with a smile upon his face—the same
ambiguous smile that had troubled his wife twenty years before,
though softened now by age, suffering, and a humility almost
innocent. I picture her rising slowly, one hand upon the
chair. Between them lies an invisible country wider than all
England, peopled by their separate days; yet he takes one
step, and then another.

Was his return happy? In a modest fashion, perhaps. Habit,
which had expelled him, may also have received him again.
The cup set at his place, the sound of his
name in another voice, the nightly extinguishing of lamps—these little
ceremonies could stitch even so monstrous a rent, though the
seam must always show. He loved his wife thereafter, we
are told. Let us hope he learned, at last, that
love is not a sentiment watched through windows, but a
place occupied, a burden accepted, a presence continually renewed.

For each of us stands within an unseen order of
human sympathies. Family, friendship, labour, promise, recognition—these make the humble
constellation by which we know where we are. Step aside
from it for a whim, if you dare; the world
will close the opening. Your chair will gather dust, then
be moved; your name will ache, then fade; the faces
that defined you will turn, with puzzled courtesy, upon a
stranger.

And should you return after twenty years, you may indeed
find the door unlocked and the fire burning.

But take heed, lest the man who enters be no
more than the outcast shadow of the man who went
away.

# The Brass of Abraham Duke

On the morning when Abraham Duke was to ride southward
and offer his remaining youth to General Washington, his wife
Catherine stood before the tall clock in the keeping-room, with
her head set in that angry cant which, though it
spoiled the sweetness of her face, could not altogether destroy
the handsome fall of her red cloak from shoulder to
heel.

"Where is it?" said she.

The clock behind her ticked gravely, and its black walnut
case, newly adorned with wheels and flowers of yellow metal,
cast across the floor a shadow shaped somewhat like a
man with a narrow head.

Abraham buckled the strap of his cartridge-box before he replied.

"I shall not tell you."

"Then thou dost own it is hidden?"

"I own what I have told thee already. The money
is safe."

"Money!" cried Catherine. "Call it by its name. Gold. Thy
father's gold, and now thy gold, and by the law
and the minister's blessing somewhat mine also. Fifty years of
hoarding in a sea-chest, and Spanish pieces besides, and yet
thou wilt ride off among guns and redcoats and leave
thy lawful wife no more than twelve shillings in the
blue jar."

"There is meal in the bin, pork in the barrel,
two cows, the young oxen, flax enough for winter, and
Solomon to tend the heavier work."

"Solomon!" She gave a high sniff and glanced toward the
kitchen, where the bound boy, hearing his name, became so
intent upon polishing a pewter plate that he rubbed it
with the wrong end of the cloth. "Solomon would lose
himself between this room and the barn if there were
two doors open."

The boy's mouth fell wider, but he wisely made no
answer.

Abraham drew on his coat. He was a tall, spare
man, thirty-seven years of age, with heavy brows and a
mouth which seemed fashioned less for speech than for keeping
back speech. There are mouths which invite confidence, and mouths
which ask pardon even in silence; his was like the
straight seam of a locked purse.

"If I return," said he, "there will be no need
that any soul know where the inheritance lies. If I
fall, Parson Ebenezer Rawson has a letter sealed with my
seal. He will give it thee upon certain proof of
my death."

"Why to the parson? Am I a child? Am I
a thief?"

"I have not said so."

"Thou hast said worse by holding thy tongue."

Abraham took up his hat. For one instant his stern
face changed—not to anger, as Catherine afterward chose to remember,
but to something between fear and entreaty.

"Speak of the gold to no one," he said. "Not
to Mistress Abigail Endicott, nor thy sister, nor a pedler,
nor a neighbor at spinning. Not to Joseph Evarts's son
if he comes home from sea. To no one. Hast
thou understood me?"

"I understand that thou trustest every man in the province
before thy wife."

"I trust no man."

The distinction was exact and therefore useless, as exact distinctions
commonly are when brought against a wounded heart.

Catherine's blue eyes flashed. "Perchance that is the reason no
man loves thee."

Abraham stood still. Solomon afterward declared that his master's face
became at that moment as white as candle-grease; but Solomon
was a trembling, suggestible creature, to whom memory came dressed
in the colors of whatever tale he had heard most
recently, and no one can say certainly what he saw.
Abraham only turned away, took from the settle a long
parcel wrapped in his old brown cloak, and laid it
across his arm.

"What hast thou there?" Catherine demanded.

"Parts for the clock."

"More brass flowers? Thou canst trim Time like a bride,
yet leave thy wife ignorant and poor."

"Joseph Evarts mended them."

At that name Solomon ceased even the pretense of rubbing
the plate.

Joseph Evarts, who had once been a goldsmith in Boston
and afterward, by some reverse never entirely explained, had settled
in the village as a maker of clocks, spoons, buckles,
coffin-plates, sword-hilts, and whatever else could be coaxed by hammer
or fire out of metal, lived beyond the salt meadow
in a gray house near the creek. He and Abraham
Duke had been close companions from boyhood, though it was
remarked that Abraham had visited him by night with increasing
frequency during the preceding month. Men who know nothing of
a private business are seldom troubled for lack of reasons
to explain it.

"I must take these back," said Abraham.

"On the day thou leavest?"

"He had no time to bring them."

"Then why did he carry them away?"

Abraham looked at her.

"I will not tell thee."

Those five words, which he spoke neither loudly nor cruelly,
had more power to injure Catherine than a blow. A
blow may be repented, forgiven, or at the least displayed
to sympathetic eyes; but a refusal, repeated and repeated, enters
the mind and works there like a fermenting canker, changing
innocent things to proofs and silence to insult.

She followed him through the porch into the pale September
morning. A mist lay over the meadows, and the sea
beyond them was one dull sheet, without horizon or sail.
His mare stood saddled by the stone wall.

"Abraham," she called.

He turned, perhaps hoping that tenderness had at last overcome
rancor.

"If thou art killed," said Catherine, "I shall find it
before the parson comes with thy precious letter."

His face closed again.

"Farewell, Catherine."

He mounted and rode away with the wrapped clock-pieces under
his cloak.

Before noon he returned without them, and with a dark
stain upon one cuff.

He said it was rust.

At one o'clock Solomon came running from the direction of
the creek, stumbling over stones and once falling flat in
the road. His eyes showed white around their rims, and
his lips moved before sound came.

"Master—master—Mistress Duke—"

"What ails thee?" cried Catherine.

"Goodman Evarts—he—there be blood—"

Abraham caught him by the shoulder.

"Speak plain."

Solomon pointed with both hands toward the gray house.

"Father Evarts is killed."

They found Joseph Evarts in his workshop beside the little
brick furnace, lying upon his back with his leather apron
stiffening beneath him. A narrow sword wound had entered below
his ribs. The drawers stood open, the crucibles had been
overturned, and scraps of silver, brass, and copper lay scattered
across the floor; yet a silver cup remained upon the
bench, and money was discovered later in a jar of
sand. The disorder might have been the work of robbers,
but it might also have been made to resemble the
work of robbers, which is the manner in which suspicion
begins multiplying its own substance.

Abraham knelt, touched his friend's neck, and closed the staring
eyes.

"Who hath done this?" whispered Catherine.

"I know not."

"Thou wert here this morning."

"So I was."

"And those clock-pieces?"

Abraham rose. The parcel he had carried was nowhere in
the room.

"They concerned him and me."

"Where are they?"

"I shall not tell thee."

Even Solomon stared then.

The alarm brought neighbors, the constable, and Parson Rawson, a
stooping divine whose charitable temper was continually at war with
the terrifying conclusions of his theology. By sunset there were
twenty persons in the gray house and twice as many
accounts of the murder. A stagecoach had passed on the
Boston road; two strange sailors had drunk cider at the
ordinary; a red-coated scout had been seen three days earlier
beyond the point; and Joseph Evarts, it was whispered, had
possessed secret knowledge concerning smuggled plate.

No one openly accused Abraham Duke. He was a man
leaving for war, and war confers upon a departing man
a brief nobility which village malice is reluctant to disturb.
Yet it was known that he had been the last
familiar person to visit Joseph; that there had been a
stain upon his cuff; that he would not explain the
parcel; and that the dead man's sword, a slender dress
weapon of his own making, was gone from its pegs.

Abraham remained through the night, answered every lawful question with
painful exactness, and rode at dawn to join his company.

Before he went, he put his remaining hand—for he then
possessed both—upon Solomon's shoulder.

"Serve thy mistress faithfully."

"Aye, master."

"Let no stranger within the house after sundown."

"Nay, master."

"And if thou seest a light in Joseph Evarts's house,
or a boat in the creek when no honest boat
should be there, tell the constable. Do not go near."

Solomon's legs began trembling in anticipation of these sights.

Catherine, who heard, said sharply, "Wherefore should there be lights
or boats?"

Abraham looked toward the clock. Its pendulum swung behind the
glass, and the polished disk at the end caught the
dawn in a dull yellow gleam.

"I cannot tell thee."

Then he was gone.

During the first week Catherine searched only where any prudent
wife might search for papers or emergency money left by
a husband gone to battle. She examined the desk, shook
out the account books, thrust her fingers behind drawers, and
opened the little Bible in which Abraham's mother had pressed
funeral rosemary. She did this calmly and told Solomon she
sought a receipt.

During the second week she searched the chamber. She unstitched
the feather bed, probed the mattress with a knitting needle,
climbed upon a stool to sound the rafters, and removed
the loose board where, as a bride, she had hidden
Abraham's first love-letter. There was nothing there except the letter
itself, and she read it twice with a contempt which
did not prevent her from weeping.

By October her search had become a labor as fixed
as churning and more absorbing than prayer. She dug beneath
the hearthstones. She emptied the flour chest and sifted every
handful. She sounded walls with the handle of a knife,
crawled beneath the house, pried at the well curb, and
sent Solomon into the loft with orders to inspect each
knot in each beam.

"He hath buried it," she said one morning.

"Mayhap," answered Solomon, whose safest word was always mayhap.

"Nay, he feared damp. Gold doth not rust, but leather
and boxes rot. He would have chosen a dry place."

"Mayhap the smokehouse."

"I searched it."

"The barn?"

"Twice."

"The clock?"

She wheeled toward him so suddenly that he dropped an
armful of wood.

The tall clock stood against the north wall, ticking with
an air of infinite knowledge and limited sympathy. Its newly
fitted ornaments—wheels at the corners, a pair of climbing vines,
and a round sun-face above the dial—were of yellow metal
which Joseph Evarts had declared to be brass. Catherine's breathing
quickened.

"Help me move it."

They dragged the clock into the middle of the floor.
Catherine opened its case, removed the weights, examined the pendulum,
thrust her arm into its wooden body, and tapped the
back until Solomon feared she would beat it apart.

She found no cavity.

"The base," she said.

They turned the clock upon blankets and removed the bottom
board. No coins fell out; no leather packet appeared; there
were only dust, a dead beetle, and one bent pin.
Catherine sat back upon her heels, her cap awry and
her face reddened.

Solomon, wishing to console her, said, "It be a rare
bright pendulum."

"Brass," she answered bitterly. "Nothing but brass."

She set the clock upright, and Time resumed its motion.

People knew of the inheritance, for secrecy, once divided between
husband and wife, is like water divided between joined hands:
some portion commonly escapes. Catherine had told Mistress Abigail Endicott
before Abraham's departure that old Gideon Duke's estate was greater
than men supposed. After Abraham left, she told her sister
that it consisted of gold; she told the parson that
its value might preserve the farm; she told the cooper's
wife that her husband had hidden it from her; and,
when these admissions brought sympathy rather than discovery, she repeated
them with additions.

She was not, as certain charitable women afterward maintained, greedy
in the simplest sense. The farm yielded enough, and she
never coveted silks or a carriage. What she desired was
to know. Each hour of ignorance renewed the original affront;
every humble object in the house seemed admitted to a
confidence denied her. The bread oven might know. The clock
might know. The iron shovel, the bed knobs, and the
handles of the high chest might all, in their dumb
fashion, be laughing at her.

She came to hate the parson's letter most of all.

In November she put on her red cloak, placed its
feathered hood with care despite rain, and walked to the
parsonage. Parson Rawson received her in a study so crowded
with books that scarcely a bare inch of wall remained.
Some volumes proved all men damned from Adam; others damned
only infants unbaptized; and several, with greater ingenuity, damned mankind
by reason of the love of God.

"I would see the letter," said Catherine.

"I cannot permit it."

"Only the outside."

"The outside containeth no comfort."

"I would see whether it be thick."

Parson Rawson folded his hands.

"Mistress Duke, thy husband's direction is explicit."

"Doth it say thou mayst not give it me until
he is dead?"

"It saith precisely so."

"Then if he be reported killed?"

"I must have confirmation."

"What sort?"

"A declaration from an officer, or from two credible witnesses."

"And if his body be lost?"

"We must await reasonable certainty."

Catherine leaned forward, and the red plume beside her cheek
trembled. "How many months make a woman a widow, Reverend
Rawson?"

"No number of months without evidence."

"Then he hath bound me even beyond death."

The parson's expression softened. "Thy husband meant to protect thee."

"From what?"

"I know not."

"From me, perchance."

The old man looked at the locked drawer of his
desk.

Catherine saw the glance.

"Is it there?"

He did not answer, which was answer enough.

That night she considered returning with Solomon and forcing the
drawer, but she retained sufficient religion to fear theft from
a minister more than theft from a husband. Human morality
is often no broader than a doorstep.

Winter passed. News came irregularly: fighting in New Jersey, hunger
among the troops, a victory exaggerated at the ordinary, a
defeat denied from the pulpit. Abraham sent two letters. The
first contained twelve lines concerning the cattle, the roof, and
Solomon's duties. The second said that he remained alive and
in tolerable health. Neither mentioned Joseph Evarts or the gold.

Joseph's son Harry was then at sea upon a privateer
and did not learn of his father's death until spring.
He arrived by stagecoach, a lean young man of twenty-five,
darkened by wind and sun, and went directly to the
gray house. There he remained alone for three days.

On the fourth he called upon Catherine.

"My father's strongbox hath been opened," he said.

"So was every drawer in the place."

"The papers are disturbed."

"Robbers."

"Perchance."

He looked very steadily at her. He possessed his father's
gray eyes, but not his father's softness about the mouth.

"Did Goodman Duke speak of business with my father?"

"He spoke of nothing if silence might wound me more."

Harry glanced toward the tall clock. "My father made those
ornaments."

"Aye. Abraham carried parts to him the morning he died."

"What parts?"

"I know not. They were wrapped."

"Were they brought back?"

"Not that I saw."

Harry went to the clock and touched the sun-face above
the dial.

"My father's workmanship," he murmured.

Catherine watched him. "Did he fashion secret boxes?"

"He could."

"Could he hide gold in a clock?"

Harry turned. "What gold?"

Thus she told him, though Abraham had commanded her not
to tell any soul, and though Harry's father had been
murdered on the very day when he handled the mysterious
parcel. When the telling was done, Harry's face had become
guarded.

"Did thy father know the hiding place?" she asked.

"I cannot say."

"Thou canst, but wilt not. Men are all sworn into
one brotherhood against wives."

"Nay, Mistress Duke. I know only that my father left
a page torn from his ledger, and that the entry
before it nameth Abraham Duke."

"What entry?"

Harry hesitated.

"Metal received," he said.

"What metal?"

"The word is blotted."

Gold, both thought; neither spoke it.

Harry searched his father's house, but whatever knowledge Joseph Evarts
had possessed had vanished with him. He returned to sea
before summer, carrying suspicion as another man might carry a
keepsake.

The war dragged onward. Coastal alarms became common. Sometimes a
cannon was heard far out upon the water; sometimes redcoats
landed at an unguarded cove to seize cattle, powder, or
a suspected rebel. Men who called themselves patriots and men
who were merely thieves learned to profit equally from darkness.

Catherine grew thin from labor and discontent. Her blue eyes
acquired a peering brightness; her lips drew downward; yet she
continued to arrange her red cloak becomingly whenever she went
abroad, for vanity is no more cured by misery than
a rosebush is cured of flowering because it grows near
a grave.

One evening in late October, as she and Solomon returned
from gathering apples, she saw a flicker behind an upper
shutter of the deserted Evarts house.

She stopped.

"Didst thou see?"

Solomon's basket slid from his hands, and apples rolled down
the lane.

"Nay."

"Thou didst."

"I saw no light."

"There it is again."

A narrow yellow line appeared and vanished.

"Master said we must tell the constable," whispered Solomon.

Catherine stared at the house. The creek beyond it was
hidden by reeds, but from that direction came the faint
knock of wood against wood.

A boat.

Abraham's warning returned to her so clearly that it was
as if he spoke beside her: If thou seest a
light in Joseph Evarts's house, or a boat in the
creek when no honest boat should be there, tell the
constable. Do not go near.

Poor Catherine would have done well to heed both light
and boat; but warnings are rarely strongest at the hour
when they should command us. Resentment answered in Abraham's voice:
I cannot tell thee.

"Go home," she said.

"What wilt thou do?"

"Nothing. I dropped my glove."

"Thy gloves be on thy hands."

She struck the basket from his grasp. "Go!"

Solomon ran. Catherine waited until he disappeared, then approached the
gray house.

The door stood ajar. Within, the workshop smelled of damp
ashes, old metal, and the sea. A hooded lantern burned
upon the bench. Two men were sorting papers into a
sack. A third, broad-shouldered and bearded, stood near the brick
furnace with Joseph's ledger open before him.

Catherine drew back, but her cloak caught upon a nail.

The sound was slight.

The bearded man lifted his head.

"Well," he said, "Mistress Duke."

She knew him then as Elias Vane, who had once
worked for Joseph Evarts and had disappeared after being accused
of clipping coins. He smiled, and the smile was more
frightening because it contained recognition.

"Thou seekest the old man's secret also," he said.

"I know no secret."

"But thou hast spoken much of gold."

Catherine fled. She reached the lane before one of the
men caught her red cloak and dragged her backward.

Solomon, meanwhile, arrived at the farm, shut the door, barred
it, and remained against it panting. His first thought was
to obey his mistress; his second, to obey his absent
master; his third, that to step again into the dark
would cause his immediate death. The third prevailed.

When Catherine returned an hour later, she came alone.

Her hood was torn, and one cheek was bruised. She
told Solomon she had fallen.

"Were there men?" he asked.

"Nay."

"A light?"

"No."

"A boat?"

"Nothing. Thou art a fool and would make ghosts of
glowworms."

Yet through the night she sat beside the fire, starting
whenever wind touched the shutters. Once she went to the
clock and placed her ear against its wooden breast.

The next morning she visited the parson.

"Give me the letter," she said.

"Mistress Duke—"

"There are men who know of the gold."

"By what means?"

She would not confess that her own speech had spread
the tale.

"Abraham trusted Joseph Evarts."

"That is probable."

"Joseph was killed because of it."

Parson Rawson rose. "Hast thou evidence?"

"I have fear."

"Then tell the constable what caused it."

"I can tell him nothing."

Here was the disease of Abraham's silence reproduced in Catherine:
what had injured her, she now practiced; what she had
condemned, she imitated. So do wrongs propagate themselves, not merely
from the strong to the weak, but from the wounded
to every hand that offers aid.

The parson refused the letter.

That afternoon Solomon was sent to Mistress Endicott's with butter.
Catherine watched him down the road, then barred the door.
She placed a musket upon the table, though she had
never fired one. She searched again, more wildly than before.

She tore the lining from Abraham's winter coat. She emptied
salt from its box. She thrust a crowbar beneath the
hearth. Nothing answered her except dust, splinters, and the inexorable
ticking of the clock.

At dusk a knock came.

"Who is there?"

No reply.

A second knock sounded at the back.

Catherine seized the musket. She had forgotten to prime it.

The front door shook beneath a blow, then burst inward.
Elias Vane entered with two men behind him.

"Where is Duke's gold?" he asked.

"I know not."

He laughed. "Thou hast searched a year and knowest nothing?"

"He hid it from me."

"We watched thee visit the parson."

"The letter is his."

"Where?"

"In the parson's desk."

Vane's smile vanished. "We opened that desk last night."

Catherine felt hope rise and perish in the same breath.
If the letter was not there, Parson Rawson had moved
it; yet the robbers would not believe her ignorance.

They bound her to a chair. They questioned her until
questioning became cruelty, and cruelty, failing in its object, became
anger. They held her hand near the hearth coals. They
twisted the red cloak around her throat. They dragged her
from room to room and forced her to watch as
they broke chests, ripped beds, and pried boards from the
walls.

"Where?" Vane demanded.

"I know not."

"Where?"

"He would not tell me."

"Where?"

She began laughing—not from courage, nor madness entirely, but at
the monstrous justice of it. She had spent a year
enraged because Abraham would not entrust her with the secret;
now she was tortured because strangers could not believe that
he had not.

At last she cried, "The clock!"

The men smashed open its case. They split the back,
tore away the dial, and shook the works upon the
floor. Brass wheels, screws, and fragments lay among the ashes.
The pendulum rolled beneath the settle.

They found no coins.

Vane bent over Catherine.

"Thou hast lied."

"Nay," she whispered. "I only hoped."

When Solomon returned at dawn, he found the door open,
the rooms ruined, and his mistress beside the cold hearth.
Her face was turned toward the broken clock. The torn
red cloak covered her from chin to feet with a
dreadful decorum.

The robbers had failed to discover the treasure.

At least, so people said.

Parson Rawson had moved Abraham's sealed letter from his study
to a cavity beneath the pulpit on the previous evening,
prompted by Catherine's fear. For this prudence he afterward thanked
Providence, though Providence had not preserved Catherine; but men are
grateful for the portion spared and pious concerning the portion
lost.

Solomon's account of the light and the boat was doubted
at first, then believed too readily. Elias Vane was never
captured. One of his companions was hanged years later for
piracy, yet confessed nothing concerning Catherine Duke. No one ever
knew certainly which man struck the final blow.

The farm stood empty for four months.

Then Abraham returned.

He came in March, riding in a jolting cart, his
coat pinned across the place where his left arm had
been. His hair had grayed at the temples. Fever had
hollowed his cheeks, and the erect carriage with which he
had gone to war was bent as if an invisible
burden hung from his neck.

Solomon saw the cart and fled into the barn. Shame
had fattened his fear through every intervening month, and he
believed Abraham's first act would be to kill him for
cowardice.

Instead Abraham called his name.

Solomon came creeping out.

"Where is thy mistress?"

The boy—no longer quite a boy—fell upon his knees.

"Dead, master."

Abraham's face did not alter.

"How?"

"Men came. I left her. She sent me away. I
should have fetched the constable. I was afeared."

Abraham looked at the house, where wind moved a loose
shutter.

"Rise," he said.

Solomon could not.

"Rise."

"Wilt thou not curse me?"

"I have no curse left which hath not found its
mark."

He entered the ruined keeping-room and stood before the remains
of the clock. The outer case had been repaired enough
to stand, but its ornamented crown lay upon the table,
and the works were gathered in a wooden bowl. Abraham
touched the broken dial with his one hand.

Then, for the first time, his composure failed.

He made no cry. He sank upon the settle and
bent his head until it nearly touched his knees. Solomon,
watching from the door, thought he prayed. Perhaps he did;
but grief and prayer sometimes assume the same posture while
speaking to different powers.

Parson Rawson came before sunset. He told Abraham all that
was known and returned the sealed letter.

"It was prepared for thy wife," he said.

Abraham put it into the fire.

The wax melted first; the paper curled black around the
edges; and whatever directions might have enriched Catherine passed into
smoke.

"Why?" asked the parson.

"She is dead."

"The wealth might yet sustain thee."

Abraham watched the letter burn.

"I have no wealth."

The parson looked around the wrecked room. "Men will say
otherwise."

"Men say what their hunger requireth."

It was then remembered that Joseph Evarts had been murdered
after receiving metal from Abraham, and that Abraham had offered
no explanation. Suspicion, which had lain thinly over his name
like evening mist, darkened and hardened.

At first the village pitied him. Women brought broth. Men
repaired a portion of the roof. Mistress Abigail Endicott mended
his shirts, though she remarked to every person who would
listen that a man with clean linen had less excuse
for a dirty conscience.

But Abraham discouraged charity by receiving it without disclosure. When
asked about the inheritance, he denied possessing it. When asked
why Joseph had altered the clock, he refused to answer.
When Harry Evarts returned and confronted him, Abraham would say
only that Joseph had been his friend.

"My father named thee in his ledger," Harry said.

"I know."

"The page following was torn out."

"I know."

"Thou wert the last man seen with him."

"I was among the last."

"What didst thou carry beneath thy cloak?"

"His work."

"What work?"

Abraham's mouth closed.

Harry's hands tightened. "Did my father hide thy gold?"

Abraham looked toward the dismantled clock, which he had begun
patiently to restore. On the table lay its yellow pendulum,
its ornaments, and the branching hands that had once pointed
out the hours.

"I will not tell thee."

Harry struck him.

The blow threw Abraham sideways, for a one-armed man has
poor balance. He steadied himself against the wall and said
nothing.

Harry turned pale. "Defend thyself, then! Speak one word that
an honest man might speak."

"I loved thy father."

"Didst thou kill him?"

"No."

It was the clearest denial Abraham ever gave, but by
then it sounded insufficient. A man who refuses explanations teaches
others to distrust even truth when at last he utters
it.

Harry left the house believing him guilty.

Years went by, and the republic for which Abraham had
surrendered his arm came into being with much ringing of
bells, drinking of rum, collecting of taxes, and complaint that
liberty was not so profitable as had been expected. Men
who had hidden from British patrols became heroes by recollection;
men who had fought were occasionally given certificates which merchants
discounted with patriotic severity.

Abraham's farm declined. With one arm he could neither plow
straight nor reap quickly. Solomon remained two years, then left
when his term expired, after begging Abraham to come with
him to Providence.

"Thou wilt starve here," he said.

"Perchance."

"There be work thou canst do."

"Perchance."

"Sell the clock, then. Or the brass fittings. Goodman Pike
saith old brass bringeth a fair price."

Abraham's expression became so terrible that Solomon backed toward the
door.

"Touch nothing in this house."

"I meant no wrong."

"Go."

Solomon went, and thereafter told people that Abraham guarded the
brass more jealously than other men guarded silver. This observation,
joined to older rumors, produced new ones. Some said the
fittings contained hollow spaces filled with coin. Some said Joseph
Evarts had been forced to melt the inheritance and that
Abraham killed him to recover it. Some said Catherine discovered
the truth and Abraham's accomplices murdered her. There was even
a tale that Abraham had secretly returned from the army
on the night of her death, though regimental records placed
him in Pennsylvania.

Truth moves on lame feet; rumor hath wings.

Children were forbidden to cross the Duke field. Travelers saw
candlelight in the house and were told that the one-armed
murderer counted gold until dawn. In fact Abraham often lacked
candles. The glow came from pine knots or from the
thin fire he fed with broken furniture.

He sold the cows, the oxen, the south acreage, and
at last Catherine's red cloak, which had been washed and
folded in a chest. He would not sell the clock.
He would not sell the andirons, though he had no
wood to burn between them. He would not sell the
fire tongs, shovel, warming-pan, bed knobs, drawer handles, candlesticks, or
the bright rosettes upon the best chairs. People thought this
attachment proof of madness or guilt.

Both judgments contained some truth and missed the greater.

Harry Evarts married Elizabeth Vale, a composed young woman who
possessed less imagination than Harry and therefore, on many occasions,
more justice. She listened to the story of his father's
death and asked, "If Abraham Duke slew him for gold,
where is the gold?"

"He hid it."

"Why doth he starve?"

"To preserve his secret."

"From whom?"

"From the law."

"Yet if no man can find the gold, how would
its use betray him?"

Harry could not answer. In secret he watched Abraham's house.
He saw the old soldier return from the woods carrying
sticks beneath his remaining arm. He saw him sit for
hours before an empty hearth. Once, at dusk, he saw
the man polish the brass disk of the clock's pendulum
with the ragged edge of his sleeve.

"Guilt," Harry said.

"Or grief," Elizabeth replied.

The winter of 1798 was long remembered. The harbor froze
farther than any living man had seen; trees split in
the night with sounds like muskets; and wind drove powdered
snow against doors until houses seemed buried in flour. It
was said the cold killed crows upon the wing, though
this was likely an improvement added in summer.

Abraham had no stored wood by January. The town officers,
reluctant to maintain a suspected murderer, offered him a place
in the poorhouse.

"I remain here," he said.

Parson Rawson, now very old, visited him with bread.

"The sin of pride," said the parson, "is no less
because it weareth the garments of endurance."

Abraham sat before the empty hearth. The andirons shone dimly
beneath soot.

"And the sin of judgment?" he asked.

"That belongeth to God."

"Then wherefore hath the village stolen it?"

Rawson laid the bread on the table. "Tell them the
truth."

"They would not believe it."

"Tell it nevertheless."

Abraham looked at the clock, fully restored now, ticking like
Time itself in the half-dark room.

"The truth would condemn more than me."

"Joseph Evarts?"

Abraham's eyes closed.

The parson leaned forward. "Did Joseph conceal thy inheritance?"

"He preserved it."

"How?"

Abraham's mouth tightened.

Even at that hour he could not speak. His secrecy
had ceased to be a decision and become his nature,
as the shell of a nut begins as protection but
ends as prison.

The parson departed. Abraham did not touch the bread while
he was watched.

A week later Mistress Endicott found it frozen upon the
table.

No one saw Abraham alive after the second day of
February. Snow had blocked the road, and the house seemed
abandoned. On the fifth, Harry Evarts dreamed of his father
standing beside a furnace and striking a yellow bar with
a hammer. There was nothing supernatural in this, for Harry
had thought of little else through half his life; yet
he woke troubled and went at noon to the Duke
farm.

The door was unlatched.

Inside, the cold had a stillness different from the cold
outdoors. Frost furred the nails. The water pail was solid.
The clock had stopped at twenty minutes past four.

Abraham sat upon the floor beside the hearth, his back
against the settle and his one hand closed over a
paper. His face, though sunken and gray, wore an expression
of peace which it had perhaps not known since the
morning he left for war.

Harry stood looking down at him.

Upon the paper was written:

**Harry Evarts.**

Harry opened the dead hand and took the letter.

Elizabeth, who had followed him, entered as he broke the
seal. Sunlight came through a gap in the shutters and
lay across the room in a narrow golden bar.

"Read it," she said.

Harry read:

> Harry Evarts—
>
> Thy father was an honest man.
>
> When my father's inheritance came to me, I feared
seizure by British troops, thieves, tax officers, or false friends.
Joseph advised that gold was safest when no man knew
it for gold. I delivered all into his keeping, and
he fashioned it according to our agreement.
>
> Elias Vane learned that Joseph worked the metal. He
demanded the secret. Joseph refused. I found him dying, and
removed one paper bearing the inventory, lest his killers obtain
it. I concealed this from the constable because the goods,
if tested, would show what Joseph had done, and men
would name him counterfeiter, thief, or receiver of unlawful plate.
I thought to protect his honor and my estate.
>
> I protected neither.
>
> I did not tell Catherine, because she spoke hastily.
Her death is the wage of my distrust, though another
hand slew her.
>
> I did not tell thee, because anger had already
judged me.
>
> I have used none of it. To sell one
piece was to expose all and to raise again the
charge against thy father. Pride also held me. Let no
man excuse me by naming love alone.
>
> The ornaments of the tall clock.
>
> The hands and pendulum.
>
> The andirons.
>
> The shovel and tongs.
>
> The warming-pan.
>
> The candlesticks.
>
> The handles of the chests and cupboards.
>
> The knobs of the bedsteads.
>
> The rosettes and nails upon the chairs.
>
> The buckles in the blue jar.
>
> The sun-face above the clock.
>
> Gold.

Harry lowered the letter.

For a moment neither he nor Elizabeth spoke. Then the
winter sun moved from behind a cloud, and the room,
so poor, so bare, so long despised, answered it.

The pendulum shone where it hung motionless behind the glass.
The andirons glimmered beneath their coat of ashes. Each handle,
knob, rosette, flower, nail, and fitting kindled in its appointed
place, and the tall clock, broken by robbers, restored by
a starving man, and stopped at the hour of his
death, stood crowned with a radiant sun.

Harry touched one of the andirons. Soot came away beneath
his thumb, and under it gleamed the unmistakable deep yellow
of old gold.

"My father," he whispered.

Elizabeth looked at Abraham's body beside the empty hearth, at
the bread frozen hard upon the table, and at the
wealth distributed through every common object of the room.

"He told the truth," she said.

Too late.

The clock was wound again after Abraham Duke was buried.
Its pendulum swung in an arc of gold; its hands
measured hours for men who believed they understood the value
of time; and the andirons, when fire was laid between
them at last, appeared in the shifting light to wink
with infernal understanding and mockery.

The village ceased saying that Abraham had murdered Joseph Evarts.
It began instead to say that it had never truly
believed him guilty. Parson Rawson preached upon false witness, Harry
cleared his father's name, Solomon wept when he heard the
letter, and Mistress Endicott observed that Abraham might have prevented
everything by speaking plainly at the beginning—which was true, though
she omitted to reckon how many others might have prevented
something by listening charitably before the end.

As for the treasure, Harry took only enough to pay
Abraham's debts and place stones above Abraham and Catherine. The
remainder he caused to be melted, all except the clock's
pendulum, which he left untouched as a memorial to his
father's craft and another man's terrible silence.

It may yet be seen, people say, swinging behind dark
glass in a house near the salt meadow, though the
house has changed hands many times and the sea has
eaten half the old road. Upon bright afternoons the disk
catches the sun, and a child, seeing it, may mistake
it for brass.

So did Catherine.

So did the robbers.

So did all the village.

And Abraham Duke, who knew it for gold, froze and
starved beside it.

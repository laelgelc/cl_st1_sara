### THE TOMB OF THE SEVENTY-ONE

In the ruined cloister of San Salvador de Oña, where
the swallow now builds above the broken stalls and the
wind chants matins through arches long deserted, there was formerly
to be seen a tomb of black marble, much defaced
by time. Upon its lid lay the effigies of a
cavalier and his lady, their hands folded, their heads resting
upon embroidered cushions, and at their feet a lion and
a little hound, those ancient emblems of courage and fidelity.
Along the sides were carved seventy armed men, all kneeling
toward a small representation of the Holy Sepulchre; but rain,
neglect, and the knives of idle pilgrims had rendered the
inscription scarcely intelligible.

An aged sacristan, who regarded doubt as a species of
impiety, affirmed that the monument covered Don Munio Sancho de
Hinojosa, once the flower of Castilian knighthood, and Donna Maria
Palacin, his gentle spouse. From a worm-eaten chronicle, preserved in
the episcopal library at Burgos, I have gathered the particulars
of their history.

Don Munio held a strong castle upon the borders, in
those olden days when the plains of Castile and the
green valleys of Andalusia were divided between cross and crescent,
and when the trumpet at dawn might summon a cavalier
either to the mass or to battle. He was redoubtable
in arms, stern toward the insolent, compassionate toward the vanquished,
and so exact in all points of honour that his
bare word was esteemed a stronger security than the seals
of princes.

One summer evening, as he returned with certain of his
followers from a foray, his scouts brought word that a
company of Moors was passing through a neighbouring forest. Don
Munio gave the signal, and his cavaliers, couching their lances,
issued from among the pines. They expected to encounter a
troop of warriors laden with spoil; but instead they beheld
a bridal procession.

At its head rode the young Abadil, nephew to the
alcayde of a distant fortress. His turban was clasped with
an emerald; his caftan was of violet silk, sewn with
silver stars; yet he carried no weapon save a jewelled
dagger, rather an ornament of rank than an instrument of
defence. Beside him, upon a milk-white palfrey, rode his bride
Allifra, hidden beneath a veil of gauze, through which the
brightness of her eyes appeared like starlight through summer mist.
Around them were matrons, maidens, musicians, pages bearing caskets, and
servants leading mules laden with carpets, perfumes, sweetmeats, and garments
of ceremony.

The Christians surrounded them before so much as a cry
could be raised. The minstrels ceased their piping; the maidens
huddled together; and old women called upon the Prophet with
great volubility. Abadil alone preserved his composure. He dismounted, approached
Don Munio, and spoke in the Castilian tongue:

"Cavalier, fortune hath placed us in thy power. We are
unarmed, and go to celebrate our nuptials beneath my father's
roof. Take ransom of me, if such be the custom
of war; but let no fear or dishonour approach these
women, and especially this maiden, who hath trusted her happiness
to my keeping."

Don Munio regarded him steadfastly; then, turning to his cavaliers,
he said: "Shall it be told that Castilian lances were
lowered against bridal veils? By Saint James, we have made
no captives, but found guests upon the road."

He saluted Allifra with profound reverence and invited the whole
company to his castle. The drawbridge was lowered; banners floated
from the battlements; trumpets sounded in the gate; and Donna
Maria, attended by her ladies, came forth to receive the
trembling bride. When Allifra learned that no insult was intended,
she raised her veil, and her beauty filled the rugged
hall with wonder.

For fifteen days the castle wore the aspect of a
royal court. Christian and Moor sat together beneath canopies of
damask; oxen were roasted whole in the outer court; fountains
ran with wine; minstrels contended with Arabian musicians; pages bore
dishes of venison, peacocks in their plumage, rice flavoured with
saffron, honeyed fruits, and delicate conserves from Valencia. There were
tiltings at the ring, dances by torchlight, hawking in the
meadows, and songs upon the battlements when the moon arose.

The nuptials were solemnised according to the customs of Abadil's
people, for Don Munio held that hospitality should not command
the conscience of a guest. He bestowed upon the bride
a girdle of pearls and upon the bridegroom a Castilian
charger, coal-black, high-crested, and fierce in the eye. Donna Maria
added robes of brocade, caskets of ivory, and a small
silver mirror, saying that a young wife should always find
one faithful counsellor in her household.

At length Don Munio and twenty cavaliers escorted the bridal
company beyond the dangerous passes. When they parted, Abadil embraced
his host.

"Should thy life ever rest upon my arm," said he,
"may that arm perish before it fail thee."

"Remember only," replied Don Munio, "that mercy becomes the brave
better than vengeance."

Such, in old times, were the courtesy and generosity of
a Spanish cavalier.

Years passed; kings died; castles changed their banners; and many
a warrior who had ridden beneath the sun slept beneath
a stone. Don Munio's beard became silver, but his frame
remained erect and his spirit unshaken. Around him had gathered
seventy cavaliers, tried in a hundred encounters, men of iron,
bound to him not by pay alone, but by affection
and renown.

When the King of Castile summoned his vassals against a
great invasion of the Moors, Don Munio buckled on his
armour for what he believed would be the last time.
He and his followers had resolved, when that campaign was
ended, to hang their shields in the hall and make
pilgrimage to Jerusalem, there to offer their orisons at the
Holy Sepulchre.

On the morning of departure Donna Maria stood with him
in the chapel. She was no longer young, yet her
countenance retained that tenderness which age purifies rather than destroys.
While she fastened a cross of red cloth upon his
mantle, her fingers trembled.

"My lord," said she, "I have dreamed of a long
procession entering our gate, but there was neither trumpet nor
song."

"Dreams," replied Don Munio, smiling, "are shadows cast by fear
upon sleep."

"And I beheld thee among the company, yet thou didst
not answer me."

He laid his gauntleted hand upon her head. "One battle
more, dear heart, and I shall exchange the sword for
the pilgrim's staff. Pray that I return with honour."

"I have never prayed otherwise," said Donna Maria; and she
watched him ride away beneath his banner, followed by the
seventy, until the last lance-point vanished among the hills.

The armies met upon a broad plain, bordered by olive
groves and dusty streams. All day the earth shook beneath
charging squadrons; the air was darkened by arrows; drums rolled
from the Moorish lines, and Christian trumpets replied; pennons rose,
fell, and disappeared in clouds of dust. Don Munio fought
near the royal standard, and wherever his black shield was
seen, the Castilians took courage.

Toward evening the king, carried too far by the ardour
of pursuit, found himself separated from the main body and
encompassed by Moorish horsemen. His standard reeled; his guards fell
one by one; and already a cry of triumph ran
through the hostile ranks.

"Now is the time," said Don Munio.

He and his seventy cavaliers closed around their sovereign. They
formed a living wall of shields and breastplates, giving ground
step by step while the king was urged toward safety.
Their banner was torn to ribbons; their lances splintered; their
horses sank beneath them. Still they fought with sword and
mace, calling upon Saint James, until the Castilian host, perceiving
the peril of its monarch, rallied and swept forward.

The king was saved. Not one of the seventy-one remained
standing.

In the fury of that last encounter, Don Munio met
a Moorish chief mounted upon a black charger. Age had
dimmed the Christian's sight, dust obscured his devices, and neither
warrior knew the other. They struck together. Don Munio's sword
glanced from the Moor's helmet; the Moor's lance entered beneath
the old cavalier's arm, and he fell without a word.

The victor sprang down and knelt to take the name
of so valiant an enemy. He unclasped the battered helmet;
the white hair fell back; and Abadil gazed upon the
face of his benefactor.

For a time he neither moved nor spoke. Then he
uttered a cry so piercing that the soldiers gathering around
him drew back in awe.

"Woe is me! This is the hand that spared my
joy, and mine is the hand that hath extinguished his
life!"

He cast away his sword, lifted Don Munio's head upon
his knee, and called him by name; but the good
cavalier had passed beyond praise or repentance. Abadil wept openly.
He caused the body to be washed, embalmed with myrrh
and aloes, clothed in a mantle of black velvet, and
laid upon a bier beneath Don Munio's tattered banner. The
seventy cavaliers were placed upon wagons behind him, each with
his sword upon his breast.

Thus the procession approached the border castle.

Again the drawbridge was lowered; again banners hung upon the
battlements; again ladies, knights, pages, and servants assembled in the
gate. But the banners were shrouded in black; the trumpets
gave forth a broken note; no minstrels played, and no
bride smiled beneath a veil. Abadil walked before the bier
with uncovered head, leading the same black charger Don Munio
had given him many years before.

Donna Maria descended the steps. When she beheld the horse
without its rider, she understood her dream.

"Where is my lord?" she asked.

Abadil knelt before her. "Lady, here lies the noblest enemy
that ever gave mercy to the unfortunate. Unknowingly I slew
him. Ask my life, my treasures, my children, and they
are thine; but ask me not to forget, for remembrance
is now my punishment."

Donna Maria looked upon his grief and answered: "Rise, Abadil.
My husband taught thee mercy; I will not dishonour his
lesson beside his bier."

Then she bent over Don Munio, kissed his pale forehead,
and spoke no more that day.

Abadil paid for a magnificent tomb, with alabaster angels, shields
of Castile, arabesques carved by Moorish craftsmen, and seventy armed
figures kneeling toward Jerusalem. He remained until Don Munio and
his followers had been committed to consecrated ground; then he
departed in silence, carrying no trophy from the battle.

Donna Maria survived her lord but a few months. Her
attendants often found her seated beside the tomb, her hands
folded upon the cold marble. When she died, she was
buried at Don Munio's side, and the little hound of
fidelity was sculptured at her feet.

There remains, however, one circumstance which the cautious reader may
find difficult, though the old chroniclers record it without embarrassment.

On the very day of the battle, at the hour
of sunset, the sacristan of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem
beheld seventy-one armed pilgrims enter the sacred church. Their armour
was hacked and stained; their faces were pale; and at
their head walked an aged knight bearing a broken sword.
They passed the guards without challenge, ranged themselves before the
Sepulchre, and knelt.

No spur rang upon the pavement, no mail clashed, no
breath disturbed the lamps. They prayed in perfect silence. When
the sacristan approached, the foremost cavalier arose, inclined his head
toward the holy place, and vanished; and the seventy vanished
after him, one by one, like figures erased from an
illuminated page.

Thus did Don Munio and his faithful company accomplish in
death the pilgrimage vowed in life.

These facts are set down by Fray Alonso de Arévalo,
royal historiographer, who cites two monks, one sacristan, three inscriptions,
and a letter bearing an episcopal seal. Whoever doubts them
must therefore dispute not with me, but with authority, antiquity,
marble, parchment, and a bishop.

### The Distribution of Fear

Along the Rio Grande, where the river performs its muddy
and undecided office between two republics, courage was once a
commodity more diligently advertised than cattle, land, or patent liver
medicines. Every man carried some of it visibly at his
belt; a few wore it in the angle of the
hat; others displayed it by sitting with their backs to
open doors, a posture fatal to comfort but beneficial to
reputation. There were also those who possessed courage in the
heart, though this was considered an inconvenient place for it,
being concealed from the public.

Texas Ranger Bob Buckley had more courage than any man
between Brownsville and the Pecos.

He was also a coward.

Let us be accurate in this distinction, for justice, having
once mislaid her spectacles on the frontier, has suffered ever
since from the testimony of excited witnesses. Buckley was no
coward in conduct. He went where bullets travelled thickest, arrested
men whose biographies consisted chiefly of funeral notices, and entered
dark rooms ahead of companions who would have cheerfully followed
him had there been more light. But his interior arrangements
were those of a rabbit during a thunderstorm.

Before danger his mouth dried, his hands chilled, his knees
weakened, and his heart struck his ribs with the industrious
regularity of a prisoner signalling through a wall. He anticipated
death in detail. He saw the bullet before it was
fired, felt the knife before it was drawn, and imagined
the sorrowful assembling of his comrades around a grave whose
inscription would certainly contain an error in spelling.

Yet Buckley always went forward.

It may be argued by professors, philosophers, and gentlemen who
have never been shot at that this constituted bravery of
a high order. Buckley himself did not so regard it.
To him, bravery meant the absence of fear; and because
fear attended him like an unpaid creditor, he considered himself
a fraud. He believed that some day, at the decisive
moment, his legs would declare their independence and carry him
toward safety. That they had not yet done so he
attributed to temporary confusion.

His comrades, perceiving only the public performance and not the
private rehearsal, regarded him with affectionate awe.

"Bob ain't got nerves," said Ranger Pete Harkness one evening
in the Blue Coyote Saloon. "Doc says nerves is strings
or wires or somethin' that tells a man when to
jump. Bob must've been born without the apparatus."

"Triggernometry," corrected Slim Wingo, who had attended school through three
rainy seasons. "That's the scientific name for it."

"Triggernometry is how you aim a cannon over a hill,"
said Sergeant Ames.

"Then Bob's got it," Slim concluded triumphantly. "He can aim
himself over any hill where trouble is waitin'."

Buckley sat among them, polishing the cylinder of his revolver
as a duchess might examine a questionable pearl. He smiled
because smiling was expected. Within him, an icy little voice
observed that these idiots would probably stand around making similar
remarks on the day he failed.

The Blue Coyote was full of heat, tobacco smoke, card
players, flies, and that strenuous ease peculiar to border towns
after sunset. Las Cruces del Norte—called simply Cruces by inhabitants
too occupied to pronounce municipal grandeur—had lately enjoyed a cattlemen's
convention, a barbecue, two disputed elections, and a bullfight. These
gently social relaxations had left only seven wounded and one
man married against his better judgment. The town therefore considered
itself unusually tranquil.

Bud Dawson, proprietor of the Blue Coyote, leaned across the
bar. Bud was a square-built man with pale eyes, a
red moustache, and the serene manner of one who had
survived his enemies by lending them poor liquor.

"You boys heard Leandro Garcia swore he'd come back for
me?" he asked.

Pete Harkness inspected the amber corruption in his glass.

"Garcia swears considerable," he said. "Most of it ain't grammatical."

"He says I informed on him."

"Did you?"

"I conveyed some facts to parties legally interested."

"You informed."

Bud wiped the bar. "The rangers got every crossing watched.
Garcia couldn't get through if he came disguised as the
President's grandmother."

At that moment an old Mexican woman shuffled past the
saloon door. She wore a black shawl drawn over her
head, carried a reed basket, and leaned upon a crooked
stick. Her back was bent into the interrogatory posture of
extreme age. She paused in the glare outside, coughed, and
peered in with eyes that seemed filmed by eighty years
of smoke, sorrow, and economy.

No one noticed her except Buckley.

He noticed everything that might conceal a weapon.

The woman passed on. Buckley's constricted lungs released a little
air, and he condemned himself for suspecting grandmothers.

Consider that at that time the border was guarded with
impressive vigilance. At each ford sat armed men whose duty
was to prevent Leandro Garcia, bravo, cattle thief, gambler, singer
of murderous love songs, and general disturber of the peace,
from entering Texas. The bridges were watched; the trails were
patrolled; even the goats crossing the shallows were looked upon
with official distrust. But law, like civilization, has rarely been
able to decide what to do with an old woman
carrying eggs.

That afternoon the ancient traveller had approached the Santa Rosa
ford. She complained of rheumatism, taxes, ungrateful sons, and the
price of corn. She opened her basket to show six
eggs, four peppers, and a saint's medal. The guards apologised
for troubling her and helped her across.

A mile north of the river she entered a mesquite
thicket. There the years fell from her shoulders. The shawl
came off; the bent spine straightened; the grey wig was
tossed upon a cactus; the reed basket disclosed, beneath a
false bottom, a short revolver and a knife. Leandro Garcia
emerged from old age with the agility of a devil
escaping a sermon.

He was a slight, handsome man, yellow-brown and black-eyed, with
a moustache drawn as delicately as two strokes of ink.
Vanity shone upon him more brightly than gun oil. He
considered vengeance one of the fine arts and himself its
principal academy.

At eight o'clock he entered the rear door of the
Blue Coyote.

Bud Dawson had just finished saying, "No Mexican peacock is
goin' to make me nervous," when a shot broke the
sentence.

Bud turned slowly, his expression wounded more by contradiction than
by lead, and descended behind the bar with his hands
among the glasses. Garcia stood in the doorway, smoke lifting
from his pistol.

"For Leandro Garcia," he said, "there are no guarded borders."

Then Buckley moved.

To the spectators he moved with magnificent calm. He rose,
drew, fired once, and struck the lintel an inch from
Garcia's head as the fugitive vanished. In truth, Buckley had
experienced in those three seconds an entire lifetime of terror,
including infancy, funeral, and adverse judgment. The room stretched; the
lamplight went white; his fingers seemed borrowed from a corpse.
But the habits of duty, which are often stronger than
the passions of men, put the revolver into his hand.

Bud groaned behind the bar.

"Shoulder," he said. "Plum got me, but he ain't collected."

Buckley reached the rear door. Beyond it an alley ran
toward the dishonourable fringe of huts, corrals, ash heaps, and
tethered burros that adorned the eastern jowl of Cruces. Garcia
was already beyond sight.

"He'll make for Arroyo Seco," Sergeant Ames said, coming up
behind. "We'll get horses."

"No time," Buckley answered. "He's afoot. I'll trail him."

"Alone?"

Buckley's heart made a convulsive effort to climb into his
throat.

"He'll hide if a troop comes. Bring men around the
south end."

He stepped into the alley before they could reply.

"Look at that," breathed Slim Wingo. "Bob's chasin' Garcia like
a hungry coyote after a lame hen."

"Horatius," said Pete.

"Who?"

"Old Roman feller that held a bridge by himself."

"What load did he use?"

Buckley did not hear the discussion. He was beyond the
last houses, following Garcia's narrow boot marks through moonlit dust.
He walked quickly because speed looked like confidence, and because
if he slowed he feared he might stop.

Now, while destiny, that extravagant stage manager, was moving one
armed man and one terrified ranger toward Arroyo Seco, she
had already placed a woman there. The woman was searching
for a snake.

Her name in the playbills was Alvarita, Queen of the
Serpent Tribe, Priestess of the Coiled Death, and Fearless Daughter
of the Jungle. Her name at baptism had been Alviry
Jones.

Her mother, a competent widow in Illinois, still addressed letters
to "Miss Alviry Jones, Snake Business, Travelling," and enclosed recipes
for pickles.

Alvarita was the principal adornment of Professor Zeigler's Grand Consolidated
Congress of Wonders, then encamped outside Cruces. Each night she
entered a cage containing seven rattlesnakes, two copperheads, a sluggish
boa constrictor, and Kuku, the great python; Kuku, the magnificent;
Kuku, eleven feet of painted muscle and professional ill temper.
She lifted them, draped them about her, kissed their cold
heads, and rebuked them in a voice that caused strong
men in the front row to remember forgotten prayers.

She feared nothing.

This assertion is not offered in the careless manner of
advertisements. Alvarita truly lacked the ordinary mechanism of alarm. Thunder
amused her. Horses that bit were invited to reconsider. Once,
when a drunken cowboy fired through her tent, she took
the pistol from him and bent the barrel against a
wagon wheel—not greatly, perhaps, but sufficiently for narrative purposes. She
had looked into the lidless eyes of serpents so long
that danger seemed to her merely another performer waiting for
a cue.

Alvarita was dark, though not by ancestry; the sun, theatrical
greasepaint, and a constitutional friendship with vivid colours had achieved
the effect. Her hair was black and heavy; her eyes
could strike sparks from masculine vanity; her figure possessed that
fortunate decision of line which artists describe at length and
women observe at once. She wore a crimson shirt-waist, a
short riding skirt, high boots, and a silver-studded belt. Against
the dun landscape she moved like a banner carried into
a quarrel.

Kuku had escaped.

At sundown a careless boy named Melvin had left the
python's travelling chest unlatched while pursuing a pie. By the
time remorse overtook appetite, Kuku was gone. His trail led
from the circus camp through grass, across a dry field,
and toward Arroyo Seco.

Professor Zeigler wrung his hands.

"Find him!" he cried. "He is insured against fire, flood,
railway collision, and the act of Providence, but not against
walking away!"

"I'll bring him," said Alvarita.

"Take men."

"Men scare him."

"Kuku is a python!"

"And men are men. I know which is harder to
manage."

So she went alone, carrying a forked catching stick, a
coil of rope, and a lantern she did not light
because the moon had spread a pale, sufficient wash across
the country.

Arroyo Seco had once contained a stream. The stream, after
viewing the prospect, had perished. What remained was a winding
ravine cut between chalky banks, cluttered with boulders, huisache, prickly
pear, and cottonwoods that maintained the melancholy expression of creditors
at an empty estate. The moon poured silver into it,
but shadows lay under the banks in black, secret pools.

Alvarita descended, examining the dust.

"Kuku!" she called. "Kuku, you overgrown bootlace! Come here!"

Something rustled in the brush. She approached without hesitation and
found only a lizard departing on urgent private business.

Far behind her, Buckley reached the rim of the arroyo.
Garcia's tracks disappeared down the slope.

The ranger stopped.

Fear had been accumulating in him with compound interest. In
the saloon there had been men and lights. Across open
country there had been at least distance. Here were shadows,
rocks, and a man who could wait behind either with
a pistol. Buckley's breath came shallow. Cold moisture crept beneath
his collar. Every cactus developed the profile of an assassin;
every pale stone became a crouching shoulder.

He wanted to turn back.

No philosophy, anthem, or ancestral obligation can make those five
words less true. He wanted safety with an appetite more
passionate than hunger. He pictured Sergeant Ames arriving soon; Garcia
could be surrounded; there was no sense in one man
walking into ambush. Reason presented a hundred excellent arguments, each
suspiciously well dressed.

Buckley drew both revolvers.

"You're goin' down," he told himself.

His legs felt filled with wet sand.

"You blamed counterfeits, you're goin' down."

He went down.

Slowly, slowly, placing each boot where the gravel would not
slide, he descended into the ravine. His guns pointed ahead,
but his wrists trembled. A twig snapped somewhere below. He
froze. Silence followed—a thick, listening silence that magnified the beat
of his heart until he believed Garcia must hear it
and aim by the sound.

Then a woman's voice floated through the darkness.

"Kuku!"

Buckley nearly fired at the moon.

Meanwhile Alvarita had found clearer signs of the python: a
broad undulation through dust, a patch of polished scale caught
against thorn, and the deep impression where Kuku had turned
around a stone. She knelt to examine them.

"Señorita," said a voice behind her, "do not move."

Alvarita looked over her shoulder.

Leandro Garcia stood ten feet away. His revolver had been
lost while descending the bank; a loose stone had betrayed
him, sending weapon and dignity in different directions. But the
knife remained hidden inside his sleeve, and his empty hands
were spread with plausible courtesy.

"You startled my snake," said Alvarita.

"I ask pardon."

"You'll ask something else if you interfere again."

Garcia smiled. He knew her from the circus posters pasted
along the customs shed. Those chromatic documents portrayed her reclining
among pythons while a volcano erupted in the rear for
no explained reason.

"Ah, the famous Alvarita. I have seen you kiss death."

"You mean Kuku. He doesn't like strangers."

"I also," Garcia said, "do not like strangers. Especially those
who may tell the rangers where I go."

Alvarita rose. Her catching stick lay on the ground beyond
her boot.

"What's that to me?"

"You will remain here until I have gone."

"Will I?"

Garcia's smile thinned. "If you cry out, I will be
obliged to hurt you."

Alvarita laughed.

It was not the timid silver tinkle prescribed for heroines.
It was a frank, warm laugh with a contemptuous edge.

"You haven't got a gun."

"I require none for a woman."

"That opinion," said Alvarita, "has shortened several careers."

She stepped toward the catching stick.

Garcia caught her wrist.

The action was swift, but Alvarita was swifter. She twisted,
struck him under the chin with the heel of her
hand, and nearly tore free. He seized her other arm,
and they stood locked for an instant—she straight and furious,
he astonished that a creature advertised in tights should possess
the resistance of a wagon spring.

Then Buckley came around the bend.

He saw Garcia holding the woman. Garcia saw two revolvers
levelled at him. Alvarita saw a tall man in ranger
khaki emerge from shadow with a face pale beneath its
tan and eyes fixed upon danger as though danger were
an appointment he regretted but intended to keep.

"Let her go," Buckley said.

His voice was steady. This surprised all three of them,
Buckley most of all.

Garcia released Alvarita and lifted his hands.

"My pistol is lost," he said. "You will not shoot
an unarmed man."

It was the frontier's mad code that a gentleman might
perforate an enemy in the midst of any active disagreement,
but must become hospitable the instant that enemy misplaced his
weapon. Buckley knew Garcia was a murderer. He knew, too,
that searching him at gunpoint would be sensible. But sense
and mandatory pride have rarely occupied the same pair of
boots.

Buckley holstered one revolver, then the other.

"We'll settle it even," he said.

Fear screamed within him. It showed him Garcia's fist crushing
his mouth, Garcia's boot finding his ribs, Garcia recovering the
lost pistol, Garcia triumphing. Buckley's hands were numb. He had
never wanted his guns more desperately.

"Don't be a fool," Alvarita said. "Keep him covered."

Buckley turned his eyes toward her.

She looked at him.

There are moments upon which science has no mortgage. Let
no one ask or presume to tell precisely what crossed
the moonlit air between the ranger and the snake woman.
Electricity is a convenient servant in these affairs, but it
has been overworked by poets. Magnetism may be proposed, though
magnets do not ordinarily cause cowards to become lions or
serpent queens to discover eyelashes.

They merely looked.

Buckley saw the dark fire of her eyes, the proud
lift of her head, the crimson oasis of her shirt
against the grey ravine. He saw fearlessness embodied—fearlessness natural, careless,
and complete. And suddenly, with no trumpet from heaven and
no administrative delay, that fearlessness was his.

The constriction left his chest.

His mouth was no longer dry. His hands warmed. The
thousand prophetic deaths that had always clamoured before danger fell
silent as birds at eclipse. Garcia stood before him merely
as a slender man with two hands, two feet, and
an excessive opinion of himself.

Buckley smiled.

It was the first entirely fearless thing he had ever
done.

Alvarita, beholding that smile, felt a disturbance more alarming than
rattles, thunder, biting horses, or drunken cowboys. Her heart made
one quick, foolish leap. The ravine seemed suddenly lonely. Garcia
seemed suddenly dangerous. Buckley's shoulders appeared broad enough to have
been designed by Providence for protective purposes.

She lowered her eyes.

This had never happened to her before. She was indignant,
but not so indignant as she ought to have been.

Garcia saw only that the ranger had discarded his guns.

"You are brave," he said.

"Maybe," Buckley answered. "Come find out."

Garcia rushed him.

The first blow glanced from Buckley's cheek. He stepped aside
and drove his fist into Garcia's body. Garcia folded, recovered,
and struck twice with feline speed. Buckley blocked one, received
the other on his jaw, and felt no fear—only information.
The fact that a fist hurt seemed simple and almost
interesting.

They circled in the white dust.

Garcia fought with the agile malice of a street knife:
feints, kicks, darting blows, and sudden retreats. Buckley fought as
rangers and farm boys often did, with less ornament but
an earnest desire to conclude the discussion. Garcia's knuckles split
Buckley's lip. Buckley's fist landed over Garcia's eye. The Mexican
staggered against the bank, slipped, and came forward crouching.

Alvarita stood beside the rock, hands clasped beneath her chin.

"Look out!" she cried.

There was a new note in her voice. It was
not command. It was concern, tremulous and personal, dressed in
the ancient costume of helplessness. Had Professor Zeigler heard it,
he would have cancelled the evening performance and summoned a
physician.

Garcia's hand moved to his sleeve.

Moonlight ran along steel.

Buckley saw the knife appear. Once, the sight would have
turned his bones to lead. Now time opened spaciously before
him. He saw the position of Garcia's wrist, the angle
of the blade, the left foot planted too far forward.
He felt no haste.

Garcia struck.

Buckley caught his wrist, turned beneath the arm, and bent
it backward. The knife fell. Garcia snarled and clawed for
Buckley's eyes. Buckley released him, stepped half a pace, and
delivered the good plain border knock-out blow—unscientific, inelegant, and so
thoroughly persuasive that Garcia descended upon the dust without requesting
clarification.

He lay still.

Buckley looked at his fist.

"Well," he said.

Alvarita ran to him.

"Are you hurt?"

"Split lip."

"You might have been killed!"

The Queen of the Serpent Tribe took his arm as
though the ravine were an ocean and he the sole
remaining piece of dry land. Buckley looked down at her
hand. It was a strong hand, browned by the sun
and marked with a faint white scar from a copperhead's
tooth. Yet it rested on his sleeve with the uncertain
pressure of a girl crossing ice.

"You told me to keep him covered," he reminded her.

"I didn't know you were going to fight like that."

"Like what?"

"Like—like a man with no sense at all."

"That's how it's generally done."

Voices sounded up the ravine. Lanterns winked among the trees,
bobbing downward in a line. Sergeant Ames arrived first, followed
by Pete, Slim, two deputies, and a volunteer who had
come because he mistook the expedition for a barbecue.

They found Garcia unconscious, the knife in the dust, Buckley
with a bleeding lip, and Alvarita still holding the ranger's
arm.

Pete Harkness surveyed the scene.

"Horatius," he said solemnly.

Slim nodded. "Plum triggernometrical."

Sergeant Ames kicked Garcia's knife away. "Bob, you fool, why'd
you throw down your guns?"

"He didn't have one."

"He had a knife."

"Found that out."

Ames looked at the unconscious fugitive and then at Buckley.
"You always did have more sand than brains."

Buckley waited for the familiar inward accusation, the whisper that
he had been terrified and had merely concealed it. It
did not come. He looked at Garcia with calm curiosity
and understood that something had altered permanently. Courage, which he
had so long manufactured by painful personal labour, now appeared
to have established itself in him without rent.

The discovery did not make him proud. It made him
wonder why natural heroes received so much credit for enjoying
themselves.

Garcia stirred and cursed weakly.

The deputies bound his hands.

"What about you, ma'am?" Sergeant Ames asked Alvarita. "How'd you
come to be out here?"

"I was looking for Kuku."

Every man except Buckley stepped back.

"Kuku who?" asked Slim.

"My python."

"How much python?"

"Eleven feet."

The volunteer remembered another engagement and departed.

"You mean there's a snake eleven foot long loose in
this arroyo?" Pete demanded.

Alvarita, who forty minutes earlier would have laughed at the
panic, moved nearer to Buckley.

"He's gentle with me," she said. "But perhaps—perhaps I shouldn't
be alone."

Pete stared. He had seen Alvarita at the circus put
her head between the jaws of a boa. That she
should now speak in the modest conditional seemed to him
a greater wonder than any exhibited under canvas.

"We'll hunt it," Buckley said.

"Oh, no!" Alvarita exclaimed. "Not tonight. It's dark."

The men looked at the moon, which illuminated the ravine
with the impartial brilliance of a government inquiry.

Buckley suppressed a smile.

Sergeant Ames assigned two men to take Garcia back to
town. Pete and Slim followed, escorting the prisoner and discussing
whether Buckley's victory had been pugilistic, geometrical, or Roman. Ames
went to report at the circus camp. In the untidy
manner of fate, Buckley and Alvarita were left to walk
toward Cruces together.

They climbed from the arroyo.

The country lay wide and silver before them. Mesquite shadows
spread like black lace; far away, the lamps of the
town burned in a trembling row; a coyote called from
the hills with the lonely arrogance of one who had
contributed nothing to the evening but wished to be remembered.

Alvarita still held Buckley's arm.

"You can let go," he said gently. "Ground's level."

"I know."

She did not let go.

For a while they walked without speaking. Buckley, who had
faced bullets under compulsion and Garcia without fear, now found
himself disconcerted by silence. This was not the old terror.
It contained warmth rather than ice and seemed less concerned
with death than with saying something foolish.

"Alvarita your real name?" he asked.

She hesitated. "Alviry."

"Alviry," he repeated.

"You laugh and I'll push you in a cactus."

"I like it."

She looked up quickly, then down again.

"My mother named me after an aunt who could lift
a flour barrel," she said. "Professor Zeigler changed it. He
said Alviry didn't suggest castanets."

"Do you play castanets?"

"No."

"Thought not."

"You're making fun of me."

"Wouldn't dare."

This was untrue. He dared everything now, but had begun
to perceive that courage includes the wisdom not to exercise
itself unnecessarily.

They passed through a patch of tall grass. Something moved
beside the path. Alvarita tightened her grasp.

"What was that?"

"Grasshopper."

"I don't like them jumping where a person can't tell."

Buckley stopped and faced her.

"You handle rattlesnakes."

"That's different."

"How?"

"They rattle first."

He considered this logic, which was perfect in emotional mathematics
and defective everywhere else.

A pale shape hung from a mesquite branch ahead. Alvarita
drew back with a gasp.

Buckley lifted it. "Piece of paper."

"I knew that."

"Sure."

"I did."

The crimson priestess of coiled death, whose picture had caused
hardened stockmen to purchase front-row tickets, now walked so close
to Buckley that their shoulders touched. Her voice had softened;
her commands had become questions; even her stride, once imperious
enough to lead an army or a circus parade, shortened
itself to his pace. Womanhood—by which the narrator means not
a universal condition but that special, theatrical variety employed by
moonlight and romance—had overtaken her with scandalous suddenness.

Buckley understood none of this in theoretical terms. He only
knew that Alviry's hand felt right upon his arm and
that he would have confronted Garcia's extended family to keep
it there.

They reached the road.

A small object moved across the moonlit dust.

Alvarita stopped.

The object advanced by arching itself delicately in the middle
and drawing its rearward portions toward its frontward ambitions. It
was green, furred, and perhaps two inches long. Nature, after
creating the python, had produced this diminished edition as a
marginal joke.

Alvarita stared.

The caterpillar lifted its head.

She screamed.

It was a scream of such crystalline sincerity that an
owl left a nearby cottonwood, a tethered horse in Cruces
broke its rope, and Kuku—who at that moment lay comfortably
digesting a rabbit beneath a warm rock thirty yards away—opened
one golden eye.

Alvarita leaped into Buckley's arms.

He caught her. She hid her face against his chest
and pointed with a trembling finger.

"Take it away!"

Buckley looked down at the caterpillar. The caterpillar, having no
reputation to maintain, continued across the road.

"That?" he asked.

"Don't let it come near me!"

"It's headed for Mexico."

"Make sure."

Buckley carried her around it.

He was laughing when she looked up, but she did
not push him into a cactus. Instead she blushed—a performance
more extraordinary than her serpent act and for which no
admission was charged.

Behind them, unseen in the ravine, Kuku slid from beneath
his rock and began the leisurely journey back toward the
circus, perhaps offended by neglect, perhaps summoned by hunger, or
perhaps moved by that rude instinct for comedy which survives
in creatures unburdened by human dignity.

Ahead, the lamps of Cruces waited. Garcia would go to
jail; Bud Dawson would recover and enlarge his wound in
every retelling; the rangers would add another impossible chapter to
Buckley's fame. No one would ever know that until that
moonlit glance he had been afraid. No one, perhaps, needed
to know. For courage compelled by fear had served him
honourably, and courage without fear would serve him more comfortably.

As for Alvarita, Queen of the Serpent Tribe, she remained
in Buckley's arms until the caterpillar was at least twenty
feet behind them and two international boundaries removed in her
imagination.

Thus were the scales balanced: the coward received fearlessness, and
the fearless woman received a protector; Kuku regained his liberty
for one moonlit evening; and a harmless caterpillar, unpaid and
unnamed upon the programme, performed the final miracle.

Viva la pequeña oruga!

The lake was red that evening—not the wicked red which
comes before a gale, but a placid, rosy glow spread
over Lake Pontchartrain as though the sun had spilled a
glass of claret upon it. The pines stood black and
fragrant along the shore; the galleries of Mandeville blossomed with
white gowns and fluttering fans; and the New Orleans steamer
came puffing toward the pier like a fat old gentleman
trying to arrive before supper.

If you lived in Mandeville, you went to meet that
steamer.

You might expect nobody, and you might despise everybody aboard;
still, you went. The steamer brought letters, millinery, gossip, Americans,
and other disturbances from the world beyond the lake. Mandeville
considered that world loud, hurried, and insufficiently acquainted with good
manners. Yet the town watched for it every afternoon.

On this particular evening, Grandpere Colomes sat near the end
of the pier, tapping his cane against the planks. He
wore a coat of cream linen, although the day was
warm enough to melt wax, and his silver mustaches curled
fiercely upward.

Beside him stood his granddaughter, Juanita Alvarez.

Ah, Juanita! You should have seen her then—petite, straight as
a young cane, with black hair coiled low and a
pair of Spanish eyes which could be velvet at one
moment and gunpowder the next. Her dress was pink, with
a golden rose at her breast, and Mandeville knew very
well why she wore those colors.

Grandpere knew, too.

"Dat foolish boat," he said, glaring beyond the pier.

A slim white craft skimmed over the red water. At
its mast fluttered two ribbons, one pink, one gold, and
along its bow, in letters newly painted, shone the name:

JUANITA.

At the tiller sat Mercer Grangeman, blond, broad-shouldered, brown from
sun and salt. He was an American sailor, which, in
Grandpere's judgment, placed him somewhere between an untrained mule and
a mosquito.

"Grandpere," said Juanita sweetly, "the Juanita is the fastest boat
on the lake."

"Pah!"

"She defeated Monsieur Dufour's Étoile Tuesday."

"Monsieur Dufour had indigestion."

"And Captain Renaud's Belle Créole on Friday."

"No wind."

"There was an excellent wind."

"Too much wind."

Juanita's mouth curved, but before she could reply, Mercer swept
his cap from his yellow head and brought the boat
neatly alongside.

"Good evening, Mademoiselle Alvarez."

"Good evening, Captain Grangeman."

Grandpere struck the pier with his cane.

"Captain! Every Americain who puts one foot in a boat
becomes captain. If he put one foot in a kitchen,
perhaps he become chef!"

Mercer laughed, which made matters worse.

The regatta was to be held the following afternoon. It
was Mandeville's proudest summer ceremony, more important than elections and
nearly as important as Mass. Nine boats would race from
the pier to the red buoy near Goose Point, round
it, and return. For three weeks sails had been patched,
hulls scraped, wagers whispered, and old quarrels polished until they
shone like family silver.

But nobody talked of anything except the Juanita.

It was not merely that Mercer had named his boat
for Grandpere Colomes's granddaughter. He had painted her in Juanita's
colors. He had also announced, with American plainness, that he
meant to win.

That night beneath the Alvarez gallery, while Grandpere snored behind
green shutters, Juanita leaned over the rail. Below her, among
the oleanders, Mercer stood bareheaded.

"If you win," she whispered, "I shall marry you."

Mercer looked up. Moonlight made him very pale and very
young.

"Juanita—"

"If you lose, I shall perhaps marry you anyway. But
not so soon."

A shutter creaked.

"Go!" she breathed.

He went over the garden wall so quickly that even
the lizards were astonished.

Mandeville knew of the promise by breakfast.

Do not ask how. A town may have no ears
that you can see, yet hear a whisper through three
walls and a feather pillow. By noon, ladies who had
never spoken to Mercer were wishing him victory, while gentlemen
who had wagered against him began to regret their patriotism.
Grandpere alone stalked through the streets declaring, "Un Americain, pah!"
to anyone who had leisure to listen.

The afternoon came hot and magnificent. Flags flew from the
pier; children climbed pilings; parasols opened like bright flowers. The
lake lay blue under a white sky, smiling with that
innocent expression which should always make sensible people suspicious.

The nine boats took their places.

There was the Étoile, the Belle Créole, the St. Tammany,
the Dauphine, the Petit Coq, the Marie Louise, the Pelican,
the old Bon Espoir, and last of all the Juanita,
light and sharp, her ribbons dancing.

Juanita stood beside Grandpere. She wore white today, but the
golden rose was at her breast, and pink streamers trembled
from her hat.

The signal pistol cracked.

Away they went!

White sails leaped; booms swung; water curled silver at the
bows. The crowd shouted each name as though noise itself
might drive a favorite forward.

"Étoile!"

"Belle Créole!"

"Juanita! Juanita!"

Mercer caught the first clean breath of wind. His boat
sprang ahead, not like a heavy thing made of timber
and canvas, but like a gull suddenly remembering its wings.
The St. Tammany followed; then the Étoile; then all the
rest in a white, straining crescent.

"One boat's length!" cried somebody.

"Two!"

"Bravo, Mercer!"

Grandpere became interested in the fastening of his glove.

At the red buoy the Juanita rounded beautifully. She leaned
until her pink ribbon kissed the water, rose laughing, and
turned for home with a lead of fifty yards.

Then the lake changed her mind.

From the south came a line of darkness—at first no
wider than a mourning ribbon, then a wall, violet at
the base and green-black above. The pines ceased murmuring. The
gulls fled inland. Across the bright water ran a sudden
shiver.

Old Captain Valcour lifted his face.

"Storm," he said.

The word passed through the crowd, low and swift.

"Storm."

"But the boats—"

"Mon Dieu, the boats!"

The first gust struck the pier, tearing hats away and
turning parasols inside out. Women cried aloud. The flags snapped
like pistols. Far upon the lake, the nine white sails
fluttered in confusion against the advancing blackness.

Grandpere's glove fell unnoticed.

"Juanita," he said, and for the first time his voice
was old.

She did not answer. Her eyes were fixed upon the
small white boat that bore her name.

The rain came sideways. It beat the galleries, hissed in
the sand, and erased the opposite shore. Waves rose where
smooth water had been, shouldering one another like angry men.
Their crests showed white teeth. Thunder rolled so heavily that
the pier trembled.

Out on the lake, victory ceased to matter.

Mercer saw the Petit Coq lose her jib and swing
helplessly broadside. He saw the Bon Espoir, with two old
men aboard, wallowing behind the fleet. Ahead lay Mandeville, but
between the boats and the pier the gale drove straight
across their course. To continue racing was to scatter them.
To scatter was to lose them.

Mercer lowered his sail.

"What is he doing?" cried Juanita.

The Juanita turned away from home.

For one dreadful instant Mandeville believed he had lost control.
Then he reached the Petit Coq, shouted something, and pointed
westward. Her crew obeyed. He drove on toward the Belle
Créole, then the Étoile. One by one the boats shortened
sail.

Captain Valcour clutched Grandpere's arm.

"He knows! There is an inlet beyond Marigny Point. Pines
shelter it. If they follow—if they stay together—"

But could they hear him in that shrieking wind? Could
they see the channel through the rain? The lake sprang
at them, wave after wave. Sails vanished and reappeared like
pale ghosts.

Mercer brought the Juanita across the fleet. He stood braced
at the tiller, his blond hair plastered against his forehead,
one arm raised. He shouted until his throat burned. The
St. Tammany fell into line. The Dauphine followed. Then the
Marie Louise, the Pelican, the damaged Petit Coq.

"One, two, three," counted Juanita.

Lightning opened the lake from end to end.

"Four, five, six—"

The Bon Espoir was missing.

"No," whispered someone. "There! Behind the Étoile!"

Seven. Eight. Nine.

All nine!

Mercer led them west, not homeward but toward the narrow
sheltered water behind Marigny Point. The Juanita climbed each black
ridge and plunged down in a smother of foam. Once
her mast bent so low the pink-and-gold ribbons disappeared in
the waves. A groan rose from the pier.

Then rain swallowed everything.

There was nothing to do but wait.

Mandeville went indoors because the wind drove it there, but
it did not turn away. Faces watched from every shutter.
Candles were lit in the church. Women prayed in French,
Spanish, and English, taking no chances upon the language best
understood in heaven. Grandpere stood at the end of the
pier until two men forced him beneath the warehouse roof.
Juanita remained beside him, white and composed, her wet golden
rose crushed beneath her fingers.

The storm spent its fury quickly, as violent tempers sometimes
do. The thunder retreated. Rain softened to a whisper. Presently
people crept from doorways as chickens creep from shelter after
a downpour.

The lake was gray now, heaving and sullen.

No sails appeared.

An hour passed.

Then a child cried, "Là-bas!"

Near Marigny Point a white triangle emerged. Another followed it.
Then another.

The church bell began to ring.

Slowly they came: one, two, three, the sails bruised and
torn, the boats weary as horses after battle. At their
head moved the Juanita, her gold ribbon gone, her pink
ribbon hanging by a thread.

"But every boat?" demanded Grandpere. "Count!"

We counted. Mandeville counted with one breath.

Eight.

There were only eight.

A silence fell sharper than the storm.

Then from behind the Pelican appeared the little Petit Coq,
towed by the Bon Espoir.

Nine.

Nine boats, and every sailor!

How Mandeville shouted! Men flung hats into the muddy water;
women laughed and sobbed; children danced upon the planks. The
town cheered the Étoile, the Bon Espoir, the Petit Coq—but
when the Juanita touched the pier, the cry became one
great rolling name.

"Mercer! Mercer! Mercer!"

He stepped ashore soaked, bleeding at one temple, and looking
surprised by the uproar. Juanita passed Grandpere without asking permission.
She went straight into Mercer's arms before all Mandeville, which
pretended to be shocked and missed not one instant of
it.

"You did not win," she said, laughing through tears.

"No."

"You turned away from the finish."

"Yes."

"And saved them all."

"They saved one another. I only showed the way."

She kissed him.

Grandpere approached very slowly. The crowd made room. He inspected
Mercer from wet boots to bleeding forehead, as though searching
for some final defect.

"At least," he said, "you handle a boat—not altogether badly."

"Thank you, sir."

"And you have courage, perhaps."

"Perhaps."

Grandpere's mustaches twitched.

"For an Americain," he pronounced, "you are almost equal to
a Frenchman."

That was surrender, though he would have challenged anyone who
called it so.

The following week the Alvarez house shone with lanterns. Pink
ribbons crossed the gallery; golden roses crowded the tables; fiddles
sang until the pines themselves seemed to dance. There was
a betrothal supper, and Grandpere drank three toasts to France,
two to Juanita, and, after midnight, one to Mercer.

As for the lake, she lay quiet beneath the moon,
pretending innocence.

But Mandeville knew her moods. Mandeville also knew that courage
has no single country, that love is a stubborn sailor,
and that sometimes an outsider must turn away from victory
before a town will welcome him home.

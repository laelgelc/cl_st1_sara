### The Crossing

When Isaiah Fomitch came to fetch me, dawn had not
yet separated the snow from the sky. The yard, the
sheds, the paling, and the bare willow by the gate
were all of one bluish-grey colour; and only the kitchen
chimney, from which a thin smoke crept reluctantly, showed black,
as though somebody had drawn a charcoal line upon a
dirty sheet of paper.

Isaiah sat in a low sleigh drawn by a creature
which he called a pony, though it resembled in turn
a donkey, a woolly poodle, a mortar set upon four
sticks, and, when it moved, a millstone rolling uphill. It
was brown about the muzzle, white at the belly, and
covered elsewhere by mats of winter hair, from which pieces
of straw projected. One could not look at it without
thinking that Providence, having begun to make a horse, had
been called away and had permitted an underpaid apprentice to
finish the work.

"Get in," said Isaiah. "The devil take all ceremoniousness. We
have forty-two versts before dinner and perhaps eternity afterward."

He was clerk to the excise office, but drink had
reduced his official dignity to a polished brass button on
a coat which had long ceased to possess either colour
or shape. One half of his beard was red and
pointed, whilst the other, singed years ago when he had
fallen asleep over a lamp, remained short, grey, and curled
close to his cheek. Beneath his left ear he carried
an enormous swelling, smooth and violet, which pushed his head
sideways and gave him the expression of a man perpetually
listening to some distant but offensive remark.

His eyes, when sober, were intelligent and melancholy. That morning
they were moist and amiable.

"You have been drinking," I said, getting into the sleigh.

"Not drinking. Fortifying. It is a distinction recognised by science,
the Church, and all persons who travel before sunrise."

He tucked a rug about my knees, struck the pony
with the ends of the reins, and cried:

"Go on, professor! Though you have no education, at least
make an effort!"

The pony shook its whole body as a wet dog
does, turned its head to see whether obedience were unavoidable,
and started.

We were going to visit Isaiah's elder brother, Pavel Fomitch,
who had once taught arithmetic and Church Slavonic at the
district school. For twenty-three years Pavel had demonstrated fractions, corrected
handwriting, and endured the sons of merchants; then consumption had
settled in his chest, and the Zemstvo had settled him
in a village twenty versts beyond the Volga, where he
lay dying in the house of a deacon's widow.

The previous evening a messenger had brought a note. It
contained only six words: "If Isaiah wishes, let him come."
Isaiah had read it, laughed, cursed the messenger for bringing
bad news at supper, and gone to the tavern. Two
hours later he came to my lodging and asked me
to travel with him.

"He taught me everything," he said as we passed the
last houses of the town. "Reading, swearing, subtraction, and the
doctrine that mankind is ungrateful. I perfected myself chiefly in
the third and fourth."

"You mean subtraction?"

"I mean mankind."

The road ran across bare fields from which the snow
had almost disappeared. Here and there, in hollows and beneath
the black hedges, winter still lay in soiled heaps, like
bandages cast off by a convalescent. The earth was soft
above and frozen beneath. The runners sliced through brown slush,
struck stones, and scraped over patches of sand with a
sound that set one's teeth on edge.

The sky hung low. Heavy clouds, blue-grey at their lower
edges and leaden above, moved from the west without haste,
as though condemned to labour and already exhausted by it.
There was no wind, yet the damp entered one's sleeves,
collar, boots, and thoughts. Ravens walked in the furrows with
lowered heads. Pools had gathered upon the road; in each
pool the cheerless sky looked at itself and seemed dissatisfied.

Spring in those parts did not arrive like a young
girl with flowers. It came like a sick tax collector:
coughing, muddy, suspicious, and demanding everything at once.

For the first ten versts Isaiah talked without interruption. He
abused the road, the district administration, physicians, school inspectors, and
his pony, whom he alternately called Socrates, Vermin, and Your
Excellency. Then his speech became philosophical.

"There are men," he said, "who, if they see another
man drowning, will pull him out. A simple impulse! They
take off their coat, jump in, risk pneumonia, save him.
Admirable. But afterward they will not rest until they have
instructed him how he ought to breathe, dress, vote, plough,
pray, and educate his daughters. Rescue is not enough for
them. They must rearrange his soul."

"Perhaps they mean well."

"A flea also means well when it bites. It must
live."

He stared at the pony's tail and sighed.

"My brother meant well. He spent his life improving boys.
The boys improved into shopkeepers, policemen, two usurers, and one
veterinary surgeon. Now Pavel coughs blood into a rag, and
they send him Easter greetings printed in Moscow."

"You are unjust."

"Naturally. Justice is expensive. An excise clerk cannot afford it."

Near the village of Rakitino we overtook a peasant cart
sunk to its axles in mud. A woman in a
man's boots was pulling at the horse's bridle, whilst an
old man pushed from behind. Isaiah stopped, descended with groans,
and helped them. He cursed the old man for choosing
the road, the woman for being born, the horse for
obeying bad masters, and me for not pushing in the
correct manner. When the cart came free, the woman crossed
herself and offered him two eggs.

"Keep them," Isaiah shouted. "Do you imagine I am a
saint?"

Then, when we were again seated, he looked back until
the cart disappeared.

"They have no oats," he said. "Did you see the
horse's ribs?"

"You refused the eggs."

"I dislike eggs."

This was untrue. At the inn, an hour later, he
ate three.

By ten o'clock the fields had widened, the clouds had
sunk lower, and the damp had become a fine rain
which neither fell nor floated but simply existed everywhere. The
pony's coat darkened. Isaiah's swelling shone. We met nobody. The
road, pale and tortuous, crawled before us across the plain
and disappeared into a band of mist.

I thought of Pavel Fomitch waiting beyond the river. A
dying man waits differently from other men. For us each
delay is an insult, because we imagine time belongs to
us; for him an hour is no longer property but
judgment. A broken harness, a lame horse, floating ice—small things
to the healthy—may stand between the dying and a final
word. Yet even whilst I reflected thus, my principal concern
was whether my left boot had begun to leak.

At noon the land sloped gently downward. A dark line
appeared in the distance, and above it gulls turned like
scraps of paper tossed into the air.

"The Volga," said Isaiah. "Now we shall see whether Russia
permits us to cross herself."

The river was in flood.

Its broad surface, muddy and blue-black, stretched beneath the clouds
with the swollen, unhealthy look of a body covered in
sores. Great plates of ice drifted upon it. Some were
white and smooth, some yellow with sand, others grey and
porous, gnawed at the edges as though by enormous teeth.
They turned slowly in the current, mounted one upon another,
separated, collided again, and sometimes broke with a muffled report.
Along the bank lay shattered ridges resembling scales, bones, broken
crockery, and the cast-off armour of winter.

The ferry stood idle on the farther side. Near our
bank a little landing-place descended into the water, but half
of it had been carried away. Two empty tar barrels,
a coil of rope, and a black boat turned bottom
upward lay in the mud. Behind them was a low
hut, crooked as an old cap. No smoke came from
its chimney.

Three sleighs and a covered cart were already waiting.

"Ah!" Isaiah said. "Civilisation."

A tall man in a long raccoon coat walked up
and down the bank. He had a red moustache, close-cropped
grey hair, and a face of that official pinkness which
suggests warm rooms, nourishing soups, and habitual indignation. Each time
he reached the broken landing he looked across the river,
drew out his watch, and thrust it back into his
waistcoat as though punishing it.

This was the district judge, Nikita Petrovitch Sineokoff. I had
seen him once at a Zemstvo dinner, where he had
spoken for forty minutes on moderation and eaten until midnight.

Beside an open sleigh sat the miller Mamaieff, a round,
soft man wearing a greasy astrakhan cap. His pale eyebrows
were raised in permanent astonishment, whilst his small, cunning eyes
contradicted them. His whiskers spread over his cheeks like two
flour brushes. He held a silver cigarette-case and smiled before
anybody had spoken, so as to be prepared for agreement.

The third traveller, a merchant's agent, slept in the covered
cart with his mouth open and took no part in
what followed, except to wake occasionally and ask whether the
boats had come.

The judge stopped before us.

"You are crossing?" he demanded.

"If the river has no objection," Isaiah replied.

"No boats. No ferrymen. Nothing! I have been here since
nine."

"Then Your Honour possesses accurate information."

"Where is the fellow?"

"What fellow?"

"The ferryman, the peasant, the man responsible. Kireelka!"

The judge turned toward the hut and shouted with such
violence that gulls rose from the ice.

"Kireelka! Kirill! You blackguard!"

For some moments there was no answer. Then something stirred
behind the overturned boat. A man unfolded himself from the
shelter of it and came toward us.

He was of uncertain age. He might have been thirty-five
or sixty. His back was bent, his narrow chest sunken,
and his long arms hung from a ragged brown armiah
like ropes from a broken well. His boots were wrapped
with bark and twine. A woollen cap, eaten by moths
and weather, covered one ear but not the other. His
beard grew in yellowish patches, and his face, creased, bruised
by cold, and pointed at the chin, had the appearance
of having been half masticated by some large animal which
had rejected him as insufficiently nourishing.

Yet his eyes were clear. They glanced once at the
judge, once at the ice, once at our sleigh, and
seemed to have learned everything required.

"Why are the boats not here?" asked the judge.

Kireelka took off his cap.

"They are coming, Your Honour."

"Coming! I can see they are not here. Why were
they not brought yesterday?"

"Yesterday the ice was standing."

"Then why not this morning?"

"This morning it began moving."

"I ask why the boats are not here!"

"They are on the other side."

The miller laughed softly and immediately changed the laugh into
a cough.

"You hear him?" said the judge, appealing to us. "That
is the peasant mind. I put a plain question. Where
are the boats? He answers: on the other side. As
though I had inquired where Constantinople is!"

"Just so," Mamaieff said. "They are very literal. Extremely literal,
and yet—if one may say so—not precise."

Kireelka looked at the miller's boots.

"Why did you leave them on the other side?" the
judge continued.

"The water took the small boat in the night. The
big ones could not be brought against the ice."

"Could not! There is always 'could not' with you. What
is your duty?"

"To keep the crossing."

"And have you kept it?"

"That depends upon circumstances."

The judge's moustache seemed to rise.

"Circumstances! Do you hear? He speaks of circumstances. You are
paid by the Zemstvo, are you not?"

"Six roubles for the season."

"Paid! Therefore you must perform your duty."

"The season is long," said Kireelka.

"What?"

"The season is long, Your Honour."

Isaiah climbed from the sleigh and embraced the air with
both arms.

"Your Honour," he said, bowing, "permit me to congratulate you.
Before us stands political economy in boots. Six roubles divided
by the season—that is not payment but a rumour of
payment."

The judge looked at him.

"Who are you?"

"Isaiah Fomitch, clerk, sinner, and younger brother to Pavel Fomitch,
formerly master at the district school."

"I remember Pavel. A respectable man."

"Consumption has made him even more respectable. We are going
to him."

"Then you should be serious."

"I am always serious. It is my face that has
been drinking."

The judge turned from him with visible distaste.

"What is to be done?" I asked Kireelka.

He put on his cap, then removed it again because
the judge was still looking at him.

"We wait."

"For how long?"

"Until they come."

"Soon?" called the merchant's agent from his cart.

"Soon," Kireelka replied.

The agent nodded and went back to sleep.

We unhitched the pony and tied it near the hut,
where it at once began chewing the rotten straw packed
between the logs. The judge resumed walking. Mamaieff offered us
cigarettes. Isaiah accepted three, placing two behind his ear.

The rain thickened, though no drops could be seen. Across
the river, half hidden by mist, low cottages and a
church cupola appeared and vanished. From that distant bank came,
at intervals, a shout or the dull knocking of wood.
Each time we all turned, but no boats emerged.

Waiting reveals men more thoroughly than danger. In danger they
may become brave, and bravery adorns them; but in waiting
they are deprived of adornment. They must live with their
stomachs, their boots, their watches, and the poor company supplied
by their own thoughts.

After half an hour the judge demanded tea. Kireelka explained
that there was neither tea nor samovar in the hut.

"What do you live on, then?"

"Just as God gives."

"God does not boil water."

"If Your Honour wishes, I will make a fire."

"With what?"

Kireelka indicated the hut.

The judge stared at him.

"You propose to burn the ferry station?"

"There is nothing else dry."

"There!" cried the judge. "That is precisely what I say.
Give a peasant public property, and he will burn it
to make tea."

"Yet if he does not burn it," said Isaiah, "Your
Honour reproaches him for failing to provide tea. A difficult
science, administration."

Mamaieff examined his cigarette.

"The fundamental problem," he said carefully, "is lack of foresight.
Our moujik lives only for the present hour. He does
not say in August: winter will come. He does not
say in the morning: perhaps at noon a distinguished official
will desire tea."

"Exactly!" said the judge. "No foresight. No conception of duty.
No gratitude."

He pointed at Kireelka, who had crouched near the overturned
boat.

"Look at him. We emancipated him. We gave him courts,
schools, local government. What has he made of himself? Rags!
Vodka! Theft!"

"Six roubles for the season," Isaiah murmured.

"I am speaking generally."

"Generally, six roubles becomes a very considerable sum."

The judge ignored him.

"I have administered this district for eighteen years. Do not
talk to me of peasants. I know them. If you
are gentle, they lie. If you are severe, they complain.
If you lend, they do not repay. If you do
not lend, you are called a bloodsucker. Education? They send
the worst child to school, and keep the strong ones
to work. Medicine? They hide the sick until he is
dying, then accuse the doctor. Roads? They steal the timber.
Bridges? They steal the nails."

Mamaieff nodded after every item.

"The nails especially," he said.

Isaiah looked toward the moving ice.

"Perhaps they eat the nails."

"Do not play the fool."

"I am not playing. Hunger performs stranger miracles than religion."

"Hunger!" the judge exclaimed. "Always hunger. Why are they hungry?
Because they drink."

"Why do they drink?"

"Because they are ignorant."

"Why are they ignorant?"

"Because they refuse education."

"And why do they refuse education?"

"Because they drink."

Isaiah smiled respectfully.

"A perfect wheel, Your Honour. The State might harness it
to a mill."

Mamaieff's eyes brightened, but he did not laugh.

The judge stopped walking.

"You are one of those clever gentlemen who excuse everything.
Sentimentality! You see a peasant in rags and immediately blame
the government. Has he no will? No responsibility? Take this
Kireelka. Last summer he pulled two women out of the
river. Risked his life. A courageous act; I praised him
before the whole village. Three weeks later he stole a
bridle."

Kireelka scratched his cheek.

"Did you steal it?" I asked.

He considered.

"It was lying loose."

"In Shustoff's stable," said the judge.

"The door was not fastened."

"You sold it in town!"

"There was need."

"You see?" The judge spread his hands. "Hero and thief!
What is one to do with such material?"

"One might first feed it," Isaiah said.

Kireelka glanced at him quickly.

The miller now entered the discussion with the confidence of
one who had discovered that his opinion was shared by
authority.

"The peasant character is complex," he said. "Very complex. One
cannot call it simply bad. No, no. He may rescue
a child, certainly; he may pray sincerely, certainly; he may
even return money found on the road. But give him
a sack of flour to watch, and—well!—circumstances arise."

"What circumstances?" I asked.

Mamaieff smiled.

"Family circumstances. Economic circumstances. Thirst. A cousin. There are always
cousins."

"Have you ever lost flour through theft?"

"Continually."

"Do your scales weigh correctly?"

His eyebrows climbed higher.

"My dear sir, a mill is not an academy of
metrology. There is dust, damp, friction—natural loss."

"That depends upon circumstances," said Kireelka.

For a second Mamaieff's smile disappeared. Isaiah slapped his knee
and coughed into his sleeve.

The judge looked sternly at Kireelka.

"You are becoming insolent."

"Just as Your Honour wishes."

"What does that mean?"

"Nothing."

"Then why say it?"

Kireelka lowered his head. Submission descended over him like a
familiar garment; yet beneath it his eyes remained awake, and
in their narrow light there was something which neither the
judge's office nor Mamaieff's flour had managed to grind away.

The wind came at last, a wet breath from the
river. It pushed the ice toward our bank, where the
plates rose, grated, and broke. A long black fissure opened
in the middle of the stream, closed, and opened farther
down. The river seemed to turn painfully beneath a scab.

Kireelka sprang up.

"Keep back," he said.

Nobody obeyed at first. A large floe struck the landing,
climbed upon it, and split. One half slid toward the
judge. Kireelka seized him by the sleeve and pulled him
aside. The block, thick as a stove, crushed a barrel
and carried its staves into the water.

The judge jerked his arm free.

"You tore my coat."

"It was the ice."

"You must have warning posts here."

"The posts went in the flood."

"Then put new ones."

"Yes, Your Honour."

"And a rope."

"Yes."

"And the landing must be repaired."

"Yes."

"Why has it not been repaired?"

"The water has not gone down."

"Excuses!"

Kireelka returned to his place. Isaiah watched him with a
crooked smile.

"Your Honour," he said, "he has just preserved the district
judiciary."

"It was nothing."

"Precisely. Had you been drowned, the annual report would also
have called it nothing."

Toward one o'clock our hunger began to make itself known.
It entered the conversation modestly at first, in pauses and
glances toward the sleighs. Then it grew bold. Mamaieff searched
under his seat and found an empty paper which had
once contained cheese. I discovered two peppermint lozenges fused together
in my pocket. The judge had left his provisions with
his servant, who, expecting the ferry to be working, had
driven by another road to meet him on the farther
bank.

Isaiah possessed a flask, but nothing solid.

"We shall have dinner across the river," the judge announced.

"Soon," said Mamaieff.

"Soon," the merchant's agent repeated, waking.

Kireelka stood on a hummock and shaded his eyes. Far
off, between moving fields of ice, two black specks had
appeared.

"The boats?" we asked together.

"The boats."

"How long?"

"That depends upon the ice."

"An hour?"

"Maybe."

"Two?"

"Maybe."

The judge groaned.

There are moments when all political convictions, distinctions of rank,
memories of honour, and hopes of immortality withdraw before a
piece of sausage. One may love mankind at noon and
hate it at a quarter past if breakfast was omitted.
Hunger does not refute philosophy; it merely takes the chair
and postpones the debate.

We crowded into the hut.

It was scarcely higher than a kennel. The stove had
fallen in, the window was stuffed with a woman's old
skirt, and muddy water covered half the floor. A plank
served as a bench. On the wall hung an icon
so blackened that the saint had disappeared, leaving only a
silver hand and one reproachful eye. The smell was compounded
of tar, wet wool, smoke, fish, and that sour human
poverty which persists after the human being has gone outdoors.

The judge sat on the plank. Mamaieff balanced himself on
an upturned pail. I stood near the doorway. Isaiah lay
upon a heap of rope, sighed loudly, and said:

"Pavel is waiting."

"We shall cross," I told him.

"He may not."

For the first time that day his voice was sober.

He took out his flask, looked at it, and put
it back.

"My brother never drank," he said. "That is why he
has no practice in dying. A drunkard dies a little
every day and learns the procedure."

The judge softened.

"Pavel Fomitch was a useful man. Strict, but useful. He
once punished my nephew for writing 'cow' with an unnecessary
letter."

"Perhaps the boy wished to improve the cow," Isaiah said.

"He taught discipline. That is what our schools lack now."

"Schools lack roofs."

"Roofs too. But chiefly discipline."

Mamaieff agreed that roofs without discipline were of limited benefit.

Kireelka entered and crouched by the doorway. As he bent,
his armiah opened slightly, and I saw beneath it, pressed
against his shirt, the rounded shape of something wrapped in
cloth.

Mamaieff saw it also.

"What have you there, my friend?"

Kireelka closed the coat.

"Nothing."

"Nothing round?" asked Isaiah.

"A rag."

The judge leaned forward.

"Show it."

Kireelka did not move.

"What is it?"

"Bread."

A silence followed. The word seemed to fill the hut.

"How much bread?" asked Mamaieff gently.

"A little."

"Bring it out," said the judge.

"It is black bread."

"Do you suppose we expected cake? Bring it out."

Kireelka hesitated, and this hesitation, no longer than a breath,
offended us more than a refusal would have done. We
were educated men, travellers delayed by public negligence, and hungry;
he was the ferryman, paid—however poorly—to serve the crossing. It
seemed to us for that instant that the loaf, by
being near us, had entered the public domain.

"Come, come," said Mamaieff. "We shall pay you."

Kireelka drew from beneath his shirt a small loaf wrapped
in a piece of sacking. It was dark, flattened, and
hard at the crust. One side bore the grey mark
of his shirt. He placed it upon the plank.

"Have you a knife?" asked the judge.

Kireelka produced a short knife from his boot.

"A clean one?"

He wiped it upon his sleeve.

The judge frowned, took out a penknife, and cut the
loaf. It resisted. He pressed harder; crumbs fell upon his
raccoon coat. The inside was wet, heavy, and nearly black.
Strands of chaff showed in it, and something which might
have been a husk or a beetle's wing.

Mamaieff examined his portion.

"What flour is this?"

"Rye."

"Rye? My dear fellow, this has passed near rye but
has not been introduced."

"Last year's," Kireelka said.

"Ground where?"

"At the mill."

"Which mill?"

Kireelka looked at Mamaieff.

"Yours."

Isaiah laughed so violently that his swelling shook.

Mamaieff smelled the bread.

"Impossible. There is sand in it."

"The millstones," said Kireelka.

"No, no. A millstone does not produce sand unless improperly
managed by the customer."

"How does the customer manage a millstone?" I asked.

"Indirectly," said Mamaieff.

We ate.

The bread was sour, gritty, damp, and possessed a bitterness
which remained at the back of the tongue. The judge
chewed with anger, as though compelling a witness to confess.
Mamaieff removed two pieces of straw, displayed them, and then
ate them with the rest. Isaiah swallowed his share in
three bites. I had intended to leave half for Kireelka;
but hunger persuaded me first to make the half smaller,
then to take one more mouthful, and finally to consider
that payment would compensate him.

The merchant's agent appeared in the doorway.

"Is there food?"

"Too late," said Isaiah. "Education has consumed it."

The last crust lay on the judge's palm.

He looked at Kireelka.

"Have you eaten?"

"I am not wanting."

"You fellows always say that, then fall ill. Here."

He broke the crust, gave a small piece to Kireelka,
and ate the larger himself.

"How much?" Mamaieff asked, opening his purse.

Kireelka looked at the coins.

"As you please."

"Come, name a price."

"It was bread."

"I know it was bread."

"It cost nine kopecks."

Mamaieff handed him ten.

"Keep the change."

Kireelka put the coin into his shirt.

"There," said the judge. "You see? Fair dealing. Nobody oppresses
you. If you have something useful, society pays."

Isaiah wiped crumbs from his beard.

"Society even allows a profit of one kopeck."

The bread revived us sufficiently to renew the question of
why Kireelka and men like him were poor.

"Their diet is appalling," said Mamaieff. "Such bread destroys initiative."

"Their lack of initiative produces such bread," replied the judge.
"Do not confuse cause and effect."

"Both, perhaps. A circle."

"The circle again," said Isaiah.

The judge crossed his legs.

"Let us be practical. What does this man require? First,
sobriety."

"Yes," said Mamaieff.

"Second, elementary education."

"Undoubtedly."

"Third, habits of thrift."

"Essential."

"Fourth, respect for law and property."

"Above all."

"Fifth, cleanliness."

"Certainly."

Kireelka listened with his head lowered.

"Do you hear?" the judge asked.

"Yes."

"Do you agree?"

"Just as you wish."

"No! Not as I wish. As reason requires. Do you
understand thrift?"

"To keep what one has."

"Exactly. Do you save money?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"There is none."

"You earn."

"Sometimes."

"And spend."

"Yes."

"On vodka."

"Sometimes."

"There!"

"Sometimes on taxes," Kireelka added.

"Taxes are not spending. Taxes are obligations."

"Sometimes on flour."

Mamaieff smiled indulgently.

"If one buys wisely, flour is not costly."

"Sometimes on the priest, sometimes on the doctor, sometimes on
rope, sometimes on a coffin."

"Why rope?" I asked.

"For the boats."

"The Zemstvo does not provide it?" said Isaiah.

Kireelka shrugged.

"Six roubles for the season," Isaiah repeated.

The judge became impatient.

"You always have an answer. That is the misfortune. Instead
of understanding, you answer. How many children?"

"Four living."

"Do they attend school?"

"The eldest did."

"Why did he stop?"

"He has no boots."

"Let him go barefoot."

"In snow?"

"In my boyhood we endured hardship."

"You had boots," said Kireelka.

The judge stared.

"How do you know?"

"Your father was a general."

Mamaieff bent over to conceal his smile.

"That has nothing to do with it. Do you wish
your son to remain ignorant?"

"No."

"Then send him."

"With no boots?"

"Earn boots."

"How?"

"Work!"

"At what?"

"At anything! You see, gentlemen? Always the same. We offer
a road, and they ask for boots. We offer a
school, and they ask for bread. Give the boots, and
they will ask for a coat. Give the coat, and
they will complain that the road is long."

"The road is long," Kireelka said.

Isaiah closed his eyes.

"My brother used to say that a child without breakfast
cannot decline a noun."

"Nonsense," said the judge. "I often worked before breakfast."

"Voluntarily?"

"Circumstances required it."

Kireelka raised his eyes, but said nothing.

Outside, wood struck ice with a hollow blow. Kireelka sprang
up and ran to the bank. We followed.

The boats had come nearer. There were two of them,
broad, black, and heavy, lashed side by side. Three ferrymen
stood in them with long poles. They were not rowing;
in such ice rowing was impossible. They pushed from floe
to floe, found channels, withdrew, advanced, and sometimes remained motionless
whilst the current carried the whole white confusion past them.
Their bodies appeared small against the river.

Kireelka shouted directions. The men answered, but wind and grinding
ice tore the words apart.

"Why do they not come straight?" demanded the judge.

"They would be crushed."

"They are drifting downstream."

"They must go down to come up."

"What absurdity!"

"The current."

"A competent man overcomes the current."

Kireelka gave no answer. He ran along the bank, seized
a rope, and threw one end over his shoulder.

For nearly an hour we watched the boats approach. It
was hard work, and dangerous. Once the nearer boat tilted
so steeply upon a floe that a ferryman fell to
his knees. Once the lashings groaned and the vessels separated
by an arshin before pulling together again. The men shouted
without ceasing. Kireelka answered from the bank, now commanding, now
pleading, now abusing them with an inventiveness that made Isaiah
listen admiringly.

At last a ferryman caught the thrown rope. Kireelka wrapped
it twice around a post and leaned backward. His thin
body became rigid; the armiah opened, revealing his ribs beneath
the shirt. The boats struck the ice at the bank,
vanished behind an upthrust slab, reappeared, and came grinding into
the muddy inlet.

We cheered.

The judge immediately looked at his watch.

"Three hours and forty minutes."

The ferrymen climbed ashore, wet to their thighs and breathing
heavily. One was an old man with a white beard;
another had blood running from a cut on his hand.
The third, a broad youth, asked Kireelka:

"Have you anything to eat?"

"Afterward," Kireelka said.

The judge approached.

"Who is in charge?"

The old ferryman removed his cap.

"Why were the boats not brought earlier?"

The old man looked at the river.

"There was ice."

"I am aware there was ice. I have eyes."

Kireelka began loading the sleighs before the inquiry could continue.
The runners were removed, luggage carried aboard, and the horses
led separately. Our pony refused the plank. It planted all
four legs, lowered its head, and became a furry anchor.

"Go on, professor!" Isaiah cried. "The Volga contains no excise
inspectors!"

The pony backed into the judge.

"Control your beast!"

"It recognises rank and seeks protection."

Kireelka covered the pony's eyes with his cap and led
it aboard. The animal trembled but followed him gently.

The judge's horse, a tall black mare with better breeding
and worse sense, reared, struck the plank, and nearly fell
between the boat and landing. Kireelka caught the bridle. The
broad ferryman pushed from behind, the old man pulled, and
Mamaieff called advice from a safe distance.

"Take care of the left fetlock! The left! No, the
animal's left!"

When all were loaded, it became clear there was no
room for Kireelka.

"You will come in the second passage," said the old
ferryman.

Kireelka nodded.

"Who will direct them?" asked the judge.

"They know."

"Do they?"

The old man's face darkened.

"We came."

"Yes, yes. Let us not waste time."

We stood crowded between sleighs, horses, ropes, and luggage. The
boats sank low. Muddy water washed over the floorboards. Isaiah
held the pony by the halter and whispered obscenities into
its ear in a tone of tenderness.

Kireelka untied us. For the first time since morning he
wore an expression not of submission, caution, or concealed laughter,
but anxiety. He looked at the ice, then at the
old ferryman.

"Keep above the long floe," he called. "Do not enter
the black water by the point. There is a whirl
there."

"We know."

"And when the white ridge turns, let it pass before
pushing."

"We know!"

Kireelka shoved the boat from the bank with both hands.
The pole slipped, and he went to one knee in
the water. He rose without looking at us.

The current took us.

At once the bank began to move away, and with
it Kireelka, the hut, the broken barrels, and the familiar
mud which had lately seemed so detestable. The open river
was worse.

Ice surrounded us. It scraped the sides with a slow,
tearing sound; it struck the bow and spun away; it
rose in blue-green masses through which bubbles and black weeds
could be seen. The ferrymen pushed with their poles, not
against the bottom, which was too deep, but against the
floes themselves. We advanced sideways, downstream, backward, then forward again.

Nobody spoke of education.

The judge clutched the side with one hand and his
coat with the other. Mamaieff's eyebrows had descended at last,
giving him a look of ordinary fear. The merchant's agent
prayed. I watched the water wash across my boots and
thought, not of mortality or the vanity of rank, but
of the papers in my inner pocket.

Isaiah alone continued talking.

"Easy, Socrates," he told the pony. "If we drown, remember
that ignorance is the cause. You should have attended school."

"Silence!" shouted the judge.

"Your Honour desires silence."

A floe struck us. The boats separated with a crack;
the lashings stretched, and one rope snapped. The black mare
screamed. All men, educated and uneducated, official and unofficial, seized
whatever lay nearest. Mamaieff embraced a sack of harness. I
caught the judge about the waist; he pushed me away.
Isaiah fell against the pony, which accepted him with philosophical
resignation.

The broad ferryman leaped across the gap and tied a
new rope.

"Pole!" cried the old man.

The wounded ferryman thrust. His pole broke.

For several seconds we drifted toward a wall of piled
ice. It towered above the gunwale in jagged sheets, white
at the top, transparent beneath, with black water pulsing in
its cracks. It did not seem to move, and this
immobility was more terrible than movement.

"Back! Back!" shouted everybody.

The old ferryman drove his pole between two floes. The
broad youth took another. Isaiah released the pony and helped.
I found a broken plank and pushed without knowing against
what. Even the judge seized a boathook.

The boats turned. The wall passed so close that it
brushed Mamaieff's cap from his head. He watched the cap
float away, held upright for a moment by its fur,
then vanish under the ice.

"My cap," he said.

No one comforted him.

The danger passed. A channel opened, long and black as
a road at night. The ferrymen entered it, and for
several minutes we moved freely. The farther bank emerged from
the mist: willows, wet sand, huts, the church cupola, and
three figures waiting near a cart.

The judge recovered his voice.

"They overload these boats."

The old ferryman said nothing.

"There ought to be regulations."

"There are," Isaiah replied.

"Then they ought to be enforced."

"Certainly."

"Life belts, for example."

"Upon the peasants or the regulations?"

The judge pretended not to hear.

As we neared the farther bank, the ice thinned. Little
fragments turned in the brown water like broken mirrors. The
boats grated upon sand. Men jumped ashore and pulled us
in.

The judge's servant ran down with a basket and a
fur rug. Behind him waited Mamaieff's overseer and the merchant
agent's driver. The smell of roast chicken escaped from the
basket. It was so rich, so warm, so civilised, that
all our morning discourse vanished before it.

"Thank God," said the judge.

He stepped ashore without looking back at the ferrymen.

Mamaieff's overseer offered him an old felt hat. The miller
put it on and lamented his astrakhan cap.

"A gift from my wife. Genuine Bokharan. Seven roubles."

"The ice is dishonest," said Isaiah. "It takes fur and
leaves heads."

We unloaded the sleighs. The judge's servant spread food upon
the seat: chicken, white rolls, boiled eggs, a jar of
pickled cucumbers. The judge invited us to take something, but
his invitation had the constrained tone of a man who
hopes generosity may be acknowledged without being exercised.

Isaiah shook his head.

"My brother is waiting."

I accepted an egg. Hunger, though temporarily silenced by Kireelka's
bread, had recovered its arguments.

The ferrymen prepared to return. The judge called the old
man.

"Take care going back. And tell Kireelka I shall report
the delay. Not vindictively, understand. For the good of the
service."

"Yes, Your Honour."

"He must learn foresight."

"Yes."

"And repair the landing."

"Yes."

"And put up warning posts."

"Yes."

Mamaieff, chewing chicken, added:

"Tell him also not to keep bread under his shirt.
It becomes damp."

The boats pushed off.

On the opposite bank Kireelka remained a small brown figure
among the grey ice and black mud. He had dragged
the broken barrel aside and now stood at the end
of the landing, his armiah flapping around his narrow legs.
Behind him the abandoned hut leaned toward the river as
though listening.

Isaiah shaded his eyes.

"Poor devil," he said.

The judge wiped grease from his moustache.

"Do not be sentimental. We paid him."

"We paid him for nine kopecks of bread," said Isaiah.
"Perhaps his hunger requires ten."

"That fellow will spend the kopeck on vodka."

"Then we have contributed to trade."

The judge climbed into his sleigh.

"Every discussion with you becomes absurd."

"Not every discussion. Only those concerning reality."

The boats had reached the first ice. The ferrymen pushed
slowly upstream before attempting the crossing. Kireelka followed them along
the bank. At first he waved his arms, apparently giving
directions. Then he made a trumpet of his hands and
shouted.

The wind carried away the beginning. We heard only:

"—bread!"

The ferrymen turned.

Kireelka ran farther along the shore, slipped, recovered, and shouted
again, this time with all the strength of his little
body:

"Matvei! When you come back, bring a loaf!"

The old ferryman answered something.

"What does he say?" asked Mamaieff.

Kireelka's reply came clearly over the water:

"The gentry have eaten my last one!"

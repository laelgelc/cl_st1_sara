## The Two Pumpkin Seeds

Of all the stories their father told them when the
wind went crying round the corners of the house, and
the fire snapped as if it had something private to
laugh about, the children liked best the story of the
Two Pumpkin Seeds.

They liked it because it was an old story, and
because they knew exactly where to interrupt it, and because
it had no moral.

"It has so," said Ruth.

"It has not," said Ned.

"It has a moral when papa tells it right."

"That is why it generally has none," said Ned.

Their father, who was sitting before the fire with Ruth
on one knee and no room at all for Ned
on the other, though Ned had made three attempts to
get there, said that a moral was a very serious
thing and ought not to be brought into the parlor
without wiping its feet.

"Mamma makes everybody wipe their feet," said Ruth.

"Not everybody," said Ned. "The minister didn't last Sunday."

"The minister brought no mud," said their father.

"He brought a tract."

"Well, that is different. A tract is supposed to be
clean, even when nobody reads it. But if you two
continue to discuss theology, I shall not tell about the
pumpkins."

"We weren't discussing theology," said Ned.

"What were you discussing?"

"The minister's shoes."

"That is how theology generally begins. Now be still."

So Ned lay down on the hearth-rug with his chin
in his hands, and Ruth tucked her feet under her
father's coat, although he said they were colder than two
little tombstones, and the story began.

Once upon a time there were two pumpkin seeds.

"Were they brothers?" asked Ruth.

"No."

"Sisters?"

"No."

"Then what were they?"

"They were pumpkin seeds," said her father. "That is enough
relationship for any seeds. They were brought in the same
paper packet from the village store, and the packet had
a picture on it of a pumpkin so large and
yellow and smooth that no pumpkin ever raised by mortal
man could have resembled it without being accused of pride."

"What was printed on the packet?" Ned asked.

"'Royal Mammoth Golden Globe, warranted to astonish the county.'"

"Did it?"

"The packet astonished the farmer, because it cost him five
cents more than he had expected. But the seeds did
not know that. They lay together in the warm, dry
kitchen through the winter, and listened to the kettle singing
and the clock ticking and the farmer saying that spring
would never come; and then, when spring did come, he
said it had come too early and would ruin everything."

"That's like Mr. Barlow," said Ned.

"It is like every farmer who understands his profession. At
last the good man carried the packet out to the
field, made a number of little hills in the rich
earth, and planted the seeds. These two fell into the
same hill, side by side, and there they lay in
the dark, with the soft dirt over them and the
rain coming down through it."

"Could they breathe?" Ruth asked.

"Perfectly."

"How?"

"Through their elbows."

"Seeds haven't elbows."

"That is why they can breathe so perfectly through them.
Nothing gets in the way."

Ned said this was nonsense and he would pound his
father with the sofa pillow if there was any more
of it; so their father promised to be as botanical
as a professor and went on.

One of the seeds was a good little pumpkin seed.
It was contented, obedient, modest, useful, industrious, and so perfectly
well-behaved that it was a wonder the other seeds did
not bury it deeper out of jealousy.

The other was proud and restless. No sooner did the
rain soak through its brown shell than it began to
think what an ordinary thing it would be merely to
grow into a pumpkin.

"What else could it grow into?" Ned asked.

"That was precisely the difficulty. But it hoped to overcome
it by ambition. It said to the good seed, 'Are
you going to lie here all your life?'

"'No,' said the good seed. 'I am going to sprout.'

"'And after that?'

"'I shall grow into a vine.'

"'And after that?'

"'I hope to blossom.'

"'And then?'

"'If the weather is favorable, and the bugs don't eat
me, and the farmer hoes me properly, I shall make
one fine pumpkin, or perhaps two.'

"'And what will become of them?'

"'They will be taken to the county fair, or put
into the cellar, or made into Thanksgiving pies.'

"'Pies!' cried the proud seed. 'Is that all your ambition?
To be cut up with a knife, stewed in a
kettle, mixed with milk and eggs and spice, poured into
a crust, baked brown, and eaten with a fork?'

"'I should consider it an honorable end,' said the good
seed. 'Pumpkins were made for pies.'

"'Speak for yourself,' said the proud seed. 'I was made
for glory.'"

"What kind of glory?" Ruth asked.

"Pumpkin-glory."

"There isn't any."

"There was going to be. The ambitious seed had invented
it. It had heard the farmer's daughters talking about morning-glories
climbing over the fence by the garden, with blue and
purple blossoms all over them; and it had determined to
climb there too, and hang out great golden flowers, and
become a pumpkin-glory."

"That's silly," said Ned.

"Ambition generally appears silly to persons lying in the dirt,"
said his father.

"But the other seed was lying in the dirt too."

"It was lying there modestly. There is a great difference."

By and by the two seeds swelled, split their brown
coats, thrust down roots and pushed up two green heads.
They came out of the ground on a warm morning,
with the sunshine pouring over the pumpkin patch and the
dew shining on every clod, and a crow sitting on
the fence waiting to see whether anything worth stealing had
appeared during the night.

The good little vine spread its leaves close to home.
It let the farmer hoe round it, and was thankful.
When he pulled up the weeds, it did not complain
that he had disturbed its privacy; when he banked the
earth about its roots, it did not say he was
smothering its individuality; and when the rain fell, it drank
what it could and did not ask why it had
not been lemonade.

The proud vine behaved quite differently. It looked across the
patch toward the garden fence, where the morning-glories were already
twining about the pickets, and it said, "That is my
proper society."

Then it began to crawl that way.

It had a hard journey. The farmer had planted it
to grow east, but the fence stood west; and the
vine turned round so often in deciding how to go
that it tied a small knot in itself and had
to spend the greater part of one afternoon getting loose.

"Could a vine untie itself?" asked Ned.

"With its fingers."

"Vines haven't fingers either."

"They have tendrils, which are fingers that have been left
out in the rain."

Ned reached for the sofa pillow, but Ruth caught it,
because the story had not yet reached the carving, and
she did not wish their father disabled beforehand.

The proud vine crept between two stones, over a board,
beneath a thistle and into a bed of burdocks. The
burdocks caught at it; the thistle scratched it; a hen
stepped upon it; and a field mouse nibbled one of
its youngest leaves. Still it pulled and stretched, and stretched
and pulled, and whenever it was ready to give up
it looked at the morning-glories waving on the fence and
said, "Society always demands sacrifices."

"What did the morning-glories say?" Ruth asked.

"They said nothing. They were rather exclusive."

"Why?"

"They had been planted from a packet that cost ten
cents."

At last the ambitious vine reached the fence. It sent
up one tendril, and the tendril curled round the lowest
rail. Then it pulled and pulled until the vine rose
out of the weeds. It sent up another tendril, then
another, and climbed slowly, painfully, proudly, leaf after leaf, until
its yellow blossoms looked over the top.

"Good morning," it said to the morning-glories.

"Good morning," said they, for they were polite, though not
cordial.

"I am a pumpkin-glory."

"A what?"

"A pumpkin-glory. I belong to the great family of decorative
climbers."

The morning-glories looked at one another. They had small blue
trumpets, delicate leaves and slender stems, while the pumpkin vine
had broad rough leaves, yellow blossoms as big as saucers,
and a stalk that looked like a green rope with
a bad temper.

"You seem rather—substantial," said one of the morning-glories.

"I come of an old and nourishing family," replied the
pumpkin vine.

"You are pulling the fence down," said another.

"That is because the fence was not built with sufficient
regard to art."

And the proud vine went on climbing.

Meanwhile the good vine remained in the pumpkin patch. At
first it made several blossoms and thought it might raise
a family of pumpkins; but it soon found that the
soil was dry and the summer hot, and that if
it tried to do everything it would do nothing very
well.

So it resolved to devote all its strength to one
pumpkin.

"There!" said Ruth. "That was good."

"Maybe it was selfish," said Ned. "It killed all the
little pumpkins for one."

"It did not kill them," said his father. "They merely
changed their minds."

"What did they change them into?"

"Blossoms again."

"That cannot happen."

"Not often. It was a very unusual season."

The one chosen pumpkin began as a green knob beneath
a blossom; then it became the size of a marble,
then of an apple, then of a large round cheese,
and still it grew. Every morning the farmer came to
look at it. Every evening his wife came to look
at it. On Sundays, after meeting, the neighbors came to
look at it and said they had seen larger pumpkins
in Vermont, although none of them had ever been in
Vermont.

The farmer fed the vine with rich earth and barnyard
dressing, and carried water to it when the well was
low; and the vine drank and worked and spread its
broad leaves over the pumpkin to shade it from the
hottest sun. It did not climb fences or seek fashionable
acquaintances. It did not even look toward the morning-glories. It
put all its strength into being useful.

The pumpkin grew until the farmer's youngest boy could no
longer put his arms round it; then until the oldest
boy could not; then until both boys together could not,
although they took hold of hands on one side and
stretched so hard on the other that their mother said
they would pull their shoulders out and be unable to
carry wood all winter.

Its green skin turned yellow, then orange, then a splendid
deep gold. It lay upon the earth as round as
the harvest moon, only considerably heavier and not so difficult
to reach.

"How heavy?" Ned asked.

"Two hundred and ninety-seven pounds."

"That's too heavy."

"Very well. It weighed one hundred and eighty-nine."

"That's still a great deal."

"Then we shall make it ninety-six pounds and lose the
county prize."

"No," said Ruth; "let it be heavy."

So it weighed two hundred and ninety-seven pounds, not counting
three ounces of dust and one grasshopper that sat on
it during the weighing.

When the county fair came, the farmer loaded the pumpkin
on a wagon. It took himself, his two sons, the
hired man, the school-teacher, the blacksmith, six neighbors and a
Democrat to lift it.

"Why a Democrat?" asked Ned.

"They could not find another neighbor."

"Are we Democrats?"

"You are nothing yet but children, and ought to be
thankful."

They rolled the pumpkin upon a bed of straw and
drove it to the fairgrounds. There were red apples in
pyramids, white onions in braids, carrots as long as canes,
cabbages with heads larger than the heads of the men
who raised them, jars of preserves, loaves of bread, quilts,
lace, butter, honey, and one cucumber so crooked that the
committee could not decide which end to measure from.

But the great pumpkin surpassed them all.

People crowded round it. Men tapped it with their knuckles
and looked wise. Women said they should hate to cut
it. Boys crawled behind it and pretended it had rolled
over them. A photographer took its picture, though the pumpkin
moved at the last instant and looked blurred.

"Pumpkins can't move for pictures," said Ned.

"Everybody moves for pictures. That is what photographers are for."

At last the judges came. They were three solemn farmers
with badges pinned to their coats, and they walked around
the pumpkin seven times. One measured it with a tape.
One weighed it. The third asked whether it had been
raised honestly, and the farmer said it had never told
a lie in its life.

Then they tied a blue ribbon to its stem and
awarded it first prize.

The good vine, lying far away in the quiet patch,
heard the news from a bee who had visited the
fair, and was pleased, though not conceited. It said, "I
am glad I did my duty."

"Did the vine get any prize?" Ruth asked.

"No. The farmer got four dollars."

"That wasn't fair."

"The vine had no pocket."

"It could have kept the ribbon."

"The pumpkin kept that until the farmer's wife took it
off to put in the family Bible."

Ned said that was not where ribbons belonged, and his
father said it was an old family Bible with room
in it for almost anything.

After the fair, the great pumpkin was carried home and
kept in the cellar until Thanksgiving week. Then the farmer's
wife, her two daughters, her sister from Ohio, and a
neighbor who had only come to borrow nutmeg set about
making pies.

They cut the pumpkin open.

"Did it hurt?" asked Ruth softly.

"Not after the first cut."

"Why not?"

"Because after that it knew there was no use making
a fuss."

They scooped out the seeds, pared away the rind, sliced
the golden flesh and filled the largest iron kettle. Then
it boiled and boiled, and simmered and simmered, and the
women stirred and stirred, until the kitchen windows were clouded,
the stove was red, the cat had gone under the
porch for air, and the hired man said he could
smell Thanksgiving halfway to the barn.

They mixed the pumpkin with milk and eggs, brown sugar,
ginger, cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg and a pinch of salt; and
they rolled crusts, filled plates and carried pies to the
oven until every shelf was full. Then they set more
pies on the warming shelf, more on the pantry table,
more on the windowsills and two on top of the
clock, though one of those stopped the clock at twenty
minutes past three.

"How many pies?" asked Ned.

"Forty-seven."

"From one pumpkin?"

"Forty-seven large pies, twelve small ones and a tart shaped
like a cow."

"Why a cow?"

"There was not enough dough left for a horse."

On Thanksgiving Day the family ate the pies, and praised
them. The farmer ate three pieces. His wife ate one.
The boys ate four apiece, because they had been warned
not to. The hired man ate six and then leaned
back in his chair and said he had always considered
pumpkin a light food.

So the obedient seed achieved its desire. It had grown
into a fine pumpkin, won a prize, and become good
Thanksgiving pies.

"And was eaten," said Ned.

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"All except the rind, the stem and the blue ribbon."

"Then I don't see that it gained much."

"You must wait for the moral."

"You said there wasn't one."

"I said it ought to wipe its feet."

The father shifted Ruth to the other knee, because the
first had gone to sleep, and continued.

Now the ambitious pumpkin vine on the fence had not
prospered so comfortably. It had climbed into the morning-glories and
spread over them until they complained they could not see
the sun. Its leaves caught the wind like umbrellas; its
weight bent the top rail; and the farmer, seeing it
there, said, "What in creation is that pumpkin doing on
the fence?"

"Seeking a wider sphere," said the vine.

But the farmer did not understand pumpkin language and thought
the leaves were rattling.

The vine made a single blossom, bright yellow and proud.
When the blossom faded, a little pumpkin formed in its
place; but instead of resting upon the earth, where pumpkins
commonly rest when they have any sense, it hung from
the fence.

At first it was round. Then, as it became heavier,
it began to pull out.

It pulled out, and pulled out, and pulled out.

"Show us," said Ruth.

Her father stretched his neck until his chin nearly touched
the ceiling.

"Not like that," said Ned. "You look like a goose."

"It was a goose-shaped pumpkin for several days."

"No, it wasn't."

"How can you know? You were not there."

"It ended long-necked. You always say so."

"Very well. It grew long in the neck. The part
nearest the stem stayed narrow, while the lower part swelled
into a lumpy golden head. It looked like a pear,
if a pear had been brought up carelessly; or like
a yellow bird without wings; or like the Sunday-school superintendent
seen upside down."

Ruth laughed so hard that she slid from her father's
knee, and Ned said the Sunday-school superintendent was bald, not
yellow.

"Only in summer," said their father.

The hanging pumpkin was not pleased with its shape.

"This is the result of elevation," it told the morning-glories.
"Persons in high positions are always misunderstood."

"You are stretching our vines," they replied.

"My influence naturally extends."

"You have broken three of us."

"Reform is often painful."

As the season advanced, frost blackened the leaves. The morning-glories
shriveled, and the pumpkin vine itself grew dry and brittle,
but the strange pumpkin remained hanging on the fence with
its long neck and swollen head.

The farmer did not take it to the fair. He
looked at it once, laughed, and said it was not
fit for anything.

"Then he was wrong," said Ruth, who knew what came
next.

"He was nearly always wrong before breakfast. His youngest boy,
Caleb, heard him and began to think. Caleb was twelve
years old and had freckles enough for a family of
five. He could whistle through his fingers, climb the barn
by the lightning rod, imitate every rooster in the township,
and never shut a gate unless somebody was watching."

"That isn't a good boy," said Ruth.

"He was not a good boy before dinner either."

Caleb wanted a jack-o'-lantern for Thanksgiving night. Ordinarily jack-o'-lanterns belonged
to Hallowe'en, but Caleb had been too busy on Hallowe'en
putting a dead snake in the schoolmistress's desk, and had
not made one.

"Did the snake bite her?" Ruth cried.

"It had been dead since August."

"That was mean anyway."

"It was educational. She had been teaching natural history."

On the afternoon before Thanksgiving, Caleb cut the crooked pumpkin
from the fence and carried it to the woodshed. The
pumpkin was delighted.

"At last," it thought, "I am to be admired. No
common pie for me. I shall become an ornament."

First Caleb sliced off the top of its head.

Ruth put both hands over her own head.

"Did he scalp it?"

"Completely. Then he scooped out its seeds and soft insides
with a bent iron spoon."

"Ugh," said Ruth.

"Do it again," said Ned.

"The pumpkin found the operation unpleasant, but told itself that
beauty requires suffering. When Caleb scraped the inside of its
long neck, it was ticklish; when he scraped the bottom,
it was not. Then he took his knife and cut
two triangular eyes."

The father made two triangles with his fingers and peered
through them.

"Were they both the same size?" asked Ned.

"One was as large as the pantry window and the
other as small as a buttonhole."

"That would look foolish."

"It looked distinguished. Next Caleb cut a nose shaped like
the ace of spades, and below it he cut a
wide mouth with six square teeth."

"Five," said Ruth.

"Six."

"You always say five."

"One had grown since last year."

"Pumpkin teeth don't grow after they're cut."

"Then Caleb cut five teeth and left a place where
the sixth had been knocked out in a political argument."

Finally Caleb put a candle inside, lighted it, replaced the
top and carried the lantern to the farmhouse.

It was a remarkable sight. The long narrow neck rose
from the round lower portion like the neck of some
dreadful yellow animal; the candlelight shone through the eyes; the
mouth grinned; and smoke leaked from beneath the scalped top.

Caleb put it on a small table near the front
door, where everyone entering the house must see it.

This was important, because the Thanksgiving gathering was to be
the largest the family had ever had. The farmer's father
and mother were coming, and his wife's father and mother,
and his three brothers and four sisters, and their husbands
and wives, and their sons and daughters, and their sons'
wives and daughters' husbands, and their babies, and two cousins
who were not certain which side of the family they
belonged to but came to dinner on both.

"How many people?" Ned asked.

"Seventy-four."

"That house wouldn't hold them."

"They did not all enter at once. Some were always
out looking for their hats."

There were uncles from Indiana, aunts from Ohio, cousins from
the next county, a great-uncle who was ninety and insisted
upon carrying his own chair, four young women named Mary,
six boys named John, three babies named after the farmer,
and one little girl named Euphemia who cried whenever anybody
pronounced it.

They arrived in wagons, buggies, carts and one old sleigh
set upon wheels. Horses filled the barn; coats filled the
bedroom; hats covered the bed; and mittens lay everywhere. The
kitchen boiled and baked. The dining-room table was extended with
every spare board in the house, including one shelf from
the pantry and a door borrowed from the summer kitchen.

When each guest entered and saw the jack-o'-lantern, he stopped
and laughed.

The old men said, "Well, I swan!"

The aunts said, "Did you ever?"

The young women squealed.

The boys declared they were not frightened and then pushed
one another nearer to it.

The babies stared at the glowing eyes and forgot to
cry, except Euphemia, who cried because everyone else had stopped.

The pumpkin was proud beyond measure. This, it thought, was
pumpkin-glory indeed. Nobody had laughed at the great prize pumpkin
at the fair in such a delighted manner. Nobody had
set the pie pumpkin by the front door. People had
merely eaten that one and forgotten it, while here the
whole family gathered to admire.

"See?" said Ned. "The bad pumpkin did better."

"It had not reached the worst part."

"I like the worst part," said Ruth, edging closer to
her father.

Dinner began at one o'clock and continued until nearly four,
because the first table had to eat before the second,
and the second before the third, while the smallest children
were fed in the kitchen whenever they could be caught.

There was roast turkey, goose, chicken, ham, potatoes, sweet potatoes,
turnips, onions, celery, pickles, cranberry sauce, bread, biscuits, preserves, apple
butter and pease.

"Peas," said Ned.

"Pease."

"P-e-a-s."

"In modern language, perhaps. These were old-fashioned pease and required
an extra e."

One turkey leg was so tough that the great-uncle tried
to cut it with his pocketknife; and when that failed,
Caleb carried it to the woodshed and clamped it in
the carpenter's vise.

"Did he eat it then?" asked Ruth.

"No. He sharpened a saw."

After dinner the grown people sat in the parlor and
talked about the relatives who had not come. The children
ran through the rooms. The young men stood by the
front door and tried to look as though they had
never eaten more than one piece of pie. The young
women stood near the window and looked as though they
had noticed this.

Every few minutes somebody wound up the music box, and
it played "Home, Sweet Home" so slowly that two of
the smaller boys fell asleep between the first "home" and
the second.

The jack-o'-lantern burned steadily on its little table. Its eyes
glowed. Its nose shone. Its mouth grinned wider than any
polite mouth ought to grin in a house full of
company.

Caleb had cut the mouth across a weak place in
the pumpkin's rind. The heat of the candle dried it,
and the weight of the long neck pressed downward. After
a while a little crack started at one corner of
the mouth.

"Which corner?" asked Ned.

"The left."

"Whose left?"

"The pumpkin's."

"It was facing the room."

"Then the room's right."

"That doesn't tell—"

"If you ask another question about its geography, I shall
move the crack to the back of its head."

The crack crept slowly upward. Nobody noticed it, because Aunt
Sophronia was telling how her rooster had crowed continuously for
three days after a thunderstorm.

"Why did he?" Ruth asked.

"He thought the thunder was another rooster challenging him."

"For three whole days?"

"Until he became hoarse. Then Aunt Sophronia wrapped his throat
in red flannel and put wet compresses on his head."

Ned said one could not put wet compresses on a
rooster without being pecked, and his father admitted that Aunt
Sophronia had to wait until the rooster was too weak
to resist.

While everybody listened, the crack in the pumpkin lengthened. The
mouth sagged a little at one end. The candle flame
jumped, and the strange face seemed to wink.

Caleb saw it and winked back.

Then the mouth split farther.

It had been a wide grin before; now it became
a dreadful, crooked gape, with one side running almost to
the pumpkin's ear, if it had possessed an ear, which
it did not.

At that moment the farmer's old mother came into the
hall.

She was a small, thin lady in a black dress
and white cap, and she had left her spectacles in
the parlor. Without them she could see large things but
not understand them, which is a condition not confined to
grandmothers.

She stopped before the jack-o'-lantern.

The candle flared.

The pumpkin's mouth cracked suddenly from the last tooth to
the base of its long neck, and the whole grinning
face appeared to open at her.

The old lady screamed.

"How loud?" Ruth whispered.

"So loud that a pan fell from a nail in
the kitchen, four babies began crying, a horse broke its
halter, Aunt Sophronia's rooster—who was twenty miles away—tried to crow,
and every young man in the room instantly pretended he
had not been frightened."

Then the grandmother leaned backward.

The farmer caught her.

His wife caught the farmer.

Aunt Sophronia caught the farmer's wife.

Two daughters caught Aunt Sophronia.

Their husbands caught the daughters.

Six cousins caught the husbands.

The great-uncle caught nobody, because he was still carrying his
chair, but three small boys caught him, and the chair
caught Euphemia.

"Did it hurt her?" asked Ruth.

"No, but somebody called her Effie, so she cried."

For one instant the whole family swayed together like a
field of corn in a high wind. Then somebody stepped
on the tail of the house cat. The cat sprang
upon the table where the water pitcher stood. The pitcher
tipped into Uncle Hiram's lap. Uncle Hiram jumped backward against
the parlor door. The door struck Caleb, and Caleb struck
the little table.

The jack-o'-lantern tottered.

Everyone saw it.

It leaned toward the wall, reconsidered, leaned toward the stairs,
balanced on the edge of the table, and then fell
to the floor.

"Smash?" said Ned.

"Smash," said his father.

The long neck broke in two. The lower part split
open. The candle rolled out, still burning, and set fire
to a small strip of carpet.

Three aunts screamed "Fire!"

Four uncles shouted "Water!"

The farmer's wife brought a dishpan.

A cousin brought the coffee pot.

The hired man brought a bucket from the pump, stumbled
over Uncle Hiram, and threw half the water upon the
grandmother, who had just recovered from the pumpkin.

The other half went down the great-uncle's boots.

Caleb stamped on the burning carpet, and the flame went
out. But all the doors had been thrown open, and
outside, in the barnyard, the farmer's largest pig had been
watching for an opportunity.

"How could he watch from the pigpen?" Ned asked.

"He stood on another pig."

"That pig would move."

"It had been trained as a platform."

The pig was a broad, pink, determined animal named Solomon,
although he had never shown wisdom except in discovering food.
During the confusion a little cousin had opened the pigpen
gate to see whether Solomon liked cranberries, and had forgotten
to close it.

Solomon came trotting across the yard.

He entered the kitchen, where he ate six biscuits, a
pan of turnips and the corner of Aunt Sophronia's shawl.
Then he followed the shouting into the hall.

There upon the floor lay the broken pumpkin.

Solomon stopped. He sniffed. He saw the candle, which had
been blown out by the bucket water but was still
warm and smelled of tallow. He saw the golden rind
and the soft pumpkin flesh scattered over the rug.

The farmer shouted, "Ketch him!"

The hired man shouted, "Head him off!"

Uncle Hiram shouted, "Who's got my hat?"

Caleb seized the pig by one ear, but Solomon had
never permitted his opinions to be changed through his ears.
He shook Caleb off, lowered his snout and began to
eat.

He ate the broken neck.

He ate the grinning mouth.

He ate the triangular eyes, one after the other.

He ate the nose shaped like the ace of spades.

Then, before anyone could prevent him, he swallowed the candle.

"Whole?" asked Ruth.

"Whole."

"Didn't it burn him?"

"It had gone out."

"Didn't the wick tickle?"

"Solomon sneezed once and continued."

The farmer, his two sons, the hired man, four uncles,
seven cousins, the blacksmith—who had come late but always enjoyed
catching pigs—and one young woman who wished to show she
was not afraid, all pursued Solomon round the hall.

He went under the dining-room table. They went after him.

He went between the great-uncle's legs. The great-uncle sat down
suddenly in the chair he had been carrying.

Solomon upset a stool, dragged a tablecloth six feet, swallowed
the last piece of pumpkin rind, and ran out through
the kitchen with twenty-three people behind him.

"How do you know there were twenty-three?" Ned asked.

"The grandmother counted them."

"She had lost her spectacles."

"She counted by sound."

Across the yard ran Solomon, with Caleb nearest, the hired
man next, the farmer next, the blacksmith next, the young
woman next, then all the uncles, cousins and children who
could find the door. The pig went round the pump,
under the wagon, through a flock of hens and into
the barn.

The hens flew in every direction. One landed on Uncle
Hiram's head and mistook his hair for straw. Another flew
through the kitchen window and settled upon the Thanksgiving table,
where it ate pease with perfect composure.

Solomon reached the pigpen at last, pushed open the gate
and lay down in the mud, looking so innocent that
the farmer could not prove anything against him except by
the smell of candle smoke upon his breath.

That was the end of the proud pumpkin.

"And the pig ate it candle and all," said Ruth,
satisfied.

"Candle and all," repeated her father.

The fire had burned low while the story went on.
Outside, the wind shook the bare branches against the windows.
For a minute the children listened, pleased with the comfortable
thought that they were indoors and that no jack-o'-lantern with
a splitting mouth stood by the door.

Then Ned said, "Well, what is the moral?"

"The moral," said his father, assuming his most serious voice,
"is that a good little pumpkin vine remains where it
is planted, attends to its duty, does not climb fences,
does not seek the company of morning-glories, and becomes a
prize pumpkin and honorable Thanksgiving pies; while a proud and
ambitious pumpkin vine wanders from home, pushes itself into society,
grows crooked, becomes a frightful jack-o'-lantern, terrifies its grandmother, breaks
its neck, sets fire to the carpet, and is eaten
by a pig."

"But the good pumpkin was eaten too," said Ned.

"That was different."

"How?"

"It was eaten with a fork."

"It was still eaten."

"By respectable people," said Ruth.

"Some of them ate four pieces."

"That does not make them unrespectable."

"The hired man ate six."

"He was hungry."

Ned sat up on the rug. "If you are a
pumpkin, what difference does it make whether people eat you
or a pig eats you? You are gone anyway."

"A very great difference," said his father. "People use plates."

"Solomon might have used one if they had given him
time."

"People say grace."

"Did all seventy-four say it?"

"At the same moment."

"Then nobody could hear anybody else."

"That is sometimes an advantage."

Ned considered. "The bad pumpkin had more fun. It climbed
the fence, talked to morning-glories, frightened gramma, started a fire,
and made everybody chase a pig."

"It also had its head cut off and its insides
scraped out."

"The good pumpkin was cut into forty-seven pies."

"Forty-seven large pies, twelve small ones and a tart shaped
like a cow," Ruth reminded him.

"So it had the worse time."

"But it won a blue ribbon," said Ruth.

"The farmer kept the ribbon."

"It became useful."

"The other one was useful too. It made everybody laugh."

"It made gramma scream."

"Maybe she liked screaming."

"Grandmothers do not," Ruth said firmly.

Their father looked into the coals and said there were
grandmothers and grandmothers, and he had known one who enjoyed
screaming so much that she kept a mouse in the
pantry for the purpose.

Ned reached at once for the sofa pillow.

"You promised," he said.

"I promised to be botanical. This is zoology."

The pillow struck him on one side of the head,
and Ruth, after a brief struggle between loyalty and delight,
pounded him from the other side with both hands. He
protested that this was the reward of all men who
attempted to instruct the young, but they continued until he
surrendered.

"All right," he said. "There is no moral."

"There is so," said Ruth, stopping.

"What is it?"

She thought for a while, with her head on one
side. "It is better to be eaten by people than
by a pig."

"Why?"

"Because it is."

"That," said her father, "is the soundest sort of moral.
No one can argue with it."

"I can," said Ned.

"You can argue with the multiplication table."

"Seven times eight is fifty-six."

"Not when pumpkins are concerned."

Ned lifted the pillow again, but just then their mother
came to the doorway and asked why the parlor sounded
as if fifty boys were throwing furniture.

"There are only two of us," said Ruth.

"Papa exaggerated," said Ned.

"I merely said it sounded like fifty," their father explained.

"It didn't," said Ned.

"It did when all fifty winked together. The room grew
perfectly dark."

Ned threw the pillow, Ruth squealed, and their father caught
them both; and the moral, which had been standing uncertainly
on the hearth-rug, took that opportunity to slip out of
the room without wiping its feet.

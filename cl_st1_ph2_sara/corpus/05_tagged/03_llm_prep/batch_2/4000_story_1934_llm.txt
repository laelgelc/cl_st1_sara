## LAWYER QUINCE AND THE LOST KEY

In Little Haven, where a man's reputation travelled more quickly
than the carrier's cart and generally arrived in worse condition,
there lived a shoemaker named Quince. His trade was the
mending of boots, but his profession, as he understood it,
was the interpretation of the law.

No one could say precisely where he had acquired his
legal knowledge. He possessed three old books without covers, a
bundle of newspapers tied with string, and a cousin who
had once swept the steps of a county court. Upon
these foundations he had built an authority which, from a
distance, had every appearance of solidity.

When a dispute arose over a boundary, a pig, a
broken promise, or the ownership of apples fallen from one
garden into another, the injured party commonly went to Quince.
He would lay aside his awl, pinch his chin between
finger and thumb, and say that the matter required careful
consideration. Careful consideration generally occupied as much time as was
needed to finish the boot in hand. He would then
deliver an opinion so roundly expressed that his customer went
away convinced, though not always enlightened.

For this reason he was known throughout the village as
Lawyer Quince.

His wife called him Abel.

On a warm afternoon in June, when the sunlight lay
sleepily upon the dusty road and even the flies seemed
unwilling to prosecute their business, Farmer Rose entered Quince's workshop.
He found the lawyer sitting in his shirt-sleeves, with a
boot between his knees and a row of wooden pegs
held in his mouth.

Rose was a broad, red man, whose face suggested that
he had been born in a state of annoyance and
had since found little reason to alter it. He removed
his hat, wiped his forehead with a blue handkerchief, and
looked toward the open door.

"This here's private," he said.

Quince removed the pegs from his mouth and arranged them
carefully on the bench.

"Anything told to me in consultation," he replied, "is sacred."

"Who said anything about consultation?"

"It is the legal term."

"Then consultation it is. But I don't want no gab
about it."

Quince leaned over and closed the lower half of the
door. The upper half remained open, since complete privacy was
not regarded favourably in Little Haven.

"You may proceed," he said.

Rose placed his hat upon a last, discovered the indignity,
and moved it to a stool.

"S'pose," he began, "a man was to find a young
feller trespassing in his stable."

Quince nodded gravely.

"S'pose this young feller had climbed over a wall after
dark, crept across the yard, and got into that stable
without leave nor licence."

"A clear trespass."

"S'pose he weren't after horses."

"That may affect the motive, but not the act."

"S'pose," continued Rose, looking out into the road, "he was
after the man's daughter."

Quince's eyebrows rose, and immediately lowered again under professional control.

"Ah."

"Not stealing of her," Rose added. "Courting."

"There are cases," said Quince, "in which the distinction is
narrow."

Rose breathed heavily through his nose.

"I caught him there last night. Him and her was
talking over the half-door as if the place belonged to
'em. So I pushed him inside, shot the bolt, and
there he is."

Quince put down his awl.

"Locked in?"

"Locked in."

"Since last night?"

"I give him breakfast."

"That was prudent."

"Bread and water."

"That was economical."

"What I wants to know is this: can I keep
him?"

Quince crossed one leg over the other and considered the
ceiling. Farmer Rose watched with the respectful impatience of a
man who desired approval rather than advice.

"The stable," said Quince at last, "is yours?"

"It is."

"The yard is yours?"

"Every brick and stone."

"The young man entered without permission?"

"He did."

"Then," said Quince, "speaking broadly, it is your stable, and
he had no right to be in it."

Rose's face softened.

"That's what I said."

"A man who puts himself where he has no right
to be," Quince continued, "must accept the natural consequences."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it's his look-out."

Rose gave a slow nod.

"S'pose he asked to be let out?"

"You are under no obligation to put yourself to inconvenience."

"S'pose the key was—mislaid?"

Quince looked at him.

"Mislaid?"

"Or lost."

"That," said the lawyer, after a pause, "would naturally prevent
you from unlocking the door."

Farmer Rose's left cheek twitched.

"And if folks asked questions?"

"You would state the facts. He trespassed. You secured your
property. The key, owing to circumstances beyond your control, cannot
at present be found."

Rose picked up his hat.

"Much obliged to you."

"One moment," said Quince. "Who is the young man?"

But Rose had already opened the door.

"A trespasser," he replied. "And it's his look-out."

He walked away with a steadiness which might have deceived
anyone less accustomed than Quince to the motions of a
triumphant man.

The lawyer watched him pass the window. A small uneasiness,
too indefinite to be admitted as thought, moved within him.
Then he returned to the boot and drove in three
pegs with unusual force.

Half an hour later Hogg the butcher appeared at the
doorway. Hogg was a little man of great public spirit
who assisted at every village quarrel without once making it
less difficult. He leaned over the half-door, folded his arms,
and regarded Quince with sparkling eyes.

"Afternoon, Lawyer."

"Afternoon."

"Busy?"

"As you see."

"Rose been here?"

Quince examined the edge of the sole.

"I never discuss clients."

"Ah," said Hogg. "Then he has."

Quince took another peg.

"Wonderful thing, law," Hogg continued.

"It is."

"Wonderful thing, a father's feelings, too."

Quince's hammer stopped.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. Only young Ned's got plenty of time to think
about both."

Quince turned slowly.

"What Ned?"

"Your Ned."

The shoemaker rose so suddenly that the boot dropped to
the floor.

"My Ned?"

"Ain't you heard? Rose caught him courting Celia in the
stable and locked him up. Whole village knows it. I
thought you knowed, seeing as Rose come here to make
it legal."

For perhaps five seconds Quince displayed that speechless admiration which
attends the discovery that one has sharpened the knife for
one's own throat.

"My son," he said at last, "is locked in Rose's
stable?"

"Since last night."

"And Rose came to me—"

"For advice," said Hogg. "Very respectful of him."

Quince snatched his coat from a nail and put it
on inside out. Hogg assisted him in correcting this error,
though not before two neighbours had paused at the window.

"He obtained my opinion under false pretences," Quince said.

"How?"

"He concealed a material fact."

"What fact?"

"That it was Ned."

Hogg considered this.

"You didn't ask till he was going."

"The identity of the party was essential."

"Didn't sound essential when you was telling him it was
his stable."

Quince buttoned his coat.

"You have heard only one side."

"I heard yours through the door," said Hogg. "It carried
beautiful."

By the time Quince reached Rose's farm, a modest crowd
had collected in the yard. Word that the lawyer was
coming to release his own son by legal argument had
proved more attractive than ordinary employment.

Rose stood beside the stable door with his thumbs in
his waistcoat. Behind the door, someone was whistling.

Quince pushed through the onlookers.

"Rose," he said, "open that stable."

Rose blinked at him.

"Why?"

"You know why."

"Key's lost."

A ripple of emotion passed through the crowd.

Quince drew himself up.

"This proceeding is unlawful."

"You said it weren't."

"I said nothing of the kind."

"You said it was my stable."

"So it is."

"You said he'd no right to be in it."

"He had no right to enter, perhaps, but—"

"You said it was his look-out."

"Under certain conditions."

"You said if the key was lost I couldn't unlock
the door."

"I did not advise you to lose it!"

"No," Rose agreed. "It happened natural."

There was another movement among the spectators. Hogg, who had
arrived by a shorter path, nodded warmly to Rose. His
admiration had transferred itself without effort.

Quince approached the door.

"Ned!"

The whistling stopped.

"That you, Dad?"

"Yes. Are you hurt?"

"Not a bit."

"Has he fed you?"

"Bread and water. There's plenty of water."

Rose looked modestly at the ground.

"Ned," called Quince, "you are being unlawfully detained."

"Am I?"

"Most certainly."

"Can I claim damages?"

"Do not complicate the issue."

Ned's face appeared at a small square opening above the
manger. He was a cheerful, brown-haired young man, and confinement
had not improved his respect for solemnity.

"Celia all right?" he asked.

"She will be," said Rose, "when you've give up courting
her."

"Then she won't be all right for a good while."

"You stop your nonsense and swear you'll leave her alone."

"Can't."

"Won't, you mean."

"That too."

Rose turned to Quince.

"There. You hear him. Obstinate."

"He takes it from his mother," said Quince.

At this moment Mrs Quince arrived at the gate, having
been informed of the affair by three separate messengers. She
took in the stable, the crowd, her husband, and Rose.

"Abel," she said, "get that boy out."

"The law," Quince replied, "is being obstructed."

"Then don't stand talking to it."

Rose raised one hand.

"I ain't unreasonable. Let Ned promise not to go near
my Celia again, and maybe I'll find the key."

"Maybe you'll find it now," said Mrs Quince.

"Can't be done."

She gave him a look under which smaller men had
confessed to lesser crimes. Farmer Rose, however, had a daughter
and an audience, and was therefore upheld by principle.

Quince coughed.

"We will leave," he said, "before the situation deteriorates into
violence."

"Whose violence?" asked his wife.

"Madam, let us leave."

As they turned away, Ned called from the opening.

"Don't worry, Mother. I'm learning the farming."

"You're in a stable," said Rose.

"First lesson, ain't it?"

"And you'll stay there."

"That'll give me time to get used to the place
before I'm your son-in-law."

Rose became extremely red. Ned resumed whistling, and the crowd
dispersed in a condition of contentment.

At supper that evening, Quince ate little and spoke much.
He explained that Rose's action was opposed to natural justice,
common usage, ancient liberty, and several other authorities which had
not occurred to him in the workshop.

Mrs Quince listened while cutting a piece of cheese.

"You told him he could do it," she said.

"On a hypothetical statement."

"You told him the stable was his."

"It is."

"And Ned had got into it."

"He had."

"And the key might be lost."

"That was not an instruction."

"It was near enough for Rose."

Quince pushed his plate away.

"The fellow behaved dishonourably."

"So did you, giving advice before asking who was to
suffer by it."

"A lawyer cannot permit family feeling to influence his judgement."

"No," said Mrs Quince. "He waits till afterwards."

Quince went out into the garden. The evening was still,
and voices from the road informed him that the village
had lost none of its interest. Somewhere beyond the roofs
lay Rose's farm, with Ned in the stable and Celia
doubtless shut in her room. He walked twice round the
gooseberry bushes, pausing at each corner as though a loophole
might grow there.

The following morning he called again upon Rose.

The stable prisoner was seated on an upturned bucket, visible
through the opening and engaged in a serious conversation with
Hogg, who had brought him tobacco.

"That ain't bread nor water," said Rose, appearing from the
barn.

"Tobacco's neither," Hogg replied. "Strictly speaking."

"Be off."

Hogg withdrew to the gate, where he could hear just
as well.

Quince produced a folded paper.

"I have reduced my revised opinion to writing."

"Don't want it."

"You are falsely imprisoning my son."

"He can go when he promises."

"You cannot impose conditions."

"I ain't imposing 'em. Door is."

"You deliberately detained him."

"My property. He trespassed."

"You cannot find the key because you have made no
proper search."

Rose thrust both hands into his pockets, brought out a
clasp knife, string, two nails, a coin, and a fragment
of turnip, and placed them on a barrel.

"No key."

"Search the house."

"Done it."

"The barn."

"Done it."

"The pond."

"A key in the pond wouldn't be no use."

"You know where it is."

Rose sighed.

"Lawyer, you told me plain. Owing to circumstances beyond my
control, it cannot at present be found."

From the gate came a sound which Hogg converted into
a cough.

Quince folded his paper with dignity.

"How long do you intend to continue this outrage?"

"Till he gives up Celia."

"Never!" said Ned.

"Then," Rose replied, "he's settled it hisself."

The days which followed were among the most legally active
in the history of Little Haven. Quince consulted his books,
wrote letters he did not send, and hinted darkly at
warrants. Rose continued to supply bread and water with scrupulous
regularity. Ned, who received additions to his diet through the
stable window after nightfall, gained rather than lost cheerfulness.

The stable became a recognised stopping-place. Men on their way
to work inquired after the prisoner's health. Children brought him
daisies. Hogg offered to sell tickets, but abandoned the proposal
when Rose demanded half.

Celia herself was not permitted into the yard. Her father
watched her with a vigilance which, like most excessive vigilance,
prevented him from observing anything else. She appeared obedient, spoke
little, and performed her household tasks with such calmness that
Rose suspected her continually without discovering what to suspect.

On the fifth evening he announced that she was to
go to her aunt at Market Barton.

"You'll start by the ten o'clock coach tomorrow," he said.

Celia lowered her eyes.

"Yes, Father."

"You'll stay there till you've got some sense."

"Yes, Father."

"And you ain't to write to him."

"No, Father."

Rose, who had prepared himself for tears and argument, was
unsettled by this submission.

"You understand?"

"Yes, Father."

"Then why d'you look like that?"

"What like?"

"As if you'd swallowed a joke."

Celia arranged the supper plates.

"I don't know, Father."

"You'd best not."

News of her departure reached Ned that night by means
of Hogg, whose sympathy for true love had increased after
Celia gave him sixpence.

Ned received the information quietly.

"I'll get out," he said.

"How?" asked Hogg.

"Door, likely."

"It's locked."

"So folks tell me."

"Key's lost."

Ned smiled.

"Is it?"

Hogg pressed him for an explanation, but the young man
lay down in the straw and declined to improve the
public knowledge.

The next morning the coach drew up before Rose's gate
in a cloud of dust. Celia's box was strapped upon
the roof. The driver sounded his horn, Rose shouted for
his daughter, and Mrs Rose searched the house.

Celia was nowhere to be found.

For ten minutes the farm was examined from attic to
dairy. Rose looked beneath beds, behind curtains, and once, in
the confusion of his feelings, inside a flour bin. The
coachman waited until his patience became visible, then drove away
with Celia's box and no Celia.

By eleven o'clock the whole village knew she had disappeared.

Quince did not hear of it immediately. He was at
work in his cottage workshop, which stood a little apart
from the house. Behind it was a narrow tool shed
containing leather, lasts, old harness, a broken chair, and all
those articles which a prudent man keeps because he has
not needed them for twenty years.

Shortly before the coach arrived at Rose's farm, Quince had
gone into this shed for a strip of hide. He
found Celia sitting upon the broken chair.

He stared at her.

"What are you doing here?"

Celia raised one finger to her lips.

"Miss Rose!"

She closed her eyes.

"Are you unwell?"

No answer.

Quince stepped nearer. Celia's breathing became deep and regular, though
perhaps a little too regular for nature.

"Celia," he said.

She murmured in her sleep.

"Dear me. How strange. I've come into Mr Quince's shed."

Quince stood very still.

"If only someone was to lock the door," she continued
faintly, "Father mightn't send me away."

The lawyer rubbed his chin.

"But, oh dear, Mr Quince would never think of that."

Quince closed one eye.

"And if he did," said the sleeper, "Father would make
him open it."

The lawyer glanced toward the yard.

"Unless the key was lost," Celia added.

There was a silence.

Quince took the key from the inside of the lock.

"Most singular case of somnambulism," he observed.

Celia's mouth twitched.

"Of course," she murmured, "someone would have to tell Ned
where the key was."

Quince leaned down. Her eyes remained shut as he whispered
several words. He then went outside, locked the shed, and
put the key where, for the present, it had every
legal appearance of being lost.

An hour later Farmer Rose came up the road at
a pace which left Hogg and the miller struggling behind
him. His face was darker than usual, and his hat
was on the back of his head.

"Quince!" he shouted.

The shoemaker looked up from his bench.

"Good morning."

"Where's Celia?"

"Your daughter?"

"You know she is."

"I know who she is. I do not know why
you ask me where."

Hogg reached the doorway and bent double, overcome by exertion
and anticipation. Miller Wicks arrived behind him, white with flour
and grinning broadly.

"She's here," Rose said.

"Indeed?"

"Someone seen her come this way."

"In Little Haven," said Quince, "someone sees everything, especially what
has not occurred."

Rose thrust his head into the workshop, looked under the
bench, and opened a cupboard.

"You require a warrant for that," said Quince.

"I require my daughter."

A faint sound came from the shed.

Rose wheeled round.

"What's in there?"

"Tools."

The sound came again, this time resembling a sneeze politely
suppressed.

Rose strode across the yard and tried the door.

"Locked!"

"It frequently is."

"Celia!"

"Father?" came her voice.

Rose shook the handle.

"What are you doing in there?"

"I don't know, Father. I must have fallen asleep."

Quince picked up his awl. Hogg straightened slowly and looked
from one man to the other with dawning reverence.

"Open this door," Rose said.

Quince tapped a peg into a sole.

"I should be delighted."

"Then do it."

"Unfortunately, the key is lost."

The miller turned away and blew his nose with great
violence.

Rose's face passed through several shades.

"You locked her in!"

"My shed," said Quince.

"What?"

"My property. She entered without leave nor licence."

"She's my daughter!"

Quince nodded sympathetically.

"A material fact, no doubt, but not one which she
mentioned on entering."

"You let her out!"

"She had no right to be there."

"Quince—"

"It is her look-out."

Hogg removed his cap. His admiration, after several unsettled days,
had returned wholly to the lawyer.

Rose advanced until his waistcoat almost touched Quince's apron.

"You done this a-purpose."

"Such an allegation would require proof."

"You know where that key is."

Quince searched his pockets. He brought out a knife, a
piece of chalk, waxed thread, and a short pencil. Having
placed these in a neat row, he spread his hands.

"No key."

"You've hid it."

"Lost," Quince corrected. "Owing to circumstances beyond my control."

The miller sat down on a log and gave himself
up to emotion.

Rose seized the workshop doorpost.

"I'll break the shed open."

"That would be damage to property," said Quince. "Hogg and
Wicks are witnesses."

"Glad to be," said Hogg.

"I'll fetch an axe."

"Aggravated damage."

"Then what am I to do?"

Quince considered the question with the calm of a physician
whose own family was well.

"There is one circumstance," he said slowly.

"What?"

"My son may know where the key is."

Rose stared.

"Ned?"

"Ned."

"How'd he know?"

"Professional confidence."

"You ain't got professional confidence with your own son."

Quince looked offended.

"A lawyer may have confidence with anybody."

From inside the shed Celia said, "It's rather dark, Father."

Rose pulled at the door again.

"Where is Ned?"

"In your stable, I believe."

"I know where he is!"

"Then the difficulty is reduced."

The farmer strode out of the yard, followed by the
entire company. Quince removed his apron and followed at a
more judicial pace. Along the road, people emerged from cottages
and gardens, attaching themselves to the procession as burrs attach
themselves to a coat. By the time they reached the
farm, Rose's domestic concern had acquired a full public attendance.

He went straight to the stable, drew a key from
beneath a loose brick by the pump, and unlocked the
door.

The crowd expressed no surprise. In Little Haven, a lost
key was expected to remember its whereabouts when properly encouraged.

Ned stepped out, brushed straw from his coat, and stretched.

"Found it, then?" he asked.

Rose pointed toward the road.

"Come with me."

"What for?"

"You know where Quince's shed key is."

Ned looked at his father.

Quince looked at the sky.

"Might," said Ned.

"You'll fetch it."

"On conditions?"

"No conditions!"

"Then I might forget."

Rose's fists closed.

"Ned," said the miller mildly, "don't strain a man past
his natural capacity."

"What d'you want?" Rose demanded.

"Celia."

"She's in the shed."

"I know."

"You can let her out."

"So I can."

"Then do it!"

Ned smiled.

"Gladly."

They returned to Quince's cottage, the procession now increased by
Mrs Quince and Mrs Rose, who had met it on
the road and received explanations from six persons at once.

At the shed, Ned reached beneath a stack of cut
leather lying on a shelf beside the workshop door and
produced the key.

Rose made a choking noise.

"It was there all along!"

"Was it?" said Quince. "I searched my pockets."

Ned unlocked the shed. Celia stepped into the sunshine, looking
rested and composed. Her father seized her by the arm.

"You planned this!"

"Did I, Father?"

"You hid in there."

"I fell asleep."

"You told him to lock you in."

"I was asleep."

"You told Ned where the key was."

"I never spoke to Ned."

This last statement was so plainly true that it caused
Rose greater irritation than all the rest.

Ned offered Celia his hand. She took it.

Rose looked at the joined hands, at Quince, at Hogg,
at the miller, and finally at the crowd. No man
enjoys finding himself defeated, but to be defeated by one's
daughter, one's prisoner, and one's own borrowed argument before most
of one's neighbours is a severe test of parenthood.

"Very well," he said. "Go with him."

Celia looked up.

"Father?"

"Go on. If you choose him agin me, you ain't
no daughter of mine."

The laughter died. Even Hogg found the ground interesting.

Celia released Ned's hand.

"I don't choose anyone against you, Father."

"You've made a fool of me."

"No," said Quince. "Strictly speaking, Rose, you began that proceeding
yourself."

Mrs Quince nudged him sharply.

The miller rose from the log and dusted flour from
his knees.

"Come now, Rose," he said. "You locked up his lad,
and he locked up your girl. You lost a key,
and he lost another. Ned's out, Celia's out, and nobody's
a penny poorer except for the bread."

"And my dignity," Rose muttered.

"Haven't seen much use made of dignity in Little Haven,"
said the miller. "Likely it'll mend."

Hogg stepped forward, sensing that peace was gaining the advantage.

"Wicks is right. Finest joke I've heard of in years.
No harm meant on either side."

Rose turned upon him.

"You was laughing loudest."

"I laugh impartial," Hogg explained.

Ned stood beside Celia, his expression respectful but not repentant.

"I'll work for her," he said. "I've told you that."

"You've got no farm."

"I know a bit about stables now."

"You're a fool."

"Then I'll need a sensible wife."

Celia looked away, but not before her father saw her
smile.

Quince pinched a little snuff from a box and regarded
Rose over his fingers.

"There are," he said, "two possible courses. The first is
that we continue. You disown Celia, Ned marries her, Mrs
Rose weeps, Mrs Quince blames me, and the village talks
of nothing else until Christmas."

"They'll do that anyhow," said Hogg.

"The second course," Quince continued, "is that we acknowledge a
certain equality in the matter."

"What equality?"

"You made use of my opinion. I made use of
your precedent."

Rose rubbed his jaw.

"You're calling it quits?"

"I am prepared to consider such a settlement."

"You started it with your damned law."

"You started it with your stable."

"You told me I could."

"You failed to disclose the prisoner's name."

"You never asked."

"You concealed it."

"You never asked," Rose repeated.

Quince drew breath, but the miller placed one floury hand
upon his shoulder.

"The case is too deep for daylight," Wicks said. "Best
take it into the Haven Arms."

The suggestion altered the atmosphere. The Haven Arms stood across
the road, its door open and its cool interior pleasantly
visible. From within came the measured clink of glasses, a
sound which has settled more English disputes than abstract justice
can properly claim.

Rose looked toward the inn.

"I ain't paying," he said.

"Legal costs," said Quince, "should be divided."

"I'll stand first jug," said the miller.

Hogg immediately offered to assist in drinking it.

Mrs Rose went to her daughter and straightened the collar
of her dress.

"You frightened me," she said.

"I'm sorry, Mother."

"You might have got dust on that skirt."

Celia accepted the rebuke as pardon. Ned offered Mrs Rose
his arm. She did not take it, but neither did
she object when he walked beside them.

Farmer Rose remained by the shed. His anger had not
disappeared, but it had become conscious of its audience and
was looking for a respectable exit.

Quince waited.

"At least," Rose said, "you admit you hid the key."

"I admit nothing."

"You told Ned where it was."

"Professional confidence."

Rose's mouth moved unwillingly at one corner.

"You old rogue."

Quince closed one eye.

"Your expression is actionable."

"Come and act on it, then."

They crossed the road together.

By sunset it had been agreed that Ned might court
Celia openly on two evenings a week and Sunday afternoons,
subject to good behaviour and the state of the weather.
Rose refused to discuss marriage, which everyone understood to mean
that the marriage would take place after harvest. Quince gave
no further legal advice that evening, though he delivered several
opinions concerning barley, taxation, and the proper construction of a
boot.

Hogg repeated the story to each newcomer, placing himself nearer
the centre of events on every occasion. By nine o'clock
he had discovered the key, released Ned, and persuaded Rose
to accept the match. The miller listened without protest, being
secure in the knowledge that truth, like flour, was improved
in village handling by a little extra weight.

When Quince returned home, Mrs Quince set cold mutton before
him.

"You got Ned out, then," she said.

"I did."

"Celia too?"

"By a carefully reasoned application of established principle."

"She thought of it, I suppose."

Quince removed his coat.

"The law," he said, "requires an instrument."

"And she found one."

He looked at her, then applied himself to the mutton.

Outside, the voices from the inn drifted along the quiet
road. Someone called for another jug; someone else declared that
he had known all along how the business must end.
Across the fields, Rose's stable stood open to the evening
air, while behind Quince's workshop the tool shed remained securely
unlocked.

The lawyer had both keys in his pocket.

He said nothing about them.

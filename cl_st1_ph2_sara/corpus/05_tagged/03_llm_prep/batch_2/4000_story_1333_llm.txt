I have managed ball clubs through rain, mutiny, lame arms,
weak batting, bad hotels, worse umpires, and directors who wanted
a pennant bought for the price of a fungo bat.
But the hardest proposition I ever tackled was Whit Hurtle
when he fell in love.

His name was Whitman Hurtle, though no one called him
anything but Whit or Rube. He came to Worcester from
a farm beyond the last telegraph pole in western Pennsylvania,
and when he joined us he had one suit, a
pair of boots that could have stopped grounders, and an
arm that made old players talk to themselves.

He was six feet two, broad as a barn door,
and shy as a rabbit. The first day he walked
into the clubhouse he carried a carpetbag and asked me
if I was ``Mister Connolly, the baseball conductor.''

``Manager,'' I said.

``Yes, sir. I reckon that's what I mean.''

Then I put him in the box against Hartford. He
struck out fourteen men and broke the catcher's thumb.

After that nobody cared what he called me.

By the middle of July we were two games behind
Providence and playing the fastest ball in the league. Whit
had won thirteen out of sixteen. Radbourne, my captain and
catcher, declared there wasn't another twirler east of Chicago who
had Whit's speed or his drop. He could put a
ball over the inside corner like driving a nail, and
when he cut loose with his high one it came
up to the plate looking no bigger than a white
pea.

Then, all at once, he went bad.

A pitcher generally advertises his troubles. If his arm is
sore, he favors it. If he has been sitting up
with a sick friend—and ball players had a great many
sick friends in those days—his eyes tell the story. If
he has lost his nerve, you can see it when
the first man reaches first base.

But Whit fooled me.

His arm was sound. He ate enough for three. He
slept nine hours. Yet Hartford knocked him out in four
innings, Springfield beat him with scratch singles, and Albany, which
couldn't hit a balloon with a plank, made twelve safeties
off him.

I watched him closely. His speed was there, but he
wasn't putting anything behind it. He pitched like a man
listening for a noise in the grand stand.

In the fifth inning of the Albany game I found
the noise.

There was a girl sitting three rows back of our
bench, dressed in white and carrying a red parasol. I
knew her by sight. Everybody around the grounds knew her.
She was Nan Brown, daughter of old Silas Brown, who
owned mills, horses and more Worcester real estate than was
healthy for one citizen. Nan came to every home game,
kept a scorecard, knew the batting averages, and could tell
a spitball from an outcurve before half the league batters
could.

She was a peach to look at, too—brown eyes, dark
hair, a quick color, and a smile that had put
several sensible young men off their feed.

That afternoon she was not watching Whit.

She was talking to Henderson.

Henderson was our new pitcher, a handsome, curly-haired fellow from
New York. He wore his uniform as if he had
invented it and could sit on the bench without getting
dust on his trousers. He had a fair curve, a
quicker tongue, and a yellow streak that I had not
yet been able to prove. Women liked him. Men generally
waited for evidence.

Every time Whit turned on the slab, his eyes slid
toward Nan. Every time they did, he saw her laughing
at something Henderson had whispered.

Whit walked two men, hit another, and served the next
batter a straight one down the middle.

Crack!

The ball went into left for three bases.

I yanked Whit and sent in Barrett. As Whit came
to the bench he did not look at me. He
looked at Nan. She was still smiling at Henderson.

That evening Whit came to my room at the hotel.

He stood in the doorway twisting his hat.

``Come in,'' I said. ``If you've come to borrow money,
I haven't any. If you've come to resign, sit down
first.''

He sat down.

``Mister Connelly, I reckon I got to quit.''

``You reckon wrong.''

``I ain't no good to this club.''

``That is the first accurate statement you've made in three
weeks.''

His face went red, but he did not get angry.
Whit could take more blunt talk than most men because
he never suspected malice until it kicked him.

``I'm costin' you games,'' he said.

``You are. Providence will send you a silver loving cup
if you keep it up.''

``Then let me go home.''

``Why?''

He bent the brim of his hat until I expected
it to crack.

``I can't tell you.''

``You don't need to. Her name is Nan Brown.''

He came out of that chair like an exploding boiler.

``Who told you?''

``Nobody. I have eyes. Sit down before you hit the
ceiling.''

He sat, breathing hard.

``It ain't her fault,'' he said. ``Don't you go blamin'
her.''

``I blame you. A pitcher who carries a girl into
the box with him is working with too much weight.''

``She don't care nothin' about me.''

``She did before Henderson arrived.''

His jaw quivered.

``She likes him. He talks fine, an' he wears them
city clothes. I'm only a country dub.''

``You're the best pitcher in the league.''

``That ain't the same.''

``It ought to be.''

``It ain't.''

That ended my first lesson in the strangest game on
the schedule. I knew baseball, but I did not yet
know that a man may believe in his fast ball
while doubting his own necktie.

Whit got up.

``Please let me go, Mister Connelly.''

``No.''

``I'm askin' fair.''

``And I'm answering fair. Worcester cannot win the pennant without
you. There are nine hundred thousand farmers suffering from love
at this minute, but they are still harvesting the wheat.
You signed a contract.''

He looked wounded.

That was too much business and not enough heart, so
I tried another pitch.

``Whit, the boys trust you. Radbourne has caught you until
his left hand looks like a roast. Do you want
to tell him it was wasted? Do you want Providence
to say they chased you home?''

His shoulders came up.

``No, sir.''

``Then stay. Beat Providence. Win the pennant. After that you
can go back to your farm and mourn until snow
covers you.''

He thought it over.

``I'll stay,'' he said. ``But I reckon I won't be
much use.''

``That's my worry.''

When he had gone, I put on my coat and
went to see Milly.

Milly and I were to be married in October, provided
Worcester won the pennant. If we lost, I expected the
directors to shoot me, and that would have postponed the
ceremony.

She listened while I explained Whit, Nan and Henderson. Milly
had gray eyes that never hurried and a way of
waiting until a man had finished making a fool of
himself.

``Well?'' I said.

``Whit loves Nan.''

``I discovered that.''

``Nan likes Whit.''

``Then she conceals it like a professional.''

``She likes Henderson to admire her. That is different.''

``It's the same thing to a pitcher losing control.''

``What have you said to Whit?''

I told her.

``You appealed to his loyalty, his pride and his contract,''
she said. ``Did it occur to you to appeal to
Nan?''

``I don't manage Nan.''

``That is fortunate for Nan.''

``Can you get her interested in Whit again?''

Milly smiled.

``I might get her interested in getting him interested.''

That sounded like a double steal with both runners asleep,
but Milly generally knew what she was doing.

I tackled my end of the scheme the next morning.

Henderson had won five games for us, but I had
seen enough of him. He had laughed while Whit was
being hammered. No real pitcher laughs at another pitcher in
trouble, even if the fellow is after his job.

I called Henderson into the office.

``You are released,'' I said.

He stared at me.

``What's the joke?''

``You.''

``You can't release me in a pennant race.''

``I just did.''

``My arm is right. I won Monday.''

``You won because we made eleven runs.''

His face hardened.

``This is about that hayseed.''

``Partly.''

``And Miss Brown?''

``Mostly not. But if you want to think it is,
I can't stop you.''

``Providence will take me.''

``They need pitching.''

``And when I beat you, you'll look pretty.''

``I always look pretty.''

He laughed without humor.

``You're making the biggest bonehead play of your life, Connelly.''

That sentence has been spoken to every manager since the
game was invented. Sometimes it is even true.

By afternoon Henderson was gone. Whit saw the empty locker
and asked no questions, but he pitched batting practice with
a little more life.

Milly brought Nan to the grounds the next day.

They came early, while the club was warming up. Nan
wore blue, and Whit, who was throwing near third base,
nearly hit Radbourne in the head when he saw her.

Nan sat beside Milly back of our bench. For two
innings she ignored Whit with great care. A girl who
takes that much trouble not to look at a man
is at least aware of his location.

Springfield scored in the third. Two scratch singles, a sacrifice
and an error by our shortstop put Whit in the
hole. He walked the next batter on four pitched balls.

``He's thinking about her,'' I said to Milly.

``Then give her something useful to do.''

I went back to Nan.

``Miss Brown, you are ruining my pitcher.''

She opened her eyes.

``I? Why, Mr. Connelly!''

``You. He can't find the plate when you're around.''

``Then perhaps I should leave.''

``That would finish him. Tell him to pitch.''

``Doesn't he know that already?''

``Tell him there is something in it for him.''

She looked toward the box. Whit was digging at the
dirt with his spikes.

``What sort of something?'' she asked.

``Use your imagination.''

Nan's eyes flashed.

``You are an extremely impertinent man.''

``I am also two games behind Providence.''

She tore a corner from her scorecard, wrote on it
and folded it twice.

``If this makes matters worse, I shall blame you.''

``Everybody does.''

I sent the note out by the bat boy. Whit
opened it behind his glove.

His ears turned scarlet.

Then he struck out the batter.

He struck out the next one, too.

From that inning on Springfield did not reach first base.
Whit's speed began to jump. His curve broke under the
bat, and his slow ball floated up like a soap
bubble. He retired nineteen men in order. We scored twice
in the seventh and won, two to one.

After the last out Whit came off the field looking
dazed and happy.

``What did she write?'' I asked.

He folded the note smaller and pushed it into his
shirt.

``Private.''

``Was it tactical?''

``I reckon.''

Later Milly told me. Nan had written:

``If you win, you may call at eight. If you
shut them out from now on, perhaps you may have
a kiss.''

That was good coaching. I ought to have left it
there.

Instead, I tried to improve it.

Whit came to my room at seven that evening wearing
a new gray suit, a high collar and a necktie
that looked as if it had been wounded.

``How do I act?'' he asked.

``Naturally.''

``I don't know how to act natural with her.''

``Be aggressive.''

``Aggressive?''

``Certainly. Women admire decision. Don't sit there saying 'yes, ma'am'
and 'no, ma'am' until she mistakes you for a hired
hand. Take command.''

``What'll I talk about?''

``Her.''

``What about her?''

``Tell her she is beautiful.''

``She knows that.''

``Then prove you know it.''

He swallowed.

``An' the kiss?''

That was where I committed the error that nearly cost
us the pennant.

``She promised it. Collect.''

``Supposin' she won't?''

``Don't ask too many questions. A fellow who shakes off
every sign never pitches a game. Get your nerve, Whit.
Tell her she belongs to you.''

He looked alarmed.

``She don't.''

``That is not the point. Confidence wins.''

There are moments when a manager should be gagged by
his friends. Milly was not there, and Providence was, so
fate let me talk.

Whit went to Nan's house.

At nine-thirty he came back.

His collar was wilted. His tie hung sideways. There was
a red mark on his cheek that might have come
from a hand.

``Well?'' I said.

``You're a liar.''

``That is no way to report.''

``She don't admire decision.''

``What happened?''

He stood over me, fists clenched.

``She give me the kiss. On the cheek. Then she
laughed an' said I'd earned it. I remembered what you
said. I caught hold of her.''

I began to feel cold.

``How hard?''

``I reckon too hard. I kissed her. Then I told
her she belonged to me.''

``What did she say?''

``She said she didn't belong to nobody, least of all
an ignorant bully. Then she slapped me.''

``Did you apologize?''

``No.''

``Why not?''

``You told me to be aggressive.''

``There is such a thing as relief pitching.''

``I waited. She waited. Then she pointed at the door.''

``And you left?''

``I reckon I couldn't climb out the window.''

He went out mad at Nan, mad at me and
worst of all mad at himself.

The next morning Milly arrived before breakfast.

``What did you tell that poor boy?'' she demanded.

``Sound general principles.''

``He seized Nan like a train robber.''

``He misunderstood.''

``Nan says he almost crushed her.''

``Whit is powerful.''

``And then he told her she belonged to him.''

``That phrase may have been too advanced.''

Milly looked at me as if reconsidering October.

``Did he apologize?''

``Apparently not.''

``Then why are you sitting here?''

``Where should I sit?''

``You should find him and make him apologize.''

I found Whit at the park. He was pitching balls
against the clubhouse wall hard enough to loosen bricks.

``Go see Nan,'' I said.

``No.''

``Apologize.''

``I ain't apologizin' for carin' for her.''

``Apologize for behaving like a Comanche.''

``You said—''

``Forget what I said.''

``I wish I could.''

He threw another ball. It hit the wall and came
back like a shot.

``She called me an ignorant bully,'' he said. ``She thinks
I ain't fit to wipe Henderson's shoes.''

``She did not mention Henderson.''

``She was thinkin' him.''

``Whit, a woman can be angry at one man without
thinking of another.''

He shook his head.

``I won't crawl.''

There was the country pride in him, thick and stubborn.
It had carried him out of a poor farm and
into professional baseball. Now it sat on his chest and
choked him.

Nan waited two days for an apology. None came.

On the third day a Providence newspaper announced that Henderson
had signed with that club. On the fourth, Nan went
driving with him.

The news reached Whit before the horses had cooled.

Against Hartford that afternoon he lasted one inning.

We lost six out of eight. Providence went four games
ahead. The directors called me a visionary, an assassin and
a man who had released a pennant. One of them
asked why Whit remained in the box.

``Because he is the only pitcher who can catch Providence,''
I said.

``He can't catch a train.''

``Wait.''

Waiting is easy for a manager when other people do
it.

Whit moved through those days like a man under water.
He ate little, spoke less, and pitched with no snap.
The boys tried to jolly him, but Radbourne stopped them.

``Leave the Rube alone,'' he said. ``He's got a fever
no doctor knows how to measure.''

Milly worked on Nan. I worked on Whit. Neither of
us made a run.

Nan was hurt, and hurt pride is a tough club
to pitch against. She admitted Whit had been sincere, but
sincerity, she said, did not excuse assault.

``He is ashamed,'' Milly told her.

``Then he should say so.''

``He thinks you despise him.''

``He gave me no opportunity to say what I think.''

``You went driving with Henderson.''

``Mr. Henderson apologizes when he steps on my gown.''

``Mr. Henderson steps on more serious things without noticing.''

That was Milly's report. I wished I had been there
to applaud.

The schedule finally brought us to the last Providence series
in Worcester. Three games. If Providence took two, the race
was practically over. Henderson was announced to pitch the opener.
I had already decided on Whit, though announcing it would
have caused half the city to demand my examination by
doctors.

The evening before the game Milly brought Nan to my
office.

Nan entered with her chin high.

``Milly says you wish to speak to me.''

``I do. Sit down.''

``I prefer to stand.''

``So do runners with new uniforms, but it wastes energy.''

She sat.

I had managed holdouts, drunks, spitball artists, loafers and one
shortstop who believed he was Napoleon. None of them made
me as careful as Nan Brown did then.

``You know Henderson is pitching tomorrow,'' I said.

``Yes.''

``Whit pitches for us.''

Her fingers tightened on her parasol.

``Is that wise?''

``No. It is necessary.''

``He has been pitching badly.''

``Because you have been treating him badly.''

She rose.

``If that is all—''

``It isn't. Sit down, please.''

The ``please'' did it. She sat again.

``Whit behaved like a fool,'' I said. ``Partly because I
coached him like one. I told him to take command.
That advice is useful with an infield and dangerous in
a parlor.''

The frost in her face thawed one degree.

``He might have known better.''

``He was learning. You know how he was when he
came here. Couldn't buy a collar without asking the clerk
if it was regulation. He has learned hotels, trains, signs,
crowds and city talk. But no one taught him you.''

``I am not a course of study, Mr. Connelly.''

``You have final examinations.''

Milly coughed to hide a laugh.

I leaned forward.

``Henderson is smooth. He says the right thing because he
has said it to a dozen girls. Whit said the
wrong thing because he had never cared enough about anybody
to practice.''

Nan looked down.

``He did not apologize.''

``No. That is his fault. But he has paid for
it. Every batter in the league has been collecting.''

``This is about your pennant.''

``Certainly it is. I am a manager. But I would
rather lose it with Whit than win it with Henderson.
Henderson quit on his teammates twice. Whit never quit until
he thought you had made a joke of him.''

``I never made a joke of Whit.''

``He does not know that.''

For a while she said nothing. Through the office window
we could see the empty field turning purple in the
evening. The box was a pale patch in the center
of the diamond.

At last Nan asked, ``Do you truly think Whit is
the better pitcher?''

``By a mile.''

``And the better man?''

``By two miles.''

Milly said softly, ``You know it, Nan.''

Nan walked to the window. When she turned back her
color had risen.

``What do you expect me to do?''

``Send him a message.''

``Saying what?''

My managerial instinct got up and stretched.

``Give him something to pitch for.''

Milly frowned at me, but Nan's eyes brightened. She had
the spirit of a gambler.

``Very well,'' she said. ``I shall choose between them by
tomorrow's game.''

``That is not quite fair. A pitcher can lose through
errors.''

``Then you had better see that your men do not
make any.''

``What exactly will you write?'' Milly asked.

Nan took a card from her purse.

``The better pitcher shall know my answer after the game.''

``Suppose Worcester loses?'' I said.

``I said the better pitcher, not the winning pitcher.''

I should have paid more attention to that distinction. Whit
did not.

I carried the message to his room myself. He read
it three times.

``She means if I beat Henderson, she'll—''

``She will give you her answer.''

``An' if he beats me?''

``Then you'll get an answer anyway.''

His face grew grim.

``He ain't goin' to beat me.''

That was the first living thing he had said in
a month.

The grounds filled early next day. Worcester shut its factories
at noon, or might as well have, for every operative
with fifty cents came to the park. Men sat along
the foul lines. Boys climbed the fences. The grand stand
shivered under stamping feet. Providence brought five hundred rooters with
bells, horns and the belief that the pennant already belonged
to them.

Nan sat with Milly in a box near first base.
She wore white again and held no parasol. Henderson saw
her during practice and lifted his cap. She smiled.

Whit saw that.

``Don't look there,'' I told him.

``I ain't.''

``Then stop not looking so hard.''

He tightened his belt.

``I know what I got to do.''

Radbourne warmed him up beneath the stand.

The first pitch struck Radbourne's mitt with a sound like
a pistol.

He threw it back slowly.

``That'll do,'' he said. ``Save a few.''

Providence batted first. Their leadoff man, Brady, was the best
waiter in the league, a pest who could foul off
good balls until a pitcher grew old. Whit gave him
a fast one over the corner. Strike one. A drop.
Strike two. Then the high bullet.

Brady swung late.

One out.

The crowd roared Whit's name. He did not hear it.
Or if he did, he stored it somewhere behind Nan's
card.

He struck out the second man and made the third
roll weakly to short.

Henderson came in smiling. He had style, I'll grant him
that. He used an easy motion and bent the ball
across the outside edge. Our first two batters went out
on flies. Radbourne drove a single through second, but Henderson
caught him napping with a quick throw.

No score.

For three innings neither side got a man past first.
Whit was wonderful. He worked without waste, putting every ball
where Radbourne held the glove. Henderson was nearly as good
and had the better luck. Two drives that should have
been hits went straight into Providence gloves.

In the fourth Providence got its first break.

Lennox chopped a slow one toward third. McCarthy charged, scooped
it barehanded and threw low. Our first baseman blocked the
ball, but it kicked away.

Error.

The next batter bunted. Whit pounced on it and fired
to second. The throw beat Lennox, but our shortstop dropped
it.

Two errors. Nobody out.

The Providence crowd found its voice.

``Take your time!'' I shouted.

Whit looked toward Nan's box. Henderson, coaching at first, was
grinning.

The third batter tried to sacrifice. Whit's drop fooled him,
and he popped the ball into the air. Whit lunged,
got one hand under it, and the ball struck his
palm, rolled along his wrist and fell.

Bases full.

Not one ball had been hit out of the infield.

Calamity breathed in the very dust.

The clean-up man, Sloan, came up. He was a left-handed
hitter who murdered fast balls. Radbourne called for curves. Whit
bent two across. Sloan missed one and fouled the other.
With two strikes Radbourne signaled for the high ball.

Whit shook him off.

Radbourne signaled again.

Another shake.

I knew what Whit wanted. He wanted to throw his
speed past Sloan, man against man. It was dangerous, proud
baseball.

Radbourne finally set the target.

Whit wound up and let go.

The ball streaked at Sloan's shoulders. He swung. The bat
cracked, and for one sick second I thought it was
gone.

It sailed toward deep right. Our fielder backed to the
ropes, caught it and threw home. Lennox scored. The other
runners held.

One run. One out.

Whit struck out the next man on three balls and
forced the last to ground to second.

Providence led, one to nothing, on two errors and a
fly.

As Whit came to the bench I said, ``They haven't
earned a thing.''

``Scoreboard don't say that.''

``Neither does Nan's message.''

He glanced at me.

``She'll choose the winner.''

``She said better pitcher.''

``Same thing.''

There is no arguing with a man during his own
execution.

In our half Radbourne doubled down the line. The crowd
got up. I sent in a bunter, but Henderson fielded
the ball beautifully and caught Radbourne at third. Then he
made our next man hit into a double play.

Henderson walked toward the bench with applause rolling after him.
He tipped his cap toward Nan.

Whit's face went white beneath the tan.

``Easy,'' I said. ``There are five innings.''

``I'm easy.''

He was as easy as an erupting volcano.

In the fifth he struck out the side.

In the sixth Providence stole another run from fate.

With two out Brady hit a twisting foul behind first.
Our first baseman reached it, got both hands on it
and dropped it. Brady then fouled five pitches and scratched
a single over short.

He stole second on a pitchout because Radbourne's throw struck
the runner and bounded into center. Brady reached third.

Lennox hit a slow roller. Whit came off the slab,
made a perfect throw—and the umpire called Lennox safe.

I ran out.

``He was out by a yard!''

``He was safe,'' said the umpire.

``You couldn't see a brass band at sunrise.''

``Back to the bench, Connelly.''

``If he was safe, I am a Sunday-school teacher.''

``Then teach from the bench.''

The crowd howled. I made my protest long enough to
give Whit time to cool, then retired before the umpire
helped me.

Brady had scored.

Two to nothing.

Whit struck out the next batter. As he left the
field, the Providence men shouted at him from their bench.
He never turned his head.

Henderson retired us in order in the sixth. His curve
was breaking sharper, and he knew it. Confidence sat on
him like a tailored coat.

``He's got us,'' one of our substitutes muttered.

Radbourne heard.

``Say that again and I'll put you in the grand
stand.''

In the seventh Whit gave up a clean double, the
first hard hit off him. The runner reached third on
a sacrifice. One out.

I brought the infield in.

The batter hit a fierce bounder toward short. Our man
knocked it down but could not hold it. The runner
dashed home.

The shortstop recovered and threw.

The ball and runner reached the plate together. Radbourne blocked
him, rolled over, and came up holding the ball.

``Out!''

The Worcester crowd exploded.

Whit struck out the next man. Still two to nothing.

We began our seventh with a single. The next batter
drove a dark streak toward right center. I was already
waving the runner around when the Providence center fielder made
one of the greatest catches I ever saw. He ran
with his back to the diamond, stretched out and speared
the ball inches above the grass.

Our runner, nearly at third, could not get back.

Double play.

Henderson laughed as he walked to the box. It was
a little laugh, but Whit heard it from the bench.

``I'll kill him,'' Whit said.

``Do it according to league rules.''

He looked at me, and for the first time that
day there was a trace of his old grin.

In the eighth Providence put two men on with scratch
hits. Henderson came to bat.

The crowd gave him a great hand, and Nan sat
still in her box.

Henderson dug in and called time to wipe his hands.
He wanted everybody to see him there against Whit. Two
pitchers, one pennant, one girl. Even an umpire could understand
the situation.

Whit threw a curve. Henderson watched it.

Strike one.

He threw inside. Henderson jumped back.

Ball one.

Another curve broke low.

Ball two.

Radbourne went out to talk.

``What did he say?'' I asked when he returned later.

``I told Whit the batter was Henderson, not Providence.''

The next pitch was a fast ball on the outside
corner. Henderson swung and missed.

Two and two.

Then Whit threw the finest ball of his life. It
started at Henderson's head, seemed to stop, and dropped across
the plate at his knees.

Henderson froze.

``Strike three!''

For one heartbeat the park was silent. Then came such
a roar as I have never heard before or since.
Men threw hats. Women waved scorecards. The bleachers stamped until
dust shook out from under them.

Henderson protested. The umpire turned away.

As Whit passed him going to the bench, Henderson said
something low.

Whit stopped.

I was over the rail in an instant.

``Keep walking!'' I shouted.

Whit's fists doubled, but Radbourne caught his arm.

``Save it,'' Radbourne said. ``We need three runs.''

Whit came in without looking toward Nan.

We had six outs left.

Henderson got through the eighth, but he was tiring. Radbourne
worked him for a walk with two gone. Our next
batter hit a line drive straight at the second baseman.

Three outs left.

Before the ninth I caught Whit by the sleeve.

``Hold them.''

``I have.''

``Hold them once more.''

He went into the box carrying defeat on his face
and lightning in his arm.

Brady fouled off seven pitches, then struck out on a
drop. Lennox singled cleanly to left. The third man bunted,
and Whit threw him out. Lennox reached second.

Two out.

Sloan came up. One hit would finish us.

Whit's first pitch was low. His second was outside. Radbourne
walked halfway to the slab and barked at him.

Whit nodded.

He drove over two strikes. Sloan fouled the next into
the crowd. Then another. Then another.

The noise faded. Every soul in the park leaned toward
the box.

Whit wound up.

Sloan swung.

A high fly rose over second base. Our shortstop settled
beneath it and squeezed it.

Out!

Whit had allowed six hits and no earned runs. He
had struck out eleven. Yet Providence led two to nothing.

He walked to the bench slowly. He did not glance
at Nan. In his mind the decision had already been
scored.

Henderson went out for the ninth with a swagger that
was not quite as easy as before.

Our first batter, McCarthy, took two balls, then lashed a
single over second.

The crowd stirred.

I sent Barrett to bat. Henderson fed him curves. Barrett
fouled one, missed one and then poked a Texas leaguer
behind first.

Two on. Nobody out.

Now the park came alive.

``Hit it out!''

``Get Henderson!''

``Worcester! Worcester!''

The sound rolled against us like surf.

Our leadoff man bunted. Henderson charged and threw to third.
McCarthy slid under the tag.

Safe!

Bases full.

No one out.

Two runs behind.

I looked at Whit.

He sat at the end of the bench with his
hands between his knees, staring at the ground.

``Whit!''

He raised his head.

``We're not dead.''

The next batter struck out.

One away.

Radbourne came up. He had caught a magnificent game and
hit Henderson twice. If I could have chosen one man,
I would have chosen him.

Henderson tried to make him bite at an outside curve.
Ball one. Then a fast strike. Radbourne fouled the next.

One and two.

Henderson wasted a high one.

Two and two.

He came back with his curve. Radbourne swung.

The ball shot between first and second. The Providence first
baseman dived full length and stopped it with his bare
hand. From his knees he tossed to Henderson covering.

Radbourne was out.

McCarthy scored. Barrett reached third.

Two to one.

Two out.

The tying run stood ninety feet away. The winning run
was at second.

The crowd was one long howl.

Our last batter was little Danny Cole, a weak hitter
with more courage than weight. Henderson's face had lost its
smile.

First pitch—ball.

Second pitch—strike.

Third pitch—low.

Two and one.

I signaled Danny to hit away.

Henderson threw a fast ball. Danny met it square.

Crack!

The ball flew toward left, rising over the shortstop. Barrett
raced home. The crowd burst loose. I was shouting before
the ball began to fall.

Then Providence's left fielder came tearing in. He dove headfirst.

For an instant we saw nothing but dust.

The fielder rolled over and held up the ball.

Out.

The game was over.

Providence, two. Worcester, one.

Our men stood where defeat found them. Barrett was halfway
home. Danny had rounded first. Radbourne flung his mask against
the bench.

Across the field the Providence players mobbed Henderson as if
he had conquered the world.

Whit rose.

His face was empty.

``Where are you going?'' I asked.

``Away.''

``You pitched the better game.''

``He won.''

``Two unearned runs!''

``He won,'' Whit repeated.

He looked once toward Nan's box. It was empty.

That finished him.

He walked through the clubhouse, changed his clothes and left
before the crowd had cleared. I tried to follow, but
the directors caught me, the newspaper men surrounded me, and
an angry citizen demanded to know why I had not
ordered Danny to bunt with two strikes.

By the time I escaped, Whit was gone.

I found Milly outside the players' gate.

``Where is Nan?'' I asked.

``Looking for Whit.''

``She left before the last out.''

``She left to reach the clubhouse.''

``He went out the rear door.''

Milly gave me the sort of look reserved for managers
and lost children.

``Then find him.''

We searched the hotel, the station and the streets near
the grounds. No Whit. I began to think he had
started walking to Pennsylvania.

At last Radbourne remembered that Whit sometimes went to the
empty practice field beyond the freight yards when he wanted
to be alone.

We found him there at dusk.

He sat on the low fence with his suitcase beside
him. Beyond him the field was rough and brown, and
boys' bases, made of grain sacks, lay pale in the
grass.

``Train doesn't leave for an hour,'' he said when he
saw us.

``You're not taking it,'' I answered.

``I stayed like I promised. I done my best. It
wasn't enough.''

``You were magnificent.''

``Losin' magnificent is still losin'.''

Then Nan stepped from behind Milly.

Whit came off the fence.

For a second he looked as he had the day
he first entered my office—big, awkward, frightened and ready to
run.

Nan walked toward him.

``You left before I could speak to you,'' she said.

``I know the answer.''

``Do you?''

``Henderson won.''

``Providence won.''

``Same thing.''

``No, Whit.''

He looked at her then.

Nan's voice softened.

``My message did not say I would choose the winner.
It said I would choose the better pitcher.''

Whit swallowed.

``He beat me.''

``Your fielders helped him. His fielders saved him. You gave
up no earned runs, struck out eleven, and struck out
Henderson himself with two men on.''

Even in that hour I felt proud of her scorekeeping.

Whit shook his head.

``The paper'll put a loss after my name.''

``Then the paper is not choosing for me.''

He stared at her as if the language had become
difficult.

``You mean—''

``I mean you were the better pitcher.''

``An' the better man?'' I prompted.

Milly stepped on my foot.

Nan moved closer to Whit.

``You behaved very badly in my parlor,'' she said.

His head dropped.

``I know it.''

``You hurt me.''

``I didn't aim to.''

``You should have apologized.''

``I was ashamed. An' proud. An' I reckoned you wanted
Henderson.''

``That was no excuse.''

``No, ma'am.''

``Well?''

Whit looked helplessly at me.

For once I shook my head. I had coached enough.

He faced Nan again.

``I'm sorry,'' he said. ``I'm sorry I grabbed you, an'
sorry I talked like I owned you. I don't. I
know that now. I reckon I knew it then, only
Mister Connelly—''

``Leave me out of this,'' I said.

Nan tried not to smile.

Whit continued, ``I ain't smooth. I can't say things like
Henderson. But I never cared for anybody the way I
care for you. If you tell me to go, I'll
go. Only I wanted you to know that.''

Nan stood very still.

Then she held out her hand.

``You need not go, Whit.''

He took her hand carefully, as if it were a
new ball with the cover loose.

``You mean I—''

``I mean I have chosen the better pitcher.''

``That's all?'' he asked, and immediately looked terrified by his
own courage.

Nan's color deepened.

``For tonight.''

Whit glanced at me again.

``Don't ask him,'' Nan said. ``His advice is reckless.''

``Mine won the game,'' I protested.

``You lost the game,'' Milly said.

``The game on the scoreboard, maybe.''

Nan laughed. Whit's face opened into the slowest, biggest grin
I ever saw.

They walked away together across the rough field, Whit carrying
his suitcase in one hand and Nan's hand in the
other. He held the second more gently.

We did not win the pennant that year. Providence took
it by one game, and Henderson spent the winter telling
people he had pitched them to victory. The record book
gives him credit, which shows what record books know.

Whit won four straight after that day. He married Nan
the next spring, learned to wear a necktie without strangling,
and pitched seven years in fast company. Whenever he got
wild, Nan had only to wave her scorecard.

As for me, I married Milly in October despite the
pennant. She has managed me ever since.

And when people ask about the greatest pitching duel I
ever saw, I do not mention the score first. I
tell them how one man won the game, another man
lost it, and the loser carried off the prize.

That is baseball.

Also, according to Milly, that is love.

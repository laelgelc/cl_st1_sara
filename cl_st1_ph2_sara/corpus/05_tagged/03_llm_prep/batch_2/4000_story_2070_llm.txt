### The Country Behind the Words

Berwick, in Maine, was already an old village when I
first knew it, though to a child nothing can be
old which is still alive and full of daily business.
The river ran past us with an air of having
urgent matters at the sea; the falls kept up their
solemn roaring; and beyond the last houses the fields went
softly toward the dark borders of pine and hemlock. There
were willows along the low ground, orchards behind weather-stained farmhouses,
and roads which appeared to lead nowhere, but which in
fact went to lonely clearings, forgotten mills, and families whose
histories reached as far backward as the village records. I
knew, without understanding, that wars had passed this way. There
were tales of alarms and blockhouses, of frightened women and
gallant men, of Indians moving without a sound through woods
that no longer existed. A shallow hollow beside a pasture
might be pointed out as the site of an old
garrison, and a blackened beam in some kitchen ceiling had
survived both fire and fighting. Thus history was never wholly
confined to books. It had left marks upon the very
ground.

Two rivers, broadening toward tide-water, had given the town its
livelihood and its character. Timber came down from the great
forests; gundelows and packets went out with the tide; ships
were built, laden, and sent away to ports whose names
had a magnificence beyond any fairy tale. Surinam, Martinique, Havana,
and Kingston were household words among us, though Boston appeared
to me a more astonishing distance than all the West
Indies together. There were old men who had seen palms
and flying fish, who had slept under foreign stars, and
who could yet be found of an afternoon mending a
stone wall or bargaining gravely over a load of hay.
Their sea-tanned faces and slow country speech made an alliance
of the whole world with Berwick.

My grandfather had been concerned in the timber and West
India trade during its better days, and his house bore
many pleasant witnesses to that intercourse. I remember red earthen
jars of olives, dark lumps of sugar, spices, strange shells,
and pieces of mahogany which had a deeper colour than
any native wood. There were cupboards whose contents seemed to
have arrived by their own private navigation, and maps where
the tracks of voyages were faintly marked. Captains came to
see him; farmers came also; and men from the upper
country, bringing news of timber-pines, freshets, broken bridges, and oxen
lost through the river ice. As a little girl I
was not expected to understand their business, and therefore was
permitted to hear a surprising quantity of it.

I have often thought that nothing could have been better
for one who was afterward to write stories than that
accidental society. The captains spoke of storms, mutinies, yellow fever,
privateers, and narrow passages among reefs; but they spoke with
equal seriousness of a neighbour's new barn or the price
of potatoes. The country people, on their part, could make
an adventure of carrying a sick cow through February, or
of finding a bear in the corn. Each man had
his own turn of phrase, his own estimate of courage,
and his own manner of withholding the most important part
until the proper moment. I learned very early that two
persons may relate the same occurrence and tell two entirely
different truths.

One of the old sailors, Nathan Lord, had a famous
story which I demanded so often that he came at
last to begin it whenever he saw me. He had
been a young fellow aboard a Berwick vessel lying in
a southern port, where a much larger foreign ship had
boasted the strongest crew on the waterfront. There was to
be a trial of strength with a heavy spar, or,
to speak exactly, a contest in which the men lifted
and shifted it by hand. The foreign sailors laughed when
Nathan and his companions came forward.

"Go home to your little river," their mate called. "This
timber was grown for men."

Nathan, who was small and dry as a winter apple,
looked at the great fellow and said, "Our river carries
timber enough. It don't have to talk about it."

The Berwick men took the spar, raised it cleanly, and
laid it where the others had failed to put it.
Nathan always paused here, while I waited for a fight,
a chase, or at least a broken head.

"And what did the foreign captain say?" I would ask.

"He asked where we learned the trick."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him we practised on toothpicks at home."

This conclusion never appeared to me worthy of the preparation,
yet the old men laughed until they wiped their eyes.
Much later I understood that Nathan's victory lay less in
the lifting of the spar than in the preservation of
his provincial dignity.

The forests which supplied those ships seemed inexhaustible, and people
spoke of them as if they were another ocean, always
replenished beyond the horizon. In winter the great trunks came
trailing behind yoked oxen, the chains clanking, the runners crying
upon the snow, and the breath of men and cattle
white in the bitter air. Some of the pines were
so broad that two children could not meet hands around
them. I remember hearing prodigious accounts of trees which furnished
masts for royal ships, and I dare say the trees
gained a foot in girth whenever the story was repeated.
Yet even after allowance has been made for honest exaggeration,
they were magnificent. They were cut wastefully because their abundance
concealed their value. Such trees are unreplaceable.

The decline of the old commerce came by degrees, though
in memory it seems sudden. My grandfather died, and with
him much of the familiar traffic of the house disappeared.
Then the Civil War drew men and money into sterner
channels; ships ceased to come to the river as they
had done; wharves grew quiet; grass pushed between the planks;
and boys fished where merchants had once watched their cargoes.
The village did not perish, but it turned inward. Farming
remained, and the mills still called people to their labour,
yet the easy conversation with distant ports was broken. Old
captains sat before the shops and corrected one another's recollections.
Their ships went on sailing, but only in talk.

I felt my grandfather's death as a child feels such
a loss—chiefly through the changed look of rooms, the unexplained
silence of grown people, and the cessation of small delights
which had seemed part of nature. My father afterward became
the chief companion and influence of my early life. He
was a physician, and his profession took him into every
kind of house, from the best square mansion in the
village to a cabin where snow entered under the door.
Whenever he could allow it, he carried me with him
on his drives.

Those expeditions were my true schooling. We went over hill
roads where the sleigh creaked beneath us and every stone
wall wore a round white cap; along muddy spring lanes
bordered by alder and birch; through summer woods fragrant with
fern, sweet-fern, and sun-warmed pine; and across autumn fields where
the maples stood scarlet above gray barns. Sometimes I waited
in the chaise while he visited a patient, and watched
hens scratch under lilac bushes, or an old woman shake
a cloth at the kitchen door, or children stare at
me from behind a rain-streaked window. Sometimes I was taken
inside and placed near the stove, where I heard the
clock tick, the kettle mutter, and low anxious voices from
the bedroom.

My father knew the people not merely by their illnesses
but by their histories. He remembered who had lost a
son at sea, who had paid a brother's debt, who
could not be trusted with money, and who, though poor,
would walk ten miles to serve a neighbour. He had
humour without unkindness and patience without solemnity. On our homeward
drives he explained what I had failed to see.

"That man was frightened," I once said of a farmer
who had answered every question in a loud, cheerful voice.

"No," my father replied; "he was trying not to let
his wife know that he was frightened."

At another house I praised a woman's pleasant manners.

"She has pleasant manners," he said, "and a hard heart.
You must not confuse polish with kindness."

Then, after a moment, lest he teach me suspicion rather
than discernment, he added, "But do not be too ready
to judge. Half the time we see people on their
worst day."

This was excellent counsel for life and still better counsel
for stories.

He had a dislike of affectation, whether in conduct, dress,
or language. When I began to write descriptions and adorned
every brook with silver, every cloud with pearl, and every
old woman with either angelic goodness or dreadful mystery, he
laughed at me in the kindest manner.

"Did the brook look like silver?" he asked.

"It was very bright."

"Then say it was bright."

I protested that such a word was too plain.

"Plain words are often the strongest. Tell the thing just
as it is. If you know it well enough, it
will be interesting."

That sentence followed me for years. Indeed, it follows me
still.

Our house was full of books, though they were not
arranged with the severe order of a great library. They
accumulated wherever a reader had left them: histories on the
parlour table, voyages beside the sitting-room window, poetry in bedrooms,
medical works in my father's office, and old sermons in
remote cupboards. I read beyond my understanding and below it,
without system and with immense delight. A dull book never
injured me, for I abandoned it; a good one opened
a door which I went through at once. There were
evenings when one of the family read aloud while the
others sewed, wrote letters, or listened with hands folded. The
lamplight fell upon familiar faces, and outside the river and
the wind made their own dark commentary.

Like many children, I wrote rhymes before I had anything
to say. Mine concerned dead birds, moonlight, soldiers, and the
sea, though I knew almost nothing of the sea except
its sailors. I kept these productions hidden, while secretly desiring
that they should be discovered and praised. Once, when my
father came unexpectedly into the room, I thrust a poem
into an arithmetic book and pretended to be absorbed in
fractions. Since I had previously shown no devotion to fractions,
the deception was imperfect.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Sums."

"Then why have your sums rhymed?"

He read the poem, returned it without ridicule, and only
said, "You have put too many sunsets into one evening."

I was wounded then, but he was right.

Prose came later and felt more natural, as walking feels
more natural than performing steps before company. I began to
set down the people whom I knew: a solitary woman
who kept her dead sister's geraniums alive through twenty winters;
a lame fisherman who had never caught enough fish but
always possessed more than enough news; a farmer who spoke
to his oxen with tenderness and to his family as
if they were trespassing cattle. I changed names, altered houses,
and sometimes contrived incidents, but I tried to preserve the
cadence of a voice, the gesture of a hand, the
particular silence by which one neighbour differed from another.

At first I believed that worthy subjects must be sought
elsewhere. Great stories, I thought, belonged to castles, battlefields, and
foreign cities, not to a village shop smelling of molasses
and leather, nor to a back road where a widow
waited for the weekly stage. Yet the more closely I
looked, the richer the village became. There were pride, sacrifice,
jealousy, courage, and disappointed love within sight of our meeting-house
steeple. A man might never travel farther than Portsmouth and
still possess a heart as difficult to chart as any
ocean. A woman who had spent her life within three
rooms might have endured a campaign of which no historian
would ever hear.

I remember the day when this knowledge became almost a
conviction. My father and I had driven to see an
old patient who lived beyond the mills, in a low
house under two enormous elms. She was nearly blind and
had little besides her bed, a chest of drawers, three
blue plates, and a worn red shawl. While my father
attended to her, she asked me to look from the
eastern window and tell her whether the apple tree had
blossomed.

"It has," I said. "There are a great many flowers."

"Are they white?"

"White, and a little pink."

She smiled. "My husband planted that tree the year we
were married. I always liked to know when it came
out."

Her husband had been dead more than forty years. Nothing
further was said, but all the way home the blossoming
tree stood before me. No castle, I thought, could contain
more history than that poor yard, if only one knew
how to tell it.

Shyness delayed me more than want of purpose. I could
write in solitude with considerable boldness, but the thought of
another person reading my pages filled me with alarm. When
at nineteen or twenty I prepared a sketch for submission,
I copied it in a disguised hand and signed an
assumed name, as if the editor might otherwise send an
officer to Berwick to expose me. I walked about with
the envelope for half a day. At last I posted
it, and immediately wished to recover it.

The weeks of waiting were long enough to contain several
lifetimes of hope and humiliation. I imagined the manuscript laughed
over, neglected under a pile of nobler work, or returned
with some brief and annihilating judgment. Every arrival of the
mail made my courage fail. Then came the letter from
the *Atlantic*. My sketch had been accepted.

I read the words once, twice, and again, not because
they were difficult but because they changed the boundaries of
the world. The quiet roads, the river, the faded captains,
the farmers and lonely women, the winter oxen and summer
fields—all that familiar country had found passage into print. I
had not needed to escape it. I had needed to
see it.

As I write now, much that formed my childhood has
vanished. The greatest pines are gone; the old trade survives
chiefly in account books and stories; houses have burned or
been altered; voices once unmistakable are silent. Yet I cannot
think the village poor in its decline, for every place
keeps more than the inattentive eye perceives. Its wealth lies
not only in what remains, but in what has been
faithfully remembered.

I would say to every boy and girl who longs
to make something, yet complains that life has provided no
proper material: learn first the place where you are. Learn
its roads in rain and snow, its oldest trees, its
common speech, its trades, its prejudices, its courage, and its
secret kindness. Listen when old people repeat themselves. Observe the
room as carefully as the person who sits within it.
Do not hurry to decorate the truth. Tell people and
things just as they are, and, if you have looked
with patience and affection, they will prove extraordinary enough.

Berwick gave me no castle and no court, but it
gave me a river running toward the world, a countryside
filled with human histories, books within reach, and a father
who taught me to look twice. I have always counted
these among the greatest of gifts.

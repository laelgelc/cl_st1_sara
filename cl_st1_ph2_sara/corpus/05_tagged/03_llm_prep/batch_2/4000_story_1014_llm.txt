## Black River, Black Night

It was a bitter, bitter cold night, and dark beyond
all ordinary arrangements of darkness.

There are nights which merely extinguish the lamps of heaven
and leave the town to take care of itself; but
this night had put out everything. It had put out
the shore, the bridges, the warehouses, the ships, the smoke,
and the river itself. It had blackened the bells in
the steeples, the iron rings in the quays, the windows
of sleeping houses, and the eyes of such unfortunate cats
as had business abroad. The few lamps burning beside the
Thames appeared not to illuminate the darkness, but to exhibit
small specimens of it in glass cases.

As for the east wind, it had plainly come to
London with a grievance.

It had swept mummy dust from Egyptian tombs, worried the
dry beards of prophets in desert pictures, sharpened itself upon
the snows of Asia, whistled between the horns of unknown
mountain goats, frightened caravans, irritated crocodiles, insulted sphinxes, and finally
rushed down the Thames to cut me into geographical sections.
Having accomplished all this without warming to its work, it
blew through my coat as if the coat had been
entered on some celestial inventory as "one sieve, woollen."

My companion, Police Constable Pea of the Thames Division, seemed
less affected.

"You don't call this cold?" said I.

"Middling," said Pea.

"Middling! If the North Pole were brought to London, planted
in Trafalgar Square, and presented with the freedom of the
City, it would ask for a fire."

Pea coughed.

I had already discovered that when Pea coughed in a
certain short and official manner, he meant that I was
holding forth; so I held in.

We were concealed under the black shelter of a wharf
near Southwark Bridge, watching the tide and waiting for three
gentlemen whose expected arrival had not been publicly advertised. A
loaded barge lay out upon the river. The information was
that the gentlemen proposed to visit it without the inconvenience
of asking permission, and to relieve it of certain copper,
rope, and other portable anxieties.

In a low boat beneath us sat two more river
policemen. They were invisible, except when one shifted an oar
and caused a dull gleam to move through the obscurity
like the wink of a drowned man. No word passed
between them. The tide lapped at the piles, chains creaked
in the tiers of shipping, and somewhere an engine gave
a melancholy groan, as though London, asleep above us, had
dreamed of debt.

"Will they come?" I whispered.

"If they mean coming."

"And if they do not mean coming?"

"They won't."

There was an exactness in Pea's logic which the philosophers
might envy.

For nearly an hour we listened to the dark. Every
sound became a criminal. A loose rope was a skulking
footstep; a piece of floating wood was a guilty boat;
a rat behind a cask was a desperate gang furnished
with pistols. Once a black shape glided from beneath an
arch, and all three policemen became suddenly attentive. The shape
proved to be an empty lighter revolving slowly with the
tide, and appeared so ashamed of the alarm it had
caused that it turned its broad end upon us.

At last Pea gave the signal. Nothing was doing.

"They've got wind of us," he said.

"They could hardly fail to-night," I replied, "for there is
wind enough for every thief in London to have a
private supply."

Pea coughed again.

We descended to the boat, seated ourselves among cloaks, lanterns,
stretchers, ropes, hooks, and other articles suggestive of shipwreck, capture,
resuscitation, and rheumatism, and pulled upward towards Waterloo Bridge.

The river at that hour was not water, but a
long moving grave dug through the heart of the city.
Black walls stood upon either side; black vessels lay in
black ranks; black funnels breathed blacker vapours; and from the
bridges overhead there fell an occasional footstep, solitary and distinct,
as if some invisible giant were pacing from one century
to another. Yet lights trembled here and there—at a tavern
window, in a watch-house, aboard a steamer—and made the darkness
seem not less complete, but more industrious.

At Waterloo we landed.

The bridge frowned above us with its stone brows, and
under a little glazed shelter at the toll stood the
man whom I shall call Waterloo; not because that was
his name, but because he had collected so much of
the bridge into his person that no other designation would
have fitted him. He was grey, square, weather-marked, and solid.
His coat was stone-coloured; his cap was architectural; his eyes
had the patient glitter of two lamps that had seen
much fog. Had Parliament desired to commemorate the battle cheaply,
it might have set him upon a pedestal and saved
the cost of bronze.

"Cold, Waterloo?" asked Pea.

"I've know'd it colder," said he, "and I've know'd it
warmer. What I say is, weather's weather. There's no getting
over it by making observations."

This rebuke being plainly intended for me, though we had
never met, I respected him immediately.

"How long have you served here?" I asked.

"Five-and-twenty year, come Lady Day. Days, nights, Sundays, Christmases, fogs,
frosts, floods, funerals, fireworks, and election mobs. I've took toll
off dukes, donkeys, madmen, mail-carts, soldiers, sweeps, and one escaped
camel. Camel wouldn't pay. He bit."

"Has the bridge been eventful?"

"Eventful?" Waterloo repeated. "Why, bless you, sir, a bridge sees
more human natur' than a churchyard, and sees it afore
burial."

He warmed his hands at a small iron stove, which
looked incapable of warming anything more considerable than a sixpence,
and began.

"There was a woman, now—youngish woman, shawl over her head—come
past me one winter morning about two. Quiet as milk.
She giv' me a look, but she hadn't no vehicle,
so there was no business between us. Presently a cab
comes rattling up, and the driver calls, 'Seen a woman?'
I says, 'I've seen several in my time.' He says,
'One with a red shawl.' I says, 'Gone across.'

"Off he jumps, and off he runs. I thought from
his manner there might be trouble, so I went after
him as far as duty permits, which ain't far, toll
being a jealous occupation. We found her with one foot
on the parapet and him holding the other.

"'Martha,' says he, 'don't!'

"'I will,' says she.

"'Think of the children,' says he.

"'I am thinking of 'em,' says she, 'and that's why.'

"Then he began promising—promising work, promising kindness, promising never to
strike her no more, promising meat of Sundays, promising everything
a man can promise when his wife has one leg
over eternity. She came down at last. He kissed her,
and she scratched him; which I considered healthy on both
sides. Police took 'em home."

"And did he keep his promises?"

Waterloo looked at me with pity.

"Sir," said he, "I collects tolls. I don't collect miracles."

He rubbed his palms and continued.

"Another man went over clean. A clerk by the look
of him, nice boots, white hands, and a paper parcel
under his arm. Paid the foot toll—though he needn't, being
determined not to complete the journey—and says, 'Good-night.'

"'Good-morning,' says I, for it was after twelve.

"'It will never be morning for me,' says he.

"Before I could get the sense of it, he was
up and over. We got him out lower down, but
his morning had come without him. In the parcel was
a plum cake."

"A plum cake?"

"Plum cake. Bought for his little girl, it turned out.
Queer thing to carry to death, ain't it?"

Not so queer, I thought. If Death keeps a gate—and
what great establishment is without one?—perhaps the poor man hoped
to soften the gatekeeper.

Waterloo had already turned to livelier records.

"Then there's the regulars. There's Old Three-Ten."

"Why is he called that?"

"Because he crosses every morning at three-ten and every evening
at three-ten, and has done these eighteen year. Nobody knows
where he goes. He wears a brown coat, carries a
blue bag, and bows to the third lamp from this
end. Never bows to no other lamp. Once the lamp
was took down for repair, and he bowed to the
hole.

"Then there's the General, who ain't a general and never
was. He marches over with a cane under his arm,
orders the omnibuses to form square, and reports the weather
to the Duke of Wellington's statue. Very civil unless opposed.
A cabman laughed at him once, and the General laid
siege to the cab for seven hours.

"And there's the lady in white feathers, who crosses whenever
there's a full moon and asks whether a green carriage
has passed. First time I says no. Second time I
says no. Third time I ventures to ask who's in
it. 'My husband,' says she, 'and he has been dead
since 1829.' Well, I don't dispute with widows. Green carriages
may keep a different time."

"Do many mad people come this way?"

"Scores. Some mad with fever, some with drink, some with
money, and some with the want of it. Money madness
is the quietest and often rides in a private carriage.
Drink madness sings. Want madness looks over the parapet."

Waterloo glanced down the line of the bridge, where the
lamps receded in pale succession and the wind swept between
them like an invisible broom.

"One fellow come naked to the waist, roaring he was
the sun. Wery poor likeness on a freezing night. We
wrapped him in a horse-cloth, which he said was a
cloud, and he was satisfied. Another brought a cage with
no bird in it and warned us not to let
the tiger loose. Another said the river belonged to him
by inheritance and demanded back toll for all vessels since
King John. I told him to apply in writing.

"But the dangerous ones don't always announce themselves. I had
a man walk up quiet, put down a penny, and
strike me with a hammer. Here."

Waterloo removed his cap and showed a dent in his
forehead of a kind which a sculptor would have considered
excessive.

"Did it injure you seriously?" I asked.

"Not so much as might be expected. Doctor said the
head was uncommon hard, and my wife said it was
always wrong in it afore."

"Was the man taken?"

"Pea took him," said Waterloo. "Pea cut into him by
the third lamp and brought him back by the collar."

Pea admitted the achievement with a modest nod, as though
he had caught an umbrella in a gust.

"Robberies?" I prompted.

"Many attempted; fewer done. There was two coves in a
gig, one foggy night, stops here and asks change for
a sovereign. I gives it. They drives off. Sovereign bad.
That's robbery with manners. Next week they comes again, forgetful
of my picter-like memory. 'Change for a sovereign?' says they.
'Certainly,' says I, and changes their gig for a police
van.

"Then there's bilking the toll. You'd wonder what a respectable-looking
person will do to save a penny. Men lies under
straw; boys clings under carts; women declares a goat is
an infant; carriers says a live pig is personal luggage.
One gentleman shut himself in a coffin. Horse bolted, coffin
slid out, gentleman rose in the middle of the bridge
and demanded his hat. I says, 'Dead or alive, sir,
you owes a penny.'

"Another chap drives through fast and cries, 'Fire!' thinking I'll
let him pass. So I stops him. He says, 'Why
d'you stop me when there's a fire?' I says, 'Where?'
He says, 'Somewhere.' That cost him toll and a summons.
We gets 'Fire!' 'Murder!' 'Doctor!' and 'Queen's business!'—anything to save
a copper. I always hopes one of 'em may be
true, but up to now Her Majesty has never sent
a cabbage cart across in her name."

Waterloo's duties, however, had not been all counterfeit sovereigns and
patriotic vegetables.

"Mobs are worst," he said. "A mob has plenty of
hands and no head, so it can hit you without
knowing why. Election night, I've had this window stove in,
box carried off, money scattered, and myself pushed halfway to
Lambeth by parties expressing the British constitution. Once they pitched
a watchman over the rail. He caught a lamp-post and
hung there shouting 'Murder!' Crowd shouted 'Fire!' Some said 'Police!'
and others 'Shame!'—all wery useful. We hauled him up.

"Prize-fight people are rough, too. They comes back full of
gin and glory, with their own faces knocked into every
shape except the human. One asks me what I'm looking
at. I says I'm trying to make out. He hits
the window. I hits the bell. Pea arrives. Scientific proceedings
conclude."

"You have been assaulted often, then?"

"Twenty-one times serious, not counting language. Language don't bruise, though
some of it ought. Been cut twice, struck with sticks
seven times, bottle once, paving-stone once, horse-whipped twice, and bit
by the camel aforesaid. The camel was the most spiteful,
but the paving-stone was heavier."

At this point a cart approached, and Waterloo became at
once less a storyteller than a mechanism of the bridge.
The horse clopped up, the driver leaned down, the toll
passed, the gate moved, and the cart vanished into the
northern dark. Waterloo had taken the coin without interrupting the
sentence in which he observed that people were "a mixed
lot." I could not but admire in him that combination
of philosopher and turnpike which long service had produced.

"Any recent attempt at suicide?" Pea asked.

"Last Tuesday. Boy of fifteen. Lost his place at a
printer's, feared to go home. Caught him climbing. I says,
'What are you doing?' He says, 'Nothing.' I says, 'Do
it on this side.' He cries. Giv' him tea. His
father come for him and began with a strap. I
took the strap away and advised the father that if
beating cured despair, Parliament would have thrashed the whole country
cheerful years ago."

"Good," said I warmly.

"No," said Waterloo. "Common sense."

There are men who do a humane action and blush
if you name it, as though kindness were a youthful
indiscretion. Waterloo was one.

We bade him good-night. He resumed his place beside the
frosted glass, with London passing before him in pennies—each penny
attached to a story, each story crossing out of sight.

Our galley waited below. We pushed off, and Waterloo Bridge
went backward into darkness, carrying its old collector, its mad
generals, dead husbands, bad sovereigns, suicidal clerks, evasive goats, and
ungrateful camel with it.

The tide had turned. We drifted eastward.

"Now," said Pea, "you wanted to know how we work."

I did, though my hands no longer wanted to know
anything except the interior of my pockets.

Pea explained that the visible police upon the river formed
but part of its defence. Boats lay hidden in recesses
under wharves, behind tiers, beside steps, and in narrow cuts
where no casual eye would seek them. They watched silently
and moved at a signal. A thief who believed the
river empty might find a galley at his stern, another
across his bows, and an officer rising beside him with
the disagreeable punctuality of conscience.

"We don't want to catch 'em if we can stop
'em," said Pea. "Prevention's better."

This struck me as remarkable modesty in a profession popularly
imagined to live upon captures, as fishermen live upon fish.

"What classes of river thieves are there?"

"All sorts. Ship thieves, wharf thieves, cargo thieves, coal thieves,
rope thieves, mud thieves. Some works in boats, some aboard,
some from shore. Some's regular hands that steals while employed.
A man may be loading honest with his right hand
and stealing with his left."

Pea described the night raiders. They watched a vessel whose
crew had gone ashore or drunk themselves into navigational insensibility;
approached with muffled sculls; climbed by chain, cable, or rudder;
cut loose bales, brass fittings, stores, cordage, or anything that
could be converted into gin; lowered the spoil into a
boat; and disappeared among the shipping, where one black boat
was no more distinguishable from another than one lie in
a political speech.

"Suppose the crew wakes?"

"Then there may be a fight. Knives, handspikes, marline-spikes, anything
handy. River thieves don't like noise, but they'll make it."

We passed between high-sided vessels lying close together. Their hulls
towered above us like blind houses in a drowned street.
A loose block knocked somewhere overhead; water sucked in the
narrow spaces; rigging made little lamentations in the wind.

"A man might vanish here very easily," I said.

"Many has."

Pea gave no ornament to the words, and therefore they
fell heavily.

He next instructed me in the offences of labourers employed
about cargo. Certain Lumpers, Truckers, and casual hands developed a
surprising enlargement during the day's work. A lean man entered
a warehouse in the morning and came out at dusk
with sugar in his boots, coffee in his cap, tobacco
beneath his shirt, silk round his middle, nails in his
pockets, and perhaps a small article of ironmongery disposed along
the spine. Providence had given him one body; felony supplied
several additions.

"We had one," said Pea, "with two bottles of brandy
tied inside his trousers, a ham under his coat, and
a pound of tea in each sock."

"How did he walk?"

"Religious-like. Very slow and solemn."

"And how was he discovered?"

"He sat down."

Smugglers formed a more ambitious nobility. They dealt in concealed
compartments, false bottoms, innocent barrels with guilty interiors, boats that
fished for everything except fish, and respectable riverside premises whose
cellars communicated with the Thames by ways unknown to surveyors
and well known to excise officers. A cargo might be
landed in morsels—one man carrying a little, ten men carrying
a little, a hundred men knowing nothing whatever about it.

Then came the Dredgermen.

They hovered about the river with hooks and nets, ostensibly
recovering coal, rope, metal, and lawful waifs from the bed
of the Thames. Many were honest. Others possessed a peculiar
fortune by which valuable objects happened to sink wherever they
intended to dredge.

"A man aboard a ship steals copper," said Pea. "Can't
carry it ashore. Throws it over at a place agreed.
Dredger comes by later and brings it up. Or goods
is dropped from a wharf at high water and found
at low. Property goes into the river respectable and comes
out stolen."

Thus the Thames became an accomplice, receiver, hiding-place, highway, witness,
and attempted destroyer of evidence. It held the city's secrets
in suspension: knives, coins, pistols, keys, watches, drowned cats, drowned
hopes, wedding rings flung away in anger, tools cast off
in fear, and now and then the dreadful human evidence
which no tide could make innocent.

"What about coal?" I asked.

"Coal's stolen everywhere. From barges, from wharves, from the mud.
Children picks it, women carries it, men carts it. Hard
to tell theft from hunger sometimes."

"And does the law tell?"

"The law tries. Hunger don't always wait."

Again, Pea's plain sentence silenced the essay I was preparing.

We went on through the Pool, where the shipping thickened
into a forest manufactured by man after he had used
up the natural kind. Masts crossed the sky; spars pointed
nowhere; funnels loomed like extinguished volcanoes. The river widened and
narrowed, twisted past stairs and docks, thrust black tongues into
the land, and received from the city dim noises of
wheels, bells, dogs, machinery, quarrels, and songs. In one waterside
tavern a chorus burst out as we passed, declaring the
singer's determination never to forsake Susan. Susan, if experienced, would
have waited for daylight and evidence.

Our concealed boats found no prey that night. Once we
chased a skiff which pulled away suspiciously, but it contained
only two frightened apprentices, a sack of potatoes, and a
terrier whose conscience was apparently the least easy of the
party. Once a lantern flashed from a warehouse door and
went out; once Pea listened beneath a moored brig and
heard nothing more felonious than a sailor snoring with such
power that the vessel might have claimed steam assistance.

"No catch," said I.

"Quiet river," said Pea.

"Then we have failed?"

"No. If they kept ashore because we was out, we
done our work."

At Wapping we landed by narrow steps glazed with frost.
The station stood ready and wakeful while the neighbourhood slept,
or quarrelled, according to its domestic habits.

Inside, warmth received me so unexpectedly that I could almost
have embraced the stove, though its official position discouraged familiarity.
A sergeant showed me the charge books: broad volumes in
which the night-side of London had been pressed flat into
ink. Drunk and disorderly; assault; suspected goods; attempted robbery; found
wandering; rescued from drowning. Each brief entry was a shut
door with a human commotion behind it.

We saw the equipment rooms, where life-belts, ropes, grapnels, drags,
lanterns, stretchers, handcuffs, blankets, spare clothing, and restoratives were arranged
with military neatness. Here were the instruments for pulling a
man from the river, securing him if violent, warming him
if senseless, carrying him if injured, and returning him, when
possible, to the complicated burden of being alive.

In a cell lay a drunken man.

He had folded himself upon the bench in a shape
not contemplated by anatomy and was addressing an imaginary magistrate.

"I'm a respectable character," he murmured.

The sergeant said he had attempted to fight three sailors,
a lamplighter, his wife, and a door.

"The door," added the sergeant, "gave him that eye."

The prisoner opened the other eye.

"Door took advantage," he said, and slept again.

Last of all, I was shown the blankets, warming apparatus,
brandy, and other provisions kept for the drowned or nearly
drowned. They had been used many times. Some persons recovered;
some did not. No flourish attended either result. The entry
was made, the equipment cleaned, the blanket folded, and the
river went on.

Before leaving, I looked back through the station window towards
the unseen Thames. All night the police boats had glided
under bridges, behind vessels, past stairs and wharves—concealed, patient, and
mostly uncelebrated. They had captured no dramatic villain for my
benefit. They had only made villainy cautious; only kept hands
from cargo, knives from bodies, and despairing feet from parapets;
only watched while London slept.

Pea accompanied me to the door.

"Well," he said, "you've seen it."

"I have seen," I replied, "the black artery of a
monstrous city, bearing upon its tide crime, commerce, misery, courage,
gin, coal, plum cake, and the British Constitution—"

Pea coughed.

I shook his hand and stepped into the cold.

Then, abandoning philosophy, dignity, and all literary observation, I ran
towards Tower Hill as hard as I could go; for
the east wind instantly resumed its unfinished business upon my
person, and I had become persuaded that the warmest truth
about the Thames Police was the station stove.

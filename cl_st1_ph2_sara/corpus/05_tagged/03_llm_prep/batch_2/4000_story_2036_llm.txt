### A Matter of Habit

We were discussing the tyranny of habit in the smoke-room
of the *Macedonia*, somewhere between Gibraltar and the Needles.

There were three of us at first: Withers, who held
opinions upon every subject and generally held them across the
gangway; Bellamy, who disputed whatever Withers asserted as a point
of personal honour; and myself, who had learned during five
days of rough weather that the safest contribution to maritime
philosophy is an occasional nod.

The sea had fallen quiet after dinner. The lamps swung
no more than was necessary to remind us that we
were afloat, and through the open port came a warm
wind, bearing a faint suggestion of oil, salt water, and
the cook's arrangements for breakfast. Withers was smoking a cigar
whose odour suggested bilge water, scorched hair, and the reopening
of an ancient family vault.

Bellamy endured it for several minutes.

"My dear Withers," he said at last, "is that thing
alight by accident, or are you performing some religious penance?"

Withers examined the cigar.

"Three for twopence at Malta," he replied. "They are not
so good as those I bought at Port Said, but
the flavour improves toward the middle."

"There are poisons of which the same may be said,"
returned Bellamy. "Put it out."

"I cannot. I have smoked them for twelve years."

"That is no reason for continuing."

"It is the best of reasons. Habit, my dear fellow,
is the only reliable educator. Repetition makes everything easy—virtue, vice,
matrimony, early rising, dishonesty, and even a cheap cigar."

"Virtue does not become easy."

"Then you have not practised it sufficiently."

Withers spoke without heat. He was one of those men
who will concede any fact except the one under discussion.

"You confuse ease with appetite," said Bellamy.

"Not at all. Appetite is merely habit after it has
ceased to be ashamed of itself. I began these cigars
from economy when I was reading for the Bar. The
first made me ill, the second made my laundress ill,
and the third brought a policeman upstairs under the impression
that the chimney was on fire. At the end of
a month I could smoke one without assistance. At the
end of a year I preferred it. I once accepted
a Havana from a wealthy client and was obliged to
throw it away. It had no character."

"It had tobacco," said Bellamy.

"That was the defect. One misses the string, the lamp-black,
and the full-bodied taste of brown paper. Good tobacco now
seems to me thin and effeminate."

He drew at his cigar with the contented expression of
a man inhaling the air of his native hills.

"It is the same with wine," he continued. "At my
old club we had a claret at eighteenpence the bottle.
Nobody knew where it came from. The steward said Bordeaux,
but I always suspected Birmingham. It had a metallic finish
and left the tongue with a distinct impression of having
been fined ten shillings and costs. New members complained of
it. Old members drank nothing else."

"Because you were old fools."

"Possibly. But we were comfortable fools. A bottle was stolen
one night by two burglars, who drank it in the
pantry. We found one insensible and the other attempting to
confess to crimes committed before he was born. Yet I
grew so accustomed to that claret that, when the committee
changed the bin, I lost both appetite and spirits. For
three weeks I was dead beat. The doctor ordered champagne,
but it was useless. I recovered only when the steward
discovered eleven forgotten bottles behind the coal."

Bellamy shook his head.

"You merely acquired degraded tastes."

"That is my argument. Persist in anything long enough and
you no longer endure it; you require it. The moralist
calls the process discipline when he approves the result and
corruption when he does not. Nature makes no such distinction."

"You would say, I suppose, that a man might become
accustomed to being kicked downstairs?"

"If kicked regularly, at the same hour and by the
same person, he might eventually find the omission disquieting."

"That is nonsense."

"Then listen."

Withers settled himself more deeply in his chair. His cigar
had now reached what he considered its improving portion, and
Bellamy moved nearer the ventilator.

"There was a man in Camden Town," said Withers, "named
Jephson. He kept a shop dealing in lamps, kettles, devotional
prints, paraffin, and those small china dogs which are always
represented as looking for somebody. He married a woman of
exceptional energy. Mrs. Jephson possessed the domestic virtues in a
highly concentrated form. She was industrious, economical, sober, clean, punctual,
and entirely unbearable.

"She rose at six and found fault with the weather.
At seven she found fault with the breakfast; at eight
with Jephson; and from nine until eleven with the girl.
She complained while dusting, argued while sewing, and delivered judgments
upon her neighbours during meals. She never lost her temper,
because she was never out of it. Her voice had
the penetrating quality of a knife-grinder's wheel, and she could
make the words 'Yes, dear' sound like an accusation of
felony.

"Jephson was a mild man. At first he answered her.
Then he defended himself. Then he apologised. After ten years
he merely removed his boots and listened. She talked him
to sleep every night. Her customary subjects were his want
of ambition, his mother's manners, the price of coal, and
the circumstance that he had once, in 1879, brought home
haddock when specifically requested to procure plaice. She would begin
upon the haddock at a quarter to eleven, pass through
his mother at five minutes past, and reach coal as
the clock struck half-past. By that time Jephson was asleep.

"After nineteen years Mrs. Jephson died."

Bellamy looked almost relieved.

"Her death was sudden," continued Withers, "and Jephson was genuinely
afflicted. People said he would enjoy the quiet when he
became used to it. He never became used to it.
The house oppressed him. The kettle boiled without criticism; the
clock ticked without reproach; and when he dropped ash upon
the hearth nobody referred to his father.

"He could not sleep. He went to bed at ten,
at eleven, at midnight. He tried hot milk, cold baths,
patent medicines, theological works, and a volume of parliamentary speeches.
Nothing answered. The doctor told him his nerves were unsettled
by bereavement.

"'It is not bereavement,' said Jephson. 'It is silence.'

"He hired a woman to read aloud in the passage,
but she had a pleasant voice. He lodged for a
week beside a railway arch, yet the trains merely woke
him. What he needed was not noise. He needed personal
abuse, administered in a familiar key and supported by detailed
evidence.

"At the end of eleven months he married again."

"To cure insomnia?" asked Bellamy.

"Substantially. The second Mrs. Jephson was a widow from Holloway.
Before proposing, he made careful inquiries. She was recommended as
ill-tempered, suspicious, and hard to please. Her references were excellent.
A former lodger declared that she had reduced a retired
sergeant-major to tears over the use of a towel.

"The marriage was a success from the first night. She
waited until Jephson had extinguished the candle, then observed that
a man with common consideration would not breathe so loudly.
She continued by criticising his method of hanging his trousers,
his conduct at the wedding breakfast, and the vulgar shape
of his ears.

"Jephson was asleep in seven minutes.

"He afterwards told me that happiness is not the possession
of what is pleasant, but the regular provision of what
one has learned to expect."

Bellamy smiled.

"I admit your Jephson," he said, "as an amusing invention.
I do not admit your premise."

"I can prove the premise from philosophy, physiology, or the
domestic life of any married friend you choose to name."

"You cannot prove that habit can override natural conditions."

"It overrides almost everything."

"Not time, for example."

"Why not?"

"Because noon is noon. A man cannot habituate himself into
treating noon as midnight."

At this point a fourth person, who had been sitting
in the corner with an unlighted pipe and a newspaper
folded upon his knee, looked up.

He was a quiet, spare man with grey whiskers, a
brown travelling suit, and the air of one accustomed to
being overlooked by waiters. We had seen him at meals,
but none of us knew his name. He spoke apologetically,
as if asking permission to close a window.

"I once knew a man," he said, "who did precisely
that."

Bellamy turned to him.

"Precisely what?"

"Regarded noon as midnight—or, more accurately, treated one o'clock in
London as if it were the hour his body expected
elsewhere."

Withers beamed.

"You see?"

"I see a gentleman preparing to support you," said Bellamy.
"That is not the same thing."

"The circumstances were rather singular," said the stranger.

"If they are true," replied Bellamy, "I will believe them."

"That is generous."

"I will go farther. I will bet half a sovereign
that I believe every word."

The stranger considered this.

"You might find it difficult."

"I have believed worse things before breakfast."

He placed half a sovereign upon the little table. Withers
did the same, declaring that he would wager upon truth
wherever found. The stranger did not produce any money of
his own.

"I cannot bet," he explained. "I am to tell the
story."

We admitted the justice of this and ordered whisky.

"The man's name," he began, "was Hiram B. Kedge, of
Stonington, Connecticut. He was a drysalter from nine to four
and a Presbyterian in his leisure moments. For twenty-five years
he lived according to a schedule which had the simplicity
of a hymn and the authority of a mortgage.

"He rose at seven. At seven-five he shaved. At seven-twenty
he conducted family prayers; at seven-forty he ate breakfast; and
at eight-ten he left his house. He reached his office
at nine exactly, except on Sundays, when he reached chapel
at ten-thirty exactly. He lunched at one, returned to business
at two, and departed at four.

"At four-fifteen he rode a chestnut mare for an hour.
At six he dined. At seven-thirty he played whist with
Colonel Spafford, Dr. Pym, and a dealer in agricultural machinery
named Root. At ten he ate an apple, drank one
glass of water, wound his watch, kissed his wife, and
retired. At ten-fifteen he slept.

"There were no variations. His children were born, his partners
failed, governments fell, and several improvements were made in drysalting;
but Mr. Kedge rose, shaved, prayed, ate, laboured, rode, dined,
played, and slept at the appointed minutes.

"The town relied upon him. Shopkeepers corrected their clocks when
he passed. The chapel bell was rung from his appearance
at the corner. It was said that an astronomer, finding
an error in the sun, had written to Kedge for
confirmation.

"He was not a cheerful man, but he was dependable.
Cheerfulness is a quality; punctuality is a system.

"Then his mother's brother died in London.

"The uncle, Mr. Silas B. Kedge, had established a profitable
business in indigo, hides, glue, and certain gums better known
to commerce than to polite society. Hiram was sole legatee
and executor. The English concern could not be transferred to
Connecticut, and Hiram's own business was easily disposed of. He
therefore removed with his wife, two unmarried daughters, and a
coloured servant named Washington to a house near Regent's Park.

"The voyage disturbed him less than was expected. He altered
his watch each day upon information supplied by the purser,
though he suspected the purser of loose principles. On landing
at Liverpool he was in sound health and excellent spirits.

"The disorder began in London.

"On his first morning he failed to rise at seven.
Mrs. Kedge called him, the daughters called him, and Washington
entered with hot water, dropped the can, and withdrew in
alarm. Mr. Kedge continued asleep until one in the afternoon.

"He was horrified. During twenty-five years he had never slept
past seven, except once under chloroform, and even then he
asserted that the surgeon had taken an unfair advantage of
him.

"He hurried to the City without breakfast and arrived shortly
before three. At four he attempted to leave from habit,
but found himself strangely incapable of doing so. His mind
had only just become active. By six he was conducting
three interviews at once and dictating a letter concerning a
cargo of gum tragacanth with a rapidity that exhausted two
clerks.

"At seven his family persuaded him to dine. He could
eat nothing. The sole of his boot, he declared, would
have been equally appetising and less overdone. At one in
the morning, however, he came downstairs in his nightshirt and
demanded beefsteak, buckwheat cakes, coffee, and an apple.

"The next day was the same. He woke at one,
became fit for work at three, desired exercise near midnight,
felt hungry at one, lively at two, sociable at three,
religious at four, and sleepy at five.

"The family attempted reform. Alarm clocks were placed beside him.
One rang at seven, one at seven-five, and a third
at seven-twenty. He rose, silenced them with great determination, shaved
his pillow, prayed into the washstand, and returned to bed.

"Doctors were consulted. The first prescribed iron; the second prohibited
iron; the third declared that the London atmosphere had affected
the liver; and a fourth, who charged three guineas, said
the trouble was American.

"They took Mr. Kedge to Brighton. He slept until one
with a view of the sea. They took him to
Scotland. He slept until one amid mountains. They placed his
bed north and south, east and west, and once diagonally.
The point of the compass made no difference.

"Meanwhile the business suffered. He reached the office when respectable
merchants were considering luncheon. He became efficient only as his
clerks grew pumped out. At eight in the evening he
was at his best, but the City had gone home.
He wrote furious letters at midnight and complained at one
in the afternoon that nobody answered them.

"His health declined. He could not digest dinner at seven,
because he was not hungry; when hunger arrived after midnight,
the household was asleep and the kitchen fire out. He
abandoned riding because he desired it when the Row was
occupied chiefly by policemen, cats, and persons unwilling to explain
their presence.

"Mrs. Kedge feared for his reason. The daughters feared they
would never be invited anywhere. Washington alone remained calm. He
fried beefsteaks at one in the morning and regarded England
as a country in which the clocks had been badly
managed.

"The explanation came from the younger daughter, Miss Sophronia, who
had attended lectures.

"She found her father one night at half-past three, sitting
upright in the drawing-room, reading a sermon and eating cold
mutton. She examined the problem in all its bearings.

"'Father,' she said, 'what time is it now in Stonington?'

"Mr. Kedge calculated.

"'About half-past ten in the evening.'

"'And what would you ordinarily be doing?'

"'Preparing for bed.'

"'That is what you are doing now.'

"On the following day, at one, she asked the question
again.

"'It is seven in the morning at home,' said Kedge.

"'And you have just awakened.'

"They compared each stage. When London called him to breakfast,
his internal arrangements considered it the middle of the night.
When London presented dinner, Stonington expected luncheon—or possibly nothing at
all; the calculations varied according to whether Sophronia included longitude,
summer time, the ship's daily corrections, and an error made
by her father's watch on the third Tuesday out. But
the broad fact remained. Mr. Kedge had crossed the Atlantic;
his habits had not.

"'Your body is keeping Stonington time,' said Sophronia.

"The doctors rejected the theory because Miss Sophronia had not
charged a fee. Mr. Kedge accepted it immediately.

"He had altered his watch, he said, but he had
neglected to alter himself.

"The family then attempted to perform the alteration by degrees.
Each day he was to rise fifteen minutes earlier, dine
fifteen minutes earlier by his personal reckoning, and retire fifteen
minutes earlier. It failed. His system could tolerate punctuality or
chaos, but not compromise. At noon he was incapable; at
one he woke. At twelve-thirty he was merely awake and
incapable, which was worse.

"After six weeks he announced his decision. If his habits
would not conform to London, London—so much of it as
belonged to him—must conform to his habits.

"He established a Stonington day in Regent's Park.

"He rose at one in the afternoon, shaved at one-five,
and conducted family prayers at one-twenty. The servants at first
objected to prayers in the afternoon, but Mr. Kedge pointed
out that devotion is not invalidated by longitude. Breakfast was
served at one-forty. He reached his office at three and
worked until ten.

"This necessitated new clerks. He engaged young men with theatrical
connections, literary ambitions, or debts, all of whom were accustomed
to being unavailable in the morning. The business improved. Foreign
telegrams were answered while competitors slept. A merchant in New
York described the arrangement as enterprise.

"At ten-fifteen Mr. Kedge rode in the Row. The gates
were inconvenient, but he obtained permission after corresponding with three
departments and proving that the mare was a Presbyterian. He
encountered little society. He preferred this, having always considered society
an interruption to exercise.

"At midnight he returned home. Dinner was served at one
in the morning. Mrs. Kedge endured this badly at first,
but habit, as your friend observed, is stronger than preference.
Within three months she could consume soup at one, roast
beef at half-past, and plum pudding at two without more
than ordinary melancholy.

"Whist presented a difficulty. Colonel Spafford, Dr. Pym, and Root
were in Connecticut; and respectable Englishmen declined to begin cards
at half-past two in the morning. Mr. Kedge advertised discreetly
and was introduced to a small club in Soho whose
members played throughout the night.

"They were not, perhaps, the associates he would have chosen
in daylight. One had been warned off several racecourses; one
supported himself by explaining an infallible system; and the third
was known as the Bishop owing to a misunderstanding concerning
church plate. But they played a rigid game and never
suggested stopping before dawn. Mr. Kedge regarded their morals as
defective but their attendance as exemplary.

"At four he ate his apple and drank his glass
of water. At four-ten he woke the family for prayers.

"This was the severest part of the arrangement. Mrs. Kedge
and the daughters could dine at one and even tolerate
Soho acquaintances at whist, but to be summoned from bed
at four-ten and required to sing 'Awake, My Soul' offended
something deeper than convenience.

"Mr. Kedge was firm. He had always conducted family prayers
at seven-twenty Stonington time, and a man who yields in
religion, he said, will soon become irregular in business.

"At five he retired. He slept through the rattle of
carts, the cries of vendors, the postman's knock, and the
piano lessons next door. At one he rose refreshed.

"His health returned. His appetite became punctual, his digestion Presbyterian,
and his temper sufficiently dry for commerce. The business prospered
beyond its former condition. He was mocked, of course. The
evening papers called him 'The Man Who Lives To-morrow' and
'The Transatlantic Owl.' Strangers waited near his house at dawn
to see him draw the blinds and go to bed.

"He minded none of it.

"Sunday alone remained troublesome. When he was prepared for morning
worship, every chapel was closed; when the chapels opened, he
was asleep. He tried an early service once, but dozed
during the sermon and said 'Good night' to an elder
at the door.

"He therefore hired a retired missionary to preach in the
drawing-room at half-past four on Sunday mornings. Attendance consisted of
the Kedge family, Washington, two sleepy maids, and occasionally the
Bishop from the Soho club, who had nowhere else to
go. Mr. Kedge took up the collection himself and entered
it carefully in a book.

"He lived in this manner for seventeen years. He never
returned to America, but America, in the only sense important
to him, never ceased to regulate his meals.

"And that," concluded the stranger, "is why I say a
man may treat noon as midnight if he has treated
it so elsewhere for twenty-five years."

No one spoke immediately.

The ship's engines continued their steady vibration. Somewhere overhead a
sailor dragged a chain across the deck. Withers relighted the
remnant of his cigar, which had gone out during the
account, and the renewed smoke reached us with almost personal
malice.

Bellamy looked at the stranger. He appeared about to question
the longitude, the missionary, the mare, or possibly the Presbyterian
digestion. Then he looked at the half-sovereign upon the table.

Without a word he pushed it across to the stranger,
rose, and left the smoke-room.

The stranger placed the coin in his waistcoat pocket.

Withers watched Bellamy go.

"Conviction," he said, "is also a matter of habit."

But I noticed that he did not ask for his
own half-sovereign back.
